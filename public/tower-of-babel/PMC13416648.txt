Clinical Readiness of Artificial Intelligence Models for MB2 Canal Detection in Maxillary Molars: A Scoping Review
Authors: Amal S. Shaiban, Mohammed A. Alobaid, Omar Saeed Alqahtani, Riyadh Alroomy, Ahmad Jabali, Raid Abdullah Almnea, Wafa H. Alaajam, Vini Mehta, Mohammed M. AlMoaleem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/objectivesThe second mesiobuccal (MB2) canal detection in maxillary molars represents a notable challenge in endodontics. This scoping review (SR) attempts to map the existing research on artificial intelligence (AI)-based models in MB2 detection, delineating the current stage of clinical readiness and gaps associated with routine clinical implementation.MethodsA comprehensive, independent search was conducted in three databases (PubMed, Embase, and Scopus) to synthesize evidence from inception to 20 February 2026, adhering to PRISMA 2020 guidelines and PRISMA-Scr checklist. Study designs, including observational, diagnostic accuracy, as well as experimental designs investigating human permanent maxillary molars employing AI approaches, including machine learning or deep learning models, were included.ResultsFrom 1218 identified records, 5 studies met inclusion criteria. All 5 studies reported strong diagnostic performance of AI models in detecting MB2 canals with favourable diagnostic performance in both deep learning and machine learning models. The clinical readiness assessment using the Technology Readiness Level – Implementation Science framework reported progression of all 5 studies to level 4. Overall risk of bias (RoB) assessment using the QUADAS-2 tool reflected some concerns, highlighting unclear or insufficient methodological components and low overall applicability concerns. RoB assessment was visualized via the ROBVIS tool.ConclusionsOverall, AI models demonstrate favourable diagnostic performance for MB2 canal identification; however, current evidence highlights important limitations in their translational readiness.Clinical relevanceAI-based tools show near-expert accuracy for MB2 detection on cone beam computed tomography, but all current models remain at the prototype stage without real-world validation. They should be used as decision-support adjuncts, not standalone diagnostic systems.

Introduction

Accurate detection of the MB2 canal in maxillary molars represents one of the most complicated challenges in modern endodontics.1,2 Anatomical studies have established that the MB2 canal is present in about 69% to 93% of maxillary first molars,3 but is often missed during conventional root canal treatment, resulting in persistent apical periodontitis and higher retreatment rates.4 The small diameter of the MB2 canal, its complicated curvature course, and common apical convergence with the main mesiobuccal canal contribute significantly to the challenge of detection. Conventional two-dimensional radiography, while widely available, provides only limited5 diagnostic reliability for MB2 localization due to superimposition, geometric distortion, and low contrast resolution.6

Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) has significantly improved the visualization of internal root anatomy, allowing for volumetric analysis with isotropic voxels and increasing the accuracy of canal morphology assessments.7 However, interpretation of CBCT is highly dependent on the experience of the clinician, voxel size, and control of artifacts, which may lead to interobserver variability even among expert endodontists.8 Visual inspection of hundreds of axial, coronal, and sagittal slices is very time-consuming and prone to human mistakes in cases of indistinct or partially calcified canal outlines. All these limitations have hastened investigations into the role of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) as diagnostic aids to enhance the precision, speed, and reliability of detecting MB2 canals.9

AI, in particular deep learning (DL) algorithms-convolutional neural networks (CNN), U-Net, and you only look once (YOLO)-have produced promising results in the identification of anatomic landmarks and subtle radiographic features for dental imaging.10,11 In particular, for endodontics, these models learn spatial and intensity relationships autonomously, enabling them to segment canal structures within CBCT volumes while minimizing bias from operator influence.12,13 Initial results are promising. For example, Mansour et al14 demonstrated an F1-score of 0.93 and recall of 1.00 with a combined CNN + U-Net model that had been trained on MB2 detection. Shetty et al15 reported an area-under-the-curve value of 0.89 in both neural-network and logistic-regression classifiers that were trained over high-resolution CBCT scans. Duman et al16 utilized the YOLOv5x framework and achieved an F1-score of 0.87 with mean average precision of 0.88, ensuring the feasibility of single-stage AI detection and segmentation. This evidence is promising but solely focuses on the diagnostic accuracy of the AI frameworks, showcasing its potential in increasing the diagnostic precision of CBCT image interpretation while minimizing interexaminer inconsistency. Beyond technical performances, there is a substantial literature gap regarding the clinical readiness of these AI models in regular clinical practice.

The translation of AI into dental practices has been constrained,17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23 as most literature has used retrospective datasets collected in controlled laboratory settings, which do not project variability associated with real-world dental practices, including but not limited to different patients’ demographics, image quality, and time constraints.24, 25, 26 Common challenges are limited diversity in the dataset, high risk of bias (RoB), and lack of external validation.27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34 Critical barriers include restricted generalizability of AI applications, cost-effectiveness, and AI tools’ integration into routine dental workflows, which compromises their application across diverse populations and existing imaging modalities.35, 36, 37 Ethical and legal consequences, such as liability during misdiagnosis, data security, and risk of overdependence on AI applications, further downgrade its widespread applicability.21, 22, 23,38, 39, 40, 41, 42

Hence, to overcome similar challenges, it is imperative to understand the extent of validation, clinician involvement, workflow integration, and feasibility for clinical applicability associated with proposed AI-based models in MB2 detection, which goes beyond accuracy. Therefore, the current scoping review (SR) was conducted to map the nature and extent of existing research on AI-based models in MB2 detection, delineating types of AI approaches used, imaging modalities applied (CBCT, periapical radiographs), and the current stage of clinical readiness. This SR will not only provide a comprehensive overview of the evidence map as a guide for future research priorities; it will also bridge the gap between AI-based models’ performance in controlled laboratory settings and routine dental practices, thereby fostering clinically beneficial AI development towards MB2 canal detection.

Materials and methods

Study protocol

We performed this SR to synthesize the existing evidence on AI applications for MB2 detection in maxillary molars and to assess their clinical readiness using an evidence-mapping approach. The SR was conducted under the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidelines,43 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)44 (Figure 1) and PRISMA checklist for Scoping Review (PRISMA-ScR)45 (Table S1). Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted on three databases: PubMed, Embase, and Scopus from inception to 20 February 2026. The PCC (Population–Concept–Context) framework was the foundation to establish extensive search strings with P (Population): Human maxillary first molars evaluated (in vivo/in vitro/retrospective/cross-sectional) for the presence or morphology of MB2 canal; C (Concept): AI–based approaches for MB2 canal detection, classification, segmentation, and morphological assessment; C (Context): Dental imaging including MB2 detection using CBCT and other radiographic approaches, performed in clinical or laboratory settings. The search strategy was formulated using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and Boolean operators (‘AND’, ‘OR’). Each search strategy was individually tailored using controlled vocabulary terms, including MeSH, Emtree, and free-text keywords related to AI, ML, DL, neural networks (NN), CBCT, and MB2 detection. The included studies further underwent cross-citation screening to retrieve any more potentially relevant literature. The in-depth search strategy utilized in this review is provided in Table S2.

Eligibility criteria and study selection

Eligible studies were selected based on the following criteria: (1) full text studies conducted on human subjects or extracted teeth and published in English language; (2) study designs including observational, diagnostic accuracy, as well as experimental designs such as retrospective, prospective, cross-sectional, or validation frameworks were eligible; (3) studies that investigated human permanent maxillary molars in which the focus was on the detection or segmentation or morphological assessment of the MB2 canal using CBCT or other radiographic imaging; (4) AI approaches, including but not limited to ML or DL models like CNNs, U-Net, support vector machines (SVMs), random forests (RFs), or any variant neural architecture developed for MB2 canal detection, localization, or segmentation; (5) study which includes a reference standard such as expert clinical or radiologic evaluation, micro–CT, or validated ground-truth annotations to assess diagnostic accuracy; (6) studies reporting quantitative metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, precision, recall, area under the curve, F1-score, Dice coefficient (DCE), or prevalence of MB2 canals, were considered. Excluded studies include those on nonhuman samples, primary teeth, or phantom models; those not using AI-based detection; and those without reference standards or measurable outcomes. Non-English publications lacking translation, inaccessible full texts, unreviewed preprints, case reports, reviews, conference abstracts, and editorials were also excluded.

The study selection process took place in two stages and was conducted independently by two reviewers (DN, AM). Search outcomes from the databases were extracted in RIS format and exported to Rayyan software for study selection. Rayyan software is a web-based software used to facilitate SR screening. Although Rayyan has AI-assisted features for streamlining the screening process, these features were not used in this review.40 All screening decisions were taken manually by the reviewers to maintain methodological rigour and consistency. Any conflicts during the study selection process were solved by mutual consensus and by a third reviewer (VM) as and when required. At first, duplicated records were removed, and then initial screening was done based on the title and abstract of the reviews. After initial screening, the full text of the selected studies was further screened for final inclusion (Figure 1).

Data extraction

The extracted data from the included studies were compiled in an MS Excel (version 2019) spreadsheet. The extracted data included the following characteristics: author, published year, region (Country), study design, AI model/architecture used, imaging modality, sample (patients/scans), teeth/roots used, image segmentation software used, data groups (classification), performance matrices (sensitivity/specificity/accuracy/positive predictive value/negative predictive value/precision/area under curve [AUC]/F1 score/Recall/DCE/logistic regression [LR], Naïve Bayes [NB], SVM, K-nearest neighbours [kNN], RF, NN), reference standard (ground truth used), ethical approval, AI checklist used and Technology Readiness Level (TRL) related information.

Assessment of TRL

The TRL is a universal tool for the assessment of progress and development of a technology from its research inception to complete implementation in a real-world or operational setting.46 Primarily developed by NASA in 1970 as a 7-level assessment, it was modified in 1995, with the addition of two levels (levels 8-9). In healthcare scenarios, TRL was adapted in the field of pharmaceuticals, medical-related products (eg, treatment models, healthcare delivery strategies), and health information technologies.47, 48, 49 However, due to complex parameters and challenges related to adaptation in implementation research contexts, TRL – Implementation Science (TRL-IS) was developed.50 TRL-IS assesses the technological progress in implementation research from basic principles to prerelease/release deployment, keeping intact the 9 levels. TRL-IS framework was applied in our SR to determine the translational maturity of AI technologies used in the included studies. This will not only help us in assessing the clinical readiness stage of AI-based applications for detection of MB2 canal, it will also help in identifying translational gaps hindering routine clinical implementation. The map of TRL-IS with its grouping and implementation phases has been demonstrated in Table 2, and a brief description of each step has been given in Table S3. For each of the TRL-IS levels, the assessment was done based on ‘yes’ or ‘no’. Each of the included studies was carefully assessed through the lens of TRL-IS contexts to map their respective readiness levels. Here, ‘readiness’ terms refer to ‘The level of preparedness of an application of the emerging scientific knowledge to be used in the real world and for its release, marketing, commercialization or open access’.50 To enhance TRL-IS assessment. Operational criteria were predefined for each readiness level before the evaluation. TRL-IS levels 1 to 4 were considered achieved when studies demonstrated basic conceptual development, proof-of-concept, prototype development, and internal validations under controlled conditions. Completion of TRL-IS level 5 required evidence of independent external validation. Levels 6 to 9 required evidence of assessment under relevant clinical conditions, real-world deployment, implementation, regulatory preparedness, or routine clinical usage. Each study was assigned the highest TRL-IS level for which all predefined criteria were achieved. Two reviewers (DN and AM) independently performed the assessments using a standardized data extraction form. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and where consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (VM) adjudicated the final assessments.

Although several frameworks have been reported to evaluate AI translation and implementation in healthcare research, for example, Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis + AI (TRIPOD+AI) and Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging, these tools primarily focus on assessing the reporting quality, methodological rigour, and regulatory considerations rather than implementation readiness. Hence, in this study, we have also used TRIPOD+AI to assess the reporting guidelines. TRL-IS was considered in this study to assess the TRL as it specifically evaluates the progression of AI model/framework from conceptual development to real-world implementation, which aligns with our study objectives.

Quality assessment

The Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies tool (QUADAS-2)51 was used to assess the methodological quality (RoB and applicability concern) of the included studies. The QUADAS-2 tool consists of 4 domains. First, RoB regarding data selection was assessed under ‘consecutive or random patient enrolment’, ‘case-control design’, and ‘inappropriate exclusions’. Second, RoB in the index test was assessed under ‘independent assessment (blinding)’ and ‘usage of prespecified thresholds’. Third, ROB in the reference standard was examined using ‘reference standard validation’ and ‘independent assessment (blinding)’. Fourth, RoB in flow and timing was assessed under ‘appropriate interval between index test and reference standard’, ‘reference standard applied to all patients’, and ‘analysis done including all patients’. The same tool was used for assessing applicability concerns in three domains. First, applicability concerns for data selection were assessed under ‘disparity between the included patients and the review question’. Second, applicability concerns for the index test were assessed via ‘disparity between the test, its conduct, or interpretation and the review question’. Last, applicability concerns for the reference standard were assessed via ‘disparity between the target condition as per the reference standard and the review question’. Each domain was rated for its RoB and applicability concerns with respect to arriving at the overall methodological quality of the studies. For the visualization of the RoB assessment of all the included studies, the ROBVIS visualization tool was used.52 Although the QUADAS-2 tool is widely accepted for the assessment of ROB in diagnostic accuracy studies, it was not specifically framed for AI-based diagnostic models. Therefore, certain AI-specific methodological concerns, such as data leakage, overfitting, hidden test-set contamination, model drift, and algorithmic bias, may not be completely assessed under the QUADAS-2 framework. Nevertheless, this tool was selected because of its wide acceptability and structured assessment of methodological quality across key domains.

Assessment of adherence to reporting guidelines across included studies was assessed using the TRIPOD+AI tool.53 It is a 27-item checklist that provides guidelines for studies developing AI-based models for diagnosis or prognosis to evaluate their performance. This tool was applied in our SR because all the included studies cover prediction models, including but not limited to ML methods. TRIPOD+AI checklist is an addition to TRIPOD 2015 comprising title (item 1), abstract (item 2), introduction (items 3 and 4), methods (items 5-17), open science practices (item 18), patient and public involvement (item 19), results (items 20-24), and discussion (items 25-27). Some items consist of multiple subitems, total comprising of 52 checklist subitems (Table S4).

Data synthesis

The findings in our SR have been synthesized using a narrative approach illustrated with tables and figures to map the characteristics and clinical readiness level of the included studies. This synthesis will highlight the nature of existing AI applications used in MB2 detection, identify gaps regarding validation as well as routine clinical workflow integration, and highlight concerns related to RoB and applicability, thereby guiding research priorities and methodological progression required to complement AI-based MB2 detection modalities from experimental stage to clinical implementation.

Results

Study selection

A systematic search across 3 databases retrieved a total of 1218 studies comprising 465 studies from PubMed, 654 from Embase, and 99 from Scopus. After removing 337 duplicate articles, 881 were screened for title and abstract. From these, 7 studies were considered relevant for full-text retrieval and screening. Among the 7 studies considered, 2 were excluded due to the unavailability of full-text articles, despite attempts to contact the corresponding authors. The remaining 5 studies satisfied the predefined inclusion criteria and were included in the SR. The detailed screening and selection process is depicted in the PRISMA flowchart (Figure 1).

Characteristics of included studies

The included studies comprised a diverse range of diagnostic accuracy designs evaluating AI applications for detecting or segmenting the MB2 canal in maxillary molars using CBCT and other periapical radiographs. Out of 5 included studies, 4 studies14, 15, 16,54 were retrospective in nature, with one employing an in vitro experimental design approach55 (Table 1). Various DL architectures have been used in the included studies, such as 3D U-Net (CNN-based),54 YOLOv5,16 and BasicVSR++ (DL SR model).55 Mansour et al14 has employed two step model (CNN + U-Net), and 6 ML models (LR, NB, SVM, kNN, RF, NN) were used in Shetty et al15 The predominant imaging modality used in all the studies was CBCT (n = 5), varying from field-of-view scans to axial sections. Four out of 5 studies14, 15, 16,54 have employed retrospective scans from clinical registries as datasets in their study; however, Ji et al55 have used extracted teeth for their diagnostic accuracy assessment. Three studies have mentioned image segmentation software used, with the majority being ITK-SNAP (open-source free software version 3.8.0). The validation approaches for the AI-based diagnostic application have been predominantly internal cross-validation for all the included studies, with no reports on external and multicentre validation. Almost all studies have used the ground truth as the reference standard, where 4 studies14, 15, 16,54 have considered consensus from experienced Oral and Maxillofacial Radiologists and 1 study55 has considered micro–computed tomography (Micro-CT) as the gold standard reference. Diagnostic performance of AI models based on various tasks

Classification models

Mansour et al14 described a tailored 2-step DL approach that integrated a CNN for image classification with a U-Net–based model for segmentation. The system reported strong diagnostic performance, reaching an F1 score of 0.93, an overall accuracy of 0.87, and a recall of 1.00. The segmentation module performed consistently with a DCE of 0.79 for the testing dataset. Shetty et al15 carried out a comparative analysis of six ML models using features extracted from a pretrained Inception v3 network. Their NN, LR, and SVM models showed almost similar diagnostic efficiencies (LR-AUC: 0.885; CA: 0841; F1: 0.841; Precision: 0.841, NN-AUC: 0.903; CA: 0.838; F1: 0.837; Precision: 0.837, SVM-AUC: 0.887; CA: 0.819; F1: 0.815; Precision: 0.826). Among the proposed models, the NN model gave the best overall performance. The interrater reliability coefficient between expert annotations was 0.85, which further confirmed the reliability of the ground truth and the robustness of model evaluation.

Detection, localization, and segmentation models

Albitar et al54 applied a 3D U-Net architecture to detect and segment unobstructed MB2 canals in endodontically treated maxillary molars. The model has yielded a sensitivity score of 0.80, a specificity of 1.00, and an overall accuracy of 0.90, with positive and negative predictive values of 1 and 0.83. Duman et al16 employed YOLOv5x, a DL framework for one-stage identification and segmentation of MB2 canals in CBCT axial sections. The algorithm gave a sensitivity of 0.92, a precision of 0.83, an F1 score of 0.87, and an AUC of 0.84. They have also reported a mean average precision of 0.88 at an intersection over union threshold of 0.5, indicating real robustness in performance (Table 1).

Super-resolution (SR) reconstruction models

Ji et al55 have employed BasicVSR++, a DL SR model, in CBCT, synchrotron radiation computed tomography, and Micro-CT imaging modalities for detection of the MB2 canal. Keeping Micro-CT score as the gold standard (100%), CBCT has reported an accuracy of 72%, and synchrotron radiation computed tomography showed 92%.

RoB and applicability concerns

Assessment of RoB and applicability concerns was done using the QUADAS-2 tool. The RoB was assessed in four domains: data selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. In terms of data selection, index test and reference standard, RoB showed ‘some concerns’ for all 5 studies; however, in flow and timing, the RoB was rated as low (Table 3, Figure 2). All the studies reflected ‘some concerns’ in the overall RoB assessment, highlighting unclear or insufficient methodological components, specifically regarding sampling strategy, blinding process, predefined thresholds, as well as reference standard evaluation, which might create uncertainty in the validity. On the other hand, applicability concerns were found to be high for 20% of the studies towards data selection and were reported low for all the studies regarding the index test (100%) and reference standard (100%) (Figure 3). The high applicability concern is attributed to Ji et al55 as their data selection (in vitro experiment including extracted teeth) deviates from the intended review question of the SR. The overall applicability concern was reported as low. Adherence to reporting standards

The TRIPOD reporting checklist showcased overall adherence as 51%, with the majority of the domains reporting an adherence rate of more than 80% (Figure 5 and Table S4). Reporting adherence was 40% for title, 57.7% for abstract, and 60% for target population and intended purpose. Nonadherence (0%) was observed in items including but not limited to health inequalities between sociodemographic groups, data preprocessing, blinding process, handling of missing data, and heterogeneity. Adherence to the source of data mentioned was 80%; the dates of data collection were not mentioned by any of the included studies. It is noteworthy that important factors such as sample size justification, clear definition of predictors, as well as outcomes and demographic characteristics of the outcome assessors, had an adherence rate of less than 70%. Discussion

Undetected canals are frequently observed in maxillary molars, which come into play during endodontic procedures.56,57 The identification of the MB2 canal is challenging, even for experienced dental surgeons, owing to its angulation and variable appearance.58, 59, 60 The occurrence of MB2 canals in maxillary molars has demonstrated variability based on race, age, and sex.57,61,62 Various methodologies, including but not limited to direct vision, canal staining, selective dentin removal, dental operating microscopes, periapical radiographs, and CBCT, have been used to detect MB2 canals.62,63 Of these, CBCT has been widely used to assess root canal structure, including MB2 canal detection, considering its high 3-D visualization and resolution with accuracy, less distortion, and a noninvasive procedure.62,64 A previous systematic review and meta-analysis by Dashti et al65 have descriptively examined the efficacy of various AI models in the identification of the MB2 canal in maxillary first molars, demonstrating significant detection potential enhancing accuracy, ease, and reliability. However, there is a dearth of information regarding the progress of these promising AI models from laboratory-setting experiments to routine clinical implementation. Hence, this SR attempts to present a consolidated assessment of the clinical readiness of AI-based applications for MB2 canal detection in maxillary molars with an evidence mapping approach. An SR approach was considered appropriate here as the objective was extended beyond narrative synthesis to systematically map the available evidence in characterizing the methodological and reporting practices related to the existing AI applications.

In all 5 included studies, AI-based models have demonstrated consistent high performance, with sensitivity and accuracy values greater than 0.80, indicating near-expert or even higher diagnostic performance. These findings collectively confirm that AI-based models can be a reliable adjunct in CBCT interpretation for endodontic diagnostics to tackle one of the most persistent clinical challenges-missed canal identification. However, high diagnostic performance metrics such as AUC, F1 score, sensitivity, or precision do not necessarily translate into real-world clinical benefit, workflow integration, or improved patient outcomes. Improvement in clinical utility requires demonstration of high diagnostic decision-making capability, workflow efficiency, interoperability with the existing CBCT software platforms, chairside usability, acceptable computational requirements, clinician acceptance, remarkable treatment outcomes, cost-effectiveness, and sustainable reimbursement pathways under real-world scenarios.

In our review, a diverse range of potential AI models have been used for MB2 detection, such as U-Net by Albitar et al,54 Mansour et al14 (along with CNN), YOLOv5x by Duman et al,16 BasicVSR++ by Ji et al,55 and 6 ML models (LR, NB, SVM, kNN, RF, NN) by Shetty et al.15 Likewise, these models have been used for various other dental procedures for example, CNN has been employed in automated detection system for endodontically treated teeth,66 tooth detection and segmentation,67 C-shaped canal predictions,68 diagnosis of deep caries and pulpitis on periapical radiographs,69 application of YOLOv5x in detection of root canal obturation from noisy radiographs,70 NN in measuring working length using multifrequency impedance71 and U-net for tooth and pulp segmentation in CBCTs.72 The findings in our SR follow the general line of advancement in AI applications for dental diagnostics. DL systems, particularly based on CNNs, U-Net, and transformer-based architectures, have already shown high accuracy with dental caries, pulp-chamber segmentation, and periapical lesion detection tasks.73, 74, 75 In scenarios such as MB2 detection, these models have shown their ability to learn hierarchical representations of root canal morphology from images, by which they can recognize subtle grayscale variations and small calcified canal pathways that may be overlooked by the human observer in CBCT slices. It is also noteworthy that the included studies addressed heterogeneous AI tasks instead of uniform application. Hence, direct comparison of diagnostic performance across studies should be done with caution, as varied AI tasks might follow distinct developmental, validation, and translational pathways. Similarly, although several DL and ML models have demonstrated favourable diagnostic performance, direct comparison between them should be interpreted with caution because the included studies varied across sample sizes, AI tasks, datasets, architectures, and outcome measures. Additionally, the heterogeneity of CBCT acquisition protocols across studies, such as differences in voxel size, field of view, scanner type, reconstruction algorithms, and image quality, may significantly influence the model performance and generalizability, as it limits the transferability of AI models developed under specific imaging conditions to other clinical settings. Therefore, future studies should report imaging parameters comprehensively and assess model performance across various CBCT systems and acquisition protocols to improve clinical applicability and generalizability.

The technological readiness mapping of the AI-based models used in our SR revealed that all the studies had TRL-IS level not exceeding 4 (prototyping), which shows that they have completed the conceptual stage. None of the included studies have progressed beyond internal validation under laboratory settings, which highlights a longstanding gap between conceptualization and real-world deployment. These findings are comparable with the study conducted by Nassani et al76 assessing TRL of robotics and AI in dentistry, where they observed that approximately half of the studies have not exceeded level 3 (proof of concept). Also, our findings align with Grischke et al77 who demonstrated that approximately 75% of studies are within level 3 of TRL. However, it is noteworthy that these studies have employed the TRL framework, which is not fit for purpose in health implementation research; hence, the researchers should use the TRL-IS framework, which is the customized and updated version of TRL suitable for implementation-related science in health and social research.50 However, it is noteworthy that the exhibited methodological heterogeneity across the included studies with respect to AI architectures, sample characteristics, validation approaches, and reference standards may influence the interpretation of the TRL-IS assessment. Therefore, the interpretation of the readiness level should be taken as an indication of the current stage of development of the individual AI applications rather than a direct comparison among the included studies.

Assessment using the QUADAS-2 framework showed certain methodological limitations within the current body of evidence, reporting some concerns in overall RoB. Almost all the included studies didn’t clarify the data selection process, which might raise the chances of selection bias, which limits representativeness. In terms of index test and reference standard, it was not clear in the included studies whether the test was performed independently of the reference standard’s results and vice versa. Lack of reporting of blinding methods underscores the probable RoB in diagnostic review, where the reference standard might influence the index test result. Hence, future studies need to clarify the blinding methods involved in the index test as well as the reference standard. Another crucial methodological consideration is the quality of the reference standard used for model development and evaluation. Most included studies relied on expert interpretation or consensus-based annotations as the ground truth for MB2 canal identification. Although expert consensus remains the most practical reference standard in endodontic imaging-based research, MB2 canal detection itself is subject to interobserver variability because of anatomical complexity, canal calcification, image quality, and variation in clinical experience. Consequently, annotation errors or inconsistencies may introduce bias into model training and performance evaluation. Future studies should also provide reporting of annotation procedures, numbers and expertise of annotators, interobserver agreement measures, and methods used to resolve disagreements to enhance the reliability of AI-based MB2 detection models.

Furthermore, none of the studies clarify the measurement threshold selection. In the case of AI-based models, failure in prespecifying cut-offs might inflate reported accuracy. Collectively, stringent reporting on sample selection strategy, blinding methods, and threshold specification measures is paramount in maintaining methodological rigour and mitigating RoB for sustained translational progress in AI-based MB2 detection models. In case of applicability concerns, the QUADAS-2 framework was low in all the included studies. Four studies14, 15, 16,54 reported low concerns in all three domains (data selection, index test, and reference standard), showcasing an overall low applicability concern of the AI-based models. This implies that, although methodological limitations were observed in RoB assessment, the target data/population, applied index tests, and reference standards were in alignment with the review questions of our SR. Contrastingly, Ji et al55 reported high applicability concerns due to its in vitro study design using extracted teeth instead of clinical imaging datasets.

TRIPOD-AI checklist was used in this SR to support editors, peer reviewers, policymakers, and end users in comprehending the AI-driven research done for MB2 canal detection in maxillary molars in order to enhance its translational component. An overall adherence rate of 51% was observed in our review, showcasing moderate compliance across the key domains. The compliance rate was comparatively higher than the rate observed in a study conducted by Arsiwala-Scheppach et al78 (33.3%), which assessed the comprehensive ML application in the dental field. Although the review question was different among the reviews, it is noteworthy that Arsiwala-Scheppach et al have employed the TRIPOD 2015 checklist, a previous version of the TRIPOD-AI checklist. Though most of the domains, for example, data availability, ethical compliance, declaration of conflict of interest, limitations, as well as future implications – reported 100% adherence, substantial items had poor adherence to the reporting standards. These reporting deficiencies may limit transparency and make independent appraisal challenging; however, they do not necessarily indicate poor model performance. Also, the interpretation of the TRIPOD-AI adherence should extend beyond the overall percentage, as certain reporting items have significant methodological importance compared to others. For instance, deficiencies in reporting missing data handling, blinding procedures, heterogeneity assessments, and external validation are particularly relevant. Among these, inadequate reporting of external validation and blinding procedures is especially important, as these factors may affect the generalizability and reliability of the model performance. These methodological limitations underscore important areas where improvement is pivotal in future research focusing on AI-based MB2 detection.

From a clinical perspective, AI-based applications have the potential transformative role in dentistry,79, 80, 81 particularly in various endodontic practices, including the detection of MB2 canals in maxillary molars. This includes the application in the detection of periapical lesions,82 identification of root canal orifice,83 and root canal evaluation.84 Even with the CBCT, MB2 canals are often neglected, with superadded varying diagnostic consistency among clinicians.85,86 AI-based applications that flag the MB2 canal’s presence could not only save time but also improve outcomes with minimal errors. Though AI must not replace clinical decision-making, its implementation should complement the diagnosis/detection of the MB2 canal. However, beyond diagnostic performance, its successful clinical implementation necessitates careful consideration of ethical, legal, and regulatory factors. The development and validation of AI models depend on large CBCT datasets, which underscores the need for privacy protection, data deidentification, and procedures related to secure data-sharing. Moreover, medico-legal responsibilities remain unclear when AI-based MB2 detection fails to identify the canal, potentially contributing to missed diagnosis or treatment failure. Hence, it is imperative for AI-based dental software to undergo suitable regulatory evaluation and approval before routine clinical application to ensure safety, reliability, and clinical effectiveness. Additionally, future AI-based research requires robust compliance with regulatory frameworks that govern the software as a medical device before routine real-world clinical application. Addressing these challenges will be important for the translation of AI-based MB2 detection systems from experimental framework into routine dental practice. Future research should emphasize balancing continued algorithmic innovation with greater focus on external validation, implementation research, and progression through higher stages of the TRL-IS framework. Although the development of novel AI algorithms may enhance diagnostic performance, existing models also warrant equal attention towards improving clinical applicability, generalizability, and real-world effectiveness. Future research should also prioritize multicentre collaborations involving diverse patient populations and imaging datasets to ensure broader clinical applicability across different healthcare settings. This is particularly relevant in the case of MB2 canal detection, as the prevalence and anatomical characteristics of the MB2 canal vary across diverse demographic populations.

There are several strengths in our SR. First, this SR has attempted to provide a comprehensive overview of the technological readiness of the AI applications in the detection of MB2 canals using the TRL-IS framework. Second, by incorporating methodological quality assessment of the included studies using the QUADAS-2 and TRIPOD-AI checklists, this review provides a multidimensional assessment of the current landscape in emerging AI applications for MB2 detection. Third, we have used the updated reporting standards as compared to the previous studies, for example, TRIPOD-AI rather than TRIPOD 2015, TRL-IS framework rather than TRL, which supports the credibility of our findings. However, there are certain limitations that needs attention. First, the current evidence base is extremely limited, comprising only 5 studies. Hence, the findings from this study should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory evidence rather than a definitive assessment. Second, the exclusion of interdisciplinary databases such as IEEE Xplore, Web of Science, and Google Scholar might have limited the study identification, given the rapidly evolving nature of AI research in dental applications. Third, heterogeneity in AI-based applications, sample size, and imaging modalities complicates the applicability of standard methodological frameworks, which challenges evidence synthesis. Fourth, the predominance of retrospective single-centre datasets may restrict the generalizability of the findings, given the variability in MB2 canal prevalence and anatomical structures across diverse populations. Fifth, lack of clarification towards ground truth labels and related annotation procedures for MB2 canal identification may have hampered the reliability factor of the performance metrics, as MB2 detection itself is subject to interobserver variability. Lastly, the studies lack standardized guidelines for training AI models, validation, and reporting, which limits transparency, comparability across studies, and routine clinical integration.

Conclusions

AI models have demonstrated promising diagnostic performance for the detection and segmentation of MB2 canals in maxillary molars from CBCT and radiographic images. However, current evidence also highlights important limitations in the TRL of these AI models. Methodological limitations, including inadequate reporting of sample size determination and index tests, limited validation, and lack of real-world testing, indicate that the existing models remain at an early stage of translational maturity. More importantly, the findings from this review should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of eligible studies and significant methodological heterogeneity. Future research should balance continued algorithmic development with comprehensive external validation, multicentre involvement and real-world implementation of the existing frameworks. The use of standardized tools such as QUADAS-2, TRIPOD-AI reporting guidelines, and TRL-IS may improve the quality, transparency, and clinical applicability of future research. While AI-based MB2 detection frameworks have shown potential, further evidence is required to achieve routine clinical adoption.
