Proprioceptive-augmented virtual reality influences the kinematic properties of movement imitation
Authors: Ambra Bisio, Monica Biggio, Elisa Soldi, Alice Massa, Costanza Iester, Laura Bonzano, Marco Bove
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVirtual Reality (VR) has been proposed as a tool in rehabilitation to create more realistic, interactive, and engaging environments that can enhance the sense of embodiment, and thus possibly the effect of the treatment itself. Previous research has shown that combining movement observation with somatosensory stimulation may further improve training outcomes. The present study aimed to examine whether a protocol integrating the observation of human movements within an VR environment with proprioceptive stimulation, thereby establishing a Proprioceptive-Augmented virtual Reality (PAR) paradigm, could enhance participants’ ability to imitate the dynamic properties of movements presented in VR.MethodsTwenty-three healthy young adults were asked to imitate the features of a flexion–extension forearm movement observed in an immersive VR environment, either while receiving or not receiving 80 Hz muscle tendon vibration that induced an illusory sensation of movement congruent with the observed direction (PAR). The imitation task was performed with the stimulated forearm either after the stimulation had ended (OFFLINE imitation) or concurrently with it (ONLINE imitation). Participants’ ability to reproduce the avatar’s mean and maximal velocities was assessed (reproduction error). Root means square error (RMSE) between avatar and participants’ trajectory was calculated. In addition, a questionnaire evaluating the sense of embodiment was administered after the OFFLINE imitation task under both conditions, as well as the evaluation of the vividness evoked by PAR and VR.ResultsIn the OFFLINE task the reproduction error was lower in PAR than in VR, while the opposite was obtained in the ONLINE task. In the OFFLINE task no difference emerged between PAR and VR RMSE values, whilst in the ONLINE task PAR RMSE was higher than that in VR. PAR evoked a higher sense of embodiment and higher vividness compared to VR when considering all the domain tested (body ownership, sense of agency, location of the body).ConclusionsThese findings indicate that proprioceptive stimulation in the PAR condition positively affected movement reproduction during the OFFLINE imitation task, likely benefiting from the heightened sense of embodiment and vividness elicited in this condition. This suggests that muscle tendon vibration can enhance the perception of self-motion in VR, enabling richer interaction strategies and offering promising prospects for sensorimotor rehabilitation.

Background

Among neurorehabilitation strategies, several treatment protocols have been based on the hypothesis stating that a neural network including motor areas can be activated even during observation or imagery of a motor action [1–3]. Action Observation Treatment (AOT) is a widely recognized rehabilitation approach based on the activation of the mirror neuron system. Imitation is intrinsically linked to this process, as it implies action observation (AO) followed by the actual execution of movements, which helps building or rebuilding motor memory [4]. In clinical practice, a typical AOT session involves the patient observing video clips of daily actions (e.g., drinking from a cup, washing hands) followed by the physical execution of the same tasks [4].

The use of Virtual Reality (VR) has been proposed to administer AOT to provide more realistic, interactive, and motivating environments that can enhance the sense of embodiment [5], and thus possibly the effect of the treatment. Indeed, if the visual stimulus is provided in a VR immersive environment, in a first-person perspective, the sense of ownership (i.e., the illusory perception persons might have that an artificial body part is their own), the sense of agency (i.e., the sensation of recognizing themselves as the cause of the actions of the virtual avatar) and the location of the body (the sensation that a substitute body or body part, such as an avatar or a rubber hand, belongs to the self) evoked by such condition might improve participants’ ability to imitate the movement of a virtual limb [6]. Furthermore, the use of a VR-based setup ensured a constant and fully controlled visual environment across conditions, thereby minimizing potential confounding effects of visual input on motor performance [7–10]. In this context, imitation and immersion emerge as two core pillars for maximizing the efficacy of AOT and related neurorehabilitation protocols.

An issue that warrants further investigation is the optimal timing of imitation, specifically whether it should occur concurrently with AOT or VR exposure (simultaneous, ONLINE imitation), or be implemented subsequently (sequential, OFFLINE imitation) [11]. Indeed, AOT is most effective when observation is followed immediately by physical practice [12], as this process facilitates the consolidation of motor memory traces activated by AO [4]. The timing of imitation in VR-based studies varies depending on the rehabilitation goal, encompassing both ONLINE imitation [1, 13], and OFFLINE imitation [14].

Another point concerns the sensory feedback patients receive during these types of interventions. A recent review on the effectiveness of AOT classified the quality of the evidence as moderate for hand function, low for arm function, and very low for motor performance and quality of life [12]. Indeed, AOT alone could not be enough for patients with severe sensorimotor impairments degrading afferent sensory feedback, thereby weakening imitation quality and limiting motor skill consolidation during rehabilitation [15].

A promising rehabilitation approach involves both integrating AOT delivered through VR with conventional sensorimotor training [16], or exploiting multisensory stimulation, thus combining VR with auditory pacing [1, 13], or haptic feedback [14]. In the same vein, a promising strategy to enhance the effectiveness of VR-based interventions is to integrate somatosensory inputs that are tightly coupled with movement execution, such as proprioceptive feedback, thereby delivering a more comprehensive and embodied sensorimotor training experience. Proprioception plays a key role in sensing the position and movement of the body segments independently from vision. Among the specialized proprioceptors involved in this process are muscle spindles, which can be activated by proprioceptive stimulation consisting in muscle tendon vibration at a frequency ranging between 80 and 100 Hz [17]. Proprioception can also refer to the afferent information from Golgi tendon organs. Indeed, proprioceptive feedback is primarily mediated by muscle spindles, which detect changes in muscle length and movement velocity, and by Golgi tendon organs, which sense muscle tension and force generation, thereby contributing to motor control. Muscle tendon vibration might also activate Golgi tendon organs, although it stimulates mainly muscle spindles. However, the activation of muscle spindles, through muscle tendon vibration, can drive kinesthetic illusion due to muscle lengthening information, whilst Golgi tendon organs mainly monitor active muscle tensions and forces rather than muscle stretching [18].

At the same time, mechanical cutaneous receptors, which are not specialized proprioceptors, can also contribute to proprioceptive body representation, in particular for the aspects related to haptic information. It should be noticed that, although the term haptic feedback often includes proprioception, in the present study we distinguish proprioceptive signals arising from artificial stimulation of muscle spindle from cutaneous haptic information based on their distinct neurophysiological underpinnings. Indeed, vibration with lower frequency and lower amplitude displacements does not evoke the activation of muscle spindles but only of cutaneous receptors whilst muscle tendon vibration at a frequency ranging between 80 and 100 Hz are specific of muscle spindles [19].

Across a series of studies, we showed that combining AO and proprioceptive stimulation promotes plasticity in the primary motor cortex (M1) [20]. Thus, it can be hypothesized that administering proprioceptive stimulation through muscle tendon vibration may further enrich VR experience creating an augmented reality scenario (Proprioceptive-Augmented virtual Reality, PAR), potentially increasing the effectiveness of the treatment on both movement execution and perception [21].

The aim of this study was to explore the possibility of using PAR to improve the dynamic properties of motor imitation, which is a simple and effective approach to test both the occurrence and the quality of motor learning. To achieve this goal, we tested healthy participants’ ability to imitate in OFFLINE and ONLINE conditions the movement observed when immersed in the VR or PAR environment. Further, after OFFLINE imitation, we evaluated the sense of agency and ownership.

In this task, we adopted a novel PAR protocol with enhanced ecological validity, as it employed vibration durations (about 1 s) consistent with spontaneous movements [20], unlike previous protocols using prolonged stimulation (10 s) to elicit kinesthetic illusions [19, 22]. Although longer stimulation is typically used to induce fully developed kinesthetic illusions, previous evidence suggests that proprioceptive afferences can emerge within shorter time windows [23] giving raise to illusory movement sensation [24]. In our paradigm, the aim was not to elicit a sustained illusion per se, but rather to provide a brief, task-relevant proprioceptive input temporally aligned with the observed movement. This approach better approximates real movement timing and may facilitate future applications in motor learning and rehabilitation contexts where task realism is critical.

Materials and methods

This study aimed to investigate whether PAR could be used to enhance participants’ ability to imitate the kinematic properties of the observed movement.

Participants

To determine the appropriate sample size, an a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.7 (Düsseldorf, Germany) [25] considering a two-tailed t-tests, with an effect size equal to 0.7, a power of 0.80, and an alpha level of 0.05. A minimum of 19 participants was required. We contacted a total of 25 participants. Twenty-three healthy participants (12 men and 11 women, mean age = 26.96 ± 6.16 years) volunteered to participate in this experiment. Inclusion criteria were: age between 18 and 40 years, right handedness. Exclusion criteria included a documented history of neurological or psychiatric disorders, cognitive impairment, and musculoskeletal conditions affecting the upper limb.

The study was approved by the local ethics committee (Comitato Etico per la Ricerca di Ateneo – CERA, Prot. N° 2023/84, 16/11/2023) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Before the first experimental session, participants read and signed a written informed consent form.

Experimental set up

The experimental paradigm consists of the observation and imitation of an elbow flexion–extension movement performed by an avatar. Each participant wore a virtual reality headset (Meta Quest 3) and experienced a first-person point-of-view simulation of a room and of the avatar, giving the impression of physically standing inside it. The avatar performed three consecutive flexion-extension movements with the right forearm. These movements were captured on a real performer by means of Xsens motion capture system (Movella, California, US), exported as Filmbox animations (.fbx) and then retargeted onto the avatar through Unity software (version 2022.3.28f1) (sampling time 100 ms). For this reason, the three flexion–extension cycles were very similar but not identical in terms of duration and amplitude. The average duration (± standard deviation) of the flexion–extension cycle was 3.08 ± 0.05 s (range: 3.03 –3.13 s; mean movement frequency: ∼ 0.33 Hz), while the average amplitude was 95.6 ± 51.3° (range from minimum flexion to maximum extension: 24.7–164.7°). The starting posture corresponded to full arm extension.

In addition, two proprioceptive stimulators were positioned over the insertion points of the right biceps and triceps muscles by means of adjustable straps. Proprioceptive stimulation was delivered using mechanical vibrator custom-designed by Ermit srl (Genoa, Italy). Each device consisted of small, brushed DC motors (12 V, max 6300 RPM) able to deliver a vibratory signal and adjustable straps for secure positioning. The pair of stimulators delivered vibration at a fixed frequency of 80 Hz, known to activate the muscle spindle with a vertical displacement of 2–3 mm [24, 26, 27] and to evoke an illusory sensation of forearm extension (when stimulating the biceps) and flexion (when stimulating the triceps). The frequency of vibration was the same for all the participants. During preliminary tests by the manufacturer, an accelerometer mounted on the system and in contact with the body confirmed that the vibration frequency deviated from the imposed value by no more than − 3 Hz. The administration of the visual stimulus and subsequent activation of the stimulators were controlled through a custom-designed graphical interface written in C#. Depending on the phase of the illusory movement to be evoked (flexion or extension), the corresponding stimulator is activated. In particular, the stimulators activate when exceeding a threshold, based on the absolute value of the angular velocity of the movement performed by the avatar and corresponding to 1.0 deg/s. The estimated activation latency was approximately 200 ms on average, which is shorter than previously reported latencies for motor system engagement during action observation (250–320 ms) [28], and is therefore unlikely to interfere with the intended temporal coupling between visual and proprioceptive signals.

Kinematic data of the movements of the participants’ upper arm were recorded using an iPad Pro (1920 × 1080, 30 Hz), positioned on a tripod to capture the flexion-extension movements on the sagittal plane. The tripod was maintained in the same position during the whole experiment. Colored markers were attached to the participant’s right shoulder, elbow, and wrist. While we acknowledge that the use of motion capture systems based on infrared or inertial technologies would allow for a more precise characterization of movement kinematics, the video-based acquisition at a sampling frequency of 30 Hz adopted in this study can be considered appropriate given the slow nature of the movement under investigation. This approach is consistent with previous studies that have successfully employed similar methodologies to capture the kinematic features of whole-body and upper limb movements [29, 30]. The choice of this setup was motivated by its simplicity, while remaining appropriate given the expected temporal characteristics of the movement, with limited high-frequency components.

Experimental procedure

A graphical representation of the experimental procedure is shown in Fig. 3.

All participants completed two experimental conditions: Virtual Reality (VR) and Proprioceptive–Augmented virtual Reality (PAR). In the VR condition, in each trial, participants observed three consecutive flexion-extension movements of the avatar’s right forearm within the immersive virtual reality environment, whereas in the PAR condition, they simultaneously received the proprioceptive stimulation evoking an illusory sensation of movement coherent with that observed in the VR environment.

These two conditions were presented in a random order, with two tasks per condition: OFFLINE and ONLINE. In both tasks, participants were involved in a voluntary imitation paradigm. Specifically, in the OFFLINE task, participants were asked to observe the avatar while executing the movement and subsequently reproducing it, imitating its spatiotemporal characteristics. A total of five trials were performed for each condition (ten in total), after which participants completed a questionnaire designed to assess the sense of embodiment (see Sense of embodiment questionnaire section). In the OFFLINE condition, following both PAR and VR, participants were asked to rate the vividness of the illusory movement sensation using a Likert scale ranging from 0 to 10, where 0 indicated “no illusion” and 10 indicated a “strong illusion of real movement”. In the ONLINE task, participants were instructed to perform the movement simultaneously with its observation, in both conditions.

Sense of embodiment questionnaire

In the OFFLINE task, participants’ sense of embodiment, with reference to the stimulation phase, was assessed using a modified version of the questionnaire proposed by Gonzalez-Franco and Peck [6]. This was done to evaluate the contribution of the proprioceptive stimulation added to VR in PAR condition compared to VR alone. This standardized questionnaire is designed to evaluate the extent to which a user experiences embodiment over a virtual avatar. For this study, a subset of 10 items was selected from the original questionnaire to assess the three key components of embodiment: body ownership, sense of agency, and location of the body. The questions considered for evaluating the body ownership were Q1 “I felt as if the virtual arm was my arm”, Q2 “It felt as if the virtual arm I saw was someone else”, and Q3 “It seemed as if I might have more than one arm”. The questions used to evaluate the sense of agency were Q6 “It felt like I could control the virtual arm as if it was my own arm”, Q7 “The movements of the virtual arm were caused by my movements”, Q8 “I felt as if the movements of the virtual arm were influencing my own movements, and Q9 “I felt as if the virtual arm was moving by itself ”. Finally, for the location of the body, Q14 “I felt as if my arm was located where I saw the virtual arm”, Q15 “I felt out of my body”, Q16 “I felt as if my (real) arm were drifting toward the virtual arm or as if the virtual arm were drifting toward my (real) arm” were asked to the participants.

Participants rated each statement on a Likert scale ranging from “Strongly Disagree” (-3) to “Strongly Agree” (3). The final score was calculated by summing the ratings for each component, which were computed as follows: Sense of ownership score = Q1-Q2-Q3; Sense of agency score = Q6 + Q7+Q8-Q9; Location of the body score = Q14-Q15 + Q16.

Data analysis

Kinematic data analysis

OpenCV (v4.11.0) (Open Source Computer Vision Library) and Python (v3.13.2) were used for image recognition and kinematic data analysis. To calibrate the system the conversion factor from pixels to centimeters was calculated using an AR marker (DICT_ARUCO_ORIGINAL) placed within the scene, automatically detected by the OpenCV library. The centers of the markers were then manually selected in the first frame of each video to determine their coordinates on the xy-plane. The Lukas-Kanade Optical Flow algorithm (available in OpenCV) [31] was used to track the points throughout the video by iterating over each frame. Using these three reference points, the joint angle (θ) was computed through vector-based mathematical formulas and subsequently used to derive kinematic parameters. In particular, the angular velocity profiles of the participants’ movements, including three flexion-extension movements, were obtained. The raw data were first smoothed using a custom function implementing a moving average filter (window size 10 samples; sample frequency 30 Hz; effective cut-off frequency 1.3 Hz) [32], producing an overall low-pass effect that reduced noise and fluctuations in the data. The absolute angular velocity was then computed as the absolute value of the first derivative of angular displacement. Mean and maximal angular velocity (Vmean and Vmax, respectively; deg/s) were calculated from the signal as the average and maximum velocity values over the interval between the beginning and the end of the task, thus including the three flexion and extension movements. To define the onset and offset of the task, we chose a threshold corresponding to 5% of the maximum absolute value of the movement velocity profile.

Furthermore, in order to provide a quantification of spatial accuracy frame-by-frame over the entire profile, we computed the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) between the avatar’s trajectory and the participant’s trajectory in both the OFFLINE and ONLINE tasks. To ensure comparability, trajectories were first normalized in duration using the “interp1d” function in Python, resampling all traces to a fixed length of 101 samples.

Statistical analysis

The outcome parameters used to evaluate the participants’ ability to imitate the avatar were the absolute differences between the avatar’s and the participants’ Vmean values (Vmean error, deg/sec), and Vmax values (Vmax error, deg/sec), and the RMSE values. The normality of the residual was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of Q–Q plots.

In the OFFLINE task, the residuals of both Vmean error and Vmax error were normally distributed. In the ONLINE task, the residuals of Vmean error were normally distributed, whereas those associated with Vmax error deviated from normality. Nevertheless, since linear mixed models are generally robust to moderate violations of normality, and given that the deviations were mainly observed in the tails of the distribution rather than in its central portion, as indicated by the Q–Q plots, we decided to analyze both Vmean error and Vmax error across tasks and conditions using linear mixed models. Specifically, for each task (OFFLINE, ONLINE), separate linear mixed models were applied to these variables, with CONDITION (2 levels: VR, PAR) as fixed factor, and PARTICIPANTS as random factor.

Because the assumption of normally distributed residuals was violated for the RMSE values in both tasks, two Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMMs) were conducted. In each model, CONDITION (2 levels: VR and PAR) was included as a fixed effect, while PARTICIPANTS were included as a random effect.

The three scores evaluating different dimensions of the embodiment in the OFFLINE task were compared by means of one-sample t-test against zero in both PAR and VR conditions. Furthermore, to compare the sense of the embodiment in the two conditions, linear mixed models were applied on the sense of ownership, sense of agency and location of the body scores, considering CONDITION (2 levels: VR, PAR) as fixed factor, and PARTICIPANTS as random factor.

The significance level was set at 0.05. Partial eta-squared was calculated to determine effect size. According to Richardson et al. [33], partial eta-squared values of approximately 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 indicate small, medium, and large effects, respectively.

The vividness of the illusory movement evoked by PAR and VR in the OFFLINE task was compared by means of Wilcoxon test.

Spearman correlation analyses were used to relate the PAR/VR vividness with the average Vmean error, Vmax error, and RMSE values. Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons was applied (new significance level p = 0.05/3 = 0.0167).

In the text, data are reported as mean ± standard error. The analysis was performed with SPSS Statistics 23 software.

Sense of embodiment

One-sample t-tests showed that body ownership score was significantly higher than 0 in PAR (2.61 ± 0.78, t = 3.29, p = 0.003) but not in VR (0.30 ± 0.78, t = 0.40, p = 0.69). A significantly lower value compared to 0 was found in the VR sense of agency (-4.39 ± 0.92, t=-4.98, p < 0.001), whilst no difference was found in PAR (-1.30 ± 0.92, t=-1.37, p = 0.18). Both VR (2.09 ± 0.60) and PAR (3.48 ± 0.59) scores related to the location of the body were significantly higher than 0 (VR t = 3.27, p = 0.003; PAR t = 6.31, p < 0.001).

The analyses on the three parameters evaluating the sense of embodiment showed a significant effect of CONDITION (body ownership: F(1, 22) = 7.42, p = 0.012, ƞ2=0.25; sense of agency: F(1, 22) = 6.83, p = 0.016, ƞ2=0.24; location of the body: F(1, 22) = 5.78, p = 0.025, ƞ2=0.21), indicating higher sense of embodiment in PAR than in VR. Vividness of the illusory movement

The result of the Wilcoxon test showed a significant difference between vividness values evoked by PAR and VR (Z = -3.31, p = 0.001). Vividness reported after PAR (6.2 ± 0.46) was significantly higher than that associated to VR (3.48 ± 0.56). No significant correlations were found between vividness ratings, in either the PAR or VR conditions, and any of the imitation performance outcome measures.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to investigate the ability of healthy participants to reproduce the dynamic features of upper limb movements experienced in a standard VR condition and in a novel PAR condition. They were asked to perform two different tasks: OFFLINE, employing a sequential approach in which they first observed the action and then executed it [14]; ONLINE, in which observation and imitation were performed simultaneously.

Overall, the results showed that participants successfully imitated the observed movements in both conditions. However, in both tasks, the angular velocity of the observed movement was underestimated. This result is consistent with a previous study [34], whereby even when the participants were explicitly required to imitate the kinematic characteristics of the movement, they were unable to reproduce the movement faithfully, tending toward slower execution.

Notably, the two experimental conditions produced distinct and opposite effects across the tasks.

In the OFFLINE task, the PAR condition yielded a velocity profile more closely matching that of the avatar acting as a model, while in the ONLINE task it resulted in greater deviations from the model.

Given that muscle tendon vibration is known to affect proprioception, including the perception of joint position and movement speed [35], it is plausible that proprioceptive stimulation influenced the way participants perceived the velocity of the observed movement. This may have led to a more (in the OFFLINE task) or less (in the ONLINE task) accurate reproduction, depending on the type of task and the relative weighting of visual and proprioceptive information.

OFFLINE imitation

During OFFLINE imitation, Vmean error and Vmax error were significantly lower in the PAR than in the VR condition. These findings suggest that proprioceptive information congruent with the observed movement is integrated with visual information, improving the ability to reproduce both the average and peak velocities. The result aligns with the idea that proprioceptive stimuli may complement and strengthen visual feedback, leading to improvements in motor learning [36], and may enhance the internal representation of the observed movement [37, 38].

Furthermore, these results extend previous evidence demonstrating that the combination of VR and proprioceptive stimulation enhances the ability of reproducing the kinematic feature of the observed movement [21]. The importance of combining dynamic visual information with proprioceptive stimulation has been demonstrated by Le Franc and colleagues [39, 40] showing that the wrist movement illusion induced by tendon vibration is significantly enhanced by congruent VR visual feedback (a moving virtual hand) compared to a static or hidden hand in both healthy participants and in patients with a chronic stroke. More generally, previous studies from our group have shown that observing a video of a human action, combined with proprioceptive stimulation congruent with the observed movement, induces a more vivid illusory experience of movement and a long-lasting increase in primary motor cortex excitability [19, 20, 22]. Altogether, these findings suggest that either action observation or VR paradigms benefit from proprioceptive stimulation, which likely acts as a means to enhance and consolidate information acquired through vision, thereby improving movement perception and inducing cortical plasticity.

This interpretation is further supported by the analysis of the embodiment questionnaire proposed at the end of the OFFLINE task. Participants reported a statistically stronger sense of body ownership, agency, and perceived body location in the PAR compared to the VR condition. The improved movement reproduction observed in the presence of proprioceptive stimulation may therefore be partly attributed to the higher embodiment evoked during the PAR condition. Scientific literature suggests that the sense of agency and the sense of ownership are dynamic and context-dependent, varying with sensory, cognitive, and environmental conditions. A recent review by Girondini and colleagues [41] concluded that, in the presence of multisensory stimulation such as visuotactile stimulation, congruency between sensory signals enhances the sense of ownership. In the present study, VR alone did not, on average, evoke changes in the sense of ownership (as shown by the results of the t-test). This may be because participants remained still, leading to an incongruence between visual and proprioceptive afferent signals that may have hindered the recognition of the avatar’s limb as their own. In contrast, when participants experienced an illusory displacement in PAR, the congruence between visual and proprioceptive information may have promoted the sense of ownership [42–44].

The incongruency in VR, as opposed to the congruency in PAR, might also explain the results related to the sense of agency. Indeed, in the PAR condition, although the sense of agency did not differ significantly from 0, it was significantly higher than in the VR condition, in which it significantly decreased below 0. This finding suggests that VR alone disrupted participants’ sense of agency toward the avatar. Both PAR and VR significantly shifted the perceived location of the body toward the avatar’s limb, as scores in both conditions were significantly higher than 0. However, the addition of proprioceptive stimulation further enhanced this effect, with PAR scores being significantly higher than those observed in VR. Taken together, these findings suggest that proprioceptive stimulation not only provides additional sensorimotor information but also reinforces participants’ embodied perception of the observed movement, enhancing the overall reproduction accuracy in the OFFLINE task. No embodiment assessment was administered after the ONLINE task, as the simultaneous motor execution could have interfered with participants’ judgments. Therefore, no conclusions can be drawn regarding differences in embodiment between the OFFLINE and ONLINE tasks.

Furthermore, participants reported a significantly greater vividness of the illusory movement during PAR compared with VR. A previous study conducted by our group [19] suggested that vividness may represent a marker of plasticity, as it was associated with the effectiveness of the combined protocol (action observation plus proprioceptive stimulation) in inducing long-lasting increases in M1 excitability. In the present study, the more vivid perception of movement may likewise have contributed to enhancing the sensorimotor integration processes underlying imitation performance. Although participants reported greater vividness of the illusory movement during PAR, and PAR was associated with better imitation performance than VR, no significant correlations emerged between vividness ratings and imitation ability. This finding suggests that vividness may not directly predict individual imitation performance, but rather reflect a broader sensorimotor state induced by proprioceptive stimulation that facilitates action imitation. In this framework, proprioceptive stimulation may enhance the coupling between observed and executed actions, while subjective vividness represents only one aspect of this process and may therefore not relate to behavioral performance.

At the same time, proprioceptive stimulation may have also enhanced the saliency of the visual input, helping participants focus more effectively on the kinematic features of the avatar’s movements and thereby improving imitation performance.

The superiority of PAR in enhancing imitation performance was not confirmed by the RMSE analysis, as RMSE values were comparable between PAR and VR conditions. Since RMSE provides information about the accuracy with which the spatial features of the observed movement are reproduced on a frame-by-frame basis, the absence of differences between conditions may indicate that proprioceptive stimulation preferentially facilitated the temporal rather than the spatial aspects of movement imitation. Indeed, although movement velocity inherently reflects both temporal and spatial components, the RMSE findings suggest a possible dissociation in the contribution of proprioceptive stimulation to these two dimensions of motor performance.

ONLINE imitation

During the ONLINE task, Vmean and Vmax errors were significantly lower in the VR than in the PAR condition. A plausible explanation is that the simultaneous delivery of artificial proprioceptive cues during movement may have altered participants’ natural proprioceptive feedback, creating an illusion that led them to misperceive their actions and consequently impaired accurate movement planning and thus reproduction [37, 45, 46]. Such interference may hinder rather than facilitate imitative skills, resulting in decreased movement speed during the PAR condition and, consequently, greater reproduction errors. This conclusion applies also to RMSE values, which were significantly higher in PAR than in VR. All these findings are in line with and extend those presented by recent a recent study showing that, in a VR environment, vivid arm flexion-extension illusions induced by muscle tendon vibration can affect the perception of movement trajectory, leading the virtual arm to appear to move farther than the real arm [47]. In other words, vibration can boost vision-induced illusion in a VR environment. Therefore, vibrating the antagonist muscle tendon leads to an “under-shooting” error, a sign that the virtual hand is perceived as moving faster and farther than reality [48]. Therefore, when PAR was concomitant with a real movement, as in the ONLINE imitation task, we can assume that participants significantly increased their error in estimating and reproducing the observed movement also for what concerns its kinematic properties. Conversely, the lack of conflicting proprioceptive cues in the VR condition may have promoted a more accurate reproduction of movement. Unfortunately, after the ONLINE task, vividness ratings were not collected; therefore, we cannot speculate on its potential role in this condition. This should be considered a limitation of the study.

It is worth noting that the OFFLINE and ONLINE tasks differ not only in the timing of imitation, but also in their underlying neurophysiological mechanisms. Specifically, in the OFFLINE task participants first perceived and processed the stimulation and, only subsequently, produced imitative performance. In contrast, in the ONLINE task these processes occurred simultaneously. This may have generated an interference effect due to the competition for attentional and sensorimotor resources required to concurrently process both the artificially induced proprioceptive afferent input and the proprioceptive information arising from voluntary movement, potentially contributing to the deterioration of imitation performance. This interference effect is known for the visual modality [49], but may apply also for proprioceptive afferences.

Although all these findings unveil issues in using a PAR condition during ONLINE imitation, suggesting to exclude the use of this stimulation in this type of task, they may also suggest that PAR condition can allow for the perception of virtual movements that are wider and faster than real ones.

This could be particularly useful for creating immersive VR experiences where physical space is limited and amplitude and velocity of movements need to be reduced also for safety reasons.

Finally, it is worth noting the novelty of the PAR condition used in the present study which lies in its greater ecological validity with respect to previous ones. In fact, in earlier studies, participants observed movements considerably longer in duration (about 10s) to give enough time for the kinesthetic illusion to emerge [19, 22]. On the contrary, in a recent study we proposed a duration of proprioceptive stimulation typical of spontaneous movements (about 1s) [20]. This choice was made for a future practical application of PAR as a tool to enhance motor learning in rehabilitative context where it is essential that the features of the observed actions closely mirror those encountered in real-life scenarios. Considering this future application, we would like to clarify that the present study represents a first step towards more complex experimental designs aimed at investigating the potential of the proposed approach. Further studies will be necessary to fully assess its efficacy and the persistence of its effects beyond the stimulation period.

Future studies and limitations

All these results suggest that proprioceptive stimulation activated a sensorimotor integration process, leading to improved imitation performance when the motor response occurred immediately after the stimulation, but poorer performance during simultaneous imitation. It should be noted that the present study was designed as a proof-of-concept to assess the feasibility of integrating VR with proprioceptive stimulation and to explore its immediate effects on motor imitation. In this context, the limited number of repetitions was intended to capture initial responses, while future studies should investigate protocols with a higher number of repetitions to better address learning-related processes. Furthermore, the single-joint forearm movement investigated in the present study should be considered as a first step toward understanding the effects of proprioceptive stimulation on motor imitation. Although our findings support its effectiveness in imitating this simple single-joint movement, whether similar effects extend to more complex, multi-joint movements typical of real-life actions remains to be established and should be addressed in future studies. Finally, the present study focused on the comparison between PAR and VR and therefore did not include a condition combining AO with tactile vibratory stimulation that did not elicit an illusory movement sensation. Although previous evidence suggests that long-lasting neurophysiological changes are specifically associated with the combination of AO and proprioceptive stimulation inducing a congruent movement illusion [19], the absence of such a control condition does not allow the complete dissociation of illusion-specific effects from potential nonspecific sensory or attentional contributions associated with vibratory stimulation.

Conclusions

The proprioceptive stimulation delivered in the PAR condition positively influenced movement imitation ability during the OFFLINE imitation task. Interestingly, the sense of embodiment was higher in the PAR than the VR condition. The opposite result observed during ONLINE imitation can indicate the integration of an artificial proprioceptive input with the natural one driving participants to alter the velocity during online movement reproduction.

Despite these differences, there is convergence on the notion that visuo-proprioceptive integration is powerful. In VR applications, this means designers can use muscle tendon vibration to extend virtual movements, improve immersion, and make avatar movements feel more natural. The most important conclusion is that adding congruent muscle tendon vibration enhances the sense of self-motion in VR beyond what vision alone can achieve, enabling richer interaction techniques and providing a rationale for future studies investigating its potential use in sensorimotor rehabilitation after stroke.
