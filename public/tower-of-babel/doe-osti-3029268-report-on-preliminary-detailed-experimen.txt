 ABSTRACT

Advanced nuclear power technologies will use higher temperatures as a means to extract energy at a
higher efficiency than current plants and therefore put a larger demand on the structural materials.
Improved performance of structural materials could enable greater safety margins, longer plant lifetimes,
and reduce maintenance costs. Alloy 709 is championed as the next generation of austenitic alloys for
advanced nuclear reactors. In parallel to the ASME code case pursued, the AMMT program is initiating a
neutron irradiation campaign to provide first-of-a-kind engineering data to establish operational design
parameters and how the mechanical response is modified by environmental factors. This document refines
the AMMT neutron irradiation campaign to a 4 year program to support the generation of creep
knockdown factors for Alloy 709 and welded Alloy 709. The campaign is divided among two national
laboratories, Oak Ridge National Laboratory and Idaho National Laboratory, to use the strengths of each
laboratory. Through a cooperative plan, time-independent properties and time-dependent properties will
be obtained across a large temperature window, nominally 300°C to 800°C, damage levels up to 10 dpa,
and with and without the impacts of transmutation produced helium.

1. Introduction

 Nuclear power holds a promise to provide cheap, reliable energy source for the ever-increasing
demand in the United States. While the current fleet of Light Water Reactors (LWRs) provide gigawatt
scale baseload power, many applications can be served by smaller power units. The growing industry is
developing nuclear technology concepts around non-light water reactor technologies, such as molten salt
cooled, sodium cooled, or gas cooled. All of these concepts will attempt to use higher temperatures as a
means to extract energy at a higher efficiency than current plants and therefore put a larger demand on the
structural materials. Improved performance of structural materials could enable greater safety margins,
longer plant lifetimes, and reduce maintenance costs.
 Alloy 709, also known as UNS S31025 [1], is championed by the Advanced Reactor
Technologies program as the next generation of austenitic alloys for advanced nuclear reactors. Alloy 709
has a nominal composition of Fe20Cr25Ni with Mn, Mo, and Nb added to form precipitating phases. The
program down selected a heat treatment of 1100°C solution anneal with a 775°C heat treatment for 10
hours to form MX, M23C6, and most importantly a fine density of Nb-rich precipitates on dislocations [2].
The Nb-rich precipitates pin the dislocations and lead the alloy to better creep properties than the
traditional widely used type 304 and 316 stainless steels. Significant property characterization is already
under way [3] and planned for inclusion in the American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME)
Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code (BPVC) section 3 division 5 for “Rules for Construction of Nuclear
Facility Components-Division 5-High Temperature Reactors” [4]. However, the required dataset for
inclusion in the BPVC does not include environmental effects, such as corrosion or radiation, which are
needed to support the use of Alloy 709 in the nuclear industry.
 The AMMT program proposed a neutron irradiation campaign in 2024 to capture the evolution of
properties for the plate form of A709. In order to utilize a material in nuclear construction, both as
fabricated and irradiated properties must be sufficient for the targeted use case within an acceptable safety
margin. The irradiation campaign needs to provide engineering data to establish operational design
parameters and how the mechanical response to environmental factors are modified. Because the
fabrication of engineering scale components will require welds to join pieces of this alloy, a variation of
A709 used for welding must also be included and evaluated. The neutron irradiation campaign will
advance the scientific understanding of A709 through determining the processing-structure-properties
relationships. These relationships are important to extending the use case for A709 to different facilities
with distinct environments such as neutron spectra, neutron fluence rates, temperatures, and time in
service. This document refines the previously proposed irradiation plan from 2024, shown in Figure 1 and

presented in Ref. [5], and details the prioritized experimental plan to produce knockdown factors. The
plan combines the expertise from multiple institutions to utilize the nation’s facilities in an efficient
manner.

2. Joint Experimental Plan for Irradiation of alloy A709 in HFIR and ATR

 The original proposed plan identified many parameters and activities to develop an operational
envelope for the plate and weld forms of A709. The planned operational window ranges from 300°C to
650°C with the upper application temperature bound to 800°C. The expected displacement damage from
the neutron environment for the structural applications of this alloy are up to 10 dpa. While intended for
fast neutron spectrums, dedicated test reactors for fast neutron spectrums are not available in the United
States. Therefore, the neutron spectrums for this campaign are planned to be the mixed spectrum of the
HFIR and use a cadmium shroud to increase the fast to thermal neutron ratio in the ATR. In phase 1 of
this portion of the campaign, designated as HFIR-A1 and ATR-A1 in Figure 1, both time-independent
properties and limited time-dependent properties will be assessed. Time-independent properties include
fracture toughness, and tensile properties. The time-dependent properties focus on the creep and creep
crack growth performance of A709 following irradiation. In-situ collection of time-dependent properties
is planned for future phases of the program. Microstructural characterization throughout the program will
help determine the origins of the observed mechanical properties.
 Through discussions between Idaho National Laboratory and Oak Ridge National Laboratory, a
subset of the original plan is presented below in Figure 2, to accomplish a majority of the broader plan
within the known time and budget constraints of the program. Both facilities will perform irradiation
experiments on a combination of samples for time-independent and time-dependent properties targeting
insertion dates in fiscal years 2027 and 2028. A range of target temperatures from 300°C to 800°C are
included to gather properties As creep is primarily a concern at high temperatures, the irradiation
campaign limits the investigation of time-dependent properties to a similar range as the creep tests already
performed on the as-prepared material from 600°C to 800°C. Time-independent properties at lower

temperatures are driven by microstructure evolution inducing embrittlement and swelling, nominally
500°C and below, and thus captured in this plan. Specimens to evaluate time-independent properties still
are included at the higher temperatures to provide a comparison to the as-prepared properties.
 This plan makes use of the strengths of each facility. The high neutron fluence rate of the HFIR
allows for higher displacement levels of 2 and 10 dpa to be obtained with a crossover point for the HFIR
and ATR at 2 dpa. In addition, INL will also target 1 dpa to provide data on the progression and evolution
of mechanical properties over a range of relevant damage levels when coupled with the higher dose
irradiations performed at HFIR. The larger diameter flux trap positions in ATR allow for bigger sample
geometries than in the flux trap of the HFIR removing some of the anticipated specimen size effects when
comparing with the non-irradiated properties. Details of the intended capsules and sample geometries are
detailed in later sections within this document. Through the measurement of properties from a thermal
neutron rich environment, the HFIR, and a thermal neutron shielded facility in the ATR, the distinction in
environments and thus in properties will provide conservative estimates of how transmutation induced
helium impacts the mechanical performance. This first-of-a-kind data at relatively low dpa will provide
insight to help interpret the properties of alloy 709 in an upcoming irradiation campaign at the JOYO
sodium-cooled fast reactor test facility [6] up to 160 dpa at temperatures up to 550°C.

2.1 TIME-INDEPENDENT PROPERTIES

 Baseline engineering data for the design of components starts with time-independent properties.
Tensile properties such as yield stress, uniform elongation, ultimate tensile strength, and total elongation
provide valuable design parameters. Bombardment with neutrons changes each of these properties and

thus, each must be measured. Irradiation at both the HFIR and the ATR will generate time-independent
properties using traditional tensile tests and slow strain rate tests. Tensile properties will be measured both
at room temperature and at the target irradiation temperature to assess radiation induced changes in
performance.
 The mixed spectrum of the HFIR will generate more transmutant helium than the cadmium
shielded neutron energy spectrum for the ATR irradiation. Thus, high temperature helium embrittlement
(HTHE) becomes a possible life limiting degradation mechanism. Slow strain rate tests will be performed
on a subset of specimens at their target irradiation temperature to assess susceptibility to HTHE from
strain rates of 10-3 to 10-7 s-1. ORNL will perform high temperature tests focused on strain rates of 10-3, 10-
4
  , and 10-5 s-1 while INL will perform tests focused on strain rates of 10-5, 10-6, and 10-7 s-1. Each facility
will perform some experiments within the strain rates of the other facility for direct comparison.
 Embrittlement of the material either through the production of helium or through the accumulation
of radiation induced defects will also affect the load carrying capacity which can be investigated by
performing fracture toughness tests on compact-tension specimens. To the extent possible, CT specimens
designed for the INL insertions are in accordance with ASTM standard E1820 [7] and will be tested to
produce traditional J-R curves. By utilizing the results from the uniaxial tensile tests, it will be possible to
accurately calculate a size dependent fracture toughness value using the appropriate data limitations for
power law fitting as prescribed in the ASTM standard. Initial fracture toughness testing will be conducted
at room temperature, and fractography will be performed after CT specimens have been cracked
completely in half post test.

2.2 TIME-DEPENDENT PROPERTIES

 Key design parameters for structural materials are the time-dependent properties of creep, fatigue,
and creep-fatigue. Obtaining these properties during in-core experiments requires sophisticated equipment
and dedicated programs for each set of measurements. The AMMT program decided to prioritize post-
irradiation creep properties as the initial data to support the alloy’s use in nuclear environments. The
creep properties without irradiation are available as a function of temperature and stress are shown in
Figure 3 from Ref [3]. Because of the irradiation times and long duration of creep tests, priority is given
to provide first-of-a-kind data within the timeframe of the program using a limited number of irradiation
and test temperatures, three stress conditions, and a single damage level of 2 dpa. The conditions will
provide initial datasets to refine future irradiation test programs. The specimen geometries to be used by
each facility are detailed in later sections.

Figure 3. Creep rupture lifetimes for Alloy 709 as a function of temperature and stress from Ref [3] with targeted post-irradiation
creep measurements highlighted.

 Due to the high target temperatures for many Alloy 709 applications, it will be important to
investigate how creep affects crack propagation in prototypic environments. The same CT geometry used
for fracture toughness testing will be used for creep crack growth measurement at the 600, 700, and 800
°C conditions. Utilizing newly developed capabilities at INL it will be possible to determine if a steady-
state creep crack growth rate regime exists and generate first of a kind data which can be used to inform a
safe design operation envelope. With a limited number of CT specimens, creep crack growth rate testing
will only be conducted at the same temperature as the irradiation. Testing will be conducted as close as
reasonably achievable to ASTM E1457-19 [8].

2.3 CAPSULE DESCRIPTIONS

 This section outlines each of the different capsules and the samples that will be included in each
irradiation vehicle. The location of individual specimens may be rearranged depending on capsule
capacity or the availability of space. Other non-sample components of the capsules, such as spacers,
springs, chevrons or other filler materials may be altered to maintain the target design temperatures,
damage levels, and compatibility with sample material types.

2.3.1 Planned General Tensile Capsules for HFIR insertion

 To get the desired tensile property data, the HFIR insertions will use standardized specimen and
capsule designs. Tensile specimen geometry will be the miniature SS-J2 tensile geometries housed in the
General Tensile (GENTEN) drop-in capsule. A general rendering of the GENTEN capsule is shown in

Figure 4. The GENTEN capsule can hold up to 36 SS-J2s using a gas gap with a tailored gas composition
to achieve target temperatures with 12 silicon carbide passive thermometry pieces for estimating the
irradiation temperature after the experiment completes. Other associated materials are there for structural
and safety purposes. More details on the GENTEN capsule can be found in Ref. [9]. To accomplish the
irradiation conditions displayed in Figure 2, 12 GENTEN capsules will be needed to include the
combination of 5 variants of Alloy 709, the precipitate hardened variants from two vendors (ATI PH-1
and Carson PH-1), a weld of the alloy, a solution annealed version of the ATI heat (ATI SA) and an
advanced manufactured variant. A summary of the irradiation conditions for the capsules to gather time-
independent data is listed in Table 1.

2.3.2 Status of the Development of the Mini-buttonhead Creep capsule for HFIR insertion

 To obtain measurements of creep performance, there must be a drop-in capsule in the same spirit
as the GENTEN design. The original irradiation plan for A709 proposed using a 1 mm thick version of
the SS-J geometry (SS-J4) as the previous 0.5mm and 0.75mm thick geometries displayed significant size
effects as the thickness was decreased and led to an increase in creep rupture lifetime compared to larger
specimens [10]. Per the ASTM standard for creep testing [11], miniature specimens are allowed as long as
they fit the general requirements of the designs in the tensile test standard [12]. However, even the
smallest allowable subsize geometry does not fit within the dimensions of an irradiation capsule.
 To minimize the impacts of the size effect, ORNL developed a miniature creep specimen known
as the mini-button head (MBH) geometry under the ART program between the miniature SS-J geometry
and the ASTM standard specifications. A comparison of the MBH with the smallest subsize creep
specimen permitted in the standard is shown in Figure 5a. The overall gauge length of the MBH is nearly
identical with a reduction of the gauge diameter from a quarter inch (6.3 mm) to an eighth of an inch (3.2
mm). The reduction in overall size enables the MBH to fit within the HFIR materials irradiation capsule
geometry. A preliminary design of a new capsule to house the MBH is displayed in Figure 5b and
designated as the MBH creep capsule or MBHCRP capsule.

Figure 5. (a) comparison of an MBH specimen with the smallest subsize specimen allowed under ASTM standards and (b) the
inclusion of the MBH into a proposed HFIR capsule design.

 This new proposed MBHCRP capsule design contains one MBH specimen per capsule encased in
a housing made from the same material with six SS-J miniature tensile specimens and six associated
silicon carbide thermometry pieces. The MBHCRP capsule would allow for the measurement of time-
independent and time-dependent properties from specimens within the same capsule, determination of
temperature gradients along the capsule length, near direct comparison to already existing thermal creep
data from the MBH geometry to produce knock down factors, and lastly a standardized material-agnostic
design for creep specimens to enable future use in DOE programs. Similar to the GENTEN capsules
described in Section 2.3.1, MBH capsule contents were designed around the creep rupture lifetimes
shown in Figure 3, and include the ATI PH-1 and Carson PH-1 heats, a weld of alloy, and ATI SA. The
planned irradiation conditions are displayed in Table 3. Two temperatures and three stress levels per
temperature were chosen to provide knockdown factors near the upper bounds of the anticipated use cases
for Alloy 709. To obtain the full irradiation matrix, the program will need to field approximately 30
MBHCRP capsules. To confirm the feasibility of the irradiation matrix, preliminary thermal modeling
was performed in ANSYS using nominal HFIR operating parameters, thermal properties from the
previous AMMT irradiation campaign, and tuning the gas gap size and composition. The preliminary
analysis is shown in Figure 6 for 700°C and Figure 7 for 800°C highlight the specimen average and the
average in the gauge region. This analysis provided confidence that the MBHCRP capsule will meet
program objectives. Detailed analysis based on capsule position within the HFIR and likely design
changes will be performed throughout FY26 as the capsule is refined.

2.3.3 Capsule design for ATR irradiations

Material irradiated in the ATR will utilize the Irradiation System for High-Throughput Acquisition
(ISHA-1) capsule design [13], which was created specifically to facilitate drop-in capsule experiments.
Within each capsule, there is the capacity to hold multiple specimen geometries which will be selected to
ensure that both the time dependent and time independent properties outlined in previous sections can be
investigated at each temperature and dose condition. Two different renderings of the specimen fixture
bodies which fit within the outer ISHA-1 capsule are shown below. The first shows a specimen holder
which contains both SSJ tensile specimens similar to those being irradiated within the HFIR GENTEN
assembly along with small round compact tension specimens which will be used to collect fracture
toughness and creep crack growth data.

Figure 8. Rendering of the split SSJ/CT fixture body containing SSJ tensile specimens and small round compact tension
specimens which is compatible with the ISHA-1 drop-in capsule design.

Each holder of this design has the capacity to hold 13 compact-tension specimens and 12 SSJ tensile
specimens which are 1 mm thick. Since the relatively large CT specimens restrict the total number of SSJ
tensile specimens that can fit within each individual capsule, an additional specimen holder comprised
solely of SSJ type specimens was designed. Each dose and temperature target for the ATR experiments
will include multiple capsules to reach the total specimen requirements for programmatic PIE. The
current specimen holder for an all SSJ capsule currently has a maximum capacity of 24 SSJ tensile
specimens which are 1 mm thick. However, design efforts are currently underway to increase this
capacity to 36 while maintaining the current levels of passive instrumentation.

2.4 TARGET TIMELINES

 Both the HFIR and ATR irradiation programs anticipate the first insertion of capsules in FY27. In
both cases, in-core space and operational limitations force the campaigns to be pursued across multiple
cycles. Anticipated timelines for each Anticipated timelines for each individual irradiation campaign are
shown in Figure 10 for the HFIR and Figure 11 for the ATR. Each campaign is designed to have high
priority post-irradiation data produced as quickly as possible with the remaining cycles used to fill in gaps
within the temperature and damage level space.
 The HFIR portion of the campaign requires five HFIR cycles with nominally 2 dpa gained per
cycle. The GENTEN capsules will likely be placed farther from the core centerline and held in the core
for either 1 cycle or 5 cycles depending on the required damage level. The amount of thermometry and

the small gauge lengths of the SS-J2 tensile specimens allow for a reasonable temperature uniformity
along each sample in these positions. The larger gauge length of the MBH would have noticeable
temperature gradients in the farther positions and therefore, positions close to the centerline of the HFIR
will be pursued. However, centerline core positions are very limited. By requesting the same core
positions for each cycle for the MBHCRP capsules, the number of core positions needed is minimized.
Both the GENTEN and MBHCRP capsules have iterated through ORNL’s conceptual design phase, as
discussed in Ref. [14], and moved to the preliminary design phase with experiment design. Fabrication
orders are anticipated in FY26Q2 for the GENTEN capsules to prepare for insertion in FY27.

Figure 10. A timeline for the completion of the HFIR-A1 portion of the AMMT neutron irradiation campaign.

3. Conclusions

The AMMT program is initiating a neutron irradiation campaign to provide first-of-a-kind engineering
data to establish operational design parameters and how the mechanical response is modified by
environmental factors outside of the requirements for an ASME code case. The 4-year campaign is
divided among two national laboratories, Oak Ridge National Laboratory and Idaho National Laboratory,
to use the strengths of each laboratory. Through a cooperative plan, time-independent properties and time-
dependent properties will be obtained across a large temperature window, nominally 300°C to 800°C,
damage levels up to 10 dpa, and with and without the impacts of transmutation produced helium.
Anticipated properties include tensile properties, fracture toughness, creep knock down factors, and
microstructural information. HFIR will use a standardized GENTEN capsule design and a new creep
specimen capsule design and ATR will use the ISHA-1 capsule to generate irradiated A709 to gather
these properties. Irradiation planning will continue in FY26 with insertions targeted for FY27.
