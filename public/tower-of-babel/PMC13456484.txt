Base-Promoted Benzylic C–H Amination Reactions Across a Wide pK a Spectrum Enabled by 2‑Halobenzofuran and -Indole Oxidants
Authors: Vivian N. Nguyen, Garrett A. Hoteling, Michael D. Delost, Kayla L. Constantini, William B. Hughes, Robert S. Paton, Jeffrey S. Bandar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We disclose a base-promoted method to directly couple benzylic C–H bonds of alkyl­(hetero)­arenes with aniline pronucleophiles using 2-halobenzofurans and -indoles as novel halogen oxidants. We also extend this reactivity to the dual use of hexamethyldisilazide (HMDS) reagents as bases and nucleophilic ammonia surrogates to access primary benzyl amines via a simple deprotection and isolation protocol. This methodology leverages halogen transfer (X-transfer) reactivity to merge deprotonation, halogenation, and nucleophilic substitution into one synergistic process that is critical for reaction success. Here, a thermodynamically uphill deprotonation event generates transient benzyl carbanionic and halide intermediates which circumvent well-known complications of dimerization in metalation-halogenation sequences and amine overalkylation from benzyl halide substitution. These features enable novel scope and selectivity opportunities over traditional C–H metalation chemistry and established radical- or nitrene-based C–H amination strategies. With reactivity guided by C–H acidity trends, this protocol is effective for polyalkylarenes, hard-to-oxidize alkyl­(hetero)­arenes, and substrates with oxidation-prone functional groups that are unsuitable or unselective in alternative mechanistic approaches. New benzofuran and indole X-transfer reagents (XTRs), which are compatible with amine nucleophiles and bases, allow the expansion of X-transfer oxidative coupling to a substantially wider pK a range than previously accessible. Structural studies of these easily-prepared reagents explain their enhanced productivity for C–H amination and provide guidelines for their continued application to other oxidative C–H coupling reactions.

Introduction

The direct coupling of benzylic C–H bonds with N-pronucleophiles is an ideal approach toward benzyl amine substructures that are widely valued in pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, and other commercially relevant applications (Figure a, left). , C–H coupling reactions can offer increased efficiency and modularity compared to traditional reductive amination or substitution routes that first necessitate a carbonyl or alkyl halide group, respectively. , Although functional group conversion-based routes are widely used, they present challenges when the prefunctionalized starting materials are expensive, not commercial, or not already present within a complex molecule (Figure a, right). In these cases, access to a desired benzyl amine is dependent on multistep syntheses that install functionality through mechanisms governed by a limited set of reactivity trends (e.g., Friedel-Crafts acylation or Wohl-Ziegler radical halogenation). , In this regard, direct C–H amination methods are especially useful when they offer alternative site-selectivity or functional group tolerance over traditional routes.

Background and motivation for a new approach to benzylic C–H amination and aniline coupling reactions.

While the benefits of benzylic C–H amination have motivated much progress over the past several decades, there remain outstanding limitations among existing approaches. Most advances have been enabled by strategic applications of C–H nitrene insertion, hydrogen atom abstraction (HAA), or arene single electron oxidation processes (Figure b, left). − These approaches are highly developed for accessing N-protected benzyl amine derivatives, which require separate and often nontrivial deprotection reactions. In contrast, protocols to directly couple benzylic C–H bonds with common amine pronucleophiles, such as anilines, are underdeveloped. To advance C–H amination capabilities, we herein report two methods that couple alkyl­(hetero)­arenes with anilines or a practical ammonia surrogate through a base-promoted oxidative pathway that offers new synthetic opportunities (Figure b, right).

Given the wide occurrence of N-aryl benzyl amines in bioactive compounds, there is a need for a general C–H amination protocol applicable to diverse alkylarene and aniline coupling partners. This would circumvent multistep routes wherein N-derivatization (e.g., Buchwald-Hartwig coupling of N–H benzyl amines) is required after C–H amination. , However, the use of aniline coupling partners for benzylic C–H amination is underdeveloped because anilines often undergo competing arene or N-oxidation side reactions (Figure b, left). To avoid these undesired processes, a common requirement is to use a large excess of the alkylarene substrate and/or less-oxidizable anilines. In this regard, Warren pioneered the use of HAA reagents with Cu catalysis for C–H coupling of electron-deficient anilines in 2012, which was expanded to electron-rich anilines by the Sun group, with both protocols employing solvent quantities of alkylarene. , Oxidative photocatalytic methods have been recently reported by Gong for enantioselective coupling of N-aryl glycines with secondary alkylarenes (down to 5 eq) and by Yan, who used Ni-doped CsPbBr3 perovskite nanocrystals to couple four aniline examples with equimolar ethylbenzene. ,

In contrast to the limited development of aniline C–H coupling reactions, substantial progress has been made for producing N-protected benzyl amines from alkylarenes. This is due in part to the high compatibility of N-protected amine sources in C–H activation techniques and the value of primary amines as both products and synthetic intermediates (Figure a, right). , For example, a common approach is to use weakly nucleophilic amine sources (e.g., nitriles) that are compatible with the strongly oxidizing conditions needed to generate benzyl cation intermediates. Alternatively, HAA-initiated processes to generate benzyl radicals that undergo amination through metal-catalyzed or radical-polar crossover pathways are also well developed, primarily to provide N-Ts, -acyl, or –N3 products. , Metal-catalyzed C–H insertion reactions that employ N-oxidants as nitrene precursors, such as iminoiodinane or N–Cl compounds, are also extensively developed (Figure b, left). ,, Two recent notable protocols directly provide primary benzyl amines using hydroxylamine-based reagents inspired by heme-protein reactivity, as detailed by Arnold (using evolved enzymes) and a report from Yu, Chen, and Jia (using porphyrinoid-supported Fe catalysts). As documented in several reviews, these well-established C–H amination approaches are most commonly governed by substrate C–H bond strengths (e.g., selectivity for 2° over 1° C–H bonds), oxidation potentials (e.g., use of oxidizable arenes), or directing groups. − This background indicates the high potential utility of a deprotonative C–H amination method that proceeds with distinct selectivity and scope guidelines, especially if it can yield primary amines via a simple isolation procedure.

We proposed to advance C–H amination methodology through a positive halogen transfer (X-transfer) reactivity platform. Our group has previously used 2-halo­(benzo)­thiophenes as oxidants for Brønsted base-promoted C–H coupling reactions that merge C–H deprotonation, halogenation, and nucleophilic substitution events into one compatible sequence (Figure a). − Although stepwise traditional metalation and halogenation is a seemingly similar process, stoichiometric metalation of benzylic C–H bonds requires harsh bases (e.g., n-BuLi/TMPH/KO-t-Bu superbase mixture) that are incompatible with many functional groups. More importantly, subsequent quenching of the benzyl organometallic intermediate with a halogen electrophile typically leads to dimerization to form 1,2-diarylethanes. Thus, while deprotonative benzylic C–H halogenation could present unique synthetic opportunities over alternative activation strategies, it is an underdeveloped route for net oxidative C–H amine coupling. Stahl reported a recent advance in this regard for the mild base-promoted benzylic C–H halogenation of 2- and 4-alkylpyridines for use in subsequent substitution reactions, while Sarpong and Alabugin have developed intramolecular base-promoted amination reactions using I2 or O2 as oxidants, respectively. ,

Background and considerations for a base-promoted X-transfer approach to benzylic C–H amine coupling reactions.

In contrast to stoichiometric metalation chemistry, X-transfer-enabled methods use relatively weaker bases to promote an uphill C–H deprotonation event that generates transient carbanionic intermediates. These intermediates undergo selective C-halogenation and in situ substitution with a heteroatom nucleophile. This platform unlocks the benefits of reactivity guided by C–H acidity trends, including the ability to activate traditionally difficult-to-oxidize arenes (e.g., N-heteroarenes), the tolerance of oxidatively prone groups or weak C–H bonds, and distinctive site-selectivity in polyalkylarenes. − As an additional comparison, traditional radical C–H halogenation is often incompatible with nucleophilic groups within the molecule (e.g., amines) and is prone to overhalogenation and poor site-selectivity. In the context of preparing benzyl amines, if isolation of the desired benzyl halide is possible, the overalkylation of primary amines under basic conditions is a common challenge.

Our prior use of X-transfer reactivity for benzylic C–H alcohol coupling reactions laid the groundwork for developing a general intermolecular benzylic C–H amination method (Figure b). The reported etherification protocol uses alkoxide bases to functionalize moderately acidic C–H bonds (pK a ∼30–40 in DMSO) of methylarenes. , We sought to develop an amination method that is applicable to a much wider range of alkylarene substrates, including less acidic and secondary variants. Our recent disclosure of a styrene aziridination method that relies on an intramolecular β-aryl C–H amination reaction suggested this reactivity is possible, but would require key advances to be made. To achieve this goal, we herein disclose the development and properties of benzofuran- and indole-based X-transfer reagents (XTRs) that are more compatible with stronger bases and nucleophilic amine anions than our previously reported 2-halo­(benzo)­thiophene reagents. Thus, a new generation of XTRs are highly productive when used with hexamethyldisilazide (HMDS) bases for benzylic C–H aniline coupling and amination reactions.

Results and Discussion

Reaction Optimization for Aniline Coupling

We set out to develop a benzylic C–H aniline coupling protocol for 1-ethylnaphthalene (1) and 2-ethyl-6-methoxynaphthalene (2), two substrates that are beyond the previous pK a boundary and are 2° alkylarenes, which are unproductive in our etherification method (Scheme a). These substrates were first evaluated using our reported etherification conditions with 2-methoxy-N-methylaniline (3) as the model N-pronucleophile. The use of KO-t-Bu base and 18-crown-6 additive (2 eq each) with 2-iodo-3-phenylbenzothiophene (XTR 1) gives low yields of the benzyl amine products (4 and 6) with elimination observed as a competing pathway (5). We reasoned that a more kinetically active base is required to facilitate deprotonation of these less acidic substrates. This led us to investigate HMDS bases that have been reported to promote benzylic C–H deprotonation through favorable cation–π interactions. , The use of KHMDS instead of KO-t-Bu led to slightly improved amination yields for both substrates without competing elimination. However, we observed that a substantial amount of XTR 1 undergoes unproductive dehalogenation over the desired X-transfer process under these conditions.

We hypothesized that a less electrophilic XTR would be more compatible under the stronger base conditions yet maintain X-transfer capability given the more nucleophilic carbanionic intermediates generated from these relatively less acidic substrates. We reasoned that different heterocycle classes could provide more significant and streamlined electronic modulation compared to the modification of the benzothiophene core. To this end, we prepared the 2-halogenated benzofuran and N–Me-indole analogues of XTR 1 and found that the use of 2-iodo- and 2-bromoindole XTRs substantially improve the amination yields (up to 77% yield for 4 and 55% yield for 6, Scheme a). A more detailed investigation into the structure–activity relationships of these haloarenes is provided later in this Article.

We next investigated the amination of 2,6-dichlorotoluene (7, Scheme b) to extend this method to more acidic alkylarenes. Use of the conditions from our benzylic C–H etherification protocol with 2-iodothiophene (XTR 7) and KO-t-Bu led to 77% amination yield (8) with 10% of a tert-butyl ether side product (9). Replacement of KO-t-Bu with NaHMDS gives lower amination yield but no competitive substitution from the bulkier HMDS base. We speculate that, like XTR 1 with KHMDS, the reduced yield is a consequence of undesired 2-iodothiophene consumption by the HMDS base. To address this, we tested XTRs 1–6 and found that 2-iodobenzofuran and -indole reagents provide high yields (up to 88% with XTR 2).

The 2-halobenzofuran and -indole oxidants represent a new generation of XTRs that not only display improved reactivity over the benzothiophene platform but are also easier to prepare (Scheme c). A substitution and cyclodehydration sequence is used to access the benzofuran core, while a Fischer indole synthesis and methylation reaction are used to make the indole core. These reactions were conducted on >25 g scale, do not require column chromatography for purification, and will allow for easy analogue studies in the future. The halogenation steps for XTRs 2 and 6 use NIS and NBS, respectively, and enable facile regeneration of the XTR from completed oxidative coupling reactions. We note that while 2-iodoindole XTR 3 is an effective oxidant for C–H amination, its preparation requires a less practical 2-lithiation/iodination sequence. Thus, we selected XTRs 2 and 6 to employ as easily accessible oxidants that promote benzylic C–H amination across a wide pK a spectrum.

In general, the use of XTR 2 is suited for methylarenes and more acidic secondary substrates, while XTR 6 gives higher yields for less acidic variants. This reaction is operable on electron-deficient to -neutral methylarenes (8, 10, and 11) and electron-deficient to -rich ethylarenes (4, 6, and 12). Substrates 8 and 11 illustrate that steric hindrance from ortho-substituents is well-tolerated. N-Heteroaryl substrates that are challenging to oxidize under SET-initiated methods or that could coordinate to metal catalysts are amenable, such as alkylquinolines (13), -pyridines (14 and 15), and -pyrazines (16). These substrates are also notable as isolation of their corresponding benzyl halides is often complicated by competitive formation of ammonium salts (e.g., N-benzylpyridinium salts). Other medicinally relevant scaffolds like tetrahydroimidazopyridine (17) also undergo amination. A variety of potentially base-sensitive functional groups are tolerated, such as nitriles (18) and elimination-prone β-aryl amines (19). Free –OH groups that could inhibit C–H deprotonation are tolerated, including a carboxylic acid (20) and a primary alcohol that does not undergo oxidation (21). Substrates with relatively acidic aryl C–H bonds undergo chemoselective benzylic amination without aryl X-transfer side products observed (e.g., pyrazine 16 and benzofuran 12); however, we note that 1,3-azole substructures with an acidic C–H at the 2-position are not tolerated under these conditions. To highlight the unique site-selectivity of a base-promoted oxidative protocol, substrates 22 and 23 undergo amination at the most acidic C–H bond, offering precise and contrasting reactivity to radical-based methods that are either unselective or are guided by C–H bond strengths. ,, This also includes substrate 18 that is selective for a methyl group over an ethyl group. In cases where the yields are moderate, the mass balance is primarily comprised of remaining starting material and small amounts of elimination side product. The low yields are therefore partially addressable when the remaining starting material can be recovered, as was done for 15 with a 27% isolation of the remaining alkylarene. We also note that substrates with tertiary benzylic sites do not undergo C–H amination, and that either no reactivity is observed or the substrate undergoes competing desaturation.

Table b illustrates the generality of aniline coupling partners with 1-ethylnaphthalene (1) as a model substrate using XTR 6. Relatively hindered secondary N-alkyl (24) and more acidic N,N-diaryl (25) anilines are well-tolerated, as are endocyclic variants like benzomorpholine (26) and tetrahydroquinoline (27). Primary anilines with electron-donating (28) and -withdrawing groups (29) are effective coupling partners, including those with multiple ortho-substituents (28). Base-sensitive arenes like indazole (30) are tolerated, as are oxidation-prone groups like thioethers (29) and electron-rich arenes (31 and 32). A variety of anilines containing potential competing sites of functionalization undergo selective coupling. This is seen for 4-vinylaniline (33) that avoids both hydroamination and polymerization, as well as anilines with mildly acidic benzyl (34) and aryl C–H bonds (31) that do not undergo competing X-transfer. Additionally, chemoselective N–H coupling occurs for anilines that contain free alcohols (35) and alkylamines (36), albeit in moderate yields. This selectivity enables the direct use of medicinally relevant and heteroaryl amines, including imatinib fragment 37, aminopyridines (38 and 39), and a benzyl amine-containing aniline (40).

Studies on New X-Transfer Reagent Properties

The use of 2-halobenzofurans and -indoles represents a significant departure from the (benzo)­thiophene oxidant platform our lab has previously used in oxidative C–H coupling methods. , We therefore sought to determine how these new oxidants lead to improved performance. Two competing factors must be balanced for a haloarene to successfully enable a base-promoted oxidative C–H coupling reaction via X-transfer. The haloarene must first be stable to the basic conditions to avoid undesired SNAr or protodehalogenation processes, while at the same time, be sufficiently electrophilic at the halogen to facilitate transfer to the desired carbanionic intermediate. ,, These considerations were accordingly examined for the series of XTRs investigated in Scheme .

To assess the stability trends of the XTRs under the anionic amination conditions, time profile studies were conducted with each haloarene in the presence of aniline 3 and KHMDS (Scheme a). For iodobenzofuran XTR 2, progressive mass balance loss occurs over a 24 h period as the material converts to a mixture of protodehalogenated and indiscernible products. Meanwhile, bromobenzofuran XTR 4 is steadily consumed primarily through an SNAr reaction with the aniline (59% yield after 24 h). These observations are consistent with halogen electronegativity and SNAr reactivity trends, where iodine is more electropositive than bromine and bromoarenes are more SNAr active than iodoarenes. ,, Next, the benzothiophene reagents XTR 1 and XTR 5 are comparatively more stable but still undergo slow and steady consumption over 24 h. In this case, no SNAr is observed but the halogen electrophilicity trends are upheld, such that the bromo analogue retains more of the halogen. Finally, both indole reagents XTR 3 and 6 are highly stable toward the anionic conditions over 24 h.

We next sought to rationalize the ease of X-transfer to carbanionic intermediates by comparing XTR structural properties. The use of electrostatic potential (ESP) maps allows for the visualization of electron deficiency at the backside of the C–X bond, a property known as the σ-hole. − Consistent with halogen electronegativity trends, iodine is more electropositive than bromine within each heteroarene class. These maps also reveal that the benzofuran analogues have the greatest positivity at the halogen, while the indole halogens have slightly greater positive potential than the benzothiophene variants, consistent with the relative electronegativity of the proximal O-, N- and S-atoms. We also assessed the stability of the carbanionic species that would form upon X-transfer through density functional theory (DFT) pK a calculations for the corresponding C–H bonds. , The calculated pK a values show that the benzofuran carbanion is similar in stability to the benzothiophene, whereas the indole anion is significantly higher in energy. This collective insight suggests that the differences in X-transfer propensity between benzofuran and benzothiophene are driven by C–X polarity, whereas the differences between the benzothiophene and indole are driven by developing charge stabilization.

With the information in Scheme , several key takeaways can be made to rationalize and predict the reactivity of the haloarene oxidants. However, it is first important to note that the overall efficacy between each XTR and C–H substrate is dependent on various competing factors, such as base stability and differences in X-transfer activation energy barriers. Furthermore, the XTR must avoid multiple undesired pathways (e.g., SNAr or direct reactions with the various anions in solution). Therefore, rather than ranking the absolute electrophilicity of one XTR over another, our studies provide important insight on the requisite balancing of stability versus reactivity for a C–H bond of a given acidity. Nonetheless, we can generally describe the benzofurans as the most halogen-electrophilic and least base stable, while the indoles are the least halogen-electrophilic and most base stable.

This trend is discernible in the optimal XTR-substrate matches from Scheme and Table . For example, more acidic alkylarenes generate less nucleophilic carbanionic species and thus require a more electrophilic XTR. Such a haloarene is consequently less base stable but is accommodated for by the ability to use less-active bases for deprotonation. An example of this “matched” scenario is seen with 2,6-dichlorotoluene (7), where less-active NaHMDS base is used with the more electrophilic XTR 2. In contrast, a more active base is needed for the deprotonation of less acidic alkylarenes and is more suited for 2-haloindoles. These XTRs have greater base tolerance and the halogen electrophilicity, while lower, is sufficient for the more nucleophilic carbanionic intermediate. An example of this “matched” scenario is seen with 2-ethyl-6-methoxynaphthalene (2), where more-active KHMDS base is used in conjunction with the less electrophilic XTR 6. On the other hand, “mismatched” scenarios occur if a less-electrophilic XTR is used for more acidic alkylarenes, or vice versa, and do not provide high yields. An example “mismatched” scenario is 2,6-dichlorotoluene (7) with XTR 6, where only 1% yield of amination (8) is obtained. Overall, while benzothiophene XTRs can provide moderate yields in C–H amination reactions, the new benzofuran and indole XTRs are better tailored to accommodate a broad pK a spectrum of alkylarenes. These observed matching trends are shown in table form in the Supporting Information.

We conducted several additional control studies to support the proposed reaction pathway and reactivity trends; these are described in more detail in the Supporting Information. First, the basic conditions used in the optimized conditions were subjected to alkylarene C–H deuterium incorporation experiments. Rapid benzylic deuteration is observed, which is consistent with facile and reversible benzylic C–H deprotonation. Next, preliminary DFT modeling of the transition state structures for bromine transfer from representative XTR models to the 1-ethylnaphthalene benzylic carbanion show that 2-bromobenzofuran and -thiophene have similar and lower (by ∼4 kcal/mol) barriers for halogenation than an indole, with all energies accessible at the respective reaction temperatures (<25 kcal/mol). These results corroborate the XTR structural trends in Scheme and our hypothesis that reaction success is dictated by a balance of stability and reactivity, which can be semiquantitatively benchmarked based on the results in Table . In general, the less base-tolerant 2-iodobenzofuran XTR 2 is effective for more acidic benzylic C–H bonds (i.e., pK a below ∼36 in DMSO), but undergoes decomposition when applied to the stronger base conditions required for less acidic substrates. Conversely, the more base-tolerant 2-bromoindole XTR 6 is most effective for substrates with a higher pK a (i.e., ∼39-45 in DMSO), but is unreactive for significantly more acidic substrates. Finally, we note that independent subjection of the postulated benzyl bromide intermediates to the basic reaction conditions with aniline provide the expected benzyl amine products. Although we have not directly observed a benzyl halide intermediate during the course of this work, the observation of styrene (i.e., elimination) side products are consistent with such intermediates. Furthermore, in a prior report on ArCF2H oxidative coupling using 2-bromothiophenes, we directly observed ArCF2Br intermediates, which are known to undergo slower substitution than nonfluorinated benzyl halides.

Development of a Benzylic Primary Amination Method

With the identification of more tailored XTRs, we proposed that their utility for C–H amination could be expanded through the dual use of HMDS reagents as a base and nucleophile to produce TMS-protected benzyl amines. This application could provide a practical route to primary benzyl amines given the availability of HMDS bases and the potential to remove N-TMS groups through a simple protocol (Scheme a). We began this effort by investigating representative electron-rich, -neutral, and -deficient methylarenes (41–43) using HMDS reagents and XTRs 1–6 in 1,3-dimethyl-2-imidazolidinone (DMI). We observe the same XTR-substrate matching trends, where XTR 6 gives the highest yield for the least acidic substrate (o-NMe2-toluene, 41), XTR 4 works best for electron-neutral substrate 1-methylnaphthalene (42), and XTR 2 is best for the most acidic substrate 7-chloro-8-methylquinoline (43). Although XTR 4 undergoes facile SNAr under the aniline coupling conditions (Scheme a), HMDS bases do not engage in this competing process, likely due to their steric bulk (Scheme b). Thus, XTR 4 allows for better reactivity matching to improve yields for less acidic and electron-neutral methylarenes. We note that ethylarenes produce desaturation products as opposed to amines under these conditions; control reactions in the Supporting Information illustrate that secondary benzyl bromides undergo preferential elimination when treated with HMDS bases.

We next sought to develop a simple isolation procedure for the corresponding primary amines using an acid-base workup process (Scheme c). This required us to investigate several strategies, as the selective cleavage and purification of N–TMS-protected benzyl amines to yield primary amines has not been generalized. An obvious approach would be to treat the crude reaction solution with aqueous HCl to cleave the TMS groups followed by an acid–base extraction; however, numerous attempts at this procedure resulted in impure products and low yields due to various complications, such as the incompatibility of TMSCl as a byproduct. We next examined organic-soluble acids that could promote simultaneous deprotection and benzyl ammonium salt precipitation. After trying simple monoacids (e.g., HCl, TFA, and p-TsOH) without success, we were inspired by the frequent use of diacids in large scale process routes for amine salt generation. Commonly used diacids, like succinic or adipic acid, did not form precipitates, while others, such as maleic and itaconic acid, were not general. We found that the use of inexpensive oxalic acid in THF readily forms solid salts that are easily collected and triturated for a broad range of substrates. The oxalate ammonium salts can then be neutralized with aqueous NaOH and extracted to provide pure benzyl amine products without chromatography.

These selectivity trends (1° over 2°/3°) are distinct from other mechanistic approaches for installing an amino group onto benzylic C–H bonds (e.g., nitrene insertion or HAA methods) that tend to provide poor or alternative selectivity for similar polyalkylarenes. ,, We note that diamines (i.e., two benzylic positions functionalized) are observed as separable side products for 63 and 64.

Table c demonstrates the utility of this method for preparing benzyl amines from inexpensive starting materials where the corresponding benzyl halides (for substitution reactions) or aldehydes (for reductive amination) are not commercial or are substantially more expensive (67-71). This includes substrates containing functional groups that could potentially undergo undesired side reactions, such as aryl halides (67 and 68), alkenes (69), amides (70), and alkynes (71). While ethylarenes undergo preferential elimination over amination, we found certain diarylmethanes are competent substrates to access valuable diarylmethylamine substructures, which can be difficult to form via reductive amination of diarylketones. Specifically, less acidic variants like substrate 72 provide moderate yields, while more acidic diarylmethanes lead to only dimerization upon oxidation, as illustrated in the Supporting Information.

Table d illustrates the potential utility of this C–H amination protocol in a variety of medicinally and commercially relevant contexts. For example, C–H amination obviates a multistep route that uses a toxic benzyl halide en route to amine 73, an intermediate for the hypertension medication irbesartan. , The preparation of 73 was conducted on a 5 g scale where XTR 4 was regenerated from the recovered protodehalogenated benzofuran byproduct. This protocol can also be used to install amines on more complex medicinal compounds, such as prilocaine (74) that has two acidic N–H bonds and tolnaftate (75) with a thiocarbamate group. For prilocaine, amine cyclization to form amidine 74 occurs upon TMS-deprotection. The amination of additional commercial products, including LCD-relevant alkylfluoroarenes (76) and a benzotriazole-based sunscreen component (77) also provide high yields.

Conclusions

In summary, we have advanced benzylic C–H amination through practical methods for aniline coupling and primary amine synthesis with distinct scope and selectivity trends. This work substantially expands X-transfer-based reactivity through the development of new, easily-prepared haloarenes that are more effective for use with strong bases and nucleophilic amines. This extends X-transfer activation to a wider pK a range of benzylic C–H bonds and enables the use of secondary substrates. Finally, our structural studies point to the importantance ofbalancing the required base strength with the reactivity of a C–H substrate, pronucleophile, and haloarene oxidant. In this regard, 2-halobenzofurans and -indoles accommodate a wide array of coupling partners. Furthermore, the discovery that other classes of haloarenes can serve as effective oxidants suggests that simpler and more atom-economical variants can be identified to address potential large-scale utility concerns. We therefore anticipate that this strategy and modularity will allow the further diversification of coupling partners and advancement of X-transfer oxidative coupling. While the specific conditions detailed in this report are not immediately applicable to other classes of C­(sp 3)–H and N–H bonds, we are currently making strategic adjustments to the base and halogen oxidant strengths to further expand C–H amination methodology.
