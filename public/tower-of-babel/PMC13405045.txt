Anxiety–depression interaction and somatic symptoms in functional gastrointestinal disorders: a retrospective cross-sectional study from gastroenterology outpatient clinics
Authors: Shili Xiao, Pan Ran, Ling Li, Tianwei Deng, Jijia Gou, Yuqin He, Can Jiang, Sha Liu, Luofang Zhang, Linlin Chen, Li Kuang, Min Yang, Xin Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to explore the psychosomatic state of patients with functional gastrointestinal disorders (FGIDs), with the hypothesis that depression and anxiety are closely associated with somatic symptoms in patients with FGIDs.DesignRetrospective cross-sectional study.SettingThe study was conducted in gastroenterology outpatient clinics.ParticipantsThe analysis included 655 patients with FGIDs treated between March 2021 and December 2024.InterventionsThere were no interventions in this observational study.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome measure was the somatisation subscale score of the Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90-SOM). Secondary outcome measures included scores of the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS). Heart rate variability (HRV) was also measured.ResultsPatients with FGIDs demonstrated relatively high SAS and SDS scores. Multivariable linear regression showed that female gender and higher SAS score were associated with higher SCL-90-SOM scores after adjustment for available covariates. SDS score showed a negative coefficient after adjustment for SAS, which may reflect adjustment-related suppression or symptom-scale overlap rather than a protective effect of depressive symptoms. A statistically significant interaction between SAS and SDS on somatic symptoms was observed using standardised variables (B=0.042, 95% CI 0.018 to 0.067, Pinteraction<0.001).ConclusionsAmong patients with FGIDs in this single-centre outpatient sample, anxiety symptoms were strongly associated with higher somatic symptom burden. Depressive symptoms modified the association between anxiety and somatic symptoms in the interaction model. However, interpretation is limited by the cross-sectional design, possible symptom-scale overlap and residual confounding. These findings support further prospective studies and suggest that assessment of co-occurring anxiety and depressive symptoms may be clinically relevant in FGID care.Keywords: Depression & mood disorders, Anxiety disorders, China

Introduction

The prevalence of anxiety and depression among outpatients at gastroenterology clinics has risen significantly, as many patients seek medical care due to gastrointestinal discomfort.1 Anxiety and depression may be associated with autonomic regulation and somatic symptom burden.2 3 Somatic symptoms, characterised by the manifestation of psychological distress through physical discomfort,4–6 were highly prevalent among patients in gastroenterology clinics.7 8 This phenomenon is also particularly pronounced in functional gastrointestinal disorders (FGIDs), which include pain, fatigue and visceral hypersensitivity without clear organic pathology, complicating both diagnosis and treatment outcomes.9 10

Existing studies have verified the close associations among anxiety, depression and somatic symptoms in multiple populations.11–16 Although psychological comorbidities were always recognised as correlates of somatic symptom pathophysiology in patients with FGIDs through bidirectional gut-brain axis dysregulation,4 12 13 the interaction of anxiety-depression on somatic symptoms remained poorly understood. Crucially, it remains poorly understood whether their interaction amplifies or alleviates the severity of somatic symptoms.

Based on the above background, this study aimed to examine factors associated with somatic symptoms in patients with FGIDs and to assess whether depressive symptoms modified the association between anxiety symptoms and somatic symptoms.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted in the Gastroenterology Clinics of the Featured Medical Centre of Army Medical University (Daping Hospital). Data were systematically collected from patients who visited the gastroenterology clinics from March 2021 to December 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients had completed the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS), Symptom Checklist-90-Somatisation (SCL-90-SOM) and heart rate variability (HRV) during their treatment in the Clinic of Gastroenterology and (2) The outpatient diagnosis included FGIDs. The exclusion criteria included: (1) Patients with missing information or those who did not attend the gastroenterology clinics, (2) Patients with serious organic lesions, critical illnesses that prevented them from completing the scales and other tests and (3) Age <18 years. The institutional review board granted a waiver of informed consent for this retrospective study, and the research was conducted in a manner consistent with the Declaration of Helsinki (Clinical trial number: not applicable). Patient and public involvement

None. This was a retrospective study based on chart review, so patients were not provided with a brief description of any patient involvement.

Demography data and questionnaires

The demographic data included gender and age. Gender and age information were directly obtained from outpatient system records.

SAS and SDS, originally developed by Zung in 1971 and 1965, were psychometrically validated self-report instruments that used a 20-item scale to quantify past-week anxiety and depressive symptom severity, respectively.17 18 Based on the standardised scoring algorithm, the symptoms of anxiety and depression were defined if the SAS scores were ≥50 points and the SDS scores were ≥53 points, respectively. Both instruments demonstrated strong reliability and clinical utility for initial symptom screening across diverse populations.19 20

SCL-90, developed by clinical psychologists Derogatis et al in 1973, represented a multidimensional psychopathology inventory.21 The SCL-90-SOM subscale constituted a specialised 12-item somatic symptoms cluster derived from the original SCL-90. Focused on psychosomatic manifestations, it assessed physical symptoms potentially mediated by psychological factors, including headaches, gastrointestinal distress and musculoskeletal pain. This subscale demonstrated advantages in large-scale epidemiological studies due to its brevity, high discriminant validity and strong test-retest reliability.22–24 Although the SCL-90-SOM is not FGID-specific, it is a well-validated measure of somatic symptom burden that captures the non-gastrointestinal distress commonly comorbid with FGID.25–27

HRV measurement

In the outpatient setting, patients were instructed to sit in a relaxed posture, rest, and listen to soothing music for 5 min. During this period, RR interval signals were collected using the PEM-D Psychosomatic Holistic Assessment and Intervention System, developed by Zhongsheng Kaixin Company.

Frequency-domain analysis was then performed to analyse the RR interval spectrum and derive low-frequency and high-frequency components. The variable reported in the tables as the HRV-derived ANS index refers to the HRV-based output generated from this frequency-domain assessment. This index was used as a marker related to autonomic regulation, rather than as a direct or comprehensive measure of global autonomic nervous system function.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSSAU, V.25.0, Online Application Software. Continuous variables with approximately normal distributions are presented as mean±SD, while variables with non-normal distributions are presented as median and 25th and 75th percentiles. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Before statistical analysis, patients with missing data in the variables required for model fitting were excluded. Therefore, the analyses were based on complete cases. No missing-value imputation was performed. Associations between demographic characteristics, questionnaire variables and SCL-90-SOM scores were first examined using univariable linear regression. Variables with p<0.05 in the univariable linear regression analyses were then included in the multivariable linear regression models. Standard linear regression models with conventional standard errors were used. Robust standard errors were not applied in the primary analyses. To examine the interaction between anxiety and depressive symptoms, an interaction model was constructed using SPSSAU. Before model fitting, continuous independent variables were standardised to facilitate comparison of effect sizes and to reduce scaling differences between variables. The interaction term was then constructed based on the standardised SAS and SDS scores. All tests were two-sided, and p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. No formal correction for multiple testing was performed.

Findings

Patient characteristics

As shown in figure 1b and table 1, this study enrolled 655 patients with a mean age of 45.07 years. The cohort consisted of 274 male patients (41.83%) and 381 female patients (58.17%). Among the patients, 198 (30.23%) exhibited anxiety symptoms, while 378 (57.71%) exhibited depressive symptoms. Mean scores (± SD) were 45.79±9.98 for the SAS, 53.63±11.76 for the SDS, and 1.84±0.60 for the SCL-90-SOM subscale. The median HRV-derived ANS index was 0.50, with the 25th percentile at 0.33 and the 75th percentile at 0.97.

Interaction analysis

The interaction term between anxiety and depression exhibited a significant association with somatic symptoms (B=0.042, p<0.001, figure 2a). In the interaction model, SAS showed a positive association with SCL-90-SOM (B=0.451, p<0.001), with a coefficient larger than that of the other variables. In the multivariable model including both SAS and SDS, the coefficient for SDS became negative. Given the positive univariable association between SDS and SCL-90-SOM, this negative coefficient should not be interpreted as evidence that depressive symptoms reduce somatic symptoms. Rather, it may reflect adjustment for the shared variance between anxiety and depressive symptoms, possible suppression effects, or overlap between symptom scales.

Discussion

This study focused on patients with FGIDs and explored the complex relationship between anxiety symptoms, depressive symptoms and somatic symptoms. First, this study observed a high detection rate of anxiety (30.23%) and depression (57.71%) symptoms in patients with FGIDs (figure 1b), and the median scores of SAS and SDS were both at a relatively high level (table 1). This result is consistent with previous studies, suggesting that psychosocial factors are closely related to symptom burden in FGIDs.28–30

Second, gender, anxiety symptoms and depressive symptoms were associated with somatic symptom burden in the regression analyses. In particular, female gender and anxiety symptoms remained positively associated with SCL-90-SOM after adjustment for available covariates. This finding is highly consistent with the results of numerous previous studies.11 31–34 This suggests that biological factors (such as hormone differences and pain perception thresholds),35 36 sociocultural factors (such as differences in acceptance of expressions of physical discomfort)37 and psychological coping styles, etc,38 may jointly lead to female patients being more likely to experience and report physical discomfort. Therefore, if gender differences are ignored, it may affect the understanding of symptoms and ultimately lead to the inability to provide precise and effective medical services for patients with FGIDs of different genders.

An interesting finding of this study is the significant interaction between anxiety and depression (SAS×SDS, figure 2a). The negative coefficient for SDS after adjustment for SAS may reflect shared variance between anxiety and depressive symptoms, possible suppression effects or symptom-scale overlap. Slope analysis further depicted the dynamic pattern of this interaction (figure 2b). When patients have a high level of depression, the positive predictive association of anxiety with somatic symptoms is magnified, manifested as an increase in anxiety leading to a more intense growth of somatic symptoms (with a steeper slope). Conversely, at a low level of depression, the predictive association of anxiety with somatic symptoms is relatively weak (with a gentler slope).

Finally, HRV has been reported to be related to psychological stress and autonomic regulation.39–41 In this study, the HRV-derived ANS index was associated with SCL-90-SOM in the univariable analysis, but this association was not statistically significant after adjustment for available covariates. The association between HRV-derived ANS index and somatic symptoms may also be influenced by transient physiological states, medication use, sleep and other unmeasured clinical factors.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, because this was a cross-sectional study, causal relationships with SCL-90-SOM could not be inferred. Second, the single-centre design may limit the generalisability of the findings, as patients from a university-affiliated tertiary medical centre may differ from those in primary care settings or other regions in terms of symptom severity, disease duration and comorbidity profiles. Third, although adjusted models were used, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Several potentially important confounders were not systematically available in the dataset and therefore could not be included in the multivariable models. These factors include gastrointestinal symptom severity and disease duration, psychiatric diagnosis or psychiatric history, use of antidepressants, anxiolytics, antispasmodics, proton pump inhibitors, prokinetics or other relevant medications, sleep disturbance, major medical comorbidities and lifestyle factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption and caffeine intake. The absence of these variables may have led to residual confounding and may have influenced the observed associations between anxiety symptoms, depressive symptoms, their interaction and somatic symptom burden. Fourth, the results may also be affected by reporting bias, such as recall bias, and by selection bias because only patients who fully completed all scales were included. Finally, data on specific FGID subtypes, such as IBS, functional dyspepsia, functional constipation or overlapping FGID subtypes, were not consistently documented in clinical records during routine care. Accordingly, FGIDs were analysed as a combined group. This heterogeneity should be considered when interpreting the findings. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted as associations after adjustment for available covariates rather than as evidence of definitive independent or causal effects.

Conclusions

In conclusion, among patients with FGIDs in this single-centre outpatient sample, anxiety symptoms were strongly associated with higher somatic symptom burden. Depressive symptoms modified the association between anxiety symptoms and somatic symptoms in the interaction model. These findings are limited by the cross-sectional design, residual confounding and the use of self-report symptom scales. Further prospective studies are needed to clarify the temporal and clinical relationships among anxiety, depressive symptoms and somatic symptom burden in FGIDs. Assessment of co-occurring anxiety and depressive symptoms may be clinically relevant in FGID care.
