Discourse Diversity in Multi-Turn Empathic Dialogue
Hongli Zhan, Emma S. Gueorguieva, Javier Hernandez, Jina Suh, Desmond C. Ong Junyi Jessy Li

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) produce responses rated as highly empathic in single-turn settings ( 4 ; 26 ) , yet they are also known to be formulaic generators that reuse the same lexical patterns, syntactic templates, and discourse structures across tasks ( 22 ; 46 ; 36 ) . Less attention has been paid to whether this formulaicity extends to the level of discourse moves , i.e., what a response does for the person it is addressing. This question is especially consequential for empathic dialogue, where effective support demands not just a kind response at one moment but varied strategies as a conversation unfolds ( 55 ) . Indeed, prior work shows that LLMs already reuse the same tactic sequences more than human supporters in single-turn settings ( 18 ) . We extend this analysis to multi-turn conversations and find that the rigidity compounds: once a tactic appears in a supporter turn, LLMs reuse it in the next at nearly double the rate of humans ( 0.50 0.50 – 0.56 0.56 vs. 0.27 0.27 ). This pattern holds across LLMs serving as natural supporters in real emotional support conversations, and is invisible to standard similarity metrics. To address this gap, we introduce Mint ( M ulti-turn I nter-tactic N ovelty T raining), the first reinforcement learning framework to optimize discourse move diversity across multi-turn empathic dialogue. The best Mint variant combines an empathy quality reward with a cross-turn tactic novelty signal, improving aggregate empathy by an average of 25.3 % 25.3\% over vanilla across 1.7 1.7 B and 4 4 B models while reducing cross-turn discourse move repetition by 26.3 % 26.3\% on the 4 4 B model, surpassing all baselines including quality-only and token-level diversity methods on both measures. These results suggest that what current models lack is not empathy itself, but the ability to vary their discourse moves across the arc of a conversation. We publicly release our data and code at https://github.com/honglizhan/mint-empathy .

Empathy Tactics: LLMs Loop Across Turns

This section shows LLMs use repeated discourse moves in multi-turn conversations. We first define a taxonomy of empathy discourse moves based on theories in psychology, and then train a sentence-level tagger to detect them using human-annotated data. When applied to multi-turn conversations, the tagged data show that LLMs lock into the same moves from one turn to the next (§
3.1
).

A Taxonomy of Empathy Discourse Moves (Tactics).

When people respond empathically, they draw on a range of communicative moves: validating feelings, inviting elaboration, paraphrasing concerns, offering advice, or sharing their own reactions. We refer to these discourse moves as
empathy tactics
. We use a taxonomy of
10
empathy tactics from
18
, synthesized from psychology literature and refined through iterative sentence-level coding by three independent raters with substantial inter-annotator agreement. Tactics capture a level of granularity that matters for our purpose: they are specific enough to be reliably identified in text, yet general enough to characterize what a response is trying to
do
for the person it addresses. Each tactic serves a distinct discourse function, and skilled supporters select among them as the conversation evolves. Table
3
lists the
10
tactics; full definitions and psychological grounding are in Appendix
A
.

4.1
Task Formulation

We treat multi-turn empathic supporter response generation as a turn-level reinforcement learning (RL) problem. Let
\{x_{0},y_{0},x_{1},y_{1},\dots,x_{t}\}
be the dialogue history at turn
t
, where
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
denote empathy seeker and supporter messages respectively. The policy
\pi_{\theta}
conditions on this history and generates a candidate supporter response
y_{t}
, with the goal of producing responses that are both empathic on their own and tactically diverse at the discourse level across conversational turns.

To optimize this objective, we use GRPO
(
47
)
, and sample
N
candidate responses for each dialogue history. Each candidate is scored by a base empathy quality reward
\mathcal{R}_{q}
, augmented with a tactic diversity reward that compares the current turn’s tactic profile against the preceding turn’s (§
4.2
). All reward components are min-max normalized within each rollout group before combination.

4.2
Tactic Diversity Reward

A key challenge in optimizing for tactic diversity is defining what “diverse” means in context. Simply rewarding the use of many different tactics would ignore the conversation history: a response that deploys
validation
,
advice
, and
questioning
is tactically broad, but if the previous turn already used the same three tactics, the conversation as a whole still feels repetitive. Our diversity reward captures two complementary aspects: whether the current turn differs from the previous one (
cross-turn novelty
), and whether it spreads across multiple tactics rather than relying on just one (
within-turn breadth
). We formalize these as a KL divergence term
D_{\text{KL}}(Q_{t}\|P_{t-1})
and an entropy term
H(Q_{t})
, described below.

Cross-Turn Tactic Novelty via KL Divergence.

How different is the current turn’s tactic profile from the conversation context? We measure this as the KL divergence between the current turn’s tactic distribution
Q_{t}
and the reference distribution
P_{t-1}
from the immediately preceding supporter turn:

D_{\text{KL}}(Q_{t}\|P_{t-1})=\min\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}Q_{t}(k)\log\frac{Q_{t}(k)}{P_{t-1}(k)},\;\tau\right)

(1)

where
k
indexes the
K{=}10
tactics and
\tau=5
is a clipping threshold that prevents extreme reward values when the two distributions are nearly disjoint. Both distributions are Laplace-smoothed to handle zero counts:

Q_{t}(k)=\frac{c_{t,k}+\alpha}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}c_{t,j}+K\alpha},\qquad P_{t-1}(k)=\frac{c_{t-1,k}+\alpha}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}c_{t-1,j}+K\alpha}

(2)

Here,
c_{t,k}
is the number of sentences in
y_{t}
tagged with tactic
k
by the tactic tagger
\mathcal{T}
; we set
\alpha=0.1
in all experiments. Higher values of
D_{\text{KL}}(Q_{t}\|P_{t-1})
indicate greater departure from the previous turn’s tactic mix, directly rewarding the model for shifting its empathic support strategy as the conversation progresses (clipped at
\tau{=}5
; Eq.
1
).

Within-Turn Tactic Breadth via Entropy.

While the KL term encourages the model to vary tactics across turns, it does not directly reward using multiple tactics within a single response. We additionally include an entropy term to complement the KL signal:

H(Q_{t})=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}Q_{t}(k)\log Q_{t}(k)

(3)

A response that relies on a single tactic receives zero entropy, while one that draws on different empathy tactics in the same turn receives a higher value. If the tactic tagger
\mathcal{T}
detects no tactic in
y_{t}
,
H(Q_{t})
defaults to zero. We examine the interaction between the KL and entropy terms in our experiments (§
5
).

5.2
Evaluation: Perceived Empathy and Tactic Diversity

Neither empathy quality nor tactic diversity alone tells the full story: a model can score high on

by repeating one effective pattern every turn, and a model can achieve low

by diversifying at the expense of coherent support. We therefore evaluate on both axes jointly. For perceived empathy, we adopt the Lend-an-Ear framework

The original framework scores at the conversation level; we adapt it to the turn level by presenting the judge with dialogue history up to the current turn and a criterion-specific few-shot prompt (Appendix

). Using

served via vLLM

5.4
Methods

Baselines.

We compare against baselines from three families, including
prompt-based
methods,
quality-only RL
that optimizes solely for empathic quality, and
quality RL with token-level diversity
that incorporates an entropy-based diversity objective into the policy optimization on top of the quality reward.

To elicit empathic responses, we first experiment with
6

prompt-based
methods:
1) Vanilla
, where the model is instructed to respond empathically with no mention of specific tactics, serving as our weakest baseline.
2) Tactic Prompt
enriches the system prompt with definitions of all ten empathy tactics and instructs the model to use a variety of them across turns.
3) Tactic Prompt + History
extends the Tactic Prompt by injecting each preceding turn’s tactic usage into the prompt, instructing the model to prioritize tactics it has not yet used. We also combine each prompt condition with verbalized sampling
(
71
)
, an inference-time diversity technique that generates multiple candidates and selects one via probability-weighted sampling (see Appendix
H
for details), yielding
4) VS (Vanilla)
,
5) VS (Tactic Prompt)
, and
6) VS (Tactic Prompt + History)
.

To test whether optimizing directly for empathic quality changes the picture, we evaluate
RL with a quality-only reward
:
7) PsychoCounsel

(
72
)
, a Bradley-Terry reward model trained on over
36
k preference pairs from counseling dialogues, grounded in professional psycho-counseling theory (details in Appendix
H
). We denote this reward as
Q
and train the base model with GRPO using

Finally, to test whether token-level diversity can complement empathic quality, we evaluate

8) R1-Zero-Div

64

, which keeps

Mint

Methods.

Mint
introduces diversity directly into the reward function at the level of discourse moves, rather than regularizing the per-token distribution as in R1-Zero-Div. Building on the quality reward
Q
established above, we augment it with the cross-turn KL divergence term
D_{\text{KL}}
and the within-turn entropy term
H
defined in §
4
. All three components are min-max normalized to
[0,1]
within each rollout group so that quality and diversity operate on the same scale. We evaluate three main configurations:
1) Q + D
{}_{\text{KL}}
, which adds the cross-turn KL term to encourage the model to depart from its recent tactic distribution, reducing repetitive tactic patterns across turns;
2) Q + H
, which adds the within-turn entropy term to reward breadth of tactic usage within a single response, pushing the model to draw on a broader mix of strategies rather than defaulting to the same dominant tactics; and
3) Q + D
{}_{\text{KL}}
+ H
, the full
Mint
objective, which jointly encourages cross-turn departure and within-turn breadth. For this combined variant, we weight the two diversity terms by
0.5
each so that their total contribution matches the scale of the quality reward (i.e.,
R=Q+0.5(D_{\text{KL}}+H)
).

Format and Length Constraints.

All methods share the same format constraint: responses must read as natural, conversational messages with no visible tactic labels or meta-commentary. Since response length has been shown to correlate strongly with reward model scores
(
51
)
, we apply a length penalty during RL training that scales down rewards for responses exceeding a
200
-token target.

Conclusion

LLMs reuse the same empathic discourse moves across turns at nearly double the rate of human supporters, a pattern invisible to standard similarity metrics. We make this visible through a psychologist-curated taxonomy of empathy tactics. To address it, we introduce
Mint

, the first RL framework, to our knowledge, to optimize discourse move diversity in multi-turn empathic dialogue. By combining an empathy quality reward with a cross-turn KL divergence signal,
Mint
improves aggregate empathy by
25.3\%
over Vanilla while reducing cross-turn tactic repetition by
26.3\%
on the
4
B model. Our experiments further reveal that token-level diversity does not reach discourse moves, and that within-turn tactic breadth alone degrades empathy, underscoring that diversity must be defined at the level of discourse functions and anchored by quality.

Ethics Statement

Safety Measures.

Our training data are drawn from publicly available sources (WildChat
(
75
)
and SENSE-7
(
56
)
), and our evaluation data come from the Lend-an-Ear dataset
(
24
)
. We applied filtering to exclude conversations containing potentially harmful, offensive, or illegal content. We performed masking on named entities in the seeker messages and supporter responses, and ensured that no personally identifiable information was retained.

Limits on Evaluation Protocol.

A key limitation of our work is that we do not evaluate
Mint

in fully interactive conversations. When generating multi-turn dialogues, we produce each supporter response conditioned on the gold conversation context: the original seeker messages and the original supporter responses from prior turns. The model never conditions on its own previous outputs, because replacing a gold supporter turn with a model-generated one would invalidate the seeker’s subsequent message, which was written in response to the original. This means our evaluation measures whether
Mint
can diversify tactics given a fixed conversational trajectory, but not whether it can sustain that diversity when its own responses shape where the conversation goes next. A more ecologically valid evaluation would have real users chat directly with our trained system over multiple turns. However, this would require participants to share emotionally distressing personal experiences with an AI in real time, raising serious ethical concerns around psychological risk, informed consent, and data privacy that necessitate carefully designed safeguards (e.g., access to human support, opt-out mechanisms, post-session debriefing). We view our turn-level evaluation as a necessary first step that establishes tactic diversity as a trainable and measurable objective, and leave live interactive evaluation to future work once the appropriate ethical infrastructure is in place.

Advice.

Providing ideas for actionable solutions or coping strategies that the empathy-seeker could employ in the face of their situation. Giving advice has been linked to positive outcomes for the advice-giver
(
15
;
44
)
. Advice-giving has also been suggested to be an important part of being empathetic
(
14
)
. Example statements of this tactic include:

•

If I were you I would see a therapist.

•

You might want to look into taking a melatonin supplement.

•

You should go get some ice cream!

•

Definitely talk to your boss about it.

Assistance.

Offering to personally do something for or with the empathy-seeker to aid them. This also includes offering personal contacts (friends/family/etc.) that could potentially aid the empathy-seeker. Research has found that helping results in positive consequences like feelings of belongingness and gratitude in those helped. Alongside benefits for those helped, helpers also receive a range of benefits from the act of helping; altruism has been positively associated with health and wellbeing, as well as an increased likelihood to ask for help in a future instance
(
2
;
31
;
45
)
. Essentially, Assistance extends an invitation for help from the support-giver to the support-seeker. Research shows there are benefits of strong social networks for collecting information, among other benefits
(
52
)
. Example statements of this tactic include:

•

I’m here for you if you want to talk.

•

Come stay with me for a while.

•

You can borrow my [x]!

•

Can I do anything to help?

Emotional Expression.

An empathy-giver’s communication of their own feelings, reactions, or thoughts to the empathy-seeker as a result of hearing the empathy-seeker’s story. Expressing emotions like concern or compassion toward someone seeking support is an important way to show them that they (and their feelings) are being invested in. This is an integral part of building rapport and responding empathically
(
14
)
. Any use of emojis or emoticons in text is also considered an expression of this tactic. Example statements of this tactic include:

•

I’m so sorry to hear that.

•

I’m so happy for you.

•

I think she can appreciate that sentiment.

•

Your friend is weird, I don’t understand that at all.

•

Wow, what a beautiful story.

Empowerment.

Positive, uplifting statements about the empathy-seeker’s character and capability to handle their given situation. Empowering an empathy-seeker through things like compliments can increase feelings of belonging and create a bond between them and the person they’re speaking to
(
76
)
. Example statements of this tactic include:

•

You’re so strong.

•

You are going to get through this.

•

You’re going to succeed at anything you do.

Information.

Offering official resources that an empathy-seeker could turn to for help (e.g., links to websites, phone numbers, organizations), or stating information that may assist in answering the empathy-seeker’s questions, calming their anxieties, and potentially guiding them to a solution for their situation (if applicable). Within this component we also include well-intentioned, but factually incorrect statements that are
presented
as factually correct
and stated with the intent to help
the empathy-seeker. Example statements of this tactic include:

•

Providing official resources:
Here’s the link to [x] website…

•

Factual statements:
Flying is the safest form of travel.

•

Statements justifying advice:
Sunshine is good for your health.

•

Statements presented as factually correct:
Everyone is a narcissist.

Paraphrasing.

An empathy-giver’s perceived understanding of the situation, feelings, or experiences they inferred from the empathy-seeker. Particularly, we define an expression of Paraphrasing as an empathy-giver’s communication of the empathy-seeker’s feelings back to them. This is particularly important because an empathy-giver’s communication of their cognitive understanding establishes their invested interest in the empathy-seeker, which is vital for forging trust and bonds between the two
(
62
)
. Example statements of this tactic include:

•

I’m hearing that you feel overwhelmed.

•

It sounds like you’ve been through a lot recently.

•

You must be so excited!

•

You said that you’re up for a promotion soon.

Questioning.

Questions aimed at improving understanding of the empathy-seeker’s feelings, experiences, or situation. Asking questions for further clarification or more information indicates an active interest in the empathy-seeker, which is another important aspect of expressing empathy
(
14
;
34
)
. Example statements of this tactic include:

•

What happened?

•

How are you feeling?

•

What do you think about [x]?

Reappraisal.

Statements prompting the empathy-seeker to engage in cognitive reappraisal. Cognitive reappraisal is a strategy to change the way one thinks about a given situation in order to change its emotional impact
(
17
;
5
;
33
)
. This component is distinct from reframing, which is a much more nebulous concept about changing the way one views situations, emotions, or thoughts. Reappraisal has been found to be psychologically beneficial for the person practicing it
(
58
)
, though its benefits have been found to depend on the context in which it’s used
(
59
)
. Example statements of this tactic include:

•

It wasn’t your fault.

•

[x] was out of your control.

•

Remember that this separation is temporary.

•

[x] doesn’t mean that you’re not intelligent or capable.

Self-Disclosure.

An empathy-giver sharing personal information about themselves or acknowledging similar past feelings and/or experiences to the empathy-seeker. Self-Disclosure is an integral component of relationship development and has been positively associated with relationship quality and satisfaction
(
11
;
53
;
3
;
54
)
. Revealing personal information about oneself to another establishes intimacy, promotes openness, and fosters depth within that relationship. Additionally, it’s been found that self-disclosure in online contexts is as effective as face-to-face contexts for relationship development
(
12
)
. Example statements of this tactic include:

•

I’ve felt the same way.

•

I’ve had that happen to me before too.

•

I have two children and they’re always getting into trouble.

Validation.

Statements that reassure, normalize, or validate an empathy-seeker’s feelings. Research shows that validating someone’s feelings results in positive affect, particularly regarding the validation of physical pain
(
28
;
13
)
. This suggests that the validation of emotional pain likely results in similar positive affect, which would aid in establishing rapport between an empathy-giver and empathy-seeker. Example statements of this tactic include:

•

Everyone has feelings like this.

•

I know it’s hard right now.

•

I feel you. / I hear you. / I see you.

•

Your feelings are valid.

•

You’re not overreacting.

G.1
Empathic Communication Framework

The following framework is included verbatim in every evaluation prompt. It provides seven annotated seeker–supporter exchanges illustrating common empathic-support pitfalls, together with more empathic alternatives. Adapted from
24
.

Prompt: Empathic Communication Framework

You are an empathic communication expert. You are assessing empathic support provided by the supporter to the seeker. Use the following framework to guide your assessment.

Asking Open-Ended vs. Closed-Ended Questions

Seeker:

“I’ve been feeling so detached from everything lately. Like, I’m just going through the motions without really being there.”

Supporter:

“Do you feel like you’re going through depression?”

Annotation:

•

What Happened: The supporter shows some curiosity about the seeker’s state (“Are you depressed?”), but the question is closed-ended (yes/no). This tends to limit the seeker’s elaboration.

•

More Empathic Alternative:

–

Validate: “That sounds really tough. It sounds like you feel numb or disconnected.”

–

Open-Ended: “Can you tell me more about what feeling detached is like for you?”

This approach acknowledges the seeker’s emotional distress and invites them to share more.

Validating Before Problem-Solving

Seeker:

“I feel overwhelmed and exhausted all the time, like I’m running on empty.”

Supporter:

“What ways have you tried to recharge yourself?”

Annotation:

•

What Happened: The supporter immediately offers a problem-solving angle (“How are you recharging?”) rather than pausing to validate the seeker’s exhaustion.

•

Why It’s Suboptimal: It overlooks the intense emotions (“overwhelmed,” “running on empty”) just mentioned.

•

More Empathic Alternative:

–

Mirror/Validate: “Overwhelmed and running on empty — wow, that sounds really draining.”

–

Empathic Follow-Up: “What’s been weighing on you the most lately?”

By first reflecting the seeker’s emotional language and showing understanding, the supporter helps the seeker feel heard and accepted before moving on to any next step.

Avoiding Unsolicited Advice

Seeker:

“I used to do yoga, but I haven’t had the time or energy lately. I’m so drained.”

Supporter:

“I think sometimes it’s important to push ourselves to enjoy things we used to — yoga could be so relaxing.”

Annotation:

•

What Happened: The supporter gives direct advice (“push ourselves” to do yoga) instead of focusing on how drained the seeker feels.

•

Why It Feels Off: Even if the advice is well-intentioned, the seeker just said they have no energy. This comes across as not fully listening.

•

More Empathic Alternative:

–

Validate: “It must be discouraging when even yoga, something you used to love, feels like a chore.”

–

Ask Permission to Advise: “I have some ideas that might help — would you like to hear them, or do you just need me to listen right now?”

Soliciting permission respects the seeker’s autonomy and ensures they actually want advice.

“It’s Not About the Nail”: Listening vs. Fixing

Seeker:

“I’ve tried taking short breaks, but nothing seems to help much. I’m so overwhelmed.”

Supporter:

“Have you reached out to a therapist? Maybe problem-solving with them would help.”

Annotation:

•

What Happened: The supporter jumps into “fix it” mode (therapist referral), which can be helpful — if the seeker asked for solutions.

•

Why It Can Miss the Mark: The seeker just revealed deep distress (“overwhelmed”), which needs emotional acknowledgment.

•

More Empathic Alternative:

–

Mirror: “Taking breaks hasn’t helped, and you’re still feeling overwhelmed — that must be so frustrating.”

–

Explore: “Can you say more about what’s weighing on you the most?”

Normalizing Struggle and Encouraging Without “Shoulds”

Seeker:

“Part of me feels like I should be okay by now. Maybe talking to someone could help.”

Supporter:

“I think you should. You’ll come out with a new mindset.”

Annotation:

•

What Happened: The supporter is telling the seeker what they “should” do (“go to therapy”), which can feel pushy.

•

Why “Should” Can Alienate: It positions the supporter as an authority figure and can sound like “I know better than you.”

•

More Empathic Alternative:

–

Validate and Normalize: “It’s completely understandable to feel hesitant or think you ‘should’ be okay. We all need help sometimes.”

–

Encourage Autonomy: “If you’re open to it, talking with a therapist can be a safe place to figure out these overwhelming feelings.”

Expressing Concern and Care (Non-Abandonment)

Seeker:

“I’m feeling alone. I don’t want to bother anyone with my problems.”

Supporter:

“Is there anyone else you can talk to about this? Maybe a friend or family member?”

Annotation:

•

What Happened: The question might be well-meaning, but it sidesteps the fact that the seeker is coming to you now.

•

Why It Might Feel Off: The seeker just expressed feeling alone and not wanting to be a burden. Asking if they’ve tried someone else can unintentionally push them away.

•

More Empathic Alternative:

–

Direct Compassion: “I’m so sorry you feel alone. I’m here to listen if you want to share more.”

–

Invite Elaboration: “Could you tell me more about what feeling alone looks like for you day to day?”

Encouraging Exploration of Feelings

Seeker:

“I just lost my job today. It feels like a punch to the gut. I’m scared and overwhelmed, especially at 52.”

Supporter:

“I’m sure 52 is still young. You can do anything you put your mind to. Have you looked at unemployment yet?”

Annotation:

•

What Happened: The supporter rushes into reassurance (“52 is young!”) and problem-solving (unemployment benefits).

•

Why It May Feel Minimizing: The seeker’s fear and shock (“punch to the gut”) get glossed over.

•

More Empathic Alternative:

–

Acknowledge Shock: “Oh no — losing a job like that must feel like such a blow. I’m so sorry you’re going through this.”

–

Invite Reflection: “What scares you most about this next chapter?”

By acknowledging the impact first, you show you’re truly hearing the emotional weight of the situation.

Key Takeaways

•

Validate First, Solve Later: Before offering suggestions, reflect the emotion (“That sounds really tough”) so they feel heard.

•

Ask Permission for Advice: “Would you like some ideas, or do you just need me to listen right now?” This keeps the seeker’s agency intact.

•

Use Open-Ended Questions. Instead of “Are you depressed?” try “What does feeling detached look like for you day to day?”

•

Mirror Their Words: Repeat keywords or phrases (“overwhelmed,” “running on empty,” “punch to the gut”) to show you’ve genuinely absorbed what they said.

•

Offer Empathy, Not Just Reassurance: Saying “Oh, that’s scary. I’m here for you” goes further than “You’ll be fine!” because it acknowledges real pain.

•

Express Non-Abandonment: Phrases like “I’m not going anywhere; I care about you” remind them they have support, even if no immediate solution exists.

Final Note: All of these examples underscore one major theme: when someone is hurting, the main goal is to make them feel seen and heard, not to instantly fix the problem. If you do give advice, ask if they want it first. Above all, empathic listening requires genuine curiosity about their experience, consistent emotional validation, and reassurance that you will remain present with them in their distress.
