Antitumoral activities of λ‑carrageenan oligosaccharide- based nanoparticles
Authors: Jennifer Saliba, Manon Porta Zapata, Claudia Miranda-Perez de Alejo, Rachida Mersni, Ainhize Urkola-Arsuaga, Benjamin Musnier, Julien Cherfan, Alma Sánchez-Téllez, Thierry Maugard, Denis Biard, Luc Bousset, Kevin Baranger, Céline Trinh Le Vi Kieu, Véronique Gigoux, Jesùs Ruiz-Cabello, Ingrid Fruitier-Arnaudin, Susana Carregal-Romero, Hugo Groult
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polysaccharides (PS) are key building blocks for bioactive nanomaterials in oncology, as they enhance colloidal stability, enable chemical functionalization, support controlled delivery systems, and, for selected families, provide targeting or therapeutic activities. However, despite their high biomedical potential, PS families such as λ-carrageenan (λ-CAR) have remained virtually unexplored in nano-oncology, as poor solubility, restrictive rheological properties, and undesired anticoagulant and pro-inflammatory effects constitute a major barrier to systemic use in vivo. In a recent study, we showed that depolymerized λ-carrageenan oligosaccharides (λ-COS) overcome these limitations and enable the design of stable hybrid nanoparticles embedding free Mn²⁺ ions and ultrasmall ferrite cores (λ-COS NP), exhibiting excellent colloidal stability, in vivo compatibility, and efficient MRI contrast capabilities. Building on this work, the present study investigates the inherent antitumor potential of these “unlocked” λ-COS NP, harnessing the known complementary bioactive properties of the λ-COS scaffold and those of the ferrite/Mn2+ cargo. The λ-COS NP effectively inhibited the migration of MDA-MB-231 triple-negative breast cancer cells and suppressed heparanase (HPSE) activity, a key enzyme involved in tumor invasiveness. Moreover, λ-COS NP were efficiently internalized by RAW 264.7 macrophages and reprogrammed M2-like polarized cells toward an antitumor phenotype by restoring TNF-α and nitric oxide production. Preclinical studies in an MDA-MB-231 xenograft mouse model revealed efficient tumor accumulation and MRI detectability following intravenous administration, and a 50% reduction in tumor growth at the end of the therapeutic assays. Collectively, these results highlight λ-COS NP as promising tumor microenvironment–targeted nanotherapeutics with integrated bioactivities and imaging-tracking potential.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04651-4.

Introduction

To overcome the limitations of conventional cancer chemotherapies, such as the need of high-doses administration due to inefficient biodistribution or systemic toxicity accompanied by severe side effects, nano-oncology has emerged as one of the most promising strategies [1]. A major focus within this field is the development of multifunctional nanoparticles (NP) capable of selectively accumulating at tumor sites, delivering therapeutic agents or exerting intrinsic therapeutic effects, and generating imaging contrast for diagnostic and treatment-monitoring purposes [2]. Collectively, these features contribute to improved early detection, treatment precision, and outcome assessment [3, 4].

First-generation cancer nanotherapeutics primarily focused on delivering antimitotic drugs through passive targeting mechanisms, such as the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, or through ligand-mediated interactions with tumor cell surface biomarkers. Although these strategies have improved therapeutic efficiency and tolerability compared to the administration of free drugs, several limitations remain. Once NP enter the tumor stroma, they encounter a complex tumor microenvironment (TME) composed of a heterogeneous extracellular matrix (ECM) network, various immune cells, stromal components, and a dynamic array of biochemical factors [5–7]. In this intricate milieu, NP undergo multiple bio-interactions and matrix entrapment processes, particularly immune cell phagocytosis, before reaching cancer cells, thereby limiting their therapeutic impact.

To address these challenges, recent efforts have focused on engineering TME-targeted NP that exploit specific physicochemical or biological features of the TME [8]. The goal is twofold: first, to improve targeting, enable controlled drug delivery, or generate specific imaging contrast [9, 10]; and second, to exert therapeutic effects on key pro-tumoral pathways in the tumor microenvironment, such as immune suppression, angiogenesis, and cancer cell invasiveness and metastasis [11].

Among the promising materials for constructing TME-targeted NP, polysaccharides (PS) are gaining attention [12]. These natural biopolymers are valued for their biocompatibility and versatility, serving not only as stabilizing coatings or scaffolds in NP formulations [13] but also as targeting ligand [14] or bioactive entities [12, 15]. In particular, their structural similarity to glycosaminoglycan components of the ECM confers unique pharmacological properties in the regulation of pro-tumoral pathways within the TME. While early applications focused on a limited number of PS, new types, including lambda-type carrageenan (λ-CAR), a marine PS extracted from red seaweed, show promise for these applications.

However, λ-CAR suffers from low solubility and undesirable acute pro-inflammatory and anticoagulant properties, which limit its pharmacological use, particularly in systemic administration for cancer management. In recent years, oligosaccharide derivatives (λ-COS) obtained through depolymerization processes have been proposed with improved innocuity, enhanced bioavailability, and encouraging pharmacological profiles, including anti-angiogenic [16], anti-migratory, and anti-invasive activities [17], as well as potent immunomodulatory effects [18]. In particular, they have shown potential in regulating heparanase (HPSE), an enzyme overexpressed in most cancers with a large panel of pro-tumoral activities, including TME remodeling.

We recently reported that one of these λ-COS derivatives can serve as a versatile nanometric scaffold for the fabrication of highly stable hybrid NP of about 80 nm, embedding both extremely small ferrite cores (λ-COS NP) and complexed Mn2+ ions (stable in Mili-Q water but undergoing a controlled partial release of 20% in serum). This nanoplatform exhibits attenuated undesirable properties usually attributed to native λ-CAR, including reduced pro-inflammatory and anticoagulant activities, as well as improved suitability for systemic in vivo use. These NP also showed good magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contrast performance, arising from both the ferrite cores and the released Mn ions [19]. Building on these findings, the present study investigates the multifunctional, TME-targeted therapeutic potential of λ-COS NP in the context of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), exploiting the complementary bioactivities of the λ-COS matrix and the Mn2+/ferrite cargo. Indeed, both components have been independently reported to restore antitumoral functions of the inactivated tumor-associated macrophages (TAM) in advanced cancers [20, 21].

First, while not central to our approach, we evaluated the cytotoxicity of λ-COS NP in the MDA-MB-231 triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cell line. Next, we assessed the ability of λ-COS NP to regulate HPSE activity, especially its catalytic activity against heparan sulfate (HS), using in vitro enzymatic inhibition assay, quantification of the active HPSE and HS levels in MDA-MB-231 cell culture, and immunofluorescence studies with a custom-developed sulfated HS-specific scFv antibody. We also investigated the effects of λ-COS NP on the partially HPSE-dependent migration of MDA-MB-231 cancer cells, as well as its interactions with murine RAW 264.7 macrophages. Strong NP uptake without loss of macrophage (MΦ) viability supports possible immunomodulatory activity. Specifically, we examined whether λ-COS NP could reprogram the expression of three antitumor effector molecules on polarized MΦ toward an M2-like pro-tumoral phenotype. Taken together, these results highlight the bioactive potential of λ-COS NP within the TME, supporting their relevance for targeted and personalized cancer therapy in conjunction with MRI-based tracking as an innovative theranostic system.

Experimental section

Synthesis and characterization of λ-COS NP

The synthesis and characterization of λ-COS NP are reported in our previous work [19]. Briefly, λ-COS was first synthesized using H2O2-based radical depolymerization of native λ-CAR (FMC Biopolymer, USA) and left to react with H2O2 at 60 °C for 30 h. The resulting λ-COS obtained had a number average molar mass Mn of 3.9 kDa and a degree of sulfation (DS) of 22%.

λ-COS NP were synthesized by preparing a 1 mL solution of λ-COS (40 mg) in deionized H2O with the addition of 30 µM MnCl2·4H2O in HCl 0.05 M and 37 µM FeCl3·6H2O in 0.25 mL H2O. Then, 0.25 mL of hydrazine (64–65%) was added to the mixture prior to microwave irradiation at 70 °C for 3.45 min. NP were purified by gel filtration on a PD10 desalting column using milliQ-H2O as the eluent. The freshly synthesized NP had a concentration of 10.4 mg·mL-1 in λ-COS (determined by Azure A dosage, according to a previously published procedure) [22], 0.57 mg·mL-1 in Fe, and 0.37 mg·mL-1 in Mn (determined by ICP-MS) and had a Z-average of 79 nm (assessed by DLS).

Cell culture conditions

TNBC cells (MDA-MB-231) and murine macrophages (RAW264.7) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, LGC Standards S.a.r.l. – France Office, Molsheim, France). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified medium (DMEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Villebon-sur-Yvette, France) supplemented with 10% decomplemented FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (both from Gibco). All cell lines were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 and were mycoplasma-free (MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit, Lonza, Basel, Switzerland).

Cell metabolic activity assay

In a 96-well plate, 104 cells were seeded per well in 200 µL of the appropriate cell culture medium and incubated for 24 h to allow adhesion. The medium was then replaced with 200 µL of fresh medium containing native λ-CAR, λ-COS, or λ-COS NP at 0, 10, 50, 100, or 200 µg·mL-1 (sugar content). After 24 h of exposure, 25 µL of 3-(4, 5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2, 5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) solution was added to each well, followed by incubation for 3 h. The supernatant was then removed and replaced with 100 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Merck) to dissolve the formed formazan crystals before measuring the absorbance at 540 nm (BMG Labtech FLUOstar Omega, Germany).

Annexin V-PI flow cytometry-based apoptosis assay

MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in 6 well plates (2.5 × 105 cells per well) for 24 h and then treated with 100 µg·mL-1 in sugar content of native λ-CAR, λ-COS, or λ-COS NP. After 24 h, the cells were collected by trypsinization (TrypLE, Gibco, Fisher Scientific) and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing Ca2+ and Mg2+ (Gibco, Fisher Scientific). They were then stained with Annexin V-FITC (BioLegend; 5 µL per 106 cells) for 15 min in the dark at room temperature (RT). PI (Propidium Iodine; 5 µg · mL-1) was then added, and the stained cells were analyzed using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer and FlowJo software.

Qualitative cellular uptake of λ-COS NP

Cells were seeded in 24-well plates at 105 cells per·well for 24 h. Next, the cells were treated with cell culture medium containing 100 µg·mL-1 of λ-COS NP (assessed by Azure A assay) or not (control condition). After 24 h of incubation, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution, and λ-COS NP internalization was qualitatively assessed by microscopic observation (ZEISS microscope, Axio Observer 7, magnification: × 10 NA 0.25, ZEISS, Germany) using a Prussian blue nuclear fast red staining kit (Abcam®). Images were constructed using the Fiji software.

Quantitative cellular uptake of λ-COS NP

5.0 × 10⁴ MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in 35 mm culture dishes and incubated for 72 h with the λ-COS NP at a concentration of 100 mg·mL− 1 in COS. Following exposure, the cells were detached by trypsinization and washed twice with PBS 1X. The resulting cell pellets were digested in 65% HNO3 (Optima) and heated at 60 °C for 3 h to ensure complete mineralization. The digests were subsequently diluted with MQ-H2O to obtain a final acid concentration of 2% HNO₃ and analyzed by ICP–MS using an iCAP Q instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

In vitro HPSE enzymatic assay

Anti-HPSE activity was measured using an in vitro enzymatic assay based on a previously published Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET) method using labelled HS [22]. Briefly, the test was performed using the HPSE assay toolbox (Cisbio Assay) and human recombinant HPSE (R&D Systems). In a 96-well white half-area plate (Corning® #3693), 7.5 µL of MilliQ H2O (as blank) or sample (NP solution, realized in quadruplicates) was added with 7.5 µL of HPSE in buffer 1 (HPSE 400 ng·mL-1 in Tris-HCl at pH 7.5, 0.15 M NaCl, and 0.1% CHAPS) and preincubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Then, 15 µL of biotin-HS-Eu (K) in buffer 2 (1 ng·µL-1 in 0.2 M sodium acetate/acetic acid buffer, pH 4.5) was added to trigger the reaction, and the plate was incubated for 15 min at 37 °C. Finally, 10 µL of Streptavidin-XL665 in buffer 3 (10 ng·µL-1 in 0.1 M NaPO4 buffer at pH 7.4, 0.8 M KF, 0.1% BSA) was added prior to 5 min of incubation at 37 °C. After 60 µs excitation at λex = 337 nm, the fluorescence was recorded at λem1 = 620 nm and λem2 = 665 nm using a spectrofluorometer (BMG Labtech FLUOstar Omega) with a high-time-resolved fluorescence module. The percentage of HS degradation (%D) was calculated using the following equation.

Western blot analysis

Proteins from whole cell lysates were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Merck Millipore, Molsheim, France) supplemented with a mix of protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Cell Signaling, Ozyme, Saint-Cyr-l’Ecole, France) and quantified using the Lowry method (Bio-Rad, Marnes-la-Coquette, France). Equal amounts (30 µg) of proteins from the cell lysates or supernatants were loaded onto a 10% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes using a transblot system (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked with 5% half-fat milk solution (w/v) for 1 h in Tris Buffered Saline containing 0.1% Tween20 (TBS-T) at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following antibodies: anti-HPSE antibody (1/2000, Proteintech Europe, Manchester, UK) or anti-GAPDH (1/2000, Proteintech Europe). Membranes were washed three times with TBS-T and then incubated with the appropriate horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary IgG antibody (1/2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at room temperature for 1h30. Immunodetection was performed using the Clarity Western ECL kit (Bio-Rad) with a Chemidoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad, France). Optical density was measured using the ImageLab software. All optical density plots represent values that were normalized to the loading controls.

ELISA assay on HS and HPSE expressions

In a 6-well plate, MDA-MB-231 TNBC cells were seeded at 5 × 105 per well, then treated with different samples (λ-CAR, λ-COS, or λ-COS NP) at a concentration of 100 µg·mL-1 (sugar content) for 24 h incubation. HS or HPSE were quantified in the culture medium and lysates using ELISA kits provided by Elabscience® (Houston, Texas, USA) and LSBio® (Newark, CA, USA), respectively.

HPSE and HS fluorescence staining

MDA-MB-231 TNBC cells were seeded at 1 × 10⁵ cells per well in 300 µL DMEM onto sterilized glass coverslips placed in 24-well plates. After 24 h, the medium was removed, and the cells were treated with a HPSE enzymatic inhibitor, OGT2115 (MedChemTronica, HY-100898) or candidates (λ-CAR, λ-COS, or λ-COS NP) at a concentration of 100 µg·mL-1 (sugar content) for an additional 24 h. Following treatment, coverslips were collected, and the cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. The fixed cells were then washed with PBS and incubated in a blocking buffer (PBS supplemented with 3% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100) for 1 h at room temperature with gentle agitation. After washing, primary staining was performed using an anti-HPSE recombinant antibody (1/100, Proteintech Europe, UK) and an anti-HS antibody (1/10, a custom-developed sulfated HS-specific scFv antibody) applied using the one-spot technique (30 µL/spot), followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C. The coverslips were then washed thrice with PBS and incubated with the appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (HPSE: 1/800 goat anti-mouse 488, and HS: 1/500 goat anti-rabbit 555, 1/500, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h at room temperature with agitation. After three additional PBS washes, the coverslips were mounted on glass slides using ProLong™ Gold mounting medium with DAPI and allowed to cure overnight at room temperature. Fluorescence images were acquired the following day using a ZEISS microscope (Axio Observer 7, magnification: × 20 NA 0.8, ZEISS, Germany) under identical exposure conditions for all samples.

HS-specific scFv antibody (HS4C3)

The sequence of the HS4C3 scFv originates from Damen et al., 2024 [23] and van Kuppevelt et al., 1998 [24]. It was fused at the N-terminus with a secretion peptide (human interleukin-2 signal sequence) [25] and at the C-terminus with the constant region of a mouse or rabbit immunoglobulin. This transcription cassette is inserted into a replicative pEBV vector upstream of a CMV promoter [26]. These vectors enable strong and stable expression in HEK 293 cells (CRL-1573™). After transfection, cells are selected with puromycin (0.4 µg·mL-1), and the supernatant is collected 4–6 days later. The supernatant is centrifuged to remove cellular debris and then diluted 50% in EIAG buffer [0.1% BSA fraction V (Serva 11930.03), 150 mM NaCl, 0.01% sodium azide, 50% glycerol, 377 mM K2HPO4, 93 mM KH2PO4]. The diluted supernatants are stored at -20 °C. Our HS4C3 scFv antibody has been validated using immunocytochemistry (ICC) and immunohistochemistry (IHC).

Migration assay

To study the effects of the different candidates on the migration of MDA-MB-231 cells, specific 96-well plates coated with fibronectin (Oris™ cell migration assay, fibronectin-coated Platypus Tebubio®) were used. These plates feature stopper barriers that confine initial cell seeding to the outer annular regions before the removal of stoppers, allowing migration in a central cell-free detection zone of 2 mm in diameter. Cells were first seeded in migration plates with stoppers at a concentration of 105 cells per well (150 µL). After 24 h of adhesion, the culture medium was replaced with samples diluted in culture medium (DMEM, Gibco, Fisher Scientific) with only 2% FBS to limit cell proliferation. In addition to the control (untreated cells) and candidates tested at 100 µg·mL-1 in sugar content, reference molecules for migration (positive controls) were included: suramin sodium salt (Sigma Aldrich) at 20 µM, low molecular weight heparin (Mn: 3.6 kDa, DS: 38.5%) synthetized in-house [27] from native heparin (Molekula®) at 100 µg·mL-1 and Batimastat® (Tocris Bioscience No.29.611) at 10 µM. After adding the treatments, the stoppers were removed to allow migration process to occur. Live cell monitoring of cell migration was performed using a ZEISS microscope (Axio Observer 7, magnification: × 10 NA 0.25, ZEISS, Germany) for 24 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The images were analyzed, and the migration surface was quantified as the percentage of the surface occupied by the cells in the initial empty area using an homemade macro with adapted filters and thresholds in the Fiji software.

M1-like and M2-like MΦ polarization

In a 96-well plate, RAW 264.7 cells were seeded at 5 × 104 cells per well in 200 µL of DMEM containing FBS 10% and P/S 1%. MΦ were polarized toward the M1 phenotype by incubation with 1 µg·mL-1 Escherichia coli lipopolysaccharide (LPS O111:B4, L2630, Sigma Aldrich) and 20 ng·mL-1 interferon-gamma (IFN-γ, Peprotech, Thermo Fisher Scientific). M2 phenotype was obtained by supplementing the cells with 20 ng·mL-1 of interleukin-4 (IL-4, Peprotech). After 24 h of polarization, the polarizing stimulators were removed by aspirating the culture supernatants.

Measurement of nitric oxide (NO) production

For each MΦ phenotype (M0, M1, and M2), fresh culture medium containing 100 µg·mL-1 (sugar content) of either λ-CAR, λ-COS, or λ-COS NP was added for a further 24 h incubation. Then, 150 µL of culture supernatants of each condition were collected in cell-free wells and mixed with 100 µL deionized water and 50 µL of Griess reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A standard calibration curve was obtained by diluting a 1 mM stock standard solution of NO on the day of analysis. Following 30 min of incubation at RT, the absorbance was measured at 548 nm using a plate reader (Fluostar Omega, BMG Labtech).

ELISA assay on TNF-α and IL-6 markers

In a 6-well plate, 5 × 105 cells per well were treated with different samples (λ-CAR, λ-COS, or λ-COS NP) at a concentration of 100 µg·mL-1 (sugar content) for 24 h incubation. TNF-α and IL-6 levels in the cell supernatants of the differently polarized MΦ were quantified using kits provided by Abcam (Cambridge, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

In vivo inhibition of tumor growth

Animal handling was conducted in accordance with the European Council Directive 2010/63/UE. All experimental procedures were approved by the CIC biomaGUNE Ethical Committee and local authorities (Diputación Foral de Guipuzcoa, PRO-AE-SS-279, and PRO-AE-SS-225).

Six-week-old female Athymic-Foxn 1 nu/nu mice were orthotopically injected unilaterally with 1.5·106 MDA-MB-231 cells in 50 µL of 50∶50 matrigel/DMEM into the fourth abdominal fat pad. Tumor growth was monitored externally using a digital caliper. Two weeks post inoculation, a group of animals (n = 9) were injected intravenously with 100 µL of λ-COS (containing 1 mg·mL-1 Fe) via the tail vein. The control group received an equivalent volume of saline intravenously at the same time point.

Four animals from the group treated were subjected to MRI at different time points (baseline, 30 min, 4 h, and 24 h) after administration of the NP. T2 maps were obtained using an MSME (multi-slice spin echo) sequence. The TE values were varied in 30 steps ranging from 9 ms to 270 ms and synchronized respiration (TR 1600 ms). The acquisition was carried out with fat suppression, 1 average, 320 × 160 points, a Field of View of 8.0 cm x 4.0 cm, 14 slices with a slice thickness of 1.0 mm and 0.25 mm gap between slices. T2 maps were created with “mapT2.3pars.v20.py”. The quantification for the liver, kidneys, tumor, spleen and brain was performed with T2 maps using ImageJ software (version 1.53t).

Ex vivo experiments

The main organs (heart, lung, liver, spleen, brain, kidneys and tumor) were removed and fixed with formalin solution, neutral buffer, 10% and embedded in paraffin. 4 μm sections mounted on glass slides were deparaffinized with xylene and ethanol at decreasing concentrations and then washed with distilled water. Then different stainings were performed.

For H&E staining Procedure, the tissue section slides were subjected to hematoxylin solution, Harris Modified (Sigma-Aldrich) staining for 5 min. Afterward, the slides were rinsed in a 1% HCl solution and counterstained with Eosin Y solution (Sigma-Aldrich). A series of rinses in increasing ethanol and xylene concentrations ensued before the slides were mounted using synthetic mounting medium (DPX).

For PB staining Procedure, the slides were then stained for 1 h at 60 °C with Perls solution obtained by mixing the same quantity of 7% potassium ferrocyanate trihydrate in MilliQ water and 7% HCl. After being washed in water, the samples were stained with 0.1% nuclear fast red-aluminum sulphate solution (Sigma-Aldrich) for 2 min. Finally, the samples were washed with increasing concentrations of ethanol and xylene and mounted in synthetic mounting medium (DPX).

For Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis, antigen retrieval (H-3300-250) was then performed in a steamer using a specific solution for 20 min and 1 h of cooling. After three washes in TBS-Tween20 0.1%, hydrogen peroxidase inhibition is carried out in a humidity chamber incubating with Endogenous blocking solution for 10 min at room temperature. After three washes, samples were blocked with a solution of 1% BSA and 2% goat serum in TBS for 30 min at room temperature. Samples were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, using a dilution of 1/1000 for the anti-mannose receptor antibody (ab64693, Abcam) and 1/200 for the anti-iNOS antibody (ab3523, Abcam). After three additional washes, samples are incubated with secondary antibodies for 30 min at room temperature, using a dilution of 1/1000 for goat anti-rabbit HRP (ab6721, Abcam). Signal amplification is performed using VECTASTAIN® Elite Avidin-Biotin Complex (ABC)-HRP Detection Kit (PK-6100) during 30 min at RT. After three TBS washes, DAB labeling is performed with ImmPACT® DAB EqV Peroxidase (HRP) Substrate (SK-4105), stopping the reaction with MilliQ water. Finally, samples are counterstained with Mayer´s hematoxylin for 1 min, washed with tap water, and after a dip in 0.1% HCl solution they are dehydrated from ethanol to xylene and mounted using DPX mounting medium. Images were captured using a Cell Axio Observer or Axioscan 7 Microscope.

Toxicological endpoints measurements

8 female Foxn1 nu/nu athymic mice were injected intravenously via the tail vein with either a saline solution (control group, n = 4) or 100 µL of a λ-COS suspension containing 1 mg·mL− 1 of Fe (n = 4). 48 h after administration, blood samples were obtained via cardiac puncture and urine samples were collected directly from the bladder at the time of sacrifice. Blood samples were collected in BD Microtainer tubes without anticoagulant (Ref. 365968), kept at room temperature for 30 min, and subsequently centrifuged at 1000 × g for 30 min. The serum was separated, and both serum and urine samples were stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) activities, as well as urinary albumin (ALB) and total protein (TP) levels, were determined using a Spin230 analyser (Spinreact, Barcelona, Spain) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Standard quality controls were performed prior to each analysis, and all measured values fell within the ranges specified by the manufacturer.

Statistical analysis

One-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to identify significant differences between the control and experimental groups. All experimental data were obtained from at least three independent experiments, except migration assays and ELISA, which were performed at N = 2, and in vitro HPSE inhibition, which was performed at N = 1. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8 for Windows. A probability (p) value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Synthesis of λ-COS NP

λ-COS, derived from native λ-CAR, was obtained via a radical-based depolymerization method using H2O2. Then, λ-COS NP were synthesized with λ-COS along with FeCl3 and MnCl2 as metallic precursors through a microwave-assisted method. The resulting λ-COS NP can be described as λ-COS-based hybrid NP incorporating ferrite NP cores and embedding Mn2+ ions (Fig. 1). Their physicochemical characteristics and application as MRI contrast agents are detailed in our previously published article [19].

Effects and interactions of λ-COS NP on MDA-MB-231 TNBC cells viability

As an initial assessment to explore the various potential bioactivities of λ-COS NP, MTT cell viability assays were conducted on the MDA-MB-231 TNBC cell line following treatment with doses ranging from 0 to 200 µg·mL-1 (sugar content) (Fig. 2A). Free λ-CAR was included when applicable to assess the effect of depolymerization, along with free λ-COS to help elucidate the origin of any observed bioactivity and the influence of ligand-packing of λ-COS in NP form. After 24 h of incubation, no significant reduction in cell metabolic activity was observed, regardless of the candidate or dose used, except for free λ-COS at the maximum dose of 200 µg·mL-1, which induced a moderate decrease of 30% in viability. To further investigate the potential cytotoxic effects, a flow cytometry-based Annexin V/PI assay was used to assess apoptosis. The analysis summarized in Fig. 2B showed no pro-apoptotic effects at 100 µg·mL-1 after 24 h, for either free λ-COS or λ-COS NP, indicating the absence of cell lysis. However, a significant apoptotic cell population of 13% was observed after λ-CAR treatment compared to untreated cells. This apparent discrepancy may be explained by the relatively low proportion of apoptotic cells, which is likely insufficient to produce a measurable reduction in global metabolic activity in the MTT assay.

The cellular uptake of λ-COS NP was evaluated using brightfield microscopy. After 24 h of incubation with λ-COS NP, MDA-MB-231 cells were fixed and stained with Prussian Blue to visualize iron. As shown in Fig. 2C, the images revealed a moderate NP internalization.

Cellular uptake of the λ-COS NP by MDA-MB-231 cells was quantified after 72 h of exposure by ICP–MS through the measurement of intracellular Fe and Mn levels (Figure S1). For iron, cells exposed to the λ-COS NP exhibited an average Fe content, 2.6-fold higher than that of untreated cells, with a statistically significant difference between the two conditions. These quantitative results are consistent with the qualitative observations presented in Fig. 2C, where Prussian blue staining revealed moderate NP internalization on MDA-MB-231 cells. Mn was also quantified in both untreated and λ-COS NP-treated cells. The treated cells displayed a 5.6-fold increase in Mn content relative to the control group, with an even stronger level of statistical significance. This more pronounced and robust difference likely reflects the absence of substantial endogenous Mn, in contrast to iron, whose physiological intracellular levels and associated variability contribute to larger standard deviations. Altogether, the significant increases in both Fe and Mn concentrations following exposure to the λ-COS NP provide strong evidence for efficient NP internalization by the MDA-MB-231 cells.

Overall, these data are consistent with previous studies, which mostly report on the cytotoxic activities of λ-CAR [29], whereas such effects are rarely described for λ-COS [22]. Instead, λ-COS have been studied for other types of antitumoral effects, such as immunomodulation, anti-angiogenic, or anti-invasiveness properties [17, 30]. λ-COS NP were moderately taken up by MDA-MB-231 cells, in contrast to control human embryonic kidney cells HEK293 cellular uptake, as described in our previous work [19]. This suggests a selective interaction with malignant cells, which is probably due to the overexpression of cell-surface receptors that can recognize anionic sugars, such as λ-CAR or λ-COS [31, 32]. Although it is known that internalized ferrite cores usually elicit a cytotoxic response (since Fe and metabolized Mn can interfere with critical physiological processes such as oxygen transport, DNA synthesis for Fe, or as enzyme cofactors for Mn [33, 34]), surprisingly, no mitochondrial viability damage or cellular apoptosis was observed in MDA-MB-231 cells upon NP treatment. This is probably due to the limited internalization in the case of MDA-MB-231 compared to other cancer cell lines that have much higher expression of galactose receptors-mediated endocytosis, as hypothesized by Chen et al. [35]. Overall, our results raise intriguing possibilities regarding the mechanisms of action of λ-COS NP, suggesting that the part of the internalized NP could influence other critical cancer cells pathways than the apoptotic pathways, while the other (external) part could target biomolecular and cellular components within the TME, potentially leading to alternative therapeutic strategies that regulate immune-suppression, angiogenesis or tumor’s invasiveness [36, 37].

Regulation of HPSE by λ-COS NP and effects on MDA-MB-231 TNBC cell migration

One of the targets of NP arising from the λ-COS scaffold is heparanase (HPSE), an enzyme overexpressed in many types of cancers that plays a pivotal role in tumor progression. It is first synthesized as a latent 65 kDa pro-form before proteolytic cleavage in lysosomes, yielding a catalytically active 50/8-kDa heterodimeric mature configuration. In its mature form, HPSE is the only mammalian enzyme able to cleave the HS chains of HS proteoglycans (HSPGs), thereby triggering a myriad of pro-tumoral signaling and metabolic pathways, depending on HPSE trafficking and the subcellular localization of the HPSE/HS axis. At the cell surface, HS degradation contributes to ECM remodeling and the release of numerous HS-bound signaling molecules (growth factors, chemokines, and cytokines), promoting cancer cell migration and invasion, angiogenesis, immune suppression, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and exosome biogenesis [38]. Within the lysosomes, where HPSE activation occurs, HS cleavage affects the turnover of HSPGs and has been implicated in the modulation of autophagy and endo-lysosomal trafficking with consequences for survival signaling [39]. A fraction of HPSE can also translocate to the nucleus, where it modulates nuclear HS, thereby affecting chromatin structure and transcriptional programs. In addition to its catalytic activity, growing evidence indicates that both the 65 kDa pro-form and the mature enzyme also exert non-enzymatic functions that support tumor progression, including direct regulation of gene expression and modulation of autophagy [40]. Thus, HPSE represents a promising pharmacological target, and marine sulfated PS and their OS derivatives have been extensively studied as potential regulators because of their structural similarity to the enzyme’s natural substrate, HS. Among them, the λ-CAR and several OS derivatives have been pointed out as promising enzymatic inhibitors, with additional potential to modulate the HPSE metabolism including trafficking, maturation and expression [17, 41, 42].

In vitro enzymatic inhibition of HPSE

Using a FRET-based assay that monitors the degradation of labeled HS, λ-CAR was confirmed to bind to and inhibit HPSE enzymatic activity, with a measured IC50 value of 3.9 ± 0.7 µg·mL-1 (Fig. 3A). Consistent with previous findings showing that the inhibitory effect of λ-CAR-based species strongly depends on their molecular weight (chain length) and degree of sulfation, λ-COS exhibited reduced potency, with an IC50 value of 18 ± 5 µg·mL-1, yet still within a range previously reported as suitable for in vivo anticancer applications [22, 43]. Interestingly, the IC50 value of λ-COS NP (15 ± 3 µg·mL-1) was comparable to that of free λ-COS, reflecting that interaction with HPSE remains possible to λ-COS even when used as a scaffold for NP. Finally, the moderate decrease in anti-HPSE activity observed for λ-COS and λ-COS NP compared to λ-CAR should be balanced by the concomitant strong reduction of the acute pro-inflammatory behavior and anticoagulant effect of the native PS, which is undesirable in cancer treatment. Indeed, in a previous article, we previously demonstrated that depolymerization is key to alleviate these two adverse activities to provide a safe biocompatible coating suitable for systemic administration [19].

Effects of λ-COS NP on MDA-MB-231 TNBC cells migration

Encouraged by these results, we assessed the effects of λ-COS and λ-COS NP on the migration of MDA-MB-231 cells, a mechanism in which HPSE is one of several contributing factors, using a wound healing assay with an Oris™ cell migration plate. Batimastat, a matrix metalloprotease inhibitor, and suramin were included as standard positive controls for migration inhibition, acting through HPSE-unrelated and partially HPSE-related inhibition mechanisms, respectively [44–46]. Heparin was also included as the golden sulfated sugar-based standard for HPSE inhibition [47, 48].

After stopper removal, treatments were performed at a dose of 100 µg·mL-1 in sugar content for all candidates in a 2% FBS-supplemented medium to limit any biases from cell proliferation, and migration in the detection zone was monitored over 24 h (Fig. 3B and Figure S2). The results showed that both Batimastat and suramin significantly decreased cancer cell migration compared to the untreated control, with both reaching 65% inhibition at 24 h. However, their kinetic behaviors differed, with a 6 h delayed effect in the case of suramin. The strong effect obtained with suramin is in favor of an HPSE-dependent mechanism; however, it should be noted that this antimigratory drug has a broad range of mechanisms. For instance, it can also block the purinergic cell surface receptors P2Y2 and P2 × 7, which are upregulated in breast cancer cells and implicated in migration and invasion promotion [49, 50].

Regarding the candidates, λ-CAR and λ-COS exhibited promising inhibitory effects of 50 and 65%, respectively, against MDA-MB-231 cell migration after 24 h. Both compounds had a rapid onset similar to that of Batimastat. Interestingly, the depolymerization process, which is required to improve bio-acceptability, did not alter this beneficial effect. These results are consistent with previously published results obtained with other OS derived from λ-CAR [17, 22]. λ-COS NP revealed similar behavior to that of free λ-COS but with more moderate inhibition levels in the 30–50% range. This difference might be due to the reduced conformational flexibility of the sugar chains when used as a scaffold in the NP, which could limit their biointeractions that trigger migration-related pathways. This could also result from differences in extracellular/intracellular distribution, which are influenced by the ligand-packing effect on cell internalization. Nonetheless, the antimigratory activity of λ-COS NP remains promising.

Interestingly, λ-COS, and to a lesser extent λ-COS NP, exhibited stronger overall anti-migratory activity than heparin, despite the latter’s much higher inhibitory potency against HPSE enzymatic activity (IC50 of 0.47 µg·mL-1 in sugar content) [22]. In a previous study, we proposed that heparin may preferentially interact with other biological targets within complex cell culture media, unrelated to the migration of cancer cells. It was also suggested that λ-COS could engage in more specific inhibitory interactions with HPSE, while additionally modulating its proper metabolism, including expression, maturation and trafficking [51].

Effects of λ-COS NP on HPSE/HS axis in MDA-MB-231 cells

To assess whether HPSE regulation contributed to the anti-migratory effects observed for λ-COS and λ-COS NP, several experiments were performed. First, levels of the latent HPSE (65 kDa pro-form) and catalytically active HPSE (50/8-kDa heterodimeric configuration) were estimated by western blotting in both the culture supernatants and cell lysates (Fig. 3C-E), the latter reflecting intracellular HPSE together with any pericellular enzyme remaining associated with the cell membranes, also catch in lysates. To complement the results, active mature HPSE was also estimated by ELISA assay (Fig. 3F).

In the untreated supernatants, only the pro-form was detected on the blot as illustrated in Fig. 3C, consistent with the HPSE trafficking described in the literature, which reports that cells predominantly secrete the latent pro-enzyme, whereas the mature active form is generated within endo-lysosomal compartments and acts primarily in the pericellular and intracellular spaces (catch in cell lysates) rather than being released as a soluble enzyme in the supernatant. Coherently, ELISA detected only low concentrations of active enzymes in the supernatants (Fig. 3F), probably under the threshold values of western blot sensibility. Significant differences between the control and OGT (a small-molecule reference inhibitor of HPSE) / λ-CAR groups were observed, while the level remained the same for free λ-CO and NP.

The results on cell lysates were more informative, detecting the presence of both the endogenous pool of latent HPSE and catalytically active HPSE in the blots. The OGT molecule again significantly altered the levels of both HPSE forms, suggesting that it may not act solely through direct enzymatic inhibition but also as a modulator of HPSE expression. Concerning native λ-CAR, treatment decreased both pro- and mature HPSE, further confirmed by ELISA assay in case of mature HPSE, confirming a clear pattern of down-regulation activity of the target. About the sugar-based candidates, λ-COS also decreased both pro- and mature HPSE levels (Fig. 3C-E), although less markedly than λ-CAR, while λ-COS NP appeared to affect only active HPSE expression, though not significantly in case of ELISA quantification (Fig. 3F). Such modulation of HPSE metabolism, in addition to potential enzymatic inhibition, has been previously described in the literature [51].

In the second step, HS was quantified in the culture supernatants and cell lysates of MDA-MB-231 cells using ELISA (Fig. 3G). HS levels provide a partial readout of HPSE catalytic activity, which may be modulated either by direct inhibition of the mature form by candidates and/or by alterations in HPSE expression, both leading to an increased HS detection. In supernatants, HS detected in this assay likely originated from soluble shed-HSPGs, referred to here as HS(s). Soluble sole HS fragments generated by HPSE cleavage may also be present but are largely not detected as the epitope is probably lost when the HS chains become too short. In cell lysates, detected HS arises from all endogenous pools, including endo-lysosomal HSPGs as well as cytoplasmic and nuclear HS, referred to here as HS(e). As a first example, 24 h-OGT treatment induced a small increase in HS(s) levels (Fig. 3G), likely reflecting better preservation of HS chains due to direct inhibition of HPSE enzymatic activity or downregulation of the active form. Surprisingly no significant change in HS(e) levels was observed, despite the reduced expression of the mature HPSE form highlighted above. In contrast, for λ-COS and λ-COS NP, HS(s) levels were similar or even lower to those of the untreated control, whereas a trend toward higher HS(e) levels was observed. However, as they also modulate active HPSE expression in lysates, at this stage it is difficult to decipher the input of a direct HPSE inhibition.

To further confirm and explore the potential effects of the candidates on the HPSE/HS axis in MDA-MB-231 cells, immunofluorescence analysis was performed as a final step, using a better-characterized HS-specific scFv antibody (HS4C3) detecting enriched 6 − 0 sulfated GlcNS motif (Fig. 3H). Consistent with the western blot and/or ELISA data, treatment with sugar-based compounds led to a more diffuse staining of endogenous HPSE (green) compared to untreated cells, for which punctate cytoplasmic staining was also occasionally observed, reflecting a decrease of expression. Further, HS staining (red) was detected in the cytoplasm of untreated MDA-MB-231 cells and showed apparent co-localization with the nucleus (although the use of wide-field imaging prevents a definitive conclusion). In the case of λ-COS and λ-COS NP treatment, the HS red staining was clearly more intense, suggesting better preservation of HS chains thanks to the activity of the candidates. However, surprisingly, it was present almost exclusively in regions that co-localized with the blue nuclear signal.

Interactions of λ-COS NP with macrophages

When NP extravasate into the tumor stroma, they encounter a complex microenvironment where they undergo multiple interactions with biomacromolecules and various cell actors before reaching cancer cells, especially when they are internalized by tumor-associated macrophages (TAM). This aspect, often overlooked in nano-oncology approaches, might explain the limited efficiency of NP-targeting cancer cells observed in some cases, but could also be advantageously exploited in pharmacological strategies aimed at reprogramming TAM [52, 53].

MΦ are key players in the immune response and can adopt a broad spectrum of metabolic states ruled by the differential expression of multiple cellular messengers and cell surface receptors, depending on the stage of the inflammatory condition or pathological context. In advanced cancers, TAM predominantly exhibit an M2-like phenotype, contributing to tumor progression, especially through the production of signaling molecules that promote immune suppression, cancer cell proliferation and invasion, tissue remodeling, and angiogenesis. Selectively reprogramming M2-like pro-tumoral MΦ toward an M1-like tumor-suppressor phenotype is now an asserted cancer therapeutic strategy, leading to a significant immuno-restorative effect and decreased tumor growth [54]. For this strategy, iron oxide or ferrite NP [55–57], free Mn2+ ions [58], and λ-CAR [20] appear as promising suitable polarizing agents.

Our approach involved evaluating the effects of λ-COS NP, along with free λ-COS and λ-CAR for comparison, on immortalized murine MΦ (RAW 264.7) in their basal state (M0), as well as on representative polarized models of M1- and M2-like phenotypes induced by exposure to LPS/IFN-γ or IL-4, respectively. Since the primary goal was to reprogram MΦ without causing immune cell toxicity, MTT assays and NP uptake studies were first conducted to confirm the safe interaction of NP with MΦ across all phenotypes (Fig. 4). As expected, none of the tested compounds reduced the viability of murine MΦ after 24 h of treatment, regardless of their phenotypes (Fig. 4A).

Interestingly, a substantial uptake of λ-COS NP by RAW 264.7 cells in the M0 and M2-like phenotypes was qualitatively observed at 24 h using bright-field microscopy after fixation and Prussian blue staining (Fig. 4B). Microscopic images revealed the presence of λ-COS NP within the cells, as indicated by blue spots corresponding to iron staining.

Beyond the possible therapeutic outcome, this strong internalization of λ-COS NP in MΦ could also have an influence on imaging applications [59]. At the diagnostic level, when ferrite NP are recognized and taken up by MΦ, they should enhance the magnetic resonance (MR) signal on T2-weighted images due to aggregation of the ferrite cores, which markedly increases the T2* effect. Additionally, for λ-COS NP, the release of complex Mn2+ following lysosomal digestion of the NPs may further alter the T1-weighted MR signals. Overall, the specific signal intensity signature detected confirmed NP accumulation specific to each patient, supporting personalized therapeutic approaches. Moreover, it may be correlated with TAM quantification, offering a non-invasive approach to assist in treatment-related decision-making [60].

Reprogramming effects of macrophages by λ-COS NP

Encouraged by the strong NP uptake observed, we assessed the possible reprogramming of MΦ by examining three representative markers of the M1-like phenotype: nitric oxide (NO) as a strong metabolic effector associated with the M1-phenotype [61], TNF-α as a central signaling molecule, and IL-6, a cytokine found in both M1 and M2 with a more complex regulatory role [62]. They were quantified in the supernatant of the differently polarized MΦ cultures after 24 h of treatment with the candidates at a concentration in λ-CAR or λ-COS of 100 µg·mL-1, sugar content (Fig. 5).

The response obtained in M0 MΦ confirmed a previous study showing that λ-CAR triggers an acute pro-inflammatory response that can be deleterious for use in systemic administration, as illustrated by a marked increase in TNF-α expression and moderate elevation of IL-6 and NO. In contrast, λ-COS and λ-COS NP elicited a much more moderate response suitable for safe use in vivo, with levels closer to those of untreated control cells, especially for the metabolic effectors, NO and IL-6. Analysis of the untreated M2- and M1-like MΦ controls further confirmed that the expression of these three markers was downregulated in the tumor-supportive phenotype (M2), while being strongly secreted into the supernatant of the tumor-suppressor phenotype (M1).

Promisingly, when MΦ were polarized into the M2 phenotype, the configuration that best mimics the state of TAM in the TME, significant increases in NO and TNF-α levels were measured in the case of λ-COS NP treatment compared to the untreated control, reaching values close to those observed in the M1-like model (Fig. 5A and B). Concerning IL-6, no reprogramming effect by λ-COS NP was observed; however, this cytokine is known to have a more complex regulatory role and often intervenes at a late stage of inflammation (Fig. 5C) [63]. Overall, these result suggest the potential specific reprogramming of the M2-deactivated MΦ into M1-tumor suppressor phenotype by the λ-COS NP. In addition, values obtained from M1-like polarized MΦ showed that the NP tempered marker expression instead of inducing cumulative effects, thus fulfilling the desired balance of activity sought in such immunomodulation approaches [54, 64].

This promising bioactivity seems to originate from the ferrite cores or Mn2+ contained in the NP because the λ-COS coating alone did not induce NO or TNF-α overexpression in the M2 cell culture supernatant. Nevertheless, the surface coating of ferrite NP remains crucial because of their role in the interaction with MΦ membrane-associated proteins, to trigger the uptake of the NP, before that internalized cores can efficiently modulate the different immunomodulatory pathways [65].

Taken together, these results indicated that the λ-COS NP may produce a promising antitumoral activity by reprogramming o TAM towards M1-like tumor suppressor phenotype, which is associated with notable tumor growth retardation and enhance the overall antitumoral activity of the λ-COS NP [55, 66].

Targeting antitumor effect in vivo

Inspired by these results and to complete this evaluation, the tumoral retention and tumor growth inhibition by λ-COS NP was evaluated in a xenograft (MDA-MB-231) mouse model. The experimental protocol is illustrated in Fig. 6A. Briefly, cells embedded in a Matrigel were inoculated in the mammary gland of 6-weeks nude mice separated in two groups (n = 9) and tumors were allowed to grow for 7 days. Next, a first intravenous administration of λ-COS NP doses (100 µL at 1 mg·mL-1 of Fe) was performed for the treated group before MRI-based biodistribution experiments recorded up to 24 h. Other doses were administered on day 15 and day 21, followed by the sacrifice of the mice one week later for further histology and ex vivo experiments.

Beginning with MRI analysis, a 7 Tesla scanner was used, and images were acquired using a multi slice spin echo sequence to generate T2 relaxometric maps before injection (basal), and then at 30 min, 4 h and 24 h after dose administration (Fig. 6B and Figure S3). Mean T2-values from selected regions of interest (ROI) in main organs of interest and in tumor were calculated from these maps by pooling together all pre- and post-contrast datasets from 4 mice and are displayed in Fig. 6C. The images and ROI quantification showed a strong signal reduction in the liver and kidneys 30 min after injection. The liver T2 decrease persisted up to 24 h, originating from progressive NP elimination in this organ, as confirmed by microscopy of tissue sections stained by Prussian blue (Figure S4). Whereas the kidney’s signal, corresponding to the transiently circulating NP fraction, remained at 4 h but returned to the baseline value at 24 h. Overall, this behavior is consistent with the main biodistribution pattern previously observed in healthy mice [19]. Notably, a modest significant T2 decrease was also observed in the xenograft, suggesting the retention of a NP fraction within the tumor. Iron oxide cores were also detected by microscopy in tumor tissue, confirming that a fraction of NP reached the tumor to possibly exert a therapeutic activity (Fig. 6D and Figure S5). Yet, this result is especially encouraging given the intravenous route chosen for administration, which is normally typically associated with rapid NP clearance by the reticuloendothelial system (RES) [67].

Indeed, as shown in Fig. 6E, the treated group exhibited a much flatter tumor growth curve compared to the control group, with average volume remaining below 200 mm3 at the end of experiment (day 29), versus 400 mm3 for untreated mice. Interestingly, this result was obtained with only 3 administrations and tumor growth inhibition rapidly reached 40% after 1st injection and was subsequently maintained (Fig. 6F). Also, Fe content found in tumors at the end of the protocol from the NP-treated group was quantified by ICP–MS (n = 4) and expressed as ng of Fe per mg of dry tumor mass (Fig. 6G). Intratumoral Fe levels appeared to mildly correlate with higher tumor growth inhibition, suggesting that enhanced a NP accumulation may be associated with the antitumoral activity of λ-COS NP.

The body mass gains of treated mice were slightly higher throughout the protocol, as shown in Fig. 6H and hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) staining showed no obvious histological changes in major organs (Figure S6), suggesting no significant toxicity of the NP formulation as a first basic indication.

To further assess the in vivo toxicity of the λ-COS NP, several toxicological endpoints were measured in a control group (4 mice) and in an NP-treated group (4 mice), 48 h following a single administration (Figure S7). Serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were measured as representative markers of hepatocellular and tissue injury, since these intracellular enzymes are released into the bloodstream following cell damage, particularly of hepatocytes (Figure S7A and S7B). No significant differences in ALT and AST concentrations were observed between the control and NP-treated groups. These results suggest that the λ-COS NP did not appear to induce detectable liver injury in treated mice despite their substantial liver accumulation, particularly within Kupffer cells. Urinary albumin (ALB) and total protein (TP) levels were next quantified, as renal dysfunction can result in abnormal urinary protein excretion (Figure S7C and S7D). Similarly to ALT and AST, the concentrations of these two markers were comparable between the control and NP-treated groups, with no statistically significant differences, indicating that kidney function seems to remain unaffected. Taken together, these data provide further evidence of the favorable systemic safety profile of λ-COS NP following systemic administration and are consistent with the histopathological analysis (Figure S6), in which H&E staining revealed no difference in the integrity of the liver and kidney tissues for both groups.

As an initial readout to investigate the mechanisms underlying the therapeutic efficiency of the λ-COS NP and to link the in vivo effects with the bioactivities described above, the expression and state of macrophage phenotype between treated and control mice was evaluated by IHC analysis on whole tumor tissue section (Fig. 7A). iNOs and CD206 staining were selected as representative markers of M1-like tumor-suppressor and M2-like pro-tumoral MΦ, respectively. Treated mice displayed higher levels of iNOS and CD206 staining, supported by, respectively, a significant three-fold and two-fold increase in their average chromogen 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB)-positive area (Fig. 7B). This result suggests enhanced TAM recruitment and infiltration with a hybrid activation profile, and possibly some degree of TAM reprogramming. This was supported by the analysis of the iNOS/CD206–positive area ratio, which was increased in mice treated with λ-COS NP compared to the control group, indicating a more pro-inflammatory, anti-tumor macrophage milieu (more M1-like vs. M2-like TAM), a state often associated with improved tumor control [68–70]. Several studies have demonstrated that COS can not only modulate MΦ activity, but also exhibit broader immunoenhancement properties, including NK cell activity, lymphocyte proliferation or effects on primary immune organs such as spleen and thymus [18, 71–73]. In a second readout, the spleen index (weight of the dry spleen in mg normalized by the body weight of the corresponding animal in g) was therefore calculated (Fig. 7C) [18, 72]. It revealed a significant increase in the NP-treated group compared to the control group by 1.4-fold. This elevated ratio may indicate enhanced immune cells infiltration and/or accumulation in response to the treatment, consistent with a potential immunomodulating effect of the λ-COS NP.

Conclusions

As an alternative to the limitations of anti-proliferative NP that target cancer cells, recent approaches have focused on nanotherapeutics that act on more accessible targets within the tumoral microenvironment (TME). In a previous study, λ-COS was proposed as a novel NP-based scaffold that serves as both a coating to encapsulate ferrite cores and as a macromolecular chelator for Mn2+ ions. Since λ-COS, ferrite cores, and Mn2+ have each been independently reported to exert unique activities on various TME targets, we sought to further investigate the intrinsic antitumor properties of this λ-COS NP formulation.

As expected, λ-COS NP did not affect cell viability or induce apoptosis in MDA-MB-231 cells. However, they seem to inhibit HPSE enzymatic activity in vitro, a key factor in ECM remodeling within the TME, known to be involved in multiple protumor pathways, such as migration. λ-COS NP effectively demonstrated a significant effect against MDA-MB-231 cancer cell migration, in part possibly due to HPSE regulation. However, the highly anionic nature of λ-COS may disrupt ECM organization or interfere with cell–matrix interactions, as well as modulate pro-migratory signaling in many other ways. Therefore, further investigation is needed to deepen the underlying mechanisms, for instance, studying potential interference with glycoprotein receptors (such as selectins or integrins), or with signaling cascades involved in cytoskeleton dynamics.

We next demonstrated that λ-COS NP were internalized by RAW 264.7 MΦ, without affecting cell viability. This behavior may be advantageous for both TAM-targeted diagnostic applications and immunomodulatory therapeutic strategies. Indeed, in MΦ polarized towards a pro-tumoral M2-like phenotype, NP treatment successfully restored the expression of two key tumor-suppressive factors, TNF-α and NO, adding a valuable immunotherapeutic dimension to their antitumoral profile. The absence of these effects following treatment with λ-COS alone suggests that the observed immunomodulatory activity observed with NP is likely due to the effects induced by the ferrite/Mn2+ payload. This promising result should be deepened, for instance, by expanding the analysis to other representative cytokine messengers (IL-10, TGF-β, IFN-γ) or MΦ cell-surface markers (such as CD86 M1 markers and CD163, CD204/206 M2 markers) to confirm the potential of these NP as specific strong immunomodulatory agents.
