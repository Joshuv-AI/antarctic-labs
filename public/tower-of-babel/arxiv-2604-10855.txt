Sample Average Approximation for Distributionally Robust Optimization with ϕ \phi -divergences
Yan Li

Abstract
It is well known that estimating the expectation of any given bounded random variable with values in [ − B , B ] [-B,B] has a sample complexity of 𝒪 ⁡ ( B 2 / ϵ 2 ) \mathcal{O}(B^{2}/\epsilon^{2}) that is independent of the underlying probability measure. We show that this property can no longer hold when evaluating the worst-case expectation of the random variable, where the probability measures defining the expectation belong to a ϕ \phi -divergence ball centered at some nominal measure P P . Specifically, the sample complexity and its dependence on the nominal measure can be completely characterized by the growth of the divergence function. When the divergence function ϕ \phi exhibits superlinear growth, a P P -independent sample complexity can be obtained for sample average approximation, which depends only on the growth of ϕ \phi , the radius of the divergence ball, and the target precision. We also provide sample complexity lower bounds and demonstrate the optimality of the obtained bounds for commonly used ϕ \phi -divergences. On the other hand, when superlinear growth does not hold for ϕ \phi , we show that for any estimation method, evaluating the worst-case expectation has a P P -dependent sample complexity lower bound that can be made arbitrarily large by changing P P .



1
Introduction

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
be a measurable space and
P
be a probability measure on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
.
Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
be a convex lower semicontinuous function with
\phi(1)=0
and
\phi(x)=+\infty
for
x<0
.
For
\tau\geq 0
, consider

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{\tau}(P)=\left\{\zeta\in\mathfrak{D}:\int_{\Omega}\phi(\zeta(w))dP(w)\leq\tau\right\},

where
\mathfrak{D}
denotes the set of probability density functions with respect to
P
.
Let
X:\Omega\to[-B,B]
be
\mathcal{F}
-measurable for some
B\geq 0
.
The distributionally robust functional corresponding to
\phi
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(X)=\sup_{\zeta\in\mathfrak{D}}\int_{\Omega}X(\omega)\zeta(\omega)dP(\omega),~\mathrm{s.t.}~\zeta\in\mathfrak{M}_{\tau}(P).

(1.1)

Going forward, we refer to
\phi
as the divergence function associated with
\mathcal{R}(X)
.

Consider the following sample average approximation (SAA) of (
1.1
).
That is, for any
n>0
, let
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}
denote the samples drawn independently from
P
, and let
P_{n}
be the corresponding empirical distribution.
Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)=\sup_{\zeta\in\mathfrak{D}}\int_{\Omega}X(\omega)\zeta(\omega)dP_{n}(\omega),~\mathrm{s.t.}~\zeta\in\mathfrak{M}_{\tau}(P_{n}).

(1.2)

We are interested in the number of samples
n
needed for (
1.2
) to be an accurate approximation of (
1.1
).

Clearly, when
\tau=0
,
\mathcal{R}(X)
reduces to the expectation, and from Hoeffding’s inequality one can take no more than
\mathcal{O}(B^{2}/\epsilon^{2})
samples to estimate
\mathcal{R}(X)
via
\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)
up to an
\epsilon
-accuracy.
The situation seems to become unclear for
\tau>0
.
Sample complexity bounds have been obtained for various concrete
\phi
-divergences (e.g., total variation, Kullback-Leibler,
\chi^{2}
-distance, conditional value-at-risk) in the context of distributionally robust optimization
[
3
,
4
]
or Markov decision processes (MDPs)
[
9
,
7
,
8
]
.
It is worth mentioning here that in the context of MDPs, the obtained sample complexity bounds typically depend on the nominal measure
P
via its minimal probability, and hence are intrinsically restricted to finite sample spaces.
On the other hand, for a class of Cressie-Read divergence functions,
[
3
]
obtains the sample complexity lower and upper bounds independent of nominal measure
P
.

Our problem of interest is to provide a relatively complete characterization for the sample complexity when estimating
\mathcal{R}(X)
with general divergence functions.
The essential observation in this manuscript is that the sample complexity and its dependence on the nominal measure can be completely characterized by the growth of the divergence function.

Definition 1.1
.

We say that
\phi
has sublinear growth if
\lim_{x\to\infty}\phi(x)/x<\infty
,
and superlinear growth if
\lim_{x\to\infty}\phi(x)/x=\infty
.

Indeed, if
\phi
grows superlinearly at infinity, we establish a
P
-independent sample complexity bound for the sample average approximation
\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)
that only depends on the growth of
\phi
and the radius
\tau
.
On the other hand, if
\phi
grows sublinearly,
then one can construct a class of problems for which we can obtain an information-theoretic sample complexity lower bound that depends on
P
, which can be further made arbitrarily large by changing the nominal measure
P
.

2
Sample Complexity for Sublinear Growth

Throughout our discussion in this section, we make the following assumption on the divergence function
\phi
.

Assumption 2.1
.

There exists
\delta>0
such that
[1-\delta,1+\delta]\subseteq\mathrm{int}(\mathrm{dom}(\phi))
.

2.1
Sample Complexity Lower Bound

We begin with the following lower bound on
\mathcal{R}(X)
for a family of reference measures
P
supported over
\Omega
with two elements.

Lemma 2.1
.

Consider
\Omega=\left\{\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\right\}
,
\mathcal{F}=2^{\Omega}
,
X(\omega_{1})=1
and
X(\omega_{2})=0
.
Suppose
\lim_{x\to\infty}{\phi(x)}/{x}<\infty
, then for any
\tau>0
, there exist
r_{\phi,\tau},p_{\phi,\tau}>0
such that for any
P
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
with
0<P(\omega_{1})\leq p_{\phi,\tau}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(X)\geq r_{\phi,\tau}.

Here
r_{\phi,\tau}
and
p_{\phi,\tau}
are constants depending only on the divergence function
\phi
and radius
\tau
.

Proof.

Let
p_{i}=P(\omega_{i})
and
\zeta_{i}=\zeta(\omega_{i})
for
i\in\left\{1,2\right\}
.
Recall that the constraint
\zeta\in\mathfrak{M}_{\tau}(P)
requires

\displaystyle\phi(\zeta_{1})p_{1}+\phi(\zeta_{2})p_{2}\leq\tau;~\zeta_{1}p_{1}+\zeta_{2}p_{2}=1,~\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\geq 0.

(2.1)

Since
\lim_{x\to\infty}\phi(x)/x<\infty
,
there exist
L_{\phi},G_{\phi}\geq 1
such that
\phi(x)/x\leq G_{\phi}
for
x\geq L_{\phi}
.
In addition, given Assumption
2.1
,
\phi(1)=0
and the fact that
\phi
is convex, for any
\tau>0
, there exists

r_{\phi,\tau}>0
such that

\sup_{x\in[1-r_{\phi,\tau},1+r_{\phi,\tau}]}\phi(x)\leq\tfrac{\tau}{2}.

Note that without loss of generality we can assume

r_{\phi,\tau}\leq\min\left\{{\tau}/{2G_{\phi}},1\right\}
.

Our goal is to show the existence of
p_{\phi,\tau}>0
such that for any probability measure
P
with
p_{1}\in(0,p_{\phi,\tau}]
, one can construct a corresponding
\zeta\in\mathfrak{M}_{\tau}(P)
with

\mathbb{E}_{P}[\zeta X]
(and hence
\mathcal{R}(X)
) bounded away from
0
.

Let
k\geq 1
be some constant to be determined later.
Denote
p_{\phi,\tau}=\tfrac{\tau}{kG_{\phi}L_{\phi}}
, and let
\zeta_{1}=\tfrac{\tau}{kG_{\phi}p_{1}}
.
Note that for any
p_{1}\in(0,p_{\phi,\tau}]
, we have

\zeta_{1}\geq L_{\phi},

and hence

\phi(\zeta_{1})p_{1}=\phi\left(\tfrac{\tau}{kG_{\phi}p_{1}}\right)\cdot\tfrac{kG_{\phi}p_{1}}{\tau}\cdot\tfrac{\tau}{kG_{\phi}}\leq\tfrac{\tau}{k},

where the last inequality follows from the definition of
G_{\phi}
and
L_{\phi}
.
Given this observation and the choice of
\zeta_{1}
, to satisfy (
2.1
), it suffices to have

\displaystyle\tfrac{\tau}{kG_{\phi}}\leq 1,~\sup_{p_{2}\in[1-p_{\phi,\tau},1]}\phi\left(\tfrac{1-{\tau}/\left(kG_{\phi}\right)}{p_{2}}\right)p_{2}\leq\tfrac{(k-1)\tau}{k}.

By choosing
k=\tfrac{\tau}{G_{\phi}r_{\phi,\tau}}
, given the definition of
r_{\phi,\tau}
, we have
k\geq 2
and
\tfrac{\tau}{kG_{\phi}}=r_{\phi,\tau}\leq 1
.
Since
\phi
is convex, to satisfy the above condition, it suffices to have

\displaystyle\max\left\{\phi\left(\tfrac{1-r_{\phi,\tau}}{1-p_{\phi,\tau}}\right),\phi\left(1-r_{\phi,\tau}\right)\right\}\leq\tfrac{\tau}{2}.

Note that the choice of
k
implies
p_{\phi,\tau}=\tfrac{r_{\phi,\tau}}{L_{\phi}}\leq r_{\phi,\tau}
, and hence the above condition is immediately satisfied by the definition of
r_{\phi,\tau}
.
In this case, we have

\displaystyle R(X)\geq\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\zeta X\right]=\zeta_{1}p_{1}=\tfrac{\tau}{kG_{\phi}p_{1}}\cdot p_{1}=\tfrac{\tau}{kG_{\phi}}=r_{\phi,\tau},

where the last inequality follows from the choice of
k
.
∎

With Lemma
2.1
, we proceed to establish the sample complexity lower bound for sample average approximation (
1.2
).

Theorem 2.1
.

Under the same setup as in Lemma
2.1
, let
\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)
be defined as in (
1.2
).
For any
p\in(0,p_{\phi,\tau})
, there exists
P
over
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
such that

\displaystyle\left\lvert{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(X)-\mathcal{R}(X)\right\rvert\geq{r_{\phi,\tau}},

with probability at least
1/2
,
if
n\leq 1/(2p)
.

Proof.

Let us adopt the same notation as in Lemma
2.1
.
For any
p\in(0,p_{\phi,\tau})
, define
P
over
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
with

P(\omega_{1})=p\in(0,p_{\phi,\tau})
.
From Lemma
2.1
, we have
\mathcal{R}(X)\geq r_{\phi,\tau}
.
Given the definition of
X
, it is clear that
\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)=0
if
P_{n}(\omega_{1})=0
,
and consequently

\left\lvert{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(X)-\mathcal{R}(X)\right\rvert\geq{r_{\phi,\tau}}.

Note that

\displaystyle P^{\otimes n}\left(P_{n}(\omega_{1})=0\right)=(1-p)^{n}\geq 1-pn\geq 1/2,

where the last inequality follows from
n\leq 1/(2p)
.
∎

We can indeed strengthen the lower bound for SAA to the following information-theoretic lower bound for estimating
\mathcal{R}(X)
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Under the same setup as in Lemma
2.1
, for any
p\in(0,p_{\phi,\tau})
, and any estimator
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(X)
that is
\mathcal{F}^{\otimes n}
-measurable,
there exists
P
over
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
such that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(X)-\mathcal{R}(X)\right\rvert\geq\tfrac{r_{\phi,\tau}}{2},

with probability at least
1/4
,
if
n\leq 1/(2p)
.

Proof.

Let us adopt the same notation as in Lemma
2.1
.
Define a pair of distributions
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
with

P_{0}(\omega_{1})=0
and
P_{1}(\omega_{1})=p\in(0,p_{\phi,\tau})
.
To facilitate our discussion, let us write
\mathcal{R}^{P}(X)
to indicate the dependence of
\mathcal{R}
on
P
.
Then from Lemma
2.1
and
\mathcal{R}^{P_{0}}(X)=0
, we have

\left\lvert\mathcal{R}^{P_{1}}(X)-\mathcal{R}^{P_{0}}(X)\right\rvert\geq r_{\phi,\tau}.

From Le Cam’s method, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{R}_{n}(X)}\max_{P\in\left\{P_{0},P_{1}\right\}}P^{\otimes n}\left(\left\lvert\widehat{R}_{n}(X)-R^{P}(X)\right\rvert\geq\tfrac{r_{\phi,\tau}}{2}\right)\geq\tfrac{1-\mathrm{TV}(P_{0}^{\otimes n},P_{1}^{\otimes n})}{2}=\tfrac{(1-p)^{n}}{2}\overset{(a)}{\geq}\tfrac{1-np}{2}\overset{(b)}{\geq}\tfrac{1}{4},

where
(a)
follows from Bernoulli’s inequality and
(b)
follows from
n\leq 1/(2p)
.
∎

It could be interesting to note that the above sample complexity lower bounds suggest that when the divergence function
\phi
exhibits sublinear growth, the necessary number of samples for estimating
\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)
depends on the nominal measure
P
and can be made arbitrarily large.
That is, for an arbitrary number of samples
n
, there always exists
P
over
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
such that the difference between true risk
\mathcal{R}(X)
and its estimate
\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)
is bounded below by constant
r_{\phi,\tau}
with probability at least
1/4
.
This should be contrasted with the risk-neutral case, where
\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[X(\omega)\right]
can be estimated up to
\epsilon
-accuracy with a sample complexity of
\mathcal{O}(B^{2}/\epsilon^{2})
that is independent of
P
.
On the other hand, when the underlying reference measure
P
is fixed a priori, we will discuss the approach to obtain an instance-dependent (i.e., depending on
P
and
X
) sample complexity upper bound in Section
3
.

3
Sample Complexity for Superlinear Growth

In this section, we turn our attention to
\phi
that exhibits superlinear growth, i.e.,
\lim_{x\to\infty}\phi(x)/x=+\infty
.
Let us define the corresponding growth function.

Definition 3.1
.

Suppose
\lim_{x\to\infty}\phi(x)/x=+\infty
.
The growth function associated with divergence
\phi
is

\displaystyle g_{\phi}(x)=\inf_{x^{\prime}\geq x}\tfrac{\phi(x^{\prime})}{x^{\prime}}

(3.1)

with
\mathrm{dom}(g_{\phi})=[D_{\phi},\infty)
for some
D_{\phi}\geq 1
.
Accordingly, we define its inverse function as

\displaystyle g_{\phi}^{-1}(y)=\inf\left\{x:x\geq D_{\phi},~g_{\phi}(x)\geq y\right\}.

3.1
Sample Complexity Lower Bound

Similar to Section
2
, we first present a sample complexity lower bound for estimating
\mathcal{R}(X)
.
To this end, we construct the following counterpart to Lemma
2.1
that establishes a lower bound of
\mathcal{R}(X)
for a set of nominal measures
P
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Consider
\Omega=\left\{\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\right\}
,
\mathcal{F}=2^{\Omega}
,
X(\omega_{1})=B
and
X(\omega_{2})=0
, where
B\geq 1
.
Suppose
\lim_{x\to\infty}{\phi(x)}/{x}=\infty
.
Then under Assumption
2.1
, there exists
\epsilon_{\phi,\tau}>0
, such that for any
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{\phi,\tau})
,
there exists
P
over
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(X)\geq\epsilon,~P(\omega_{1})=\tfrac{\epsilon/B}{g_{\phi}^{-1}(\tau B/(2\epsilon))}.

Proof.

Let us restrict our attention to the nominal measures
P
that have full support over
\Omega
,
and denote
p=P(\omega_{1})
.
From (
1.1
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(X)=\max\left\{Bq:~q\in[0,1],~p\phi\left(\tfrac{q}{p}\right)+(1-p)\phi\left(\tfrac{1-q}{1-p}\right)\leq\tau\right\}.

(3.2)

We proceed to show that there exists
\epsilon_{\phi,\tau}>0
, such that for any
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{\phi,\tau})
, one can construct the corresponding
P
for which
q=\epsilon/B
is feasible to (
3.2
).
Indeed if this is the case, then
\mathcal{R}(X)\geq Bq=\epsilon
.
To this end, it suffices to show the existence of
p(\epsilon)
such that

\displaystyle p(\epsilon)\phi\left(\tfrac{\epsilon/B}{p(\epsilon)}\right)\leq\tfrac{\tau}{2},~\phi\left(\tfrac{1-\epsilon/B}{1-p(\epsilon)}\right)\leq\tfrac{\tau}{2},~p(\epsilon)\in(0,1).

(3.3)

Consider taking
p(\epsilon)=\tfrac{\epsilon/B}{g_{\phi}^{-1}(\tau B/(2\epsilon))}
in the above.
Clearly if
p(\epsilon)\in(0,1)
, the first and third conditions of (
3.3
) are satisfied.
On the other hand, from Assumption
2.1
,
\phi(1)=0
and the fact that
\phi
is convex, for any
\tau>0
, there exists

1\geq r_{\phi,\tau}>0
such that

\sup_{x\in[1-r_{\phi,\tau},1+r_{\phi,\tau}]}\phi(x)\leq\tfrac{\tau}{2}.

Hence to satisfy the second condition in (
3.3
) it suffices to have

1-r_{\phi,\tau}\leq\tfrac{1-\epsilon/B}{1-p(\epsilon)}\leq 1+r_{\phi,\tau}
,
which in turn can be satisfied if
\epsilon\leq r_{\phi,\tau}
and
p(\epsilon)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
.
Note that
p(\epsilon)
is non-decreasing in
\epsilon
, consequently there exists
\epsilon_{\phi,\tau}>0
, such that for
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{\phi,\tau})
, all three conditions in (
3.3
) can be satisfied.
∎

With Lemma
3.1
in place, we can establish the following information-theoretic sample complexity lower bound when estimating
\mathcal{R}(X)
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Under the same setup as in Lemma
3.1
, for any
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{\phi,\tau})
, and any estimator
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(X)
that is
\mathcal{F}^{\otimes n}
-measurable,
there exists
P
over
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
such that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(X)-\mathcal{R}(X)\right\rvert\geq\tfrac{\epsilon}{2},

with probability at least
1/4
,
if
n\leq\tfrac{g_{\phi}^{-1}(\tau B/(2\epsilon))B}{2\epsilon}
.

Proof.

Let us adopt the same notation as in Lemma
3.1
.
Define a pair of distributions
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
with

P_{0}(\omega_{1})=0
and
P_{1}(\omega_{1})=\tfrac{\epsilon/B}{g_{\phi}^{-1}(\tau B/(2\epsilon))}
.
Let us write
\mathcal{R}^{P}(X)
to indicate the dependence of
\mathcal{R}
on
P
.
Then from Lemma
3.1
and
\mathcal{R}^{P_{0}}(X)=0
, we have

\left\lvert\mathcal{R}^{P_{1}}(X)-\mathcal{R}^{P_{0}}(X)\right\rvert\geq\epsilon.

From Le Cam’s method, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{R}_{n}(X)}\max_{P\in\left\{P_{0},P_{1}\right\}}P^{\otimes n}\left(\left\lvert\widehat{R}_{n}(X)-R^{P}(X)\right\rvert\geq\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}\right)\geq\tfrac{1-\mathrm{TV}(P_{0}^{\otimes n},P_{1}^{\otimes n})}{2}=\tfrac{(1-P_{1}(\omega_{1}))^{n}}{2}\overset{(a)}{\geq}\tfrac{1-nP_{1}(\omega_{1})}{2}\overset{(b)}{\geq}\tfrac{1}{4},

where
(a)
follows from Bernoulli’s inequality and
(b)
follows from

n\leq\tfrac{g_{\phi}^{-1}(\tau B/(2\epsilon))B}{2\epsilon}
.
∎

We proceed to show that the standard
\mathcal{O}(B^{2}/\epsilon^{2})
sample complexity bound in the risk-neutral case also carries over to estimating
\mathcal{R}(X)
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Under the same setup as in Lemma
3.1
, there exists
\overline{p}_{\phi,\tau}>0
that only depends on
\phi
and
\tau
, such that
for any estimator
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(X)
that is
\mathcal{F}^{\otimes n}
-measurable,
there exists
P
over
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
such that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(X)-\mathcal{R}(X)\right\rvert\geq\tfrac{\epsilon}{8},

with probability at least
1/4
,
for any
n\leq\tfrac{{\overline{p}_{\phi,\tau}}B^{2}}{8\epsilon^{2}}
and
\epsilon\in(0,\tfrac{B}{4})
.

Proof.

Let us adopt the following notation.
For any distribution

P
over
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
, we write
p=P(\omega_{1})
.
Conversely for any
p\in[0,1]
, we write
P
to indicate the distribution over
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
with
P(\omega_{1})=p
.
Let us also write
\mathcal{R}(p)
in short for
\mathcal{R}^{P}(X)
.
We first note that

\lim_{p\to 0}\mathcal{R}(p)=0.

Indeed, suppose this is not the case, then there exists
\left\{p_{n}\right\}
and
l>0
such that
\mathcal{R}(p_{n})\geq lB
.
Let
Q_{n}
be the corresponding
({l}/{2})
-worst-case distribution for
P_{n}
. Then we have
q_{n}=Q_{n}(\omega_{1})\geq l/2
, yet

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}q_{n}=\lim_{n\to 0}p_{n}\tfrac{\phi\left({q_{n}}/{p_{n}}\right)}{\phi\left({q_{n}}/{p_{n}}\right)p_{n}/q_{n}}\overset{(a)}{\leq}\tau\tfrac{q_{n}/p_{n}}{\phi\left({q_{n}}/{p_{n}}\right)}\overset{(b)}{=}0,

where
(a)
follows from feasibility of
Q_{n}
and
(b)
follows from
\lim_{x\to\infty}\phi(x)/x=\infty
. Hence we obtain a contradiction.
Consequently, there exists
0<\overline{p}_{\phi,\tau}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(p)\leq\tfrac{B}{2},~\forall p\leq\overline{p}_{\phi,\tau}.

(3.4)

For any
\epsilon\leq\tfrac{1}{4}
, let
P,P^{\prime}
be a pair of distributions with
p^{\prime}=(1-\epsilon)p+\epsilon\geq p
, where
p\leq\overline{p}_{\phi,\tau}
.
Clearly we have

\left\lvert p^{\prime}-p\right\rvert=p^{\prime}-p=\epsilon(1-p)\leq\epsilon
. We proceed to lower bound the difference
\left\lvert\mathcal{R}(p)-\mathcal{R}(p^{\prime})\right\rvert
.
Let
q=Q(\omega_{1})
, where
Q
is the
(\epsilon B/4)
-worst-case distribution for
P
.
Define
\overline{q}=(1-\epsilon)q+\epsilon
, then

\displaystyle p^{\prime}\phi\left(\tfrac{\overline{q}}{p^{\prime}}\right)+(1-p^{\prime})\phi\left(\tfrac{{1-\overline{q}}}{1-p^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=p^{\prime}\phi\left(\tfrac{(1-\epsilon)q+\epsilon}{(1-\epsilon)p+\epsilon}\right)+(1-p^{\prime})\phi\left(\tfrac{1-q}{1-p}\right)

\displaystyle=p^{\prime}\left[\phi\left(\tfrac{(1-\epsilon)p}{(1-\epsilon)p+\epsilon}\cdot\tfrac{q}{p}+\tfrac{\epsilon}{(1-\epsilon)p+\epsilon}\right)\right]+(1-p^{\prime})\phi\left(\tfrac{1-q}{1-p}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{\leq}p^{\prime}\cdot\tfrac{(1-\epsilon)p}{(1-\epsilon)p+\epsilon}\phi\left(\tfrac{q}{p}\right)+(1-p^{\prime})\phi\left(\tfrac{1-q}{1-p}\right)

\displaystyle\leq p\phi\left(\tfrac{q}{p}\right)+(1-p)\phi\left(\tfrac{1-q}{1-p}\right)\leq\tau,

where
(c)
follows from
\phi
being convex and
\phi(1)=0
.
The above observation then immediately implies

\mathcal{R}(p^{\prime})\geq\overline{q}B

and subsequently

\mathcal{R}(p^{\prime})-\mathcal{R}(p)\geq(\overline{q}-q-\tfrac{\epsilon}{4})B=[\epsilon(1-q)-\tfrac{\epsilon}{4}]B\geq\tfrac{\epsilon B}{4},

where the last inequality follows from (
3.4
) and that
p\leq\overline{p}_{\phi,\tau}
.
Hence by invoking Le Cam’s method,
we obtain

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{R}_{n}(X)}\max_{\widetilde{P}\in\left\{P,P^{\prime}\right\}}\widetilde{P}^{\otimes n}\left(\left\lvert\widehat{R}_{n}(X)-R^{\widetilde{P}}(X)\right\rvert\geq\tfrac{\epsilon B}{8}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\tfrac{1-\mathrm{TV}(P^{\otimes n},(P^{\prime})^{\otimes n})}{2}\overset{(d)}{\geq}\tfrac{1-\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}(P^{\otimes n}\|(P^{\prime})^{\otimes n})/2}}{2}=\tfrac{1-\sqrt{n\mathrm{KL}(P\|P^{\prime})/2}}{2}\overset{(e)}{\geq}\tfrac{1-\sqrt{n\mathrm{\chi}^{2}(P,P^{\prime})/2}}{2}\overset{(f)}{\geq}\tfrac{1-\sqrt{2n\epsilon^{2}/p}}{2},

where
(d)
follows from Pinsker’s inequality,
(e)
follows from the fact that

\mathrm{KL}(P,P^{\prime})\leq\chi^{2}(P,P^{\prime})
,
and
(f)
follows from

\chi^{2}(P,P^{\prime})=\tfrac{(p-p^{\prime})^{2}}{p^{\prime}(1-p^{\prime})}\leq\tfrac{4\epsilon^{2}}{p}
given that
p\leq p^{\prime}=(1-\epsilon)p+\epsilon\leq\tfrac{3}{4}
.
The desired claim then follows from taking
p=\overline{p}_{\phi,\tau}
.
∎

In view of Theorem
3.1
and
3.2
, the necessary number of samples for obtaining an
\epsilon
-accurate estimate of
\mathcal{R}(X)
is lower bounded by

\displaystyle\Omega\left(\max\left\{\tfrac{g_{\phi}^{-1}(\tau B/(2\epsilon))B}{2\epsilon},\tfrac{B^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right\}\right).

(3.5)

We will later revisit this lower bound when we discuss the sample complexity for commonly used
\phi
-divergences in Section
4
.

3.2
Sample Complexity Upper Bound

We proceed to establish the sample complexity upper bound for estimating
\mathcal{R}(X)
that is independent of the nominal measure
P
.
Our basic idea is to replace in (
1.1
) the divergence function
\phi
by its truncated variant
\phi_{L}
, where
\phi_{L}(\cdot)=\phi(\cdot)+\mathbbm{1}_{[0,L]}(\cdot)

and
\mathbbm{1}_{[0,L]}(\cdot)
denotes the set indicator function of
[0,L]
.
Note that
\phi_{L}
is also a valid divergence function whenever
L\geq 1
.
We first prepare a few technical observations that will prove to be useful in our ensuing discussions.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
\lim_{x\to\infty}\phi(x)/x=+\infty
.
Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{L}(X)=\sup_{\zeta\in\mathfrak{D}}\int_{\Omega}X(\omega)\zeta(\omega)dP(\omega),~\mathrm{s.t.}~\int_{\Omega}\phi_{L}(\zeta(w))dP(w)\leq\tau.

(3.6)

Then for any
\epsilon>0
, let
L\geq g_{\phi}^{-1}(\tfrac{2B\tau}{\epsilon})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(X)-\epsilon\leq\mathcal{R}_{L}(X)\leq\mathcal{R}(X).

Proof.

For any
\zeta
feasible to (
1.1
), from
[
2
, Lemma A.1]
, there exists
\widetilde{\zeta}
feasible to (
3.6
), such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}[{\widetilde{\zeta}X}]\geq\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\zeta X\right]-B\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\zeta-L\right]_{+}.

(3.7)

Let
g_{\phi}
be the growth function defined as in (
3.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\zeta-L\right]_{+}\leq\int_{\zeta\geq L}\zeta(\omega)dP(\omega)=\int_{\zeta\geq L}\tfrac{\phi(\zeta(\omega))}{\phi(\zeta(\omega))/\zeta(\omega)}dP(\omega)\overset{(a)}{\leq}\int_{\zeta\geq L}\tfrac{\phi(\zeta)}{g_{\phi}(L)}dP(\omega)\overset{(b)}{\leq}\tfrac{\tau}{g_{\phi}(L)},

(3.8)

where
(a)
follows from the definition of
g_{\phi}
and
L\geq D_{\phi}
, and
(b)
follows from the fact that
\zeta
is feasible to (
1.1
).
Now let
\zeta_{\epsilon}
be an
(\epsilon/2)
-optimal solution to (
1.1
), from (
3.7
) and (
3.8
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{L}(X)\geq\mathbb{E}_{P}[{\widetilde{\zeta}X}]\geq\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\zeta_{\epsilon}X\right]-B\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\zeta_{\epsilon}-L\right]_{+}\geq\mathcal{R}(X)-\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}-\tfrac{B\tau}{g_{\phi}(L)},

(3.9)

from which the proof is completed.
∎

With Lemma
3.2
in place, we will study the sample complexity for estimating
\mathcal{R}_{L}(X)
via sample average approximation.
To facilitate our discussion, let
\mathcal{R}_{n,L}
be defined as in (
1.2
), with
\phi
replaced by
\phi_{L}
.
Let us recall the following representation for the dual problem to (
1.1
).

Lemma 3.3

(
[
6
]
)
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(X)=\inf_{\lambda>0,\mu\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{f(\lambda,\mu)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\lambda\tau+\mu+(\lambda\phi)^{*}(X-\mu)\right]\right\}.

(3.10)

In addition, there exist
\lambda^{*}\geq 0,\mu^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
attaining the infimum above.

From Lemma
3.3
, we can immediately obtain the following observation.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any divergence function
\phi
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P^{\otimes n}}\left[\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)\right]\leq\mathcal{R}(X),

where
\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)
is defined as in (
1.2
).

We then proceed to establish that the minimization problem in (
3.10
) indeed can be restricted to a bounded set, for which one can control the Lipschitz continuity of
f(\lambda,\mu)
.

Lemma 3.5
.

For any divergence function
\phi
,
(\lambda^{*},\mu^{*})
that is optimal to (
3.10
), we have

\displaystyle\mu^{*}\in[-B,B],~\lambda^{*}\in[0,{2B}/{\tau}].

Hence we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(X)=\inf_{\lambda\in[0,2B/\tau],\mu\in[-B,B]}\left\{f(\lambda,\mu)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\lambda\tau+\mu+(\lambda\phi)^{*}(X-\mu)\right]\right\}.

(3.11)

In addition, for any
\underline{\lambda}\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(X)\leq\inf_{\lambda\in[\underline{\lambda},2B/\tau],\mu\in[-B,B]}f(\lambda,\mu)\leq\mathcal{R}(X)+\underline{\lambda}\tau.

(3.12)

Proof.

For any
\lambda\geq 0
, we have
0\in\partial(\lambda\phi)(1)
as
1
is a minimizer of
\phi
,
and hence
1\in\partial(\lambda\phi)^{*}(0)
.
Now define
f(\lambda,\mu;\omega)=\lambda\tau+\mu+(\lambda\phi)^{*}(X(\omega)-\mu)
, i.e.,

f(\lambda,\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[f(\lambda,\mu;\omega)\right]
.
For any
\lambda\geq 0
, if
\mu\geq B
, we have
X(\omega)-\mu\leq 0
.
From monotonicity of subdifferential, we have
\partial_{\mu}(\lambda\phi)^{*}(X(\omega)-\mu)\leq 1
, and hence

\displaystyle d(\lambda,\mu;\omega)=1-\partial_{\mu}(\lambda\phi)^{*}(X(\omega)-\mu)\geq 0,~\forall d(\lambda,\mu;\omega)\in\partial f(\lambda,\mu;\omega).

Consequently we have
\partial_{\mu}f(\lambda,\mu)\geq 0
for any
\mu\geq B
.
Similarly, one can establish that
for any
\mu\leq-B
,
\partial_{\mu}f(\lambda,\mu)\leq 0
.
Hence from monotonicity of subdifferential, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Argmin}}_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}}f(\lambda,\mu)\subseteq[-B,B],~\forall\lambda\geq 0.

(3.13)

In addition, since
\phi(1)=0
, we have that for any
\lambda\geq 0
,

(\lambda\phi)^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\geq 0}xy-\phi(x)\geq y
.
This in turn implies

\displaystyle f(\lambda,\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[f(\lambda,\mu;\omega)\right]\geq\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\lambda\tau+\mu+X(\omega)-\mu\right]=\lambda\tau+\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[X(\omega)\right],~\forall\lambda\geq 0,\mu\in\mathbb{R}

From Lemma
3.3
, we also have

\inf_{\lambda\geq 0,\mu\in\mathbb{R}}f(\lambda,\mu)=\mathcal{R}(X)\leq B
.
Hence from the above observation, for any
(\lambda^{*},\mu^{*})
that is optimal to (
3.10
), we have

\displaystyle B\geq f(\lambda^{*},\mu^{*})\geq\lambda^{*}\tau+E_{P}[X(\omega)]\geq\lambda^{*}\tau-B,

from which we obtain
\lambda^{*}\leq\tfrac{2B}{\tau}
.
Finally, note that for any
0\leq\lambda\leq\lambda^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle(\lambda\phi)^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\in\mathrm{dom}(\phi)}yx-\lambda\phi(x)\geq\sup_{x\in\mathrm{dom}(\phi)}yx-\lambda^{\prime}\phi(x)=(\lambda^{\prime}\phi)^{*}(y),

and consequently

\displaystyle f(\lambda,\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\lambda\tau+\mu+(\lambda\phi)^{*}(X(\omega)-\mu)\right]\geq\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\lambda\tau+\mu+(\lambda^{\prime}\phi)^{*}(X(\omega)-\mu)\right]=f(\lambda^{\prime},\mu)+(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})\tau,

from which we obtain (
3.12
).
∎

We proceed to show that
f({\lambda,\mu})
defined in (
3.11
) is Lipschitz continuous
whenever the divergence function
\phi
is replaced by its truncated version
\phi_{L}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

For any divergence function
\phi
, any
L\geq 1
and
\underline{\lambda}>0
,
we have that

\displaystyle f_{L}(\lambda,\mu;\omega)\coloneqq\lambda\tau+\mu+(\lambda\phi_{L})^{*}(X(\omega)-\mu)

(3.14)

is Lipschitz continuous w.r.t
\mu
(resp.
\lambda
) with modulus
L+1
(resp.
\tfrac{2(1+L)B}{\underline{\lambda}}+\tau
)
over domain

[\underline{\lambda},{2B}/{\tau}]\times[-B,B]
.
In addition, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert f_{L}(\lambda,\mu;\omega)\right\rvert\leq(3+2L)B,~\forall\mu\in[-B,B],~\lambda\in[0,{2B}/{\tau}].

(3.15)

Proof.

For any
\lambda\geq 0
, since
(\lambda\phi_{L})^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\in[0,L]\cap\mathrm{dom}(\phi)}yx-\lambda\phi(x)
, for any
\left\lvert y\right\rvert\leq 2B
, we have

(\lambda\phi_{L})^{*}(y)\geq y\geq-2B
.
On the other hand, let

\displaystyle K_{L}(y,\underline{\lambda})=\left\{x\in[0,L]\cap\mathrm{dom}(\phi):yx-\underline{\lambda}\phi(x)\geq-2B\right\}.

Note that
K_{L}(y,\underline{\lambda})
is non-empty for
\left\lvert y\right\rvert\leq 2B
.
For any
\lambda\geq\underline{\lambda}>0
, if
x\notin K_{L}(y,\underline{\lambda})
, we have

\displaystyle yx-\lambda\phi(x)\leq yx-\underline{\lambda}\phi(x)<-2B\leq(\lambda\phi_{L})^{*}(y).

Consequently we have that for
\left\lvert y\right\rvert\leq 2B
,

\displaystyle(\lambda\phi_{L})^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\in[0,L]\cap\mathrm{dom}(\phi)\cap K_{L}(y,\underline{\lambda})}yx-\lambda\phi(x).

Hence for any
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\geq\underline{\lambda}
and any
\left\lvert y\right\rvert\leq 2B
,
we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert(\lambda\phi_{L})^{*}(y)-(\lambda^{\prime}\phi_{L})^{*}(y)\right\rvert\leq\sup_{x\in[0,L]\cap\mathrm{dom}(\phi)\cap K_{L}(y,\underline{\lambda})}\left\lvert(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})\phi(x)\right\rvert\leq\tfrac{2(1+L)B}{\underline{\lambda}}\left\lvert\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}\right\rvert,

where the last inequality follows from

\phi(x)\leq\tfrac{yx+2B}{\underline{\lambda}}\leq\tfrac{2(1+L)B}{\underline{\lambda}}
for any
x\in K_{L}(y,\underline{\lambda})
and
\left\lvert y\right\rvert\leq 2B
.
This implies
f_{L}(\cdot,\mu;\omega)
is Lipschitz continuous with modulus
\tfrac{2(1+L)B}{\underline{\lambda}}+\tau
.
In addition, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert(\lambda\phi_{L})^{*}(y)-(\lambda\phi_{L})^{*}(y^{\prime})\right\rvert\leq\sup_{x\in[0,L]\cap\mathrm{dom}(\phi)}\left\lvert x(y-y^{\prime})\right\rvert\leq L\left\lvert y-y^{\prime}\right\rvert,

from which we conclude that

f_{L}(\lambda,\cdot;\omega)
is Lipschitz continuous with modulus
L+1
, and that

\left\lvert f_{L}(\lambda,\mu;\omega)\right\rvert\leq\tfrac{2B}{\tau}\cdot\tau+B+2BL\leq(3+2L)B
for any

\mu\in[-B,B],~\lambda\in[0,{2B}/{\tau}].

∎

We are now ready to establish the sample complexity of estimating
\mathcal{R}(X)
via either
\mathcal{R}_{n,L}(X)
or
\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose
\lim_{x\to\infty}\phi(x)/x=\infty
.
For any
\epsilon>0
, let

L(\epsilon)=g_{\phi}^{-1}(\tfrac{32B\tau}{\epsilon})
,

\underline{\lambda}(\epsilon)=\tfrac{\epsilon}{8\tau}
,
and define

\displaystyle\overline{L}_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)={L(\epsilon)+1+\tfrac{2(1+L(\epsilon))B}{\underline{\lambda}(\epsilon)}+\tau},~~M_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)=(3+2L(\epsilon))B.

(3.16)

Then the following holds.

(1)

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
and

n\geq\tfrac{128M_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)^{2}\log(256B^{2}\overline{L}^{2}_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)/(\tau\epsilon^{2}\delta))}{\epsilon^{2}}
,
we have with probability at least
1-\delta
that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathcal{R}(X)-\mathcal{R}_{n,L(\epsilon)}(X)\right\rvert\leq\epsilon.

(2)

For any

n\geq\tfrac{512M_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)^{2}\log(8192B^{3}\overline{L}^{2}_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)/(\tau\epsilon^{3}))}{\epsilon^{2}},

we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(X)-\epsilon\leq\mathbb{E}_{P^{\otimes n}}\left[\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)\right]\leq\mathcal{R}(X).

Proof.

From Lemma
3.5
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{L}(X)

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\lambda\in[\underline{\lambda},2B/\tau],\mu\in[-B,B]}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[f_{L}(\lambda,\mu;\omega)\right]\leq\mathcal{R}_{L}(X)+\underline{\lambda}\tau,

(3.17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{L,n}(X)

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\lambda\in[\underline{\lambda},2B/\tau],\mu\in[-B,B]}\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}\left[f_{L}(\lambda,\mu;\omega)\right]\leq\mathcal{R}_{L,n}(X)+\underline{\lambda}\tau.

(3.18)

For any
\varepsilon>0
, let
\mathcal{N}_{1}
(resp.
\mathcal{N}_{2}
) be an
\varepsilon
-net of
[\underline{\lambda},2B/\tau]
(resp.
[-B,B]
).
For any
\lambda\in[\underline{\lambda},2B/\tau]
and
\mu\in[-B,B]
, let

\lambda_{\varepsilon}
and
\mu_{\varepsilon}
be their corresponding closest points in
\mathcal{N}_{1}
and
\mathcal{N}_{2}
respectively.
From Hoeffding’s inequality and (
3.15
) in Lemma
3.6
, we have that for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[f_{L}(\lambda_{\varepsilon},\mu_{\varepsilon};\omega)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}\left[f_{L}(\lambda_{\varepsilon},\mu_{\varepsilon};\omega)\right]\right\rvert\leq(3+2L)B\sqrt{\tfrac{2\log(4B^{2}/(\tau\varepsilon^{2}\delta))}{n}},~\forall\lambda\in[\underline{\lambda},2B/\tau],\mu\in[-B,B].

Combining the above observation with Lemma
3.6
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[f_{L}(\lambda,\mu;\omega)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}\left[f_{L}(\lambda,\mu;\omega)\right]\right\rvert\leq(3+2L)B\sqrt{\tfrac{2\log(4B^{2}/(\tau\varepsilon^{2}\delta))}{n}}+2\left(L+1+\tfrac{2(1+L)B}{\underline{\lambda}}+\tau\right)\varepsilon.

By choosing
L=L(\epsilon)
,
\underline{\lambda}=\underline{\lambda}(\epsilon)
and
\varepsilon=\tfrac{\epsilon}{8\overline{L}_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[f_{L}(\lambda,\mu;\omega)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}\left[f_{L}(\lambda,\mu;\omega)\right]\right\rvert\leq M_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)\sqrt{\tfrac{2\log(256B^{2}\overline{L}^{2}_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)/(\tau\epsilon^{2}\delta))}{n}}+\tfrac{\epsilon}{4}.

(3.19)

Combining the above observation with (
3.17
), (
3.18
), together with
\underline{\lambda}(\epsilon)={\epsilon}/\left(8\tau\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathcal{R}_{L}(X)-\mathcal{R}_{n,L}(X)\right\rvert\leq M_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)\sqrt{\tfrac{2\log(256B^{2}\overline{L}^{2}_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)/(\tau\epsilon^{2}\delta))}{n}}+\tfrac{3\epsilon}{4}

with probability
1-\delta
.
From Lemma
3.2
, since
L(\epsilon)=g_{\phi}^{-1}(\tfrac{32B\tau}{\epsilon})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(X)-\tfrac{\epsilon}{8}\leq\mathcal{R}_{L}(X)\leq\mathcal{R}(X).

(3.20)

Given this and

n\geq\tfrac{128M_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)^{2}\log(256B^{2}\overline{L}^{2}_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)/(\tau\epsilon^{2}\delta))}{\epsilon^{2}},

we obtain that with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathcal{R}(X)-\mathcal{R}_{n,L}(X)\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\mathcal{R}_{L}(X)-\mathcal{R}_{n,L}(X)\right\rvert+\tfrac{\epsilon}{8}\leq\epsilon.

In addition, given that

\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)\geq\mathcal{R}_{n,L}(X)
, from (
3.20
) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P^{\otimes n}}\left[\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)\right]\geq\mathbb{E}_{P^{\otimes n}}\left[\mathcal{R}_{n,L}(X)\right]\geq\mathcal{R}(X)-M_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)\sqrt{\tfrac{2\log(256B^{2}\overline{L}^{2}_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)/(\tau\epsilon^{2}\delta))}{n}}-\tfrac{7\epsilon}{8}-2\delta B.

We can then conclude the proof by taking
\delta=\tfrac{\epsilon}{32B}
and

n\geq\tfrac{512M_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)^{2}\log(8192B^{3}\overline{L}^{2}_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)/(\tau\epsilon^{3}))}{\epsilon^{2}}.

∎

It should be noted that the estimation guarantee we obtain in Theorem
3.3
for the sample average estimator
\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)
is stated in expectation.
This can be improved to high probability with essentially the same number of samples.
To this end, the following observation directly controls the Lipschitz continuity of
f(\lambda,\mu)
in (
3.11
) without the need for truncation
\phi_{L}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Suppose
\lim_{x\to\infty}\phi(x)/x=\infty
.
For any
\underline{\lambda}>0
,
define
L_{(\phi,B,\underline{\lambda})}=g_{\phi}^{-1}({\tfrac{4B}{\underline{\lambda}}})
.
We have

\displaystyle\inf_{\lambda\in[\underline{\lambda},2B/\tau],\mu\in[-B,B]}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[f(\lambda,\mu;\omega)\right]=\inf_{\lambda\in[\underline{\lambda},2B/\tau],\mu\in[-B,B]}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[f_{L}(\lambda,\mu;\omega)\right],

whenever
L\geq L_{\phi,B,\underline{\lambda}}
.

Proof.

Similar to Lemma
3.6
, one can readily show that for
\left\lvert y\right\rvert\leq 2B
,

\displaystyle(\lambda\phi)^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\in\mathrm{dom}(\phi)\cap K(y,\underline{\lambda})}yx-\lambda\phi(x),

where

K(y,\underline{\lambda})=\left\{x\in\mathrm{dom}(\phi):yx-\underline{\lambda}\phi(x)\geq-2B\right\}

is non-empty.
Hence

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathrm{dom}(\phi)\cap K(y,\underline{\lambda})}\phi(x)\leq\sup_{x\in\mathrm{dom}(\phi)\cap K(y,\underline{\lambda})}\tfrac{2B(1+x)}{\underline{\lambda}}\leq\tfrac{2B(1+L_{(\phi,B,\underline{\lambda})})}{\underline{\lambda}}.

where the last inequality follows from the fact that

\tfrac{\phi(x)}{x}\leq\tfrac{2B+2B/x}{\underline{\lambda}}\leq\tfrac{4B}{\underline{\lambda}}
for any
x\in K(y,\underline{\lambda})
,
x\geq 1
, and
\left\lvert y\right\rvert\leq 2B
, which in turn implies

x\leq g_{\phi}^{-1}(\tfrac{4B}{\underline{\lambda}})=L_{(\phi,B,\underline{\lambda})}
for any
x\in K(y,\underline{\lambda})
.
∎

In view of Lemma
3.7
, Theorem
3.3
readily implies the high probability convergence for the SAA estimator
R_{n}(X)
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Suppose
\lim_{x\to\infty}\phi(x)/x=\infty
.
For any
\epsilon>0
, let

\underline{\lambda}(\epsilon),L(\epsilon)
,

\overline{L}_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)
and
M_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)
be defined as in (
3.16
).
Then for any
\delta\in(0,1)
,
we have with probability at least
1-\delta
that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathcal{R}(X)-\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)\right\rvert\leq\epsilon,

whenever

n\geq\tfrac{128M_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)^{2}\log(256B^{2}\overline{L}^{2}_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)/(\tau\epsilon^{2}\delta))}{\epsilon^{2}}
.

Proof.

The proof follows from Lemma
3.7
, together with the same lines after (
3.19
) in Theorem
3.3
.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

It should be noted that Theorem
3.3
and
3.4
can also be applied to divergence functions
\phi
with sublinear growth, provided that the estimation accuracy
\epsilon
is not too small such that
L(\epsilon)
is well-defined.

Instance-dependent Upper Bound.

One can also strengthen Theorem
3.3
to the following instance-dependent sample complexity bound when estimating
\mathcal{R}(X)
.
It could be perhaps worth mentioning here that the obtained instance-dependent bound holds for any divergence function
\phi
and does not require its superlinear growth.

Theorem 3.5
.

For
\epsilon>0
,
let
\zeta^{*}_{\epsilon}
be an
(\epsilon/8)
-optimal solution of (
1.1
).
Define

\displaystyle L(\epsilon)=\inf\left\{L:\mathbb{E}_{P}[\zeta^{*}_{\epsilon}-L]_{+}\leq\epsilon/8\right\}.

Let
\underline{\lambda}(\epsilon),\overline{L}_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)
and
M_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)
be defined as in (
3.16
).
Then both claims in Theorem
3.3
hold.

Proof.

It suffices to note that
L(\epsilon)
is well-defined. Indeed, since
\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\zeta^{*}_{\epsilon}-L\right]_{+}\leq\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\zeta^{*}_{\epsilon}\right]=1
, the dominated convergence theorem implies that

\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\zeta^{*}_{\epsilon}-L\right]_{+}=0
.
The rest of the claims then follow directly from similar lines as in Theorem
3.3
.
∎

In view of Theorem
3.3
and
3.4
, the sample complexity for obtaining an
\epsilon
-accurate
\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)
as an estimate of
\mathcal{R}(X)
is bounded by

\mathcal{O}(M_{\phi,\tau}^{2}/\epsilon^{2})=\mathcal{O}(B^{2}L(\epsilon)^{2}/\epsilon^{2})
.
It turns out that this can be further improved by refining our argument in Theorem
3.3
, which we proceed to discuss in the next section.
The same refinement can also be made for the instance-dependent sample complexity presented in Theorem
3.5
.

3.3
Improved Sample Complexity Upper Bound

In this section, we discuss some improvements to the obtained sample complexity in Theorem
3.3
and
3.4
.
Note that since
\mathcal{R}(X+c)=\mathcal{R}(X)+c
, without loss of generality we can assume
0\leq X\leq B
.
Given this and the fact that
\phi^{*}(y)\geq y
, we have

\displaystyle f_{L}(\lambda,\mu;\omega)=\lambda\tau+\mu+(\lambda\phi_{L})^{*}(X(\omega)-\mu)\geq\lambda\tau+\mu+X(\omega)-\mu\geq 0,

(3.21)

for any
L\geq 0
and
(\lambda,\mu)\in[\underline{\lambda},2B/\tau]\times[-B,B]
.

We begin by noting that Theorem
3.3
only uses the boundedness of
f_{L}
over the domain of our interest
(\lambda,\mu)\in[\underline{\lambda},2B/\tau]\times[-B,B]
.
On the other hand, from Lemma
3.5
, it is clear that at the optimal dual variables
(\lambda^{*},\mu^{*})
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[f_{L}(\lambda^{*},\mu^{*};\omega)\right]\leq B+\underline{\lambda}\tau
.
Given this and (
3.21
), one can immediately control the variance of
f_{L}(\lambda^{*},\mu^{*};\omega)
by its mean, which implies an order of
\mathcal{O}(M_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)B)
instead of
\mathcal{O}(M_{\phi,\tau}^{2}(\epsilon))
.
This suggests that one can potentially strengthen Theorem
3.3
and
3.4
by a direct application of Bernstein’s inequality.
To proceed, we recall the following variants of Bernstein’s inequality.

Lemma 3.8
.

For any random variable
Z
over
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
with
0\leq Z\leq M
.
Let
P_{n}
denote the empirical distribution defined over
n
independent samples from
P
.
We have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}\left[Z\right]-\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[Z\right]}\leq 4\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{M{\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[Z\right]}\log(2/\delta)}{n}}+\tfrac{M\log(2/\delta)}{n}\right),

(3.22)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[Z\right]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}\left[Z\right]}\leq 4\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{M{\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}\left[Z\right]}\log(2/\delta)}{n}}+\tfrac{M\log(2/\delta)}{n}\right).

(3.23)

Proof.

Note that
\mathrm{Var}(Z)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{2}\right]\leq M\mathbb{E}\left[Z\right]
, from Bernstein’s inequality, we have

from which we conclude the proof.

Remark 3.2
.

In view of Theorem
3.6
, the number of samples to ensure
\left\lvert\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)-\mathcal{R}(X)\right\rvert\leq\epsilon
is at the order of

\mathcal{O}(BM_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon)/\epsilon^{2})=\mathcal{O}(L(\epsilon)B^{2}/\epsilon^{2})
.
This improves the sample complexity obtained in Theorem
3.3
and
3.4
by an order of
L(\epsilon)=\mathcal{O}(g_{\phi}^{-1}(B\tau/\epsilon))
.
As we will see in Section
4
, such an improvement already yields an optimal sample complexity for estimating conditional value-at-risk
\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(X)
,
where the corresponding divergence function is given by
\phi(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{[0,1/\alpha]}(x)

[
6
]
.

In a nutshell, Theorem
3.6
utilizes the fact that the variance of
f_{L(\epsilon)}(\mu,\lambda;\omega)
at the optimal solution
(\mu^{*},\lambda^{*})
over the domain of our interest
(\lambda,\mu)\in[\underline{\lambda}(\epsilon),2B/\tau]\times[-B,B]
is bounded by its mean
\mathcal{O}(BM_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon))
,
and that

\mathbb{E}_{P}[f_{L(\epsilon)}(\mu^{*},\lambda^{*};\omega)]
provides a close approximation to
\mathcal{R}(X)
given Lemma
3.2
.
It is not difficult to see that the same approach can be extended to controlling the difference

\displaystyle\Delta(\mu,\lambda;\omega)=f_{L(\epsilon)}(\mu,\lambda;\omega)-f_{L(\epsilon^{\prime})}(\mu,\lambda;\omega)

where
\epsilon\leq\epsilon^{\prime}
(which implies
L(\epsilon)\geq L(\epsilon^{\prime})
).
A key observation which we will utilize is that the mean of the above difference at the optimal solution
(\mu^{*},\lambda^{*})
can be substantially smaller than
\mathcal{O}(BM_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon))
.
Indeed, from Lemma
3.2
one can readily show that such difference is at the order of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{\prime}M_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon))
instead of
\mathcal{O}(BM_{\phi,\tau}(\epsilon))
.
This suggests that instead of directly discussing
f_{L(\epsilon)}(\mu,\lambda;\omega)
,
one can instead control a sequence of differences that sums up to
f_{L(\epsilon)}(\mu,\lambda;\omega)
.
We formalize this observation as follows.

\varepsilon>0
, let
\mathcal{N}_{1}
(resp.
\mathcal{N}_{2}

\varepsilon

[\underline{\lambda},2B/\tau]
(resp.

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\Delta_{i}(\lambda_{\varepsilon},\mu_{\varepsilon};\omega)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}\left[\Delta_{i}(\lambda_{\varepsilon},\mu_{\varepsilon};\omega)\right]}

\displaystyle 4\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{M_{i+1}{\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}\left[\Delta_{i}(\lambda_{\varepsilon},\mu_{\varepsilon};\omega)\right]}\log(4B^{2}/(\tau\varepsilon^{2}\delta))}{n}}+\tfrac{M_{i+1}\log(4B^{2}/(\tau\varepsilon^{2}\delta))}{n}\right)

.
Consequently it suffices to choose

\displaystyle n\geq\tfrac{\sup_{\epsilon^{\prime}\in[\epsilon,B]}4096M(\epsilon^{\prime})\epsilon^{\prime}\log(4B^{2}/(\tau\varepsilon^{2}\delta))\lceil\log_{2}(B/\epsilon)\rceil^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}+\tfrac{32M(\epsilon)\log(4B^{2}/(\tau\varepsilon^{2}\delta))\lceil\log_{2}(B/\epsilon)\rceil}{\epsilon}

to ensure

\left\lvert\mathcal{R}(X)-\mathcal{R}_{n,L_{k}}(X)\right\rvert\leq\epsilon
.
The proof for

\left\lvert\mathcal{R}(X)-\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)\right\rvert\leq\epsilon

follows from a similar argument as above by using Lemma
3.7
.
The desired claim then follows from the choice of
\varepsilon=\tfrac{\epsilon}{16\log_{2}(B/2)\overline{L}(\epsilon)}
.
∎

4
Application to Common
\phi
-divergences

We are now ready to instantiate the sample complexity bounds obtained in Section
2
and
3
for some commonly used
\phi
-divergences
[
1
, Table 1]
.
We begin by showing that for a class of
\phi
-divergences, obtaining an
\epsilon
-accurate estimation of
\mathcal{R}(X)
can require an arbitrarily large number of samples.

Corollary 4.1

(Essential supremum)
.

For
\mathcal{R}(X)=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}X
, obtaining an
\epsilon
-accurate estimation of
\mathcal{R}(X)
can require an arbitrarily large number of samples.

Proof.

Clearly
\mathcal{R}(X)=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}X
corresponds to choosing
\phi(\cdot)\equiv\mathrm{1}_{[0,\infty)}
in (
1.1
).
The desired claim follows from invoking Theorem
2.2
.
∎

Corollary 4.2

(Burg entropy)
.

For
\mathcal{R}(X)
corresponding to Burg entropy, obtaining an
\epsilon
-accurate estimation of
\mathcal{R}(X)
can require an arbitrarily large number of samples.

Proof.

The desired claim follows from invoking Theorem
2.2
, and that
Burg entropy corresponds to
\phi(x)=-\log x+x-1
.
∎

Corollary 4.3

(Neyman
\chi^{2}
-divergence)
.

For
\mathcal{R}(X)
corresponding to Neyman
\chi^{2}
-divergence, obtaining an
\epsilon
-accurate estimation of
\mathcal{R}(X)
can require an arbitrarily large number of samples.

Proof.

The desired claim follows from invoking Theorem
2.2
, and that
Neyman
\chi^{2}
-divergence corresponds to
\phi(x)={(x-1)^{2}}/{x}
.
∎

Corollary 4.4

(Variation distance)
.

For
\mathcal{R}(X)
corresponding to the variation distance, obtaining an
\epsilon
-accurate estimation of
\mathcal{R}(X)
can require an arbitrarily large number of samples.

Proof.

The desired claim follows from invoking Theorem
2.2
, and that
variation distance corresponds to
\phi(x)=\left\lvert x-1\right\rvert
.
∎

Corollary 4.5

(Hellinger distance)
.

For
\mathcal{R}(X)
corresponding to the Hellinger distance, obtaining an
\epsilon
-accurate estimation of
\mathcal{R}(X)
can require an arbitrarily large number of samples.

Proof.

The desired claim follows from invoking Theorem
2.2
, and that
Hellinger distance corresponds to
\phi(x)=(\sqrt{x}-1)^{2}
.
∎

We next proceed to establish sample complexity upper bounds and demonstrate their optimality by comparing with the lower bound in (
3.5
).

Corollary 4.6

(Conditional value-at-risk)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}(X)=\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(X)
.
For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, the number of samples
n
to ensure
\left\lvert\mathcal{R}(X)-\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)\right\rvert\leq\epsilon
with probability
1-\delta
can be bounded by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\tfrac{B^{2}}{\alpha\epsilon^{2}}+\tfrac{B}{\alpha\epsilon}\right).

Proof.

The conditional value-at-risk
\mathcal{R}(X)=\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(X)
corresponds to
\phi(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{[0,1/\alpha]}
.
In view of Theorem
3.6
, the sample complexity upper bound is given by

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\tfrac{B^{2}}{\alpha\epsilon^{2}}+\tfrac{B}{\alpha\epsilon}\right).

∎

From (
3.5
), the sample complexity lower bound is given by

\Omega\big({\max\{\tfrac{B}{\alpha\epsilon},\tfrac{B^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\}}\big)
,
which differs from the obtained upper bound in Corollary
4.6
by a factor of
1/\alpha
.
It is not difficult to refine our argument in Theorem
3.2
for conditional value-at-risk and obtain a sample complexity lower bound of

\Omega(\tfrac{B^{2}}{\alpha\epsilon^{2}})
.
To see this, note that for conditional value-at-risk, we have
\mathcal{R}(p)=B\min\left\{p/\alpha,1\right\}
, where
\mathcal{R}(p)
is defined in the proof of
Theorem
3.2
.
Consequently one can readily find a close pair
(p^{\prime},p)
with
\mathcal{R}(p)=Bp/\alpha
and
\mathcal{R}(p^{\prime})=Bp^{\prime}/\alpha
.
Then the lower bound of
\Omega(\tfrac{B^{2}}{\alpha\epsilon^{2}})
follows from similar lines as in Theorem
3.2
.
Combining this observation with Corollary
4.6
, the obtained sample complexity for conditional value-at-risk is indeed optimal.

Corollary 4.7

(Kullback-Leibler divergence)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}(X)=\sup\left\{\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[X\right]:\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)\leq\tau\right\}
.
For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, the number of samples
n
to ensure
\left\lvert\mathcal{R}(X)-\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)\right\rvert\leq\epsilon
with probability
1-\delta
can be bounded by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\tfrac{B\exp(\tau/\epsilon)}{\epsilon}\right).

The above sample complexity bound is also optimal.

Proof.

We have
\phi(x)=x\log x-x+1
. In view of Theorem
3.7
, the sample complexity upper bound is given by

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\tfrac{B\exp(\tau/\epsilon)}{\epsilon}\right),

which matches the lower bound in (
3.5
) through direct verification.
∎

It appears to us that Corollary
4.7
provides the first sample complexity bound for estimating distributionally robust functional generated by
\mathrm{KL}
-divergence, without any dependence on the nominal measure. The obtained complexity bound also matches the lower bound and hence is optimal. It is perhaps worth mentioning that the obtained results do not require the sample space to be finite (cf.,
[
9
,
7
,
8
]
).

Corollary 4.8

(Cressie-Read family
[
3
]
)
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\left\{-1,+1\right\}
, let the divergence function be
\phi_{k}(x)=\tfrac{x^{k}-kx+k-1}{k(k-1)}
.
Then the following holds.

(1)

If
k>1
, then for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, the number of samples
n
to ensure
\left\lvert\mathcal{R}(X)-\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)\right\rvert\leq\epsilon
with probability
1-\delta
can be bounded by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\epsilon^{-\max\left\{2,{k}/\left(k-1\right)\right\}}\right).

The above sample complexity bound is also optimal.

(2)

If
k<1
, then obtaining an
\epsilon
-accurate estimation of
\mathcal{R}(X)
can require an arbitrarily large number of samples.

Proof.

The first part of the claim follows from the direct application of Theorem
3.7
and the lower bound in (
3.5
).
The second part of the claim follows from Theorem
2.2
.
∎

In view of Corollary
4.8
, the sample average approximation estimator
\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)
obtains the optimal sample complexity bound of

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\epsilon^{-\max\left\{2,{k}/\left(k-1\right)\right\}}\right)

for the Cressie-Read family of divergences when
k>1
.
The obtained sample complexity bound recovers the optimal bound obtained in
[
3
]
.
In addition, for
k<1
, Corollary
4.8
states that estimating
\mathcal{R}(X)
may require an arbitrarily large sample complexity.

5
Conclusion

Before we conclude our discussions, we briefly discuss some potential problems of interest.
First, it could be interesting to extend the sample complexity results to the case when decision variables are involved, i.e.,
\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}(X_{\theta})
with a compact
\Theta
.
In this case, it appears that one can readily develop sample approximation-based methods
[
5
]
for solving
\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}(X_{\theta})
via the truncated variant
\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}_{L}(X_{\theta})
(cf., Lemma
3.2
,
3.5
and
3.6
).
Second, it remains rewarding to study the asymptotic normality of the sample average estimator
\mathcal{R}_{n}(X)
and compare the result with the risk-neutral case.
Lastly, one could further consider studying the sample average approximation for general coherent risk measures.
