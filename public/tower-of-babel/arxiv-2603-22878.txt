Introduction

1.1
Background

Recurrence sequences occupy a central place in modern Number theory, with their properties and Diophantine aspects attracting sustained research interest. The Fibonacci sequence and its companion Lucas sequence are among the most celebrated examples, and both admit natural higher-order generalizations. For an integer

k\geq 2
, the
k
-Fibonacci sequence
\{F_{n}^{(k)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is defined by the linear recurrence

F_{n}^{(k)}=F_{n-1}^{(k)}+F_{n-2}^{(k)}+\cdots+F_{n-k}^{(k)},\quad\text{for all}\ n\geq 2,

subject to the initial conditions
F_{2-k}^{(k)}=\cdots=F_{-1}^{(k)}=F_{0}^{(k)}=0
and
F_{1}^{(k)}=1
. Setting
k=2
recovers the classical Fibonacci sequence, and the family
\{F_{n}^{(k)}\}
has attracted considerable attention from algebraic and combinatorial perspectives alike.
The companion sequence of
k
-Lucas numbers
\{L_{n}^{(k)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
satisfies the same recurrence,

L_{n}^{(k)}=L_{n-1}^{(k)}+L_{n-2}^{(k)}+\cdots+L_{n-k}^{(k)},\quad\text{for all}\ n\geq 2,

but is distinguished by the initial values
L_{2-k}^{(k)}=\cdots=L_{-1}^{(k)}=0,~L_{0}^{(k)}=2,~L_{1}^{(k)}=1
. The case
k=2
yields the classical Lucas sequence, which has found applications across Number theory, cryptography, and primality testing. Both
k
- families exhibit a rich arithmetic structure that continues to motivate new investigations.

For a prime
p
and an integer
\mathfrak{a}\geq 1
, the expression

\displaystyle(p+1)p^{\mathfrak{a}}-1,

is a natural prime-base analogue of the classical Thabit-type number
3\cdot 2^{\mathfrak{a}}-1
. Indeed, when
p=2
, we recover the Thabit numbers
3\cdot 2^{\mathfrak{a}}-1
, for an integer
\mathfrak{a}\geq 1
. The identity

\displaystyle(p+1)p^{\mathfrak{a}}=\left[(p+1)p^{\mathfrak{a}}-1\right]+1,

shows that one is searching for terms in a linear recurrence for which the successor has a rigid multiplicative structure “
a prime power times its successor
”. This arithmetic structure is the basic input throughout the paper.

The arithmetic study of special Number-theoretic sequences inside linear sequences has a long history. For example, Altassan and Alan proved in
[
1
]
that the only Mersenne numbers
2^{\mathfrak{a}}-1
, that intersect with the sequence of
k
-Lucas numbers are
1=L_{1}^{(k)}
,
3=L_{2}^{(k)}
and
7=L_{4}^{(2)}
.

In this paper, we study the Diophantine equation

\displaystyle L_{n}^{(k)}=(p+1)p^{\mathfrak{a}}-1,

(1.1)

where
p=2^{\ell}\pm 1
is a Mersenne or Fermat prime, and
n\geq 2
,
k\geq 2
,
\mathfrak{a}\geq 1
,
\ell\geq 1
are positive integers. Note that for
n=0,1
, we have
L_{n}^{(k)}=2,1
and the Diophantine equation (
1.1
) has no positive solutions
p
and
\mathfrak{a}
.

Some useful formulas and inequalities involving
L_{n}^{(k)}

In this section we collect some useful formulas and inequalities concerning
k
-generalized Lucas numbers. First, it is known that the formula

\displaystyle L_{n}^{(k)}=3\cdot 2^{n-2}\quad{\text{\rm holds~for}}\quad 2\leq n\leq k.

Furthermore,

L_{k+1}^{(k)}=3\cdot 2^{k-1}-2.

Using induction, one proves easily that the inequality

L_{n}^{(k)}\leq 3\cdot 2^{n-2}-2\qquad{\text{\rm holds~for~all}}\qquad n\geq k+1.

(2.1)

The
k
-generalized Lucas numbers can be expressed in terms of the
k
-generalized Fibonacci numbers via the formula

L_{n}^{(k)}=2F_{n+1}^{(k)}-F_{n}^{(k)}.

(2.2)

To prove the above formula, it is enough to first notice that it holds for
n=-(k-2),\ldots,0,1,2
and then to invoke the fact that both sequences
\{L_{n}^{(k)}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
and
\{2F_{n+1}^{(k)}-F_{n}^{(k)}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}

satisfy the same
k
th order linear recurrence to conclude that the above formula holds for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Another useful formula is

L_{n}^{(k)}=2L_{n-1}^{(k)}-L_{n-(k+1)}^{(k)}

for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
(to see why it holds, replace the left-hand side above by
L_{n-1}^{(k)}+L_{n-2}^{(k)}+\cdots+L_{n-k}^{(k)}
, cancel a term
L_{n-1}^{(k)}
on both sides of the resulting equation, and recognise that
the resulting formula is just the formula representing
L_{n-1}^{(k)}
as the sum of the previous
k
terms of the sequence). Reducing the above formula modulo
2
we get

L_{n}^{(k)}\equiv L_{n-(k+1)}^{(k)}\pmod{2},

showing that
\{L_{n}^{(k)}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is periodic modulo
2
with period
k+1
. This fact is so useful that we record it as a lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

The sequence
\{L_{n}^{(k)}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is periodic modulo
2
with period
k+1
.

Now let us recall some more formulas related to the sequence of
k
-generalized Lucas numbers. Its characteristic polynomial

g_{k}(X)=X^{k}-X^{k-1}-\cdots-X-1,

is irreducible in
\mathbb{Q}[X]
. This polynomial has exactly one real root greater than 1, denoted by
\alpha:=\alpha(k)
, and all its other roots lie inside the unit circle in the complex plane. Further, this real root lies within the range

\displaystyle 2\left(1-2^{-k}\right)<\alpha<2,\qquad{\text{\rm for~all}}\qquad k\geq 2.

(2.3)

If we need to refer to all the roots of
g_{k}(X)
, then we label them as
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
with the convention that
\alpha:=\alpha_{1}
. For all
n\geq 0
and
k\geq 2
, it was proved in
[
4
]
that the
k
-generalized Lucas numbers are bounded by powers of
\alpha
as

\displaystyle\alpha^{n-1}\leq L_{n}^{(k)}\leq 2\alpha^{n}.

(2.4)

Moreover, if we compare (
1.1
) and (
2.4
), we deduce that

\displaystyle p^{2}\leq p^{\mathfrak{a}+1}=p\cdot p^{\mathfrak{a}}<(p+1)p^{\mathfrak{a}}=1+L_{n}^{(k)}\leq 3\alpha^{n},

which implies that

\displaystyle p<\sqrt{3}\alpha^{n/2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathfrak{a}\leq n.

(2.5)

Next, we define the function

f_{k}(x):=\frac{x-1}{2+(k+1)(x-2)},\qquad x\in{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\left\{2-{2}/{(k+1)}\right\}.

We have

\frac{df_{k}(x)}{dx}=-\frac{(k-1)}{(2+(k+1)(x-2))^{2}}<0,\qquad{\text{\rm for all}}\qquad x>0.

In particular, inequality (
2.3
) implies that

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}=f_{k}(2)<f_{k}(\alpha)<f_{k}(2(1-2^{-k}))\leq\dfrac{3}{4},

(2.6)

for all
k\geq 3
. It is easy to check that the above inequality holds for
k=2
as well. Further, it is easy to verify that
|f_{k}(\alpha_{i})|<1
, for all
2\leq i\leq k
, where
\alpha_{i}
are the remaining
roots of
\Psi_{k}(x)
for
i=2,\ldots,k
.

The Binet formula for the general term of the
k
-Lucas numbers is given by

L_{n}^{(k)}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(2\alpha_{i}-1)f_{k}(\alpha_{i})\alpha_{i}^{n-1},\qquad{\text{\rm for all}}\quad n\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

Since
\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
are inside the unit circle, one would say that only the first term
(2\alpha_{1}-1)f_{k}(\alpha_{1})\alpha_{1}^{n-1}
already approximates
L_{n}^{(k)}
and indeed the approximation

\displaystyle\left|L_{n}^{(k)}-f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}\right|<\frac{3}{2},\qquad{\text{\rm for~all}}\quad n\geq 2-k

(2.7)

appears, for example, in
[
4
]
. This tells us that most of the size of
L_{n}^{(k)}
comes from the dominant term involving the real root
\alpha
, while the other terms contribute very little. A better estimate than (
2.7
) appears in Section 3.3 page 14 of
[
2
]
, but with a more restricted range of
n
in terms of
k
. It states that

\displaystyle\left|f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}-3\cdot 2^{n-2}\right|<3\cdot 2^{n-2}\cdot\frac{36}{2^{k/2}}\qquad{\text{\rm provided}}\qquad n<2^{k/2}.

(2.8)

Another useful combinatorial formula for the
k
th generalized Fibonacci numbers is due to Cooper and Howard in
[
9
]
and states that

\displaystyle F_{n}^{(k)}=2^{n-2}+\sum_{j=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n+k}{k+1}\right\rfloor-1}C_{n,j}\,2^{n-j(k+1)-2},\quad\text{where}\quad C_{n,j}:=(-1)^{j}\left(\binom{n-jk}{j}-\binom{n-jk-2}{j-2}\right).

(2.9)

In Equation (
2.9
), we make the convention that
\displaystyle\binom{n}{m}=0
if either
n<m
or one of
n
or
m
is negative.
Via equation (
2.2
), it yields the following formula for the
k
th generalized Lucas number:

\displaystyle L_{n}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=2F_{n+1}^{(k)}-F_{n}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=2\left(2^{n-1}+\sum_{j=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n+1+k}{k+1}\right\rfloor-1}C_{n+1,j}\,2^{n-j(k+1)-1}\right)-2^{n-2}-\sum_{j=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n+k}{k+1}\right\rfloor-1}C_{n,j}\,2^{n-j(k+1)-2}

\displaystyle=3\cdot 2^{n-2}+\sum_{j=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n+1+k}{k+1}\right\rfloor-1}4C_{n+1,j}\,2^{n-j(k+1)-2}-\sum_{j=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n+k}{k+1}\right\rfloor-1}C_{n,j}\,2^{n-j(k+1)-2}.

(2.10)

Lastly here, we recall one additional simple fact from calculus. If
x\in\mathbb{R}
satisfies
|x|<1/2
, then

\displaystyle|\log(1+x)|

\displaystyle<|x-x^{2}/2+-\dots|<|x|+\frac{|x|^{2}+|x|^{3}+\dots}{2}<|x|\left(1+\frac{|x|}{2(1-|x|)}\right)<\frac{3}{2}|x|.

(2.11)

In a similar way, we obtain the lower bound
|\log(1+x)|>\frac{1}{2}|x|
provided that
|x|<1/2
. We shall use these inequalities later in the proof of the main result.

4.1
Linear forms in logarithms

To estimate how small a nonzero linear combination of logarithms of algebraic numbers can be, we apply a result known as a Baker-type lower bound. While several such bounds are known in the literature, we make use of a version due to Matveev, as presented in
[
5
]
. Before we can formulate such inequalities we need the notion of height of an algebraic number recalled below.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\gamma
be an algebraic number of degree
d
with minimal primitive polynomial over the integers

a_{0}x^{d}+a_{1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+a_{d}=a_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\gamma^{(i)}),

where the leading coefficient
a_{0}
is positive. Then, the logarithmic height of
\gamma
is given by

h(\gamma):=\dfrac{1}{d}\Big(\log a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\max\{|\gamma^{(i)}|,1\}\Big).

In particular, if
\gamma
is a rational number represented as
\gamma:=p/q
with coprime integers
p
and
q\geq 1
, then
h(\gamma)=\log\max\{|p|,q\}
.
The following properties of the logarithmic height function
h(\cdot)
will be used in the rest of the paper without further reference:

\displaystyle h(\gamma_{1}\pm\gamma_{2})

\displaystyle\leq h(\gamma_{1})+h(\gamma_{2})+\log 2;

\displaystyle h(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{\pm 1})

\displaystyle\leq h(\gamma_{1})+h(\gamma_{2});

\displaystyle h(\gamma^{s})

\displaystyle=|s|h(\gamma)\quad{\text{\rm valid for}}\quad s\in\mathbb{Z}.

With these properties, it was easily computed in Section 3 of
[
3
]
that

\displaystyle h\left(f_{k}(\alpha)\right)<3\log k,\qquad\text{for all}\qquad k\geq 2.

(4.1)

A linear form in logarithms is an expression

\Lambda:=b_{1}\log\gamma_{1}+\cdots+b_{t}\log\gamma_{t},

where for us
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{t}
are positive real algebraic numbers and
b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}
are nonzero integers. We assume,
\Lambda\neq 0
. We need lower bounds
for
|\Lambda|
. We write
{\mathbb{K}}:={\mathbb{Q}}(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{t})
and
D
for the degree of
{\mathbb{K}}
.
We start with the general form due to Matveev, see Theorem 9.4 in
[
5
]
.

Theorem 4.2

(Matveev, see Theorem 9.4 in
[
5
]
)
.

Put
\Gamma:=\gamma_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots\gamma_{t}^{b_{t}}-1=e^{\Lambda}-1
. Assume
\Gamma\neq 0
. Then

\log|\Gamma|>-1.4\cdot 30^{t+3}\cdot t^{4.5}\cdot D^{2}(1+\log D)(1+\log B)A_{1}\cdots A_{t},

where
B\geq\max\{|b_{1}|,\ldots,|b_{t}|\}
and
A_{i}\geq\max\{Dh(\gamma_{i}),|\log\gamma_{i}|,0.16\}
for
i=1,\ldots,t
.

4.2
Reduction methods

Typically, the estimates from Matveev’s theorem are excessively large to be practical in computations. To refine these estimates, we use a method based on the LLL-algorithm. While the Baker-Davenport reduction is often sufficient for linear forms in two or three logarithms, the LLL-algorithm offers a more robust and generalized approach for three or more logarithms, as is the case in our study (see
[
11
,
7
]
). We next explain this method.

Let
k
be a positive integer. A subset
\mathcal{L}
of the
k
-dimensional real vector space
{\mathbb{R}^{k}}
is called a lattice if there exists a basis
\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{Z}b_{i}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}b_{i}\mid r_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

We say that
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
form a basis for
\mathcal{L}
, or that they span
\mathcal{L}
. We
call
k
the rank of
\mathcal{L}
. The determinant
\text{det}(\mathcal{L})
, of
\mathcal{L}
is defined by

\displaystyle\text{det}(\mathcal{L})=|\det(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k})|,

with the
b_{i}
’s being written as column vectors. This is a positive real number that does not depend on the choice of the basis (see
[
6
]
, Section 1.2).

Given linearly independent vectors
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we refer back to the Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization technique. This method allows us to inductively define vectors
b^{*}_{i}
(with
1\leq i\leq k
) and real coefficients
\mu_{i,j}
(for
1\leq j\leq i\leq k
). Specifically,

\displaystyle b^{*}_{i}

\displaystyle=b_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mu_{i,j}b^{*}_{j},~~~\mu_{i,j}=\dfrac{\langle b_{i},b^{*}_{j}\rangle}{\langle b^{*}_{j},b^{*}_{j}\rangle},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the ordinary inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Notice that
b^{*}_{i}
is the orthogonal projection of
b_{i}
on the orthogonal complement of the span of
b_{1},\ldots,b_{i-1}
, and that
\mathbb{R}b_{i}
is orthogonal to the span of
b^{*}_{1},\ldots,b^{*}_{i-1}
for
1\leq i\leq k
. It follows that
b^{*}_{1},b^{*}_{2},\ldots,b^{*}_{k}
is an orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Definition 4.3
.

The basis
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}
for the lattice
\mathcal{L}
is called reduced if

\displaystyle\|\mu_{i,j}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2},\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq j<i\leq n,~~\text{and}

\displaystyle\|b^{*}_{i}+\mu_{i,i-1}b^{*}_{i-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{4}\|b^{*}_{i-1}\|^{2},\quad\text{for}\quad 1<i\leq n,

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the ordinary Euclidean length. The constant
{3}/{4}
above is arbitrarily chosen, and may be replaced by any fixed real number
y
in the interval
{1}/{4}<y<1

(see
[
10
]
, Section 1)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a
k-
dimensional lattice with reduced basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
and denote by
B
the matrix with columns
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
.
We define

l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\min_{x\in\mathcal{L}}||x-y||\quad;~~y\not\in\mathcal{L}\\
\min_{0\neq x\in\mathcal{L}}||x||\quad;~~y\in\mathcal{L}\end{array}\right.,

where
||\cdot||
denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. It is well known that, by applying the
LLL–algorithm, it is possible to give in polynomial time a lower bound for
l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)
, namely a positive constant
\delta
such that
l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)\geq\delta
holds (see
[
11
]
, Section V.4).

Lemma 4.4

(
[
11
]

S=4.8\cdot 10^{87}
and
T=6.01\cdot 10^{43}
. Choosing
c_{3}:=216
and
c_{4}:=\log 2
, we get
k/2\leq 296
and so
k\leq 592
. This contradicts our working assumption
k>600
, completing the analysis of the sub-case
p<n^{10}
and
k>600
.

5.2.3
The sub-case
p>n^{10}

In this case, we go back to (
1.1
) and note that since
L_{n}^{(k)}=(p+1)p^{\mathfrak{a}}-1
is always odd, then Lemma
2.1
tells us that

n\equiv r\equiv 1,2\pmod{k+1}.

Now, if
p=2^{\ell}-1
, then

L_{n}^{(k)}=(p+1)p^{\mathfrak{a}}-1=2^{\ell}(2^{\ell}-1)^{\mathfrak{a}}-1\equiv-1\pmod{2^{\ell}},

and if
p=2^{\ell}+1
, then

L_{n}^{(k)}=(p+1)p^{\mathfrak{a}}-1=(2^{\ell}+2)(2^{\ell}+1)^{\mathfrak{a}}-1\equiv 2\cdot 1-1\equiv 1\pmod{2^{\ell}}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle L_{n}^{(k)}\equiv\pm 1\pmod{2^{\ell}}.

(5.10)

On the other hand, since
n\equiv r\equiv 1,2\pmod{k+1}
, then the parts (i) and (ii) of Lemma
3.1
tell us that

\displaystyle L_{n}^{(k)}\equiv(4m+1)(-1)^{m}\pmod{2^{k-1}}\qquad\text{or}\qquad L_{n}^{(k)}\equiv(4m^{2}+6m+3)(-1)^{m}\pmod{2^{k}},

(5.11)

respectively, for all
k>600
. Looking at (
5.10
) and (
5.11
), we obtain that

\displaystyle(4m+1)(-1)^{m}\equiv\pm 1\pmod{2^{\min\{k-1,\ell\}}}\qquad\text{or}\qquad(4m^{2}+6m+3)(-1)^{m}\equiv\pm 1\pmod{2^{\min\{k-1,\ell\}}}.

Thus,
2^{\min\{k-1,\ell\}}
divides one of
4m
,
4m+2
,
4m^{2}+6m+2
or
4m^{2}+6m+4
. All these numbers are non-zero and are less that
10n^{2}
.

If
\min\{k-1,\ell\}:=\ell
, then
2^{\ell}\leq 10n^{2}
. This further implies that
p=2^{\ell}\pm 1<10n^{2}+1
, which is not possible because
p>n^{10}
and
n\geq k+2\geq 4
. Therefore, we always have
\min\{k-1,\ell\}:=k-1
. For this reason,

2^{k-1}<10n^{2}
and hence

\displaystyle k<10\log n\qquad\text{and}\qquad\log k<2\log n.

(5.12)

Also,

\displaystyle 2^{\mathfrak{a}+1}\leq(p+1)p^{\mathfrak{a}}-1=L_{n}^{(k)}<3\cdot 2^{n-2}<4\cdot 2^{n-2}=2^{n},

implies that
\mathfrak{a}<n-1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle p^{\mathfrak{a}}=(2^{\ell}\pm 1)^{\mathfrak{a}}=2^{\mathfrak{a}\ell}\left(1\pm\dfrac{1}{2^{\ell}}\right)^{\mathfrak{a}}.

When
p
is a Fermat prime, we have

2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}<(p+1)p^{\mathfrak{a}}=(2^{\ell}+2)(2^{\ell}+1)^{a}=2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}\left(1+\frac{2}{2^{\ell}}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{2^{\ell}}\right)^{\mathfrak{a}}.

Note that

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{1}{2^{\ell}}\right)^{\mathfrak{a}}<e^{\log(1+1/2^{\ell})\mathfrak{a}}<e^{{\mathfrak{a}}/2^{\ell}}<1+\frac{2{\mathfrak{a}}}{2^{\ell}}<1+\frac{2n}{2^{\ell}},

where we used the fact that
e^{x}<1+2x
for
x\in(0,1/2)
, with
x:={\mathfrak{a}}/2^{\ell}<n/(p-1)\leq n/n^{10}=1/n^{9}<1/2
. Thus,

2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}<(p+1)p^{\mathfrak{a}}<2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}\left(1+\frac{2}{2^{\ell}}\right)\left(1+\frac{2n}{2^{\ell}}\right)<2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}\left(1+\frac{2+2n}{2^{\ell}}+\frac{4n}{2^{2\ell}}\right)<2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}\left(1+\frac{2n+3}{2^{\ell}}\right),

where we used the fact that
2^{\ell}=p-1\geq n^{10}>4n
. In particular,

\left|(p+1)p^{\mathfrak{a}}-2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}\right|<\frac{2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}(2n+3)}{2^{\ell}}.

(5.13)

This was when
p
is a Fermat prime. But the same inequality holds when
p
is a Mersenne prime. Indeed, in this case

\displaystyle 2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle(p+1)p^{\mathfrak{a}}=2^{\ell}(2^{\ell}-1)^{\mathfrak{a}}=2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{\ell}}\right)^{\mathfrak{a}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}e^{\mathfrak{a}\log(1-1/2^{\ell})}>2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}e^{-\mathfrak{a}/2^{\ell-1}}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle 2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}\left(1-\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{2^{\ell-1}}\right),

which implies that

\left|(p+1)p^{\mathfrak{a}}-2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}\right|<\frac{2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}{\mathfrak{a}}}{2^{\ell-1}}<\frac{2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}(2n)}{2^{\ell}},

which is slightly stronger than (
5.13
).

To proceed, we use (
2.7
) to rewrite (
1.1
) as

\displaystyle\left|f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}-2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}-((p+1)p^{\mathfrak{a}}-1)+((p+1)p^{\mathfrak{a}}-1)-2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}-((p+1)p^{\mathfrak{a}}-1)\right|+\left|((p+1)p^{\mathfrak{a}}-1)-2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}-L_{n}^{(k)}\right|+\left|((p+1)p^{\mathfrak{a}}-1)-2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}-L_{n}^{(k)}\right|+\left|(p+1)p^{\mathfrak{a}}-2^{\mathfrak{a}\ell+1}\right|+1

\displaystyle<\dfrac{3}{2}+\dfrac{2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}(2n+3)}{2^{\ell}}+1=\dfrac{5}{2}+\dfrac{2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}(2n+3)}{2^{\ell}}.

Now, dividing through by
2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}
gives

\displaystyle\left|f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}\cdot 2^{-(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}-1\right|

\displaystyle<\dfrac{5}{2\cdot 2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}}+\dfrac{2n+3}{2^{\ell}}<\dfrac{2n+5}{2^{\ell}}\leq\dfrac{2n+5}{p-1}.

(5.14)

Let

\displaystyle\Gamma_{3}:=f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}\cdot 2^{-(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}-1=e^{\Lambda_{3}}-1.

It is clear that
\Lambda_{3}\neq 0
, otherwise we would have

\displaystyle 2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}

\displaystyle=(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}f_{k}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\alpha-1}{2+(k+1)(\alpha-2)}(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}.

We have already showed that conjugating the above relation by some automorphism of the Galois group of the splitting field of
g_{k}(x)
over
\mathbb{Q}
which sends
\alpha
to
\alpha_{i}
for some
i>1
and then taking absolute values, we get that

\displaystyle 2^{21}=2^{(20+1)\cdot 1}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}}=\left|\dfrac{\alpha_{i}-1}{2+(k+1)(\alpha_{i}-2)}(2\alpha_{i}-1)\alpha_{i}^{n-1}\right|

\displaystyle<\dfrac{|\alpha_{i}-1|\cdot|2\alpha_{i}-1|\cdot|\alpha_{i}|^{n-1}}{k-1}\leq\dfrac{2\cdot 3\cdot 1}{k-1}\leq 6,

which is absurd. In the above we used the fact that
n\geq k+2
, so
n\geq 4
and
p>n^{10}
, so
2^{\ell}\geq p-1\geq n^{10}\geq 4^{10}=2^{20}
, so
\ell\geq 20
. So, we can apply Theorem
4.2
to
\Gamma_{3}
. The algebraic number field containing the following
\gamma_{i}
’s is
\mathbb{K}:=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
. We have
D=k
,
t:=3
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle:=2,

\displaystyle\qquad\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle:=\alpha,

\displaystyle\qquad\lambda_{3}

\displaystyle:=(2\alpha-1)f_{k}(\alpha),

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle:=-(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a},

\displaystyle\qquad b_{2}

\displaystyle:=n-1,

\displaystyle\qquad b_{3}

\displaystyle:=1.

Since
h(\gamma_{1})=\log 2
, we take
A_{1}:=k\log 2
. Moreover,
h(\gamma_{2})=(\log\alpha)/k<0.7/k
, so we take
A_{2}:=0.7
.
For
A_{3}
, we first compute

\displaystyle h(\gamma_{3})

\displaystyle:=h\left((2\alpha-1)f_{k}(\alpha)\right)\leq h\left(2\alpha-1\right)+h\left(f_{k}(\alpha)\right)

\displaystyle<2\log 2+\log\alpha+3\log k<6\log k,

for all
k\geq 2
. So, we can take
A_{3}:=6k\log k
. Here, we can take
B:=n^{2}
. Now, by Theorem
4.2
,

\displaystyle\log|\Gamma_{3}|

\displaystyle>-1.4\cdot 30^{6}\cdot 3^{4.5}\cdot k^{2}(1+\log k)(1+\log(n^{2}))\cdot(k\log 2)\cdot 0.7\cdot 6k\log k

\displaystyle>-4\cdot 10^{12}k^{4}(\log k)^{2}\log n.

(5.15)

Comparing (
5.14
) and (
5.2.3
), we get

\displaystyle\log(p-1)

\displaystyle<\log(2n+5)+4\cdot 10^{12}k^{4}(\log k)^{2}\log n

\displaystyle<\log(8n)+4\cdot 10^{12}k^{4}(\log k)^{2}\log n.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\log p

\displaystyle<5\cdot 10^{12}k^{4}(\log k)^{2}\log n.

Using the bounds on
k
from (
5.12
), we obtain

\displaystyle\log p

\displaystyle<2\cdot 10^{17}(\log n)^{7}.

Now, we go back to Lemma
5.2
and obtain

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle<3.6\cdot 10^{15}k^{4}(\log k)^{3}(\log p)^{3}

\displaystyle<3.6\cdot 10^{15}(10\log n)^{4}(2\log n)^{3}\left(2\cdot 10^{17}(\log n)^{7}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle<2\cdot 10^{55}(\log n)^{21}.

We now apply Lemma
4.6
with
x:=n
,
r:=21
,
T:=10^{55}
and obtain

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle<2^{21}\cdot 2\cdot 10^{55}\left(\log 2\cdot 10^{55}\right)^{21}<7\cdot 10^{105}.

Furthermore, we use the bound on
k
from (
5.12
) to obtain

\displaystyle k<10\log n<10\log(7\cdot 10^{105})<2438.

Therefore, since
p>n^{10}
, we always have

\displaystyle n<7\cdot 10^{105}\qquad\text{and}\qquad k<2438.

(5.16)

We now use these bounds to find an absolute bound on
\log p
and hence
\ell
. To do this, we revisit (
5.14
) and note that since
p>n^{10}>4^{10}
for all
n\geq k+2
, we obtain the inequality

\left|e^{\Lambda_{3}}-1\right|=|\Gamma_{3}|<0.5,

which leads to
|\log(1+\Gamma_{3})|<1.5|\Gamma_{3}|
. Thus,

\displaystyle\left|\log\left(f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\right)-(\ell+1)\mathfrak{a}\log 2+(n-1)\log\alpha\right|<\dfrac{1.5(2n+5)}{p-1}<\dfrac{12n}{p-1}.

Now, for each
k\in[2,2438]
, we perform the LLL-algorithm to obtain a lower bound for the smallest nonzero value of the linear form above, constrained by integer coefficients with absolute values not exceeding
n^{2}<5\cdot 10^{211}
. So, we consider the lattice

\mathcal{A^{**}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
\lfloor C\log(1/2)\rfloor&\lfloor C\log\alpha\rfloor&\lfloor C\log\left(f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\right)\rfloor\end{pmatrix},

with
C:=3.75\cdot 10^{635}
and
y:=(0,0,0)
. Applying Lemma
4.4
, we obtain

c_{1}:=10^{-203}\quad\text{and}\quad\delta:=8.2241\cdot 10^{212}.

Using Lemma
4.5
, we get
S=7.5\cdot 10^{423}
and
T=7.51\cdot 10^{211}
. Since
\delta^{2}\geq T^{2}+S
, and selecting
c_{3}:=12n<8.4\cdot 10^{106}
and
c_{4}:=1
, we obtain
\log(p-1)\leq 1219
. Furthermore, since
p=2^{\ell}\pm 1
, we get that
\ell\leq 1760
.

To conclude, we wrote a program in SageMath to find all primes
p
of the form
p=2^{\ell}\pm 1
, with
1\leq\ell\leq 1760
. We obtained a list of 15 primes corresponding to only

\ell=2,3,5,7,13,17,19,31,61,89,107,127,521,607,1279,

for Mersenne primes, and a list of 5 primes corresponding to only

\ell=1,2,4,8,16,

for Fermat primes.

To finish this off, we reduce the bound on
n
from (
5.16
). Since
\ell\leq 1760
, the prime
p
belongs to a fixed finite set of
19
primes. For each such prime
p
and each
k\in[2,2438]
, we revisit (
5.2.1
) and recall that

\left|e^{\Lambda_{1}}-1\right|=|\Gamma_{1}|<\dfrac{5}{\alpha^{n}}<0.5,

for
n\geq 5
, which gives
|\Lambda_{1}|<1.5|\Gamma_{1}|
via (
2.11
). Therefore,

\displaystyle\left|\log\left(\frac{p+1}{(2\alpha-1)f_{k}(\alpha)}\right)+\mathfrak{a}\log p-(n-1)\log\alpha\right|<\frac{7.5}{\alpha^{n}}.

(5.17)

The integer coefficients
\mathfrak{a}
and
n-1
satisfy
|\mathfrak{a}|,|n-1|<n<7\cdot 10^{105}
. For each pair
(k,p)
with
k\in[2,2438]
and
p=2^{\ell}\pm 1
with
\ell\leq 1760
, we apply the LLL-algorithm to obtain a lower bound for the smallest nonzero value of the linear form (
5.17
). We consider the lattice

\mathcal{B}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
\lfloor C\log p\rfloor&\lfloor C\log\alpha\rfloor&\lfloor C\log\left((p+1)/((2\alpha-1)f_{k}(\alpha))\right)\rfloor\end{pmatrix},

with
C:=1.029\cdot 10^{318}
and
y:=(0,0,0)
. Applying Lemma
4.4
, we obtain

c_{1}:=10^{-98}\quad\text{and}\quad\delta:=1.501\cdot 10^{107}.

Using Lemma
4.5
, we conclude that
S=1.47\cdot 10^{212}
and
T=1.051\cdot 10^{106}
. Using
c_{3}:=7.5
and
c_{4}:=\log\alpha
, we get
n\leq 1202
.

Since
n\leq 1202
, the condition
p>n^{10}
forces
p>1202^{10}>6.2\cdot 10^{30}
. Among the
19

Mersenne and Fermat primes with
\ell\leq 1760
, the only ones satisfying this lower bound are those with

\ell\geq 107
, namely

p\in\{2^{107}-1,\ 2^{127}-1,\ 2^{521}-1,\ 2^{607}-1,\ 2^{1279}-1\}.

We then use SageMath to find all solutions to (
1.1
) with
k\in[2,2438]
,
n\in[k+2,1202]
, primes
p=2^{\ell}\pm 1
restricted to these five values, and
\mathfrak{a}\in[1,n]
. We find no solutions in this range.
This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
. ∎
