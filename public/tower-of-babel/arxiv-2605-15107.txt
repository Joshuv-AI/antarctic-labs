1.
Introduction

We use the standard
q
-series notation as for instance in
[
5
, Chapter 1]

(a;q)_{0}:=1,\qquad(a;q)_{n}:=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1-aq^{j}),\qquad(a;q)_{\infty}:=\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}(1-aq^{j}),

and

(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r};q)_{n}:=(a_{1};q)_{n}\cdots(a_{r};q)_{n}.

For a series
F(q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}A(n)q^{n}
, put

F(q)\big|\mathcal{S}_{N,r}:=\sum_{n\equiv r\,({\rm mod}\,N)}A(n)q^{n}.

The present authors studied the series

S(q):=\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{(q;q^{2})_{m}^{2}q^{2m}}{(-q^{2};q^{2})_{m}}

in connection with two-color partitions with odd smallest part
[
3
]
. The present authors conjectured a Hecke-type formula for the even part of
(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}S(q)
. Banerjee and Bringmann proved this conjecture by completing both sides to modular objects and then using Sturm’s theorem
[
4
]
. At the end of their paper they asked whether a more direct proof could be found, for example by using Bailey pairs, and they also suggested studying the odd residue classes.

The main result is a two-variable refinement. Let
a
be an indeterminate, or a nonzero complex number, and define

(1)

S_{a}(q):=\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{(aq;q^{2})_{m}(q/a;q^{2})_{m}q^{2m}}{(-q^{2};q^{2})_{m}},\qquad P_{a}(q):=(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}S_{a}(q).

Thus
S_{1}(q)=S(q)
.

The following theorem gives the two parity parts of
P_{a}(q)
.

Theorem 1
.

For
|q|<1
,

(2)

P_{a}(q)\big|\mathcal{S}_{2,0}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}q^{6n^{2}+4n}(1+q^{4n+2})\sum_{j=-n}^{n}(-1)^{j}a^{2j}q^{-2j^{2}},

and

(3)

P_{a}(q)\big|\mathcal{S}_{2,1}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n+1}q^{6n^{2}+10n+3}(1+q^{4n+4})\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}\big(a^{2j+1}+a^{-2j-1}\big)q^{-2j(j+1)}.

We first record the basic symmetries in the parameter.

Remark 1
.

From (
1
),

S_{a^{-1}}(q)=S_{a}(q),\qquad P_{a^{-1}}(q)=P_{a}(q),

and

S_{-a}(q)=S_{a}(-q),\qquad P_{-a}(q)=P_{a}(-q).

Thus replacing
a
by
-a
leaves the even part unchanged and changes the sign of the odd part. The specializations
a
and
a^{-1}
are identical.

Putting
a=1
gives the two identities originally wanted for
S(q)
. The first is the even Hecke-type identity.

Corollary 2
.

For
|q|<1
,

(4)

(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}S(q)\big|\mathcal{S}_{2,0}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}q^{6n^{2}+4n}(1+q^{4n+2})\sum_{|j|\leq n}(-1)^{j}q^{-2j^{2}}.

The second is the corresponding odd identity.

Corollary 3
.

For
|q|<1
,

(5)

(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}S(q)\big|\mathcal{S}_{2,1}=2\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n+1}q^{6n^{2}+10n+3}(1+q^{4n+4})\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}q^{-2j(j+1)}.

The odd identity separates the two odd residue classes modulo
4
.

Corollary 4
.

We have

(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}S(q)\big|\mathcal{S}_{4,1}=0,\qquad(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}S(q)\big|\mathcal{S}_{4,3}=(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}S(q)\big|\mathcal{S}_{2,1}.

Equivalently, if

S_{r,N}:=S(q)\big|\mathcal{S}_{N,r},

then

(6)

S_{1,4}=0,\qquad S_{3,4}=S_{1,2}.

The specialization
a=i
gives a companion whose odd part vanishes.

Corollary 5
.

Let
i^{2}=-1
, and set

T(q):=\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{(-q^{2};q^{4})_{m}q^{2m}}{(-q^{2};q^{2})_{m}}.

Then

(7)

(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}T(q)\big|\mathcal{S}_{2,1}=0,

and

(8)

(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}T(q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}q^{6n^{2}+4n}(1+q^{4n+2})\sum_{j=-n}^{n}q^{-2j^{2}}.

The primitive sixth-root specialization gives another finite-theta companion.

Corollary 6
.

Let
\rho=e^{\pi i/3}
and put

C_{r}:=\rho^{r}+\rho^{-r}=2\cos(\pi r/3)\qquad(r\in\mathbb{Z}).

Also set

U(q):=\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{(-q^{3};q^{6})_{m}q^{2m}}{(-q;q^{2})_{m}(-q^{2};q^{2})_{m}}.

Then

(9)

(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}U(q)\big|\mathcal{S}_{2,0}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}q^{6n^{2}+4n}(1+q^{4n+2})\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{n}C_{j}q^{-2j^{2}}\right),

and

(10)

(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}U(q)\big|\mathcal{S}_{2,1}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n+1}q^{6n^{2}+10n+3}(1+q^{4n+4})\sum_{j=0}^{n}C_{j-1}q^{-2j(j+1)}.

The coefficients
C_{j}
are periodic of period
6
:

C_{0}=2,\quad C_{1}=1,\quad C_{2}=-1,\quad C_{3}=-2,\quad C_{4}=-1,\quad C_{5}=1.

The primitive cubic-root specialization gives the complementary cyclotomic companion.

Corollary 7
.

Let
\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}
and put

D_{r}:=2\cos(\pi r/3)\qquad(r\in\mathbb{Z}).

Also set

V(q):=\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{(q^{3};q^{6})_{m}q^{2m}}{(q;q^{2})_{m}(-q^{2};q^{2})_{m}}.

Then

(11)

(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}V(q)\big|\mathcal{S}_{2,0}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}q^{6n^{2}+4n}(1+q^{4n+2})\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{n}D_{j}q^{-2j^{2}}\right),

and

(12)

(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}V(q)\big|\mathcal{S}_{2,1}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n+1}q^{6n^{2}+10n+3}(1+q^{4n+4})\sum_{j=0}^{n}D_{j+2}q^{-2j(j+1)}.

The coefficients
D_{j}
are periodic of period
6
:

D_{0}=2,\quad D_{1}=1,\quad D_{2}=-1,\quad D_{3}=-2,\quad D_{4}=-1,\quad D_{5}=1.

For reference, we recall the form of Bailey’s transform that will be used; see Andrews
[
2
, Chapter 3]
and Warnaar
[
7
]
for background on Bailey pairs, Bailey’s lemma, and conjugate Bailey pairs. A pair
(\alpha_{R},\beta_{N})_{R,N\geq 0}
is a Bailey pair relative to
1
, with base
q
, if

(13)

\beta_{N}=\sum_{R=0}^{N}\frac{\alpha_{R}}{(q;q)_{N-R}(q;q)_{N+R}}.

A conjugate Bailey pair
(\gamma_{R},\delta_{N})_{R,N\geq 0}
, with the same base, satisfies

(14)

\gamma_{R}=\sum_{N=R}^{\infty}\frac{\delta_{N}}{(q;q)_{N-R}(q;q)_{N+R}}.

Then Bailey’s transform says that

(15)

\sum_{N\geq 0}\beta_{N}\delta_{N}=\sum_{R\geq 0}\alpha_{R}\gamma_{R},

whenever the sums converge.

2.
Two elementary lemmas

The
q
-binomial coefficient is defined by
[
1
, Definition 1]

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{M}{K}_{q}:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{(q;q)_{M}}{(q;q)_{K}(q;q)_{M-K}},&0\leq K\leq M,\\[6.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We shall need the finite
q
-binomial theorem
[
1
, Eq. (3.3.6)]

(16)

(z;q)_{m}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^{j}q^{j(j-1)/2}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{m}{j}_{q}z^{j}

and Euler’s expansion
[
1
, Eq. (2.2.6)]

(17)

(z;q)_{\infty}=\sum_{v\geq 0}\frac{(-1)^{v}q^{v(v-1)/2}z^{v}}{(q;q)_{v}}.

In the proof below, Euler’s expansion will be used in the special form

(18)

(-zq^{k+1};q)_{\infty}=\sum_{v\geq 0}\frac{z^{v}q^{v(k+1)+v(v-1)/2}}{(q;q)_{v}}.

The following lemma can be seen as a finite form of Euler’s pentagonal theorem.

Lemma 1
.

For
N\geq 0
, let

(19)

D_{N}(q):=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}(-1)^{j}q^{j(3j+1-2N)/2}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{N-j}{j}_{q}.

Then

(20)

D_{N}(q)=\begin{cases}(-1)^{s}q^{-s(3s-1)/2},&N=3s,\\[3.0pt]
(-1)^{s}q^{-s(3s+1)/2},&N=3s+1,\\[3.0pt]
0,&N=3s+2.\end{cases}

Proof.

The initial values are

D_{0}(q)=1,\qquad D_{1}(q)=1,\qquad D_{2}(q)=0.

We shall prove that, for
N\geq 3
,

(21)

D_{N}(q)=-q^{2-N}D_{N-3}(q).

The formula (
20
) follows at once by iterating this recurrence.

We use the elementary
q
-Pascal identities
[
1
, Eqs. (3.3.3)-(3.3.4)]

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{M}{K}_{q}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{M-1}{K}_{q}+q^{M-K}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{M-1}{K-1}_{q}

and

(1-q^{K})\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{M}{K}_{q}=(1-q^{M-K+1})\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{M}{K-1}_{q}.

Applying the first identity to
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{N-j}{j}_{q}
gives two terms. In the
second term, the
j=0
contribution is zero; after replacing
j
by
j+1
, that
term becomes

-\sum_{j}(-1)^{j}q^{j(3j+3-2N)/2}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{N-2-j}{j}_{q}.

Hence

D_{N}(q)=\sum_{j}(-1)^{j}q^{j(3j+1-2N)/2}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{N-1-j}{j}_{q}-\sum_{j}(-1)^{j}q^{j(3j+3-2N)/2}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{N-2-j}{j}_{q}.

Apply the first
q
-Pascal identity once more to the first sum. The part containing

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{N-2-j}{j}_{q}
combines with the second sum and gives a factor
1-q^{j}
.
Using the second displayed identity and then shifting
j
down by one, we obtain

D_{N}(q)=-q^{2-N}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor(N-3)/2\rfloor}(-1)^{j}q^{j(3j+1-2(N-3))/2}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{N-3-j}{j}_{q}.

The sum on the right is
D_{N-3}(q)
. This proves (
21
), and hence the lemma.
∎

Lemma 2
.

For
|q|<1
,

(22)

\displaystyle(q;q)_{\infty}\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{q^{k}(z^{2}q^{k+1};q)_{k}(-zq^{k+1};q)_{\infty}}{(q;q)_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\geq 0}(-1)^{s}z^{3s}q^{3s^{2}+2s}(1+zq^{2s+1}).

Proof.

Let the left side of (
22
) be
L(z)
. By (
16
), with the variable there replaced by
z^{2}q^{k+1}
, and by Euler’s expansion in the form (
18
),

(z^{2}q^{k+1};q)_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j}z^{2j}q^{j(k+1)+j(j-1)/2}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k}{j}_{q},

and

(-zq^{k+1};q)_{\infty}=\sum_{v\geq 0}\frac{z^{v}q^{v(k+1)+v(v-1)/2}}{(q;q)_{v}}.

Substitution, followed by the change of variables
k=j+\ell
, gives

L(z)=\sum_{j,v\geq 0}(-1)^{j}z^{2j+v}q^{(3j^{2}+3j+v^{2}+2jv+v)/2}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{j+v}{j}_{q}.

Thus

(23)

[z^{N}]L(z)=q^{N(N+1)/2}D_{N}(q).

By Lemma
1
, the only nonzero coefficients of
L(z)
are

[z^{3s}]L(z)=(-1)^{s}q^{3s^{2}+2s},\qquad[z^{3s+1}]L(z)=(-1)^{s}q^{3s^{2}+4s+1}.

This is exactly (
22
).
∎

3.
Proof of the two-variable theorem

We use the following special case of MacMahon’s finite form of Jacobi’s triple product
[
6
, p. 75]
:

(24)

(zq;q)_{M}(z^{-1};q)_{N}=\sum_{j=-N}^{M}(-1)^{j}q^{j(j+1)/2}z^{j}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{M+N}{N+j}_{q}.

Taking
M=N
and
z=a/q
in (
24
) gives

(25)

(a;q)_{N}(q/a;q)_{N}=\sum_{j=-N}^{N}(-1)^{j}a^{j}q^{j(j-1)/2}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{2N}{N+j}_{q}.

Now apply (
25
) with
q
replaced by
q^{2}
and with
a
there replaced by
aq
. This gives

(26)

(aq;q^{2})_{N}(q/a;q^{2})_{N}=\sum_{h=-N}^{N}(-1)^{h}a^{h}q^{h^{2}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{2N}{N+h}_{q^{2}}.

After division by
(q^{2};q^{2})_{2N}
this says that

(27)

\beta_{N}(a):=\frac{(aq;q^{2})_{N}(q/a;q^{2})_{N}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{2N}}

is a Bailey pair relative to
1
, with
q
replaced by
q^{2}
in (
13

(28)

\alpha_{0}(a)=1,\qquad\alpha_{R}(a)=(-1)^{R}q^{R^{2}}\big(a^{R}+a^{-R}\big)\quad(R\geq 1).

The conjugate pair used with it is obtained from

(29)

\delta_{N}=(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}q^{2N}\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{2N}}{(-q^{2};q^{2})_{N}}.

For
R\geq 0
set

\displaystyle\gamma_{R}

\displaystyle=\sum_{N=R}^{\infty}\frac{\delta_{N}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{N-R}(q^{2};q^{2})_{N+R}}

(30)

\displaystyle=q^{2R}(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{q^{2k}(q^{4R+2k+2};q^{2})_{k}(-q^{2R+2k+2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{k}}.

Here we used

(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}=(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}(-q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}.

Lemma
2
, with
q
replaced by
q^{2}
and
z=q^{2R}

(31)

\gamma_{R}=q^{2R}\sum_{s\geq 0}(-1)^{s}q^{6s^{2}+(6R+4)s}(1+q^{2R+4s+2}).

Thus Lemma

is the evaluation of the conjugate Bailey pair.

Since

P_{a}(q)=\sum_{N\geq 0}\beta_{N}(a)\delta_{N},

Bailey’s transform (
15
), with
q

q^{2}

\displaystyle P_{a}(q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{R\geq 0}\alpha_{R}(a)\gamma_{R}

This is the basic identity from which both parity parts follow.

Since (
31

q
, the term
q^{h^{2}}\gamma_{|h|}
has the same parity as
h
. Therefore the even part of (
32
) is obtained by putting

(33)

P_{a}(q)\big|\mathcal{S}_{2,0}=\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}}a^{2r}q^{4r^{2}}\gamma_{2|r|}.

For

, (

q^{4r^{2}}\gamma_{2r}=q^{4r^{2}+4r}\sum_{s\geq 0}(-1)^{s}q^{12rs+6s^{2}+4s}(1+q^{4r+4s+2}).

Putting

(34)

q^{4r^{2}}\gamma_{2r}=(-1)^{r}q^{-2r^{2}}\sum_{n\geq r}(-1)^{n}q^{6n^{2}+4n}(1+q^{4n+2})\qquad(r\geq 0).

The same formula with
r

P_{a}(q)\big|\mathcal{S}_{2,0}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}q^{6n^{2}+4n}(1+q^{4n+2})\sum_{|r|\leq n}(-1)^{r}a^{2r}q^{-2r^{2}},

For the odd part, put

P_{a}(q)\big|\mathcal{S}_{2,1}=-\sum_{r\geq 0}\big(a^{2r+1}+a^{-2r-1}\big)q^{(2r+1)^{2}}\gamma_{2r+1}.

From (

Now put

3

4.
Concluding comments

The proof gives a direct
q
-series answer to Banerjee and Bringmann’s question. MacMahon’s finite Jacobi identity supplies the Bailey pair, and Lemma
2
evaluates the conjugate Bailey pair. The Bailey transform then gives the two-variable identity in one step. The specialization
a=1
gives the original even Hecke-type identity and the odd identity. The odd identity shows that one odd residue class vanishes and that the other is the whole odd part.

The parameter
a
records the Jacobi index. This explains why the even part contains the Laurent powers
a^{2j}
, whereas the odd part contains the paired powers
a^{2j+1}+a^{-2j-1}
. The symmetry
P_{-a}(q)=P_{a}(-q)
shows that replacing
a
by
-a
only changes the sign of the odd part. The case
a=i
is especially simple: the paired odd weight vanishes, and so the whole odd part is zero. The specializations
a=e^{\pi i/3}
and
a=e^{2\pi i/3}
give two cyclotomic companions, with periodic coefficients
2\cos(\pi j/3)
in the finite theta sums.
