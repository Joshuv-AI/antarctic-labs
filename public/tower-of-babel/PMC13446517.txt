Fetal Brain Abnormalities Following Laser Surgery for Twin‐to‐Twin Transfusion Syndrome: Imaging Patterns and Evolution
Authors: Yada Kunpalin, Elka Miller, Ingrid Larsen, Kamini Raghuram, Catherine Windrim, Nimrah Abbasi, Greg Ryan, Tim Van Mieghem, Shiri Shinar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionSevere brain injury has been reported following fetoscopic laser ablation (FLA) for twin‐to‐twin transfusion syndrome (TTTS), but imaging patterns and temporal evolution remain poorly defined. This study aimed to characterize the progression of severe brain injury after FLA.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective case series of TTTS pregnancies with severe brain injury identified after FLA at the Ontario Fetal Center (June 2023–March 2025). FLA was performed for Quintero stage ≥ II and selected stage I cases. Severe abnormalities were defined as severe intraventricular haemorrhage or destructive lesions. Serial ultrasound and fetal MRI were reviewed and categorized as early (< 4 weeks) or late (≥ 4 weeks) post‐FLA.ResultsFifteen fetuses from 14 pregnancies were identified. Mean gestational age at FLA was 19.7 ± 3.2 weeks; 66.7% were stage III–IV and 60% were donor twins. Early imaging (n = 8) most commonly showed periventricular haemorrhagic infarction (62.5%) and cerebellar haemorrhage (25.0%). Late imaging (n = 13) predominantly demonstrated cerebral volume loss (76.9%), often involving the parietal lobes, with polymicrogyria (38.5%) and Wallerian degeneration (30.8%). Findings remained unchanged after 4 weeks.ConclusionBrain injury after FLA evolves from acute haemorrhagic/ischaemic insults to irreversible destructive changes. These preliminary findings suggest a consistent pattern of injury that warrants further prospective study.

Introduction

Twin‐to‐Twin Transfusion Syndrome (TTTS) is a significant complication occurring in 10%–15% of monochorionic‐diamniotic twin pregnancies. It results from an imbalance in blood flow between the twins due to placental vascular anastomoses, causing significant circulatory disturbances [1]. If untreated, TTTS is associated with very high perinatal morbidity and mortality, with severe cases reaching 70%–100% mortality for both twins [1]. The standard treatment for TTTS is fetoscopic laser ablation (FLA) of the placental vascular anastomoses, which has been shown to improve survival and reduce complications compared to serial amnioreduction [2]. Although FLA significantly lowers the incidence of brain injury compared to amnioreduction [3], brain injury has still been reported in approximately 2% of fetuses and 5% of neonates [4, 5]. Neurodevelopmental impairment occurs in approximately 14% of TTTS survivors treated with FLA [6]. While much of this impairment is attributed to prematurity, a proportion may occur prenatally following FLA [5].

Severe brain injury can stem from vascular events occurring before, during and/or after the FLA procedure. Haemodynamic changes can lead to inadequate blood flow, ischaemia, reperfusion damage or haemorrhage due to the fragility of cerebral blood vessels [5]. Consequently, both ischemic and haemorrhagic brain injuries may occur [4]. However, limited data are available regarding the timing, pattern or evolution of severe brain injury following FLA for TTTS—an essential gap that hinders optimal planning and timing of neuroimaging, identification of relevant findings and understanding of the natural history of such injuries. This study aims to characterize the patterns and evolution of any severe brain injuries that were detected following FLA treatment.

Materials and Methods

We report a case series of TTTS cases that were treated with FLA at the Ontario Fetal Center, (OFC) between June 2023 and March 2025 and were subsequently noted to exhibit severe brain abnormalities. This study period was selected because a dedicated neurosonography follow‐up clinic for post‐FLA pregnancies was established. The OFC is a joint program between the Fetal Medicine Unit and Neonatal Unit at Mount Sinai Hospital and the Hospital for Sick Children in Toronto. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Boards at Mount Sinai Hospital (REB# 17–0087‐C) and the Hospital for Sick Children (REB# 1000080667).

FLA was offered for TTTS cases at Quintero stage II or greater, and for stage I cases with symptomatic polyhydramnios (short cervix or regular contractions) or progression. All procedures were performed using a diode laser (Dornier MedTech GmbH, Weßling, Germany) to coagulate all communicating placental anastomoses, followed by ablation of the entire vascular equator (Solomon technique). For this study, we only included cases that had a follow‐up neuroimaging at our institution that were identified with severe brain abnormalities either prenatally or early postnatally, provided the injury was considered to have occurred before birth. This included white matter injury or cerebral volume loss detected on early postnatal imaging (< 3 weeks). IVH that was only identified after birth was excluded [7]. Severe brain injury included intraventricular haemorrhage (IVH) grade III and periventricular haemorrhagic infarction (PVHI) (formerly grade IV IVH) [8], diffuse ischaemic white matter changes, periventricular leukomalacia (PVL), encephalomalacia, post‐migrational polygyria, schizencephaly, porencephaly, cortical volume loss, cerebellar hypoplasia affecting one or both lobes and microphthalmia. PVHI was characterized by hyperechogenic areas in the periventricular white matter associated with ipsilateral IVH, frequently extending into the adjacent parenchyma [8]. PVL appeared as focal cystic lesions in the periventricular white matter on ultrasound, with diffuse white matter injury and gliosis on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Encephalomalacia was identified as areas of brain softening and parenchymal loss, typically seen as hyperintense regions on T2‐weighted MRI sequences. Post‐migrational polygyria was defined by multiple abnormally small, malformed gyri. Schizencephaly was characterized by clefts extending from the ventricular surface to the cortical surface, with the clefts lined by dysplastic gray matter, while porencephaly involved fluid‐filled cystic cavities within the cerebral hemispheres communicating with the ventricles. Wallerian degeneration was diagnosed when volume loss or signal changes along affected white matter tracts were seen, particularly in the corticospinal pathway (cerebral peduncle) on MRI [9]. Vermian and cerebellar hypoplasia were defined by a vermian height or trans‐cerebellar diameter (TCD) measuring < 5th percentile for gestational age (GA) [10, 11, 12]. Microphthalmia was diagnosed when the orbital diameter of one eye was < 10th percentile for GA [13]. Cortical volume loss was identified by reduced cortical thickness. We excluded cases with less severe isolated IVH limited to the germinal matrix or confined to the lateral ventricles, without associated ventriculomegaly (grade I and II IVH), as well as cases with evident pre‐existing brain injury prior to FLA.

Collected maternal and fetal variables included parity, GA at TTTS diagnosis, FLA treatment and brain injury diagnosis, fetal weight, TTTS stage at intervention, umbilical artery (UA), middle cerebral artery (MCA), and ductus venosus Doppler ultrasound findings just prior to FLA. Cardiac abnormalities in the recipient twin, such as tricuspid regurgitation or functional pulmonary atresia, were also recorded. Post‐operative assessment included ultrasound on day one after FLA and weekly surveillance for four consecutive weeks, followed by routine biweekly surveillance, as for all MCDA twins, thereafter. Obstetric complications related to FLA were documented, including incomplete laser ablation, recurrent TTTS, twin anemia‐polycythaemia sequence (TAPS), selective fetal growth restriction (sFGR), single or double intrauterine fetal demise (sIUFD/dIUFD), selective fetal reduction, preterm pre‐labour rupture of membranes (PPROM), GA and mode of delivery.

Fetal neurosonography was routinely scheduled between 28 and 30 weeks' GA, unless injury was suspected on screening assessment earlier. MRI was obtained (within 1 week) whenever abnormalities were suspected on brain US (either during routine weekly TTTS follow‐up) or during dedicated neurosonography or when neurosonography was incomplete/suboptimal due to technical limitations. All neurosonography examinations were performed using a GE Voluson E10 system or expert 22 (GE Healthcare, Zipf, Austria), utilizing either 2D or 3D transabdominal or transvaginal probes, depending on fetal presentation. Imaging followed the International Society of Ultrasound in Obstetrics & Gynecology (ISUOG) guidelines, with acquisition in three orthogonal planes [14, 15]. All digitally stored images were independently reviewed by two fetal neurosonographers (Y.K. and S.S). Fetal brain MRI was performed using a 3.0 T MAGNETOM Skyra scanner (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). Imaging consisted of a single‐shot fast spin‐echo T2‐weighted sequence obtained in three orthogonal planes, diffusion weighted images in two planes, axial T1 and SWI to the brain. The slice thickness was 3–4 mm. All fetal and neonatal MRIs were reviewed by a senior pediatric neuroradiologist (E.M. with 25 years of experience in fetal imaging). Imaging was categorized as early (< 4 weeks post‐FLA) or late (≥ 4 weeks post‐FLA), regardless of GA at assessment, to assess the progression of brain injury. Findings from the first and last abnormal scans were documented for both ultrasound and MRI.

Postnatal ultrasound was performed as per routine NICU protocol for neonates with a history of TTTS treated by FLA who were born at < 32 weeks or with a birth weight < 1500 g. Cranial ultrasound was conducted through the anterior and mastoid fontanelles using standard protocols. When suspicious brain abnormalities were identified on postnatal ultrasound, postnatal brain MRI was performed. MRI sequences included standard T1‐and T2‐weighted, DWI and SWI imaging.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages (n, %). Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data, or as median with interquartile range (IQR) for non‐normally distributed data. Data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov‐Smirnov test. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.3.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

During the study period, 162 FLA procedures were performed (327 fetuses)(Figure 1). Fifteen fetuses (4.6%) from 14 pregnant women had significant brain abnormalities which were identified after FLA. Among the 15 cases, complete pre‐FLA neurosonography was feasible in 3/15 cases (20%). In the remaining 12 cases, complete pre‐FLA neurosonography was not feasible due to technical limitations inherent to imaging the stuck donor twin and/or severe polyhydramnios in the recipient twin, limiting the ability to perform transvaginal neurosonography. However, standard axial cranial planes were obtained in these cases and demonstrated no evidence of brain abnormalities prior to the procedure. The mean GA at FLA was 19.7 ± 3.2 weeks. The majority of cases had TTTS stage 3 or 4 (66.7%, 10/15) and 60% (9/15) involved the donor twins. Four cases (26.7%) had post‐operative complications after FLA including IUFD of the co‐twin immediately after FLA (n = 1), incomplete laser ablation and subsequent TAPS (n = 1). Two cases later developed sFGR with persistent absent end diastolic velocity (AEDV) in the umbilical artery. PPROM (< 34 weeks GA) occurred in three pregnancies (20%). Selective fetal reduction post FLA was performed in 26.7% (4/15) cases and all expectantly managed cases resulted in preterm birth with a mean GA at delivery of 30.7 ± 3.7 weeks. Late neuroimaging (performed > 4 weeks after FLA) was available for 13 patients, with a median interval of 51 days following the procedure (IQR 42–76 days). The median GA at the time of assessment was 27.4 weeks (IQR 25.6–31.3 weeks). Five of eight patients who had early neuroimaging underwent repeat imaging during this later period; the median interval between scans was 40 days (IQR 29–48 days). Of these repeat studies, three were performed postnatally and two prenatally. The timing of these follow‐ups corresponded to a median post‐menstrual age of 33.7 weeks (IQR 30.1–36.9 weeks). The most common abnormality observed during this period was cerebral parenchymal volume loss (76.9%, 10/13), predominantly involving the parietal lobes (80.0%, 8/10), followed by the frontal and occipital lobes (30%, 3/10 each). Consistent with a destructive process, post‐migrational polygyria and Wallerian degeneration were identified in 38.5% (n = 5) and 30.8% (n = 4) of the patients, respectively. Additional destructive changes that were noted were schizencephaly (23.1%, n = 3) and porencephaly (7.7%, n = 1). Notably, bilateral cerebellar volume loss, and ocular anomalies (hyperechogenic lens and microphthalmia) were each observed in two cases (15.4%). Ventriculomegaly was only observed in one case (7.7%). We identified two cases in the late prenatal period with recent prenatal haemorrhage: one with IVH and one with subpial haemorrhage. Both occurred in patients with sFGR with significant weight discordance and in both cases. AEDV in the umbilical arteries had newly developed 1–3 weeks before brain abnormalities were first identified (Table 2).

Among cases with both early and late neurosonography (n = 5), initial findings typically reflected a haemorrhagic and/or ischemic insult such as PVHI or cerebellar haemorrhage progressing to more extensive destructive changes later, characterized by cerebral parenchymal or cerebellar hemispheric volume loss in the late period. In cases with available repeat late neurosonography (n = 5), the majority of the findings, including parenchymal volume loss, post‐migrational polygyria, schizencephaly and porencephaly (80%, 4/5), remained stable over time, showing neither improvement nor progression, even postnatally. In one case, however, subpial haemorrhage and grade 1 IVH were observed during the late period after FLA, with progression of the latter to cystic PVL documented 41 days later.

Discussion

Principle Findings

In this cohort of pregnancies treated with FLA for TTTS, in which severe brain abnormalities were identified following the procedure, early neuroimaging most commonly revealed PVHI and cerebellar haemorrhage, suggestive of an acute haemorrhagic or ischemic insult. These early findings often progressed, as expected, to destructive changes, including parenchymal and cerebellar volume loss, as was observed on late imaging. Cerebral parenchymal volume loss, particularly involving the parietal lobes, was the most prevalent late finding, often accompanied by other destructive changes such as post‐migrational polygyria, Wallerian degeneration, schizencephaly, and porencephaly. Notably, ventriculomegaly was not commonly observed in these cases. In a subset with longitudinal assessments, most abnormalities remained unchanged.

Prior Studies

A recent systematic review estimated the overall incidence of brain injury following FLA for TTTS to be approximately 2%, although there was substantial heterogeneity among the included studies [4]. In our series the incidence is approximately 4.6%. The most commonly reported injuries were ischaemic lesions (30.4%), followed by destructive lesions (23.9%), ventriculomegaly (19.9%), and haemorrhagic lesions (15.3%) [4]. Another recent study categorized lesions into diffuse and focal types, reporting severe volume loss and ventriculomegaly in 17% as the most common diffuse injury, and IVH in 34% as the most frequent focal lesion [5]. In most studies, fetal brain MRI was performed at a standard GA window between 28 and 32 weeks; however, the interval between FLA and imaging was often unspecified, limiting knowledge about onset and progression of the injury [16, 17, 18]. The majority of fetuses with brain abnormalities in our cohort had TTTS Quintero stage III or IV. Several cases also developed additional monochorionic complications after FLA, including, TAPS and sFGR. Recurrent TTTS, post‐laser TAPS and inter‐twin weight discordance have been previously identified as risk factors for brain injury, likely due to their contribution to ongoing or worsening haemodynamic instability [5, 16, 17].

In our cohort, the majority of early brain abnormalities began with PVHI, followed by cortical volume loss with associated destructive lesions. The identification of PVHI and its sequelae suggests that haemodynamic disturbances may underlie these injuries. Fetal hemodynamic instability before, during or shortly after laser surgery may disrupt the pressure‐passive nature of cerebral circulation, contributing to vascular injury—mechanisms analogous to those seen in extremely preterm neonates [19, 20]. PVHI starts as hemorrhage in the germinal matrix. This region, located beneath the ependymal lining of the lateral ventricles, is highly vascularized with many neural progenitor cells of high metabolic demands and few stabilizing pericytes. Thus, it is particularly vulnerable to injury [21, 22]. In preterm neonates, IVH commonly occurs within the first 48–72 h following a haemodynamic insult or after birth, with PVHI typically evolving within the first week [23, 24]. Consistent with this timeline, PVHI was the earliest and most common brain abnormality post‐FLA detected in our cohort. As expected, this injury progressed to more significant parenchymal volume loss, cortical dysplasia and cystic degeneration. Furthermore, PVHI may lead to Wallerian degeneration, characterized by downstream axonal and myelin loss distal to the site of injury [25, 26]. In this setting, the corticospinal tract is particularly affected, with downstream thalamic and cerebral peduncle volume loss often visible on neuroimaging, reflecting disruption of thalamocortical pathways14. In both preterm infants and our cohort (n = 5), the parietal lobes were most commonly involved, followed by the frontal and occipital lobes [26]. The predominance of parietal lobe involvement likely reflects the venous anatomy of the immature periventricular white matter. The peritrigonal region contains a dense network of medullary veins that drain into the germinal matrix, making it particularly susceptible to venous congestion and subsequent haemorrhagic infarction [27]. Recently, van Oldenmark et al. highlighted perinatal arterial ischemic stroke as a potentially underrecognize neurological complication in TTTS survivors [28]. In our cohort, early neuroimaging most demonstrated PVHI and cerebellar haemorrhage, which later evolved into destructive sequelae. Overt arterial ischemic stroke was identified in only one case, which may reflect differences in imaging timing.

Similar to previous studies, two cases of cerebellar haemorrhage resulting in hypoplasia and dysplasia were also observed in our cohort [5, 16, 17]. Cerebellar haemorrhage can be recognized as a complication of IVH involving the fourth ventricle and it is also a well‐known cause of cerebellar hypoplasia [8]. In neonates, cerebellar haemorrhage is primarily linked to extreme prematurity and low birth weight. However, compromised hemodynamic stability is another significant risk factor [29]. The fetal cerebellum is especially vulnerable to haemorrhage due to its immature and highly vascular structure, particularly within the proliferative external granular layer [30]. Rapid cerebellar growth in late gestation increases metabolic demand, making it more susceptible to injury during hypoxia, anemia, or hemodynamic instability. Additionally, cerebral autoregulation is underdeveloped in premature infants and may be particularly deficient in the cerebellum [31]. Ybarra et al. highlighted that worsening hypotension in extremely premature neonates, especially those requiring inotropes, is associated with larger cerebellar haemorrhage volumes [32]. We hypothesize that hemodynamic instability may be the primary cause of cerebellar haemorrhage and hypoplasia in cases of TTTS following FLA therapy.

Interestingly, two fetuses in our cohort were identified with unilateral microphthalmia and corneal opacity (one recipient and one donor). To date, only two case reports have described ocular anomalies, specifically Peters' anomaly, in association with TTTS, both with and without fetoscopic laser intervention [33, 34]. This condition arises from disruption in the normal development of the anterior segment of the eye, influenced by a combination of genetic predisposition and environmental insults. In both reported cases, it was hypothesized that prolonged fetal anemia and hypotension, beginning early in gestation, may have impaired normal corneal and lens development [33, 34]. In our cohort, the presence of widespread cerebral injury alongside ocular anomalies raises the possibility of a thromboembolic or haemorrhagic event occurring pre‐ or peri‐operatively, potentially interfering with both cerebral and ocular development.

Clinical Implications

Significant brain abnormalities following FLA can, in some cases, be detected as early as the first week post‐procedure with careful ultrasound surveillance, although fairly uncommon. PVHI is often the earliest identifiable lesion. Cerebellar haemorrhage is another important early finding. Notably, ventriculomegaly was not commonly observed in this cohort, emphasizing the need for detailed assessment of the ventricular walls and surrounding brain parenchyma in the early post‐procedure period. These focal lesions may later evolve into diffuse volume loss, especially involving the parietal lobes, as well as other destructive changes, such as cerebellar volume loss. More rarely, ocular anomalies, microphthalmia, may present. These findings typically become apparent from 4 weeks after the procedure, even at GAs < 28 weeks, and generally remain unchanged. Thus, imaging performed from 2 to 4 weeks post‐FLA is likely to capture the end‐stage pattern of brain injury. However, we also identified two cases of late‐onset haemorrhage in association with sFGR and newly developed AEDV of the umbilical artery after FLA, suggesting that additional complications may contribute to delated brain injury [35]. Thus, continued neuroimaging surveillance may therefore be warranted in these cases.

Research Implications

Future studies should explore the potential contribution of perioperative hemodynamic, cardiac disturbances and placental contribution to the development of cerebral injury. A better understanding of these associations may help refine surgical approaches and mitigate the risk of brain injury during and after FLA.

Strength and Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, its case series design comes with unavoidable disadvantages, including missing data, small sample size, and variability in imaging acquisition before FLA. Additionally, only fetuses/infants who remained under local follow‐up at our center underwent comprehensive neuroimaging assessment after FLA, introducing referral and follow‐up bias toward more clinically complicated cases. Therefore, the cases described in this series should be interpreted as descriptive observations rather than the true incidence of brain injury. In addition, imaging surveillance varied by managing physician, leading to variability in the extent of pre‐FLA neuroimaging and technical difficulty in imaging the “stuck” twin as well as the “poly” twin, timing of follow‐up neuroimaging and performance of MRI after the FLA. Lastly, postnatal neurodevelopmental implications of brain injury are not available as our cohort is still too young for formal clinically meaningful assessments. A prospective longitudinal follow‐up study with formal neurodevelopmental assessment is currently being planned to systematically collect long‐term outcome data in this population.

Despite these limitations, our study has several strengths. This study represents a focused contemporary cohort of the last 2 years from a single experienced tertiary center examining the evolution of brain injury following FLA in TTTS. We incorporated both early and late neuroimaging across prenatal and postnatal periods, allowing for longitudinal assessment of cerebral injury progression.

Conclusions

Severe brain injury following FLA for TTTS typically begins as an early focal ischaemic or haemorrhagic insult, progressing to more diffuse and often irreversible changes. The most common early findings (< 4 weeks post‐FLA) were PVHI, ventriculomegaly and cerebellar haemorrhage. In the later period (≥ 4 weeks post‐FLA), cerebral and cerebellar parenchymal volume loss destructive lesions were the predominant findings, reflecting the chronic sequelae of earlier insults. These preliminary findings suggest a consistent pattern of injury that warrants further prospective study. We strongly suggest that all cases of TTTS treated by FLA should have detailed neuroimaging approximately 4 weeks following FLA procedure regardless of gestational age and procedural course.
