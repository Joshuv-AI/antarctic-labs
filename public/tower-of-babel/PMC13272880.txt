Association Between Inflammatory Cytokines (IL‐6 and IL‐10) and Trauma Severity: Correlations With Shock Index and ABC Score in Trauma Patients—A Prospective Observational Study
Authors: Rodrigo Gonçalves de Oliveira, Marina Tiemi Shio, André Luis Lacerda Bacchi, Ana Paula Ribeiro, Rodrigo Olivio Sabbion, Patrícia Colombo‐Souza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsTrauma is a leading cause of disability and mortality worldwide, with outcomes influenced by inflammatory responses. This study evaluated the association between trauma severity scores—Shock Index (SI) and Assessment of Blood Consumption (ABC) score—and systemic cytokine levels (IL‐6 and IL‐10) to assess their potential role in predicting clinical outcomes in trauma patients.MethodsA prospective observational study was conducted with 20 adult trauma patients admitted to a tertiary hospital in São Paulo, Brazil (August 2022–December 2022). Blood samples were collected at admission (T0) and 48 h later (T1). Serum IL‐6 and IL‐10 were measured by ELISA. Severity was classified using SI (heart rate/systolic blood pressure; > 1 indicates hypovolemic shock) and ABC score ( ≥ 2 parameters suggest high transfusion risk). Statistical analysis used the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test (paired comparisons), Mann–Whitney for variation, and Spearman's correlation with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (two‐tailed α = 0.05).ResultsPatients were predominantly male (18/20, 90%) with blunt trauma (12/20, 60%). IL‐10 levels decreased significantly from T0 to T1 (median [IQR]: 7.7 [−1.5 to 20.2] pg/mL vs. −1.4 [−5.4 to 5.9] pg/mL; median difference = −7.2 pg/mL, 95% CI −16.1 to −1.2; p = 0.013). IL‐6 remained stable (p = 0.65), resulting in an increased IL‐6/IL‐10 ratio (p = 0.012). IL‐6 at T0 correlated positively with ABC score (Spearman's ρ = 0.50, 95% CI 0.08–0.77, p = 0.03). IL‐10 at T0 correlated positively with Shock Index (ρ = 0.47, 95% CI 0.04–0.75, p = 0.04).ConclusionTrauma severity scores (ABC and SI) were associated with dynamic cytokine changes, particularly declining IL‐10, suggesting a shift toward a pro‐inflammatory state 48 h post‐injury. These findings support the potential integration of cytokine profiling with clinical scores to improve trauma risk stratification and guide early intervention.

Background

Trauma is the most important cause of disability, morbidity, and mortality, especially in the young/adult population. Every year, approximately 5.8 million people die from trauma, constituting one of the main public health problems in many countries, regardless of socioeconomic development [1, 2, 3].

Trauma victims are treated in the emergency room according to the Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) guidelines, developed by the American College of Surgeons (ACS) [4]. In addition to the clinical evaluation, which includes anamnesis and a physical examination focused on the trauma, other indices are used to assess the patient's potential severity and determine the possible need for blood component transfusion. In particular, the Shock Index (SI) and the Assessment of Blood Consumption Score (ABC) are valuable tools for assessing hemodynamic stability in trauma patients [5, 6]. In fact, while SI is used for early diagnosis of hypoperfusion, the ABC score helps identify those at increased risk of hemodynamic instability and the need for immediate intervention [7, 8, 9].

Beyond these aspects, it is known that in trauma, an inflammatory response is present with the release of several mediators, which include cytokines that act as an essential part of the healing process [10, 11]. Several inflammatory cytokines are involved in the immune response to injury and trauma, including interleukins (IL)‐1, IL‐6, and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF‐α). These cytokines are produced by various cell types, mainly immune cells, in response to trauma and exert corollary effects on inflammation, the immune response, and tissue repair [12, 13].

Although pro‐inflammatory cytokines, particularly IL‐6, are essential players of the inflammatory response, excessive IL‐6 levels have been associated with worse outcomes in trauma patients, including increased mortality and the risk of developing complications [14, 15, 16, 17]. On the other hand, IL‐10, a classic anti‐inflammatory cytokine, has been associated with a better prognosis in trauma victims, as it can modulate the inflammatory response, thereby reducing excessive inflammation and protecting the body's tissues from additional damage [13, 14, 15]. Therefore, while IL‐6 levels can act as a biomarker of inflammatory response intensity, IL‐10 levels can serve as a biomarker of how the body controls/modulates this inflammation [18, 19, 20].

It has been suggested that the combination of severity scores and data related to inflammatory biomarkers can contribute not only to the identification of trauma victims with greater potential severity, but also to defining early monitoring protocols for these patients by identifying those with a higher chance of unfavorable outcomes [18, 21]. Based on it, in the present study, we aimed to evaluate not only the systemic concentration of the cytokines IL‐6 and IL‐10 at the time of trauma patient admission and 48 h after, as well as the association of the circulating level of these cytokines with the severity of the trauma, assessed by SI and ABC scores, to enhance our knowledge regarding the combination of these prominent biomarkers and indices in a short‐time after the admission of trauma patient.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This is an observational, analytical, prospective study with two time points (admission, T0; and 48 h later, T1). The study included patients over 18 years of age admitted to a municipal hospital in São Paulo, Brazil, specialized in trauma care, from August 2022 to December 2022, who remained hospitalized for at least 48 h. Patients who died or were discharged within 48 h were excluded from the analysis. This inclusion criterion was necessary to obtain two time points (admission and 48 h) but may introduce survival bias, which is acknowledged as a limitation.

During the study period, 123 trauma patients were treated; 83 did not meet eligibility criteria, eight declined to participate, and 32 agreed to participate. Of these, 20 completed the 48‐h hospitalization and were included in the final analysis (Figure 1).

Flow chart of the sample description.

Ethical Aspects

The Research Ethics Committee of Santo Amaro University (UNISA; approval No. 5,469,772) and the Research Ethics Committee of Hospital Municipal do Campo Limpo (approval No. 5,535,752) approved this study. All volunteers received information about risks and benefits and provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Resolution No. 466/2012 of the Brazilian National Health Council.

Trauma Severity Assessment

Trauma severity was assessed at admission using two instruments: the SI—calculated as heart rate divided by systolic blood pressure, with values > 1 indicating hypovolemic shock and a potentially critical condition— and the ABC score, based on four parameters (penetrating trauma mechanism, systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg, heart rate > 120 bpm, and positive Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma [FAST]), where patients with two or more parameters were classified as ABC‐positive (high risk for massive transfusion). These indices were used as surrogate markers of hemodynamic severity and transfusion risk, not as anatomical severity scores such as the Injury Severity Score (ISS). Patients were categorized into three severity groups: SI‐positive only (n = 12), both SI and ABC‐positive (n = 6), and ABC‐positive only (n = 2).

Blood Sample Collection

Blood samples were collected into tubes without anticoagulant at T0 (immediately after admission and initial care) and T1 (48 h later). Serum aliquots (minimum 500 μL) were obtained after coagulation and centrifugation at 850g for 15 min and stored at −80°C until analysis.

Cytokine Measurement

Serum concentrations of IL‐6 and IL‐10 were determined using commercial ELISA kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Vienna, Austria: IL‐10, BMS215‐2; IL‐6, 88‐7066‐22). Patient sera were diluted 1:2 or 1:4, and all samples were run in duplicate according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Data Collection

The following data were extracted from electronic medical records at admission: age, sex, trauma mechanism (blunt vs. penetrating), means of transport to hospital, vital signs (systolic and diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate), FAST result, medications administered (sedatives, analgesics, neuromuscular blockers, opioids), need for orotracheal intubation, and length of hospital stay. Outcomes such as intensive care unit (ICU) admission, need for mechanical ventilation, and in‐hospital mortality were also recorded when available. All data were collected prospectively using a standardized case report form.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 and R 4.2.2 (bootstrap). Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test; non‐parametric methods were used because cytokine data were skewed and included values below the detection limit (recorded as negative numbers). Descriptive data are reported as median with interquartile range (IQR). The Wilcoxon signed‐rank test (two‐tailed) compared IL‐6, IL‐10, and their ratio between T0 and T1. Effect size for paired comparisons is reported as the median difference (T1–T0) with its IQR. Spearman's rank correlation (ρ) assessed associations between cytokines and severity scores (ABC as binary, Shock Index as continuous). For each correlation, we report ρ, 95% bootstrap confidence interval (bias‐corrected and accelerated, 2000 replicates), and exact p value.

A two‐tailed α = 0.05 was prespecified. p values are reported per journal convention: p < 0.001 as “p < 0.001”; 0.001 ≤ p < 0.01 rounded to three decimals; p ≥ 0.01 rounded to two decimals. No power analysis was performed due to the exploratory nature of the study; findings in small subgroups (e.g., n = 2) should be interpreted cautiously.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 20 trauma patients completed the 48‐h hospitalization and were included in the analysis (Figure 1). The majority were male (18/20, 90%), with ages ranging from 18 to 56 years (median 30 years, IQR 24–40). Regarding trauma severity classification at admission: 12 patients (60%) were positive only on the Shock Index (SI > 1), two patients (10%) only on the ABC score ( ≥ 2 parameters), and six patients (30%) were positive on both scores.

Blunt/contusion trauma occurred in 12 patients (60%): among these, 7/12 (58%) resulted from falls or motorcycle accidents, 3/12 (25%) from falls from height, 1/12 (8%) from a fall from a moving vehicle, and 1/12 (8%) was found on a public road with an unknown mechanism. Penetrating trauma occurred in 8 patients (40%): 6/8 (75%) due to cold weapons (knives, sharp objects) and 2/8 (25%) due to firearms.

Regarding transport to the hospital: 9/20 (45%) were brought by the Rescue Unit of the São Paulo Military Police Fire Department, 6/20 (30%) by the Mobile Emergency Care Service, and 5/20 (25%) sought care independently.

Upon admission, 13/20 (65%) required sedative drugs for orotracheal intubation (fentanyl infusion plus propofol), 3/20 (15%) received neuromuscular blockers (succinylcholine), 3/20 (15%) received analgesics (dipyrone) plus a non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drug (ketoprofen), and 4/20 (20%) received weak opioids (tramadol hydrochloride).

Association Between Cytokine Levels and Trauma Severity Scores

Table 3 presents the correlations between serum cytokine levels at admission and the clinical severity scores. IL‐6 at T0 correlated positively with the ABC score (binary: positive vs. negative), with patients having a positive ABC score (n = 8) showing higher IL‐6 levels than those with a negative ABC score (n = 12) (Spearman's ρ = 0.50, 95% CI 0.08–0.77, p = 0.03). Additionally, IL‐10 at T0 correlated positively with the Shock Index (continuous variable), where higher Shock Index values were associated with higher IL‐10 levels (Spearman's ρ = 0.47, 95% CI 0.04–0.75, p = 0.04). No other significant correlations were found between cytokine levels and severity scores at T1 (data not shown).

Discussion

Here, we were able to demonstrate that: (i) while circulating IL‐6 levels were unchanged, (ii) not only a significant reduction of IL‐10 levels, which led to a lower IL‐6/IL‐10 ratio, (iii) but also a minor variation of their levels, was evidenced 48 h after (T1) trauma patients were admitted to the hospital. In addition, (iv) positive correlations between IL‐6 and IL‐10 levels at T0 and T1 time points were found, as well as (v) other interestingly positive associations between systemic IL‐6 levels and ABC score, and between IL‐10 levels and SI values.

It is known that the criteria used to classify the severity of the trauma are primarily based on the type of trauma and, consequently, the level of resources needed to assist the patient, in addition to subjective criteria of severity [8, 9]. In this sense, the SI and ABC Score, commonly used to determine the need for blood transfusion, could also be considered prominent tools routinely used to refine the definition of trauma patient severity [6, 9]. However, it is worth noting that, although these tools can substantially enhance the identification of severity and hemodynamic stability in trauma patients, they are not substitutes for a thorough clinical assessment and interpretation of the data received [10, 11].

Beyond these pieces of information, it has been suggested that the burden of trauma could potentially lead to the failure of the individual's immune system, which can be elicited by an unbalanced systemic inflammatory status in the trauma patient [17, 22]. Therefore, the analysis of the systemic concentration/ratio of cytokines in trauma patients can help in the management and outcomes of those patients [17], since the failure of the immune system can favor the progression of sepsis or even death in case of infection or contamination [23].

Based on studies that investigated the association between the circulating cytokine levels and the severity of trauma in patients, it was suggested that high levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines may be correlated with greater severity of trauma [11, 15, 24, 25, 26]. Corroborating this suggestion, it was reported that high serum IL‐6 levels were related to post‐traumatic death [17]. In fact, the initial physiological response of patients to trauma involves the induction of an inflammatory response, also mediated by cytokines, which modulates the immune system's response to tissue injury by repairing the damage through a balance between pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐6, and anti‐inflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐10. However, it is worth noting that, to date, the routine determination of these biomarkers for patients seen in emergency care remains unfeasible from an operational and financial perspective, as it consumes significant time and depletes available laboratory resources.

Another point that deserves to be highlighted is that, despite investigations into the association between cytokines and trauma severity, it is still not entirely clear how this association manifests, as it may vary by trauma type and individual patient response. Additionally, in agreement with the literature, cytokine levels can be influenced by other factors, such as age, sex, pre‐existing medical conditions, and medications used in the treatment of trauma [1, 27]. Therefore, further studies are necessary to enhance our understanding of this context.

Several factors beyond trauma severity may influence cytokine levels, including surgical interventions, mechanical ventilation, nosocomial infections, and individual patient characteristics (age, sex, comorbidities). In the present study, we did not collect detailed data on these variables, nor were we able to perform multivariable adjustment due to the small sample size. Therefore, the observed associations between IL‐6/IL‐10 and severity scores may be confounded by unmeasured factors. Future studies with larger cohorts and comprehensive data collection should address these limitations.

Interestingly, our observation that serum IL‐6 levels remained unchanged at 48 h after the patient's admission suggests that the inflammatory response elicited by the trauma reached a “plateau”. However, the significant decrease in circulating IL‐10 levels observed at that moment directly contributed to the increased IL‐6/IL‐10 ratio and indicated a marked shift in systemic status toward a pro‐inflammatory profile. These data allow us to suggest that a perpetuation of systemic inflammation is present after trauma induction, which can lead to the risk of exhausting the patient's reserve and may denote a worse prognosis, as well as reinforce the proposition that the assessment of cytokines with different properties, both isolated and the ratio analysis between these molecules, can enhance our accuracy regarding the inflammatory balance.

In addition to these data, it is worth citing that the higher variation of circulating IL‐6 levels, found in the subtraction of T1 and T0 values, in which 50% of patients had increased and 45% decreased in the IL‐6 levels, can corroborate the lack of significant alterations observed in our cohort of trauma patients.

The clinical utility of cytokine measurements in acute trauma care is currently limited by the turnaround time of conventional ELISA (approximately 4–6 h). In our study, measurements at 48 h post‐admission were not intended to guide immediate resuscitation but rather to provide prognostic information for later risk stratification. For bedside use, rapid point‐of‐care assays for IL‐6 and IL‐10 would be necessary. Until such technologies become available, cytokine profiling remains a research tool rather than a routine clinical test. Nonetheless, our findings support the concept that inflammatory balance (IL‐6/IL‐10 ratio) may help identify patients at risk of sustained pro‐inflammatory states who could benefit from targeted immunomodulation.

A possible explanation for these findings could be the use of different types of medication at the time of patient admission, as these can interfere with the activated inflammatory response and, consequently, influence cytokine levels. Although we did not observe any association between the medications applied to the patients participating in this study and the cytokines evaluated, other studies have shown that the use of some medications, such as propofol, can influence the increase in the IL‐6/IL‐10 ratio [27] in alcoholic patients undergoing surgery, favoring the anti‐inflammatory effect of this drug. In the studies by Muroi et al. [25] and Aganovic‐Musinovic et al. [28], although surgery increased inflammatory biomarkers, propofol use did not affect inflammation. It decreased the incidence of perioperative neurocognitive disorders. In our study, in addition to propofol, our patients used analgesics and anti‐inflammatory drugs (sodium dipyrone and ketoprofen, respectively), which may have contributed to the wide variation of IL‐6 levels found in those individuals. Considering these findings, further studies are needed to better understand the consequences of this cytokine's variation in post‐trauma patients.

In addition, we found that patients who reached severity scores showed a positive correlation with systemic concentrations of the pro‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐6 or the anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐10, both at admission (T0) and after 48 h (T1). These data suggest that, even if a systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) were elicited, a compensatory anti‐inflammatory response (CARS) was also induced in patients with potentially severe trauma. Taken together, these findings could reinforce the proposition that circulating cytokine levels, particularly IL‐6 and IL‐10, can help predict the severity of trauma and guide patient treatment, as they showed a strong correlation with SI or ABC values.

Although the present study presents interesting results, interpretation needs to be cautious due to several limitations: (1) single‐center design; (2) modest sample size (n = 20), with particularly small subgroups (e.g., ABC‐only, n = 2); (3) short follow‐up (48 h) with only two time points, which may not capture the full dynamic inflammatory response; (4) exclusion of patients who died or were discharged before 48 h, introducing survival bias; (5) absence of anatomical severity scores (e.g., ISS); (6) lack of multivariable adjustment for potential confounders (surgery, ventilation, infection); (7) no collection of clinical outcomes such as ICU admission, ventilator days, or mortality; and (8) the relatively long turnaround time of cytokine assays limits immediate clinical applicability.

Conclusion

Based on the findings of this study, we were able to demonstrate that, even though the circulating IL‐6 levels showed an expressive variation between our cohort of patients, which led to their values not significantly different during the study, particularly the prominent reduction of circulating levels of the anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐10, could drive a shift of systemic status to a pro‐inflammatory profile 48 h after trauma patient admission. In addition, the close positive association between the commonly used criteria for blood transfusion in trauma patients (ABC Score and Shock Index) and levels of IL‐10 or IL‐6 can reinforce the possibility of their use to better define severity criteria for the patients treated, allowing us to suggest that these evaluations may serve both as screening criteria and prognostic markers, to establish and guide the appropriate treatment.
