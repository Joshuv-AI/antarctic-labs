Organization, neuronal composition, and dynamics of neuronal ensembles in the songbird auditory forebrain
Authors: Felipe A. Cini, Luke Remage-Healey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryNeurons involved in auditory learning are typically considered singly, but coincident activity of neuronal “ensembles” could be important for sensory processing. In songbirds, a higher-pallial region, the caudomedial nidopallium (NCM), is crucial for auditory learning. NCM neurons adapt to repeated presentation of song stimuli, a form of online recognition memory. Yet, how complex song stimuli are encoded by ensembles of excitatory and inhibitory neurons is unknown. Using high-density electrophysiology, we unveiled neuronal ensembles operating at high temporal precision across the NCM. Multiple ensembles are concurrently activated by songs, heterogeneously composed, topographically proximate, and biased toward excitatory members. NCM ensembles adapt to song playback and, notably, become more stimulus-selective over tens of minutes, accompanied by fast remodeling. Altogether, our results show that songs are conveyed by multiple, parallel dynamic network ensembles. These findings advance our knowledge of the composition, dynamics, and network reorganization of ensembles as complex sensory stimuli become increasingly familiar.

Introduction

Much of our understanding of the cognitive processes, such as sensory processing, memory, and information integration arises from studies of single neurons. Yet, the recurring patterns of coordinated activity of groups of neurons—neuronal ensembles—have long been proposed to play a crucial role in these processes.1,2 New technologies have allowed for the detection of a large number of neurons in a single recording, enabling the identification and systematic study of neuronal ensembles. Local ensembles, detected through correlated neuronal activity, have been shown to effectively encode information beyond the capability of single neurons.3,4,5,6,7,8 Additionally, recent in silico modeling suggests that the correlated neuronal activity allows the scaling of memory capacity and nonlinear computations.9 Understanding these emergent properties may provide critical insights into how networks of neurons encode and store information. However, key ensemble properties, including their cellular composition, stability, selectivity, and optimal synchronization time between members, remain largely unknown.

Inhibitory interneurons play a key role in generating gamma oscillations (30–100 Hz) and promoting local network synchronization, while at the same time constraining noise correlations.10,11,12,13 Most studies of network ensembles have focused exclusively on principal/excitatory cells and/or not differentiated the ensemble membership by cell type.14,15 Therefore, there is a significant gap in understanding the contributions of inhibitory vs. excitatory cell types to ensemble membership and their interactions.

Studies of neuronal ensembles have been mostly gleaned from rodent species, but we now have an opportunity to expand this specific reference frame. Songbirds, especially zebra finches (Taeniopygia guttata), offer valuable insights into auditory processing and learning. Zebra finches are a closed-ended, non-parasitic songbird species that rely heavily on vocal communication for various purposes, including pair bonding, individual recognition, and mating.16,17 The caudomedial nidopallium (NCM), a region of the secondary auditory pallium in songbirds, plays a key role in auditory stimulus categorization, auditory learning, species-specific recognition, and novelty detection.18,19,20,21,22,23 Studies in NCM have largely focused on single-unit activity encoding of sounds or sound features,18,23,24,25,26 and excitatory and inhibitory neurons in NCM can be distinguished by their electrophysiological waveforms in vivo and ex vivo.27 Inhibitory neurons show stronger sound-evoked responses, higher firing rates, and have lower stimulus selectivity than excitatory neurons,27,28,29,30,31 matching similarly divergent response properties in mammals. Thus, understanding the ensemble activity in this system can help uncover broader principles of auditory processing and communication in a vocal learning species.

A characteristic feature of NCM neurons is their fast stimulus-specific adaptation (SSA) when presented repeatedly with the same auditory stimulus.18,19,20 This adaptation represents a form of cellular memory in which neural responses adjust dynamically to reflect recent stimulus-related information and experience. In rodent cortex, circuit dynamics during SSA show the suppression of phase coupling between spikes and local field potential in the beta range32 and neuronal ensembles that detect novel, non-adapted stimuli.33 Furthermore, two distinct classes of inhibitory neurons, parvalbumin (PV) and somatostatin (SST), play different roles in SSA, with SST facilitating adaptation.34,35 However, the dynamics of ensemble behavior during SSA remain unclear in any system. We hypothesize that neuronal ensembles undergo rapid remodeling during adaptation, retaining efficient encoding for familiar stimuli and updating their representation to enable rapid stimulus recognition in a cell type-specific manner.

To address how neuronal ensembles contribute to auditory information encoding, we examined their stability during SSA in the NCM. Recent studies have described groups of neurons as “ensembles” collected statistically across recordings separated in time from multiple animals, providing glimpses of the role of different cell types in songbird population auditory processing and decoding.36,37 Here, we build on these advances by using high-density silicon-recording probes to simultaneously record dozens of neurons and isolate concurrently activated neuronal ensembles. In this way, we can now investigate network ensemble adaptation and stability to gain insights into the mechanisms underlying auditory plasticity and memory. Our study also explores principles of ensemble composition, particularly the role of inhibitory vs. excitatory neurons, and the temporal precision of neuronal synchronization. These findings show that ensembles undergo fast remodeling during SSA, that ensemble membership is biased toward excitatory units, and that they synchronize within an ultra-short, 6-ms timescale.

Results

Single unit activity in accordance with prior reports

We performed electrophysiology recordings from the NCM, a higher order auditory processing region that has been shown to play a crucial role in novelty and individual- and species-specific recognition,22,23,25,31,38 by using 64-channel silicon probes during the playback of conspecific songs, heterospecific songs (Bengalese finch, Lonchura striata domestica), or white noise (Figures 1A and 1C). Well-isolated single units were classified as either putative inhibitory (peak-to-peak [ptp] duration < 0.35 ms, Figure 1A) or putative excitatory (ptp duration ≥ 0.35 ms) based on the average waveform (2,000 samples) of that unit (Figure 1B). We note that Spool and colleagues (2021) determined the molecular identity of extracellular waveforms in NCM to excitatory vs. inhibitory neurons using optotagging and a similar ptp duration threshold. Out of all units recorded (birds = 8, recordings = 14, single units = 759), the majority were putative excitatory (putative excitatory: mean = 67.23%, SD = 13.08%; putative inhibitory: mean = 32.77%, SD = 13.08%; Figure 1D). Cells in the NCM were auditory responsive (Figure 1C), and there was an interaction between response by the cell type and stimulus type (b = −0.204, SEM = 0.38, Z score = −5.33, p < 0.001 (Figure 1E). Putative inhibitory units responded more strongly to conspecific songs than to heterospecific songs (mean difference = −0.41, 95% CI [−0.74, −0.07], p = 0.006), while putative excitatory units responded equally strongly to both song types (mean difference = −0.04, 95% CI [−0.25, −0.16], p = 0.987). Furthermore, the putative inhibitory units showed an overall greater evoked response to auditory playback than the putative excitatory units (b = −0.613, SEM = 0.027, Z score = −22.98, p < 0.001). Next, we examined the units’ selectivity based on their response percentage to different conspecific songs, finding that the putative excitatory units were more selective than putative inhibitory units (b = −11.86, SEM = 0.92, Z score = −13.67, p < 0.001; Figure 1F). Ensemble detection and composition

After verifying reliable single-unit activity, we investigated how these units work together to process auditory information by examining the coordinated neuronal ensemble activity (Figure 2A). Here, we define an ensemble as a group of neurons recorded simultaneously from the same recording site that fire together within a short (< tens of ms) time frame. To detect ensembles (Figures 2D and 2E), we binned the data in a selected time bin (see below) and then normalized the firing rate of each unit. In the current literature, the synchronization time frame for neurons in an ensemble has not been fully settled. Therefore, we tested a series of bin sizes to identify the optimal duration for ensemble detection and function. Although not statistically significant, the 6-ms bin size had the highest mutual information (MI) (Figure 2B) and the second highest accuracy with the Bayesian decoder (Figure 2B). Consequently, we performed further analyses using the ensembles detected with the 6-ms bin size. Of the 759 neurons detected, most participated in at least one ensemble, 57.05% participated in only one ensemble, 22.40% participated in multiple ensembles, and 20.55% were not part of any ensemble (Figure 2C). The identified ensembles with this time bin were also auditory responsive and exhibited narrow temporal precision (Figures 3A and 3B), showing greater evoked response to songs than to white noise (b = −1.093, SEM = 0.112, Z score = −9.75, p < 0.001; Figure 3C). NCM ensembles were as song selective as putative excitatory units (Kolmogorov-Smirnov [KS] test ensembles vs. putative excitatory, p ≥ 0.05) but more selective than putative inhibitory units (KS test putative inhibitory vs. ensembles, p < 0.05; KS test putative excitatory vs. ensembles, p < 0.05; Figure 3D). Ensembles also showed distinct, non-overlapping response patterns to individual song stimuli (Figures 2F, 2G, 3A, and 3B) with strikingly narrow (<20 ms) activation epochs. Thus, in principle, NCM ensembles are an emergent feature among member neurons for highly refined, song-selective response properties. What is the topographic and cellular composition of ensembles? We characterized the composition and distribution of the members of detected NCM ensembles and compared them with non-co-members (defined as neurons that are not members of the same ensemble) and to members of randomly shuffled ensembles, using electrode location as a proxy for unit location. Ensemble members were physically closer to each other than randomly defined ensembles (Figure 4C). The maximum distance between the two members was also shorter than that between non-members and also than those in the random distribution, as most pairwise distances between members were within <100-mm recording separation distance (Figure 4D). We also analyzed the ensemble composition, finding that most were heterogeneous and biased toward putative excitatory units compared with randomly generated ensembles (KS = 0.21, p < 0.001; Figure 4B). Although the recorded NCM neurons were, on average, 32.77% putative inhibitory and 67.23% excitatory, exclusively putative excitatory ensembles comprised 37.23% of the population, while 6.57% were exclusively putative inhibitory (Figure 4B), and most were mixtures of both putative inhibitory and putative excitatory neurons. Ensemble composition was not found to be directly related to ensemble size (Pearson’s R = 0.051, p = 0.558). However, the ensemble composition scaled with time bin; we found that the proportion of putative excitatory members increased as time bins expanded (Figure S2). Ensemble adaptation, stability, and selectivity

Do songbird auditory ensembles simply reflect correlated activity patterns? Given that ensembles are defined by the correlated activity of neurons and that most NCM ensembles are biased toward excitatory members, we examined the pairwise correlation between cell types (Figure 5A). Inhibitory neurons typically exhibit higher correlated activity than excitatory neurons in many systems. However, considering that there are more putative excitatory members in ensembles than expected by chance (Figure 4B), one might reason that the putative inhibitory neurons in our data may show weaker pairwise correlations, leading to their under-representation in the detected ensembles (Figure 5A). We found that the overall correlation among inhibitory neurons was stronger than that for excitatory units (b = 0.07, SEM = 0.0006, Z score = −114.82, p < 0.001; Figure 5B). We also confirmed that the ensemble members had higher correlation values than non-co-members (b = 0.012, SEM = 0.0009, Z score = −13.10, p < 0.001; Figure 5B). Because different cell types have different baseline firing rates, we reasoned that the increased correlation values in inhibitory cells are, on average, due to their higher firing rates. To address this question, we circularly shuffled the data to preserve each neuron’s firing structure, creating a shuffled correlation distribution (Figure 5C). The correlation distribution of inhibitory-inhibitory, inhibitory-excitatory, and excitatory-excitatory pairs of the observed data differed significantly from each other (Figure 5C, bottom), while the circularly shuffled data had significant overlap in the correlation distribution (Figure 5C, top). Therefore, we observed that even though inhibitory neurons have generally high pairwise correlation values, this does not predispose NCM ensembles toward more inhibitory cell membership. SSA and ensemble dynamics

This indicates that the NCM tracks recently heard songs and responds robustly to novel songs, a feature considered to reflect learning and novel stimulus recognition. How stable is the ensemble membership composition over the course of stimulus adaptation? Adaptation occurred over a relatively short period, approximately 25 min, during random playback of 8 different auditory stimuli (Figures 6A and 6B). We aimed to determine whether ensembles remain stable or undergo changes during this period of adaptation. We identified three patterns of ensemble activation in the recordings (Figures S3A and S3B): (1) stable ensembles, which did not change their neuronal composition throughout the recording; (2) strengthening ensembles, where ensemble members showed increased co-firing; and (3) remodeling ensembles, where one or more members of the ensemble stopped firing in synchrony with the rest. Despite the rapid adaptation, most ensembles remained stable over time, maintaining a consistent composition, with only a few undergoing rapid remodeling or increased connectivity (Figure S3C). The proportion of inhibitory/excitatory neurons did not differ across these categories of activation pattern (Figure S3D). Therefore, most ensembles appear to keep track of recently heard stimuli using SSA in a similar trajectory as their underlying single units, and this result suggests that the ensemble neuronal composition could be stable through the SSA period.

The above analysis could inherently favor detecting membership stability because the ensemble detection relies on correlated activity during the entire recording. To address this potential bias, we divided the recording into two-halves—before (the first 13 trials) and after adaptation (the last 12 trials)—and performed ensemble detection on each segment. After identifying ensembles, we compared those detected in the first and second halves, and ensemble membership was then compared using the Jaccard similarity index. If the index was higher than 0.9, the ensembles were defined as stable (7.38% of the ensembles; Figure 6C). If the index was greater than or equal to 0.33 and smaller than 0.9, the ensembles were considered the same but remodeled (59.84% of the ensembles; Figure 6C). If the index was below 0.33, the ensemble was considered entirely new, with the original ensemble no longer existing (32.79% of the ensembles; Figure 6C). Therefore, NCM ensembles showed marked plasticity in their membership over the course of these recordings.

Next, considering the fast remodeling of ensembles during the SSA, we investigated whether these changes were driven by specific cell types. We found that post-SSA, 34.2% of the ensembles showed an increase in the proportion of inhibitory cells, 39.7% of the ensembles showed an increase in excitatory members, and 26.0% showed no change in the proportion of excitatory/inhibitory cells. Furthermore, we examined whether the members of remodeled ensembles were physically closer or farther apart than the members of ensembles before adaptation. We found that most ensemble members, after adaptation, were detected in channels that were more proximal than before adaptation (56.64%; Figure 6D). In summary, when separating the recording into two-halves, we found that only a small number of ensembles stay stable through adaptation, and most ensembles, after adaptation, undergo remodeling or are newly formed. The newly formed ensembles showed an increase in the participation of inhibitory neurons, and membership favors shorter physical distances between neurons.

Lastly, given that SSA may reflect a form of cellular memory, we examined the ability of neuronal ensembles to discriminate between distinct auditory stimuli, before and after adaptation. Post-adaptation, the song selectivity for ensembles was significantly enhanced (p = 0.03; Figure 6F), consistent with increased tuning specificity following familiarity with the recent stimulus environment. However, decoding accuracy (i.e., the ability of ensembles to distinguish one stimulus from another), assessed via a Bayesian classifier, did not differ significantly from pre- to post-SSA (Figure 6G). This lack of change in decoding performance held across remodeling, stable, and newly recruited ensembles (Figure 6H). While both stable and newly formed ensembles exhibited a trend toward improved classification accuracy, these differences did not reach statistical significance. Together, these observations indicate that, despite remodeling, adaptation, and dynamics in selectivity, the ability of ensembles to carry stable stimulus-related activity is well preserved over time.

Discussion

Neuronal ensembles have been shown to play a crucial role in sensory encoding and memory formation, in particular via increasing the extraction of stimulus signal-to-noise ratio compared with the activity of individual neurons.14,42,43 However, some of their fundamental properties remain unclear. In this study, we provide insights into several of these properties. Coordinated neuronal ensembles can be stable over different timescales, ranging from a few minutes up to several weeks.44 Nevertheless, if exposed to a new environment or learning regime, the ensemble representation can drift and change composition.44 Our results show that most ensembles in a songbird pallial region undergo fast remodeling during the short acquisition phase of SSA and become more selective post-adaptation. It is likely, therefore, that ensembles recruit co-activated neurons to efficiently encode stimuli and integrate newly learned sounds with previously existing networks that represent familiar sounds.

Although ensembles of inhibitory neurons have been suggested to coordinate the activity of excitatory neurons,10,45,46,47,48,49 there is a gap in the understanding of how both excitatory and inhibitory neurons contribute to ensemble membership and co-membership. Our findings indicate that most NCM ensembles are biased toward excitatory members, more than expected by chance. In the NCM, the latency to first spike after stimulus onset is shorter for inhibitory neurons than for excitatory neurons.27,29 This feedforward network architecture, combined with the predominance of excitatory members in ensembles, supports the idea that a small population of inhibitory neurons can coordinate excitatory activity within ensembles.

Another key aspect in understanding the nature and function of neuronal ensembles is the timing of their coordinated activity.42 There is significant variability in the temporal frames used to study ensembles, stemming from methodological constraints (e.g., electrophysiology vs. calcium imaging), analytical approaches (e.g., functional ensembles defined by activity during stimulus playback or spike timing within one local field potential oscillation), and brain region/species. Most ensemble timing studies have been performed in rodents and have employed time windows between 10 and 50 ms to analyze the coordinated activity among neurons.50,51,52,53 While ensembles exclusively comprising PV neurons have been shown to synchronize at 2.4 ms,54 here, we show that the ensembles composed of both inhibitory and excitatory neurons in songbirds are coordinated within a very fast time window of <10 ms. This rapid neuronal synchronization supports the idea that songbirds perceive their auditory world in a finer temporal frame than many mammalian species,55 which is likely key to their rapid vocal communication signaling.

The rapid coordination of NCM ensembles (6 ms), combined with the observation that most neurons are part of only one ensemble, suggests that in songbirds, the pallium contains fast synaptic connections that coordinate this activity. This coordination could be due to chemical or electrical synapses, given that gap junction proteins are widely expressed in the songbird pallium.56 Understanding the fast ensemble coordination via electrical synapses could be important to unpacking highly time-variant stimuli such as songs with high temporal precision.

Furthermore, we have shown that most ensemble members are spatially proximate. Although our study did not investigate the reasons for spatial proximity, this could be explained by shared synaptic inputs. Field L2a, a primary auditory region in songbirds, and OV (ovoidalis: auditory thalamus) project topographically to the NCM,57 which may help explain the short distances we observed. This suggests that NCM ensembles are largely influenced by these inputs. However, we also observed long-range membership, which could indicate the integration of neurons from multiple low-level inputs supporting the high-order auditory processing observed in the NCM.

Zebra finches are a closed-ended vocal learner, meaning that they learn to vocalize species-typical song during a brief period of their life,16 and after the critical period has closed, they do not learn new songs. Therefore, adult zebra finches might show less flexible and dynamic ensembles compared with open-ended learner species that are constantly being exposed to a less stable auditory social environment where their own partners can change their songs multiple times over their lifetime.

In conclusion, we provide evidence that during SSA, the local neuronal network undergoes restructuring. Additionally, we demonstrate that the excitatory and inhibitory neurons can be part of the same neuronal ensemble and that the time frame for ensemble synchronization in songbirds is exceptionally fast.

Limitations of the study

Although we classified the cells as inhibitory or excitatory, we lack detailed information about their subtypes, such as PV or SST interneurons.58,59 Thus, it remains unclear which inhibitory or excitatory cell subtypes contribute to their composition. Regarding the timing of ensembles, while we identified 6 ms as potentially the optimal bin size, different ensembles may be synchronized on different timescales. Some ensembles might exhibit very fast coordinated activity, while others—particularly those composed exclusively of excitatory cells—might synchronize more slowly. Future experiments could systematically examine different optimum time bins to help resolve this question. The ensemble dynamics we report here are consistent with short-term stability or remodeling, and future studies could investigate how the social communication context and neuromodulators shape the NCM ensemble dynamics. Although the unsupervised model that combines PCA with ICA that we used here is widely used,50,60,61,62,63 the threshold ensemble activity and ensemble membership are determined by null hypothesis distribution, which has not been linked to the physiological phenomena14 and may pose difficulties for direct extraction of biological significance. Furthermore, the implementation of PCA and ICA makes assumptions about the statistical properties of the data. These assumptions, such as linearity or independence of components, may not fully capture the complexity of neural dynamics. If these assumptions are violated, the detected assemblies might not represent real, biologically meaningful neural groups. Other methods have been used to identify temporally clustered neuronal activity as ensembles, such as co-activity that happens within sharp-wave ripple oscillations,54 pattern completion-based activation,64 and time sequence activity patterns,6 could be employed in the future to evaluate similar patterns in the zebra finch NCM.

Experimental model and study participant details

Male and female adult zebra finches (n = 8, 4 females, >90 days old) from the University of Massachusetts Amherst colony were used for the experiment. Birds were not actively breeding during the experiments (single-sex cages) and were on a 14:10 h light-dark cycle. All procedures were in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Massachusetts Amherst (Protocol #2019-0058).

Method details

Surgery and electrophysiological recording

Birds were food-deprived for 1 h before surgery, anesthetized with isoflurane (1–2% in O2), and then placed on the stereotaxic apparatus (Kopf) with the head tilted at a 45o angle. Bilateral craniotomy was performed at markings 0.9 mm anterior and 0.7 mm lateral respective to the bifurcation of the midsagittal sinus, and then the meninges were resected. A small craniotomy on the anterolateral part of the skull for the implantation of a silver ground wire using cyanoacrylate and dental cement. Craniotomies were sealed with Kwik-Cast (World Precision Instruments). A custom-made headpost was lowered on the beak and fixed to the skull with dental cement. Recordings from both hemispheres were performed 1–3 days after surgery.

For the electrophysiological recordings we used open source, 64-channel silicon probes (model 64G) from the Masmanidis Lab – UCLA.66 Electrode impedance was set between 300 and 500 kOmhs and confirmed on the day of the recording before insertion. Birds were awake restrained and head-fixed for the entirety of the recording. After the bird was secured, the Kwik-Cast was removed and the probe was inserted medio-caudally in register with markings. To confirm probe location, the probe was briefly dipped in DiI (Diluted in 100% ethanol) and allowed to dry before descent ∼1.6 mm from the brain surface.

The probes had 64 channels on two shanks, 300 μm apart, with 32 channels each. The channels within one probe were arranged along a 525 μm long axis, and the space between two contact points was 25 μm vertical spacing and 20 μm horizontal spacing. Recordings were amplified and digitized by a 64-channel amplifier and evaluation board (RHD2000 series; Intan Technologies) and sampled at 30 kHz using Open Ephys software. An Arduino Uno (Arduino) was connected to the recording computer to deliver TTL pulses to the evaluation board’s DAC channel bracketing the beginning and end of the audio stimuli (described below) to optimize detection during analysis. Audio playback and TTL pulses were controlled by a custom-made MATLAB (MathWorks) script, which also controlled the Arduino and sent a copy of the audio analog signal to the evaluation board ADC channel. Each bird underwent one recording per hemisphere per day. Following recordings, birds were transcardially perfused with paraformaldehyde 4% in 0.025 M phosphate. Brains were extracted and placed in 30% sucrose and stored in −80 °C.

Stimuli

32 song files from unrelated birds were randomly and equally split into four sets, containing 8 songs each. For each animal, one set was used per hemisphere (search stimuli to locate NCM were non-songs). This ensured that each bird heard novel stimuli to avoid long-term adaptation in NCM firing states across sessions and days.18,22,57 Therefore, a novel stimulus set was used for each recording to control for familiarity effects.

Each stimulus set consisted of five conspecific songs, two heterospecific songs, and a white noise pulse of typical song duration, repeated 25 times each in pseudorandomized order. The interstimulus interval was pseudorandom within the interval 5 +- 2s. The speaker was positioned 30 cm in front of the animal, equidistant to each ear. The sound level was amplified to peak ∼65 dB as measured by a sound level meter at the animal’s position (RadioShack). Playback trial duration lasted around 25 min. Recordings were performed inside a Faraday cage inside an anechoic booth (Industrial Acoustics; ambient sound transmission losses ranging 30–40 dB). Recordings were made from each hemisphere on the same day or 1 day apart. For each animal’s first recording, the starting hemisphere was randomized and then counterbalanced between sexes. The stimulus set was also initially randomized, then counterbalanced across sexes and hemispheres, but the subset selected for each was always randomized.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Electrophysiology analysis

Single-unit sorting was done with Kilosort4.65 Sorting results were manually curated in Phy (https://github.com/cortex-lab/phy), and well-isolated units were used (high signal-to-noise ratio; low violation of refractory period, low contamination with other units; segregation in waveform PCA space). After sorting (mean = 54.35 units detected per recording site, SD = 14.9), 2000 waveforms were selected randomly for each single unit. The waveform features were aligned, then averaged, and their waveform features were measured (peak-to-peak duration and ratio) in MATLAB.

Normalized Z score evoked responses to each stimulus were calculated by the formula:Z−score=S¯−B¯var(S)+varB−2covar(S,B)Where S and B are the stimuli and spontaneous firing rates across stimulus trials, respectively. After computing the Z score by stimulus, those were averaged to yield a single Z score per unit per stimulus type (conspecifics, heterospecific, white noise). Adaptation rates were calculated using trials 6–25, which is the most linear phase of the adaptation profile in NCM.67,68 For each stimulus, the stimulus firing rate across trials was normalized by the firing rate on trial 6. Then, a linear regression was calculated between trials 6 and 25. For each stimulus type, the average adaptation slope across stimuli was used for each unit. SSA analysis focused on conspecific songs because it is known to be stronger for behaviorally relevant stimuli,18,22 such as conspecific vocalizations. Since conspecific songs are more salient and ecologically meaningful to zebra finches, we prioritized these stimuli to assess SSA effects.

Ensemble detection

We identified neuronal ensembles by combining principal component analysis (PCA) and independent component analysis (ICA).69 Spike times for the whole recording, evoked and spontaneous activity, were binned, creating a spike matrix where each row represented the activity of a single-unit and each column indicated the number of spikes within each time bin (Figure 1A). The activity of each unit was then normalized through z-scoring, followed by calculating the correlation matrix over the normalized spike matrix for all pairs of neurons.

To determine the number of ensembles in each recording, we applied a PCA to the z-scored spike matrix to obtain the eigenvalues. Eigenvalues that were higher than the upper limit of the Marcenko-Pastur distribution70 were considered to be the result of significantly correlated activity. We then applied a fast ICA over the eigenvectors of the significant eigenvalues. The resulting independent components (ICs) of the fast ICA represent each neuron’s contribution to its respective ensemble. Ensemble activation strength was calculated by projecting the ICs back onto the z-scored spike matrix.69

Neurons whose IC weight exceeded the mean weight of the ensemble by ±1.5 standard deviations from the distribution of all IC weights were considered members of the ensemble. The threshold for considering an ensemble to be activated was 3 standard deviations of the activation strength distribution.

Evoked activation z-scores and adaptation profiles for the ensembles were created using a similar calculation as above, with single neurons Z score evoked firing rate and adaptation; however, instead of using spikes the activation of the ensemble was defined by the 99.9th percentile of the activation strength distribution. A limitation of this approach is that the PCA can only identify linearly separable components and the ICA assumes that the components are non Gaussian. Even with these limitations, the ensemble identification strategy we employed is most commonly adopted and has been used in a variety of systems and species.60,61,71,72,73

Measuring ensemble remodeling/stability

To identify the extent to which ensembles undergo remodeling during SSA, we measured the trend of activation strength of the ensemble throughout the recording using the non-parametric Mann-Kendall trend test.74 If the p value was below 0.05 and the trend was negative, we classified the ensemble as “remodeling”, if the p value was below 0.05 and the trend was positive it was classified as “strengthen connectivity”, and if the p value was higher than 0.05, it was classified as a “stable ensemble”.

One concern with the above analysis scheme is that it only acquires ensemble membership once integrated across the entire recording, perhaps leading to an over-estimation of ensemble stability. To address this, we segmented the recording in half and performed ensemble detection on the two-halves separately. After detecting the members of the ensembles, we compared their composition in the first and second half using the Jaccard Similarity Score (Pérez-Ortega et al., 2021). Given that the stimuli often last several seconds and may span multiple time bins, we modified the model to account for multiple ensemble activation per stimulus. The RST for a stimulus is calculated as the average ensemble activation across all bins pre- and during the stimulus. Decoding accuracy was estimated using leave-one-out cross-validation, where one trial was held out as the test observations and the remaining trials were used to estimate the Gaussian likelihood parameters for each stimulus. This procedure was repeated across multiple time bins size yielding the highest mean decoding accuracy, which was selected as optimal ensemble detection.

Mutual information

To help disambiguate between optimal time bin for ensemble synchronization, mutual information (MI) was calculated between the ensemble evoked response (RST) and the stimulus, for each ensemble and bin size. Here, RST refers to the time-resolved activation strength of a given ensemble computed at a given temporal bin size for the duration of the stimulus playback minus the period that preceded the playback. MI was computed using the mutual_info_regression function from the scikit-learn package in python.80 This function calculates MI based on the entropy estimation from k-nearest-neighbors distance.81 This approach does not assume linearity or normal distribution. The resulting MI values reflect the amount of stimulus-related information carried by each ensemble for each temporal bin-size.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in Python.82,83,84,85 To compare unpaired groups, we used general linear mixed models (GLM). For single unit analysis, the GLM model included stimulus type, cell type, sex and hemisphere, while bird ID was considered a random effect. For ensemble level analysis, the GLM model included stimulus type, and sex, while hemisphere and bird ID were considered random effects. For post hoc analysis we used the Tukey test. To determine if two samples are drawn from the same distribution, we used the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test. Statistical details of the experiments and analysis, including statistical results, sample size (n), and measures of center and dispersion are indicated in the results text and figure legends.
