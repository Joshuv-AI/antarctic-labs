Belonging and life satisfaction among migrants: the roles of identity, social comparison, and discrimination
Authors: Devrim Dumludag, Ozge Gokdemir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examines how belonging, identity orientations, social comparison patterns, and perceived discrimination are associated with life satisfaction among Turkish migrants in the Netherlands. The analysis is based on cross-sectional survey data from 1,006 respondents. Ordered logit models are used to assess the associations between identity orientations, comparison patterns, perceived discrimination, and two outcomes: life satisfaction and sense of belonging to the Netherlands. The results indicate that identifying as Dutch or as a world citizen, as well as placing greater importance on comparisons with Dutch natives, is positively associated with both life satisfaction and host-country belonging. In contrast, emphasizing comparisons with origin-based reference groups and reporting higher levels of perceived discrimination are negatively associated with these outcomes. Participation in non-governmental organizations and longer residence in the Netherlands are positively associated with belonging. These patterns are robust across model specifications. The findings highlight the role of cognitive and evaluative dimensions of integration, alongside socio-economic factors, in understanding migrant well-being.Keywords: Sense of belonging, Life satisfaction, Social comparison, Identity, Discrimination, Turkish immigrants, The Netherlands

Introduction

A substantial body of research has examined the relationship between migration, integration, and subjective well-being, consistently documenting that immigrants in Europe tend to report lower life satisfaction than members of the native population [1–5]. Comprehensive reviews of the migration and happiness literature further show that migrants’ well-being is associated with a complex interplay of structural, social, and psychological factors [6–9]. Explanations for well-being gaps have typically emphasized structural conditions such as employment, income, education, and language proficiency [10]. While these factors are undoubtedly important, they do not fully account for variation in migrants’ subjective evaluations of their lives. Increasingly, attention has shifted toward psychological processes particularly identity, social comparison, and belonging as key correlates of integration and well-being.

Among these dimensions, sense of belonging reflects not merely participation in social institutions, but an internalized perception of one’s place within a collective context. It can therefore be understood both as an outcome of psychological integration and as a dimension of subjective well-being. Despite its conceptual relevance, belonging is often treated descriptively rather than as a process embedded in broader cognitive and identity-based mechanisms. In this study, belonging is examined not only as a component of life satisfaction, but also as an outcome associated with identity orientations, social comparison patterns, and perceived discrimination. This approach allows us to analyze belonging and life satisfaction as related yet analytically distinct dimensions of psychological integration.

Building on this perspective, the study conceptualizes belonging as shaped by two interrelated psychological processes: identity orientation and social comparison. Drawing on social identity theory [11] and social comparison theory [12], it focuses on how individuals evaluate their position within the social environment. Social identity theory suggests that individuals derive part of their self-concept and well-being from group membership and perceived inclusion, whereas social comparison theory emphasizes that individuals evaluate their status relative to relevant reference groups. Together, these frameworks provide a basis for examining how identity orientations and comparison patterns are associated with belonging and life satisfaction. Identity labels alone, such as identifying as Dutch, Turkish, or European may not fully capture psychological integration. Rather, the direction and salience of social comparison may constitute a more immediate cognitive mechanism through which migrants interpret their status, inclusion, and progress.

In this sense, comparison orientation is not merely a demographic or migration-related characteristic, but a cognitive frame that structures how individuals interpret their social environment. Consistent with recent work emphasizing the flexibility of comparison groups among migrants, individuals may evaluate their position relative to multiple reference groups, including host-society members, co-ethnic communities, and populations in the country of origin [13]. Comparisons with members of the host majority may reflect an outward-oriented frame associated with engagement in the host society and with perceived opportunities for upward mobility, whereas comparisons confined to co-ethnic or origin-based groups may reflect a more inward-looking frame of reference. These orientations are likely to be associated with both attachment to the host society and overall life satisfaction, as they relate to perceptions of status, recognition, and opportunity.

Intercultural identification further complicates this process. Integrating elements of heritage and host-country identity can function as a psychological resource, being associated with higher self-esteem and social connectedness [14–17]. Conversely, experiences of discrimination may disrupt this integration process by signaling exclusion, thereby being associated with weaker belonging and lower well-being [18–20]. Importantly, discrimination and comparison processes may be interrelated: exclusionary experiences may shift comparison frames, while comparison frames may shape how exclusion is perceived and interpreted.

Focusing on Turkish migrants in the Netherlands, one of Europe’s most established and socio-economically heterogeneous migrant communities, this study integrates identity orientations, comparison patterns, and perceived discrimination within a unified psychological framework. Rather than treating these variables as parallel predictors, it conceptualizes them as components of a cognitive identity system through which migrants evaluate their position in the host society. By examining their joint associations with both belonging and life satisfaction, the study aims to examine whether comparison orientation constitutes an empirically distinct dimension beyond identity labels alone.

Specifically, the analysis examines the associations between identity orientations, social comparison patterns, discrimination, belonging, and life satisfaction. Based on the theoretical framework, we expect that (i) stronger attachment to the host society is positively associated with life satisfaction; (ii) more inclusive identity orientations (e.g., identification as Dutch or as a world citizen) are positively associated with belonging and well-being; (iii) comparison oriented toward the host majority is positively associated with both outcomes, whereas comparison with co-ethnic or origin-based groups is negatively associated; and (iv) perceived discrimination is negatively associated with both belonging and life satisfaction. These expectations guide the empirical analysis.

Conceptual framework and descriptive overview

This section first outlines the conceptual framework and then provides descriptive statistics to place the empirical analysis.

Life satisfaction

Life satisfaction constitutes a central subjective indicator of overall well-being and reflects the extent to which individuals evaluate their lives positively and holistically [21–23]. Since the mid-twentieth century, research across psychology, economics, and sociology has examined its determinants across social, demographic, economic, institutional, and environmental domains [24–28]. In empirical applications, life satisfaction is typically measured through global self-evaluation items on ordered scales, commonly ranging from 0 to 10, allowing for both descriptive and regression-based analysis.

Table 1 shows that the mean life satisfaction score among Turkish migrants in the Netherlands is 6.72 (SD = 2.21). This level is below the national Dutch average of approximately 7.5 [29, 30] consistent with prior studies documenting comparatively lower well-being among non-Western migrant groups in the Netherlands [3, 5, 20, 31–34]. This pattern places the present sample within the broader literature on migrant well-being gaps.

Life satisfaction varies across migration cohorts. Respondents born in the Netherlands report the highest average level (7.38), closely approximating the national benchmark. Individuals who arrived before age 15 report a mean of 7.20, whereas those who migrated at age 15 or later report a substantially lower average of 6.30. These differences are consistent with the interpretation that earlier arrival is associated with more favorable life evaluations, potentially reflecting earlier exposure and socialization within the host context.

Variation is also observed across duration of residence. As shown in Table 1, individuals residing in the Netherlands for 30–39 years report relatively high mean levels (6.89), followed closely by recent arrivals (6.84). Life satisfaction is lower among those with 10–19 years of residence (6.44), before increasing modestly among those residing for more than 40 years (6.67), although the latter subgroup is relatively small. While these patterns are descriptive, they suggest a non-linear association between time spent in the host society and subjective well-being.

Although the observed differences are moderate in magnitude, additional tests indicate that they are statistically significant. In particular, individuals who arrived before age 15 report significantly higher life satisfaction than those who arrived later (mean difference ≈ 0.83, p < 0.001), suggesting that the observed variation is unlikely to be driven by random fluctuations.

Taken together, the descriptive evidence indicates that both generation and duration of residence are associated with variation in life satisfaction. Whether these associations persist once identity orientations, comparison patterns, discrimination experiences, and socio-economic factors are taken into account is examined in the regression analysis in "Results" secion.

Sense of belonging

Sense of belonging has been described as a fundamental human motivation [35], capturing individuals’ perceived acceptance and embeddedness within a social context. In migration research, belonging refers to the spatial and emotional domain in which individuals experience themselves as “at home” [36]. Prior studies emphasize that belonging operates across multiple levels, such as neighborhood, city, and nation, and may extend across both host and origin contexts [37–42].

In the present study, belonging is measured through respondents’ self-identification of where they primarily feel they belong, with response options including neighborhood, city, the Netherlands, Türkiye, or other. A binary indicator is constructed to capture whether respondents identify the Netherlands as their primary place of belonging. As shown in Appendix Table 5, a majority of respondents (55 percent in the full sample) identify Türkiye as their primary place of belonging, indicating that attachment to the origin country remains salient even among long-term residents. Smaller proportions select neighborhood (5 percent), city (14 percent), or other locations (4 percent), highlighting the multidimensional nature of belonging among migrant populations.

As reported in Table 1, 22 percent of the full sample indicate belonging primarily to the Netherlands. This proportion increases to 32 percent among Dutch-born respondents and to 29 percent among those who arrived before age 15, whereas only 15 percent of those who migrated at age 15 or later select the Netherlands as their primary place of belonging. These differences mirror the generational gradients observed in life satisfaction and suggest that earlier exposure to the host context is associated with stronger host-country attachment.

Belonging also varies by duration of residence. Approximately 30 percent of individuals residing 30–39 years in the Netherlands identify the country as their primary place of belonging, compared to 13–14 percent among those with less than 20 years of residence. Although the proportion is slightly lower (28 percent) among those residing more than 40 years, this group remains among the highest across duration categories.

Descriptively, longer exposure to the host context is associated with stronger attachment to the Netherlands and higher life satisfaction. However, these patterns remain descriptive at this stage. The regression analysis below examines whether these associations persist once psychological processes such as identity orientations, comparison patterns, and perceived discrimination are taken into account.

Data and methods

Study design and data collection

This study is based on cross-sectional survey data collected between September 2012 and June 2013 to examine psychological integration processes among Turkish migrants living in the Netherlands. The survey includes a unique combination of variables on identity orientations, social comparison across multiple reference groups, perceived discrimination, and belonging, dimensions that are rarely jointly available in more recent datasets. As the analysis focuses on the relationships between these psychological and social mechanisms rather than on temporal dynamics, the data provide a suitable basis for examining the proposed associations.

The final dataset consists of 1,006 respondents. Data were collected primarily through face-to-face interviews conducted in Turkish at the Turkish Consulate in Rotterdam. Participation was voluntary, and the survey lasted approximately 30 min. To increase coverage and variation, additional respondents were recruited from different cities in the Netherlands through direct contact.1

The sampling strategy aimed to capture a heterogeneous group of Turkish migrants across regions and socio-economic backgrounds. While the sample is not fully representative of the broader Turkish migrant population in the Netherlands, it exhibits substantial variation in demographic, socio-economic, and migration-related characteristics, allowing for meaningful analysis of the relationships between key variables.

Variables and measurement

The questionnaire included more than 30 items covering life satisfaction, belonging, identity orientations, social comparison patterns, perceived and experienced discrimination, civic participation, and socio-demographic characteristics. Descriptive statistics are reported in Table 5 in the Appendix.

Dependent variables

The two dependent variables in the regression analyses are life satisfaction and sense of belonging.

Life satisfaction was measured using the widely employed single-item question: “All things considered, how satisfied are you with your life as a whole these days?” Responses were recorded on an eleven-point scale ranging from 0 (completely dissatisfied) to 10 (completely satisfied). Consistent with standard practice in well-being research [43], the variable is treated as ordinal in the main analyses.

Sense of belonging was measured by asking respondents where they feel they belong. Participants selected one of five categories: the neighborhood where they live, the city where they live, the Netherlands, Türkiye, or another place. To focus on host-society attachment, a binary indicator was constructed that takes the value 1 if the respondent selected “the Netherlands” and 0 otherwise. While belonging is inherently multidimensional, this operationalization isolates primary host-country attachment, which is central to the study’s focus on integration outcomes. The implications of this dichotomization are discussed in the limitations section.

Independent variables

Identity orientations were measured through multiple self-identification categories. Respondents were allowed to select more than one identity label, including Turkish, Muslim, Dutch citizen, European Union citizen, world citizen, and other. Each identity was coded as a binary indicator and treated as a distinct orientation rather than as part of a single latent construct.

Social comparison was measured by asking respondents: “What is the importance of comparing yourself with the following groups?” The reference groups include Dutch natives, Turkish migrants in the Netherlands, other migrants in the Netherlands, and people living in Türkiye. Responses were recorded on a three-point scale ranging from completely unimportant to very important. Each comparison orientation is analyzed separately, consistent with social comparison theory, which emphasizes target-specific comparisons rather than assuming a single underlying comparison tendency. In line with the conceptual framework, identity orientations and comparison patterns are modeled as observed and analytically distinct dimensions, rather than as indicators of a latent construct.

Perceived and experienced discrimination were captured using two binary indicators. The first measures whether the respondent has ever felt discriminated against because of their ethnicity, while the second captures whether the respondent perceives discrimination to exist in the Netherlands. These variables reflect distinct but related dimensions of perceived exclusion and are included separately in the analyses.

Control variables

The regression models control for a range of demographic and socio-economic characteristics commonly associated with subjective well-being. These include gender, age (and age squared to account for non-linear effects), years since migration, generational status, household income, household size, number of children, marital status, education, employment status, religiosity, participation in non-governmental organizations, and a retrospective measure of life satisfaction five years prior.

The retrospective life satisfaction measure is included to account for baseline differences in subjective well-being. Although it does not provide a true panel structure, it captures prior life evaluations that may reflect relatively stable individual characteristics, such as personality traits or persistent life circumstances, thereby helping to reduce potential omitted variable bias.

Analytical strategy

The empirical analysis examines the associations between identity orientations, social comparison patterns, discrimination, and two outcomes: life satisfaction and belonging to the Netherlands. The analytical approach is guided by the expectations outlined in the introduction, with an emphasis on assessing whether consistent patterns of association emerge across conceptually distinct variables. Rather than relying on stepwise model building, the analysis focuses on the stability and structure of associations across specifications.

Given the ordinal nature of life satisfaction (0–10 scale), ordered logit models are used as the primary specification. This approach allows for estimating relationships across ordered response categories without imposing cardinality assumptions. Sense of belonging is treated as a binary outcome and estimated using logistic regression. The proportional odds (parallel lines) assumption underlying the ordered logit model was tested using standard post-estimation procedures, and no systematic violations were detected. Pseudo R2 values are reported as indicative measures of model fit.2

Given that belonging is analyzed both as an outcome and in relation to life satisfaction, its potential role as a mediating mechanism was also explored. To this end, a structural equation model (SEM) with bootstrap standard errors was estimated. While identity and comparison variables are significantly associated with belonging, belonging is not significantly associated with life satisfaction, and no statistically significant indirect effects are observed. These findings do not support a mediating role for belonging. Accordingly, the analysis proceeds with separate regression models, allowing for more transparent interpretation of the observed associations.3

In addition, subgroup analyses based on age at arrival are conducted to examine whether the observed associations differ across migration cohorts. This distinction is motivated by research highlighting age at arrival as a key determinant of socialization processes and integration trajectories. The subgroup analysis therefore allows for assessing whether patterns observed in the full sample vary across cohorts with differing levels of exposure to the host-country context.

Results

This section presents empirical findings in a structured manner. We first report the main results for life satisfaction ("Life satisfaction" section) and belonging to the Netherlands ("Sense of belonging to the Netherlands" section), in line with the expectations outlined in the Introduction. We then examine whether these patterns differ across migration cohorts ("Age-at-arrival subsample analyses" section), followed by a series of robustness checks ("Robustness checks" section).

The analysis focuses on the associations between identity orientations, social comparison patterns, discrimination, and the two outcome variables: life satisfaction and sense of belonging to the Netherlands. Given the ordinal nature of life satisfaction (0–10 scale), ordered logit models are used as the primary specification, with heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors reported throughout. As a robustness check, all models were re-estimated using ordinary least squares (OLS), yielding substantively similar patterns of association and indicating that the findings are not sensitive to the choice of estimator. All coefficients are reported as log-odds estimates. Results at the 10% level are interpreted as suggestive rather than conclusive.

Life satisfaction

Consistent with the expectations outlined in the Introduction, Model 1 in Table 2 reports the estimated associations with life satisfaction. Household income shows the strongest positive association (β = 1.155, p < 0.01), highlighting the role of economic resources in subjective well-being. Among integration-related variables, belonging to the Netherlands is positively associated with life satisfaction at the 10% level (β = 0.294, p < 0.10), although this relationship should be interpreted with caution. In contrast, belonging at more local levels (neighborhood or city) is not statistically significant. Identity orientations do not exhibit independent associations with life satisfaction once other factors are taken into account.

In contrast, social comparison patterns display consistent and substantively meaningful associations. Placing greater importance on comparison with Dutch natives is positively associated with life satisfaction (β = 0.252, p < 0.05). Conversely, assigning higher importance to comparisons with Turkish migrants in the Netherlands (β = − 0.791, p < 0.01) or with people living in Türkiye (β = − 0.791, p < 0.01) is negatively associated with life satisfaction. Comparisons with other migrant groups are not statistically significant.

These findings are consistent with the broader literature on relative income and social comparison, which emphasizes that subjective well-being depends not only on absolute resources but also on the reference groups individuals use for evaluation [44–46].

Finally, experienced discrimination is negatively associated with life satisfaction (β = − 0.577, p < 0.01), while perceived discrimination does not reach statistical significance.

In sum, life satisfaction is most strongly associated with economic resources, prior well-being, experienced discrimination, and the direction of social comparison. Identity categories do not show independent associations once these factors are accounted for.

Sense of belonging to the Netherlands

In line with the second set of expectations, Model 2 in Table 2 reports the estimated associations with belonging to the Netherlands. In contrast to life satisfaction, the pattern of associations differs across domains.

Household income is not significantly associated with belonging, suggesting that economic resources alone are not closely linked to emotional attachment to the host society. In contrast, civic engagement appears more relevant. Participation in NGO activities is positively associated with belonging (β = 0.313, p < 0.05), indicating that social and institutional involvement is related to stronger attachment.

Identity orientations display clearer associations in this model than in the life satisfaction model. Identifying as a Dutch citizen is positively associated with belonging at the 10% level (β = 1.132, p < 0.10), while identifying as a world citizen is strongly positively associated (β = 1.252, p < 0.01). Other identity categories are not statistically significant. These findings suggest that more inclusive or outward-oriented identities are associated with stronger attachment to the Netherlands.

Social comparison variables again show consistent and robust associations. Placing greater importance on comparison with Dutch natives is positively associated with belonging (β = 0.644, p < 0.01). In contrast, emphasizing comparisons with Turkish migrants in the Netherlands (β = − 0.647, p < 0.01) or with people in Türkiye (β = − 0.488, p < 0.01) is negatively associated with belonging. Comparisons with other migrant groups are not statistically significant. The direction of comparison toward the host majority versus toward co-ethnic and origin groups appears closely linked to variation in host-country attachment.

Perceived discrimination is negatively associated with belonging (β = − 0.555, p < 0.05), whereas experienced discrimination is not statistically significant. This pattern suggests that generalized perceptions of exclusion are more closely related to belonging than specific reported experiences.

In sum, belonging to the Netherlands is most strongly associated with duration of exposure to the host context, civic engagement, identity orientations, perceived discrimination, and the direction of social comparison. In contrast to life satisfaction, belonging is not significantly associated with income or most socio-demographic characteristics, underscoring its more relational and psychological nature.

To further assess the role of belonging in the relationship between identity, social comparison, discrimination, and life satisfaction, a structural equation model with bootstrap standard errors (5,000 replications) was estimated. Identity and comparison variables are significantly associated with belonging, and discrimination is negatively related to belonging. However, belonging is not significantly associated with life satisfaction (p = 0.119), and bootstrap tests do not support statistically significant indirect effects. These results do not provide evidence for a mediating role of belonging. Detailed results are available upon request.

Age-at-arrival subsample analyses

To examine whether the associations reported in Table 2 vary across migration cohorts, separate models were estimated for two groups defined by age at arrival. Following established migration research [47], respondents who migrated after age 14 are classified as first generation, whereas those who arrived before age 15 include the 1.5, second, and third generations.

Age 15 is frequently used as a developmental threshold in migration studies [47]. By this stage, individuals have typically consolidated key aspects of identity and socialization shaped by family and cultural context [48]. Migration after this period may therefore be associated with distinct adaptation patterns compared with earlier arrival [49]. Estimating separate models allows for examining whether the associations between identity orientations, comparison patterns, discrimination, and well-being differ across cohorts with differing socialization experiences.

The results presented in Table 3 indicate that, among respondents who arrived before age 15, several patterns mirror those observed in the full sample. Placing greater importance to comparison with Dutch natives is positively associated with life satisfaction (β = 0.382, p < 0.05), whereas emphasizing comparison with people in Türkiye is negatively associated (β = − 0.334, p < 0.05). Household income remains positively associated with well-being (β = 0.904, p < 0.05), and perceived discrimination is negatively associated (β = − 0.576, p < 0.05). These results suggest that economic resources, outward-oriented comparison, and perceived exclusion are jointly associated with variation in subjective well-being within this cohort.

A stronger sense of belonging to the city of residence is positively associated with life satisfaction (β = 0.579, p < 0.05), highlighting the salience of local attachment among later-arriving migrants. These differences suggest that the timing of migration is associated with variation in how identity, comparison orientation, and integration experiences relate to subjective well-being.

Across both subsamples, host-oriented comparison patterns display clear and systematic associations. Among respondents who arrived before age 15, placing greater importance on comparison with Dutch natives is strongly and positively associated with belonging (β = 0.790, p < 0.01), whereas emphasizing comparison with Turkish migrants in the Netherlands (β = − 0.812, p < 0.01) or with people in Türkiye (β = − 0.497, p < 0.05) is negatively associated. A similar pattern is observed among those who migrated after age 14: comparison with Dutch natives remains positively associated at the 10% level (β = 0.579, p < 0.10), while comparison with Turkish migrants (β = − 0.626, p < 0.10) and with people in Türkiye (β = − 0.591, p < 0.05) is negatively associated. These findings highlight the consistent role of comparison targets in relation to variation in host-country attachment across cohorts.

Identity orientations display a more selective pattern. Identifying as a world citizen is strongly and positively associated with belonging in both groups (β = 1.328, p < 0.01 for earlier arrivals; β = 1.709, p < 0.01 for later arrivals), with larger coefficients among those who migrated after age 14. Other identity categories do not reach statistical significance, suggesting that broader, more inclusive identity orientations are more closely associated with attachment to the host country.

Differences across cohorts become more apparent when considering civic engagement and discrimination. Among respondents who arrived before age 15, participation in NGO activities is positively associated with belonging (β = 0.371, p < 0.05), indicating that civic involvement is linked to stronger attachment in this group. In contrast, this association is not statistically significant among those who migrated later in life. For this latter group, perceived discrimination is negatively associated with belonging (β = − 0.890, p < 0.05), whereas this relationship is not statistically significant among earlier arrivals. These patterns suggest that the association between perceived societal exclusion and belonging is more pronounced among later-arriving migrants.

Life satisfaction does not exhibit a statistically significant association with belonging in either subsample, reinforcing the earlier finding that the two constructs, while conceptually related, capture distinct dimensions of integration.

The subgroup analyses indicate that the associations between comparison patterns and belonging remain broadly consistent across migration cohorts. However, the relative importance of civic participation and perceived discrimination differs by age at arrival, suggesting that the timing of migration is associated with variation in how social engagement and exclusion experiences relate to attachment to the host society.

Robustness checks

To evaluate the stability of the findings, several robustness analyses were conducted. First, multicollinearity diagnostics were assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF) estimated from equivalent OLS specifications. For the key predictors, particularly social comparison variables, discrimination measures, identity categories, and belonging indicators, VIF values were below 3.0, indicating no evidence of challenging multicollinearity.

Second, the ordered logit models were re-estimated using ordinary least squares (OLS). The direction, magnitude, and statistical significance of the main associations, especially those related to comparison orientation, discrimination, income, and belonging, remained substantively similar, indicating that the findings are not sensitive to the choice of estimator.

Third, alternative model specifications excluding selected blocks of control variables (e.g., socio-demographic or identity covariates) did not materially alter the core associations between comparison orientation, discrimination, belonging, and life satisfaction.

Finally, as an additional robustness test, an alternative host-territory belonging indicator was constructed by combining attachment to the neighborhood, city, or the Netherlands. Logistic regression results were substantively consistent with the main specification: comparison with Dutch natives remained positively associated with belonging, whereas comparison with people in Türkiye and perceived discrimination were negatively associated. Years since migration and participation in non-governmental organizations also retained positive associations. These results indicate that the observed relationships are not dependent on defining belonging narrowly at the national level.

Although the cross-sectional design limits causal inference and all measures are self-reported, the consistency of the results across estimators and specifications suggests that the findings are not driven by model specification or multicollinearity.

Discussion

This study examined how identity orientations, social comparison frames, perceived discrimination, and belonging are jointly associated with life satisfaction among Turkish migrants in the Netherlands. Rather than conceptualizing integration solely in structural or demographic terms, the findings point to the importance of cognitive and evaluative processes through which individuals interpret their position in the host society. In particular, the results highlight that psychological integration is not only shaped by who individuals identify as, but also by how they evaluate themselves relative to relevant social groups.

A central insight emerging from the analysis is that social comparison orientation is more consistently associated with both subjective well-being and host-country attachment than identity categories alone. Across models, outward-oriented comparison, particularly with Dutch natives, is associated with higher life satisfaction and stronger belonging, whereas inward-oriented comparison with co-ethnic groups or with people in Türkiye is associated with lower levels of both outcomes. This pattern suggests that comparison orientation functions as a key cognitive mechanism through which migrants evaluate status, opportunity, and inclusion, beyond what can be explained by demographic characteristics or identity labels.

The direction of these associations is consistent with social comparison theory. Comparison with the host majority may reflect an outward-oriented frame linked to engagement with broader societal reference groups and perceived opportunities for upward mobility. In contrast, comparison confined to co-ethnic or origin-based groups may reflect a more bounded evaluative frame, potentially associated with more limited perceptions of opportunity and recognition. From this perspective, variation in well-being and belonging appears to be closely tied to the reference groups individuals use to interpret their social position.

In comparison, identity orientations display a more selective and domain-specific role. While identifying as a world citizen and in some cases as Dutch is positively associated with belonging, identity categories are less consistently related to life satisfaction. This suggests that self-categorization alone may be insufficient to account for variation in well-being without considering the evaluative frameworks through which individuals interpret their circumstances.

The relationship between belonging and life satisfaction further supports the interpretation that these outcomes capture distinct dimensions of integration. Although belonging is positively associated with life satisfaction, mediation analyses do not provide evidence that it serves as a mechanism linking identity or comparison to well-being. This indicates that belonging and life satisfaction may operate as parallel, rather than sequential, dimensions of psychological integration, each shaped by overlapping but distinct processes.

The results also underscore the role of perceived and experienced discrimination. Both are negatively associated with well-being and belonging, consistent with prior research showing that exclusionary experiences undermine subjective well-being and attachment [19, 20]. At the same time, the findings suggest that belonging is particularly sensitive to perceived recognition and fairness within the host society, highlighting that integration involves not only participation but also the subjective experience of inclusion.

Structural factors such as civic participation and length of residence are positively associated with belonging, indicating that engagement and exposure contribute to attachment to the host society. However, these factors show weaker and less consistent associations with life satisfaction compared to comparison orientation and discrimination. This distinction suggests that while structural integration may facilitate attachment, evaluative and cognitive processes play a more central role in shaping subjective well-being.

These findings contribute to a growing body of research emphasizing the importance of relative evaluation in diverse societies. While much of the migration literature has focused on structural indicators such as income, employment, and education, the present results reinforce the idea that how individuals evaluate their position relative to relevant reference groups is closely associated with well-being outcomes [44]. By jointly examining identity orientations and comparison frames, the analysis highlights that evaluative processes represent a distinct dimension of integration that complements structural explanations.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the observed associations may reflect reciprocal dynamics. Second, belonging is operationalized as a binary indicator, which does not capture its multidimensional nature. Third, identity is measured through categorical self-labels rather than graded scales, potentially limiting sensitivity to identity complexity. Finally, the reliance on self-reported measures raises the possibility of common-method variance, although diagnostic checks do not indicate severe specification concerns.

Conclusion

This study contributes to research on migration and well-being by demonstrating that social comparison orientation is consistently associated with both life satisfaction and belonging among Turkish migrants in the Netherlands. The findings suggest that well-being in multicultural contexts is shaped not only by economic resources and formal integration, but also by how individuals evaluate their position relative to relevant reference groups. More broadly, the results highlight the importance of incorporating cognitive and evaluative dimensions, alongside structural indicators, into analyses of integration.

The findings also underscore that belonging is not merely a function of legal status or duration of residence, but is closely associated with relational and evaluative processes linked to perceived recognition and inclusion. From a policy perspective, this suggests that efforts aimed at reducing discrimination, fostering inclusive civic engagement, and encouraging cross-group interaction may contribute to both structural and psychological aspects of integration.

Future research employing longitudinal and multi-method designs could further examine how identity orientations and comparison frames evolve over time, and how these processes relate to changes in well-being in increasingly diverse societies. The results suggest that understanding integration requires attention not only to where migrants are positioned, but also to how they interpret and evaluate that position within the host society.
