The Truncation Blind Spot: How Decoding Strategies Systematically Exclude Human-Like Token Choices
Esteban Garces Arias, Nurzhan Sapargali, Christian Heumann, Matthias Aßenmacher

Abstract
Standard decoding strategies for text generation, including top- k k , nucleus sampling, and contrastive search, select tokens based on likelihood, restricting selection to high-probability regions. Human language production operates differently: tokens are chosen for communicative appropriateness rather than statistical frequency. This mismatch creates a truncation blind spot: contextually appropriate but statistically rare tokens remain accessible to humans yet unreachable by likelihood-based decoding. We hypothesize this contributes to the detectability of machine-generated text. Analyzing over 1.8 million texts across eight language models, five decoding strategies, and 53 hyperparameter configurations, we find that 8–18% of human-selected tokens fall outside typical truncation boundaries. Simple classifiers trained on predictability and lexical diversity achieve remarkable detection rates. Crucially, neither model scale nor architecture correlates strongly with detectability; truncation parameters account for most variance. Configurations achieving low detectability often produce incoherent text, indicating that evading detection and producing natural text are distinct objectives. These findings suggest detectability is enhanced by likelihood-based token selection, not merely a matter of model capability. Codebase and data: https://github.com/EstebanGarces/human_vs_machine .

This paper investigates a mechanistic explanation rooted in how decoding strategies select tokens.
The dominant approaches to open-ended text generation—top-
k
sampling
(
9
)
, nucleus sampling
(
18

(

Human language production operates differently. Psycholinguistic models describe how communicative intentions are transformed into word choices based on contextual appropriateness rather than statistical frequency
(
28
;
21
;
15
)
. A rare but precisely appropriate word remains accessible to a human writer, while a common but contextually unsuitable word may be discarded. This asymmetry—likelihood-based truncation excluding statistically rare tokens regardless of appropriateness, versus intent-driven selection excluding contextually inappropriate tokens regardless of frequency
(
42
)
—creates what we term the
truncation blind spot
: tokens that are communicatively valuable but statistically rare, accessible to human writers yet systematically excluded by likelihood-based decoding. This mechanism may relate to recent observations that different language models converge on similar outputs for open-ended queries
(
25
)
, potentially because likelihood-based decoding constrains all models to similar regions of token space. We hypothesize that this distributional mismatch contributes to the detectability of LLM-generated text (cf. Figure
1
), generating specific empirical predictions tested through four research questions:

RQ1:

Blind Spot Magnitude:
To what extent does likelihood-based truncation exclude tokens that humans actually select?

RQ2:

Detection Signal:
Does the distributional mismatch provide a reliable signal for distinguishing machine-generated from human-written text?

RQ3:

Mechanism Primacy:
Is detectability primarily determined by model characteristics or by the truncation mechanism itself?

RQ4:

Quality Trade-off:
Can decoding configurations achieve low detectability while maintaining text quality?

Since we cannot directly observe human token-selection distributions, we use measurable proxies:
predictability
(average log-likelihood under a reference model) and
lexical diversity
(
n
-gram variety). We conduct a large-scale empirical study using over 1.8 million text samples from eight language models across five decoding strategies and 53 hyperparameter configurations.

Our findings reveal that: (1) the truncation blind spot is substantial, with 8–18% of human-selected tokens excluded under standard configurations (
RQ1
); (2) simple classifiers using only predictability and diversity achieve over 96% detection accuracy (
RQ2
); (3) detectability is primarily determined by truncation parameters rather than model scale or architecture, with configurations spanning near-random to near-perfect classification (
RQ3
); and (4) low-detectability configurations often produce incoherent text, indicating that evading detection and producing natural text are distinct objectives (
RQ4
). Importantly, this phenomenon persists across Transformer, state-space (Mamba), and RNN-based (RWKV) architectures, confirming that the truncation mechanism itself is the primary determinant.

2
Theoretical Framework: Likelihood-Based vs. Intent-Driven Selection

We now formalize the comparison between likelihood-based truncation (as implemented in standard decoding strategies) and intent-driven selection (as conceptualized in human language production).

2.1
Likelihood-Based Truncation in Language Models

Let
\mathcal{M}
be an autoregressive language model with vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
. For each token
x_{t}\in\mathcal{V}
at step
t
, given a context
\mathbf{x}_{<t}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{t-1})
, the model produces a categorical distribution vector
\mathbf{p}_{t}
on the probability simplex
\Delta^{|\mathcal{V}|-1}
, where each entry is the scalar probability
P_{\mathcal{M}}(x_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{<t})
.

A
decoding strategy
is a function
\mathcal{S}:\Delta^{|\mathcal{V}|-1}\to 2^{\mathcal{V}}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
that maps
\mathbf{p}_{t}
to a truncation set
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
. Selection of the next token is then performed on the distribution
\mathbf{p}_{t}
renormalized over
\mathcal{T}
. Common decoding strategies include top-
k
sampling, which retains the
k
most probable tokens:

\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{x}_{<t})=\{x\in\mathcal{V}\mid\text{rank}(x\mid\mathbf{x}_{<t})\leq k\}

where

\footnotesize\text{rank}(x_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{<t})=\left|\left\{v\in\mathcal{V}:P_{\mathcal{M}}(v\mid\mathbf{x}_{<t})\geq P_{\mathcal{M}}(x_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{<t})\right\}\right|

with ties broken by a deterministic rule (e.g., lexicographic order).
Similarly, in nucleus (top-
p
) sampling, the truncation set
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{x}_{<t})
contains the minimum number of top-ranked tokens whose cumulative probability mass reaches the threshold
p
. Other decoding strategies, such as contrastive search and beam search, also operate on high-probability tokens, scoring them according to some auxiliary criteria.

We formally define a neural text generator as a tuple
\theta=(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{S})
, which yields a specific truncation set
\mathcal{T}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{<t})
for a given context. The
excluded region

\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{<t})=\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{T}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{<t})
contains all tokens with zero probability of being selected under the generator
\theta
.

2.2
Intent-Driven Selection and the Blind Spot

Human language production can be conceptualized as drawing from a distribution
P_{\mathcal{H}}(x_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{<t},\mathcal{I})
, where
\mathcal{I}
captures latent parameters encoding the speaker’s communicative intent, including discourse goals, audience modeling, register constraints, and pragmatic considerations
(
28
;
42
)
. While neither
\mathcal{I}
nor the functional form of
P_{\mathcal{H}}
are directly observable, we can characterize the human selection mechanism conceptually through its realizations.

A human writer typically chooses words based on qualitative criteria such as contextual appropriateness, grammatical well-formedness, and stylistic expectations
(
38
;
7
)
. Unlike the likelihood-driven selection of LLMs, human selection is not primarily governed by statistical frequency. Consequently, these two mechanisms produce distinct partitions of the vocabulary for any given context; the human truncation set,
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbf{x}_{<t})
, does not necessarily correspond to the model’s truncation set,
\mathcal{T}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{<t})
.

.

2.3
Quantifying the Blind Spot

We are ultimately interested in quantifying the extent to which human-generated tokens fall into the blind spot at any generation step
t
. Formally, we seek to estimate the probability of the event that, given the context
\mathbf{x}_{<t}
, a random token
X_{t}
lies in the intersection of the human inclusion set and the model’s exclusion region under the true token distribution, i.e., the distribution of the human speaker:

\displaystyle P_{\mathcal{H}}(X_{t}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbf{x}_{<t})

\displaystyle\cap\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{<t}))

\displaystyle=P_{\mathcal{H}}(X_{t}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbf{x}_{<t})\land X_{t}\notin\mathcal{T}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{<t})).

Let
\mathbf{x}^{H}=(x^{H}_{1},\ldots,x^{H}_{T})
denote an observed sequence of human-generated tokens. To bridge the gap between empirical observation and theoretical probability, we ground our analysis in information theory
(
40
;
6
)
. Acknowledging that natural language is non-stationary over arbitrary timescales, we assume local stationarity within homogeneous thematic contexts, such as formal journalism, encyclopedic discourse, and scientific writing. Accordingly, we model the human token sequence as a realization of a stochastic process
\{X^{H}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
that admits an ergodic approximation, thereby justifying the substitution of time averages for ensemble averages over the finite sample of our corpus.
We define the
exclusion indicator

Z_{t}
as a binary random variable representing the event that a human token
X^{H}_{t}
falls outside the model’s truncation set given the history
\mathbf{X}^{H}_{<t}
:

Z_{t}=\mathbf{1}\left[X^{H}_{t}\notin\mathcal{T}_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}^{H}_{<t})\right],

where
\mathbf{1}[\cdot]
is the indicator function. Since the mapping from the sequence
X^{H}
to the exclusion status is a time-invariant transformation, the derived process
\{Z_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
inherits the properties of stationarity and ergodicity from
\{X^{H}_{t}\}
.
Under the human distribution
P_{\mathcal{H}}
, the event
X^{H}_{t}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbf{X}^{H}_{<t})
occurs with probability 1 by definition. Consequently, the joint probability of the blind spot simplifies to the marginal probability of exclusion:

\displaystyle P_{\mathcal{H}}(X^{H}_{t}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbf{X}^{H}_{<t})\cap\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}^{H}_{<t}))

\displaystyle=P_{\mathcal{H}}(X^{H}_{t}\notin\mathcal{T}_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}^{H}_{<t}))

\displaystyle=P(Z_{t}=1)

By the ergodic theorem, as
T\to\infty
, the time average of the ergodic process
\{Z_{t}\}
converges to its expected value
E[Z_{t}]=P(Z_{t}=1)
. Therefore, for a sufficiently large corpus
T
, we can estimate this probability via the empirical
human token exclusion rate
:

\mathcal{R}_{\text{excl}}(\mathbf{x}^{H};\theta)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\left[x^{H}_{t}\notin\mathcal{T}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{H}_{<t})\right]

(1)

This rate serves as a tractable proxy for the volume of the blind spot, measuring the proportion of communicatively valid tokens that are rendered unreachable under a specific neural text generator
\theta
.

2.4
Measurable Proxies for the Blind Spot

Since neither
P_{\mathcal{H}}
nor the blind spot can be directly observed, we identify two measurable text properties that should reflect the distributional mismatch between likelihood-based and intent-driven selection.

Predictability.

If machine text is constrained to high-probability tokens while human text includes selections from the blind spot, machine text should exhibit systematically higher average log-likelihood under a reference language model. This follows directly from the truncation mechanism: tokens in
\mathcal{T}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{<t})
are, by construction, those assigned highest probability by
\mathcal{M}
.

Lexical Diversity.

Truncation also constrains token
variety
. By excluding rare tokens and concentrating probability mass on common alternatives, likelihood-based decoding should produce text with elevated
n
-gram repetition. Human writers, selecting tokens for communicative appropriateness rather than statistical rank, access a broader effective vocabulary—including domain-specific terms, precise modifiers, and unusual collocations that populate the blind spot.

These metrics are complementary: predictability captures the
local
effect of truncation (each token drawn from a high-probability region), while diversity captures the
cumulative
effect (restricted token variety across the sequence). Together, they operationalize the theoretical distinction developed in Sections
2
–
2.4
in terms of measurable text properties, enabling the empirical tests reported in Section
5
.

3
Related Work

The detection of machine-generated text has received substantial attention as LLMs have become more capable
(
23
)
. Approaches span several categories.
Zero-shot statistical methods
exploit properties of model probability distributions: DetectGPT
(
33
)
identifies AI-generated text by observing that such text occupies negative curvature regions of the model’s log probability function, while perplexity-based methods like GPTZero
(
46
)
leverage the observation that machine text exhibits lower perplexity and reduced burstiness compared to human writing.
Trained classifiers
learn to distinguish human from machine text using labeled corpora
(
41
;
47
;
45
)
, though these approaches face challenges in generalization across models and domains.
Watermarking schemes
embed statistical signatures during generation:
26
propose partitioning the vocabulary into “green” and “red” lists and biasing generation toward green tokens, enabling post-hoc detection. However,
27
demonstrate that paraphrasing attacks can evade multiple detection methods, including watermarking, reducing DetectGPT accuracy from 70.3% to 4.6%. Our work differs from these approaches by focusing on
why
detection succeeds rather than developing new detection methods; we trace detectability to the structural properties of likelihood-based truncation.

The choice of decoding strategy profoundly affects generated text quality and characteristics
(
48
;
11
)
.
19
identify the “curious case of neural text degeneration”: despite high model quality, maximization-based decoding (e.g., beam search) produces bland, repetitive text, while sampling from the full distribution introduces incoherence. They propose nucleus (top-
p
) sampling to truncate the “unreliable tail” of the distribution.
9
earlier introduced top-
k
sampling for story generation, and
43
propose contrastive search, which balances likelihood with a diversity penalty. From an information-theoretic perspective,
31
analyze locally typical sampling, arguing that human language exhibits information content close to the conditional entropy at each step, and propose sampling from this “locally typical set.” This connects to psycholinguistic findings that humans produce text with relatively uniform information density
(
29
;
13
)
. We build on this work by connecting decoding choices to detectability: the very mechanisms that improve text quality (truncating low-probability tokens) create systematic distributional differences from human text.

Our framework draws on established findings in psycholinguistics and information theory. Surprisal theory
(
17
;
29
)
posits that processing difficulty is proportional to a word’s surprisal (negative log probability in context), a prediction confirmed across multiple languages
(
49
)
.
28
provides a foundational model of human speech production, describing how communicative intentions are transformed into linguistic output through conceptualization, formulation, and articulation stages. The uniform information density hypothesis
(
22
)
suggests that speakers distribute information relatively evenly across utterances, avoiding both highly predictable and highly surprising elements.
40
early demonstrated that human text exhibits specific entropy characteristics. These findings suggest that human language production is guided by communicative efficiency rather than pure likelihood maximization, a distinction central to our truncation blind spot hypothesis.

4.4
Metrics

Predictability.

Given a text
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{T})
, a prompt
\mathbf{c}
, and a reference model
\mathcal{M}_{\text{ref}}
, we define predictability as:

\text{Pred}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{c})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log P_{\mathcal{M}_{\text{ref}}}(x_{t}\mid[\mathbf{c}:\mathbf{x}_{<t}])

(2)

where
[\mathbf{c}:\mathbf{x}_{<t}]
denotes the concatenation of the prompt and the preceding tokens. Higher predictability values (less negative) indicate that the reference model assigns higher probability to the observed tokens

Following our definition in Equation
2
, we use OPT-2.7B
(
50
)
as the reference model
\mathcal{M}_{\text{ref}}
, consistent with prior work on coherence evaluation
(
44
)
. If machine text is constrained to high-probability tokens, it should exhibit elevated predictability (less negative values) compared to human text.

Lexical Diversity.

We measure
n
-gram diversity as the product of unique-to-total ratios:

\text{Div}(\mathbf{x})=100*\prod_{n=2}^{4}\frac{|\text{unique }n\text{-grams}(\mathbf{x})|}{|\text{total }n\text{-grams}(\mathbf{x})|}

(3)

Low diversity indicates repetitive text, a known consequence of deterministic or heavily truncated decoding. High diversity may indicate either genuine lexical richness or incoherent randomness.

4.5
Classification Setup

To quantify how well these proxy metrics distinguish human from machine text, we train binary classifiers using only predictability and diversity as features. We employ Random Forest
(
4
)
(RF, 500 trees), Logistic Regression (LR), and Naive Bayes (NB) with 80-20 stratified splits following
5
. The use of simple classifiers is intentional: high classification performance with minimal features would indicate that machine text exhibits systematically different statistical properties, consistent with the truncation blind spot hypothesis.

5
Results

5.1
The Magnitude of the Blind Spot (RQ1)

We first examine whether the blind spot—that is, the set of tokens that humans select but that likelihood-based truncation excludes—is empirically substantial. Figure
2
illustrates this phenomenon, showing token ranks for human-written text with excluded tokens highlighted. Our analysis of human token exclusion rates across truncation configurations (detailed in Appendix
A.2
) reveals substantial exclusion under commonly used settings. At
k=10
, a setting within the range of typical deployments, approximately 18% of human-selected tokens would be unreachable. Even at
k=50
, roughly 8% remain excluded. For nucleus sampling, the commonly used
p=0.9
setting excludes approximately 8% of human tokens. The distribution of ranks for human-selected tokens shows that while 72–78% of human tokens fall within the top 5 ranks, a substantial tail extends beyond typical truncation thresholds: approximately 5–7% of human tokens have ranks exceeding 100. The divergence between median rank (1.2–1.8) and mean rank (50–155) indicates a heavy-tailed distribution. Humans occasionally select tokens that models assign very low probability, and these selections constitute the blind spot. Notably, the tokens excluded by truncation are not uniformly distributed across word types. Consistent with psycholinguistic findings that content words carry higher information load than function words
(
35
;
17
)
, we observe that excluded tokens disproportionately consist of content-bearing elements: domain-specific terminology, proper nouns, and semantic alternatives to more generic expressions. Function words (determiners, prepositions, conjunctions), by contrast, consistently occupy high-probability ranks and are rarely excluded. This asymmetry aligns with information-theoretic accounts of language: function words provide structural scaffolding with low surprisal, while content words carry the novel semantic information that distinguishes specific communicative acts
(
40
;
29
)
. WikiText shows the heaviest tail (mean rank 155.0), consistent with encyclopedic writing requiring specialized vocabulary that may be underrepresented in general training corpora. A detailed analysis of the
linguistic composition
of the blind spot is provided in Appendix
A.13
.

5.3
Independence from Model Scale and Architecture (RQ3)

A key prediction of the blind spot hypothesis is that detectability should be largely independent of model scale and architecture, since the truncation mechanism itself, not the specific model, creates the distributional mismatch. Our analysis confirms this prediction (detailed results in Appendix
A.3
). Model capability does not predict detectability: LLaMA 3, despite strong benchmark performance, produces the most detectable text (AUC-ROC = 0.993), while Falcon 2 achieves the lowest detectability (AUC-ROC = 0.945). GPT2-XL, a smaller and older model, is more detectable than several recent models with substantially more parameters. No clear correlation emerges between parameter count and detection rates. To isolate the effect of model scale independently of architectural and training differences, we conducted a complementary analysis in which detectability (AUC-ROC) is regressed on parameter count while conditioning on model family, dataset, and decoding strategy. Under this specification, the coefficient of
\log(\text{params})
is not statistically significant, indicating that
within model families
, increasing scale does not reliably reduce detectability. In contrast, architectural differences remain significant. Controlling for scale, dataset, and decoding strategy, non-Transformer architectures exhibit substantially higher detectability than Transformers. This suggests that high detectability is
not unique to Transformer architectures
; rather, it persists in non-Transformer models when generation relies on likelihood-based truncation (see Appendix
A.8
for details).

The Detectability-Quality Dissociation (RQ4)

A critical question is whether low detectability entails high quality. Our analysis indicates these objectives are dissociable (cf. Table

and Figure

in Appendix

controlled unpredictability
of human writing: token choices that are statistically surprising yet contextually appropriate. Lax truncation achieves surprise without appropriateness. This dissociation means detectability should not proxy for quality. Evaluation must distinguish between: (1) coherent but predictable text (easily detected), (2) coherent and varied text (approximates human patterns), and (3) incoherent random text (evades detection but lacks quality). The fundamental challenge is reducing predictability while maintaining contextual appropriateness—precisely what intent-driven selection provides and likelihood-based truncation lacks. Figure

7
Conclusion

We investigated why machine-generated text remains detectable, proposing that likelihood-based truncation creates a systematic blind spot: tokens that are contextually appropriate but statistically rare become inaccessible during generation. Our analysis of over 1.8 million texts provides evidence consistent with this explanation. Under standard truncation settings, 8–18% of human-selected tokens fall outside typical truncation boundaries (
RQ1
). This manifests as elevated predictability in machine text, enabling simple classifiers to achieve over 96% detection accuracy using only predictability and diversity features (
RQ2
). Detectability varies primarily with truncation parameters rather than model scale or architecture, spanning from near-random to near-perfect classification across configurations (
RQ3
). Configurations achieving low detectability often do so through incoherence rather than naturalness, indicating that detection evasion and text quality are distinct objectives (
RQ4
). These findings suggest that detectability is partly a structural consequence of how current decoding strategies select tokens. The observation that different models produce similarly detectable text despite maintaining distinct high-probability regions points to a shared exclusion pattern as the source of the detection signal.

Limitations.

(1) Predictability and diversity both proxy for distributional mismatch but do not capture contextual appropriateness; text can achieve human-like statistics while remaining incoherent. (2) Intent-driven selection is a theoretical construct; we infer human preferences from written text, which incompletely reflects the generative process. (3) Our scope is constrained to English text from three domains (fiction, encyclopedia, news) using segments of up to 256 tokens; patterns may differ across languages, genres, or longer contexts. The binary human-machine distinction also simplifies collaborative or post-edited scenarios. (4) Exclusion rate analysis relies on five open-source models; results may vary with other architectures.

Future Work.

Our findings motivate three directions: (1)
Decoding mechanisms approximating intent-driven selection
—incorporating pragmatic appropriateness, discourse coherence, or communicative value into token selection. (2)
Alternative training procedures
explicitly rewarding human-like lexical diversity or information density, since current alignment methods (e.g., RLHF) do not address this gap. (3)
Adaptive truncation
that dynamically adjusts constraints by context—relaxing bounds for content-bearing positions while maintaining stricter thresholds for function words.

Impact Statement

We use publicly available datasets without personally identifiable information. We acknowledge that understanding detectability could potentially inform attempts to evade detection; however, we believe the scientific value of understanding why machine text is detectable outweighs this risk, particularly as our findings point toward improving generation quality rather than merely evading detection. We declare no conflicts of interest.

A.1.1
Summary of Evidence

Our results provide evidence consistent with the hypothesis that the detectability of machine-generated text arises from a distributional mismatch between likelihood-based and intent-driven token selection. Under typical truncation settings, 8–18% of human-selected tokens would be excluded, demonstrating that the blind spot is empirically substantial (
RQ1
). Machine text exhibits systematically higher predictability than human text, and this predictability serves as the primary signal enabling detection with simple classifiers (
RQ2
). Stricter truncation increases both the exclusion rate and detectability; relaxing truncation reduces both. Model scale and architecture do not predict detectability; truncation parameters do (
RQ3
). These patterns are consistent with the mechanistic explanation that likelihood-based truncation creates a systematic blind spot. Finally, configurations achieving low detectability often do so through incoherence rather than naturalness (
RQ4
).

A.1.2
Why Detection Generalizes Across Models

The modest cross-model overlap in truncation sets (5–10%, see Appendix
A.11
) indicates that different models maintain distinct high-probability regions, yet produce similarly detectable text. This pattern supports the interpretation that models share a common exclusion pattern rather than a common inclusion pattern. All models assign low probability to the same type of tokens: those serving specific communicative purposes but appearing rarely in training data. The blind spot is defined by the structure of likelihood-based truncation itself, not by any particular model’s learned distribution.

A.1.3
Limitations of Current Approaches

The finding that low detectability can be achieved through incoherence as easily as through genuine naturalness exposes a fundamental limitation. Current decoding strategies face a tradeoff: strict truncation produces coherent but detectable text (restricted to high-probability tokens), while permissive truncation produces less detectable but often incoherent text (including inappropriate low-probability tokens alongside appropriate ones). The core problem is that likelihood-based truncation cannot distinguish between two types of low-probability tokens: those that are contextually appropriate (and would be selected by a human pursuing specific communicative goals) and those that are contextually inappropriate. Permissive truncation admits both; strict truncation excludes both. Addressing this limitation would require mechanisms that can identify contextually appropriate tokens regardless of their probability ranking.

A.13

Having established that the blind spot is quantitatively substantial, we now examine its
linguistic composition

Numerals and Modifiers Show Highest Exclusion.

The highest exclusion rates occur for numerals (

Proper nouns, while still showing high exclusion (

Domain-Specific Patterns.

Figure

WikiText shows high noun exclusion, consistent with encyclopedic writing’s reliance on technical terminology. BookCorpus shows the highest adjective and adverb exclusion, reflecting the descriptive richness of fiction. These domain-specific patterns underscore that the blind spot reflects the exclusion of communicatively important content.

Frequency Does Not Explain Exclusion.

A potential alternative explanation is that exclusion rates simply reflect word frequency: rare words of any category would be excluded. Figure
9
(C) tests this hypothesis by plotting exclusion rate against corpus frequency for each POS category. The correlation is negligible, indicating that semantic category, not raw frequency, determines exclusion likelihood. Determiners are frequent
and
rarely excluded (
9.0\%
); numerals are infrequent
and
often excluded (
55.8\%
). But nouns, despite constituting the largest category by token count, still show
37.9\%
exclusion—far above function words of comparable frequency. The asymmetry reflects the linguistic distinction between closed-class function words (few items, predictable usage) and open-class content words (many items, context-dependent selection).

Implications.

This analysis reveals that the blind spot is
semantically structured
: likelihood-based truncation systematically excludes the content-bearing elements that carry distinctive meaning. The tokens lost to truncation are not random low-frequency items but precisely the numerals, modifiers, and domain-specific terms that would distinguish one informative text from another. This finding might provide an explanation for why machine-generated text, despite being grammatically well-formed, often reads as generic: the truncation mechanism preserves grammatical scaffolding (function words) while eroding semantic specificity (content words).
