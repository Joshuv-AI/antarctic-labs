Cerebral debranching and zone 1 thoracic branch endoprosthesis in setting of right sided aortic arch aneurysm
Authors: Amanda Rushing, Cassandra Soto, Vanessa Sandra, Christopher Smolock, James McKinsey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present the case of a 66-year-old man with a right-sided aortic arch with aneurysmal dilation to 6.7 cm. The patient was treated with a complex cerebral debranching and thoracic branch endoprosthesis (TBE) with stenting into the right common carotid artery. After intervention, the proximal TBE stent deployment just distal to the origin of the left common carotid artery with patent right common carotid artery stent and cerebral bypasses. Our case presents a novel intervention using TBE and complex cerebral debranching for aortic arch aneurysmal dilation in the setting of a right-sided aortic arch.Keywords: Right sided aortic arch, Thoracic branch endoprosthesis, Cerebral debranching, Zone 1

Right-sided aortic arches are rare with less than 0.1% incidence.1 Right-sided aortic arches can be associated with congenital syndromes such as DiGeorge syndrome but are largely clinically silent.2 There are three main variations of right-sided aortic anatomy well described by the Edwards' aortic classification system.3 Type 1 is mirror-image branching where the first branch off the aortic arch is the left innominate artery followed by the right common and right subclavian arteries. Type 2 is an aberrant left subclavian artery in which arch anatomy from proximal to distal is left common carotid, right common carotid, right subclavian, and left subclavian arteries. Type 3 is an isolated left subclavian artery in which the left subclavian artery arises from the ductus arteriosus with connection to the pulmonary artery. The most common clinical presentations for right sided aortic arch pathology are dysphagia and a Kommerell diverticulum.

All endovascular aortic devices are designed for left-sided arches, and the complex cerebral anatomy of right-sided arches requires innovative techniques. There is little data on endovascular interventions for right-sided aortic arch aneurysms. Currently, there are no case reports of the use of a thoracic branch device [thoracic branch endoprosthesis (TBE); W. L. Gore & Associates] in the setting of a right-sided aortic arch aneurysm. We present the case of a 66-year-old man with a right-sided aortic arch with aneurysmal dilation to 67 mm at the origin of the aberrant left subclavian artery. The patient was treated with complex cerebral debranching and TBE with stenting into the right common carotid artery.

Methods

Written consent was obtained from the patient before preparation of the manuscript. Our patient was a 66-year-old-man with medical history of smoking, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and heart failure with an ejection fraction of 25% who presented with an incidental finding of right-sided aortic arch aneurysm. The patient's current medication included Tamsulosin, Metoprolol, Lisinopril, Lasix, Lipitor, and Aspirin. The patient was allergic to penicillin, and surgical history included placement of an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator 2 years earlier. The patient was followed closely by his cardiologist who found the asymptomatic aneurysmal arch on a routine transthoracic echocardiogram, and he was referred to outpatient vascular surgery. On physical examination, the patient ambulated independently, easily flexed and extended his neck, and had palpable bilateral radial and femoral pulses. Computed tomography (CT) angiography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis was ordered. TeraRecon (Aquarius) (Fig 1) and three-dimensional spin (Supplementary Video, online only) were created to analyze the aortic arch. CT angiography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis revealed a type 2 right-sided aortic arch aneurysm with maximum dilation of 67 mm at the origin of the aberrant left subclavian artery with tapering to 44 mm at the right subclavian artery (Supplementary Figs 1 and 2, online only). Normal aortic diameter was present at the right common carotid artery at 31 mm and the left common carotid artery at 34 mm. The right-sided arch aneurysm extended proximally with the origin of the aneurysm at the right subclavian artery and extending the length of the arch with termination to just distal to the left subclavian artery. The following aortic arch diameters are listed: left common carotid artery (34 mm), left common carotid artery (31 mm), right subclavian artery (44 mm), left subclavian artery (67 mm), and descending thoracic aorta (28 mm).

Results

The patient was deemed high risk for open repair and was offered a two-staged hybrid procedure with complex cerebral debranching and an endovascular aortic branch device (Fig 2, A). Our first stage intervention was a left carotid subclavian artery bypass with proximal left subclavian artery embolization using an Amplatz II plug just proximal to the left vertebral artery origin (Fig 2, B). The patient recovered and returned for stage 2 which was a right carotid subclavian bypass with proximal right subclavian artery embolization followed by a zone 1 TBE with the portal stent into the right common carotid artery (Fig 3, A).

Intravascular ultrasound guidance was used at the beginning of the stage 2 intervention to measure the proximal and distal landing zones. After imaging, the right carotid subclavian bypass was performed with a 7 mm ringed polytetrafluorethylene graft. We were able to directly access the right carotid to subclavian bypass via a conduit and place a through and through wire from the right common carotid artery to the right common femoral artery. Radial or brachial access could have been used as well, but given the right common carotid artery was exposed for the bypass, we felt this to be the best option without need for further arterial access. We then deployed the 45 × 150 mm TBE graft with deployment just distal to the takeoff of the left common carotid artery. It is important to note that due to the aberrant anatomy, advance planning on three-dimensional reconstruction was used to ensure proper alignment of the device to the outer wall without significant arch manipulation. The through-and-through wire from the right common carotid to the common femoral artery allowed the TBE device to align against the outer wall of the aorta and splay in a fashion similar to that of normal arch anatomy. We then deployed the longest available portal, 8 × 60 mm side branch, via the through-and-through wire into the right common carotid artery with planned extension with a second 8 × 60 mm portal to obtain adequate extension. Next the right subclavian artery was embolized via direct access of the right subclavian artery using interlock coils with embolization just proximal to the origin of the right vertebral artery. Due to ectasia of the descending thoracic aorta, the decision was made to extend the distal seal zone with a 37 × 37 × 150 mm thoracic endovascular aortic repair device (conformable thoracic aortic graft; W. L. Gore & Associates). The conduit off the right carotid subclavian bypass was ligated.

After intervention, angiography illustrated proximal TBE stent deployment just distal to the origin of the left common carotid artery with patent right common carotid artery stent and cerebral bypasses. Six week and 2.5 years postoperative CT showed no sac expansion or stent migration (Fig 3, B).

Discussion

Right-sided arch pathology requires novel intervention as standard open and endovascular techniques are tailored to left-sided arches. Moreover, right-sided arches have variant cerebral anatomy that requires careful consideration as to maintain cerebral and upper-extremity perfusion. Current multibranch thoracic endografts include Endospan's NEXUS Thoracic Stent, Terumo's RelayBranch Thoracic Stent, Cook's Zenith Branched Thoracic Stent, and Gore's Thoracic Branch Device (only commercially available devices). To ensure minimal arch manipulation of this right-sided arch aneurysm, we opted for Gore TBE device with one portal cannulation. The TBE device was originally approved for zone 2 in September 2025 with the portal into the left subclavian artery.4 The device was granted added Food and Drug Administration approval for zones 0 and 1 in June 2025.5 Currently, there is only one case report that uses TBE in the setting of a right-sided arch dissection, but there are no case reports on the use of TBE in aneurysm alone.6 Our case report illustrates a unique approach to a right-sided arch aneurysm which details careful preoperative planning, cerebral debranching, and proper device selection.

Conclusions

Our case presents a novel intervention using TBE and complex cerebral debranching for aortic arch aneurysmal dilation in the setting of a right-sided aortic arch.
