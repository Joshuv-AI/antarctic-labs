 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 The purpose of this CRADA is to produce at minimum 100 g of specified (99.8 wt%) acetonitrile
 from the nitrilation reaction using commercially available biologically derived acetic acid. This
 CRADA will also investigate the separation of pullulan from fermentation broth.

 Task 0 Results:

 Six staff members from Crysalis were onboarded as contingent workers at NLR. Each of them

 Crysalis staff were trained to operate the “Emperor” reactor in Field Test Laboratory Building
 (FTLB) 101 laboratory at NLR.

 Task 1: Nitrilation reactions (Deliverables 2 – 6)

 Initially, the nitrilation reactor and associated FTIR were both cleaned, set-up, and calibrated
 (Deliverable 2). Minimum pre-calcination temperature was identified using thermogravimetric
 analysis (TGA) from 23 -500 °C performed in duplicate using conditions as described in Karp et
   al., 2017 (doi: ) (Deliverable 3).

 Nitrilation reaction optimization will take place in the Emperor reactor using a design of
 experiments (DoE) approach, using a four factor, four level design as described in Cao et al.,
 2018 (doi: 10.1021/acsnano.8b04726), with titanium dioxide (TiO2) catalyst performed every
 other day for a total of 16 runs performing catalyst cleanout and reloading between each run
 (Deliverable 4). Specifically, the four factors will be temperature (290, 300, 310, 320 °C), weight
 hourly space velocity (WHSV) (0.15, 0.30, 0.45, 0.60), NH3:AcOH (acetic acid) molar ratio
   (1:1, 2:1, 3:1, 4:1), and water content in the AcOH feed (0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0%). The catalyst in
 these runs will be pre-calcined at the minimum regeneration temperature identified in
 Deliverable 11. The AcOH flow rate will be maintained at 0.1-mL/min (allowing reaction of a
 50-mL syringe in one 8-hour run), with WHSV adjusted by loading different amounts of catalyst.
 The main characterization of the product in these experiments will be through the Emperor's
 integrated online Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) detector, to determine
 acetonitrile yield, though some samples may also be analyzed at the Contractor’s facilities by gas
 chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) to determine product purity. Data from
 Deliverable 12 will inform a machine learning model as described in Cao et al., 2018 to
 determine optimal conditions.

 Using the optimal conditions identified in Deliverable 12, a longer series of reaction runs will be
 performed using catalyst regeneration between each reaction (Deliverable 5). Catalyst
 regeneration performance will be tracked using the Emperor’s integrated online FTIR detector to
 determine acetonitrile yield, where a 10% loss in nitrilation activity will indicate deactivated
 catalyst. Ideally, catalyst regeneration will be carried out using an online procedure, but, if this is
 not possible due to safety concerns, etc., the catalyst can also be unloaded, regenerated in an
 offline furnace, and re-loaded into the reactor between nitrilation reactions. Learnings from
 Deliverables 11-13 will be used to generate a minimum of 100 g of acetonitrile (CAN), which
 will have a downstream use in Task 4 (Deliverable 6).

 Task 1 Results:

 Nitrilation reaction optimization took place in the Emperor reactor using a design of experiments
 (DoE) approach, using a four factor, four level design as described in Cao et al., 2018, with
 titanium dioxide (TiO2) catalyst performed every other day for a total of 16 runs performing
 catalyst cleanout and reloading between each run (Deliverable 4).

 No decrease in catalyst activity was observed, so no catalyst regenerating technique was
 employed. An initial experiment investigated an opportunity to reduce the "burn-in" period of the
 catalyst (i.e., the time it takes to reach steady state) by using an offline "regeneration" of just
 static air in a muffle furnace. The results of this experiment showed no difference from untreated
 material. 37 h time-on-stream was reached with no observable decrease in activity.
 Learnings from Deliverables 11-13 were used to generate 100 g of ACN, which was
 subsequently used in Task 4 (Deliverable 6).

 Crysalis staff, under the supervision of NLR staff, utilized the reactor to test and optimize the
 conversion of acetic acid to acetonitrile based on the previous work by Karp et al., on production
 of renewable acrylonitrile. Utilizing the design of experiments procedure, it was determined that
 0.9 hr-1 WHSV and a 5:1 NH3:acetic acid feed ratio is optimal. Catalyst regeneration was
 necessary for the production run. Production of acetonitrile was scaled to 100g, and Nitto Denko
 employees were trained in the nitrilation and acetonitrile production processes.

 Task 2: Acetonitrile purification (Deliverables 7 – 9)

 A salting-out approach will be assessed for acetonitrile purification using either potassium
 carbonate (K2CO3) or potassium acetate (KC2H3O2) at room temperature, with the aim of
 achieving 99 wt% acetonitrile, as described by Renard et al., 1965 (doi: 10.1021/je60032a009)
 (Deliverable 7). Purity of acetonitrile will be assessed by the Contractor via gas chromatography-
 flame ionization detection (GC-FID), coulometric Karl Fischer (KF) titration (for residual
 water), thermogravimetric analysis TGA (for residue on evaporation), and ultraviolet-visible
 (UV-Vis) (for absorbance at 200- 400 nm), as well as density and refractive index. For these
 experiments, mock mixtures of acetonitrile, H2O, NH3, and K2CO3/ KC2H3O2 will be used to
 validate literature parameters and establish a performance range. Experiments will be performed
 in triplicate by stirring samples in vials and then allowing phase separation to equilibrate
 overnight, at a range of initial compositions (approximately ~25 compositions). All samples will
 be analyzed by GC-FID, and a subset of the most promising organic phase compositions (likely
 < 10 samples) will undergo full KF, TGA, and UV-Vis analysis. If salting out proves

 5

 unsatisfactory to reach 99 wt% acetonitrile, distillation in combination with an entrainer
 (ethylene glycol or xylene) will be explored, using a spinning band distillation system.

 The spinning band distillation system (possibly incorporating (ethylene glycol or xylene as an
 entrainer) will be used on the crude ACN stream obtained in Deliverable 15 to perform
 atmospheric pressure distillation for removing the remaining ~1% H2O as an azeotrope, leaving
 >99.8% acetonitrile in the residual (Deliverable 8). Purity of acetonitrile will be assessed by the
 Contractor via GC-FID, coulometric KF titration (for residual water), TGA (for residue on
 evaporation), DSC, and UV-Vis (for absorbance at 200 - 400 nm), as well as density and
 refractive index. Upon establishing a technique to reach 99.8% purity with mock mixtures, this
 technique will be applied to crude ACN feeds recovered from the nitrilation reactor, which also
 requires separating NH3 in an initial distillation (Deliverable 9). We will use the spinning band
 system for this initial purification, at which point the mixture will very closely resemble the
 mock mixtures save for trace impurities and potentially the capture solvent.

 Task 2 Results:

 Experiments were conducted with ethylene glycol in the spinning band, but this approach was
 ultimately less efficient because it did not remove acetone, took longer than salting out, and
 contaminated the sample from other work on the spinning band. The spinning band system was
 used for this initial purification, at which point the mixture closely resembled the mock mixtures
 saved for trace impurities and the capture solvent. Greater than 99.9% purity was attained by GC
 on both mock mixtures and material produced by Emperor. The key impurities were acetone,
 acetamide, 3-methyl-2-butenenitrile, toluene, and mesitylene. The UV absorbance specification
 was met on two separate batches of material produced by Emperor. After the salting out, the
 ACN was passed through columns of activated carbon, 3A molecular sieves, and activated
 alumina to achieve the purity and UV spec. Figure 4 below details the experimental protocol for
 high purity purification.

 A "standard" GC-MS-FID method was used in the early stages of purification, which could
 quantify down to ~30 ppm of most of the impurities. In the later stages, a "low split" version of
 the standard method was used to qualitatively analyze down to ~3 ppm of the impurities. Below
 that level, a UV-Vis was used.

 For UV-Vis analysis, stoppered cuvettes and Eppendorf syringes were required because UV-
 absorbing impurities were introduced from other pipet types (or the rubber bulb used to pull up
 and expel the sample). Similar impurities were introduced in the case of unstoppered cuvettes
 from incidental contact with nitrile gloves while loading the sample into the UV-Vis
 spectrometer. It was suspected, but not conclusively proven, that the introduced impurities are
 fatty amides used as anti-stick or lubricating agents on the gloves, pipets, and pipet bulbs.

 Task 3: Pullulan separations (Deliverables 10-13)

 Pullulan is widely used in the pharmaceutical industry to produce capsuls for drug delivery. It
 can be produced as a metabolite from Aureobasidium pullulans. The Participant intends to
 produce >40 L of pullulan broth that will be provided to the Contractor for separation by rotating
 ceramic disc (RCD) filtration. For each 10 L batch of broth, the Contractor will use specified
 membranes ranging from 0.2-5 μm pore size. During this process the Participant will observe the
 procedure and the Contractor will measure mass balance, nitrogen content, and conductivity. The
 Contractor will also provide final HPLC samples and the filtered broth back to the Participant.
 Each filtration process is allocated for two weeks with two week breaks between for the
 Participant to digest the data and prepare for the following run. The filtration steps are 5 μm
 followed by 1 μm (Deliverable 10), 1 μm followed by 0.2 μm (Deliverable 11), and 0.2 μm
 (Deliverable 12). In. addition to the pullulan filtration, the rotating ceramic disc (RCD) will be
 employed for the filtration of acetic acid fermentation broth utilizing the 0.2 μm filter
 (Deliverable 13).

 Task 3 Results:

 20 L of BDO broth was successfully filtered. 40 L of pullulan broth was suspended after ~0.5 L
 of filtrate was collected, and nothing further was pursued.

 Task 4: Cocura Oil

 Task 4.1: Cocura Oil Feedstocks

 Participant 1 and Contractor will identify a feedstock that may result in more efficient cocura oil
 production using A. pullulans. Contractor will supply lignocellulosic hydrolysate and Participant
 1 will culture the A. pullulans in a lignocellulosic hydrolysate media. The use of lignocellulosic
 hydrolysate media may produce cocura oil more efficiently than other feedstocks.

 Task 4.1 Results:

 Experiments found the titer was equivalent and yield was higher than the glucose control, but
 productivity was lower. So based on usage, the lignocellulosic hydrolysate produced cocura oil
 more efficiently than other feedstocks. The results of other feedstocks tested were similar to the
 glucose control, so the efficiency of the lignocellulosic hydrolysate relative to the other
 feedstocks was similarly higher based on sugar usage.

 Task 4:2: Dehydroxygenation (HDO) of Cocura Oil

 Participant 1 and Contractor will identify a suitable hydrotreating catalyst to produce sustainable
 aviation fuel (SAF)-range hydrocarbons from cocura oil. Participant 1 will supply cocura oil to
 Contractor and Contractor will hydrotreat the cocura oil into SAF using molybdenum carbide
 (Mo2C) and cobaltmolybdenum (CoMo) catalysts. The hydrotreating with the Mo2C and CoMo
 catalysts may facilitate alkanes with longer chain lengths, and may thus create a more
 advantageous SAF blendstock than other catalysts.

 Task 4.2 Results:

 Mo2C, NiMoS, Pd/C, Pt/SAPO-11, and Ni/SAPO-11 were tested in trickle-bed flow reactors for
 the hydrotreating of cocura oil. NiMoS and Ni/SAPO-11 had significantly lower activity toward
 Cocura Oil deoxygenation, and the products contained substantial undesirable alkenes and
 oxygenates (~10% in NiMoS, and ~41% in Ni/SAPO-11).The c atalysts generated C9:C10 ratios
 of 1.93 for Mo2C, 29.4 for Pd/C, and 36.5 for Pt/SAPO-11. Mo2C was selected as the optimal
 Cocura Oil hydrotreating catalyst due to the high conversion, low C9:C10 ratio, and low cost due
 to earth-abundant composition and lack of Platinum Group Metals (PGM). Collected SimDist
 curves for pure Jet A fuel, as well as for 5 wt% and 10 wt% hydrotreated-Cocura / Jet A blends.
 The average deviation in distillation temperature was 1.2 °C for the 5% blend and 5.8 °C for the
 10% blend.

 Participant 1 and Contractor determined that Mo2C was the most promising catalyst tested,
 giving the highest selectivity to HDO (i.e., C10 products) over de-Cox (i.e., C9 products). It is
 believed to be suitable for producing SAF-range hydrocarbons from cocura oil, with further
 development.
