Head-to-head comparison of 18F-PSMA and 18F-FDG PET/CT in locoregionally advanced head and neck squamous cell carcinoma: a pilot study
Authors: Hádila da Silva Veras Sousa, Victor Cabral Costa Ribeiro Heringer, Matheus Yung Perin, Nájua Abou Arabi Silveira, Simone Kuba, Ligia Traldi Macedo, Gustavo Jacob Lourenço, Carlos Takahiro Chone, Carmino Antônio de Souza, Celso Dario Ramos, Carmen Silvia Passos Lima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•18F-FDG, a marker of glycolytic activity, has been used for HNSCC staging.•The role of 18F-PSMA-1007, a marker of angiogenesis, in HNSCC is unknown.•This study compared 18F-FDG and 18F-PSMA-1007 PET/CT in 14 patients with HNSCC.•Tumors were better detected by 18F-FDG PET/CT than by 18F-PSMA-1007 PET/CT.•Both tracer uptakes show glycolysis and neoangiogenesis coexist in HNSCC.Keywords: Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, 18F-FDG PET/CT, 18F-PSMA PET/CT, Diagnosis, Physiopathology

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is recognized as the eighth most prevalent cancer worldwide, accounting for approximately 771,307 new cases and 384,631 deaths annually.1 The incidence of HNSCC is higher among males, tobacco users, and individuals with a history of alcohol abuse,2,3 as well as among individuals infected with human papillomavirus (HPV).3, 4, 5

In clinical practice, therapeutic management of HNSCC is determined by the primary tumor site and staging.6, 7, 8 Staging and follow-up of HNSCC are traditionally performed through computed tomography (CT).9 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG), a marker of glycolytic activity, has been used in positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT), which enables comprehensive evaluation of locoregional disease extent and distant metastases.8,10 Surgical resection (SR) and radiotherapy (RT) represent the main approaches for patients with localized disease,11 while patients with metastatic disease require platinum-based chemotherapy,12, 13, 14 and multimodal treatment is indicated for patients with locally advanced tumors.7,15

Despite the above-mentioned procedures, the overall survival (OS) rate for patients with HNSCC remains around 40%–50% at five-years,16,15 highlighting the need for improved strategies for tumor characterization, staging, treatment, and treatment monitoring.

Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA), a marker of neoangiogenesis in tissues, was initially recognized for its overexpression in prostate cancer cells.17,18 Gallium-68 PSMA PET/CT scans have been used for detecting metastases of prostate cancer,19 and lutetium-177 PSMA has been indicated for treatment of castration-resistant metastatic prostate cancer.20 Previous studies have demonstrated PSMA expression in non-prostatic malignancies. Incidental detection of lung, lymphoma, thyroid, and base-of-tongue primary malignancies was identified by 68Ga-PSMA PET/CT in patients with prostate cancer.21 Renal cell carcinoma, breast cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, and glioblastoma were also identified by PSMA PET/CT.22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28 PSMA uptake was observed in adenoid cystic carcinoma, salivary duct carcinoma,29 and concomitant prostate cancer and SCC of the oropharynx30 and larynx31 cases, but the clinical role of PSMA PET/CT in HNSCC remains undefined. In this context, the current study aimed to investigate the role of PSMA PET/CT in the characterization of HNSCC, considering 18F-FDG PET/CT as the reference method among imaging modalities.

We hypothesized that, in HNSCC, 18F-PSMA-1007 PET/CT may provide biologically distinct information from 18F-FDG PET/CT by preferentially reflecting tumor-associated neoangiogenesis, whereas FDG predominantly reflects glycolytic metabolism within the tumor microenvironment. Therefore, both tracers may have complementary clinical value for lesion characterization and for depicting intratumoral and interlesional heterogeneity.

Methods

Patients

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted at the Clinical Oncology Service of the General Hospital of the University of Campinas from September 4, 2023, to December 12, 2024.

Inclusion criteria were age >18-years, histopathological diagnosis of HNSCC involving the oral cavity, pharynx, or larynx, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status ≤2 and locoregionally advanced disease at the time of PET/CT, defined as AJCC 8th edition stage III–IVB (M0) at presentation and/or locoregionally advanced locoregional recurrence/progression requiring multimodality management. One patient with a prior stage II primary was included because imaging was performed in the setting of locoregionally advanced recurrence. All participants provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included nasopharyngeal tumors, pregnancy, prior history of another malignant neoplasm, severe comorbidities, insufficient medical records, and refusal to participate in the study.

For the purposes of this study, the term locoregionally advanced HNSCC refers to non-metastatic disease (M0) evaluated either at initial diagnosis or at locoregional recurrence. When present, distant lesions were recorded as metastatic sites for detection-rate analysis; however, metastatic disease was not a separate inclusion stratum.

The confirmation of HNSCC at diagnosis, relapse and metastasis, and the identification of tumor grading were based on histological exam, in accordance with World Health Organization (WHO) criteria.32 Tumor staging was defined using the the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) criteria.6

Imaging examinations

Each patient underwent two imaging examinations: an 18F-FDG PET/CT and an 18F-PSMA-1007 PET/CT. Both scans were performed within a 24h interval to ensure temporal proximity, allowing for a detailed comparison between glycolytic metabolism and neoangiogenesis status in tumor lesions.

Prior to the injection of 18F-FDG, patients fasted for at least six hours and underwent stimulated hydration. Additionally, serum glucose levels were confirmed to be below 200 mg/dL.33 In contrast, no specific preparation was required prior to the administration of 18F-PSMA-1007. FDG and PSMA PET/CT images were acquired 60 and 120 minutes after the intravenous administration of 0.10 mCi/kg 18F-FDG and 0.11 mCi/kg of 18F-PSMA-1007, covering the region from the vertex to the thighs, using a dedicated PET/CT scanner (Biograph mCT40, Siemens Healthcare, USA). Furthermore, all patients underwent delayed imaging two hours after injection, specifically targeting the head and neck region. CT parameters included 5 mm axial reconstruction with automatic dose modulation, and PET acquisitions were performed in 3D mode with a duration of 90 seconds per bed position. Images were reconstructed and displayed in axial, coronal, and sagittal planes.

The PET/CT images were independently analyzed by two board-certified nuclear medicine physicians. PET/CT findings were interpreted by visual analysis integrated with CT. A lesion was classified as positive when it showed focal uptake clearly higher than the uptake in surrounding normal tissues (adjacent physiological uptake), not explained by physiological distribution, with or without a corresponding suspicious CT abnormality. A lesion was classified as negative when uptake was absent or compatible with physiological activity and there was no suspicious CT correlate. Findings were considered indeterminate when uptake was mild/equivocal and/or when CT findings were nonspecific, such as small lymph nodes without definitive malignant features or inflammatory-appearing changes. Discrepancies between readers were resolved by consensus.

The reference standard for lesion status was established by a combined analysis of pathological and clinical data. For the primary tumor, positivity was defined by histopathological confirmation (biopsy or surgical specimen), in accordance with WHO criterio.32 For metastatic lesions, histopathology was used whenever available. When biopsy was not feasible, lesions were classified as true metastases based on clinical-radiological follow-up, defined as typical malignant behavior on serial imaging (progressive increase in size/number, appearance of new lesions in expected metastatic sites) and/or a concordant response pattern to oncologic treatment. Lesions without histological confirmation and without unequivocal progression/response on follow-up were considered unconfirmed.

Quantitative interpretation (MFS VB20, syngo.via MM Oncology, Siemens Medical Solutions, USA) was conducted on all PET/CT images to determine total body tumor burden. FDG and PSMA uptake in tumor lesions was quantified using the maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax). Coregistration of FDG PET/CT and PSMA PET/CT images ensured accurate overlap of functional and morphological data. Combined analysis of FDG PET/CT and PSMA PET/CT findings with clinical and pathological data was conducted by the study’s principal investigator and the nuclear medicine physicians, ensuring a comprehensive and integrated assessment. The SUVmax of the primary tumor and the metastatic site lesions were assessed for both radiopharmaceuticals (FDG and PSMA). The total numbers of radiotracer-avid lesions were also analyzed individually for each radiotracer and for lesions demonstrating uptake with both radiotracers simultaneously.

Statistical analysis

Data were collected through a semi-structured questionnaire, tabulated and organized using Microsoft Office Excel version 2016, and analyzed using the JASP software (version 0.18.3). Descriptive statistics were expressed as mean, median, standard deviation, interquartile range, and range, as appropriate. Given the small sample size and the non-parametric distribution of the dataset, comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U-test. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee under approval number 30476720.9.0000.5404, in accordance with international ethical guidelines for research involving human subjects. All patients included in the study provided written informed consent after receiving detailed explanations regarding the study’s objectives, procedures, potential risks, and benefits, ensuring their voluntary and informed participation.

Results

The demographic and clinicopathological characteristics of the 14 patients included in the study are presented in Table 1. The median age was 61-years, with a predominance of male, smokers, and alcoholic patients. Most patients had good functional performance, one or more comorbidities, moderately differentiated tumors, and predominantly AJCC 8th edition stage III–IVB disease (M0) at presentation; one patient had a prior stage II primary but was imaged during locoregionally advanced recurrence. The tumors were located predominately in the oropharynx, larynx or oral cavity. Nine patients were analyzed at diagnosis and five patients at disease recurrence.

These PSMA-negative primaries involved the oropharynx (patient 2), oral cavity (patient 10), and larynx (patient 14). Discussion

The current study provides a head-to-head comparison of 18F-FDG and 18F-PSMA-1007 PET/CT in locoregionally advanced HNSCC, demonstrating that while FDG remains superior for tumor detection, PSMA offers complementary insights into tumor biology, particularly in characterizing angiogenesis and intratumoral heterogeneity.

It was observed that HNSCC lesions were more consistently identified by 18F-FDG PET/CT than by 18F-PSMA-1007 PET/CT, particularly for metastatic lesions, and 18F-FDG PET/CT outperformed 18F-PSMA-1007 PET/CT in sensitivity (100% vs. 76.9% for primary lesions; 100% vs. 46.2% for metastases).

Although PSMA uptake is biologically linked to tumor-associated neovasculature, the PSMA PET/CT detection rate observed in our cohort was lower than might be expected from the reported frequency of vascular PSMA expression in HNSCC histopathological series. In our study, 18F-PSMA-1007 demonstrated visually relevant uptake in the primary tumor in 10/13 evaluable patients (76.9%) and detected metastatic disease in 6/13 patients (46.2%), whereas FDG detected all evaluable primary and metastatic lesions. Tissue-based data have shown frequent, yet heterogeneous, endothelial PSMA expression in HNSCC, with a graded distribution of microvessel involvement and higher vascular PSMA expression in metastatic lymph nodes than in reactive nodes.34,35 Therefore, histologic vascular PSMA positivity does not necessarily translate into lesion detectability on PET, consistent with lesion-dependent heterogeneity of endothelial expression.

In fact, FDG PET/CT has assumed an important role in the initial assessment36 and early detection of recurrences in HNSCC.37,38 More recently, the diagnostic potential of PET/CT using radiotracers targeting PSMA, already established in prostate cancer, has been investigated in other solid tumors.22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29

In HNSCC, synchronous SCC (squamous cell carcinoma) of the oropharynx was identified in a retrospective study involving 764 patients with prostate cancer,21 an invasive SCC of the oropharynx was detected during prostate cancer staging,30 and, more recently, the use of a dual-tracer PET/CT approach with 18F-FDG and 18F-PSMA-1007 demonstrated FDG and PSMA uptake in both prostate and laryngeal carcinoma.31 Considering that PSMA uptake has been previously reported only in incidental cases of HNSCC, this is the first study that analyzed PET/CT using radiotracers targeting PSMA in a series of HNSCC patients. In addition, as 18F-FDG PET/CT enables the detection of glycolytic activity across the tumor microenvironment,8,10 and PSMA uptake reflects tumor neo vasculature,34,35 the results of the current study present, for the first time, preliminary evidence that metabolic reprogramming and neoangiogenesis are hallmarks of HNSCC.

It is worth commenting that one false positive finding was attributed to PSMA PET/CT. In patient 12, an increased PSMA uptake was observed in a unbiopsied sclerotic bone lesion located in the right sacral wing, without corresponding metabolic activity on FDG PET/CT or anatomical findings on CT. A high prevalence of nonspecific bone uptake on PSMA-1007 PET in patients with prostate cancer has been reported,39 and this finding was validated with follow-up PSMA PET/CT.40 Such nonspecific uptake may be attributed to PSMA expression in the neovasculature associated with inflammatory conditions and changes in vascular permeability.41

In the current study, it was observed that the mean SUVmax values in primary lesions detected by FDG PET/CT at 1 and 2h were higher than those detected by PSMA PET/CT, with an increase in both tracers’ uptake over time. In a review of PSMA-targeted radiopharmaceuticals in non-prostatic tumors, it was reported that SUVmax values observed on PSMA PET/CT were generally lower than those seen in prostate cancer, and that tracer distribution tended to be more heterogeneous.42 Although HNSCC was not included in that analysis, it was emphasized that in most of the evaluated tumors, PSMA expression was confined to the tumor neovasculature.43,44 In another report, when synchronous prostate and laryngeal carcinomas were evaluated by both tracers, the laryngeal carcinoma exhibited intense FDG uptake with only mild PSMA avidity, while the prostate carcinoma demonstrated the opposite pattern.31 The results of the current study support the concept that 18F-FDG PET/CT demonstrates more intense uptake and more diffuse distribution in HNSCC when compared to PSMA PET/CT.

Interestingly, it was also observed in the current study that patients with large lesions presented markedly different intralesional patterns, as in patient 6. FDG showed central uptake in the primary lesion, aligning with its affinity for hypoxic, glycolytically active tumor cores.45 PSMA exhibited peripheral uptake, likely due to PSMA expression in tumor-associated neovasculature.34 The central necrosis or hypoxia may limit PSMA expression to viable peripheral regions. This spatial dichotomy underscores the complementary roles of FDG (metabolic activity) and PSMA (angiogenesis) in HNSCC characterization. Similar findings have been reported in prostate cancer43 and glioblastoma,26 where PSMA uptake correlates with tumor microvessel density rather than epithelial proliferation. The distinct uptake patterns observed may have therapeutic implications: FDG’s central dominance supports its role in staging and surveillance, particularly for identifying aggressive, hypoxic tumor subclones resistant to radiotherapy.46 PSMA’s peripheral localization suggests potential utility for guiding anti-angiogenic therapies (e.g., VEGF inhibitors) in PSMA-avid subgroups and for selecting patients for PSMA-targeted radioligand therapy (e.g., 177Lu-PSMA), as proposed for other solid tumors.47

The current study has some limitations. The small sample size, both at initial diagnosis and at recurrence, and the single-center design may limit the statistical power and the generalizability of the findings. Another point is that the proliferative index Ki-67 as well as molecular characterization were not available for these patients, precluding correlation with the metabolic and/or angiogenic activity observed on imaging studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our data present preliminary evidence that 18F-FDG PET/CT is indispensable for HNSCC staging while 18F-PSMA-1007 provides complementary angiogenic profiling, contributing to tumor characterization. Further larger studies with patients at diagnosis and relapse from various services and with well tumor characterization have to be conducted to define consistent conclusions about the roles of PET/CT with 18F-FDG and 18F-PSMA-1007 in staging of HNSCC and in the selection of candidates for targeted therapies.
