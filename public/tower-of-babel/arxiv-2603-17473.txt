Abstract
. Let 𝗆𝖲𝗍 n \operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n} be the plactic-like monoid obtained by factoring the free monoid over a finite alphabet 𝒜 n \mathcal{A}_{n} by the meet of the stalactic congruence and its dual. In this paper, we prove that 𝗆𝖲𝗍 n \operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n} can be equipped with multiple involutions, and divide these involutions into ⌊ n 2 ⌋ + 1 \lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor+1 types. A faithful representation of 𝗆𝖲𝗍 n \operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n} under each of these involutions is obtained. We give transparent combinatorial characterizations of identities for 𝗆𝖲𝗍 n \operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n} under each involution, and so the finite basis problem and identity checking problem for them are solved.

2.1.
Words and identities

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a nonempty alphabet and
\mathcal{X}^{*}=\{x^{*}\,|\,x\in\mathcal{X}\}
be a disjoint copy of
\mathcal{X}
.
Elements of
\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{X}^{*}
are called
variables
, elements of the free involution monoid
F_{\mathsf{inv}}^{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{X})=(\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{X}^{*})^{+}\cup\{\varepsilon\}
are called
words
, and elements of the free monoid
\mathcal{X}^{\star}=\mathcal{X}^{+}\cup\{\varepsilon\}
are called
plain words
. A word
\mathbf{u}
is a
factor
of a word
\mathbf{w}
if
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{a}\mathbf{u}\mathbf{b}
for some
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in F_{\mathsf{inv}}^{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{X})
.

Let
\mathbf{u}\in F_{\mathsf{inv}}^{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{X})
be a word and
x,y\in\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{X}^{*}
be distinct variables. The
content

\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{u})
of
\mathbf{u}
is the set of variables that occur in
\mathbf{u}
. Denote by
\operatorname{\mathsf{occ}}(x,\mathbf{u})
the number of occurrences of
x
in
\mathbf{u}
. The
plain projection

\overline{\mathbf{u}}
of
\mathbf{u}
is the plain word obtained from
\mathbf{u}
by removing all occurrences of the symbol
∗
.
The
length

|\mathbf{u}|
of
\mathbf{u}
is the number of variables occurring in
\mathbf{u}
.
The
initial part
[resp.
final part
] of
\mathbf{u}
, denoted by
\operatorname{\mathsf{ip}}(\mathbf{u})
[resp.
\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\mathbf{u})
], is the word obtained from
\mathbf{u}
by retaining the occurrence of each variable
x\in\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{u})
satisfying
x\not\in\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{u}_{1})
[resp.
x\not\in\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{u}_{2})
] where
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}_{1}x\mathbf{u}_{2}
.
For any
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{X}^{*}
such that
\overline{x_{1}},\overline{x_{2}},\ldots,\overline{x_{n}}\in\mathcal{X}
are distinct variables, let
\mathbf{u}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
denote the word obtained from
\mathbf{u}
by retaining only the variables
x_{1},x_{1}^{*},x_{2},x_{2}^{*},\ldots,x_{n},x_{n}^{*}
.

An
identity
[resp.
plain identity
] is an expression
\mathbf{u}\approx\mathbf{v}
, where
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
are words in
(\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{X}^{*})^{+}
[resp.
\mathcal{X}^{+}
]. The identity
\overline{\mathbf{u}}\approx\overline{\mathbf{v}}
is called the
plain projection
of
\mathbf{u}\approx\mathbf{v}
.
We write
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}
if
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
are identical. An identity
\mathbf{u}\approx\mathbf{v}
is
non-trivial
if
\mathbf{u}\neq\mathbf{v}
.
An identity
\mathbf{u}\approx\mathbf{v}
is
balanced
if
\mathsf{occ}(x,\mathbf{u})=\mathsf{occ}(x,\mathbf{v})
for any
x\in\mathsf{con}(\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v})
.

An involution semigroup
(S,^{*})

satisfies
an identity
\mathbf{s}\approx\mathbf{t}
, if for any substitution
\varphi:\mathcal{X}\to S
, the elements
\varphi(\mathbf{s})
and
\varphi(\mathbf{t})
of
S
coincide; in this case,
\mathbf{s}\approx\mathbf{t}
is also said to be an
identity of

(S,^{*})
. For any involution semigroup
(S,^{*})
,
a set
\Sigma
of identities of
(S,^{*})
is an
identity basis
for
(S,^{*})
if every identity of
(S,^{*})
is deducible from
\Sigma
.
An involution semigroup is
finitely based
if it has some finite identity basis; otherwise, it is
non-finitely based
. The
finite basis problem
for involution semigroups is the decision problem of determining whether a given semigroup is finitely based. The
identity checking problem

Check-Id
(S,^{*})
for an involution semigroup
(S,^{*})
is the decision problem whose
instance is an arbitrary identity
\mathbf{u}\approx\mathbf{v}
, and the answer to such an instance is ‘YES’
if
S
satisfies
\mathbf{u}\approx\mathbf{v}
, and ‘NO’ if it does not.

Remark 2.1
.

Note that assigning the unit element to a variable
x
in an identity is effectively the same as removing all occurrences of
x
and
x^{*}
. Therefore any involution monoid that satisfies an identity
\mathbf{s}\approx\mathbf{t}
also satisfies the identity
\mathbf{s}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]\approx\mathbf{t}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
for any distinct variables
\overline{x_{1}},\overline{x_{2}},\ldots,\overline{x_{n}}\in\mathcal{X}
.

2.2.
The left-stalactic and right-stalactic monoids

Let
\mathcal{A}=\{1<2<3<\cdots\}
denote the ordered infinite alphabet and
\mathcal{A}_{n}=\{1<2<3<\cdots<n\}
the ordered finite alphabet.
The combinatorial objects and insertion algorithms related to the left-stalactic and right-stalactic monoids are given in the following.

A
stalactic tableau
is a finite array of variables of
\mathcal{A}
in which columns are top-aligned, and two variables appear in the same column if and only if they are equal.

Algorithm 1

(
[
14
, Section 3.7]
)
.

Input a stalactic tableau
T
and a variable
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
If
a
does not appear in
T
, add
a
to the left of the top row of
T
; if
a
does appear in
T
, add
a
to the bottom of the column in which
a
appears. Output the new tableau.

Let
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}
and
\mathbf{w}=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}^{\star}
. Then the stalactic tableau
{\rm P}^{\leftarrow}_{\operatorname{\mathsf{rSt}}}(\mathbf{w})
of
\mathbf{w}
is obtained as follows: reading

\mathbf{w}
from right-to-left, one starts with an empty tableau and inserts each variable in
\mathbf{w}
into a stalactic tableau according to Algorithm
1
.
For example,
{\rm P}^{\leftarrow}_{\operatorname{\mathsf{rSt}}}(36131512665)
is given as follows:

Define the relation
\equiv_{\mathsf{r}}
by

\mathbf{u}\equiv_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{v}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad{\rm P}^{\leftarrow}_{\mathsf{rSt}}(\mathbf{u})={\rm P}^{\leftarrow}_{\mathsf{rSt}}(\mathbf{v})

where
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
are words over
alphabet
\mathcal{A}
. It turns out that the relation
\equiv_{\mathsf{r}}
is a congruence on
\mathcal{A}^{\star}
which is called the
right-stalactic congruence
. The infinite-rank
right-stalactic monoid

\operatorname{\mathsf{rSt}}_{\infty}
is the factor monoid
\mathcal{A}^{\star}/_{\equiv_{\mathsf{r}}}
and the
right-stalactic monoid
of rank
n
, denoted by
\operatorname{\mathsf{rSt}}_{n}
, is the factor monoid
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}/_{\equiv_{\mathsf{r}}}
.

A new algorithm by can be obtained by replacing “left” with
“right” in Algorithm 1.
Using this new algorithm, one can compute a unique stalactic tableau from
a word
\mathbf{w}=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}^{\star}
: starting from the empty tableau, read
\mathbf{w}
from left-to-right and insert its variables one-by-one into the tableau. The resulting tableau is denoted by
{\rm P}^{\rightarrow}_{\operatorname{\mathsf{lSt}}}(\mathbf{w})
. Thus the
left-stalactic congruence

\equiv_{\mathsf{l}}
, the infinite-rank
left-stalactic monoid

\mathsf{lSt_{\infty}}
and
left-stalactic monoid
of rank
n

\mathsf{lSt_{n}}
are defined in a similar manner with the right-stalactic congruence and the right-stalactic monoid.

For any
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}^{\star}
, the
evaluation
of
\mathbf{u}
, denoted by
\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{u})
, is the
tuple of non-negative integers, indexed by
\mathcal{A}
, whose
a
-th element is
\operatorname{\mathsf{occ}}(a,\mathbf{u})
, thus this tuple describes the number of each variable in
\mathcal{A}
that appears in
\mathbf{u}
. It is immediate from the definition of the monoid
\operatorname{\mathsf{rSt}}_{\infty}
[resp.
\operatorname{\mathsf{lSt}}_{\infty}
] that if
\mathbf{u}\equiv_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{v}
[resp.
\mathbf{u}\equiv_{\mathsf{l}}\mathbf{v}
], then
\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{v})
, and hence it makes sense to define the evaluation of each element of
\operatorname{\mathsf{rSt}}_{\infty}
[resp.
\operatorname{\mathsf{lSt}}_{\infty}
] to be the evaluation
of any word representing it. Note that
\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{v})
implies that
\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{v})
. By the definitions of the left-stalactic and right-stalactic congruences, the following result holds.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
6
, Section 2.3]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}
. Then
\mathbf{u}\equiv_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{v}
if and only if
\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{v})
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\mathbf{v})
; and
\mathbf{u}\equiv_{\mathsf{l}}\mathbf{v}
if and only if
\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{v})
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{ip}}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{\mathsf{ip}}(\mathbf{v})
.

Note that neither the left-stalactic monoid nor the right-stalactic monoid is anti-automorphic. Then neither the left-stalactic monoid nor the right-stalactic monoid can be equipped with an involution.
In fact, the left-stalactic and right-stalactic monoids of rank
n
are anti-isomorphic.

Proposition 2.3
.

The monoids
\operatorname{\mathsf{rSt}}_{n}
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{lSt}}_{n}
are anti-isomorphic for each finite
n
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be any permutation on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
.
Then for any
\mathbf{w}=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}
, define a map
\overline{\sigma}
from
\operatorname{\mathsf{rSt}}_{n}
to
\operatorname{\mathsf{lSt}}_{n}
given by
\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{w})=\sigma(a_{k})\sigma(a_{k-1})\cdots\sigma(a_{2})\sigma(a_{1})
.
For any
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}
, if
\mathbf{u}\equiv_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{v}
, then
\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{v})
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\mathbf{v})
by Lemma
2.2
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}_{1}b_{1}\mathbf{u}_{2}b_{2}\cdots\mathbf{u}_{k}b_{k}
such that
\mathbf{u}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\mathbf{u})=b_{1}b_{2}\cdots b_{k}
. It follows from
\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\mathbf{v})
that
\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\mathbf{v})=b_{1}b_{2}\cdots b_{k}
. Then by
\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{v})
,
\mathbf{v}
can be written into

\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}_{1}b_{1}\mathbf{v}_{2}b_{2}\cdots\mathbf{v}_{k}b_{k}
satisfying
\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{v}_{1}\mathbf{v}_{2}\cdots\mathbf{v}_{k})=\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{u}_{1}\mathbf{u}_{2}\cdots\mathbf{u}_{k})
. By the definition of
\overline{\sigma}
,

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\sigma(b_{k})\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{u}_{k})\sigma(b_{k-1})\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{u}_{k-1})\cdots\sigma(b_{1})\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{u}_{1}),

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=\sigma(b_{k})\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{v}_{k})\sigma(b_{k-1})\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{v}_{k-1})\cdots\sigma(b_{1})\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{v}_{1}),

and so
\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{u}))=\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{v}))
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{ip}}(\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{u}))=\sigma(b_{k})\sigma(b_{k-1})\cdots\sigma(b_{1})=\operatorname{\mathsf{ip}}(\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{v}))
. Hence
\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{u})\equiv_{\mathsf{l}}\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{v})
by Lemma
2.2
. Symmetrically,
\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{u})\equiv_{\mathsf{l}}\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{v})
implies
\mathbf{u}\equiv_{\mathsf{r}}\mathbf{v}
. Thus
\overline{\sigma}
is well-defined and injective. Let
\sigma^{-1}
be the reverse of
\sigma
.
For any
\mathbf{w}=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}
, it is routine to verify that
\overline{\sigma}(\sigma^{-1}(a_{k})\sigma^{-1}(a_{k-1})\cdots\sigma^{-1}(a_{2})\sigma^{-1}(a_{1}))=\mathbf{w}
. Thus
\overline{\sigma}
is surjective, and so
\overline{\sigma}
is bijective.

For any
\mathbf{w}=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{k},\mathbf{w}^{\prime}=a^{\prime}_{1}a^{\prime}_{2}\cdots a^{\prime}_{\ell}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}
,

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\overline{\sigma}(a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{k}a^{\prime}_{1}a^{\prime}_{2}\cdots a^{\prime}_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\sigma(a^{\prime}_{\ell})\sigma(a^{\prime}_{\ell-1})\cdots\sigma(a^{\prime}_{1})\sigma(a_{k})\sigma(a_{k-1})\cdots\sigma(a_{1})=\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{w}^{\prime})\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{w}).

Therefore
\overline{\sigma}
is an anti-isomorphism from
\operatorname{\mathsf{rSt}}_{n}
to
\operatorname{\mathsf{lSt}}_{n}
.
∎

2.3.
The meet-stalactic monoid and its involutions

For any
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{A}^{\star}
, define
{\rm P}_{\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}}(\mathbf{u})
to be the stalactic tableau pair
({\rm P}^{\rightarrow}_{\mathsf{lSt}}(\mathbf{u}),{\rm P}^{\leftarrow}_{\mathsf{rSt}}(\mathbf{u}))
. Define the relation

\mathbf{u}\equiv_{\mathsf{m}}\mathbf{v}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad{\rm P}_{\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}}(\mathbf{u})={\rm P}_{\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}}(\mathbf{v})

for all
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{A}^{\star}
. It is easy to see that

\equiv_{\mathsf{m}}=\equiv_{\mathsf{r}}\wedge\equiv_{\mathsf{l}}
. Thus the relation
\equiv_{\mathsf{m}}
is a congruence which is called the
meet-stalactic congruence
.
Define the infinite-rank
meet-stalactic monoid

\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{\infty}
to be the factor monoid
\mathcal{A}^{\star}/\equiv_{\mathsf{m}}
and the
meet-stalactic monoid
of rank
n
, denoted by
\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n}
, to be the factor monoid
\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}/\equiv_{\mathsf{m}}
.
Now by the definition of the meet-stalactic congruence and Lemma
2.2
, the following result holds.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}
. Then
\mathbf{u}\equiv_{\mathsf{m}}\mathbf{v}
if and only if
\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{v})
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{ip}}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{\mathsf{ip}}(\mathbf{v})
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\mathbf{v})
.

By
[
6
, Proposition 3.1]
, the monoid
\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{\infty}
can also be presented by
\langle\mathcal{A}|\mathcal{R}_{\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{\infty}}\rangle
where

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{\infty}}=

\displaystyle\{(a\mathbf{u}ab\mathbf{v}a,a\mathbf{u}ba\mathbf{v}a):a,b\in\mathcal{A}_{n},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}\}

(2.1)

\displaystyle\cup

\displaystyle\{(a\mathbf{u}ab\mathbf{v}b,a\mathbf{u}ba\mathbf{v}b):a,b\in\mathcal{A}_{n},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}\}.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, a presentation for the monoid
\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n}
can be obtained by restricting generators and relations of the above presentation to the generators in
\mathcal{A}_{n}
.

Proposition 2.5
.

All involutions of
\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n}
are determined by all elements of order 1 and 2 in the symmetric group on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
∗
is an involution of
\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n}
. Note that in relations (2.1), the evaluation of words are preserved. Then
for any
k\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
, there exists some
\ell\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
such that
k^{*}=\ell
and
\ell^{*}=k
.
Thus
∗
is determined by a permutation
\sigma
on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
satisfying
\sigma^{2}=\sigma_{0}
where
\sigma_{0}
is the identity permutation on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
.

Let
\sigma
be any permutation on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
satisfying
\sigma^{2}=\sigma_{0}
. Then for any
\mathbf{w}=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}
, define a map from
\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n}
to itself given by
\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{w})=\sigma(a_{k})\sigma(a_{k-1})\cdots\sigma(a_{2})\sigma(a_{1})
.
For any
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}
, if
\mathbf{u}\equiv_{\mathsf{m}}\mathbf{v}
, then
\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{v})
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{ip}}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{\mathsf{ip}}(\mathbf{v})
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\mathbf{v})
by Lemma

. Without loss of generality, we may assume
\mathbf{u}=b_{0}\mathbf{u}_{1}b_{1}\mathbf{u}_{2}b_{2}\cdots\mathbf{u}_{k}b_{k}
such that
\mathbf{u}_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}
and
b_{0},b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{k}
are the first or the last occurrences of all variables in
\mathbf{u}
. It follows from
\operatorname{\mathsf{ip}}(\mathbf{v})=\operatorname{\mathsf{ip}}(\mathbf{u})
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\mathbf{v})=\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\mathbf{u})

that
b_{0},b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{k}
are the first or the last occurrences of all variables in
\mathbf{v}
. Then by
\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{v})=\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{u})

\mathbf{v}

\mathbf{v}=b_{0}\mathbf{v}_{1}b_{1}\mathbf{v}_{2}b_{2}\cdots\mathbf{v}_{k}b_{k}

\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{v}_{1}\mathbf{v}_{2}\cdots\mathbf{v}_{k})=\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{u}_{1}\mathbf{u}_{2}\cdots\mathbf{u}_{k})
. By the definition of
\overline{\sigma}
,

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\sigma(b_{k})\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{u}_{k})\sigma(b_{k-1})\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{u}_{k-1})\cdots\sigma(b_{1})\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{u}_{1})\sigma(b_{0}),

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=\sigma(b_{k})\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{v}_{k})\sigma(b_{k-1})\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{v}_{k-1})\cdots\sigma(b_{1})\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{v}_{1})\sigma(b_{0}),

and so
\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{u}))=\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{v}))
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{ip}}(\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{u}))=\operatorname{\mathsf{ip}}(\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{v}))
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{u}))=\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{v}))
. Hence
\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{u})\equiv_{\mathsf{m}}\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{v})
by Lemma
2.4
. Symmetrically,
\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{u})\equiv_{\mathsf{m}}\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{v})

\mathbf{u}\equiv_{\mathsf{m}}\mathbf{v}

is well-defined and injective. Since
\sigma^{2}=\sigma_{0}
,
\sigma^{-1}=\sigma
.
For any

\overline{\sigma}

\overline{\sigma}
is bijective.

For any

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\overline{\sigma}(a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{k}a^{\prime}_{1}a^{\prime}_{2}\cdots a^{\prime}_{\ell})

\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n}

3.
Representations of
\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n}
with involution

In
[
8
, Theorem 4.4]
, Cain et al. have given a faithful representation of the right-stalactic monoid. According to this result, a faithful representation of
\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n}
under each involution is obtained.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
8
, Theorem 4.4]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{S}
be a commutative unital semiring with zero containing an element
of infinite multiplicative order. The right-stalactic monoid of rank
n
admits a faithful representation by upper triangular matrices of size
n^{2}
over
\mathbb{S}
having block-diagonal structure with largest block of size
2
(or size
1
if
n=1
).

Denote by
\psi_{r}
the representation of right-stalactic monoid given in
[
8
, Theorems 4.3 and 4.4]
. Let
\sigma
be any permutation on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
. For any
\mathbf{w}=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}
, define a map
\overline{\sigma}
from
\operatorname{\mathsf{lSt}}_{n}
to
\operatorname{\mathsf{rSt}}_{n}
given by
\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{w})=\sigma(a_{k})\sigma(a_{k-1})\cdots\sigma(a_{2})\sigma(a_{1})
. By Proposition 2.3,
\overline{\sigma}
is an anti-isomorphism between
\operatorname{\mathsf{rSt}}_{n}
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{lSt}}_{n}
. Define a map
\psi_{\ell}:\mathsf{lSt}_{n}\rightarrow UT_{n^{2}}(\mathbb{S})
given by
\mathbf{w}\mapsto(\psi_{r}(\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{w})))^{D}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

The map
\psi_{\ell}:\mathsf{lSt}_{n}\rightarrow UT_{n^{2}}(\mathbb{S})
is a faithful representation of
\mathsf{lSt}_{n}
.

Proof.

Clearly,
\psi_{\ell}
is injective by its definition. It suffices to show that for any
\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}
,

\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{w}_{1}\mathbf{w}_{2})=\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{w}_{1})\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{w}_{2})
.
Denote by
\mathbf{w}_{1}=b_{1}b_{2}\cdots b_{m}
and
\mathbf{w}_{2}=c_{1}c_{2}\cdots c_{n}
. Then

\displaystyle\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{w}_{1}\mathbf{w}_{2})

\displaystyle=\psi_{\ell}(b_{1}b_{2}\cdots b_{m}c_{1}c_{2}\cdots c_{n})

\displaystyle=(\psi_{r}(\overline{\sigma}(b_{1}b_{2}\cdots b_{m}c_{1}c_{2}\cdots c_{n})))^{D}

\displaystyle=(\psi_{r}(\sigma(c_{n})\sigma(c_{n-1})\cdots\sigma(c_{1})\sigma(b_{m})\sigma(b_{m-1})\cdots\sigma(b_{1})))^{D}

\displaystyle=(\psi_{r}(\sigma(c_{n}))\psi_{r}(\sigma(c_{n-1}))\cdots\psi_{r}(\sigma(c_{1}))\psi_{r}(\sigma(b_{m}))\psi_{r}(\sigma(b_{m-1}))\cdots\psi_{r}(\sigma(b_{1})))^{D}

\displaystyle=(\psi_{r}(\sigma(b_{1})))^{D}(\psi_{r}(\sigma(b_{2})))^{D}\cdots(\psi_{r}(\sigma(b_{m})))^{D}(\psi_{r}(\sigma(c_{1})))^{D}(\psi_{r}(\sigma(c_{2})))^{D}\cdots(\psi_{r}(\sigma(c_{n})))^{D}

\displaystyle=(\psi_{r}(\sigma(b_{m}))\psi_{r}(\sigma(b_{m-1}))\cdots\psi_{r}(\sigma(b_{1})))^{D}(\psi_{r}(\sigma(c_{n}))\psi_{r}(\sigma(c_{n-1}))\cdots\psi_{r}(\sigma(c_{1})))^{D}

\displaystyle=(\psi_{r}(\sigma(b_{m})\sigma(b_{m-1})\cdots\sigma(b_{1})))^{D}(\psi_{r}(\sigma(c_{n})\sigma(c_{n-1})\cdots\sigma(c_{1})))^{D}

\displaystyle=(\psi_{r}(\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{w}_{1})))^{D}(\psi_{r}(\overline{\sigma}(\mathbf{w}_{2})))^{D}

\displaystyle=\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{w}_{1})\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{w}_{2}),

as required.
∎

Recall that for
i=0,1,2,\dots,\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
,
\sigma_{i}
is the permutation which can be decomposed into
i
disjoint 2-cycles and
\sigma_{i}
induces an involution
{}^{\sigma_{i}}
on
\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n}
.
For any
\mathbf{w}=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}
, define a map
\overline{\sigma_{i}}
from
\operatorname{\mathsf{lSt}}_{n}
to
\operatorname{\mathsf{rSt}}_{n}
given by
\overline{\sigma_{i}}(\mathbf{w})=\sigma_{i}(a_{k})\sigma_{i}(a_{k-1})\cdots\sigma_{i}(a_{2})\sigma_{i}(a_{1})
.
By Proposition 2.3,
\overline{\sigma_{i}}
is an anti-isomorphism between
\operatorname{\mathsf{rSt}}_{n}
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{lSt}}_{n}
. Define
\psi_{\ell}^{(i)}:\mathsf{lSt}_{n}\rightarrow UT_{n^{2}}(\mathbb{S})
given by
\mathbf{w}\mapsto(\psi_{r}(\overline{\sigma_{i}}(\mathbf{w})))^{D}
.
By Theorem 3.2,
\psi_{\ell}^{(i)}:\mathsf{lSt}_{n}\rightarrow UT_{n^{2}}(\mathbb{S})
is a faithful representation of
\mathsf{lSt}_{n}
.
Denote by
\mathsf{diag}\{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},\dots,\Lambda_{n}\}
the block diagonal matrix

\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda_{1}&&&\\
&\Lambda_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\Lambda_{n}\end{pmatrix}

where
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},\dots,\Lambda_{n}
are square matrices.
Define a map
\psi^{(i)}:\mathsf{mSt}_{n}\rightarrow UT_{2n^{2}}(\mathbb{S})
given by
\mathbf{w}\mapsto\mathsf{diag}\{\psi_{\ell}^{(i)}(\mathbf{w}),\psi_{r}(\mathbf{w})\}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

The map
\psi^{(i)}:(\mathsf{mSt}_{n},^{\sigma_{i}})\rightarrow(UT_{2n^{2}},^{D})
is a faithful representation of
(\mathsf{mSt}_{n},^{\sigma_{i}})
for
i=0,1,2,\dots,\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
.

Proof.

By the definition of
\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n}
and Theorems 3.1 and 3.2,
\psi^{(i)}
is a faithful representation of
\mathsf{mSt}_{n}
. To show that
\psi^{(i)}
is a faithful representation of
(\mathsf{mSt}_{n},^{\sigma_{i}})
, we only need to show that for any
\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}
,
\psi^{(i)}(\mathbf{w}^{\sigma_{i}})=(\psi^{(i)}(\mathbf{w}))^{D}
. It suffices to show that for any
j\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
,
\psi^{(i)}(j^{\sigma_{i}})=(\psi^{(i)}(j))^{D}
.
By the definition of
\psi^{(i)}
,

\displaystyle\psi^{(i)}(j^{\sigma_{i}})

\displaystyle=\psi^{(i)}(\sigma_{i}(j))

\displaystyle=\mathsf{diag}\{\psi_{\ell}^{(i)}(\sigma_{i}(j)),\psi_{r}(\sigma_{i}(j))\}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{diag}\{(\psi_{r}(\sigma_{i}(\sigma_{i}(j))))^{D},\psi_{r}(\sigma_{i}(j))\}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{diag}\{(\psi_{r}(j))^{D},\psi_{r}(\sigma_{i}(j))\}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{diag}\{(\psi_{r}(\sigma_{i}(j)))^{D},\psi_{r}(j)\}^{D}

\displaystyle=(\mathsf{diag}\{\psi_{\ell}^{(i)}(j),\psi_{r}(j)\})^{D}

\displaystyle=(\psi^{(i)}(j))^{D},

as required.
∎

4.
Identities of
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n},^{\sigma_{0}})

In this section, we investigate the identities of
\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n}
with involution
{}^{\sigma_{0}}
determined by the identity permutation on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
.
We prove that for all finite
n\geq 2
,
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n},^{\sigma_{0}})
satisfy the same identities. Further,
we give characterizations of the identities satisfied by them; based on these results,
the finite basis problem and identity checking problem for
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n},^{\sigma_{0}})
are solved.

4.1.
Embedding into a direct product of copies of
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{2},^{\sigma_{0}})

For
n>2
, we show that
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n},^{\sigma_{0}})
can be embedded into a direct product of copies of
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{2},^{\sigma_{0}})
.
For any
i,j\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
with
i<j
, define a map
\varphi_{ij}
from
\mathcal{A}_{n}
to
\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{2}
in the following way: for any
k\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
,

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle\longmapsto\begin{cases}[1]_{\operatorname{\mathsf{m}}}&\text{if }k=i,\\
[2]_{\operatorname{\mathsf{m}}}&\text{if }k=j,\\
\left[\varepsilon\right]_{\operatorname{\mathsf{m}}}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

It is routine to verify that the map
\varphi_{ij}
can be extended to a homomorphism from
(\mathcal{A}^{\star}_{n},^{\sigma_{0}})
to
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{2},^{\sigma_{0}})
.
In fact, the following result holds.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}
. Then
\mathbf{u}\equiv_{\mathsf{m}}\mathbf{v}
if and only if
\varphi_{ij}(\mathbf{u})\equiv_{\mathsf{m}}\varphi_{ij}(\mathbf{v})
for all
1\leq i<j\leq n
.

Proof.

To prove the necessity, it suffices to show that for any
i,j
with
1\leq i<j\leq n
, if
\mathbf{u}\equiv_{\mathsf{m}}\mathbf{v}
, then
\varphi_{ij}(\mathbf{u})\equiv_{\mathsf{m}}\varphi_{ij}(\mathbf{v})
.
If
\mathbf{u}\equiv_{\mathsf{m}}\mathbf{v}
, then
\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\mathbf{v}),\operatorname{\mathsf{ip}}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{\mathsf{ip}}(\mathbf{v})
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\mathbf{v})
by Proposition
2.4
. In particular, the number of occurrences of
i
and
j
in
\mathbf{u}
is the same as that of
\mathbf{v}
, respectively; and the order of
i
and
j
occurring in
\mathbf{u}
is the same as that of
\mathbf{v}
. Note that
the occurrences of
i
in
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
correspond to the the occurrences of
1
in
\varphi_{ij}(\mathbf{u})
and
\varphi_{ij}(\mathbf{v})
, respectively;
and the occurrences of
j
in
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
correspond to the the occurrences of
2
in
\varphi_{ij}(\mathbf{u})
and
\varphi_{ij}(\mathbf{v})
, respectively.
Therefore
\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\varphi_{ij}(\mathbf{u}))=\operatorname{\mathsf{ev}}(\varphi_{ij}(\mathbf{v}))
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{ip}}(\varphi_{ij}(\mathbf{u}))=\operatorname{\mathsf{ip}}(\varphi_{ij}(\mathbf{v}))
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\varphi_{ij}(\mathbf{u}))=\operatorname{\mathsf{fp}}(\varphi_{ij}(\mathbf{u}))
, that is
\varphi_{ij}(\mathbf{u})\equiv_{\mathsf{m}}\varphi_{ij}(\mathbf{v})
by Proposition
2.4
.
The sufficiency can be obtained symmetrically.
∎

For each
n\geq 3
, let
I_{n}
be the index set

\left\{(i,j):1\leq i<j\leq n\right\}.

Now, consider the map

\phi_{n}:(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n},^{\sigma_{0}})\longrightarrow\prod\limits_{I_{n}}(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{2},^{\sigma_{0}}),

whose
(i,j)
-th component is given by
\varphi_{ij}([\mathbf{w}]_{\mathsf{m}})
for
\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}
and
(i,j)\in I_{n}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

The map
\phi_{n}
is an embedding.

Proof.

It follows from the definition of
\phi_{n}
and Lemma
4.1
that
\phi_{n}
is a homomorphism and for any
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\star}
,
\mathbf{u}\equiv_{\mathsf{m}}\mathbf{v}
if and only if
\phi_{n}([\mathbf{u}]_{\mathsf{m}})=\phi_{n}([\mathbf{v}]_{\mathsf{m}})
. Therefore
\phi_{n}
is an embedding.
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

For any
n\geq 3
,
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n},^{\sigma_{0}})
and
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{2},^{\sigma_{0}})
satisfy exactly the same identities.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.2
, for any
n\geq 3
,
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n},^{\sigma_{0}})
can be embedded into a direct product of
\binom{n}{2}
copies of
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{2},^{\sigma_{0}})
.
Thus
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n},^{\sigma_{0}})
is in the variety generated by
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{2},^{\sigma_{0}})
. Since
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{2},^{\sigma_{0}})
is a submonoid of
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n},^{\sigma_{0}})
for any
n\geq 3
,
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{2},^{\sigma_{0}})
is in the variety generated by
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{n},^{\sigma_{0}})
.
Therefore they satisfy exactly the same identities.
∎

(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{4},^{\sigma})
is defined by the identities (
1.1
) and (
1.2
).

Proof.

Clearly,
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{4},^{\sigma})
satisfies the identities (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) by Theorem
6.4
.
It suffices to show that each non-trivial identity satisfied by
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{4},^{\sigma})
can be deduced from (
1.2
).
Let
\Sigma
be the set of all non-trivial balanced identities satisfied by
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{4},^{\sigma})
but not deducible from (
1.2
). Suppose that
\Sigma\neq\emptyset
. Note that any balanced identity
\mathbf{u}\approx\mathbf{v}
in
\Sigma
can be written
uniquely in the form
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}a\mathbf{w}\approx\mathbf{v}^{\prime}b\mathbf{w}
where
a,b
are distinct variables and
|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}|=|\mathbf{v}^{\prime}|
. Choose an identity, say
\mathbf{u}\approx\mathbf{v}
, from
\Sigma
such that when it is written as
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}a\mathbf{w}\approx\mathbf{v}^{\prime}b\mathbf{w}
,
the lengths of the words
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}
are as short as possible. Since
\mathbf{u}\approx\mathbf{v}
is balanced, we have
\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime}a)=\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{v}^{\prime}b)
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{occ}}(x,\mathbf{u}^{\prime}a)=\operatorname{\mathsf{occ}}(x,\mathbf{v}^{\prime}b)
for any
x\in\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime}a)
.

Since
\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime}a)=\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{v}^{\prime}b)
, we have
b\in\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})
, and so
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}a=\mathbf{u}_{1}bc\mathbf{u}_{2}
with
b\neq c
and
b\not\in\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{u}_{2})
. Since
\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime}a)=\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{v}^{\prime}b)
, we have
c\in\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{v}^{\prime})
, and so
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}=\mathbf{v}_{1}c\mathbf{v}_{2}
with
c\not\in\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{v}_{2})
. Thus
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}_{1}bc\mathbf{u}_{2}\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}_{1}c\mathbf{v}_{2}b\mathbf{w}
.
On the one hand, we claim that
b\in\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{u}_{1})
or
c\in\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{u}_{1})
. If
b,c\not\in\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{u}_{1})
, then
\operatorname{\mathsf{occ}}(b,\mathbf{u}^{\prime}a)=1
, and so
\mathbf{u}[b,c]
starts with
b
but
\mathbf{v}[b,c]
does not, which contradicts (ii) of Theorem
6.4
.
On the other hand,
we claim that
b\in\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{u}_{2}\mathbf{w})
or
c\in\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{u}_{2}\mathbf{w})
. If
b,c\not\in\operatorname{\mathsf{con}}(\mathbf{u}_{2}\mathbf{w})
, then
\mathbf{u}[b,c]
ends with
c
but
\mathbf{v}[b,c]
does not, which contradicts (ii) of Theorem
6.4
.
Therefore, the
identities (
1.2
) can be used to convert
\mathbf{u}_{1}bc\mathbf{u}_{2}\mathbf{w}
into
\mathbf{u}_{1}cb\mathbf{u}_{2}\mathbf{w}
. By repeating this process, the word
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}^{\prime}a\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{u}_{1}bc\mathbf{u}_{2}\mathbf{w}
can be converted into the word
\mathbf{u}_{1}c\mathbf{u}_{2}b\mathbf{w}
by the
identities (
1.2
).

Clearly the identities
\mathbf{u}_{1}c\mathbf{u}_{2}b\mathbf{w}\approx\mathbf{u}^{\prime}a\mathbf{w}\approx\mathbf{v}^{\prime}b\mathbf{w}
hold in
(\operatorname{\mathsf{mSt}}_{4},^{\sigma})
. Note that
|\mathbf{u}_{1}c\mathbf{u}_{2}b\mathbf{w}|=|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}a\mathbf{w}|=|\mathbf{v}^{\prime}b\mathbf{w}|
and words in the identity
\mathbf{u}_{1}c\mathbf{u}_{2}b\mathbf{w}\approx\mathbf{v}^{\prime}b\mathbf{w}
have a longer common suffix than words in the identity
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}a\mathbf{w}\approx\mathbf{v}^{\prime}b\mathbf{w}
. Hence
\mathbf{u}_{1}c\mathbf{u}_{2}b\mathbf{w}\approx\mathbf{v}^{\prime}b\mathbf{w}\notin\Sigma
by the minimality assumption on the lengths of
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}
, that is,
\mathbf{u}_{1}c\mathbf{u}_{2}b\mathbf{w}\approx\mathbf{v}^{\prime}b\mathbf{w}
can be deducible from (
1.2
). We have shown that
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}a\mathbf{w}\approx\mathbf{u}_{1}c\mathbf{u}_{2}b\mathbf{w}
can be deducible from (
1.2
). Hence
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}a\mathbf{w}\approx\mathbf{v}^{\prime}b\mathbf{w}
can be deducible from (
1.2
), which contradicts
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}a\mathbf{w}\approx\mathbf{v}^{\prime}b\mathbf{w}\in\Sigma
. Therefore
\Sigma=\emptyset
.
∎
