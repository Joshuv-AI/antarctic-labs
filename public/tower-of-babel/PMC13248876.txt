Adolescent Online Gaming in Jordan: A Cross‐Sectional Study on Habits and Health Perceptions Through the Health Belief Model
Authors: Haitham Khatatbeh, Tariq Al‐Dwaikat, Moawiah Khatatbeh, Viktoria Premusz, Mohanad Khatatbeh, Osama Alkouri, Nour Alrida, Mohammed AlBashtawy, Ied Al‐Sadoon, Amira M Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPlaying online games is one of the main activities in leisure time for adolescents but it has started to cause serious concerns about their mental and physical health.PurposeThe current study, based on the Health Belief Model (HBM), examines the relationship between online gaming habits and health perceptions among adolescents in Jordan. HBM provides a framework to understand how perceptions of susceptibility to harm and cues to action influence health behaviors, mainly through hours of playing games a day.MethodologyThis cross‐sectional study utilized a sample of 403 adolescents aged 10–17 years, employing a Google form to assess daily gaming duration, types of games played, and perceptions of physical and mental health. We used SPSS 24.0 to do both descriptive and inferential statistics.ResultsParticipants spent approximately 3.1 h daily on gaming. More than a quarter of participants did not perceive gaming as harmful to physical or mental health, suggesting low perceived susceptibility. Also, 36.7% had not received any counseling on the potential harms of online gaming, highlighting a gap in cues to action. Among those who received guidance, parents were recognized as the principal source of counseling. The study found significant negative correlations among age, daily gaming hours, and perceptions of physical and mental health. Regression analyses revealed that older age, type of game played, and increased daily hours of gaming significantly predicted poorer physical health ratings (β = −0.220, p < 0.01; β = −2.838, p < 0.05, β = −0.310, p < 0.01, respectively). On the other hand, older age (β = −0.199, p < 0.05), increased daily gaming hours (β = −0.176, p < 0.05), and believing that online games harm mental health (β = −0.285, p < 0.01) were significant predictors of poorer mental health perceptions.ConclusionsThe study results underscore the necessity for initiatives designed to foster healthier gaming habits and improving awareness of potential harms among adolescents, parents, teachers, school nurses, and healthcare practitioners in Jordan.Keywords: adolescent, counseling, gaming, habit, Health Belief Model, perception

What Is Known?

HBM is a well‐known model that helps us understand health behaviors, especially how perceptions of susceptibility to harm and cues to action influence behavior change.

What Is New?

This study utilizes HBM to examine adolescent online gaming, providing a distinctive insight into how perceptions of harm (perceived susceptibility) and counseling regarding the dangers of internet gaming (cues to action) affect gaming habits and health outcomes.

What Is Significant for Clinical Practice?

The results highlight the need for targeted counseling to develop healthier gaming habits.

Introduction

Online gaming is nowadays very popular, especially among adolescents, on the accounts that it provides various options for entertainment, social engagement, and also cognitive development (Purwaningsih and Nurmala 2021; Yang et al. 2022). However, adolescents' lives are claimed to be tremendously affected by online gaming (Ali et al. 2022; Putra et al. 2023). Particularly, online gaming poses significant questions which have to do with its claimed harmful effects on adolescents (Chao and Yu 2023), especially their physical and mental health (Putra et al. 2023).

Previous studies indicated that physical and mental health of adolescents may be influenced by sociodemographic traits and online gaming habits. These factors and habits encompass age (Ali et al. 2021; Hawi et al. 2018; Zhu et al. 2021), gender (Chaabane et al. 2021; Keogh 2022; Phan et al. 2020), the type of games played, and duration of gaming sessions (Chan et al. 2022; Yu et al. 2022). In general, the overuse of online games can lead to sedentary lifestyles that affect physical health metrics (Putra et al. 2023). These metrics encompass weight (Shen et al. 2021), bone density (Pehlivanturk Kizilkan et al. 2024), low back pain (Hakala et al. 2006), various musculoskeletal problems (Mutlu et al. 2024), and sleep patterns (Gundogdu and Eroglu 2022; Hawi et al. 2018; Lin et al. 2021; Mutlu et al. 2023). Likewise, the competitive and intense nature of most online games may have an impact on mental health (Putra et al. 2023), including stress and anxiety levels (Lin et al. 2021; Xu et al. 2023), emotional control (Zhao et al. 2021), and potentially suicidal ideation (Xie et al. 2023). Nonetheless, not all studies report harmful outcomes; some find neutral or even positive effects under specific conditions. For example, a study demonstrated that playing violent video games with motion‐capture technology did not increase aggression (Charles et al. 2013). In another study by Soylu et al. (2023), it was found that aggression cannot be predicted based on the years and also hours that are spent playing online games.

According to the literature, online gaming can have benefits and can also have threats. Therefore, online gaming needs to be thoroughly evaluated. Such evaluations need to be mainly focused on the relationship between online gaming behaviors, health issues, as well as awareness of the potential risks. It is worth noting that the worldwide literature highlights a complex link between the positive and negative implications of online gaming. Nevertheless, there are still few thorough observations from the Middle East.

It is worth noting that screen time and digital literacy are being increasingly highlighted in the literature. Nevertheless, the manner in which adolescents approach potential risks of online gaming is still vague. It is also still vague whether adolescents have received advice about how to safely approach online games (Putra et al. 2023). Knowing these attitudes and the source of their counseling is necessary to make effective health promotion programs (Khatatbeh et al. 2024; Putra et al. 2023).

Some parts of the Health Belief Model (HBM) that this study is based on are perceived susceptibility and cues to action (Norman and Conner 2017). This offers a robust framework for understanding how adolescents' perceived susceptibility to health issues and cues to action influence their gaming habits; and how their gaming habits shape their health perceptions. We assume that adolescents' online gaming habits and how they feel about their physical and mental health are affected by their perceived susceptibility to harm from gaming and their exposure to cues to action, such as counseling. The HBM constructs used in this study also showed that demographic traits are important. Consistent with HBM principles, health‐related perceptions are rarely the result of a single factor but develop from the interaction of modifying factors (demographic traits), perceived susceptibility, and cues to action. Therefore, this study adopts a holistic approach by examining these constructs all together to account for their collective influence on adolescent health perceptions. The scope of the study and data accessibility led to a purposeful selection of particular constructs that belong to the HBM. Although HBM is a broad framework, the current study focuses solely on the risk perceptions and instant triggers that are particularly pertinent to teenagers' gaming behaviors.

The findings of this study can assist parents, school nurses, healthcare professionals, teachers, and legislators in formulating effective strategies to encourage healthier online gaming habits among adolescents. The study aims to assess (1) online gaming habits among adolescents such as daily hours spent gaming and types of games usually played; (2) adolescents' perceived physical and mental health, and their access to and sources of counseling on the potential impacts of online gaming; (3) differences in adolescents' perceived physical and mental health based on their demographic traits and online gaming habits; and (4) which gaming habits that serve as predictors of adolescents' perceived physical and mental health.

Methods

Design and Setting

This descriptive cross‐sectional study was carried out from February 8 to April 7, 2025, in governmental schools in northern Jordan. In Jordan, governmental schools are for only one gender. We chose this time frame to avoid major exam times and lower stress levels as a confounder. The study focused on adolescents aged 10–17 years who were enrolled in the chosen schools and possessed internet access to participate in the online survey. In order to adequately depict the overall adolescent population from the targeted schools, no restrictions were made based on mental health condition, gaming behaviors, or any other demographic variables.

Participants

Using a convenience sample technique, the researchers in the present study selected Ministry of Education (MoE) schools that were accessible and eager to participate. Particularly, the researchers included a number of schools that consented to take part and could assist in administering online consent forms and questionnaires.

For the present study, the researchers determined that approximately 400 volunteers were needed. This number of volunteers was predicated based on an 80% statistical power, a 5% margin of error, a 95% confidence level, and a 20% likelihood of having an Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD). Particularly, the researchers sent parents extra consent forms (500) to allow for potential response failures. Out of the 500 forms, 420 (84%) were sent back to the researchers. Specifically, 403 out of 420 (96%) surveys were completed successfully, yielding an overall response rate of 80.6% (403 out of 500).

Data Collection

To fulfill ethical standards of research, a Google form link was sent to parents or guardians of potential participants. This link explained the study's objectives, voluntary participation, confidentiality guarantees, and the right to withdraw from the study at any time without consequence. After gaining parental consent, a separate Google form link containing the study questionnaire was sent to adolescents through their parents or guardians.

Before data collection, the selected schools' teachers underwent online training about data collection procedures and ethical considerations. Researchers provided teachers with links to both the parental consent form and the adolescent questionnaire for dissemination.

Instruments

The instrument was designed to collect data that was useful for our research questions and fit with key HBM constructs. As shown in Table 1, it included questions about adolescents' age and gender. Other questions were about adolescents' online gaming habits, specifically daily gaming hours and the type of online game often played (e.g., fighting/wars, football/other sports, fashion/dress, racing/cars, building/designing, simulation, other games like cards, chess, music, and puzzles). To assess aspects of perceived susceptibility regarding health threats, participants were asked about their perceived impact of online gaming on both physical health (0 = No and 1 = Yes) and mental health (0 = No and 1 = Yes). Furthermore, to explore cues to action, the survey included questions on whether counseling about online gaming harms had been received (0 = No and 1 = Yes). If counseling had been received, participants were also asked to identify the source. Possible sources included parents, friends/peers, relatives, schoolteachers, nurses/physicians, or social media. Lastly, participants rated their perceived physical health and perceived mental health on a 5‐point Likert scale, with 1 being “very weak” and 5 being “very good.”

Pilot Testing

It is worth noting that there was no need to modify the questionnaires' terminology because it was easily comprehensible to schoolchildren. On the other hand, the researchers randomly selected 30 completed surveys for a pilot study. The intent was to evaluate the feasibility of the surveys' content and feasibility of data gathering procedures as well.

The results of the pilot study stated that the Likert‐type items had a moderate level of reliability, as indicated by the surveys' Cronbach's alpha of 0.701. This result was not taken into account in the analysis of the questionnaires included in the main study.

The Likert‐type items of the questionnaires included in the main study showed adequate reliability thanks to the Cronbach's alpha result of 0.681.

Ethical Considerations

The “Declaration of Helsinki,” which established moral guidelines for human subjects, was followed in the present study. Further, since the present study involves minors, the Institutional Review Board at Jerash University granted the researchers an ethical authorization (no: 1/2/2024/2025) in order to adhere to the highest ethical guidelines. This authorization was first obtained for a previous more extensive correlational study that sought to investigate the complex effects of internet gaming on kids, as well as teenagers.

Several facts were fully explained to potential participants. These facts include the goal of the study, the fact that participation is entirely voluntary for both parents and students, and the fact that they can leave the study at any moment without facing any repercussions. After that, both the students and their parents gave their consent for the participation in the present study.

Further, in order to keep the data as confidential as possible, participants' information was made anonymous by removing any identifying information that could be related to them. In addition, access to the data was limited to the research team. This step was accomplished by keeping the data in encrypted digital forms during the study and analysis. The STROBE checklist (Von Elm et al. 2014) was used in reporting this research.

Statistical Analysis

The responses which were collected through Google forms were automatically merged into a spreadsheet which made data management and analysis easy. The data analysis was conducted using SPSS 24 (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 24.0). Descriptive analysis was done using mean and standard deviation (for continuous variables) and frequencies and percentages (for categorical variables).

Because the data were not normally distributed, Spearman correlation was used to examine the bivariate correlations among the variables of the study which were HBM modifying factors (e.g., age and gender), behavioral outcomes (gaming habits: daily hours spent playing, and type of online game usually played), perceived susceptibility (perceived physical and mental impacts of online gaming), and health perception (perceived physical and mental health).

The Mann–Whitney U Test was also used to see if perceived physical and mental health scores differed according to binary variables (e.g., participant's gender). Similarly, when comparing more than two groups (e.g., age classifications and types of online gaming), the Kruskal–Wallis H Test was employed.

Following the validation of the linear regression assumptions (linearity, homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity), the researchers built two models based on the conventional method. It is worth noting that the researchers employed the conventional method (and avoided the stepwise selection) in order to deliver assessment of predictors based on theories. This procedure assisted in studying the distinct effect of gaming behaviors while accounting for demographic characteristics.

As proposed by earlier research, these models can examine variables as a set of interrelated predictors. These predictors can simultaneously combine HBM modifying factors (age and gender), components of perceived susceptibility, and also triggers to action (Norman and Conner 2017). It is worth noting that the solidity of the results in the Middle Eastern setting is achieved by this rigorous, exploratory methodology. Further, listwise deletion technique was utilized to limit the analysis to only complete cases (volunteers who got guidance, n = 255).

Results

Participant Demographics and Habits of Online Gaming

The findings presented in Table 2 indicate that the sample of 403 respondents was almost equally divided by gender (53.6% male and 46.4% female). Also, the average time spent on gaming was 3.1 h per day per participant. Concerning the type of game usually played, fighting games were the most popular (32.3%), followed by football (22.3%). In terms of health perceptions based on HBM, a significant part of the participants considered online gaming not harmful to children's physical (28.8%) and mental (27.5%) health. This implies that there might be a lack of awareness regarding the potential health impacts of gaming (perceived susceptibility). A visual representation in Figure 1 indicates that 36.7% of the sample did not undergo counseling concerning the negative effects of online gaming. So, pointing out a major shortage in the provision of cues to action. The counseling on the negative effects of online gaming was mostly given by parents (66.7%), thus, parents were the primary source of cues to action in the family setting. The majority of the participants rated their physical (46.4%) and mental (43.4%) health as very good.

Spearman's Correlation

The results of bivariate correlation (N = 403) showed several significant relationships among demographic traits, gaming habits, perceptions of harm, and perceived physical and mental health as it is displayed in Table 3. Among the health outcomes and HBM constructs, perceived physical health was correlated positively with participants' gender (r = 0.166, p < 0.01). On the other hand, perceived physical health shows a strong negative correlation with participants' age (r = −0.240, p < 0.01), and the daily hours spent playing (r = −0.207, p < 0.05); and a moderate negative correlation with the belief that online games harm physical health (r = −0.114, p < 0.05). Likewise, perceived mental health showed a negative correlation with age (r = −0.215, p < 0.01), daily hours spent playing (r = −0.167, p < 0.01), and the belief that online games harm mental health (r = −0.236, p < 0.01). Additionally, there was a significant positive correlation (r = 0.510, p < 0.01) between both perceived physical and mental health.

Variations in Health Perceptions According to Participant Traits and Gaming Habits

Table 4 shows the results of the Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis H tests. The results include several significant differences in how participants rated their physical health based on their characteristics and habits of online gaming. For instance, the ratings of physical health significantly varied across participants' age groups (χ 2(4) = 25.64, p < 0.001). Those who rated their physical health as “Very weak” (Mean Rank = 290.5) and “Weak” (Mean Rank = 236.3) were mostly older participants. On the other hand, those who rated their physical health as “Very good” (Mean Rank = 172.1) were mostly younger participants. Besides age, the physical health ratings varied based on the beliefs about whether online gaming harms physical health (χ 2(4) = 10.48, p < 0.05). Those who rated their physical health as “Very weak” had a higher mean rank (202.4) when it came to agreeing that gaming is harmful. So, the more agreement that games are harmful to adolescents' health, the worse to rate their own health. This lines up with the HBM's idea of perceived susceptibility. Most importantly, statistically significant variations were observed in the physical health ratings across different online gaming habits, such as daily gaming hours (χ 2(4) = 19.48, p < 0.01). Participants who rated their physical health as “Very weak” (Mean Rank = 243.6) and “Weak” (Mean Rank = 250.9) have reported spending more hours of gaming per day, compared to those with “Good” (Mean Rank = 198.3) and “Very good” (Mean Rank = 181.7) physical health ratings.

In the same way, the Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis H tests indicated that there were substantial discrepancies in the mental health ratings of the participants according to their characteristics and online gaming practices. For instance, a significant difference was found in the perceived mental health between younger and older adolescents (χ 2(4) = 19.48, p < 0.01). That's it, those who perceived their mental health as “Very weak” (Mean Rank = 249.7) were mostly older adolescents. On the other hand, those who perceived their mental health as “Very good” (Mean Rank = 174.2) were mostly younger adolescents. Also, a statistically significant difference was found in the perceived mental health depending on the participant's belief that online games harm mental health (χ 2(4) = 22.73, p < 0.001). Participants who thought that their mental health is “Very weak” (Mean Rank = 206.3) showed a stronger agreement that online games harm mental health, again reflecting an interplay with perceived susceptibility. Additionally, daily gaming hours were significantly associated with perceived mental health (χ 2(4) = 20.13, p < 0.001). Higher daily gaming hours correlated with lower perceived mental health. Specifically, the “Very weak” (Mean Rank = 208.8) and “average” (Mean Rank = 205.5) mental health categories showing higher mean ranks compared to “Good” (Mean Rank = 189.9) mental health category.

Regression Analysis

As shown in Table 5, two multiple linear regression models were conducted to identify predictors of self‐rated physical and mental health. For physical health perceptions, the regression model was statistically significant with an F (8, 246) = 7.717 and p = 0.000. This proposes that the model explained a significant portion of the variance in the physical health ratings. The adjusted R 2 value of 0.175 suggests that about 17.5% of the variance in the physical health ratings can be justified by the predictors included in the model. Three variables significantly predicted physical health. First, age (β = −0.220, t = −3.636, p < 0.001, 95% CI [−0.100, −0.030]). Second, the type of online games played was significant (β = −0.179, t = −2.838, p < 0.01, 95% CI [−0.099, −0.018]). Finally, the number of hours spent playing online games daily showed the strongest association (β = −0.310, t = −5.174, p < 0.001, 95% CI [−0.162, −0.073]). These results might indicate that older age, certain types of online games, and increased daily gaming hours are related to lower physical health perceptions. On the other hand, the regression model of mental health perceptions was also statistically significant with an F(8, 246) = 4.494 and p = 0.000. However, the adjusted R 2 value of 0.099 indicates that the model explained about 9.9% of the variance in the mental health ratings. Significant predictors of the perceived mental health included age (β = −0.199, t = −3.141, p < 0.01, 95% CI [−0.113, −0.026]) and the number of hours spent playing online games daily (β = −0.176, t = −2.810, p < 0.01, 95% CI [−0.134, −0.023]). Additionally, the perception that online games are harmful to the mental health of children/adolescents was a significant predictor (β = −0.285, t = −3.926, p < 0.001, 95% CI [−1.122, −0.372]). These findings might indicate that older age, increased daily gaming hours, and the perception that online games harm mental health (reflecting perceived susceptibility) are related to mental health ratings.

Discussion

The study aimed to assess adolescents' online gaming habits, their self‐rated physical and mental health, the relationship among study variables, and predictors of their physical and mental health ratings. It was indicated by our findings that the average gaming time for the participants was 3.1 h per day which is in consideration of the fact that gaming is the major activity of adolescents. In alignment with previous studies (Lin et al. 2021; Tang et al. 2017), fighting and football games were the most favorite in this study.

From the perspective of HBM, a significant finding was that more than one‐quarter of participants stated that online gaming is not harmful to their physical or mental health. This indicates low perceived susceptibility for the risks associated with excessive online gaming. Also, more than one‐third of participants have always been in the dark about these harms and thus have never received any counseling about such harms. Concerning those who did, parents formed the common source of counseling. These interactions act as very important critical cues to action, thus demonstrating the top significance of parental education in the instillation of healthy behaviors. Notwithstanding diverse habits and counseling, the participants rated their health perceptions very highly, with almost half of the participants appraised their physical and mental health as “very good.”

The ratings of physical health were significantly higher in males than females. This might be secondary to cultural contexts where males are more active in sports or outdoor physical activities than females (Chaabane et al. 2021), or to a potential reporting bias regarding physical discomforts (Keogh 2022). On the other hand, the rating of physical health declined with age, likely due to older adolescents undergoing more academic stress, having less time for leisure activities and adopting poorer health habits (Zhu et al. 2021).

Concerning gaming habits, the negatively associated daily gaming hours with physical health ratings were manifested as sedentary lifestyle impact (Chan et al. 2022). Furthermore, the results showed that gamers reporting poor physical health were more likely to consider gaming harmful. This suggests that the personal experience of discomfort serves to strengthen the perceived susceptibility and severity of risk (Yildiz Durak et al. 2023), as the consequences move from abstract possibilities to lived realities.

The age, the amount of time spent playing each day, and the perception that playing online games is bad for one's mental health all showed a negative trend in the mental health ratings. Furthermore, perceptions of mental and physical health were found to be correlated significantly, as well as positively. These findings emphasize how these two aspects of health (mental and physical) are related (Buchan et al. 2021). It is worth noting that the previous studies on scholastic stress, isolation from other peers, and decreased physical activity mostly came in line with the reduction in mental health indicators with age and heavy gaming (Högberg 2021; Sikorska et al. 2021).

It should be mentioned that adolescents who believed that gaming was unhealthy generally displayed low ratings on the mental health surveys. This indicates a high responsiveness to the effects of gaming (Rikkers et al. 2016). It may also indicate that these adolescents who already struggle (due to online gaming) tend to blame themselves for their gaming habits (Hou et al. 2022).

These trends were reinforced by the Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis H tests, which revealed that people who thought that gaming is harmful or who spent the most time gaming had far lower health ratings. Further, multiple linear regression analyses supported the preceding results. Particularly the regression analyses revealed that age and gaming hours spent each day are reliably negative indicators of physical, as well as mental health.

Interestingly, physical health ratings were found to be strongly predicted by the kind of online games. This implies that the sorts of the online games have distinct effects on health due to their distinct cognitive and physical needs. In addition, the conceived negative effects of gaming continued to be a major predictor of mental health. This supports the HBM's premise that a key component of health self‐assessment involves cognitive beliefs about hazard.

Limitations

The current research came with some deficiencies. These deficiencies have to do with the study's design, methods, and collection of data, all of which could potentially impact the interpretation of HBM. First, it is difficult to demonstrate a clear causal link between gaming practices and health attitudes, because of the cross‐sectional approach. Second, the HBM framework was applied only to the constructs of perceived susceptibility and cues to action. Third, the reliance on single item self‐rated measures for perceived physical and mental health introduces a risk of common‐method bias and may lack the depth and reliability provided by multi‐item scales. Fourth, linear regression was used conducted on the 5‐point ordinal outcomes (perceived physical and mental health), which could have resulted in some bias. Lastly, the convenience sampling method limits the extent to which the findings can be generalized to other populations in Jordan or other Middle Eastern contexts.

Implications and Recommendations

The major implications of the study are targeted to the stakeholders that are focused on the health of young people in the digital era (Figure 2). The results, indeed, point to the necessity of targeted actions and educational measures that are capable of properly controlling the adverse online gaming effects on physical and mental health.

To be more specific, the study points to the necessity of the brief teacher‐ and nurse‐led health education sessions in schools and screening of students for susceptibility to adverse health consequences of online gaming. Such sessions should simply explain the health effects of excessive online gaming and the different types of games that can harm health. Also, the school nurses need to be assisted in their discussions about the balance of gaming habits with emphasis on sleep hygiene and physical activity. Nurses at schools can play a crucial role in identifying in‐danger teenagers. Nurses may also give teenagers the assistance they need to decrease the risk and make enhancements.

Parents may make use of the conclusions of the present research; particularly, these conclusions may serve as a resource for guidance regarding the potential negative impacts of online gaming. So, this means that there is a need for programs that aim at strengthening parents' digital literacy, as well as their communication skills, which will in turn enable them to efficiently control the amount and the type of games played by their kids. Parents should also be encouraged to not only engage their kids in discussions about the positive and negative aspects of online gaming but also to involve school nurses and other health professionals who could provide workshops or resources that teach parents how to set limits on their kids' gaming.

In terms of future research priority, longitudinal studies should be the first among studies that aim to prove causal relationships between gaming habits and both physical and mental health consequences over time. This methodology will be able to inform the medical field of the negative or positive effects of online gaming. Also, qualitative studies will be beneficial to explore the nuances of each different gaming type that lead to negative health outcomes. Moreover, it would be valuable to employ more objective measures of physical and mental health to complement self‐reported data with a more comprehensive understanding of the effects of online gaming. Besides, it is important to focus on the effectiveness of harm counseling on adolescent health and on how to enhance healthier gaming habits among adolescents in Jordan and other Middle Eastern contexts. Such studies would provide more understanding of how to bridge the gap between awareness and practice.

Conclusion

On the whole, the results of the present study show that the connection between teenagers' health and online gaming in Jordan is a complicated phenomenon; this phenomenon is influenced by a variety of environmental and cognitive variables. According to the HBM, many teenagers in Jordan are unaware of the detrimental effects of gaming. This indicates a low level of cognition about the impact susceptibility of online gaming. In other words, this implies that a large number of teenagers do not consider themselves to be in danger, which perhaps explains the long hours spent on gaming as reported in the present research.

Additionally, age, gender, and game type were found to be major factors that can shape teenagers' attitudes of physical, as well as mental health. Further, variables that may cause an unbalanced lifestyle are highlighted by the association between increased gaming hours and health scores.

Parents nowadays may act as the main counseling source for some adolescents. Nevertheless, more than one‐third of the participants reported a significant “counseling gap”; this gap can be a major field for intervention. Further, public health initiatives can assist teenagers by compensating for the “counseling gap”; these initiatives can be carried out by targeting specific educational programs. This, in turn, can encourage the development of better gaming practices, and can possibly improve health ratings.
