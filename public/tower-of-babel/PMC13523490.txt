“My story is good… It’s not like that for all”: Uneven access to contraception among incarcerated women in New South Wales, Australia
Authors: Helene Smith, Basil Donovan, Jocelyn Jones, Tony Butler, Paul Simpson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvidence indicates high levels of unmet contraceptive need among justice-involved women, linked to intersecting vulnerabilities such as housing instability, substance use, violence and poor mental health. In Australia, limited research has explored how women in prison navigate access to family planning services. This study aimed to examine the experiences of incarcerated women in New South Wales (NSW) in accessing contraception and making fertility-related decisions. MethodsThe study formed part of the Second Sexual Health and Attitudes of Australian Prisoners (SHAAP-2) study. Seventeen women aged 19–45 were recruited from two NSW correctional centres. Semi-structured qualitative interviews were conducted face-to-face or via video call, recorded, transcribed, and analysed thematically in NVivo14. Coding drew on the Behavioural Model for Vulnerable Populations, supplemented by inductive thematic analysis to capture cross-cutting themes. Content analysis was also applied to describe contraceptive needs.ResultsThree overarching findings were identified. First, women’s access to contraception was less shaped by mistrust of providers than by system unreliability. While some reported supportive care, many described unanswered requests, long delays, and unsafe practices such as attempting implant removals. Second, incarceration prompted reflection and deliberate fertility planning. Women re-evaluated what it meant to be “ready” for motherhood, some seeking to delay pregnancy until achieving stability, others eager to conceive quickly after release, due to a sense of lost time. Third, women’s choices were constrained by side-effects and limited method options. Repeated failures with pills, injections, or devices sometimes led women to sterilisation as the “only certain” option, though some later regretted this decision.ConclusionsPrisons represent a critical but underutilised setting for meeting reproductive health needs. Despite policy commitments to equivalence of care, contraceptive services in custody remain neglected. Findings highlight the need for stronger structural supports including timely service pathways and effective pre-release planning, to ensure women can access safe, flexible, and non-coercive contraception. Addressing these gaps is essential to upholding reproductive rights and reducing health inequities for incarcerated women.

Background

Globally, the rate of female incarceration has increased more than that of males, by 60% compared to 22% between 2000 and 2022 [1]. While global data on the proportion of incarcerated women of reproductive age is lacking, estimates from countries like the United States (US) suggest that around 75% of incarcerated women fall into this category [2]. In Australia, a recent inmate health survey reported that 93.2% of women entering prison were offered a pregnancy test, suggesting that most women fall into the child-bearing age category [3]. This growing population strengthens the case for gender-responsive healthcare in custody [4, 5], recognising women’s distinct health needs and importantly, ensuring access to comprehensive sexual and reproductive health services including contraception for all individuals with the capacity for pregnancy, including some trans men and non-binary people.

In Australia, the question of how to provide these services in the carceral context is shaped by a distinctive service and financing context compared to what is available in the wider community. Medicare is Australia’s universal health insurance scheme in the community, supporting access to general practice and contraceptive care services such as contraceptive device insertions and removals at low or no cost. It is complimented by the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) which subsidises the cost of many prescription medicines and devices (including contraceptives), reducing out-of-pocket costs for eligible people. Both are funded and administered by the federal government [6] However, during incarceration, health care is funded and organised separately by state and territory governments through different jurisdictional governance arrangements rather than a single national model, and access to Medicare and PBS is therefore generally suspended [7]. In New South Wales (NSW), Australia’s most populous state, health care for people in custody is delivered by the NSW Health Department through the Justice Health and Forensic Mental Health Network, creating a service environment that is organisationally and operationally distinct from community primary care [8].

In NSW, primary care services within correctional facilities are typically delivered by general practitioners and nurses employed through Justice Health. Oral contraceptives and injectable methods are theoretically all available on-site, with access to external referrals if recommended by a health care practitioner. Access to these services however is dependent upon access to trained clinical staff or referral to external hospital or specialist services under escort [9]. In addition, although basic health care is generally provided at no cost to people in NSW custody, the lack of PBS coverage means medicines and devices are funded through state/territory prison health budgets; as a result, medications provided in custody may differ from community prescribing, and the range of available contraceptive pills and devices (and access to procedural methods) may vary by jurisdiction and facility [9]. This means that women in custody must rely on prison health services, rather than the usual community Medicare/PBS pathways, for contraceptive care; in practice, this can shape which methods and procedures are available, how women access them, and how quickly care can be provided.

Studies in the US and Canada have suggested that justice-involved women have a high level of demand for contraceptive services when they are in the community and also in preparation for release [10–12], coupled with high rates of unmet need [13–15]. In Australia, although unmet need for contraception has not been directly measured in correctional settings (i.e. comparing contraceptive use/access with preferences to avoid pregnancy), surveys of incarcerated women indicate that only 33% of women reported using any form of modern contraception prior to incarceration [16]. Whilst these figures do not take into account pregnancy intention, they are significantly lower than the 81% of women in the community who reported contraception use at their last sexual encounter [17]. In Australia, incarcerated women also report becoming pregnant at a younger age, typically between 18 and 20 years old [9, 16, 18–20], compared to an average age of 29 years among women in the general population [21]. They also experience significantly higher rates of pregnancy termination, ranging from 43% to 47% [18, 19, 22, 23] compared to 16% in the general population [24]. Furthermore, one Australian study which examined post-release dispensing of medication, found that after six months, only 8% of women had accessed either contraceptive pills, or long-acting reversible contraception (LARC), with particularly low uptake amongst Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander women [25].

This suggests that women who experience incarceration report different patterns of contraceptive use and pregnancy experiences than women in the general population. These patterns could reflect constrained access to preferred methods and discontinuities in care, but they could also reflect different or changing fertility intentions in contexts shaped by instability, violence, substance use, and disrupted partnerships. Pregnancy intentions are also not neatly binary concepts and may shift with life circumstances, consequently, applying general-population fertility norms to marginalised groups risks misinterpretation. In addition, self-reported reproductive goals and pregnancy intentions may be shaped by stigma, fear of judgement, and social desirability pressures, concerns that can be amplified in carceral contexts, potentially contributing to under-reporting or reframing of both contraceptive non-use and pregnancy outcomes [26]. Furthermore, higher reported pregnancy termination may indicate barriers to timely, acceptable contraception for some women, but may also reflect abortion as a patient-controlled means of fertility management when other methods are inaccessible, unacceptable, or not desired. This ambiguity underscores the need to examine not only “intention” but also the conditions under which women feel able to disclose intentions and preferences, and how measurement may be distorted by stigma surrounding abortion, substance use, and ‘good motherhood’ norms [27].

Accordingly, an important evidence gap in the Australian correctional context is whether, when, and how women’s reproductive goals, including the desire to avoid pregnancy, to conceive, or to parent, are supported through access to acceptable, voluntary, and non-coercive contraceptive care and continuity across custody and release. It is also important to understand whether contraceptive needs and desired care pathways differ for women with trauma histories or mental ill-health, given evidence (from non-carceral settings) that trauma and mental illness can shape contraceptive preferences, pregnancy intentions, and interactions with SRH services [28].

Without Australian qualitative evidence on how women actually seek, access, and experience contraceptive care in custody, it is difficult to know whether current policies are functioning as intended, where access breaks down, and what reforms are needed to support equitable and non-coercive care.

Incarcerated women frequently represent highly vulnerable populations who face intersecting challenges. In Australia, prison census data indicates incarcerated women consist of an overrepresentation of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women [29], and women experiencing mental illness [9], histories of trauma [16], homelessness [9], and substance use [9]. Several intersecting factors, particularly sexual coercion/violence and socioeconomic disadvantage, as well as housing instability and substance use, have been associated with unintended pregnancy in community samples [30–33]. Furthermore, women in Australian prisons are typically detained for relatively short sentences. For example, in NSW, the average duration of incarceration for women is less than a year, regardless of whether they are on remand or sentenced [34]. This makes it especially important to consider reproductive health care for incarcerated women, including pregnancy prevention and planning, as they prepare for reintegration—where they may encounter psychological, social, and structural barriers to accessing primary care [9, 10, 35–38].

However, while incarceration may provide a point of contact for people who have been underserved or disengage in health care in the community [39], the coercive and restrictive nature of imprisonment raises significant concerns about whether reproductive healthcare can be delivered in ways consistent with reproductive justice principles. Reproductive justice extends beyond access to services or formal legal rights alone, and emphasises the right to bodily autonomy, to decide freely whether and when to have children, and to parent in safe and supportive conditions. In this context, this includes issues related to bodily autonomy, voluntary and informed decision-making, freedom from coercion, and continuity of care during and after custody [4, 5, 40, 41]. This tension is illustrated in a mixed-methods study from the US, which found that women who had contact with the criminal legal system more commonly reported receiving contraception counselling as well as having undergone sterilisation procedures, they were also more likely to later express a desire to have those procedures reversed. Furthermore, qualitative interviews emphasised that counselling encounters were often shaped by judgment, shame, and in some cases coercion to limit fertility from actors inside and outside the criminal legal system [4]. These findings underscore that increased service contact can coexist with constrained agency, reinforcing the need for explicitly non-coercive, rights-based contraceptive care and safeguards against reproductive control in carceral environments.

Furthermore, in many prison health systems, including Australia, women access family planning services through parallel health systems that are funded separately from general population health care mechanisms [42–44]. According to Justice Health policies, all broad sexual and reproductive health needs should be met during incarceration, including menopause care, pap smears and access to abortion services [45]. Menopause management and termination of pregnancy are therefore available in principle within custodial health care, and both warrant dedicated examination beyond the scope of this paper. These systems are frequently reported to be under-funded, under-staffed and lacking in specialised services for women, partly due to their smaller numbers compared to the male prison population [46–48].

International qualitative studies, predominantly from North America, with a smaller number from other settings, describe strong demand for contraception amongst incarcerated women, alongside concerns about method choice and procedural quality (e.g. LARC insertion/removal), coercion, and continuity after release [49–52]. However, no comparable qualitative studies have been conducted in Australia. This is an important gap because the Australian prison context differs in several ways that are likely to shape contraceptive access pathways and experiences of care. These include variation in prison healthcare governance across states and territories, different models of health service delivery within correctional settings, and a distinct population profile, including the over-representation of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women and women from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds. Furthermore, previous research has not examined the specific processes by which incarcerated women access contraception, including how they request services, navigate institutional gatekeeping, receive care, and articulate their contraceptive preferences. As a result, overseas evidence cannot simply be assumed to apply to Australian prisons, and Australia-specific qualitative evidence is needed to identify whether similar barriers operate here, how they are mediated by local service arrangements, and what responses are appropriate in this setting.

This study sought to address that gap by describing the individual experiences of 17 incarcerated women in relation to contraception access, either in the community or during incarceration in New South Wales, Australia, in order to generate context-specific evidence on how contraceptive care is navigated in custody and where barriers to equitable, voluntary, and timely access arise.

Methods

Study aim & design

This study was conducted as part of a PhD thesis, whose overall aim was to explore whether young and adult justice-involved women have an unmet need for reproductive health services and examine associated enabler and barriers to access. The specific aim for this component of the study was to describe the experiences of women in prison seeking out and accessing contraception. Qualitative data was also gathered within a broader population-based study exploring the sexual health, behaviours, and attitudes of adult prisoners in NSW, the Second Sexual Health & Attitudes of Australian Prisoners (SHAAP-2) study. The SHAAP-2 study combined a population-based survey with in-depth qualitative interviews conducted with a purposefully selected group of participants. For the broader SHAAP-2 survey, women were randomly selected from participating prisons using lists provided by corrective services, and the research team rather than local prison staff conducted the initial recruitment. The qualitative component drew on women who completed the survey, expressed interest in follow-up contact, and were then purposefully selected to ensure diversity in age, contraception history, and future fertility intentions. The findings from the quantitative component of the study have yet to be published.

The purpose of the analysis for this specific paper was to explore the factors that either support or hinder access to reproductive health care, including contraception services, both during imprisonment and in the community prior to incarceration. Data collection took place between 2024 and 2025.

Study setting & description of current contraception policies

In December 2024 NSW, Australia’s most populous state, had 865 adult women held in custody [34]. Of these, 33% identified as Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander, with the average age of the female prison population reported as 36.9 years. Just over half (54.4%) of the women were on remand, while the remaining 45.6% were serving custodial sentences [34].

In Australia, heallth care in prisons is funded by individual states and is not part of the universal Medicare system available in the community. In NSW, this responsibility falls under the NSW Ministry of Health, which delivers services through Justice Health NSW. Justice Health NSW policy mandates that women in custody must have access to health care services equivalent to those available in the community, including reproductive health and contraception services, whether it be to support pregnancy prevention or menstrual / hormonal management needs, as well as other reproductive health needs such as access to menopause care management, pap-smears and/or abortion services [53].

Participants in this study were recruited from two state-run correctional centres in NSW that house women. Each facility accommodated a combination of sentenced and remanded individuals under various security levels—minimum, medium, and maximum.

Participant recruitment

For participation in the SHAAP-2 survey, women were randomly selected from selected prisons using lists provided by corrective services. The sampling and initial recruitment process was conducted by the research team rather than local correctional centre staff, so that prison staff were not involved in deciding who was approached for participation. Complete privacy regarding recruitment was not possible within the prison environment, because correctional staff needed to facilitate access to selected participants. However, consent procedures and interviews were conducted privately where feasible, staff were not present during interviews, participation was voluntary, and no identifiable information or individual responses were shared with corrective services.

Local staff’s role was limited to practical facilitation of access to selected participants within the prison environment. Selected women were then invited to meet with the research team, and, if interested, complete the survey. After completing the SHAAP-2 survey, women were asked if they would like to take part in a follow-up qualitative interview. Following the completion of survey data collection, the research team reviewed survey data from women who expressed interest in follow-up contact to identify those eligible for qualitative interview participation. Eligibility criteria included being aged between 18 and 49 years, as well as assignment of female or intersex at birth. Among eligible women, participants were purposefully selected to ensure diversity in contraception history, future fertility intentions, and age.

The inclusion of women up to age 49 years was intentional, aiming to ensure that the perspectives of older women were represented. This approach reflects demographic changes in Australia, where the rate of births among women in their 40s has nearly doubled over the past ten years [34]. Including this group also allowed participants to reflect on their past experiences with contraception needs and ability to access services.

Data collection

A semi-structured interview guide was created to explore participants’ experiences in seeking or accessing reproductive health care, both while incarcerated and in the community. Topics included services related to family planning and contraception, use of general health services, and their aspirations or intentions for life after release, including future plans regarding children and relationships. Although not the focus of this analysis, participants were also asked about their relationships while in custody, access to sexually transmissible infection testing and exposure to sexual and physical violence, among other things.

Most interviews lasted no longer than an hour. They were conducted either face-to-face at the correctional facility (N = 10) or remotely via video conferencing (N = 7). Where interviews were conducted face-to-face, a private room was arranged, with the door kept open. A guard was on duty nearby but out of hearing range of the interviews. Calls conducted via video conferencing were conducted in a private video conference cubical, where no-one else could hear the conversation. At the end of the interview, participants were asked to reflect on whether anything had been raised for them that had caused them distress, and if so, if they would like to be connected to support networks. Any requests for additional support were then sent to the designated Registered Nurse at the facility to follow-up and arrange for the participant.

Data analysis

All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed, and imported into NVivo14 for analysis. A coding framework, guided by the Behavioural Model for Vulnerable Populations [54], was developed to examine overall access to health care, including family planning and fertility services. The lead author (HS) conducted an initial round of coding independently, with a focus on the multiple layers influencing access to different aspects of reproductive health care. This included analysis of demographic and social characteristics of incarcerated women, their specific health needs, and broader structural enablers and barriers to care. Codes were initially analysed within the context of the framework, followed by the identification of cross-cutting themes beyond the set structure of the framework, using both deductive and inductive thematic analysis methods [55]. A content analysis was also carried out to summarise participants’ contraceptive needs.

After the preliminary analysis, two co-authors (PS and BD) reviewed the identified themes and supporting quotes, providing critical feedback on the draft findings. Each member of the research team reflected on their own potential biases during the interpretive process to ensure a balanced and nuanced final analysis.

Over three-quarters (76%) stated that they did not want to be become pregnant when they were released. Of the 17 women, 12 (71%) expressed a need for access to family planning services, either for general advice around contraception options, side-effect support with current contraception, removal of an IUD or implant, or support in having a tubal ligation reversed.

Summary of findings

Three over-arching themes were identified that reflected participants’ contraceptive needs and the challenges they faced in accessing appropriate services. These themes reflected that women experience (1) structural barriers to accessing contraception before, during, and after incarceration; (2) a strong sense of hope and intentionality about having children in the future; and (3) difficulties in finding a contraceptive method that works for them. As a result, some women felt that the closed nature of the prison environment could, in practical terms, make accessing contraception feel more straightforward than in the community. This was because women did not need to organise transport, find a bulk-billing provider, take time off work, remember appointments, or manage the instability in their lives that had previously made contraceptive access difficult. Rather than needing to seek services out themselves, some perceived that services could come to them. In this sense, prison was described less as an ideal setting and more as a context in which some practical barriers to care were temporarily reduced.

Uneven access

Women from both prison locations described mixed experiences of trying to access contraception during incarceration. Some had commenced contraception or had devices removed while in prison, including IUDs and implants. In some cases, this followed discussion with a healthcare provider at intake or occurred in preparation for release for women serving longer sentences; in others, women had simply made a request. For those who were able to access care, experiences were generally described very positively.

In this sense, contraceptive access was experienced not as a systematically guaranteed health service, but as something unevenly mediated through staff responsiveness, interpersonal rapport, and institutional discretion. Many women emphasised that this pattern was not unique to family planning but reflected broader difficulties in accessing prison primary healthcare more generally. For some, this perception discouraged them from seeking contraception at all while incarcerated.

Uncertainty around release

Many of the women interviewed wanted to be able to arrange contraception care before they left prison. However, many felt that pre-release planning could be challenging due to several factors. Firstly, many women were uncertain as to when they would be released either because they were on remand or whilst they had been sentenced for some offences, they had other charges pending. For others the exact timing of the potential for parole was constantly shifting.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first qualitative study to provide detailed account of how women in Australian navigate access to contraception whilst incarcerated. Our analysis found that contraceptive care for women in Australian prisons is constrained less by mistrust of providers than by system unreliability. Despite this, women demonstrated active, intentional fertility planning whilst in custody.

Our findings align with past reporting on access to contraception in carceral settings globally in three respects. Firstly, in the description of structural barriers including unreliable access pathways [43, 56, 57] and weak pre-release planning procedures [43, 58, 59]. Secondly, participants’ accounts of pre-incarceration instability, marked by cycles of deprivation, trauma and addiction, mirror findings from justice-involved women both in Australia [38, 60, 61] and North America [51, 62, 63], outlining similar pathways through which these circumstances restrict opportunities to engage with health systems including access to contraceptive care. Thirdly, our study echoed reports from the global literature of incarcerated women describing prison as a period of reflection and intentional reproductive decision-making with women in custody exhibiting strong reproductive agency and making deliberate fertility choices shaped by both constraints and opportunities in prison [43].

Participants also reported comparable experiences in relation to challenges with LARC removals [43, 57, 64]. Whilst some women reported obtaining removals of IUDs and implants on request, others described prolonged unanswered requests and reported unsafe removal practices, including non-clinical cutting out of implants. Participants linked their inability to access LARC removals to the low prioritisation of such services and to the inconsistent access that characterises all health care within the carceral system. While this explanation may be accurate on the surface, it may also reflect patterns described in global literature, which highlight how paternalistic approaches shape the institutional treatment of incarcerated women [65]. These approaches, underpinned by assumptions among health care providers about women’s poor decision-making capacity, can manifest in subtle delays and de-prioritisation when LARC removals are requested [43, 51].

The central barriers observed here are described as not only logistical, but also governed by discretionary gatekeeping and everyday interactions, with women describing access to contraceptive care as arbitrary, and accounts suggesting subtle micro-aggressions and everyday resistance when women request device removal, producing de facto control over continuation rather than patient choice. These findings suggest that improving contraceptive care in custody requires clear, timely, and patient-led access pathways for both initiation and removal, rather than systems dependent on staff discretion or repeated requests. Evidence, primarily from the US, supports the value of providing contraception in custody rather than relying on community referral after release [10]. It also points to specific service design features that may help: separation of counselling from procedure to reduce coercion risk, integration of contraception within broader women’s health care, and reliable follow-up for side-effects or removal requests. For example, the San Francisco County Jail Women’s Health Specialty Clinic embedded contraception within a broader women’s health service, with routine access to women’s health assessment, on-site LARC provision, and documented follow-up capacity for LARC-related concerns including removal [66]. A Rhode Island prerelease programme found that contraceptive initiation increased markedly when services were offered in custody rather than only after release [10, 49]. More broadly, national US data suggest that only a small number of systems, such as Cook County Jail, Rhode Island Department of Corrections, and Rikers Island, have formalised in-custody contraceptive provision through federally funded partnerships [67]. This suggests that further work is needed This suggests that further work is needed not only to identify successful models, but to adapt models that prioritise timely access, removal on request, and reduced gatekeeping in custodial settings.

Our study also identified a notable point of contrast with the Australian setting. While several studies in North America have reported fear or distrust of family planning providers within correctional systems [49, 50, 56, 57], such sentiments were not reported by our participants. Instead, women who had been able to access services described professional and considerate care, with frustrations centred on limited and inconsistent availability. This suggests that, in Australian prisons, improving service availability and reliability may yield greater benefit than interventions focused on patient trust however more studies may be needed to determine how generalisable this finding is.

In addition, our findings contribute new information in two main domains. First, we corroborate prior research that incarceration can be a period of reflection in which women make deliberate fertility decisions, either delaying pregnancy until life circumstances improve or seeking to conceive soon after release in response to perceived loss of time [43]. We extend this work by linking intentions to explicit method preferences, showing that many women favour flexible, non-permanent options that align with their desired timelines, and by illustrating how incarceration prompts an active re-evaluation of what it means to be ready for becoming a mother, beyond questions of timing alone.

Second, we advance understanding of how contraceptive side-effects, acceptability, and constrained method choice interact in custody. Prior studies note heterogeneity in satisfaction with LARCs, with some women discontinuing due to intolerable side-effects, mistrust, or concerns about invasiveness, and shifting to less effective methods [43, 57, 68]. Our data adds detailed accounts of navigating successive method failures, balancing perceived “naturalness,” invasiveness, and side-effect burden against fertility goals. Repeated failure sometimes reframed sterilisation as the “only sure” option; yet even definitive choices were reconsidered as circumstances and relationships changed. These trajectories illustrate resilience amid constraint, while structural barriers to LARC removal, low prioritisation, and limited method availability compound personal health considerations.

Overseas implementation examples, predominantly from the US illustrate feasible ways to address the structural barriers identified here. Successful initiatives include strengthening pre-release referral opportunities [10], In East Tennessee (USA), a public health department partnership across 15 county jails delivered in-jail education and voluntary access to highly effective methods, with documented reach and estimated unintended pregnancies averted [69]. In San Francisco (USA), an on-site women’s health service embedded within jail health care provided IUD/implant insertion using a two-visit protocol explicitly designed to mitigate coercion concerns, demonstrating feasibility and safety signals in routine practice [66]. While evaluated case studies remain limited outside the US, these examples offer practical, transferable components, rapid access pathways, on-site provision where possible, strong consent safeguards, and deliberate linkage to community services, that directly map onto the barriers described by participants [43].

Taken together, the findings underscore the need for contraceptive services in carceral health systems that are accessible, non-coercive, and flexible, including reliable pathways for method switching and timely LARC removal. These findings can also be read as a decarceration and health-equity signal. Many participants described significant barriers to contraceptive access in the community, suggesting unmet need and uneven access predates custody, like other unmet health needs is exacerbated by social disadvantage [9, 60]. From this perspective, improving contraceptive care in custody is necessary but not sufficient: reducing churn through short and uncertain incarceration and strengthening accessible, community-based contraceptive pathways, particularly for women facing compounded socioeconomic barriers, may be equally important for improving continuity and autonomy.

We also acknowledge several limitations. Firstly, although thematic saturation appeared to be reached, our sample of 17 participants was drawn from only two NSW prisons, which may limit transferability across facility types and to other Australian jurisdictions. Secondly, recruitment was purposive from the SHAAP-2 survey and, alongside low survey consent in some facilities, may have introduced selection bias towards women more comfortable discussing sensitive topics related to sexual activity. In addition, several women who had consented to participate in an interview had been released or transferred by the time the interviews took place, this may have excluded the views of women serving shorter sentences. As a result, the views presented here may not be fully reflective of the prison’s sampled. Finally, despite efforts to prioritise Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women, they were under-represented relative to their proportion in custody (12% vs. 33%), constraining meaningful subgroup analysis and suggesting the need for more culturally safe, co-designed recruitment strategies.

However, we believe these limitations do not materially undermine the contribution of the study as the narratives presented provide rich, contextually grounded accounts of service access, and the patterns we identify are consistent with, and add nuance to, emerging evidence in comparable settings.

Conclusion

This study highlights the importance of comprehensive and person-centred contraceptive care which addresses the full reproductive continuum, from counselling and pregnancy intention assessment to method initiation, continuation, switching, and timely LARC removal. Priorities include reliable access to services; robust pre-release planning for all incarcerated women including women on short-sentences or remandees, adequate workforce resourcing and governance that curbs arbitrary decisions; and routine monitoring of wait times and outcomes on requests. Alongside strengthening comprehensive, non-coercive contraceptive care in custody, efforts to reduce avoidable incarceration and improve equitable access to community-based contraception are likely critical to addressing the upstream constraints described by participants.
