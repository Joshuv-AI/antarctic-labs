Disagreement-Regularized Importance Sampling for Adversarial Label Corruption
Csongor Horváth , &Ida-Maria Sintorn &Prashant Singh

Abstract
Standard Importance Sampling (IS) collapses under label corruption because high-norm examples — prioritized for variance reduction — are often adversarial outliers. We formalize this misalignment using an ε \varepsilon -contamination model and propose Disagreement-Regularized Importance Sampling ( DR-IS ), a sub-sampling method based on loss rank-disagreement across independent proxy ensemble. We prove finite-sample concentration bounds showing that the empirical rank disagreement of bulk corrupted examples is bounded above, and that of boundary-clean examples bounded below, both at rate O ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( N / δ ) / K ) O(\sqrt{\log(N/\delta)/K}) with probability 1 − δ 1-\delta ; when the structural expectation gap Δ ′ \Delta^{\prime} between the two groups is positive and the boundary-clean set is at least as large as the selected subset, these bounds certify strict separation and control the contamination rate of the selected subset. Empirically, DR-IS remains robust under targeted high-norm attacks that break magnitude-based methods such as the Error L 2 L_{2} -norm (EL2N) on benchmark datasets. DR-IS complements training-dynamics approaches like Area Under the Margin ranking (AUM), offering improved robustness in the loss-aligned regime alongside explicit finite-sample concentration certificates and a contamination bound limiting noise leakage from the statistical tail of corrupted points.

Introduction

While Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) is ubiquitous, uniform mini-batch sampling ignores varying example informativeness. Sampling proportional to gradient norms minimizes estimator variance
(
Strohmer and Vershynin 2009
;
Zhao and Zhang 2015
;
Needell et al. 2016
;
Csiba and Richtárik 2018
)
, a principle adapted in deep learning via efficient surrogates: distributed gradient-norm estimation
(
Alain et al. 2015
)
, last-layer error norms or loss
(
Katharopoulos and Fleuret 2018
;
Jiang et al. 2019
)
, pre-trained proxies
(
Coleman et al. 2020
)
, and robust upper bounds
(
Johnson and Guestrin 2018
)
. We identify a critical failure in such
magnitude-based
rules, including online batch selection
(
Loshchilov and Hutter 2016
)
and prioritized experience replay
(
Schaul et al. 2016
)
. These methods assume high gradient norms signify informativeness; however, under
\varepsilon
-contamination, corrupted examples often yield the highest norms. This causes importance sampling (IS) to over-allocate mass to noise, which over-parametrized networks then memorize
(
Zhang et al. 2017
)
. We instead propose a robust pruning method that leverages ensemble disagreement to avoid these high-loss outliers.

2
Background: The Failure Mode of Importance Sampling

To understand why data pruning techniques fail under noise, we first examine the foundations of optimal importance sampling and identify which step breaks down under contamination.

Empirical Risk Minimization and Optimal IS

Consider a training set
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
and loss
f_{i}(w)=\ell(h_{w}(x_{i}),y_{i})
. Empirical risk minimization (ERM) targets
w^{\star}\in\arg\min_{w}\widehat{R}(w)
, where
\widehat{R}:=\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i}f_{i}
. At step
t
, an importance-weighted gradient estimator
g_{t}=(Np_{i_{t}}^{t})^{-1}\,\nabla f_{i_{t}}(w_{t})
is constructed using sampling distribution
p^{t}
. For any
p_{i}^{t}>0
,
g_{t}
is an unbiased estimator of
\nabla\widehat{R}(w_{t})

(
Zhao and Zhang 2015
)
. Minimizing the estimator variance — equivalent to minimizing the second moment
\mathbb{E}[\|g_{t}\|^{2}]
— yields the optimal distribution proportional to gradient norms
(
Needell et al. 2016
;
Zhao and Zhang 2015
;
Katharopoulos and Fleuret 2018
)
:

p^{t,\star}_{i}\;=\;\frac{\|\nabla f_{i}(w_{t})\|}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|\nabla f_{j}(w_{t})\|}.

(1)

Empirical vs. true variance reduction

While (
1
) minimizes the variance of the
empirical
gradient, its utility relies on the assumption that
\widehat{R}
closely tracks the true risk
R
. In clean settings, prioritizing high-norm samples effectively reduces variance for both. However, under
\varepsilon
-contamination
(
Huber 1992
;
Diakonikolas and Kane 2019
)
, this equivalence collapses. Corrupted examples
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}
typically exhibit the largest gradient norms, causing (
1
) to over-allocate mass to harmful noise. This accelerates the memorization of outliers in over-parameterized models
(
Zhang et al. 2017
)
. Consequently, minimizing empirical variance becomes a "wrong objective" when the empirical distribution diverges from the true one — a failure mode we verify in
Section

6.2
.

3
Related Work

IS vulnerability necessitates balancing convergence speed with robustness. We contrast our disagreement-based approach with existing pruning and noise-robustness frameworks.

Data Pruning and Curricula

Most data pruning methods rely on surrogates of the gradient-norm rule (
1
). EL2N and GraNd
(
Paul et al. 2021
)
score samples by mean error across initializations, while others utilize forgetting events
(
Toneva et al. 2019
)
, gradient-cover coresets
(
Mirzasoleiman et al. 2020
;
Killamsetty et al. 2021
)
, or holdout-loss baselines
(
Mindermann et al. 2022
, RHO-LOSS,)
. However, magnitude-based pruning typically collapses under targeted corruption (
Sections

6.2
and
F
).
DR-IS
inverts this logic: where EL2N averages magnitude scores, we leverage their variance to capture a robustness signal that the mean discards.

Mislabeled Data Identification

Instead of relying on loss magnitude, addressing label noise requires identifying the corrupted data directly. Methods like co-teaching
(
Han et al. 2018
)
, DivideMix
(
Li et al. 2020
)
, and Early-Learning Regularization
(
Liu et al. 2020
)
modify the learning process without explicit sample weighting. Closer to our work, AUM
(
Pleiss et al. 2020
)
and Dataset Cartography
(
Swayamdipta et al. 2020
)
employ training-dynamics statistics (margin trajectories and prediction variability) to flag mislabelled samples.
DR-IS
and AUM are complementary: AUM exploits
temporal
fluctuations within a single proxy, whereas
DR-IS
exploits
cross-sectional
disagreement across independent proxies. We treat AUM as representative of this family, as our structural arguments regarding sub-optimality (
Theorem

2
) and noise recovery (
Table

2
) likely extend to related training-dynamics scores.

Ensemble Disagreement

Using model disagreement as a signal is well-established in active learning via Query by Committee
(
Seung et al. 1992
)
, Bayesian active learning
(
Gal et al. 2017
)
, and deep ensemble
(
Lakshminarayanan et al. 2017
;
Kendall and Gal 2017
)
.
DR-IS
adapts this paradigm to the SGD-IS setting. Instead of selecting examples for labeling, we identify which already-labeled examples provide safe, informative gradients, building a connection between disagreement and noise filtration formalized in the high-quantile separation of
Theorem

1
.

4
Theoretical Framework

Contamination model

We adopt an
\varepsilon
-contamination model in the spirit of

(
Huber 1992
)
, adapted to the empirical setting where the contamination acts on training labels.

Assumption 1

(
\varepsilon
-contaminated training set)
.

The training set is partitioned
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{clean}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}
with
|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}|=\varepsilon N\in\mathbb{Z}
for
\varepsilon\in[0,1/2)
.
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{clean}}\overset{i.i.d}{\sim}P_{\mathrm{clean}}
are clean data samples, while
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}\sim P_{\mathrm{corr}}
are arbitrary corrupted samples, which may depend only on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{clean}}
but not the proxy ensemble.

Proxy ensemble and rank disagreement

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the distribution over hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
induced by the proxy training procedure (randomizing seeds, initializations, data order, and possibly architecture). We draw an i.i.d. ensemble
h_{1},\dots,h_{K}\sim\mathcal{P}
. For each
h\in\mathcal{H}
, let
\rho_{i}(h)\in(0,1]
be the normalized loss rank of example
i
. The
empirical rank disagreement
is the sample variance of these ranks:

\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bigl(\rho_{i}(h_{k})-\overline{\rho}_{i}\bigr)^{2},\quad\text{where }\overline{\rho}_{i}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\rho_{i}(h_{k}).

(2)

Structural assumptions

Informative disagreement relies on the relative stability of corrupted points versus the instability of clean boundary points.

Assumption 2

(Quantile concentration of corrupted ranks)
.

There exist
\tau,\gamma\in(0,1)
and
\alpha_{\text{trim}}\in[0,1)
such that for the bulk
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{(1-\alpha_{\text{trim}})}
(the
1-\alpha_{\text{trim}}
least rank-variant samples in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}
):

\mathbb{P}_{h\sim\mathcal{P}}(\rho_{i}(h)\geq 1-\tau)\geq 1-\gamma\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{(1-\alpha_{\text{trim}})}.

(3)

Assumption 3

(Boundary disagreement)
.

There exists a subset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bdry}}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{clean}}
and

\tau_{\mathrm{bdry}}>0
such that

\operatorname{Var}_{h\sim P}\!\bigl[\rho_{j}(h)\bigr]\;\geq\;\tau_{\mathrm{bdry}}^{2}
for every
j\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bdry}}
.

Assumption 4

(Average tail variance)
.

The average population variance across the escaping corrupted tail
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\text{tail}}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{(1-\alpha_{\text{trim}})}
is bounded:
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\text{tail}}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\text{tail}}}\text{Var}_{h\sim\mathcal{P}}[\rho_{i}(h)]\leq v_{\text{tail}}
.

The dependence restriction in
1
is satisfied by all label-flipping schemes that are functions of the dataset alone, including uniform random flips and the targeted high-norm flips we use in our experiments.

2
leverages the "simplicity bias" of SGD: proxies prioritize dominant features over contradictory corrupted labels, keeping corrupted loss ranks consistently high across
\mathcal{P}

(
Pleiss et al. 2020
, like AUM,)
. Conversely,
3
targets clean points near the decision boundary whose marginal status makes them highly sensitive to stochasticity in the training pipeline. For these "hard" examples, minor shifts in initialization or mini-batch ordering cause the model to oscillate between correct and incorrect predictions, inducing significant fluctuations in loss rank across the ensemble. This sensitivity distinguishes boundary data from both "easy" clean samples (consistently low loss) and corrupted samples (consistently high loss), producing the distinct bimodal disagreement signal observed in
Figure

1
. Finally,
4
parameterizes the empirical reality that the tail’s rank-variance decays continuously rather than adopting worst-case theoretical limits.

4.1
Separation by disagreement

Theorem 1

(High-Quantile Separation)
.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
. Under Assumptions
1
–
4
, with probability
\geq 1-\delta
, the following hold:

(i)

Bulk Corrupted:
For all
i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{(1-\alpha_{\text{trim}})}
, we have
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}\leq\theta^{*}
, where

\theta^{*}:=\Bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{K}\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{\tau^{2}}{4}+\gamma\Bigr)+\sqrt{\tfrac{\log(2N/\delta)}{2K}}.

(4)

(ii)

Boundary Clean:
For all
j\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bdry}}
, it holds that

\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{j}\;\geq\;\Bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{K}\Bigr)\,\tau_{\mathrm{bdry}}^{2}-\sqrt{\tfrac{\log(2N/\delta)}{2K}}.

(5)

(iii)

Separation and Contamination:
Let
\Delta^{\prime}:=(1-1/K)(\tau_{\text{bdry}}^{2}-\tau^{2}/4-\gamma)
. If
\Delta^{\prime}>2\sqrt{\log(2N/\delta)/(2K)}
, then
\theta^{*}
strictly separates
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bdry}}
from
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{(1-\alpha_{\text{trim}})}
. Let
S_{\alpha}
denote the
\alpha N
examples with the largest
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}
. If additionally
|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bdry}}|\geq\alpha N
, then under strict separation
S_{\alpha}
contains no bulk corrupted examples, and the contamination rate from
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\mathrm{tail}}
satisfies:

\frac{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}\cap S_{\alpha}|}{|S_{\alpha}|}\;\leq\;\frac{\alpha_{\text{trim}}\cdot\varepsilon}{\alpha}\cdot\min\left(1,\frac{v_{\text{tail}}}{\tau^{2}/4+\gamma}\right).

(6)

Proof sketch.

The proof first establishes uniform concentration of the empirical variance
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}
around its expectation using McDiarmid’s inequality
(
McDiarmid 1989
)
. It then bounds these expectations: an upper bound for bulk corrupted points is derived via the law of total expectation under Assumption
2
, while Assumption
3
directly provides a lower bound for boundary clean points. If the gap between these expectations,
\Delta^{\prime}
, outpaces the maximum concentration error,
\theta^{*}
acts as a strict separating threshold. When additionally
|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bdry}}|\geq\alpha N
, every top-
\alpha N
example lies above
\theta^{*}
, and the contamination bound (
6
) follows by applying Markov’s inequality under Assumption
4
to the population variance of the escaping tail points. The full proof appears in
Section

A.1
.
∎

Interpreting
Theorem

1
.

Theorem
1
establishes
O(\sqrt{\log(N/\delta)/K})
finite-sample guarantees that provide a rigorous foundation for rank-variance as a filtering signal. It exploits the contrast between the rank stability of corrupted labels — which proxies consistently penalize — and the rank volatility of clean boundary examples induced by training stochasticity. Strict separation is guaranteed when the expectation gap
\Delta^{\prime}
dominates the bidirectional McDiarmid radius. While parts (i) and (ii) offer certificates for each group, part (iii) formalizes contamination control in the high-disagreement subset
S_{\alpha}
. Notably, (
6
) proves that the contamination in
S_{\alpha}
is strictly limited by the "escaping tail"
\mathcal{D}_{\text{corr}}^{\text{tail}}
, effectively isolating the influence of outliers that evade the simplicity bias of the proxies.

The separation condition
\Delta^{\prime}>0
remains intentionally conservative because the Popoviciu step assumes a maximally bimodal distribution for population variance and McDiarmid’s inequality accounts for worst-case coordinate-wise deviations rather than smoother empirical concentration. Consequently, while a formal certificate for CIFAR-10 scale parameters might suggest
K\gtrsim 200
, this represents a theoretical upper bound rather than a practical requirement — and notably loose estimation for
\tau
and
\tau_{bdry}
was used. In practice, the bimodal separation is sufficiently sharp to ensure robustness with as few as
K=3
proxies. Theorem
1
thus constitutes a rigorous worst-case certificate for disagreement-based selection; the consistent empirical gap above these bounds is a consequence of worst-case concentration inequalities rather than a ceiling on the method.

4.2
Structural vulnerability of magnitude-based IS

We complement
Theorem

1
with a structural lower bound on the corrupted sampling mass under any non-decreasing loss score.

Theorem 2

(Adversarial inflation of magnitude scores)
.

Let
s:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be any score that is a non-decreasing function
of the per-sample loss (in particular, gradient norm or loss value).
Under Assumption
1
, the corresponding IS distribution

p_{i}^{s}\propto s_{i}
satisfies

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}}p_{i}^{s}\;\geq\;\varepsilon\cdot\frac{s_{\min}^{\mathrm{corr}}}{s_{\max}^{\mathrm{full}}},\qquad\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}}p_{i}^{s}}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{clean}}}p_{i}^{s}}\;\geq\;\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{s_{\min}^{\mathrm{corr}}}{\,\overline{s}^{\mathrm{clean}}\,},

where
s_{\min}^{\mathrm{corr}}:=\min_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}}s_{i}
,

\overline{s}^{\mathrm{clean}}:=\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{clean}}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{clean}}}s_{j}
, and

s_{\max}^{\mathrm{full}}:=\max_{k}s_{k}
.
Whenever
s_{\min}^{\mathrm{corr}}\geq\alpha\cdot s_{\max}^{\mathrm{full}}
(loss-aligned contamination) the corrupted mass
is bounded below by
\alpha\varepsilon
irrespective of the clean-data
geometry.

The proof is a straightforward computation (
Section

A.2
).
The result implies that under loss-aligned contamination, no choice of non-decreasing score within this family can avoid an
\Omega(\varepsilon)
allocation to
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}
.
Theorem

2
is therefore an impossibility result for magnitude-based IS as a class, rather than being a property of any particular implementation — and motivates moving to a non-monotone score, as we do in the next section by aggregating proxy ranks via variance rather than mean.

5
Disagreement-Regularized Importance Sampling (
DR-IS
)

Motivated by the separation properties in
Theorem

1
, we propose a subsampling method, which uses the
rank-disagreement score
across an ensemble of
K
cheap proxies trained on
\mathcal{D}
for
T_{\mathrm{proxy}}
epochs. By aggregating loss ranks via (
2
),
DR-IS
distinguishes "boundary" clean data, which is highly sensitive to proxy stochasticity — from the "bulk" corrupted data that remains consistently high-loss.
In the following a static and an online version of this idea is introduced. For better understanding more details with pseudocode is shared in
Appendix

B
.

Static Pruning (Main Method)

Given a keep-fraction
\alpha\in(0,1]
,
DR-IS
selects the
\alpha N
examples with the highest
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}
:

\mathcal{S}_{\textsc{DR-IS}{}}=\mathrm{Top}\bigl(\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{N};\lfloor\alpha N\rfloor\bigr).

According to
Theorem

1
, this set
\mathcal{S}_{\textsc{DR-IS}{}}
prioritizes the clean boundary
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bdry}}
while theoretically excluding the corrupted bulk
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{(1-\alpha_{\text{trim}})}
whenever the separation gap
\Delta^{\prime}
is positive. The target model is trained on
\mathcal{S}_{\textsc{DR-IS}{}}
for
1/\alpha
times more epochs, keeping total gradient steps constant relative to full-dataset training.

Online IS (Extension)

Define the online sampling distribution for unbiased gradient estimation:

q_{i}^{\mathrm{DR}}\propto\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}+\xi\,\overline{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}},\quad\text{with }\overline{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}=\tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_{j}\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{j}.

(7)

The smoothing constant
\xi>0
is an insensitive tunable hyperparameter (
Section

C.5
) ensuring scale-invariance. While
q_{i}^{\mathrm{DR}}
deviates from the variance-minimizing distribution (
1
),
Theorem

2
identifies this deviation as the necessary mechanism to prevent importance sampling from over-allocating mass to high-loss outliers.

Implementation and Cost

Proxies are trained directly on
\mathcal{D}
, to prevent information leakage inherent in pre-training on auxiliary clean data (as in
Coleman et al. 2020
). This cost is amortized: proxies are an order of magnitude smaller than the target model and are trained once. With
K=3
,
T_{\mathrm{proxy}}=40
proxy-epochs (rank-variance scored
at epoch
20
), and
10\times
smaller proxies, the total computational
overhead remains below
10\%
of the
T_{\mathrm{target}}=160/200
-epoch
CIFAR-10/100 target schedules.

Clean-Data Dynamics

By design,
DR-IS
favors "boundary-clean" examples over "easy" ones. This boundary-heavy selection proves beneficial when samples near the decision surface carry more unique information (e.g., on CIFAR-100), though it can slightly degrade performance in settings where easy examples are foundational (see
Section

6.1
). The online variant (
7
) remains robust, tracking uniform SGD performance within
\sim 1\,
pp on clean data while offering superior stability under contamination (
Section

6.2
).

7
Limitations and Discussion

A unified view: from mean rank to rank variance.

The central insight of this work is that the simplicity bias of SGD proxies provides a more robust signal for importance sampling than gradient magnitude. Standard IS collapses because adversarial outliers exhibit high loss;
Theorem

1
establishes that those same outliers exhibit
low cross-sectional variance
across an independent proxy ensemble, while clean boundary examples exhibit high variance. Moving from
mean
rank to
variance
of ranks (
DR-IS
) simultaneously filters the corrupted bulk and identifies the informative clean boundary, forming a bimodal regularity that
Theorem

1
certifies non-asymptotically with explicit contamination control that no magnitude-based score can match (
Theorem

2
).

The clean-data cost and its mitigation.

By design,
DR-IS
prioritizes hard boundary examples over easy clean examples: a minor deficit on simple tasks (
-0.64\,
pp on CIFAR-10) versus a gain on more complex tasks (
+0.56\,
pp on CIFAR-100). This demonstrates that the value of disagreement-based selection scales with
distributional complexity
; the richer the feature space, the more informative the boundary-disagreement signal may become. Under adversarial contamination, gains reach
+9.5\,
pp over uniform SGD at
25\%
targeted noise. The online variant (
7
) reweights rather than discards, tracking uniform SGD within
{\sim}1\,
pp on clean data and is insensitive to the smoothing constant
\xi
. In static settings, boundary bias can be mitigated by mixing a
(1-k)
-fraction of uniform samples, allowing
Theorem

1
’s contamination bound to degrade gracefully, though mixing with magnitude-based IS sacrifices noise resilience rapidly for minimal gain (
Section

D.6
).

Structural assumptions, failure modes, and the breakdown point.

The robustness of
DR-IS
rests on two core assumptions.
2
requires proxies to consistently penalize corrupted labels that are well matched to loss-aligned adversarial attacks, but not to natural noise where labels may be plausible, explaining the parity on Food-101.
3
requires the hypothesis prior to have sufficient spread; otherwise, the disagreement signal collapses. Furthermore, the subset contamination control in
Theorem

1
(iii) strictly relies on the existence of sufficient boundary clean data (
|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bdry}}|\geq\alpha N
). If a dataset is simple, such that its boundary is exceedingly sparse, or if the selected subset fraction
\alpha
is large, the high-disagreement subset
S_{\alpha}
will exhaust the clean boundary capacity and admit examples from the escaping corrupted tail.
When these conditions fail, the method inverts its selection bias, as shown in the
\mathbb{R}^{20}
synthetic study (
Appendix

G
). Both conditions are empirically verifiable via the
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
histogram (
Figure

1
) prior to deployment. Finally, the
\varepsilon<1/2
constraint is precisely Huber’s breakdown point
(
Huber 1992
)
, a universal limit the method operates within but does not transcend.

Theory–practice gap and practical scope.

Our
K
-ablation demonstrates the efficiency of this bimodal separation: although worst-case gaps are negative,
Theorem

1
’s bulk-separated gap
\Delta^{\prime}
remains positive, certifying why
DR-IS
rejects
{>}90\%
of corruption with just
K=3
proxies. By targeting high-quantile separation to exclude the corrupted bulk (
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{(1-\alpha_{\text{trim}})}
), the theory provides predictive contamination bounds even when outliers evade the proxy’s simplicity bias.

The computational cost is amortized and readily parallelized, achieving a total overhead below 10% with no auxiliary clean data required. Scores must be recomputed if the dataset changes substantially, and for very large datasets, the prototype score of
Sorscher et al. 2022
provides an inexpensive pre-filter. Static pruning inevitably discards some clean data, whereas the online variant preserves it at the cost of maintaining sampling weights.

Comparison to AUM.

Both
DR-IS
and AUM reject adversarial corruption effectively, but through distinct mechanisms:
DR-IS
exploits rank disagreement across independent proxies, while AUM utilises margin trajectories within a single proxy.
Theorem

1
provides a uniform finite-sample identification guarantee that is qualitatively distinct from the
pairwise
AUROC bound derivable for AUM (
Section

A.3
,
Theorem

3
). Specifically, our theorem ensures that with probability
1-\delta
, every example in the corrupted bulk (
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{(1-\alpha_{\mathrm{trim}})}
) remains below the separation threshold, from which an explicit contamination bound for the escaping tail follows (
Theorem

1
(iii)). This deterministic upper bound on subset noise leakage provides a level of safety that pairwise expectations, which permit individual ordering failures cannot offer. Furthermore, the parameters in
Theorem

1
are bounded explicitly via the ensemble size
K
and empirically estimable rank-statistics, whereas AUM’s discriminative gap
\Delta_{0}
remains a posited population quantity. Empirically,
DR-IS
offers improved fidelity at moderate corruption and lower keep-fractions, while AUM provides superior stability under extreme noise (
\varepsilon\geq 0.40
). Given their distinct overhead profiles and orthogonal signals, the two methods constitute complementary tools for robust training.

Concluding remarks.

Disagreement-Regularized Importance Sampling improves the efficiency of importance sampling mitigating the vulnerability to adversarial label corruption by redirecting sampling mass to high-disagreement boundary examples. Natural extensions include feature corruption and covariate shift. Future investigations should address the optimal epoch selection for proxy scoring, the impact of boundary-heavy selection on data bias, the targeted integration of boundary selection with easy-sample retention, and combining the disagreement selection idea with other methods for case-specific advantages.

A.1
Proof of
Theorem

1

Proof.

Fix any
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
. We first establish the concentration of the empirical rank-disagreement
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}
around its expectation, and then strictly bound this expectation from above for the bulk corrupted points
i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{(1-\alpha_{\text{trim}})}
and from below for the boundary clean points
j\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bdry}}
. After that, the separation and subset contamination bounds are shown.

Step 1: bounded-differences concentration.

The statistic
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}=\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}(h_{1},\dots,h_{K})
is a function of
K
independent random proxy models drawn from
\mathcal{P}
, with each normalized rank satisfying
\rho_{i}(h_{k})\in[0,1]
. Let
h^{\prime}_{k}
be a replacement for
h_{k}
, leaving the remaining
K-1
proxies unchanged. Writing
\rho_{m}:=\rho_{i}(h_{m})
and
\rho^{\prime}_{k}:=\rho_{i}(h^{\prime}_{k})
, we can expand the biased sample variance
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{m}\rho_{m}^{2}-\bar{\rho}^{2}
. The empirical variance changes by:

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}-(\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i})^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{m=1}^{K}\rho_{m}^{2}-\bar{\rho}^{2}\right)-\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{m\neq k}\rho_{m}^{2}+\frac{1}{K}(\rho^{\prime}_{k})^{2}-(\bar{\rho}^{\prime})^{2}\right),

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{k}^{2}-(\rho^{\prime}_{k})^{2}}{K}-(\bar{\rho}+\bar{\rho}^{\prime})(\bar{\rho}-\bar{\rho}^{\prime}).

Because
\rho_{k},\rho^{\prime}_{k}\in[0,1]
, the maximum change in the sample variance for a single bounded variable substitution is exactly
(K-1)/K^{2}
, which is strictly bounded by
1/K
. Thus,
|\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}-(\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i})^{\prime}|\leq 1/K
for all
k
.

By McDiarmid’s inequality
[
McDiarmid 1989
]
, setting the substitution bounds
c_{k}=1/K
such that
\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}^{2}=K(1/K^{2})=1/K
, we obtain:

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}]\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2t^{2}}{\sum c_{k}^{2}}\right)=2\exp\left(-2Kt^{2}\right).

Setting the right-hand side to
\delta/N
yields
t=\sqrt{\frac{\log(2N/\delta)}{2K}}
. Applying a union bound over all
N
training examples guarantees that the following global concentration event holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\forall i:\;\left|\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}]\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{\log(2N/\delta)}{2K}}\right)\geq 1-\delta.

(8)

All subsequent bounds are evaluated conditionally on the deterministic event
Equation

8
.

Step 2: expectation upper bound on the bulk
\mathbf{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{(1-\alpha_{\text{trim}})}}

For each
k
,
h_{k}
is drawn independently from
\mathcal{P}
. The standard identity for the expected sample variance gives:

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}]=\left(1-\frac{1}{K}\right)\text{Var}_{h\sim\mathcal{P}}[\rho_{i}(h)].

For
i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{(1-\alpha_{\text{trim}})}
, let
X=\rho_{i}(h)
.
2
states that the event
\mathcal{A}=\{X\geq 1-\tau\}
occurs with probability
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})\geq 1-\gamma
. To upper-bound the variance of
X
, we use the fact that the variance of a random variable is the infimum of expected squared distances to any constant
c
. Choosing
c=1-\tau/2
, we have:

\text{Var}(X)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(X-\left(1-\frac{\tau}{2}\right)\right)^{2}\right].

By the law of total expectation, we split this across
\mathcal{A}
and its complement
\mathcal{A}^{c}
:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(X-\left(1-\frac{\tau}{2}\right)\right)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(X-\left(1-\frac{\tau}{2}\right)\right)^{2}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{A}\right]\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(X-\left(1-\frac{\tau}{2}\right)\right)^{2}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{A}^{c}\right]\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}^{c}).

Conditioned on
\mathcal{A}
,
X\in[1-\tau,1]
. The maximum possible distance between
X
and the midpoint
1-\tau/2
is exactly
\tau/2
, so the squared distance is strictly bounded by
\tau^{2}/4
. Conditioned on
\mathcal{A}^{c}
,
X\in[0,1-\tau)
. Since both
X
and
1-\tau/2
are constrained to the interval
[0,1]
, their absolute difference is strictly less than 1, trivially bounding the squared distance by 1. Therefore:

\text{Var}_{h\sim\mathcal{P}}[\rho_{i}(h)]\leq\left(\frac{\tau^{2}}{4}\right)(1-\gamma)+(1)(\gamma)\leq\frac{\tau^{2}}{4}+\gamma.

Substituting this into the expectation identity and applying the concentration bound (
8
), we obtain the simultaneous upper bound for all bulk corrupted points:

\forall i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{(1-\alpha_{\text{trim}})}:\quad\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{K}\right)\left(\frac{\tau^{2}}{4}+\gamma\right)+\sqrt{\frac{\log(2N/\delta)}{2K}}:=\theta^{*}.

Step 3: expectation lower bound on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bdry}}
.

For any
j\in D_{\text{bdry}}
,
3
enforces a minimum population variance
\text{Var}_{h\sim\mathcal{P}}[\rho_{j}(h)]\geq\tau_{\text{bdry}}^{2}
. Applying the bias-corrected expectation identity gives
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{j}]\geq(1-1/K)\tau_{\text{bdry}}^{2}
. Combined directly with the lower tail of the concentration bound (
8
), this yields:

\forall j\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bdry}}:\quad\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{j}\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{K}\right)\tau_{\text{bdry}}^{2}-\sqrt{\frac{\log(2N/\delta)}{2K}}.

Step 4: separation and subset contamination bound.

Subtracting the pre-concentration upper expectation of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{(1-\alpha_{\text{trim}})}
from the lower expectation of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bdry}}
defines the proxy variance gap
\Delta^{\prime}:=(1-1/K)(\tau_{\text{bdry}}^{2}-\tau^{2}/4-\gamma)
. Strict separation occurs when the lowest empirical variance in the boundary set exceeds the highest empirical variance in the bulk corrupted set. By the bounds derived in Steps 2 and 3, this is guaranteed when the expectation gap outpaces the maximum bidirectional concentration error:

\Delta^{\prime}>2\sqrt{\frac{\log(2N/\delta)}{2K}}.

When this holds,
\theta^{*}
acts as a strict, non-overlapping threshold separating
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bdry}}
entirely from
D_{\text{corr}}^{(1-\alpha_{\text{trim}})}
.

We now bound the contamination rate in
S_{\alpha}
, the subset of
\alpha N
examples with the highest empirical disagreement. Under strict separation, every
j\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bdry}}
satisfies
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{j}>\theta^{*}
by part (ii). The additional hypothesis
|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bdry}}|\geq\alpha N
guarantees that the
\alpha N
-th largest
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
value exceeds
\theta^{*}
, so
S_{\alpha}
is drawn exclusively from examples above
\theta^{*}
. Because event (
4
) holds globally, no bulk corrupted point can exceed
\theta^{*}
; thus, the only corrupted examples capable of entering
S_{\alpha}
belong to the escaping tail
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\text{tail}}
.
For any tail point
i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\text{tail}}
to successfully exceed the separation threshold (
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}>\theta^{*}
), its pre-concentration expectation must satisfy:

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}]\;>\;\theta^{*}-\sqrt{\frac{\log(2N/\delta)}{2K}}\;=\;\Bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{K}\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{\tau^{2}}{4}+\gamma\Bigr).

Applying the bias-corrected variance identity
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}]=(1-1/K)\operatorname{Var}_{h\sim P}[\rho_{i}(h)]
, we see that a tail point can only survive the threshold if its underlying population variance satisfies:

\operatorname{Var}_{h\sim P}[\rho_{i}(h)]>\frac{\tau^{2}}{4}+\gamma.

(9)

We bound the number of tail points satisfying (
9
) using Markov’s inequality. Let
Z
be a random variable representing
\operatorname{Var}_{h\sim P}[\rho_{I}(h)]
for an index
I
drawn uniformly at random from
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\text{tail}}
. By
4
, the expectation of
Z
over this uniform draw is bounded by
v_{\text{tail}}
. Because variance is strictly non-negative, Markov’s inequality gives:

\mathbb{P}_{I\sim U(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\text{tail}})}\Bigl(Z>\frac{\tau^{2}}{4}+\gamma\Bigr)\;\leq\;\frac{\mathbb{E}[Z]}{\tau^{2}/4+\gamma}\;\leq\;\frac{v_{\text{tail}}}{\tau^{2}/4+\gamma}.

This ratio (capped at 1) represents the absolute maximum fraction of the tail that can systematically clear the threshold
\theta^{*}
. The maximum raw number of corrupted examples in
S_{\alpha}
is therefore the size of the tail multiplied by this surviving fraction:

|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}\cap S_{\alpha}|\leq|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\text{tail}}|\cdot\min\left(1,\frac{v_{\text{tail}}}{\tau^{2}/4+\gamma}\right)=\alpha_{\text{trim}}\varepsilon N\cdot\min\left(1,\frac{v_{\text{tail}}}{\tau^{2}/4+\gamma}\right).

Dividing by the capacity of the selected subset
|S_{\alpha}|=\alpha N
, we obtain the final guaranteed upper bound on the contamination rate:

\frac{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}\cap S_{\alpha}|}{|S_{\alpha}|}\;\leq\;\frac{\alpha_{\text{trim}}\cdot\varepsilon}{\alpha}\cdot\min\left(1,\frac{v_{\text{tail}}}{\tau^{2}/4+\gamma}\right).

This concludes the proof.
∎

A.2
Proof of
Theorem

2

Proof.

For any non-negative score
s
, the IS distribution is

p_{i}^{s}=s_{i}/\sum_{k}s_{k}
. The total mass on the corrupted set
is:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}}p_{i}^{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}}s_{i}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}s_{k}}\;\geq\;\frac{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}|\,s_{\min}^{\mathrm{corr}}}{N\,s_{\max}^{\mathrm{full}}}\;\geq\;\varepsilon\,\frac{s_{\min}^{\mathrm{corr}}}{s_{\max}^{\mathrm{full}}}.

For the ratio, the denominator is equal to

|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{clean}}|\cdot\overline{s}^{\mathrm{clean}}=(1-\varepsilon)N\cdot\overline{s}^{\mathrm{clean}}
, while the
numerator is bounded below by

|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}|\,s_{\min}^{\mathrm{corr}}=\varepsilon N\cdot s_{\min}^{\mathrm{corr}}
:

\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}}p_{i}^{s}}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{clean}}}p_{i}^{s}}=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}}s_{i}}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{clean}}}s_{i}}\;\geq\;\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{s_{\min}^{\mathrm{corr}}}{\overline{s}^{\mathrm{clean}}}.

The loss-aligned contamination condition
s_{\min}^{\mathrm{corr}}\geq\alpha\cdot s_{\max}^{\mathrm{full}}
is stated directly as a hypothesis of the theorem. Substituting into the first displayed inequality immediately yields
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}}p_{i}^{s}\geq\alpha\varepsilon
.
∎

A.3
An AUROC Identification Argument for AUM

Pleiss et al.
Pleiss et al. 2020
motivate AUM through the simplicity bias of SGD and provide intuitive justification for why clean and mislabeled samples should accumulate different margins; we are not aware of a finite-sample identification guarantee in the published literature analogous to
Theorem

1
. We sketch one candidate analysis here under stylized assumptions, both to clarify the relationship to
Theorem

1
and to make precise the qualitative difference between AUM-style training-dynamics scoring and
DR-IS
-style ensemble disagreement. The result is a
pairwise AUROC bound
, qualitatively weaker than the subset-level finite-sample guarantees that
Theorem

1
provides for
DR-IS
.

Setup.

Fix a training set
D=\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{clean}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}
. SGD on

D
produces a random trajectory

\Theta=(\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(T)})
, with randomness in
initialization and minibatch sampling. For a labeled example
(x,y)
,
the per-step margin is:

M^{(t)}(x,y;\theta):=z_{\theta,y}(x)-\max_{k\neq y}z_{\theta,k}(x),

and the trajectory-averaged AUM is:

A_{T}(x,y;\Theta):=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}M^{(t)}\!\bigl(x,y;\theta^{(t)}\bigr).

Define the population AUM:

Z(x,y):=\mathbb{E}_{\Theta}\!\left[A_{T}(x,y;\Theta)\right],

a deterministic function of
(x,y)
once
D
is fixed, and the residual

\varepsilon(x,y;\Theta):=A_{T}(x,y;\Theta)-Z(x,y)
, which satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{\Theta}[\varepsilon(x,y;\Theta)]=0
for every
(x,y)
.

Let
I\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{clean}})
and

J\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}})
be drawn independently of each
other and of
\Theta
. Throughout we work conditionally on
D
, so

I\perp J\perp\Theta
.

A.3.1
Assumptions

Assumption 5

(Population gap)
.

Writing
\mu_{c}:=\mathbb{E}_{I}[Z(x_{I},y_{I})]
and

\mu_{w}:=\mathbb{E}_{J}[Z(x_{J},y_{J})]
, there exists
\Delta_{0}>0
such that

\mu_{c}-\mu_{w}\geq\Delta_{0}
.

This is the empirically-motivated input. Pleiss
et al.
Pleiss et al. 2020
justify it heuristically via the simplicity
bias of SGD; a per-step lower bound on the difference of expected margin
updates between clean and corrupted examples (e.g., as a consequence of
clean/corrupted gradient alignment under cross-entropy near
initialization) telescopes to give such a
\Delta_{0}
. We treat

5
as a posited input.

Assumption 6

(Sub-Gaussian population spread)
.

Both
Z(x_{I},y_{I})-\mu_{c}
under
I\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{clean}})

and
Z(x_{J},y_{J})-\mu_{w}
under
J\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}})

are
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian.

Assumption 7

(Trajectory concentration of pair-differences)
.

There exists
\nu(T)
with
\nu(T)\to 0
as
T\to\infty
such that for
every fixed pair
(i,j)
with
i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{clean}}
,

j\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}
, the random variable

\varepsilon(x_{i},y_{i};\Theta)-\varepsilon(x_{j},y_{j};\Theta)
is

\nu(T)
-sub-Gaussian as a function of
\Theta
.

7
is a joint statement about pair-differences,
weaker than independent marginal sub-Gaussianity of each residual — the
trajectory acts on both points, so the residuals are not independent.
Under (i) bounded margins
|M^{(t)}|\leq B
(e.g., via softmax
composition with weight decay or gradient clipping) and (ii) uniform
mixing of the SGD chain with mixing time
\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}
, Paulin’s
Markov-chain Hoeffding inequality
Paulin 2015
applied
to
f(\theta):=M(x_{i},y_{i};\theta)-M(x_{j},y_{j};\theta)
and bounded by

2B
, yields
\nu(T)=2B\sqrt{2\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}/T}
. The
mixing-time hypothesis is the core effective component and is most defensible in
the regularized constant-step regime studied
by
Dieuleveut et al. 2020
,
Raginsky et al. 2017
.

A.3.2
AUROC bound

Theorem 3

(AUROC bound for AUM)
.

Under
Assumptions
5
–
7
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(A_{T}(x_{I},y_{I};\Theta)>A_{T}(x_{J},y_{J};\Theta)\right)\;\geq\;1-\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta_{0}^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}+2\nu^{2}(T)}\right).

Proof.

Let
W:=A_{T}(x_{I},y_{I};\Theta)-A_{T}(x_{J},y_{J};\Theta)
and decompose

W=S+N
with:

S:=Z(x_{I},y_{I})-Z(x_{J},y_{J}),\qquad N:=\varepsilon(x_{I},y_{I};\Theta)-\varepsilon(x_{J},y_{J};\Theta).

S
is
\sigma((I,J))
-measurable;
N
depends on
(I,J,\Theta)
.

Step 1 (mean).
By independence of
I
and
J
,

\mathbb{E}[S]=\mu_{c}-\mu_{w}\geq\Delta_{0}
. By definition of

\varepsilon
,
\mathbb{E}[N\mid I,J]=0
a.s., so
\mathbb{E}[N]=0

and
\mathbb{E}[W]\geq\Delta_{0}
.

Step 2 (sub-Gaussianity of
S
).
The terms

Z(x_{I},y_{I})-\mu_{c}
and
Z(x_{J},y_{J})-\mu_{w}
are independent
(
I\perp J
) and each
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian by

6
. Hence,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda(S-\mathbb{E}[S])}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda(Z(x_{I},y_{I})-\mu_{c})}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{-\lambda(Z(x_{J},y_{J})-\mu_{w})}\right]\leq e^{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}/2}\cdot e^{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}/2}=e^{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}},

i.e.,
S-\mathbb{E}[S]
has variance proxy
2\sigma^{2}
.

Step 3 (conditional sub-Gaussianity of
N
).
By

7
, conditional on
(I,J)
,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda N}\,\big|\,I,J\right]\leq e^{\lambda^{2}\nu^{2}(T)/2}\quad\text{a.s.}

Step 4 (assembly via the tower property).
Since
S
is

\sigma((I,J))
-measurable, the inner conditional MGF pulls

e^{\lambda(S-\mathbb{E}[S])}
outside cleanly:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda(W-\mathbb{E}[W])}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda(S-\mathbb{E}[S])}\cdot\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda N}\,\big|\,I,J\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\lambda^{2}\nu^{2}(T)/2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda(S-\mathbb{E}[S])}\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\lambda^{2}\nu^{2}(T)/2}\cdot e^{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}}=\exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}(2\sigma^{2}+\nu^{2}(T))}{2}\right).

So
W-\mathbb{E}[W]
has variance proxy
2\sigma^{2}+\nu^{2}(T)
.
Crucially, independence of
S
and
N
is
not
required; only the
conditional MGF bound for
N
given
(I,J)
is used.

Step 5 (tail bound).
Since
\mathbb{E}[W]\geq\Delta_{0}
, the
event
\{W\leq 0\}
is contained in

\{W-\mathbb{E}[W]\leq-\Delta_{0}\}
, therefore,

\mathbb{P}(W\leq 0)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta_{0}^{2}}{2(2\sigma^{2}+\nu^{2}(T))}\right)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta_{0}^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}+2\nu^{2}(T)}\right).\qed

Fallback under marginal-only sub-Gaussianity.

If only the marginal residuals
\varepsilon(x_{i},y_{i};\Theta)
and

\varepsilon(x_{j},y_{j};\Theta)
are individually
\nu(T)
-sub-Gaussian
(with no joint claim on the difference), the triangle inequality on the
sub-Gaussian Orlicz norm gives

\|\varepsilon(x_{i},y_{i};\cdot)-\varepsilon(x_{j},y_{j};\cdot)\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq 2\nu(T)
conditional on
(I,J)
. The argument above then yields:

\mathbb{P}\!\left(A_{T}(x_{I},y_{I};\Theta)>A_{T}(x_{J},y_{J};\Theta)\right)\;\geq\;1-\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta_{0}^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}+8\nu^{2}(T)}\right),

losing a factor of four in the noise term but converging to

\mathrm{AUROC}\to 1
at the same rate as
T\to\infty
.

Comparison with
Theorem

1
.

Theorem

3
is qualitatively weaker than

Theorem

1
along two axes. (i)
Type of
statement:
it is a pairwise AUROC bound averaged over independent draws
from
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{clean}}\times\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{corr}}
, whereas

Theorem

1
provides uniform one-sided bounds that, with probability

1-\delta
, constrain the rank disagreement of
every
bulk corrupted
point from above and of
every
boundary-clean point from below;
when
\Delta^{\prime}>0
and
|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bdry}}|\geq\alpha N
these simultaneously imply
separation of the two groups. A high AUROC permits
individual ordering failures — a single corrupted point may rank above
many clean points without violating the bound, which the uniform
bound rules out when its separation condition holds. (ii)
Verifiability of the gap.

\Delta_{0}
in
5
is a population-level statistic over
SGD trajectories, hard to estimate empirically without running many
trajectories. In
Theorem

1
the analogous quantities
\tau
and

\tau_{\mathrm{bdry}}
are statistics of the proxy ensemble and are
empirically estimated in
Section

D.4
. We note that the resulting nominal gap
\Delta
is negative in our CIFAR setup under the worst-case parameterization (
\widehat{\tau}^{2}_{\mathrm{corr,max}}
dominates
\widehat{\tau}^{2}_{\mathrm{bdry,median}}
), so
Theorem

1
’s sufficient separation condition is not certified in this regime regardless of
K
; the empirical success is explained separately in §
7
. Nevertheless the control parameter
K
remains directly observable and cheaply scalable.
Deriving
\Delta_{0}
from primitive assumptions on SGD dynamics under
contamination remains open.

For the MNIST

0.05

0.9

Findings.

As shown in
Figure

4
and
Figure

5
, the specific choice of proxy architecture — ranging from single-layer FC networks to scikit-learn SGD classifiers — has a limited impact on the resulting separation scores. Interpreting this through our theoretical framework, this architectural independence is expected: the validity of
Theorem

1
relies on the presence of a "simplicity bias" (
2
) and boundary stochasticity (
3
), rather than deep-learning-specific artifacts. As long as a proxy hypothesis class possesses sufficient capacity to prioritize easy clean data while consistently struggling to memorize random noise, the necessary rank-variance signal will emerge. The slight performance edge of the multi-seed FC ensemble is thus attributable to specific hyperparameter tuning rather than a fundamental necessity of deep homogeneous ensembles. Consequently, practitioners can safely prioritize computational constraints when selecting proxies, confident that the theoretical separation guarantees hold across diverse model families.

D.6
Hybrid score ablation

The hybrid score
q_{i}\propto\beta\|\nabla f_{i}\|+(1-\beta)\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}

(
\beta\in[0,1]
) interpolates between gradient-norm IS (
\beta=1
)
and
DR-IS
(
\beta=0
).

Table

8
reports CIFAR-10/VGG-19/
\alpha=0.25
results.

\beta=1
outperforms
\beta=0
on clean data by
1.83\,
pp
(
88.65\%
vs
86.82\%
) but collapses under
25\%
targeted noise;

\beta=0
retains
81.41\%
. No interior
\beta
dominates
\beta=0

on the clean–adversarially-robust Pareto frontier: even
\beta=0.25

sacrifices
4\,
pp of robustness for
0.1\,
pp of clean accuracy. The two
signals do not combine usefully under the protocol studied here.

This catastrophic degradation at intermediate
\beta
is a direct consequence of the structural vulnerability outlined in
Theorem

2
. The two components of the hybrid score operate on fundamentally incompatible scales under contamination. Because the
DR-IS
score
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}
is derived from normalized ranks (
\rho_{i}\in(0,1]
), its maximum possible value is strictly bounded. Conversely, the gradient norm
\|\nabla f_{i}\|
is unbounded and grows disproportionately large for corrupted outliers.
Consequently, even for small values of
\beta
, the massive magnitude of the corrupted gradients completely overshadows the bounded disagreement signal. The corrupted outliers effectively "hijack" the sampling mass. This dynamic instantly shatters the strict separation gap (
\Delta^{\prime}>0
) established in
Theorem

1
, forcing the top-
\alpha
subset to ingest the corrupted bulk and reintroducing the adversarial mass allocation that universally plagues magnitude-based IS.

The original motivation for the hybrid score is to mitigate
DR-IS
’s "clean-data cost" — the tendency to aggressively filter out "easy" clean samples in favor of the clean boundary. To achieve this without sacrificing robustness, interpolating
DR-IS
with
uniform sampling
may be a theoretically more sound approach.
Consider a uniform-hybrid policy
q_{i}\propto(1-k)(\frac{1}{N})+k\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}
. This mix would successfully reintroduce easy clean samples into the training batches under limited contamination, without actively rewarding high-loss outliers. Because uniform sampling is magnitude-independent, it strictly bypasses the adversarial amplification detailed in
Theorem

2
. Under a uniform mix, the rigorous contamination bound derived in
Theorem

1
(iii) would degrade gracefully toward the base dataset corruption rate
\varepsilon
, rather than collapsing entirely as it does when mixed with gradient norms. Thus, blending rank-variance with uniform probability provides a safe mechanism to tune the boundary-heavy bias of
DR-IS
.

The original RHO-LOSS
[
Mindermann et al. 2022
]
scores each
example by
\mathcal{L}(x;w_{\mathrm{target}})-\mathcal{L}(x;w_{\mathrm{ref}})

Approximate variant.
The target-loss term is replaced by the proxy-ensemble mean loss, sharing infrastructure with
DR-IS

Faithful variant.
A ResNet-20 reference model is trained on a

5{,}000
-sample holdout for
30
epochs; the score is recomputed every

5
target epochs with the sampler rebuilt accordingly.

As detailed in
Table

3
, both variants severely underperform uniform random sampling across all settings. For example, at
25\%

74.52\%
accuracy and DR-IS-online achieves
84.04\%

10.00\%\pm 0.00\%
) and the faithful variant struggles significantly (

).

The failure mode is
not
magnitude-amplification (
Theorem

2
): rather, the extreme per-seed accuracy standard deviations—ranging from
17.68
to
45.59
percentage points on clean and
10\%
targeted noise settings—indicate sampler-collapse divergence onto small, high-score subsets that the target model then memorizes. This is a known fragility of difference-based scoring with sparse non-zero scores, distinct from adversarial amplification. We found no configuration of either variant that recovers competitive accuracy on clean or noisy CIFAR-10 under our keep-fraction protocol.
