Yi Ru-Ya Zhang

Abstract
. We study a weak formulation of Serrin’s overdetermined boundary value problem in planar Jordan domains with rectifiable boundary. Our first result establishes that, within the class of rectifiable Jordan Smirnov domains, the corresponding harmonic quadrature identity, equivalent to Serrin’s overdetermined problem, necessarily implies that the domain is a disk. Subsequently, we construct a family of rectifiable, non-Smirnov Jordan domains that nonetheless satisfy the same quadrature identity, thereby demonstrating the sharpness of the Smirnov regularity assumption. Consequently, there exists a nontrivial Jordan domain with rectifiable boundary satisfying the weak formulation of Serrin’s overdetermined system in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} .

1.
Introduction

A classical theorem of Serrin asserts that a bounded
C^{2}
domain

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
which supports a solution of the overdetermined
boundary value problem

-\Delta u=1\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad u=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,\qquad\partial_{\nu_{\rm in}}u=c\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega

must be a ball. Here
\nu_{\rm in}
denotes the inward unit normal and

c
is a constant. Serrin’s original proof
[
33
]
is based on
the moving plane method, while Weinberger later gave a proof using a

P
-function argument
[
35
]
. Since then, Serrin-type
overdetermined problems have become a central theme in elliptic PDE,
potential theory, and geometric analysis; see, for example,
[
4
,
6
,
30
,
5
]
and the surveys
[
32
,
29
]
for additional discussion of related results.

The purpose of this paper is to study a weak form of the problem in planar domains with rough boundary, and to
identify a sharp rigidity threshold. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
bounded domain such that
\partial\Omega
is countably

(n-1)
-rectifiable and

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\Omega)<\infty.

We say that
u
satisfies Serrin’s overdetermined condition in the weak
sense if its zero extension, still denoted by
u
, belongs to

W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and

(1.1)

\Delta u=c\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\!\lfloor_{\partial\Omega}-\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}\,dx\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Equivalently,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u\,\Delta\varphi\,dx=c\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,dx\qquad\forall\,\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

A rigidity theorem for this weak formulation was proved in

[
16
]
. Namely, if
\Omega
is a bounded domain whose boundary
satisfies the uniform upper density condition

(1.2)

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\Omega\cap B_{r}(x))\leq Lr^{n-1}\qquad\forall\,x\in\partial\Omega,\quad 0<r<r_{0},

for some
L>0
and
r_{0}>0
, then any solution of
(
1.1
) forces
\Omega
to be a ball. Related approaches in
Lipschitz domains were developed in
[
10
,
9
]
.
These results are in the same spirit as weak forms of Alexandrov’s theorem
for sets of finite perimeter
[
8
]
.

1.1.
From Serrin’s condition to harmonic quadrature identities

The weak Serrin condition naturally gives rise to a harmonic quadrature
identity. Indeed, suppose that
u
satisfies (
1.1
), and
let
h
be harmonic in an open neighborhood of
\overline{\Omega}
. Taking
a cut-off function
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}
such that
\eta\equiv 1
near

\overline{\Omega}
, and testing (
1.1
) against
\eta h
,
we obtain

(1.3)

\int_{\Omega}h\,dx=c\int_{\partial\Omega}h\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

If, in addition,
\Omega
is regular for the Dirichlet problem, then
(
1.3
) extends to all harmonic functions continuous up
to the boundary:

(1.4)

\int_{\Omega}h\,dx=c\int_{\partial\Omega}h\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\qquad\forall\,h\in C(\overline{\Omega}),\quad\Delta h=0\text{ in }\Omega.

Thus Serrin’s overdetermined condition may be viewed, at least in regular
domains, as a quadrature identity comparing volume measure in
\Omega
with
surface measure on
\partial\Omega
.

This identity is substantially stronger than the classical one-point
mean-value properties. Epstein and Schiffer proved that if
0\in\Omega
and

\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}h\,dx=h(0)\qquad\forall\,h\in C(\overline{\Omega}),\quad\Delta h=0\text{ in }\Omega,

then
\Omega
is a ball centered at the origin
[
15
]
.
On the other hand, boundary mean-value identities of the form

(1.5)

a\int_{\partial\Omega}h\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=h(0)\qquad\forall\,h\in C(\overline{\Omega}),\quad\Delta h=0\text{ in }\Omega

are much more flexible. In the plane, Keldysh–Lavrentiev and
Duren–Shapiro–Shields constructed non-ball domains satisfying
(
1.5
); higher-dimensional analogues were later obtained by
Lewis–Vogel
[
24
,
13
,
26
]
.
The identity (
1.4
) lies between these two phenomena:
it is a boundary quadrature identity, but it involves the full interior
volume measure rather than a single point mass.

In planar simply connected domains, the identity (
1.4
) belongs to a circle of problems connecting Serrin-type overdetermined boundary conditions, quadrature domains, arclength quadrature identities, and analytic content. Khavinson
[
20
]
studied this problem under additional boundary regularity assumptions and formulated the conjecture that disks are the only Jordan domains satisfying (
1.4
). The analytic-boundary case and several closely related free-boundary and approximation-theoretic formulations were developed in subsequent works; see, for instance,

[
14
,
23
,
3
,
22
,
1
,
21
]
, as well as
[
18
,
19
]
for related approximation problems involving harmonic vector fields. The present paper addresses the rough simply connected case and shows that, within rectifiable Jordan domains, the Smirnov condition is exactly the threshold between rigidity and flexibility.

1.2.
Main results

We now specialize to the planar case. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
be a
bounded Jordan domain with rectifiable boundary, and let

\phi:\mathbb{D}\to\Omega

be a conformal map. Since
\partial\Omega
is rectifiable, one has

\phi^{\prime}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{D})
. We say that
\Omega
is a Smirnov domain if

\phi^{\prime}
is an outer function. Equivalently,

(1.6)

\log|\phi^{\prime}(w)|=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1-|w|^{2}}{|e^{it}-w|^{2}}\log|(\phi^{\prime})^{*}(e^{it})|\,dt,\qquad w\in\mathbb{D}.

This condition is independent of the choice of the conformal map.

Our first theorem proves the rigidity part of the theory. It is a stronger version of
[
16
]
in the plane. Note that a Smirnov domain does not necessarily satisfy (
1.2
), while the other direction holds; see e.g.
[
31
, Theorem 7.6 & Exercise 7.3]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
be a bounded Jordan domain with
rectifiable boundary. Assume that
\Omega
is a Smirnov domain. If
there exists
c>0
such that (
1.4
) holds,
then
\Omega
is a disk.

The proof proceeds by combining the quadrature identity with Smirnov’s theory of analytic functions. Starting from (
1.4
), we first derive a boundary Cauchy-orthogonality relation. Within the Smirnov class, this relation yields a bounded holomorphic function whose boundary trace encodes the overdetermined normal derivative. This construction enables us to obtain a weak Serrin-type solution and to recover Weinberger’s
P
-function method in a low-regularity planar setting. The principal analytical tool is the theory of nontangential boundary limits of Hardy-class functions in the unit disk; see also
[
10
]
.

The Smirnov assumption in Theorem
1.1
is sharp. Our
second theorem constructs non-Smirnov rectifiable Jordan domains satisfying
the same quadrature identity.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exist
a_{0}>0
, a family

\{\Omega_{a}\}_{0<a<a_{0}}
of bounded rectifiable Jordan domains, and
constants
c(a)>0
, such that none of the domains
\Omega_{a}
is a
Smirnov domain and (
1.4
) holds.
Moreover, each
\Omega_{a}
is invariant under rotation by
\pi/2
.

Consequently, Khavinson’s conjecture holds in the Smirnov class but fails in
the full class of rectifiable Jordan domains. Equivalently, the Smirnov
property is the exact threshold, within the class considered here, between
rigidity and flexibility for the planar Serrin-type quadrature identity.
Moreover, this construction also provides a counterexample to the rigidity of (
1.1
) within the class of bounded rectifiable Jordan domains in the plane; see Lemma
2.1
below for details.

Recall that, a domain
\Omega
is called a
quasidisks
, and its boundary is called a
quasicircle
if

\Omega=\phi(\mathbb{D})
for some global quasiconformal mapping
\phi\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
. Here
\phi
is quasiconformal if
\phi
is a homeomprhism in
W^{1,\,1}_{{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{\,loc\,}}}
-class and satisfies the pointwise distortion inequality for some
K\geq 1
,

|D\phi|^{2}(x)\leq KJ_{\phi}(x)\quad\text{ for almost every $x$},

where
J_{\phi}
denotes the Jacobian of
\phi
. Observe that every quasiconformal mapping is Hölder continuous; see e.g.
[
2
, Chapter 13]
for more information.
For every
0<\alpha<1
, the examples in Theorem
1.2
may be chosen so that
\partial\Omega
is a
C^{0,\,\alpha}
-quasicircle, in the sense that the boundary homeomorphism from
\mathbb{T}
to
\partial\Omega
and its inverse are in
C^{0,\,\alpha}
-class. Consequently, Lipschitz regularity is sharp within the Hölder quasicircles scale for the weak Serrin rigidity problem.

The construction in Theorem
1.2
is based on a
refined Duren–Shapiro–Shields mechanism. A purely singular branch is not
sufficient for (
1.4
): Unlike
(
1.5
), the identity (
1.4
)
couples the boundary arclength density with the interior Jacobian density of
the conformal map. Toward this problem, we first show that such a pure singular construction is impossible in a perturbative regime; see Proposition
4.1
. We then correct the singular factor by
solving a nonlinear fixed-point equation for an outer factor. The
four-fold symmetry removes the low Fourier modes corresponding to the
Möbius degeneracies of the disk, making the linearized operator invertible.

Finally, we briefly address the higher-dimensional setting. The preceding examples indicate that the uniform upper density condition (
1.2
) is likely (close to) optimal for obtaining rough Serrin-type rigidity in dimensions greater than
2
, as is particularly suggested by the results in
[
28
]
for (
1.5
).
Nonetheless, the planar construction relies in an essential way on the inner-outer factorization of functions in the unit disk, and no analogous factorization theory is currently available in higher dimensions. Moreover, the proof of Theorem
1.2
also implies the construction would be more complicated that the one by Lewis–Vogel
[
26
]
. Consequently, the question of whether there exist higher-dimensional, non-spherical domains satisfying the weak Serrin condition remains open and constitutes a distinct problem, which shall be treated in a separate investigation.

1.3.
Organization of the paper

Section
2
collects the conformal mapping and harmonic
analysis tools used throughout the paper, including Smirnov domains,
inner–outer factorization, the Poisson balayage operator, and a small-data
version of the Duren–Shapiro–Shields construction. In
Section
3
we prove Theorem
1.1
.
The proof derives a boundary orthogonality condition from the quadrature
identity, converts it into a weak Serrin solution in the Smirnov class, and
then applies Weinberger’s argument. In Section
4

we prove Theorem
1.2
. We first rule out the
purely singular perturbative branch and then construct the desired
non-Smirnov examples by solving the nonlinear fixed-point problem.

2.
Preliminaries

Let us consider the case when
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a Jordan domain
with rectifiable boundary and satisfies (
1.4
). Let

\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
, and let

\varphi:\mathbb{D}\to\Omega

be a conformal map. By the Carathéodory–Osgood theorem,
\varphi
extends
homeomorphically to
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. Since
\Gamma
is rectifiable, one has

\varphi^{\prime}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{D})
; see
[
12
, Chapter 10, §10.2]
. Equivalently,
the boundary parametrization

z(t):=\varphi(e^{it}),\qquad 0\leq t\leq 2\pi,

is absolutely continuous, and

\int_{0}^{2\pi}|(\varphi^{\prime})^{*}(e^{it})|\,dt<\infty,\qquad ds=|z_{t}(t)|\,dt=|(\varphi^{\prime})^{*}(e^{it})|\,dt\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in[0,2\pi],

where
(\varphi^{\prime})^{*}
denotes the a.e. nontangential boundary trace of
\varphi^{\prime}
.

Since
\varphi^{\prime}
has no zeros in
\mathbb{D}
, its canonical factorization has the form

\varphi^{\prime}=S\,\Phi,

where
S
is singular inner and
\Phi
is outer. In accordance with
[
12
, Chapter 10, §10.3]
, a
Smirnov domain
can be characterized equivalently by the condition
S\equiv 1
in the canonical factorization of
\varphi^{\prime}
, or, equivalently, by the requirement that
\varphi^{\prime}
is an outer function.
Rectifiable Jordan domains need not be Smirnov, and we refer to e.g.
[
12
, Chapter 10]
for more information on Smirnov domains.

We first give a comment on Theorem
1.2
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
be one of the domains constructed in
Theorem
1.2
. Then
(
1.4
) yields a weak solution of
(
1.1
).

Proof.

Since
\Omega
is Jordan, for any
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}),
there exists a harmonic extension
H_{\varphi}
of
\varphi|_{\partial\Omega}
. Moreover, since
\Omega
is a quasidisk, one has for
v=\varphi-H_{\varphi}\in W^{1,\,2}_{0}(\Omega)
. Then by applying the integration by parts twice,

\int_{\Omega}u\Delta\varphi\,dA=\int_{\Omega}u\Delta v\,dA=-\int_{\Omega}Du\cdot Dv\,dA=-\int_{\Omega}v\,dA.

Thus, according to the definition of
v
and applying (
1.4
),

\int_{\Omega}u\Delta\varphi\,dA=-\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,dA+\int_{\Omega}H_{\varphi}\,dA\\
=-\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,dA+c\int_{\partial\Omega}H_{\varphi}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}=-\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,dA+c\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi\,d\mathcal{H}^{1},

which is exactly the weak formulation of (
1.1
). Thus it is also an example for non-disk domains satisfying the weak formulation of Serrin.
∎

For a complex-valued function
f\in W^{1,1}_{\rm loc}(\Omega)
, we write

\partial_{z}f=\frac{1}{2}(f_{x}-if_{y}),\qquad\partial_{\bar{z}}f=\frac{1}{2}(f_{x}+if_{y}).

If
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
, then the directional derivative of
f
in the direction
\theta

is

(2.1)

Df(z)\theta=\theta\,\partial_{z}f+\overline{\theta}\,\partial_{\bar{z}}f=2\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\theta\,\partial_{z}f\bigr).

See
[
2
, Section 2.4 and Section 2.9]
.

We also recall Green’s formula in complex notation

[
2
, Theorem 2.9.1]
: if
U\subset\mathbb{C}
is a bounded domain with
rectifiable boundary and
f,g\in W^{1,1}(U)\cap C(\overline{U})
, then

(2.2)

\int_{U}\bigl(\partial_{z}f+\partial_{\bar{z}}g\bigr)\,dA(z)=\frac{i}{2}\int_{\partial U}\bigl(f\,d\overline{z}-g\,dz\bigr).

2.1.
Disk-side analytic tools

We now collect the disk-side tools that will be used in the non-Smirnov construction.
Write the standard normalization

dm(e^{it}):=\frac{dt}{2\pi}\qquad\text{on }\mathbb{T},\qquad da(z):=\frac{dA(z)}{\pi}\qquad\text{on }\mathbb{D},

so that
dm(\mathbb{T})=da(\mathbb{D})=1
. For
z\in\mathbb{D}
and
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
we denote by

P_{z}(\zeta):=\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{|\zeta-z|^{2}}

the normalized Poisson kernel, so that
\int_{\mathbb{T}}P_{z}(\zeta)\,dm(\zeta)=1
. For
h\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T},dm)
we write

P[h](z):=\int_{\mathbb{T}}P_{z}(\zeta)h(\zeta)\,dm(\zeta),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D},

for its Poisson extension. For
G\in L^{1}(\mathbb{D},da)
we define the boundary balayage operator

(2.3)

({\mathcal{T}}G)(\zeta):=\int_{\mathbb{D}}P_{z}(\zeta)G(z)\,da(z),\qquad\zeta\in\mathbb{T}.

We emphasize that
{\mathcal{T}}G
is defined on
\mathbb{T}
, the boundary of
\mathbb{D}
.
Moreover, by Fubini’s theorem,

(2.4)

\int_{\mathbb{D}}P[h](z)G(z)\,da(z)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}h(\zeta)({\mathcal{T}}G)(\zeta)\,dm(\zeta)

whenever
h\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T},dm)
and
G\in L^{1}(\mathbb{D},da)
.

For
0<\alpha<1
we write
C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
for the real Hölder space on
\mathbb{T}
, endowed with the norm

\|h\|_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}:=\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}+\sup_{\xi\neq\eta}\frac{|h(\xi)-h(\eta)|}{|\xi-\eta|^{\alpha}}.

We also use the
4
-fold symmetric mean-zero real-valued function subspace

(2.5)

X_{\alpha}^{4}:=\Bigl\{h\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T};\,\mathbb{R}):h(i\zeta)=h(\zeta)\text{ for all }\zeta\in\mathbb{T},\ \int_{\mathbb{T}}h\,dm=0\Bigr\}.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
0<\alpha<1
. Then the operator
{\mathcal{T}}
defined in (
2.3
) maps
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
boundedly into
C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
. More precisely, there exists
C_{\alpha}>0
such that

(2.6)

\|{\mathcal{T}}G\|_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C_{\alpha}\|G\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})}\qquad\text{for every }G\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}).

Moreover, for every
0<\delta<1
,

(2.7)

\bigl\|{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{1}_{\{1-\delta<|z|<1\}}G)\bigr\|_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C_{\alpha}\|G\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})}\,\delta^{1-\alpha}\log\frac{2}{\delta}.

Proof.

Write
z=re^{i\theta}
and
\zeta=e^{i\phi}
, and in polar coordinates,

P_{r}(t):=\frac{1-r^{2}}{1-2r\cos t+r^{2}}.

Then

({\mathcal{T}}G)(e^{i\phi})=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}P_{r}(\phi-\theta)G(re^{i\theta})\,dm(e^{i\theta})\,2rdr.

The
L^{\infty}
bound in (
2.6
) is immediate from positivity of the Poisson kernel and the normalization
\int_{\mathbb{T}}P_{r}(t)\,dm(e^{it})=1
.

To estimate the Hölder-seminorm, fix
h\in\mathbb{R}
. Since

\|P_{r}(\cdot+h)-P_{r}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T},dm)}\leq 2,

and, by the fundamental theorem of calculus,

\|P_{r}(\cdot+h)-P_{r}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T},dm)}\leq|h|\int_{0}^{1}\|P_{r}^{\prime}(\cdot+sh)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T},\,dm)}\,ds\leq|h|\,\|P_{r}^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T},dm)},

it is enough to bound
\|P_{r}^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}}
. A direct calculation gives

P_{r}^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{2r(1-r^{2})\sin t}{(1-2r\cos t+r^{2})^{2}},

so the standard estimate

(2.8)

\|P_{r}^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T},dm)}\leq\frac{C}{1-r}

holds for some absolute constant
C>0
. Therefore

(2.9)

\|P_{r}(\cdot+h)-P_{r}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T},dm)}\leq C\min\!\left\{1,\frac{|h|}{1-r}\right\}.

Using (
2.9
) and the definition of
{\mathcal{T}}G
,

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{T}}G(e^{i(\phi+h)})-{\mathcal{T}}G(e^{i\phi})|

\displaystyle\leq\|G\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})}\int_{0}^{1}2r\,\|P_{r}(\cdot+h)-P_{r}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T},dm)}\,dr

\displaystyle\leq C\|G\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})}\int_{0}^{1}2r\min\!\left\{1,\frac{|h|}{1-r}\right\}dr

\displaystyle\leq C\|G\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})}\,|h|\log\frac{2}{|h|}.

Since
|h|\log(2/|h|)\leq C_{\alpha}|h|^{\alpha}
for
0<\alpha<1
, this proves (
2.6
).

To prove (
2.7
), set
A_{\delta}:=\{z\in\mathbb{D}:1-\delta<|z|<1\}
. The same argument for the
L^{\infty}
-bound in (
2.6
) yields

\|{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{1}_{A_{\delta}}G)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\|G\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})}\,da(A_{\delta})\leq 2\delta\,\|G\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})}.

Now if
|h|\geq\delta
, then the estimate above gives directly

|{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{1}_{A_{\delta}}G)(e^{i(\phi+h)})-{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{1}_{A_{\delta}}G)(e^{i\phi})|\leq 4\delta\,\|G\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})}.

If
|h|<\delta
, then by (
2.9
)

\displaystyle\quad|{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{1}_{A_{\delta}}G)(e^{i(\phi+h)})-{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{1}_{A_{\delta}}G)(e^{i\phi})|\leq C\|G\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})}\int_{1-\delta}^{1}2r\min\!\left\{1,\frac{|h|}{1-r}\right\}dr

\displaystyle\leq C\|G\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})}\int_{0}^{\delta}\min\!\left\{1,\frac{|h|}{r}\right\}dr\leq C\|G\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})}\,|h|\left(1+\log\frac{\delta}{|h|}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C|G\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})}|h|^{\alpha}|h|^{1-\alpha}\left(1+\log\frac{\delta}{|h|}\right)\leq C_{\alpha}|G\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})}|h|^{\alpha}\delta^{1-\alpha}.

Thus in both cases,

\frac{|{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{1}_{A_{\delta}}G)(e^{i(\phi+h)})-{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{1}_{A_{\delta}}G)(e^{i\phi})|}{|h|^{\alpha}}\leq C_{\alpha}\|G\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})}\,\delta^{1-\alpha}\log\frac{2}{\delta},

which proves (
2.7
).
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
0<\alpha<1
, and let
\{G_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
such that

\sup_{j}\|G_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})}<\infty.

Assume that
G_{j}\to G
locally uniformly in
\mathbb{D}
. Then

{\mathcal{T}}G_{j}\to{\mathcal{T}}G\qquad\text{in }C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}).

The same conclusion remains true if
G_{j}
is replaced by
G_{j}Q_{j}
, provided
\{Q_{j}\}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
and converges locally uniformly.

Proof.

Fix
0<\delta<1
. On the compact disk
(1-\delta)\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, the convergence is uniform, so by (
2.6
)

\bigl\|{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{1}_{\{|z|\leq 1-\delta\}}(G_{j}-G))\bigr\|_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}\to 0.

For the boundary strip
A_{\delta}=\{1-\delta<|z|<1\}
, (
2.7
) gives

\sup_{j}\bigl\|{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{1}_{A_{\delta}}(G_{j}-G))\bigr\|_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C_{\alpha}\sup_{j}\|G_{j}-G\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})}\,\delta^{1-\alpha}\log\frac{2}{\delta}\leq C_{\alpha}^{\prime}\delta^{1-\alpha}\log\frac{2}{\delta}.

Since
\delta
is arbitrary, the claim follows. The final statement can be proved in exactly the same way, since the product of two locally uniformly convergent bounded sequences converges locally uniformly and remains uniformly bounded.
∎

2.2.
The Duren–Shapiro–Shields construction

We next recall a version of the Duren–Shapiro–Shields construction
[
13
]
. Towards this, we firstly introduce the Herglotz transform associated with a finite real Borel measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{T}
:

(2.10)

\mathcal{H}[\nu](z):=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\zeta+z}{\zeta-z}\,d\nu(\zeta),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

Its real part is the Poisson integral of
\nu
,

(2.11)

\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{H}[\nu](z)=P[\nu](z):=\int_{\mathbb{T}}P_{z}(\zeta)\,d\nu(\zeta).

If
d\nu=w\,dm+d\mu
with
w\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T},dm)
real-valued and
\mu
singular, then the inner–outer factorization of
\exp(-\mathcal{H}[\nu])
shows that its radial boundary values satisfy

(2.12)

\bigl|\exp(-\mathcal{H}[\nu])^{*}(\zeta)\bigr|=e^{-w(\zeta)}\qquad\text{for }m\text{-a.e. }\zeta\in\mathbb{T}.

See
[
12
, Chapter 2 and Chapter 10]
and
[
31
, Chapter 7]
.

We also recall the Zygmund class
A^{*}
. A continuous
2\pi
-periodic function
\psi
belongs to
A^{*}
if there exists
A>0
such that

(2.13)

|\psi(t+h)+\psi(t-h)-2\psi(t)|\leq A|h|\qquad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{R},\ h\in\mathbb{R}.

Following the definition in
[
13
]
, a finite
real Borel measure

\nu
on
\mathbb{T}
is said to be of
class
A^{*}
if one (equivalently every) cumulative distribution function of
\nu
satisfies (
2.13
). In particular, if
w\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
and

V(t):=\int_{0}^{t}w(e^{is})\,ds,

then

|V(t+h)-2V(t)+V(t-h)|\leq 2\|w\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}|h|,

so
w\,dm
is automatically of class
A^{*}
. Moreover, there exists a nonzero positive singular measure
\sigma
of class
A^{*}
on
\mathbb{T}
; see particularly the discussion following
[
13
, Theorem 1]
. These facts will be used in the construction of the non-Smirnov domain, and we refer to
[
31
, Section 7.3]
for more information.

The following lemma presents the small-data version of the construction of the class of domains introduced by Duren, Shapiro, and Shields (abbreviated as DSS), and we say these domains are in DSS-class. For the reader’s convenience, we restate the argument of DSS within our present framework. The main idea of their construction is to use the Ahlfors–Weill theorem (see
[
25
, Chapter II, §5.1]
or
[
31
, Theorem 1.12]
) with small boundary data.

Lemma 2.4

(
[
13
]
)
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
with the following property. Let

d\nu:=w\,dm+d\mu

be a finite real Borel measure on
\mathbb{T}
, where
w\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
is real-valued and
\mu
is a nonnegative singular measure of class
A^{*}
. Assume that

\|w\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}+\|\mu\|_{A^{*}}\leq\varepsilon_{0}.

Define

(2.14)

F_{\nu}(z):=\mathcal{H}[\nu](z),\qquad f_{\nu}(z):=\int_{0}^{z}e^{-F_{\nu}(\xi)}\,d\xi.

Then
f_{\nu}
is conformal in
\mathbb{D}
, extends homeomorphically to
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, and maps
\mathbb{D}
onto a rectifiable Jordan domain. Moreover, if
\mu\neq 0
, then the image domain is not Smirnov.

Proof.

[
13
, Formula (6)-(11), Proof of Theorem 1]
shows that the
A^{*}
condition for a measure
\nu
is equivalent to an estimate of the form

(2.15)

|F_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)|\leq\frac{C\|\nu\|_{A^{*}}}{1-|z|},\qquad z\in\mathbb{D},

where
C>0
is an absolute constant and
\|\nu\|_{A^{*}}
denotes any fixed equivalent
A^{*}
norm of the cumulative function of
\nu
. Then by Cauchy’s estimate applied to
F_{\nu}^{\prime}
on the circle of radius
\frac{1+|z|}{2}
,

(2.16)

|F_{\nu}^{\prime\prime}(z)|\leq\frac{C\|\nu\|_{A^{*}}}{(1-|z|)^{2}},\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

Since
f_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)=e^{-F_{\nu}(z)}
, its Schwarzian derivative is

S_{f_{\nu}}=F_{\nu}^{\prime\prime}-\frac{1}{2}(F_{\nu}^{\prime})^{2}.

Combining (
2.15
) and (
2.16
), we obtain

(1-|z|^{2})^{2}|S_{f_{\nu}}(z)|\leq C\|\nu\|_{A^{*}}+C\|\nu\|_{A^{*}}^{2}.

If
\varepsilon_{0}>0
is chosen small enough, then one get

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{D}}(1-|z|^{2})^{2}|S_{f_{\nu}}(z)|\leq 2.

Now one can apply the well-known the Ahlfors–Weill–Nehari criterion to conclude that,
f_{\nu}
is univalent in
\mathbb{D}
and admits a quasiconformal extension to the sphere. In particular,
f_{\nu}
extends homeomorphically to
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, and
f_{\nu}(\mathbb{D})
is a Jordan domain.

The rectifiability criterion in
[
13
, Page 250, Proof of Theorem 1]
now applies: Since the singular part
\mu
is nonnegative and
e^{-w}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T},dm)
(indeed
w\in L^{\infty}
), the boundary curve of
f_{\nu}(\mathbb{D})
is rectifiable. Finally,
[
13
, p. 247]
states that the presence of a non-trivial singular factor is a property of the image domain itself. Therefore, the image domain is not Smirnov as long as
\mu\neq 0
.
∎

3.
The Smirnov case

Since (
1.4
) is invariant under translation, we may assume that

\int_{\Gamma}z\,ds=0.

If
f\in C(\overline{\Omega})
is holomorphic in
\Omega
, then its real and imaginary
parts are harmonic. Hence (
1.4
) implies

(3.1)

c\int_{\Gamma}f\,ds=\int_{\Omega}f\,dA.

In particular, for every analytic polynomial
\mathcal{P}
,

c\int_{\Gamma}\mathcal{P}(z)\,ds=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{P}(z)\,dA(z).

Applying (
2.2
) to
f\equiv 0
and
g(z)=\mathcal{P}(z)\overline{z}
, we also get

\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{P}(z)\,dA(z)=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{\bar{z}}\bigl(\mathcal{P}(z)\overline{z}\bigr)\,dA(z)=\frac{1}{2i}\int_{\Gamma}\mathcal{P}(z)\,\overline{z}\,dz.

Combining the two identities gives

(3.2)

0=\int_{\Gamma}\mathcal{P}(z)\left(c\,ds-\frac{1}{2i}\,\overline{z}\,dz\right)=\int_{\Gamma}\mathcal{P}(z)\,g(z)\,ds,

where

g(z):=c-\frac{1}{2i}\,\overline{z}\,\tau(z),\qquad\tau(z):=\frac{dz}{ds}

is the positively oriented unit tangent to
\Gamma
, defined for
ds
-a.e.

z\in\Gamma
.

Since
dz=\tau\,ds
and
|\tau|=1
, (
3.2
) is equivalently written as

(3.3)

\int_{\Gamma}\mathcal{P}(z)\,b(z)\,dz=0\qquad\text{for every analytic polynomial }\mathcal{P},

where

b(z):=\overline{z}-2ic\,\overline{\tau(z)}.

This is exactly the boundary relation observed by Khavinson in the smooth/Smirnov
setting; see
[
20
, Theorem 1(ii)]
.

It is convenient to use Duren’s notation

E^{1}(\Omega):=\Bigl\{F\in\mathcal{O}(\Omega):(F\circ\varphi)\varphi^{\prime}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{D})\Bigr\},

which is independent of the particular Riemann map
\varphi
; see

[
12
, Chapter 10]
.

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
1.1
, which uses the non-tangential limit of Hardy functions in the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
; see also
[
10
]
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
, and let
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\to\Omega
be the Riemann map
fixed above. By (
3.3
) and

[
12
, Theorem 10.4]
, the Cauchy integral of
b
defines a holomorphic function

F\in E^{1}(\Omega)
whose nontangential boundary values satisfy

(3.4)

F^{*}(z)=b(z)=\overline{z}-2ic\,\overline{\tau(z)}\qquad\text{for }ds\text{-a.e. }z\in\Gamma.

Equivalently,

(3.5)

(F\circ\varphi)\varphi^{\prime}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{D}).

Since
\Omega
is Smirnov,
\varphi^{\prime}
is outer; see
[
12
, Chapter 10,
§10.3]
. Hence (
3.5
) implies that

F\circ\varphi\in N^{+}
.
Here
N^{+}
denotes the Smirnov class in
\mathbb{D}
; see
[
12
, Section 2.5]
.

On the other hand, (
3.4
) shows that

(F\circ\varphi)^{*}(e^{it})=\overline{z(t)}-2ic\,\overline{\tau(t)}\qquad\text{for a.e. }t\in[0,2\pi],

so
(F\circ\varphi)^{*}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
. Therefore

F\circ\varphi\in N^{+}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}),

and
[
12
, Theorem 2.11]
yields

(3.6)

F\circ\varphi\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}).

Let
G
be a primitive of
F
in
\Omega
, so that
G^{\prime}=F
. Then

(G\circ\varphi)^{\prime}=(F\circ\varphi)\varphi^{\prime}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{D}).

By
[
12
, Theorem 3.11]
,
G\circ\varphi
extends continuously to

\overline{\mathbb{D}}
and its boundary values on
\mathbb{T}
are absolutely continuous.

Step 1: A function
u
satisfies Serrin’s overdetermined system
. Define

u(z):=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}G(z)-\frac{1}{4}|z|^{2}+C_{0},

where
C_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
will be chosen later. Since
G
is holomorphic,

(3.7)

\Delta u=-1\qquad\text{in }\Omega.

Also,

(3.8)

4\partial_{z}u=F-\overline{z}.

We next show that the boundary trace of
u
is constant. Set

U(t):=u(z(t))=u(\varphi(e^{it})).

Since
G\circ\varphi
is absolutely continuous on
\mathbb{T}
and
\partial\Omega
is rectifiable, which ensures the absolute continuity of the mapping
t\mapsto\varphi(e^{it})
, it follows that the function
U
is absolutely continuous on
[0,2\pi]
. For a.e.
t
,

U^{\prime}(t)=Du(z(t))\cdot z_{t}(t)=|z_{t}(t)|\,\partial_{\tau}u(z(t)).

By (
2.1
),

\partial_{\tau}u=2\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\tau\,\partial_{z}u\bigr).

Using (
3.8
) and (
3.4
), we obtain

2\partial_{z}u=-ic\,\overline{\tau}\qquad\text{for }ds\text{-a.e. on }\Gamma,

hence

\partial_{\tau}u=2\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\tau\,\partial_{z}u\bigr)=\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\tau(2\partial_{z}u)\bigr)=\operatorname{Re}(-ic)=0\qquad\text{for }ds\text{-a.e. on }\Gamma.

Therefore
U^{\prime}(t)=0
for a.e.
t
. Since
U
is absolutely continuous, it must be constant.
Thus by choosing
C_{0}
suitably, we may assume that

(3.9)

u=0\qquad\text{on }\Gamma.

Let
\nu_{\rm in}=i\tau
and
\nu_{\rm out}=-i\tau
denote the inward and outward unit
normals to
\Gamma
. Again by (
3.8
) and
(
3.4
),

\partial_{\nu_{\rm in}}u=2\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\nu_{\rm in}\partial_{z}u\bigr)=\operatorname{Re}\bigl(i\tau(2\partial_{z}u)\bigr)=\operatorname{Re}\bigl(i\tau(-ic\,\overline{\tau})\bigr)=c

for
ds
-a.e. point of
\Gamma
. Thus

(3.10)

\partial_{\nu_{\rm in}}u=c\qquad\text{for }ds\text{-a.e. on }\Gamma.

Step 2: A maximum principle for
|Du|
.
Now we are ready to follow the argument of Weinberger
[
35
]
.
Let us pull everything back to the unit disk. Define

v:=u\circ\varphi,\qquad\widetilde{F}:=F\circ\varphi,\qquad W:=\widetilde{F}-\overline{\varphi}.

By (
3.6
),
\widetilde{F}\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
, so
W
is bounded
and harmonic in
\mathbb{D}
. By Fatou’s theorem for bounded analytic functions

[
12
, Theorem 1.3]
,
\widetilde{F}
has a.e. nontangential boundary values on

\mathbb{T}
, and (
3.4
) yields

(3.11)

W(re^{it})\longrightarrow W^{\ast}(e^{it}),\quad W^{*}(e^{it})=-2ic\,\overline{\tau(t)}\qquad\text{for a.e. }t\in[0,2\pi].

Hence

|W^{*}|=2c\qquad\text{a.e. on }\mathbb{T}.

Since a bounded harmonic function is the Poisson integral of its boundary values,

|W(w)|=\left|\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}P_{w}(e^{it})\,W^{*}(e^{it})\,dt\right|\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}P_{w}(e^{it})\,|W^{*}(e^{it})|\,dt\leq 2c

for every
w\in\mathbb{D}
.
Furthermore, by the chain rule and (
3.8
),

(3.12)

4\partial_{w}v(w)=\bigl(4\partial_{z}u\bigr)(\varphi(w))\,\varphi^{\prime}(w)=\bigl(F(\varphi(w))-\overline{\varphi(w)}\bigr)\varphi^{\prime}(w)=W(w)\varphi^{\prime}(w).

Therefore

(3.13)

|Du(\varphi(w))|=\frac{|Dv(w)|}{|\varphi^{\prime}(w)|}=\frac{2|\partial_{w}v(w)|}{|\varphi^{\prime}(w)|}=\frac{|W(w)|}{2}\leq c\qquad\text{for every }w\in\mathbb{D}.

Moreover, since
v
is continuous on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}

and
v=0
on
\mathbb{T}
, we have
v\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\mathbb{D})
. By the conformal
invariance of the Dirichlet integral,
u\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
.
In particular,

u\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)\cap W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega).

By (
3.7
), (
3.9
), and the maximum
principle,

(3.14)

u>0\qquad\text{in }\Omega.

We next show the boundary convergence for the radial derivative of

v=u\circ\varphi
. Recall that

4\partial_{z}u=F-\bar{z},\quad\widetilde{F}:=F\circ\varphi,\quad W(w):=\widetilde{F}(w)-\overline{\varphi(w)},

together with (
3.11
) and (
3.12
).
Since
\varphi^{\prime}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{D})
, we have

\varphi^{\prime}(re^{it})\longrightarrow(\varphi^{\prime})^{\ast}(e^{it})\quad\text{in }L^{1}(\mathbb{T})

as
r\to 1
. Also, as
W
is bounded in the sense
\sup_{0<r<1}\|W(re^{it})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}<\infty
,
(
3.11
) implies

W(re^{it})\varphi^{\prime}(re^{it})\longrightarrow W^{\ast}(e^{it})(\varphi^{\prime})^{\ast}(e^{it})\quad\text{in }L^{1}(\mathbb{T});

indeed, as
r\to 1
,

\displaystyle\left\|W(re^{it})\varphi^{\prime}(re^{it})-W^{\ast}(e^{it})(\varphi^{\prime})^{\ast}(e^{it})\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle\leq\|W(re^{it})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\left\|\varphi^{\prime}(re^{it})-(\varphi^{\prime})^{\ast}(e^{it})\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle+\left\|\bigl(W(re^{it})-W^{\ast}(e^{it})\bigr)(\varphi^{\prime})^{\ast}(e^{it})\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\longrightarrow 0.

Now for real-valued
v
,

\partial_{r}v(re^{it})=2\operatorname{Re}\left(e^{it}\partial_{w}v(re^{it})\right)=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\left(e^{it}W(re^{it})\varphi^{\prime}(re^{it})\right).

Hence

\partial_{r}v(re^{it})\longrightarrow\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\left(e^{it}W^{\ast}(e^{it})(\varphi^{\prime})^{\ast}(e^{it})\right)\quad\text{in }L^{1}(\mathbb{T}).

Finally, since

\tau(t)=\frac{z_{t}(t)}{|z_{t}(t)|}=\frac{ie^{it}(\varphi^{\prime})^{\ast}(e^{it})}{|(\varphi^{\prime})^{\ast}(e^{it})|},

we have

\overline{\tau(t)}=\frac{-ie^{-it}\overline{(\varphi^{\prime})^{\ast}(e^{it})}}{|(\varphi^{\prime})^{\ast}(e^{it})|}.

Using (
3.11
) again, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\left(e^{it}W^{\ast}(e^{it})(\varphi^{\prime})^{\ast}(e^{it})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\left(e^{it}(-2ic\,\overline{\tau(t)})(\varphi^{\prime})^{\ast}(e^{it})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\left(-2c\,|(\varphi^{\prime})^{\ast}(e^{it})|\right)

\displaystyle=-c\,|(\varphi^{\prime})^{\ast}(e^{it})|.

Consequently,

(3.15)

\partial_{r}v(re^{it})\longrightarrow-c\,|(\varphi^{\prime})^{\ast}(e^{it})|\quad\text{in }L^{1}(\mathbb{T})

as
r\to 1
.

Step 3: The volume identity.

We next pull back (
1.4
) to the unit disk.
If
H
is harmonic in
\mathbb{D}
and continuous on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, then

h:=H\circ\varphi^{-1}
is harmonic in
\Omega
and continuous on
\overline{\Omega}
.
Applying (
1.4
) to
h
and changing variables, we obtain

(3.16)

\int_{\mathbb{D}}H(w)\,|\varphi^{\prime}(w)|^{2}\,dA(w)=c\int_{0}^{2\pi}H(e^{it})\,|(\varphi^{\prime})^{*}(e^{it})|\,dt.

Set

I:=\int_{\Omega}u\,dA=\int_{\mathbb{D}}v(w)\,|\varphi^{\prime}(w)|^{2}\,dA(w),

M:=\int_{\Omega}|z|^{2}\,dA(z)=\int_{\mathbb{D}}|\varphi(w)|^{2}\,|\varphi^{\prime}(w)|^{2}\,dA(w),

and

B:=\int_{\Gamma}|z|^{2}\,ds=\int_{0}^{2\pi}|\varphi(e^{it})|^{2}\,|(\varphi^{\prime})^{*}(e^{it})|\,dt.

Let

g(w):=|\varphi(w)|^{2}.

Since
\varphi
is holomorphic,

\Delta g=4|\varphi^{\prime}|^{2}\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{D}.

Since
v=0
on
\mathbb{T}
,
\varphi\in H^{\infty}
, one can pass
r\to 1
in Green’s formula (
2.2
) and get

(3.17)

\int_{\mathbb{D}}Dv\cdot Dg\,dA=-4\int_{\mathbb{D}}v|\varphi^{\prime}|^{2}\,dA=-4I.

Moreover, since
\partial_{r}v\to-c|(\varphi^{\prime})^{*}|
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
according to (
3.15
),

\int_{\mathbb{D}}Dv\cdot Dg\,dA=\int_{\mathbb{D}}g|\varphi^{\prime}|^{2}\,dA-c\int_{0}^{2\pi}|\varphi(e^{it})|^{2}|(\varphi^{\prime})^{*}(e^{it})|\,dt=M-cB.

Thus,

(3.18)

4I+M=cB.

Next define the bounded harmonic function

J(w):=\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\varphi(w)\widetilde{F}(w)\bigr).

Since
J
need not extend continuously to
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, we approximate it by

\widetilde{F}_{r}(w):=\widetilde{F}(rw),\qquad J_{r}(w):=\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\varphi(w)\widetilde{F}_{r}(w)\bigr),\qquad 0<r<1.

Each
J_{r}
is harmonic in
\mathbb{D}
and continuous on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, so
(
3.16
) applies to
J_{r}
. Since

\widetilde{F}\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
, the family
\{J_{r}\}_{0<r<1}
is uniformly bounded,

J_{r}\to J
locally uniformly in
\mathbb{D}
, and

J_{r}(e^{it})\to J^{*}(e^{it})
for a.e.
t
. Hence dominated convergence yields

(3.19)

\int_{\mathbb{D}}J(w)\,|\varphi^{\prime}(w)|^{2}\,dA(w)=c\int_{0}^{2\pi}J^{*}(e^{it})\,|(\varphi^{\prime})^{*}(e^{it})|\,dt.

We first compute the boundary term in (
3.19
). On
\mathbb{T}
,

\widetilde{F}^{*}(e^{it})=\overline{\varphi(e^{it})}-2ic\,\overline{\tau(t)},

hence

J^{*}(e^{it})=|\varphi(e^{it})|^{2}+\operatorname{Re}\bigl(-2ic\,\varphi(e^{it})\overline{\tau(t)}\bigr).

If we identify
z=x_{1}+ix_{2}
with
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, then

x\cdot\nu_{\rm out}=\operatorname{Re}(z\overline{\nu}_{\rm out})
, and since
\nu_{\rm out}=-i\tau
,

\operatorname{Re}\bigl(-2ic\,z\,\overline{\tau}\bigr)=-2c\,x\cdot\nu_{\rm out}.

Therefore

c\int_{0}^{2\pi}J^{*}(e^{it})\,|(\varphi^{\prime})^{*}(e^{it})|\,dt=cB-2c^{2}\int_{\Gamma}x\cdot\nu_{\rm out}\,ds.

By the divergence theorem,

\int_{\Gamma}x\cdot\nu_{\rm out}\,ds=\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{div}x\,dA=2|\Omega|.

Hence

(3.20)

\int_{\mathbb{D}}J(w)\,|\varphi^{\prime}(w)|^{2}\,dA(w)=cB-4c^{2}|\Omega|.

We next compute the interior term (
3.19
). Since

\widetilde{F}=\overline{\varphi}+W
,

J|\varphi^{\prime}|^{2}=|\varphi|^{2}|\varphi^{\prime}|^{2}+\operatorname{Re}(\varphi W)\,|\varphi^{\prime}|^{2}.

Also,

\partial_{w}g=\varphi^{\prime}(w)\,\overline{\varphi(w)},\qquad\partial_{\overline{w}}g=\overline{\varphi^{\prime}(w)}\,\varphi(w),

and, since
W\varphi^{\prime}=4\partial_{w}v
,

Dv\cdot Dg=4\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\partial_{w}v\,\partial_{\overline{w}}g\bigr)=4\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\partial_{w}v\,\overline{\varphi^{\prime}}\,\varphi\bigr)=\operatorname{Re}(\varphi W)\,|\varphi^{\prime}|^{2}.

Thus

\int_{\mathbb{D}}J|\varphi^{\prime}|^{2}\,dA=M+\int_{\mathbb{D}}Dv\cdot Dg\,dA.

Therefore, (
3.17
) implies

(3.21)

\int_{\mathbb{D}}J(w)\,|\varphi^{\prime}(w)|^{2}\,dA(w)=M-4I.

Comparing (
3.20
) and (
3.21
), we obtain

(3.22)

M-4I=cB-4c^{2}|\Omega|.

Subtracting (
3.22
) from
(
3.18
) gives

(3.23)

2\int_{\Omega}u\,dA=c^{2}|\Omega|.

Step 4: Weinberger’s
P
-function argument.

According to
[
35
]
, this follows immediately after applying (
3.13
) and (
3.23
). We omit the details here. This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Non-Smirnov case

We now turn to the non-Smirnov side and use the Duren–Shapiro–Shields (DSS) construction to show that the strong quadrature identity (
1.4
) behaves very differently from the one-pole pseudosphere condition of Lewis–Vogel.

The proposition below rules out the most naive DSS-class, namely a fixed purely singular branch, which was considered by
[
24
]
. This means that one cannot expect simple non-Smirnov domains satisfying (
1.4
).

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\mu
be a nonzero positive singular measure of class
A^{*}
on
\mathbb{T}
, and define

F_{\mu}(z):=\mathcal{H}[\mu](z),\qquad f_{a}(z):=\int_{0}^{z}e^{-aF_{\mu}(\xi)}\,d\xi,\qquad a>0.

Suppose that there exists a sequence
a_{j}\downarrow 0
such that each
f_{a_{j}}
is conformal in
\mathbb{D}
, extends homeomorphically to
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, and maps
\mathbb{D}
onto a rectifiable Jordan domain
\Omega_{a_{j}}
. Assume that for every
j
there exists
c_{j}>0
such that

(4.1)

\int_{\Omega_{a_{j}}}h\,dA=c_{j}\int_{\partial\Omega_{a_{j}}}h\,ds\qquad\text{for every }h\in C(\overline{\Omega_{a_{j}}}),\ \Delta h=0.

Then
\mu=0
. In particular, no nontrivial fixed pure-singular DSS branch can produce a perturbative family of domains satisfying (
1.4
).

Proof.

For each
j
set
f_{j}:=f_{a_{j}}
and
\Omega_{j}:=\Omega_{a_{j}}
. Since
a_{j}\mu
is purely singular, (
2.12
) gives

(4.2)

|f_{j}^{\prime*}(\zeta)|=1\qquad\text{for }m\text{-a.e. }\zeta\in\mathbb{T}.

Let
H\in C(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
be harmonic in
\mathbb{D}
and set
h:=H\circ f_{j}^{-1}
. Then
h\in C(\overline{\Omega_{j}})
is harmonic in
\Omega_{j}
. Changing variables in (
4.1
) and using (
4.2
), we obtain

(4.3)

\int_{\mathbb{D}}H(z)e^{-2a_{j}P[\mu](z)}\,da(z)=2{c_{j}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}H(\zeta)\,dm(\zeta)

for every harmonic
H
on
\mathbb{D}
.

Fix
n\geq 1
and take
H(z)=z^{n}
. Since
\int_{\mathbb{T}}\zeta^{n}\,dm(\zeta)=0
, (
4.3
) yields

M_{n}(a_{j}):=\int_{\mathbb{D}}z^{n}e^{-2a_{j}P[\mu](z)}\,da(z)=0\qquad\text{for every }j.

As
P[\mu]\in L^{1}(\mathbb{D},da)
and

\frac{1-e^{-2aP[\mu](z)}}{a}\to 2P[\mu](z)\qquad\text{for a.e. }z\in\mathbb{D},

dominated convergence gives

M_{n}^{\prime}(0)=-2\int_{\mathbb{D}}z^{n}P[\mu](z)\,da(z).

Since
M_{n}(a_{j})=0
along a sequence converging to
0
and

M_{n}(0)=\int_{\mathbb{D}}z^{n}\,da(z)=0,

we get
M_{n}^{\prime}(0)=0
and therefore

(4.4)

\int_{\mathbb{D}}z^{n}P[\mu](z)\,da(z)=0\qquad\text{for every }n\geq 1.

Using (
2.11
), Fubini’s theorem, and the identity

\int_{\mathbb{D}}z^{n}P_{z}(\zeta)\,da(z)=\frac{\zeta^{n}}{n+1}\qquad\text{for }\zeta\in\mathbb{T},

we obtain from (
4.4
)

0=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{D}}z^{n}P_{z}(\zeta)\,da(z)\right)d\mu(\zeta)=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\zeta^{n}\,d\mu(\zeta)\qquad(n\geq 1).

Thus all positive Fourier coefficients of
\mu
vanish. Since
\mu
is a real finite measure, all negative Fourier coefficients also vanish. Therefore
\mu
is a constant multiple of Lebesgue measure. Since
\mu
is singular, this constant must be zero. Hence
\mu=0
, a contradiction.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Proposition
4.1
indicates that, the equation (
1.5
) for pseudoballs is very difference from the strong quadrature identity (
1.4
). Indeed, pseudoballs are compatible with a pure singular branch, while the strong quadrature identity is not.

Moreover, observe that disk has a
2
-dimensional family of Möbius-center changes, which means the linearized quadrature equation around the disk has an extra finite-dimensional kernel generated by the infinitesimal symmetry group of the disk. This also makes a big difference from the pseudoball case. Then, instead of performing a Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction to quotient out those symmetry directions, we impose a symmetry class below so that those directions are forbidden. This gives us the desired counterexample below.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Choose a nonzero positive singular measure
\sigma
of class
A^{*}
on
\mathbb{T}
. Consider the
4
-fold symmetrization of
\sigma
defined by

\mu:=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=0}^{3}(R_{\pi k/2})_{\#}\sigma,

where
R_{\pi k/2}(\zeta)=i^{k}\zeta
. Then
\mu
is again nonzero, positive, singular, and of class
A^{*}
, together with its Fourier coefficients satisfying

(4.5)

\widehat{\mu}(n)=0\qquad\text{whenever }4\nmid n.

Set

M_{\mu}:=P[\mu].

Fix
0<\alpha<1
and let
X_{\alpha}^{4}
be the Banach space defined in (
2.5
). For every real-valued
W\in X_{\alpha}^{4}
and
a\geq 0
, consider the nonlinear operator

G_{W,a}(z):=\exp\bigl(-2(P[W](z)+aM_{\mu}(z))\bigr),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D},

which corresponds to the interior Jacobian density (see (
4.13
) below), and define

(4.6)

\Psi(W,a):=\log{\mathcal{T}}(G_{W,a})+W-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(\log{\mathcal{T}}(G_{W,a})+W\bigr)\,dm.

Note that, since
\mu
is a positive mesure, then
M_{\mu}\geq 0
. In addition, since
a>0
, the defitnion of
G_{W,a}
yields

0<G_{W,a}(z)\leq e^{2\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}}\qquad\text{for all }z\in\mathbb{D}.

Moreover, if
|z|\leq 1/2
, then

M_{\mu}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}P_{z}(\zeta)\,d\mu(\zeta)\leq 3\mu(\mathbb{T}),

since
P_{z}(\zeta)\leq 3
when
|z|\leq 1/2
. Therefore

G_{W,a}(z)\geq e^{-2\|W\|_{L^{\infty}}-6a\mu(\mathbb{T})}\qquad\text{for }|z|\leq 1/2.

Again by
P_{z}(\zeta)\geq 1/3
for
|z|\leq 1/2
, we obtain the uniform lower bound

(4.7)

{\mathcal{T}}(G_{W,a})(\zeta)\geq\frac{1}{3}e^{-2\|W\|_{L^{\infty}}-6a\mu(\mathbb{T})}\,da(D(0,1/2))>0\qquad\text{for every }\zeta\in\mathbb{T}.

Hence the logarithm in (
4.6
) is well defined. In addition, by Lemmas
2.2
and
2.3
, the map
(W,a)\mapsto\Psi(W,a)
is continuous from
X_{\alpha}^{4}\times[0,\infty)
to
X_{\alpha}^{4}
.

Since
P[W]
and
M_{\mu}
are
4
-fold symmetric, and due to the rotation invariance of the Poisson kernel,
\Psi(W,a)
belongs to
X_{\alpha}^{4}
whenever
W\in X_{\alpha}^{4}
.
We claim that there exists
a_{0}>0
and a unique continuous map

[0,a_{0})\ni a\mapsto W(a)\in X_{\alpha}^{4},\qquad W(0)=0,

such that

(4.8)

\Psi(W(a),a)=0\qquad\text{for every }0\leq a<a_{0}.

This is proven by a contraction argument.

Step 1: Regularity of the nonlinear operator.

For fixed
a
, the map
W\mapsto\Psi(W,a)
is Fréchet differentiable. Indeed, since
P[H]
is bounded whenever
H\in X_{\alpha}^{4}
,

D_{W}G_{W,a}[H]=-2G_{W,a}P[H],

and a direct chain-rule computation gives

(4.9)

D_{W}\Psi(W,a)[H]=H-2\left(\frac{{\mathcal{T}}(G_{W,a}P[H])}{{\mathcal{T}}(G_{W,a})}-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{{\mathcal{T}}(G_{W,a}P[H])}{{\mathcal{T}}(G_{W,a})}\,dm\right).

Again by Lemma
2.2
and Lemma
2.3
, the map
(W,a)\mapsto D_{W}\Psi(W,a)
is continuous as a map into
\mathcal{L}(X_{\alpha}^{4},X_{\alpha}^{4})
.

Step 2: The linearized operator.

At
(W,a)=(0,0)
we have
G_{0,0}\equiv 1
, so (
4.9
) becomes

D_{W}\Psi(0,0)=I-2K,\qquad K:={\mathcal{T}}\circ P.

For the Fourier mode
{\bf e}_{n}(\zeta)=\zeta^{n}
, according to the normalized measure, we have

P[{\bf e}_{n}](re^{i\theta})=r^{|n|}e^{in\theta},

and

\displaystyle K({\bf e}_{n})(e^{i\varphi})=

\displaystyle\ \int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left(\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}r^{|\ell|}e^{i\ell(\theta-\varphi)}\right)r^{|n|}e^{in\theta}\,dm(\theta)2r\,dr

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ 2e^{in\varphi}\int_{0}^{1}r^{|n|+1}\,dr=\frac{1}{|n|+1}e^{in\varphi}=\frac{1}{|n|+1}{\bf e}_{n}(e^{i\varphi}).

Thus

K({\bf e}_{n})=\frac{1}{|n|+1}{\bf e}_{n}.

Since functions in
X_{\alpha}^{4}
have only Fourier modes
n\in 4{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}
, all eigenvalues of
I-2K
on
X_{\alpha}^{4}
are

\frac{|n|-1}{|n|+1}>0.

Thus the kernel of
I-2K
on
X_{\alpha}^{4}
is trivial. On the other hand,
K
is compact on
X_{\alpha}^{4}
since

K:X_{\alpha}^{4}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})\xrightarrow{\ P\ }L^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})\xrightarrow{\ {\mathcal{T}}}C^{\beta}(\mathbb{T})\hookrightarrow X_{\alpha}^{4}

for every
\beta\in(\alpha,1)
, where the last embedding is compact. Hence
I-2K
is invertible on
X_{\alpha}^{4}
by the Fredholm alternative. Denote its inverse by
A^{-1}
.

Step 3: Contraction.

Now we run Banach fixed point theorem to find a solution curve
W(a),a\in(0,\,a_{0}].

Recall
\Psi(0,0)=0
and
(W,a)\mapsto D_{W}\Psi(W,a)
is continuous, there exist
\epsilon_{0}>0
and
a_{0}>0
such that

(4.10)

\|A^{-1}(D_{W}\Psi(W,a)-A)\|_{\mathcal{L}(X_{\alpha}^{4})}\leq\frac{1}{2}\qquad\text{whenever }\|W\|_{X_{\alpha}^{4}}\leq\epsilon_{0},\,0\leq a\leq a_{0},

where
A:=D_{W}\Psi(0,0)=I-2K
. Shrinking
a_{0}
if necessary, continuity of
a\mapsto\Psi(0,a)
and
\Psi(0,0)=0
also yield

(4.11)

\|A^{-1}\Psi(0,a)\|_{X_{\alpha}^{4}}\leq\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{2}\qquad\text{for }0\leq a\leq a_{0}.

For
0\leq a\leq a_{0}
define

\Gamma_{a}(W):=W-A^{-1}\Psi(W,a).

If
\|W\|_{X_{\alpha}^{4}}\leq\epsilon_{0}\ll 1
, then by the fundamental theorem of calculus along the segment
t\mapsto tW
in the Banach spaces
X^{4}_{\alpha}
,

\Psi(W,a)-\Psi(0,a)=\int_{0}^{1}D_{W}\Psi(tW,a)[W],dt.

Thus, by writing

\Gamma_{a}(W)=-A^{-1}\Psi(0,a)+\left[W-A^{-1}(\Psi(W,a)-\Psi(0,a))\right],

we conclude from (
4.10
) and (
4.11
) that

\displaystyle\|\Gamma_{a}(W)\|_{X_{\alpha}^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq\|A^{-1}\Psi(0,a)\|_{X_{\alpha}^{4}}+\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\|A^{-1}(D_{W}\Psi(tW,a)-A)\|\,\|W\|_{X_{\alpha}^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|W\|_{X_{\alpha}^{4}}\leq\epsilon_{0}.

Thus
\Gamma_{a}
maps the closed ball
\overline{B}_{\epsilon_{0}}\subset X_{\alpha}^{4}
to itself. Furthermore,
W_{1},W_{2}\in\overline{B}_{\epsilon_{0}}
, then again by the fundamental theorem of calculus and (
4.10
),

\displaystyle\|\Gamma_{a}(W_{1})-\Gamma_{a}(W_{2})\|_{X_{\alpha}^{4}}\leq

\displaystyle\ \sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|A^{-1}(D_{W}\Psi(W_{2}+t(W_{1}-W_{2}),a)-A)\|\|W_{1}-W_{2}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{2}\|W_{1}-W_{2}\|_{X_{\alpha}^{4}}.

Therefore
\Gamma_{a}
is a contraction on
\overline{B}_{\epsilon_{0}}
. Thus, according to the Banach fixed-point theorem, there exists a unique
W(a)\in\overline{B}_{\epsilon_{0}}
such that

\Gamma_{a}(W(a))=W(a),

that is, (
4.8
) holds. Then the continuity of
a\mapsto W(a)
follows from the standard parameter dependence of contractions.

Now since functions in
X_{\alpha}^{4}
are
4
-fold symmetric and have mean zero, (
4.8
) is equivalent to the existence of a positive constant
C(a)
such that

(4.12)

{\mathcal{T}}\bigl(G_{W(a),a}\bigr)=C(a)e^{-W(a)}\qquad\text{on }\mathbb{T}.

Step 4: Build the DSS datum.

Define a real Borel measure on
\mathbb{T}
by

d\nu_{a}:=W(a)\,dm+a\,d\mu.

As noted in Section 2, the absolutely continuous part
W(a)\,dm
is of class
A^{*}
, while the singular part is
a\mu
. Since
W(a)\to 0
in
X^{4}_{\alpha}
, in particular

\|W(a)\|_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}\to 0.

as
a\to 0
, the
A^{*}
norm of
\nu_{a}
tends to
0
as well. Hence Lemma
2.4
applies for all sufficiently small
a>0
. Define

F_{a}:=\mathcal{H}[\nu_{a}],\qquad f_{a}(z):=\int_{0}^{z}e^{-F_{a}(\xi)}\,d\xi,\qquad\Omega_{a}:=f_{a}(\mathbb{D}).

Then
f_{a}
is conformal in
\mathbb{D}
, extends homeomorphically to
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, and
\Omega_{a}
is a bounded rectifiable Jordan domain.

By (
2.11
) and (
2.12
),

(4.13)

|f_{a}^{\prime}(z)|^{2}=e^{-2(P[W(a)](z)+aM_{\mu}(z))}=G_{W(a),a}(z)\qquad z\in\mathbb{D},

and

|f_{a}^{\prime*}(\zeta)|=e^{-W(a)(\zeta)}\qquad\text{for }m\text{-a.e. }\zeta\in\mathbb{T}.

Therefore (
4.12
) can be rewritten as

(4.14)

{\mathcal{T}}(|f_{a}^{\prime}|^{2})=C(a)|f_{a}^{\prime*}|\qquad\text{on }\mathbb{T}.

Step 5: Conclusion.

Let
H_{h}\in C(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
be harmonic in
\mathbb{D}
with
h
being its boundary trace on
\mathbb{T}
. Using (
2.4
) and (
4.14
), we get

\int_{\mathbb{D}}H_{h}(z)|f_{a}^{\prime}(z)|^{2}\,da(z)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}h(\zeta){\mathcal{T}}(|f_{a}^{\prime}|^{2})(\zeta)\,dm(\zeta)=C(a)\int_{\mathbb{T}}h(\zeta)|f_{a}^{\prime*}(\zeta)|\,dm(\zeta).

Now let
G:=H_{h}\circ f_{a}^{-1}
. Since
f_{a}
extends homeomorphically to
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, we have
G\in C(\overline{\Omega_{a}})
, and
G
is harmonic in
\Omega_{a}
. Applying changing variables in the last identity and recalling that
da=dA/\pi
and
ds=2\pi|f_{a}^{\prime*}|\,dm
, we obtain

\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\Omega_{a}}G\,dA=\frac{C(a)}{2\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega_{a}}G\,ds.

Hence

\int_{\Omega_{a}}G\,dA=c(a)\int_{\partial\Omega_{a}}G\,ds,\qquad\text{ with }\ c(a):=\frac{C(a)}{2}.

Conversely, if
G\in C(\overline{\Omega_{a}})
is harmonic in
\Omega_{a}
, then

H:=G\circ f_{a}

belongs to
C(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
and is harmonic in
\mathbb{D}
.
Thus the arbitrariness of
G
yields (
1.4
).

Finally, the singular part of
\nu_{a}
is
a\mu\neq 0
for every
a>0
, so Lemma
2.4
implies that
\Omega_{a}
is not a Smirnov domain. As
\mu
and
W(a)
are
4
-fold symmetric, the same is true of
f_{a}
and
\Omega_{a}
. The proof is complete.
∎
