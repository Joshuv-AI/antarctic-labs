Large Language Model as Token Compressor and Decompressor
Wenbing LiZikai SongJielei ZhangTianhao ZhaoJunkai LinHuipeng GuoYiran Wang, Wei Yang

Abstract
In this paper, we establish the novel insight that an off-the-shelf LLM can function as an excellent token compressor and decompressor. To demonstrate, we design a self-expressive autoencoding learning framework fine-tunes a pretrained LLM to translate long texts into a compact internal language of discrete, variable-length latent codes, termed Z-tokens, and to reconstruct the original text exactly from them. The resulting representation is content-adaptive: semantically dense segments receive more Z-tokens, while redundant or predictable regions are aggressively compressed, via lightweight LoRA-based adapter heads. Empirically, our method achieves up to 18 × \times token reduction on Wikipedia, CNN/DailyMail, HotpotQA, and Qulac-style long-query datasets, while preserving reconstruction fidelity and downstream performance. This simple yet effective design supports applications including prompt compression and autoregressive generation directly in the Z-token space, offering a potential pathway toward token-efficient long-context reasoning.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable success in various fields
Brown et al. 2020
;
Touvron et al. 2023
. However, their ability to handle long contexts remains fundamentally limited by the quadratic complexity of the attention mechanism
Vaswani et al. 2023
. As sequence lengths grow to tens or even hundreds of thousands of tokens, the computational and memory overhead of the attention mechanism makes large-scale inference, long document understanding, and multi-hop retrieval prohibitively expensive.

To mitigate this, significant research has focused on token compression, which aims to distill lengthy sequences into shorter, information-dense representations
Ge et al. 2024
;
Snell et al. 2022
;
Chevalier et al. 2023
;
Mu et al. 2024
.
Existing approaches can be broadly categorized into two categories. Heuristic token merging methods, such as similarity-based merging
Bolya et al. 2023
;
Zhong et al. 2025
, attention-based pruning
Kim et al. 2022
, and saliency-guided aggregation
Li et al. 2023
, attempt to remove or merge redundant tokens based on local similarity or attention importance scores. While these techniques are effective in shortening sequence lengths,
tokens that appear redundant due to similarity may carry discourse or pragmatic meaning that is difficult to recover. The second category includes learning-based or autoencoding methods such as AutoCompressor
Chevalier et al. 2023
, ICAE
Ge et al. 2024
, or Gist Tokens
Mu et al. 2024
. AutoCompressor and ICAE generate fixed-length compressed context vectors that can be re-expanded for downstream tasks by training auxiliary networks or soft embedding modules. Gist Tokens force the model to compress cues into fixed-length lexical units through an attention masking mechanism.
These methods often treat compression as an external, auxiliary task, decoupled from the LLM’s primary generative reasoning process. The resulting continuous embeddings or latent codes are non-lexical and lack transparency, making them difficult to interpret or control.
Moreover, their reliance on fixed compression ratios prevents adaptation to varying information density and typically forgoes faithful reconstruction of the original input, limiting their applicability in scenarios that demand verifiability or fine-grained access to the source content.

In this work, we depart from prevailing heuristic and learned-embedding approaches by introducing a novel insight: leveraging the semantic knowledge of the LLM to serve as both a token compressor and decompressor. Our method is predicated on the fundamental insight that LLMs, trained on vast corpora, are not merely statistical models but powerful engines for semantic abstraction and conceptual distillation
Wei et al. 2022
;
Bommasani et al. 2022
;
Delétang et al. 2024
. We observe that natural language is inherently compressive; it exhibits a strong tendency to encode complex ideas into compact lexical units. Idioms such as “double-edged sword” encapsulate intricate trade-offs, and metaphors like “tip of the iceberg” summarize extensive underlying complexities. This phenomenon implies that LLMs itself is capable of efficient compression.
Methodologically, we propose an autoencoding training paradigm, where an input sequence
X
is mapped into a compressed sequence of discrete Z-tokens,
Z
, by a LLM compressor, and subsequently reconstructed or continued by a LLM decompressor. The training objective can be viewed as a form of bilingual translation, where the model learns to ’encode’ natural language into its own abstract, internal language and ’decode’ it back. The compressed latent
\{Z\}
serves as a semantic scaffold, forcing the model to perform its core reasoning—such as logic, inference, and narrative planning—on the compact symbolic representation before projecting it back to the surface token level. This mechanism ensures that compression is an act of semantic distillation rather than rote memorization; the model must first understand the input’s meaning to produce an effective Z-token sequence that supports subsequent generation.
Our design is more flexible, capable of dynamically lengthening compressed text based on semantic density, and seamlessly integrating with sliding windows to support the processing of longer texts. In terms of task design, we support not only text reconstruction and generation but also various downstream tasks such as summarization and long-text QA. We discovered and explained the polysemy of Z-tokens, an interesting phenomenon, and experimentally demonstrated that it is controllable rather than chaotic, providing a novel perspective on text compression.

We conducted extensive experiments on six large-scale datasets—Wikipedia, CNN/DailyMail, HotpotQA, NarrativeQA, QASPER and QuALITY—for tasks such as text reconstruction, continuation writing, summarization, and long-text question answering. The results show that our method achieves state-of-the-art performance on all tasks, improves inference speed by 4.6
\times
, and significantly reduces memory usage, fully validating the effectiveness and efficiency of the method.

While prior works used discrete bottlenecks as auxiliary modules, we internalize this semantic layer within the LLM’s reasoning flow, enabling discrete tokens to serve as learned, interpretable units for compressing and reasoning over long contexts.

3
Methods

In this section, we first present the overall framework, then detail the compressor, decompressor, and inferencer. We further analyze the multi-semanticity of Z-tokens, offering an interpretable view of how they encode and organize information.

Our goal is to adapt a pretrained LLM into a compressor and decompressor. The compression and decompression works as follows: given an input sequence
\mathbf{X}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{N}\}
, the compressor generates a variable-length tokens
\mathbf{Z}=\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,z_{K}\}
, where
K\ll N
.
We consider two usage modes for the decompressor:
1).
Decompressor as inferencer
: the decompressor directly consumes Z-tokens to perform downstream reasoning, so the compressor acts as a learned prompt compressor;
2).
Decompressor as reconstructor
: the decompressor reconstructs surface text from Z-tokens, while a separate LLM inferencer operates in the Z-token space to enable abstract-space reasoning.
Since the compressor maps an
N
-token input to a much shorter Z-token sequence of length
K\ll N
, subsequent attention-based computation scales as
\mathcal{O}(K^{2})
instead of
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
, yielding a theoretical compute and memory saving of approximately
(N/K)^{2}
and enabling faster inference, lower memory usage, and effectively longer contexts.

3.1
LLM Compressor

Given an input token sequence
\mathbf{X}
, the compressor generates a varying length Z-tokens
\mathbf{Z}
. To preserve the prior of pre-trained LLM, we extend the original vocabulary to
[\mathcal{V}_{\text{base}},\mathcal{V}_{z}]
, where
\mathcal{V}_{\text{base}}
is the original vocabulary,
\mathcal{V}_{z}
is the extended vocabulary for Z-Tokens. Z-Tokens only uses
\mathcal{V}_{z}
. Unlike previous work (e.g., ICAE or AutoCompressor) that directly learns fixed-length soft embeddings, our compressor generates
Z
in an autoregressive manner:

p_{\phi}(\mathbf{Z}|\mathbf{X})=\prod_{t=1}^{K}p_{\phi}(z_{t}|\mathbf{X},z_{1}\dots z_{t-1}),

(1)

Therefore, the model can dynamically determine the lexical units needed to generalize a given context, thus achieving variable-length compression, similar to how humans express concepts of varying complexity using flexible sentence lengths. This aligns with the native processing method of LLM, requiring no intrusive modifications and thus forming an elegant and consistent approach.
Compared to previous embedding-based compression methods, our autoregressive design provides the compressor with several key advantages: variable-length compression, allocating more Z-Tokens to complex contexts and fewer to simple contexts; abstract composition by progressively learn to construct semantics in discrete steps, consistent with the sequential nature of language; interpretability, each Z-Token becomes a symbolic unit that can be reused in different contexts, similar to morphemes in natural language. To ensure end-to-end training while maintaining discreteness, we use a Gumbel-Softmax estimator to sample Z-Token only from
\mathcal{V}_{z}
:

z_{t}=\mathbf{GumbelSoftmax}(\mathbf{h}_{t}),\,\,z_{t}\in\mathcal{V}_{z}

(2)

This results in a soft embedding
e_{\text{soft}}
during training. We apply a pass-through estimator to combine differentiable gradients with discrete decoding:

e=e_{\text{hard}}+[e_{\text{soft}}-\mathbf{sg}(e_{\text{soft}})],

(3)

where
e_{\text{hard}}
corresponds to the embedding obtained by argmax. This hybrid technique maintains a stable gradient flow without sacrificing the discretization of the symbols.

Sliding window compression.
For excessively long sequences, our method naturally adapt to sliding windows compression: the input sequence is segmented into overlapping windows of length
W
and stride
S
(typically
W=1024
and
S=256
), each independently compressed into
\mathbf{Z}_{i}
. For adjacent segments, we enforce a consistency constraint on overlapping regions to ensure semantic coherence:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{overlap}}=1-\cos(\mathbf{Z}_{i}^{\text{overlap}},\mathbf{Z}_{i+1}^{\text{overlap}}),

(4)

we can then concatenate tokens of compressed segments
\{\mathbf{Z}_{i}\}
for long-context reconstruction or inference.

3.2
LLM Decompressor

The LLM decompressor serves as the core component for translating the compressed Z-tokens back to meaningful semantic outputs.
Our decompressor supports two distinct usage paradigms: the inferencer and the reconstructor.
For both paradigms, the decompression process is modeled as conditional autoregressive generation, with shared training objectives and constraints that ensure stability and semantic fidelity.
Formally, the decompression process follows the distribution:

p_{\theta}(\mathbf{Y}|\mathbf{Z})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid y_{1}\dots y_{t-1},\mathbf{Z}),

(5)

depending on the use paradigms, the sequence
\mathbf{Y}
varies. For reconstructor task,
\mathbf{Y}
is the input sequence to compressor
\mathbf{X}
. While for inferencer task, such Question-Answering (QA),
\mathbf{Y}
is the response to
\mathbf{X}
.
\mathbf{Z}
represents the Z-Tokens generated by the compressor.
To ensure a linguistic foundation, the decompressor is trained under lexical constraints: in each decoding step, the output logit vector is restricted to within the pre-trained model’s original vocabulary
\mathcal{V}_{\text{base}}
:

\tilde{p}_{\theta}(y_{t}|y_{1}\dots y_{t-1},\mathbf{Z})=\textbf{Softmax}\left(W_{\text{base}}\mathbf{h}_{t}\right),

(6)

where
\mathbf{h}_{t}
represents the decoder’s hidden state at step
t
, and
W_{\text{base}}
is the embedding matrix of the original vocabulary set. This constraint forces the model to use human-understandable vocabulary to express internal abstract representations, effectively translating the private latent “language” back into natural language. Empirical evidence shows this vocabulary constraint improves training stability and ensures semantic fidelity across tasks.

For training, the general form of the loss is a cross-entropy term that maximizes the likelihood of generating correct sequence given the Z-Tokens:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{tr}}=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid y_{1}\dots y_{t-1},\mathbf{Z}),

(7)

Training entirely under teacher forcing introduces exposure bias, where the model learns to rely on true labels never seen during inference. To mitigate this mismatch, we employ a “schedule sampling” strategy: each lexical input is either extracted from the true value with probability
1-p
or from the model’s own previous output with probability
p
. The probability
p
increases linearly during training, allowing the model to gradually transition from TF decoding to free decoding, enhancing robustness to its own errors and improving generalization.

The final training loss for the decompressor integrates the reconstruction loss with the compressor’s regularization terms to ensure end-to-end consistency:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{tr}}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}}+\beta\mathcal{L}_{\text{com}},

(8)

We find that
\lambda\approx 0.1
and
\beta\approx 0.25
achieve stable convergence and consistent latent information utilization.

3.3
Decompressor as Inferencer

The decompressor as inferencer paradigm applies the decompressor to downstream tasks where the compressor acts as a learned prompt compressor. The decompressor performs semantic reasoning based on the Z-token sequence, enabling tasks such as continuation, summarization, and question answering directly based on the compressed Z-Tokens.
This inference process is formulated as:

\text{Inference:}\quad\mathbf{Y}^{*}=\arg\max_{\mathbf{Y}}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{Y}|\mathbf{Z}),

(9)

where
\mathbf{Y}
represents the output of a specific task (e.g., a summary or answer). The same autoregressive mechanism supports both reconstruction and inference, as the Z-tokens encode sufficient semantic information to generalize beyond the original input. This paradigm leverages the computational efficiency of the compressed space to achieve faster inference with lower memory footprint.

3.4
Decompressor as Recontructor

The decompressor as recontructor paradigm positions the decompressor as a reconstructor of the input text to the compressor from Z-tokens, while a separate LLM operates in the Z-token space between compression and reconstruction to enable reasoning purely in the Z-Token space. This design decouples compression from high-level reasoning, allowing the inferencer LLM to focus on high-level semantics without handling redundant surface-level tokens. In this case, we first train the decompressor using Eqn.
8
with the target sequence
\mathbf{Y}
as the compressor input
\mathbf{X}
. Then we compress both the prompt sequence
\mathbf{X}
and the response sequence
\mathbf{Y}
into Z-Token sequences
\mathbf{Z}^{\textbf{p}}
and
\mathbf{Z}^{\textbf{r}}

The separate LLM inferencer conduct autoregressive generation entirely on Z-Tokens, treating them as an internal symbolic language, as:

p_{\psi}(\mathbf{Z}^{\textbf{r}}\mid\mathbf{Z}^{\textbf{p}})=\prod_{t=1}^{|\mathbf{Z}^{\textbf{r}}|}p_{\psi}(\mathbf{Z}^{\textbf{r}}_{t}\mid\mathbf{Z}^{\textbf{p}},z^{\textbf{r}}_{1}\dots z^{\textbf{r}}_{t-1}),

(10)

where
\psi
represents the parameters of the inference LLM trained or adapted for symbolic reasoning. This allows operations such as summarization, continuation, and question answering to be performed in the latent space without returning to surface tokens.

A key advantage of reasoning in the Z-Token space lies in its semantic density and computational efficiency. Each Z-Token encodes multiple surface tokens, enabling the model to infer long-range dependencies with significantly reduced context length.

Essentially, this paradigm treats Z-Tokens as a symbolic interface between compression and reasoning; the model no longer needs to reconstruct before thinking, but can think directly using its own abstract language. This not only brings a huge boost in speed and scalability but also provides a new window into how LLMs internally represent and manipulate semantic knowledge.

3.5
Interpretability Analysis

We observe that the learned codebook
\mathcal{V}_{Z}
is reused in heterogeneous inputs: the same Z-tag index can appear in different contexts but yields different representations after decompression; conversely, the same input may have multiple valid Z sequences at different sampling temperatures. Unlike traditional tokenizers, where one token corresponds to one subword, this suggests that Z-tags function as abstract, context-dependent semantic units, rather than deterministic mappings.

Qualitative evidence.

Z-tokens operate at a conceptual level instead of encoding surface wording.
1)
Semantic synonymy:
sentences such as
“The government implemented sweeping economic reforms to address the financial crisis”
and
“Officials enacted major fiscal restructuring to mitigate the debt emergency”
share overlapping Z-tokens (e.g.,
z_{42},z_{87}
) that capture the idea of government-led economic intervention, even though the decompressed texts differ lexically.
2)
Syntactic alternation:

“Climate change threatens agricultural productivity and requires immediate attention”
vs.
“Agricultural productivity is threatened by climate change, which requires immediate action”
map to shared tokens (e.g.,
z_{156},z_{203}
) encoding concepts like environmental threat and urgency, while reconstruction realizes them with different word orders.
3)
Domain-level drift:

“Advanced technology drives innovation in transportation systems”
and
“Cutting-edge solutions accelerate progress in mobility infrastructure”
align on tokens such as
z_{487},z_{99}
that represent technological progress, later re-expanded into context-appropriate phrasings (e.g.,
modern technology
vs.
state-of-the-art methods
).

Interpretation.

Unlike standard tokenization where symbols correspond to fixed subwords, Z-tokens are
polysemous encodings
whose concretization depends on surrounding codes and decoding context. They achieve higher compression by abstracting away surface variability: multiple paraphrases of the same concept are folded onto a shared discrete code, enabling faithful reconstruction of meaning with surface-level diversity.

Measuring semantic consistency.

To verify that this polysemy is
structured
rather than chaotic, we measure the contextual consistency of each Z-token across its occurrences.
Let
\mathcal{O}(z_{i})=\{o_{1},\dots,o_{N_{i}}\}
denote the set of occurrences of
z_{i}
in a corpus.
For each occurrence
o_{p}
, let
\mathbf{ctx}(o_{p})
be a fixed-width window of surface text around
o_{p}
, and let
\mathcal{F}(\cdot)
be a frozen semantic encoder (e.g., BERT) that maps text to an embedding and measure the similarity through
\mathbf{sim}(\cdot)
. The activation consistency is defined as:

\mathcal{C}(z_{i})=\frac{1}{\binom{N_{i}}{2}}\sum_{p<q}\textbf{sim}\!\left(\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{ctx}(o_{p})),\,\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{ctx}(o_{q}))\right).

(11)

On our evaluation set, the mean consistency is
0.75\pm 0.11
, indicating that identical Z-token identities tend to appear in semantically coherent neighborhoods; the polysemy is regular and concept-driven.

This ambiguity naturally raises the question: how to transform ambiguity into a deterministic output during decompression? Z-token decoding is not performed in isolation, but rather under three conditions: the complete Z sequence, autoregressive prefixes, and constraints from the base vocabulary. First, we input the complete sequence
Z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{K})
into the decompressor, narrowing the broad terms down to specific semantic regions through adjacent Z-labels. Second, each decoding step is conditioned on the generated text
x_{<t}
; once the model generates a specific term, subsequent terms are guided towards that semantic direction, rather than irrelevant meanings. Finally, we restrict logits to the pre-trained vocabulary
\mathcal{V}_{\text{base}}
, leveraging the power of LLM to ensure that abstract terms appear as frequently occurring and well-trained natural language terms, rather than arbitrary substitutions. These three constraints transform ambiguity into a context-dependent implementation, just as words in natural language may be ambiguous, but sentences are usually clear within their context.

4
Experiment

In this section, we conduct extensive experiments on six datasets: HotpotQA, QuALITY, NarrativeQA, QASPER, Wikipedia, and CNN/DailyMail to demonstrate the effectiveness of our method.

4.1
Datasets

We train the LLM on Wikipedia for reconstruction and continuation tasks. This dataset provides rich linguistic and factual patterns, enabling the model to learn general compression and reconstruction capabilities. For long-text question answering tasks, we used the HotpotQA
Yang et al. 2018
, QuALITY
Pang et al. 2022
, NarrativeQA
Kočiský et al. 2017
, and QASPER
Dasigi et al. 2021
datasets. HotpotQA contains over 113k question-answer pairs, each including multiple supporting paragraphs from Wikipedia. This dataset emphasizes multi-hop reasoning, requiring the model to integrate different documents to answer questions. QuALITY is a long-context multiple-choice question-answering dataset containing 150 articles with an average length of 5,000 tokens and a total of 6,737 questions. NarrativeQA consists of long narrative texts such as novels and movie scripts, designed to test a model’s ability to understand and reason over extended contexts. QASPER is a dataset for question answering over research papers, requiring models to extract or infer information from long and complex academic documents. These datasets aim to explore holistic understanding and long-term reasoning capabilities, providing a challenging testing ground for long-context compression and understanding. For summarization tasks, we used the CNN/DailyMail dataset which is a large-scale news summarization benchmark containing over 30k article-summary pairs, serving as a standard testing ground for evaluating abstract summarization and long-text compression capabilities.

4.2
Baselines and imImplementation Details

Our experiments used the Qwen3-0.4B, Qwen3-1.7B, and Qwen3-4B models. For the LoRA
Hu et al. 2021
configuration, We use LoRAs to LLM’s all project linears and set the
r
to 128,
\alpha
to 256, and dropout to 0.1. We compared our method with state-of-the-art methods, including ICAE
Ge et al. 2024
, GistToken
Mu et al. 2024
, AutoCompressor
Chevalier et al. 2023
, and LLOCO
Tan et al. 2024
: ICAE compresses the input into a fixed-length memory slot; GistToken compresses the input into a fixed-length token using attention masking; AutoCompressor compresses the input in segments into a fixed-length summary vector; and LLOCO uses a fine-tuned AutoCompressor and a base LLM for contextual compression. To ensure a fair comparison, all basic settings were kept consistent across all methods, and the Z-token size is 8k. All experiments used six NVIDIA GPUs: two A800-80G and four V100-32G. Each configuration is equipped with one Intel Xeon Gold 6348 CPU. The software environment includes Ubuntu 22.04 LTS, Python 3.12, PyTorch 2.5.1, and CUDA 12.4. Unless otherwise specified, the base LLM used in the experiments is Qwen3-0.6B.

4.3
Metrics

For text reconstruction and continuation tasks, we use BLEU-4 as the core evaluation metric. Additionally, we report the perplexity score (PPL), which quantifies the difficulty of the model predicting the text. For the QuALITY dataset, we report the exact match (EM) score. For QuALITY, NarrativeQA, and QASPER, we report the F1 score. For the CNN/DailyMail summarization task, we provide ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, and ROUGE-L scores. To reduce bias from randomness and ensure the reliability of the results, all experiments were independently repeated three times, and the average score for each experiment is reported.

4.5
Ablation Study.

We first explore the impact of schedule-sampling on free-decoding performance in a Wikipedia reconstruction task. We use ICAE as the baseline, which relies on Teacher-Forcing during decoding. This approach leads to exposure bias; the model performs well during training, but its performance rapidly declines during free decoding due to the autoregressive accumulation of errors. To mitigate this issue, we introduce schedule-sampling during training
3.2
, allowing the model to learn to address this bias during training. It’s important to note that our goal is not to completely eliminate exposure bias, but rather to verify whether the model’s robustness to free decoding can be effectively learned. Therefore, we only set the sampling probability to gradually increase from 0 to 0.5. While
p\rightarrow 1
further improves the stability of free decoding, it also slows down the convergence speed and may negatively impact language fluency. Under this controlled setting, the results in Table
7
show that Schedule-Sampling effectively improves the quality of free decoding reconstruction and is beneficial for real-world reasoning scenarios.

To assess the impact of the number of Z-tokens, we conduct experiments on HotpotQA and evaluate settings of 1k, 2k, 4k, 8k, and 10k. The results are shown in Table
8
. When the number of Z-tokens is too small, the compressed representation becomes overly compact and fails to retain sufficient relational and contextual information, resulting in poorer QA performance. As the number of Z-tokens increases, more semantic structure is preserved and performance improves accordingly. However, beyond a certain point (e.g., from 8k to 10k), the performance gain becomes marginal, indicating that the representation has reached a saturation region where key reasoning information is already captured. This reveals a balance between compression rate and performance, where adequate semantic coverage is achieved without incurring unnecessary computational overhead.

Conclusion.

In this paper, we propose a discretization method that integrates compression and generation into the same LLM, allowing the model to naturally compress long texts into variable-length, controllable, and interpretable Z-tokens before executing downstream tasks, achieving a maximum inference speedup of 3.4× and significant memory savings. Extensive experiments across multiple tasks have demonstrated the effectiveness of this ”LLM speaks its own short language” approach, providing a novel perspective on text compression. Future work could focus on two key areas: first, designing a global Z-token coordination mechanism for long-range dependencies across windows; and second, making the compression process task-aware, preserving factual or argumentative structures as needed.
