Xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis presenting as a mass-like renal lesion mimicking neoplasia in a dog: first documented case and diagnostic considerations
Authors: Hamidreza Moosavian, Shahram Jamshidi, Mir Sepehr Pedram, Niloufar Kumar, Maryam Mahdipour, Arezoo Ramezani, Mahsa Fazli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundXanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis (XGP) in humans is a rare and severe form of chronic renal inflammation, characterized by extensive destruction of renal parenchyma and the formation of granulomatous tissue with lipid-laden macrophages.Case presentationWe present the case of a 6-year-old male Pomeranian dog exhibiting mild lethargy over the past month. Imaging studies, including abdominal ultrasonography and an abdominal computed tomography (CT) scan, revealed a markedly enlarged right kidney with multiple hypoattenuating areas. A radical nephrectomy was performed to remove the affected kidney. Histopathological examination of the nephrectomy specimen confirmed a diagnosis of XGP, demonstrating extensive parenchymal destruction, abscess formation, and infiltration of CD68 + vacuolated macrophages.ConclusionTo the best of our knowledge, this is the first documented case of XGP in a dog. This report highlights the importance of considering XGP in the differential diagnosis of canine patients presenting with imaging findings suggestive of renal masses or abscesses.

Background

Xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis (XGP) is a rare and aggressive variant of chronic pyelonephritis, characterized by the destruction of renal parenchyma and its replacement by lipid-laden macrophages (xanthoma cells), chronic inflammatory cells, and fibrosis [1, 2]. First described in humans in 1916 [1], it remains unreported in the canine species.

In humans, XGP predominantly affects middle-aged women and is strongly associated with specific risk factors, most notably chronic urinary obstruction and infection, frequently in the presence of renal calculi [1, 2]. Recurrent urinary tract infections and diabetes mellitus are also common predisposing conditions [2]. The typical clinical presentation includes fever, flank pain, malaise, and weight loss [1–3]. Urine cultures most commonly yield gram-negative aerobic bacteria, with Escherichia coli and Proteus mirabilis being predominant isolates [1, 2].

The diagnosis in humans is heavily supported by characteristic abdominal computed tomography (CT) findings. These typically include an enlarged kidney with multiple, hypodense, rounded parenchymal lesions representing xanthogranulomatous abscesses, which often demonstrate peripheral (rim) enhancement following contrast administration [1–3]. The frequent presence of a central calculi (staghorn calculus) is a hallmark feature. This mass-like imaging appearance is critical, as it can closely mimic renal neoplasia, such as renal cell carcinoma, creating a significant diagnostic challenge [2, 3].

Given the complete absence of documented canine cases in the veterinary literature, the clinical and pathological features of this condition in dogs are unknown. This report describes the first case of XGP in a dog, detailing its clinical presentation, diagnostic imaging hallmarks, and histopathological features, with particular emphasis on its deceptive presentation as a mass-like renal lesion.

Case presentation

A six-year-old neutered male Pomeranian dog was presented with a one-month history of mild, progressive lethargy. The dog was adequately vaccinated and had no prior history of significant medical illness. On physical examination, the dog was alert and in good nutritional condition. Vital parameters were within normal limits, thoracic auscultation revealed a normal cardiac rhythm, and no significant lymphadenopathy or abdominal pain was elicited.

Diagnostic imaging

Abdominal ultrasonography (Philips Affinity 70) identified a markedly enlarged and architecturally distorted right kidney with moderate vascularity on Doppler examination. The parenchyma contained multiple, poorly demarcated hypoechoic regions (Figure 1A). The contralateral kidney, liver, and other abdominal organs appeared ultrasonographically normal. Thoracic radiographs were within normal limits.

To further characterize the renal lesion, a pre- and post-contrast computed tomography (CT) study was performed using a 2-slice Siemens Somatom helical scanner (Figs. 1B, C1 and C2). Non-contrast images were acquired, followed by post-contrast images in the portal venous phase (approximately 60 s after intravenous administration of iohexol at 600 mg/kg). The scan revealed significant enlargement of the right kidney (maximum dimensions: 5.0 cm height x 4.6 cm width x 6.6 cm length). The parenchyma demonstrated heterogeneous enhancement with multiple, non-enhancing, low-attenuation areas (pre-contrast Hounsfield Units [HU]: ~20.57; post-contrast HU: ~23.04), creating a pattern reminiscent of the “bear paw sign” described in human medicine. The mass was in direct contact with and caused mild compression of the lateral wall of the caudal vena cava and abutted the proximal aspect of the right pancreatic limb. The left kidney had normal size and shape (maximum dimensions: 2.52 × 2.64 × 3.9 cm H/W/L).

Diagnostic workup

A complete blood count (CBC) revealed a mild neutrophilia (12.5 × 10³/µL; RI: 3.0–11.5) and lymphopenia (0.8 × 10³/µL; RI: 1.0–4.8). Serum biochemistry showed elevations in alkaline phosphatase (720 U/L; RI: 10–150), gamma-glutamyltransferase (15 U/L; RI: 0–10), and total bilirubin (0.8 mg/dL; RI: 0–0.4), suggestive of cholestasis. Renal parameters, including blood urea nitrogen (18 mg/dL; RI: 10–30) and creatinine (0.9 mg/dL; RI: 0.5–1.5), were within reference intervals.

Urinalysis performed on samples obtained via cystocentesis revealed inactive sediment, with no significant pyuria, hematuria, or bacteriuria. In the absence of overt inflammatory findings and given the primary imaging suspicion of a renal neoplasm (discussed below), urine culture was deemed non-diagnostic at that stage and was not pursued prior to surgical intervention.

Surgical intervention and findings

Based on the imaging findings highly suggestive of a primary renal neoplasm, an exploratory laparotomy was performed. Anesthesia was induced with an intravenous injection of ketamine (10 mg/kg; 10% solution) and diazepam (0.2 mg/kg). The animal was then intubated with an endotracheal tube, and anesthesia was maintained using isoflurane (1–3%) delivered via a volume-regulated ventilator (Fabius Draeger Plus). Intraoperatively, the right kidney was markedly enlarged, soft, and friable in consistency, with some adhesions to the surrounding perirenal tissues. Dissection was complicated by partial effacement of normal anatomical planes, consistent with chronic severe inflammation. The renal hilum was carefully dissected, and the vascular pedicle was identified and ligated without incident. The kidney demonstrated adherence to the lateral aspect of the caudal vena cava, requiring sharp dissection; however, no vascular injury occurred. A right radical nephrectomy was completed without intraoperative complications. The surgical site was irrigated with sterile saline, and the patient recovered from anesthesia uneventfully. Postoperative analgesia was provided with morphine (0.5 mg/kg, IV, every 4 h) for 24 h after surgery. Over the next two days, morphine was administered every 12 h, and concurrently gabapentin was given orally (10 mg/kg, every 12 h). A prophylactic postoperative course of cefazolin (25 mg/kg, IV, every 12 h) was initiated and administered for 8 days.

Gross, cytological, and histopathological findings

On cut section, the nephrectomy specimen exhibited severe parenchymal destruction, dilation of the pelvicalyceal system, and loss of corticomedullary distinction (Fig. 2). The tissue varied in color from brownish to yellowish, and the renal pelvis contained purulent and necrotic debris.

Cytological examination of intraoperative impression smears from the cut surface (Figure 3A) revealed a mixed population dominated by histiocytic cells with abundant, variably vacuolated cytoplasm, consistent with foamy macrophages. These were admixed with neutrophils, lymphocytes, and cellular debris.

Histopathological evaluation of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections identified four key morphological regions: (1) Extensive parenchymal necrosis and inflammation replacing normal renal architecture; (2) Large aggregates of vacuolated macrophages (foam cells) and histiocytes adjacent to necrotic foci; (3) Peripheral remnants of glomeruli and tubules; and (4) Interspersed zones of fibrosis with associated hyperemia, hemorrhage, and lymphocytic infiltrates (Figs. 3B-G). Immunohistochemistry for CD68 confirmed strong immunoreactivity within the population of foamy macrophages (Fig. 3H). The collective cytological, histopathological, and immunohistochemical findings were diagnostic for XGP.

Follow-up

The patient recovered well from surgery. One month postoperatively, follow-up serum biochemistry indicated normalization of the previously elevated bilirubin and liver enzymes as alkaline phosphatase (144 U/L; RI: 10–150), gamma-glutamyltransferase (9 U/L; RI: 0–10), and total bilirubin (0.2 mg/dL; RI: 0–0.4). The dog remained clinically stable during a six-month follow-up period. Serial abdominal ultrasounds of the contralateral kidney showed no evidence of XGP or other abnormalities, and the owner reported complete resolution of the dog’s prior lethargy.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this report describes the first documented case of naturally occurring XGP in a dog. This diagnosis was reached through the integration of imaging findings, classic histopathological features, and the exclusion of other key differentials, highlighting a novel renal entity in veterinary medicine with significant implications for clinical practice.

Renal ultrasonography plays a critical role in characterizing focal parenchymal lesions. Solid mass lesions, which disrupt normal renal architecture and alter the renal contour, may be hyperechoic, hypoechoic, or of complex echogenicity; distinguishing specific cell types by ultrasound alone is not possible [4]. While a smooth margin and minimal architectural disruption may suggest a benign process, invasive malignant neoplasms typically cause gross architectural distortion. The echogenicity of renal neoplasia is variable and inconsistent, influenced by factors such as hemorrhage, necrosis, fibrosis, and mineralization, often resulting in a complex appearance. Importantly, non-neoplastic conditions, including granulomas, abscesses, and organizing hematomas, can present as solid masses with similarly complex echotexture, making them ultrasonographic mimics of neoplasia [4]. The imaging features in the present case perfectly illustrate this diagnostic challenge. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed a markedly enlarged right kidney with distorted renal architecture and multiple hypoechoic areas, aligning with the description of a complex solid mass. The subsequent CT scan further detailed a heterogeneously enhancing mass with multiple non-enhancing areas, a pattern characteristic of both aggressive neoplasms and severe inflammatory pseudo-tumors like XGP. As noted in the literature, these shared imaging features made definitive preoperative differentiation between renal cell carcinoma and an inflammatory mass impossible based on imaging alone [5]. This underlines the critical limitation of imaging and validates the necessity of histopathological examination, which in this case revealed the classic granulomatous and macrophagic infiltrate of XGP rather than neoplastic tissue.

On the other hand, the CT findings in this canine case demonstrate significant consistency with the classic imaging features of XGP as documented in human medicine. In humans, the diffuse form of XGP is classically characterized on contrast-enhanced CT by an enlarged, kidney containing multiple, rounded low-attenuation areas, which represent dilated calyces or xanthomatous collections filled with debris and lipid-laden macrophages [2]. As seen in the reported dog, these hypodense areas are often surrounded by enhancing rims due to inflammatory hypervascularity, creating a characteristic multiloculated appearance known as the “bear’s paw” sign [6]. Identification of the bear paw sign on CT, especially in conjunction with clinical features, strongly suggests a diagnosis of XGP [7].

The definitive diagnosis of XGP was based on the characteristic histopathological triad observed in humans: (i) destruction of renal parenchyma and its replacement by granulomatous inflammatory tissue; (ii) the presence of large, sheet-like aggregates of vacuolated, lipid-laden macrophages (foam cells); and (iii) an admixture of lymphocytes, plasma cells, and fibrosis [1, 2]. The gross appearance of the nephrectomy specimen, a reniform mass with yellow-tan discoloration, purulent pelvic debris, and loss of corticomedullary distinction, further supported this diagnosis [1, 8]. Immunohistochemical confirmation of strong CD68 expression within the foamy macrophages substantiated their histiocytic lineage [2]. It is crucial to note that while CD68 positivity confirms a macrophage-rich process, it is not specific to XGP and can be seen in other granulomatous inflammations and, rarely, in certain neoplasms; thus, its interpretive value lies in its integration with the overall histological pattern [2]. The principal differential diagnoses for a mass-forming renal lesion in a dog include neoplasia, a primary renal abscess, pyelonephritis, and other granulomatous diseases [4].

Cytologically and histologically, the lesion lacked the cohesive epithelial clusters, nuclear atypia, and architectural patterns (e.g., tubular, papillary) diagnostic of renal cell carcinoma (RCC) [2]. While both RCC and XGP can present with clear or vacuolated cells, the background in this case was dominated by a mixed inflammatory infiltrate and necrosis rather than a monomorphic neoplastic population. A simple renal abscess was excluded by the expansive, granulomatous nature of the inflammation and the abundance of foamy macrophages, which are not typical of a neutrophilic abscess cavity. Chronic pyelonephritis was ruled out by the sheer magnitude of histiocytic infiltration and parenchymal replacement, which exceed the typical lymphoplasmacytic interstitial inflammation of chronic infection. Finally, the absence of fungal elements on histopathology argued against mycotic granulomatous disease. In humans, XGP is strongly associated with chronic urinary obstruction and infection, most often in the context of nephrolithiasis, with Escherichia coli and Proteus mirabilis being common isolates [1, 2, 8, 9].

Urinary calculi are not a consistent feature of XGP, and the condition can sometimes present with an absence of overt clinical manifestations [1, 5, 9].

In the present case, no calculi were identified on imaging or during surgery, and a preoperative urine culture was not performed due to the inactive urine sediment and high suspicion for neoplasia. This represents a significant limitation, as it precludes microbiological confirmation and constrains causal inference. The absence of urolithiasis suggests that the development and progression of granulomatous pyelonephritis in this patient may have occurred through other pathways, independent of urolithiasis. It is plausible that, in some cases, XGP may arise from chronic bacterial infection independent of urolithiasis. This could involve a low-grade infection leading to incomplete bacterial degradation by macrophages, resulting in the characteristic lipid accumulation, a mechanism previously proposed in human medicine [2, 10]. The postoperative resolution of the dog’s lethargy and cholestatic liver enzyme elevations suggests a hepatic dysfunction secondary to the inflammatory renal mass, a phenomenon commonly reported in human XGP [11, 12]. Human studies indicate that approximately half of patients with XGP develop hepatic dysfunction and elevated liver enzymes [11]. The presence of chronic inflammation and increased levels of inflammatory cytokines are considered contributing factors to this hepatic dysfunction [11, 12]. In such patients, nephrectomy is consistently followed by a decline in liver enzyme levels and improvement in liver function [11, 12]. Similarly, in the present case, elevated bilirubin and cholestatic liver enzymes, including alkaline phosphatase and gamma-glutamyl transferase, were observed in the absence of detectable hepatic lesions on ultrasound. These abnormalities returned to normal levels within one month following nephrectomy.

This case highlights that XGP is a formidable mimicker of renal neoplasia in dogs. Practitioners should consider XGP in the differential diagnosis for any adult dog presenting with a unilateral, solid, or complex renal mass, particularly when imaging reveals suggestive features such as an enlarged kidney with multiple, non-enhancing, low-attenuation areas that may create a “bear’s paw” appearance on CT. In cases where imaging is highly suggestive and the lesion is unilateral, as in this dog, nephrectomy remains both the definitive diagnostic procedure and the curative treatment [1, 8]. Intraoperative tissue impression smears can provide rapid supportive evidence. Ultimately, histopathological examination is required for a definitive diagnosis.

Conclusion

This case represents the first documented occurrence of XGP in a canine patient. The gross, imaging, and histopathological features are strikingly similar to those described in humans. XGP should be included in the differential diagnosis for dogs presenting with mass-forming renal lesions, as it can mimic neoplasia both clinically and on imaging studies. Awareness of this entity can refine diagnostic approaches and underscores the continued paramount importance of histopathology in achieving a final diagnosis for atypical renal masses.
