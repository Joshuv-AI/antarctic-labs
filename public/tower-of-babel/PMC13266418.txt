CEBP and ZEB2 alterations define three distinct subtypes of B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia
Authors: Rathana Kim, Marie Passet, Hugo Bergugnat, Loïc Vasseur, Thibaut Leguay, Françoise Huguet, Mathilde Hunault, Tom Sexton, Melha Benlebna, Julie Renard, Emmanuel Raffoux, Cédric Pastoret, Cathie Erb, Laureen Chat, Eric Delabesse, Stéphanie Gachet, Caroline Bonmati, Marie Balsat, Thorsten Braun, Audrey Bidet, Nicolas Duployez, Carlos Graux, Yves Chalandon, Philippe Rousselot, Patrice Chevallier, Jean Soulier, Véronique Lhéritier, Hervé Dombret, Nicolas Boissel, Emmanuelle Clappier

ABSTRACT
AbstractB‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B‐ALL) is a heterogeneous malignancy driven by diverse genetic alterations. Among these, CEBP family genes and ZEB2 are recurrently involved, yet the spectrum of genomic mechanisms and their clinical impact remain incompletely defined. Integrated genomic analyses of a cohort of 992 Philadelphia‐negative adult B‐ALL patients revealed multiple mechanisms of enhancer hijacking‐mediated deregulation of CEBPA, CEBPB, CEBPD, and CEBPE, including IGH and several non‐IGH fusions, as well as noncoding mutations in regulatory regions. Combined with gene expression analysis, we identified three distinct subtypes, defined by co‐occurring CEBP and ZEB2 p.H1038R alterations (CEBP/ZEB2, n = 18 cases); isolated CEBP alterations (CEBPalt, n = 43), associated with frequent IKZF1 deletions and FLT3 deregulation; and isolated ZEB2 p.H1038R mutation (ZEB2alt, n = 15), associated with various additional genomic hits targeting ZEB2 and enhancing mutant ZEB2 expression. The three subtypes exhibited distinct clinical features, including age distribution (patients with CEBP/ZEB2 and ZEB2alt B‐ALL were younger) and sex bias (female and male predominance in CEBPalt and ZEB2alt, respectively). Early treatment responses and outcomes also differed: patients with CEBP/ZEB2 B‐ALL had a favorable early response, in contrast to patients with ZEB2alt B‐ALL, who had high levels of minimal residual disease and a dismal prognosis. Collectively, our findings define CEBP and ZEB2 alterations as drivers of genetically and clinically distinct subtypes of adult B‐ALL and provide a rationale for subtype‐specific risk stratification. Preclinical experiments in CEBPalt B‐ALL patient‐derived xenografts demonstrated sensitivity to FLT3 inhibition, highlighting a potential therapeutic vulnerability.

INTRODUCTION

B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B‐ALL) is a heterogeneous neoplasm characterized by a broad spectrum of genetic drivers that shape transcriptional programs, disease biology, and clinical outcomes. 1 Advances in high‐throughput sequencing have enabled the identification of more than 20 genetic subtypes, many of which have immediate diagnostic, prognostic, or therapeutic relevance. 2 Despite these advances, a fraction of B‐ALL cases still lack an identifiable driver lesion, and several provisional entities remain poorly characterized.

Chromosomal translocations involving CEBP family genes (CEBP) and the immunoglobulin heavy chain (IGH) locus were reported nearly two decades ago as recurrent, albeit rare, lesions in B‐ALL. 3 , 4 , 5 Yet, despite their early description, it remained unclear whether these rearrangements defined a distinct clinico‐biological entity, owing in part to their low frequency, heterogeneous genomic configurations, and inconsistent detection by conventional cytogenetics. More recently, large transcriptomic analyses have delineated a cluster enriched for IGH::CEBPE rearrangements and a recurrent missense variant in ZEB2 p.H1038R. 6 This cluster was proposed as a novel molecular entity, namely “mutated ZEB2 (p.H1038R)/IGH::CEBPE,” now listed as a provisional subtype in the International Consensus Classification (ICC). 7 However, the boundaries and genomic basis of this putative entity remain insufficiently resolved. First, although functional redundancy between ZEB2 p.H1038R and IGH::CEBPE has been hypothesized to explain the presence of one or the other alteration in cases from this subtype, no experimental evidence supports this model. Second, while alterations involving other CEBP family members have been reported, it is unknown whether they drive similar transcriptional programs; so far, they are not incorporated into the provisional definition. Third, ZEB2 mutations are not exclusive to this cluster and can be detected in other B‐ALL subtypes, questioning their specificity and driver role. 8 Finally, no study to date has systematically evaluated the clinical features and outcome of patients with CEBP or ZEB2 alterations in large cohorts. Taken together, these gaps highlight the need for a comprehensive definition of the biological spectrum, transcriptional signatures, and clinical relevance of CEBP and ZEB2 alterations in B‐ALL.

Here, we addressed these questions in a large cohort of B‐ALL in adolescents and adults. We identified diverse genomic alterations targeting CEBP and ZEB2 genes, which shape heterogeneous transcriptomic patterns. We uncovered two novel molecular subtypes distinct from CEBP/ZEB2, that is, CEBPalt and ZEB2alt, which are clinically relevant. In CEBPalt, we showed deregulation of the FLT3/RAS pathway and the efficacy of FLT3 inhibition in a preclinical model, suggesting potential therapeutic opportunities.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients

The study included all patients with a diagnosis of Philadelphia‐negative (Ph‐) B‐ALL enrolled in the GRAALL‐2005 and ‐2014 (18–59 years) and EWALL‐INO (≥55 years) trials (ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers NCT03249870, NCT02617004, and NCT03249870, respectively 9 , 10 , 11 ), for whom samples and molecular annotations were available (n = 265/525, 591/642, and 109/131, respectively). Additional patients aged 16–64 years in whom alterations of CEBP or ZEB2 genes were identified during routine diagnostic work‐up were also included, comprising 20 patients at diagnosis and 7 at relapse. Cytogenetic analyses were performed in local laboratories, whereas molecular analyses were centrally conducted at the hematology laboratory of Saint‐Louis hospital. All samples were obtained after informed consent, and the study was approved by the local institutional review board.

Molecular analyses

Mononuclear cells from pre‐treatment bone marrow (BM) or peripheral blood samples were isolated and assessed by flow cytometry for blast infiltration before nucleic acid extraction. Whole‐transcriptome sequencing (WTS) was performed in 538 cases, including all cases without subtype‐specific driver identified by cytogenetic analyses. WTS libraries were prepared using the Illumina stranded mRNA library preparation kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Reads were aligned to the hg38 human genome assembly, and raw counts were calculated using STAR 2.5.2b. 12 Gene expression was quantified by log2 of counts normalized using DESeq2's median of ratios, as previously described. 13 Comparison analyses were performed by the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. The t‐distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (tSNE) analysis was performed with the R package Rtsne with a perplexity value of 30 using the top differentially expressed genes across samples (list of genes in Table S1). Subtype prediction was performed using the ALLCatchR_bcrabl1 tool. 14 Hierarchical clustering was performed with the R DESeq2 package using top differentially expressed genes across samples (list of genes in Table S2). In some cases, whole‐genome and whole‐exome sequencing (WGS and WES) data were obtained through the 2025 French Genomic Medicine Initiative (PFMG2025). 15 In unresolved cases, optical genome mapping (OGM, Bionano Genomics) and capture‐based sequencing of CEBP genes and surrounding sequences were performed (hg38 chr19:33,289,924‐33,312,544; chr20:50,180,573‐50,202,700; chr8:47,726,903‐47,748,174; and chr14:23,107,026‐23,130,266). Screening for recurrent mutations and copy‐number variations in B‐ALL was performed by capture‐based targeted sequencing (see gene panel in Table S3), as previously described. 13 ATAC‐seq was performed using a protocol adapted from Buenrostro et al. 16 ATAC‐seq reads were adapter‐ and quality‐trimmed from raw FASTQ files using Trimmomatic, aligned to hg38 human genome assembly with Bowtie2, processed with SAMtools and Picard to filter low‐quality and duplicate reads, and peaks were called with MACS2, after which normalized coverage tracks were generated with deepTools for visualization in IGV. 4C‐seq was performed as previously described focusing on CEBPB promoter interactions and with a two‐step polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to incorporate barcode sequence (primer sequences available on request). 13 , 17 The primer‐trimmed sequences were mapped to the hg38 human genome assembly with Bowtie and assigned to DpnII restriction fragments. A translocation with chromosome 13 was identified by abnormally high numbers of “interchromosomal” 4C‐seq reads at this chromosome. The translocation region was visualized by plotting the running means of 4C‐seq sequence counts over sliding windows of 25 fragments.

Patient‐derived xenografts experiments

Patient‐derived xenografts (PDX) were established using NSG mice as previously reported. 18 Briefly, 1 million viable leukemic cells were xenografted via intrabone injection into NSG mice previously irradiated with 1.25 Gy. The leukemic load (hCD45+/hCD19+ cells) was monitored on BM aspirates by flow cytometry. Animals were clinically and biologically monitored until the end point was reached in accordance with the local ethical rules and home office license (47448‐2024020909144467). For in vivo treatment, 1 million PDX cells were injected into the tail vein of NSG mice previously irradiated with 1.25 Gy. After 3 weeks (G14_0372) or 4 weeks (EI_030), mice were subjected to BM aspirates, and all mice with >0.1% blast infiltration were randomized into treatment groups (six mice per group). Mice were treated with vehicle or gilteritinib (20 mg/kg once daily) by oral gavage for 6 weeks. Every 2 weeks, mice were subjected to BM aspirates for engraftment follow‐up. At the end of treatment, the BM and spleen were collected, and harvested cells were analyzed by flow cytometry. Gilteritinib (MCE HY‐12432) was resuspended at 3 mg/mL in 50% PBS + 50% PEG300 (MCE HY‐Y0873) and sonicated until the suspension was homogeneous.

Statistical analysis

Comparisons for categorical and continuous variables between groups were performed with Fisher's exact test and Mann–Whitney or Kruskal–Wallis tests, respectively. Event‐free survival (EFS) was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the last follow‐up date censoring patients alive and considering failure to complete remission (CR) after induction, relapse, and death as events. Outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared by Cox proportional hazards regression models. The proportional hazards assumption was checked by examination of Schoenfeld residuals and Grambsch and Therneau tests. The cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR) was calculated from the CR date to the date of relapse, censoring patients alive without relapse at the last follow‐up date. Relapse and death in CR were considered as competitive events. CIR were estimated using nonparametric estimators and compared using the Gray test. Statistical analyses were performed with STATA software (STATA 18.5, StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX). All P values were two‐sided, with P < 0.05 denoting statistical significance.

RESULTS

Diverse genomic mechanisms of CEBP deregulation in adult B‐ALL

Rearrangements of CEBP family genes with the IGH locus were identified through evidence of translocations on karyotype, OGM, and/or detection of IGH::CEBP fusion by WTS. These analyses revealed IGH fusions involving CEBPA (n = 19), CEBPB (n = 13), CEBPD (n = 12), and CEBPE (n = 5), corresponding to t(14;19)(q32;q13), t(14;20)(q32;q13), t(8;14)(q11;q32), and t(14;14)(q11;q32) or inv(14)(q11q32), respectively (Figure 1A and Table S4). High expression of the corresponding CEBP genes was observed, consistent with deregulation driven by IGH regulatory elements (Figure 1B). In B‐ALL cases lacking an identifiable driver lesion, OGM or WGS uncovered additional rearrangements involving CEBP genes with non‐IGH partners. A t(13;20)(q12.3;q13.3) translocation with a breakpoint located close to the CEBPB gene was identified in three patients (Figure 1C). 4C‐seq analysis demonstrated chromatin interactions between the CEBPB promoter and a region upstream of the lncRNA LINC00426, which is highly expressed in B‐ALL, suggesting re‐targeting of LINC00426 regulatory sequences to the CEBPB promoter (Figure 1D). Additional fusions involving CEBPA with IL7R locus, and CEBPB with ZCCHC7 nearby PAX5, were identified (one case each, Figure 1E). All these rearrangements were associated with high expression of the corresponding CEBP gene (Figure 1B), supporting a mechanism of transcriptional activation by juxtaposition to active regulatory sequences, similar to IGH rearrangements.

In cases with high CEBPA expression despite the absence of detectable chromosomal rearrangements, we performed capture‐based sequencing of the whole CEBPA gene and surrounding regions. In seven cases, we identified insertions or duplications clustered within a short region located 2.3 kb downstream of CEBPA (Figure 1F). These cases displayed high and monoallelic CEBPA expression (Figure 1B and Table S5). Consistently, ATAC‐seq in a representative mutated case (B_SL8) revealed increased chromatin accessibility in this region compared with other B‐ALL samples (Figure 1F), supporting cis‐deregulation of CEBPA through the acquisition of a neo‐enhancer.

Altogether, genomic alterations leading to deregulation of CEBP genes were identified in 61 patients: CEBPA (n = 27), CEBPB (n = 17), CEBPD (n = 12), or CEBPE (n = 5) (Figure 1G). Within the GRAALL‐2014 study, which prospectively enrolled adults aged 18–59 years with Ph‐ B‐ALL, 35 of 591 evaluable patients (6%) had B‐ALL with a CEBP alteration. These findings reveal a relatively high and previously underappreciated frequency of CEBP deregulation in adult B‐ALL, driven by diverse and often cryptic genomic mechanisms.

Distinct molecular subtypes defined by CEBP and ZEB2 alterations: CEBPalt, CEBP/ZEB2, and ZEB2alt

We next analyzed gene expression profiles of B‐ALL cases with CEBP alterations within our cohort of Ph‐negative B‐ALL with available WTS data (n = 538). t‐SNE analysis integrating annotations of known genomic drivers showed that CEBP‐altered cases segregated clearly from established molecular subtypes, supporting their role as primary oncogenic drivers (Figure 2A). However, CEBP‐altered cases did not form a tight cluster, indicating substantial transcriptional heterogeneity. Importantly, this heterogeneity was not attributable to the specific CEBP family genes involved (Figure 2A, bottom left).

We therefore hypothesized that additional cooperating events may shape the transcriptomic landscape of B‐ALL with CEBP alterations. Given the previously reported relation between IGH::CEBPE and ZEB2 p.H1038R mutation, we examined the distribution of ZEB2 alterations across the cohort (Figure 2B). Eighteen cases had both a ZEB2 p.H1038R and a CEBP alteration, which accounted for 30% of all CEBP‐altered cases, while 15 cases carried ZEB2 p.H1038R in the absence of any known driver. Ten additional cases with ZEB2 mutations, including 6 p.H1038R and 4 p.Q1072K/R, were detected within well‐defined subtypes, mainly ZNF384 and DUX4, without major impact on their characteristic transcriptional signatures (Figure 2B).

Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of cases harboring at least one CEBP or ZEB2 alteration, excluding those assigned to established subtypes, identified three groups (Figure 2C). The largest cluster comprised all cases with isolated CEBP alteration (n = 42, hereafter termed CEBPalt). A second cluster included 11 cases, all of them having both CEBP alteration and ZEB2 p.H1038R mutation, defining a highly coherent subtype referred to as CEBP/ZEB2. A third cluster consisted of 15 cases with an isolated ZEB2 p.H1038R mutation. Seven additional cases having both CEBP and ZEB2 alterations could not be clearly assigned to any cluster (thereafter referred to as CEBP/ZEB2 other).

To understand if the CEBP/ZEB2 subtype here defined was similar to the group previously defined by the presence of IGH::CEBPE or ZEB2 p.H1038R, 6 , 7 we used the ALLCatchR tool 14 for B‐ALL subtype prediction. All but one case from the CEBP/ZEB2 cluster matched the previously defined subtype with good confidence, whereas the other cases with either CEBP or ZEB2 alterations did not (Table S6). Our results thus indicate that the CEBP/ZEB2 subtype is defined both at the genomic and transcriptomic levels by the co‐occurrence of a CEBP alteration, potentially involving other family members than CEBPE, and the ZEB2 p.H1038R mutation, together with a distinct gene expression signature. Notably, most cases in our adult cohort had isolated CEBP alterations or ZEB2 p.H1038R mutation and did not belong to this subtype.

We then examined the cluster comprising 15 cases with ZEB2 p.H1038R mutation in the absence of CEBP alteration nor any other known driver lesion. Strikingly, a second alteration targeting ZEB2 was detected in all cases (Figure 2C). These alterations involved diverse additional structural alterations involving the ZEB2 locus, including IGH::ZEB2 fusions (n = 2), gene duplications (n = 4), structural variants (SVs) in the noncoding region upstream of ZEB2 (n = 4), and partial chromosomal uniparental disomy (n = 1) (Figure 2D,E and Table S7). Additional ZEB2 mutations were also observed (n = 4). p.H1038R variant allele frequencies (VAFs) indicated homozygosity or allelic imbalance in seven cases, consistent with copy‐neutral loss of heterozygosity or duplication of the mutant allele (Figure 2F and Table S7). WTS analyses further showed skewed expression of the mutant allele in cases with IGH::ZEB2 or SVs upstream of ZEB2, suggesting transcriptional cis‐deregulation through enhancer retargeting. Altogether, ZEB2 alterations were highly enriched in this cluster, which we therefore designated as ZEB2alt, defining a novel molecular subtype characterized by a homogeneous gene expression signature.

CEBPalt is a distinct subtype characterized by FLT3 deregulation

We next investigated the spectrum of cooperating genetic lesions across the CEBPalt, CEBP/ZEB2, and ZEB2alt subtypes (Figure 3A,B and Table S8). Genomic alterations affecting CDKN2A and the RAS signaling pathway were the most frequent events (45% and 44%, respectively) and occurred at similar frequencies across clusters. By contrast, several lesions showed strong cluster‐specific associations, further supporting the existence of biologically distinct subtypes. Most striking, IKZF1 deletions were highly enriched in the CEBPalt cluster while being rare in the other clusters (n = 26/42, 62%, vs. n = 2/33, 6%). Alterations in chromatin remodeling genes were common overall (49%) but displayed marked cluster bias: KDM6A alterations predominantly affected the CEBPalt cases (n = 9/42, 21%), whereas EZH2 alterations were largely restricted to the ZEB2alt cluster (n = 6/15, 40%). KMT2D mutations were observed in both CEBPalt and CEBP/ZEB2 cases. CEBPalt had also frequent gains of chromosome X (n = 13/43, 30%), chromosome 5 (n = 9/43, 21%), and chromosome 21 (n = 7/43, 16%).

Strikingly, FLT3 genomic alterations were frequent and highly specific to the CEBPalt cluster (n = 21/42, 50%). These included internal tandem duplications, a broad range of non‐canonical mutations, and SVs at the PAN3 locus, previously shown to enhance FLT3 expression through enhancer hijacking (Figure 3C). 19 Consistently, FLT3 expression levels were significantly higher in the CEBPalt cluster compared with other B‐ALL cases (Figure 3D), indicating that aberrant FLT3 signaling plays a central role in the biology of this subtype.

Collectively, these data define three distinct molecular subtypes of B‐ALL characterized by distinct patterns of CEBP and ZEB2 genomic alterations. Among these, the CEBPalt subtype is the most prevalent in adults and is characterized by frequent IKZF1 alterations and deregulated FLT3 signaling, suggesting potential therapeutic vulnerabilities.

CEBPalt B‐ALL are sensitive to FLT3 inhibition

FLT3 inhibitors are well established in the treatment of FLT3‐mutated acute myeloid leukemia (AML), with current investigations extending beyond cases with FLT3 mutations. Conversely, their therapeutic relevance in B‐ALL has remained largely unexplored, likely due to the low overall prevalence of FLT3 alterations in this disease. To investigate the therapeutic potential of FLT3 inhibition in CEBPalt B‐ALL, we generated two PDX models of this subtype with FLT3 alteration (EI_030 case with FLT3 p.A680V mutation and G14_0372 case with PAN3 deletion), and evaluated the efficacy of gilteritinib in vivo (Figure 3E). In both models, gilteritinib treatment significantly delayed leukemic progression, as evidenced by reduced blast infiltration in the BM and spleen. Although gilteritinib monotherapy did not achieve durable disease control, these findings indicate that CEBPalt B‐ALL exhibits sensitivity to FLT3 inhibition.

CEBPalt B‐ALL patients had a median age of 37 years, comparable to the remaining cohort, whereas patients with CEBP/ZEB2 and ZEB2alt B‐ALL were younger (median 24 and 21 years, respectively). A marked sex bias was observed, with a female predominance in the CEBPalt group and a strong male predominance in the ZEB2alt group (sex ratio 0.4 and 4.0, respectively). Initial white blood cell counts, central nervous system (CNS) involvement, and immunophenotypic distribution did not differ between the three groups, nor from other B‐ALL.

Early response to therapy differed substantially across subtypes. All CEBP/ZEB2 patients achieved a favorable BM response at Day 15 compared with other B‐ALL patients treated in the GRAALL‐2005/2014 trials (100% vs. 57.7%, P = 0.007). However, it did not translate into better minimal residual disease (MRD) response (end‐of‐induction MRD ≥ 10−4 [37.5% vs. 57.7%, P = 0.73]). By contrast, ZEB2alt patients showed poor responses to treatment: two patients failed to achieve CR after induction, and all other evaluable patients had end‐of‐induction MRD ≥ 10−4, most with levels ≥ 10−3, while MRD remained ≥ 10−4 after the first consolidation course. Patients with CEBPalt had a favorable initial response to corticosteroids, but BM response at Day 15, MRD levels after induction, and first consolidation course were similar to those in other B‐ALL patients.

In line with early treatment responses, marked prognostic differences were observed between patients with CEBPalt, CEBP/ZEB2, and ZEB2alt (Figure 4A,B). Patients with ZEB2alt had dismal outcomes, with inferior EFS compared to CEBP/ZEB2 patients (hazard ratio [HR] 2.93, 95% CI [1.08–7.97], P = 0.035), supporting the existence of distinct clinical entities despite similar age distributions. Compared with other B‐ALL patients treated in the GRAALL‐2005/2014 trials (Figure 4C,D), patients with ZEB2alt also had inferior EFS (HR 2.27, 95% CI [1.01–5.09], P = 0.046) and an increased CIR (specific hazard ratio [SHR] 3.37, 95% CI [1.39–8.17], P = 0.007). Patients with CEBPalt had intermediate outcomes, with a trend toward increased CIR (SHR 1.55, 95% CI [0.90–2.54], P = 0.11) compared with other B‐ALL patients, with half of relapses occurring after 2 years.

Altogether, clinical and outcome features indicate that CEBPalt, CEBP/ZEB2, and ZEB2alt B‐ALL display distinct patterns of age, sex distribution, early treatment response, and relapse risk, supporting distinct disease entities with clinical relevance.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we provide an integrative analysis of CEBP family and ZEB2 gene alterations in adult B‐ALL by combining extensive genomic, transcriptomic, and clinical data. Based on whole‐genome profiling by OGM or WGS, we identified a broad spectrum of cryptic structural variants, including IGH and non‐IGH fusions as well as noncoding regulatory lesions affecting CEBP genes, which are frequently missed by transcriptome‐based analyses alone. This comprehensive approach led to the identification of three clinically subtypes with distinct transcriptional profiles and co‐mutational patterns: CEBP/ZEB2, CEBPalt, and ZEB2alt.

The CEBP/ZEB2 subtype was defined by the co‐occurrence of CEBP alterations and the ZEB2 p.H1038R mutation. This subtype overlaps with the provisional B‐ALL entity “ZEB2 (p.H1038R)/IGH::CEBPE,” previously defined by the presence of either an IGH::CEBPE fusion or a ZEB2 p.H1038R mutation. 6 , 7 Our findings refine this definition by showing that the association of CEBP and ZEB2 alterations characterizes this subtype, whereas isolated CEBP or ZEB2 alterations drive distinct molecular entities rather than incomplete or overlapping forms of a single subtype. While functional redundancy between IGH::CEBPE and ZEB2 p.H1038R has been proposed, our data instead support a model of oncogenic cooperation. In addition, CEBP alterations were not restricted to CEBPE but also involved other CEBP family members, most frequently CEBPA, as well as CEBPB and CEBPD, suggesting convergent functional programs.

CEBPA is a key regulator of hematopoiesis and myeloid differentiation, and its inactivation plays a central role in AML leukemogenesis. 20 , 21 , 22 In contrast, CEBPA is normally silenced in B‐cell progenitors to enable the establishment of the lymphoid program. 23 , 24 Our data indicate that ectopic expression of CEBPA or other CEBP family members in B‐cell precursors initiates a leukemogenic program in a lymphoid context. Although experimental models have shown that forced CEBPA expression in B cells can induce myeloid transdifferentiation, 25 B‐ALL cases with CEBP alterations in our cohort fully retained their lymphoid identity, with no evidence of myeloid lineage promiscuity based on immunophenotype nor lineage switch during disease evolution (data not shown).

The CEBPalt subtype represents a relatively common entity in adult B‐ALL, accounting for nearly 6% of adult Ph‐negative B‐ALL cases in the GRAALL‐2014 cohort. This subtype was not identified in prior studies of unselected pediatric B‐ALL, but it shares similarities with a recently described subtype in Down syndrome‐associated ALL. 26 In line with this, two patients with CEBPalt B‐ALL in our cohort had Down syndrome. A striking feature of CEBPalt B‐ALL was the high prevalence of FLT3 deregulating alterations. While FLT3 mutations are frequent in AML, they occur in only 5%–6% of B‐ALL cases. 27 , 28 In contrast, at least half of CEBPalt cases harbored FLT3 genomic lesions, with high FLT3 expression and recurrent N/KRAS mutations further supporting a central role for FLT3/RAS pathway activation. In addition, the frequent presence of IKZF1 alterations, together with aberrant kinase signaling, is reminiscent of Ph‐like ALL. 29 Growing evidence indicates that FLT3 deregulation, whether genetically or epigenetically driven, as in the ZNF384 subtype, contributes to B‐ALL leukemogenesis and may represent a therapeutic vulnerability. 30 , 31 , 32 Our preclinical data provide further support for the use of FLT3 inhibitors in a genomics‐guided therapeutic approach.

We also describe a novel molecular entity, the ZEB2alt subtype, defined by a wide spectrum of ZEB2 genomic alterations, including point mutations, structural variants, and regulatory lesions, leading to skewed expression of the ZEB2 p.H1038R mutation in the absence of other driver lesions. A study in pediatric B‐ALL previously reported a few unresolved cases with ZEB2 p.H1038R and additional ZEB2 lesions. 8 Our findings extend these observations by establishing ZEB2alt as a distinct molecular subtype with a unique transcriptional program. Collectively, our findings suggest a context‐dependent role of ZEB2 mutations in B‐ALL, likely acting as a primary oncogenic driver in ZEB2alt, an essential cooperative lesion in CEBP/ZEB2, or a secondary, dispensable event in other subtypes.ZEB2 is a transcription factor that has recently emerged as a key regulator of hematopoiesis, including in the B‐cell lineage, mainly acting as a transcriptional repressor. 33 The p.H1038R mutation affects the carboxy‐terminal Cys2His2 zinc fingers involved in DNA binding and E‐box recognition, likely disrupting zinc coordination and impairing DNA interaction. Multiple independent alterations in ZEB2alt converging toward skewed expression of the mutant allele suggest selective pressure favoring the mutant, which is consistent with a potential dominant‐negative or neomorphic effect. Further functional studies will be required to clarify the precise molecular mechanisms by which ZEB2 p.H1038R contributes to leukemogenesis.

Importantly, the CEBP/ZEB2, CEBPalt, and ZEB2alt subtypes were clinically relevant since associated with distinct patient characteristics and treatment responses. ZEB2alt predominantly affected young adult males and was associated with high MRD levels and a high incidence of relapse, translating in dismal prognosis. CEBPalt occurred more frequently in female patients and was associated with suboptimal treatment response and a high rate of late relapse occurring after the intensive treatment phase. Patients with CEBP/ZEB2 B‐ALL were younger and exhibited favorable early responses with intermediate outcomes, although patient numbers were limited in our adult cohort.

In conclusion, our study establishes CEBP and ZEB2 alterations as key drivers of genetically and clinically distinct subtypes of adult B‐ALL, providing a rationale for refined molecular classification and risk stratification. The frequent involvement of the FLT3/RAS pathway in CEBPalt B‐ALL highlights a potential therapeutic opportunity through targeted FLT3 inhibition, as supported by our PDX experiments, which may be particularly relevant during maintenance therapy when most relapses occur. Future studies should investigate the mechanistic interplay between CEBP deregulation and FLT3/RAS signaling, as well as the functional consequences of ZEB2 alterations, to further guide precision therapy in adult B‐ALL.
