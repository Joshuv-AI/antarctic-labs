 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 Electrolysis has been a commercially available product for a while and electrolyzers have been a
 proven capability to provide additional benefits (e.g. controllable load for grid services) in
 addition to production of hydrogen. The hydrogen output is typically compressed for storage and
 dispensing. Compression adds cost and decreases system reliability. Honda’s electrolyzer
 systems have been developed to include electrochemical compression to leverage the production
 system itself for at least partial compression. In this project, the team will evaluate Honda’s PEM
 based electrochemical compression system. The system is capable of compressing hydrogen up
 to 70 MPa electrochemically.

 Validation testing is the next step to accelerate this technology into the marketplace, as the
 validation will provide needed data under a variety of operation conditions and controls. These
 operating conditions and controls are based on over a decade of NLR research and development
 with low-temperature electrolysis. The validation testing will include preparing NLR’s site for
 third party evaluation, benchmark testing of Honda’s stack and system, and simulating operation
 connected to renewables or in a grid service profile. NLR’s Energy System Integration Lab will
 be the location for the electrolyzer validation research and integrated into the Hydrogen
 Infrastructure Test & Research Facility (HITRF). This will build into the existing retail style
 hydrogen fueling station for a fully integrated experimental setup.

 Summary of Research Results:

 Task 1: Site Preparation and Test Plan Development

 The electrolyzer testing will be performed at NLR's Energy Systems Integration Facility in
 Golden, CO. NLR has existing infrastructure that supports electrolyzer technologies of various
 sizes. NLR's current capabilities include: controllable AC/DC power supplies - up to 250 VDC
 and 4,000 ADC, DI water cleanup and pumps, hydrogeil/water separators - up to 3 MPa, heat
 exchangers for temperature control - up to 75°C, and hydrogen drying per SAE J2719 standards -
 up to 7 MPa. All of these balance of plant components can be leveraged for this testing however,
 NLR's test facility is not currently set up to receive electrochemically compressed stacks at 70
 MPa. During this task, NLR will work closely with Honda engineers to upgrade NLR's current
 electrolyzer stack test bed to ensure that they can meet the full range of operation parameters for
 their electrolysis system. Once a good understanding of the test parameters is established, NLR
 will make the necessary upgrades to the support systems to achieve the test goals of this project.
 During this task a test plan will be agreed upon between the two parties. The next two tasks
 describe a general test plan that will be used as a basis for the agreed upon test plan in Task I.

 2

 Task 2: Stack and System Benchmark Testing

 NLR will perform benchmark stack testing of Honda’s electrolyzer stack. The benchmark testing
 includes collecting stack efficiency, in kWh/kg, at varying stack temperatures and stack cathode
 pressures. The stack efficiency map will include multiple cathode pressure levels (IO - 70 MP a)
 and stack temperatures (20 - 60°C). The efficiency map will be generated at different values of
 stack power to get a full performance curve of the stack and/or system. Results will be compared
 against current mechanical compression technologies.

 Hydrogen quality will be confirmed through dew point sensors rated for the operating pressures
 of the system. A goal of less than S parts per million per volume (ppmv) of water in the
 hydrogen will be set as a success for the quality metric. NLR will work closely with the Honda
 engineers to determine if there are any other metrics, besides water, that need to be measured to
 confirm hydrogen quality.

 NLR has the ability to individually measure cell voltages down to the mV range with its
 Individual Cell Voltage (IVA) monitoring system. IVA is a solid state device that can capture
 over I 00 cell voltage channels simultaneously. If voltage tabs are provided by Honda, NLR will
 be able to use this device to look at individual cell voltage efficiency and this device will lead to
 insight to how the stack shares voltage over the full range of operation.

 Task 3: Renewable Source am/ Grid /11tegration Testing

 During this task, NLR will use their controllable AC/DC power supplies to simulate different
 renewable or regulation profiles for regions identified as potential deployment sites for this
 electrolyzer. NLR has the ability to simulate PV or wind for any region in the world and can
 simulate the load profiles from these regions on the electrolyzer system. NLR could also provide
 grid integration testing by region either by controlling their AC/DC power supplies or by
 utilizing their grid simulators within the Energy Systems Integration Facility and front end
 controller in partnership with Idaho National Lab. For this task, NLR will run 3 - 4 weeks' worth
 of testing that simulates the electrolyzer operating in different regions -either PV or wind - where
 the unit would be deployed. The results will be compared against other low temperature
 electrolyzer systems to benchmark the Honda electrolyzer system capabilities for intermittent
 and variable operation, while integrated with downstream hydrogen infrastructure.

 Task 4: Economic Assessment

 A direct comparison will be made between the DOE benchmark Hydrogen Analysis Production
 Modules (H2A) Proton Exchange Membrane (PEM) electrolysis systems and Honda's high
 pressure electrolyzer system. The initial assessment will consider annual analysis of Honda's
 system operating at peak capacity. Cost savings due to elevated output pressure will be
 accounted for in the compression, storage, and delivery (CSD) tab of the H2A forecourt model.1
 A basic sensitivity analysis will be performed to understand the economic effect of the cost of
 hydrogen by different cost and performance parameters. Incentives and credits may also be
 included. This study assumes an annual overview based on average capacity, rather than
 economic analysis of transient use cases (e.g. transient solar or wind profiles). The cost estimates

 Background
 Most hydrogen used today is produced from reforming of natural gas. Another path gaining
 traction for production is electrolysis of water, or splitting water, using an electrolyzer. The
 electricity used by the electrolyzer can be obtained from renewable energy sources such as solar
 and wind harnessed by photovoltaics and wind turbines. The output of most electrolyzers is low
 pressure hydrogen which then needs to be compressed to be able to fuel vehicles to a nominal
 pressure of 70MPa. Mechanical gas compressors are generally used to increase the pressure. The
 compressors are expensive and require costly maintenance. One potential solution that removes
 the mechanical compression is to produce hydrogen by electrolysis in a stack that can support
 high differential pressure.

 Introduction
 NLR, with funding from DOE and in partnership with Honda, conducted hardware testing,
 validation and analysis of Honda’s High Pressure Electrolyzer (HPE). Honda’s HPE produces
 hydrogen fuel up to 82MPa removing the need for the purchase, maintenance and operation of a
 hydrogen gas compressor.

 According to DOE record #9013, it takes approximately 3kWh/kg to mechanically compress
 hydrogen from 20bar to 700bar. From stations reporting data to the NFCTEC, reported in
 CDP#35, the average compression energy at hydrogen fueling stations was 3.32 kWh per kg
 dispensed. This does not include energy required to fill tube trailers for the stations that receive
 compressed gas delivery.

 Beyond the energy costs, hydrogen gas compressors are expensive to purchase and are the
 second leading maintenance item overall from hydrogen retail stations as seen in Figure 1 and
 are continually a high maintenance item at stations as seen in Figure 2.

 General Testing Protocol
 The beginning and end of each test followed the same general procedure as outlined here. Before
 beginning any test profile, the balance of plant was brought online. This included starting and
 verifying the flow rates of all purge air and water through the stack. Once the system was
 verified to be operating normally, the electrical current applied to the stack was raised from 0 to
 250 amps at a rate of 50 amps/minute. Once this baseline current value was reached, the stack
 was allowed to reach the temperature and pressure specified for the testing. The temperature was
 regulated via the heat exchanger shown in Figure 3. This heat exchanger allowed for active
 cooling via exchange with the building’s secondary chilled water supply. At the lowest outlet

 pressures, the lack of active heating resulted in the stack temperature remaining below the
 desired temperature because of the reduced heating as generated within the stack itself.

 At the conclusion of each testing, the stack was allowed to reside at the nominal current value of
 250 amps for 10 minutes in order to stabilize. After this stabilization period, the Honda PLC took
 control of the DC power supply and slowly allowed the stack to depressurize while also
 managing the applied current. This slow depressurization generally took about 1 to 2 hours to
 complete when depressurizing from the maximum outlet pressure of 82 MPa and was necessary
 to avoid damaging the stack via rapid depressurization. In emergency situations, the stack can
 depressurize rapidly, although this risks damage to the stack membrane.

 Characterization Testing
 An initial set of tests was conducted at a variety of temperature and pressure combinations.
 These tests included an I-V curve conducted from 60-280 Amps and a second I-V curve
 conducted from 60-400 Amps. The characterization tests also included a load-step test that
 ranged from 144 Amps to 400 Amps and back to 144 Amps, residing at each setpoint for 5
 seconds. These tests were repeated at each combination of four pressures (2 MPa, 40 MPa, 70
 MPa, and 82 MPa) and three temperatures (25 C, 40 C, and 54 C).

 One of the benefits of electrolyzers is that they are generally able to respond quickly to changes

 Genevieve Saur, Spencer Gilleon, Sam Sprik, Jacob Thorson, Michael Penev

 Background
 The primary question to be answered was the trade-offs between electro-chemical compression
 and mechanical compression as it relates to station costs with onsite hydrogen generation. The
 2014 H2A Current Distributed PEM electrolysis case study1,2 was used to examine the question
 using case studies. The technology being compared was the Honda electrolyzer. The following
 technical specifications were supplied:

 Table 1. Honda Electrolyzer Technical Specifications Supplied

 Peak flow rate (kg/day) 2.5
 High Pressure Hydrogen Output (MPa) 82
 Stack Electricity Consumption (kWh/kg) 67.5
 BoP Electricity Consumption (kWh/kg) 20.8

 The default H2A distributed case studies look at hydrogen production at 1,500 kg/day which is
 much higher than the Honda electrolyzer being compared. Several modifications were made to
 accommodate a more balanced comparison within certain constraints.

 H2A Current Distributed PEM Electrolysis Case Study
 Modifications

 Several structural changes were made to the Distributed model to allow for high pressure inputs
 to the ‘Refuel Station – GH2’ tab in the model. Please see addendum for equation changes.

 1) The Main compressor power calculation was changed to disallow negative power
 consumption. Refer to cell ‘Refueling Station – GH2’!B187. This effectively caused any
 compressors to be zero’d in the station capital cost calculations.
 2) The main compressor electricity consumption was revised to disallow negative electricity
 consumption. Refer to cell ‘Refueling Station – GH2’!B357.

 These changes are mentioned because the results will differ slightly compared to the
 downloadable case studies which will error if high pressure production is introduced.

 Hardware Test Setup at NLR
 The Honda high pressure electrolyzer was installed at the NLR Energy Systems Integration Lab
 (ESIL) at the Energy Systems Integration Facility (ESIF) in Golden, CO, USA. NLR developed
 a controller that was integrated with the Honda controller. The Honda system was provided DC
 power and temperature-controlled DI water using NLR equipment. The oxygen separator and
 dilution air were also provided by NLR. Hydrogen and oxygen were vented through the
 corresponding laboratory venting systems. Several iterations between the Honda team and the

 NLR team took place to ensure all the potential hazards were identified and mitigated to ensure
 safe operation.

 Safety Review
 Before equipment was sent to NLR, NLR researchers traveled to Japan to view the system setup
 at Honda and go over HAZOPs. This continued at NLR with more PHA reviews.

 Controller Integration
 NLR developed a controller that integrated with Honda controller.

 Water Supply
 DI water provided by house system was filtered and monitored.

 DC Power
 Magna power supply provided the DC power.

 Oxygen Separator
 Water levels in the oxygen separator were monitored. Purge air was used to dilute the oxygen
 and any hydrogen that was present in the return water.

 Hydrogen Venting
 The hydrogen generated by the system was vented through the laboratory hydrogen vent used by
 multiple projects.

 Laboratory Space
 Controllers and power supply were placed at a safe distance from the hydrogen equipment.
 Equipment was monitored with IR sensors.

 Proprietary Considerations
 Privacy panels were installed so visitors touring the lab could not see the Honda proprietary
 stack and system.

 Commissioning/Decommissioning
 Honda was on-site to help commission and make sure equipment was functioning as expected.
 After testing, the equipment was decommissioned and sent back to Honda.
