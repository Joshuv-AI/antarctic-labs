Computing Witnesses Using the SCAN Algorithm
Fabian Achammer 0009-0002-3799-6393, Stefan Hetzl 0000-0002-6461-5982, Renate A. Schmidt 0000-0002-6673-3333

Abstract
Second-order quantifier elimination is the problem of finding, given a formula with second-order quantifiers, a logically equivalent first-order formula. While such formulas are not computable in general, there are practical algorithms and subclasses with applications throughout computational logic. One of the most prominent algorithms for second-order quantifier elimination is the saturation-based SCAN algorithm. In this paper we show how the SCAN algorithm on clause sets can be extended to solve a more general problem: namely, finding a witness for the second-order quantifiers that results in a logically equivalent first-order formula. In addition, we provide a prototype implementation of the proposed method.



keywords
Second-order quantifier elimination, SCAN algorithm, Formula equations, Saturation theorem proving

The SCAN Algorithm

The SCAN algorithm
1
1

1

SCAN stands for “Synthesizing Correspondence Axioms for Normal logics”. This stems from the fact that it was originally intended for a particular application and its more general applicability has been realized later on
25
takes as input a formula
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
, where
\varphi
is first-order
and then transforms
\varphi
into its clausal normal form
N
, which might include a Skolemization step.
Next,
N
is saturated by a
purification
process:
SCAN picks a clause
C
and a designated
\overline{X}
-literal
L\in C
.
All non-redundant constraint resolution inferences (defined below) between
C
(on that literal
L
) and the rest of the clauses are performed.
In addition, constraint factors (defined below) of
C
are added, and constraint elimination and redundancy criteria are used to simplify the resulting clauses.
If all non-redundant consequences have been generated,
C
is deleted from the clause set and the process is repeated with a new clause and designated
\overline{X}
-literal.
This process might not terminate, but if we reach a point where no clause contains an
\overline{X}
-literal the result is a clause set
N^{\ast}
such that
\exists\overline{X}\,N\equiv N^{\ast}
.
SCAN attempts to reverse the Skolemization step from the beginning, if performed, and returns a first-order formula logically equivalent to
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
.
As we present our results for clause sets, we do not deal with reverse Skolemization.

The calculus underlying the SCAN algorithm has three parts: inference steps, a notion of redundancy, and deletion steps that remove clauses that are no longer required.
We devote a subsection to each of these parts.
Then we introduce a notion of derivation for the SCAN algorithm and give some properties of the calculus.

Our description of the calculus underlying SCAN is more detailed than other descriptions in the literature, e.g.,
25
;
24
.
We will note at the appropriate points how our description differs.
Most of the additional complexity comes from a more detailed analysis of the purification process, in particular the deletion of a clause after all non-redundant inferences have been performed.
This serves three purposes:

1.

It lays the groundwork for the construction of witnesses that we present in
Section

4
.

2.

It separates the redundancy criteria used for the removal of redundant clauses that are already part of the clause set (backward redundancy) from the redundancy criteria used to determine whether a clause generated during the purification process should be kept (forward redundancy).
This is useful as our witness construction depends on the forward redundancy notion, but not so much on the backward redundancy notion.

3.

It allows us to give a new correctness proof of SCAN for a specific set of forward redundancy criteria.

Furthermore, our description makes the notion of SCAN derivation explicit, which is important for the witness construction in the next section.

3.1
Inference Steps

The following inference steps are used to add clauses to the active clause set.
The goal is to generate sufficiently many first-order consequences from the given second-order clause set such that existing second-order clauses can be deleted in a later deletion step.
The inference calculus resembles an ordinary resolution calculus, with the difference that resolution and factoring introduce constraints between the terms of the resolved literals instead of applying unification.
Unification is introduced as a constraint elimination step.
We collect the inference steps in the inference system
\mathcal{I}
.

Definition 1
.

The inference system
\mathcal{I}
is given by the following rules.

Constraint resolution:

C\lor L(\overline{t})

C^{\prime}\lor L(\overline{t^{\prime}})^{\perp}

\mathrm{Res}

\overline{t}\not\simeq\overline{t^{\prime}}\lor C\lor C^{\prime}

where
L
is a literal and w.l.o.g.
C\lor L(\overline{t})
and
C^{\prime}\lor L(\overline{t^{\prime}})^{\perp}
are assumed to be variable-disjoint.
The clause
\overline{t}\not\simeq\overline{t^{\prime}}\lor C\lor C^{\prime}
is called the
resolvent
of this inference.

Constraint factoring:

C\lor L(\overline{t})\lor L(\overline{t^{\prime}})

\mathrm{Fac}

\overline{t}\not\simeq\overline{t^{\prime}}\lor C\lor L(\overline{t})

where
L
is a literal.
The clause
\overline{t}\not\simeq\overline{t^{\prime}}\lor C\lor L(\overline{t})
is called the
factor
of this inference.

Constraint elimination:

\overline{t}\not\simeq\overline{t^{\prime}}\lor C

C\sigma

\overline{t}

\overline{t^{\prime}}

It is important to separate unification from the introduction of constraints since otherwise we would not be able to infer certain first-order consequences.
For example, the clause set
\left\{X(a),\neg X(c)\right\}
has the first-order consequence

(in fact, we have
\exists X\,(X(a)\land\neg X(c))\equiv a\not\simeq c

and

are not unifiable.

Compared to other descriptions of the SCAN algorithm, we add constraint elimination as an inference rule. This allows the calculus to simulate ordinary resolution and factoring by appending a constraint elimination inference after the corresponding constraint resolution or constraint factoring inference.
This also shows refutational completeness of our inference calculus.

Lemma 2

\mathcal{I}
is sound and refutationally complete for first-order logic without equality.

In practice constraint resolution and constraint factoring is often restricted to
\overline{X}
-literals, i.e., those literals whose symbols are to be eliminated.
While this reduces the search space for inferences, this strategy is not refutationally complete, e.g., if

Particular search strategies to improve performance are discussed in

3.2
Redundancy

To remove unnecessary clauses from the active clause set we employ a notion of redundancy.
Some of the simplest examples are the removal of tautologies and the removal of clauses that are subsumed by a clause in the active clause set.

Definition 3
.

Let
C,C^{\prime}
be clauses.
We say
C
is a tautology
(or
C
is tautological
) if there is a literal
L
with
L\in C
and
L^{\perp}\in C
.
We say
C
subsumes
C^{\prime}
(in symbols:
C\trianglelefteq C^{\prime}
) if there is a substitution
\sigma
with
C\sigma\subseteq C^{\prime}
.
For a clause set
N
we say
N
subsumes
C^{\prime}
(in symbols:
N\trianglelefteq C^{\prime}
) if there is a clause
C\in N
with
C\trianglelefteq C^{\prime}
.
We say
C
is redundant in
N
if
C
is a tautology or
N
subsumes
C
.

We use this to introduce a redundancy deletion rule in our calculus:

Backward Redundancy deletion:

N\cup\left\{C\right\}

\mathrm{RedDel}

N

if
C
is redundant in
N
.

Note that
\mathrm{RedDel}
preserves logical equivalence.

We can also simplify clauses by eliminating variable constraints inside clauses.
To that end consider the following rule.

Variable elimination:

N\cup\left\{v\not\simeq t\lor C\right\}

\mathrm{VarElim}

N\cup\left\{C[v\leftarrow t]\right\}

where
v
is a variable which is not a proper subterm of
t
.

This is a special case of constraint elimination, but preserves logical equivalence, hence it is a simplification, not an inference.

One can add further redundancy elimination rules as long as they preserve logical equivalence between the clause sets.
We refer to the rules given here as
backward
redundancy rules as they act on a clause set that already contains a redundant clause and remove it.
In the next subsection, besides discussing other deletion rules, we give a notion of
forward
redundancy which is used during the purification process to determine whether a generated clause should be kept.

3.3
Deletion Steps

For a given clause set
N
the goal of SCAN is to compute a set

of first-order clauses such that
\exists\overline{X}\,N\equiv N^{\ast}
.
We discussed the inference rules that add new clauses to the clause set and a notion of redundancy.
Using the existentially quantified nature of the problem, we can also remove second-order clauses from the clause set under certain conditions.

One deletion rule is
extended purity deletion
.

Extended purity deletion:

N

\mathrm{ExtPurDel}_{X}^{p}

N\setminus\left\{C\in N\ |\ \text{$C$ contains $X$}\right\}

for

, where

is a predicate variable and every clause in

containing

with

The other deletion rule, the

on one of its literals

The condition

Theorem 7
.

Let
N
be a clause set and let
P
be a pointed clause such that

is purified in

N\Rrightarrow\mathrm{Res}_{P}^{<\omega}(N)
.

This is an important property for our purposes as it facilitates the witness construction in
Section

Often the side condition of purified clause deletion is given as: All non-redundant inferences between
P
and the rest of the clause set
N
have been performed
24
.
Our description of the purified clause deletion rule differs from this one in two aspects.

1.

We do not require that all constraint factors of
P
have been generated.
While in implementations we still want to generate the constraint factors to retain refutational completeness of the calculus the generation of the constraint factors is not necessary for the purified clause deletion rule to be correct, i.e., to preserve the logical equivalence
\exists\overline{X}\,(N\cup\left\{P\right\})\equiv\exists\overline{X}\,N
.

2.

We use a specific forward redundancy criterion to determine whether a given constraint resolvent
R
needs to be added to the clause set, namely we check
N\trianglelefteq_{L^{\perp},\mathrm{ve}}R
where
L
is the designated literal of the pointed clause
P
to be deleted.
This differs from the usual side condition in three ways:

(a)

We take variable elimination into account during subsumption. For example, the constraint resolvent between
P=\underline{X(a)}
and
Q=\underline{\neg X(v)}\lor B(v)
is
\mathrm{Res}(P,Q)=a\not\simeq v\lor B(v)
which after variable elimination becomes
B(a)
.
Assume
B(a)
is already in the clause set.
Then
B(a)
does not subsume the constraint resolvent
a\not\simeq v\lor B(v)
directly, but it does subsume the logically equivalent variable eliminated clause
B(a)
.
Thus, taking variable elimination into account during subsumption avoids having to add resolvents to
N
for which a logically equivalent clause already exists in
N
.

(b)

This subsumption relation requires that the subsuming substitution
\sigma
does not identify any
L^{\perp}
-literals in the subsuming clauses.
This is important for our proof of
Theorem

7
above.

(c)

We do not treat tautological clauses as forwardly redundant here.
Note that the redundancy deletion rule can still be applied to remove tautological clauses, but doing it eagerly could remove tautological clauses that are necessary for
P
being purified in
N
.

The following example shows that it is necessary to not treat tautological clauses as forwardly redundant in the purified clause deletion rule as it would otherwise violate

be clauses and
L
a literal.
Since
\trianglelefteq{L}
is decidable it suffices to show that for any given clause

there are only finitely many
E^{\prime}
with
E\rightarrow_{\mathrm{ve}}^{\ast}E^{\prime}
.
In every
\rightarrow_{\mathrm{ve}}
-step the number of literals reduces by at least one since the constraint literal is removed and a substitution is applied to the rest of the clause.
Thus there are only finitely many

with

and exhaustively checking

for all of them gives a decision procedure for
\trianglelefteq_{L,\mathrm{ve}}
.

3.

For a given finite

and a pointed clause

we have that
\mathrm{Res}_{P}^{\leq 1}(N)
is finite.
Since
\trianglelefteq_{L^{\perp},\mathrm{ve}}
is decidable we exhaustively check

for all

and get a decision procedure for

being purified in

. ∎

The goal for the rest of this subsection is to prove
Theorem

.
To prove the theorem and to better understand the introduced relations we lay some groundwork.
The following lemma shows that the introduced relations are all sound and in the case of
\rightarrow_{\mathrm{ve}}
and
\rightarrow_{\mathrm{ve}}^{\ast}
even equivalence-preserving.

Lemma 10
.

Let
C,C^{\prime}
be clauses and
L
a literal.
Then

1.

C\rightarrow_{\mathrm{ve}}C^{\prime}
implies
C\equiv C^{\prime}
,

2.

C\rightarrow_{\mathrm{ve}}^{\ast}C^{\prime}
implies
C\equiv C^{\prime}
,

3.

C\trianglelefteq_{L}C^{\prime}
implies
C\Rrightarrow C^{\prime}
,

4.

C\trianglelefteq_{L,\mathrm{ve}}C^{\prime}
implies
C\Rrightarrow C^{\prime}
and

5.

C\trianglelefteq_{L,\mathrm{ve}}^{\ast}C^{\prime}
implies
C\Rrightarrow C^{\prime}
.

Proof.

1.

For the left-to-right direction let
\mathcal{M}
be a model of
C
.
Then
\mathcal{M}\models C[v\leftarrow t]
.
Since
C[v\leftarrow t]=t\not\simeq t\lor C^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{M}\not\models t\not\simeq t
we get
\mathcal{M}\models C^{\prime}
.
For the right-to-left direction let
\mathcal{M}
be a model of
C^{\prime}=C_{0}[v\leftarrow t]
and let
\theta
be an environment.
If
\mathcal{M},\theta\models v\not\simeq t
, then
\mathcal{M},\theta\models C
and we are done.
Otherwise
\mathcal{M},\theta\models v\simeq t
in which case
\mathcal{M},\theta\models C_{0}
.
Again,
\mathcal{M},\theta\models C
and we are done.

2.

Follows by induction on the length of a
\rightarrow_{\mathrm{ve}}
-path between
C
and
C^{\prime}
using
1
.

3.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model of
C
and let
\sigma
be
L
-injective in
C
such that
C\sigma\subseteq C^{\prime}
.
Then
\mathcal{M}\models C\sigma
and since
C\sigma\subseteq C^{\prime}
we get
\mathcal{M}\models C^{\prime}
.

4.

We have
C\trianglelefteq_{L}C^{\prime\prime}
for some
C^{\prime\prime}
with
C^{\prime}\rightarrow_{\mathrm{ve}}^{\ast}C^{\prime\prime}
.
Now let
\mathcal{M}
be a model of
C
.
By
3
we get
\mathcal{M}\models C^{\prime\prime}
which then by
2
implies
\mathcal{M}\models C^{\prime}
.

5.

Follows by induction on the length of a
\trianglelefteq_{L,\mathrm{ve}}
-path between
C
and
C^{\prime}
using
4
. ∎

Also note the following properties and relationships between the introduced subsumption relations.

Lemma 11
.

Let

.

Note the following result about

Lemma 12
.

Let
C
and
C^{\prime}
be clauses and let
L
be a literal.
If

, then there exists a clause
C_{L}
which results from
C
by a finite number of applications of
\mathrm{Fac}
- and
\mathrm{ConstrElim}
-inferences such that
C_{L}\trianglelefteq_{L}C^{\prime}

Proof.

Since
C\trianglelefteq C^{\prime}
there is a substitution
\sigma
such that
C\sigma\subseteq C^{\prime}
.
We say a pair of
L
-literals
(L^{\prime},L^{\prime\prime})
is
\sigma
-unified in

if
L^{\prime},L^{\prime\prime}\in C
,
L^{\prime}\neq L^{\prime\prime}
and
L^{\prime}\sigma=L^{\prime\prime}\sigma
.
Now let

be the number of
\sigma
-unified pairs in

We show that for all
l\in\mathbb{N}
, all clauses
C
and substitutions
\sigma
with
C\sigma\subseteq C^{\prime}
and

there exists a clause
C_{L}
which results from
C
by a finite number of applications of
\mathrm{Fac}
- and
\mathrm{ConstrElim}
-inferences in
\mathcal{I}
such that

.

We do this by induction on

.
If
k(C,\sigma)\leq 0
, then no two distinct literals in
C
are unified by
\sigma
, i.e.,
\sigma
is
L
-injective in
C
so
we have
C_{L}:=C\trianglelefteq_{L}C^{\prime}
.

Now to the induction step.
Let
k(C,\sigma)\leq l+1
.
If
k(C,\sigma)\leq l
, then the statement follows by the induction hypothesis.
So let
k(C,\sigma)=l+1
.
Then there exists a
\sigma
-unified pair in
C
.
Let
(L(\overline{t}),L(\overline{s}))
be such a pair.
Then
\overline{t}\sigma=\overline{s}\sigma
and

.
Applying constraint factoring to

results in
C_{f}:=\overline{t}\not\simeq\overline{s}\lor L(\overline{t})\lor C_{0}
.
Since
\sigma

\overline{t}
and
\overline{s}
there exists a most general unifier
\tau

Then applying constraint elimination to

Since

is a most general unifier of
\overline{t}
and
\overline{s}
and
\sigma
is a unifier of
\overline{t}
and
\overline{s}
there exists a substitution
\rho
with
\sigma=\tau\rho
.
Then we get
C_{fc}\rho=L(\overline{t}\tau\rho)\lor C_{0}\tau\rho=L(\overline{t}\sigma)\lor C_{0}\sigma=C\sigma\subseteq C^{\prime}
.

Now let
(L_{1},L_{2})
be a
\rho
-unified pair in
C_{fc}
.
Then
L_{1}=L^{\prime}\tau
and
L_{2}=L^{\prime\prime}\tau
for some

.
Since

we have
L^{\prime}\neq L^{\prime\prime}
.
Furthermore
(L^{\prime},L^{\prime\prime})
is a
\sigma
-unified pair in

.
Therefore there is a surjective mapping from the

to the

.

Furthermore

since

does not contain the
\sigma
-unified pair

.
Therefore
k(C_{fc},\rho)<k(C,\sigma)
and by induction hypothesis there exists a
C_{L}
which results from
C_{fc}
by a finite number of applications of
\mathrm{Fac}
- and
\mathrm{ConstrElim}
-inferences from
C_{fc}
and which satisfies
C_{L}\trianglelefteq_{L}C^{\prime}
.
Since
C_{fc}
results from
C
by a finite number of
\mathrm{Fac}
- and
\mathrm{ConstrElim}
-inferences this means
C_{L}
results from
C
by a finite number of
\mathrm{Fac}
- and
\mathrm{ConstrElim}
-inferences which concludes the proof.
∎

4
Resolution Witnesses

In this section we introduce an algorithm that constructs witnesses for a given
\mathcal{C}
-derivation.
We first make our notion of witness precise:

Definition 22
.

Let
\varphi
be a first-order formula.
A predicate substitution
\sigma
is a
witness of
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
if
\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)\subseteq\overline{X}
and
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi\equiv\varphi\sigma
.

For all
\sigma
we have
\varphi\sigma\Rrightarrow\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
, thus it suffices to prove
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi\Rrightarrow\varphi\sigma
to show that
\sigma
is a witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
.

Given a
\mathcal{C}
-derivation, the idea of our algorithm is to construct a witness back-to-front: Let
D=(S_{1},\dots,S_{m})
be an
\overline{X}
-eliminating
\mathcal{C}
-derivation from
N
.
Then the final clause set
N_{m}(D)
does not contain any variables from
\overline{X}
and thus any substitution
\sigma_{m}
with
\mathrm{dom}(\sigma_{m})\subseteq\overline{X}
is a witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,N_{m}(D)
.

For all
1<i\leq m
and a witness
\sigma_{i}
for
\exists\overline{X}\,N_{i}(D)
we now construct a witness
\sigma_{i-1}
for
\exists\overline{X}\,N_{i-1}(D)
by prepending a substitution
\tau_{S_{i}}
that depends on the derivation step
S_{i}
, i.e.,
\sigma_{i-1}=\tau_{S_{i}}\sigma_{i}
.
Thus
\sigma_{0}=\tau_{S_{1}}\dots\tau_{S_{m}}\sigma_{m}
is a witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,N
.
The algorithm is illustrated in the following figure.

\begin{array}[]{rcccccccl}N=N(D)_{0}&\xrightarrow{S_{1}}&N(D)_{1}&\xrightarrow{S_{2}}&\quad\dots&N(D)_{m-1}&\xrightarrow{S_{m}}&\ N(D)_{m}\\[3.00003pt]
\sigma_{0}&\xleftarrow{\tau_{S_{1}}}&\sigma_{1}&\xleftarrow{\tau_{S_{2}}}&\quad\dots&\sigma_{m-1}&\xleftarrow{\tau_{S_{m}}}&\ \sigma_{m}\end{array}

For this to work the substitutions
\tau_{S_{i}}
need to have the following property.

Definition 23
.

Let
S
be a
\mathcal{C}
-derivation step from
N
to
N^{\prime}
.
A predicate substitution
\tau
is called
witness-preserving across
S
if for all witnesses
\sigma
of
N^{\prime}
the substitution
\tau\sigma
is a witness of
\exists\overline{X}\,N
.
If
N^{\prime}\Rrightarrow N\tau
, then we call
\tau

witness-transforming
across
S
.

The substitutions involved in our witness construction are all witness-transforming.
We show that this also makes them witness-preserving.

Lemma 24
.

Let
S
be a
\mathcal{C}
-derivation step from
N
to
N^{\prime}
.
If
\tau
is witness-transforming across
S
, then
\tau
is witness-preserving across
S
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be a witness of
\exists\overline{X}\,N^{\prime}
.
We need to show that
\tau\sigma
is a witness of
\exists\overline{X}\,N
, i.e., that
\exists\overline{X}\,N\Rrightarrow N\tau\sigma
.
Since all
\mathcal{C}
-derivation steps are sound by
Lemma

18
we get (i)
\exists\overline{X}\,N\Rrightarrow\exists\overline{X}\,N^{\prime}
.
Since
\sigma
is a witness of
\exists\overline{X}\,N^{\prime}
we get (ii)
\exists\overline{X}\,N^{\prime}\Rrightarrow N^{\prime}\sigma
.
By assumption
\tau
satisfies
N^{\prime}\Rrightarrow N\tau
so we get (iii)
N^{\prime}\sigma\Rrightarrow N\tau\sigma
.
Taking together (i), (ii) and (iii) we get
\exists\overline{X}\,N\Rrightarrow N\tau\sigma
, i.e.,
\tau\sigma
is a witness of
\exists\overline{X}\,N
.
∎

\mathrm{Res}(P,R_{i}(\overline{s_{i}}(\overline{w}),\overline{z_{i}})[\underline{L^{\prime}}])\in\mathrm{Res}_{P}^{\leq k_{j}+1}(L(\overline{c})^{\perp})

Then for all

Now to the right-to-left direction.
Let

.

By induction hypothesis we then have

R^{\prime}:=C_{0}(\overline{w})\lor\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{q}R_{i}(\overline{s_{i}}(\overline{w}),\overline{z_{j}})\lor P^{\prime}(\overline{c},\overline{z_{j}})\in\mathrm{Res}_{P}^{\leq k}(C).

Lemma 33

Let

\displaystyle\mathrm{Res}_{P}^{<\omega}(C)

\displaystyle=\left\{C_{0}(\overline{w})\lor\bigvee_{i=1}^{q}R_{i}(\overline{s_{i}}(\overline{w}),\overline{z_{i}})\ |\ R_{1}(\overline{c},\overline{z_{1}}),\dots,R_{q}(\overline{c},\overline{z_{q}})\in\mathrm{Res}_{P}^{<\omega}(L(\overline{c})^{\perp})\right\}

Proof.

Let

\mathrm{Res}_{P}^{<\omega}(C)=R_{P}(C)

Then by

To show the other inclusion inclusion

.
Then we have

\displaystyle R_{P}(C)

Lemma 34

Let

be an
X

We have
C=C_{0}\lor\bigvee_{i=1}^{q}L(\overline{s_{i}})

Then

5
Fixpoint Witnesses

In this section we show that the witnesses defined in the previous section can be expressed in fixpoint logic.
We only focus on greatest fixpoints as our results are naturally expressed in terms of these.

Definition 41
.

We define the set of
fixpoint formulas
as the smallest sets such that

1.

every first-order formula is a fixpoint formula,

2.

the set of fixpoint formulas is closed under Boolean connectives and first-order quantification and

3.

if
\varphi(\overline{u})
is a fixpoint formula where
\overline{u}
is a tuple of
k
variables,
Y
is a predicate variable of arity
k
,
Y
is positive in
\varphi
and
\overline{t}
is a tuple of
k
terms then
(\mathrm{gfp}_{Y,\overline{u}}\varphi(\overline{u}))(\overline{t})
is a fixpoint formula. The predicate variable
Y
is considered bound inside
\mathrm{gfp}_{Y}\alpha
.

If
\varphi(\overline{u})
is a fixpoint formula then we call
\lambda\overline{u}.\varphi(\overline{u})
a
fixpoint predicate
.
If
\alpha
is a fixpoint predicate of arity
k
and
Y
is a predicate variable of arity
k
such that
Y
is positive in
\alpha
then we define
\mathrm{gfp}_{Y}\alpha:=\lambda\overline{u}.(\mathrm{gfp}_{Y,\overline{v}}\alpha(\overline{v}))(\overline{u})
.
A witness
\sigma
is called a
fixpoint witness
if
\sigma(X)
is a fixpoint predicate for all
X\in\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)
.

A well-known result due to Knaster and Tarski is the existence of least and greatest fixpoints for monotone functions.

Theorem 42

(Knaster-Tarski theorem)
.

Let
M
be a set, denote by
\mathcal{P}(M)
the powerset of
M
and let
f:\mathcal{P}(M)\to\mathcal{P}(M)
be monotone, i.e.,
f(M_{1})\subseteq f(M_{2})
for all
M_{1},M_{2}\subseteq M
.
Then there exists a unique set
\mathrm{GFP}(f)\subseteq M
such that
f(\mathrm{GFP}(f))=\mathrm{GFP}(f)
and for all
M^{\prime}\subseteq M
with
f(M^{\prime})=M^{\prime}
we have
M^{\prime}\subseteq\mathrm{GFP}(f)
.

Proof.

See, e.g.,
23
.
∎

This allows us to define the semantics of greatest fixpoint formulas.

Definition 43
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a structure with domain
M

\theta
be an environment and let
\varphi=(\mathrm{gfp}_{Y,\overline{u}}\psi(\overline{u}))(\overline{t})

is a fixpoint formula,

\psi

We extend the semantics for first-order formulas so we can assume that
\mathcal{M},\theta\models\psi

to fixpoint formulas and fixpoint predicates.

We collect a few properties about fixpoint predicates that follow from their semantics.

Proposition 44
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be fixpoint predicates and let

be a predicate variable that is positive in

.
Then

\mathrm{gfp}_{Y}\alpha\equiv\alpha[Y\leftarrow\mathrm{gfp}_{Y}\alpha]

\beta\Rrightarrow\mathrm{gfp}_{Y}\alpha
.

We now show that
\mathrm{\ell Res}_{P}
can be expressed as a greatest fixpoint predicate.

Definition 45
.

Let
P=\underline{L(\overline{t}(\overline{v}))}\lor C(\overline{v})\lor\bigvee_{i=1}^{p}L(\overline{t_{i}}(\overline{v}))^{\perp}
be a pointed clause where

is an
X

L^{\perp}
-literals.
Let

be a fresh predicate variable with the same arity as

We set

\displaystyle\alpha_{P,Y}

\displaystyle:=\lambda\overline{u}.L(\overline{u})^{\perp}\land\forall\overline{v}\,(\overline{u}\not\simeq\overline{t}(\overline{v})\lor C(\overline{v})\lor\bigvee_{i=1}^{p}Y(\overline{t_{i}}(\overline{v}))).

Lemma 46
.

Let
P
be a pointed clause where the designated literal is an
X
-literal and let
Y
be a fresh predicate variable with the same arity as
X
.
Then
\mathrm{\ell Res}_{P}\equiv\mathrm{gfp}_{Y}\alpha_{P,Y}
.

Proof.

Let
P=\underline{L(\overline{t}(\overline{v}))}\lor C(\overline{v})\lor\bigvee_{i=1}^{p}L(\overline{t_{i}}(\overline{v}))^{\perp}
be a pointed clause where
L
is an
X
-literal and
C
does not contain
L^{\perp}
-literals.
Furthermore let
\overline{c}
be a tuple of fresh constants.
We show both directions of the equivalence.

\Lleftarrow
: Let
\beta
be a fixpoint of
\alpha_{P,Y}
, i.e.,
\beta\equiv\alpha_{P,Y}[Y\leftarrow\beta]
.
We show
\beta\Rrightarrow\lambda\overline{c}.\mathrm{Res}_{P}^{\leq k}(L(\overline{c})^{\perp})
by induction on
k
.
We have

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle\equiv\alpha_{P,Y}[Y\leftarrow\mathrm{gfp_{Y}}\beta]

\displaystyle=\lambda\overline{u}.L(\overline{u})^{\perp}\land\forall\overline{v}\,(\overline{u}\not\simeq\overline{t}(\overline{v})\lor C(\overline{v})\lor\bigvee_{i=1}^{p}\beta(\overline{t_{i}}(\overline{v}))).

Thus
\beta\Rrightarrow\lambda\overline{c}.L(\overline{c})^{\perp}
which proves the statement for
k=0
.
For the induction step we show
\beta\Rrightarrow\lambda\overline{c}.\forall\overline{w}\,R(\overline{c},\overline{w})
for all
R(\overline{c},\overline{w})\in\mathrm{Res}_{P}^{\leq k+1}(L(\overline{c})^{\perp})
.
If
R(\overline{c},\overline{w})\in\mathrm{Res}_{P}^{\leq k}(L(\overline{c})^{\perp})
the statement follows by the induction hypothesis.
Otherwise
R(\overline{c},\overline{w})\in\mathrm{Res}_{P}^{\leq k}(\mathrm{Res}(P,L(\overline{c})^{\perp}))

Note that

\mathrm{Res}(P,L(\overline{c})^{\perp})=\overline{c}\not\simeq\overline{t}(\overline{v})\lor C(\overline{v})\lor\bigvee_{i=1}^{p}L(\overline{t_{i}}(\overline{v}))^{\perp}.

Thus by
Lemma

32
there are
k_{1},\dots,k_{p}\in\mathbb{N}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{p}k_{i}\leq k
and for all
1\leq i\leq p

R_{i}(\overline{c},\overline{z_{i}})\in\mathrm{Res}_{P}^{\leq k_{i}}(L(\overline{c})^{\perp})

R(\overline{c},\overline{w})=\overline{c}\not\simeq\overline{t}(\overline{v})\lor C(\overline{v})\lor\bigvee_{i=1}^{p}R_{i}(\overline{t_{i}}(\overline{v}),\overline{z_{i}})
.
Since
\sum_{i=1}^{p}k_{i}\leq k
we get
k_{i}\leq k
for all
1\leq i\leq p
and thus by induction hypothesis we have
\beta\Rrightarrow\lambda\overline{c}.\forall\overline{z_{i}}\,R_{i}(\overline{c},\overline{z_{i}})
and in particular
\beta(\overline{t_{i}}(\overline{v}))\Rrightarrow R_{i}(\overline{t_{i}}(\overline{v}),\overline{z_{i}})

Therefore

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle\Rrightarrow\lambda\overline{c}.\forall\overline{v}\,(\overline{c}\not\simeq\overline{t}(\overline{v})\lor C(\overline{v})\lor\bigvee_{i=1}^{p}\beta(\overline{t_{i}}(\overline{v})))

\displaystyle\Rrightarrow\lambda\overline{c}.\forall\overline{v}\,(\overline{c}\not\simeq\overline{t}(\overline{v})\lor C(\overline{v})\lor\bigvee_{i=1}^{p}R_{i}(\overline{t_{i}}(\overline{v}),\overline{z_{i}}))

\displaystyle\equiv\lambda\overline{c}.\forall\overline{w}\,R(\overline{c},\overline{w}).

This means we have

\beta\Rrightarrow\lambda\overline{c}.\mathrm{Res}_{P}^{<\omega}(L(\overline{c})^{\perp})\equiv\mathrm{\ell Res}_{P}.

By
Proposition

\mathrm{gfp}_{Y}\alpha_{P,Y}\equiv\alpha_{P,Y}[Y\leftarrow\mathrm{gfp}_{Y}\alpha_{P,Y}]

\beta=\mathrm{gfp}_{Y}\alpha_{P,Y}
.

\Rrightarrow
:
We show that
\mathrm{\ell Res}_{P}
is a fixpoint of
\alpha_{P,Y}
with respect to

\mathrm{\ell Res}_{P}\equiv\alpha_{P,Y}[Y\leftarrow\mathrm{\ell Res}_{P}]

Applying

\mathrm{\ell Res}_{P}\Rrightarrow\mathrm{gfp}_{Y}\alpha_{P,Y}

Note that

\displaystyle\mathrm{\ell Res}_{P}(\overline{c})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Res}_{P}^{<\omega}(L(\overline{c})^{\perp})=\left\{L(\overline{c})^{\perp}\right\}\cup\mathrm{Res}_{P}^{<\omega}(\mathrm{Res}(P,L(\overline{c})^{\perp})).

So we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{\ell Res}_{P}(\overline{c})

\displaystyle\equiv L(\overline{c})^{\perp}\land\mathrm{Res}_{P}^{<\omega}(\overline{c}\not\simeq\overline{t}(\overline{v})\lor C(\overline{v})\lor\bigvee_{i=1}^{p}L(\overline{t_{i}}(\overline{v}))^{\perp}).

Then by
Lemma

we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{\ell Res}_{P}(\overline{c})

\displaystyle\equiv L(\overline{c})^{\perp}\land\forall\overline{v}\,(\overline{c}\not\simeq\overline{t}(\overline{v})\lor C(\overline{v})\lor\bigvee_{i=1}^{p}\mathrm{\ell Res}_{P}(\overline{t_{i}}(\overline{v})))=\alpha_{P,Y}[Y\leftarrow\mathrm{\ell Res}_{P}],

6
First-Order Witnesses

In this section we introduce a witness construction method that can produce first-order witnesses.

Definition 50
.

A witness
\sigma
is called
first-order
, if its range consists only of first-order predicates.

For all
\mathcal{C}
-derivation steps except
\mathrm{PurDel}_{P}
,
\tau_{S}
is first-order. Thus, it remains to consider purified clause deletion: we have a clause set
N
and a pointed clause
P
which is purified in
N
, and we need to find a predicate substitution
\tau
such that
N\Rrightarrow(N\cup\left\{P\right\})\tau
and the range of
\tau
only consists of first-order predicates.
There is a class of pointed clauses
P
, introduced in
1
, for which the method from
Section

4
already provides first-order witnesses.

Definition 51
.

Let
P
be a pointed clause with designated literal
L
whose predicate symbol is
X
.
Then
P
is called
one-sided
if all
X
-literals in
P
have the same polarity as
L
.

Lemma 52
.

Let
P
be a one-sided pointed clause.
Then the range of
\tau_{\mathrm{PurDel}_{P}}
only contains first-order predicates.

Proof.

We have
P=\underline{L(\overline{t})}\lor C
for some literal
L(\overline{t})
and clause
C
.
Since
P
is one-sided,
C
contains no
L^{\perp}
-literals so we get
\mathrm{\ell Res}_{P}(\overline{c})=\left\{L(\overline{c})^{\perp},\overline{c}\not\simeq\overline{t}\lor C\right\}
, i.e.,
\mathrm{\ell Res}_{P}(\overline{c})
is finite which means that the range of
\tau_{\mathrm{PurDel}_{P}}
only consists of first-order predicates.
∎

In this section we define a new class for which we can produce first-order witnesses that encompasses one-sided pointed clauses.
It is defined based on a graph that is induced by
N
and
P
.
To define this graph we lay some groundwork.

Definition 53
.

Let
C
be a clause and let
L
be a literal.
Then denote by

the subclause of
C
that consists exactly of the
L
-literals of

, i.e.,
C_{L}:=\left\{L^{\prime}\in C\ |\ \text{$L^{\prime}$ is an $L$-literal}\right\}
.

The following definition introduces a concept that is necessary for the definition of the graph.

Definition 54
.

Let
N
be a clause set, let
P
be a pointed clause and let

be the designated literal of
P
.
Denote by

the set of
P
-resolvable pointed clauses in

Lemma 67

Let

7
Equality and First-Order Background Theories

In this section we show how to use equality reasoning and treat first-order background theories with respect to WSCAN.

7.1
Equality Reasoning

So far the inference calculus
\mathcal{I}
uses equality only for introducing constraints and eliminating them via constraint elimination.
However, our method can also be augmented to construct witnesses in the presence of full equality by adding a paramodulation rule.

C\lor s\simeq t

C^{\prime}(r)

\mathrm{ParMod}

(C\lor C^{\prime}(t))\sigma

where
s,t,r
are terms and
\sigma
is a most general unifier of
s
and
r
.
This makes the inference system refutationally complete for first-order logic with equality.

Proposition 77
.

\mathcal{I}+\mathrm{ParMod}
is sound and refutationally complete for first-order logic with equality.

Proof.

Resolution with factoring together with paramodulation and reflexivity resolution is refutationally complete, see, e.g.,
43
.
So it suffices to show that we can simulate the reflexivity resolution rule

t\not\simeq t\lor C

C

Note that this is an instance of the constraint elimination rule since the identity is a most general unifier of
t
and
t
.
∎

For any sound inference rule
S
added to
\mathcal{I}
we can show that the identity substitution is witness-transforming across

like in the case of constraint resolution, constraint factoring and constraint elimination.
Thus, by setting
\tau_{\mathrm{ParMod}}:=\mathrm{id}
the witness construction proceeds like before.
However, adding a new inference rule does increase the search space for finding
\mathcal{C}
-derivations so this affects performance.

7.2
First-Order Background Theories

Let
T
be a first-order theory, i.e., a set of closed first-order formulas.
We consider WSOQE with respect to the background theory
T
, i.e.,
the
WSOQE problem with respect to
T
is the problem of, given a first-order formula
\varphi
and a tuple of predicate variables
\overline{X}
, finding a substitution
\sigma
with
\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)\subseteq\overline{X}
such that

T\models\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi\leftrightarrow\varphi\sigma.

In that case we call
\sigma
a
WSOQE-witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
with respect to
T
.
For finitely axiomatizable theories
T
we can reduce this problem to the ordinary WSOQE problem.

Proposition 79
.

Let
T
be a finitely axiomatizable first-order theory, let
\varphi
be a first-order formula and let
\sigma
be a substitution with
\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)\subseteq\overline{X}
.
Then the following are equivalent.

1.

\sigma
is a WSOQE-witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
with respect to
T
.

2.

\sigma
is a WSOQE-witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,(T\land\varphi)
.

Proof.

1

\implies

2
: It suffices to show
\models\exists\overline{X}\,(T\land\varphi)\rightarrow(T\land\varphi)\sigma
.
Since
\sigma
is a WSOQE-witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
with respect to
T
we have
T\models\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi\leftrightarrow\varphi\sigma
.
In particular, we get
T\models\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi\rightarrow\varphi\sigma
.
By the deduction theorem and since
T
is finite we get
\models T\rightarrow(\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi\rightarrow\varphi\sigma)
.
By propositional equivalences we then get
\models(T\land\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi)\rightarrow(T\land\varphi\sigma)
.
Since
T
does not contain any variables from
\overline{X}
we further get
\models\exists\overline{X}\,(T\land\varphi)\rightarrow(T\land\varphi)\sigma
.

2

\implies

1
:
It suffices to show
T\models\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi\rightarrow\varphi\sigma
.
Since
\sigma
is a WSOQE-witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,(T\land\varphi)
we get in particular
\models\exists\overline{X}\,(T\land\varphi)\rightarrow(T\land\varphi)\sigma
.
Since
T
does not contain any variables from
\overline{X}
this is equivalent to
\models(T\land\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi)\rightarrow(T\land\varphi\sigma)
.
Thus we get
\models(T\land\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi)\rightarrow\varphi\sigma
and by propositional equivalences we have

\models T\rightarrow(\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi\rightarrow\varphi\sigma)
.
Since
T
consists of closed formulas we then get
T\models\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi\rightarrow\varphi\sigma
.∎

7.3
Graph reachability problems

We introduce a class of background theories where WSCAN can find solutions using equality reasoning although finding a derivation can be challenging in practice.
Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite directed graph and
I,F\subseteq V
.
We want to find a subset
X\subseteq V
of nodes which contains
I
(the initial nodes), is closed under the edge relation of
G
and avoids all nodes in
F
(the fail nodes).
In this case we call
X
a
graph reachability solution for
(G,I,F)
.

We can encode graph reachability problems in the following way.
Consider the set of vertices
V=\left\{1,\dots,n\right\}
and let
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
be distinct fresh constant symbols standing for the nodes in the graph.
Furthermore, let
E
be a binary predicate symbol denoting the edge relation of the graph
G
.
Then the theory of the graph
G
can be axiomatized by a first-order clause set
T(G)
given by

\displaystyle\left\{a_{i}\not\simeq a_{j}\ |\ i\neq j\in V\right\}

\displaystyle\cup

\displaystyle\left\{E(a_{i},a_{j})\ |\ \text{$i$ and $j$ are connected by an edge in $G$}\right\}

\displaystyle\cup

\displaystyle\left\{\neg E(a_{i},a_{j})\ |\ \text{$i$ and $j$ are not connected by an edge in $G$}\right\}

\displaystyle\cup

\displaystyle\left\{u\simeq a_{1}\lor\dots\lor u\simeq a_{n}\right\}.

To be a graph reachability solution,
X
has to satisfy the following clause set.

\displaystyle R(I,F):=\left\{X(a_{i})\ |\ i\in I\right\}\cup\left\{\neg X(a_{i})\ |\ i\in F\right\}\cup\left\{\neg X(u)\lor\neg E(u,v)\lor X(v)\right\}.

Then
(G,I,F)
has a graph reachability solution if and only if
T(G)\models\exists X\,R(I,F)
.
Furthermore, if
(G,I,F)
has a graph reachability solution, then a
\mathrm{WSOQE}
-witness for
\exists X\,R(I,F)
with respect to
T(G)
gives us a first-order definition of a graph reachability solution for
(G,I,F)
.
By
Proposition

79
such a solution is a solution to the ordinary WSOQE problem for
\exists\overline{X}\,(T(G)\land R(I,F))
.

8
Implementation

The presented method is implemented in the Scala programming language
45
as part of the General Architecture for Proof Theory (GAPT) software package
19
, which provides useful data structures for developing algorithms in computational logic, including terms, formulas and clauses.
The prototype is available in version 2.19.0 of GAPT (
https://www.logic.at/gapt/release_archive.html
).
Section 8.4 of the GAPT user manual details how to use the prototype.

To evaluate our witness construction method, we implemented the SCAN algorithm so that, in addition to finding a logically equivalent first-order clause set, it provides a
\mathcal{C}
-derivation from which our method extracts a witness.

In the following, let
\overline{X}
be the tuple of predicate variables to be eliminated.
Our implementation operates in two alternating phases.
First, we run a preprocessing phase where the clause set is simplified by eagerly applying variable elimination, subsumption deletion, tautology deletion and, if applicable, extended purity deletion.
As part of this phase we also add non-redundant constraint factors of all clauses in the clause set.

The second phase is the purification phase, where we pick a clause
C
from the active clause set and an
\overline{X}
-literal
L\in C
which induces the pointed clause
P=C[\underline{L}]
.
Denote by
N
the rest of the active clause set.
We repeatedly add those constraint resolvents
R
between
P
and
N
such that
N\not\trianglelefteq_{L^{\perp},\mathrm{ve}}R
.
During this phase we also eagerly apply variable elimination and subsumption deletion, but to avoid the issues of
Example

8
we do not perform tautology deletion.
This phase runs until the pointed clause
P
is purified in the rest of the clause set, at which point it is removed by purified clause deletion.

Afterwards, the two phases are repeated until no more
\overline{X}
-literals occur in the active clause set.
During the saturation process all performed derivation steps are stored in a
\mathcal{C}
-derivation which is later used to construct a witness.

The choice of pointed clause during the purification phase determines the outcome of the saturation process.
Different choices can lead to termination or non-termination.
This also affects which
\mathcal{C}
-derivations are found.
To find all possible
\mathcal{C}
-derivations from a given clause set,
we implemented backtracking on top of the saturation process, which yields
\mathcal{C}
-derivations based on different choices of pointed clauses.
This backtracking can be performed on-demand and allows us to find multiple derivations and therefore multiple witnesses.

After an
\overline{X}
-eliminating
\mathcal{C}
-derivation is found, we compute a corresponding witness according to
Definition

71
.
For the derivation steps
S
which are not purified clause deletion the computation of
\tau_{S}
is straightforward.
For purified clause deletion steps
N\cup\left\{P\right\}/N
we calculate the minimal
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
P
is
k
-acyclically purified in
N
by iterating all
P
-purification subsumptions
s
for
N
and computing a maximal path length of
G(N,P,s)
while also checking for acyclicity of
G(N,P,s)
.
If
P
is not
k
-acyclically purified in
N
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we resort to computing
\mathrm{\ell Res}_{P}
up to redundancy using the purification process from the SCAN implementation and then use the predicate substitution
\tau_{\mathrm{PurDel}_{P}}
from
Definition

29
.
This might not terminate, but there are cases where
\mathrm{\ell Res}_{P}
is finite up to redundancy while
P
is not
k
-acyclically redundant in
N
.
Since
\mathrm{\ell Res}_{P}
is potentially infinite, we provide parameters that limit the number of derivation steps performed in its computation.

Our prototype implementation has been tested on 44 examples from the file

examples/predicateEliminationProblems.scala
included in GAPT 2.19.0.
These examples were either created by us or selected from the literature.
The tests were run using the OpenJDK
21.0.4 Java Runtime with 16GB heap space on a MacBook Pro M2 with 32GB RAM.
We first run our implementation of the SCAN algorithm to get a derivation and measure the execution time and the length of the derivation.
We limit the search to derivations that eliminate the predicates in at most 50 derivation steps.
Furthermore, for each example we set a timeout of 10 seconds.
If a derivation could be computed, we run the witness computation based on that derivation and measure its execution time separately.
Furthermore, we measure input clause set size and the resulting witness size (if found).
We measure the size of a literal by the number of non-logical symbols it contains.
The size of a clause is given by the sum of the sizes of its literals and the size of a clause set is given by the sum of the sizes of its clauses.
The size of a witness is measured by the number of logical and non-logical symbols it contains.
See the
runWscanBenchmark
method in the file
testing/src/main/scala/testWscan.scala
for the exact test protocol.

The aggregate results are as follows: The input size ranged from 2 to 177 with an average of 17.705.
For 40 of the 44 examples (90.91%) a derivation was found.
Their lengths ranged from 0 (this example had no second-order quantifiers) to 34 with an average of 7.975.
In the remaining 4 cases the timeout of 10 seconds was reached without finding a derivation.
For the 40 terminating examples the running times for SCAN ranged from 0.119 milliseconds to 6181.111
milliseconds with an average of 375.435 milliseconds.
For all of the 40 examples where SCAN terminated, a witness could be computed as well.
The running times for the witness computation given the derivation ranged from 0.009 milliseconds to 4.684 milliseconds with an average of 0.619 milliseconds.
The corresponding witness sizes ranged from 0 (this example had no second-order quantifiers) to 54 with an average of 6.900.
This shows that witness computation takes significantly less time than finding the SCAN derivation itself.

We have not optimized our implementation for large clause sets.
Furthermore, we are not aware of a sufficiently large database of examples to test our method on.
Thus, a more thorough empirical evaluation is left as future work.

9
Discussion and Future Work

In this section we discuss several aspects of our work and possible directions for future work.

First-Order Witnesses for
\overline{X}
-Eliminating Derivations

We have presented two methods for witness construction: one that produces a fixpoint witness for arbitrary
\overline{X}
-eliminating
\mathcal{C}
-derivations, and one that produces a first-order witness for
\overline{X}
-eliminating
\mathcal{C}
-derivations with acyclic purification annotations. We conjecture that it is possible to produce first-order witnesses whenever there is an
\overline{X}
-eliminating
\mathcal{C}
-derivation from a clause set
N
without any further assumptions.

Conjecture 81
.

Let
N
be a clause set.
If there is an
\overline{X}
-eliminating
\mathcal{C}
-derivation from
N
, then there is a first-order
\mathrm{WSOQE}
-witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,N
.

Recursion-Free Horn Clauses

Program verification problems are often encoded as constrained Horn clauses
8
where unknown predicates
X
stand for loop invariants necessary to prove the correctness of a given program.
Solving constrained Horn clauses means finding an interpretation of the unknown predicates
X
.
This is a restriction of the

-problem: Given a formula

with predicate variables in
\overline{X}
, find a first-order predicate substitution
\sigma
such that
\models\varphi\sigma
.
Solving constrained Horn clauses then means solving
\mathrm{FEQ}
where
\varphi
is a constrained Horn clause set.
A particular fragment of constrained Horn clauses, namely

Horn clause sets, has been widely studied, see, e.g.,

;

.

Definition 82
.

Let

be a clause set and let

be predicate variables.
We write

if there is a clause

and

occurs positively in

Denote by

the transitive closure of

.
For a tuple of predicate variables
\overline{X}
we say

\overline{X}
if
\prec_{N}
is acyclic on
\overline{X}
, i.e., there is no predicate variable

.

We suspect that SCAN terminates on this class of clause sets and that our method can produce a
\mathrm{WSOQE}
-witness.

.

Let
N
be a Horn clause and let
\overline{X}
be a tuple of predicate variables such that
N
is recursion-free in
\overline{X}
.
Then there is an
\overline{X}
-eliminating

-derivation from

.

One can then check whether the produced

It may be possible to extend this procedure based on redundancy criteria from resolution theorem proving in order to extend the class where it terminates beyond that of recursion-free constrained Horn clause sets.

Constructing Multiple Witnesses

Different
\mathcal{C}
-derivations can result in different, non-equivalent witnesses. Thus, our method can construct multiple witnesses for a given input clause set.
As mentioned above, our prototype obtains different
\mathcal{C}
-derivations by backtracking over different choices of pointed clauses in the purification process of SCAN.

In practice, certain witnesses might be preferred over others, e.g., based on size.
Accounting for additional constraints like these when constructing witnesses is an avenue for future work.
An additional avenue is to analyze the relative logical strength of different witnesses.

Limitations for Finding Witnesses

The original SCAN algorithm uses Skolemization if the input formula is not in clause form and after the saturation process tries to undo the Skolemization to find a logically equivalent first-order formula.
While this reverse Skolemization is not possible in general, it does work in some cases, for example, if the input formula has the form
\exists\overline{u}\,\varphi
where
\varphi
is quantifier-free.
We discuss such an example where the SCAN algorithm terminates and finds a SOQE-solution, but where no WSOQE-witness exists.

The input formula is
\Phi=\exists X\,\exists u\,\exists v\,(X(u)\land\neg X(v))
.
Skolemization of the first-order part yields the clause set
N=\left\{X(a),\neg X(b)\right\}
with Skolem constants
a
and
b
.
An
X
-eliminating
\mathcal{C}
-derivation from
N
is given by

Assume

\mathcal{M}=\left\{m_{1},m_{2}\right\}

\mathcal{M}\models\exists X\,\exists u\,\exists v\,(X(u)\land\neg X(v)).

Since
\alpha

This shows a fundamental limitation for finding witnesses, even if SOQE-solutions exist.
For practical applications it would be useful to add Skolemization and reverse Skolemization steps to the implementation and investigate classes of formulas where witness construction succeeds in the presence of Skolemization.

Quantifier Alternations

We have discussed the WSOQE problem in the context of existential quantifiers, but one can also consider the dual problem for universal quantifiers:
Given a formula
\forall\overline{X}\,\varphi
with first-order
\varphi
, find first-order predicates
\overline{\alpha}
such that

\forall\overline{X}\,\varphi\equiv\varphi[\overline{X}\leftarrow\overline{\alpha}].

One can reduce this problem to the existential case since for all first-order predicates
\overline{\alpha}
we have
\forall\overline{X}\,\varphi\equiv\varphi[\overline{X}\leftarrow\overline{\alpha}]
if and only if
\exists\overline{X}\,\neg\varphi\equiv\neg\varphi[\overline{X}\leftarrow\overline{\alpha}]
by writing
\forall\overline{X}\,
as
\neg\exists\overline{X}\,\neg
.
This way, one can solve the problem for an arbitrary quantifier prefix by successively eliminating alternating blocks of existential and universal quantifiers starting with the innermost block.

While the reduction above works if one has a general WSOQE method that can handle any input formula,
our method only
works when the input is a clause set,
i.e., the domain variables are universally
quantified.
The above reduction introduces a negation on the input formula,
meaning that we would now have to deal with existential quantifiers on
the domain variables as well, e.g., via Skolemization.
However, as stated before there are fundamental limitations when introducing Skolemization.
For input formulas where the first-order part is quantifier-free our method is applicable though.

Methods for computing forgetting solutions and uniform

For applications such as knowledge processing, agent applications and

Using a WSOQE-solution instead of a SOQE-solution has the advantage that the structure of the
existing knowledge base is left largely intact.
Functionality to return witnesses could

10
Conclusion

We introduced WSCAN, an extension of the SCAN algorithm on clause sets that computes witnesses for existential second-order quantifiers and thereby solves the more general WSOQE-problem.
Given an
\overline{X}
-eliminating derivation
D
from a clause set
N
, we can produce a witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,N
.
This witness may be infinite in general. However, if all purified clause deletions of
D
have a certain finiteness property, such as being acyclically purified, then we can guarantee that the witness is first-order.
To the best of our knowledge there are currently no other WSOQE-algorithms applicable to arbitrary clause sets.
This work paves the way for new applications of the SCAN algorithm in a variety of areas,
for example, modal correspondence theory, knowledge representation or verification.

On a more abstract level, we see this work as a contribution to bridging the gap between
second-order quantifier elimination (SOQE) and solving formula equations (FEQ), which will be of interest to the
respective communities investigating these problems.
SOQE has been studied mostly in the context of modal logic, description logics, knowledge representation and answer set programming.
On the other hand, FEQ is studied (usually in different formalisms and under different names)
in the verification and automated (inductive) theorem proving communities.
We believe that the complex of problems consisting of SOQE, WSOQE, and FEQ provides a suitable

common logical foundation
for work that is done on all of these topics, as has also been suggested in
53
.
This perspective not only allows us to relate these seemingly different problems to one another
on a logical level, but also stimulates cross-fertilization in terms of practical solution
ideas, techniques, and algorithms.
