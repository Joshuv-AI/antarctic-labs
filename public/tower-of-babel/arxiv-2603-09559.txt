Edward Troccoli & Christoph Federrath

Abstract
Turbulence plays a critical role in the atmosphere, oceans, engineering, and astrophysics. The dissipation (heating) induced by turbulent flows is particularly important for the thermodynamics and chemistry of interstellar clouds, yet its structure and statistics remain poorly understood. Using high-resolution turbulence simulations with controlled explicit viscosity, we study the kinetic energy dissipation rate, ε kin \varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}} , across subsonic and supersonic regimes. We find that dissipation lags large-scale kinetic energy injection events by 1.64 ± 0.21 1.64\pm 0.21 and 0.48 ± 0.07 0.48\pm 0.07 turbulent turnover times in subsonic and supersonic turbulence, respectively. Correlations show ε kin ∝ | ∇ × 𝐯 | 2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}\propto|\nabla\times\mathbf{v}|^{2} (vorticity squared) in the subsonic regime, where density fluctuations are negligible, while in the supersonic regime dissipation is primarily correlated with density, ε kin ∝ ρ 3 / 2 \varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}\propto\rho^{3/2} . A spectral analysis demonstrates that achieving numerical convergence of ε kin \varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}} across all scales is challenging, especially in the subsonic case, even at 2048 3 2048^{3} resolution. Nonetheless, subsonic dissipation is clearly localised on small vorticity-dominated scales, while supersonic dissipation spans many scales, combining elongated, thin shocks with small-scale vorticity. Finally, we determine the fractal dimension of ε kin \varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}} . In the subsonic regime, intense dissipation is predominantly organised in flattened vortex filaments embedded in thin shearing layers on small scales, becoming more volume-filling at larger scales. In the supersonic regime, ε kin \varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}} exhibits a fractal dimension between 1 and 2 across nearly all scales, likely reflecting shock surfaces and their intersections forming filaments.

Introduction

Turbulence remains a largely unsolved physical process
(
38
;
5
)
. It is fundamentally chaotic, characterised by an energy cascade from large scales (driving) to small scales (dissipation). The study of turbulence is relevant in both industrial and academic contexts, with key applications in accurate weather prediction, climate modelling, ocean mixing
(
26
;
63
)
, and in engines and turbines
(
27
;
39
)
. Moreover, many astrophysical phenomena are strongly influenced, or even controlled, by turbulent flows, such as those in the interstellar medium (ISM)
(
22
;
58
)
, where turbulence plays a key role in star formation
(

;

;
46
;
29
;
52
;
20
;
45
)
. Despite its importance, turbulence — and in particular its dissipation — remains poorly understood.

The dissipation of kinetic energy has significant implications
(
64
;
55
)
in astrophysics. In general, turbulent dissipation governs the rate at which large-scale injected energy is converted into heat, thereby catalysing chemical reactions and altering the thermodynamics of star-forming clouds
(
30
;
10
)
. Dissipation is a highly intermittent process
(
53
;
MomferratosLesaffreFalgaronePineaudesFor^ets2014
;
66
)
, observed primarily on small scales
(
32
;
11
;
48
;
12
)
,
a feature that has been confirmed in laboratory experiments

(
1
)
.It occurs via two main pathways: small-scale vortices
(
6
;
24
)
, and — in the case of supersonic turbulence relevant for molecular clouds — via shocks
(
4
;
40
;
21
)
. These structures thus provide signatures of dissipation and are a main focus of this study.

Small-scale vortices associated with dissipation are typically found in subsonic turbulence and tend to occupy 3D space in a fractal manner
(
48
)
. Specifically, the observed correlation with vorticity arises because most of the dissipation occurs in the shear layers wrapped around vortex tubes, where the turbulent rate of strain is strongest
(
50
)
, producing thin, ribbon-like dissipative structures
(
51
)
. In contrast, shocks dominate supersonic turbulence, appearing as elongated, thin sheets which, upon collision, form filamentary structures at their intersections. Shock collisions therefore localise dissipation into intermittent structures
(
53
)
and introduce an additional channel for supersonic dissipation beyond vortex-driven processes.

In this work, we determine the structure and statistics of dissipation by implementing a new method to measure the rate at which local kinetic energy dissipates in fully-developed turbulence. We use numerical simulations on discretised grids up to
2048^{3}
cells, evolving the kinetic energy equation of hydrodynamics. Combined with the standard hydrodynamical equations, particularly the momentum equation, this allows us to compute the local dissipation rate. At each time step, we calculate the kinetic energy conventionally using the momentum and continuity equations, and compare it to the evolved kinetic energy equation. The difference between these quantities, per unit time, directly yields the dissipation rate. This formulation naturally accounts for both explicit (viscous) and numerical (resolution-dependent) dissipation. To minimise the latter, we include explicit viscosity terms and perform simulations at varying grid resolutions to assess convergence.

2
Methods

2.1
Hydrodynamic equations

The turbulent systems studied here are described by the compressible, three-dimensional, hydrodynamic (HD) equations, given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=0,

(1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v})+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot(2\nu\rho\bm{\mathcal{S}})+\rho\mathbf{F},

(2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}E+\nabla[(E+p)\mathbf{v}]

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot[2\nu\rho\mathbf{v}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{S}}]+\rho\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{F},

(3)

where
\rho
denotes the density,
\mathbf{v}
the velocity,
p
the thermal pressure, and
\otimes
denotes the outer product of two vectors, i.e.,
\mathbf{q}\otimes\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{q}\mathbf{q}^{\mathrm{T}}
. The total energy density,
E=\rho e_{\textrm{int}}+\rho|\mathbf{v}|^{2}/2
, is the sum of the internal and kinetic energy densities, respectively. Explicit viscous dissipation is included through the traceless rate of strain tensor,
\mathbf{\mathcal{S}}_{ij}=(1/2)(\partial_{i}v_{j}+\partial_{j}v_{i})-(1/3)\delta_{ij}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}
, with the amount of kinematic viscosity determined by
\nu
. To close the HD equations we use a polytropic equation of state (EOS)
p=c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\rho
, such that the gas remains isothermal with a constant sound speed
c_{\mathrm{s}}
. It should be noted that this EOS implies that the baroclinic vorticity is zero
(
47
)
. As turbulence transports energy from large to small scales, where it is dissipated, we need to drive the turbulence on large scales, which is achieved by the acceleration field described through the
\rho\mathbf{F}
term in Equation (
2
). Here, we work in the purely HD case (no magnetic fields), and leave the case including magnetic fields and its dissipation for future work.

2.2
Kinetic energy equation

Since we are primarily interested in studying the rate of kinetic energy dissipation, we first need to consider the evolution equation of the kinetic energy,
E_{\mathrm{kin}}
. The evolution equation for
E_{\mathrm{kin}}
is derived by first computing the dot product of the momentum Eq. (
2
) with
\mathbf{v}
. After a few steps involving use of the continuity Eq. (
1
), we find (see Appendix
A
for details),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}E_{\mathrm{kin}}+\nabla\cdot\left[(E_{\mathrm{kin}}+p)\mathbf{v}\right]=p\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}.

(4)

This equation describes the evolution of kinetic energy without any dissipation, neither from physical, explicit terms, through the viscosity set in Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
), nor by numerical effects. We also note that the turbulence forcing term (
\mathbf{F}
) is not included here, because we want to measure dissipation, not injection. In fact, the forcing term is operator split from the HD time step, and as explained below, it is therefore not part of the HD update and consequently not relevant for computing the dissipation rate.

2.3
Dissipation rate

Using Eq. (
4
), we obtain the ‘perfectly’ evolved kinetic energy, without contributions from dissipation, i.e., from viscous numerical and explicit terms, which act to reduce
E_{\mathrm{kin}}
via transfer to thermal energy on small scales. However, in a standard hydrodynamic code, Eq. (
4
) is normally not evolved. Instead, the kinetic energy is obtained after solving the continuity and momentum Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
) as
(1/2)\rho|{\mathbf{v}}|^{2}
, and this quantity is subject to dissipation. Thus, by evolving the standard set of hydro Eqs. (
1
)–(
3
) together with Eq. (
4
), we obtain the dissipated kinetic energy as the difference between
E_{\mathrm{kin}}
from Eq. (
4
) and
(1/2)\rho|{\mathbf{v}}|^{2}
.

In practice, at the start of a HD time step
\Delta t
, at time
t
, we (re)set
E_{\mathrm{kin}}(t)=(1/2)\rho(t)|{\mathbf{v}(t)}|^{2}
and update it via Eq. (
4
) to obtain
E_{\mathrm{kin}}(t+\Delta t)
. Meanwhile, the standard set of HD equations is solved as well, in particular Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
), to obtain the updated density,
\rho(t+\Delta t)
and velocity,
\mathbf{v}(t+\Delta t)
, which defines a kinetic energy after the HD time step
(1/2)\,\rho(t+\Delta t)\,|{\mathbf{v}(t+\Delta t)}|^{2}
, but this one was subject to dissipation. The difference between these two quantities then defines the kinetic energy dissipation rate
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}
through

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}(t+\Delta t)\equiv\frac{E_{\mathrm{kin}}(t+\Delta t)-(1/2)\,\rho(t+\Delta t)\,|{\mathbf{v}(t+\Delta t)}|^{2}}{\Delta t}.

(5)

The quantity
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}
is therefore defined locally in each grid cell. We can obtain the total dissipation rate per time step by integrating over space.

2.4.1
Basic simulation code

We solve the HD equations (Eqs.
1
–
3
) with a modified version of the astrophysical code FLASH
(
25
)
(based on version 4), using the HLL5R 5-wave approximate Riemann solver
(
3
;
65
)
. This is done on a uniformly discretised, triply-periodic, 3D grid with side length
L
and
N^{3}
grid cells.

2.4.2
Turbulence driving

To drive turbulence and maintain a constant rms kinetic energy injection rate
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{inj}}
, we require an acceleration field,
\mathbf{F}
, which appears in Eq. (
2
). To construct
\mathbf{F}
we use an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process
(
9
;
59
;
17
)
as implemented in the publicly available turbulence driving module
TurbGen

(
16
)
, which generates a time sequence of random Fourier modes via the OU process. All simulations presented in this paper employ
TurbGen
with a turbulence driving pattern with characteristic driving wave number
k_{\mathrm{driv}}=2
, where
k
is in units of
2\pi/L
, i.e., the driving scale is
\ell_{\mathrm{driv}}=1/2
in units of
L
. Thus, energy is primarily injected on half the box scale. The driving amplitude falls off parabolically on both sides of the peak, reaching exactly zero at
k=1
and
3
. Thus, only a narrow range of wave numbers is driven, maximising the self-consistent development of fully-developed turbulence for
k\gtrsim 3
.

The large-scale eddy turnover time is the characteristic time scale of the turbulence, defined as
t_{\mathrm{turb}}=\ell_{\mathrm{driv}}/v_{\mathrm{turb}}
, where
v_{\mathrm{turb}}
is the turbulent velocity dispersion on the driving scale. For brevity,
t_{\mathrm{turb}}
will be referred to as the turnover time throughout the paper. The driving amplitude is set such that the sonic Mach number,
{\mathcal{M}}=v_{\mathrm{turb}}/c_{\mathrm{s}}=0.2
and
5
for the subsonic and supersonic simulations, respectively. Turbulence becomes fully developed after
\sim 2t_{\mathrm{turb}}

(
18
;
54
)
, and therefore all measurements of turbulent quantities will focus on
t/t_{\mathrm{turb}}\geq 2
.

2.4.3
Initial conditions and simulation parameters

We compare subsonic (
{\mathcal{M}}=0.2
) and supersonic (
{\mathcal{M}}=5
) simulations, and study numerical convergence by using grid resolutions of
N=256
,
512
,
1024
, and
2048
, as summarised in Tab. (
1
). We set the sound speed
c_{\mathrm{s}}=1
along with the initial density
\rho=\langle\rho\rangle=1
. Therefore, all quantities with units of length, time, velocity, density, and wave number are presented in units of
\ell_{\mathrm{driv}}
,
t_{\mathrm{turb}}
,
c_{\mathrm{s}}
,
\langle\rho\rangle
and
2\pi/L
, respectively. Kinetic energy is expressed in units of
\langle\rho\rangle{\mathcal{M}}^{2}c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}
, and energy dissipation/injection rates in units of
\langle\rho\rangle{\mathcal{M}}^{2}c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}t_{\mathrm{turb}}^{-1}
. Spatial derivatives are in units of
\Delta x^{-1}
, with the cell side length
\Delta x=L/N
.

Across all simulations, we prescribe a constant kinematic viscosity
\nu
,
(
41
;
36
)
as opposed to a constant dynamic viscosity,
\mu=\nu\rho
, so that we can set and control a global Reynolds number

\mathrm{Re}=\frac{\ell_{\mathrm{driv}}v_{\mathrm{turb}}}{\nu}.

(6)

This allows us to set a fixed
\mathrm{Re}
in both the subsonic and supersonic set of simulations. Using a fixed dynamic viscosity would not allow us to set a clean Reynolds number, as
\rho
varies substantially in the supersonic case. Setting a fixed
\nu
instead of a fixed
\mu
further ensures that the dissipation wave number,
k_{\nu}
, is a global quantity, determined by the Reynolds number
(
35
)
. Moreover, there is an additional complication with setting
\mu
constant in supersonic turbulence, because with constant
\mu
, the time step
\Delta t\sim\Delta x^{2}/\nu=\Delta x^{2}\rho/\mu
would become density-dependent. This leads to the computations becoming prohibitively expensive given the very large density variations present in supersonic turbulence, with under-densities of several orders of magnitude relative to the mean density.

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}
, which we defined in Section

We see that the dissipation rate in the subsonic regime is visually volume filling compared to very localised shock filaments in supersonic flows. Nevertheless, as a function of space, there are orders of magnitude variation in

in both regimes. In the subsonic case, dissipation structures are present as thick, somewhat elongated structures, and are frequent throughout the domain. This is in contrast to the supersonic case where the dissipative structures span the domain as very long and narrow shocks. It is important to note that as we produce slices of the

We also observe that the bottom left panel of Figure (

(

. Therefore, we do not expect a correlation of the dissipation rate with density, but instead perhaps with the vorticity of the gas
(

structures. Moreover, throughout the domain, we observe a complex network of interacting shocks, which intermittently collide, forming knot-like structures. Within these collisions, the gas behaves highly chaotically and introduces vorticity at the site of the collision due to the contribution of a logarithmic density gradient term in the vorticity evolution equation

. This indicates that these collisions may provide an additional channel for dissipation.

In the following, we provide a quantitative investigation of the correlations of

with density and vorticity, as well as the dimensionality of these structures through fractal analysis. Before that, however, we examine the time evolution and temporal correlations between the injection and dissipation of kinetic energy.

3.2.2
Time correlation between injection and dissipation

We see in the bottom panels of Figure
2
that there is a time lag between
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{inj}}
and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}
, in that dissipation seems to lag injection of energy. Large-scale kinetic energy injected into the system falls down the turbulent cascade in some finite time, which we define as the ‘time lag’,
\Delta t_{\mathrm{inj-diss}}
. We expect some correlation between peaks of dissipation and peaks of injection, which is due to the large-scale driving,
\mathbf{F}
in Eq. (
2
). This is indeed what is observed in the bottom panels of Figure (
2
) throughout the
10\,t_{\mathrm{turb}}
. We determine the time lag between injection and dissipation by first defining the time-lagged difference between injection and dissipation, as

q(t,\Delta t_{\mathrm{inj-diss}})\equiv\varepsilon_{\mathrm{inj}}(t)-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}(t+\Delta t_{\mathrm{inj-diss}}).

(7)

We then compute a normalised time-integral of this,

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{inj-diss}}(\Delta t_{\mathrm{inj-diss}})=\frac{\int\left(q(t,\Delta t_{\mathrm{inj-diss}})-\frac{\int q(t,\Delta t_{\mathrm{inj-diss}})\,dt}{\int dt}\right)^{2}\,dt}{\int\varepsilon_{\mathrm{inj}}(t)\,dt},

(8)

which provides the time lag of maximum correlation between injection and dissipation when
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{inj-diss}}(\Delta t_{\mathrm{inj-diss}})
is minimal.

In practice, we take the dissipation and injection rate of the Sub/SupN2048 simulations and partition them into two time windows of
1<t_{\mathrm{turb}}<5
and
5<t_{\mathrm{turb}}<9
and then shift them by
\Delta t_{\mathrm{inj-diss}}
, which we continuously increase from
0\,t_{\mathrm{turb}}
to
2\,t_{\mathrm{turb}}
in steps of
t_{\mathrm{turb}}/100
. For each
\Delta t_{\mathrm{inj-diss}}
, we compute
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{inj-diss}}
, as shown in Figure
6
. From this we find the
\Delta t_{\mathrm{inj-diss}}
for which
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{inj-diss}}
is minimised, which yields
\Delta t_{\mathrm{inj-diss}}/t_{\mathrm{turb}}=1.64\pm 0.21
and
0.48\pm 0.07
for SubN2048 and SupN2048, respectively. We also compute
\Delta t_{\mathrm{inj-diss}}
for all other resolutions and report them in column 6 of Tab.
1
, where we find convergence for
N\gtrsim 1024
. As we partition our time domain into two windows, we also obtain an uncertainty in
\Delta t_{\mathrm{inj-diss}}
across the two subsets.

Physically, the value of
\Delta t_{\mathrm{inj-diss}}
that minimises
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{inj-diss}}
is the time it takes for large-scale injection of energy to cross the turbulent ‘cascade’ to where this energy dissipates, which may be on different scales, but likely primarily on small scales. The immediate implication of the measured values of
\Delta t_{\mathrm{inj-diss}}
is that dissipation occurs on timescales of the order of
t_{\mathrm{turb}}
, but is a factor of
\sim 1.5\times
slower than
t_{\mathrm{turb}}
for subsonic turbulence and a factor
\sim 2\times
faster than
t_{\mathrm{turb}}
for supersonic turbulence. There are three phenomena that may contribute to this. First, it is likely related to the steeper velocity spectrum in supersonic compared to subsonic turbulence
(
21
)
. Second, it is possibly a consequence of shocks ‘jumping’ the cascade, i.e., introducing non-locality in wave number space, compared to subsonic turbulence, where a
k

k

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}

Having established a strong correlation between dissipation and density in the supersonic regime, we now turn to the subsonic case. Here, the relevant quantity is the vorticity of the velocity field. The 2D PDFs of

case on the other hand is dominated by shocks. In isolation, these shocks correspond to 2D planes, however, when they collide they form 1D filaments. Therefore, to quantify the dimensionality of these dissipation structures on different scales, we now perform a fractal dimension analysis of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}
in both the subsonic and supersonic regime.

3.5.1
Method

Multiple definitions and estimators have been proposed to quantify the fractal dimension of hierarchical structures such as turbulence, including the box-counting, perimeter–area, and mass–radius methods
(
43
;
44
;
49
;
57
;
37
;
15
)
.
Given that
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}
is a real-valued field embedded in 3D, we adopt the mass–radius approach, constructing
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}(\ell)
and inferring the scaling exponent from the relation

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}(\ell)\propto\ell^{D},

(9)

where
\ell
denotes the diameter of a spherical region and
D
is the inferred fractal dimension. In general,
D\to D(\ell)
may itself vary with
\ell
, in which case the field is referred to as multifractal. It has been shown that multifractal models of turbulence can also be theoretically linked to the Navier-Stokes equations
(
7
)
.

We begin by identifying the grid cell with the maximum value of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}
. Concentric spheres of increasing diameter
\ell
are then constructed around this position, with
\ell
varied to span the entire domain, i.e.
\ell\in[\Delta x,L]
in increments of
2\Delta x
. For each sphere, we compute and record the total enclosed
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}
, applying triply periodic boundary conditions to avoid truncation at the domain boundaries. A linear regression is then performed in log–log space over a local range of
\ell
to determine
D
within that interval. Specifically, we measure
D
near the dissipation scale
\ell_{\nu}

(
35
, estimated for
\mathrm{Re}=2500
following)
and on large scales,
0.2\leq\ell/L\leq 0.6
, to quantify the global, volume-filling dimensionality of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}
.

We note for context that an alternative, complementary route to probing the geometry of dissipation is via intermittency analysis of velocity structure functions
(
24
)
. In multifractal models, the anomalous scaling exponents of the velocity structure functions can be related to the spectrum of singularities of the dissipation field, and hence to its effective fractal (or multifractal) dimension. This approach has been used extensively in subsonic turbulence
(
61
;
7
)
and extended to supersonic regimes relevant for molecular clouds
(
2
;
60
;
33
)
. A systematic intermittency-based comparison of our datasets is left for future work.

3.5.2
Results

Figure
5
shows
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}(\ell/L)
for the subsonic and supersonic case, and for different numerical resolutions. Broadly comparing the subsonic and supersonic cases, we see that there is considerably more resolution- and scale-dependence of the fractal dimension
D
than in the supersonic case, which shows a relatively uniform power-law scaling. We quantify
D
by fitting power laws with Eq. (
9
) in two regions of
\ell
: one on small scales, near the dissipation scale,
\ell_{\nu}
, where dissipation is maximal (cf. Fig.
4
), and another one on large scales to quantify the overall volume-filling dimensionality of the dissipation structures. Both power-law fits are performed over a factor of
3
in scale, providing sufficient scale range to fit a power law data and at the same time providing a scale-local measure of
D
.

In the case of subsonic turbulence we find
D=1.99\pm 0.07
near
\ell_{\nu}
, i.e. close to 2D, which implies that intense dissipation is predominantly organised in thin shear layers that appear as ribbon- or sheet-like structures. These sheets occur in close association with flattened vortex tubes, consistent with experimental and numerical studies showing clusters of vortex tubes embedded in layer-like regions of strong shear, with sheet-like dissipation adjacent to the tubes
(
50
;
51
;
23
)
. In Figure
1
(top left panel), we saw that the dissipation is volume filling on scales that are comparable to the box size. Thus, another power law is fitted for
0.2\leq\ell/L\leq 0.6
, which indicates a fractal dimension of
D=2.56\pm 0.18
in the subsonic case, indicative of a fairly volume-filling quantity. While we do not perform an explicit fit for the fractal dimension for
\ell\lesssim\ell_{\nu}
due to non-convergence for
\ell\lesssim\ell_{\nu}
, we see an indication that on very small scales, the data is compatible with
D\sim 1
, which indicates thin vortex filaments — however, this result is not conclusive given the large resolution dependence in that scale range.

Conversely, the fractal dimension of the supersonic dissipation rate is relatively invariant throughout all scales. Indeed, we see that near the dissipation scale
\ell_{\nu}
, the fractal dimension is
D=1.60\pm 0.07
, which suggests that the dissipation rate is neither exactly 1D nor 2D, but instead a combination of both. This indicates that the structures formed are a mixture of dissipation sheets (
D=2
)
(
55
)
and filaments (
D=1
), both due to shocks surfaces, where the latter represents intersections of those. As this balance is not even (i.e.,
D\gtrsim 1.5
), suggesting that sheets are slightly more frequent than filaments. This is consistent with the fact that an intersection requires at least two dissipation sheets. For the large-scale power law fit in the region of
[0.2,0.6]\ell/L
we find
D=1.35\pm 0.05
, lower than in the dissipation range. This indicates that on box size scales, the dissipation is not predominately structured in planes, but is now more weighted towards filamentary structures. Finally we note that the supersonic case may also have contributions from vortex dissipation on small scales (cf. bottom right-hand panel of Fig.
3
), and hence,
D
may be somewhat biased towards more 2D structures on small scales, similar to the subsonic case. This contribution from the vorticity arises due to a logarithmic density gradient term in the vorticity evolution equation
(
47
;
14
)
meaning regions of high density variation (e.g. shocks) lead to the observed vorticity. This is further supported by the sonic scale
\ell_{\mathrm{s}}
shown in Fig.
5
, which is in this case located close to
\ell_{\nu}
.

4
Conclusions

In this work, we used high-resolution numerical simulations to study the statistics and structure of dissipation in subsonic and supersonic turbulence. We solve the hydrodynamic equations with explicit viscosity corresponding to a Reynolds number of
\mathrm{Re}=2500
for different grid resolutions from
256^{3}
to
2048^{3}
cells to study numerical convergence of all analysis quantities, in particular the kinetic energy dissipation rate,
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}
. We quantify its morphology (Section
3.1
), time evolution (Section
3.2
), correlation with density and vorticity (Section
3.3
), power spectra (Section
3.4
), and fractal dimension (Section
3.5
). Our key results are summarised below:

1.

We assessed the morphology of the dissipation rate, and the density through visualisations (see Figure
1
). In the subsonic case, we find that there is qualitatively no correlation between
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}
and
\rho
but instead with vorticity,
|\nabla\times\mathbf{v}|
. This differs from the supersonic case in which we observe a clear correlation between
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}
and
\rho
due to the presence of shocks.

2.

We then considered the time evolution of the sonic Mach number and dissipation rate. We find that fully-developed turbulence is achieved at
t=2\,t_{\mathrm{turb}}
and only use data beyond this time for subsequent analyses. The time lag between a kinetic energy injection event, and its eventual dissipation is measured as
\Delta t_{\mathrm{inj-diss}}/t_{\mathrm{turb}}=1.64\pm 0.21
for
{\mathcal{M}}=0.2
and
\Delta t_{\mathrm{inj-diss}}/t_{\mathrm{turb}}=0.48\pm 0.07
when
{\mathcal{M}}=5
. This indicates that given some large-scale injection event, we expect that energy to dissipate a factor
\sim 3\times
faster in supersonic turbulence compared to subsonic turbulence.

3.

A quantitative correlation study between
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}
and both the density
\rho
, and vorticity
|\nabla\times\mathbf{v}|
is conducted through PDFs (see Figure
3
). We confirm that the subsonic dissipation rate has no correlation with density but instead follows a relationship
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}\propto|\nabla\times\mathbf{v}|^{2}
; in particular, it is the rate of strain that correlates with the square of vorticity. Conversely, the dissipation rate in supersonic turbulence primarily correlates with the density as
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}\propto\rho^{3/2}
, and there is additional weaker correlation also with the vorticity or rate of strain.

4.

Fourier spectra of the velocity field (see Figure
4
) are broadly consistent with previous works
(
31
;
4
)
but require a compensation of
k^{-1.6}
and
k^{-2.2}
to flatten the scaling (‘inertial’) range in subsonic and supersonic regimes, respectively. The spectra of the dissipation rate reveal that dissipation in subsonic turbulence is remarkably challenging to properly simulate, showing almost no convergence at high wave numbers, even at a
2048^{3}
grid resolution and moderate Reynolds number of
2500
, motivating future work. Nonetheless, a peak in the spectra at around
k\sim 30
–
50
shows that the dissipation is restricted to small scales in subsonic turbulence. The supersonic case instead shows that dissipation is present at effectively the same power on all scales, with two weak peaks at around
k\sim 10
and
k\sim 10^{2}
indicating that shocks causing dissipation
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}\propto\rho^{3/2}
are both thin and long structures with an added small-scale dissipation component from vortices.

5.

Finally, a fractal study (see Figure
5
) of the dissipation rate is used to determine the fractal dimension of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}
near the dissipation scale
\ell_{\nu}
, and also on box-size scales. In subsonic turbulence we find
D\sim 2
near
\ell_{\nu}
, indicating that intense dissipation is predominantly organised in thin shear layers associated with flattened vortex tubes, consistent with previous experimental work
(
23
)
. In contrast, the dissipation rate in supersonic turbulence has a dimensionality of
D\sim 1.6
near the dissipation scale and
D\sim 1.4
on large scales, reflecting that dissipation occurs primarily in shock sheets (
D=2
) and their filamentary intersections (
D=1
), consistent with the visual analysis of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}
in Figure
1
.

The results of this work quantify and emphasise the fundamentally distinct statistics and structure of kinetic energy dissipation in subsonic and supersonic turbulence. This may provide theoretical input for studies on the heating properties of turbulence in the interstellar medium, its locality, scale dependence, structure, and statistics. Future work needs to address the challenges in achieving numerical convergence of the wave number-dependence of the dissipation rate in subsonic turbulence, as well as the role of magnetic fields, i.e., by solving the full magnetohydrodynamic equations. As an extension of the analysis we provide, one could quantify dissipation localisation further by computing the fraction of total dissipation rate,
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{kin}}
, arising from the most dissipative volume fraction, following
55
. This is particularly relevant for modelling chemistry/phase changes driven by intermittent heating.

Appendix A
Derivation of kinetic energy equation

Here we provide the derivation of the kinetic energy equation in Section
2.2
. To proceed, we will require both Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
). The kinetic energy is
\propto\rho|{\mathbf{v}}|^{2}
, so first take the dot product of Eq. (
2
) with
\mathbf{v}
and observing that it can also be conveniently expressed in component form as

v_{i}\partial_{t}(\rho v_{i})+v_{i}\partial_{j}(\rho v_{i}v_{j})=-v_{i}\partial_{i}p.

(10)

Applying the product rule and using
v_{i}\partial_{t}v_{i}=\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{t}v_{i}^{2}
and
v_{i}\partial_{j}v_{i}=\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{j}v_{i}^{2}
, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\rho(\partial_{t}v_{i}^{2}+v_{j}\partial_{j}v_{i}^{2})+\frac{1}{2}v_{i}^{2}(\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{j}(\rho v_{j}))=-v_{i}\partial_{i}p.

(11)

The continuity equation eliminates the second term. Recognising the remaining terms as a divergence, we write

\partial_{t}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^{2}\right)+\partial_{j}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^{2}v_{j}\right)=-v_{i}\partial_{i}p.

(12)

Defining the kinetic energy density
E_{\mathrm{kin}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^{2}
, this becomes

\partial_{t}E_{\mathrm{kin}}+\nabla\cdot(E_{\mathrm{kin}}\mathbf{v})=-\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla p.

(13)

Finally, using
\nabla\cdot(p\mathbf{v})=p\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla p
, we obtain

\partial_{t}E_{\mathrm{kin}}+\nabla\cdot\left[(E_{\mathrm{kin}}+p)\mathbf{v}\right]=p\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v},

(14)

as stated in Section
2.3
.
