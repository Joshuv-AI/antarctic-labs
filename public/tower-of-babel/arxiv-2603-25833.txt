Shion Andrew, Juan Mena-Parra, Haochen Wang, Antonios Argyriou, Kiyoshi W. Masui, Bridget C. Andersen, Kevin Bandura, Matt Dobbs, Nina V. Gusinskaia, Afrokk Khan, Adam E. Lanman, Mattias Lazda, Calvin Leung, Kenzie Nimmo, Robert Pascua, Aaron B. Pearlman, Alexander W. Pollak, Gurman Sachdeva, Kendrick Smith

Abstract
The sensitivity of radio telescopes is becoming increasingly limited by the presence of radio frequency interference (RFI), which will worsen as the radio spectrum becomes more crowded. One context where this poses a challenge is the field of fast radio burst (FRB) science, where there is increasing scientific interest in capturing as large of a population of bursts as possible and accurately measuring their celestial coordinates using interferometry . With several modern radio facilities actively collecting data for large FRB surveys that will be transformative to the field, properly mitigating unwanted interference is essential for the science goals of these surveys to be met. In this work, we present variations of a spatial filter based on the Karhunen–Loève (KL) Transform to enhance the sensitivity of radio interferometers and demonstrate its applicability to FRB detection and localization. We derive a particular variation of the filter for the case of point-like radio pulses, which we show reduces to the maximum-signal-to-noise beamformer. We apply this filter to CHIME/FRB baseband data and demonstrate its capability to enhance the sensitivity and overall localization rate of CHIME/FRB Outriggers. We compare the cross-correlation signal-to-noise obtained using the spatial filter with that obtained using a spectral-kurtosis RFI flagger for a sample of 100 FRBs recorded by CHIME and its Outriggers, and show that this filter will double the total number of FRBs successfully localized with the CHIME/FRB Outrigger telescopes. While demonstrated here in the context of CHIME/FRB Outriggers, the spatial filter presented in this work—which we have made publicly available—is broadly applicable to other interferometric radio facilities engaged in FRB science and transient detection, including next-generation telescopes such as CHORD, DSA-2000, BURSTT, and CHARTS.



I
Introduction

Modern radio telescopes are continually pushing the limits of sensitivity in an effort to detect fainter and more distant sources. However, in practice the sensitivity of radio observations is often limited by sources of radio-frequency interference (RFI), which in many cases can be several orders of magnitude brighter than the desired astrophysical signal
(
30
;
2
;
9
)
. With RFI becoming increasingly prevalent and the radio band becoming more crowded, simultaneous progress in the development of RFI mitigation techniques is needed.

There are many different levels at which RFI mitigation strategies are employed, from physical measures at the telescope site
(
33
)
to post-processed data analysis techniques. The latter typically involves some kind of data flagging—first detecting then removing RFI in time, frequency, and/or spatial domains
(
14
;
31
;
35
;
32
, see e.g.)
. It is important to stress that the efficacy of different RFI mitigation algorithms is highly context specific, depending on properties of the radio telescope being used, the astrophysical signal of interest, and the local RFI environment.

One particularly powerful RFI mitigation strategy well-suited to radio interferometers is spatial filtering of RFI from the desired signal through adaptive beamforming
(
37
)
. This technique is adaptive because the beamforming
weights—which determine the effective beam response—are derived from the measured spatial covariance of the RFI across the array
. Since no a-priori knowledge about the RFI source is required, this technique is particularly useful for types of RFI that are unpredictable in time and frequency. It can also be useful when very faint signals are degraded by persistent, low-level and broadband RFI that cannot be temporally or spectrally excised from the desired signal with traditional flagging methods. Since spatial filters involve “spatially excising” out RFI, they perform better the more separable the signal of interest is relative to the unwanted interference in antenna space. In addition to depending on the array geometry and total number of antennas, the separability will depend on the spatial signatures of both the interference and the signal. For instance, point-like sources produce well-defined and highly coherent spatial responses, in contrast to sources that are extended or diffuse. When both the signal of interest and the unwanted interference are compact and originate from different directions, their spatial modes are unlikely to have strong overlap.

There are several reasons
why
spatial filters are particularly relevant for studying fast radio bursts (FRBs). FRBs are extragalactic radio transients of unknown astrophysical origins, and because there is a growing scientific interest in localizing as many FRBs as possible to their host galaxies, several next-generation interferometers are being commissioned to capture and precisely localize a larger population of bursts
(
17
;
38
;
22
;
13
;
6
)
. Both the point-like and transient nature of FRBs, as well as the large interferometric arrays that are being constructed to observe them, align naturally with the capabilities of spatial filters, which can adaptively concentrate and suppress sensitivity in different directions. While spatial filtering can be significantly complicated in contexts such as imaging by its capacity to distort beam shape
(
3
)
, this is irrelevant when studying point sources fully described by a single spatial mode. Moreover, the problem of “subspace smearing”
(
18
)
—which arises when the source of interference moves significantly relative to the telescope over the time of the target observation and is therefore spatially “smeared”—is also significantly mitigated in the context of FRB science where pulses are observed on
\lesssim 1
s timescales.

For very-long-baseline-interferometry (VLBI) networks, differing RFI environments at each site lead to even larger data loss because cross-correlation is only possible in spectral windows that are free of RFI at all sites. Consequently, for “core-outrigger” type networks composed of a central interferometric detector outfitted with smaller stand-alone interferometers—such as the Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment / Fast Radio Burst (CHIME/FRB) Outriggers
(
13
)
—fast radio bursts detected by the core can be lost in VLBI cross-correlations due to RFI contaminating additional channels at the outrigger stations. As one of the leading survey-oriented experiments aiming to maximize the total number of precisely localized FRBs, where flux-limited sensitivity directly constrains the accessible survey volume, CHIME/FRB Outriggers will depend heavily on RFI mitigation strategies to fully realize its science goals.

In this work, we demonstrate the performance of a Karhunen–Loève Transform (KLT) based spatial filter on improving the signal-to-noise of radio pulses in baseband data from the CHIME/FRB instrument. This work is particularly interested in mitigating the number of astrophysical transient sources lost to RFI
(
32
, for the related issue of “false positives”, which are particularly relevant in real time backend searches for transients, see)
. We demonstrate that one of the central advantages of applying this filter in CHIME/FRB baseband data is recovering pulses that were
previously unusable for VLBI
.

In Section
II
, we develop a formalism to describe the response of an interferometer to a polarized radio sky. In Section
III
we present the KL beamformer in the context of signal-to-noise optimization and demonstrate its application for optimal filtering of point source emission. We compare the results of the conventional beamformer to the KL beamformer on real CHIME data in Section
IV
, and demonstrate its utility within the context of very long baseline interferometry with CHIME/FRB Outriggers. Caveats and future work are discussed in Section
V
.

III.1.2
Signal-to-Noise optimization and KL beamforming

As shown in Section
III.1.1
, the pointed beams of the conventional beamformer maximize signal power in a particular direction of the sky. However, as explained in
24
, this beamformer is not necessarily optimal in that it does not necessarily maximize the signal-to-noise ratio,
S/N
, given by

\displaystyle S/N=\frac{\bm{b}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\bm{b}}{\bm{b}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\bm{b}}.

(17)

In the CHIME/FRB real-time transient search backend, it is
S/N
, and not signal power, that is used to determine whether a transient event becomes an FRB candidate and whether baseband data are saved to disk for further analysis. Likewise, in CHIME/FRB Outriggers,
S/N
, rather than signal power, is used to assess the reliability of VLBI fringes and to determine whether a refined FRB localization is possible.

A non-trivial
\mathbf{F}
(
\mathbf{F}\not\propto\mathbf{I}
) can arise from noise couplings between the antennas of the array or from unwanted sky signals—including RFI. In those cases we can optimize the beamforming weights to enhance signal power while controlling the effect of these unwanted signals on the measured noise. The optimization problem becomes

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{b}}{\text{Max}}\left\{\frac{\bm{b}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\bm{b}}{\bm{b}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\bm{b}}\right\}.

(18)

Since multiplying
\bm{b}
by a scalar leaves the
S/N
unchanged, we can restate the problem as

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{b}}{\text{Max}}\left\{\bm{b}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\bm{b}\right\},\hskip 14.45377pt\text{subject to}~~\bm{b}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\bm{b}=1.

(19)

Following the procedure in Section
III.1.1
leads to

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}\bm{b}=\lambda\mathbf{F}\bm{b}

(20)

which is a generalized eigenvalue problem for
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{F}
, and is also the base for the KLT (also known as the signal-to-noise eigendecomposition).
For the particular case of Equation
9
, the optimal KL beamformer is

\displaystyle\bm{b}=\frac{\mathbf{F}^{-1}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}}{\left(\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}^{-1}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}\right)^{1/2}}

(21)

and the optimal
S/N
is

\displaystyle\left(S/N\right)_{KLT}=\lambda=N\sigma_{s}^{2}~\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}^{-1}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}.

(22)

Note that when
\mathbf{S}
consists of a single mode the KL beamformer is
\bm{b}\propto\mathbf{F}^{-1}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}
and belongs to the family of other well known beamformers including the matched filter
(
29

and the minimum variance distortionless response (MVDR) beamformer
(
4
)
. These filters have the same basic form, with the normalization constant set by the desired normalization of the synthesized beam. Also note that when
\mathbf{F}\propto\mathbf{I}

Multiple signal modes, polarization, and the KL transform

In Section
III.1.1

3

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{B}}{\text{Max}}\left\{\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{B}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}\right)\right\},\hskip 14.45377pt\text{subject to}~~\mathbf{B}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{I}

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{F}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{\Lambda}

\displaystyle\bm{\tilde{d}}=\mathbf{B}^{\dagger}\bm{d}

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\tilde{d}}\bm{\tilde{d}}^{\dagger}\rangle=\mathbf{\tilde{D}}=\mathbf{B}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{F})\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{\Lambda}+\mathbf{I}.

In this new basis, the generalized eigenvalues in the diagonal of
\mathbf{\Lambda}
represent the signal-to-noise power measured in the respective eigenmode, that is, the

\bm{b}_{k}

\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle(S/N)_{KLT,k}=\bm{b}_{k}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\bm{b}_{k}=\lambda_{k}.

(27)

If we know that the power of the signal of interest is contained within specific modes we can project onto their respective subspace by forming
\mathbf{\tilde{B}}
, a matrix whose columns have only the modes of interest.
The optimal value of our objective function in Equation
18

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{\tilde{B}}=\mathbf{I}_{k}}{\text{Max}}\left\{\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\tilde{B}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{\tilde{B}}\right)\right\}=\sum_{k~\text{a signal mode}}\lambda_{k}.

(28)

If the signal is contained in a few modes (
\mathbf{S}
not full rank), then the remaining generalized eigenvalues should be zero and adding them should not affect the total
S/N
. In practice, the generalized eigenvalue spectrum will have a steep transition, with a few large values corresponding to signal power, followed by several residual eigenvalues that are small but not zero due to effects such as finite integration times for covariance estimation, rounding errors, etc.

The KLT is the closed-form solution to the optimization problem in Equation
28
, with well-understood and computationally efficient algorithms available for solving the associated generalized eigenvalue decomposition. However, there is more than one way to extend the single-mode
S/N
optimization problem in Section
III.1.2
to multiple modes, and it is not immediately obvious that Equation
28
is the most appropriate performance metric to optimize.

For instance, based on Equations
17
and
18
, one may instead formulate a criterion based on a more direct definition of the total signal power to total noise power ratio for a signal spanning several independent modes:

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\tilde{B}}=\mathbf{I}_{k}}{\text{Max}}\left\{\frac{\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\tilde{B}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{\tilde{B}}\right)}{\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\tilde{B}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{\tilde{B}}\right)}\right\}.

(29)

When
k=1
the formulations in Equations
28
and
29
coincide, and the KLT provides the optimal solution (Section
III.1.2
). For
k>1
, however, the two formulations optimize different objectives. The trace ratio in Equation
29
maximizes the total
S/N
of the
k
-dimensional subspace, whereas Equation
28
maximizes the sum of the individual mode
S/N
values, equivalent to selecting the leading generalized eigenmodes after pre-whitening the data.

Which approach to use will depend on the specific application and computational constraints. While conceptually appealing as a direct generalization of the total
S/N
, the trace ratio problem does not admit a closed-form solution in general and is not always solved by the KLT
(
28
;
15
, for detailed reviews on the topic in the context of machine learning and high-dimensional data analysis see)
. Obtaining the true optimum requires iterative algorithms that typically involve solving an eigenvalue problem (or comparably expensive operations) at each iteration, making the approach computationally more demanding for large datasets.

In this work, we adopt the objective function in Equation
28
because of its simple implementation via the KLT and because its closed-form solution facilitates the polarization analysis presented in Appendix
B.1
. It will also facilitate future analyses of the robustness of the multi-mode filter to calibration, pointing, and polarization errors. Moreover, performing a single generalized eigenvalue decomposition directly yields the
S/N
associated with each mode, which simplifies the selection of modes with minimal noise contamination when additional compression of the multi-mode data is required.

III.3
Estimating Covariances

The KL filter requires a model for
\mathbf{S}
and an estimate for
\mathbf{F}
. Since the flux of the source only changes
\mathbf{S}
by an overall constant, only the steering vector of the beam (given by
\bm{\hat{n}}_{s}
) and polarization state of the source (given by
\mathbf{C}
) need to be specified to model
\mathbf{S}
in the case of a point source and a properly calibrated interferometer. The two main sources of error in estimating
\mathbf{S}
for a point source that is stationary over the time of observation are gain errors—which arise from imperfect antenna calibration—and pointing errors—which arise from not knowing the true target location (
\bm{\hat{n}}_{s}
). In Appendix
A

For frequency channels that are either interference or noise dominated, the eigenvalues characterizing all contaminants will be much larger than those characterizing the target signal, in which case

IV
Application to CHIME/FRB data

CHIME and its Outriggers are each stand-alone interferometers operating from 400-800 MHz. CHIME itself contains 1024 dual-polarized feeds, its k’niPatn k’l
\left.{}_{\mathrm{\smile}}\right.
stk’masqt Outrigger (KKO) contains 64 dual-polarized feeds, and its Outriggers in Green Bank (GBO) and Hat Creek (HCO) contain 128 dual-polarized feeds
(
12

19

13

. Upon detection of a radio transient, CHIME sends triggers to all its Outriggers to record
\sim

\mu
s). The real-time CHIME/FRB detection backend, which searches for transients over beams that are fixed across the sky, is able to localize the transient to several arcminutes, which can be refined to sub-arcminute precision with a finer beamforming grid search conducted offline using the recorded baseband data
(
27

. In either case, a precise enough localization is known such that the signal of interest
S

A.3
for discussion on the effect of pointing errors).

Since the science goals of most radio interferometers require much more precise calibration for interferometry than for polarimetry, modeling the polarization state of
\mathbf{S}
is generally not nearly as accurate as modeling its interferometric state. Due to the practical difficulties of modeling the full polarization state of
\mathbf{S}
, particularly at the Outrigger telescopes, we perform the filter on each polarization state of CHIME baseband data independently—this is equivalent to the treatment presented in Section
III.1.2
.

IV.2
CHIME/FRB Outriggers

The need for a large sample of precisely localized FRBs has motivated a new generation of instruments typically consisting of a “central core” interferometer and “outrigger” or “auxiliary” antennas
(
38
;
22
;
13
, e.g.,)
. While outrigger antennas that form long baselines with the central core are needed to obtain the angular resolution necessary for precise localizations, they are generally not included in the real-time FRB backend search—which are compute limited and typically optimized to maximize the field of view/FRB detection rate. Instead, only the central core, which contains most of the collecting area, participates in the FRB search, and upon an FRB detection sends triggers to the outrigger antennas so that the FRB data is recorded and localization can be done offline.

In the case where the only source of noise is thermal, the signal-to-noise ratio of a correlation function scales with the number of independent pairs as
\sqrt{N_{\rm{pairs}}}
. This means that the signal-to-noise in an autocorrelation at a single station where there are
N_{1}
antennas (
N_{\rm{pairs,auto}}=N_{1}^{2}/2
) differs from the signal-to-noise in a cross-correlation between two different stations with
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
antennas by a factor of
\sqrt{2}
if
N_{1}=N_{2}
since
N_{\rm{pairs,cross}}=N_{1}N_{2}
. The fact that, in the ideal case, a factor of
\sqrt{2}
can be recovered in cross-correlation signal-to-noise is why outrigger stations often contain less than half the collecting area of the central core
(
13
)
. However, in reality the noise environments—and in particular, the RFI environments—can be dramatically different for Outrigger sites that are sufficiently far away from the central core. As such, FRBs with a signal in the central core are not guaranteed to be detectable in cross-correlation, which both reduces the total localization rate and complicates Outrigger selection effects. Moreover, for VLBI networks that rely on a large fractional bandwidth to fit out systematics
(
13
, see)
, the localization precision is degraded in the presence of RFI even if fringes are obtained.

Naively, one might expect RFI to “correlate out” when cross-correlating baseband data between two telescopes on long (
\gg 10\,\rm{km}
) baselines. Even in an idealistic Gaussian interference case, this can only hold if the RFI amplitude is no larger than the desired signal by
\sim\sqrt{M}
—where the integration period and therefore the number of time samples
M
is not adjustable in the case of transients.
In other words, RFI tends to be too bright and transient events too short-lived for the RFI to sufficiently decorrelate when averaging over a pulse
. This assumption is further exacerbated in the case where the total collecting area (total number of feeds over the entire network) is concentrated at an individual station where RFI
is
expected to correlate (i.e., the total number of baselines
long enough for the RFI to decorrelate
is small), as in the case where each station is a standalone interferometer. For instance, Figure
3
shows the non-overlapping nature of RFI environments at CHIME as compared to its Outrigger stations.

IV.2.2
Broader impact on VLBI recovery rate

The central goal of the CHIME/FRB Outriggers project is to localize a large fraction of CHIME-detected FRBs to within their host galaxies. Based on the collecting areas of the Outrigger cylinders, this in principle should be possible for all FRBs with a CHIME
S/N
of 10 even after accounting for direction-dependent systematics
(
13
)
, but in practice is complicated by the existence of strong persistent RFI corrupting
\sim 20-30\%
of the band at each telescope site in a largely non-overlapping way. This matters the most for the faintest FRBs with fluxes comparable to the noise floor of the RFI, of which there are also the most bursts at CHIME and its Outrigger stations.

We highlight the central utility of this filter within the context of CHIME/FRB Outriggers in Figure
6
. In order to obtain a representative sample, we examined data that was obtained over different parts of the year to capture the fluctuations in RFI environments and system temperature that occur on
\sim
monthly timescales. More specifically, we compare the distribution of cross-correlation signal-to-noises for a sample of all the Outrigger-triggered FRBs that were successfully recorded at all stations between February to August 2025. There is a clear boost in signal-to-noise over the entire sample—and critically, the number of “localizable” FRBs above a minimum reliable signal-to-noise threshold is nearly doubled after applying the filter.

V
Conclusion

We have presented a spatial filter for improving the signal-to-noise ratio of interferometric measurements based on the KL Transform. We test our algorithm on CHIME/FRB data, and show that the filter not only improves the CHIME signal-to-noise of an FRB pulse by several orders of magnitude in RFI-contaminated channels, but also consistently improves the signal-to-noise over the full band. We also show that this filter can be applied independently to CHIME and each of its Outrigger stations to improve the signal-to-noise of VLBI cross-correlations. Finally, we demonstrate that one particularly useful application of this filter is maximizing the total localization rate of radio transients for core+outrigger-like telescopes designed similar to CHIME/FRB Outriggers.

More broadly, we have also outlined a framework in which variations of this filter can be described and tested, as well as the caveats that pointing errors, gain errors, and nonlinearities in the correlator can introduce to the filter’s performance. Although we have focused on a particular variation of the filter in this work, the KL formalism presented is flexible enough to be extended to other contexts. For instance, given that RFI is typically polarized, it is likely that this filter can be made significantly more effective with the incorporation of polarimetry as additional discriminating information. Higher-order moments beyond the covariance can also be used to excise sources of interference from the data using visibility beams. The software associated with this work is publicly available on Github
4
4

4

https://github.com/shionandrew/KLT_filter_public
and includes some of the aforementioned extensions. We hope that some of the framework presented in here can be built on and eventually tested in future work.

compares to a conventional beamformer. It also allows us to investigate the effect of common systematics, including calibration and pointing errors.

\mathbf{S}
is given by Equation
9

\mathbf{F}

(A1)

The signal of interest represented as a point source in the direction of
\bm{\hat{n}}_{s}
, the second term is an unwanted signal represented as a point source in the direction
\bm{\hat{n}}_{r}

The statement about the
KL

\mathbf{S}

\sim 0.01
rad for phases) within the clean parts of the band
(
10
)
. However, the CHIME
real-time processing
pipeline does not directly generate calibration gains for frequency channels that are permanently contaminated by
bright
RFI
since the application of the default calibration algorithm results in biased gains. Instead,
we must obtain solutions offline by interpolating gains from clean neighboring channels. This interpolation operation can result in gain errors well above
\sim
10%, especially across wide RFI bands where the interpolation is large (several 10s of MHz).

We can model phase errors in the calibration solution as an antenna-dependent multiplicative phase term
d_{i}\rightarrow e^{i\delta\phi_{i}}d_{i}
that is constant during the observation of the transient source. When applying this error to the toy model described in Section
A
, the model for our data covariance becomes

\tilde{\mathbf{D}}=\mathbf{\Phi}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{\Phi}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{\Phi}(\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{F})\mathbf{\Phi}^{\dagger}

(A7)

where
\mathbf{\Phi}=\text{diag}(e^{i\delta\phi_{0}},e^{i\delta\phi_{1}},...,e^{i\delta\phi_{N-1}})
. We do not know these residual terms, but typically have an idea about their scale and statistics. In the simplest case we assume
\langle\delta\phi_{i}\rangle_{\phi}=0
and
\langle\delta\phi_{i}\delta\phi_{j}\rangle_{\phi}=\sigma_{\phi}^{2}\delta_{ij}
. Here,
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\phi}
denotes average over phase error realizations. We will assume that, although the calibration is imperfect, the position of the source of interest is known. This means that the conventional beamformer is still
\bm{b}=\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}
and

\tilde{\mathbf{F}}=\mathbf{\Phi}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{\Phi}^{\dagger}=N\sigma_{r}^{2}\mathbf{\Phi}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Phi}^{\dagger}+\mathbf{I}\hskip 14.45377pt\rightarrow\hskip 14.45377pt\langle\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{F}}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}\rangle_{\phi}=N\sigma_{r}^{2}\langle|\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Phi}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}|^{2}\rangle_{\phi}+1

(A8)

where

\langle|\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Phi}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}|^{2}\rangle_{\phi}=\sum_{ij}\langle e^{i\delta\phi_{i}}e^{-i\delta\phi_{j}}\rangle_{\phi}v_{s,i}^{*}v_{r,i}v_{s,j}v_{r,j}^{*}

(A9)

A closed-form expression can be found in the case where the phase errors are Gaussian
(a reasonable approximation if the errors are smaller than about a radian)

\displaystyle\langle e^{i\delta\phi_{i}}e^{-i\delta\phi_{j}}\rangle_{\phi}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i=j\\
\langle e^{i\delta\phi_{i}}\rangle_{\phi}\langle e^{-i\delta\phi_{j}}\rangle_{\phi}=e^{-\sigma_{\phi}^{2}}&\text{if }i\neq j\end{cases}

(A10)

\displaystyle=e^{-\sigma_{\phi}^{2}}+(1-e^{-\sigma_{\phi}^{2}})\delta_{ij}.

(A11)

Thus we have

\displaystyle\langle|\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Phi}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}|^{2}\rangle_{\phi}

\displaystyle=e^{-\sigma_{\phi}^{2}}|\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}|^{2}+\frac{1-e^{-\sigma_{\phi}^{2}}}{N}

(A12)

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{F}}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}\rangle_{\phi}

\displaystyle=N\sigma_{r}^{2}\left(e^{-\sigma_{\phi}^{2}}|\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}|^{2}+\frac{1-e^{-\sigma_{\phi}^{2}}}{N}\right)+1

(A13)

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{S}}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}\rangle_{\phi}

\displaystyle=N\sigma_{s}^{2}\langle|\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Phi}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}|^{2}\rangle_{\phi}=N\sigma_{s}^{2}\left(e^{-\sigma_{\phi}^{2}}+\frac{1-e^{-\sigma_{\phi}^{2}}}{N}\right).

(A14)

This provides us with the average
(S/N)_{BF}
:

(S/N)_{BF,\phi}=N\sigma_{s}^{2}\cdot\frac{\displaystyle e^{-\sigma_{\phi}^{2}}+\frac{1-e^{-\sigma_{\phi}^{2}}}{N}}{\displaystyle N\sigma_{r}^{2}\left(e^{-\sigma_{\phi}^{2}}|\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}|^{2}+\frac{1-e^{-\sigma_{\phi}^{2}}}{N}\right)+1}.

(A15)

In the case of the
KL
beamformer, we estimate
the
covariance of the contaminant source and noise from the (now miscalibrated) off-pulse data.
The
beamformer
will have
the form
\bm{b}\propto\tilde{\mathbf{F}}^{-1}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}
, where
\tilde{\mathbf{F}}^{-1}=\mathbf{\Phi}\mathbf{F}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}^{\dagger}

since
\mathbf{\Phi}
is diagonal consisting of phase terms only
. Thus

\displaystyle\langle\bm{b}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{F}}\bm{b}\rangle_{\phi}

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{F}}^{-1}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}\rangle_{\phi}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{N\sigma_{r}^{2}}{N\sigma_{r}^{2}+1}\langle|\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Phi}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}|^{2}\rangle_{\phi}

(A16)

\displaystyle\langle\bm{b}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{S}}\bm{b}\rangle_{\phi}

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Phi}\mathbf{F}^{-1}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{F}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}\rangle_{\phi}

\displaystyle=N\sigma_{s}^{2}\left\langle|\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Phi}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}|^{2}-\frac{N\sigma_{r}^{2}}{N\sigma_{r}^{2}+1}(\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Phi}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{v}_{r}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Phi}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}}+\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Phi}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{v}_{s}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Phi}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}})+\left(\frac{N\sigma_{r}^{2}}{N\sigma_{r}^{2}+1}\right)^{2}|\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}|^{2}|\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Phi}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}|^{2}\right\rangle_{\phi}

(A17)

which provides

(S/N)_{KLT,\phi}=N\sigma_{s}^{2}\cdot\frac{\displaystyle\left(e^{-\sigma_{\phi}^{2}}+\frac{1-e^{-\sigma_{\phi}^{2}}}{N}\right)\left(1-\frac{N\sigma_{r}^{2}|\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}|^{2}}{N\sigma_{r}^{2}+1}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{N\sigma_{r}^{2}}{N\sigma_{r}^{2}+1}\right)^{2}\frac{\left(1-e^{-\sigma_{\phi}^{2}}\right)}{N}|\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}|^{2}\left(1-|\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}|^{2}\right)}{\displaystyle 1-\left(\frac{N\sigma_{r}^{2}}{N\sigma_{r}^{2}+1}\right)\left(e^{-\sigma_{\phi}^{2}}|\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}|^{2}+\frac{1-e^{-\sigma_{\phi}^{2}}}{N}\right)}.

(A18)

A.3
Pointing errors

We now consider the case that calibration is correct but the source direction
\theta_{s}
is incorrectly modeled as
\tilde{\theta}_{s}=\theta_{s}+\delta\theta
where
\delta\theta
follows the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}=(0,\sigma_{\theta})
. This means that beamformer
\bm{b}
is perturbed due to the imperfect knowledge of
\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}
.
As before, we consider a single CHIME cylinder and take
v_{s,j}=e^{i2\pi jx_{\lambda}\sin\theta_{s}}/\sqrt{N}
for simplicity.
For a small perturbation
\delta\theta\ll 1
to the modeled source direction, a perturbed conventional beamformer takes the form
\bm{b}=\mathbf{\Phi}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}
, where

\Phi_{kl}=e^{i2\pi kx_{\lambda}\cos\theta_{s}\delta\theta}\delta_{kl}=e^{ik\delta\gamma}\delta_{kl}

(A20)

where
\delta\gamma=2\pi x_{\lambda}\cos\theta_{s}\delta\theta
.
This provides

\displaystyle\langle\bm{b}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\bm{b}\rangle_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=N\sigma_{r}^{2}\langle|\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Phi}{{}^{\dagger}}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}|^{2}\rangle_{\gamma}+1

(A21)

\displaystyle\langle|\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Phi}{{}^{\dagger}}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}|^{2}\rangle_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{kl}\langle e^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}i(k-l)\delta\gamma}\rangle_{\gamma}v_{s,k}^{*}v_{r,k}v_{s,l}v_{r,l}^{*}

(A22)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{kl}e^{-(k-l)^{2}\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}/2}e^{-i2\pi kx_{\lambda}(\sin\theta_{s}-\sin\theta_{r})}e^{i2\pi lx_{\lambda}(\sin\theta_{s}-\sin\theta_{r})},

(A23)

where
\sigma_{\gamma}=2\pi x_{\lambda}\cos\theta_{s}\sigma_{\theta}
. The expression above looks like the truncated version of a 2D discrete-time Fourier Transform (DTFT).
If we define the function
\mathcal{R}(y,z)
as

\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(y,z)=\frac{1}{N^{2}}DTFT\left\{e^{-(k-l)^{2}\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}/2}w(k,l)\right\}(y,z),\hskip 14.45377ptw(k,l)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }0\leq k,l\leq N-1\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}

(A24)

Then

\displaystyle\langle\bm{b}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\bm{b}\rangle_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=N\sigma_{r}^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(u,-u)+1,\hskip 14.45377ptu=x_{\lambda}(\sin\theta_{s}-\sin\theta_{r})

(A25)

\displaystyle\langle\bm{b}^{\dagger}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{S}}\bm{b}\rangle_{\phi}

\displaystyle=N\sigma_{s}^{2}\langle|\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Phi}{{}^{\dagger}}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}|^{2}\rangle_{\gamma}=N\sigma_{s}^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(0,0),

(A26)

which provides

(S/N)_{BF,\gamma}=N\sigma_{s}^{2}\cdot\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(0,0)}{N\sigma_{r}^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(u,-u)+1}.

(A27)

In the case of the KLT filter, the beamformer has the form
\bm{b}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{F}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}
. A similar calculation gives

(S/N)_{KLT,\gamma}=N\sigma_{s}^{2}\cdot\frac{\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(0,0)-\frac{2N\sigma_{r}^{2}}{N\sigma_{r}^{2}+1}\Re\left\{\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(0,-u)\right\}+\left(\frac{N\sigma_{r}^{2}}{N\sigma_{r}^{2}+1}\right)^{2}|\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{v}}_{r}|^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(u,-u)}{\displaystyle 1-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{N\sigma_{r}^{2}}{N\sigma_{r}^{2}+1}}\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(u,-u)}

(A28)

where
\Re\{x\}
denotes the real part of the number
x
. Figure
10
compares the signal-to-noise performance of the conventional and KL beamformer for different pointing errors according to Equations
A27
and
A28
.
The performance of both the conventional and KL filter degrade with larger pointing errors, and depending on the location of the RFI relative to the target of interest, can reduce the relative signal-to-noise gain seen by the KL filter over the conventional beamformer.

B.1
Polarimetric
KLT
Filter

Because RFI is often highly polarized, including polarimetry in the
KLT
filter should result in a much cleaner separation of RFI modes from the signal. Here we assume that the polarization of the source is known and discuss the
implementation of a multi-mode KLT filter for polarization, its performance, and the constraints it provides on the optimal total
S/N
discussed in Section
III.2
.

To start, let us look at the response of one of the dual-polarization antennas of our array to the point source signal in Equation
7
. If the
i
-th and
j
-th elements form a dual-polarization antenna pair, then the fringing term in Equation
7
is unity. Let
i
be mostly sensitive to the
X
polarization and
j
to the
Y
polarization of emission. If the polarization leakage is negligible, then

\displaystyle\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}d_{i}\\
d_{j}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}d_{i}^{*}&d_{j}^{*}\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}|A_{i}^{X}(\bm{\hat{n}}_{s})|^{2}(T_{s}+Q_{s})&A_{i}^{X}(\bm{\hat{n}}_{s})A_{j}^{Y*}(\bm{\hat{n}}_{s})(U_{s}-iV_{s})\\
A_{j}^{Y}(\bm{\hat{n}}_{s})A_{i}^{X*}(\bm{\hat{n}}_{s})(U_{s}+iV_{s})&|A_{j}^{Y}(\bm{\hat{n}}_{s})|^{2}(T_{s}-Q_{s})\end{bmatrix}.

(B1)

If in addition, the antenna beams are calibrated out, we have

\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}d_{i}\\
d_{j}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}d_{i}^{*}&d_{j}^{*}\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle=\mathbf{C}(\bm{\hat{n}}_{s})=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}T_{s}+Q_{s}&U_{s}-iV_{s}\\
U_{s}+iV_{s}&T_{s}-Q_{s}\end{bmatrix}

(B2)

where
(T_{s},~Q_{s},~U_{s},~V_{s})
are the Stokes parameters of the source. This matrix by construction is hermitian and positive semi-definite, and has eigenvalues

\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}(T_{s}\pm T_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}ps}}),\hskip 14.45377ptT_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}ps}}^{2}=Q_{s}^{2}+U_{s}^{2}+V_{s}^{2}\leq T_{s}^{2}

(B3)

where
T_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}ps}}
is the source polarized intensity. The respective eigenvectors are

\bm{\hat{v}_{+}}=\begin{bmatrix}e^{-i\chi_{s}}\cos(\psi_{s}/2)\\
\sin(\psi_{s}/2)\end{bmatrix},\hskip 14.45377pt\bm{\hat{v}_{-}}=\begin{bmatrix}-e^{-i\chi_{s}}\sin(\psi_{s}/2)\\
\cos(\psi_{s}/2)\end{bmatrix}

(B4)

where
\tan\chi_{s}=U_{s}/V_{s}
and
\cos\psi_{s}=Q_{s}/{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}_{ps}
.

Returning to the response of the full array, if we choose our antenna index mapping
i
such that the first subset of
N
antenna elements is
X
polarized and the second subset is
Y
polarized, then the point source visibility matrix can be written in block matrix form as

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{S}^{XX}&\mathbf{S}^{XY}\\
\mathbf{S}^{YX}&\mathbf{S}^{YY}\end{bmatrix}

(B5)

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{2}\begin{bmatrix}(T_{s}+Q_{s})\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{X}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{X\dagger}&(U_{s}-iV_{s})\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{X}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{Y\dagger}\\
(U_{s}+iV_{s})\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{Y}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{X\dagger}&(T_{s}-Q_{s})\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{Y}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{Y\dagger}\end{bmatrix}

(B6)

where
\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{X}
and
\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{Y}
are defined as in Equation
9
for the
X
and
Y
polarized antenna elements respectively. Note that if we choose our indexing such that the
i
-th and
N+i
-th array elements correspond to the two elements of the same dual-polarization antenna we have
\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{X}=\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{Y}
.

It is more useful for our KLT analysis to write
\mathbf{S}
in terms of its eigendecomposition. In this form, the total data covariance can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{2}(T_{s}+T_{ps})\bm{\hat{v}}_{s1}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s1}^{\dagger}+\frac{N}{2}(T_{s}-T_{ps})\bm{\hat{v}}_{s2}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s2}^{\dagger}+\mathbf{F}

(B7)

where

\bm{\hat{v}}_{s1}=\begin{bmatrix}e^{-i\chi_{s}}\cos(\psi_{s}/2)\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{X}\\
\sin(\psi_{s}/2)\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{Y}\end{bmatrix},~\bm{\hat{v}}_{s2}=\begin{bmatrix}-e^{-i\chi_{s}}\sin(\psi_{s}/2)\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{X}\\
\cos(\psi_{s}/2)\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{Y}\end{bmatrix}.

(B8)

Note that
\mathbf{S}
is at most rank 2. Even with Equation
B1
when the antenna beams are not known but the polarization leakage is negligible the matrix
\mathbf{S}
can be written in a similar form using the transformation shown in case 3 of Section
III.1.1
and re-scaling the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{S}
. In the more general case where the beams are unknown and there is polarization leakage,
\mathbf{S}
will also be at most rank 2, but each block matrix in Equation
B5
will include combinations of all the Stokes paramerters
. For the purpose of demonstrating the KLT analysis we will use Equation
B7
.

First, note that if the source is completely polarized we have
T_{ps}=T_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}
and
\mathbf{S}
becomes rank 1 with a single non-zero eigenvalue
NT_{s}
and respective eigenvector
\bm{\hat{v}}_{s1}
. This problem can be solved using the methods of Sections
III.1.1
and
III.1.2
. In the more general case that the signal is partially polarized
\mathbf{S}
will be rank 2.

It can be shown
(
34
)
that the KLT can be implemented as a sequence of three linear transformations

\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{P}_{F}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{F}^{-1/2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{P}}

(B9)

where
\mathbf{P}_{F}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{F}
are the unitary matrix that diagonalizes
\mathbf{F}
and its respective diagonal eigenvalue factorization. That is

\mathbf{P}_{F}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{P}_{F}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{F}.

(B10)

Note that the matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{F}^{-1/2}
is well defined because
\mathbf{F}
is hermitian and positive definite, so all its eigenvalues are positive.

If we apply the first two transformations to the data we obtain

\bm{d^{\prime}}=\mathbf{P}_{F}^{\dagger}\bm{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{D^{\prime}}=\mathbf{P}_{F}^{\dagger}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}_{F}=\mathbf{P}_{F}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{P}_{F}+\mathbf{\Sigma}_{F}

(B11)

\displaystyle\bm{d^{\prime\prime}}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{F}^{-1/2}\bm{d^{\prime}}\rightarrow\mathbf{D^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{F}^{-1/2}\mathbf{D^{\prime}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{F}^{-1/2}

(B12)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{F}^{-1/2}\mathbf{P}_{F}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{P}_{F}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{F}^{-1/2}+\mathbf{I}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{S^{\prime\prime}}+\mathbf{I}

The last transformation
\mathbf{P}
in Equation
B9
is a unitary matrix that diagonalizes
\mathbf{S^{\prime\prime}}
, that is

\mathbf{P}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S^{\prime\prime}}\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{\Lambda}.

(B13)

Applying this last transformation to
\bm{d^{\prime\prime}}
leads to Equation
26
. Instead of doing that, let us check what
\mathbf{D^{\prime\prime}}
looks like for our partially polarized example

\displaystyle\mathbf{D^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{|\bm{\alpha}_{1}|^{2}\bm{\hat{\alpha}}_{1}\bm{\hat{\alpha}}_{1}^{\dagger}+|\bm{\alpha}_{2}|^{2}\bm{\hat{\alpha}}_{2}\bm{\hat{\alpha}}_{2}^{\dagger}}_{\mathbf{S^{\prime\prime}}}+\mathbf{I}

(B14)

where

\displaystyle\bm{\alpha}_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}(T_{s}+T_{ps})}~\mathbf{\Sigma}_{F}^{-1/2}\mathbf{P}_{F}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s1}

(B15)

\displaystyle|\bm{\alpha}_{1}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{2}(T_{s}+T_{ps})~\bm{\hat{v}}_{s1}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{F}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{F}^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{F}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s1}

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{2}(T_{s}+T_{ps})~\bm{\hat{v}}_{s1}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}^{-1}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s1}

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{\alpha}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{\alpha}_{1}}{|\bm{\alpha}_{1}|}=\frac{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{F}^{-1/2}\mathbf{P}_{F}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s1}}{\left(\bm{\hat{v}}_{s1}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}^{-1}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s1}\right)^{1/2}}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\bm{\alpha}_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}(T_{s}-T_{ps})}~\mathbf{\Sigma}_{F}^{-1/2}\mathbf{P}_{F}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s2}

(B16)

\displaystyle|\bm{\alpha}_{2}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{2}(T_{s}-T_{ps})~\bm{\hat{v}}_{s2}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}^{-1}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s2}

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{\alpha}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{\alpha}_{2}}{|\bm{\alpha}_{2}|}=\frac{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{F}^{-1/2}\mathbf{P}_{F}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s2}}{\left(\bm{\hat{v}}_{s2}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}^{-1}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s2}\right)^{1/2}}.

Note that the vectors
\bm{\hat{v}}_{s1}
and
\bm{\hat{v}}_{s2}
are orthonormal and they are the two eigenvectors of
\mathbf{S}
that span its image. However,
\bm{\alpha}_{1}
and
\bm{\alpha}_{2}
are not necessarily orthogonal:

\displaystyle\bm{\alpha}_{1}^{\dagger}\bm{\alpha}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{2}\sqrt{T_{s}^{2}-T_{ps}^{2}}~\bm{\hat{v}}_{s1}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}^{-1}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s2}.

(B17)

B.1.1
\bm{\alpha_{1}},~\bm{\alpha_{2}}
orthogonal

If
\bm{\hat{v}}_{s1}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}^{-1}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s2}=0
then
\bm{\alpha}_{1}
and
\bm{\alpha}_{2}
are orthogonal and the matrix
\mathbf{S^{\prime\prime}}
in Equation
B14
is already written in terms of its eigendecomposition. In this case, it is easy to check that
\bm{\hat{\alpha}}_{1}
and
\bm{\hat{\alpha}}_{2}
are the two eigenvectors of
\mathbf{S^{\prime\prime}}
with non-zero eigenvalues. Their respective eigenvalues are
|\bm{\alpha}_{1}|^{2}
and
|\bm{\alpha}_{2}|^{2}
. This also means that
\lambda_{1}=|\bm{\alpha}_{1}|^{2}
and
\lambda_{2}=|\bm{\alpha}_{2}|^{2}
are the two non-zero generalized eigenvalues of
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{F}
.

Note that since

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{\alpha}}_{a}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S^{\prime\prime}}\bm{\hat{\alpha}}_{a}=|\bm{\alpha}_{a}|^{2},\hskip 14.45377pta=1,2

(B18)

then

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{P}_{F}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{F}^{-1/2}\bm{\hat{\alpha}}_{a}\right)^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\left(\mathbf{P}_{F}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{F}^{-1/2}\bm{\hat{\alpha}}_{a}\right)=\bm{b}_{a}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\bm{b}_{a}=|\bm{\alpha}_{a}|^{2}.

(B19)

Similarly

\displaystyle\bm{b}_{a}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\bm{b}_{a}=1.

(B20)

Thus, the two generalized eigenmodes (or KL beamformers) that contain signal power are

\displaystyle\bm{b}_{a}=\mathbf{P}_{F}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{F}^{-1/2}\bm{\hat{\alpha}}_{a}=\frac{\mathbf{F}^{-1}\bm{\hat{v}}_{sa}}{\left(\bm{\hat{v}}_{sa}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}^{-1}\bm{\hat{v}}_{sa}\right)^{1/2}},\hskip 14.45377pta=1,2

(B21)

and the optimal signal-to-noise is

\displaystyle(S/N)_{KLT}=

\displaystyle|\bm{\alpha}_{1}|^{2}+|\bm{\alpha}_{2}|^{2}

(B22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N}{2}(T_{s}+T_{ps})~\bm{\hat{v}}_{s1}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}^{-1}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s1}+\frac{N}{2}(T_{s}-T_{ps})~\bm{\hat{v}}_{s2}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}^{-1}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s2}

B.1.2
\bm{\alpha_{1}},~\bm{\alpha_{2}}
not orthogonal

If
\bm{\hat{v}}_{s1}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}^{-1}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s2}\neq 0
then
\bm{\alpha}_{1}
and
\bm{\alpha}_{2}
are not orthogonal and they are not eigenvectors of
\mathbf{S^{\prime\prime}}
.

Even without the eigenvalue decomposition of
\mathbf{S^{\prime\prime}}
we can read
(S/N)_{KLT}
from Equation
B14
. By using Equation
B13
we have

\displaystyle(S/N)_{KLT}=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}(\mathbf{\Lambda})=\text{Tr}(\mathbf{S^{\prime\prime}})

(B23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\bm{\alpha}_{1}|^{2}+|\bm{\alpha}_{2}|^{2}

so the total
S/N
in this case will be the same as in Equation
B22
.

In order to know the individual
S/N
contribution of each signal mode we need to find the eigenvalue decomposition of
\mathbf{S^{\prime\prime}}
, or at least the two eigenvectors that span its image.

From inspection, the two eigenvectors will have the form

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}_{\pm}=\bm{\alpha}_{1}+\kappa_{\pm}e^{-i\phi}\bm{\alpha}_{2}

(B24)

where

\displaystyle e^{-i\phi}=\frac{\bm{\alpha}_{2}^{\dagger}\bm{\alpha}_{1}}{|\bm{\alpha}_{2}^{\dagger}\bm{\alpha}_{1}|}

(B25)

and

\displaystyle\kappa_{\pm}=\frac{-|\bm{\alpha}_{1}|^{2}+|\bm{\alpha}_{2}|^{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(|\bm{\alpha}_{1}|^{2}-|\bm{\alpha}_{2}|^{2}\right)^{2}+4|\bm{\alpha}_{1}^{\dagger}\bm{\alpha}_{2}|^{2}}}{2|\bm{\alpha}_{1}^{\dagger}\bm{\alpha}_{2}|}.

(B26)

The corresponding eigenvalues are

\displaystyle\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\left[|\bm{\alpha}_{1}|^{2}+|\bm{\alpha}_{2}|^{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(|\bm{\alpha}_{1}|^{2}-|\bm{\alpha}_{2}|^{2}\right)^{2}+4|\bm{\alpha}_{1}^{\dagger}\bm{\alpha}_{2}|^{2}}\right]

(B27)

which confirms Equation
B23
.

Thus, the two generalized eigenmodes (or KL beamformers) that contain signal power are

\displaystyle\bm{b}_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}_{F}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{F}^{-1/2}\bm{\hat{\beta}}_{\pm}

(B28)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\bm{\beta}_{\pm}|}\mathbf{F}^{-1}\left[\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}(T_{s}+T_{ps})}~\bm{\hat{v}}_{s1}+\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}(T_{s}-T_{ps})}~\kappa_{\pm}e^{-i\phi}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s2}\right].

(B29)

B.1.3
Constraints on optimal total
S/N

When the source is completely polarized its covariance matrix
\mathbf{S}
consists of a single mode and the KL beamformer optimizes the total
S/N
. In the more general case of a partially polarized source,
\mathbf{S}
consists of two independent modes. As explained in Section
III.2
, the KLT optimizes the sum of per-mode
S/N
values (Equation
28
), but not necessarily the total
S/N
(Equation
29
). However, as we will show in this section, the KLT provides bounds for the optimal total
S/N
, and in special cases is equivalent to the solution which optimizes the total
S/N
.

If the matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}=\left[\bm{b}_{1},~\bm{b}_{2}\right]
represents two orthonormal beamformers (i.e.
\bm{b}_{k}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{l}=\delta_{kl}
) acting on the full polarization data, then the total
S/N
becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\tilde{B}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{\tilde{B}}\right)}{\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\tilde{B}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{\tilde{B}}\right)}=\frac{\sum_{k}\bm{b}_{k}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\bm{b}_{k}}{\sum_{k}\bm{b}_{k}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\bm{b}_{k}}

(B30)

Since the total number of modes (described by index
k
) is 2, we can expand Equation
B30
as

\displaystyle\frac{\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\tilde{B}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{\tilde{B}}\right)}{\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\tilde{B}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{\tilde{B}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\bm{b}_{1}}{\sum_{k}\bm{b}_{k}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\bm{b}_{k}}\cdot\frac{\bm{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\bm{b}_{1}}{\bm{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\bm{b}_{1}}+\frac{\bm{b}_{2}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\bm{b}_{2}}{\sum_{k}\bm{b}_{k}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\bm{b}_{k}}\cdot\frac{\bm{b}_{2}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\bm{b}_{2}}{\bm{b}_{2}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\bm{b}_{2}}

(B31)

\displaystyle\leq\text{Max}\left\{\frac{\bm{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\bm{b}_{1}}{\bm{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\bm{b}_{1}},\frac{\bm{b}_{2}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\bm{b}_{2}}{\bm{b}_{2}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\bm{b}_{2}}\right\}

where the first line follows from the fact that
\mathbf{F}
is positive definite (
\bm{b}_{2}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\bm{b}_{2}
and
\bm{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\bm{b}_{1}
are positive) and the Max function in the second line denotes the maximum of the two single-mode
S/N
arguments. Also, from Section
III.1.2
we know that for any unit-norm beamformer
\bm{b}
acting on
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{F}
we must have

\displaystyle\frac{\bm{b}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\bm{b}}{\bm{b}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\bm{b}}\leq\lambda_{*}

(B32)

where
\lambda_{*}
is the largest generalized eigenvalue of the pair
\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{F}
. This implies that Equation
B30
must follow the inequality:

\displaystyle\frac{\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\tilde{B}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{\tilde{B}}\right)}{\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\tilde{B}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{\tilde{B}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{*}.

(B33)

Equipped with Equation
B33
, let’s now consider the special case where the source is unpolarized (
Q_{s}=U_{s}=V_{s}=0
). From Equations
B5
and
B6
,
\mathbf{S}
is rank-2 with two identical non-zero eigenvalues and block diagonal with

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}^{XX}=\frac{NT_{s}}{2}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{X}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{X\dagger},\hskip 7.22743pt\mathbf{S}^{YY}=\frac{NT_{s}}{2}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{Y}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{Y\dagger},\hskip 7.22743pt\mathbf{S}^{XY}=\mathbf{S}^{YX}=\mathbf{0}.

(B34)

Furthermore, we can always choose the indexing of our dual-polarization antenna array such that
\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{X}=\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{Y}
(Section
B.1
), so
\mathbf{S}^{XX}=\mathbf{S}^{YY}
. In addition, if the sky contamination is also unpolarized and the receivers are identical without cross-polarization noise terms, then
\mathbf{F}
will also have a similar block diagonal structure with
\mathbf{F}^{XX}=\mathbf{F}^{YY}
. The two identical blocks of the system imply that the pair
\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{F}
will have two identical non-zero generalized eigenvalues
(\lambda_{*},~\lambda_{*})
, with
\lambda_{*}
being the only non-zero generalized eigenvalue of the pair
\mathbf{S}^{XX}-\mathbf{F}^{XX}
, that is (Section
III.1.2
)

\displaystyle\underset{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{b}^{X\dagger}\bm{b}^{X}=1}}{\text{Max}}\left\{\frac{\bm{b}^{X\dagger}\mathbf{S}^{XX}\bm{b}^{X}}{\bm{b}^{X\dagger}\mathbf{F}^{XX}\bm{b}^{X}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{*}

(B35)

where
\bm{b}^{X}
represents a single-polarization beamformer. Note that we use a normalization constraint (unit vector norm) that is different to the constraint in Equation
19
(unit denominator), but that leaves the
S/N
and the optimization procedure unchanged. Also, from Equation
21
we know that the vector

\displaystyle\bm{b}_{*}^{X}=\frac{\mathbf{F}^{-1}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{X}}{\left(\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{X\dagger}\mathbf{F}^{-2}\bm{\hat{v}}_{s}^{X}\right)^{1/2}}

(B36)

is the optimal solution for Equation
B35
, where the different value of the denominator in this case is determined by the unit vector norm constraint. This means that if we set

\bm{b}_{1*}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{b}_{*}^{X}\\
\bm{0}\end{bmatrix},~\bm{b}_{2*}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}\\
\bm{b}_{*}^{X}\end{bmatrix}\hskip 14.45377pt\rightarrow\hskip 14.45377pt\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{*}=\left[\bm{b}_{1*},~\bm{b}_{2*}\right]

(B37)

we have

\displaystyle\frac{\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\tilde{B}}_{*}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{\tilde{B}}_{*}\right)}{\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\tilde{B}}_{*}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{\tilde{B}}_{*}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2~\bm{b}_{*}^{X\dagger}\mathbf{S}^{XX}\bm{b}_{*}^{X}}{2~\bm{b}_{*}^{X\dagger}\mathbf{F}^{XX}\bm{b}_{*}^{X}}=\lambda_{*}.

(B38)

It follows from Equations
B33
and
B38
that, in the unpolarized limit,
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{*}
as given by Equations
B36
and
B37
provides the optimal total
S/N
and

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\tilde{B}}=\mathbf{I}_{2}}{\text{Max}}\left\{\frac{\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\tilde{B}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{\tilde{B}}\right)}{\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\tilde{B}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{\tilde{B}}\right)}\right\}_{unpolarized}=\lambda_{*}.

(B39)

Thus, the unpolarized case is a rank-2 trace ratio problem that can be solved by finding the KL beamformer for a single polarization (a simpler, rank-1 problem).

The previous statement is not true in general. However, from the work of
28
on the localization of the optimum for the general rank-
k>1
trace ratio problem it follows that, in the most general partially polarized case (including partially polarized source and contamination, as well as potential co- and cross-polarization noise couplings), the non-zero generalized eigenvalues will provide bounds for the optimal total
S/N
, that is

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}\leq\underset{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\tilde{B}}=\mathbf{I}_{2}}{\text{Max}}\left\{\frac{\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\tilde{B}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{\tilde{B}}\right)}{\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\tilde{B}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{\tilde{B}}\right)}\right\}\leq\lambda_{1}

(B40)

where
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}
are the two non-zero generalized eigenvalues of
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{F}
.

B.2
Visibility space

Sometimes the interferometric data available is not in the form of baseband voltages but as a per-frequency estimate
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}
of the
2N\times 2N
visibility matrix (Equation
2
). This means that
\tilde{D}_{ij}
represents an estimate of the correlation between antenna elements
i
and
j
in the form of Equation
30
, and that
\left\langle\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\right\rangle=\mathbf{D}
.

In this case, one can form beamformed visibility beams by taking linear combinations of visibilities

y=\sum_{ij}w_{ij}^{*}\tilde{D}_{ij}=\bm{w}^{\dagger}\text{vect}(\tilde{\mathbf{D}})=\text{Tr}(\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{D}})

(B41)

where
\text{vect}(\tilde{\mathbf{D}})
is the column-vectorized version of
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}
formed by stacking its columns,
\bm{w}
is the length-
(2N)^{2}
visibility beamforming vector, and
\mathbf{W}
is its corresponding
2N\times 2N
matrix representation.

Voltage beamformers discussed in previous sections are a sub-class of visibility beamformers. To see this, note that if
\bm{b}^{\dagger}\bm{d}(t_{m})
is a beamformed voltage beam of the
m
-th voltage data sample, then the corresponding visibility beam is

\displaystyle y_{b}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}\left|\bm{b}^{\dagger}\bm{d}(t_{m})\right|^{2}=\bm{b}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\bm{b}=\text{Tr}(\mathbf{W}_{b}^{\dagger}\tilde{\mathbf{D}})

(B42)

where

\mathbf{W}_{b}=\bm{b}\bm{b}^{\dagger}.

(B43)

Thus, voltage beams are the sub-class of visibility beams that are “factorizable”.

In the case of visibility beamformers the
S/N
is typically measured as the ratio of the average signal power

\bar{y}_{S}=\left\langle y_{S}\right\rangle=\bm{w}^{\dagger}\text{vect}(\mathbf{S})

(B44)

to the root mean square noise and sky interference power
(
36
)
,
\Delta y_{F}
, where

(\Delta y_{F})^{2}=\left\langle|y_{F}|^{2}\right\rangle-|\left\langle y_{F}\right\rangle|^{2}=\bm{w}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathcal{F}}\bm{w}

(B45)

and

\bm{\mathcal{F}}=\left\langle\text{vect}(\tilde{\mathbf{F}})\text{vect}(\tilde{\mathbf{F}})^{\dagger}\right\rangle-\text{vect}(\mathbf{F})\text{vect}(\mathbf{F})^{\dagger}

(B46)

is the covariance matrix of the noise and sky interference visibilities.

This means that the optimal visibility beamforming weight vector
\bm{w}
can be found by maximizing
|\bar{y}_{S}|^{2}/(\Delta y_{F})^{2}
or, equivalently, by solving the optimization problem

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{w}}{\text{Max}}\left\{\frac{\bm{w}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathcal{S}}\bm{w}}{\bm{w}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathcal{F}}\bm{w}}\right\}.

(B47)

where we defined the rank-1 matrix
\bm{\mathcal{S}}=\text{vect}(\mathbf{S})\text{vect}(\mathbf{S})^{\dagger}
.

From Section
III.1.2
, we know that the KLT provides the solution for Equation
B47
and that the optimal visibility KL beamformer is

\displaystyle\bm{w}\propto\bm{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\text{vect}(\mathbf{S}).

(B48)

Note that by the same argument presented in Equation
30
,
N^{2}
time samples are now needed to enforce that F is well-measured, which for CHIME would require seconds of data.

In the case that the noise and the sky interference signals are Gaussian, the matrix
\bm{\mathcal{F}}
takes a simple form
(
23
)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\mathcal{F}}_{(ij)(kl)}=\frac{F_{ik}F_{jl}^{*}}{M},\hskip 28.90755pt\left(\mathbf{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\right)_{(ij)(kl)}=M\left(F^{-1}\right)_{ik}^{*}\left(F^{-1}\right)_{jl}

(B49)

and the optimal visibility beamformer is equivalent to the solution presented in Equation 32 of
24
. In general, however, given that many sources of RFI are non-Gaussian
(
35
)
, Equation
B49
- will not hold, and the full noise and the sky interference covariance given by Equation
B46
will need to be estimated from the visibility data.
