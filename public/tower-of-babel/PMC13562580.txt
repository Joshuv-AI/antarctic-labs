Prevalence and factors associated with sexual abuse among in-school adolescents in Ogun State, Southwest, Nigeria
Authors: Eunice Oluwakemi Ogunmodede, Dolapo Emmanuel Ajala, Rafiat Omotayo Ishola, Sorunke Bolutife Joshua, Taiwo Omotayo Dosumu, Ronke Gbonjubola Ajala, Ifeoluwa Elizabeth Alao, Adetunmise Oluseyi Olajide
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sexual abuse among adolescents remains a significant public health and child protection concern, particularly in low- and middle-income settings. However, evidence on its prevalence and associated factors among in-school adolescents in Southwest Nigeria remains limited. This study assessed the prevalence and factors associated with sexual abuse among in-school adolescents in Ogun State, Southwest Nigeria, using the Ecological Model of Violence. A school-based analytical cross-sectional study was conducted among 353 in-school adolescents aged 10–19 years in Abeokuta South Local Government Area, Ogun State. Participants were selected through multistage sampling from three secondary schools. Data were collected using a structured self-administered questionnaire. Sexual abuse was defined as any self-reported non-consensual sexual experience. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and multivariable logistic regression. Variables were grouped according to the Ecological Model into individual, interpersonal, and socio-cultural factors. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The prevalence of self-reported sexual abuse was 34.3%. Schoolmates were the most frequently reported perpetrators. At the individual level, gender was significantly associated with sexual abuse, with males showing lower odds of reporting abuse compared with females (AOR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.31–0.92). At the interpersonal level, adolescents from monogamous families had higher odds of reporting sexual abuse (AOR = 4.61, 95% CI: 2.45–8.68), while those living with both parents had lower odds (AOR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.14–0.49). At the socio-cultural level, religion was significantly associated with sexual abuse, with Christians showing higher odds compared with Muslims (AOR = 3.43, 95% CI: 2.00–5.89). Age was not significantly associated after adjustment. Sexual abuse among in-school adolescents in the study setting is relatively high and is associated with factors operating across individual, family, and socio-cultural levels. Strengthening school safeguarding systems and family-based protective structures may help address adolescent vulnerability.

Introduction

Sexual abuse among adolescents remains a significant public health and human rights concern globally due to its wide-ranging physical, psychological, and social consequences1,2. Adolescents who experience sexual abuse are at increased risk of depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, sexually transmitted infections, unintended pregnancy, poor academic performance, and long-term revictimization in adulthood1,3–5. Despite increased global attention to child protection, sexual abuse during adolescence continues to be underreported and inadequately addressed5,6, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where structural and social barriers often limit disclosure and response mechanisms.

In sub-Saharan Africa, sexual abuse among adolescents remains a significant public health and child protection concern7. Although numerous studies8–11 have documented its occurrence, prevalence estimates vary considerably across countries and settings due to differences in study populations, operational definitions of sexual abuse, reporting practices, and measurement approaches. This variability complicates direct comparisons across studies and limits the ability to draw context-specific conclusions regarding the burden of sexual abuse and the factors associated with its occurrence. Consequently, locally generated evidence is needed to better understand the magnitude and contextual correlates of adolescent sexual abuse within specific populations and settings.

In Nigeria, school-based studies have reported notable levels of sexual victimization among adolescents, with estimates ranging from low to high proportions depending on setting and methodology. However, much of the existing evidence is fragmented, often focusing on single communities or combining heterogeneous populations, which limits comparability and weakens the ability to understand context-specific patterns.

To better understand the multifactorial nature of adolescent sexual abuse, this study is guided by the Ecological Model of Violence otherwise called Social Ecological Model (SEM). This framework conceptualizes sexual abuse as the result of interacting factors operating at multiple levels: individual, interpersonal, community, and societal. At the individual level, characteristics such as age and sex may influence vulnerability and exposure. At the interpersonal level, family structure, caregiving arrangements, and parental supervision may shape adolescents’ risk environments and access to protection. At the community and societal levels, prevailing gender norms, stigma surrounding sexual abuse, cultural silence on sexuality, and weak enforcement of child protection laws may contribute to both vulnerability and underreporting. This model provides a structured lens for examining how these factors operate simultaneously rather than in isolation12,13.

Despite the usefulness of this framework, many existing studies14–16 in Nigeria have not consistently applied it in analyzing adolescent sexual abuse. Factors are often examined in an uneven way without clear distinction between levels of influence, resulting in limited explanatory depth.

In addition, there remains limited context-specific evidence focusing exclusively on in-school adolescents in Ogun State. Most Nigerian studies6,16,17 either combine in-school and out-of-school adolescents or focus on populations engaged in high-risk economic activities such as street hawking or informal labour. While these studies are valuable, they do not fully capture the unique school-based environment where adolescents spend a significant proportion of their time and may experience distinct forms of vulnerability, particularly from peers, schoolmates, and other trusted individuals within their immediate social environment. Furthermore, differences in urban–semi-urban dynamics within Ogun State may influence exposure patterns, reporting behaviour, and access to protective mechanisms, yet these contextual factors remain insufficiently explored.

Understanding the prevalence and factors associated with sexual abuse among in-school adolescents is therefore essential for informing targeted prevention strategies. Evidence specific to school-going adolescents can guide the development of interventions that strengthen safeguarding policies, improve parental and school-based supervision, and address harmful norms that perpetuate silence and underreporting.

Given this gap, the present study aimed to determine the prevalence of sexual abuse and identify factors associated with sexual abuse among in-school adolescents in Ogun State, Southwest Nigeria, using the Ecological Model of Violence as an analytical framework. By applying this framework, the study examines how individual and interpersonal factors jointly contribute to vulnerability within the school context, thereby generating evidence to support contextually appropriate prevention and response strategies. We hypothesized that female adolescents, younger adolescents, and those living in disrupted family structures or with non-biological caregivers would have higher odds of experiencing sexual abuse compared with their counterparts.

Materials and methods

A school-based analytical cross-sectional, quantitative study was conducted to assess the prevalence and factors associated with sexual abuse among in-school adolescents in Abeokuta South Local Government Area (LGA), Ogun State, Southwest Nigeria. The cross-sectional design allowed for the assessment of statistical associations between exposure variables and self-reported sexual abuse at a single point in time; however, temporal relationships and causal inferences could not be established.

Abeokuta South LGA is an urban and semi-urban educational setting with a heterogeneous population and a mix of public secondary schools serving adolescents from diverse socio-economic backgrounds.

Study population

The study population comprised in-school adolescents aged 10–19 years enrolled in the selected secondary schools, consistent with the World Health Organization definition of adolescence18.

Students were eligible for inclusion if they were within the age range of 10–19 years, present in school during the period of data collection, and willing to participate. In addition, written informed consent was obtained from parents or guardians for participants below 18 years of age, while assent was obtained directly from the adolescents.

Students were excluded from the study if they were outside the specified age range, had spent less than one academic term in the school, sick, pregnant or declined participation. Adolescents whose parents or guardians did not provide consent were also excluded from participation.

Sampling size

The sample size was calculated using n = Z2p(1-p)/d2. Where, n is sample size, Z = the standard normal deviate, set at 1.96 (for 95% confidence interval), p = prevalence rate, d = desired degree of accuracy (0.05). A prevalence (p) of 30% was adopted based on prior research on adolescent sexual abuse in Nigeria14. With a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96) and a margin of error (d) of 0.05, the minimum sample size estimated was 323. To account for incomplete responses, a 10% non-response adjustment was added, yielding a final sample size of 353 adolescents.

Although the sample size was calculated primarily to estimate the prevalence of sexual abuse, its adequacy for multivariable logistic regression was also assessed. A total of 121 respondents reported sexual abuse, and five explanatory variables were included in the final regression model, yielding approximately 24 events per variable (EPV). This exceeded the commonly recommended minimum of 10 EPV for logistic regression models19, indicating adequate statistical power and a low likelihood of model overfitting.

Sampling technique

A multistage sampling technique was employed to select study participants.

Stage 1: Selection of schools

A sampling frame comprising all government-approved public secondary schools in Abeokuta South Local Government Area was obtained from the Ogun State Ministry of Education. From this list, three secondary schools were selected using simple random sampling by balloting without replacement. Each eligible school was assigned a unique identification number, and three schools were randomly selected.

Stage 2: Allocation of sample size to selected schools

The total sample size of 353 was proportionately allocated to each selected school based on the student population of eligible adolescents in the respective schools. The number of students selected from each school was determined using the formula: ni = (Ni/N) × n, where ni represents the sample allocated to each school, Ni represents the eligible student population in the selected school, N represents the total eligible student population across the three schools, and n represents the overall study sample size.

Stage 3: Selection of students within schools

Within each selected school, students were first stratified according to class level (junior and senior secondary classes) to ensure representation across educational levels. The allocated sample for each school was distributed proportionately across the eligible classes based on class enrolment figures.

Class registers served as the sampling frame for student selection. Using the class register of each selected class, eligible students were selected through systematic random sampling. The sampling interval (k) was calculated by dividing the total number of eligible students in the class by the number of students required from that class. A random starting point was selected from the first k students on the register, after which every kth student was selected until the required sample size was attained.

Where a selected student did not meet the eligibility criteria or declined participation, the next eligible student on the register was selected.

Study instrument

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire adapted from a previous study in Nigeria14, which has been previously used to assess sexual abuse experiences among adolescents in Nigeria. The instrument was divided into two sections: Section A, which captured sociodemographic characteristics (such as age, gender, class level, religion, family type, and living arrangement), and Section B, which assessed respondents’ experiences of sexual abuse, including forms, perpetrators, frequency, and disclosure patterns. The adapted instrument underwent a rigorous face and content validation process. Experts in adolescent health, public health, and gender-based violence reviewed the questionnaire to evaluate the relevance, clarity, and appropriateness of each item. Their feedback focused on the accuracy of constructs, cultural appropriateness, and clarity of wording. All suggested modifications were incorporated, and further refinement was made in consultation with the study supervisor. This process ensured that the questionnaire adequately measured the constructs of interest, thereby strengthening its face and content validity.

A pilot study involving approximately 10% of the calculated sample size was conducted among adolescents in a secondary school outside the study area. The pilot exercise was used to assess the comprehensibility, acceptability, and administration procedures of the instrument. Feedback obtained during the pilot study informed minor revisions to item wording and questionnaire sequencing before commencement of the main survey. Because the questionnaire primarily comprised socio-demographic variables and factual questions relating to experiences of sexual abuse, perpetrators, disclosure patterns, and school environment perceptions rather than a multi-item psychometric scale, internal consistency reliability testing using Cronbach’s alpha was not considered appropriate.

Study variables and theoretical framework

The study was guided by the Ecological Model of Violence, which conceptualizes sexual abuse as resulting from interacting factors operating at multiple levels: individual, interpersonal, and socio-cultural contexts12.

Dependent variable

The outcome variable was self-reported experience of sexual abuse among in-school adolescents. Sexual abuse was defined as any unwanted or non-consensual sexual experience involving coercion, force, manipulation, pressure, or inability to provide informed consent, including sexual touching, attempted sexual intercourse, or completed sexual intercourse. Only experiences meeting this definition were included in the estimation of prevalence and in the classification of forms of abuse.

Method of data collection

The collection of data was conducted after obtaining ethics approval from the Health Research Ethics Committee of the Ogun State Ministry of Education, Science and Technology. Permission to conduct this research was also sought and obtained from the administrative authorities of each selected secondary school. Before actual data collection commenced, the school authorities were fully briefed on the purposes, procedures, and expected roles to play in facilitating contact with eligible students.

Data collection was conducted by trained research assistants with prior orientation on ethical approaches to sensitive research involving adolescents.

Questionnaires were administered in private classroom settings during free periods without the presence of teachers or school administrators to minimize social desirability bias and encourage honest disclosure. Participants were seated apart to ensure privacy and confidentiality during questionnaire completion. No identifying information, including names or admission numbers, was collected.

Participants were informed that their responses would remain confidential and would not affect their academic standing. Completed questionnaires were collected immediately after completion to reduce peer influence and data loss.

Because the study addressed sensitive experiences, participants were also informed that they could skip questions they were uncomfortable answering or withdraw from the study at any stage without penalty.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered, cleaned, and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize respondents’ characteristics and experiences of sexual abuse. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations.

The study used a non-weighted school-based sample; therefore, analyses were not weighted, and findings reflect associations within the sampled population of in-school adolescents.

The analysis was guided by the Ecological Model of Violence, which conceptualizes sexual abuse as the result of interacting factors operating at multiple levels. Accordingly, explanatory variables were classified into individual-level factors (age, gender, and class level), interpersonal-level factors (family type and living arrangement), and socio-cultural factors (religion).

Bivariate analyses were conducted using Pearson’s chi-square test to examine associations between each independent variable and self-reported experience of sexual abuse. Crude odds ratios (CORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated to quantify the magnitude and direction of observed associations.

Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was subsequently performed to identify factors independently associated with sexual abuse. Consistent with the ecological framework, variables were selected for inclusion in the multivariable model based on theoretical relevance rather than solely on statistical significance at the bivariate level. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals were reported.

Prior to model estimation, multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using both variance inflation factor (VIF) and collinearity diagnostics. The VIF results are presented in Table 1. Variables were considered to exhibit problematic multicollinearity if VIF values exceeded 10 or if tolerance values were below 0.1, while condition index values above 30 with substantial variance proportions (> 0.50) for two or more variables within the same dimension were also used as additional diagnostic criteria. No evidence of problematic multicollinearity was identified, as all VIF values ranged from 1.053 to 1.205 and all tolerance values were above 0.1, while the highest condition index was 16.24.

Given the cross-sectional nature of the study, findings are presented as factors associated with sexual abuse and should not be interpreted as evidence of causality or temporal relationships.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was formally given for the study by the Health Research Ethics Committee of Ogun State Ministry of Education, Science and Technology with approval number PL545VoLIV30.

All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant ethical guidelines and regulations for research involving human participants, including the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Permission was also given by the principals of the participating schools. Written informed consent was obtained from parents/guardians of minors, while adolescents provided assent. Participation was voluntary, and respondents were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without penalty.

Participants who disclosed abuse were provided with information on available school guidance counsellors and referral pathways to appropriate adolescent health and psychosocial support services in the study area.

Results

Respondents’ characteristics

A total of 353 in-school adolescents participated in the study. The mean age of respondents was 13.03 ± 1.95 years. More than half (55.5%) were aged 10–13 years, while 44.5% were aged 14–17 years. Females constituted 52.7% of the sample, whereas males accounted for 47.3%. Slightly more than half of the respondents (51.8%) were in junior secondary classes. Most respondents were from monogamous families (69.7%) and lived with both parents (78.2%). Christianity and Islam accounted for 48.7% and 51.3% of respondents, respectively (Table 2).

Regarding the forms of abuse experienced, sexual intercourse was the most commonly reported (14.4%), followed by kissing (12.5%) and unwanted touching of the breast, buttocks, or penis (7.4%). More than half of those who experienced abuse reported that it occurred once (19.0%), while 9.3% reported two episodes and 5.9% reported multiple episodes.

Among respondents who experienced sexual abuse, only 16.1% reported the incident, whereas 18.1% did not disclose their experience to anyone. Friends were the most common recipients of disclosure (6.8%), followed by older siblings (4.2%), parents (3.7%), and teachers (1.4%).

Perceptions of sexual abuse within the school environment revealed that 39.1% of respondents believed sexual abuse was common in their school, while 57.2% reported that verbal sexual harassment occurred frequently. Furthermore, 34.8% perceived physical sexual abuse to be common, and 70.0% believed that most incidents of sexual abuse in their school went unreported (Table 3).

At the individual level, age group (χ² = 0.741, p = 0.389), class level (χ² = 0.004, p = 0.951), and gender (χ² = 1.967, p = 0.161) were not significantly associated with sexual abuse.

At the interpersonal level, both family type and living arrangement were significantly associated with sexual abuse. Adolescents from monogamous families had higher odds of reporting sexual abuse compared with those from polygamous families (COR = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.16–0.50; p < 0.001). Similarly, adolescents living with a single parent or other caregivers were more likely to report sexual abuse than those living with both parents (COR = 2.60, 95% CI: 1.55–4.36; p < 0.001).

At the socio-cultural level, religion was significantly associated with sexual abuse (χ² = 8.562, p = 0.003). Respondents identifying as Muslims had lower odds of reporting sexual abuse compared with Christians (COR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.33–0.81).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis

Variables representing individual, interpersonal, and socio-cultural level factors were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model based on the ecological framework guiding the study.

After adjustment for other variables, gender, religion, family type, and living arrangement remained significantly associated with sexual abuse. Male adolescents had lower odds of reporting sexual abuse than females (AOR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.31–0.92; p = 0.022). Respondents identifying as Christians had higher odds of reporting sexual abuse compared with Muslims (AOR = 3.43, 95% CI: 2.00–5.89; p < 0.001) (See Table 5).

At the interpersonal level, adolescents from monogamous families had higher odds of reporting sexual abuse compared with those from polygamous families (AOR = 4.61, 95% CI: 2.45–8.68; p < 0.001). The logistic regression model was statistically significant (Omnibus χ² = 59.144, p < 0.001) and explained between 15.4% and 21.3% of the variance in sexual abuse (Cox & Snell R² = 0.154; Nagelkerke R² = 0.213). The model correctly classified 72.5% of cases. However, the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was statistically significant (χ² = 74.716, p < 0.001), suggesting that the model may not have fully captured all factors associated with sexual abuse among respondents.

Discussion

This study assessed the prevalence and factors associated with self-reported sexual abuse among in-school adolescents in Abeokuta South Local Government Area, Ogun State, Southwest Nigeria. The findings show that sexual abuse remains a significant problem among adolescents in the study setting, with more than one-third (34.3%) of respondents reporting experience of sexual abuse. This level of exposure highlights that sexual abuse is not a rare occurrence among school-going adolescents and underscores the need for sustained attention within school and family environments.

The prevalence of sexual abuse identified in this study is consistent with findings from other school-based studies in Nigeria20–22 that report substantial levels of adolescent sexual abuse. The pattern of perpetrators identified in this study including schoolmates, friends, and intimate partners also reflects the interpersonal nature of adolescent sexual abuse in school environments. The prominence of peer and acquaintance perpetrators highlights the importance of interventions that extend beyond stranger-danger narratives and focus on peer relationships and school safety.

The perception of respondents regarding sexual abuse within the school environment is also notable. A considerable proportion of students reported that sexual harassment occurs frequently in their schools and that most cases go unreported. This perception is important because it reflects a possible culture of silence and underreporting, which may hinder institutional response and protection mechanisms. Low reporting rates observed in this study further support this concern, suggesting that many cases remain unaddressed6.

A key finding from this study is the variation in reported sexual abuse by gender. Male adolescents had lower odds of reporting sexual abuse compared with females in the adjusted model. While gender differences in sexual abuse reporting have been documented in previous studies23–26, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as it may reflect both differences in actual exposure and differences in disclosure patterns. Cultural norms that discourage boys from reporting victimization may also contribute to underreporting among males23,27,28, while girls may be more likely to recognize and report abusive experiences23,29. Therefore, the observed association may partially reflect reporting behaviour rather than only differences in exposure.

Religion was also significantly associated with reported sexual abuse, with adolescents identifying as Christians showing higher odds of reporting sexual abuse compared to those identifying as Muslims. This finding should not be interpreted as a causal effect of religion. Rather, it may reflect contextual or behavioural differences such as variations in disclosure practices, school composition, social environments, or community norms surrounding reporting of sensitive experiences. Given the complexity of religion as a socio-cultural variable, further qualitative and context-specific studies are needed to better understand this association.

Family structure and living arrangement emerged as important interpersonal-level correlates. Adolescents from monogamous family settings had higher odds of reporting sexual abuse compared with those from polygamous families, while those living with both parents had lower odds of reporting sexual abuse compared with those living with single parents or other arrangements. These findings suggest that adolescents’ family environments may be associated with their experiences of sexual abuse. Adolescents who do not live with both parents or who come from different family structures may have varying levels of supervision, guidance, and support, which could influence their vulnerability to abuse. Similar associations between family circumstances and sexual abuse have been reported in previous studies among adolescents in Nigeria and other sub-Saharan African settings30,31. However, because of the cross-sectional design, it is not possible to determine temporal relationships between family structure and abuse experience.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it is theory-driven, guided by the Ecological Model of Violence, which enabled a structured examination of factors associated with sexual abuse across individual, interpersonal, and socio-cultural levels. This strengthens the conceptual coherence of the analysis and reduces arbitrary selection of variables. Second, the study employed a relatively adequate sample size (n = 353) and used multistage sampling within selected schools, which improved internal validity and reduced selection bias within the study setting.

Despite these strengths, this study has limitations. First, the cross-sectional design does not permit causal inference or establishment of temporal relationships; thus, findings should be interpreted as associations only.

Second, the study was limited to in-school adolescents in three secondary schools within one local government area in Ogun State, which restricts generalizability, particularly to out-of-school adolescents and wider populations in Southwest Nigeria.

Third, the use of self-reported data on a sensitive issue may have introduced recall bias and social desirability bias, potentially leading to underreporting despite confidentiality measures.

Fourth, although sexual abuse was operationally defined to include a range of non-consensual sexual experiences involving coercion, force, manipulation, pressure, or inability to provide informed consent, the study did not conduct separate analyses based on the type or severity of abuse experienced. This limits the ability to distinguish patterns across different forms or intensities of sexual abuse.

Fifth, key contextual variables such as socioeconomic status, parental education, household income, exposure to violence, and substance use were not included, leading to possible residual confounding and reduced explanatory power of the model.

Finally, although the logistic regression model showed acceptable explanatory and classification performance, the significant Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test suggests potential model misfit. No sensitivity analyses or influence diagnostics were conducted to further assess model stability. This may limit the robustness of the model and indicates that some relevant predictors of sexual abuse may not have been captured in the analysis.

Conclusion

This study found that more than one-third of in-school adolescents in the study setting reported experiencing sexual abuse, highlighting a substantial burden within the school-going population. Guided by the Ecological Model of Violence, the study identified significant associations between sexual abuse and factors operating at individual, interpersonal, and socio-cultural levels. Specifically, gender, family type, living arrangement, and religion were significantly associated with self-reported sexual abuse.

These findings underscore the multifaceted nature of adolescent sexual abuse and suggest that vulnerability may be shaped by a combination of personal characteristics, family environments, and broader socio-cultural contexts. Further studies incorporating broader structural determinants and longitudinal designs are needed to deepen understanding of factors associated with adolescent sexual abuse.

Implications and recommendations

The findings have important implications for adolescent health, school safeguarding, and child protection programming. The high prevalence of sexual abuse observed among respondents suggests a need for strengthened school-based safeguarding systems, including confidential reporting mechanisms, adolescent-friendly support services, and comprehensive sexuality education that addresses consent, personal safety, and help-seeking behaviours.

The associations observed with family type and living arrangement highlight the importance of parental and caregiver engagement in adolescent protection efforts. Programmes aimed at strengthening caregiver supervision, communication, and support may contribute to creating safer environments for adolescents. Furthermore, the observed differences across gender and religion suggest that interventions should be sensitive to the social and cultural contexts in which adolescents live and disclose abuse experiences.

Given that a substantial proportion of respondents perceived sexual abuse to be underreported within their schools, efforts to improve awareness, reporting pathways, and access to psychosocial support services are warranted.
