Differential Privacy for Symbolic Trajectories via the Permute-and-Flip Mechanism
Alexander Benvenuti, Huaiyuan Rao, Matthew Hale

Abstract
Privacy techniques have been developed for data-driven systems, but systems with non-numeric data cannot use typical noise-adding techniques. Therefore, we develop a new mechanism for privatizing state trajectories of symbolic systems that may be represented as words over a finite alphabet. Such systems include Markov chains, Markov decision processes, and finite-state automata, and we protect their symbolic trajectories with differential privacy. The mechanism we develop randomly selects a private approximation to be released in place of the original sensitive word, with a bias towards low-error private words. This work is based on the permute-and-flip mechanism for differential privacy, which can be applied to non-numeric data. However, a naïve implementation would have to enumerate an exponentially large list of words to generate a private word. As a result, we develop a new mechanism that generates private words without ever needing to enumerate such a list. We prove that the accuracy of our mechanism is never worse than the prior state of the art, and we empirically show on a real traffic dataset that it introduces up to 55 55 % less error than the prior state of the art under a conventional privacy implementation.



I
Introduction

With the proliferation of data-driven systems, interest has arisen in
developing
techniques to privatize the user data they require, e.g., in traffic systems
[
1
,
2
]
and smart power grids
[
3
]
. Absent such protections, observers may make accurate inferences about sensitive information, such as home occupancy
[
4
]
and daily traveling routines
[
5
]
. These systems require user data to function,
and thus there exists a need to preserve users’ privacy
across a broad range of systems.

Therefore, in this work we develop a framework for privatizing trajectories generated by symbolic systems.
Symbolic systems generate sequences of non-numeric data, which are often represented as words or strings over a finite alphabet.
An example class of such systems is Markov chains, in which trajectories are sequences of states that may represent user locations in domestic time use
[
6
]
, the intersections
traversed by a user
in a traffic system
[
7
]
, or websites a user has visited
[
8
]
. Such trajectories may be sensitive, and
we develop a privacy framework for them.

We use differential privacy to develop this framework. Differential privacy is a statistical notion of privacy that was developed in the computer science literature to protect sensitive database entries when databases are queried
[
9
]
. Differential privacy has been used in data-driven systems because of its desirable properties, specifically that it is (i) robust to side information, in that learning additional information about the underlying sensitive data does not substantially weak differential privacy, and (ii) immune to post-processing, in that post-hoc computations on differentially private data do not weaken its protections. These properties have led to the development of differential privacy frameworks for filtering
[
10
]
, multi-agent control
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
, and optimization
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
, among others.
These works and others in control and optimization have
implemented differential privacy by using the Gaussian and/or Laplace mechanisms to add noise to sensitive
data (or functions thereof).

Symbolic systems present a challenge because noise cannot be added to non-numeric data.
Prior work in
[
20
,
21
]
developed privacy
for symbolic systems by implementing the exponential mechanism, which is
designed for non-numeric data.
More recently, the permute-and-flip mechanism
[
22
]
has emerged as an improvement on the exponential mechanism. It offers accuracy that is equal to or better than the exponential mechanism in all cases,
and it is often the optimal mechanism for generating private outputs (in a sense we make precise
in Section
III
).

However, the permute-and-flip mechanism can have prohibitive time complexity over large output spaces, and
the development of efficient, domain-specific implementations is an open problem
[
22
]
.
In this work, we solve that open problem
for symbolic systems.
Our approach consists of first randomly selecting a number of errors for the private output word to have, and
then constructing an automaton that uniformly samples from the set of all private output words
that are (i) the same length as the sensitive input word and (ii) have the selected number errors.

To summarize, we make the following contributions:

•

We develop a mechanism for privatizing symbolic trajectories based on the permute-and-flip mechanism
(Mechanism
1
, Theorem
1
).

•

We bound the expected Hamming distance between a sensitive trajectory and its privatized form,
and we show it is never worse than the previous state of the art (Theorem
2
).

•

We specialize this mechanism to Markov chains (Mechanism
2
, Theorem
3
).

•

We empirically evaluate our approach on a real traffic dataset and show it incurs
55\%

less error than the prior state of the art (Section

).

II
Background and Problem Statements

II-A

Symbolic Systems

Symbolic systems may be defined in terms of finite-state automata, which we define next.

Definition 1

(Finite State Automaton)
.

A finite state automaton (FSA) is a tuple
A=(Q,\Sigma,q^{0},\delta,F)
, where
Q
is a set of states,
\Sigma
is an input alphabet,
q^{0}\in Q
is the initial state,
\delta:Q\times\Sigma\to Q
is transition function between states, and
F\subseteq Q
is the set of accepting states. We use
Q^{n}
to denote the set of all state sequences of length
n
and
Q^{*}
to denote the set of all finite state sequences. We similarly define
\Sigma^{n}
as the set of all words of length
n
over
\Sigma
.

Given an FSA
A=(Q,\Sigma,q^{0},\delta,F)
, if the transition function
\delta
is nondeterministic, i.e.,
\delta:Q\times\Sigma\to 2^{Q}
, then
A
is called a nondeterministic finite-state automaton (NFA). A run on an NFA
A=(Q,\Sigma,q^{0},\delta,F)
induced by a word
w=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{n}\in\Sigma^{n}
is a sequence of states
q=q_{0}\cdots q_{n}\in Q^{n}
such that
q_{0}=q^{0}
and
q_{i+1}\in\delta(q_{i},\sigma_{i+1})
.
The automaton
A
accepts a word
w
if the final state of the induced run is an accepting state
q_{n}\in F
. The set of all words accepted by the automaton
A
is its language, denoted by
\mathcal{L}(A)
.

Throughout this work, we use the Hamming distance to compare two words of the same length,
and it is denoted
d(w,v)
for words
w,v
, which is equal to the number of positions in which the corresponding symbols differ, i.e.,
d(w,v)=|\{i\mid w_{i}\neq v_{i}\}|
.

II-B

Markov Chains

Markov chains are a widely used class of symbolic systems.
A discrete time stochastic process
(Y_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
on a state space
\mathcal{Y}
is a Markov chain if it satisfies the Markov property, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{t+1}=y_{t+1}\mid Y_{1}=y_{1},Y_{2}=y_{2},\ldots,Y_{t}=y_{t}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{t+1}=y_{t+1}\mid Y_{t}=y_{t}\right).

Throughout this work, we denote Markov chains by a tuple
(\mathcal{Y},T,y_{0})
, where
\mathcal{Y}
is the state space,
T
is the transition probability matrix, and
y_{0}\in\mathcal{Y}
is the initial state. The probability of transitioning from state
y_{i}
to state
y_{j}
is
T_{y_{i},y_{j}}=\mathbb{P}\left(y_{j}\mid y_{i}\right)
.
State
y_{j}
is
feasible
from state
y_{i}
if
T_{y_{i},y_{j}}>0
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, fix a time horizon
[n]
, and let
\mathcal{Y}^{n}
denote the set of all sequences of length
n
with initial state
y_{0}
. Any such sequence can be identified with a word
w=y_{0}\cdots y_{n}\in\mathcal{Y}^{n}
.
The word
w
is feasible
for a given Markov chain
if
T_{y_{t+1},y_{t}}>0
for all
t\in[n-1]
.
The set of all feasible words of length
n
is
denoted
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Y}^{n})
.

II-C

Differential Privacy

Given an alphabet
\Sigma
, we provide privacy to a sensitive word
w\in\Sigma^{n}
by using differential privacy.
The goal of differential privacy is to make “similar” pieces of data appear approximately indistinguishable. The notion of “similar” is defined by an adjacency relation.

Definition 2

(Word Adjacency;
[
20
]
)
.

Fix a length
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and an adjacency parameter
b\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.
Two words
w,v\in\Sigma^{n}
are said to be adjacent if
d(w,v)\leq b
.

Differential privacy is enforced by a randomized mapping called a “mechanism”, which we denote by
\mathcal{M}
.

Definition 3

(Word Differential Privacy;
[
20
]
)
.

Fix a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, an adjacency parameter
b\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, a word length
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, and a privacy parameter
\epsilon>0
. A mechanism
\mathcal{M}:\Sigma^{n}\times\Omega\to\Sigma^{n}
is word
\epsilon
-differentially private if, for all
w,v
adjacent in the sense of Definition
2
and all
L\subseteq\Sigma^{n}
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{M}(w)\in L\right)\leq e^{\epsilon}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{M}(v)\in L\right).

The parameter
\epsilon
quantifies the strength of privacy, and a smaller value of
\epsilon
implies stronger privacy. Typical values of
\epsilon
range from 0.1 to 10
[
26
]
.

III
Mechanism Design and Analysis

In this section, we solve Problems
1
and
2
.
Given an NFA
A=(Q,\Sigma,q^{0},\delta,F)
and a sensitive word
w=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{n}\in\Sigma^{n}
, to enforce word differential privacy we randomly generate a private
word
w^{\prime}=\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\cdots\sigma^{\prime}_{n}\in\Sigma^{n}
. To do so, we input the sensitive word
w
into an NFA we define in this section.
We design that NFA so that its state trajectories are

III-A

Mechanism Design

This subsection solves Problem
1
.
As described in the Introduction, we use the permute-and-flip mechanism to privatize
non-numerical data. It assigns probabilities to all possible outputs based on a utility score, which encodes how well a private output word
w^{\prime}
approximates a sensitive input word
w
. Throughout this work, we use the utility function

u(w,w^{\prime})=-d(w,w^{\prime}),

(1)

which
encodes the fact that
w^{\prime}
is a better approximation for a sensitive input word
w

if it differs from
w
by fewer symbols.

In the next lemma and definition, we consider private output words that are in some pre-specified set
L\subseteq\Sigma^{n}
, which is the set of possible
private output words that are feasible for a given system.
If all words in
\Sigma^{n}
are feasible,
then we may set
L=\Sigma^{n}
. However, some state transitions are not possible in some systems, such as Markov chains, and we allow for
L\neq\Sigma^{n}
for problems in which only a
subset of the words in
\Sigma^{n}

are feasible.
To calibrate the privacy mechanism we use,
we first require the sensitivity of the utility function.

Lemma 1

(Sensitivity;
[
20
]
)
.

Fix an alphabet
\Sigma
, a word length
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, a set
L\subseteq\Sigma^{n}
, and an adjacency parameter
b\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. Then the sensitivity of the utility function
u
from (
1
) is

\Delta u=\max_{v\in L}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{1},w_{2}\in L\\
\adj_{n,b}(w_{1},w_{2})=1\end{subarray}}|u(w_{1},v)-u(w_{2},v)|\leq b.

(2)

Next we formally state the permute-and-flip mechanism

Definition 4

(Permute-and-Flip;
[
22
]
)
.

Fix an alphabet
\Sigma
, a word length
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, a set
L\subseteq\Sigma^{n}
, and an adjacency parameter
b\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. For a sensitive input word
w\in L\subseteq\Sigma^{n}
, the permute-and-flip mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{PF}
selects the private output word
w^{\prime}\in L
with probability

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{M}_{PF}(w)=w^{\prime}\right)=\exp\left(\frac{\epsilon u(w,w^{\prime})}{2\Delta u}\right)\Psi(b,L,w^{\prime}),

(3)

where

\Psi(b,L,w^{\prime})=\sum_{k=0}^{|L|}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq L\\
|S|=k\\
w^{\prime}\not\in S\end{subarray}}\prod_{s\in S}\exp\left(\frac{\epsilon u(w,s)}{2\Delta u}\right).

(4)

A direct implementation of the permute-and-flip mechanism requires knowledge of the Hamming distance between the sensitive input word and every word in
\Sigma^{n}
to compute
\Psi(b,L,w^{\prime})
. There are
m^{n}
total strings of length
n
on an alphabet of
m
symbols, and enumerating all such strings
is prohibitive for large sensitive input words or large alphabets.
The authors in
[
21
]
faced the same challenge when implementing the exponential mechanism for privatizing words, and they developed an efficient implementation for selecting a private output word which does not require enumerating all strings of length
n
.

We seek to develop a similar framework that implements the permute-and-flip mechanism. Inspired by the approach in
[
21
]
, we use the modified Hamming distance automaton.

Definition 5

(Modified Hamming distance automaton;
[
21
]
)
.

Fix an alphabet
\Sigma
and a word length
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. For a word
x\in\Sigma^{n}
and a distance
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, the modified Hamming distance NFA (MNFA) is an NFA
A_{x,\ell}=(Q_{x,\ell},\Sigma,q^{0},\delta,F_{n,\ell})
such that
\mathcal{L}(A_{x,\ell})
is the set of all words of length
n
with Hamming distance from

equal to
\ell
. Each state

can transfer to another state by a policy
\mu(\cdot,\cdot\mid q_{i}):Q_{x,\ell}\times\Sigma\to[0,1]
, where
\mu(q_{i+1},\sigma_{i+1}\mid q_{i})
is the probability that the input symbol
\sigma_{i+1}
causes a transition from state
q_{i}
to state
q_{i+1}
.

VI
Conclusion

We have presented a framework for privatizing symbolic trajectories based on the permute-and-flip mechanism, answering an open question and directly improving upon the state of the art. We have proved that our framework is, at worst, equivalent in expected error to the prior state-of-the-art mechanism, and empirically we find up to a
55.7\%
reduction in error at word
5
-differential privacy. Future work will develop an online mechanism for implementing the permute-and-flip mechanism
in real time as trajectories are generated.

Proof of Theorem 1

w^{\prime}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{M}_{1}(w)=w^{\prime}\right)

(13)

Proof of Theorem 2

We begin by deriving the upper bound in the theorem statement.
When using
[
21
, Mechanism 3]
, the probability of selecting a Hamming distance
\ell
is

\mathbb{P}_{Exp}\left(\ell\right)=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon\ell}{2b}\right)\binom{n}{\ell}(m-1)^{\ell},

where
\frac{1}{Z}
is a normalization constant. Substituting this expression into the probability distribution
from (
7
) in Mechanism
1
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\ell\right)=\mathbb{P}_{Exp}\left(\ell\right)\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)Z,

(15)

where
\mathbb{P}\left(\ell\right)
denotes the probability of selecting a Hamming distance
\ell
under Mechanism
2
.
For
\mathbb{P}\left(\ell\right)
to be a valid probability mass function, we require
\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\ell\right)=1
. Then,

Z=\frac{1}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}_{Exp}\left(\ell\right)\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{Exp}\left[\Phi\right]}.

(16)

Substituting (
16
) into (
15
) gives

\mathbb{P}\left(\ell\right)=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{Exp}\left(\ell\right)\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)}{\mathbb{E}_{Exp}\left[\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)\right]}.

(17)

Taking the expectation of
\ell
using the probability mass function in (
17
) gives

\mathbb{E}\left[\ell\right]=\frac{\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\ell\mathbb{P}_{Exp}\left(\ell\right)\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)}{\mathbb{E}_{Exp}\left[\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)\right]}.

(18)

From the Law of the Unconscious Statistician
[
29
]
, (
18
) becomes

\mathbb{E}\left[\ell\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{Exp}\left[\ell\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{Exp}\left[\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)\right]}.

From the definition of the covariance, we find

\mathbb{E}\left[\ell\right]=\mathbb{E}_{Exp}\left[\ell\right]+\frac{\text{Cov}\left(\ell,\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)\right)}{\mathbb{E}_{Exp}\left[\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)\right]}.

(19)

Since the identity map
\ell\mapsto\ell
is strictly increasing
and
\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)
is decreasing in
\ell
from
[
30
, Lemma 4]
, we have that
\text{Cov}\left(\ell,\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)\right)\leq 0
from Chebyshev’s sum inequality
[
31
]
.
Additionally, because
\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)>0
by definition,

\mathbb{E}_{Exp}\left[\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)\right]>0
. As a result,

\mathbb{E}\left[\ell\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{Exp}\left[\ell\right].

The distribution
\mathbb{P}_{Exp}\left(\ell\right)
is equal to
\Binom(n,q)
where
q=\frac{(m-1)e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{2b}}}{1+(m-1)e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{2b}}}
. Then

\mathbb{E}_{Exp}\left[\ell\right]=\frac{nC}{1+C},

where
C=(m-1)e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{2b}}
.

Next we derive the lower bound in the theorem statement. Returning to (
19
), we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\ell\right]=\mathbb{E}_{Exp}\left[\ell\right]+\frac{\text{Cov}\left(\ell,\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)\right)}{\mathbb{E}_{Exp}\left[\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)\right]}.

From Grüs’s inequality
[
32
, Chapter X]
,
we have

|\text{Cov}\left(\ell,\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)\right)|\\
\leq\frac{1}{4}(\sup_{\ell}\ell-\inf_{\ell}\ell)(\sup_{\ell}\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)-\inf_{\ell}\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell))\\
=\frac{n}{4}(\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},0)-\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},n)).

(20)

Because
\text{Cov}\left(\ell,\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)\right)\leq 0
,

\text{Cov}\left(\ell,\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)\right)\geq-\frac{n}{4}(\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},0)-\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},n)).

(21)

Substituting
\mathbb{E}_{Exp}\left[\ell\right]
and (
21
) into (
19
) gives

\mathbb{E}\left[\ell\right]\geq\frac{nC}{1+C}-\frac{n(\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},0)-\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},n))}{4\mathbb{E}_{Exp}\left[\Phi(b,\mathscr{L},\ell)\right]}.

Finally, the random variable
\ell

is always contained in the interval

[0,n]
. Thus, from the Hoeffding inequality
[
33
]
,

\mathbb{P}\left(|\ell-\mathbb{E}\left[\ell\right]|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp(-\frac{2t^{2}}{n^{2}}).

∎
