Explaining and Preventing Alignment Collapse in Iterative RLHF
Etienne Gauthier, Francis Bach, Michael I. Jordan

Abstract
Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) typically assumes a static or non-strategic reward model (RM). In iterative deployment, however, the policy generates the data on which the RM is retrained, creating a feedback loop. Building on the Stackelberg game formulation of this interaction, we derive an analytical decomposition of the policy’s true optimization gradient into a standard policy gradient and a parameter-steering term that captures the policy’s influence on the RM’s future parameters. We show that standard iterative RLHF, which drops this steering term entirely, suffers from alignment collapse : the policy systematically exploits the RM’s blind spots, producing low-quality, high-reward outputs whose feedback reinforces the very errors it exploits. To mitigate this, we propose foresighted policy optimization (FPO), a mechanism-design intervention that restores the missing steering term by regularizing the policy’s parameter-steering effect on RM updates. We instantiate FPO via a scalable first-order approximation and demonstrate that it prevents alignment collapse on both controlled environments and an LLM alignment pipeline using Llama-3.2-1B.

Introduction

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is the dominant approach to aligning large language models (LLMs) with human intent
(
11
)
. However, it is fundamentally limited by reward over-optimization, an instance of the general phenomenon known as Goodhart’s Law
(
8
;
29
;
36
)
: learned policies exploit imperfections in the reward model, achieving high scores while deviating from true human preferences
(
67
;
25
)
. To mitigate these blind spots, state-of-the-art alignment pipelines have shifted toward iterative RLHF. In this regime, the RM is periodically retrained on new preference data generated by the active policy
(
52
;
4
;
70
;
3
)
. By continually retraining the RM on data generated by the current policy, iterative updates are intended to provide a robust, adaptive target for alignment. This coupling transforms the alignment process into a dynamic system where the policy’s current behavior determines the RM’s future training distribution, a coupling that raises significant challenges for analysis and understanding.

Recent literature formalizes the iterative RLHF process as a bilevel optimization problem, a structure conceptually equivalent to a Stackelberg game
(
45
;
20
;
12
;
71
)
. Building on this insight, we show how the dynamic coupling fundamentally alters the policy’s optimization landscape. By analytically unrolling the bilevel optimization, we derive the exact steering gradient that the policy exerts on the RM’s future weights. This derivation exposes the true
implicit effective reward
that a game-theoretically optimal, foresighted policy maximizes. This effective reward is not merely the proxy reward, but the sum of the proxy reward and a
parameter-steering
term. As our analysis reveals, an optimal policy is naturally self-correcting: it anticipates the RM’s future learning dynamics, automatically discounting regions where the proxy currently overestimates true utility and upweighting regions where it underestimates. Thus, optimizing the true effective reward naturally guards against reward hacking by prioritizing long-term alignment over myopic exploitation.

With this perspective in hand, we turn to the critical limitation of standard iterative RLHF that employ myopic optimizers such as REINFORCE
(
73
)
or PPO
(
61
)
. We define
alignment collapse
as the systematic failure of this myopic trajectory to converge to the true, safe Stackelberg equilibrium. Because the myopic policy drops the corrective steering term, it greedily exploits the RM’s blind spots, forcing a pathological feedback loop that diverges from true human utility.

To cure this optimization myopia, we propose a mechanism-design intervention that fundamentally restores the Stackelberg dynamics. By incorporating the analytical steering term, we construct a targeted penalty that explicitly regularizes the policy, forcing it to account for its parameter-steering influence on the RM’s updates. Because this intervention is formulated strictly as an augmented penalty applied during the policy optimization phase, it integrates readily into current RLHF pipelines. Furthermore, to overcome the prohibitive computational cost of the inverse Hessian matrices typically required for exact bilevel optimization, we implement our penalty using a highly scalable, first-order relaxation based on the TracIn method
(
26
)
.

(1)
We unroll the iterative Stackelberg game to prove the policy’s true optimization landscape is governed by an implicit effective reward (the proxy reward plus a parameter-steering term), and show that ignoring this steering term directly causes alignment collapse. 2.1
Iterative RLHF

We begin by formally defining the RLHF setup, building on the framework introduced by
11
and later extended to LLM training by
79

\mathcal{X}
be the input space (prompts) and
\mathcal{Y}

The standard RLHF pipeline consists of three phases:
(1) Supervised fine-tuning:

(3) Reinforcement learning:
A policy
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is optimized to maximize the expected reward assigned by
r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
, subject to a KL divergence constraint which enforces that updates remain local to
\pi_{\rm ref}

Reward modeling: Pointwise vs. pairwise learning.

In practice, RMs are typically trained on pairwise human preference comparisons, for example using a Bradley-Terry (BT) loss
(
7
)
. However, analyzing dynamic coupling directly in a pairwise regime is cumbersome notationally due to the joint distribution over multiple generated responses. To ensure a clear and intuitive exposition of the geometric mechanics behind alignment collapse, we first develop our core theoretical framework assuming an abstract pointwise reward setting, where the RM minimizes the expected loss over single pairs
(x,y)
:

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{\mathcal{X}},y\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot|x)}\left[\ell(x,y;\boldsymbol{\phi})\right]+\Omega(\boldsymbol{\phi}),

(2)

where
\Omega(\boldsymbol{\phi})
is a twice continuously differentiable (
C^{2}
) regularization term (e.g.,
L_{2}
regularization). For empirical simulations, we use the mean squared error (MSE)
\ell(x,y;\boldsymbol{\phi})=\frac{1}{2}(r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x,y)-U(x,y))^{2}
against a true utility oracle
U
. Once the parameter-steering incentives are established in this idealized setting, we rigorously extend all results to the standard pairwise BT regime in Section
5
.

Dynamic coupling.

In iterative deployment, phases 2 and 3 of the RLHF procedure are coupled in a loop: the process alternates between updating the RM parameters
\boldsymbol{\phi}
and the policy parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. The training distribution for the RM is not fixed; it is dynamically generated by the current policy
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. As a result, the RM is trained to minimize
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})
based on data induced by the policy, while the policy is optimized to maximize
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})
based on the RM’s evaluations. This tight feedback loop between the two naturally motivates a game-theoretic formulation of the iterative RLHF procedure.

influence functions

Section

2.3
Stackelberg alignment game

The interdependence between the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and
\boldsymbol{\phi}
defines a coupled dynamical system. A simpler structure emerges, however, when the updates occur on sufficiently separated timescales, e.g., when the RM is trained close to convergence between policy updates. In this regime, each update can be viewed as approximately responding to the current state of the other: the RM adapts to the policy-induced data distribution, while the policy update accounts for how its behavior steers future reward estimates. This interaction gives rise to an implicit hierarchical structure, in which one component shapes the environment seen by the other, and the latter adapts in response. Consequently, the iterative RLHF pipeline can be viewed as approximating a Stackelberg game which induces a bilevel optimization problem.

Following
45
and
12
, the policy optimizer naturally plays the role of the
Leader
, committing to parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
to maximize its objective
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})
. The RM optimizer acts as the
Follower
, observing the policy’s induced data distribution to select a best-response
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\Phi
that minimizes its loss
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})
. This sequential interaction induces the following bilevel optimization problem:

\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}F(\boldsymbol{\theta})\coloneqq\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\quad\text{such that}\quad\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}).

(4)

3
The Implicit Effective Reward and Alignment Collapse

We now derive the true optimization landscape underlying policy optimization in the Stackelberg game. The key object is the total derivative
\frac{dF}{d\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, which accounts for how the Follower’s optimal parameters
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}
respond to the Leader’s choice of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

Assumption 3.1

(Regularity)
.

Throughout the rest of this paper, we make the following assumptions:

(A1)

The prompt-response space
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
is compact.

(A2)

The policy density
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x,y)
is
C^{2}
in
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, and the reward function
r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x,y)
and the per-sample loss
\ell(x,y;\boldsymbol{\phi})
are
C^{2}
in
\boldsymbol{\phi}
, for all
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. Furthermore,
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y|x)>0

for all
\boldsymbol{\theta},x,y
.

(A3)

The RM loss
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\cdot)
is strongly convex in
\boldsymbol{\phi}
for all
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

Assumptions (A1) and (A2) are standard mild regularity conditions. The strict positivity requirement
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y|x)>0
guarantees that the KL divergence in the Leader’s objective remains well-defined. While often left implicit in alignment literature, this is naturally satisfied whenever the policy uses a softmax output layer, as is standard for LLMs. Assumption (A3) is a more crucial structural requirement standard in bilevel optimization; it guarantees that the RM possesses a unique global minimum, preventing degenerate Follower responses.

Under Assumption
3.1
, the objectives
\mathcal{J}
and
\mathcal{L}
are
C^{2}
in both
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and
\boldsymbol{\phi}
, and
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\cdot)
is strongly convex in
\boldsymbol{\phi}
for all
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
. Using standard implicit differentiation techniques from continuous bilevel optimization
(
54
;
59
)
, the Follower’s best-response map
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is differentiable, with Jacobian:

Lemma 3.2
.

The Jacobian of the Follower’s best response map is:

\frac{d\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}{d\boldsymbol{\theta}}=-\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*})\right]^{-1}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}).

(5)

By applying the chain rule to the total objective
F(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and substituting this Jacobian, we decompose the policy’s true optimization landscape into two distinct components:

Theorem 3.3
.

The total gradient of the Leader’s objective
F(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is:

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}F(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}=\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))^{\top}}_{\text{Standard Policy Gradient}}-\underbrace{\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)^{\top}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\right]^{-1}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))}_{\text{Parameter-steering Gradient}}.

(6)

This decomposition exhibits the core flaw in modern alignment pipelines. The first term is the policy gradient used by standard algorithms (e.g., REINFORCE or PPO), which treats the RM as a static entity. The second term is the
parameter-steering gradient
, which captures how the policy’s current outputs warp the RM’s future parameters.

3.1
Mechanics of parameter steering

The Jacobian in Lemma
3.2
gives no immediate intuition about what kind of object the policy is implicitly optimizing. We now show that this Jacobian admits a far more informative form: the parameter-steering gradient takes exactly the same shape as the classical policy gradient theorem
(
68
;
73
)
, with influence functions playing the role of the per-sample reward:

Theorem 3.4
.

The sensitivity of the RM is the expected influence function weighted by the policy score function:

\frac{d\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}{d\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{\mathcal{X}},y\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot|x)}\left[\mathcal{I}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x,y)\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y|x)\right)^{\top}\right].

Intuitively, Theorem
3.4
says that the RM’s parameters shift in the direction of the influence of samples the policy is most likely to generate: the more a sample contributes to the RM’s loss landscape, and the more the policy can increase its probability, the more it pulls the RM’s parameters.

To quantify how this parametric shift translates into actual reward gains for the Leader, we define the
global reward gradient direction
:

\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{\mathcal{X}},y\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot|x)}[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x,y)],

(7)

writing
\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\coloneqq\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))
at the Follower’s optimum.

Theorem 3.5
.

The parameter-steering gradient component in Theorem
3.3
can be rewritten as the expected inner product between the global reward gradient direction and the influence vector, weighted by the policy score function:

\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\right)^{\top}\frac{d\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}{d\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{\mathcal{X}},y\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot|x)}\left[\left\langle\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\mathcal{I}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x,y)\right\rangle\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y|x)\right)^{\top}\right],

where
\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is defined in Equation (
7
).

In other words, the parameter-steering gradient
is
a policy gradient, but with respect to an implicit per-sample reward
\left\langle\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\mathcal{I}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x,y)\right\rangle
that scores how well a sample’s influence on the RM aligns with the direction in which the policy collects reward globally. Samples whose generation pushes the RM toward overestimating their own reward receive a positive implicit bonus.

3.2
The implicit objective of iterative RLHF

With both components of the total gradient now sharing a common score-function structure, we can seamlessly combine them. By substituting the influence-based steering gradient obtained in Theorem
3.5
back into the total derivative decomposition from Theorem
3.3
, we reveal the unified, true optimization landscape the policy optimizer actually faces.

Corollary 3.6
.

Under Stackelberg dynamics, optimizing the total Leader objective
F(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is equivalent to performing a standard policy update (with the usual KL penalty) against the implicit effective reward:

\tilde{r}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x,y)\coloneqq r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}(x,y)+\langle\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\mathcal{I}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x,y)\rangle.

(8)

Corollary
3.6
implies that global optimization in the true Stackelberg game requires maximizing an effective reward
\tilde{r}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Standard iterative RLHF algorithms, by contrast, are myopic: they optimize solely the proxy reward
r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
, completely ignoring the parameter-steering term
\langle\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\mathcal{I}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x,y)\rangle
.

We call the resulting failure mode
alignment collapse
: the myopic policy exploits the RM’s blind spots, and retraining the RM on this exploitative data reinforces the very errors being exploited. Over successive iterations, this creates a pathological feedback loop, driving the system into a regime dominated by short-sighted reward manipulation rather than genuine alignment.

Remark 3.7

(Why the Stackelberg equilibrium is better aligned)
.

Due to the finite capacity of neural networks and the inherent noise in human preference labels, the RM is inevitably misspecified, so a higher objective value does not imply higher true utility. However, this misspecification is not uniform: the RM is well-calibrated in regions with dense training coverage, but highly inaccurate in out-of-distribution regions. The pathology of myopic optimization is that it systematically drives the policy toward poorly calibrated regions of the RM, where overestimation is largest, and retraining on this data reinforces these errors. A foresighted policy avoids this by internalizing its impact on the RM, keeping the policy in regions where the proxy reward is a more reliable estimate of true utility. Our goal is not to correct the RM’s misspecification, but to prevent its amplification.

4
Mechanism Design: FPO

We now introduce a mechanism-design intervention that explicitly restores the parameter-steering term identified in Corollary
3.6
. By forcing the policy to account for its steering effect on the RM’s future parameters, we recover the self-correcting dynamics of the true Stackelberg equilibrium.

Definition 4.1
.

We define the
foresighted policy optimization (FPO)
objective as the standard policy objective augmented with a parameter-steering correction, denoted
\mathcal{R}_{\rm FPO}
:

\mathcal{J}_{\rm FPO}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})=\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{\mathcal{X}},y\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot|x)}\left[\mathcal{R}_{\rm FPO}(x,y)\right],

(9)

where
\gamma>0
is a coefficient controlling the strength of the parameter-steering correction, and
\mathcal{R}_{\rm FPO}(x,y)\coloneqq\langle\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}),\mathcal{I}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x,y)\rangle
.

From exact steering to TracIn.

While the exact steering term
\mathcal{R}_{\rm FPO}(x,y)=\langle\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{r}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}),\mathcal{I}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x,y)\rangle
, with
\mathcal{I}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x,y)=-H_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}^{-1}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell(x,y;\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*})
, formally internalizes the policy’s steering effect, computing the inverse Hessian
H_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}^{-1}
is intractable in high-dimensional settings. We therefore instantiate FPO via a scalable first-order relaxation: (1) we approximate the influence function by its loss gradient by assuming
H_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}\approx I
. This approximation mirrors gradient-based optimizers without adaptive preconditioning. Extending FPO with diagonal approximations analogous to those in Adam
39
or Adagrad
22
is a natural direction, which we leave to future work; (2) we replace the global reward gradient
\bar{\mathbf{g}}_{r}
with the local sample gradient
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x,y)
; and (3) we evaluate the proxy online using current parameters
\boldsymbol{\phi}
rather than best-response parameters
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}
. This leads to the following connection to the TracIn influence estimator
(
26
)
:

Proposition 4.2
.

In the iterative RLHF setting, applying the TracIn method to measure a generated sample’s self-influence, defined as how training the RM on a sample
(x,y)
changes the proxy reward it assigns to that exact same sample, yields an estimator exactly proportional to the relaxed inner product:
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\rm FPO}(x,y)\coloneqq\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x,y),-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell(x,y;\boldsymbol{\phi})\rangle
.

Note that while standard TracIn is computed post-hoc across stored checkpoints,
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\rm FPO}
is evaluated online during iterative training, requiring only a single additional gradient evaluation per sample.

An oracle-free penalty.

Evaluating
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\rm FPO}
defined in Proposition
4.2
under the MSE loss yields
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\rm FPO}(x,y)=-(r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x,y)-U(x,y))\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x,y)\|^{2}
, which decomposes into an
overconfidence error

r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x,y)-U(x,y)
and a
gradient magnitude
term
\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x,y)\|^{2}
. Because the ground-truth
U
is unobservable in practice, we construct a deployable mechanism by absorbing the expected overconfidence into the hyperparameter
\gamma
. This yields the practical, oracle-free penalty:
\bar{\mathcal{R}}_{\rm FPO}(x,y)\coloneqq-\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x,y)\|^{2}
. While this loses the exact bidirectional self-correction of the Stackelberg game,
\bar{\mathcal{R}}_{\rm FPO}
acts as a regularizer that universally penalizes the policy for exploiting highly volatile regions. Despite this relaxation, we show in Section
6
that the resulting penalty empirically outperforms standard iterative RLHF. Table
1
summarizes the resulting taxonomy of penalties
\mathcal{R}_{\rm FPO}
,
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\rm FPO}
, and
\bar{\mathcal{R}}_{\rm FPO}

5
Extension to Pairwise Comparisons

Our theoretical framework up to this point has assumed a pointwise reward learning setting to isolate the geometric mechanics of parameter steering. In practice, however, modern iterative RLHF pipelines optimize the RM using pairwise preference comparisons, via the BT model for example. We now extend our analysis to show that alignment collapse also arises in the pairwise setting.

5.1
Pairwise Stackelberg dynamics

To rigorously formulate the pairwise game, we model the data generation process of iterative RLHF. For a given prompt
x\sim P_{\mathcal{X}}
, the policy generates a response
y\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot|x)
, while a second response
y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\rm ref}(\cdot|x)
is drawn from a reference policy.
2
2

2

Note that if we allow the reference policy to also depend on
\boldsymbol{\theta}

Let
P^{*}(y\succ y^{\prime}|x)
denote the unobservable ground-truth human preference probability. The RM minimizes the expected BT cross-entropy loss:

\mathcal{L}_{BT}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{\mathcal{X}},y\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot|x),y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\rm ref}(\cdot|x)}\left[\ell_{BT}(x,y,y^{\prime};\boldsymbol{\phi})\right]+\Omega(\boldsymbol{\phi}),

where
\ell_{BT}(x,y,y^{\prime};\boldsymbol{\phi})=-P^{*}(y\succ y^{\prime}|x)\log P_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(y\succ y^{\prime}|x)-P^{*}(y^{\prime}\succ y|x)\log P_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(y^{\prime}\succ y|x)
and the RM’s predicted preference is
P_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(y\succ y^{\prime}|x)=\sigma\left(r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x,y)-r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x,y^{\prime})\right)

7
Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Work

In this work, we unrolled the Stackelberg game formulation of iterative RLHF and showed that standard myopic policy optimization omits a crucial parameter-steering term, inevitably driving the system toward alignment collapse. To address this, we introduced foresighted policy optimization (FPO), a mechanism that regularizes the policy’s impact on future reward model parameters and restores the self-correcting dynamics of the Stackelberg equilibrium.

While FPO provides a principled geometric defense against reward hacking, our framework has several limitations that motivate future work. First, our derivation of the steering gradient relies on a strong convexity assumption for the follower objective. Although standard in bilevel optimization to ensure a unique best response, this assumption does not hold globally for overparameterized neural reward models, and extending our analysis to non-convex settings is an important direction. Second, our LLM experiments serve as a proof of concept and remain limited in scale. Despite these limitations, this work provides a rigorous foundation for understanding failure modes in iterative alignment and offers a promising direction for more robust alignment of future models.

Appendix A
Related Work

RLHF and iterative alignment.

The RLHF pipeline was introduced by
11
and scaled to LLMs by
79
,
67
, and
52
. Modern deployments adopt an iterative variant in which the RM is periodically retrained on policy-generated data
[
4
,
70
,
3
]
.
74
and
21
provide theoretical and practical recipes for online iterative RLHF. Our work complements this line by showing that, even with iterative RM updates, myopic policy optimization creates a pathological feedback loop that standard analyses do not capture. More recently,
76
introduced Self-Rewarding Language Models, where the model acts as its own reward judge during iterative alignment; extending our Stackelberg analysis to this setting, where the policy and reward model share parameters, is an interesting direction for future work. Similarly,
10
study the dynamics of long-term iterative alignment pipelines, analyzing the coupling between policy generation and preference data within a generalized preference model. While
66
recently used the term
alignment collapse
to describe how second-order geometric curvature degrades safety during static supervised fine-tuning, our work identifies a distinct, game-theoretic form of alignment collapse unique to iterative RLHF, driven by the active parameter-steering dynamics between the policy and the reward model.

Reward overoptimization and hacking.

The phenomenon of reward hacking, where optimizing an imperfect proxy reward degrades performance on the true, unobservable reward, has been formally characterized as a fundamental limitation of proxy alignment
[
65
]
. Empirically,
25
demonstrate this overoptimization in LLMs, showing that proxy reward increases while true quality degrades as optimization pressure grows, in accordance with Goodhart’s Law.
58
extend these scaling laws to direct alignment algorithms. Several mitigations have been proposed, including reward model ensembles
[
15
,
23
]
, constrained optimization
[
48
]
, and reward shaping techniques
[
24
]
. These approaches treat the RM as a static object and seek to make it more robust. In contrast, our work identifies a failure mode that arises specifically from the dynamic coupling between the policy and the RM in iterative training: the policy’s data generation warps the RM’s future parameters, creating a self-reinforcing feedback loop that static robustness cannot address.
62
and
56
identify sycophancy (the tendency of models to tailor responses to perceived user or RM preferences) as a primary symptom of overoptimization. Our work provides a causal mechanism for this behavior: sycophancy is an optimal strategy for a policy that can steer the RM into regions of high-reward overestimation. Our theoretical framework demonstrates that regularizing the reward gradient is a principled defense against overoptimization, a high-level intuition shared by recent gradient-based regularizers
[
2
,
51
]
and by landscape-flatness analyses of the RLHF objective
[
60
]
. The underlying mechanisms are nevertheless distinct: those works derive penalties from static flatness-accuracy bounds assuming a frozen RM, whereas our penalty arises from the iterative coupling itself and targets the sensitivity of the RM’s parameters under retraining rather than the flatness of the policy loss.
1
address the related distribution-shift problem in iterative RLHF via importance-weighted RM training, which is complementary to our parameter-steering perspective.

Stackelberg games and bilevel optimization in alignment.

Recent literature has increasingly analyzed RLHF through the lens of game theory. One prominent approach frames alignment as a simultaneous zero-sum game to find a Nash equilibrium
[
49
,
69
]
. However, when a distinct reward model is actively maintained and retrained, the dynamics become strictly sequential.
9
were among the first to formalize iterative RLHF as a bilevel optimization, with the RM as upper level and the policy as follower, and explicitly computed the steering gradient via implicit differentiation. The complementary Stackelberg formulation, with the policy as leader and the RM as follower, was introduced by
45
, who propose a nested gradient algorithm without analytically unrolling the bilevel structure.
12
consider a Stackelberg game between the policy and an adversarial preference distribution to improve data efficiency.
20
formulate iterative RLHF as bilevel optimization and develop principled penalty-based algorithms, sidestepping the inner problem via reward-policy equivalence.
53
introduce Stackelberg learning from human feedback, which frames preference optimization as a sequential game between two policies rather than between a policy and a reward model.
71
study the Stackelberg game from the RM’s perspective for inference-time alignment. On the computational side,
57
propose Nyström-based methods to stabilize inverse Hessian computation.
63
develop the first convergent penalty-based algorithm for bilevel RL, where the penalty enforces lower-level stationarity; their method requires approximately solving the RM training to convergence at every outer iteration and applies to general bilevel RL problems. Like
63
,
77
adopt the reverse Stackelberg ordering, and their penalty enforces optimality conditions rather than restoring an analytical steering term. Methodologically, our gradient derivation builds on the classical use of the Implicit Function Theorem in continuous bilevel optimization
[
54
,
59
]
. Our work differs from all of the above in its analytical alignment contribution: among formulations that adopt the natural Stackelberg ordering
[
45
]
, we are the first to unroll these exact dynamics, reveal the implicit effective reward, decompose it into a proxy reward and a parameter-steering term, and formally prove that dropping this term is the geometric cause of alignment collapse. Furthermore, our analytical decomposition of the steering gradient opens new avenues for alignment auditing. Because myopic RLHF leaves the parameter-steering gradient non-zero at convergence, this residual provides a natural target for sequential testing and equilibrium-monitoring frameworks
[
28
]
, suggesting a path toward dynamically detecting alignment collapse during deployment without requiring ground-truth utility oracles.

Influence functions in machine learning.

Influence functions, originating from robust statistics
[
14
]
, were adapted to deep learning by
40
to trace model predictions back to training data.
26
proposed TracIn as a scalable first-order alternative that avoids inverse Hessian computation. These tools have been used for data valuation, debugging, and understanding memorization.
47
apply influence functions to trace RLHF outcomes back to specific human feedback samples, demonstrating their utility for diagnosing alignment failures. Their analysis is post-hoc, whereas our FPO mechanism uses influence functions online as a penalty during training.

Performative prediction.

The dynamic coupling in iterative RLHF is conceptually related to performative prediction
[
55
]
, where a deployed model influences the distribution it is evaluated on.
46
and
17
study convergence in such settings. Closely related,
27
analyze how strategic agents can coordinate data submissions to steer the parameters of a learning platform; an analogous steering effect arises in our setting, with the policy playing the role of a strategic agent whose data generation shapes the RM’s future parameters, although the steering is implicit and emerges endogenously from optimization rather than explicit coordination. Our Stackelberg formulation can thus be viewed as a performative prediction problem where the policy (the deployed model) shapes the RM’s training distribution, and the RM (the prediction target) adapts in response. Our concept of alignment collapse also shares structural parallels with model collapse
[
64
]
, where generative models trained on their own output suffer from progressive distribution shift and loss of information. Our work identifies a failure mode in the RLHF objective, where the collapse is driven by the strategic exploitation of the reward signal rather than passive recursive distillation. Our finding that myopic RLHF converges to a misaligned equilibrium also mirrors recent results in performative reinforcement learning, where standard algorithms achieve performative stability rather than true optimality
[
5
]
.

Appendix B
Informal Mechanics of Alignment Collapse

The formal analysis in Section
3
derives the parameter-steering term via implicit differentiation. Here we provide complementary intuition by decomposing the proxy reward into true utility and estimation error, and tracing the resulting feedback loop step by step.

Decompose the proxy reward at iteration
t
into the true utility and an estimation error:

r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}}(x,y)=U(x,y)+\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}}(x,y).

A myopic policy optimizer maximizes the expected proxy reward. The policy gradient decomposes as:

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{\mathcal{X}},y\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot|x)}[r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}}(x,y)]=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{x,y}[U(x,y)]+\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{x,y}[\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}}(x,y)]}_{\text{hacking gradient}}.

The optimizer cannot distinguish true utility from error, so the hacking gradient shifts probability mass toward responses where the estimation error is important. These large positive errors concentrate in poorly calibrated regions where the RM’s training data is sparse.

The RM is then retrained on data generated by the updated policy
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}}
. Even if the RM corrects its overestimation in the exploited region, the policy parameters have already shifted toward that region. By the time the RM corrects a local overestimation, the policy has already migrated into new uncalibrated regions, perpetuating the exploitation cycle.

By penalizing the policy for generating responses in regions of high local gradient norms, precisely the poorly calibrated regions where
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}}
is large and exploitable, FPO suppresses the hacking gradient, preventing the policy from initiating the displacement cycle.

This confirms that penalizing RM sensitivity alone is sufficient to mitigate alignment collapse in practice, even when the residual error direction is unknown.

C.3.1
Extended methodological details

Architecture and parameter scope.

The policy
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is initialized from Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct
[
30
]
and adapted via LoRA
[
37
]
with rank
r=16
,
\alpha=32
, and dropout
0.05
, applied to the attention projections (
q_proj
,
k_proj
,
v_proj
,
o_proj
). The base policy is loaded in 4-bit NF4 quantization with double quantization and bfloat16 compute
[
19
]
, and the policy parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is the LoRA adapter only. The RM
r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
is initialized from
OpenAssistant/reward-model-deberta-v3-base

[
32
,
41
]
, loaded in fp16 with the backbone frozen; the RM parameter
\boldsymbol{\phi}
is the weights of the classification head. The preference oracle is a frozen Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct loaded in 8-bit quantization
[
18
]
, used both to generate reference responses
y^{\prime}
and to provide ground-truth utility scores
U(x,y)
.

Oracle utility.

Following the RLAIF paradigm
[
42
]
, we treat the frozen oracle as a stationary proxy for human preference. For a tokenized response
y
given a prompt
x
, the oracle utility is defined as
U(x,y)=-\mathcal{L}_{\rm CE}(y|x)+5
, where
\mathcal{L}_{\rm CE}
is the per-token cross-entropy of the oracle on
(x,y)
. The constant offset and the clipping range
[-15,5]
are used purely for numerical stability and centering, and have no effect on the relative ordering of responses.

Iteration loop.

Prompts
x
are sampled sequentially from the
train_prefs
split of UltraFeedback
[
16
]
; the simulation runs for
T=500
iterations, one prompt per iteration. At each step:

1.

The frozen oracle generates a reference response
y^{\prime}
, and the active policy generates
N=8
candidate responses
y_{1},\dots,y_{N}
. All generations use nucleus sampling
[
34
]
with
\text{top-}p=0.9
, temperature
0.8
, and
max_new_tokens
=
32
(minimum 5).

2.

For each candidate
y_{i}
, the policy selects the winner
y^{*}=\argmax_{i}\big[r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x,y_{i})+\gamma\,\mathcal{R}_{\rm FPO}(x,y_{i},y^{\prime})\big]
, where
\mathcal{R}_{\rm FPO}
is either zero (standard RLHF),
\bar{\mathcal{R}}_{\rm FPO}
(practical), or
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\rm FPO}
(relaxed), with strength fixed at
\gamma=10
.

3.

The practical penalty
\bar{\mathcal{R}}_{\rm FPO}
is computed as
\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x,y_{i}),\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x,y_{i})-r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x,y^{\prime}))\rangle
, with gradients taken with respect to the RM classification head only. The relaxed penalty multiplies this inner product by the overconfidence proxy
\sigma(r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x,y_{i})-r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x,y^{\prime}))-\sigma(U(x,y_{i})-U(x,y^{\prime}))
, where
\sigma
is the logistic sigmoid (cf. Table
1
).

4.

The RM is updated by minimizing the BT cross-entropy loss against the soft oracle preference
\sigma(U(x,y^{*})-U(x,y^{\prime}))
, over the classification head parameters only, using a learning rate of
\eta_{\rm F}=5\times 10^{-5}
.

5.

The policy is updated by maximizing the log-likelihood of the winning response:
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}+\eta\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y^{*}\mid x)
, with
\eta_{\rm L}=2\times 10^{-5}
.

Both updates use the 8-bit paged AdamW optimizer
[
19
,
44
]
with gradients accumulated over four steps before each optimizer step.

Evaluation protocol.

After training, each model generates answers for all 817 prompts of the TruthfulQA generation split
[
43
]
using greedy decoding with
max_new_tokens
=
64
. Each pair of models is compared in a blind, side-by-side judgment by Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct served via the Together API at temperature
0
; the order of the two responses is randomly permuted per prompt to remove positional bias
[
72
,
78
]
. The exact prompt template provided to the judge is:

Respond in valid JSON format: {"reasoning": "...", "winner": "A"}

Appendix D

D.1
Proof for Section
2

For completeness, we formally establish that the infinitesimal perturbation introduced in Proposition
2.1
preserves the strong convexity required for the uniqueness of the optimal reward parameters.

Lemma D.1
.

Under the setting and assumptions of Proposition
2.1
, there exists an
\varepsilon_{\max}>0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{\max}]
, the perturbed loss
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\coloneqq\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})+\varepsilon\ell(x,y;\boldsymbol{\phi})
remains strongly convex. Consequently, the perturbed optimizer
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}_{\varepsilon,(x,y)}
exists and is strictly unique.

Proof.

By the assumptions of Proposition
2.1
, the unperturbed loss is strongly convex, meaning its Hessian satisfies
H_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\coloneqq\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})\succeq\mu I
for some
\mu>0
.

The Hessian of the perturbed loss is given by:

\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\phi})=H_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}+\varepsilon\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell(x,y;\boldsymbol{\phi}).

By Weyl’s inequality (see, e.g.,
35
), the minimum eigenvalue of the perturbed Hessian can be lower bounded as:

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\right)\;\geq\;\lambda_{\rm min}\left(H_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\right)+\varepsilon\,\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell(x,y;\boldsymbol{\phi})\right).

Since
\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell(x,y;\cdot)
is continuous over the compact space
\Phi
, there exists a constant

M\coloneqq\sup_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\big\|\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell(x,y;\boldsymbol{\phi})\big\|

such that
\|\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell(x,y;\boldsymbol{\phi})\|\leq M
, where
\|\,.\,\|
denotes any induced matrix norm. Using the bound
\lambda_{\rm min}(A)\geq-\|A\|
, we obtain

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\right)\;\geq\;\mu-\varepsilon M.

Setting
\varepsilon_{\max}=\mu/(2M)
, it follows that for all
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{\max}
, the perturbed Hessian satisfies
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\right)\geq\mu/2>0
. Therefore, the perturbed loss
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\phi})
is strongly convex, guaranteeing that the optimizer
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}_{\varepsilon,(x,y)}
exists and is unique.
∎

D.2
Proofs for Section
3

Lemma D.2

(Lemma
3.2
)
.

The Jacobian of the Follower’s best response map is:

\frac{d\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}{d\boldsymbol{\theta}}=-\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*})\right]^{-1}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}).

Proof.

Because
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\cdot)
is strongly convex, the Follower’s optimization admits a unique global minimum
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. This minimum strictly satisfies the first-order stationarity condition:

\Psi(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\boldsymbol{\theta})\coloneqq\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))=0

Under Assumption
3.1
,
\mathcal{L}
is jointly
C^{2}
, meaning
\Psi
is continuously differentiable. We apply the Implicit Function Theorem by differentiating this identity with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
:

\frac{d}{d\boldsymbol{\theta}}\Psi(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\phi}}\frac{d\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}{d\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}=0

Substituting
\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\phi}}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{2}\mathcal{L}
and
\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\mathcal{L}
, we obtain:

\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{2}\mathcal{L}\right)\frac{d\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}{d\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\mathcal{L}=0

The strong convexity of
\mathcal{L}
guarantees that the Hessian
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{2}\mathcal{L}
is strictly positive definite and, consequently, invertible. Solving the linear system for the Jacobian yields the result.
∎

Theorem D.3

(Theorem
3.3
)
.

The total gradient of the Leader’s objective
F(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is:

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}F(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}=\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))^{\top}}_{\text{Standard Policy Gradient}}-\underbrace{\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)^{\top}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\right]^{-1}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))}_{\text{Parameter-Steering Gradient}}.

Proof.

Applying the chain rule to the total objective
F(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))
:

\frac{dF}{d\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\phi}}\cdot\frac{d\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}{d\boldsymbol{\theta}}.

Note that
\frac{dF}{d\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is a row vector (Jacobian). Taking the transpose to match the gradient notation
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}F
(column vector) and substituting
\frac{d\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}{d\boldsymbol{\theta}}
from Lemma
3.2
yields the theorem.
∎

Theorem D.4

(Theorem
3.4
)
.

The sensitivity of the RM is the expected influence function weighted by the policy score function:

\frac{d\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}{d\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{\mathcal{X}},y\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot|x)}\left[\mathcal{I}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x,y)\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y|x)\right)^{\top}\right].

Proof.

The Follower’s objective is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{\mathcal{X}},y\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot|x)}[\ell(x,y;\boldsymbol{\phi})]+\Omega(\boldsymbol{\phi})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\ell(x,y;\boldsymbol{\phi})\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y|x)P_{\mathcal{X}}(x)\,dy\,dx+\Omega(\boldsymbol{\phi}).

We first compute the gradient w.r.t.
\boldsymbol{\phi}
. Since
\mathcal{Z}
is compact and functions are smooth, we differentiate under the integral sign:

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell(x,y;\boldsymbol{\phi})\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y|x)P_{\mathcal{X}}(x)\,dy\,dx+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\Omega(\boldsymbol{\phi}).

Next, we compute the mixed partial derivatives
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\mathcal{L}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\mathcal{L})
. Note that
\boldsymbol{\phi}
is held constant in this partial derivative. Therefore, the term
\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\Omega)
vanishes:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell(x,y;\boldsymbol{\phi})\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y|x)\right)^{\top}\,dy\,P_{\mathcal{X}}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell(x,y;\boldsymbol{\phi})\left(\frac{1}{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y|x)}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y|x)\right)^{\top}\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y|x)\,dy\,P_{\mathcal{X}}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{\mathcal{X}},y\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot|x)}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell(x,y;\boldsymbol{\phi})\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y|x)\right)^{\top}\right].

Recall the definition of the influence function
\mathcal{I}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x,y)=-\big[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\big]^{-1}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell(x,y;\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))
from Equation (
3
). Substituting this expression into Lemma
3.2
:

\displaystyle\frac{d\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}}{d\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=-\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\right]^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{\mathcal{X}},y\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot|x)}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell(x,y;\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y|x)\right)^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{\mathcal{X}},y\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot|x)}\left[\underbrace{-\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\right]^{-1}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell(x,y;\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))}_{=\mathcal{I}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x,y)}\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y|x)\right)^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{\mathcal{X}},y\sim\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot|x)}\left[\mathcal{I}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x,y)\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y|x)\right)^{\top}\right].

Proposition D.7

(Proposition
4.2
)
.

In the iterative RLHF setting, applying the TracIn method to measure a generated sample’s self-influence, defined as how training the RM on a sample
(x,y)
changes the proxy reward it assigns to that exact same sample, yields an estimator exactly proportional to our relaxed inner product:
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\rm FPO}(x,y)\coloneqq\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x,y),-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell(x,y;\boldsymbol{\phi})\rangle
.

Proof.

For clarity, we use
z
to denote
(x,y)
. In standard supervised learning, TracIn evaluates the influence of a training data point
z_{train}
on a test point
z_{test}
by approximating the change in the test loss after a single gradient descent step. Let
\ell_{train}
and
\ell_{test}
denote the respective loss functions. If the model updates its parameters via a gradient step
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell_{train}(z_{train};\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t})
, the first-order Taylor expansion yields the resulting change in the test loss:

\displaystyle\ell_{test}(z_{test};\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t+1})-\ell_{test}(z_{test};\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t})

\displaystyle\approx\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell_{test}(z_{test};\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}),\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\eta\big\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell_{test}(z_{test};\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}),\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell_{train}(z_{train};\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t})\big\rangle

To rigorously map this to our iterative RLHF setting, we must formalize the objectives of the two players:

1.

The training point (
z_{train}=z
): The Follower trains on the Leader’s generated action
z
. Its training objective is the pointwise prediction error:
\ell_{train}(z;\boldsymbol{\phi})=\ell(z;\boldsymbol{\phi})
.

2.

The test point (
z_{test}=z
): The Leader evaluates the RM on that exact same action
z
to maximize the proxy reward
r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)
. To frame reward maximization as a test loss to be minimized, we define the Leader’s objective as
\ell_{test}(z;\boldsymbol{\phi})=-r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)
. Note that, without loss of generality, we can omit the KL term, as it does not depend on
\boldsymbol{\phi}
and we are only interested in gradients w.r.t.
\boldsymbol{\phi}
.

Because the generated action
z
serves simultaneously as the Follower’s training data and the Leader’s test evaluation point, applying TracIn measures the system’s self-influence. Substituting our specific Stackelberg objectives into the TracIn formulation yields:

\displaystyle\text{TracIn}(z,z)

\displaystyle=\big\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell_{test}(z;\boldsymbol{\phi}),\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell_{train}(z;\boldsymbol{\phi})\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\left(-r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)\right),\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell(z;\boldsymbol{\phi})\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z),-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\ell(z;\boldsymbol{\phi})\big\rangle

This rigorously recovers the inner product
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\rm FPO}(z)
. While the strict Taylor expansion produces a scaling factor of
\eta
, our FPO mechanism scales the overall steering penalty by a tunable hyperparameter

(Equation (

\eta
is absorbed into
\gamma
during hyperparameter tuning, allowing us to drop it from the final objective without loss of generality.
∎

Proposition D.10

(ii)

(i) Collinearity. At each iteration, the Leader maximizes

\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\left[h_{\sigma}(\epsilon)\right]=\nabla_{y}r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}).

Therefore:

\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})=\nabla_{y}r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{\top}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\beta\,\mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left[r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\frac{\beta}{2}\|\mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|^{2}\right].\qed

In the experiments, we set
y=\mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
as the
deterministic action, so the policy’s objective reduces to

J(y)=r_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(y)-\frac{\beta}{2}\|y\|^{2}
.
