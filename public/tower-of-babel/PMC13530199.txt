Role of HIV-1 in IL-8 response of human stellate cells to gram-positive microbial products
Authors: Rabab O. Ali, Lumin Zhang, Naichaya Chamroonkul, Parissa Tabrizian, Myron Schwartz, Ganesh Gunasekaran, Thomas Schiano, Maria Isabel Fiel, Steven C. Ward, Meena B. Bansal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patients living with human immunodeficiency virus 1 (PLWH) develop accelerated liver fibrosis, but the exact mechanism remains unknown. Activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs)—a central driver of hepatic fibrogenesis—is influenced by various factors, including viral infection, hepatocellular injury, chronic immune activation, gut barrier dysfunction, and microbial translocation. The role of gram-positive microbial products in human immunodeficiency virus 1 (HIV-1) infection–associated liver inflammation and fibrosis remains poorly understood. This study investigates the effect of lipoteichoic acid (LTA), a major gram-positive bacterial component, on HSCs in the context of HIV-1 infection. Human primary HSCs (pHSCs) were isolated from liver tissues of HIV-1–infected and uninfected individuals undergoing hepatic resection. Inflammatory responses of HSCs to LTA stimulation were measured via ELISA in patient-derived HSCs, and in vitro Lx2 cells (a human HSC line) before and after HIV-1BaL exposure. Western blotting, ChIP-qPCR and RNA-seq were used on Lx2 cells to explore relevant gene associations. LTA modestly induced interleukin-8/CXCL8 (IL-8) production in HSCs, but this response was heightened in both patient-derived HIV-1 exposed pHSCs and in vitro HIV-1BaL exposed Lx2 cells. IL-8 mRNA levels were higher in HIV-1–infected tissues compared to uninfected controls. LTA exposure increased IL-8 mRNA in LX2 cells, and IL-8 treatment of Lx2 cells increased α-SMA and COL1A1 expression. Transcriptomic analysis on Lx2 cells co-exposed to LTA and HIV-1BaL suggested a link between histone acetylation and IL-8. HIV-1 exposure in patient-derived pHSCs and Lx2 cells was linked to increased IL-8 response of HSCs to LTA. In vitro IL-8 treatment of Lx2 cells supports a role of IL-8 in pro-fibrogenic signaling in HSCs. These findings link gut-derived gram-positive microbial products and IL-8 activation in HSCs, providing important insights to guide future mechanistic studies and potential therapeutic targets for managing HIV-associated liver disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56186-1.

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus 1 (HIV-1) infection is marked by profound depletion of CD4⁺ T cells, leading to systemic immunosuppression. As a result, patients living with HIV-1 (PLWH) are more susceptible to opportunistic and community-acquired bacterial infections. For instance, the risk of Streptococcus pneumoniae infection is 100 times higher in HIV-1–infected individuals than in the general population1. Early during infection, the loss of gut-associated CD4⁺ T cells compromises intestinal barrier integrity, facilitating the translocation of microbial products into systemic circulation2. Notably, studies have reported that serum markers of microbial translocation3,4 and gut barrier disruption5 may persist despite antiretroviral therapy (ART) and suggested microbial translocation as a driver of sustained immune activation in PLWH6.

HIV-induced damage to the gastrointestinal mucosa leads to the translocation of both gram-positive and gram-negative microbial products from the gut lumen into the liver via the portal vein7. While gram-negative products typically translocate more efficiently following mucosal disruption8, gram-positive bacteria also enter the circulation frequently9, either through the same routes or secondary infections. Although extensive research has explored the effects of gram-negative components like lipopolysaccharide (LPS) on immune activation, liver injury, and fibrogenesis in HIV10,11, the influence of gram-positive microbial products remains comparatively understudied. Lipoteichoic acid (LTA), a major component of the gram-positive bacterial cell walls, functions as a pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP) capable of modulating host immunity12–14. Similar to LPS, LTA triggers CD14-dependent nitric oxide release in vascular smooth muscle cells, macrophages, and mononuclear cells15. Elevated serum LTA levels have been detected in PLWH, correlating with heightened autoantibody production and immune activation16. Given the liver’s frontline exposure to gut-derived microbial products via the portal vein, the impact of LTA—particularly on hepatic stellate cells (HSCs)—in HIV-associated chronic liver disease remains insufficiently explored.

End-stage liver disease continues to be a major cause of mortality in HIV-positive patients despite successful ART, highlighting ongoing hepatic dysfunction and progressive fibrosis17. Numerous studies have shown a positive correlation between circulating HIV RNA levels and advanced fibrosis in HIV mono-infected patients18–20. Additionally, co-infection with hepatitis C virus (HCV) accelerates fibrosis progression threefold compared to HCV mono-infection21. While cross-talk between HSCs and liver macrophages is critical for HSC activation22, HSCs are the primary mediators of hepatic fibrogenesis. Our prior work23,24 revealed that HIV-1 infection enhances the sensitivity of liver macrophages to gram-negative microbial stimuli such as LPS, promoting elevated production of pro-inflammatory cytokines including interleukin-8 (IL-8/CXCL8). However, whether these cytokines directly stimulate HSC activation remains unclear.

In this study, we performed in vitro experiments to examine the inflammatory responses of primary human-derived HSCs (pHSCs)—isolated from liver tissues of both HIV-1–infected and uninfected individuals —upon LTA stimulation. This was followed by experiments on in vitro HSC cell lines (Lx2 cells) under experimental HIV-1BaL exposure conditions. Our findings suggest that HIV-1 exposure, both on patient-derived pHSCs and in vitro Lx2 cells, amplifies IL-8 production by HSCs in response to LTA. Furthermore, in vitro studies on IL-8 treated Lx2 cells supports a link between IL-8 and pro-fibrogenic signaling in HSCs. Materials and methods

Ethics statement

De-identified liver resection specimens from HIV-1–positive individuals (n = 7) with effectively suppressed viral loads on ART were provided by Drs. Schwartz, Ganeskaran, and Fiel. Control liver tissues (n = 10) were obtained from the Mount Sinai Biobank and included samples from patients undergoing liver resection for colon cancer metastases (n = 9) or cholangiocarcinoma (n = 1). All tissue samples were collected during liver transplantation or tumor resection procedures and were processed as formalin-fixed, frozen, or fresh in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM). The study was approved by the Mount Sinai Institutional Review Board (GCO #06-0523 and #10-1211). Patients included in this study provided written consent from 21/08/2018-20/07/2019 to have their data used for research. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Liver tissue samples from both HIV-1–infected and uninfected individuals were used for IL-8 immunohistochemistry (IHC). Blood samples were collected from HIV-positive participants enrolled in a metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) study (GCO#17-02410). Isolation of primary human hepatic stellate cells

Primary HSCs (pHSCs) were isolated from surgical liver tissue specimens using collagenase perfusion and tissue mincing. Cells were washed with PBS (4×) and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 minutes. Following resuspension in DMEM, cells underwent density gradient centrifugation with 25% and 50% Percoll (Pharmacia, Sweden) as described previously27. pHSCs were enriched by adherence to tissue culture plates and cultured for two weeks. Purity was confirmed via α-SMA immunostaining.

HIV-1BaL exposure in primary human hepatic stellate cells

Purified HIV-1BaL (NIH AIDS Reagent program, Bethesda, MD, USA) was generated by infecting primary human monocyte-derived macrophages (from donor buffy coats). Virus was harvested from culture supernatants, concentrated using a 20% sucrose cushion, and titrated by serial dilution in ghost-CXCR4+-CCR5+ cells (NIH AIDS Reagent program, Bethesda, MD, USA). To assess IL-8 responses to HIV-1BaL and/or lipoteichoic acid (LTA) stimulation, Lx2 cells or primary human hepatic stellate cells were seeded in 12-well plates and stimulated at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1 for 6hrs. Then, the cells were washed three times with PBS to remove unbound virus and subsequently cultured in fresh medium.

Tissue immunohistochemistry

Liver tissue sections (prepared in duplicate) were rehydrated, processed for antigen retrieval, and incubated overnight at 4°C with anti–human IL-8 antibody (NOVUS, Cat. NBP2-33819, 1:200 dilution) in a humidified chamber. IL-8 staining was detected using the ImmPress™ Duet Double Staining Polymer Kit (Cat. MP-7724). Four random fields per section were imaged at 100× magnification using Zen software with an LSM 880 laser-scanning microscope. IL-8 signal intensity was quantified using Fiji ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD).

Histone extraction and acetylation ELISA

pHSCs and Lx2 cells (1 × 10⁶) were seeded in 10 cm dishes and exposed to HIV-1BaL virus (NIH AIDS Reagent program, Bethesda, MD) for 48 hours. Total histones (200 ng) were extracted using the EpiQuick™ Total Histone Extraction Kit (Cat. OP-0006) and quantified for acetylation using a histone acetylation ELISA Kit (Cat. P-4022-96) per manufacturer protocols.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using RNeasy spin columns (Qiagen, Cat. 74136) and treated with RNase-free DNase (Qiagen, Chatsworth, CA). Reverse transcription was performed using the Clontech RNA-to-cDNA EcoDry Kit. RT-qPCR was conducted on a Roche LightCycler 480 II using SYBR Green PCR master mix (Bio-Rad, Cat. 1708884) with primers at 300 nM (primer sequences in Table 1). Reactions were run in triplicate. Cycle thresholds (CTs) were normalized to α-actin.

IL-8 ELISA

HSCs were treated under various conditions for 24 hours. Supernatants were collected at designated time points, and IL-8 levels were measured using the Human IL-8 ELISA Kit (Affymetrix, Cat. 88-8086-88).

CHIP RT-PCR assay

pHSCs (1 × 10⁶) were exposed to HIV-1BaL virus (NIH AIDS Reagent program, Bethesda, MD) for 48 hours. Chromatin was prepared using the EZ-Zyme™ Chromatin Prep Kit (Cat. 17-375), and ChIP assays were performed with Magna ChIP™ G Kits (Cat. 17409). IL-8 promoter activation was quantified using SYBR Green RT-PCR. CHIP-seq data was analyzed with Bowtie228, Macs callpeak29, Samtools30, Bedtools31, and deepTools32. H4K5 peaks were visualized using IGV.

RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA from Lx2 cells treated with medium, HIV-1BaL (MOI = 0.1), and/or LTA (1 μg/mL) was sequenced (n = 3 per group). RNA quality was assessed by a Nano Drop 2000 Bioanalyzer, and RNA-Seq was performed by Mount Sinai NGS Core. Reads were aligned with STAR33, quantified with HTSeq34, and analyzed with DESeq235 using hg19 annotation. Pathway analysis used Cytoscape with ClueGO 14 and R packages: clusterProfiler36, enrichplot37, and Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA)38.

Western blot analysis

Proteins from liver tissues were extracted with T-PER Reagent (Thermo, Cat. 78510). Equal protein amounts were loaded onto NuPAGE 4–12% Bis-Tris gels, transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were washed with blotting buffer (1× 0.1% Tween20 PBS) and blocked for 60 minutes in blotting buffer containing 10% low-fat powdered milk. Membranes were washed 3 times with blotting buffer. Blots were probed with primary antibodies (1:1000) overnight at 4°C and secondary antibodies (1:1000) for 1 hour at room temperature. Target protein signals were developed with TMB (SeraCare Life Sciences Inc., Milford, MA) and visualized using the Amersham Imager 600. Antibodies included α-SMA (NOVUS, Cat. NB300-978), Collagen I (Rockland Immuno- chemicals, Cat. 009-001-103), H4K5 (NOVUS,NB21-2024), and GAPDH (Santa Cruz, Cat. sc-66163). Membranes were cut prior to antibody incubation according to target molecular weights. Some full-length membrane images were not retained during the initial data acquisition. All available original blot images and experimental replicates are provided in the Supplementary Information. Densitometry was performed using ImageJ and normalized to β-actin.

Statistical analysis

Data are reported as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 5.0 (La Jolla, CA). Two-group comparisons used Student’s t test or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, as appropriate. Multi-group comparisons used one-way ANOVA with Kruskal–Wallis test. Co-localization in IHC was quantified using Fiji ImageJ and Pearson’s r. Unadjusted P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant; P = 0.05–0.09 was considered a trend. Non-significant differences are noted as P ≥ 0.1 unless stated otherwise.

Results

HIV-1BaL exposure amplifies IL-8 response of pHSCs to LTA stimulation

To examine how lipoteichoic acid (LTA) affects primary human hepatic stellate cells (pHSCs), three independent patient-derived pHSC lines from HIV-1 uninfected donor liver tissues (control pHSCs) were treated with LTA. Secretion of IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, and TNF-α was assessed before and after 48-hour exposure to HIV-1BaL. LTA treatment alone did not significantly induce IL-6, TNF-α, or IL-10 secretion in either condition (Supplemental Figure. 1). To validate these findings, patient-derived pHSCs isolated from HIV-1–infected livers (HIV+ pHSCs) were stimulated with LTA. Two out of three HIV+ pHSC showed significantly increased IL-8 secretion with LTA (Fig. 1B). Similarly, in vitro Lx2 cells (an experimental human HSC line) co-stimulated with LTA and HIV-1BaL displayed synergistic IL-8 induction (Fig. 1C). Lastly, the C-C chemokine receptor type 5 (CCR5) antagonist Maraviroc reduced p24 levels in Lx2 cells (Fig. 1D), and attenuated IL-8 production in response to LTA (Fig. 1E).

Discussion

End-stage liver disease remains a major cause of mortality among PLWH, with multiple studies showing a positive correlation between HIV viremia and advanced fibrosis in HIV mono-infection17–20. Studies have suggested how microbial translocation and gut barrier disruption may persist despite ART3–6, but the specific mechanisms involved in the liver’s response to gram-positive microbial products is not fully elucidated. Our in vitro study on hepatic stellate cells exposed to HIV-1 and LTA suggest a pathological role of LTA during chronic HIV-1 infection.

Elevated serum IL-8 levels (≥46.5 pg/mL) have been independently associated with liver-related mortality and the need for liver transplantation42,43. Although IL-8 is elevated in mono-infection44, its pathological role in HIV-associated liver fibrosis remains uncertain. Some studies have found no correlation between IL-8 levels and fibrosis in HIV/HCV co-infection, while others suggest elevated IL-8 predicts a greater risk of fibrosis progression45,46. In our HIV-1 mono-infected cohort, IL-8 levels were significantly higher in viremic individuals with advanced fibrosis compared to non-viremic counterparts. Interestingly, this distinction was present only in individuals with advanced fibrosis. Immunohistochemistry of patient-derived HIV-1–infected liver tissues demonstrated increased hepatic IL-8 expression, as well as increased expression of fibrogenic markers i.e., α-SMA and collagen I in the presence of HIV-1 infection. We are unable to confirm whether this enhanced expression of IL-8, TGF-β, and α-SMA is a direct or indirect effect of HIV infection on liver fibrosis. Additionally, three in vitro Lx2 cell lines co-exposed to HIV-1BaL and LTA showed an inverse association between mRNA expression of IL-8 and α-SMA gene ACTA2 (Supplementary Figure 5). This suggests that the relationship between α-SMA and HIV exposure may be independent of IL-8. However, given the correlative nature of the RNA-seq analysis, this discrepancy warrants further validation in future studies.

Previous studies have identified IL-8 as a marker of inflammatory response to liver injury in HSCs pretreated with TNF-α47,48. Furthermore, LTA has been shown to promote IL-8 production through NF-κB activation in HSCs pre-primed with TNF-α and IL-1β49,50. In our study, LTA alone was sufficient to stimulate IL-8 secretion in the absence of such priming, and this effect was significantly enhanced by HIV-1 exposure in both patient-derived HIV+ pHSCs and in vitro HIV-1BaL–exposed HSC cell lines. There was no difference in TNF-α or IL-1β production, and RNA-seq analysis did not show a significant association between IL-8 expression and genes in the canonical NF-κB pathway following HIV-1 and LTA co-exposure (Supplementary Fig. 5). As these findings are associative, future mechanistic studies are warranted to explore alternative pathways involved in HSCs response to microbial products.

Transcriptomic analysis on in vitro HSCs suggests a link between IL-8 expression and aminoacylation. Previous research has shown that IL-8 promoter acetylation contributes to its post-translational regulation51 and that HDAC inhibitors can suppress IL-8–driven inflammation in various disease contexts52. In our in vitro experiments, treatment of both patient-derived HIV+ pHSCs and HIV-1BaL–exposed Lx2 cells with HDAC inhibitor TSA increased IL-8 responses to LTA. Through ELISA, ChIP-seq, and ChIP-qPCR, we found enrichment of H4K5 acetylation at the IL-8 promoter in HIV-1BaL–exposed Lx2 cells. This modification has been shown to promote chromatin remodeling and recruitment of critical binding sites in IL-8 promoter i.e., transcription factors AP-1, NF-IL6, and NF-κB53. Although H4K5 acetylation enrichment at the IL-8 promoter is consistent with transcriptional activation, it does not establish the specific HDAC regulators involved. Similarly, TSA is a broad HDAC inhibitor and does not identify the specific HDAC(s) involved. Thus, these associative findings require future mechanistic studies for validation as well as identification of specific HDAC regulators that may be involved in IL-8 activation.

There are a few notable limitations in our study including an inability to distinguish HIV-1 exposure from productive infection. Additionally, commercial LTA can be contaminated with LPS or other PAMPs that drive IL-8 via TLR4. Lastly, results of RNA-seq are mostly correlative and require future experimental studies to validate the role of histone acetylation in HSCs responses to microbial stimuli in HIV-associated liver disease. These correlative findings, however, do align with prior studies in cystic fibrosis54–57 and hepatitis B virus infection58, where histone hyperacetylation was shown to drive IL-8 expression in epithelial or hepatocellular cells.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that HIV-1 exposure amplifies the IL-8 response of patient-derived pHSCs to a gram-positive microbial product called LTA. In vitro experiments on HSC cell lines further suggests a role of IL-8 in pro-fibrogenic signaling upon LTA exposure. Lastly, correlative analyses of gene expression suggests an association between histone acetylation and IL-8 activation in HIV-1BaL–exposed in vitro HSC cell lines, a concept that requires future mechanistic studies for validation. Overall, our findings link gut-derived gram-positive microbial products and IL-8 activation in HSCs, providing important insights to guide future mechanistic studies and potential therapeutic targets for managing HIV-associated liver disease.
