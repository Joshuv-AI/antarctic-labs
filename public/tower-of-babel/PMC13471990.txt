Selective Electrochemical NOx Reduction to N2 for Sustainable Ammonia Energy Systems
Authors: Zeliang Wu, Yixin Chen, Zuxin Wen, Xianbiao Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAmmonia‐based energy systems offer a pathway toward carbon‐free energy, yet NOx emissions from ammonia combustion hinder the realization of a sustainable closed‐loop. Electrochemical NOx reduction reaction (eNOxRR) provides a promising route to convert NOx to benign N2 under mild conditions. However, under the low NOx concentrations typical of practical exhaust streams, N2 selectivity is limited by competing hydrogenation, hydrogen evolution, and inefficient intermediate utilization. Here, we present a mechanistic perspective on selective NOx‐to‐N2 conversion, highlighting that eNOxRR proceeds through *NO‐mediated pathways with N2O as a key intermediate, while low surface coverage restricts N–N coupling. We propose that integrating tandem catalysis with interfacial microenvironment regulation offers a unified strategy to enhance N2 selectivity. Finally, we outline key challenges for practical implementation under gas‐phase and dilute conditions, providing design principles for efficient electrochemical denitrification. This framework has the potential to enable scalable NOx removal and support a closed‐loop ammonia energy system.Keywords: ammonia combustion exhaust, ammonia energy systems, electrochemical denitrification, electrochemical NOx reduction, electrocatalytic NOx removal, N–N coupling, NOx‐to‐N2 conversion, tandem catalysis

Introduction

Ammonia (NH3) has emerged as a promising hydrogen carrier and carbon‐free fuel in the global clean energy transition due to its high hydrogen density (17.6 wt.%), moderate liquefaction conditions (−33°C at 1 bar), carbon‐free composition, and well‐established global transport infrastructure [1, 2, 3]. Despite these advantages, the high auto‐ignition temperature (651°C) and low laminar flame velocity of NH3 hinder stable and efficient combustion in conventional engines and burners [4, 5]. Partial cracking of NH3 to produce a blended fuel of H2, N2, and NH3 is a widely adopted strategy, as the presence of H2 substantially increases flame speed and improves combustion stability [6, 7, 8]. Nevertheless, a major concern persists: the formation of nitrogen oxides (NOx, e.g., NO, NO2, N2O) during NH3 combustion, which are hazardous air pollutants contributing to acid rain, smog formation, and serious health effects [9, 10, 11]. In particular, N2O is a long‐lived greenhouse gas with a global warming potential nearly 300 times that of CO2, posing climate risks even at trace levels [12]. To enable environmentally responsible deployment of a sustainable NH3 energy system, post‐combustion NOx must be removed efficiently and selectively.

Currently, industrial NOx removal is primarily achieved through selective catalytic reduction (SCR), where NOx is converted to N2 over catalysts using externally supplied NH3 or urea as the reducing agent and hydrogen source, typically at temperatures of 300–500°C [13, 14]. While effective, such systems require external reductant dosing, elevated thermal input, and large reactor volumes, which may limit their applicability in compact or decentralized NH3 energy platforms [15, 16]. In contrast, the electrochemical NOx reduction reaction (eNOxRR) can directly utilize renewable electricity, operate under mild conditions, use water (H2O) as the green hydrogen source, and eliminate the need for continuous reductant injection, enabling compact and modular device architectures (Scheme 1) [9, 10, 17]. Despite the rapidly growing interest in eNOxRR, current research has largely centered on NOx‐to‐NH3 conversion, driven by efforts to develop alternative NH3 synthesis pathways. A proposed concept is tandem plasma‐electrocatalytic NH3 synthesis, in which non‐thermal plasma first converts N2 into NOx, followed by electrochemical reduction of these intermediates to NH3. However, the overall process remains fundamentally constrained by the high energy demand of plasma activation [3]. More importantly, this paradigm is not aligned with the requirements of NOx mitigation, where the objective is the complete removal of NOx rather than its conversion into another regulated pollutant such as NH3 [18]. In practical NH3 combustion exhaust, NOx is present at low concentrations (typically 100–1000 ppm) and must be removed efficiently under mild conditions, making the selective formation of benign N2 the only viable target [19]. However, achieving electrochemical conversion of NOx‐to‐N2 is intrinsically more challenging than NOx‐to‐NH3. While NOx reduction generally proceeds via stepwise protonation of nitrogen intermediates, favoring NH3 formation, N2 production instead requires coupling between partially reduced species (e.g., *NO, *N, or *NHx). This pathway is highly sensitive to the surface coverage of reactive intermediates and becomes increasingly unfavorable under dilute NOx conditions, where the probability of N–N coupling is intrinsically limited [9, 20]. As such, the selectivity of NOx electroreduction is governed by a fundamental competition between protonation and N–N coupling pathways. As a result, despite its clear environmental and system‐level relevance, selective electrochemical NOx‐to‐N2 conversion remains relatively underexplored. Addressing this gap requires a mechanistic understanding of reaction intermediates and pathways, which can guide the rational design of catalysts, interfacial microenvironments, and reactor architectures to promote N–N coupling and enable efficient and selective N2 formation.

Ammonia utilization and NOx mitigation pathways. NH3 can be transported and stored as an energy carrier and subsequently utilized for power generation or as fuel in transportation and marine engines. During NH3 combustion, nitrogen oxides (NOx) may be formed as by‐products. These emissions can be mitigated through conventional selective catalytic reduction (SCR) or emerging electrochemical reduction technologies, converting NOx into benign N2.

In this perspective, we develop a mechanistically informed view of electrochemical NOx reduction, focusing on pathways that enable selective N2 formation under dilute conditions relevant to practical exhaust streams. We first explore the elementary reaction network centred on the *NO intermediate and identify the key branching points that govern the competition between protonation‐driven pathways and N–N coupling processes, with N2O emerging as a critical intermediate in the multistep reduction sequence. Building on this framework, we examine how catalyst surface properties and interfacial microenvironments, including NOx coverage, electrolyte pH, and the identity of solvated ions, regulate intermediate stabilization, proton transfer kinetics, and coupling probability, thereby shaping product selectivity. These insights provide a basis for designing catalytic systems that favor N–N coupling over excessive hydrogenation, including tandem catalytic architectures that decouple NOx‐to‐N2O and N2O‐to‐N2 steps, as well as reactor configurations that enhance NOx availability under low‐concentration conditions. Finally, we discuss the challenges associated with translating these principles into practical electrochemical systems operating under realistic combustion exhaust conditions, and outline opportunities for integrating mechanistic understanding with materials and system design to enable efficient NOx removal in NH3‐based energy technologies.

Mechanistic Origins of Selectivity in eNOxRR

Electrochemical NOx reduction is generally considered to proceed through multiple pathways initiated by NOx adsorption and activation on the catalyst surface. Notably, NO typically constitutes more than 95% of NOx in combustion exhaust; its transformation pathways dominate the overall reaction pathway (Figure 1A) [19]. Among the minor NOx species, N2O follows a comparatively straightforward electroreduction pathway, as N2 is the only thermodynamically and experimentally observed nitrogen‐containing product [9, 12]. In contrast, the electrochemical reduction of NO2 is more complex due to its inherent instability in aqueous environments. NO2 readily undergoes disproportionation to form nitrate (NO3 −) and nitrite (NO2 −), both of which are subsequently reduced to the same key adsorbed intermediate, *NO [21, 22]. This convergence highlights the central role of *NO in governing downstream reaction pathways and product selectivity. It is important to recognize that the electrochemical reduction of NO3 − and NO2 − to N2 shares fundamental mechanistic features with eNOxRR process. These systems have been extensively investigated and consistently identify *NO as the key adsorbed intermediate governing downstream reactivity [23, 24]. This common intermediate establishes a mechanistic continuity between liquid‐phase nitrate and nitrite reduction and gas‐phase NOx electroreduction, as both routes proceed through a shared set of surface species, including *NO, *N, and *N2O [20, 25]. Consequently, insights from NO3 − and NO2 − reduction, particularly regarding intermediate stabilization, surface coverage effects, and N–N coupling pathways, provide a valuable foundation for understanding and controlling selectivity toward N2 formation. Nevertheless, this perspective focuses on gas‐phase NOx electroreduction in membrane electrode assembly (MEA) configurations, where NO dominates under practical exhaust conditions.

The adsorption configuration and initial protonation pathway of *NO on the catalyst surface together serve as a critical determinant of product selectivity. Depending on whether *NO binds through its oxygen or nitrogen atom, distinct reaction pathways are triggered (Figure 1A) [26]. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations suggest that O–terminal bound *NO can follow a stepwise protonation sequence (*ON→*ONH→*ONH2), and further protonation at distinct sites yields single‑N products such as NH3 or NH2OH [20, 26]. In contrast, N–terminal bound *NO has been proposed to follow more diverse reaction pathways. Notably, the initial protonation of adsorbed *NO is strongly catalyst dependent. For example, protonation preferentially occurs at the oxygen atom of *NO on Pd(100), where the reaction barrier for *NOH formation is 0.48 eV lower than that for *ONH formation. In contrast, Cu(100) favors protonation at the nitrogen atom, with the reaction barrier for *NOH formation being 0.29 eV higher than that for *ONH formation (Figure 1B) [27]. DFT studies generally propose that N‐terminal bound *NO undergoes successive hydrogenation and deoxygenation to generate the key intermediate *N, although direct experimental evidence for adsorbed *N remains limited [20]. The surface coverage of *N plays a critical role in determining product selectivity. At low *N coverage, N–N coupling is unfavorable and over‑hydrogenation to NH3 dominates. At high *N coverage, N–N coupling becomes more probable, yet direct formation of the N≡N triple bond in N2 remains thermodynamically disfavored (bond energy ∼941 kJ mol−1) [28]. A more plausible pathway may involve hetero‑coupling between *N and *NO to form adsorbed *N2O, followed by facile two‑electron deoxygenation to N2 [20]. While this pathway is possible, N–N bond formation is more widely considered to proceed through the dimerization of adsorbed *NO with NO molecules from the solution [29]. In this mechanism, adsorbed *NO reacts with NO to form a surface‐bound NO dimer (*NONO), which is subsequently protonated and rearranged to yield *N2O before final reduction to N2. Multiple spectroscopic and surface‐science studies have confirmed the presence of NO dimers on metal surfaces, establishing this mechanism as the most widely accepted route for N2O formation in NOx electroreduction [20, 30, 31, 32]. In contrast, *N–*NO heterocoupling to *N2O is mechanistically plausible and supported by theoretical reaction networks and indirect product trends, but direct operando evidence for this elementary step remains limited.

Following N2O formation, its subsequent electroreduction to N2 proceeds through a comparatively simpler reaction pathway. Upon adsorption on the catalyst surface, *N2O undergoes two successive proton‐coupled electron transfer steps to form the key intermediate *N2OH, followed by hydrogenation‐assisted N–O bond cleavage to produce N2. Despite this relatively simple reaction sequence, N2O interacts only weakly with most transition‐metal surfaces. DFT calculations show that the adsorption free energy of *N2O (ΔG(*N2O)) is higher than ΔG(*H) on most transition metals, with only the most H‐phobic metals, such as Zn, Pb, and Bi, satisfying ΔG(*N2O) < ΔG(*H) (Figure 1C) [33]. These results suggest that N2O electroreduction is readily outcompeted by the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) on most transition‐metal catalysts, highlighting HER as the principal competing pathway during the N2O‐to‐N2 step.

To avoid confusion between beneficial hydrogenation and competing side reactions, it is useful to distinguish the two sequential stages of the NOx‐to‐N2 pathway (Figure 2A). In the NO‐to‐N2O step, limited proton/electron transfer may participate in the formation or rearrangement of coupling‐capable intermediates, but excessive hydrogenation of *NO‐ or N‐containing species competes with N–N coupling and shifts selectivity toward single‐N products such as NH3 and NH2OH. Therefore, this stage requires balancing hydrogenation with sufficiently high NO‐derived intermediate coverage to favor N–N coupling. By contrast, in the N2O‐to‐N2 step, hydrogenation‐assisted N–O bond cleavage is an essential part of N2 formation, while the primary competing process is HER rather than over‐hydrogenation to other N‐containing products. Thus, selective N2 formation requires not the complete suppression of hydrogenation, but the preferential use of interfacial *H or proton/electron transfer for N–O bond cleavage while minimizing HER and excessive hydrogenation of NO‐derived intermediates.

Given the strong dependence of pathway preference and product distribution on catalyst identity and interfacial microenvironment, direct mechanistic evidence under operating conditions is essential. Identifying active sites and elucidating catalytic mechanisms in the eNOxRR process requires a multiscale characterization framework spanning the gas phase, catalyst surface structure, catalyst‐electrolyte interface, and electrolyte environment (Figure 2B) [34, 35]. At the macroscopic level, analysis of gaseous and liquid products using techniques such as gas chromatography‐mass spectrometry (GC‐MS), ion chromatography (IC), nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), and online differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) enables quantitative evaluation of catalytic activity and selectivity while revealing the sequence of product formation during NOx reduction. For instance, on Pd and Cu catalysts, the onset potential for N2O formation is significantly more positive than that for N2, supporting a sequential pathway in which NO is first reduced to N2O and subsequently converted to N2 [9]. At the catalyst level, structural characterization is essential because many electrocatalysts undergo significant reconstruction under electrochemical conditions, which can fundamentally alter the nature of the active sites [36]. Operando techniques such as X‐ray diffraction (XRD) and X‐ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) enable direct tracking of catalyst phase evolution and coordination environments during reaction, thereby linking dynamic structural changes to catalytic performance and N–N bond formation. Complementary ex situ characterization further probes catalyst stability and compositional changes. X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) before and after reaction reveals surface chemical‐state evolution, while inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis of the electrolyte quantifies metal dissolution [37].

More fundamentally, the catalyst‐electrolyte interface represents the central region where elementary reaction steps occur. In situ spectroscopic techniques can directly probe adsorbed intermediates and their binding configurations on catalyst surfaces. Spectroscopic identification of key intermediates such as *NO, together with real‐time product analysis, allows the evolution pathways of surface species to be resolved. For instance, bridge‐adsorbed *NO on catalyst surfaces tends to facilitate N–O bond cleavage compared with top adsorption, thereby suppressing NO–NO coupling [38]. In another study, several distinct adsorption configurations of *NO on Pt were directly monitored by operando attenuated total reflection surface‐enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (ATR–SEIRAS). When combined with DEMS, these results revealed that different *NO adsorption configurations govern distinct reduction pathways, thereby dictating reaction selectivity and product distribution [39]. Specifically, N2O is the sole product at potentials more positive than the hydrogen region and originates from the reduction of weakly adsorbed NO species, whereas the formation of NH3 and NH2OH occurs exclusively within the hydrogen region and is attributed to the reaction between strongly adsorbed NO and adsorbed hydrogen.

Tandem Catalytic Pathways Toward N2 Formation

The mechanistic analysis above suggests that N2 formation in eNOxRR proceeds via N2O as a key intermediate, rather than via direct formation of the N≡N bond. On most transition metal catalysts, N2O is formed with high selectivity, while its subsequent reduction to N2 remains kinetically and energetically limited. This intrinsic mismatch between the facile formation of N2O and the sluggish conversion of N2O to N2 represents a fundamental bottleneck in achieving high N2 selectivity. However, other key device‐level metrics, including inlet NOx concentration, single‐pass conversion, energy efficiency, and operational stability, remain unavailable in many reported studies. Future work should report these parameters systematically to enable more rigorous benchmarking and to better assess the practical viability of eNOxRR systems. In this context, tandem catalytic pathways offer a rational strategy to decouple these elementary steps. Instead of relying on a single catalyst to simultaneously promote N–N coupling and N–O bond cleavage, tandem systems enable the sequential optimization of NOx‐to‐N2O and N2O‐to‐N2 transformations on distinct active sites. Such a pathway effectively reshapes the reaction network, suppressing competing protonation routes and channeling intermediates toward N2 formation.

Experimental studies across a range of transition metal catalysts consistently support this mechanistic picture. Among the dual‐N products, N2O is preferentially formed over N2 during NO reduction on most metals. For example, on Pd, the Faradaic efficiency for N2O can reach up to 95% at approximately 0.7 V versus RHE, with negligible N2 formation, and even at more negative potentials, N2O formation remains dominant across Pd, Ag, Pt, Ni, Co, and Fe (Figure 3A) [9, 43]. This persistent selectivity toward N2O reflects a key limitation in the subsequent reduction of N2O to N2, even though N–N coupling can occur to form N2O under these conditions. In particular, the consistently weak stabilization of *N2OH on transition metal surfaces renders the protonation of *N2O unfavorable relative to its desorption, thereby limiting its further conversion to N2. Notably, the nearly identical activity trends for N2 and N2O production across different metal catalysts indicate that these products do not arise from independent pathways (Figure 3B). Instead, this strong correlation suggests a mechanistic coupling in which N2 is predominantly formed via the further reduction of N2O, rather than through a parallel direct route. This observation provides direct experimental support for a sequential mechanism in which N2O serves as a key intermediate along the pathway to N2 formation [9]. However, the reduction of *N2O is highly sensitive to the adsorption strength of oxygenated intermediates. Strong *O binding leads to surface poisoning and suppresses N2O conversion, whereas overly weak *O binding renders N–O bond cleavage energetically unfavorable [20]. This trade‐off highlights an intrinsic catalytic limitation that prevents most single catalysts from achieving high N2 selectivity.

These mechanistic constraints directly motivate the use of tandem catalytic pathways. By decoupling the NO‐to‐N2O and N2O‐to‐N2 steps onto distinct catalytic sites, it becomes possible to independently optimize N–N coupling and N–O bond cleavage, thereby overcoming the limitations imposed by scaling relationships on single catalysts. Experimental observations further support this design strategy. While Pd, Pt, and Fe exhibit high selectivity toward N2O formation in eNORR, Cu shows a markedly different behavior, producing predominantly NH3 over a wide potential range and exhibiting limited dual‐N product formation (Figure 3C) [9, 40]. In contrast, Cu displays significantly higher activity and selectivity for N2 production in N2O reduction compared with Pd and Pt (Figure 3D). These contrasting catalytic behaviors underscore the importance of step‐specific catalyst selection in tandem systems. Accordingly, catalysts that promote N–N coupling, such as Pd, Fe, and Pt, are desirable for the initial NO‐to‐N2O step, whereas catalysts that facilitate N–O bond cleavage and suppress competing hydrogen evolution, including Cu, Co, and Ag, are more suitable for the subsequent N2O‐to‐N2 conversion.

Given that different catalysts exhibit distinct advantages for the NOx‐to‐N2O and N2O‐to‐N2 steps, integrating these complementary catalytic functions becomes essential for achieving efficient overall NOx reduction. Such integration can be realized through multiscale tandem strategies, spanning reactor‐level, catalyst‐level, and active‐site‐level designs (Figure 4). Notably, tandem catalytic strategies have been successfully demonstrated in electrochemical CO2 reduction, where they enable selective formation of multi‐carbon products [44, 45]. At the reactor level, a tandem configuration in which NOx is first converted to N2O in an upstream unit and subsequently reduced to N2 in a downstream unit provides a conceptually straightforward strategy to decouple N–N coupling from N–O bond cleavage (Figure 4A). However, the overall efficiency of such systems is critically governed by single‐pass conversion and intermediate purity. Incomplete NOx‐to‐N2O conversion or the presence of residual NO in the downstream feed can lead to parasitic reactions, as NO is preferentially reduced over N2O on many catalysts due to its lower reduction overpotential, resulting in undesired NH3 formation [9]. This also suggests that the impact of co‐fed NO on the selectivity of N2O reduction to N2 remains an important yet underexplored question. For example, on catalysts such as Cu and Co, which exhibit high activity toward NO reduction, residual NO may dominate surface reactions and suppress N2O conversion, whereas more selective catalysts for N2O reduction, such as Ag, may offer improved tolerance to mixed feeds [9]. This highlights the necessity of achieving high N2O yield and controlled intermediate delivery, or alternatively introducing separation and enrichment steps, albeit at the expense of system complexity.

(C) Active site‐level tandem mechanism, in which spatially or electronically differentiated surface sites within a single catalyst facilitate sequential NO activation, N–N coupling, and N–O bond cleavage, enabling efficient N2 formation through cooperative site interactions.

At the catalyst level, a tandem catalytic architecture can be constructed by integrating distinct catalyst layers on a single electrode within a single reactor, thereby enabling sequential NO‐to‐N2O and N2O‐to‐N2 transformations across different catalytic layers (Figure 4B). In the CO2 electroreduction, the tandem catalysts have been introduced to enhance the selectivity for multi‐carbon products by first generating CO as an intermediate [46, 47, 48, 49, 50, 51]. In eNOxRR, this design principle can be applied, whereby one catalyst promotes NOx‐to‐N2O conversion via N–N coupling, whereas a second catalyst facilitates the subsequent reduction of N2O to N2 while suppressing competing hydrogen evolution (Figure 4B). The effectiveness of such catalyst‐level tandem systems is, however, not solely dictated by intrinsic catalytic activity but critically governed by intermediate transport and reaction competition between sequential steps. In layered architectures, N2O generated on the NO‐to‐N2O catalytic layer must desorb, diffuse, and re‐adsorb onto adjacent N2O‐to‐N2 catalytic sites, rendering the overall process sensitive to mass transport limitations and local concentration gradients. In contrast, physically mixed catalysts can shorten diffusion pathways but introduce competitive adsorption, where NO preferentially occupies active sites and suppresses N2O conversion. Consequently, the spatial arrangement of catalyst components, whether stratified or intermixed, plays a decisive role in determining overall selectivity. Under these constraints, effective tandem catalysis requires not only appropriate catalyst selection but also kinetic matching between N2O generation and consumption rates. Catalysts with high activity toward NO reduction, such as Cu and Co, tend to favor NH3 formation and can suppress N2O utilization when NO is present, and therefore should be employed with caution. In contrast, Ag‐based catalysts, which exhibit more balanced selectivity for both NO‐to‐N2O and N2O‐to‐N2 steps, provide more suitable platforms for tandem integration. Achieving efficient N2 formation thus requires coordinated control over catalyst composition, spatial arrangement, and intermediate flux across coupled reaction steps. An additional consideration for tandem catalyst design is the compatibility of the operating potential windows. Current experimental studies indicate that, for most reported transition‐metal catalysts, NO‐to‐N2O conversion is favored at relatively positive potentials (typically > −0.17 V vs. RHE), whereas efficient N2O‐to‐N2 conversion generally requires more negative potentials (typically < 0 V vs. RHE) [9]. The limited overlap between these optimal operating windows significantly constrains catalyst selection for catalyst‐level tandem systems. Unlike reactor‐level tandem systems, where the two reactions can be independently operated at their respective optimal potentials, catalyst‐level tandem systems require both reactions to proceed within a common electrochemical environment. Consequently, catalyst combinations exhibiting high individual activity may not necessarily perform efficiently when integrated. For example, a Ni catalyst favoring NO‐to‐N2O conversion ( > −0.05 V vs. RHE) and a Fe catalyst favoring N2O‐to‐N2 conversion ( < −0.32 V vs. RHE) would be difficult to operate efficiently within the same electrochemical reactor [9]. Therefore, catalyst selection should consider not only catalytic activity and selectivity, but also the compatibility of their operating potential windows and potential‐dependent behaviors. Given this limitation, pulsed electrolysis may offer a promising strategy to alleviate this mismatch by dynamically alternating between potential regimes optimized for NO‐to‐N2O formation and N2O‐to‐N2 reduction.

At the active‐site level, tandem catalysis can be further advanced by introducing distinct yet cooperative active sites within a single catalyst, such that sequential NO‐to‐N2O and N2O‐to‐N2 transformations occur in close spatial proximity (Figure 4C). Compared with reactor‐level and catalyst‐level tandem designs, this configuration may enhance the local utilization of molecular N2O by shortening the desorption‐diffusion–re‐adsorption distance between neighboring sites, thereby reducing intermediate loss and improving the probability of sequential N2O‐to‐N2 conversion. This proximity effect is particularly important under dilute NOx conditions, where low surface coverage limits the probability of sequential reactions. In this context, the central design challenge lies in constructing catalysts in which different active sites perform complementary functions without introducing detrimental competition. One class of sites should favor NO adsorption and N–N coupling to generate N2O, while adjacent sites should preferentially activate N2O and facilitate N–O bond cleavage while suppressing competing hydrogen evolution reaction (HER). The effectiveness of such systems therefore depends on both the intrinsic reactivity of each site and the efficiency of intermediate transfer between them. If N2O formation and consumption are not kinetically matched, intermediates may either desorb prematurely or be outcompeted by parallel NO reduction pathways.

Alloy catalysts provide a promising platform for realizing such site‐level tandem architectures, owing to their multi‐element composition and tunable surface configurations. The associated modulation of electronic structure, geometric strain, and chemical bifunctionality may strengthen the interaction between the catalyst surface and NOx species, lower kinetic overpotentials, and optimize interfacial charge transfer. Recently, CuAg alloy catalysts have been demonstrated to effectively promote N2O adsorption, hydrogenation, and N–O bond cleavage, leading to significantly enhanced single‐pass conversion of N2O and improved N2 selectivity [42]. However, challenges similar to those discussed above may still arise. Competitive adsorption between NO and N2O can cause NO to be preferentially reduced on Cu sites to NH3 rather than forming N2O, thereby disrupting the desired sequential tandem pathway. One possible strategy is to introduce additional sites with higher intrinsic eNORR activity, such as Pd or Pt, alongside Cu‐ or Ag‐like sites to construct multicomponent systems such as high‐entropy alloys (HEAs). However, multicomponent catalysts do not automatically guarantee tandem selectivity. If NO is preferentially reduced on Cu‐rich sites, the pathway may shift toward deeper hydrogenation to NH3 rather than N–N coupling to N2O. Therefore, effective HEA design should simultaneously control NO activation/N–N coupling sites and N2O reduction/N–O cleavage sites. Experimental studies have shown that Pd and Pt achieve the same eNORR current density at substantially more positive potentials than Cu and Ag, suggesting that Pd‐/Pt‐like sites may preferentially initiate NO reduction and promote N2O formation [9]. In this scenario, neighboring Cu‐, Ag‐, or Co‐like sites could further facilitate N2O activation and hydrogenation‐assisted N–O cleavage to N2 while suppressing HER. Nevertheless, the N2O‐to‐N2 step should not be regarded as insensitive to site identity. Although N2O reduction involves fewer nitrogen‐containing products than NO reduction, its Faradaic efficiency toward N2 remains strongly governed by *N2O/*O/*OH binding and intrinsic HER activity. Successful tandem catalysis further requires efficient transfer of the generated N2O between neighboring active sites. Considering the generally weak adsorption of N2O on transition‐metal catalysts, *N2O transfer is more likely to occur through desorption and re‐adsorption of molecular N2O rather than direct migration of strongly adsorbed intermediates, although the exact transfer mechanism remains to be experimentally verified [52]. Thus, HEA‐based tandem catalysis requires precise control over site identity, spatial proximity, adsorption energetics, and operating potential compatibility. More broadly, this design can be viewed as a local flux‐matching problem, in which the rates of N2O generation, transfer, and consumption must be balanced within a single catalytic framework. While multicomponent catalysts such as HEAs also offer potential advantages in structural stability, site differentiation, and tunable adsorption properties, with the potential to break conventional scaling relationships due to lattice distortion, electronic effects, and sluggish diffusion, their application in eNOxRR remains largely unexplored [53, 54, 55]. Developing such catalysts will require precise control over site distribution, adsorption energetics, compatibility of the operating potential windows, and interfacial reaction environments to ensure that cooperative tandem reactivity is achieved rather than masked by competing parallel pathways.

Microenvironmental Regulation of Selectivity in eNOxRR

Beyond catalyst design, the interfacial microenvironment plays a decisive role in governing eNOxRR selectivity. A central parameter is the relative surface coverage of *NO and *H, which dictates the competition between N–N coupling and protonation pathways. Under aqueous conditions, co‐adsorption of *H is unavoidable, and the balance between *NO and *H coverage determines whether nitrogen intermediates undergo coupling to form N2O/N2 or sequential hydrogenation to NH3 and NH2OH [9, 26]. For example, on Pd, high NO coverage favors N–N coupling, leading to N2O and N2 as dominant products, whereas at low NO coverage, increased *H coverage promotes NH3 formation (Figure 5A,B) [9]. This trend is general across catalysts with different intrinsic selectivity, highlighting surface coverage as a universal descriptor for reaction pathway selection. In practical systems, NOx coverage can be modulated through mass transport and operating conditions, including reactant partial pressure, electrode configuration, and applied potential. Gas‐diffusion electrodes, for instance, can enhance local NO concentration and promote N–N coupling, whereas planar electrodes under diffusion‐limited conditions tend to favor hydrogenation pathways due to lower NO availability [26]. Similarly, for the subsequent N2O‐to‐N2 step, gas‐diffusion electrodes with optimized gas‐fed flow channels can enhance N2O mass transport, whereas engineering the electronic structure and geometric strain of catalysts strengthens N2O adsorption and activation, thereby facilitating hydrogenation‐assisted N–O bond cleavage and promoting N2O conversion to N2 [12, 42]. These effects underscore that controlling local reactant flux is as critical as catalyst design in steering product selectivity.

Variations in NOx coverage regulate the adsorption configuration of reaction intermediates and the probability of N–N coupling, thereby influencing the formation of N2O, N2, and NH3. (C) and (D) effect of electrolyte pH on eNOxRR. Variations in pH alter proton availability and the interfacial reaction environment, thereby influencing hydrogenation steps and N–N coupling. (E) and (F) Cation and anion effect on eNOxRR. Different ionic species alter the interfacial electric field, solvation structure, and stabilization of reaction intermediates, thereby regulating reaction kinetics and product distribution.

The electrolyte pH provides a second key lever by regulating proton availability at the interface. Lower pH increases *H coverage and facilitates proton‐coupled reduction steps, thereby favoring NH3 formation and hydrogen evolution (Figure 5C). In contrast, alkaline conditions suppress excessive protonation and promote N–N coupling pathways, leading to enhanced selectivity toward N2O and N2 (Figure 5D) [9, 39]. Importantly, under high‐current operation, the local pH at the electrode surface can deviate significantly from the bulk value due to proton consumption or OH− generation, making interfacial pH a dynamic parameter that must be considered in catalyst and reactor design [9]. Recent eNOxRR modeling and in situ studies increasingly identify local pH as a critical design parameter rather than a fixed background condition [52].

Beyond coverage and pH, electrolyte composition further modulates the interfacial microenvironment through cation‐ and anion‐specific effects [56, 57]. Systematic studies of cation and anion effects in eNOxRR remain scarce, but insights from other electrocatalytic systems, particularly electrocatalytic nitrate reduction and CO2 electroreduction, provide valuable mechanistic insights (Figure 5E,F). Cations with different hydration energies regulate the interfacial microenvironment by modifying the local electric field, interfacial water structure, and proton‐transfer kinetics, thereby altering interfacial water dissociation [58]. The *H generated from water dissociation subsequently governs hydrogenation kinetics and product selectivity. Strongly hydrated cations, such as Li+, Na+, and Mg2+, possess tightly bound hydration shells with high desolvation energies, whereas weakly hydrated cations, such as K+, Cs+, and tetraethylammonium, exhibit more labile hydration structures and can approach the electrode more closely, compressing the Stern layer and generating a stronger local electric field, thereby modulating interfacial water dissociation and the generation of *H (Figure 5E) [58]. For the NOx‐to‐N2O step, the key is to balance the surface coverage of *H and NOx‐derived intermediates. Excessive *H may promote undesired hydrogenation toward NH3 while simultaneously accelerating HER, whereas insufficient *H may limit hydrogenation despite increasing the probability of N–N coupling. By contrast, the subsequent N2O‐to‐N2 step has different mechanistic requirements, where N2O adsorption and hydrogenation‐assisted N–O bond cleavage become the key processes, and HER is the only major competing reaction. Catalysts with intrinsically low HER activity, such as Cu and Ag, have demonstrated efficient N2O reduction [12, 42]. Regulating interfacial water dissociation through solvated cations may further improve the preferential utilization of *H for N2O reduction, although this strategy remains to be systematically explored. Anions can also influence reaction pathways through competitive adsorption or indirect effects on local chemistry (Figure 5F). For example, Cl− was found to decrease the activity of nitrate reduction by competitively adsorbing on the catalyst surface, whereas anodically generated free chlorine can subsequently oxidize cathodically produced NH3 to N2. The enhanced N2 formation originates from a subsequent NH3 oxidation process rather than direct promotion of the N2O‐to‐N2 step [59, 60]. Additionally, carbonate and phosphate anions compete for adsorption sites and buffer the interfacial pH. This buffering strongly affects hydrogenation steps from *NO to *NH, thereby promoting high NH3 selectivity [61]. By analogy, in eNOxRR, it is important to consider how different cations and anions modulate hydrogen adsorption and local surface pH. Taken together, microenvironmental regulation can be viewed as a complementary strategy to tandem catalyst design, providing dynamic control over intermediate coverage, proton activity, and interfacial structure. By tuning these parameters in concert, it becomes possible to bias the reaction network toward N–N coupling and efficient N2 formation, particularly under the dilute and transport‐limited conditions relevant to practical NOx removal.

Challenges and Opportunities for Practical eNOxRR Systems

While recent advances have elucidated key mechanistic factors governing eNOxRR in liquid electrolytes, translating these insights into practical electrochemical systems remains a central challenge. In particular, it remains unclear whether catalyst descriptors and reaction conditions identified in aqueous environments remain valid in solid‐state electrolytes and membrane‐electrode assemblies (MEAs). In liquid systems, parameters such as pH, cation hydration, and anion identity govern interfacial reactivity, whereas in MEA configurations, these effects must be regulated through ionomer chemistry, membrane transport properties, and interfacial humidity. Identifying catalyst descriptors and operating conditions that are robust across electrolyte environments is, therefore, essential for bridging fundamental understanding and device implementation.

Beyond catalyst translation, practical deployment of eNOxRR also requires reactor configurations compatible with realistic NH3 combustion exhaust conditions. For example, the exhaust temperature of NH3‐fueled gas turbines typically exceeds 500°C, whereas current electrochemical NOx reduction devices based on low‐temperature MEA architectures generally operate below ∼80–100°C [62]. This temperature mismatch requires substantial cooling of the flue gas prior to electrochemical treatment, resulting in significant thermal losses. Developing eNOxRR systems that operate at intermediate temperatures ( < 300°C) offers a promising pathway toward practical denitrification (Figure 6). In such systems, heat exchange can be used to recover sensible heat from flue gas and sustain stack operation, followed by electrochemical NOx reduction under controlled conditions.

Flowchart of the electrochemical NOx removal process in simulated industrial scenarios. High‐temperature exhaust gas is first cooled to an intermediate temperature through a heat exchanger, while the recovered heat is used to preheat the NOx electrolyzer. The NOx‐containing stream is then fed into a gas diffusion electrode system, where tandem catalytic sites combined with surface modification enrich NOx at the catalyst interface, enabling efficient conversion of NOx to N2.

Under these conditions, efficient NOx utilization becomes a key challenge. As discussed above, sluggish N–N coupling under dilute NOx concentrations necessitates strategies to enhance local reactant availability. Gas diffusion electrodes integrated with gas‐fed flow configurations can significantly improve NOx mass transport and interfacial contact, thereby promoting effective conversion [42]. However, the relatively inert nature of N2O introduces an additional limitation, as its weak interaction with catalyst surfaces hinders adsorption and activation [12, 63]. Therefore, beyond enhancing mass transport, surface modification with high‐temperature‐stable, high‐surface‐area materials (e.g., covalent organic framework (COF) [64] or zeolite [65]) may increase the surface coverage of N2O, thereby facilitating efficient tandem conversion. Ultimately, the realization of practical eNOxRR systems requires the co‐optimization of catalyst stability, electrolyte compatibility, and reactor design under intermediate‐temperature conditions. In addition, scaling from laboratory configurations to industrial systems introduces further challenges, particularly in mass transport and heat management, which must be addressed to enable efficient and scalable NOx‐to‐N2 conversion.

Practical NH3 combustion exhaust presents additional engineering challenges beyond catalyst and reactor design. Unlike idealized NOx/N2 feed streams, real exhaust contains dilute NOx together with O2, H2O vapor, and trace impurities under dynamically varying operating conditions. O2 may compete for cathodic electrons through oxygen reduction and may also alter the local NO/NO2/N2O balance, thereby influencing the availability of key NO‐derived intermediates. Water vapor must also be carefully managed: sufficient humidity is required to maintain membrane conductivity and interfacial proton/water transport, whereas excessive water can induce electrode flooding and gas‐transport limitations in GDE‐based systems. In addition, impurities such as SOx, particulates, and alkaline or metal‐containing species may poison catalytic sites, degrade ionomers or membranes, and block gas‐diffusion pathways. Fluctuating NOx concentrations further complicate reaction control by dynamically changing *NO coverage and the probability of N–N coupling, which may shift product selectivity between N2O/N2 formation and competing hydrogenation pathways. Moreover, membrane durability, gas crossover, product separation, and the energy penalty associated with flue‐gas cooling, conditioning, and electrochemical operation should be considered when evaluating practical systems. Therefore, future studies should benchmark catalyst and device performance under realistic exhaust compositions and transient operating conditions, while reporting comprehensive metrics including full‐cell voltage, single‐pass conversion, nitrogen balance, product distribution, and long‐term stability.

Conclusion and Future Perspectives

This Perspective identifies selective NOxto‐–N2 conversion as a central challenge for closing the NH3 energy cycle, where low NOx concentrations, competing hydrogenation pathways, and inefficient intermediate utilization collectively limit performance. Mechanistically, eNOxRR proceeds through *NO‐mediated pathways with N2O as a key intermediate, but the low surface coverage of reactive species under practical conditions renders N–N coupling intrinsically inefficient, necessitating strategies that simultaneously enhance intermediate generation, retention, and consumption. Addressing these limitations requires coordinated design across multiple length scales. At the catalyst level, materials must be engineered to decouple N–N coupling from N–O bond cleavage, enabling efficient tandem conversion while suppressing the competing HER. At the interfacial level, controlling *NO and *H coverage through microenvironmental regulation is critical to bias reaction pathways toward N2 formation. At the reactor level, maximizing NOx utilization through improved mass transport and intermediate management, including gas‐fed configurations and local enrichment strategies, is essential under dilute feed conditions. Importantly, this Perspective highlights the need to shift research focus from NOx‐to‐NH3 pathways toward selective NOx‐to‐N2 conversion, particularly under gas‐phase conditions relevant to practical exhaust streams.

Looking forward, several priorities emerge. First, catalyst design should focus on enabling site differentiation, breaking scaling relationships, and improving the compatibility of the operating potential windows to achieve simultaneous optimization of *NO, *N2O, and *O binding, while enabling efficient sequential NO‐to‐N2O and N2O‐to‐N2 conversion. Second, systematic studies under realistic operating conditions, including low NOx concentrations and gas‐phase feeds, are needed to establish reliable activity and selectivity descriptors. Third, the development of electrochemical systems operating at intermediate temperatures will be critical to minimize thermal penalties associated with flue gas treatment. Fourth, operando characterization techniques capable of resolving intermediate dynamics and local reaction environments are essential to validate proposed mechanisms and guide rational design. In particular, future mechanistic studies should clarify both the relative kinetics of hydrogenation‐assisted N–O bond cleavage and competing hydrogenation pathways, and the mechanism of intermediate transfer between neighboring active sites, including whether tandem catalysis proceeds through desorption–re‐adsorption of molecular N2O or migration of surface‐bound intermediates, thereby providing a stronger mechanistic foundation for the rational design of tandem catalysts. For meaningful techno‐economic comparison, future studies should report normalized energy consumption, for example, per mole of NOx removed or per volume of treated exhaust gas, together with full‐cell voltage, Faradaic efficiency toward N2, single‐pass NOx conversion, gas flow rate, and long‐term stability. These metrics should be evaluated under realistic NH3 combustion exhaust conditions and benchmarked against established SCR technology, while also considering the additional energy penalty associated with flue‐gas cooling, conditioning, and balance‐of‐plant operation. Advancing selective NOx‐to‐N2 conversion in gas‐phase electrochemical systems will be essential for translating eNOxRR into practical denitrification technologies. Progress along these directions will enable the transition of eNOxRR from fundamental studies toward practical implementation, providing a viable pathway for efficient NOx removal and contributing to a closed‐loop and sustainable NH3 energy system.
