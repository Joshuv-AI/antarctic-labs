It is time to get out your 1980 calendar and block
out the weekend of May 30th to June 1st. These are the
dates of the grand and glorious 35th Reunion of the dis-
tinguished Class of 1945 !

A highly motivated Reunion Committee is hard at work
laying out plans for the best ever get-together of our
Class. Our Headquarters will again be at the Little Red
Schoolhouse; Archie Messenger is developing a scheme to
prod us into some special athletic activities but plans
are also being made to assure plenty of time to renew
old bonds and to have a joyful, springtime return to
Amherst.

The most important thing now is that you commit to
be there. If you have been an infrequent reuner in prior
years, it is even more important that you commit to come
back this time. The key to a successful Reunion is that
we all be there together.

We're working to hold the costs of the Reunion to as
low a level as possible and will have a firm figure on
this shortly. In the meantime, we do need some working
capital and would appreciate an advance registration fee
of $25.00. The balance will be collected later.

Please fill out the enclosed reply form and return
it in the enclosed envelope letting us know of your plans.

I look forward to hearing from you soon and to seeing
you on May 30th.

Cordially yours,

Charlie Gadsden

Our lives have been busy indeed these last months. Ever
since the Iranians took our hostages in November, I've been
about as busy as is possible. This has been as difficult a
crisis as I have ever seen our Government confront. The
failure of our rescue effort last month was a great disappoint-
ment to us all. We will keep on trying one way or another.

On top of that, in February my mother passed on peacefully
and quietly. This meant a lot of work for Pat and me in closing
out her and Father's house and selling it. That is behind us
now, and we are in fact happily settled in a new home of our
own. After I retired from the Navy a year and one-half ago,
we had to give up living in Navy houses. We've bought a lovely
townhouse on the bank overlooking the Potomac on the Virginia
side of the river. Pat has had a wonderful time decorating it
and getting settled in. The Navy treated us well with lovely
homes for seven years, but Patricia is happier to have her own.

We're going back to Amherst in a few weeks for my 35th
and from there for a day or two vacation with the Chairman of
the Board, George Shinn, at his summer home in Vermont. In
addition. Bill Webster and Bill Liedtke will be joining us.

It is such a treat to have Bill Webster here in the FBI position.
We work together a great deal. Our friendship, which goes back
to 1941, has let us build a closer rapport between our two
agencies than has ever existed before. Both of our staffs know
that if they don't get along with each other they're going to be
in trouble when the Directors are such good friends, see each
other socially, and compete with each other regularly on the
tennis court!

SUBJECT: Lectures Delivered at Pace University

1. I traveled to Pace University in Pleasantville, NY
on 7-8 April 1980 to deliver two lectures. The first was
entitled "The Brezhnev Years: A Preliminary Appraisal,"

and was given to Pace's John H. Buchsbaum Historical Society.
It was open to the general public, and approximately 90
people attended. The second was a talk about the CIA and
the job of a political analyst delivered to a class of
approximately 30 students, who are taking a course entitled
"History of U.S. Intelligence."

2. This was the third time that I have been invited

to lecture at Pace, where I did my undergraduate work. In
1977, after returning from a summer in the Soviet Union, I
gave a talk on my experiences there. The following year I
delivered a lecture on the Soviet and American reactions to
the Hungarian Revolution and the Suez Crisis of 1956, which
will ke published in the Journal of Contemporary Historv
next spring. ~ £ -

3. Prior publicity of my visit to Pace and the an-
nouncement that Admiral Turner will be the commencement
speaker at Pace's graduation this June generated a protest
letter from six faculty members that was published in the
university newspaper the week before my visit. Despite this,
and my public identification as a CIA employee, my reception
was friendly-- even enthusiastic. There was not the slightest
hint of hostility from any of the questioners.

5. The students in the class on "The History of U.S.
Intelligence," displayed a keen understanding of the job we
do at CIA and a sympathy for the difficulties we encounter.

The course, which is one of the few of its kind in the country
is taught by Dr. John Norman, a former analyst with OSS and
INR. Many of the questions concerned the procedures and pit-
falls of intelligence analysis. One or two questioners,
inevitably, asked me to explain alleged CIA intelligence
"failures," specifically Iran and Afghanistan. I replied
that on the former there was a failure to foresee the in-
tensity of the Islamic revolution but that there are not
many around, including the mullahs, who accurately predicted
the course of events. On Afghanistan, I contended that we
were right on top of the situation. And while not being able
to predict the exact date of the Soviet invasion, we gave
US policy-makers ample warning. To a question about which
US intelligence agency produced the highest quality intel-
ligence, I responded that, being a totally objective observer,
I would have to say CIA. Doctor Norman, despite his loyalty
to INR, concurred.

6. Doctor Norman also expressed an interest in bringing
other CIA speakers to address his class in the future. I
suggested that it may be possible to arrange a trip to the
headquarters building for future classes.
