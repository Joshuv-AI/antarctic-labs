Agentic GraphRAG: Navigating Unstructured Financial Data with Collaborative AI
Arthur Capozzi, Dirk Helbing

Abstract
We present a collaborative agentic GraphRAG framework for expert analysis of commercial registry data. Public registries are often formally accessible, yet difficult to use in practice because they combine structured records with large volumes of unstructured legal text. This limits conventional keyword and vector-only retrieval, especially for multi-hop, temporal, and entity-centric investigations. Our approach builds a Neo4j knowledge graph through a three-phase pipeline: (i) deterministic ingestion of strong nodes from verified structured fields, (ii) LLM-based extraction of weak nodes from unstructured notices, and (iii) deterministic identity resolution and deduplication. On top of this graph, we introduce an analytical modular agent that integrates zero-shot intent routing, a bounded reflection loop, secure tool-mediated graph access, and state-aware response synthesis. A human-in-the-loop dashboard exposes evidence and execution traces to support transparency and auditability. We evaluate the framework on the Swiss Official Gazette of Commerce, a multilingual corpus of more than seven million publications over seven years. We further contribute a multi-tier evaluation protocol covering entity-resolution precision, tool-routing behavior, answer quality, and multi-turn conversational performance. Across automated, human-curated, and conversational benchmarks, the proposed agentic GraphRAG system consistently outperforms a standard agentic vector-RAG baseline, with strong gains in correctness, answer relevance, information recall, turn success rate, and context carryover accuracy. The architecture is modular, reproducible, and transferable to other commercial gazettes and public-sector registry systems.

Introduction

Recent advances in large language models have made agentic AI systems increasingly capable of performing complex retrieval and reasoning tasks
[
36
,
17
]
. At the same time, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as a practical strategy for grounding these systems in external knowledge sources. However, conventional vector-based RAG architectures remain limited when the target domain contains highly structured relations, temporal dependencies, and entity-resolution challenges. In such settings, graph-based retrieval offers a more suitable foundation, as it enables multi-hop traversal over explicit relationships while still supporting natural-language interaction
[
50
,
30
]
.

In this paper, we present a novel agentic AI architecture built on top of a GraphRAG framework and designed for expert analysis over commercial registry data.
We apply and evaluate this system on the Swiss Official Gazette of Commerce (SHAB).
This dataset spans seven years and comprises more than seven million legal and technical documents. Important facts are often scattered across many notices, expressed in the three main official languages, and distributed between metadata fields and free-text legal descriptions.
Consequently, although Switzerland is widely recognized for the transparency and accessibility of its public records
[
4
]
, this openness conceals a practical paradox: while free access exists in principle, effective analytical access remains limited in practice.

The architecture presented in this study addresses this issue by providing a comprehensive end-to-end pipeline. First, we create a Neo4j knowledge graph from the SHAB records. This process involves combining a deterministic ingestion of structured registry fields with a LLM-based extraction of latent actors from unstructured event text.
Second, we develop an analytical agent that operates on this graph via a controlled agentic workflow involving intent routing, limited tool usage, and state-aware response synthesis. The resulting system functions as a domain expert, answering natural-language questions, exploring corporate networks, tracing event histories, and performing higher-level analytical queries over the registry.

Although an autonomous AI agent can quickly synthesize information, analytical and exploratory applications in the financial context require high levels of trust, transparency, and traceability
[
19
,
37
]
. To bridge the gap between the LLM’s automated reasoning and the deterministic evidence stored in the knowledge graph, the proposed pipeline includes a human-in-the-loop exploratory dashboard. This interface enables users to inspect entity profiles, visualize graph neighborhoods, navigate event histories, and verify the structured evidence supporting the agent’s responses. Thus, the platform treats the LLM as an interactive component within an analytical environment, ensuring that machine reasoning remains auditable and grounded in the underlying graph.

The proposed architecture is intentionally generalizable beyond the specific Swiss setting. The same ingestion and retrieval design can be applied to other official gazettes of commerce or public registry systems, especially ones where legal and corporate information is scattered among structured records and unstructured publications.
This task may be simpler in other countries, since the Swiss corpus is more complex due to the coexistence of multiple official languages and the highly decentralized nature of its cantonal registries. This introduces significant regional variations in legal terminology and document formatting.
For this reason, the contribution of this paper is not limited to a single, country-specific application, but rather extends to a broader class of public-sector and regulatory data environments.
Furthermore, because certain regulatory and corporate environments demand strict data privacy, the architecture’s modular design integrates a dedicated privacy-preserving abstraction layer. This ensures that the framework can be safely deployed, even when handling highly sensitive or confidential information.

A second contribution of this work concerns evaluation. Although agentic RAG systems are increasingly common in both academia and industry, their assessment remains fragmented and often informal, with few exceptions in the literature
[
47
,
22
]
. To address this gap, we introduce a multi-tiered evaluation framework that measures graph integrity, tool behavior, answer quality, factual correctness, contextual completeness, and multi-turn conversational robustness. During the evaluation process, the proposed agentic GraphRAG system is evaluated against a standard, simpler agentic vector-RAG baseline, and consistently achieves substantially stronger performance across the main retrieval and answer-quality benchmarks. In this sense, this study introduces both a new expert system architecture and a reproducible methodology for evaluating agentic GraphRAG systems in realistic financial exploration settings.

Finally, to ensure full scientific transparency and reproducibility, all code used for data collection, graph construction, agent implementation, evaluation, and the exploratory dashboard is freely available.

Literature review

Querying financial and corporate records is notoriously complex. Companies frequently change their names or ownership, and sudden structural changes can disrupt the data. The truly valuable information is often buried under an exceptionally low signal-to-noise ratio.
In addition, complex economic relationships are often concealed within large amounts of unstructured text and numerical data that lack obvious causal links.
Traditionally, analyzing these complex financial records relies heavily on manual processing and deep domain knowledge, which makes extracting multi-hop features time-consuming and error-prone
[
41
]
.

Researchers have long used expert systems to navigate the complexity of financial datasets. These systems have expanded far beyond basic forecasting to include critical areas such as corporate registries, fraud detection, and bankruptcy prediction.
For example,
27

Similarly,
26
demonstrated the effectiveness of algorithmic expert systems for evaluating financial health and predicting corporate insolvency.

Because financial decisions require high accountability, explainability is a crucial aspect of bankruptcy prediction. For this reason, several studies focus on developing models that offer clear explanations of their reasoning.
For example,
8
proposed a genetic algorithm that generates clear, feature-weighted counterfactual explanations, making bankruptcy prediction models more interpretable and trustworthy.
Building on this approach,
31

2.1
Retrieval-augmented generation

Retrieval-augmented generation is an architecture that significantly enhances the capabilities of large language models. In fact, unlike traditional models, RAG systems do not rely solely on internal training memory. First, they search an external database for documents relevant to a user’s prompt. Then, they retrieve the most pertinent information and feed it to the language model. This enables the system to generate highly accurate, contextually grounded responses. This approach mitigates the tendency of artificial intelligence to hallucinate facts and enables the system to synthesize specialized, up-to-date knowledge
[
29
]
.

There are several notable examples of RAG systems applications in financial technology (fintech).
To assist private investors with financial report analysis,
25
showed that a tailored RAG system with high-quality components could significantly improve the accuracy and relevance of the answers, especially for qualitative queries on well-structured documents.
Other specialized RAG frameworks combine multi-modal preprocessing, multi-path retrieval, and domain-specific re-ranking to overcome the challenges of extracting information from complex, multi-modal financial documents
[
42
]
.
The unification of tabular and textual data processing is another area of focus, with efforts underway to overcome the limitation of flattening tables into plain text by maintaining structural awareness
[
11
]
.
Further RAG approaches retrieve financial information from third-party sources, such as news platforms or social media, to improve the accuracy of sentiment analysis on financial reports
[
45
]
.

Despite their power, standard RAG systems face significant challenges when processing financial databases. One major weakness is that traditional RAG relies on semantic similarity, which often yields generic, boilerplate risk factors rather than salient, firm-specific details.
Additionally, standard vector-based RAG often destroys the inherent structure of financial documents. For instance, it treats metadata, text, and tables as distinct entities, severing the essential logical links between numerical data and their accompanying textual explanations.
Another limitation of traditional RAG systems is their inability to answer global questions spanning an entire text corpus or large subsets of it. For example, they cannot reliably answer comprehensive aggregation queries, such as “What are the top ten cryptocurrency companies sorted by share capital in city X?”

2.2
Knowledge graphs

A knowledge graph is a structured representation of information that organizes entities as nodes and their relationships as edges. This type of semantic network allows machines to understand context, grasp complex hierarchies, and perform sophisticated, multi-hop reasoning across large datasets. For this reason, knowledge graphs are an ideal format for mapping the intricate, interconnected nature of financial markets and corporate structures.

Several studies have investigated the creation of knowledge graphs from unstructured financial data.

20

Financial knowledge graphs have also been used in combination with event embeddings learned from financial news, to support quantitative investments
[
7

.

Beyond basic construction, researchers use graph-based methods to tackle other complex financial tasks. For example, these techniques are used to detect fraudulent transactions
[

39

38

or predict stock revenues
[

and new business creation
[

. Other recent studies show that graph attention networks can model new economic connections derived from financial documents to analyze market momentum spillovers
[
10
]
.

2.3
GraphRAG

GraphRAG is an extension of standard retrieval architectures which replaces traditional, flat document databases with structured knowledge graphs.
GraphRAG systems generally follow a three-step process
[
48
]
.
First, during the construction phase, an AI model processes a collection of text documents, identifying key concepts and the links between them to form a unified network.
Second, during the search phase, the system analyzes the user’s question and scans the graph to find the most relevant nodes, edges, or clusters of nodes. Finally, during the synthesis stage, an LLM combines the retrieved information with the user’s original question to formulate a final response.

In this domain, one of the pioneering works
[
13
]
demonstrated that standard RAG struggles with query-focused summarization due to its lack of global context. GraphRAG, on the other hand, captures the complex terminology and relationships inherent in massive datasets by traversing explicit relational edges rather than relying purely on vector similarity.
Other benchmarks have also shown that, in scenarios requiring multi-hop reasoning, graph context-aware generation models significantly outperform current state-of-the-art RAG methods
[
24
]
.

2.4
Agentic AI

Agentic AI refers to systems in which autonomous agents work individually or collaboratively within decentralized environments. These agents interpret data, reason through complex objectives, and execute sophisticated actions without continuous human intervention. These agents can break down massive problems into smaller subtasks and adapt to open-world environments by combining foundational language models with planning and decision-making capabilities.

33
conducted a survey that provides a thorough examination of how autonomous agents enhance scalability and decision-making in complex industrial and analytical applications.
Agentic capabilities are also becoming increasingly valuable and sought after in the fields of expert systems and finance
[
3
,
28
,
14
]
.
For example, autonomous systems are now actively participating in the processing of financial information, supporting decisions, and executing workflows
[
35
]
. They have moved beyond simple task automation to formulate and evaluate end-to-end trading and risk management strategies.
In this direction,
23
developed a multi-agent collaboration system designed to enhance decision-making in financial investment research.

The intersection of agentic AI and RAG systems is a novel and rapidly developing frontier
[
36
]
. In 2024,
1
introduced Self-RAG, a novel framework that trains a model to adaptively retrieve information on demand and use special reflection tokens to evaluate the retrieved text and its own generated responses. A similar example is RAG-Critic, a framework that enhances the reliability of AI systems by employing an automated critical workflow to detect errors and prompt the system to self-correct before providing a final response
[
12
]
.
Another application is dynamic network optimization, in which an interactive AI framework could use RAG to create a real-time knowledge base for autonomous network management
[
46
]
.

2.5
Research Gap

Despite being in the early stages of development, agentic AI, knowledge graphs, and graphRAG are already being applied effectively across various domains.
However, their combination remains a largely unexplored area of research, with very few exceptions in the literature
[
32
,
21
]
.
The gap is even broader in the fintech context, despite the fact that registries, documents, and financial data are perfectly tailored for a combination of these new technologies.
Thus, the aim of this paper is to fill this gap and explore the intersection of agentic AI and GraphRAG in order to create a framework for exploring business registers in a way that has never been possible before.

The Swiss Official Gazette of Commerce

To validate the proposed GraphRAG architecture, we collect and process a comprehensive dataset derived from Open Government Data, namely the Swiss Official Gazette of Commerce
1
1

1

https://www.shab.ch/
. Serving as the central federal publication platform in Switzerland, SHAB publishes thousands of legally binding notices daily regarding critical corporate life-cycle events, such as company formations, bankruptcies, leadership changes, and liquidations. The necessary code to download, parse, and process this registry data is freely available in a public repository
2
2

2

https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19590491
.

The SHAB dataset spans seven years (from September 2018 to September 2025), and contains 7,014,137 raw records.
Each official publication record is characterized by rich, structured metadata, including its primary and sub-rubric, date of creation, geographic location, and language. The rubric code identifies the broad legal nature of the event, such as the registration of a new company, a change of board members, or a corporate liquidation. However, the specific technical details of these events as well as the identities of the connected companies and secondary actors are embedded within an associated unstructured, free-text payload.
In order to successfully reconstruct the full corporate network, these hidden relationships need to be extracted directly from the gazette’s text. We use an LLM-driven pipeline for this task because raw registry entries lack strict formatting, vary in length, and are published in one of the three most widely spoken official languages in Switzerland. For example, a standard bankruptcy suspension notice (rubric KK06) can contain only 163 characters, while complex bankruptcy proceedings (rubric KK08) can comprise 100 times as many characters of dense legal text referencing various creditors and liquidators.

4
Proposed system architecture

This section outlines the architecture designed to transform raw registry files into an interactive analytic tool. The system consists of three main components. First, the data pipeline (Section
4.1
) ingests data and performs topological resolution to populate a Neo4j knowledge graph. Second, the analytical agent (Section
4.2
) interprets human questions, iteratively querying the knowledge graph and repeating its exploration loop until it successfully retrieves the correct answer. Third, the exploratory dashboard (Section
4.3
) connects the backend logic with a human-in-the-loop interface for visual verification.

4.1
Data ingestion pipeline

As shown in Figure
1
, the pipeline consists of three distinct phases designed to capture explicit deterministic relationships and hidden semantic connections. First, we parse structured entities to build “strong nodes.” Second, we use an LLM to extract “weak nodes” from unstructured text. Finally, we achieve deterministic graph resolution through tokenization and database cleanup.

4.1.1
Graph schema and node taxonomy

The proposed agentic GraphRAG system is based on a highly structured Neo4j knowledge graph.
All nodes in the knowledge graph inherit a
BaseNode
label, which applies a global unique identifier (UID) index to enable sub-millisecond database lookups across millions of entities. The schema organizes the corporate network into four primary node types:
Company
,
Person
,
Event
, and
NameHub
.

Company
nodes represent legal entities. They store verified metadata extracted directly from the official registry, including the Swiss UID, legal form, nominal and paid-in capital, business purpose, and registered address.

Person
nodes represent individuals involved in corporate events, such as founders, board members, and liquidators.

NameHub
nodes act as abstract bridging entities for identity resolution. Because historical documents frequently contain spelling variations and transcription errors, these hubs link different string mentions to a single canonical identity, ensuring stable graph traversals.

Event
nodes represent the official publication records. Rather than storing only the raw text, these nodes aggregate sparse metadata, such as the publication titles, resolution dates, and affected municipal land, along with the main legal text in a single
full_text
property.

Semantic edges interconnect the nodes to map the relational logic of the network.

Person
nodes and
Company
nodes are never connected directly to each other, but always through an
Event
node.
For example,
HAS_EVENT
links companies to their chronological publications,
ACTED_IN
connects individuals to specific corporate resolutions, and
HAS_NAME
binds entities to their corresponding
NameHub
.
The complete mapping of semantic roles to edge types is detailed in the Appendix Table

4.1.2
Phase 1: Structured Ingestion (Strong Nodes)

The first phase of the data ingestion pipeline processes the original registry data row by row to generate “strong nodes.” These nodes represent ground-truth entities derived directly from the structured, verified metadata fields within the official register.
The metadata for
Company
nodes includes specific attributes, such as a standardized business identification number (UID), legal form, registered address, and capital. For
Person
nodes, the pipeline ensures idempotent (repeatable without duplication) database ingestion.

Event
nodes are constructed by aggregating sparse metadata columns into a
full_text
string. This maximizes the contextual density of the event for the downstream LLM analysis (phase 2). Finally, deterministic edges are generated based on explicit column mappings. For example, a buyer is linked to an acquired company via an
ACQUIRED_FROM
edge, and a debtor is connected to a bankruptcy event via an
INVOLVED_IN
edge.

4.1.3
Phase 2: LLM-Driven Unstructured Extraction (Weak Nodes)

A major challenge in processing historical commercial data is that secondary actors, such as liquidators, creditors, and temporarily appointed directors, are often only mentioned in the unstructured free text of the gazette notice. To capture these latent actors, phase 2 uses a large language model (specifically
gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18
with enforced JSON object formatting and a temperature of 0.0) to perform named entity recognition and relationship extraction on the
full_text
property of the event nodes.
To optimize API costs and maximize signal-to-noise ratio, this unstructured extraction is strictly limited to high-value event sub-rubrics. These include new foundations (rubric
HR01
with 350,717 events), bankruptcies (rubrics
KK03
with 47,608 events,
KK02
with 36,050 events, and
KK06
with 31,409 events), and liquidations (rubrics
LS01
with 30,451 events and
LS02
with 45,110 events).

A detailed system prompt instructs the LLM to extract
Person
and
Company
entities and infer their semantic roles (e.g., “Creditor” or “Liquidator”). To ensure high-quality data, the prompt enforces three main extraction rules. First, the model must split compound names and extract individuals listed conjunctively as separate JSON objects. Second, the model must disambiguate generic legal authorities (e.g., a bankruptcy office, or “Konkursamt”) by appending the relevant city identified in the text. Third, the system applies a deterministic stoplist (e.g. “SHAB”) to filter out official gazette publishers and prevent metadata from polluting the graph.

To efficiently handle the massive scale of event nodes, the extraction pipeline leverages a batched architecture with two primary engineering constraints. To prevent LLM token hallucinations and minimize context window usage, complex alphanumeric UIDs are mapped to small integers in the prompt payload. In addition, event texts are truncated to 2,000 characters. The LLM then returns a JSON object keyed by these integers, which the pipeline safely translates back to true database UIDs. Additionally, phase 2 employs adaptive recursive retry. If the LLM fails to return a valid JSON structure or times out on a large batch, the algorithm splits the batch in half and attempts extraction again. This ensures continuous ingestion without manual intervention.
Entities extracted dynamically in this LLM phase are classified as “Weak Nodes.”

During this phase, every
Company
and
Person
node, both weak and strong, is linked via a
HAS_NAME
edge to a central
NameHub
node constructed from this alphabetical key. The
NameHub
therefore acts as an intermediate identity layer.

Combining the historical structured data from phase 1 with the LLM-extracted natural language from phase 2 introduces considerable orthographic instability into the graph. Real-world entities frequently appear in structurally distinct formats, such as “John Doe” (standard first-last format), “Doe, John” (the comma-first legal convention), or “John A. Doe” (with a middle initial added by a different clerk). Furthermore, significant overlap often occurs between the dynamically extracted entities and the pre-existing structured nodes. If these redundant nodes were left unresolved, they would cause token-order hallucinations during graph traversal. This would ultimately compromise the reliability of the agent’s deductive logic.

4.1.4
Phase 3: Identity Resolution

Name inconsistencies are resolved by applying an algorithm called
generate_hub_key
to all
Company
and
Person
nodes, whether they originate from strong structured data or weak LLM extractions. This algorithm normalizes the name string through
alphabetical tokenization
: it lowercases the text, strips non-alphanumeric characters, splits the string into discrete tokens, sorts them alphabetically, and concatenates them.
After this, both “Doe, John” and “John Doe” deterministically yield the identical key
doejohn
. This mechanism is intentionally strict. Nodes are linked to the same central
NameHub
if the resulting normalized key is identical, byte-for-byte, and if no fuzzy tolerance is introduced. As a result, borderline cases such as names differing only by a middle initial are conservatively rejected.

Because ingestion happens in parallel batches, particularly during the LLM extraction stage, duplicate
NameHub
nodes can occasionally be created for the same normalized key. To eliminate this redundancy, the pipeline includes a
NameHub resolution
step: a deduplication query scans all existing hubs, recomputes their alphabetical tokenization keys, groups those that share the same canonical key, and selects a single surviving master hub for each duplicate set.
All incoming
HAS_NAME
edges are then rewired from the redundant hubs to the master hub.

Following the merger of duplicate hubs, the pipeline performs a
weak node absorption
step to further clean up the graph topology. This procedure targets any weak node that shares both a
NameHub
and an event connection with a strong node. The system recognizes this pattern and infers that the weak node is simply an LLM-extracted duplicate of a structured registry entity that is already present in the event context. The redundant weak node is then deleted and its edges reconnected, leaving only the verified strong node.
These two cleanup stages work together to ensure that the
NameHub
architecture is not just a string-normalization layer, but rather a complete identity-resolution mechanism. This mechanism unifies aliases across both structured and unstructured ingestion, while also preserving graph precision for downstream retrieval and reasoning.

The final Neo4j knowledge graph comprises almost 5 million relationships and over 5 million nodes. Of these, 992,522 are strong nodes and 718,736 are weak nodes. Complete statistics can be found in the Appendix Table
7
.

4.1.5
Privacy-Preserving Abstraction Layer

Although public registries such as SHAB promote transparency, downstream analytical applications typically require strict compliance with data protection regulations (e.g., GDPR). To reconcile these competing needs, we introduce an optional abstraction layer that preserves privacy by obfuscating personally identifiable information prior to final deployment.
This layer runs as a post-processing operation immediately following the structural identity resolution phase.

The abstraction mechanism relies on a cryptographic hash function, HMAC-SHA256
[
2
]
. When activated, the pipeline extracts sensitive textual properties from the knowledge graph, including corporate nomenclature, registered geographical locations, and unstructured event descriptions. These vectors are then mathematically transformed into fixed-length strings and rewritten into the database via bulk update operations.
Consequently, the knowledge graph retains all the complex topologies and entity interdependencies required for advanced algorithmic analysis while keeping raw, identifiable data hidden from the reasoning agent.

To ensure forensic traceability and enable authorized operators to reconstruct the data subsequently, the framework saves a translation table that maps the hashed tokens back to their original plain-text vectors.

4.2
Analytical agent

The analytical agent manages data retrieval, interprets user questions, and autonomously explores the Neo4j knowledge graph through a controlled tool-based workflow. Rather than querying the database directly, the agent translates natural-language requests into executable back-end API calls. These calls are mapped to highly optimized queries written in Cypher, a declarative graph database language
[
18
]
.

Conventional LLM systems that rely on a single monolithic prompt frequently struggle with complex database interactions. In practice, this approach often leads to incorrect tool selection, contextual hallucinations, inefficient graph traversal, and increased exposure to unsafe query generation. To address these limitations, the analytical agent is implemented as a structured multi-layered architecture. As illustrated in Figure
2
, the agent architecture consists of three components: a zero-shot
intent router
that classifies the user query and restricts the available tools; an
agentic reflection loop
that iteratively selects tools, generates JSON arguments, and self-corrects failed actions; and a final
response synthesis
stage governed by a strict internal state machine that controls follow-up behavior and output formatting.

This architecture allows the agent to retrieve factual, multi-hop information from the graph while maintaining the flexibility of a natural-language interface. At the same time, the separation between routing, tool execution, and synthesis constrains the model’s behavior sufficiently to make complex analytic querying both reliable and operationally safe.

4.2.1
Intent router

A structural vulnerability of standard agentic AI is tool overload. Providing an agentic AI with an extensive list of available database tools frequently impairs its reasoning ability, causing it to force complex queries through incorrect tools or hallucinate parameters to fill the schema.

To resolve this “cognitive overload,” the proposed architecture employs a zero-shot intent router.
As illustrated in Figure
2
, before exploring the KG, an LLM evaluates the user query against a concise system prompt.
The router strictly classifies the request into one of five predefined semantic intent categories, which map directly to internal system flags:
search_companies
for entity disambiguation,
explore_network
for relational and structural traversals,
get_node_history
for temporal and event-based tracking,
analytics
for macro-level aggregation, or
all_tools
for highly ambiguous or complex multi-hop explorations.
Appendix Table
8
provides a comprehensive breakdown of the semantic intents and their associated tools.

Based on this classification, the system dynamically restricts the tool payload injected into the primary agent’s context.
For standard explorations focused on specific entities, the router loads a suite of foundational search and traversal tools. This guaranties that the agent can definitively identify an entity and map its corporate connections.
Conversely, for macro analytics involving high-level statistical questions, such as determining the top companies by capital, the system blocks access to exploratory tools and loads only statistical aggregation functions.
This enforced restriction prevents the LLM from performing inefficient operations, such as manually paginating through hundreds of company records to calculate a sum.

4.2.2
Agentic reflection loop

Even when the intent router restricts the action space to a small subset of relevant tools, a single tool call is often insufficient to resolve a user request. For instance, an entity name may be misspelled, the selected tool may require an unknown identifier, or the initial retrieval step may yield no results. If the system were to stop after one unsuccessful attempt, the results would often be incomplete or inaccurate. For this reason, the second phase of the analytical agent architecture is implemented as a bounded agentic reflection loop.

As illustrated in Figure
2
, this loop receives as input the subset of tools selected by the intent router and runs for at most four iterations on the
gpt-4o-mini
model. Each iteration consists of four sequential steps. In step A, the LLM interprets the user’s query and examines the accumulated conversational and retrieval context to determine which tool to invoke next. In step B, it generates a structured JSON payload containing the tool name and its arguments. In step C, the Python backend intercepts the payload and maps it to a predefined semantic endpoint, which then queries the Neo4j knowledge graph. In step D, the backend returns the result of the tool execution to the agent. If the call was successful, the retrieved JSON is appended to the context window; if it failed or returned an empty result, the backend injects deterministic feedback explaining the failure and suggesting the next valid recovery step.

This loop allows the agent to gradually improve its retrieval strategy by using multiple predefined semantic endpoints (step C). The primary disambiguation endpoint is named
search_companies
, and can identify candidate entities through fuzzy matching and tolerate minor typographical errors or partial names. Once a valid entity has been identified, the agent can continue with tools such as
explore_network
or
get_node_history
. If structured lookup fails, the loop can pivot to
global_text_search
, which queries a Neo4j Lucene full-text index of raw publication texts and returns localized lexical matches. Finally,
execute_custom_cypher
is reserved as a last-resort analytical interface when predefined semantic tools cannot satisfy the request. To preserve database integrity, this endpoint is strictly constrained by regex-based guards that block destructive mutation keywords such as
CREATE
,
DELETE
, and
MERGE
.

The key property of this design is that each iteration is informed by the outcome of the previous one. If a tool returns an empty dataset, the next LLM call does not restart from scratch; instead, it receives the failed result together with backend-injected procedural feedback, allowing it to revise its plan. The model may then switch to a search step to recover a missing identifier, reformulate its arguments, or fall back to a broader text retrieval when graph matching is insufficient. The loop terminates when either the maximum iteration threshold is reached or the model determines that enough evidence has been gathered to answer the user’s request.

4.2.3
Response Synthesis

The final phase of the agent architecture is responsible for turning the raw outputs of the retrieval loop into a user-facing answer and for maintaining a coherent dialogue across turns. Before the answer is generated, the current conversational state is evaluated to determine what the agent is allowed to present next. Only after this control step has been completed does the system invoke the final synthesis call that produces the natural-language response.

This intermediate control layer is necessary because multi-turn LLM agents often fail to preserve procedural context. Even when prior dialogue history is available, they may re-run tools that have already been used, repeat exhausted retrieval paths, or suggest analytically invalid next steps
[
44
,
49
]
. To avoid these failure modes, the architecture introduces a strict state machine (SSM) between retrieval and final response generation. In the agentic reflection loop, the system inspects the returned JSON payload together with the execution trace and the full conversational history. Based on this information, the system computes the current analytical state and injects a deterministic system instruction into the context during step B of the agentic reflection loop. This instruction will later be consumed by the synthesis model.

Formally, the SSM is defined by the tuple
(S,\Sigma,\delta,s_{0})
, where
S=\{S_{0},S_{1},S_{2},S_{3},S_{4}\}
is the finite set of operational states,
\Sigma
is the input alphabet of tool execution events and user intents inferred from conversational history,
\delta:S\times\Sigma\rightarrow S
is the deterministic transition function, and
s_{0}=S_{0}
is the initial state. The transition logic determines which instruction is injected into the synthesis context before the final answer is produced:

•

State
S_{0}
(Disambiguation Mode):
This state is entered when a query returns multiple matching entities. The injected instruction forces the synthesis step to output a complete Markdown table of the candidate entities and to conclude with the suggestion:
“Which of these entities would you like to investigate further?”

•

State
S_{1}
(Dossier Mode):

S_{0}\rightarrow S_{1}
is triggered when a single entity is identified or when the user selects one entity from the disambiguation table. The synthesis step is then constrained to output a dossier-style entity profile and to offer the two valid next directions: network exploration or event history.

•

State
S_{2}
(Network Explored):

S_{1}\rightarrow S_{2}
is triggered when the network has already been explored and
has_network
is True. If
has_history
is still False, the injected instruction directs the synthesis step toward event history; otherwise, the system transitions toward deep text search.

•

State
S_{3}
(History Explored):

S_{1}\rightarrow S_{3}
is triggered when node history has already been shown and
has_history
is True. If
has_network
is still False, the synthesis step is instructed to suggest network exploration; otherwise, it transitions toward deep text search.

•

State
S_{4}
(Deep Text Search):
This terminal state is reached when both
has_network
and
has_history
are True, or when the user explicitly requests deeper corroboration after structured retrieval has been exhausted. In this case, the state machine forces the next retrieval step to be
global_text_search
, and the synthesis step presents the corresponding unstructured evidence.

Once the appropriate state instruction has been injected, the final LLM call performs the actual response synthesis. This call receives the tool outputs in JSON form, the execution trace of the current turn, the prior conversational history, and, when available, the GUI-provided contextual anchor as described in Section
4.3
. Using this enriched context, the model converts the raw structured data into the required presentation format, such as a Markdown result table, a dossier card, or a concise explanatory summary.

5
Experimental setup and evaluation methodology

To empirically validate the agentic GraphRAG architecture, we design a multi-tiered evaluation framework comprising three distinct datasets, each tailored to test a specific aspect of the system’s performance.
The first dataset (named
automated dataset
) is derived directly from the SHAB domain and is intended for testing broad analytical coverage. This dataset comprises 300 realistic data analytics questions, which are evenly divided into three levels based on difficulty: direct retrieval (level 1), multi-hop traversal (level 2), and temporal/complex aggregation (level 3).
To ensure ground-truth fidelity and eliminate human annotation bias, the dataset is generated programmatically via “Reverse RAG.” First, complex Cypher queries extract actual subgraphs from the Neo4j database. Then, GPT-5 (version
gpt-5-2025-08-07
) synthesizes these subgraphs into natural language questions and expected answers.
Specifically, the pipeline executes four distinct classes of Cypher queries to seed the language model: (1)
Direct Extraction
of node properties to generate level 1 questions; (2)
Multi-hop Corporate Hierarchies
and (3)
NameHub Resolution
paths to formulate complex level 2 relational queries; and (4)
Temporal Event Histories
collecting chronological publication sequences to construct advanced level 3 temporal questions. This automated, graph-seeded generation procedure ensures that each evaluation query receives a factually accurate, deterministic response based on the original graph structure.

The second benchmark (named
golden dataset
) expands the first dataset into an evaluation dataset of 60 questions, manually curated by the authors. This benchmark focuses on realistic, entity-centric questions with straightforward, easy-to-evaluate answers.
This corpus is stratified into easy, medium, and hard categories. It combines direct factual retrieval tasks, such as registered addresses and dissolution notices, with demanding person-centered graph questions. These harder questions involve identifying affiliations or connected companies and persons.

Finally, to evaluate the system’s conversational quality and context retention, we manually create a third dataset (named
conversational dataset
) comprising ten realistic, multi-turn conversations related to the SHAB domain. Inspired by established benchmarks such as CoQA (Conversational Question Answering)
[
34
]
and QuAC (Question Answering in Context)
[
9
]
, this dataset tests the agent’s ability to handle follow-up questions, resolve co-references, and maintain a coherent exploration thread throughout an in-depth corporate data analysis.

We test Tier 2 on the first
automated dataset
, while Tier 3 is evaluated using both the first
automated dataset
and the second manually curated
golden dataset
. Finally, we apply the third
conversational dataset
to evaluate Tier 4.

5.1
RAG Baseline

In order to define a baseline, we implement a standard agentic RAG system. Based on the
all-MiniLM-L6-v2
embedding model
[
40
]
, this system uses a ChromaDB vector database to store all the original event documents.
The ingestion is optimized using a batch size of 500 to efficiently process all the SHAB documents. During the retrieval phase, the system identifies the top
k=5
most semantically similar text chunks. Similar to the agentic GraphRAG, the RAG Baseline’s generation step leverages the
gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18
model with a temperature of 0.0 to ensure greedy token selection and provide highly deterministic outputs.
The two systems are then evaluated side by side in Tiers 3 and 4.

5.2
Tier 1: Entity Resolution

The Tier 1 evaluation verifies that the underlying knowledge graph is topologically sound, meaning that duplicated entities are grouped conservatively, and canonical identities are reliably resolved. More specifically, it tests the empirical efficacy of the alphabetical tokenization implemented by the
generate_hub_key
algorithm described in Section
4.1.4
. The evaluation is performed on a sample of 1,000
NameHub
nodes and is intended to validate the deterministic deduplication layer on which all downstream graph traversal depends.
A
NameHub
acts as the standardized master node, while the nodes connected to it through
HAS_NAME
edges represent the various spelling variations and text mentions that the system decided to merge under that single identity.
To avoid trivial cases (hubs with only one connected component) and extreme statistical outliers (for example, highly ambiguous surnames linked to many components), the sampling query only selects hubs with between two and 20 connected nodes.

Evaluation Procedure.
For each sampled
NameHub
, the script iterates over all distinct merged names connected to that hub and compares each one to the hub’s representative name. Thus, the evaluation does not compute all pairwise combinations among merged names; instead, it performs one comparison per distinct merged name against the central
NameHub
label. For each such comparison, the evaluation computes the Levenshtein similarity ratio
S(A,B)
between the original string variant (
A
) and the hub name (
B
), defined as:

S(A,B)=\frac{|A|+|B|-\text{Lev}(A,B)}{|A|+|B|}

(1)

where
|A|
and
|B|
denote the character lengths of the two strings, and
\text{Lev}(A,B)
is the Levenshtein distance, i.e., the minimum number of single-character insertions, deletions, or substitutions required to transform one string into the other.

A merge is classified as a true positive if either the similarity score satisfies
S(A,B)>0.6
or the alphabetically sorted token sets of the two strings match exactly. This second criterion is important because it correctly captures legal formatting variations such as comma-first name order (e.g., “Doe, John” versus “John Doe”) and related token-order permutations that would otherwise appear artificially distant under standard string-level similarity measures.

Fundamentally, Tier 1 computes the precision of the deterministic deduplication layer. Precision is defined as the fraction of evaluated merged-name comparisons that correspond to legitimate, verifiable co-references. Recall is intentionally excluded from this evaluation. Calculating true recall would require identifying every missed match (false negative) across the entire corpus. Given that the registry used in this work spans more than seven million documents, such an exhaustive manual audit is not realistically achievable.

5.3
Tier 2: Agent Trajectory

A fundamental limitation of large language models is their non-deterministic nature. In complex GraphRAG deployments, an agent that is only given a list of database tools may attempt to execute structured graph traversals and unstructured text searches simultaneously, without first checking whether the target entity exists. This could lead to execution loops, erroneous node UIDs, and context window exhaustion. To address this, as described in Section
4.2.3
, the agentic GraphRAG is designed around a strict state machine framework, structurally constrained by disabling parallel tool execution.

Tier 2 evaluation examines the following question: When confronted with a realistic query, does the agentic GraphRAG select the correct tools in the correct order and recover properly when an initial lookup fails?

Instead of evaluating the semantic quality of the agent’s final written response, which is the scope of the RAGAS-based Tier 3 evaluation, this tier assesses the agent’s entire execution trajectory. Evaluating the trajectory level is essential because the process by which an agent arrives at an answer is as important as the answer itself. An agent that retrieves the correct answer through an arbitrary or erratic sequence of tool calls cannot be trusted in formal or compliance contexts, as the process cannot be audited or reproduced.

Evaluation Procedure.
To prevent conversational drift and enforce a controlled evaluation environment, a fresh agentic GraphRAG instance is invoked for each question of the
automated dataset
. A trace callback function intercepts and records every internal log message generated by the agent during execution. This includes the name of each tool invoked, the arguments provided, and the result status returned, all in strict chronological order. The output is a complete, ordered execution transcript for every evaluated question. Wall-clock latency is measured from the moment the agent is invoked to the moment the final response is returned.
Upon completion, four distinct behavioral metrics are computed, each measuring a specific and independent invariant of the agent’s state machine.

(i) Tool Selection Accuracy
(TSA) quantifies the fraction of queries for which the agent’s first tool invocation is a structured graph operation. Formally:

\text{TSA}=\frac{|\{q\in Q:\text{tool}_{1}(q)\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{struct}}\}|}{|Q|}

(2)

where
\mathcal{T}_{\text{struct}}
is the set of admissible structured first-tool
search_companies
,
get_node_history
, or
explore_network
, as described in Section
4.2.1
. This broader definition of admissible first-tools is necessary because questions that embed an entity UID explicitly should correctly trigger an analytical function as the first call, without an intermediate name-resolution step.

(ii) Fallback Activation Rate
(FAR) measures the fraction of queries for which
global_text_search
is activated at any point in the trajectory:

\text{FAR}=\frac{|\{q\in Q:\texttt{global\_text\_search}\in\text{Trajectory}(q)\}|}{|Q|}

(3)

A moderate fallback rate is expected and ideal from an operational standpoint, confirming that the agent correctly recovers from failed structured lookups. However, an excessively high rate would indicate chronic underutilization of the graph structure.

(iii) Average Reasoning Steps
(ARS) reports the mean number of distinct tool invocations per query:

\text{ARS}=\frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{q\in Q}|\text{Trajectory}(q)|

(4)

This metric quantifies the agent’s cognitive efficiency. Excessively high values suggest redundant retrieval loops and unnecessary API expenditure, while unusually low values may indicate premature termination of the answer.

(iv) Query Success Rate
(QSR) measures the fraction of queries for which the execution trajectory contains at least one successful data retrieval event. Formally:

\text{QSR}=\frac{|\{q\in Q:\text{Success}(q)=\texttt{True}\}|}{|Q|}

(5)

For this metric, a query is scored as successful if
any
relevant structured or unstructured data is returned from the database.

5.4
Tier 3: RAGAS

Although Tier 1 validates the structural integrity of the graph and Tier 2 validates the agent’s query execution, neither measures the quality, accuracy, nor comprehensibility of the agent’s final response.
The Tier 3 evaluation addresses the following critical question: does the agent produce answers that are accurate, grounded, and complete?
This is measured along three distinct axes. First, is the agent’s response based on facts retrieved from the database, or does it hallucinate? Second, does the response directly address the user’s question? Third, does the response contain all the information that an ideal answer would contain?

These three dimensions correspond to the evaluation axes of the RAGAS framework
[
15
]
, which is a widely adopted methodology for assessing RAG systems. In its canonical formulation, RAGAS evaluates the following: (1)
Faithfulness
, or whether the answer is grounded in the retrieved context, (2)
Answer Relevance
, or whether the answer addresses the actual question, and (3)
Context Relevance
, or whether the retrieved context is focused and contains as little irrelevant information as possible.

Tier 3 evaluates the agentic GraphRAG system and the vector RAG baseline in parallel across both the
automated dataset
(300 questions) and the
golden dataset
(60 questions). To leverage the ground-truth data available in these benchmarks, we adapt the standard RAGAS metrics in two ways.
First, across both datasets, we replace Context Relevance with
Information Recall
. Because both datasets provide expected answers, we can move beyond a simple retrieval-side diagnostic to directly measure completeness. Information Recall assesses exactly how much of the expected answer is successfully captured in the agent’s response.
Second, for the
golden dataset
specifically, we replace Faithfulness with
Correctness
. Because this benchmark features manually curated and verified reference answers, we can evaluate true factual accuracy rather than just grounding. Whereas Faithfulness only checks if an answer is supported by the retrieved context (even if that context itself is wrong), Correctness ensures the agent’s response strictly matches the verified truth without errors or contradictions. Ultimately, Information Recall ensures the generated answer is complete, while Correctness ensures it is factually accurate.

Evaluation Procedure.
This evaluation tier relies on an LLM-as-a-Judge methodology
[
47
,
22
]
. Specifically, the evaluator calls GPT-5 (version
gpt-5-2025-08-07
) once for each target metric and question. During these calls, the system provides a tightly constrained scoring prompt that instructs the model to output a single floating-point value between 0.0 and 1.0. To ensure a fair comparison and to minimize the impact of any systematic bias in the judge’s calibration, the same judge model is used to evaluate both the GraphRAG system and the baseline. 9
provides additional details on the evaluation metrics and the prompts.

5.5
Tier 4: Conversations

While Tier 3 evaluates single-shot answers, real analysts progressively refine their queries, use contextual clues like pronouns, and shift between structural and historical exploration. To capture this multi-turn dynamic, we introduce a new benchmark based on ten simulated conversations. These dialogues are designed to mirror realistic analytic workflows and explicitly test the system’s contextual carryover, state retention, and conversational robustness.

The conversations are structured around different
conversation goals
, each of which addresses a distinct form of contextual continuity. Some test whether the agent can preserve the active person and the names of shared activities across follow-up turns. Others test whether the agent can preserve the active company and its recovered location across short follow-up exchanges.
More complex conversations require the system to preserve an active person or company together with shared-activity names, while correctly handling a subsequent subject shift.

The evaluation is performed at two levels: the turn level and the conversation level. At the turn level, each response is evaluated based on Correctness, Answer Relevance, and Information Recall, consistent with the
golden dataset
in Tier 3. For Tier 4, we also introduce other conversation-level metrics. First, we compute
Turn Success Rate
, which measures the fraction of turns within a conversation that are answered successfully. Second, we measure
Context Carryover Accuracy
, which captures whether the system correctly preserves the active entity, target company, or historical reference across follow-up turns that require contextual memory. Finally, for the agentic GraphRAG approach, we additionally compute
Tool Transition Accuracy
, which measures whether the agent follows the expected structured research path. For instance, the agent transitions from entity lookup to network exploration and then to node history as needed.

Evaluation Procedure.
The agentic GraphRAG and the vector RAG baseline are evaluated on the same set of multi-turn conversations. For both agents, the full prior dialogue history is provided at each turn, requiring the systems to resolve references under identical contextual conditions. The GraphRAG approach is evaluated using its native, stateful, tool-calling architecture. The baseline receives the same conversational history, along with the retrieved vector context. Scoring is conducted using the LLM-as-a-Judge methodology, employing GPT-5 (version
gpt-5-2025-08-07
)
For each turn, the judge produces a score between 0.0 and 1.0 for each target metric using a constrained evaluation prompt. Conversation-level scores are then deterministically aggregated from the turn-level outputs.

provides more information on the evaluation metrics and the prompts for Tier 4.

When evaluated on the

In other words, if the model generates a response strictly based on the retrieved context, it will receive a high faithfulness score even if that context is entirely irrelevant to the original query.

In terms of computational latency, the vector RAG baseline is significantly faster, operating in an average of 4.32 seconds per response. When evaluated on the manually curated

The agentic GraphRAG achieves average scores between 0.83 and 0.88, while the vector RAG baseline lags significantly behind with scores between 0.12 and 0.25. As expected, however, the baseline vector approach remains much faster on average.

While the

For instance, during an Answer Relevance evaluation of the

The expected answer is the full street address and postal code, specifically “Street Name 19, 5300 City A”. The agent responded by stating that, as of January 2024, the company’s address had changed to City A, noting its previous location in City B. However, the response omitted the exact street name and postal code. Instead of awarding partial credit for correctly identifying the municipality and the recent relocation, the LLM-as-judge gave the response an Answer Relevance score of 0.10, deeming it nearly entirely irrelevant.

Similar degrees of severity are observed with the Information Recall metric.
When asked to disambiguate a specific

The agent provided a perfectly structured Markdown table detailing the individual’s name and the two companies they were involved with, as well as their roles in those organizations. However, the judge drastically reduced the information recall score to 0.55 because the agent omitted the raw, internal alphanumeric user UIDs, which are generally irrelevant to human readers. Despite this omission, the agent successfully captured the core semantic relationships.

This penalizing behavior also applies to the
golden dataset

The expected response is a simple, comma-separated list of the two correct company names. Although the agent correctly identified both companies, it presented them in a detailed table that included location, connection role, event dates, and a standard disclaimer explaining the probabilistic nature of entity resolution. Because the agent was verbose and included helpful metadata instead of just a list, the judge penalized the response, giving it an Answer Relevance score of 0.40.

At the turn level, the agentic GraphRAG achieves Correctness and Information Recall scores of 0.760 and 0.742, respectively, compared with baseline scores of 0.344 and 0.317.

It also improves Context Carryover Accuracy from 0.433 to 0.683. These results confirm that the graph-based system better preserves the active entity, maintains the analytic thread, and resolves follow-up references across turns.
Finally, the agentic GraphRAG achieves a Tool Transition Accuracy of 0.900, showing its ability to consistently navigate the underlying state machine and execute the intended structured workflow.

To better contextualize and understand the aggregate results in Table
5
, it is useful to consider a few representative failure cases that reveal how conversational scoring can penalize even localized interpretation errors.
The first example of error propagation occurred in a company connection conversation. When asked to list companies connected to a given firm, the agent included the liquidated form of the same firm as a separate entity, despite it being the same firm after a legal status change.
Since this variant was not part of the expected answer, the response was penalized.
The error propagated into the next follow-up question, in which the agent was asked to count the connected companies. After the liquidated variant was introduced as a separate entity, subsequent related answers were also incorrect and scored lower.

A second example concerns the Turn Success Rate metric. During a conversation, the agent responded to a location-based follow-up question by providing the address of a newly registered branch office instead of the company’s actual headquarters. This mistake occurred because the underlying registration text contained information about both the branch and the headquarters, and the model selected the most recent address instead of the legally relevant headquarters location.
Because subsequent turns in the same conversation depended on the initial entity state, the error propagated through the rest of the conversation and negatively affected later turn-level scores.

Taken together, the results from all four tiers provide a consistent overview of the proposed system’s strengths.
At the data level, the entity-resolution pipeline achieves high precision while maintaining a conservative merge policy. At the agent level, analyses of routing and tool execution confirm that the system primarily follows the intended structured retrieval pathways and only activates fallback mechanisms when necessary.
The agentic GraphRAG architecture substantially outperforms the vector baseline on both automated and manually curated benchmarks at the answer-quality level, especially in terms of correctness, relevance, and information recall.
Conversational evaluation shows that these advantages extend to multi-turn exploratory settings, where retaining context and maintaining procedural coherence are crucial.
It is important to note that we do not evaluate the agentic GraphRAG against the baseline on global aggregation queries, such as ranking top companies. This comparison is intentionally omitted because standard vector-RAG architectures are structurally incapable of answering such macro-level questions, whereas the GraphRAG system can handle them natively.
Overall, the results support the claim that an agentic knowledge graph architecture is significantly more accurate and robust than a standard vector-RAG baseline in the context of expert-oriented exploration of commercial registry data.

7
Conclusion

This study presents a novel GraphRAG architecture tailored for expert-oriented analysis of large-scale commercial registries. Beyond its technical contribution, the system has broader practical implications. It empowers users to explore public information that, in principle, is freely accessible, yet difficult to use in practice due to its scale, fragmentation, and the complexity of legal documents. The architecture bridges the gap between formal openness and effective accessibility by transforming millions of registry publications into a navigable graph coupled with an auditable analytical agent and an interactive dashboard.
Moreover, because this framework provides strict auditability and control over data retrieval, it can be deployed beyond public records. With the integration of a dedicated anonymization layer (Section
4.1.5
), the same architecture can safely be deployed in highly sensitive, private environments, such as internal banking compliance networks, healthcare patient registries, and proprietary corporate databases. In these environments, unlocking complex data must be balanced with rigorous privacy standards.

Another important feature of this agentic system is its modularity. The architecture is explicitly designed so that data ingestion, routing, tool use, and response synthesis are treated as separate processes.
This makes the system extensible while maintaining operational control. It also allows developers to integrate new tools and more advanced analytical functions into the back end without substantially increasing the risk of agent overload or prompt inefficiency.

The evaluation framework developed in this study is a valuable contribution in its own right. In addition to providing a multi-tiered assessment of graph quality, tool behavior, answer quality, and conversational robustness, the framework highlights the practical challenges of evaluating agentic systems with automated, LLM-based judges. Qualitative analysis reveals that contextually rich or procedurally correct responses may be penalized if scoring mechanisms are overly sensitive to formatting or compression. These findings suggest that evaluating complex, multi-hop reasoning systems should combine quantitative benchmarks with qualitative inspection to better capture their analytical utility.

Despite these strengths, several limitations remain. First, although the evaluation framework is broad and multi-tiered, it is not possible to exhaustively measure all aspects of performance at this scale. In particular, true recall is intentionally excluded from the Tier 1 entity-resolution evaluation. Calculating recall would require identifying every missed match across a corpus of more than seven million historical publications, which would demand an impractical amount of manual review. For this reason, the evaluation emphasizes precision and controlled benchmark-based validation rather than full-corpus recall estimation.

A second limitation is that, although the SHAB domain provides a rich and realistic benchmark, it remains specific to a single institutional setting with its own legal vocabulary, document conventions, and multilingual complexity. While the architecture is generalizable, further validation on other national commercial gazettes or public registry systems would strengthen its external validity. In addition, the current system relies on LLM-based judgment for several evaluation components. Although this approach is practical and scalable, it may remain sensitive to output formatting and other subtle prompt effects.

Future work can therefore proceed in several directions. On the data side, the graph-construction pipeline could be extended with richer entity normalization, stronger multilingual capabilities, and additional document sources beyond the SHAB register. On the agent side, new domain-specific tools, more advanced analytical functions, and improved long-horizon conversational planning could be integrated into the modular back end. On the evaluation side, future research could expand the manually curated conversational benchmark, incorporate human expert assessments, and test transferability to other public-sector and regulatory corpora.

In summary, this work suggests that GraphRAG systems provide a promising basis for trustworthy AI in public-interest analytics when paired with controlled agentic reasoning and transparent interfaces.

Appendix C
Tier 3 Evaluation Metrics

Table
9
details the Tier 3 evaluation metrics, their underlying mechanisms, operational importance, and the specific LLM-as-a-judge prompts utilized to compute the final scores.

Table 9:
Tier 3 evaluation metrics, their mechanisms, operational importance, and corresponding LLM-as-a-judge prompts.

Metric

What it measures

Why it matters

Scoring prompt

Faithfulness

Whether the agent’s answer is fully grounded in the retrieved context, meaning that every factual claim in the answer can be traced back to the data the system actually retrieved from the database.

While an answer may be fluent and well-structured, it can still contain made-up facts that were never present in the database. A score of 1.0 means that all of the statements in the response are directly supported by the retrieved context. A score of 0.0 means the response is not supported by the data. This metric is used for the large automatically generated benchmark, where grounding to retrieved evidence is crucial to the evaluation process.

“Given the context and the answer, compute a faithfulness score between 0.0 and 1.0. 1.0 means the answer is 100% derived from the context. 0.0 means the answer states completely fabricated facts. Output ONLY a float number.”

Correctness

Whether the agent’s answer is factually consistent with the verified reference answer, regardless of how the supporting context was retrieved.

Correctness is used for the manually curated benchmark, where each question has an explicitly verified ground-truth answer. In this setting, the main concern is not only whether the answer is grounded in retrieved evidence, but whether it is factually right with respect to the validated reference answer. This metric is especially useful when the benchmark is designed for direct answer checking rather than retrieval-side diagnostics.

“Given the question, the expected answer, and the actual answer, compute a correctness score between 0.0 and 1.0. 1.0 means the actual answer is factually correct and captures the expected answer well. 0.0 means the actual answer is factually wrong or fails to answer the question correctly. Be tolerant to differences in wording, formatting, ordering, or level of detail, as long as the substance is correct. Output ONLY a float number.”

Answer Relevance

Whether the answer directly and completely addresses the user’s question, regardless of the retrieved context.

Even if a system never misrepresents a fact, it can still produce answers that sidestep the question, respond to a different question, or bury the key information in irrelevant detail. Answer relevance measures whether the agent understood the question and shaped its response accordingly. This measure is evaluated independently of context to isolate this failure mode from faithfulness and correctness.

“Given the question and the answer, compute an answer relevance score between 0.0 and 1.0. 1.0 means it perfectly answers the question directly. 0.0 means it dodges the question entirely. Output ONLY a float number.”

Information Recall

The fraction of the expected answer’s core information that the agent’s response successfully captures.

Faithfulness or correctness together with relevance can show that an answer is grounded or factually valid, but they do not measure if it is complete. For example, if a user asks for a company’s full timeline of events, the answer should include every key milestone, not just a subset. Information Recall, which is computed by comparing the agent’s answer to the correct answer, directly quantifies completeness. It is the most sensitive metric to the richness of the retrieved context; systems that retrieve incomplete evidence produce lower recall scores, even if their answers are faithful, correct, and relevant.

“Compare the Expected Answer to the Actual Answer. Score from 0.0 to 1.0 how much of the Expected Answer’s core information is present in the Actual Answer. Output ONLY a float number.”

Metric

What it measures

Why it matters

Scoring prompt / logic

Turn Success Rate

The fraction of turns in a conversation that are successfully answered according to the conversational scoring rule.

In multi-turn evaluation, a system may occasionally answer the final question correctly while still making several local errors along the way. Turn Success Rate captures this dialogue-level stability by rewarding systems that remain consistently correct and relevant across the full interaction, rather than only at the final turn. This is especially important in exploratory and analytical workflows, where small early errors may propagate and degrade the rest of the conversation.

For each turn, success is assigned deterministically after the judge scores the answer. A turn is marked successful if the normalized exact match equals 1.0, or if
Correctness

\geq 0.80
, or if
Correctness

\geq 0.70
and
Answer Relevance

\geq 0.70
. The final Turn Success Rate is the average of these binary turn outcomes over all turns in the conversation.

Context Carryover Accuracy

Whether the system correctly preserves the active entity, prior references, and relevant facts across follow-up turns that require memory of earlier dialogue state.

Conversational systems often fail not because they cannot answer isolated questions, but because they lose track of who or what is being discussed. Context Carryover Accuracy measures the agent’s ability to preserve the active person, company, event, or previously identified fact across elliptical follow-up questions such as counting, summarizing, or resolving pronouns. It is therefore a direct measure of conversational continuity.

This metric is computed deterministically over the subset of turns tagged with
context_carryover
,
entity_memory
, or
temporal_memory
. For these turns, the same binary success rule used in Turn Success Rate is applied. Context Carryover Accuracy is then defined as the average success over this memory-sensitive subset only.

Tool Transition Accuracy

Whether the GraphRAG Agent follows the expected structured tool sequence for a given conversational turn.

The graph-based architecture is explicitly designed around a controlled exploratory workflow, in which different tools are used for different semantic intentions. Tool Transition Accuracy therefore measures procedural correctness: not just whether the answer is right, but whether the agent reached it through the intended structured reasoning pathway. This is particularly important for validating the internal state machine and the sequential retrieval logic of the agentic GraphRAG.

This metric is only computed for the agentic GraphRAG. For each turn that specifies an
expected_tool_behavior
, the execution trace is inspected to determine whether at least one of the expected tools was actually called. A hit receives a score of 1.0 and a miss receives 0.0. Tool Transition Accuracy is the average of these binary hits across all turns for which an expected tool behavior is defined.
