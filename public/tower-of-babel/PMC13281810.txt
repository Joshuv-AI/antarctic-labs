Universal diseased-site targeting via glycolysis-driven lactic acid gradient
Authors: Zheng Cao, Xueqing Cheng, Xiulian Lu, Qian He, Qiong Dai, Liyun Zhang, Xiang Zhang, Jeffrey Harding, Shili Xu, R. Michael van Dam, Meiyiming Wang, Yuxi Li, Pei-Yu Chiou, Chaoyong Liu, Jing Wen, Yunfeng Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Targeted delivery of protein therapeutics remains challenging for translating biologics into effective treatments. Here, we introduce a universal strategy leveraging elevated glycolysis, a hallmark of many pathological states, and its resulting extracellular acidification as a navigational cue. Therapeutic proteins are encapsulated within pH-responsive polymer shells that remain near-neutral at physiological pH but gradually gain positive charge under acidic conditions. This dynamic charge modulation allows nanocapsules to sense pH gradients between healthy and diseased tissues, directing them toward pathological sites. Unlike receptor-mediated targeting that operates over nanometer scales, this receptor-independent approach enables long-range targeting. In vivo models of cancer, chronic inflammation, and acute injury demonstrate selective accumulation of encapsulated proteins at diseased sites, enhancing therapeutic efficacy while reducing systemic toxicity. By transforming a ubiquitous metabolic signature into a directional driving force, this lactate acid gradient–mediated targeting (LaGET) platform offers a previously underexplored paradigm for targeted delivery of protein therapeutics.

INTRODUCTION

Conventional drug delivery strategies frequently suffer from off-target effects, poor bioavailability, and insufficient accumulation at sites of pathology, all of which limit clinical efficacy (1–3). In contrast, targeted drug delivery offers a transformative approach by directing therapeutics specifically to diseased tissues, thereby enhancing drug concentration at the site of action while minimizing systemic toxicity (4, 5). This improved spatial precision allows for lower therapeutic doses and reduces collateral damage to healthy tissues. By enhancing localization and specificity, targeted delivery also helps overcome physiological barriers, rendering it particularly effective for conditions such as cancer, chronic inflammation, and acute injury (6–8).

To achieve selective delivery, a range of targeting strategies has been developed, including passive, active, stimuli-responsive, and immune- or cell-based approaches (9–12). Passive targeting has traditionally been explained by the enhanced permeability and retention effect, but emerging work highlights additional mechanisms that contribute to tumor accumulation, including energy-dependent trans-endothelial transport and phagocyte-mediated trafficking and retention (13–15). Active targeting uses functional ligands, such as monoclonal antibodies, peptides, or aptamers that bind selectively to overexpressed receptors on diseased cells, facilitating receptor-mediated uptake (16–18). Stimuli-responsive systems are designed to release payloads in response to environmental cues such as acidic pH, elevated redox potential, or enzymatic activity (19–24). Immune- and cell-based strategies harness the intrinsic homing abilities of immune cells (e.g., engineered T cells and macrophages) or mesenchymal stem cells, which migrate toward sites of inflammation, malignancy, or injury (25, 26). While each of these methods offers specific advantages, their efficacy is often limited by biological heterogeneity, variability in receptor expression, and the complexity of the diseased microenvironment (27, 28).

A unifying feature of many pathological states is elevated glycolysis and the resulting dysregulation of pH homeostasis due to lactic acid over-production (29–32). Under physiological conditions, extracellular pH is tightly maintained near 7.4 via buffering systems, respiratory regulation, and renal function (33). In contrast, pathological tissues, particularly tumors and inflamed sites, exhibit extracellular acidification due to elevated glycolytic flux (34, 35). For example, cancer cells preferentially use aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect), resulting in lactate accumulation and local acidification (29). Likewise, activated immune cells in inflamed tissues up-regulate glycolysis in response to proinflammatory cues, contributing to extracellular acidosis (36–38). These processes establish a pH gradient, with a more acidic core surrounded by near-physiological pH in adjacent tissue, a physicochemical hallmark of many diseased sites but not healthy tissues (39, 40).

We present herein lactate acid gradient–mediated targeting (LaGET) as universal strategy for diseased-site targeting. Biological surfaces, including cell membranes and extracellular matrix components such as phospholipids, glycoproteins, and sulfated proteoglycans, carry net negative charge (41, 42). In pathological tissues, excess protons diffuse outward from the acidic core, forming a continuous pH gradient (39, 40). Drug carriers engineered to remain near-neutral at physiological pH but to acquire increasing positive charge in response to the acidification become progressively protonated as they move inward along this gradient. This leads to a simultaneous increase in electrostatic attraction and a decrease in physical separation between the carrier and negatively charged tissue. The result is a spatially decreased electrochemical free energy and amplified electrostatic interactions that drive the carrier directionally toward the diseased site. In contrast to receptor-ligand strategies (43, 44), which require specific molecular recognition, this mechanism relies solely on physicochemical features shared broadly across pathological environments, namely, extracellular acidity and surface negativity, offering a receptor-independent and generalizable platform for targeted drug delivery.

RESULTS

Development of glycolysis-responsive protein nanocapsules

To validate this concept, we developed a pH-responsive nanocapsule system for targeted delivery of protein therapeutics. As shown in Fig. 1A, N,N-dimethylaminoethyl methacrylate (DMAEMA), poly(ethylene glycol) methyl ether acrylate (PEGMA), and a hydrolyzable cross-linker, polylactic acid diacrylate (PLA), were assembled around individual protein molecules through noncovalent interactions. In situ free-radical polymerization generated a thin polymer shell encapsulating each protein, forming nanocapsules, n(Protein) (45). Using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as a model cargo, the resulting n(BSA) displayed a uniform size of ~20 to 30 nm, as confirmed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 1B and fig. S1) and dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Fig. 1C). The polydispersity index of n(BSA) (0.273) is similar to that of native BSA (0.248), indicating good particle homogeneity and dispersity in physiological environments. The composition and molecular weight of the polymer shell on the protein surface were further examined using 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and gel permeation chromatography (GPC), respectively (figs. S2 and S3A). The successful synthesis of n(BSA) was further validated with x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), where the enhanced peak signal of n(BSA) at 286 eV compared with that of native BSA indicated the presence of PEGMA (C─O─C) and DMAEMA (C─N) in the polymer shell (fig. S3, B and C). Upon exposure to decreasing pH, protonation of the DMAEMA moieties led to a linear increase in the zeta potential of n(BSA) from 1 mV at pH 7.5 to 5.7 mV at pH 5.5 (Fig. 1D), endowing n(BSA) with a pH-responsive surface charge profile. The copolymer of PEGMA and DMAEMA exhibited a comparable pH-dependent increase in zeta potential over the same range (fig. S3D), whereas native BSA remained negatively charged (~−10 mV) (fig. S3E). These trends indicate that the observed pH responsiveness of n(BSA) originates from the DMAEMA-containing polymer shell.

The near-neutral charge of n(Protein) under physiological conditions can support its prolonged systemic circulation. Upon encountering the lactic acid gradient in diseased tissues, progressive protonation enhances the electrostatic interactions between n(Protein) and the negatively charged disease tissues. Such interactions were first quantitatively analyzed using finite element analysis in a physiologically relevant pH environment. Assuming a pH of 5.5 at a diseased site and a proton diffusion coefficient of 9.3 × 10−5 cm2 s−1 in body fluids (46, 47), the analysis revealed the formation of a continuous pH gradient extending from 5.5 at the pathological core to 7.4 in surrounding tissue over a distance of ~4 mm (Fig. 1E and fig. S4, A and B). This spatial range substantially exceeds the characteristic dimensions of mammalian cells (10 to 30 μm) and capillaries (5 to 10 μm), offering a physiochemical landscape that drug carriers can navigate.

Within this pH gradient, n(Protein) exhibited a progressive increase in zeta potential from ~1 to ~6 mV, consistent with protonation of the N,N-dimethylaminoethyl groups in the shells (Fig. 1F). This charge modulation led to a substantial amplification of electrostatic interactions with negatively charged diseased tissues. When simplifying such interactions without considering the complicated biological features of extracellular matrix, the attractive electrostatic force acting on n(Protein) increased exponentially near the acidic core (fig. S4C), while the interaction force with healthy tissue remained relatively constant and markedly weaker than the diseased site. As a result, n(Protein) was preferentially propelled along the pH gradient, leading to significantly enhanced accumulation at the diseased site relative to surrounding healthy tissue (Fig. 1G and fig. S4D). Similar results were also observed when assessing the targeting process at capillary-scale resolution, in which the pH gradient was confined within a 200-μm distance (48), reflecting the typical separation between capillaries and adjacent pathological tissues (fig. S4, E to H). These results quantitatively demonstrate that spatially increasing electrostatic interactions, arising from a pH-responsive surface charge regulation, effectively direct n(Protein) migration toward pathological sites.

To further rationalize this behavior under realistic biological conditions, where factors like steric confinement and osmotic partitioning can modulate n(Protein) migration, we formulated a free-energy model in which n(Protein) experiences an electrochemical free-energy landscape G(r) coupled to the local pH field pH(r). For a free-energy function G(r,pH), its spatial gradient follows the multivariable chain rule∇G(r)=(∂G∂r)pH+(∂G∂pH)r∇pH(r)

Here, (∂G∂r)pH represents the pH-independent spatial contributions, such as steric confinement and Donnan partitioning, supporting the passive retention of n(Protein) in the absence of a pH gradient (49). In contrast, the second term captures the pH gradient-coupled charge regulation of n(Protein), which introduces a directional bias wherever ∇pH(r)≠0. We then parameterized the pH dependence through the effective surface charge of n(Protein), using zeta potential ζ=ζ(pH) as an experimentally accessible proxy(∂G∂pH)r=(∂G∂ζ)r∂ζ∂pH

In tissue environments (negatively charged), increasing positive ζ is expected to lower the free energy of local partitioning and association, implying (∂G∂ζ)r<0, consistent with Donnan-type enrichment and strengthened nanoparticle-tissue interactions (50). Experimentally, ζ increases monotonically as pH decreases within our measured pH range (Fig. 1D), giving ∂ζ∂pH<0 (linear fit slope ≈ −2.2). Together, these relations yield (∂G∂pH)r>0, such that decreasing pH lowers the electrochemical free energy. Consequently, the pH-gradient term provides a thermodynamic driving force that biases n(Protein) migration and retention toward decreased sites with lower pH. As illustrated in Fig. 1H, the lactic acid–driven pH gradient progressively increases the surface charge of n(Protein) as it approaches the acidic core, which steepens the free energy landscape (Fig. 1I) and promotes both the migration and retention at diseased sites.

This LaGET mechanism is inherently generalizable and differs from the current targeting strategies (the passive, active, stimuli-responsive, and immune- or cell-based approaches), providing a precise and efficient strategy for drug delivery across a wide range of diseased microenvironments. By harnessing such intrinsic biochemical gradients, this approach provides a broadly applicable and scalable strategy for targeted delivery to tumors, inflammatory lesions, acute injury, and other sites of metabolic dysfunction.

n(Protein) targets elevated glycolysis in tumor cells and spheroids

We first investigated the responsiveness of n(Protein) to elevated glycolysis, an essential characteristic of tumor cells, in tumor cells. As a model system, 4T1 murine breast cancer cells were incubated with either free BSA or n(BSA), both labeled with carboxytetramethylrhodamine (TAMRA). To mimic the acidic conditions of the tumor microenvironment induced by the Warburg effect, lactic acid was added to the culture medium at concentrations of 5, 10, or 15 mM, reducing the pH from 7.4 to 7.1, 6.8, and 6.5, respectively (fig. S5A). Cells treated with free BSA displayed minimal fluorescence across all conditions (Fig. 2A). In contrast, n(BSA)-treated cells exhibited strong fluorescence intensifying with increasing lactic acid concentrations, indicating enhanced uptake under increasingly acidic conditions (Fig. 2B). These results confirm that n(BSA) undergoes protonation in response to lactic acid–induced acidification, resulting in elevated surface charge and enhanced cellular internalization by strengthening electrostatic interactions with negatively charged cell membrane. To validate the role of the N,N-dimethylaminoethyl moieties in this response, 4T1 cells were incubated with n(BSA)–poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG), nanocapsules synthesized from PEGMA and PLA but lacking DMAEMA under similar condition. The cells with n(BSA)-PEG exhibited negligible fluorescence across all pH conditions (fig. S5B), confirming that the glycolysis-responsive behavior of n(BSA) is mediated by the N,N-dimethylaminoethyl moieties. No notable differences in cell viability were observed between n(BSA)- and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)–treated cells, supporting the biocompatibility of n(Protein) (fig. S5C).

We then assessed the responsiveness of n(Protein) to elevated glycolysis in a 4T1 tumor spheroid model that recapitulates the lactic acid–rich microenvironment characteristic of solid tumors (fig. S6A). Consistent with the results from monolayer cultures, spheroids treated with free BSA exhibited minimal fluorescence, whereas those treated with n(BSA) displayed markedly enhanced fluorescence throughout the spheroid, indicating effective penetration and accumulation of n(BSA) (fig. S6B). Flow cytometry analysis corroborated these findings, revealing high fluorescence intensity in cells isolated from both the periphery and core of n(BSA)-treated spheroids, while fluorescence in BSA-treated spheroids remained negligible (fig. S6, C to E). Meanwhile, it was found that the cells in n(BSA)-treated spheroid showed more intense fluorescence in the periphery than in the core, collectively confirming that n(BSA) not only efficiently penetrated through and accumulated within the tumor spheroids but also effectively entered the tumor cells.

Furthermore, to assess the ability of n(Protein) to extravasate, penetrate through, and accumulate within tumor-like tissues, tumor spheroids were embedded in microfluidic chips. Culture medium containing either n(BSA) or free BSA was perfused under controlled flow conditions to maintain the integrity and sustain the microenvironment of the embedded spheroids (Fig. 2C and fig. S7, A and B). Spheroids treated with free BSA exhibited weak fluorescence after 24 hours, consistent with limited uptake observed in static culture. In contrast, n(BSA)-treated spheroids showed markedly higher fluorescence intensity as early as 6 hours, particularly along the spheroid periphery, indicating effective initial penetration. By 24 hours, fluorescence was significantly enhanced throughout the spheroid, suggesting robust penetration and accumulation of n(BSA) within the tumor model (Fig. 2D). This progressive enrichment implies that gradient-driven electrostatic interactions not only navigated n(BSA) migration toward the spheroid but also promoted its penetration through the spheroid, in response to the outward diffusion of protons excessively produced by the tumor cells.

We next evaluated the controlled release of n(Protein) in response to acidic tumor microenvironment. Rituximab (RTX), the first US Food and Drug Administration–approved monoclonal antibody for cancer treatment and a clinically standard of care for multiple B cell malignancies, was selected as a model therapeutic protein for nanocapsule formulation [n(RTX)]. RTX and n(RTX) were incubated in buffers at pH 6.0 and pH 7.4, representing the acidic tumor microenvironment and physiological conditions, respectively (51). Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay measurements revealed sustained release of RTX over 7 days under both conditions, with a consistently higher release rate at pH 6.0, attributed to accelerated hydrolysis of the PLA cross-linkers under acidic conditions (Fig. 2E).

Last, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of RTX released from n(RTX) against tumor cells. Under conventional two-dimensional (2D) culture conditions, native RTX and n(RTX) exhibited comparable cytotoxicity against 2F7 cells, a CD20+ human B cell non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (NHL) cell line (fig. S7C). The therapeutic efficacy was further assessed in 3D tumor spheroids derived from 2F7 cells. The spheroids were treated with either free RTX or n(RTX), and both the treatments suppressed the growth of the spheroids to a similar extent, indicating that as-released RTX remained biologically active (Fig. 2, F and G). Notably, n(RTX) induced significantly greater cell death, with pronounced damage observed in the spheroid core, in contrast to free RTX (Fig. 2F). The enhanced cytotoxicity observed is attributable to improved penetration and accumulation of n(RTX) within the spheroid, combined with pH-triggered release and enhanced cellular uptake, collectively demonstrating the therapeutic advantage of targeted delivery via pH-responsive nanocapsules. In summary, these in vitro studies demonstrate that n(Protein) effectively responds to glycolysis-induced acidification in the tumor microenvironment, affording controlled release of protein therapeutics, enhanced tumor penetration, increased cellular uptake, and improved therapeutic efficacy.

n(Protein) targets elevated glycolysis-associated microenvironment in tumors, chronic inflammation, and acute injury

We first evaluated the tumor targeting capability of n(Protein) in a murine model of triple-negative breast cancer. The model was established by subcutaneous injection of 4T1 cells (5 × 105) into the right flank of BALB/c mice. One week after engraftment, Alexa Fluor 680–labeled BSA or n(BSA) (5 mg/kg) was intravenously administered, and biodistribution was monitored using longitudinal in vivo fluorescence imaging.

Compared with free BSA, which displayed a serum half-life of 5.2 hours, n(BSA) exhibited a markedly prolonged half-life of 29.2 hours, attributed to its near-neutral surface charge and PEGylated shell. This extended circulation led to a fourfold increase in overall drug exposure, with area under the curve rising from 1292 to 4640 (Fig. 3A). While BSA showed negligible tumor accumulation, n(BSA) generated detectable tumor fluorescence within 30 min postinjection, with intensity steadily increasing over 48 hours (Fig. 3B). Quantification confirmed time-dependent accumulation, peaking at 24 hours and remaining elevated thereafter (Fig. 3C). Ex vivo imaging with an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) of the major organs harvested at 48 hours postinjection further confirmed enhanced accumulation of n(BSA) in tumors. BSA-treated mice exhibited minimal signal in all tissues, including tumors, due to rapid clearance (Fig. 3D). In contrast, n(BSA)-treated mice displayed strong fluorescence in tumors and moderate signal in liver and spleen. Quantitative analysis revealed that n(BSA) achieved 15.1-fold higher tumor accumulation compared with BSA and modest increases in liver (3.6-fold), spleen (2.6-fold), kidney (2.5-fold), and heart (2.1-fold) (Fig. 3E). Notably, tumor accumulation exceeded hepatic uptake, a primary site of nanoparticle clearance, highlighting the effective tumor-targeting capacity of n(BSA).

In addition, to further probe the role of DMAEMA moieties in the in vivo targeting, we synthesized a positively charged, non–acid-responsive control nanocapsule, n(BSA)-ns, incorporating the permanently cationic monomer N-(3-aminopropyl)methacrylamide hydrochloride (APm), which remains charged under physiological pH. In vivo, n(BSA)-ns showed reduced tumor accumulation and increased liver uptake, particularly during the first 6 hours postinjection (fig. S8, A and B), confirming that the pH-dependent protonation of DMAEMA is critical for tumor-specific targeting while minimizing off-target accumulation in the reticuloendothelial system.

For further quantification, BSA and n(BSA) were radiolabeled with zirconium-89 (89Zr) and intravenously injected into 4T1 tumor-bearing mice, followed by positron emission tomography (PET)/computed tomography (CT) imaging over 48 hours (fig. S9A). 89Zr-labeling is a strategy commonly used in radiolabeling of protein therapeutics for translational and clinical studies (52–54). All PET images were decay corrected for the radioactive half-life of 89Zr. In mice treated with BSA-89Zr, the 89Zr signals rapidly accumulated in the liver and remained absent from tumors. In contrast, mice treated with n(BSA-89Zr) exhibited a progressive increase in tumor uptake, while the signal in the cardiac and hepatic regions predominantly reflected intravascular blood pool activity, consistent with prolonged systemic circulation and reduced hepatic retention (Fig. 4A). Quantitative analysis revealed that tumor signal in n(BSA-89Zr)–treated mice increased from 2% percent injected dose per cubic centimeter (ID/cc) at 30 min to ~20% ID/cc at 48 hours, representing over a 10-fold enhancement compared with that in BSA-89Zr (<2% ID/cc throughout) (Fig. 4B). Consistent with reduced nonspecific uptake, n(BSA-89Zr)–treated mice showed substantially lower hepatic accumulation compared with BSA-89Zr–treated mice. Liver signal in the BSA-89Zr group peaked at 50% ID/cc at 2 hours and remained elevated at 43% ID/cc at 48 hours. In contrast, n(BSA-89Zr) showed only 22 and 26% ID/cc at the corresponding time points (Fig. 4C). Ex vivo biodistribution corroborated these findings: n(BSA-89Zr) achieved 25% ID/g in tumors versus 1% ID/g for BSA-89Zr, a ~25-fold improvement, while liver uptake was halved (35% ID/g versus 80% ID/g) (Fig. 4D). Other major organs, including the heart, lungs, kidney, and spleen, exhibited comparable levels between the two groups (Fig. 4D and fig. S9, B and C), confirming the tumor specificity of n(BSA-89Zr).

We next investigated whether this strategy could extend to nontumor pathologies. We first examined the ability of n(Protein) to target chronic inflammation sites in a collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) rat model of rheumatoid arthritis, a chronic autoimmune disorder characterized by persistent joint inflammation, synovial hyperplasia, and progressive cartilage and bone destruction. Sprague-Dawley rats were immunized with bovine type II collagen and Freund’s adjuvant on days 0 and 7 and successful model establishment was confirmed by arthritis index scoring. To evaluate the targeting capability of n(Protein), tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α) inhibitor (TNFi), a standard clinical therapy for arthritis patients (55), was selected as a model therapeutic. On day 14, Cy7-labeled TNFi or n(TNFi) (4 mg/kg) was administered intravenously. While free TNFi showed only mild fluorescence at inflamed joints, n(TNFi) produced markedly stronger signals, with ~1.3- and 2.4-folds increase at 3 and 20 hours postinjection, respectively (Fig. 4, E and F, and fig. S10A). Notably, the injection sites of collagen and adjuvant also exhibited strong signals due to associated injection injury, indicating the ability of n(Protein) to target injury sites.

We then evaluated the ability of n(Protein) to target acute injury sites in a muscle injury model and a spinal cord injury model. In the muscle injury model, mice received a right thigh incision followed by intravenous injection of Cyanine 5 (Cy5)-labeled BSA or n(BSA) 2 hours postsurgery. Fluorescence imaging at 1 hour postinjection showed over twofold higher fluorescence intensity at the injury site in n(BSA)-treated mice versus controls (Fig. 4G and fig. S10B). In the spinal cord injury model, mechanical trauma was induced at the thoracic (T9) segment, followed by tail vein injection of Cy5-labeled BSA or n(BSA) 2 hours postinjury. Fluorescence imaging revealed weak and transient signal in BSA-treated mice, whereas n(BSA) produced strong, sustained fluorescence that peaked at 12 hours and remained elevated at 24 hours, showing three- and twofold higher intensity, respectively, than BSA (Fig. 4, H to J, and fig. S10C).

Together, these in vivo studies demonstrate that n(Protein) achieves effective and selective accumulation in tumors, sites of chronic inflammation, and acute injury while reducing nonspecific uptake by the liver and other major organs. Notably, this glycolysis-targeted platform consistently achieves tumor accumulation exceeding 20% ID/g, surpassing the efficiency of many current active targeting systems such as monoclonal antibodies and antibody-drug conjugates, which typically reach 5 to 20% ID/g (56, 57), highlighting its potential as a universal and high-efficiency targeting strategy.

n(Protein) improves therapeutic efficacy and reduces adverse effects for solid and metastatic tumor treatment

Immune checkpoint inhibitors such as anti–programmed death protein 1 (aPD-1) have transformed cancer therapy by activating endogenous immune responses to eliminate tumor cells (58). However, their clinical use is frequently complicated by immune-related adverse events (irAEs), including pneumonitis, colitis, and hepatitis, due to nonspecific immune activation in healthy organs such as the lungs, liver, and gastrointestinal tract (59, 60). Building upon the ability of n(Protein) to selectively accumulate in diseased tissues via gradient-driven electrostatic interactions, we investigated here whether this targeting mechanism could translate into improved therapeutic efficacy and reduced systemic toxicity in vivo in both solid and metastatic tumor models.

The efficacy of n(Protein) for treatment of solid tumor was evaluated in the triple-negative breast cancer model. Mice with 4T1 murine breast tumor were intravenously administrated with free aPD-1 or nanocapsules of aPD-1 [n(aPD-1)] (150 μg per mouse), in combination with intraperitoneal administration of doxorubicin (DOX; 5 mg/kg), on days 7 and 14 postengraftment. Compared with PBS controls, both aPD-1 and DOX monotherapy moderately suppressed tumor growth, while their combination (aPD-1/DOX) provided greater inhibition. Notably, n(aPD-1) monotherapy outperformed free aPD-1, achieving tumor suppression comparable with that of aPD-1/DOX. The most pronounced effect was observed in the n(aPD-1)/DOX group, which maintained tumor volumes below 160 mm3 throughout the study (Fig. 5, A to C, and fig. S11A). Immunohistochemical staining of Ki67, a marker of tumor cell proliferation, revealed decreased proliferative activity in tumors from n(aPD-1)–treated mice relative to aPD-1 or PBS groups. This reduction was further enhanced in combination treatments, with the n(aPD-1)/DOX group exhibiting the lowest Ki67 expression among all treatment groups (fig. S11B). These findings highlight the enhanced therapeutic potential of glycolysis-targeted immunotherapy both as a monotherapy and in combination with chemotherapy.

Besides the improved efficacy, RNA sequencing was also performed for lung tissue, one of the organs most frequently affected by irAEs, to assess whether the improved targeting of n(aPD-1) also mitigated systemic immune toxicity. Treatment with free aPD-1 significantly altered gene expression in the lung, affecting 543 transcripts and activating inflammatory pathways associated with leukocyte migration, neutrophil-mediated immunity, and reactive oxygen species production (Fig. 5, D and E). Furthermore, phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase–Akt and Ras signaling pathways, which are implicated in lung inflammation and closely associated with patients with irAEs after cancer treatment (61), were up-regulated in the aPD-1 group (Fig. 5F). In contrast, n(aPD-1)–treated mice exhibited minimal gene expression changes (87 genes) and did not activate these inflammatory pathways (fig. S12, A to C), indicating reduced off-target immune activation and improved safety profile.

The efficacy of n(Protein) for treatment of metastatic tumors was evaluated in a human B cell NHL model, which was constructed by intravenously injecting luciferase-expressing 2F7 cells (2 × 106) in immunodeficient NOD/SCID/IL-2Rγnull (NSG) mice. 2F7 cells are a human CD20+ B cell lymphoma cell line known for its aggressive growth and metastatic dissemination in vivo, allowing longitudinal tracking of tumor burden via bioluminescence imaging. Mice were treated with PBS, free RTX, or n(RTX) (5 mg/kg) on weeks 1, 2, and 3 postengraftment (fig. S13A). PBS-treated mice exhibited rapid disease progression, with a survival rate of 20% by day 27 (Fig. 6, A and B). Free RTX treatment modestly delayed tumor growth and extended survival to day 33 in 20% of mice. In contrast, n(RTX) induced potent tumor suppression, with all treated mice surviving to day 35 and showing markedly reduced bioluminescence signals.

Besides the enhanced tumor suppression, metastatic burden in major organs was examined by flow cytometry analysis. Free RTX significantly reduced tumor load in the spleen and liver but was largely ineffective in controlling tumor dissemination to the lungs, kidneys, and brain (Fig. 6C and fig. S13, B and C). In contrast, treatment with n(RTX) led to a substantial reduction in tumor burden across all analyzed organs, demonstrating broad and effective suppression of metastatic spread. Histological examination further corroborated these findings. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of kidney sections from PBS-treated mice revealed extensive tumor infiltration, while kidneys from RTX-treated mice showed residual disease with multiple tumor foci (Fig. 6D and fig. S13D). Notably, n(RTX)-treated mice exhibited only a single, small residual lesion, underscoring the enhanced therapeutic efficacy of the glycolysis-responsive formulation. Collectively, these results demonstrate that although RTX can partially limit metastatic progression, it fails to provide comprehensive control across multiple organs. In contrast, n(RTX) achieves significantly superior tumor suppression, markedly reducing metastatic burden in both lymphoid and nonlymphoid tissues and extending overall survival in this aggressive model of systemic lymphoma. These in vivo results collectively underscore the therapeutic advantages of this delivery strategy, which not only provides improved therapeutic efficacy but also reduced immune-related toxicity in healthy organs, as demonstrated in both solid and metastatic tumors model.

In addition to therapeutic efficacy, we systemically evaluated the biocompatibility of n(aPD-1) and n(RTX), together with their non–acid-responsive controls [n(aPD-1)-ns and n(RTX)-ns]. Notably, the treatment of aPD-1, n(aPD-1)-ns, and n(aPD-1) at 150 μg per mouse did not cause detectable body-weight loss over the 2-week observation period (Fig. 7, A and B, and fig. S14A). Liver and kidney functions were further assessed by serum biochemistry. At 24 hours postinjection, alanine transaminase (ALT; Fig. 7C and fig. S14B), aspartate transaminase (AST; Fig. 7D and fig. S14C), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN; fig. S14D) remained comparable with those in the PBS group, indicating no acute hepatic or renal impairment. These markers also remained stable at 2 weeks postinjection (Fig. 7, E and F; and fig. S14, E to H). Consistently, H&E staining of major organs showed no histopathological abnormalities relative to the PBS-treated controls (Fig. 7G and fig. S14I), supporting the favorable biocompatibility of n(aPD-1).

To further probe the safety margin of n(Protein), we performed a dose-ranging toxicity using RTX. Mice intravenously received RTX, n(RTX)-ns, or n(RTX) at 10, 20, 40, or 100 mg/kg (Fig. 7H), corresponding to human equivalent doses of ~30, 60, 120, or 300 mg/m2, respectively (62). Notably, the highest dose is comparable to the clinical dose used for treating B cell hematologic malignancy (63). Across all dose levels and formulations, animals maintained stable body weight over 2 weeks, with no observable signs of distress (Fig. 7I and fig. S15A). Serum ALT, AST, and BUN measured 24 hours after dosing showed negligible changes relative to PBS controls (Fig. 7, J to L; and fig. S15, B to E), suggesting the absence of acute liver or kidney toxicity at all tested doses. At 2 weeks postinjection, biomarkers, including ALT, AST, BUN, and creatinine, remained comparable across groups (Fig. 7, M to P, and fig. S16, A to D), and H&E staining of major organs revealed no apparent tissue injury or inflammation at any dose (fig. S17). Collectively, these results indicate that n(Protein) is well tolerated up to 100 mg/kg, substantially above commonly used preclinical dosing of RTX and many other protein therapeutics. This safety profile should arise from the time-dependent biodegradation of the polymer shell and systemic clearance of resulting fragments (64). Future work could further define the upper tolerated dose in settings where unusually high dosing is required.

DISCUSSION

Targeted delivery of therapeutics remains a central challenge in drug development, particularly for protein-based therapeutics such as monoclonal antibodies, cytokines, and enzymes (65, 66). Despite advances in ligand-based and stimuli-responsive delivery systems, many approaches are limited by receptor heterogeneity, immune clearance, and lack of universal applicability across disease types. In this study, we report a glycolysis-responsive platform that leverages pathological acidification to enable long-range, gradient-driven targeting of diseased tissues. This strategy exploits the widespread feature of extracellular acidification in tumors, chronic inflammation, and acute injury, arising from enhanced glycolytic metabolism, as a universal physicochemical cue for selective accumulation of therapeutic proteins.

Unlike conventional targeting strategies that rely on short-range interactions, such as antigen-antibody binding or receptor-ligand recognition, our approach leverages gradient-coupled electrostatic partitioning to promote delivery. Short-range interactions typically operate over nanometer distances (1 to 10 nm) and therefore require direct proximity between the carrier and its cognate binding partner. In vivo, these interactions can be constrained by the heterogeneity and spatial distribution of receptors, limited tissue penetration, and competitive inhibition in complex biological environments (67, 68). By contrast, the targeting mechanism in this study is mediated by a tissue-scale pH field and therefore operates over micrometer to millimeter length scales. Finite element analysis revealed that, in response to a physiologically relevant pH gradient from pH 7.4 in surrounding tissue to pH 5.5 at diseased site, n(Protein) exhibit a continuous increase in surface charge. This monotonic increase in surface potential toward diseased tissues strengthens interactions with the negatively charged tissue matrix. From a thermodynamic perspective, coupling between charge regulation and the pH gradient generates an electrochemical free energy landscape in which the pH-dependent energy decreases toward more acidic regions, providing a directional driving force that biases migration and enhances local retention.

Mechanistically, this strategy transforms a conserved metabolic abnormality, extracellular acidification due to up-regulated glycolysis, into a directional targeting force. The n(Protein), formed by encapsulating individual protein molecules in thin, pH-responsive polymer shells, remains near-neutral under physiological pH to ensure long circulation. Upon entering acidic microenvironments, shell protonation increases surface charge, generating spatially intensified electrostatic interactions that actively guides the n(Protein) toward diseased tissues rich in anionic extracellular matrix components such as sulfated proteoglycans, glycoproteins, and phospholipids. Compared with traditional antibody-drug conjugates or ligand-functionalized nanocarriers, which typically achieve 5 to 20% ID/g tumor accumulation and rely on specific short-range interactions, the n(Protein) system consistently achieved higher accumulation across disease models. The receptor-independent, physicochemical mechanism of targeting allows the platform to bypass biological variability in surface markers and to dynamically respond to spatial gradients that are conserved across a broad spectrum of pathological conditions. In parallel, several pH-activated delivery strategies have leveraged tumor glycolysis-driven acidification to gate local payload release and thereby enhance efficacy while reducing systemic exposure, including pH-responsive cytokine formats using ionomers (23, 24). Conceptually, these approaches use tumor acidity as an on-site activation switch that modulates drug availability once the carrier has reached the tissue. By contrast, our platform uses the acidity of different diseased sites as the metabolic cue to create a tissue-scale targeting force through charge modulation and electrostatic partitioning.

The effectiveness of this targeting mechanism was validated across multiple in vitro and in vivo models. In solid tumors, n(Protein) showed significantly enhanced accumulation and tissue penetration relative to free proteins. In a metastatic lymphoma model, n(Protein) achieved broad reduction of tumor burden across lymphoid and nonlymphoid organs, including lungs, kidneys, and brain—tissues typically inaccessible to free antibodies. When loaded with therapeutic agents such as aPD-1 or RTX, these nanocapsules achieved superior tumor suppression, prolonged survival, and reduced systemic toxicity. Notably, RNA sequencing of lung tissues demonstrated that n(aPD-1) mitigated immune-related inflammatory responses associated with checkpoint blockade, by restricting drug activity to acidic diseased sites and sparing healthy tissues.

Overall, this study introduces a fundamentally different approach to targeted delivery, one that shifts from proximity-dependent molecular recognition to long-range, gradient-guided navigation. Glycolysis-responsive n(Protein) exploits disease-induced extracellular acidification to generate directional electrostatic forces that operate over biologically relevant distances, enabling efficient and selective delivery of protein therapeutics. This universal mechanism overcomes key barriers in nanomedicine, including limited targeting depth, off-target toxicity, and biomarker heterogeneity (69, 70). Future work will extend this platform to additional protein modalities and validate targeting, effectiveness, and safety in large-animal models. In parallel, integrating gradient-guided targeting with orthogonal strategies, such as pH-triggered release or ligand-based targeting may further improve targeting efficiency and therapeutic index. By harnessing conserved physicochemical gradients rather than molecular identity, this work establishes a distinct paradigm for precision drug delivery.

In summary, this work presents a universal strategy for targeted delivery of therapeutics based on gradient-driven electrostatic interactions that exploit extracellular acidification, a conserved hallmark of diseased tissues. Therapeutic proteins are individually encapsulated within thin, pH-responsive polymer shells to form n(Protein) that remain near-neutral in circulation but become increasingly positively charged in acidic environments. As they traverse the endogenous pH gradient surrounding pathological sites, these n(Protein) experience a spatially intensified force that actively directs their migration, penetration, and retention in diseased tissues. This receptor-independent mechanism enables precise delivery across a broad range of pathological conditions, including solid tumors, metastatic cancers, chronic inflammation, and acute injuries, while enhancing therapeutic efficacy and minimizing systemic toxicity. By harnessing conserved metabolic cues and physicochemical gradients, this platform offers a broadly applicable approach for safe and effective protein-based therapies.

Synthesis and characterization of nanocapsules

To prepare BSA nanocapsules [n(BSA)], BSA was dissolved in 1× PBS at a concentration of about 20 mg/ml and dialyzed overnight at 4°C to remove the residual inorganic salts. The concentration of BSA was measured by its absorption at 280 nm. BSA was first conjugated with acrylate moieties by reacting with NAS (20:1, n/n, NAS:BSA) at 4°C for 2 hours and dialyzed overnight against PBS at 4°C. Subsequently, PEGMA (5100:1, n/n, PEGMA:BSA), DMAEMA (900:1, n/n, DMAEMA:BSA), and BIS (600:1, n/n, BIS:BSA) were added to the BSA solution. The in situ polymerization was initiated with APS (600:1, n/n, APS:BSA) and TEMED (2:1, w/w, TEMED:APS). The reaction mixture was kept at 4°C for 2 hours and dialyzed in Hepes buffer (25 mM, pH 7.5) overnight to remove the unreacted molecules. n(BSA) was then purified with an anion exchange column (Q-Sepharose) using Hepes buffer (25 mM, pH 7.5) as the elution buffer. To prepare n(BSA)-PEG, the DMAEMA monomer was replaced with equal molar ratio of PEGMA. For imaging experiments, APm was added to n(BSA) at low molar ratio (50:1, n/n, APm:BSA) for the conjugation of fluorescent dyes. To prepare TNFi nanocapsules [n(TNFi)], PEGMA (24,000:1, n/n, PEGMA:TNFi), DMAEMA (4000:1, n/n, DMAEMA:TNFi), APm (240:1, n/n, APm:TNFi), and BIS (2800:1, n/n, BIS:TNFi) were added to the solution. The in situ polymerization was initiated with APS (2800:1, n/n, APS:TNFi) and TEMED (2:1, w/w, TEMED:APS). The reaction mixture was kept at 4°C for 2 hours and dialyzed in 1× PBS overnight to remove the unreacted molecules. n(TNFi) was then purified with a Phenyl Sepharose CL-4B column (Cytiva). To prepare aPD-L1 or RTX nanocapsules, antibodies were used as received. PEGMA (10200:1, n/n, PEGMA:antibody), DMAEMA (1800:1, n/n, DMAEMA:antibody), GDMA (600:1, n/n, GDMA:antibody), and PLA-PEG-PLA diacrylate (600:1, n/n, PLA-PEG-PLA:antibody) were added to the antibody solution. The in situ polymerization was initiated with APS (1000:1, n/n, APS:BSA) and TEMED (2:1, w/w, TEMED:APS). To synthesize non–pH-responsive (ns) nanocapsules as a control, DMAEMA was replaced with equal molar ratio of APm. The reaction mixture was kept at 4°C for 2 hours and dialyzed in 1× PBS overnight to remove the unreacted molecules. aPD-L1 or RTX nanocapsules were then purified with a Phenyl Sepharose CL-4B column. For NMR characterization, the PEGMA/DMAEMA/PLA-PEG-PLA copolymer was synthesized using the same procedure as for nanocapsule preparation, but without proteins. All reagents were dissolved in D2O for NMR measurements. After 2 hours of polymerization, the reaction mixture was purified with a Zeba spin desalting column (7K molecular weight cutoff), and the eluent was used for the 1H NMR study. For GPC characterization, the PEGMA/DMAEMA/PLA-PEG-PLA copolymer was synthesized using the same procedure as for NMR. After 2 hours of polymerization, the solution was purified with a Zeba spin desalting column (7K molecular weight cutoff). To measure the molecular weight of polymer chains, the solution pH was adjusted to 1.0 with hydrochloric acid to degrade the cross-linker, and the resulting copolymer was freeze-dried prior to GPC analysis. The XPS and GPC characterization was performed by eceshi (test IDs: 2601272771 and 2602038530).

Detection of cell-nanocapsule interactions in 4T1 cells

To investigate the cell-nanocapsule interactions between 4T1 cells and n(BSA), BSA was first conjugated with TAMRA for flow cytometry tracking. 4T1 cells were seeded at 20,000 cells per well in a 24-well plate and cultured overnight. Native BSA and nanocapsules were then added to the culture medium at 5 μg/ml in the presence of different l-lactic acid concentrations (0, 5, 10, and 15 mM). The cells were incubated with different samples for 4 hours, and the cell-nanocapsule interactions were measured with flow cytometry. For fluorescent microscope imaging, cells treated with different samples were washed twice with 1× PBS, and the cell-nanocapsule interactions were evaluated with fluorescent microscope.

Nanocapsule accumulation in 3D tumor spheroids

To prepare 3D tumor spheroids of 4T1 cells, 50 μl of 2% agarose was added to each well of a 96-well plate, and the plate was stored at 4°C for 12 hours. 4T1 cells were seeded at 5000 cells per well in the agarose-coated plate, and the plate was then centrifuged at 1000g for 10 min. Following the centrifugation, Matrigel was added to the medium at 1% (v/v), and the spheroids were cultured for 3 days before use. 3D tumor spheroids of 2F7 cells were prepared with the same methods (seeded at 10,000 cells per well).

To test the nanocapsule penetration in 3D tumor spheroids, n(BSA) or native BSA and other nanocapsule samples were added into the culture at 10 μg/ml. The samples were incubated with spheroids for 4 hours, followed by fluorescent imaging with microscope or flow cytometry analysis of nanocapsules. To test the cytotoxicity of RTX or n(RTX) in 2F7 spheroids, RTX or n(RTX) was added to the spheroids at 5 μg/ml. The samples were incubated for 48 hours, followed by fluorescent imaging with microscope to track damage to spheroids. For the coincubation experiments of 2F7 spheroids and human PBMCs, 1 × 106 PBMCs were added to the spheroids with RTX or n(RTX) and incubated for 48 hours. To test the cytokine expression level, cell activation cocktail (BioLegend) was added to the cultures and incubated for 4 hours, followed by the staining of proinflammatory cytokines.

To evaluate nanocapsule interactions with tumor spheroids in the microfluidic chip, the microfluidic chip was developed. To fabricate the microfluidic channel chip, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) (Sylgard 184, 10:1 base-to-curing agent) was mixed, stirred for 5 min to ensure homogeneity, and degassed in a vacuum desiccator for 30 min to remove trapped air bubbles. An antiadhesion coating [trichloro(1H,1H,2H,2H-perfluorooctyl)silane] was applied to the AggreWell800 (STEMCELL Technologies) before pouring the PDMS mixture to ease demolding. After curing at 70°C for 8 hours, the PDMS was peeled off to obtain an inverse soft mold, which was cut into 8 mm–by–8 mm square pieces. These pieces were coated with an additional antiadhesion layer to prevent bonding with subsequent layers and covered with a second PDMS pour. The second layer was also cured at 70°C for 8 hours, followed by peeling to yield soft PDMS AggreWell800 replicas with an 8 mm–by–8 mm pattern for cell culturing. A microfluidic channel mold was created using a transparent acrylic sheet cut by a CO2 laser with a 2-mm-wide, 1-mm-high channel and a 1 cm–by–1 cm middle area for alignment with the AggreWell800 features. Degassed PDMS was poured into this mold, cured at 70°C for 8 hours. After peeling off, inlet and outlet holes were punched carefully to ensure proper alignment and flow control. The PDMS layers were then oxygen plasma treated for 60 s and bonded together with alignment. The assembled chip was baked at 70°C for 1 hour to strengthen the bond. The final device was tested for fluidic integrity using dyed water to visualize flow paths and was sterilized via autoclaving or ethanol treatment followed by rinsing with deionized water to ensure biocompatibility. 4T1 tumor spheroids were cultured in agarose-coated plate for 3 days before transferring into the chip using a 1-ml syringe. The chip was incubated overnight before using for experiments. n(BSA) or BSA sample was diluted to 10 μg/ml using cell culture medium, and the medium was then flowed through the chip at 15 μl/min for 24 hours. The accumulation of n(BSA) or BSA in tumor spheroids at different time points was then evaluated by fluorescent imaging with microscope.

PET/CT imaging experiments

To engraft the animal model, 5 × 105 4T1 cells were injected to the right flank of female BALB/c mice (8 weeks old). To prepare 89Zr-labeled BSA (BSA-89Zr) or (n(BSA)-89Zr), BSA was first labeled with 1-(4-isothiocyanatophenyl)-3-[6,17-dihydroxy-7,10,18,21-tetraoxo-27-(N-acetylhydroxylamino)-6,11,17, 22-tetraazaheptaeicosine] thiourea (p-SCN-Bn-DFO) (5:1, n/n, p-SCN-Bn-DFO:BSA) for 1 hour at room temperature. n(BSA) was then synthesized using the same recipe discussed above. 89Zr-oxalate was obtained from 3D Imaging LLC. On arrival, 89Zr-oxalate was diluted with 40% (v/v) 2 M Na2CO3 and allowed to incubate for 3 min. The activity was then diluted 2.5 times with 1 M N-(2-hydroxyethyl)piperazine-N-(2-ethanesulfonic acid) (pH 7.0). BSA-89Zr and n(BSA)-89Zr were then prepared by incubating DFO-conjugated BSA or n(BSA) with 89Zr-oxalate for 1 hour at room temperature at about 148 to 185 kilobecquerels/mg (4 to 5 mCi/mg). Radiolabeling efficiency was measured by instant thin-layer chromatography (Biodex Medical Systems) using 20 mM citrate buffer, pH 5.6, as the mobile phase with a Wizard 3″ 1480 Automatic Gamma Counter (PerkinElmer).

Seven days after tumor engraftment (tumor volume of ~50 mm3), two groups of 4T1 tumor bearing female BALB/c mice (8 weeks old) received tail vein injection of BSA-89Zr or n(BSA)-89Zr (n = 6) at 120 μCi in 100 μl per mouse. Three animals in each group were imaged with microPET (energy window of 350 to 650 keV) and microCT (voltage of 80 kilovolt peaks, current of 150 μA, 720 projections, 200-μm resolution) on a GNEXT PET/CT scanner (Sofie Biosciences, Dulles, VA). MicroPET/CT images were acquired at 0.5, 2, 4, 6, 24, and 48 hours postinjection. The PET images were reconstructed using the 3D-OSEM/MAP algorithm (24 subsets and three iterates) with random, attenuation, and decay correction. The CT images were reconstructed using a Modified Feldkamp Algorithm. Amide software (version 1.0.5) was used to quantify PET signal intensity in organs in the co-registered PET /CT images. In addition, the other three mice per group were euthanized at 24 hours postinjection for ex vivo measurements of tissue uptake of BSA-89Zr or n(BSA)-89Zr. Tumor, heart, lung, liver, spleen, kidney, and brain were harvested and weighted, followed by radioactivity measurements using a Wizard 3″ 1480 Automatic Gamma Counter (PerkinElmer).

Arthritis model

The CIA rat model was build using Sprague-Dawley rats. The animals received primary and secondary immunizations on day 0 and day 7, respectively, using bovine type II collagen (Sigma-Aldrich) and Freund’s adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich). TNFi and n(TNFi) were first labeled with Cy7 before the injection. The samples were administered through tail vein injection (4 mg/kg), and the biodistribution was investigated 3 and 20 hour postinjection using IVIS imaging. After 20 hours, the animals were euthanized and major organs and joint tissues were collected for ex vivo imaging using IVIS.

Muscle injury model

To build the mouse muscle injury model, female BALB/c mice (8 weeks old) underwent a right thigh muscle incision through scalpel cutting. Two hours after the injury, mice received Cy5-labeled BSA or n(BSA) through tail vein injection (5 mg/kg). The biodistribution was investigated at 1 hour postinjection using IVIS imaging.

Spinal cord injury model

To build the spinal cord injury model, female BALB/c mice (8 weeks old) were anesthetized, and the hair on the back of the mice was removed. The back was then disinfected, and a sterile scalpel blade was used to make a longitudinal incision ~1.5 cm longer over the T9 vertebral body. Muscles surrounding the vertebral body were bluntly dissected, and microsurgical scissors were used to remove the T9 vertebral lamina, fully exposing the dura mater. Using a precision spinal cord impactor (RWD Life Science), the spinal cord at T9 was affected centrally with a velocity of 1 m/s, a depth of 1 mm, and a dwell time of 1 s to produce complete spinal cord injury. Two hours after the injury, mice received Cy5-labeled BSA or n(BSA) through tail vein injection (9 mg/kg). The biodistribution was investigated at 1, 6, 12, and 24 hours postinjection. After 24 hours, the animals were euthanized and major organs and spinal cord tissues were collected for ex vivo imaging using IVIS.

Therapeutic efficacy of RTX nanocapsules [n(RTX)] in 2F7-bearing mice

To measure the tumor growth, mice received intraperitoneal injection of 100 μg of luciferin, and the tumor progression was measured by luminescence signals from the IVIS system. The body weight of animals was recorded every day for 14 days. At 24 hours, blood samples from all mice were collected for biochemistry analysis. At 14 days, all the animals were euthanized. The blood samples were collected for biochemistry analysis, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were harvested for H&E staining to evaluate tissue damage. For all the biosafety experiments, serum samples were sent to Servicebio Company for screening (test ID: SWR02981435). The histological analysis was performed by Servicebio Company (test ID: SWR02994182).

RNA sequencing and data analysis

After the evaluation of the therapeutic efficacy of n(aPD-1) in 4T1-bearing mice, lungs from mice treated with PBS, aPD-1, or n(aPD-1) were collected. Single-cell suspensions were obtained by passing the tissues through a MACS SmartStrainer (30 μm). The red blood cells (RBCs) were lysed with RBC lysis buffer (BioLegend). RNA was then extracted with the QIAamp RNA blood mini kit. The samples were then sent to Novogene (Sacremento, CA) for library preparation and sequencing. Raw sequencing reads underwent quality assessment using FastQC (v0.11.9). Adapter sequences and low-quality bases were trimmed using Trim Galore! (v0.6.7) using Cutadapt (v3.4). Trimmed reads were aligned to the GRCm38 reference genome using STAR (v2.6.1d). Transcript-level quantification was performed using Salmon (v1.10.1). Postalignment processing included sorting and indexing of aligned reads using SAMtools (v1.16.1). Duplicate reads were identified and marked using Picard MarkDuplicates (v3.0.0). Transcript assembly and quantification at the transcript level were completed using StringTie (v2.2.1). Genome-wide coverage tracks in bigWig format were generated using BEDTools (v2.30.0) and UCSC utilities (v377). Quality control assessments including alignment quality, duplication rate analysis, library complexity evaluation, and transcript integrity checks were performed using RSeQC (v3.0.1), Qualimap (v2.2.2-dev), and dupRadar (v1.28.0). Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 (v1.28.0), and results were visualized using minus vs. average (MA) plots to identify significantly differentially expressed genes. Functional enrichment analyses, including Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment, were performed using clusterProfiler, with results visualized through KEGG pathway plots and GO term enrichment plots. Comprehensive QC metrics were summarized and visualized using MultiQC (v1.10.1) and custom scripts in R (v4.2.1).
