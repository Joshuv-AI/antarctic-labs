Sustained mucosal delivery of SAMT-247 via an intravaginal ring reduces the risk of SIVmac251 acquisition in vaccinated macaques
Authors: Mohammad Arif Rahman, Emmanuel K. Woode, John A. Moss, Massimiliano Bissa, Manjula Gunawardana, Anna Gutowska, Luca Schifanella, Isabela Silva de Castro, Sophia Brown, James D. Stamos, Sarkis Sarkis, Marco Robello, Amalia E. Castonguay, Irina Butkyavichene, Cynthia A. Pise-Masison, Hyoyoung Choo-Wosoba, Melvin Doster, Aian Neil Alilin, Kristin Elizabeth Killoran, Joshua Kramer, Matthew W. Breed, Timothy Cardozo, Daniel H. Appella, Marc M. Baum, Genoveffa Franchini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe assess the combined effect of sustained mucosal delivery of the anti-HIV small molecule S-acyl-2-mercaptobenzamide thioester 247 (SAMT-247) via an intravaginal ring (IVR) and ΔV1DNA/ALVAC-SIV/ΔV1gp120/alum (ΔV1-SIV) immunization in female macaques. The combined approach reduces the risk of vaginal SIVmac251 infection by 82.8%, with 58% of animals remaining uninfected following 14 weekly low-dose challenges; about half of the initially protected animals also remain uninfected during a second challenge phase. Compared with a historical vaccine-only cohort, continued IVR use shows a trend toward improved protection. Protection correlates with the expansion of tolerogenic CD73+ dendritic cells, IL-10+ macrophages, IL-17-producing NKp44+ innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), and antibody-mediated natural killer (NK) cytotoxic activity targeting the V2 helical conformation. In vitro studies support ex vivo observations that SAMT-247 enhances gp120-reactive anti-inflammatory mucosal immunity and NK activity. These findings support sustained IVR-based delivery of SAMT-247 combined with V1-deleted immunogens as a promising strategy to prevent vaginal HIV transmission.

Introduction

The HIV epidemic remains a significant public health challenge, with approximately 40.8 million (37.0–45.6 million) people living with HIV worldwide by the end of 2024.1 Despite advances in prevention strategies, like pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), approximately 39,000 people aged 13 years and older were diagnosed with HIV in the United States in 2024 alone.2,3 Biomedical interventions such as long-acting lenacapavir, which targets the HIV-1 capsid protein and was recently FDA approved and administered biannually for PrEP, represent important progress.4 But lenacapavir faces limitations including high costs, potential drug resistance, and healthcare access requirements.5 Another PrEP option, long-acting cabotegravir, an integrase strand transfer inhibitor, has shown higher effectiveness than oral PrEP, although breakthrough infections have been associated with delayed diagnosis and HIV resistance.6 An effective HIV vaccine might offer notable advantages, such as lower cost, minimal ongoing follow-up, and durable protection. A safe and effective HIV vaccine would be essential for controlling the HIV epidemic.7,8,9,10,11,12,13

The multiple low-dose mucosal SIVmac251 macaque challenge model has successfully predicted HIV vaccine efficacy clinical trial outcomes.11 Notably, it aligned with findings from the RV-144 trial in Thailand,14 in which HIV acquisition was prevented without detectable neutralizing antibodies and with minimal CD8 cytotoxic responses,15 highlighting the model’s pre-clinical value.11,16 Binding antibodies to the envelope variable region 2 (V2) of HIV or simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV) were identified as correlates of protection in both humans and macaques.15,16,17,18 Optimization of that vaccine regimen to ΔV1DNA/ALVAC-SIV/ΔV1gp120/alum (ΔV1-SIV vaccine) has shown improved immunogenicity and relative efficacy,10,19,20 although virus eradication after mucosal challenge occurred in only 50%–65% of macaques. A breakthrough in virus clearance was observed when vaccinated macaques were treated with the gel-formulated zinc-finger inhibitor, S-acyl-2-mercaptobenzamide thioester 247 (SAMT-247) microbicide, 4 h prior to SIVmac251 vaginal exposure, reducing SIV acquisition risk by 92.7%, with 80% of animals clearing the virus.10 SAMT-247 microbicide targets the viral nucleocapsid protein NCp721,22 and inhibits various HIV strains, including multidrug-resistant isolates, in vitro.23 SAMTs induce zinc ejection, inactivating the nucleocapsid protein, leading to the production of immature viral particles,24 and have maintained efficacy in the presence of cervical mucus with no toxicity to human cervical tissue, suggesting promising clinical potential.25 SAMT-247 also synergistically enhanced immune responses elicited by the ΔV1-SIV vaccine.10

Intravaginal rings (IVRs) enhance drug delivery,26,27,28,29,30,31,32,33 providing controlled and sustained release, directly to the vaginal mucosa, of contraception,28 hormone therapy,29 and antiretroviral drugs for the prevention of HIV transmission.30,31,32,33 Limitations are minor, like potential discomfort, vaginal irritation, discharge or infection, and difficulty with regular replacement. To leverage IVR advantages and avoid compliance issues associated with per-exposure gel dosing, we developed an IVR designed for continuous, slow release of SAMT-247 microbicide. In this study, SAMT-247 microbicide delivered via IVR achieved an average mucosal exposure of approximately 40% of that obtained with the gel formulation in our prior work. Despite this lower dose, SAMT-247, in combination with vaccination, reduced the risk of SIVmac251 acquisition in female macaques (82.8% vaccine efficacy), with approximately 58% of animals remaining uninfected after 14 weekly challenges. In a second challenge series, continued SAMT-247 IVR use in these initially protected animals was associated with sustained protection relative to naive controls and a trend toward improved protection relative to a historical vaccine-only cohort. Ex vivo and in vitro stimulation of mucosal cells with SIV gp120, as a surrogate for virus encounter, suggested that SAMT-247 can enhance gp120-reactive protective innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) and tolerogenic myeloid subsets. The positive correlation between the mean IVR drug release rate and acquisition outcome further suggests that optimizing a higher SAMT-247 release rate may provide better protection. Collectively, these data highlight the potential of SAMT-247 to enhance mucosal immune responses and synergize with vaccines to contribute to HIV eradication.

Results

Study design and experimental framework for SAMT-247 IVR assessment

We conducted six linked studies to evaluate IVR safety, pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and immunologic correlates of risk of virus acquisition. Study 1 assessed whether insertion and replacement of an empty IVR altered susceptibility to repeated low-dose vaginal SIVmac251 challenge (Figure S1A). Study 2 characterized in vivo drug release from SAMT-247 loaded IVRs (S-IVRs) over 14 versus 28 days to inform replacement intervals (Figure S1D). Study 3 provides historical efficacy context for per-exposure gel dosing of SAMT-247 in combination with ΔV1-SIV vaccine (Figure S1F). Study 4 tested S-IVR delivery alone or in combination with the ΔV1-SIV vaccine regimen during a 14-exposure vaginal challenge series (Figure 1B). Study 5 evaluated outcomes during a second challenge series in animals protected in study 4 and vaccine-only group from study 3, with or without continued S-IVR use (Figure 1H). Empty intravaginal ring devices alone do not increase the risk of SIVmac251 acquisition in macaques

The IVR used in this study was designed with a flexible silicone scaffold, an inner polycarbonate cassette containing the drug, a rate-controlling membrane, and a polycarbonate cap with openings exposing a portion of the membrane to vaginal fluids (Figure 1A). The drug, SAMT-247, formulated in monoolein paste, forms nanoscale channels that facilitate drug diffusion when in contact with vaginal fluids. Additional control of drug release is achieved by the rate-controlling membrane.

In study 1, we investigated whether the insertion and removal of IVRs could increase SIVmac251 acquisition risk via vaginal micro-disruptions (Figure S1A). Ten macaques had unmedicated, empty-IVRs inserted and replaced every 2 weeks from weeks 13–31, with weekly SIVmac251 challenges starting at week 17, for a total of 14 exposures. The results demonstrated no difference in the rate of SIVmac251 acquisition between the empty-IVR treated macaques and control animals, whether concurrent (n = 3) (Figure S1B, p = 0.49), historical (n = 37) (Figure S1B, p = 0.34), or combined (Figure S1C, p = 0.33), proving that IVR insertion does not increase infection risk.

Next, we evaluated mucosal drug levels by placing our S-IVRs in macaques for either 14 days (group 1) or 28 days (group 2) (study 2; Figure S1D). After removal, the daily drug release rates were similar, averaging 0.97 ± 0.25 mg/day for group 1 and 0.84 ± 0.53 mg/day for group 2 (Figure S1E); thus, we decided to replace the IVR every 4 weeks in the subsequent studies.

The combination of vaccination and SAMT-247-loaded IVR decreases the risk of SIVmac251 acquisition

SAMT-247 gel, administered 4 h before each exposure, produced high vaginal tissue concentrations of metabolite D, a virucidal compound.34,35 The concentrations of SAMT-247 metabolites were approximately 2-fold higher than those achieved with current S-IVRs (study 3; Figures S1F–S1H). In study 4, we evaluated whether the continuous delivery of SAMT-247 by IVR compensates for lower tissue drug levels relative to gel dosing. We further study whether insertion and replacement of S-IVRs every 4 weeks, prior to and during a virus challenge, alone or in combination with vaccination, reduces vaginal SIVmac251 acquisition in macaques (Figure 1B). Twelve macaques immunized with the ΔV1-SIV vaccine regimen and ten naive macaques began receiving S-IVRs at week 13 (1 week after the final vaccination, or the equivalent time point for naive animals). S-IVRs were replaced every 4 weeks at weeks 16, 20, 24, 28, and 32. Beginning at week 17, all macaques were exposed to SIVmac251 weekly for up to 14 weeks. To estimate cumulative per-exposure risk of SIVmac251 acquisition, we included historical (n = 37) and concurrent (n = 3) naive controls, as well as naive animals that received empty IVRs (n = 10). All the animals were challenged with the same SIVmac251 day 8 stock36 under standardized conditions in the same facility, and their SIVmac251 acquisition rates did not significantly differ (p = 0.50; Figure S1I).

The combination of vaccine/S-IVR significantly reduced the risk of SIVmac251 acquisition by 82.8% compared with naive control groups (p < 0.0001; power of analysis = 0.99; Figure 1C), with seven animals remaining uninfected. In contrast, S-IVR alone in naive animals showed only a trend toward reduced risk (p = 0.08, Figure 1D), mirroring prior SAMT-247 gel results without vaccination.10 Virus acquisition significantly differed between the vaccine/S-IVR group and the S-IVR alone group (p = 0.04, Figure 1E). Notably, the mean SAMT-247 release rate correlated with delayed viral acquisition in the vaccine/S-IVR group (R = 0.74, p = 0.009; R = 0.50, p = 0.14; Figures S1J and S1K), and drug release rates significantly differed between infected and uninfected SAMT-247 animals (p = 0.003; Figure 1F). Interestingly, across different IVR replacement intervals, the drug release rate varied both within individual animals over time and between animals (Figure S1L). The efficacy of vaccine/S-IVR did not significantly differ from the vaccinated group that received SAMT-247 gel in a prior study (p = 0.25; Figure 1G), indicating similar effectiveness of SAMT-247 delivered by IVR or gel. These findings indicated that continuous SAMT‑247 delivery by IVR, when combined with ΔV1‑SIV vaccination, significantly reduces vaginal SIVmac251 acquisition risk.

Sustained mucosal protective immunity by continuous vaginal delivery of SAMT-247 to vaccinated macaques

We next explored whether continuing S-IVR use after the initial 14 exposures to SIVmac251 might be associated with sustained protection during a second challenge series. In study 5 (Figure 1H), we enrolled 19 vaccinated animals that remained uninfected after the first challenge series and divided them into two groups. Group 1 included the seven vaccine/S-IVR animals that remained uninfected in study 4 and continued receiving S-IVRs (replaced every 4 weeks) through week 64. Group 2 consisted of 12 vaccinated animals from a historical cohort, six of which received SAMT-247 gel during the first challenge round and six that did not. None of the group 2 animals received continued SAMT-247 treatment during the interval. Both groups were re-exposed to up to 11 weekly SIVmac251 exposures from weeks 49–60.

The vaccine/S-IVR group showed significant protection compared with naive animals after second round of challenge (p = 0.0005; Figure 1I). Within the 12 vaccinated animals, there was no difference in SIVmac251 acquisition between those previously treated with SAMT-247 gel and those not treated (p = 0.95, Figure S1M), so their data were combined. This combined vaccinated group also showed significant protection compared with naive animals following a second round of challenge exposure (p = 0.02; Figure 1J). However, there was only a weak trend toward decreased virus acquisition in the vaccine/S-IVR group compared with the vaccinated-only group after the first (p = 0.16; Figure 1K) and second rounds of challenge exposures (p = 0.19; Figure 1L), likely due to the study’s limited power. Therefore, the conclusion that continuous SAMT-247 IVR treatment enhances the durability of vaccine efficacy compared with the vaccine alone remains hypothetical until larger animal groups can fully evaluate this possibility.

SAMT-247 augments in vivo gp120-reactive mucosal CD73+mDC, DC10, and macrophages producing IL-10

A central objective of this study was to investigate whether topical SAMT-247 treatment could enhance and sustain vaccine-induced immunity at mucosal sites. Previous analyses showed that the ΔV1-SIV vaccine engages the ATP/adenosine axis, leading to mucosal anti-inflammatory responses mediated by tolerogenic dendritic cells (DCs) and infiltrating monocytes, which are associated with a reduced risk of SIV infection.10,16,20,37,38

As SAMT-247 administered vaginally is also detected in rectal tissue, rectal (rather than vaginal) mucosal biopsies were collected to avoid vaginal tissue disruption, which could confound challenge outcomes (Figure 1H). Samples were collected from the vaccine/S-IVR group (n = 12) prior to any treatment (pre), 1 week after the final vaccination and before IVR insertion (week 13), and 2 weeks after IVR insertion (week 15). Additional samples were collected at week 46 from the seven animals that remained uninfected in study 4, prior to the second challenge series. Longitudinal phenotypic and functional analyses were performed on mucosal myeloid cell subsets, with and without SIV gp120 stimulation, as a surrogate of mucosal tissue encounter with SIV using the gating strategy shown in Figure S2.

DCs are central in shaping adaptive immunity.39 The vaccine/S-IVR recruited a subset of mucosal SIV gp120-reactive CD73+ myeloid DCs (mDCs), consistent with prior studies of V1-deleted vaccines40 (p = 0.001; Figure 2A). These cells remained elevated at week 46 (p = 0.03). The frequency of CD73+mDC at week 15 correlated with decreased viral infection risk only following the first round of viral challenge (R = 0.69, p = 0.02; Figure 2B), suggesting a role for CD73-mediated adenosine production in modulating mucosal immune responses.41 Strikingly, long-term SAMT-247 treatment nearly doubled the frequency of mucosal DC10 cells (Figure 2C), with their levels at weeks 15 and 46 strongly correlating with reduced infection risk in both challenge rounds (R = 0.80, p = 0.004; R = 0.87, p = 0.03; Figure 2D). The SIV gp120-reactive DC10 subset producing IL-10 also increased post-vaccine/S-IVR (p = 0.002; Figure 2E) and was associated with decreased risk with the first challenge round (R = 0.67, p = 0.02; SAMT-247 long-term treatment also increased frequencies of IL-10-producing gp120-reactive CD14+CD16− and CD14−CD16+ monocyte subsets (p = 0.04 and p = 0.03, respectively; Figures 2H and 2I). These subsets correlated with reduced infection risk after the second challenge round (R = 0.91, p = 0.01; R = 0.87, p = 0.03; Figures 2J and 2K). Additionally, SIV gp120-reactive IL-10+CD14−CD16+ cells at week 15 correlated with decreased risk following the first challenge (R = 0.76, p = 0.007; Figure 2K) and with the mean SAMT-247 release rate (R = 0.61, p = 0.04; Figure 2L). In stark contrast, SIV gp120-reactive IFN-γ+CD14+ and IFN-γ+CD16+ cells decreased to baseline levels by week 46 following SAMT-247 treatment (Figures S3A and S3B). These cell subsets were associated with increased virus acquisition risk after the first challenge round (R = −0.61, p = 0.04; R = −0.63, p = 0.03; Figures S3C and S3D). Furthermore, at week 15, the frequency of gp120-reactive CD14+ IFN-γ-producing mucosal cells negatively correlated with tolerogenic CD73+ mDCs (R = −0.69, p = 0.02; Figure 2M).

Collectively, these findings indicate that prolonged SAMT‑247 delivery by IVR reinforces vaccine-induced mucosal immune regulation by expanding gp120-reactive CD73+ mDCs, DC10 cells, and IL‑10 producing monocyte/macrophage populations associated with reduced SIV acquisition risk, while limiting pro-inflammatory IFN‑γ producing myeloid responses linked to increased susceptibility.

SAMT-247 showed a trend toward improved monocyte engulfment capacity

Efferocytosis and monocyte engulfing capability in blood, functions promoted by IL-10, remained comparable in vaccine/S-IVR animals throughout the study (Figures S3E and S3F). CD14+ efferocyte frequencies were similar between vaccine/S-IVR and vaccine-only groups at weeks 15 and 46 (Figures S3G and S3H). However, monocyte engulfing capability, while similar at week 15 (Figure S3I), trended higher at week 46 in vaccine/S-IVR animals compared with vaccine-only animals (p = 0.07; Figure 2N). A representative example of the efferocytosis assay output and gating strategy is shown in Figure S3J. We observed that efferocytosis-related measures showed partial consistency across early time points. Animals with higher responses at week 13 tended to remain relatively higher at week 15 (R = 0.80, p = 0.005; Figure S3K). However, this relationship was not maintained uniformly at later time points, suggesting that efferocytosis capacity is not solely a fixed intrinsic trait of specific animals. Efferocytosis may be influenced by time-varying immunologic and mucosal factors including vaccine-induced epigenetic programming20 and/or local drug exposure. The CD14+ efferocyte engulfing capacity positively correlated with gp120-reactive IL-10+CD14−CD16+ cells (R = 0.60, p = 0.04; Figure 2O) and negatively with IFNγ+CD14−CD16+ cells at week 15 (R = −0.61, p = 0.04; Figure 2P). Collectively, these data demonstrate that SAMT-247 augmented mucosal frequencies of tolerogenic DCs, leading to an increase in anti-inflammatory macrophages and infiltrating monocytes producing the pro-resolution IL-10 cytokine and mediating efferocytosis.

Sustained mucosal homeostatic ILCs and NK cytocidal function in vaccinated macaques treated with SAMT-247

Several studies have shown that ΔV1-SIV immunogens increase the frequency of mucosal SIV gp120-reactive NKp44+ ILCs producing IL-17, which are associated with decreased risk of SIV infection,10,37,38 likely by maintaining gut mucosa homeostasis. Indeed, prolonged SAMT-247 treatment maintains the frequency of these ILCs above baseline (p = 0.03; Figure 3A). Their frequencies at weeks 15 and 46 correlated with reduced virus acquisition risk during the first (R = 0.61, p = 0.04) and second (R = 0.81, p = 0.04) challenge rounds, respectively (Figure 3B). The frequency of these IL-17+ NKp44+ ILCs also correlated with the mean SAMT-247 release rate in the vagina (R = 0.60, p = 0.04; Figure 3C) and with DC10 frequency (R = 0.59, p = 0.04; Figure 3D). Discussion

We demonstrate in a repeated low-dose vaginal SIVmac251 challenge model that continuous mucosal delivery of SAMT-247 from an IVR contributes to reduced viral acquisition risk. SAMT-247 IVR delivery alone showed only a modest, non-significant trend toward protection. In contrast, combining the SAMT-247 IVR (S-IVR) with V1-deleted SIV envelope immunogens delivered via DNA/ALVAC and protein/alum platforms (ΔV1-SIV vaccine) produced a significant reduction in acquisition risk. In the primary efficacy study (study 4), the combined vaccine/S-IVR regimen achieved 82.8% vaccine efficacy versus naive controls, with 58% of animals remaining uninfected after 14 weekly exposures. Based on prior work,10 the vaccine/S-IVR versus naive comparison (n = 12 versus 37) was expected to be well powered (estimated power = 0.99), whereas the vaccine/S-IVR versus vaccine-only comparison (n = 12 versus 18) was predicted to be underpowered (estimated power = 0.19). Accordingly, vaccine/S-IVR animals were significantly protected relative to naive controls in both challenge series (studies 4 and 5), while comparisons to a historical vaccine-only cohort showed only a trend and should be interpreted as exploratory. A larger, adequately powered cohort is, therefore, required to definitively quantify incremental benefit over vaccination alone and to support durability-focused endpoints.

In an earlier study, gel-based SAMT-247 applied 4 h prior to each challenge decreased acquisition risk by 92.7% when combined with the ΔV1-SIV vaccine regimen10; however, repeated per-exposure gel dosing is not practical for real-world use. Notably, although vaginal tissue drug levels were lower with vaccine/S-IVR than with vaccine/gel, the mean IVR release rate correlated with delayed acquisition and with multiple immune correlates, suggesting that continuous delivery can partially compensate for lower peak tissue concentrations. In the current study, we sought to explore whether SAMT-247 exerts immunomodulatory activity in vivo. To this end, we performed longitudinal rectal biopsies, building on earlier findings that vaginal administration of SAMT-247 results in detectable metabolites in rectal tissue. Supporting this concept, prior work has shown that intravaginal administration of 5% tenofovir (TFV) gel leads to drug detection in the rectum of macaques.45 Similarly, intravaginal dosing with emtricitabine (FTC)/TFV gel in macaques resulted in rapid distribution of FTC and TFV to rectal tissues and luminal fluids, achieving a 4.5-fold reduction in rectal simian-human immunodeficiency virus (SHIV) infection risk compared with placebo gel.46 Furthermore, in the MTN-014 phase 1 trial, TFV and its active metabolite, TFV diphosphate, were detected in rectal samples of women following vaginal gel administration.47 In our study, extended vaginal delivery of SAMT-247 enhanced the vaccine-induced immunity in the rectal compartment and reinforced the anti-inflammatory environment generated by the ΔV1-SIV vaccine. Collectively, these findings support the testable hypothesis that combining a vaccine with S-IVR may also provide adjacent protection against rectal infection.

Mucosal immune responses increased by week 15 following S-IVR insertion compared with week 13 and remained above baseline at week 46, despite partial waning over time. Determining whether sustained SAMT-247 delivery meaningfully improves the durability of vaccine-elicited mucosal immunity would require studies with concurrent vaccine-only mucosal time points and prespecified longitudinal sampling. Our in vitro stimulation experiments (study 6) supported the ex vivo observations and demonstrated SAMT-247-associated modulation of immune phenotypes. Ex vivo analyses identified key immune correlates of protection associated with delayed acquisition, including increased frequencies of mucosal tolerogenic DC10, gp120-reactive IL-10+CD14−CD16+ cells, maintenance of IL-17+NKp44+ ILCs, enhanced NKG2A+ NK activity, and V2-specific ADCC (Figures 2, 3, 4, S5, and S7). The gp120-reactive IL-10+ DC10 subset was positively correlated with NKp44+ ILCs. In turn, NKp44+ ILCs together with gp120-reactive CD73+ mDC, DC10, and CD14+CD16− cells correlated with higher V2-specific ADCC, which was associated with reduced risk of SIV acquisition (Figures 6 and S8). Efferocytosis, a previously described correlate of decreased risk elicited by ΔV1-SIV vaccination,10,20,37 also associated here with gp120-reactive IL-10+CD14−CD16+ cells, supporting a role for these cells in mucosal efferocytic activity. Model of protective immune responses associated with vaccine efficacy

Schematic representation of the proposed immunologic network linking mucosal innate programming to systemic antiviral effector functions and reduced acquisition risk. gp120-reactive mucosal IL-10+ DC10 cells are positively associated with CD73+ mDCs, suggesting coordinated regulation or functional crosstalk between regulatory and antigen-presenting myeloid subsets. These tolerogenic mucosal programs are also associated with gp120-reactive IL-17+ NKp44+ ILCs and IL-10+ infiltrating monocytes, consistent with a broader pro-resolution mucosal milieu. In turn, these mucosal immune features are linked to downstream systemic effector mechanisms, including V2-targeted ADCC and efferocytosis. Elevated ADCC may contribute to clearance of infected target cells, while enhanced efferocytosis facilitates removal of apoptotic cells and may limit secondary inflammation and preserve tissue homeostasis. Together, these coordinated mucosal and systemic immune responses are proposed to restrict early viral seeding and contribute to vaccine-associated protection.

We observed substantial variability in SAMT-247 release by IVR across and within animals over time. Physiologic factors that could plausibly contribute to the release of SAMT-247 from the IVR include cervicovaginal fluid volume and composition,48 local pH,49 vaginal microbiota,50,51 and hormonal status/estrous cycle effects on epithelial and mucus dynamics.52 Consistent with the limited protection observed for S-IVR alone, SAMT-247 delivery by IVR at the exposure levels achieved here appears insufficient to provide robust protection against this stringent challenge model without vaccination. Prior studies, however, demonstrated protection with SAMT-247 alone when applied as a gel 3 h (but not 4 h) before SIVmac251 challenge,53 emphasizing that delivery kinetics and local exposure are critical determinants of efficacy. Definitive characterization of dose dependence will be achievable through a sufficiently powered study employing IVRs engineered to deliver distinct, well-separated release rates across groups, enabling rigorous exposure-response modeling. Future investigations with larger cohorts will also provide the statistical power needed to resolve the biological and microenvironmental factors underlying the observed heterogeneity in IVR drug release and its relationship to dose-dependent outcomes.

Protection against neutralization-resistant SIVmac251 was achieved in the absence of measurable neutralizing antibodies. Instead, coordinated mucosal DC, monocyte, NK cell, and ILC activity appeared critical for driving ADCC and efferocytosis. This highlights the importance of reinforcing the mucosal barrier and preserving tissue homeostasis when confronting highly pathogenic challenge, such as SIVmac251. Together, our data suggest that this protective vaccine/S-IVR approach warrants translation toward clinical evaluation.

Limitations of the study

Prior in vitro work using cryopreserved peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from vaccinated animals has shown that SAMT-247 can modulate vaccine-induced immunity,10 and the present study extends those findings by providing complementary ex vivo evidence that SAMT-247 enhances mucosal immunity. However, one of the limitations of the current study is the absence of a concurrent vaccine-only group to compare side-by-side analyses of mucosal immunity (from vaccinate animals treated or not with SAMT-IVR). This study was not powered to compare infection outcomes across the different treatment groups, as it was designed to assess outcomes relative to naive animals. Likewise, assessment of the potential reduction in infection risk following protracted SAMT-247 IVR treatment was limited by the relatively small number of animals. Finally, MHC genotyping was not performed. Although host genetic factors, including specific MHC alleles, can influence susceptibility to infection, our previous study in a larger cohort did not detect an effect of the Mamu-A1 or Mamu-B∗17 alleles on acquisition of the same SIVmac251 viral stock in naive control animals.16

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

Macaques were housed at the National Institutes of Health and maintained in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and Public Health Service Policy for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The facility has an active OLAW assurance (A4149-01), is accredited by AAALAC International, and all animal use was humanely conducted and approved by the National Cancer Institute’s Animal Care and Use Committee (NCI-ACUC).

Animals were closely monitored daily for any signs of illness, with appropriate medical care provided as needed. Measures were taken to ensure the welfare of the animals and minimize discomfort. They were socially housed per the approved ACUC protocol and based on social compatibility, except during the viral challenge phase when they were individually housed. All clinical procedures, including biopsy collection, administration of anesthetics and analgesics, and euthanasia, were carried out under the direction of a laboratory animal veterinarian. Animals were fed daily with a fresh diet of primate biscuits augmented by edible enrichment including rotating items such as fruits, vegetables, nuts, forage, and other food items to maintain not only body weight and normal growth but also provide environmental enrichment. Psychological well-being was monitored by staff routinely and physical enrichment, including sanitized toys, destructible enrichment (cardboard and other paper products), and audio and visual stimulation, was provided.

The pharmacokinetic study utilized eight female Indian rhesus macaques, aged between 3 and 5 years, obtained from the free-range breeding colony on Morgan Island, South Carolina. At the initiation of the study, all macaques were confirmed to be negative for Simian Immunodeficiency Virus (SIV), simian retrovirus (SRV-D), Simian T cell Leukemia Virus (STLV) and Macacine herpesvirus 1 (BV) by serology (STLV, SRV-D, SIV, BV) and PCR (SRV-D). The animals were randomized based on age and weight into two groups: group 1 received the S-IVR for 14 days, while group 2 received the S-IVR for 28 days. This design was implemented to investigate the drug release profile of the IVR over different durations.

For the vaccine/S-IVR study, thirty-five Indian rhesus macaques, aged between 3 and 4 years, were employed, also sourced from the free-range breeding colony on Morgan Island, South Carolina. At the study’s initiation, all macaques were confirmed negative for SIV, simian retrovirus, and STLV. The macaques were randomized based on age and weight into four groups: group 1 received an empty-IVR (10 naive animals), group 2 received the ΔV1-SIV vaccine followed by S-IVR (12 naive animals), group 3 received S-IVR (10 naive animals), and 3 naive animals served as concurrent controls. Historical naive control macaques, sourced from various locations, were also included in the statistical analysis.

Additionally, rectal tissue from five male and six female rhesus macaques aged 3–4 years was used for an in vitro study. These macaques were from a separate study that received the ΔV1DNA/ALVAC-HIV/ΔV1gp120/alum vaccine (male, n = 3; female, n = 3) or ΔV1DNA/ALVAC-HIV/ΔV1gp120/ALFQA vaccine (male, n = 2; female, n = 3), and samples were collected 7 months post the last vaccination.

Animals were subjected to up to 14 viral challenges for 1st round of challenges and 11 viral challenges for 2nd round of challenges; the number of challenges required to achieve infection was compared across all three treatment groups using the Wald test of the proportional hazards model. The power of the test at the two-sided 0.05 level was assessed between the treatment groups and naive animals. The group sizes for the vaccine/S-IVR study were determined based on a previous study that compared 20 macaques treated with the vaccine/SAMT-247 gel to 37 control animals. Infection rates for the vaccine/S-IVR group were expected to be consistent with the previously observed rate of 0.016 in the vaccine/SAMT-247 gel group. With 12 animals in this group and 37 historical naive animals as controls, the power calculation was estimated to be 0.99. For the S-IVR group, sizes were based on a prior SAMT-247 gel study comparing 6 treated macaques to 37 controls. Infection rates for the S-IVR group were projected to match the previously observed rate of 0.11 in the SAMT-247 gel group. With 10 animals in this group and 37 historical naive animals as controls, the power calculation was estimated to be 0.64. The Empty-IVR group sizes were determined based on historical infection rates of 37 naive controls, with an anticipated infection rate of 0.22. This group included 10 animals, and the power calculation, including 37 historical naive animals as controls, was estimated to be 0.11. To enhance statistical power, data from 37 historical naive controls, intravaginally challenged with the same dose and lot of SIVmac251, were incorporated into the control group. There was no significant difference in the rate of SIV acquisition between the concurrent and historical controls (p = 0.71). It should be noted that all the retrospective power analysis were conducted based on 10,000 simulated datasets, given the observed infection rates of the corresponding groups.

Intravaginal ring design and SAMT-247 formulation

The IVRs used in the current study consist of a design that will be described in detail elsewhere. Key dimensions of the device include: [ring cross-sectional diameter = 4.5 mm; ring outer diameter = 15.5 mm; central reservoir = 10.5 mm W × 13.5 mm L x 5.75mm H; reservoir volume = 153 μL. The unmedicated silicone toroidal structure serves to support the drug-releasing cassette when the IVR is in place. A macaque-sized cassette holds ca. 150 mg of formulation, or the kernel, in this case SAMT-247 suspended in monoolein (1:1, w/w). The release of SAMT-247 from the IVR is modulated by a rate-controlling membrane consisting of expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE). The IVRs were designed to release ca. 1 mg d-1 SAMT-247 in vivo.

Empty-IVR group study

To assess whether the insertion of an unmedicated empty intravaginal ring (empty-IVR) could increase SIVmac251 acquisition by potentially causing micro-disruptions in the vaginal wall, animals were assigned to receive the empty IVR. The device was placed and maintained for a duration of 18 weeks, with replacements occurring every two weeks. Four weeks after the initial IVR insertion, the animals were subjected to 14 weekly low-dose challenges with SIVmac251 until infection was confirmed. No samples were collected from this group for immunological assay. Blood was collected before and during challenge phase to confirm SIVmac251 infection.

Immunization and challenge

Macaques in the vaccine/S-IVR group were immunized at weeks 0 and 4 with DNA encoding SIVgp160ΔV1 (2 mg per dose) and SIV239gag (1 mg per dose) in a total volume of 1 mL PBS. The DNA was administered intramuscularly in both thighs (0.5 mL per thigh). At week 8, the macaques received an ALVAC vector encoding gag/pro/env (wild-type env) in the right thigh at a dose of 10ˆ8 pfu in 1 mL PBS. At week 12, the macaques were boosted with the same ALVAC vector plus SIVgp120ΔV1 protein (400 μg per dose in 500 μL PBS plus 500 μL 2% Alhydrogel). The ALVAC was administered in the right thigh, while the 1 mL dose of Env protein plus alum was administered in the left thigh.

At week 13, the animals received the S-IVR, which was replaced at weeks 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, 36, 40, 44, 52, 56, and 60, and removed at week 64. Beginning at week 17, all macaques were challenged intravaginally on a weekly basis with 1 mL of a SIVmac251 at a concentration of 4000 TCID50/mL. Up to 14 challenges were administered until the macaques tested positive for SIV, as assessed by droplet digital PCR.37,38 Animals that were not infected by the first round of challenges were maintained for 18 weeks after the last challenge. At week 49, these animals received up to 11 additional challenges.

For the vaccine-only arm, we included 12 animals from a previous study,10 comprising 6 animals from the vaccine/SAMT-247 gel group and 6 animals from the vaccine-only group. The macaques in the vaccine/SAMT-247 gel group received 2 mL doses of the microbicide SAMT-247 as a gel intravaginally 4 h before each SIV challenge. The gel formulation contained 0.8% SAMT-247 in HEC gel (2.7% Natrosol cellulose 250HX Pharma, 0.01% DMSO, and 0.9% saline). Macaques in the vaccine-only group received HEC gel lacking SAMT-247. These 12 animals were not infected after the first round of challenges. The protected animals were maintained for 18 weeks after the last challenge. At week 49, these animals received up to 11 additional challenges without any further intervention and were considered as the vaccine-only group for the second round of challenges.

Rhesus macaques were vaccinated, and samples were collected both during the vaccination period and following vaginal exposure to SIVmac251. Due to the nature of the study, the same material could not be obtained multiple times from the same animal.

Challenge virus

Current and historical animals were challenged using the same SIVmac251 Day 8 virus stock,36 under standardized conditions, in the same facility, same route of administration, same dosing strategy, and same infection monitoring procedures were consistent across the historical vaccine studies and the current study.

Viral titers were determined using standardized procedures on primary rhesus macaque cells.54 Briefly, CD8-depleted PBMCs from three SIV-naïve rhesus macaques were stimulated with plate-bound anti-CD3 for three days, plated at 1 × 10ˆ5 cells per well in 96-well plates, and infected in triplicate with serial 5-fold dilutions of virus. After 24 h, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove residual input virus and then maintained in 100 U/mL IL-2 for three weeks. Cell-free culture supernatants were collected and analyzed for SIV p27 antigen by ELISA (ABL), and the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) was calculated using the Reed-Muench method.54 While minor cohort-related variation is unavoidable across studies conducted over time, the core virological reagents and challenge procedures were standardized to minimize variability, supporting the appropriateness of these comparisons.

Method details

Viral RNA load quantification

The RNA copies of SIVmac251 in plasma were quantified using Digital Droplet PCR (ddPCR) as described in prior studies.37,38 Briefly, following RNA extraction, nucleic acid samples were prepared and mixed with specific primers, fluorescent probes, and Supermix from the One-Step RT-ddPCR Advanced Kit (BioRad, Catalog No. 1864022). The primers used in this assay were as follows: SIV gag forward primer: 5′-GCAGAGGAGGAAATTACCCAGTAC-3′ and SIV gag reverse primer: 5′-CAATTTTACCCAGGCATTTAATGTT-3'. The system partitions 20 μL of the RNA extract from plasma into approximately 20,000 nanoL droplets, each containing a random distribution of the target and/or background RNA. The reverse transcriptase (RT) and Taq DNA polymerase from the Bio-Rad one-step ddPCR kit are initially inactive and become activated at a temperature of 50°C. Within each droplet, the double-quenched SIV gag probe (5'-/56FAM/TGTCCACCTGCCATTAAGCCCGA-3') was included at the start of the reaction. The plate was then transferred to a beta-prototype droplet reader (optical reader). Poisson statistics were utilized to quantify the proportion of positive droplets, thereby determining the number of target RNA templates and calculating the absolute viral RNA levels precisely in copies/μL. Based on fluorescence amplitude, each nanoliter droplet was classified as either positive or negative. The fraction of fluorescent droplets was used to determine the target concentration in the sample. The concentration of viral RNA in copies per milliliter (copies/mL) was calculated using the formula: copies/μL (Volume of Master Mix per well/Volume of template)∗ plasma dilution factor/(volume of plasma used for extraction/elution volume). The assay’s limit of quantification (LOQ) was set at 50 RNA copies per milliliter of plasma.

Vaginal tissue bioanalysis sample preparation

Vaginal tissue biopsies were weighed upon collection and immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Samples were stored and transported at −80°C. Samples were thawed on wet ice for 30 min prior to addition of a dissociative enzymatic cocktail (500 μL), consisting of RPMI 1640 with fetal bovine serum (7.5% v/v), collagenase (300U mL−1, LS005275, Worthington Biochemicals, Lakewood, NJ), DNase (2 U mL−1, 18047019, Worthington Biochemicals), elastase (12 U mL−1, LS006363, Worthington Biochemicals), hyaluronidase (60 U mL−1, LS005477, Worthington Biochemicals), and FTC as the IS (1.0 μg mL−1). The digestions were carried out at 37°C for 2 h with brief vortex agitation (2 × 5 s) every 15 min. Samples then were macerated with a disposable, polypropylene microcentrifuge tube pestle (USA Scientific, Ocala, FL) and incubated at 37°C for an additional 30 min, with brief vortex agitation (2 × 5 s) every 15 min, following the addition of a second aliquot (500 μL) of enzyme mix (vide supra). The resulting solutions were centrifuged at 12,000 RPM, 25°C, 10 min. Supernatant aliquots (100 μL) were transferred to a V-bottom 96-well plate, and then transferred again to a 96-well, 2 mL Impact protein precipitation filter plate (CE0-7565, Phenomenex) where they were mixed with acetonitrile (300 μL) and processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting purified samples were dried overnight in a SpeedVac concentrator system at a medium heat setting. The dried samples were reconstituted in 0.1% v/v formic acid in high purity water (100 μL), shaken and centrifuged as above, and analyzed immediately by LC-MS/MS.

Bioanalysis calibration standards and quality controls

A minimum of six standards and a minimum of three quality controls were prepared in naive vaginal tissue matrix samples in accordance with FDA guidelines, except Met-B and Met-C where the samples were prepared in blank extraction matrix due to their instability. The samples were processed in tandem with the pharmacokinetic studies above and analyzed alongside by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS).

Quantification of SAMT-247 and its metabolites in biological samples

The concentration of SAMT-247 and its metabolites was measured by LC-MS/MS using a 5 μL injection volume and an HPLC system consisting of a model G1367A well-plate autosampler and a model G1376A binary pump (1100 Series, Agilent Technologies) interfaced to an API 3000 triple quadrupole tandem mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, Framingham, MA) with a Turbo Ion Spray electrospray ionization source. An Agilent Zorbax Eclipse XDB-C18 Rapid Resolution column (2.1 × 50 mm; 3.5 μm) controlled at 40°C was the stationary phase. The following gradient program (0.8 mL min−1 flow rate) was used (A, 0.1% v/v formic acid in water; B, 0.1% v/v formic acid in acetonitrile): 4.3 min ramp from 100:0 A:B to 65:35 A:B; 0.7 min ramp from 65:35 A:B to 100:0 A:B; 1.5 min hold at 100:0 A:B resulting in a total run time of 6.5 min. Analyte retention times were, in order of elution: FTC (IS), 1.52 min; Met-B, 2.20 min; Met-A, 2.37 min; SAMT-247, 2.42 min; Met-D. 2.53 min; Met-C, 3.11 min. The measured transition ions, m/z, under ESI+ ionization mode was: SAMT-247, parent 267.0 amu, product, 225.1 amu; Met-A, parent 239.3 amu, product, 222.1 amu; Met-B, parent 225.3 amu, product, 137.1 amu; Met-C, parent 447.2 amu, product, 223.1 amu; Met-D, parent 223.3 amu, product, 209.2 amu; FTC (IS), parent 249.0 amu, product, 130.0 amu.

The method used ≥ 8-point standard curves prepared in blank (i.e., unmedicated) biological matrix –except Met-B and Met-C where the standards were prepared in blank extraction matrix due to their instability– and showed linearity in excess of an R2 value of 0.98 for Met-A and Met-D. Three separately prepared quality control samples were analyzed at the beginning and end of each sample set to ensure accuracy and precision within 20%, in accordance with FDA bioanalytical validation criteria.55

The analytical measuring range for SAMT-247 metabolites in vaginal samples is 1–200 ng/sample for Met-A and 91–25,000 ng/sample for Met-D. This range is defined by the lower limit of quantitation (LLOQ) and the upper limit of quantitation (ULOQ). Samples with analyte concentrations above the ULOQ are diluted to fall within the quantifiable range and then reanalyzed to ensure accurate measurement. The results were normalized to weight of tissue biopsies and reported as ng mg−1. Concentration conversion from ng mg−1 to μM was performed to allow meaningful, direct comparisons across analytes and was based on the assumption that the matrix has a density of 1 g mL−1.

V2-specific ADCC assay using NK cells isolated from autologous animals

The V2-specific antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) assay was conducted using autologous animal NK cells as effector cells, following established protocols.56 Cryopreserved plasma samples collected at 5 weeks post-final immunization (week 17) and 3 weeks prior to the second round of challenge (week 46) were analyzed.

Briefly, 1 × 10ˆ6 GFP-expressing EGFP-CEM-NKr-CCR5-SNAP target cells were coated with 50 μg of ΔV1 gp120 protein for 2 h at 37°C, washed, and labeled for 30 min at room temperature with SNAP-Surface Alexa Fluor 647 (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA). 50 μL of target cells was added to plate.To block the binding of plasma anti-V2 antibodies to gp120, the gp120 coated target cells were incubated with 1 μg of purified F(ab')2 fragments from the NCI05 monoclonal antibody in 50 μL medium was added for 1 h at 37°C. Plasma samples were heat-inactivated, and 50 μL of plasma were diluted 1:250 (for a final 1:1000 dilution) for the assay. NK cells were isolated from PBMCs of the vaccinated animals at week 13 (before insertion of S-IVR), week 15 (2 weeks after the insertion of S-IVR), and week 46 (3 weeks prior to the second round of challenge) using PE anti-NKG2A (Z199; cat. #IM3291U, Beckman Coulter, Brea, California, USA) and anti-PE microbeads (cat. #130-048-801, Miltenyi Biotec, MD, USA). These NK cells from the autologous animals were used as effector cells at an effector/target (E/T) ratio of 5:1 and 50 μL of the effector cells were added to the plate.

The cocultured cells were incubated at 37°C for 2 h, washed with PBS, and resuspended in 200 μL of a 1% paraformaldehyde in PBS solution. Cells were acquired by flow cytometry using a BD FACSymphony A5 cytometer equipped with a high throughput system (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using FlowJo software version 10.10.1. Specific killing was calculated as the loss of GFP from the SNAP-Alexa647+ target cells. Target and effector cells cultured in the presence of R10 media served as background controls. Normalized percent killing was calculated using the following formula: (killing in the presence of plasma or plasma + F(ab')2 - background)/(killing in the presence of positive control - background) × 100. V2-specific ADCC killing was determined by subtracting the ADCC killing in the presence of NCI05 F(ab')2 from the ADCC killing in the absence of F(ab')2.10,20,37,38

The V2-specific ADCC assay was conducted in the vaccine-only group using plasma samples collected at 5 weeks post-final immunization (week 17) and 3 weeks prior to the second round of challenge (week 46), along with NK cells isolated from week 46.

Efferocytosis assay

The frequency of CD14+ cells capable of conducting efferocytosis and their ability to engulf apoptotic cells were determined using the Efferocytosis Assay kit (cat. #601770, Cayman Chemical Company, Ann Arbor, MI).10,20,37 Cryopreserved PBMCs from vaccinated animals collected at week 13 (prior to the insertion of S-IVR) and week 15 (two weeks post-insertion of S-IVR) were utilized to isolate CD14+ cells. CD14+ cells were isolated using non-human primate CD14 MicroBeads (#130-091-097) and AutoMACS Pro (Miltenyi Biotec) from 10 × 10ˆ6 cryopreserved PBMCs. Following isolation, the cells were stained with CytoTell Blue provided in the kit and washed three times and was used as effector cells in the assay. Apoptotic neutrophils collected from a naive macaque were used as target cells. Neutrophils were isolated by processing fresh blood collected in EDTA tubes using Ficoll-Paque (GE Healthcare) and subsequent incubation with Dextran. After isolation, neutrophils were stained with CFSE, washed three times with R10 media (RPMI media, 10% FBS, and 1× anti-anti), incubated with R10 media containing Staurosporine to induce apoptosis, and washed twice. Effector and target cells were cocultured at a ratio of 1 effector to 3 target cells in R10 media at a concentration of 1 × 10ˆ6 cells/mL and incubated overnight at 37°C. Concurrently, CFSE-stained apoptotic neutrophils and CytoTell Blue-stained CD14+ cells were cultured alone as controls. Following incubation, cells were washed once with PBS and resuspended in 1% paraformaldehyde. All cells were analyzed by flow cytometry using a FACSymphony A5 and FACSDiva software (BD Biosciences). Further analyses were performed using FlowJo software version 10.10.1 (TreeStar, Inc.). Cells were gated as follows: FSC/SSC/single cells/CytoTell Blue+/CFSE+. The percentage of CD14+ efferocytes was calculated as the percentage of CFSE+ cells within the CytoTell Blue+ (CD14+) cell population. The engulfing capability was determined by measuring the median fluorescence intensity of CFSE in CFSE+ CytoTell Blue+ cells.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted without assuming normal distribution or equal variances of the data; therefore, non-parametric tests were employed. For comparing continuous variables between two paired or unpaired groups, the two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test and the two-tailed Mann–Whitney test were used, respectively. Differences between groups in the number of challenges before viral acquisition were assessed using the log rank (Mantel-Cox) test within the discrete-time proportional hazards model. The average per-risk challenge of viral acquisition was estimated by dividing the total number of observed infections by the number of administered challenges. Correlation analyses were performed using the non-parametric Spearman-rank correlation method, with exact permutation and approximate two-tailed p values were calculated. As this research was exploratory in nature, all p values are reported as nominal values without adjustment for multiple comparisons. No animals or data points were excluded from the analyses. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 11.
