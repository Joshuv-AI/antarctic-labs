A systematic review identifying fitness metrics indicative of simulated astronaut extravehicular activity task performance
Authors: Nicole C. Strock, Alyssa N. Varanoske, Jakob R. Allen, Dillon J. Frisco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To prepare for safe and successful completion of surface operations during future missions to the Moon and Mars, understanding the relationships between fitness and simulated extravehicular activity (EVA) performance is imperative. This systematic review included twelve studies [366 astronaut and non-astronaut subjects (206 male, 154 female, 6 unspecified)] that reported cardiorespiratory or muscular fitness data in relation to EVA task performance and/or simulated EVA intensity outcomes. Databases searched (through May 2025) included PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and NASA Technical Reports Server. The AXIS critical appraisal tool for cross-sectional studies assessed study quality. Results were synthesized in tabular and graphical formats. Thirty-eight fitness metrics were identified, with 8 cardiovascular and 12 muscular metrics demonstrating associations with simulated EVA task performance outcomes. This systematic review identified fitness metrics of relevance for future exploration EVAs and provides important considerations for exercise countermeasure and monitoring capability development. Funded by NASA Human Research Program.

Introduction

For the first time in over 50 years, NASA’s Artemis program is preparing for a sustained human presence on the Lunar surface (NASA’s Plan for Sustained Lunar Exploration and Development), bringing new opportunities and challenges for spaceflight exploration. One such challenge will be the need to physically prepare astronauts for regular Lunar Extravehicular Activities (EVAs; up to 5 EVAs for short-duration missions, or 3–4 per week for sustained missions)1,2, with projected Artemis Notional EVA timelines planning for sustained effort of up to 8 h (6 ± 2 h)2. Frequency and duration of Artemis EVAs will exceed previous Apollo experiences, which averaged approximately 4 h per EVA and 3 EVAs per mission (NASA EVA Data). In combination with a larger exploration spacesuit3,4, the demands associated with Artemis partial-gravity (g) EVAs can be expected to exceed those of previous Apollo EVAs and microgravity EVAs performed on the International Space Station (ISS)5, as tasks are expected to be of greater complexity.

Exposure to microgravity manifests in well-documented systemic physiological deconditioning, with potential to yield performance-limiting impacts6. Therefore, the maintenance of physical fitness (i.e., cardiorespiratory and muscular) through exercise is necessary for crewmembers to remain healthy enough to carry out nominal and off-nominal operations. Understanding which elements of fitness may be most indicative of EVA performance will provide critical information for physical preparation prior to and during surface exploration missions to help ensure safe and successful completion of Lunar EVA mission objectives. Planned exploration EVAs will involve science, engineering, and maintenance objectives that may require a range of physical workloads to ambulate, utilize tools and collection equipment, deploy science packages, and transport/stow materials1. Additionally, critical tasks and contingency scenarios, compounded with the expected performance deficits due to spaceflight deconditioning, may require greater physical fitness. Studies employing simulated EVA tasks performed at high efforts provide an indication of workloads and fitness necessary for the successful completion of tasks in challenging and potentially off-nominal situations.

Prioritizing health and fitness represents an attainable risk mitigation strategy for planetary EVAs7. To ensure that astronauts are physically prepared for Artemis missions and that appropriate in-mission exercise countermeasures and monitoring capabilities are provided8, exploration fitness standards should be developed and validated based on the expected objectives and tasks. Currently, fitness standards for partial-g extraterrestrial surface EVAs introduced in the most recent NASA 3001 Standards (NASA-STD-3001) include thresholds for aerobic capacity (VO2max; 36.5 mL/kg/min) and muscular strength (1-Repetition Maximum [1-RM] bench press: 1.0 × body weight; 1-RM deadlift: 1.6 × body weight); however, these have not been validated with respect to Artemis-associated EVA operations. Additionally, exploration vehicle mass and volume constraints may limit the equipment available to monitor fitness. Therefore, investigating alternative fitness metrics may help guide future monitoring capability needs for NASA, other spaceflight agencies, and private companies developing standards for exploration vehicles and exercise countermeasures.

While Apollo missions represented an impressive feat of testing and expansion of human space exploration capabilities yielding critical lessons learned9 that influenced the development of exploration programs, many questions remain regarding the physical factors associated with safe and successful EVA performance. Until humans return to the Moon and set foot on Mars, research involving 1 g and partial-g offload analog simulations provide invaluable evidence to inform the optimization of human performance and EVA readiness. As such, the purpose of this systematic review of literature is to explore the relationship between fitness characteristics and simulated EVA task performance. A consolidation of findings from previous 1 g and analog investigations can be used as a basis for future research to validate exploration fitness standards in relation to EVA task performance for Artemis design reference mission objectives.

Methods

The present systematic review of literature followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement guidelines10,11 and was conducted to address the following research question: “How does fitness (cardiorespiratory and muscular) relate to functional task performance for EVAs or EVA simulations?”

Exclusion criteria included review papers and manuscripts that did not report any fitness outcomes in relation to EVA task performance and/or simulated EVA intensity outcomes. Selection process

Studies were screened and reviewed by two reviewers (NS, AV) using the Rayyan web app for systematic reviews12. Citations first underwent an initial title and abstract screening to exclude studies that did not address the study research question or meet PICOS criteria. Full-text articles were retrieved for studies that passed initial screening. For discrepancies, a third reviewer was consulted (JA).

Data collection process and data items

Data was extracted and managed via an Excel spreadsheet. Data included subject demographics (number of participants, sex, age, body mass), description of methods employed by authors for task circuit, standalone task, EVA simulation (descriptions, suited or gravity condition, pace, intensity, failure), fitness metrics, identified thresholds for task performance (e.g., fitness thresholds associated with task completion/failure or time to completion), and reported associations/relationship to performance outcomes. Data were summarized in tabular and graphic formats.

Study risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias and quality of study design and reporting were assessed using the AXIS critical appraisal tool for cross-sectional studies13. This tool was chosen as it more closely applied to the design of studies included in this review. The 20-component tool was used to determine the quality and risk of bias of a study based on study design and reporting considerations. For each component question, reviewers answered “yes”, “no”, or “do not know” based on the information included within the study. Two reviewers (N.S., A.V.) completed the assessment independently, and a third reviewer (J.A.) completed the assessment for specific components where there was disagreement in scoring to serve as a tie-break. A figure was compiled based on the consensus of responses; because several components were not directly applicable to studies, as they pertained to “non-responders” and reviewers unanimously scored these components as “do not know” for each study, these components were not included in the final figure.

Of these studies, 6 utilized designated task circuits/testing28–30,33,34,36, 7 investigated standalone EVA tasks28,31,32,35,37–39, and 2 incorporated an EVA simulation32,37 (Table 3). A majority of studies (11/12) were conducted in 1 g conditions, 1 study included Lunar and Martian offload conditions39, and 2 studies were performed at 1 g with Lunar (20 mL/kg/min30; targeting 17–20 mL/kg/min35) and Martian (30 mL/kg/min30; pacing with the addition of weights added to torso and extremities35) intensities achieved through specified ranges of aerobic expenditure.

For EVA task circuits, subjects performed a variety of EVA-inspired tasks, including those requiring large muscle groups such as object carry/transport, climbing (treadmill hill, stairs, ladders), seat egress, hatch opening, and emergency crew rescue, as well as fine motor tasks such as manipulating connections and tool use (drilling, wrenching) (Table 3). The number of distinct tasks included in a task circuit ranged from 3 to 6 distinct tasks, performed in a 1 g shirt-sleeve condition, with the exception of 3 studies which utilized externally weighted suits33,34 or a weighted pack with distributed weights36. In 5 of 6 task circuit studies, subjects completed circuits as fast as possible, with average completion time ranging from 20 to 36 min. One task circuit employed pacing strategies to mimic Lunar or Martian EVA VO2 intensities30. Two task circuit studies required subjects to complete multiple rounds of the circuit, ranging from 5 to 15 rounds29,30. Three task circuit studies reported task circuit failure rates29,30,34.

Of the studies included, 7 of 12 included standalone tasks, such as a traverse (ranging from 1.5 to 10 km)28,35,39, hill climb32,35,37, or capsule egress31. Three studies reported time to completions for standalone traverse tasks (10 km: 61–95 min28,39) or capsule egress (54.9 ± 19.4 s31). One study looking at standalone tasks used pacing strategies to mimic Lunar or Martian EVA VO2 intensities35.

Of the EVA simulation and task circuit studies reviewed, only 6 involved subjects performing EVA tasks in some form of a suit or suit simulator32–34,36,37,39. Of the suited studies, 1 included simulated 1/6 g (Lunar) and 3/8 g (Martian) offload39, and 2 included data for simulated 1 g EVAs32,37. No studies where the goal was to determine fitness thresholds required for EVAs included a high-fidelity suited condition.

Cardiorespiratory fitness in relation to EVA tasks/simulated EVA

EVAs require sustained efforts that range in intensity depending on tasks included in surface operations timelines. For exploration missions, tasks may include constructing and maintaining outposts, deploying scientific packages, and sample collection, which may require traverse over long distances in a weighted spacesuit. Understanding the minimum level of cardiorespiratory fitness necessary to aid in safe and successful completion of mission objectives is imperative. As such, multiple studies have been conducted to evaluate which components of cardiorespiratory fitness best relate to EVA-specific task performance28–31,34,35 (Table 4). Correlates of task performance (Fig. 2A)

Aerobic capacity: One of the key physiological factors that influences endurance exercise performance, aerobic capacity (VO2pk or VO2max)40, was the most common aerobic fitness metric examined in studies with EVA task circuits or standalone tasks. This was assessed using various methods throughout the studies, including treadmill, arm crank, cycle ergometer, and rowing ergometer. Values were reported as either VO2pk or VO2max within each study, and consistency is maintained herein. All but 1 study39 reported VO2pk or VO2max as relative to body mass (i.e., mL/kg/min). When relating VO2max to completion time for various EVA task circuits28,29, there were significant negative associations for arm crank (r = −0.41 to −0.66) and treadmill (r = −0.43 to −0.74) VO2max, such that individuals with higher VO2max values (i.e., greater cardiorespiratory fitness) resulted in shorter time to completion (i.e., better performance). In one study that parsed out the relationship between VO2pk and completion time in male and female subjects, negative associations tended to be stronger for males than females (treadmill VO2max: r = −0.78 [M] vs. r = −0.60 [F]; arm crank VO2max: r = −0.69 [M] vs. r = −0.52 [F])28.

For studies that included standalone tasks, such as a 10 km walkback, treadmill VO2max (r = −0.64) was negatively associated with completion time28. Negative associations tended to be stronger for male (r = −0.73) vs. female (r = −0.51) subjects for treadmill VO2max and 10 km walkback time28. For capsule egress, neither cycle (p = 0.08) nor rowing VO2pk (p = 0.07) was correlated with egress time. Even subjects with low VO2pk (≤20 mL/kg/min) were able to complete capsule egress in 2 minutes31.

Gas exchange threshold (GET)/ventilatory threshold (VT): The GET, denoted as the non-linear increase in VCO2 vs. VO2, is a noninvasive method to estimate the lactate threshold41. Due to strong correlation with endurance performance and indication of submaximal workload42, GET has been explored as an alternative metric for EVA-related performance. When relating GET to completion time for various EVA task circuits, there were significant negative associations for arm crank (range r = −0.34 to −0.51) and treadmill GET (range r = −0.45 to −0.58), expressed as mL/kg/min28,29. For certain EVA task circuits, sex differences in the relationship between GET and completion time emerged. For instance, in the physical abilities field test presented by Ade and colleagues28, arm crank GET was significant for both male (r = −0.48) and female (r = −0.47) subjects, while treadmill GET was only significantly related to completion time in male subjects (r = −0.73). Additionally, while arm crank GET expressed as a percentage of VO2pk, was associated with completion time for the physical abilities test (r = −0.33), treadmill GET was not. For standalone tasks, such as a 10 km walkback, treadmill GET (r = −0.48) expressed as mL/kg/min, was negatively associated with completion time28; however, negative association was only present in male subjects (r = −0.59)28. Treadmill GET expressed as a percentage of VO2pk was not associated with completion time for 10 km walkback.

Aerobic Peak Power Output (PPO): Few studies have examined the relationship between PPO and task performance. In one study, PPO obtained during incremental VO2pk tests assessed via cycle ergometer was not correlated with egress time (p = 0.44)31. However, egress time was inversely associated with rowing PPO normalized to kg body weight (r = −0.60, p = 0.03), indicating that the higher the PPO, the quicker the egress time31.

Critical power/critical speed: Tests of critical power (CP) provide an indication of exercise tolerance and may inform fatigue development43. While CP is commonly assessed on a cycle ergometer, the power-time relationship is conserved with other modalities (e.g., running) but is presented as critical speed (CS), when intensity is assessed in units of speed instead of power43,44. Previous research demonstrated that CS, in comparison to GET or VO2max, was a better determinant of endurance exercise performance45,46. Few studies examined CP or CS in relation to EVA task performance. Arm crank CP was associated with time to completion for EVA task circuits (r = −0.61 to −0.7028,29) and was found to be an independent predictor of the lowest performance quartile, yielding a predictive capability of 82.9%29. Treadmill CS was associated with time to completion for EVA task circuits (r = −0.56 to −0.8228,29) and 10 km walkback (r = −0.8528), and relationships were comparable among male (r = −0.82 to −0.88) and female (r = −0.78 to −0.82) subjects28. One study examined rowing CP and found that this metric was not associated with egress time (p = 0.1531).

Thresholds for success/completion time

Aerobic capacity: Several studies reported distinct thresholds, as identified through regressions, to be associated with EVA task circuit performance. In one such study, cycle VO2pk thresholds for four tasks (capsule egress, ambulation and supply transfer, rescue drag, and hill climb and descent) were comparable, ranging from 27.4 to 27.6 mL/kg/min34. Similar results in cycle VO2pk thresholds were demonstrated in an investigation by Sutterfield et al.35, where Lunar intensities resulted in a threshold of 26 mL/kg/min, yielding 100% sensitivity for determining failure. However, Martian simulated efforts for these tasks required greater fitness as the surface traverse threshold for subjects who completed the task was 40 mL/kg/min, and hill climb was 30.5 mL/kg/min35. Additionally, another investigation found treadmill VO2pk to be the best predictor of Martian field test success30, although no other aerobic capacity methods were utilized. VO2max values with 50% and 75% probability of finishing were 38.4 mL/kg/min and 40.0 mL/kg/min, respectively30. Additionally, those who maintained efforts <65% of VO2max could complete the Martian field test, with non-finishers working at 78% of VO2max30.

GET/VT: When performing field tests at greater intensities (e.g., Martian), GET for non-finishers was lower than those who finished (21.9 vs. 27.1 mL/kg/min), and average field test VO2pk values exceeded their GET (p = 0.005)30. Treadmill GET was an independent predictor of Martian field test failure. GET values with 50% and 75% probability of finishing were 20.1 mL/kg/min and 25.1 mL/kg/min30. Univariate logistic regression indicated that cycle VT was a significant predictor of task performance for surface traverse and hill climb tasks35. Additionally, cycle VT with 100% sensitivity for test failure was determined for surface traverse (Lunar: 16 mL/kg/min; Martian: 28.8 mL/kg/min) and hill climb tasks (Lunar: 14.8 mL/kg/min; Martian: 18.7 mL/kg/min)35.

Aerobic PPO: Univariate logistic regression indicated that both cycle and rowing PPO were significant predictors of task performance for surface traverse and hill climb tasks35. Additionally, cycle and rowing PPO thresholds were determined for surface traverse (Lunar: 188 W; Martian: 238–262.5 W) and hill climb tasks (Lunar: 188 W; Martian: 188 W)35. Cycle thresholds had strong sensitivity for identifying EVA test failure (100% sensitivity), while also performing well to minimize the identification of false positives (e.g., correctly identifying test completion; 62.5–89.7% specificity). Rowing thresholds had strong sensitivity for identifying EVA test failure (100% sensitivity), but lower specificity (33.3–68.9%).

Critical power/critical speed: Treadmill CS was found to be the single best predictor of 10 km walkback time, while CS and arm crank CP were predictors of field test performance time28 and rowing CP was a significant predictor of surface traverse and hill climb tasks35. Rowing CP thresholds, which indicated 100% sensitivity for test failure, were determined for successful completion of surface traverse (Lunar: 99.7 W; Martian: 170 W) and hill climb (Lunar: 124 W; Martian: 123 W)35. Thresholds emerged for arm crank CP, such that individuals with CP > 70.5 W were at low risk for having time to completion in the lowest performance quartile, while CP < 39.8 W were at high risk of having time in the lowest performance quartile29. Logistic regression models including arm crank CP had 90.2% sensitivity (correctly classified 46/51) and 63.2% specificity (correctly classified non finishers 12/19)29. No thresholds were investigated for arm crank CP or treadmill CS.

Muscular fitness metrics

Thresholds were identified as specific values determined through regression or receiver operator curves as being related to task performance. Relationships were reported as r, r2, or Somers’ D values.

Correlates of task performance (Fig. 2A, B)

Strength-isometric: Isometric strength is defined as the maximum voluntary force produced while in a static position. Isometric testing can be completed across a variety of muscle groups and joint positions, and is reported to be valid, reliable, and safe, making it an attractive method of assessing strength in multiple populations47. Maximal isometric force for leg press and knee extension accounted for at least 37% of the variance in completion time models for functional tasks primarily requiring lower body effort and ambulation, including supine and upright seat egress (r2 = 0.42–0.68), ladder climb (LP: r2 = 0.52–0.60), recovery from fall (r2 = 0.39–0.54), and object carry (r2 = 0.37–0.52; classified as both lower and upper body task)33. Maximal isometric force for bench press accounted for more variance in completion time models for upper body functional tasks, including hatch opening (r2 = 0.67) and object carry (r2 = 0.34), but less for construction activity board (r2 = 0.07)33. One study examined correlations between handgrip strength and EVA task performance, reporting inverse relationships between handgrip maximal voluntary contraction and time to complete material transport (r = −0.49, p < 0.05) and device operations tasks (r = −0.47, p < 0.01)29, such that a stronger grip strength resulted in quicker time to completion (i.e., better performance).

Similarly, changes in maximal voluntary isometric leg press force and functional performance outcomes in bed rest subjects and astronauts who completed 6-month-long missions aboard the ISS38 were seen. Reductions in leg press strength were negatively associated with impairments (i.e., increased completion time) on 3 of the 7 functional tasks, including seat egress and walk (D = −0.305, p < 0.001), recovery from fall (D = −0.363, p < 0.001), and object translocation (D = −0.285, p < 0.001). Additionally, changes in leg press strength were marginally associated (after controlling for False Detection Rate and multiple associations, 0.003 < p < 0.004) with reduced ladder climb time (D = −0.189, p = 0.004) and hatch opening strength (D = 0.175, p < 0.004). Changes in upper body strength were not significantly related to changes in any functional test outcome in this investigation (p > 0.05).

Strength-isokinetic: Another widely used type of strength assessment is isokinetic testing, defined as exercise that dynamically loads contracting muscle to its maximum capability while the speed of the movement remains constant throughout the entire range of motion. Maximal knee extension torque (60°/s) accounted for the most variance in models for completion time on the supine and upright seat egress and walk (r2 = 0.42–0.47), followed by ladder climb (r2 = 0.41), recovery from fall (r2 = 0.40)33. Additionally, relationships between isokinetic strength and functional task performance were all weaker than those between leg press strength and performance on functional tasks. Taylor et al.36 found that combined with knee extension total work and knee flexion total work, the lower-body strength measures accounted for 61.5% of the variability (though not significant, p = 0.15) in total time to complete the task battery (individual contributions of knee strength parameters were not reported). When performance on additional occupational strength tests (i.e., hand drilling and wrenching strength) was added to the model, 99% of the variability in time to completion could be explained.

Muscular Power: Muscular power is a function of the ability to produce force at high velocities of movement and has consistently demonstrated strong correlations with the performance of tasks of daily living48,49. Leg press peak power (normalized to body weight) accounted for the most variance in models for ladder performance (r2 = 0.59), whereas upright seat egress, object carry, and rise from fall were between r2 = 0.37–0.4933,34. Bench press peak power (normalized to body weight) accounted for the most variance in models for hatch opening performance (r2 = 0.62)33. Results from bed rest and spaceflight data indicated that lower body maximum power was significantly negatively related to performance on seat egress and walk (Somers D = -0.319), recovery from fall (D = −0.361), and object translation (D = −0.414), while upper body maximum power was not significantly correlated with any of the functional fitness tasks38. No associations between countermovement jump and function performance were reported34. The Wingate test is a common assessment of anaerobic capacity and peak power (presented in Ryder et al.34 as peak power achieved for 5 s of the 30-s test); however, no studies included in this review reported correlations between Wingate peak power and performance on any functional outcome.

Muscular endurance: Muscular endurance is the ability to perform repeated high-force and velocity movements without fatiguing. Leg press muscle endurance accounted for the most variance in models supine seat egress performance (r2 = 0.51) and ladder climb (r2 = 0.56)34, and was negatively associated with recovery from fall (Somers D = −0.462) and object translation (D = −0.325)38. Two-minute body weight squats were significantly negatively correlated with 10 km walkback performance (r = −0.23), physical abilities test (r = −0.41)28, and material transport field test (r = −0.36)29. Upper body muscle endurance, as measured by the pushup test, was negatively related to performance on the material transport field test (r = −0.42) and device operations field test (r = −0.59)29. Upper body muscle endurance measured by bench press total work was strongly predictive of hatch opening (r2 = 0.73), but was less predictive of performance on upright seat egress, object carry, and rise from fall (r2 = 0.04–0.21)33. Handgrip endurance was only assessed in one study29, but was not significantly related to performance on the material transport field test or device operations field test.

Thresholds for success/completion time

Strength-Isometric: Thresholds for unilateral isometric knee extension strength in the Ryder et al.33 study were relatively consistent across functional tasks requiring lower-body involvement (i.e., upright seat egress, rise from fall, supine seat egress, ladder climb, and object carry), ranging from 4.5–5.9 N/kg. Similarly, Ryder et al.34 reported thresholds for capsule egress, ambulation and supply transfer, crewmember rescue, and hill climb and descent tasks ranging from 5.34 to 5.54 N/kg. Thresholds for leg press isometric strength in Ryder et al.33 were highest for upright seat egress (17.8 N/kg), followed by similar thresholds for other tasks assessed (13.1–13.9 N/kg). Thresholds for leg press isometric strength in Ryder et al.34 were similar between all functional tests examined, ranging from 17.3 to 17.65 N/kg. The bench press threshold was ~4× greater for hatch opening (13.0 N/kg) than for object carry (3.4 N/kg) and construction activity board (3.1 N/kg)33. Additionally, thresholds for bench press isometric strength in Ryder et al.34 were similar between all functional tests examined, ranging from 6.39–6.67 N/kg. Only one investigation assessed full-body isometric strength via IMTP34. Similar thresholds for IMTP were observed between all functional tests examined, ranging from 18.12 to 18.53 N/kg.

Strength-Isokinetic: Ryder et al.34 reported similar isokinetic strength thresholds across muscle groups for each functional task assessed, including those reported for concentric knee extension (1.83–1.86 Nm/kg), eccentric knee extension (2.38–2.44 Nm/kg), concentric knee flexion (0.93–0.94 Nm/kg), eccentric knee flexion (1.40–1.43 Nm/kg), concentric ankle plantarflexion (1.21–1.22 Nm/kg), eccentric ankle plantarflexion (1.76–1.78 Nm/kg), concentric ankle dorsiflexion (0.33 Nm/kg), eccentric ankle dorsiflexion (0.57–0.58 Nm/kg), trunk extension (3.78–3.84 Nm/kg), and trunk flexion (1.88–1.90 Nm/kg). Thresholds for concentric knee extension in Ryder et al.33 showed more dispersion and were highest for upright and supine seat egress (1.8–1.9 Nm/kg), followed by rise from fall and object carry (1.7 Nm/kg), and then ladder climb (1.0 Nm/kg).

Muscular Power: Performance thresholds for leg press peak power reported in Ryder et al.34 were comparable across functional tasks (i.e., capsule egress, ambulation, and supply transfer, crewmember rescue, and hill climb and descent), ranging from 14.8 to 15.1 W/kg. Countermovement jump power thresholds were larger than leg press power values, ranging from 31.12 to 31.86 W/kg for the same tasks. Performance thresholds for bench press peak power reported in Ryder et al.34 were comparable for the same tasks, ranging from 6.22 to 6.49 W/kg. Only one study investigated Wingate peak power and values for capsule egress, ambulation and supply transfer, rescue drag, and hill climb and descent were closely related (range 5.58–5.83 W/kg)34.

Muscular Endurance: Performance thresholds for muscular endurance were comparable across functional tasks (i.e., capsule egress, ambulation and supply transfer, crewmember rescue, and hill climb and descent): leg press endurance (73.81–76.76 J/kg), isokinetic knee extension (22.84–23.34 J/kg), isokinetic knee flexion (12.75–12.98 J/kg), bench press (25.08–26.50 J/kg)34.

Simulation reported efforts

To determine the fitness level required for future exploration mission surface operations, physiological workload documented in EVA simulation studies and standalone tasks may provide additional insight.

Offload simulations/standalone tasks: In offload environments (NASA’s Partial Gravity Simulator POGO, simulating 1/6 g [Lunar] and 3/8 g [Martian]), standalone tasks, particularly those performed in Martian offload, were performed at high metabolic effort. When suited in 1/6 g, treadmill ambulation efforts ranged from 16.7–29.3 mL/kg/min, while 3/8 g suited condition yielded efforts ranging from 27.6–35.7 mL/kg/min39. For a 10 km walkback task in the Mark III suit at 1/6 g offload, average VO2 values of 2.0 ± 0.3 L/min, which equated to 50.8 ± 6.1% VO2pk (range 45–61% VO2pk)39. Average rate of perceived exertion was 11.8 ± 1.6 out of 20, which corresponded to “light” to “somewhat hard” categorization39; however, it is unknown how intensity (both quantified at percentage of aerobic capacity or perceived exertion) would compare when performed at a Martian offload.

1 g simulations/standalone tasks: Several studies performed in 1 g (i.e., without gravitational offloading) reported metrics of fitness and their relationship to simulation efforts and standalone EVA task performance. Similar to offloaded investigations, simulations performed at Martian intensities required greater fitness than Lunar efforts. Sutterfield et al.35 demonstrated that treadmill traverse performed in 1 g at Lunar intensities averaged 24.0 ± 1.8 mL/kg/min, equating to approximately ~67% of VO2pk, while the Martian traverse averaged 28.7 ± 1.8 mL/kg/min, equating to approximately ~80% VO2pk. Interestingly, failure rates for Martian traverse were more than 2× that of Lunar failure rates (38.5% vs. 13%)35. Additionally, a Lunar intensity 2 km hill climb was performed at 20 ± 2.3 mL/kg/min (~56% VO2pk) and a Martian hill climb at 26.0 ± 0.7 mL/kg/min (~73% VO2pk), where again a greater failure rate was seen with the Martian intensity task (25% vs. 13%)35.

In two studies that examined self-paced 2 h 1 g EVA simulations in a Mars analog suit designed to mimic geological surveying, efforts yielded average intensities of 35.9 ± 0.9 mL/kg/min37 and 81–85% of heart rate max32,37. VO2 was predictive of distance covered during EVA (r2 = 0.34), with greater oxygen demand per time period for fitter subjects (r2 = 0.86)37.

Critical appraisal for study and reporting quality

Using the AXIS tool, each study (Fig. 3) included clear study objectives, target populations, and outcomes that seemed appropriate to meet the aims. There were no indications of funding sources or conflicts of interest that would impact interpretation. Of these studies, 10 of 12 studies yielded assessments that indicated that study design, methods, and results were reported in a manner reflective of a quality investigation. While effect size and power estimates were not reported for any studies, information provided indicated that objectives, study design, and methodology were sufficiently described and appropriate to draw conclusions from the results reported. Two of the 12 studies did not report methods or results in a way that yielded confidence in findings. Discussion

This systematic review aimed to consolidate findings to identify which components of fitness were most related to EVA task and simulated EVA performance. From the 12 studies included in the review, 13 cardiorespiratory and 25 muscular fitness metrics were identified. Of the cardiorespiratory metrics, 8 were associated with performance. Treadmill VO2max and treadmill CS had the strongest association with EVA task and simulated EVA performance, where greater fitness was related to greater performance (i.e., quicker task completion times). Of the muscular metrics, 12 were associated with performance, with isometric leg press and bench press yielding the strongest predictive values of muscular strength measures and isotonic bench press yielding the strongest predictive value of endurance measures. Findings presented herein focus on 1 g participants and provide evidence that is predominantly associative in nature and appears to be driven by the task and context tested. While limitations of extending 1 g findings to future exploration settings exist, the identification of these key fitness metrics may provide useful information for refinement of NASA-STD-3001 and the development of future exploration exercise capabilities and monitoring systems.

Of the key physiologic factors that influence endurance exercise performance, aerobic capacity (VO2pk or VO2max)40 was the most common aerobic fitness metric, examined in 7/12 studies with EVA task circuits or standalone tasks. VO2pk assessed via treadmill demonstrated the strongest relation to task performance compared to other methods (i.e., arm crank)28,29, particularly for ambulatory tasks like 10 km walkback. Additionally, several studies determined VO2pk thresholds for task completion when performed at different intensities (e.g., Lunar [low: 25.7–26.1 mL/kg/min] vs Martian [moderate: 35–40 mL/kg/min] intensity)28,29,35; however, more research is needed to confirm that these intensities are representative of exploration EVA metabolic rates. Submaximal indicators of effort, such as VT (or GET), also demonstrated associations with EVA task performance. Because crewmembers may be limited by suit mobility, consumables conservation, and operations planning to prioritize safety and mission success, it is unlikely that prolonged maximal intensity efforts will be seen, unless in contingency scenarios. Therefore, submaximal workload indicators may be a candidate fitness metric of interest for further investigation. As both aerobic capacity and VT/GET require metabolic assessment for accuracy, research and testing involving portable gas exchange systems suitable for in-flight use in different gravitational environments is warranted.

Of the fitness assessments, CS (via treadmill) and CP (via arm crank) demonstrated the strongest relation to EVA task performance28,29, while other investigators identified thresholds for task success using rowing CP35. Because exploration EVAs are planned to be of extended duration (i.e., 4–8 h in length), these indicators of exercise tolerance and endurance43,45,46 may be of great interest; however, the considerations for applicability for fitness monitoring are dependent on balancing crew time, availability of in-flight exploration resources, and vehicle constraints. In particular, CP assessed via rowing35 presents a viable candidate for in-flight fitness monitoring that maximizes efficiency and limits consumables. Rowing CP can be completed in less than 10 min (warmup, 3-min “all-out” effort, and cool down)35 and could be performed without metabolic gas analysis, if metabolic monitoring was not available. Additionally, CP could be adapted for use on exploration exercise countermeasures or assessed via a cycle ergometer, increasing the operational applicability for fitness monitoring pre-, in-, and post-mission. Assessing the feasibility of performing CP within an exploration vehicle, including the determination of loads and dynamics to the vehicle, would be necessary prior to implementation. Conversely, while treadmill CS had the greatest association reported among all cardiovascular fitness metrics, the current utility of the test in an astronaut population may be limited. Because deconditioned crewmembers may be most vulnerable to injury early post-flight, treadmill running may not be advisable due to sensorimotor disruptions. Additionally, the assessment of CS described in Ade et al.28, required 4 separate bouts of running at speeds ranging from 90 to 120% VO2pk (ranging 2–15 min) on 4 separate occasions, which would not be feasible with respect to crew time and scheduling. Importantly, due to size, power, and mass constraints of vehicles, a treadmill is not currently planned as an exercise countermeasure on future exploration missions.

Data from the current manuscript indicate that tests involving ambulation (i.e., treadmill VO2pk, VT, CS) are related to simulated task performance in 1 g subjects. Additionally, in crewmembers, treadmill-based exercise confers unique benefits to the neurovestibular and sensorimotor systems50,51 and may positively impact EVA performance. Together, these findings highlight the utility of ambulatory-based assessments and exercise, supporting the need for treadmills to be included in the countermeasure systems of long-duration missions. Innovative treadmill designs that fit within the spacecraft mass, volume, and power constraints are urgently needed to ensure the countermeasure modalities needed to maintain crew health and performance are available during extended-exploration missions. Research is needed, and is currently underway, to determine the magnitude of impact that the lack of treadmill exercise has on crewmember health and performance, and may yield further data to justify a treadmill for exercise and monitoring purposes in future exploration missions.

Regarding lower body strength assessments most related to EVA task performance, isometric strength, particularly for leg press, demonstrated the strongest relationship to task performance. This relationship was strongest for tasks requiring lower body effort, such as seat egress and ladder climb33. Leg press isometric strength thresholds for task success range from 13.1 to 17.8 N/kg and are dependent on task33,34. For upper body strength assessments, isotonic bench press for endurance had the strongest relationship to hatch opening, but weaker relationships were seen with other upper body tasks, such as object carry or activity board33. Thresholds for isotonic bench press ranged from 25.08 to 26.50 J/kg34. Isometric testing is reliable and safe47 and has also been a part of the NASA research testing battery52 to assess changes in astronaut strength pre- to post-flight. Recently, IMTP has been included as a measure of in-flight strength being tested on current exercise hardware in several ongoing flight studies. As such, isometric assessments represent a favorable form of muscular testing that could be utilized for exploration mission fitness characterization.

Interestingly, while 1-RM assessments are used in strength standards for NASA crewmembers, no investigations included in this review examined 1-RM measures in relation to task performance. While 1-RM testing is considered the non-laboratory “gold standard” for dynamic strength assessments53 and generally regarded as safe across terrestrial populations54, spaceflight deconditioning can lead to spinal changes and atrophy of intrinsic spinal and lumbar paraspinal muscles55, potentially increasing the risk of injury in returning crew56 and possibly preventing the performance of a true maximum effort. Because these 1-RM standards had not previously been tested for their utility as performance indicators of EVA and were not included in any of the studies included in this literature review, understanding the relevance of these assessments in this population, who may be at increased risk of injury, is essential. While 1-RM measures were not assessed in the studies included in this literature, numerous other muscular metrics (e.g., isometric strength for lower body [leg press], upper body [bench press], and whole body [mid-thigh pull]), demonstrated strong relationships or predictive capabilities for simulated EVA task performance in terrestrial subjects, warranting further exploration into the potential utility for NASA standards with microgravity exposed crewmembers.

It is apparent that the relationship between fitness indicators and task performance is dependent on the design of the task/test and is an important consideration when interpreting the findings presented in this review. Designing a test or circuit that incorporates “failure” is one approach taken by several of these research investigations. Pushing subjects to failure allows for a better comparison of “maximal” fitness tests to “maximal” task performance; however, sequential back-to-back maximal tasks as part of a circuit may not be as representative of what might occur during EVAs due to suit metabolic, environmental, and consumable limits, which is a limitation of the current research presented herein. Using Apollo missions for reference, the average metabolic expenditure for Apollo 11-17 Lunar EVAs57 was 906 BTU/h (~11 mL/kg/min), with higher metabolic rates reported for tasks like a geological station activity (1393 BTU/h; ~16 mL/kg/min), ALSEP deployment (1297 BTU/h; ~15.5 mL/kg/min), and overhead task (1167 BTU/h; ~15 mL/kg/min), representing relatively low workloads. However, there were a few instances of heavier workloads. During EVA 1 of Apollo 16, the Lunar module pilot (LMP) carried, dropped, and re-assembled 2 large packages (21 pounds lunar weight each) during a total duration of 4.62 min58. The metabolic rate increased to 2300 BTU/h (~24 mL/kg/min) when the LMP carried the re-assembled packages 180 feet in 60 s, one of the highest recorded during Apollo missions, but quickly recovered to 1282 BTU/h (~16 mL/kg/min) within 3 min58. This example demonstrates that short, intense efforts with a rest between tasks may be more representative of EVA activity, as opposed to sustained intense effort to failure, for durations ranging from 20 to 36 min, as was employed by several of the task circuit studies28,29. Additionally, as EVAs represent one of the most perilous undertakings of spaceflight exploration7, performance on EVAs often includes methodical and controlled movements to reduce the risk of injury and decompression sickness, and workload intensities are limited by suit consumables and environmental control conditions; therefore, short-duration, high-intensity task circuits could far exceed the metabolic demand experienced during surface operations. Ultimately, tasks selection for the determination of minimum fitness required for surface EVAs is critical; understanding how tasks selected relate to expected EVA performance requires higher fidelity investigations in analog environments for a more realistic representation of end-to-end EVA.

Additional limitations to this study exist. Many studies presented data for correlations between fitness and task performance, but associations do not indicate whether improvements in fitness level will definitively lead to improvements in EVA performance. Heterogeneity existed with respect to EVA tasks tested and duration of assessment, which resulted in a wide range of values identified as thresholds for certain tasks and the inability to perform a meta-analysis of correlations. Beyond the standalone traverse task performed in offloaded conditions in a single study39, the fitness required to perform other operational tasks in offloaded gravitational conditions, over an extended period of time, is unknown. Similarly, most task circuit studies included were performed in shirt-sleeve conditions on level surfaces, which do not account for potential biomechanical constraints and limitations imparted by the pressurized spacesuit59 or added metabolic cost of uneven terrain14, which may alter and influence movement strategies needed to successfully complete tasks. Because few laboratories exist around the world to allow for these higher-fidelity investigations (i.e., appropriate offloading or suited testing), the research presented herein represents the best indication of how fitness may influence EVA task performance, given the obvious limitations. While this review focused on specific domains of fitness in relation to task performance in a bivariate manner, fitness is not the sole predictor of EVA performance, with other physiological systems impacted by microgravity exposure, change in gravitational fields, or influence of high-stress environment playing contributing roles.

In summary, results from this systematic review highlight that aerobic capacity, lower body strength (leg press), and upper body endurance and strength (bench press) yield the strongest relationships to EVA task performance studied in terrestrial populations, mostly in low-fidelity environments (1 g weighted suit, 1 g unsuited, etc). These findings may help inform revisions to the NASA-STD-3001 and provide information for consideration as exercise countermeasure and monitoring systems are developed for exploration missions. Ultimately, to best address how fitness will impact surface EVAs on the Moon and Mars, higher fidelity investigations, such as those including Lunar or Martian gravity offloading, are warranted.
