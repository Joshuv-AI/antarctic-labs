Hydrogen suppressed tumor growth during chronic intermittent hypoxia via modulating macrophage polarization
Authors: Qing-qing Liu, Chen-Bing Zhao, Zhuo Wang, Mengfang Sun, Ke-Rong Qi, Han Liu, Qian Li, Yashuo Zhao, Ya-Jing Guo, En-Sheng Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lung cancer remains a major challenge in clinical treatment, as current therapeutic strategies often fail to effectively halt disease progression. Chronic intermittent hypoxia (CIH), a hallmark pathological feature of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), has been implicated in promoting tumor invasion and metastasis. This study aimed to investigate the potential of hydrogen as an innovative adjunctive therapy for lung cancer. To evaluate the therapeutic effects of hydrogen, both in vitro and in vivo models were established. In vitro, an intermittent hypoxia (IH) tumor cell-macrophage co-culture system was used to assess cell proliferation, migration, and macrophage polarization. In vivo, tumor growth was monitored in a CIH mouse model, and tissue samples were subsequently analyzed via immunohistochemistry and western blot. Our results demonstrated that hydrogen exerted significant antitumor effects in vivo. Mechanistically, this effect was associated with a shift in macrophage polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and suppression of the CCL2-CCR2 signaling axis. In addition, in vitro studies revealed that hydrogen directly inhibited lung cancer cell survival and migration, and downregulated key components of the CCL2-CCR2 pathway, mirroring the effects observed with a CCR2 inhibitor. These findings highlighted that hydrogen treatment suppressed tumor growth by modulating the tumor immune microenvironment and inhibiting angiogenesis. Collectively, our results suggested that hydrogen may represented a novel and promising therapeutic strategy for lung cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57825-3.

Introduction

In recent years, lung cancer has remained the predominant cause of cancer-related mortality globally, presenting a substantial challenge to public health1. The post-surgical survival rate for lung cancer is notably lower than that of many other prevalent cancers, a disparity potentially attributable to the challenges associated with perioperative chemotherapy or radiotherapy in effectively targeting micro-metastatic disease2,3. Recently, gas therapy has garnered considerable attention as a novel approach in cancer treatment4,5. Among various gases, such as nitric oxide (NO), carbon monoxide (CO), and hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), hydrogen exhibits comparable anti-cancer properties and mechanisms. Importantly, hydrogen does not bind to hemoglobin in the bloodstream, thereby avoiding the toxic effects linked to hemoglobin interaction. Numerous animal studies have confirmed the safety of hydrogen administration5,6.

Due to its low molecular weight, hydrogen is capable of diffusing directly into tissues through the cell membrane, thereby demonstrating anti-oxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic properties7. Hydrogen exerted anti-tumor effects through the indirect regulation of gene expression8. A study involving 82 cases of advanced cancer treatment further corroborated the anti-tumor efficacy of hydrogen inhalation9. Additionally, hydrogen-rich water was found to counteract weight loss induced by gefitinib and naphthalene, thereby improving overall survival rates10. Clinical investigations into hydrogen therapy for patients with stage IV colon cancer have demonstrated that hydrogen inhalation reduced programmed death-1 (PD-1) expression on CD8⁺ T cells in peripheral blood, decreased CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion, and improved patient prognosis11. Furthermore, clinical studies have indicated that hydrogen inhalation significantly ameliorated immunosenescence in patients with non-small cell lung cancer after a two-week period9.

Inflammation serves as a pivotal contributor to carcinogenesis in both murine models and human subjects12. Within the tumor microenvironment (TME), macrophages, termed tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), display phenotypic plasticity and heterogeneity. This heterogeneity is apparent across various stages of tumor development, underscoring the capacity of TAMs to adapt to environmental stimuli, resulting in phenotypic polarization from pro-inflammatory (M1-like) to anti-inflammatory (M2-like) states13–16. Hypoxia is intricately linked to unfavorable cancer prognoses, with the hypoxic pathway frequently being activated during oncogenesis17. Our prior investigations have demonstrated that chronic intermittent hypoxia (CIH) induced lung cancer progression was linked to an inflammatory response18. In a murine model of lung cancer, changes in TAMs induced by CIH have been correlated with negative cancer outcomes19. The chemokine C-C motif ligand 2 (CCL2) has been extensively implicated in the recruitment of TAMs20. During tumor metastasis, CCL2 facilitates the recruitment of C-C chemokine receptor type 2 (CCR2)⁺ inflammatory monocytes and differentiated macrophages, thereby activating the chemokine cascade within these cells and effectively promoting tumor metastasis21,22. Whether hydrogen could attenaute CIH-induced tumor growth by modulating macrophage polarization is unexplored. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the hypothesis that hydrogen alleviated CIH-induced tumor growth by modulating macrophage polarization via the CCL2-CCR2 axis.

Results

Hydrogen reduced physiological conditions in CIH mice

First, we assessed the effects of hydrogen treatment with CIH mice. As shown in Fig. 1A, during CIH exposure, measurements included water intake, tumor mass, and behavioristics were detected. Compared with the CON group, mice in the CIH group exhibited a reduction in food intake after 21 days, whereas those in the H₂ and cisplatin groups showed improved, especially in H₂ group (Fig. 1B). Water intake increased following CIH exposure; these changes were reversed in the H₂ and cisplatin groups (Fig. 1C). Compared with the CON group, the body weight decreased in the CIH group, and the body gained after hydrogen treated (Fig. 1D). Tumor volume and weight was significantly elevated under CIH conditions but was markedly reduced in both the H₂ and Cisplatin groups (Fig. 1E-F). The open field test (OFT) revealed that the CIH group spent less time and traveled a shorter distance in the central zone, whereas the H₂ group displayed enhanced spontaneous activity (Fig. 1G-I). Collectively, these results suggested that hydrogen treatment ameliorated physiological conditions in CIH-exposed mice and suppressed tumor proliferation.

Hydrogen inhibited tumor proliferation and angiogenesis in CIH mice

To further elucidate the mechanism by which hydrogen suppresses tumor proliferation, we performed immunohistochemical (IHC) and western blot analyses. IHC analysis demonstrated a significant upregulation of the proliferation marker Ki67 in the CIH group. This CIH-induced elevation of Ki67 was mitigated by subsequent administration of either hydrogen or cisplatin (Fig. 2A-B). We next assessed the expression of key proliferation-related proteins, matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9), matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP2), and vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF-A) by western blot. The analysis revealed a concomitant upregulation of all three proteins in the CIH group relative to controls (Fig. 2C-F). Notably, the enhanced expression of these proteins was suppressed upon treatment with hydrogen or cisplatin (Fig. 2C-F). Taken together, these data provided compelling evidence that hydrogen exerted its protective effect by suppressing CIH-induced tumor proliferation.

Hydrogen regulated macrophage polarization in CIH mice

We next assessed the tumor immune microenvironment during CIH. F4/80 served as a macrophage marker for evaluating macrophage infiltration. Further immunofluorescence image revealed that the lower M1 marker (inducible nitric oxide synthase, iNOS) and the higher M2 marker (mannose receptor, CD206) were show in CIH group compared to the CON group (Fig. 3A,B). Following treatment with hydrogen or cisplatin, the levels of iNOS and CD206 tended to normalize (Fig. 3A-B). Concurrently, IHC analysis showed that the pro-inflammatory cytokines interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) were down-regulated in the CIH group, whereas the anti-inflammatory cytokines interleukin-4 (IL-4), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) were up-regulated (Fig. 3C-D). Hydrogen or cisplatin treatment effectively reversed these changes, elevating pro-inflammatory and reducing anti-inflammatory factor levels (Fig. 3C-D). Additionally, western blot analysis showed that the increased protein levels of immune checkpoint molecules, PD-1 and programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1), in CIH mice were suppressed after hydrogen or cisplatin treatment (Fig. 3E-G). Collectively, these results demonstrated that hydrogen ameliorated CIH-induced dysregulation of the tumor immune microenvironment.

CCL2/CCR2-mediated the development and progression of lung cancer

Next, we further analyzed the potential molecular targets through which hydrogen exerted its protective effects. The cancer genome atlas (TCGA) database analysis to reveal CCL2 expression in lung cancer subtypes, including lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), and small cell lung cancer (SCLC). It showed the elevated CCL2 expression in LUSC and SCLC when compared to normal tissues (Fig. 4A). At the same time, and high CCL2 expression was associated with poorer survival in patient of LUSC and LUAD (Fig. 4B). The CCL2 influences the development and progression of lung cancer through the infiltration of immune cells (Fig. 4C). The CCR2 was also increased in the LUSC and LUAD with poor survival, involving in the development and progression of lung cancer (Fig. 4D-F). Western blot indicated that the expression of CCL2 and CCR2 were all increased the CIH group, compared to the CON group. The high levels of CCL2 and CCR2 were was reduced after hydrogen and cisplatin treatment (Fig. 4G-I). These findings suggested that hydrogen may participate in tumor development by regulating CCL2-CCR2 signal pathway.

Hydrogen exerted tumor-suppressive via CCL2-CCR2 signaling pathway

To definitively interrogate the involvement of the CCL2-CCR2 axis in hydrogen-mediated protection, we implemented a pharmacological blockade using the CCR2 antagonist HY-108323. Initial viability assessment via cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay confirmed that 50 µM HY-108323 effectively impaired the proliferation of both RAW264.7 and Lewis lung cancer (LLC) cells (Fig. 5A-B). A scratch wound healing assay further established that the pro-migratory phenotype conferred by intermittent hypoxia (IH) was significantly attenuated by either HY-108323 or hydrogen treatment (Fig. 5C). Subsequent immunophenotyping by flow cytometry uncovered that IH drove a phenotypic shift in macrophages, marked by a reduction in the M1 marker iNOS and a concomitant rise in the M2 marker CD206. This shift was nullified by intervention with HY-108323 or hydrogen (Fig. 5D-E). Finally, western blot analysis provided mechanistic evidence, showing that the IH-induced upregulation of key pathway and effector proteins (CCL2, CCR2, PD-1, PD-L1, VEGF-A, and MMP2) was collectively suppressed by both treatments (Fig. 5F-L). This multi-faceted evidence strongly confirmed that the anti-tumor effects of hydrogen through the CCL2-CCR2 signal pathway.

Discussion

Lung cancer is the most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally. Its mortality rate is projected to far surpass that of gastric and breast cancers in the forthcoming decades23–25. Given that the majority of patients receive a diagnosis at advanced stages and exhibit resistance to standard treatments, current therapeutic approaches encompass surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and biotherapy; nonetheless, overall survival rates remain suboptimal26. Clinical investigations have demonstrated that inhalation of hydrogen gas markedly enhanced immune system function in patients with NSCLC after a two-week period9. The cyclical occurrence of hypoxia and normoxia, driven by tumor expansion and extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, facilitates neovascularization and subsequent vascular collapse27,28. CIH has been demonstrated to enhance angiogenesis and induce cancer stem cell-like properties in a primary lung cancer mouse model29,30. The present study demonstrated that hydrogen therapy could mitigate tumor proliferation induced by CIH and influenced the polarization state of tumor-associated macrophages. CIH is known to facilitate drug resistance, cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in NSCLC31. Furthermore, other studies have indicated that CIH accelerated tumor growth, migration, and metastasis in various malignancies, including melanoma, breast cancer, and prostate cancer32–36. Consistent with our findings, CIH exposure for 21 days resulted in increased tumor volume and promoted liver metastasis in mice.

Ki67 staining demonstrated an increase in tumor proliferation within the CIH group. VEGF-A is pivotal in vasculogenic mimicry during tumor progression, playing a crucial role in both vasculogenic mimicry37 and angiogenesis38. Targeted inhibition of VEGF-A has been shown to suppress tumor angiogenesis39. Beyond its role in angiogenesis, VEGF40 and MMP241 are also involved in tumor cell proliferation, differentiation, invasion, metastasis, and immune evasion. Both MMP242,43 and MMP9 are essential proteases that facilitate vascular growth and the release of VEGF-A, and they are closely associated with tumor metastasis and carcinogenesis44.

Immune cells have been proposed as a potential mechanism linking CIH and cancer30. In a murine model of lung cancer, CIH-induced modifications in TAMs correlate with poorer prognostic outcomes19. As the predominant immune cells within the TME, TAMs could polarize into M1 and M2 phenotypes, thereby playing dual roles in tumor progression45–47. TAMs are significantly associated with tumor size, differentiation, invasion depth, lymph node metastasis, and tumor node metastasis (TNM) staging in lung cancer48. Macrophages facilitate metastasis by enhancing tumor cell motility, invasion, and extravasation; promoting angiogenesis; and contributing to immune suppression and chemotherapy resistance49–51. Metastasis-associated macrophages differ from primary TAMs and express markers associated with inflammatory monocytes, macrophages, and CCR221,52. Furthermore, macrophages communicate with other cells through secreted products to coordinate inflammatory or homeostatic responses53.

M1 macrophages are known to produce pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-12, and IL-2354,55, whereas M2 macrophages secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β56. Inflammation associated with cancer significantly contributes to disease progression17. For example, IL-4 enhances the expression of CCR1, CCL2, and CCL3, facilitating the recruitment and functional activation of monocytes and macrophages57,58. The interplay between inflammation, growth factors, and DNA-damaging agents like iNOS further increases the risk of cancer initiation and progression59. Ultimately, TAM polarization is directly regulated by cancer cells within the TME60. The polarization state of macrophages determines their functional role within TAMs. The M1 phenotype exhibits anti-tumor activity by inhibiting tumor cell growth and serves as a positive prognostic indicator for patient survival17,60,61. Conversely, M2 TAMs promote tumor progression through various mechanisms, including the stimulation of cancer cell proliferation via mitogenic factors such as fibroblast growth factor (FGF), TGF-β, and epidemal growth factor (EGF), as well as the facilitation of angiogenesis62,63. This pro-angiogenic response is amplified under hypoxia, where TAM-derived VEGFA stimulates chemotaxis and upregulates MMP9, leading to ECM degradation and the release of bioactive VEGF-A63. Among these factors, TGF-β plays a central role in angiogenesis and is clinically linked to aggressive disease64. Therapeutic modulation of this axis is possible, as evidenced by hydrogen-rich water intervention, which shifts polarization towards the pro-tumorigenic M2 phenotype65.

CCL2, or monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), is a key chemokine regulating immune cell recruitment and activation66. Its elevated secretion is a hallmark of both primary and metastatic tumors. Across numerous cancers (e.g. breast, colorectal, prostate, melanoma, gastric, and ovarian cancer), activation of the CCL2-CCR2 axis is strongly linked to tumor progression and metastasis67. The underlying mechanism involves the recruitment of immunosuppressive cells, including monocytes/macrophages and regulatory T cells (Tregs), which in turn promote the survival, growth, and invasion of CCR2⁺ tumor cells68,69. Furthermore, CCL2 directly enhances the invasive capacity of cancer cells by upregulating MMP2 and MMP9 expression69. Functional studies confirm that silencing CCL2 suppresses primary tumor growth, lung metastasis, cancer stem cell renewal, and the recruitment of immunosuppressive macrophages70. These findings are corroborated by clinical bioinformatics analyses, which consistently associate high CCL2 expression with a poor prognosis across various tumors, underscoring its long-appreciated role in TAM recruitment (Fig. 6).

In recent years, hydrogen has emerged as a notable medical gas molecule in biomedicine, with growing interest in its application for tumor therapy71. By modulating inflammation, hydrogen demonstrates potential in preventing tumor formation and progression, while also reducing the side effects associated with chemotherapy and radiotherapy72. Its anti-tumor properties are underpinned by direct effects on cancer cell cycle progression; specifically, hydrogen inhibits the expression of key regulators like cyclin D1, CDK4, and CDK6, inducing G2/M phase arrest in various cell lines73. Furthermore, hydrogen targets cancer stem cells, leading to a significant reduction in tumor volume74. Hydrogen may also inhibit tumor cell migration by modulating pathways like RhoA/ROCK, thereby interfering with cytoskeletal reorganization essential for cell movement75. In lung cancer models, the suppressive role of hydrogen is further evidenced by its ability to downregulate structural maintenance of chromosomes 3 (SMC3) expression, thereby inhibiting the proliferation of A549 and H1975 cell lines and ameliorating disease development76. Moreover, evidence indicates that hydrogen may directly bolster the activity of specific immune effectors, such as cytotoxic T cells, to facilitate a more robust antitumor response71,74.

Regarding translational application, this study used a H₂-O₂ (2:1) gas mixture for in vivo experiments. The use of such a mixture—in both experimental and clinical settings—requires strict engineering controls and safety protocols (e.g., ventilation, leak monitoring, ignition source elimination, flame arresters, and check valves). Clinically, although this mixture has been explored in certain research and registration trials using specific delivery systems77, its widespread adoption still requires standardized risk assessments and regulatory compliance.

This preliminary study on hydrogen in tumor therapy has several limitations. The molecular mechanisms of the antitumor effects are not fully elucidated, and critical parameters including dosage and delivery route remain suboptimal. Moreover, the lack of a hyperoxia control group (oxygen alone) prevents us from ruling out the possibility that the observed tumor-suppressive effect may be partially due to oxygen rather than solely to hydrogen. Future work will focus on addressing these issues.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that hydrogen may have the potential to decrease tumor proliferation and growth under conditions of CIH, an effect that appears to be mediated, at least in part, by suppressing macrophage polarization. Furthermore, these findings imply that hydrogen may potentially serve as a safe and effective therapeutic approach for lung cancer treatment.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

Reagents: Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM, 8123153, Gibco, USA), fetal bovine serum (FBS, 10270106, Gibco, USA), Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8, K1018, Apexbio, USA), Protein quantification kit (BCA Assay, KTD3001, Abbkine Biotechnology, China), Cisplatin (2H0336803, Qilu Pharmaceutical, China), and CCR2 antagonist (HY-108323, Med Chem Express, China).

Antibodies: Ki67 (AB16667, Abcam, USA), MMP2 (BS72289, Bioworld, China), MMP9 (10375-2-AP, Proteintech, China), VEGF-A (AP0742, Bioworld, China), PD-1 (A00178, Boster, China), PD-L1 (A00109-3, Boster, China), CCL2 (BA1843, Boster, China), CCR2 (A00158-4, Boster, China), IL-6 (DF6087, Affinity, China), IL-1β (516288, , China), TGF-β (AF1027, Affinity, China), TNF-α (AF7014, Affinity, China), IL-4 (AF5142, Affinity, China), IL-10 (DF6894, Affinity, China), F4/80 (DF2789, Affinity, China) and iNOS (bs-20601P, Bioss, China), and CD206 (A02285-2, Boster, China).

Animals and cells

Male C57BL/6 N mice, aged 4 weeks and weighing 18–20 g, were procured from Beijing Weitong Lihua Experimental Animal Technology Co., Ltd. The animals were housed in pathogen-free conditions, maintained under a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with unrestricted access to food and water. All experimental procedures adhered to the guidelines outlined in the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and received approval from the Animal Care and Use Committee of Hebei University of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. DWLL202205008).

LLC cells were purchased from Wuhan Ponosi Life Technology Co., Ltd. LLC cells (1 × 10⁶ in 100 µL PBS per mouse) were subcutaneously injected into the right axilla to establish tumor-bearing mice. The mice were randomly allocated into four groups: the control group (CON), the CIH group, the CIH combined with hydrogen (CIH + H₂) group, and the CIH combined with cisplatin (CIH+Cisplatin) group. Mice in the CON group were housed in a normoxic chamber, whereas the remaining groups were placed in an intermittent hypoxic chamber. The CIH mouse model was established to simulate the conditions of OSA. The mice were housed in a regulated hypoxic chamber (OxyCycler, BioSpherix Ltd., Parish, NY, USA). The oxygen concentration cycled every 3 min by decreasing from 21% to 5% over 1.5 min and then returning to 21% over the next 1.5 min. This CIH regimen was applied for 8 h daily (9:00–17:00) over 21 days. Mice in the CIH + H₂ group were transferred daily to a transparent, sealed chamber and exposed to an H₂-O₂ gas mixture from 17:00 to 19:00 every day. This H₂-O₂ mixture (66% hydrogen, 33% oxygen) was generated via water electrolysis using a hydrogen oxygen atomizer (AMS-H-01, Shanghai Asclepios Medical Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The CIH+Cisplatin group received weekly intraperitoneal injections of cisplatin at a dose of 0.1 mL/10 g. Throughout the experimental period, food and water intake as well as body weight were monitored to assess tumor progression. On day 21, all mice were anesthetized and sacrificed by intraperitoneal injection of 2.5% tribromoethanol (0.2 mL/10 g), after which the tumors were harvested, photographed, and weighed, and the body weight of mice was measured after tumor removal.

Open field test

OFT was used to evaluate the behavioral performance of the mice. The apparatus consisted of a white, enclosed arena (50 × 50 × 40 cm) divided into 16 equal squares, with a camera mounted directly above. After a 1-hour acclimation to the testing environment, each mouse was placed in the center of the arena with a consistent starting position and orientation. Their activity was recorded over a 5-minute session using the Smart 3.0 system, which tracked total movement distance, movement time, the number of entries and the total distance traveled in the central area. The arena was thoroughly cleaned with alcohol and dried between trials to eliminate residual odors or waste.

Immunohistochemistry

Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin, subsequently prepared into 5-micrometre paraffin sections. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the samples in citrate-based antigen repair buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0).

For IHC analysis, sections were blocked with goat serum at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by the application of primary antibodies, which were incubated overnight at 4 °C. Then, the sections were washed with PBS, and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Immunohistochemical staining was enhanced using a DAB kit, and cell nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, the sections were dehydrated through a graded alcohol series, vitrificated in xylene, and sealed with neutral resin. Visualization of images and quantification of the mean density of positive cells were conducted using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

For immunofluorescence (IF) staining analysis, sections were similarly blocked with goat serum at 37 °C for 1 h to minimize non-specific binding. They were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody mixtures targeting F4/80 and iNOS, or F4/80 and CD206. After PBS washes, the corresponding fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were applied and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Following another round of PBS washes, the sections were sealed with an antifluorescence quencher containing DAPI. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (DM6B, Leica, Germany).

Western blot

The weighed tumor tissues were homogenized on ice in pre-cooled radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer containing protease inhibitor, phosphatase inhibitor, and phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. The protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit. Subsequently, the denatured protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred into polyvinylidenefluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1–2 h. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL substrate and analyzed with Image J software.

Bioinformatics analysis

To investigate the roles of CCL2 and CCR2 in lung cancer, their expression profiles in tumor and normal tissues and their association with patient survival were analyzed using the National center for biotechnology information (NCBI), Gene expression omnibus (GEO, GSE166720, GSE67061, GSE60052), and the TCGA databases. Meanwhile, the TIMER database was utilized to derive immune infiltration scores from TCGA, enabling an examination of the correlations between CCL2/CCR2 expression and immune cell abundance.

Cell culture

Mouse RAW264.7 cells, obtained from Wuhan Ponosi Life Technology Co., Ltd., were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 U/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. To simulate OSA in vitro, cells were subjected to IH by cycling the O₂ concentration between 1% and 21% every 10 min. After 24 h of IH treatment, the supernatant was collected, centrifuged, and applied to LLC cells for co-culture. The co-culture models included the following groups: CON, IH, HY-108,323, H₂, and HY-108,323 + H₂. In the H₂-treated groups, the culture medium was replaced with H₂-rich medium every 2 h.

CCK-8 assay

RAW264.7 and LLC cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. The cells were then treated with varying concentrations of a CCR2 antagonist for 24 h. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance of each well was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to the untreated control group.

Flow cytometry analysis

Following a 24-hour co-culture with LLC cells, RAW264.7 cells were collected and centrifuged. The cell pellets were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min in the dark, washed, and resuspended in PBS. Subsequently, cells were permeabilized and blocked for 45 min, incubated with a primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, and then with a diluted fluorescent secondary antibody for 1 h in the dark. After a final wash, the cells were resuspended in 100 µL of flow cytometry staining buffer (e.g., PBS with 1% FBS), and data from at least 10,000 single-cell events were acquired on a flow cytometer for analysis.

Scratch assay

LLC cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured until a confluent monolayer was achieved. On the subsequent day, a linear scratch was introduced into each well using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip. Following this, the wells were washed with PBS to eliminate dislodged cells. Transwell inserts containing pre-cultured RAW264.7 cells were then positioned into the wells. The co-culture system was subsequently exposed to the designated treatments for a duration of 24 h. Finally, cell migration was observed under a microscope.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as the mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0, lBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA), with comparisons among groups conducted by one-way ANOVA followed by LSD post hoc tests. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Graphing was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).
