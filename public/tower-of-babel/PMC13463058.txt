Confined ionic order in atomic nanowires of rare-earth chlorides unveiled via symmetry-guided structural screening
Authors: Haitao Zhang, Ryo Nakanishi, Takefumi Yoshida, Masahiko Nishijima, Carmen Herrmann, Masahiro Yamashita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Low-dimensional materials can exhibit unusual structural, electronic, and magnetic properties that arise from nanoscale confinement and quantum effects. However, determining their local atomic structures remains challenging, especially when long-range periodicity is limited and conventional crystallographic approaches are difficult to apply. Here, we show a symmetry-guided screening strategy for resolving confined one-dimensional materials by combining scanning transmission electron microscopy with reactive force-field molecular dynamics and density functional theory. We encapsulate rare-earth chlorides within single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) to form stable LnCl3@SWCNTs hybrids (Ln = Y, Gd, Dy, Er), which contain ordered one-dimensional atomic nanowires. The screening strategy identifies structural models that are consistent with experimental imaging and energetic stability, enabling local configurations to be distinguished. Electronic and magnetic analyses, particularly for Dy-based systems using complete active space self-consistent field calculations and Boltzmann transport theory, reveal how local symmetry and confinement influence their functional properties. This experiment–theory framework provides a broadly applicable route for probing short-range-ordered nanostructures, establishing structure–property correlations, and guiding the design of functional low-dimensional materials.

Introduction

Over the past two decades, low-dimensional materials have attracted considerable attention owing to their rich structural, electronic, and magnetic behaviors. Among them, the encapsulation of guest molecules and atomic nanowires within single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) has emerged as an effective strategy for probing and manufacturing matter at the nanoscale1. SWCNTs provide a one-dimensional platform for confining, stabilizing, and manipulating individual molecules and low-dimensional structures. A wide variety of species have been encapsulated in SWCNTs, including molecules2–5, metallic and non-metallic atoms6–9, fullerenes10–12, metal halides13–20, and coordination compounds21–24. The one-dimensional nanocavity of SWCNTs acts not only as a protective shell but also as a nanoscale reaction vessel, often promoting the formation of low-dimensional nanostructures25. Consistent with this confined environment, previous studies have shown that encapsulation of metal salts within SWCNTs can reshape the coordination geometry of metal centers. For example, lanthanide ions, which generally favor high coordination numbers, typically exhibit sixfold coordination after encapsulation13, whereas potassium iodide and cobalt iodide transform from sixfold to fourfold coordination19,20. Such confinement-induced changes in coordination geometry are likely to give rise to emergent physical properties distinct from those of their bulk counterparts. These discoveries have deepened our understanding of nanoscale physics and provided insights relevant to molecular electronics, quantum devices, and advanced materials engineering26–31.

Precise structural characterization of these encapsulated systems is crucial for interpreting their physical properties. In principle, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), scanning tunneling microscopy, and various spectroscopic methods can be employed to directly visualize and analyze the internal structures. However, due to their often non-crystalline nature, these systems are difficult to characterize using conventional high-performance microscopy imaging methods, X-ray diffraction, or electron diffraction techniques. This challenge is particularly pronounced for rare-earth halide encapsulated systems, first reported by the Green group13. The large ionic radii and high coordination numbers of rare-earth ions, along with their flexible, non-directional ionic bonding, make it difficult to resolve their structures, unlike typical molecules or coordination complexes, which possess well-defined topologies. To overcome these limitations and develop a universal methodology for structural resolution, we develop a symmetry-guided screening approach that integrates theoretical modeling and experimental analysis to uncover the structural configuration. Here, we selected SWCNTs with highly uniform diameters and synthesized a series of rare-earth chloride hybrids, LnCl3@SWCNTs (Ln = Y, Gd, Dy, Er), which serve as model systems to validate the proposed approach. Using scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM), we obtained several high-resolution patterns of heavy atoms. By applying the screening protocol, we were able to resolve their atomic structures and investigate the corresponding physical properties.

Results

Synthesis of LnCl3@SWCNTs hybrids

To obtain homogeneously encapsulated nanowires, anhydrous rare-earth chlorides LnCl3 were treated with SWCNTs exhibiting a narrow diameter distribution (1.4 ± 0.12 nm) using a high-vacuum sublimation method (Fig. 1). The purified and end-open SWCNTs were heated with LnCl3 under high-vacuum conditions to form LnCl3@SWCNTs hybrids (see “Methods” for experimental details). As the dominant gaseous species, monomeric LnCl3, owing to its small molecular size, can efficiently diffuse into the inner cavities of SWCNTs32–34. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) confirmed successful encapsulation in LnCl3@SWCNTs (Ln = Y, Gd, Dy, Er), yielding the LnCl3 mass fraction in the hybrids of 6.00%, 16.40%, 9.46%, and 12.03%, respectively (see Supplementary Note 1). These values indicate appreciable yet sample-dependent loading; the corresponding filling fractions are estimated later using the nanowire–SWCNT hybrid structural models. Contrary to the expectation of a single one-dimensional nanostructure14, LnCl3 forms polymorphic nanowires inside SWCNTs (Fig. 2). The zigzag nanowire structure previously observed in SWCNTs¹³ corresponds to the TZ structure in our classification. Our STEM observations further reveal several additional motifs, including the types of “X” and “O” (TX), “bamboo” (TB), “staggered” (TS), helix (TH), and “U” (TU). It is important to emphasize that, due to the limited detection range of STEM, the possible existence of as-yet unobserved nanowires cannot be excluded.

Challenges in atomic structure reconstruction

Although HAADF imaging allows us to approximate the positions of heavy atoms, a substantial amount of structural information remains inaccessible. First, the atomic positions are projected onto a two-dimensional plane, resulting in the loss of one spatial coordinate. Second, lighter atoms are obscured by the stronger scattering signals from heavier atoms, while in the annular bright-field (ABF) images, chlorine signals are masked by SWCNTs, hindering reliable identification. Nevertheless, resolving the precise structures of these chlorine atoms is essential for interpreting the LnCl3@SWCNTs’ physical properties. While earlier research has provided valuable insights into related systems, these methods prove inadequate when directly applied to the present challenges35–40.

To address this challenge, we devised a systematic workflow to resolve reliable atomic structures and provide a foundation for subsequent studies of physical properties (Fig. 3). Specifically, to tackle the first issue, we manually constructed the positions of the heavy atoms by carefully analyzing the HAADF images, examining individual cases (i.e., TX, etc.), and estimating the lost depth information. By systematically scanning each structural parameter, in principle, we were able to sample the entire space of possible configurations. Regarding the second issue, unlike organic molecules that possess well-defined topologies, the {LnCl3}n system is governed by non-directional ionic bonding, which results in a high degree of structural flexibility. In principle, structures could be optimized using density functional theory (DFT) or ab-initio molecular dynamics (AIMD). However, standard DFT optimization methods are prone to becoming trapped in local minima, while the high computational cost of AIMD makes it impractical for our purposes. Therefore, we proposed a screening method for nanowire structures based on molecular dynamics (MD), DFT, and local symmetry analysis. By employing a hierarchical structural optimization strategy—from low- to high-accuracy levels—and stepwise screening based on local symmetry, structurally unreasonable configurations are progressively eliminated. The remaining candidates are then subjected to further optimization in the subsequent stages. This process is repeated several times until structures of higher symmetry and lower energy are obtained.Fig. 3Workflow for {LnCl3}n structural screening and property calculations.Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) measurements guide the construction of conjectured {YCl3}n models. Initial atomic model construction based on STEM imaging

A preliminary estimate of the metal-atom positions is necessary to construct reasonable {LnCl3}n nanowire geometries. HAADF images with characteristic geometric features were analyzed, and the local intensity profiles were fitted with Gaussian functions to extract key parameters (pn) describing the structural motifs. Multiple local units were measured and averaged to obtain baseline values for the initial structural model. In the modeling, Y atoms were used as proxies for the other Ln atoms because of their similar ionic radii.

Using the TX model as an example, three parameters could be directly extracted from the STEM data: the width (p1), height (p2), and unit-cell length (p3) (Fig. 4b). By contrast, the depth coordinate (p4) of the two Y atoms is not accessible from the STEM image and was therefore estimated, with its upper bound constrained by the SWCNT diameter and the atomic van der Waals radius. The initial atomic model was then constructed by placing ideal planar YCl3 units around the Y atoms to define the Cl positions (Fig. 4c, d). To account for potential systematic discrepancies between the geometric parameters extracted from STEM images and those obtained after structural optimization, p1, p2, and p3 were scaled from 0.8 to 1.4 in 7 increments, whereas p4 was varied from 2.0 to 7.0 Å in 6 increments (Fig. 4e). This grid search generated 2058 geometrical configurations for the TX model and 88,249 across all scenarios. In the calculations, we found that PBE-D3 is sufficient to describe the YCl3 systems as well as the graphite system (see “Methods”). To clarify the interaction between YCl3 species and graphitic carbon, we examined the adsorption of a YCl3 monomer and a Y2Cl6 dimer on a graphene surface as simplified model systems. The calculations show that YCl3 monomers tend to polymerize on the graphene surface, indicating that the formation of polymeric {YCl3}n nanowires is favored under confinement by the curved graphitic surface of SWCNTs. Consequently, in a {YCl3}n@SWCNT system, yttrium atoms would be wrapped within the chlorine shell, effectively suppressing any yttrium-carbon interactions. Furthermore, the interaction between chlorine and carbon is relatively weak (see “Methods”). Thus, during the structural screening of {YCl3}n nanowires, the SWCNTs do not need to be explicitly modeled. Instead, simply restricting the radial diameter of the nanowires is sufficient. This significantly reduces computational cost, as it eliminates the need for an excessively large supercell to mitigate the effects of the incommensurability between a {YCl3}n system and one specific SWCNT.

It is worth noting that conventional pairwise force fields, such as the Lennard-Jones potential, are inadequate to accurately describe the Y-Cl system. Therefore, the reactive force field (ReaxFF) was selected41. It has a significant degree of flexibility, allowing the formation and breaking of chemical bonds. Due to the absence of existing ReaxFF parameters for the Y-Cl system, parameterization is required. However, the large number of parameters in the ReaxFF file (105, in this case) makes it challenging to obtain the desired results using conventional optimization algorithms (e.g., gradient descent), with the common problem of very slow convergence and easy trapping into local minima. For this reason, we propose an optimization scheme that combines genetic algorithms with machine learning assistance, implemented in a Python script named ReaxFFFitting42. To parameterize and make the optimized force field descriptive of the vast majority of scenarios, we have chosen several subsystems as references. For the YCl3 molecule, its single Y-Cl bond lengths, Cl-Y-Cl bond angles, and umbrella deformation of the Y atoms were scanned. The MD simulations were performed for YCl3 and Y2Cl6 molecules, and snapshots were taken from the trajectories. For the crystal structure, we scanned its unit cell sizes and also took the MD snapshots (Supplementary Figs. 40 and 41). The structure files of the reference subsystems and the optimized parameter files (YCl.ff) are provided as Supplementary Data 1. It is important to note that since only a limited number of reference subsystems were selected for this optimization, this approach may not be applicable to other systems that are structurally largely dissimilar to the selected reference, e.g., the Cl2 molecule or yttrium metal. The optimized parameter fails to accurately capture undercoordinated molecules, possibly due to the mismatch of its coordination penalties with the systems outside the scope of the reference subsystems. Nevertheless, for the seven representative subsystems, the optimized ReaxFF parameters qualitatively reproduce the DFT results, especially for configurations near their equilibrium geometries (Supplementary Fig. 42).

Among these structures, the TX configuration is of particular interest (Fig. 1). It consists of six distorted YCl6 octahedra connected via shared faces and shared vertices, forming a Y6Cl18 structural unit. It is hypothesized that the {Y6Cl18}n nanowires may twist within the SWCNTs, resulting in characteristic HAADF image patterns that transition from “X” to a dice-6-like, an “O” shape, and finally to a diamond-like shape. The TZ structure features coplanarly linked columns of capped trigonal prisms, whereas TH forms intertwined helical chains built from trigonal prismatic units. The remaining structures display mixed coordination motifs: TS combines octahedral and trigonal prismatic units, while TB and TU are both dominated by octahedral units but differ by the presence of a higher-coordination square antiprismatic unit (TB) or a lower-coordination square pyramidal unit (TU). Energetic stability of encapsulated nanowires in SWCNTs

To investigate the structure of {YCl3}n nanowire–SWCNT hybrids, a series of {YCl3}n@SWCNT models was constructed based on the obtained TZ and TX structures, using SWCNTs with diameters ranging from 1.3 to 1.5 nm. Structural optimization revealed that the stability of the hybrid systems is highly sensitive to the diameter of the SWCNTs. Building on symmetry-guided screening and stability analysis, we establish structurally and energetically favorable atomic models of the confined nanowires and their SWCNT hybrids as the basis for property investigations. We then employ spectroscopic techniques to probe local coordination and charge transfer and examine magnetic and electrical transport properties through combined experimental and theoretical analyses, with calculations explicitly based on the resolved nanowire geometries, enabling direct structure–property correlations.

Local coordination and charge-transfer analysis

To get the information about the coordination structure around the Ln atom, X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) was acquired for LnCl3@SWCNTs (Ln = Y, Dy, Er). In the dysprosium case, the white line peak intensity in the X-ray absorption near-edge structure spectra increased in DyCl3@SWCNTs, indicating that the atomic charge of the encapsulated Dy is slightly higher than that of the pristine DyCl3 (Fig. 8a), attributed to nanowire-to-SWCNT charge transfer. As the Dy L3-edge extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) results, the bond distances of Dy-Cl and coordination number for DyCl3@SWCNTs were calculated as (2.71 Å, 3.9 ± 0.7) and (2.52 Å, 3.7 ± 0.6) (Supplementary Table 17). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) reveals a broadened C 1s peak in LnCl3@SWCNTs, accompanied by a shift of ≈0.1 eV to lower binding energy relative to the purified SWCNTs (Fig. 8b), which can be attributed to charge transfer from the {LnCl3}n nanowires to the SWCNTs. This interpretation is further supported by the redshift of the Raman G band, which exhibits a 5–8 cm⁻¹ downshift in the hybrid relative to the purified SWCNTs (Fig. 8c). Magnetic properties and slow relaxation behavior

Based on the above analysis, we infer that encapsulation of LnCl3 within SWCNTs modifies the local coordination environment of Ln3+ ions, thereby altering the crystal field and consequently their magnetic behavior. As a representative example, Dy3+ ions, renowned for their strong magnetic anisotropy, exhibit slow magnetic relaxation in anhydrous DyCl3, with an effective energy barrier (Ueff) of 3.0 cm−1. Upon thermal treatment under high vacuum, the magnetic properties of the quenched DyCl3 are markedly enhanced: magnetic hysteresis becomes observable at 1.82 K, accompanied by a substantial increase of Ueff to 106.5 cm−1. See Supplementary Note 8.1 for details. This enhancement indicates that thermal activation induces a reconstruction of the local coordination environment, likely promoting the formation of polyhedral geometries with stronger axial crystal fields and enhanced magnetic anisotropy. Such a structural evolution is expected to directly impact the magnetic response of DyCl3 nanowires confined within SWCNTs, as discussed below.

To elucidate the underlying structure–property relationship, we next perform complete active-space self-consistent field (CASSCF) calculations. In the ideal crystal, Dy3+ adopts a distorted square antiprismatic coordination geometry with approximate C2v symmetry, with a CASSCF calculated Ueff of 42.3 cm−1 (Fig. 9f). The low experimental Ueff (3.0 cm−1) is likely associated with enhanced QTM, which lowers the apparent energy barrier. For the quenched DyCl3 system, we performed ReaxFF-MD simulations combined with CASSCF calculations. The results reveal that various [DyCln]3−n polyhedra formed during structural evolution collectively yield an averaged Ueff of 103.6 cm⁻¹ (Supplementary Table 32), in good agreement with experiment (106.5 cm−1).

Interpreting the magnetic behavior of DyCl3@SWCNT at the microscopic level is challenging because the experimental sample is inherently a mixed system. The SWCNT host possesses a diameter distribution, while the encapsulated DyCl3 nanowires may adopt several energetically similar structures according to the above structural analysis. Therefore, the measured magnetic response likely reflects the combined contribution of multiple structural motifs. Since exhaustive modeling of all possible configurations is impractical, we instead selected the 263 optimized nanowire structures for theoretical analysis. A total of 2554 [DyCln]3−n coordination polyhedra were extracted from these structures for subsequent CASSCF calculations.

Among the identified coordination geometries, octahedral, trigonal prismatic, and capped trigonal prismatic units dominate, together accounting for over 85% of all polyhedral (Fig. 9e). These geometries predominantly exhibit axial magnetic anisotropy, with ground-state Kramers doublets (KDs) characterized by high magnetic quantum numbers, a key criterion for slow magnetic relaxation. For an ideal octahedral [DyCl6]3− unit (Fig. 9g), although the highest excited KD reaches 188.7 cm−1, its symmetry induces significant mixing of angular momentum eigenstates, resulting in non-zero transition magnetic moments between KDs and a reduced Ueff of 31.1 cm−1. Slight distortions from ideal octahedral symmetry are therefore expected to suppress such mixing, leading to KDs closer to pure angular momentum eigenstates and consequently enhanced Ueff. While polyhedra with near-octahedral symmetry are abundant, their magnetic performance remains moderate, with an average Ueff of 105.8 cm−1. In contrast, the ideal trigonal prismatic [DyCl6]3− unit with D3h symmetry effectively suppresses the mixing of angular momentum eigenstates (Fig. 9h). As a result, the transition magnetic moments between KDs are minimal, giving rise to a high Ueff of 385. 8 cm−1. Even with moderate distortions, the averaged Ueff remains as high as 246.4 cm−1, although such polyhedra are relatively less prevalent. For the capped trigonal prismatic [DyCl7]4− unit (Fig. 9i), stronger mixing is observed in the excited KDs, which limits the effective barrier to 101.20 cm−1 (with an average of 102.8 cm−1 across the model). Compared to the distorted square antiprismatic coordination in the bulk crystal (Ueff = 42.3 cm−1), these calculations predict a substantial enhancement in magnetic performance. However, the experimentally measured Ueff of DyCl3@SWCNTs is lower than the theoretical values, which can be attributed to under-barrier relaxation processes, likely driven by anharmonic phonons51.

For representative single-component DyCl3 nanowires, the average Ueff values are 75.6 cm−1 (TX), 74.5 cm−1 (TZ), 141.1 cm−1 (TS), 109.0 cm−1 (TB), 276.7 cm−1 (TH), and 69.3 cm−1 (TU), respectively. The relatively high Ueff in TB arises from the dominance of trigonal prismatic coordination motifs. Guided by these insights, we further consider an idealized model system, {YCl3}13@SWCNT(7,6) (Supplementary Fig. 100), composed of trigonal-prismatic YCl6 units confined within commercially available SWCNTs. Electrical transport properties and underlying mechanism

The macroscopic electrical conductivity (σ) of LnCl3@SWCNTs, as well as that of purified SWCNT buckypaper, was investigated. The conductivity exhibits behavior typical of semiconductors, characterized by a sharp decrease at low temperature (Fig. 10a). Furthermore, there is no significant qualitative difference in conductivity trends among the different rare-earth elements. This can be attributed to the localized nature of the 4f electrons and their energetic separation from the Fermi level, which limits their direct interaction with itinerant electrons. Compared to purified SWCNTs, the hybrids demonstrate a 5- to 50-fold increase in conductivity from room temperature to low temperature. Transport measurements on LnCl3@SWCNTs reflect an ensemble-averaged conductivity arising from structural and compositional heterogeneity, which precludes a fully quantitative description of charge transport. To capture the essential physics, we employ a simplified Y6Cl18@SWCNT(14,7) model (Fig. 10d, e), with the semiconducting SWCNT(14,7) chosen as representative of the dominant nanotube species. Accordingly, the calculated electronic structures should be interpreted as indicative trends rather than direct quantitative predictions, while still offering valuable insight into how the encapsulated nanowires modulate the electronic structure of the host SWCNT. Based on the calculations, the Hirshfeld-I charge analysis yields [Y6Cl18]0.195+@[SWCNT]0.195−. These results confirm a minor degree of charge transfer from {Y6Cl18}n to the SWCNT, consistent with experimental observations from XAFS, XPS, and Raman.

The bands near the Fermi level were transformed into maximally localized Wannier functions (MLWFs, see “Methods”). These MLWFs were then employed in Boltzmann transport calculations to determine the axial conductance (σZ) of the Y6Cl18@SWCNT(14,7) model (Fig. 10b). The results show that the deformed SWCNT exhibits conductance behavior closely resembling that of the hybrid at higher temperatures. In both cases, the conductance is significantly higher than that of the pristine SWCNT. At lower temperatures, the conductance of the hybrid is only slightly greater than that of the deformed SWCNT. These findings suggest that, within this simplified model, the enhanced conductivity is primarily associated with geometrical deformation of the SWCNT, particularly the reduction in its bandgap, rather than charge transfer effects.

Discussion

LnCl3@SWCNTs (Ln = Y, Gd, Dy, Er) were synthesized via high-vacuum sublimation. TEM and STEM imaging revealed multiple new types of {LnCl3}n nanowires in the encapsulated systems. Based on HAADF imaging, we constructed structural models and employed a combination of ReaxFF-MD, DFT, and the symmetry-guided screening method to identify several structures consistent with experimental observations. This integrated approach is transferable and holds significant potential for resolving and predicting the structures of nanomaterials exhibiting short-range order.

We found that {LnCl3}n exhibits the commonly observed change in primary coordination mode upon encapsulation, which affects the magnetic behavior of the material; accordingly, DyCl3@SWCNTs exhibits slow magnetic relaxation with an energy barrier of 9.4 cm−1. Moreover, the hybrids exhibit a significant enhancement in electrical conductivity, which, based on our simplified modeling and analysis, is primarily associated with geometric deformation of the SWCNTs upon guest encapsulation, while charge transfer appears to play a secondary role, becoming more pronounced at low temperatures. This study offers a foundation for investigating the structural and physical properties of nanoscale systems with low dimension and short-range order.

Methods

Materials

Anhydrous rare-earth chlorides, LnCl3 (Ln = Y, Gd, Dy, Er; 99.99% purity), were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Concentrated hydrochloric acid (HCl, 35.0%–37.0%) was obtained from Kanto Chemical Co., Inc. Fullerene (C60, 99.5% purity) was purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, 98.0% purity, item no. 030-02105), ethanol (99.5% purity), methanol (99.5% purity), toluene (99.5% purity), and 1,2-dichloroethane (99.5% purity) were purchased from Wako Chemicals, Osaka, Japan. All chemicals were used as received without further purification. The 1.4 ± 0.12 nm SWCNTs were purchased from Meijo Nano Carbon Co. Ltd52. LnCl3-related operations were performed in a N2 filled glovebox (MBRAUN UNIlab).

Purification of SWCNTs

SWCNTs were purified according to refs. 10,23. Briefly, 1.4 nm SWCNTs (30 mg) were fragmented and oxidized in air at 500 °C for 30 min. After cooling to room temperature, the material was treated with concentrated HCl (36.5%), then filtered and washed with water. The SWCNTs were dispersed in 120 mL CTAB solution (2%) by ultrasonication, followed by addition of 120 mL HCl (36.5%). The mixture was stirred at 100 °C for 7 days, then cooled in an ice bath, filtered, and washed with water. The solid was subsequently washed once with a mixture of HCl (18%, 80 mL) and methanol (80 mL), and four times with methanol (100 mL each). The product was refluxed overnight in 300 mL methanol, filtered, and dried under vacuum. The resulting buckypaper was removed from the membrane and heated at 1200 °C for 24 h under dynamic vacuum (~10−6 torr). Finally, the purified SWCNTs (22 mg) were torn to pieces using tweezers in a quartz boat and heated to 550 °C in air for 15 min to open the sealed five-member-ring terminals.

Encapsulation process and thermogravimetric analysis

The purified SWCNTs were transferred into a Pyrex tube. Moreover, heated at 200 °C for 6 h under dynamic vacuum using a turbomolecular pump until the vacuum reached 10−7 Torr. The tube was transferred into the glove box, and the anhydrous LnCl3 salt was added to the tube with a tenfold mass of SWCNTs. The tube connected to the turbomolecular pump was again heated at 200 °C overnight. Then, the tube is sealed by a burner and heated at 550 °C for 72 h. After being slowly cooled to room temperature, the hybrid was quickly immersed in a turbid ethanol solution with C60 and dispersed using an ultrasonic cleaner for 3 min. The mixture was kept for 3 days to allow C60 to seal the SWCNTs and then filtered by polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membrane filter, washed-dispersed-filtered by the mix solution (Vmethanol: VHCl(36.5%): Vwater = 2:1:1) 4 times, methanol 2 times, toluene (to remove excessive C60) 3 times, methanol 1 time. Moreover, dried by an oil pump. The buckypaper of the hybrid was carefully removed from the filter membrane using tweezers. TGA was performed on a DTG-60H under an air atmosphere.

Synthesis of quenched DyCl3

DyCl3 (50 mg) was added to a Pyrex tube. A small amount of quartz fiber was stuffed into the open terminal to avoid contamination. The tube was heated to 200 °C under a dynamic vacuum generated by a turbomolecular pump, until the pressure reached 10−7 Torr, and then sealed with a burner. The tube was heated to 550 °C for 72 h and then allowed to cool to room temperature.

Electron microscopy

For TEM sample preparation, a very small amount of hybrid sample was dispersed in 1 mL of 1,2-dichloroethane using an ultrasonic cleaner, and then dropped onto a carbon mesh supported by a copper grid (Okenshoji Co., Ltd, NP-C15). Subsequently, methanol was added to flush out any excessive dichloroethane. The grid was heated to under 80 °C under a dynamic vacuum overnight using the turbomolecular pump. High-resolution TEM and EDX spectroscopy were carried out using a JEM2100F (acceleration voltage: 200 kV, JEOL Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) with a dry SD30GV detector (JEOL Ltd, Tokyo, Japan). Scanning TEM was performed using a JEOL JEM-ARM200F (acceleration voltage: 80 kV, JEOL Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) with a CEOS probe and image aberration correctors.

Computational methods

DFT calculations for systems with periodic boundary conditions (PBC) were performed with the VASP (ver. 6.3.1, 04 May 22) program package53–56, using the PBE exchange–correlation functional with Becke–Johnson damped Grimme’s DFT-D3 dispersion correction57–60. The projector-augmented wave pseudopotentials were applied with the kinetic energy cutoff set to 640 eV for normal optimizations and 800 eV if unit cells were optimized. For a hexagonal lattice, a Gamma-centered mesh was used, and a Monkhorst pack mesh was used for the rest. The threshold of maximum residual force was set to 0.01 eV Å−1 unless otherwise specified. For isolated molecular systems, Gaussian16 Rev. C.01 is used with the def2-TZVP basis sets61,62. MD simulations rely on the Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMMPS 15Apr2020 program package)63. To explain the magnetic properties, the electronic structure of the [DyCln]3−n polyhedra was calculated using the multiconfigurational complete active self-consistent field spin-orbit (CASSCF-SO) method as implemented in the OpenMolcas program package (version 23.10)64. Scalar relativistic effects were taken into account with the Douglas–Kroll–Hess transformation using the relativistically contracted atomic natural orbital ANO-RCC basis set with VDZP quality for all atoms65. The active space consisted of 7 electrons on the 9 f-orbitals of Dy3+ ion. The molecular orbitals were optimized at the CASSCF level in a state-average (SA) 21 sextet (S = 5/2). The wave functions obtained at CASSCF were then mixed by spin-orbit coupling by means of the RASSI approach. The crystal field parameters and the energy barriers were calculated using SINGLE_ANISO module implemented in OpenMolcas. For ease of comparison, the effective energy barriers, Ueff(T), were calculated at 10, 100, and 300 K using a method reported in refs. 66,67. If not explicitly stated, the theoretical prediction in the text of Ueff refers to Ueff(T = 100 K). The visualization was achieved VMD 1.9.368. The SWCNTs (dC-C = 1.42462 Å) were built by using the Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE) package69. The scikit-learn package was used for k-means clustering70,71. Structures of inorganic compounds were downloaded from ICSD database72, as YCl3 (ICSD 15684)73, LaCl3 (ICSD 23146)74, GdCl3 (ICSD 15387)75, TbCl3 (ICSD 63543)76, DyCl3 (ICSD 40064)77, TmCl3 (ICSD 35398)78, Graphite (ICSD 230104)79.

Verification of the exchange–correlation functional

For crystal structure optimizations, the kinetic energy cutoff was set to 800 eV. The k-point mesh settings are 6 × 4 × 6 (YCl3), 5×5×8 (LaCl3), 5 × 5 × 8 (GdCl3), 8 × 4 × 4 (DyCl3), 5 × 5 × 2 (TmCl3), and 12 × 12 × 5 (Graphite), respectively. The threshold of maximum residual force was set to 0.004 eV Å−1. It can be found from the results that the deviations in bond lengths and unit cell sizes are below 0.5% and 3%, respectively (Supplementary Table 9). Therefore, PBE-D3BJ with a high enough kinetic energy cutoff can describe the system correctly. Since Y3+ has very close ionic radii to Dy3+, Er3+, and Gd3+, and the 4f electrons do not have a significant effect on the subsequent structural explorations, yttrium is used instead of lanthanide atoms for the subsequent calculations. We use the crystal structures of common lanthanide chlorides with different space groups as an initial guess, use yttrium replaced of the lanthanide atoms in them, and perform structure optimizations. According to the results, the deviations in bond lengths and unit cell sizes are below 2.6% and 5%, respectively (Supplementary Table 10). Especially for the most important DyCl3 case, it has a very small deviation.

Here, we neglected the effect of curvature and used graphene to model the surface of SWCNT. The structure of the rectangular supercell of graphene was constructed through the crystal structure of graphite and a 14.0 Å vacuum slab was placed. As the initial geometry structure of the complex, a YCl3 or Y2Cl6 molecule was placed above graphene and kept at a suitable distance (estimated by the van der Waals radius). The geometry was optimized keeping the cell shape unchanged. The k-point mesh setting is 4 × 4 × 1. The threshold of maximum residual force was set to 0.004 eV Å−1. It can be found that the adsorption of YCl3 on graphene is relatively strong, the binding energy is around −1.0214 eV (98.55 kJ mol−1). Moreover, the structure of the YCl3 molecule deviates from the planar structure and shows an umbrella-like deformation (Supplementary Fig. 39), where the Y atom is pulled towards the graphene side, and the nearest Y-Cl distance is ≈3.0 Å. This can be interpreted as the YCl3 molecule has a low coordination number, and it needs to be close to the graphene to reduce the energy. Or it can also be explained by the electrostatic distribution of the molecule, where the YCl3 molecule has a diffuse positive charge distribution in the direction of the C3 axis due to the absence of Cl atoms to balance the charge, whereas above graphene it will show a negative charge distribution, and the two are easily attracted to each other. In contrast, the Y atoms in Y2Cl6 are wrapped by more Cl atoms, and the Y does not have a tendency to interact directly with the C atoms. Thus, the binding energy is much smaller in this case as −0.5803 eV (−55.99 kJ mol−1). And we can find that even two YCl3 molecules adsorbed on graphene are more inclined to form the dimer Y2Cl6. MD optimization of Cl− positions

Yttrium atoms were fixed during MD simulations. The temperature of chlorine atoms was controlled by using a Berendsen thermostat. The timestep was set to 0.4 fs. The annealing process was applied to the Cl atoms, as, 10–1000 K (within 4 ps), 1000 K (within 8 ps), 1000–10 K (within 4 ps).

DFT optimization of {YCl3}n nanowires

The screened structures were first optimized for the atomic positions of Y and Cl using the PBE-D3(BJ) functional with a plane-wave cutoff of 520 eV, while keeping the unit cell parameters fixed. The residual force after this step was 0.04 eV Å−1. Subsequently, a structural screening was carried out. The selected structures were then fully optimized with respect to both atomic positions and cell parameters (to ensure orthogonality), increasing the cutoff energy to 640 eV and the residual force to 0.01 eV Å−1. Structures exhibiting high relative energies (greater than −1.0 eV), or significantly large diameters, were filtered out, a process that can be repeated iteratively to refine the structures. A final optimization of the atomic positions was performed using a higher cutoff energy of 800 eV and a residual force convergence threshold of 0.008 eV Å−1, resulting in 263 structures retained for further analysis.

MD of quenched DyCl3

To simulate the quenched DyCl3 structure, we constructed a supercell containing 144 YCl3 units. The cell parameters were fixed throughout the simulation. Quenching was performed using the ReaxFF-MD, with atomic temperature controlled via a Berendsen thermostat. The integration timestep was set to 0.4 fs. The quenching protocol involved holding the system at 850 K for 40 ps, followed by rapid cooling from 850 K to 4 K over 4 ps. After quenching, the structure was further optimized using ReaxFF.

STEM simulation

STEM simulation images were simulated by the Dr. Probe package80, where a defocus value around −10 nm (which corresponds to the under-focus condition, 10 nm, in an experiment) and a spherical aberration coefficient (Cs) of 2 μm was used. To reproduce the effect of vibration, the Debye–Waller factor parameters (Biso) were set to 2.0, 2.0, and 0.2 nm2 for carbon, chlorine, and dysprosium, respectively. The intensity data was linearly mapped to a grayscale color space without Gamma correction.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy

XPS was used for quantitatively detecting the chemical shift of SWCNTs’ carbon and the encapsulation ratio. Al anode (Kα, hν = 1486.6 eV) was used. The double peaks of Ln (Y, Gd, Dy, Er) and Cl could be clearly found from LnCl3@SWCNTs, they were fitted with two Voigt GL(30) functions, corresponding to Gaussian–Lorentzian mixed line shapes with 30% Lorentzian contribution, while the area ratio was fixed according to the spin–orbit degeneracy. Raman spectroscopy

Raman spectroscopy of LnCl3@SWCNTs (Ln = Y, Gd, Dy, Er) was obtained on a HORIBA LabRAM HR-800 excited by 532 nm laser. Both the radial breathing mode (RBM) and G bands have slightly broadened after encapsulation.

Around 0.6 mg sample was dispersed into methanol by an ultrasonic cleaner, then slowly filtered by 1.0 µm PTFE membrane filter (φ = 10 mm), and dried by an oil pump. Electronic band structure and charge transport properties

For the Y6Cl18@SWCNT(14,7) model, first, a single-point calculation was performed with a kinetic energy cutoff of 800 eV and a k-point grid of 1 × 1 × 16. The electronic states were projected onto 196 p orbitals (from carbon atoms), 294 s orbitals (representing C-C σ bonds), 30 d orbitals (from Y), and 54 p orbitals (from Cl). The projections were then localized using Wannier90 to obtain MLWFs85. Subsequently, the BoltzWann module was employed to calculate the temperature-dependent electrical conductivity from 2 to 300 K using the Boltzmann transport equation based on the obtained MLWFs86. Both the deformed and pristine SWCNT(14,7) models were computed under identical conditions and using the same methodology.
