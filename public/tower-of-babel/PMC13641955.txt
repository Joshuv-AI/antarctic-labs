Annular Enlargement: Can It Be Effective for Patients With a Small Left Ventricular Outflow Tract?
Authors: Soo Jin Park, Kitae Kim, Hong Rae Kim, Jae Suk Yoo, Joon Bum Kim, Sung-Ho Jung, Cheol Hyun Chung, Jae Won Lee, Ho Jin Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAortic annular enlargement (AAE) has been suggested as a surgical technique to facilitate the implantation of a larger prosthetic valve in patients with a small aortic annulus. We investigated the differences in clinical and echocardiographic parameters between patient undergoing aortic valve replacement (AVR) with and without AAE, focusing on the impact of left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) geometry.MethodsA total of 899 patients (age, 65.5 ± 9.6 years; 414 female) who underwent AVR from 1996 to 2023 were evaluated. The primary outcomes were all-cause mortality and the trends in echocardiographic parameters, analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model. The secondary outcomes included early complications and valve-related adverse events comprising stroke, congestive heart failure, reoperation and infective endocarditis. Propensity score matching was employed for baseline adjustment.ResultsIn the entire cohort, 75 underwent AVR with AAE, and 824 received AVR-only. After pair matching, 274 patients were included in the final analysis (72 vs. 202). Larger-sized valves were implanted in the AVR-AAE group (22.0 ± 2.2 mm vs. 20.9 ± 1.6 mm, P < 0.001). Early complications, survival rates, and valve-related adverse events showed no significant differences. Echocardiography revealed similar left ventricular mass index (LVMI) regression in both groups (P = 0.057). In patients with a larger LVOT than the annulus (13.9%), the AVR-AAE group showed a rapid LVMI regression trend, while in those with a smaller LVOT than the annulus (86.1%), the AVR-only group demonstrated faster LVMI regression (P = 0.021).ConclusionWhile AAE enables larger prosthetic valve implantation without affecting clinical outcomes, its impact on LVMI regression varies based on the LVOT-annulus relationship. AAE may not have long-term benefits in patients with a smaller LVOT compared to the annulus. For optimal surgical planning, evaluation of both annulus and LVOT sizes is crucial.

INTRODUCTION

The aortic annulus is a complex, three-dimensional, crown-shaped structure that forms part of the fibrous skeleton of the heart.1 In aortic valve replacement (AVR), a small aortic annulus is typically defined as one that does not accommodate a prosthetic valve larger than 21 mm or 23 mm, as determined preoperatively or intraoperatively.23

Aortic annular enlargement (AAE) has been suggested as a surgical technique to facilitate the implantation of a larger prosthetic valve in patients with a small aortic annulus. Various surgical techniques for AAE have been introduced, based on the site of annular incision and reconstruction methods. However, debates have persisted regarding the efficacy and safety of AAE, suggesting that AAE may not yield clinical benefits while increasing the risk of postoperative complications.456

Additionally, the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT), below the aortic annulus, may impact postoperative hemodynamics in patients undergoing AVR. This may be particularly relevant in patients with a small aortic annulus, where a relatively narrow LVOT compared to the aortic annulus could limit hemodynamic improvement even after AVR with AAE.7 However, the relative size of the LVOT to the aortic annulus has rarely been considered in previous studies evaluating the impact of AAE during AVR.

Therefore, we aimed to investigate the differences in clinical and echocardiographic outcomes between patients who underwent AVR with and without AAE, while particularly analyzing how the relative size of the LVOT to the aortic annulus influenced these outcomes.

METHODS

Study population

We identified 2,816 adult patients who underwent AVR between January 1996 and January 2023. Exclusions included patients undergoing: 1) concomitant mitral valve replacement and redo AVR (n = 924); 2) AVR for pure aortic regurgitation and infective endocarditis (n = 118); or 3) AVR using sutureless or rapid deployment valves (n = 184). Furthermore, patients in whom the sizes of the aortic annulus and LVOT could not be assessed on preoperative echocardiography or computed tomography (CT) were also excluded (n = 691). The LVOT size was determined based on CT measurements, considering its ovoid shape by using the average of the anterior-posterior and transverse diameters (Supplementary Fig. 1A).7 In the absence of CT data, echocardiographic LVOT measurements were used in accordance with previous guidelines to minimize potential errors.8 Given that the LVOT is dynamic and undergoes changes in shape and diameter throughout the cardiac cycle, LVOT diameter measurements were consistently obtained during the mid-systolic phase in accordance with established protocols (Supplementary Fig. 1B).8 Finally, a total of 899 patients comprised the final study population. Of these 899 patients, LVOT measurements were obtained from echocardiography in 172 patients (19.2%), with the remaining 727 patients (80.8%) measured by CT imaging.

Surgical techniques, outcomes of interest and clinical follow-up

AVR was performed under cardiopulmonary bypass, with prosthetic valve type selected based on patient-specific factors including age, comorbidities, and anticoagulation preference. The AAE techniques, including Nicks, Nuñez, Y-incision, Manouguian, and Konno, were performed using well-described methods from a previous study.5 Briefly, the Nicks, Nuñez, and Y-incision techniques differ primarily in the extent and shape of aortic incision—the Nicks procedure involves an incision through the noncoronary sinus, while both Nuñez and Y-incision techniques involve incisions at the left-noncoronary commissure910 with different incision patterns, all requiring patch enlargement. The distinction between the Manouguian or Konno procedures and other techniques lies in the additional resections performed for enlargement, specifically involving the mitral leaflet, septum, and right ventricular outflow tract.1112

The primary outcomes of interest were all-cause mortality and serial echocardiographic parameters, including left ventricular mass index (LVMI) and parameters related to the aortic valve (AV) (trans-AV pressure gradient [PG], trans-AV peak velocity, and ejection fraction [EF]). Echocardiography was performed in all patients within 3 months before surgery, postoperatively before discharge, within one year postoperatively, and annually or biennially thereafter. Follow-up echocardiographic data were obtained up to July 2023. Based on the relative size of the LVOT to the aortic annulus in previous literature, we classified the LVOT morphology into two subgroups to analyze serial echocardiographic changes: funnel-shaped, where the LVOT diameter was greater than or equal to the annular diameter, and hourglass-shaped, where the LVOT diameter was smaller than the annular diameter (Fig. 1).7 Additionally, we estimated post-AVR functional annular diameter from the implanted valve effective orifice area (EOA) (Supplementary Table 1) and reclassified patients into post-AVR funnel-shaped and hourglass-shaped based on this estimated annulus-LVOT relationship.

LA = left atrium, LVOT = left ventricular outflow tract.

Secondary outcomes included early complications and long-term valve-related adverse events such as stroke, readmission due to heart failure, reoperation due to prosthetic valve failure, and infective endocarditis. Early complications were defined as complications associated with AVR occurring during admission or within 30 days. Readmission due to heart failure was defined as an unexpected hospitalization for the management of heart failure with resting dyspnea, radiological signs of pulmonary edema, and the need for intravenous diuretics.

Clinical follow-up data were obtained until July 2023. Patients without death or valve-related adverse events were censored at the end of follow-up.

Data analysis

Categorical variables were assessed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test and presented as frequencies and percentages. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to determine the normal distribution of continuous variables. Student’s t-test was applied for normally distributed variables, presented as mean values with standard deviations, while the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Mann-Whitney U-test) was used for non-normally distributed variables, presented as median values with interquartile range (IQR). Early major complications were evaluated using univariate logistic regression models presenting the crude odds ratios and their associated 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The analysis of time-to-event outcomes for all-cause mortality, Cox proportional hazards models were primarily used. The Cox models were used to compute crude and adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) and their corresponding 95% CIs. The proportional hazard assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals. Other time-related secondary outcomes were analyzed using competing risk regression with all-cause mortality as a competing risk. Sub-distribution hazard ratios (sHR) were estimated using the Fine-Gray model.

To address differences in baseline and operative characteristics, we conducted 1:3 pair matching between the AVR-AAE and AVR-only groups using nearest neighbor matching based on propensity scores (PSs). PS were calculated for each patient using a logistic regression model incorporating essential preoperative variables (Supplementary Fig. 2). Caliper restrictions (0.8–1.0 standard deviations for key anatomical variables) were applied to ensure matching quality. Due to these caliper constraints, some patients in the AVR-AAE group were matched to fewer than three controls, resulting in a final ratio of 1:2.8. This approach prioritized match quality over maintaining the exact 1:3 ratio, which is considered acceptable in PS matching methodology.13 Equivalence in these variables between the groups was assessed by evaluating standardized mean differences (SMDs), with SMDs < 20% indicating adequate balance.14

The relationship between LVOT diameter and aortic annulus size before and after PS matching was analyzed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), which included surgical approach, LVOT diameter, and their interaction term (LVOT × surgical approach) to evaluate whether the LVOT-annulus relationship differed between the AVR-AAE and AVR-only groups. The strength of these relationships was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R2), and beta coefficients with 95% CIs were calculated.

To establish LVOT measurement equivalence between CT and echocardiography (CT: n = 727, Echo: n = 172), we implemented three complementary statistical approaches: 1) ANCOVA examined the interaction between measurement modality and LVOT diameter; 2) Bootstrap resampling (10,000 iterations) to provide distribution-free slope comparison, particularly suitable for our unbalanced design; and 3) Fisher’s z-transformation compared correlation coefficients to assess standardized association strength.

To analyze serial echocardiographic parameters assessing AV function across the PS-matched groups, we employed a linear mixed-effects model to account for repeated echocardiographic measurements within the same patient. The model included fixed effects for treatment group (AVR with/without AAE), postoperative time (years), and their interaction term. Random intercepts and random slopes for time were included for each subject to account for individual-level baseline variability and individual differences in the rate of change over time. A first-order autoregressive (AR[1]) correlation structure was applied to model the residual temporal correlation between repeated measurements. Parameter estimates and 95% CIs were obtained using restricted maximum likelihood estimation. The analysis was performed using the ‘nlme’ package.

To address potential confounding from anatomic annular measurement limitations, we performed sensitivity analyses estimating functional annular diameter from prosthetic valve EOA. For each patient, valve-specific EOA was obtained from manufacturer specifications or published hemodynamic data (Supplementary Table 1). Functional annular diameter was calculated as 2√(EOA/π) × α, where α represents the structural-hemodynamic coefficient accounting for the known discrepancy between labeled valve size and effective orifice diameter, typically 3–12 mm with most contemporary valves showing 5–7 mm difference.1516 Patients were reclassified into estimated Hourglass (estimated functional annulus > LVOT diameter) and estimated Funnel (estimated functional annulus ≤ LVOT diameter) groups and analyze echocardiographic parameters were analyzed using the same linear mixed-effects model as described above.

All reported P values were derived from two-tailed tests, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the by the Institutional Review Board of Asan Medical Center (2023-0426, date of approval: November 15, 2023). Informed consent from individual patients was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

Of the 899 patients included in this study, 75 patients (8.3%) underwent AVR with AAE, while 824 patients (91.7%) underwent AVR alone without AAE. Baseline demographics and clinical profiles between the two groups are presented in Table 1. At baseline, patients who underwent concomitant AVR-AAE were younger (64.0 years; IQR, 55.0–71.0 vs. 66.0 years; IQR, 60.0–73.0; P = 0.017), more commonly female (69.3% vs. 43.9%; P < 0.001). The prevalence of a small aortic annulus (< 23 mm) was higher in the AVR-AAE group than the AVR-only group (60.0% vs. 19.9%; P < 0.001). The proportion of funnel-shaped LVOT, where the LVOT diameter was greater than or equal to the annular diameter, was comparable between both groups (18.7% vs. 15.9%, P = 0.645).

The systolic/diastolic dimensions of left ventricle (LV) and the size of aortic annulus and LVOT were all consistently smaller in the AVR-AAE group than the AVR-only group (Table 1). The EF was higher in the AVR-AAE group (64.0%; IQR, 61.0–66.0 vs. 63.0%; IQR, 59.0–66.0; P = 0.049) compared to the AVR-only group. The LVMI was comparable between both groups (126.6 g/m2; IQR, 102.0–146.8 vs. 129.0 g/m2; IQR, 106.5–155.7; P = 0.095).

Fig. 2 shows that the size of the annulus and LVOT, which was smaller in the AVR-AAE group in the original cohort (A), was distributed proportionally between the AVR-AAE and the AVR-only group in the matched cohort (B). Following PS matching, balanced distribution of baseline characteristics and echocardiographic/CT parameters between the two groups was achieved (beta-coefficients = 0.05, 95% CIs, −0.24 to 0.33, P = 0.736) (Supplementary Fig. 2). The equivalence of annulus and LVOT measurements between CT and echocardiography was confirmed through three complementary analyses. First, ANCOVA revealed no significant interaction (F = 2.42, P = 0.120) (Supplementary Fig. 3A), with overlapping slope CIs (CT: 0.59 mm/mm, Echo: 0.75 mm/mm) (Supplementary Fig. 3B). The reduced ANCOVA model assuming parallel slopes showed minimal model difference (ΔR2 = 0.002) (Supplementary Fig. 3C). Second, bootstrap analysis confirmed slope similarity (P = 0.506) (Supplementary Fig. 3D). Third, Fisher’s z-test showed comparable correlations between two modalities (CT: r = 0.473, Echo: r = 0.566; P = 0.136) (Supplementary Fig. 3E).

Operative profiles

The Nicks technique (48.8%) was predominantly performed for AAE, followed by the Nuñez (23.8%), Y-incision (8.8%), Manouguian (6.2%), and Konno (3.8%) techniques (Supplementary Fig. 4). Isolated AVR was performed in 52 patients (67.5%) in the AVR-AAE group, and the concomitant procedures performed are detailed in Supplementary Table 2.

In the matched cohort, the use of minimally invasive approach (P = 0.795) and the type of implanted prosthetic valve (P = 0.648) were comparable between the two groups (Table 2). The aortic cross-clamping and cardiopulmonary bypass time was significantly longer in the AVR-AAE group. However, larger-sized valves were implanted in the AVR-AAE group (22.0 ± 2.2 mm vs. 20.9 ± 1.6 mm, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3, Table 2).

Unadjusted outcomes

In-hospital or 30-day mortality occurred in 2 (2.8%) patients in the AVR-AAE group and 7 (0.8%) in the AVR-only group (P = 0.364). In addition, there was no significant difference in the incidence of early complications such as pacemaker implantation (P = 0.647), mediastinal bleeding (P = 0.297) and low cardiac output syndrome requiring mechanical circulatory support (P = 0.763). There was no significant difference in the incidence of in-hospital complications between both groups (P = 0.264) (Table 3).

The follow-up duration for the entire cohort was 631.3 patient-years (PY) in the AVR-AAE group and 5,217.3 PY in the AVR-only group, totaling 5,848.6 PY with a median follow-up of 5.9 years (IQR, 3.2 to 9.1 years). DISCUSSION

This study compared clinical and hemodynamic outcomes in patients undergoing AVR with or without AAE using PS matching to obtain cohorts with similar annulus and LVOT diameters. This approach allowed for a focused evaluation of the isolated effect of AAE. By assessing both clinical outcomes and serial echocardiographic parameters of AV and LVOT hemodynamics, this study provides a potential explanation for why AAE during AVR has failed to demonstrate consistent clinical superiority in previous studies.171819

Prior studies have defined a small aortic annulus as an annulus incapable of accommodating a prosthesis larger than 21 mm2 or an aortic annulus measuring less than 23 mm during a preoperative assessment or intraoperative direct sizing.3 AAE has been regarded as a crucial surgical option for patients with a small annulus, enabling the implantation of larger prosthetic valves. Concomitant AAE during AVR is known to benefit patients with a small aortic annulus by reducing the risk of prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM) and potentially facilitating future transcatheter valve-in-valve procedure.20

PPM has been identified as a significant risk factor for early stenosis-type structural valve degeneration and is also known to be associated with an increased mortality after AVR.16 In addition, the incidence of severe PPM after AVR is reported to be higher among patients with a small aortic annulus than those with medium or large-sized annulus.2122 Also, recent studies regarding transcatheter valve-in-valve procedure for surgical prosthesis failure have reported favorable clinical outcomes among patients with large prosthetic valves implanted during surgical AVR compared to those with small prostheses.22

Despite the lower incidence of PPM associated with AAE, recent meta-analyses1718 and the Society of Thoracic Surgeons Adult Cardiac Surgery Database19 have shown comparable survival rates between patients with and without AAE during AVR. Rather, some studies have suggested that AAE during AVR may increase perioperative mortality due to the invasive nature of the procedure.19

The lack of clinical benefits associated with AAE may be partially explained by the underlying LVOT size proportionate to the aortic annulus size and the inherent limitation of most AAE techniques not intervening with the LVOT size. In this study, overall LVMI regression rates were comparable between AVR-AAE and AVR-only groups. However, patients with funnel-shaped LVOT (diameter ≥ annular diameter) showed a faster LVMI regression trend with AVR-AAE (Fig. 5B). Conversely, patients with hourglass-shaped LVOT (diameter < annular diameter) exhibited a significantly slower rate of LVMI regression with AVR-AAE group compared to AVR-only group (Fig. 5C).

The accelerated LVMI regression observed in the AVR-only group among patients with hourglass-shaped LVOT may represent a notable finding with important clinical implications. While AAE has been theoretically expected to enhance post-AVR outcomes in patients with small aortic annulus through larger prosthetic valve insertion, our quantitative echocardiographic assessment of LVMI regression suggests that the hourglass-shaped LVOT configuration may produce results that differ from conventional expectations. Importantly, our findings do not challenge the immediate hemodynamic efficacy of AAE procedures. As demonstrated in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 5, AAE successfully facilitated implantation of larger prosthetic valves, resulting in significantly improved early postoperative hemodynamics (lower trans-AV PG and less trans-AV peak velocity) compared to AVR-only. These results clearly confirm the intended technical success of AAE in achieving superior immediate postoperative valve hemodynamics.

However, during extended echocardiographic follow-up, the trajectories of PGs and peak velocities converged between groups, while LV mass index—a comprehensive marker of reverse remodeling following AVR—demonstrated paradoxically accelerated regression in the AVR-only group, particularly among patients with hourglass-shaped LVOT morphology. This discordance between early hemodynamic improvement and long-term structural remodeling outcomes raises important questions about whether AAE alone addresses the fundamental hemodynamic limitations in patients with severely narrowed LVOTs. Furthermore, patients with a smaller LVOT-to-functional-annulus ratio (where functional annular diameter was estimated from prosthetic valve EOA) demonstrated faster progression of trans-AV PG and peak velocities, providing evidence that residual LVOT narrowing may limit long-term hemodynamic benefits despite successful AAE. Given that the majority of patients with small aortic annulus exhibit this hourglass-shaped LVOT morphology,23 these findings may help explain the comparable long-term survival and valve-related adverse event rates between surgical approaches observed in our study.

Multiple factors may contribute to these observations, but they are primarily attributable to the inherent technical constraints of AAE approaches. Aside from the Manouguian or Konno procedures, effectively enlarging a narrowed LVOT through AAE techniques remains technically unfeasible. Furthermore, when the LVOT is severely constricted, employing tilting techniques for valve implantation following annular enlargement alone is unlikely to achieve satisfactory long-term hemodynamic outcomes.24 Even the recently proposed Y-incision technique,10 designed for more effective AAE, remains controversial regarding optimal placement above the LVOT without crossing the virtual basal ring.6 These findings suggest that in patients with hourglass-shaped LVOT, addressing the annular dimension alone may not provide a comprehensive solution to the underlying anatomical constraint. Alternative therapeutic strategies merit consideration in this patient subset. Sutureless25 or transcatheter AV26 may offer superior hemodynamic performance due to larger EOAs resulting from the absence of sewing rings, potentially reducing the incidence of PPM while better accommodating complex LVOT geometries.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective, single-center design and 27-year study period introduce inherent biases and temporal variability in surgical techniques despite rigorous matching. Second, a substantial proportion of patients were excluded due to absence of preoperative imaging data, particularly before the 2000s, thereby limiting generalizability. Third, combining LVOT measurements from CT and echocardiography may introduce methodological heterogeneity, although we implemented standardized protocols and conducted multiple complementary analyses to confirm measurement agreement. Fourth, the inclusion of concomitant procedures in the AVR-AAE group beyond isolated AVR and incorporation of various AAE techniques which possess varying annular enlargement potential may have confounded outcomes; however, restricting analysis to isolated cases and subgroup stratification by AAE technique would have compromised statistical power and engendered methodological bias. Fifth, PPM could not be assessed due to incomplete EOA documentation on follow-up echocardiography.

While AAE allows for the implantation of a larger prosthetic valve at the level of aortic annulus, the present study implies that annular enlargement alone may not result in long-term benefits, particularly in patients with narrowed LVOT. Therefore, when considering performing AAE during AVR in patients with a small aortic annulus, the size of LVOT should also be taken into consideration to yield optimal clinical outcomes.
