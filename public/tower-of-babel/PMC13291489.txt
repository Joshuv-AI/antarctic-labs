TL1A/DR3 signaling deletion attenuates mucosal inflammation and alveolar bone loss in a murine model of spontaneous periodontitis
Authors: Sara Di Nicolantonio, Maria R. Miranda, Adrian Gomez-Nguyen, Davide Pietropaoli, Annalisa Monaco, Paola Menghini, Fabio Cominelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
TL1A and its receptor DR3 are key regulators of mucosal immune responses, but their role in periodontal disease is unknown. Herein, we investigated whether TL1A/DR3 signaling contributes to mucosal immune amplification and tissue-destructive inflammation in periodontitis using SAMP1/YitFc (SAMP) mice, which develop spontaneous ileitis and periodontal disease. DR3 deficiency markedly attenuated alveolar bone loss and improved periodontal architecture, restoring a phenotype comparable to healthy AKR (parental) controls. Gingival tissues from wild-type SAMP mice exhibited increased expression of both Tnfsf15 (encoding TL1A) and Tnfrsf25 (encoding DR3), with both positively correlating with disease severity. This was accompanied by elevated levels of IL-17, TNF-α, and IL-1β, and by increased numbers of CD4+ T helper cells and neutrophils. Conversely, SAMPxDR3−/− mice exhibited reduced inflammatory cytokine production and immune cell accumulation. These findings support a model in which the TL1A/DR3 axis is associated with amplification of mucosal immune responses in periodontal disease, linking effector T cell activation, increased cytokine production, and recruitment of innate immune cells. Altogether, our data identify the TL1A/DR3 cytokine-receptor pair as a potential regulator of inflammatory circuits that drive periodontal pathology. Blocking this pathway may provide a therapeutic modality for patients affected by chronic periodontitis.

TL1A and its receptor DR3 are critical regulators of mucosal immunity, functioning as costimulatory molecules that enhance effector T cell activation and promote Th1/Th17-driven inflammation (1). Dysregulation of TL1A/DR3 signaling has been strongly associated with disease severity in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and other chronic conditions, supporting its role in sustaining chronic immune activation (2). These observations collectively suggest that TL1A/DR3 may serve as a conserved amplifier of inflammation across different mucosal tissues.

In 2019, over 1 billion people were affected by severe periodontitis, representing the sixth most prevalent disease worldwide (3). Periodontal disease represents a prototypical example of dysregulated mucosal immunity, in which host-driven inflammatory pathways, rather than microbial burden alone, mediate tissue destruction and alveolar bone loss. In this context key cytokines including TNF, IL-1β and IL-6 play a critical role in mediating inflammatory responses and tissue remodeling (4). Although periodontitis and IBD, including Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), are epidemiologically and mechanistically linked (5), it remains unclear whether TL1A/DR3 is a key pathogenic factor in chronic periodontitis. In chronic periodontitis, the TL1A/DR3 pathway may contribute to the exacerbation of inflammatory responses in periodontal tissues. Therefore, we tested the hypothesis that TL1A/DR3 signaling drives mucosal immune amplification, tissue-destructive inflammation, and bone loss in SAMP mice with spontaneous CD-like ileitis and chronic periodontitis (6).

Results

DR3 Deficiency Markedly Attenuates Periodontal Bone Loss and Tissue Inflammation in SAMP Mice.

SAMPxDR3−/− mice displayed markedly reduced alveolar bone loss compared with wild-type (WT) SAMP mice at both 10 and 20 wk of age, reaching levels comparable to healthy AKR controls, as demonstrated by stereomicroscopic analysis (Fig. 1 A–C). These differences were observed during both early and late stages of disease, indicating that DR3 signaling contributes to the initiation and progression of periodontitis. Histological analysis further supported these findings, revealing decreased CEJ–ABC distance, increased alveolar bone width, reduced inflammatory-cell infiltration, and improved periodontal tissue organization in SAMPxDR3−/− mice relative to WT SAMP controls (Fig. 1 D–G). Together, these data show that DR3 deficiency attenuates periodontal bone loss and preserves periodontal tissue architecture, supporting a role for DR3 signaling in the tissue-destructive inflammatory processes driving disease progression in this model.

TL1A–DR3 Signaling Is Associated With Mucosal Immune Activation in Experimental Periodontitis.

RT-qPCR analysis of gingival tissues from SAMP mice revealed significant upregulation of Tnfrsf25 and Tnfsf15 compared to AKR controls and SAMPxDR3−/− mice, indicating increased activation of the TL1A/DR3 axis within the inflamed periodontal microenvironment (Fig. 2 A and B).

In WT SAMP mice, DR3 and TL1A expression positively correlated with alveolar bone loss, whereas this relationship was not observed in SAMPxDR3−/− mice or AKR controls, supporting a link between TL1A/DR3 axis and disease severity (Fig. 2C).

This correlation was accompanied by an enhanced proinflammatory profile and mucosal immune activation. WT SAMP mice exhibited significantly higher levels of TNF-α (P < 0.0015), IL-17 (P < 0.0232), and IL-1β (P < 0.009), compared to SAMPxDR3−/− mice, with a trend for IL-6 (P < 0.393) (Fig. 2 D and E).

Flow cytometric analysis further revealed reduced CD4+ T helper cells and neutrophils in SAMPxDR3−/− mice compared to WT SAMP controls (Fig. 2 F and G).

Collectively, these data support a model in which activation of the TL1A–DR3 axis is associated with enhanced mucosal immune activation, characterized by increased effector T cell responses, elevated proinflammatory cytokine production, and recruitment of innate immune cells in the inflamed gingival tissue. To our knowledge, these findings demonstrate a key role of TL1A/DR3 in the pathogenesis of chronic experimental periodontitis.

Discussion

This study strongly suggests that the TL1A/DR3 axis contributes to the regulation of the inflammatory response associated with alveolar bone loss in periodontal disease. In a murine model of spontaneous periodontitis and CD-like ileitis (SAMP1/YitFc), genetic deletion of DR3 was associated with attenuated disease progression and a periodontal phenotype more closely resembling that of healthy controls, indicating a potential role for DR3 in mediating disease pathology.

The reduction in alveolar bone loss observed in SAMPxDR3−/− mice, together with improved histological features, is consistent with involvement of this pathway in disease initiation and progression. Elevated levels of TL1A and DR3 have been previously linked to inflammatory processes and immune-mediated bone remodeling in systemic conditions such as IBD and rheumatoid arthritis (7, 8). Our data extend these observations to the periodontal microenvironment, showing increased TL1A expression at both the mRNA and protein levels, as well as elevated DR3 expression in inflamed gingival tissues. In our study, we did not study the cell-type-specific expression of TL1A and DR3 in the gingival lesions. However, we speculate that TL1A may be expressed by Th1 and Th17 cells, dendritic cells, macrophages as well as fibroblast (2). By comparison, DR3 is predominantly expressed on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, macrophages, B cells, and endothelial cells (2). Remarkably, recent phase 2 clinical trials have shown positive results in patients with both CD and UC treated with monoclonal antibodies against TL1A (9).

The positive correlation between Tnfrsf25/DR3 and Tnfsf15/TL1A mRNA expression levels and alveolar bone loss in WT SAMP mice, which was absent in SAMPxDR3−/− and healthy AKR controls, is consistent with an association between DR3 and disease severity, although not indicative of direct causality. Accordingly, the reduction in IL-17 expression in SAMPxDR3−/− mice suggests involvement of Th17-driven immunity, which plays a central role in chronic mucosal inflammation (10).

Although initially described as an amplifier of Th1 responses (11), the TL1A–DR3 axis is now recognized as a broader regulator of effector immunity, with particular relevance to Th17 responses (12). IL-17 promotes inflammation and induces downstream proinflammatory mediators, including TNF-α and IL-1β, which are key regulators of periodontal inflammation (13, 14). This inflammatory circuit also involves innate immunity. Reduced neutrophil frequencies in SAMPxDR3−/− mice are consistent with decreased IL-17–mediated recruitment and activation, supporting a feed-forward loop between adaptive and innate immune responses. Neutrophils also contribute to periodontal inflammation by producing proinflammatory mediators, including TNF-α and IL-1β (15).

Taken together, these findings support a model in which the TL1A/DR3 axis contributes to amplification of the inflammatory response in chronic periodontitis. However, whether this pathway directly regulates osteoclastogenesis or primarily modulates inflammation remains to be determined.

Overall, our findings identify the TL1A–DR3 axis as a previously underappreciated regulator of periodontal inflammation and support further investigation of its mechanistic role and therapeutic potential. Given emerging clinical evidence supporting TL1A blockade in inflammatory bowel disease, targeting this pathway may also hold promise for patients with concurrent intestinal and periodontal inflammation.

Material Summary

We evaluated spontaneous periodontal disease in SAMP1/YitFc, AKR/J control, SAMPxDR3−/− mice at 10 and 20 wk of age. Alveolar bone loss and periodontal tissue damage were assessed by stereomicroscopy quantification and H&E histology. Finally, gingival tissues were analyzed by RT-qPCR, ELISA, and flow cytometry to measure Tl1a/Dr3 expression, inflammatory cytokines, CD4+ T helper cells, and neutrophils.
