The moderating effect of emotional eating behaviour on body fat percentage in women
Authors: Didem Yüzügüllü, Burak Mete, Hakan Demirhindi, Mehmet Yüzügüllü
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity is a global health problem where emotional eating, linked to impulsivity, significantly complicates prevention efforts. This study examined the direct effects of emotional eating on body fat percentage and whether emotional eating moderates the relationship between lipid parameters and body fat percentage in women seeking obesity counselling.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted among 271 women who applied for counselling on obesity and healthy eating. Body fat was measured by the bioelectrical impedance method. Direct and indirect effects of emotional eating behaviour on body fat content were examined.ResultsThe mean age of 271 women included in our study was 38.2 ± 11.4 years, among them the rate of “emotional” and of “very emotional” eaters was 53.1% and 12.2%. HDL levels were lower; LDL levels were higher in “very emotional” eaters. There was a positive correlation between emotional eating and blood triglyceride or HbA1c values, and a negative correlation with HDL or physical activity level. Emotional eating level was determined as an important moderator in the relationship between blood cholesterol and LDL values or body fat percentage. In multivariate linear regression analysis, it was found that a one-unit increase in cholesterol, and HbA1c, was associated with a 0.057-unit, and 3.158-unit increase in body fat level, respectively, while a one-unit increase in HDL was associated with a 0.128-unit decrease in body fat level.ConclusionsLDL and triglyceride levels were higher in patients with higher levels of emotional eating, which was also determined as an important moderator affecting the relationship between body fat percentage and total cholesterol or LDL blood levels, in a strengthening (increasing) way.Keywords: Obesity, Emotional eating, Cholesterol, LDL

Background

Obesity, which has become a serious public health problem globally, is linked to many causes of morbidity and mortality like increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes, hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia, muscle and bone disorders and some types of cancer. In addition, it imposes a significant economic burden on society due to increased health costs, loss of labour force and decreased productivity [1]. Although healthy nutrition and regular physical activity play a critical role in the prevention and treatment of obesity, low compliance with these methods limits their effectiveness [2]. Physical activity is one of the main elements of lifestyle change in overweight or obese individuals and is defined as the process of energy expenditure involving whole-body movements. Exercise is a more planned and systematic form of physical activity [3]. Research shows that emotional control disorders and intense negative emotions are associated with the emergence of obesity and are linked to difficulties in the treatment process [4]. Emotional eating is defined as the tendency to eat in response to negative emotional states [5]. However, unlike some eating disorders, emotional eating is not directly linked to a complete loss of control and overeating [6]. Emotional eating is not classified as an eating disorder, but is considered a phenomenon associated with eating behaviour. Focusing on specific foods, the act of eating, and the emotions associated with eating, it appears that the lack of self-regulation underlying overeating has two key components: impulsivity (the tendency to react impulsively without thinking) and reward sensitivity [7, 8]. These two factors have a strong relationship with overeating and obesity. Many risk factors play a role in obesity becoming a global problem. However, it is understood that environmental and individual factors, especially emotional eating, have a complex and important effect on this process. This study aimed to examine the direct effects of emotional eating on body fat percentage and whether emotional eating moderates the relationship between lipid parameters and body fat percentage in women individuals who applied to an obesity outpatient clinic for healthy eating counselling.

Methods

Research type and sampling

This cross-sectional study comprised people who applied to Çukurova University Faculty of Medicine Public Health Department or Adana Provincial Health Directorate Obesity and Healthy Nutrition Counselling Unit for counselling services in 2024 and 2025.

Ethical approval for the study was granted by the “Çukurova University Faculty of Medicine Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee” (dated 08.04.2022, meeting number 121 and decree number 23. The ethical principles outlined in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent revisions were adhered to in the study. In addition, the study was evaluated and approved by the “Clinical Research, Non-Interventional Clinical Research, and Scientific Research and Project Studies Pre-Authorisation Evaluation Commission in Health Facilities” of the Provincial Health Directorate (dated 30.01.2023). Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants. Study method and results are reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement for cross-sectional studies.

When an effect size of 0.03, a power (1-β) of 0.99 and a type 1 error (α) of 0.01 in a double-tailed test was accepted as reference, the minimum sample size was calculated as 247. In our study, 271 individuals were reached. Only volunteers who wanted to participate in the study were included. After obtaining verbal and written consent from the participants, two questionnaires (“General Questionnaire” and the Emotional Eaters Questionnaire (EEQ)) were administered by the researchers themselves through face-to-face interviews and anthropometric measurements were made.

Inclusion criteria were to have applied for obesity and healthy nutrition counselling, to be between the ages of 18 and 75, and to volunteer to participate in the study.

Exclusion criteria were the presence of corticosteroid or hormone therapy in the last 6 months, to have participated in a weight loss programme and/or had weight loss of more than 5% in the last 12 months, being pregnant, and presence of cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, heart failure, arrhythmia, liver or kidney failure, endocrine disorders (e.g., hypothyroidism, Cushing’s syndrome), skeletal malformation or dependence on other mobile assistive devices. Individuals using weight-control medications (e.g., corticosteroids) or those with diagnosed eating disorders were also excluded from the study.

Anthropometric measurements

Anthropometric measurements were taken by a dietitian. Height was measured with a height gauge by making sure that the feet of the individuals were adjacent and that they were in the Frankfort plane (the top of the eye and auricle being at the same level). Body weight was obtained from the Tanita BC-418 MA Body Composition Analyser (Tanita Co., Tokyo, Japan) used for bioelectrical impedance analysis, as described in the “Measurement of body fat percentage” section below. No other anthropometric measurements were taken.

Measurement of body fat percentage

At the beginning of the study, the percentage of body fat mass was measured and recorded by a dietitian. Measurements were made after ensuring that the individuals had not performed any heavy physical activity in the last 24–48 h, had no metal wearing on their body, and did not have a pacemaker. To standardise measurement conditions, participants were instructed to fast for at least two hours prior to measurement, to maintain normal hydration levels (avoiding excessive fluid intake or dehydration), to refrain from alcohol consumption within 24 h before measurement, and to void their bladder immediately before the assessment. Body composition data of the participants were obtained with the bioimpedance method using Tanita’s Body Composition Analyser BC-418 MA (Tanita Co., Tokyo, Japan). The method is based on the difference in electrical impedance between lean tissue mass and fat mass. In bioelectrical impedance analysis, body fat, lean body mass, body water content, and other data are obtained by sending a weak electrical current (800 µa, 50 kHz) to the individual’s body and receiving it back through the extremities.

Biochemical tests

The study included patients making their first visit to an obesity clinic, and biochemical tests performed prior to the clinic visit (within the last month) were evaluated. These tests were routinely performed by family physicians before individuals were referred to an obesity clinic.

All routine biochemistry, hormone, and haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) samples from individuals evaluated with blood tests in the obesity and healthy nutrition outpatient clinic between the dates encompassing the study period were analysed retrospectively. The biochemical data included in the study were fasting glucose, fasting insulin, HbA1c, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), triglyceride, and insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) values. The biochemical tests evaluated in the study were performed in the Adana Provincial Public Health Laboratory, which is the reference laboratory for all primary health care institutions in the province, including family health centres. Bio-Rad Variant II Turbo device (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) was used for HbA1c analysis according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The measurement principle is based on cation exchange chromatography. A biochemical autoanalyzer (Siemens ADVIA 2400 Chemistry Analyser; Siemens Healthcare GmbH, Erlangen, Germany) was used to measure lipid (triglycerides, HDL and LDL cholesterol) and glucose levels. International physical activity questionnaire

The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), developed by Craig et al. (2003), was used to assess the physical activity level of the participants [9]. The validity and reliability study of the Turkish translation of the questionnaire was conducted by Öztürk [10]. In our study, the self-administered short form of the questionnaire, consisting of seven questions, covering the previous week, was used to assess the level of physical activity. The calculation of the total score for the short form includes the sum of the duration (in minutes) and frequency (in days) of walking, moderately vigorous activity, and vigorous activity. A score is obtained as “MET-minutes a week” by multiplying minutes, days and MET value. One MET is what you expend when you are at rest. In the calculation of the walking score, walking time (in minutes) is multiplied by 3.3 METs, by 4 METs for moderately vigorous activity and 8 METs for vigorous activity. Physical activity levels were categorised as “physical inactivity” (< 600 MET-min/week), low physical activity (600–3000 MET-min/week) and adequate physical activity (> 3000 MET-min/week) [9].

Emotional eater questionnaire (EEQ)

It is a Likert-type scale with four possible answers: “never” (0 score), “sometimes” (1 score), “generally” (2 score) and “always” (3 score). The maximum score that can be achieved is 30, and the lower the score, the healthier the eating behaviour. According to the scores obtained, four groups can be distinguished: 0−5 as “non-emotional eater”, 6−10 as “low emotional eater”, 11−20 as “emotional eater”, and 21−30 as “very emotional eater” [11]. In the present study, the EEQ was analysed using the total score approach. The validity and reliability study of the translation of the EEQ into Turkish was conducted by Arslantaş et al.; the Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.84 (95%) [12]. To assess the internal consistency of the EEQ in the current clinical sample, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated. The EEQ demonstrated good internal consistency in the present study (α = 0.79), which is consistent with the value reported in the Turkish validation study (α = 0.84) [12]. These findings support the reliability of the scale for use in a clinical adult population seeking obesity counselling.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using JAMOVI version 2.6.44 (Sydney, Australia) [13]. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the distribution. Parametric tests were used for the analysis of normally distributed data, while non-parametric tests were employed for data that did not fit the normal distribution. Normally distributed data were analyzed using parametric tests (independent samples t-test or one-way ANOVA with post-hoc Tukey), while non-normally distributed data were analyzed using non-parametric tests (Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc for group comparisons, and Spearman’s rank correlation for bivariate associations). Multivariate linear regression analysis (enter method) was used to estimate the predictors of body fat percentage. The following independent variables were entered into the model: emotional eating score (EEQ), HbA1c, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, triglyceride, HOMA-IR, age, marital status, and physical activity level (MET-min/week). Collinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF), with values < 5 considered acceptable. Estimated marginal means were calculated at low (mean – 1 SD), mean, and high (mean + 1 SD) levels of each significant predictor to illustrate the magnitude of the effects. Moderation analyses were conducted using the in JAMOVI (medmod module) with 1000 bootstrap samples. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To further illustrate the magnitude and direction of the significant predictors, estimated marginal means of body fat percentage were calculated at low (mean – 1 SD), mean, and high (mean + 1 SD) levels of each predictor, while holding all other covariates constant at their means. These results are presented in Table 5 and illustrated in Fig. 2. HbA1c: The estimated mean body fat percentage increased progressively across HbA1c levels. At low HbA1c (5.00 mmol/mol), the estimated body fat percentage was 36.8% (95% CI: 35.0–38.5). This increased to 38.2% (95% CI: 36.6–39.9) at the mean HbA1c level (5.49 mmol/mol) and further to 39.7% (95% CI: 37.8–41.7) at high HbA1c (5.99 mmol/mol). The difference between low and high HbA1c levels was approximately 2.9% points, indicating a clinically meaningful increase in adiposity with worsening glycemic control. Total cholesterol: A stronger gradient was observed for total cholesterol. At low total cholesterol (153 mg/dL), the estimated body fat percentage was 35.6% (95% CI: 33.6–37.7). This rose to 38.2% (95% CI: 36.6–39.9) at the mean level (199 mg/dL) and reached 40.9% (95% CI: 38.6–43.2) at high total cholesterol (245 mg/dL). The difference of 5.3% points between low and high cholesterol levels represents the largest effect among all predictors, highlighting the strong independent association between total cholesterol and body fat accumulation. HDL cholesterol: As expected, HDL cholesterol showed an inverse relationship. At low HDL (41.8 mg/dL), the estimated body fat percentage was 39.6% (95% CI: 37.7–41.5). This decreased to 38.2% (95% CI: 36.6–39.9) at the mean HDL level (52.1 mg/dL) and further to 36.9% (95% CI: 35.1–38.7) at high HDL (62.3 mg/dL). The 2.7% point difference between low and high HDL levels supports the protective role of HDL against adiposity. Physical activity: Higher physical activity levels were associated with lower body fat percentage. At low physical activity (176 MET-min/week), the estimated body fat percentage was 39.2% (95% CI: 37.4–41.1). This decreased to 38.2% (95% CI: 36.6–39.9) at the mean activity level (1079 MET-min/week) and further to 37.2% (95% CI: 35.5–39.0) at high physical activity (1983 MET-min/week). The 2.0% point difference, although modest, confirms that even small increases in physical activity may be associated with lower body fat.

Discussion

Emotional eating refers to overeating during dysphoric moods and is associated with weight gain, binge eating disorder, depression and poor emotion regulation skills. Studies on the subject suggest that the treatment of overweight and obesity in individuals with emotional eating behaviour should focus on emotion regulation skills, not calorie-restricted diets [14, 15]. Therefore, it can be suggested that emotional eating is an important factor associated with the control of obesity.

In our study, the relationship between emotional eating behaviour, lipid parameters, and body fat percentage was examined, and it was found that HDL levels were lower and LDL levels were higher in individuals who were categorised as “very emotional eaters” according to the EEQ [11]. Additionally, the EEQ scores showed a weak positive correlation with blood triglyceride and HbA1c values, while no correlation with BMI and body fat percentage. While no direct significant effect of emotional eating on body fat percentage was observed, it was found as an important moderator of the relationship between body fat percentage and total cholesterol, LDL, or triglyceride levels; i.e. emotional eating positively moderated the relationship between cholesterol and body fat percentage when the scores of the EEQ were increased or decreased by one standard deviation, and it positively moderated the relationship between LDL and body fat percentage at low emotional eating levels.

Emotional eating is a common eating behaviour in overweight and obese people and is considered the main target of interventions to combat obesity [14, 15]. In our study, the rate of low emotional eaters was 24.4%, that of emotional eaters was 53.1%, and that of very emotional eaters was 12.2%. In another study that applied EEQ to university students, 68% of female students and 63% of male students were found to be emotional eaters at any level, i.e. low, moderate or very emotional eaters [16]. The high rates obtained in our study drew attention to the prevalence of emotional eating behaviour among obese and overweight individuals. Being male or female was also identified as an important biological determinant of emotional eating [17]. Studies indicated that females were particularly susceptible to eating in response to negative emotions [17, 18]. The study by Péneau et al. emphasised that French adult women engaged in more emotional eating behaviours than men [19]. In our study, the effect of sex could not be evaluated because almost all of the people who applied to the Obesity and Healthy Nutrition outpatient clinic were female.

Pacheco et al. investigated the relationship between eating behaviour scores and body composition in 555 Chilean young adults, and they found that the risk of emotional eating was higher in obese adults compared to those without obesity [20]. A cross-sectional study comprising 400 young Peruvian adults reported that emotional eating was more prevalent among overweight and obese participants compared to normal weight and underweight participants [21]. A prospective study pointed out a positive relationship between emotional eating and BMI [22]. The findings suggest that emotional eating is positively associated with BMI in both males and females, with a greater association in females. Similarly, a study involving 600 Italian young adults reported that emotional eating was associated with higher BMI [23]. In contrast to previous studies, no significant relationship was found between EEQ scores and BMI or body fat percentage in our study. Although no direct association was observed between emotional eating scores and body fat percentage, emotional eating may still play an important role by influencing the strength of the relationship between metabolic risk factors and adiposity. One possible explanation is that emotional eating reflects a psychological vulnerability rather than an isolated determinant of body composition. Individuals with emotional eating behaviour are more likely to consume highly palatable, energy-dense foods in response to stress or negative emotions, which may exacerbate the metabolic impact of dyslipidemia over time. In addition, emotional eating has been linked to impaired emotion regulation, impulsivity, and stress-related activation of neuroendocrine pathways, including increased cortisol secretion, all of which may contribute to altered lipid metabolism and fat accumulation. Therefore, emotional eating may not independently predict body fat percentage in a cross-sectional analysis, but instead may act as a moderator that amplifies the effect of adverse lipid profiles, particularly elevated cholesterol and LDL levels, on adiposity.

Emotional eating had a moderator effect, and both high and low levels of emotional eating showed a moderator effect that increased the relationship between total cholesterol and BMI. This might be related to the fact that the participants were admitted to an obesity outpatient clinic and, therefore, their BMI was already higher than the general population. It is known that emotional eating often involves the consumption of high-calorie comfort foods, typically rich in sugar and fat, which are associated with excessive caloric intake and linked to weight gain over time [24]. Our study found that emotional eating was not statistically significant in the regression model (p = 0.815). Although triglyceride levels were not independently associated with body fat percentage in the multivariate regression model, the significant interaction observed in the moderation analysis suggests that the effect of triglycerides on adiposity may depend on emotional eating behaviour. In this multivariate linear regression model (enter method), the following variables were simultaneously controlled: emotional eating score (EEQ), HbA1c, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, triglyceride, HOMA-IR, age, marital status, and physical activity level (MET-min/week). BMI was deliberately excluded as a covariate due to its high collinearity with body fat percentage (r = 0.803, p < 0.001), which would have introduced statistical over-control. This is consistent with the previously demonstrated moderating role of emotional eating on body fat, rather than a direct effect. In other words, emotional eating is a factor that strengthens the relationship between lipid parameters and body fat, but it is not a direct predictor on its own. Ripoli et al. indicated that individuals with higher EEQ scores had higher HbA1c, total and LDL cholesterol values [25], and these changes in plasma lipids were associated with increased intake of energy-dense foods rich in sugar and fat in emotional eaters [26]. Mensorio et al. investigated the mediator role of emotional eating between anxiety and cholesterol in overweight and overweight individuals. Emotional eating was found to be significantly associated with higher total and LDL cholesterol levels and to act as a mediator in the relationship between anxiety and high cholesterol [27]. In a longitudinal study involving Korean men and women, emotional eating was associated with HDL levels, and HDL values were found to be lower in emotional eaters [28]. Wu et al. assessed a positive correlation between triglyceride levels and emotional eating [29]. In this context, the results of our study indicated that HDL levels were lower, while triglyceride levels were higher among those with higher emotional eating levels. We also observed that the mean LDL level was significantly higher and the mean HDL level was significantly lower among very emotional eaters. In addition, emotional eating was an important moderator in the relationship between body fat percentage and total cholesterol or LDL levels, by positively strengthening this relationship. These results were consistent with the results of the previous studies mentioned above. However, in a different study conducted with 200 adults in Istanbul, Türkiye, no statistically significant relationship was reported between emotional eating and triglyceride levels [30]. This difference might be due to the different methodology used to measure emotional eating in the study conducted in Istanbul, which was the Dutch Eating Behaviour Questionnaire.

Two studies in the United States (U.S.) of America evaluated the relationship between emotional eating and physical activity among military service members. Emotional eating was associated with higher BMI and lower physical activity among U.S. Army and other active-duty military service members [31, 32]. A cross-sectional study investigating the relationship between emotional eating and being physically active during the COVID-19 Pandemic, it was observed that physical activity might be a protective factor in the emergence of emotional eating behaviour [33]. Similar to the results of these studies, the results of our study showed a negative relationship between emotional eating and physical activity level. Emotional eaters are known to consume food as a coping or recovery strategy to manage their emotional states [34]. Although emotional eating behaviour has been so far associated with less physical activity [35], more studies are needed on whether increasing physical activity can be considered or not as a protective factor for triggering emotional eating behaviour [33, 36].

Limitations

As the type of diet is a factor that may affect body fat percentage, BMI and blood biochemical values, the fact that the type of diet (i.e., what kind of diet the person has) was not taken into account and its confounding effect could not be controlled statistically might be declared as a limitation. Secondly, there is a possibility of information bias in terms of the bioelectrical impedance method. Specifically, some participants may not have fully complied with the pre-measurement requirements (e.g., fasting for at least two hours, refraining from heavy physical activity in the 24–48 h prior to measurement, and avoiding alcohol consumption). Such non-compliance could introduce measurement error, potentially leading to an overestimation or underestimation of body fat percentage in some individuals; however, these deviations were minimised by the standardised instructions provided to participants before measurement. Finally, the cross-sectional nature of the study makes it difficult to evaluate the relationship in terms of cause and effect.

Conclusion

The results of our study indicated that LDL and triglyceride levels were higher in patients with higher levels of emotional eating, which was also determined as an important moderator affecting the relationship between body fat percentage and total cholesterol or LDL blood levels, in a strengthening (increasing) way.

Final considerations

We can conclude that the evaluation of emotional eating may be associated positively with the management, i.e. prevention and treatment of obesity, especially in obese patients with abnormal blood lipid levels.
