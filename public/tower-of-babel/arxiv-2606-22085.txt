Can Reasoning Models Detect Changes to their Chains of Thought?
Sathvik Napa, Utkarsh Singh, Chengyuan Xue, Miriam Wanner, William Walden

Abstract
There are many reasons one may want to edit a model’s chain of thought (CoT)—e.g., to prefill it with reasoning from a stronger model or to remove steps that may yield unsafe outputs. The success of these interventions plausibly depends on a model’s inability to notice them, as the model may alter its behavior if it suspects tampering. In this work, we study whether recent reasoning models are able to detect such interventions on their CoTs under a variety of conditions: both during reasoning and after it, and when prefilled both with their own CoTs and with those of other models. Broadly, we find that (i) models exhibit only very modest detection accuracy; (ii) models struggle to identify how their CoT was modified; and (iii) models are about as good at detecting changes to their own CoTs as to those of other models. 1 1 1 Code: github.com/wgantt/cot-interventions

Introduction

There are many scenarios in which one may want to edit the chain of thought (CoT) of a reasoning model. For instance, many evaluations of the
faithfulness

(

The effectiveness of CoT interventions in these scenarios plausibly depends on the model
not
noticing them. In evaluations, for example, a model capable of detecting edits to its CoT could make inferences about the true purpose of the evaluation based on those edits and strategically alter its outputs—a behavior known as
scheming

(
3
)
. To preempt such behavior, and to preserve CoT monitorability
(
12
)
, we thus need to know whether models can detect CoT modifications in the first place (
Figure 1
).

Here, we investigate this question across several recent reasoning models and experimental conditions, manipulating (i) the type of modification that is made; (ii) whether we check for modification recognition
during
CoT generation or only in the final output; (iii) whether models are prefilled with their own CoT or with those of another model; and (iv) whether models must merely detect
whether
a change has been made or instead must locate
where
that change was made. Broadly, we find that:

1.

Even when models are alerted to the possibility of modification, detection ability is generally only modestly above chance (§
4.1
–§
4.2
).

2.

Models are equally good at detecting changes to their own CoTs and to those of others, suggesting self-awareness plays no role (§
4.3
).

3.

Even when models can detect
whether
an edit was made, they struggle to detect
where
(§
5
).

Background

Many works study LLMs’
situational awareness

(
5
)
, i.e., their ability to understand themselves, their environment, and their place within it.
2
and
14

18

evaluation awareness

Effective detection of CoT modifications may require highly developed
self-awareness

19

7

11

16

Though many works modify CoTs for other purposes (e.g., faithfulness evaluations
(
23
;
4
)
), the only other study on modification
detection
is informal work by
6

(

and GPT-OSS-120B
(
1

on MATH-500
(
10

We extend their preliminary investigation with additional rigor—expanding the model suite, datasets, and variety of interventions studied—and with an explicit investigation into the role of self-awareness in detection ability (§

Detecting Changes

Here, we investigate
whether
models can detect modifications to their CoTs. For all experiments, a random 50% of CoTs are modified and the remainder are left unmodified. All plots report detection accuracy with 95% confidence intervals (denoted by error bars) and whether the result is significantly different from chance accuracy (
=50\%,\alpha=.05
) under a two-sided z-test (denoted by “*” in plots).

4.1
Completed Condition

We first consider whether models can detect whether their
completed
CoTs have been modified, using the
\texttt{D}_{\texttt{half}}
,
\texttt{E}_{\texttt{rep}}
, and
\texttt{I}_{\texttt{one}}
interventions.
The darker bars in
Figure 2
show the results. Broadly, we find that models are able to detect changes at rates modestly above chance, though often not significantly so. This is particularly surprising given how radical the interventions are: Even when removing a CoT’s entire latter half (
\texttt{D}_{\texttt{half}}
; leftmost column), accuracy is not significantly better than chance for most results on
GPQA
and
MMLU
. Further, we see similar results when replacing the
full CoT
with one from another model (
\texttt{E}_{\texttt{rep}}
; third column). Finally, no model is consistently more accurate than others across datasets and intervention types. Dataset and intervention type effects are stronger: Accuracy tends to be higher on
\texttt{I}_{\texttt{one}}
than for other interventions, and on
AIME
than on the other two datasets.

4.2
Partial Condition

Next, we assess whether models can detect changes
from within the CoT itself
. In contrast to §
4.1
, high accuracy here would imply that models can detect CoT changes
during reasoning
—a prerequisite for CoT-based scheming
(
3
)
. We modify the first half of a completed CoT for the target model (for 50% of examples) using
\texttt{E}_{\texttt{rep}}
,
\texttt{E}_{\texttt{par}}
, or
\texttt{I}_{\texttt{one}}
; prefill the target model with the (possibly modified) partial CoT; and finally have the model complete it and answer the problem.

Results are shown in the lighter bars in
Figure 2
. Here, we see qualitative similarities to results in the
completed
condition (§
4.1
), with accuracies modestly above chance (though often below; see
\texttt{E}_{\texttt{rep}}

\texttt{E}_{\texttt{par}}

\texttt{I}_{\texttt{one}}

\texttt{E}_{\texttt{rep}}

(
13

20

4.1

5
Localizing Changes

We have shown that models have only moderate ability to detect CoT modifications. We now investigate whether they are able to identify
where
modifications occur. To facilitate clear localization of changes, we make modifications involving only a single step—either inserting (
\texttt{I}_{\texttt{one}}
), deleting (
\texttt{D}_{\texttt{one}}
), or paraphrasing (
\texttt{E}_{\texttt{one}}
) a randomly chosen step within the target model’s
completed
CoT. In contrast to §
4
, we modify
all
CoTs, editing the system prompt to inform the model that its CoT has in fact been modified (and how), and to ask it to identify at which step the modification was made.

6
Conclusion

Figure 4:
Model accuracy in identifying
where
a CoT modification was made on
MMLU
.

We have investigated whether a range of current reasoning models are able to detect modifications to their chains of thought (CoTs), finding that models generally have only very modest detection accuracy—and even then, only when alerted to the possibility of modifications (§
4.1
–§
4.2
). Moreover, models are no better at detecting modifications to their
own
CoTs than modifications to
other models’
CoTs, and—to the extent they
can
detect modifications—this merely reflects a general capacity to spot abnormalities in reasoning processes (§
4.3
). Finally, we showed that models also struggle to localize
where
modifications are made, performing well only on a single intervention type (§
5
). Overall, our results thus provide strong evidence
against
a key, safety-relevant form of self-awareness in these models.

Limitations

The models we suspect are likely to be most accurate in detecting modifications to their CoTs are proprietary frontier models, whose APIs do not support CoT prefilling, and which we therefore cannot study in this work. More generally, this study necessarily can only capture reasoning models that support prefilling and that are publicly available as of May 2026. Given the rapid rate of model releases, it is conceivable that reasoning models released in the near future could exhibit much higher change detection and localization accuracy than the models investigated here. Moreover, these models could also potentially show differentially strong ability at detecting changes to their
own
CoTs. Both findings would require revisiting some of our conclusions.

We evaluate models
as released
—i.e., without any further training. However, it is conceivable that further post-training could enhance detection ability. This is a direction we would be keen to see explored in future work.

Ethics

Since our work presents an evaluation of publicly available models on widely used public benchmarks, we do not believe it raises any substantive ethical concerns.
