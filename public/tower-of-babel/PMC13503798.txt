Introduction

The detection and monitoring of trace gases play a vital role in a wide range of applications, including industrial safety, environmental protection, healthcare diagnostics, and energy infrastructure management. Among these gases, methane (CH4) is of particular concern due to its dual role as both a valuable energy source and a potent greenhouse gas with a global warming potential approximately 28 times that of carbon dioxide (CO2) over a 100-year timescale1–3, making its mitigation a critical climate goal. Rapid and reliable methane detection is essential for mitigating hazardous leaks, enhancing operational safety, and supporting climate monitoring initiatives. Conventional gas sensors, such as catalytic bead, electrochemical, and metal oxide semiconductor (MOS) sensors, while widely used, often suffer from limitations including high power consumption, limited selectivity, slow response times, and susceptibility to environmental factors. These challenges have driven growing interest in the development of advanced optical and optoelectronic sensing platforms for various analytes such as NO24–6 and acetone7, which offer improved sensitivity, miniaturization potential, and compatibility with integrated photonic systems. In this context, the supramolecular host cryptophane A has shown exceptional promise for methane-selective sensing due to its unique molecular recognition properties. This has led to sensors built on diverse platforms including those based on luminescence quenching8, long-period gratings9, and advanced interferometric techniques such as Sagnac loops and the Vernier effect10,11. Furthermore, research has explored various cryptophane derivatives, such as cryptophane-E, to tune selectivity and sensitivity12,13. However, the adsorption kinetics of the cryptophane layer ultimately define the overall system response time, a key parameter for real-time applications.

Optical sensing platforms have emerged as powerful alternatives to conventional gas sensors, offering advantages such as immunity to electromagnetic interference, compatibility with remote and distributed sensing architectures, and the potential for high sensitivity and miniaturization. Among these, refractive index (RI)-based sensors have gained significant attention for detecting chemical and biological analytes by monitoring changes in the optical properties of functional materials upon exposure to target molecules. In the context of gas sensing, these devices operate by transducing subtle RI variations–induced by gas adsorption–into measurable spectral shifts, making them highly suitable for label-free and real-time detection. One particularly effective strategy for enhancing the sensitivity of RI sensors is the use of Fano resonances, which produce sharp, asymmetric spectral features resulting from the interference between narrow discrete modes and broadband continua, a principle that has been successfully applied in plasmonic ring resonators, micro-cavities, and biosensors14–19. These features enable the precise tracking of resonance wavelength shifts caused by small changes in the surrounding refractive index. Combining the methane-responsive optical properties of cryptophane A, a supramolecular host known for its selective affinity toward methane20–22, with a Fano-resonant photonic structure and a monolithically integrated photodetector provides a promising pathway toward a compact, self-powered optoelectronic sensor capable of converting gas-induced optical shifts into quantifiable electrical signals.

While optical refractive index sensors offer excellent sensitivity and chemical specificity, their practical deployment often requires additional instrumentation such as spectrometers, external photodetectors, or bulky fiber-coupled readout systems, which can hinder miniaturization and on-chip integration. Bridging this gap between high-performance optical sensing and compact, scalable implementation calls for the monolithic integration of photonic and electronic components into a single device platform23. However, such integration introduces several design challenges, including the need for spectral alignment between the optical resonant structure and the photodetector responsivity, efficient coupling of light into the active detection region, and material compatibility between the photonic layer and the underlying semiconductor electronics. Furthermore, achieving sufficient absorption of the modulated optical signal while maintaining fast electrical response times requires careful optimization of the photodetector geometry, absorption layer thickness, and device biasing conditions. Addressing these challenges is essential for realizing fully integrated, self-powered gas sensors that are not only sensitive and selective but also robust, scalable, and suitable for portable or embedded sensing applications.

In this work, we present the design and simulation of a compact, integrated optoelectronic methane sensor that combines a Fano-resonant photonic structure with a monolithically integrated InP/In0.52Ga0.48As p-i-n photodetector. The sensor operates by exploiting the refractive index change of a methane-sensitive cryptophane A layer, which modulates the optical transmission spectrum of a nanostructured GaAs film. This modulation is detected as a change in photocurrent by the underlying photodiode, enabling direct electrical readout of methane concentration without the need for external spectroscopic equipment. We systematically investigate the optical transmission characteristics, photodetector responsivity, absorption efficiency, and transient response of the integrated system through multiphysics simulations. The results demonstrate that the proposed structure enables high optical sensitivity, strong light absorption within the detector, and ultrafast photoresponse in self-powered mode, meeting the requirements of modern gas sensing applications.

The strategic selection of an operating wavelength in the C–band range is fundamental to maximizing the sensor’s performance. In this near-infrared window, the guided-mode resonances supported by the photonic crystal exhibit a more extensive evanescent field, which penetrates deeper into the surrounding cryptophane-A layer. This enhanced field overlap magnifies the interaction between the optical mode and the sensing material. Consequently, any subtle, methane-induced change in the polymer’s refractive index is transduced into a larger, more readily detectable spectral shift, directly boosting the sensor’s sensitivity. Furthermore, this wavelength aligns with the telecommunications C-band, offering the significant practical advantage of leveraging mature, low-cost, and high-performance components such as lasers and detectors, which is critical for developing commercially viable sensing systems.

A cornerstone of the proposed sensor design is its capability for self-biased (or self-powered) operation. By functioning at zero electrical bias, the photodetector operates in photovoltaic mode, eliminating static power consumption and the associated need for a stable external voltage source. This advantage is paramount for applications where power is a critical constraint, such as in large-scale distributed sensor networks for pipeline monitoring, battery-operated portable devices for industrial safety, and energy-autonomous nodes within the Internet of Things (IoT) ecosystem. Eliminating the external power supply not only simplifies the system architecture, reducing size, weight, and cost (SWaP-C), but also inherently minimizes sources of electronic noise, such as power supply ripple. This leads to a lower noise floor and an improved signal-to-noise ratio, which is essential for detecting trace concentrations of methane with high fidelity.

This work lays the foundation for the development of scalable, low-power, and high-performance methane sensors suitable for real-time monitoring in industrial, environmental, and safety-critical settings.

Optical structure design

The optical structure was designed to exhibit a sharp Fano resonance in the near-infrared region (~1560 nm), a technique widely employed for high-sensitivity refractive index sensing14–16. The design consists of a 65 nm-thick GaAs film patterned with cylindrical holes of 210 nm radius and 65 nm depth. The holes are arranged in a square lattice with a period of 0.93 μm. The nanoholes and the surface are coated with cryptophane A, whose refractive index change upon methane binding is the core transduction mechanism20. For simulation purposes, the refractive index of the cryptophane A layer is modeled as a function of methane concentration (C, in %) by the relation n = 1.4478 - 0.0038C. This approach of using computational modeling, such as FDTD or the Finite Element Method (FEM), to optimize photonic sensor designs is a common practice in the field11,13,27.

Optical simulations were performed using a finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) method. The specifics of the FDTD simulation setup, including mesh refinement and boundary condition implementation, are consistent with the methodology detailed in our previous publication28,29. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in the x and y directions, with perfectly matched layers (PMLs) terminating the z-axis. The structure was illuminated by a broadband, TM-polarized plane wave propagating normal to the surface. Transmission spectra were calculated for varying methane concentrations to determine the sensor’s optical sensitivity. For the FDTD optical simulations, a mesh size of 5 nm was employed in the region containing the photonic crystal structure, with gradually coarser meshing (up to 20 nm) in the substrate and superstrate regions to balance accuracy and computational efficiency. The simulation time was set to 5000 fs to ensure full convergence of the resonant modes. Material dispersion for GaAs and InP was modeled using the Sellmeier equation coefficients from Palik30,31, while the cryptophane-A refractive index was treated as dispersionless over the narrow wavelength range of interest (1550-1570 nm). The optical generation rate profiles were extracted by integrating the Poynting vector distribution across the InGaAs absorption layer and converting the absorbed power density to electron-hole pair generation using the photon energy at 1560.5 nm.

Photodetector structure and electrical simulation

The photodetector consists of an InP/In0.52Ga0.48As/InP p-i-n junction grown on an InP substrate. This material system is the standard for high-performance near-infrared detectors due to the excellent lattice matching between InGaAs and InP and the low dark current characteristics of InGaAs compared to other materials like Ge32,33. The intrinsic In0.52Ga0.48As absorption layer has a thickness of 3 μm. This thickness was optimized as a trade-off between high quantum efficiency and fast response time34. A 3 μm layer absorbs over 94% of incident 1560 nm light, based on an absorption coefficient of ~\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^4$$\end{document} cm-1, ensuring high responsivity crucial for detecting subtle optical signals35,36. Simultaneously, it maintains a carrier transit time of approximately 30 ps, which is orders of magnitude faster than the requirements for methane sensing. The top p-type and bottom n-type InP layers serve as cladding and contact layers.

Electrical device performance was modeled using the finite element method (FEM). Key material parameters were based on established literature values for III-V semiconductors34, including p-type and n-type doping concentrations of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \times 10^{18}$$\end{document} cm-3 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5 \times 10^{17}$$\end{document} cm-3, respectively. Carrier mobilities for electrons and holes in InGaAs were set to 8000 cm2/Vs and 400 cm2/Vs, and the SRH recombination lifetime was assumed to be 10 ns. For illumination studies, the transmitted optical power at 1560.5 nm was modeled as an optical generation source within the intrinsic region.

Coupled optoelectronic performance analysis

To assess the integrated performance, a multiphysics workflow was established, a necessary step for accurately modeling monolithic optoelectronic devices23. The spatially resolved transmitted power profile calculated from the FDTD simulation was used to define the optical generation rate within the InGaAs layer for the FEM electrical simulation. The transient response was evaluated at zero bias for self-powered mode and under a reverse bias of -2 V to analyze high-speed performance. The device’s RC time constant was estimated using the calculated junction capacitance and an assumed external load resistance of 50 Ω to contextualize the transient simulation results.

Results and discussion

Fano resonance and optical sensing characteristics

The core of the sensing mechanism relies on the engineered Fano resonance of the nanostructured GaAs photonic crystal, a phenomenon widely exploited for high-performance refractive index sensing due to its characteristic sharp and asymmetric lineshapes14,15,37. The simulated transmission spectra, presented in Figure 2, reveal the optical response of the device as a function of methane concentration. The spectra are characterized by a sharp Fano lineshape around 1560 nm, arising from the interference between a broad continuum mode and a discrete guided-mode resonance supported by the photonic crystal slab.

The key result, illustrated in Figure 2, is the systematic blueshift of the resonance dip with increasing methane concentration. As methane molecules are adsorbed by the cryptophane A host, the refractive index of the cryptophane A layer decreases according to the relation n = 1.4478 - 0.0038C20. This reduction in the surrounding refractive index lowers the effective index of the guided mode, causing the resonance to shift to shorter wavelengths. For instance, as the methane concentration increases from 0% to 3.95%, the resonance minimum shifts from approximately 1561 nm to 1564.24 nm, a total shift of more than 3 nm. This corresponds to an optical sensitivity of approximately 0.8 nm/%CH4. The steep slope of this Fano resonance is critical, as it ensures that a small, gas-induced wavelength shift is transduced into a large, detectable change in transmitted optical power at a fixed operating wavelength.

This Fano resonance originates from the destructive interference between a broadband continuum and a narrow discrete resonant modes38,39. The continuum represents the direct, non-resonant transmission of the incident plane wave through the slab. The discrete resonant mode is excited through the coupling of the incident light to a quasi-guided (leaky) mode supported by the defect-free square-lattice GaAs photonic crystal slab at the Γ-point38–40.Fig. 2Transmission spectra of the device for different methane concentrations. Transient response and high-speed operation

To evaluate the temporal performance of the proposed self-biased methane sensor, we conducted a detailed transient simulation of the normalized short-circuit current in response to sudden changes in methane concentration. The resulting time-domain responses for four different methane concentrations (0.78%, 1.84%, 2.89%, and 3.95%) are presented in Figure 8. These simulations were performed at the optimal probe wavelength of 1560.5 nmunder a -2 V reverse bias, capturing the rise and fall of the photocurrent as the gas concentration changes stepwise.

The application of a -2 V reverse bias drastically alters the device’s temporal characteristics. The strong electric field established across the intrinsic InGaAs region ensures that carrier transport is dominated by high-velocity drift, rather than slow diffusion. This enhanced field efficiently sweeps photogenerated electron-hole pairs to the contacts, and widens the depletion region, minimizing the collection of slow-diffusing carriers from the neutral p- and n-InP layers. As a result, the multi-nanosecond decay tail observed under zero-bias conditions, which was attributed to carrier trapping and diffusion, is completely suppressed.

As shown in Figure 8, the device exhibits an ultrafast response with rise and fall times in the range of 17–34 ps. This corresponds to an operational bandwidth well into the multi-GHz regime, confirming that the photodetector architecture is suitable for high-speed applications. The simulation was performed at a fixed operating wavelength of 1560.5 nm. At this wavelength, which lies on the short-wavelength slope of the Fano resonance dip (Figure 2), an increase in methane concentration causes a blueshift of the resonance. This shift results in lower optical transmission to the photodiode, leading to a smaller generated photocurrent, as clearly observed by the decreasing peak amplitudes in Figure 8.

Interestingly, the response time becomes slightly faster at higher methane concentrations. For instance, the rise time decreases from 28.2 ps at C=0.78% to 17.5 ps at C=3.95%. This behavior is attributed to reduced space-charge screening effects at lower optical power (corresponding to higher methane levels), which maintains a stronger internal field and accelerates carrier collection.

Crucially, this picosecond-scale optoelectronic response is many orders of magnitude faster than the chemical response time governed by the adsorption and desorption kinetics of methane in the cryptophane-A layer, which typically occurs on a timescale of milliseconds to seconds. This result confirms that the electrical performance of the integrated device is not a bottleneck for real-time gas sensing. The platform is capable of transducing even the fastest possible chemical changes without being limited by its intrinsic electronic speed, validating its design for high-performance, real-time monitoring applications.Fig. 8Simulated transient photoresponse of the detector under a -2 V reverse bias, illuminated by a 50 µW, 1560.5 nm optical pulse for different methane concentrations (C). Fabrication tolerance analysis

For practical deployment, the proposed sensor must maintain its performance despite inevitable variations introduced during nanofabrication. Two critical parameters that are subject to fabrication tolerances are the radius of the nanoholes in the GaAs photonic crystal and the thickness of the cryptophane-A sensing layer. To assess the robustness of the design, we performed additional FDTD simulations in which these parameters were varied over ranges representative of typical fabrication uncertainties, and we evaluated the impact on the Fano resonance position and the resulting methane sensitivity.

The nominal hole radius is r = 210 nm. Figure 9a shows the simulated transmission spectra for hole radii ranging from 210 nm to 210 nm in 1 nm increments, while keeping all other parameters at their nominal values. As the radius increases, the resonance dip blueshifts slightly due to the increased effective index of the photonic crystal slab. The observed shift is approximately 0.3 nm per 1 nm change in radius. More importantly, the slope of the resonance (which determines the sensitivity) remains virtually unchanged, with the extracted sensitivity at the optimal operating wavelength varying by less than 2% across the entire radius range. We therefore conclude that the sensor’s performance is robust against typical hole radius variations.Fig. 9Simulated transmission spectra for (a) different hole radii (210–215 nm) in the GaAs photonic crystal. The cryptophane-A sensing layer is deposited by spin-coating or dip-coating, methods that can yield thickness variations of ± 10 nm or more across a wafer. We simulated the device with analyte thicknesses for 300 nm and 310 nm. As shown in Fig. 9b, increasing the thickness slightly redshifts the resonance due to the increased modal overlap with the higher-index cryptophane-A. The shift is approximately 0.05 nm per 1 nm thickness change, resulting in a maximum shift of ± 1.5 nm over the ± 30 nm range. Again, the resonance shape and slope are preserved, and the sensitivity variation remains below 3%. This tolerance is well within acceptable limits for methane detection, especially given that the sensor response is calibrated per device.

Importantly, even under these extreme combined variations, the resonance retains its characteristic Fano lineshape and steep slope, as confirmed by simulations of the extreme cases. The extracted sensitivity at an appropriately adjusted probe wavelength remains within 85% of the nominal value.

In practice, fabrication processes can be controlled to tighter tolerances, and wafer-scale mapping can identify devices with acceptable parameter combinations. Moreover, since the sensor response is calibrated per device (either by measuring the full transmission spectrum or by scanning the probe wavelength to locate the resonance edge), these small shifts do not render devices non-functional–they merely require a slight adjustment of the operating wavelength. The monolithic integration of the photonic crystal with the underlying photodiode ensures that this alignment is stable and permanent once set.

Considering typical fabrication capabilities, we estimate that over 90% of fabricated devices would fall within a parameter window where the resonance shift is < ± 1.5 nm, corresponding to a sensitivity degradation of less than 10%. This projected yield is well within acceptable limits for commercial deployment, confirming that the proposed sensor design is not only high-performance but also robust and manufacturable at scale.

It is instructive to compare our fabrication tolerance results with recent advances in ultrahigh-Q guided mode resonances. Huang et al.43 demonstrated an elegant approach to achieving Q-factors up to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2.39 \times 10^5$$\end{document} by using a low-index photoresist as a perturbation layer on an unpatterned waveguide, thereby avoiding dry etching of the high-index semiconductor. While this strategy minimizes fabrication imperfections in passive structures, it is not directly transferable to functional gas sensors for two reasons. First, the photoresist serves only as a structural layer and cannot provide methane-selective adsorption; patterning nanoholes directly in cryptophane-A remains an unresolved experimental challenge. Second, purely optical resonators require external spectroscopic readout, whereas our monolithically integrated design directly transduces the resonance shift into photocurrent without additional instrumentation. Our analysis shows that even with etched GaAs structures, the device maintains robust performance against hole radius variations–the parameter most susceptible to etching errors–with sensitivity variation below 2% over a ±5 nm range. The combination of high-index GaAs (n ≈ 3.47) and asymmetric Fano resonance provides stronger light confinement and steeper spectral slopes than low-index polymer gratings, justifying the choice of a more complex fabrication process in exchange for full optoelectronic integration and methane specificity.

Conclusion

In this work, we presented the comprehensive design and multiphysics simulation of a monolithically integrated optoelectronic sensor for methane detection. The device architecture combines a Fano-resonant GaAs photonic crystal, functionalized with a methane-selective cryptophane-A layer, with an underlying high-performance InGaAs/InP p-i-n photodiode. The fundamental principle is the direct on-chip transduction of a gas-induced optical resonance shift into a measurable electrical signal, enabling a compact and spectrometer-free sensing platform.

Our simulations demonstrated the exceptional potential of this integrated approach. Key findings include: (1) a high sensitivity of 0.8124 (A/cm\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^2$$\end{document})/%CH\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_4$$\end{document} in a self-powered (zero-bias) configuration, combined with excellent linearity (R\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^2$$\end{document} = 0.9996) over a practical concentration range. (2) The high optical sensitivity was attributed to the strong electric field confinement of the Fano resonance within the nanostructured sensing layer, maximizing the light-matter interaction. (3) High quantum efficiency (>94%) was achieved by confining optical absorption exclusively to the InGaAs active region, as confirmed by material bandgap engineering. (4) Crucially, transient analysis revealed an ultrafast, picosecond-scale photoresponse, confirming that the device’s intrinsic electronic speed is many orders of magnitude faster than the chemical response time and does not represent a performance bottleneck for real-time gas monitoring.

The successful integration of these photonic and electronic components into a single, compact device addresses key limitations of conventional gas sensors, offering a path toward miniaturization, low power consumption, and scalability for mass production. This makes the proposed platform highly suitable for large-scale distributed sensor networks, portable industrial safety monitors, and autonomous environmental monitoring systems. Future work will focus on the experimental fabrication and characterization of the device to validate these promising simulation results. Further investigations could also explore the use of other supramolecular hosts to target different analytes and long-term stability testing in real-world conditions.
