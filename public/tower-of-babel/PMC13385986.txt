Fine-tuning and evaluating large language models for patient safety tasks: classification of contributing factors in incident reports
Authors: Ying Wang, Lorelle Bowditch, Charlotte Molloy, Yinghua Yu, Peter Hibbert, Farah Magrabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate and compare the performance of large language models (LLMs) in identifying contributing factors (CFs) underlying patient safety incident investigations.Materials and MethodsFour open-source, lightweight LLMs, including BERT, LLaMA2, GPT2, and Phi-2 were applied to classify CFs across 6 sociotechnical system-levels encompassing 12 categories (eg, person, task, and organizational factors). Reports of real-world patient safety investigations from public health systems were extracted and labelled by domain experts (n_report/CFs = 300/1338). Data were split into training (n = 852), validation (n = 98), and test sets (n = 388). Performance was evaluated using specificity, precision, recall, and F1 scores.ResultsThe fine-tuned encoder-based BERT model achieved the highest performance, with a micro-averaged F1 score of 63.6%, outperforming all decoder-based models. Among the decoder models, Phi-2 demonstrated the strongest performance (F1 = 54.9%), exceeding both LLaMA2 and GPT2. BERT performed consistently across 6 system-levels but often misclassified “organization” as “person”.DiscussionLLMs hold promise for automating the extraction of CFs from complex safety narratives, particularly for frequently reported system-levels such as “person” and “tasks”. Such automation may substantially reduce the manual effort required to analyse reports of patient safety investigations while supporting more consistent analysis across large incident datasets.ConclusionApplying LLMs to analyse the underlying causes of patient safety incidents depends on developing high-quality, domain-specific datasets that enhance the representation of patient safety knowledge and improve model understanding of incident causation. Improving data coverage for rare system-levels is essential to address the current limitations of LLMs in capturing nuanced patient safety concepts and domain-specific reasoning.

Background and significance

Up to 1 in 8 patients experience preventable harm such as medication errors or surgical complications during their care.1–3 These patient safety incidents account for over USD 600 billion in global health expenditure each year, according to OECD estimates.4 To reduce their frequency and impact, healthcare systems routinely analyse incident reports and undertake investigations to identify contributing factors and develop recommendations for improvement.3,5 Currently, the analysis of incident and investigation data relies heavily on labour-intensive expert reviews, which consume substantial resources, particularly senior staff time.6 In one Australian state alone, this translates to an estimated annual opportunity cost of USD 80 million.7 Despite sustained efforts over the past decade, there is limited evidence that existing incident analysis and investigation methods have resulted in measurable reductions in patient harm.3

Furthermore, digital incident reporting systems have led to a sharp increase in the volume of reports. For example, over 700 000 incidents are reported annually across public health systems in Australia, yet only a few hundred high-harm incidents undergo comprehensive investigation. Consequently, vast amounts of potentially valuable incident data remain underutilized. Improving the efficiency of incident analysis and effectively leveraging these large datasets is therefore critical to generate actionable insights and implement recommendations that can meaningfully enhance patient outcomes.2,3,8

One approach to enhance the efficiency of incident analysis is to leverage machine learning (ML) methods to automatically detect patterns in incident reports and cluster them for in-depth human review.9–11 Prior research has primarily focused on classifying incidents by type and severity, which is critical for large-scale triaging and organization of incident data.12 However, few studies have addressed the investigations of serious harm incidents that aim to understand their underlying causes or contributing factors (CFs).13 This gap likely reflects the inherent complexity of the analysis of incident causation.

Like other safety-critical industries, such as aviation and transportation, patient safety incidents arise from complex interactions within sociotechnical systems.1,14 These systems describe how people and technology interact in workplaces and how these interactions shape outcomes. They operate across multiple levels, from local, organizational to national, and societal influences, requiring a systems-thinking approach that recognizes the interdependencies within the health system.1,3,6 For example, a fatal medication error occurred when penicillin was administered to a patient with a known allergy. Subsequent investigation identified multiple contributing factors: the initial absence of visual alerts in the electronic health record, then a nurse’s hesitancy to question a clinician’s decision, and a lack of pre-defined resuscitation roles that delayed the emergency response. This complex interplay between human behaviour and system-level factors demands extensive domain expertise, making it resource-intensive and far more challenging than the identification of incidents by type or severity.

Recent advancements in large language models (LLMs), pretrained on extensive and diverse corpora, have demonstrated strong capabilities in generating insights across a wide variety of clinical tasks, such as medical question answering and disease diagnosis.15–17 Despite these advances, the application of LLMs to incident causation analysis remains largely unexplored.18,19 Given their generative and continually learning nature, LLMs hold significant potential for identifying contributing factors across multiple system levels. Our previous work demonstrated that the lightweight encoder model BERT outperformed traditional ML models,8 including Support Vector Machines (SVMs),9,10 and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs),11 in identifying 10 primary incident types and 4 severity levels. However, the heterogeneity of linguistic styles in incident investigation reports, combined with the inherent complexity of patient safety concepts, continues to challenge BERT’s ability to generalize effectively. Moreover, the sensitive nature of patient safety data, along with the absence of standardized evaluation benchmarks and resource limitations (eg, unavailable GPUs for finetuning LLMs),20 has further constrained the progress of LLM-based incident analysis.

To address these limitations, we fine-tuned lightweight, open-source foundational LLMs (eg, BERT, LLaMA2, GPT-2, and Phi-2) using gold-standard datasets co-developed with patient safety experts. We then systematically evaluated and compared the performance of LLMs in classifying contributing factors from incident investigation reports, assessing their potential to improve analysis of incident causation.

Method

The classification of contributing factors from incident investigation reports using fine-tuned LLMs was conducted in 3 main steps. First, data were collected from real-world incident investigations and pre-processed to support supervised fine-tuning of the LLMs. Next, we identified LLMs and investigated strategies for fine-tuning.19 Last, the performance of each fine-tuned LLMs was evaluated.1

This study was granted ethics approvals by the Northern Sydney Local Health District (NSLHD) Human Research Ethics Committee (approval number: 2023/ETH02007) and the Queensland Metro North Health Human Research Ethics Committee (approval number: ERM110861).

Step 1: Data collection and pre-processing

A complete list of investigations of incidents rated at the highest level of seriousness from 2022 and 2023 were randomly sampled from the 3 state-level incident reporting systems in Australia, stratified by incident and investigation type. A representative sample of 100 reports per state (Safer Care Victoria [VIC], Clinical Excellence Commission [NSW] and Clinical Excellence Queensland [QLD]), and in total 300 investigation reports from 56 different Australian health services were collected.1

Identifying CFs and system-levels

Each investigation report was examined in detail, and one or more classifications were assigned to each identified CF, based on a predefined classification framework by domain experts. These CF classifications, established in our previous work, were then further deductively grouped at the healthcare system level, encompassing factors including care teams, patient and family, task design, and workload.1 The predefined classification framework integrated Systems Engineering Initiative for Patient Safety (SEIPS) principles with the AcciMap hierarchical structure.1,14,21 Within this framework, AcciMap defines the system-level hierarchy,21 while SEIPS introduces a sociotechnical perspective on the patient journey, specifically tailored for healthcare incident investigation.14 This framework provided 6 system-level elements that constitute any work situation: person, tasks, tool/technology, organization, internal environment, and external environment (Appendix SA). A representative example with corresponding CF classifications is shown in Figure 1. These CF classifications were used in model training and testing, with model performance evaluated at both the system level categories and for individual CF classifications.

CF extracts and expert annotation

The investigation reports are comprehensive, often exceeding 20 pages. To establish a gold-standard benchmark, domain experts systematically extracted the original wording of each CF explicitly implicated in the incident as a contributory factor (Figure 1). Extraction was limited to the contributing factor sections of the full investigation reports. These extracted CF narratives and their assigned system level and CF classification(s) were then combined into a dataset to serve as inputs for model fine-tuning and evaluation. For the coding of CFs, five researchers independently analysed varying portions of the dataset (33-80 reports each), with a maximum of 2 researchers working concurrently at any time.1 To ensure consistency and validity, weekly inter-rater reliability discussions were held, facilitated by a patient safety expert, to resolve ambiguous classifications and achieve consensus. For example, distinguishing organizational policies from local protocols. Coding was guided by a version-controlled codebook containing definitions and rules for classifying CFs, which was iteratively updated based on decisions from these meetings. The combination of expert-guided discussions and an evolving codebook ensured the validity, reproducibility, and methodological rigor of the classification framework, developed in collaboration with patient safety and human factors experts.

Of the 300 incident investigations, 23% (n = 70) of investigations identified no CFs and 43% (n = 130) identified 1 to 3 CFs. In total, 1338 CF classifications were included in this study.1

Pre-processing experimental datasets

Incidents frequently involve multiple CFs. Given the inherent complexity of identifying CFs and the nuanced language used in investigation reports, extracted CF wordings often contain multiple intermingled factors (as shown in Figure 1).1 This poses challenges for accurately representing or distinguishing specific domain definitions, and terminology without clear contextual information. To support effective domain knowledge acquisition by LLMs, CF extracts comprising more than 1 CF classification were reformatted into separate narratives for the training set, with each narrative corresponding to a single CF classification (eg, as highlighted in different colours in the Figure 1 above) to preserve contextual clarity. In contrast, the testing sets retained the original multi-CF classification format to evaluate model performance under realistic conditions.

In total, 852 investigation report extracts and their corresponding single CF classifications were used for model training (Table 1). The remaining 243 extracts, comprising 486 CF classifications, were allocated to validation (20%) and test sets (80%) via stratified random sampling. As shown in Table 1, the distribution of system-level categories was imbalanced. The factors of “care team,” “staff,” and “local procedures and job aids” comprised 57% of all categories, highlighting a substantial skew toward these majority classes. These training, validation, and testing subsets were applied consistently across all models to ensure a fair comparison of performance.

Step 2: Fine-tuning LLMs for identifying CFs

Selected LLMs

Due to the high sensitivity and privacy considerations of the incident investigation reports, we adopted open-source and lightweight LLMs installed on a secure research platform. All computations were performed on a virtual machine equipped with an NVIDIA A100 GPU (80 GB VRAM) and 64 GB of system RAM.

A representative set of widely used encoder and decoder base LLMs, including BERT, LLaMA2 7B, GPT-2, and Phi-2, was evaluated (Table 2). These general-purpose models, pretrained on diverse corpora using self-supervised learning strategies such as masked language modelling and next-word prediction, were selected to represent the typical range of LLM architectures and training paradigms.22 They served as benchmarks to establish baseline performance within the context of patient safety. Additionally, a decoder-based instruction model, LLaMA2-7B-chat was included. It was instruction tuned using high-quality human-annotated datasets for dialogue. However, this initial candidate was excluded from further in-depth evaluation due to suboptimal performance observed during preliminary instruction fine-tuning for this task.

Fine-tuning strategy for classification

We investigated 2 fine-tuning strategies to adapt LLMs for identifying CF classifications: sequence classification and instruction fine-tuning.8,19 In the sequence classification approach, a classification head was appended to a pretrained LLM, following a conventional supervised learning strategy. Both encoder and decoder base LLMs were fine-tuned using this approach. Instruction fine-tuning reframes the classification task as a text-to-text generation problem, offering greater flexibility at the expense of reduced precision.8 It was applied with decoder models both base and chat variants.

Sequence classification

Encoder-based models, such as BERT, are inherently well-suited for sequence classification tasks due to their bidirectional architecture and the presence of a [CLS] token designed for downstream classification.19

Decoder base models are pretrained for next word/token prediction and do not include a classification head. To adapt these models for classification, a linear classification layer is added to map the hidden representations to the target output labels. Classification logits are typically computed from the final token’s hidden state or from pooled representations of the sequence. The models are then optimized using cross-entropy loss.

Instruction fine-tuning

Decoder base models, pretrained solely for next-token prediction, have not been exposed to structured human-style instructions during pretraining. Instruction fine-tuning of these models, therefore, needs the model to understand the instructional prompt as well as map it to the appropriate output.18 This generally requires large volumes of task-specific training data, particularly for tasks that involve nuanced reasoning or multi-step instructions.

Decoder chat models, such as instruction-tuned variants of LLaMA, have undergone additional supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement alignment on human-annotated or synthetic instructional datasets. This pre-alignment allows the model to more naturally interpret prompts and follow instructions with fewer examples, increasing sample efficiency. Conceptually, chat-aligned models are better suited for flexible text-to-text classification tasks, especially in prompt-driven interactions.

Prompt design

For instruction fine-tuning, prompts comprised CF extracts, CF category labels, and brief task instructions, allowing the LLM to utilize its pre-trained knowledge to infer associations between extract content and category labels. The sequence classification approach used only CF extracts as input to predict the corresponding category. To ensure a fair comparison of each LLM’s baseline capability in interpreting patient safety narratives, no domain-specific definitions were incorporated into the instruction prompts. For example, the following system instruction was applied for CF classification:Classify the contributing factors of this report from one of these categories: care team, staff, patient and family, task design, workload, equipment and devices, workspace, local procedures and job aids, societal or external influences, policy and procedures, workforce management, and organization of work.

Classify the contributing factors of this report from one of these categories: care team, staff, patient and family, task design, workload, equipment and devices, workspace, local procedures and job aids, societal or external influences, policy and procedures, workforce management, and organization of work.

For large decoder base and decoder chat models, fine-tuning was performed using the parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) method, quantized low-rank adaptation (QLoRA).8 This approach allows fine-tuning to be conducted on commodity hardware without significant loss of accuracy, as only a small proportion of model parameters is updated during fine-tuning.

Decoder model output processing

LLMs fine-tuned via sequence classification LLMs directly produce pre-defined category labels, whereas instruction fine-tuned models may generate more free-form, creative responses due to the nature of their training. To address this, we post-process the outputs from the instruction fine-tuned models to extract information relevant to the predefined categories and match it accordingly.

The models are capable of generating any of the predefined CF categories. While we initially aimed to tune label-specific thresholds using a validation set, the limited dataset size and the relatively large number of labels (n = 12) made this impractical. Additionally, from a usability perspective, presenting 1 or 2 relevant labels is more effective for supporting manual incident analysis, as additional labels may introduce noise. Therefore, we retain the model’s primary prediction and include a secondary label only if its probability exceeds 0.5.

Step 3: Evaluation

For each CF category, we evaluated and compared LLM performance using label-specific specificity, precision, recall, and F1 score.23 In addition, micro, macro, and weighted-average F1 scores were calculated. Micro-averaging aggregates contributions from all labels and is sensitive to common labels, whereas macro-averaging computes the unweighted mean across labels, treating each equally. In a way, micro scores are dominated by the majority labels, making them especially relevant when the goal is to optimize global accuracy rather than per-class balance. Weighted averaging further adjusts macro-averaging by accounting for label frequency. This method provides a more realistic estimate of performance in imbalanced datasets, ensuring that larger classes exert greater influence on the final score. The same metrics were applied to broader system-levels.

Identifying system-levels

Overall performance

The fine-tuned encoder-based BERT model outperformed all decoder-based LLMs, achieving a micro-averaged F1 score of 63.6% (Table 3). Among the decoder LLMs, Phi-2 performed best, with an average F1 score of 54.9%, exceeding both LLaMA2 and GPT2.

While decoder base models can be adapted through either sequence classification or instruction fine-tuning, the latter consistently achieved better performance across all decoder LLMs (eg, LLAMA2 base: 40.1% vs 52.5%). Instruction fine-tuned LLMs, including LLaMA2 chat, showed particularly strong performance on common system levels (eg, person), resulting in higher micro-average scores. However, the reduction in macro-average indicates weaker performance on less frequent system levels, such as internal environment. Furthermore, decoder chat models were generally unsuitable for this classification task, as their generative outputs were not consistently restricted to the predefined classifications specified in instructions.

Performance on classifying individual system-levels

All LLMs generally tended to show stronger performance on common system-level coding. However, the encoder-based BERT model demonstrated greater consistency across both common and rare system levels. It achieved the highest F1 score on the person category (76.8%), while F1 scores for the remaining factors ranged from 47.7% to 52.2%. The confusion matrix (Figure 2) indicates that BERT misclassified organization factors as more frequent categories, such as “person” and “tool”. For example, one misclassified case stated: “Key staff had not received orientation in relation to key documents such as the Emergency Management Plan and the Business Continuity Plan, therefore impacting recognition that an emergency response was required and also meaning key risk mitigation strategies were not in place at the time of the nurse call outage.” Although the incident appeared to involve “person” at the surface wording level, the underlying contributing factor was organizational, suggesting that BERT lacks sufficient domain-specific understanding of patient safety concepts.

Compared to other decoder LLMs, which showed strong performance primarily on the person factor, Phi-2 demonstrated relatively consistent performance across 4 categories, with F1 scores ranging from 61.0% to 54.3%, except for organization (39.3%) and external environment (13.3%).

Classifying CFs

Across the 3 averaged F1 scores, the encoder BERT model outperformed all decoder models, with the Phi-2 model ranked second (Table 4). For each individual CF classification, their performance varied substantially. For example, in identifying “workload” CF, BERT achieved an F1 score of 60.9%, whereas the other models ranged from 30% to 0. Overall, BERT demonstrated superior performance across most individual CF classifications, with the exception of “organization” factor. In this category, instruction-fine-tuned LLaMA2 base (63.6%), LLaMA2 chat (64.9%), and Phi-2 (61%) outperformed BERT, which achieved an F1 score of 32.9.

Although all LLMs were generally effective at identifying the “person” factor at the system level, they frequently confused its categories. For example, misclassifying “staff” as “care team” or conflating “care team” with “patients, families and carers”. Such misclassifications contributed to lower micro-level F1 scores, as the more frequent person-related categories were often incorrectly predicted among themselves.

For BERT, the confusion matrix revealed that the model frequently misclassified “tool/technology” factors, such as local procedures job aid designed to support care teams or staff workflows, as “person” factors (Figure 3). For example, BERT misclassified a case “Categorising patient symptoms incorrectly against the state-wide rectal bleeding clinical prioritising guidelines” as local job procedures/job aids, whereas the gold standard is staff and procedures. Local procedures and job aids serve as resources to support staff decision-making. However, this misclassification suggested that the model struggled to capture the interaction between staff behaviour and supporting procedural tools within the context-sensitive domain of patient safety. These findings indicated that incorporating additional domain knowledge or applying instruction fine-tuning may enhance model performance.

Discussion

Our findings suggest that while open-source lightweight LLMs show promise, they are not yet ready for comprehensive analysis of incident causation to identify CFs. All models fine-tuned with domain expert annotations demonstrated good performance in categorizing common CFs, such as those related to “person” or “tasks”, but were less effective in detecting rare (eg, “external environment”) or higher-level CFs (eg, “organization”). Even the best-performing model, BERT, achieved an average accuracy of just 64% across 6 system-levels. These results indicate that lightweight LLMs currently lack the conceptual depth required to capture the nuanced nature of CFs in patient safety contexts. Overcoming this limitation will likely require larger, higher-quality, and domain-representative training datasets to further fine-tune the models” ability to differentiate rare and complex factors.

Nature of patient safety data

Given the limited context window of the lightweight LLMs, we used extracted phrases from patient safety investigation reports rather than the full report narratives. While these CF extracts were representative, they often lacked contextual information to fully link narrative descriptions to CF categories.1 This limitation likely constrained the models’ ability to interpret causal relationships embedded within the broader narrative structure. Additionally, the substantial variability in linguistic style across reports,20,24 coupled with the inherent conceptual complexity of the language used in patient safety, posed further challenges for general-purpose LLMs that lack pre-training on safety-specific corpora.

The granularity of the CF taxonomy also introduced difficulties for model interpretation.1 Subtle distinctions, such as “care team” (communication within team), “staff” (individual skills or cognitive performance), and “patient family” (clinical conditions and patient culture), require nuanced understanding beyond surface-level semantics. Model architecture and strategy to fine-tune LLMs

Given the limited computational resources in many patient safety settings where incidents are managed and the need for seamless deployment on portable devices such as tablets or smartphones, small open-source LLMs present a practical solution for developing and implementing models on edge devices. Accordingly, this study initiated an exploration of lightweight LLMs and examined their transferability to patient safety (Table 1).

Although BERT was the smallest among all fine-tuned models, it achieved the best performance. This can be attributed to its encoder architecture, which is inherently optimized for discriminative tasks.8 In contrast, decoder-based LLMs performed slightly worse under sequence classification but generally yield stronger results when fine-tuned through instruction-tuning. This distinction arises from the autoregressive pretraining objective of decoder architectures, which are fundamentally generative rather than discriminative. Instruction-tuning leverages natural language descriptions to model semantic relationships between inputs and outputs, preserving the pretrained distributional characteristics of the model and thereby enhancing its transferability and generalization across tasks.

GPT2, as the smallest decoder-only model with a parameter count comparable to BERT, unsurprisingly exhibited the lowest performance compared to other billion-level decoder family. However, Phi-2 outperformed LLaMA2 on the patient safety classification task despite its smaller size (2.7B vs 7B parameters). This outcome likely reflects the differences in training sources: Phi-2 was trained on a carefully curated corpus emphasizing reasoning and alignment, which enhances performance on tasks requiring structured categorization, such as mapping incident reports to patient safety factors. In contrast, LLaMA2 was pretrained on a broad, general-purpose corpus, providing robust fluency and general language understanding but limiting direct transfer to specialized classification tasks.

Benchmarks

These results suggest that, for domain-specific applications, model curation and alignment may be more critical than raw model size, indicating that well-designed training data can enable smaller models to achieve effective task performance.26 However, the sensitive nature of incident data and associated privacy constraints present significant challenges, as such data are typically stored within local reporting systems managed by individual patient safety units, limiting the creation and sharing of these essential resources. In addition, the dynamic nature of healthcare systems means incident patterns evolve constantly, underscoring the essential need for models that can learn and adapt continuously.

This gap emphasizes the critical need for robust benchmarks and gold standards within the patient safety domain.27,28 Establishing such standards requires rigorously defined concepts, precise terminology, and representative language that accurately capture the multifaceted nature of patient safety incidents.28 For example, the models often struggled to capture the nascent concept of “organization of work” in the patient safety context, which encompasses policies and procedures, as well as workforce management. Instead, these cases were frequently misclassified as contributing factors such as “staff” or “care team,” likely because these more common terms align more closely with the models’ pre-existing knowledge.

Moreover, the effective development and generalizability of LLMs in this field depend on the integration of expert reasoning, domain-specific annotations, and explanatory knowledge, such as sociotechnical hierarchies among CFs.29 This process is inherently complex due to the conceptual intricacies of CFs and variability in expert interpretation and domain expertise.1,6 Despite these challenges, we propose initiating this work by strategically redesigning prompts or integrating retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) with domain-specific knowledge sources to promote alignment with international standards while enabling local adaptation.

Real-world dataset

Ongoing collection of real-world patient safety data will enable benchmarks to evolve dynamically, supporting continuous model learning and refinement. To efficiently expand these benchmarks with minimal expert involvement, automating the extraction of CFs from the investigation reports is essential. However, this task is challenging. Investigation reports often vary in documentation requirements and safety focus across healthcare units and jurisdictions. Further, complex tables and figures used to present specific incident information further complicate the extraction of complete and relevant data from the original reports. Despite these challenges, we intend to advance this work once we achieve greater confidence in reliably identifying CFs from concise CF extracts.

Moreover, to mitigate inherent class imbalance, strategies such as oversampling rare CFs or generating synthetic data can improve representation of infrequent but high-impact factors, such as the “external environment”, thereby enhancing dataset coverage and improving model generalizability across diverse patient safety scenarios.

Limitations and future work

This study has several limitations. First, to ensure that lower-resourced patient safety management units can benefit from advances in LLMs, we focused exclusively on lightweight, open-source models. While newer closed-source models may offer greater performance, their substantial computational and energy requirements could present economic and environmental challenges. Additionally, data security requirements necessitate the use of a secure research platform for LLM deployment, limiting the use of commercial cloud environments. Second, this study fine-tuned models solely for classification tasks. Future research should explore a broader range of applications, such as question answering or incident summarization, to enhance the generalizability of these findings. Third, although the dataset was compiled from 3 health systems, it remains relatively small. The model currently relies on abstract CF extracts manually curated by experts. Automating this extraction process could enable efficient processing of larger-scale, more extensive investigation reports.

Future work will expand data collection and establish high-quality benchmark datasets to strengthen model evaluation.2 Additionally, leveraging more recent general-purpose LLMs, such as Phi-3, Gemma and Qwen, or domain-specific clinical LLMs pre-trained on medical knowledge, such as MedGemma, may further improve domain alignment. Moreover, integrating Retrieval-Augmented Generation with small LLMs and local data could improve their capacity to efficiently incorporate domain knowledge and handle increasing volumes of incident data on-site.

Conclusion

This study explored 2 fine-tuning approaches to classify 12 sociotechnical CFs from patient safety investigations. The lightweight encoder-based BERT model demonstrated strong feasibility for this classification task, highlighting the potential of LLMs to alleviate the manual effort required to identify underlying factors contributing to patient safety incidents. However, model performance was limited by rare class and multi-label complexity suggesting the “conceptual depth” of current models remains a hurdle for identifying rare and complex factors. To enhance model understanding and support practical patient safety analysis, domain expertise and co-design with safety professionals are essential.
