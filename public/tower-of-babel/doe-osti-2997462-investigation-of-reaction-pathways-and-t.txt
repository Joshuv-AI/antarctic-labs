1. Project Overview and Objectives
The overarching goal of this research is to advance the fundamental understanding of plasma-
driven chemical conversion of light hydrocarbons using dielectric barrier discharges (DBDs).
Using methane (CH4) as a model system, the primary focus is to elucidate the influence of DBD
plasma properties and environmental temperature on CH4 plasma chemistry by studying
decomposition products of the gas effluent across a broad range of conditions using experimental
instrumentation at the Princeton Collaborative Research Facility (PCRF) located at the Princeton
Plasma Physics Laboratory (PPPL). The insights obtained from this study are expected to form the
mechanistic foundation for the design and optimization of plasma-catalytic reactions of light
hydrocarbons for practical applications such as the recycling of production flare gas by
transforming the uncaptured waste into value-added resources at the source of extraction,
presenting a sustainable solution to a longstanding environmental challenge. In pursuit of this goal,
we carried out two key Objectives:

Objective 1 – The first objective was to determine decomposition products of the gas effluent of
CH4 plasmas across a range of temperatures. This objective focused on examining the gas-phase
products using Fourier transform infrared absorption spectroscopy (FTIR-AS) at PPPL PCRF
under a range of temperatures. Such measurements can be used to explain the decomposition
products of CH₄ and to gain insight into the underlying chemical reaction mechanisms, as well as
their dependence on temperature.

Objective 21 – The second objective was to investigate and understand CH4 plasma chemistry and
its correlation to plasma properties at elevated temperatures. Building upon Objective 1, this
Objective complements the experimental measurements with computational studies using the
plasma chemistry software ZDPlasKin to identify key reaction pathways that are sensitive to
plasma properties and/or temperature. This, in principle, gives more direct insight into the interplay
between plasma properties, temperature, and the chemical pathways governing CH4 conversion.

This work was complemented by an award from the PCRF2, and we worked with Dr. Sophia
Gershman and her research team at the PCRF to carry out these objectives.

2. Progress and Findings
Experimental research in support of this work was carried out between November and December
2024 by Ibukunoluwa Akintola, a Ph.D. student at the University of Notre Dame, Dr. Jinyu Yang,
a postdoctoral research associate at the University of Notre Dame, and Dr. Sophia Gersham at
PPPL PCRF. The FTIR apparatus at PCRF was modified to accommodate a furnace-heated DBD
reactor, which was supplied by the University of Notre Dame. Figure 1 shows a schematic of the
experimental apparatus. Upon completion of the experiments, the team, led by PI Go, analyzed the
FTIR spectra to interpret them within the context of current understanding of reaction pathways of
plasma-driven CH4 conversion. The team then conducted complementary plasma chemistry
simulations and analysis to understand conversion behavior at a fundamental level. The

Objective 1 – Experiments were carried out to measure the absorption spectra of the gas effluent
from a CH4/Ar DBD plasma (one-to-one gas composition ratio) at furnace temperatures T ranging
from 200 to 700 °C, with the plasma power maintained at approximately 20 W. For each condition,
the short test cell was vacuumed and then filled with gas effluent to a pressure of about 18 Torr to
avoid saturating the FTIR detector. Ten successive scans were collected, and the average was
utilized to identify the products within the gas effluent and characterize their densities through
spectrum fitting. Species present in the effluent were identified by comparison with reference data
from the NIST database. As illustrated in Figure 2(a), the absorption features were typically
concentrated in several wavenumber regions and were dominated by CH₄ absorption. Figures 2(b)
and (c) show FTIR spectra measured at 200 and 700 °C. As expected, the peak intensity of CH4
spectrum at 700 °C is appreciably greater compared to that at 200 °C, indicating the reduction in
the overall CH4 conversion in the gas effluent, which coincided with our prior investigations on
temperature inhibition4. Quantitative extraction of number densities for the measured species was
carried out by fitting the FTIR spectra with a Voigt function using lineshape parameters obtained
from the HITRAN database5.

One of the challenges in analyzing these FTIR spectra was the overlap between the absorption
features of CH4 and C2 species, as shown in Figures 2(b) and (c), which prevented direct
application of Voigt fitting of the experimental data to extract number densities. To overcome this,
a MATLAB-based spectrum analyzer was developed. Figure 3(a) illustrates a flowchart of the
developed algorithm in this work. The analyzer sequentially fits isolated spectra of C2H2 and C2H4
and synthesizes their contributions in spectral regions where overlaps with other species occurs.
These synthesized spectra are then subtracted from the experimental data to enable accurate fitting
of CH4 and, subsequently, C2H6. This procedure is iterated until the extracted number densities
consistently reproduces experimental spectra across all domains. Figure 3(b) shows an example of
the best Voigt fit for the C2H6 spectrum in the region of 3050-2850 cm-1 after subtracting
contributions of other species. The relatively large residual is primarily attributed to the limitations
of the Voigt lineshape profile and incomplete and inaccurate HITRAN databases for CH4 and C2
species.

Figure 4. (a) Extracted number densities of CH₄, C₂H₂, and C₂H₄ in the plasma effluent as a function of
temperature T between 200 and 700 °C. CH₄ remains the dominant species across all measurements, with no
appreciable trend observed as the temperature increases. (b) Relative distribution of C₂ species, defined as the
ratio of the number density of each C₂Hₓ species to the total C₂H2 and C2H4 number density. Results indicate
that the reaction tends to favor C₂H₄ formation over C₂H₂ between 300 and 500 °C, while limited or no data
could be extracted at higher temperatures due to weak absorption features.

The extracted number densities of CH4 and C2 species at each temperature are shown in Figure
4(a). Consistent with the spectral data, CH4 dominates the gas effluent across all temperatures,
with a number density nearly two orders of magnitude greater than that of the products. This is
expected and reflects the nature of CH4 dissociation driven by non-equilibrium DBD plasmas. The
ionization degree of a DBD is ≪1%, therefore the resulting product number densities remain
comparatively low. At 600 and 700 °C, the number densities of C₂H₄ and C₂H₂ could not be
extracted from the FTIR measurements because their absorption features, if present, were too weak
and obscured by background noise. These weak absorption features are likely attributed to the as
mentioned inhibition of CH4 conversion at elevated temperatures. An unexpected observation is
the absence of a clear trend in the number density of CH 4. Previous studies using different

measurement techniques have reported a decrease in CH4 conversion at elevated temperatures6,7,
whereas no clear trend is evident in the present FTIR results. Chemical reaction pathways of CH4
conversion are indirectly illustrated by the distribution of C2 species in Figure 4(b), where the ratio
is defined as the number density of a given C2Hx relative to the total number density of all measured
C2 species. While the results from 300 to 500 °C suggest that the reaction favors the formation of
C=C bonds over C≡C bonds, the lack of data at higher temperatures and the deviation observed at
200 °C make it difficult to draw definitive conclusions regarding CH 4 conversion selectivity.
Further investigation is therefore needed to complement the present study.

Objective 2 – Results of the experimental work were less conclusive because of the stochastic
nature of DBD plasmas at atmospheric pressure, the generation of coke, possible recombination
of reaction products back to CH4, and the inherent challenges of conducting FTIR measurements
and extrapolating spectra. Therefore, the original Objective 2 was slightly revised to place greater
emphasis on ZDPlasKin 8 simulations. These simulations, employed in conjunction with FTIR
measurements, enable a more comprehensive interpretation of the gas-phase products and the
corresponding reaction mechanisms that may influence product formation.

ZDPlasKin is a zero-dimensional plasma kinetic solver that determines the time evolution of
species in non-thermal plasma. To simulate experimental conditions, the reduced electric field E/N
and electron density ne were estimated based on the reactor geometry and experimental electrical
measurements. A total of 201 reactions, including 68 electron-driven and 133 homogeneous
reactions, were used to simulate the CH4/Ar plasma from 200 to 700 ℃. The simulated conversion
of CH4 from 400 to 700 ℃ was compared to previous experimental work [4] and is illustrated in
Figure 5(a), showing the inhibition of CH4 conversion in the plasma at high temperatures. Similar
to Figure 4(b), we extracted the relative ratios of C2H2 and C2H4 to understand the changes to the
distribution of C2 species with temperature. Figure 5(b) shows the simulated density ratio of C2H2
and C2H4 from 200 to 700 ℃. Simulated results show a monotonic decrease in the ratio of C2H2
and a monotonic increase in the ratio of C2H4, suggesting the reaction pathways change from
favoring C≡C bonds to C=C bonds at higher temperatures. While the overall ratios of C2H2 and
C2H4 do not match between simulated results and FTIR measurements, the observed trends in the
simulated results, however, agree with the FTIR measurement trends from 300 to 500℃.

Conclusions – Objective 1 of this research project was achieved, though the results were less
conclusive due to limitations in the experimental approach. We therefore shifted our focus on
Objective 2, where ZDPlasKin simulations were performed in conjunction with FTIR
measurements. While the comparison between simulated and experimental results was
inconclusive, both results show a change in the distribution of product species and a change in the
carbon bonding at higher temperatures. This change in the chemical pathway could be responsible
for the inhibition in CH4 conversion. To explore this further, the research team has begun using
additional tools like graphical network analysis to understand the changes to the chemical reaction
pathways and mechanisms with changing temperature, leading to a manuscript under preparation.
