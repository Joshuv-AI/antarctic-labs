Extradural spinal arteriovenous fistula mimicking schwannoma: Diagnostic pitfalls and surgical implications
Authors: Recai Engin, Orhan Aşkan, Fatih Tomakin, Cem Demirel, Vaner Köksal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpinal arteriovenous fistulas are rare vascular lesions that may present with nonspecific clinical and radiological findings, leading to diagnostic challenges. Extradural arteriovenous fistulas, in particular, can mimic more common spinal tumors such as schwannomas. We report a 37-year-old man who presented with progressive low back pain and left lower extremity numbness. Neurological examination revealed hypoesthesia in the L2-L3 dermatomes without motor deficit. Contrast-enhanced lumbar magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a well-defined, homogeneously enhancing lesion extending from L1-L3 with neural foraminal extension, suggestive of an intradural extramedullary schwannoma. Surgical exploration revealed an extradural vascular lesion with arterialized veins rather than a nerve sheath tumor. The lesion was completely resected following coagulation of feeding vessels, and posterior instrumentation was performed for stabilization. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of an extradural spinal arteriovenous fistula. Postoperative magnetic resonance imaging and digital subtraction angiography demonstrated no evidence of residual arteriovenous shunting. Retrospective review of the preoperative magnetic resonance imaging identified subtle imaging features that may have suggested a vascular etiology. This case highlights an important diagnostic pitfall and emphasizes the need to consider vascular lesions in the differential diagnosis of enhancing spinal masses, particularly when imaging findings are atypical. Introduction

Spinal arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) are rare vascular lesions characterized by abnormal direct shunting between arterial feeders and venous drainage systems within or around the spinal canal. Although the exact prevalence of spinal vascular lesions in the general population remains unclear, they are estimated to account for approximately 3%-4% of all spinal lesions [1]. These lesions may lead to progressive neurological deterioration through venous hypertension and congestion and, less commonly, may present with hemorrhage [2,3]. Clinically, patients may present with acute neurological deficits in cases of bleeding or with slowly progressive myelopathic and radiculopathic symptoms in non-hemorrhagic forms [1].

Several classification systems have been proposed to define spinal vascular lesions according to their anatomical and hemodynamic characteristics. The modified Spetzler classification is widely used and distinguishes arteriovenous fistulas from arteriovenous malformations, with further categorization based on their relationship to the dura and their angioarchitecture [4,5]. Unlike arteriovenous malformations, which contain a nidus, AVFs are characterized by direct arteriovenous shunting without an intervening nidus. Extradural spinal AVFs represent an uncommon subtype and may pose diagnostic challenges because their imaging features can overlap with those of spinal tumors.

Spinal schwannomas, in contrast, are among the most common intradural extramedullary tumors, accounting for approximately 25%-30% of all spinal neoplasms [6]. These benign tumors are typically slow-growing and often present with radicular symptoms. On magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), schwannomas generally appear hypointense on T1-weighted images and hyperintense on T2-weighted images, with homogeneous or heterogeneous contrast enhancement. Because of these clinical and radiological characteristics, schwannomas may resemble other enhancing spinal lesions.

Differentiating spinal vascular lesions from tumoral masses based solely on conventional MRI can be challenging, particularly when typical vascular signs such as serpiginous flow voids or enlarged perimedullary vessels are absent. Misdiagnosis may lead to suboptimal preoperative planning and increased intraoperative bleeding risk. Therefore, careful assessment of atypical imaging findings and consideration of vascular etiologies are important in the differential diagnosis of enhancing spinal masses.

In this report, we present a rare case of an extradural spinal AVF located between the L1 and L3 levels that was initially interpreted as a schwannoma on preoperative MRI. This case highlights a diagnostic pitfall in spinal imaging and emphasizes the need to consider vascular lesions when an enhancing spinal mass demonstrates atypical features.

Case report

A 37-year-old man presented to the outpatient clinic with progressively worsening low back pain over the preceding month, which was unrelated to physical activity. He also reported increasing numbness and subjective weakness in his left lower extremity.

On neurological examination, lumbar movements were painful. The straight leg raise and Lasegue tests were positive on the left side. Motor strength was preserved (5/5) in the left hip flexors and extensors, knee flexors and extensors, and ankle dorsiflexors and plantar flexors. Sensory examination revealed hypoesthesia in the left L2-L3 dermatomal distribution. Deep tendon reflexes were normal, and no sphincter dysfunction was present. The patient had no significant medical or family history.

Laboratory investigations were unremarkable. Contrast-enhanced lumbar MRI demonstrated a well-circumscribed lesion extending from the L1-L3 levels, with neural foraminal extension at the L2 level. The lesion appeared isointense to the spinal cord on noncontrast T1-weighted images, hyperintense on T2-weighted images, and demonstrated homogeneous contrast enhancement. Based on its apparent close relationship with the thecal sac, associated neural compression, signal characteristics, and foraminal extension, the lesion was interpreted by the neuroradiology team as an intradural extramedullary schwannoma (Fig. 1). No obvious flow voids, serpiginous vascular structures, or other imaging findings suggestive of a vascular lesion were identified on routine MRI sequences. Retrospective review of the preoperative MRI suggested that an extradural location may have been more likely than initially appreciated. The lesion demonstrated neural foraminal extension and subtle remodeling of the adjacent vertebral body, findings that were more consistent with a chronic extradural process. However, because of its apparent close relationship to the thecal sac and associated neural compression, the lesion was initially interpreted as an intradural extramedullary schwannoma. Consequently, further vascular imaging, including magnetic resonance angiography or spinal digital subtraction angiography, was not performed preoperatively.

The patient underwent surgical treatment through a posterior midline approach. Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring was used throughout the procedure. A total L2 laminectomy was performed, and the superior and inferior articular processes of the left L2 facet joint were removed to achieve adequate exposure. The dura was not opened because the lesion was found to be entirely extradural. Intraoperatively, an abnormal vascular structure consisting of arterialized veins and a prominent extradural vascular network was encountered, findings that were inconsistent with a schwannoma. Although a discrete fistulous point could not be clearly identified, the vascular nature of the lesion was evident. Intraoperative Doppler ultrasonography or angiography was not utilized.

Microsurgical dissection was performed under magnification, and the feeding vascular channels were coagulated using bipolar cautery before lesion excision. The lesion was completely removed. Estimated intraoperative blood loss was approximately 1000 mL, and the patient received 1 unit of packed red blood cells. Owing to the extent of bony resection, posterior stabilization was performed using pedicle screw instrumentation from L1 to L3 (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). Surgical specimens were submitted for histopathological examination.

The postoperative course was uneventful. Contrast-enhanced MRI obtained on postoperative day 2 demonstrated complete lesion removal without evidence of residual enhancement (Fig. 2). The patient was mobilized with a thoracolumbar brace and discharged on postoperative day 3.

(B) Axial T2-weighted MRI demonstrating the medial facetectomy defect (arrow) with adequate decompression of the spinal canal and neural foramen. (C) Anteroposterior radiograph demonstrating L1-L3 posterior pedicle screw instrumentation.

At the 1-month follow-up, the patient reported marked improvement in symptoms, and no new neurological deficits were identified. Follow-up radiographs confirmed appropriate positioning of the instrumentation. Spinal digital subtraction angiography performed 1 month after surgery included selective catheterization of the bilateral L1-L4 lumbar arteries, bilateral internal iliac arteries, the median sacral artery, and the T12 subcostal artery. No residual arteriovenous shunting or early venous drainage was identified.

Histopathological examination of the surgical specimen confirmed the diagnosis of an extradural spinal arteriovenous fistula at the L2-L3 level. The patient remains under regular clinical and radiological follow-up.

Discussion

Spinal AVFs are uncommon vascular lesions with variable clinical and radiological presentations that may result in diagnostic challenges. Although digital subtraction angiography (DSA) remains the gold standard for diagnosis, these lesions may initially be misinterpreted as spinal tumors, particularly when characteristic imaging findings such as flow voids or serpiginous vessels are absent on routine MRI [1,2]. In the present case, the lesion demonstrated homogeneous contrast enhancement and neural foraminal extension without obvious vascular features, leading to an initial diagnosis of schwannoma.

Schwannomas are among the most common intradural extramedullary tumors and frequently present with progressive radiculopathy and well-defined contrast-enhancing masses on MRI [6]. Extradural spinal AVFs, however, may occasionally mimic these imaging characteristics. In our patient, the lesion was interpreted as an intradural extramedullary schwannoma because of its apparent relationship to the thecal sac, associated neural compression, and foraminal extension. Furthermore, retrospective review of the preoperative MRI suggested that the lesion may have been extradural rather than intradural. Neural foraminal extension and subtle adjacent vertebral body remodeling were more consistent with an extradural process. These findings were underappreciated during the initial evaluation because the lesion demonstrated homogeneous enhancement, appeared closely related to the thecal sac, and produced significant neural compression. This diagnostic discrepancy highlights an important diagnostic pitfall and underscores the value of careful reassessment of imaging findings when intraoperative findings differ from the preoperative diagnosis.

Previous reports have documented several vascular and vascular-like lesions presenting as schwannoma-like masses on imaging studies, including venous angiomas, cavernomas, and epidural vascular malformations [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. A summary of these reported lesions and the imaging features contributing to diagnostic confusion is provided in Table 1. These reports highlight the potential overlap between vascular and neoplastic spinal lesions and emphasize the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis when evaluating enhancing spinal masses.

One of the principal radiological features that may help distinguish schwannomas from vascular lesions is the presence of abnormal vascular architecture. However, these findings may not always be evident on conventional MRI sequences. Advanced vascular imaging techniques, including magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) and spinal DSA, may facilitate preoperative diagnosis in selected cases. Mamaril-Davis et al. [12] emphasized that spinal vascular lesions may occasionally demonstrate atypical imaging characteristics and should therefore be considered in the differential diagnosis of enhancing spinal masses [12]. In retrospect, the lack of preoperative vascular imaging represents a limitation of our case and a key diagnostic pitfall.

The intraoperative identification of a vascular lesion significantly altered surgical management. Unlike schwannomas, which are generally well-circumscribed and relatively avascular, AVFs may be associated with substantial intraoperative bleeding and require meticulous control of feeding and draining vessels. In the present case, the lesion proved to be entirely extradural, the dura did not require opening, and the vascular nature of the lesion became evident only during surgery. The procedure was associated with an estimated blood loss of approximately 1000 mL despite careful microsurgical dissection and bipolar coagulation of feeding vessels. Earlier recognition of a vascular lesion may have influenced preoperative planning and could potentially have allowed consideration of adjunctive endovascular treatment strategies.

From a pathophysiological perspective, spinal AVFs produce abnormal arteriovenous shunting that results in venous hypertension and congestion, ultimately impairing normal spinal cord perfusion [3]. Progressive neurological deterioration may occur if these lesions remain untreated. Consequently, early diagnosis and timely intervention are essential to prevent irreversible neurological injury. Surgical treatment remains an effective therapeutic option, particularly when complete obliteration of the lesion can be achieved.

Recurrence following treatment of spinal vascular lesions is uncommon after complete obliteration but may occur in cases of incomplete treatment. A recent systematic review demonstrated higher recurrence rates following partial treatment, emphasizing the importance of complete lesion elimination whenever feasible [12]. In our patient, postoperative MRI and comprehensive follow-up DSA performed 1 month after surgery demonstrated no evidence of residual arteriovenous shunting or early venous drainage. Nevertheless, longer follow-up is required before durable long-term occlusion can be confirmed.

This case highlights several important clinical lessons. First, extradural spinal AVFs may closely mimic schwannomas both clinically and radiologically. Second, conventional MRI alone may be insufficient to establish the correct diagnosis when typical vascular findings are absent. Finally, MRA or spinal DSA should be considered when a presumed schwannoma demonstrates atypical imaging features or when the diagnosis remains uncertain, as preoperative recognition of a vascular lesion may improve surgical planning and reduce intraoperative risk.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that extradural spinal arteriovenous fistulas may closely mimic schwannomas in both clinical presentation and conventional MRI findings, resulting in potential diagnostic pitfalls. In the absence of typical vascular features, conventional MRI alone may be insufficient to distinguish these lesions from more common spinal tumors. Awareness of this possibility and careful evaluation of atypical imaging findings are essential for appropriate preoperative planning. When diagnostic uncertainty exists, additional vascular imaging with magnetic resonance angiography or spinal digital subtraction angiography should be considered to facilitate accurate diagnosis and reduce intraoperative risk.
