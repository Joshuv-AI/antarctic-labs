Acute outcomes with robotic versus laparoscopic and open hysterectomy for endometrial cancer: a retrospective cohort study from Alberta, Canada
Authors: Huong Luu, Karen JB Martins, Jeanelle Sabourin, Caitlin A Jago, Erica LW Lester, Phuong Uyen Nguyen, Tyler Williamson, Scott W Klarenbach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:We sought to compare real-world outcomes of robotic hysterectomy (RH) with laparoscopic (LH) and open (OH) hysterectomy among adults living with endometrial cancer in Alberta, Canada.Methods:We used administrative data to identify adults living with endometrial cancer who underwent a total hysterectomy (2012 to 2022) in Alberta. We applied generalized linear regression models and adjusted for confounders using propensity score overlap weighting. We presented predicted means and estimated probabilities with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).Results:We included 4555 patients. In adjusted analyses, patients who underwent RH had a shorter length of hospital stay (1.2 [95% CI 1.2 to 1.3] d) than those who underwent LH (2.0 [95% CI 1.9 to 2.1] d) or OH (3.7 [95% CI 3.6 to 3.8] d). Duration of surgery for RH was shorter than LH (172 [95% CI 169 to 174] min v. 186 [95% CI 183 to 189] min), and longer than OH (173 [95% CI 171 to 175] min v. 169 [95% CI 167 to 171] min). Compared with patients who underwent OH, those who underwent RH had reduced probabilities of blood transfusions (2.2% [95% CI 1.3% to 3.9%] v. 6.6% [95% CI 4.8% to 9.0%]), perioperative complications (12.7% [95% CI 10.2% to 15.8%] v. 20.5% [95% CI 17.3% to 24.0%]), infections (16.5% [95% CI 13.6% to 19.8%] v. 33.8% [95% CI 30.0% to 37.9%]), and 30-day acute care readmissions (11.7% [95% CI 9.2% to 14.6%] v. 17.9% [95% CI 14.9% to 21.3]); no differences were observed in comparisons with LH.Conclusion:Among adults living with endometrial cancer in Alberta, RH was associated with a shorter length of hospital stay than LH and OH; we also noted fewer blood transfusions, complications, infections, and readmissions for RH compared with OH but not LH. Findings can be used in the context of surgical volumes and outcomes, along with other considerations, to guide decisions.

Endometrial cancer is the sixth most common cancer among females, and the most prevalent gynecological cancer in high-income countries, including Canada.1,2 Rates of endometrial cancer are rising globally; in North America, the age-standardized incidence rate has increased from 35.8 cases per 100 000 people in 1990 to 86.6 cases per 100 000 people in 2019, reflecting the upsurge in the prevalence of key risk factors, particularly obesity, and an aging population.3

Most cases of endometrial cancer are diagnosed at an early stage, and total hysterectomy (removal of the uterus and cervix) with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and, often, lymph node assessment is the mainstay of initial management.2 Historically, hysterectomy in gynecological oncology has been performed using an open abdominal approach (laparotomy), but minimally invasive techniques such as conventional and robot-assisted laparoscopic approaches are increasingly being used.

Robotic surgery is a relatively new minimally invasive technology that was developed to assist surgeons by providing improved visualization, greater precision, more accurate control of instrumentation, and improved ergonomics. 4 There are, however, disadvantages to this surgical approach, including increased costs (acquisition and maintenance) and loss of tactile feedback.5 Studies have shown that both robotic hysterectomy (RH) and conventional laparoscopic hysterectomy (LH) provide clinical benefit for the management of endometrial cancer compared with open hysterectomy (OH), including decreased blood loss or transfusions, perioperative complications, length of hospital stay, and readmissions.6–9 The surgical safety and clinical effectiveness of RH and LH appears comparable across most studies, and patients who undergo RH are consistently reported to have a shorter length of hospital stay than those who undergo LH.10–13 Few studies comparing these surgical approaches for total hysterectomy have been conducted in Canada.14–16 The objective of this study was to evaluate real-world acute health and health system outcomes of RH compared with LH and OH among patients living with endometrial cancer in Alberta, Canada.

Methods

Data custodian approvals were received from Alberta Health and Alberta Health Services (the single health authority in the province) for the use of administrative health data for this study. We reported this study according to the Reporting of Studies Conducted Using Observational Routinely Collected Health Data (RECORD) statement.17

Study design

We conducted this retrospective, observational, population-based cohort study using administrative health data from Alberta from Apr. 1, 2007, to Apr. 30, 2022; the inclusion period was Apr. 1, 2012, to Mar. 31, 2022. We established the index date as the surgery date for the total hysterectomy during the inclusion period. We determined baseline characteristics during the 5-year period before the index date and measured postoperative outcomes during the 30-day postindex period.

Data source

Canadian provinces provide publicly funded health care for all residents. In Alberta, the fourth most populous province in Canada (3.9 to 4.5 million people between 2012 and 2022), health care is administered under the Alberta Health Care Insurance Plan (AHCIP), in which more than 99% of residents participate.18,19 We obtained person-level data from several databases (Discharge Abstract Database [DAD]; National Ambulatory Care Reporting System [NACRS]; Cancer Control Alberta; Surgery Database; Practitioner Claims; Drug Optimization, Sustainability, and Evaluation [DOSE]; Pharmaceutical Information Network [PIN]; Vital Statistics), which were linked to the Population Registry. This registry contains demographic information for all residents with AHCIP coverage; elements include migration in and out of the province, and birth and death indicators. Data were linked using a unique individual identifier (personal health number), then deidentified and provided to the researchers by the data custodians. The DAD contains information on recipients, services, diagnoses, and procedure interventions for patients who have been discharged from a hospital inpatient setting; fields for most responsible and secondary diagnoses are available and use codes from the Canadian version of the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10-CA) and the Canadian Classification of Health Interventions. The NACRS includes information on all patients discharged from facility-based ambulatory care clinics, including emergency departments; ICD-10-CA codes are used. Cancer Control Alberta collects information on demographics, tumour characteristics (including cancer staging), and primary treatment for all patients who received treatment at the time of their initial, confirmed cancer diagnosis. The Surgery Database contains surgery-specific information such as surgery triage level, predicted perioperative risk score, operating time, and surgeon. The Practitioner Claims database includes information on physician billing; up to 3 diagnostic codes from the Alberta-specific version of the ninth revision of the ICD (clinical modification) can be listed per visit. The DOSE database houses medication information from all hospital pharmacies. Information on community-dispensed prescription medications from pharmacies is contained within the PIN. We obtained information on deaths from Vital Statistics. Records that were duplicates or contained an invalid personal health number were discarded by the data custodians. We checked variables for missing data and inconsistencies; inconsistent data were corrected using data logic or information majority.

Cohort selection

We included adult (age ≥ 18 yr) residents of Alberta living with endometrial cancer who had a total hysterectomy between Apr. 1, 2012, and Mar. 31, 2022. Specifically, we included those who underwent a total hysterectomy (LH [1.RM.89.AA or DA] or OH [1.RM.89.LA] code in any procedure code field within the DAD) between Apr. 1, 2012, and Mar. 31, 2022, with a diagnosis of endometrial cancer (ICD-10-CA code C54.1 in the primary diagnostic field in the DAD or Alberta Cancer Registry, not including in situ carcinoma or cancer stage 0) within 5 years before or on the index date. Eligible patients were aged 18 years or older on the index date and had AHCIP coverage at least 5 years before the index date, and at least 30 days after the index date or until death, whichever occurred first. The investigational cohort included those who underwent RH (7.SF.14 in any procedure code field within the same total hysterectomy episode in the DAD); the comparison cohorts included those who underwent LH or OH with no associated code indicating a robotic surgical approach.

Study variables

Demographic characteristics recorded on the index date included age and residence (urban or rural; determined by the second digit of the postal code). We determined socioeconomic status using Pampalon’s material deprivation index (MDI).20 This index uses measures of education, employment status, and average income derived from Canadian census data at the level of the dissemination area, linkable to postal codes. It is presented by quintile of the Alberta general population from least (quintile 1) to most deprived (quintile 5).

Clinical characteristics included the Charlson Comorbidity Index (Appendix 1, Supplementary Table S1, available at www.canjsurg.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cjs.001725/tab-related-content),21,22 health conditions that could affect the choice of surgical approach or increase the risk of perioperative complications (Appendix 1, Supplementary Table S2), previous abdominal surgery (Appendix 1, Supplementary Table S2), cancer stage and histologic type,23–25 whether total hysterectomy was the initial endometrial cancer treatment, other interventions during the index surgery, triage level, predicted perioperative risk,26,27 annual hysterectomy volume by surgeon and hospital, implementation of the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery protocol at the hospital during the index surgery year, and year of the index surgery.28,29

Outcomes included the total duration of surgery, postoperative recovery time, length of hospital stay, perioperative blood transfusion, perioperative complications (overall, major, minor),30 infection (during hospital stay or < 30 d after index surgery), and 30-day acute care (emergency department or hospitalization) readmission (all-cause and postoperative complications) (Appendix 1, Supplementary Table S3).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics included counts and percentages or means and standard deviations (SDs), where applicable. We employed propensity score weighting to balance covariate distributions across patients who underwent RH, LH, or OH. We estimated the propensity scores of RH compared with LH or OH using multivariable logistic regression models, where RH was predicted by age, residence, MDI, Charlson Comorbidity Index, health conditions (cardiovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes, hypertension, mental disorders, and obesity), previous abdominal surgery, oncologic factors (cancer stage and histologic type), other interventions during the index surgery, hospital factors (annual site volume, implementation of the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery protocol), and year of the index surgery (which included accounting for the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic). We assessed the standardized mean differences for these covariates across those who underwent RH, LH, and OH before and after applying 2 weighting methods (inverse probability of treatment weights [stabilized] and overlap weights).31 We selected overlap weights as the final weighting method because it achieved exact mean balance of the covariates by emphasizing the part of the study population where the characteristics overlapped the most, leading to more accurate comparisons. We then used generalized linear regression models with overlap weights to examine relative differences in outcomes between RH and LH or OH. For count outcomes (such as length of hospital stay), we conducted generalized linear regressions with a negative binomial distribution and a log link function to estimate mean ratios, means, and mean differences, along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For binary or multinomial outcomes (such as blood transfusion [yes or no] or complications [major, minor, or none]), we applied logistic regression or multinomial logistic regression to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and probabilities of the outcomes with 95% CIs.

We performed a subgroup analysis including patients whose index hysterectomy was between Apr. 1, 2015, and Mar. 31, 2022; this allowed for the use of information from the Surgical Database to be incorporated into the analysis, including postoperative recovery time as an additional outcome, as well as surgery triage level, predicted perioperative risk score, and surgeon volume in the index year as additional covariates in the propensity score estimation.

In accordance with data custodian privacy standards, outcomes with 1 to 9 patients were reported as less than 10 (associated proportions were presented rounded to the nearest 5); where applicable, other outcomes were censored (e.g., presented as a range; associated proportions were presented by the middle number of the range) so the number of patients could not be calculated and identified.32 We applied a 2-sided significance level of 0.05 for all statistical tests. We performed analyses using SAS (version 9.4).

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the University of Alberta Research Ethics Board (no. Pro00123337) and granted an exemption from requiring written informed consent; a waiver of consent was applied.

Results

Cohort selection

Among 5011 adults living with endometrial cancer who underwent a total hysterectomy between Apr. 1, 2012, and Mar. 31, 2022, across all 25 hospitals in the province in which total hysterectomies were conducted during the study inclusion period, we included 4555 patients in the study population; 839 underwent RH, 1306 underwent LH, and 2410 underwent OH (Figure 1 and Appendix 1, Supplementary Figure S1).

Baseline characteristics

The mean age of the study population was 63 (SD 11) years, and most patients lived in urban areas (83.9%) (Table 1). The study population was less likely to be materially well off than the Alberta general population (MDI 1: 17.9% v. 20.0%; MDI 2: 17.6% v. 20.0%). Patients who underwent RH (20.4%) and OH (21.2%) were more likely to have a moderate-to-severe overall health status (Charlson Comorbidity Index score ≥ 3) than those who underwent LH (12.9%) (Table 2). The most common (> 10%) health conditions of interest in the study population were hypertension (47.8%), mental disorders (27.8%), diabetes (21.4%), obesity (20.7%), and cardiovascular disease (13.1%) (Table 2). Those who underwent RH had a higher proportion of patients living with each health condition of interest than those who underwent LH or OH, particularly obesity (Table 2). Most of the study population was classified as having stage IA disease (54.6%); 94.8% underwent a total hysterectomy as initial treatment, and 99.3% had related structures removed (ovary, fallopian tube, lymph node, or a combination) (Table 2). Discussion

In this retrospective, observational, population-based cohort study of adults who were living with endometrial cancer and underwent a total hysterectomy between 2012 and 2022 in Alberta, RH was associated with health system benefits compared with LH and OH, allowing for a shorter length of hospital stay. The total duration of surgery for RH was 15 minutes shorter than for LH and 5 minutes longer than for OH. A robotic approach was also associated with health outcome benefits compared with OH, including a lower probability of blood transfusion, perioperative complication, infection, or 30-day acute care readmission; these outcomes were not significantly different between RH and LH.

In the literature, RH is consistently reported to have a shorter length of hospital stay than LH and OH.10–13 Meta-analyses from randomized controlled trials (RCTs)11 and cohort studies10,13 have found that the length of hospital stay for people who underwent RH was between 0.2 and 0.6 days shorter than LH and almost 3 days shorter than OH.10,13 Findings from the current study are similar to these previous reports, with a length of hospital stay 0.7 days shorter with RH than LH and 2.5 days shorter than OH.

Regarding duration of surgery, studies have consistently indicated that RH has a longer duration of surgery than OH; findings are equivocal in comparisons with LH.10–13,33,34 Two systematic reviews of studies conducted from 2009 to 2014 and 2016 to 2021 reported that 8 cohort studies showed a longer duration of surgery for RH than LH, 8 studies (5 cohort, 2 case-control, 1 RCT) showed a shorter duration for RH, and 3 studies (2 cohort, 1 RCT) showed no difference;12,34 meta-analyses of RCTs11 and cohort studies10,13 found no statistically significant difference between RH and LH. A single-centre RCT of patients who underwent surgery for endometrial cancer found that, while the mean operating time for RH was significantly shorter than for LH (141 [SD 26] v. 178 [SD 32] min), the prespecified difference of 25% was not achieved.35 In our study, after controlling for confounding factors, the total duration of surgery for RH was 8% (cohort analysis) to 9% (subgroup analysis) shorter than for LH; the clinical importance and magnitude of impact of this finding on the health system is not clear.

Minimally invasive surgical approaches for hysterectomy have shown health benefits compared with OH, including reduced blood loss and fewer transfusions, perioperative complications, and readmissions.6–9 Results of a systematic review and meta-analysis that compared the safety and effectiveness of RH with LH (23 cohort studies, 1 non-RCT; RH: n = 1773, LH: n = 1982) and OH (24 cohort studies; RH: n = 1528, OH: n = 1983) for endometrial cancer found that RH had a lower risk of blood transfusions (relative risk [RR] 0.28, 95% CI 0.19 to 0.40]), perioperative complications (RR 0.37, 95% CI 0.29 to 0.49]), and readmissions (RR 0.48, 95% CI 0.30 to 0.77) than OH, while these measures were equivalent between RH and LH.13 We observed similar results in this real-world evidence study in Alberta, increasing confidence in these findings.

Limitations

As this study did not use detailed medical records, there is potential for misclassification of the cohort or measures; we used validated case definitions where available to address this limitation. It was not possible to ascertain whether the choice of surgical modality reflected access-related factors (e.g., availability of robot-assisted surgery) or individual and clinical decision-making, as this information is not contained within administrative data. To improve comparability between modalities, we accounted for potential confounders observable in administrative data using rigorous statistical methods. Nonetheless, residual confounding may still be present (e.g., anatomic complexity, individual and clinical considerations).

Conclusion

These findings contribute to the growing literature comparing RH with LH or OH for endometrial cancer. We observed that RH was associated with a shorter length of hospital stay than LH and OH. Additionally, RH was associated with fewer blood transfusions, complications, infections, and 30-day acute care readmissions than OH; these measures were similar in comparisons of RH with LH. Collectively, these findings can be used to guide decisions to optimize health care system outcomes, patient care, and patient outcomes. Access to robotic platforms may enable minimally invasive surgical approaches in patients who might otherwise undergo open surgery. Limited availability of such technology could constrain use of minimally invasive surgical approaches and contribute to disparities in surgical care.
