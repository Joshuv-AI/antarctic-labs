Diagnostic uncertainty and addiction-like reassurance seeking in patients with pulmonary nodules: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Shangqing Xu, Yan Sun, Xiaoyan Han, Kexin Ma, Yu Gu, Hongying Jin, Xiaoli Ma, Minjie Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLow-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening and the increasing use of chest computed tomography (CT) have created a large population of patients living with indeterminate pulmonary nodules (PNs) and diagnostic uncertainty. This study aimed to develop and psychometrically evaluate the Pulmonary Nodule Addiction-like Behavior Scale (PNABS) and to identify addiction-like reassurance-seeking profiles among patients with PNs across diagnostic conditions.MethodsThis cross-sectional study enrolled 1,800 adults with CT-detected PNs (4–30 mm) at a tertiary thoracic surgery center from January 2021 to December 2024. The cohort was a mixed tertiary-care PN cohort and was not restricted to either lung cancer screening-detected nodules or incidental PNs. Participants were sampled across three diagnostic conditions: preoperative without definitive pathology (n=900), postoperative benign/pre-invasive pathology (n=450), and postoperative malignant pathology (n=450). PNABS psychometric properties were evaluated using split-half exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses. Latent profile analysis identified behavioral profiles, network analysis examined conditional associations with psychological and functional measures, and hierarchical regression tested whether PNABS explained 6-month health care utilization beyond generalized anxiety.ResultsAmong 1,800 participants {mean [standard deviation (SD)] age, 55.21 (11.37) years; 920 (51.1%) female}, the PNABS showed excellent internal consistency and factorial validity [Cronbach alpha =0.947; confirmatory factor analysis (CFA): Comparative Fit Index (CFI) =0.962, Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) =0.958, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) =0.042]. Latent profile analysis supported three profiles: Adaptive, Distressed, and Compulsive. The Compulsive profile was most common in the preoperative group (47.0%) and least common in the postoperative benign/pre-invasive group (6.2%). Participants in the Compulsive profile (536/1,800, 29.8%) accounted for 41.9% of CT examinations and 43.6% of outpatient visits over 6 months. Adding PNABS to GAD-7 increased explained variance for CT examinations, outpatient visits, and excessive provider switching by 0.239–0.253 (all P<0.001).ConclusionsAmong patients with PNs, diagnostic uncertainty was associated with difficult-to-regulate reassurance seeking, particularly before definitive pathology was available. The PNABS demonstrated promising psychometric performance and captured behaviorally specific variance in health care utilization beyond general anxiety symptoms. Surveillance pathways may benefit from early communication support and behavioral triage for patients showing compulsive reassurance-seeking patterns.

Highlight box

Key findings

• The Pulmonary Nodule Addiction-like Behavior Scale (PNABS) demonstrated strong internal consistency and factorial validity in a large cross-sectional pulmonary nodule (PN) cohort.

• Three reassurance-seeking profiles were identified; the Compulsive profile was enriched in the preoperative diagnostic-uncertainty period and was associated with disproportionate 6-month health care utilization.

What is known and what is new?

• Patients with PNs frequently experience anxiety, distress, and uncertainty during surveillance; recent qualitative and prospective studies emphasize the importance of communication and psychological support.

• This study extends prior work by operationalizing addiction-like reassurance seeking as a behavior-focused construct and by showing that PNABS adds explanatory value beyond general anxiety.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• PN surveillance workflows should distinguish adaptive information seeking from compulsive reassurance seeking and incorporate early, structured communication and behavioral support for high-risk patients.

Introduction

Low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening reduces lung cancer mortality and has increased the detection of indeterminate pulmonary nodules (PNs) (1,2). In parallel, widespread chest computed tomography (CT) use in routine clinical care has increased the identification of incidental PNs (3). These two pathways are clinically distinct: screening-detected nodules are commonly managed within Lung-RADS-based screening programs, whereas incidentally detected nodules are often managed according to Fleischner Society recommendations or other nodule guidelines (4-6). Both pathways, however, can expose patients to prolonged diagnostic uncertainty and repeated surveillance (2).

The psychological consequences of PN detection are increasingly recognized. Prior studies have described distress, lung cancer worry, diminished quality of life, and uncertainty during PN surveillance (2,7). Recent studies further emphasize that communication about nodules and surveillance pathways can shape affective, cognitive, and behavioral responses, and that surgical diagnosis does not necessarily eliminate longer-term psychological distress (2,8).

Most prior work has conceptualized these responses through anxiety, depression, or health-related worry (7). These constructs are important but may not fully capture repetitive reassurance-seeking behaviors, including persistent online searching, repeated low-yield consultations, symptom checking, and difficulty stopping reassurance-seeking even when prior information has been reassuring (9,10). These behaviors overlap with cyberchondria and health anxiety, but the emphasis here is behavioral dysregulation rather than a formal addiction diagnosis (11).

We use the term addiction-like descriptively to indicate impaired control, salience, continuation despite diminishing returns or functional costs, and tolerance- or withdrawal-like experiences when reassurance is unavailable (12). This framing is consistent with behavioral-addiction discussions and with the I-PACE model, which links person-level vulnerabilities, affective and cognitive responses to triggers, and executive-control processes in the maintenance of repetitive behaviors (13). In patients with PNs, diagnostic uncertainty may act as a trigger that temporarily increases reassurance seeking; repeated short-term relief may then reinforce further checking and consultation (2).

Despite the clinical relevance of this behavior, no condition-specific instrument has been validated to measure addiction-like reassurance seeking in PN management (7). Furthermore, it remains unclear whether patients with PNs show distinct reassurance-seeking profiles and whether such profiles are associated with health care utilization beyond general anxiety symptoms (2). This study therefore aimed to: (I) develop and psychometrically evaluate the Pulmonary Nodule Addiction-like Behavior Scale (PNABS); (II) identify latent profiles of addiction-like reassurance-seeking behaviors among patients with PNs across diagnostic conditions; and (III) examine whether PNABS and its profiles are associated with psychological burden and 6-month health care utilization beyond general anxiety. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0653/rc).

Methods

Study design and participants

This observational cross-sectional study enrolled adults (age ≥18 years) with CT-detected PNs measuring 4–30 mm between January 2021 and December 2024 at a tertiary thoracic surgery center. The study population was a mixed tertiary-care PN cohort: patients could have been referred after lung cancer screening LDCT, routine health-check CT, or incidental CT performed for other indications. The study was not designed as a pure screening cohort or a pure incidental-PN cohort. This design was chosen because, in routine tertiary thoracic surgery practice, both pathways converge when patients require diagnostic counseling, surveillance planning, or surgical decision-making.

Participants were enrolled using a stratified sampling strategy to ensure adequate statistical power across diagnostic conditions (target enrollment ratio: 2:1:1 for preoperative, postoperative benign/pre-invasive, and postoperative malignant groups). Participants were recruited from 3 diagnostic conditions: (I) preoperative patients undergoing surveillance or awaiting a surgical decision without definitive pathology; (II) postoperative patients with benign/pre-invasive pathology; and (III) postoperative patients with malignant pathology.

Exclusion criteria were cognitive impairment precluding informed consent or reliable questionnaire completion, current unstable psychiatric conditions requiring active acute treatment, history of substance use disorder, and concurrent malignancy other than lung cancer. Stable depression or anxiety under maintenance treatment was not an automatic exclusion unless symptoms or treatment changes were judged likely to preclude informed consent or substantially invalidate self-report assessment. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Lanzhou University (approval No. LDYYLL2026-59), and informed consent was obtained from all individual participants.

Detection context, timing, and diagnostic conditions

This study included patients with CT-detected PNs regardless of detection source; therefore, detection source was not used as an eligibility restriction. Referral source was recorded in clinical notes but was not consistently available as a structured analytic variable in the deidentified dataset; therefore, primary analyses were stratified by diagnostic condition rather than by screening versus incidental origin. Similarly, time from first CT detection to study enrollment and time from surgery to study enrollment were available for most participants and were summarized descriptively. However, because these timing variables were not prespecified primary analytic variables and were incomplete for a subset of participants, they were not included as primary covariates in the main adjusted models.

Measures

The PNABS was developed to operationalize addiction-like reassurance seeking during PN surveillance. Item generation was based on a targeted literature review on health anxiety, reassurance seeking, cyberchondria, scan-related anxiety, and uncertainty in illness; clinical observations from PN counseling and surveillance visits; and conceptual mapping to behavioral-addiction constructs including salience, impaired control, persistence despite costs, and distress or functional impairment when reassurance is unavailable (14,15). A multidisciplinary panel reviewed items for clarity, clinical relevance, cultural portability, and avoidance of platform-specific wording. Items were refined to apply across preoperative and postoperative diagnostic conditions.

The final PNABS contains 20 items across 6 dimensions: compulsive information searching, excessive medical consultation, symptom checking, social media dependence for health information, decision-making difficulty, and loss of control and impairment. Each item is rated from 1 (never) to 5 (almost always), yielding a total score from 20 to 100, with higher scores indicating more difficult-to-regulate reassurance seeking. The full PNABS item set and scoring instructions are provided in Appendix 1.

Anxiety and depression symptoms were assessed using the GAD-7 and PHQ-9 (16,17), respectively. Sleep quality was assessed using the PSQI (18), perceived stress using the PSS-10 (19), and health-related quality of life using the EQ-5D (20).

Variables

The primary exposure was diagnostic condition at assessment, categorized as preoperative, postoperative benign/pre-invasive, and postoperative malignant. The primary outcomes were PNABS total score, PNABS profile membership, and 6-month health care utilization indicators, including CT examinations, outpatient visits, and excessive provider switching [the “excessive provider switching” was operationally defined as seeking consultation from ≥3 different physicians or medical institutions for PN-related concerns within a 6-month period despite the absence of major interval clinical changes, adapted conceptually from the “doctor-shopping” literature (21)]. Candidate predictors and potential confounders included age, sex, educational level, nodule size, and diagnostic conditions. Psychological correlates included anxiety (GAD-7), depression (PHQ-9), perceived stress (PSS-10), sleep quality (PSQI), and health-related quality of life (EQ-5D).

All questionnaire-based measures were collected using a standardized assessment procedure and identical instruments across the 3 diagnostic-condition groups, which was intended to enhance comparability of assessment.

Bias

Several steps were taken to reduce potential bias. Stratified recruitment was used to ensure representation across diagnostic conditions. Eligibility criteria were applied uniformly, and all participants completed the same standardized assessments. In adjusted analyses, age, sex, educational level, nodule size, and diagnostic condition were included as covariates to reduce potential confounding. Nevertheless, because some outcomes may be influenced by self-report, reporting bias, recall bias, and residual confounding cannot be fully excluded.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize study variables. Descriptive group comparisons used one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. Analysis of covariance adjusted for age was used only for prespecified outcome comparisons where appropriate. Psychometric validation was performed on split-half samples. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) using principal axis factoring was conducted on the derivation sample (n=900) to examine the factorial structure. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted on the validation sample (n=900) using maximum likelihood estimation. Model fit was evaluated using the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA).

Latent profile analysis used PNABS dimension scores as indicators; models with 1 to 4 profiles were compared using information criteria, entropy, class size, interpretability, and the Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test. Network analysis estimated a regularized Gaussian graphical model using the graphical lasso and EBIC model selection to model conditional associations among PNABS, GAD-7, PHQ-9, PSS-10, PSQI, and EQ-5D. Centrality and bridge centrality were estimated with nonparametric bootstrap procedures. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses evaluated PNABS discrimination for clinically significant anxiety (GAD-7 ≥10), depression (PHQ-9 ≥10), and the combined endpoint. Analyses were conducted in R version 4.2.0. All tests were 2-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To evaluate incremental validity, we conducted 2-step hierarchical regression models for each 6-month utilization outcome: CT examinations, outpatient visits, and excessive provider switching. Step 1 included GAD-7 only; step 2 added PNABS total score. We report R-squared, change in R-squared, F-change tests, standardized coefficients, and unique and shared explained variance. Directional mediation models were estimated using nonparametric bootstrap confidence intervals (CIs) with 2,000 resamples. Sensitivity analyses stratified by nodule size used a threshold of 12 mm because this approximated the cohort median and was intended as an exploratory data-driven sensitivity cut point, not as a guideline-based risk threshold.

There were no missing data for the variables included in the primary analyses; therefore, no imputation procedures were required.

Results

Participant characteristics

During the study period, 2,146 patients with PNs were screened for eligibility. Of these, 346 were excluded, including 188 who did not meet the inclusion criteria, 74 with exclusion criteria, 52 who declined participation, and 32 with incomplete assessments. A total of 1,800 participants were included in the final analyses, comprising 900 preoperative patients, 450 postoperative benign/pre-invasive patients, and 450 postoperative malignant patients. The mean [standard deviation (SD)] age of the 1,800 participants was 55.21 (11.37) years, and 920 participants (51.1%) were female. The median [interquartile range (IQR)] time from the first CT scan showing a PN to study enrollment was 31 [14–78] days overall. Among postoperative participants, the median [IQR] time from operation to study enrollment was 28 [16–45] days, with comparable intervals in the postoperative benign/pre-invasive group {29 [17–46] days} and postoperative malignant group {28 [16–44] days}. Across diagnostic conditions, significant differences were observed in age (P<0.001), nodule size (P<0.001), and nodule type (P<0.001), whereas sex (P=0.60) and educational level (P=0.96) did not differ significantly. The “postop benign/pre-invasive” group includes benign non-neoplastic lesions (e.g., hamartoma, inflammation) and precursor glandular lesions [specifically atypical adenomatous hyperplasia (AAH) and adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS)]. Consistent with the 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Thoracic Tumours, AIS is classified as a precursor lesion with 100% disease-specific survival after resection, justifying its inclusion in the favorable prognosis group alongside benign pathologies.

Psychometric evaluation of PNABS

Before latent profile analysis, the psychometric properties of the PNABS were evaluated. EFA in a split-half derivation sample (n=900) supported the prespecified 6-dimension structure. CFA in the validation sample (n=900) demonstrated excellent model fit (CFI =0.962, TLI =0.958, RMSEA =0.042, SRMR =0.035) and strong standardized loadings across items. Internal consistency was high (Cronbach α=0.947).

Latent profile analysis

Latent profile analysis supported a 3-profile solution (Table S1 in Appendix 2). The standardized PNABS dimension patterns of the Adaptive, Distressed, and Compulsive profiles are shown in Figure 2.

Group differences in PNABS

The distribution of latent profiles differed significantly across diagnostic conditions, with the Compulsive profile most frequent in the preoperative group and least frequent in the postoperative benign/pre-invasive group (Figure 3). The PNABS total scores demonstrated substantial variation across diagnostic conditions (η2=0.316). The preoperative group exhibited the highest addiction-like behavior scores [mean (SD), 63.66 (12.24)].

Distribution of latent profiles by diagnostic condition. Stacked bars show the percentage of participants classified into each latent profile within the 3 diagnostic-condition groups: preoperative, postoperative benign/pre-invasive, and postoperative malignant. P value was derived from the Pearson Chi-squared test comparing profile distribution across diagnostic conditions.

Compared with the preoperative group, PNABS scores were significantly lower in the postoperative malignant group [mean difference (MD), −12.41; 95% CI −13.98 to −10.84; P<0.001] and the postoperative benign/pre-invasive group (MD, −19.13; 95% CI: −20.68 to −17.58; P<0.001).

Notably, patients with malignant pathology maintained significantly higher PNABS scores than those with benign pathology even after surgery (MD, 6.72; 95% CI: 5.15 to 8.29; P<0.001), suggesting that diagnostic resolution does not fully alleviate behavioral maladaptation.

Network structure

In the partial correlation network (Figure S1), PNABS showed moderate conditional associations with perceived stress (partial r≈0.31), anxiety (partial r≈0.29), depression (partial r≈0.29), and quality of life (partial r≈−0.19). PNABS had the highest strength centrality (strength =1.28), followed by perceived stress (0.83) and depression (0.78). Bridge strength suggested that PNABS and quality of life connected distress-related measures to functional outcomes. Centrality estimates were stable under case-dropping bootstrap procedures, with a correlation stability coefficient (CS-coefficient) for strength centrality of 0.52, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.25.

Exploratory screening performance

ROC analyses showed that PNABS discriminated clinically significant anxiety and depression symptoms with high accuracy (Table S2 and Figure S2). For the combined endpoint (GAD-7 ≥10 or PHQ-9 ≥10), the area under the curve (AUC) was 0.910 (95% CI: 0.894–0.923), with an optimal cut-off of approximately 52 by the Youden index.

Predictors of PNABS total score

In multivariable linear regression adjusting for age, sex, education, and nodule size, diagnostic condition was the strongest independent predictor of PNABS score (Figure 4). Compared with postoperative benign patients, PNABS scores were higher in preoperative patients (B=19.17, 95% CI: 17.77–20.56; P<0.001) and in postoperative malignant patients (B=6.83, 95% CI: 5.15–8.51; P<0.001), whereas nodule size was not independently associated with PNABS (P=0.55).

Sensitivity analysis by nodule size

In sensitivity analyses stratified by nodule size (≤12 vs. >12 mm), the association between diagnostic condition and PNABS remained consistent (Figure S3). Preoperative status was associated with higher PNABS in both small and large nodules (B=20.8 and 16.1, respectively; both P<0.001), and postoperative malignant status also remained associated with higher PNABS compared with postoperative benign status (B=7.4 and 4.1, respectively; both P<0.001).

Incremental validity of PNABS for health care utilization

In hierarchical regression models predicting 6-month utilization outcomes, adding PNABS to GAD-7 substantially increased explained variance (CT examinations: R2 from 0.298 to 0.539, ΔR2=0.241; outpatient visits: R2 from 0.333 to 0.572, ΔR2=0.239; excessive provider switching: R2 from 0.320 to 0.573, ΔR2=0.253; all P<.001). Standardized coefficients were consistently larger for PNABS (β=0.639–0.658) than for GAD-7 (β=0.133–0.165), and PNABS contributed far more unique explained variance than GAD-7 across outcomes (Figure 5).

Horizontal stacked bars show variance explained (R2) in 6-month health care utilization outcomes by unique contributions of general anxiety (GAD-7), shared variance, and PNABS unique contribution (incremental validity). In mediation models, the indirect pathway GAD-7→PNABS→utilization represented 71.4–75.7% of the total association between GAD-7 and utilization (bootstrap 95% CIs excluding 0), whereas the reverse model PNABS→GAD-7→utilization represented only 11.8–14.2% (Figure S4). These patterns suggest that PNABS captures behaviorally specific variance that is more closely related to health care utilization than general anxiety severity alone.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study first developed and evaluated the PNABS and then used it to characterize reassurance-seeking phenotypes in patients with PNs. The PNABS showed strong internal consistency, a coherent 6-dimension structure, and evidence of measurement invariance across diagnostic conditions. These psychometric findings support the PNABS as a condition-specific research instrument for measuring difficult-to-regulate reassurance seeking during PN management.

Using the PNABS, we identified three clinically interprTable Sprofiles: Adaptive, Distressed, and Compulsive. The Compulsive profile was concentrated in the preoperative diagnostic-uncertainty period and was associated with substantially higher CT examinations, outpatient visits, and excessive provider switching. Importantly, PNABS added explanatory value beyond GAD-7, indicating that it captures behavioral dysregulation rather than merely severe anxiety.

Recent evidence is consistent with the clinical relevance of these findings. Qualitative work in lung cancer screening has shown that patients’ understanding of nodules and surveillance processes can influence psychological and behavioral responses, and that effective communication may reduce uncertainty and concern (2). Prospective evidence also indicates that psychological distress in patients with indeterminate PNs may not be fully resolved by surgery or follow-up alone (2,8). The present results extend this literature by providing a behavior-focused scale and by linking compulsive reassurance seeking to utilization patterns (22).

Interpretation

The addiction-like framework is intended as a descriptive and non-diagnostic heuristic. It does not imply that patients with PNs meet criteria for a behavioral addiction (13). Rather, it highlights features that are clinically recognizable in some patients: repeated checking or consultation, impaired control over reassurance seeking, disproportionate time and resource investment, distress when reassurance is unavailable, and continuation despite limited benefit (11,23). Within the I-PACE framework, diagnostic uncertainty can trigger threat appraisal and negative affect, while short-term relief after reassurance may reinforce repeated seeking and weaken goal-directed control (13,24).

The preoperative enrichment of the Compulsive profile suggests that the interval before definitive pathology may be a particularly important window for support (25). Postoperative malignant patients showed intermediate PNABS levels, suggesting that diagnostic resolution may reduce some uncertainty while continued cancer-related concerns and surveillance needs sustain reassurance-seeking behaviors in a subset of patients (8). Postoperative benign/pre-invasive patients had the lowest PNABS burden, consistent with partial relief after favorable pathology.

Clinical implications

PN surveillance pathways may benefit from a staged support model. For most patients, clear communication, lay explanations of nodule meaning, written surveillance plans, and digital health literacy guidance may prevent uncertainty from escalating (26). Patients with elevated PNABS scores may require more structured behavioral support, including reassurance-seeking regulation strategies, decision aids, scheduled follow-up communication, or referral for cognitive behavioral or acceptance-based interventions when loss of control or impairment is marked (27,28). PNABS should be considered a research and triage signal rather than a diagnostic instrument until externally validated thresholds are established.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include the large sample, the inclusion of patients across preoperative and postoperative diagnostic conditions, psychometric validation in split-half samples, and complementary person-centered and variable-centered analyses. The PNABS addresses a gap by operationalizing behavior execution and regulation that is not captured by distress scales alone.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and limits interpretation of mediation analyses. Second, this was a single-center tertiary-care cohort, which may limit generalizability. Third, the study included a mixed population of patients with screening-detected, health-check-detected, and incidentally detected PNs, but detection source was not consistently available as a structured analytic variable; therefore, the study could not directly compare screening-detected nodules with incidental PNs (5). Fourth, although time from first CT detection to enrollment and time from surgery to enrollment was summarized descriptively, these timing variables were incomplete for some participants and were not prespecified as primary covariates; therefore, residual confounding by timing cannot be excluded. Fifth, utilization outcomes were primarily self-reported; future studies should link PNABS to objective clinical utilization and functional outcomes. Finally, further psychometric work, including item-response theory/Rasch analyses, external validation, and development of a shorter version, is needed.

Future directions

Future work should include longitudinal cohorts spanning detection through treatment, linkage to objective utilization and functional outcomes, cross-cultural replication, and intervention trials that test whether targeting reassurance seeking improves distress, sleep, and quality of life.

Conclusions

This cross-sectional study identified 3 distinct addiction-like reassurance-seeking profiles among patients with PNs. The preoperative period before definitive pathology was associated with greater behavioral dysregulation and a higher likelihood of the Compulsive profile. PNABS captured behaviorally specific variance beyond general anxiety and may help identify patients who could benefit from targeted support during surveillance.
