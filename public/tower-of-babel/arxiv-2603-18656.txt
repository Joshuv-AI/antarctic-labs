Balanced Thinking: Improving Chain of Thought Training in Vision Language Models
Shaked Perek, Ben WieselAvihu DekelNimrod ShabtayEli SchwartzIBM Research shaked.perek@il.ibm.com

Abstract
Multimodal reasoning in vision-language models (VLMs) typically relies on a two-stage process: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL). In standard SFT, all tokens contribute equally to the loss, even though reasoning data are inherently token-imbalanced. Long <think> traces overshadow short but task-critical <answer> segments, leading to verbose reasoning and inaccurate answers. We propose a SCALe – Scheduled Curriculum Adaptive Loss that explicitly separates supervision over reasoning and answer segments using dynamic, length-independent weighting. Unlike vanilla SFT, which overweights the <think> segment, SCALe-SFT gradually shifts the focus from <think> to <answer> throughout training via a cosine scheduling policy, encouraging concise and well-grounded reasoning. We evaluate SCALe across diverse benchmarks and architectures. Results show that SCALe consistently improves accuracy over vanilla SFT and matches the performance of the full two-phase SFT + GRPO pipeline while requiring only about one-seventh of the training time, making it a lightweight yet effective alternative. When combined with GRPO, SCALe achieves the best overall performance, highlighting its value both as a standalone method and as a strong foundation for reinforcement refinement.

As a result, models become verbose and stylistically fluent but lack factual precision, leading GRPO-initialized checkpoints to favor reasoning style over stable, answer-oriented behavior.

To address these limitations, we propose Scheduled Curriculum Adaptive Loss Supervised Fine-Tuning (SCALe-SFT), an adaptive loss scheduling framework that differentiates between reasoning and answer segments during the supervised training phase.
SCALe-SFT explicitly separates its total loss into reasoning and answer components and assigns controllable weights to each. During training, these weights are dynamically adjusted according to a predefined schedule, initially prioritizing reasoning structure and later emphasizing answer accuracy.
By decoupling supervision across these segments and progressively scheduling their relative contributions, SCALe-SFT enables the model to gradually build coherent reasoning structures that remain logically consistent and well-aligned with the final answer.

3.1
Scheduled Weighted Loss

During the SFT stage, we train the model using an autoregressive cross-entropy objective that applies distinct weights to each section of the model’s predictions: reasoning and answer tokens.
In conventional supervised fine-tuning, the loss is defined as a standard cross-entropy (CE) over all predicted tokens, where each token contributes equally:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{vanilla}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{CE}(y_{i},\hat{y}_{i}),

(1)

with
N
denoting the total number of target tokens.
While effective for general language modeling, this uniform weighting is suboptimal for reasoning-oriented vision language tasks.
In such tasks, the model often generates long and verbose reasoning traces enclosed within
<think>...</think>
, yet evaluation metrics depend primarily on the final short answer segment (
<answer>...</answer>
).
As a result, the vanilla CE objective may overemphasize reasoning fluency at the expense of factual correctness, causing the model to “get lost in thought”, producing extended reasoning that fails to yield accurate answers. In such cases, the wrong answer can be effectively drowned out by the “thinking” loss, with this discrepancy barely reflected in the loss signal.

To address this limitation, we introduce a weighted loss that explicitly separates reasoning and answer supervision. Each output sequence is divided into two semantic segments, the reasoning span enclosed by
<think>
tags and the answer span enclosed by
<answer>
tags—allowing each segment to be weighted and optimized independently.

To prevent longer reasoning sequences from dominating the loss, each segment loss is computed in a length-independent manner.
Let
e_{i}
denote the segment (reasoning/answer) for token
i
. The segment loss for a given sample is then:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{seg}}=\frac{1}{N_{seg}}\sum_{i:e_{i}=seg}\mathrm{CE}(y_{i},\hat{y}_{i})

(2)

where
N_{\text{seg}}
is the number of tokens of the given segment.
This formulation computes mean cross-entropy ensuring that longer reasoning traces (e.g., more tokens inside
<think>...</think>
) do not reduce the influence of shorter examples.

In some cases, reasoning models generate excessively long CoT failing to terminate with a concrete answer. To discourage this behavior, the closing tag
</think>
is grouped with the subsequent
<answer>
segment during loss computation.
By treating the closing
</think>
token as part of the answer segment, the model is further encouraged to end its reasoning deliberately, avoiding unnecessarily verbose or looping thought processes.

To enable a smooth transition between supervision phases, we employ a cosine annealing schedule that gradually interpolates each weight parameter from its initial value to its target value over the course of training. Formally, for a training step
\tau\in[0,T]
, the weight
w(\tau)
is defined as:

w(\tau)=w^{(\text{end})}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bigl(w^{(\text{start})}-w^{(\text{end})}\bigr)\,\Bigl(1+\cos\!\frac{\pi\tau}{T}\Bigr)

(3)

where
w^{(\text{start})}
and
w^{(\text{end})}
denote the initial and final target values, respectively, and
T
is the total number of training steps.
This formulation ensures that the weighting evolves smoothly, avoiding abrupt transitions that could destabilize optimization.
At
\tau=0
,
w(\tau)
equals its initial value
w^{(\text{start})}
, while at
\tau=T
it converges exactly to
w^{(\text{end})}
.

Early in training, higher
w_{t}
encourages robust reasoning supervision, while later iterations shift emphasis toward answer accuracy.
Intuitively, we begin training with a higher weight on reasoning tokens to encourage the model to internalize structured, interpretable reasoning patterns early in learning, when generation behavior is unstable.
As training progresses, the reasoning weight decays toward the answer weight, shifting the objective toward factual correctness once stable reasoning structure has emerged. This behavior is illustrated in Figure
1
, where the token importance of the thinking part decreases and the answer part increases over training time.
This curriculum-like schedule improves both convergence stability and reasoning–answer alignment. The total loss is defined as:

\mathcal{L}=w_{t}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{think}}+w_{a}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{answer}}

(4)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{think}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{answer}}
denote the mean cross-entropy losses over tokens belonging to the corresponding segments, and
w_{t}
,
w_{a}
are their scheduled weights according to eq.
3
.

3.2
GRPO Reinforcement Refinement

Following the SFT stage, an additional GRPO phase can be introduced to further refine reasoning structure and factual correctness, although this stage is not mandatory for the overall training pipeline.
The total reward combines two complementary signals:

1.

Format-structure reward

r_{\text{tag}}
, which measures the correctness of the reasoning–answer tag placement.
Each generated sample is evaluated on four key locations: the opening
<think>
, the closing
</think>
, the opening
<answer>
, and the closing
</answer>
tag.
A partial score of
0.25
is awarded for each correctly placed tag, yielding:

r_{\text{tag}}=0.25\times n_{\text{correct}},

(5)

where
n_{\text{correct}}\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}
denotes the number of correctly located tags in the output sequence.
This structured reward encourages the model to maintain proper reasoning boundaries and to consistently terminate its thought process before generating the final answer.

2.

Answer-accuracy reward

r_{\text{ans}}
, which evaluates an exact match between the generated and reference answers.

The final reward is a weighted combination:

r=\lambda_{\text{tag}}\,r_{\text{tag}}+\lambda_{\text{ans}}\,r_{\text{ans}}.

(6)

This two-part signal encourages the model to maintain coherent reasoning structure while improving factual correctness.
Initialization from the weighted-loss checkpoint provides a stable transition into reinforcement optimization, leading to improved reasoning consistency and alignment.

Our two-stage training, scheduled weighted SFT followed by GRPO, jointly shapes how the model reasons and what it answers.
The length-independent weighted loss promotes interpretable, balanced reasoning during supervised learning, while GRPO fine-tunes reasoning faithfulness and answer accuracy through reinforcement feedback.

These results confirm that the gradual scheduling of reasoning weight acts as a lightweight curriculum mechanism, guiding the model to first focus on structured reasoning and later refine answer precision.

5
Conclusion

We introduced a
SCALe-SFT
– Scheduled Curriculum Adaptive Loss SFT for vision–language reasoning, a simple modification to supervised fine-tuning that applies dynamic, length-independent weighting over reasoning and answer spans.

The proposed SCALe-SFT encourages concise and well-structured reasoning, leading to more accurate final answers. It serves both as a lightweight alternative to reinforcement learning and as a stronger initialization for subsequent GRPO optimization using tag-location and answer-accuracy rewards, resulting in improved reasoning alignment.

Experiments show consistent gains over vanilla SFT and GRPO alone.
Termination analysis shows SCALe-SFT sharply reduces invalid generations (unclosed
<answer>
), thereby reducing the need for repeated inference to recover valid outputs.
Ablation study indicates that replacing the cosine schedule with fixed weights reduced performance gains, confirming that dynamic weighting is better for effective reasoning supervision.

Future work will extend the proposed framework to a broader range of and modalities and multi-image cases.
Another research direction will be to explore an automatic online weight tuning.
We also plan to apply our method to language-only reasoning datasets.
Since SCALe-SFT operates purely on token-level supervision, it can be directly applied to textual reasoning tasks without modification, providing a unified approach to structured reasoning across visual and linguistic domains.
Such extensions will help assess whether the benefits observed here, improved reasoning structure, reduced invalid terminations, and enhanced answer alignment, generalize beyond visual question answering to broader reasoning scenarios.

Appendix C
Implementation Details

Training.

All models were trained using
Deepspeed-ZeRO2
on 8
\times
H100 GPUs.
SFT training ran for one epoch over the respective Vision-R1 subsets.
We used mixed precision (
bfloat16
) and gradient checkpointing.
All models were fine-tuned using LoRA adapters with a learning rate of
1\!\times\!10^{-4}
and a maximum sequence length of
8192
tokens.
For the RL stage, we employed GRPO with a maximum generation length of
1024
tokens, generating
8
completions per prompt using sampling with temperature
1.0
(top-
p=1.0
, no top-
k
filtering). Inference was accelerated using vLLM in colocate mode.

Evaluation.

We evaluated all models using the
lmms-eval
framework.
To accommodate reasoning-based responses, we extended
lmms-eval
to parse the final answer from within the
<answer>
tags.
All metrics were calculated exclusively on the extracted final answers.
We used greedy decoding (temperature
0
) for all evaluations to ensure deterministic and reproducible results.
