Distributional Regression with Tabular Foundation Models: Evaluating Probabilistic Predictions via Proper Scoring Rules
Jonas Landsgesell, Pascal Knoll

Abstract
Tabular foundation models such as TabPFN and TabICL already produce full predictive distributions, yet the benchmarks used to evaluate them (TabArena, TALENT, and others) still rely almost exclusively on point-estimate metrics (RMSE, R 2 R^{2} ). This mismatch implicitly rewards models that elicit a good conditional mean while ignoring the quality of the predicted distribution. We make two contributions. First, we propose supplementing standard point metrics with proper scoring rules (CRPS, CRLS, and the Interval Score) and provide a head-to-head comparison of realTabPFNv2.5 and TabICLv2 with regard to proper scoring rules across 20 OpenML regression datasets. Second, we show analytically and empirically that different proper scoring rules induce different model rankings and different inductive biases during training, even though each rule is individually minimized by the true distribution. Fine-tuning realTabPFNv2.5 with scoring rules not seen during pretraining (CRLS, β = 1.8 \beta{=}1.8 energy score) yields consistent improvements on the corresponding metrics, confirming that the training loss shapes the model beyond what propriety alone guarantees. Together, these findings argue for (i) reporting distributional metrics in tabular regression benchmarks and (ii) making the training objective of foundation models adaptable (via fine-tuning or task-token conditioning) to the scoring rule relevant to the downstream decision problem.

The recent emergence of Prior-Data Fitted Networks (TabPFN)

We agree that point estimates often fail to offer the deepest insight into a problem

However, both approaches must therefore address a fundamental modeling question:

What constitutes a “better” probabilistic forecast during training?

In theory, this exact question is answered through the application of a strictly proper scoring rule. A scoring rule
S(\vec{y},\hat{\vec{p}})
is strictly proper if the expected score is minimized if and only if the forecast distribution matches the true distribution. However, there exist infinitely many scoring rules
6
. For a given decision-making context, the scoring rule should reflect the underlying risk structure. Guiding this choice is itself a hard problem and is beyond the scope of this paper; we note, however, that practitioners may often be able to narrow down the class of appropriate rules based on domain knowledge (e.g., whether left-, right-, or central-tendency errors are most important).

Beyond statistics used for evaluation, we highlight that different scoring rules, when used for optimum score estimation
6
, typically

•

have different inductive biases

;
17
;
23
(see Section

•

differ in sample efficiency at finite sample sizes (e.g., due to the estimated mean
\hat{E}[S(\vec{y},\hat{\vec{p}})]\neq E[S(\vec{y},\hat{\vec{p}})]
not being the true population mean), and

•

produce different gradient norm variances.

The statistics literature shows that a broad class of proper scoring rules — including the logarithmic, CRPS, quadratic, and energy scores — penalize under- and overestimation of distributional parameters
asymmetrically

Related Work

Tabular foundation models & benchmarks.

Prior-fitted networks (PFNs), such as TabPFN
(
9
;
10
)
and TabICL
(
19
)
, represent a fundamental shift in tabular deep learning, frequently outperforming classical gradient-boosted decision trees (GBDTs) on small-to-medium-scale benchmarks. Unlike the conventional train-then-inference paradigm, PFNs reframe supervised learning as an in-context learning (ICL) task. Instead of updating parameters during training, the model infers task structure and inductive biases directly from the dataset at inference. These models enable full-distributional outputs by design, representing a qualitative departure from point-estimate methods — a capability that existing benchmarks have not yet exploited for evaluation. Two well-known tabular benchmarks, TALENT
(
16
)
and TabArena
(
4
)
, evaluate models exclusively using point-estimate metrics such as RMSE and
R^{2}
, ignoring the quality of the full predictive distribution.

Recent work has explored adapting these models beyond their pretraining distribution.
20
introduced TabTune, a unified library for inference and fine-tuning of tabular foundation models, and
21
systematically compared meta-learning, supervised fine-tuning, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) strategies. These studies establish that fine-tuning is both feasible and beneficial — but they focus exclusively on
whether
and
how
to fine-tune in terms of parameter update strategies, treating the training loss as fixed. The question of
which
loss function to fine-tune with — and what inductive bias that choice encodes — has not been addressed. This paper fills that gap.

Proper scoring rules for probabilistic forecasting.

The theoretical foundation for evaluating probabilistic predictions via proper scoring rules was established by
6
, who characterized the space of strictly proper scoring rules and showed that each is individually minimized by the true predictive distribution.
8
further argued that requesting a point forecast without disclosing the scoring function is not a meaningful endeavor.
7
introduced threshold- and quantile-weighted scoring rules to accommodate asymmetric risk structures, providing the theoretical basis for the weighted CRPS.

17
demonstrated empirically in a probabilistic classification setting that model rankings change substantially depending on which proper scoring rule is used.
3
show that a broad class of proper scoring rules, including the logarithmic, CRPS, quadratic, and energy scores, penalize under- and overestimation
asymmetrically, which can further induce different model rankings.
22
provide a unified treatment of the finite-sample inductive biases induced by different proper scoring rules, confirming that population-level equivalence does not imply equivalence during gradient-based optimization.

While the scoring rule literature establishes that the choice of evaluation and training criterion is theoretically non-trivial and has measurable consequences for model rankings and inductive bias, this insight has not been applied to the training or evaluation of tabular foundation models — a gap this paper addresses.

Preliminaries

Classical Regression

Assume a given feature vector
\bm{X}=\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a real-valued target variable
Y\in\mathbb{R}
. Given a dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
n
observations, classical regression seeks a function

f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
that predicts
Y
from
\bm{X}
. Classical regression models a single functional of the
conditional distribution
P_{Y\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}}
. The most common
objective is minimization of the expected squared error:

f^{*}=\text{argmin}_{f}\;\mathbb{E}\bigl[(Y-f(\mathbf{X}))^{2}\bigr],

(1)

whose unique solution is the
conditional mean

f^{*}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}]
.
Conversely, the conditional median arises under MAE minimization, and the
\tau
-quantile arises under the asymmetric pinball loss
8
. In each case, the prediction problem reduces to estimating a single real number per input — a
point estimate
.
While sufficient for many tasks, point estimates cannot capture
aleatoric uncertainty
: the irreducible variability of
Y
given
\bm{x}
that persists even with infinite data
(
13
)
.

Distributional Regression

Distributional regression instead aims to model the entire conditional distribution
F(Y=y\mid\mathbf{x})=P(Y\leq y\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x})
, providing a complete description of uncertainty. The goal is to approximate
F^{*}
with a predictive distribution
\hat{F}(Y=y\mid\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{F}
, where
\mathcal{F}
denotes a suitable family of distributions over

\mathbb{R}
. This shifts the modeling question from
“what is the most likely value of
Y
?”
to
“what is the full range of plausible values of
Y
, and how probable is each?”
. Distributional regression, therefore, strictly generalizes classical regression, and the only cost is a more demanding modeling and evaluation framework — precisely what proper scoring rules provide (Section
4
).

Proper Scoring Rules.

The evaluation of probabilistic forecasts is central to both the development and comparison of statistical models.
5
characterize the goal of probabilistic forecasting as maximizing the
sharpness
of the predictive distribution subject to
calibration
. Calibration refers to the consistency between the distributional forecasts and the observation. A forecast is sharp if its predictive intervals are shorter than those of a naive probabilistic forecast. A scoring rule
S(\hat{F},y)
measures the goodness of this joint criterion by assigning a numerical score to a predictive distribution
\hat{F}
given one observed outcome
y
, providing a single measure of forecast quality
(
6
)
.

A scoring rule
S
is called
proper
with respect to a class of distributions
\mathcal{P}
if the expected score is minimized by reporting the true distribution — that is,

\mathbb{E}_{y\sim Q}[S(Q,y)]\leq\mathbb{E}_{y\sim Q}[S(P,y)]

(2)

for all
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}
, where
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim Q}[S(P,y)]=\int S(P,y)\,\mathrm{d}Q(y)
denotes the expected score under the true distribution
Q

(
6
)
. A scoring rule is
strictly proper
if equality holds if and only if
P=Q
, ensuring that the true distribution is the unique minimizer and that no misspecified forecast can achieve the same expected score.

Finite-sample inductive bias of proper scoring rules.

For any strictly proper scoring rule
S
and true conditional distribution
Q
, the expected score
\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q}[S(\hat{F},Y)]
is uniquely minimized by
\hat{F}=Q
. This might suggest that the choice of scoring rule is inconsequential — any strictly proper rule will, in the limit of
infinite data, recover the same optimal forecast. This intuition is correct asymptotically but misleading in practice.

In practice, one minimizes the
empirical
average

\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n}[S(\hat{F},y)]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S(\hat{F},y_{i}),

(3)

whose optimization path is governed by the gradient
\nabla_{\hat{F}}\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{n}[S(\hat{F},y)]
. Different strictly proper scoring rules induce fundamentally different gradient structures. For instance, the logarithmic score
S_{\log}(\hat{F},y)=-\log\hat{f}(y)
has a gradient proportional to
1/\hat{f}(y)
, which is unbounded as
\hat{f}(y)\to 0
. This causes low-density observations to contribute disproportionately large updates, often leading to numerical instability in simulation-based models.

The CRPS (continuous ranked probability score), by contrast, can be viewed through its expectation-based representation:

\text{CRPS}(F,y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(F(z)-\mathbb{I}\{y\leq z\})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z,

(4)

resulting in a bounded gradient that weights all quantile levels equally. This ensures that outliers or "tail" realizations do not dominate the training signal as they do with the logarithmic score.

As a consequence, different scoring rules induce different effective regularization, different convergence rates, and — critically — different
inductive biases
at finite sample sizes
(
17
;
22
)
. Two models trained on the same data with different strictly proper scoring rules will in general converge to different solutions (for finite samples) and achieve different rankings on held-out evaluation data.
17
demonstrate this empirically in a probabilistic classification setting. We extend this finding analytically to the regression setting via the
\beta
-energy score family in Section
5
, and demonstrate its practical consequence for tabular foundation model fine-tuning in Section
6
.

Point Forecasts and Scoring Functions.

In many practical settings, single-valued point forecasts are required rather than full predictive distributions. According to
8
, such forecasts require a directive in the form of a statistical functional, which specifies which value to report from the predictive distribution. A statistical functional is a mapping
T(F):\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
from a class of probability distributions
\mathcal{F}
to the real line — common examples include the mean, median, and quantiles.

For point forecasts, a consistent scoring function induces a proper scoring rule. A scoring function
s
is called
consistent
for a functional
T
relative to
\mathcal{F}
if

\mathbb{E}_{F}[s(t,Y)]\leq\mathbb{E}_{F}[s(x,Y)]

(5)

for all
F\in\mathcal{F}
, all
t\in T(F)
, and all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. The quality of point forecasts can be assessed through the concept of
elicitability
: a functional
T
is elicitable relative to
\mathcal{F}
if there exists a scoring function that is strictly consistent for
T
relative to
\mathcal{F}

(
8
)
. Elicitability thus ensures that optimizing the scoring function genuinely targets the intended functional. Two canonical examples are:

•

The family of Bregman divergences (and especially the example of the
squared error scoring function
) is strictly consistent for the mean functional
8
. A scoring function
s
is consistent for the mean functional if and only if it is of Bregman form
(
1
)
.

•

The
asymmetric piecewise linear (pinball) scoring function
is strictly consistent for the
\alpha
-quantile function
(
6
;
8
)
.

Proper Scoring Rules for Tabular Regression Benchmarks

In the spirit of the seminal work by Gneiting
6
;
7
;
8
, we propose supplementing standard regression benchmarks for tabular foundation models with proper scoring rules. This shifts the focus from point-wise accuracy to evaluating the full predicted conditional distribution. We describe the four rules used in our experiments below; additional rules (log score, weighted CRPS, Variogram Score) are discussed in Appendix
9.1
.

4.1
Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS)

Viewing discretized regression as multi-class classification suggests cross-entropy (the log scoring rule), but this ignores the ordinal structure of the output space: predicting
y{=}100
when the truth is
y{=}10
should be penalized more heavily than predicting
y{=}11
, yet cross-entropy treats both errors identically if they fall into different wrong bins.
To inject geometric awareness, we proposed using CRPS for fine-tuning TabPFN in December 2025
3
3

3

https://github.com/PriorLabs/TabPFN/pull/689
and observed that CRPS was adopted in TabICLv2 training in February 2026
19
.

For a discretized output space, the CRPS (equivalent to the
\beta{=}1
energy score) is:

\mathrm{CRPS}(F,y)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(F(x_{i})-\mathbbm{1}_{\{x_{i}\geq y\}}\bigr)^{2}\Delta x_{i},

(6)

where
F(x_{i})
is the predicted CDF (e.g., obtained via cumulative summation of the softmax output over the binned support).
CRPS weights all quantiles equally, which can be limiting when risk is asymmetric over the output space
7
.

Illustration of the CRPS. The green curve is the forecast CDF
F_{P}(z)
, and the red dashed line is the Heaviside step function
\mathbb{I}\{z\geq\omega\}
for observation
\omega
. The shaded blue area represents the discrepancy between the forecast and the observation; the CRPS is the integral of the square of this difference over all possible thresholds
z
.

4.2
\beta
-Energy Score

The energy score generalizes CRPS to arbitrary
\beta\in(0,2)
:

\mathbf{S}_{\beta}(F,y)=\mathbb{E}_{F}\|X-y\|^{\beta}-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{F}\|X-X^{\prime}\|^{\beta},

(7)

where
X,X^{\prime}\sim F
are independent draws. For
d{=}1
and
\beta{=}1
,
\mathbf{S}_{\beta}
reduces to the CRPS. We use
\beta{=}1.8
as a fine-tuning loss in our experiments (Section
6
).

4.3
Continuous Ranked Logarithmic Scoring Rule (CRLS)

CRLS (also called the Exceedance Probability Score) was introduced by
12
:

\mathrm{CRLS}(F,y)=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\log|F(x)+\mathbf{1}\{y\leq x\}-1|\,dx,

(8)

where the negation ensures lower scores correspond to better forecasts.
CRLS combines the distance sensitivity of CRPS with a logarithmic penalty, giving more weight to events that were predicted to be very unlikely
12
. It penalizes crude errors in the predicted mean more heavily than either CRPS or the log score alone.

4.4
Interval Score

The Interval Score
6
targets the
(1{-}\alpha)
central prediction interval:

S_{\alpha}(F,y)=(u-l)+\frac{2}{\alpha}(l-y)\mathbb{I}\{y<l\}+\frac{2}{\alpha}(y-u)\mathbb{I}\{y>u\},

(9)

where
[l,u]
are the
\alpha/2
and
1{-}\alpha/2
quantiles of
F
. We report
\mathrm{IS}_{95}
(
\alpha{=}0.05
) as one of our six evaluation metrics. This rule illustrates that different scoring rules favor optimization in distinct regions of the predictive distribution; in this extreme case, only the two boundary quantiles are penalized.

4.5
Multivariate Enhancements

For
y\in\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d>1
, univariate scores are insufficient for capturing dependence. We employ and suggest benchmarking the Energy Score (
\mathbf{S}_{\beta}
) and Variogram Score (
\mathbf{S}_{VS,p}
), as also done in
14
.

The
Energy Score
for
\beta\in(0,2)
is defined as:

\mathbf{S}_{\beta}(F,y)=\mathbb{E}_{F}\|X-y\|^{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{F}\|X-X^{\prime}\|^{\beta}

(10)

where
X,X^{\prime}\sim F
are independent. For
d=1
and
\beta=1
,
\mathbf{S}_{\beta}
reduces to the CRPS.

The
Variogram Score
of order
p>0
specifically targets the correlation structure by penalizing errors in the pairwise differences:

\mathbf{S}_{VS,p}(F,y)=\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}w_{j,k}\left(|y_{j}-y_{k}|^{p}-\mathbb{E}_{F}|X_{j}-X_{k}|^{p}\right)^{2}

(11)

where
w_{j,k}
are weights. While
\mathbf{S}_{\beta}
ensures strict propriety,
\mathbf{S}_{VS,p}
provides superior sensitivity to misspecified dependencies between components of
y
.

How Scoring Rules Shape the Optimal Forecast

To start the discussion about the choice of scoring rules for probabilistic regression, we strengthen the intuition by looking at the simpler case of
scoring functions
for eliciting point estimates.
As Gneiting emphasizes:

“Despite […] well-argued perspectives, there has been little recognition that the common practice of requesting some point forecast, and then evaluating the forecasters by using some (set of) scoring function(s), is not a meaningful endeavor. […] effective point forecasting depends on guidance or directives, which can be given in one of two complementary ways, namely, by disclosing the scoring function ex ante to the forecaster, or by requesting a specific functional of the forecaster’s predictive distribution, such as the mean or a quantile.”
8

Revisiting the simplest example of estimating the mean value of a die, we see the importance of Gneiting’s argument.
Figure
4
shows two different scoring functions from the family of Bregman power divergences for this example of a die.
We see that the optimal forecast is always 3.5 (as expected) since the expected Bregman divergences for different forecasts have the minimum at this value (solid line). However, predicting another value
c
other than 3.5 is penalized differently by the two Bregman divergences. This generalizes to proper scoring rules having different inductive biases
22
;
17
.

Several lines of work address how to choose the right rule: Oesterheld
18
formalizes “Decision Scoring Rules”; Johnstone et al.
11

17
show empirically that the ranking of forecasters changes with the scoring rule (the Spearman correlation between Brier-score and log-score rankings in their Table 1 is only 0.15).

23
discuss enforcing tail calibration when training probabilistic forecast models by choosing or regularizing scoring rules.

We port Merkle’s idea of highlighting how the choice of scoring rule matters to the discretized regression setting using the
\beta
-energy score family.

We show that the answer to the question "what is the optimal model?" depends on the choice of the scoring rule (
\beta
value).

Additionally, we show that, for specific choices of scoring rules that do not align with the pretraining task of tabular foundation models (loss function), simple aligned XGBoost models can outperform SOTA PFNs. This is argued in the paper through theoretical toy examples but also demonstrated numerically in the appendix in Section
9.4
.
This finding may be important for real-world use cases with asymmetric risk structures (e.g., finance, weather forecasting
8
).

\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{m}}\,\mathbb{E}[|\hat{m}-y|]=\Pr(y<\hat{m})-\Pr(y>\hat{m})=0\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\hat{m}=\mathrm{median}(Q).

(17)

Case
\beta=2
.

Equation (

) becomes
\min_{\hat{m}}\,\mathbb{E}[(\hat{m}-y)^{2}]
, i.e. minimisation of the

(MSE).
Setting the derivative to zero gives

\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{m}}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[(\hat{m}-y)^{2}\right]=2\,(\hat{m}-\mathbb{E}[y])=0\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\hat{m}^{*}=\mathbb{E}[y]=\mathrm{mean}(Q).

(18)

Hence the
\beta

\beta=2

of the predictive distribution.

Furthermore, the computational tractability varies substantially depending on the chosen scoring rule.”

How to best choose a scoring rule for general-purpose tabular foundation model pretraining and how to enable adaptation to business cases with asymmetric error severity remains an open question; we discuss possible routes in section

In the appendix in section

7
Outlook

Proper scoring rules provide a principled framework for evaluation and should be reported in benchmarks on tabular regression.
However, several open questions remain for tabular foundation models:

7
point out that asymmetric risk settings (finance, weather forecasting, etc.) may require optimizing for specific scoring rules or scoring functions.
We see that a fundamental tension arises between:

•

a general-purpose foundation model that is not modified after pretraining, and

•

the requirement that the optimal scoring rule be use-case-specific and therefore supplied by the user.

We see two potential routes to resolve this tension: (i) fine-tuning on a user-supplied scoring rule at deployment time, or (ii) task-token conditioning, where the model is prompted with a desired scoring rule or parameter of some scoring rule family for which it was trained to produce optimal forecasts under that rule. The second approach faces a fundamental challenge: there exist infinitely many proper scoring rules
6
, spanning infinitely many parametric families, so it is unclear whether pretraining with task tokens can generalize to arbitrary scoring objectives.
High-stakes applications (finance, pharmaceutical production) with asymmetric risk may therefore require model variants pretrained with carefully chosen task-specific conditioning or fine-tuning.

A structural limitation discussed in recent literature is that all scoring-rule-based approaches are inherently ill-suited for eliciting properties realized only in rare or tail events
15
;
2
.
Where training data is absent (e.g. in tail regions), no scoring rule can compensate; this is fundamentally a problem of
epistemic uncertainty
.
PFNs trained to optimize expected scoring rules should therefore be applied with care when risk is concentrated in the tails or outside the support of the supplied context, although they might prove to be more sample efficient than traditional methods.

9.3
Ranking of Mean-predicting models with different Bregman Divergences

For readers who find point forecasts more intuitive than probabilistic forecasts, we also examine how model rankings change under different Bregman divergences as scoring functions. Bregman divergences are precisely the class of functions
consistent
for the mean functional—that is, they uniquely identify the conditional mean as the minimizer of expected loss (see
7
for a thorough treatment of consistency and elicitability).

7
shows that Bregman functions (Bregman divergences) elicit the mean functional.
Errors for a point estimate are readily understood as residuals.
While the mean squared error (MSE) is a very common choice for regression tasks (and evaluation metrics like
R^{2}
are based on MSE), it is well-known that MSE is sensitive to outliers, and therefore it may not be the best choice for all applications.
Additionally MSE penalizes the direction of the error symmetrically, which may not be appropriate for all applications (e.g., in finance, overestimating a stock price may be less severe than underestimating it).
Bregman divergences offer a route to elicit the mean while also encoding different error preferences.

This section mirrors the ranking analysis in section
5.1
, but applied to point predictions rather than distributional forecasts. As Gneiting and Raftery note, "
a scoring function is consistent for the mean functional if and only if it is a Bregman function
, while entirely different characterizations apply to quantile functionals (generalized piecewise linear) and other properties such as ratios of expectations or expectiles. Importantly, not all functionals are elicitable—most notably, conditional value-at-risk in quantitative finance lacks an equivalent scoring function."

The key insight is that
model rankings are not invariant to the choice of Bregman divergence
. Just as different proper scoring rules rerank probabilistic forecasts (section
5.1
), different Bregman divergences rerank point predictors. This demonstrates that the scoring rule selected for model training directly shapes which predictions are encouraged, with concrete implications for which model is “best” in any given application.

We evaluate this ranking phenomenon using the power Bregman divergence family. For the generator
F_{p}(x)=\frac{|x|^{p}}{p(p-1)}
with
p>1
, the divergence is given by:

D_{p}(y,\hat{y})=F_{p}(y)-F_{p}(\hat{y})-F_{p}^{\prime}(\hat{y})(y-\hat{y})

(20)

where
F_{p}^{\prime}(\hat{y})=\frac{\operatorname{sign}(\hat{y})|\hat{y}|^{p-1}}{p-1}
.

The mean divergence across all predictions is:

\mathbb{E}[D_{p}(Y,\hat{y})]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|Y|^{p}-|\hat{y}|^{p}}{p(p-1)}-\frac{\operatorname{sign}(\hat{y})|\hat{y}|^{p-1}}{p-1}(Y-\hat{y})\right]

(21)

As the exponent
p
varies, the divergence penalizes errors with different severity: lower
p
(near 1) produces gentler penalties, while higher
p
(greater than 2) creates aggressive super-quadratic penalties. This variation in penalty structure causes model rankings to flip across the
p
domain, exactly mirroring the ranking reversals observed with proper scoring rules in section
5.1
.

9.4
Further Investigation on the Inductive Bias of Different Scoring Functions and Scoring Rules

To go beyond mere model selection in the previous section, we illustrate that the choice of a scoring rule matters for making "optimal forecasts" with machine learning models for:

•

point estimates

•

and predictive distributions

To illustrate this on a toy dataset, we show that a simple XGBoost model with a custom objective function (tailored to the evaluation metric) can beat the pretrained model although it was not pretrained on the same data and has a much smaller capacity. This is because the pretrained model was not trained to optimize the same scoring rule as the XGBoost model, and therefore it is not optimal for that scoring rule.

Key intuition is the following:
Different scoring rules or scoring functions penalize different types of errors differently, and therefore the optimal forecast under one scoring rule may not be optimal under another scoring rule.

This is a fundamental insight that has important implications for the
design and evaluation
of tabular foundation models.

The fact that the type of error matters is an insight that is not new and is very well known in probabilistic forecasting (e.g., when applying probabilistic forecasts to a Kelly-style criterion) and in the classification setting, where costs of different types of errors (false positives vs. false negatives) are asymmetric and therefore post-hoc threshold tuning is introduced to control for specific trade-offs in the types of errors.
However, in the regression setting this insight is often overlooked and the choice of scoring rule or scoring function is often not explicitly discussed or justified.

Practitioners often default to Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) and post-hoc residual analysis. However, this neglects the fact that selecting a specific scoring function at training time allows one to encode error preferences directly into the model’s inductive bias.

Bregman Divergence Families.

We employ two distinct Bregman divergence families in the optimization, each with different penalization properties:

Exponential-Quadratic Family (
\mathrm{exp\_sq}
):

Generator
\Phi(x)=\exp(kx^{2})
with first and second derivatives

\Phi^{\prime}(x)=2kx\exp(kx^{2})
and
\Phi^{\prime\prime}(x)=(2k+4k^{2}x^{2})\exp(kx^{2})
.
The divergence is
D(y,\mu)=\Phi(y)-\Phi(\mu)-\Phi^{\prime}(\mu)(y-\mu)
.
The exponential curvature creates
super-quadratic penalty growth
: as
|y-\mu|
increases, the Hessian
\Phi^{\prime\prime}(\mu)
explodes exponentially, dramatically penalizing large residuals. Higher
k
increases penalty curvature; lower
k
approaches MSE-like behavior.

Use case
: datasets with smooth error distributions where suppressing outliers is desirable.

Polynomial-Heavy Family (
\mathrm{poly\_heavy}
):

Generator
\Phi(x)=x^{p}+x^{2}
with derivatives

\Phi^{\prime}(x)=px^{p-1}+2x
and
\Phi^{\prime\prime}(x)=p(p-1)x^{p-2}+2
.
The divergence is
D(y,\mu)=\Phi(y)-\Phi(\mu)-\Phi^{\prime}(\mu)(y-\mu)
.
The term
x^{p}
(with
p>2
) creates
power-law curvature
: as
|\mu|
increases,
\Phi^{\prime\prime}(\mu)\propto\mu^{p-2}
grows polynomially. The constant
+2
term ensures strict positivity and prevents singularities at
\mu=0
. The exponent
p
controls severity:
p=2
is pure quadratic,
p=5
is highly aggressive.

Use case
: datasets with heavy-tailed or skewed error patterns where prediction scale itself affects penalty severity.

9.4.3
Scoring Rule for the Predicted Distribution

We evaluate the predicted distribution for a similar toy model as for the Bregman divergence experiments, using a weighted CRPS (wCRPS) with different weight functions: left and right tail weighted.

The weighted Continuous Ranked Probability Score (wCRPS) for a cumulative distribution function
F
and an observation
y
is defined as:

\text{wCRPS}(F,y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(F(x)-\mathbb{1}\{x\geq y\})^{2}w(x)dx

(24)

where
w(x)
is a non-negative weight function on
\mathbb{R}
. For the specific weighting scheme based on bin midpoints, let
x
denote the midpoints with
x_{\min}
and
x_{\max}
as the boundary values. The normalized position
u
is given by:

u=\frac{x-x_{\min}}{x_{\max}-x_{\min}}

(25)

The weight function
w(x)
is then implemented as:

w(x)=\begin{cases}(1-u)^{2},&\text{for left-tail weighting}\\
u^{2},&\text{for right-tail weighting}\\
1,&\text{for uniform weighting}\end{cases}

(26)

This formulation allows for the evaluation of probabilistic forecasts by emphasizing specific regions of the outcome space, such as extreme events in the tails of the distribution.

The data generating process can be described as follows. For
i=1,\ldots,n
:

\displaystyle x_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(-4,4)

\displaystyle y_{i}

\displaystyle=\cos(x_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathcal{N}(0,0.2^{2}),&x_{i}<0\\
\pm\eta_{i},&x_{i}\geq 0\end{cases}

\displaystyle\eta_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda),\quad\begin{cases}+\eta_{i}&\text{if direction = 'right'}\\
-\eta_{i}&\text{if direction = 'left'}\end{cases}

where
\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)
denotes the exponential distribution with rate parameter
\lambda=0.5
(i.e., scale
1/0.5=2.0
).

We train XGBoost version 3.2.0 with vector leaves and a custom objective for the corresponding wCRPS, which is then also used for evaluation.

The results in Table

Specifically, XGBoost shows a 14.1% improvement in the left-tail scenario and a 17.7% improvement in the right-tail scenario.
XGBoost wins on 15 out of 20 synthetic datasets in both cases.
