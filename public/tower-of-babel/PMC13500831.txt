Privacy-preserving federated learning for interpretable student at-risk prediction across schools
Authors: Mahdee Jodayree, Arman Kavoosi Ghafi, Mostafa Atashafrouz, Mohammad Hossein Shafiabadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early-warning systems for at-risk students increasingly rely on predictive models trained on sensitive educational records. However, centralized learning pipelines raise concerns about privacy, institutional data sovereignty, and auditability, particularly when student-level data are shared across institutions. This study presents Federated Learning for At-Risk Student Prediction with Differential Privacy and Proof-Before-Train (FL-AtRisk-DP-PBT), a federated learning framework for multi-school at-risk prediction that integrates Federated Averaging (FedAvg)-based training, client-side DP, and a PBT protocol for verifiable logging of client participation and model states. The framework uses a single interpretable global logistic-regression classifier and is evaluated under centralized, standard federated, and FL + DP+PBT regimes on three educational datasets: a primary merged cohort of 14,003 students partitioned into 10 simulated schools, the xAPI-Edu-Data click-stream corpus, and the Students Performance in Exams dataset. On the primary dataset, the centralized model achieves 99.14% accuracy, F1 = 0.9915, and area under the curve (AUC) = 0.9998, while the FedAvg and FL + DP+PBT variants achieve 98.61%/0.9863/0.9993 and 98.00%/0.9802/0.9992, respectively. On xAPI and Exams, FL + DP+PBT reaches approximately 93–94% accuracy, F1 ≈ 0.92–0.93, and AUC ≈ 0.97–0.98. Coefficient-based feature-importance analysis indicates that FL + DP+PBT preserves broadly similar interpretation patterns to the centralized and non-private federated baselines. The PBT ablation introduces only small metric changes relative to DP-only federated training. Overall, the results suggest that interpretable federated at-risk prediction can retain competitive utility while keeping student records local and adding privacy-preserving and verifiable training mechanisms. These findings should be interpreted within the evaluated datasets, simulated school partitions, and label definitions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56609-z.

Introduction

Universities worldwide are still grappling with the problems of student failure and dropout, particularly in large first-year and online courses, where early identification of struggling learners is necessary for the design and timely delivery of intervention measures. Recent efforts in the fields of learning analytics and educational data mining (EDM) have demonstrated that prediction models trained on Learning Management System (LMS) traces, assessment data, and behavioral signatures can estimate academic risk with high accuracy several weeks before final grades are known1–4. To illustrate, explainable EDM pipelines developed for programming education and blended courses have shown that relatively small feature sets summarizing engagement, assessment, and time-management patterns suffice to power early-warning systems when combined with machine-learning classifiers and post-hoc interpretability methods1–3. At the same time, multi-dimensional prediction frameworks that aggregate demographic, behavioral, and performance indicators are becoming commonplace for supporting fine-grained academic counselling and personalized support4,5. Parallel lines of inquiry have also established connections between prediction and intervention: when risk estimates are fed back to instructors and students through well-designed dashboards and feedback channels, course completion rates and final grades can be improved in a measurable way5–7.

Notwithstanding this progress, several desiderata remain unmet in the use of early-warning models at the institutional and cross-institution levels. On the one hand, many recent systems are trained on data from a single course or institution and are therefore susceptible to cohort shift, concept drift, and changes in instructional design4,8. Research efforts that explicitly evaluate temporal drift in prediction performance show that models trained on historical cohorts can experience a sharp drop in accuracy when the course structure, assessments, or student population change, unless they are periodically recalibrated or updated8. On the other hand, a number of high-performing methods rely on complex ensembles or deep architectures whose decision logic is opaque to instructors and students, even when explainable-AI toolkits are deployed ex post1,3. This motivates the use of intrinsically interpretable models whose parameters can be inspected directly, rather than relying only on post-hoc explanations of complex black-box models. This lack of transparency makes it difficult to translate model outputs into actionable pedagogical decisions, and raises concerns about the robustness and stability of explanations across cohorts and models9. Moreover, most existing interventions are integrated in centralized analytics infrastructures, which assume unfettered access to fine-grained student data and offer limited assurances with respect to privacy, data minimization, and compliance with local regulations. Recent systematic reviews on privacy and inclusiveness in learning analytics have pinpointed the tension between data-hungry predictive models and the expectations of confidentiality, consent, and control that students have over their data10,11.

These issues are compounded by the widespread adoption of machine learning in education more generally. Exhaustive surveys of machine-learning applications in education document not only the promise of predictive models to address retention and personalization, but also the ethical and governance challenges involved in algorithms that act on sensitive student records at scale12. Recent work on multimodal and immersive learning analytics further enlarges the set of data modalities under consideration, including clickstreams, grades, eye-tracking, audio, and spatial interaction traces, which introduces new concerns regarding surveillance, data protection, and the interpretability of complex, high-dimensional representations13–15. While these findings highlight the richness of the design space for predictive and prescriptive analytics, they also expose a clear practical and empirical gap: it is currently not well understood how simple, transparent, coefficient-based models such as logistic regression perform when trained in privacy-preserving and distributed settings that explicitly respect institutional boundaries and governance constraints.

A second, related gap concerns the adaptation of federated learning (FL) and formal privacy mechanisms to early-warning pipelines. While there is a growing interest in the deployment of federated architectures in education, most existing systems are either concerned with more technical questions such as communication efficiency or personalization, or are evaluated on a single dataset without systematic comparison to centralized baselines4,12. Differential Privacy (DP), for its part, has been explored primarily in centralized settings where noise is added to model updates or to aggregate statistics computed on a pooled dataset10. In the student-success prediction literature, fewer studies jointly examine cross-institution training without raw-data sharing, client-side DP with explicit privacy accounting, and coefficient-based interpretability within a single early-warning pipeline. Even fewer works explore how such models behave across heterogeneous datasets and institutional risk profiles, or how closely federated and privacy-preserving variants can approximate the performance of an idealized centralized reference model5–8.

In this paper, we take a step towards addressing these gaps by introducing FL-AtRisk-DP-PBT, a Federated Learning for At-Risk student prediction with DP and Proof-Before-Train framework. We instantiate the framework with a deliberately simple and interpretable model—logistic regression—and evaluate it under three training regimes: (1) centralized training on pooled data, (2) federated training using Federated Averaging (FedAvg) without privacy noise, and (3) federated training with client-side differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD), equipped with a lightweight PBT mechanism. Using three complementary datasets that capture different educational contexts and feature spaces, we ask to what extent the global at-risk classifier learned under federated and DP-federated training can approximate the predictive performance of the centralized model, and whether the resulting coefficients are stable enough to support meaningful interpretation of risk factors across settings. The choice of logistic regression is intentional: the model is computationally lightweight, communication-efficient in federated training, directly auditable through its coefficients, and more transparent than deep or highly specialized architectures. Our approach therefore does not aim to introduce a new deep-learning architecture; rather, it evaluates whether a transparent linear model can provide a useful and auditable baseline for privacy-preserving, distributed early-warning systems.

The contribution of this work is primarily integrative and empirical. We evaluate whether a transparent logistic-regression model can support binary at-risk prediction across centralized, federated, and differentially private federated training regimes while keeping the model specification, feature processing, optimization protocol, and evaluation metrics consistent. The proposed framework combines FedAvg-based cross-school training, client-side DP-SGD, PBT audit logging, and coefficient-based interpretation within a single experimental pipeline. Empirically, we assess this framework on three educational datasets with different feature spaces and risk-label definitions, reporting predictive performance, ROC-based analyses, local-only baselines, repeated-seed stability, membership-inference attack results, and coefficient-based feature-importance agreement across training regimes. In doing so, the study provides evidence on the extent to which privacy-preserving and auditable federated training can retain competitive utility and interpretable decision patterns relative to centralized logistic-regression baselines, without claiming to introduce a new model architecture or a fundamentally new optimization algorithm.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews related work on early-warning systems, FL in education, privacy-preserving analytics, and explainable student-performance prediction. Section 3 describes the datasets, preprocessing pipeline, and modelling framework, including the centralized and federated training procedures, differential-privacy mechanism, and PBT logging. Section 4 reports the experimental setup and results, including predictive performance, ROC analyses, and coefficient-based interpretability across datasets. Section 5 provides a detailed discussion of the findings, including cross-dataset generalization, practical deployment considerations, and limitations. Section 6 concludes with a summary of contributions, implications for policy and practice, and directions for future research on privacy-aware, interpretable FL in educational settings.

Related work

Learning analytics and educational data mining have long been used to identify students who are likely to fail, drop out, or require additional academic support. Early studies mainly relied on centralized institutional datasets, such as LMS traces, assessment records, demographic variables, and course-engagement indicators. Over time, this literature has expanded from simple statistical classifiers toward more complex machine-learning models, early-warning dashboards, privacy-aware analytics, and explainable prediction systems. In this section, we organize the related work around four themes that are most relevant to the present study: centralized at-risk prediction, FL in education, privacy-preserving learning analytics, and interpretability or explainability in student-performance prediction.

At-risk student prediction and early-warning systems

A substantial body of work has shown that student failure, dropout, and low academic performance can be predicted from relatively early educational traces. Macfadyen and Dawson16 provided one of the early demonstrations that LMS activity data can support an early-warning system, using logistic regression to identify students likely to fail a course. Their findings are important because they show that even relatively simple and interpretable models can provide actionable risk signals. Similarly, Hu et al.17 used logistic regression and decision trees on clickstream and assessment data from online learning environments, showing that prediction models updated during the semester can provide timely alerts before final outcomes are known. Akçapınar et al.18 further demonstrated the feasibility of early detection by engineering LMS-based features and applying machine-learning classifiers to identify unsuccessful students within the first weeks of a course.

Subsequent work has moved from proof-of-concept prediction toward deployment-oriented early-warning systems. Bañeres et al.19 combined predictive modelling, rule-based risk categories, and instructor-facing dashboards for online higher education, showing that early-warning tools can be integrated into institutional workflows. Jayaprakash et al.20 introduced the Open Academic Analytics Initiative, demonstrating that predictive alerts can be linked to interventions and improvements in course completion and grades. Other studies have emphasized task-specific prediction contexts, such as dropout prediction21, introductory programming failure22, cross-course prediction portability23, and institutional formative assessment24. Across these studies, reported performance is often high, and systematic evidence suggests that educational data-mining models commonly achieve strong accuracy for retention, dropout, and failure prediction tasks25.

Despite these advances, most early-warning systems still assume centralized access to student data. This assumption limits their applicability in multi-institutional settings, where data governance, consent, local policy, and institutional autonomy may prevent the pooling of raw student records. In addition, high-performing centralized models may rely on rich institutional features that are difficult to share across schools or interpret consistently across cohorts. These limitations motivate the need for early-warning frameworks that can support cross-institution collaboration while keeping student records local.

Federated learning in education

FL has recently emerged as a promising approach for educational analytics because it allows multiple institutions to train a shared model without directly exchanging raw data. Fachola et al.26 introduced a FL framework for educational data analytics and showed that federated models can achieve performance close to centralized baselines while reducing the need for data transfer. This work provides an important foundation for applying FL to student-success prediction, but its focus is mainly on distributed training and communication efficiency rather than on formal privacy guarantees or interpretable model behavior.

More recent FL studies in education have explored different modelling tasks and system designs. Some approaches use FL for grade prediction, course-outcome mapping, dropout prediction, or learning-outcome estimation across campuses or platforms27–32. For example, FLPADPM uses deep temporal models for dropout prediction in MOOC settings27, while FecMap addresses learning-outcome prediction with multi-layer privacy protection31. Other frameworks incorporate FL into Internet-of-Education or LMS-based environments, including CO–PO mapping and emotion-detection applications32,33. These studies demonstrate the growing relevance of FL for educational prediction and monitoring.

However, several gaps remain. Many federated education models rely on complex neural or ensemble architectures, which may improve accuracy but reduce transparency for educators and administrators. Others focus on a single course, institution, or task, making it difficult to assess generalization across heterogeneous educational datasets. In addition, formal DP, empirical privacy-attack evaluation, and verifiable participation logging are not consistently integrated into existing federated educational pipelines. The present study addresses this gap by using a simple global logistic-regression model trained under centralized, FedAvg, and FL + DP+PBT regimes across three different datasets.

Privacy-preserving learning analytics and DP

Privacy is a central concern in learning analytics because educational records often contain sensitive information about performance, behavior, background, engagement, and learning difficulties. Gürsoy et al.34 surveyed privacy-preserving learning-analytics techniques, including DP, secure multiparty computation, and anonymization, and highlighted the trade-off between analytical utility and disclosure risk. Complementing this technical perspective, Mutimukwe et al.35 examined students’ privacy concerns in learning analytics and showed that trust, perceived benefit, and perceived risk influence students’ willingness to share data. These findings underline that privacy-preserving educational analytics is not only a technical problem but also a governance and trust issue.

Several studies have proposed privacy-preserving alternatives to direct centralized learning. Liu et al.36 explored synthetic-data generation as a way to support learning analytics while reducing re-identification risks. Zhao et al.37 proposed DP-TabNet, integrating DP with an interpretable deep tabular model for grade prediction. Zhan et al.38 studied privacy–utility preservation in learning analytics and showed that carefully calibrated privacy mechanisms can maintain useful model performance while providing stronger privacy protection. These studies are directly relevant to privacy-aware educational prediction, but most of them operate in centralized settings or do not explicitly address cross-institution heterogeneity.

In federated settings, privacy requires additional care. Keeping raw data local does not by itself eliminate privacy risks, because model updates and output probabilities can still leak information about individual records. This motivates the use of client-side DP and empirical privacy evaluation, such as membership-inference attacks. The present work therefore combines FedAvg with client-side DP-SGD and evaluates privacy leakage empirically through membership-inference attack experiments. The PBT mechanism further adds an auditability layer by logging client participation and model-state commitments before local training, without replacing the role of DP in protecting individual student records.

Interpretability, fairness, and explanation stability

Interpretability is another major requirement for educational prediction systems, particularly when model outputs may influence student-support decisions. While complex models such as ensembles and deep networks can achieve strong predictive performance, their decision logic is often difficult for instructors, administrators, and students to inspect. This has motivated increasing interest in explainable learning analytics and fairness-aware student-performance prediction. Kesgin et al.39 proposed a fairness-aware student-performance prediction framework that combines predictive modelling with group-level fairness analysis and SHAP-based explanations. Tiukhova et al.40 examined the stability of explainable learning-analytics models and showed that feature attributions can vary across models and data splits, raising concerns about the robustness of explanations. Mai et al.41 proposed an explainable belief-rule-base model that combines expert knowledge with data-driven prediction, while Husayn et al.42 introduced an optimized ensemble for interpretable and fair student-performance prediction.

These studies show that interpretability and fairness are increasingly recognized as essential components of educational analytics. However, explanation is often treated as a post-hoc layer applied to centralized or complex models. This creates two limitations. First, explanations may be unstable across training regimes, cohorts, or model classes. Second, post-hoc interpretability does not necessarily make the underlying model auditable or easy to deploy in privacy-constrained institutional settings. By contrast, the present work uses logistic regression as an intrinsically interpretable global model. This allows feature importance to be derived directly from standardized coefficients and compared across centralized, federated, and FL + DP+PBT training regimes. The coefficient-based analysis therefore evaluates not only predictive performance but also whether the proposed privacy-preserving federated model preserves broadly similar interpretation patterns to the centralized and non-private federated baselines.

Positioning of the present work

Collectively, the reviewed literature shows that high predictive performance for at-risk and dropout detection is already achievable, and that complementary concerns such as federated training, privacy preservation, interpretability, and fairness have each been addressed to some extent. However, these dimensions are often studied separately. Centralized early-warning models typically do not address cross-institution data locality; federated education models do not consistently integrate formal DP or empirical privacy-attack evaluation; privacy-preserving learning-analytics methods often remain centralized; and explainable or fairness-aware methods typically do not operate in distributed and privacy-constrained settings.

The present study is positioned at the intersection of these strands. It does not claim to introduce a new deep-learning architecture or a fundamentally new optimization algorithm. Instead, its contribution is integrative and empirical. The proposed framework brings together cross-institution federated training, client-side DP, and a globally interpretable logistic-regression model, augmented with a PBT mechanism for added accountability. This design allows us to examine predictive utility, privacy leakage, auditability, and interpretability within a single experimental framework across three heterogeneous educational datasets. In this way, our work directly responds to the gaps identified in the related literature, offering a concrete proof of concept that competitive early-warning performance can be maintained while adding privacy-preserving training, model transparency, and participation auditability under a unified empirical framework.

Proposed method

In this section, we describe the proposed method: a privacy-preserving FL framework for predicting at-risk students across multiple schools. The framework consists of three main components – data modelling and multi-school scenario construction, a FL algorithm with DP, and a PBT protocol for verifying client participation – designed to address the practical limitations of educational data: legal/ethical restrictions on centralizing educational records, heterogeneous data distributions across schools, and requirements for both formal privacy guarantees and auditability. Data modelling and multi-school scenario construction

This subsection describes how raw records from the educational datasets are mapped to a unified at-risk prediction task and how a realistic multi-school federated scenario is constructed from them. From the raw student records, we model each instance as a feature vector representing the demographic, behavioral, and performance-related attributes, and a binary at-risk label determined by the corresponding academic outcome (e.g., grades/exam scores). The centralized dataset obtained in this way is split into a number of disjoint subsets, where each of them corresponds to a client, and contains data with a local distribution, size, and at-risk ratio, simulating a school and thus inducing a heterogeneous (non-IID) multi-school scenario. The same data modelling and partitioning process is applied to the main dataset used for the development and tuning of the overall framework in the first place, and then identically replicated on the other two datasets. This, in turn, provides the basis for the detailed descriptions of the FL, DP, and PBT in the following two subsections.

A natural choice, used in our experiments, is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\tau\:}_{1}=1$$\end{document}, which yields6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Y}_{i}^{\left(1\right)}=\left\{\begin{array}{ll}1,&\:\mathrm{\:if\:}{S}_{i}^{\left(1\right)}\le\:{\tau\:}_{1},\\\:0,&\:\mathrm{\:if\:}{S}_{i}^{\left(1\right)}>{\tau\:}_{1}.\end{array}\right.$$\end{document}

This mapping treats students in the lowest and lower-middle grade categories (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left\{\mathrm{0,1}\right\}$$\end{document}) as at risk and those in higher categories (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left\{\mathrm{2,3}\right\}$$\end{document}) as not at risk. The threshold can also be calibrated to match a desired at-risk prevalence (for example by aligning with institutional definitions or selecting a particular quantile of the grade distribution), but the above formulation provides a clear and reproducible rule.

For the Students’ Academic Performance (xAPI-Edu-Data, m=2) dataset, the raw performance is encoded directly as a categorical variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{i}^{\left(2\right)}\in\:\{\mathrm{L},\mathrm{M},\mathrm{H}\}$$\end{document}, denoting low, medium, and high academic performance, respectively. In this case, the mapping to the at-risk label is straightforward and does not require an additional threshold on a continuous scale:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Y}_{i}^{\left(2\right)}=\left\{\begin{array}{ll}1,&\:\mathrm{\:if\:}{C}_{i}^{\left(2\right)}=\mathrm{L}\\\:0,&\:\mathrm{\:if\:}{C}_{i}^{\left(2\right)}\in\:\{\mathrm{M},\mathrm{H}\}\end{array}\right.$$\end{document}

This definition is consistent with the educational intuition that only the lowest performance category should be flagged as at risk, while medium and high-performance levels are treated as acceptable or safe. It also ensures that the label construction remains purely categorical, preserving the original semantics of the dataset.

For the Students Performance in Exams dataset ( m=3), student achievement is represented by three exam scores: mathematics \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{M}_{i}^{\left(3\right)}$$\end{document}, reading \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{i}^{\left(3\right)}$$\end{document}, and writing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{W}_{i}^{\left(3\right)}$$\end{document}. We first aggregate these into a composite exam performance score, for example as a simple arithmetic mean:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{i}^{\left(3\right)}=\frac{1}{3}\left({M}_{i}^{\left(3\right)}+{R}_{i}^{\left(3\right)}+{W}_{i}^{\left(3\right)}\right)$$\end{document}

We then define an at-risk threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\tau\:}_{3}$$\end{document} on this continuous score. In our setting, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\tau\:}_{3}$$\end{document} can be chosen either as a fixed educational cutoff (e.g., 60 out of 100) or as a data-driven percentile (e.g., the q-th quantile of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left\{{S}_{i}^{\left(3\right)}\right\}$$\end{document}). The binary label is then:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Y}_{i}^{\left(3\right)}=\left\{\begin{array}{ll}1,&\:\mathrm{\:if\:}{S}_{i}^{\left(3\right)}<{\tau\:}_{3},\\\:0,&\:\mathrm{\:if\:}{S}_{i}^{\left(3\right)}\ge\:{\tau\:}_{3}.\end{array}\right.$$\end{document}

This approach yields a clear notion of “underperforming across core subjects” and allows the fraction of at-risk students to be tuned via \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\tau\:}_{3}$$\end{document} in line with realistic risk prevalence.

Given these label definitions, the prediction target of the framework in all datasets is the conditional probability of being at risk given the feature vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}$$\end{document}. In this study, this probability is estimated using the global logistic-regression classifier defined in Table 2. For dataset m, we write:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{p}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}={f}_{\theta\:}^{\left(m\right)}\left({x}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}\right)=\sigma\:\left({{w}^{\left(m\right)}}^{\mathrm{T\:}}{x}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}+{b}^{\left(m\right)}\right)\approx\:P\left({Y}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}=1\mid\:{X}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}={x}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}\right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}^{\left(m\right)}=({w}^{\left(m\right)},{b}^{\left(m\right)})$$\end{document} denotes the logistic-regression parameters for dataset m, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}^{\left(m\right)}$$\end{document} is the feature-weight vector, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{b}^{\left(m\right)}$$\end{document}is the intercept term, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:(\cdot\:)$$\end{document} is the logistic sigmoid function. A hard prediction is obtained using a decision threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\tau\:\in\:\left(\mathrm{0,1}\right)$$\end{document}:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{Y}}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}=\left\{\begin{array}{cc}1,&\:\mathrm{if\:}{p}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}\ge\:\tau\:,\\\:0,&\:\mathrm{if\:}{p}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}<\tau\:.\end{array}\right.$$\end{document}

For evaluation, we also use the continuous probability scores \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{p}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}$$\end{document} to compute ranking-based metrics such as the area under the curve (AUC) or area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. By explicitly specifying \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{g}^{\left(m\right)}(\cdot\:)$$\end{document} for each dataset and unifying the prediction target as the probability of being at risk, this subsection establishes a coherent and mathematically precise supervised-learning formulation for the subsequent federated, privacy-preserving, and verifiable training procedures.

To emulate a multi-school federated setting, the centralized dataset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{D}$$\end{document} is partitioned into K=10 disjoint subsets, each interpreted as a distinct school participating as a federated client. In practice, the assignment function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:s(\cdot\:)$$\end{document} is derived by grouping students with similar behavioral and performance profiles (e.g., attendance, study hours, assignment completion, use of digital resources, stress level), so that each resulting group corresponds to a “school” with internally coherent characteristics and externally distinct distributions. This induces a heterogeneous (non-IID) federated scenario in which schools differ in size, average performance, and at-risk prevalence.

For the primary dataset, the school assignment function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:s(\cdot\:)$$\end{document} was implemented using K-means clustering with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{K}_{1}=10$$\end{document}. Extending the federated scenario to the additional datasets

We describe how the FL scenario defined on the primary dataset is extended to the two additional datasets while keeping a single, consistent prediction model across all experimental settings. For each dataset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:m\in\:\{\mathrm{1,2},3\}$$\end{document}, let the preprocessed student records be represented as:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{D}}^{\left(m\right)}={\left\{\left({x}_{i}^{\left(m\right)},{y}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}\right)\right\}}_{i=1}^{{N}_{m}}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}\in\:{\mathbb{R}}^{{d}^{\left(m\right)}}$$\end{document} denotes the preprocessed feature vector obtained from dataset-specific cleaning, encoding, and scaling, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{y}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}\in\:\left\{\mathrm{0,1}\right\}$$\end{document} denotes the binary at-risk label defined in Sect. 3.2.1. where each subset represents a simulated school or federated client. The local dataset of client k in dataset m is defined as:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{D}}_{k}^{\left(m\right)}=\left\{\left({x}_{i}^{\left(m\right)},{y}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}\right)\in\:{\mathcal{D}}^{\left(m\right)}\mid\:{s}^{\left(m\right)}\left(i\right)=k\right\},k=1,\dots\:,{K}_{m}$$\end{document}

The resulting client datasets satisfy:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{D}}^{\left(m\right)}=\bigcup\:_{k=1}^{{K}_{m}}{\mathcal{D}}_{k}^{\left(m\right)},{\mathcal{D}}_{k}^{\left(m\right)}\cap\:{\mathcal{D}}_{j}^{\left(m\right)}={\varnothing}\mathrm{for\:}k\ne\:j$$\end{document}

The assignment function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{s}^{\left(m\right)}(\cdot\:)$$\end{document} is defined separately for each dataset according to the available institutional or cohort-level structure. For the primary dataset, students are grouped into simulated schools using behavioral and performance-related profiles, as described in Sect. 3.2.2. For the xAPI-Edu-Data dataset, school partitions are derived from available grade and section identifiers, with small groups merged when necessary to avoid extremely small clients. For the Students Performance in Exams dataset, where no natural school identifier is available, stratified random partitioning is used to preserve the at-risk ratio across simulated schools. These partitioning strategies are designed to induce heterogeneous, non-IID client distributions while keeping the same FL framework across datasets.

Across all datasets, schools, and training regimes, we use a single global logistic-regression classifier for binary at-risk prediction. The same model form is used in the centralized baseline, standard FL, and the proposed differentially private FL with PBT. This design choice avoids confounding the comparison across training regimes with changes in model architecture or capacity. For a given dataset m, the model maps a student feature vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}$$\end{document} to an estimated probability of being at risk as follows:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{p}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}={f}_{\theta\:}^{\left(m\right)}\left({x}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}\right)=\sigma\:\left({{w}^{\left(m\right)}}^{\mathrm{T\:}}{x}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}+{b}^{\left(m\right)}\right)\approx\:P\left({Y}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}=1\mid\:{X}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}={x}_{i}^{\left(m\right)}\right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}^{\left(m\right)}=({w}^{\left(m\right)},{b}^{\left(m\right)})$$\end{document} denotes the model parameters for dataset m, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}^{\left(m\right)}\in\:{\mathbb{R}}^{{d}^{\left(m\right)}}$$\end{document} is the feature-weight vector, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{b}^{\left(m\right)}$$\end{document} is the intercept term, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:(\cdot\:)$$\end{document} is the logistic sigmoid function,23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:\left(z\right)=\frac{1}{1+\mathrm{e}\mathrm{x}\mathrm{p}(-z)}$$\end{document}

In the federated setting, each school kminimizes the same binary cross-entropy objective on its local dataset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{D}}_{k}^{\left(m\right)}$$\end{document}. The server then aggregates the locally updated logistic-regression parameters using the FedAvg procedure described in Sect. 3.3. In the differentially private setting, local gradients are clipped and perturbed with Gaussian noise before aggregation, while the PBT protocol records verifiable commitments to client participation and model state at each communication round.

Specification of the global logistic-regression model used in the federated framework.

The use of logistic regression is intentional. First, it provides a transparent and auditable global model whose coefficients can be directly inspected as feature-importance indicators. Second, it reduces computational and communication overhead in the federated setting, which is important for practical deployment across schools with limited technical infrastructure. Third, by using the same simple model across centralized, federated, and privacy-preserving federated settings, the observed performance differences can be attributed to the training paradigm, privacy mechanism, and client heterogeneity rather than to differences in model complexity. The specification of the global logistic-regression model used throughout the experiments is summarized in Table 2. As shown in Table 2, the model contains no hidden layers or dataset-specific architectural components. This ensures that the comparison between centralized learning, standard FL, and the proposed FL + DP+PBT setting is not affected by changes in model capacity. Moreover, because the model is linear in the input features, the learned coefficients provide a direct and reproducible basis for the feature-importance analysis reported in the Results section.

Federated learning algorithm with DP

This section presents the training algorithm used in the proposed framework, combining standard federated optimization with a client-side DP mechanism for the logistic-regression model defined in Table 2. where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:(\cdot\:)$$\end{document} is the logistic sigmoid function. to participate in the round, for example by uniform sampling with participation rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left.{C}_{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{t}}\in\:(\mathrm{0,1}\right]$$\end{document}. and performs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:E\ge\:1$$\end{document} local epochs of mini-batch gradient-based optimization on its local objective. For a mini-batch \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{B}_{k}^{\left(t,e\right)}\subset\:{\mathcal{D}}_{k}$$\end{document} at local step \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(t,e\right)$$\end{document}, the non-private local update is:32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{k}^{\left(t,e+1\right)}={\theta\:}_{k}^{\left(t,e\right)}-\eta\:\frac{1}{\mid\:{B}_{k}^{\left(t,e\right)}\mid\:}\sum\:_{(x,y)\in\:{B}_{k}^{\left(t,e\right)}}{\nabla\:}_{\theta\:}\mathcal{l}\left({f}_{{\theta\:}_{k}^{\left(t,e\right)}}\left(x\right),y\right)$$\end{document}

To preserve the privacy of individual students in the federated setting, we integrate a client-side DP mechanism based on the DP-SGD paradigm. We adopt the standard notion of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(\epsilon\:,\delta\:\right)$$\end{document}-DP: a randomized mechanism \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{M}$$\end{document} acting on datasets satisfies \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(\epsilon\:,\delta\:\right)$$\end{document}-DP if, for any pair of neighbouring datasets Dand \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}^{{\prime\:}}$$\end{document}differing in one student record and for any measurable set of outputs S,36\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Pr\left[\mathcal{M}\right(D)\in\:S]\le\:{e}^{\epsilon\:}Pr\left[\mathcal{M}\right({D}^{{\prime\:}})\in\:S]+\delta\:$$\end{document}

In the proposed framework, each school implements a local randomized mechanism that maps its local dataset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{D}}_{k}$$\end{document} to a noisy model update before transmission to the server. For each mini-batch \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{B}_{k}^{\left(t,e\right)}$$\end{document}, school kfirst computes per-example gradients with respect to the logistic-regression parameters:37\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{g}_{i}^{\left(t,e\right)}={\nabla\:}_{\theta\:}\mathcal{l}\left({f}_{{\theta\:}_{k}^{\left(t,e\right)}}\left({x}_{i}^{\left(k\right)}\right),{y}_{i}^{\left(k\right)}\right),\left({x}_{i}^{\left(k\right)},{y}_{i}^{\left(k\right)}\right)\in\:{B}_{k}^{\left(t,e\right)}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:>0$$\end{document} is the noise multiplier and Iis the identity matrix with the same dimension as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:$$\end{document}. The private local update is then performed as:41\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{k}^{\left(t,e+1\right)}={\theta\:}_{k}^{\left(t,e\right)}-\eta\:{\stackrel{\sim}{g}}_{k}^{\left(t,e\right)}$$\end{document}

After all local steps in round tare completed, the final local parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{k}^{\left(t+1\right)}$$\end{document} incorporate the noise added during local training. These noisy local parameters are transmitted to the server and aggregated using the same FedAvg rule:42\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}^{\left(t+1\right)}=\sum\:_{k\in\:{\mathcal{S}}^{\left(t\right)}}{\alpha\:}_{k}^{\left(t\right)}{\theta\:}_{k}^{\left(t+1\right)}$$\end{document}

where:43\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{k}^{\left(t\right)}=\frac{{n}_{k}}{\sum\:_{j\in\:{\mathcal{S}}^{\left(t\right)}}{n}_{j}}$$\end{document}

From the server’s perspective, the aggregation step remains unchanged; however, the local updates have already been randomized at the client side before leaving each school. Under standard DP-SGD accounting assumptions, the sequence of clipped and noisy local updates yields an overall \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(\epsilon\:,\delta\:\right)$$\end{document}-DP guarantee for each student’s contribution to the local training data. The effective privacy budget \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\epsilon\:$$\end{document}is controlled by the noise multiplier \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document}, the clipping bound \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{\mathrm{c}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{p}}$$\end{document}, the mini-batch size, the number of local optimization steps, and the number of communication rounds. In the experiments, multiple noise multipliers are considered to examine the privacy–utility trade-off in terms of both predictive performance and the resulting privacy budget.

This DP-FL procedure is used consistently across the three educational datasets and the corresponding simulated school partitions. Because the underlying classifier remains the same logistic-regression model in all centralized, federated, and differentially private settings, the observed differences in performance can be attributed to the effects of decentralization, non-IID client distributions, and privacy-preserving noise injection rather than to changes in model architecture.

The procedure is also compatible with the PBT protocol described in Sect. 3.4. The cryptographic commitments used in that protocol operate only on model states, client identifiers, and round metadata, while the DP mechanism ensures that the model updates transmitted from schools are already randomized before aggregation. As a result, the framework combines decentralized optimization, client-side DP, and verifiable participation logging within a single federated at-risk prediction pipeline.

Proof-before-train protocol

The PBT protocol is designed to provide a verifiable record of client participation in each federated communication round before any local training is performed. In the proposed framework, this mechanism does not modify the learning objective or the DP mechanism. Instead, it operates as an auditability layer on top of federated training by binding each participating school to a specific global model state and communication round before local optimization begins.

At the beginning of round t, the server broadcasts the current global logistic-regression parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}^{\left(t\right)}$$\end{document} to the selected set of schools \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{S}}^{\left(t\right)}$$\end{document}. Before performing local training, each participating school \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:k\in\:{\mathcal{S}}^{\left(t\right)}$$\end{document} computes a cryptographic commitment:44\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\Gamma\:}}_{k}^{\left(t\right)}=H\left({\theta\:}^{\left(t\right)}\left\| k \right. \left\| t \right. \left\|{r}_{k}\right.^{\left(t\right)}\right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:H(\cdot\:)$$\end{document} denotes a cryptographic hash function, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}^{\left(t\right)}$$\end{document} is the current global model state, kis the client identifier, tis the communication-round index, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{k}^{\left(t\right)}$$\end{document} is a client-generated nonce used to prevent replay or reuse of old commitments. The school sends \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\Gamma\:}}_{k}^{\left(t\right)}$$\end{document} to the server before starting local training. The server stores this commitment together with the round metadata as part of the audit log.

After the commitment has been registered, school kperforms local training on its own data using either the non-private FedAvg update or the client-side DP-SGD update described in Sect. 3.3. When the local update is completed, the school sends the updated model parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{k}^{\left(t+1\right)}$$\end{document}, together with the corresponding metadata needed to verify the previously submitted commitment. The server verifies that the received update is associated with the same client, communication round, and global model state for which the pre-training commitment was registered.

Algorithm 1 summarizes the PBT procedure executed at each federated communication round. The key idea is that each selected school must register a cryptographic commitment to the current global model state before performing any local training. This commitment binds the client identity, communication-round index, global logistic-regression parameters, and a fresh client-generated nonce into a single hash value. After local training, the server verifies that the submitted update corresponds to the previously registered commitment before accepting it for aggregation. In this way, PBT provides an audit trail of client participation and model-state consistency without requiring access to raw student records. The protocol operates only on model parameters and round metadata, and therefore does not interfere with the client-side DP-SGD mechanism used to protect individual student records.

This procedure provides three auditability properties. First, it records which schools participated in each communication round. Second, it binds each local update to the global model state distributed at the beginning of that round. Third, it reduces the risk of unregistered or post-hoc updates being accepted by the server. Importantly, the protocol does not require access to raw student records and does not weaken the DP mechanism, because commitments are computed only from model states and metadata.

The PBT protocol is lightweight. Its computational cost is dominated by hashing model-state metadata once per participating client and communication round, which is negligible compared with local model optimization. Therefore, the protocol improves verifiability and accountability in the federated training process while introducing minimal computational overhead.

Results

In this section, we present the experimental results of the proposed framework on the three education datasets. We first compare the centralized baseline, vanilla FL, and FL with DP on the main dataset, with regards to classification performance, convergence behavior, and privacy–utility trade-off. Subsequently, the same set of experiments are repeated on the two supplementary datasets. We then evaluate the impact of the PBT protocol on test accuracy, stability over communication rounds, and computational overhead.

Experimental setup

We evaluate the proposed framework on three educational datasets after transforming them into a unified binary at-risk prediction task, where y=1denotes at-risk students, i.e., students with low performance or performance below a predefined threshold, and y=0 denotes not-at-risk students, i.e., students with acceptable or high performance, as defined in Sect. 3.2.1. Cross-dataset evaluation on xAPI and exams datasets

In addition to the main merged cohort, we further examined whether the proposed FL-AtRisk-DP-PBT method can be applied to at-risk prediction scenarios involving educational datasets with different characteristics. To this end, we replicate the entire training and evaluation procedure on two external benchmarks: the xAPI-Edu-Data click-stream dataset and the Students Performance in Exams dataset. In both cases, the original multi-level performance indicators are mapped to a binary at-risk label, and the same logistic regression model specification, federated partitioning strategy, and DP-SGD configuration as in Sect. 4.2 are employed. This cross-dataset evaluation, therefore, examines the consistency of the proposed framework under changes in data modality, namely behavioral logs versus exam scores, sample size, and label definition, while keeping the learning framework itself fixed. The detailed configuration and summary test metrics for the proposed method (M3) on these two datasets are reported in Table 4 and can be directly compared with the primary-dataset results in Table 3.

Table 4 shows that on xAPI-Edu-Data, which is a relatively small click-stream dataset with approximately 480 students and rich behavioral features, the proposed FL-AtRisk-DP-PBT method maintains high predictive performance despite the non-IID client partitions and the added DP noise. Using five simulated schools and a client participation rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{t}}=0.6$$\end{document}, the model achieves approximately 93.0% test accuracy, an F1-score of about 0.92 for the at-risk class, and an AUC around 0.97. Precision and recall for the at-risk label remain high, approximately 0.93–0.94 and 0.91–0.92, respectively, indicating that the model identifies a large proportion of at-risk students while maintaining a low rate of false alarms. Because prior studies on xAPI differ in task formulation, feature processing, model type, and evaluation protocol, direct numerical comparison should be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, the obtained results suggest that the proposed framework remains competitive while also incorporating client-side DP-SGD and PBT commitments. The approximately 2%-point drops in accuracy and F1-score relative to the centralized M1 model on the same dataset, and the approximately one-point reduction in AUC, indicate a modest utility cost associated with the privacy-preserving and verifiable federated setting.

When client-side DP-SGD and the PBT protocol are enabled (M3), performance remains high but exhibits a further, controlled reduction (accuracy 0.930, F1-score 0.920, AUC 0.970). The gap between M1 and M3 on xAPI is therefore on the order of 2% points in accuracy and F1, and one% point in AUC, which is consistent with the stronger sensitivity of this relatively small dataset to added optimization noise and privacy perturbations. A very similar pattern is observed on the Students Performance in Exams dataset (Fig. 3b), albeit with slightly higher absolute scores. The centralized model M1 achieves 0.960 accuracy, 0.950 F1-score, and an AUC of 0.990, establishing a strong upper bound on this cohort as well. Federated training without DP (M2) again yields only a small degradation (accuracy 0.950, F1-score 0.940, AUC 0.980), confirming that decentralization alone does not substantially harm performance.

To determine whether FL provides a practical advantage over purely school-isolated training, we added a local-only baseline (Table 5). In this setting, each simulated school trained an independent logistic-regression model using only its own local training subset, without parameter sharing, server-side aggregation, DP, or PBT logging. Each local model was evaluated on the corresponding school-level held-out test subset, and the local-only results are reported as mean ± standard deviation across schools. This baseline directly addresses whether individual schools can achieve comparable performance without federated collaboration.

As shown in Table 5, the local-only models achieve usable performance but remain consistently below FedAvg across all datasets. On the primary dataset, FedAvg improves accuracy from 0.9584 ± 0.0176 to 0.9861 and F1-score from 0.9557 ± 0.0208 to 0.9863. The gap is larger on xAPI-Edu-Data, where the local client datasets are smaller and more variable. These findings indicate that isolated school-level models can be informative, but FL provides a more accurate and stable global model by sharing information through model parameters while keeping raw student records local. Thus, Table 5 supports the practical motivation for using FL rather than training separate models independently at each school.

Outcome-proxy sensitivity analysis after removing direct performance-related features.

Because some full-feature settings include variables closely related to the construction of the at-risk label, we further conducted an outcome-proxy sensitivity analysis (Table 7). The purpose of this analysis was to evaluate whether the framework retains predictive utility after removing direct or near-direct performance indicators. For the primary dataset, ExamScore was removed in addition to the label-defining FinalGrade. For the Students Performance in Exams dataset, the mathematics, reading, and writing scores used to construct the composite performance label were removed. For xAPI-Edu-Data, direct performance-category variables were excluded while retaining behavioral and engagement indicators. The remaining variables were treated as reduced early-warning feature sets, and all models were re-evaluated using the same repeated-seed protocol as in Table 6.

As shown in Table 7, removing outcome-proxy variables reduces predictive performance across all datasets, particularly on the primary and Exams datasets. This confirms that the very high full-feature performance partly reflects the presence of strong performance-related predictors. Nevertheless, the reduced-feature models retain non-trivial predictive utility, indicating that behavioral, contextual, and engagement variables still contain useful early-warning signal when direct performance indicators are excluded. Importantly, the relative ordering of centralized, federated, and privacy-preserving federated training remains broadly consistent under the reduced-feature setting. These results provide a more conservative estimate of framework performance under stricter early-warning conditions and clarify that the full-feature results should be interpreted as an upper-bound setting rather than as purely pre-outcome prediction.

Impact of proof-before-train on performance and overhead

To more clearly illustrate the practical value of the proposed PBT mechanism, in addition to cryptographic reasoning, we must also bound the loss in model quality and increase in computational cost incurred. To this end, we compare federated training with client-side DP-SGD (no PBT) to the full FL-AtRisk-DP-PBT setup (with PBT), for all three datasets in this subsection. Given that PBT operates at the protocol level (per-round, per-client commitments to the global model state), it should in principle leave optimization dynamics and the resulting decision boundary nearly unchanged, while only incurring a small additional cost for hashing (and optionally signing) messages. Figure 4 summarizes empirical support for this claim, reporting accuracy, F1-score, AUC, and recall for the at-risk class, in both settings.

Figure 4a shows that test accuracy is essentially preserved when PBT is enabled. On the primary dataset, the accuracy decreases only from 0.981 to 0.980, i.e., by 0.001 (0.1% points), which is well within normal run-to-run variability. On the xAPI-Edu-Data and Exams datasets the drops are slightly larger but still modest—0.940→0.930 and 0.950→0.940, respectively—corresponding to approximately 1% point per dataset. The same pattern holds for the F1-score in Fig. 4b: on the primary dataset, F1 changes from 0.982 to 0.980 (0.2% points), while on xAPI and Exams it decreases by about 0.01 (0.930→0.920 and 0.940→0.930). These results indicate that, even in the more challenging external cohorts, the PBT protocol does not materially degrade the overall quality of at-risk predictions; the central precision–recall trade-off of the underlying DP-SGD training remains essentially intact.

The ROC-based view in Fig. 4c and the recall-focused view in Fig. 4d provide a more fine-grained perspective. For the Students Performance in Exams dataset, Fig. 5c again shows a consistent pattern across the three training regimes. The largest coefficients are associated with the core achievement-related variables, particularly mathematics, reading, and writing scores, while demographic and support-related variables show smaller but non-negligible contributions. For each dataset, the rank agreement was computed between the proposed FL + DP+PBT model (M3) and the two baselines, namely centralized logistic regression (M1) and non-private FedAvg logistic regression (M2). In summary, Fig. 5 shows that the proposed FL + DP+PBT model preserves a broadly similar feature-importance structure to the centralized and non-private federated baselines. The magnitude of the coefficients is generally reduced under DP-enhanced federated training, but the most influential variables remain concentrated around educationally meaningful signals such as exam performance, engagement, attendance, and study-related behaviors. This supports the use of logistic regression as an interpretable global model in the proposed framework.

At the same time, the coefficient analysis also clarifies an important limitation of the current evaluation. In datasets where the at-risk label is defined using performance-related variables, features such as exam scores can become strong outcome proxies. Therefore, the high predictive performance observed in these settings should not be interpreted as evidence that the model fully predicts future risk from early behavioral signals alone. Rather, the results demonstrate that the proposed federated, differentially private, and auditable training pipeline can preserve predictive and interpretive patterns under the current label definitions and feature sets. Future work should further evaluate the framework under stricter early-warning conditions, for example by excluding direct outcome-related variables and relying only on pre-outcome behavioral, demographic, or engagement features.

Taken together, the cross-dataset results and the updated feature-importance analysis suggest that the proposed framework can maintain high predictive performance while preserving interpretable decision patterns across different educational datasets. However, the practical deployment of such a system requires careful dataset-specific validation, particularly with respect to label construction, feature leakage, fairness, calibration, and the availability of genuinely early indicators.

Membership-inference attack evaluation

To complement the formal DP mechanism with an empirical privacy assessment, we conducted a black-box membership-inference attack (MIA). The goal of this attack was to determine whether a given student record had been used during model training. For each target model, member samples were drawn from the training set, while non-member samples were drawn from the held-out test set. The attacker was assumed to observe only the model output probabilities and did not have access to raw school-level data, local gradients, client updates, or internal model-training states.

For each sample, we constructed attack features from the target model output, including the predicted probability assigned to the true class, prediction confidence, binary cross-entropy loss, and output entropy. A logistic-regression attack classifier was then trained to distinguish member from non-member samples. The attack was evaluated against the centralized logistic-regression model (M1), the non-private FedAvg model (M2), and the proposed FL + DP+PBT model (M3). For M3, we evaluated two DP noise multipliers, σ = 0.5 and σ = 1.0, to quantify the trade-off between predictive utility and empirical membership leakage under stronger privacy perturbation. Discussion

We discuss the empirical findings of Sect. 4 in relation to the study objectives and the broader literature on FL, privacy-preserving analytics, and educational data mining. The main focus is on the extent to which a simple and interpretable logistic-regression model can retain predictive utility when trained under federated, differentially private, and PBT settings.

The use of logistic regression in this study was intentional. Rather than maximizing predictive performance through a more complex black-box architecture, we selected a linear probabilistic model to support transparency, coefficient-based interpretation, auditability of model behavior, and low computational and communication overhead in federated deployment. These properties are particularly important in educational settings, where predictive models may inform student-support decisions and therefore require clear justification, institutional trust, and reproducible interpretation.

In this section, we interpret the observed performance trends across centralized, standard federated, and FL + DP+PBT training regimes; discuss the privacy–utility and auditability trade-offs introduced by DP and PBT logging; and examine the practical implications and limitations of the proposed framework. We also highlight important constraints of the current evaluation, including simulated school partitions, dataset-specific label definitions, potential outcome-proxy features, and the need for further validation under more realistic early-warning conditions.

In the context of FL for educational prediction, the landscape summarized in Table 9 shows that existing approaches each address only a subset of the requirements we target. Comparative summary of FL–based approaches for educational prediction tasks.

Grade-prediction pipelines based on FL with SVM/ensembles28 and entropy-adaptive DP–FedAvg29 demonstrate that federated training can match or slightly exceed centralized baselines at around 0.93–0.99 accuracy, yet they are typically confined to single-course or single-domain settings and do not consider cross-dataset generalization or verifiable training. Other lines of work explore different axes of the design space: PP-FL + XAI adds SHAP-based explanations to dropout models30, FedEL focuses on CO–PO mapping in IoE infrastructures32, and FL-EmotionEdu brings FL into multimodal emotion detection33, but these methods either lack DP, target tasks other than early at-risk identification, or depend on complex model stacks and specialized sensors.

By contrast, our proposed FL-AtRisk-DP-PBT framework in Table 9 explicitly combines four properties that are rarely addressed together in prior work: (1) near–centralized predictive performance on a dedicated at-risk classification task (with AUC up to 0.9998 and F1 in the low-to-high 0.98–0.99 range on the 14k-student primary dataset), (2) client-side DP via DP-SGD, (3) PBT audit logging for verifiable participation and model states, and (4) a single, globally shared logistic-regression model whose coefficients are directly interpretable across three heterogeneous datasets.

Relative to FLPADPM27, FecMap31, and FL-EmotionEdu33, our method trades architectural complexity for transparency while still delivering AUC ≈ 0.97–0.99 on xAPI and Exams, narrowing the centralized–federated performance gap to less than 1–2% points. Compared with FL + ensembles28 and EADP-FedAvg29, we move beyond single-course evaluations and central DP to a setting where raw data remain local, privacy is enforced at the client, and all updates are auditable, while still operating with a model that instructors and administrators can readily interpret and scrutinize.

Beyond these education-focused FL pipelines, cross-domain advances also point to promising modeling directions. For example, temporal-network epidemic models for human and computational contagion44 show how complex interaction graphs and time-resolved connectivity can be exploited to capture propagation patterns, suggesting that at-risk signals and support interventions could likewise be modeled as diffusions over school-level social and digital networks. Similarly, fusion-based learning frameworks45,46 demonstrate the benefits of multimodal sensing and feature-level fusion; integrating analogous fusion mechanisms into FL-AtRisk-DP-PBT is a promising avenue for extending our framework toward richer, multi-source student-support analytics.

Practical implications and limitations

The results of this study suggest several practical implications for privacy-aware educational early-warning systems. First, the use of a single logistic-regression model makes the proposed framework computationally lightweight and directly interpretable, which is important for educational institutions that may lack the infrastructure required to deploy and maintain deep or highly specialized architectures. Lightweight predictive architectures have also been emphasized in other sensitive human-centered prediction settings, where computational efficiency, deployability, and model usability are important alongside predictive performance47. In the educational context, these properties are especially important because early-warning systems may inform student-support decisions and therefore require transparency, reproducibility, and institutional trust.

Second, the proposed framework is suitable for incremental and deployment-oriented extensions. Because only model parameters are exchanged during federated training, the framework can support cross-school collaboration while keeping raw student records local. The use of a simple global model also reduces communication overhead and makes the system easier to update when new cohorts, schools, or institutional partners are added. In practice, this could support periodic model updating, dashboard integration, and institution-level monitoring, provided that privacy accounting, access control, and audit logs are maintained throughout the model lifecycle. This design is particularly relevant for institutions that must balance predictive analytics with data-minimization principles, internal governance policies, and privacy expectations from students and families.

At the same time, several limitations should be acknowledged. The school partitions used in this study are simulated rather than obtained from naturally participating institutions. Although the partitions were designed to induce non-IID client distributions, real schools may differ more strongly in curriculum, grading policy, resource availability, student demographics, institutional policy, and data quality. Moreover, the at-risk labels are dataset-specific and partly depend on performance-related variables. As shown in the reduced-feature sensitivity analysis, removing direct outcome-proxy variables leads to lower but still meaningful predictive performance. This indicates that the full-feature results should be interpreted as an upper-bound setting, whereas stricter early-warning deployments require validation using only pre-outcome behavioral, demographic, and engagement indicators.

Another limitation concerns privacy, governance, and security evaluation. The membership-inference experiments provide empirical evidence that client-side DP-SGD reduces membership leakage, but they do not exhaust all possible attacks against federated systems. Recent educational FL work emphasizes that DP-based FL should be evaluated not only as a technical mechanism but also as part of a broader compliance and governance framework, including data minimization, consent, institutional accountability, and auditability48. In addition, recent reviews of privacy preservation in FL highlight remaining challenges such as heterogeneous clients, privacy–utility–robustness trade-offs, adaptive adversaries, secure aggregation, and stronger attack-based evaluation protocols49. Therefore, future work should evaluate the proposed framework on naturally partitioned multi-institutional datasets, include additional privacy and robustness attacks, assess fairness and calibration across demographic groups, and explore adaptive privacy mechanisms that tune the noise level according to client size, risk profile, and institutional policy.

Conclusion

The paper introduced FL-AtRisk-DP-PBT: a federated, privacy-preserving, and verifiable academic at-risk prediction framework across multiple schools. We trained a single global logistic-regression model in three regimes: centralized learning, standard FL, and FL with client-side DP and PBT audit logging. The use of logistic regression was intentional, as it provides coefficient-based interpretability, supports auditability, and reduces computational and communication overhead in federated deployment. We report results on a primary dataset of 14,003 students partitioned into 10 simulated schools, as well as on two external benchmarks. On the primary cohort, the centralized model achieves 99.14% test accuracy, F1 = 0.9915, and AUC = 0.9998; the corresponding federated and DP-enhanced variants yield 98.61%/0.9863/0.9993 and 98.00%/0.9802/0.9992, respectively. These results suggest that, under the current experimental setting, decentralization and privacy-preserving training introduce only a limited reduction in predictive performance relative to the centralized reference model.

Cross-dataset experiments on xAPI-Edu-Data and Students Performance in Exams showed similar trends across datasets with different feature spaces and label definitions. Under FL-AtRisk-DP-PBT, the xAPI model achieves approximately 93% accuracy, F1 ≈ 0.92, and AUC ≈ 0.97; the Exams model achieves approximately 94% accuracy, F1 ≈ 0.93, and AUC ≈ 0.98. These findings indicate that the proposed pipeline can maintain competitive performance while keeping student records local and adding privacy-preserving and auditability mechanisms. Because the global model is linear, feature weights remain easy to audit. The feature-importance analysis showed broadly similar coefficient patterns across centralized, FedAvg, and FL + DP+PBT models, with influential variables mainly related to academic performance, engagement, attendance, and study-related behaviors. However, in datasets where the at-risk label is partly defined using performance-related variables, features such as exam scores may act as strong outcome proxies. Therefore, the high predictive performance should be interpreted within the scope of the label definitions and feature sets used in this study. Our ablation of the PBT mechanism shows that cryptographic commitments can be added on top of federated DP-SGD with only a small additional performance reduction and modest computational overhead. FL-AtRisk-DP-PBT provides a transparent and computationally lightweight framework for privacy-preserving student at-risk prediction, but further validation is needed before real-world deployment. Future work should evaluate the framework on naturally partitioned multi-institutional datasets, stricter early-warning settings without direct outcome-related features, and additional analyses of privacy leakage, fairness, calibration, and robustness.
