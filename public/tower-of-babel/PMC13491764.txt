Recent progress of plant-derived extracellular vesicle-like nanoparticles integrated with biomaterials for skin repair
Authors: Siqi Wu, Yi Zheng, Xiang Lian, Yahong Chen, Chuandong Wang, Peng Xu, Kai Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The skin, serving as a crucial physical and immunological barrier, faces significant regenerative challenges after injury or dysfunction. Recently, plant-derived extracellular vesicle-like nanoparticles (PELNs) have emerged as a highly potent, cell-free therapeutic strategy for skin tissue engineering, offering rich bioactive components, low immunogenicity, and inherent biocompatibility. However, PELNs face limitations such as poor stability, weak targeting ability, and rapid clearance, hindering their clinical applications. The latest progress in integrating or engineering PELNs with functional biomaterial delivery systems provides a promising strategy to overcome these challenges. This review systematically explores the biological characteristics of PELNs, emphasizing the integration and engineering strategies with advanced biomaterials to enhance their therapeutic effects. Firstly, the synergistic mechanisms of PELN-biomaterial composites across diverse dermatological applications, including wound healing, skin photoaging, and inflammatory skin diseases were reviewed. Secondly, the corresponding mechanisms of immune microenvironment remodeling, angiogenesis, and the restoration of redox homeostasis were also summarized. Finally, the clinical translation and regulatory landscape with challenges and perspectives of PELN-biomaterial composites were analyzed.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Skin is the largest organ of the human body and an important barrier against external environmental factors. It plays a key role in physical and immune system [1–3]. However, factors like UV radiation, pollution, and high blood sugar can damage the skin and weaken its ability to heal. Therefore, finding ways to keep skin healthy and speed up its regeneration is essential for overall health [4, 5]. Conventional therapeutic strategies for complex skin disorders, such as chronic wounds or severe inflammatory conditions, often rely on growth factors, corticosteroids, or immunosuppressants. But these methods are expensive, rapid degradation, and can cause serious side effects over time. This makes it urgent to find safer and more effective alternatives [6, 7].

In recent decades, studies have shown that extracellular vesicles derived from patients have significant effects in skin repair. However, the availability of patient-derived extracellular vesicles is limited, and the clinical application of animal-derived EVs (such as those from bovine milk or cell cultures) is often constrained by high production costs, potential immunogenicity, and ethical concerns [8, 9]. As a result, plant-derived extracellular vesicle-like nanoparticles (PELNs) have emerged as a highly attractive alternative. Capitalizing on abundant and sustainable natural resources, PELNs offer a more accessible and cost-effective solution for skin regeneration, exhibiting remarkable potential in facilitating skin repair [10, 11]. As a natural composite carrier, they can transport various bioactive molecules. Due to their excellent biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and inherent therapeutic properties, PELNs are rapidly gaining prominence in regenerative medicine [12, 13]. They have shown efficacy in accelerating diabetic wound closure, alleviating inflammatory conditions like psoriasis, and mitigating UV-induced photoaging.

However, the application of PELNs still faces challenges such as a short lifespan, poor targeting and low survival in the complex skin environment, these issues limit their clinical application. To solve these problems, researchers focused on integrating PELNs with functional biomaterials to enhance their stability and therapeutic efficacy [14]. Advanced platforms including gelatin methacrylate, hyaluronic acid, chitosan, and metal-organic frameworks facilitate prolonged tissue retention and controlled cargo release [15, 16]. Beyond acting as carriers, these materials provide extra functions such as antibacterial activity, electrical conductivity, and UV blocking. Together with the healing potential of PELNs, they create a better microenvironment for skin repair [17–19]. In addition, engineering strategies like surface modification and functionalization can enhance PELNs targeting and stability. This leads to more precise delivery and better therapeutic results, while reducing side effects on the surrounding healthy skin [20].

This review summarizes the recent progress in combining and engineering PELNs with biomaterials for skin repair and discusses the current limitations and prospects of this field. For this purpose, this review is organized as follows: (1) the biological properties of PELNs; (2) the integration and modification strategies, such as the use of hydrogels, microneedles, and nanoparticles; (3) the synergistic mechanisms of PELN-biomaterial composites in wound healing, photoaging, and inflammatory skin diseases; and (4) the translational opportunities, current limitations, and future challenges in this field.

Biogenesis of PELNs

PELNs biogenesis involves tightly controlled mechanisms for vesicle formation and secretion, which can be categorized into three primary pathways: the multivesicular body (MVB) pathway, the exocyst-positive organelle (EXPO) pathway and the vacuolar pathway (Fig. 1) [21].

Among them, the MVB pathway is the primary route. It begins with inward budding to form intraluminal vesicles and early endosomes. These vesicles progressively collect cargo by interacting with the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi network, and mitochondria before maturing into late endosomes or MVBs. Once formed, MVBs have two fates: they can fuse with lysosomes to degrade cargo, or they can be transported to the plasma membrane for vesicle release. This transport and fusion process is precisely coordinated by microtubules, actin filaments, and Rab family proteins [22].

Plants also utilize the EXPO pathway. In this route, membrane-bound organelles fuse with the plasma membrane to release single-membrane vesicles. This pathway is essential for supporting cell wall formation and regulating the extracellular environment [23–26];

In addition, the vacuolar pathway plays a critical role in plant defense. In this mechanism, MVB contents are released through the fusion of the vacuole with the plasma membrane. For example, when Arabidopsis is infected by Pst DC3000, it uses this pathway to release antimicrobial proteins and hydrolytic enzymes into the extracellular space to combat the pathogen [27].

Despite these described pathways, the biogenesis of PELNs remains a subject of ongoing debate. While the MVB and EXPO pathways are well-documented, it is still unclear how plants selectively sort specific cargoes into these vesicles under different stress conditions. Furthermore, most current knowledge is adapted from animal exosome models. However, plant cells have unique structures such as cell walls and large vacuoles, which may fundamentally alter these secretion mechanisms [24, 28].

Isolation and Identification of PELNs

Isolation of PELNs

While PELNs and mammalian extracellular vesicles share similar isolation methods, PELNs offer several unique advantages in the purification process. First, plant materials are widely available and inexpensive, allowing for the large-scale extraction of vesicles. Second, using edible plants avoids the risk of zoonotic pathogen contamination, making the process safer for therapeutic use. Finally, the high concentration of PELNs in many plant juices simplifies the initial steps of isolation, providing a significantly higher yield compared to mammalian sources [29–31].

At present, multiple approaches have been developed for isolating and purifying PELNs. Each method has its own strengths and limitations; therefore, combining several techniques is often the best way to achieve high purity and optimal performance (Fig. 2). The currently used methods mainly include centrifugation, ultrafiltration, immunoaffinity-based isolation, microfluidics, and polymer-based precipitation [32]. Centrifugation is the most widely used strategy, relying on differential and density gradient centrifugation to exploit differences in size and density. Differential centrifugation is straightforward and tends to minimize mechanical damage and vesicle loss, but it usually delivers lower purity, whereas density gradient centrifugation yields higher-purity vesicles at the expense of a more complex, time-consuming procedure and higher requirements for equipment and technical expertise [33, 34].

Ultrafiltration separates vesicles using membrane filters with defined pore sizes and is easy to operate, faster than ultracentrifugation, and often provides higher yields with lower levels of co-isolated proteins; however, membrane clogging may cause sample loss, and factors such as temperature and solution viscosity can affect efficiency [35, 36].

Immunoaffinity-based isolation leverages antigen-antibody interactions (e.g., immunomagnetic beads) to achieve high selectivity and to enrich specific PELN subpopulations, but it is expensive, unsuitable for large-scale processing, and may disrupt vesicle structure and function [37]. Antibody residues can also interfere with downstream analyses and potentially lead to missed subpopulations.

Microfluidics separates vesicles by guiding samples through chip channels with specialized microstructures, using tailored flow patterns and microchannels to sort vesicles by size, charge, or surface markers while reducing the need for extensive ultracentrifugation, yet it typically requires specialized and costly equipment, offers low sampling efficiency, and suffers from frequent channel blockages [38].

Polymer-based precipitation (e.g., PEG) isolates vesicles through hydrophobic interactions that reduce PELNs solubility and induce precipitation during centrifugation; this approach is simple, cost-effective, and efficient, but it lacks specificity, often leaves polymer residues that may compromise downstream analyses, and generally produces lower purity, making additional purification steps necessary [39].

Identification of PELNs

According to the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) guidelines, PELNs characterization should include morphology, particle concentration, size distribution, and molecular composition [37]. Morphology and size are typically assessed by electron microscopy, while size distribution and particle concentration are commonly measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS) and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) (Fig. 2g) [40]. Although DLS is widely used, NTA is increasingly favored due to its higher resolution and its ability to quantify both size and concentration, making it more reliable for concentration determination. Across different PELNs samples, zeta potentials are generally negative (about − 10 to -30 mV), suggesting good suspension stability; this surface electrostatic property also influences vesicle interactions with biological components [41].

However, the reproducibility of these measurements remains a major challenge. Reported size, concentration, and zeta potential can vary substantially depending on isolation method, sample purity, buffer conditions, dilution, instrument settings, and data-processing procedures [42, 43]. DLS is particularly sensitive to larger particles and aggregates, whereas NTA results depend strongly on particle concentration and operator-defined acquisition parameters (Table 2) [44]. Together with the lack of standardized plant-specific EV markers and the frequent co-isolation of non-vesicular plant-derived nanoparticles, these factors limit cross-study comparability [45]. Therefore, current PELNs characterization data should be interpreted cautiously, and greater methodological standardization is needed.

Challenges in reproducibility and standardization

As mentioned above, a significant hurdle in PELNs research is the lack of reproducibility across different studies. Several factors contribute to this inconsistency: (1) Plant Source Variability: The molecular cargo of PELNs changes based on the plant’s growth conditions (e.g., soil, light, and season), age, and tissue type. PELNs isolated from the same plant species in different laboratories may vary significantly; (2) Methodological Inconsistency: Small changes in isolation parameters, such as centrifuge rotor type, temperature, and duration, can alter the yield and purity of PELNs. Currently, there is no “gold standard” protocol that is followed globally; (3) Characterization Settings: Techniques like NTA are highly sensitive to camera levels and detection thresholds. Without standardized settings, size and concentration measurements are difficult to compare; (4) Storage and Handling: PELNs are sensitive to freeze thaw cycles. Differences in buffer composition and storage temperatures can lead to vesicle aggregation or cargo degradation [46].

To improve reproducibility, the field must adopt standardized reporting guidelines, like the MISEV (Minimal Information for Studies of EVs) standards used in mammalian research. Detailed reporting of plant growth conditions and isolation parameters is essential for consistent results.

Analysis of bioactive components in PELNs

Nuclear acids

Nucleic acids, particularly small RNAs, are key contributors to PELNs bioactivity, although their effects are unlikely to occur independently of other vesicular cargos such as proteins, lipids, and metabolites. Instead, these components may act together to influence vesicle stability, uptake, and downstream signaling in recipient cells. Among vesicular RNAs, small RNAs of approximately 18–30 nt, especially miRNAs and siRNAs, have attracted considerable attention because of their potential regulatory activity [13, 47].

High-throughput sequencing has revealed diverse and source-dependent small RNA profiles in PELNs. These miRNAs regulate skin injury repair through three primary mechanisms: reducing inflammation, balancing immune responses, and promoting extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling [48, 49].

First, PELN-associated miRNAs suppress pro-inflammatory signaling by targeting key pathways, such as NF-κB and JAK/STAT [50, 51]. Second, they help maintain inflammatory homeostasis by modulating immune cells, particularly by inhibiting overactive neutrophil responses [52]. Finally, some miRNAs directly influence collagen metabolism and ECM remodeling, which are essential for tissue restoration [53].

At the molecular level, these miRNAs function through the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC). They either trigger mRNA cleavage (when the sequence matches perfectly) or block translation (when the match is partial) to control the expression of genes involved in skin healing [54, 55]. However, in many cases, mechanistic evidence remains incomplete, and conclusions are often based on predicted targets, correlative expression changes, or simplified in vitro models [56]. Therefore, although small RNAs represent promising candidate effectors of PELNs bioactivity, their roles remain plausible but incompletely validated, and future studies should place greater emphasis on dose-response relationships, cargo stoichiometry, intracellular delivery efficiency, and rigorous functional validation in vivo.

Proteins

Proteins are essential for both the bioactivity and identification of PELNs [57]. Unlike mammalian EVs, which are well-characterized in terms of proteomics characteristics, the lack of universal protein markers remains a major limitation in PELNs research. This makes it difficult to standardize isolation and compare results across different plant species. Despite this challenge, recent proteomic studies have identified several key protein families in PELNs [58].

The protein profile of PELNs primarily includes heat shock proteins (HSPs), aquaporins, annexins, and lectins. These proteins are essential for vesicle structure and cross-kingdom communication:

HSPs: These conserved proteins are often found on the vesicle membrane. They maintain cellular balance and help the vesicles respond to stress. Their presence suggests they may act as signals that help recipient human cells adapt to environmental stress.

Aquaporins: Another class of membrane-associated proteins identified in some PELNs, may contribute to membrane transport and cellular homeostasis, and have been implicated in skin-protective effects such as anti-aging activity [59–61].

Annexins: Frequently detected in vesicles from tomato and sunflower seeds, annexins are involved in membrane dynamics and calcium binding. They play a key role in membrane repair and ROS signaling, which supports tissue healing [62].

In addition, certain PELNs contain unique functional proteins. For example, defense-related proteins such as PMR5 and Gnk2 identified in sunflower seed-derived vesicles exhibit antifungal activity [63, 64]; The II-type lectin from garlic-derived vesicles can mediate vesicle internalization and exert anti-inflammatory effects through binding to the CD98 glycoprotein on the surface of mammalian cells [65]. Specific protein components in ginseng-derived vesicles have been shown to regulate macrophage polarization, suggesting their potential in immune regulation [66].

Identifying signature proteins is essential for defining PELNs purity and distinguishing them from co-isolated non-vesicular components. Historically, TET8 (a member of the tetraspanin family) has been proposed as a primary candidate due to its structural homology with the mammalian marker CD63 [67]. Early studies in Arabidopsis also identified the enrichment of syntaxin PEN1 and the ABC transporter PEN3, both of which are implicated in vesicle-mediated defense and cargo transport [40, 68].

However, the field has recently transitioned toward a more comprehensive reference compendium to address the lack of standardized markers. Recent proteomic evaluations have established a set of “bona fide” markers categorized by their phylogenetic conservation: (1) Plant-Specific Markers: Proteins such as Fasciclin-like arabinogalactan proteins (FLAs, notably FLA1, 2, and 8) and Germin-like proteins (GLPs) have emerged as robust indicators of botanical origin. Since these proteins lack mammalian homologs, they are ideal for verifying PELNs authenticity in cross-kingdom studies; (2) Conserved Structural Markers: In contrast, Aquaporins (PIPs) and V-type ATPase subunits represent highly conserved eukaryotic proteins frequently enriched in PELNs, serving as reliable structural markers across diverse plant species [45].

It is important to recognize that PELNs exhibit significant subpopulation heterogeneity. Emerging evidence suggests that markers like TET8 and PEN1 often track distinct, non-overlapping secretory pathways. Therefore, a robust characterization strategy should utilize a combination of at least one plant-specific marker (e.g., FLA) and one conserved protein (e.g., V-ATPase) to ensure the isolation of genuine extracellular vesicles. This shift toward a standardized protein reference system is a prerequisite for the reproducible clinical translation of PELN-based therapies [68].

Lipid

The lipid bilayer of PELNs is more than just a structural shell. It is a primary driver of their therapeutic efficacy and translational potential. Unlike mammalian EVs, PELN membranes are rich in phospholipids such as phosphatidic acid (PA), phosphatidylcholine (PC), and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) but contain less cholesterol and sphingomyelin. This unique composition directly dictates how PELNs interact with the complex skin microenvironment [57, 69].

The specific lipid profile of PELNs is critically linked to their bioavailability. For instance, the high content of PA and PE in PELNs facilitates membrane fusion and receptor-mediated endocytosis. In the context of skin repair, this is a major translational advantage. These lipids enhance PELNs delivery across the stratum corneum, reaching deep dermal cells more effectively [70].

Lipids in PELNs function as active signaling molecules rather than passive carriers. Lipids from ginger, garlic, and leeks (such as DLPC) have been shown to directly suppress the NLRP3 inflammasome [71, 72]. The lipid PA in ginger-derived vesicles can physically interact with bacterial proteins (e.g., HBP35 in P. gingivalis). This capability allows PELNs to function as a “dual action” platform for translation. PELNs can simultaneously inhibit pathogen growth while promoting tissue regeneration [73].

A key critical correlation is the superior stability provided by plant-specific lipids. The high PC and PE content often double that of conventional plant extracts ensures that the vesicle structure remains intact under the oxidative stress of a wound site [70]. This structural integrity is a “translational prerequisite” for developing shelf stable topical gels or microneedle patches for clinical skin therapy.

Metabolites

The metabolic profile of PELNs is a significant contributor to their functional potential in skin repair. Unlike traditional plant extracts, where bioactive compounds are directly exposed to the environment, PELNs encapsulate metabolites such as flavonoids, phenolic acids, and alkaloids within their lipid bilayer. This structure provides a protective environment that can reduce the rapid degradation of sensitive molecules caused by oxidation or UV exposure. For instance, while phenolic acids from citrus can be unstable in topical applications, their sequestration within the PELNs structure helps maintain their chemical integrity. This stability allows for a more sustained interaction with inflammatory factors like IL-1β and TNF-α, helping to modulate signaling pathways that are often overactive in damaged skin [2, 74, 75].

Furthermore, the specific metabolic diversity within PELNs provides a multi-targeted approach to tissue restoration that is difficult to achieve with isolated compounds. For example, broccoli-derived vesicles are rich in glucosinolates that trigger endogenous antioxidant responses, while ginger-derived vesicles carry 6-gingerol and 6-shogaol, which are potent inhibitors of chronic inflammation [76–78].

PELNs bridge the gap between traditional botanical knowledge and modern nanomedicine. This integration provides a robust basis for developing clinical therapies, such as shelf-stable topical gels or specialized dressings, for the treatment of skin diseases.

Synergistic interplay of bioactive components in skin repair

The therapeutic efficacy of PELNs in skin repair does not rely on a single molecule, but rather on the synergistic interplay of their diverse cargoes. This multicomponent system functions as a “functional unit” where lipids, proteins, RNAs, and metabolites work together across different stages of the healing process [79].

Lipid-Protein Cooperation for Delivery: The first level of integration occurs at the structural level. The lipid bilayer provides a stable environment that protects sensitive RNAs and metabolites from degradation. Simultaneously, membrane proteins (such as lectins or annexins) work with specific lipids to facilitate the “homing” of PELNs to target skin cells. This ensures that the bioactive cargo is not only protected but also precisely delivered to the deep dermal layers [80].

Metabolite-Protein Synergy for Immediate Relief: Upon reaching the wound site, antioxidant enzymes like SOD and metabolites like flavonoids provide immediate biochemical action. While metabolites directly scavenge ROS, proteins catalyze the breakdown of harmful oxidants, creating a rapid “dual defense” system that stabilizes the oxidative stress in the wound microenvironment [81].

RNA-Driven Long-term Reprogramming: While proteins and metabolites provide quick intervention, miRNAs offer sustained therapeutic effects by regulating gene expression. For example, after the lipids facilitate vesicle entry, these miRNAs can reprogram macrophages from a pro-inflammatory (M1) to a pro-healing (M2) state [82]. This long-term genetic regulation is supported by the metabolic environment already established by the other components. However, the practical efficacy of this synergy still faces significant questions, particularly regarding the stoichiometric sufficiency of the cargo and the difficulty in distinguishing the effects of vesicles from co-isolated plant contaminants. These experimental challenges and the broader limitations of cross-kingdom regulation remain key hurdles for clinical translation, as discussed in detail later in this review [83].

PELNs delivery and engineering strategies

Therapies based on PELNs face several challenges, including short half-life, rapid clearance, and limited targeting ability. Biomaterials and engineering modifications provide an effective platform to enhance the stability, retention, and targeted delivery of PELNs [84–86]. Hydrogel for PELNs Delivery

Hydrogels are three-dimensional networks of hydrophilic polymers that can absorb and retain large amounts of water while mimicking the extracellular matrix. This structure facilitates cell adhesion and biological signal transmission, making hydrogels an ideal carrier for PELNs. They can be classified into two main types based on their origin: natural hydrogels and synthetic hydrogels [3, 87, 88].

Natural hydrogels

Natural hydrogels are prepared from polysaccharides or proteins of natural origin, and they have excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and biomimetic properties. Common materials include hyaluronic acid (HA), sodium alginate (SA), chitosan (CS), and gelatin [85]. These materials provide a good adhesive interface and biological signals for cells, playing a crucial role in skin regeneration [89, 90]. Gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) is a notable representative of natural hydrogels, modified from gelatin through methacrylation. It combines the bioactivity of natural materials, such as RGD sequences and enzymatic degradation sites, with the photopolymerization and adjustable mechanical properties of synthetic materials [91, 92]. This allows for “in situ physical encapsulation”, where PELNs can be mixed with a precursor solution and stabilized through photopolymerization. This method, exemplified by encapsulating dandelion-derived vesicles in hydrogel dressings, allows for precise control over drug release and has shown efficacy in treating infected wounds. The results demonstrate that these dandelion-derived vesicle-laden hydrogels exhibit sustained release characteristics, with approximately 80% of the encapsulated vesicles released over 48 h in vitro. Moreover, in an in vivo wound model, hydrogel treatment significantly inhibited the growth of Staphylococcus aureus, with the wound healing rate in infected mice reaching 96.9% by day 15. However, its clinical application is still hindered by the requirement for UV light and photoinitiators, which may pose safety concerns for sensitive tissues or deep wounds [93].

Chitosan (CS) represents a different class of carrier with high clinical readiness due to its established antibacterial and hemostatic properties [94]. Its use in hybrid systems, such as the composite hydrogel delivering rhubarb-derived vesicles, highlights the advantage of combining targeted therapy with sustained anti-inflammatory release [95]. Similarly, combining Aloe vera outer skin-derived vesicles with Aloe vera gel leverages the natural synergy between the carrier and the cargo to activate defense pathways like Nrf2/HO-1 [96].

Synthetic hydrogels

Synthetic hydrogels including PEG, PVA-based systems have adaptable mechanical strength, degradation and pore size, and high stability and batch uniformity. Unlike natural polymers, these synthetic systems can be engineered with specific degradation rates to ensure a controlled and predictable release of PELNs. This structural consistency is crucial for clinical scale-up and regulatory approval, as it reduces variability and lowers the risk of immunogenicity [97–99].

These synthetic systems can be easily modified to add new functions, such as combining synthetic components with recombinant proteins (e.g., rhCOL3) or adding stimulus-responsive groups. These modifications allow the hydrogels to respond to the wound microenvironment [100]. Additionally, they protect the biological activity of PELNs from sources like houttuynia cordata or lemon and offer excellent tissue adhesion and self-healing properties. These hybrid systems promote macrophage reprogramming and angiogenesis, crucial for improving the hyperglycemic microenvironment in diabetic wounds [101, 102]. Furthermore, synthetic frameworks allow for the co-delivery of plant- and animal-derived extracellular vesicles, enabling coordinated release to tackle chronic inflammation and oxidative stress [103].

Microneedle

Microneedle (MN) arrays consist of hundreds of micrometer-sized tips that can painlessly penetrate the stratum corneum of the skin, creating microchannels within the skin to directly deliver active substances to the dermis layer. This represents a revolutionary strategy for transdermal drug delivery [104–106].

The functional diversity of MNs is largely determined by their material composition and release mechanisms. Dissolvable microneedles, typically fabricated from biocompatible polysaccharides or HA, are the most common platform for PELNs delivery. These systems consolidate the vesicle cargo into a solid matrix that dissolves upon contact with interstitial fluid [107].

Incorporating portulaca oleracea-derived vesicles into HA-based MNs has shown potential in promoting anti-inflammatory effects, re-epithelialization, and collagen synthesis [108]. To enhance therapeutic outcomes, hybrid microneedle systems, including conductive arrays using electric pulses to accelerate cellular uptake, have been developed. These are particularly effective in addressing neurovascular communication deficits in diabetic wounds. The kelp-derived vesicles loaded with CA, under electrical stimulation, achieved transdermal delivery to a depth of 480 μm and cellular internalization, with in vivo retention lasting ≥ 48 h. By day 28 of in vivo testing, the wound healing rate reached 95.61% [109]. Advanced designs also incorporate inorganic agents, like silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), into bilayer hydrogel needles, combining the anti-inflammatory effects of vesicles (e.g., from ginger) with the antibacterial properties of the matrix [110]. However, these complex designs may involve intricate manufacturing processes and raise concerns about the long-term toxicity of metallic or conductive components.

Lipid nanoparticles

Liposomes are advanced nanocarriers made of phospholipids, cholesterol, and surfactants, enabling adaptability in charge, size, and surface characteristics, making them ideal for drug delivery and vaccine design. Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) can be targeted by adjusting their surface to enhance tissue-specific accumulation and reduce off-target effects [111, 112].

The advantage of hybrid systems lies in their ability to create synergistic therapeutic effects absent in pure synthetic lipids. For instance, fusing hydrangea leaf-derived vesicles with liposomes loaded with specific anti-inflammatory agents allows for a multi-targeted intervention in the skin. Such systems can effectively suppress the synthesis of pro-inflammatory cytokines and inhibit cell apoptosis. By optimizing the lipid composition, these hybrid carriers can improve cellular uptake and alleviate skin irritation more effectively than non-hybridized liposomes. This suggests that hybridization can overcome the “bio-inert” nature of synthetic lipids, allowing the encapsulated cargo to interact more dynamically with inflammatory pathways in conditions like atopic dermatitis [113].

Metal-organic framework

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are versatile porous materials with high drug loading and stimuli-responsive release. Their fusion with PELNs combines natural biological characteristics with engineered control in creating versatile platforms in the next generation theragnostic [114, 115].

Beyond structural support, MOF-PELN hybrids can be functionalized for specific therapeutic targets. The addition of targeting ligands, such as RGD peptides, allows these platforms to concentrate in specific cell types like fibroblasts. For hypertrophic scars, these systems enable dual-action therapy. The MOF component generates singlet oxygen under light conditions. This singlet oxygen induces targeted apoptosis. The MOF component also depletes intracellular glutathione. This shows that MOFs are not simple carriers but active participants in treating skin diseases [116].

Engineering Strategies for PELNs

Membrane surface modification

Membrane surface modification is one of the most widely applied strategies in extracellular vesicle engineering, as it can enhance the circulation stability of vesicles, improve their biocompatibility, and enable targeted delivery. However, the membrane labeling of PELNs still faces certain uncertainties. As a result, current approaches primarily rely on physical or chemical methods to directly modify the vesicle surface [117]. For example, attaching antibodies (like anti-PD-L1) to the surface of fisch root-derived vesicles can greatly enhance anti-tumor activity by targeting specific immune checkpoints [118]. Furthermore, conjugating pH-sensitive complexes like heparin-based nanoparticles to grapefruit-derived vesicles increases drug-loading capacity and enables site-specific release in acidic inflammatory environments [119, 120]. These modifications allow for a more focused therapeutic intervention and a significantly improved pharmacokinetic profile compared to unmodified vesicles.

Cargo loading

The lipid bilayer and internal aqueous phase of PELNs allow them to encapsulate both hydrophilic and hydrophobic exogenous molecules such as mRNA, miRNA, siRNA, and proteins while protecting these payloads from degradation. Several methods have been developed for loading drugs into plant-derived extracellular vesicles, including co-incubation, electroporation, ultrasonication, click chemistry, freeze-thaw cycles, and osmotic shock [74].

Passive methods, such as co-incubation, are favored for their simplicity and minimal impact on vesicle morphology. However, for bulkier or more polar molecules, active interventions are often required to achieve therapeutic concentrations. Ultrasonication, for example, creates temporary pores through mechanical shear, as demonstrated by the successful integration of curcumin into tomato-derived vesicles, which significantly boosted the system’s intrinsic anti-inflammatory properties [121]. More innovative approaches have recently emerged, such as leveraging the natural photosensitivity of PELNs. By applying LED light irradiation, researchers can trigger a controlled generation of ROS that instantaneously enhances membrane permeability, allowing for precise, stimuli-responsive drug loading [122, 123]. In summary, various cargo loading methods, including novel light-triggered techniques, enable efficient and controlled delivery of therapeutic molecules using PELNs.

Membrane hybridization and coating

Inspired by biomimetic design principles, fusing or coating functionalized biological membranes with PELN membranes improves targeted delivery and adds new functionalities [124]. For example, fusing grapefruit-derived nanovesicles with gingival mesenchymal stem cell membranes allows the resulting hybrid to inherit CCR6-mediated targeting. This “cross-kingdom” synergy enables the precise delivery of immunosuppressive agents to inflamed tissues, effectively modulating the Th17/Treg balance in conditions like psoriasis and atopic dermatitis [125]. Similarly, the ginger-derived extracellular vesicles and palladium-platinum nanosheets form a biomimetic nanoplatform with good biocompatibility, capable of synergistic anti-infection therapy [126]. Overall, membrane hybridization and coating strategies enhance PELNs’ targeting ability and functionality, offering promising solutions for precise therapeutic delivery.

Clinical translation: engineering trade-offs and biological hurdles

The development of advanced delivery and engineering strategies has transformed PELNs from simple plant extracts into sophisticated therapeutic platforms [127, 128]. However, a critical evaluation reveals a distinct trade-off between the complexity of the engineering strategy and its immediate clinical translatability. While hybrid systems and surface functionalization offer superior precision and smart responsiveness, they introduce significant hurdles in standardized manufacturing and regulatory approval [129, 130].

Crucially, the clinical reality of dermatological conditions presents a highly complex and heterogeneous pathological microenvironment that varies significantly between individuals [131]. Current smart delivery platforms often lack the necessary elegance to navigate this inter-patient variability, where fluctuations in pH, ROS levels, and protease activity can vary not only by disease stage but also by individual physiology [132]. Therefore, while engineering has progressed, the adaptive intelligence of these platforms must be further enhanced to achieve truly autonomous, environment-responsive drug release.

Currently, the field must balance the pursuit of high-performance “chimeric” carriers with the practical requirements of large-scale production, stability, and long-term safety. As summarized in Table 6, the selection of an optimal delivery matrix or engineering approach depends heavily on the specific dermatological target, the required release kinetics, and the intended path to clinical adoption.

Applications of PELNs in skin repair

Wound healing

Wounds are classified as acute or chronic. Acute wounds heal predictably, while chronic wounds including diabetic ulcers, venous ulcers and pressure sores are often difficult to heal due to the influence of their underlying diseases. Infection and inflammation are important factors that hinder wound healing. Therefore, the key goals of clinical wound treatment are to reshape the skin microenvironment, control infection and promote tissue regeneration. PELNs have emerged as promising candidates due to their multi-target bioactivity, encompassing antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and pro-angiogenic properties (Table 5) [133, 134].

Antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects

Bacterial infection is a major obstacle to wound healing, especially with the rise of multi drug-resistant strains [135]. PELNs offer a dual action strategy: direct antimicrobial activity and immunomodulatory reprogramming. For example, nanovesicles from mint leaves exhibit broad spectrum antibacterial activity. When incorporated into an oxidized alginate and chitosan composite hydrogel, these vesicles effectively inhibit the growth of both Gram-positive (Micrococcus luteus) and Gram-negative (Escherichia coli) bacteria, presenting a promising approach for treating infected wounds [136].

Clinically, the infection wound of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is particularly difficult [137]. Broccoli-derived vesicles combined with gold nanoparticles have demonstrated the ability to polarize macrophages from a pro-inflammatory M1 to an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype via the NF-κB signaling pathway [138].

Remodeling the neurovascular microenvironment

Poor wound healing is driven by complex microenvironment, including AGEs, oxidative stress, chronic inflammation and impaired neurovascular regeneration. PELNs act as multi target regulators that can initiate the reconstruction of these neurovascular units [139, 140]. For instance, conductive microneedle systems loaded with caffeic acid-modified vesicles have shown the ability to synchronize Schwann cell and endothelial cell activities, promoting simultaneous angiogenesis and nerve regeneration [100]. Additionally, ginseng-derived vesicles can reprogram endothelial metabolism toward glycolysis, providing the rapid energy supply needed for cell migration and neovascularization [96, 109].

Skin-photoaging

Rising global population aging is driving a new era of delaying or reversing aging. Skin aging is the most visible marker of this process [10, 141]. Skin aging, particularly photoaging driven by chronic UV radiation, is characterized by a cascade of oxidative stress, DNA damage, and a persistent inflammatory state known as “inflammaging”. PELNs are being investigated for their ability to deliver natural bioactive molecules that interface with cellular aging pathway (Table 5) [142, 143].

Targeted antioxidant defense and cytoprotecting

Reducing photoaging requires neutralizing excess ROS and enhancing antioxidant defense. Many studies have confirmed the effectiveness of PELNs in anti-oxidation. Aloe vera-derived vesicles have been shown to promote the nuclear translocation of Nrf2, thereby upregulating protective enzymes such as HO-1 and NQO1. In photoaging models, these vesicles appear to reduce DNA oxidative damage and suppress markers of cellular senescence, such as SA-β-gal activity [144]. Similarly, vesicles from polygonum and panax ginseng have demonstrated the potential to stimulate collagen synthesis and reduce the degradation of the ECM [70, 145].

Anti-inflammatory and Immunomodulatory

Skin aging is essentially a chronic inflammation. UV induces keratinocytes and fibroblasts to release pro-inflammatory factors and show SASP, leading to persistent inflammation and ECM degradation. PELNs can break this vicious circle by regulating the key inflammatory signaling pathway like NF-κB [57, 146].

A notable strategy involves the use of hybrid systems, such as olive leaf-derived vesicles encapsulated in hyaluronic acid/tannic acid hydrogels. This composite platform has shown the ability to reduce inflammatory cell infiltration and promote collagen deposition in animal models [15]. Not only that, cabbage-derived vesicles have been reported to encourage M2 macrophage polarization, potentially shifting the wound microenvironment from a chronic inflammatory state to a regenerative one [147].

Inflammatory skin diseases

Inflammatory skin diseases, such as atopic dermatitis and psoriasis, are a group of common diseases characterized by skin barrier dysfunction, immune response disorders and chronic inflammation. Traditional treatments such as long-term use of corticosteroids or immunosuppressants often have serious side effects. Therefore, the development of new treatment strategies that are both efficient and safe has become a key research hotspot. The natural anti-inflammatory properties of PELNs provide a promising alternative treatment plan for this field (Table 5) [80, 148].

Immunomodulation and microenvironment remodeling

PELNs play a crucial role in treating autoimmune skin diseases by precisely regulating immune cell functions and cytokine networks. They modulate the maturation and antigen presentation of dendritic cells, which in turn influences T cell differentiation. Additionally, PELNs restore CD4 + T cell balance by suppressing overactive Th1 and Th17 cells and boosting regulatory T cells This shift helps transform the immune response from aggressive to tolerant, thereby reducing chronic inflammation. A typical example is the development of chimeric nanovesicles which fuse grapefruit-derived vesicles with engineered animal cell membranes overexpressing CCR6. This hybrid system aims to utilize “homing” receptors to deliver immunosuppressive agents directly to inflammatory foci. In animal models, such systems have shown an ability to reduce IL-17 and TNF-α levels, effectively shifting the immune response toward tolerance [125]. Similarly, garlic and onion-derived vesicles delivered by temperature-sensitive hydrogels have been reported to alleviate psoriasis by regulating the Nrf2/IL-17/NF-κB axis [149]. These findings further highlight the ability of PELNs to coordinate the regulation of immune homeosis through multiple pathways.

Antioxidant stress and barrier repair

Beyond direct immunosuppression, PELNs may contribute to the restoration of redox balance and the physical skin barrier. Chronic inflammation generates excessive ROS, which further degrade the skin barrier and activate pro-inflammatory signaling in a vicious cycle [150].

Comprehensive systems, such as aloe-derived vesicles incorporated into pulp-sourced hydrogels, have been designed to activate the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, upregulating antioxidant proteins like SOD to restore redox homeostasis [96]. In addition, PELNs can also help repair the skin barrier function by promoting the synthesis of barrier proteins (such as silk polymer protein filaggrin) and lipids (such as ceramide). This strengthens the skin barrier, restores its defense, blocks antigens, and stops inflammation from returning. By enhancing the structural integrity of the stratum corneum, these treatments could reduce allergen penetration and moisture loss [151].

Clinical limitations, safety concerns and translational challenges

PELNs show great potential in dermatology but moving them from the lab to the clinic remains difficult [31]. Several major obstacles hinder their transition to standard-of-care treatments: (1) Model Disparity: A primary concern is the physiological difference between rodents and humans. Mice heal mainly through skin contraction. However, human skin is more complex structurally and immunologically. Therefore, rodent models cannot accurately replicate human chronic wounds, wrinkles, or psoriasis [152, 153]; (2) Dosage and Efficacy: The therapeutic threshold for “cross-kingdom” regulation in humans is still unknown. We do not yet know if topical or scaffold-based systems deliver enough plant cargo to trigger sustained effects. This stoichiometric uncertainty remains a critical challenge [154, 155]; (3) Environmental Stability: Diseased skin has a harsh microenvironment. It is rich in proteases and has fluctuating pH levels. These factors can degrade PELNs or damage their cargo before they reach the target cells, reducing bioavailability [46, 156]; (4) Standardization and Quality Control: The field lacks standardized quality control. PELNs isolates are often heterogeneous and contain various plant metabolites. This inconsistency makes it hard to reproduce research results or gain regulatory approval [31, 157]; (5) Long-term Safety: The long-term safety of PELNs remains underexplored. While short-term safety profiles have been promising, particularly in animal models, there is a lack of comprehensive long-term toxicological studies. Moreover, the risk of immune reactions or accumulation of plant-derived materials in the body, especially with chronic use, requires further investigation [80]; (6) Safety Considerations for Plant-Derived Cargo: Though PELNs have demonstrated low toxicity and good biocompatibility in many studies, there are safety concerns regarding the plant metabolites they carry. These metabolites could elicit unexpected biological responses depending on dosage or administration route. For instance, some plant extracts, such as unprocessed aloe vera, have known adverse effects when used improperly [158, 159]. Therefore, each plant source and extraction method should be evaluated for safety on a case-by-case basis.

To overcome these hurdles, standardized dosing protocols are needed. Additionally, using large animal models, such as porcine models, will be essential. These steps are necessary to turn promising laboratory findings into viable clinical therapies.

From bench to bedside: clinical translation and regulatory landscape

Large-scale manufacturing and quality control

Scalable production and GMP-compliant manufacturing

The clinical use of PELNs for skin repair requires industrial scale production. Ultracentrifugation is the current gold standard for laboratory research [160]. However, this method is slow and difficult to scale up. To solve this, researchers are now using Tangential Flow Filtration (TFF) and Size Exclusion Chromatography (SEC) [161, 162]. These technologies allow for high-purity recovery of PELNs from large volumes of plant juice. They also help maintain the structure and bioactivity of the particles.

Beyond scaling up, production must follow Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards. This ensures that the products are safe and consistent (Fig. 4).

Standardization of quality control metrics

To ensure the safety and efficacy of PELNs in skin repair, rigorous quality control (QC) metrics must be standardized. These metrics focus on three main areas: physical characterization, molecular composition, and biological potency.

First, physical properties are measured using Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) and Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA). These tools define the particle size distribution and concentration. For clinical use, a narrow polydispersity index (PDI) is required to ensure batch consistency (Table 7) [163]. Second, the purity of PELNs must be verified. This involves measuring total protein, lipid content, and the absence of contaminants like DNA or endotoxins [164, 165]. Since PELNs are derived from plants, “fingerprint” markers such as specific lipids or proteins are used to confirm their identity.

Finally, biological potency assays are the most critical for skin repair. These assays test the functional impact of PELNs on skin cells. Common tests include measuring the rate of fibroblast migration in a “scratch assay” and tracking the down regulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines [133]. By establishing these standardized metrics, manufacturers can guarantee that each batch of PELNs will provide predictable and effective healing results when applied to clinical wounds.

Pharmaceutical stability: storage and transportation

Optimization of storage conditions and lyophilization

Ensuring the long-term stability of PELNs is a major challenge for their clinical use in skin repair. Most fresh PELNs are stored in PBS at -80 °C to prevent degradation [157, 166]. However, ultra-low temperature storage is expensive and difficult for large scale transportation [49]. Therefore, researchers are optimizing storage temperatures and buffer compositions to maintain the structural integrity of these nanoparticles. Studies show that adding cryoprotectants, such as sucrose or trehalose, can prevent the aggregation of PELNs during the freezing process.

Lyophilization is considered the most promising technique for creating “off-the-shelf” skin repair products [167]. This process removes water from the PELNs, turning them into a stable powder. To protect the delicate lipid membranes during lyophilization, “bulking agents” and “lyoprotectants” are added. These substances form a glassy matrix that shields the vesicles from ice crystal damage. Once dried, PELNs can be stored at room temperature or 4 °C for extended periods [168]. When needed, they can be easily rehydrated or mixed into biomaterial scaffolds. Optimizing these conditions ensures that the PELNs remain bioactive and ready for immediate clinical application in wound management [169].

Cold chain logistics and long-term stability

A reliable cold chain is necessary to keep PELNs effective for skin repair. These nanoparticles are very sensitive. Changes in temperature or shaking during transport can damage them. If the temperature rises, the vesicles may clump together or leak their active ingredients. This would make the treatment fail to repair damaged skin [161]. Usually, PELNs must be kept at 2–8 °C for short trips or -20 °C for long distances.

To prove these products are safe, researchers must perform long-term stability tests [170]. They monitor the particles for several months to find their “shelf-life”. New technologies are helping with this process. For example, “Smart Packaging” uses sensors to track the temperature in real time. It is also important to test the stability of PELNs when they are already mixed into hydrogels or bandages. Ensuring these products stay stable during shipping is a key step. It makes skin repair therapies easier to send to hospitals and patients around the world [171].

Regulatory framework and ethical considerations

Regulatory classification and biosafety assessment

The regulatory classification of PELNs is a major challenge for their clinical use. Currently, there is no specific category for plant-derived vesicles in international guidelines. Regulators must decide if PELNs should be treated as “herbal medicines”, “biological products” or “synthetic nanoparticles” [172, 173]. Since PELNs carry complex mixtures of proteins, lipids, and RNAs, they are often viewed as complex biological drugs. Defining their “Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient” (API) is difficult but necessary for legal approval. Clear rules are needed to help these skin repair therapies move through the regulatory process [20, 174].

Biosafety assessment is equally important for patient safety. Although PELNs come from edible plants, their safety in human skin must be proven. Toxicity tests to check for immune reactions or cell damage are needed to perform. These tests include “acute toxicity” for short-term effects and “chronic toxicity” for long-term use. For skin repair, it is vital to ensure that PELNs do not cause inflammation or allergic reactions when applied to open wounds [175]. Study of “biodistribution” of the particles is also needed to ensure they do not harm other organs after applied in skin [176].

Ethical implications in phytomedicine and nanotechnology

The use of PELNs for skin repair raises specific ethical concerns regarding nanotechnology safety. Because these particles are extremely small, they can cross biological barriers and enter cells in unique ways. The long-term effects of these nanoparticles on human health are not yet fully understood [177]. Therefore, researchers have an ethical duty to be transparent about these potential risks.

Informed consent is the most important part of this ethical process. Patients must be told exactly how the nanotechnology works before they agree to a treatment. Doctors should use simple language to explain how the PELNs might behave inside the body [178, 179]. This ensures that patients can make a truly safe and informed decision. Challenges and perspectives

The therapeutic potential of PELNs in skin regeneration is immense, yet transitioning these findings into clinical practice requires overcoming several critical hurdles.

Standardization and Quality Control: Current isolation techniques lack the yield and purity consistency required for clinical grades. The absence of universal markers and standardized quantification units hinders the comparison of results across different laboratories. Establishing GMP-compliant, scalable manufacturing processes is the first prerequisite for clinical adoption [49].

Rigorous Safety and Immunogenicity Profiling: As requested by clinical regulators, the “cross-kingdom” safety of PELNs must be scrutinized beyond simple short-term observations. Future research must evaluate the risk of chronic immune sensitization, particularly the interaction of plant-specific lipids and proteins with human Toll-like receptors. Furthermore, the long-term toxicological impact and potential systemic bioaccumulation of PELNs cargo in filtration organs (e.g., liver and spleen) remain significant black boxes that require multi-dose, long-term studies in large animal models [176].

Pharmaceutical Stability: Maintaining the functional integrity of PELNs cargo during storage is a major logistics challenge. Optimizing preservation strategies such as lyophilization with specialized cryoprotectants is essential to ensure that PELN-based products can maintain their therapeutic potency throughout their shelf life [182].

Precision Engineering and Regulatory Pathways: Current delivery systems often rely on passive release, which may not suffice for the highly dynamic and heterogeneous pathological microenvironment of human skin. The next generation of PELN-based therapies should focus on stimulus-responsive architectures that adapt to individual patient needs. Finally, as “combination products”, these engineered platforms face a complex regulatory landscape requiring extensive validation of both the biological cargo and the synthetic matrix [183, 184].

In conclusion, bridging the gap between bench and bedside will require a transdisciplinary effort. By addressing these safety and standardization challenges through rational engineering and rigorous clinical evaluation, PELNs can be transformed into a standardized, safe, and effective “standard-of-care” for skin regeneration (Fig. 5).

Conclusion

As natural bioactive nanocarriers, PELNs offer a transformative platform for skin regeneration. They effectively promote skin repair, combat photoaging, and alleviate inflammatory dermatoses.

Current engineering strategies, particularly the integration of PELNs with stimuli-responsive hydrogels, microneedles, and hybrid nanoparticles, show significant potential in enhancing cargo stability and achieving spatiotemporal control over drug release. These platforms allow PELNs to better navigate the complex and heterogeneous pathological microenvironment of human skin.

However, moving from bench to bedside requires resolving critical challenges, including the standardization of large-scale purification, the optimization of long-term storage stability, and the establishment of clear regulatory frameworks for cross-kingdom biologics. Future research should prioritize mapping precise molecular targets, evaluate long-term systemic safety and bioaccumulation, and develop patient-specific intelligent delivery systems. By combining natural bioactivity with rational engineering, PELN-based therapies can provide a safe and standardized “standard-of-care” for the next generation of dermatological treatments.
