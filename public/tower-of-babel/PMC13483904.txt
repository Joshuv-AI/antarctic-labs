Lipid trapping slows ball-and-chain inactivation in a calcium-activated potassium channel
Authors: Nattakan Sukomon, Hee-Seop Yoo, Billy J. Williams-Noonan, Chen Fan, Celine Boiteux, Alma Perrino, Simon Scheuring, Toby W. Allen, Crina M. Nimigean
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ion channel inactivation is a key modulatory mechanism that shapes action potentials and cellular excitability. In N-type (ball-and-chain) inactivation, a tethered N-terminal domain occludes the open pore. The prokaryotic MthK channel, a homolog of BK channels, undergoes such inactivation via its N-terminus. Notably, MthK inactivation was observed in liposome assays but not in decane-containing planar bilayer recordings, suggesting membrane dependence. We found that MthK inactivation progressively slowed with increasing bilayer thickness in liposomes composed of varying acyl-chain length lipids. Pore size was not a determining factor, as cryo-EM structures and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations showed similar pore dimensions across conditions, and block of a non-inactivating mutant by a peptide mimicking the N-terminal domain was largely bilayer thickness-independent. Instead, MD simulations, later confirmed with mutagenesis, revealed that N-terminal arginines form stronger interactions with lipid phosphates in thicker bilayers, thus limiting the access of the N-terminus to the pore and slowing inactivation.

Introduction

Ion channels play an important role in diverse physiological activities through regulating ion fluxes across biological membranes1. Ion channels reside in membranes and, as such, their activity can be modulated by the physical and chemical properties of the lipid bilayer2–7. Cellular membranes are remarkably diverse in their composition across species, tissues, cellular compartments, and even specialized nanodomains located in the same membrane, with individual lipids defining these characteristics8–12. As a corollary, the same ion channel protein may display different activity levels and gating features as a function of cell type, organelle, or partition in nanodomains13–19. A direct correspondence between ion channel activity, tissue/cellular location, lipid bilayer content, and exact composition of the channel protein has not been possible so far due to difficulties in analyzing the identities of the lipid and protein contents of the channel-containing membrane patch that was used for the activity recording. On the other hand, a reverse, bottom-up approach is now possible. One can purify ion channels from cells, reconstitute them in liposomes of the lipid compositions that would mimic cell membranes of interest, and analyze and compare their activities20–24. Using this approach, one can systematically characterize the activity and gating of an ion channel of known protein composition, in a wide range of membrane properties: thickness, curvature, fluidity, charge content, and specific lipid content. We use this approach here, along with atomistic molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, to dissect the mechanism of membrane thickness-dependence of ball-and-chain inactivation in a calcium-gated potassium channel, MthK.

Many potassium channels undergo ball-and-chain inactivation, also known as N-type inactivation25–35. Ball-and-chain inactivation is mediated by an N-terminal region or “ball” motif plugging the ion channel pore from the intracellular side to block ion permeation25,26,35–38. The ball is attached via a chain, typically an unstructured amino acid stretch of ~20 to ~50 residues in length, to either the channel itself or to an accessory subunit that interacts with the channel35,39–42. Generally, the ball motif is mostly hydrophobic in the first ~8 N-terminal residues, followed by hydrophilic (mostly positively charged) residues, and it tethers to the channel through the less conserved chain region27,28,33,36,37,43–45. In contrast to the ball, the roles the chain plays in ball-and-chain inactivation are still unclear, but its length was proposed to contribute to the inactivation kinetics27,28,43. Application of synthetic peptides with sequences resembling ball-and-chain domains can restore inactivation of a non-inactivating mutant of a given K+ channel or induce inactivation in different K+ channels36,37,46–51.

The first cryo-EM structures capturing ball-and-chain inactivation in ion channels have been determined by utilizing the MthK channel from M. thermoautotrophicum, a prokaryotic homolog of the large-conductance Ca2+-activated K+ (BK) channel25. Subsequent cryo-EM structures of ball-and-chain inactivated BK channels via N-termini of accessory β subunits validated this mechanism for eukaryotic channels26. MthK is a member of the tetrameric K+ channel family (see cartoon in Fig. 1a), where each subunit has two transmembrane (TM) pore-forming helices, a conserved K+ channel signature sequence in the selectivity filter and a bundle-crossing gating mechanism52–58. Additionally, a total of eight cytoplasmic Ca2+-binding (RCK) domains, homologous to those of BK channels, are connected to the TM2 helix, and their conformation changes upon Ca2+ binding gate the channel pore25,59. Approximately 20 N-terminal residues prior to the start of the TM1 helix form the ball-and-chain region. MthK channels have been extensively utilized over the past 20 years as model system for the eukaryotic BK channel in biophysical investigations since their high-resolution structure determination using X-ray crystallography52,59,61–63. Many fundamental general ion channel mechanisms have been determined by using structural and functional approaches on MthK, such as ball-and-chain inactivation25, bundle-crossing55–57, and selectivity filter gating52,54,64, selectivity mechanism61,65–68, and membrane-mediated fenestration access to the pore69. An oddity of MthK channel gating has been that over the years, ball-and-chain inactivation has only been reported with either patch-clamp experiments of spheroplasts containing MthK70, or flux assays using MthK-reconstituted liposomes25,69. Reports using single-channel current recordings in planar lipid bilayers using MthK-reconstituted in liposomes composed of the same lipids as the flux assays, never showed inactivation54,66,71,72. One of the major differences between the flux assays and the single-channel recordings is that the bilayers used for the latter also contain hydrocarbons (such as decane), previously shown to increase bilayer thickness73, suggesting that MthK inactivation is abolished in the thicker bilayers. Currently, there are no reports on how bilayer thickness or any other bilayer property may affect the gating of MthK channels. Furthermore, aside from stretch-activated channels, whose response to different bilayer properties has been investigated74–76, there are only a few reports on how any eukaryotic ion channel is affected by different bilayer properties77–81, and none on purified channels.

Here, we show that the inactivation kinetics of MthK are strongly dependent on lipid bilayer thickness using stopped-flow spectrofluorometric assays with MthK-reconstituted liposomes composed of lipids with different tail lengths. Using cryo-EM and functional assays with a synthetic ball peptide, we found that this dependence is not due to a change in the size of the protein’s open pore cavity, which is where the inactivating ball binds, as the pore remains unchanged. We also find that the connection between ball and channel is crucial. Indeed, MD simulations indicate a bilayer thickness-dependent inactivation mechanism in which the ball, when not in the pore, interacts with the lipid bilayer. Extensive enhanced-sampling simulations are used to quantify this interaction, finding that in thicker bilayers the ball becomes trapped by lipid interactions that prevent it from reaching the pore, thereby slowing, and in extremis abolishing, inactivation. This prediction is experimentally validated by a mutant where the ball domain is more hydrophobic, promoting bilayer interaction, essentially eliminating inactivation in thicker bilayers, while maintaining inactivation in thinner bilayers.

Results

Lipid bilayer thickness determines ball-and-chain inactivation kinetics of MthK

We investigated the effect of bilayer thickness on MthK inactivation (Fig. 1a) by reconstituting MthK channels into large unilamellar vesicles (LUVs) in five different lipid compositions (Supplementary Fig. 1a) with phosphatidylcholine (PC) head-group and different carbon tail lengths (ranging from 14 to 22 carbons). We labeled the lipids the following way: C14, for 14:1 (Δ9-cis) PC; C16, for 16:1 (Δ 9-cis) PC; C18, for DOPC; C20 for 20:1 (cis) PC; and C22, for 22:1 (cis) PC (Fig. 1b, full lipid names are provided in the “Methods”). All LUVs also contained POPG, at a PC:POPG ratio of 3:1. The use of PC:PG (3:1) membranes was based on experimental considerations. While PE:PG mixtures more closely resemble bacterial membranes and have historically been used in single-channel studies of prokaryotic channels, PC:PG proteoliposomes provided improved vesicle stability. In addition, phosphatidylcholine lipids offer a well-defined monounsaturated series spanning C14 to C22 acyl chain lengths, enabling systematic modulation of bilayer thickness under consistent membrane conditions. Since the only parameter that was varied was the length of the carbon tails, while everything else was kept constant, the most direct and predictable change in the bilayer property is the bilayer thickness, although other changes are also expected.

We quantified inactivation using a stopped-flow fluorometry flux assay, which measures the quenching of a liposome-encapsulated fluorophore (8-Aminonaphthalene-1,3,6-Trisulfonic Acid, ANTS) in response to Tl+ entry into the LUV through open MthK channels20,82 (Fig. 1b, c). Tl+ permeates open K+ channels, and the higher the channel activity the more Tl+ entry into the LUV and the steeper the decrease in fluorescence (Fig. 1c). From quenching curves such as those shown in Fig. 1c, we extract the Tl+ flux rates (see “Methods”82–84). We determined the time constants of inactivation (τ) by measuring the Tl+ flux rates at different time points after activation with Ca2+, starting at 12 ms (dead time of the instrument) and up to 120 s. We found that the length of the PC lipids used to make the LUVs has a massive effect on the time course of inactivation measured with flux assays, where MthK is completely inactivated in ~100 ms in C14 lipids, while in C22 lipids, there is still no inactivation at ~1 s (Fig. 1d, f and Supplementary Table 1).

In stark contrast, single-channel recording experiments using MthK reconstituted in LUVs of identical lipid compositions as above, fused into planar bilayers containing decane (“Methods”20), yielded little change in either single-channel conductance or open probability (Po) upon increasing the carbon tail length of the lipids (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c and Supplementary Table 2). We were unable to obtain single-channel recordings of MthK with C14 or shorter lipids due to unstable bilayers. Notably, single-channel recordings confirmed that 1) MthK gating behavior in PC:PG bilayers is comparable to that observed in PE:PG membranes72, supporting the use of this lipid composition for the functional analyses presented here, and 2) the changes in bilayer properties consequent to systematically changing the acyl chain length from 14 to 22 carbons did not affect any MthK channel gating or permeation property tested other than inactivation.

To determine and compare the thicknesses of all these bilayers composed of C14-C22 lipids in the presence and absence of decane, we used atomic force microscopy (AFM) (Supplementary Fig. 2a–c). We found that indeed, the decane-containing bilayers were consistently thicker than those without decane (adding approximately 1-1.5 nm to the bilayer thickness), and even the shortest decane-containing C14 bilayers are thicker than the longest, decane-free C22 lipid bilayers (Fig. 1e). Given that the time constant of MthK inactivation in LUVs in flux assays increases exponentially with bilayer thickness (Fig. 1f), it is not unreasonable that in the thick decane-containing bilayers, MthK channels no longer appear to inactivate, consistent with previous reports54,71,72.

Lipid bilayer thickness does not modulate the size of the MthK channel pore

We next set out to investigate the mechanism by which bilayer thickness slows MthK inactivation. Our first hypothesis was that the bilayer thickness might alter the properties of the pore. Specifically, we reasoned that a large hydrophobic mismatch between the long C22 lipids and the shorter transmembrane (TM) domain of MthK might lead to a straightening of the TM helices and thus narrowing of the pore. Since the pore is the receptor site for ball peptide binding, changes in the pore diameter may lead to changes in inactivation. This hypothesis predicts that the structures of open MthK channels residing in bilayers of different thicknesses will be different in the pore region. We solved the structures with single-particle cryo-EM of Ca2+-bound MthK channels reconstituted in nanodiscs comprising C14 (14:1PC/POPG, thin bilayer) and C22 (22:1PC/POPG, thick bilayer) lipids (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). As reported for previous MthK channel structures in POPE/POPG (16–18 carbon tails)25, several conformations were identified in each condition, where the gating ring is tilted with respect to the transmembrane domain by different angles, and the transmembrane domains are all low resolutions (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). Tilted classes were observed in both C14 and C22 nanodiscs; however, the magnitude of tilt was greater in C22 (Supplementary Fig. 5), potentially reflecting lipid-dependent effects such as hydrophobic mismatch. Nevertheless, we found that in all conformations and in both C14 and C22 bilayers, the pore sizes are similar. For a more detailed comparison, we used the conformations with better resolution in the transmembrane domains. We analyzed the kink angles of the TM2 helices, which determine the opening of the intracellular gate and consequently the pore size, and the distances between residues located at the intracellular gate opening. The kink angles of the 4 TM2 helices in C14 range between ~32°− 37° and are comparable to those measured in C22 (Fig. 2a, b). To further assess the effect of bilayer thickness on pore structures, we performed simulations of MthK in membranes composed of C14–C22 lipids. Gate size, defined as the distance between opposing TM2 L95 residues, was measured for both a pore domain-only model and a full-length MthK model including the RCK domain and all four N-terminal peptides (Fig. 2d). Across multiple independent simulations, the gate size showed only minor differences between the two models and did not vary systematically with lipid chain length.

Overall, both the cryo-EM structures in C14 and C22 nanodiscs and the MD simulations in the same lipid bilayers suggest that MthK adopts a similar pore size regardless of lipid bilayer thickness. We next sought functional support for this finding.

Synthetic peptide block of MthK channels displays little dependence on bilayer thickness

If the bilayer thickness modulates inactivation by affecting the open pore sizes, then block of a non-inactivating (N-terminal deletion mutant, Δ2–17) MthK25,70 by a synthetic free peptide with the same sequence as the inactivating ball (we call it N-peptide, see “Methods” for exact sequence) should have a similar dependence on bilayer thickness as the channel with the ball N-peptide attached. We tested this by incorporating non-inactivating MthK (Δ2–17) into LUVs of identical lipid compositions as above, and tested the block with 10 μM N-peptide using stopped-flow fluorometry (Fig. 3a–d and Supplementary Fig. 6a). As previously shown25, MthK (Δ2-17) does not inactivate in any of the lipids tested, but addition of N-peptide blocked flux through MthK (Fig. 3). However, in contrast to MthK with an intact N-terminal domain, block by 10 μM N-peptide had little bilayer thickness-dependence (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table 1). As a control, we also tested whether 2 mutant synthetic peptides, where L3 was changed to a D and both L3 and V5 changed to D, mutations previously shown to lead to a dramatic reduction in inactivation in MthK channels, still blocked current through MthK (Δ2–17)70 (Supplementary Fig. 6a). No block of the non-inactivating MthK was detected when 10 μM mutant N-peptide was applied (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Longer chains reduce the bilayer thickness dependence of inactivation in MthK channels

The loss of bilayer thickness dependence of inactivation when free N-peptides were used to block the channel indicated that the attachment between the inactivation ball and the channel was important. If this is correct, then extending the length of the chain between the ball and the channel should reduce the bilayer-thickness dependence of inactivation. To test this, we increased the chain length by inserting stretches of glycine and serine residues between Pro19 and Ala20, which should also increase the flexibility of the ball domain (Supplementary Fig. 6a). The extended-chain mutants with eight glycine-serine added (MthK+8GS,) displayed faster inactivation kinetics than WT in the thickest bilayers tested (C22), with unchanged kinetics in the thinner bilayers (Fig. 3b–e).

Taken together, the above results corroborated the cryo-EM data, suggesting that the physical properties of the MthK pore do not contribute significantly to the bilayer-thickness dependence of MthK inactivation, whereas chain-channel attachment and chain-length do.

Ball domain preferentially interacts with thick lipid bilayers outside of the pore

The second hypothesis tested was that the ball domain binds to alternative sites outside the pore, and that binding to such locations is favored in thicker bilayers, thus keeping the ball domain away from the pore and slowing inactivation. We first employed unbiased atomistic MD simulations of MthK channels initiated with all four N-termini outside the pore. All simulations were performed using the full-length MthK and in the same lipid compositions employed in the experiments (PC:POPG, 3:1). For comparison, we incorporated MthK into C14 or C22 lipid bilayers and simulated two independent 2-μs MD simulations for each lipid type. The N-termini were observed to bind either to the RCK gating ring or to the lipid bilayer. However, binding to the lipid bilayer was markedly stronger in C22 membranes, where phosphate groups are more directly accessible and within reach of the ball domain. Representative snapshots show N-terminal domains binding to lipid headgroups in a C22 bilayer (Fig. 4a, top), whereas such interactions were not observed in the thinner C14 bilayers. To ensure complete sampling of the four N-terminal movements, we performed extensive bias-exchange metadynamics (BE-MTD) simulations (~1.8 μs per replica, 12 replicas for each lipid type) of MthK in C14 and C22 lipid bilayers. The BE-MTD approach enforces sampling of multiple states that would otherwise not interconvert in regular MD, with 43.2 μs total sampling to ensure quantitative calculation of N-terminal domain binding free energies. In both bilayer types, a common pore-bound free energy minimum was observed (z ~ 0 Å, with set reference free energy 0 kcal/mol), arising from salt-bridge formations between N-terminal R9/K10 and TM2 residue E92, as previously identified (25), with reduced involvement of R14, which remained outside the pore (Fig. 4b).

Outside the pore, N-terminal interactions with C14 and C22 bilayers, respectively, displayed distinct free energy landscapes (compare Fig. 4b bottom, with Fig. 4c, bottom). In C14, an elevated free energy minimum was found at an “entry” site just outside the pore (~ +17 kcal/mol centered at z~ −10 Å at a distance of 10 Å from the pore axis), where the N-terminus interacts with the base of the pore and R14 exhibits lipid interactions, while E6, R9, and K10 maintain contacts with the RCK domain (Fig. 4b, left; Supplementary Fig. 7c). The remainder of the surface is of high free energy, despite the presence of elevated local minima at lower z values (z~ −30 Å), corresponding to interactions with the RCK domain (Supplementary Fig. 7c). The global minimum for C14 is therefore the pore-bound state, which is expected to lead to enhanced ball-and-chain inactivation (Fig. 4b bottom).

In C22, however, the global minimum corresponds to the membrane-interacting entry site (~ −3.3 kcal/mol, centered at z~ −11 Å, at a distance of 15 Å from the pore axis), which draws the N-terminus away from the pore (Fig. 4c, bottom; Supplementary Fig. 7f). The low free energy of the N-terminus within this site outside the pore arises from its ability to form strong interactions between its R9 side chain and lipid phosphate groups that protrude from the thick bilayer, while maintaining interaction between its R14 and the pore’s E92 residues. These interactions extend the free energy minimum laterally up to ~40 Å from the pore and to lower z values of ~ −20 Å where RCK contacts occur (Supplementary Fig. 7f). The thermodynamic data in Fig.4 thus tells us that the channel in C14 lipids will be almost exclusively in the inactivated state (pore bound terminus) at equilibrium, while the channel will experience an equilibrium mixture of open and inactivated states in C22, but which favors the open state.

In addition to the thermodynamic favoring of this strongly lipid-interacting entry site, a very high free energy barrier exists for crossing into the pore (white asterisk), corresponding to the breaking of these interactions. While the barrier connecting entry and pore sites in C14 lipids was well captured (being only of the order 10 kcal/mol), the barrier in C22 lipids is likely too high to be captured accurately to allow for a quantitative rate estimate. The data does, however, indicate that the rate of pore binding of the N-terminus in C22 will be dramatically slowed compared to C14 because of the lipid-interacting binding site outside the pore. This suggests much slower inactivation in C22 bilayers, which is consistent with the experiments.

Our analysis has also shown that, whether or not one N-terminus is bound inside the pore, the other three N-termini establish similar deep lipid-interacting free energy minima (see Supplementary Fig. 8 and discussion therein).

Increased hydrophobicity of the ball domain abolishes inactivation in thick bilayers

Experimentally validating the MD predictions through mutagenesis of the ball domain is not straightforward because any alteration in the character of this domain may change both lipid and pore-binding properties and, in turn, inactivation. In addition, binding to the bilayer can be governed by both electrostatic interactions with the lipid headgroups (as seen above in MD) and possibly hydrophobic interactions with the bilayer core (if the charges are neutralized by mutagenesis). As a consequence, while we predicted that mutagenesis of R9 and R14 will alter inactivation based on the MD results, we remained agnostic regarding specific changes in inactivation properties of the mutated MthK channels.

We proceeded to experimentally investigate the role of the basic residues in lipid interactions by mutating R9 and R14 to leucines, individually or in combination (R9L, R14L, and R9L/R14L; Supplementary Fig. 6a). Time constants for inactivation were measured using stopped-flow fluorometry in different lipid compositions (C14, C18, C22 with POPG; Fig. 5a–c). Interestingly, in both mutants where R9 was mutated to L (R9L and R9L/R14L), inactivation was effectively abolished in the thickest bilayers (C22), with no decrease in flux even 120 s after calcium activation (Fig. 5c). In contrast, in C14 lipids, the same mutants showed only moderate slowing of inactivation (Fig. 5a). This observation aligns with BE-MTD simulations, which indicated that R9 is involved in strong lipid interactions in C22 (Fig. 4c), while R14 interacts with lipids in C14 (Fig. 4b, left). Accordingly, mutation of R9 to L would be expected to have a stronger effect on lipid interactions in C22 than in C14.

Although these mutations removed the positively charged residues that were shown to interact with the phosphate groups of the lipids in the simulations, replacement with leucines did not lead to faster inactivation in the thickest bilayers, which would have been expected if the mutated ball domain displayed decreased bilayer binding. One possible explanation is that increasing the hydrophobicity of the ball domain through the R9L mutation may promote stronger interactions with the lipid bilayer. We propose that R9 is more sensitive to such changes since it is located closer to the hydrophobic tip of the ball and increases the hydrophobicity of the part of the domain that is prone to be buried in the thicker bilayers. In C22, BE-MTD simulations show that R9 side chain interacts with lipids away from the pore (Fig. 4c), so its mutation to leucine may allow deeper insertion into the thick bilayer (Fig. 5d), to further slow pore binding.

In C14 bilayers, R9 is only one of several residues contacting the RCK domain (Fig. 4b, top right, and Supplementary Fig. 7b), and mutation to leucine is therefore expected to have a smaller effect. Moreover, altered lipid interactions in the C14 entry site likely have minimal impact on inactivation rates, as only R14 maintains contact with the base of the pore. Mutation of R9 would still moderately slow inactivation in C14 because of the increased free energy by disrupting R9–E92 salt bridges within the pore (Fig. 4b, top left).

Discussion

Although prokaryotic membranes can undergo adaptive changes in lipid composition and physical properties85–87, whether membrane thickness-dependence can influence MthK channel inactivation in vivo is unknown. In this study, we instead use MthK as a model system to examine the relationship between membrane thickness and ball-and-chain inactivation. We report that the lipid bilayer can serve as an additional binding site for the N-terminal inactivating domain of MthK, thereby competing with the pore for the inactivation peptide. These results suggest that the lipid environment actively modulates the availability of the inactivation domain for pore occlusion. We reconstituted MthK into liposomes and performed functional assays, which revealed a strong correlation between inactivation rates and bilayer thickness. Importantly, this relationship could not be explained by changes in pore size (discarded based on cryo-EM structures and simulations), but instead pointed to the role of the lipid bilayer in modulating the inactivation. MD simulations further demonstrated that positively charged residues in the N-terminal inactivation peptide interacted with lipid phosphate groups. We initially hypothesized that arginine-to-leucine mutations weakened these electrostatic interactions and would favor pore entry and accelerate inactivation in thicker bilayers (e.g., C22). However, the resulting increased hydrophobicity of the R9L N-terminus instead likely biases the peptide toward the bilayer interior, thereby potentially trapping it within the membrane rather than facilitating pore occlusion (Fig. 5).

Only one binding site for a ball peptide inside the MthK pore has been structurally resolved25, leaving the conformations of the other termini undetermined and likely highly dynamic88–90. Our simulations have revealed additional binding sites for the termini outside the pore, near the pore entrance, with the N-terminus able to interact with lipids or the RCK domain. Previous studies have implicated specific lipids—such as phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP₂)—in ball-and-chain inactivation regulation, with its negatively charged headgroup proposed to interact with basic residues of the inactivation peptide91,92. In addition, lipid microdomains enriched in cholesterol and sphingolipids may modulate nearby ion channels93–98 both through direct lipid–protein contacts99–101 and by altering bilayer physical characteristics such as curvature and thickness102.

Previous work has characterized phospholipid membrane thickness using a variety of experimental and computational approaches, including small-angle neutron scattering (SANS)103, small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS)104,105, microscopic106 and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations107,108. These studies have examined bilayers composed of lipids such as DOPC, POPC, POPG, and other phosphatidylcholines spanning different acyl chain lengths and degrees of unsaturation. Bilayers formed in the presence of decane have also been reported, where the solvent can influence bilayer structure and thickness73,109. Direct comparison of absolute thickness values between reports is not straightforward because experimental conditions and analysis methods vary substantially, such as lipid-decane ratio, lipid composition, temperature, and membrane geometry, all of which can influence the apparent membrane thickness. In addition, membrane thickness itself is defined in several ways in the literature, including headgroup–headgroup distance, phosphate–phosphate spacing, hydrophobic core width, or effective parameters derived from scattering models105,110,111. Despite these methodological differences, the overall magnitude and trends of membrane thickness are broadly consistent across studies. In our MD simulations, bilayer thickness ranges from ~34.5 to 45.5 Å across the C14–C22 lipid series, corresponding to a total increase of ~11 Å. The HS-AFM measurements of bilayers without decane span ~21–42 Å across the same lipid series, with a linear fit of 1.31 Å/number of carbons. The smaller slope in the MD simulations might reflect the presence of the embedded MthK channel, which can partially buffer hydrophobic mismatch, while the larger slope in the AFM measurement could reflect an increase in sturdiness of the thicker bilayers. Importantly, both approaches show an increase in membrane thickness with increasing acyl chain length.

The increase in thickness upon decane addition likely reflects differential solvent incorporation. Previous experimental and MD studies showed that n-alkanes such as decane partition into the bilayer center with minimal perturbation of lipid structure, supporting increased hydrophobic core thickness112,113. Although decane thickens all bilayers, its relative effect is greater in short-chain membranes, where the smaller hydrocarbon core allows proportionally higher decane incorporation. As a result, thickness increases more in short-chain bilayers, reducing the relative difference between short- and long-chain lipids and flattening the dependence of thickness on chain length.

A notable feature of MthK is its unusually slow inactivation kinetics, occurring over seconds rather than the millisecond timescales typical of many K⁺ channels. We propose that this slow onset reflects sequestration of the inactivation peptide not only by non-pore proteinaceous regions, such as the large cytosolic RCK gating ring, but primarily by the lipid bilayer, thereby delaying pore entry. The dynamic conformational changes of the RCK ring, together with the flexibility of the tether linking the inactivation chain, further imply that the chain adopts multiple spatial locations throughout the gating cycle. Such positional heterogeneity increases the likelihood of transient contacts with both the pore vestibule and the surrounding bilayer.

Our data did not directly resolve the ball domain bound to the bilayer; instead, its association with lipids was inferred from MD simulations and functional assays. Previous structural studies have reported that its absence from cryo-EM maps reflected its high mobility and the need for disorder to access the pore cavity through the narrow portals between the gating ring and the S6 helices25. Moreover, density for the N-terminal peptide is absent in other MthK subunits, both in the open-inactivated and closed conformations, implying a high degree of mobility unless the peptide is stably bound in the pore. Other studies also indicated that inactivation peptides in solution exhibited conformational heterogeneity, with some displaying ordered stretches of α-helix88–90. Our simulations also suggest that the MthK ball domain remains highly mobile in the absence of pore binding and undergoes unfolding as it extends toward the RCK gating ring.

What could a physiological role be for such a bilayer thickness dependence of ball-and-chain inactivation? It had been previously shown that plasma membranes contain lipid microdomains or microdomain-like structures that are thicker and more ordered than the surrounding bilayer, and changes in bilayer thickness were shown to influence the activities of various ion channels, particularly through mediating membrane lateral stress and hydrophobic mismatch114–116. These observations raise the possibility that ion channels may sense local variations of membrane thickness and couple their gating cycle to the lipid environment. In addition, differences in lipid composition across cellular membranes could support additional regulation of ion channels. For instance, enrichment of cholesterol or sphingolipids increases membrane thickness117–119 and may influence gating kinetics of channels, which are thickness-sensitive78,115, although other physical properties of the membrane and direct modulatory roles of individual lipid species cannot be excluded. Furthermore, Golgi and endoplasmic reticulum membranes are generally thinner than the plasma membrane120–122, and would thus favor channel inactivation. As a consequence, ion channels would be mostly inactivated while trafficking through these compartments, which could be seen as a protective measure against premature activity before proper modification and localization. While these suggestions remain speculative, they suggest that membrane thickness could modulate channel function in a context-dependent manner.

Within this context, our findings highlight a previously underappreciated role of the membrane in regulating channel inactivation. We demonstrate that the lipid bilayer can act as a favorable environment for the binding of the inactivation peptide, competing with the pore and directly shaping the kinetics of channel inactivation. These results provide mechanistic insight into the unusually slow inactivation of MthK and broaden our understanding of how lipids contribute to the classical ball-and-chain mechanism. Whether similar principles extend to other channels that use ball-and-chain inactivation remains uncertain. For example, BK channels, which share a homologous ball-and-chain mechanism mediated by accessory subunits, reside in eukaryotic membranes that exhibit substantial heterogeneity in lipid composition and thickness. Although a direct link between bilayer thickness and BK inactivation has not been investigated, variations in membrane physical properties could, in principle, influence the availability or positioning of inactivation domains. Although the presence of a hydrophobic tip followed by positively charged residues is a common motif, it needs to be confirmed whether direct membrane interactions represent a general feature across channels. Nonetheless, by linking bilayer thickness to inactivation dynamics, our findings support the broader view that membrane properties can shape distinct stages of channel gating and act as critical determinants of ion channel function.

Methods

MthK expression and purification

The expression and purification of MthK including mutants (MthK Δ2-17, extended-chain, R9L, R14L, R9L/R14L) were performed as previously described with mild modification for each sample25. Some MthK constructs were cloned into pQE82a (WT, extended-chain MthK, R9L, R14L, R9L/R14L) and some in pQE82L (MthK Δ2-17) vectors with a carboxy-terminal 6xHis tag. Each plasmid was transformed into XL-1 Blue cells (Agilent). The transformed cells were grown at 37 °C in Luria-Bertani media with 200 µg/ml ampicillin until reaching a cell density (OD600) of 1. Protein expression was induced by incubating with 400 µM IPTG (US Biological) for 3 h at 37 °C for WT MthK and extended-chain, R9L, R14L, R9L/R14L MthK, and for 16 h at 24 °C for MthK Δ2–17.

For purification, cell pellets were resuspended in buffer A (100 mM KCl, 50 mM Tris, pH 7.6) with 50 µg/ml DNase (Sigma), 1 µM leupeptin/pepstatin (Roche) and 0.17 mg/ml PMSF (Roche). The cells were broken with sonication by a probe sonicator at 60-75% power. Membranes were extracted by adding 50 mM decyl maltoside (DM; Anatrace) and rotating at room temperature for 2 h. The suspension was centrifuged at 17,500 × g for 45 min at 17 °C. Then, the supernatant was applied to a Co2+ column (GE Healthcare) that was pre-equilibrated with buffer B (100 mM KCl, 50 mM Tris, pH 7.6, 5 mM DM). The column was washed using wash buffer (buffer B with 20 mM imidazole). The channel was subsequently eluted from the column with elution buffer (buffer B with 400 mM imidazole), and thrombin (Roche) was immediately added to cleave the 6xHis tag (1 unit thrombin per 6 mg protein). After incubating for 3 h at room temperature, MthK was purified on a Superdex-200 gel filtration column (GE Healthcare) in buffer B. The channel was concentrated to 10–15 mg/ml by a 50 KDa MWCO Amicon concentrator (Millipore).

Single-channel recording and data analysis

The following phospholipids were used in this study: 1,2-dimyristoleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (14:1 (Δ9-Cis) PC; hereafter referred to as 14:1PC), 1,2-dipalmitoleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (16:1 (Δ9-Cis) PC; 16:1PC), 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (18:1 (Δ9-Cis) PC; DOPC), 1,2-dieicosenoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (20:1 (Cis) PC; 20:1PC), 1,2-dierucoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (22:1 (Cis) PC; 22:1PC), 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-(1’-rac-glycerol) (sodium salt) (16:0-18:1 PG; POPG), 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (16:0-18:1 PE; POPE).

Single-channel activities of MthK were monitored using a horizontal lipid bilayer setup and a pulse protocol in Clampex (Axon Instruments)20. The decane-solubilized mixture of 16:1PC/POPG, DOPC/POPG, 20:1PC/POPG, 22:1PC/POPG, or POPE/POPG (all 3:1 ratio) (Avanti Polar Lipids) was used to form lipid bilayers. A volume of 0.5 to 1 μl of WT MthK-containing liposomes of an identical lipid mixture was applied to the cis-chamber (extracellular) to allow fusion with the existing bilayer. The trans (intracellular) solution contained 0 or 5 mM Ca2+. MthK currents were recorded with an Axopatch 200 amplifier (Axon Instruments) after applying voltage in a symmetrical 200 K+ solution (190 mM KCl, 10 mM KOH, 10 mM HEPES, pH 8.5). Clampfit 9.0 (Axon Instruments) was used to analyze single-channel data as previously described64.

MthK reconstitution into ANTS-loaded LUVs

MthK reconstitution into LUVs for stopped-flow assays was performed as previously described20,82. In short, 450 µg purified MthK and mutants were mixed into 15 mg lipid mixture containing 14:1PC/POPG, 16:1PC/POPG, DOPC/POPG, 20:1PC/POPG, or 22:1PC/POPG (all 3:1 ratio) (Avanti Polar Lipids) solubilized in reconstitution buffer (100 mM KNO3, 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.0, 35 mM CHAPS). The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min before adding 1 ml of 8-aminonaphthalene-1,3,6-trisulfonic acid disodium salt (ANTS, Life Technologies) solution (100 mM KNO3, 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.0, 75 mM ANTS). LUVs were formed by adding 1 g Bio-Beads SM-2 (Bio Rad) in an additional 1 ml reconstitution buffer and rotating for 3 h at room temperature. The resulting vesicles were extruded through a 100-nm-pore polycarbonate filter (Avanti Mini-Extruder) to form uniformly sized ANTS-loaded LUVs. The extravesicular ANTS was separated from LUVs using PD-10 desalting columns (GE Healthcare) equilibrated in assay buffer (140 mM KNO3, 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.0). The sample was adjusted to a final volume of 2.5 ml with assay buffer prior to loading onto the column, and LUVs were eluted with 3 ml of assay buffer. For stopped-flow measurements, the liposome suspension was diluted to a final volume of 30 ml (10-fold dilution) to ensure optimal signal-to-noise, consistent with previously established protocols. Protein incorporation was comparable across samples, as confirmed by SDS-PAGE analysis, and liposome formation was verified by DLS measurements. These standardized conditions ensure consistent sample preparation across lipid compositions.

Stopped-flow Tl+ flux assay and data analysis

Stopped-flow Tl+ flux assay was performed using a SX-20 stopped-flow spectrophotometer (Applied Photophysics)82–84. MthK-containing liposomes were mixed with premix solution (140 mM KNO3 and 10 mM HEPES, 34.4 mM Ca(NO3)2, pH 11.7) and incubated in a delay loop for defined times. The sample was then immediately mixed with either control solution (140 mM KNO3, 10 mM HEPES, 17.2 mM Ca(NO3)2, pH 8.5) or quench solution (94 mM KNO3, 50 mM TlNO3, 10 mM HEPES, 17.2 mM Ca(NO3)2, pH 8.5) to record ANTS fluorescence. Each sample combination was performed six to ten times.

To monitor the blocking kinetics of free peptide, Δ2–17 MthK-containing liposomes were used. A synthetic peptide of N-terminal 17 amino acids (MVLVIEIIRKHLPRVLK, GenScript) was added to the premix solution and the quench solution to achieve a final concentration of 10 µM peptide in each reaction. Channel activities and blocking activities with the synthetic peptide were estimated from the initial rate of fluorescence quenching82. The data analysis was performed using MATLAB (MatWorks). The fluorescence-quenched profile was first normalized to the maximum activity of experimental controls, and then fitted using a stretched exponential function to the first 100 ms of the quench trace. The fluorescence quench rates were calculated using the τ0 and β values from the fits82. Three independent LUV preparations were assayed, and the reported results indicate mean ± S.D, unless reported otherwise. Statistical analyses were performed on log₁₀-transformed inactivation time constants. Differences among bilayer thickness conditions were evaluated using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test. Correlation between membrane thickness and inactivation kinetics was assessed using Pearson correlation analysis. All tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Lipid bilayer thickness measurement and data analysis

The HS-AFM sample support for bilayer analysis was a 1 mm2 diameter freshly cleaved mica surface, which was glued to a glass rod sample stage that is immersed in the fluid chamber during HS-AFM operation123. 2 µl of liposome solution at ~2 mg/ml was injected into the fluid chamber of ~100 µl volume. The formation of lipid bilayers could be observed ∼5 min after the injection124. Once bilayer patches were formed, the fluid chamber was rinsed to achieve a ~ 50% membrane surface coverage. For all lipids and lipid mixtures supplemented with decane, the dried lipids were resolvated in decane to a final concentration of ~0.5 mg/ml. A 2 μl drop of lipid-decane solution was pipetted on the HS-AFM sample support, and decane was allowed to evaporate during ~2 min in a humid chamber until only a film remained on the mica surface, similar to the procedure used for planar lipid bilayer experiments. Then, the sample was slowly rinsed with imaging buffer (400 mM KCl, 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.6) that allowed the lipid-decane membranes to swell and form bilayer membrane patches.

All HS-AFM images were taken by amplitude modulation HS-AFM (SS-NEX, RIBM, Japan). The cantilevers used were ultrashort cantilevers (USC-F1.2-k0.15, NanoWorld, Switzerland) with nominal spring constant k = 0.15 N/m and resonance frequency in air f0 = 1200 kHz (~650 kHz in liquid). Images of membrane patches in scan areas of 600 nm square were acquired at 1 s per frame and 300 × 300 pixels.

HS-AFM data were analyzed in ImageJ. Height distributions histograms were analyzed using Igor Pro Gaussian fitting. The images were X-Y-flattened with a 1st order plane fit. For the analysis, ~20 frames for each lipid condition were chosen. The resulting thicknesses were compared to other membranes of the same sample and between samples of the same composition.

Nanodisc reconstitution

MthK-containing nanodiscs were prepared as previously described20,25. Purified MthK was mixed with MSP1E3 (expressed from plasmid no. 20064, Addgene) and the lipid mixture of 14:1PC/POPG or 22:1PC/POPG (all 3:1 ratio) dissolved in 100 mM KCl, 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, and 2% w/v CHAPS (Anatrace) in the molar ratios 1:2:85 (MthK(monomer): MSP1E3: lipid mixture). Nanodisc reconstitution was conducted using detergent removal with Bio-Beads SM-2 (Bio Rad, 20 mg per 100 μl mixture). After 3 h of gentle shaking at room temperature, the mixture was separated, and the same amount of new Bio-Beads was added for an additional 12 h of shaking at room temperature. The supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm Spin-X centrifugation tube filter (Costar) prior to purification using a Superose 6 (GE Lifesciences) with 100 mM KCl and 20 mM HEPES, pH 8.5. The peak fraction corresponding to MthK-containing nanodiscs was collected and concentrated using a 100 KDa MWCO Amicon concentrator (Millipore).

Grid preparation and EM data collection

The cryo-EM samples were prepared as previously described20,25. Briefly, MthK nanodiscs (~10 mg/ml) were supplemented with 3 mM Fos8-F, and 5 mM CaCl2 immediately before grid freezing.

Data collection was done as described. For the MthK 14:1 PC/POPG dataset, 8016 images were collected on a Titan Krios with a K3 direct electron detector (UMass Krios). The pixel size is 1.06 Å/pixel, and the total dose is 47.49 e/Å2. For the MthK 22:1 PC/POPG dataset, 11,358 images were collected on a Titan Krios with a K3 direct electron detector (UMass Krios). The pixel size is 1.06 Å/pixel, and the total dose is 47.41 e/Å2.

Cryo-EM data processing and model building

The cryo-EM datasets were processed similarly to the previously described protocol25. Briefly, the initial refinement was done by CryoSPARC non-uniform refinement, and the rest of the process was done in Relion 3.0. The models were refined in Phenix, with the MthK open state crystal structure as the starting model.

MD simulations of MthK in lipid bilayers of varying thickness

MD simulations using the full-length MthK channel (PDB 6U68)25 embedded in lipid bilayers containing 912 lipids (C14:POPG or C22:POPG in ratio 3:1; 228 PG and 684 PC; to match that used in the cryo EM nanodiscs), hydrated by 80,345 (C14) or 76,694 (C22) TIP3P water molecules and 150 mM KCl solution (containing 688 K+ and 400 Cl- ions, to ensure a charge neutral system), with the systems containing 383,975 (C14) or 405,854 (C22) atoms. Using the end of the Umbrella Sampling simulations from ref. 25 with the N-terminus dissociated from the pore, the terminus was replicated and attached to each of the 4 subunits of MthK using the CHARMM program125. Two independent 2.0 μs simulations were performed for each lipid bilayer. Simulations were also run with pore-domain-only models (PDB:3LDC residues 18 to 99)63 in membranes with a range of lipid chain lengths. Five pore domain-only systems were created, with 3:1 mixtures of lipids with POPG, including lipids of ranging lengths (C14, C16, C18, C20 and C22), to match experiments, with each bilayer containing 189 PC and 63 PG lipids, 8,559-10,605 water, 101-112 K+ and 26-37 Cl- ions (66,683–69,606 atoms total). MD simulations used CHARMM36 lipid126 and CHARMM22 protein parameters127 with CMAP corrections128 and TIP3P water129. Equilibration and production simulations used the NAMD program, version 2.14130. Bias-exchange metadynamics of N-terminal interactions

BE-MTD was run using GROMACS 2023.1139,140 patched with Plumed 2.9141. We ran 12 replicas for each system (C14 or C22), with different replicas including bias on each one of the 4 N-termini using defined collective variables (CVs). Representative starting structures for BE-MTD metadynamics142 simulations were sampled from the above ANTON2 simulations based on K-means clustering143,144, followed by extended equilibration (see Supplementary Methods). The CVs used to bias the different replicas included the vertical z-distance of the N-terminus (center of mass of N and O atoms in residues 4–11) to the pore (center of mass of N and O atoms of residues 95–99), lateral xy-distance of the N-terminus to the pore axis and the system’s potential energy. Although all 4 N-termini were separately biased in different replicas, only the N-terminal on one subunit (we call it subunit A) was permitted to enter the pore, with restraints defined in the Supplementary Methods. All BE-MTD simulations were run within the NPT ensemble using flat-bottom restraints to hold ions and water molecules within the selectivity filter (maintaining ion configuration S0S2S4; Supplementary Methods). The free energy surfaces shown in Fig. 4 represent Boltzmann averages of the free energies over the replicas where the N-terminus of subunit A is permitted to bind, with convergence of these surfaces demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. 7 (see Supplementary Methods).
