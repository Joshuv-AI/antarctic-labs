On continuous 2-frieze patterns
Serge Tabachnikov

Abstract
We define and study a continuous version of 2-frieze patterns, a combinatorial structure closely related with frieze patterns of Coxeter and Conway. We describe the relation of continuous 2-friezes with the moduli space of projective curves and relate the (pre)symplectic structure on the space of closed 2-friezes, considered as a cluster variety, with the Adler-Gelfand-Dikii bracket on the space of 3rd order differential operators.

Motivation: continuous frieze patterns

Frieze patterns (or, simply, friezes) were introduced by Coxeter and studied by Conway and Coxeter in the early 1970s; the subject has become very popular with the advent of the theory of cluster algebras in the 2000s; see
[
9
]
for a comprehensive survey. Recall the definition.

A frieze pattern is an array of numbers

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccccc}&0&&0&&0&&0&&\\
&&1&&1&&1&&\\
&a_{1}&&a_{2}&&a_{3}&&a_{4}&&\\
&&a_{1}a_{2}-1&&a_{2}a_{3}-1&&a_{3}a_{4}-1&&\\
&\cdots&&a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}-a_{1}-a_{3}&&a_{2}a_{3}a_{4}-a_{2}-a_{4}&&\cdots\\
&&\cdots&&\cdots&&\cdots&&\\
\end{array}

in which the relation

\begin{matrix}&N&\\
W&&E\\
&S&\end{matrix}\qquad\longmapsto\qquad EW-NS=1

holds for every elementary “diamond”. A frieze is closed if, going down, another row of 1s appear, followed by a row of 0s. The number of rows, bordered by the two rows of 1s, is the frieze pattern’s width.

Frieze patterns are discrete combinatorial objects, closely related with difference equations and polygons. A continuous counterpart of a difference equation is a differential equation, and that of a polygon is a curve. This point of view led us to define and study in
[
16
]
continuous analogs of frieze patterns. Let us briefly describe this work.

Label the entries of a frieze as

\begin{array}[]{ccc}&v_{i,j}&\\
v_{i,j-1}&&v_{i+1,j}\\
&v_{i+1,j-1}&\end{array}

with
v_{i,i}=a_{i}
, the entries of the first non-trivial row.
Then the SE diagonals of a closed frieze satisfy the 2nd order linear recurrence

V_{i+1}=a_{i}V_{i}-V_{i-1}

with
n
-periodic coefficients, whose solutions are antiperiodic:

V_{i+n}=-V_{i},

that is, the monodromy of the equation is -Id.

Consider the South-East direction as the first, and the North-East as the second coordinate direction, and replace
v_{ij}
with a smooth function of two variables
H(x,y)
:

\begin{array}[]{ccc}&v_{i,j}&\\
v_{i,j-1}&&v_{i+1,j}\\
&v_{i+1,j-1}&\end{array}\ \ \longmapsto\ \ \begin{array}[]{ccc}&H(x,y+{\varepsilon})&\\
H(x,y)&&H(x+{\varepsilon},y+{\varepsilon}).\\
&H(x+{\varepsilon},y)&\end{array}

Then the diamond rule yields
{\varepsilon}^{2}(HH_{xy}-H_{x}H_{y})=1+({\varepsilon}^{3})
, leading to the next definition.

Definition 1

A closed continuous frieze pattern is
a smooth function
F(x,y)
satisfying:

\begin{array}[]{rl}HH_{xy}-H_{x}H_{y}=1;\\
H(x,x)=0,\ H_{y}(x,x)=1,\ H_{x}(x,x)=-1&{\rm for\ all}\ x;\\
H(x,y)>0&{\rm for}\ x<y<x+T;\\
H(x+T,y)=H(x,y+T)=-H(x,y)&{\rm for\ all}\ x,y.\end{array}

One of the main results of
[
16
]
is the description of continuous friezes in terms of
SL_{2}
-equivalence classes of projective curves and the respective Hill operators. These are objects of projective differential geometry, see
[
15
]
. Here are the relevant notions.

A projective curve is a diffeomorphism
{\gamma}:{\mathbb{R}}/{\rm T}{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{RP}}^{1}.

Projective curves are considered modulo
SL_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. One lifts
{\gamma}
to a curve
\Gamma(x)\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, normalized by the condition
\det({\Gamma},{\Gamma}^{\prime})=1
. Then
{\Gamma}^{\prime\prime}(x)=-k(x){\Gamma}(x)
, that is, the coordinates of
{\Gamma}
satisfy the Hill differential equation
y^{\prime\prime}(x)=-k(x)y(x)
with
T
-periodic potential
k(x)
and
T
-anti-periodic solutions:
y(x+T)=-y(x)
. The equivalence classes of projective curves are in 1-1 correspondence with such Hill differential operators
d^{2}+k(x)
.

Theorem 1
(
[
16
]
)

All continuous frieze patterns are obtained from
SL_{2}
-equivalence classes of convex projective curves:
H(x,y)=\det(\Gamma(x),\Gamma(y)).

Explicitly, a function
f(x)
(a projective curve in affine coordinate) yields a continuous frieze

H(x,y)=\frac{f(y)-f(x)}{\sqrt{f^{\prime}(x)f^{\prime}(y)}}.

For example, if
f(x)=\tan x
, then
H(x,y)=\sin(y-x)
, and if
f(x)=x
, then
H(x,y)=y-x
.

Another result of
[
16
]
relates the (pre)symplectic form on the space of frieze patters, associated with its structure of cluster variety with the Kirillov symplectic form on the modulo space of projective curves, the space that can be regarded as a coadjoint action of the Virasoro algebra (see, e.g.,
[
15
]
for this material). One has

Theorem 2
(
[
16
]
)

The space
{\rm Diff_{+}}(S^{1})/SL_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
, equipped with Kirillov’s symplectic form, is a continuous limit of the space of frieze patterns, equipped with the cluster(pre)symplectic form.

Our goal is to obtain analogs of these results for 2-frieze patterns.

3
Continuous 2-friezes and continuous
SL_{3}
-tilings

A 2-frieze pattern (or just a 2–frieze) is a grid of numbers satisfying the local relations

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccccc}&&&B&F&&&&\\
&&A&E&D&H&&\\
&&&C&G&&&&\end{array}\qquad\longmapsto\qquad\ E=AD-BC,\ D=EH-FG,

(1)

see
[
10
]
. Note that, unlike frieze patterns, the next rows are not offset relative to the previous ones.
Similalrly to frieze patterns, we label the entries of a 2-frieze as follows:

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccccc}&&(i-2,j)&&(i-1,j+1)&&(i,j+2)&&&\\
&&(i-1,j-1)&&(i,j)&&(i+1,j+1)&&&\\
&&(i,j-2)&&(i+1,j-1)&&(i+2,j)&&&\end{array}

A closed 2-frieze is bounded from above by two rows of zeros, followed by a row of ones, and from below, by a row of ones, followed by two rows of zeros. We refer to it as the upper and lower boundary conditions.

A related notion is that of
SL_{3}
-tiling. This is an array of numbers on the same (rotated
45^{\circ}
) grid as the frieze patterns,
such that every elementary
3\times 3
determinant equals 1; see
[
3
]
. An
SL_{3}
-tiling is called tame if
every
4\times 4
determinant of the adjacent numbers vanishes; equivalently, the rank of the infinite matrix formed by this array equals 3. The relation between 2-friezes and
SL_{3}
-tilings is that the numbers
v_{i,j}
of a 2-frieze with even index
i
form an
SL_{3}
-tiling, and likewise for odd
i
(Proposition 3.3 in
[
10
]
).

Define continuous 2-friezes and continuous
SL_{3}
-tilings. First, the heuristics.
As before, replace the 2-indexed arrays of numbers by functions of two variables, and the unit shift of an index by the increase or decrease of variables by an infinitesimal quantity,
2{\varepsilon}
. This leads to the following consideration.

We have two smooth function,
F(x,y)
and
G(x,y)
. Assume that
E
in (
1
) corresponds to
G(x,y)
. Then the values of its neighbors are

A=F(x-{\varepsilon},y-{\varepsilon}),B=F(x-{\varepsilon},y+{\varepsilon}),C=F(x+{\varepsilon},y-{\varepsilon}),D=F(x+{\varepsilon},y+{\varepsilon}),

and we obtain the equation

G(x,y)=F(x-{\varepsilon},y-{\varepsilon})F(x+{\varepsilon},y+{\varepsilon})-F(x-{\varepsilon},y+{\varepsilon})F(x+{\varepsilon},y-{\varepsilon}).

Expanding to the second order in
{\varepsilon}
and normalizing by dividing by
4{\varepsilon}^{2}
yields

G(x,y)=F(x,y)F_{xy}(x,y)-F_{x}(x,y)F_{y}(x,y),

where the subscripts denote the partial derivatives. One has a similar equation where
F
and
G
are swapped.

Next consider the upper boundary condition of a 2-frieze. The coordinates of a horizontal row satisfy
y-x=const
, and we take
F(x,x)=0
as an analog of the statement that the top row consists of zeros. The row below it also consists of zeros, hence, in the linear approximation,
F(x+2{\varepsilon},y)=F(x,y-2{\varepsilon})=0
, that is,
F_{x}(x,x)=F_{y}(x,x)=0
. The row below it consists of ones, implying that

F(x,y-2{\varepsilon})=F(x+{\varepsilon},y-{\varepsilon})=F(x+2{\varepsilon},y),

thus, calculating modulo
{\varepsilon}^{3}
and normalizing, we obtain

F_{xx}(x,x)=F_{yy}(x,x)=1,F_{xy}(x,x)=-1.

Likewise for
G
.

We have an analog of the periodicity property of closed 2-friezes along its diagonals:

F(x+T,y)=F(x,y+T)=F(x,y),

where
T
is a period. We arrive at the next definition.

Definition 2

A continuous 2-frieze is a couple of smooth functions of two variables,
F(x,y)
and
G(x,y)
, satisfying the identities

G=FF_{xy}-F_{x}F_{y},\ F=GG_{xy}-G_{x}G_{y}.

(2)

A closed continuous 2-frieze satisfies the following boundary conditions:

\displaystyle F(x,x)=

\displaystyle F_{x}(x,x)=F_{y}(x,x)=0,F_{xx}(x,x)=F_{yy}(x,x)=1,

(3)

\displaystyle F_{xy}(x,x)=-1,\ F(x+T,y)=F(x,y+T)=F(x,y),

and likewise for
G
.
A closed continuous 2-frieze is positive if
F(x,y)>0
for
x<y<x+T
, and likewise for
G
.

Similarly, we have

Definition 3

A continuous
SL_{3}
-tiling is a smooth function
F(x,y)
satisfying the identity

\det\begin{pmatrix}F&F_{x}&F_{xx}\\
F_{y}&F_{xy}&F_{xxy}\\
F_{yy}&F_{xyy}&F_{xxyy}\\
\end{pmatrix}=1.

(4)

It is tame if, in addition, the identity holds

\det\begin{pmatrix}F&F_{x}&F_{xx}&F_{xxx}\\
F_{y}&F_{xy}&F_{xxy}&F_{xxxy}\\
F_{yy}&F_{xyy}&F_{xxyy}&F_{xxxyy}\\
F_{yyy}&F_{xyyy}&F_{xxyyy}&F_{xxxyyy}\end{pmatrix}=0.

An example of a closed continuous positive 2-frieze is

F(x,y)=G(x,y)=1-\cos(x-y).

The same function is an example of a tame continuous
SL_{3}
-tiling.

4
Continuos 2-friezes and projective curves

The next lemma relates continuous 2-friezes and continuous
SL_{3}
-tilings. It is an analog of Proposition 3.3 in
[
10
]
.

Lemma 4

1) Let
F
be a continuous
SL_{3}
-tiling. Define
G=FF_{xy}-F_{x}F_{y}
. Then
F=GG_{xy}-G_{x}G_{y}
, that is,
(F,G)
is a continuous 2-frieze.

2) If
(F,G)
is a closed positive continuous 2-frieze then
F
and
G
are continuous
SL_{3}
-tilings.

Proof.

In one direction, since
G=FF_{xy}-F_{x}F_{y}
, we have

G_{x}=FF_{xxy}-F_{y}F_{xx},\ G_{y}=FF_{xyy}-F_{x}F_{yy},\ G_{xy}=FF_{xxyy}-F_{xx}F_{yy},

that is, these partial derivatives of
G
are the
2
minors of the matrix (
4
).
Therefore, by the Dodgson condensation,

F=F\det\begin{pmatrix}F&F_{x}&F_{xx}\\
F_{y}&F_{xy}&F_{xxy}\\
F_{yy}&F_{xyy}&F_{xxyy}\\
\end{pmatrix}=GG_{xy}-G_{x}G_{y}.

Conversely, if
(F,G)
is a closed positive continuous 2-frieze, then

F=GG_{xy}-G_{x}G_{y}=F\det\begin{pmatrix}F&F_{x}&F_{xx}\\
F_{y}&F_{xy}&F_{xxy}\\
F_{yy}&F_{xyy}&F_{xxyy}\\
\end{pmatrix},

and canceling
F
implies that
F
is a continuous
SL_{3}
-tiling. Likewise for
G
.

\Box

Projective curves and differential operators.

By projective curves we mean parameterized locally convex curves in
{\mathbb{RP}}^{2}
, considered up to the natural action of the group
SL_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
on the projective plane. Since a non-contractible closed curve in
{\mathbb{RP}}^{2}
has an odd number of inflection points, a closed locally convex curve in
{\mathbb{RP}}^{2}
is contractible. A projective curve is
convex
if every line intersects it at most twice, multiplicity counted.

Let
{\gamma}(x)
be a projective curve. We lift it to a curve
{\Gamma}(x)\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. The lift is not unique, and we fix it by asking that
\det({\Gamma},{\Gamma}_{x},{\Gamma}_{xx})=1
. If
{\gamma}(x)=(f(x)\mathpunct{:}g(x)\mathpunct{:}1)
, then
{\Gamma}(x)=W^{-\frac{1}{3}}(f(x)\mathpunct{:}g(x)\mathpunct{:}1)
, where

W=\det\begin{pmatrix}f_{x}&g_{x}\\
f_{xx}&g_{xx}\end{pmatrix}

is the Wronskian.
The lift is considered up to the action of
SL_{3}({\mathbb{R}})
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. If
{\gamma}
is closed, so is its lift: this follows from the fact that
{\gamma}
is contractible.

Differentiating
\det({\Gamma},{\Gamma}_{x},{\Gamma}_{xx})=1
, we see that
{\Gamma}_{xxx}(x)
is a linear combination of
{\Gamma}(x)
and
{\Gamma}_{x}(x)
, that is, the components of the curve
{\Gamma}(x)
are independent solutions of the differential equation
{\Gamma}_{xxx}(x)+q(x){\Gamma}_{x}(x)+r(x){\Gamma}(x)
. If the curve is
T
-periodic, so are the functions
q(x)
and
r(x)
, and all the solutions of this differential equation are
T
-periodic as well.

Thus, to a locally convex closed projective curve
{\gamma}
there corresponds the differential operator
L=d^{3}+q(x)d+r(x)
with periodic coefficients and all periodic solutions, and projectively equivalent curves correspond to the same operator. This correspondence is 1-to-1, see
[
15
]
. If the curve is convex, the differential operator is
disconjugate
: every solution of the respective differential equation has at most two zeros per period.

To a projective curve
{\gamma}
there corresponds its dual
{\gamma}^{*}
, a curve in the dual projective plane
({\mathbb{RP}}^{2})^{*}
which consists of the tangent lines to
{\gamma}
. If
{\gamma}
corresponds to a differential operator
L
, then
{\gamma}^{*}
corresponds to the formally dual operator
L^{*}
.

Identify 3-space with its dual via a Euclidean structure. The next statement is standard.

Lemma 5

The lift of the dual curve is given by
{\Gamma}^{*}(x)={\Gamma}(x)\times{\Gamma}_{x}(x)
.

Proof.

We need to check two things: that
{\Gamma}(x)\times{\Gamma}_{x}(x)
, considered as covector via the dot product, annihilates Span
({\Gamma}(x),{\Gamma}_{x}(x))
, and that
{\Gamma}^{*}(x)
also satisfies the unit determinant condition.

The first follows from the fact that
u\times v
is orthogonal to
u
and
v
for any two vectors
u
and
v
.
For the second, one has

({\Gamma}\times{\Gamma}^{\prime})^{\prime}={\Gamma}\times{\Gamma}^{\prime\prime},({\Gamma}\times{\Gamma}^{\prime})^{\prime\prime}={\Gamma}^{\prime}\times{\Gamma}^{\prime\prime}+{\Gamma}\times{\Gamma}^{\prime\prime\prime}.

Since
\det({\Gamma},{\Gamma}^{\prime},{\Gamma}^{\prime\prime})=1
, it follows that
\det({\Gamma},{\Gamma}^{\prime},{\Gamma}^{\prime\prime\prime})=0
, that is,
{\Gamma}^{\prime\prime\prime}
is a linear combination of
{\Gamma}
and
{\Gamma}^{\prime}
. Finally,

\displaystyle\det({\Gamma}\times{\Gamma}^{\prime},({\Gamma}\times{\Gamma}^{\prime})^{\prime},

\displaystyle({\Gamma}\times{\Gamma}^{\prime})^{\prime\prime})=\det({\Gamma}\times{\Gamma}^{\prime},{\Gamma}\times{\Gamma}^{\prime\prime},{\Gamma}^{\prime}\times{\Gamma}^{\prime\prime}+{\Gamma}\times{\Gamma}^{\prime\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=\det({\Gamma}\times{\Gamma}^{\prime},{\Gamma}\times{\Gamma}^{\prime\prime},{\Gamma}^{\prime}\times{\Gamma}^{\prime\prime})=\det({\Gamma},{\Gamma}^{\prime}{\Gamma}^{\prime\prime})=1,

where we used the vector algebra identities

(u\times v)\times w=(u\cdot w)v-(v\cdot w)u,\ \det(u,v,w)=u\cdot(v\times w).

This concludes the proof.

\Box

Continuous
SL_{3}
-tilings and 2-friezes from projective curves.

Let
{\gamma}(x)\subset{\mathbb{RP}}^{2}
and
{\delta}(y)\subset({\mathbb{RP}}^{2})^{*}
be projective curves, let
{\Gamma}(x)
and
{\Delta}(y)
be their lifts to
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and
({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{*}
respectively, and let
\cdot
be the pairing of vectors and covectors. We consider pairs of curves up to the natural diagonal action of
SL_{3}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{*}
.

The next result is an analog of Lemma 4.1 in
[
10
]
.

Proposition 6

Let
F(x,y)={\Gamma}(x)\cdot{\Delta}(y)
. Then
F
is a tame continuous
SL_{3}
-tiling. Conversely, every tame continuous
SL_{3}
-tiling is obtained in this way.

Proof.

Let
U(x)
be the
3\times 3
matrix whose columns are
{\Gamma},{\Gamma}_{x},{\Gamma}_{xx}
and
V(y)
be the matrix whose rows are
{\Delta},{\Delta}_{y},{\Delta}_{yy}
. Then
V(y)U(x)
is the matrix in (
4
). By the definition of the lift,
\det U(x)=\det V(y)=1
, hence (
4
) holds.

Similarly, let
\bar{U}(x)
be the
4\times 4
matrix whose columns are
{\Gamma},{\Gamma}_{x},{\Gamma}_{xx},{\Gamma}_{xxx}
and likewise for
\bar{V}(y)
. One has
\det\bar{U}(x)=0
, and likewise for
\bar{V}(y)
. It follows that
\det\bar{V}(y)\bar{U}(x)=0
, as needed.

The converse statement follows from a general criterion for a function of two variables
F(x,y)
to be represented as
\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}(x)v_{i}(y)
; such functions are called
separable
. Define the Wronskians

W_{n}(F)(x,y)=\det((\partial^{i}_{x}\partial^{j}_{y}F)(x,y)),\ i,j=0,\ldots,n.

Assume that
W_{n-1}(F)\neq 0
everywhere and
W_{n}(F)=0
identically. Then there exist functions
u_{i}(x),v_{i}(y)
such that
F(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}(x)v_{i}(y)
, see
[
4
]
(and also
[
12
,
13
]
, where a similar result is obtained for functions of more than two variables).

In our situation, the
3\times 3
Wronskians are equal to 1, and the
4\times 4
ones vanish. Then

{\Gamma}(x)=(u_{1}(x),u_{2}(x),u_{3}(x)),\ {\Delta}(y)=(v_{1}(y),v_{2}(y),v_{3}(y)),

and
F(x,y)={\Gamma}(x)\cdot{\Delta}(y)
, as needed.

\Box

It follows that if
G
is defined by
G=FF_{xy}-F_{x}F_{y}
, then
(F,G)
is a continuous 2-frieze.

Lemma 7

One has

G(x,y)={\Delta}^{*}(y)\cdot{\Gamma}^{*}(x).

Proof.

One has the identity

(a\times b)\cdot(c\times d)=(a\cdot c)(b\cdot d)-(a\cdot d)(b\cdot c).

Since

{\Gamma}^{*}(x)={\Gamma}(x)\times{\Gamma}_{x}(x),\ {\Delta}^{*}(y)={\Delta}(y)\times{\Delta}_{y}(y),

one obtains

{\Delta}(y)^{*}\cdot{\Gamma}(x)^{*}=({\Delta}(y)\times{\Delta}_{y}(y))\cdot({\Gamma}(x)\times{\Gamma}_{x}(x))=FF_{xy}-F_{x}F_{y}=G,

as needed.

\Box

Closed positive continuous 2-friezes.

When does a pair of
T
-periodic projective curves
{\gamma}
and
{\delta}
define a closed 2-frieze and when is it positive? The answer is given by the next theorem.

Proposition 8

1) The continuous 2-frieze, corresponding to closed projective curves
({\gamma},{\delta})
, is closed if and only if
{\delta}={\gamma}^{*}
.

2) This closed continuous 2-frieze is positive if and only if
{\gamma}
is convex.

Proof.

If the continuous 2-frieze is closed then
{\Gamma}(x)\cdot{\Delta}(x)={\Gamma}_{x}(x)\cdot{\Delta}(x)=0
. This implies that

{\Delta}(x)
is proportional to
{\Gamma}^{*}(x)={\Gamma}(x)\times{\Gamma}_{x}(x)
, and since
1=\det({\Gamma},{\Gamma}_{x},{\Gamma}_{xx})={\Gamma}_{xx}\cdot({\Gamma}\times{\Gamma}_{x})
, the condition
{\Gamma}_{xx}(x)\cdot{\Delta}(x)=1
holds for
{\Delta}={\Gamma}^{*}
.

Assume that
{\Delta}={\Gamma}^{*}
, that is,
F(x,y)={\Gamma}(x)\cdot({\Gamma}(y)\times{\Gamma}_{y}(y))
. Arguing as above, we have

F(x,x)=F_{x}(x,x)=0
and
F_{xx}(x,x)=1
. Since the projective duality is an involution, we can interchange
{\Gamma}(x)
and
{\Gamma}^{*}(y)
and conclude that
F_{y}(x,x)=0,F_{yy}(x,x)=1
. Finally,

F_{xy}(x,x)={\Gamma}_{x}(x)\cdot({\Gamma}(x)\times{\Gamma}_{xx}(x))=\det({\Gamma}_{x}(x),{\Gamma}(x),{\Gamma}_{xx}(x))=-1,

as needed.

If
{\gamma}
is convex then it has the following Property T:

every the tangent line to
{\gamma}
does not have other common points with
{\gamma}
.

It follows that
F(x,y)={\Gamma}(x)\cdot{\Gamma}^{*}(y)
does not vanish for
x<y<x+T
. To show that
F(x,y)>0
, let
y=x+{\varepsilon}
. Then
F(x,y)=\frac{{\varepsilon}^{2}}{2}F_{xx}(x,x)>0
.

Conversely, assume that
F(x,y)>0
for
x<y<x+T
. We need to show that
{\gamma}
is a convex curve. Note that
{\gamma}
has Property T.
Indeed, if the tangent line at
{\gamma}(y)
contains point
{\gamma}(x)
then

0={\Gamma}(x)\cdot({\Gamma}(y)\times{\Gamma}_{y}(y))=F(x,y)
.

It remains to prove Property T implies that the curve is convex. Consider the tangent line at
{\gamma}(0)
as the line at infinity. Then the curve
{\gamma}(x),\ x\neq 0
, lies in the affine plane. If it is not convex, its convex hull is different from
{\gamma}
, hence the curve has a double tangent line, contradicting Property T.

\Box

.

Proof.

It follows from Proposition

, Lemma

, and Proposition

We summarize the results obtained so far.

Theorem 3

Closed continuous 2-frieze patterns are in 1-to-1 correspondence with projective equivalence classes of closed projective curves. A closed continuous 2-frieze pattern is positive if and only if the respective projective curve is convex.

Example 10
