Background

Seed vigor represents a seed’s ability to germinate rapidly and uniformly, produce strong seedlings, and maintain viability under diverse and challenging storage conditions. Accurate vigor assessment is essential for improving crop establishment, optimizing resource utilization, and enhancing agricultural productivity. However, conventional evaluation methods are often labor-intensive, time-consuming, and destructive. To address these limitations, this study proposes an image-based framework for classifying individual rice seed vigor [[1], [2], [3]]. The methodology is structured to support objective and reproducible assessments while supporting future deployment in practical agricultural applications.

The framework incorporates two widely adopted modeling strategies within a unified workflow. First, traditional machine learning (ML) models are developed using hand-crafted features extracted from seed images [4,5]. These features include morphological characteristics (e.g., size, shape, and structural descriptors) and color-based attributes associated with seed quality [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. This feature-engineering approach leverages domain knowledge to construct interpretable predictors and facilitate model explainability. Second, deep learning (DL) models based on convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]] are employed to automatically learn hierarchical visual representations directly from raw RGB images. By eliminating the need for manual feature engineering, CNNs can capture complex visual patterns that may not be adequately represented by predefined descriptors. In addition, a hybrid framework is explored by concatenating feature representations derived from both ML and DL approaches into a unified predictive model. Rather than introducing novel architectures, this study focuses on the systematic implementation, integration, and evaluation of established approaches under a consistent experimental framework.

To capture the dynamic nature of seed development, both single-time-point and multi-time-point image analysis strategies are examined. The single-time-point approach enables rapid assessment based on images captured at a specific growth stage, which is practical for streamlined operational workflows. In contrast, the multi-time-point framework integrates images collected across multiple developmental stages, allowing the model to incorporate temporal growth patterns into its classification process. This strategy provides a more comprehensive representation of seed vigor, particularly when developmental progression contains diagnostically relevant information. While conceptually related to existing temporal and ensemble methods, its application is structured and evaluated in the context of seed vigor assessment using standard RGB imaging.

To ensure practical applicability, Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) interpretability techniques [17] are embedded within the framework to support better understanding of model behavior and informed decision-marking. For ML models, SHAP quantifies the contribution of individual morphological and color features, while for DL models it highlights image regions that influence classification outcomes. Such interpretability mechanisms improve transparency, user trust and regulatory compliance, which are important considerations for agricultural decision-support systems. In addition to interpretability, computational efficiency and inference latency are also evaluated to assess the feasibility of real-world implementation.

Overall, the proposed framework demonstrates how standard RGB imaging can be integrated with machine learning, deep learning, temporal analysis, and interpretability techniques to enable a coherent and reusable methodological system for automated seed vigor assessment. The primary contribution lies in the structured integration and systematic evaluation of these components using both in-distribution and external validation datasets to assess model robustness and generalizability. Compared with conventional vigor assessment methods for naturally aged rice seeds as opposed to those artificially aged through high humidity and temperatures [18], the proposed framework substantially reduces evaluation time. Traditional laboratory vigor tests require 480 hours [19], while a previously published image-based classification approach used to generate ground truth annotations in this study requires approximately 100 hours. In contrast, the proposed framework predicts seed vigor using images acquired within the first 48 hours of germination. Furthermore, standard RGB imaging provides a cost-effective alternative to spectral imaging systems, which require specialized and substantially more expensive equipment [20]. Taken together, the proposed method provides a rapid, low-cost, and non-destructive early screening tool capable of approximating emergence-based vigor assessment while substantially reducing evaluation time. Beyond reducing evaluation time, the framework establishes a foundation for data-driven seed quality monitoring and broader applications in precision agriculture.

Method details

This study developed a comprehensive ML and DL framework for the automatic classification of rice seed vigor using image analysis. The workflow consisted of four stages: (1) data acquisition and ground-truth annotation, (2) data preprocessing, (3) model development using ML, DL, and hybrid approaches, and (4) evaluation of single-time-point versus multi-time-point classification strategies coupled with model interpretability analysis. Data collection and ground truth annotation

Rice seed samples were obtained from three batches cultivated in Thailand including Khao Dawk Mali 105 (Ubon Ratchathani Rice Research Center), Khao Dawk Mali 105 (Suphan Buri farmer’s field), and Pathum Thani 1 (Chainat Rice Research Center). An additional batch of Glutinous Rice 6 obtained from Ubon Ratchathani Rice Research Center, representing a different rice species from those used for model development, was reserved as an external test set to assess model generalizability. These seeds were germinated in trays having 100 seeds per tray and four trays per variety. Germination tests were conducted using the top-of-paper technique [20]. Particularly, seeds were placed on two layers of steel-blue germination blotter paper (Anchor®, Minnesota) within covered containers to minimize moisture loss and subsequently transferred to a cabinet germinator. Germination was conducted at 25°C under continuous illumination and controlled humidity conditions. Root emergence and seedling development were monitored through visual observation and image acquisition. Images were captured using a laboratory-scale imaging system [21] consisting of a digital single-lens reflex camera (Nikon D5200 with Nikkor AF-S 60 mm f/2.8 G Micro ED; Nikon) mounted on a reproduction stand. Uniform indirect illumination was provided by two vertically positioned fluorescent Tl-tubes located 100 cm on either side of the camera, with precautions taken to minimize reflections. The camera was set to manual control (ISO 500, f/16, 1/15 s, autofocus). The distance between the camera and tray was approximately 50 cm, and a positioning mask was used to ensure consistent tray placement. Images were acquired at 0, 24, and 48 hour after germination initiation.

After sample preparation, ground-truth vigor labels were generated using a radicle emergence test. The test was conducted on all seed samples, with physiological germination assessed base on 2 mm radicle emergence. Several vigor-related indices, including maximum radicle emergence, mean emergence time, emergence speed, emergence uniformity, and the area under the cumulative emergence curve, were calculated using GERMINATOR software [22]. The normalized indices were subsequently clustered using K-means clustering, with the optimal number of clusters determined by silhouette analysis [21]. All samples were categorized into two classes—high-vigor and low-vigor—which were used as ground truth annotations for model training. The clustering-based labeling approach was selected to provide an objective and reproducible method for separating seeds with relatively distinct vigor characteristics. It is worth noting that our proposed framework should be interpreted as identifying vigor-associated phenotypic patterns under the adopted labeling strategy rather than fully representing the complete characteristics of seed vigor.

Image preprocessing and data partition

A data preprocessing pipeline was developed to facilitate seed-level analysis from the original tray-level images. Individual seeds were identified using an image segmentation procedure based on YOLO26-seg model [23]. The resulting regions of interest were extracted and used for subsequent feature extraction and image-based classification. This process allowed independent analysis of individual seeds, improving the granularity of the dataset and enabling more precise model predictions. The preprocessed dataset for model development consisted of 1200 seed samples with a high-vigor to low-vigor ratio of 800:400 (67%:33%). The data were stratified into three mutually exclusive sets: 840 samples (70%) for training, 180 samples (15%) for validation, and 180 samples (15%) for testing, while maintaining the original class imbalance ratio. In addition to the in-distribution dataset, an external test set was prepared to evaluate model generalizability, consisting of 300 high-vigor seeds (75%) and 100 low-vigor seeds (25%).

Model development

For ML model development, balanced random forest classifiers were developed using handcrafted features extracted from segmented seed images. Each decision tree was trained on a bootstrap sample balanced via random resampling, ensuring that both classes contribute more equally during training and reducing bias toward the majority class. The feature set comprised morphological descriptors (as shown in Table 1) and statistical color features extracted from multiple color spaces [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. For each channel, first-order and higher-order statistics, including the mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis of pixel intensities, were computed. The color spaces considered were RGB, normalized RGB, HIS, YCrCgCb, I1I2I3, and CIELAB (Lab*). For the DL framework, three CNN architectures known for their effectiveness in image classification tasks, including ResNet18, MobileNetV2, and VGG16, were evaluated using Focal loss to mitigate class imbalance. They offer a balance of depth, efficiency, and parameter complexity suited for different trade-offs between accuracy and computational cost. Additionally, transfer learning was employed using ImageNet-pretrained weights. Two fine-tuning strategies were investigated: (1) training only the fully connected layers while freezing convolutional layers and (2) end-to-end fine-tuning of the entire network. The hybrid framework combined feature representations extracted from both ML and DL models to investigate whether integrating handcrafted and learned features could improve predictive performance.

To systematically evaluate the contribution of temporal information, experiments were conducted using both individual and combined germination time points. Single-time-point models utilized images collected at 0, 24, or 48 hour individually, whereas multi-time-point models integrated observations from multiple developmental stages (0 + 24 hour and 0 + 24 + 48 hour). For ML models, features extracted from multiple time points were concatenated into a unified feature set prior to classification. Prior to training, all features were standardized, and model hyperparameters were optimized using Optuna with three-fold stratified cross-validation [28]. The final model was retrained using the optimal hyperparameter configuration and evaluated on the validation and test datasets. For DL models, feature representations extracted from each time point were fused within the fully connected layers of the network. This design enabled the model to jointly learn from multiple time steps, capturing temporal dependencies and progressive changes in seed characteristics. By comparing these two approaches, we assessed whether incorporating multi-time-step information improved classification performance compared to analyzing individual time point separately. The corresponding model architectures, additional implementation details and hyperparameter settings are provided in Supplementary S1.

Model performance was evaluated using standard binary classification metrics derived from the confusion matrix, including accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, F1-score, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). In this study, low-vigor seeds were designated as the positive class due to their greater practical importance for seed quality assessment. Precision measures the proportion of correctly identified low-vigor seeds among all seeds predicted as low vigor, whereas recall reflects the model's ability to detect actual low-vigor seeds. The F1-score, which combines precision and recall, was used to provide a balanced assessment of classification performance. Specificity was also calculated to evaluate the model's ability to correctly identify high-vigor seeds. In addition, AUC was used to assess the model's discriminative capability across different classification thresholds. Given the class imbalance in the dataset, particular emphasis was placed on recall, F1-score, and AUC, as these metrics provide a more informative evaluation of minority-class detection than accuracy alone.

Model interpretability

To improve model interpretability, feature importance was analyzed for both ML and DL models. For the random forest models, feature importance scores and Tree SHAP values grounded in Shapely values from game theory were used to quantify the contribution of individual features to model predictions. Tree SHAP provides a consistent and interpretable measure of feature importance while accounting for complex feature interactions. For the DL models, Gradient SHAP was employed to identify image regions that contributed most strongly to classification outcomes. By estimating the contribution of individual pixels relative to a set of reference images, Gradient SHAP highlights the visual patterns driving model decisions. These approaches help identify potential biases or unexpected patterns that may affect model performance. More importantly, it enhances explainability, which is crucial for validation and communication with agricultural experts.

Method validation

An automated framework was developed to assess the vigor of individual rice seeds. Ground truth labels for training ML and DL models were assigned by categorizing seed samples into high- and low-vigor groups based on radicle emergence-related characteristics. Although seed vigor is inherently a continuous biological property, a binary classification scheme was adopted in this study to establish a practical framework and reduce labeling uncertainty arising from limited sample size along with overlapping intermediate vigor states. The distribution between these categories was 67% high-vigor and 33% low-vigor in this dataset. This classification scheme is also consistent with commercial seed grading practices, where seed lots are typically categorized into a small number of quality classes (a maximum of 2–3 classes) in order to facilitate efficient value chain management and decision-making processes.

The primary objective of this study was to identify an effective data-driven approach for rapid rice seed vigor classification using ML and DL models. To capture temporal changes in seed development, models were evaluated using both single-time-point and multi-time-point data. Table 2 summarizes the performance of the evaluated models on the internal test set, while training and validation results are provided in Supplementary S2. The relatively small performance differences between the validation and test sets suggest limited overfitting and indicate good generalization within the in-distribution dataset. For the DL models, transfer learning combined with fine-tuning consistently outperformed the corresponding models without fine-tuning, with detailed results presented in Supplementary S3.

Overall, the standalone ML and fine-tuned DL models produced comparable results, with minor differences in evaluation metrics. A hybrid framework combining handcrafted morphological and color features with features extracted from a fine-tuned VGG16 model was also investigated. Although the hybrid framework achieved better performance, the improvement was relatively marginal compared with the increased model complexity and computational requirements. Therefore, the standalone ML and DL models were selected for further analysis as they offered a more favorable balance between predictive performance and implementation simplicity. To further assess the model’s effectiveness, confusion matrices and ROC curves were generated for the top-performing ML and DL models, as shown in Fig. 2, Fig. 3. These visualizations provide a deeper view of classification accuracy, error distribution and the model’s ability to distinguish between high- and low-vigor seeds.

As shown in Table 2, multi-time-point models generally outperformed the single-time-point models, demonstrating the value of incorporating temporal information into seed vigor classification. The ablation study comparing the 0, 0 + 24, and 0 + 24 + 48 hour models further confirmed that integrating observations from multiple developmental stages consistently improved predictive performance, as seeds may exhibit varying germination patterns at different stages. Overall, ML and DL models achieved comparable performance with slight variations. Notably, the multi-time-point random forest model with morphological and color features achieved an AUC of 0.99 AUC, with all other evaluation metrics reaching 0.95. Only a small number of false positives and false negatives were, as illustrated in Fig. 2 (a). Similarly, the multi-time-point VGG16 model achieved a perfect 1.00 AUC score with slightly higher precision score (0.97) compared to ML, meaning that nearly all seeds predicted as low vigor were correctly classified. From a practical perspective, false negatives are particularly important because low-vigor seeds incorrectly classified as high vigor may adversely affect crop establishment. The best-performing ML and DL models achieved recall values of 0.95 and 0.93, respectively, indicating that only a small proportion of low-vigor seeds were missed. Further analysis of misclassified samples from confusion matrices in Figs. 2(a) and 3(a), particularly those belonging to the low-vigor class, is provided in Supplementary S4.

Single-time-point models also produced strong results, indicating that accurate vigor assessment is possible without monitoring the complete germination process. Notably, models trained on images acquired at 0 hour, before visible germination occurred, demonstrated competitive performance. This finding highlights the potential for early-stage seed screening, which is particularly attractive for practical applications where rapid decision-making is required. VGG16 consistently outperformed other DL architectures, while random forest models using color features achieved the best performance among the ML approaches. Performance generally declined at the 24 hour and 48 hour stages for ML models, likely due to the transitional nature of germination, whereas DL models, particularly VGG16 and ResNet18, remained relatively robust across all time points. This highlights the advantage of complex model architecture.

Among hand-crafted feature types, color-based features alone demonstrated relatively better performance compared to morphological features. SHAP results, as shown in Fig. 4 (a), also pinpoint that the most contributing features in the 0 hour model are predominantly color-based, spanning across multiple color channels. These features, including intensity, uniformity, and pigmentation, capture differences in seed coat color and reflectance properties, which can be associated with seed maturity, aging status, and biochemical deterioration. The strong contribution of these color features suggests that vigor-related physiological differences are already manifested in subtle visual characteristics of the seed before germination begins, allowing discrimination between high- and low-vigor seeds even in the absence of observable morphological changes. Combining both morphological and color features generally led to slight improvements in classification performance, as the learned information allows the model to capture a broader spectrum of seed characteristics. The ensemble structure of the random forest model enables it to exploit this diverse feature set efficiently.

SHAP values from random forest for (a) an early stage (0 hour) of the single-time-point model, and (b) the multi-time-point model.

Under the multi-time-point framework, the model captures informative features across several time points, suggesting that the temporal progression of these features enhances predictive power. Among all, color features alone achieved near-perfect performance, indicating that temporal information substantially enhanced their predictive value and reduced the contribution of morphological descriptors. SHAP results in Fig. 4 (b) show strong agreement regarding the most influential predictors. For example, the features l3_mean – an engineered measure derived from RGB values – at 0, 24 and 48 hour suggest the evolving color information throughout the germination process. Even with the importance of color features, it is worth noting that the solidity at 48 hour as part of morphological features appears among the top predictors. This indicates that as germination progresses, structural traits become more discriminative, likely due to the physical changes in low- versus high-vigor seeds. Additionally, the SHAP analysis reveals the direction of feature contributions. For several key color features, such as I1_skewness and I3_mean at 0 hour, higher feature values increase the probability of classification into the low-vigor class, suggesting that specific color and intensity patterns may reflect physiological deterioration associated with reduced vigor. In contrast, features such as l1_mean at 0 hour exhibit the opposite trend, where lower feature values contribute to a higher probability of low-vigor classification.

Among the CNN architectures, VGG16 outperformed other models for both the single-time-point and the multi-time-point approach, offering the best overall balance with stable precision, recall, and F1-score values. Its deep stack of convolutional layers with small filters may be particularly effective at capturing subtle texture, color, and structural variations associated with seed vigor. When using pretrained models, VGG16 often transfers better to tasks with small datasets using its well-distributed convolutional layers. ResNet18 and MobileV2, on the other hand, exhibited certain limitations. ResNet18 focuses on deep hierarchical learning through residual connections, but this may not be effective for capturing early germination patterns. It is also shallower and optimized for speed and gradient flow, which may lead to underperformance on tasks needing more detailed feature extraction. MobileNetV2 is optimized for lightweight and efficiency performance but sacrifices depth in feature extraction, which may limit its ability to identify differences in seed vigor.

For DL models, Gradient SHAP (Gradient Explainer) was applied to visualize the image regions contributing most strongly to model predictions. Fig. 5 (a) presents results from the VGG16 single-time-point model using the same seed across all observation times. The color scale of Fig. 5 at the bottom quantifies SHAP values, where values close to zero indicate low importance, while higher absolute values (positive or negative) represent greater significance. At 0 hour, the model’s attention is concentrated on dense clusters of SHAP highlighted regions along the outer contour and, particularly, the edge where the root begins to germinate. Such early-stage variations may be associated with moisture imbibition and initial physiological activation processes occurring within the seed hull, which have previously been reported to induce small but measurable changes in rice seed color and structure during germination [29,30]. These changes can be subtle and difficult to detect with the naked eye, whereas the Gradient Explainer can identify and emphasize these fine-grained patterns. At 24 hour, SHAP attributions become more spatially dispersed and relatively weaker, suggesting a transitional stage in which visible morphological differences between vigor classes remain limited. By 48 hour, the highlighted regions became more concentrated, particularly around the upper seed region, consistent with the emergence of clearer morphological differences between vigor classes. This observation is consistent with the biological expectation that later germination stages exhibit more apparent physical differences associated with seed vigor.

SHAP values from VGG16 for (a) the single time-point model, and (b) the multi-time-point model.

The multi-time-point VGG16 model shown in Fig. 5 (b) produced more concentrated and temporally consistent SHAP patterns across all observation periods. In particular, the SHAP distributions at 24 and 48 hours appeared denser and more focused, suggesting that integrating temporal information enables the model to capture more stable and globally relevant vigor-associated features. Overall, the consistency between the highlighted image regions and previously reported physiological changes during rice seed imbibition and germination increases confidence that the model captures biologically meaningful vigor-associated patterns. Nevertheless, the SHAP analysis should primarily be interpreted as a transparency and hypothesis-supporting tool that identifies influential image regions rather than providing direct mechanistic explanations of seed vigor biology.

To further assess the practical applicability of the proposed framework, model generalizability and inference latency were evaluated. Using datasets collected from different locations during model development, the models demonstrated strong generalization capability, achieving near-perfect performance on the internal test set. We further evaluated the models on an external test set consisting of rice species different from those included in the training set, as detailed in Supplementary S5. A substantial decline in performance was observed across most configurations, indicating limited generalizability under cross-species conditions. Both ML and DL models exhibited low precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC values despite maintaining moderate accuracy and specificity. These results suggest that the models tended to favor the majority class and had difficulty correctly identifying low-vigor seeds from unseen rice species. Among the single-time-point ML models, the 48 hour models generally outperformed the 0 hour and 24 hour models, indicating that later germination-stage information remains valuable for improving robustness under unseen-species conditions. DL models exhibited lower and less stable performance than ML models under these conditions. In addition, inference latency was measured by including both seed segmentation and classification. YOLO-based seed segmentation required approximately 15 milliseconds per time point, while inference time varied across model configurations. The low computational overhead demonstrates the potential of the proposed framework for rapid screening application.

Limitations

While this study demonstrates the potential and effectiveness of data-driven models for rice seed vigor classification using multi-time-point image analysis, several limitations should be addressed in future research. First, the dataset used in this study was limited in size, genetic diversity, and imaging conditions, which may not fully represent the natural variability encountered across different seed lots, varieties, and operational environments. Consequently, the strong performance observed under internal evaluation may not fully translate to unseen seed batches, imaging systems, illumination conditions, or environmental settings. Although an external validation experiment was conducted to provide an initial assessment of generalization, further evaluation on larger and more diverse datasets across multiple imaging platforms, acquisition environments, and seed batches remain necessary. Second, the multi-time-point aggregation strategy increases feature dimensionality, which may elevate the risk of overfitting, especially in data-limited scenarios. Expanding the dataset and exploring more efficient feature integration or regularization strategies would be important directions for future work. Furthermore, while the proposed framework achieved promising performance under controlled experimental conditions, practical deployment may be affected by variability in seed varieties, imaging conditions, growing environments, and hardware constraints commonly encountered in agricultural settings.

The incorporation of multi-modal data sources, such as hyperspectral or thermal imaging, may further improve vigor assessment by providing complementary information on internal seed properties beyond conventional RGB images. However, the benefits of these technologies must be balanced against the need for low-cost, high-throughput, and near-real-time operation, as specialized imaging systems and increased computational requirements may limit practical adoption. Regarding interpretability, while SHAP provides useful insights into which features or image regions influence model predictions, it does not establish causal relationships between these features and seed vigor. Further validation using domain-specific knowledge, such as physiological or biochemical markers, would strengthen the interpretability of the results. Integrating other explainable AI techniques, such as Grad-CAM and LIME, may provide complementary perspectives on model behavior and improve the robustness of interpretation.
