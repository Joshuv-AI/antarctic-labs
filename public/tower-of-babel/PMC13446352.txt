Background

Gallbladder carcinoma (GBC) is the most common malignancy of biliary tract, with high mortality and a dismal prognosis [1]. Complete surgical resection is currently the only curative therapeutic option for GBC [2]. Unfortunately, the early symptoms of GBC are usually nonspecific. Furthermore, the characteristic of GBC is easy local invasion, lymph node metastasis, vascular invasion, and distant metastasis [3]. Therefore, most patients with GBC are diagnosed with locally advanced or metastatic stage and <30% of known GBC patients are suitable for radical resection [4]. Even after curative resection, over 60% of patients develop postoperative recurrence within 5 years [5]. Currently, recommended first-line therapy for palliative chemotherapy of advanced GBC is gemcitabine and cisplatin [6]. However, the overall response rate of GBC patients with gemcitabine-based chemotherapy is <30% [7]. Moreover, conventional radiotherapy is unsatisfactory for GBC [8]. Therefore, the overall 5-year survival rate of GBC is only 5% [9]. Hence, novel therapeutic agents are crucially and urgently required for the treatment of GBC.

The ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) has been shown to play a vital role in regulating cell cycle, proliferation, apoptosis, oncogenesis, and angiogenesis [10,11]. The major functions of UPS include the degradation of intracellular misfolded, damaged, or unneeded proteins [11]. Inhibition of the UPS blocks the processing of misfolded proteins, leading to a cytotoxic accumulation of aberrant proteins that ultimately drives tumor cell death [12]. Mutations of tumor cells often cause abnormal production of proteins, and these defective proteins are also dependent on the UPS for degradation. Therefore, targeting the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway through proteasome inhibitors provides a novel therapeutic option for cancers [13]. In 2003, the first-generation proteasome inhibitor bortezomib was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of multiple myeloma. Nevertheless, the developments of drug resistance and adverse toxicities have limited its application [14]. Carfilzomib, a second-generation proteasome inhibitor, has shown higher effectiveness and well tolerance than the first-generation proteasome inhibitor in clinical trials [[15], [16], [17]]. However, bortezomib and carfilzomib need subcutaneous or intravenous administration, which reduces patient compliance. By contrast, oprozomib, an analog of carfilzomib, has the same epoxyketone pharmacophore and is the first orally available proteasome inhibitor [18]. It will improve the convenience for patients and the flexibility of dosing. Previous studies have reported that oprozomib has anti-tumor effects on multiple myeloma, head and neck cancer, glioma, liver cancer, and lung cancer [[18], [19], [20], [21], [22]]. Oprozomib could inhibit the proliferation of tumor cells in a dose-dependent manner and induce the tumor cells apoptosis [18,19,21]. Presently, the effects of oprozomib on GBC has not yet been determined.

In this study, we designed experiments in vitro and in vivo to explore the anti-tumor effect of oprozomib in GBC cells and the potential mechanisms. We found that oprozomib significantly inhibits the growth of GBC cells by inducing cell G2/M phase arrest and apoptosis. Oprozomib induced G2/M phase arrest and apoptosis of GBC cells by activating of ERK and JNK/p38 MAPK signaling pathway, respectively. Taken together, our study provided a novel preclinical rationale for oprozomib as a potential drug for treating GBC.

Materials and methods

Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Inc. (St. Louis, MO, USA). 2-(4-Amidinophenyl)-6-indolecarbamidine dihydrochloride (DAPI), Crystal Violet, Paraformaldehyde (PFA), terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) apoptosis kit, skim milk powder, RIPA lysis buffer, and BCA protein assay kit were all obtained from Beyotime (Nanjing, China). 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) imaging kit was purchased from Ribobio (Guangzhou, China). The cell cycle detection kit and the Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)/ propidium iodide (PI) apoptosis detection kit were purchased from KeyGen Biotech (Nanjing, China). Specific inhibitor of ERK (PD98059), JNK (SP600125), and p38 (SB203580) were purchased from MedChem Express (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). Triton X-100 was purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Methanol and Ethanol were obtained from Chengdu KeLong Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. (Chengdu, China). Trizol was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Primary antibodies against p21, p27, CDK1, Cdc25C, Bcl-2, Bax, Cytochrome c, Caspase-9, p38, and Ki-67 were purchased from Proteintech Group Inc. (Chicago, USA); Caspase-8, Cleaved Caspase-8, Cleaved Caspase-9, ERK, p-ERK, JNK, p-JNK, c-Jun, p-c-Jun, and p-p38 were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, USA); GAPDH was purchased from Bioss (Beijing, China).

Oprozomib inhibited GBC cells proliferation in vitro. a Chemical structure of oprozomib. b,c,d GBC cells were treated with gemcitabine or oprozomib (1 μm) for 48 h. e,f,g GBC cells were exposed to increased concentrations of oprozomib (0.1 nM, 1 nM, 10 nM, 100 nM, 1 μm, and10 μm) for 24, 48 and 72 h. Then, the cell viability assays were performed by CCK-8. h The IC50 of oprozomib after 24, 48 and 72 h treatment were determined for each cell line.

Cell lines and cell culture

The human GBC cell lines SGC-996, NOZ, and GBC-SD were used in the present study. SGC-996, NOZ, and GBC-SD were sourced from Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Science (Shanghai, China). SGC-996 and GBC-SD were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; HyClone, Utah, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA). NOZ was cultured in William’s Medium E (Genom, Hangzhou, China) containing 10% FBS (Gibco, USA). All the cells were cultured in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed by Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; AbMole BioScience, Houston, TX, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. GBC cells were seeded in 96-well plates (3 × 104 cells/well) and then treated with varied concentrations (0.1 nM, 1 nM, 10 nM, 100 nM, 1 μm, and10 μm) of oprozomib for 24, 48, and 72 h (h), respectively. The optical density (OD) values were measured by a microplate spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA) at a wavelength of 450 nm [23].

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed by cell colony formation assay and EdU assay. For cell colony formation assay, SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells were seeded in 6-well plates (5 × 102 cells/well) with or without treatment of oprozomib, and incubated for 48 h. Then, all cells were cultured for 2 weeks without oprozomib and the culture medium was changed every 3 days. The visible colonies were fixed and stained with 4% PFA and 0.1% crystal violet, respectively. The numbers of the colonies were visually counted.

For EdU assay [24], SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells were cultured in 24-well plates and then treated with various concentrations (50, 100, and 200 nM) of oprozomib or DMSO. After 48 h, the immunoassay was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocols, including cells were incubated with EdU, fixed, permeated, Apollo and DNA dyed. The cells were observed and captured under a fluorescence microscopy (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Cell cycle assay

Cell cycle assay was performed using a cell cycle detection kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions [25]. SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells were seeded in 6-well plates and then treated with oprozomib (50, 100, and 200 nM) or DMSO for 48 h. We then collected the cells and washed them with PBS (HyClone, Utah, USA). Then, the cells were fixed in ice-cold 70% ethanol at 4 °C overnight. The fixed cells were washed with PBS and then incubated with RNase A/PI at room temperature for 30 min, avoiding light. Finally, the cell cycle distribution was evaluated by flow cytometry using MODFIT software (Verity Software House).

Cell apoptosis assay

Cell apoptosis assay was performed using TUNEL apoptosis kit and the Annexin V- FITC/ PI apoptosis detection kit according to the manufacturer’s protocols [26]. For TUNEL assay, SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells were seeded in 24-well plates and then incubated with different concentrations of oprozomib (50, 100, and 200 nM). After 48 h, the TUNEL staining was performed, including cells fixed, permeated, TUNEL and DNA dyed. The cells were observed and captured under a fluorescence microscopy (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

For Annexin V-FITC/PI staining assay, SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells were cultured in 6-well plates and then treated with oprozomib (50, 100, and 200 nM) or DMSO for 48 h. We then collected the cells and washed with PBS. The cells were resuspended by binding buffer, stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI for 15 min in the dark at room temperature, and then detected by flow cytometry.

Western blot analysis

SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells were seeded in 6-well plates and then treated with DMSO or different concentrations of oprozomib. After 48 h, the cells were lysed by RIPA buffer. The protein concentration was quantified using the BCA protein assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After denaturation, an equal amount of proteins were separated by gel electrophoresis using SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA) and followed by soaking in skimmed milk for 2 h at room temperature. Then, the membranes were incubated with specific primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight and subsequently appropriate secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. The designated protein was visualized using an ECL detection reagent (4A Biotech, Beijing, China) and quantitated using the ImageLab software (BioRad, USA) [27].

Transcriptome analysis

RNA sequencing was accomplished in Wuhan SeqHealth Tech Co. Ltd. (Wuhan, China). After treatment with DMSO or oprozomib (200 nM) for 48 h, SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells were harvested and homogenized with Trizol Reagent to extract total RNA. Libraries were constructed and quantified by using Qubit 2.0 (Life Technologies, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA), then the libraries were sequenced on Illumina system for acquiring raw reads. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed by using the conditions of cutoff log2 (Fold Change) > 1 and p-value < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed to detect the biological function of DEGs and explore the significant pathways.

In vivo subcutaneous xenograft model

Animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of National Institutes of Health for the care and use of laboratory animals with the approval from the Animal Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Sichuan University (Chengdu, China, No. 20211166A). SGC-996 or GBC-SD cells (3 × 107 cells in 200 ul PBS) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank regions of the BALB/c nude mice (6–8 weeks old). After 7 days, the mice were randomly allocated into four groups (n = 5) and were treated as follows: (1) negative control group: mice received an intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection with DMSO; (2) gemcitabine group: mice received an i.p. injection of gemcitabine (10 mg/kg) every 3 days; (3) oprozomib group: mice received an i.p. injection of oprozomib (1mg/kg or 2mg/kg) every 3 days. The body weight and subcutaneous tumor volume of mice were measured every 3 days. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula V (mm3) = 0.5 × (length × width2). Thirty days later, mice were sacrificed and the tumors were harvested.

Tumor histology and immunohistochemical analysis

The isolated tumors and organ tissues (hearts, livers, kidneys, lungs, and spleens) were fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sliced in sections. Paraffin sections were dewaxed in dimethylbenzene and then rehydrated with a series of graded ethanol washes. For histologic examination, tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). For immunohistochemistry assay, tissue sections were incubated with citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a microwave oven to retrieve antigen. After cooling, tissue sections were washed with PBS, incubated with 3% H2O2 solution for 20 min at room temperature, and then sealed with goat serum. After that, tissue sections were treated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight and then incubated with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Afterward, diaminobenzidine (DAB) was used to visualize labeled proteins. Furthermore, tissue sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated and cleared. In the end, tissue sections were imaged under a microscope.

Statistical analysis

All the assays were performed in triplicate and data were presented as means ± SD. All data analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 7.0 (GraphPad, USA). The statistical differences between groups were evaluated using the one-way ANOVA or Student’s t-test. The p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Oprozomib decreased cell viability and inhibited proliferation of GBC cells

First, we investigated the inhibition effect of oprozomib on GBC cells, SGC-996, NOZ, and GBC-SD were treated with 1 μm gemcitabine or oprozomib for 48 h. The results showed that 1 μm oprozomib substantially inhibited GBC cells growth (Fig. 1b–d). Then, DMSO and different concentrations of oprozomib were used for SGC-996, NOZ, and GBC-SD treatment for 24, 48, and 72 h to further detect the inhibitory effect of oprozomib on GBC cells growth. The results from the CCK-8 assay demonstrated that a substantial decrease in the cell viability of SGC-996, NOZ, and GBC-SD in a time- and dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1e–g). The IC50 values of oprozomib in SGC-996, NOZ, and GBC-SD cells were 179.29 ± 9.51, 139.28 ± 13.69, and 147.73 ± 11.69 nM at 48 h, and 111.16 ± 8.60, 100.54 ± 9.04, and 97.27 ± 4.51 nM at 72 h (Fig. 1h), respectively. In further experiments, a treatment period of 48 h and the oprozomib concentrations of 50, 100, and 200 nM were selected to treat GBC cells based on the IC50 value. Based on the sensitivity of the three GBC cell lines treated with oprozomib for 48 h, the relatively insensitive SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells were chosen for further experiments. Colony formation assay and EdU assay were also conducted to investigate the inhibitory effect of oprozomib on the proliferation of SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells. The results of the colony formation assay showed that the number of colonies on SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells was significantly reduced in a dose-dependent approach (Fig. 2a). The EdU assay results revealed that oprozomib inhibited the proliferation of SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells significantly decreased in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2b). Taken together, these results demonstrated that oprozomib could exert a significant anti-tumor effect in the proliferation of GBC cells.

Oprozomib inhibited GBC cells proliferation in vitro. a Colony formation assay of SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells exposed to oprozomib (50, 100, and 200 nM) for 2 weeks. b EdU assay for SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells after 48 h incubation with oprozomib (50, 100, and 200 nM), and DMSO as control (×100, scale bar: 75 μm). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 relative to the control group.

Oprozomib triggered G2/M phase cell cycle arrest in GBC cells

Next, we investigated the potential mechanisms of cell cycle arrest in the proliferation of SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells by oprozomib, a flow cytometry assay and western blot analysis were conducted. The findings from the flow cytometry assay demonstrated that after oprozomib treatment (50, 100, and 200 nM) for 48 h, the proportion of the G2/M phase in SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells was significantly increased in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3a). The expression level of cell cycle-associated proteins was detected by western blotting. The results revealed that oprozomib treatment significantly upregulated the expression level of p21 and p27 in a dose-dependent manner, while the expression level of CDK1 and Cdc25C was suppressed (Fig. 3b). Collectively, these results indicated that oprozomib could inhibit the proliferation of GBC cells via G2/M phase cell cycle arrest.

Oprozomib triggered G2/M phase cell cycle arrest in GBC cells. a SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells were incubated with different concentrations of oprozomib (50, 100, and 200 nM) or DMSO and the cell cycle phase distribution were detected by flow cytometry. Cell cycle phase distribution indicated as the mean ± SD. b The expression levels of p21, p27, CDK1, and Cdc25C were determined using western blot analysis. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 relative to the control group.

Oprozomib induced apoptosis in GBC cells

To quantify the cellular damage induced in ​SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells by oprozomib, a TUNEL assay was performed. Compared to the control group, oprozomib increased the number of TUNEL-positive cells in ​SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells. The results showed that oprozomib significantly promoted the apoptosis of GBC cells in a dose-dependent approach (Fig. 4a). We next investigated the effect of oprozomib in SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells apoptosis by Annexin V-FITC/PI staining assay. GBC cells were treated with 50, 100, and 200 nM oprozomib. As shown in Fig. 4b, the early and late apoptotic cells in the lower right and upper right quadrants significantly increased after treatment with oprozomib. In contrast, the viable cells in the lower left quadrant were substantially reduced in a dose-dependent manner. Furthermore, the expression level of apoptosis-related proteins was measured by western blotting. The results demonstrated that oprozomib treatment significantly suppressed the expression level of Bcl-2 and Bcl-2/Bax in a dose-dependent approach, while the expression level of Cytochrome c, Cleaved Caspase-8, and Cleaved Caspase-9 were upregulated (Fig. 5a). There were no substantial changes in total Caspases-8 and Caspases-9. Therefore, these results revealed that oprozomib could induce apoptosis in GBC cells in a dose-dependent manner in vitro.

Anti-tumor effect of oprozomib via MAPK pathway

Then, the potential molecular mechanism of oprozomib induces G2/M phase arrest and apoptosis in GBC cells was further investigated. Subsequently, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). The RNA-seq data showed that 258 genes were upregulated and 235 genes were downregulated in SGC-996 cell after the treatment with oprozomib (Fig. 6a). In addition, 650 upregulated genes and 1080 downregulated genes were observed in GBC-SD cell (Fig. 6b). Thereafter, we performed a KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these DEGs in SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells, respectively. The results showed that the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway was altered in both SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells (Fig. 6c, d). Accordingly, the expression of the MAPK signaling pathway-associated proteins was detected by western blotting to verify the mechanism of oprozomib on the effect of MAPK signaling pathway activation. After treatment with oprozomib (50, 100, and 200 nM) for 48 h, the expression level of p-ERK, p-JNK, c-Jun, p-c-Jun, and p-p38 were significantly increased in a dose-dependent manner in SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells (Fig. 6e). There was no substantial change in total ERK, JNK, and p38. Overall, these data suggested that the anti-tumor effect of oprozomib in GBC cells was associated with the activation of the MAPK signaling pathway.

The anti-tumor effect of oprozomib in GBC cells was associated with the activation of the MAPK signaling pathway. a,b Volcano plot displaying the differentially expressed genes of SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells after oprozomib treatment. c,d KEGG analysis showed that the differentially expressed genes in SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells were enriched in the MAPK signaling Pathway. e The expression levels of ERK, p-ERK, JNK, p-JNK, c-Jun, p-c-Jun, p38, and p-p38 were detected by western blot analysis.

To further determine the specific role of the ERK, JNK, and p38 MAPK pathways in the oprozomib-mediated promotion of G2/M phase arrest and apoptosis in GBC cells, a rescue experiment was conducted. SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells were pretreated with PD98059 (ERK inhibitor), SP600125 (JNK inhibitor), and SB203580 (p38 inhibitor) for 1 h, followed by incubation with oprozomib (100 nM) for 48 h. The results showed that PD98059 (Fig. 7a, b and Supplementary Fig. S1a, b) could reverse the G2/M phase arrest and SP600125 (Fig. 8a,b and Supplementary Fig. S2a,b) and SB203580 (Fig. 8a,c and Supplementary Fig. S2a,c) could significantly restore the effects of oprozomib on cell apoptosis. Altogether, our results suggested that oprozomib induced G2/M phase arrest and apoptosis of GBC cells by activating of ERK and JNK/p38 MAPK signaling pathway, respectively.

Oprozomib inhibited tumor growth in vivo

Finally, to further confirm the anti-tumor effect of oprozomib in vivo, a xenograft mouse model was established by injection of SGC-996 or GBC-SD cells into the right flank regions of the nude mice. The xenograft-bearing mice were randomly allocated into four groups and then treated with oprozomib (1mg/kg or 2mg/kg), gemcitabine (10 mg/kg), and DMSO (negative control) intraperitoneally every 3 days for 30 days. The tumor volume and weight were significantly reduced in a dose-dependent manner after oprozomib treatment (Fig. 9a). The results of western blotting demonstrated that the oprozomib treatment significantly increased the expression level of p21, Cytochrome c, and Cleaved Caspase-9 in dissected tumor tissues (Fig. 9b), whereas reduced CDK1, Cdc25C, and Bcl-2 levels. The results were consistent with the in vitro results. In addition, the immunohistochemistry stain showed oprozomib reduced the expression level of Ki-67 and Bcl-2, while elevated the expression level of p21 and p27 (Supplementary Fig. S3b). We further explored the potential toxicity effects of oprozomib on normal tissues. There was no significant difference in the body weight between the oprozomib group and the negative control group (Supplementary Fig. S3a). Moreover, H&E staining of the organs revealed no major organ-related toxicity results (Fig. 9c). Taken together, these results indicated that oprozomib could inhibit GBC growth and exhibit no significant toxicity in vivo.

Tumor weights after the completion of treatment were significant. b The expression levels of p21, CDK1, Cdc25C, Cytochrome c, and Cleaved Caspase-9 were detected by western blot analysis. c H&E staining of the important organs (×10, scale bar: 200 μm). No apparently organ-related toxicity was observed. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 relative to the control group.

Discussion

Recently, the proteasome inhibitor oprozomib has been reported to have inhibitory effects on a variety of tumors. However, the effects of oprozomib on GBC has not yet been investigated. In the present study, we identified that oprozomib significantly inhibited the growth of human GBC cells in a dose-dependent manner in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, oprozomib induced G2/M phase arrest and apoptosis of GBC cells by activating ERK and JNK/p38 MAPK signaling pathways, respectively. There was no major organ-related toxicity in oprozomib-treated mice. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the preclinical use of oprozomib in GBC.

Consistent with the studies reported by other authors [[20], [21], [22],28], we also observed that oprozomib treatment of GBC cells resulted in decreased cell viability and inhibition of cell proliferation in a dose- and time-dependent manner. In addition, previous study has found that proteasome inhibitor arrested cell cycle progression in G2/M phase in ovarian cancer cells [29] and acute promyelocytic leukemia [30]. The cell cycle distribution was studied to determine the underlying mechanism of the anti-proliferation effects of oprozomib in GBC. In our study, oprozomib triggered G2/M phase cell cycle arrest of SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells. Cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1) plays an essential role in the cell cycle regulation of the G2 to M phase transition during mitosis [31]. However, the activity of CDK1 is regulated by Cdc25C phosphatase [32]. Moreover, the CDK inhibitors p21 and p27 also play a vital role in suppressing CDK1 activity in the G2/M phase [33,34]. Inhibition of CDK1 and Cdc25C and the accumulation of p21 and p27 cause cell cycle arrest in the G2/M phase [35,36]. Interestingly, several studies have shown that the expression of p21 can be regulated in both P53-dependent and -independent manners [[37], [38], [39], [40]]. This dual-regulatory framework suggests that the impact of oprozomib on the regulation of p21 expression may involve a highly complex molecular mechanism. In the present study, we found that oprozomib treatment significantly upregulated the expression level of p21 and p27 in a dose-dependent manner, while the expression level of CDK1 and Cdc25C was suppressed.

As we all know, promoting the apoptosis of tumor cells is an effective strategy for the treatment of cancer [41,42]. Previous studies have reported that oprozomib could induce apoptosis of various tumor cells such as multiple myeloma [18], head and neck cancer [19], glioma [20], lung cancer [22], and bladder cancer [43]. In addition, it has been found that proteasome inhibitor activates JNK/p38 MAPK signaling to promote apoptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma cells [44]. A TUNEL assay and flow cytometric assay were conducted to confirm whether oprozomib inhibits SGC-996 and GBC-SD by inducing apoptosis. We found that oprozomib induced apoptosis in GBC cells in a dose-dependent manner. Apoptosis is executed by intracellular proteases called cysteinyl aspartate specific proteinase (caspase) [45]. Cytochrome c is located inside the mitochondria. However, when cells receive the apoptotic stimulus, Cytochrome c is released into the cytosol and triggers programmed cell death through apoptosis [46,47]. Bcl-2, an anti-apoptotic family member, inhibits cell death by preventing the release of Cytochrome c from the mitochondria [48]. In the present study, we found that oprozomib significantly increases the expression of Cytochrome c, Cleaved Caspase-8, and Cleaved Caspase-9 and decreases the expression of Bcl-2 in a dose-dependent approach.

The MAPK signaling pathway includes extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), c-Jun NH(2)-terminal kinase (JNK), and p38, which plays a key role in the regulation of cells survival, proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [49]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the activation of the ERK signaling pathway could cause p-ERK accumulation, which leads to the G2/M phase arrest of tumor cells such as liver cancer cell [50], renal cancer cell [51], and cholangiocarcinoma cell [52]. This is consistent with our results and suggests that pERK, in addition to its pro-tumorigenic effect, may also have an anti-cancer effect under certain conditions. Similarly, it has been reported that the activation of JNK and p38 signaling pathways could cause p-JNK and p-p38 accumulation, and induce apoptosis in gastric cancer cell [53], liver cancer cell [54], and pancreatic cancer cell [55]. In addition, previous research has found that Oprozomib induce apoptosis by enhancing doxorubicin-induced JNK/p38 MAPK phosphorylation in triple-negative breast cancer cells [56]. In this study, RNA-seq assay showed that oprozomib treatment of SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells activated MAPK signaling pathway. Further western blot results revealed that oprozomib upregulated the expression level of p-ERK, p-JNK and p-p38 in a dose-dependent manner. The rescue experiments demonstrated that the ERK inhibitor markedly reversed the G2/M phase arrest and the JNK and p38 inhibitor significantly inhibited apoptosis in vitro. Taken together, these data suggested the ERK signaling pathway and JNK/p38 signaling pathway mediated the G2/M phase arrest and apoptosis, respectively.

Previous studies have reported that oprozomib could inhibit the growth of xenograft tumors such as multiple myeloma [18] and head and neck cancer [19]. In the present study, the xenograft tumor model was established to clarify the anti-tumor effect of oprozomib in vivo. We found that oprozomib inhibits the GBC tumor growth in vivo in a dose-dependent manner. Western blot analysis showed oprozomib treatment significantly increases the expression level of p21, Cytochrome c, and Cleaved Caspase-9 in dissected tumor tissues, whereas reduces CDK1, Cdc25C, and Bcl-2 levels. These results were consistent with the in vitro results. Furthermore, there was no significant weight loss observed in the four groups and H&E staining of the organs revealed no major organ-related toxicity, suggesting safety and no apparent toxicity in vivo. Overall, oprozomib could inhibit GBC growth and without inducing significant toxicity in vivo.

Conclusions

In summary, our results demonstrated that oprozomib could exert anti-tumor effects in vitro and in vivo, including inhibition of proliferation of GBC cells, as well as induction of G2/M phase arrest and apoptosis. Mechanistically, the ERK signaling pathway and JNK/p38 signaling pathway mediated the G2/M phase arrest and apoptosis, respectively. Therefore, we believe our study provides preclinical evidence for understanding the mechanism and the development of oprozomib-based regimen as a novel potential chemotherapeutic agent against GBC in the future. In addition, we hope that further validation with organoids and PDX models in the future to provide more preclinical evidence for the anti-tumor effects of oprozomib in GBC.
