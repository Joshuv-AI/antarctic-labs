A Discrete Radon Transform Based on the Area of Cube-Plane Intersection
Robert Beinert, Jonas Bresch, Michael Quellmalz

Abstract
The Radon transform is a fundamental tool for analyzing data in tomographic imaging, optimal transport, crystallography, and geometric analysis. Numerical computations require an accurate discretization. To deal with voxelized images and objects, we derive a closed-form, piecewise polynomial expression for the Radon transform of an axis-aligned cube in arbitrary dimension 𝒅 \bm{d} . Building on this formula, we propose a discrete Radon transform in ℝ 𝒅 \bm{\mathbb{R}^{d}} that is both analytically exact for voxelized data and computationally efficient. For improved numerical stability, we introduce a regularized variant replacing the Radon transform of a cube, i.e. the ( 𝒅 − 𝟏 ) \bm{(d-1)} -dimensional area of the intersection between that cube and a hyperplane, by the 𝒅 \bm{d} -dimensional volume of the intersection between the cube and a thin slab around the hyperplane. Numerical experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach in several applications including 3D shape matching, classification, and sliced Wasserstein barycenters. The computational efficiency in higher dimensions is verified by a comparison with Monte Carlo integration.

Introduction

In imaging, inverse problems, and geometric analysis
the Radon transform holds a pivotal role
by linking functions to their integrals over hyperplanes.
Thereby,
it encodes geometric information
about a function
that can be probed analytically
and reconstructed algorithmically.
Understanding its behavior
for simple geometric bodies
is thus both practically and theoretically important.
In particular,
the problem of computing the

(d-1)
-dimensional volume
of the intersection between a hyperplane
and an axis-aligned hypercube

[
68
]

and Pólya
[

]
.
The latter article “Berechnung eines bestimmten Integrals” (“Computation of a certain integral”)
seems not to be well-known
and apparently unknown to
[

]
.
More recently, sharp and asymptotic bounds
and extremal results were obtained
in
[

,

,

.

From the geometric-analysis side,

While prior works give sharp bounds

have been missing in a single, unified form, cf. Remark

.

From the computational imaging side,

However, many existing discrete schemes either reduce the 3D problem

II
Multi-Dimensional Radon transform

II-A

Basic definitions

For an absolutely integrable function
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

and a fixed direction
\bm{\theta}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}\coloneqq\{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|{\bm{x}}\|=1\}
,
the
Radon transform
is defined as

R_{\bm{\theta}}[f](t)\coloneqq\int_{H_{\bm{\theta}}(t)}f({\bm{x}})\,\mathrm{d}\sigma,\qquad t\in\mathbb{R},

(1)

where
\sigma
denotes the surface measure on the
hyperplane

H_{\bm{\theta}}(t)\coloneqq\{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\langle{\bm{x}},\bm{\theta}\rangle=t\}.

(2)

The Radon transform is well-defined for almost all
t\in\mathbb{R}

and obeys the symmetry

R_{-\bm{\theta}}[f](-t)=R_{\bm{\theta}}[f](t)
.

Furthermore,
the
Fourier transform
is defined by

\mathcal{F}_{d}[f]({\bm{v}})\coloneqq(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f({\bm{x}})\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{v}}\rangle}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}},\qquad{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(3)

The Fourier and Radon transform are related
via the Fourier slice theorem
[
54
]
,
which
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
\bm{\theta}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}

states

\mathcal{F}_{1}[R_{\bm{\theta}}[f]](s)=(2\pi)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\mathcal{F}_{d}[f](s\bm{\theta}).

(4)

For
a>0
,
we denote the
indicator function
of the interval
(-a,a]
by

\mathbf{1}_{a}(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }x\in(-a,a],\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\\
\end{cases}

(5)

and
the indicator function of the
hypercube

(-{\bm{a}},{\bm{a}}]\coloneqq\bigtimes_{i=1}^{d}(-a_{i},a_{i}],\qquad\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0},

(6)

by

\mathbf{1}_{\bm{a}}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\mathbf{1}_{a_{1}}(x_{1})\cdots\mathbf{1}_{a_{d}}(x_{d}),\qquad\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(7)

Furthermore, we define the
positive part
of
x\in\mathbb{R}
by

(x)_{+}\coloneqq\begin{cases}x,&\text{if }x>0,\\
0,&\text{if }x\leq 0.\\
\end{cases}

(8)

We frequently use its
\ell
-th power
(x)_{+}^{\ell}
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
where we set
0^{0}\coloneqq 0
.
Moreover,
we define the vector

{\bm{x}}^{\circ}\coloneqq{\bm{x}}|_{\supp({\bm{x}})}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell},\qquad{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

whose
j
-th entry is the
j
-th non-zero entry of
{\bm{x}}

and where

\ell=\|{\bm{x}}\|_{0}\coloneqq|\supp({\bm{x}})|

denotes the number of non-zero entries of
\bm{x}
.
The product of all entries of
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is denoted by

P({\bm{x}})\coloneqq\prod_{j=1}^{d}x_{j},

(9)

and the componentwise product of two vectors
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

by
{\bm{x}}\varodot{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The Fourier transform of
\mathbf{1}_{{\bm{a}}}

with
{\bm{a}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}

can be expressed
as

\mathcal{F}_{d}[\mathbf{1}_{{\bm{a}}}]({\bm{v}})=\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}\sinc(a_{j}v_{j}),

(10)

see
[
56
, Ex. 2.3]
,
with the
cardinal sine
function

\sinc(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{\sin(x)}{x},&x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\},\\
1,&x=0.\end{cases}

II-B

The Radon transform of a hypercube

The next theorem
provides a closed form for the Radon transform
of the indicator function
\mathbf{1}_{{\bm{a}}}
of the hypercube
(-{\bm{a}},{\bm{a}}]
.

(-{\bm{a}},{\bm{a}}]\cap H_{\bm{\theta}}(t)

Theorem 1
.

Let
\bm{\theta}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
and
{\bm{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
.
Furthermore,
let

.
Then,
for the Radon transformation
of the hypercube,

A_{\bm{\theta}}^{{\bm{a}}}(t)\coloneqq\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\theta}}[\mathbf{1}_{{\bm{a}}}](t)

holds

\displaystyle\hskip-10.0ptA_{\bm{\theta}}^{{\bm{a}}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{d-\ell}P({\bm{a}})}{P(({\bm{a}}\varodot\bm{\theta})^{\circ})\,(\ell-1)!}

(11)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\sum_{{\bm{k}}\in\{-1,1\}^{\ell}}P({\bm{k}})\,\big(t+\langle{\bm{k}},({\bm{a}}\varodot\bm{\theta})^{\circ}\rangle\big)_{+}^{\ell-1}

(12)

The proof
requires the following lemma on convolutions.
Related results about splines
can be found in
[
36
]
,
[
19
, § 1]
.
The
convolution
of two functions

is defined as

(g*h)(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(y)h(y-x)\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(13)

Iteratively,
we denote the
k
-fold convolution
of
k
functions
g_{j}\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R})

by

\bigast_{j=1}^{k}g_{j}\coloneqq g_{1}\ast...\ast g_{k}.

(14)

The Fourier convolution theorem
[
56
, Thm. 2.15]

relates the Fourier transform
and the convolution
of two function
by

\mathcal{F}_{1}[g*h]=\mathcal{F}_{1}[g]\cdot\mathcal{F}_{1}[h],

(15)

which directly extends to the
k
-fold convolution from (
14
).

Lemma 2
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
{\bm{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{>0}
.
Then the
k
-fold convolution of indicator functions is

\bigast_{j=1}^{k}\mathbf{1}_{b_{j}}(t)=\tfrac{(2\pi)^{\nicefrac{{(1-k)}}{{2}}}}{(k-1)!}\sum_{{\bm{k}}\in\{-1,1\}^{k}}P({\bm{k}})(t+\langle{\bm{k}},{\bm{b}}\rangle)_{+}^{k-1},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(16)

Proof.

For
k=1
,
we have

\mathbf{1}_{b_{1}}(t)=(t+b_{1})_{+}^{0}-(t-b_{1})_{+}^{0},\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(17)

Now consider
{\bm{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{\geq 0}

and define for
{\bm{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

the by the last component reduced vector
{\bm{c}}^{\prime}\coloneqq(c_{1},\dots,c_{k-1})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
.
Then, we have for any

that

\displaystyle\Bigl(\mathbf{1}_{b_{k}}*\tfrac{(2\pi)^{\nicefrac{{(2-k)}}{{2}}}}{(k-2)!}\hskip-5.0pt\sum_{{\bm{k}}^{\prime}\in\{-1,1\}^{k-1}}\hskip-5.0ptP({\bm{k}}^{\prime})(t+\langle{\bm{k}}^{\prime},{\bm{b}}^{\prime}\rangle)_{+}^{k-2}\Bigr)(t)

(18)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{(2\pi)^{\nicefrac{{(1-k)}}{{2}}}}{(k-2)!}\sum_{{\bm{k}}^{\prime}\in\{-1,1\}^{k-1}}\int_{t-b_{k}}^{t+b_{k}}P({\bm{k}}^{\prime})(s+\langle{\bm{k}}^{\prime},{\bm{b}}^{\prime}\rangle)_{+}^{k-2}\,\mathrm{d}s

(19)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{(2\pi)^{\nicefrac{{(1-k)}}{{2}}}}{(k-1)!}\Bigg[\sum_{{\bm{k}}^{\prime}\in\{-1,1\}^{k-1}}P({\bm{k}}^{\prime})(t+b_{k}+\langle{\bm{k}}^{\prime},{\bm{b}}^{\prime}\rangle)_{+}^{k-1}

(20)

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt-P({\bm{k}}^{\prime})(t-b_{k}+\langle{\bm{k}}^{\prime},{\bm{b}}^{\prime}\rangle)_{+}^{k-1}\Bigg]

(21)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{(2\pi)^{\nicefrac{{(1-k)}}{{2}}}}{(k-1)!}\sum_{{\bm{k}}\in\{-1,1\}^{k}}P({\bm{k}})(t+\langle{\bm{k}},{\bm{b}}\rangle)_{+}^{k-1}.\qed

(22)

Proof of Theorem

By (

\begin{split}&\hskip-5.0ptV_{\bm{\theta}}^{{\bm{a}}}(t_{1},t_{2})=\frac{2^{d-\ell}P({\bm{a}})}{P(({\bm{a}}\varodot\bm{\theta})^{\circ})\,\ell!}\sum_{{\bm{k}}\in\{-1,1\}^{\ell}}P({\bm{k}})\\
&\times\Big(\,\big(t_{2}+\langle{\bm{k}},({\bm{a}}\varodot\bm{\theta})^{\circ}\rangle\big)_{+}^{\ell}-\big(t_{1}+\langle{\bm{k}},({\bm{a}}\varodot\bm{\theta})^{\circ}\rangle\big)_{+}^{\ell}\Big).\end{split}

(32)

Proof.

The slab
S_{\bm{\theta}}(t_{1},t_{2})
from (
31
)
is the union of hyperplanes (
2
),
namely

S_{\bm{\theta}}(t_{1},t_{2})=\bigcup_{s\in[t_{1},t_{2}]}H_{\bm{\theta}}(s).

Since
\|\bm{\theta}\|=1
,
we have

\displaystyle V_{\bm{\theta}}^{{\bm{a}}}(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{(-{\bm{a}},{\bm{a}}]\cap S_{\bm{\theta}}(t_{1},t_{2})}1\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\!\!\!\!A_{\bm{\theta}}^{{\bm{a}}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Integrating (
11
) for
t
,
we obtain (
32
).
∎

The volume converges to the area
A_{\bm{\theta}}^{{\bm{a}}}
if the width of the slab approaches zero, in particular it holds

\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\;\tfrac{1}{2\varepsilon}V_{\bm{\theta}}^{{\bm{a}}}(t-\varepsilon,t+\varepsilon)=A_{\bm{\theta}}^{{\bm{a}}}(t),\qquad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}.

(33)

Remark 7

(Related results)
.

Explicit volume formulas have been stated
in different forms in
[
59
,
8
,
57
]
.
However,
this literature considered the special cases of (
32
),
where either
\bm{\theta}={\bm{e}}/\sqrt{d}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}

and
{\bm{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
is arbitrary
[
59
]
,
or
\bm{\theta}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
is arbitrary
and
{\bm{a}}={\bm{e}}
is fixed
[
8
,
41
,
57
]
,
where
{\bm{e}}\coloneqq(1,\dots,1)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
These two cases are related via a coordinate transform:
Let
{\bm{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}

and note that (
32
) remains valid for
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
we perform the coordinate transform
{\bm{x}}={\bm{a}}\varodot{\bm{y}}

with
\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}=P({\bm{a}})\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{a}}
.
Then
{\bm{x}}\in(-{\bm{a}},{\bm{a}}]

if and only if
{\bm{y}}\in(-{\bm{e}},{\bm{e}}]

and

{\bm{x}}\in H_{{\bm{e}}}(t)\Leftrightarrow\langle{\bm{e}},{\bm{x}}\rangle=t\Leftrightarrow\langle{\bm{a}},{\bm{y}}\rangle=t\Leftrightarrow{\bm{y}}\in H_{{\bm{a}}}(t),

for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Hence,
we have

\displaystyle V_{{\bm{e}}}^{{\bm{a}}}(t_{1},t_{2})=\int_{(-{\bm{a}},{\bm{a}}]\cap S_{{\bm{e}}}(t_{1},t_{2})}1\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\int_{(-{\bm{e}},{\bm{e}}]\cap S_{{\bm{a}}}(t_{1},t_{2})}1\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{y}}

(34)

\displaystyle=P({\bm{a}})\cdot V_{{\bm{a}}}^{{\bm{e}}}(t_{1},t_{2}).

(35)

and therewith, by (
33
), it follows

A_{{\bm{e}}}^{{\bm{a}}}(t)=P({\bm{a}})A_{{\bm{a}}}^{{\bm{e}}}(t),\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

III-B

Discretization of the Radon transform

For a numerical realization of the Radon transform
on the voxelized objects,
we need a discrete model.
For
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
we define the symmetric index set

\mathcal{I}_{N}\coloneqq\left\{\nicefrac{{-N+1}}{{2}},\nicefrac{{-N+3}}{{2}},\dots,\nicefrac{{N-1}}{{2}}\right\}

and the regular grid
\mathcal{I}_{N}^{d}
as
d
-fold Cartesian product.
We consider a
d
-dimensional image
F\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{I}_{N}^{d}}
with
N
voxels in each dimension.
Each voxel
{\bm{n}}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})\in\mathcal{I}_{N}^{d}
is imagined
as cube with side length (i.e. voxel size)
s>0

centered at
s\cdot{\bm{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.,
the cube
(s({\bm{n}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}),s({\bm{n}}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})]
.
Then
F
corresponds to the piecewice constant function

f_{F}({\bm{x}})\coloneqq\sum_{{\bm{n}}\in\mathcal{I}_{N}^{d}}F({\bm{n}})\,\mathbf{1}_{(s({\bm{n}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}),s({\bm{n}}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})]}({\bm{x}}),\qquad{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(36)

The cubes touch but do not overlap.

Let
\bm{\theta}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
By linearity,
we have

\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\theta}}[f_{F}](t)=\sum_{{\bm{n}}\in\mathcal{I}_{N}^{d}}F({\bm{n}})\,\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\theta}}\big[\mathbf{1}_{(s({\bm{n}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}),s({\bm{n}}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})]}\big](t).

(37)

The shift identity for the Radon transform,

\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\theta}}[f(\cdot-{\bm{y}})](t)=\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\theta}}[f](t-\langle\bm{\theta},{\bm{y}}\rangle),\qquad{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

follows directly from the definition (
1
).
Together with the observation that

\mathbf{1}_{(s({\bm{n}}-\frac{1}{2}),s({\bm{n}}+\frac{1}{2})]}({\bm{x}})=\mathbf{1}_{\nicefrac{{s}}{{2}}{\bm{e}}}({\bm{x}}-s\cdot{\bm{n}}),

we obtain that

\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\theta}}[f_{F}](t)=\sum_{{\bm{n}}\in\mathcal{I}_{N}^{d}}F({\bm{n}})\,\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\theta}}\big[\mathbf{1}_{\nicefrac{{s}}{{2}}{\bm{e}}}\big](t-s\langle{\bm{n}},\bm{\theta}\rangle).

(38)

Then we have

\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\theta}}[f_{F}](t)=\sum_{{\bm{n}}\in\mathcal{I}_{N}^{d}}F({\bm{n}})\,A_{\bm{\theta}}^{\nicefrac{{s}}{{2}}{\bm{e}}}(t-s\langle{\bm{n}},\bm{\theta}\rangle)

(39)

with
A_{\bm{\theta}}^{{\bm{a}}}
given in (
11
).
Since the box
\nicefrac{{s}}{{2}}(-{\bm{e}},{\bm{e}}]
has a diagonal of
\nicefrac{{s\sqrt{d}}}{{2}}
,
the summands in (
39
) vanish
if
|t-s\langle n,\bm{\theta}\rangle|>\nicefrac{{s\sqrt{d}}}{{2}}
.
For improved stability, we replace
A_{\bm{\theta}}^{{\bm{a}}}(t)
by its regularized variant
(2\varepsilon)^{-1}V_{\bm{\theta}}^{{\bm{a}}}(t-\varepsilon,t+\varepsilon)
, see (
32
).

Remark 8

(Existing approaches)
.

Besides our approach, there are other ways to discretize the Radon transform.
Most work covers only the case
d=2
.
A simple, well-known approach
[
14
]
projects the center of a voxel to the line
\bm{\theta}^{\perp}
, and then uses binning in
t
.
For general
d
, we set
b
as bin size in
t
,
and approximate

\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\theta}}[f_{F}](t)\approx\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{n}}\in\mathcal{I}_{N}^{d}\\
|\langle{\bm{n}},\bm{\theta}\rangle-t|<b\end{subarray}}F({\bm{n}}).

This approach is usually implemented
by first computing the projections
\langle{\bm{n}},\bm{\theta}\rangle

for all voxels
{\bm{n}}
and then binning them to a grid in
t
.
Therefore, this approach is generally faster than ours, but less accurate.
It can be improved by splitting each voxel into subvoxels.
The convergence of different approaches was analyzed in
[
33
,
16
]
.

Another approach, known as slant stacking
[
69
]

in 2D discretizes the line integral by a quadrature on the line
\bm{\theta}^{\perp}
,
where the function values of
f
are obtained via interpolation of
F
;
taking
f_{F}
here corresponds to a the nearest neighbor interpolation.
Discrete convolutions in combination with data interpolation was utilized for the 2D Radon transform in
[
2
]
.
Monte Carlo approximations were discussed in
[
1
,
20
,
24
]
.

Unfortunately,
there are fewer readily available implementations of the 3D Radon transform.
Although some packages like
RadonKA
2
2

2

Julia package
RadonKA

https://github.com/roflmaostc/RadonKA.jl

[
70
]
provide “3D Radon transforms”,
these are vectorized versions of the 2D Radon transform
(i.e. integrating along all lines perpendicular to the third coordinate)
rather than
integrating along planes.
In
[
69
]
,
closed forms for few simple objects,
e.g. Gaußian bells
and ellipsoids in 3D
are provided.

III-D

Classification via NR-CDT
[
10
]

The Radon transform can be utilized
for the classification of images
using the so-called R-CDT approach
[
39
]
.
Its extension, the NR-CDT
[
9
,
11
]
,
is invariant to affine transformations,
and was applied to
pattern recognition
[
10
]
.
Differently,
we apply the theory on voxels
and not on meshes (i.e. point clouds),
which makes the computation of the 3D Radon transform crucial.
We first revise the construction of the NR-CDT
for functions,
and then provide the numerical results
on the ModelNet10 dataset
for comparison with the feature extractors from Section
III-C
.

III-D
1
Normalized Radon cumulative distribution transform

Let
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be nonnegative
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f({\bm{x}})\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}=1
.
Following
[
10
]
,
we define the
Radon cumulative distribution transform
(Radon-CDT)
of
f
by

\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\theta}}[f]}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\xi\mapsto\inf\Bigl\{s\in\mathbb{R}\Bigm|\int_{-\infty}^{s}\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\theta}}[f](t)\,\mathrm{d}t>\xi\Bigr\}.

(41)

This is the quantile function, i.e., the generalized inverse
of the cumulative distribution function
of
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\theta}}[f]
, see
[
9
,
10
]
.
We define the
mean
and
standard derivation
of a function
g\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}([0,1])
by

\mathrm{mean}(g)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}g(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,\;\;\mathrm{std}(g)^{2}\coloneq\int_{0}^{1}(g(s)-\mathrm{mean}(g))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Following
[
10
]
,
the
normalized Radon-CDT
(NR-CDT)

\mathcal{N}_{\bm{\theta}}[f]\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\;t\mapsto\frac{\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\theta}}[f]}(t)-\mathrm{mean}(\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\theta}}[f]})}{\mathrm{std}(\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\theta}}[f]})},

(42)

is well defined
if
\supp(f)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is compact
and
\dim(\supp(f))>d-1
, cf.
[
10
, Prop. 4, Lem. 1]
.

Finally, we define the
max-normalized
R-CDT (
m
NR-CDT)

\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}[f]\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\xi\mapsto\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}}\mathcal{N}_{\bm{\theta}}[f](\xi).

(43)

The
m
NR-CDT is invariant
under affine transformations, see
[
9
, Thm. 1]
.
More specifically,
consider for a function
f

its affine transformation

f_{{\bm{A}},{\bm{y}}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\;{\bm{x}}\mapsto f({\bm{A}}^{-1}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}})),

(44)

for
{\bm{A}}\in\mathrm{GL}(d)
and
{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
then it holds

\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}[f_{\bm{A},{\bm{y}}}]=\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}[f].

(45)

III-D
2
Numerical results

We apply the
m
NR-CDT
on both datasets,
i.e. the reduced (bathtubs, chairs, and dressers)
and entire ModelNet10 dataset,
where each class consists of ten samples.
The discretization
uses three parameters:
i) the number of evaluation points for the CDT;
ii) the equispaced radii
as in Section
III-C3
;
iii) the angles
\bm{\theta}
,
which are not restricted to grids
unlike in Section
III-C
.
There are many notions of “almost equispaced” points
\bm{\theta}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{2}

such as spherical designs
[
71
,
28
]

or quasi-Monte Carlo designs
[
64
,
31
]
.
We chose
the Fibonacci points
[
29
,
26
]

given in spherical coordinates (

IV
Approximation and Means of Empirical Data

In contrast to Section
III
,
which relies on a voxel-based data structure,
here we are interested in a free-support setting for measures.
Firstly, we consider clustering in 2D and 3D,
more precisely, we aim to fit a given empirical measure
by a mixture of measures on cubes
via the sliced Wasserstein distance,
see Section
IV-A
.
Secondly,
we compute sliced Wasserstein barycenters,
see Section
IV-B
.

IV-A

Optimal histogram via sliced Wasserstein distance

),

The uniform mixture

(red cubes)
approximates the optimal histogram for the
empirical measure
\Delta_{n}
(blue dots).

Approximating an empirical measure

\Delta_{n}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{1}}{{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{{\bm{x}}_{i}}
,
where
\delta_{{\bm{x}}}
denotes the Dirac measure
at a point
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
by another distribution
is a important task
[
13
,
38
]
.
We are interested in linear combinations

\Upsilon_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\gamma_{j}u_{j},

(47)

where
u_{j}
is the uniform measure on a cube

({\bm{c}}_{j}-{\bm{w}}_{j},{\bm{c}}_{j}+{\bm{w}}_{j}]

with center
{\bm{c}}_{j}\in\mathbb{X}

and width
{\bm{w}}_{j}\in\mathbb{X}
,
and
\gamma_{j}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{u_{j}(\mathbb{X})}}{{\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}u_{\ell}(\mathbb{X})}}
.
Note that the ansatz of minimizing the Kullback–Leibler divergence or any other Csiszár divergence to the given measure
is not defined in this case
[
21
]
.

An alternative approach is to consider
the sliced Wasserstein distance.
To this end, the
Wasserstein distance

[
65
]

of measures
\chi,\eta\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
is defined by

\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(\chi,\eta)\coloneqq\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\chi,\eta)}\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}|x-y|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y),

(48)

where
\Pi
is the set of measures
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R})
with marginals
\chi
and
\eta
.
We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(\chi,\eta)=\int_{0}^{1}|F_{\chi}^{[-1]}(s)-F_{\eta}^{[-1]}(s)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\rho(s)

(49)

for some reference measure
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})

with
\chi\ll\rho
,
with the cumulative distribution function
F_{\chi}(s)\coloneqq\chi((-\infty,s])

and its generalized inverse
g^{[-1]}(t)\coloneqq\inf\{s\mid g(s)>t\}
.
The
sliced Wasserstein distance

of
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

is defined by

\mathrm{SW}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\theta}}[\mu],\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\theta}}[\nu])\,\mathrm{d}u_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}(\bm{\theta}),

(50)

where
u_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
is the uniform measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}

and the Radon transform of measures,
corresponding to (
1
) for the density,
is defined by

\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\theta}}[\mu]\coloneqq\langle\cdot,\bm{\theta}\rangle_{\#}\mu=\mu\circ(\langle\cdot,\bm{\theta}\rangle)^{-1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}),\;\;\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}.

(51)

Compared to the Wasserstein distance, its sliced version combines similar properties with faster computation, cf.
[
51
,
55
,
60
,
61
]
.

Then, we propose to approximate the empirical measure
\Delta_{n}
by solving

\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{c}},{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{X}^{k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{SW}_{2}^{2}(\Upsilon_{k},\Delta_{n}),

(52)

where
\Upsilon_{k}
is parameterized by
\bm{c}
and
\bm{w}
.
For the numerical computation,
the Wasserstein distance
\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}
is computed by (
49
),
the Radon transform of
\Upsilon_{k}
is computed by a linear combination of (
11
),
and the Radon transform (
51
)
of the empirical measure is given by

\mathcal{R}_{\bm{\theta}}[\Delta_{n}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\langle{\bm{x}}_{i},\bm{\theta}\rangle},

(53)

where we apply nearest neighbor interpolation.

We provide two experiments
for
d\in\{2,3\}

and consider
\Delta_{n}
sampled from the uniform measure on
[-1,-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}]^{d}\cup[\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},1]^{d}

with
n=40
samples for
d=2
and
n=60
for
d=3
.
We use
101
radii equispaced in
[-\sqrt{d},\sqrt{d}]
,
and
128
angles
equispaced on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
,
and Fibonacci points (
46
) on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
We solve (
52
),
with the ADAM optimizer
[
25
]
using 100 epochs,
learning rate
0.05
, smoothing parameters
(0.9,0.99)
,
and initialization
{\bm{c}}_{1,2}=\pm\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}{\bm{e}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

and
{\bm{w}}_{1,2}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{10}}{\bm{e}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The Radon transforms of the target
\Delta_{n}
and optimal measure
\Upsilon_{2}

are visualized in Fig.

.
The iterates are visualized in Fig.

We observe that the computed measures
\Upsilon_{n}

perfectly cluster in both settings.
The corresponding Radon transforms
reproduce the main character
of the target.

[

we approximate the intersection area of
(-1,1]^{d}

and the hyperplane

A_{\bm{\theta}}^{{\bm{e}}}(t)\approx\tfrac{1}{2\varepsilon}V_{\bm{\theta}}^{{\bm{e}}}(t-\varepsilon,t+\varepsilon)=\tfrac{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mathcal{U}((-1,1]^{d})}[|\langle\bm{\theta},{\bm{x}}\rangle-t|\leq\varepsilon]}{2^{1-d}\varepsilon}

(55)

with
N
random samples of
{\bm{x}}
from the uniform distribution
\mathcal{U}((-1,1]^{d})

,

samples with

,
and
\varepsilon=10^{-3}
for the Monte Carlo sampling.

to Sobol’ points
[
67
]
in
[0,1]^{4}

and normalizing to the sphere,
following the construction in
[
43
, § 5.2]
.

11
)
for
128
angles
\bm{\theta}

and
128
radii
t

takes less than 1 second,
whereas

for just
1
angle and
128

seconds
