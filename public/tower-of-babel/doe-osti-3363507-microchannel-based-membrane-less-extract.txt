 Executive Summary
 This final report provides an overview of the Project's entire duration, covering July 1,
2021 to December 31, 2023. It primarily focuses on the achievements, technological
developments, and unique challenges the team faced while working on separating and extracting
Lithium from produced waters.
 The project's primary aim was to create an integrated, high-throughput, membrane-less,
and modular microfluidic platform that could extract Lithium from unconventional sources.
We have successfully met all goals and milestones envisioned in the SOPO document.
 The most critical primary milestones, including the Go-No-Go milestone (refer to the
Gantt chart in the Appendices), were successfully accomplished. We demonstrated phase
separation (>90%) and extraction (>85%) performance in the MPSE using synthetic, and
representative produced water composition feed at 50 ml/min total flow through MPSE 36. We
have also performed a parametric study of the MPSE operations, beyond the scope of SOPO,
exploring operating conditions of current and broader interest. The extended investigation of
operational parameters is concurrent with our efforts to seek further development of the MPSE
technology beyond the scope of the Project. Along these lines of development, we have made
efforts to be responsive to DOE calls for technological developments of other types of resources
(beyond PW) for the recovery of Critical Materials and higher TRL development (beyond TRL
4). These include:
 During the work on this Project, we developed and implemented three innovative
technical approaches that emerged from our efforts to successfully meet the Project milestones.
The innovative & original technical approaches developed and implemented in this Project are
now the contributions to process engineering that could be clearly credited to the Project. First,
Convergent Design Approach is a comprehensive feedforward & feedback loop of four design
phases: i) design for functionality, ii) design for manufacturing, iii) design for sustainability, and
iv) design for market. Next was Process Intensification. A major aim of this Project was to create
an innovative phase separation & extraction microscale-based technology for Li separation –
thus the words microchannel-based in the Project title. A microscale-based technology is
intrinsically in the center of the Process Intensification domain as defined by its unique
principles[1]. Therefore, Process Intensification was implicitly envisioned in the Project’s
SOPO. Lastly, Time Scale Analysis is a novel tool for discovering the needs and directions of
Process Intensification implementations in any process technology.[2,3] This Project is fully
credited for developing and implementing the three novel technical approaches mentioned above.
These are general contributions to process engineering that emerged from this Project.
 Beyond the original SOPO scope, the OSU-U.Pitt research group utilized a Convergent
Design methodology, integrating first-principles mathematical modeling with experimental
validation on the Minimum Development Vehicle. By creating these Digital Twins, the team
rapidly assessed manufacturing iterations to support TEA analysis. This framework further
enabled the development of advanced Surface Modification Techniques, where hydrophobic and
oleophobic coating strategies were optimized via Digital Twin tools and validated through
rigorous 100-hour longevity testing.
 TEA Analysis: The closing efforts of this Project were focused on the TEA analysis. TEA
analysis had two primary functions: i) enabling critical assessments of design variations withing

the Concurrent Design Approach, thus enabling evolution of the MPSE design to reach faster-
better-cheaper alternatives; and ii) to create a bridge between the accomplishments of this Project
and future projects of higher TRL, beyond TRL 6 level. It is important to note that the TEA
model created in the Project stirred the technological solutions for the recovery of critical
materials toward a vision of a very profitable modular plant that has unique zero-waste water
discharge signature. More importantly, thanks to our experimental performance data and
conservative assumptions, the TEA model predicts minimal technological and investment risks.
Low cost of a modular unit of a nominal capacity of [1000 tons of Li2CO3 /year] positions the
MPSE based technology within the reach of community investors, thus offering a paradigm shift
in the development of critical technologies.
 The project successfully navigated two primary challenges: solvent selection and
manufacturing adaptation. Restricted by the SOPO to existing literature for lithium recovery, the
team identified a critical need for a "material excellence program" to develop next-generation
solvents, eventually concluding with a preliminary investigation into promising Ionic Liquids
(ILs). Simultaneously, COVID-19 supply chain disruptions forced a pivot from traditional
manufacturing to advanced additive methods at ATAMI-OSU. By transitioning from stainless
steel to 3D-printed polymer substrates, the team achieved a transformative three-order-of-
magnitude reduction in manufacturing costs and compressed prototyping timelines from several
months to just two days.
 The MPSE technology offers significant energy, environmental, and economic
advantages by overcoming the traditional bottlenecks of phase-separation hardware and
contactor size. Unlike conventional mixer-settlers or membrane-based systems, MPSE operates
without moving parts or fouling-prone membranes, achieving robust performance even with
challenging, viscous, or particulate-heavy feeds. Key performance metrics include an energy
intensity reduction of 5–50x (3–40 kJ/m³) compared to incumbent technologies and a dramatic
reduction of processing time to under 60 seconds, which drastically reduces the physical plant
footprint. These technical efficiencies translate into superior economic outcomes; for a 100 t/year
Li2CO3 facility, implementing MPSE is projected to nearly halve contactor CAPEX (from
$6.08M to $3.01M) and significantly increase the project's Net Present Value (NPV), derisking
new investment and enabling distributed critical-mineral processing configurations.
 The commercialization of MPSE technology is being spearheaded by Vigsur Dynamics
Inc., which has adopted a structured, parallel approach to technical and business development
since its formation in January 2026. Following extensive customer discovery and engagement
with the Oregon State University accelerator, Vigsur Dynamics is working to establish a business
model that transitions from pilot demonstrations to modular hardware sales, ultimately aiming
for a "build-own-operate" service strategy. Current technical milestones—including 100 hours of
continuous operation, superior energy efficiency, and successful 6-unit modular scale-up—
provide a foundation for this transition. Backed by ongoing IP licensing and a growing network
of industrial and venture advisors, the company is actively de-risking the platform to replace
conventional mixer-settler systems in the critical minerals market.

 Introduction
 Solvent Extraction is the industry standard for high-purity metal recovery, yet traditional
macro-scale equipment is often hindered by long residence times and high energy consumption.
To address these inefficiencies, this work introduced the Membrane-less Phase Separation &
Extraction (MPSE) platform—a modular, microfluidic technology adapted from NASA-
developed gas-liquid separation. By utilizing a "numbering-up" approach for infinite scalability
and micro-structures that generate capillary pressure gradients, the MPSE technology enables
continuous, gravity-independent phase separation without the fouling risks of membranes. This
integrated system achieves superior mass transfer and significantly lower energy requirements
compared to conventional methods, effectively overcoming the phase-separation bottleneck
common in both macro and micro-extraction processes.

 The MPSE platform aimed to intensify the extraction of lithium and rare earth elements
from unconventional sources like geothermal fluids and shale gas produced water through a
modular, multi-stage solvent extraction process. Initial goals involved optimizing operational
parameters—such as residence time and flow ratios—to maximize extraction efficiency and
phase separation. In collaboration with the University of Pittsburgh, the project enhanced the
MPSE devices with specialized surface functionalization to improve phase separation, followed
by a comparative performance analysis against conventional mixer-settlers. The project utilize
techno-economic assessments to benchmark the progression of platform development and
employed additive manufacturing to achieve the precise spatial tolerances required for high-
throughput, modular industrial applications.

 The overall aim of this project was to develop an integrated, high-throughput, membrane-
less, and modular microfluidic platform to extract Lithium from unconventional sources. While
selecting the best solvents (available & reported in open communications) was essential, our
work primarily focused on intensifying the extraction and phase separation operations. Our
Membraneless Phase Separation & Extraction (MPSE) is benchmarked relative to the industrial
processes, i.e., mixer-settlers or Karr column, under similar operating conditions. The project
encompasses three essential directions: development of the platform design, evaluation of the
manufacturing method & cost, and testing the performance commensurate to the TRL-4 level.

There were four main objectives of the Project:

 a. Demonstrate the effectiveness (phase separation efficiency, extraction efficiency, and
 realizable processing capacities) of the microscale-based Membranelles Phase Separation
 & Extraction technology.

 b. Extraction of Lithium from Model Synthetic Feed: Using an aqueous feed with the
      following composition: Li+ = 0.35(g/L) as LiCl; Mg2+ = 95 (g/L); Na+ = 1.8 (g/L); K+ =
 0.6 (g/L). We will achieve an Extraction and Phase-separation efficiency (with respect to
 the technical equilibrium[1]) as described in the milestones M1.1.1 and M3.2.1[2].

 c. Extraction of Lithium from Industrial Lithium Feed: Using Shale Gas Produced Water
 provided by Venture Engineering & Construction, Inc., expected to be sourced from

 12

 Marcellus shale, we will achieve an extraction efficiency (with respect to equilibrium[3]),
 and phase-separation efficiencies as described in the milestone M3.2.1 and M3.3[4].

 d. Demonstrate the feasibility of integrating intensified solvent extraction with continuous,
 membrane-less and fouling-resistant phase separation in the MPSE- units and
 demonstrate the amenability of this platform to modularization via scale-up/number-up
 approach.

 It is important to note that the Proposed Project did not seek the development of solvents,
extractant, and diluents. We used the state of the art of organic compound development for the
extraction of Li from real-life unconventional streams.

 This DOE funding had a significant impact on advancing the MPSE from both the
technological and application fronts. On the technological platform side, we addressed
challenges on the path toward operating at industrial capacities. This included controlling the
operation of multiple MPSE units (i.e., a single complete stage with mixing, extraction, and
separation) in parallel, either on a single plate or across multiple plates. Proper control of
pressure and flow distribution was validated, along with operation stability. Operation stability
refers to surface fouling or degrading of surface properties, clogging or obstruction of channels
and micromixers, and the ability of the system to recover from dynamic short-term disturbances,
such as temporary loss of flow.

 On the application side, we were able to develop an entire Li extraction process on the
MPSE platform. This placed us in an ideal position exiting the project to pursue next-stage
funding, build new industrial partnerships, and expand on existing partnerships. Additionally, as
mentioned earlier, this platform and application translated to the extraction and separations of
REE, another critical material target of DOE-AMO. Through this project, we were able to
demonstrate the path or framework to develop new process applications, such as REE extraction.
This served as an important case study of MPSE process development in the continued evolution
of the technology but also a valuable example to share with collaborators.

 Background
Fundamentals about Liquid-Liquid Extraction in Microscale-Based Devices
 We are compelled to introduce the Fundamentals of the MPSE design & operation to
properly understand the physics of the innovation that enabled functionality of the MPSE. More
importantly, proper understanding of the physical fundamentals enabled ‘liberal’ changes in the
MPSE architecture, manufacturing, scale-up (numbering-up), and changes in material properties
involved in operation and construction of the MPSE devices. The proper understanding of the
fundamentals also enabled our overall enhanced design approach that includes complex feed
forward and feedback sequences of design steps – design for functionality – design for
manufacturing – design for market – design for sustainability, which we termed “Concurrent
Design Approach.” While sequencing of different design steps is only relatively new in the

 The fundamentals of our technical approach are based on the introduction of forces into
microscale structures that facilitates movement and distribution of multiphase flow. Notably, the
movement of a discrete phase in multiphase flow (Gas-Liquid, Liquid-Liquid, and Gas-Liquid-
Liquid) is facilitated with these forces, thus creating conditions for the directed movement of any
or all phases present in the multiphase flow.
 In general, the gradient of energy potential (ex. chemical potential, field potentials) is
necessary to create a force. Thus, gradient properties of microscale structures, gradient material
properties or gradients in operating conditions may result in gradient potential energy for any or
all fluids in multiphase flow, providing that these properties are in some way connected to
potential energy (universal chemical potential). One could portray our technical approach, being
facilitated by the creation of local forces based on the local gradients of size, local gradient
material properties, and local gradient of operating conditions.
 Multiphase flow in engineered microscale-based structures (such as microchannels or
micropost features on a lamina – see Figure 2), is subject to a variety of forces (viscous, inertial,
pressure, gravity, buoyancy, etc.), some of which we readily neglect in macroscopic applications.
However, in specific applications like blood processing devices, fermentations, petrochemical
processes, boiling, condensation, liquid-gas separations, liquid-liquid separations, liquid-liquid-
gas separations, fluid processing in microgravity, etc., are greatly influenced with forces that
naturally arise in multiphase flow.

 The net effect of this situation is the presence of ‘free energy’ at the surface. The excess

interface surface is, for whatever reason, curved with a local radius ‘r’ (or alternatively curvature
1/r) the molecules at the interface experience an inward (towards the concave side of the
interface surface) pulling force while not being attracted as strongly by the molecules in the
adjacent phase. In the bulk of the liquid, each molecule is pulled equally in all directions by
nearby liquid molecules, resulting in a zero net force. The fluid’s resistance to elastic
deformation balances this inward oriented force on a curved interface; thus, the internal pressure
of liquid has to rise.
 In a two-phase liquid-gas flow (air-water for example) in porous solid media or
engineered microscale structures, interface surfaces assume a geometrical position such that all
forces (including all other pertinent forces) balance each other; otherwise, the flow of fluids
occurs. This geometrical accommodation at the interface surfaces is (macroscopically)
characterized by the contact angle, which is the angle of the tangent which the interface surface
makes with the solid surface. The diagrams in Figure 3 B and C illustrate the hydrophobic and

Liquid-Liquid Extraction (LLE) of Lithium*
 With the highest redox potential value and heat capacity of any solid element, lithium
became an integral component of numerous renewable energy, transportation, communication,
information, and defense technologies [4]. Knowing that 65% of lithium global end-use markets
were dedicated to Lithium-Ion Battery (LIB) in 2020, and given the expected 5 to 10 folds
growth in the battery market during the next decade, the demand for lithium is projected to
increase by over 40 times in 2040 relative to 2020 [5–8]. It is estimated that 65% of economic
concentrations of lithium are globally found in brines; continental 59%, geothermal 3%, and
oilfield brine 3%, with most resources located in South America [4]. Along with adsorption, and
ion exchange, LLE is considered a promising lithium extraction process, particularly suited for
brines with modest lithium concentrations such as geothermal brines in the United States
[4,6,9,10]. It is expected that the recovery of lithium from geothermal brines in the U.S. can
constitute a reliable and secure domestic supply of that element [10]. In the literature, LLE has
been extensively used for the successful recovery of lithium from brines [9,11–20], recycled LIB
leachates [21–24], seawater [25,26], produced water [27–29], along other real or synthetic
sources. Multiple commercial extractants have been used for the recovery of lithium and REE
[30] such as D2EHPA [31], Versatic 10 [23], Cyanex 923 [32], ionic liquids such as Aliquat 336
[16,20,27], and Tributyl Phosphate (TBP) [9,13,14,17,21]. TBP, a neutral organophosphorus, is
one of the most widely used and investigated extractants of lithium from brine and other sources.
This popularity is attributed to its high extraction efficiency, selectivity, simple mechanism,
reduced cost, and low consumption, especially when coupled with ferric chloride (FeCl3) co-
extractant [9,19]. On the other hand, organic diluents are needed to reduce the extractant’s
viscosity and density thus promoting lithium mass transfer [9,18]. While multiple diluents such
as MIBK, kerosene, and 2-octanol have been reported with TBP [7,11,14,18], Diethyl Succinate
(DS) was favored given its non-toxicity, high flash point, low volatility, and minimal solubility
with water [9]. Another advantage of DS is its high polarity, which allows the polar metal-
extractant complex to easily dissolve in the organic phase [9]. Moreover, FeCl3 as a co-extractant
for TBP has been found to significantly increase the extraction performance via the formation of
FeCl4- anion [9,11,18,19]. The TBP/FeCl3 system extracts lithium from brine via the following
reactions [9,19]:

Micro-Extractors for Critical Minerals Liquid-Liquid Extraction (LLE)*
 Micro-extractors include multiple “passive” and “active” micromixers operating under
different two-phase flow patterns [35–38]. Although the exact mechanism of mass transfer
depends on the specific architecture and fluidic conditions, these devices generally seek to
minimize the solute diffusional lengths and times, maximize the interfacial surface area, while
developing internal convective flows to intensify mass transfer. As a result, they allow for
extraction times of mere seconds instead of minutes observed in conventional systems, with mass
transfer coefficients two to three orders higher [27,39–44]. In hydrometallurgy and other
applications, “passive” micro-extractors are most commonly used given their simplicity and
independence from external power sources [35]. Simple T- and Y-junction micromixers
connected to straight capillary extraction channels were extensively used to create slug/droplet
two-phase flow for the recovery of lithium [25,45,46], lanthanum (La) [47], yttrium (Y) [48],
copper (Cu) [49], americium (Am) [50], uranium (U) [51], among many other metals [44]. A
3D-Printed Y-shaped microreactor was recently developed for the extraction of indium (In) and
iron (Fe) [52]. Efficient serpentine, spiral, or helical capillary extraction channels were also
investigated to separate metals such as praseodymium (Pr)/neodymium (Nd) [53], cerium (Ce)
[54], lithium [55], and calcium (Ca) [56]. Parallel extraction channels that achieve mass transfer
across the interface without phase mixing were reported for the separation of Y/Eu (europium)
[57], heavy/light REE [58], among other elements [59]. Despite their merits, these previous
systems were mainly low-throughput, requiring an extremely large number of units to reach
industrial production levels [57,60]. Alternatively, high-throughput micro-extractors were
adopted for hydrometallurgical applications; re-entrance flow microreactors were used for the
separation of Co [61], nickel (Ni) [60,61], and other metals [62]. Their conical microchannels
create intensive convection which increases the interfacial area and regenerates it to improve
mass transfer. Complex micro-extractors housing a large array of microchannels were also
developed, such as the counter-current interdigital micromixer [63] and a 3D-Printed zigzag
microchannels device [64,65]. These systems were successfully used for the separation of Cu,
platinum (Pt), palladium (Pd), rhodium (Rh), and other metals.
 One of the simplest yet very effective high-throughput micro-extractors is the Coiled-
Flow Inverter (CFI) [44,66]. While multiple design variations exist, a standard CFI consists of a
square structure supporting a helical coil tube with alternating 90˚ bends located at equal tube
length intervals [44,67]. Mixing in CFI is intensified by the centrifugal forces in the helical tubes
acting on the fluids perpendicularly to the direction flow. These forces create secondary flows
critical for cross-sectional mixing via Dean vortices. Additionally, flow inversion after each 90˚
bend leads to the rotation of these Dean vortices and the inversion of the cross-sectional velocity

systems or other high-throughput micro-extractors has not been investigated.
 The two-phase flow regime in micro-extractors not only impacts mass transfer efficiency
and mechanism, but also the difficulty of the subsequent phase separation in micro-separators
[40,66,71]. While multiple two-phase flow patterns were reported in the literature [41,42,66,76–
79], three main groups can be distinguished: parallel/annular flow, slug flow, and
droplet/dispersed droplet flow [66,80]. The appearance of these regimes is contingent on
multiple parameters such as the relative ratio of fluidic forces (inertial, viscous, interfacial, and
gravitational), surface wettability, the ratio of phases, and architectural features [41,77–79]. As
shown in figure 3-1, a trade-off exists between increased extraction efficiency and difficulty of
phase separation [66,80]. While the parallel/annular flow requires minimal phase separation, the
small interfacial surface area, reduced mass transfer, instability of the interface, and low
throughput limits its application [40,81,82]. Conversely, the interfacial tension-dominated slug
flow is desired given its higher mass transfer and throughputs, in addition to the relative ease of
separation [35,41,83]. This intensification of mass transfer is promoted by the small diffusional
distances and increased interfacial surface area [35,76,84,85]. Additionally, the shear between
the moving slugs and the walls induces strong internal convective circulation within the slugs
that accelerates solute exchange [35,39,76,81,86]. These Taylor-like vortices regenerate the
interfacial surface, redistribute the solute within the slug, and reduce the interfacial thickness
[39,76,86,87]. Once the slug size becomes smaller than the microchannel diameter, a
droplet/dispersed droplet regime starts to emerge. This flow is characterized by an increase in
interfacial surface area and mass transfer coefficient, but also phase separation challenges
[41,80]. It develops under inertial or viscous-dominated conditions, with microdroplets’
diameters down to 5-150 μm for the dispersed droplet regime [38,40,41,88,89]. In this pattern,
mass transfer is also enhanced by the strong internal circulation within the droplets due to inertial
deformation [41,76]. Extraction in this regime was reported to be 20-50 times faster than in slug
flow [40,41,76,90].

Process Intensification
Microchannel-based Membraneless Phase Separation and Extraction Technology
 Process intensification (PI) in chemical manufacturing targets redesigning manufacturing
processes to make them significantly more efficient, often resulting in smaller, safer, and more
sustainable operations. This approach aims to reduce energy consumption, minimize waste, and
enhance the overall efficiency and effectiveness of chemical production. This class of
innovations has emerged due to new driving forces such as environmental and community
impacts, global competitiveness, and new markets. PI in microfluidic solvent extractors can
revolutionize extraction, recycling, and purification of critical materials. With the increasing
supply risks and demand for the critical materials, and the separation bottlenecks in conventional
technology, there is an ever-growing need for efficient liquid-liquid micro-separators.
 LLE is considered the primary industrial process for the extraction of critical minerals
such as lithium and other valuable metals.[2,35,91,92] Its popularity stems from its cost-
effectiveness, scalability, simplicity, and high material purity yielding up to 99.99%.[2,93]
Mixer-settlers are by far the most widely conventional operation in LLE, while column and
centrifugal extractors are dedicated to specialized applications.[2,94] In mixer-settlers,
gravitational liquid-liquid separation is considered a process bottleneck, requiring 3X the
residence times of its mixing stage. For mixing times of 3-20 minutes, a settling period of 9-60
min can be expected, extending up to weeks if stable emulsions are formed.[37,42,78,86] Micro-
extractors and micro-separators intensify LLE by accelerating these steps to seconds instead of
minutes or hours.[37,79] Microchannels also offer a noticeable reduction in power requirements
compared to conventional extractors.[76] For these reasons, achieving industrial throughputs via
numbering-up LLE microfluidic units promises important advantages such as increased process
efficiency, reduced chemical and energy consumption, smaller footprint, in addition to flexible
investment and operation costs.[41,66,83] LLE can be fundamentally transformed if intensified
microscale-based extraction can be realized.

Project Goals and Objectives
 The overall aim of this project was to develop an integrated, high-throughput, membrane-
less, and modular microfluidic platform to extract Lithium from unconventional sources. While
selecting the best solvents (available & reported in open communications) was essential, our
work primarily focused on intensifying the extraction and phase separation operations. Our
Membraneless Phase Separation & Extraction (MPSE) is benchmarked relative to the industrial
processes, i.e., mixer-settlers or Karr column, under similar operating conditions. The project
encompasses three essential directions: development of the platform design, evaluation of the
manufacturing method & cost, and testing the performance commensurate to the TRL-4 level.

There were four main objectives of the Project:

 a. Demonstrate the effectiveness (phase separation efficiency, extraction efficiency, and
 realizable processing capacities) of the microscale-based Membranelles Phase Separation
 & Extraction technology.

 b. Extraction of Lithium from Model Synthetic Feed: Using an aqueous feed with the
      following composition: Li+ = 0.35(g/L) as LiCl; Mg2+ = 95 (g/L); Na+ = 1.8 (g/L); K+ =

 c. Extraction of Lithium from Industrial Lithium Feed: Using Shale Gas Produced Water
 provided by Venture Engineering & Construction, Inc., expected to be sourced from

 An industrial-scale LLE intensification of critical minerals holds major economic and
environmental benefits. For that purpose, low-cost, efficient, high-throughput, and mass-
producible microfluidic units with amenability for numbering-up must be developed. In this
work, we manufacture and test the first low-cost, recyclable, and high-throughput 3D-Printed
Micro-Separator Monolith (MPSE) for a continuous, capillary-based, membrane-free, and
gravity-independent liquid-liquid separation. 3D printing, in the form of the widely accessible
Fused Deposition Modelling (FDM), allowed an inexpensive, automated, portable, and waste-
free production of these recyclable and polymeric MPSE units [95–100]. Additionally, this
manufacturing technique grants the versatility of rapid prototyping, in-situ production, and the
use of a wide range of polymeric materials to obtain desired surface characteristics and
physicochemical properties.
 The monolithic nature and design of the device rescind the need for internal sealing,
fouling-prone membrane, and surface treatment. The MPSE was integrated with an upstream CFI
to intensify lithium extraction from synthetic brine using TBP/FeCl3/DS. This application served
as a model case for broader hydrometallurgical processes. The CFI was previously identified as a
simple and effective micro-extractor with good pre-disposition for industrial applications yet
with limited hydrometallurgical use [71–74]. The extraction and separation performance of the
CFI-MPSE system, in addition to its power requirement, were assessed under the optimum
conditions for lithium extraction and different flow patterns and conditions. To the best of our
knowledge, no previous high-throughput microfluidic system to intensify extraction and phase
separation has been developed for hydrometallurgical applications. The manufacturing aspects
and functionality of this system render it particularly suited for earth-bound modular LLE
applications, in addition to in-situ resource extraction for the space-industry [101,102]. *

Architecture and Manufacturing of the 3D-Printed Micro-Separator Monolith (MPSE)
 The 3D-printed MPSE enclosed 36 individual sloped channels numbered-up radially for
parallel operation. Each sloped channel achieved capillary-based phase separation as described in
the fundamentals of device operation in section 3.2.1. The channels’ architecture was identical,
with “floor” and “ceiling” surfaces angled at 6.3˚, leading to minimum and maximum sides’
depths of 530 µm and 2000 µm, respectively, and a channel width of 14.6 mm. Figure 9 shows
the different sections of the MPSE from horizontal and vertical cross-section perspectives; The
cone-like inlet manifold ensured the even distribution of the two-phase flow to all 36 channels
while minimizing the regions of fluid stagnation and promoting coalescence. The cone was
angled at 73.8˚relative to the horizontal plane to maintain structural integrity during 3D printing
and was supported by 4 symmetrical beams reaching the MMS’ outer structure. The two-phase
flow was subsequently separated in the sloped channels extending upwards for 55.7 mm, with
the wetting and non-wetting fluids respectively directed toward the smaller and larger sides of
the channels. At the end (top) of each sloped channel, the separated fluids exited via two outlets
located on opposite lateral sides of the channel. The outlets’ positions and geometries within a
sloped channel are shown in Figure 9. Finally, similar fluid phases exiting from the 36 channels
were recombined in an outlet manifold and subsequently directed out of the device via their
respective ports. The MPSE had a total internal flow volume of 47.7 ml, with an effective
separation volume (only slope channels) of 37 ml. The circular footprint of MPSE had a diameter
of 45.3 mm, and a total vertical length of 105.7 mm. Given its monolithic nature, the device did
not require any form of internal sealing and offered a “plug-and-play” operational capability. All
3D drawings were performed on Dassault Systèmes' SolidWorks 2021-2022.
 MPSE’s architectural features were tailored to be effectively producible by low-cost
commercial 3D printers. The device was thus successfully printed using Fused Deposition
Modelling (FDM) technique on a Prusa i3 MK3S+ 3D printer (Prusa Research A.S.; Prague,
Czech Republic). The printer was equipped with the standard 0.4 mm nozzle. PETG was the
print material of choice given its chemical compatibility with our used solvents and desirable
surface-wetting properties.
 The PETG filament (Overture 3D; Missouri City, TX) had a diameter of 1.75 mm, and no
material or surface treatment steps were carried out before or after printing. The MPSE design
was imported into the PrusaSlicer 2.4.2 software as a binary STL file with 18484 triangle
elements. The device was then vertically positioned with its inlet facing the build plate as shown
in (figure 3-4). Subsequently, support material was exclusively added to the MPSE’s exterior
while avoiding any obstruction to the interior flow volume. The infill density was set to 15% and
the print layer height to 0.15 mm. These settings ensured good print quality within a reasonable
print time. Prusa’s recommended temperature settings for PETG were used, with a nozzle

temperature of 230 °C for the first layer, and 240 °C for the other layers. The bed temperature
was 85 °C for the first layer, and 90 °C for the other layers. The print time was estimated at 17

Two-Phase Flow Regimes in the CFI
 As can be noticed in figure 3-8, three different flow patterns emerge in the CFI: regular
slugs at Q-, -,
 2)2 = 20 ml/min, irregular slugs at Q 2)2 = 30 and 40 ml/min, and dispersed droplets at

non-wetting dispersed phase synthetic brine is yellow. At equilibrium, both solutions are pale
yellow due to ferric iron transfer, with the brine slightly darker. The occurrence of the two-phase
patterns is contingent on the ratios of the interfacial, inertial, and viscous forces in the extraction
system, and they determine the rate and mechanism of mass transfer [37,41,61,71,76,88].
 At Q-,
 2)2 = 20 ml/min, small and uniform brine slugs of approximately 5 mm in length
appeared throughout the CFI almost immediately after the T-mixer, suggesting a fluidic behavior
dominated by interfacial forces. These surface tension forces lead to the spontaneous
segmentation of the two-phase flow, and subsequent formation of the slugs, while preserving the
stability of their interface by resisting the inertial and viscous stresses as they pass through the

coiled tube [71,85]. The low Capillary (Ca = = ) and Weber (We = = ) numbers of the organic
and aqueous phases at 20 ml/min highlight the dominance of the interfacial force over the
viscous and inertial forces in the CFI, respectively (Table 3). While not captured by our cameras,
a thin film of the wetting TBP/DS less than 25 µm in thickness is expected to be surrounding the
brine slugs [84]. We calculated an interfacial surface area of approximately 544 m2/m3 under this
regime [103].
 As the flow rates are increased to 30 and 40 ml/min, an increase of the viscous and
inertial forces relative to the interfacial force occurs in the T-mixer and CFI, reflected by higher
Ca and We numbers. Slugs are formed only after few centimeters downstream from the T-mixer,
with a jetting effect observed before that. This effect has been reported experimentally and
numerically under higher Ca numbers [104]. It is expected that as the flow experiences a sudden
drop in velocity transitioning from the T-mixer (ID=0.5 mm) to the much larger CFI (ID=3.175
mm), the segmentation of the two-phase flow by the interfacial forces, which ultimately leads to
the formation of slugs takes time to occur. Furthermore, downstream in the CFI, the instability of
the slugs’ interface by inertial and viscous stresses as they pass through the tube results in their
deformation and subsequent potential merging [61,75,78,79,88,89]. As shown in Figure 14,
larger irregular slugs are observed in this regime, especially at the higher flow rate of 40 ml/min,
where slug lengths exceeding 2 cm were observed. This disturbance of the slug structure results
in a substantial decrease of the interfacial specific surface area available for mass transfer and an
increase of diffusional lengths [41,75]. A similar disruption of slug uniformity at high flow rates
accompanied by a decrease in mass transfer was reported for the extraction of critical minerals
from highly viscous feeds in CFI [61,75].

performance in MPSE is not solely governed by interfacial forces. The interactions of the
interfacial, inertial, viscous, and to a lesser extent gravitational forces, create the force landscape
in the spacetime domain that dictates MPSE’s functionality. While a Computational Fluid
Dynamics (CFD) mathematical model is warranted to capture the local dynamic forces exerted
on the two-phase interface in MPSE, the overall effects of those fluidic forces on separation can
be outlined. The hydrodynamic pressure drop (∆PKL/ ) is a dependent variable relating geometric
dimensions, total flow rate (Q-, 2)2 ), and dynamic viscosity (μ), thus combining inertial and viscous
terms. Consequently, the relative magnitudes of ∆PKL/ and ∆P. at a given orifice is a reliable
predictor of separation performance used to assess previous micro-separators [40,71,82,94,108].
A sudden separation failure, reflected by a breakthrough of the non-wetting phase across an
opening, was expected when ∆PKL/ exceeded the co-linear opposing ∆P. in these systems. In
MPSE however, the direction of ∆PKL/ (z-dimension) is perpendicular and not co-linear to ∆P.C32
(x-dimension) Figure 15B. Consequently, even when ∆PKL/ > ∆P.C32 , the lateral movement of a
non-wetting droplet towards the wider edge of the channel can be partially retained. For a non-
wetting droplet located at the narrowest side of the channel’s entrance, the relative ratios of
∆PKL/ to ∆P.C32 should be such that, the time needed for the droplet to reach the aqueous outlet
(τM3N ) is smaller or equal to the residence time of the droplet in the channel (τ*3M ). Consequently,
for this simple illustrative case, three different scenarios can be identified:
 • Case 1: ∆PKL/ <<< ∆P.C32 : Successful separation, τM3N <<< τ*3M , with droplet trajectory ≈
 width of the sloped channel (14.60 mm)

 39

 • Case 2: ∆PKL/ >>> ∆P.C32 : No separation, τM3N >>> τ*3M , with droplet trajectory ≈ length
 of the sloped channel (55.7 mm)
 • Case 3: Comparable ∆PKL/ and ∆P.C32 : Successful separation provided that τM3N ≤ τ*3M .
 Therefore, for a fixed sloped channel geometry, the separation performance can be
improved by operating under higher interfacial tensions (σ) and reduced wetting angles (θ),
leading to greater interfacial forces ∆P.C32 (Equation 16). Conversely, the separation efficiency
can be expected to drop with increasing flow rates (Q-,  2)2 ) and fluids viscosities (μ), translating to
higher ratios of inertial and viscous forces relative to interfacial forces (∆PKL/ /∆P.C32 ). It is
worth noting that operating with highly viscous fluids such as synthetic brine (𝜇OPQRS = 5.2 cP)
and TBP/DS (𝜇 TOU/VW = 3.08 cP) is expected to augment the viscous drag acting on the lateral
movement of the non-wetting phase. This in turn results in detrimental effects on separation.
3D COMSOL models were developed to obtain reliable ∆PKL/ and velocity estimates in an
MPSE sloped channel for both the wetting TBP/DS and the non-wetting brine (Figure 16). These
single-phase models reflect the highest pressures and velocities expected in our experimental
runs at Q-,
 2)2 = 60 ml/min/MMS. ∆PKL/ values of 4.5 and 8.5 Pa were found for TBP/DS and
brine, respectively. Hence, the actual two-phase system is expected to have ∆PKL/ values ranging
between these limits depending on phase composition in the channel. Comparing the minimum
and maximum ∆PKL/ values to ∆P.C32 in Figure 15A, it can be deduced that ∆P.C32 values greater
than ∆PKL/ can be generated in the MPSE even under these unfavorable separation conditions.
The maximization of ∆P.C32 can be particularly achieved under large droplets with high ∆P.D
and ∆P.E differential, and smaller channel depths.
 Finally, by placing the MPSE in a vertical position, the gravitational forces will be
exerted perpendicularly to ∆P.C32 . Therefore, the impact of gravity on the lateral two-phase
separation (x-dimension) is negligible. The gravitational effect will be limited to its influence on
fluid velocities in the z-dimension due to buoyancy.

conditions of high viscosity (𝜇OPQRS = 5.2 cP, 𝜇 TOU/VW = 3.08 cP), limited interfacial tension (σ
= 12.8 mN/m), and high wetting angle (θ = 72˚), satisfactory phase separation can be achieved in
MPSE up to 50 ml/min. To the best of our knowledge, high-throughput separation of
hydrometallurgical feeds in micro-separators is rare in the literature, with the MPSE operating at
flow rates x5 higher than previously reported values. Additionally, separating the highly-efficient
dispersed droplet flow regime at high throughputs without the need for a coalescer is a
distinctive ability of the MPSE [40,66,94]. These operational capabilities can be ascribed to
MMS’ design, which allows the internal numbering-up of 36 sloped channels, and the successful
distribution of the two-phase flow amongst them. Consequently, effective interfacial capillary
pressures (∆P.C32 ) can be generated in each channel, permitting both separation intensification on
an individual level and a scale-up of throughput on a collective level.
 The excellent separation observed up to 40 ml/min indicates that the non-wetting brine
was completely directed toward the deeper edge of the sloped channel by ∆P.C32 , and in the
process displacing TBP/DS to the narrower side. As expected, and similar to other micro-
separators, operating under slug-dominated regimes did not present major separation challenges,
owing to the slugs’ large size relative to dispersed droplets and their fast coalescence [40,66,71].
Additionally, these favorable experimental results, coupled with COMSOL’s pressure drop
estimates (Figure 15A and Figure 16A), suggest that ∆PKL/ was sufficiently low relative to
∆P.C32 to ensure complete phase separation, satisfying the τM3N ≤ τ*3M condition. Conversely, an
expected drop in separation performance was seen under the efficient yet hard-to-separate
dispersed droplet regime at Q-,2)2 of 50 and 60 ml/min. It was interesting to observe that the purity
of the outlet flow at the aqueous exit port (V%#$ ) remained relatively high, while it was
disproportionally lower at the organic port (V%)*+ ). This discrepancy happened despite forcing
the outlet flow rates from the aqueous and organic ports to be equal. These high levels of V%#$
strongly suggest that the ∆P.C32 pressures generated by the sloped channels served their purpose
of forcing the aqueous brine towards the wider end of the channels and ultimately to the aqueous
outlet. This conclusion is further supported by the complete absence of brine droplets in the
outlet streams at 50 and 60 ml/min, possibly indicating that most of the aqueous parcels in MPSE
sufficiently coalesced to exceed the critical diameter of 530 µm required for ∆P.C32 generation.
 Furthermore, as shown in Figure 15A, the magnitude of ∆P.C32 relative to ∆PKL/
remained considerable enough to induce phase separation even at 60 ml/min. As for the low
V%)*+ values, they can be directly attributed to the large ∆PKL/ pressures exerted by the highly
viscous brine at elevated flow rates near the organic exit port. These significant ∆PKL/ pressures
overcome the repelling ∆P.C32 generated by the less viscous TBP/DS, leading the aqueous brine
to break through the organic outlet along TBP/DS. Similar behavior has been repeatedly
observed in previous micro-separators [71,94]. The velocity profile in the sloped channel
predicted by our COMSOL 3D model at 60 ml/min captures this performance deterioration near
the organic outlet. As shown in Figure 16B, the sharp velocity increase near that narrow port
promotes the surge of ∆PKL/ responsible for the brine’s breakthrough. This effect can occur
regardless of the satisfactory separation happening in previous parts of the sloped channel.
Furthermore, it is expected that this preferential exit of the aqueous brine from the outlets will
ultimately induce an accumulation of the organic phase in the device based on mass balance
considerations. Consequently, it is projected that after this build-up reaches a certain critical

 43

point, the wetting organic phase will be forced to exit unhindered through the exit ports, possibly
bringing the phase purities at both outlets to similar levels. This “build-up” and “release” may
amount to a periodic cycle, and a deeper investigation of this behavior is warranted. Finally,
based on the findings above, it is expected that repositioning the outlets to the channel’s sides
can improve separation by decreasing the velocity gradients near the exit region. Additionally, a
post-printing surface treatment of the polymer to increase its hydrophilicity and/or
hydrophobicity can allow for higher throughput processing by increasing the magnitude of
∆P.C32 . Both tasks are being pursued in our laboratories.

Ionic Liquid Testing
 Ionic liquid (IL) is a promising candidate for solvent extraction due to its outstanding
properties, including high thermal stability, negligible volatility, nonﬂammability, tunable
solubility and viscosity, and good thermal and radiation stability. Specifically, the low volatility
is critical to decarbonization. Although not in our original plan, we have investigated IL as the
extraction solvent for Li extraction using our MPSE technology.
 First, we have reproduced the bulk Li extraction using IL as the extraction solvent as
reported previously. A previous study has shown that 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium
bis(triﬂuoromethylsulfonyl) imide ([BMIM][NTF2]), with TBP as the extractant, can achieve
single extraction efficiency of Li as high as ~92% under optimal conditions.[116] Before we
conduct Li extraction using the MPSE device, we tested bulk extraction using the same optimal
conditions reported in the literature. For bulk extraction, 2.02 g/L lithium ions were added to DI
water to mimic Salt Lake water brine (aqueous phase) reported in the literature. The organic
phase contains 90 vol.% of TBP and 10 vol.% of BMIM-NTF2. An optimal organic-to-aqueous
phase ratio of 2:1 was used for bulk extraction. The oil-water mixture was then well-mixed and
settled for Inductively Coupled Plasma (ICP) characterization to determine the Li ion
concentration in both aqueous and organic phases. ICP results indicated the extraction efficiency
of Li bulk extraction was ~84%, showing that BMIM-NTF2 is effective for Li extraction.
 Then we conducted lithium extraction experiments utilizing our single-channel MPSE

 60

device without any coating. Here we maintained the same liquid mixture but with a reduction in
the organic-to-aqueous (O/A) ratio from 2:1 to 1:1, necessitated by the restrictions imposed by
dual syringe pumps. We also substituted [EMIM][NTF2] for [BMIM][NTF2] in the current setup
since [BMIM] [NTF2] has been discontinued by the vendor. However, these two ionic liquids are
closely analogous. In the MPSE setup, both syringes were loaded with 50 mL of the organic
mixture (ionic liquid + TBP) and 50 mL of saltwater brine. An inlet flow rate of 5 mL/min was
applied to enhance the separation efficiency. Surprisingly, we observed a reversed separation
phenomenon, resulting in 72 vol% of organic substances, with a total volume of 36 mL, being
discharged from the water port, and 63 vol% of the water phase, with a total volume of 57 mL,
exiting from the organic port. Figure 39 illustrates the distinctive interface of this separation,
which is different from what we observed in our previous setup with a simple water/hexadecane
model system.

Techno-Economic Analysis

 Further information gathered as part of this project allowed a more sophisticated vision
for the overall process flow as well as the assessment of economics. This analysis further
supports the idea of a pre-concentration step for all aqueous feedstocks. Upon analysis it was
found that there is sizable advantage in removing water to make the concentrate which then
reduces both the size of the recover/treating MPSE system as well as the consumption of
extraction chemicals. As a crude illustration: if the aqueous feed has a total dissolved solid
(TDS) level of 5 wt% then a feed of 1.0 metric ton of solids would require 20 MT of solution.
Concentrating the feed to 25 wt% TDS reduces the water content and would only require about 4
MT of solution. In terms of feed volume for extraction this would provide a rough reduction
from about 5,280 gallons to 1,060 gallons. This translates to much smaller equipment and much
lower extraction solvent consumption as well as reduced waste generation.
 Further investigation found that there were multiple approaches to concentrating the
aqueous feed stream. This can be accomplished intentionally by adopting existing designs for
water desalination. Many approaches are already in large scale application and can be easily
licensed or even purchased as turn-key units thus obviating the need for a unique
design/development effort. Available common technologies include thermal evaporation,
mechanical vapor recompression, multiple-effect evaporation, and reverse osmosis. Other
desalination approaches that are still in developmental stages were also considered including
solvent desalination and super-critical desalination. However, given the commercial availability

 62

of existing approaches, the latter were not pursued. A primary benefit of adopting a
concentration process step was production of purified water as a byproduct. This is a particular
attraction in arid regions where pure water is often in short supply. The researchers found that
concentrated high TDS aqueous streams are often readily available from existing desalination
process units and, because of this, further design work was done using concentrated feed streams
(usually at least 20 wt% or 200,000 ppmw) which obviated an investment in desalination steps.
 The TEA further suggested that the modular MPSE design can sustain an economic
advantage over a very wide scaling range. This is illustrated in the figure which shows the
estimated capital investment per annual metric ton of lithium carbonate production for a
conventional stick-built (CSB) plant using conventional mixer/settler technology compared with
a modular MPSE plant. This suggests that any advantage from scale that accrues to CSB design
is more than offset by the advantages associated with modular chemical process intensification
(MPSI) design and fabrication. These advantages arise from shop fabrication of the modules,
cost benefits associated with design replication (only do engineering one-time, bulk ordering of
standardized components, learning curve benefits with craft personnel and others). Furthermore,
modular designs do not require complex site preparation and deep foundations which sharply
reduces the time and cost of site installation. Analysis suggests that the optimum size of a single
train modular MPSE process would be around 2,500 MT (up to 5,000) of product per year.
Additional capacity is achieved by installing parallel process trains (this is referred to as
numbering-up) as needed. These additional process trains would be expected to be more cost
effective by benefiting from the engineering and site experience of the first installation.

 In summary, MPSE extraction designs are the enabling technology to energize use of
liquid-liquid extraction at a modular capacity level. It does this by removing the former
constraint on LLE imposed by required large economies of scale due to the high capital and the

 66

operating/maintenance cost resulting from many moving parts and complexity. MPSE offers
lower capital from advanced manufacturing techniques and has no moving parts. For example, a
modular unit producing 10 metric tons of lithium carbonate per year is projected to use about 7
MPSE assemblies (each using 15 MPSE units) with a combined weight of about 500 kg. This
compares to conventional mixer/settler designs which require 8 mixed tanks ranging between 70
and 200 gallons. These would weigh in excess of 4,000 kg. The MPSE cost is still under study

costs for the MPSE units are about $5/unit or $500 for the same capacity as $8,000-$10,000 for
even simple mixer/settlers. Another consideration that arose during this project was
incorporation of a feedstock concentration step at the front end of the envisioned process. The
idea is multi-fold: first is to remove a large amount of the water from the feed stream prior to the
extraction step which reduces the overall load on the MPSE system; second is to increase the
metal salt concentration which should result in higher lithium extraction efficiency; third, the
concentrator would provide a 'pure' water stream which would be suitable for at minimum
agricultural and non-potable human use. In many cases the front-end concentration step is
obviated by using the depleted discharge from an existing desalination unit. An ancillary effort
was also directed at reducing the overall water consumption of the process, making it more
attractive in arid regions. A global benefit is that the process becomes indifferent to the source
of the feed water making it applicable to many sources such as produced water, flowback,
geothermal, RO brine, and many more. Although design, costing, and optimization work
continues, directionally all factors support previous economic conclusions.

 As part of the Digital Twin package, our group created a process flow sheet and
simulation representing a larger view of the lithium extraction process, going beyond the first-

necessary for a design tool that will work with the Techno-Economic Analysis. Two processes
were developed: 1) the conventional process that uses traditional unit ops for the extraction and
separation unit operations and 2) the MPSE process that replaces that portion with the MPSE
technology.

 72

 The overall process is adapted from Zhou et al. (2022) which proposed the LLE process
for extracting lithium from salt-lake brines and performed preliminary experiments using
benchtop batch mixing and centrifuge separation.[9] Our process consists of four LLE
operations: 1) Extraction, 2) Washing, 3) Stripping, and 4) Regeneration. We decided to
implement a conventional counter-current flow LLE tower or column for each of the four
operations. Alternatively, we could implement mixer-settler unit operations in the process
simulation. Brine, containing Li, enter the extraction operation with solvent or diluent diethyl
succinate or MIBK, containing extractant TBP or co-extractant FeCl3. The solvent stream
containing Li moves to the washing operation where it is washed with HCl + MgCl2 to remove
the other metal ions. Next the solvent stream continues to the stripping operation with HCl +
NaCl, where Li is finally sent for further product preparation to Li2CO3. The solvent stream
continues to the regeneration operation with Mg(OH)2 and MgCO3. The regenerated solvent
effluent is recycled back to the first extraction process while the aqueous effluent is recycled to
the washing process. Both process simulations were nearly identical except for the extraction
process which either implements the LLE tower or MPSE.
 One challenge we continue to work on, during and beyond the project, was the scarcity of
thermodynamic data or multi-component interaction parameters to simulate the chemical
equilibrium in these LLE processes. Our team continued to review existing data and even
considering moving to a model system using more common chemical components. Our
alternative plan, and one we have utilized in previous work, is to dictate the extent of extraction
and separation based on real results from our own work and from literature. This has the
advantage of grounding the simulation in real performance (i.e., the technical equilibrium) but
also with the drawback of limiting design flexibility, particularly staging. In either scenario, the
process simulation provides us valuable feedback regarding mass and energy balance, and
equipment sizing for the TEA and became an integral component of the Digital Twin Design
Tool.

 Benefits Assessment
 Commercial implementation of MPSE is expected to provide substantial energy,
environmental, and economic benefits for lithium extraction and broader hydrometallurgical
separations. MPSE addresses a major bottleneck in solvent extraction flowsheets: the contactor
and phase-separation hardware, which still drives plant footprint, solvent inventory, energy
consumption, reliability, and build time. Conventional solvent extraction contactors such as
mixer-settlers, columns, and centrifugal contactors are proven technologies, but they generally
rely either on gravity settling and large residence volumes or on mechanically intensified
equipment with rotating elements and seals. Emerging intensified systems, including
microseparators and membrane-based approaches, can reduce footprint and improve mass-
transfer rates, but published studies report deterioration under dispersed flow regimes and
viscous feeds, which are common in hydrometallurgical applications [40,66,94]. Membrane-
based systems are also susceptible to fouling and replacement burdens when processing
particulate-containing real feeds [117]. MPSE is intended to retain the advantages of intensified
solvent extraction while avoiding common failure modes associated with membranes, moving
parts, and assembled seals. This is consistent with our experimental results showing robust
performance on high-Mg/Li synthetic brines, produced water, and other challenging aqueous
streams.

 A primary anticipated benefit of MPSE is lower energy consumption. MPSE operated at
approximately 3-40 kJ/m3 on viscous lithium-relevant feeds, compared with literature
benchmark values of approximately 150-250 kJ/m3 for mixer-settlers, 175-250 kJ/m3 for
rotating disk impinging-stream contactors, and 850-2600 kJ/m3 for centrifugal contactors [76].
These results indicate that MPSE could significantly reduce the specific energy demand of
solvent extraction, especially in lithium and critical-minerals flowsheets that require multiple
stages, translating to roughly 5-50x lower energy intensity per unit volume than incumbent
systems. Lower energy demand would reduce operating cost and indirect greenhouse gas
emissions associated with electricity use.

 MPSE is also expected to provide major benefits in processing speed and footprint
reduction. Our results show extraction and phase separation in less than 60 seconds. In contrast,

 74

conventional mixer-settlers typically require approximately 3-20 minutes of mixing and 9-60
minutes of settling per stage [57,118,119], and published reports show that settling can become
much longer and operationally problematic when stable emulsions form [119,120]. Faster stage
completion directly increases throughput per unit equipment volume and reduces the size of the
required contactor train. This is consistent with broader process-intensification literature and
reported space-time-yield differences between conventional and intensified systems [121],
including values of approximately 75 L L⁻¹ day⁻¹ for mixer-settlers versus 1000-8000 L L⁻¹ day⁻¹
for MPSE. With the expected reduction in equipment size, MPSE can support deployment
directly at industrial sites and near distributed resource streams, reducing plant footprint,
shortening residence time, and enabling more modular processing configurations.

 A further important benefit is improved economic performance. Techno-economic
analysis for a 100 t/year Li2CO3 case indicates that replacing a conventional mixer-settler
contactor train with MPSE could reduce contactor CAPEX from approximately $6.08 million to
$3.01 million, increase project NPV from approximately $4.2 million to $7.2-7.8 million,
improve IRR from approximately 17% to 45-55%, and shorten breakeven from approximately
5.8 years to 2-2.5 years. Project planning also indicates a shorter deployment pathway, with
roughly one year of shop fabrication plus site preparation rather than roughly two years of field
buildout for conventional systems. These gains are especially relevant to lithium projects where
high capital intensity and long time-to-deployment can delay adoption. A recent review of
lithium extraction technologies also suggests that solvent extraction can remain economically
competitive with other lithium-recovery approaches, with one source reporting indicative costs
of roughly $2800/ton Li2CO3 for solvent extraction, compared with approximately $3000 for
membranes, $4500 for electrochemical approaches, and $7000 for adsorption, under that
source’s assumptions [122]. MPSE’s objective is to improve the economics of the solvent-
extraction block within these broader lithium flowsheets while enabling staged CAPEX rather
than a single large buildout.

 From an environmental and safety standpoint, MPSE is expected to provide meaningful
benefits through reduced solvent inventory and lower holdup volume. Measurements show a
total setup volume of less than 100 mL for MPSE, compared with approximately 0.50-7.10 L
reported for small mixer-settler systems [121]. Lower solvent holdup can reduce solvent losses,
leak and emissions risk, startup and shutdown waste, and the volume of hazardous organic
solvent present in the system at any time. These benefits are reinforced by MPSE’s monolithic
seal-less architecture, which avoids membranes, dynamic seals, and rotating hardware, thereby
reducing common sources of leakage, component failure, and maintenance-driven waste. The
development of tailored ionic-liquid solvent systems adds a further environmental benefit, as
these systems are intended to reduce solvent losses and offer near-zero vapor pressure, reducing
volatile emissions and reagent loss.

 Another key expected benefit is improved operability on difficult real-world feeds.
Published work on state-of-the-art microseparators shows that separation quality can deteriorate
under highly dispersed flow conditions and with viscous fluids [40,66,94]. Membrane-based
liquid-liquid separators are also reported to be vulnerable to particulates in real feeds, often
requiring membrane replacement on timescales ranging from days to weeks [117]. In contrast,
MPSE retains strong separation under dispersed flow regimes and with highly viscous or impure
feeds, including synthetic high Mg/Li brines and produced water. This is a significant

 75

commercial and environmental advantage because many lithium and critical-minerals streams
are variable, impurity-rich, and operationally challenging. A contactor architecture that can
maintain performance under such conditions may widen the range of resources that are
economically processable and reduce process instability, pretreatment burden, and waste
generation in industrial operation.

 MPSE may also create broader system-level environmental and climate benefits for
lithium and rare earth processing. For lithium, many direct lithium extraction and polishing
flowsheets still require downstream impurity control and purification, where solvent extraction
remains relevant [122]. MPSE’s value is therefore not necessarily to replace every non-solvent-
extraction lithium technology, but rather to make the solvent-extraction block smaller, lower-
energy, faster, and easier to deploy. For rare earth elements, solvent extraction is widely
recognized as the dominant industrial separation method, typically implemented through large
multi-stage extraction trains using mixer-settlers and, in some cases, columns [123,124]. A
faster, lower-holdup, modular contactor architecture such as MPSE could therefore create value
without requiring a change in the underlying chemistry or separation logic of REE processing.

 Beyond direct process intensification, MPSE may also reduce lifecycle emissions by
enabling greater utilization of lower-footprint brines and waste-derived feedstocks relative to
conventional hard-rock supply. If used to recover lithium from geothermal brines, produced
water, and selected waste or tailings streams, MPSE could support pathways with around half the
CO2 intensity of today’s hard-rock supply. In a long-range climate scenario, if MPSE were to
enable roughly 30% of global demand across relevant critical-mineral supply chains by 2050, it
could yield on the order of 20-30 MtCO2e/year in direct upstream savings and approximately 0.5
GtCO2e/year in system-level mitigation by reducing bottlenecks for lower-footprint lithium and
rare earth supply used in EV batteries and permanent-magnet wind turbines. These are scenario-
based estimates rather than direct measured device-level outcomes, but they illustrate the broader
climate value of lowering the energy and infrastructure intensity of solvent extraction while
unlocking more distributed, lower-carbon resource pathways.

 Additional environmental benefits may arise from MPSE’s monolithic, single-material,
additively manufactured construction. MPSE has a straightforward pathway to end-of-life
recycling through regrind or remelt, unlike more complex multi-material assemblies that
incorporate bonded alloys, elastomers, membranes, and rotating components. Combined with
modular deployment near waste-generation or resource sites, this design could reduce
transportation burden, improve circular recovery from produced water, geothermal brines,
industrial wastewater, mine tailings, and recycling leachates, and lower the overall
environmental burden associated with critical-minerals separations.

 Overall, the expected benefits of MPSE extend beyond direct process intensification.
Commercial deployment could reduce energy use, indirect emissions, solvent inventory, plant
footprint, and capital cost while improving deployment speed, operability, reliability, and access
to lower-carbon resource pathways. These attributes are particularly relevant to DOE priorities in
domestic critical-mineral supply chains, where technologies that enable smaller, more modular,
and more efficient lithium and rare-earth separations could support wider deployment of
domestic refining capacity, increased circular recovery from waste streams, and improved
processing of unconventional resources.

 Commercialization
 The primary technology transfer pathway for MPSE is commercialization through Vigsur
Dynamics Inc., which was formed in January 2026 to advance the MPSE into a deployable
modular solvent extraction platform for lithium and other critical minerals separations. Vigsur
has treated commercialization as a structured parallel effort alongside technical development,
rather than as a future step after research completion. The company has already engaged in
accelerator and commercialization programming, including participation in Oregon State
University accelerator and AID Fund-related activities, and has conducted over 30 customer
discovery interviews with relevant stakeholders across critical minerals, separations, and
industrial processing. These efforts have been used to refine the commercial use case, target
markets, value proposition, and adoption pathway for MPSE.

 Vigsur’s commercialization model is staged to align with the company’s technical
maturity, organizational capabilities, and market-entry pathway. In the near term, Vigsur’s
revenue model is expected to center on paid pilot, demonstration, and process-development
engagements that validate MPSE on customer-relevant feedstocks and generate the technical and
economic data needed for commercial deployment. As the technology matures, Vigsur expects to
transition to modular hardware deployment through equipment sales, supported installations, and
selective lease or service-based arrangements that preserve recurring revenue and maintain close
integration with customer operations. Over the longer term, Vigsur’s preferred strategic model is
build-own-operate separation services for selected applications, where the company can capture
greater value through direct operation of MPSE-based systems rather than only selling
equipment. This staged progression is intended to reduce adoption risk, build commercial
credibility, preserve strategic control over process know-how, and create continuity from early
validation to scalable recurring revenue. This approach is also consistent with the company’s
broader commercialization materials, which already emphasize pilot validation, hardware
deployment, service and maintenance, EPC-linked channels, and longer-term recurring value
streams.

 Current commercialization status is early but active and credible. MPSE has
demonstrated lithium solvent extraction on high Mg/Li synthetic brines and produced water,
shown energy use substantially below incumbent solvent extraction systems, achieved more than
100 hours of continuous operation in representative liquid-liquid separation duty, and
demonstrated preliminary numbering-up feasibility through a 6-unit module with approximately
±5% flow uniformity. A preliminary techno-economic analysis has also been completed,
showing the potential for significant reductions in contactor capital cost and strong improvement
in project economics relative to conventional mixer-settler systems. In parallel, the intellectual
property position is being advanced and licensing is in process with Oregon State and the
University of Pittsburgh. Together, these efforts establish a strong foundation for pilot-scale de-
risking and commercial engagement.

CHANGE OF MPSE ARCHITECTURE — Pin-Plates vs. Inclined Plates
 Continuation of the Project beyond the Go/No-Go Milestone confronted the well-
documented challenges of the immediate post-pandemic period — namely, a severely disrupted
supply chain. The manufacturing vendor specializing in sinker-EDM micromachining
unexpectedly extended delivery times for a series of MPSE devices by nine months, while
simultaneously raising costs substantially as the vendor struggled to rebuild its skilled labor
force. Faced with these time and cost pressures, and in agreement with the DOE project
management team, the project team elected to explore novel MPSE design solutions. This effort
yielded several significant and unexpected breakthroughs:
 • Drawing on the same fundamental principles (Young-Laplace Law) that underpinned the
 original design, the team developed a novel architectural variant of the MPSE — the
 Inclined-Plate design. This design is substantially simpler to manufacture, as the
 microscale pin features are entirely eliminated.
 • By replacing microscale pin features with inclined plate assemblies, the manufacturing
 approach was shifted from sinker-EDM machining in stainless steel to 3D printing in
 polymers.
 • The practical impacts of these changes proved exceptionally valuable for both the design
 process and the project's commercialization prospects:
 • Manufacturing cost decreased dramatically by approximately a factor of 20 — confirmed
 by the project's Techno-Economic Analysis (TEA) as the single most impactful factor in
 reducing capital investment.

 80

 • Scale-up of the MPSE process to industrial nominal capacities became simpler and
 harmonious with the numbering-up concept.
 • The cycle time to manufacture a single research prototype dropped from several months
 to 24 hours.
 • Additive manufacturing (3D printing) was available directly in the team's own laboratory,
 eliminating dependence on external vendors and removing supply chain constraints
 entirely.
 The functionality, manufacturability, and performance of the newly conceived MPSE
architecture were, again, experimentally confirmed under the same operating conditions used in
the first phase of the Project.
 PRODUCED WATER SAMPLES VARIABILITY — Representative Produced Water
Composition
 In the second phase of the Project, MPSE performance was evaluated against field
samples of produced waters. This task was challenged with variability issues in field sample
quality. Samples originating from the same field did not consistently show the same or similar
compositions. Even standardized storage conditions for nominally identical samples introduced
substantial variation in operating conditions, thus making reproducible extraction and phase
separation difficult to achieve. Following consultation with the DOE PD team, it was determined
that a stable, field-representative sample composition must be defined and used throughout the
remainder of the Project.
 By reviewing a database of several hundred U.S. Geological Survey analyses of produced
waters and selecting 200 surveys reporting lithium concentrations of at least 200 ppm, the team
defined a statistically representative real-field sample composition, which was then held constant
for the duration of the Project. Using this stable reference composition, all project Goals and
SMART Milestones were successfully met. The solvent was 1M D2EHPA in kerosene for the
first stage and 1.5M D2EHPA, 0.3M TBP in kerosene for the second stage, both at 1:1 flow
ratios.
 Stabilizing the variability of field samples by defining a statistically representative real-
field sample composition was the most important lesson learned from the Project. Satisfactory
MPSE performance — and, by extension, favorable TEA outcomes — can only be achieved by
carefully tuning a set of interdependent MPSE operating parameters.

CHARACTERISTIC TIMES (CT)
 These tunable operating parameters are collectively defined as process Characteristic
Times (CT). The concept and role of Characteristic Times in Process Intensification (PI) were
published by team members shortly before the start of the Project.[2,3] The principal
Characteristic Times available for tuning MPSE operation are listed below:
 • tmrtƒx - axial mean residence time of the feed stream [s]
 • tmrtsx - axial mean residence time of the solvent stream [s]
 • tldtsy - lateral-drift time of a solvent droplet [s]

 81

 • tex85% - solute extraction time to 85% of technical equilibrium [s]
 • tDƒ - solute diffusion time in the feed phase [s]
 • tDss - solute diffusion time in the solvent phase [s]
 Characteristic Times serve as the primary tool in Process Intensification, evaluated
against an appropriate objective function.

COMMERCIALIZATION
 The overall success of the Project was underpinned by two principal factors:
 Process Intensification, arising from the inherently microscale-based architecture of the
MPSE device, which guarantees intensification of all transport phenomena. Microscale-based
unit operations universally improve transport phenomena by a factor of 10×–100× relative to
conventional macroscale designs.
 Change of MPSE architecture from Pin-Plates to Inclined Plates. Although this change
was originally driven by external supply chain constraints, it proved pivotal in reducing
manufacturing cost and improving the scalability of MPSE devices. While the operating
efficiency of the Inclined-Plate MPSE is comparable to that of the equivalent Pin-Plate device,
the shift in manufacturing approach dramatically reduced both cost and production time. The
team's deep understanding of extraction process fundamentals and multiphase microfluidic
dynamics — rooted in interfacial transport phenomena on curved surfaces — was central to
conceiving a novel MPSE design that successfully designed-out the most difficult and costly
manufacturing features.
 This reduction in manufacturing cost has enabled the team to take decisive steps toward
commercialization of an end-to-end processing module for the extraction and recovery of critical
materials based on MPSE technology. At the time of writing, the team has:
 i. submitted a patent application for the novel MPSE design;
 ii. applied for grants to support development and fabrication of the end-to-end
 processing module; and
 iii. initiated the process of forming a start-up company based on OSU–U of Pitt.
 intellectual property.

 Recommendations
 Different aspects of the MPSE extraction & phase separation technology reached (at
minimum) the TRL-4 level. More importantly, the technology appears to have characteristics of
platform technology, appropriate for implementation in most of CM extractions and separations
applications. The wide MPSE application capabilities emerged from the exquisite knowledge of
fundamentals governing the extraction and separation processes. The design features of the
MPSE associated with architecture, materials, manufacturing methods, etc..., provided variety of
“control knobs” that could be easily used to adjust the performance of an MPSE device. Having
these capabilities, and Digital Twin software turned into a design tool, makes the MPSE ready

 82

for the development/demonstration at the TRL-6.
 We therefore recommend extending the MPSE technology to the TRL-6 level, including
integration with the complementary operations characteristic for the entire CM extraction &
separation process system.
 The TEA work (end-to-end process analysis) within this project already points to a
direction of development & demonstration of the MPSE technology. We believe that the
development of a modular demonstration unit (TRL-6) is the right and appropriate next step in
integration of the MPSE into CM extraction & separation applications. We have performed an
initial Environmental and Life Cycle Analysis of an CM recovery application and, to our
surprise, discovered that a Zero-Waste-Water-Discharge concept would yield a modular plant
design, not only responsive to environmental considerations, but also becoming promoter of an
enhanced commercial success. Clean water recovery in an CM recovery process is a new
paradigm in decarbonization of the extractive industry.
