Abstract
. P. Di Francesco first introduced the “Aztec triangle” in his study of the relationship between the twenty-vertex model and domino tilings. He conjectured an exact formula for the number of tilings of the Aztec triangle, and it has since been proved by several authors. In an attempt to prove the conjecture, M. Ciucu showed that the tiling number of the Aztec triangle divides the tiling number of a new region called the “cruciform region,” a superposition of two Aztec rectangles. Ciucu proved that the number of domino tilings of a cruciform region is given by a simple product formula. In this paper, we generalize Ciucu’s tiling formula by providing a generating-function formula for the cruciform region.

2.1.
Local Graphical Transformations

Consider an infinite lattice on the plane, such as the square lattice or the triangular lattice. The
fundamental regions
in the square lattice and the triangular lattice are the unit squares and the equilateral triangles of side length

1
, respectively; we call the latter
unit triangles
. The union of any two fundamental regions sharing an edge is called a
tile
, for example, the domino in the square lattice and the unit rhombus (or unit lozenge) in the triangular lattice. A
tiling
of a lattice region is a way to cover the region by tiles so that there are no gaps or overlaps. In the case of the square lattice, we call the tilings the
domino tilings
, and the
lozenge tilings
in the case of the triangular lattice. See Jim Propp’s survey papers
18
;
20
for more aspects of tilings. Each tile can carry a
weight
, and the weight of a tiling is defined to be the product of the weights of its tiles.
We denote by
\operatorname{T}(\mathcal{R})
the sum of weights of all tilings of the region
\mathcal{R}
. If
\mathcal{R}
does not accept any tiling, we set
\operatorname{T}(\mathcal{R})=0
. In the unweighted case, i.e., each tiling has weight
1
,
\operatorname{T}(\mathcal{R})
is exactly the number of tilings of
\mathcal{R}
.

The
(planar) dual graph

G
of a region
\mathcal{R}
is the graph whose vertices are the fundamental regions in
R
and whose edges connect exactly two fundamental regions sharing an edge. For example, the dual graph of the cruciform region
\mathcal{C}_{9,6}^{3,4,5,2}
in Figure
1.1
is shown in Figure
2.1
. In this paper, we always consider the
45^{\circ}
-rotated version of the dual graph of a cruciform region. We denote by
C_{m,n}^{a,b,c,d}
the dual graph of the cruciform region
\mathcal{C}_{m,n}^{a,b,c,d}
, and we call
C_{m,n}^{a,b,c,d}
a
cruciform graph
. A
(perfect) matching
in a graph is a collection of vertex-disjoint edges that covers all vertices of the graph. If the edges of
G
carry weight, we define the weight of a matching to be the product of the weights of all its edges. Then
\operatorname{M}(G)
denotes the sum of weights of all perfect matchings in
G
. There is a natural weight-preserving bijection between the tilings of a region
\mathcal{R}
and the perfect matchings of its dual graph
G
, i.e.,
\operatorname{M}(G)=\operatorname{T}(\mathcal{R})
. In certain cases, it is convenient to enumerate the matchings of the dual graph rather than the tilings of the region directly.
