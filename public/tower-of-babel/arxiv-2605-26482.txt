Sophie Zhu

Abstract

1.
Introduction

The objective of this paper is to understand the closure of the image of divisor functions. For a complex number
r
, the
divisor function

\sigma_{r}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is defined by

\sigma_{r}(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}d^{-r},

where
\mathbb{N}=\mathbb{Z}_{>0}.

Since the mid-1980s, various authors have investigated the image
\sigma_{r}(\mathbb{N}).

In 1986, Laatsch showed that
\sigma_{1}(\mathbb{N})
is dense in
[1,\infty)

[
5
]
. In 2000, Weiner asked what can be said about
\overline{\sigma_{r}(\mathbb{N})},
which is the closure of the image of
\sigma_{r}

[
7
]
. To this, in 2015 and 2016, Defant determined various fundamental topological properties of
\overline{\sigma_{r}(\mathbb{N})}.
In particular, he showed that
\overline{\sigma_{r}(\mathbb{N})}
is connected for
r\in\mathbb{R}
if and only if
r\in(0,\eta],
where
\eta
is the unique real number satisfying

\frac{2^{\eta}}{2^{\eta}-1}\frac{3^{\eta}+1}{3^{\eta}-1}=\zeta(\eta)

and
\zeta
is the Riemann zeta function
[
2
]
.
In 2017, Sanna proved that when
r>1,
the number
C_{r}
is finite, giving an algorithm to compute
\overline{\sigma_{r}(\mathbb{N})}

[
6
]
, and Defant showed that
C_{r}
is finite when
\Re(r)>1

[
3
]
.
In 2018, Zubrilina gave explicit lower and upper bounds on
C_{r}

[
8
]
. In particular, she gave a lower bound of order
\pi(r),
where
\pi(r)
is the number of primes less than or equal to
r
, and an upper bound that is exponential in
r
. Additionally, she showed that
C_{r}
does not assume all positive integral values.

In this paper, we introduce a generalized version of the divisor function, defined as follows, and study its properties.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{C},
and let
K
be a finite extension of
\mathbb{Q}
. Let
R:=\mathscr{O}_{K}
be the ring of integers of
K
, and let
I_{K}
be the set of integral ideals in
\mathscr{O}_{K}
. Let
\chi
be a Dirichlet character.
Then define the
ideal divisor function on
K
twisted by
\chi
as the function
\sigma_{r,K,\chi}:I_{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
by

\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(I)=\sum_{J\mid I}N(J)^{-r}\chi(N(J)),

where
N
denotes the ideal norm.
Define
C_{r,K,\chi}
to be the number of connected components of
\overline{\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(I_{K})}.

We now establish the notation we use throughout the paper. We fix a real number
r>1
and a finite extension
K
of
\mathbb{Q}.
For
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, let
\pi(x)
denote the number of primes less than or equal to
n
and
\pi_{m}(x)
denote the number of primes less than or equal to
n
that do not divide
m
. Denote the trivial character by
\chi_{0}
; note that the function
\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{0}}
is precisely
\sigma_{r}.
In Sections
5
and
6
, we use
C_{r,K}
to denote
C_{r,K,\chi_{0}}.
In addition, we say a character
\chi
is
real
if it assumes values in
\{-1,0,1\}.

This paper is structured as follows.
In Section
2
, we prove that
C_{r,K,\chi}
is finite, extending Sanna’s proof that
C_{r}
is finite in
[
6
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
r>1
be a real number. let
K
be a finite extension of
\mathbb{Q},
and let
\chi
be a real character. Then
C_{r,K,\chi}
is finite.

In Section
3
, we prove that when
r
is fixed and as
\chi
varies,
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}
assumes all sufficiently large integer values; that is,

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
r>1
be a real number. There exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq N}\subseteq\{C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}:\chi\text{ is a character}\}.

In particular, we show that when
r
is large enough,
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}
assumes all positive integral values.

Theorem 1.4
.

There exists a positive real number
r_{0}
such that for any
r>r_{0},
we have
\{C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}:\chi\text{ is a character}\}=\mathbb{N}.

In Section
4
, we give a lower bound on
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}
that improves upon the lower bound given by Achenjang and Berger in
[
1
, Theorem 5.2]
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
r\geq 3,
and let
\chi
be a character of modulus
m
. Then
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}\geq 2^{\pi_{m}(2r-2)-\delta_{m}},
where
\delta_{m}
equals 0 if
m
is even and equals 1 otherwise.

Generalizing this result, in Section
5
, we give a lower bound on
C_{r,K}
for finite Galois extensions
K
of
\mathbb{Q}
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
K/\mathbb{Q}
be a finite Galois extension. For every
\varepsilon>0,
there exist constants
\mu_{K,\varepsilon},r_{K,\varepsilon}>0
such that if
r>r_{K,\varepsilon},
then

\log C_{r,K}>\mu_{K,\varepsilon}\left(\frac{r^{\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}}}{\log r}\right).

This result implies that fixing
K
, we have
\sup_{r>1}C_{r,K}=\infty.
As a complement to this result, in Section
6
, we instead fix
s=[K:\mathbb{Q}]
and
r
and understand how
C_{r,K}
behaves as
K
changes.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
r>1
be a real number and
s\geq 2
be an integer. Then

\sup_{\text{degree-}s\text{ extensions }K/\mathbb{Q}}C_{r,K}=\infty.

We briefly illustrate some rough intuition for our results.
Below are the plots of the image of the divisor function
\sigma_{2,\mathbb{Q},\chi}
on the set
\{1,2,\dots,10^{6}\},
where
\chi
is the principal character
\chi_{1}
mod 1 (the constant character) and
\chi_{5}
mod 5, respectively.

Figure 1.
The image
\sigma_{2,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{1}}(\{1,2,\dots,10^{6}\}).

Figure 2.
The image
\sigma_{2,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{5}}(\{1,2,\dots,10^{6}\}).

As we can reasonably guess from these two figures, the numbers of connected components
C_{2,\mathbb{Q},\chi}
are finite. Indeed, they both have 3 connected components; let
I_{1,1},I_{1,2},
and
I_{1,3}
be the connected components for
\chi_{1}
, and let
I_{5,1},I_{5,2},
and
I_{5,3}
be the connected components for
\chi_{5}.
We notice that

\displaystyle I_{i,1}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{2,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{i}}(\{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}:2\nmid n,3\nmid n\})

\displaystyle I_{i,2}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{2,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{i}}(\{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}:2\nmid n,3\mid n\})

\displaystyle I_{i,3}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{2,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{i}}(\{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}:2\mid n\})

for

In this paper, we will prove this observation that these connected components are determined by the divisibility of the input integer by primes and use it to deduce results about

in general.

2.
Finiteness of
C_{r,K,\chi}
for
r>1
and real
\chi

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.2
.

Throughout this section, let
\chi
be a real character with modulus
m
.
Order the prime ideals in
\mathscr{O}_{K}
by increasing ideal norm; arbitrarily order prime ideals of the same norm. Denote the
i
-th prime ideal in this ordering by
\mathfrak{p}_{i}.

Let
S_{m}
be the finite set
\{i\in\mathbb{N}:\gcd(N(\mathfrak{p}_{i}),m)\neq 1\}
of indices of prime ideals whose norm are not coprime to
m
. Let
\mathscr{N}_{j}:=\{I\in I_{K}:\text{for all }1\leq i\leq j,\mathfrak{p}_{i}\nmid I\}
denote the set of ideals whose prime ideal factors have index larger than
j
.

To prove the theorem, we prove a series of useful lemmas.

The following lemma establishes a density of norms of certain prime ideals in
\mathscr{O}_{K}
. Note that it holds for any choice of character
\chi
with modulus
m
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Consider the prime ideals
\mathfrak{p}
in
\mathscr{O}_{K}
such that
\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}))=1
, and let
\{\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence of these ideals once ordered by increasing ideal norm. Then

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{N(\mathfrak{p}_{i+1}^{\prime})}{N(\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{\prime})}=1.

Proof.

Because there are finitely many prime ideals in
K
that ramify in
K(\zeta_{m}),
it is sufficient to prove the lemma for the sequence
\{\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of prime ideals
\mathfrak{p}
in
K
that are unramified in
K(\zeta_{m})
, satisfy
\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}))=1,
and are ordered by non-decreasing ideal norm
N
.

Let
F=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})
and
L=K(\zeta_{m}).

Consider the isomorphism

\displaystyle\text{res}:\text{Gal}(K(\zeta_{m})/K)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\text{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})/\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})\cap K).

Viewing
\text{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})/\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})\cap K)
as a subgroup of
\text{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})/\mathbb{Q})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times},
the Dirichlet character
\chi
restricts to a character

\tilde{\chi}:=\chi\circ\text{res}

of
\text{Gal}(K(\zeta_{m})/K).

For any prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
in
K
that is unramified in
K(\zeta_{m})/K
and lies over
p\mathbb{Z},
the map
res
sends
\operatorname{Frob}_{\mathfrak{p},K(\zeta_{m})/K}
to

\operatorname{Frob}_{p,\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})/\mathbb{Q}}^{f_{p}}\in\text{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})/\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})\cap K),

where
f_{p}
is the inertia degree of
\mathfrak{p}
over
p\mathbb{Z}.

Now, let
H:=\ker\tilde{\chi}
, which is a nonempty subgroup of
\text{Gal}(K(\zeta_{m})/K).
For any
\sigma\in H,
consider the prime ideals
\mathfrak{p}
in
K
that are unramified in
K(\zeta_{m})
and satisfy
\operatorname{Frob}_{\mathfrak{p},K(\zeta_{m})/K}=\sigma.
Ordering these prime ideals by non-decreasing ideal norm
N
, we denote the resulting sequence by
\{\mathfrak{p}_{\sigma,i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}.
Because
\tilde{\chi}(\operatorname{Frob}_{\mathfrak{p},K(\zeta_{m})/K})=\chi(N(\mathfrak{p})),
we see that

\{\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}=\bigsqcup_{\sigma\in H}\{\mathfrak{p}_{\sigma,i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}.

By Chebotarev’s density theorem, we have

\#\{\mathfrak{p}_{\sigma,i}:N(\mathfrak{p}_{\sigma,i})\leq X\}\sim c_{\sigma}\frac{X}{\log X}

for some constant
c_{\sigma}>0,
so

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{N(\mathfrak{p}_{\sigma,i+1})}{N(\mathfrak{p}_{\sigma,i})}=1.

As this holds for each
\sigma
in the finite set
H
, it follows that

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{N(\mathfrak{p}_{i+1}^{\prime})}{N(\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{\prime})}=1.

∎

Definition 2.2
.

For
r>1
, a nonnegative integer
i
, and a finite extension
K/\mathbb{Q},
define

\zeta_{K,i}(r)=\prod_{k>i}\frac{1}{1-N(\mathfrak{p}_{k})^{-r}}.

For a positive integer
m
, further define

\zeta_{K,i,m}(r)=\prod_{k>i;k\not\in S_{m}}\frac{1}{1-N(\mathfrak{p}_{k})^{-r}}.

Let
j_{+}=\min\{j\in\mathbb{N}\setminus S_{m}:\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}_{j}))=1\}.

For each integer
j\geq j_{+}
, let
\mathfrak{q}_{j}
be a prime ideal with the largest norm less than or equal to
N(\mathfrak{p}_{j})
such that
\chi(N(\mathfrak{q}_{j}))=1.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
6
, Lemma 2.2]
)
.

There exist only finitely many
j\in\mathbb{N}\setminus S_{m}
satisfying
j\geq j_{+}
such that

\zeta_{K,j,m}(r)<1+N(\mathfrak{q}_{j})^{-r}.

Proof.

Consider the sequence
(y_{j})_{j\geq j_{+}}
defined by

y_{j}:=\frac{\zeta_{K,j,m}(r)}{1+N(\mathfrak{q}_{j})^{-r}}.

For any
j\geq j_{+},
if
j+1\in S_{m}
, then
y_{j+1}=y_{j}.
If
j+1\not\in S_{m},
then we compute

\frac{y_{j+1}}{y_{j}}=(1+N(\mathfrak{q}_{j})^{-r})\cdot\frac{1-N(\mathfrak{p}_{j+1})^{-r}}{1+N(\mathfrak{q}_{j+1})^{-r}}<(1+N(\mathfrak{q}_{j})^{-r})\cdot\frac{1-N(\mathfrak{p}_{j+1})^{-r}}{1+N(\mathfrak{p}_{j+1})^{-r}},

because
N(\mathfrak{q}_{j+1})\leq N(\mathfrak{p}_{j+1}).
Now, let
\mathfrak{Q}_{j}
be a prime ideal
\mathfrak{Q}
with the least norm greater than
N(\mathfrak{q}_{j})
such that
\chi(N(\mathfrak{Q}))=1.
Then
N(\mathfrak{Q})\geq N(\mathfrak{p}_{j+1}).
The function from
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{R}
defined by
x\mapsto\frac{1-x}{1+x}
is decreasing, so

\frac{y_{j+1}}{y_{j}}<(1+N(\mathfrak{q}_{j})^{-r})\cdot\frac{1-N(\mathfrak{p}_{j+1})^{-r}}{1+N(\mathfrak{p}_{j+1})^{-r}}<(1+N(\mathfrak{q}_{j})^{-r})\cdot\frac{1-N(\mathfrak{Q}_{j})^{-r}}{1+N(\mathfrak{Q}_{j})^{-r}}.

By Lemma
2.1
, we have

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\frac{N(\mathfrak{Q}_{j})}{N(\mathfrak{q}_{j})}=1.

Then there exists
j_{*}
such that if
j>j_{*},
then
\frac{N(\mathfrak{Q}_{j})}{N(\mathfrak{q}_{j})}<2^{1/r}
and thus that
N(\mathfrak{Q}_{j})^{-r}>(2N(\mathfrak{q}_{j})^{r}+1)^{-1}.
As a result, for
j>j_{*},
we find

\frac{y_{j+1}}{y_{j}}<(1+N(\mathfrak{q}_{j})^{-r})\cdot\frac{1-(2N(\mathfrak{q}_{j})^{r}+1)^{-1}}{1+(2N(\mathfrak{q}_{j})^{r}+1)^{-1}}=1.

Therefore, the sequence
\{y_{j}\}_{j\geq j_{+}}
is eventually non-increasing. Yet
\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}y_{j}=1,
so
y_{j}\geq 1
for all
j>j_{*}.
The lemma’s statement follows.
∎

The above lemma allows us to make the following definition.

Definition 2.4
.

Define
j_{K,\chi}(r)
to be the least element
j_{0}
of
\mathbb{N}\setminus S_{m}
such that
j_{0}\geq j_{+}
,

(1)

for all
j\in\mathbb{N}\setminus S_{m}
with
j>j_{0},
we have
\zeta_{K,j,m}(r)\geq 1+N(\mathfrak{q}_{j})^{-r},
and

(2)

for all
j>j_{0}
with
\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}_{j}))=-1,
we have

\frac{1}{1-N(\mathfrak{p}_{j})^{-r}}<1+N(\mathfrak{q}_{j})^{-r}.

We note that such an element
j_{K,\chi}(r)
exists. By Lemma
2.3
, sufficiently large
j
satisfy condition (1). When
\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}_{j}))=-1,
we have
N(\mathfrak{q}_{j})<N(\mathfrak{p}_{j}),
and the inequality in the second bulleted condition is equivalent to
N(\mathfrak{q}_{j})^{r}+1<N(\mathfrak{p}_{j})^{r},
which holds for sufficiently large
j
.

For a prime ideal
\mathfrak{p},
define

\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathfrak{p}^{\infty})=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathfrak{p}^{i})=\frac{1}{1-\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}))N(\mathfrak{p})^{-r}}.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
6
, Lemma 2.3]
)
.

For
r>1,
we have

\overline{\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathscr{N}_{j_{K,\chi}(r)})}=\left[\prod_{k>j_{K,\chi}(r);\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}_{k}))=-1}1-N(\mathfrak{p}_{k})^{-r},\prod_{k>j_{K,\chi}(r);\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}_{k}))=1}\frac{1}{1-N(\mathfrak{p}_{k})^{-r}}\right].

Proof.

Denote the interval above by
[c,d].

Take
x\in[c,d].
We define a sequence
(x_{j})_{j\geq j_{K,\chi}(r)}
as follows. Let
x_{j_{K,\chi}(r)}=c.
For any
j>j_{K,\chi}(r),
we define
x_{j}
using the following process.
If
\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}_{j}))=0,
then set
x_{j}=x_{j-1}.

If
\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}_{j}))=1,
then let
a_{j}
be the largest nonnegative integer
a
(including
\infty
) such that
x_{j-1}\cdot\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathfrak{p}_{j}^{a})\leq x,
and let

x_{j}=x_{j-1}\cdot\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathfrak{p}_{j}^{a_{j}}).

If
\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}_{j}))=-1,
then if
x_{j-1}\cdot\frac{1}{1-N(\mathfrak{p}_{j})^{-r}}\leq x
, let
b_{j}=1
, and let

x_{j}=x_{j-1}\cdot\frac{1}{1-N(\mathfrak{p}_{j})^{-r}}.

Otherwise, let
b_{j}=0
, and let
x_{j}=x_{j-1}.
Since the sequence
(x_{j})_{j\geq j_{K,\chi}(r)}
is non-decreasing and bounded, it converges, and let its limit be
\ell.
Note that
\ell\leq x
.
If there are infinitely many
j>j_{K,\chi}(r)
with
\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}_{j}))=1
and
a_{j}<\infty
, then
x<x_{j-1}\cdot\frac{1}{1-N(\mathfrak{p}_{j})^{-r}}
for infinitely many
j>j_{K,\chi}(r),
so
x\leq\ell
and thus
x=\ell.

If there are infinitely many
j>j_{K,\chi}(r)
with
\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}_{j}))=-1
and
b_{j}=0
, then a similar argument gives
x=\ell.
Otherwise, there must only be finitely many
j>j_{K,\chi}(r)
for which
a_{j}<\infty
and only finitely many
j>j_{K,\chi}(r)
for which
b_{j}=0.

Let
j^{\prime}
be the least integer greater than equal to
j_{K,\chi}(r)
such that for each
j>j^{\prime}

with
j\not\in S_{m},
the following two conditions hold: when
\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}_{j}))=1,
we have
a_{j}=\infty,
and when
\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}_{j}))=-1
, we have
b_{j}=1.
Then for any
j>j^{\prime},
we have

x_{j}=x_{j^{\prime}}\cdot\prod_{j^{\prime}<k\leq j;k\not\in S_{m}}\frac{1}{1-N(\mathfrak{p}_{k})^{-r}}

and thus that

\ell=x_{j^{\prime}}\cdot\prod_{k>j^{\prime};k\not\in S_{m}}\frac{1}{1-N(\mathfrak{p}_{k})^{-r}}.

If
j^{\prime}=j_{K,\chi}(r),
then

\ell=c\cdot\prod_{k>j_{K,\chi}(r);k\not\in S_{m}}\frac{1}{1-N(\mathfrak{p}_{k})^{-r}}=d

and
\ell\leq x\leq d,
so
\ell=x.

Otherwise, if
j^{\prime}>j_{K,\chi}(r),
then by the minimality of
j^{\prime}
, we have
j^{\prime}\not\in S_{m}.
Thus, either
\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}_{j^{\prime}}))=1
and
a_{j^{\prime}}<\infty,
or
\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}_{j^{\prime}}))=-1
and
b_{j^{\prime}}=0.

If
a_{j^{\prime}}<\infty,
then

\displaystyle x_{j^{\prime}}\cdot\prod_{k>j^{\prime};k\not\in S_{m}}\frac{1}{1-N(\mathfrak{p}_{k})^{-r}}=\ell\leq x

\displaystyle<x_{j^{\prime}-1}\cdot\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathfrak{p}_{j^{\prime}}^{a_{j^{\prime}}+1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{x_{j^{\prime}}}{\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathfrak{p}_{j^{\prime}}^{a_{j^{\prime}}})}\cdot\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathfrak{p}_{j^{\prime}}^{a_{j^{\prime}}+1})

\displaystyle\leq x_{j^{\prime}}(1+N(\mathfrak{p}_{j^{\prime}})^{-r})

\displaystyle=x_{j^{\prime}}(1+N(\mathfrak{q}_{j^{\prime}})^{-r}),

where the last equality holds because
\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}_{j^{\prime}}))=1.
This contradicts condition (1) in the definition of
j_{K,\chi}(r).
Similarly, if
b_{j^{\prime}}=0,
then

\displaystyle x_{j^{\prime}}\cdot\prod_{k>j^{\prime};k\not\in S_{m}}\frac{1}{1-N(\mathfrak{p}_{k})^{-r}}=\ell\leq x

\displaystyle<x_{j^{\prime}-1}\cdot\frac{1}{1-N(\mathfrak{p}_{j^{\prime}})^{-r}}

\displaystyle=x_{j^{\prime}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-N(\mathfrak{p}_{j^{\prime}})^{-r}}

\displaystyle<x_{j^{\prime}}(1+N(\mathfrak{q}_{j^{\prime}})^{-r}),

where the last inequality holds due to the condition (2) in the definition of
j_{K,\chi}(r).
This contradicts condition (1) in the definition of
j_{K,\chi}(r)\leq j^{\prime}.
Hence,
j^{\prime}=j_{K,\chi}(r),
so
x=\ell.

∎

Lemma 2.6

(
[
6
, Lemma 2.4]
)
.

For any
1\leq j\leq j_{K,\chi}(r),

\overline{\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathscr{N}_{j-1})}=\bigcup_{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}}\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathfrak{p}_{j}^{a})\cdot\overline{\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathscr{N}_{j})}.

Proof.

It is clear that the right-hand side is contained in the left-hand side. To see the reverse containment, take
x\in\overline{\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathscr{N}_{j-1})}.
Then there exists a sequence
\{I_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathscr{N}_{j-1}
such that
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(I_{i})=x.
For each
i>0,
let
a_{i}
be the largest power of
\mathfrak{p}_{j}
dividing
I_{i}.
If
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded, then there exists a nonnegative integer
a_{0}
that is attained infinitely many times in this sequence; let
\{i_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
be the corresponding subindices. Then

\frac{x}{\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathfrak{p}_{j}^{a_{0}})}=\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{r,K,\chi}\left(I_{i_{\ell}}\mathfrak{p}_{j}^{-a_{0}}\right)\in\overline{\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathscr{N}_{j})}.

If
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is unbounded, there exists a subsequence
\{a_{i_{\ell}}\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
that converges to
\infty.
Then

\frac{x}{\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathfrak{p}_{j}^{\infty})}=\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(I_{i_{\ell}}\mathfrak{p}_{j}^{-a_{i_{\ell}}})\in\overline{\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathscr{N}_{j})}.

∎

Lemma 2.7

(
[
6
, Lemma 2.5]
)
.

Let
j>0,
and let
0<\alpha<\beta.
The set

\bigcup_{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}}\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathfrak{p}_{j}^{a})\cdot[\alpha,\beta]

is a finite
union of closed bounded intervals. In particular, if
\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}_{j}))=1
and
a_{0}
is the least nonnegative integer such that

\frac{\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathfrak{p}_{j}^{a_{0}+1})}{\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathfrak{p}_{j}^{a_{0}})}\leq\frac{\beta}{\alpha},

then the set above is the finite union of precisely
a_{0}+1
pairwise disjoint closed bounded intervals.

Proof.

We consider the three possible cases of the value of
\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}_{j})).
If
\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}_{j}))=0,
then the desired union is simply
[\alpha,\beta].
Next, suppose
\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}_{j}))\neq 0.
Because
\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathfrak{p}_{j}^{a})
converges to
\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathfrak{p}_{j}^{\infty})
as
a
tends to
\infty,
there exists
A
for which
a\geq A
implies the intervals
\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathfrak{p}_{j}^{a})[\alpha,\beta]
and
\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathfrak{p}_{j}^{a+1})[\alpha,\beta]
intersect. The total union can thus be express as the union of at most
A+1
pairwise disjoint closed bounded intervals. In particular, when
\chi(N(\mathfrak{p}_{j}))=1,
if we take
A
to be
a_{0},
then the total union consists of precisely
a_{0}+1
pairwise disjoint closed bounded intervals.
∎

We are ready to prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

By Lemma
2.5
,
\overline{\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathscr{N}_{j_{K,\chi}(r)})}
is a single bounded closed interval. We use downward induction to show that for each
1\leq j\leq j_{K,\chi}(r),
the closure
\overline{\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathscr{N}_{j-1})}
is a finite union of pairwise disjoint closed bounded intervals. By Lemmas
2.6
and
2.7
, the claim holds for
j=j_{K,\chi}(r).
Suppose the claim holds for some
j=j^{\prime}
; that is,
\overline{\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathscr{N}_{j^{\prime}})}=\bigsqcup_{t\in T}C_{t}
, where
T
is finite and each
C_{t}
is a bounded closed interval. By Lemma
2.6
, we have

\overline{\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathscr{N}_{j^{\prime}-1})}=\bigcup_{t\in T}\left(\bigcup_{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}}\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathfrak{p}_{j^{\prime}}^{a})\cdot C_{t}\right).

For each
t\in T,
the inner union is a finite union of closed bounded intervals by Lemma
2.7
, so
\overline{\sigma_{r,K,\chi}(\mathscr{N}_{j^{\prime}-1})}
is the finite union of closed bounded intervals. Since
\mathscr{N}_{0}=I_{K},
the theorem is proved.
∎

3.
Range of
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}
when
r
is fixed

In this section, we prove some results about the values of
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}
for
r>1
and a Dirichlet character
\chi.
In
[
4
, Theorem 3.3]
, Defant shows that for
r\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Re(r)>1,
we have
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{0}}=\infty.
One can deduce from the theorem’s proof that for a fixed character
\chi,
we have
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}=\infty.
In this vein, we fix
r>1
and determine what values
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}
can attain for arbitrary real character
\chi.

In particular, we will prove Theorem
1.3
, which asserts that for any
r>1,
there exists large enough
N\in\mathbb{N}
for which
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}
can attain any integral value at least
N
.
The key idea of the proof is that increasing the number and the magnitude of the distinct primes dividing a character’s modulus forces additional jumps in the divisor function’s range, thus generally increasing the number of connected components in the range. Thus, the most natural collection of characters we can use to witness and control these effects in the range are principal characters, which are determined entirely by the set of distinct prime factors of their moduli.

In this section, we use notation in a similar fashion to that of the previous section. Fix
r>1.
Let
p_{j}
be the
j
-th rational prime and
\mathscr{N}_{j}
be the set of positive integers not divisible by the first
j
primes. For any positive integer
m
, let
\chi_{m}
be the principal modulus-
m
character. Lastly, recalling Definition
2.4
, set
j_{m}:=j_{\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m}}(r).

We proceed to define some new notation to select the characters
\chi
in order for
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}
to attain any sufficiently large integral value. We define
\ell:=\max(2,j_{1})
and

i_{0}=\begin{cases}j_{1}&\text{if }j_{1}\geq 2\\
\min\bigl\{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}:\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r)<1+3^{-r}\}&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Note that
i_{0}\geq\ell,
so we finally define the sequence of positive integers
(m_{i})_{i\geq i_{0}}
by

m_{i}:=\frac{1}{p_{\ell}}\prod_{p\leq p_{i}}p.

The moduli
m_{i}
are chosen so that
p_{\ell}
is the unique prime that contributes Euler factors to the image of
\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}
. In particular, the prime
p_{\ell}
creates “copies” of
[1,\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r)]
in
\overline{\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}(\mathbb{N})}
. Increasing
i
shrinks the lengths of these interval copies and thus increases the number of connected components.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
i\geq i_{0}.
Then
(C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i}}})_{i\geq i_{0}}
is a non-decreasing sequence that is not eventually constant. In particular, for any
i\geq i_{0},
we have

C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i}}}=\left\lceil\log_{p_{\ell}^{-r}}\frac{\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r)-1}{\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r)-p_{\ell}^{-r}}\right\rceil.

Proof.

Let
i\geq i_{0}.
We first show that
j_{m_{i}}=\ell.
It is clear that
j_{+}=\ell.
Now, take any
j\in\mathbb{N}\setminus S_{m_{i}}
for which
j>\ell.
Because
m_{i}
is divisible by each
p_{k}
with
k\leq i
except
p_{\ell},
this implies
j>i.
Hence,
\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},j,m_{i}}(r)=\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},j}(r).
For any
j>i,
we have
\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},j}(r)\geq 1+p_{j}^{-r}
because
i\geq i_{0}.
Thus,
\ell
satisfies the defining property of
j_{m_{i}}.

Since
\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i}}}(p_{j}^{a})=1
for
1\leq j\leq i
with
j\neq\ell,
we apply Lemma
2.6
for
1\leq j\leq\ell
and subsequently apply Lemma
2.5

to obtain

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i}}}(\mathbb{N})}

\displaystyle=\overline{\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i}}}(\mathscr{N}_{1})}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}}\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i}}}(p_{\ell}^{a})\cdot\overline{\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i}}}(\mathscr{N}_{\ell})}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}}\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i}}}(p_{\ell}^{a})\cdot[1,\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},j_{m_{i}},m_{i}}(r)].

Because
j_{m_{i}}=\ell,
we have
\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},j_{m_{i}},m_{i}}(r)=\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},\ell,m_{i}}(r).
As each prime
p_{k}
with
\ell<k\leq i
divides
m_{i},
we have
\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},\ell,m_{i}}(r)=\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r).
Thus, we have

\overline{\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i}}}(\mathbb{N})}=\bigcup_{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}}\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i}}}(p_{\ell}^{a})\cdot[1,\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r)].

Now, let
a_{i}
be the least nonnegative integer such that

(3.1)

\frac{\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i}}}(p_{\ell}^{a_{i}+1})}{\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i}}}(p_{\ell}^{a_{i}})}\leq\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r).

By Lemma
2.7
, we have
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i}}}=a_{i}+1.

Note that the left hand side of the equation is

\frac{1+p_{\ell}^{-r}+\cdots+p_{\ell}^{-(a_{i}+1)r}}{1+p_{\ell}^{-r}+\cdots+p_{\ell}^{-a_{i}r}}=\frac{1-(p_{\ell}^{-r})^{a_{i}+2}}{1-(p_{\ell}^{-r})^{a_{i}+1}}.

Algebraic manipulation shows that

C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i}}}=a_{i}+1=\left\lceil\log_{p_{\ell}^{-r}}\frac{\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r)-1}{\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r)-p_{\ell}^{-r}}-1\right\rceil+1=\left\lceil\log_{p_{\ell}^{-r}}\frac{\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r)-1}{\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r)-p_{\ell}^{-r}}\right\rceil.

As
i
increases, the expression inside the ceiling increases and approaches
\infty
because
\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r)
approaches 1 from the right. Thus,
(C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i}}})_{i\geq i_{0}}
is non-decreasing and not eventually constant.
∎

We proceed to prove Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

For each
i
, define
y_{i}=\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r).

By Lemma
3.1
, it suffices to prove that
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i+1}}}-C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i}}}\leq 1
for any
i\geq i_{0}.
Recall from Lemma
3.1
that
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i}}}=a_{i}+1,
where
a_{i}
is the least nonnegative integer satisfying Equation (
3.1
).
We essentially show that the sequence

\left(\frac{1-(p_{\ell}^{-r})^{k+2}}{1-(p_{\ell}^{-r})^{k+1}}\right)_{k\geq 0}

converges to 1 at a faster rate than
(\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r))_{i\geq i_{0}}
does.
In particular, we prove that for any
k\geq 0
and
i\geq i_{0},
we have

\frac{\frac{1-(p_{\ell}^{-r})^{k+3}}{1-(p_{\ell}^{-r})^{k+2}}-1}{\frac{1-(p_{\ell}^{-r})^{k+2}}{1-(p_{\ell}^{-r})^{k+1}}-1}<\frac{\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i+1}(r)-1}{\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r)-1}.

This proves the desired, because if
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i+1}}}-C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i}}}>1
for some
i\geq i_{0},
then
a_{i+1}\geq a_{i}+2.
This would imply that there exists
k\geq 0
such that

\frac{1-(p_{\ell}^{-r})^{k+2}}{1-(p_{\ell}^{-r})^{k+1}}\leq y_{i}\text{ and }\frac{1-(p_{\ell}^{-r})^{k+3}}{1-(p_{\ell}^{-r})^{k+2}}>y_{i+1},

which would contradict the claim.

For any
k\geq 0,
we find that

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{1-(p_{\ell}^{-r})^{k+3}}{1-(p_{\ell}^{-r})^{k+2}}-1}{\frac{1-(p_{\ell}^{-r})^{k+2}}{1-(p_{\ell}^{-r})^{k+1}}-1}

\displaystyle=p_{\ell}^{-r}\cdot\frac{1-(p_{\ell}^{-r})^{k+1}}{1-(p_{\ell}^{-r})^{k+2}}

\displaystyle<3^{-r}

because
\ell\geq 2.

On the other hand, for any
i\geq i_{0},
we find that

\displaystyle\frac{\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i+1}(r)-\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i+2}(r)}{\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r)-\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i+1}(r)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\frac{1}{1-p_{i+2}^{-r}}-1\right)\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i+2}(r)}{\left(\frac{1}{1-p_{i+1}^{-r}}-1\right)\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i+1}(r)}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{i+2}^{-r}(1-p_{i+1}^{-r})}{p_{i+1}^{-r}}

\displaystyle>2^{-r}(1-3^{-r}),

where the last step follows from Bertrand’s postulate and the fact that
p_{i+1}\geq p_{2}=3
. Inductively, we conclude that for any
k\geq 0,
we have

\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i+k}(r)-\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i+k+1}(r)>(2^{-r}(1-3^{-r}))^{k}(\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r)-\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i+1}(r))>(3^{-r})^{k}(\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r)-\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i+1}(r)).

As a result,

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i+1}(r)-1

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i+k}(r)-\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i+k+1}(r)

\displaystyle>(3^{-r}+(3^{-r})^{2}+\cdots)(\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r)-\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i+1}(r))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3^{r}-1}(\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r)-\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i+1}(r)),

so we find that

\frac{\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i+1}(r)-1}{\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},i}(r)-1}>\frac{\frac{1}{3^{r}-1}}{\frac{1}{3^{r}-1}+1}=3^{-r}.

Then setting
N
in the theorem statement to be
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{i_{0}}}}
gives the theorem.
∎

We proceed to prove Theorem
1.4
, where we show that when
r
is large enough, we can describe
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}
in greater detail.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

We first show the existence of a real number
r_{0}>1
such that

\sum_{n\geq 3}n^{-r}<2^{-r}\text{ and }\sum_{n\geq 4}n^{-r}<3^{-r}

hold for any
r>r_{0}.
Indeed, we can bound

\sum_{n\geq 3}n^{-r}\leq 3^{-r}+\int_{3}^{\infty}x^{-r}\;dx=3^{-r}\left(1+\frac{3}{r-1}\right),

which is less than
2^{-r}
for sufficiently large
r
. By the same reasoning, choosing
r
to be large enough ensures the second inequality.

Now, let
r>r_{0}.
Then

\zeta(r)=1+2^{-r}+\sum_{n\geq 3}n^{-r}<1+2\cdot 2^{-r}<\frac{1+2^{-r}}{1-2^{-r}}

and hence
\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},1}(r)=\zeta(r)(1-2^{-r})<1+2^{-r}.
Similarly, we have

\zeta(r)=1+2^{-r}+3^{-r}+\sum_{n\geq 4}n^{-r}<1+2^{-r}+2\cdot 3^{-r}<\frac{1+3^{-r}}{(1-2^{-r})(1-3^{-r})}

and hence
\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},2}(r)=\zeta(r)(1-2^{-r})(1-3^{-r})<1+3^{-r}.
Consequently,
j_{1}\geq 2,
so
\ell=\max(2,j_{1})=j_{1}.

Now, we show that
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m_{j_{1}}}}=2
. By Lemma
3.1
, we wish to show that

1<\log_{p_{j_{1}}^{-r}}\frac{\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},j_{1}}(r)-1}{\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},j_{1}}(r)-p_{j_{1}}^{-r}}<2.

This is equivalent to showing that
\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},j_{1}}(r)<1+p_{j_{1}}^{-r}
and

\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},j_{1}}(r)>\frac{(p_{j_{1}}^{-r})^{2}+p_{j_{1}}^{-r}+1}{p_{j_{1}}^{-r}+1}.

The first inequality follows from the minimality of
j_{1}
. We proceed to check the second inequality. Let
q=p_{j_{1}}
and let
q_{*}=p_{j_{1}+1}
. By the definition of
j_{1},
we have

\prod_{p>q}\frac{1}{1-p^{-r}}>\frac{1+q_{*}^{-r}}{1-q_{*}^{-r}}.

The right hand side of the inequality is a decreasing function in
q_{*}
, and Bertrand’s Postulate tells us that
q_{*}<2q,
so the desired follows from showing

\frac{1+q^{-r}+(q^{-r})^{2}}{1+q^{-r}}<\frac{1+(2q)^{-r}}{1-(2q)^{-r}}.

Let
x=q^{-r}.
The inequality above equates to
x^{2}+(2-2^{r})x+2>0,
which we need only prove holds for
x\leq 2^{-r}.
If
1<r<2,
then
2-2^{r}>-2,
so
x^{2}+(2-2^{r})x+2>x^{2}-2x+2=(x-1)^{2}+1>0.
Now, suppose
r\geq 2.
Note that
x^{2}+(2-2^{r})x+2
is decreasing on
x\in(0,2^{-r}]
because its derivative is
2x+2-2^{r}<0.
Therefore,
x^{2}+(2-2^{r})x+2\geq(2^{-r})^{2}+(2-2^{r})(2^{-r})+2>0,
so the inequality holds.

Using Lemma
3.1
, we have now shown that for any
r>r_{0},
any integer greater than 1 occurs as
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}
for some character
\chi
. To conclude, we note that there exists a character
\chi
such that
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}=1.
Let

m=\prod_{k\leq j_{1}}p_{k}.

Because
\chi_{m}(p_{k})=0
for
k\leq j_{1},
we have
\overline{\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m}}(\mathbb{N})}=\overline{\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{0}}(\mathscr{N}_{j_{1}})}.
As
j_{1}=j_{\mathbb{Q},\chi_{0}}(r),
we then apply Lemma
2.5
to conclude
\overline{\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m}}(\mathbb{N})}=[1,\zeta_{\mathbb{Q},j_{1}}(r)]
and thus
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi_{m}}=1.

∎

4.
A lower bound for
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.5
, which gives a lower bound on
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}
for any
r\geq 3
and any character
\chi
. This result improves upon
[
1
, Theorem 5.2]
, which gives a lower bound on
C_{r,\mathbb{Q}}
for any
r>1
. Inspired by the proof of
[
1
, Theorem 5.2]
, the key mechanism behind the proof of Theorem
1.5
is to partition the natural numbers based on prime factors bounded by functions of
r
and the modulus of
\chi
, which then capture explicit gaps in the range of the associated divisor function.
To begin, we prove the following useful lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
r>1.

(1)

For any integer
0<d\leq r-1,
we have

d^{-r}>\sum_{n=d+1}^{\infty}n^{-r}.

(2)

For any odd integer
d\leq 2r-2,
we have

d^{-r}>\sum_{n\geq d+2;2\nmid n}n^{-r}.

Proof.

(1) is proved in
[
1
, Lemma 5.1]
. (2) follows from noting that

\sum_{\text{odd }n=d+2}^{\infty}n^{-r}<\int_{\frac{d-1}{2}}^{\infty}(2x+1)^{-r}dx=\frac{d^{-r+1}}{2(r-1)},

which is less than or equal to
d^{-r}
when
d\leq 2r-2.

∎

We are ready to prove Theorem
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
\theta
be a nonzero complex number. Then any complex number
x\in\mathbb{C}
can be written uniquely in the form
a\theta+b\theta i,
where
a,b\in\mathbb{R}.
Define
\Re_{\theta}
by
\Re_{\theta}(a\theta+b\theta i)=a.

We begin by partitioning
\mathbb{N}
. To do so, we first define the map
B_{1}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N})
by
B_{1}(x)=\{d\in\mathbb{N}:d\mid x,d\leq r-1,\gcd(d,m)=1\}.
Next, we define the map
B_{2}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{N})
by

B_{2}(n)=\begin{cases}\{d\in\mathbb{N}:d\mid n,r-1\leq d\leq 2r-2,\gcd(d,2m)=1\}&\text{ if }2\not\in B_{1}(n)\\
\emptyset&\text{ if }2\in B_{1}(n).\end{cases}

Finally, define
A(n)=(B_{1}(n),B_{2}(n)).

Note that this determines a partition of
\mathbb{N}
whose parts are
A^{-1}(S)
for
S\in A(\mathbb{N}).
We wish to show that for any distinct
S_{1},S_{2}\in A(\mathbb{N}),
the sets
\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}(A^{-1}(S_{1}))
and
\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}(A^{-1}(S_{2}))
have disjoint closures. Write
S_{1}=A(x)
and
S_{2}=A(y)
for distinct
x,y\in\mathbb{N}.

We consider separately the cases
B_{1}(x)\neq B_{1}(y)
and
B_{1}(x)=B_{1}(y)
. Assume first that
B_{1}(x)\neq B_{1}(y)
. Suppose without loss of generality that the smallest integer
d_{0}\leq r-1
coprime to
m
that divides precisely one of
x
and
y
lies in
B_{1}(x)
. Let
\theta=\chi(d_{0}).
Take
x^{\prime}
such that
B_{1}(x^{\prime})=B_{1}(x)
and
y^{\prime}
such that
B_{1}(y^{\prime})=B_{1}(y).
For each
d<d_{0},
the difference of the terms for
d
in
\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}(x^{\prime})-\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}(y^{\prime})
is 0: if
\gcd(d,m)>1,
then
\chi(d)=0,
and otherwise,
d\in B_{1}(x^{\prime})
if and only if
d\in B_{1}(y^{\prime})
by the minimality of
d_{0}.
Then we have

\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}(x^{\prime})-\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}(y^{\prime})=\theta d_{0}^{-r}+\sum_{d\mid x^{\prime};d>d_{0}}\chi(d)d^{-r}-\sum_{d\mid y^{\prime};d>d_{0}}\chi(d)d^{-r}.

Applying
\Re_{\theta}
to both sides, we obtain

\Re_{\theta}(\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}(x^{\prime}))-\Re_{\theta}(\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}(y^{\prime}))\geq d_{0}^{-r}-\sum_{d>d_{0}}d^{-r}>0

by Lemma
4.1
.
Thus, the closures of
\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}(A^{-1}(A(x))
and
\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}(A^{-1}(A(y))
are disjoint.

It remains to consider the case
B_{1}(x)=B_{1}(y).
Then
B_{2}(x)\neq B_{2}(y),
so one of these sets is nonempty, meaning
2\not\in B_{1}(x)=B_{1}(y).
If
m
is even, then
\chi(d)=0
for even
d
. If
m
is odd, because
r-1\geq 2
, the fact that
2\not\in B_{1}(x)=B_{1}(y)
implies that
x
and
y
have no even divisors. The argument then proceeds in a similar fashion to the case
B_{1}(x)\neq B_{1}(y).
Let
d_{0}
be the smallest integer that is in precisely one of the two sets
B_{2}(x)
and
B_{2}(y).
Without loss of generality, we assume
d_{0}\in B_{2}(x)\setminus B_{2}(y).
Let
\theta=\chi(d_{0}),
and take
x^{\prime}
such that
A(x^{\prime})=A(x)
and
y^{\prime}
such that
A(y^{\prime})=A(y).
When comparing
\Re_{\theta}(\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}(x^{\prime}))
and
\Re_{\theta}(\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}(y^{\prime}))
, we use the fact that the contributions from divisors
d<d_{0}
are the same. In addition, there are no contributions from even divisors. Indeed, if
m
is even, then
\chi(d)=0
for even
d
; if
m
is odd, because
r-1\geq 2
, the fact that
2\not\in B_{1}(x)=B_{1}(y)
implies that
x
and
y
have no even divisors. More explicitly, we compute

\displaystyle\Re_{\theta}(\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}(x^{\prime}))-\Re_{\theta}(\sigma_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}(y^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\Re_{\theta}\left(\sum_{d\mid x^{\prime};d<d_{0}}d^{-r}\chi(d)+d_{0}^{-r}\theta+\sum_{d\mid x^{\prime};d\geq d_{0}+2;d\text{ odd}}d^{-r}\chi(d)\right)

\displaystyle\text{ \; }-\Re_{\theta}\left(\sum_{d\mid y^{\prime};d<d_{0}}d^{-r}\chi(d)+\sum_{d\mid y^{\prime};d\geq d_{0}+2;d\text{ odd}}d^{-r}\chi(d)\right)

\displaystyle=d_{0}^{-r}+\Re_{\theta}\left(\sum_{d\mid x^{\prime};d\geq d_{0}+2;d\text{ odd}}d^{-r}\chi(d)-\sum_{d\mid y^{\prime};d\geq d_{0}+2;d\text{ odd}}d^{-r}\chi(d)\right)

\displaystyle\geq d_{0}^{-r}-\sum_{d\geq d_{0}+2;d\text{ odd}}d^{-r},

which is positive by Lemma
4.1
. Thus, we have
C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}\geq|A(\mathbb{N})|.

It remains to give a lower bound for
|A(\mathbb{N})|.
Let
P
be the set of odd primes
p\leq 2r-2
that are coprime to
m
. For each subset
S\subseteq P,
define

n_{S}=\prod_{p\in S}p.

Because
n_{S}
is odd,
2\not\in B_{1}(n_{S}),
so
B_{2}(n_{S})=\{d\in\mathbb{N}:d\mid n_{S},r-1\leq d\leq 2r-2,\gcd(d,2m)=1\}.
Each prime
p\in S
is recorded either in
B_{1}(n_{S})
(if
p\leq r-1
) or in
B_{2}(n_{S})
(if
r-1\leq p\leq 2r-2
). Thus, distinct choices of subsets
S
of
P
give rise to distinct values of
A(n_{S})
. Consequently, we have

C_{r,\mathbb{Q},\chi}\geq|A(\mathbb{N})|\geq 2^{|P|}=2^{\pi_{m}(2r-2)-\delta_{m}},

where
\delta_{m}
equals 0 if
m
is even and equals 1 if
m
is odd.
∎

5.
A lower bound for
C_{r,K}

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.6
, which gives a lower bound for
C_{r,K}
for finite Galois extensions
K
of
\mathbb{Q}
and generalizes the lower bound that
[
1
, Theorem 4.1]
gives when
K=\mathbb{Q}
. The proof’s key mechanism is inspired by that of the previous section and of
[
1
, Theorem 4.1]
. We partition the set of integral ideals in
\mathscr{O}_{K}

based on the multiplicities of their divisor ideals whose norms are bounded by a
function
h_{K}(r)
depending on
K
and
r
. This partition captures explicit
gaps in the range of the associated divisor function; thus, the problem reduces to
lower-bounding
h_{K}(r)
and counting how many distinct partition classes are
created.
The new feature when handling a number field
K
, rather than just
\mathbb{Q}
,
is that distinct ideals can have the same norm. Then in order to bound

h_{K}(r)
, we control the number of ideals of each fixed norm using the
coefficients of the Dedekind zeta function. Finally, we use Chebotarev’s density
theorem to estimate the number of rational primes that split completely in
K
, giving a
lower bound for the number of gaps created.

We begin by defining some notation that we use throughout this section. We fix a degree-
s
Galois extension
K/\mathbb{Q}.

For
n\geq 1,
we define
a_{K}(n)
to be the number of integral ideals
I
in
K
such that
N(I)=n.
Then the Dedekind zeta function can be written as

\zeta_{K}(r)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{K}(n)}{n^{r}}.

In addition, we define
b_{K}(n)
be the number of prime ideals
\mathfrak{p}
in
K
such that
N(\mathfrak{p})=n.
Lastly, for any ideal
I\subseteq\mathscr{O}_{K},
we define
m_{I}(n)
to be the number of integral ideals
J
in
K
for which
J\mid I
and
N(J)=n
. Then the divisor function can be written as

\sigma_{r,K}(I)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{m_{I}(n)}{n^{r}}.

We first prove a general “theoretical” lower bound on
C_{r,K}
that generalizes the partition method used in the previous section.

Definition 5.1
.

Fix
r>1.
We define
h_{K}(r)
to be the largest integer
h\geq 1
such that for each integer
2\leq d\leq h,
the inequality

d^{-r}>\sum_{n>d}\frac{a_{K}(n)}{n^{r}}

holds. If no such
h\geq 2
exists, then define
h_{K}(r)=1.

Theorem 5.2
.

For any
r>1,
we have

C_{r,K}\geq\prod_{n=2}^{h_{K}(r)}(b_{K}(n)+1).

Proof.

Let
h=h_{K}(r).
Define the map
A:I_{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{h-1}
by

A(I)=(m_{I}(2),m_{I}(3),\ldots,m_{I}(h)).

Note that this determines a partition of
I_{K}
whose parts are
A^{-1}(S)
for
S\in A(I_{K}).
We wish to show that for any distinct
S_{1},S_{2}\in A(I_{K}),
the sets
\sigma_{r,K}(A^{-1}(S_{1}))
and
\sigma_{r,K}(A^{-1}(S_{2}))
have disjoint closures. Write
S_{1}=A(\mathfrak{a})
and
S_{2}=A(\mathfrak{b})
for distinct
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\in I_{K}.
Let
d_{0}
be the smallest integer
2\leq d_{0}\leq h
such that
m_{\mathfrak{a}}(d_{0})\neq m_{\mathfrak{b}}(d_{0}),
and we assume without loss of generality that
m_{\mathfrak{a}}(d_{0})>m_{\mathfrak{b}}(d_{0}).

Now, let
\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}
and
\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}
be ideals in
K
such that
A(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime})=A(\mathfrak{a})
and
A(\mathfrak{b}^{\prime})=A(\mathfrak{b}).
From the definition of
d_{0},
we have
m_{\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}}(d_{0})-m_{\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}}(d_{0})\geq 1.
Thus, we observe

\displaystyle\sigma_{r,K}(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime})-\sigma_{r,K}(\mathfrak{b}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{m_{\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}}(n)-m_{\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}}(n)}{n^{r}}

\displaystyle\geq d_{0}^{-r}-\sum_{n=d_{0}+1}^{\infty}\frac{m_{\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}}(n)}{n^{r}}

\displaystyle\geq d_{0}^{-r}-\sum_{n=d_{0}+1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{K}(n)}{n^{r}}.

Because
d_{0}\leq h_{K}(r),
the definition of
h_{K}(r)
implies that the last expression above is positive and thus that
\sigma_{r,K}(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime})>\sigma_{r,K}(\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}).

Thus, we have shown that the closures of
\sigma_{r,K}(A^{-1}(S_{1}))
and
\sigma_{r,K}(A^{-1}(S_{2}))
are disjoint. It follows that
C_{r,K}\geq|A(I_{K})|,
so it remains to determine a lower bound for
|A(I_{K})|.
We do so in a fashion analogous to that of the previous section. For each
2\leq n\leq h,
denote the prime ideals of norm
n
by

\mathfrak{p}_{n,1},\ldots,\mathfrak{p}_{n,b_{K}(n)}.

For each tuple
(i_{n})_{2\leq n\leq h},
where
0\leq i_{n}\leq b_{K}(n),
define the ideal

I_{(i_{n})}=\prod_{n=2}^{h}\prod_{j=1}^{i_{n}}\mathfrak{p}_{n,j}.

We check that distinct tuples
(i_{n})
give distinct values of
A(I_{(i_{n})}).
Let
\textbf{i}=(i_{n})
and
\textbf{j}=(j_{n})
be distinct tuples, and let
n_{0}
be the smallest integer for which
i_{n_{0}}\neq j_{n_{0}}.
We show
m_{I_{\textbf{i}}}(n_{0})\neq m_{I_{\textbf{j}}}(n_{0}).
The prime ideals dividing
I_{\textbf{i}}
and
I_{\textbf{j}}
that are of norm less than
n_{0}
are identical. Thus, the ideals dividing
I_{\textbf{i}}
and
I_{\textbf{j}}
that are not prime and are of norm equal to
n_{0}
are also identical. Hence,
m_{I_{\textbf{i}}}(n_{0})-m_{I_{\textbf{j}}}(n_{0})
equals the difference in the number of prime ideals dividing
I_{\textbf{i}}
and
I_{\textbf{j}}
that have norm
n_{0},
which is
i_{n_{0}}-j_{n_{0}}\neq 0.

Therefore,

C_{r,K}\geq\#\{(i_{n})_{2\leq n\leq h}:0\leq i_{n}\leq b_{K}(n)\}=\prod_{n=2}^{h}(b_{K}(n)+1).

∎

Now, we aim to bound
a_{K}(n)
in terms of
n
.
To do this, we first define the function

d_{s}(n)=\#\{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{s})\in\mathbb{N}^{s}:n_{1}n_{2}\cdots n_{s}=n\}

for any positive integer
s.
The function
d_{s}(n)
is clearly multiplicative. For any prime
p
and nonnegative integer
e
, we see that

d_{s}(p^{e})=\#\{(e_{1},\ldots,e_{s})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}:e_{1}+\dots+e_{s}=e\}=\binom{e+s-1}{s-1}.

Therefore, we have the general formula

d_{s}(n)=\prod_{p^{e_{p}}\|n}\binom{e_{p}+s-1}{s-1}.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
K/\mathbb{Q}
be a degree-
s
Galois field extension. For any
n\geq 1,
we have
a_{K}(n)\leq d_{s}(n).

Proof.

The Dedekind zeta function can be expressed as

\zeta_{K}(x)=\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\subseteq\mathscr{O}_{K}}(1-N(\mathfrak{p})^{-x})^{-1}.

Because
a_{K}
and
d_{s}
are multiplicative, we fix a rational prime
p
and prove
a_{K}(p^{a})\leq d_{s}(p^{a})
for any positive integer
a
. Let
f_{p}
be the inertia degree of a prime ideal in
K
lying above
p
, and let
r_{p}
be the number of distinct prime ideals in
K
lying above
p
. Then

\zeta_{K}(x)=\prod_{p}(1-p^{-f_{p}x})^{-r_{p}}.

Note that
a_{K}(p^{a})
is precisely the coefficient of
p^{-ax}
in
\zeta_{K}(x)
and is thus the coefficient of
p^{-ax}
in the
p
-Euler factor
(1-p^{-f_{p}x})^{-r_{p}}.
Because
f_{p}\geq 1
and
r_{p}\leq s,
the coefficient of
p^{-ax}
in the expansion of
(1-p^{-f_{p}x})^{-r_{p}}
is less than or equal the coefficient of
p^{-ax}
in the expansion of
(1-p^{-x})^{-s}.
The latter coefficient is precisely
d_{s}(p^{a}),
as desired.
∎

Next, we bound
d_{s}(n)
in terms of
n
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Fix a positive integer
s
. For any
\varepsilon>0,
there exists a constant
\eta_{s,\varepsilon}>0
such that
d_{s}(n)\leq\eta_{s,\varepsilon}n^{\varepsilon}
for any
n\geq 1.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0.
Recall the closed formula

d_{s}(n)=\prod_{p^{e_{p}}\|n}\binom{e_{p}+s-1}{s-1}.

Each binomial coefficient
\binom{e+s-1}{s-1}
is bounded above by
e^{s-1}
for large enough
e
. This polynomial growth is dominated eventually by exponential growth, so there exists
E
such that if
e>E,
then

\binom{e+s-1}{s-1}\leq 2^{\frac{e\varepsilon}{2}}.

We then split the product in
d_{s}(n)
using
E
; that is, we write
d_{s}(n)=d_{s,1}(n)d_{s,2}(n),
where we define

d_{s,1}(n)=\prod_{p^{e_{p}}\|n,1\leq e_{p}<E}\binom{e_{p}+s-1}{s-1}\text{ and }d_{s,2}(n)=\prod_{p^{e_{p}}\|n,e_{p}\geq E}\binom{e_{p}+s-1}{s-1}.

We can bound the second product as follows.

d_{s,2}(n)\leq\prod_{p^{e_{p}}\|n}2^{\frac{e_{p}\varepsilon}{2}}\leq\prod_{p^{e_{p}}\|n}p^{\frac{e_{p}\varepsilon}{2}}=n^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}

We proceed to bound the first product. Let

M=\max_{1\leq e<E}\binom{e+s-1}{s-1}.

Then
d_{s,1}(n)\leq M^{\omega(n)},
where
\omega(n)
is the number of distinct prime factors of
n
. Using the standard upper bound
\omega(n)=O(\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}),
we find that

\displaystyle d_{s,1}(n)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(\log M\cdot\omega(n))

\displaystyle=\exp\left(O_{s,\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\delta_{s,\varepsilon}\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\right)\text{ for some constant $\delta_{s,\varepsilon}$ and large enough }n

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\log n\right)\text{ for large enough }n.

Therefore, we have
d_{s}(n)=d_{s,1}(n)d_{s,2}(n)\leq n^{\varepsilon}
for large enough
n
. Consequently, accounting for the finitely many smaller values of
n
, there exists a constant
\eta_{s,\varepsilon}>0
such that
d_{s}(n)\leq\eta_{s,\varepsilon}n^{\varepsilon}
for any
n\geq 1.

∎

Thus, we have shown that for any finite Galois extension
K/\mathbb{Q}
and
\varepsilon>0,
there exists a constant
\eta_{K,\varepsilon}:=\eta_{s,\varepsilon}
such that
a_{K}(n)\leq\eta_{K,\varepsilon}n^{\varepsilon}
for any positive integer
n
. We use this bound to show that the function
h_{K,\varepsilon}(r)
we define next is a lower bound for
h_{K}(r)
. Then we apply Theorem
5.2
and Chebotarev’s density theorem to prove Theorem
1.6
.

Definition 5.5
.

Let
K/\mathbb{Q}
be a finite Galois extension, and let
\varepsilon>0.
For
r>\varepsilon+1,
define

h_{K,\varepsilon}(r)=\left\lfloor\left(\frac{r-\varepsilon-1}{\eta_{K,\varepsilon}}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}}\right\rfloor.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

We show
h_{K,\varepsilon}(r)\leq h_{K}(r).
Let
2\leq d\leq h_{K,\varepsilon}(r).
Because
a_{K}(n)\leq\eta_{K,\varepsilon}n^{\varepsilon}
for any positive integer
n
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n>d}\frac{a_{K}(n)}{n^{r}}

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{K,\varepsilon}\sum_{n>d}n^{-r+\varepsilon}

\displaystyle<\eta_{K,\varepsilon}\int_{d}^{\infty}x^{-r+\varepsilon}\;dx

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta_{K,\varepsilon}}{r-\varepsilon-1}d^{-r+\varepsilon+1},

where the last equality holds because
r>\varepsilon+1.
The fact that
d\leq h_{K,\varepsilon}(r)
implies

\frac{\eta_{K,\varepsilon}}{r-\varepsilon-1}d^{-r+\varepsilon+1}\leq d^{-r}.

The computation above thus gives

\sum_{n>d}\frac{a_{K}(n)}{n^{r}}<d^{-r},

verifying
h_{K,\varepsilon}(r)\leq h_{K}(r).

Recall that by Theorem
5.2
, we have

C_{r,K}\geq\prod_{n=2}^{h_{K,\varepsilon}(r)}(b_{K}(n)+1).

We then restrict this product to the rational primes that split completely in
K
in order to apply Chebotarev’s density theorem. In particular, we have

\displaystyle C_{r,K}

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{p\leq h_{K,\varepsilon}(r)\text{ splits completely in }K}(b_{K}(p)+1)

\displaystyle=(s+1)^{\#\{p\leq h_{K,\varepsilon}(r):p\text{ splits completely in }K\}}.

Notice that
h_{K,\varepsilon}(r)\asymp_{K,\varepsilon}r^{1/(1+\varepsilon)}
; that is,
h_{K,\varepsilon}(r)
is bounded above and below by
(K,\varepsilon)
-dependent constant multiples of
r^{1/(1+\varepsilon)}
for
r
sufficiently large.
Chebotarev’s density theorem then gives us

\displaystyle\#\{p\leq h_{K,\varepsilon}(r):p\text{ splits completely in }K\}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{s}\frac{h_{K,\varepsilon}(r)}{\log h_{K,\varepsilon}(r)}

\displaystyle\asymp_{K,\varepsilon}\frac{r^{1/(1+\varepsilon)}}{\log r}.

Because
s
is dependent on
K
, we conclude that there exist constants
\mu_{K,\varepsilon}
and
r_{K,\varepsilon}
for which

\log C_{r,K}>\mu_{K,\varepsilon}\left(\frac{r^{1/(1+\varepsilon)}}{\log r}\right)

holds for any
r>r_{K,\varepsilon}.

∎

6.
Unboundedness of
C_{r,K}
when
r
and
[K:\mathbb{Q}]
are fixed

The previous section showed that for a fixed number field
K
, the number of connected components
C_{r,K}
tends to
\infty
as
r
tends to
\infty.
A natural next question is whether
C_{r,K}
exhibits the same behavior when
r
is fixed and
K
is varied. In this section, we prove Theorem
1.7
, which answers this question in the positive. Indeed, we show the stronger result that
C_{r,K}
can be arbitrarily large when
r
and
[K:\mathbb{Q}]
are fixed and
K
is varied.
To prove Theorem
1.7
, we define a notion of
mighty norms
that generalizes terminology defined in
[
8
]
, after which we outline the main ideas of the proof.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
d>1
be the norm of some prime ideal in
K
. We say
d
is an
r
-mighty norm
of
K
if

(6.1)

1+d^{-r}>\prod_{N(\mathfrak{p})>d}(1-N(\mathfrak{p})^{-r})^{-1}.

Like Zubrilina’s definition, the existence of
r
-mighty norms correspond to the existence of gaps in
\sigma_{r,K}(I_{K}),
which we make precise below.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
d_{1}<d_{2}<\dots<d_{M}
be
r
-mighty norms of
K
. Then
C_{r,K}\geq M+1.

Proof.

Fix
d:=d_{i}
for some
1\leq i\leq M.
We first show the existence of a gap in
\overline{\sigma_{r,K}(I_{K})}
associated to
d
. Let
S
be the set of ideals
I
in
K
such that some prime ideal factor
\mathfrak{q}
of
I
satisfies
N(\mathfrak{q})\leq d.
For any
I\in S,
we have
\sigma_{r,K}(I)\geq 1+d^{-r}.
Meanwhile, for any
I\in I_{K}\setminus S,
we have
\sigma_{r,K}(I)\leq\prod_{N(\mathfrak{p})>d}(1-N(\mathfrak{p})^{-r})^{-1}.
Therefore,
\overline{\sigma_{r,K}(S)}\cap\overline{\sigma_{r,K}(I_{K}\setminus S)}=\emptyset.

Now, we verify that for any
1\leq i<j\leq M,
the gap intervals

\left(\prod_{N(\mathfrak{p})>d_{j}}(1-N(\mathfrak{p})^{-r})^{-1},1+d_{j}^{-r}\right)\text{\; and \;}\left(\prod_{N(\mathfrak{p})>d_{i}}(1-N(\mathfrak{p})^{-r})^{-1},1+d_{i}^{-r}\right)

are disjoint. We see easily that

\prod_{N(\mathfrak{p})>d_{i}}(1-N(\mathfrak{p})^{-r})^{-1}\geq(1-d_{j}^{-r})^{-1}>1+d_{j}^{-r},

so the intervals are disjoint. Thus, there are at least
M
many gap intervals in
\overline{\sigma_{r,K}(I_{K})},
so
C_{r,K}\geq M+1.

∎

For an arbitrary positive integer
M
, we aim to construct a sequence of rational primes
p_{1}<p_{2}<\cdots<p_{M}
and a degree-
s
number field
K
in which
p_{1},\ldots,p_{M}
are
r
-mighty norms. By Lemma
6.2
, this would immediately give Theorem
1.7
.
The idea is to exploit the effect of a prime ideal’s splitting behavior on its norm. Notice that rational primes
p
that split completely in a number field
K/\mathbb{Q}
have “smaller” norms
p
, whereas rational primes that are inert in
K
have “larger” norms
p^{s}
. Hence, if the primes
p_{1}<p_{2}<\dots<p_{M}
are spaced widely enough apart and if they split completely while nearby primes remain inert in
K
, then
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{M}
are
r
-mighty norms of
K
. The first lemma we introduce achieves the first goal of constructing such a sequence of rational primes, and the second lemma we introduce achieves the second goal of showing the existence of a number field
K
with the desired splitting behaviors.

Lemma 6.3
.

Fix a real number
r>1,
an integer
s\geq 2,
and an integer
M\geq 1.
There exists a sequence of rational primes
p_{1}<p_{2}<\dots<p_{M}
satisfying the following conditions.

(1)

(a)

p_{1}>\max(2,s)^{s}.

(b)

\log(1-x)^{-1}\leq 2x
for
x=p_{1}^{-r}.

(c)

\log(1+x)\geq\frac{x}{2}
for
x=p_{1}^{-r}.

(Note that (b) and (c) thus also hold for any
0\leq x\leq p_{1}^{-r}.
)

(2)

For each
2\leq i\leq M,
we have
p_{i}^{1/s}>p_{i-1}.
In particular, defining for each
1\leq i\leq M
the set
S_{i}=\{q\text{ prime}:p_{i}^{1/s}<q\leq p_{i}\},
the sets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{M}
are pairwise disjoint.

(3)

For any
1\leq j<i\leq M,
we have

s\cdot\sum_{q\in S_{i}}q^{-r}<\frac{1}{12M}p_{j}^{-r}.

(4)

For any
1\leq i\leq M,
we have

\sum_{\text{prime }q>p_{i}}q^{-sr}<\frac{1}{12}p_{i}^{-r}.

Before we give a technical proof of this lemma, we briefly explain the later necessity of each condition. Recall that our goal is to show
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{M}
are
r
-mighty norms for some degree-
s
number field
K
.
Condition (1) allows us to compare the right-hand side of Equation (
6.1
) for
d=p_{i}
with simpler expressions in
p_{i},
such as
\sum_{N(\mathfrak{p})>p_{i}}N(\mathfrak{p})^{-r}.
Then conditions (2), (3), and (4) allow us to give an upper bound on
\sum_{N(\mathfrak{p})>p_{i}}N(\mathfrak{p})^{-r}
that we then compare to the left-hand side of Equation (
6.1
) for
d=p_{i}.

Proof of Lemma
6.3
.

We construct the desired sequence of primes recursively. We first construct the desired
p_{1}.
Condition (1) can be imposed by taking
p_{1}
sufficiently large, because

\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log(1-x)^{-1}}{x}=\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log(1+x)}{x}=1.

Now, suppose we have chosen
p_{1},\ldots,p_{i-1}
to satisfy the above conditions; we aim to choose
p_{i}
such that
p_{1},\ldots,p_{i}
satisfy the four conditions. Condition (2) clearly just requires
p_{i}
to be sufficiently large. Next, we consider condition (3). Because
r>1,
we may write

\sum_{q\in S_{i}}q^{-r}\leq\sum_{q>p_{i}^{1/s}}q^{-r}<\int_{p_{i}^{1/s}}^{\infty}x^{-r}\;dx=\frac{(p_{i}^{1/s})^{-r+1}}{r-1}\ll_{r}p_{i}^{\frac{1-r}{s}}.

Therefore, we have

s\cdot\sum_{q\in S_{i}}q^{-r}\ll_{r,s}p_{i}^{\frac{1-r}{s}}.

The right-hand side tends to 0 as
p_{i}
tends to
\infty,
so we can choose
p_{i}
sufficiently large to bound it from above by
\frac{1}{12M}\min_{1\leq j<i}p_{j}^{-r}.
Lastly, we consider condition (4). Similarly, because
s\geq 2
and
r>1
and thus
sr-1>1,
we see that

\sum_{\text{prime }q>p_{i}}q^{-sr}\ll_{r}p_{i}^{-sr+1}.

Because
sr-1>r,
we can choose
p_{i}
sufficiently large to bound the left-hand side from above by
\frac{1}{12}p_{i}^{-r}.

∎

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
s\geq 2
be an integer, and let
S
and
T
be disjoint finite sets of rational primes. Furthermore, assume each prime in
S
is greater than
s
. Then there exists a degree-
s
extension
K/\mathbb{Q}
such that each prime in
S
splits completely in
K
and each prime in
T
remains inert in
K
.

Proof.

We aim to construct a monic irreducible polynomial
f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
such that, modulo each prime in
S\cup T,
its factorization type corresponds to the desired splitting behavior. We then can apply the Dedekind–Kummer theorem to show that
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
, where
\alpha
is a root of
f(x),
is a desired choice of
K
.

For each
p\in S,
we choose a monic degree-
s
polynomial
f_{p}(x)=x^{s}+c_{p,s-1}x^{s-1}+\cdots+c_{p,0}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]
that splits into
s
distinct linear factors over
\mathbb{F}_{p}.
This is feasible because
s<p.
Furthermore, for each
p\in T,
we choose a monic irreducible polynomial
f_{p}(x)=x^{s}+c_{p,s-1}x^{s-1}+\cdots+c_{p,0}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[x]
of degree
s
. Finally, fix some prime
q\not\in S\cup T,
and let
f_{q}(x)=x^{s}+q.
We show there exists a monic degree-
s
polynomial
f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
such that

f(x)\equiv\begin{cases}f_{p}(x)\pmod{p}&\text{ for }p\in S\cup T\\
f_{q}(x)\pmod{q^{2}}\end{cases}

For each
0\leq i\leq s-1,
we apply the Chinese remainder theorem to obtain
n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
n_{i}\equiv c_{p,i}\pmod{p}
for each
p\in S\cup T
and
n_{i}\equiv q\pmod{q^{2}}
if
i=0
and
n_{i}\equiv 0\pmod{q^{2}}
else. Then
f(x)=x^{s}+n_{s-1}x^{s-1}+\cdots+n_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
satisfies the desired congruences.

Note that
f
is an Eisenstein polynomial because
f(x)\equiv x^{s}+q\pmod{q^{2}},
so it is irreducible. Let
\alpha
be a root of
f,
and let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha).
To apply the Dedekind–Kummer theorem to
K
for each
p\in S\cup T
, we must check that
p\nmid[\mathscr{O}_{K}:\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]].
Fix
p\in S\cup T
. We have the identity

\text{disc}(f)=[\mathscr{O}_{K}:\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]]^{2}\cdot\text{disc}(K).

Now, notice that
f(x)\pmod{p}
is squarefree; when
p\in S,
this is by construction, and when
p\in T,
we note that any irreducible polynomial over a finite field is separable and thus squarefree. Therefore,
p\nmid\text{disc}(f)
. Using the fact that
\text{disc}(K)\in\mathbb{Z}
because
\alpha
is an algebraic integer, we obtain
p\nmid[\mathscr{O}_{K}:\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]].
Thus, the Dedekind–Kummer theorem applies at each
p\in S\cup T.
If
p\in S,
then
f(x)\equiv f_{p}(x)\pmod{p}
splits into
s
distinct linear factors, so
p
splits completely in
K
; if
p\in T,
then
f(x)\equiv f_{p}(x)\pmod{p}
is irreducible, so
p
remains inert in
K
.
∎

We are ready to prove Theorem
1.7
.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Let
M
be an arbitrary positive integer. By Lemma
6.3
, we have a sequence of rational primes
p_{1}<p_{2}<\dots<p_{M}
satisfying the four conditions specified in the lemma’s statement. Now, note that we can choose a real number
X>p_{M}
such that

s\cdot\sum_{q>X}q^{-r}<\frac{1}{12}p_{M}^{-r}.

The left-hand side is bounded above by some
r
-dependent constant multiple of
X^{-r+1}
and thus tends to 0 as
X
tends to
\infty,
so making
X
large enough ensures the validity of the above inequality.
Now, let

S=\bigcup_{i=1}^{M}S_{i},

where
S_{i}
are defined in the statement of Lemma
6.3
, and let
T
be the set of rational primes
q\not\in S
such that
q\leq X.
Applying Lemma
6.4
to these choices of
S
and
T
, we have a degree-
s
extension
K/\mathbb{Q}
such that
p_{1},\ldots,p_{M}
split completely in
K
and any rational prime
q\not\in S
for which
q\leq X
is inert.

Fix
1\leq i\leq M.
Because
p_{i}
splits completely in
K
, it is a norm of a prime ideal in
K
. We prove that
p_{i}
is an
r
-mighty norm of
K
; this would imply
C_{r,K}\geq M+1,
proving the theorem. Recall that this requires showing

\prod_{N(\mathfrak{p})>p_{i}}(1-N(\mathfrak{p})^{-r})^{-1}<1+p_{i}^{-r}.

By condition (1) in Lemma
6.3
, we have
\log(1-x)^{-1}\leq 2x
for any
x\leq p_{1}^{-r}.
Therefore, it suffices to show

(6.2)

2\sum_{N(\mathfrak{p})>p_{i}}N(\mathfrak{p})^{-r}<\log(1+p_{i}^{-r}).

We proceed to bound the sum on the left-hand side by considering the three following types of rational primes
q
that
\mathfrak{p}
can lie over.

•

q\leq X
is a rational prime that splits completely in
K
. The norm of any prime ideal lying above
q
is
q
, so by condition (2) of Lemma
6.3
, if
q>p_{i},
then
q\in S_{j}
for some
j>i
. Therefore, the total contribution of prime ideals
\mathfrak{p}
lying above such
q
satisfying
N(\mathfrak{p})>p_{i}
to the summation is bounded from above by

s\cdot\sum_{j>i}\sum_{q\in S_{j}}q^{-r}<\sum_{j>i}\frac{1}{12M}p_{i}^{-r}<\frac{1}{12}p_{i}^{-r},

where the first inequality is condition (3) of Lemma
6.3
.

•

q\leq X
is a rational prime that remains inert in
K
. If
q\leq p_{i}^{1/s},
then
N(q\mathscr{O}_{K})=q^{s}\leq p_{i},
so
q\mathscr{O}_{K}
does not contribute to the summation. Because
q\leq X
is inert in
K
, it does not lie in
S_{i},
so
q\not\in(p_{i}^{1/s},p_{i}].
Lastly, if
q>p_{i}
, then the sole prime ideal
q\mathscr{O}_{K}
lying above
q
contributes
q^{-sr}
to the summation. The total contribution of these inert
q\leq X
is

\sum_{\text{prime }q>p_{i}}q^{-sr}<\frac{1}{12}p_{i}^{-r},

where the inequality is condition (4) of Lemma
6.3
.

•

q>X
is a rational prime. Then
q>p_{i},
so any prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
lying above
q
satisfies
N(\mathfrak{p})>p_{i}.
There are at most
s
prime ideals in
K
lying above
q
, and each one has norm at least
q
. Thus, the contribution of such ideals to the summation is bounded above by

s\cdot\sum_{q>X}q^{-r}<\frac{1}{12}p_{M}^{-r}\leq\frac{1}{12}p_{i}^{-r},

where the middle inequality follows from the definition of
X
.

Summing the contributions from these three cases, we conclude that

\sum_{N(\mathfrak{p})>p_{i}}N(\mathfrak{p})^{-r}<\frac{1}{4}p_{i}^{-r}.

Then Equation (
6.2
) holds if

2\left(\frac{1}{4}p_{i}^{-r}\right)=\frac{1}{2}p_{i}^{-r}<\log(1+p_{i}^{-r}).

This holds true by condition (1) in Lemma
6.3
, so we have shown
p_{i}
is an
r
-mighty norm of
K
.
∎
