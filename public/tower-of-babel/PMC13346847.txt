Chitosan coating effects on biological response to niobia-modified yttria-stabilized zirconia
Authors: Le Na Lau, Chae-Ryeong Cha, Satoshi Imazato, In-Sung Luke Yeo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSEThe impact of chitosan (Ch) surface modification on the osseointegration of niobia-modified yttria-stabilized zirconia (Y,Nb-TZP) implants was assessed using a rabbit tibia model.MATERIALS AND METHODSY,Nb-TZP and SLA discs and implants were prepared and coated with Ch of 95% degree of deacetylation (DDA). MC3T3-E1 cells and human gingival fibroblasts were cultured onto discs (n = 3) for cell proliferation, adhesion, osteogenic differentiation and gene expression analysis. Specimen surface characteristics were assessed using scanning electron microscopy and confocal laser scanning microscopy. Eight rabbits underwent tibial implant placement and were euthanized at 2 and 8 weeks after surgery (n = 4). Bone-to-implant contact (BIC) and bone area (BA) were quantified through histomorphometric evaluation. One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was used for statistical analysis (P < .05) unless otherwise specified.RESULTSCh-coated Y,Nb-TZP discs supported cell attachment, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation in vitro, with increased osteogenic and early focal adhesion gene expression, compared with uncoated controls. However, in vivo evaluation in rabbit tibiae showed no improvement in bone-to-implant contact or bone area around Ch-modified implants relative to uncoated Y,Nb-TZP or SLA controls, and osseointegration did not occur in the Ch-coated groups.CONCLUSION.Although Ch promoted osteogenic activity in vitro, these effects did not translate in vivo, and the coating failed to enhance osseointegration on Y,Nb-TZP implants. This gap underscores the complexity of host responses and the need to refine Ch properties for further evaluation under clinically relevant conditions.

INTRODUCTION

Over the past few decades, dental implants have evolved into a reliable solution for replacing missing teeth, significantly enhancing patients’ masticatory function and overall quality of life.12 Current implant systems vary in surface topography, wettability, chemical composition, and modification methods.34 These surface properties directly affect osseointegration by modulating interactions with host osteoblasts during new bone formation.5 Successful osseointegration is essential, as various biological and mechanical factors can significantly affect bone attachment and long-term stability.67 To accelerate and improve this process, numerous additive and subtractive surface-modification approaches have been developed.89 Such chemical and physical alterations are designed to enhance clinical predictability and overall treatment success.10

Produced by the partial deacetylation of chitin, chitosan (Ch) is a copolymer of glucosamine and N-acetylglucosamine that has been FDA approved for medical use.111213 Owing to the presence of multiple amino groups on its main chain, it exhibits high reactivity and readily forms salts with acids.1415 Among natural polymers, the usage of Ch in implant dentistry appears to be the most promising as it offers inherent antibacterial effects and a chemical composition similar to that of the bone extracellular matrix.516 Although Ch has been reported as either bactericidal or bacteriostatic, its antibacterial mechanism remains unclear.1718 Proposed contributors include the amino groups in its structure and the source of the chitin.17 Ch demonstrates excellent biocompatibility, hydrophilicity, and biodegradability, while remaining non-toxic.1419 These features promote protein adsorption and cell adhesion on titanium (Ti) surfaces, enhancing implant osseointegration.2021 Ch is also reported to be osteoconductive, aid wound healing, and exert anti-inflammatory effects.1022 Together, these properties have driven extensive investigation of Ch as a surface coating to improve osseointegration across both in vitro and in vivo studies. Ch exhibits strong film-forming properties, enabling its use as a coating material for dental implants and supporting good compatibility with osteoblasts and fibroblasts.14 Although Ch-based coatings on Ti implants have been widely explored, achieving strong adhesion to the metal surface remains a challenge, regardless of whether electrophoretic deposition, layer-by-layer assembly, casting, spin coating, or dip coating is used.232425 In this study, we selected dip coating—a simple, low-cost method suitable for small substrates, to produce thin Ch layers that can be densified by heat treatment. The process allows reasonable control over coating thickness26 and relies on a balance between viscous forces, surface tension, gravity, and substrate withdrawal speed.27

Evidence from the early 2000s confirmed that sandblasted and acid-etched (SLA) 3 mol% yttria-stabilized zirconia (3Y-TZP) matched similarly treated Ti in osseointegration, supporting zirconia implant commercialization.2829 Nonetheless, applying the same surface treatment to 3Y-TZP produces a surface morphology unlike that of Ti, as their hardness and crystal structures differ, which in turn alters the bone response.30 Despite its routine use as a reference surface for Ti implants, SLA treatment has been reported to produce limited bone responses on 3Y-TZP, suggesting that alternative surface strategies should be explored.3132 One approach to mitigating these limitations involves co-stabilizing zirconia with yttrium and niobium oxide (Y,Nb-TZP). This compositional modification affects lattice organization and grain morphology, contributing to improved fracture resistance and decreased surface hardness.33 Prior studies have also demonstrated that Y,Nb-TZP maintains mechanical performance compatible with dental prosthetic applications while showing lower fracture susceptibility.34 Y,Nb-TZP also supports osteoblast activity at levels comparable to or exceeding that of 3Y-TZP, and animal data demonstrate osseointegration similar to SLA-treated Ti.35

Although Ch has been promising in engineering and implant applications,1036 its performance and potential for bone-related uses require further development. Furthermore, previous studies have generally been centered on the use of Ch with Ti surfaces.37 Although Ch coatings have been widely studied on Ti surfaces, the biological performance of Ch-modified (Y,Nb)-TZP implants has not yet been reported. Given the ability of Ch coatings to enhance surface biocompatibility, the present study investigated the biological responses to Ch application on (Y,Nb)-TZP and SLA-treated Ti surfaces under both in vitro and in vivo conditions. We hypothesized that Ch-treated surface would be more biocompatible than uncoated surface at the cell-surface interfaces and at the bone—implant interface.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Specimen preparation

SLA Ti and Y,Nb-TZP discs (Ø10 mm × 1 mm); and SLA Ti and Y,Nb-TZP implants (Ø3.4 mm × 8 mm) were manufactured for this study. Following fabrication, all discs and implants were visually inspected to confirm the absence of defects and subsequently allocated into four experimental groups based on surface modification: Y,Nb-TZP, SLA, Y,Nb-TZP-Ch and SLA-Ch. Prior to further surface processing, all specimens underwent sandblasting with alumina (Al2O3) particles to increase surface roughness before subjected to surface treatments. For in vitro experiments, SLA Ti discs were produced from commercially pure grade 4 Ti (Point Implant) using computer numerical control machining (CINCOM L20; Citizen Machinery Co., Ltd., Nagano, Japan), followed by hydrochloric acid etching (approximately 11.5 M) after sandblasting. In contrast, Y,Nb-TZP discs were prepared by sintering compacted zirconia powder (Vatech MCIS, Suwon, Korea). Thirty-two implants were included in the in vivo evaluation: 16 SLA-treated titanium implants obtained commercially (Deep Implant System, Seongnam, Korea) and 16 zirconia implants fabricated in-house using identical powder and molds to ensure consistent macroscopic geometry. For surface characterization, six discs and six implants per group were prepared using identical fabrication protocols. The Ch coating procedure is described in detail below. Unmodified SLA titanium specimens were used as the control group. Prior to usage and Ch coating, all specimens were sterilized with ethylene oxide (EO) gas.

For the in vitro experiments, murine pre-osteoblasts, MC3T3-E1, and human gingival fibroblasts were seeded onto discs of each group to assess cell adhesion, proliferation, and gene and protein expression. In vivo experiments were conducted using a rabbit tibial model arranged according to a 4 × 4 Latin square design, employing screw-type implants.

Ch coating

The Ch coating protocol was adapted from the method reported by Vakili et al. with minor procedural adjustments.1438 1% (w/v) chitosan (≥ 95% degree of deacetylation (DDA); 100-200 mPa.s viscosity from shrimp shells; Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was added to 1% (v/v) acetic acid (Daejung Chemicals & Metals Co., Ltd., Siheung, Korea) solution and stirred for 12 h using a magnetic stirrer. The discs and implants were coated with Ch by immersing them in the prepared solution, ensuring full surface coverage. After coating, they were left to dry in a drying oven at 50% relative humidity to form a uniform film and to prevent cracking or deformation. Specimens were left under ultraviolet (UV) sterilization overnight in the drying oven before usage. This method of sterilization in SLA-Ch and Y,Nb-TZP-Ch groups was preferred as other methods, such as EO gas, may leave harmful residues and potentially damage the coating’s molecular structure, increasing its susceptibility to degradation.3940 However, the exact effects of sterilization on the coating’s molecular stability and mechanical properties remain unclear. SLA and Y,Nb-TZP group discs and implants did not receive any surface coating and were sterilized by EO gas only. Based on certain in vitro findings, the choice of sterilization method appears to have minimal impact on initial mineralization.4142

Surface characteristics

Surface microstructure of the discs and implants (n = 3) was qualitatively assessed using field emission-scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; S-4700, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan), and elemental composition was analyzed by energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS; EMAX, Horiba, Northampton, UK) integrated with the FE-SEM system. To evaluate surface topography, surface roughness parameters—including arithmetical mean roughness (Ra), root mean square roughness (Rq), root mean square height (Sq), arithmetical mean height (Sa), and developed interfacial area ratio (Sdr) (n = 3)—were measured using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM; LSM-800, Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany).

Cell culture

Dental implants are placed within the jawbone such that the prosthetic crown remains exposed above the gingival margin, resulting in portions of the implant surface interfacing simultaneously with osseous and soft gingival tissues.4 To assess the in vitro effects of Ch, MC3T3-E1 cells were used to evaluate its role in osteogenesis, while human gingival fibroblasts were selected to examine its cytocompatibility. This choice reflects the importance of stable gingival attachment in preventing inflammation and reducing the risk of implant failure.43 MC3T3-E1 murine pre-osteoblastic cells (CRL-2593; ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were purchased and cultured in α-minimum essential medium (α-MEM; Cytiva, Logan, UT, USA) supplemented with a 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Cytiva, Logan, UT, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Cytiva, Logan, UT, USA). Osteogenic media includes 10 mM β-glycerophosphate and 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid. Primary human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs; PCS-201-018, ATCC) were cultured in fibroblast basal medium (PCS-201-030, ATCC) supplemented with a growth kit (PCS-201-041, ATCC). All cells were cultured at 37℃ in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2, and the medium was replaced every 3 days.

Cell proliferation of MC3T3-E1 and HGF

MC3T3-E1 and HGF cell proliferation was evaluated using methyl thiazolyl tetrazolium (MTT) assay (Roche, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). MC3T3-E1 cells were seeded onto discs and cultured for 1, 3 and 5 days, whereas HGFs were cultured for 1, 4, 7, 10, and 14 days. For both cell types, seeding was performed at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well (n = 3) in 24-well plates. Following the culture period, MTT reagent (50 µl/well) was added and incubated for 4 hours at 37℃ under 5% CO2. The resulting formazan crystals were solubilized by adding 500 µL of solubilization buffer to each well and allowing the plates to stand overnight. The following day, aliquots of the dissolved solution were transferred to 96-well plates for spectrophotometric analysis. Two measurements were made for each disc. Cell proliferation was determined by measuring optical density (OD) at 570 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

Immunostaining, morphological observation and cell adhesion of MC3T3-E1 cells

MC3T3-E1 cells were seeded onto each disc (n = 3) at 1 × 104 cells per well in 24-well plates and cultured for 1, 3 and 5 days. After phosphate buffered saline (PBS) washing, fixation was performed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 30 min, followed by 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min. To suppress nonspecific binding, samples were treated with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min. Visualization of cell adhesion and cytoskeletal morphology was achieved by staining filamentous actin with Alexa Fluor 488 phalloidin (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), with nuclei subsequently labeled using DAPI (Fluoroshield; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The discs were mounted in confocal imaging dishes and examined using CLSM (LSM 980; Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany). Image acquisition and quantitative analysis were performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Evaluation of alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity in MC3T3-E1 cells

MC3T3-E1 cells were cultured onto discs (n = 3) at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 24-well plates and maintained in growth medium. Upon reaching full confluence, the culture medium was replaced with an osteogenic medium. ALP activity was assessed after 4 and 7 days of osteogenic induction (MAK447; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). OD was measured at 405 nm immediately after substrate addition, and again after 4 min using a microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

Evaluation of MC3T3-E1 cell mineralization

Mineralized matrix formation was assessed by Alizarin Red S staining. Following 14 and 28 days of culture, cells were fixed with 10% formalin, rinsed with ultrapure water, stained with 1% Alizarin Red S for 5 min, and washed thoroughly. Bound Alizarin Red dye was extracted using acetic acid and subsequently neutralized with ammonium hydroxide. Absorbance at 405 nm was recorded using a microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

Real-Time Reverse Transcription Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR) of MC3T3-E1 and HGF cells

MC3T3-E1 cells were cultured on discs (n = 6) in 12-well plates at 2 ×105 cells/well for 7 and 14 days. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). RNA concentration and purity were assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with acceptable A260/A280 ratios ranging from 1.5 to 1.9. For cDNA synthesis, 0.5 µg of total RNA was reverse transcribed using SuperScript IV reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Quantitative PCR reactions were carried out using 1 µL of synthesized cDNA in a reaction mixture containing PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Amplification was carried out using QuantStudio 3 (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) under the following conditions: 50℃ for 2 minutes, 95℃ for 2 minutes followed by 40 cycles of 95℃ for 1 s and 60℃ for 30 s. Primer for target gene amplification was obtained from Macrogen (Seoul, Korea). Primer sequences for RUNX2, OCN, ALP, COL1A1 and GAPDH were designed based on sequences reported by Kwak et al.4 Cycle threshold (Ct) values were used to compute relative expression levels, applying the 2^-ΔΔCt formula and normalizing to GAPDH. Ct values were automatically determined, and all data were normalized to the average ΔCt of the control group (SLA). Samples with Ct values above 38 were excluded, as the no-template control also showed a Ct > 38. RT-qPCR expression data were normalized using GAPDH as the endogenous control.

Similarly, the entire process was repeated for HGFs cultured on discs (n = 6) in 12-well plates at 2 × 105 cells/well for 1, 2, 24 and 48 hours. Primer sequences for TAL, ITGB1, FAK, VCL and GAPDH were adopted from those reported by Kwak et al.4

Cell adhesion of HGF

Early cell adhesion was assessed by seeding HGFs (n = 3) onto discs in 24-well plates at 1 × 105 cells/well and incubating for 0.5, 1, or 2 h. PBS washing was performed at each time point, after which cells were fixed in 4% PFA for 30 min, and nuclei were labeled with DAPI (1 µg/mL). Adherent cells were imaged by CLSM (LSM 980, Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany) and quantified using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

in vivo surgery

Eight male New Zealand white rabbits (3 – 4 months old, weighing approximately 2.5 kg; Hanlim Laboratory Animal Inc., Hwaseong, Korea) were used for the in vivo experiment portion of this study. All experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Cronex (CRONEX-IACUC: 202501004, Hwaseong, Korea) and were conducted in compliance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. The rabbits were divided into two groups of four. Each rabbit received two implants per tibia, inserted into osteotomy sites (Ø3.2 mm) drilled from the medial tibial surface. Implant placement followed a split-plot design. One group was euthanized after 2 weeks, the other after 8 weeks. Prior to surgery, anesthesia was administered using an intravenous combination of tiletamine hydrochloride and zolazepam hydrochloride (5 mg/kg each, Yuhan Company, Seoul, Korea), along with intramuscular xylazine hydrochloride (4.7 mg/kg, Yuhan Company, Seoul, Korea). The hind limbs were shaved and disinfected with betadine. Lidocaine (2%) containing epinephrine (1 : 100000) (Yuhan Company, Seoul, Korea) was locally administered at the surgical site. A 3 – 5 cm linear incision was made, and the skin and fascia were reflected using a periosteal elevator to expose the tibia. Drilling was performed in three sequential steps using Ø2.0 mm, Ø2.5 mm, and Ø3.2 mm drill bits, penetrating only the upper cortical bone. Implants were inserted up to the boundary between the macro- and micro-threaded regions, spaced 10 mm apart. Two implants were installed in each tibia, and four implants were placed in an experimental animal, which had one implant from each group according to a 4 × 4 Latin square. Closure involved subcutaneous suturing with 4-0 coated Vicryl (Ethicon, Somerville, MA, USA) and skin suturing with 4-0 blue nylon (Woorhi Medical, Namyangju, Korea). Postoperatively, rabbits received enrofloxacin (4 mg/kg, Biotril, Komipharm International, Siheung, Korea) and meloxicam (0.4 mg/kg, Metacam, Labiana Life Science, Terrassa, Spain) for three days to prevent infection and manage pain. Rabbits were individually identified with bilateral ear ink markings and housed in separate cages to avoid confusion.

Histomorphometric Analysis

At 2- and 8-week post-implantation, the experimental animals were euthanized via an intravenous overdose of sodium pentobarbital (Hanlim Pharm. Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea) at ≥ 100 mg/kg, administered through the marginal ear vein. Euthanasia procedures complied with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020). Tibial specimens with integrated implants were isolated by careful dissection, fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, and prepared as uncalcified resin-embedded blocks. These blocks were sectioned into 50 µm thick slices. For histomorphometric evaluation, the specimens collected at both time points were stained with Goldner’s Masson trichrome to visualize bone-to-implant contact (BIC) and bone area (BA). The stained slides were examined using a light microscope (DM2700M, Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). Image analysis was conducted using ImageJ software (U.S. National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). To assess BIC and BA, measurements were obtained from the three most representative consecutive threads of each implant. The BIC and BA ratios were calculated for each thread individually, and the mean values were then determined for analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Data are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise stated. Group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple-comparison procedure applied for post hoc testing. A two-tailed P value of less than .05 was considered indicative of statistical significance. For non-parametric data, the Kruskal–Wallis test was employed to assess differences in elemental composition of discs and implants, as well as ΔCt values associated with osteogenesis-related gene expression. When applicable, pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction with a significance level set at α = 0.05.

RESULTS

Surface characteristics of disc specimens

Following sample preparation, we investigated the changes in surface morphology of discs with FE-SEM (Fig. 1A). The SLA group exhibited a rough, honeycomb-like surface characterized by sharp peaks and deep fossae. The Y,Nb-TZP group revealed a high-density, grainy texture with surface grains approximately 100 – 200 nm in size. Although this group also showed surface undulations similar to the SLA group, its peaks and pits were noticeably more rounded and less pronounced. After subjected to Ch coating, SLA-Ch and Y,Nb-TZP-Ch surfaces showed evident film coverage that appears uniform, homogeneous and free of significant cracks or pores. The Ch-coated specimens exhibited significantly smoother surfaces than the uncoated ones (P < .001) (Fig. 1B). Both Y,Nb-TZP and Y,Nb-TZP-Ch groups showed significantly lower Ra and Rq levels compared to the SLA and SLA-Ch groups (P < .001). The results of EDS-based surface elemental analysis are listed in Table 1. Ch-coated specimens had a higher proportion of carbon compared to their uncoated counterparts.

MC3T3-E1 cell proliferation and adhesion

Fig. 2A shows the results of MTT-based analysis of MC3T3-E1 proliferation, revealing a significant, time-dependent increase in cell numbers on all specimens, particularly evident by Day 5. Cell proliferation on Day 5 was markedly higher in the SLA-Ch and Y,Nb-TZP-Ch groups relative to SLA, with the Y,Nb-TZP group showing the most pronounced increase (P < .001). Cell adhesion analysis using CLSM images is presented in Fig. 2B. The results mirrored the cell proliferation trend, showing a time-dependent increase in adherent cells across all groups. On Day 5, the Y,Nb-TZP-Ch group exhibited significantly greater cell adhesion than the SLA group (P < .001). In contrast, the SLA-Ch group showed a significant increase in adhesion compared to the SLA group only on Day 3. Notably, the Y,Nb-TZP group demonstrated the highest cell adhesion on Days 1, 3, and 5 compared to all other groups (P < .001).

Osteogenic activity of MC3T3-E1 on Ch-coated discs

To assess whether the Ch coating influences osteoinductive activity, ALP activity and mineralization were measured in preosteoblasts cultured on discs under osteogenic conditions. ALP activity, an indicator of early osteoblastic differentiation, was analyzed at defined time points.4 On Day 7, cells on SLA-Ch discs showed the highest ALP expression among all groups, followed by SLA, Y,Nb-TZP, and Y,Nb-TZP-Ch (Fig. 3A). However, a statistically significant difference between the SLA-Ch and SLA groups was only observed on Day 4 (P < .05). Late-stage osteogenic differentiation was evaluated by Alizarin Red S staining to quantify calcium deposition (Fig. 3B). By Day 14, cells cultured on Y,Nb-TZP-Ch discs exhibited the highest level of mineralization among all groups, with a statistically significant difference compared to the Y,Nb-TZP group (P < .001). On Day 28, mineralization peaked on SLA-Ch discs, indicating the highest osteoinductive activity at that time point, followed by the SLA, Y,Nb-TZP-Ch (P < .05), and Y,Nb-TZP groups (P < .001).

Osteogenesis-related gene expression in MC3T3-E1

To assess osteogenic differentiation, MC3T3-E1 cells cultured on the various disc surfaces were analyzed via RT-qPCR for expression of key osteogenic markers, including alkaline phosphatase (ALP), runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2), collagen type I alpha 1 (COL1A1), and osteocalcin (OCN) (Fig. 4). Relative gene expression levels are reported as fold changes, with the SLA group used as the reference and normalized to a value of 1. Due to the limited sample size, statistical comparisons among the four groups were conducted using the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by Bonferroni-corrected post hoc pairwise analyses (α = 0.05). Preosteoblasts cultured on Ch-coated Ti discs showed higher expression levels of all four target genes compared to uncoated discs on both Day 7 and Day 14. Notably, the expression of OCN was 5-fold higher on Day 7 and 6-fold higher on Day 14 in the SLA-Ch group compared to the SLA group. OCN expression was significantly greater in SLA-Ch than in the Y,Nb-TZP group on Day 7 (P < .05). On Day 14, RUNX2 transcript levels were significantly elevated in the SLA-Ch group relative to the Y,Nb-TZP group (P < .05). COL1A1 expressions were also increased in cells cultured on SLA-Ch surfaces, showing a significant difference compared with Y,Nb-TZP at Day 7 and compared with the SLA group at Day 14 (P < .05). Additionally, ALP expression was markedly enhanced in the SLA-Ch group compared to Y,Nb-TZP on both Day 7 (P < .001) and Day 14 (P < .05).

HGF cell proliferation, cell adhesion, and gene expression

Cell proliferation on the four disc surfaces was assessed using MTT assay. As shown in Fig. 5A, cell numbers significantly increased over time (P < .001), reaching a peak on Day 10 and then declining by Day 14 in all groups except SLA, which showed continued growth beyond Day 10. In the Ch-coated specimens, cell proliferation increased sharply and significantly between Day 4 and Day 10 compared to the SLA group (P < .001). Among all groups, the Y,Nb-TZP group exhibited the highest level of cell proliferation starting from Day 4 onward.

Early cell adhesion, which plays an important role in regulating subsequent cell growth and proliferation, was further evaluated (Fig. 5B). The number of attached cells per 417 µm2 was calculated using CLSM images. The number of adherent HGFs increased over time in all groups. After 2 hours, the SLA-Ch group demonstrated the greatest number of attached cells, corresponding to a 58% increase relative to baseline, followed by the SLA, Y,Nb-TZP-Ch, and Y,Nb-TZP groups. Although these trends were observed, intergroup differences did not reach statistical significance. Nonetheless, the findings indicate that Ch coating may promote early HGF adhesion on SLA surfaces.

Expression levels of adhesion-related genes, including integrin β1 (ITGB1), talin (TAL), focal adhesion kinase (FAK), and vinculin (VCL), were evaluated by RT-qPCR and visualized using a heatmap (Fig. 5C). On SLA-Ch discs, all four genes showed elevated expression at 1 and 2 hours compared to uncoated samples. Over time, the differences between Ch-coated and uncoated substrates gradually decreased. Overall, these results suggest that Ch coating enhances the early transcriptional activation of VCL, TAL and FAK on SLA surfaces and contributes to maintaining this upregulation during the initial adhesion phase.

Implant surface characteristics and histomorphometric analysis of osseointegration

Surface features of the implants, as visualized by FE-SEM, are presented in Fig. 6A. The SLA-treated Ti implant surface displayed a distinct cratered topography with sharp peaks and deep valleys as a result of large-grit sandblasting followed by acid etching. In comparison, the Y,Nb-TZP implant surface appeared relatively smoother but maintained a grainy or nanostructured texture, giving the surface a fine mosaic or pebble-like appearance. Ch-coated specimens retained features of both surface types but were overlaid with a visible film, producing a smoother and more subdued surface appearance. Surface roughness parameters, taken via CLSM, are presented in Fig. 6B. SLA Ti implants had the roughest surface with Sa value of 1.8 µm ± 0.1 µm, Sq value of 2.3 µm ± 0.2 µm and Sdr value of 192.4% ± 28%. These values decreased after Ch coating. The EDS analysis revealed the atomic composition of the implants (Table 2), which closely resembled that of the discs used in the in vitro experiment. Ti and carbon were the major elements detected on the implant surface. Ch-coated implants revealed a higher carbon content compared to uncoated implants.

Eight rabbits were sacrificed at 2 and 8 weeks after implantation. Light microscope images used for histomorphometric analysis of osteogenesis around the implants are presented in Fig. 7A. The Y,Nb-TZP-Ch group exhibited sparse new bone formation at Week 2, which persisted through Week 8, accompanied by increased connective tissue and moderate bone maturation. The SLA-Ch group also showed minimal new bone formation and a predominance of fibrous connective tissue at Week 2, with only slight bone apposition and signs of inflammatory cell infiltration by Week 8. Part of the peri-implant spaces were filled with soft tissue or voids, indicating poor osseointegration. In contrast, both the Y,Nb-TZP and SLA groups (without Ch coating) demonstrated successful osseointegration within the cortical bone, with new bone forming between implant threads. SLA group at 8 weeks showed substantial bone growth that conforms closely to the implant surface, with dense mineralized bone surrounding the implant, reflecting robust bone-to-implant integration. BIC and BA ratios are depicted in Fig. 7B and 7C. At both Week 2 and Week 8, the Ch-coated groups exhibited significantly lower BIC and BA values compared to the Y,Nb-TZP and SLA groups (P < .001). Among all the groups, the SLA-Ch group recorded the lowest values (BIC: 12.3 ± 5.3% at Week 2, 20.3 ± 19.5% at Week 8; BA: 24.2 ± 4.3% at Week 2, 31.6 ± 12.5% at Week 8). In contrast, the SLA group achieved the highest BIC ratio at Week 8 (81.3 ± 2.4%), while the Y,Nb-TZP group showed the highest BA ratio at Week 8 (65.6 ± 6.1%).

DISCUSSION

Ch is widely applied in bone regeneration strategies owing to its biocompatibility, anti-inflammatory properties, and ability to support tissue formation.10 Accordingly, SLA Ti and Y,Nb-TZP discs were coated with Ch at 95% DDA to evaluate how preosteoblast cells and HGFs respond to this surface modification.

In the in vitro experiments, Ch-coated discs exhibited superior cell adhesion, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation of MC3T3-E1 cells compared with unmodified Y,Nb-TZP and SLA Ti surfaces, as demonstrated by the MTT assay and immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 2). Likewise, HGF attachment and proliferation were higher on Ch-coated discs (Fig. 5), indicating that Ch may promote early cell adhesion on SLA surfaces. RT-qPCR analysis for osteogenic markers (RUNX2, ALP, OCN, and COL1A1) further confirmed the enhanced differentiation of cells, with the highest gene expression levels observed in the Ch-coated groups. This aligns with findings by Alnufaiy et al., who likewise observed increased ALP expression following Ch-coated surface modification.10 The superior performance of these coatings may be attributed to the increased amino group content of high-DDA Ch, which promotes bonding to the implant surface.4 RUNX2, known as the master transcription factor required for initiating osteoblast differentiation, was upregulated, supporting the role of Ch in accelerating early osteogenic commitment.9 RUNX2 expression is typically elevated during the early stages of osteogenesis, peaking around one week after cell culture, and subsequently declines as osteoblasts mature and mineralize.444 Consistent with this pattern, our results showed higher RUNX2 expression during the early phase of differentiation compared with Day 14 in Ch-coated groups (Fig. 4). RUNX2 promotes the expression of OCN, a key regulator of bone formation, and a representative marker of late-stage osteoblast differentiation and mineralization.7 This is also reflected in the results of our study.

Contrary to the in vitro results, in vivo analysis showed that Ch-coated implants did not achieve osseointegration and demonstrated poorer performance compared with the Y,Nb-TZP and the benchmark SLA Ti implants. Several factors could have influenced these outcomes. In our study, we used highly purified Ch with a DDA of ≥ 95% as a surface coating for our implants. Simard et al. investigated how Ch with different DDA levels influences neutrophil behavior, specifically their migration to sites of injury and activation during wound repair.12 Their findings showed that compared to 80% DDA, 95% DDA Ch did not trigger neutrophil chemotaxis, superoxide generation, or the release of intracellular granules by polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs), indicating limited ability to recruit or activate these cells.12 PMNs can adopt dendritic-like phenotypes and regulate adaptive immunity, with their responses shaped by the surrounding environment.612 Accordingly, the DDA of Ch is a key determinant of PMN activation and its potential therapeutic use.6 As DDA increases, Ch becomes less soluble and more resistant to lysosomal degradation.23 In vivo, Ch with 50 – 80% DDA is gradually degraded and cleared through enzymatic and cellular pathways.12 Their results show that Ch within the 80 – 95% DDA range can trigger markedly different biological responses, underscoring the need to specify DDA when performing biological assays.12 Impaired neutrophil chemotaxis is therefore associated with delayed healing and lower implant success rates.12

The dip-coating process employed in our study is a simple, inexpensive way to deposit thin chemical layers.38 It allows reasonable control over coating thickness and enables fine adjustment of the amount of material deposited and the final film thickness. Ch loading in 1% acidic solution was considered adequate, although the optimization of the Ch loading amount, degradation rate and antibacterial effect still warrants further investigation.1538 Nonetheless, Ch-based coatings have been reported to possess limited stiffness and strength, especially in humid environments.11 As dip-coated implants rely on weaker covalent bonding rather than metallic bonding, the coating may crack or delaminate under mechanical stresses during implantation.5

To further account for the discrepancy between the favorable in vitro outcomes and the lack of osseointegration in vivo, it should be acknowledged that osteogenic assays have limited predictive value for bone—implant integration.6 In vivo, new bone formation typically progresses from the advancing front of regenerating trabecular bone rather than from preosteoblasts directly adherent to implant surfaces. Moreover, bioactive coatings that actively recruit inflammatory cells may unintentionally promote bone resorption.19 Supporting this concept, Hoemann et al.6 reported that scaffolds coated with 99% DDA Ch induced chondrogenesis rather than osteogenesis in fractured trabecular bone, despite promising in vitro results. Conversely, scaffolds treated with 83% DDA Ch elicited inflammatory angiogenesis accompanied by bone resorption, effects partly attributed to a neutrophil-mediated immune response. The accumulation of neutrophils or eosinophils within bone tissue can activate osteoclasts, enhance bone remodeling, and ultimately delay bone regeneration.6

Other studies have also achieved similar results in vivo. In a study conducted by López-Valverde et al.,14 Ch-coated Ti implants exhibited reduced BIC at the crestal and mid-level of the implants compared to uncoated Ti implants. Spin-Neto et al.13 evaluated Ch-coated implants of varying molecular weight and observed dense fibrovascular tissue accompanied by acute inflammation on histologic sections, without any osseointegration. This is consistent with what we observed in our study. Although inflammation is essential at the onset of healing, prolonged or excessive inflammation can delay or even prevent new bone formation.22 The authors suggested that the sterilization protocol may have contributed to the inflammatory response.22 Notably, they also used UV irradiation for 12 hours, as in our study.

From a clinical perspective, the present findings highlight that favorable in vitro cellular responses to implant surface modifications do not necessarily translate into predictable osseointegration in vivo. Although Ch coating enhanced osteogenic activity and cell adhesion in vitro, it did not improve bone integration around Y,Nb-TZP implants in the rabbit tibia model. Therefore, implant surface modifications intended for clinical use should be evaluated through comprehensive in vivo studies before clinical trials, as biological responses at the bone—implant interface may differ substantially from those observed in cell-based experiments.

There were several limitations in this study. First, the in vivo experiment included a relatively small number of animals (n = 8). This sample size was selected in accordance with ethical considerations for animal experimentation and the principle of reduction, while still allowing preliminary evaluation of osseointegration in an exploratory preclinical model. Although a formal a priori power calculation was not performed, the number of animals used is comparable to those commonly reported in similar rabbit tibia implant studies. Nevertheless, the limited sample size may reduce the statistical power of the in vivo findings, and further studies with larger cohorts and formal power analyses are warranted to confirm these results. Second, minor variations in the Ch coating, such as those in thickness, uniformity, and adhesion strength, may have influenced the results. Findings from the rabbit tibia model cannot be directly applied to humans, as rabbits exhibit limited cortical bone remodeling and a much faster bone turnover and healing rate.37 Future studies should explore how the degree of deacetylation, concentration, and molecular weight of Ch influence its osteogenic potential. Further clinical studies are needed to determine the long-term performance of Y,Nb-TZP implants across different clinical applications.

CONCLUSION

Although Ch-coated surfaces supported osteogenic activity in vitro, these effects did not translate into the in vivo setting, and Ch coating did not enhance osseointegration. This disparity underscores the complexity of biological responses in living systems and highlights the need to refine Ch’s material properties and evaluate its performance under clinically relevant conditions before it can be reliably applied to Y,Nb-TZP implants. A well-orchestrated early immune response may play a role in determining the effectiveness of Ch coatings rather than their application alone.
