The Bernstein–von Mises theorem for Bayesian one-pass online learning
Jeyong Lee, Junhyeok Choi, Dongguen Kim, Minwoo Chae

Abstract
Bayesian online learning provides a coherent framework for sequential inference. However, its theoretical understanding remains limited, particularly in the one-pass setting. Existing theoretical guarantees typically require the mini-batch sample size to diverge, a condition that fails in the one-pass regime. In this paper, we propose a new Bayesian online learning algorithm tailored to the one-pass setting, which incorporates a warm-start phase to ensure stable sequential updates. For this algorithm, we show that the sequentially updated posterior attains the optimal convergence rate. Building on this, we establish an online analogue of the Bernstein–von Mises theorem, which guarantees valid uncertainty quantification without diverging mini-batch sample sizes. Our analysis is based on a novel theoretical framework that differs fundamentally from existing approaches in the online learning literature. Numerical experiments on generalized linear models show that the proposed method matches the performance of the batch estimator while outperforming existing online procedures.



1
Introduction

Online learning refers to a statistical framework for settings where data arrive sequentially, typically arising in large-scale or streaming environments. Unlike batch learning, which constructs an estimator using the entire dataset at once, online learning updates parameters sequentially as new observations become available. Each update relies only on the previous estimate and the current observation without revisiting past data. This requirement is justified by memory constraints that make it impractical to store the full dataset and by computational considerations, since recomputing a batch estimator at each step can be prohibitively expensive. In practice, online updates are often performed using small mini-batches of data. A particularly stringent formulation is the one-pass setting, in which each observation is processed individually and contributes exactly once to the update.

Motivated by these practical constraints, online learning methods have been extensively developed across a wide range of statistical models. Representative examples include topic models
(
12
,
38
,
14
)
, Dirichlet process mixtures
(
13
)
, matrix factorization
(
22
)
, survival analysis
(
40
,
39
,
7
)
, and quantile regression
(
6
,
19
)
.

From a theoretical perspective, however, the online learning setting imposes structural constraints that fundamentally distinguish it from classical batch learning. In particular, since data arrive sequentially, the total sample size is typically unknown, making it difficult to tune hyperparameters that depend on a fixed sample size. Moreover, as past observations are not revisited, errors or biases introduced at early stages may propagate through subsequent updates and affect the entire trajectory of the estimator. Consequently, the statistical analysis of online estimators departs from the classical M-estimation framework, necessitating new analytical tools. Understanding the theoretical behavior of online estimators has therefore become an important problem.

In the frequentist literature, stochastic gradient descent (SGD) is a standard approach to online learning. Due to its recursive structure and computational efficiency, SGD, originally introduced by
28
, and its variants have been extensively studied. As pioneering works,
29
and
27
independently proposed averaging schemes for SGD iterates, with
27
establishing asymptotic normality of the resulting estimator. More recently,
34
introduced stabilized variants with improved finite-sample performance, and subsequent works have focused on establishing valid uncertainty quantification for SGD-based estimators
(
5
,
41
,
18
,
9
)
. Importantly, these results extend naturally to the one-pass setting while retaining their theoretical guarantees.

On the other hand, the Bayesian framework provides a conceptually appealing alternative, as it updates a distribution over the parameter space in a sequential manner. The posterior distribution at each step can be used as the prior for the next step, allowing for a coherent learning procedure in the online setting. However, except in conjugate models, exact posterior updates are generally intractable, necessitating approximation at each step
(
25
,
30
)
. This has led to the development of Bayesian online learning methods that approximate the posterior within a tractable family, often using variational techniques
(
3
,
21
,
24
,
15
,
16
)
.

Despite these methodological advances, the theoretical understanding of Bayesian online learning remains limited. Most existing works focus on algorithmic design, while rigorous statistical guarantees are relatively scarce. To address this gap, recent work by
17
has shown that sequentially updated distributions can be asymptotically equivalent to the full posterior based on the entire dataset. However, such results rely on diverging mini-batch sample sizes and therefore do not fully capture the one-pass setting. Moreover, empirical evidence in
17
suggests that when the mini-batch size is small, statistical inefficiency may arise. These observations highlight the need for Bayesian online learning methods tailored to the one-pass setting, together with a rigorous theoretical analysis of their statistical properties.

Motivated by this gap, we propose a Bayesian online learning procedure tailored to the one-pass setting and establish its theoretical validity. The proposed method is inspired by variational approximation, where the expected log-likelihood is replaced by a local quadratic approximation, leading to a tractable update with a closed-form expression. This construction yields a computationally efficient procedure that is well-suited for the one-pass setting. However, the local quadratic approximation is valid only in a neighborhood of the true parameter, and errors arising in early updates may propagate through subsequent updates in the one-pass setting. This motivates initializing the recursion with a sufficiently accurate estimate. To this end, the method incorporates a warm-start phase that ensures the estimator enters a local neighborhood before the online recursion begins.

We further establish an online analogue of the Bernstein–von Mises (BvM) theorem by analyzing the asymptotic behavior of the sequentially updated posterior distributions. This result shows that, even in the fully online regime, the proposed posterior achieves asymptotic efficiency and provides valid frequentist uncertainty quantification, without requiring diverging mini-batch sizes, in contrast to
17
. To establish this result, we develop a theoretical framework adapted to the algorithmic structure of the procedure, enabling a refined non-asymptotic analysis. This framework differs from existing techniques in the SGD literature and avoids the strong structural assumptions typically imposed in their analyses. See Section
3.3
for details.

Finally, we complement our theoretical findings with extensive numerical experiments, demonstrating that the proposed method achieves strong empirical performance compared to existing approaches across a range of settings. In particular, the experiments highlight the importance of the warm-start phase, as omitting this phase can lead to substantially slower convergence in certain settings. See Section
4
for details.

2
Bayesian online learning algorithm

In this section, we first introduce the general framework of Bayesian online learning, and then present a concrete one-pass algorithm motivated by this framework.

2.1
Bayesian online learning framework

For
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we denote by
{\bf D}_{1:N}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N})
the collection of all samples up to the first
N
observations.
Let
\Pi_{0}
denote the initial prior on
\Theta
. The corresponding posterior distribution is
\Pi(\cdot\mid{\bf D}_{1:N})
, which we refer to as the
N
-th full posterior. In online learning, we assume that the data become available sequentially in the order
{\bf D}_{1},{\bf D}_{2},\ldots,{\bf D}_{T}
, where
{\bf D}_{t}=(Y_{N_{t-1}+1},Y_{N_{t-1}+2},\ldots,Y_{N_{t}})
represents the
t
-th mini-batch, with
N_{0}=0
. For simplicity, we assume that each mini-batch has the same size, denoted by
n
, so that
N_{t}=nt
for all
t
.

In practice, many Bayesian online learning methods can be characterized as recursive processes that sequentially apply an update step followed by an approximation step to maintain computational tractability. This general framework with a variational approximation was analyzed in our previous work
(
17
)
, where we considered a sequence
(\Pi_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{OVB}}})_{t\leq T}
of approximated posterior distributions starting from
\Pi_{0}^{\textnormal{\rm{OVB}}}=\Pi_{0}
. For
t\geq 1
, let
\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}(\cdot\mid{\bf D}_{t})
be the posterior distribution obtained by updating the prior
\Pi_{t-1}^{\textnormal{\rm{OVB}}}
with the data
{\bf D}_{t}
via Bayes’ rule. Specifically, the updated posterior is given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}(\mathcal{A}\mid{\bf D}_{t})=\dfrac{\int_{\mathcal{A}}\exp\left\{-\sum_{i\in I_{t}}\ell_{i}(\theta)\right\}\mathrm{d}\Pi_{t-1}^{\textnormal{\rm{OVB}}}(\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\left\{-\sum_{i\in I_{t}}\ell_{i}(\theta)\right\}\mathrm{d}\Pi_{t-1}^{\textnormal{\rm{OVB}}}(\theta)}

(2.1)

for any measurable
\mathcal{A}\subset\Theta
, where
I_{t}=\{N_{t-1}+1,\ldots,N_{t}\}
.
Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a variational family of probability measures on
\Theta
.
Since
\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}(\cdot\mid{\bf D}_{t})
is computationally intractable in general, we approximate the updated posterior by projecting it onto the class
\mathcal{Q}
. The approximated posterior is defined as

\displaystyle\Pi_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{OVB}}}=\argmin_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}K\big(Q;\>\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}(\cdot\mid{\bf D}_{t})\big),

(2.2)

where

\displaystyle K(P;Q)=\int\log\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}Q}\right)\mathrm{d}P

(2.3)

denotes the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence. Thus, we obtain the sequence
(\Pi_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{OVB}}})_{t=1}^{T}
of approximated posterior distributions, where
\Pi_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{OVB}}}\in\mathcal{Q}
for every
t\geq 1
.
This general procedure has been applied to many popular Bayesian models; see

3
,
21
,
24
,
7
.

It is well known that minimizing the KL divergence as in (
2.2
) is equivalent to minimizing the negative evidence lower bound (ELBO); that is,

\displaystyle\Pi_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{OVB}}}=\argmin_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{Q}\Big[\sum_{i\in I_{t}}\ell_{i}(\theta)\Big]+K\big(Q;\>\Pi_{t-1}^{\textnormal{\rm{OVB}}}\big)\right\},

(2.4)

where
{\mathbb{E}}_{Q}
denotes the expectation with respect to
Q
. Hence, with the Gaussian variational family
\mathcal{Q}=\{Q=N(\mu,\bm{\Omega}^{-1}):\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p},\bm{\Omega}\in\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{++}}^{p}\}
, the online learning procedure for obtaining
(\Pi_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{OVB}}})_{t=1}^{T}
reduces to successive optimization problems over
{\mathbb{R}}^{p}\times\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{++}}^{p}
. Our earlier paper
(
17
)
analyzed theoretical properties of
(\Pi_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{OVB}}})_{t=1}^{T}
under the Gaussian variational family
\mathcal{Q}
.

2.2
Bayesian online one-pass algorithm

Now we introduce a new Bayesian online learning method tailored to the one-pass setting. Accordingly, we focus on the case
n=|{\bf D}_{t}|=1
for all
t
.

In the one-pass setting, solving the ELBO optimization at each iteration incurs a substantial computational cost. To address this, we replace the log-likelihood term in (
2.4
) with its second-order local approximation. This leads to a modified version of (
2.4
):

\displaystyle\Pi_{t}=\argmin_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{Q}\big[\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(\theta)\big]+K\big(Q;\>\Pi_{t-1}\big)\right\},

(2.5)

where
\Pi_{t-1}=N(\theta_{t-1},\bm{\Omega}_{t-1}^{-1})
and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(\theta)=\ell_{t}(\theta_{t-1})+\langle\nabla\ell_{t}(\theta_{t-1}),\theta-\theta_{t-1}\rangle+\dfrac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}\ell_{t}(\theta_{t-1}),(\theta-\theta_{t-1})^{\otimes 2}\rangle.

(2.6)

Under the Gaussian variational family
\mathcal{Q}
, the problem in (
2.5
) admits a closed-form solution. For
Q=N(\mu,\bm{\Omega}^{-1})
, we define the surrogate negative ELBO by

\displaystyle F(Q)=F(\mu,\bm{\Omega}^{-1})=\mathbb{E}_{Q}\big[\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(\theta)\big]+K\big(Q;\>\Pi_{t-1}\big).

The quadratic structure of
\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(\cdot)
yields

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{Q}\big[\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(\theta)\big]=\langle\nabla\ell_{t}(\theta_{t-1})-\nabla^{2}\ell_{t}(\theta_{t-1})\theta_{t-1},\mu\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\nabla^{2}\ell_{t}(\theta_{t-1})\big(\mu^{\otimes 2}+\bm{\Omega}^{-1}\big)\Big)+C,

where
C
is a constant independent of
\mu
and
\bm{\Omega}
.
Moreover, the KL divergence between
Q
and
\Pi_{t-1}
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big(\log|\bm{\Omega}_{t-1}^{-1}|-\log|\bm{\Omega}^{-1}|+\langle\bm{\Omega}_{t-1},(\mu-\theta_{t-1})^{\otimes 2}\rangle+\operatorname{tr}(\bm{\Omega}_{t-1}\bm{\Omega}^{-1})-p\Big).

Combining these expressions, the gradients of
F(\mu,\bm{\Omega}^{-1})
are given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}\,F(\mu,\bm{\Omega}^{-1})

\displaystyle=\nabla\ell_{t}(\theta_{t-1})+\nabla^{2}\ell_{t}(\theta_{t-1})(\mu-\theta_{t-1})+\bm{\Omega}_{t-1}(\mu-\theta_{t-1}),

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\Omega}^{-1}}\,F(\mu,\bm{\Omega}^{-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big(\nabla^{2}\ell_{t}(\theta_{t-1})-\bm{\Omega}+\bm{\Omega}_{t-1}\big).

Solving the first-order optimality conditions yields the closed-form updates:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \bm{\Omega}_{t}&=\bm{\Omega}_{t-1}+\nabla^{2}\ell_{t}(\theta_{t-1}),\\
\theta_{t}&=\theta_{t-1}-\bm{\Omega}_{t}^{-1}\nabla\ell_{t}(\theta_{t-1}).\end{aligned}

(2.7)

Thus, the one-pass update takes a fully explicit recursive form, making it computationally appealing.
Moreover, if
\nabla^{2}\ell_{t}(\theta_{t-1})
is a rank-
1
matrix of the form
u_{t}v_{t}^{\top}
for some
u_{t},v_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
, then the inverse
\bm{\Omega}_{t}^{-1}
admits the recursive representation

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}_{t}^{-1}=\bm{\Omega}_{t-1}^{-1}-\dfrac{\bm{\Omega}_{t-1}^{-1}u_{t}v_{t}^{\top}}{1+v_{t}^{\top}\bm{\Omega}_{t-1}^{-1}u_{t}}\bm{\Omega}_{t-1}^{-1},

which allows us to avoid computationally expensive matrix inversions.

However, the validity of this update relies on a local quadratic approximation and may deteriorate under poor initialization. The update in (
2.7
) can be interpreted as a stochastic analogue of Newton’s method, which is well-known to be sensitive to initialization.
To illustrate this, consider the objective function
f(x)=\sqrt{x^{2}+1}
with minimizer
x_{\star}=0
.
The corresponding Newton update is given by

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=x_{t}-\dfrac{\nabla f(x_{t})}{\nabla^{2}f(x_{t})}=x_{t}-\dfrac{x_{t}(x_{t}^{2}+1)^{-1/2}}{(x_{t}^{2}+1)^{-3/2}}=-x_{t}^{3},\quad t\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},

which converges to
0
if and only if
|x_{0}|<1
.
Hence, a careful initialization is crucial for the stability of Newton-type methods.

To alleviate such dependence on initializations, we adopt a warm-start strategy, which ensures that the estimator is sufficiently close to
\theta_{\star}
before the online recursion begins. To ensure such localization, we employ a batch estimator. To formalize this idea, for the initial prior
\Pi_{0}=N(\theta_{0},\bm{\Omega}_{0}^{-1})
, we introduce the following batch maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimator:

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{MAP}}}=\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}\left\{\sum_{s=1}^{t}\ell_{s}(\theta)+\dfrac{1}{2}\big\|\bm{\Omega}_{0}^{1/2}(\theta-\theta_{0})\big\|_{2}^{2}\right\},\quad t\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(2.8)

We now describe the proposed Bayesian online one-pass algorithm.

(
Alg
)

Let
t_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
If
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
t\leq t_{0}
, we use batch updates:

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}_{t}=\bm{\Omega}_{0}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\nabla^{2}\ell_{s}(\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{MAP}}}),\quad\theta_{t}=\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{MAP}}}.

If
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
t>t_{0}
, we use online updates

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Omega}_{t-1}+{\bf H}_{t},

\displaystyle\theta_{t}

\displaystyle=\theta_{t-1}-\bm{\Omega}_{t}^{-1}g_{t},

where

\displaystyle g_{t}=\nabla\ell_{t}(\theta_{t-1})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p},\quad{\bf H}_{t}=\nabla^{2}\ell_{t}(\theta_{t-1})\in\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{+}}^{p}.

(2.9)

The above scheme consists of a warm-start phase followed by an online update phase.
In (
Alg
), the phase transition time
t_{0}
determines the length of the initial warm-start phase.
For
t\leq t_{0}
, we compute the batch estimator
\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{MAP}}}
based on the full sample
{\bf D}_{1:t}
and set
\theta_{t}=\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{MAP}}}
. For
t>t_{0}
, we switch to a one-pass update. This yields the sequence
(\Pi_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with
\Pi_{t}=N(\theta_{t},\bm{\Omega}_{t}^{-1})
.

The choice of
t_{0}
plays an important role in both theoretical and empirical performance. In Section
3
, we provide a sufficient condition on
t_{0}
under which stable online updates are possible; see (
A5
). Moreover, empirical results in Section
4
illustrate the necessity of the warm-start phase, showing that, in certain settings, omitting it leads to substantially slower convergence.

While the online updates in (
Alg
) resemble those of existing algorithms
(
1
,
10
, e.g.,)
, the ordering of the updates is slightly different. Specifically, existing approaches take the following updates:

\displaystyle\theta_{t}

\displaystyle=\theta_{t-1}-\bm{\Omega}_{t-1}^{-1}g_{t},

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Omega}_{t-1}+{\bf H}_{t}.

The key difference lies in the update of
\theta_{t}
: whether one uses
\bm{\Omega}_{t-1}
or
\bm{\Omega}_{t}
.
This subtle discrepancy results in a significant difference in the theoretical analysis.
Since
\bm{\Omega}_{t-1}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable, we have
{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{\Omega}_{t-1}^{-1}g_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=\bm{\Omega}_{t-1}^{-1}{\mathbb{E}}[g_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]
, which facilitates the analysis.
In contrast, this property no longer holds when
\bm{\Omega}_{t}
is used.
For this reason,
1
modified the ordering of the updates to facilitate the theoretical analysis, while initially considering an update scheme similar to ours; see (3.1)–(3.6) in
1
. This motivates the development of a new theoretical framework tailored to our algorithm, which we present in detail in Section
3.2.1
.

3
Theoretical results

In this section, we establish a theoretical justification for
\Pi_{t}
proposed in Section
2.2
.
Our goal is to show that
\Pi_{t}
becomes asymptotically equivalent to the full posterior
\Pi(\cdot\mid{\bf D}_{1:t})
in an appropriate sense. We refer to this result as the
online BvM theorem
.

We begin in Section
3.1.1
by introducing the assumptions required for the analysis.
In Section
3.1.2
, we establish the
\ell_{2}
-convergence rate of
\theta_{t}
and the online BvM theorem for
\Pi_{t}
. In Section
3.2
, we present the proof strategy and the main ideas underlying the analysis. Section
3.3
highlights the key distinctions between our approach and existing methods. Finally, in Section
3.4
, we verify that the assumptions in Section

Basic setup:

This condition imposes basic regularity, identifiability, and conditions on the initial prior parameters.

(

For each

where

(3.5)

(3.6)

with

(3.7)

The constants

, and

3.1.2
Main results

We now present the main results: the
\ell_{2}
-convergence rate and the online BvM theorem.
The estimation rate of
\theta_{t}
serves as a key ingredient in establishing the online BvM theorem.
We therefore begin by deriving a non-asymptotic
\ell_{2}
-convergence rate of
\theta_{t}
to
\theta_{\star}
for
t\geq t_{0}
. To this end, for
t\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we define

\displaystyle V_{t}=\big\|\bm{\Omega}_{t}^{1/2}(\theta_{t}-\theta_{\star})\big\|_{2}^{2},\quad\Delta_{t}=\theta_{t}-\theta_{\star}.

(3.9)

The quantity
V_{t}
measures the deviation
\Delta_{t}
under the local geometry induced by
\bm{\Omega}_{t}
.
Since (
Alg
) involves the coupled evolution of
(\theta_{t},\bm{\Omega}_{t})
, analyzing
V_{t}
rather than
\|\Delta_{t}\|_{2}
directly is natural in this context.

For
\mathrm{x}>0
, let
\mathscr{E}_{1:4}(\mathrm{x})=\mathscr{E}_{1}(\mathrm{x})\cap\mathscr{E}_{2}(\mathrm{x})\cap\mathscr{E}_{3}(\mathrm{x})\cap\mathscr{E}_{4}(\mathrm{x})
, which collects the high-probability events specified in Section
3.1.1
. In Theorem
3.1
, we show on
\mathscr{E}_{1:4}(\mathrm{x})
that the growth of
V_{t}
remains sufficiently controlled, which in turn yields the optimal
\ell_{2}
-rate up to logarithmic factors.

Theorem 3.1

(Rates of convergence)
.

For given
\mathrm{x},r>0
, suppose that (
A0
)–(
A2
) and (
A4
)-(
A5
) hold.
Then, on
\mathscr{E}_{1:4}(\mathrm{x})
, we have for any
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
t\geq t_{0}

\displaystyle V_{t}\leq K\left(p\log t+\mathrm{x}\right),\quad\|\theta_{t}-\theta_{\star}\|_{2}\leq K\sqrt{\dfrac{p\log t+\mathrm{x}}{t}},\quad\theta_{t}\in\Theta_{r},

where
K=K(K_{2:5})>0
.

We now turn to the central result of this paper: the
online BvM theorem
. Our goal is to establish that

\displaystyle d_{V}\Big(\Pi_{t},N\big(\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}},{\bf F}_{t,\theta_{\star}}^{-1}\big)\Big)\vee d_{V}\Big(\Pi_{t},\Pi(\cdot\mid{\bf D}_{1:t})\Big)=o_{P}(1),

where
d_{V}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the total variation distance:

\displaystyle d_{V}\left(P,Q\right)=\sup_{\mathcal{A}}\left|P(\mathcal{A})-Q(\mathcal{A})\right|,

(3.10)

with the supremum taken over all measurable sets
\mathcal{A}
, and
P
and
Q
denoting probability distributions.
Let
Q_{\textnormal{\rm{BvM}},t}=N\big(\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}},{\bf F}_{t,\theta_{\star}}^{-1}\big)
. By the classical BvM theorem for batch data, it is well-known that

\displaystyle d_{V}\big(Q_{\textnormal{\rm{BvM}},t},\Pi(\cdot\mid{\bf D}_{1:t})\big)=o_{P}(1).

For detailed statements, we refer to the classical asymptotic theory
(
36
, Section 10.2)
and its non-asymptotic counterpart
(
17
, Section 7)
. Since

\displaystyle d_{V}\big(\Pi_{t},\Pi(\cdot\mid{\bf D}_{1:t})\big)\leq d_{V}\big(\Pi_{t},Q_{\textnormal{\rm{BvM}},t}\big)+d_{V}\big(Q_{\textnormal{\rm{BvM}},t},\Pi(\cdot\mid{\bf D}_{1:t})\big)=d_{V}\big(\Pi_{t},Q_{\textnormal{\rm{BvM}},t}\big)+o_{P}(1),

it suffices to bound
d_{V}(\Pi_{t},Q_{\textnormal{\rm{BvM}},t})
, which constitutes our main contribution.
The following theorem establishes this bound.

Theorem 3.2

(Online BvM)
.

For given
\mathrm{x},r>0
, suppose that (
A0
)–(
A5
) hold.
Then, there exists a constant
M_{2}=M_{2}(K_{6},L_{3},\overline{\lambda}_{0})>0
such that, on
\mathscr{E}_{1:4}(\mathrm{x})
, for any
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
t>M_{2}t_{0}
,

\displaystyle d_{V}\big(Q_{\textnormal{\rm{BvM}},t},\Pi_{t}\big)

\displaystyle\leq K\epsilon_{{\rm app},t},

\displaystyle d_{V}\big(\Pi_{t},\Pi(\cdot\mid{\bf D}_{1:t})\big)

\displaystyle\leq K\epsilon_{{\rm app},t}+d_{V}\big(Q_{\textnormal{\rm{BvM}},t},\Pi(\cdot\mid{\bf D}_{1:t})\big),

where
K=K(K_{1:5},L_{1:3},\overline{\lambda}_{0},r)>0
and

\displaystyle\epsilon_{{\rm app},t}=\epsilon_{{\rm app},t}(t_{0},p,\mathrm{x})=\sqrt{\dfrac{\log^{2}(t/t_{0})(p\log t+\mathrm{x})^{2}}{t}}.

(3.11)

Theorem
3.2
ensures that
\Pi_{t}
provides valid uncertainty quantification with explicit finite-sample error rates. For a fixed
\alpha\in(0,1)
, consider the following Wald-type confidence and credible sets:

\displaystyle\widehat{C}_{1:t}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\left\{\theta\in\Theta:\big\|{\bf F}_{t,\theta_{\star}}^{1/2}(\theta-\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}})\big\|_{2}^{2}\leq\chi_{p,\alpha}^{2}\right\},

\displaystyle\widehat{C}_{t}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\left\{\theta\in\Theta:\big\|\bm{\Omega}_{t}^{1/2}(\theta-\theta_{t})\big\|_{2}^{2}\leq\chi_{p,\alpha}^{2}\right\},

where
\chi_{p,\alpha}^{2}
denotes the
(1-\alpha)
quantile of the
\chi_{p}^{2}
distribution. Here,
\widehat{C}_{1:t}(\alpha)
represents the standard frequentist confidence set based on MLE, while
\widehat{C}_{t}(\alpha)
is the credible set based on the sequentially updated posterior
\Pi_{t}
.
We now investigate the relationship between these two sets.
As a consequence of Theorem
3.2
, the following inclusions hold on
\mathscr{E}_{1:4}(\mathrm{x})
:

\displaystyle\widehat{C}_{1:t}(\alpha+\epsilon_{t})\subset\widehat{C}_{t}(\alpha)\subset\widehat{C}_{1:t}(\alpha-\epsilon_{t})

for some
\epsilon_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
with
\epsilon_{t}=O(\epsilon_{{\rm app},t})
. Therefore,
\widehat{C}_{t}
inherits the frequentist validity of
\widehat{C}_{1:t}
at the rate
\epsilon_{{\rm app},t}
.

3.2
Intuition and strategy of the proof

3.2.1
Rates of convergence

We outline the key idea of the analysis. The starting point is the following observation: the sequence
(V_{t})_{t\geq t_{0}}
admits a tractable recursive representation, which forms the basis of our non-asymptotic analysis.
Specifically, we establish a recursive inequality for
V_{t}
of the form

\displaystyle V_{t+1}\leq V_{t}+\epsilon_{t}V_{t}^{3/2}+u_{t+1}\ \text{ on }\mathscr{E}_{1:4}(\mathrm{x}),

where
\epsilon_{t}
is sufficiently small to ensure stability of the recursion, while
(u_{t})
is controlled through a suitable bound on its cumulative sum.
Here,
\epsilon_{t}V_{t}^{3/2}
represents the remainder term arising from the quadratic approximation in (
Alg
). The main difficulty lies in controlling the superlinear term
\epsilon_{t}V_{t}^{3/2}
and the cumulative effect of
(u_{t})
. To handle this difficulty, we imposed the assumptions in Section
3.1.1
, whose rationale we discuss throughout this subsection. Under these assumptions, the desired bound follows from a careful induction argument over dyadic time blocks.

A key feature of (
Alg
), viewed as a second-order SGD method, lies in the randomness of its effective step-size. To clarify this point, consider the following standard first-order update:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\theta}_{t}=\widetilde{\theta}_{t-1}-\eta_{t}g_{t},\quad t\in{\mathbb{N}},

where
\eta_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
denotes the step-size. A careful choice of the sequence
(\eta_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is crucial in online one-pass first order methods
(
34
,
5
, e.g.,)
. It is typically specified deterministically to ensure desirable statistical properties of the iterates.
In (
Alg
), by contrast, the spectrum of
\bm{\Omega}_{t}^{-1}
plays the role of an effective step-size.
Since
\bm{\Omega}_{t}
depends on the past iterates
(\theta_{s})_{s=t_{0}}^{t-1}
, it is inherently random.
Consequently, controlling the spectrum of
\bm{\Omega}_{t}
requires uniform control over the past trajectory. This can be ensured as long as
(\theta_{t})
stays within a suitable local region such as
\Theta_{r}
, in which
\bm{\Omega}_{t}
remains well behaved. Assumption (
A1
) ensures precisely this local regularity: the minimum eigenvalue of
\bm{\Omega}_{t}
grows linearly in
t
, uniformly over trajectories confined to
\Theta_{r}
.

With the local regularity from (
A1
) in hand, we now return to the recursive analysis of
V_{t}
.
Based on (
Alg
), we can establish the following identity (see Lemma
A.1
):

\displaystyle V_{t}=V_{t-1}+\langle{\bf H}_{t},\Delta_{t-1}^{\otimes 2}\rangle-2\langle g_{t},\Delta_{t-1}\rangle+\langle g_{t},\bm{\Omega}_{t}^{-1}g_{t}\rangle,\quad t\in{\mathbb{N}}\text{ with }t>t_{0}.

(3.12)

Although (
3.12
) is exact, it is not directly amenable to analysis.
We therefore extract its essential structure, reducing (
3.12
) to a relation that depends only on the current magnitude
V_{t}
, up to incremental terms whose cumulative effect can be controlled over time. The following lemma formalizes this reduction.

Lemma 3.3
.

For a given
\mathrm{x}>0
, suppose that (
A0
)–(
A2
) and (
A4
)–(
A5
) hold.
Then, on
\mathscr{E}_{1:4}(\mathrm{x})
, we have

\displaystyle V_{t+1}\leq V_{t}+\epsilon_{t}V_{t}^{3/2}+u_{t+1},\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{N}}\text{ with }t_{0}\leq t<t_{\textnormal{\rm exit}},

for some non-negative sequence
(\epsilon_{t})_{t\geq t_{0}}
and real-valued sequence
(u_{t})_{t>t_{0}}
satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{r=s}^{t}\epsilon_{r}\leq Ks^{-1/2},\quad\sum_{r=t_{0}+1}^{t}u_{r}\leq K^{\prime}p\log t+K^{\prime\prime}\big(\mathrm{x}+\log t\big),\quad\forall s,t\in{\mathbb{N}}\text{ with }t_{0}\leq s<t\leq t_{\textnormal{\rm exit}},

where
K=K(K_{2},L_{1:2})
,
K^{\prime}=K^{\prime}(K_{4})
and
K^{\prime\prime}=K^{\prime\prime}(K_{3},K_{5})
.

The refined recursion reveals two key sources of growth: the superlinear term
\epsilon_{t}V_{t}^{3/2}
, and the cumulative increment
U_{t}=\sum_{s=t_{0}+1}^{t}u_{s}
. A suitable control on the growth of
V_{t}
requires balancing these two contributions.

Controlling the superlinear term.

We first consider the superlinear term. To control the growth of
V_{t}
, the term
\epsilon_{t}V_{t}^{3/2}
must remain negligible relative to
V_{t}
. Assumptions (
A1
)–(
A2
) ensure this by imposing linear growth of the eigenvalues of
\bm{\Omega}_{t}
and local smoothness of
\ell_{t}
. These conditions yield
\epsilon_{t}\asymp t^{-3/2}
, so that the cumulative superlinear effect remains controlled.

The phase transition time
t_{0}
plays an important role in this control as well. Beyond ensuring that the iterates enter the local region
\Theta_{r}
,
t_{0}
must be large enough to make
\epsilon_{t}
small at the onset of the recursion: when
\epsilon_{t}\asymp t^{-3/2}
, we have
\sum_{s=t_{0}}^{t}\epsilon_{s}\asymp t_{0}^{-1/2}
, so that a larger
t_{0}
directly suppresses the cumulative superlinear effect. This motivates the requirement
t_{0}\geq M(p\log p+\mathrm{x})
in (
A5
), with
M>0
depending on the regularity constants.

Controlling the cumulative increment.

We next consider the cumulative increment
U_{t}
. Even with
\epsilon_{t}
small,
V_{t}
may still diverge if
U_{t}
grows too quickly: once
U_{t}
pushes
V_{t}
upward, the superlinear term amplifies this growth and can drive
V_{t}
to grow rapidly. To analyze
U_{t}
, we examine the representation

\displaystyle u_{t+1}=2\big(\ell_{t+1}(\theta_{\star})-\ell_{t+1}(\theta_{t})\big)+\langle g_{t},\bm{\Omega}_{t}^{-1}g_{t}\rangle.

Assumption (
A4
) imposes a uniform bound on the partial sums of
(u_{t})
, directly controlling
U_{t}
.

Induction over dyadic blocks.

Lemma
3.3
alone does not yield the desired bound on
V_{t}
. To obtain such a bound, we employ a carefully constructed inductive argument in Proposition
B.2
. The strategy is as follows. For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
t_{k}=(2^{k}t_{0})\wedge t_{\textnormal{\rm exit}}
, and partition the time axis
\{t\in{\mathbb{N}}:t\geq t_{0}\}
into dyadic blocks:

\displaystyle\{t_{0},\ldots,t_{1}\},\quad\{t_{1},...,t_{2}\},\quad\ldots\quad\{t_{k_{\ast}-1},...,t_{k_{\ast}}\},

where
k_{\ast}=\inf\{k\in{\mathbb{N}}:t_{\textnormal{\rm exit}}\leq 2^{k}t_{0}\}
. We assume that
k_{\ast}>2
for simplicity; the general case is covered in Proposition
B.2
.

The induction proceeds along these blocks. Suppose that
V_{t_{k}}\leq r(t_{k},p,\mathrm{x})
for some
k<k_{\ast}
, where
r(t,p,\mathrm{x})
is of order
p\log t+\mathrm{x}
. Lemma
B.1
then ensures that, on
\mathscr{E}_{1:4}(\mathrm{x})
, this bound propagates to the next block:

\displaystyle V_{t}\leq r(t,p,\mathrm{x}),\quad\forall t\in\{t_{k},...,t_{k+1}\}.

Iterating this argument over successive dyadic blocks yields a uniform control of
V_{t}
up to the exit time
t_{\textnormal{\rm exit}}
. See Section
B
for details. Although the above argument is confined to
t\leq t_{\textnormal{\rm exit}}
, the resulting bound in fact implies
\theta_{t_{\textnormal{\rm exit}}}\in\Theta_{r}
, which contradicts the definition of
t_{\textnormal{\rm exit}}
. Consequently,
t_{\textnormal{\rm exit}}=\infty
, and hence the argument extends to all
t\geq t_{0}
. This establishes Theorem
3.1
.

3.2.2
Online Bernstein–von Mises theorem

We now turn to the analysis of the online BvM theorem. Our goal is to establish the asymptotic equivalence between
\Pi_{t}
and
Q_{\textnormal{\rm{BvM}},t}
. To this end, we introduce a useful technical bound. For
Q_{1}=N(m_{1},{\bf V}_{1}^{-1})
and
Q_{2}=N(m_{2},{\bf V}_{2}^{-1})
with sufficiently small
\|{\bf V}_{2}^{-1/2}{\bf V}_{1}{\bf V}_{2}^{-1/2}-{\bf I}_{p}\|_{2}
, Lemma
D.1
yields

\displaystyle d_{V}\left(Q_{1},Q_{2}\right)\lesssim\big\|{\bf V}_{1}^{1/2}\big(m_{1}-m_{2}\big)\big\|_{2}\vee\big\|{\bf V}_{2}^{-1/2}{\bf V}_{1}{\bf V}_{2}^{-1/2}-{\bf I}_{p}\big\|_{\rm F}.

(3.13)

By (
3.13
), bounding
d_{V}(\Pi_{t},Q_{\textnormal{\rm{BvM}},t})
reduces to proving that

\displaystyle\big\|{\bf F}_{t,\theta_{\star}}^{1/2}\big(\theta_{t}-\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}\big)\big\|_{2}\vee\big\|\bm{\Omega}_{t}^{-1/2}{\bf F}_{t,\theta_{\star}}\bm{\Omega}_{t}^{-1/2}-{\bf I}_{p}\big\|_{\rm F}=o_{P}(1).

(3.14)

Propositions
3.4
and
3.5
address the first and second term, respectively.

We now outline the key insight underlying (
3.14
). Given the rates of convergence of
(\theta_{t})
, the second term can be bounded using the smoothness of the map
\theta\mapsto\nabla^{2}\ell_{t}(\theta)
. The main difficulty therefore lies in the first term. Since
{\bf F}_{t,\theta_{\star}}
is of order
t
, bounding the first term in (
3.14
) is essentially equivalent to proving

\displaystyle\big\|\theta_{t}-\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}\big\|_{2}=o_{P}(t^{-1/2}).

(3.15)

Although Theorem
3.1
and (
A5
) imply that both estimators attain (nearly) optimal convergence rates, these rates alone yield at best
\|\theta_{t}-\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}\|_{2}=O_{P}(t^{-1/2})
up to logarithmic factors, which falls short of (
3.15
). A direct analysis of the error between
\theta_{t}
and
\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}
is therefore required.

To obtain a sharp upper bound for the first term in (
3.14
), we show that
\theta_{t}
and
\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}
are minimizers of closely related optimization problems. From this perspective, the difference between
\theta_{t}
and
\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}
can be analyzed via the discrepancy between the objective functions. To formalize this idea, we introduce the following notation.
For
\theta\in\Theta
,
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
t>t_{0}
, let

\displaystyle L_{1:t}(\theta)=\sum_{r=1}^{t}\ell_{r}(\theta),\quad\widetilde{L}_{t}(\theta)=\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(\theta)+\dfrac{1}{2}\big\|\bm{\Omega}_{t-1}^{1/2}(\theta-\theta_{t-1})\big\|_{2}^{2}.

(3.16)

By construction,
\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}
and
\theta_{t}
are the minimizers of
L_{1:t}
and
\widetilde{L}_{t}
, respectively.

The key step is to show that
\widetilde{L}_{t}(\theta)
can be viewed as a perturbed version of
L_{1:t}(\theta)
, from which the closeness of
\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}
and
\theta_{t}
follows.
To make this precise, we define the approximation error between
\ell_{t}(\cdot)
and
\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(\cdot)
as

\displaystyle\eta_{t}(\theta)=\ell_{t}(\theta)-\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(\theta),\quad\theta\in\Theta.

(3.17)

Lemma
A.2
then gives

\displaystyle L_{1:t}(\theta)-\widetilde{L}_{t}(\theta)=\sum_{s=t_{0}+1}^{t}\big\{\eta_{s}(\theta)\big\}+L_{1:t_{0}}(\theta)-\dfrac{1}{2}\big\|\bm{\Omega}_{t_{0}}^{1/2}\big(\theta-\theta_{t_{0}}\big)\big\|_{2}^{2}+\operatorname{const.}

This decomposition reveals two sources of discrepancy: the cumulative approximation error
\sum_{s=t_{0}+1}^{t}\eta_{s}(\theta)
and the warm-start discrepancy
L_{1:t_{0}}(\theta)-\tfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{\Omega}_{t_{0}}^{1/2}(\theta-\theta_{t_{0}})\|_{2}^{2}
.

Building on this decomposition, we now analyze the discrepancy between
\theta_{t}
and
\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}
.
Since
\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}
and
\theta_{t}
are minimizers of
L_{1:t}
and
\widetilde{L}_{t}
respectively, we have

\displaystyle\nabla L_{1:t}(\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}})=0,\quad\nabla\widetilde{L}_{t}(\theta_{t})=0,

which implies that

\displaystyle\nabla

\displaystyle L_{1:t}(\theta_{t})-\nabla L_{1:t}(\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}})=\nabla L_{1:t}(\theta_{t})=\nabla L_{1:t}(\theta_{t})-\nabla\widetilde{L}_{t}(\theta_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=t_{0}+1}^{t}\nabla\eta_{s}(\theta_{t})+\nabla L_{1:t_{0}}(\theta_{t})-\bm{\Omega}_{t_{0}}(\theta_{t}-\theta_{t_{0}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=t_{0}+1}^{t}\nabla\eta_{s}(\theta_{t})+\nabla L_{1:t_{0}}(\theta_{t})-\nabla L_{1:t_{0}}(\hat{\theta}_{t_{0}}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}})-\bm{\Omega}_{t_{0}}(\theta_{t}-\theta_{t_{0}}).

If
\theta_{t}
,
\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}
and
\hat{\theta}_{t_{0}}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}
are sufficiently close and the map
\theta\mapsto\nabla^{2}L_{1:t}(\theta)
is smooth, Taylor expansion gives

\displaystyle\nabla L_{1:t}(\theta_{t})-\nabla L_{1:t}(\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}})\approx{\bf F}_{t,\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}}\big(\theta_{t}-\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}\big),\quad\nabla L_{1:t_{0}}(\theta_{t})-\nabla L_{1:t_{0}}(\hat{\theta}_{t_{0}}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}})\approx{\bf F}_{t_{0},\hat{\theta}_{t_{0}}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}}\big(\theta_{t}-\hat{\theta}_{t_{0}}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}\big),

from which we obtain

\displaystyle\big\|{\bf F}_{t,\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}}^{1/2}(\theta_{t}-\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}})\big\|_{2}\lesssim\left\|{\bf F}_{t,\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}}^{-1/2}\sum_{s=t_{0}+1}^{t}\nabla\eta_{s}(\theta_{t})\right\|_{2}+\left\|{\bf F}_{t,\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}}^{-1/2}{\bf F}_{t_{0},\hat{\theta}_{t_{0}}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}}\big(\theta_{t}-\hat{\theta}_{t_{0}}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}\big)\right\|_{2}+\left\|{\bf F}_{t,\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ML}}}}^{-1/2}\bm{\Omega}_{t_{0}}(\theta_{t}-\theta_{t_{0}})\right\|_{2}.

Consequently, bounding the right-hand side of the above display is the main challenge in the analysis.

The dominant term in the above display is the first one, which captures the cumulative approximation errors. Recall from (
2.6
) that
\widetilde{\ell}_{t}(\theta)
is a quadratic approximation of
\ell_{t}(\theta)
. Accordingly, the gradient of the approximation error

can be written as

\displaystyle\nabla\eta_{s}(\theta)=(\overline{{\bf H}}_{s}(\theta)-{\bf H}_{s})(\theta-\theta_{s-1}),

where

. This representation shows that the approximation error is governed by the local variation of the hessian, and the distance between
\theta
and
\theta_{s-1}
. Hence, if

is locally smooth and

remains sufficiently close to

By the estimation rates in Theorem

with

3.3
Comparison with existing analyses

We now compare our approach with existing analyses of second-order methods.
Consider the following update:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\theta}_{t+1}=\widetilde{\theta}_{t}-{\bf C}_{t}g_{t+1},\quad t\in{\mathbb{N}},

where
{\bf C}_{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{++}}^{p}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable. A key difficulty in analyzing such methods lies in the dependence of
{\bf C}_{t}
on the past iterates
(\widetilde{\theta}_{s})_{s<t}
. The sequence
({\bf C}_{t})
, which serves as a data-driven “preconditioning” matrix, induces a circular dependence: stability of
(\widetilde{\theta}_{t})
requires regular behavior of
{\bf C}_{t}
, while controlling
{\bf C}_{t}
in turn relies on the convergence of
(\widetilde{\theta}_{t})
. Our analysis resolves this circular dependence, without relying on the stringent assumptions commonly imposed in existing analyses. To highlight this contrast, we first review existing approaches and their limitations.

Existing analyses typically bypass this difficulty by imposing structural conditions on
{\bf C}_{t}
.
For instance,
34
consider a deterministic sequence
({\bf C}_{t})
that converges to a fixed limit and satisfies suitable spectral bounds throughout the iteration. Extensions to random matrices
(
20
,
10
)
similarly assume almost sure convergence of
{\bf C}_{t}
. These assumptions effectively decouple the recursion from the evolution of
(\widetilde{\theta}_{t})
, allowing the two sequences to be analyzed independently.

However, these conditions are difficult to verify in practice, since
{\bf C}_{t}
is typically data-dependent and must be estimated jointly with
(\widetilde{\theta}_{t})
. To address this, existing works often first establish almost sure convergence of
(\widetilde{\theta}_{t})
via stochastic approximation techniques, such as the Robbins–Siegmund theorem
(
8
,
23
)
. Under additional conditions, the convergence of
(\widetilde{\theta}_{t})
in turn yields regular behavior of
({\bf C}_{t})
.

Nevertheless, this approach does not fully resolve the circular dependence. Establishing almost sure convergence of
(\widetilde{\theta}_{t})
itself typically requires a priori control of the eigenvalues of
({\bf C}_{t})
. While these eigenvalue conditions are weaker than direct structural assumptions on
({\bf C}_{t})
, they remain difficult to verify. As a result, existing works often resort to non-trivial modifications of the update rule, such as truncation schemes
(
1
)
or diagonal loading techniques
(
10
)
, to enforce the desired spectral control.

Returning to our approach, we analyze the recursion directly through a carefully constructed inductive argument combined with an exit time analysis. This allows us to control the coupled system
(\theta_{t},\bm{\Omega}_{t})
simultaneously, thereby resolving the circular dependence without relying on asymptotic arguments, almost sure spectral control, or algorithmic modifications of
\bm{\Omega}_{t}
.
Consequently, we obtain a fully non-asymptotic characterization with uniform high-probability control over the entire trajectory, reflecting the intrinsic dynamics of the algorithm rather than externally imposed regularity conditions.

4
Numerical experiments

In this section, we present numerical experiments to complement our theoretical findings and to evaluate the empirical performance of the proposed Bayesian online one-pass (BOO) algorithm. Our primary goal is to assess both the estimation accuracy and the validity of uncertainty quantification in an online setting.

We compare our method against several benchmark methods: first-order stochastic gradient descent (SGD), averaged SGD (ASGD), and two variants of the proposed method—BOO without warm-start (
t_{0}=0
) and BOO with
t_{0}=\lceil p\log(p\vee 3)+5\rceil
(corresponding to
M=1
and
\mathrm{x}=5
in (
A5
)). Here, the ASGD algorithm corresponds to the running average of the iterates
(\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{SGD}}})
from SGD; that is, we consider the following:

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{ASGD}}}=t^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\hat{\theta}_{s}^{\textnormal{\rm{SGD}}}.

To ensure a fair comparison in terms of initialization, we additionally consider warm-started versions of SGD and ASGD, denoted by wSGD and wASGD. These estimators share the same initialization
t_{0}=\lceil p\log(p\vee 3)+5\rceil
as BOO with warm-start, and are initialized using the batch estimator
\hat{\theta}_{t_{0}}^{\textnormal{\rm{MAP}}}
. Specifically, wSGD is defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{wSGD}}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\theta}_{t-1}^{\textnormal{\rm{MAP}}},\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{N}}\text{ with }t\leq t_{0}

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{wSGD}}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\theta}_{t-1}^{\textnormal{\rm{wSGD}}}-\zeta_{t}g_{t},\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{N}}\text{ with }t>t_{0}

with step-size
\zeta_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. The wASGD is defined analogously as the running average of
(\hat{\theta}_{t}^{\textnormal{\rm{wSGD}}})
.

We consider generalized linear models, specifically logistic and Poisson regression. For the simulation, we use a total sample size
n=10^{4}
with varying dimensions. For the data generating process, we consider a sequence of observations
{\bf Y}=(Y_{t})_{t\in[n]}
whose joint probability measure is
{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{\star}}
, where
\theta_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a unit-norm vector defined as

\theta_{\star}=\frac{v}{\|v\|_{2}}
with
v=(v_{j})_{j\in[p]}
and
v_{j}=(-1)^{(j-1)}j
.
All reported results are obtained by averaging over 500 independent repetitions.
For SGD, we set the step-size
\zeta_{t}=\zeta_{0}t^{-\alpha}
and tune
(\zeta_{0},\alpha)
. We found that the performance of SGD is highly sensitive to the choice of
(\zeta_{0},\alpha)
.
Accordingly, we fix
\alpha=0.501
, which was empirically observed to yield strong performance, and set
\zeta_{0}=1/2
for both logistic and Poisson regression.

We focus on two types of evaluation metrics. For estimation, we evaluate the
\ell_{2}
-error
\|\theta_{t}-\theta_{\star}\|_{2}
. For inference, we examine the frequentist coverage of the credible set constructed from
\Pi_{n}
.
To conduct statistical inference for SGD, we adopt the

approach in
5

. For a confidence level
\alpha\in(0,1)
and coordinate index

We first consider point estimation performance. Under the independent design, the first-order methods—SGD and ASGD—perform slightly worse than BOO with
t_{0}=29
. In contrast, under the correlated design, their performance deteriorates substantially, indicating that first-order procedures are more sensitive to the dependence structure of the covariates. The BOO estimators exhibit a different pattern. In the logistic model, both BOO with
t_{0}=0
and BOO with
t_{0}=29
achieve performance comparable to that of the MLE, regardless of the dependency structure. In the Poisson model, however, only BOO with
t_{0}=29
remains comparable to the MLE, whereas BOO with
t_{0}=0
shows a noticeable loss of accuracy. Overall, these results indicate that the second-order structure of BOO, combined with a suitable warm-start phase, leads to substantially improved robustness with respect to the design structure.

This pattern becomes more apparent when we turn to uncertainty quantification. Under the correlated design, the coverage probability of SGD collapses, due to the substantial bias of
\hat{\theta}_{n}^{\textnormal{\rm{ASGD}}}
. A similar issue arises for BOO with
t_{0}=0
in the Poisson regression model,
which also exhibits invalid coverage. By contrast, BOO with
t_{0}=29
continues to deliver coverage probabilities close to those of the MLE, together with comparable interval lengths. Consequently, BOO with
t_{0}=29
is the only online method considered here that consistently matches the benchmark performance of the MLE in both estimation and inference.

These findings support two conclusions. First, the second-order BOO method provides robustness to the design structure that is not shared by the first-order alternatives. This robustness is already visible in point estimation, and becomes even more evident in coverage performance. Second, the warm-start phase is essential for stable one-pass Bayesian updating, especially in more challenging settings such as Poisson regression.

5
Conclusion

Despite the growing literature on Bayesian online learning, most existing methods have been guided primarily by heuristic ideas, and rigorous theoretical guarantees have remained scarce.
To the best of our knowledge,
17
provided the first theoretical justification for Bayesian online learning, showing that sequentially updated posteriors can deliver valid uncertainty quantification. However, their analysis requires the mini-batch sample size to diverge, which is typically a stringent condition in the online setting. Motivated by this limitation, we proposed in this paper a new Bayesian online learning algorithm that addresses this shortcoming, and established that it achieves the optimal convergence rate and valid uncertainty quantification under the one-pass online setting.

As a concluding remark, we offer a conjecture regarding the use of the quadratic approximation
in our algorithm. Since Bayesian online learning inherently requires approximating the posterior at each step, we conjecture that, under the one-pass constraint, using the exact likelihood may in fact be theoretically
less
favorable than employing an approximation. This view is empirically supported by the experiments in our earlier work
(
17
, Section 9)
, and we believe it is closely tied to the geometry of posterior projection under consideration. A rigorous theoretical investigation of this phenomenon is of considerable interest, and we leave it for future work.

We assume the following:

1.

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}\bigg(\sum_{s\in I_{t}(C_{2})}x_{s}x_{s}^{\top}\bigg)\geq C_{3}t,\quad\lambda_{\max}\bigg(\sum_{s=1}^{t}x_{s}x_{s}^{\top}\bigg)\leq C_{4}t.

2.

3.

For the phase transition time

4.

Let

\sigma_{t}^{2}=b^{\prime\prime}(x_{t}^{\top}\theta_{\star})

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[e^{\lambda(\epsilon_{t}^{2}-\sigma_{t}^{2})}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]\leq\exp\left(\nu^{2}\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\right),\quad\forall|\lambda|\leq 1/\alpha.

Then, there exists an event
\mathscr{E}(\mathrm{x})
with

such that on
\mathscr{E}(\mathrm{x})\cap\mathscr{E}_{0}

with
t_{0}<t
and

\displaystyle\sum_{s=t_{0}+1}^{t}\langle\nabla\ell_{s}(\theta_{s-1}),\bm{\Omega}_{s}^{-1}\nabla\ell_{s}(\theta_{s-1})\rangle\leq K\Big(p\big(\log(t/t_{0})+1\big)+\mathrm{x}+\log t\Big),

Proof.

For simplicity of notation, we introduce the following notations:

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{t}=b^{\prime}(x_{t}^{\top}\theta_{t-1}),\quad\mu_{t}=b^{\prime}(x_{t}^{\top}\theta_{\star}),\quad\hat{\sigma}_{t}^{2}=b^{\prime\prime}(x_{t}^{\top}\theta_{t-1}),

\displaystyle\delta_{t}=\hat{\mu}_{t}-\mu_{t},\quad g_{t}=\nabla\ell_{t}(\theta_{t-1}),\quad{\bf H}_{t}=\nabla^{2}\ell_{t}(\theta_{t-1}).

\mathscr{E}_{0}

\displaystyle\sum_{s=t_{0}+1}^{t}\langle g_{s},\bm{\Omega}_{s}^{-1}g_{s}\rangle

({\rm i})_{t}

(C.3)

({\rm ii})_{t}
and
({\rm iii})_{t}

C.2
Example: general setup

Proposition C.5

(General version of (
3.6
) in (
A4
))
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
be a measurable space equipped with a filtration
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
.
Let
\Theta={\mathbb{R}}^{p}
be the parameter space, and
\{{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
be a family of probability measures on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
.
Let
\mathbb{F}
-adapted real-valued data stream
(Y_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
\mathbb{F}
-adapted
\Theta
-valued sequence
(\widetilde{\theta}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
be given.
We assume the following:

1.

For
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
Y_{t}
admits a jointly measurable family of conditional densities

\displaystyle(\omega,\theta,y)\mapsto p_{t,\theta}(y\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})(\omega),\quad(\omega,\theta,y)\in\Omega\times\Theta\times{\mathbb{R}}

such that for every
\theta\in\Theta
and all measurable
A\subset{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta}\big(Y_{t}\in A\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big)=\int_{A}p_{t,\theta}(y\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\mathrm{d}y,\quad{\rm a.s.}

2.

For
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the support
S_{t}=\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}:p_{t,\theta}(y\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})>0\}
is independent of
\theta\in\Theta
almost surely.

Let
\ell_{t}(\theta)=-\log p_{t,\theta}(Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
.
Then, for any fixed true parameter
\theta_{\star}
and
\mathrm{x}>0
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{\star}}\bigg(\sup_{t\geq 1}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\big(\ell_{s}(\theta_{\star})-\ell_{s}(\widetilde{\theta}_{s-1})\big)\leq\mathrm{x}\bigg)\geq 1-e^{-\mathrm{x}}.

Proof.

Let
M_{0}=1
. For
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
, define the likelihood ratio process
(M_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
as follows:

\displaystyle M_{t}=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\dfrac{p_{s,\widetilde{\theta}_{s-1}}(Y_{s}\mid\mathcal{F}_{s-1})}{p_{s,\theta_{\star}}(Y_{s}\mid\mathcal{F}_{s-1})}.

By the definition of
\ell_{t}(\theta)
, we have for any
t\in{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\ell_{t}(\theta_{\star})-\ell_{t}(\widetilde{\theta}_{t-1})

\displaystyle=-\log p_{t,\theta_{\star}}(Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})+\log p_{t,\widetilde{\theta}_{t-1}}(Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})

\displaystyle=\log\dfrac{p_{t,\widetilde{\theta}_{t-1}}(Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})}{p_{t,\theta_{\star}}(Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})}.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\log M_{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\log\dfrac{p_{s,\widetilde{\theta}_{s-1}}(Y_{s}\mid\mathcal{F}_{s-1})}{p_{s,\theta_{\star}}(Y_{s}\mid\mathcal{F}_{s-1})}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\ell_{s}(\theta_{\star})-\ell_{s}(\widetilde{\theta}_{s-1}).

Note that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta_{\star}}\big[M_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]

\displaystyle=M_{t-1}\int\dfrac{p_{t,\widetilde{\theta}_{t-1}}(y\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})}{p_{t,\theta_{\star}}(y\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})}p_{t,\theta_{\star}}(y\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=M_{t-1}\int p_{t,\widetilde{\theta}_{t-1}}(y\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\mathrm{d}y=M_{t-1},\quad{\rm a.s.},

where
{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta_{\star}}
denotes the expectation under
{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{\star}}
. By applying Ville’s maximal inequality for non-negative (super)martingale, for any
\lambda>0
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{\star}}\Big(\sup_{t\geq 1}M_{t}\geq\lambda\Big)\leq\dfrac{{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta_{\star}}M_{0}}{\lambda}\leq\lambda^{-1}.

For any
\mathrm{x}>0
with
\lambda=e^{\mathrm{x}}>0
, therefore, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{\star}}\Big(\sup_{t\geq 1}\log M_{t}\geq\mathrm{x}\Big)\leq e^{-\mathrm{x}},

which completes the proof.
∎

Appendix D
Technical lemmas

D.1
Lemmas for main results

Lemma D.1
.

For
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
and
\bm{\Omega}_{1},\bm{\Omega}_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{++}}^{p}
, let
Q_{1}=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{1},\bm{\Omega}_{1}^{-1}),Q_{2}=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{2},\bm{\Omega}_{2}^{-1})
.
Suppose that

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{\Omega}_{2}^{-1/2}\bm{\Omega}_{1}\bm{\Omega}_{2}^{-1/2}-{\bf I}_{p}\right\|_{2}\leq 0.684.

Then,

\displaystyle d_{V}\left(Q_{1},Q_{2}\right)\leq\dfrac{1}{2}\Bigg(\left\|\bm{\Omega}_{1}^{1/2}\left(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|\bm{\Omega}_{2}^{-1/2}\bm{\Omega}_{1}\bm{\Omega}_{2}^{-1/2}-{\bf I}_{p}\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}\Bigg)^{1/2}.

Proof.

By Pinsker’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle d_{V}\left(Q_{1},Q_{2}\right)\leq\bigg(\dfrac{1}{2}K\left(Q_{1},Q_{2}\right)\bigg)^{1/2}.

By the definition of KL divergence,
K\left(Q_{1},Q_{2}\right)
is equal to

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\bigg[\left\|\bm{\Omega}_{1}^{1/2}\left(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{\Omega}_{2}^{-1/2}\bm{\Omega}_{1}\bm{\Omega}_{2}^{-1/2}-{\bf I}_{p}\right)-\operatorname{logdet}\left(\bm{\Omega}_{2}^{-1/2}\bm{\Omega}_{1}\bm{\Omega}_{2}^{-1/2}\right)\bigg].

Let
(\lambda_{j})_{j\in[p]}
be eigenvalues of
\mathbf{B}=\bm{\Omega}_{2}^{-1/2}\bm{\Omega}_{1}\bm{\Omega}_{2}^{-1/2}-{\bf I}_{p}
. Then, the last display is represented by

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\bigg[\left\|\bm{\Omega}_{1}^{1/2}\left(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\log(1+\lambda_{j})\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{2}\bigg[\left\|\bm{\Omega}_{1}^{1/2}\left(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{j}^{2})\bigg]=\dfrac{1}{2}\bigg[\left\|\bm{\Omega}_{1}^{1/2}\left(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\bigg[\left\|\bm{\Omega}_{1}^{1/2}\left(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{B}^{2})\bigg]=\dfrac{1}{2}\bigg[\left\|\bm{\Omega}_{1}^{1/2}\left(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|\mathbf{B}\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}\bigg].

where the first inequality holds by
\max_{j\in[p]}|\lambda_{j}|\leq 0.684
.
It follows that

\displaystyle d_{V}\left(Q_{1},Q_{2}\right)\leq\bigg(\dfrac{1}{2}K\left(Q_{1},Q_{2}\right)\bigg)^{1/2}\leq\dfrac{1}{2}\bigg[\left\|\bm{\Omega}_{1}^{1/2}\left(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|\mathbf{B}\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}\bigg]^{1/2},

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma D.6

Lemma D.7
.

Let
\tau\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Let
\bm{\Omega}_{\tau}\in\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{++}}^{p}
and
({\bf H}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with
{\bf H}_{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{+}}^{p}
for any
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Suppose that

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}_{t}=\bm{\Omega}_{t-1}+{\bf H}_{t},\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{N}}\text{ with }t>\tau.

Then, for any
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
\tau<t
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{s=\tau+1}^{t}\langle\bm{\Omega}_{s}^{-1},{\bf H}_{s}\rangle\leq{\rm logdet}(\bm{\Omega}_{t})-{\rm logdet}(\bm{\Omega}_{\tau}).

Proof.

Let
\tau\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
\tau<t
.
For
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
s\geq\tau
, let

\displaystyle{\bf A}_{s}=\bm{\Omega}_{s}^{-1/2}{\bf H}_{s}\bm{\Omega}_{s}^{-1/2}.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}_{s-1}=\bm{\Omega}_{s}-{\bf H}_{s}=\bm{\Omega}_{s}^{1/2}({\bf I}_{p}-{\bf A}_{s})\bm{\Omega}_{s}^{1/2},

which implies that

\displaystyle\det(\bm{\Omega}_{s-1})=\det(\bm{\Omega}_{s})\det({\bf I}_{p}-{\bf A}_{s}).

It follows that

\displaystyle{\rm logdet}(\bm{\Omega}_{s})-{\rm logdet}(\bm{\Omega}_{s-1})=-{\rm logdet}({\bf I}_{p}-{\bf A}_{s}).

Let
(\lambda_{j}({\bf A}_{s}))_{j=1}^{p}
be a sequence of all eigenvalues of
{\bf A}_{s}
.
Since
{\bf A}_{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{+}}^{p}
and
\bm{\Omega}_{s}\succ{\bf H}_{s}
, we have
\lambda_{j}({\bf A}_{s})\in[0,1)
.
Hence, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}({\bf A}_{s})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}({\bf A}_{s})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{p}-\log\big(1-\lambda_{j}({\bf A}_{s})\big)=-\log\prod_{j=1}^{p}\big(1-\lambda_{j}({\bf A}_{s})\big)=-{\rm logdet}(1-{\bf A}_{s}),

where the inequality holds by

\displaystyle z\leq-\log(1-z),\quad\forall z\in[0,1).

Also, we have
\operatorname{tr}({\bf A}_{s})=\operatorname{tr}(\bm{\Omega}_{s}^{-1}{\bf H}_{s})=\langle\bm{\Omega}_{s}^{-1},{\bf H}_{s}\rangle
. It follows that

\displaystyle{\rm logdet}(\bm{\Omega}_{s})-{\rm logdet}(\bm{\Omega}_{s-1})=\langle\bm{\Omega}_{s}^{-1},{\bf H}_{s}\rangle.

By telescoping sum, therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{s=\tau+1}^{t}\langle\bm{\Omega}_{s}^{-1},{\bf H}_{s}\rangle\leq{\rm logdet}(\bm{\Omega}_{t})-{\rm logdet}(\bm{\Omega}_{\tau}).

This completes the proof.
∎
