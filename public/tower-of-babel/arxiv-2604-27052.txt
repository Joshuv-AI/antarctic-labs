Man, Machine, and Mathematics
Akshunna S. Dogra

Abstract
Nonlinear models and optimization methods have successfully tackled a rapidly growing set of problems in recent years. Indeed, a relatively small toolbox of such models and methods can provide sufficient performance across a large landscape of tasks: deep learning alone has made significant recent contributions in scientific modelling, natural language processing, visual analysis, etc. A similar relationship exists between physical theories and phenomena, where many applications and observations emerge neatly from remarkably minimal foundations. It is natural to wonder if sparse unified frameworks could be built to steer discussion and discovery in the fields concerned with learning, optimization, and modelling. In this work, we posit and examine a possible outline for such a unified theory, interpreting the notion of “learning” in a broad sense. In particular, we pursue our goals by viewing learning as an inter-connected process on multiple levels: problem setup, choosing methods, and the analysis of their interplay via imposed optimisation dynamics. We begin by proposing a precise yet versatile definition for “solvable” problems. We then define the “parametrised methods” by which their solution(s) may be “learned”. Our goal is to sketch a “universal convergence theorem”, specifying how and when solvable problems become amenable to the methods chosen for them. We find these constructions reduce the study of learning down to remarkably few ideas and tools - many of which are simply adapted from existing ones in dynamical systems theory, geometry, and fundamental physics.



Keywords:
Mathematics of Learning; Physics of Learning; Gradient flows; Universal Approximation Theorem;  Deep Learning; Smale-Hirsch Immersion theory; Lyapunov functions; Desingularization; Direct Limits.

Introduction

The broad notion of “learning” underpins biological activity across many levels of life. Indeed, in the human context, it acquires many-fold meaning: the biologically encoded notions of learned behavior, socio-economic activities, the machine based context added by modern computing, etc, all invoke it in myriad ways. We may see its signature in activities as disparate as mammals understanding spatio-temporal positioning, training a classifier, estimating functions using Neural/Kolmogorov Arnold Networks (NN/KAN), etc. Roughly speaking, it is the capacity of both machine and biological entities to incorporate information in their pursuit of some ill-to-precisely defined objective.

Within the computational context, a few modelling methods and heuristic principles are the cornerstones for a significant portion of modern optimisation tasks. Across computational disciplines as varied as natural language processing to numerically solving PDEs, the basic schema of finding
i)
a loss/control map,
ii)
some parameterization of the target space, and
iii)
an apt parameter update rule, is enough to generate sufficiently powerful models of desired solutions
56

14
;
32
;
94

This bears strong resemblance to the influence of unified theories in physics - seemingly complex and unrelated phenomena emerging neatly from sparse and fundamental descriptions. Generalization and unification have been powerful forces in guiding the development of physics for centuries. Formal theories with a sparsity of rules describing a multitude of phenomena are considered objects of great beauty
98
, in addition to their obvious utilities vis a vis analysis and applications. This analogy motivates our search for a sparse and unified theory for learning.

Unsurprisingly, this goal is far too ambitious and ill-defined. We will substantially narrow our focus to characterize learning pursued via “parameterized methods”, where a model and its parameters evolve under some optimisation rule to produce better estimates of the desired target/solution. Even this goal is too broad and ill-defined, but we can now pose questions with a hope of being answered. The following ones lie at the heart of our pursuit:

To what extent can we formalise the notion of a solvable problem and a parametrised method? (Sec.

Yet, somewhat poetically, a lot of the connective tissue between these facets is also supplied by ideas originating in physics: the tools needed for learning about our universe often being the tools that describe how we learn.

An informal summary of the desired results, motivations, and layout of this work

Our first task is generating formal proxies for “well-behaved” or “solvable” problems. Roughly speaking, problems are represented by maps
{\mathbf{F}}:G\to H
acting between apt metric spaces. We call them well-behaved if there exists a “solution(s)”
\Phi\in G
, a neighbourhood
\mathcal{B}_{\Phi}\subset G
, and a loss functional
\mathcal{L}:G\to\mathbb{R}
, such that something resembling the following “gradient flow” can be well-defined, converges to
\Phi
, and minimises both
\mathcal{L}
and
|{\mathbf{F}}|
(Defn.
2.1
):

\frac{d}{dt}g(t)=-\nabla\mathcal{L}[g],\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\mathcal{B}_{\Phi}\ni g(s)_{s\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}\implies g(t)\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}\Phi

(1)

Assuming
G
has sufficient structure to support differentials, variants of the Lojasiewicz inequality
C.18
provide some of the weakest known conditions on
\mathcal{L}
and Eq.
1
for such convergence guarantees. We note its simplest form below:

|\mathcal{L}[g]-\mathcal{L}[\Phi]|^{\alpha}\leq C\|\nabla\mathcal{L}[g]\|,\qquad g\in\mathcal{B}_{\Phi},\textbf{ }\alpha\in[1/2,1),\textbf{ }C>0,

(LI)

However, even if
LI
holds, often
\dim(G)=\infty
and Eq.

\displaystyle\,\,\,\quad\frac{d}{dt}{\mathbf{w}}(t)=-\nabla\mathscr{L}[{\mathbf{w}}],\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\qquad\quad{\mathbf{w}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{M},

(2a)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{A}({\mathbf{w}}(t))=-{\mathcal{A}_{{\mathbf{w}}}}{\mathcal{A}_{{\mathbf{w}}}^{\dagger}}\nabla\mathcal{L}[\mathcal{A}({\mathbf{w}}(t))],\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\mathcal{A}({\mathbf{w}}(t))\in G_{M}

We restrict ourselves to the settings where to have learned something is to have identified something “correct” from a list of possibilities - to have picked out a solution
\Phi
from a target space
G

\mathcal{L}

\mathcal{L}

\Phi

We need to convert this line of reasoning into a versatile yet precise mathematical proxy (as sketched in Sec.
1.1
). However, before getting into specifics, is this a sufficiently powerful structure to support many kinds of “learning” (computational and otherwise)? As an example, when solving differential equations, this is often how existence and well-posedness is shown: one defines an appropriate variational energy
\mathcal{L}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(G)

is a significantly weaker result than we can obtain
54
. Unsurprisingly, it is often possible (and even trivial) to establish that a problem of practical interest is solvable
in principle
. But can Eq.
5
solve it
in practice
?

Unfortunately, Eq.
5
can’t be directly realized on a computer since they cannot handle generic infinite dimensional dynamics. We need a computationally accessible parameterization of
\mathcal{L}
and
G
that inherits the convergence properties of Eq.
5
, even when using parameters nonlinearly. However, to make sense of that statement, we need to define parameterization, architectures, update rules, and the trade-off between linear vs nonlinear methods.

3
The Method (Machine)

Now that we have established a definition for “solvable problems”, let us consider the methods used to solve them. Our focus will be on parametrised methods: those seeking to estimate a solution by iteratively refining some countable number of parameters to produce better and better models. We borrow AI/ML terminology to call the mapping that parametrises
G
using
M
parameters, an “architecture”
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to G
. Its image
G_{M}=\{\mathcal{A}({\mathbf{w}}):{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\}
is called the model set for obvious reasons. A “method” is then simply characterised by the chosen architecture(s)
\mathcal{A}
, its parameters
{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, its models
\mathcal{A}({\mathbf{w}})\in G_{M}
, and the rules for evolving
{\mathbf{w}}
(Fig.
3
a
).

Classical modelling methods are often linear w.r.t. parameters, even when comprised of nonlinear expressions: indeed, many such methods like regression, Fourier analysis, etc, comprise their own well-established fields. However, both ML and biological learning leave this paradigm by using nonlinear parametrisations (Fig.
3
b
), usually through a base function (or activation)
\phi
. We will need results that exemplify the balance between these two choices.

The fitness of a method can be judged by how densely and efficiently its chosen model sets cover
G
. Consider
d(G_{M},\Phi)
, the distance between the best
M
parameter model of
\mathcal{A}
and
\Phi
. As
M\to\infty
for a class of choices, we want
d(G_{M},\Phi)\to 0
. Fortunately, universal approximation theorems already provide this in many
B.1
settings
35
;
66
;
50
. Further, it is relatively simple to ensure that the model set of a linear
\mathcal{A}
is at least
M
dimensional within
G
(
M
-rank matrices are dense within the space of
M\times M
matrices). As such, a nonlinear method is more useful if and only if
G_{M}
has dimension
M
(at least mostly),
while
offering denser coverage than a linear
\mathcal{A}
does.

Euclidean spaces
\mathbb{R}^{M}
will serve as the parameter spaces of choice when
M<\infty
. When
M=\infty
, two choices of considerable interest emerge:
l^{2}
(the space of square summable sequences) and
\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
(the inductive limit of convergence
C.10
on
\mathbb{R}^{M}
- the set of all sequences that are
eventually
non-zero for only a finite number of their elements). However, as
\mathbb{R}^{\infty}\subset l^{2}
and
l^{2}
is a Hilbert space, the reader may keep
l^{2}
in mind for most of what follows.

Once an architecture
\mathcal{A}
is chosen, the next question is how we are to refine its parameters. The natural choice is to use
{\mathbf{w}}
update rules that expect to lower the value of
\mathcal{L}
every time
{\mathbf{w}}
is refined. The properties of
\mathscr{L}[{\mathbf{w}}]\coloneqq\mathcal{L}\circ\mathcal{A}({\mathbf{w}})
and its gradient w.r.t.
{\mathbf{w}}
are critical in such considerations, even if we do not use them explicitly.

Note again that we are making a strong distinction between
parameters
and
data
: the parameters produce models: these models may or may not then be refined using available data.
\mathbb{R}^{M}
represents the parameter space, while
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
will represent the input data space if it is relevant. As a practical matter, the choice of
M
and the parametric loss functions may or may not be related to
d
or the number of available datapoints (say
N
). However, our focus is on the parametric/model dynamics once the choices are made - the role of data is implicit, not explicit.

In summary, the definition of an architecture should incorporate well studied linear (or near-linear) techniques, but with room for the wide variety of modern nonlinear methods. It should be well-suited to working with a finite collection of tunable parameters (a constraint of working with computers), but capable of extension to a countable number of parameters (so we can take limits). It should be regular enough to support gradient flows, but rich enough to produce model sets that cover complete separable spaces with sufficient density, if given enough parameters.

3.2
Parametric optimisation and its connection to gradient flows

We now need a notion that translates the nominal loss and problem into parametrised forms, using the parameters and generated models of an architecture
\mathcal{A}
. Further, we would want to enforce that optimisation under these parametrisations leads to better models. Fundamental notions from Lyapunov and gradient theory are needed here.

Definition 3.6

(Parametric Loss and Gradient Flows)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an architecture. We call
\mathscr{L}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{M}\times G_{M})
a parametric loss function for
\mathcal{L}
, if
\mathcal{L}
is a strict Lyapunov function
C.6
for the following dynamical system:

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathbf{w}}(t)=-\nabla\mathscr{L}[\mathcal{A}({\mathbf{w}}),{\mathbf{w}}],\qquad\qquad\qquad{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathcal{A}({\mathbf{w}})\in G_{M}

Remark 3.7

(Update/Optimisation Rule)
.

Unless stated otherwise, for a given
\mathcal{L}
, we shall define the parametric loss for
\mathcal{A}
by
\mathscr{L}[{\mathbf{w}}]\coloneqq\mathcal{L}[\mathcal{A}({\mathbf{w}})]
and associated update/optimisation rule by the following gradient flow:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\mathbf{w}}(t)=-\nabla\mathscr{L}[{\mathbf{w}}(t)]=-{\mathcal{A}_{{\mathbf{w}}}^{\dagger}}\nabla\mathcal{L}[\mathcal{A}(t)],\qquad\qquad\qquad{\mathbf{w}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{M}

(8a)

\displaystyle\implies\frac{d}{dt}{\mathcal{A}(t)}=-{\mathcal{A}_{{\mathbf{w}}}}\nabla\mathscr{L}[{\mathbf{w}}(t)]=-\Theta(t)\nabla\mathcal{L}[\mathcal{A}(t)],\qquad\quad\textbf{ }\mathcal{A}(t)\in G_{M}

(8b)

where we let
\mathcal{A}({\mathbf{w}}(t))\equiv\mathcal{A}(t),\Theta({\mathbf{w}}(t))\equiv\Theta(t)
, etc. Note that
all
ODEs with a strict Lyapunov function
f
are effectively a gradient flow w.r.t.
f
under the correct coordinate chart
(
3
, Thm. 1)
. Thus, if our optimisation method has
\mathcal{L}
(and therefore
\mathcal{L}\circ\mathcal{A}
) as a Lyapunov function (a natural expectation from any differentiable optimisation schema), the choices made in Remark
3.7
are extremely well justified vis a vis analysis.

We are now able to formalise the notion of having a
good initial model
or more precisely, being well-initialized:

Hypothesis 3.8

(Well initialised)
.

Let
\,\,\frac{d}{dt}{\mathbf{w}}(t)=\mathcal{F}[\mathcal{A}({\mathbf{w}}),{\mathbf{w}},t]\,\,
represent some evolution equation over the parameters of
\mathcal{A}
. We say
\mathcal{A}
is well-initialized under the evolution equation with parameters
{\mathbf{w}}(0)
, if there exists
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
s.t.
\mathcal{A}(t)\in\mathcal{B}_{\Phi}
.
\mathcal{A}(0)\equiv\mathcal{A}({\mathbf{w}}(0))
is called a well-initialized model.

Remark 3.9
.

Note the optimization flows can be imposed using more complex rules than Eq.
8

3.8
is generic, our discussions ahead will strictly lower bound the expected performance of the “optimal” choices.

Well-initialisation may seem like an unreasonably strong hypothesis but methods like stochastic gradient descent (SGD) imply we can often obtain such models if

is non-empty
(

(see App.

phase. Further, “good guesses” are often validated “heuristically” - we do not prescribe formal rules for validating models obtained this way. Regardless, the convergence phase needs better understanding and algorithms, specially when well-initialisation is a practical reality with coarse-grained guesses

;
92
;
103

However, before we can establish the parametric equivalent of Lemma

\theta,\Theta,\mu

in

4.3
Architecture expansions and sparsification

Historically, architectures were built to work with the least number of parameters possible, owing to the laborious human calculations involved and principles like Occam’s razor. Until recently, computational modelling methods also adhered to those philosophies since larger models cost more. In contrast, machine learning (ML) often leans heavily in the opposite direction, with the advent of
powerful computers (and GPUs) having spurred a philosophy of “more is better”
88
. However, sparsification/pruning of architectures (modifying
\mathcal{A}
via removal of redundant parameters to save on computational costs) remains a highly active sub-area due to the high compute costs
9
;
25
;
67
;
93

Thus, the principled expansion and pruning of architectures are important facets of parametrised learning. Poetically, they can be seen as two sides of a single motivation: to change
\mathcal{A}

without
losing performance. We want the simplest model set geometry that can support the optimisation dynamics as efficiently and long as needed.

For example, often
\dim(G)=\infty
. We cannot usually ensure

contains
\Phi

with

(expand, then learn). But how do we add parameters without losing already obtained performance and when do we do so?

A natural idea would be to expand

and access new gradients at local equilibria
\mathcal{A}({{\mathbf{w}}^{*}})

\mathcal{A}({{\mathbf{w}}^{*}})
during the expansion. Ideally, expansions would fit our geometric viewpoint in a manner that recovers the “over-parametrised limit” results too

;
47
, which are mathematically simpler but computationally infeasible.

Similarly, we would want
\mathcal{A}
pruned in a manner that reduces
M
as much as possible
without
impeding the optimisation dynamics. A natural choice would be the non-equilibria points
\mathcal{A}({\mathbf{w}})
where

becomes degenerate

77
.

4.3.2
A prototype for a universal convergence theorem

Further,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\mathbf{w}}_{i+1}(t)=-\nabla\mathscr{L}[{\mathbf{w}}_{i+1}(t)]\implies\frac{d}{dt}{\mathcal{A}(t)}=-\Theta_{i+1}(t)\nabla\mathcal{L}[\mathcal{A}(t)]

4.3.3

We have learnt how to expand

a reverse ECI process that prunes away parameters whenever the model manifold becomes degenerate

In

at

5
Limitations and Future Directions

We have built a framework that covers a large universe of problems and methods
(
21
, Ch. 3)
. However, it would benefit from deeper results and more engagement in the following directions (in increasing order of difficulty):

•

Target spaces: weakening the requirements on
G
to it being a metric and perhaps merely topological space.

•

Model sets: allow more geometrically complex model-sets within
G
than essentially differentiable manifolds.

•

Costs: theory can only be directly tested up to
M\sim 10^{4}
and the tests cost more than the actual optimisation.

•

Update rules: incorporate stochastic
{\mathbf{w}}(t),\mathcal{A}(t)
dynamics alongside essentially differentiable ones. Further, while methods with
\mathcal{L}
as a Lyapunov map are gradient flows, it would help a lot more if we also knew how.

•

Existing theories: other ideas in statistical and deep learning theory can be built atop the framework but it is not immediately clear what we could add to those ideas beyond the facets we have discussed already.

•

Well-initialization: powerful assumption and we offer nothing beyond the “expectation” SGD can provide it.

•

Data: acknowledged to have a strong implicit role (setting up
\mathcal{L}
, choosing
\mathcal{A}
, etc) that we offer no insight on.

We ask
G
and
\mathcal{A}
to have differentiable structure partly because it is useful in proving the non-degeneracy of the model sets. However, gradient flow theories exist and are well understood for metric spaces without Riemannian structure
1
. If
G
could be a metric (or perhaps even topological) space without losing our results on
G_{M}
, we would directly access a wider span of problems. Similarly, if
\mathcal{A}
could parametrise
G
using more complex structures, without losing the geometrical and dynamical properties we have relied upon for convergence results, we would significantly expand the validity and usefulness of these ideas.

Indeed, even though
\mu({\mathbf{w}})
is estimable at each
{\mathbf{w}}
(Sec.
4.1
), the cost of such estimation can scale as
\mathcal{O}(M^{3})
at each
t
. A direct approach to such estimation can rapidly become unviable (modern ML architectures can have
M>10^{12}
). It is perhaps unsurprising that we have only trialled and built applications in the regime of low-cost scientific modelling, where
M\sim 10^{2}
(SHAPER
2
, FINDER
73
) to
10^{4}
(NN DE solvers
18
;
38
;
70
;
89
;
87
, small transformers
77
, and tracking optimization dynamics
17
;
20
;
19
). In this setting, our results can be actually implemented to guide choices and heuristics. If
M\gtrsim 10^{5}
, hope and indirectly probe are the only concrete choices. Further, we only have local convergence rates for a fixed
i
in an ECI: convergence rates to
\Phi
w.r.t.
i
are unknown.

The framework is built with piece-wise regular (essentially differentiable) flows over
\mathbb{R}^{M},G_{M}
in mind. However, while it can directly support stochastic and even more irregular optimisation dynamics, we have no insight on how or why it would be beneficial to do so from within this perspective. Similarly, while there is a coordinate chart under which
good
learning dynamics are gradient flows, unless we explicitly obtain that chart, the practical value of Remark
3.7
is limited to loose bounds. Indeed, if
\dim(G)<\infty
, most of our results are inferior to existing ones
8
.

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) offers us a weak guarantee: we can
expect
to achieve well-initialised states in
due time
(convergence in law). However, we have no generic ways of validating if that actually happens: the desired result would be to say that well-initialisation happens almost surely for any
reasonable
set of initial parameters. Applications can often involve setting up rough estimates using SGD or other external means and then optimizing from them. For example, in
2
, optimization over real datasets happens after obtaining rough pre-estimates using cheaper methods. Similarly, in
18
, ECIs are activated at low loss values
hoping
that implies
\mathcal{B}_{\Phi}
has been reached. Clearly, this is not a completely crippling issue - but it is a critical gap and we have offered nothing on it.

To make matters worse, we have implicitly assumed that more practical matters like conditioning, regularisation, generalisation, etc, are being taken care of by choosing or transforming
{\mathbf{F}},G,\mathcal{L},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{X}
, etc, “appropriately”. This is a significant assumption, and often
the
non-trivial issue in a practical problem. The available data is usually our strongest tool in making these choices. A better unified theory would have a component that caters to how such decisions can be made in a data-driven manner. For example,
{\mathbf{F}}
may be too ill-conditioned in its original form or
G
too sensitive a space to initial conditions. A sequence of data-driven transforms on
\mathcal{L}
or
\mathcal{A}
may exist that converts them from being computationally intractable to feasible: this perspective gives us few hints on what those may be. As of now, other ideas in statistical and deep learning theory are far more useful in such considerations
78
;
95
.

Our distinctive strategies lay in separating the problem and the method at the outset and then showing how their combinations result in properties that can be quantified explicitly and
a priori
, in terms of their independent ones. Future work will focus on resolving the challenges we have noted as rising out of such choices and developing practical applications based on the strengths of our perspective.

6
Conclusions and related works

Our goal was to sketch how a unified theory of learning could be built. We assembled a prototype by (
i
) establishing when a problem is solvable in principle, (
ii
) encapsulating the balance between linear and nonlinear methods to solve it, and (
iii
) investigating how the problem and method mix to govern parametric optimisation profiles.

Unsurprisingly, our endeavours are situated well within the span of a rich existing literature, especially of the last two decades. This work is a synthesis of existing ideas on convex optimization
8
, NTK theory
24
;
47
;
58
, its extensions beyond least square losses
48
and infinite widths
83
;
84
;
99
,
a priori and a posteriori
error bounds for NN DE solvers
18
;
43
;
55
;
59
;
70
, characterization of optimization dynamics
7
;
16
;
17
;
19
;
29
;
57
;
69
, constraint enforcement in scientific ML
18
;
70
, deep learning failure regimes
53
;
85
;
97
, etc. Consequently, our claim and aim is not some radical breaking of the mould: it is the construction of a broad framework that seamlessly encompasses existing empirical observations and theoretical findings, while unearthing new methods and questions.

We broke down the utility of both nonlinear and linear learning methods into three types of properties: density of
G_{M}
in the target space
G
, geometrical and dynamical properties inherited by
\mathscr{L}
and
G_{M}
from
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{A}
, and the optimisation dynamics used to force the models to
\Phi
.
We did not establish novel density results for model sets since Universal Approximation Theorems exist for many choices of
G

;
35
;
50
;
55
;
74
;
86
. However, they were augmented by invoking manifold like structures on
G_{M}
to better study the possible dynamics.

Solvable (or well-behaved) problems were shown to transmit convergence properties to their parametrised analogues by adaptations on the Lojasiewicz Inequality (LI,
62
), especially when paired with the manifold structures invoked on the model sets. The results on forcing a path to the solution by going through a sequence of expansions on
\mathcal{A}
adapt some conventional ideas from Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK

;
57

Thm.
4.4
and
4.14
are key distinguishing features, since the limited setting of over-parameterized architectures has been previously used to characterize optimization dynamics and guarantee arbitrarily good solutions. However, they struggle to describe empirical optimization dynamics, being fundamentally rooted in a computationally inaccessible setting

that forces an essentially convexified framework at odds with ground realities.

Recent modifications
60
;
83
;
84
have gone further to address these limitations: indeed, we are strongly influenced by
60
;
81
. However, they are either limited to problems over finite datasets

;
84

60

19

;
41

We are agnostic to the number of parameters, show how to scale architectures up and down without losing performance, and approach the over-parametrised regime through a feasible and self-contained schema.

C.6
and
[
34
]
), etc. Validation of such models is a nascent discipline
[
72
]
.

B.3
UAP is countability

When
G,H
are Hilbert spaces (or their tangent spaces are), requiring a countable Hilbert dimension is equivalent to assuming separability: both assumptions are not generically true even for Hilbert spaces. However, they are implicit if we are using discrete methods. Absent separability, there would be elements in
G
that can’t be computationally modelled to arbitrary accuracy.

Since we already assumed
G
usually has a countable Hilbert dimension, this assumption essentially requires that the union over the space of finite models is rich enough to be dense in
G
. It is a universal approximation property (UAP) assumption, which is reasonable since UAP for NNs has been proven for large classes of function spaces and NNs
[
39
,
49
,
101
,
102
]
. Indeed, in many ways, such results are one of the bedrocks of machine learning.

C.5
Neural Tangent Kernels

Since the Neural Tangent Kernels serve as an important inspiration and object for this work, we provide a deeper look at how they originated. Let us begin with objects originally termed Neural Tangent Kernel and contrast their heavy reliance on the data and input as a control for optimisation dynamics.

C.5.1
Conventional Neural Tangent Kernels

The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) was introduced in
[
47
]
and has been shown to serve as a control for optimization dynamics in some idealized settings. There are several related objects that the literature may be alluding to when using the term “NTK”, depending on how the parameters of the architecture are initialised, the relationship to the sample set, etc. Here, we note the two most common forms (in the main body, we work with our interpretation).

A standard definition views the NTK as the kernel for the feature map
(\cdot)\to\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{i}}\mathcal{A}({\mathbf{w}})(\cdot)
. Formally, let
\mathcal{A}({\mathbf{w}})(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

\Theta_{n_{1},n_{2}}({\mathbf{w}})(x,y)=\sum_{1}^{M}\Bigg[\frac{\partial}{\partial{w_{i}}}\mathcal{A}_{n_{1}}({\mathbf{w}})(x)\Bigg]\Bigg[\frac{\partial}{\partial{w_{i}}}\mathcal{A}_{n_{2}}({\mathbf{w}})(y)\Bigg]

(16)

where
\mathcal{A}_{n_{i}}({\mathbf{w}})(x)
represents the
n_{i}^{th}
output of the model for an input
x
. This object was shown to be a control for optimization dynamics under the class of empirical risk minimization problems.

Another essentially equivalent formulation, from
[
57
]
, involves
unrolling
or concatenating the model function over the set of sample inputs
\mathcal{X}
, so that we have a function of models over each
x_{a}\in\mathcal{X}
. We then create a Jacobian of this vector of functions with respect to the parameters. Assuming our model is a
J

\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
to
\tilde{\mathcal{Y}}

\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{X}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{A}^{x_{1}}_{1}\\
:\\
\mathcal{A}^{x_{N}}_{1}\\
\mathcal{A}^{x_{1}}_{2}\\
:\\
\mathcal{A}^{x_{N}}_{J}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\qquad\qquad\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{X}}_{{\mathbf{w}}}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}^{x_{1}}_{1}}{\partial w_{1}}&...&\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}^{x_{1}}_{1}}{\partial w_{M}}\\
:&...&:\\
\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}^{x_{N}}_{1}}{\partial w_{1}}&...&\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}^{x_{N}}_{1}}{\partial w_{M}}\\
\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}^{x_{1}}_{2}}{\partial w_{1}}&...&\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}^{x_{1}}_{2}}{\partial w_{M}}\\
:&...&:\\
\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}^{x_{N}}_{J}}{\partial w_{1}}&...&\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}^{x_{N}}_{J}}{\partial w_{M}}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\qquad\qquad\Theta^{\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{X}}_{{\mathbf{w}}}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{X}}_{{\mathbf{w}}})^{\dagger}

C.6
Stochastic Gradient Flows

A well-studied and used stochastic gradient flow that is of relevance to Eq.
8
is given by:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\mathbf{w}}(t)=-\nabla\mathscr{L}[{\mathbf{w}}(t)]+\alpha(t)\beta(t)\xi(t),\qquad\qquad\alpha(t)=\sqrt{\frac{c}{\log(2+t)}}

(17)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{A}(t)=-\Theta(t)\nabla\mathcal{L}[\mathcal{A}(t)]}+\alpha(t)\Bigg[\frac{\beta^{2}(t)}{2}\mathcal{A}_{{\mathbf{w}}{\mathbf{w}}}+{\mathcal{A}_{{\mathbf{w}}}}\beta(t)\xi(t)\Bigg]

is a Gaussian white noise term,
\alpha(t)
is the annealing schedule, and
\beta(t)
is the standard deviation. The corresponding Fokker-Planck equation for the density
\rho_{\mathbf{w}}(t)
is:

\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{\mathbf{w}}(t)=\nabla\cdot\Bigg(\rho_{\mathbf{w}}(t)\nabla\mathscr{L}[{\mathbf{w}}]+\alpha(t)\frac{\beta^{2}({\mathbf{w}},t)}{2}\nabla\rho_{\mathbf{w}}(t)\Bigg)

(18)

Under Eq.

\xi

\mathcal{L}
is coercive and well-behaved, and

is non-empty, almost every

can lead to a well-initialised

: we have a convergence in law to the global minimizer of

[

]
. Once we are near that global minimizer, we can drop the noise terms to recover Eq.
8
with a well-initialised model (we can view the annealing schedule

as the term doing this for us).

Unfortunately, this convergence is not almost surely (it does not happen with probability 1 for all initial conditions). Further, logarithmic annealing schedules are costly and inverse polynomial ones are used in practice: it is common practice to obtain coarse estimates and then find better models through finer methods
[

,

,

]
.
