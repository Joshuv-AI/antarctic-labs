Distorting Kerr Images with Parity-Odd Scalar Hair
Qian Wan, Yehui Hou, Yang Huang, Peng-Cheng Li, Minyong Guo, Bin Chen

Abstract
We investigate thin-disk imaging of Kerr black holes with synchronized scalar hair, focusing on backreacted parity-odd excited states of a complex scalar field minimally coupled to Einstein gravity. The spacetime displays a core-double-torus lensing structure, with a central black hole surrounded by two scalar clouds. We study the dependence of the images on hair strength and viewing angle, identifying a weak-hair regime close to Kerr. With increasing hair, the photon ring and shadow region shrink and become more distorted. In the strong-hair regime, gravitational lensing produces new features, including multiple disconnected shadow components, crescent-shaped structures, and signatures of chaotic lensing. For nearly edge-on viewing angles, repeated equatorial crossings generate nested ring-like patterns. These results highlight possible geometric signatures of black holes with excited scalar hair.



I
Introduction

Directly resolving black holes has long been a major goal in physics. The Event Horizon Telescope has now provided horizon-scale observations of the supermassive black holes in M87 and at the Galactic center
Akiyama et al. 2019
;
Akiyama et al. 2022
, sharpening interest in black hole observations as a probe of strong-field gravitational lensing, because the observed morphology is determined by photon propagation in the near-horizon spacetime. In particular, departures from the Kerr geometry, including self-gravitating hair, can modify null geodesic structures and leave characteristic observational signatures
Cunha et al. 2015
;
Vincent et al. 2016
;
Afrin et al. 2021
;
Rosa et al. 2023
;
Huang et al. 2025a
.

An important example is furnished by scalarized black holes, which arise from a synchronization condition at the threshold of superradiance
Hod 2012
;
Herdeiro and Radu 2014a
;
Herdeiro and Radu 2015
;
Hod 2014
;
Benone et al. 2014
;
Herdeiro and Radu 2014b
;
Kleihaus et al. 2015
;
Herdeiro et al. 2015
;
Hod 2015
;
Herdeiro et al. 2016
;
Delgado et al. 2019
.
Their lensing properties have been studied in a variety of settings
Cunha et al. 2015
;
Vincent et al. 2016
;
Afrin et al. 2021
;
Cunha et al. 2016
;
Gyulchev et al. 2024
;
Huang et al. 2025b
;
Rosa et al. 2023
;
Rosa et al. 2024
;
Gyulchev et al. 2026
;
Chen et al. 2026
;
Deliyski et al. 2026
.
Of particular interest are Kerr black holes (KBHs) with parity-odd scalar hair, in which the excited scalar field produces a qualitatively distinct matter distribution and spacetime geometry
Wang et al. 2019
;
Kunz et al. 2019
. Yet their imaging properties remain unexplored. In these solutions, the energy density forms two toroidal lobes away from the equatorial plane, altering the ergoregion and light-ring structure and, under synthetic celestial-sphere illumination, producing multiple and chaotic shadows
Huang et al. 2025b
. It is therefore natural to ask how these spacetimes would appear under more realistic emission geometries.

Emission from an accretion disk offers a natural probe of geodesic structures in the strong-field region and provides a more astrophysically relevant setting. Compared with background illumination, self-lensing in a hairy KBH–disk system can reveal additional structures, including photon-ring substructure, inner shadows, other novel dark regions, chaotic patterns
Gyulchev et al. 2026
, as well as frequency-shift signatures
Medeiros et al. 2022
;
Bernshteyn et al. 2026
. In this work, we study the optical appearance of KBHs with parity-odd scalar hair illuminated by a thin disk. The spacetimes are constructed numerically by solving the Einstein–Klein–Gordon equations with a stationary, axisymmetric ansatz, and the corresponding observables are obtained through backward ray tracing. We investigate how the bright ring structure, shadow morphology, and associated chaotic features vary with the scalar charge and the observer inclination. By contrasting these results with those for KBHs and for celestial-sphere illumination, we identify observational signatures characteristic of parity-odd scalar hair.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we review the setup of hairy KBHs and main results of parity-odd solutions. In Sec.
III
, we present the imaging results and discuss the characteristic features. In Sec.
IV
, we summarize our conclusions. Throughout this work, we use geometric units with
G=c=1
.

II
Black Hole Scalarization

In this section, we briefly review the basic properties of scalar-hairy black holes and introduce the parity-odd solutions considered in this work. Details of the numerical implementation can be found in Refs.
Huang et al. 2025a
;
Huang et al. 2025b
.

We consider Einstein gravity minimally coupled to a complex scalar field
\Psi
with mass
\mu
, described by the action

S=\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\left(\frac{R}{16\pi}-\nabla^{\mu}\Psi^{*}\nabla_{\mu}\Psi-\mu^{2}\Psi^{*}\Psi\right),

(1)

where
R
is the Ricci scalar. Variation of the action yields the Einstein–Klein–Gordon equations

\displaystyle R_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}R=8\pi T_{\mu\nu},

(2)

\displaystyle(\nabla^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}-\mu^{2})\Psi=0,

with energy-momentum tensor

T_{\mu\nu}=2\nabla_{(\mu}\Psi^{*}\nabla_{\nu)}\Psi-g_{\mu\nu}\left(\nabla^{\alpha}\Psi^{*}\nabla_{\alpha}\Psi+\mu^{2}\Psi^{*}\Psi\right).

(3)

To construct stationary and axisymmetric solutions, we adopt the metric ansatz
Kunz et al. 2019

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}=

\displaystyle-N_{1}\,\mathrm{e}^{f_{0}}\mathrm{d}t^{2}+N_{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{f_{1}}(\mathrm{d}r^{2}+r^{2}\mathrm{d}\theta^{2})

(4)

\displaystyle+N_{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{f_{2}}r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\,(\mathrm{d}\varphi-W\,\mathrm{d}t)^{2},

where
f_{i}(r,\theta)
(
i=0,1,2
) and
W(r,\theta)
are unknown functions, and

N_{1}(r)=\left(\frac{r-r_{h}}{r+r_{h}}\right)^{2},\quad N_{2}(r)=\left(1+\frac{r_{h}}{r}\right)^{4}.

(5)

Here
r_{h}\geq 0
denotes the horizon location.
The scalar field is assumed to take the bound-state form

\Psi(x^{\mu})=\phi(r,\theta)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(m\varphi-\omega t)},

(6)

with frequency
\omega<\mu
and azimuthal number
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Backreaction generically makes
\phi(r,\theta)
non-separable.
Regularity at the horizon and the absence of scalar flux imply the synchronization condition
Herdeiro and Radu 2014a

\omega=m\,\Omega_{h}\,,

(7)

where
\Omega_{h}
is the horizon angular velocity.
This condition ensures that the scalar field is invariant along the horizon generator
\chi=\partial_{t}+\Omega_{h}\partial_{\varphi}
, i.e.
\mathcal{L}_{\chi}\Psi=0
, so that no scalar flux crosses the horizon. Physically, it corresponds to the threshold of superradiance
Press and Teukolsky 1972
;
Brito et al. 2015
: modes with
\omega<m\Omega_{h}
are amplified, while those with
\omega>m\Omega_{h}
are absorbed, and only the synchronized case allows stationary bound states.
At spatial infinity, asymptotic flatness requires the metric to approach Minkowski spacetime, while the scalar field decays as

\phi\sim r^{-\alpha}e^{-\sqrt{\mu^{2}-\omega^{2}}\,r},

(8)

reflecting the localized bound state. The power-law factor encodes subleading Coulomb-type corrections and does not affect the leading exponential falloff.

On the symmetry axis, elementary flatness excludes conical singularities. The behavior of the scalar field across the equatorial plane is determined by its parity. For parity-odd configurations,

\phi(r,\theta)=-\phi(r,\pi-\theta),\quad\Rightarrow\quad\phi(r,\pi/2)=0,

(9)

so that the scalar field possesses a nodal plane at the equator. Although the scalar amplitude is antisymmetric, the energy-momentum tensor remains equatorially symmetric, being quadratic in the field. As a result, the scalar energy density is redistributed away from the equatorial plane, forming two off-equatorial lobes
Wang et al. 2019
.

This nontrivial angular structure modifies the multipolar distribution of the matter content and, consequently, the spacetime geometry, in contrast to parity-even configurations where the scalar field typically peaks at the equator. Such differences are expected to affect the effective potential for geodesic motion, providing a distinctive imprint on strong-field observables.

The spacetime is characterized by conserved global charges: the ADM mass
M
and total angular momentum
J
, together with a Noether charge associated with the global
U(1)
symmetry,

Q_{\Psi}=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\bm{x}\sqrt{-g}\,j^{0},\quad j^{\mu}=\mathrm{i}\bigl(\Psi\nabla^{\mu}\Psi^{*}-\Psi^{*}\nabla^{\mu}\Psi\bigr).

(10)

For stationary, axisymmetric solutions described by Eq.(
4
), Eq.(
6
), the ADM mass and angular momentum admit the decomposition

M=M_{h}+M_{\Psi},\quad J=J_{h}+J_{\Psi},

(11)

where
M_{\Psi}
,
J_{\Psi}=mQ_{\Psi}
are the scalar-field contributions
Herdeiro and Radu 2014a
. The quantities
M_{h}
and
J_{h}
, defined by Komar integrals on the horizon, satisfy the Smarr relation
M_{h}=2T_{h}S+2\Omega_{h}J_{h}

Herdeiro and Radu 2015
. To quantify the scalar contribution, we further introduce

q=\frac{J_{\Psi}}{J},

(12)

which measures the fraction of angular momentum stored in the scalar field. For co-rotating solutions,
q\in[0,1]
: the Kerr limit is recovered as
q\to 0
, while
q\to 1
approaches a boson-star configuration
Kaup 1968
;
Ruffini and Bonazzola 1969
.

III
Disk Imaging

In this section, we study the image of the hairy black hole illuminated by an equatorial disk. To extract main morphological features, we adopt a geometrically thin, optically thin disk with a synthetic emissivity profile. This simplified setup captures the essential lensing signatures while minimizing astrophysical uncertainties.

III.1
Disk emission

We model the accretion flow as a stationary, equatorial disk extending from the horizon to an outer radius
r_{\rm out}=50M
, beyond which the emission is negligible. For the class of solutions considered here, the equatorial circular geodesics admit an innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO) outside the horizon. Outside the ISCO, matter moves on Keplerian geodesics with four-velocity
u^{\mu}=u^{t}(1,0,0,\Omega_{\rm K})
, where

\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm K}=\frac{-\partial_{r}g_{t\phi}+\sqrt{(\partial_{r}g_{t\phi})^{2}-\partial_{r}g_{\phi\phi}\,\partial_{r}g_{tt}}}{\partial_{r}g_{\phi\phi}},

(13)

\displaystyle u^{t}=\sqrt{\frac{-1}{g_{\phi\phi}\Omega_{\rm K}^{2}+2g_{t\phi}\Omega_{\rm K}+g_{tt}}}.

following from the normalization condition
u^{\mu}u_{\mu}=-1
and the geodesic equations in a stationary, axisymmetric spacetime
Chandrasekhar and Thorne 1985
.
Inside the ISCO, stable circular motion ceases and the plasma plunges toward the black hole. We approximate this region by a ballistic flow that conserves the specific energy
E_{\rm ISCO}
and angular momentum
L_{\rm ISCO}
inherited from the ISCO. The four-velocity is then given by

\displaystyle u^{t}=-g^{tt}E_{\rm ISCO}+g^{t\phi}L_{\rm ISCO},

(14)

\displaystyle u^{\phi}=-g^{t\phi}E_{\rm ISCO}+g^{\phi\phi}L_{\rm ISCO},

\displaystyle u^{r}=-\sqrt{\frac{g_{tt}(u^{t})^{2}+2g_{t\phi}u^{t}u^{\phi}+g_{\phi\phi}(u^{\phi})^{2}+1}{-g_{rr}}},

where the negative sign of
u^{r}
corresponds to inward motion.

Photons emitted from the disk are strongly lensed before reaching a distant observer and may intersect the equatorial plane multiple times. Each intersection contributes independently to the observed intensity. Using the Lorentz-invariant relation
I_{\nu}/g^{3}=\text{const.}
along null geodesics
Lindquist 1966
, the total observed specific intensity can be written as

I_{\nu_{o}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\text{max}}}g_{n}^{3}f_{n}J_{n}\,,

(15)

where
n
labels the
n
-th equatorial crossing,
g_{n}=\nu_{o}/\nu_{n}
is the redshift factor, and
J_{n}
is the local emissivity evaluated at the emission point. The factor
f_{n}
accounts for the incident angle dependence; for simplicity we set
f_{n}=1
, which has been shown not to qualitatively affect the image morphology
Gralla et al. 2019
.

For the emissivity, we adopt a synthetic, isotropic profile of the form

\ln J(r)=p_{1}\ln\left(r/M\right)+p_{2}\left[\ln\left(r/M\right)\right]^{2},

(16)

with parameters
p_{1}=-2
and
p_{2}=-1/2

Chael et al. 2021
. This phenomenological model reproduces the radially concentrated emission expected in realistic accretion flows.

Although simplified, this disk prescription captures the main features of black hole images
Hou et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
;
Meng et al. 2025
;
He et al. 2026
;
Ou et al. 2026
. More sophisticated effects, such as finite disk thickness or anisotropic emission
Hou et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
;
Zhou et al. 2026
;
Wan et al. 2026
, primarily modify fine structures without altering the shadow and photon ring. Therefore, the qualitative features discussed below are expected to be robust.

III.2
Image Morphology

We compute black hole images using a standard backward ray-tracing scheme. To enable a consistent comparison across different solutions, the observer is fixed as a zero-angular-momentum observer located at
r_{o}=100M
.

III.2.1
Small scalar charge regime

We begin with the small scalar charge regime. Fig.
2
shows the images of
\text{I}_{0.304}^{0.01}
for several viewing angles. The overall morphology closely resembles that of a vacuum KBH: an asymmetric central dark region surrounded by a narrow bright ring. The bright ring corresponds to the photon ring, formed by photons that orbit the black hole and cross the emission region multiple times
Gralla et al. 2019
, while the central dark region represents the direct image of the equatorial horizon (the inner edge of the disk), often referred to as the inner shadow
Chael et al. 2021
.

A direct comparison with the corresponding vacuum KBH (same ADM mass
M
and angular momentum
J
) shows that a moderate scalar charge primarily induces an overall rescaling of the image, without significantly altering its detailed morphology. As the inclination angle
\theta_{o}
increases, both the hairy and vacuum cases exhibit the same qualitative evolution: the photon ring transitions from nearly circular to a characteristic “D-shape”, while the inner shadow becomes progressively flattened into a hat-like structure.

Stronger scalar hair could modify the size and shape of the rings and shadows.
To quantify these differences, we introduce geometric diagnostics for both the photon ring and the inner shadow. For a given closed convex curve, we define its centre on the image plane as

x_{c}=(x_{\text{max}}+x_{\text{min}})/2
and
y_{c}=(y_{\text{max}}+y_{\text{min}})/2
,
where the subscripts “max” and “min” denote the maximum and minimum values of the abscissa (ordinate).

;
Cunha et al. 2015

(17)

where
i=\mathrm{PR}
and
\mathrm{IS}
denote the photon ring and inner shadow, respectively. We also define the average radius as

Owing to its parity-odd structure, the orbital dynamics and photon trajectories are strongly altered, giving rise to disconnected shadow regions and signatures of chaotic scattering, similar to that reported in
Gyulchev et al. 2026
.

For nearly face-on views (left column of Fig.
3
), the central shadow shrinks as
q
increases. At the same time, a secondary dark region emerges at larger radii, evolving from a crescent into a ring, and eventually giving way to a bright crescent-like structure as
q\to 1
(bottom-left panel). We have verified that both disconnected dark regions correspond to direct projections of the equatorial horizon, i.e., null geodesics that connect the equatorial horizon to the observer without re-crossing the equatorial plane. In the horizonless limit (boson star), one expects the inner shadow to disappear for typical accretion models
Olivares et al. 2020
;
Rosa et al. 2023
.
Compared to the celestial-sphere illumination results shown in Fig.
6
in the Appendix, disk imaging provides additional information about strong lensing by such hairy black holes.

To elucidate the origin of the crescent-like structure, we analyze photon trajectories associated with selected points on the observer’s screen for the solution
\text{V}_{0.997}^{0.01}
at
\theta_{o}=5^{\circ}
(Fig.
4
). The trajectories exhibit qualitatively distinct behaviors. Some photons (e.g., red curve) cross the equatorial plane multiple times, indicating the presence of unstable photon orbits
Gralla et al. 2019
. Others (purple and green curves) are captured by the horizon, forming the inner and outer shadow regions. In contrast, photons in between (yellow curve) escape to infinity, producing bright structures between the shadows.

Overlaying these trajectories with the scalar field distribution
|\phi(r,\theta)|^{2}
reveals that the scalar hair induces strong, spatially inhomogeneous lensing. In particular, it can deflect trajectories that would otherwise miss the observer (e.g., green curve), while frame dragging further distorts photon paths (yellow curve), leading to multiple inflection points in their projections. This interplay between scalar-field-induced lensing and rotation underlies the formation of disconnected shadows and complex image morphology.

As the viewing angle increases, the image becomes progressively more distorted: shadow boundaries develop irregular shapes, and merging or splitting of shadow components can occur. Simultaneously, relativistic Doppler boosting enhances emission from approaching regions of the disk and suppresses receding ones, producing a pronounced brightness asymmetry. In addition, some images exhibit irregular, fine-scale brightness patterns, which are indicative of chaotic scattering, i.e., a sensitive dependence of photon trajectories on their initial conditions

These maps reveal the presence of nested, ring-like structures composed of photon trajectories undergoing multiple equatorial crossings. Their boundaries are highly intricate and correlate closely with the shadow and bright ring features seen in the images, suggesting that they originate from nearby families of unstable photon orbits undergoing repeated winding. In some regions, photons cross the equatorial plane hundreds or even thousands of times, indicating strong oscillatory motion.
The fraction of high-crossing trajectories increases with the viewing angle, implying that inclined observers probe regions where photons are more likely to undergo repeated near-equatorial oscillations. Nevertheless, even for

IV
Summary

We investigated the thin-disk images of Kerr black holes with parity-odd scalar hair and compared them with those of vacuum Kerr black holes, examining how the image morphology changes as the scalar charge increases. For moderate scalar charge
q
, the hairy solutions largely preserve the lensing structure of their Kerr counterparts, with the dominant effect being a reduction in the sizes of the photon ring and inner shadow.

As
q
becomes sufficiently large, the image morphology undergoes a qualitative transition. In this regime, the scalar cloud dominates the exterior geometry and acts as an additional lens outside the horizon. The resulting core–double-halo lensing structure reorganizes the null geodesic flow and gives rise to disconnected shadow regions, chaotic ring-like patterns, and, near the extremal scalarized limit, the near disappearance of the central shadow. Relative to celestial-sphere illumination, disk emission partially masks some shadow features and can reduce the apparent size of the dark region. At the same time, the combination of gravitational lensing, redshift, and disk emissivity produces a richer and more observationally relevant phenomenology, including strong brightness asymmetry, bright arcs, and elongated dark bands.

Taken together, these results identify a set of imaging signatures that could distinguish parity-odd scalarized black holes from Kerr black holes, especially in the strong-scalarization regime where the lensing structure is qualitatively modified. A natural next step is to move beyond the idealized thin-disk model considered here and examine these spacetimes in dynamical accretion flows, where time variability and plasma effects may reshape the observable signatures and determine which features remain robust. This direction is reinforced by recent studies showing that time-dependent simulations provide a powerful framework for discriminating among a broad class of non-Kerr spacetimes
Huang et al. 2018
;
Jiang et al. 2024
;
Uniyal et al. 2025
;
Xia et al. 2026
. In such magnetized plasma environments, it would also be worthwhile to investigate complementary observables, particularly light curves and spatially resolved polarimetric signatures.
