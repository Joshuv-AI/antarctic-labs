Standardized nursing terminologies in primary care: a qualitative approach to the experience of nursing professionals
Authors: Raquel Sánchez-Ruano, Marcos Pascual-García, Pedro López-Paterna, Manuel Rich-Ruiz, Elena Polentinos-Castro, Cristina María Lozano-Hernández, Ana Palmar-Santos, Angel Martín-García, Milagros Rico-Blázquez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStandardized Nursing Terminologies are used to describe diagnoses, interventions, and care outcomes. This study explores the use of Standardized Nursing Terminologies among Primary Care nurses, particularly in the context of frailty syndrome, and identifies barriers to and facilitators of their integration into clinical practice.MethodsDescriptive-interpretive qualitative study with a phenomenological approach. Purposive and snowball sampling techniques were used to select 33 Primary Care nurses involved in healthcare, teaching, management, and research in Spain. Three focus groups and fourteen semi-structured interviews were conducted in 2024. The qualitative data were transcribed, aggregated, analysed, and interpreted using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsThree 3 main themes related to the use of Standardized Nursing Terminologies were identified: (1) The value of a common language: professional autonomy; (2) From standardization to action: integration into clinical practice; and (3) Skills-building: towards Nursology. Participants described language standardization as a driving force for professional development and the use of real-world data in research. The NANDA-I, NIC, and NOC terminologies were the most widely used; however, they generated certain ambivalence due to their complexity, cost, and need for regular updates, and were seldom used in clinical practice. Study participants identified several facilitators of their use, such as their usefulness in structuring clinical reasoning, improving continuity of care, planning resources, and addressing complex care. They also identified barriers, primarily insufficient training, poor usability, scant enforcement, bureaucratic approach, and the absence of a clearly defined organizational strategy. In the context of frailty syndrome, Standardized Nursing Terminologies were reported to promote comprehensive care, although their use was limited, training scarce, and evidence of their impact lacking. Participants also referred to the role of Nursology in fostering research in person-centred, outcome-oriented care, and highlighted leadership as key to strengthening a professional identity based on a reflective and thoughtful-critical approach to nursing.ConclusionsStandardized Nursing Terminologies hold significant potential to transform nursing care; however, their integration into Primary Care remains limited. Frailty syndrome illustrates the gap between this potential and its practical implementation. Bridging this gap requires targeted training strategies, strong institutional support, and leadership committed to fostering person-centred, evidence-based practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04745-2.Keywords: Standardized nursing terminology, Nursing diagnosis, Primary health care, Community health nursing, Tool use behaviour, Professional practice, Professional practice gaps, Qualitative research

Introduction

Standardized Nursing Terminologies (SNT) are a structured set of terms that describe clinical judgements (diagnoses), interventions and outcomes in nursing practice [1, 2]. One of the most widely used language systems is the NNN, which consists of the NANDA International (NANDA-I) taxonomy for diagnoses, the Nursing Interventions Classification (NIC) [3], and the Nursing Outcomes Classification (NOC) [4]. NANDA-I defines a nursing diagnosis (ND) as “a clinical judgement concerning a human response to health conditions / life processes, or a susceptibility to that response, that is recognized in an individual, family, or community. A nursing diagnosis provides the basis for selection of nursing interventions to achieve outcomes for which the nurse has accountability” [5, 6].

SNTs help standardize clinical record-taking [7] and promote their use in research and in the development of evidence-based practice guidelines [8–11]. However, according to international studies, these terminologies do not meet quality standards and remain underutilized. Limitations identified include incomplete coverage of relevant clinical concepts, limited empirical validation, challenges in interoperability between electronic systems, and inconsistent application in clinical practice [12, 13]. Nevertheless, a positive attitude toward NDs increases their adoption in clinical practice. use. A recent systematic review evaluating the impact of nursing record systems on clinical practice and healthcare outcomes called for qualitative studies exploring the relationship between practice and information use [14].

In Spain, nurses represent nearly half of the workforce within the National Health System [15], they still find it difficult to systematically and accurately document the challenges they address in patient care [16], leading to their contributions to health outcomes often being overlooked [17].

NANDA-I was first introduced in Spain in 1996, followed by NIC and NOC in 2004. All these taxonomies have gradually been included in electronic medical records (EMR) [18] and clinical reports since 2010 [19, 20], particularly in Primary Care (PC). In certain regions, other systems that are more akin to natural language are employed, such as the Spanish ATIC (Architecture, Terminology, Interface-information and Knowledge) terminology [21–24].

In a recent study in more than 13,000 complex chronic patients, the authors observed that 84% of EMRs contained functional parameters recorded by nurses, but only 47% contained NDs [25]. Although 23% of patients presented clinical frailty, only 9% of EMRs contained an ND of “Frailty Syndrome” suggesting that this condition is underdiagnosed. These data illustrate the need to improve the identification and recording of frailty in PC, particularly in view of the growing international interest in the diagnosis, treatment and prevention of this syndrome [26, 27]. The prevalence of frailty syndrome - a process that compromises functionality and increases the risk of geriatric syndromes − [28] ranges from 4.9% to 27.3%, depending on the definition used [27, 29, 30]. Due to its multifactorial, multimorbid nature, frailty syndrome should be treated on a case-by-case basis [31], particularly in PC [32, 33].

Nurses identify signs of frailty by observing the patient’s everyday activities, such as the time taken to open a door or to walk from one point to another [34], by identifying signs of Fried’s phenotype [35], or by administering the Tilburg Frailty Indicator [36] or the SPPB (Short Physical Performance Battery) test [37]. NANDA-I includes specific NDs such as “Risk for frailty syndrome (NANDA 00357)” and “Frailty Syndrome (NANDA 00353) " [6] that give nurses a degree of autonomy in addressing this syndrome [38].

In Spain, various guidelines on frailty syndrome have been published, including the Ministry of Health’s “Consensus Document on the Prevention of Frailty in the Elderly” [39], which assigns nurses the responsibility of diagnosing these individuals and assessing home environments and associated risks. Family and Community Health nurses, one of the 7 nursing specialties in Spain [40], have advanced skills in the management of people with chronic diseases. They also take measures to protect the health and safety of adults in their family and community environment, a context especially conducive to addressing frailty syndrome [41].

The primary objective of this study was to explore the extent to which nurses use SNTs in PC, particularly in the context of frailty syndrome. The secondary objective was to identify the barriers to and facilitators of the use of SNTs in clinical practice.

Methods

Design

This study was conducted within a phenomenological framework involving focus groups and in-depth interviews [42, 43]. Focus groups were used to explore group dynamics and understand shared perspectives, providing a broader view of the collective experience. Individual interviews offered more in-depth insight into the experience of participants who were experts in SNTs or frailty syndrome, or who held managerial positions. These profiles were considered potentially disruptive to group dynamics, as differences in expertise or hierarchical status may influence group interaction and inhibit open discussion among participants [44].

To ensure adequate reporting, the study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies (COREQ) checklist.

Setting

Primary care in Spain, is delivered within the Spanish Healthcare system, which is publicly financed and provides universal coverage and free access at the point of care. Healthcare organization is decentralized, and primary care services are managed by the different autonomous regions. As a result, electronic medical record systems vary across regions and there is no single national platform. However, primary care activity is partially standardized through a national minimum dataset used for health system monitoring and research [45]. The implementation and use of standardized nursing terminologies also differ between regional health services, leading to heterogeneous levels of adoption in clinical practice. Care for patients with frailty is guided by national recommendations issued by the Ministry of Health [39], which are subsequently adapted and implemented by regional health authorities according to their organizational contexts.

Participants and sampling

Purposive sampling was initially used in order to maximize the diversity. Initial participants were identified through key informants and professional networks, including nursing associations and scientific forums related to standardized nursing terminologies and frailty care. In subsequent phases, the preliminary analysis guided participant selection. Variations in the data collected led the research team to consider unanticipated nursing roles, such as resident training, information systems management, and healthcare research. These additional subjects were recruited using snowball sampling, in which existing participants referred colleagues with relevant expertise in these emerging fields.

Data collection

This study was conducted by a team of 8 nurses (4 women and 4 men) and a physician who specializes in anthropology (female), all of whom are engaged in academic, research, or clinical practice within the Spanish National Health System. After consulting the literature, the research team drew up an interview guide consisting of a series of broad, open-ended questions. The initial script was piloted [48] with a group of 4 nurses, which allowed the identification of terms that were unclear and the reordering of questions to facilitate a more natural flow of conversation. All modifications were incorporated into the final version (Additional file 2). The guide and all interviews were conducted in Spanish. To ensure data accuracy and cultural equivalence in reporting, the transcripts were translated into English by professional translators with expertise in qualitative health research, and the translations were subsequently carefully reviewed by the research team.

In order to avoid a more clinical setting, the focus groups were conducted in person in a university lecture hall in Madrid. Individual interviews with participants from the Canary Islands, Madrid, and the Basque Country were conducted using video conferencing.

Focus groups lasted between 120 and 180 min and the interviews between 60 and 90 min. The focus groups were led by the principal investigator with a senior researcher as observer. The interviews were conducted by the principal investigator. Notes were taken during the interview. No other people were present.

Both the principal and senior investigators are female nurses with experience in interviews and focus groups. The interviewer had no prior personal relationship with any of the participants. At the start of the discussion, the interviewer introduced herself by name. The participants also outlined their academic background.

The recordings were transcribed by the principal investigator and reviewed by the rest of the team. As the transcripts were deemed to be faithful to the source material, there was no need to check the content with the interviewees or to re-interview the participants; nevertheless, in some cases standard punctuation was added to improve legibility.

Data analysis

Four investigators from different professional backgrounds analysed the transcripts and field notes using descriptive-interpretive thematic analysis based on the phases suggested by Braun and Clarke [46, 49]. The thematic analysis checklist is included in the supporting information.

During the analysis, each researcher involved in the analysis recorded their personal reflections in a diary. Minutes were taken of the analysis process and the agreements reached in each session, and all decisions taken on the theory and methodology were recorded in a research diary that was made available to the entire team. The analysis was performed by pairs of researchers who triangulated their findings before reaching a consensus that was then shared with the rest of the research team.

By repeatedly reading and checking the texts using an inductive-deductive approach, the team was able to generate themes and subthemes related to the study objectives from a hermeneutic perspective and compiled an index of codes assigned to meaningful data. This process was carried out organically and reflectively, with the themes being the final product of the iterative coding. Atlas.ti (version 25) was used to help organize and code the data.

From this perspective, the subjectivity of the researchers is considered an analytical tool Analytical rigor was ensured by soliciting input from co-researchers and ‘critical friends’ to enhance the depth and quality of the analysis [50].

The research followed the criteria of credibility, transferability, reliability and confirmability proposed by Guba and Lincoln [51]. The aim of the sampling strategy was to recruit individuals from many different backgrounds and fields of expertise who could provide an in-depth analysis of the phenomenon being studied. To facilitate transferability, a detailed description of the setting and the characteristics of the participants was drawn up. The confirmability of the study was supported by the study notes and by the discussions held between colleagues and members of the research team. Finally, a consensus was reached on the various agreements and disagreements regarding the themes analysed. To validate the findings, each author read each draft of the research report independently and then pooled their opinions to reach a consensus on a final draft.

Results

The data were organized into 3 main themes and 8 subthemes generated from the analysis: (a) The value of a common language: professional autonomy; (b) From standardization to action: integration into clinical practice; and (c) Skills-building: towards Nursology. In this context, Nursology refers to the development of nursing as a scientific discipline and has been discussed in Spanish-language scholarship under the term cuidadología [52]. Themes 1- The value of a common language: professional autonomy

Language standardization was considered to be a key element in the development of the nursing profession. Participants commented that the use of SNTs has helped consolidate professional identity and autonomy. They also noted that new tools, such as artificial intelligence (AI) — which rely on pattern recognition — will not be usable without SNTs.

The use of SNTs raises the profile of the profession and of nursing as a science in its own right. And if we do not record data in SNTs, they are not documented as having been performed by a nurse (FG1).

They have definitely played a major role in the development of the profession at one time […] They have facilitated the development of that aspect of independent work. (FG3)

AI looks for terms that are repeated, that’s how it’s trained […] So, if we don’t achieve that, AI won’t help us nurses advance either (I12).

The NNN system was identified as the most widely implemented and recognized. Spanish nurses acknowledge its value as a shared language and its potential for raising the profile of the nursing profession. Its drawbacks, however, are its conceptual and terminological complexity and its lack of cultural, linguistic and professional adaptation.

The language doesn’t come from the Spanish language; It comes from elsewhere. It’s been included in the university, but the nursing profession, the profession itself, does not absorb it in the same way as it was absorbed by university 30 years ago. (FG1)

In the same way as a scale written in, for example, English is validated in Spanish, the taxonomy should also be adapted to the care provided in Spain, because what’s done in the United States might be completely different from what we do here (FG2).

Another difficulty mentioned was the periodic updating of the different taxonomies, even though teaching and research updating is part of the development of the profession. “The result of research and progress” (FG3) that should be consolidated with validation studies.

Every 2 years - that’s too many changes. They should do it every 5 years, maybe… (FG1).

Very few of the NDs included in the classification have been clinically validated [.] and when it comes to clinical practice two things happen, there are elements that you have identified and that are not included in the diagnosis, and there are other elements that are in the diagnosis but that you never see in your practice (I10).

Participants also find that various updates have led to more standardization, better translation, and more natural language, all of which could facilitate the integration of SNTs into daily practice.

We’ve had difficulties with translations for a long time, and I can safely say that NANDA now is nothing like the NANDA of the 1990s. First, because the translations have improved slightly, and second, because NANDA has made a significant effort to try to standardize the taxonomy (I10).

Finally, some participants wondered whether there was commercial motivation behind the frequent updates. However, they believe that cost is not the main barrier to updating and using SNTs. In this regard, other participants mentioned that free, general taxonomies, such as SNOMED CT (Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine - Clinical Terms), can be used as an alternative.

NANDA is a company and has its interests. I think that the fact that we have to renew the book every two years is also highly suspicious (FG3).

I don’t think that cost is a determining factor when it comes to use (…) If we think it’s useful, the cost is not really going to bring the system down - it’s a question of priorities… (I10).

I think I’ll use more generalized taxonomies rather than those that are more specific to the profession, such as SNOMED, which is a general, free system that, though not for nurses, contains good translations of the terms that can then be converted (I12).

In the Spanish region of Catalonia, some institutions have implemented ATIC terminology. Participants felt that this option overcomes any reluctance to use SNTs because it reduces the terminological complexity and the feeling of strangeness generated by NNN. However, some suggested that its greater acceptance could be due to its alignment with a more biomedical approach, making it more familiar to certain professionals.

I think the aim of the ATIC was to overcome this barrier. The problem isn’t that they don’t use the process, it’s not that they don’t use the methodology. It’s that they don’t use the language. Because the language is strange (FG3).

I know that so-called interface terminology, I understand that it is different, that it can seem more practical or more plastic to someone from a field more focused on classical biomedical language (I6).

Themes 2- From standardization to action: integration into clinical practice

The integration of SNTs into clinical practice varies by region, although overall they are seldom used in PC in Spain.

The research we do using what we nurse record, shows us that we don’t do much because little is recorded, but since it is not recorded, we cannot really validate the impact of our interventions (I12).

The participants identified several factors that influenced the integration of SNTs, especially their perceived usefulness, the usability of the input systems, the training provided, and the organizational framework.

First, in terms of usefulness, one of the main facilitators identified was the way that SNTs help nurses structure their thinking and focus on health outcomes. Others mention their usefulness in monitoring chronic conditions, arriving at psychosocial diagnoses, the promotion of health and disease prevention, or addressing less prevalent problems. There is broad consensus on their usefulness in continuity of care, where they improve professional communication.

In the use of language, there is growing evidence that it improves health outcomes […] because it allows us to interpret messages in a care-centred way. But it is not only an expressed language, but also a thought language first of all…it is that cognition centred on a care language structure that keeps the nurse where they need to be, focused on the person (I6).

If I am absent, there is another nurse who knows perfectly well what I am doing and where I am heading, so that the care is continuous (I4).

Furthermore, regarding human resources planning, SNTs have the potential to estimate the complexity of care, workloads, and consultation time.

A nurse who performs techniques may have a workload of 5,000 and one that performs complex interventions may have a workload of 1,000, because if I have to deal with anxiety, grief, cope with chronic pathologies… those interventions, according to the NIC, last between 45 min and an hour (I10).

Despite the facilitators identified, there are many barriers to effective implementation of SNTs in PC. One of the main drawbacks, according to some professionals, is that these terminologies have no clinical meaning, and they view them as a time-consuming bureaucratic burden that does not benefit the health of their patients.

There is a large percentage who see it as an additional burden. And in the end, it means taking on more work and more burdens when… It makes no difference to your patient whether you do that part of the care plan or not (FG1).

On this subject, some participants suggested strategies to normalize the use of SNTs, such as sharing care plans with patients to foster understanding and empowerment.

The patient should know that we are creating a care plan, what his or her ND is, set common goals, talk about it with the patients so that they become familiar with it (FG2).

Secondly, the main barrier to usability, according to the participants, is the design of the EMR systems, which are not user-friendly, include far too many options, do not include search engines, and make care plans hard to find.

In medical records, it’s like a very separate section; no importance is given to care plans or to entering care plans. They don’t have a place where they are really given that prominence (FG1).

Everyone’s main complaint is that the module is very cumbersome and time-consuming, so you don’t use it and then you don’t see its applicability because there is also a lack of basic training (I5).

On the other hand, computing environments that include decision support systems and visual coding were highly rated, as they facilitate the integration of SNTs in clinical information systems. Participants also suggested developing a version of NANDA adapted to each environment to improve accessibility.

It’s domain-based and allows you to perform a comprehensive assessment, including all assessment scales. It’s intuitive; the system suggests a care plan, a diagnosis, interventions, and NOC results. (I2)

Modify NANDA to make it more accessible to people who don’t really use it, make a mini NANDA. I mean, you can’t be presented with 300 activities, because when I get to number 12, I’ve decided not to do any more activities (FG1).

Managers emphasize that the drawbacks are not always due to the clinical information system, but to a lack of training in SNTs. They believe that users must have in-depth knowledge of the tool to ensure its application in clinical practice.

With care plan training, there’s a tendency to think that you learn with the app, and I always tell them, you have to know about this, know how to do it on paper, and then use the app as a tool. […] but if I don’t have it in here (points to head), I can’t have it in there (points to the computer) (I11).

Thirdly, the analysis also identified structural limitations in academic and continuing professional development. Training in SNTs was described as insufficient. This is primarily because pathology-centred teaching predominates in universities, and SNTs tend to be presented as a submodule of medical diagnoses, which prevents students from understanding the human response-centred approach.

If we don’t train nurses from a nursing perspective, it’s very difficult to leave the biomedical paradigm to one side, because if I learn to be a nurse by studying diseases, it’s very difficult for me to then work on human responses. If what you study is diabetes, how a person responds to diabetes doesn’t really matter to you, right? (I10)

The main change involves changing your glasses, in terms of seeing and understanding what care languages are for and how we should use them […] we teachers need to be responsible for presenting them more in terms of care than of illness, and to do that we don’t have to change the content or the pedagogical methods or anything, just the mentality of the teachers themselves, who are often the first to be skeptical and ignore them (I6).

Participants believe that a thorough review of the training approach is needed, and that SNTs need to be taught across the board from the start of training, taking a care-centred perspective and structuring training in ND by levels of complexity.

Languages are learned by teaching them from the start and across the board. That’s the only way. […] Universities, care plans, you have to introduce them across the board. Not a methodology module (FG1).

Our curriculum should be organized by diagnoses (FG3).

They also emphasized the need to strengthen continuing and specialized training with practical, contextualized, tutor-driven systems. In specialized training, the role of the tutor is considered key, and they should be qualified in the use of SNTs.

You, the trainer, go where people are working, there you train them in groups, but you also help them individually with their questions, and are available there as the go-to training professionals, so that people consult you, so that everything does not end in a course with a beginning and an end (I6).

You’re training your future professionals; you need to be clear what the skills are, and you can’t ignore them. Perhaps we should accredit tutors’ skills (I10).

The disconnect between theory and practice is a persistent barrier that can only be overcome by applying SNTs in real-world settings with hand-on teaching support to achieve a seamless transition from classroom to clinical practice.

That’s where we need to work on the language system, depending on each clinical setting and the healthcare context that each group of professionals needs, not from a generic one (I6).

Finally, organization was identified as a key factor in the effective implementation of SNTs. The introduction of mandatory use of SNTs was considered a definite facilitator, although the participants noted that effective implementation depends on the degree of enforcement and oversight.

Good progress was made when it became mandatory to include the SNT, we should take advantage of that (FG1).

Despite this, discourse shows that the main barrier is the lack of an institutional strategy, characterized by shifting decisions, a lack of coherence, and inadequate resources.

Management does not have any type of strategy regarding nursing languages (FG1).

A different manager comes along and says this is nonsense, so everything we’ve achieved to get to this point is meaningless, and we’re back to square one, and when we go back and forth we drive nurses crazy […] It‘s very driven by policies, by organizations, and that sometimes doesn’t help, because it makes it look like a matter of what’s in and what’s out (I10).

Participants believe it necessary to rely on compliance with the regulatory framework that mandates the use of the SNT, institutional support, and ongoing training.

We know that training is directly related to the use of languages (I10).

We must make a firm institutional commitment; it’s not enough to teach two courses a year (I6).

However, organizations have included SNTs in management indicators and incentive programs, although in many cases this has distorted its clinical purpose and turned it into a sort of administrative tool.

The use of nursing methodology has been increasing, possibly because indicators with both an economic incentive and support linked to your own nursing management have been included in the program agreement (I8).

That plan will never be finalized (speaking of a risk ND). Management wants them to be closed so they can be evaluated… So, you close it, but you must open it again, because the same problem still exists (FG1).

To avoid this distorted use of NDs as a means of achieving certain standards and obtaining incentives, some participants proposed moving toward measuring indicators of real impact on health outcomes, rather than just quantifying records.

They should not only consider my NDs, but also to a certain extent my interventions in the medium or long term, because we can apply care plans to anyone. But what real work are we doing? Measure the outcomes in this person (FG2).

Initially, we had to measure their use quantitatively, but then we have to move forward… We must have indicators that measure the complexity of care. […] Measure qualitatively, not quantitatively, because it is very easy to make 100 worthless NDs a month that have no impact on patient care (I10).

The participants also acknowledged that their work environment, rather than the training received, determines the actual application of the SNT. If SNTs are not used in their workplace, training never transitions from the theoretical to the practical and is eventually forgotten.

It depends a lot on where you work and the importance given [to SNTs]; if you work in a place where there is a systematic approach to working with languages, you end up using the training you’ve received, but if you work at a place where they never work with languages, then that training is forgotten (I10).

Frailty syndrome is one of the clinical scenarios that most clearly show both the potential and limitations of using SNTs. Frailty syndrome requires a comprehensive, preventive approach in which the PC nurse plays a key role and is therefore the ideal setting in which to analyse the value of nurse diagnoses in clinical practice. Despite its prevalence, frailty is seldom recorded. Participants noted that specific nursing diagnoses (NDs)— “Risk for frailty syndrome” and “Frailty syndrome”—are not universally available because some EMR taxonomies have not yet been updated, so these diagnoses cannot be entered.

At our Health Centre we have a frailty group. So, we administer an SPPB test, and if the patient is frail, there’s a physical exercise group. But there is no ND. (FG2)

I think that right now here in the Canary Islands the ND is most widely used (I14).

It addresses not only physical problems, but also psychological and social problems. “It seems to me a good label to work with (FG3).

However, other participants pointed out that specific frailty diagnoses are used less often than more widespread and objective screening tools, such as the SPPB. Participants doubted whether these NDs are taught in undergraduate training and emphasized the key role that teachers play in including them.

I think it’s an obsolete diagnosis, not only because of the complexity of the syndrome in this case, but also because of the implementation of such objective tools in clinical practice, such as the SPPB test (FG3).

These diagnoses are addressed in, for example, adult and geriatric nursing, as that is where they should be addressed. Then, of course, there’ll be a professor who doesn’t know how to diagnose, has no idea, and doesn’t even mention it. It means that there is already, from the start, a limitation (FG3).

Low rates of utilization and reporting also limit the generation of evidence on the effectiveness of nursing interventions in frailty syndrome. This in turn holds back the development of the profession and its capacity to demonstrate its impact on health outcomes.

We think that nurses do a good job in treating frailty, but there is no evidence, there are hardly any studies (I9).

This is compounded by lack of nursing leadership; although nurses contribute to national strategic documents, the absence of standardized NDs in these frameworks is a missed opportunity for both professional development and public health improvement.

In line with the Ministry’s approach to preventing frailty, there is no common frailty register. In other words, we are detecting frailty, but we can’t incorporate it with a standard code… There’s no ICD, and it hasn’t occurred to anyone that NANDA has that ND. […] It’s a lost opportunity for the profession, but not only for the profession, it’s a lost opportunity for patients (I5).

Theme 3. Skills-building: towards nursology

Participants stressed that more investment must be made in research to effectively promote the use of SNTs. Building nursing knowledge is a complex process that requires a philosophical approach and critical thinking. Academia is the ideal field for this, without forgetting that this knowledge is “grounded” in clinical practice. Higher education and research play a key role in supporting professional development, showcasing the impact of care, and demonstrating the effectiveness of interventions.

We also have to transcend that paradigm that precedes the theoretical model and goes to the philosophy of the method, understand many of the paradigms of complexity […] and Nursology, where does that come from? What does it mean? Where is it discussed? That’s the origin, the genesis of the scientific method (I6).

We have to think about what we do, what we are, how we impact our patients, and we have to reflect a lot on what we want our profession to be (I10).

If you want to see how you impact care outcomes, the difference is between having or not having a nurse caring for patients, you have to look for tools that help you do that, and I think languages are an important tool to show that (I10).

However, the participants have their misgivings about the validity of SNTs in epidemiological studies due to their underuse and coding problems. Some believe that these biases are common to all fields that use secondary sources and are part of the scientific process and development of the profession.

All researchers accept the data in their clinical records as valid […] But when we talk about epidemiology, we make a more global, populational interpretation (I6).

There is much room for development in these languages, let’s not push ourselves too hard. In terms of that development, what we need to do is apply them to have more data and to be able to investigate more about them (I3).

The participants emphasized that to effectively promote the use of SNTs, nursing leadership will need to be stronger and take a critical, proactive approach that transcends the technical application of language and connects it with the deeper meaning of care. Some participants agreed that the lack of institutional commitment limits implementation of these terminologies and generates resistance.

There is no leadership from above or below. Except for certain centres that have clear leadership in terms of care (I5).

We must take part in all decision-making bodies to make sure they don’t lose the care perspective (I12).

The participants emphasized that nursing leadership must begin by strengthening professional identity, which they believe to be poorly defined. They believe that this should be taken as a collective construct in which professional identity is reconsidered from a self-critical perspective and care tools need to be introduced. They also mentioned the need to reach a broad consensus on which SNTs to adopt and to ensure their consistent implementation in clinical practice.

I think few people understand clearly what we are here for […] I would really like to ask nurses that, I mean, what they think it means to be a nurse and what I think is that there is no clear model (I7).

Feeling that you have a common language should be a collective identity, I think it would be a dreadful shortcoming in our profession… if they weren’t there, someone would have to invent them (I13).

It’s our responsibility towards the profession, because if we draw attention to it, it will stimulate the development of the profession, we will have a basis for research, we will have a basis for seeing where we have failed and be able to improve (I3).

I believe that nothing works without a good consensus with the people who are going to use it (I12).

Finally, the participants discussed the need to include the role played by other professionals in caring for patients, and the position of nurses in this context. Using SNTs as clinical language allows nurses to communicate with other professionals. To achieve this, participants suggest that other groups should also be trained in their use.

For some time now, I have been seeing the term Care used by other professionals; this is all very well, but I think that us nurses should also commit to making their care evidence-based, and we’ll get that evidence from using SNTs, the most methodological part (I8).

Discussion

Main findings of the study and comparison with other studies

This study explores nurses’ experience with Standardized Nursing Terminologies in the PC setting, particularly in the context of frailty syndrome.

One of the most striking findings is the remarkable similarity of the opinions expressed, regardless of the participant’s professional profile and years of experience. This consistency contrasts with the findings reported in other studies, where the authors observed that attitudes changed depending on the participant’s professional trajectory [53] and field of practice, with those in a managerial position being more favourable [54].

SNTs are perceived as a tool with a high potential for structuring clinical thinking, improving continuity of care, facilitating research, and helping to improve health outcomes. However, they are still rarely used in clinical practice due to technological, organizational and training barriers. These difficulties are particularly evident in nursing diagnosis, which is considered to be more complex than interventions (NIC) or outcomes (NOC), even though the same logic is used in all 3 coding systems.

This was also reported in previous studies, such as that of Ríos Jiménez et al. (n = 1,813) [55], in which more than 80% of participants considered that NDs were difficult to implement in practice, and 78% felt that they did not adequately reflect the care administered or the clinical outcomes. Similarly, a study performed in Norway [56] found that the lack of training in clinical, the unintuitive design of the data entry systems, and the fragmentation of information made it difficult to integrate SNTs into clinical practice. Some of the participants thought SNTs are useful in guiding the application of evidence-based interventions, an opinion also reported in a study performed in Brazil in patients with Impaired Physical Mobility (NANDA 00085) [57].

Similarly, a study performed in Minnesota [58] showed that nurses had a neutral or slightly negative attitude towards NDs, which coincides with the critical view expressed by many participants in the present study, especially in the healthcare setting. However, they contrast with those obtained in Sweden [13], where nurses said that SNTs promote continuity of care and help them identify patient needs and select more appropriate interventions. This discrepancy could be due to differences in the degree of institutional integration, the design of the EMR system, or professional culture.

One of the major differences in opinion between the participants involves the purpose of the SNT: while teaching and research nurses understand it as a tool for structuring care, many PC nurses perceive it as an end in itself that is associated with clinical records or economic incentives. This instrumental view can undermine the epistemological value of language as a mediator of clinical judgement and care.

The standardization of professional languages is a fundamental tool used across scientific disciplines [59], including nursing [60, 61], due to its capacity to systematically collect and structure data, enabling study, comparison, and the development of models at both individual and epidemiological levels. These taxonomies enable researchers to examine how nursing diagnoses are addressed through nursing interventions and translated into measurable patient outcomes, thereby supporting the development of nursing knowledge and theoretical frameworks that explain these relationships [62]. Consequently, the lack of consistent use of standardized terminologies may result in care needs being insufficiently identified, which can compromise resource allocation and hinder the generation of new evidence [60, 63].

Furthermore, nursing-specific terminologies provide an additional level of specificity that supports more person-centred and contextualized care. By focusing on human responses and care needs rather than on diseases or reasons for consultation, they contribute to a more holistic approach that moves beyond the reductionist biomedical perspective often embedded in other classifications [64]. The use of SNT demonstrates significant improvements and predictive capacity in various patient- and organization-related outcomes [65]. While more generic terminologies may enhance interoperability across disciplines, they may dilute the focus on care, as they are not specifically designed to capture human responses and identify nursing-specific needs.

Regarding taxonomic complexity, the participants raised an interesting question: Is NANDA-I too complex, or does it simply reflect the inherent complexity of ND? The use of ND involves differential reasoning, contextualization, and a holistic approach that often clashes with the fragmented, biomedical logic of the healthcare setting. This shift in cognitive approach seems to be one of the main challenges nurses face.

In an attempt to make the taxonomy more practical, the participants suggested adapting NANDA-I to different settings, designing a different nursing service portfolio based on the prevalence of different care needs in different clinical settings. This could improve the quality of care by making it more accessible in each setting.

The need to periodically update NANDA-I generated mixed opinions: some participants felt that it was positive, as it facilitates the incorporation of new evidence and allows the classification to be adapted to different contexts, while others considered it to be an additional burden for clinical nurses, and speculated that the need for frequent updates could be driven by scientific or economic interests. The fact that NANDA-I is not free for use by institutions was also mentioned as a potential limitation.

On the subject of training, participants found the approach to be excessively theoretical and biomedical, and to bear little relationship to clinical practice. This disconnect between training and real-world care led to negative opinions on the usefulness of NANDA, which could partly explain why SNTs are so rarely used. This finding coincides with a study performed in Romania [66], in which a positive belief in NDs was associated with a more receptive attitude, a greater intention to use, and more frequent use of these diagnoses.

Across the board, it was found that the increasing workload and the technical orientation of modern professional development also contribute to the underutilization of SNTs. This is echoed in the results of a study in Nigeria [67], where the main barriers identified were lack of knowledge, lack of staff, high workload, and low motivation.

A particularly relevant finding is the poor integration of the ND " Frailty syndrome (NANDA 00353) “, even though this phenomenon is highly prevalent in PC and the ND could be useful in caring for these patients in other settings in Spain [68]. Although this ND is included in EMR systems in some regions, many nurses reported that it was not available in their system, or that they were unaware of the diagnosis, or simply did not use it. This situation highlights the gap between the clinical complexity of frail patients and the system’s ability to record, raise awareness of and respond to their needs from a nursing perspective. For these reasons, frailty syndrome is an excellent example of how the lack of integration of SNTs can compromise person-centred care planning.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several methodological strengths. The combination of individual interviews and focus group discussions enabled the collection of additional experiences and perspectives on the use of SNTs, facilitated discourse comparison, and triangulation to enrich the analysis. The sample included professionals from a broad range of clinical, teaching, management, and research backgrounds who contributed different perspectives that led to a richer, more contextualized understanding of the phenomenon studied.

The data were analysed using thematic coding, and the categories were developed by consensus after a series of reviews and exchange of interpretations, a process that strengthened the internal consistency of the results.

Some limitations should also be mentioned: although the individual interviews included participants from different regions, the focus groups were conducted exclusively in Madrid region. Furthermore, the fact that the ND “Frailty syndrome” is not included in the EMR in all territories (including Madrid) limited the number of discourses on the use of this diagnosis collected from each group. To compensate for this, nurses with expertise in frailty syndrome and a clinical nurse from a region where this ND is included in the EMR were interviewed.

Finally, participation was voluntary, and this could have favoured the inclusion of professionals with greater awareness of or interest in SNTs. However, the diversity of profiles and perspectives obtained suggests that the study population is a good representation of the plurality of modern nursing.

Implications of the study findings

The use of SNTs in PC should transcend theory to become a strategic tool in the planning, monitoring, and evaluation of nursing care. Integrating these terminologies in clinical practice would allow managers to distribute care loads based on the complexity of care, raise awareness of specific interventions in community settings, and guide clinical decisions on less prevalent but highly relevant problems, such as frailty syndrome.

From both a training and organizational standpoint, SNTs should be incorporated across all levels of undergraduate education, specialist training, and clinical learning models, using applied methodologies and guided support.

Including SNTs in service portfolios, management programs, and resource allocation criteria would consolidate human response-based nursing, strengthen the professional role, and contribute to outcome-based care.

At the same time, there is a need for research into the impact of SNTs on health outcomes, their applicability in different clinical settings, and how they strengthen practise-based nursing knowledge.

Conclusions

Language standardization helps build professional skills by strengthening professional autonomy and documenting the clinical judgement of nurses, despite the difficulties derived from its conceptual complexity and poor cultural and linguistic adaptation.

In PC, SNTs are useful in structuring treatment and improving continuity of care; however, their practical integration remains limited. The approach to frailty syndrome is a good example of this disconnect between theoretical potential and actual use, which is due to technological and organizational barriers, and insufficient and poorly contextualized training.

Overcoming these limitations requires coordinated strategies in which professional leadership, institutional commitment, and scientific development come together to organize training, research, management, and clinical practice. This will also help promote professional cohesion and allow nurses to engage with other professionals from other fields using the language of care within an outcome- and human response-based culture.
