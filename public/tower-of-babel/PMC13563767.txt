Association of athlete’s heart and ACE I/D genotype with later-life left ventricular hypertrophy: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yuki Someya, Eri Miyamoto-Mikami, Mizuki Takaragawa, Noriyuki Fuku, Hisashi Naito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background“Athlete’s heart” refers to the physiological cardiac hypertrophy observed in competitive athletes, which reverts to normal after retirement from competition. However, few studies have examined long-term follow-up. This study investigated whether former college athletes with a history of an athlete’s heart have a higher risk of developing heart disease later in life and examined its association with angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) insertion (I)/deletion (D) polymorphisms.MethodsA total of 980 participants (mean age, 52.0 ± 15.7 years) from the Juntendo University Alumni Study were analyzed. Information on Athlete’s heart during college and subsequent heart disease, including left ventricular hypertrophy, was obtained through self-administered questionnaires in 2018. The ACE I/D genotype was determined based on the rs4341 C/G polymorphism.ResultsOf all participants, 177 (18.1%) reported Athlete’s heart during college, and 15 (1.5%) were diagnosed with left ventricular hypertrophy later in life. Former athletes with Athlete’s heart had a 7.3-fold higher prevalence of left ventricular hypertrophy than those without such a history. Moreover, carriers of the D allele showed a further increased risk (odds ratio, 13.63; 95% confidence interval, 1.31–142.41).ConclusionsThese findings suggest that regular cardiac monitoring may be warranted to identify potential late-onset cardiac remodeling in former athletes with Athlete’s heart carrying the ACE D allele.

Background

One cause of sudden death in athletes is hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [1]. Maron et al. reported that 28 of 29 athletes who died suddenly had structural cardiovascular abnormalities, 14 of whom had hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [2]. On the other hand, a specific physiological adaptation of the heart, called Athlete’s heart, has been observed in elite athletes and those who continue to train at high intensities [3]. For example, athletes participating in endurance sports such as marathon running, cycling, and long-distance swimming often demonstrate enlarged cardiac chambers, whereas those involved in resistance sports such as weightlifting, wrestling, and powerlifting generally show greater cardiac wall thickening [4]. The signs of Athlete’s heart include small increases in the size of the ventricles and atria and in the width of the main blood vessels. As this is similar to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, the distinction between Athlete’s heart and heart disease is emphasized [3].

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is believed to be caused by genetic mutations that result in myocardial thickening. For example, rare mutations in genes encoding sarcomeric proteins have been the focus of research; however, some non-sarcomeric gene polymorphisms, such as those in genes related to the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, are also known to modify the phenotype of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [5]. In a previous study, among the genes related to the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, the expression of an angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) mRNA in the heart was reported to be significantly higher in spontaneously hypertensive rats (showing pathological cardiac hypertrophy) than in the control and exercise groups, and there was no significant difference in the expression of ACE mRNA between the control and exercise groups [6]. These results suggest that ACE contributes to the development of pathological cardiac hypertrophy but not to physiological cardiac hypertrophy induced by exercise training. The human ACE gene is located on chromosome 17 and contains a polymorphism comprising an insertion (I) or deletion (D) of 287-base pairs. The ACE I/D polymorphism is associated with serum and tissue ACE activity; the DD genotype is reported to increase ACE activity compared to the ID or II genotype [7]. Therefore, the D allele of the ACE I/D polymorphism may increase the risk of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Indeed, the D allele of the ACE I/D polymorphism has been reported to increase the prevalence of left ventricular hypertrophy, left ventricular mass, and relative wall thickness in middle age [8–11].

In contrast, it has been reported that the D/D and I/D genotypes have a stronger effect on left ventricular hypertrophy than the I/I genotype in endurance athletes [12] and that exercise-induced left ventricular hypertrophy in male Caucasian military recruits is greater in D/D genotype carriers than in I/I genotype carriers [13]. However, these findings were cross-sectional, and it is difficult to assess whether they represent physiological adaptations induced by training or pathological cardiac hypertrophy induced by predispositions, including genetic factors and training [3]. Although athletes with the D allele or Athlete’s heart need to be checked for their health status after retiring from competitions, there are few prognostic studies on athletes, and it is unclear whether they are diagnosed with cardiac disease after retiring from competitions.

In this study, we investigated whether former college athletes who were diagnosed with Athlete’s heart during college were diagnosed with left ventricular hypertrophy later in life and whether this was associated with an ACE I/D polymorphism.

Methods

Study design and participants

The Juntendo Alumni Study was a retrospective and prospective cohort study. We have previously shown that physical fitness and college-age characteristics are associated with the future development of diseases such as diabetes mellitus and hypertension in the same cohort [14, 15]. The participants were alumni of the Physical Education School at Juntendo University who graduated between 1956 and 2018. Most participants belonged to university sports clubs, including track and field, gymnastics, soccer, and judo clubs, and they underwent regular training and competitions in their respective sports. Therefore, most of the participants were competitive athletes during their university years.

This was a post hoc analysis using data from the Juntendo University Alumni Study; 2,804 alumni who participated in at least one follow-up examination between 2007 and 2018 were included. Of the 2,804 participants, 1,070 provided saliva samples for genetic studies in 2017 and 2018. Among the remaining alumni, 58 who did not return the questionnaire in 2018 and 32 who did not complete the questionnaire properly were excluded. Thus, 980 participants were included in our final analysis (Fig. 1). We then categorized participants into two groups based on whether they had Athlete’s heart (n = 177) or normal hearts (n = 803) or into three groups based on their ACE I/D polymorphism: D/D (n = 117), I/D (n = 462), and I/I (n = 401).

Fig. 1Flowchart of study participants

Flowchart of study participants

The participants’ characteristics were examined using the Juntendo University Alumni Study database. The following information was collected in the 2018 questionnaire: age (years), height (cm), weight (kg), body mass index (kg/m2), specialized sport, frequency of club activities in college (days/week), time spent in club activities in college (minutes/day), physical activity (Mets・hours/week), sedentary time (hours/day), and disease history. This study determined the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, stroke, and cerebral hemorrhage among the participants. Specialized sports are categorized into nine groups based on the peak static and dynamic components achieved during competitions [16].

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Juntendo University in 2007 (No. 19–131), 2016 (No. 28–30), 2017 (No. 29–182), 2020 (No. 2020-29), 2021 (No. 2020-29), and 2022 (No. 2021-76 and 2022-52). All data were anonymized prior to the analysis to ensure participant privacy. Informed consent was obtained from all study participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Determination of prevalence of athlete’s heart and left ventricular hypertrophy

The prevalence of Athlete’s heart and heart disease, including left ventricular hypertrophy, was determined based on self-administered questionnaires completed in 2018. In the survey, alumni were asked whether they had been diagnosed with Athlete’s heart during a school medical examination as a college student and whether left ventricular hypertrophy had been diagnosed by a physician using an electrocardiogram (ECG) examination within 3 years prior to the survey. Because ECG findings were not verified by echocardiography, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, or medical records, left ventricular hypertrophy was defined as self-reported ECG-based left ventricular hypertrophy.

Genotyping of ACE I/D polymorphism

For total DNA extraction, 2 ml of saliva was collected from each participant into an Oragene DNA collection tube (OG-500; DNA Genotek Inc., Ontario, Canada). Total DNA was extracted from the salivary samples according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The ACE C/G (I/D) rrs4341 polymorphism was genotyped using TaqMan® SNP Genotyping Assays (Assay ID: C_29403047_10). The ACE I/D polymorphism was determined using the ACE C/G polymorphism, which was in perfect linkage disequilibrium with the I/D genotype as follows: C/C as II, C/G as ID, and G/G as DD.

Statistical analyses

Differences in the prevalence of characteristics between these groups were then compared using one-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and the chi-squared test or the Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test for categorical variables. All statistical tests were two-tailed with a 5% significance level. Logistic regression analysis was then used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for left ventricular hypertrophy. In this study, the group with the ACE I/I genotype (no D allele) was used as the reference and was adjusted for age in 2018 (continuous variable), sex (male or female), and year of college enrollment (continuous variable). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 for Windows software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL).

Results

Characteristics of study participants

The mean age of all participants (822 males and 158 females) was 52.0 ± 15.7 years. Among them, 177 participants (18.1%; 155 males and 22 females) were diagnosed with Athlete’s heart as college students. Table 1 shows the characteristics of the normal heart and Athlete’s heart cohorts. Participants with Athlete’s heart were more likely to have participated in club activities during their college years (p = 0.019). Among participants with Athlete’s heart who participated in specialized sports, 61.4% were involved in sports requiring > 70% maximal oxygen uptake and < 50% maximal voluntary contraction (ⅠC and ⅡC according to the American College of Cardiology classification of sports) [16]. The highest prevalence of Athlete’s heart (30.4%) was in sports with > 70% maximal oxygen uptake and < 20% maximal voluntary contraction (IC category), such as endurance running and soccer (p < 0.01; data not shown). Within 3 years before the 2018 survey, 15 patients (1.5%) were diagnosed with left ventricular hypertrophy on electrocardiography. The association between left ventricular hypertrophy and professional sports at college age was not significant (p = 0.357). Figure 2 shows the prevalence of left ventricular hypertrophy according to the presence or absence of Athlete’s heart. Participants who were diagnosed with Athlete’s heart as college students had a 7.3-fold higher prevalence of left ventricular hypertrophy than those who were not (p < 0.001).

ACE genotype and the risk of left ventricular hypertrophy in participants with Athlete’s heart during college

Table 2 shows the characteristics of the participants and the prevalence of each disease according to the ACE I/D polymorphism in participants with Athlete’s heart during college. There were no significant differences in age, body composition, current physical activity, or physical activity during college between the three groups. Discussion

In this study, we investigated whether former college athletes who were diagnosed with Athlete’s heart during college were diagnosed with left ventricular hypertrophy later in life and whether this was associated with an ACE I/D polymorphism. In this cohort, nearly 20% had been diagnosed with Athlete’s heart as college students and less than 2% had been diagnosed with left ventricular hypertrophy by electrocardiography at follow-up. Participants who had been diagnosed with Athlete’s heart as college students had a 7.3-fold higher prevalence of left ventricular hypertrophy than those who had not. In addition, after adjusting for potential risk factors, we found that former college athletes with Athlete’s heart and the D/D genotype of the ACE I/D polymorphism were both independently associated with left ventricular hypertrophy in later life.

There have been few reports of follow-ups in athletes with Athlete’s heart [17], and this study is the first report of a long-term follow-up of over 30 years. Second, this study showed that participants who had been diagnosed with Athlete’s heart as college students had a higher prevalence of left ventricular hypertrophy than those without Athlete’s heart. This finding raises the important possibility that some of the physiological adaptations of the heart in athletes may not have been restored after retirement from competition or that pathological heart disease may have been missed by the school physical examination. Therefore, more detailed medical screening may be needed to distinguish between Athlete’s heart and heart disease.

Our study also showed that former college athletes with Athlete’s heart and the D/D genotype of the ACE I/D polymorphism had a much higher prevalence of left ventricular hypertrophy those with Athlete’s heart and the I/I genotype. The association between the D allele of the ACE I/D polymorphism and increasing prevalence of left ventricular hypertrophy, left ventricular mass, and relative wall thickness in middle-aged individuals (nonathletes) has been reported [8–11]. In particular, Ueno et al. reported that the D/D genotype was associated with concentric cardiac hypertrophy [9]. Therefore, in D/D genotype carriers, cardiac hypertrophy, which was thought to be Athlete’s heart at college age, may have been pathological cardiac hypertrophy. Individuals with the D/D genotype may exhibit negative (or inappropriate) physiological responses to high-intensity exercise. Although cardiac hypertrophy in athletes (Athlete’s heart) is a physiological adaptation to training and has been reported to be reversible after the cessation of training [18], athletes who continue to train at high intensities may require genetic testing to differentiate Athlete’s heart from heart disease, as reported in a recent review [18]. In addition, athletes with the D/D genotype of the ACE I/D polymorphism may require continued long-term medical monitoring even if cardiac hypertrophy is a physiological adaptation.

This study had several limitations. First, we could not accurately diagnose the Athlete’s heart and left ventricular hypertrophy because we did not have access to raw electrocardiograms and other examination data. Therefore, the high-potential waveform found in normal heart status may have been included. In addition, because the Athlete’s heart status was determined using self-administered questionnaires, recall bias may have occurred, particularly among older participants. In addition, changes in the diagnostic criteria, screening practices, and clinical interpretation of Athlete’s heart over time may have resulted in a non-standardized classification. Furthermore, we could not distinguish between cardiac hypertrophy and enlargement in Athlete’s heart. However, this study is the first to follow athletes over time and examine the association between ACE genotype and left ventricular hypertrophy. Second, we analyzed alumni who responded to the follow-up questionnaires, which may have introduced a self-selection bias. For example, participants who responded to the follow-up questionnaires may have been healthier. However, the prevalence of hypertension and diabetes mellitus in this study was slightly higher than in the general population (hypertension: 22.5%, diabetes mellitus: 6.6%) [19, 20]. In addition, the prevalence of hypertension and diabetes mellitus was higher in the D/D genotype carriers, but the difference was not statistically significant. Although these diseases and medications, such as ACE inhibitors, have been reported to be associated with left ventricular hypertrophy, residual confounding factors cannot be completely excluded. In the future, we would like to include detailed echocardiographic measurements and medication status to determine whether former athletes have physiological or pathological hypertrophy.

Conclusion

Athletes with Athlete’s heart and carriers of the D allele of the ACE I/D polymorphism were more likely to present with left ventricular hypertrophy later in life. These findings suggest that such athletes may require regular health screenings focusing on cardiac function.
