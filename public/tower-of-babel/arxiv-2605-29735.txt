On Strongly Hopfian Mixed abelian Groups
Peter V. Danchev, Patrick W. Keef

Abstract
. The classes of abelian groups that are (uniformly) strongly Hopfian abelian groups, and dually, (uniformly) strongly co-Hopfian abelian groups have been studied by several authors, including Abdelalim (2015) and Abdelalim-Chillali-Essanouni (2015). This paper extends these investivations in the case of (genuinely) mixed groups. For example, it is shown that a reduced group that is (uniformly) strongly co-Hopfian will always be (uniformly) strongly Hopfian. In addition, a result of Chekhlov-Danchev (submitted) characterizing when a torsion-free group is (uniformly) strongly Hopfian is generalized to the case of (global) Warfield groups.

1.
Introduction and Background

All groups considered and studied in the present research paper are simultaneously
additive
and
abelian
; most of the basic notation and terminology are in agreement with the classical books
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
. In general,
G
will denote an abelian group with torsion
T
, and if
p
is a prime,
T_{p}
will be its
p
-torsion subgroup; so
T=\oplus_{p}T_{p}
. As usual, all “summand” means “direct summand”. We will often use other standard conventions, such as
A\leq G
to indicate that
A
is a subgroup of
G
,
A\leq^{\oplus}G
to mean
A
is a summand of
G
, “rank” for “torsion-free rank” and “iff” for “if and only if.”

A group
G
is called
Hopfian
iff it is
not
isomorphic to any of its
proper
quotients; that is, each of its surjective endomorphisms is injective, i.e., an automorphism. Mimicking
[
1
]
, a group
G
is said to be
strongly Hopfian
(or simply
SH
) provided that, for each endomorphism
\phi
of
G
, the ascending chain

\ker\phi\leq\ker\phi^{2}\leq\dots\leq\ker\phi^{n}\leq\dots

is eventually stationary, i.e., it stabilizes at some place.

If we let
G^{n}:=\phi^{n}(G)
, then it is readily checked that
G
is SH iff, for all endomorphisms
\phi:G\to G
, there is an integer
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\phi
restricts to an injection on
G^{n}
; we call such an
n
(if it exists) an
SH-exponent
for
\phi
. If this holds, then
\phi
also restricts to an injection on
G^{m}\leq G^{n}
for all
m\geq n
, so that
\phi
restricts to an isomorphism
G^{m}\cong G^{m+1}
and
\ker\phi^{n}=\ker\phi^{m}
for all
m\geq n
. In particular, if
G
is SH and
\phi
is surjective, then
G^{n}=G^{n+1}=G
, so that we can conclude that
\phi
is an automorphism, i.e.,
G
is Hopfian.

We say
G
is
uniformly SH
if there is a fixed
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
that is an SH-exponent for all endomorphisms
\phi:G\to G
. Obviously, if
G
is uniformly SH, then it is SH.

Dually, if the ascending chain
\ker\phi^{n}
is replaced by the descending chain
\phi^{n}(G)=G^{n}
, then we arrive at the notion of
strongly co-Hopfian
(or simply Sco-H) groups, as well as
uniformly
Sco-H. The difference between these two notions is well illustrated by the (easily checked) fact that a torsion-free group of finite rank will always be SH (in fact, uniformly SH), but it will be Sco-H iff it is divisible.

Characterizing any of the above four classes of groups quickly reduces to the case of reduced groups (see, for example, Corollary
2.9
). And if
G
is reduced and a member of any of these four classes, it will follow that for any prime
p
, that
T_{p}
will be finite, and in particular, it will be bounded (see, for example, Proposition
2.1
). It follows from Theorems 2.1 from both
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
that if
G=T
is reduced and torsion, then it will be in any of these classes iff there is a fixed exponent
e\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for all primes
p
,
T_{p}
has at most
p^{e}
elements (see also Corollary
2.2
).

Two of the most studied classes of mixed abelian groups are the
sp-groups
and the
(global) Warfield groups
, and these classes will be the focus of this paper, as well.
To say
G
is an sp-group means that the inclusion
T=\oplus_{p}T_{p}\hookrightarrow\prod_{p}T_{p}=:P
extends to a pure embedding
G\hookrightarrow P
(i.e.,
G/T
is divisible). Again, when studying the groups in any of these classes, we may assume
G
is reduced and each
T_{p}
is finite, and in this case (by Proposition
2.11
),
G
will be an sp-group iff
G/T
is divisible. This class is the focus of Section
2
.

By
[
5
, Proposition 2.3]
, if
G
is a reduced Sco-H group, then
G
must be an sp-group. One of the main results of this paper (Theorem
2.14
) improves upon this by showing that
any
reduced Sco-H (or uniformly Sco-H) group is necessarily SH (respectively, uniformly SH). When might a converse of this result hold, i.e., if
G/T
is divisible, when can we conclude that
G
is Sco-H? We verify this in two general cases: When
G
is SH and
G/T
has finite rank (Proposition
2.15
), and when
T
is SH (Proposition
2.18
).

Recall that a group is
Warfield
if it is a direct summand of a group with a simple presentation. In our discussions, since we are concerned with groups whose
p
-torsions,
T_{p}
, are all finite (and hence bounded), these can be decomposed into groups of torsion-free rank 1; again, with finite
p
-torsion (see Proposition
3.1
). We denote the class of such rank 1 groups with bounded
p
-torsion by
{\mathcal{B}}
; so any SH Warfield group will be a direct sum of groups in
{\mathcal{B}}
. Clearly, any torsion-free group of rank 1 has a simple presentation, and is an element of
{\mathcal{B}}
. It follows that the class of Warfield group that are torsion-free coincides with the completely decomposable groups.

The theory of Warfield groups makes extensive use of a category called
WALK
, where two homomorphism
f,g:H\to G
are considered
WALK-equivalent
if
(f-g)(H)\leq G
(see, for e.g.,
[
8
, Section 15.8]
). This allows us to generalize the notion of the
type
of a rank one torsion-free group to the
WALK-type
of an element of
{\mathcal{B}}
. Using this, we are able to characterize exactly when a Warfield group
G
whose torsion
T
is SH also satisfies that property using the WALK-types of its summands in a given decomposition of
G
into groups from
{\mathcal{B}}
(Corollary
3.4
). And since a completely decomposable group is just a Warfield group with
T=0
(which is obviously SH), this characteriation generalizes the main result of
[
4
]
(see Corollary
3.5
).

Deciding when a general Warfield group is SH, when we are not given that
T
is SH, is a much more difficult problem. We provide an explicit solution of only one case of that problem: when
G
has rank 1 and each
T_{p}
is cyclic (Proposition
4.1
). The difficulty of that computation is evidence that giving a complete characterization of all groups that are SH, especially those whose torsion is not SH, may ultimately be a problem of insurmountable difficulty.

2.
SH groups and sp-Groups

Often, for some fixed endomorphism
\phi:G\to G
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we will denote
\phi^{n}(x)=:x^{n}\in G^{n}
. A sequential way to express the definition of SH groups is as follows:

A group
G
is SH iff, for each endomorphism
\phi:G\to G
, there is an
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
x\in G
, if
x^{n}\neq 0
, then
x^{m}\neq 0
for all
m\geq n
.

Again, a value
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
satisfying the above will be an SH-exponent for the endomorphism
\phi
, and a group
G
is SH if, and only if, every map
\phi:G\to G
has an SH-index. Clearly, if
n
is an SH-exponent for
\phi
, then so is each natural number
n^{\prime}\geq n
.

The following two claims are easily verified:

(1.A) If
G
is a torsion-free group of finite rank
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then
n
is an SH-exponent for any endomorphism
\phi:G\to G
; so,
G
is uniformly SH.

(1.B) Suppose
G=\oplus_{i\in I}G_{i}
, where each
G_{i}
is fully invariant in
G
and
\phi:G\to G
is an endomorphism of
G
whose restriction to
G_{i}
is
\phi_{i}
. If
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
is an SH-exponent for each
\phi_{i}
, then
n
will also be an SH-exponent for
\phi
.

We start our work with the following crucial observation, which actually is
[
1
, Proposition 2.7]
(we, however, include a brief proof for the convenience of the reader).

Proposition 2.1
.

If
G
is an SH group, then each
T_{p}
is finite.

Proof.

Applying the definition with
\phi
given by multiplication by
p
, then, for some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, it must be that
G[p^{n}]=G[p^{n+1}]
, which means that
T_{p}=G[p^{n}]
is bounded. Therefore,
T_{p}\leq^{\oplus}G
, and if
T_{p}
were infinite, then for some
m\leq n
there would be a summand
K\leq^{\oplus}T_{p}\leq^{\oplus}G
such that
K\cong{\mathbb{Z}}(p^{m})^{(\omega)}
, which would contradict that
G
is SH giving the claim.
∎

Remember that a group
G
is
co-Hopfian
provided that it is
not
isomorphic to any of its
proper
subgroups, that is, each its injective endomorphism is surjective, i.e., an automorphism. In addition, the group
G
is
strongly co-Hopfian
or just
Sco-H
for short, provided that the descending chain

G^{1}\geq G^{2}\geq\dots\geq G^{n}\geq\dots

is eventually stationary, i.e., for each endomorphism
\phi
of
G
, it stabilizes at some place (see, for example,
[
2
]
or
[
5
]
).

The following assertion is just a restatement of Theorems 2.1 from both
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
.

Corollary 2.2
.

Suppose
G
is a reduced torsion group. Then, the following are equivalent:

(1)
G
is SH.

(2)
G
is uniformly SH.

(3)
G
is Sco-H.

(4)
G
is uniformly Sco-H.

(5) There is an integer
e\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that, for all primes
p
,
G_{p}
has cardinality at most
p^{e}
.

We note the following important fact, which parallels
[
5
, Theorem 2.13]
:

Proposition 2.3
.

If
G
is a uniformly SH group, then
T
satisfies the five conditions above; e.g., it is SH.

Proof.

Suppose
n
is a uniform SH-exponent for
G
. If
p
is any prime, then since in conjunction with Proposition
2.1
each
T_{p}
is finite, there is a decomposition
G=T_{p}\oplus H_{p}
(see
[
7
]
). It thus follows that
n
will also be a uniform SH-exponent for
T_{p}
. So, by point (1.B) listed above,
n
will also be a uniform SH-exponent for
T=\oplus_{p}T_{p}
, as required.
∎

We will use the following immediate consequence of Corollary
2.2
and Proposition
2.3

Corollary 2.4
.

If
G=H\oplus K
, where
H
and
K
are uniformly SH, then
T
is SH.

It is important to emphasize, that just because
G
is SH, or Sco-H (but
not
uniformly so), it is possible for
T
to fail to be SH (which in this case is the same as failing to be Sco-H). We will return to this point repeatedly. Again, if
G
is reduced with one of these properties, it
will
follow that each
T_{p}
is finite, just that they will not necessarily all be of cardinality at most
p^{e}
for some fixed
e\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

We proceed now by proving a few more technicalities.

Lemma 2.5
.

Suppose
H
is a fully invariant subgroup of
G
,
\phi:G\to G
is an endomorphism,
\hat{\phi}:H\to H
is its restriction to
H
and
\overline{\phi}:\overline{G}:=G/H\to\overline{G}
is the induced endomorphism of
\overline{G}
. If
n_{1}
is an SH-exponent for
\hat{\phi}
and
n_{2}
is an SH-exponent for
\overline{\phi}
, then
n=n_{1}+n_{2}
is an SH-exponent for
\phi
.

Proof.

Suppose
x\in G
,
x^{n}\neq 0
and
m\geq n
; we need to show that
x^{m}\neq 0
. To that end, suppose first that
x^{n_{2}}\not\in H
. Therefore, since
n_{2}
is an SH-exponent for
\overline{\phi}
and
n_{2}\leq n\leq m
, we can infer that
x^{m}\not\in H
, so that, in particular,
x^{m}\neq 0
, as required.

If the last paragraph does not pertain, it follows that
y:=x^{n_{2}}\in H
. Now, since

y^{n_{1}}=(x^{n_{2}})^{n_{1}}=x^{n}\neq 0

and
m-n_{2}\geq n-n_{2}=n_{1}
, we can conclude that

0\neq y^{m-n_{2}}=(x^{n_{2}})^{m-n_{2}}=x^{m},

completing the proof.
∎

The following statement generalizes
[
1
, Proposition 2.5]
and is an immediate consequence of Lemma
2.5
.

Corollary 2.6
.

Suppose
H
is a fully invariant subgroup of
G
. If
G/H
and
H
are both SH (or uniformly SH), then
G
is also SH (respectively, uniformly SH).

Notice that Corollary
2.6
has a number of immediate consequences like these:

Corollary 2.7
.

Suppose
G
has torsion part
T
such that both
T
and
G/T
are SH (or uniformly SH), then
G
is also SH (respectively, uniformly SH).

Corollary 2.8
.

[
1
, Proposition 2.5]

Suppose
G=H\oplus K
, where
H
is fully invariant in
G
(i.e.,
Hom
(H,K)=0
). Then,
G
is SH (or uniformly SH) iff both
H
and
K
are SH (or, respectively, uniformly SH).

Proof.

The direction
(\Rightarrow)
follows at once from the (fairly obvious) fact that SH groups are closed under summands (compare with
[
1
]
). The direction
(\Leftarrow)
follows directly from Corollary
2.6
.
∎

Corollary 2.9
.

Suppose
G=D\oplus R
, where
D
is divisible and
R
is reduced. Then,
G
is SH (or uniformly SH) iff both
D
and
R
are SH (or, respectively, uniformly SH).

Corollary 2.10
.

Suppose
G=T\oplus H
, where
T
is torsion and
H
is torsion-free. Then
G
is SH (or uniformly SH) iff both
T
and
H
are SH (or, respectively, uniformly SH).

We will denote the
p
-height
of
x\in G
by
|{x}|_{p}
, or simply by
|{x}|
if there is no danger of confusion.

The group
G
with torsion subgroup
T
is said to be an
sp-group
if
G\cong G^{\prime}
, where
T\leq G^{\prime}\leq P:=\prod_{p}T_{p}
, with
G^{\prime}/G
divisible. We will almost exclusively be concerned with the following special case of this concept.

Proposition 2.11
.

Suppose
G
is a reduced group with bounded
p
-torsion (i.e., each
T_{p}
is bounded). Then,
G
is an sp-group iff
G/T
is divisible.

Proof.

Necessity being immediate from the definition of sp-groups, suppose that
G/T
is divisible. It is easy to see that
P=\prod_{p}T_{p}
will be the completion of
T
in the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-adic topology. Since
T
is dense in the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-adic topology on
G
, the inclusion
T\hookrightarrow P
extends to a homomorphism
\phi:G\to P
; so, it will suffice to show that
\phi
’s kernel, which is the first Ulm subgroup, denoting it by
\widetilde{G}:=\{x\in G:|x|_{p}\geq\omega
for all primes
p\}
, is divisible.

To that purpose, let
p
be some arbitrary prime and suppose that
p^{k}T_{p}=0
. If
x\in\widetilde{G}
, then there is a
y\in G
such that
|y|_{p}>k
and
py=x
. If
|y|_{p}<\omega
, then we can find a
z\in G
such that
|y|<|z|_{p}
and
pz=x
. But this would imply that
y-z
is a non-zero element of
T_{p}
of height
|y|_{p}>k
, which cannot be since
p^{k}T_{p}=0
. So,
|y|_{p}\geq\omega
. Since multiplication by
p
preserves all
q
-heights (where
q\neq p
is another prime), we can infer that
|y|_{q}=|x|_{q}\geq\omega
. Therefore,
y\in\widetilde{G}
so that
\widetilde{G}
is
p
-divisible. Since
p
was arbitrary, it follows that
\widetilde{G}
is divisible; but, since
G
is reduced, we can conclude that
\widetilde{G}=0
, as required.
∎

The following conclusion then comes from Propositions
2.1
and
2.11
.

Corollary 2.12
.

Suppose
G
is a reduced SH group. Then
G
is an sp-group iff
G/T
is divisible.

By
[
5
, Proposition 2.3]
, if
G
is a reduced Sco-H group, then
G
must be an sp-group and each
T_{p}
must be finite (and hence bounded). So, when discussing when a group with the SH property has the Sco-H one, or visa versa, there is no loss of generality in assuming that we are working with reduced sp-groups.

We will use the following helpful observation more than once.

Proposition 2.13
.

Suppose
G
is an sp-group with bounded
p
-torsion. If
\gamma:G\to G
is an endomorphism, then
G^{\prime}:=\gamma(G)
is an sp-group with bounded
p
-torsion
G^{\prime}\cap T=\gamma(T)
.

Proof.

Put
T^{\prime}:=\gamma(T)
; since
G^{\prime}/T^{\prime}
is an epimorphic image of
G/T
, it follows that
G^{\prime}/T^{\prime}
is divisible. Manifestly,
T^{\prime}\leq T
so that
T/T^{\prime}
is the torsion of
G/T^{\prime}
. Since
T
has bounded
p
-torsion, so does
T/T^{\prime}
and, in particular,
T/T^{\prime}
will be reduced. This forces that
(G^{\prime}/T^{\prime})\cap(T/T^{\prime})=0
, so that
G^{\prime}/T^{\prime}
will be torsion-free (and divisible). This yields that
G^{\prime}\cap T=T^{\prime}
, and thanks to Proposition
2.11
,
G^{\prime}
will be an sp-group, as stated.
∎

We now want to discuss which reduced groups that are (uniformly) SH are also (uniformly) Sco-H, and visa versa. The next result shows that one implication is generally valid.

Theorem 2.14
.

Suppose
G
is a reduced group. If
G
is Sco-H (or uniformly Sco-H), then
G
is SH (or, respectively, uniformly SH).

Proof.

Again
[
5
, Proposition 2.3]
implies that
G
must be an sp-group and each
T_{p}
must be finite (and hence bounded).

Suppose
\phi:G\to G
is an endomorphism and
\hat{\phi}
is its restriction to
T
. For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, set
G^{n}:=\phi^{n}(G)
and
T^{n}:=\phi^{n}(T)
. So, Proposition
2.13
allows us to conclude that each
G^{n}
is an sp-group with torsion
T^{n}
. Since
G
is assumed to be Sco-H, for some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
we can conclude that
\phi
restricts to a surjection
G^{n}\to G^{n}=G^{n+1}
. This insures that
T^{n}=T^{n+1}=\phi(T^{n})
. But, since each
T^{n}_{p}
is finite, this ensures that
\hat{\phi}
is actually an automorphism of
T^{n}
.

If we now set
P^{n}:=\prod_{p}T^{n}
, it follows that
\hat{\phi}
extends to an automorphism
\gamma
on
P^{n}
; in particular, this
\gamma
is injective. Note that
\phi
and
\gamma
agree on
T^{n}
, so they must agree on
G^{n}\leq P^{n}
, which guarantees that
\phi
is also injective on
G^{n}
, as required.
∎

So, our question reduces to attempting to describe which reduced groups that are (uniformly) SH are also (uniformly) Sco-H. Again, since every (uniformly) Sco-H group is an sp-group (compare with
[
5
]
), we may assume that
G/T
is divisible. The following illustrates one easy case where this is an equivalence.

Proposition 2.15
.

Suppose
G
is a reduced group of finite rank. Then,
G
is Sco-H (or uniformly Sco-H) iff
G
is SH (or, respectively, uniformly SH) and
G/T
is divisible.

Proof.

If
G
is (uniformly) Sco-H, then we know it is an sp-group, so that
G/T
is divisible. And by Theorem
2.14
,
G
is (uniformly) SH.

Conversely, suppose
G
is (uniformly) SH and
G/T
is divisible. Referring to Propositions
2.1
and
2.11
,
G
must be an sp-group. Let
\phi:G\to G
be an endomorphism and, for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
G^{n}=\phi^{n}(G)
. Since
G
is SH, for some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
we can conclude that
\phi
restricts to an injection
G^{n}\to G^{n}
. If, as usual,
T^{n}:=\phi^{n}(T)=G^{n}\cap T
, then each
T^{n}_{p}
is finite and
G^{n}/T^{n}
is divisible of finite rank. It now follows from
[
11
, Theorem 2.6]
that
G^{n}
is co-Bassian, and hence co-Hopfian. Therefore,
\phi
restricted to
G^{n}
must actually be surjective, proving that
G
will be (uniformly) SH, as expected.
∎

The following is obviously a restatement of Proposition
2.15
.

Corollary 2.16
.

If
G
is a reduced sp-group of finite rank, then
G
is (uniformly) SH iff it is (uniformly) Sco-H.

The following important example shows that the torsion subgroup of a group with SH may not have that property.

Example 2.17
.

There is a reduced sp-group
G
of rank 1 that is SH, but the torsion subgroup
T
of
G
is not SH.

Proof.

Basically, we can just let
G
be the group from Example 2.15 of
[
5
]
. That group was clearly reduced and had rank 1, so was of finite rank, and since it was an sp-group, the factor-group
G/T\cong{\mathbb{Q}}
was divisible. Now, since in that work it was observed that
G
is Sco-H, Theorem
2.14
or Proposition
2.15
or Corollary
2.16
all tell us that
G
is also SH. And in view of Corollary
2.2
, the group

T\cong\oplus_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}{\mathbb{Z}}(p_{n}^{n})

obviously fails to be SH, as asserted.
∎

We next aim to demonstrate that for reduced sp-groups of infinite rank, the class of SH groups is
properly
larger than the class of Sco-H groups. The following gives a simple way of constructing sp-groups that are uniformly SH.

Proposition 2.18
.

Suppose
G
is a reduced sp-group. Then
G
is uniformly SH iff
T
is (uniformly) SH.

Proof.

We may assume
T\leq G\leq P:=\prod_{p}T_{p}
and
G/T
is torsion-free divisible.

Further, assume first that
T
is SH, so that it is uniformly SH in virtue of Corollary
2.2
. Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
be an SH-index for all endomorphisms of
T
. To verify that
G
is uniformly SH, suppose that
\phi:G\to G
is an endomorphism; let
\hat{\phi}
be its restriction to
T
. It thus follows that
\hat{\phi}
is injective on
T^{n}:=\phi^{n}(T)
. Referring to Proposition
2.13
, if
G^{n}=\phi^{n}(G)
, then
G^{n}
is also an sp-group. However, Proposition
2.1
allows us to derive that each
T_{p}
is finite, so that each
T^{n}_{p}
is finite too. This assures that
\hat{\phi}
is actually an automorphism of
T^{n}
.

Furthermore, as in the proof of Proposition
2.15
, if we let
P^{n}:=\prod_{p}T_{p}^{n}
, it follows that
\hat{\phi}
extends to an automorphism
\gamma
on
P^{n}
; in particular, this
\gamma
is injective. Note that
\phi
and
\gamma
must agree on
G^{n}\leq P^{n}
, which means that
\phi
is also injective on
G^{n}
, as required.

The converse follows directly from Proposition
2.3
.
∎

The next construction substantiates our claim above:

Example 2.19
.

There is a reduced sp-group
G
that is SH, but not Sco-H.

Proof.

In
[
3
, Example 20]
an sp-group
G
was constructed such that, for each prime
p
,
T_{p}
was either 0 or isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}(p)
. In particular, looking at Proposition
2.18
,
G
will be SH. On the other hand, it was observed there that this
G
fails to be co-Hopfian, so that it also fails to be Sco-H, as required.
∎

We conclude this section with a couple of results on direct sums of (uniformly) SH groups.

Proposition 2.20
.

Suppose
G=H\oplus K
is a reduced sp-group. Then
G
is uniformly SH iff
H
and
K
are uniformly SH.

Proof.

Necessity being clear, suppose
H
are
K
are uniformly SH. By Corollary
2.4
,
T
will be SH. So, by Proposition
2.18
,
G
is uniformly SH.
∎

Proposition 2.21
.

Suppose
G=H\oplus K
, where
K
is finite (and hence torsion). Then
G
is SH iff
H
is SH.

Proof.

Necessity again being clear, suppose that
H
is SH (and
K
is finite). An elementary induction shows that it suffices to assume that
K
is a
p
-group for some prime
p
. If
S
is the torsion subgroup of
H
, then
S_{p}
will also be finite, and hence a summand of
H
. Suppose
H=H^{\prime}\oplus S_{p}
, so that
G=H^{\prime}\oplus S_{p}\oplus K
. Now,
S_{p}\oplus K
will be finite, so trivially SH. In addition, the
p
-torsion of
G
,
S_{p}\oplus K
, is fully invariant in
G
. As
H^{\prime}
is a summand of
H
, and hence SH, by Corollary
2.8
,
G
will also be SH.
∎

3.
SH groups and (global) Warfield groups

Recall that a group is
Warfield
if it is a summand of a simply presented group. In particular, it is clear that a summand of a Warfield group will be Warfield. In addition, as any torsion-free rank 1 group clearly has a simple presentation, any totally decomposable torsion-free group is simply presented, and hence Warfield.

A perhaps more useful approach to Warfield groups involves the notion of a
nice decomposition basis
, i.e., a subset
\{z_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subseteq G
such that
Z=\oplus_{i\in I}\langle z_{i}\rangle
is a free and nice subgroup of
G
such that all
p
-height functions restricted to
Z
respect the above decomposition. Then,
G
is Warfield precisely when it has a nice decomposition base such that
G/Z
is totally projective (see, for instance,
[
8
, Theorem 15.7.6]
as well as, for a more detailed information, we refer the interested reader to
[
10
]
). Fortunately, we are only concerned with Warfield groups with bounded
p
-torsion, where the deep theory of (global) Warfield groups is considerably more straightforward (compare with
[
6
]
as well).

Recall that if
N\leq G
,
p
is a prime and
\alpha
is an ordinal, then

U_{\alpha}^{p}(N)=\{x\in N:|x|_{p}\geq\alpha\land|px|_{p}>\alpha+1\}

is the
\alpha
th
p
-Ulm factor
of
N
in
G
. There is a natural injection
U_{\alpha}^{p}(N)\to U_{\alpha}^{p}(G)
, and we say
N
is
tight
in
G
if this is an isomorphism for all primes
p
and ordinals
\alpha
(compare also with
[
6
]
). This is equivalent to saying that all of the relative Ulm invariants of
N
in
G
are 0. Again, since we are only concerned with groups with bounded
p
-torsion, our ordinal
\alpha
need only take on finite values.

In what follows, let
{\mathcal{B}}
be
the class of all groups of rank 1 with bounded
p
-torsion
. Notice the pretty simple observation, as shown below, that every
G\in{\mathcal{B}}
is Warfield.

The following statement is useful for our further discussions.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
G\in{\mathcal{B}}
, then
G
is Warfield. In addition, if
G
is an arbitrary Warfield group with bounded
p
-torsion, then either
G
is torsion or
G=\oplus_{i\in I}G_{i}
, where each
G_{i}\in{\mathcal{B}}
.

Proof.

Let
G\in{\mathcal{B}}
. If
\langle z\rangle=Z\leq G
is an infinite cyclic subgroup, then it is well known that
Z
must be nice in
G
. For every prime
p
, the
p
-torsion-subgroup of
G/Z
will be the direct sum of a divisible group and a bounded group, and hence totally projective. It, therefore, follows that
z
alone constitutes a nice decomposition basis for
G
, proving that it is Warfield.

Suppose now that
G
is any Warfield group with bounded
p
-torsion; we may clearly assume that
G
is not torsion. Let
\{z_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a nice decomposition basis for
G
. For each
i\in I
, it is pretty clear that we can construct a group
A_{i}\in{\mathcal{B}}
containing
Z_{i}:=\langle z_{i}\rangle
such that the
p
-height functions on
A_{i}
, restricted to
Z_{i}
, agree with the original
p
-height functions from
G
on
Z_{i}
, and for which
Z_{i}
is a tight subgroup of
A_{i}
(for example, if
Z_{i}\leq H\leq G
is so that
H/Z_{i}
is the torsion subgroup of
G/Z_{i}
, then
G/H
is torsion-free and hence
H
is isotype in
G
. Clearly,
T
is also the torsion of
H
, which has rank 1. It now follows from

[
11
, Theorem 1]
that
H=A_{i}\oplus T^{\prime}
, where
Z_{i}
is tight in
A_{i}
). Let
S
be a torsion group with bounded
p
-torsion such that the Ulm invariants of
S
all agree with the relative Ulm invariants of
Z=\oplus_{i\in I}\langle z_{i}\rangle
in
G
. We have set things up so that the identity function
Z\to Z
extends to an isomorphism

G\to(\oplus_{i\in I}A_{i})\oplus S,

giving our desired decomposition.
∎

If
G
and
G^{\prime}
are in
{\mathcal{B}}
, we write
G\precsim G^{\prime}
if there is a homomorphism
\phi:G\to G^{\prime}
such that
\phi(G)
is not contained in the torsion subgroup
T^{\prime}
of
G^{\prime}
. Note that this last condition is equivalent to requiring that the induced homomorphism,
\overline{\phi}:G/T\to G^{\prime}/T^{\prime}
, is non-zero and, in fact, if it is non-zero, it plainly must be injective. In particular, this means that
\phi:G\to G^{\prime}
is witness to
G\precsim G^{\prime}
exactly when the kernel of
\phi
is contained in
T
. In other language, we are requiring that
\phi
is non-zero in the category
WALK
(see, for example,
[
8
, Section 15.8]
).

It follows that, if
G\precsim G^{\prime}
, then the (ordinary) types
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
of
G/T
and
G^{\prime}/T^{\prime}
satisfy
\tau\leq\tau^{\prime}
. However, suppose
G={\mathbb{Q}}
and
G^{\prime}
is any reduced sp-group of rank 1 with bounded
p
-torsion. Then,

G/T\cong G^{\prime}/T^{\prime}\cong{\mathbb{Q}},

but, since
G^{\prime}
is reduced, there is no (non-zero) homomorphism
G\to G^{\prime}
; so the converse does not hold.

We will say now that
G
and
G^{\prime}
have the same
WALK-type
if
G\precsim G^{\prime}\precsim G
, and when this happens, we write
G\approx G^{\prime}
. It is fairly clear that
\approx
is an equivalence relation on
{\mathcal{B}}
. If
G,G^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{B}}
and
G\approx G^{\prime}
, it is also clear that the rank 1 torsion-free groups
G/T
and
G^{\prime}/T^{\prime}
have the same type (regarded in the usual sense).

If
G
is a group and
x\in G
, then the
height matrix
of
x
, written as
{\mathbb{H}}_{G}(x)
has, for each prime
p
, a row consisting of the
p
-height sequence of
x
. If
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then the equality
m{\mathbb{H}}_{G}(x)={\mathbb{H}}_{G}(mx)
holds always.

The following statement gives us a concrete description of when elements of
{\mathcal{B}}
have the same WALK-type as defined above.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose
G,G^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{B}}
(so they have rank 1). Then, the following four statements are equivalent:

(1)
G\approx G^{\prime}
.

(2) If
z\in G
and
z^{\prime}\in G^{\prime}
are non-torsion elements, then

m{\mathbb{H}}_{G}(z)=m^{\prime}{\mathbb{H}}_{G^{\prime}}(z^{\prime})

for some
m,m^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

(3)
G\cong A\oplus S
and
G^{\prime}\cong A^{\prime}\oplus S^{\prime}
, where
A\cong A^{\prime}
have rank 1 and tight infinite cyclic subgroups, say
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
(so that both
S
,
S^{\prime}
are torsion).

(4)
G\oplus B\cong G^{\prime}\oplus B^{\prime}
for some torsion groups
B
and
B^{\prime}
with bounded
p
-torsion.

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2): Suppose
\alpha:G\to G^{\prime}
and
\alpha^{\prime}:G^{\prime}\to G
bear witness to
G\precsim G^{\prime}\precsim G
. Let
Z=\langle z\rangle\leq G
and
Z^{\prime}:=\langle z^{\prime}\rangle\leq G^{\prime}
. Replacing
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
by some multiples, we may assume
\alpha(Z)\leq Z^{\prime}
and
\alpha^{\prime}(Z^{\prime})\leq Z
. Suppose also
\alpha(z)=k^{\prime}z^{\prime}\in Z^{\prime}
and
\alpha^{\prime}(z^{\prime})=kz\in Z
. It follows that

{\mathbb{H}}_{G}(z)\leq{\mathbb{H}}_{G^{\prime}}(\alpha(z))=k^{\prime}{\mathbb{H}}_{G^{\prime}}(z^{\prime}))\leq k^{\prime}{\mathbb{H}}_{G}(\alpha^{\prime}(z^{\prime}))=kk^{\prime}{\mathbb{H}}_{G}(z)).

Observe that, if
p
is not a divisor of
k
or
k^{\prime}
, then this implies that
z
and
z^{\prime}
have the same sequence of
p
-heights. And for each prime divisor
p
of either
k
or
k^{\prime}
, the
p
-height sequences of
z
and
z^{\prime}
eventually look like either
(m,m+1,m+2,\dots)
(where
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
) or
(\infty,\infty,\infty,\dots)
; in particular, after some initial segments, these
p
-height sequences must be equal. Since there are a finite number of such primes, it is readily seen that (2) is true.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(3): Replacing
z
by
mz
and
z^{\prime}
by
m^{\prime}z
, there is no loss of generality in assuming that
{\mathbb{H}}_{G}(z)={\mathbb{H}}_{G^{\prime}}(z^{\prime})
. It follows that
z\to z^{\prime}
extends to a
p
-height-preserving isomorphism
Z\to Z^{\prime}
. It also follows from
[
11
, Theorem 1]
that
G=A\oplus S
, where
Z
is tight in
A
and, similarly,
G^{\prime}\cong A^{\prime}\oplus S^{\prime}
. Since
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
will be nice subgroups of
A
and
A^{\prime}
respectively, all of whose relative Ulm invariants are 0, we can deduce that
Z\cong Z^{\prime}
extends to
A\cong A^{\prime}
, as required.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(4 ): Letting
B=S^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}=S
, we have

G\oplus B=(A\oplus S)\oplus S^{\prime}\cong(A^{\prime}\oplus S^{\prime})\oplus S=G^{\prime}\oplus B^{\prime}.

(4)
\Rightarrow
(1): It straightforwardly follows that the composition, say
\gamma
, of the natural homomorphisms

G\to G\oplus B\cong G^{\prime}\oplus B^{\prime}\to G^{\prime},

after modding out by their respective torsion subgroups, becomes a sequence of isomorphisms

G/T\cong(G\oplus B)/(T\oplus B)\cong(G^{\prime}\oplus B^{\prime})/(T^{\prime}\oplus B^{\prime})\cong G^{\prime}/T^{\prime}.

In particular, we cannot have
\gamma(G)\leq T^{\prime}
, so that
G\precsim G^{\prime}
. The reverse relation is analogous, ensuring the proof.
∎

The following criterion gives a complete description of the uniformly SH Warfield groups.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose
G=\oplus_{i\in I}G_{i}
is a Warfield group with torsion subgroup
T
such that each
G_{i}\in{\mathcal{B}}
, and let
T_{i}
be the corresponding torsion subgroup of
G_{i}
so that
T=\oplus_{i\in I}T_{i}
.

(1) If
G
is SH, then there is an
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that there is no subset
\{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{n-1},i_{n}\}
of
I
with

n
-elements such that

G_{i_{1}}\precsim G_{i_{2}}\precsim\cdots\precsim G_{i_{n-1}}\precsim G_{i_{n}}.

(2)
G
is uniformly SH iff there is a positive integer
n
as in (1) and
T
is SH.

Proof.

Considering (1), we argue by contrapositive, so assume the ordering condition fails. Put

L:=\{(j,k)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}:j\leq k\}.

We claim that there is a subset
\{G_{j,k}:(j,k)\in L\}
of (distinct) elements of
\{G_{i}:i\in I\}
such that, if
1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq k
, then
G_{j,k}\precsim G_{j^{\prime},k}
: To begin, let
G_{1,1}
be arbitrary. Suppose
k>1
and we have chosen
G_{j,k^{\prime}}
for all
(j,k^{\prime})\in L
with
k^{\prime}<k
satisfying our conditions. By our hypothesis, we can find

n=k(k+1)/2=1+2+\cdots+k

elements of the
G
s that are totally ordered by the operation
\precsim
. Evidently, at least
k
of these elements do not appear in the list of
G_{j,k^{\prime}}
with
k^{\prime}<k
chosen previously. Labeling these
k
elements by
(j,k)
with
1\leq j\leq k
in ascending order using
\precsim
completes our inductive definition.

Define
\phi:G\to G
as follows: If
(j,k)\in L
with
j<k
, let
\phi
on
G_{j,k}
be a homomorphism that bears witness to
G_{j,k}\precsim G_{j+1,k}
. We also define
\phi
to be 0 on each
G_{k,k}
, as well as on any
G_{i}
, where
i
is an index that does not appear in the list
G_{j,k}
for
(j,k)\in L
.

Furthermore, for any
k
, let
z_{k}\in G_{1,k}
be a non-torsion element. It is readily seen that
z_{k}^{k-1}\neq 0
, but
z_{k}^{k}=0
. This gives that
G
is not SH, as required.

For point (2), the implication
(\Rightarrow)
follows from (1) and Proposition
2.3
, so assume
T
is SH and
n
is as in (1). As
T
will also be uniformly SH, there is an
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
which is a uniform SH-exponent for
T
.

Clearly, the existence of such an
n
implies that, if
I^{\prime}\subseteq I
is non-empty, then
\{G_{i}:i\in I^{\prime}\}
will have a maximal element under the relation
\precsim
.

Let
I_{1}\subseteq I
be the collection of indices of those summands that are maximal with respect to
\precsim
. Next, let
I_{2}\subseteq I\setminus I_{1}
be the collection of indices of the maximal elements of the remaining groups, again under the relation
\precsim
. Note that, if
i\in I_{2}
, then for some
i^{\prime}\in I_{1}
we must have
G_{i}\prec G_{i^{\prime}}
. Continuing to define
I_{3},I_{4},\dots
in this way, the last sentence tells us that it must terminate after at most
n
stages, i.e.,
I
will be the disjoint union
I_{1}\cup\cdots\cup I_{n}
.

Apparently, defining
i\sim i^{\prime}
to mean
G_{i}\approx G_{i^{\prime}}
gives an equivalence relation on
I
, and each equivalence class is contained in precisely one
I_{j}
and has at most
n
elements. Letting

\mathcal{I}_{j}:=\{[i]~|~i\in I_{j}\}

be the corresponding partition of
I_{j}
into equivalence classes, again each such equivalence class in
\mathcal{I}_{j}
will have at most
n
elements.

Let
K_{0}:=\emptyset
and, for
j=1,\dots,n
, let

K_{j}:=I_{1}\cup\cdots\cup I_{j}.

And for
j=0,1,\dots,n
, let

H_{j}:=T+\oplus_{i\in K_{j}}G_{i};

so, in particular,
H_{0}=T
. Obviously,
H_{0}=T
is fully invariant in
G
, and we assert that, if
j=1,2,\dots,n
, then
H_{j}
is, as well: This basically follows since if
j<j^{\prime}
, and
i\in I_{j}
and
i^{\prime}\in I_{j^{\prime}}
, then we cannot have that
G_{i}\precsim G_{i^{\prime}}
, so that any homomorphism
\sigma:G_{i}\to G_{i^{\prime}}
must satisfy
\sigma(G_{i})\leq T_{i^{\prime}}
, i.e.,
\sigma
is 0 in the category WALK.

We next prove by induction, for
j=0,1,\dots,n
, that
H_{j}
has the number
m+jn
as a uniform SH-exponent. This is true by definition for
j=0
, so assume it holds for
j-1<n
. So, let
\phi
be any endomorphism of
H_{j}
. Note that
\phi
induces an endomorphism

\overline{\phi}:\overline{H}:=H_{j}/H_{j-1}\to\overline{H}

and, in view of Lemma
2.5
, it suffices to show that
n
is an SH-exponent for
\overline{\phi}
.

Note that
\overline{H}\cong\oplus_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{j}}C_{\alpha}
, where each
C_{\alpha}=\oplus_{i\in\alpha}(G_{i}/T_{i})
is torsion-free of rank at most
n
. If
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}
are distinct elements of
I_{j}
, and
i\in\alpha
,
i^{\prime}\in\alpha^{\prime}
, then
G_{i}
and
G_{i^{\prime}}
are not comparable via the operation
\precsim
, meaning that if
\gamma:G_{i}\to G_{i^{\prime}}
, then we must have that
\gamma(G_{i})\subseteq T_{i^{\prime}}
. It thereby follows that
\overline{\phi}(C_{\alpha})\leq C_{\alpha}
for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}_{j}
. Exploiting now point (1.A) quoted above, this restriction has SH-exponent
n
, so by point (1.B) alluded above, the map
\overline{\phi}
also has SH-exponent
n
, thus finishing the proof.
∎

We now note some variations on the last result.

Corollary 3.4
.

Suppose
G=\oplus_{i\in I}G_{i}
is a Warfield group, where each
G_{i}\in{\mathcal{B}}
and
T
is SH. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)
G
is uniformly SH;

(2)
G
is SH;

(3) There is an
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that there is no subset
\{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{n-1},i_{n}\}
of
I
with

n
-elements such that

G_{i_{1}}\precsim G_{i_{2}}\precsim\cdots\precsim G_{i_{n-1}}\precsim G_{i_{n}}.

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2) is obvious, (2)
\Rightarrow
(3) is by Theorem
3.3
(1) and (3)
\Rightarrow
(1)
is by Theorem
3.3
(2).
∎

Again, in the torsion-free case, the Warfield groups agree with the completely decomposable groups, and what we have termed the WALK-type of a rank 1 torsion-free group
G
agrees with its type,
\tau(G)
in the usual sense. So, if
T=0
in the last result (which is trivially SH), we then arrive at the following assertion, which is a restatement of the main result of
[
4
]
:

Corollary 3.5
.

Suppose
G
is a completely decomposable torsion-free group. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)
G
is uniformly SH;

(2)
G
is SH;

(3) There is an
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that there is no subset
\{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{n-1},i_{n}\}
of
I
with

n
-elements such that

\tau(G_{i_{1}})\leq\tau(G_{i_{2}})\leq\cdots\leq\tau(G_{i_{n-1}})\leq\tau(G_{i_{n}}).

We finish this section with an application on direct sums and summands of uniformly SH Warfield groups.

Proposition 3.6
.

Suppose
G=H\oplus K
is a Warfield group. Then
G
is uniformly SH iff
H
and
K
are both uniformly SH.

Proof.

Necessity being obvious, suppose
H
and
K
are uniformly SH. By Corollary
2.4
,
T
is SH.

According to
[
10
]
, both
H
and
K
are also Warfield groups.
Suppose
H=\oplus_{i\in I}H_{i}
and
K=\oplus_{j\in J}K_{j}
, where each
H_{i}
and
K_{j}
are in
{\mathcal{B}}
. Suppose also
n_{H}
and
n_{K}
are as in the statement of Corollary
3.4
, witnessing that
H
and
K
, respectively, are SH. If we let
n=n_{H}+n_{K}
, it is not too hard to verify that the condition of Corollary
3.4
is satisfied for
G
using this value
n
, so that
G
is also uniformly SH, as asked for.
∎

4.
More Warfield groups

Deciding when a Warfield group is SH, as opposed to the uniformly SH Warfield groups described in Corollary
3.4
, appears to be a decidedly more difficult problem. The following non-trivial argument handles just one simple case of this question.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose
G\in{\mathcal{B}}
(is a Warfield group of rank 1) with a tight subgroup
Z=\langle z\rangle
of rank 1 such that each
T_{p}
is cyclic. For any prime
p
, let
T_{p}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}(p^{e_{p}})
and if
e_{p}\neq 0
, let
j_{p}>0
be the size of the single jump in the
p
-height sequence of
z
, and suppose that jump takes place between
|{p^{k_{p}-1}z}|_{p}
and
|{p^{k_{p}}z}|_{p}
. So, the
p
-height sequence of
z
satisfies

m_{p}:=|{z}|_{p}<\cdots<m_{p}+k_{p}-1=|{p^{k_{p}-1}z}|_{p}<m_{p}+k_{p}+j_{p}=|{p^{k_{p}}z}|_{p}.

Then
G
is SH iff there is an
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
e_{p}\leq M\min\{j_{p},k_{p}\}
for all primes
p
.

Proof.

We begin our argument by giving an explicit description of what this
G
must look like by giving it a specific simple presentation.

Let
\ell_{p}:=\min\{j_{p},k_{p}\}
for all primes
p
. In addition, let
\sigma_{p}=e_{p}+j_{p}+1
when
j_{p}\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and if
j_{p}=\infty
, let
\sigma_{p}=\infty
. Let
x_{p}\in G
satisfy
p^{m_{p}}x_{p}=z
, and let
\{y^{i}_{p}\}_{0<i<\sigma_{p}}\subseteq G
be such that

py^{1}_{p}=p^{k_{p}}z=p^{m_{p}+k_{p}}x_{p}

and
py^{i}_{p}=y^{i-1}_{p}
whenever
1<i<\sigma_{p}
. Note that
e_{p}=m_{p}+k_{p}
and
T_{p}=\langle x_{p}-y_{p}^{e_{p}}\rangle
.

Suppose
G
is SH; we need to find an integer
M>0
such that
e_{p}\leq M\ell_{p}
for all primes. In doing so, we will define a homomorphism
\phi:G\to G
such that
\phi(z)=0
, so that
\phi(G)\leq T
. Since
G
is SH, there is an
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\textrm{ker}(\phi^{M})=\textrm{ker}(\phi^{M+1})
, and we will show that this
M
must satisfy the indicated inequality. We construct
\phi
by defining it one prime at a time, specifying each
\phi(x_{p})
and
\phi(y^{i}_{p})
.

We consider two possible cases:

Case 1
–
j_{p}\leq k_{p}<\infty
, so that
\ell_{p}=j_{p}
: In this case we set

\phi(x_{p})=0\ \ \mathrm{and}\ \ \phi(y^{e_{p}+j_{p}}_{p})=y^{e_{p}}-x_{p}\in T_{p}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}(p^{e_{p}}).

It follows that

\phi(x_{p}-y^{e_{p}}_{p})=\phi(x_{p})-\phi(p^{j_{p}}y^{e_{p}+j_{p}})=p^{j_{p}}(x_{p}-y^{e_{p}}_{p}),

i.e.,
\phi
restricts to multiplication by
p^{j_{p}}
on
T_{p}
. Since
T_{p}
is cyclic, it follows that

0=\phi^{M}(x_{p}-y^{e_{p}}_{p})=p^{M\cdot j_{p}}(x_{p}-y^{e_{p}}_{p}).

This gives that
e_{p}\leq M\cdot j_{p}=M\cdot\ell_{p}
, as asserted.

Case 2
–
k_{p}\leq j_{p}
, so that
\ell_{p}=k_{p}
: In this case, we set

\phi(x_{p}):=p^{k_{p}}(x_{p}-y^{e_{p}}_{p})\in T_{p}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}(p^{e_{p}})\ \ \mathrm{and}\ \ \phi(y^{i}_{p})=0\ \ \mathrm{for\ all}\ i>0.

Note that

\phi(z)=\phi(p^{m_{p}}x_{p})=p^{m_{p}+k_{p}}(x_{p}-y^{e_{p}}_{p})=p^{e_{p}}(x_{p}-y^{e_{p}}_{p})=0,

as required.

It now follows that
\phi(x_{p}-y^{e_{p}}_{p})=p^{k_{p}}(x_{p}-y^{e_{p}}_{p})
, i.e.,
\phi
restricts to multiplication by
p^{k_{p}}
on
T_{p}
. Again, since
T_{p}
is cyclic, it follows that

0=\phi^{M}(x_{p}-y^{e_{p}}_{p})=p^{M\cdot k_{p}}(x_{p}-y^{e_{p}}_{p}).

This forces that
e_{p}\leq M\cdot k_{p}=M\cdot\ell_{p}
, as claimed.

Conversely, suppose we are given such an integer
M
; we want to show
G
is SH. To that goal, let
\phi:G\to G
be an endomorphism with kernel
K
.

Suppose first that
K\cap Z=0
, i.e.,
K\leq T
. It thus follows that there is an
N
such that, for all primes
p>N
, if we consider the endomorphism of
p
-localizations
\phi_{(p)}:G_{(p)}\to G_{(p)}
, we can conclude that
\phi_{(p)}
restricted to
Z_{(p)}
is an automorphism of
Z_{(p)}
. It thereby follows that, for all
p>N
and
k<\omega
, the map
\phi
induces an automorphism
U_{k}^{p}(G)\to U_{k}^{p}(G)
. However, since each
T_{p}
is finite, it follows that
\phi
restricts to an automorphism of
T_{p}
. If
S=\oplus_{q<N}T_{q}
, which is finite, we can infer that
K\leq S
. Similar reasoning shows that, if
K^{j}
is the kernel of
\phi^{j}
, then
K^{j}\leq S
. However, since
S
is finite and
K^{j}\leq K^{j+1}
, it follow that for some
j<\omega
we have
K^{j}=K^{j+1}
, as desired.

So, we may assume that
K\cap Z\neq 0
, i.e.,
\phi(G)\leq T
(it is here that we need to use our
M
).

We claim that it will suffice to show that, for almost all primes
p
, we have
T_{p}^{M}=0
: Suppose we have verified this. It then follows that
G^{M+1}\leq T^{M}
must be finite. But this informs us for some
n\geq M+1
that
\phi
must be an isomorphism on
G^{n}
.

Now, to verify for almost all primes
p
that
T_{p}^{M}=0
, since each
T_{p}
is cyclic, it suffices to check that for almost all primes
p
we must have

\phi(G[p^{\ell_{p}}])=0.

In fact, let
\mathcal{F}
be the (finite) set of primes such that
\phi(z)\in\oplus_{p\in\mathcal{F}}T_{p}
. We will inspect now the above condition whenever
p
is not in
\mathcal{F}
. In fact, localizing at some
p\not\in\mathcal{F}
, we may assume that
G=G_{(p)}
and
\phi(z)=0
.

We again break into two distinguish cases:

Case 1
–
j_{p}\leq k_{p}<\infty
: It follows that
G[p^{\ell_{p}}]=G[p^{j_{p}}]
is generated by

p^{k_{p}-j_{p}}z-y_{p}^{j_{p}}
. We know that
\phi(z)=0
, so that
\phi(p^{k_{p}-j_{p}}z)=0
. In addition,

y_{p}^{j_{p}}=p^{e_{p}}y_{p}^{e_{p}+j_{p}}
which ensures that
\phi(y_{p}^{j_{p}})=0
, completing this case.

Case 2
–
k_{p}\leq j_{p}
: It follows that
G[p^{\ell_{p}}]=G[p^{k_{p}}]
is generated by

z-y_{p}^{k_{p}}
. We know that
\phi(z)=0
. Since
e_{p}+k_{p}\leq e_{p}+j_{p}
, we obtain

y_{p}^{k_{p}}=p^{e_{p}}y_{p}^{e_{p}+k_{p}}
. Thus, we can deduce that
\phi(y_{p}^{k_{p}})=0
, completing this case, and hence the whole proof.
∎

The group of

. To see that, let
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},\dots
be the collection of all primes. In
P:=\prod_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}{\mathbb{Z}}(p_{n}^{n})
, we let
z
be the vector whose
n

1\in{\mathbb{Z}}(p_{n}^{n})
. Define the group
G
as that which satisfies
T\leq G\leq P
with
G/\langle{z}\rangle
being the torsion subgroup of
P/\langle{z}\rangle
. So, for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the
p
-height sequence of
{z}
is given by

(0,1,2,\cdots,n-1,\infty,\infty,\infty,\dots)

In the notation of Proposition
4.1
, for each prime
p_{n}
