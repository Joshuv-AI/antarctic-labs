Integrating developmental and macroevolutionary approaches reveals a staminodial origin of all alternisepalous organs in Caryophyllaceae
Authors: Riley J. Rees, Andrea D. Appleton, John J. Schenk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPremiseThe identity of sterile floral organs in second‐whorl, alternisepalous positions within Caryophyllaceae remains contentious, having been described as staminodes, “petaloids,” or petals homologous with those of other Pentapetalae. Limited ingroup and outgroup sampling and floral developmental data and inadequate phylogenetic comparative methods in previous studies have added to this uncertainty. Crucially, staminodes have not been analyzed as discrete characters, leaving their homology with other structures unresolved.MethodsWe reconstructed a chronogram of 775 species using five fossil calibrations for Caryophyllaceae and outgroups. Floral traits of 613 species, including number and position of petal‐like structures, staminodes, and stamens, were quantified. Stochastic character mapping estimated ancestral character states and transition rates. Literature reviews provided developmental data to characterize common floral forms; transition models were used to assessed patterns of floral development and evolution.ResultsStaminodes evolved at the root of an apetalous Caryophyllaceae and were maintained throughout the backbone, being lost multiple times and becoming petal‐like, especially in the Plurcaryophyllaceae in which a second whorl of stamens evolved in the alternisepalous position through the splitting of the alternisepalous primordia. The Caryophyllaceae crown ancestor likely had a single whorl of antesepalous stamens alternating with alternisepalous staminodes.ConclusionsCaryophyllaceae flowers are apetalous: All alternisepalous sterile structures are staminodes. Since their origin, staminodes have subsequently undergone multiple instances of loss, reduction, and elaboration. Their evolutionary history parallels patterns in early angiosperm lineages, making Caryophyllaceae a valuable system for investigating the role of staminodes in floral function and diversification.

The evolutionary origin and subsequent innovations of flowers are considered key factors that drove the diversification of angiosperms (Endress, 2011; APG, 2016; O'Meara et al., 2016). Petals are especially important to species diversification because they are specialized to attract diverse pollinators, driving reproductive isolation among species (Hofmann, 1994; Schemske and Bradshaw, 1999; Whittall and Hodges, 2007; Irish, 2009). Petal evolution has been remarkably dynamic, characterized by multiple independent instances of both loss and gain, with gains arising from the developmental cooption of different organs, including sepals and stamens (Ronse De Craene, 2007; Irish, 2009; Endress, 2011; Brockington et al., 2012). The dynamic nature of floral evolution is exemplified well in the Caryophyllales, in which petal‐like structures were initially lost in the core Caryophyllales clade and subsequently regained multiple times through modification of other floral organs (Ronse De Craene, 2008; Brockington et al., 2009). In Aizoaceae, for example, andropetals are hypothesized to have evolved from the sterilization of stamens (Brockington et al., 2009). In contrast, bracteopetals are derived from sepals in Portulacaceae, Cactaceae, and relatives (Ronse De Craene, 2007; Brockington et al., 2009). In Caryophyllaceae, however, the homology of petaloid structures remains debated.

Caryophyllaceae is ecologically, morphologically, florally, and taxonomically diverse, with approximately 2200–3000 species that are distributed across all seven continents (Bittrich, 1993). The family is currently classified into 11 tribes (Harbaugh et al., 2010), many of which exhibit highly divergent floral morphologies (Bittrich, 1993; Figure 1). Flowers generally have four‐to‐five sepals, a whorl of four‐to‐five stamens in antesepalous positions, and two‐to‐five carpels (Bittrich, 1993; Ronse De Craene, 2013). The flowers often have organs located in alternisepalous positions that have been described as stamens, petals, “petaloids,” staminodes, or a combination thereof (Chaudhri, 1968; Bittrich, 1993; Rabeler and Hartman, 1993; Ronse De Craene and Brockington, 2013; Wei and Ronse De Craene, 2019; Appleton and Schenk, 2021). The homology of the sterile structures in these positions remains a subject of debate, but two primary hypotheses have emerged. Ronse De Craene (2007), Ronse De Craene and Brockington (2013), Leins et al. (2001), and others described the sterile structures in some species, such as Silene, as petaloids or andropetals, suggesting or outright claiming a staminodial origin. We refer to this as the staminodial hypothesis, in which andropetaloids/staminodes evolved from stamens in an apetalous ancestor, a view supported by ancestral character state estimations (Greenberg and Donoghue, 2011). This staminodial hypothesis was later challenged by Wei and Ronse De Craene (2019), who postulated that the core Caryophyllales clade was not ancestrally apetalous. Instead, they proposed that Caryophyllaceae petals are homologous with those in other Pentapetalae. According to this hypothesis, Caryophyllaceae retained true petals from a common ancestor deeper in the eudicot clade, while other lineages within Caryophyllales lost petals in parallel.

Wei and Ronse De Craene (2019) postulated, while critically emphasizing the lack of homology assessment and the complexities of floral evolution and development in the Greenberg and Donoghue (2011) study, that ancestral petals in Caryophyllaceae were present but subsequently lost in several derived genera. Although the Wei and Ronse De Craene (2019) study had several strengths, including broader outgroup inclusion and developmental data, it was limited by (1) sparse sampling at the tribal and species levels (sampling only18 ingroup taxa) and (2) statistically inconsistent phylogenetic comparative methods, relying on parsimony to infer ancestral states and their transitions despite its well‐known limitations (Schluter et al., 1997; Revell and Harmon, 2022). Limitations from both studies call into question the robustness of their evolutionary conclusions. More problematic, however, is that neither study directly included staminodes as character data in their analyses, even as numerous studies hypothesized staminodes are present in Caryophyllaceae (e.g., Leins et al., 2001). Excluding staminodes from consideration when inferring the origin of petal‐like structures fails to account for the possibility that the showy petaloid structures in some Caryophyllaceae genera (e.g., Silene) may share an evolutionary origin with staminodes present elsewhere in the family (e.g., Paronychia).

Andropetals are another term for what Botnaru and Schenk (2019) referred to as visually coopted staminodes (sterile stamens that have experienced dorsal‐ventral flattening with marginal expansions and often pigmentation to visually attract pollinators), and we will refer to the structures as staminodes here. Leins et al. (2001) inferred a staminodial identity in Silene and Agrostemma, consistent with the interpretation of visually coopted staminodes, but the other 80–90 genera were not surveyed. Other staminode functional types (sensu Botnaru and Schenk, 2019), however, have been identified in Caryophyllaceae. Appleton and Schenk (2021) and Schenk and Appleton (2023), for example, demonstrated that the sterile alternisepalous structures in Paronychia (Paronychieae) and Pollichia (Polycarpaeae) were vestigial staminodes, and Wei and Ronse De Craene (2019) came to the same conclusion in Gymnocarpos (Paronychieae). Although staminodes have not been formally analyzed, they are important to consider because they occupy the same floral position as the “petals,” “andropetals,” or “petaloids” described by previous studies in other Caryophyllaceae genera, raising questions about the homology of sterile structures in alternisepalous positions across the family. Are what Appleton and Schenk (2021) and others called staminodes in alternisepalous positions homologous with what Ronse De Craene (2013) and others called petals or petaloids in other clades?

Here we asked: What are the sterile structures in alternisepalous positions, when did they first evolve, and how have they changed across the Caryophyllaceae phylogeny? We drew upon the strengths of the Greenberg and Donoghue (2011) study to densely sample the Caryophyllaceae phylogeny and combined them with the strengths of the Wei and Ronse De Craene (2019) study to analyze floral development data. We then expanded on those studies by collecting data from herbarium specimens to assess positional homology and quantify floral structures; reconstructing a densely sampled, robust, time‐calibrated phylogeny; and applying best practices in phylogenetic comparative methods. In addition, our study combined analyses to characterize the diversity and evolution of floral development in Caryophyllaceae. Applying a developmental approach allowed us to assess patterns in androecial evolution across the Caryophyllaceae phylogeny, with a particular focus on staminode evolution. Through our multifaceted approach, we gained novel insight into the floral evolution of Caryophyllaceae to explain the origin of its many floral forms.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

To infer floral evolution in Caryophyllaceae, we applied macroevolutionary and evolutionary developmental (evo‐devo) approaches with the goal of comprehensively sampling the Caryophyllaceae phylogeny, reconstructing a time‐calibrated chronogram, and applying phylogenetic comparative methods best practices (sensu Revell and Harmon, 2022) to ensure our results were robust to statistical limitations.

Phylogenetic reconstructions

Our objective was to achieve broad and even representation of species across the Caryophyllaceae phylogeny by targeting sequences from all major lineages and proportionally sampling within genera according to species diversity. We utilized three markers to reconstruct the Caryophyllaceae phylogeny: matK (Johnson and Soltis, 1995; Appendix S1) and trnL‐trnF (Taberlet et al., 1991) from the chloroplast genome, and the nuclear ribosomal ITS1, 5.8S, and ITS2 regions (Markos and Baldwin, 2001). All three markers are commonly applied in phylogenetic studies to resolve major Caryophyllaceae clades (e.g., Fior et al., 2006; Harbaugh et al., 2010; Schenk et al., 2018) and within subclades (e.g., Sakai et al., 2006; Schenk et al., 2018). The use of multiple regions from different genomes limited biases from any given marker, and noncoding regions are common in phylogenomic studies because natural selection does not act as strongly on them (Taberlet et al., 1991). Sequences were downloaded from GenBank (Clark et al., 2016) for as many species of Caryophyllaceae that met the following criteria: The sampling was proportional to the species diversity within each genus to avoid overrepresenting some groups that have had greater research attention (e.g., Silene), and species needed to be represented by sequences from at least two markers. Exceptions were made for genera in which no species had at least two markers available to represent as many genera as possible. In situations where a species was represented by more than a single sequence for a particular gene, we included all sequences and then removed duplicates after the preliminary alignments and phylogenetic analyses, preferring the longest sequence if the sequences of the species formed a monophyletic group. If species were recovered as nonmonophyletic, sequences were scrutinized in MESQUITE v3.10 (Maddison and Maddison, 2009) to assure that nonmonophyly was not due to misidentification, poor sequence quality, or unoptimized alignments. Misidentified taxa (identified by being placed in the wrong tribe or in an aberrant position compared to the other markers) and poor‐quality sequences were removed when homology among sites was difficult to assess. This approach allowed us to more carefully assess species and their relationships while avoiding aberrant results.

The nomenclature of Caryophyllaceae has experienced significant changes, with species being transferred across several genera (e.g., Dillenberger and Kadereit, 2014). To accommodate nomenclatural changes, we referenced recent systematic works, including Flora of North America North of Mexico (Rabeler and Hartman, 1993), published works (e.g., Hartman and Rabeler, 2004; Sharples and Tripp, 2019), and Kew's online Plants of the World (https://powo.science.kew.org/), while updating names according to recent treatments.

Once sequences were reduced to a single read per taxon, individual gene alignments were generated in MAFFT 7.150b (Katoh, 2013) with 2000 iterations. Mafft alignments were then visually scrutinized, and a few minor adjustments were made only if doing so reduced homoplasy. Alignments for all three markers were then combined, and data partitions and DNA substitution model‐fit were assessed in IQ‐TREE 1.6.12 (Nguyen et al., 2014). The best‐fit model and partition strategy was then used for phylogenetic inference in RAxML‐NG (Stamatakis, 2006), optimized with maximum likelihood. Ten RAxML analyses were independently run with different seed numbers to more thoroughly explore tree space than a single analysis, and an 11th analysis was run with the phylogeny produced by IQ‐TREE as the starting tree. Among the 11 phylogenetic analyses, we chose the phylogeny with the largest log‐likelihood value to represent our best phylogenetic hypothesis of Caryophyllaceae.

We reconstructed a chronogram to be applied as a phylogenetic hypothesis in all phylogenetic comparative approaches. A chronogram was inferred in BEAST 2 2.7.6 (Bouckaert et al., 2019), a Bayesian relaxed‐clock‐dating‐approach that allowed fossil calibrations to be integrated as priors, while accounting for uncertainty in our estimates of phylogenetic reconstructions, fossil priors, and model parameters. We conducted three independent analyses to evaluate convergence and stationarity, and all analyses were done without phylogenetic constraints to account for phylogenetic uncertainty. We applied the best‐fit GTR + I + G model with four gamma categories and unpartitioned data as estimated as the best strategy with IQ‐TREE. Starting values for all parameters were estimated from a preliminary 50‐million‐generation run to allow the chains to more quickly reach stationarity. We applied Yule and uncorrelated lognormal‐relaxed‐clock models in analyses that started with a randomly generated phylogeny. Operators were adjusted based on our preliminary analysis to allow the chains to spend proportionally more time estimating important parameters (e.g., phylogeny) compared to less important and easier to infer parameters (e.g., DNA transitions). Chains were run for 200 million generations, sampling every 2000 steps. We applied a 50% burn‐in for all three analyses. Convergence of the runs was assessed in the R 4.6.0 (R Development Core Team, 2005) package rwty (Warren et al., 2017). Convergence, stationarity and sampling were assessed in Tracer 1.7.2 (Rambaut et al., 2018). We summarized the BEAST analysis with a 95% maximum clade credibility (MCC) tree from a single run with TreeAnnotator 2.7.6 (Drummond and Rambaut, 2007).

To time‐calibrate the chronogram, we initially applied six fossil calibrations. Three of the fossils were from Caryophyllaceae—Caryophylloflora paleogenica G.J. Jord. & Macphail, Periporopollenites polyoratus (Couper) Stover & Partridge, and Stellaria L.—and their placement in the phylogeny and priors were justified by Schenk et al. (2018). Because our sampling was more in depth throughout Caryophyllales than in the study by Schenk et al. (2018), we applied three additional calibrations from the Paleobiology Database (https://paleobiodb.org/) for clades outside of Caryophyllaceae. Additional fossils included Polyporina cribraria Srivastava from the Upper Cretaceous to calibrate the crown of Amaranthaceae with a lognormal prior and mean of 1, standard deviation of 0.5, and offset of 66 million years (My) as done by Xu et al. (2024) to create a 95% distribution between 67.2–72.2 million years ago (Ma). A calibration based on a Miocene seed fossil of Parvangula randeckensis Hiltermann & Schmitz calibrated clade 1 of Chenopodieae, which was applied with a lognormal prior, a mean of 1, standard deviation of 0.5, and offset of 23.3 My following Xu et al. (2024), creating a 95% distribution between 24.5–29.5 Ma. The last fossil was Polygonocarpum johnsonii Manchester & O'Leary, a fruit fossil from the late Cretaceous (Maastrichtian), which was used as a prior on the crown of the Polygonaceae clade. We applied a lognormal prior, mean of 1, standard deviation of 0.5, and a 66 Ma offset to create a 95% distribution between 67.2 and 72.2 Ma following Yao et al. (2019). A preliminary analysis was run in BEAST 2 without sequence data for 50 million generations to determine whether fossil calibration priors interact. The results of our preliminary analysis suggested that the Caryophylloflora paleogenica calibration interacted with the other calibration priors. A second preliminary analysis with C. paleogenica removed produced posterior distributions for the five remaining fossil calibrations that were consistent with the prior distributions. We, consequently, removed C. paleogenica in the final analyses.

Developmental data

Differentiating between staminodes and petals can be challenging (Ronse De Craene et al., 1998). When structural identity is ambiguous, developmental studies are often needed to reveal homology (Ronse De Craene and Smets, 2001; Hufford, 2003; Appleton and Schenk, 2021; Jeiter and Smets, 2023; Schenk and Appleton, 2023). We assessed developmental patterns using our previous studies, supplemented with other published SEMs or detailed descriptions of developing flowers through a literature search of Caryophyllaceae and outgroups. We used the terms “floral development” and the taxonomic group of interest (Achatocarpaceae, Aizoaceae, Amaranthaceae, Caryophyllaceae, Molluginaceae, Paronychieae, Petiveriaceae, Phytolaccaceae, Polygonaceae, and Sileneae) in a search of Google Scholar (https://scholar.google.com/) and Scopus (https://www.scopus.com/). We targeted studies that included images at key developmental stages, beginning with the initiation of the floral meristem, then the initiation of the first stamen, staminode, or petaloid primordia, and ending with the completion of floral morphogenesis. To define the developmental diversity in Caryophyllaceae and close relatives, we examined micrographs to decipher unique floral forms and developmental series by considering the presence and absence of whorls, novel floral organs (=staminodes), developmental order of structures, and relative positions of organs within the flower. Although interesting from a developmental standpoint, we did not consider developmental abnormalities in Scleranthus perennis, Stellaria media, or Sagina apetala (Kraft, 1916; Rohweder, 1970; Ronse De Craene et al., 1998) as unique forms in analyses. In addition, Schiedea (Sclerantheae) has antesepalous nectaries, that Harris et al. (2012) determined to be receptacular, a treatment we follow here.

Morphological characterization

Herbarium specimens of Caryophyllaceae and selected outgroups were characterized with the aid of a stereomicroscope for sepal number, petaloid/staminode number and position, and stamen number and position. Our goal was to sample flowers from all species represented in our phylogeny. Specimens came from eight herbaria: Ohio University [BHO], Indiana University [IND], Ohio State University [OS], Harvard University [GH], Missouri Botanical Garden [MO], University of Michigan [MICH], The Smithsonian Museum of Natural History [US], and New York Botanical Garden [NY]. For species that were sampled in the phylogeny but we failed to locate herbarium specimens, information from floras and identification keys supplemented our herbarium specimen data (Täckholm et al., 1956; Allan, 1961; Tutin et al., 1964; Davis, 1967; Migahid, 1978; Blamey and Grey‐Wilson, 1989; Wagner et al., 1990; Miller and Cope, 1996; Dequan et al., 2001; Malyschev and Peschkova, 2003; Stace, 2010; Rabeler and Hartman, 2020).

Phylogenetic comparative methods

A central objective of this study was to determine the identity of all sterile floral structures occupying alternisepalous positions. From the outset, it was evident that the terms staminode and “petaloid” have been used inconsistently and often ambiguously in the Caryophyllaceae literature (Schenk and Appleton, 2021). As a result, it has remained unclear whether petals, petaloids, and staminodes represent the same structure described under different terminologies (i.e., are homologous) or instead constitute distinct and evolutionarily independent floral organs. The results of our developmental analyses provide strong evidence that structures previously described as petals and petaloids are homologous with staminodes (see Results and Discussion). To further evaluate this hypothesis within an explicit evolutionary framework, we employed a phylogenetic comparative approach. If petaloids and staminodes are truly distinct organs, their evolutionary trajectories could be at least partially independent; conversely, if they are homologous, their distributions across taxa will exhibit strong negative correlation. Accordingly, we scored species with structures described as staminodes as possessing staminodes, and species bearing dorsiventrally flattened sterile structures that were petal‐like in appearance as possessing petaloids, and then tested for correlated evolution between these two binary traits using Pagel's (1994) correlated binary analysis in the R package phytools (Revell, 2012). This framework allowed us to assess whether the presence of one structure statistically depends on the presence of the other. Under the hypothesis of homology, we predicted a strong reciprocal pattern, such that species possessing petaloids would lack staminodes, and species with staminodes would lack petaloids. In this way, we were able to combine developmental data with phylogenetic comparative data to assess and refine our understanding of characters and their states (Jeiter and Smets, 2023).

To gain insight into the macroevolutionary processes of floral evolution in Caryophyllaceae and relatives, we applied ancestral character estimations to infer the states of floral characters and their evolutionary transitions through time. We estimated ancestral states with stochastic character mapping (Huelsenbeck et al., 2003), a Bayesian approach that applies explicit models of evolution and accounts for uncertainty in state inferences. We scored species for the presence or absence of staminodes, presence or absence of petaloid staminodes (noting that this analysis explored the evolution of a showy staminodial whorl, not the presence of petals sensu Wei and Ronse De Craene [2019], see below), the number of stamens, and the number of androecial whorls. Stamen numbers were binned categorically as flowers having 1–5 (representing a single whorl), 6–10 (representing two whorls), or >10 (representing three or more whorls) stamens. The number of androecial whorls included the number of stamens and staminodes and was analyzed categorically as 1–5, 6–10, or >10 structures. We did not explore sepal evolution as all species in Caryophyllaceae have four or five sepals (Ronse De Craene, 2013), rendering this trait of little interest for our questions. Variation in carpels is also quite interesting in the group (see Wei and Ronse De Craene, 2020), but not a focus of our current research.

A model of trait evolution was first assessed by combining the floral‐trait data with the MCC chronogram from the BEAST 2 analysis and fitting three transition models to the data. The first was an equal rates (ER) model that assumed transitions among all character states were identical. We then fit a second model onto our data that allowed for all transition rates to be unequal, referred to as the all‐rates‐different (ARD) model. A symmetrical (SYM) model was the last one we considered, which allowed rates to change among states, but forward and reverse transitions among the respective transitions were constrained to be equal. The SYM model was only applied in the multistate traits because the SYM and ARD models are identical for binary states. The three models were fit onto the data sets independently in the R package ape (Paradis et al., 2004), and model fit was assessed with Akaike's information criterion (AIC; Akaike, 1974) scores, where we preferred more parameter‐rich models only if they were lower than two AIC units compared to less‐parameterized models. The best‐fit model from the above analyses was then applied in stochastic character mapping analyses in the R package phytools. We generated 10 × 104 stochastic maps on the MCC chronogram and conducted analyses with sampling every 100 generations following a burn‐in of 300 generations.

To determine the ways in which development evolved in Caryophyllaceae and outgroups, we categorized floral development into unique forms based on the number of structures, developmental order of structures, and the relative positions of structures along the floral axis. We then pruned the chronogram to retain only species for which we had developmental data. In cases in which we had developmental data for a species that was not represented in our phylogeny, we randomly selected another species from the same genus to represent the lineage if there was one present, otherwise, we excluded the species from analyses. We examined floral evolution with two approaches. The first approach was a model fitting approach that fit seven transition models onto the developmental data and phylogeny. The models included an ER, ARD, and SYM model and four custom models, which included a one‐step equal rates (1‐stepER) model, a one‐step equal rates that does not account for development order of whorls (1‐stepNo), a one‐step all rates different (1‐stepARD) model, and a one‐step symmetrical rates (1‐stepSYM) model. The one step models constrained transitions between states to occur in a single, stepwise fashion (see Figure 2). For example, a lineage with no androecial structures occupying alternisepalous positions (e.g., Amaranthus form; Figure 2) could not evolve alternisepalous stamens and have them evolved into staminodes in a single transition (e.g., Amaranthus form to Spergularia form) because it would require two evolutionary events, it was therefore not allowed. The difference between the 1‐stepER model and the 1‐stepNo model was whether the developmental order among the structures was considered. The 1‐stepNo model was agnostic to developmental order, while the other one‐step models considered a change in the developmental order of whorls as an evolutionary event. The seven models were fit onto the data with the R package ape. We estimated AIC scores for each model and preferred more complex models only if their AIC scores were lower by more than two units.

Stochastic character mapping (Huelsenbeck et al., 2003; Bollback, 2006) was applied as a second approach to understand the evolution of floral development in Caryophyllaceae. We used the R package phytools that applied the best‐fit model from the above model‐fitting approach to our pruned developmental chronogram and conducted 20,000 stochastic simulations, sampling as outlined above. The results were then summarized to infer the ancestral character states of the developmental forms for each node.

RESULTS

Phylogenetic reconstructions

Our final data matrix consisted of 775 tips, which included 686 tips from Caryophyllaceae and 89 outgroup species. The combined sequence matrix included 6125 base pairs, which was comprised of 974 sites from the ITS, 3013 sites from matK, and 2136 sites from trnL‐trnF. The RAxML analyses varied in log‐likelihood scores between –179,824.356 (obtained once in the third run with a random starting tree; Appendix S2) and –179,835.339 (also obtained with a random starting tree; figure not shown). The RAxML analysis conducted with an IQ‐TREE starting tree did not yield the highest log‐likelihood value (–179,827.725) and was more than two log‐likelihood units lower than the best‐tree.

The majority of the 11 currently recognized Caryophyllaceae tribes were monophyletic, but we recovered a clade that consisted of Arenaria lancangensis, A. polytrichoides, A. densissima, A. oreophila, and Thylacospermum caespitosum (treated as the tribe Thylacospermeae here, see Discussion) that was sister to a Sclerantheae plus Sagineae clade (Appendix S2). Additional tribal relationships included Caryophylleae as sister to Sileneae plus Eremogoneae, Alsineae as sister to Arenarieae, which was sister to the Sclerantheae/Sagineae/Thylacospermeae clade (Appendix S2). A grade was also recovered that included the Corrigioleae as sister to the remaining members of Caryophyllaceae, followed by Paronychieae, Polycarpaeae, and the Plurcaryophyllaceae A.K. Greenberg & M.J. Donoghue clade (Appendix S2).

The relaxed clock dating analysis recovered Caryophyllaceae as sister to a Amaranthaceae plus Achatocarpaceae clade, which diverged 47.76–50.93 Ma(Figure 3). Caryophyllaceae was monophyletic with strong support (BPP = 1), and its crown age was 36.39–45.64 Ma (stem age: 47.76–50.93 Ma). The 11 previously recognized tribes plus Thylacospermeae were all monophyletic with high posterior probabilities (BPP = 1; Figure 3), and tribal relationships were reconstructed identical to those in the RAxML analysis. The Caryophyllaceae backbone was also strongly supported (Appendix S3), with all BPP values >0.95 except for the split between Paronychieae and all remaining tribes excluding Corrigioleae, which was 0.87. Strong support was generally identified within tribes, with BPP support = >0.95 for most of the clades (Appendix S3).

Phylogenetic comparative methods

We applied a phylogenetic comparative framework to evaluate the structures described in the Caryophyllaceae literature as petals/petaloids/staminodes with the explicit goal of testing whether these terms refer to the same underlying organ. Model comparison using AIC scores strongly favored the correlated‐evolution model over the independent model (P = 0.0004), indicating that the presence of “petals” was significantly associated with the absence of staminodes. Although a subset of species lacked both structures, a clear and consistent pattern emerged across the dataset: species bearing staminodes did not possess “petals,” and species with “petals” reciprocally lacked staminodes. Taken together with the developmental evidence and additional lines of evidence discussed in detail in the Discussion, these results are supportive the conclusion that all sterile structures occupying the outermost alternisepalous positions in Caryophyllaceae are homologous. Accordingly, we coded these structures as a single character in subsequent analyses.

We characterized flowers from herbarium specimens for 613 species that were included in our phylogenetic analyses. Ancestral character estimations were conducted for staminode presence or absence, presence or absence of an elaborated petaloid staminode, number of stamens, and total androecial (stamen plus staminode) number. The ER model fit the data best for the staminode data, although the ΔAIC for the more complex ARD model was 1.39. The ARD model fit the stamen number data best, with an ΔAIC value of 10.51 for the SYM model and 58.70 for the ER model. The SYM model fit the data best for the androecium number, with a ΔAIC value of 4.50 compared to the ARD model and 26.48 compared to the ER model. The ER model fit the data best for the petaloid‐staminode state, with the ΔAIC of the more complex ARD model differing by 0.330.

Staminode evolution was dynamic in Caryophyllaceae and outgroups. Staminodes were present in 482 species. Stochastic character mapping inferred 17.24 changes in the presence or absence of staminodes, which was associated with 3.76 changes from staminodes being absent to present, and 13.48 in the reverse direction. Staminodes were inferred to be present at the crown node of Caryophyllaceae, but not earlier in Caryophyllales (Figure 4; Appendix S4). An independent origin of staminodes was inferred to be derived later in Amaranthaceae. The majority of staminode transitions occurred within Caryophyllaceae, with, especially, numerous losses of staminodes. Staminodes, however, were inferred to be present across the entire Caryophyllaceae backbone (Figure 4).

One hundred and fifty‐two species had five or fewer stamens, while the majority, 455 species, had between six and 10, and six species had more than 10 stamens. All species in Caryophyllaceae in our analyses had 10 or fewer stamens. The stochastic character mapping results were uncertain whether Caryophyllaceae had 1–5 or 6–10 stamens at the root node, the former of which had the highest posterior probability (Figure 4; Appendix S5). The analysis inferred 71.86 transitions, with most transitions (41.11) being from 1–5 to 6–10 stamens, representing a duplication of the androecial whorl.

We evaluated the number of times that staminodes were petaloid; that is, they were visually coopted staminodes that were dorsiventrally flattened and marginally expanded. Our data identified 450 of 613 species had petaloid staminodes. Stochastic character mapping inferred 23.60 changes, with the majority (17.72) being from petaloid to nonpetaloid staminodes and 5.88 transitions in the opposite direction (Figure 4; Appendix S6). Our analysis inferred nearly equal posterior probabilities for each state at the Caryophyllaceae root, but the clade that included the Polycarpaeae and Plurcaryophyllaceae was inferred to contain petaloid staminodes and 10 stamens with the highest posterior probability. A loss of petaloid staminodes in Plurcaryophyllaceae was associated with the entire loss of the staminode whorl, rather than the staminodes evolving into vestigial staminodes, as could have occurred in Paronychieae and some Polycarpaeae.

Eighty‐nine species had five or fewer androecial structures, 131 species had 6–10, and 393 species had greater than 10. Androecium number evolved frequently across the phylogeny, with 97.20 total inferred transitions (Appendix S7). The majority of transitions involved reductions between flowers with greater than 10 to those with 6–10 androecial structures. The states along the Caryophyllales chronogram backbone were uncertain, with the highest posterior probabilities being character states with either 1–5 or 6–10 androecial structures (Appendix S7). The Caryophyllaceae crown node, however, was inferred to have 6–10 androecial structures with the highest posterior probability (Figure 4).

Combining the ancestral character estimate results, a new model of floral evolution in Caryophyllaceae emerged in which floral evolution was driven by the (1) splitting of the androecial primordium at alternisepalous positions and (2) loss of structures, be they staminodes, alternisepalous stamens, or antesepalous stamens (Appendix S8). Between the stem and crown of Caryophyllaceae, we inferred an apetalous ancestor with five antesepalous stamens and five alternisepalous staminodes. We observe that ancestral form (which we call the Spergularia form) persisted in many of the lineages that formed a grade near the root of the family (e.g., Paronychia), followed by the loss of alternisepalous staminodes in other lineages (e.g., Ortegia hispanica), rendering flowers that are apetalous with five antesepalous stamens. In the Plurcaryophyllaceae, we also observed a transition from the ancestral form to a form (which we call the Silene form) in which the alternisepalous staminodial primordia splits to form both a stamen and staminode at that position (Appendix S8). Although this form persists in many lineages, we do observe several transitions from this form, which includes one in which there is a loss of alternisepalous stamens, producing flowers with five antesepalous stamens and five alternisepalous staminodes (a re‐evolved Spergularia form). The other transition from the Silene form involved the loss of alternisepalous staminodes, which represents the re‐evolution of the Scleranthus form (Appendix S8). Those flowers are characterized with five antesepalous stamens and five alternisepalous stamens. From that form we also observe another transition that included the additional loss of antesepalous stamens, producing flowers with only five alternisepalous stamens (e.g., Colobanthus quitensis).

Developmental approach

Floral development data were collected for 59 species from 15 published studies (Kraft, 1916; Hill, 1989; Ronse De Craene and Smets, 1993; Hofmann, 1994; Ronse De Craene et al., 1998; Smissen and Garnock‐Jones, 2002; Harris et al., 2012; Luo et al., 2012; Brockington et al., 2013; Ronse De Craene and Brockington, 2013; Li et al., 2016; Wei and Ronse De Craene, 2019, 2020; Appleton and Schenk, 2021; Bull–Hereñu et al., 2022; Ronse De Craene, 2024). Nine species had developmental data but no phylogenetic data and they were replaced with congenerics (Appendix S1). Floral development and structural data from Caryophyllaceae and outgroups were classified into six forms (Figures 2 and 5). We recognized three forms in the outgroup taxa that were sampled in our phylogeny. The Glinus form was categorized as being apetalous with two alternating whorls of stamens in which the alternisepalous stamens developed first. The Glinus form was represented by Glinus, some Mollugo, and Achaetocarpus. The Mollugo form was similar to the Glinus form, but it lacked stamens in antesepalous positions. The Amaranthus form was apetalous and contained only a single whorl of stamens that developed in antesepalous positions, differing from the Mollugo–form in the position of the stamens only. The Amaranthus form was represented by Amaranthus and Gomphrena.

Three developmental and structural forms were observed in Caryophyllaceae (Figure 2; Appendices S9, S10). The Spergularia form was apetalous and contained stamens and staminodes. The antesepalous stamens were the first to develop after the sepals, followed by the alternisepalous staminodes (Appendix S9). Examples of this developmental form included Paronychia, Pollichia, Scleranthus, Spergularia, and Drymaria. The Scleranthus form was a second form in Caryophyllaceae characterized by being apetalous with two whorls of stamens: the antesepalous whorl developed after the sepals, followed by the alternisepalous whorl. The Scleranthus form was found in some species of Scleranthus. The third floral form in Caryophyllaceae was the Silene form. The Silene form was characterized by two whorls of stamens, with the antesepalous stamens developing before the alternisepalous stamens, or nearly at the same time. The Silene form differed from other forms because staminode and stamen organs at alternisepalous positions were derived from the splitting of the androecial primordia during development.

We inferred 12 single‐step transitions among the developmental forms (see arrows in Figure 2). Among them, three transitions that involved transitions between the Glinus form did not consider developmental order (the Glinus form was unique in having alternisepalous positions developing before antesepalous positions; corresponding to the 1‐StepNo model; depicted as dashed‐arrows in Figure 2). The transition from the Spergularia and Silene forms to the Amaranthus form was inferred to be unidirectional (depicted as gray arrows in Figure 2), as the reverse direction for both transitions would involve two steps (e.g., 1‐StepER model). Our model‐fitting approach determined that the 1‐StepER model fit the data the best compared to the six other models based on AIC scores (Table 1). Stochastic character mapping that applied the 1‐StepER model inferred the root of the phylogeny to be the Spergularia form, with the Glinus, Mollugo, and Amaranthus forms evolving from it in the outgroups and the Silene form evolving from it in Caryophyllaceae (Figure 6). A few reversals were observed in Spergularia, Drypis, and Sagina. From the Silene form, we observed multiple transitions to the Scleranthus form (Figure 6).

DISCUSSION

Sterile, petal‐like structures have independently evolved and been lost multiple times in flowers throughout the evolution of angiosperms (Ronse De Craene, 2007; Endress, 2011). These structures have arisen from a variety of organs—including sepals, stamens, and bracts—and exhibit extensive morphological and functional diversification, including variation in size, shape, margin characteristics, pigmentation, and scent (Endress, 2011; Ronse De Craene and Brockington, 2013). Even within the core Caryophyllales, for example, petaloid sepals or staminodes have independently evolved at least five times (Stegnospermataceae, Aizoaceae, Portulacaceae clade, Caryophyllaceae, and Molluginaceae; Ronse De Craene, 2008). In Caryophyllaceae, petal‐like structures similarly evolved through the sterilization of stamens, mirroring patterns observed in other angiosperm clades (Endress, 2011). Following their origin, these structures underwent significant evolutionary modifications in Caryophyllaceae, likely driven by selection pressures associated with entomophilous pollination (Brockington et al., 2009).

Caryophyllaceae are similar to other families in the core Caryophyllales clade that inherited apetaly, which was hypothesized to be the result of an early shift to wind pollination in Caryophyllales (Ronse De Craene, 2007). Our results suggest that a whorl of alternisepalous stamens were present between the stem and crown lineage of Caryophyllaceae, followed by the subsequent sterilization of the alternisepalous whorl, both of which occurred before the formation of the crown lineage (Figure 4). Although this transition would require two evolutionary events, we inferred that transitions would have 5.9–11.37 My to occur (Figure 3), considerably enough time for multiple evolutionary transitions. In some of the earlier‐diverged Caryophyllaceae clades, such as Corrigioleae and Polycarpaeae, the alternisepalous staminodes were visually coopted and became petaloid staminodes, a condition that is common in staminodial flowers in other families (e.g., Botnaru and Schenk, 2019). Alternatively, petaloid staminodes could have evolved even earlier along the stem Caryophyllaceae lineages and been inherited by the crown clades, but our data were unable to discriminate among the two hypotheses (Figure 4). In other lineages, such as Paronychieae, staminodes are vestigial, although cooption might have occurred in a few species as evidenced by slight elaboration of staminode size compared to stamen filaments (Appleton and Schenk, 2021). In Paronychia, a second, independent origin of staminodes in carpellate flowers of P. chartacea and P. minima occurred, in which the second whorl of staminodes unexpectedly did not coopt the same developmental pathway as staminodes in alternisepalous positions (Schenk and Appleton, 2023).

Our results are the first to explicitly hypothesize that all sterile, alternisepalous organs in Caryophyllaceae flowers are homologous as staminodes that originated a single time, regardless of their elaborations or reductions (Figure 4; Appendix S8). We postulate that staminodes were present along the entire backbone, but were lost multiple times (Appendix S8), a result supported by our phylogenetic comparative analyses (Figure 4). Our study is also the first to hypothesize that the Silene form, which has flowers with two alternating whorls of stamens and a sterile alternisepalous whorl that shares a primordium with fertile alternisepalous stamens, evolved through the addition of an alternisepalous whorl of stamens, rather than the addition of a whorl of staminodes, which split from the primordia that ancestrally formed staminodes. We further hypothesize that the Scleranthus form, which has two whorls of stamens (both antesepalous and alternisepalous) and no staminodes, evolved by the loss of the alternisepalous staminode whorl. The other major transition from the Silene form involved the loss of stamens, resulting in flowers that have only staminodes in alternisepalous positions and stamens in antesepalous positions. Exemplar species include Drypis spinosa, Sagina japonica, and Spergularia marina, all of which have the Spergularia form. For those species, the Spergularia form is secondarily derived following the loss of inner alternisepalous stamens (Figure 6), rather than being homologous with the earlier lineages that have this form (e.g., Corrigioleae). Our hypothesis ultimately proposes a scenario where staminodes evolved at the crown or stem Caryophyllaceae, that an addition of the alternisepalous stamen whorl occurred at the Plurcaryophyllaceae clade through a splitting of the primordia, and that the majority of floral evolution in the family has occurred through the loss of either staminodes, stamens, or both, in alternisepalous or antesepalous positions (Appendix S8).

Evidence of staminodial flowers in Caryophyllaceae

Our results support previous studies that hypothesized an apetalous ancestral Caryophyllaceae flower, a condition also inferred for other Caryophyllales families (Brockington et al., 2013; Ronse De Craene, 2013). Our hypothesis deviates from others in that we infer that an alternisepalous stamen whorl evolved into staminodes somewhere between the stem and crown of the family and that staminodial flowers appear to have been largely retained across the Caryophyllaceae phylogeny, although staminodes were notably lost multiple times (Figure 4; Appendix S8). We acknowledge the novelty of this hypothesis, and below we provide extensive developmental evidence from our own results and previously published studies in support of a staminodial origin for all sterile, alternisepalous structures in Caryophyllaceae.

Perianth and androecial development in Caryophyllaceae follows a conserved sequence: sepals are initiated first, followed by antesepalous stamens, and finally the alternisepalous structures, which develop as stamens or staminodes (which include what others termed petals; Ronse De Craene, 2013; Wei and Ronse De Craene, 2019). The alternisepalous whorl is central to ongoing debates concerning whether the sterile organs are petals or staminodes. Not only does what have been referred to as staminodes or petals share positional homology, but we emphasize that both stamens or staminodes that develop in alternisepalous positions share the same developmental order, that is they form after antesepalous stamens (Figure 2), developmental data that supports their homology. In contrast, close relatives, such as Macarthuria, show a different developmental order, where alternisepalous petal‐like structures arise before the androecial whorls (Wei and Ronse De Craene, 2019). This developmental series of whorls contrasts with the typical Pentapetalae order of sepals, petals, stamens, and carpels. In species with the Silene form, floral morphogenesis slightly diverges, as the sterile structures and stamens both emerge from a common primordium located in the alterniseplaous position (Hofmann, 1994; Ronse De Craene, 2013), suggesting androecial affinity. Importantly, the initiation of the primordium occurs after the antesepalous stamens, consistent with an androecial derivation. Another compelling developmental line of evidence for staminodial identity was proposed by Brockington et al. (2013) and Ronse De Craene (2013), who noted that all sterile structures arise from an androecial ring. Further supporting the staminodial hypothesis, morphogenesis of stamens and staminodes are often morphologically indistinguishable across the family (Ronse De Craene et al., 1998). Later developmental patterns continue to support the staminodial hypothesis. Leins et al. (2001) noted developmental similarities between thecae and the bilobed or appendaged petaloid structures of Caryophyllaceae, concluding that the latter are also androecial in origin. Similar lines of developmental evidence were applied in genera with structures that are uncontroversially staminodes (e.g., Wei and Ronse De Craene, 2019; Appleton and Schenk, 2021; Schenk and Appleton, 2023). Appleton and Schenk (2021), for example, collected developmental data for 14 species of Paronychia that unequivocally demonstrated androecial affinity of the sterile alternisepalous organs. The same conclusion that the structures were staminodes was made in Gymnocarpos by Wei and Ronse De Craene (2019). At the macroevolutionary scale, reconciling the presence of sterile alternisepalous structures being called staminodes in some clades and petals in others is difficult to justify. Indeed, our correlated binary analysis revealed a strong negative correlation between the presence of “petals” (as treated by Wei and Ronse De Craene, 2019) and staminodes across species, suggesting that divergent terminologies are being used for homologous structures. The strongly negative correlation of staminodes and “petals” reflects inconsistent naming conventions rather than underlying developmental or evolutionary differences. Within Caryophyllaceae, the distinction between staminodes and inversely “petals” often hinges on morphological elaboration: staminodes retain stamen‐like traits and are mostly filamentous, while structures called “petaloids” exhibit dorsiventral flattening, marginal expansion, and often pigmentation. We argue, however, that such distinctions are continuous across the family and thus arbitrary. Given the overwhelming evidence of an androecial identity, we conclude that all sterile structures in alternisepalous positions in Caryophyllaceae should be uniformly identified as staminodes. We are not the first to propose this interpretation, at least in part. Ronse De Craene (2007) referred to these organs as “andropetaloids” in some species, explicitly recognizing their androecial affinity. Leins et al. (2001) and Ronse De Craene and Brockington (2013) also classified some species as staminodial, we concur with those authors.

Wei and Ronse De Craene (2019) hypothesized that what we identify here as staminodes in Caryophyllaceae are instead homologous with petals of Pentapetalae and that the Caryophyllales backbone experienced numerous losses of petals across evolutionary history. They hypothesized that flowers at the base of Caryophyllaceae had 10 stamens, a condition common among other Pentapetalae. To explain the common primordia of stamens and what they called petals in the Silene form, they hypothesized that a developmental delay in petal expression occurred and that petal morphogenesis did not occur until after stamen morphogenesis at alternisepalous positions. Ronse De Craene (2024) emphasized this example as a heterochronic shift in petal expression, hypothesizing that a delay in expression of petal genes along the floral axis resulted in the morphogenesis of stamens before petals. The Wei and Ronse De Craene (2019) and Ronse De Craene (2024) alternative hypothesis of floral evolution in Caryophyllaceae postulated a heterochronic shift in petal expression, interpreting sterile alternisepalous structures as true petals instead of staminodes in some, but not all Caryophyllaceae species. Our results are inconsistent with a petal hypothesis. Ancestral character estimates inferred that staminodes evolved at the base of an apetalous Caryophyllaceae, and their verbal model of petal evolution does not account for the many losses of petals throughout Caryophyllales (e.g., Aizoaceae, Amaranthaceae, Cactaceae, Portulacaceae). If the petal hypothesis is true, in groups like Paronychieae and Polycarpaeae, petals would have to be lost multiple times followed by either (1) the evolution of staminodes from alternisepalous stamens if they were ancestrally present or (2) the evolution of stamens in alternisepalous positions followed by their transitions to staminodes if they were not ancestrally present. Our results and others (Brockington et al., 2012) offer a much more parsimonious explanation in which petaloidy evolved throughout Caryophyllales primarily through the cooption of both stamens and sepals.

Evolution of developmental forms

Our survey of developmental forms in Caryophyllaceae identified three basic forms that we named after taxa: Spergularia form, Silene form, and Scleranthus form. All forms had an outermost sepal whorl that developed first and stamens in antesepalous positions that developed second. The alternisepalous position of the androecium, however, is where the majority of floral evolution in Caryophyllaceae flowers occurred. In those positions, flowers either had a stamen, staminode, or a primordium that split to generate both a stamen and staminode. A model‐fitting approach of developmental data found support for a model that allowed for only a single evolutionary transition to occur at a time, performing better than the three models that allowed for evolutionary change in any direction and rate (i.e., ER, SYM, and ARD models; Table 1). Ancestral state estimations of the developmental forms inferred the crown node of Caryophyllaceae to have the Spergularia form with the greatest posterior probability, which was conserved across the backbone from the root to the Plurcaryophyllaceae clade, in which case we observed the evolution of the Silene form. The difference between the Spergularia form and the Silene form is the presence of the second whorl of alternisepalous stamens, supporting the results of the ancestral state estimation of single traits (Figure 4) that the staminodes were homologous across the Caryophyllaceae backbone and alternisepalous stamens evolved through the splitting of the staminodial primordia. The Silene form likely evolved a single time, but was lost multiple times, giving rise to the Scleranthus form through the loss of the staminodes and the Spergularia form through the loss of the alternisepalous stamens.

In the outgroup, we identified three additional developmental forms that overlapped with our sampling. Those forms included the Mollugo‐, Glinus‐, and Amaranthus forms (Figure 2), all of which were apetalous. The Mollugo form had a single whorl of alternisepalous stamens, the Amaranthus form had a single whorl of antesepalous stamens, and the Glinus form had two alternating whorls of stamens. What made the Glinus form unique and different than the Scleranthus form is that the alternisepalous whorl developed before the antesepalous whorl (Brockington et al., 2013). The only result from the developmental ancestral state estimation in Figure 6 that was different than the single‐trait analysis in Figure 4 was the Spergularia form being inferred as the highest posterior probability along the outgroup backbone. Such a result likely reflects poor outgroup sampling of developmental form analysis rather than reality. Iresine diffusa and Alternanthera philoxeroides (Amaranthaceae) were inferred to contain the Spergularia form, which might have driven the result, but no other taxa in the outgroup outside of Amaranthaceae that we investigated had the Spergularia form state. Additional developmental studies across Caryophyllales are needed to test the robustness of the results.

We also identified an additional form that we referred to as the Macarthuria form, but were unable to analyze it because we did not have a representative in our phylogeny. The Macarthuria form contained a petaloid structure and two whorls of stamens. The “petaloids” developed after the sepals, followed by the two whorls of stamens that developed simultaneously (or too close to distinguish based on the data at hand). The Macarthuria form was similar in the developmental order as other Pentapetalae and different than the centrifugal development in Caryophyllaceae flowers. The homology of the Macarthuria petaloid‐structure and staminodes in Caryophyllaceae needs further investigations given the relatively close relationship of the families (Yao et al., 2019).

Taxonomic implications

Our phylogenetic results were largely consistent with the results of other family‐level analyses. Similar to Harbaugh et al. (2010) and Greenberg and Donoghue (2011), we determined there to be a grade of lineages that consisted of (Corrigioleae, (Paronychieae, (Polycarpaeae, and Plurcaryophyllaceae))) (Figure 3; Appendix S3), but differed in tribal relationships within Plurcaryophyllaceae. All three above mentioned studies were consistent in reconstructing a sister relationship between Alsineae and Arenarieae and between Sclerantheae and Sagineae. The three analyses also all recovered a clade that consisted of Sileneae, Eremogoneae, and Caryophylleae, but tribal relationships among and within those clades are inconsistent among studies, with our study inferring a Sileneae and Eremogoneae sister relationship that differed from the other two studies. Further investigation is necessary to robustly resolve backbone relationships within Plurcaryophyllaceae, and target‐capture phylogenomic approaches (e.g., Johnson et al., 2018; Fabre et al., 2025) offer a powerful means of achieving this goal.

Another important phylogenetic result includes the recognition of a 12th tribe: Thylacospermeae. Thylacospermum caespitosum was recovered as sister to the remaining Eremogoneae members in Harbaugh et al. (2010); however, it was associated with the Sperguleae in Greenberg and Donoghue (2011), a result supported by Dillenberger and Kadereit (2014). Both of our phylogenetic analyses recovered a clade that consisted of Arenaria lancangensis, A. polytrichoides, A. densissima, A. oreophila, and Thylacospermum caespitosum, which was placed as sister to the Sclerantheae and Sagineae clade with strong support (BPP = 1). This clade was also recovered by Xu et al. (2019) with additional Arenaria species included, however, they recovered the clade, which they referred to as the Thylacospermum‐Dolophragma clade (after Thylacospermum and Arenaria subgenus Dolophragma), as sister to an Alsineae plus Arenarieae clade with strong support. As pointed out by Xu et al. (2019), species in the clade are perennials that form cushions at high elevations and share sepals with round‐obtuse apices and capsules with three valves that distally extend past the sepal lobes. Thylacospermum was placed in its own tribe, Thylacospermeae, by Pusalkar (2015) without strong justification, but given our results and those of Xu et al. (2019), we advocate for its recognition as the 12th tribe pending additional phylogenetic investigations. We also advocate for the inclusion of Arenaria subgenus Dolophragma in the Thylacospermeae. The relationship of Thylacospermeae to other tribes, however, needs to be addressed with additional data.
