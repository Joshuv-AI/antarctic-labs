Decomposition of degree-regular graphs into quasi-random pairs without the Regularity lemma
Béla Csaba

Abstract
The Szemerédi Regularity Lemma, in combination with the Blow-up Lemma, form the Regularity Method, a fundamental tool in graph embeddings, albeit restricted to very large and dense graphs. We propose an alternative vertex-partitioning framework that remains effective even as the density tends to zero and without requiring astronomically large vertex sets. This approach, while narrower in scope, extends regularity-type techniques to relatively small graphs previously inaccessible to the Regularity Method. As an application, we use this novel vertex-partitioning method for bipartite packing problems.

Introduction

The Regularity Lemma of Endre Szemerédi
[
21
]
together with the Blow-up Lemma
[
12
,
13
]
constitutes the so-called Regularity Method. This is an extremely powerful tool, which has found numerous applications in graph embedding problems. However, due to the Regularity Lemma, it is only useful for very large graphs whose edge density is bounded below by some positive constant.

We shall employ an alternative method that in some cases may serve as a substitute for the Regularity Lemma, and which remains applicable even when the density tends to zero (although slowly). Moreover, it does not require that the number of vertices in the graph is extremely large. This alternative method also provides regular pairs, and can therefore be used in conjunction with the Blow-up Lemma.

A result
[
7
]
of Gowers, improved further by Conlon and Fox
[
2
]
, asserts that the number of clusters in the Regularity Lemma has a tower-type
lower bound. Consequently, in general one cannot partition the vertex set of a graph into disjoint clusters so that
almost all edges are in quasirandom cluster pairs. In our approach only a tiny proportion of the edges of the graph
is kept. These edges are arranged into vertex disjoint bipartite subgraphs.
The union of these subgraphs is a super-matching in which every vertex is a cluster, and every super-edge is a pair of clusters with quasirandom edge distribution between them. Our starting point is an earlier edge decomposition theorem of the author
[
5
]
. We use this result in conjunction with a randomized algorithm in order to obtain the vertex partition
having the above properties.

In our approach the graph to be decomposed cannot be arbitrary: it is crucial that the degrees
are about the same. Fortunately, this is not a strong restriction in many cases, we demonstrate this
via an application for bipartite packing problems in degree-regular or approximately degree-regular

For every vertex

Given a set

we let

the number of neighbors of

in

is sometimes denoted by

If we write

If it is clear from the context, that a graph in question is bipartite, we may only write out

”.

The

The

Given numbers

If

2.1

Regular pairs and bundles

Let
\mathcal{B}
denote the class of
balanced
bipartite graphs, that is, bipartite graphs having equal-sized parts, and for a positive integer
m,
let
{\mathcal{B}}_{m}
denote the class of balanced bipartite graphs having
m
vertices in both parts. If a bipartite graph has unequal vertex parts, we call it
unbalanced
.

Definition 2.1

Let
0<\varepsilon,\delta<1
be real numbers.
We say that a bipartite graph
H=(A,B)
is an
\varepsilon
-regular pair, if for every
X\subseteq A,

Y\subseteq B

with
|X|\geq\varepsilon|A|
and
|Y|\geq\varepsilon|B|
we have

|d_{H}(A,B)-d_{H}(X,Y)|\leq\varepsilon.

We call
H
an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-super-regular pair, if in addition every
v\in A
has at least
\delta|B|
neighbors and every
u\in B
has at least

\delta|A|
neighbors.

We call
H
an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-bundle, if it is an
\varepsilon
-regular pair, and for every vertex
v\in A
we have

deg(v)=\delta|B|\pm\varepsilon|B|,
and
deg(u)=\delta|A|\pm\varepsilon|A|
for every
u\in B.

Note, that an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-bundle is also an
(\varepsilon,\delta-\varepsilon)
-super-regular pair,
and has density
\delta\pm\varepsilon.

The following well known fact below will prove to be useful, the proof is omitted.

Fact 2.2

Assume that
H=(A,B)
is an
\varepsilon
-regular pair with density
d_{H}.
Let
A^{\prime}=\{x\in A:deg(x)<(d_{H}-\varepsilon)|B|\}
and

A^{\prime\prime}=\{x\in A:deg(x)>(d_{H}+\varepsilon)|B|\}.
Similarly, let
B^{\prime}=\{x\in B:deg(x)<(d_{H}-\varepsilon)|A|\}
and

B^{\prime\prime}=\{x\in B:deg(x)>(d_{H}+\varepsilon)|A|\}.
Then
|A^{\prime}|,|A^{\prime\prime}|\leq\varepsilon|A|
and
|B^{\prime}|,|B^{\prime\prime}|\leq\varepsilon|B|.

We will use the so called Slicing lemma
[
15
]
at various points in the paper.

Fact 2.3

Assume that
H=(A,B)
is an
\varepsilon
-regular pair with density
d_{H},
and for some
\alpha>\varepsilon
let
A^{\prime}\subset A,

|A^{\prime}|\geq\alpha|A|
and
B^{\prime}\subset B,

|B^{\prime}|\geq\alpha|B|.
Then
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
is an
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-regular pair with

\varepsilon^{\prime}=\max\{\varepsilon/\alpha,2\varepsilon\}
and for its density
d^{\prime}
we have
|d^{\prime}-d|<\varepsilon.

We are going to need the lemma below.

Lemma 2.4

Let
0<\varepsilon<1/10
and
3\varepsilon\leq d\leq 1/2,
and assume that
F\in\mathcal{B}_{m}
is an
\varepsilon
-regular pair with density
d.

Then there is a
(3\varepsilon,d)
-bundle
H\subset F,

H\in\mathcal{B}_{m_{1}}
with density
d\pm\varepsilon

such that
m_{1}\geq(1-2\varepsilon)m.
Moreover,
e(H)\geq e(F)-2\varepsilon m^{2}-2\varepsilon(d-\varepsilon)m^{2}\geq e(F)-4\varepsilon m^{2}.

Proof:

Discard the vertices that have less than
(d-\varepsilon)m
neighbors, and then those, which have more than
(d+\varepsilon)m
neighbors. By Fact
2.2
we discarded at most

2\varepsilon m
vertices from both parts of
F.
Then, if necessary, we discard some further vertices from the larger part to make the subgraph balanced. This way we have obtained
H,
which has
m_{1}\geq(1-2\varepsilon)m
vertices in both parts.
Clearly, if we take arbitrary subsets of sizes at least
2\varepsilon m_{1}\geq\varepsilon m
from both parts, the density of the subgraph spanned by
these subsets is
d\pm\varepsilon
by
\varepsilon
-regularity of
F.
Hence, the density of
H
is
d\pm\varepsilon.

Since every remaining vertex that belongs to
H
lost at most
2\varepsilon m
neighbors, this shows that

(d-3\varepsilon)m_{1}\leq(d-3\varepsilon)m\leq deg(v)\leq(d+\varepsilon)m\leq(d+3\varepsilon)m_{1}

for every vertex
v\in V(H),
here the last inequality follows from the upper bounds for
\varepsilon
and
d.
Finally, we get the claimed lower bound for
e(H)
since from
e(F)
we subtracted
at most
(d-\varepsilon)m
edges for every discarded vertex that had low degree, and at most
m
edges for every high degree discarded vertex.

\Box

Corollary 2.5

Let
0<\varepsilon<1/10
and
2\sqrt{\varepsilon}\leq d\leq 1/2.
If
F\in\mathcal{B}_{m}
is an
\varepsilon
-regular pair with density

d,
then it contains a
(3\varepsilon,d)
-bundle
H\subset F,

H\in\mathcal{B}_{m_{1}}
with density
d\pm\varepsilon

such that
m_{1}\geq(1-2\varepsilon)m,
and

Proof:
This follows easily from the condition that
4\varepsilon\leq d^{2}.

\Box

2.3

Criterion for quasi-randomness

Working with random subgraphs of a regular pair spanned by random subsets is crucial
in the paper.
For proving quasi-randomness of a random subgraph we obtain this way, we use the following result by Kohayakawa and Rödl from
[
11
]
:

Theorem 2.7

Let
\eta
be a constant with
0<\eta<1.
Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with

(A,B)
a pair of disjoint, nonempty subsets of
V
with
|A|\geq 2/\eta.
Set

\rho=d(A,B)=e(A,B)/(|A|\cdot|B|).
Let
\mathcal{D}
be the collection of all pairs
\{x,x^{\prime}\}
of vertices of
A
for which

(i)

deg(x,B),deg(x^{\prime},B)\geq(\rho-\eta)|B|,

(ii)

deg(x,N(x^{\prime})\cap B)\leq(\rho+\eta)^{2}|B|.

Then if
|\mathcal{D}|>(1-5\eta)|A|^{2}/2,
the pair
G[A,B]
is
(16\eta)^{1/5}
-regular.

The vertex decomposition theorem

The edge decomposition theorem below by the author
[
5
]
is based upon a result by Peng, Rödl and Ruciński
[
20
]
.

Theorem 3.1

Let
G=(V,E)\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
with density
d_{G},
and let
0<d<1
and
0<\varepsilon<1/2
such that
n>\exp(50\log(1/d)/\varepsilon^{2}).

Then there exist natural numbers
m=m(\varepsilon,d)
and
K=K(\varepsilon,d)
such that the following holds:

E(G)
can be written as the edge-disjoint union of bipartite graphs
H_{1},\ldots,H_{K}\in\mathcal{B},
and another, exceptional graph
H_{0},
such
that for every
i\geq 1,

H_{i}
is an
\varepsilon
-regular pair with
m_{i}\geq m
vertices in both parts and density
d_{i}\geq(1-\varepsilon/3)d,

while
H_{0}
has density less than
d.
Furthermore,

m\geq\frac{1}{2}d^{50/\varepsilon^{2}}n

and

K\leq 8\frac{d_{G}}{d}\cdot d^{-100/\varepsilon^{2}}.

Observe, that if
d>d_{G},
then the theorem holds with
K=0.
The theorem becomes useful when

d_{G}\geq d.
It is also clear, that one needs
m\gg 1/\varepsilon,
which in turn implies
\varepsilon=\Omega(\sqrt{\log(1/d)/\log n}).

In Theorem
3.1
the edge set is partitioned into
K=K(\varepsilon,d)
regular pairs, where
K(\varepsilon,d)
is a single exponential function
of
\varepsilon
and
d.
Since the vertex parts of different regular pairs may intersect, this reduces the applicability of the above theorem.
Still, one can use it under relatively general circumstances for certain kind of vertex decompositions.
Our goal is to show that for graphs in which the vertex degrees
are linear (or slightly sublinear) in
n
and the graph is almost degree-regular, Theorem
3.1
can be applied for finding a
vertex partition
, which, although
one has to discard the majority of edges, proves to be useful for embedding problems.

The main decomposition theorem of the paper is as follows.

Theorem 3.2

Let
0<\varepsilon<1/10
be a number, and assume that
d
is a real number such that
10\varepsilon^{1/5}\leq d<1/3.

Assume further that
G=(V,E)\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
with bipartition
V=A\cup B
and
r(1-\lambda)\leq deg(v)\leq r

for every
v\in V,
where
r\geq d^{1/3}n
and
0\leq\lambda\leq d^{4}/2.

If
n>\exp(150\log(1/d)/\varepsilon^{2}),
then there exists a natural number
K\leq 8\frac{d_{G}}{d}\cdot d^{-100/\varepsilon^{2}}
such that
G
admits the following decomposition:

(i)

A=A_{1}\cup\dots\cup A_{K}\cup A_{0}
and
B=B_{1}\cup\dots\cup B_{K}\cup B_{0},

where
A_{i}\cap A_{j}=B_{i}\cap B_{j}=\emptyset,
whenever
i\neq j,

(ii)

|A_{0}\cup B_{0}|<8\sqrt[3]{d}n,

(iii)

|A_{i}|,|B_{i}|\geq d^{101/\varepsilon^{2}}\cdot\frac{n}{4}=\Omega\left(n^{1/3}(\log n)^{-1/2}\right)
for every

1\leq i\leq K,

(iv)

||A_{i}|-|B_{i}||\leq 2\varepsilon^{2}|A_{i}|
for every
1\leq i\leq K,

(v)

the bipartite subgraphs
G[A_{i},B_{i}]\subset G
for
i\geq 1
are all
(\varepsilon^{\prime},d_{i})
-bundles, where

\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq(64\varepsilon)^{1/5}
and
d_{i}\geq d-2\varepsilon.

Before turning to the proof of the theorem, let us discuss some details and features of it.
The sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{K}
and
B_{1},\ldots,B_{K}
are called the
non-exceptional clusters
of the decomposition,
and the sets
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
are the
exceptional clusters,
analogously to the decomposition of the Regularity lemma.
Note, that the exceptional clusters, while can be made small by choosing
d
to be small, could be much larger than the
non-exceptional clusters. Analogously to the Regularity lemma, sometimes we will call the union of the

G[A_{i},B_{i}]

(i\geq 1)
bundles the
reduced graph
of
G.
The vertices of this reduced graph are the clusters, the edges are the

G[A_{i},B_{i}]
-bundles, these form a matching in the reduced graph.

We will use Theorem
3.1
as a black box when proving Theorem
3.2
. Note, that the bound for
n
as a function of
\varepsilon
and
d
is not the same in the two theorems,
in the second one the constant multiplier in the exponent is three times larger. We need it for conditions
(iii)
and
(iv).

Having said this, we also remark that the we did not optimize on the constants in the theorem.

Theorem
3.1
has an algorithmic version, Theorem 5.1 in
[
5
]
. It allows one to formulate an algorithmic version of Theorem
3.2
, which can be proved similarly to Theorem
3.1
.
However,
\varepsilon
is raised to a larger power in it, and here we do not focus on algorithmic applications, so we decided to work with Theorem
3.1
.

One can express a lower bound for
\varepsilon
and
d
as functions of
n.
Easy calculation, which we leave for the reader, shows the following fact.

Fact 3.3

We have

\varepsilon\geq\left(\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\right)^{1/2}\quad\quad{\rm and\ }\quad\quad d\geq 10\left(\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\right)^{1/10}.

Note that while the Regularity lemma gives a decomposition of the vertex set of a graph into clusters such that the bipartite subgraphs between
almost all
pairs of clusters are quasirandom, and, crucially, we still have
almost all
edges,
the above theorem gives a much weaker decomposition. In particular, the
G[A_{i},B_{i}]
bundles may have only a very small fraction of the edges of
G
itself.
Due to this fact, one would need extra work for providing connections between clusters that do not
belong to the same “super-edge” when using Theorem
3.2
for embedding a large
connected subgraph (see in
[
4
]
), but this problem does not emerge in
the applications we consider in the second part of the paper.

3.1

Proof of Theorem
3.2

The proof consists of a randomized algorithm and its analysis. We begin with applying Theorem
3.1
for
G
with parameters
\varepsilon
and
d\ (\geq 10\varepsilon^{1/5}),
assuming that

n>\exp(150\log(1/d)/\varepsilon^{2}).
We denote by
H_{1},\dots,H_{K}
the
\varepsilon
-regular pairs
in the decomposition we obtain this way, and
H_{0}
denotes the
exceptional
bipartite subgraph
having density less than
d.
Since
m\geq\frac{1}{2}d^{50/\varepsilon^{2}}n,
using the lower
bound for
n
we have that
m\geq n^{2/3}/2.

Next we transform every regular pair into a bundle: we apply Lemma
2.4
for every
H_{i},

i\geq 1,
and for simplicity we keep the notation for the
H_{i}
bundles. By Corollary
2.5
we do not increase the number of edges of
H_{0}
by more than

d\cdot e(G)\leq dn^{2}
edges, so the new
H_{0}
has density at most
2d.

After this we assign a probability
p(H_{i})
to every non-exceptional bundle
H_{i}

(i\geq 1)
:

p(H_{i})=\frac{(d(H_{i})-\varepsilon)m_{i}}{r}.

Observe, that if
v\in V(H_{i}),
then
deg(v,H_{i})/m_{i}\in d(H_{i})\pm\varepsilon,
hence, if we randomly choose an edge incident to
v,
then the probability that this edge belongs
to
H_{i},
is about
p(H_{i})

For every
v\in V(G)
we define a set: let
S_{v}=\{i:i\geq 1,v\in V(H_{i})\}.
Next we estimate the sum of the
p(H_{i})

probabilities for
i\in S_{v}.

Lemma 3.4

For every
v\in V(G)
we have

1-\frac{deg(v,H_{0})}{r}-d^{4}\leq\sum_{i:i\in S_{v}}p(H_{i})\leq 1-\frac{deg(v,H_{0})}{r}.

Proof:
We begin with the upper bound:

\sum_{i:i\in S_{v}}p(H_{i})=\sum_{i:i\in S_{v}}\frac{(d(H_{i})-\varepsilon)m_{i}}{r}\leq\sum_{i:i\in S_{v}}\frac{deg(v,H_{i})}{r}\leq\frac{r-deg(v,H_{0})}{r}=1-\frac{deg(v,H_{0})}{r}.

Next we prove the lower bound. Recall, that
0\leq\lambda\leq d^{4}/2,
and every vertex has degree in the
interval
[r(1-\lambda),r].

Using that
(d(H_{i})+\varepsilon)m_{i}
is an upper bound for the degrees of vertices in the bundle
H_{i}

and
deg(v)\geq r-d^{4}r/2,
we have

\sum_{i:i\in S_{v}}(d(H_{i})+\varepsilon)m_{i}\geq r-\frac{d^{4}r}{2}-deg(v,H_{0}),

which implies that

\sum_{i:i\in S_{v}}(p(H_{i})+\frac{2\varepsilon m_{i}}{r})\geq 1-\frac{d^{4}}{2}-\frac{deg(v,H_{0})}{r}.

Since
\varepsilon\leq d^{5}/10,
we have

\sum_{i:i\in S_{v}}2\varepsilon m_{i}\leq\sum_{i:i\in S_{v}}d^{5}m_{i}/5\leq d^{4}\sum_{i:i\in S_{v}}d(H_{i})m_{i}/5\leq\frac{d^{4}r}{4}.

This implies that

1-\frac{deg(v,H_{0})}{r}-d^{4}\leq\sum_{i:i\in S_{v}}p(H_{i})\leq 1-\frac{deg(v,H_{0})}{r},

proving what is desired.
\Box

Next for every
v\in V(G)
we define

p_{0}(v)=1-\sum_{i:i\in S_{v}}p(H_{i}).

The above lemma easily implies the following.

Corollary 3.5

For every
v\in V(G)
we have
that

\frac{deg(v,H_{0})}{r}\leq p_{0}(v)\leq\frac{deg(v,H_{0})}{r}+d^{4}.

Given a number
\psi\in(0,1)
, let
L=\{v\in V(G):deg(v,H_{0})\geq\psi n\}.

Fact 3.6

We have

|L|\leq\frac{2e(H_{0})}{\psi n}\leq\frac{4dn^{2}}{\psi n}=\frac{4dn}{\psi}.

In order to obtain the partitions of
A
and
B
as stated in the theorem, we use a randomized algorithm. Given any vertex
v\in V(G),
we “roll
a dice” which has
|S_{v}|+1
faces, the numbers written on the faces are the elements of
S_{v}
and
0.

The probability of the outcomes are as follows:

P({\rm the\ outcome\ is\ }i\in S_{v})=p(H_{i}),

and

P({\rm the\ outcome\ is\ }0)=p_{0}(v).

Note, that the number of faces and the probabilities of the outcomes may differ for different vertices.

Next for every vertex we roll its dice, independently from other vertices. Say, that vertex
v\in A.
If the outcome
is some
j\in S_{v},
then we put
v
into
A_{j}.
Otherwise, if the outcome is 0,
v
will belong to
A_{0}.
Analogous procedure is applied to the vertices in
B.

After finishing this random distribution procedure for every vertex, we obtain the subgraphs

G[A_{i},B_{i}]
for every
1\leq i\leq K.
Observe, that every vertex in
V(H_{i})
had the same
probability
p(H_{i})
for belonging to
A_{i}\cup B_{i},
that is,
G[A_{i},B_{i}]
is a random induced subgraph of
H_{i}.

Moreover,
p(H_{i})>dm/(2r)\gg n^{-1/3}(\log n)^{-1/2}
for
i=1,\dots,K,
using Fact
3.3
, since
m_{i}\geq n^{2/3}/2
and
r\leq n.
We
need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.7

Assume that
F[X,Y]
is an
(\varepsilon,d)
-bundle with
|X|=|Y|=k,
where
k
is a sufficiently large integer, and
\varepsilon,d
are two real numbers such that
0<d<1/3
and

1/\sqrt{\log k}<\varepsilon\leq d/10.

Let
p
be a real number
with
(30\log^{3}k)/k\leq p\leq 1.

Assume that
X_{R}
is a random subset of
X,
such that every
x\in X
belongs to
X_{R}

with probability
p,
independently from other vertices. We obtain
Y_{R}\subseteq Y
analogously.
Then with probability at least
1-1/k^{3},
the induced subgraph
F[X_{R},Y_{R}]\subset F[X,Y]
is a

((64\varepsilon)^{1/5},d-2\varepsilon)
-bundle with density
\rho\in[d-2\varepsilon,d+2\varepsilon],

moreover,
(1-\varepsilon^{2})pk\leq|X_{R}|,|Y_{R}|\leq(1+\varepsilon^{2})pk.

Proof:
Throughout the proof of the lemma we say that an event holds with high probability,
if it holds with probability at least
1-1/k^{5}.

For estimating the cardinalities of
X_{R}
and
Y_{R}
we use Theorem
2.6
(the Chernoff bound) with parameter

\mu=\varepsilon^{2}\geq 1/\log k.

Note, that
\mathbb{E}|X_{R}|=\mathbb{E}|Y_{R}|=pk\geq 30\log^{3}k.
Then we have

P(||X_{R}|-pk|\geq\mu pk)\leq 2e^{-\mu^{2}pk/3}\leq 2e^{-10\log k}<k^{-5}.

Similar bound holds for
|Y_{R}|.

In order to prove that
F[X_{R},Y_{R}]
is
(64\varepsilon)^{1/5}
-regular, we will check the conditions of Theorem
2.7
.
We begin with determining high probability upper and lower bounds for the
density
\rho
of
F[X_{R},Y_{R}].
Clearly,

\frac{\min\{deg(x,Y_{R}):x\in X_{R}\}}{|Y_{R}|}\leq\rho=\frac{\sum_{x\in X_{R}}deg(x,Y_{R})}{|X_{R}|\cdot|Y_{R}|}\leq\frac{\max\{deg(x,Y_{R}):x\in X_{R}\}}{|Y_{R}|}.

Since
F[X,Y]
is an
(\varepsilon,d)
-bundle, every vertex in
X
has degree in the interval
[(d-\varepsilon)|Y|,(d+\varepsilon)|Y|].

Applying the Chernoff bound we have

(1-\varepsilon)(d-\varepsilon)p|Y|\leq deg(x,Y_{R})\leq(1+\varepsilon)(d+\varepsilon)p|Y|

and

(1-\varepsilon)(d-\varepsilon)p|X|\leq deg(y,X_{R})\leq(1+\varepsilon)(d+\varepsilon)p|X|

for every
x\in X
and
y\in Y
with
high probability. Hence, we have

\frac{\min\{deg(x,Y_{R}):x\in X_{R}\}}{|Y_{R}|}\geq\frac{(d-\varepsilon)(1-\varepsilon)p|Y|}{(1+\varepsilon)p|Y|}=(d-\varepsilon)\left(1-\frac{2\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\right)\geq d-2\varepsilon,

and

\frac{\max\{deg(x,Y_{R}):x\in X_{R}\}}{|Y_{R}|}\leq\frac{(d+\varepsilon)(1+\varepsilon)p|Y|}{(1-\varepsilon)p|Y|}=(d+\varepsilon)\left(1+\frac{2\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\right)\leq d+2\varepsilon.

That is,
(d-2\varepsilon)|Y_{R}|\leq deg(x,Y_{R})\leq(d+2\varepsilon)|Y_{R}|
for every
x\in X,
which implies that
d-2\varepsilon\leq\rho\leq d+2\varepsilon.
Analogously, we have that
(d-2\varepsilon)|X_{R}|\leq deg(y,X_{R})\leq(d+2\varepsilon)|X_{R}|
for every
y\in Y

with high probability.

Using the above lower and upper bounds for
\rho
we can verify condition
(i)
of Theorem
2.7
: every
x\in X_{R}
has degree at least
(d-2\varepsilon)|Y_{R}|\geq(\rho-4\varepsilon)|Y_{R}|
with high probability.

For condition
(ii)
of Theorem
2.7
we need upper bounds for the co-degrees of the vast majority of vertex pairs in
X_{R}.

We define a subset of
X
for every
x\in X
: let
\widehat{X}(x)=\{x^{\prime}\in X:deg(x,N(x^{\prime}))>(d+\varepsilon)^{2}|Y|\}.

Since
F[X,Y]
is an
(\varepsilon,d)
-bundle,
|\widehat{X}(x)|\leq\varepsilon|X|
for every
x\in X.
By the Chernoff bound,

|X_{R}\cap\widehat{X}(x)|\leq 2\varepsilon|X_{R}|
for every
x\in X
with high probability.

Assume now, that
x,x^{\prime}\in X_{R}
such that
x^{\prime}\in X_{R}-\widehat{X}(x).
Then the expected number of neighbors
of
x
in
N(x^{\prime})\cap Y_{R}
is at most
(d+\varepsilon)^{2}p|Y|.

The high probability lower bound
|Y_{R}|\geq(1-\varepsilon^{2})p|Y|
implies that
\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon^{2}}|Y_{R}|\geq(1+\varepsilon)p|Y|.

Since
\varepsilon\leq d/10
and
d<1/3,
we get that
(d+\varepsilon)^{2}/(1-\varepsilon^{2})\leq(d+2\varepsilon)^{2}.
Using the Chernoff bound we
have

deg(x,N(x^{\prime})\cap Y_{R})\leq(d+\varepsilon)^{2}(1+\varepsilon^{2})p|Y|\leq(d+2\varepsilon)^{2}|Y_{R}|\leq(\rho+4\varepsilon)^{2}|Y_{R}|

with high probability.

Throughout the proof we have to control the probabilities of less than
k^{2}
events, each having probability at least
1-1/k^{5}
:

•

the cardinalities of
X_{R}
and
Y_{R}
are close to their expectation,

•

every vertex in
X\cup Y
has about the expected number of neighbors in
Y_{R}
and
X_{R},
respectively,

•

the number of common neighbors of the
\{x,x^{\prime}\}
pairs , where
x^{\prime}\in X_{R}-\widehat{X}(x),
are also close
to their expected values,

•

for every
x\in X
the cardinality of
X_{R}\cap\widehat{X}(x)
is close to its expectation.

Hence, the intersection of these events has probability at least
1-k^{2}/k^{5}=1-1/k^{3}.

Therefore, Theorem
2.7
implies that
F[X_{R},Y_{R}]
is a
(64\varepsilon)^{1/5}
-regular pair. Recall, that

(d-2\varepsilon)|Y_{R}|\leq deg(x,Y_{R})\leq(d+2\varepsilon)|Y_{R}|
for every
x\in X
and
(d-2\varepsilon)|X_{R}|\leq deg(y,X_{R})\leq(d+2\varepsilon)|X_{R}|
for every
y\in Y.
Since
(64\varepsilon)^{1/5}>4\varepsilon,

F[X_{R},Y_{R}]
is a
((64\varepsilon)^{1/5},d-2\varepsilon)
-bundle.

\Box

We apply Lemma
3.7
for every bundle
H_{1},\dots,H_{K}.
Recall, that the size of the parts of
any
H_{i}
are at least
n^{2/3}/2,
this number plays the role of
k
in Lemma
3.7
. Hence, the probability
that a random subgraph of some
H_{i}
bundle fails to be
((64\varepsilon)^{1/5},d-2\varepsilon)
-bundle, is at most
2/n^{2}.
There
are
K<n
bundles, therefore, with probability at least
1-2/n
the random subgraphs
G[A_{1},B_{1}],\dots,G[A_{K},B_{K}]
are all
((64\varepsilon)^{1/5},d-2\varepsilon)
-bundles. The bounds
|A_{i}|,|B_{i}|=\Omega(n^{1/3}/\sqrt{\log n})
are also implied by Lemma
3.7
,
since
m\geq n^{2/3}/2
and
p\geq n^{-1/3}(\log n)^{-1/2}.

Finally, we estimate the cardinality of the set
A_{0}\cup B_{0}
from above. Recall the definition of
the set
L
: for a given parameter
\psi\in(0,1)
this set contains those vertices that have at least
\psi n
edges incident to them from
H_{0}.
Assume first, that
v\in V(G)-L.
Then
p_{0}(v)<d^{4}+\psi n/r.
Even in the worst
case, when all vertices of
L
belong to
A_{0}\cup B_{0},
we have

\mathbb{E}|A_{0}\cup B_{0}|<(d^{4}+\psi n/r)n+|L|\leq d^{4}n+\frac{\psi n^{2}}{r}+\frac{4dn}{\psi},

here we used Fact
3.6
.
By Theorem
2.6
, we have that with high probability

|A_{0}\cup B_{0}|\leq 2d^{4}n+\frac{2\psi n^{2}}{r}+\frac{5dn}{\psi}.

Set
\psi=d^{2/3}.
Using that
r\geq d^{1/3}n,
we get that the following upper bound holds
with high probability:

|A_{0}\cup B_{0}|\leq 2d^{4}n+2d^{1/3}n+5d^{1/3}n<8d^{1/3}n.

With this we have finished the proof of Theorem
3.2
.
\Box

4

Packing of bipartite graphs

Given two graphs,
G
and
H,
an
H
-packing
in
G
is a collection of vertex disjoint copies of
H

in
G.

Clearly, the more vertex disjoint copies of
H
we want to find in
G,
the harder the task.
If
G
admits an
H
-packing which covers every vertex, we call it a
perfect
H
-packing
or
an
H
-factor.

A special case of
H
-packings is the well-understood case of matchings, that is, when
H
is a single edge. When
H
is
a clique on at least three vertices, the problem becomes considerably harder. If
G
is an
n
-vertex graph,
r\geq 2
is an
integer,
r
divides
n,
and
\delta(G)\geq(1-1/r)n,
then
G
has a perfect
K_{r}
-packing (the case
r=3

is proved by Hajnal and Corrádi
[
3
]
, the case of
r\geq 3
is the Hajnal-Szemerédi theorem
[
9
]
).

Alon and Yuster
[
1
]
, using the Regularity lemma, generalized the Hajnal-Szemerédi theorem for almost
perfect packings by a fixed
H,
leaving out
o(n)
vertices. Their theorem was improved by Komlós, Sárközy and Szemerédi: they proved that for every
H
there exists a constant
C_{H}
such that if
G
is an
n
-vertex graph,
n
is divisible
by
|V(H)|
and the minimum degree of
G
is at least
(1-1/\chi(H))n+C_{H},
then
G
has a perfect
H
-packing.
For the case when
\chi(H)\geq 3,

Kühn and Osthus
[
17
]
refined the above result, and proved tight
bounds, depending on the chromatic number and the critical chromatic number of
H.

It turns out that the case
\chi(H)=2
is different, it received special attention.
In
[
16
]
Kühn and Osthus proved the theorem below
on packing with arbitrary bipartite graphs.

For stating their theorem we need to introduce a notation: given numbers
a\geq b,
we say that a
graph
G
is

(a\pm b)
-regular if its minimum degree is at least
a-b
and its maximum degree is at most
a+b.

The theorem below is Theorem 1.1. in
[
16
]
.

Theorem 4.1

Given a bipartite graph
H
and constants
0<c,\alpha\leq 1,
there exist positive
numbers
\gamma=\gamma(c,\alpha)
and
n_{0}=n_{0}(H,c,\alpha)
such that every
(cn\pm\gamma n)
-regular graph
G
of order
n\geq n_{0}
has an
H
-packing which covers all but at most
\alpha n
vertices of
G.

They also conjectured that if
G
is
cn
-regular (so there is no “slackness” in the degrees), then there is an
H
-packing leaving at most a constant
number of vertices in
G
uncovered. This question was open for nearly twenty years, until recently, when Letzter, Methuku and Sudakov
[
18
]
proved the conjecture.

Theorem 4.2

For every bipartite graph
H
and every
0<c\leq 1,
there are constants
C=C(H,c)
and
n_{0}=n_{0}(H,c)
such that every

r
-regular graph
G
of order
n\geq n_{0},
where
r\geq cn,
has an
H
-packing that covers all but at
most
C
vertices of
G.

The proofs of Theorem
4.1
and Theorem
4.2
use the Regularity lemma. Hence, the threshold numbers
n_{0}
and the constant
C
are of tower type. Moreover, these
proofs work only if
H
has bounded degree. The proof of Theorem
4.2
is quite involved, and
in addition to the Regularity lemma it also uses other recent powerful techniques on expanders.

It turns out, that if
H
is unbalanced,
then one can find such an
H
-packing relatively easily, which leaves out only a constant number of vertices,
even if the degrees in
G
are just approximately the same.
The following result was proved by Kühn and Osthus
[
16
]
.

Theorem 4.3

Given a bipartite graph
H
whose vertex classes have different size and a
constant
0<c\leq 1,
there exist
\gamma=\gamma(H,C)>0
and
C=C(H,c)
such that every

(cn\pm\gamma n)
-regular graph
G
has an
H
-packing which covers all but at most
C
vertices of
G.

Since the above theorem, similarly to the previous ones, was proved using the Regularity lemma, the constant
C
has a tower-type dependence on
H
and
c.

Our first result on bipartite
H
-packing is the following.

Theorem 4.4

Let
\varepsilon,d
be real numbers such that
0<\varepsilon<1/10
and
20\varepsilon^{1/5}\leq d<1/3.

Assume that
G=(V,E)
is an
(1\pm\frac{d^{4}}{10})\rho
-regular graph
on
n
vertices with
\rho\geq 3d^{1/3}n.
Assume further that
H
is a bipartite graph on
h
vertices with
maximum degree
D\geq 1.

If

•

n>2\exp(150\log(1/d)/\varepsilon^{2})
and

•

h\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{1/5}}{16D}\exp(-(D+101/\varepsilon^{2})\log(1/d))3^{-D}n,

then
G
has an
H
-packing, which covers all but at most
5d^{1/3}n
vertices of
G.

We emphasize, that in Theorem
4.4
the graph
H
may have unbounded size and degree, and
G
is allowed to have vanishing density. By Fact
3.3
, one may choose
\rho=o(n)
if
\rho\geq 3d^{1/3}n

and
d=\Omega((\log\log n/\log n)^{1/30}).
Besides,
n
can be single-exponential in a polynomial of

1/\varepsilon,

unlike in the Regularity lemma. Hence, Theorem
4.4
extends Theorem
4.1
in several ways,
and even Theorem
4.2
in that it works for graphs of “practical size”, and the size of
H

may grow with
n.

We also consider the bipartite packing problem of unbalanced bipartite graphs, and the closely related
packing by subdivison problem. Given a graph
H,
we obtain a
subdivision
of it by replacing edges
of
H
with internally vertex-disjoint paths. The resulting graph is denoted by
TH,
as
H
is a topological
minor of it.

We focus on the special case of the 1-subdivision, when
every edge
of
H
is replaced by internally
vertex-disjoint paths of length 2. It is easy to see, that the 1-subdivision of any graph is bipartite, since 1-subdivisions cannot contain odd cycles.

In 2002 Versträete
[
22
]
conjectured that for every graph
H
and
\eta>0
there exists a threshold number

r_{0}
such that if
G
is an
n
-vertex,
r
-regular graph with
r\geq r_{0},
then it contains a
TH
-packing
that leaves at most
\eta n
vertices uncovered in
G.

Recently this conjecture was proved by Montgomery, Petrova, Ranganathan and Tan
[
19
]
. Note, that
r_{0}
does not depend on
n,
so it is not surprising that the
\eta n
“error” is unavoidable.
Theorem
4.3
for packing with unbalanced bipartite graphs, as observed by Kühn and Osthus, implies a stronger bound for packing by subdivisions, only leaving out a (very large) constant number of vertices. However, this works only for dense graphs, and the mentioned constant has a tower type bound.

Our theorem for packing with an unbalanced bipartite graph is as follows.

Theorem 4.5

Let
H
be a bipartite graph with vertex parts
X
and
Y
such that
|X|>|Y|.
Set
h=|X|+|Y|.

Assume that
\varepsilon,\rho
and
d
are constants and
n
is
number such that following are satisfied:

•

0<\varepsilon<1/10,

•

10\varepsilon^{1/5}\leq d<1/100,

•

\rho\geq 10d^{1/9}n.

•

n>\exp(150\log(1/d)/\varepsilon^{2}).

Assume further that
G=(V,E)
is an
(1\pm\frac{d^{4}}{10})\rho
-regular graph
on
n
vertices. Then there exists a number

C=C(H,\varepsilon,d)\leq\frac{8h}{d}\cdot d^{-100/\varepsilon^{2}}
such that
G
has an
H
-packing, leaving out at most
C
vertices.

Following the observation by Kühn and Osthus
[
16
]
, Theorem
4.5
implies the corollary below.

Corollary 4.6

Given a graph
H
without isolated vertices which is not a union of cycles and
a constant
0<c\leq 1,
there exist
\gamma=\gamma(H,c)>0
and
C=C(H,c)
such that every

(cn\pm\gamma n)
-regular graph
G
has a packing with 1-subdivisions of
H
which covers all but at most
C
vertices of
G.

Proof:
The statement follows from the fact that if
H
is not a union of cycles,
then it has more edges than vertices, hence, the 1-subdivision of
H
must be unbalanced.
Let
\varepsilon,d
and
n
be numbers that satisfy the conditions in Theorem
4.5
, and set
\rho=cn,

\gamma=d^{4}c/10.
Applying Theorem
4.5
finishes the proof.

\Box

Before turning to the proofs of Theorem
4.4
and
4.5
, let us have a further remark on the size of
H
in Theorem
4.4
. Denote
R(H)
the Ramsey number of

H,
that is, the least integer
n
such that in any 2-coloring of the edges of
K_{n},
some
monochromatic copy of
H
must always be formed. Graham, Rödl and Ruciński
[
8
]

proved that for every
D\geq 1
and
h\geq 6
there exists a bipartite graph
H
on
h
vertices with maximum degree at most

D
which satisfies
R(H)>2^{cD}h
for a positive constant
c.

Now set
N=R(H)-1,
and consider any 2-coloring of the edges of
K_{N}.

Denote
F
the subgraph containing the edges of the majority color, then
F
has
N
vertices and density
d_{F}\geq 1/2.

By Theorem
3.1
,
F
contains an
\varepsilon
-regular pair with parts having cardinality at least
n/2
where

n=2^{-50/\varepsilon^{2}}N.
Let
G
denote this
\varepsilon
-regular pair.
The bound on
R(H)
implies that
H\not\subset G,
even though
h\leq 2^{-cD}N=2^{50/\varepsilon^{2}-cD}n,
while by Theorem
4.4
if

\widetilde{h}\leq C_{\varepsilon}\exp(-(D+101\log(1/d)/\varepsilon^{2}))n/D,
then
G
admits an almost perfect
\widetilde{H}
-packing
for some bipartite graph
\widetilde{H}.

Hence, the graphs by which we can pack
G
are not significantly smaller than the ones which can be found in
G
at all – for that we choose
\varepsilon,d
and
r
to be constants and the graph
H
with
D=\Delta(H)
so that
cD\gg 50/\varepsilon^{2}.

The proofs of the packing theorems

The proofs of our theorems rely on the vertex decomposition of Theorem
3.2
,
we make use of the quasirandomness of the pairs in the supermatching. However, for proving Theorem
4.4
we use different tools from those used for proving Theorem
4.5
.

5.1

The proof of Theorem
4.4

We need a result, which can be found e.g. in the survey
paper
[
6
]
on the dependent random choice method by Fox and Sudakov; the following is Theorem 6.1 in
[
6
]
.

Theorem 5.1

Let
H
be a bipartite graph with
h
vertices and maximum degree
D\geq 1.
If
d>0
and

G
is a graph with
n\geq 8Dd^{-D}h
vertices and at least
d\binom{n}{2}
edges, then
H
is a subgraph of
G.

With this we are ready for the proof of Theorem
4.4
.

Proof:
If
H
is balanced, let
H^{\prime}=H.
If not, then let
H^{\prime}
be the disjoint union of two copies of

H
arranged so that
H^{\prime}
becomes balanced. In either case, we may assume that
H^{\prime}
has
2h
vertices.

If
n
is odd, leave out an arbitrary vertex of
G.
For simplicity we keep the notation
n
for the number of vertices. Then divide
V
randomly into two parts,
A
and
B
such that
|A|=|B|=n/2.
For that, first distribute the vertices of
G
into two sets,
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime},
using random coin flipping. By Chernoff’s bound

||A^{\prime}|-|B^{\prime}||\leq 10\sqrt{n\log n}
with high probability, moreover, for every
v\in V
we have that

deg(v,A^{\prime}),deg(v,B^{\prime})=(1\pm d^{4}/5)\rho/2
(here we also use that

d\gg 1/n^{c}
for any fixed
c>0
). Next, if
|A^{\prime}|>|B^{\prime}|,
then choose
|A^{\prime}|-n/2
vertices of
A^{\prime}

arbitrarily, and put them into
B^{\prime}.
Similarly, if
B^{\prime}
is larger, we put
|B^{\prime}|-n/2
vertices into
A^{\prime}.
Denote the
two parts we obtained by
A
and
B,

then

deg(v,A),deg(v,B)=(1\pm d^{4}/4)\rho/2
for every
v\in V.

Set
r=(1+d^{4}/4)\rho/2.
Simple computation shows that every degree in

G[A,B]
is at least
(1-d^{4}/2)r,
and at most
r.

Now we can apply Theorem
3.2
to
G,
with
n
replaced by
n/2.

We obtain the
(\varepsilon^{\prime},d_{i})
-bundles
G[A_{i},B_{i}],
where
\varepsilon^{\prime}<4\varepsilon^{1/5}.

For every
1\leq i\leq K
set
m_{i}=|A_{i}|\approx|B_{i}|.
Then
m_{i}\geq d^{101/\varepsilon^{2}}n/8.

Fix an arbitrary
i\geq 1.
Assume that we have already embedded vertex disjoint copies of

H^{\prime}
into
G[A_{i},B_{i}],
and the vacant parts
A^{\prime}_{i}\subset A_{i}
and
B^{\prime}_{i}\subset B_{i}
are not
too small:
|A^{\prime}_{i}|\geq\varepsilon^{\prime}|A_{i}|
and
|B^{\prime}_{i}|\geq\varepsilon^{\prime}|B_{i}|.
Then
e(G[A^{\prime}_{i},B^{\prime}_{i}])\geq(d-2\varepsilon^{\prime})(\varepsilon^{\prime}m_{i})^{2}

by
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-regularity, therefore,
G[A^{\prime}_{i},B^{\prime}_{i}]
has density
>d/3.
By Theorem
5.1
we can find a copy of
H^{\prime}
in
G[A^{\prime}_{i},B^{\prime}_{i}],
if

2\varepsilon^{\prime}m_{i}\geq 8D(d/3)^{-D}2h.

Substituting the bound for
m_{i}
we
conclude that
G[A^{\prime}_{i},B^{\prime}_{i}]
is sufficiently large and dense in order to contain a copy of
H^{\prime}.

We repeate this procedure for every
1\leq i\leq K
until the number of vacant vertices drops below

\varepsilon^{\prime}m_{i}.
Since
H^{\prime}
is balanced and the
G[A_{i},B_{i}]
pairs are almost balanced, when this happens,
altogether less than
3\varepsilon^{\prime}m_{i}
vertices remain vacant.
Hence, the total number of uncovered vertices in non-exceptional clusters
is at most
3\varepsilon^{\prime}n/2.

Since
|A_{0}|+|B_{0}|\leq 8d^{1/3}n/2
and
3\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq 12\varepsilon^{1/5}<d,
we proved what was desired.

\Box

5.2

The proof of Theorem
4.5

The beginning is identical to that of the proof of Theorem
4.4
. First divide
V
into two parts,
A
and
B
randomly, as in the proof of Theorem
4.4
, then apply Theorem
3.2
for
G[A,B].

Next we distribute the vertices of
A_{0}\cup B_{0}
among the non-exceptional clusters
A_{i},B_{i},
where
i\geq 1,

as evenly as possible. We want to assign the vertices of
A_{0}\cup B_{0}
so that no cluster will receive many of them. We say that a vertex
v

can be assigned to a non-exceptional cluster
A_{i},
if
deg(v,B_{i})\geq\rho|B_{i}|/(3n);
similarly,
v
can be assigned
to a non-exceptional cluster
B_{i},
if
deg(v,A_{i})\geq\rho|A_{i}|/(3n).

Given an arbitrary
v\in A_{0}\cup B_{0},
there are more than
\rho/3
vertices in those
non-exceptional clusters to which
v
can be assigned: this follows from the fact that
v
can have
less than
|A_{0}\cup B_{0}|<4d^{1/3}n<\rho/3
neighbors in
A_{0}\cup B_{0},

and the number of neighbors of
v
in those clusters to which we cannot assign
v
is
at most
\rho/3.

There
are at most
4d^{1/3}n
vertices to be assigned to non-exceptional clusters, and for each such
vertex we can choose from clusters having a total of at least
\rho/3
vertices. Hence, even if all
vertices of
A_{0}\cup B_{0}
can be assigned to the same subset of clusters, we can do the assignment
relatively evenly: if a cluster has
m
vertices, then at most
4d^{1/3}nm/(\rho/3)\leq 12d^{2/9}m

vertices are assigned to it.

The vertices that we distributed this way are called
exceptional
vertices,
and the complement of them is the set of
non-exceptional
vertices.

Our first goal is to incorporate the exceptional vertices into embedded copies of
H.
Say, that
(S,T)

is a non-exceptional cluster pair with
|T|=m,
where
S_{0}\subset S
includes the exceptional vertices that were assigned
to the cluster, and we define
T_{0}\subset T
analogously.

Let
S_{1}\subset S-S_{0}
and
T_{1}\subset T-T_{0}
be random subsets, both are obtained by independent coin flippings. Consider the subgraphs
(S_{0},T_{1})
and
(S_{1},T_{0}).
We are going to embed copies of
H

into these subgraphs until there are only a constant number of vertices remain uncovered in
S_{0},

respectively,
T_{0}.
This is done using a simple greedy method, discussed below.

Assume that we have found
k
vertex disjoint copies of
H
in
(S_{0},T_{1}),
and there are still
s
uncovered vertices in
S_{0}.
We make sure that the larger part,
X
is always mapped to vacant part of
S_{0}.

Hence, less than
|S_{0}|\leq 12d^{2/9}m<25d^{2/9}|T_{1}|
vertices are not vacant in
T_{1}.

In the beginning every
v\in S_{0}
had at least
\rho|T_{1}|/(4n)\geq 2d^{1/9}|T_{1}|

neighbors in
T_{1}
with high probability using the Chernoff bound. Hence, more than
d^{1/9}|T_{1}|
of these are still vacant. Set
\delta=d^{1/9}.
We need the following.

Claim 5.2

Let
S^{\prime}\subset S_{0}
be an arbitrary subset. Then there exists
u\in T_{1}
such that
deg(u,S^{\prime})\geq\delta|S^{\prime}|.

Proof:
Count the edges between
S^{\prime}
and
T^{\prime}_{1},
where
T^{\prime}_{1}
denotes the vacant part of

T_{1}.
This number is
at least
|S^{\prime}|\cdot\delta|T^{\prime}_{1}|.
Taking the average for vertices in
T^{\prime}_{1}
we obtain what was desired.
\Box

We repeatedly apply Claim
5.2
, and get the vacant vertices
u_{1},\ldots,u_{t}\in T_{1}

such that
deg(u_{i},N(u_{1},\dots,u_{i-1})\cap S^{\prime}_{0})\geq\delta^{i}|S^{\prime}_{0}|
for
1\leq i\leq t,
where

S^{\prime}_{0}
denotes the (shrinking) subset of vacant vertices in
S_{0}.

If
t=|Y|
and
\delta^{t}|S^{\prime}_{0}|\geq|X|,
then a new copy of
H
can be embedded.
It is easy to see that, since
h
is a constant, we can proceed this way until
|S^{\prime}_{0}|
goes below
a certain constant, which is at most
h/\delta^{h}.

Repeat this procedure for the bipartite subgraph
(S_{1},T_{0}).
After finishing it, we denote the subset of vacant vertices left in
S
by
S_{2}
and the subset of vacant vertices left in
T
by
T_{2}.

Consider now the new pair
(S_{2},T_{2}).
Since
(S,T)
was an
(\varepsilon^{\prime},d)
-super-regular pair, the new pair is
(3\varepsilon^{\prime},d/3)
-super-regular: we may have
3\varepsilon^{\prime}
instead of
\varepsilon^{\prime}
since the new cluster sizes
are at least about half of the previous ones, and we added only a constant number of new high degree vertices
(these are the exceptional vertices). Moreover,
S_{2}
contains the random subset
S-S_{1}
and
T_{2}
contains the random subset
T-T_{1}.

Hence, by Chernoff’s bound every vertex in
S_{2}\cup T_{2}
has sufficiently many neighbors in the opposite cluster.

Since
H
is a fixed, unbalanced bipartite graph on
h
vertices, the size of the larger part divided
by the size of the smaller part is larger than
1+1/h.
Using this, we may assign (but not map) copies of
H,
one by one,
so that vertices of
X
is assigned to the larger cluster (recall, that
|X|>|Y|
). We proceed this way until we
have assigned enough copies of
H
for balancing out the clusters: set
\Delta=||S_{2}|-|T_{2}||
and
\Delta_{H}=|X|-|Y|
, then for balancing we need
\lfloor\Delta/\Delta_{H}\rfloor
copies of
H.

Next we let
H^{\prime}
be the union of two copies
H,
such that both vertex parts of
H^{\prime}
are
X\cup Y.

We continue the assignment with the copies of
H^{\prime}.

It is easy to see that after finishing the assignment, the number of vertices assigned to
S_{2}\cup T_{2}
is larger than
|S_{2}\cup T_{2}|-h.

Finally, we apply the Blow-up lemma
[
12
,
13
]
for embedding the vertex disjoint copies of

H
we assigned to the pair
(S_{2},T_{2}).

Repeate this procedure for every

(A_{i},B_{i})
(
i\geq 1
) pair in the decomposition. Using the previous observation,
at most
C\leq K\cdot h\leq\frac{8h}{d}\cdot d^{-100/\varepsilon^{2}}
vertices remain uncovered.

\Box
