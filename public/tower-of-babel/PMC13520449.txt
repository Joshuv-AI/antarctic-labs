Short-term assessment of non-surgical periodontal therapy reduces oral pathogens and pro-inflammatory cytokines, enhancing clinical outcomes in patients with chronic kidney disease
Authors: Mutita Wongsuwanlert, Pornpen Sangthawan, Praphansri Ruangsri, Rawee Teanpaisan, Supontep Teerakanok, Nuntiya Pahumunto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeriodontal diseases have been reported to be associated with chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression. Thus, this study aimed to evaluate non-surgical periodontal therapy (NSPT) in patients with CKD disease progression by monitoring oral parameters, pathogen levels, cytokine profiles, and kidney function.MethodsForty-five participants (age 60.0 ± 8.5 years) were examined at baseline and at 3 months after NSPT for oral parameters, oral pathogens (Porphyromonas gingivalis, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Candida albicans), cytokines (IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, IL-10), and kidney functions (serum creatinine, eGFR, urine protein-creatinine ratio).ResultsAt baseline, the CKD patients were diagnosed with stage II–IV periodontitis and exhibited measurable periodontal pathogen levels (P. gingivalis, 1.2 log CFU/mL; F. nucleatum, 4.4 log CFU/mL; A. actinomycetemcomitans, 0.3 log CFU/mL; and C. albicans, 4.1 log CFU/mL). Concurrently, pro-inflammatory cytokine concentrations were recorded at 0.2–4.4 pg/mL for IL-6, 1.9–25.5 pg/mL for IL-8, and 0.0–26.1 pg/mL for TNF-α. Conversely, IL-10 levels ranged from 0.0 to 13.2 pg/mL. After 3 months of NSPT, the levels of pathogens (1.1–10.9 fold) and cytokines (1.2–2.6 fold) were significantly reduced, while IL-10 levels significantly increased (0.7–1.9 fold). The number of patients showing improvement for serum creatinine and urine protein-creatinine ratio in CKD patients was 59.1–71.4% and 60.9–90.0%, respectively.ConclusionOur findings suggest that NSPT may benefit CKD patients by reducing oral pathogens and inflammation while increasing IL-10. There was evidence of a possible trend towards enhanced kidney function in CKD patients treated with NSPT.Trial registrationThe study was registered in the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20240516002; registered date 16 May 2024), Thailand.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08906-2.

Introduction

A significant association was observed between severe periodontitis and poor renal outcomes in patients with pre-dialytic chronic kidney disease (CKD), which reinforces the clinical bidirectional relationship between these two conditions [1, 2]. The two-way interaction between periodontitis and CKD is marked by several pathophysiologic mechanisms, such as an impaired immune system, systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and dysbiosis of oral microbiota [3, 4]. The overgrowth of oral pathogens (Porphyromonas gingivalis, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, and Tannerella forsythia) in the dental pockets [5] can cause inflammation and damage to periodontal tissue. Specifically, P. gingivalis and A. actinomycetemcomitans have the strongest evidence of systemic dissemination and association with adverse outcomes in chronic conditions such as CKD [5]. F. nucleatum was included for its crucial role as a biological 'bridge' that facilitates the coaggregation of early and late bacterial colonizers [6], while C. albicans is increasingly recognized as a key opportunistic fungal pathogen that synergistically exacerbates bacterial virulence in periodontal pockets [7]. Previous studies showed that patients with CKD had 20% pocket depths showing moderate periodontitis, and 11% clinical attachment loss (CAL) showing severe periodontitis. Moreover, a correlation between periodontitis and other systemic diseases, such as chronic viral hepatitis [8], gastrointestinal cancers, and cardiovascular disease [9]. This systemic problem is driven by the continuous translocation of bacteria and pro-inflammatory cytokines [e.g., interleukin-6 (IL-6), Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α)] into the bloodstream [9, 10], thereby exacerbating distant organ inflammation. Given this well-established oral-systemic axis, understanding how periodontal intervention alters systemic inflammatory markers remains critical [11].

A systematic review and meta-analysis reported a correlation between CKD progression and periodontal diseases, as a higher prevalence of severe periodontal diseases was observed in stage 3 CKD patients than in stage 2 patients [11–13]. Studies have reported that P. gingivalis, A. actinomycetemcomitans, and F. nucleatum are more common in patients with CKD than in healthy individuals, as they can enter the bloodstream via the periodontal epithelium and cause bacteremia [11, 14, 15]. It contributes to diabetic renal inflammation by triggering glomerulosclerosis, macrophage activation, and increased pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, and transforming growth factor-β), leading to kidney damage and altered protein accumulation [11, 14, 15], interfering with cell cycle progression, activating caspase-1 in an NLRP3-dependent manner, secreting danger signaling ASC, and facilitating the release of high mobility group box 1 protein [11].

Cell death and inflammation also play an important role in the kidney; thus, the removal of periodontal pathogens may improve inflammatory markers in CKD patients. Previous studies have shown that periodontal treatment decreased C-reactive protein levels, IL-6, and blood urea nitrogen in CKD patients [16, 17]. Also, the glomerular filtration rate, probing depth, clinical attachment loss, and bleeding on probing all significantly improved [18]. However, there has been no study reporting changes in periodontal pathogens and cytokine levels (IL-8 and IL-10) after non-surgical periodontal therapy. IL-8 is a chemoattractant driving neutrophil-mediated local periodontal destruction and contributing to systemic endothelial dysfunction in CKD. Conversely, IL-10 is the resolution of clinical inflammation and the restoration of host immune homeostasis. Thus, this study aimed to investigate the effects of non-surgical periodontal therapy (NSPT) on reducing oral pathogens and pro-inflammatory cytokines while increasing anti-inflammatory cytokines to promote clinical outcomes (periodontal health and kidney function) in patients with CKD. The null hypothesis was that NSPT does not significantly modify periodontopathogen levels, cytokine levels, or kidney function parameters in CKD patients.

Methods

Subject population

The study protocol was approved before commencement by the Institutional Ethics Committees of the Faculty of Medicine (REC.64578–14-1; approved 31 March 2022) and the Faculty of Dentistry (EC6506-025; approved 28 June 2022) at Prince of Songkla University. The trial was retrospectively registered with the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20240516002; registered 16 May 2024).

The sample size was determined to be 52, based on an effect size of 0.25 (medium effect), with an α-error of 0.05 and a β-error of 0.05 (our pilot study). The primary outcome of this study was the reduction of oral pathogens and inflammatory cytokines, and the secondary outcome was the clinical enhancement in CKD patients. The study was conducted from August 2022 to February 2024 at the outpatient clinic of Songklanagarind Hospital, Songkhla, Thailand. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The inclusion criteria were: a diagnosis of stage 2–4 CKD [19], age between 30 and 70 years, had at least 12 remaining teeth, had not received periodontal treatment in the preceding 6 months, and had not used antibiotics within the past 3 months. The exclusion criteria included those having periodontal treatment within 6 months, acute kidney failure in the last 3 months, use of immunosuppressive medication, and those with hematocrit < 30%. Intervention

Non-Surgical Periodontal Therapy (NSPT) was performed by the calibrated periodontists. Three calibrated periodontists (each with > 10 years of experience) performed all NSPT procedures. Inter-examiner agreement was established through standardized calibration sessions conducted before patient enrollment, with an acceptable intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC ≥ 0.85). Full-mouth scaling and root planing were completed in two visits within one week, treating two quadrants per session. Supragingival debridement was performed using an ultrasonic scaler (Cavitron Select SPS, Dentsply Sirona, York, PA, USA) with a universal tip (FSI-SLI-10S) at medium power, followed by subgingival root planing using site-specific Gracey curettes (3–4, 7–8, 11–12, 13–14; Hu-Friedy). No chemical adjuvants, including local or systemic antimicrobials or antiseptic irrigation, were used during or following the NSPT procedures. Oral hygiene instructions (OHI) were provided to all participants, encompassing the modified Bass toothbrushing technique performed twice daily for a minimum of 2 min using fluoride-containing toothpaste (≥ 1,000 ppm fluoride), and once-daily interdental cleaning with dental floss. Patients were permitted to withdraw if they experienced discomfort or adverse reactions. Compliance with OHI was monitored through daily written logs maintained by participants, and protocol adherence was reviewed at each follow-up visit.

A no-treatment control group (no NSPT) was not included due to ethical considerations, as withholding standard periodontal therapy from CKD patients would have been inappropriate. However, each patient served as their own control through baseline assessments, allowing us to evaluate the effects of the intervention over time.

Sample collection and clinical evaluation

Whole blood (10 mL) was collected from patients with CKD, and the serum was separated by centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 5 min at 4°C. The collected serum was analyzed to determine serum creatinine levels and quantify the concentrations of inflammatory markers (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-8, and IL-10). Urine samples were collected for the protein-creatinine ratio. The parameters were measured at baseline and followed up at three months.

Full-mouth periodontal examinations were performed by three certified periodontists using a UNC-15 probe (~ 0.25 N force). Calibration was conducted before the study and repeated one hour later, achieving intra- and inter-examiner Kappa scores of > 0.85. Probing depth (PD), clinical attachment level (CAL), and bleeding on probing (BOP) were recorded on a standardized charting form at each visit (Supplementary 1). PD may be slightly overestimated due to calculus-induced probe deflection; this is a recognized standard limitation that does not invalidate post-treatment comparisons. Periodontitis staging was based on the 2018 American Academy of Periodontology (AAP) criteria. Unstimulated saliva (1 mL) from participants was collected using spitting into a centrifuge tube, while pooled supragingival and subgingival plaques were obtained using a sterile curette and weighed before pathogen detection. All clinical data and samples from CKD patients were collected at baseline and at the 3-month follow-up.

Detection of periodontal pathogens using the real-time PCR method

Unstimulated saliva (1 mL) and pooled plaque (supra and subgingival plaque) were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min. The pellets were added with 20 mg/mL lysozyme and incubated for 1 h. DNA was extracted using a DNA extraction kit (PureDireX, Taiwan), and the level of pathogens was measured by real-time PCR.

A 20-μL real-time PCR reaction comprised 5 μL of purified DNA template from saliva or plaque samples, 3 μL of RNAase-free water, 10 μL of SensiFAST SYBR® No-ROX mix (Bioline Reagent Ltd., Foster City, CA, USA), and 2 μL of a specific primer (confirmed using standard reference strains: P. gingivalis ATCC33327, F. nucleatum ATCC25865, A. actinomycetemcomitans ATCC33384, and C. albicans ATCC90028 before clinical analysis): P. gingivalis: 5’-CCTACGTGTACGGACAGAGCTATA-3’ and 5’-AGGATCGCTCAGCGTAGC ATT-3’ [20], A. actinomycetemcomitans: 5’-ACGCAGACGATTGACTGAATTTAA-3’ and 5’-GATCTTCACAGCTATATGGCAGCTA-3’ [14], C. albicans 5’-CACGACGGAGTTTCACAA GA-3’ and 5’-CGATGGAAGTTTGAGGCAAT-3’ [21], and F. nucleatum: 5’-GGATTTATTGG GCGTAAAGC-3’ and 5’- GGCATTCCTACAAATATCTACGAA-3’). The reaction was amplified using the CFX96 TouchTM Real-Time PCR detection system (BioRad, Foster, CA, USA). The real-time PCR conditions were run at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 °C for 2 min and 40 cycles at 95 °C for 20 s; at 60 °C for bacterial strains (P. gingivalis, A. actinomycetemcomitans, and F. nucleatum) and 55 °C for C. albicans for 20 s; and at 72 °C for 25 s. The quantities of bacteria in the samples were calculated using a standard curve. A linear standard curve was plotted for each bacterial species from log CFU/mL compared to the corresponding Cq and showed a high correlation coefficient (R2 > 0.99). The quantities of bacteria in the saliva samples were reported as log CFU/mL, whereas those in the plaque samples were normalized by the plaque weight and expressed as log CFU/g. All real-time PCR reactions for saliva and plaque samples were performed in duplicate.

Determination of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines using the ELISA method

Cytokine levels (IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, and TNF-α) in the serum samples were measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The detection ranges and limits of detection of the assays were as follows: IL-6 (range: 0.08-5.00 pg/mL, sensitivity: 0.03 pg/mL), IL-8 (range: 0.39-25.00 pg/mL, sensitivity: 0.10 pg/mL), TNF-α (range: 0.31–20.00 pg/mL, sensitivity: 0.13 pg/mL), and IL-10 (range: 0.39–25.00 pg/mL, sensitivity: 0.03 pg/mL). The ELISA plate was coated with 1X capture antibody in coating buffer and incubated overnight at 4°C. The plate was washed 3 times with wash buffer, and the wells were blocked with 200 µL of 1X ELISA/ELISPOT diluent at room temperature for 1 h. Then the plate was washed, and the sample or standard cytokine (IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, and TNF-α) was added and incubated overnight at 4°C. The plate was washed 3 times, and then the 1X detection antibody was added to the wells. The plate was incubated for 1 h at room temperature and was washed 3 times with the wash buffer. Diluted avidin-HRP was added to the wells and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. The plate was washed 3–5 times with wash buffer and 1X tetramethylbenzidine substrate solution. The reaction was incubated for 15 min, and then 2 N H2SO4 was added. The plate was measured for absorbance at OD450 subtraction with OD570. The concentration of cytokines was determined and presented as pg/mL.

Measurement of kidney functions (serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and urine protein creatinine ratio)

Serum creatinine and the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) were determined from serum samples. The eGFR was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation. The urine protein creatinine ratio was assessed in urine samples. The detection methods for the serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and urine creatinine utilized enzymatic methods (Abbott, Altavista, Virginia, USA), whereas the urine protein was evaluated using the urease test (Abbott, Altavista, Virginia, USA). The nephrologist interpreted this information and provided it to the researchers at baseline and after 3 months of follow-up. The number of patients showing improvement was calculated as (the number of kidneys that improved after NSPT/the number of patients in that group) × 100.

Data analysis

Demographic data (teeth, PD, CAL, BOP) were presented as mean ± SD. Pathogen levels were shown as boxplots with 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles with the mean. Pro-inflammatory cytokine and IL-10 concentrations were shown as scatter plots with the mean, while kidney function was shown as a paired line plot. The changes before and after treatment were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (IBM SPSS Statistics 29.0.0.0). A p-value ≤ 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Demographic data of patients with CKD

The study was carried out from August 2022 to February 2024. A total of ninety subjects met the eligibility criteria for this study. One subject was excluded, and thirty-seven subjects declined to participate. Of the 52 patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) enrolled in this study, 7 patients (13.5%) missed their 3-month follow-up appointment due to relocation (n = 4) or did not attend the scheduled appointment (n = 3). Ultimately, 45 out of 52 subjects (86.5%), including 12 females (26.7%) and 33 males (73.3%), with a mean age of 60.0 ± 8.5 years, participated in this study. In this study, most patients were classified as having stage 3 CKD (62.2%; 28 subjects), followed by stage 4 (22.2%; 10 subjects) and stage 2 (15.6%; 7 subjects).

Periodontal pathogen levels and cytokine concentration before and after NSPT in patients with CKD

The patients with CKD were found to have oral pathogens in the saliva and dental plaque (both supra and subgingival plaque). In saliva at the baseline, the amount of Candida albicans was 4.1 (3.2, 5.4) log CFU/mL, Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans 0.3 (0.0, 2.6) log CFU/mL, Fusobacterium nucleatum 4.4 (0.3, 6.1) log CFU/mL, and Porphyromonas gingivalis 1.2 (0.0–2.9) log CFU/mL (Fig. 2). Supra and subgingival plaque presented similar pathogen levels to saliva but they were generally higher, as follows: C. albicans 6.5 (1.8, 9.8) and 6.8 (5.3, 9.8) log CFU/g, A. actinomycetemcomitans 2.7 (1.8, 4.4) and 2.6 (0.0, 5.2) log CFU/g, F. nucleatum 7.7 (6.7, 8.6) and 7.7 (5.7, 8.3) log CFU/g, and P. gingivalis 3.9 (2.7–5.6) and 5.1 (0.0, 6.3) log CFU/g, respectively (Supplementary 2 and Supplementary 3). Notably, patients with CKD in stage 4 showed significantly higher levels of oral pathogens compared to the other stages (p ≤ 0.05). After NSPT, the pathogens in the oral cavity of all patients with CKD decreased significantly, as shown in Fig. 2, Supplementary data 2, and Supplementary data 3 (p ≤ 0.05). The pathogens in saliva, supra, and subgingival plaque decreased 1.2–7.3 fold, 1.6–10.9 fold, and 1.1–2.0 fold, respectively, compared to the baseline. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the effects of non-surgical periodontal therapy (NSPT) on kidney function in Thai patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), specifically exploring how reducing oral pathogens and inflammation may influence renal outcomes. Baseline analysis revealed that patients in CKD stages 2–4 concurrently exhibited stage II-IV periodontitis. Additionally, all participants presented with quantifiable baseline levels of periodontal pathogens (including P. gingivalis, C. albicans, and F. nucleatum) alongside pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α). Following NSPT, the levels of these pathogens and pro-inflammatory markers decreased, while anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10) increased. Furthermore, concurrent improvements in both periodontal parameters and kidney function were observed across all CKD stages.

Periodontal pathogens in deep periodontal pockets can induce pro-inflammatory cytokines that destroy the connective tissue around the teeth and possibly impair kidney function due to the increase in cytokines. It is well known that P. gingivalis is more prevalent in patients with CKD, followed by the prevalence of A. actinomycetemcomitans and F. nucleatum [14]. A study found that C. albicans was increasingly prevalent in immunocompromised individuals, eventually leading to renal failure, resulting in severe morbidity and mortality [22]. Our study showed high levels of these microorganisms in the oral cavity of patients with CKD, particularly in CKD stage 4, suggesting that the virulence of these bacteria, including their adhesion, invasion, biofilm formation, and regulation of host immunity, may directly or indirectly influence the progression of CKD. They also affected kidney function by triggering phagocytosis, adhesion signaling, inflammatory responses, and inducible nitric oxide synthase [11]. Our study used DNA-based methods for microbial identification; both viable and nonviable microorganisms are detected; consequently, the estimated bacterial and yeast abundance may be high. However, this finding was similar to previous studies demonstrating that C. albicans levels were higher in subjects with chronic periodontitis (4.5–5.6 log CFU/mL) compared to those in healthy individuals (0.0–4.7 log CFU/mL) [23, 24].

These pathogens can trigger the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α) by various virulence factors such as LPS, fimbriae, gingipain, leukotoxin, and chitin. These cytokines triggered renal inflammation, which affected renal damage in glomerulonephritis and was involved in the progression of renal insufficiency through its cytotoxic and pro-inflammatory effects [25, 26]. Three months after NSPT, periodontal pathogens in the oral cavity and pro-inflammatory cytokines in the serum of patients with CKD revealed a decrease compared to the baseline due to the removal of dental biofilm, which substantially reduced 80% of the pathogens. A previous study found that after NSPT for 3 months, the levels of pathogens (P. gingivalis and A. actinomycetemcomitans) were reduced compared to the baseline; however, no reduction was observed in F. nucleatum [16]. The reduction in pathogens was related to a significant decrease in antigenic load, leading to less pro-inflammatory stimulation. Furthermore, after six months of NSPT, serum levels of the inflammatory marker high-sensitivity C-reactive protein significantly decreased in CKD patients [14]. This attenuation of systemic inflammation is closely related to improvements in CKD status, highlighting the potential role of periodontal therapy in modulating systemic disease outcomes. Normally, in CKD patients, systemic inflammation is a main contributor to disease progression. Patients with lower circulating levels of IL-10 tend to exhibit more severe inflammation, leading to a faster decline in kidney function. Our study found that after NSPT, IL-10 levels increased from 2.2 pg/mL to 4.4 pg/mL (Cohen’s d ≈ 1.02), suggesting enhanced anti-inflammatory activity. This change may help inhibit excessive inflammatory responses, regulate immunosuppression, delay tissue fibrosis, and promote tissue repair [27]. Although absolute IL-10 remained low, the observed elevation may help modulate systemic inflammation in CKD patients, complementing the reductions in pro-inflammatory cytokines.

Our study did not use the plaque index to measure the periodontal parameters because the primary outcomes were focused on microbiological levels and inflammatory outcomes. While probing depth (PD) and bleeding on probing (BOP) indicate the effectiveness of gingival inflammation, clinical attachment loss (CAL) shows only partial restoration of periodontal support. The periodontal conditions (PD and CAL) were greater in dialysis patients than in healthy individuals [20, 28]. The results were consistent in that all CKD patients with periodontitis could be attributed to their high age (average age 60.0 ± 8.5 years), as age was one of the risk factors for periodontitis. Moreover, ninety percent of patients with CKD in this study revealed poor oral hygiene. Approximately 10% of the patients had never visited a dentist, and over 50% had not undergone a dental examination within the past year. After 3 months of NSPT, the CKD patients showed clinical improvements in oral parameters (PD = 1–3 mm and CAL = 0 mm increase while reducing BOP), indicating the impact of NSPT and oral hygiene instruction to improve dental health. According to Raeesi et al. [29], dental education programs, along with scaling and root planing for 3 months, could enhance the quality of life, periodontal parameters, and survival rates of dialysis patients. However, in some patients with CKD, there was no difference from the baseline due to poor oral hygiene.

The prevalence and incidence of CKD have constantly increased by more than 10% in many countries, resulting in approximately 1.1 million deaths worldwide [24]. CKD progression is influenced by several key confounding factors, such as diabetes status, level of hypertension control, dietary habits, medication use, smoking behavior, and oral hygiene adherence. These factors may also affect both periodontal inflammation and renal outcomes. Our study found that patients with CKD had periodontitis stages II-IV, which in turn impacted kidney function (high serum creatinine and a high urine protein creatinine ratio). These findings suggest an association between periodontal disease severity and impaired kidney function. Our study showed that more than 70% the number of the patients showed improvement (with a reduction in serum creatinine and the urine protein creatinine ratio) after NSPT. These findings suggest that effective NSPT may have an advantageous impact on renal function, potentially mediated by reductions in inflammation and pathogen levels. Previous studies revealed that C-reactive protein and IL-6 decreased in patients with CKD after NSPT (2.7 mg/L after 3 months and 3.56 mg/L after 6 months) [4, 30]. Periodontal treatment also had a statistically significant positive effect on the glomerular filtration rate of patients with CKD [4, 16, 17]. A possible explanation for this is that the successful reduction of oral pathogens and pro-inflammatory triggers may be a beneficial intervention to improve the quality of life of patients with CKD, protecting them from significant morbidity and mortality, and serving as a crucial preventive measure to improve their prognosis. Our study demonstrated significant associations between serum creatinine and pro-inflammatory cytokines both before NSPT (p < 0.001, r = 0.641) and after NSPT (p < 0.001, r = 0.653). Additionally, the reduction in IL-8 levels correlated significantly with the decrease in F. nucleatum (p = 0.024, r = 0.367). Thus, the reduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines and periodontopathogen may slow the progression of kidney disease.

The biological plausibility of our findings strongly supports the shared inflammatory pathways linking periodontitis and CKD. Because periodontal infection contributes significantly to the systemic inflammatory load, the marked reduction in keystone pathogens and pro-inflammatory cytokines, alongside the increase in anti-inflammatory IL-10 following NSPT, reinforces this bidirectional relationship [16]. The mitigation of periodontal inflammation via NSPT may therefore help alleviate the systemic immunologic burden in CKD patients, which could theoretically explain the observed trends toward improved kidney function [16]. However, the absolute clinical changes in renal parameters were modest. As these early shifts may still fall within the range of normal physiological variability, they highlight disease stabilization rather than clear renal recovery. Nonetheless, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size and the heterogeneity of the study population limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the lack of a control group, required by constraints in the clinical setting, prevents the establishment of direct causal links. Finally, the reliance on a per-protocol analysis rather than an intention-to-treat approach, as well as the absence of data imputation methods when applying regression models, may have introduced analytical bias.

Conclusion

These findings suggest an association between periodontal disease severity and impaired kidney function. Non-surgical periodontal therapy (NSPT) was associated with reductions in both systemic inflammation and bacterial levels in CKD patients in the short term. Specifically, pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α) and periodontal pathogens (P. gingivalis, F. nucleatum, A. actinomycetemcomitans, and C. albicans) decreased by 1.1- to 10.9-fold, while levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 increased by over 1.9-fold from baseline. Concurrently, all clinical periodontal parameters (probing depth, clinical attachment level, and bleeding on probing) improved. While kidney function parameters (serum creatinine and urine protein-creatinine ratio) showed moderate trends toward improvement, these shifts may still fall within normal physiological variations. Future studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up periods are required to confirm the long-term clinical relevance of NSPT on renal outcomes.
