JUNE 30, 2026

 ABSTRACT

 Air-ingress following a depressurized loss-of-forced-cooling (DLOFC) event is a challenging, multi-
physics safety scenario for High-Temperature Gas-Cooled Reactors (HTGRs), involving coupled gas com-
position transport, buoyancy-driven flow redistribution, graphite oxidation, and structural heat-up. Despite
its importance — air ingress is a key scenario identified in the PIRT process for the HTGRs — existing
system-level safety codes have lacked the integrated capability to simulate the complete event sequence
with high confidence. This report documents the development, validation, and demonstration of three new
capabilities in the SAM code to address this gap: (1) a multi-component gas mixture flow model with binary
diffusion to track the helium-air composition and its effect on system density and flow; (2) a 0-D graphite
oxidation model based on the Roes correlation, including oxygen consumption and exothermic heat release;
and (3) an isentropic critical flow model for accurate representation of primary system depressurization
through a break.

 These capabilities are validated against two benchmark experiments. The NSTF heavy-gas ingress
experiment validates the multi-component flow model: SAM correctly reproduces the rapid buoyancy-
driven flow stagnation and subsequent natural circulation recovery driven by composition-dependent den-
sity changes. The NACOK graphite oxidation experiment validates the oxidation model: SAM predicts a
bottom-level graphite weight loss of 25%, in close agreement with the measured 24%, and reproduces
the strong axial nonuniformity and block-geometry dependence of oxidation, at a level comparable to the
SPECTRA and TINTE codes.

 The validated capabilities are then exercised together in an integrated, reactor-scale simulation of a
DLOFC air-ingress transient in a simplified HTR-PM pebble-bed reactor. In a single calculation spanning
approximately 8 days, SAM reproduces the complete accident sequence: rapid depressurization, density-
and diffusion-driven air ingress over ˜15 hours, onset of buoyancy-driven natural circulation, exothermic
graphite oxidation with a peak fuel temperature at ˜62 hours, and eventual passive cooldown. These results
demonstrate that SAM now provides the nuclear community with a preliminarily validated, modern system-
level tool for HTGR air-ingress safety analysis, filling a recognized capability gap. Future extensions to
broaden species tracking, improve oxidation chemistry, and refine the reactor model are discussed.

1 Introduction

 High-Temperature Gas-Cooled Reactor (HTGR) is one of the candidates for the Gen-IV reactor designs
and is currently under active development worldwide. There are two typical designs of HTGRs: the pebble-
bed type and the prismatic type. In the pebble-bed HTGR, the reactor core is filled with fuel pebbles
containing tristructural isotropic (TRISO) particles. In the prismatic type HTGR, TRISO particles are packed
into pellet forms and turned into long fuel rods inserted in hexagonal graphite blocks in the core. In both
HTGR designs, helium is commonly used as the primary coolant because of its chemical inertness, low
neutron absorption cross section, and relatively high specific heat capacity. However, due to its low density,
HTGRs are pressurized to increase the density of helium, enhancing heat removal capability.

 The use of pressurized helium in HTGRs presents some unique challenges, especially under accident
scenarios. In an event of a de-pressurized loss of forced cooling (DLOFC) accident where the core is
de-pressurized, the system pressure drops significantly such that the low density of helium may hinder
the formation of natural circulation within the core. Under these conditions, heat is removed primarily by
conduction and thermal radiation from the core to the decay heat removal system. In this scenario, fuel
temperature may rise significantly above the nominal value during normal operating condition and risk being
damaged. In addition, the evacuation of helium may cause air from the reactor cavity to enter the core.
During this so-called air-ingress scenario, graphite in the pebble fuels and the reflectors in the core may
undergo oxidation by reacting with the oxygen in the air. This may compromise the integrity of the graphite
and release fission products from the fuel pebbles into the reactor cavity or weaken the graphite structures
located under the pebble bed. The importance of the air ingress scenario is evident as it is one of the scenarios
whose safety related phenomena are identified using the Phenomena Identification and Ranking Table (PIRT)
process for the High-Temperature Gas Cooled Reactor–Pebble-Bed Module (HTR-PM) [1].

 Air ingress can occur as a consequence of a DLOFC event, in which a loss of confinement integrity (e.g.,
a break in the vessel or connecting piping) is followed by a loss of coolant inventory. The process typically
unfolds in four stages:

 1. Depressurization – Helium coolant rapidly vents to the containment or environment until the primary
system pressure equilibrates with the surroundings.

 2. Density-Driven and Diffusion-Driven Gas Exchange – The higher density of air relative to helium
promotes buoyancy-driven mixing and stratification, leading to gradual air replacement in the lower regions
of the reactor. Depending on the core geometry and flow resistance, upward air transport into the core can be
governed either by molecular diffusion or by buoyancy. This slow stage may persist for a long time before
loop-wide flow is established.

 3. Onset of Loop-wide Natural Circulation – As air accumulates and the density difference between
the core and the return path grows, the buoyancy force eventually overcomes the core hydrostatic head and
frictional losses, establishing a sustained, loop-wide natural circulation that delivers a continuous supply of
oxygen to the hot core.

 4. Graphite Oxidation and Core Heat-Up– Oxygen transported into the core reacts with graphite
structural components and fuel element blocks. Oxidation is exothermic and can lead to material loss,
weakening of structural integrity, and potential reconfiguration of the flow field.

2

 The safety importance of air-ingress phenomena has motivated decades of experimental and modeling
work. Key experimental campaigns have been carried out to investigate the fundamental physics. A variety
of computational approaches and tools have been used or developed by many researchers to investigate the
effects of air-ingress in HTGRs, ranging from system-level codes such as TINTE, SPECTRA, and GAMMA
to higher fidelity Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) codes [2–9]. The inherently multiphysics character of
the air-ingress scenario that combines thermal fluids phenomena with chemical reactions presents substantial
challenges to these computational tools. Furthermore, there are also difficulties due to the difference in the
time scales of the mechanisms with which air enters the core. Significant gaps remain in predicting coupled
multi-physics behavior across scales, particularly for the transition from molecular diffusion–dominated
ingress to buoyancy-driven convection, and for oxidation effects on flow patterns and structural integrity.
As a result, the coupled simulation of an HTGR air-ingress event — from primary system depressurization
through diffusion-dominated ingress, natural circulation onset, graphite oxidation, and passive cooldown —
in a single system-level calculation remains a capability gap.

 The System Analysis Module (SAM) code [10] is under development and supported by DOE-NE’s
Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS) program as a modern system-level modeling
and simulation tool for advanced non-light water reactor safety analyses. The SAM code capabilities for
HTGR applications have been extensively demonstrated in the simulations of various HTGR designs, such as
MHTGR-350 (General Atomics) [11], a generic pebble bed gas-cooled reactor [12, 13], the HTR-PM [14],
Holos Quad (Holos Gen) [15], and Horizontal-Compact HTGR (MIT/Boston Atomics) [16], under various
transient conditions. Extensive SAM code validation for High-Temperature Gas-Cooled Reactor (HTGR)
applications have also been performed, including the OECD/NEA High Temperature Test Facility (HTTF)
thermal-hydraulics benchmark [17, 18], and the ongoing code benchmark activities using High Temperature
Engineering Test Reactor (HTTR) data. To extend SAM’s applicability to air-ingress scenarios — which
require tracking gas composition changes, modeling graphite oxidation, and accurately representing primary
system depressurization — three new modeling capabilities have been developed: a multi-component gas
mixture flow model with binary diffusion, a 0-D graphite oxidation model based on the Roes correlation, and
an isentropic critical flow model for break depressurization.

 This report documents the SAM capabilities developed to model the air-ingress event and presents the
corresponding validation and demonstration studies. The following sections first describe the underlying
mechanisms and progression of air ingress in detail. The newly implemented SAM modeling capabilities for
air-ingress scenarios are then introduced. Subsequently, these capabilities are validated against the Natural
Convection Shutdown Heat Removal Test Facility (NSTF) heavy-gas ingress experiment and the NACOK
air-ingress experiments. Finally, a demonstration analysis of an air-ingress transient using SAM is presented,
followed by a summary and a discussion of future work. Together, these capabilities establish SAM as a
validated, system-level tool for HTGR air-ingress safety analysis, supporting HTGR licensing efforts and
filling a recognized gap in the community’s modeling toolkit.

2 Air-ingress Events in GCR

 This section provides a detailed description of the air-ingress scenario in HTGRs and a review of
prior experimental and modeling studies. A loss-of-forced-cooling (LOFC) accident is considered as a
critical event for a High-Temperature Gas-Cooled Reactor (HTGR). In general, there are two types of LOFC
accidents, namely the pressurized loss of forced cooling (PLOFC) and the de-pressurized loss of forced
cooling (DLOFC). During PLOFC, the reactor is scrammed and the helium blower is tripped, while the
primary circuit of the reactor remains intact. Some possible initiating events that could lead to PLOFC
include the inadvertent withdrawal of a control rod, uncontrolled acceleration of the helium blower, loss of
offsite power, and loss of feed water [1]. Under PLOFC conditions, natural circulation of the pressurized
helium can be established, thereby facilitating decay heat removal from the core to the reactor cavity cooling
system (RCCS). The maximum fuel temperature is generally not considered a major concern during PLOFC
as it is expected to remain well below the safety limit due to the enhanced heat removal from the natural
circulation in the core.

 By contrast, the DLOFC is typically characterized by rapid de-pressurization of the core and the primary
circuit followed by an emergency shutdown of the reactor. Possible initiating events include unisolable breaks
of the pressure boundary, such as an rupture of instrumentation tube. Due to the low helium density in the
de-pressurized system, natural circulation cannot be established, which leads to less effective redistribution
and removal of decay heat than in the PLOFC. Thermal radiation and conduction become the dominant
decay heat removal mechanism during DLOFC. An extreme form of DLOFC originates from the so-called
double ended guillotine break (DEGB) at the horizontal coaxial duct of the core, which is considered a
Beyond Design Basis Accident (BDBA) in HTGRs [19]. In this scenario, the rapid de-pressurization of the
system is followed by the ingress of air into the core, initially through molecular diffusion and then buoyancy
effect. Eventually, if the buoyancy force surpasses the pressure drop of the core, a stable natural circulation is
established. The ingress of air into the core can lead to the graphite oxidation, which may compromise the
integrity of the fuel and release fission products into the environment. At the same time, the lower graphite
structures and reflectors can also undergo oxidation that can potentially challenge the structural integrity of
the core.

 The air ingress event in HTGRs is described in detail by Oh and Kim [20] and is illustrated in Figure
2.1. Following the DEGB, the core is rapidly de-pressurized and helium is discharged from the core. After
de-pressurization, air in the containment enters the reactor vessel through the bottom part of the horizontal
co-axial pipe forming a stratified flow due to the difference in air and helium densities. At this stage, molecular
diffusion due to the difference in air concentration inside and outside the reactor can also play a role in
introducing air into the bottom plenum. However, several studies compared the time scale of stratified flow
to the time scale of molecular diffusion and found that the latter had negligible effects at this stage of the
transient [5, 20].

 After the bottom plenum of the reactor is filled with air, the temperature difference between the air inside
and outside the vessel results in a density gradient that in turn drives a counter-current flow in the bottom
plenum. At this stage, the counter-current flow persists because there is always a temperature difference
between the air inside and outside of the vessel. At the same time, due to heating in the lower plenum,
some of the air from the counter-current flow will flow into the reactor core if the buoyancy force in the
lower plenum is sufficient to overcome the hydrostatic head and frictional pressure drop in the core, thus

4

initiating the onset of natural circulation. Conversely, if the buoyancy force is insufficient to overcome the
hydrostatic head and core frictional pressure drop, the flow of air from the lower plenum to the core will
be dominated by molecular diffusion. In the study by Oh and Kim [20], by comparing buoyancy force to
hydrostatic head, it was found that the flow of air into the prismatic core of the 600 MWth Modular High
Temperature Gas-Cooled Reactor (MHTGR) was dominated by buoyancy force; whereas in the Naturzug
im Core mit Korrosion (NACOK) test facility molecular diffusion was the dominant mechanism due to
insufficient buoyancy force from the air in the lower plenum to overcome the hydrostatic head of the core.
Liu et al. [5] performed similar calculation for the HTR-PM and found that the flow of air into the pebble
bed core was dominated by molecular diffusion. These studies highlight that the design of the core plays an
important role in determining the dominant mechanism for air to flow from the lower plenum into the core
during an air ingress scenario. Finally, as air continues to enter the core, and the density difference between
the gas mixture in the core and the riser channels eventually grows enough to provide sufficient buoyancy
force to generate a stable natural circulation, a massive air-ingress is initiated.

 Figure 2.1: Air ingress scenario in HTGR.

 Once air enters the lower plenum and the core, oxidation occurs between the oxygen in the air and the
graphite pebbles and structures. Oxidation is essentially a two-step process that involves the diffusion of
oxidizing gas into the graphite and the chemical reactions between the gas and the graphite. Several possible
chemical reactions are listed as Eqs. 2-1 to 2-4.

                                               C + O2 → CO2                                              (2-1)

 The graphite oxidation rate is strongly temperature dependent [19]. Generally, the oxidation of graphite
can be divided into three temperature-dependent regimes: (1) the chemical regime for temperature below
500 ◦ C, (2) the in-pore diffusion controlled regime for temperature ranging from 500 - 900 ◦ C, and (3) the
boundary layer diffusion controlled regime for temperature higher than 900 ◦ C [21], as illustrated in Fig. 2.2.

 Figure 2.2: Temperature regimes of graphite oxidation.

 In the chemical regime, the chemical reaction rate between the graphite and reaction gas is relatively
low, which allows the oxidizing gas to penetrate deep into the graphite. Due to the uniform concentration of
reaction gas in the graphite, the oxidation rate tends to be relatively uniform across the depth of the graphite.
The oxidation rate in this regime is mainly controlled by the chemical reaction rate, and the oxidation rate is
shown to increase with temperature. In the in-pore diffusion controlled regime, the chemical reaction rate is
faster but the gas can still diffuse into the graphite. The reaction rate is determined by the diffusion rate of
the oxidizing gas into the graphite and the chemical reaction rate. This means that deeper in the graphite
the reaction rate is slower due to a lower concentration of oxidizing gas. In the boundary layer diffusion
controlled regime, the chemical reaction rate of graphite and oxidizing gas is fast, which hinders the diffusion
of oxidizing gas into the graphite. As a result, the reaction occurs only near the surface of the graphite with
almost no reaction deep inside the graphite. Given the fast chemical reaction rate, the oxidation rate in this
regime is mainly dictated by the rate of diffusion of oxidizing gas into the graphite. Factors affecting the

6

diffusion rate and chemical reaction can be categorized into two main groups, namely the internal factors of
graphite materials and the environmental factors during oxidation. The former comprises factors such as the
porosity of the graphite, the impurity content the graphite, and the grain size of the graphite; whereas the
latter comprises factors such as the reaction temperature, reaction gas flow rate, reaction gas concentration,
ambient pressure, and graphite irradiation [19].

 Past experimental works carried out to study graphite oxidation primarily focused on investigating the
mechanisms of graphite oxidation and the changes in graphite physicochemical properties after oxidation.
An extensive review of these studies was provided by Zhang et al. [19]. Additionally, other researchers
performed experiments to study the mechanisms of air ingress, including the reverse U-tube facility by
Hishida and Takeda [22], the NACOK facility [23], and the h-loop by Gould et al. [24]. These studies focused
on the formation of loop-wide natural circulation through molecular diffusion and/or buoyancy effects after
de-pressurization.

 Several computational studies have also been performed to model the air ingress phenomenon in HTGRs
using system-level and CFD software. One of the system-level codes used was GAMMA by Lim and No
[2] to model the High Temperature Engineering Test Reactor (HTTR)-simulated test facility, and by Kim
et al. [25] to model the MHTGR-600 reactor. The SPECTRA code was used by Zheng and Stempniewicz
[3] to model the NACOK facility. Another widely used code was TINTE, which was used by Zheng and
Stempniewicz [3] to model the NACOK facility and numerous researchers to model the HTR-PM reactor
[4–7]. RELAP5-3D was used by Davis et al. [26] to model the nitrogen/air ingress in the reverse U-tube and
NACOK facilities. The work by Davis et al. [26] focused solely on the flow of nitrogen/air into the facilities
without the consideration of oxidation, whereas, some forms of oxidation models were included in the other
studies. Kim et al. [25] considered only the C/O2 reaction in their GAMMA model. Similarly, Zheng
and Stempniewicz [3] included only the C/O2 reaction in their TINTE model, though, an additional C/CO2
reaction was considered in their SPECTRA model. The models by Lim and No [2], Liu et al. [5], and Xu et
al. [4, 6, 7] considered the C/O2 , C/CO2 , and CO/O2 reactions. In these studies, oxidation rate was modeled
using a series of correlations that were dependent on factors such as temperature, partial pressure, gas velocity,
and gas molar concentration. Furthermore, due to different concentration of oxygen and temperature, the
oxidation rate and the dominance of oxidation reactions were shown to vary spatially within the core.

 CFD analysis was performed by Oh et al. [8] to study the patterns of the stratified flow and counter-
current flow in the lower plenum after the core of the 600 MWth MHTGR reactor is de-pressurized. Liu et
al. [27] performed CFD analysis to investigate the influence of temperature and Reynolds number on the
graphite oxidation shape function, defined as the relation between the maximum and the average burn-off
of a pebble, under different pebble stacking configurations. Ferng and Chi [9] used CFD to investigate the
characteristics of graphite oxidation corrosion in the HTR-10 reactor. Franken et al. [28] constructed a CFD
model of the h-loop to study the transition of air ingress from molecular diffusion to natural circulation.

 The above discussion highlights that air ingress in an HTGR is a complex phenomenon that remains
an active area of research. The onset of natural circulation in the core is shown to be dependent on the
reactor design. On top of that, the oxidation of graphite after the ingress of air into the core adds further
complexity to the problem. Previous analysis tools have addressed important parts of this problem, but has
certain limitations. Legacy system analysis code such as RELAP5-3D have demonstrated capabilities for
molecular diffusion and natural circulation, but published applications have generally focused on selected
phenomena rather than the full accident sequence with graphite oxidation and long-term response. Codes

 7

such as GAMMA+, SPECTRA, and TINTE have been used to model reactor-scale air-ingress transients
and include varying oxidation treatment, but their applicability is often tied to specific reactor concepts,
legacy implementations, or limited extensibility to additional coupled physics and new designs. CFD studies
have provided valuable insight into local stratification, countercurrent flow, and lower-plenum mixing, but
are typically limited to shorter time scales, reduced domains, or simplified chemistry, and therefore are
not practical as the primary tool for routine full-plant multi-day safety analysis. Therefore, integrating
air-ingress capability in a modern system analysis code such as SAM is important for HTGR safety analysis
and licensing. Built on the MOOSE multiphysics framework, SAM can perform whole-plant air-ingress
analysis extensible to new reactor designs and can be coupled to other physics such as neutronics, structure
mechanics, fuel performance, and other models as needed. It is also developed under modern software quality
assurance practices with verification and validation, so that a single, well-supported tool can serve needs for
safety analysis and licensing for advanced HTGR designs. The next section describes the capabilities recently
developed in the SAM code to model the air-ingress event.

3 Developed capabilities for Air-ingress Modeling

 To model the full sequence of HTGR air-ingress accidents, three related capabilities were developed
in SAM: a multi-component gas mixture flow model with molecular diffusion, an isentropic critical flow
model for a small break discharge, and a graphite oxidation model with oxygen consumption and heat release.
Collectively, these models cover the principal phases of the accident sequence introduced in Section 2: the
critical flow model represents the initial choked depressurization discharge; the multi-component flow model
captures the gas mixing and the density-driven and diffusion-driven ingress, and later loop-wide natural
circulation; and the graphite oxidation model represents the oxygen consumption and exothermic heat release
that govern the later oxidation stage. Each capability is described in turn below.

3.1 Multi-component Flow Model

 As discussed before, when a rupture happens on the main coolant pipes on the body of the reactor
pressure vessel during a DLOFC accident in the HTGR, the air ingress scenario proceeds from diffusion to
natural circulation, which leads to considerable rate of air ingress to severely oxidize the fuel and core [29].
To capture the graphite oxidation damage to the fuel and core because of potential air ingress during DLOFC,
it is important to track the temporal and spatial distributions of different gas species. For this reason, a multi-

component 1 and 2, is implemented in SAM. Assuming mechanical and thermal equilibrium between the

user-defined oxygen partial pressure and can be integrated over the solid surface to estimate the total oxidation
in a graphite volume. The Roes correlation captures the combined dependence of oxidation rate on graphite
temperature, local oxygen availability, and gas velocity. It represents the two principal rate-limiting processes
of graphite oxidation. One is the chemical-kinetics rate that governs the reaction at lower temperatures, where
oxygen is abundant at the surface and the intrinsic reactivity of the graphite limits consumption. The other is
the mass-transfer-limited supply of oxygen across the boundary layer to the graphite surface; This process
depends on the gas velocity and dominates at higher temperatures, where the surface reaction is fast and the
overall rate is controlled by how quickly oxygen can be delivered.

 To fully capture the limiting effect of oxygen consumption on the progression of oxidation, the calculated
rate should impose a sink term on the oxygen component of the multi-component gas mixture and introduce
carbon dioxide in its place. However, this adds several complexities, including change in the diffusivity
and overall enthalpy of the mixture components. In the current scope, we focus on the impact of oxidation
once air ingression is well-established while still considering the role of oxygen consumption in limiting
the rate of oxidation. As an engineering simplification, we model the transport of oxygen as a passive scalar
concentration that advects with the mixture flow velocity with an additional sink term SR determined by the
local oxidation rate R and boundary conditions informed by the multicomponent gas mixture model.

4 Validation

4.1 NSTF Heavy-gas Ingress Event Validation

 The Natural Convection Shutdown Heat Removal Test Facility (NSTF) heavy-gas ingress experiment is
selected as a validation case for the SAM multi-component gas flow modeling capability. This experiment
measured the response of a buoyancy-driven loop when the inlet gas composition transitions from air to a
denser non-air gas. Under this transient, the system response is governed not only by the imposed heat load
and hydraulic resistance, but also by the spatial and temporal evolution of gas composition, the corresponding
changes in mixture density, and the heat up of the gas inventory within the heated channels. The experiment
therefore provides a valuable validation case of the developed SAM multi-component gas flow model for
air-ingress events analysis, especially for the system level behavior associated with composition-dependent
buoyancy effects.

4.1.1 NSTF Facility Description

 The NSTF is a large-scale integral effects experimental facility constructed at Argonne National
Laboratory to study passive air-cooled RCCS behavior under prototypic natural circulation conditions
[37]. The facility is designed at half-scale of the RCCS of the 350 MWth MHTGR [38] and preserves the
major flow and heat transfer features of an air-cooled cavity cooling system, including the inlet downcomer,
inlet plenum, heated cavity with riser channels, outlet plenum, and chimney section. The overall facility
height is nearly 26 m, providing a substantial elevation difference between the inlet and outlet regions and
allowing development of a measurable natural circulation driving head. The heated wall is driven by electric
heaters to simulate the RPV outer surface, and the facility is instrumented with high resolution sensors that
measure mass flow rate, temperature distributions, and gas composition. These features make the NSTF well
suited for validation of system analysis codes for passive decay heat removal applications.

 During normal operation, ambient air is drawn into the facility through the inlet region and flows
downward through the inlet downcomer. The gas then enters the inlet plenum, where it is distributed to
twelve parallel riser ducts. Heat is supplied to the riser section through the electrically heated wall, increasing
the gas temperature and reducing its density as it flows upward. The heated gas enters the outlet plenum and
discharges through two chimney ducts to the atmosphere. The density difference between the relatively cool
gas in the inlet downcomer and the hot gas in the riser and chimney sections produces the buoyancy head that
drives the air circulation. The NSTF facility is illustrated schematically in Fig. 4.1.

4.1.2 Heavy-gas Ingress Experiment

 The heavy-gas ingress test was designed to examine the effects of introducing a dense, non-air gas
into the inlet of an air-cooled RCCS flow path. This scenario is motivated by the potential for an external
heavy-gas released from nearby chemical processing facilities that enters the passive air-cooled RCCS of
HTGRs. Ingress of a heavy gas can modify the natural circulation driving head and thereby reduce the
effectiveness of passive heat removal. The NSTF experiment therefore provides valuable data for validating

 In the test, the facility was first operated at the normal air-cooled condition. After the transient begins,
the inlet gas is switched to argon. Argon was selected as the heavy gas for the experiment. Although carbon
dioxide is also relevant to industrial ingress scenarios, argon provides a clear density effect while avoiding
some of the additional exposure hazards associated with carbon dioxide. To perform the test, an argon

end of the duct that isolated the argon inventory from the NSTF loop. The enclosure was filled with argon

argon with an oxygen concentration below approximately 0.4%. At the beginning of the transient, bypass
vent valves were opened and the mating seal at the lower end of the air duct was broken. This transition
established a flow path that allowed argon from the enclosure to be drawn into the inlet downcomer.

 Oxygen sensors were installed in both the argon enclosure and the inlet plenum to track the local
evolution of gas composition during stagnation and recovery, while loop mass flow rate was measured
as the primary integral indicator of natural circulation performance. Together, the flow rate and oxygen
concentration measurements provide the principal validation targets for the SAM simulation. A companion
transient was also performed with air placed in the enclosure prior to transient initiation. This air transition
test confirmed that the mechanical actions associated with opening the enclosure path and breaking the mating
seal did not by themselves cause the observed loss of natural circulation; the strong response observed in the
argon test is therefore attributed to the gas composition change and associated density effect rather than to the
actuation procedure itself.

used in the NSTF experiment.

4.1.4 Results and Discussion

 Transient simulation is performed for the full NSTF heavy-gas ingress test. A null transient is first run
to establish the nominal air-cooled steady-state operating conditions. The transient begins at 28.65 hr and is
initialized from this converged steady state by switching the form loss coefficients of the air duct and argon
enclosure paths, as described in the preceding section. The measured response and the corresponding SAM
prediction are compared below in three phases that follow the evolution of the transient: rapid argon entry
into the inlet region, degradation of natural circulation toward near stagnation, and heat up and recovery.

 In the experiment, the inlet plenum oxygen concentration decreased sharply from the normal air value
immediately after the transition, dropping to approximately 11% within about 2 s and to approximately 1.8%
within about 6 s. This rapid decrease indicates that the inlet plenum became argon-rich very quickly after
the enclosure was opened. The SAM calculation reproduces this rapid depletion: the predicted inferred
oxygen concentration in the inlet plenum, shown in Fig. 4.5, drops sharply after the argon path is opened
and then rises to an intermediate value of approximately 10% as a mixture of air and argon flows from the
enclosure into the inlet plenum. Because the enclosure and inlet plenum are modeled as zero-dimensional
well-mixed volumes, SAM predicts an average composition rather than the local measured value. Short
period oscillations are also observed in the predicted mass flow rate immediately after the transition (see
Fig. 4.6). The short initial spike and oscillations in the predicted mass flow rate are considered to be primarily
physical, arising from the abrupt reconfiguration of the inlet flow path when the enclosure path is suddenly
opened with higher buoyancy force. The spike and oscillations dissipate within a few seconds and do not
affect the predicted long-term flow degradation or recovery behavior, so they have no impact on the validation
conclusions.

 After argon entered the downcomer and inlet plenum, the measured loop flow rate decreased rapidly
and approached an essentially stagnant condition within approximately 90 s. The system mass flow rates

4.2 NACOK Validation

4.2.1 NACOK Facility Description

 The NACOK (Naturzug im Core mit Korrosion, “natural convection in the core with corrosion”)
facility was constructed at Jülich Research Centre in Germany to investigate phenomena associated with

 29

air-ingress accidents in HTGRs [42–44]. The test facility can carry out experiments in different configurations
and different graphite materials, under different temperature profiles and varying boundary conditions. A
schematic overview of the facility geometry is shown in Fig. 4.8. The NACOK configuration represents a
reverse U-tube flow path that reproduces the essential circulation route after rupture of the coaxial duct in
the HTR primary system [42]. Ambient gas enters through a horizontal lower inlet, flows upward through a
tall heated experimental channel containing ceramic or graphite internals, passes through a horizontal cross
connection near the top, and then descends through a vertical return tube representing a cold-helium riser
channel in the reactor side reflector. The inlet and outlet are located at the same geodetic elevation, thereby
mimicking double ended guillotine break (DEGB) accident flow path. The NACOK facility can also be
reconfigured into an open-chimney arrangement by disconnecting the return leg and venting the test channel
directly to the atmosphere. This chimney configuration has been used for air ingress experiment carried out
on October 23, 2008 [44].

 The main experimental channel has a square cross section of 0.30 m × 0.30 m and is assembled from
modular welded sections of approximately 800 mm height fabricated from high-temperature austenitic furnace
steel, with a wall thickness of 6 mm [42]. In the reverse U-tube configuration, the maximum height difference
is approximately 7.334 m, including a packed-spheres section of about 5.0 m and an upper freeboard region
representing the upper plenum. In the open-chimney configuration, the total effective height can reach about
8.0 m. The return tube has an inner diameter of 0.125 m, equal to that of the horizontal inlet tube, and the
upper horizontal connection between the experimental channel and return leg is approximately 1.6 m long
center-to-center.

 Independent electrical furnace sections are installed around both the main experimental channel and the
return tube, allowing prescribed axial wall-temperature distributions to be imposed [42]. The installed power

30

is 12.0 kW per heated section of the main channel and 3.0 kW per heated section of the return tube, for a
total installed heater capacity of about 147 kW. This arrangement permits wall temperatures up to roughly

 NACOK is heavily instrumented and has been used as a benchmark facility for validation of reactor

 Because NACOK provides simultaneous measurements of flow rate, pressure loss, temperature, and

RELAP5-3D, ATTICA3D, and related models [44–46]. It therefore serves as an important validation for the
SAM graphite oxidation capability developed in this work.

4.2.3 SAM Model Description

 The SAM model of the NACOK graphite oxidation experiment is based on the chimney test configuration
on October 23, 2008 described in Section 4.2.2. Because the bulk gas during the test is primarily air, and the
main quantity of interest is the graphite oxidation rate, the present model employs the SAM passive scalar
transport capability with air as the fluid rather than the full multi-component gas formulation described in
Section 3.1. Oxygen is treated as a passive scalar advected with the bulk flow and consumed locally in the
graphite block channels through a sink term provided by the graphite oxidation model. The associated heat
release is imposed as a heat source in the graphite heat structures. A schematic of the SAM model is shown
in Fig. 4.10.

 The flow path is constructed using one-dimensional and zero-dimensional SAM components. A short
horizontal inlet pipe, modeled as a PBOneDFluidComponent, supplies air to the inlet plenum. The inlet pipe
hydraulic diameter is 0.125 m, consistent with the experimental inlet tube. The inlet plenum is represented by
a zero-dimensional PBVolumeBranch with a flow area of 0.09 m2 , corresponding to the cross section of the
0.30 m × 0.30 m experimental channel. This plenum is located immediately below the heated graphite-block
stack and distributes the incoming air to the parallel graphite-block channels. The three axial graphite-block
levels are modeled explicitly. As in the SPECTRA NACOK model of Ref. [44], the differences in thermal
and oxidation properties between the UCAR PCEA and SGL NBG-17 graphite grades are considered to be
negligible, and the two graphite types are therefore not distinguished in the SAM model. At each level, the
flow region is accordingly divided into two parallel one-dimensional channels. One channel represents the
Type 1 block geometry, which consists of 14 holes with a diameter of 16 mm, and the other represents the
Type 2 geometry, which consists of two holes with a diameter of 26.5 mm. Adjacent block levels within
the same channel are connected by PBVolumeBranch components that represent the small inter-block gas
volumes. Above the third graphite-block level, a PBVolumeBranch with a flow area of 0.09 m2 merges the
two parallel channel systems. The upper chimney section is represented by a 0.725 m long one-dimensional
pipe, followed by the 0.30 m high ceramic sphere packed bed and a 6.075 m long exhaust pipe. The ceramic
sphere bed is modeled as a pipe with the SAM built-in KTA pebble bed pressure drop closure correlation. The
chimney discharges to a PBTDV boundary condition maintained at atmospheric pressure.

 The solid graphite block is represented using two-dimensional cylindrical PBCoupledHeatStructure
components coupled to the corresponding one-dimensional graphite-block flow channels. Because each
equivalent one-dimensional channel represents the combined flow area of multiple holes in the physical
graphite blocks, the actual multi-hole geometry is collapsed into an equivalent cylindrical conduction problem.
To preserve the effective lateral heat-transfer behavior between the graphite solid and the gas channels, an
effective thermal conductivity is assigned to the graphite material. Each graphite heat structure is surrounded
by a cylindrical steel wall heat structure representing the NACOK experimental channel wall. The graphite
and steel wall heat structures are coupled across the annular gap through RadiationHeatTransfer. Axial
radiative exchange between adjacent graphite-block levels is also represented by SurfaceCoupling objects
connecting the top and bottom surfaces of successive graphite heat structures. This treatment enables the
model to capture both radial heat transfer from the wall to the graphite and axial radiation between neighboring
block levels.

 The integral graphite mass loss provides the primary validation metric for the NACOK test. The
total graphite mass decreased from 129.538 kg before the experiment to 118.794 kg after the experiment,

nonuniform in the axial direction. On average, the bottom level lost about 19% of its mass on average,
whereas the middle and top levels lost roughly 4% and 2%, respectively. This distribution indicates that

geometry also affected the mass loss. At the bottom level, the Type 1 blocks oxidized more rapidly than the
Type 2 blocks. The influence of graphite grade was smaller than the influence of geometry, SGL NBG-17
showed slightly greater oxidation than UCAR PCEA [44].

5 Reactor-scale Demonstration of Air-Ingress Events

 The air-ingress modeling capabilities developed in SAM are demonstrated together in an integrated,
reactor-scale simulation. This demonstration problem is a de-pressurized loss-of-forced-cooling (DLOFC)
air-ingress transient in a simplified model of the HTR-PM reactor. The objective is not to reproduce the full
plant in detail, but to demonstrate that the new SAM capabilities can capture the sequence of depressurization,
diffusive air ingress, buoyancy-driven flow, oxygen transport, graphite oxidation, oxidation heat release, and
passive heat removal over a long transient. This section therefore summarizes the HTR-PM reactor and the
air-ingress accident scenario, describes the simplified SAM model used for the demonstration, and discusses
the resulting transient behavior.

5.1 The HTR-PM Reactor

 The High-Temperature Reactor Pebble-bed Module (HTR-PM) is a modular pebble-bed HTGR demon-
stration plant built at Shidao Bay in Shandong Province, China, and is the only commercial-scale modular
HTGR currently in operation [48]. Its design based on the German pebble-bed reactor program from the
1960s to 1990s and on the Chinese HTR-10 test reactor [49] during the 2000s; the overall plant layout is
shown in Fig. 5.1. The HTR-PM has a core thermal power of 250 MWth . It uses helium coolant at 7 MPa,
heated from a core inlet temperature of 250 ◦ C to an outlet temperature of 750 ◦ C at a primary mass flow rate
of about 96 kg/s. The cylindrical pebble-bed core is 3 m in diameter with an equivalent active height of about
11 m. The reactor core contains roughly 420,000 pebbles, each 6 cm in diameter. The pebble bed core is
surrounded by radial reflector, top cavity, lower, and upper reflectors. The radial reflector includes various
orifices for the control rod channels, Kleine Absorber Kugel Systeme (KLAK) channels (shutdown system),
and fluid riser channels, surrounded by a steel core barrel and a steel reactor pressure vessel (RPV).

 During normal operation, helium enters the core through the outer channel of the coaxial duct and flows
upward through the riser channels in the side reflector into the top plenum. It then passes downward through
the pebble bed, and enters the bottom plenum before leaving the core through the inner channel of the coaxial
duct to the steam generator. After being cooled in the steam generator, the cold helium flows upward along
the steam generator vessel and returns to the blower, completing the primary circuit (Fig. 5.1) [49].

 HTR-PM is designed for inherent safety: the small module power, the low power density, the large
graphite heat capacity, and decay heat is removed passively by conduction and thermal radiation from the
core, through the reflector, core barrel, and RPV, to a reactor cavity cooling system (RCCS). The RCCS is a
passive system of water-cooling panels — vertical cooling tubes welded to a cylindrical plate — mounted on
the reactor-cavity wall; during accidents, heat is transferred from the RPV surface to the panels by thermal
radiation and natural convection in the cavity, and the heated water rises to air–water heat exchangers in an
air-cooling tower that ultimately rejects it to the atmosphere.

Event sequence. The imposed transient proceeds through the following phases:

 1. Depressurization. The primary system blows down from 7 MPa to atmospheric pressure over about
 20 s as the break opens. It is assumed that the reactor scrams immediately after the break and the
 fission power drops onto the HTR-PM decay-heat curve (to about 6% of nominal at scram). The gas
 surrounding the break is set to containment air at ambient temperature (≈20 ◦ C).

 2. Density-driven and diffusion-driven air ingress. After depressurization, buoyancy-driven mixing
 and stratification leading to gradual air enters in the lower plenum. Then air advances into the core
 slowly by molecular diffusion.

 3. Onset of natural circulation and graphite oxidation. As air accumulates in the core, the buoyancy
 overcomes the loop resistance and the loop-wide natural circulation begins, which thereafter continu-
 ously draws fresh air and oxygen up through the hot core. The oxygen delivered to the hot pebbles is
 consumed by graphite oxidation, which depletes the oxygen and releases heat at the pebble surface.

 4. Long-term passive cool down. As the decay heat continues to decrease, heat transfer from the RPV to
 the RCCS eventually exceeds the heat generation, causing the core temperature to decline and leaving
 the reactor in a passively cooled state.

5.3 SAM Model

 For the present capability demonstration, the HTR-PM reactor is represented by a simplified SAM
model, shown in Fig. 5.2. The detailed primary loop and RCCS are not included. The pebble bed core
is modeled as a single equivalent channel that preserves the principal HTR-PM parameters: the 3 m core
diameter, the 6 cm pebble diameter, the 0.39 bed porosity, the 7 MPa helium pressure, the 250 ◦ C inlet
temperature, and the 250 MW reactor power. The corresponding flow area and hydraulic diameter follow
from these values. The radial heat-conduction path of the reactor is represented explicitly by concentric
cylindrical solid structures: an inner graphite reflector, a riser gap, an outer graphite reflector, a core barrel,
and the RPV, which reproduces the conduction path of the real reactor.

 The SAM model includes several simplifications relative to the detailed reactor design. First, the core is
a single equivalent one-dimensional channel, so the radial power and temperature distribution within the bed
is not resolved. Second, the full primary loop is not modeled. The steam generator, blower, and the remainder
of the primary circuit are omitted. The core flow path is bounded only by prescribed pressure boundary
conditions applied at its inlet and outlet pipes, which impose the depressurization transient and supply the
ambient air. Third, to model the buoyancy-driven mixing and stratification after depressurization, the lower
plenum is connected to another volume branch, outlet branch, which connected two horizontal outlet pipes
with slightly different elevations. The density difference between the outside air and the light helium drives
air enters the outlet branch through the lower outlet pipe and helium leaves through the upper outlet pipe.
Last, the RCCS is not modeled explicitly; instead, the RPV outer surface radiates directly to a 333 K ambient.

 This simplified reactor representation is built from the SAM one-dimensional and zero-dimensioanl
components with multi-component gas model. The core is represented with a PBCoreChannel component
with sphere pebble fuel, and KTA pebble bed heat transfer and friction closures. The riser, the outlet
pipes, and the inlet piping are PBOneDFluidComponents; the plena and flow junctions are zero-dimensional
PBVolumeBranch components; and the loop terminates at given pressure boundary conditions with PBTDV
components. Reactor decay heat is supplied through a ReactorPower component using the HTR-PM
decay-heat curve.

 All flow components use the multi-component gas mixture equation of state for a mixture of helium
and air, with a diffusivity of 7.92 × 10−4 m2 /s [20]. Oxygen is treated as a passive scalar advected with the
bulk flow and consumed locally in the core through a sink term provided by the graphite oxidation model.
The graphite oxidation rate is evaluated with the Roes model of Section 3.3 as a function of the local oxygen
partial pressure, gas velocity, and pebble surface temperature; it supplies both the oxygen sink term and the
chemical heat release. In this demonstration the oxidation heat is deposited onto the solid pebble surface
as a surface heat flux. At the high pebble temperature reached during the transient, oxidation is primarily
in boundary layer controller regime, according to the oxidation theory in Section 2. So the reaction occurs
near the surface of the pebble. The radial solid structures are PBCoupledHeatStructure components (the
inner and outer graphite reflectors, the riser gap, core barrel, and RPV). A radiative boundary condition
(CoupledRadiationHeatTransferBC) is applied on the external surface of the RPV outer wall.

then held constant, so that the slow diffusive ingress, the onset of natural circulation, the graphite oxidation,
and the passive cool down all develop as part of the solution rather than being imposed.

5.4 Results

 As described before, the air ingress event begins with the depressurization process. Figure 5.3 shows the
boundary pressure during the depressurization: from the 7 MPa steady state value, the pressure decreases to
atmospheric over the 100–120 s window as the break happens, with the inlet and outlet boundaries blowing
down together. This depressurization causes rapid loss of the helium inventory and sets the stage for air
ingress.

progressed far enough that the buoyancy force can overcome the flow resistance, the natural circulation

6 Conclusions

 This report describes the development, validation, and demonstration of new SAM capabilities for
modeling air-ingress phenomena in HTGRs, a recognized multi-physics challenge for which the community
has lacked a validated, integrated system-level tool. To represent the helium–air gas behavior during air
ingress, a multi-component gas mixture model with diffusion was implemented in SAM. A 0-D graphite
oxidation model based on the Roes correlation was developed, including oxygen consumption and oxidation
heat release. A critical flow model was also implemented to improve the representation of primary system
depressurization following a break. These capabilities were validated against the NSTF heavy-gas ingress
experiment and the NACOK air-ingress facility. The NSTF heavy-gas ingress experiment exercised the
SAM multi-component flow model in a buoyancy-driven cooling loop whose inlet composition transitions
from air to a denser gas, argon; SAM captured the composition-dependent density changes and the resulting
system-level flow response. The NACOK experiment provided the validation of the SAM graphite oxidation
models against a measured total graphite mass loss. SAM reproduced the axial nonuniformity and the
block geometry dependence of the oxidation, predicting a bottom-level weight loss of about 25% against
the measured ∼24% and matching the measured bottom level temperatures. Overall, SAM reproduced the
principal experimental trends and the key transient behaviors relevant to air-ingress analysis. The SAM
graphite oxidation prediction is comparable to those from the SPECTRA (Roes correlation) and TINTE
analyses of the same benchmark, supporting the use of SAM for system-level HTGR air-ingress simulations
with coupled graphite oxidation.

 The developed models were then applied in an integrated, reactor-scale demonstration of a depressurized
loss-of-forced-cooling (DLOFC) air-ingress transient in a simplified HTR-PM pebble-bed reactor. In a
single simulation spanning approximately 8 days, SAM reproduced the expected accident progression: rapid
depressurization from 7 MPa to atmospheric in 20 s, density-driven flow in the lower regions, diffusion-
dominated air ingress into a stalled stratified loop over approximately 15 hours, onset of buoyancy-driven
natural circulation, exothermic graphite oxidation reaching a peak fuel temperature at roughly 62 hours, and
eventual passive cooldown as vessel heat rejection overtakes the decay and oxidation heat sources. These
results demonstrate that the newly implemented models in SAM can reproduce the complete sequence of
HTGR air-ingress events in a single integrated calculation.

 Future work will focus on several extensions and improvements to the capabilities developed in this
work. The current gas mixture model is limited to two components and will be extended to additional
components, together with further improvements in numerical robustness and continued verification and
validation. The graphite oxidation model currently represents only direct oxidation by oxygen and does not
yet include the C + CO2 → 2CO, reaction or its thermal feedback. Finally, the present HTR-PM analysis
is intended as a capability demonstration rather than a detailed reactor analysis; future applications will
incorporate a multi-channel core representation, more realistic break and flow-path geometries, and detailed
modeling of the RCCS. Together, these improvements will progressively close the remaining gaps between
the current capability demonstration and a full-fidelity, licensing-quality air-ingress analysis of the HTGRs.
The present work represents a significant step toward that objective, establishing SAM as a validated, modern
system-level tool for HTGR air-ingress safety analysis in support of the NEAMS program’s mission.
