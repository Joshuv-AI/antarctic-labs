Symbolic computation in cubic Jordan matrix algebras and in related structures
Torben Wiedemann

Abstract
. We present CubicJordanMatrixAlg , a GAP package for symbolic computation in cubic Jordan matrix algebras and in related Lie-theoretic structures. As an application, we use it to compute certain (commutator) relations in F 4 F_{4} -graded groups that were constructed by De Medts and the author from cubic Jordan matrix algebras.

1.
Introduction

Probably the most well-known example of a cubic Jordan matrix algebra is

(1)

\Her_{3}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{R})\coloneq\left\{\,A\in\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathbb{R}&\mathbb{C}&\mathbb{C}\\
\mathbb{C}&\mathbb{R}&\mathbb{C}\\

with the structure of a cubic Jordan algebra (which by
[
4

“the same”
as a cubic norm structure). A similar construction is possible in much higher generality by replacing

with a commutative ring

\mathbb{C}
with a composition algebra
1

The purpose of the GAP

1.1 Convention.

1.2 Definition (

A

1.3 Convention.

For the rest of this article, we will simply write

1.4 Example.

Composition algebras over commutative rings (such as the classical examples of complex numbers, quaternions and octonions over the real numbers) are conic algebras
[
4
, 19.13]
. For any commutative ring

, any
k
-algebra that is projective of rank

is a conic algebra
[

, 16.4]
.

In addition to the intricate identities that are satisfied in alternative rings, any conic algebra

satisfies identities involving its trace, norm and conjugation, such as the following.

1.5 Identities.

For all
a,b,c
in a conic algebra
C
over a commutative ring

, the following hold:

(1)

\overline{a+b}=\overline{a}+\overline{b}
,
\overline{ab}=\overline{b}\overline{a}
,
\overline{\overline{a}}=a
and
\overline{ta}=t\overline{a}

[

(2)

\contr(ab)=\contr(ba)

\contr\big((ab)c\big)=\contr\big(a(bc)\big)
,
\contr(\overline{a})=\contr(a)
.
[
4
, (16.5.7), 16.13, 17.12 (a)]

(3)

n(\overline{a})=n(a)

,

.
[
4
, (16.5.2), (16.5.7), (16.12.3), 17.12 (a)]

(4)

\contr(a)1_{C}=a+\overline{a}

[
4

(5)

(6)

\contr(a^{2})=\contr(a)^{2}-2n(a)

[

Note that some of these are equations in

1.6 Goal.

As for alternative rings, we would like to decide whether a given expression is an identity in conic algebras—that is, if it is valid in every conic algebra (or in the ground ring of any conic algebra). The purpose of
CubicJordanMatrixAlg
is more humble: Given an expression
f
for which we would like to decide whether it is an identity in conic algebras,
CubicJordanMatrixAlg
can compute a
“simplified”

such that

is an identity in conic algebras if and only if
\tilde{f}
is. In many examples that arise in applications, we have
\tilde{f}=0
, in which case
CubicJordanMatrixAlg
has proven that
f
is an identity. In other cases, it remains to prove by hand that
\tilde{f}
is an identity, but this is usually much easier than for
f
. However,
CubicJordanMatrixAlg
cannot prove that an expression is
not
an identity.

3.
Cubic Jordan matrix algebras

3.1 Notation.

For the rest of this article, we denote by
k
a commutative ring and by
(C,n)
a conic algebra with trace
\contr
and conjugation
\overline{\>\cdot\>}
. We also fix a triple
\Gamma=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3})\in k^{3}
of structure constants and we denote by
\mathcal{P}\coloneq\{\,(1,2,3),(2,3,1),(3,1,2)\,\}
the set of cyclic permutations of
(1,2,3)
. For simplicity, we assume that
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}
are invertible, though some of our statements remain valid without this assumption.

In this section, we briefly define the notion of cubic Jordan matrix algebra as in
[
4
, 36.4]
. They provide examples of cubic Jordan algebras. The precise axioms of the latter are irrelevant for our purposes, but can be found in
[
4
, 33.1, 33.4]
and
[
2
, 2.4]
(for cubic norm structures, which are
“equivalent to”
cubic Jordan algebras by
[
4
, 34.6]
) and
[
4
, 29.1]
(for general Jordan algebras). We will also show how to perform symbolic computations in cubic Jordan matrix algebras with
CubicJordanMatrixAlg
.

3.2 Remark.

Denote by
e_{ij}
the
(3\times 3)
-matrix with
1
at position
(i,j)
and
0
everywhere else. Put
e_{i}\coloneq e_{ii}
and
u[jl]\coloneq\gamma_{l}ue_{jl}+\gamma_{j}\overline{u}e_{lj}
for all
u\in C
,
(i,j,l)\in\mathcal{P}
. If the diagonal matrix
\Gamma=\operatorname{diag}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3})
is invertible and
C
is a faithful
k
-algebra (so that we may identify
k
with
k1_{C}\subseteq C
), then we may define
\Her_{3}(C,k,\Gamma)
as the
k
-module of
(3\times 3)
-matrices over
C
that are fixed under (the involution)
A\mapsto\Gamma^{-1}\overline{A}^{T}\Gamma
and whose diagonal elements lie in
k1_{C}

[
4
, 36.2]
. Any such matrix may be written in a unique way as
\xi_{1}e_{1}+\xi_{2}e_{2}+\xi_{3}e_{3}+u_{1}[23]+u_{2}[31]+u_{3}[12]
for
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3}\in k
,
u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\in C
. This observation motivates the following more general definition of
\Her_{3}(C,k,\Gamma)
, which is equivalent to the previous one if
\Gamma
is invertible and
C
is faithful.

3.3 Definition.

As a
k
-module, the
cubic Jordan matrix algebra over
C
with respect to
\Gamma
is
\Her_{3}(C,k,\Gamma)\coloneq k^{3}\oplus C^{3}
. We will write an element
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3},u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
of
\Her_{3}(C,\Gamma)
(with
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3}\in k
and
u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\in C
) as a a formal expression

\xi_{1}e_{1}+\xi_{2}e_{2}+\xi_{3}e_{3}+u_{1}[23]+u_{2}[31]+u_{3}[12]=\sum_{(i,j,l)\in\mathcal{P}}\big(\xi_{i}e_{i}+u_{i}[jl]\big).

We also put
a[ji]\coloneq\overline{a}[ij]
for
a\in C
and
(i,j,l)\in\mathcal{P}
as well as
t[ii]\coloneq te_{i}
for
t\in k
and
i\in\{\,1,2,3\,\}
. If
\Gamma=(1,1,1)
, we simply write
\Her_{3}(C,k)
for
\Her_{3}(C,k,\Gamma)
, as in
section

1
. Further, we will usually write
\Her_{3}(C,\Gamma)
for
\Her_{3}(C,k,\Gamma)
, leaving the base ring
k
implicit.

In the following, we put
J\coloneq\Her_{3}(C,\Gamma)
. As a
k
-module,
J
does not depend on
\Gamma
, but we define maps

\displaystyle N\colon J\rightarrow k,\quad\sharp\colon J\rightarrow J,\quad T\colon J\times J\rightarrow k,\quad\times\colon J\times J\rightarrow J

that do depend on
\Gamma
and which
turn
J
into a cubic norm structure. They are given by the following explicit formulas in
[
4
, 36.4]
:

\displaystyle N(x)

\displaystyle\coloneq\xi_{1}\xi_{2}\xi_{3}+\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}\gamma_{3}\contr(u_{1}u_{2}u_{3})-\sum_{(i,j,l)\in\mathcal{P}}\gamma_{j}\gamma_{l}t_{i}n(u_{i}),

\displaystyle x^{\sharp}

\displaystyle\coloneq\sum_{(i,j,l)\in\mathcal{P}}\Big(\big(\xi_{j}\xi_{l}-\gamma_{j}\gamma_{l}n(u_{i})\big)e_{i}+(-\xi_{i}u_{i}+\gamma_{i}\overline{(u_{j}u_{l})})[jl]\big),

\displaystyle x\times y

\displaystyle\coloneq\sum_{(i,j,l)\in\mathcal{P}}\Bigl(\big(\xi_{j}\eta_{l}+\eta_{j}\xi_{l}-\gamma_{j}\gamma_{l}n(u_{i},v_{i})\big)e_{i}+\big(-\xi_{i}v_{i}-\eta_{i}u_{i}+\gamma_{i}\overline{(u_{j}v_{l}+v_{j}u_{l})}\big)[jl]\Bigr),

\displaystyle T(x,y)

\displaystyle\coloneq\sum_{(i,j,l)\in\mathcal{P}}\big(\xi_{i}\eta_{i}+\gamma_{j}\gamma_{l}n(u_{i},v_{i})\big)

for all
x=\sum_{(i,j,l)\in\mathcal{P}}(\xi_{i}e_{i}+u_{i}[jl])\in J,y=\sum_{(i,j,l)\in\mathcal{P}}(\eta_{i}e_{i}+v_{i}[jl])\in J
. The maps
N
,
\sharp
,
T
are called the norm, adjoint and (bilinear) trace of
J
, respectively. In the case
k=\mathbb{R}
,
C=\mathbb{C}
, and
\Gamma=(1,1,1)
, they correspond precisely to the determinant, the adjugate map and the Killing form
(x,y)\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}(xy)
where
\operatorname{Tr}
denotes the usual matrix trace.

By
[
4
, 33.9]
, the map

\displaystyle U\colon J\times J\rightarrow J

\displaystyle,(x,y)\mapsto U_{x}y\coloneq T(x,y)x-x^{\sharp}\times y

gives
J
the structure of a Jordan algebra
(in the sense of
[
4
, 29.1]
). Its linearisation is

\displaystyle\{\>\>\cdot\>,\>\cdot\>,\>\cdot\>\>\}\colon J\times J\times J\rightarrow J

\displaystyle,(x,y,z)\mapsto U_{x+z}y-U_{x}y-U_{z}y=T(x,y)z+T(y,z)x-(z\times x)\times y.

We will also write
D_{x,y}(z)\coloneq\{\>x,y,z\>\}
for
x,y,z\in J
.

3.4 Example (Peirce decomposition).

For all
(i,j,l)\in\mathcal{P}
, we denote by
J_{ii}\coloneq ke_{i}
and
J_{ij}\coloneq J_{ji}\coloneq C[ij]
the components of

(3)

\{\>J_{ij},J_{jl},J_{lm}\>\}\subseteq J_{im}\qquad\text{for all }i,j,l,m\in\{\,1,2,3\,\}.

Using that

for all

\{\>J_{12},J_{12},J_{32}\>\}\subseteq J_{23}

Further, one can show that
\{\>J_{ij},J_{lm},J_{pq}\>\}=\{0\}

in a way which allows us to apply (

Finally, we connect the root groups
(U_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in F_{4}}
to the theory of root graded groups.

4.9 Root graded groups.

Let
\Phi
be a finite root system. A
\Phi
-graded group
is a group
G
together with a family
(V_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\Phi}
of subgroups of
G
satisfying certain axioms, most importantly:

(1)

[V_{\alpha},V_{\beta}]\subseteq\langle V_{\gamma}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\gamma\in\Phi,\gamma=i\alpha+j\beta\text{ for some }i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}\rangle
for all non-proportional
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
.

(2)

For each
\alpha\in\Phi
, there exists an
\alpha
-Weyl element
: An element
w_{\alpha}\in V_{-\alpha}V_{\alpha}V_{-\alpha}
such that
V_{\beta}^{w_{\alpha}}=V_{\beta^{\sigma(\alpha)}}
for all
\beta\in\Phi
.

For a precise definition of root graded groups, which generalise Jacques Tits’ notion of RGD-systems, see
[
12
, 2.5.2]
. One of the main results of
[
12
]
is that for any
F_{4}
-graded group
(G,(V_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in F_{4}})
, there exist a commutative ring
k^{\prime}
and a conic
k^{\prime}
-algebra
C^{\prime}
which
coordinatise

G
(see
[
12
, 10.7.8]
). This means that there exist an isomorphism
\theta_{\alpha}\colon k^{\prime}\rightarrow V_{\alpha}
for each long
\alpha\in F_{4}
and an isomorphism
\theta_{\beta}\colon C^{\prime}\rightarrow V_{\beta}
for each short
\beta\in F_{4}
such that certain explicit commutator relations involving the structural maps of
C
are satisfied. The commutator relations that we have verified in
4.8
are examples of the required relations.

4.10 The existence problem for
F_{4}
-graded groups.

The coordinatisation result for
F_{4}
-graded groups in
4.9
gives rise to the following existence question: Given
k
and
C
, does there exist an
F_{4}
-graded group that is coordinatised by
C
? This question was the initial motivation for the work in
[
2
]
. It is shown in
[
2
, 11.15]
that the group
G
generated by the root groups
(U_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in F_{4}}
from
4.7
is indeed
F_{4}
-graded and coordinatised by
C
, so the question has a positive answer. We have already seen in
4.8
how to verify some commutator relations in
G
, and the remaining commutator relations can be verified with
CubicJordanMatrixAlg
in a similar way.

The biggest computational effort, however, lies in the verification of Axiom
4.9

2
. Let
\Delta
be the root base of
F_{4}
from (
4
). By
[
12
, 2.2.7]
, it suffices to show that there exists a
\delta
-Weyl element for each
\delta\in\Delta
. Let
\delta\in\Delta
be arbitrary and put
w_{\delta}\coloneq\theta_{-\delta}(-1)\theta_{\delta}(1)\theta_{-\delta}(-1)\in G
. We show that
w_{\delta}
is a
\delta
-Weyl element by verifying with
CubicJordanMatrixAlg
(and a few final steps by hand) that

\displaystyle\theta_{\alpha}(a)^{w_{\delta}}\in\{\,\theta_{\alpha^{\sigma(\delta)}}(a),\theta_{\alpha^{\sigma(\delta)}}(-a),\theta_{\alpha^{\sigma(\delta)}}(\overline{a}),\theta_{\alpha^{\sigma(\delta)}}(-\overline{a})\,\}\quad\text{and}\quad\theta_{\beta}(t)^{w_{\delta}}\in\{\,\theta_{\beta^{\sigma(\delta)}}(t),\theta_{\beta^{\sigma(\delta)}}(-t)\,\}

for all short
\alpha\in F_{4}
, long
\beta\in F_{4}
and all
t\in k
,
a\in C
. This requires up to four equality tests of automorphisms (as in
4.8
) for each short
\alpha\in F_{4}
and up to two such tests for each long
\beta\in F_{4}
. In total, we have to perform
\lvert F_{4}\rvert\cdot\lvert\Delta\rvert=48\cdot 4
such computations to show that
U_{\gamma}^{w_{\delta}}=U_{\gamma^{\sigma(\delta)}}
for all
\gamma\in F_{4}
,
\delta\in\Delta
.

6.
Comparison to the literature

We are not aware of any literature or software that discusses symbolic computation in conic algebras.
However, there has been some work on the related problem of symbolic computation in alternative algebras.
Recall from
section

1
the problem of deciding whether an element of a free nonassociative
k
-algebra is an identity in alternative
k
-algebras.
The computer algebra system
Albert

[
5
,
8
,
7
,
6
]
solves this problem (and similar problems for arbitrary classes of nonassociative algebras defined by certain identities) under the restriction that the ground ring
k
is a field of characteristic not
2
. The assumption that
2
is invertible in
k
is necessary because Albert works with the linearised alternative laws

(6)

(aa^{\prime})b+(a^{\prime}a)b=a(a^{\prime}b)+a^{\prime}(ab)\quad\text{and}\quad(ab)b^{\prime}+(ab^{\prime})b=a(bb^{\prime})+a(b^{\prime}b)\quad\text{for all }a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime}\in A,

which are equivalent to the usual alternative laws (
2
) under this assumption. The assumption that
k
is a field is necessary because Albert works by solving linear equation systems. Further, for implementation reasons, Albert only supports prime fields
k
of order at most
251
.

A different approach to this decision problem, which uses term rewriting, is taken in
[
11
]
. It works over
k=\mathbb{Z}
, but is only guaranteed to be complete if all
“monomials”
in the identity
f
that we want to verify have total degree at most
4
. Thus if
f
is an identity that contains a higher-degree monomial, then this approach might fail to detect that
f
is an identity. However, we are not aware of any publicly available software implementation of the ideas in
[
11
]
.
