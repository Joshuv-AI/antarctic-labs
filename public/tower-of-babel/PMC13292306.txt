Same war, different homes: mental health outcomes of displaced versus non-displaced adolescents during war
Authors: Or Segal, Hadas Hevron, Noam Weinbach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Adolescents are especially vulnerable to the psychological consequences of war, and displacement may add an additional burden beyond conflict exposure. However, the specific effects of war-related internal displacement on adolescent mental health remain understudied. This study is the first to compare mental health outcomes between displaced and non-displaced adolescents during an active war.Method: The study included 200 Israeli adolescents (aged 14–18) who were assessed 4–8 months after the outbreak of the Israel–Hamas war. Half were internally displaced due to heightened risk in their residential region. Mental health outcomes included emotional distress (depression, anxiety, stress), emotion regulation difficulties, and intolerance of uncertainty.Results: The groups were demographically comparable. Displaced adolescents reported greater emotional distress, driven primarily by higher levels of stress and depression. They also experienced greater emotion regulation difficulties and higher intolerance of uncertainty compared to their non-displaced peers, which also mediated the association between displacement and distress.Conclusions: Internal displacement during war is associated with increased emotional distress among adolescents beyond war exposure, which likely occurs through elevated emotion regulation difficulties and intolerance of uncertainty. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions to support adolescents in war settings, with particular attention to the challenges associated with displacement.

Introduction

Individuals living in war-affected regions are exposed to chronic threat, violence, and pervasive uncertainty, conditions that are consistently associated with marked deterioration in mental health and emotional well-being (Chudzicka-Czupała et al., 2023; Kurapov et al., 2023; Mejia et al., 2023). While exposure to war affects individuals across the lifespan, adolescents appear particularly vulnerable to its psychological consequences (Gonçalves Júnior et al., 2022; Henrich & Shahar, 2013; Tefferi, 2007). Adolescence is a developmental period characterised by heightened emotional reactivity and ongoing maturation of emotion regulation capacities (Silvers, 2022), which may limit adolescents’ ability to manage sustained stress and uncertainty. Consistent with this vulnerability, adolescents living in war-afflicted regions show elevated rates of depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), with symptoms that may persist long after the conflict ends (Barenbaum et al., 2004; Brajša-Žganec, 2005; Dimitry, 2012; Singhal, 2019).

Within the broader context of war exposure, displacement may represent a distinct and particularly disruptive stressor. Displacement involves forced relocation from one’s home environment, either within national borders (i.e. internal displacement) or across countries (i.e. external displacement), and is accompanied by abrupt disruptions to daily routines, social networks, education, and a sense of stability. Although relocation may provide physical safety, it often entails prolonged uncertainty regarding housing, schooling, community belonging, and future prospects (Gonçalves Júnior et al., 2022; Singhal, 2019). For adolescents, such instability may place exceptional demands on developing emotion regulation capacities and intensify difficulties tolerating uncertainty, thereby exacerbating emotional distress beyond the effects of war exposure alone (Otorkpa et al., 2024).

In line with this perspective, difficulties in emotion regulation have been identified as a key mechanism in the development of psychopathology among adolescents exposed to war and forced migration (Amone-P’Olak et al., 2019; Khamis, 2023). Effectively managing emotional reactions is suggested to play an important protective role against poor mental health outcomes among war-affected individuals (Keleynikov et al., 2025). In addition, intolerance of uncertainty may represent a particularly relevant emotional process in the context of displacement during ongoing conflict, which involves pervasive and prolonged uncertainty regarding safety, routines, and future prospects. Studies have shown that intolerance of uncertainty predicts poorer mental health among displaced refugees (Nickerson et al., 2023), mediates the link between fear of war and mental health outcomes (Najem et al., 2025), and is generally elevated among individuals exposed to war (Sowan & Baziliansky, 2024) and refugees (Bilewicz et al., 2024; Moscardino et al., 2025; Özmen, 2025). However, research examining the role of emotion regulation difficulties and intolerance of uncertainty among internally displaced adolescents remains limited.

Despite growing recognition of the psychological burden associated with war-related displacement, research in this area faces important limitations. Studies of displaced adolescents frequently encompass heterogeneous forced migration contexts, including family separation, economic deprivation, and restricted access to education and healthcare (Bennouna et al., 2020). These co-occurring adversities are themselves potent predictors of emotional distress, making it difficult to disentangle the specific contribution of displacement from the broader accumulation of stressors. A recent scoping review confirmed elevated mental health risks among conflict-displaced children and adolescents but also emphasised that research on internally displaced youth remains scarce and predominantly qualitative (Otorkpa et al., 2024). Moreover, most studies lack a comparison group of adolescents who were exposed to the dangers and stressors of war but were not displaced, limiting conclusions regarding displacement-specific effects.

Comparisons between displaced and non-displaced adolescents who share similar demographic characteristics and are exposed to a common war context are therefore needed to isolate the unique psychological impact of displacement. Such designs allow for examination of whether displacement confers additional risk beyond war exposure itself, and whether this risk is reflected not only in elevated distress but also in underlying processes related to emotion regulation and tolerance of uncertainty.

The outbreak of the Israel-Hamas war on 7 October 2023, created conditions that uniquely permit such a comparison. A large-scale internal evacuation of Israeli civilians residing near the Gaza Strip and the northern border of Israel resulted in the relocation of thousands of families to temporary residences in central regions of the country. At the same time, rocket attacks continued to threaten much of Israel, exposing both displaced and non-displaced adolescents to ongoing war-related danger. Notably, a recent study of non-displaced Israeli adolescents found that over half reported clinical levels of anxiety and trauma-related symptoms, attributed to repeated missile attacks, media exposure to wartime atrocities, acquaintance with individuals who were killed or abducted, and sustained disruption of educational routines (Haham et al., 2025). This context provides a rare opportunity to examine the mental health consequences of internally displaced compared to non-displaced youth who are both exposed to similar war-related threats.

The goal of the current study was to assess the emotional consequences of war-related internal displacement among adolescents. We recruited 200 Israeli adolescents during the war, half of whom were internally displaced and half of whom were not. Importantly, both groups were exposed to ongoing war-related threats, including frequent rocket attacks, allowing for a comparison that holds exposure to active hostilities relatively similar across groups. Mental health outcomes included emotional distress, operationalised as symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress, as well as difficulties in emotion regulation and intolerance of uncertainty. These processes were examined as potential correlates of distress that may be particularly sensitive to the instability and unpredictability inherent in displacement. In addition, exploratory analyses examined whether displacement moderated the associations between emotion regulation difficulties, intolerance of uncertainty, and emotional distress.

We hypothesised that displaced adolescents would report higher levels of emotional distress, greater difficulties in emotion regulation, and higher levels of intolerance of uncertainty compared to non-displaced adolescents. We further hypothesised that greater emotion regulation difficulties and higher intolerance of uncertainty would be associated with higher levels of emotional distress, with these associations expected to be stronger among displaced adolescents.

Methods

Participants

A total of 200 adolescents aged 14–18 were recruited for this study (see sample characteristics in Table 1). Recruitment began in February 2024, and all data were collected by May 2024 (4–8 months after the onset of the war). Half of the participants (n = 100) and their families were internally displaced, with 31 participants evacuated from their homes near the Gaza border and 69 from their homes near the northern border of Israel due to risks of ground invasion. The remaining half (n = 100) consisted of adolescents residing in communities outside the official evacuation zones, as defined by Israeli authorities based on proximity to the Gaza and northern borders and perceived risk of ground incursion. Although these adolescents were also exposed to missile threats and active warfare, they were not evacuated from their homes, allowing for a comparison between groups with shared exposure to war-related stressors but differing in displacement status. Inclusion criteria for both groups: (1) native Hebrew speakers, (2) aged 14–18 years, (3) currently living in Israel, (4) access to a computer. Exclusion criteria included: (1) diagnosis of a psychiatric disorder (screened via self-report), (2) direct major trauma as a result of the current war (e.g. personal exposure to armed militants, physical injury, or witnessing such events), (3) loss of or abduction of a first-degree family member in the current war. These exclusion criteria were applied to reduce the confounding effects of severe war-related trauma and to better isolate the psychological impact of displacement within an ongoing conflict context.

The sample size was determined via a power analysis using G*Power (Faul et al., 2007), which showed that 176 participants would be sufficient to reveal a difference between two independent groups with a power > 95%, a-priori alpha set at .05, expecting a medium effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.5. We decided to recruit 200 participants due to potential data loss.

Procedure

The study was approved by the University of Haifa institutional review board (032/24) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The data for the current study were drawn from baseline assessments collected before participation in a larger intervention study. Participants in both groups were recruited through public advertisements inviting adolescents to take part in a study examining their experiences during the war, without reference to the subsequent intervention to prevent anticipation effects.

Participants were recruited via advertisements on social media and through youth coordinators in organisations that support displaced communities. An initial screening was conducted via phone with the adolescent’s parents to assess eligibility based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. After parental informed consent was obtained, adolescents signed an assent form. Participants completed a set of self-report questionnaires assessing mental health outcomes via an online platform (Qualtrics). Participants received a gift voucher as compensation for their time.

Measures

Demographic questionnaire

We collected data on gender, age, grade, family socioeconomic status, religion, and residential location before the war. Additionally, a measure of perceived social support was included (Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support; MSPSS; Zimet et al., 1988) to ensure that potential differences between mental health outcomes are not explained by differences in social support.

The Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (DASS-21; Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995)

The DASS is a 21-item self-report questionnaire assessing anxiety, depression, and stress. It provides three subscales: (1) Depression, (2) Anxiety, and (3) Stress, and a total score indicating general psychological distress. Cronbach’s α in the present study was .94. The Hebrew version of the DASS-21 has shown good convergent and discriminant validity (Anghel, 2020).

Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS; Gratz & Roemer, 2004)

The DERS is a 36-item self-report questionnaire that assesses emotion regulation abilities. It provides a total score, with a higher score representing greater difficulties in emotion regulation. Cronbach’s α in the present study was .87. A Hebrew version of the DERS has shown good convergent and discriminant validity (Erez & Gordon, 2025).

The Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale-Situation-Specific Version (IUS-SS; Mahoney & McEvoy, 2012). The IUS-SS is a 12-item self-report questionnaire assessing intolerance of uncertainty in relation to a current stressful situation (e.g. war in the current study). A higher total score represents a greater difficulty coping with uncertainty. Cronbach’s α in the present study was .91. The Hebrew version of the IUS-SS has demonstrated good convergent and discriminant validity (Ben Zaken Linn et al., 2025; Mana et al., 2025).

Results

Out of 200 participants recruited for the study, data from two participants were lost due to a technical error.

Demographic characteristics

There were no differences between the groups in age, gender, socioeconomic status, country of birth, or perceived social support (see Table 1). Non-displaced adolescents had more years of education; however, the raw difference was relatively minor.

Primary outcomes

Independent samples t-tests were conducted to examine differences between displaced and non-displaced adolescents across psychological outcomes, and the Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied to control for false discovery rate due to multiple comparisons. In line with the hypothesis, the results showed higher levels of psychological distress, intolerance of uncertainty, and difficulties in emotion regulation among displaced adolescents (see Table 2). Depression, anxiety, and stress subscales and norms

Given the significant group difference in the DASS-21 total score, we further examined the subscales separately (see Figure 1). Displaced adolescents reported moderate levels of depression based on DASS-21 norms (M = 7.07, SD = 4.91), which were significantly higher than those of non-displaced peers, who reported mild levels of depression (M = 5.66, SD = 4.62), t(196) = –2.09, p = .038, Cohen’s d = –0.30. Similarly, displaced adolescents reported moderate levels of stress (M = 9.60, SD = 4.99), significantly higher than the mild stress levels reported by non-displaced adolescents (M = 7.38, SD = 5.26), t(196) = –3.03, p = .003, Cohen’s d = –0.43. In contrast, no significant group difference was found for anxiety: displaced adolescents (M = 5.26, SD = 4.97) and non-displaced adolescents (M = 4.81, SD = 4.47) both reported mild anxiety levels, t(196) = –0.68, p = .499, Cohen’s d = –0.10. Secondary analyses – moderation

Two moderation models using PROCESS (Hayes, 2022) were conducted to assess if the link between emotion regulation/intolerance of uncertainty and emotional distress was moderated by displacement. In the first model, emotional distress (DASS-21 total score) was entered as the dependent variable, intolerance of uncertainty (IUS-SS) as the predictor, and displacement (displaced / non-displaced) as the moderator. In the second model, emotion regulation difficulties (DERS total score) were used as the predictor. To facilitate interpretation, all continuous predictors were standardised (z-scores) before analysis.

The first model was significant, F(3, 194) = 83.65, p < .001, accounting for 56.4% of the variance in emotional distress (R² = .564). Intolerance of uncertainty significantly predicted emotional distress, B = 20.56, SE = 1.83, t(194) = 11.21, p < .001, 95% CI [16.96, 24.21]. The main effect of displacement was not significant, B = 1.25, SE = 2.56, p = .62, 95% CI [−3.79, 6.30]. In contrast to the hypothesis, the interaction between intolerance of uncertainty and displacement was not significant, B = –1.35, SE = 2.57, p = .599, 95% CI [−6.41, 3.71].

The second model was also significant, F(3, 194) = 74.98, p < .001, explaining 53.7% of the variance (R² = .537). Emotion regulation difficulties were a strong predictor of emotional distress, B = 19.52, SE = 1.81, t(194) = 10.80, p < .001, 95% CI [15.95, 23.08]. The effect of displacement was nonsignificant, B = 2.20, SE = 2.63, p = .404, 95% CI [−2.99, 7.39] and, in contrast to the hypothesis, the interaction between emotion regulation difficulties and displacement was not significant, B = –0.38, SE = 2.64, p = .887, 95% CI [−5.58, 4.84].

Exploratory post-hoc mediation analyses

Given that displacement was associated with elevated intolerance of uncertainty and emotion regulation difficulties, and that displacement was not significantly associated with emotional distress when controlling for these proximal emotional processes, we carried out a mediation analysis to examine if the association between displacement and emotional distress occurs through these processes. Specifically, two exploratory mediation analyses were conducted. In the first model, emotional distress (DASS-21 total score) was entered as the dependent variable, intolerance of uncertainty (IUS-SS) as the mediator, and displacement (displaced / non-displaced) as the predictor. The total effect of displacement on emotional distress was significant, B = 8.16, SE = 3.74, z = 2.19, p = .029, 95% CI [0.84, 15.48]. Displacement significantly predicted intolerance of uncertainty, B = 3.73, SE = 1.50, z = 2.48, p = .013, 95% CI [0.78, 6.67], and intolerance of uncertainty significantly predicted emotional distress, B = 1.85, SE = 0.12, z = 15.65, p < .001, 95% CI [1.62, 2.08]. Importantly, the indirect effect of displacement on emotional distress through intolerance of uncertainty was significant, B = 6.90, SE = 2.81, z = 2.45, p = .014, 95% CI [1.39, 12.41] and the direct effect of displacement was not, B = 1.26, SE = 2.54, p = .619, 95% CI [−3.71, 6.23].

In the second model, emotion regulation difficulties (DERS total score) were entered as the mediator. Displacement significantly predicted emotion regulation difficulties, B = 8.19, SE = 3.72, z = 2.20, p = .028, 95% CI [0.90, 15.48], which in turn significantly predicted emotional distress, B = 0.73, SE = 0.05, z = 14.82, p < .001, 95% CI [0.63, 0.82]. Similar to the previous model, the indirect effect of displacement on emotional distress through emotion regulation difficulties was significant B = 5.96, SE = 2.74, z = 2.18, p = .029, 95% CI [0.60, 11.33], while the direct effect did not reach significance B = 2.20, SE = 2.60, z = 0.84, p = .399, 95% CI [−2.91, 7.30].

Discussion

The present study examined the emotional consequences of war-related internal displacement among adolescents. Consistent with our hypotheses, displaced adolescents reported higher levels of emotional distress, greater intolerance of uncertainty, and more pronounced difficulties in emotion regulation compared to their non-displaced counterparts. Group differences in emotional distress were primarily driven by higher levels of depression and stress among displaced adolescents, whereas anxiety levels did not differ between groups. In contrast to our expectations, displacement did not moderate the associations between intolerance of uncertainty or difficulties in emotion regulation and emotional distress. However, a post-hoc mediation analysis suggested that displacement may be associated with emotional distress through these processes.

War-related displacement during adolescence has long been recognised as a significant risk factor for emotional distress, with numerous studies documenting elevated rates of depression, anxiety, and PTSD among war-exposed youth (Barenbaum et al., 2004; Brajša-Žganec, 2005; Dimitry, 2012; Singhal, 2019). However, much of the existing literature has been constrained by heterogeneous samples in which displacement co-occurs with additional post-displacement adversities, such as poverty, family separation, and limited access to education and healthcare (Bennouna et al., 2020). These overlapping stressors complicate efforts to disentangle the specific psychological contribution of displacement from the broader effects of war exposure, leaving open the question of whether displacement confers additional risk beyond exposure to armed conflict itself.

The current study helps address this gap by comparing displaced and non-displaced adolescents sampled during an active war, at a time when exposure to threat was ongoing, influencing both groups. Both groups reported above-normal levels of depression, anxiety, and stress relative to normative data, and the groups were comparable in demographic characteristics. Although displacement cannot be experimentally manipulated, this design provides an approximation of the impact of displacement on adolescents’ emotional distress by holding exposure to active hostilities relatively constant. Within this context of pervasive war-related stress, displaced adolescents exhibited higher levels of depression, stress, intolerance of uncertainty, and difficulties in emotion regulation than their non-displaced peers. These findings are consistent with the notion that difficulties in emotion regulation and intolerance of uncertainty represent key mechanisms underlying psychological distress in contexts of prolonged adversity and instability, such as war and forced displacement (Keleynikov et al., 2025; Khamis, 2023; Nickerson et al., 2023; Sowan & Baziliansky, 2024)

Given that both emotion regulation difficulties and intolerance of uncertainty are strong predictors of emotional distress, we hypothesised that these associations would be stronger among displaced adolescents. In contrast with our hypothesis, displacement did not moderate the relationships between these proximal emotional processes and emotional distress. Additionally, the main effect of displacement on emotional distress was not significant when controlling for intolerance of uncertainty and emotion regulation difficulties. This suggests that displacement does not contribute uniquely to emotional distress, beyond proximal emotional processes. One possible explanation is that the effect of displacement on emotional distress occurs through intolerance of uncertainty and emotion regulation difficulties. Post-hoc mediation analyses were consistent with this possibility, showing a significant indirect effect of displacement on emotional distress through both intolerance of uncertainty and emotion regulation difficulties, while the direct effects of displacement on emotional distress were not significant. However, given the cross-sectional nature of the study and the lack of temporal separation between variables, these analyses cannot support causal inferences or establish mediation in a strict sense, but they do suggest that differences in emotion regulation and intolerance of uncertainty may represent key pathways through which displacement is associated with emotional distress.

From a clinical perspective, these findings underscore the importance of intolerance of uncertainty and emotion regulation difficulties as key treatment targets for war-affected individuals, given their potential role as pathways through which war-related displacement impacts emotional distress. Hence, the findings provide support for the use of emotion-focused interventions for war-affected populations (e.g. WHO’s Early Adolescent Skills for Emotions [EASE] and Dialectical Behavior Therapy–based interventions; (Dawson et al., 2019; Segal et al., 2025)). While such interventions may be broadly beneficial for war-exposed youth, displaced adolescents may require earlier, more intensive, or more sustained support to address the cumulative burden associated with prolonged instability, disruption of routines, and uncertainty regarding the future.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design and the absence of pre-war baseline data preclude causal inference regarding the impact of displacement on mental health outcomes and limit the ability to determine the extent to which observed differences reflect pre-existing vulnerabilities versus war-related effects. Longitudinal studies, ideally incorporating pre- and post-war assessments, are needed to clarify the temporal dynamics between displacement, regulatory processes, and emotional distress. Second, variability in exposure to specific war-related stressors, such as proximity to hostilities, frequency of alarms, and media exposure, was not systematically assessed and may have contributed to individual differences in distress. In addition, trauma exposure and daily stressors were not directly assessed in the current study. According to ecological models of war-related distress (Miller & Rasmussen, 2010), cumulative trauma exposure and ongoing stressors are central mechanisms underlying mental health outcomes in conflict settings. These factors may be particularly elevated among displaced adolescents and could partly account for their higher levels of distress found in our study. Additionally, to reduce the influence of extreme exposure, adolescents who experienced severe and direct war-related trauma were excluded, given its potential confounding impact on group comparisons. However, this decision was at the cost of a more selective sample that does not fully represent the broader population of war-affected youth. Third, the sample was predominantly female, which may limit generalizability and likely contributed to the overall elevated levels of reported distress, given consistent evidence that adolescent girls report higher levels of depression and stress than boys (Bennouna et al., 2020; Dimitry, 2012). However, it is important to note that gender differences cannot account for the reported group differences, as the proportion of males and females was equivalent between groups.

In conclusion, this study highlights the vulnerability of adolescents experiencing war-related internal displacement and suggests that this vulnerability is reflected in elevated emotional distress beyond the effects of war exposure. By examining displaced and non-displaced adolescents within the same active conflict, the findings help to clarify the role of displacement in emotional distress and related regulatory difficulties. Furthermore, the study suggests that difficulties in emotion regulation and intolerance of uncertainty are likely key treatment targets for alleviating emotional distress among war-affected individuals. Future research employing longitudinal designs and more diverse samples is needed to further elucidate the long-term trajectories of risk and resilience among displaced youth and to inform the development of targeted, mechanism-focused interventions.
