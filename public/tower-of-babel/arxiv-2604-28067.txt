Hallucination, Abstention, and Recursive Inseparability
Takuma Imamura

Abstract
. The impossibility of eliminating hallucination, understood here as incorrect definite answers, in sufficiently expressive yes-or-no formal domains is an immediate consequence of classical undecidability theorems. This note does not revisit that forced-answer obstruction as its main claim. Instead, it attempts to formally describe the corresponding limitation for abstaining systems. Abstention can trivially avoid hallucination if the system is allowed to abstain on every input; the substantive question is how large the domain of guaranteed correct non-abstaining answers can be. We formulate this question using separation in the arithmetical hierarchy. Given disjoint sets A A and B B , any system that answers Yes on all queries indexed by A A and No on all queries indexed by B B induces a separator of A A from B B . By combining this observation with the classical existence theorem of Δ n 0 \Delta_{n}^{0} –inseparable pairs of Σ n 0 \Sigma_{n}^{0} –sets, we yield a computability-theoretic trade-off between avoiding hallucination by abstention and maintaining a large domain of guaranteed coverage.

1.
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are widely used as general-purpose question-answering
systems. A common failure mode is now called
hallucination
:
the system gives a fluent and seemingly authoritative answer that
is false, unsupported, or unverifiable. The term is modern, but the
phenomenon has several logically distinct components. Some are empirical
and engineering-specific, involving training data, retrieval, calibration,
reinforcement learning from human feedback, user interfaces, benchmark
design, and deployment incentives. Others are worst-case mathematical
obstructions.

This note concerns only the latter kind of obstruction. We use the
term
hallucination
in a deliberately narrow formal sense: an
incorrect definite answer to a yes-or-no query. This convention is
not intended to capture every use of the term in the LLM literature.
It isolates the part of the issue that can be stated purely in computability-theoretic
terms.

The forced-answer case is already standard. If a computable system
is required to answer every sufficiently expressive yes-or-no query
by either
\mathrm{Yes}
or
\mathrm{No}
, then some answer must be wrong. This
is just the usual undecidability argument, for example the halting
problem, going back to the classical work of
1
;
12
.
Recent machine-learning papers have formulated inevitability or lower-bound
results for hallucination in different settings. For example,
3

prove a statistical lower bound for calibrated language models, while

14
formulate an inevitability claim for computable LLMs
in a computable-world framework. These results should be distinguished
from the simpler recursion-theoretic point used here.

The subject of this note is not the forced-answer impossibility itself.
The subject is the trade-off that remains after allowing
abstention
,
a mitigation for hallucinations. An abstaining system may answer “I
do not know,” “undetermined,” “could not solve,” or “abstain”
instead of giving a definite yes-or-no answer. This idea has a long
history under names such as the reject option, selective classification,
and selective prediction. It has also become central in LLM reliability
research, where abstention is studied as a way to reduce hallucination
and improve safety. See e.g.
13
.

Abstention trivially prevents hallucination if the system abstains
on every input. Thus the substantive question is a coverage question:
on how large a class of inputs can a hallucination-free system be
guaranteed to give correct non-abstaining answers? The answer below
is an immediate consequence of recursive inseparability. If the required
answered domain contains both sides of a
\Delta_{n}^{0}
–inseparable
pair of
\Sigma_{n}^{0}
–sets, then the system’s Yes-answer set
must itself be non-
\Delta_{n}^{0}
. Such a system cannot be computable
in
0^{\left(n-1\right)}
, where
0^{\left(n-1\right)}
denotes
the
\left(n-1\right)
–th Turing jump.

No new theorem of computability theory or learning theory is claimed.
To put it bluntly, such mathematical inevitability results are essentially
immediate consequences of classical recursion theory, and their origins
can be traced back to the 1930s–1940s. The purpose is just to state
the abstention trade-off explicitly in terminology that is close to
current discussions of LLM hallucination.

For the basic definitions and results on computability theory and
meta-mathematics, we refer to
8
;
5
;
2
.

2.
Formal model of hallucination and abstention

Fix a finite alphabet
\Sigma
and let
\Sigma^{\ast}
be the set
of finite strings over
\Sigma
. An AI system is idealised extensionally
as a total finite-output function. Without abstention, the output
set is

\set{\mathrm{Yes},\mathrm{No}}.

With abstention, the output set is

\set{\mathrm{Yes},\mathrm{No},\mathrm{Abstain}}.

The internal architecture of the system is irrelevant for the arguments
below. It may be neural, symbolic, statistical, rule-based, or hybrid;
only its input-output behavior is used.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, and let

q_{A}\colon\mathbb{N}\to\Sigma^{\ast}

be a computable map encoding the query:

q_{A}\left(x\right)\colon\quad\text{Is $x\in A$?}

For example, if
A
is defined by a formula
\varphi\left(x\right)
,
i.e,

A=\set{x\in\mathbb{N}}{\mathbb{N}\models\varphi\left(x\right)},

then
q_{X}\left(x\right)
may be the string asking whether
\varphi\left(x\right)

holds.

Definition 2.1

(Formal hallucination)
.

Let

F\colon\Sigma^{\ast}\to\{\mathrm{Yes},\mathrm{No},\mathrm{Abstain}\}

be a total function (representing the extensional input–output relation
of an AI system). On the query family
q_{A}
, a
hallucination

occurs at
x\in\mathbb{N}
if

F\left(q_{A}\left(x\right)\right)=\begin{cases}\mathrm{No},&x\in A,\\
\mathrm{Yes},&x\notin A.\end{cases}

Note that the output
\mathrm{Abstain}
is not counted as a hallucination.

Definition 2.2

(Soundness and coverage (restricted completeness))
.

The system
F
is said to be
sound
for
A
on the query
family
q_{A}
if it has no hallucinations on that family, that is,

F\left(q_{A}\left(x\right)\right)=\mathrm{Yes}\implies x\in A,

and

F\left(q_{A}\left(x\right)\right)=\mathrm{No}\implies x\notin A.

For
D\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, the system
F
is said to be
complete

on
D
if

F\left(D\right)\subseteq\set{\mathrm{Yes},\mathrm{No}}.

Thus, if
F
is sound and complete on
D
, then it gives a correct
definite answer to every
q_{A}
–query indexed by
D
.

We use the standard convention for the arithmetical hierarchy:
\Delta_{1}^{0}

is the class of computable sets, and for general
n\geq 1
, the
\Delta_{n}^{0}
–sets
are precisely the sets computable in
0^{\left(n-1\right)}
, where

0^{\left(n-1\right)}
denotes the
\left(n-1\right)
–th Turing
jump. (For example,
0^{\prime}
–computability coincides with computablility
in the limit.)

3.
Abstention, coverage, and inseparability

We now formulate the abstention trade-off.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be disjoint sets. A set
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}

is said to
separate

A
and
B
if

A\subseteq S\subseteq\mathbb{N}\setminus B.

For
n\geq 1
, a pair
\left(A,B\right)
of disjoint sets is called

\Delta_{n}^{0}
–inseparable
if no
\Delta_{n}^{0}
–set
separates
A
and
B
.

Classical recursion theory tells us that such pairs exist.

Fact 3.2
.

For every
n\geq 1
, there exists a pair
\left(A,B\right)
of disjoint

\Sigma_{n}^{0}
–sets that is not
\Delta_{n}^{0}
–separable.
(Note that the
\Pi_{n}^{0}
–set
\mathbb{N}\setminus B
separates

A
and
B
.)

This is a classical theorem of recursion theory; see e.g.
5
.

Fix a
\Delta_{n}^{0}
–inseparable pair
\left(A,B\right)
of

\Sigma_{n}^{0}
–sets, and
S
be its
\Pi_{n}^{0}
–separator,
i.e.

A\subseteq S\subseteq\mathbb{N}\setminus B.

Let
q_{S}
be a computable encoding of the query “Is
x\in S
?”.
Since
S
is
\Pi_{n}^{0}
, this may be taken to be a formal query
asking for the truth value of a
\Pi_{n}^{0}
–formula defining

S
.

For a total abstaining system

F\colon\Sigma^{\ast}\to\{\mathrm{Yes},\mathrm{No},\mathrm{Abstain}\},

denote its yes-answer set by

Y_{F}:=F^{-1}\left(\mathrm{Yes}\right).

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
A,B,S,q_{S},F,Y_{F}
be as above. Suppose that
F
satisfies

x\in A\implies F\left(q_{S}\left(x\right)\right)=\mathrm{Yes}

and

x\in B\implies F\left(q_{S}\left(x\right)\right)=\mathrm{No}.

Then

S_{F}:=q_{S}^{-1}\left(Y_{F}\right)

separates
A
and
B
. Consequently,
Y_{S}
is not
\Delta_{n}^{0}

and
F
is not
0^{\left(n-1\right)}
–computable.

Proof.

If
x\in A
, then
F\left(q_{S}\left(x\right)\right)=\mathrm{Yes}
, so
x\in q_{S}^{-1}\left(F^{-1}\left(\mathrm{Yes}\right)\right)=S_{F}
.
If
x\in B
, then
F\left(q_{S}\left(x\right)\right)=\mathrm{No}\neq\mathrm{Yes}
,
so
x\in\mathbb{N}\setminus S_{F}
.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
\left(A,B\right)
be a
\Delta_{n}^{0}
–inseparable
pair of
\Sigma_{n}^{0}
–sets, and let
S
be a
\Pi_{n}^{0}
–separator
of
\left(A,B\right)
. There is no total
0^{\left(n-1\right)}
–computable
function

F\colon\Sigma^{\ast}\to\{\mathrm{Yes},\mathrm{No},\mathrm{Abstain}\}

that is sound for
S
and complete on
A\cup B
. Or equivalently,
every total
0^{\left(n-1\right)}
–computable hallucination-free
system must abstain on at least one query (in fact, infinitely many
queries) indexed by
A\cup B
.

For
n=1
, the corollary says that no total computable hallucination-free
abstaining system can be complete on both sides of a recursively inseparable
pair of computably enumerable sets.

Remark 3.5
.

Abstention is treated here as an explicit observable output. We do
distinguish abstention from non-termination. If non-termination is
admitted as a separate behavior, then one obtains a different partial-computation
problem. For instance, for disjoint computably enumerable sets
A

and
B
, a partial computable procedure can enumerate their elements
simultaneously, output
\mathrm{Yes}
on elements that enter
A
, output

\mathrm{No}
on elements that enter
B
, and diverge elsewhere.

However, even in this case, we cannot escape the impossibility result
mentioned above. Provided that the non-termination is admitted, an
AI system can be formulated as a partial function

F\colon\Sigma^{\ast}\rightharpoonup\set{\mathrm{Yes},\mathrm{No},\mathrm{Abstain}}.

It is well-known that there exists an
F^{\prime}
–computable (computable
using the oracle for the
F
–halting problem) total function

\tilde{F}\colon\Sigma^{\ast}\to\set{\mathrm{Yes},\mathrm{No},\mathrm{Abstain}}

that extends
F
. The above result can now apply to
\tilde{F}
.

4.
Example: formal theories

The preceding example is just a corollary

Let
T
be a consistent recursively axiomatisable theory that extends,
or interprets, a weak arithmetic such as Robinson arithmetic
Q

or the first-order theory for discrete ordered semirings. More generally,
assume that
T
is in the usual scope of the Gödel–Rosser incompleteness
theorem (see
9
;
6
). Define

\displaystyle A_{T}

\displaystyle:=\set{\left\lceil\varphi\right\rceil}{T\vdash\varphi},

\displaystyle B_{T}

\displaystyle:=\set{\left\lceil\varphi\right\rceil}{T\vdash\neg\varphi}.

The set
A_{T}
is the set of theorems of
T
, and
B_{T}
is the
set of refutable sentences (anti-theorems) of
T
. Since
T
is
consistent, they are disjoint. Since
T
is recursively axiomatisable,
both are recursively enumerable (i.e.
\Sigma_{1}^{0}
). The pair

\left(A_{T},B_{T}\right)
is recursively inseparable (see
10
).

By
3.4
, there is no pair
\left(q,F\right)

of computable functions
q\colon\mathbb{N}\to\Sigma^{\ast}
and
F\colon\Sigma^{\ast}\to\set{\mathrm{Yes},\mathrm{No},\mathrm{Abstain}}

with abstention such that

F\left(q\left(x\right)\right)=\begin{cases}\mathrm{Yes},&x\in A_{T},\\
\mathrm{No},&x\in B_{T}.\end{cases}

Remark 4.1
.

By contrast, if
T
is decidable, then both
A_{T}
and
B_{T}

are decidable. In that case, the inseparability obstruction above
does not apply. Presburger arithmetic, the fragment of the true arithmetic
with only the addition symbol, is a standard decidable theory of the
natural numbers with only addition (
7
); the first-order
theory of real closed fields is another classical decidable theory
(see
11
). In such cases there is, in principle, an algorithm
that answers all
T
–settled queries correctly. This theoretical
possibility says nothing about whether a deployed LLM will do so.

5.
Conclusion

In sufficiently expressive undecidable domains, any computable system
forced to give a definite answer to every query must sometimes be
wrong. This is a direct consequence of classical undecidability. Abstention
changes the obstruction into a coverage trade-off. A system can avoid
hallucination by abstaining, but recursive inseparability shows that
the set of guaranteed correct non-abstaining answers may have to be
sharply restricted. If correctness is required on both sides of a

\Delta_{n}^{0}
–inseparable pair, then the Yes-answer set of the
system must be non-
\Delta_{n}^{0}
. Thus a total finite-response
system computable in
0^{\left(n-1\right)}
cannot provide such coverage
while remaining hallucination-free.

These observations do not make modern machine-learning work on hallucination
redundant. The computability argument is a worst-case theorem about
effective input-output functions. It does not estimate empirical hallucination
rates, evaluate calibration, design uncertainty estimators, compare
abstention policies, or analyse user incentives. Those questions are
specific to machine learning and human-computer interaction, and they
have independent scientific and practical value.

For example, abstention and selective-prediction research studies
how to trade coverage against empirical risk, how to estimate uncertainty,
and how to decide when a model should refuse to answer. In the LLM
setting, this includes work on truthfulness benchmarks such as
4
,
abstention methods and evaluations surveyed by
13
,
and abstention training such as
15
. These empirical
studies are distinct from the computability-theoretic impossibility
proved above. The classical result explains why perfect coverage is
impossible in worst-case formal domains. The machine-learning literature
studies how often hallucinations occur in deployed systems, which
training objectives and benchmarks amplify or mitigate them, and how
interfaces can encourage calibrated abstention rather than confident
falsehood.
