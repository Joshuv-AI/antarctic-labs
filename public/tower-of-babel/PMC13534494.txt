Developing strategies to enhance sustainable urban stormwater management: the case of Dambi Dollo Town, Kellem Wollega Zone, Ethiopia
Authors: Dajane Kana Darkushe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sustainable urban stormwater management integrates the urban water cycle into planning and design processes to improve resilience and environmental performance. This study develops strategies to promote sustainable stormwater management across multiple spatial scales, from individual plots and building blocks to neighborhoods and entire urban catchments. An exploratory mixed-methods approach was employed, combining quantitative and qualitative data. Quantitative data were obtained through field observations and questionnaire surveys to assess stormwater runoff patterns, land use characteristics, and drainage infrastructure conditions. Qualitative data were collected through semi-structured interviews with municipal officials, planners, and residents, supported by document analysis of relevant policies and plans. In addition, GIS and design tools were used to support the visualization and conceptual development of proposed strategies. The findings indicate that existing stormwater management practices in Dambi Dollo town are largely ineffective, relying on short-term and reactive measures rather than integrated, long-term solutions. Key challenges include institutional gaps, particularly the absence of stormwater-specific policies; technical limitations, such as a shortage of skilled professionals; and social barriers, including limited community awareness of stormwater and solid waste management. Field observations further reveal infrastructural deficiencies, including inadequate drainage systems and insufficient green spaces, which exacerbate runoff and flooding risks. The study proposes a set of sustainable urban stormwater management strategies applicable to both developed and redeveloping areas, emphasizing green infrastructure, multi-scale interventions, and stakeholder engagement. These strategies provide a practical framework for enhancing urban resilience and environmental sustainability in resource-constrained settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56737-6.

Introduction

Stormwater has long been recognized as a major contributor to urban flooding due to its significant impacts on both living and non-living environments1. Across cities and towns worldwide, vegetation and topsoil are often removed to make way for buildings, roads, and other infrastructure, while drainage networks are installed to manage runoff2. However, these systems frequently become overwhelmed, particularly during high-intensity rainfall events, when runoff discharge exceeds the drainage capacity of stormwater systems, leading to overflow into sewerage or municipal drainage lines and contributing to urban flooding and environmental degradation3,4. In many African urban areas, recent studies indicate increasing rainfall intensity and variability, resulting in greater volumes of stormwater runoff from impervious surfaces5. This has led to more frequent and severe flood events, damaging infrastructure and disrupting urban systems6. In addition to these impacts, stormwater runoff transports suspended and dissolved pollutants, including sediments, nutrients, heavy metals, and petroleum hydrocarbons, particularly from road surfaces, thereby degrading water quality and posing risks to urban ecosystems and public health7.

Urban stormwater management has traditionally focused on reducing peak flows, controlling pollution, and mitigating downstream flooding8,9. It is increasingly viewed as an integrated approach to managing the entire urban water cycle through urban planning and design10,11. In developed countries, decentralized and small-scale source control measures, such as stormwater infiltration facilities, are widely implemented to manage runoff and reduce pollution from heavy rainfall12,13. For example, stormwater infiltration is mandatory for new developments in Switzerland, while countries such as Germany, Sweden, Denmark, Australia, and the United States actively promote source control strategies and sustainable management practices (Marsalek & Chocat, 2018). Similar approaches have been adopted in developing countries such as Brazil, Chile, and Ghana, where on-site stormwater treatment and reuse systems are increasingly being implemented8,11.

Recent advances in flood hazard and urban stormwater assessment increasingly emphasize the integration of hydrodynamic modeling, remote sensing technologies, and machine learning approaches to improve flood prediction accuracy and urban resilience. Ahmad et al.14 demonstrated that integrating hydrodynamic simulations with ensemble machine learning algorithms such as Random Forest (RF) and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) significantly improves flood susceptibility assessment and spatial flood prediction in complex environments. Their study highlighted the importance of combining HEC-RAS hydrodynamic simulations, GIS, and machine learning techniques to generate reliable flood susceptibility maps and support disaster management planning. Similarly, Hanif et al.15 emphasized that high-resolution satellite-derived land-cover datasets substantially improve hydrological simulations and runoff estimation by providing more accurate representation of impervious surfaces, vegetation cover, and drainage characteristics affecting urban flood behavior. The study further demonstrated that integrating remote sensing data with machine learning classifiers such as Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Random Forest enhances land-cover classification accuracy and supports more reliable watershed and stormwater analyses. Furthermore, Ullah et al.16 conducted sensitivity analysis of a two-dimensional (2D) flood inundation model and found that uncertainties in parameters such as terrain elevation, Manning’s roughness coefficient, inflow discharge, and boundary conditions can considerably influence flood depth and inundation extent predictions. Their findings emphasized the importance of model calibration and uncertainty evaluation for improving flood-risk assessment and hydrodynamic simulation reliability. Likewise, Tariq et al.17 developed a hydrodynamic-based flood-risk management tool to assess the redistribution of expected annual flood damages within floodplains and demonstrated its importance for sustainable floodplain management, infrastructure planning, and disaster-risk reduction. These integrated approaches collectively demonstrate that combining geospatial technologies, remote sensing datasets, hydrodynamic simulations, and advanced analytical techniques provides a more comprehensive framework for sustainable urban stormwater and flood-risk management under increasing urbanization and climate variability.

Sustainable Urban Stormwater Management (SUSWM) aims to improve the built environment by minimizing the negative impacts of stormwater, preserving ecosystems, enhancing water quality, and reducing urban flood risks8,9,18,19. Accordingly, this study was conducted to develop strategies that enhance sustainable urban stormwater management in Dambi Dollo town. The need for such strategies arises from the town’s extensive impervious surfaces, including buildings, roads, and infrastructure, which have led to the loss and neglect of green spaces capable of absorbing stormwater runoff. The area experiences a prolonged rainy season from May to October, during which surface water accumulates on roads and overflows into adjacent areas, accelerating road deterioration. Additionally, the discharge of liquid waste contributes to overflow, while both liquid and solid wastes frequently block drainage systems. These challenges pose serious threats to the surrounding environment, including Borta Lake and several small rivers within the town. Therefore, the main objective of this study is to develop sustainable urban stormwater management strategies for Dambi Dollo town, aimed at reducing environmental degradation, protecting physical infrastructure, and minimizing public health and economic losses.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

Dambi Dollo is one of the oldest towns in the Oromia National Regional State, located in the south western part of the region. It lies approximately 652 kilometers from the national and regional capital, Finfinne (Addis Ababa). The town serves as the administrative center for both Sayo District and Kellem Wollega Zone. As of 2011, Dambi Dollo had a population of over 37,339 and covered a total land area of approximately 4350 hectares. Geo- graphically, the town is situated between latitudes 8°3007N and 8°4624N, and longitudes 34°4606E and 34°5000E. Located in the south western highlands, Dambi Dollo experiences rainfall for about six to seven months annually20. The study area encompasses all parts of Dambi Dollo town.

Research type

This study employed a descriptive research design to examine the causes and impacts of stormwater management challenges from social, economic, and environmental perspectives. Descriptive research focuses on existing conditions, prevailing practices, and the attitudes or effects being observed21. This approach was selected for its relevance in capturing detailed data from a broad range of respondents and for its ability to reflect the current realities of stormwater management practices in the town.

Sample techniques and size

The study was conducted in one kebele (the smallest administrative unit in Ethiopia) within Dambi Dollo town. To ensure the reliability and manageability of the data, a convenience sampling technique, a non-probability sampling method, was used to select respondents who were readily available and had direct experience or professional knowledge of stormwater runoff impacts in the study area. These respondents included local residents affected by stormwater-related challenges, as well as municipal experts involved in urban planning and infrastructure management. A total of 72 respondents participated in the main questionnaire survey, while 5 additional respondents were selected for a pilot study, resulting in a total sample size of 77. In addition, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 6 key informants, including municipal officials, urban planners, and infrastructure experts, to gain deeper insights into stormwater management practices and challenges. Data were collected through structured questionnaires and interview guides.

Data collection method

This study employed an exploratory mixed-methods approach, integrating quantitative and qualitative data to assess stormwater management practices and challenges in the study area. Primary data were collected through field observations, questionnaire surveys, and semi-structured interviews. Field observations were conducted to assess drainage conditions, runoff behavior, and visible impacts on the built and natural environment. Questionnaire surveys were administered to local residents to capture household-level experiences and perceptions of stormwater-related problems, while semi-structured interviews were conducted with municipal officials, urban planners, and infrastructure experts to explore institutional practices, technical limitations, and management challenges. Secondary data were collected to complement primary data and included policy documents, planning manuals, official reports, and photographic records, obtained from relevant municipal offices such as the Dambi Dollo Town Beautification and Greenery Office, Disaster Risk Management Office, Physical Infrastructure Office, and Health Office. The integration of multiple data sources enhanced the reliability and validity of the findings through methodological triangulation22.

Data analysis method

The collected data were analyzed using a combination of quantitative and qualitative techniques to ensure comprehensive interpretation. Quantitative data obtained from questionnaire surveys were analyzed using SPSS software, applying descriptive statistical methods to examine the impacts of stormwater on the natural and built environments. Qualitative data from interviews and field observations were analyzed through thematic analysis, supported by photographic documentation to identify key patterns, challenges, and contextual factors influencing stormwater management. Secondary data were reviewed and synthesized to support the interpretation of findings and to assess existing policies and institutional frameworks. In addition, Geographic Information Systems (GIS) and design software tools (e.g., AutoCAD, ArchiCAD, Adobe Illustrator, and Photoshop) were used to support the visualization, mapping, and conceptual development of proposed stormwater management strategies, rather than for quantitative spatial analysis. The results were presented using tables, charts, graphs, maps, and visual photographs, accompanied by descriptive and analytical discussions to ensure clarity and coherence.

Results and discussion

Demography and Socio-economic characteristics of the respondents

This part deals with socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents in sex, age, educational level, marital status, and family size. In sex distribution about 52.8% of the respondents were male while the remaining 47.2% were female. Also, the age of the respondents was categorized in to (21-24) 13.9%, (25-54) 63.9%, (55-64) 15.3%, those 65 and above years 6.9%. The proportion of the respondents significantly was within 25-54 years and 55-64 years that account for 63.9 and 15.3% respectively. The socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents indicate that approximately 6.9% were aged 65 years and above. This group may be more vulnerable to stormwater runoff impacts due to reduced physical capacity and limited economic activity23,24. The educational profile of respondents shows that the majority are literate, which has important implications for stormwater management25. Literate residents are more likely to understand risks, adopt adaptation strategies, and support the implementation of mitigation measures, including raising awareness among less-educated community members25. Previous studies indicate that socio-demographic and economic characteristics significantly influence community resilience to urban flooding and stormwater impacts8,12. In addition, age plays a critical role in vulnerability assessment, as different age groups exhibit varying levels of exposure and adaptive capacity26. Younger populations generally have greater physical capacity to respond to and recover from flood events, thereby contributing to local risk reduction efforts23, Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, 2022).

These measures are primarily implemented by local residents to reduce the impacts of inadequate stormwater management, including flooding and infrastructure damage. This finding is consistent with the classification proposed by Qiao30, which groups sustainable stormwater management practices into: (i) source control, (ii) on-site control (e.g., green roofs and rain gardens), (iii) slow transport systems (e.g., swales and ditches), and (iv) downstream control (large-scale facilities). Recent studies further demonstrate that integrating hydrodynamic modeling, remote sensing, and machine learning techniques can significantly improve flood hazard assessment and urban stormwater planning, particularly in rapidly urbanizing areas vulnerable to flooding14,15. Similarly, sensitivity analysis of two-dimensional flood inundation models and hydrodynamic-based flood-risk management tools have been shown to enhance the understanding of runoff dynamics and support effective flood mitigation strategies16,17. The effectiveness of such decentralized and nature-based approaches in improving urban hydrological performance has been widely documented31, Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, 2022).

Overall, the results suggest that small-scale and decentralized stormwater management practices are commonly adopted at the household and neighborhood levels, indicating a community-driven adaptation to stormwater challenges and underscoring the need to formally integrate these practices into urban planning and policy frameworks.

Proposed sustainable urban stormwater management strategies

Increasing runoff and flood risk require urban planners and water management professionals to adopt more effective strategies to manage both the volume and velocity of stormwater32, Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, 2022). The proposed sustainable urban stormwater management strategies in this study focus on improving runoff quality and reducing runoff volume. These strategies incorporate Water Sensitive Urban Design (WSUD) approaches, which integrate stormwater management into urban planning and design to minimize environmental degradation while enhancing urban sustainability and livability27,33,34.

The study area is characterized by inadequate planning and design of stormwater management systems. Existing drainage infrastructure is insufficient to accommodate high stormwater discharge volumes, particularly during intense rainfall events, resulting in frequent overflows. This finding highlights the urgent need for improved and integrated stormwater management strategies that enhance system capacity, promote infiltration, and reduce flood risk. Recent studies further emphasize the importance of combining grey and green infrastructure solutions to improve urban drainage system performance under changing climatic conditions28, Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change [IPCC], 2022). In addition, advances in flood modelling highlight the importance of robust analytical approaches for understanding flood dynamics and associated risks. For example, sensitivity analyses of two-dimensional flood inundation models demonstrate the critical role of parameter calibration in accurately simulating flood extents and depths16, while hydrodynamic-based tools have been developed to assess flood risk and quantify expected annual damages under different scenarios17. Furthermore, integrating hydrodynamic modelling with remote sensing and ensemble machine learning techniques has been shown to significantly improve flood hazard susceptibility assessment in urban environments14. Likewise, the use of satellite-derived land cover data combined with hydrological simulations and machine learning approaches enhances the accuracy of runoff estimation and urban flood analysis15. However, the application of such data-intensive modelling approaches requires detailed hydrological and topographic data, which are often unavailable in data-constrained urban contexts such as Dambi Dollo.

Accordingly, the proposed strategies were developed based on the stormwater runoff characteristics and associated impacts in the study area. These approaches promote the direct infiltration of rainwater into the natural ground and allow stormwater to be passively conveyed into designated amenity areas following heavy rainfall events35. The strategies comprise a combination of structural and non-structural stormwater management practices, implemented at both on-site and off-site levels, and are designed to align with the natural environment while ensuring functionality, safety, and aesthetic acceptability36. Accordingly, these control measures are intended to efficiently manage surface runoff while minimizing pollutant loads and mitigating adverse impacts on the water quality of receiving rivers. Integrating sustainable urban stormwater management principles with broader urban planning frameworks can significantly reduce environmental degradation while enhancing urban resilience9,37, Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, 2022). These approaches can be implemented through a combination of source control, on-site control, multi-site control, slow transport, and downstream control measures, which collectively improve hydrological performance and reduce urban flood risk28,38. The scale of application ranges from individual households and streetscapes to neighborhood blocks and entire urban catchments, reflecting the multi-scalar and nature-based approach required for effective stormwater management29,39.

Recent advances highlight the growing role of hydrodynamic modelling, remote sensing, and machine learning techniques in improving flood hazard assessment and risk management. These approaches enhance the ability to simulate flood dynamics, capture spatial variability, and support evidence-based decision-making40,41. For instance, integrating hydrodynamic modelling with remote sensing and ensemble machine learning techniques has significantly improved flood hazard susceptibility assessment in urban areas14. Similarly, the integration of satellite-derived land cover data with hydrological simulations and machine learning methods has enhanced the accuracy of runoff estimation and flood prediction under varying land-use conditions15. In addition, sensitivity analyses of two-dimensional flood inundation models demonstrate the importance of parameter calibration in accurately simulating flood depth and spatial extent16, while hydrodynamic-based flood-risk management tools have been developed to assess floodplain vulnerability and estimate expected annual damages under different flooding scenarios17. These approaches enhance the ability to simulate flood dynamics, capture spatial variability, and support evidence-based decision-making42,43. However, their application typically requires high-resolution hydrological, topographical, and climatic datasets, as well as significant technical capacity. In data-constrained urban environments such as Dambi Dollo, these requirements limit their practical implementation. The proposed street design was developed by the author, drawing upon established standards and prior research. Narrow streets have been shown to intensify stormwater overflow during rainfall, leading to prolonged inundation and environmental impacts47. To mitigate these challenges, the design adopts dimensions from the town’s structural plan: arterial streets at 30 m, collector streets at 16 m, local streets at 8–12 m, and a 4 m greenway along river buffer zones48.

Naturalized drainage systems are integrated, with standardized channels of 1.2 m and circulation areas of 15–20% (totalling 2 m), supported by grassed or vegetated swales. These features promote slow conveyance, infiltration, and evapotranspiration of stormwater. Grassed channels, protected by turf cover, are recommended for roadside ditches, medians, and small watercourse diversions due to their environmental compatibility36.

Street surface materials are selected to align with the structural plan and local conditions: porous asphalt for arterial streets, cobblestone for collector and local streets, and porous materials for greenways. This approach reduces impervious surfaces and enhances infiltration, consistent with findings that minimizing hard surfaces such as parking lots and roads reduces stormwater runoff32.

Conclusion

This study aimed to develop strategies to enhance sustainable urban stormwater management (SUSWM) and reduce the impacts of urban runoff in Dambi Dollo town. The findings indicate that the existing stormwater management system is inadequate, relying primarily on short-term and reactive measures such as drainage channel cleaning, temporary earth embankments, and gravel covering. These approaches are insufficient to manage increasing stormwater volumes and runoff velocities, particularly during intense rainfall events, thereby limiting long-term system resilience. To address these limitations, the study emphasizes the need for integrated and sustainable approaches that combine green infrastructure and hydrological controls, including source control (e.g., stormwater harvesting and green roofs), on-site control (e.g., permeable pavements and retention ponds), slow transport systems (e.g., vegetated swales and sustainable drainage systems), and downstream control (e.g., riverside buffers and green spaces). The effective implementation of these measures requires coordinated planning and design by municipal authorities, supported by technical expertise, adequate funding, and active community participation. However, this study is limited by its focus on a single urban context and the absence of long-term monitoring data. Future research should evaluate the performance of the proposed strategies through pilot projects that assess the effectiveness of each control measure under local conditions. Establishing baseline indicators is essential in this regard. A practical monitoring framework incorporating low-cost approaches; such as citizen-based water level observations, photographic documentation of drainage conditions, household surveys on flood impacts, and basic water quality testing-can provide a foundation for evidence-based evaluation. Overall, integrating these strategies with accessible monitoring tools can enhance both the scientific robustness and policy relevance of stormwater management in resource-constrained urban environments and support the development of more resilient and sustainable urban systems.
