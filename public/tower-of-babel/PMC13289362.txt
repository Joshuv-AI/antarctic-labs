Joint progression and determinants of hypertension and type 2 diabetes among adults in post-conflict Tigray, Ethiopia: implications for chronic disease care in fragile health systems
Authors: Abdukadir Nigus Mohammed, Edris Abdu Debesay, Ashagire Zenebe Woldearegawi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) frequently co-occur and synergistically increase the risk of cardiovascular and renal complications and premature mortality. This dual burden is particularly critical in post-conflict settings, where disruptions in healthcare infrastructure and chronic disease management may accelerate disease progression. Following the recent conflict in Tigray, northern Ethiopia, health services and medication supply systems were severely affected. We examined the joint longitudinal progression and determinants of hypertension and T2DM in a post-conflict clinical setting.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted at Ayder Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Mekelle, Ethiopia. Medical records of 1024 adults (≥18 years) with hypertension and/or T2DM and at least three follow-up visits (December 2022–April 2024) were reviewed. Repeated measures of systolic/diastolic blood pressure and fasting blood glucose (FBG) were analysed using a joint linear mixed-effects model to simultaneously estimate correlated trajectories and shared random effects. Demographic, behavioural, clinical and healthcare utilisation variables were included as fixed effects. Model assumptions and goodness-of-fit were assessed.ResultsParticipants had a mean age of 49 years and mean body mass index of 27.2 kg/m²; 60.0% were female and 81.9% resided in urban areas. Overweight and behavioural risk factors were common. Increasing age and body weight were independently associated with higher longitudinal blood pressure and FBG levels (p<0.05). Smoking, alcohol use and lower visit frequency were significantly associated with poorer glycaemic control. The joint model demonstrated a significant positive association parameter between blood pressure and FBG trajectories, indicating correlated disease progression.ConclusionsHypertension and T2DM progress in a closely interrelated manner in post-conflict Tigray, influenced by both modifiable and non-modifiable factors. Integrated chronic disease management, strengthened follow-up systems and resilient supply chains are essential during health system recovery in fragile settings.

Background

Hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) are leading global contributors to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.12 Their coexistence substantially increases the risk of complications including cardiovascular disease, kidney failure, stroke and premature death.34 The burden of these conditions is rising in low- and middle-income countries, including Ethiopia, driven by urbanisation, dietary transitions, physical inactivity and increasing overweight and obesity.156

In Ethiopia, lifestyle behaviours such as smoking, alcohol consumption, khat chewing and low physical activity have been associated with poor blood pressure and glycaemic control.7–10 Socioeconomic status, gender differences and healthcare access further influence chronic disease outcomes.11–13

The recent conflict in Tigray severely disrupted healthcare infrastructure, medication supply chains and continuity of chronic disease services. In fragile health systems, such disruptions may worsen disease control and long-term outcomes. However, limited evidence exists on the longitudinal joint progression of hypertension and T2DM in post-conflict settings.

This study therefore examined the joint progression and determinants of hypertension and T2DM among adults in post-conflict Tigray to inform integrated chronic disease care in fragile health systems.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort study at Ayder Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (ACSH) in Mekelle, Tigray, Ethiopia. ACSH is a tertiary referral hospital that serves both urban and rural communities and provides specialised care for non-communicable diseases, including hypertension and T2DM.

The study covered medical records from 1 December 2022 to 30 April 2024, representing the post-conflict period during which chronic disease services were being re-established and strengthened in the region.

Study population

The study included adults aged 18 years and older who had a documented diagnosis of hypertension and/or T2DM and attended at least three follow-up visits during the study period.

Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure of ≥140 mm Hg and/or diastolic blood pressure of ≥90 mm Hg on at least two separate occasions, or current use of antihypertensive medication.

T2DM was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥6.5% or current use of antidiabetic medication.

Patients with gestational diabetes or other specific types of diabetes were excluded. Individuals were classified based on confirmed clinical diagnosis or ongoing treatment. Temporary variations in blood pressure or glucose readings did not alter diagnostic classification.

Sampling procedure

From a total of 3148 eligible patients, 1024 participants were selected using stratified random sampling based on residence (urban and rural). Stratification was applied to ensure adequate representation of both groups, given known differences in lifestyle patterns, healthcare access and potential disruptions to services during and after the conflict.

Within each stratum, participants were selected using computer-generated random numbers.

Data collection and quality assurance

Data were extracted from both paper-based and electronic medical records using a standardised and pretested data extraction form. The tool was piloted before full data collection to ensure clarity and consistency.

Four trained healthcare professionals (two nurses and two physicians) conducted the data extraction under close supervision. To ensure data quality, 10% of records were randomly selected for independent audit and discrepancies were resolved through cross-checking with the original patient files.

The dataset used for the present study is available as online supplemental file 1 and online supplemental file 2. Detailed descriptions of the study variables and coding procedures are provided in online supplemental table S1.

We evaluated missing data prior to analysis. Because this was a longitudinal study with repeated measurements, mixed-effects models were used, allowing inclusion of participants with incomplete follow-up data under the assumption that data were missing at random.

Variables

The primary outcomes were repeated measurements of systolic blood pressure (mm Hg), diastolic blood pressure (mm Hg) and fasting blood glucose (FBG) (mmol/L), obtained from routine follow-up visits and extracted from medical records.

Explanatory variables included demographic (age, sex, residence, marital status, education, income), clinical (weight, body mass index (BMI), family history of hypertension or diabetes and frequency of follow-up visits), and behavioural factors (smoking, alcohol use, khat chewing, physical activity and dietary habits). All variables were recorded during standard chronic disease care.

Physical activity was categorised as never (no regular exercise), rarely (<3 times per week) and frequently (≥3 times per week). Detailed variable definitions and coding used in the analysis are provided in online supplemental table S1.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participants’ baseline characteristics, including the mean number of follow-up visits. To assess the longitudinal progression of disease outcomes, linear mixed-effects models were employed to account for repeated measurements within individuals over time. Time was defined as the number of follow-up visits for each participant and was treated as a continuous variable in the mixed-effects models.

Initially, separate models were fitted for blood pressure and FBG. Subsequently, a joint linear mixed-effects model was applied to simultaneously estimate the trajectories of both outcomes. This approach allowed us to account for the correlation between blood pressure and glucose levels while capturing shared individual-level variability.

Interaction terms were explored based on clinical relevance, including interactions between visit frequency and sex, visit frequency and residence, and age and sex. Only statistically significant interactions that improved model fit, as evaluated using the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC), were retained in the final model.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SAS V.9.4.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 1024 adults with hypertension and/or T2DM were included in the study. The mean age of participants was 49 years, with a mean weight of 64 kg and a mean BMI of 27.2 kg/m², indicating that most participants were overweight. The mean baseline blood pressure was 148/106 mm Hg. The majority of participants were female (60.0%) and resided in urban areas (81.9%). Regarding educational status, 80.3% had primary education, 14.2% had secondary or higher education and 5.5% were illiterate. Approximately 79.9% were living with a partner.

Behavioural characteristics showed a high prevalence of modifiable risk factors. More than half of the participants were current smokers (54.2%), 45.5% reported alcohol consumption and 33.7% reported khat chewing. Nearly half (46.5%) reported no regular physical activity, while 27.0% exercised frequently and 26.6% exercised rarely. Dietary patterns indicated that 42.9% consumed meat rarely and 43.3% consumed vegetables frequently. In addition, 52.7% of participants reported a family history of hypertension or diabetes (table 1).

Factors associated with hypertension and type 2 diabetes

In the joint linear mixed-effects model, both non-modifiable and modifiable factors were associated with longitudinal changes in blood pressure and FBG. Increasing age was significantly associated with higher trajectories of both outcomes over time. Female participants showed slightly higher blood pressure levels but lower FBG compared with males. Rural residence was associated with lower baseline blood pressure and FBG compared with urban residence, although differences in progression patterns were observed during follow-up. These findings indicate that demographic characteristics influence disease trajectories in this population (table 2).

Among modifiable factors, higher body weight and BMI were positively associated with increases in both blood pressure and FBG. Smoking and alcohol consumption were associated with poorer control of both outcomes, whereas non-smokers and participants who did not consume alcohol had lower levels of blood pressure and FBG. In addition, more frequent follow-up visits were associated with better glycaemic control. The joint model demonstrated a statistically significant positive association between blood pressure and FBG trajectories, indicating correlated progression of hypertension and type 2 diabetes over time. This finding supports the presence of shared longitudinal dynamics between the two conditions (table 2).

Interaction effects

Interaction terms were included in the joint model to examine whether the longitudinal trajectory of FBG differed across key demographic groups over time. These interactions were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, particularly sex, residence and age, and were retained only if statistically significant and if they improved model fit according to AIC and BIC. Other tested interaction terms were not statistically significant and were therefore excluded from the final model.

The results showed significant interactions between visit number and sex, visit number and residence, and age and sex. Female participants demonstrated greater improvement in FBG over follow-up compared with males (visit×sex: −0.38 mg/dL, 95% CI −0.75 to −0.01; p=0.01). Urban residents experienced faster reductions in FBG during follow-up compared with rural residents (visit×residence: −0.28 mg/dL, 95% CI −0.52 to −0.04; p=0.01). Additionally, the age×sex interaction indicated that older female participants had slightly higher FBG trajectories compared with males of similar age (1.54 mg/dL, 95% CI 0.25 to 2.83; p=0.02) (table 3).

Joint determinants of hypertension and T2DM

In the joint linear mixed-effects model, FBG was analysed as the outcome while accounting for repeated measurements and the correlation between blood pressure and glucose trajectories. Several demographic, clinical, behavioural and healthcare utilisation factors were independently associated with FBG levels after adjustment.

Higher body weight and the presence of hypertension were significantly associated with higher FBG levels over time. Specifically, each 1 kg increase in body weight was associated with a 0.73 mg/dL increase in FBG (95% CI 0.03 to 0.86; p<0.01). In addition, higher blood pressure was associated with increased FBG (β=0.58; 95% CI 0.02 to 0.92; p<0.01), indicating interconnected progression of the two conditions within the joint model framework (table 4).

Discussion

This study examined the longitudinal progression and determinants of hypertension and T2DM among adults receiving care in post-conflict Tigray, Ethiopia. By using a joint modelling approach, the findings demonstrate that blood pressure and FBG progress in a correlated manner, highlighting the close interrelationship between these two chronic conditions in a fragile health system context. These results reinforce the need for integrated chronic disease management, particularly in post-conflict settings where healthcare systems face structural challenges.1415

Age and anthropometric measures were consistently associated with disease progression. Increasing age was linked to higher levels of both blood pressure and FBG, in line with previous studies from Ethiopia and other sub-Saharan African countries.16–18 Similarly, higher body weight and BMI were significantly associated with worsening trajectories of both outcomes, supporting well-established evidence that excess adiposity contributes to insulin resistance and elevated blood pressure.171920 These findings underline the importance of weight management and early prevention strategies within chronic disease programmes.

Sex and residence differences were also observed. Female participants demonstrated more favourable FBG trajectories compared with males, consistent with studies reporting better treatment adherence and healthcare engagement among women in Ethiopia.21–23 Rural residence was associated with lower baseline levels of blood pressure and FBG, although progression patterns differed over time. Urban–rural disparities in non-communicable disease burden and management have been documented in previous Ethiopian studies, reflecting inequities in healthcare access, continuity of care, and socioeconomic conditions.24–26 These findings suggest the importance of tailoring interventions to geographic and contextual realities.

Lifestyle behaviours were strongly associated with outcomes. Smoking, alcohol consumption and physical inactivity have previously been linked to hypertension and diabetes in Ethiopian populations.78101127 In this study, non-smokers and individuals who did not consume alcohol had significantly better blood pressure and glycaemic control. In addition, more frequent follow-up visits were associated with improved glycaemic outcomes, supporting evidence that continuity of care and regular monitoring are critical for chronic disease management.2829 These results highlight the importance of strengthening patient follow-up systems and adherence support.

The joint modelling analysis demonstrated a significant positive correlation between hypertension and T2DM progression, confirming their bidirectional relationship and shared risk profile.273031 Interaction effects further indicated that disease trajectories varied by sex, residence, and follow-up frequency, suggesting that integrated management strategies should consider subgroup differences to improve effectiveness.

Finally, the post-conflict setting in Tigray likely contributed to challenges in chronic disease control due to disruptions in healthcare infrastructure, interruptions in medication supply, workforce displacement and increased psychosocial stress.1415 In fragile health systems, these disruptions can negatively affect continuity of care and long-term disease outcomes. Therefore, strengthening integrated hypertension–diabetes services, ensuring consistent medication availability, expanding community-based screening, and implementing outreach or mobile services should be prioritised during health system recovery efforts.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It used a joint longitudinal modelling approach to examine the correlated progression of hypertension and T2DM, providing a more comprehensive analysis of multimorbidity. The use of repeated measurements allowed assessment of disease trajectories over time, and stratified random sampling improved representation of both urban and rural participants. The inclusion of demographic, clinical and behavioural variables also enabled evaluation of important determinants and interaction effects.

However, the study relied on secondary medical records, which may have contained incomplete or inconsistently recorded information. Some lifestyle and psychosocial factors were not fully captured, which may have led to residual confounding. In addition, the findings may not be generalisable beyond the study setting, and the retrospective design does not allow causal conclusions.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the progression of hypertension and T2DM in post-conflict Tigray is influenced by demographic, clinical, lifestyle and healthcare-related factors. The significant correlated progression of both conditions highlights the need for integrated management rather than separate treatment approaches. Modifiable factors such as body weight, smoking, alcohol use, physical activity and regular follow-up visits represent important targets for intervention.

In the context of a fragile, post-conflict health system, strengthening integrated chronic disease services, ensuring continuity of care and medication supply, expanding community-based screening and improving patient education are essential. These findings support ongoing health system recovery efforts and provide a foundation for future research aimed at building resilient non-communicable disease services in similar settings.
