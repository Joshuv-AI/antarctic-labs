From Laboratory to Clinic: Translational Medicine Paradigm of Polymyxin B Nanopreparations for Overcoming Drug-Resistant Bacterial Infections
Authors: Na Li, Yong Cao, Ming Li, Dan Zheng, Wen Zhong, Chengli Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe global prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative bacterial infections has posed a severe threat to public health security. As the “last line of defense” antibiotic against such infections, polymyxin B is severely limited in clinical application by dose-dependent nephrotoxicity (25–50% incidence), neurotoxicity, and the global spread of mcr-mediated resistance. Nanotechnology overcomes these limitations through three key mechanisms: (1) targeted enrichment at infection sites to reduce systemic exposure; (2) controlled drug release to minimize peak concentration-related toxicity; and (3) synergistic co-delivery to reverse bacterial resistance. This review systematically summarizes the design principles and research progress of polymyxin B nanopreparations, focuses on analyzing the core issues encountered in their clinical translation, including insufficient biosafety verification, limited targeted delivery efficiency, immature large-scale production technology, and lack of drug resistance prevention and control systems. However, these nanopreparations still face critical translational challenges including unclear long-term toxicity mechanisms, poor biofilm penetration, and lack of standardized quality control. Combined with the latest research findings, targeted solutions are proposed, including optimization of carrier material biocompatibility, construction of intelligent responsive targeting systems, establishment of standardized production processes, and development of multi-dimensional drug resistance monitoring strategies. This review aims to provide theoretical support and technical guidance for the clinical translation of polymyxin B nanopreparations, promote their clinical application in the treatment of MDR bacterial infections, and offer a reference paradigm for the development of novel antibacterial preparations.Keywords: polymyxin B, nanopreparations, multidrug-resistant bacteria, clinical translation, targeted delivery, drug resistance prevention and control

Introduction

Global Challenge of Drug-Resistant Bacterial Infections and Clinical Predicament of Polymyxin B

Global Antimicrobial Resistance Crisis and Unmet Clinical Needs

With the widespread use and even overuse of antibiotics, bacterial drug resistance has become one of the most severe challenges in the global public health sector of the 21st century. According to World Health Organization (WHO) statistics, the annual global death toll from drug-resistant bacterial infections has exceeded 700,000 and is projected to surpass 10 million by 2050.1–3 Among these pathogens, Gram-negative bacteria such as Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE), multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (MDR-PA), and multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (MDR-AB) have become particularly difficult clinical challenges in anti-infective therapy due to their unique outer membrane barrier and the rapid spread of drug resistance genes.4–7 In the context of the continuously declining efficacy of traditional antibiotics, Polymyxin B, a polypeptide antibiotic, has been listed by the WHO as a “critically important antimicrobial agent” due to its specific binding capability to the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria and its potent bactericidal activity. Consequently, it has become a last-resort option for treating infections caused by MDR Gram-negative bacteria.8–10

Polymyxin B: Chemical Structure, Derivatives and Clinical Applications

Polymyxin B is a cyclic cationic polypeptide antibiotic produced by Bacillus polymyxa, consisting of a cyclic heptapeptide ring linked to a linear tripeptide side chain and a fatty acid tail. The fatty acid tail is essential for its antibacterial activity, as it inserts into the bacterial outer membrane and disrupts lipid bilayer integrity. Major derivatives of polymyxin B include colistin (polymyxin E) and colistimethate sodium (Figure 1), which differ slightly in amino acid composition and exhibit similar antibacterial spectra but different pharmacokinetic profiles.9 Polymyxin B is administered intravenously for the treatment of severe systemic infections caused by MDR Gram-negative bacteria, with a recommended daily dose of 1.5–2.5 mg/kg divided into two or three doses.

Mechanism of Action of Polymyxin B

The primary mechanism of action of polymyxin B involves electrostatic interaction between its positively charged amino groups and the negatively charged phosphate groups of LPS in the bacterial outer membrane. This interaction displaces divalent cations (Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺) that stabilize the LPS layer, leading to membrane permeabilization and leakage of intracellular contents. At higher concentrations, polymyxin B can also insert into the inner bacterial membrane and cause further damage. Additionally, polymyxin B exhibits anti-endotoxin activity by neutralizing LPS and preventing its recognition by the host immune system.10

Mechanisms of Polymyxin B Resistance

Bacterial resistance to polymyxin B is mediated by multiple mechanisms, including: (1) acquisition of plasmid-borne mcr genes (most commonly mcr-1),2 which encode phosphoethanolamine transferases that modify LPS and reduce polymyxin B binding; (2) chromosomal mutations in genes involved in LPS biosynthesis (eg, pmrA/B, phoP/Q);11 (3) biofilm formation, which provides a physical barrier that limits drug penetration;12 and (4) efflux pump overexpression, which actively removes polymyxin B from bacterial cells.13 The global spread of mcr-1 has led to a decline in the clinical efficacy rate of polymyxin B from 90% to below 60% in some regions.10

Traditional Strategies to Overcome Polymyxin Resistance

Traditional strategies to address polymyxin resistance include combination therapy with other antibiotics (eg, β-lactams, carbapenems, tigecycline), dose optimization, and therapeutic drug monitoring. However, these strategies have limited efficacy and are often associated with increased toxicity. Therefore, there is an urgent need for novel approaches to enhance the efficacy and safety of polymyxin B.14

Advantages and Research Status of Nanotechnology in Polymyxin B Delivery

The rise of nanotechnology has provided a new perspective for solving the clinical application problems of polymyxin B.15 By loading or modifying drugs on nano-carriers (such as liposomes, polymers, peptides, etc), polymyxin B nanopreparations can achieve three core breakthroughs: (1) Targeted enrichment: Using specific antigens on the bacterial surface (such as LPS)16 or characteristics of the infected microenvironment (such as acidic pH, high ROS levels) to achieve precise delivery of drugs at the infection site and reduce accumulation in normal tissues;17–19 (2) Controlled release: Through the responsive design of carrier materials (such as pH response, enzyme response), drugs are released on demand at the infection site to maintain effective therapeutic concentration while reducing peak concentration-related toxicity;20–22 (3) Synergistic efficacy enhancement: Achieving multiple effects of “antibacterial-anti-inflammatory- reversing drug resistance” by co-delivering other antibacterial agents (such as niclosamide, β-lactam antibiotics) or immunomodulators (such as dexamethasone);23,24 (4) Biofilm penetration: The small-size effect (10–200 nm) of nano-carriers enables them to penetrate the dense structure of bacterial biofilms and eliminate persister bacteria within the biofilms.25–27

Up to now, a large number of studies have confirmed the superiority of polymyxin B nanopreparations. For example, the polymyxin B-modified fosfomycin liposomes constructed by Gugu et al28 increased the antibacterial activity against MDR Escherichia coli by 4 times compared with free drugs, and reduced the toxicity to renal tubular epithelial cells by 60%;29 the polymyxin B nanocapsules developed by Yuk et al reduced the accumulation of drugs in the kidneys by 30% in a mouse model of Gram-negative bacterial sepsis, and increased the survival rate of mice from 30% to 80%. However, despite the excellent performance of polymyxin B nanopreparations in vitro experiments and animal models, their translation from laboratory to clinic still faces many bottlenecks, such as insufficient biosafety verification, immature large-scale production technology, and lack of clinical efficacy prediction models.30 Based on relevant research literature, this review systematically summarizes the research progress of polymyxin B nanopreparations, deeply analyzes the obstacles to their clinical translation, and proposes feasible solutions to lay the foundation for the clinical application of such preparations.31

Statistical Analysis

All quantitative data presented in this review are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise specified. Statistical significance was determined using Student’s t-test for comparisons between two groups and one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and a P-value < 0.01 was considered highly statistically significant. Liposomal Nanopreparations: Balance Between Biocompatibility and Targeting

As one of the earliest nano-carriers used in clinical practice, liposomes have good biocompatibility, degradability, and modifiability, and are ideal platforms for polymyxin B delivery. Their core design strategies include: (1) Surface modification to enhance targeting: Achieving specific enrichment of drugs at the infection site by modifying polymyxin B (targeting bacterial LPS), antibodies (such as TLR4 antibodies), or peptides (such as RGD peptides) on the liposome surface;36–38 (2) Formulation optimization to reduce toxicity: Reducing non-specific binding between liposomes and renal cells and lowering nephrotoxicity by adjusting phospholipid composition (such as using hydrogenated soybean phospholipids) or adding cholesterol;39 (3) Co-loading to achieve synergistic effects: Simultaneously exerting antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects by co-loading polymyxin B with other drugs (such as dexamethasone, fosfomycin).33,40

Zhu et al prepared polymyxin B-modified fosfomycin liposomes by the thin-film hydration method. The preparation achieves specific recognition of MDR Gram-negative bacteria through the specific binding of polymyxin B to bacterial LPS. In vitro experiments show that the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) against Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae are 0.5 μg/mL and 1 μg/mL, respectively, which are 4 times lower than those of free polymyxin B (MICs of 2 μg/mL and 4 μg/mL); in a mouse model of pulmonary infection, the preparation can reduce the pulmonary bacterial load by 104 CFU/g, and the degree of renal injury (measured by serum creatinine level) is 60% lower than that of the free drug group.33 Rocha et al38 loaded polymyxin B into nanostructured lipid carriers (NLC) containing dexamethasone acetate. The preparation not only exerts antibacterial effects through polymyxin B but also inhibits excessive inflammatory responses induced by infection through dexamethasone. In a rat model of skin infection, it can reduce the number of leukocyte infiltrations at the infection site by 50% and accelerate wound healing (healing time shortened from 14 days to 7 days). In addition, the injectable polymyxin B liposomes developed by Cern et al extend the in vivo circulation time (half-life from 2 hours to 8 hours) by modifying the surface with PEGylated phospholipids (DSPE-PEG2000), and no obvious nephrotoxicity is observed in preclinical experiments on healthy volunteers.13

Liposomal polymyxin B preparations exert their therapeutic effects through multiple mechanisms. The liposomal bilayer structure protects polymyxin B from degradation in the bloodstream and reduces its non-specific binding to renal tubular epithelial cells. Surface modification with targeting ligands enhances the accumulation of liposomes at the infection site through receptor-mediated endocytosis. Once internalized by bacteria or phagocytes at the infection site, liposomes release polymyxin B in response to the acidic pH or high enzyme concentration of the intracellular environment. Quantitative studies have shown that liposomal formulations increase the cellular uptake of polymyxin B by MDR bacteria by 3–5 times compared with free drugs, and exhibit sustained release kinetics with 80% drug release over 24 hours in acidic conditions.

Despite their advantages, liposomal polymyxin B preparations have several limitations: (1) Relatively low drug loading capacity (typically 5–10%), which requires high carrier doses and may increase carrier-related toxicity; (2) Limited stability during storage, with drug leakage rates reaching 25% after 1 month at 4°C; (3) Poor targeting specificity when relying solely on polymyxin B for targeting, which may lead to disruption of normal intestinal flora; and (4) High production costs associated with liposome manufacturing.41

Polymeric Nanopreparations: Breakthrough in Controlled Release and Drug Resistance Reversal

Polymeric nano-carriers have the advantages of strong structural adjustability, high drug-loading efficiency, and good stability. Their core value in polymyxin B delivery lies in achieving controlled drug release and reversing bacterial resistance. Common polymeric carriers include poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), chitosan, polyethyleneimine (PEI), and novel biodegradable polymers (such as poly(2-oxazoline)), etc. Their design strategies mainly include: (1) Responsive release design: Designing pH-responsive or enzyme-responsive polymeric carriers to achieve precise drug release at the infection site by utilizing the characteristics of the infected microenvironment (such as pH 5.5–6.5, high esterase concentration);42,43 (2) Polymer-drug self-assembly: Forming self-assembled nanocomplexes through electrostatic or hydrophobic interactions between polymers and polymyxin B, enhancing the destructive effect of drugs on the bacterial outer membrane and reversing bacterial resistance;34,44,45 (3) Biofilm penetration optimization: Improving the penetration ability of nanopreparations into bacterial biofilms by adjusting the hydrophilicity-hydrophobicity or surface charge of polymers.46–48

Zhao et al constructed self-assembled nanocomplexes using the electrostatic interaction between polymyxin B and anionic polymers (polyaspartic acid). The complexes can promote the entry of polymyxin B into bacteria through the destructive effect of polymers on the bacterial outer membrane, thereby reversing the phenotypic resistance of bacteria to polymyxin B. In vitro experiments show that the MIC of the complex against polymyxin-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa decreases from 64 μg/mL to 8 μg/mL, and the biofilm clearance rate reaches 90% (only 40% in the free drug group).49 Hu et al34 combined polymyxin B with biodegradable polymers (polycaprolactone-polyethylene glycol block copolymers) through reaction-induced self-assembly technology to form pH-responsive nanoparticles. The nanoparticles remain stable in the normal physiological environment (pH 7.4) (drug release rate < 10%) and rapidly release drugs in the acidic infected microenvironment (pH 5.5) (24-hour release rate > 80%). In a mouse model of peritonitis, the preparation can effectively eliminate intra-abdominal MDR bacteria without obvious renal injury (renal histopathological score is 70% lower than that of the free drug group).50 Sousa et al stabilized DNA micelles with polymyxin B. The high permeability of DNA micelles can penetrate the dense structure of Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilms, increasing the drug concentration within the biofilm by 10 times, and the clearance rate of bacteria within the biofilm reaches 95%, which is significantly better than that of free polymyxin B (40%).26

Polymeric nanopreparations enhance the therapeutic efficacy of polymyxin B through several key mechanisms. The electrostatic interaction between cationic polymyxin B and anionic polymers forms stable nanocomplexes that protect the drug from degradation. Responsive polymer design allows for on-demand drug release at the infection site, minimizing systemic exposure. Additionally, some polymers themselves exhibit antibacterial activity by disrupting bacterial membranes or inhibiting bacterial enzyme activity, leading to synergistic effects with polymyxin B. Quantitative studies have demonstrated that polymeric formulations can increase the biofilm penetration depth of polymyxin B from 20 μm to 80 μm, and reduce the expression of bacterial efflux pump genes by 60–80%.51,52

The main limitations of polymeric polymyxin B preparations include: (1) Potential toxicity of cationic polymers such as PEI, which can cause hemolysis and complement activation at high concentrations; (2) Slow degradation rates of some polymers, leading to long-term tissue accumulation; (3) Batch-to-batch variability in polymer synthesis, which affects the reproducibility of nanoparticle properties; and (4) Limited understanding of the in vivo fate of polymeric carriers and their degradation products.53–55

Peptide-Based Nanoparticles: Integration of “Delivery-Antibacterial” Dual Functions

Peptide carriers have biocompatibility, antibacterial activity, and targeting properties. Combined with polymyxin B, they can achieve the dual functions of “carrier delivery-drug antibacterial”. Their core design strategies include: (1) Antimicrobial peptide-polymyxin B conjugation: Conjugating peptides with broad-spectrum antibacterial activity (such as cecropin, defensin) with polymyxin B to enhance antibacterial activity and expand the antibacterial spectrum through synergistic effects;56–58 (2) Peptide-mediated targeted delivery: Achieving precise delivery of polymyxin B by utilizing the specific binding of peptides (such as lactoferrin peptides, bacterial chemotactic peptides) to bacterial surface receptors;59,60 (3) Peptide self-assembled nanoparticles: Forming nanoparticles through peptide self-assembly to load polymyxin B, reducing the contact between drugs and normal tissues and lowering toxic and side effects.61,62

Zhen et al designed ciprofloxacin peptide-based nanoparticles, conjugating polymyxin B with antimicrobial peptides (cecropin A). Through the synergistic effect of peptide targeting and polymyxin B antibacterial activity, the preparation expands the diameter of the inhibition zone against MDR Gram-negative bacteria (such as CRE, MDR-PA) by 30–50% compared with free drugs. In a mouse model of pulmonary infection, it can reduce the pulmonary bacterial load by 103 CFU/g and reduce the pulmonary inflammatory response (IL-6 level decreased by 40%).63 Liu et al constructed polymyxin B nanoassemblies through the coacervation complexation between polyanionic peptides (polyglutamic acid) and polymyxin B. The assemblies can avoid non-specific binding between polymyxin B and renal cells. In a rat model, the renal drug concentration is reduced by 40%, while the pulmonary drug concentration is increased by 2 times, which is suitable for the treatment of pulmonary MDR bacterial infections.64 The peptide-nanoparticle system developed by Pal et al loads polymyxin B into lactoferrin peptide-modified nanoparticles. Through the specific binding of lactoferrin peptides to lactoferrin receptors on the bacterial surface, targeted drug delivery is achieved. The antibacterial activity against MDR Acinetobacter baumannii is 8 times higher than that of free drugs, and there is no obvious hemolytic toxicity (hemolysis rate < 2%).35

Peptide-based nanopreparations offer unique advantages due to their dual functionality as both carriers and antibacterial agents. Antimicrobial peptides act synergistically with polymyxin B by targeting different components of the bacterial membrane, leading to enhanced membrane disruption. Peptide targeting ligands specifically bind to receptors overexpressed on bacterial surfaces, increasing the local concentration of polymyxin B at the infection site. Self-assembled peptide nanoparticles protect polymyxin B from enzymatic degradation and reduce its systemic toxicity. Quantitative studies have shown that peptide-conjugated polymyxin B exhibits 5–10 times higher binding affinity to bacterial LPS than free polymyxin B, and can be internalized by bacteria within 15 minutes of exposure.65–67

The limitations of peptide-based polymyxin B preparations include: (1) High cost of peptide synthesis, which limits large-scale production; (2) Susceptibility to proteolytic degradation in vivo, leading to short circulation half-lives; (3) Potential immunogenicity of exogenous peptides, which may induce antibody production and reduce efficacy upon repeated administration; and (4) Relatively low drug loading capacity compared with polymeric carriers.

Composite Nanopreparations: Integration of Multifunctional Synergy

Composite nanopreparations integrate the advantages of multiple carriers to achieve efficient delivery, synergistic antibacterial effects, and drug resistance reversal of polymyxin B, which is a cutting-edge direction in current research. Their core design strategies include: (1) Co-delivery of multiple drugs: Co-loading polymyxin B with other antibacterial agents (such as niclosamide, vancomycin) to enhance antibacterial activity and delay the development of drug resistance through the synergistic effect of different antibacterial mechanisms;68 (2) Nano-carrier-phage combination: Combining polymyxin B nanopreparations with phages to enhance the killing effect of nanopreparations on bacteria by utilizing the specific lytic effect of phages;69,70 (3) Nano-carrier-immunomodulator combination: Co-loading polymyxin B with immunomodulators (such as interferon-γ) to enhance anti-infection effects by regulating host immune responses.71–75

Yi et al constructed amphiphilic mPEG-PLGA copolymer nanoparticles to co-deliver polymyxin B and niclosamide. Among them, niclosamide can inhibit the activity of bacterial efflux pumps and reverse bacterial resistance to polymyxin B. In vitro experiments show that the MIC of the preparation against polymyxin-resistant Gram-negative bacteria is reduced to below 4 μg/mL, which is 16 times lower than that of free polymyxin B; in a mouse model of systemic infection, the preparation can extend the survival time of mice from 5 days to 14 days, and the degree of renal injury is significantly reduced.23 Dong et al developed a ROS-responsive nanoprodrug-engineered phage system, combining polymyxin B nanoprodrugs with phages targeting MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa. The system destroys the bacterial outer membrane through the specific lytic effect of phages, and at the same time, the ROS-responsive nanoprodrugs release polymyxin B at the infection site, achieving the synergistic effect of “targeted lysis-precise drug release”. In a mouse model of pneumonia, it can reduce the pulmonary bacterial load by 105 CFU/g without obvious systemic toxicity.69 Luo et al constructed a nano-system by combining peptide-mimicking poly(2-oxazoline) as an adjuvant with polymyxin B. The adjuvant can enhance the destructive effect of polymyxin B on the bacterial outer membrane and expand the antibacterial spectrum (covering Gram-positive bacteria such as MRSA). The MIC against MRSA is reduced from > 256 μg/mL to 32 μg/mL.58 As comprehensively evaluated in Figure 3, composite nanoformulations achieve the most balanced performance profile, excelling simultaneously in antibacterial efficacy, safety enhancement, and targeting precision, thereby representing the most promising candidate for clinical translation.

Composite nanopreparations exert their superior therapeutic effects through the integration of multiple mechanisms. Co-delivery of drugs with different mechanisms of action targets multiple bacterial pathways simultaneously, reducing the probability of resistance development. Nano-carrier-phage combinations leverage the specificity of phages for bacterial targeting and the potent bactericidal activity of polymyxin B. Immunomodulator co-delivery enhances host immune responses, helping to clear residual bacteria and prevent reinfection. Quantitative studies have demonstrated that composite formulations can reduce the required dose of polymyxin B by 80–90% compared with monotherapy, while maintaining equivalent or superior antibacterial efficacy.

Despite their promise, composite nanopreparations face several challenges: (1) Complex formulation design and manufacturing processes, which increase production costs and quality control difficulties; (2) Potential interactions between different components, which may affect the stability and efficacy of the preparation; (3) Limited understanding of the in vivo pharmacokinetics and biodistribution of multi-component systems; and (4) Regulatory challenges associated with the approval of combination products.

Comparative Analysis of Polymyxin B Nanopreparation Strategies

Mechanistic Basis of Nanopreparation-Mediated Therapeutic Benefits

Mechanisms of Enhanced Antibacterial Efficacy

Nanopreparations enhance the antibacterial efficacy of polymyxin B through multiple mechanisms: (1) Increased local drug concentration at the infection site through targeted delivery;76–78 (2) Improved biofilm penetration due to the small size and surface properties of nanoparticles;79 (3) Protection of polymyxin B from degradation by bacterial enzymes;66 (4) Synergistic effects between the carrier and polymyxin B;80–82 and (5) Inhibition of bacterial efflux pumps through co-delivery of efflux pump inhibitors.83,84

Mechanisms of Reduced Toxicity

Nanopreparations reduce the toxic side effects of polymyxin B primarily by: (1) Decreasing systemic exposure through targeted delivery and controlled release;74 (2) Reducing peak plasma concentrations, which are associated with nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity;8 (3) Preventing non-specific binding of polymyxin B to renal tubular epithelial cells and neuronal cells;44 and (4) Allowing for lower total doses of polymyxin B due to enhanced efficacy.

Mechanisms of Resistance Reversal

Nanopreparations reverse bacterial resistance to polymyxin B through: (1) Increasing the intracellular concentration of polymyxin B by disrupting bacterial membranes;85 (2) Co-delivering resistance reversers such as niclosamide, which inhibit efflux pumps;83 (3) Preventing biofilm formation and disrupting existing biofilms;18 and (4) Targeting multiple bacterial pathways simultaneously, making it more difficult for bacteria to develop resistance.86

Mechanisms of Immunomodulation

Some nanopreparations exhibit immunomodulatory effects by regulating the host immune response. They can reduce excessive inflammatory responses by neutralizing LPS and inhibiting pro-inflammatory cytokine production. Additionally, they can enhance phagocytosis of bacteria by macrophages and neutrophils, promoting bacterial clearance.87,88

Pharmacokinetic and Pharmacodynamic Optimization for Clinical Translation

Pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD) considerations are critical for the successful clinical translation of polymyxin B nanopreparations. Nanopreparations significantly alter the PK profile of polymyxin B by extending its circulation half-life, increasing its volume of distribution, and changing its tissue accumulation pattern. The optimal PK/PD index for polymyxin B is the area under the concentration-time curve over the MIC (AUC/MIC). Nanopreparations can achieve higher AUC/MIC ratios at lower doses compared with free drugs, leading to improved efficacy and reduced toxicity.89–91

Dose optimization strategies for polymyxin B nanopreparations should be based on PK/PD modeling and simulation. Factors such as patient age, weight, liver and kidney function, and infection severity should be taken into account when determining the optimal dose and administration frequency. Therapeutic drug monitoring should be implemented to ensure that drug concentrations remain within the therapeutic window.92,93

Despite the excellent antibacterial activity and safety of polymyxin B nanopreparations in basic research, their translation from laboratory to clinic still needs to break through the following four core bottlenecks, which involve multiple dimensions such as biosafety, targeting efficiency, production process, and drug resistance prevention and control, directly restricting the process of their clinical application (Figure 4).

Biosafety Verification: Lack of Comprehensive Evaluation from Short-Term Toxicity to Long-Term Risks

Biosafety is the primary prerequisite for the clinical translation of nanopreparations. However, the current safety evaluation of polymyxin B nanopreparations still has significant deficiencies:

Unclear Mechanism of Carrier-Related Toxicity

Although some nano-carriers (such as cationic polymer PEI, unmodified liposomes) can enhance the targeting of polymyxin B, they may induce toxic and side effects such as hemolysis, complement system activation, and inflammatory reactions. Mushtaq et al found that unmodified PLGA nanoparticles have a hemolysis rate of 15% at high concentrations (>1 mg/mL), and although the hemolysis rate decreases to 5% after modification with polymyxin B, it is still higher than the clinically acceptable level (<2%);94 in addition, cationic carriers may bind to anionic phospholipids on the cell membrane, destroy the integrity of the cell membrane, and lead to cell apoptosis.95–98

Neglected Long-Term Toxicity and Tissue Accumulation Risks

Most existing studies focus on short-term toxicity (such as renal injury and liver injury within 72 hours), lacking safety data from long-term administration (such as 28 days, 90 days). Cern et al found in long-term administration experiments on rats that although the short-term nephrotoxicity of polymyxin B liposomes is lower than that of free drugs, mild atrophy of renal tubular epithelial cells still occurs after continuous administration for 4 weeks, suggesting that carrier materials may accumulate in renal tissues and have potential risks for long-term use;13 in addition, factors such as the particle size, surface charge, and degradation products of nano-carriers may affect their metabolism and excretion in the body, thereby inducing long-term toxicity.99–101

Imperfect Immunogenicity Evaluation System

As exogenous substances, nano-carriers may be recognized as “foreign bodies” by the body’s immune system, triggering antibody production or activation of immune cells (such as macrophages, dendritic cells), leading to immunogenic reactions. Metselaar et al showed that after 3 administrations of peptide-modified polymyxin B nanoparticles in mice, anti-peptide antibodies appear in the serum, and the antibody titer increases with the number of administrations, which may affect the long-term efficacy and safety of the preparation; in addition, nano-carriers may activate the complement system, triggering allergic reactions (such as urticaria, anaphylactic shock).102,103

Targeted Delivery Efficiency: Insufficient Adaptability from Laboratory Models to Clinical Scenarios

Precise targeting of the infection site and effective drug penetration are the keys to ensuring the efficacy of polymyxin B nanopreparations. However, the current targeting efficiency of the preparations still cannot meet clinical needs:

Insufficient Specificity and Affinity of Targeting Ligands

Most preparations achieve targeting by relying on the binding of polymyxin B itself to bacterial LPS, but LPS is highly conserved in Gram-negative bacteria, which cannot distinguish pathogenic bacteria from normal intestinal flora, and may lead to intestinal flora imbalance. Zhang et al found that liposomes targeting only polymyxin B not only accumulate in the pulmonary infection focus but also accumulate in large quantities in the intestines in mice, resulting in a 30% reduction in the number of intestinal Escherichia coli and disrupting the intestinal microecological balance 64; in addition, the affinity of some targeting ligands (such as peptides, antibodies) is low, which cannot effectively compete for receptors on the bacterial surface, leading to reduced targeting efficiency.104,105

Insufficient Biofilm Penetration Ability to Meet Clinical Needs

In chronic infections, bacteria often form biofilms (such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilms in the lungs of patients with cystic fibrosis, Staphylococcus aureus biofilms in the feet of diabetic patients). The dense structure of biofilms (such as extracellular polysaccharides, proteins) can hinder the penetration of nanopreparations, resulting in incomplete clearance of bacteria within the biofilm. Huwaitat et al showed that the penetration depth of polymyxin B-mimetic lipopeptide nanoparticles into biofilms is only 20 μm, while the thickness of biofilms is usually 50–100 μm, resulting in a clearance rate of bacteria within the biofilm of less than 50%; in addition, factors such as the acidic microenvironment and nutrient deficiency within the biofilm may affect the stability and drug release of nanopreparations.106,107

Low Penetration Efficiency of Deep Tissue Barriers

For deep infections such as central nervous system infections and bone infections, nanopreparations need to penetrate physiological barriers such as the blood-brain barrier and periosteal barrier, but their penetration efficiency is generally low. Jiang et al found that in a rat model of brain infection, the concentration of polymyxin B-peptide conjugated nanoparticles in the brain is only 5% of the blood concentration, which is much lower than the effective therapeutic concentration (>10 μg/mL); in the treatment of bone infections, nanopreparations need to penetrate the periosteum and cortical bone to reach the bone marrow cavity, while the penetration rate of existing preparations into bone tissue is less than 20%.108

Large-Scale Production and Quality Control: Technical Gap from Laboratory Preparation to Industrial Production

Large-scale production and stable quality control are the core guarantees for the clinical translation of polymyxin B nanopreparations. However, the current technical system still has significant defects:

Poor Scalability of Production Processes

Most laboratory preparation processes (such as emulsification-solvent evaporation method, self-assembly method) are difficult to scale up, and there are large differences between batches. Aflakian et al found that Scalability issues exist in the development of polymyxin B nanopreparations;30 in addition, laboratory processes usually adopt batch production mode, which has low production efficiency and high cost, and is difficult to meet clinical needs.109

Insufficient Formulation Stability to Meet Storage and Transportation Requirements

Nanopreparations are easily affected by factors such as temperature, pH, and ionic strength, leading to aggregation, drug leakage, carrier degradation, and other problems, resulting in poor stability. A growing body of evidence highlights the instability of polymyxin B liposomal formulations as a major hurdle for clinical translation. Lipid-based carriers are inherently prone to physical and chemical degradation, including lipid hydrolysis, oxidation, fusion, and drug leakage during storage, with stability highly dependent on temperature. The encapsulated drug itself is also susceptible to degradation; polymyxin B in aqueous solution shows marked decomposition after 48–72 h, particularly in neutral pH conditions. Freeze-drying, often used to improve long-term stability, can introduce additional issues—suboptimal lyophilization protocols lead to increased particle aggregation, reduced encapsulation efficiency, and prolonged reconstitution time. These combined stability challenges underscore the need for robust formulation strategies to ensure the quality and efficacy of polymyxin B nanopreparations.110

Lack of Quality Control Standards

At present, there is no unified quality standard for polymyxin B nanopreparations, and the detection methods and qualified ranges of key quality indicators (such as particle size, Zeta potential, drug encapsulation efficiency, in vitro release rate, microbial limit) have not been clarified. Pointed out that the particle size control ranges of polymyxin B nanoparticles in different studies vary greatly (20–200 nm), and the detection methods of drug encapsulation efficiency (such as high-performance liquid chromatography, dialysis method) are also not unified, making it impossible to horizontally compare the efficacy and safety of the preparations; in addition, the in vivo pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD) evaluation indicators of nanopreparations also lack standardization.61,74

Drug Resistance Prevention and Control and Clinical Efficacy Verification: Incomplete Evidence Chain from in vitro Experiments to Clinical Application

The development of drug resistance and the clarification of clinical efficacy are the core basis for the approval of polymyxin B nanopreparations. However, existing studies still have significant deficiencies:

Insufficient Evaluation of Drug Resistance Induction Risks

Although some nanopreparations can reverse bacterial resistance in the short term, long-term use may induce new resistance mechanisms. Found that after 10 consecutive generations of use of nanoparticles combining polymyxin B with non-antibiotic drugs (such as fluconazole), the bacterial resistance to polymyxin B increases by 8 times, which may be related to drug-induced expression of bacterial efflux pumps or gene mutations; in addition, the drug release kinetics of nanopreparations may affect the selection pressure of bacteria, thereby promoting the production of drug-resistant bacteria (Figure 5).111–113

Significant Differences Between Preclinical Animal Models and Clinical Scenarios

Most existing studies adopt acute infection models in immunodeficient mice (such as intraperitoneal injection of bacteria to construct sepsis, tracheal instillation of bacteria to construct pneumonia), which are quite different from chronic infections in clinical patients (such as foot infections in diabetic patients, pulmonary infections in patients with cystic fibrosis). Pointed out that the efficacy of nanopreparations in acute infection models may be overestimated, while in chronic infection models, due to the formation of biofilms by bacteria and low host immune function, the bioavailability and efficacy of drugs are significantly reduced compared with acute models; in addition, there are differences in metabolic pathways and physiological structures between animal models and humans, leading to inaccurate prediction of clinical efficacy.114–116

Severe Lack of Clinical Data

At present, no polymyxin B nanopreparations have entered the clinical trial stage, lacking human PK/PD data, clinical efficacy data, and safety data. Makabenta et al found that most studies only provide PK data in mice (such as half-life, blood concentration-time curve), while there are significant differences in the activity of liver metabolic enzymes (such as CYP450 enzymes) between humans and mice, which may lead to differences in the metabolic behavior of drugs in humans and animal models;117 in addition, individual differences such as age, weight, liver and kidney function of clinical patients may also affect the efficacy and safety of the preparations, which need to be further verified through clinical trials.

Optimization Strategy of Biosafety: From “Short-Term Toxicity Control” to “Long-Term Safety Guarantee”

Screening and Modification of Biocompatible Carrier Materials

(1) Screening of low-toxicity carriers: Priority is given to selecting biodegradable and low-toxicity carrier materials, such as PEG, hyaluronic acid, chitosan, polycaprolactone (PCL), etc. For example, Modified polymyxin B polymer nanoparticles with chitosan, reducing the hemolysis rate from 10% to below 2%; as a natural polysaccharide, chitosan has good biocompatibility and antibacterial activity, and the hemolysis rate of polymyxin B nanoparticles modified by chitosan is < 1%, and it can be degraded into non-toxic glucosamine by lysozyme in the body.118,119

(2) “Stealth” modification of carrier surface: Surface modification with PEG, red blood cell membrane, macrophage membrane, etc, to reduce the interaction between carriers and immune cells and lower immunogenicity. For example, Encapsulated polymyxin B nanocapsules in red blood cell membranes, utilizing the “self-recognition” characteristics of red blood cell membranes to avoid phagocytosis by macrophages, extend the in vivo circulation time (half-life extended by 2 times), and no anti-carrier antibodies are detected after 5 consecutive administrations in mice, and the nephrotoxicity is 60% lower than that of free drugs; in addition, cell membrane wrapping technology can also reduce the hemolytic toxicity of carriers.120,121

(3) “Carrier-drug” reversible binding strategy: Binding polymyxin B to carriers through pH-sensitive bonds (such as hydrazone bonds) or enzyme-sensitive bonds (such as peptide bonds), and the carriers are rapidly degraded after drug release to reduce tissue accumulation. For example, the ester bond-linked polymyxin B-peptide nanoparticles developed. can be completely degraded into non-toxic amino acids and small molecule peptides in the body without obvious tissue accumulation; pH-sensitive bonded nanoparticles remain stable in the normal physiological environment and rapidly release drugs in the acidic infected microenvironment, reducing exposure to normal tissues.44

Improvement of Long-Term Toxicity and Immunogenicity Evaluation Systems

(1) Establishment of long-term administration animal models using large animals (eg, rats) enables comprehensive safety evaluation of polymyxin B nanopreparations through histopathology, blood biochemical indices (eg, creatinine, urea nitrogen), and immune function detection (eg, cytokine levels). In a 7-day rat study, polymyxin B formulated with sodium deoxycholate sulfate micelles (PMB-SDCS) significantly reduced serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen elevations compared to free polymyxin B, with less renal tubular necrosis on histopathology, demonstrating improved nephrotoxicity profile. Furthermore, polymyxin B itself enhances immune responses by promoting IFN-α and IFN-γ production in plasmacytoid dendritic cells across species, indicating that long-term administration of nanopreparations warrants monitoring of T-cell subsets and cytokine levels to detect potential immunotoxicity or immunogenicity. Combining these approaches—routine renal function and histopathological examination together with immune status assessment—provides a robust framework for evaluating the long-term safety of polymyxin B nanopreparations.71,122

(2) Standardization of immunogenicity evaluation methods: Referring to the ICH S6 (R1) guideline, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), flow cytometry, and other methods are used to detect the levels of anti-carrier antibodies, complement activation products (such as C3a, C5a), and cytokines (such as IL-6, TNF-α) in serum to evaluate the immunogenicity of the preparations. For example, Wang et al determined the safe number of administrations (≤3 times) by detecting the titer of anti-peptide antibodies in peptide-modified nanoparticles through ELISA;68 in addition, immunogenicity should be evaluated in different species of animals (such as mice, rats, dogs, sheep) to provide reference for clinical research.123

Construction of Intelligent Responsive Targeting Systems: Improvement of Targeting Efficiency and Tissue Penetration

As illustrated in Figure 7 and Table 3, the construction of intelligent responsive targeting systems for polymyxin B nanopreparations integrates three synergistic mechanisms: (1) a dual-targeting strategy that combines infection microenvironment responsiveness (pH, ROS, enzymes) with bacterial surface receptor recognition (TLR4, siderophore receptors); (2) enhanced biofilm penetration through surface modification with biofilm-disrupting enzymes (hyaluronidase, protease) and stepwise release systems; and (3) improved deep tissue barrier penetration employing transferrin-mediated blood-brain barrier crossing and bone-specific targeting approaches. This integrated design framework enables precise drug release at infection sites, achieving >95% biofilm clearance rates and increasing brain drug concentrations to 20% of plasma levels, thereby addressing the critical targeting efficiency bottlenecks identified in clinical translation.

Translational Pathway for Polymyxin B Nanoformulations from Laboratory to Clinic

Double Targeting Strategy: Synergy Between Infected Microenvironment Targeting and Bacterial Targeting

(1) Infected microenvironment targeting: Design responsive targeting systems by utilizing the characteristics of the infected site, such as acidic pH, high ROS levels, and high enzyme concentration. For example, dual-functional polymyxin B nanoparticles with pH-responsive and bacterial LPS targeting. The nanoparticles remain inert in the normal physiological environment (pH 7.4), activate the targeting function in the acidic infected microenvironment (pH < 6.5), and bind to bacterial LPS, significantly reducing the risk of intestinal flora imbalance 64; ROS-responsive nanoparticles release drugs at the infection site with high ROS to achieve precise drug release.124

(2) Bacterial surface receptor targeting: Design targeting ligands by utilizing specific receptors on the bacterial surface (such as siderophore receptors, flagellin receptors) to improve targeting specificity. For example, TLR4 antibodies on the surface of polymyxin B liposomes. TLR4 is highly expressed at the infection site of MDR Gram-negative bacteria; siderophore-modified nanoparticles enter bacteria through bacterial siderophore receptors, enhancing the killing effect on intracellular bacteria.33

Optimization of Biofilm Penetration Ability

(1) Surface modification of carriers to enhance penetration: Destroy the extracellular polysaccharide and protein structure of biofilms by modifying biofilm-disrupting agents (such as hyaluronidase, protease) or co-delivering biofilm-disrupting agents on the carrier surface to enhance the penetration ability of nanopreparations. For example, Sousa et al modified hyaluronidase on the surface of DNA micelles of polymyxin B, which can increase the biofilm penetration depth from 20 μm to 80 μm, and the biofilm clearance rate reaches 95%; nanoparticles modified with surfactants (such as Tween 80) can reduce the surface tension of biofilms and promote penetration.

(2) “First destruction-then sterilization” stepwise release system: Design double-layer structured nanoparticles, with the outer layer loading biofilm-disrupting agents (such as niclosamide, EDTA) and the inner layer loading polymyxin B. The outer layer drugs are first released to destroy the biofilm, and then the inner layer drugs are released to kill bacteria. For example, the niclosamide-polymyxin B co-delivered nanoparticles developed by Yi et al first release niclosamide to inhibit bacterial efflux pumps and destroy biofilms, then release polymyxin B to kill bacteria, with a biofilm clearance rate of 98%;23 EDTA can chelate calcium ions in the biofilm, destroy the biofilm structure, and synergize with polymyxin B.

Penetration Strategies for Deep Tissue Barriers

(1) Blood-brain barrier penetration: Modify nanoparticles with targeting ligands (such as transferrin, insulin, lactoferrin) and combine with penetration enhancers (such as mannitol, urea) to enhance blood-brain barrier penetration. For example, Kalimuthu et al modified transferrin on polymyxin B-peptide conjugated nanoparticles. Transferrin binds to transferrin receptors on the blood-brain barrier surface and enters brain tissue through receptor-mediated endocytosis. Combined with mannitol, it can temporarily open the tight junctions of the blood-brain barrier, increasing the brain drug concentration to 20% of the blood concentration; in addition, focused ultrasound technology can be used to temporarily destroy the blood-brain barrier and enhance the penetration of nanopreparations.

(2) Bone tissue penetration: Load polymyxin B nanoparticles into biomaterials such as bone cement and poly(lactic acid) scaffolds to achieve sustained enrichment of drugs in bone tissue through local sustained release. For example, polymyxin B PLGA nanoparticles with bone cement and implanted them into a rat model of bone infection. The drug can be continuously released for 28 days, the bone tissue drug concentration is maintained above the MIC, and the bacterial clearance rate reaches 90%; in addition, osteocalcin antibodies, hydroxyapatite, etc. can be modified to enhance the binding between nanoparticles and bone tissue.

Establishment of Standardized Production Processes and Quality Control Systems

Optimization and Scaling-Up of Large-Scale Production Processes

(1) Selection of scalable production technologies: Adopt microfluidic technology, spray drying technology, continuous stirred-tank reactor (CSTR), etc. to achieve large-scale production of nanopreparations. Polymyxin B liposomes using microfluidic technology. By precisely controlling the fluid flow rate and mixing ratio, the particle size distribution (PDI 0.15–0.20) of pilot-scale products (10 L) is not significantly different from that of laboratory samples (PDI 0.12–0.18), and the drug encapsulation efficiency is > 90%; spray drying technology can achieve rapid drying of nanoparticles, which is suitable for the production of heat-sensitive drugs.

(2) Optimization and verification of production processes: Optimize production process parameters (such as ultrasonic power, emulsification time, material ratio) through orthogonal experiments, response surface methodology, etc. to ensure the stability of product quality. For example, Elfadil et al optimized the preparation process of polymyxin B nanoparticles through response surface methodology, determining the optimal ultrasonic power (300 W), emulsification time (10 min), and material ratio (1:5), so that the difference in drug encapsulation efficiency between batches is < 5%; in addition, process verification (such as equipment verification, method verification) is required to ensure the reliability and repeatability of the process.

Improvement of Formulation Stability

(1) Formulation optimization to enhance stability: Add stabilizers (such as sucrose, mannitol, BSA), adjust pH (such as pH 6.0–7.0), and control ionic strength (such as adding 0.9% NaCl) to reduce nanoparticle aggregation and drug leakage. For example, Elfadil et al added sucrose (mass ratio 1:1) to the formulation of polymyxin B nanoparticles. Sucrose can form a glassy structure to inhibit nanoparticle aggregation, reducing the drug leakage rate to below 5% after 6 months of storage at 4°C; BSA can adsorb on the surface of nanoparticles to form a protective film, reducing drug leakage.

(2) Application of advanced packaging technologies: Adopt vacuum freeze-drying, inert gas packaging, light-proof packaging, and other technologies to extend the storage period of the preparations. For example, polymyxin B liposomes adopt vacuum freeze-drying technology to remove water, avoid microbial contamination and drug degradation. After 12 months of storage at 4°C, the particle size and drug encapsulation efficiency of the rehydrated product have no significant changes; inert gas (such as nitrogen) packaging can prevent drug oxidation.

Establishment of Quality Control Standards

Determination of key quality indicators and standardization of detection methods: Referring to the relevant guidelines for nano-drugs in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia, United States Pharmacopeia, and European Pharmacopoeia, combined with the characteristics of polymyxin B, the following key quality indicators are determined:

(1) Particle size and zeta potential: Detected by dynamic light scattering (DLS). It is recommended that the particle size be controlled at 50–150 nm (conducive to in vivo circulation and tissue penetration), and the Zeta potential be controlled at −20 mV to +10 mV (reducing aggregation and non-specific binding);

(2) Drug encapsulation efficiency and drug-loading capacity: Detected by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), dialysis method, or ultracentrifugation method. The drug encapsulation efficiency should be ≥ 80% (ensuring efficacy and reducing free drug toxicity), and the drug-loading capacity should be ≥ 5% (reducing carrier dosage and lowering carrier-related toxicity);

(3) In vitro release rate: Detected by dialysis bag method or flow-through cell method. The 24-hour release rate in the acidic infected microenvironment (pH 5.5–6.5) is ≥ 80%, and the 24-hour release rate in the normal physiological environment (pH 7.4) is ≤ 30%;

(4) Microbial limit and sterility: Detected by membrane filtration method. It should meet the requirements of sterile preparations (such as bacterial and fungal counts < 10 CFU/mL, no pathogenic bacteria);

(5) Purity and impurities: Detected by HPLC or mass spectrometry. The purity of polymyxin B should be ≥ 98%, and impurities (such as degradation products, carrier residues) should be ≤ 0.1%.71,73

Standardization of in vivo PK/PD Evaluation Indicators

Detect PK parameters such as blood concentration-time curve (AUC), peak concentration (Cmax), half-life (t1/2), and clearance rate (CL), as well as PD parameters such as bacterial clearance rate and drug concentration at the infection site in animal models, establish a PK/PD model, and determine the optimal dosage and administration frequency.

Optimization of Preclinical Animal Models

(1) Establishment of chronic infection models: Construct chronic infection models using diabetic mice, cystic fibrosis mice, etc. to simulate the infection status of clinical patients. Diabetes in mice through streptozotocin, then constructed a chronic foot infection model by injecting MDR bacteria into the foot. In this model, bacteria form biofilms and the host immune function is low, which is consistent with the characteristics of foot infections in clinical diabetic patients; the cystic fibrosis mouse model forms chronic pulmonary infections through tracheal instillation of MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa, simulating the infection status of clinical cystic fibrosis patients.

(2) Application of immunocompetent animal models: Avoid overestimating the efficacy of preparations using immunodeficient animal models, and use immunocompetent animals to evaluate the efficacy and safety of preparations. For example, in a normal rat model of pulmonary infection, the bioavailability and efficacy of polymyxin B nanoparticles are evaluated. The results show that the pulmonary drug concentration is 30% lower than that in the immunodeficient model, which is closer to clinical reality; in addition, the efficacy should be verified in different species of animals to provide reference for clinical research.

(3) Construction of combined pathological models: Construct animal models of infection combined with liver and kidney function injury to evaluate the safety and efficacy of preparations in special populations. For example, construct a rat model of renal insufficiency through unilateral nephrectomy, then infect with MDR bacteria to evaluate the nephrotoxicity and efficacy of polymyxin B nanoparticles. The results show that the nephrotoxicity in the renal insufficiency model is 70% lower than that of free drugs; in addition, elderly animal models should be constructed to evaluate the applicability of preparations in elderly patients.

Stepwise Promotion Strategy for Clinical Translation

(1) Phase I clinical trials: Conducted in healthy volunteers, focusing on evaluating the safety (such as nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity, allergic reactions) and PK parameters (such as AUC, Cmax, t1/2, CL) of the preparation, and determining the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) and recommended phase II dose (RP2D). For example, a dose-escalation design can be adopted, starting from a low dose, gradually increasing the dose, and monitoring adverse reactions; in addition, the PK differences of the preparation in volunteers of different genders and ages should be evaluated.131

(2) Phase II clinical trials: Conducted in a small number of MDR bacterial infection patients (such as 100–200 cases), adopting a randomized, controlled design (such as using free polymyxin B as a control), evaluating the efficacy (such as bacterial clearance rate, clinical symptom improvement rate, length of hospital stay) and safety of different doses, and determining the optimal administration regimen (such as dose, administration frequency, course of treatment). For example, in CRE infection patients, evaluate the bacterial clearance rate of polymyxin B nanoparticles. If the clearance rate is increased by more than 30% compared with the control group, it indicates clinical value; in addition, the efficacy of the preparation in different infection types (such as pneumonia, sepsis, urinary tract infections) should be evaluated.132

(3) Phase III clinical trials: Conducted in a large number of patients (such as more than 1000 cases), further verifying the efficacy and safety of the preparation, evaluating the risks of long-term use, and providing sufficient evidence for drug registration. For example, in a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, controlled trial, compare the clinical cure rate, mortality rate, and adverse reaction rate of polymyxin B nanoparticles with free polymyxin B. In addition, drug-drug interaction studies should be carried out to evaluate the interaction between the preparation and other drugs (such as nephrotoxic drugs, neurotoxic drugs).

Conclusion and Perspective

Through the innovative design and functional optimization of carrier materials, polymyxin B nanopreparations have shown great potential in reducing toxic and side effects, enhancing antibacterial activity, and reversing bacterial resistance, providing a new strategy for the treatment of MDR Gram-negative bacterial infections. This review systematically summarizes the design principles and research progress of polymyxin B nanopreparations, deeply analyzes the bottlenecks encountered in their clinical translation, such as biosafety, targeting efficiency, production process, and drug resistance prevention and control, and proposes targeted solutions combined with a number of latest research findings, including the screening and modification of biocompatible carriers, the construction of intelligent responsive targeting systems, the establishment of standardized production processes and quality control systems, and the implementation of multi-dimensional drug resistance prevention and control strategies.

Future research directions can focus on the following aspects: (1) Development of intelligent responsive nanopreparations: Combining multiple characteristics of the infected microenvironment (such as pH, ROS, enzymes), designing “multiple responsive” release systems to further improve targeting precision; (2) Exploration of personalized nanopreparations: Customizing exclusive preparations through microfluidic technology according to the patient’s infection type, drug-resistant bacteria species, and liver and kidney function to achieve “precision medicine”; (3) Integration of combined technologies: Combining nanopreparations with phage therapy, immunotherapy, CRISPR-Cas9 technology, etc. to achieve the synergistic effect of “antibacterial-immune regulation-gene editing” and improve treatment effects; (4) Acceleration of clinical translation: Strengthening industry-university-research cooperation, promoting polymyxin B nanopreparations to enter the clinical trial stage, accumulating human PK/PD data and clinical efficacy data, and realizing clinical application at an early date.

It is believed that with the continuous breakthrough of technology and the deepening of clinical research, polymyxin B nanopreparations will become an important means for the treatment of MDR bacterial infections in the near future, making important contributions to overcoming the global public health problem of drug-resistant bacterial infections.
