Smartphone addiction as utility-reducing consumption: evidence from university students in Bangladesh
Authors: Apu Chandra Das
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe rapid increase in smartphone usage among university students has raised concerns about problematic use and its implications for mental well-being. While most existing studies adopt psychological perspectives, limited research interprets excessive smartphone use through an economic lens. Drawing on theories of rational addiction and time allocation, excessive smartphone use can be conceptualized as a form of utility-reducing consumption, where time spent on smartphones displaces welfare-enhancing activities such as physical activity and social interaction. This study aims to examine the determinants of smartphone addiction within a behavioral economic framework, focusing on its association with mental well-being and time-use-related behaviors among university students in Bangladesh.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 500 university students in Bangladesh using a structured online questionnaire. Smartphone addiction risk was measured using the Smartphone Addiction Scale–Short Version (SAS-SV), while mental well-being and self-esteem were assessed using the WHO-5 and Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale. Binary logistic regression models were estimated to identify factors associated with high-risk smartphone addiction.ResultsApproximately 54% of respondents were classified as high risk for smartphone addiction. Low mental well-being was significantly associated with higher odds of smartphone addiction (OR = 1.99, 95% CI: 1.14–4.79).ConclusionThe findings suggest that smartphone addiction can be interpreted as a utility-reducing behavior associated with diminished mental well-being within a time allocation framework. Behavioral factors appear more relevant than demographic characteristics in explaining problematic smartphone use. However, due to the cross-sectional design, causal relationships cannot be established.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28246-8.Keywords: Smartphone addiction, Rational addiction, Behavioral economics, Mental health and human capital, Time allocation

Introduction

The widespread use of smartphones around the world changes the way we communicate, access information, and spend our time every day of our lives, especially university students. Research shows that smartphones, despite their proven utility, can have detrimental effects when overused—lower-wellbeing, less sleep and lower academic performance. Several empirical studies have repeatedly demonstrated consistent relations between problematic smartphone use and the mental health issues such as depression, anxiety, and lower self-esteem [7, 11]. The results suggest that too much smartphone use may be symptomatic not just of increased technology availability but even of greater behavioral and psychological ills.

Low- and middle-income countries like Bangladesh have witnessed a rapid expansion of the digital sphere and increased smartphone access among students, while institutional mental health support is weak. Prevalence of problematic smartphone use and association with mental health, self-esteem and behavioral coping mechanisms are found to be high among previous studies conducted in Bangladesh [4, 5]. Nonetheless, the majority of studies continue to be anchored in psychological perspectives, exploring prevalence and correlates but not specifically discussing the overarching welfare implications associated with excessive smartphone use.

Behavioral economics can explain excessive smartphone use as utility-decreasing consumption. Utility, in standard economic theory, is the value an individual receives from consumption and time allocation. This stuff becomes utility-negative if extreme use generates short-term gratification while amortizing overall quality of life downward because it displaces time from better options. Based on the theory of time allocation [1], people are constrained within a fixed amount of time and must divide their time between competing activities. More time spent on smartphones therefore reduce welfare-increasing activities like physical activity and face-to-face social interaction. On the one hand, rational addiction theory [2] and behavioral models of self-control [9] O’Donoghue & Rabin, 1999) propose overconsumption of addictive goods based on present bias and boundedly rational willpower leading to short-term utility maximization at the expense of long-term life satisfaction.

In this context, the main variables under consideration in this research can be considered as proxies for utility-reducing consumption. Mental well-being represents the outcome domain of utility; poorer mental well-being indicates lower level of overall welfare. Physical inactivity and social isolation are alternative usage of time that are normally welfare-lowering, while displacement effects from heavy smartphone use may be represented by a decreased desire to participate in these activities. Mental human capital, which includes self-esteem (Grossman, 1972), determines decision-making and the tendency toward addictive actions. Collectively, these factors serve as an empirical proxy for examining the link between overuse of smartphone technology and decreases in individual welfare generated through psychological consequences and mechanisms relating to time allocation.

While these theoretical discussions are insightful, there is scarce empirical evidence, especially in the context of Bangladesh, that combines the theory of behavioral economics with the observed phenomena of smartphone addiction. The vast majority of prior research focuses on these variables separately rather than through a single lens that integrates psychological outcomes with time-use trade-offs. Such a gap restricts smartphone addiction as a socially relevant behavioral phenomenon with welfare implications.

Therefore, the specific objective of this study is to examine whether smartphone addiction among university students in Bangladesh is associated with indicators of utility-reducing consumption, particularly mental well-being and time allocation variables such as physical activity and social interaction, using a behavioral economic framework. To achieve this, the study employs cross-sectional survey data and estimates multivariate logistic regression models to analyze the relationship between smartphone addiction risk and selected psychological, behavioral, and socio-demographic factors.

Literature review

Problematic smartphone use and psychological correlates

The rapid rise in smartphone usage has led to extensive research on excessive smartphone use, or “Problematic Smartphone Use”. Unsurprisingly, these studies have been primarily among university students. Early work mainly addressed questions like what proportion of students use smartphones a lot. It found that higher rates of depression, anxiety, stress, and lower performance, regardless of subject, were all strongly associated with heavy smartphone use [11]. Subsequent reviews showed that a hallmark of mobile phone addiction across different countries and regions is poor or deteriorating mental health, whereas personality traits such as age and gender show little correlation [7].

More recent empirical investigations suggest that “PSU” encapsulates broader behavioral regularities and coping strategies to deal with self-regulation difficulties, rather than limited exposure to modern technology (Elhai et al. 2017; Busch & McCarthy 2021). When they are under psychological distress, some people may find e-communication gives them short-term relief. This is the sort of account that is supported by recent findings in both developed and developing societies: around the world, mental health instability emerges as a major predictor for smartphone addiction.

Behavioral economic perspectives on addictive digital consumption

From an economic perspective, problematic smartphone use at least potentially reflects behavior framed by two contemporary behavioral paradigms: inter-temporal decision-making and constrained self-control. Rational addiction theory [2] offers one basic model in which current consumption of an addictive good is a function of past consumption and the future expected utility that depends on it. Complementary insights from time allocation theory [1] indicate that individuals can allocate only a limited amount of time to competing activities, suggesting that additional time spent on smartphones will crowd out some productive and health-promoting behaviors.

In these models, behavioral extensions emphasize how present bias and self-control problems shape consumption choices [9] O’Donoghue & Rabin 1999). Overconsumption of smartphones is likely motivated by a pattern of short-term utility maximization that comes at long-term well-being costs. Moreover, the cross-sectional nature of this kind of research--as in the present study--means these frameworks are better seen as concepts that guide researchers. Forward-looking parameters of addiction, such as discount rates and addiction capital, are not usually found in survey data. This limits our ability to make dynamic inferences about how addictive processes might evolve empirically.

Mental health, self-esteem, and behavioral vulnerability

A growing body of literature points out the central role of mental health and self-esteem in shaping vulnerability to addiction. According to empirical research, individuals with poor mental health or low self-esteem are more prone to be addicted to smartphones [4, 5]. Such relationships tend to be explained in terms of coping behavior, in which people use smartphones to relieve stress, loneliness, or negative emotional states.

From an economic perspective, mental health can be viewed as a form of human capital that affects both current satisfaction and future productivity (Grossman 1972). With lower mental well-being, an individual is likely to have lower self-control and more impulsive decision-making driven (to a greater extent) by craving.

However, although these explanations make sense in theory, it's still worth emphasizing that most empirical studies including this one establish connections rather than deliberate causes. Also, the association between mental health and smartphone use could conceivably work in either direction (Busch & McCarthy 2021).

Role of physical activity and social interaction

However, recent studies show that alternative use of time also shapes smartphone behavior. Beyond psychological factors, physical activity and face-to-face social interaction are often found to be negatively associated with problematic smartphone use, suggesting they may serve as substitutes for excessive digital engagement [10, 14]. These findings closely follow time allocation theory, which asserts that individuals allocate scarce time across activities. While empirical evidence indicates that individuals engaging in physical activity or social interaction on a more regular basis tend to report fewer problems with smartphone addiction, the strength as well as statistical significance of these relationships, Shogun/vary from one study to another. This suggests that behavioral substitution effects may play a role, but their magnitude could also depend on contextual factors and individual differences.

Evidence from Bangladesh

The booming mental health problems faced by young adults in Bangladesh are underpinned by recent studies on smartphone addiction. Spurred by rapid digitalization, this new field of study reports heavy incidence rates among university students and consistently points to psychological factors especially depression, anxiety, and low self-esteem as major correlations [4] (Islam et al.), [5]. There is also evidence suggesting that heavy smartphone use leads to disrupted sleep, lower academic engagement, and poor well-being.

Nonetheless, most research in Bangladesh proceeds from a psychological perspective, integrating economic interpretations of behavior only rarely, such as intertemporal choice or opportunity cost. In consequence, the broader behavioural mechanisms that promote heavy smartphone use remain largely unexplored in this context.

Research gap and contribution

When it comes to literature, there are two primary issues and shortfalls. The first of these is the lack of empirical (behavioral data). While some research in this area has begun to emerge, for example, see Bangladeshi studies on the topic of smart phone addiction that uses economic behavior theory as a foundation and aligns closely its theory with exactly the proper data have not been forthcoming. Secondly, very few studies take account of both the self-motivated factors and mental health, self-esteem, physical activity and social interaction in an integrated empirical framework. This makes it difficult to interpret the problem from one discipline's point of view only as a kind of behavioral pattern that reduces welfare in general. The present study closes this gap by examining the associations between smartphone addiction risk and key psychological, behavioral, and socio-demographic factors among university students in Bangladesh using cross-sectional data. Through framing our inquiry within the language of behavioral economics and being clear about for what constitutes theory based on observations or common belief but not necessarily dependent on data, this study provides a more integrated contextually grounded view of problem smartphone use in developing-country settings than extant literature.

Methodology

Research design and data sources

This study takes a quantitative cross-sectional research design and uses primary survey data from students at public universities in Bangladesh. Those students that this study targets are enrolled as undergraduate or graduate degree candidates in public universities.

Data were collected using a structured, anonymous online questionnaire administered through Google Forms. The survey link was distributed to students via social media platforms (e.g., Facebook groups, university networks) and peer-sharing mechanisms. Data collection was conducted during the academic year 2024. Participation was voluntary, and respondents provided informed consent before completing the survey. A non-probability convenience sampling approach was employed due to the absence of a comprehensive sampling frame of university students. The survey relied on voluntary participation and peer distribution. While this approach facilitates data collection in resource-constrained settings, it may limit the representativeness of the sample. Mandatory responses help reduce missing data, but they may introduce some response bias (e.g., satisficing or non-differentiation). Although the findings of this survey based on non-probability sampling may not represent all university students in Bangladesh, the results give some context for how serious an issue smartphone addiction may be. Consideration must be given when interpreting these results.

A cross-sectional design is appropriate for looking at something such as smartphone addiction aspects that mix the constructs of behavior, psychology, and sociology in a setting where no extensive longitudinal data are available.

Measurement of smartphone addiction

The dependent variable is the potential for smartphone addiction, as measured by the Smartphone Addiction Scale–Short Version (SAS-SV) as proposed by [8]. SAS-SV is a validated 10-item scale that is used in Asia as an international reference standard.

On a six-point Likert scale, the SAS-SV asks respondents to rate each of 10 items. The minimum and maximum summed scores for these questions range from 10 to 60: more points imply greater risk for addiction. The scale has been previously applied in the Bangladeshi context. For example, [6] used the SAS-SV to examine problematic smartphone use and subjective well-being among Bangladeshi youths, confirming its applicability in local settings.

According to established diagnosis criteria, males scoring ≥31 and females ≥33 were classified as being at high risk of smartphone addiction. Those below these cut-offs scored low risk. Respondents scoring below these thresholds were classified as low risk. This binary classification is consistent with prior studies and enables the application of logistic regression analysis. In the current sample the Cronbach ′s alpha of the SAS-SV scale is 0.87 and it thus shows good internal consistency.

Measurement of psychological and behavioral variables

Mental well-being and self-esteem mental well-being

Mental well-being was measured using the WHO-5 Well-Being Index, a widely validated instrument assessing subjective psychological well-being. The scale consists of five items rated on a six-point Likert scale. Raw scores are summed and transformed to a scale ranging from 0 to 100, with scores below 50 indicating poor mental well-being.

The WHO-5 has demonstrated strong reliability and validity across different populations and countries [13]. In the Bangladeshi context, the Bangla version of the WHO-5 has also been validated, showing satisfactory internal consistency and construct validity [3]. Following standard practice, scores below 50 were used to indicate low mental well-being. Self-esteem was assessed using the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES), a widely used 10-item instrument measuring global self-worth [12]. Responses are recorded on a four-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 0 to 30. Based on established thresholds, respondents were categorized into low and normal self-esteem groups.

Both instruments have been extensively validated in prior research. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha values were 0.82 for WHO-5 and 0.79 for RSES, indicating good internal consistency. For empirical analysis, both variables were dichotomized following established cut-offs to facilitate interpretation in logistic regression models.

Physical activity and social interaction

Physical activity was measured according to the frequency of exercise outside organized sports and divided into three categories: (i) 0–1 time per week, (ii) 2–3 times per week and (iii) more than 3 times per week.

Social interaction was gauged using the number of days per week respondents are spending time with friends outside academic activities and categorized in the same way. Expressed in concrete terms, these two categories are proxies for alternative time use which capture behavior associated with the consumption patterns inherent to smartphones in any allocation framework. For empirical approaches, the variables of mental wellness and self-esteem were dichotomized at established and common clinical and research cut-points. This method allows for results to be interpretable in a public health context and is comparable to earlier studies that have used similar cut-points. However, dichotomization can result in loss of information and statistical power. To deal with this issue, we performed sensitivity analyses using alternative specifications, including continuous versions of these variables. Results were qualitatively similar when using alternative forms of the categorization, indicating that conclusions drawn from analyses are not contingent on choice of categorical form.

Robustness checks

Several robustness checks were performed to evaluate the stability of the estimated associations. First, alternative model specifications were estimated by sequentially introducing key psychological variables. Second, sensitivity analyses were conducted using alternative cut-off thresholds and continuous specifications of psychological variables to assess the impact of dichotomization. Third, subgroup analyses were performed across sex and levels of physical activity to explore potential heterogeneity. Across all specifications, the direction and statistical significance of the main findings remained consistent, supporting the robustness of the results.

Statistical analysis

A few of the basic logistic regression assumptions were examined. First, note that because the survey is at the individual level, we assume independence of observations. Second, multicollinearity in explanatory variables was diagnosed according to Variance Inflation Factors (VIF), showing all values lower than the usually accepted limit, which confirms serious concerns of multicollinearity. That said, continuity of interpretation in logit for continuous predictors is a concern and was considered where possible; our key psychological variables are categorized using psychometrically justified cut-offs, which alleviates this problem. Lastly, while the cross-sectional nature of the data precludes causal interpretation, potential endogeneity between psychological variables and smartphone addiction cannot be completely excluded. Consequently, the estimated associations should be considered correlational rather than causal. Descriptive statistics summarize participant characteristics, and group differences are assessed using Pearson’s chi-square tests. Variables included in the multivariate model were selected primarily based on theoretical relevance and prior empirical literature. The normality of continuous variables (e.g., mental well-being and self-esteem scores prior to categorization) was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Multicolinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with values below 10 indicating no serious multicolinearity. Model fit was assessed with the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and Nagelkerke’s pseudo R2. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS (version 29), and a p-value of <5% was considered statistically significant.

Mental well-being is the most consistent moderator of smartphone addiction risk across both specifications. The fully adjusted model shows that the odds of being classified as high risk are higher among students with low well-being than among those with normal well-being, and this relationship remained statistically significant after adjusting for all covariates. Low self-esteem is also positively associated with smartphone addiction risk, although the effect size is smaller than the previous factors and less precise in the fully-adjusted model. Age and gender do not display statistically significant ties in either specification, suggesting that these demographic groups within the sample are not strongly differentiated in terms of smartphone addiction risk. Alternative models that include seasonality and incidence capture time-use patterns consistent with the lower observed risk. Higher social interaction and physical activity levels are negatively associated with the odds of smartphone addiction but fail to achieve statistical significance in the fully adjusted model. The associations of living arrangement and student employment status are no longer stable when controlling for psychological variables in the model. It suggests that the relationships observed over these variables are sensitive to model specification. Finally, The goodness-of-fit of the adjusted logistic regression model was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. The test indicated that the model fits the data adequately (χ2 = 6.87, p = 0.55), suggesting no significant difference between observed and predicted values. Furthermore, the Nagelkerke’s pseudo R2 value was 0.21, indicating that approximately 21% of the variation in smartphone addiction risk is explained by the variables included in the model.

Thus, the results show strong associations between psychological characteristics and smartphone addiction risk; however, due to the cross-sectional nature of this study, these should be interpreted as correlational.

Robustness checks

A series of sensitivity analyses is conducted to assess the robustness of the main findings. First, alternative model specifications that add psychological variables in stages before full adjustment obtain qualitatively similar results. Second, using more stringent cut-off thresholds for the SAS-SV scale does not significantly change the direction or statistical significance of the key coefficients. Third, subgroup analyses by gender and stratifying smartphone addiction risk by levels of physical activity show that the association remained relatively stable across groups.

Overall, the main patterns of the baseline model remain, and robustness tests suggest that the estimated associations are not sensitive to model specification or sample composition. In particular, the association between lower mental well-being and higher odds of smartphone addiction risk is consistent across specifications.

Nonetheless, these results should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects, given the cross-sectional nature of the data.

Discussion

In the multivariate analysis, only mental well-being showed a significant association with smartphone addiction even after adjusting all the selected variables. In particular, the likelihood of smartphone addiction among students with low mental well-being was significantly greater than that among students with normal mental well-being. The results, therefore, bring into focus psychological health as a major predictor of smartphone addiction for university students in Bangladesh.

From a behavioral economics perspective, this result can be understood under rational addiction theory and self-control models. According to rational addiction theory, it is possible that individuals engage in an addictive behavior when past consumption decreases current utility (Gary [2]. In contrast, behavioral models focus on present-biased tastes and bounded willpower (David [9] Matthew Rabin 1999). For this reason, students who are not in a good state of mental well-being may be more susceptible to the immediate gratification which excessive smartphone use provides, and subsequently re-inforce addictive behaviors. However, we do not directly estimate forward-looking behavior or addiction dynamics, and therefore these two frameworks simply represent interpretive lenses and not mechanisms that we empirically test.

Self-esteem, quality of life, physical activity, and social interaction were associated with smartphone addiction in crude analyses, but these associations were no longer statistically significant after adjustment. This indicates that the possible mechanisms through which they operate may involve mental well-being, or more likely mental well-being may be a confounding variable. Notably, the attenuation of the self-esteem effect in the adjusted model suggests that mental-wellbeing captures more general psychological vulnerability (beyond self-perception alone), which corroborates findings in the literature [4, 7].

Likewise, socio-demographic factors, such as age and gender, were not significantly associated with smartphone addiction in the multi-variant model. In other words, psychological determinants might be more influential than the most fundamental socio demographic factors in accounting for maladaptive smartphone use, in accordance with previous studies [4, 7]. Overall, the findings emphasize that mental well-being is the most robust predictor of smartphone addiction in this study population. Interventions aimed at reducing problematic smartphone use should therefore prioritize mental health support and psychological well-being among university students.

However, several limitations should be considered. Due to the cross-sectional design, causal relationships cannot be established. Additionally, all variables were self-reported, which may introduce reporting bias. Finally, the sample was limited to public university students, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. In addition to its limitations, this study has several notable strengths. First, the analysis employs a multivariate framework that controls for a range of socio-demographic, behavioral, and psychological factors, allowing for a more robust assessment of the independent association between mental well-being and smartphone addiction. Second, the study integrates insights from behavioral economics, including rational addiction theory and self-control models, providing a theoretically grounded interpretation of the findings rather than a purely descriptive analysis. Third, the focus on university students in Bangladesh contributes to the limited empirical literature on smartphone addiction in low- and middle-income country contexts, offering context-specific evidence that is often underrepresented in existing research. Finally, the inclusion of multiple lifestyle and psychosocial variables enables a more comprehensive understanding of smartphone use behavior, helping to identify mental well-being as the most consistent predictor after adjustment.

Policy implications

The results indicate that treatment for problematic smartphone use might not be purely a technological or lifestyle concern; rather, it should be considered a behavioral issue with likely consequences for individual well-being and academic functioning. From an economic perspective, excessive smartphone use may correlate with decreased well-being and possibly longer-term impacts on human capital accumulation (Grossman, 1972; [11]. However, these interpretations must be interpreted within the limitations of the study’s cross-sectional design.

First, the high correlation between mental well-being and risk of smartphone addiction indicates that interventions targeting general mental health may be relevant in this context. Policies aimed at bolstering students’ psychological health counseling services, stress-management programs, and early screening for anxiety and depression may also correlate with healthier behavior in digital spaces. These enhance behavioral economic models that assert that consumers respond less to savings products if they feel restricted and are encouraged by the presence of self-control and present-biased preferences [9] O’Donoghue & Rabin, 1999), although the current study does not directly assess these mechanisms.

First, the findings suggest that certain non-digital activities are associated with differences in smartphone use for physical activity and social interaction. Cyber psychology, Behavior, and Social Networking: Structured Physical Activity, Engagement with the Campus Environment, and Social Interaction Opportunities: Methods to Promote Balanced Use of Time. These approaches are consistent with time allocation theory [1] and may be most applicable in resource-constrained contexts such as Bangladesh [14].

Third, the results indicate that individuals should be cautious when using restrictive approaches to smartphone use. Although limiting accessibility or screen time might be an option, broader behavioral and psychological variables are also associated with smartphone use patterns, according to the results. Supportive interventions for example, digital well-being tools, feedback on usage patterns, or proactive self-regulation techniques may thus complement more traditional policy-driven initiatives [8].

Finally, from a macroeconomic perspective, increases in student well-being could also be linked to longer-term improvements associated with human capital and productivity. As university students are an important part of tomorrow's workforce, policies promoting mental well-being and healthy digital participation can also pay off on a larger societal level. Yet, this warrants further longitudinal investigation to better characterize these associations.

Limitations

There are number of limitations that should be taken into account for interpretation of results from this study. First, the non-probability nature of the sampling and online survey due to COVID-19 may reduce generalizability with respect to the overall results that could be expected in university students across Bangladesh. Additionally, social media and peer-sharing recruitment is likely to create a selection bias whereby students with greater online engagement were overrepresented.

Second, cross-sectional data by nature cannot establish an inferent causal chain and offer little insight into the direction of those associations. Thus, the identified relationships must be interpreted as correlational but not ultimately causal.

Third, all measures were conducted with self-reported data so they are subject to potential biases in responses (i.e. recall bias and social desirability bias). While validated tools were used to increase reliability of self-report measures, it is impossible to eliminate these biases.

Lastly, the use of dichotomized versions of the WHO-5 Well-Being Index and Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale may have led to a potential information and statistical power loss when using continuous measures.

Conclusion

Using a behavioral economic approach based on rational addiction, time allocation, and human capital frameworks, this study investigates smartphone addiction risk among university students in Bangladesh. Using cross-sectional survey data gathered from 500 students and multivariate logistic regression, the analysis suggests that lower levels of mental well-being and lower self-esteem are consistently associated with a higher risk for smartphone addiction, while basic socio-demographic characteristics show little variation across groups. A lower observed risk is associated with time spent in physical activity and social interaction, consistent with alternative uses of time.

Interpreted through a behavioral economics lens, these findings could suggest increased susceptibility for short-term digital activities among low well-being participants. Nonetheless, the current work does not directly observe inter-temporal preferences, discount rates, or addiction capital. Thus, the theoretical frameworks are applied as interpretive lenses rather than empirically tested mechanisms.

The robustness of the associations across alternative specifications, outcome definitions, and subsample analyses suggests that the identified associations are not sensitive to model specification or sample composition. However, because this study was cross-sectional, the results should be interpreted as correlational and do not indicate causal relationships. These relationships may be bidirectional and could be confounded by unobservables.

Policy-wise, these results may indicate that smartphone addiction risk is associated with broader psychological and behavioral factors. Interventions that support student well-being and are associated with balanced time use like mental health services, physical activity, and social engagement may be pertinent in this regard. In a broader perspective, the study adds to the literature by expanding primarily psychological evidence into the behavioral economics concept in the context of low- to middle-income countries.

Future research might use longitudinal or experimental designs to investigate the dynamic processes of smartphone use and the potential long-term implications for well-being and human capital outcomes more accurately.
