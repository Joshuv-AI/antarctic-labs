Complement system-related gene signatures implicated in the recurrence of glioma
Authors: Yanqi Sun, Xiaozhang Bao, Yuheng Yang, Peinan Zhang, Nan Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlioma represents the most common primary malignant tumor of the central nervous system. The complement system, as a key component of the tumor microenvironment (TME), participates in tumor progression by mediating inflammatory responses and immune regulation. However, the specific mechanisms whereby complement system-related genes drive glioma recurrence remain unclear, and robust molecular biomarkers for recurrence prediction are absent. Therefore, this study aims to explore how these genes facilitate glioma progression and recurrence, and to construct a gene signature for recurrence risk prediction.MethodsBy analyzing data from public databases Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) and Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) and employing differential expression analysis, univariate Cox regression, multivariate Cox regression, and Bioinformatic regression analyses, we identified six core genes (CFI, DIABLO, DLGAP5, FANCL, FCER2, TLR2) and constructed a recurrence risk score model. Furthermore, we externally validated the risk score model using recurrence-free survival (RFS) data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database to assess its predictive performance.ResultsPatients in the high-risk group exhibited significantly shorter overall survival (OS). At the mechanistic level, the expression of core genes was potentially closely associated with the C5-C5AR1 complement axis and M2 macrophage infiltration. The observed increase in immune cell infiltration, upregulation of immune checkpoints, and enhanced chemoresistance in the high-risk group suggest that the core genes may promote tumor progression by modulating an immunosuppressive microenvironment via the complement pathway. Analysis of chemotherapeutic drug sensitivity further revealed a more pronounced drug-resistant phenotype in the high-risk group. The prognostic performance of the model was validated using a nomogram, which demonstrated strong predictive ability.ConclusionsThis study provides a novel complement system-related gene-based predictive model for glioma recurrence, which may provide a potential biomarker for prognosis assessment and personalized treatment, as well as new insights into the role of the complement system in the immune microenvironment of glioma.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Based on Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) cohort 1, this study constructed a glioma recurrence risk prediction model of complement system-related genes (CSRGs) containing six hub genes: CFI, DIABLO, DLGAP5, FANCL, FCER2, and TLR2. The model exhibited reliable predictive performance in CGGA cohort 2 and The Cancer Genome Atlas-recurrence-free survival cohort, stratifying patients into high- and low-risk subgroups with distinct immune infiltration, immune checkpoint expression and chemosensitivity. Mechanistic analyses revealed these hub genes may remodel immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment via the C5/C5AR1 axis and M2 macrophage polarization. A nomogram combining risk scores and clinicopathological features supported individualized prognostic evaluation. This work identifies candidate biomarkers for glioma stratification and precision therapy, and offers a novel perspective on complement-mediated glioma regulation.

What is known and what is new?

• Glioma is a highly aggressive brain tumor characterized by a pronounced immunosuppressive microenvironment. Existing molecular subtypes have limited utility predicting patient recurrence and response to immunotherapy. As a key regulatory module of the tumor immune microenvironment, CSRGs remain underexplored regarding glioma patient prognosis.

• This study constructed a glioma recurrence risk model based on CSRGs. The model correlated with prognosis, immune landscape, tumor microenvironment and chemosensitivity. Furthermore, C5AR1-related pathways may drive M2 macrophage-mediated immunosuppressive microenvironment. These findings provide new directions to explore complement-mediated mechanisms in glioma progression and develop personalized therapeutic strategies.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The glioma recurrence risk model identifies potential prognostic biomarkers for glioma patients. Furthermore, the uncovered C5AR1-related pathway may drive M2 macrophage-mediated immunosuppressive microenvironment, guiding immunotherapy design.

• This model necessitates experimental validation and further prospective clinical validation to establish its clinical utility.

Introduction

Glioma is the most common primary malignant tumor of the central nervous system (CNS) and originates from glial cells or their precursors. Major subtypes include glioblastoma (GBM), astrocytoma, and oligodendroglioma (1). According to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification system, gliomas are graded by malignancy into lower-grade gliomas (LGG) (WHO grades 1–2) and higher-grade gliomas (HGG) (WHO grade 3–4), with WHO grade 4 gliomas designated as GBM (2). These tumors are characterized by high invasiveness, rapid proliferation, and frequent and extensive infiltration. Despite current treatment modalities, including surgical resection, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, the 5-year survival rate remains approximately 6%, with a median overall survival (OS) of approximately 14–20 months (3). A high propensity for recurrence is a prominent hallmark of glioma (4), driven by multiple factors such as the extent of tumor infiltration, status of the tumor microenvironment (TME), and genetic alterations (5-7).

Among these drivers, the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) plays a critical role in mediating therapeutic resistance and recurrence. As a key component of TIME, the complement system is essential for mediating inflammatory responses, eliminating pathogens, and clearing dead cells and cellular debris. Accumulating evidence indicates that pathological activation of the complement system within the TIME promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion of glioma cells by regulating inflammatory responses and immunosuppressive stromal cells (8-10).

However, the correlation between complement system-related genes (CSRGs) and the prognosis of patients with glioma, particularly those with recurrent disease, remains insufficiently explored. In this study, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data and the corresponding clinical information of patients with glioma were retrieved from public databases. Bioinformatic approaches were employed to analyze the associations of CSRGs with OS and treatment sensitivity in patients with glioma, and their potential relationship with immune cell infiltration was further investigated. Therefore, the objective of this study was to develop and externally validate a CSRG signature for predicting OS in patients with glioma. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1-0374/rc).

Methods

Cohorts and CSRG selection

The messenger RNA (mRNA) expression data for normal brain tissue were obtained from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project (https://www.gtexportal.org/). A glioma cohort of 693 cases was retrieved from the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) database (http://www.cgga.org.cn/) and used for screening differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and constructing a prediction model. An independent cohort of 325 cases was also obtained from the CGGA database for external validation. Data preprocessing was conducted as follows: samples with missing survival or critical clinical information were excluded from the analysis. Gene identifiers were converted to their corresponding official gene symbols, and genes with no expression across all samples were discarded. Following the exclusion of cases with incomplete clinical information, the resulting cohorts comprised 516 cases and 291 cases, designated as CGGA cohort 1 and CGGA cohort 2, respectively.

CSRGs were acquired from the Human Biological Pathway Unification database (https://pathcards.genecards.org/) and the HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee database (https://www.genenames.org) using the keyword “complement”. The selection criteria were as follows: (I) the gene name or official annotation included the term “complement”; (II) manual curation confirmed the gene’s involvement in complement activation, regulation, or receptor function. Duplicate gene symbols were eliminated, gene symbols were standardized, and non-protein-coding genes were excluded from consideration, yielding a final list of 257 CSRGs; (III) furthermore, the ComBat function from the sva R package was employed to correct for batch effects between the GTEx cohort and CGGA cohort 1, using the data source as a batch variable; (IV) the final sample size satisfied the statistical criterion of at least 10 events per predictor variable, which is essential for constructing multivariable prognostic models in oncology research, thereby ensuring the robustness of the subsequent regression and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analyses. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Differential expression analysis

DEGs between the GTEx cohort and CGGA cohort 1 were identified using limma R package. Only genes with an adjusted P<0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| >0.585 (absolute fold change >1.5) were defined as DEGs.

Screening of prognosis-related complement genes

Based on the GTEx normal brain cohort and CGGA cohort 1, we established six differential expression comparison groups: normal brain vs. primary low-grade glioma (pLGG), normal brain vs. recurrent low-grade glioma (rLGG), normal brain vs. primary GBM (pGBM), normal brain vs. recurrent GBM (rGBM), low-grade glioma (LGG) vs. rLGG, and GBM vs. rGBM. Genes exhibiting consistent expression patterns across at least four of these groups were defined as DEGs. Candidate genes were then obtained by intersecting these consensus DEGs with CSRGs.

To assess the relationship between the candidate genes and OS of glioma patients, a univariate Cox regression analysis was conducted using the survival R package. Following this, multivariate Cox regression was performed to elucidate the independent associations of the identified genes and clinical features with OS. Only those genes that achieved a significance level of P<0.05 in the multivariate model were recognized as independent prognostic factors and were selected for subsequent model construction.

Construction and validation of the recurrence risk model

The independent prognostic complement-related genes identified through univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were incorporated into a LASSO Cox regression model to further refine the gene subset for optimal predictive performance while mitigating the risk of overfitting. The glmnet and survival R packages were employed for this analysis, with the optimal penalty parameter λ determined via 10-fold cross-validation, whereby the minimum lambda (lambda.min) was selected. Genes exhibiting non-zero regression coefficients were retained for the construction of the recurrence risk score model.

The risk scores for each patient in CGGA cohorts 1 and 2 were subsequently calculated. Patients were categorized into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median risk score as the cutoff point. Kaplan-Meier (K-M) survival analysis, accompanied by log-rank tests, was employed to assess survival differences between these groups. Additionally, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were utilized to evaluate the predictive performance of the risk score model.

Validation of the model for recurrence‑free survival (RFS) in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) glioma

To further validate the predictive ability of the model for disease progression, we obtained transcriptomic data for GBM and LGG from TCGA database, followed by gene annotation. The risk score for each sample was calculated using the previously established formula, and the relationship between risk scores and survival time was visualized through sample distribution plots. RFS was selected as the study endpoint, defined as the duration from the date of diagnosis to the first occurrence of disease recurrence or death from any cause. The median risk score served as the cutoff to stratify patients into high- and low-risk groups.

K-M curves were generated to compare RFS between these stratified groups, and the significance of the differences was evaluated. Furthermore, a ROC curve was constructed based on risk scores and RFS status to assess the predictive capability of the risk score for the prognostic status of GBM patients at 1-, 3-, and 5-year intervals. Additionally, patients were stratified for each of the six core genes into high- and low-expression groups based on the median expression values. K-M curves were employed to compare RFS between these groups, and ROC curves were constructed for assessing their prognostic predictive ability.

Analysis of expression correlation among core genes

To elucidate the regulatory relationships among the core genes, we utilized the R package “corrplot” to compute and visualize the expression correlations among the six core genes.

Evaluation of potential therapeutic targets of the genes in risk model based on single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA)

To further explore whether the genes in our risk model could serve as potential therapeutic targets, we used ssGSEA implemented in GSVA R package to examine the relationship between gene expression and cell proliferation. The gene set “CELL_PROLIFERATION_GO_0008283,” which contains genes involved in the cell proliferation pathway, was downloaded from GSEA database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/).

Stromal and immune score determination for the glioma microenvironment

We used “ESTIMATE” R package to estimate the immune, stromal, and ESTIMATE scores for each patient in the two CGGA cohorts. A higher ESTIMATE score indicated lower tumor purity.

Immune infiltration and functional analysis

The CIBERSORT R package (version 0.1.0) was used to estimate the infiltration levels of 22 immune cell subtypes in all samples. Differences in immune infiltration between the high- and low-risk groups were evaluated for statistical significance, and the results were visualized using boxplots. Additionally, based on the 29 immune cell marker genes reported by He et al. (11), ssGSEA was performed to assess intergroup differences in immune response-related functions.

Evaluation of response to immunotherapy based on the recurrence risk score

We analyzed the relationship between the recurrence risk score and common immune checkpoints using limma R package. We also analyzed the relationship between the genes in our recurrence risk model and the checkpoints. The IMvigor210 dataset was selected to verify the predictive power of the proposed model.

Investigation of complement pathway associations and immune evasion potential

First, we calculated the Spearman correlation coefficients between the six core genes and complement‑related genes (C3, C5, C3AR1, C5AR1) and visualized the results as correlation heatmaps. We further used the linkET and ggplot2 R packages to visualize the correlations among C5, C5AR1, and the six core genes.

Second, we computed the Hallmark complement pathway enrichment score for each sample using the ssGSEA algorithm. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the association between TLR2 expression and this pathway score. We then compared complement pathway activity between high‑ and low‑TLR2 expression groups using an independent samples t‑test, with P<0.05 considered statistically significant.

Third, we employed the CIBERSORT algorithm to quantify immune cell infiltration levels and to explore their associations with the core genes. Focusing on M2 macrophages, we retrieved six M2 marker genes (MRC1, CSF1R, ARG1, CD68, VSIG4, CD163) from the CellMarker 2.0 database (12). Spearman correlation analysis was performed using the corrplot R package to calculate and visualize the correlations between the six core genes and these M2 markers.

Finally, we extracted M2 macrophage infiltration data from the CIBERSORT output. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to analyze the correlation between M2 infiltration level and the expression of C5AR1. Independent samples t‑tests were also used to compare M2 infiltration levels and C5AR1 expression between the high‑ and low‑risk groups.

Chemotherapy sensitivity analysis

R package “OncoPredict” is designed to predict in vivo or cancer patient drug responses and biomarkers using cell line screening data from Maeser et al. (13). We used it to predict the response of patients with glioma to chemotherapy drugs based on gene expression and to calculate the sensitivity value for each patient.

Nomogram analysis of risk score with clinicopathological characteristics

The nomogram was constructed by incorporating all relevant clinical characteristics and recurrence risk scores to predict OS at 1, 3, and 5 years in patients with GBM from the CGGA dataset. The concordance index (C-index) was used to evaluate the discriminative capacity of the nomograms. A calibration curve approaching the 45-degree diagonal line indicated excellent predictive accuracy. Finally, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to assess the predictive capability of the nomogram for all clinical characteristics according to the nomogram score for each patient.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.4.3. A two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. For survival analyses, univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to evaluate the prognostic significance of each clinical factor, with hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. The significance of regression coefficients was assessed using the likelihood ratio test, which yields a chi‑square (χ2) statistic. Kaplan-Meier curves were constructed to compare survival distributions between groups, and the differences were tested using the log‑rank test. To examine the association between patient subgroups and clinical factors in the heatmap, the chi‑square test was used for categorical variables. For correlation analyses, Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was employed. For comparisons between two independent groups, Student's t‑test was applied for normally distributed continuous variables; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U test was used as a non‑parametric alternative.

Construction of the recurrence risk score system

After performing LASSO regression analysis, the six core genes (CFI, DIABLO, DLGAP5, FANCL, FCER2, and TLR2) were screened (Figure 2A). Based on their corresponding λ values (Figure 2B), the recurrence risk score for each patient in CGGA cohorts 1 and 2 was calculated using the formula: risk score = 0.0474 × Exp (CFI) − 0.0586 × Exp (DIABLO) + 0.2253 × Exp (DLGAP5) + 0.0991 × Exp (FANCL) − 0.0022 × Exp (FCER2) + 0.0846 × Exp (TLR2). Patients were classified into high- and low-risk groups (Figure 2C,2D). As shown in Figure 2C,2D, patients in the high-risk group had a higher incidence of death and shorter survival times in the two CGGA cohorts. Similarly, in the K-M analysis, patients in the high-risk groups had a significantly worse OS than those in the low-risk groups, and the ROC curves suggested that the classifier may have superior predictive ability (Figure 2E,2F). The regression coefficients of the six core genes (CFI, DIABLO, DLGAP5, FANCL, FCER2, TLR2) derived from LASSO regression were applied to calculate the individual recurrence risk score, which was the core parameter for the prognostic model construction. Furthermore, we identified the recurrence risk score model as an independent prognostic factor using univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses (Table 2). As shown in Figure 2G, in glioma, CFI, DIABLO, DLGAP5, FANCL, and TLR2 were positively correlated with each other. In contrast, FCER2 was negatively correlated with DLGAP5 and FANCL, but showed no significant correlation with CFI, DIABLO, or TLR2.

Validation of the recurrence risk model in the TCGA cohort

In the TCGA glioma cohort, patients in the high-risk group exhibited significantly shorter RFS compared to those in the low-risk group (P<0.001). Time-dependent ROC curves indicated that the risk score demonstrated favorable predictive performance for 1-, 3-, and 5-year RFS (Figure 3A). Moreover, patients in the high-risk group experienced a higher mortality rate and shorter OS (Figure 3B).

Survival analyses of the six individual core genes revealed that high expression levels of CFI, DIABLO, DLGAP5, FANCL, and TLR2 were significantly associated with shorter RFS (P<0.05), while FCER2 did not show significant prognostic relevance in this cohort (P>0.05). Collectively, these findings validate the favorable predictive performance of the recurrence risk model initially constructed using the CGGA cohort in the external TCGA validation cohort.

Using “ESTIMATE” R package, we acquired the stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores between high-risk and low-risk groups in CGGA cohorts 1 and 2. The results showed that all three indicators were significantly higher in the high-risk group than in the low-risk group (Figure 6A,6C). Differential immune cell infiltration profiles between high- and low-risk groups in CGGA cohorts

Using the CIBERSORT algorithm, we quantitatively assessed the infiltration proportions of 22 immune cell subsets. In both CGGA cohort 1 and cohort 2, the infiltration levels of most immune cell subsets were significantly higher in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group (Figure 6B,6D). Furthermore, ssGSEA analysis indicated that the enrichment scores for immune-related functions were generally elevated in the high-risk group relative to the low-risk group (Figure 6E,6F).

Evaluation of response to immunotherapy based on recurrence risk score

As shown in Figure 6E,6F, in the two CGGA cohorts, the expression of key factors in the immune response process was higher in the high-risk group than in the low-risk group. Further analysis of the association between the recurrence risk score and common immune checkpoints showed that the recurrence risk score positively correlated with SIGLEC7, PDCD1LG2, HAVCR2, LILRB1, LILRB2, and LILRB4 (Figure 7A). Independent analysis of the association between six genes and immune checkpoints showed that all six genes were closely related to immune checkpoints (Figure 7B). The IMvigor210 dataset, which includes patients with bladder cancer, was then selected, and these patients were divided into low- and high-risk groups. The K-M curves of the survival analysis showed that the model had a significant difference in prognosis between the high- and low-risk score groups for bladder cancer (P<0.05) (Figure 7C). Analysis of immunotherapy effect data in the database revealed that the treatment group had lower risk scores than the untreated group (Figure 7D). Therefore, our recurrence risk model has potential as a reference for predicting tumor responses to immunotherapy.

Correlation analysis between the six core genes and complement-related genes revealed that C5 and C5AR1 were significantly correlated with all six genes (TLR2, FCER2, FANCL, DLGAP5, DIABLO, CFI) (P<0.05; Figure 8A,8C), suggesting that these core genes may be linked to the C5/C5AR1 complement axis. Furthermore, in both CGGA cohort 1 and cohort 2, TLR2 expression levels exhibited a significant positive correlation with the overall activity of the complement pathway (P<0.001; Figure S1).

Further analysis of immune escape-related immune cells indicated that the expression levels of CFI, TLR2, DLGAP5, and FANCL were positively correlated with M2-type macrophages and dendritic cells (DCs), while showing negative correlations with naive B cells, follicular helper T cells, and naive CD4+ T cells. In contrast, DIABLO and FCER2 displayed the opposite expression patterns (Figure 8B). Focusing on M2-type macrophage markers, correlation analysis demonstrated that CFI, TLR2, DLGAP5, and FANCL were positively correlated with these markers, while FCER2 was negatively correlated. The correlation for DIABLO was not statistically significant (Figure 8D).

In the overall cohort, Spearman correlation analysis confirmed a positive correlation between M2-type macrophage infiltration and the expression of C5AR1 (P<0.001; Figure 8E). Collectively, these findings suggest that C5AR1 may drive macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype in high-risk gliomas, contributing to the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment that facilitates tumor progression and recurrence.

Discussion

As the most common primary brain tumor of the CNS, gliomas feature a TME composed of heterogeneous components, including tumor cells, immune cells, and the extracellular matrix (14). Interactions between these components can accelerate tumor development, progression, and immune escape (15,16). The current standard treatment for gliomas is surgery combined with TMZ chemotherapy and radiotherapy (17,18). However, owing to the development of TMZ resistance, patient prognosis remains unsatisfactory (19). Furthermore, the invasive growth pattern of gliomas and critical functional structures often limit the extent of surgical resection, making complete eradication of the lesion difficult. This leads to a poor patient prognosis and a high risk of postoperative recurrence (20). Therefore, identifying reliable predictive biomarkers for recurrence and prognosis may be of great significance for guiding precise medical interventions.

The glioma microenvironment is a dynamically complex ecosystem centered around tumor cells, consisting of various cellular components (such as immune and glial cells) and non-cellular components (such as cytokines and complements) that interact with tumor cells. These complex interactions influence tumor cell growth, invasion, and resistance (21). The complement system, as a critical regulatory module within the TIME, is increasingly recognized for its role in signaling pathways mediating the malignant progression of glioma. Existing studies have demonstrated that the complement system may play a critical regulatory role in the progression of lung cancer, influencing tumor initiation and development through mechanisms such as modulating local inflammatory responses, activating intracellular complement pathways, and enhancing tumor cell drug resistance (22). In the field of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) research, further evidence confirms that complement system activation may drive the transformation of the TME toward a tumor-promoting phenotype via the generation of anaphylatoxins such as C3a and C5a, thereby creating favorable conditions for the proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of HCC cells (23). Given the increasingly recognized importance of the complement system in tumors and their immune microenvironment, this study developed a recurrence risk signature for glioma based on six complement-related genes (CFI, DIABLO, DLGAP5, FANCL, FCER2, and TLR2). To further address the specific relevance of recurrence, the robustness of the model was evaluated using RFS data from the external TCGA cohort. In this external validation cohort, patients stratified into the high-risk group exhibited significantly shorter RFS compared with the low-risk group, thereby supporting the model’s utility in predicting glioma recurrence risk.

Complement factor I (CFI) is an inhibitor of the complement system and is involved in complement activation pathways (24). CFI can cleave C3b and C4b through the participation of cofactors, thereby exerting an immunomodulatory effect by inhibiting excessive activation of the complement system (25). Hallam et al. found that CFI expression is significantly upregulated in glioma and that its high expression predicts poor patient prognosis, suggesting that CFI could serve as a potential predictive biomarker for glioma. DLGAP5 is a product of a cell cycle regulatory gene that promotes microtubule polymerization and spindle formation (26). As an effector of the Ran GTPase, DLGAP5 is involved in regulating the stability of mitotic kinetochore fibers (27). Chen et al. confirmed that expression of DLGAP5 is upregulated in gliomas. Patients with high DLGAP5 expression have lower survival rates, and silencing DLGAP5 inhibits glioma cell proliferation (28). FANCL is a Fanconi anemia complementation group (FANC) gene. Its mono-ubiquitination facilitates the formation of FANCI-FANCD2 nuclear foci, which recruit numerous Fanconi anemia proteins involved in homologous recombination repair. These proteins participate in processes, such as DNA incision, translesion synthesis, and interstrand cross-link (ICL) removal (29). Some studies have suggested that FANCL mutations contribute to tumorigenesis in patients with non-Fanconi anemia cancer (30,31). TLR2 is a pattern recognition receptor for pathogen-associated molecular patterns and is capable of initiating innate immunity and bridging innate and adaptive immunity (32). Wang et al. demonstrated that TLR2 activation upregulates the expression of matrix metalloproteinases 2 and 9 (MMP2 and MMP9), thereby promoting glioma invasion (33).

DIABLO was found to be a protective factor in this model. It relieves the inhibitory effects of multiple inhibitors of apoptosis protein (IAP) family members and is thus considered a key regulator of apoptosis in mammalian cells (34). Pandey et al. confirmed that DIABLO directly interacts with mitochondrial phosphatidylserine decarboxylase and inhibits cancer cell proliferation by regulating phospholipid metabolism and phosphatidylethanolamine synthesis (35). FCER2 is another protective gene expressed in various cell types, including monocytes/macrophages, eosinophils, follicular DCs, intestinal epithelial cells, and B cells. FCER2 can enhance immune function by upregulating IL-4 receptor expression in human B cells, promoting IL-4 signaling, and favoring class switching to increase IgE synthesis (36).

Using Cox regression analysis, K-M curves, and ROC curves, we suggested that the recurrence risk score model was an independent prognostic factor. Stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores revealed that patients in the high-risk group had lower tumor purity and a greater proportion of stromal components within their TME. Immune infiltration analysis revealed significant differences in the proportions of immune cells between the high- and low-risk groups.

We also employed the CIBERSORT algorithm to systematically evaluate the tumor immune infiltration landscape. The analysis revealed that the expression levels of six core genes (TLR2, FCER2, FANCL, DLGAP5, DIABLO, and CFI) were significantly and positively correlated with the infiltration of M2 macrophages. These findings suggest that these genes may be involved in establishing an immunosuppressive glioma microenvironment.

We further analyzed the association between these six core genes and key complement genes (C3, C5, C3AR1, and C5AR1) and observed that they all exhibited a statistically significant correlation (P<0.05) with C5 and mostly correlated with C5AR1 expression. Several studies have further confirmed that in glioma, elevated C5AR1 marks higher grade and shorter survival; the C5-C5AR1 axis may drive immune evasion by polarizing macrophages to an immunosuppressive state and disabling T cells, fueling progression and chemoresistance (37). Furthermore, M2 macrophages have been confirmed to be key factors in shaping the immunosuppressive microenvironment and promoting GBM progression. Considering their potential role in M2 macrophage infiltration and the tumor-promoting function of the C5a/C5AR1 axis, we speculated that the six core genes might participate in the regulation of the glioma immune microenvironment indirectly by modulating the C5a/C5AR1 complement pathway.

The six genes identified in this study are highly associated with glioma cell immunity, invasion, growth, or apoptosis. A recurrence risk model was developed based on these genes. This model significantly correlated with the prognosis of patient with glioma, immune landscape, TIME, and chemotherapy sensitivity. Furthermore, this study proposes a complementary system-related gene signature that may provide new insights and references for clinical treatment. However, there are several limitations in this study. First, additional biological experiments are required to validate the accuracy of the model. Second, although the risk model was externally validated using RFS data from the TCGA cohort, all data were retrospectively collected from public databases; prospective clinical validation in independent, multi-center cohorts is still needed. Finally, despite the validation, some systematic errors were inevitable.

Conclusions

In summary, this study identified six complement-related hub genes linked to glioma prognosis and established a recurrence prediction model validated in CGGA and TCGA cohorts. These genes may drive immunosuppressive microenvironment formation via the C5-C5AR1 axis and M2 macrophage polarization. A nomogram based on this signature enables personalized prognosis evaluation. Further experiments and multicenter cohorts will clarify gene functions and confirm the model’s translational value. This signature offers novel theoretical support for glioma clinical treatment.
