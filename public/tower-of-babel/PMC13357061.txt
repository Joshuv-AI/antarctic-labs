Do we need to adapt the American Joint Committee on Cancer staging system after neo-adjuvant treatment in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma?
Authors: Christina T. Muijs, Gursah Kats-Ugurlu
Published: 2026

The prognosis of patients with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) is determined not only by their primary tumor stage but also by the degree of response to neo-adjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) (1). Independent of pretreatment stage, patients who achieve a complete pathologic response (pCR) demonstrate a markedly better prognosis than those with incomplete or non-response (1,2). This is why, the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) classification system included post-neoadjuvant treatment stage grouping for esophageal cancer (3). Nevertheless, accurate prognostic stratification after neo-adjuvant treatment remains challenging (4-6).

Both the AJCC 8th edition and the Japanese Classification 12th edition show substantial stage overlap when used for survival stratification after nCRT in ESCC. Survival crossovers have been demonstrated between stages II and III using the AJCC 8th edition, whereas crossovers between stages I and II as well as between stages III and IV were observed with the Japanese Classification 12th edition (7). These observations underscored the need for an improved post-neoadjuvant staging.

Recently, Park et al. (8) developed a revised version of the yp-stage classification for ESCC patients treated with nCRT followed by esophagectomy, demonstrating superior prognostic performance compared with the AJCC 8th edition. Using recursive partitioning with a tree-structured regression approach, the authors identified four distinct yp-stages. Major distinctions relative to the AJCC staging included classifying complete responders (ypT0N0) exclusively as stage I disease, whereas the AJCC system grouped ypT1-2N0 in stage I as well. In addition, the system proposed by Park et al. categorizes tumors with limited nodal involvement (ypT0–2N1) as stage II and classifies all ypT4 tumors as stage IV. Together, this new post-neoadjuvant treatment classification system seems to outperform the AJCC system for ESCC patients, reflected by a higher C-index (66 vs. 62%) and favorable event reclassification [net reclassification improvement for events (NRIe) ~0.17] (8), suggesting better differentiation between the different stages, especially stage II and III.

Notably, the AJCC 8th edition (3) does not differentiate between esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) and ESCC in the post-neoadjuvant setting, despite well-established differences in tumor biology, treatment response, and clinical outcomes between these histologic subtypes. Accumulating evidence indicates that pCR rates, recurrence patterns, and survival outcomes differ substantially by histologic subtype. A systematic review by Gaber et al. (9) showed that ESCC patients achieve higher pCR rates than those with EAC (approximately 32% vs. 22%), while pCR is consistently associated with significantly better overall survival (OS). Histology-specific refinement of staging systems may therefore be an important step.

Partial or mixed response is more difficult; limited nodal involvement in otherwise well-responding tumors may reflect more favorable tumor biology as compared to partial or non-responders with persistent nodal involvement. Zhao et al. (10) reported worse 5-year OS in ESCC patients with ypT+N+ disease compared with ypT0N+ disease (29.8% vs. 57.3%), while patients with ypT+N0 disease demonstrated a 5-year OS of 63.8% (10). These findings seem to be in line with the re-classification by Park et al. (8). Unfortunately, Zhao et al. did not differentiated between ypN1, ypN2, and ypN3 disease. Similar limitations were present in the study by Yun et al. (11), who stratified ESCC patients after neoadjuvant treatment into three groups based on tumor response and nodal status without further nodal subclassification. Further support for refined nodal stratification comes from Miyata et al. (12), who evaluated the prognostic impact of pathologic lymph node status after nCRT and surgical resection. Survival differed significantly among patients with no positive lymph nodes, 1 positive node and 2–4 positive lymph nodes (3-year survival rate, 57.1% vs. 40.5% vs. 17.6%, P<0.01). There was no significant difference in survival between patients with 2–4 positive lymph nodes and 5 or more positive nodes (12). These findings further support the newly proposed staging system by Park et al. (8).

However, when interpretating the clinical implications of this newly proposed yp-stage system, the treatment context in which it was developed should also be considered. The development cohort primarily received nCRT consisting of 5-fluorouracil and cisplatin combined with radiotherapy, later adapted to a hypofractionated schedule, while the validation cohort received oxaliplatin combined with titanium silicate-1 alongside a radiation dose of 46 Gy. These regimens differ substantially from the CROSS protocol (13), which is widely used in Western practice and consists of weekly carboplatin and paclitaxel administered concurrently with 41.4 Gy radiotherapy. Differences in chemotherapy backbone, systemic exposure, and radiation dose may influence treatment response patterns. Consequently, the distribution of post-treatment ypT and ypN categories, and therefore the prognostic performance of a staging system, may be partly influenced by the specific neoadjuvant regimen employed. Therefore, validation of the proposed staging system in ESCC patients treated with alternative nCRT regimens, including the CROSS regimen, would be highly valuable. Such validation could help determine the generalizability of the classification across different therapeutic strategies, thereby supporting broader clinical implementation.

Accurate post-nCRT risk stratification of oncologic outcomes is essential for reliable prognostic counseling and may also inform the design of clinical trials, particularly those evaluating adjuvant treatment strategies, such as immunotherapy. Nevertheless, several limitations warrant consideration. The work by Park et al. (8) focused exclusively on Korean populations, using a development cohort from the Samsung Medical Center and a validation cohort from the Asan Medical Center. While ESCC predominates in Asia, the generalizability of these findings to Western populations remains uncertain. Furthermore, the long study period [1994–2018] encompasses substantial evolution in treatment strategies, including advances in radiotherapy techniques, which may have influenced oncologic outcomes.

In conclusion, the revised yp-stage classification proposed by Park et al. (8) represents a meaningful contribution toward improved prognostic stratification in ESCC patients, following nCRT. The study highlights the importance of response-adapted staging and histology subtype-specific risk assessment. Future external validation in contemporary, non-Asian cohorts, particularly in patients treated with CROSS-based nCRT, will be essential to determine its broader applicability and potential integration into future international staging systems.
