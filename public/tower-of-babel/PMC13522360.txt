Reference Percentiles for Macular Vessel Density by OCT Angiography and Their Association with Systemic Diseases in Chinese Adults
Authors: Xiaoxuan Zhu, Kai Yang, Jinxia Yan, Renjie Zhang, Wenya Chen, Mingye Yang, Wei Lian, Ziyu Li, Shasha Ding, Zhenqing Wang, Yudie Wang, Lulin Lou, Wanru Zhao, Yingjin Li, Yunguang Zhang, Jia Qu, Fan Lu, Lele Cui, Ming Li

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo establish macular vessel density reference percentiles stratified by age, sex, and axial length (AL) and investigate their association with systemic diseases.DesignCommunity-based, cross-sectional study.ParticipantsA total of 6879 participants aged 20–60 years were recruited from the Jidong community. Among them, 3247 healthy individuals enrolled from August 2019 to January 2023 were included in the reference sample, and 774 eligible participants recruited between May and October 2023 comprised the analysis sample.MethodsAll participants underwent OCT angiography (OCTA). Based on reference sample, we modeled the distribution of parafoveal superficial capillary plexus (SCP) and deep capillary plexus (DCP) vessel density and derived age-, sex-, and AL-specific reference percentiles using generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape. In the analysis sample, parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density measurements were transformed into adjusted z-scores. We then examined the associations of systemic diseases with adjusted z-scores and the likelihood of vessel density below the 25th or 10th percentile.Main Outcome MeasuresReference percentiles for parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density and their associations with systemic diseases.ResultsIn the reference sample (mean [standard deviation] age, 40.39 [8.47] years; 1384 [42.62%] men), we established continuous reference percentiles for parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density stratified by age, sex, and AL. In the analysis sample (mean [standard deviation] age, 40.39 [9.72] years; 395 [51.03%] males), diabetes was associated with lower parafoveal DCP vessel density z-scores (β, –0.56; 95% confidence interval, –0.86 to –0.25; P < 0.001) after adjustment for age, sex, AL, education, current smoking, current drinking, and body mass index. Furthermore, diabetes was associated with an increased risk of reduced DCP vessel density, both at the <25th percentile and <10th percentile.ConclusionsThis study established continuous, age-, sex-, and AL-adjusted reference percentiles for parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density in healthy Chinese adults using the Optovue OCTA device, and highlighted the association between diabetes and reduced parafoveal DCP vessel density. The reference percentiles present a promising framework for the individualized interpretation of macular vessel density, while external validation across other populations and OCTA platforms is warranted before broader clinical application.Financial Disclosure(s)The author has no/the authors have no proprietary or commercial interest in any materials discussed in this article.Keywords: Optical coherence tomography angiography, Macular vessel density, Superficial capillary plexus, Deep capillary plexus, Systemic diseases

Retinal OCT angiography (OCTA) is a well-established noninvasive, high-resolution imaging technique for visualizing the microvasculature of retina.1 Recently, OCTA has been widely used for the assessment of many ocular and systemic vascular diseases, such as diabetic retinopathy, glaucoma, and diabetes.2, 3, 4 Unlike traditional fundus photography, OCTA can detect early-stage microvascular impairments and automatically provide quantitative metrics, such as the macular vessel density in the superficial capillary plexus (SCP) and deep capillary plexus (DCP), which are effective for evaluating capillary-level lesions. Many OCTA studies have revealed reduced macular vessel density in patients with retinal vascular diseases, particularly in those with diabetic retinopathy, and this reduction is also associated with worsened disease severity.5, 6, 7 Furthermore, multiple investigations have reported that diabetes patients without diabetic retinopathy exhibit decreased macular vessel density.8, 9, 10 Thus, owing to its strong correlation with pathological processes, as well as its advantages in terms of time efficiency, accessibility, and high repeatability,11 macular vessel density is emerging as a potential biomarker for predicting, diagnosing, and monitoring various retinal and systemic vascular diseases.

Accurate interpretation and evaluation of a given macular vessel density value relies mainly on comparisons with the normative reference percentiles obtained from individuals without diseases. Several previous OCTA studies have demonstrated the normative database of macular vessel density in adults from hospital-based and cohort-based populations,12, 13, 14 However, limited population-based studies have reported normative data in healthy individuals without common systemic diseases, such as hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases, all of which may potentially impact OCTA measurements.3 Furthermore, OCTA measurements have been reported to vary by age, sex, and potential ocular biometry such as axial length (AL).15, 16, 17 Notably, a recent comprehensive review of 156 OCTA studies further demonstrated a general age-related decline in macular vessel density across adult population.18 Yet existing studies on normative datasets have primarily focused on reporting average vessel density and its associations with these covariates, rather than providing continuous percentile-based reference distributions that allow direct interpretation of an individual measurement relative to age-, sex-, and AL-matched healthy peers. Therefore, new reference percentiles for macular vessel density derived from a large healthy population are needed.

In this study, we aimed to develop continuous age-, sex-, and AL-specific reference percentiles for parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density in healthy Chinese adults and investigate the associations of systemic diseases with adjusted parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density z-scores and the likelihood of having parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density below the 25th or 10th percentile.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This prospective, cross-sectional, community-based study utilized data from the Jidong Eye Cohort Study. The design and methodology of the Jidong Eye Cohort Study have been detailed previously.19,20 The study participants were recruited between August 2019 and October 2023 in the Jidong community (Tangshan city, northern China). All participants underwent detailed ophthalmic and physical examinations and biological sampling and completed comprehensive questionnaires. This study complied with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and obtained approval from the Ethics Committee of the Jidong Oil-Field Staff Hospital of the Chinese National Petroleum Corporation (NO. 2018 YILUNZI1) and the Eye Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (NO. 2021-074-K-63-01). Written informed consent was obtained from each study participant during recruitment.

The flow chart of the study design is shown in Figure 1. In our study, a total of 6879 participants aged 20 to 60 years underwent OCTA. After quality control, 6682 participants with high-quality OCTA scans were included and then divided into 2 samples: (1) a reference sample, consisting of individuals enrolled from August 2019 to January 2023 and (2) an analysis sample, comprising participants initially recruited from May 2023 to October 2023 who had no prior follow-up data. The 2 samples were separated by recruitment period rather than random allocation, allowing reference percentiles derived from earlier-recruited participants to be applied to later-recruited participants, closely resembling their application in clinical settings. For both samples, we excluded individuals with ocular disorder (e.g., diabetic retinopathy, glaucoma, and nystagmus), best-corrected visual acuity worse than 0.2 logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution, high intraocular pressure (>21 mmHg), or AL outside the 22–26 mm range or missing AL data. Additionally, individuals with systemic diseases (including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or cardiovascular diseases) were excluded only from the reference sample. In detail, among the 5658 participants enrolled during the earlier period, 3247 healthy individuals were ultimately included in the reference sample for modeling the distribution of parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density and deriving the adjusted reference percentiles. Of the remaining 1024 participants who were subsequently recruited, we included 774 eligible participants in the analysis sample to investigate the association of systemic diseases with parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density adjusted z-scores and percentiles. Details of the specific exclusion criteria and the corresponding numbers for each sample are presented in Figure 1.

Systemic Assessment

All the participants underwent standardized clinical examinations and biological sampling (including the collection of blood, urine, and tongue coating samples) during their interviews. Demographic characteristics, including lifestyle factors and medical history, were collected through interviewer-administered questionnaires.21 Anthropometric measurements, including body weight, height, systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure, were obtained from each participant. Body mass index was subsequently calculated. Fasting blood samples were collected and analyzed to determine the levels of fasting blood glucose, total triglyceride, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol. In this study, hypertension was defined as having a self-reported hypertension history, a systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg, or a diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg. Similarly, diabetes mellitus was defined based on either a self-reported diabetes history or a fasting blood glucose concentration ≥7.0 mmol/L. Cardiovascular diseases were defined based on self-reported medical history.

Ophthalmic Examination

A comprehensive ocular examination was performed for each participant. Best-corrected visual acuity was tested with a standard logarithmic visual acuity chart at a distance of 5 m from the participants. Noncycloplegic autorefraction was obtained using an autorefractor (KR800; Topcon). Refraction status was subsequently recorded as the spherical equivalent (the sphere and half the cylindrical power). Axial length and central corneal thickness were measured with a Lenstar 900 (Optical Biometer; Haag-Streit). Intraocular pressure was measured using a noncontact tonometer (TX-20; Canon). Examinations of the ocular anterior segment were performed using a slit lamp microscope. The conditions of the posterior segment were assessed with 45° digital fundus photography (CR2AF; Canon).

OCTA Measurement

Retinal microvascular imaging was conducted using a commercial spectral-domain OCTA device (RTVue XR Avanti incorporated with AngioVue software; version 2017.1.0.155; Optovue). A 3 × 3 mm2 macular scan (304×304 A-scans) was acquired per eye. Each OCTA scan was independently checked by 2 experienced clinicians (X.Z. and K.Y.) to exclude images with poor quality (i.e., a quality index below 7, decentralization, segmentation errors, or significant motion artifacts). The AngioVue software automatically segmented the retinal capillary plexus into SCP (internal limiting membrane to inner plexiform layer) and DCP slabs (inner plexiform layer to outer plexiform layer). The incorporated projection artifact removal algorithm automatically eliminates the 3-dimensional projection artifacts in the DCP slabs, thereby enhancing the image quality. The macular vessel density was automatically quantified as the proportion of perfused vascular area within regions of interest, and presented with the ETDRS grid, which comprises 2 concentric circles with 1-mm and 3-mm diameters. In this study, parafoveal measurements were selected as representative regions for the average macular vessel density. We utilized the right eye for analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as the mean (standard deviation), and categorical variables as the frequency (percentage). Independent t tests and chi-square tests were used for comparisons of continuous and categorical variables. For each sex, we employed the generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS) to model the distribution of parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density and determine its associations with age and AL based on the reference sample.22 Unlike linear or generalized linear models that estimate only the mean of the response variable, GAMLSS is a flexible statistical framework that allows the location, scale, skewness, and kurtosis of the response distribution to be modeled as smooth functions of covariates such as age and AL, thereby yielding accurate continuous reference percentiles. We evaluated several candidate distributions appropriate for continuous data, including the Normal, Box-Cox-Cole-Green, Box-Cox-t, and Box-Cox-power-exponential distributions. We ultimately determined the optimal distributions as the Box-Cox-Cole-Green distribution and used a cubic spline smoothing function to fit the parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density percentiles according to the Bayesian Information Criterion. Q tests and worm plots were used to evaluate the goodness-of-fit of the model. Finally, the sex-, age- and AL-adjusted percentiles (fifth, 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th, 90th, 95th) and z-score values (–2, –1, 0, 1, and 2) of parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density among adults were computed according to the optimal model, and the percentile curves were plotted.

Parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density measurements from the analysis sample were transformed into corresponding age-, sex-, and AL-adjusted z-scores according to the optimal model. We examined the association of systemic diseases with adjusted parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density z-scores using multivariable generalized linear models, and with the odds of having a parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density below the 25th or 10th percentile using multivariable logistic regression models. Model 1 was adjusted only for sex, age and AL, while Model 2 was further adjusted for education, current smoking status, current drinking status, and body mass index. Associations are presented as β or odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For all analyses, a P value <0.05 indicated statistical significance. Statistical analysis was performed with SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc) and R software (version 4.3.3; http://cran.r-project.org) with the gamlss package (version 5.4-22; https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/gamlss/index.html).

Results

Derivation of Adjusted Reference Percentile and Z-Score Values for Parafoveal SCP and DCP Vessel Density Based on the Reference Sample

We assessed 3247 healthy participants aged 20 to 60 years (mean [standard deviation] age: 40.39 [8.47] years; 1384 men [42.62%] and 1863 women [57.38%]) in the reference sample to derive the parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density reference percentiles and z-score values. The mean [standard deviation] parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel densities in males were 49.60 [3.13]% and 51.34 [3.10]%, respectively, both of which were lower than those in females (50.05 [2.91]% and 53.74 [2.96]%; all P < 0.001). The characteristics of these healthy participants and the distribution of parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density across age and AL subgroups are presented by sex in Tables S1 and S2, respectively (available at https://www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

The distribution of parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density and its associations with age and AL were independently modeled for men and women using the GAMLSS model. The derived equations for the parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density are shown in Table S3 (available at https://www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Our results revealed a nonlinear relationship between age and both parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel densities. In both sexes, parafoveal SCP vessel density remained stable until approximately age 40, after which it decreased (Fig 2), whereas parafoveal DCP vessel density stabilized until approximately age 35 before it underwent a more rapid decline (Fig 3). Furthermore, parafoveal SCP vessel density exhibited a nonlinear relationship with AL in both sexes, peaking at approximately 24.5 mm before a subtle decline (Fig S1, available at https://www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Parafoveal DCP vessel density was inversely associated with AL in both sexes (Fig S2, available at https://www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

Association of Systemic Diseases with a Parafoveal SCP and DCP Vessel Density Below the 25th or 10th Percentile

We defined reduced parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density in 2 thresholds based on the age-, sex-, and AL-adjusted percentiles: below the 25th percentile (<P25) and below the 10th percentile (<P10). When the <P25 threshold was used, diabetes was significantly associated with an increased risk of reduced parafoveal DCP vessel density even after full adjustment for potential confounding factors (odds ratio, 2.55; 95% CI, 1.40 to 4.66; P = 0.002). Similarly, diabetes was also associated with a relative risk of 2.75 (95% CI, 1.41 to 5.38; P = 0.003) for reduced parafoveal DCP vessel density at the <P10 threshold (Table 3). However, no significant association was observed between any systemic disease and reduced parafoveal SCP vessel density.

Sensitivity Analysis

We performed a sensitivity analysis to examine the specific association of diabetes by excluding participants with hypertension and cardiovascular diseases. Diabetes still remained significantly associated with both lower parafoveal DCP vessel density z-scores and an increased likelihood of reduced parafoveal DCP vessel density.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first community-based study to establish age-, sex-, and AL-adjusted reference percentile and z-score values for parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density using the Optovue OCTA device in healthy Chinese adults. Furthermore, our findings demonstrated that diabetes was significantly associated with a lower parafoveal DCP vessel density z-score and an increased risk of reduced parafoveal DCP vessel density. Our findings facilitate the standardized and individualized interpretation of OCTA metrics in clinical practice and highlight the potential role of macular vessel density as a biomarker for microvascular damage.

In our reference sample, the mean parafoveal vessel density measured using the Optovue OCTA device was 49.86 ± 3.01% in the SCP and 52.72 ± 3.24% in the DCP (Table S1). These findings differed slightly but remained comparable to those of previous studies using the same OCTA device. Teo et al12 reported slightly lower parafoveal SCP (48.0 ± 5.4%) and DCP (51.5 ± 4.2%) vessel density in Singapore population, whereas Abay et al14 reported marginally higher values for both parafoveal SCP (52.05 ± 4.62%) and DCP (54.78 ± 4.39%) in Turkey participants. The discrepancies across studies may be attributed to variations in OCTA analytical software or baseline population characteristics, including mean age, sex distribution, and ethnicity (Table S8, available at https://www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Notably, to minimize confounding effects, we derived normative values from participants without ocular disorders (e.g., diabetic retinopathy and glaucoma) or common systemic diseases (including diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular diseases) that might have influenced macular vessel density. Additionally, we developed reference percentiles that account for variations in age, sex, and AL, enabling more individualized interpretation of OCTA measurements.

Our findings revealed that compared with men, women presented greater parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density. Similarly, Teo et al12 observed that female sex was associated with higher global SCP and DCP vessel density. You et al13 reported significantly lower global SCP and DCP vessel density in males in a Hong Kong Chinese cohort, although this sex difference remained significant only for the SCP in the multivariable analysis. However, sex-related differences in macular vessel density remain inconclusive, with some studies reporting no significant differences (Table S8).23,24 These sex-specific differences we observed may be partly attributed to the effects of sex hormones. Estrogen is considered to promote vascular health, and its supplementation has been reported to improve ocular blood flow in postmenopausal women, whereas testosterone may increase vascular risk.17,25,26 Nevertheless, future studies are warranted to confirm the observed sex-specific differences and elucidate the underlying mechanism involved.

Our results demonstrated that parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density decreased nonlinearly with age in both sexes. The parafoveal SCP vessel density remained relatively stable until approximately 40 years of age before declining, while the parafoveal DCP vessel density began a more rapid decline at approximately 35 years. This trend aligned with our earlier findings in the Jidong Eye Cohort Study.17 A recent comprehensive review of 156 OCTA studies has demonstrated a general age-related reduction in macular vessel density across adult populations.18 However, the specific age at which the decline begins has varied across previous studies. Pujari et al15 observed that parafoveal SCP vessel density appeared to decrease from the fourth decade onward, although a statistically significant reduction was only observed from the fifth decade. Hashmani et al23 similarly reported the onset of parafoveal SCP vessel density decline at approximately 50 years of age. These discrepancies likely stem from variations in population characteristics, sample size, and OCTA acquisition and analysis protocols across studies (Table S8). This age-related capillary dropout may result from microvascular occlusion and atrophy,14 secondary to endothelial dysfunction and pericyte loss.27,28 The DCP vessel density appears more vulnerable than the SCP, likely due to its terminal vascular architecture and higher metabolic demands.29 However, future longitudinal studies are still needed to further validate and elucidate the age-related changes in parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density.

Our study further investigated the effect of AL on the distribution of parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density, which is critical for accurate clinical interpretation. We observed that parafoveal SCP vessel density exhibited a nonlinear relationship with AL, peaking at approximately 24.5 mm followed by a subtle decrease, whereas parafoveal DCP vessel density was inversely associated with AL, aligning with previous studies. Youssef et al30 reported lower parafovea SCP vessel density in eyes with an AL >24.5 mm, while Lin et al31 observed reduced overall SCP vessel density in moderate and high myopia patients compared to emmetropes. For parafoveal DCP vessel density, most studies have reported a significant inverse association with AL.30,32 However, several studies have found no association between AL and either SCP or DCP vessel density.33,34 These heterogeneous findings could be explained by variations in study design, such as differences in OCTA devices or acquisition protocols, sample sizes, population characteristics, or AL inclusion ranges (Table S8. We suggest that AL affects macular vessel density measurements via both lateral magnification effects and authentic biological changes secondary to ocular elongation.16,31,32 Future studies are warranted to validate the impact of AL on the distribution patterns of parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density with AL between 22 and 26 mm.

We found that diabetes was significantly associated with reduced parafoveal vessel density only in the DCP, consistent with most prior studies in diabetes patients without diabetic retinopathy.9,35 This reduction might be attributed to hyperglycemia-induced capillary loss, which is mediated by oxidative stress, inflammation, pericyte apoptosis, endothelial dysfunction, and leukostasis.36 However, we observed no association in the SCP, which contradicted several findings.8,37 These inconsistencies could be attributed to differences in diabetes duration and severity, as well as variations in OCTA analytical software versions, and scan protocols across studies (Table S8). The DCP appears more susceptible to early hyperglycemic-induced injury due to its unique characteristics, including higher metabolic demand, more complex vascular architecture, and its greater distance from larger arterioles.3 Furthermore, we observed no association between hypertension or cardiovascular diseases and macular vessel density, which was similar to previous studies.38, 39, 40 However, several studies reported reduced parafoveal DCP vessel density in patients with hypertension,41,42 and reduced nasal parafoveal SCP vessel density in those with cardiovascular diseases.43 This discrepancy may be explained by differences in diseases duration, stage, or the proportion of well-controlled patients, as well as variations in OCTA devices or acquisition protocols across studies. Future research would be required to further investigate their associations.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, as our study findings were exclusively derived from Chinese adult participants in the Jidong community, the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic populations may be limited. Future multicenter studies involving diverse ethnic groups are needed to validate and extend these findings. Second, the applicability of our reference percentile values may also be limited by OCTA device-specific characteristics, as OCTA measurements may vary across different manufacturers and software versions. The reference percentiles established from the Optovue OCTA platform, the most widely used device, require external validation on other OCTA systems, and future efforts to standardize imaging protocols and analytical methods will be crucial for reducing the interinstitutional variability. Third, our study only focused on parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density, and future studies are warranted to extend this framework to additional OCTA parameters such as foveal avascular zone area and vessel length density. Fourth, the 25th and 10th percentile thresholds used to define reduced macular vessel density were adopted for analytical purposes based on the distribution of this reference population, and should not be interpreted as cut points validated for clinical use. Prospective validation is warranted before adopting them as diagnostic definitions of abnormality. Additionally, the cross-sectional design of this study precludes the ability to elucidate the causal relationship between systemic diseases and reduced macular vessel density. Longitudinal studies are needed to investigate the temporal association between systemic diseases and progressive alterations in SCP and DCP vessel density. Finally, the medical history of neurological or neurodegenerative conditions such as Alzheimer disease was not collected at baseline and therefore not used as the exclusion criteria for the reference sample. However, the prevalence of these conditions is extremely low within our age range of 20 to 60 years,44 minimizing the potential impact of this limitation.

In conclusion, we provided age-, sex-, and AL-specific reference percentiles for parafoveal SCP and DCP vessel density measured using the Optovue OCTA device in healthy Chinese adults using GAMLSS statistical model. Using these reference data, we confirmed that diabetes was associated with an increased risk of reduced parafoveal DCP vessel density. The reference percentiles established in this study enable the individualized clinical interpretation of OCTA metrics, thereby improving early detection and monitoring of microvascular impairment. Further external validation across other populations and on different OCTA devices is warranted before broader clinical application.
