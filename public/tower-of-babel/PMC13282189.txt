Selective intraoperative cholangiography: A retrospective single-centre analysis compared with national data and implications for surgical training
Authors: Jens Schwarz, C. Reithmann, M. Rothe, H. Vogelsang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeIntraoperative cholangiography (IOC) can assist in visualising the biliary anatomy during laparoscopic cholecystectomy, particularly in cases with unclear anatomical conditions, and may thereby help to prevent complications such as bile duct injury. Despite its potential relevance, the method is infrequently used in many hospitals and is often not included in surgical training curricula. The aim of this study was to quantify the selective, on-demand use of IOC in a specialised visceral surgery centre, compare local data with national statistics, and interpret the findings in the context of international recommendations on surgical safety and education.Materials and methodsIn this retrospective analysis, all cholecystectomies performed at a visceral surgery centre between July 2015 and June 2023 were evaluated regarding the use and indications for IOC. In addition, national data from the Federal Statistical Office (2015–2021) were analysed to assess nationwide trends. The results were further interpreted in light of international literature and Delphi consensus statements on safe cholecystectomy.ResultsOf a total of 1,313 cholecystectomies, IOC was performed in 34 cases (2.6%), mainly to clarify biliary anatomy. The conversion rate in the centre was 1.4%, and the incidence of major bile duct injuries (≥ Neuhaus type B[1]) was 0.07%. On the national level, the utilisation of IOC showed a decreasing trend over the observation period.ConclusionOn-demand intraoperative cholangiography is a safe and well-established technique that can facilitate anatomical orientation in selected difficult cases. The present single-centre data suggest that it can be integrated into clinical practice as an intraoperative problem-solving tool. Despite clear international recommendations and high appraisal in methodological consensus processes, its use remains limited in clinical practice and insufficiently represented in surgical training. Given the continued relevance of bile duct injuries, systematic integration of selective IOC into surgical education programmes appears warranted.

Introduction

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) is one of the most frequently performed visceral surgical procedures in Germany, with more than 170,000 operations conducted annually [1]. Despite established safety measures—particularly the “Critical View of Safety” (CVS) [2]—bile duct injury (BDI) remains a clinically relevant complication, occurring more often in male patients, in the presence of inflammatory or atypical biliary anatomy, and after previous upper abdominal surgery [3, 4]. BDIs are associated with significant morbidity and socioeconomic burden; in the United States, the annual economic impact has been estimated to exceed one billion USD [5].

Over the past two decades, the standardisation of technique and improved training have substantially reduced BDI rates after LC (from 0.83% in 2000 to 0.11% in 2014), whereas rates in open procedures have remained at approximately 0.29% [1, 6]. Importantly, modern open cholecystectomy represents a negatively selected subset of complex cases, so the higher injury rate likely reflects case difficulty rather than inferiority of the open approach.

Despite these advances, intraoperative risks persist. Selective intraoperative cholangiography (IOC) can help to clarify biliary anatomy, detect stones or leaks, and support intraoperative decision-making in complex cases [7]. Historically performed routinely during both open and later laparoscopic cholecystectomy [8], IOC use shifted towards a selective approach [9]. Nevertheless, several studies have reported lower major injury rates with routine IOC [10]. Current utilisation varies considerably: only a minority of centres use IOC routinely, many apply it selectively, and others rarely perform it at all—limiting exposure during surgical training [11, 12]. International consensus statements identify IOC as a core technical skill and training-relevant competency for safe cholecystectomy [11, 13].

In contrast to international recommendations, the German S3 guideline does not recommend intraoperative cholangiography, either routinely or selectively, primarily due to limited evidence for a reduction in bile duct injury rates, as well as concerns regarding availability, expertise, and procedural effort [14]. A nationwide survey from 2001 already demonstrated marked heterogeneity in clinical practice: 6% of hospitals performed IOC routinely, 49% selectively, and 43% did not use any intraoperative imaging [15].

Against this background, the present study aimed to retrospectively analyse the selective use of IOC in a specialised visceral surgery centre, compare these findings with national Diagnosis-Related Groups (DRG) data, and interpret the results in the context of current guideline recommendations, patient safety, and surgical training.

Materials and methods

This study was designed as a retrospective single-centre data analysis. The objective was to systematically assess the use of selective IOC during cholecystectomy at a visceral surgery centre and to compare these findings with national DRG data on IOC utilisation. In addition, the results were interpreted in the context of international recommendations on surgical safety and training in hepatobiliary surgery.

All cholecystectomies performed between July 2015 and June 2023 were included. Data collection was carried out retrospectively using the hospital information system. Recorded parameters comprised the total number of cholecystectomies performed (laparoscopic, converted, or primarily open), the indication for surgery (elective or non-elective, defined as within 72 h of indication), the performance and indication for IOC (clarification of biliary anatomy, detection of bile leakage, or assessment of bile flow), operative time, conversion rate, and the occurrence of bile duct injuries, which were classified according to Neuhaus et al. [6]. Relevant bile duct injuries were defined as type B or higher, corresponding to injuries involving the extrahepatic bile ducts that typically require interventional or surgical management. In our institution, IOC was primarily performed on demand intraoperatively in cases of unclear biliary anatomy or suspected bile leakage rather than as part of a broader selective strategy for suspected choledocholithiasis. EUS and ERC were available on a 24/7 basis through close interdisciplinary collaboration with the gastroenterology department.

For contextualisation of the single-centre findings, nationwide data were retrieved from the German Diagnosis-Related Groups (DRG) hospital discharge database, the mandatory national registry maintained by the German Federal Statistical Office (Statistisches Bundesamt). This comprehensive dataset includes anonymised information on all inpatient cases in Germany, with diagnoses coded according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10-GM) and procedures recorded using the German Operation and Procedure Classification (OPS). Within this database, annual case numbers for cholecystectomy, the frequency of IOC, and conversion rates were analysed, including evaluation of temporal trends for the period 2015–2021.

To qualitatively assess the educational and research relevance of IOC, international Delphi consensus processes and expert recommendations were also reviewed.

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received formal approval from the responsible institutional ethics committee.

Results

Patient cohort and overall operations

Between July 2015 and June 2023, a total of 1,313 primary cholecystectomies were performed as a main procedure. Of these, 1,287 (98.0%) were initiated laparoscopically, 26 (2.0%) were performed as primarily open surgery, and 18 (1.4%) required conversion to open surgery, resulting in an overall conversion rate of 1.4%.

Use of intraoperative cholangiography (IOC)

In all cases where IOC was performed, conventional radiographic contrast cholangiography was used. After dissection of the cystic duct, a catheter was inserted and contrast medium was injected under fluoroscopic guidance to visualise the biliary tree. According to operative documentation, IOC was carried out in 34 cases (2.6%). Of these, 29 were laparoscopic and five were primary open procedures. In two cases, conversion to open surgery was required following IOC, due to a bile duct injury in one case and uncontrolled intraoperative bleeding in the other.

The most common indication for IOC was clarification of biliary anatomy (28 cases, 82.4%), followed by assessment of bile outflow (8 cases, 23.5%) and identification of bile leakage (3 cases, 8.8%).

One major bile duct injury was recorded during the entire study period, classified as type D1 according to Neuhaus [6], corresponding to an injury rate of 0.07%. In addition, two peripheral bile leaks (type A2) arising from accessory ducts in the gallbladder bed were detected (Table 1).

National data

Nationwide DRG data from the Federal Statistical Office showed that between 2015 and 2021, 168,000–178,000 cholecystectomies were performed annually in Germany. During this period, the conversion rate declined significantly (p < 0.001) from 4.33% in 2015 to 3.59% in 2021. In parallel, the rate of intraoperative cholangiography (including radiographic and fluorescence techniques) showed a significant downward trend (p = 0.034), with a peak of 3.02% in 2017 and a decrease to 2.56% in 2021. Differentiation by IOC technique (radiographic vs. fluorescence) was not possible based on the available dataset (Fig. 1).

Discussion

This retrospective single-centre analysis demonstrated that IOC was performed in 34 of 1,313 cholecystectomies (2.6%) over an eight-year period. Most procedures occurred in non-elective settings (73.5%), and the overall conversion rate was 1.4%, which is lower than reported national rates (3.5–4.3%) during a comparable period [1]. IOC was primarily used to clarify biliary anatomy, with secondary indications for bile flow assessment and detection of bile leakage.

In comparison with nationwide DRG data, our centre demonstrates a similarly low utilisation of IOC but a markedly lower conversion rate. While this observation may reflect differences in case selection, surgical expertise, or institutional practice patterns, it does not allow causal conclusions regarding the effect of IOC on conversion rates. Accordingly, the present findings suggest that on-demand IOC can be integrated into clinical practice without imposing a substantial procedural burden.

When considered together, the low conversion rate and the targeted use of IOC in our cohort suggest that selective, on-demand cholangiography may support intraoperative decision-making in complex cases. However, given the retrospective design and the low number of events, no causal relationship between IOC utilisation and reduced conversion or bile duct injury rates can be inferred from the present data. In our series, IOC was performed in 34 patients, of whom only two (5.9%) required conversion to open surgery, both due to intraoperative complications (one bile duct injury and one uncontrolled bleeding), rather than failure of anatomical clarification. However, on a national level both conversion and IOC rates declined in parallel, indicating that improvements in conversion rates are likely multifactorial and not solely attributable to IOC. The very low rate of major BDI in our cohort should therefore not be attributed to IOC alone. Rather, it likely reflects a multifactorial safety framework, including surgical expertise, standardised operative technique, use of the Critical View of Safety, interdisciplinary availability of gastroenterology, and selective intraoperative imaging in difficult cases.

National DRG data (2015–2021) revealed a consistently low adoption of IOC across Germany, with a peak of 3.02% in 2017 followed by a decline to 2.56% in 2021, while conversion rates decreased from 4.3% to 3.5% (p < 0.001). Despite technological progress and procedural standardisation, IOC remains underused in routine surgical practice. In our institution, utilisation remained stable over time, reflecting a consistent selective, on-demand application strategy. Notably, in our cohort the rate of IOC (2.6%) exceeded the conversion rate (1.4%), whereas national data demonstrate the opposite pattern, with conversion rates consistently exceeding IOC utilisation. This discrepancy may indicate a more proactive intraoperative use of cholangiography in selected cases at our institution.

The optimal strategy for IOC—routine versus selective—continues to be debated. Practice varies widely between healthcare systems. Scandinavian countries, particularly Sweden, advocate routine IOC, whereas many other countries employ selective approaches with usage rates below 5% [10, 16]. An analysis of US Medicare data found that approximately 18% of hospitals perform no IOC at all, underscoring global heterogeneity [17]. Meta-analyses and systematic reviews have reported benefits of both strategies, notably reduced bile duct injury (BDI) and lower conversion rates [18, 19]. A Swedish meta-analysis estimated that routine IOC reduces the risk of BDI by approximately 43% compared with selective use (0.36% vs. 0.53%; odds ratio 1.43, 95% CI 1.22–1.67) [16]. However, these differences may partly reflect variations in clinical indication and perioperative pathways. In healthcare systems with routine IOC, the procedure is often used not only for anatomical clarification but also for the detection of common bile duct stones, potentially compensating for less frequent use or availability of preoperative imaging modalities such as MRCP or EUS, as well as preoperative endoscopic interventions such as ERC. The increasing availability of MRCP may additionally reduce the need for IOC solely for anatomical assessment in some centres. Consequently, differences in IOC strategy may be confounded by differences in patient selection, diagnostic pathways, and overall management of biliary disease.

The utilisation of IOC may also be influenced by the availability of gastroenterological expertise and preoperative endoscopic procedures such as ERC. In settings with liberal access to ERC, intraoperative imaging may be used more selectively, whereas in environments with limited endoscopic availability, IOC may serve as an important intraoperative diagnostic adjunct. This highlights that IOC utilisation reflects not only surgical preference but also interdisciplinary structures and resource availability. In our institution, IOC was predominantly performed or supervised by general surgeons with substantial experience in gallbladder and hepatobiliary surgery.

From an educational perspective, IOC has been identified as a key competency for safe laparoscopic cholecystectomy. In a Delphi consensus by Pucher et al., IOC ranked fourth among technical measures to prevent BDI and first in research priority [13]. Similarly, Iwashita et al. emphasised its relevance as a core component of surgical training [20]. However, recent data from the United States demonstrate a worrying decline in trainee exposure: national ACGME operative logs (2012–2023) show a significant reduction in IOC cases per resident (25.1 to 21.6; p = 0.02), with the lowest numbers recorded in university-based programmes [17]. Notably, overall IOC utilisation in the United States remains substantially higher than in Germany, indicating that even under conditions of more frequent use, trainee exposure is declining. This suggests that the decline in training exposure may be even more pronounced in healthcare systems with very low IOC utilisation, such as Germany. This erosion of experience may contribute to future skill gaps and could ultimately affect patient safety.

In contrast, the German S3 guideline advises against both routine and selective IOC, citing time, resource, and expertise constraints [14]. This conservative German position contrasts with international consensus statements that recognise IOC as an important safety measure and educational tool [13, 16, 20]. The present results demonstrate low complication and conversion rates in a cohort with selective, on-demand IOC use. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and cannot establish a direct protective effect of IOC. A nationwide survey from 2001 reported that approximately half of German hospitals performed IOC selectively, while nearly the other half did not use intraoperative imaging at all [15]. In light of persistently low nationwide IOC rates, this raises the hypothesis that IOC use remains concentrated in a limited number of centres, potentially associated with differences in surgical expertise and outcomes.

Alternative imaging modalities, such as fluorescence cholangiography using indocyanine green (ICG) and laparoscopic and robotic intraoperative ultrasound, provide radiation-free visualisation of biliary anatomy without cystic duct cannulation and have shown promising safety profiles [21, 22]. Nevertheless, both methods remain limited in availability and require further validation before widespread implementation. By contrast, conventional IOC with fluoroscopy and contrast injection remains a readily available, standardised, and reliable tool in most surgical departments.

As with all retrospective single-centre studies, this analysis has limitations. IOC cases were identified through OPS coding, which may lead to underreporting if documentation was incomplete. The retrospective design precludes causal inference, and the modest number of IOC cases limits statistical power. Furthermore, reported bile duct injury rates depend on the definition applied; in this study, only injuries classified as Neuhaus type B or higher were considered clinically relevant, which may limit comparability with studies using broader definitions including minor bile leaks. The use of preoperative MRCP, EUS, and ERC was not systematically quantified in the present analysis, although these modalities were readily available at our institution and likely influenced the low rate of IOC use. Furthermore, national registry data did not include reliable information on bile duct injury rates, limiting direct comparison between institutional and nationwide safety outcomes. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. In line with Delphi consensus statements identifying IOC as a high-priority research topic, there remains a need for well-designed prospective studies to further define its role in contemporary surgical practice [13].

Conclusion

On-demand intraoperative cholangiography remains an infrequently used but clinically relevant adjunct during cholecystectomy. In our single-centre cohort, its targeted application was feasible and associated with low conversion and complication rates, particularly in non-elective cases requiring anatomical clarification. In contrast, nationwide German data demonstrate persistently low and even declining IOC utilisation, suggesting a gap between clinical practice, training, and the potential role of IOC in operative safety. The main message of the present study is therefore not to advocate routine IOC, but to highlight that, in a healthcare system with very low IOC utilisation, on-demand IOC may remain an important problem-solving technique in difficult cholecystectomy and should not disappear from surgical training.
