Optimizing the sustainability assessment, mechanical properties and drying shrinkage of concrete containing groundnut shell ash using RSM modeling
Authors: Naraindas Bheel, Waleligne Molla Salilew, Ibtihaj Saad Rashed Alsadun, Faizah Mohammed Bashir, Zahra Andleeb, Zeineb Ben Houria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The past decade has seen increased research interest on the possible alternatives to cement in concrete and mortar, with materials that are environmentally friendly, economical, and socially inherent. However, recycling, reusing, and regenerating methods may be used to harness the potential benefits of agricultural/industrial wastes as alternatives. The utilization of these recyclables as supplementary and alternative resources yields notable energy conservation and a reduction in cement consumption, thus contributing to the mitigation of carbon dioxide (CO₂) emissions in the environment. Moreover, groundnut shell ash (GSA) is utilized as a substitution for cement in the mixture. The proportion of cement substitution with GSA ranged from 0% to 20% in order to examine the characteristics of concrete and mortar. Concrete samples were cast and tested at 7th, 14th, 28th, and 90th days. Cube, cylinder, and prism specimens were cast for assessing the compressive, flexural, and splitting tensile strengths of concrete consistently. It was discovered that the highest compressive and splitting tensile strengths were 13.39% and 12.69%, respectively, over the control at 90 days, and the flexural strength was 10.64% at 28 days of curing over the control for 10% GSA cement replacement. Also, the water absorption, and drying shrinkage dropped with the increased content of GSA. Besides, the utilization of GSA in the mixture has reduced the embodied carbon. Furthermore, utilizing ANOVA, response prediction approaches were produced at a 95% significance level. The models demonstrated R² ranging from 96 to 99%. Therefore, the research study’s findings indicate that the addition of GSA in concrete positively affects the concrete and mortar properties and recommends a 10% substitution of cement in the mixture.

Introduction

Concrete is one of the most versatile and frequently utilized building materials on the planet1. This is the most extensively utilized construction material as compared to stones, bricks, steel, and lumber. Concrete is utilized in a variety of construction projects, including houses, bridges, highways, hospitals, schools, and business centers. It is produced using a mixture of Portland cement (PC), aggregates, water, and admixtures. Owing to the increasing volume of concrete production, the need for PC is increasing more and more2,3. Cement consumption is currently expected to be over 12 million tons annually, and it is improving now than ever4. Cement manufacturing is highly harmful to the atmosphere because it produces high amount of heat as well as a lot of CO2s. Similarly, the raw materials used to make PC harm the environment and deplete our natural resources5.

The usage of natural assets, including aggregates, and PC as a construction element in the production of concrete, has resulted in significant ecological destruction6. Concrete preparation is the primary source of greenhouse gas emissions from an environmental standpoint. Concrete manufacturing is assessed to be around 1.6 billion tons annually around the world, accounting for 7% of global CO2 emissions7. Given these environmental considerations and the expenses associated with constructing ingredients, the scarcity of raw constituents, and the increased need for energy, the technique of repurposing solid waste is becoming increasingly popular around the world8.

Currently, researchers are making efforts to reduce the usage of PC in matrix and substitute PC with suitable ingredients, which will provide properties comparable to PC in conventional concrete. The use of industrial and agricultural wastes is being emphasized because they are harmful to the environment9. The secure disposal of these materials requires significant effort, yet still imposes a burden on the environment. Their incorporation into concrete brings about significant benefits, proving not only economically feasible but also decreasing the reliance on PC and consequently helping to safeguard the environment10–12. The usage of waste from agriculture and industries in concrete might also facilitate the secure disposal of these materials. Many experts have carried out experiments to see whether there are any alternatives to cement that may be used as a substitute. As a result, researchers have been looking at the effective and real-world usage of pozzolanic waste constituents as a cement substitute13. Researchers claim that using extra cementitious elements in concrete assists in declining the negative ecological consequences of the PC manufacture methods14.

There is significant energy conservation and decrease in PC consumption by using the waste product as a cementitious component, which decreases the CO2 emissions in the atmosphere15. Besides, the usage of cementitious ingredients in matrix may cause in a significant improvement in strength of material. Therefore, extensive study has been conducted on a variety of supplemental items, such as rice husk ash16–19, metakaolin, millet husk ash, and wheat straw ash20,21, palm shale oil, bauxite mining waste22. In this research work, the use of ground nut shell ash as a partial cement replacement material in concrete.

Groundnut shell is an important agricultural waste derived from cereal crops, and it instigates to atmosphere contamination since agriculturalists burn it in the open grounds after it dries. When groundnut shell waste is properly burned in a controlled environment, however, it can produce a substance with cementitious qualities that utilized as SCM in composite23,24. Besides, much amount of the ash is formed during the burning of biofuels, due to the properties of volcanic ash; most of them are thrown into landfills and may be applied as SCM25. Mahmud et al.26 determined the properties of matric with GSA as SCM and found up to 20% replacement of GSA as suitable. Reddy et al.27 investigated concrete of SCM with GSA which was produced in India and recommended cement replacement of up to 10% GSA. Another study by Ikumapayi18 used GSA which was produced in Nigeria as SCM in concrete and recommended 12% GSA as a SCM in matrix. Furthermore, Karthikeyan et al.28 studied the performance of concrete with GSA (produced in India) partial replacement of cement and found 15% GSA replacement suitable. From the foregoing, it is apparent that GSA produced from some countries as a cementitious material in concrete and sandcrete blocks have been studied and obtained different quantities of GSA replacement as suitable. It is possible that GSA produced from other countries may provide different results in concrete or sandcrete blocks. There is therefore the need to also study the properties of concrete produced with GSA from Pakistan as cementitious material, to find out if it will provide different a result.

The process of extracting seeds from groundnuts or peanuts from their pods yields the agricultural byproduct known as groundnut shells. The worldwide peanut output in 2014 amounted to around 42.31 million tons and is predictable to see moderate annual growth. Approximately 25–40% of the peanut crop is comprised of peanut shells and 2.5% of ash is generated for every ton of shell. The use of agricultural and industrial garbage in concrete might also facilitate the secure disposal of those resources29,30. Thus, world production of groundnut ash can reach 420,000 tons. While ash has little economic value, using it as a building material will provide a significant benefit over putting it in a landfill, which will pollute the environment. Therefore, this research study goals at examining the performance properties of concrete combined with GSA. To investigate the performance properties, mechanical properties, drying shrinkage and embodied carbon of concrete were evaluated by applying response surface methodology (RSM) for modeling and optimization.

Moulding and curing of specimens

A mechanical mixing technique was applied to ensure a uniform mix of the materials. The mix ratio used was 1:2:4 (PC: FA: CA), and the water-cement ratio used was 0.50. The PC extent was replaced by 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% GSA as SCM. The quantities and composition of the ingredients applied for the mix design are revealed in Table 3. After mixing each mix design, a slump test was conducted and the specimens were prepared in metal moulds (cubes, cylinders, and prisms).

For this study, the mixtures were made with the incorporation of different percentages of GSA as shown in Table 3 and three specimens (100 × 100 × 100 mm cube, 200 × 100 mm cylinder, and 100 × 100 × 500 mm prism) were prepared in accordance with ASTM C19232. The concrete specimens were demoulded and cured at 7, 14, 28, and 90 days for testing. For each curing day, three concrete cubes were selected for each of the mixed variables for testing. The average value for all the three samples at each mix variable was taken as the value of a particular test.

Testing method

Slump test

It was performed in compliance with BS EN 12350-2:200933 to evaluate the workability of fresh concrete. The slump cone was positioned on a flat, non-absorbent surface and filled with fresh concrete in three uniform layers; each compacted with 25 strokes of a tamping rod. Upon removing the surplus concrete, the mould was carefully elevated vertically, enabling the concrete to settle. The slump value was assessed as the disparity between the initial height of the mould and the peak of the collapsed concrete, reported in millimeters.

Hardened properties

Cubical samples (100 × 100 × 100 mm) were cast to assess the compressive strength of the mixture by monitoring the code BS EN 12390-334. The specimen is positioned centrally between the platens of the compression testing apparatus, and the load is applied steadily and without impact at a constant stress rate of 0.6 ± 0.2 MPa/s until fracture occurs. Similarly, cylinder specimens (100 × 200 mm) were used for examining the splitting tensile strength by following the BS EN 12390-635. The sample is horizontally placed between the loading strips, and a constant load is delivered at a consistent rate to achieve a stress increment of 0.04 ± 0.01 MPa/s until fracture develops. These samples were cured for 7, 14, 28, and 90 days. In the same way, the flexural strength was determined on prism (500 × 100 × 100 mm) by ensuing BS EN 12390-536 using the central point load. The load is employed constantly at a rate that results in an increase in severe fibre stress of 0.04 ± 0.02 MPa/s until failure ensues. Furthermore, the modulus of elasticity was conducted on cylindrical samples (300 × 150 mm) by ensuing BS EN 12390-1337 after 7, 14, and 28 days. The water absorption of concrete was determined on cubical samples (100 × 100 × 100 mm) by observing BS 188138 at 28 days. Furthermore, the drying shrinkage was monitored on prisms (280 × 50 × 30 mm) at 40 days using BS ISO 1920-839. The porosity of concrete was assessed in compliance with ASTM C642. The specimens were subjected to oven drying, water saturation, and weighed in both air and water, with porosity estimated from the recorded masses and given as a percentage.

Results and discussion

Workability of concrete

It was evaluated by the implementation of a slump test. The maximum slump recorded was 62 mm with 0% of GSA concrete (control) and the smallest slump was 29 mm at 20% GSA as SCM in mixture as demonstrated in Fig. 2. It has been concluded from Fig. 2 that as the amount of GSA rises in concrete, the workability declines. The drop in slump implies reduced workability of the concrete. The decrease in workability was 11.29, 29.03, 41.94, and 53.26%, respectively for replacement of 5, 10, 15, and 20% GSA by cement in concrete than control mix. The reduced workability can be ascribed to increased absorption of water by the concrete owing to increase content of GSA content in the concrete. This observation agrees well with Ige et al.40 that the slump of concrete decreases as there is the increased content of ashes in concrete, because of the fineness of the ashes. Lakshmi et al.41 ascribed that the addition of ashes to concrete augments the cohesiveness of concrete mix and improved its stiffness due to the fineness of the ashes. Nevertheless, the workability of all mixtures was greater than 20 mm and therefore appropriate for structural uses. But, in the situation where greater workability is required, the waste engine oil or superplasticizer can be utilized to enhance the workability of matrix with the addition of GSA content.

Compressive strength (CS) of concrete

Three concrete cubes were cast and the CS value was used for representing the result in Fig. 3. The highest CS were 21.06, 26.10, 30.30, and 31.75 MPa at 10% GSA replacement of cement in concrete, correspondingly at 7, 14, 28, and 90 days. Conversely, the least CS were 17.20, 22.00, 25.40, and 27.00 MPa at 20% GSA replacement of cement in concrete, respectively at 7, 14, 28, and 90 days. The differences in CS of GSA replacement of cement concrete as against the control at 90 days of curing were 8.9, 13.39, 3.50, and − 3.58%, respectively for 5, 10, 15, and 20% GSA content. The 5, 10, and 15% GSA content in concrete provided increased strength over the control with the highest being the 10% GSA replacement of cement. The increased strength can be ascribed to the high content of silica in GSA and the fine particles of the GSA which act like a filler in the concrete. On the contrary, the CS is enhanced due to the improvement of wet packing density42–44. The CS of the concrete was reduced with the improved proportion of GSA at 20% content. The reduction is a result of reduced PC hydration as the GSA keeps less content of cementitious property than the PC. By using a higher percentage of GSA to replace cement, a shortfall of CaO may develop in the concrete, thus resulting in low C-S-H formations in the concrete. Therefore, beyond 10% of GSA as SCM in composite, the strength of concrete will be affected. According to earlier studies45,46, a 10% cement replacement with ashes is enough for achieving an improved CS of concrete. Earlier study recommended that the amount of GSA higher than 10% will be high for producing rigid concrete10. Nonetheless, the CS of all specimens at 28 days were superior than 25 MPa, therefore concrete specimens containing GSA can be used for structural works.

The STS result of the concrete is demonstrated in Fig. 4. However, the 10% GSA replacement of cement achieved the highest STS of 2.08, 2.64, 3.25, and 3.55 MPa, respectively at 7, 14, 28, and 90 days. The least STS was obtained by the 20% GSA replacement of cement at 1.68, 2.18, 2.92, and 3.02 MPa, respectively for 7, 14, 28, and 90 days of curing. The differences in STS of GSA replacement of cement in concrete compared with the control at 90 days of curing were 9.52, 12.69, 5.08, and − 4.13%, respectively for 5, 10, 15, and 20% GSA content. Again, the 5, 10, and 15% GSA content in concrete provided higher strength than that of control; the highest have been the 10% GSA replacement of cement. The fineness of the ash particles covers all of the gaps in the concrete composite, resulting in dense concrete with an improved tensile strength of up to 10% of GSA, according to Khandelwal47. After 10% of GSA, the excess amount of silica in the mix affects the chemical reaction which results in a reduction in the STS. These remarks are comparable to the findings of other findings when the usage of 10 to 15% RHA enhanced the STS of concrete48,49.

The result yielded coefficient of determination (R²) values of 0.9822, 0.9863, 0.9911, 0.9863 and 0.9812, consistently for 0, 5, 10, 15 and 20% PC substituted with GSA in the concrete. The result suggests that the CS of the GSA in concrete is a good predictor of the STS. The result also means 98.3, 98.6, 99.1, 98.6, and 98.1% (respectively for 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20% GSA content) of the variability in the CS and STS of the concrete could be explained by the accumulation of GSA.

Flexural strength (FS) of concrete

The highest FS was attained at 10% GSA as SCM in concrete with a strength of 4.06, 4.64, and 5.82 MPa, consistently at 7, 14, and 28 days as shown Fig. 6. Conversely, the least FS was obtained at 20% PC replaced with GSA in concrete with a strength of 3.4, 3.94, and 4.95 MPa at 7, 14, and 28 days consistently. The differences in FS of GSA as SCM in concrete as against the control at 28 days of curing were 6.27, 10.65, 5.32, and − 5.89%, respectively for 5, 10, 15, and 20% GSA content. The 5, 10 and 15% PC replaced with GSA in concrete provided increased strength over the control with the highest being the 10% GSA content. This observation correlates with the previous study50 result which informed that the FS of composite enhanced with RHA was achieved up to 15% ash inclusion. The reasons for the FS increase between 5 and 15% inclusion of GSA can be as a result of the fine ash particles that seal all the spaces in the concrete47. The dip in strength at 20% GSA inclusion in the concrete can be a result of the high level of silica in the mix that affects the chemical reaction leading to a reduction in the FS47.

The MOE of concrete measures its toughness or resistance to deformation. The highest modulus of elasticity was achieved at 10% GSA replacement of cement in concrete by 28.02 GPa, 31.20 GPa, and 32.80 GPa at 7, 14, and 28 days, while the control obtained the least as shown in Fig. 7. The enhancement in the MOE of the GSA as SCM in concrete when using 15% of GSA in concrete on 28 days correspondingly. Moreover, the usage of GSA more than 15% in concrete, it gets reduced after 28 days. It is clear to see that the MOE of the concrete amplified with growth in the GSA content up to 15% by the weight of cement. This rise in MOE of composites can be owing to a stronger interface bond between the PC matrix and aggregate51. It is reported that improving the ITZ can improve the MOE49,50. The findings of the research observed that the MOE is enhanced with the inclusion of GSA content as a cementitious component in concrete.

Water absorption (WA) of concrete

Figure 8 presents the impact of GSA on WA of the concrete on 28 days. It was identified that the WA declined with a rise in the extent of GSA. The rate of WA was 3.66, 3.05, 2.47, 2, and 1.39%, respectively for 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20% PC replaced with GSA. This decline in WA is related with the fineness of GSA constituent in composite which decreased the micro-pores that are left by other constituents of concrete. The WA of the concrete with 20% GSA as SCM was 62% less than that of the control. This finding aligns with prior research52,53 that WA of concrete with the accumulation of ashes is lower as compared to concrete without ashes. This is owing to the fineness of ash which can seal pores in the concrete and result in better water-resistance of the concrete. The continuous augmentation in resistance of the concrete against WA can be ascribed to the finer particles of the GSA which act as a filler and seal the pores in the concrete. Therefore, as the GSA content increased, there is also a corresponding increase in resistance against WA of the concrete.

Drying shrinkage (DS) of concrete

To effectively manage cracks, it is critical to recognize the characteristics of DS and its initiation during building construction. The result indicates that the DS of concrete is decreased when consuming GSA contents up to 20% at 5 to 40 days as shown in Fig. 9. The outcome has been determined that the DS reduced with increased GSA content in the concrete. This means that the greater the GSA content the lesser the DS. According to CCA54, correct concrete mix design is one of the mechanisms that results in minimum concrete shrinkage. Previous research55–59 has shown that growing the extent of RHA in composite reduces DS dramatically. This reduction in DS can be ascribed to the finer particles of the GSA which act as a filler and seal the pores, thereby reducing DS. On the other hand, the mechanical interlocking in the transition zone could be attributed to pore size and grain size enhancement techniques. Thus, when shrinking is a problem, using GSA as part of a strategy to limit its effect can assist. A comparable trend of research was observed by Lai et al.43 where the shrinkage is reduced with rising in the extent of SCM (like Ash) rises. This decrease in DS is owing to the wet packing density of concrete.

Porosity of concrete

Figure 10 shows the porosity of concrete combined with GSA as SCM. The eperimental findings show that the decrease in concrete porosity with higher Groundnut Shell Ash (GSA) substitution for cement results from a synergy of physical filling, pozzolanic activity, and microstructural densification processes. GSA particles, being finer and more angular than cement grains, serve as micro-fillers that occupy the interstitial gaps between cement particles and aggregates, reducing the volume of capillary pores and forming a denser, more compact matrix. The elevated silica content in GSA engages in pozzolanic reactions with calcium hydroxide released throughout cement hydration, ensuing in the development of extra C-S-H gel that accumulates within existing pore spaces, thus enhancing the pore structure by transforming larger capillary pores into smaller, less interconnected gel pores. The creation of secondary C-S-H reduces the total calcium hydroxide concentration, which is more soluble and porous, substituting it with a denser, more stable hydration product. The comparatively slower and prolonged pozzolanic reaction rate of GSA, in contrast to the fast initial hydration of Portland cement, facilitates more uniform and thorough densification over time, hence reducing the formation of linked pore networks. The synergistic impact of physical pore obstruction by unreacted ash particles and chemical pore refinement via secondary hydration products leads to a convoluted, discontinuous pore network characterized by diminished total porosity and critical pore diameter, thereby markedly improving the impermeability and durability of the concrete.

Sustainability assessment of concrete

The influence of PC production on the climate has become a prominent subject of study that specialists are actively examining and seeking potential alternatives for it. This process entails replacing recyclable materials with cement. The transportation, processing, and sorting of each waste product used as a possible alternative for concrete result in the generation of embedded carbon throughout the entire procedure. In contrast to PC, waste has a much-reduced composition of EC. In order to achieve industry acceptance as an environmentally friendly concrete, it is essential to consider the presence of embedded carbon via the integration of GSA. Cost analysis of sustainable concrete

In order to measure the viability of GSA as a SCM in mix, it is compulsory to estimate and evaluate its impact on the total price of concrete. Despite the fact that the GSA is derived from waste products and was acquired without charge, there are expenses related with the processing of this waste. The cost comprises the expenses associated with the transportation of materials from their respective origins to the laboratory required for the concrete mixing process. The GSA underwent a series of processes, including the drying process, crushing, and sieving process, which resulted in increased costs. In this experimental study, GSA was utilized as SCM up to 20% for investigating the slump, mechanical properties, drying shrinkage and sustainability assessment of concrete. Based on test findings, it is possible to conclude:

The consumption of GSA as SCM in composite causes a decline in the workability of mix as the GSA absorbs more water.
