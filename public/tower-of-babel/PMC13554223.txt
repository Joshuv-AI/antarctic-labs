Distributable screen-printed soil pH sensor demonstrates robust response across variable soil conditions
Authors: Juan Pablo Cisneros-Barba, Catherine A. Crichton, Taylor Sharpe, Madhur Atreya, Gregory L. Whiting
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Management of soil pH is critical for maintaining crop productivity, but high-spatiotemporal measurement of pH in soils can be a challenge due to sampling methods, recalibration requirements, and instrumentation costs. To address these limitations, a distributable, screen-printed carbon-alizarin-based soil pH sensor designed for long-term continuous operation in soils was studied. Square-wave voltammetry is employed to determine the alizarin redox peak position in a current-voltage scan, which is proportional to pH and is relatively invariant over time. The printed sensors were tested across varying soil moisture content, compaction levels, and soil types, confirming robustness over different environmental and installation conditions without the need for recalibration. Integration of the electrodes onto a printed circuit board addressed with a custom low-cost, low-power, weatherized potentiostat enabled autonomous data logging and continuous operation for months under field conditions, demonstrating reliable in situ pH sensing. These results highlight the suitability of this approach for use in collecting spatiotemporally dense pH data directly and continuously in soils.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57457-7.

Introduction

pH is a vital determinant of crop health and soil fertility1. The potential of hydrogen (pH) can directly influence plant growth, nutrient availability, and soil microbial activity2. Continuously monitoring pH levels in the growing medium enables improved resource allocation and higher agricultural yields by enabling the delivery of a tailored mixture of nutrients to plants3.

Four distinct factors within acidic soils that adversely affect plant growth have been identified. These factors encompass the presence of excessive quantities of soluble manganese, iron, and aluminum in certain soil types, an insufficient supply of calcium, magnesium, and molybdenum, a high content of insoluble iron and aluminum phosphates, and a significant reduction of microbial activity and organic matter turnover4.

Mitigating the impacts of these factors is achievable through on-field pH control. Lime application is a well-established method for controlling soil acidity5. However, the precise application of lime at suitable rates and in appropriate quantities is a critical determinant in enhancing crop yields6. pH monitoring is therefore indispensable for precise soil acidity management. Ensuring optimal growing conditions through real time pH regulation is useful for optimizing crop yield, resource utilization, and minimizing the labor invested in crop management6.

Typically, for in-field pH evaluation a soil sample is collected, mixed with water and reacted with a pH indicator dye, resulting in a pH-dependent color change enabling the determination of the soil’s plant-available nutrient levels7. Soil pH test kits are an easy and inexpensive way to quickly estimate soil acidity. However, they can be inaccurate8, require intensive human labor and careful tracking, and do not provide a continuous signal.

Various types of electronic soil pH sensors also exist, with different advantages and challenges. Metal probe pH meters, for example, are inexpensive and straightforward to use but can be inaccurate9. Glass electrode pH sensors are the most common tool for determining the pH of aqueous solutions, offering highly precise measurements through straightforward and established chemical procedures. However, their fragility makes them prone to breakage, they require frequent calibration to ensure optimal accuracy, and are typically too expensive for distributed high-spatial density deployments in agricultural settings10.

Solid-state pH sensors which do not require glass or reference solutions can also be used. This approach offers improved robustness, chemical resistance, and high impact strength, addressing limitations associated with traditional glass electrodes11 and increasing their compatibility with a wider range of environments. These types of sensors often use ion-sensitive materials such as iridium oxide, antimony, and other pH-sensitive metals12,13, or conductive polymers like polythiophene and polyaniline14 to measure pH-dependent voltage changes. However, the manufacturing of high-quality solid-state pH sensors can be complex and costly, limiting their scalability and widespread adoption15. Additionally, these sensors can still suffer from signal drift or degradation over time16.

Additive manufacturing techniques such as screen printing present a robust, low-cost, and scalable alternative to overcome limitations of typical fabrication methods. For example, printed alizarin-based pH sensors have previously been studied17–19. These sensors function through a redox mechanism in which the potential of the alizarin redox peak shifts linearly with pH. This peak-position-based measurement allows for pH sensing that is relatively resistant to drift as it is not strongly impacted by current variation. However, the study of these sensors has primarily focused on controlled aqueous environments or short-term measurements. Building from these studies, this work specifically addresses requirements for distributed, long-term, in-situ soil monitoring.

In recent work, we described a reliable screen-printed voltammetric pH sensor consisting of a carbon–Alizarin/Nafion working electrode with a Ag–AgCl/polyvinyl butyral (PVB)–NaCl reference electrode20. This sensor exhibited a robust and reproducible response in buffered and unbuffered media, using both 2- and 3-electrode configurations, indicating its potential applicability for distributed soil monitoring. This report describes three specific advances compared with previous studies: a systematic evaluation of sensor performance across diverse soil conditions, including varying moisture content, compaction levels, and soil textures, to assess suitability for direct in situ measurements; a PCB-based sensor redesign that improves mechanical robustness, reduces fabrication complexity, and enables facile integration with readout electronics; and the demonstration of long-term (three-month) autonomous outdoor field operation using a custom low-cost weatherized potentiostat, validating the system’s operational stability under real field conditions.

The design of the printed pH sensors and the general concept of use is shown in Fig. 1. The pH sensitivity of these devices is based on the use of an alizarin-functionalized working electrode. The specific proton sensitive redox reaction that alizarin goes through when exposed to acids and bases has been shown to behave linearly approximating a Nernstian slope of -59.2 mV/pH21. In acidic conditions, the quinone group of alizarin accepts electrons, experiencing a reduction, forming dihydroxy groups. Meanwhile under alkaline conditions, the deprotonation of hydroxyl groups stabilizes the quinone form, making the molecule less likely to gain electrons and shifting the reduction potential to more negative values22.

The voltage peak position of this redox reaction can be found using square wave voltammetry (SWV).As the applied potential increases it will reach a point where the alizarin can accept or donate electrons, when this point is reached a peak in current occurs corresponding to the reduction potential. The voltage at which this peak happens shifts linearly with pH17. Since the determination of pH depends on the position of this redox peak, this approach is rather robust against signal drift over time, because while the current may change, the position of the peak should remain relatively constant.

In previous work, we implemented several design features aimed at enhancing the stability and longevity of the sensor. The Ag-AgCl reference electrode was coated with a polyvinyl butyral – sodium chloride (PVB-NaCl) reservoir to minimize chloride ion loss, thereby providing a more stable reference potential compared to bare Ag-AgCl electrodes20,23. In addition, a Nafion membrane was applied to the working electrode, serving as an ion-selective barrier that permits proton transport while limiting alizarin leaching and dissolution into the surrounding medium17,20. Together, these features contributed to improved sensor performance and extended operational lifetime, but the impact of various soil types and conditions on the sensor response was not considered.

To further optimize fabrication and deployment ability and to enable integration with readout electronics, we developed a rigid PCB-based sensor design. This printable and PCB-compatible fabrication approach enables low-cost, scalable manufacturing of mechanically robust sensors suitable for autonomous and distributable in-soil deployment, distinguishing this implementation from prior carbon-alizarin-based pH sensors primarily demonstrated in laboratory or aqueous environments. This sensor design reduces manufacturing complexity by halving the number of printed layers, while also improving mechanical robustness and the reliability of electrical contacts, thereby lowering noise and facilitating field deployment. The PCB architecture incorporates two working electrodes, which can be alternated to extend sensor lifetime, and protects conductive traces from mechanical abrasion. Additionally, the use of a polyvinyl butyral – potassium chloride (PVB-KCl) reference salt reservoir provides a more stable and well-characterized alternative to NaCl24. Collectively, these modifications enhance reproducibility, robustness, and ease of use, making the sensor design more suitable for long-term soil deployment.

Materials and methods

pH sensor fabrication

The flexible pH sensors are screen printed using a TF-100 Screen Printer from Micro Printing Systems (MPS) Intl. on a flexible polyethylene naphthalate substrate (PEN, TEONEX Q51, TOYOBO Co) with a 325.09 stainless-steel mesh screen (E80 emulsion formulation, Sefar, Inc.). Three 49 mm-long traces are initially printed using a commercially available carbon ink (DuPont 7102). The ink is then cured for 30 min at 110 °C, with the left trace serving as the counter electrode (CE).

Subsequently, two layers of commercial Ag-AgCl ink (Nagase ChemteX, Cl-400) are printed: the first layer is another trace that covers the right carbon trace, which is then cured under the same conditions, followed by a 2 mm diameter circle on top, that is once again cured to create the base of the reference electrode (RE). A commercial dielectric ink (DuPont 5036) is then applied to encapsulate the traces, except for their ends, and is subjected to the same curing process as the previous layers.

After curing, 2 µL of a PVB-NaCl (1.58 g:1 g) mixture, suspended in 20 mL methanol, is drop-cast onto the Ag-AgCl circle in the RE to form a salt reservoir, and is dried under ambient conditions for at least one hour. After this the RE is completed by submerging it in a 3 M NaCl solution for 12 h.

For the working electrode (WE), a carbon-alizarin composite ink (10 wt% alizarin in DuPont 7102 carbon ink) is printed onto the middle trace, forming a 5 mm diameter circle, which is cured at 110 °C for 30 min. Finally, a 1 wt% Nafion 117 solution in ethanol is spin-coated on top of the WE circle at 1000 RPM for 30 s and then cured at 110 °C for 15 min.

In order to make contact to the electrodes adhesive, copper tape is applied to each trace, followed by the placement of 255 µm diameter flexible silicone wires on top, followed by another layer of copper tapeto keep the wires in place and ensure electrical contact.

A rigid polylactic acid (PLA) casing is 3D printed using a Raise 3D Pro2 printer. This casing consists of 2 parts, the lid and the stake. To ensure the device is waterproof, the assembly is secured to the lid using closed-cell ultra-conformable foam mounting tape. The lid is then filled with FlexSeal Leak Sealer, completely encasing the copper tape and exposed wires. The waterproof sensor can then slide in and snap-fit into the stake (Fig. 1d). This assembly allows for easy insertion into soil without damaging the printed sensor, positioning the working electrode (WE) at a consistent depth of 5 cm and making it simple to remove and replace a sensor as needed.

In addition to the flexible PEN sensors, deployable PCB-based pH sensors were fabricated using the same TF-100 Screen Printer on 1.6 mm FR4 TG135 PCBs (JLCPCB) with an ENIG surface finish of 0.0508 μm and an outer copper weight of 28.35 g, using the same 325.09 stainless-steel mesh screen and emulsion formulation. Two working electrodes (WEs, 5 mm diameter) were printed using the previously described carbon–alizarin ink and cured at 110 °C for 10 min, followed by a second layer cured at 110 °C for 30 min. After cooling for at least 10 min on a metal tray, a 1 wt% Nafion 117 solution in ethanol was spin-coated at 1000 RPM for 15 s onto both WEs. Excess Nafion on the CE and RE pads was removed with Kimtech Kimwipes and ethanol. The Nafion membrane was then cured at 110 °C for 15 min.

The reference electrode (5 mm diameter) was subsequently printed using the commercial Ag-AgCl ink. The first layer was applied and cured at 110 °C for 10 min, followed by a second layer cured at 110 °C for 30 min. After cooling, 17 µL of a PVB-KCl (1.58 g:1 g) mixture, suspended in 20 mL methanol was drop-casted onto the RE to form a salt reservoir and was dried under ambient conditions for two hours. The electrode was then completed by immersion in 3 M KCl solution for 12 h.

The PCB was wired using 22-gauge, 4-conductor cable (DEKIEVALE), with red, black, white, and yellow assigned to WE1, CE, WE2, and RE, respectively. A rigid PLA casing was fabricated on a Prusa i3 MK3 3D printer, consisting of three components: the sheath to hold the PCB and enable insertion into the soil, the presser which screws into the sheath to secure the PCB in place and form a dam for insulating the wires, and a travel sheath which protects the printed electrodes during transport. To insulate and weatherproof the device, the presser cavity was filled with Rubber Clear FlexSeal Leak Sealer and dried under ambient conditions for at least 24 h.

Sensor interrogation and data analysis

For this study, we conducted SWV measurements using an EmStat3 Blue from Palm Sense, operating through Palm Sense PS Trace software. The parameters were set as follows: a current range from 100 nA to 1 mA, an equilibration time of 5 s, initial voltage at -0.1 V, end voltage at 0.75 V for PEN sensors and 0.8 V for PCB sensors, a step of 0.005 V, an amplitude of 0.1 V, and a frequency of 30 Hz for PEN sensors and 15 Hz for PCB sensors.

Moving average baseline subtraction was performed using the built-in tool in PS Trace for each measurement, with a window size of 2 and a max number of sweeps of 500, for the data to be consistent and minimize computing time. The voltage for the reduction potential was determined using a polynomial fit to the data.

To properly measure and control the pH of soils, a GroStar GS2 Soil pH Pen Tester was used and calibrated after every set of measurements using pH 7 and pH 4 buffer. When required, soil pH was controlled using Bloom City’s pH Up (potassium hydroxide) or pH Down (phosphoric acid) Professional Grade pH Adjuster.

Field data was collected using custom electronics to autonomously perform SWV at regular 30-minute intervals. The parameters were set as follows: a feedback resistor gain of 350 kΩ, initial voltage at 0 V, end voltage at 0.625 V, an amplitude of 0.03 V, a step of 0.005 V, and a frequency of 10 Hz.

Custom potentiostat electronics

For long-term outdoor experiments, a low-cost, low-power field potentiostat platform was developed around the LMP91000 programmable analog front-end (Texas Instruments). The LMP91000 functions as a configurable potentiostat, maintaining a controlled potential between the working and reference electrodes while measuring the resulting current flowing at the working electrode via its internal transimpedance amplifier. Firmware and libraries for interfacing with the LMP91000 were based on the KickStat platform developed by Hoilett, et al.22.

The LMP91000 uses a serial I2C connection to communicate with the microcontroller, and can enable various electrochemical techniques, including the SWV approach used here. The waveform itself is generated by the microcontroller, which applies a sequence of discrete potential steps to the LMP91000 bias input to produce the voltammetric scan. Using its transimpedance amplifier, the LMP91000 can take voltage measurements from the resulting current flowing through the electrodes and save this signal alongside the applied voltage sequence to reconstruct the voltammogram.

Readout electronics were built around the Adafruit Feather platform (Adafruit, Inc.) to support various logging and telemetry configurations. Open-source PCB designs also enable future simplified integrations to drive the platform cost and size down further. Initial field-deployed prototypes log data locally to an SD card. Feather boards of identical form factor can enable future telemetry options, including WiFi, Bluetooth, ESP-NOW, and LoRa protocols.

Extreme low power logging was enabled by incorporating a low-power timer into the design, the TPL5110 Nano Power Timer (SparkFun, Inc.). A logging frequency is chosen via dip switch; this selection determines how long the system stays in low-power mode between interrogations. In deep sleep mode, the system consumes approximately 35 nA of power, enabling long-term field deployments. A 2500 mAh battery supplied power to the system (Adafruit, Inc.).

For outdoor deployments, the electronics were housed in a low-profile ASA enclosure (New Age Enclosures) with milled through-holes to allow waterproof signal cable connection and LED feedback via an IP68 rated light pipe (Bivar). 3D-printed features were added to the enclosure to prevent screws from being lost during field deployments.

Soil sampling and sample preparation

Three types of soil were used. The first was a commercially available Miracle-Gro Potting Mix. This potting mix contains a blend of silica particles, perlite, compost, and other organic materials. To reduce the risk of mechanical damage to the sensor, we removed larger rocks (over 4 mm in diameter) and wooden debris (over 15 mm in length). Prior to experimentation, the potting soil was dried in an oven at 110 °C for 48 h to ensure complete removal of water content from the sample25, in order to allow precise control of soil moisture content through controlled addition of aqueous solutions.

The other two soil types were collected manually from six unmanaged public-land locations around Boulder, Colorado. These sites were chosen using the USDA Web Soil Survey database and included the zones NnB, AcA, VaB, LoB, NuA, and RnB. At each location, a random sampling pattern was used to identify five points where soil was taken from 15 cm deep holes with a 20 cm diameter. The soil from these five points was combined in a bucket and thoroughly mixed. 0.45 kg of soil from each location was provided to the Soil-Water-Plant Laboratory (SWPL) at Colorado State University for texture analysis via hydrometer testing. Results and discussion

Sensor response in soil

To establish a relationship between the voltage peak position of our readings and soil pH, a series of measurements at a constant gravimetric water content (GWC) was conducted using various potting mix soil samples with pH values ranging from 4 to 8 at a GWC of 60%. After preparing the soil samples, the printed pH sensor was inserted into the sample, and the soil was compressed around the sensor to ensure optimal contact with the electrodes. Two conditioning scans were performed prior to data collection. The first was taken immediately after insertion to verify adequate electrical contact between the sensor electrodes, the soil, and the connecting wires; this scan was inspected for signal quality and discarded. Once suitable contact was confirmed, a second conditioning scan was performed to allow the electrode–soil interface to equilibrate and to define the start of the measurement period (t = 0). This second scan was also discarded, and timed data collection began immediately after, with measurements recorded at t = 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 min.

Five different sensors were interrogated in each sample, recording six measurements per sensor at 10-minute intervals. After averaging the values and performing linear fitting, a negative slope of 50.27 mV/pH and an R2 of 99.24% was found, which aligns with previously published work17,26, which reported a near Nernstian sensitivity for this type of sensor. In addition to establishing the calibration relationship, the use of five independent sensors allowed us to study sensor-to-sensor variability. Table 2 reports the average peak voltage and population standard deviation calculated by pooling all 30 measurements (5 sensors × 6 measurements per sensor) at each pH level. This pooled standard deviation captures both sensor-to-sensor fabrication variability and within-sensor measurement repeatability. Dividing by the measured sensitivity of 50.27 mV/pH yields the equivalent pH variation, which ranged from ± 0.19 to ± 0.33 pH units across the evaluated range. This level of variability is small relative to the full measurement range and is primarily attributed to minor fabrication variations in printed electrode geometry, alizarin distribution, and reference electrode salt reservoir formation. Individual calibration of sensors before deployment allows for a custom correlation coefficient between voltage and pH, granting maximum accuracy.

Effect of soil moisture content

Moisture content is a key soil parameter since it directly affects microbial activity27, nutrient uptake28 and root growth29. Increasing GWC affects the hydrogen ion concentration, leading to a rise in pH as the soil to water ratio of the sample increases30. Additionally, increasing soil moisture will directly impact ion mobility and soil electrical conductivity31,32, which in turn will affect the current of the measurements and the height of the peaks. However, the voltage position of the peak is governed by the redox reaction that alizarin undergoes and is therefore independent of solution conductivity.

To assess the reliability of our printed sensor under varying moisture conditions, we conducted three experiments using three different sensors at eight distinct GWC levels. A sample containing 30 g of Miracle-Gro Potting Mix at 0% GWC was hydrated with pH Up, pH Down, and potassium biphthalate pH 6 buffer solution until the measured pH using the GS2 Tester was equal to 6, selected as the midpoint of the pH 4–8 range evaluated in this study, and the GWC was 30%. A sensor was then inserted into the soil to take measurements for 70 min. Following each set of measurements, the sensor was removed, 3 g of pH 6 buffer solution were added and thoroughly mixed into the soil to increase the GWC by 10% while maintaining a constant pH, and the sensor was reinserted. This process was repeated until a GWC of 100% was achieved, with all three sensors undergoing the same procedure.

Table 3 shows a comparison between the average voltage readings at each GWC to the theoretical pH 6 peak voltage average of 437.9 mV, obtained through the previously discussed calibration. Despite the expected changes in the soil electrical conductivity due to the GWC increase, the peak voltage remained stable across the evaluated moisture range. We calculated an absolute error of 5.70mV or 0.085pH units. This value matches well with the accuracy of ± 0.1 pH units which is typically quoted for commercial soil pH sensors33–35. These data suggest that the sensor signal is not significantly impacted by the moisture level or associated conductivity changes of the soil in the range of 30–100% GWC.

Effect of soil compaction

Soil compaction level is a critical factor in agricultural management as it affects soil porosity36, root penetration37 and water drainage38. Current farming techniques that rely on heavy machinery increase the chances of soil compaction39, making it impossible to ignore as a variable for soil sensing. Soil compaction may limit nutrient and mineral levels in the soil40,41, potentially affecting pH values. In our case, soil compaction around the sensor is of particular importance since it directly affects the contact between the soil and the WE.

To evaluate the impact of compaction on sensor-soil contact and signal quality, experiments were conducted using Miracle-Gro Potting Mix at 60% GWC. Potting mix has a characteristically low bulk density, in our case approximately 0.31 g/cm2, below the typical range of agricultural soils, which is typically between 1.1 and 1.6 g/cm242. This low-density material provides a heterogenous interface between the sensor surface and the soil, greater soil-sensor contact variability, and is more easily compressible than higher density soils. This creates a useful experimental condition to study the impact of sensor placement and soil disturbance on the observed redox peak position provided by the pH sensor.

Since pH was not a variable in these trials, the samples were hydrated with deionized water only. After the sensor was inserted in the sample, the soil was compacted using metal weights at 65.1 kPa for 20 s reaching an approximate bulk density of 0.32 g/cm2. Immediately after removing the weights a conditioning reading was taken, followed by continuous measurements every 30 min.

For the control experiment, the soil remained undisturbed over a four-day period, except for pH measurements taken every 24 h using the GS2 pH probe. In contrast, the second experiment was subjected to re-compaction of the soil every 24 h after taking pH measurements. On the first day, 65.1 kPa was applied again, followed by 80.9 kPa on the second and third days, which increased its bulk density to approximately 0.33 g/cm2. On the fourth day, the soil was not compacted, and measurements were continued for an additional 12 h prior to terminating the experiment (Fig. 4).

Figure 4c depicts a direct correlation between compaction and the magnitude of peak current. This is expected, as compaction is expected to improve the physical contact between the soil and the working electrode surface, increasing the effective electroactive area and enhancing ion transport to the electrode, which in turn raises the magnitude of the measured current. In the compacted soil, we observed a 50% recovery in peak current height after the third compression, with smaller increases after the first and second compressions. Meanwhile, in the undisturbed soil, the peak current stabilized within the first six hours and remained fairly constant thereafter.

Crucially, while compaction affected peak current magnitude, it did not impact the peak voltage position which is used to determine pH (Fig. 4a). Since peak position is governed solely by the alizarin redox reaction, it remains independent of contact quality or current magnitude in this experiment. Both sensors tracked pH changes as measured by the GS2 probe, suggesting that within the low bulk density range tested here, the redox peak position is not significantly altered by variations in mechanical disturbances in the soil.

Effect of soil texture

The results presented thus far are based on experiments conducted in a potting mixture. However, this substrate differs from natural soils in texture and composition, as potting mix is primarily composed of peat moss, processed forest products, and compost43, while natural soil mainly consists of minerals and organic matter derived from plant, animal, and microbial residues44. Soil minerals can be classified by their particle size into clay (< 2 μm), silt (2–50 μm), and sand (50 μm–2 mm)45.

To assess the sensor’s performance in different soil textures, calibration experiments were conducted using two natural soils: sandy clay loam (20% clay, 10% silt, and 70% sand) and clay loam (39% clay, 23% silt, and 38% sand). For each of these soil types, three sensors were tested following the sensor response calibration methodology discussed earlier.

Due to the differences in the physical properties of the soil samples, the relationship between GWC and water holding capacity varies between soil types. Therefore, the GWC for each soil type was selected based on the minimum moisture level required to achieve a homogeneous mixture after pH adjustment, rather than as an experimental variable under investigation. As demonstrated in section “Effect of soil moisture content”, GWC was shown to not significantly affect the sensor’s peak voltage measurement, and therefore these differences in moisture content do not significantly impact the validity of the calibration results. In order to achieve a homogeneous mixture in the sandy clay loam soil sample, the GWC was lowered to 40% and a Thinky AR-100 planetary mixer was used after each pH adjustment to ensure complete mixing. The clay loam soil was also mixed using a planetary mixer at 80% GWC for the same reason.

The results displayed in Fig. 5b show a slope of 50.47 mV/pH (R2 = 99.88%) for clay loam, and a slope of 58.53 mV/pH (R2 = 99.61%) for sandy clay loam. Both slopes closely matched the 50.27 mV/pH found in Miracle-Gro Potting Mix with similar R2 values, confirming that the sensor can accurately measure pH across soils with varying physical properties through simple linear fitting.

The two soils were selected to span contrasting ends of the agricultural texture spectrum, from a sand-dominated to a clay-dominated composition as this loam-type soil range is representative of the most preferred soils used in agriculture. The consistent sensor response across both confirms our sensor’s ability to reliably measure pH across these soil types through simple linear fitting.

Continuous pH sensing

Continuous soil pH monitoring presents several challenges due to factors like drift, soil heterogeneity, the need for manual calibration and adverse environmental conditions46–48. The sensor used in this study was designed to operate continuously without recalibration during deployment, and regardless of environmental conditions, allowing it to remain in the soil for extended periods of time. Prior to installation, each sensor undergoes an initial calibration to establish the relationship between peak voltage and pH to ensure maximum accuracy. Once in soil, no maintenance or recalibration is needed throughout the deployment period, reducing labor and human error, as well as ensuring its practical use for long-term monitoring until replacement is necessary. Because removal from soil may mechanically damage the Nafion membrane, the salt reservoir, or the printed traces, these sensors are intended for single-use deployments.

Three long-term experiments were conducted to validate the accuracy of the pH sensor over time. The experiments were done in Miracle-Gro Potting Mix at 60% GWC hydrated with DI water, since controlling the pH of the soil was not important for this experiment. The sensor was inserted into the soil and connected to the potentiostat. The soil was watered by hand or by an automated syringe pump through the experiment to maintain a relatively stable GWC throughout the experiment. pH measurements were also taken sporadically using the GS2 Pen Tester.

The experiments spanned 7 days, logging data every 30 min. By the end of the experiment, the sensors were still successfully logging data. The Pearson Correlation Coefficient observed between the manual pH readings and the sensor’s peak voltage measurements were -0.895, -0.837 -0.961 for each experiment, yielding an average R value of 0.898 and an R2 value of 80.59%. These results demonstrate that our sensor can reliably operate for extended periods of time, providing accurate measurements without the need for recalibration and human intervention after sensor installation (Fig. 6).

Long-term outdoor sensor deployment

In order to capture high spatio-temporal density pH data in field conditions, deployable systems that incorporate both the sensor and the readout electronics are needed. To provide robustness, reduce sensor-to-sensor variability, manufacture time, and noise level, as well as enable easy integration with readout electronics, we used FR4 PCBs as the substrate for long-term outdoor experiments.

The PCB-substrate sensors retain the same active materials, three-electrode configuration and readout technique as the PEN-substrate sensors: a carbon–alizarin working electrode, a Ag-AgCl reference electrode, and a Nafion membrane, with interrogation carried out via SWV peak position detection. The PCB substrate itself is not expected to significantly influence sensor response. The PVB-NaCl in the salt reservoir was replaced with PVB-KCl to provide additional reference electrode stability for long-term experiments24, and several layout iterations were tested to optimize electrode spacing and size. As shown in Fig. 7b, the PCB sensors display a similar response and sensitivity as the sensors made using a PEN substrate and follow the same linear calibration behavior as the PEN sensors, confirming that the sensing characteristics validated in section “Sensor response in soil”– and “Continuous pH sensing” are preserved in this format.

A custom, low-cost, weatherized field potentiostat system was designed and manufactured to enable continuous autonomous sampling. This system is based on the KickStat platform49, which uses a Texas Instruments LMP91000 analog front end potentiostat.

A sensor using a PCB substrate was deployed in soil outdoors, close to the Sustainability and Environmental Engineering Laboratory at the University of Colorado Boulder (40.0090° N, 105.2415° W). The sensor was buried next to a drip irrigation line to ensure adequate moisture levels in the soil throughout the length of the experiment. For this deployment, data was logged locally to an SD card for periodic collection. Future versions of this system could incorporate wireless telemetry to further automate data collection and enable real-time user response to sensor data.

The sensor was installed on April 21st, 2025 (Fig. 7a), the soil around it was compacted by hand to ensure contact with the electrodes, and three conditioning readings were taken to ensure that custom weatherized readout electronics were successfully logging data. Following installation, the measured peak position exhibited a gradual drift over approximately one week before stabilizing at around 0.34 V, corresponding to pH 7 based on calibration for that batch of sensors and the custom potentiostat (Fig. 7b). This initial stabilization period is attributed to the settling of the electrode–soil interface and to the equilibration of the printed Ag–AgCl reference electrode. Conditioning and burn-in behavior are commonly reported in printed Ag–AgCl reference electrodes23 with settling times that can vary from hours to multiple weeks50–52. Importantly, once stabilized, the peak position remained consistent and provided reliable measurements without requiring recalibration.

Following the first major rainfall event, the sensor detected a rapid increase in soil pH to approximately 7.9, which gradually returned to around pH 7 as the soil dried. The observed rise in soil pH is consistent with the mobilization of carbonates and bicarbonates naturally present in Colorado soils, which act as pH buffers and can temporarily elevate soil pH when wetted despite the acidity of the rainwater itself53. Additionally, heavy rainfall can leach alkaline compounds from deeper soil layers to the surface, further contributing to a transient pH increase in soils with high buffering capacity such as this one54. The absence of a comparable pH response during subsequent rainfall events in May is consistent with this interpretation, as the available alkaline reserves are likely to have been partially depleted by the first event, and as the soil had remained at a higher moisture level due to drip irrigation since that point, reducing the magnitude of additional leaching.. Significant influence of soil moisture on the sensor signal can be ruled out as a contributor to this observed pH change, as demonstrated in section “Effect of soil moisture content”.

During deployment, a diurnal oscillation in the measured pH signal was consistently observed, typically within ± 0.1 pH units and reaching approximately ± 0.25 pH units during the largest daily fluctuations. Soil temperatures, determined at a research station for a partially irrigated site at a depth of 5 cm, approximately 33 km from the test site55, are shown in Fig. S1 to provide an estimation of the soil temperature changes at the pH sensor test site. This temperature data shows typical daily swings of up to 10 °C. These temperature variations can impact the sensor signal due to the temperature dependence of the Ag–AgCl reference electrode and may contribute in part to the observed diurnal oscillations in peak position. In general, these changes are relatively small and occur at a predictable frequency, and are therefore not expected to obscure major soil chemistry events in many agricultural monitoring applications, where rapid changes and overall trends would be more critical to monitor than small temperature-dependent changes and diurnal cycling. However, if increased accuracy is required, a soil temperature sensor could readily be incorporated into the PCB to enable post-processing temperature compensation of the peak position.

After more than three months in the field and over 5500 interrogation cycles, the sensor continued collecting viable data without any human intervention. A weekly sample of the recorded raw data is shown in Fig. 7c. Although a gradual increase in current and reduction in peak height above baseline were observed over time, the shape of the redox peak remained clearly discernible, allowing for accurate detection of the peak position over the extended deployment. These results demonstrate that the PCB-based printed sensor and custom weatherized potentiostat can reliably operate under real field conditions for several months, maintaining functional stability and pH sensitivity.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a robust response for a solid-state potentiometric printed pH sensor over a range of soil conditions. The screen-printed sensor presents a readily scalable, deployable, low-maintenance option for continuous in-situ soil pH monitoring, potentially enabling high spatial and temporal density data captureautonomously. By relying on the voltage peak position, this approach mitigates the influence of significant environmental factors that impact current measurements, providing a relatively invariant signal across moisture levels, soil types, and soil compression state, with an observed accuracy of 0.1 pH units in the evaluated pH range under laboratory conditions. Results from initial deployments with a PCB-based sensor and a low-cost weatherized potentiostat demonstrate the potential to scale this technology to enable real-time soil pH management for precision agriculture.
