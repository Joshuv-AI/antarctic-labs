Graph Property Inference in Small Language Models: Effects of Representation and Inference Strategy
Michal Podstawski

Abstract
Recent progress in language modeling has expanded the range of tasks that can be approached through natural language interfaces, including problems that require structured reasoning. However, it remains unclear how effectively limited-capacity language models can infer formal properties of relational structures when those structures are presented in textual form. Understanding the conditions under which structured reasoning succeeds or fails is essential for applying small models in graph-based domains. We conduct a systematic study of graph-theoretic property inference in small instruction-tuned language models, isolating the roles of input representation and reasoning strategy. Across a diverse set of local and global graph metrics, we find that structural performance is highly sensitive to how relational information is organized. Representations that preserve neighborhood structure consistently improve estimation stability and ordinal consistency, while multi-branch reasoning yields the most reliable aggregate gains across configurations. These results show that graph property inference in small language models depends critically on representational organization and inference design. Structural competence is therefore shaped not only by model scale, but by how relational information is encoded and how predictions are elicited. The findings identify practical levers for improving structured inference under constrained model capacity.



1
Introduction

Language models are increasingly applied to tasks that require reasoning over relational data. Yet these models operate over linear token sequences rather than explicit structural representations, raising the question of how effectively they can infer formal properties of structured inputs.

Graphs provide a controlled testbed for examining this issue. They encode relational dependencies in a precise and well-defined manner and support diverse properties spanning local statistics, global connectivity, and combinatorial invariants. When processed by language models, however, graphs must be serialized into text, and their structural content must be recovered implicitly from sequential encodings.

Although prior work has explored graph-related reasoning tasks, systematic evidence on graph-theoretic property estimation remains limited. In particular, it is unclear how strongly performance depends on (i) the choice of serialization format and (ii) the inference strategy used to elicit predictions. Advances such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT)
[
1
]
and Graph-of-Thoughts (GoT)
[
2
]
suggest that reasoning structure can materially influence outcomes, but their interaction with representational design in graph settings has not been carefully isolated.

In this work, we conduct a controlled empirical study of graph property inference in small instruction-tuned language models. We compare adjacency-list and edge-list serializations under direct prediction, CoT reasoning, and GoT aggregation, evaluating performance across heterogeneous graph metrics.

Our results show that representation format systematically affects structural estimation, with adjacency-list encodings yielding lower normalized error and higher rank consistency. Multi-branch aggregation further improves macro-level performance. Together, these findings clarify how representation and inference design shape structural reasoning in limited-capacity language models.

Related Work

2.1
Reasoning in Language Models

Prompt-based reasoning has significantly advanced the problem-solving capabilities of language models. Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting demonstrates that explicitly eliciting intermediate reasoning steps substantially improves performance on arithmetic, symbolic, and logical reasoning tasks
[
1
,
3
]
. Subsequent work introduced self-consistency decoding, which aggregates multiple reasoning paths to improve robustness and accuracy
[
4
]
.

More recently, structured multi-branch reasoning frameworks such as Graph-of-Thoughts (GoT) generalize linear reasoning chains into graph-structured reasoning traces, enabling aggregation over alternative inference paths
[
2
]
. These approaches show that reasoning structure and aggregation mechanisms can materially influence performance, particularly for tasks requiring distributed evidence integration.

However, most evaluations of these techniques focus solely on mathematical benchmarks or commonsense reasoning tasks
[
6
,
7
]
. Their effectiveness for graph-theoretic property estimation, especially in small language models (SLMs), remains largely underexplored.

2.2
Graph Reasoning with Language Models

Recent work has investigated the ability of language models to perform graph-related reasoning tasks, including shortest-path computation, connectivity analysis, and symbolic graph manipulation
[
8
,
10
]
. These studies such as systematically evaluate structural reasoning capabilities and reveal both emergent competence and significant limitations
[
9
]
. Additional analyses highlight challenges in algorithmic generalization and multi-hop reasoning
[
11
]
.

While these works primarily evaluate task-level accuracy on individual reasoning problems, less attention has been given to systematic estimation of heterogeneous graph-theoretic metrics. Moreover, prior studies largely focus on large-scale models, leaving the structural inference capabilities of small scale SLMs insufficiently characterized.

2.3
Structured Representations and Inductive Bias

Transformers process token sequences without explicit structural inductive bias
[
12
]
. Consequently, the serialization format of structured inputs can significantly influence model behavior. Prior work on graph neural networks (GNNs) demonstrates that architectures with explicit relational inductive bias are well suited for structured data
[
13
,
14
]
. Graph transformer variants further attempt to incorporate structural encodings directly into attention mechanisms
[
15
]
.

In contrast, language models must infer structure implicitly from textual encodings. Research in structured prompting and code representation suggests that grouping semantically related elements improves reasoning stability and locality of attention
[
16
]
. Adjacency-list encodings naturally cluster neighbor information by node, potentially aligning better with transformer attention mechanisms than edge-list representations, which fragment relational information across token pairs.

Our work builds on these insights by empirically isolating the effects of serialization format and inference strategy on graph property estimation in small language models.

2.4
Positioning of This Work

Unlike prior studies that primarily evaluate discrete graph reasoning tasks or single-problem accuracy, we perform a controlled multi-factor analysis across:

•

Representation format (adjacency vs. edge list)

•

Inference strategy (baseline, CoT, GoT)

•

Multiple heterogeneous graph properties

By combining regression-based error metrics with ordinal consistency measures, our study provides a systematic characterization of structural sensitivity in small language models. This enables a more nuanced understanding of how representation design and reasoning scaffolding affect graph-theoretic inference under limited model capacity.

3
Experimental Protocol

Design Objective.

The experimental setup isolates two factors affecting structural inference in small language models: (i) graph serialization format and (ii) inference strategy. All other components - model size, decoding configuration, output constraints, and evaluation metrics - are held constant across conditions.

Prompts, generation settings, and evaluation scripts are released for reproducibility.

Models.

We evaluate two small instruction-tuned transformer models: Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct
[
18
]
and Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct
[
19
]
. These models were selected as representative high-performing instruction-tuned models in the 3B parameter class, based on publicly reported rankings on the LLM Leaderboard
[
17
]
. Using instruction-tuned models minimizes variance due to formatting instability, allowing clearer isolation of representation and inference effects.

Data.

Evaluation is conducted on benchmark graphs from the TinyGraphEstimator dataset
[
20
]
. The dataset consists of undirected, unweighted, and connected graphs spanning a range of sizes and structural configurations. For each graph, we evaluate twelve graph-theoretic properties covering degree statistics, clustering measures, path-based metrics, and discrete invariants. Specifically, these include: minimum degree, maximum degree, mean degree, degree standard deviation, graph density, average clustering coefficient, global clustering coefficient (transitivity), triangle count, average shortest path length, diameter, and chromatic number.

Graph Serialization.

Each graph is serialized in one of two formats:

•

Adjacency list (Adj):
node-wise neighbor grouping.

•

Edge list (Edge):
ordered edge pairs without grouping.

Both representations include explicit
(n,m)
headers to control for graph size information.
The two encodings differ only in token organization, enabling evaluation of locality effects independent of content.

Inference Strategies.

Three inference modes are implemented:

•

Baseline:
direct prediction under deterministic decoding.

•

Chain-of-Thought (CoT):
structured reasoning prompt under deterministic decoding.

•

Graph-of-Thoughts (GoT):
multi-branch stochastic sampling with aggregation.

Baseline and CoT use greedy decoding. GoT generates
B=15
independent branches per graph using temperature
T=0.7
. Random seeds are fixed for reproducibility.

GoT Aggregation.

GoT candidates are filtered using generic plausibility constraints without access to ground-truth values. Aggregation is performed via the median across branches, with integer rounding applied to discrete parameters.
Mean and mode aggregation are logged for robustness analysis.

Output Constraining.

All generations are constrained using JSON schema enforcement to ensure syntactic validity and eliminate formatting artifacts.

Evaluation Metrics.

Performance is evaluated using normalized error and rank-based consistency measures.
For each graph property, we compute the standard-deviation-normalized root mean squared error (NRMSE
{}_{\text{std}}
):

\text{NRMSE}_{\text{std}}=\frac{\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{y}_{i}-y_{i})^{2}}}{\sigma(y)},

where
\hat{y}_{i}
denotes the predicted value,
y_{i}
the ground truth, and
\sigma(y)
the empirical standard deviation of the target distribution.
This normalization scales prediction error relative to the intrinsic dispersion of each property, enabling meaningful comparison across heterogeneous metrics (e.g., density vs. triangle counts).
Macro-level comparisons aggregate NRMSE
{}_{\text{std}}
across properties.

To assess ordinal structural sensitivity independently of magnitude accuracy, we additionally report Spearman rank correlation (
\rho
).
This measures whether models preserve the relative ordering of graphs with respect to a given property, even when absolute estimates are imperfect.

For integer-valued properties
we further report exact-match accuracy and within-1 accuracy.
The latter captures bounded approximation behavior, which is particularly informative for discrete combinatorial invariants where small deviations may remain structurally meaningful.

4.5
Summary of Findings

Overall, the results demonstrate three consistent patterns:

•

Representation matters: adjacency-list encoding systematically improves normalized error and rank consistency.

•

Multi-branch reasoning helps: GoT yields the strongest macro-level improvements across models.

•

Structured sensitivity exists: despite moderate absolute error, models exhibit meaningful ordinal consistency and bounded approximation of discrete invariants.

Together, these findings indicate that small language models exhibit measurable structural awareness when graph inputs are serialized in structured formats and evaluated using appropriate reasoning strategies.

5
Discussion

The results reveal a nuanced picture of structural reasoning in small language models.
Although high-precision estimation of heterogeneous graph metrics remains challenging, performance is systematically shaped by both input representation and inference strategy.

The most consistent finding is the influence of serialization format.
Adjacency-list encoding reduces normalized error and improves rank consistency across both evaluated models.
This suggests that grouping neighbors by node better supports local aggregation of structural information within transformer architectures.
Edge-list representations, by contrast, distribute relational information across disjoint token pairs, potentially increasing attention fragmentation.

Inference strategy further modulates performance.
GoT aggregation yields the strongest macro-level improvements. The multi-branch structure of GoT may help integrate distributed structural cues that are difficult to consolidate through single linear reasoning traces. CoT reasoning exhibits more variable effects, indicating that explicit reasoning steps alone do not guarantee improved structural estimation.

Taken together, these findings indicate that small language models exhibit measurable structural awareness when provided with appropriately structured inputs and reasoning scaffolding, even though full graph-theoretic precision remains beyond current capabilities.

6
Limitations

This study isolates representation and inference strategy under controlled conditions, but several factors constrain the scope of the conclusions.

First, the evaluation is restricted to two 3B-scale instruction-tuned models. Although this design enables focused analysis of limited-capacity systems, the findings may not generalize directly to larger architectures or models trained under different regimes.

Second, graphs are serialized as text without explicit structural inductive bias.
Although this reflects realistic language-model usage, it inherently constrains representational efficiency compared to graph neural networks or specialized architectures.

Third, evaluation focuses on property estimation rather than procedural graph reasoning (e.g., explicit shortest-path construction).
Future work could examine step-wise structural reasoning to better understand intermediate representation dynamics.

Finally, macro-averaged normalized metrics aggregate heterogeneous properties with different statistical characteristics. Although normalization mitigates
scale effects, interpretation of absolute error values should be made cautiously.

7
Conclusion

This study provides a systematic empirical analysis of graph property inference in small language models.
We demonstrate that both representation format and inference strategy materially influence structural estimation performance.

Adjacency-list serialization consistently reduces normalized error and improves rank consistency relative to edge lists.
Graph-of-Thoughts aggregation yields the strongest macro-level improvements, suggesting that multi-branch reasoning partially compensates for structural integration limitations in small transformer models.
Despite moderate absolute errors across heterogeneous metrics, models exhibit meaningful ordinal sensitivity and can approximate discrete invariants within bounded error.

These findings show that structural reasoning in small language models is strongly conditioned on representational organization and inference design. More broadly, the results identify concrete methodological levers for improving structured inference in limited-capacity systems.
