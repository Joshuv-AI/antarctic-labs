Association of periodontal status with patient-reported outcomes: oral health-related quality of life and dental anxiety in a cross-sectional study
Authors: Resül Çolak, Murat İnanç Cengiz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) and dental hygiene fear across periodontal status groups, including periodontally healthy individuals and patients classified according to different stages of periodontitis, and to examine their associations with sociodemographic, behavioral, and clinical periodontal parameters.Materials and methodsThis single-center, cross-sectional observational study included 999 participants recruited from the Periodontology Clinic of Zonguldak Bülent Ecevit University Faculty of Dentistry between December 2018 and December 2019. Participants were classified into three groups according to periodontal status: periodontally healthy individuals (n = 399), those with combined Stage I–II periodontitis (n = 300), and those with combined Stage III–IV periodontitis (n = 300). Sociodemographic characteristics, oral hygiene habits, dental attendance patterns, and smoking status were recorded using structured questionnaires. Periodontal parameters, including the plaque index, gingival index, probing depth, clinical attachment level, and tooth loss, were assessed clinically. Oral health-related quality of life and dental hygiene fear were assessed using the Oral Health Impact Profile-14 (OHIP-14) and the Dental Hygiene Fear Scale (DHFS), respectively.ResultsThe OHIP-14 total scores increased progressively with periodontal disease severity, with the highest scores observed in the combined Stage III–IV periodontitis group. Significant intergroup differences were found for most of the OHIP-14 domains, except for psychological discomfort. The DHFS scores also differed significantly among the groups and were highest in patients with combined Stage III–IV periodontitis. The OHIP-14 scores were significantly associated with marital status, education level, smoking status, tooth brushing frequency, dental visit frequency, and time since the last dental visit. DHFS scores were higher in females, married individuals, and those with lower educational levels. The OHIP-14 showed positive correlations with DHFS and clinical periodontal parameters, including plaque index, gingival index, probing depth, clinical attachment level, tooth loss, and age.ConclusionsPeriodontitis severity was associated with a poorer oral health-related quality of life and higher dental hygiene fear. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating patient-reported outcomes into periodontal assessment and care. Nevertheless, the possibility of residual confounding from unmeasured socioeconomic and systemic factors cannot be excluded.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28122-5.Keywords: Periodontitis, Oral health-related quality of life, Dental fear, OHIP-14, Dental Hygiene Fear Scale

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic, non-communicable inflammatory disease associated with a subgingival dysbiotic biofilm and an altered host immune response, and is characterized by the progressive destruction of periodontal supporting tissues [1]. Clinically, the disease may manifest as clinical attachment loss, bleeding on probing, periodontal pocket formation, and radiographic alveolar bone loss; if left untreated, it may ultimately lead to tooth mobility and loss [2]. Global data indicate that severe periodontitis affected approximately 1.1 billion individuals in 2019, highlighting its continuing relevance as a major public health problem worldwide [3].

Despite its high burden, periodontal disease may remain unnoticed in its early stages. As the disease progresses, however, clinical signs and symptoms such as gingival bleeding, swelling, pain, and tooth mobility associated with supporting tissue loss may become more apparent [4, 5]. These symptoms are often reported together with difficulties in daily oral functions and reduced engagement in oral hygiene practices or routine dental care [6]. Individuals with periodontal disease may report difficulties in chewing, speech, nutrition, and general well-being [7, 8]. Therefore, evaluation of periodontitis may benefit from consideration of patient-reported outcomes, particularly oral health-related quality of life and dental fear, in addition to clinical periodontal parameters, as these measures may reflect the functional, psychological, and behavioral burdens associated with the disease.

Oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) reflects the perceived impact of oral diseases on an individual’s functional, psychological, and social well-being [9]. In individuals with periodontal disease, an impaired OHRQoL has been reported to be associated with clinical consequences such as pain, tooth loss, and functional limitations [10]. The short form of the Oral Health Impact Profile, the OHIP-14, is a validated and widely used instrument that has been translated into several languages to assess the OHRQoL in adult populations. Previous studies using the OHIP-14 have consistently reported that periodontal disease is associated with a poorer OHRQoL [11, 12]. Moreover, systematic reviews have reported associations between poorer OHIP-14 scores and greater periodontitis severity as well as more extensive periodontal tissue destruction [13, 14]. Additionally, previous intervention studies have reported improvements in OHRQoL following periodontal treatment, highlighting its relevance as a patient-centered outcome measure [15, 16].

In addition to quality of life, dental fear and anxiety may represent another important patient-centered dimension of periodontal disease. Previous studies have reported that individuals with periodontal disease tend to present higher levels of dental anxiety, and that dental anxiety is associated with periodontal health indicators [17, 18]. Higher levels of dental anxiety have been associated with less frequent dental attendance, reduced use of preventive periodontal care, poorer oral hygiene behaviors, and lower treatment compliance [19]. Therefore, these behavioral factors may be important to consider when evaluating periodontal health. Ng and Leung reported that dental fear was associated with periodontal clinical attachment loss and OHRQoL [20]. Similarly, higher levels of dental anxiety have been linked to poorer OHRQoL scores [21]. Moreover, a study conducted in individuals with periodontitis reported that self-perceptions related to dental anxiety and OHRQoL were associated with patient-centered aspects of periodontal disease. Therefore, it was suggested that these variables may provide valuable complementary information during periodontal assessment [17].

Various scales, including the Dental Anxiety Scale (DAS), Modified Dental Anxiety Scale (MDAS), and Dental Fear Survey (DFS), have been used to assess dental fear and anxiety [22–24]. However, these instruments primarily focus on general dental treatment anxiety and may be limited in their ability to capture fear specifically related to periodontal care and dental hygiene procedures. In response to this need, the Dental Hygiene Fear Scale (DHFS) was developed based on the theoretical model proposed by the Dental Fears Clinic at the University of Washington and includes dimensions such as specific dental hygiene stimuli, generalized anxiety, distrust of dental personnel, and fear of catastrophe [25]. The Turkish validity and reliability study emphasized that anxiety related to dental hygiene treatment is as important as anxiety related to other dental procedures and that the DHFS can be used as a valid and reliable instrument for evaluating clinical factors associated with this type of fear [26].

Although previous studies have demonstrated an association between periodontitis and impaired oral health-related quality of life, most available evidence has focused either on clinical periodontal parameters or on general dental anxiety measures. In addition, studies simultaneously evaluating oral health-related quality of life and dental hygiene–specific fear across clearly defined periodontal status groups based on the current classification system remain limited. Therefore, evaluating both OHIP-14 and DHFS scores may help characterize the patient-centered aspects associated with different periodontal status groups.

Nevertheless, studies evaluating the OHIP-14 and DHFS together across different periodontal status groups based on the current periodontal disease classification remain limited. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to evaluate oral health-related quality of life and dental hygiene fear in periodontally healthy individuals and in individuals with different severities of periodontitis.

Materials and methods

Study design, ethics, and consent

The present study was designed as a single-center, cross-sectional observational study and was reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines, and the completed STROBE checklist is provided as Supplementary File 1. The study was conducted at the Periodontology Clinic of Zonguldak Bülent Ecevit University Faculty of Dentistry between December 2018 and December 2019.

Prior to the initiation of the study, ethical approval was obtained from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Zonguldak Bülent Ecevit University (approval number: 2018/21 − 7). All participants were informed about the study procedures, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant before enrollment. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population, groups, and criteria

Participants were categorized into three groups according to their periodontal status and disease stage, based on the 2017 World Workshop on the Classification of Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases and Conditions [27]. Accordingly, the individuals were classified as periodontally healthy (Group 1), having combined Stage I–II periodontitis (Group 2), and having combined Stage III–IV periodontitis (Group 3).

The inclusion criteria were defined separately for each group. For all groups, participants were required to be literate, aged between 18 and 75 years, systemically healthy, not pregnant or lactating, have no history of chemotherapy or radiotherapy, and have not received any periodontal treatment within the last six months.

Periodontally healthy individuals (Group 1) were defined as having a mean probing depth of ≤ 3 mm, no clinical attachment loss, no signs of gingival inflammation, bleeding on probing < 10%, and no radiographic evidence of interproximal alveolar bone loss.

Participants in the combined Stage I–II periodontitis group (Group 2) were defined as having clinical attachment loss (CAL) of 1–2 mm (Stage I periodontitis) or 3–4 mm (Stage II periodontitis), radiographic bone loss confined to the coronal third of the root (0–15% or 15–33%), no history of tooth loss due to periodontitis, and probing depth ≤ 5 mm. Horizontal bone loss patterns were also evaluated.

Participants in the combined Stage III–IV periodontitis group (Group 3) were required to present with clinical attachment loss (CAL) ≥ 5 mm, probing depth (PD) ≥ 6 mm, radiographic bone loss extending to the middle or apical third of the root (≥ 33%), presence of Class II or III furcation involvement, Class II tooth mobility, and a history of ≥ 5 teeth lost due to periodontitis. Vertical (≥ 3 mm) and/or combined bone defects were also identified radiographically.

Individuals who did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded from the study. A total of 999 participants were included in the final analysis and categorized into three groups: periodontally healthy (n = 399), combined Stage I–II periodontitis (n = 300), and combined Stage III–IV periodontitis (n = 300) groups. This grouping was not intended to report the stages of the 2017 World Workshop classification separately, but was established in accordance with the primary aim of the study and the statistical comparison framework. Accordingly, Stage I and Stage II cases were combined into a Stage I–II periodontitis group, whereas Stage III and Stage IV cases were combined into a Stage III–IV periodontitis group. This approach represents an analytical grouping derived from the 2017 World Workshop classification rather than a direct stage-by-stage application of the original classification. Participants with incomplete questionnaire data, missing periodontal records, or insufficient radiographic information for periodontal classification were excluded from the final analysis. Only participants with complete clinical, radiographic, and questionnaire data were included in the statistical analyses.

Data collection and questionnaires

Before the clinical examination, a structured questionnaire was administered to collect the “participants' sociodemographic characteristics”, oral hygiene habits, and dental history. The recorded variables included age, sex, marital status, education level, and smoking status. Participants were also asked about their oral hygiene behaviors, including tooth brushing frequency, frequency of dental visits, and whether they had visited a dentist within the past year.

Oral health-related quality of life was assessed using the Oral Health Impact Profile-14 (OHIP-14), which was previously validated in Turkish by Mumcu et al. [28]. The scale consists of 14 items evaluating seven dimensions: functional limitation, physical pain, psychological discomfort, physical disability, psychological disability, social disability, and handicap. Each item was scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (almost always). The final score was calculated by summing the responses to all 14 items, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 56, according to the Likert scoring system [29]. Higher scores indicate a greater negative impact of periodontal disease on the quality of life [30].

Dental fear was evaluated using the Dental Hygiene Fear Scale (DHFS), which consists of 16 items. Ay et al. established the Turkish validity and reliability of this scale [26]. Responses were recorded using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (very often).

Periodontal assessment variables

All periodontal parameters were assessed by a single calibrated examiner (R.Ç.). A full-mouth periodontal examination was performed using a standardized periodontal probe (UNC-15, Hu-Friedy®, Chicago, IL, USA), and all measurements were obtained at six sites per tooth (mesiobuccal, distobuccal, mid-buccal, mesiolingual, distolingual, and mid-lingual). The assessed periodontal parameters included the Plaque Index (PI), Gingival Index (GI), probing depth (PD), and clinical attachment level (CAL).

Plaque Index (PI)

Assessed according to the Silness and Löe index to evaluate the amount of microbial dental plaque. The score ranged from 0 (no plaque) to 3 (abundant plaque and/or calculus) [31] .

Gingival Index (GI)

Evaluated using the Löe and Silness index to determine the presence and severity of gingival inflammation. Scores ranged from 0 (healthy gingiva) to 3 (severe inflammation with spontaneous bleeding) [32].

Probing Depth (PD)

Measured in millimeters using the same periodontal probe positioned parallel to the long axis of the tooth without applying excessive force.

Clinical Attachment Level (CAL)

Recorded as the distance between the cemento-enamel junction and the base of the periodontal pocket.

For each participant, full mouth mean values of all periodontal parameters were calculated. Based on these clinical measurements in combination with radiographic findings, participants were classified into three groups (periodontally healthy, combined Stage I–II periodontitis, and combined Stage III–IV periodontitis) according to periodontal disease severity.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated based on the prevalence of periodontal disease reported in a previous large-scale study by Liu et al. [18]. In that study, periodontal disease was identified in 366 of 1,203 individuals; therefore, the expected prevalence was accepted as 30.4%. Using the formula n = Z² × p × (1 − p) / d², with a 95% confidence level and a 3% margin of error, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 904 individuals. Considering a possible 10% data loss, the target sample size was increased to 995 participants. Accordingly, the completion of the present study with 999 participants was considered sufficient to meet the required sample size. In addition to this prevalence-based sample size estimation, a sensitivity power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.7 for the multivariable linear regression models. With a total sample size of 999, α = 0.05, power = 0.90, and 13 predictors, the minimum detectable effect size was Cohen’s f² = 0.0227, indicating that the study had sufficient power to detect small effects in the adjusted regression analyses.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and jamovi software (version 2.3.28). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons of non-normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for two-group comparisons, whereas the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparisons among periodontal groups. Bonferroni correction was applied for post-hoc pairwise comparisons. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test. The relationships between variables were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median (minimum–maximum), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To assess the sample-specific internal consistency reliability of the patient-reported outcome measures, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated separately for the OHIP-14 and DHFS scales using item-level responses.

Crude and adjusted linear regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association between periodontal status and OHIP-14 and DHFS total scores. Separate models were constructed for each outcome variable. In the crude models, periodontal status was included as the only independent variable. In the adjusted models, periodontal status was included as the main independent variable, whereas age, sex, marital status, education level, smoking status, toothbrushing frequency, and frequency of dental visits were entered as covariates. The periodontally healthy group was used as the reference category. Regression results were expressed as unstandardized β coefficients with 95% confidence intervals. The models were checked for multicollinearity.

Intra-examiner reliability was evaluated using repeated measurements performed by the same examiner (R.Ç.) in 60 participants with a two-week interval. For periodontal clinical parameters, including Plaque Index, Gingival Index, probing depth, clinical attachment level, and radiographic bone loss, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated using patient-level mean values. Intra-examiner agreement for periodontal disease diagnosis was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Intra-examiner reliability was assessed using repeated measurements. Cohen’s kappa coefficient for periodontal disease diagnosis indicated excellent agreement (κ = 0.958, p < 0.001). All intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) values calculated for periodontal clinical parameters were above 0.90, demonstrating excellent intra-examiner reliability.

In total, 999 individuals who presented to the periodontology clinic were evaluated (484 males, 515 females; mean age: 35.95 ± 10.74 years). Participants were categorized into three groups according to periodontal status: Group 1 (healthy), Group 2 (Combined Stage I–II periodontitis), and Group 3 (Combined Stage III–IV periodontitis). Age differed significantly among the groups (p < 0.001; 30.13 ± 9.24, 35.23 ± 7.93, and 44.42 ± 9.56 years for Groups 1, 2, and 3, respectively).

The distribution of sociodemographic characteristics and oral hygiene behaviors across the groups is presented in Table 1. Sex distribution was similar across the groups (p = 0.163). There were statistically significant differences between the groups in terms of marital status and education level (p < 0.001). The proportion of married individuals was 35.1% in Group 1, 63.3% in Group 2, and 88.3% in Group 3. Similarly, education level differed significantly across the groups (p < 0.001), with lower education levels being more prevalent in the periodontitis groups.

When oral hygiene habits were evaluated, toothbrushing frequency was found to be lower in the combined Stage III–IV periodontitis group (p < 0.001). Dental attendance patterns also differed significantly between the groups (p < 0.001). Regular dental visits were more common in the healthy group, whereas visits driven by complaints were more frequent in the periodontitis groups.

Additionally, the proportion of individuals who had visited a dentist within the past year was higher in the healthy group (p < 0.001). A history of periodontal treatment was also more commonly reported among healthy individuals (p < 0.001).

Periodontal clinical parameters (PI, GI, PD, and CAL) increased progressively across the groups, with the lowest values observed in the healthy group and the highest in the combined Stage III–IV periodontitis group (p < 0.001 for all).

OHIP-14 total and subdomain scores differed across the groups (Table 2). The total OHIP-14 score increased significantly from Group 1 to Group 3 (median: 7, 10, and 14, respectively; p < 0.001). Across the OHIP-14 subdomains, healthy individuals (Group 1) and the combined Stage I–II periodontitis group (Group 2) exhibited comparable functional limitation and physical pain, whereas the combined Stage III–IV periodontitis group (Group 3) differed significantly from both (p < 0.001). In contrast, physical disability, psychological disability, social disability, and handicap differed across all groups (p < 0.001). However, no significant differences in psychological discomfort were identified among the groups (p = 0.399).

DHFS scores also showed statistically significant differences across the groups (Table 2) and increased with the severity of periodontitis (p < 0.001), with post hoc comparisons indicating no significant difference between healthy individuals (Group 1) and the combined Stage I–II periodontitis group (Group 2), while the combined Stage III–IV periodontitis group (Group 3) differed significantly from both. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.806 for the OHIP-14 and 0.748 for the DHFS, indicating good internal consistency reliability for the OHIP-14 and acceptable internal consistency reliability for the DHFS in the present sample.

To evaluate the potential heterogeneity within the combined Stage III–IV periodontitis group, Stage III and Stage IV cases were compared in terms of OHIP-14 and DHFS scores. Stage III cases constituted 70.7% of this group, whereas Stage IV cases accounted for 29.3%. No statistically significant differences were observed between Stage III and Stage IV cases in OHIP-14 total scores or DHFS total scores. Among the OHIP-14 subdomains, only the handicap domain differed significantly between Stage III and Stage IV cases (p = 0.041), whereas the remaining subdomains showed no significant differences. Given the smaller proportion of Stage IV cases within Group 3, the statistical power of this subgroup comparison may have been limited. These findings are presented in Supplementary Table S1. The associations between OHIP-14 and DHFS scores and sociodemographic and behavioral factors are presented in Table 3. OHIP-14 scores did not differ according to sex (p = 0.072). However, higher scores were observed among married individuals (p = 0.001), and OHIP-14 scores decreased as the level of education increased (p = 0.001). OHIP-14 scores also differed significantly according to smoking status and daily smoking frequency (p = 0.001 for both). When oral hygiene habits were evaluated, OHIP-14 scores decreased with increasing toothbrushing frequency (p = 0.001). Similarly, more frequent dental visits were associated with lower OHIP-14 scores (p = 0.001), and individuals who had visited a dentist within the past year had lower scores (p = 0.001). To further evaluate whether periodontal status was associated with patient-reported outcomes independently of sociodemographic and behavioral factors, crude and adjusted linear regression analyses were performed (Table 5). In the crude model, both combined Stage I–II periodontitis and combined Stage III–IV periodontitis were associated with higher OHIP-14 scores compared with the periodontally healthy group (β = 1.48, 95% CI: 0.37–2.60; β = 5.00, 95% CI: 3.88–6.11, respectively). After adjustment for age, sex, marital status, education level, smoking status, toothbrushing frequency, and frequency of dental visits, only combined Stage III–IV periodontitis remained significantly associated with higher OHIP-14 scores (β = 3.93, 95% CI: 2.42–5.45). For DHFS scores, combined Stage III–IV periodontitis was significantly associated with higher scores in the crude model (β = 1.69, 95% CI: 0.34–3.04); however, this association was no longer significant after adjustment (β = 0.97, 95% CI: −0.83–2.78). In the full adjusted models including all covariates, smoking status and irregular dental attendance were also associated with higher OHIP-14 scores, while female sex and lower educational level were associated with higher DHFS scores (Supplementary Table S2).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study aimed to evaluate the oral health-related quality of life and levels of dental fear in individuals with different stages of periodontitis and in periodontally healthy individuals. Overall, the findings indicate that periodontal status is associated with both patient-reported quality of life and dental fear. In addition, the observed relationship between OHIP-14 scores and DHFS suggests that the association between periodontal status and patient-reported outcomes may extend beyond clinical parameters and may involve psychological and quality-of-life dimensions.

Patient-centered outcomes have become increasingly important in periodontology because clinical parameters alone may not fully reflect the perceived burden of disease. Oral health-related quality of life measures allow periodontal disease to be evaluated not only through clinical parameters, but also in relation to daily life, functional comfort, and psychosocial well-being. The OHIP-14 is a widely used short-form instrument for assessing the functional, psychological, and social consequences of oral conditions [33]. The Turkish version of the OHIP-14 has been validated by Mumcu et al. and shown to have strong internal consistency [28], supporting its use in the present study population. Recent studies have also shown that oral conditions are associated with poorer OHRQoL through pain, functional limitations, psychological distress, and dental complications [34, 35]. This supports the broader relevance of patient-reported outcomes in understanding the burden of oral diseases beyond clinical parameters.

The present study showed a stepwise increase in OHIP-14 total scores with more advanced periodontal status, indicating that poorer oral health-related quality of life was more frequently observed in individuals with more advanced periodontal disease. This finding is consistent with systematic reviews and meta-analyses reporting that periodontitis is negatively associated with oral health-related quality of life [14, 36]. The more pronounced impairment observed in the combined Stage III–IV periodontitis group indicates that advanced periodontal findings may be associated with a greater functional, aesthetic, and psychosocial burden. Similarly, previous studies have reported that severe periodontitis is associated with poorer quality of life than mild or moderate periodontitis [37, 38].

In the present study, periodontal stages were not analyzed as fully separate categories according to the original 2017 classification. Instead, Stage I–II cases were grouped together as Group 2, whereas Stage III–IV cases were grouped together as Group 3, in accordance with the primary analytical framework of the study. This approach was used to facilitate the comparison and statistical modelling of patient-reported outcomes. However, this grouping may have reduced clinical granularity, particularly because Stage IV periodontitis may involve additional complexity factors such as advanced tooth loss, masticatory dysfunction, occlusal collapse, or loss of vertical dimension. Therefore, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed by comparing Stage III and Stage IV cases within Group 3. No statistically significant differences were observed between Stage III and Stage IV cases in OHIP-14 total scores or DHFS total scores; only the OHIP-14 handicap subdomain differed significantly between the two stages. Although these findings suggest that the grouping used in Group 3 did not substantially alter the overall pattern of patient-reported outcomes, potential stage-specific differences may have remained undetected because Stage IV cases represented a smaller proportion of Group 3. Future studies specifically designed to evaluate periodontal stages separately, with a more balanced distribution of Stage III and Stage IV cases, may provide more detailed evidence regarding stage-specific differences in oral health-related quality of life and dental hygiene fear.

The regression analyses further clarified the extent to which periodontal status was independently associated with patient-reported outcomes. While OHIP-14 scores were higher in both periodontitis groups in the crude models, the adjusted model showed that this association remained significant only for the combined Stage III–IV periodontitis group. This finding suggests that poorer oral health-related quality of life may be particularly evident in advanced periodontal disease. For DHFS scores, the crude association observed in the combined Stage III–IV periodontitis group was no longer significant after adjustment for sociodemographic and behavioral covariates. This suggests that dental hygiene fear may be influenced more strongly by patient-related factors such as sex and education level than by periodontal disease severity itself. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as adjusted associations rather than causal effects.

OHIP-14 domain findings showed that functional limitation and physical pain did not differ between healthy and combined Stage I–II periodontitis groups, suggesting that early periodontal changes may be less perceptible to patients. In contrast, differences in physical disability, social disability, and handicap indicate that OHRQoL impairment becomes more multidimensional in advanced disease. The lack of difference in psychological discomfort is consistent with reports that some psychological domains may be less sensitive to periodontal severity [38], while the overall domain pattern supports previous findings [17, 39].

Dental fear reflects the psychological burden related to periodontal care. Dental anxiety is common in the Turkish population, with reported prevalence rates of 21.3%–23.5% [40]. DHFS scores were highest in the combined Stage III–IV periodontitis group in crude comparisons, consistent with studies linking periodontal disease and poorer OHRQoL with higher dental fear or anxiety [10, 18]. Although dental fear has been associated with treatment avoidance and periodontal progression [6, 18, 41], the cross-sectional design prevents determining whether fear precedes, follows, or coexists with poorer periodontal status.

Sociodemographic characteristics provide important context for interpreting patient-reported outcomes. Sex distribution did not differ among periodontal groups, and OHIP-14 scores were not significantly associated with sex, which is consistent with previous studies [42–44]. However, DHFS scores were higher in women, in line with studies reporting higher dental fear levels among women [40, 45–47].

Age increased with periodontal disease severity, supporting evidence that periodontal burden accumulates with age [48, 49]. Although OHIP-14 scores correlated positively with age, age was not independently associated with OHIP-14 after adjustment, which may help explain inconsistent reports showing either improved [50, 51] or poorer OHRQoL in older individuals [52, 53]. Similarly, age was not crudely correlated with DHFS [54, 55], but its negative adjusted association partly supports studies reporting age-related differences in dental fear [56, 57].

Marital status was associated with periodontal disease severity and patient-reported outcomes. Married individuals were more frequent in the combined Stage III–IV periodontitis group and had higher OHIP-14 scores, contrasting with studies reporting poorer quality of life among single individuals [57], or no marital status-related difference [58]. However, this association was only borderline significant for OHIP-14 after adjustment (β = 1.12, 95% CI: −0.09 to 2.35, p = 0.071). Similarly, higher DHFS scores among married individuals in unadjusted analysis were no longer significant after adjustment (β = 1.02, 95% CI: −0.44 to 2.49, p = 0.171), consistent with studies reporting no association with dental fear [40, 59].

Educational level may partly explain differences in periodontal status and patient-reported outcomes. Primary school graduates were more frequent in the combined Stage III–IV periodontitis group, whereas university and postgraduate graduates were more common among periodontally healthy individuals, consistent with previous reports [42, 60]. Although OHIP-14 scores decreased with higher education in unadjusted analysis, education was not independently associated with OHIP-14 after adjustment. In contrast, higher education remained significantly associated with lower DHFS scores, supporting studies linking higher education with lower dental fear [6, 42, 61, 62], despite inconsistent findings in dental anxiety research [63, 64].

Behavioral factors were mainly related to periodontal status and oral health-related quality of life. Regular toothbrushing and dental visits were more common among periodontally healthy individuals, whereas irregular toothbrushing and complaint-driven visits were more frequent in the combined Stage III–IV periodontitis group. In the adjusted model, dental visit frequency remained significantly associated with OHIP-14 scores, with higher scores among individuals who attended occasionally or only when needed, supporting previous findings linking irregular dental attendance with poorer periodontal status and oral health-related quality of life [65–69]. In contrast, toothbrushing frequency was not independently associated with OHIP-14, and neither behavioral variable was significantly associated with DHFS scores, consistent with Ay et al. [70].

Among behavioral risk indicators, smoking showed a particularly clear association with periodontal status and quality of life. Smoking-related variables differed significantly among the groups, with smoking and higher daily cigarette consumption being more frequent in the periodontitis groups, particularly in the combined Stage III–IV periodontitis group. This finding is consistent with previous studies identifying smoking as an important risk factor for periodontitis and showing a relationship between smoking intensity and periodontal disease severity [71, 72]. Smokers also had higher OHIP-14 scores than non-smokers, indicating poorer oral health-related quality of life. This result is in line with studies reporting an association between smoking and lower oral health-related quality of life [73–75].

Correlation analyses further supported the relationship between clinical periodontal deterioration and patient-perceived oral health burden. Weak but significant positive correlations were observed between OHIP-14 scores and PD, CAL, GI, PI, and tooth loss, suggesting that poorer periodontal clinical status was associated with worse OHRQoL. However, the statistically significant correlations between DHFS scores and clinical periodontal parameters were very weak in magnitude; therefore, these findings should be interpreted as statistically detectable associations in a large sample rather than clinically strong relationships. Because CAL, PD, and tooth loss contributed to the classification of periodontal severity, the observed correlations between these parameters and OHIP-14 or DHFS should be interpreted with caution, as part of these associations may reflect the underlying disease classification framework. Clinical consequences such as gingival redness, bleeding, gingival recession, tooth loss, and persistent halitosis may be associated with lower self-esteem and poorer quality of life [39, 76]. Similarly, our previous cross-sectional pilot study using periodontal risk assessment showed that higher plaque index, gingival index, and probing pocket depth were key factors associated with increased periodontal risk in young adults, supporting the clinical relevance of these parameters in periodontal risk evaluation [77]. Similar associations between periodontal clinical parameters and OHIP-14 scores have also been reported in previous studies [39, 61, 69, 78].

Finally, the positive correlation between DHFS and OHIP-14 scores indicates that higher dental fear is associated with poorer oral health-related quality of life. This relationship may be explained by its effects on dental pain, care avoidance, eating difficulties, sleep problems, and daily functioning [20, 79, 80]. However, associations between periodontal status, OHRQoL, and dental fear should be interpreted cautiously because of possible unmeasured confounding factors.

Taken together, these findings indicate that periodontal disease burden should be interpreted beyond clinical parameters, as severity intersects with sociodemographic factors, oral health behaviors, quality of life, and dental fear. A major strength of this study is its large sample size (n = 999) and three-level assessment of periodontal disease severity. Moreover, evaluating clinical periodontal parameters together with OHIP-14 and DHFS enabled a broader assessment of the biological and psychosocial dimensions of periodontal disease. From a clinical perspective, OHIP-14 and DHFS assessments may provide complementary information in periodontal care by identifying patients with poorer perceived oral health-related quality of life or higher dental fear. These patient-reported dimensions may support individualized communication, oral hygiene motivation, and patient-centered treatment planning.

The present study has some limitations. Its single-center, clinic-based design in Turkey may have introduced selection or referral bias and may limit the generalizability of the findings to the general population and to populations outside Turkey. Periodontal stages and grades were not analyzed separately according to the 2017 classification, which may have masked stage- and grade-specific differences in oral health-related quality of life and dental hygiene fear. Moreover, the causes of tooth loss were not separately classified; therefore, the association between tooth loss and OHIP-14 may not exclusively reflect the functional burden of periodontal destruction. Due to the cross-sectional design, causal relationships and temporal directions cannot be established, and reverse causation cannot be excluded. Patient-reported measures may have introduced response or self-reporting bias, and residual confounding may remain despite adjustment because some potentially relevant covariates were not available in the dataset. In particular, socioeconomic indicators such as income and occupation, systemic inflammatory conditions such as diabetes mellitus, and previous traumatic dental experiences could not be included in the adjusted models. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered fully independent of these unmeasured factors. Although the initial sample size calculation was based on periodontal disease prevalence, this limitation was partly addressed by an additional sensitivity power analysis for the multivariable linear regression models.

Conclusions

More severe periodontal status, particularly combined Stage III–IV periodontitis, was associated with poorer oral health-related quality of life, and this association remained evident after adjustment for potential confounding factors. Although higher dental anxiety scores were observed in individuals with advanced periodontitis in the crude analyses, this association was attenuated after adjustment. Poorer clinical periodontal parameters were also associated with worse patient-reported outcomes. These findings suggest that periodontal evaluation may benefit from consideration of patient-reported outcomes, particularly oral health-related quality of life, as complementary measures for a more patient-centered approach to periodontal care.
