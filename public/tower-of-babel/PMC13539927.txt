Effects of Cladophora glomerata and Limnospira platensis on growth and photosynthetic pigments of safflower (Carthamus tinctorius L.)
Authors: Göksal Sezen, Selim Doblan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntensive use of synthetic fertilizers has raised significant concerns due to their adverse impacts on the environment, soil quality, and human health. This has increased interest in sustainable agricultural inputs that can improve crop performance while reducing dependence on fertilizers. Algae-based biostimulants are promising in this context because they contain nutrients and bioactive compounds that may improve plant growth, physiological activity, and yield.MethodsThis study evaluated the effects of Limnospira platensis and Cladophora glomerata on growth and yield components of safflower (Carthamus tinctorius L.) cv. Remzibey-05. Different concentrations of L. platensis (control, 0.25%, 0.50%, 0.75%, 1.00%, and 1.25%) and C. glomerata (control, 1%, and 2%) were applied either individually or in combination. Treatments were applied at three growth stages, including rosette, stem elongation and branching, and flowering. Agronomic, physiological, and yield-related traits, including plant height, root length, flower head diameter, fresh plant weight, root weight, flower head weight, number of flower heads, number of seeds per flower head, seed yield per plant, 1000-seed weight, chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoid contents, were recorded at harvest.ResultsAll treatments significantly improved the recorded traits compared with the untreated control (p < 0.05). The greatest increase in plant height (45.3%) was recorded with the sole application of 1% L. platensis. The combined application of 1.25% L. platensis and 1% C. glomerata caused the highest increases in fresh plant weight and root weight (64.5% and 91.8%, respectively). The highest increase (72%) in Chlorophyll a content was recorded with the combined application of 1% L. platensis and 2% C. glomerata. Similarly, the combined application of 1.25% L. platensis and 1% C. glomerata increased seed yield per plant and 1000-seed weight by 210% and 116%, respectively, compared with the control.ConclusionCombined application of L. platensis and C. glomerata significantly improved safflower growth, photosynthetic pigment accumulation, and yield-related traits under greenhouse conditions. The combined application of 1.25% L. platensis and 1% C. glomerata resulted in the highest seed yield per plant and 1000-seed weight. These results indicate that the combined use of algae-based biostimulants may represent a promising low-input approach for enhancing safflower productivity while reducing reliance on synthetic fertilizers. Field-based validation across contrasting environments is required to confirm their agronomic consistency and support their wider use in sustainable safflower production.

Background

Excessive use of fertilizers and pesticides since the middle of the 20th century has significantly improved global crop production. Nevertheless, long-term reliance on fertilizers caused significant environmental, soil, and human health concerns [1–3]. Continued use of synthetic fertilizers alters soil pH, reduces microbial diversity, reduces organic matter content, and creates uneven nutrient balance in the soil. Runoff increases nutrient loading in surface waters, contaminates groundwater, and contributes to emissions of climate-warming gases [4–6]. Moreover, rising fertilizer prices impose a substantial financial burden on farmers, particularly in developing countries. These challenges have intensified the need for sustainable alternatives that can maintain or improve crop productivity while reducing environmental damage and production costs [4–6].

Algae are among the most promising biological alternatives for use as fertilizers and plant growth promoters. Both microalgae and macroalgae contain a wide range of bioactive compounds that can support plant growth and improve crop performance. These bioactive compounds include macro- and micronutrients, amino acids, polysaccharides, vitamins, and phytohormone-like substances. Algal biomass and extracts can accelerate seed germination, stimulate root development, enhance nutrient uptake, improve photosynthetic efficiency, and increase plant tolerance to environmental stresses [7–12]. From an ecological perspective, algal-based inputs can support soil health, reduce chemical losses to the environment, preserve microbial diversity, and promote more sustainable farming systems. Economically, they offer farmers an opportunity to reduce input costs, stabilize yields under resource-limited environments, and improve the profitability of crop production in marginal or low-input environments [10, 13].

Algae have a significant value in organic and sustainable farming systems since they provide a renewable, low-input source of nutrients and growth-promoting compounds without many of the environmental risks associated with synthetic chemicals. Their use also aligns with the principles of a circular economy by converting abundant natural biomass or waste-derived resources into valuable agricultural inputs. In this way, algal-based products can reduce dependence on fossil fuel–based fertilizers and contribute to the long-term maintenance of soil fertility [6, 11]. Controlled greenhouse experiments are essential for evaluating algal-based treatments under stable conditions. They enable researchers to isolate treatment effects, optimize dose and timing of application, and identify potential synergistic responses before progressing to more variable field trials [13, 14]. Such work provides essential baseline evidence for developing practical, science-based recommendations for farmers.

Limnospira platensis (Gomont) K.R.S. Santos & Hentschke 2023 (Synonym: Spirulina platensis (Gomont) Geitle) belongs to the cyanobacterium group and forms filaments. It has high levels of protein, vitamins, and minerals, as well as auxins and cytokinins. It promotes shoot growth, chlorophyll synthesis, and stress tolerance in various crops [14, 15]. Cladophora glomerata (Linnaeus) Kützing, 1843, is a freshwater macroalga commonly found in river systems. It contains large amounts of polysaccharides, amino acids, and trace elements that can improve soil structure and root growth [16, 17]. Although both types have shown promising results when used alone, information on their combined use remains limited.

Safflower (Carthamus tinctorius L.) is an important oilseed crop belonging to the Asteraceae family. It is well adapted to arid and semi-arid environments and is cultivated for edible oil, industrial dyes, and medicinal purposes. Its cultivation has expanded in water-limited regions due to its tolerance to drought and high-temperature stress [18, 19]. However, safflower productivity in less fertile soils is often constrained by limited nutrient availability and rising fertilizer costs. Algal-based applications may provide a practical and environmentally sound strategy to improve safflower growth and yield while reducing dependence on chemical inputs and supporting the goals of organic and sustainable agriculture.

Despite growing interest in algae-based biostimulants, little is known about the effect of the combined use of microalgae and freshwater macroalgae as natural inputs on safflower production under fertilizer-free conditions. Hence, the present study tested the individual and interactive effects of locally sourced L. platensis and C. glomerata on safflower growth, physiological performance, and yield-related traits. The objective was to determine whether individual or combined use of different doses of these algae could improve safflower performance under controlled greenhouse conditions without the addition of mineral fertilizer. It was hypothesized that algae-based treatments would enhance vegetative growth, photosynthetic pigment accumulation, and seed production compared with the untreated control. It was further hypothesized that the combined application of L. platensis and C. glomerata would yield stronger responses than either applied individually.

Materials and methods

Algae preparation

Cladophora glomerata

Cladophora glomerata was collected manually with a rake from shallow waters along the banks of the Euphrates River in Şanlıurfa, Türkiye, during April 2022 (Fig. 1). The fresh biomass was immediately transported to the laboratory, and visible impurities, including stones and shells, were removed by hand. The material was then washed several times with tap water to eliminate sand, mud, epiphytic plants, and other adhering debris. After washing, the biomass was dried in a hot-air oven at 40 °C until a constant weight. The dried material was ground into a fine powder using a laboratory blender and stored in sealed containers at − 20 °C until use. This preparation procedure follows commonly used methods for processing freshwater macroalgae intended for use as plant growth-promoting and soil-amending materials [9, 16, 17].

Limnospira platensis

The living culture of L. platensis was obtained from the Faculty of Fisheries, Çukurova University, Türkiye. Limnospira platensis was cultivated in Zarrouk’s Spirulina medium adjusted to pH 9.5, using 20-L transparent plastic bottles as culture vessels. The cultures were maintained at 30 ± 2 °C under a 16-hour light/8-hour dark cycle using cool-white LED lamps that provided 4000 ± 100 lx. Air pumps kept up constant aeration. Biomass was harvested during the late exponential growth phase (8–10 days after inoculation) by filtration through a 20-µm plankton mesh. The collected biomass was washed three times with distilled water, dried at 40 °C until constant weight, ground into a fine powder using a high-speed blender, and stored at − 20 °C until use. These cultivation, harvesting, and drying procedures are consistent with commonly used protocols for preparing L. platensis biomass for fertilizer or biostimulant applications [8, 12, 15].

The chemical and biochemical composition of the two algal species used in this study has recently been characterized and reported [20–22]. Their composition has also been documented in several related studies [9, 23, 24]. These studies have indicated that both species are rich sources of essential macro- and micronutrients, as well as bioactive compounds, including phytohormones and other plant growth-promoting substances.

The selected concentrations of L. platensis (0.25%, 0.50%, 0.75%, 1.00%, and 1.25% w/v) and C. glomerata (1% and 2% w/v) were based on previous dose–response studies conducted with these and related algal species. These concentration ranges have been reported to stimulate plant growth without causing phytotoxic effects across different crops and experimental conditions [8, 12, 15, 17].

Experimental setup

Seeds of safflower cultivar Remzibey-05 were obtained from the GAP Agricultural Research Institute in Şanlıurfa. Eight seeds were sown in each 7-L plastic pot filled with 6 kg of air-dried field soil that had been passed through a 2-mm sieve. The soil was collected from the 0–20 cm layer of an uncultivated site. Before sowing, the soil was characterized as alkaline and calcareous, with pH 8.5, electrical conductivity 0.1 dS m⁻¹, CaCO₃ 21.1%, total N 0.051%, available P 48.6 kg ha⁻¹, exchangeable K 1204 kg ha⁻¹, and organic matter 1.68%. After emergence, seedlings were thinned to three uniform, healthy plants per pot.

Different concentrations of L. platensis (control, 0.25%, 0.50%, 0.75%, 1.00%, and 1.25% w/v) and C. glomerata (control, 1%, and 2% w/v) were applied either individually or in combination (Tables 1, 2 and 3). The treatments were applied at equal volumes during three growth stages, i.e., rosette, stem elongation and branching, and flowering. No mineral fertilizer was applied to any treatment, including the control. This fertilizer-free design allowed the observed plant responses to be attributed to the algal applications and enabled direct comparison with the untreated control.

Pots were irrigated with tap water to maintain soil moisture at ~ 60–70% of field capacity. Irrigation was applied every 5 days from sowing to flowering and every 4 days from flowering to harvest. The final irrigation was applied 10 days before harvest to minimize possible interference with pigment measurements.

Fresh algal suspensions were prepared in tap water on the day of application and applied as a soil drench at the rosette stage (25 days after sowing), stem elongation and branching (58 days after sowing), and flowering (75 days after sowing). The required amount of dry algal biomass for each treatment was suspended in a final volume of 200 mL per pot at each application. The experiment was arranged in a randomized complete block design with three replicates for each of the 18 treatment combinations. The greenhouse study was conducted under natural daylight from 30 May to 5 September 2023. The mean daytime temperature was 34 °C, with a maximum of 45 °C during the experimental period. Plants were harvested at full maturity (101 days after sowing). No visible symptoms of pest or disease infestation were observed during the experiment.

Growth parameters

Plant height, root length, capitulum diameter, number of capitula per plant, fresh plant weight, root fresh weight, capitulum fresh weight, number of seeds per capitulum, total grain weight per plant, and 1000-grain weight were recorded at harvest using standard procedures [25, 26]. All fresh weight measurements were taken immediately after harvest to minimize moisture loss. These traits were selected because they represent key indicators of vegetative growth, reproductive development, and yield formation in safflower and are commonly used to evaluate the effects of algal-based biostimulants on crop performance [14, 15, 17].

Chlorophyll and carotenoid contents

Leaf samples for pigment analysis were collected at the time of the final irrigation (10 days before harvest) and stored at − 20 °C until analysis. For extraction, 0.5 g of fresh leaf tissue was homogenized in 50 mL of 95% ethanol. The homogenate was incubated in a water bath at 80 °C for 30–35 min and then cooled to room temperature. The extract was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 10 min, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 664, 649, and 470 nm using a Thermo Scientific Multiskan GO spectrophotometer. Chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and total carotenoid contents were calculated according to Vaghela et al. where C is the pigment concentration in the extract, V is the final extract volume, and W is the fresh weight of leaf tissue used for extraction.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to test the main effects of L. platensis and C. glomerata doses, as well as their interaction. Normality and homogeneity of variance were checked using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. When necessary, data were log- or square-root-transformed to meet model assumptions. Significant treatment effects were further compared using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed in JMP Pro 16 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Results are presented as means of three replicates, and different letters within each column indicate significant differences according to Tukey’s HSD test at p < 0.05 [28, 29].

Results

Vegetative growth traits

The individual and combined applications of L. platensis and C. glomerata significantly improved all vegetative growth traits compared with the untreated control (p < 0.05; Table 1). Plant height increased from 14.80 cm in the control to 27.10 cm under the sole application of 1% L. platensis (83% increase). Combined application of 1.25% L. platensis and 2% C. glomerata increased root length by 51% compared with the control. Similarly, the combined application of 1.25% L. platensis and 1% C. glomerata increased root fresh weight from 0.05 to 0.61 g plant⁻¹ and whole-plant fresh weight from 0.62 to 1.75 g plant⁻¹. Overall, these findings indicate that the combined application of both algae enhanced shoot and root development in safflower under fertilizer-free greenhouse conditions.

The improvement in vegetative growth can be partially attributed to the nutritional and bioactive composition of the applied algae. L. platensis is rich in nitrogen, magnesium, iron, and growth-regulating compounds, which may support chlorophyll formation, photosynthetic activity, and biomass accumulation. In contrast, C. glomerata contains polysaccharides, amino acids, and trace elements that may contribute to root development, nutrient acquisition, and overall plant vigor [15, 17]. The positive responses observed in several combined applications suggest complementary effects between the nutrient- and phytohormone-rich cyanobacterium and the polysaccharide-rich freshwater macroalga. Increases in root length and fresh root weight are particularly important because a more developed root system can improve water and nutrient uptake under the high-temperature greenhouse conditions used in the experiment. Similar growth-promoting effects of algal applications have been associated with improved antioxidant protection, including higher activity of enzymes such as superoxide dismutase, catalase, and ascorbate peroxidase, which help protect plant tissues against oxidative stress [14, 30].

Reproductive traits

Algal applications significantly affected (p < 0.05) reproductive and yield-related traits (Table 2). Capitulum diameter increased from 0.89 cm in the control to 1.63 cm under the sole application of 1% L. platensis (83% increase). Similarly, the number of capitula plant− 1 increased from 1.10 to 2.45 capitula plant⁻¹ with the sole application of 1.25% L. platensis. The most significant yield improvements were recorded under the combined application of 1.25% L. platensis and 1% C. glomerata. The combined application increased total seed weight per plant from 0.19 to 0.59 g and 1000-seed weight from 45.56 to 98.34 g (210% and 116% increase, respectively). Capitulum fresh weight also increased by 294%. These results indicate that algal-based treatments enhanced reproductive development and improved seed formation in safflower under fertilizer-free greenhouse conditions [15, 31].

The improvements in reproductive traits were likely associated with enhanced photosynthetic capacity and more effective allocation of assimilates to developing capitula and seeds. This interpretation is supported by the observed increases in photosynthetic pigments, which may have strengthened source activity and improved carbon supply during reproductive growth [15, 31]. The results suggest that algal applications supported not only vegetative development but also seed formation, probably through a combination of nutrient supply, growth-regulating compounds, and improved physiological activity [16, 31].

Chlorophyll and carotenoid contents

Photosynthetic pigment contents exhibited the strongest response to algal application (p < 0.05; Table 3). The combined application of 1% L. platensis and 2% C. glomerata increased chlorophyll a from 10.1 to 36.6 mg g⁻¹ FW, chlorophyll b from 7.5 to 25.5 mg g⁻¹ FW, and total carotenoids from 6.9 to 22.5 mg g⁻¹ FW compared to the control (262%, 240%, and 226% increase, respectively). The chlorophyll a/b ratio increased from 1.35 in the control to 1.44 under this treatment, and reached its highest value of 1.81 with 1.25% L. platensis + 2% C. glomerata. The parallel increase in chlorophylls and carotenoids suggests that algal treatments enhanced pigment accumulation and may have improved photosynthetic capacity and photoprotective potential under the high-temperature greenhouse conditions [14, 32, 33].

The increase in pigment contents may be linked to the nutritional and bioactive composition of the applied algae. Nitrogen and magnesium are structural components of chlorophyll, while iron is involved in chlorophyll biosynthesis and photosynthetic electron transport. In addition, growth-regulating compounds such as auxins and cytokinins may contribute to delayed pigment degradation and improved leaf physiological activity [14, 15]. The highest pigment values were generally observed in combined treatments, suggesting that L. platensis and C. glomerata may have acted in a complementary manner to enhance pigment accumulation and support photosynthetic performance under high-temperature greenhouse conditions. Similar responses have been associated with improved antioxidant protection in plants treated with algal biostimulants, although antioxidant enzyme activity was not directly measured in the present study [29, 34]. Overall, the results indicate a clear positive association between algal application and photosynthetic pigment accumulation in safflower.

This study was conducted under greenhouse conditions over a single growing season, with three replicates per treatment and a single safflower cultivar. These factors limit the direct extrapolation of the findings to open-field conditions, where soil heterogeneity, seasonal variation, and biotic interactions may influence crop responses [13, 14]. Therefore, multi-year, multi-location field trials, preferably with additional safflower cultivars and greater replication, are needed to confirm the stability and agronomic relevance of the observed responses under practical farming conditions.

Discussion

The results of the current study indicated that introducing Cladophora glomerata and Limnospira platensis into the soil (either alone or in combination) significantly improved vegetative growth, reproductive traits, and pigment content for photosynthesis in safflower under greenhouse conditions [14, 15]. No mineral fertilizer was applied to any treatment, including the untreated control. This fertilizer-free design allowed the observed plant responses to be attributed primarily to the algal applications rather than to external inputs of synthetic macro- or micronutrients.

The most significant increases in photosynthetic pigments were recorded with the combination of 1% L. platensis and 2% C. glomerata. These improvements are thought to be closely linked to the nutrient composition and bioactive compounds provided by the algae. L. platensis supplies substantial amounts of nitrogen, magnesium, and iron along with phytohormones such as auxins and cytokinins that may promote pigment biosynthesis and stability under abiotic stress. The polysaccharide- and amino acid-rich C. glomerata likely complemented these effects by improving root development and nutrient uptake efficiency. Consistent with previous reports, algal biostimulant-induced increases in photosynthetic pigments may be associated with improved antioxidant protection, including higher activities of superoxide dismutase, catalase, and ascorbate peroxidase [14, 15, 29, 34]. Comparable improvements in photosynthetic performance have been reported in other plant species treated with Limnospira/Spirulina and freshwater macroalgae, where the responses were attributed to combined nutrient supply, growth-regulating compounds, and enhanced antioxidant protection [12].

High-temperature stress severely impacts safflower productivity. Rahnama et al. [32] demonstrated that elevated temperatures can reduce safflower yield by 53–57%, through inhibition of chlorophyll biosynthesis, accelerated chlorophyll degradation, reduced chlorophyll a and total chlorophyll contents, and a shortened grain-filling period [35–39]. Algal treatments appeared to mitigate these adverse effects in the present study by maintaining higher pigment levels, which may have supported sustained photosynthetic activity during reproductive growth. This physiological improvement was reflected in the significant gains observed under combined algal application, particularly with 1.25% L. platensis + 1% C. glomerata. These responses suggest improved source–sink coordination, with enhanced photoassimilate production and more effective allocation of assimilates to developing seeds [31].

The increased root fresh weight and whole-plant fresh weight may be explained by the complementary composition of the two algal materials. C. glomerata is rich in polysaccharides and amino acids, whereas L. platensis provides a nitrogen-rich biomass with additional bioactive compounds [16, 17]. Previous studies on Cladophora species have shown that their polysaccharide fractions can promote root development and improve nutrient acquisition, which is consistent with the stronger root and biomass responses observed in the present study [9].

The magnitude of the response observed in safflower was comparable to the improvmeents reported in other crops. Shedeed et al. [15] reported 35–45% increases in plant height and 50–60% increases in chlorophyll content in lupin (Lupinus luteus) following the application of L. platensis. Similarly, Rady et al. [30] observed 40–65% improvements in biomass and grain yield in common bean under stress conditions after treatment with Spirulina extracts. For C. glomerata, earlier work on soybean showed 25–50% increases in root length and chlorophyll content [16, 40]. The responses recorded in the present study, therefore, indicate that safflower is highly responsive to algal biostimulant application, particularly under fertilizer-free greenhouse conditions.

The combined algal treatments produced distinct advantages for several growth and physiological traits. The application of 1.25% L. platensis with 1% C. glomerata resulted in the greatest increases in plant and root biomass, whereas 1% L. platensis combined with 2% C. glomerata produced the highest photosynthetic pigment contents. These treatment-specific responses suggest that the two algal materials may contribute through partly different but complementary mechanisms. The nitrogen-rich and bioactive compound profile of L. platensis may support chlorophyll formation and biomass accumulation, while the polysaccharides, amino acids, and trace elements supplied by C. glomerata may enhance root development, nutrient acquisition, and stress tolerance. This interpretation is consistent with earlier reports showing that combined microalgal and macroalgal applications can improve plant performance more effectively than single-species treatments [10, 12].

The present findings are consistent with the growing evidence supporting the role of algal biostimulants in improving crop performance. Previous studies have shown that algal-based inputs may enhance plant growth through multiple, partly overlapping mechanisms, including mineral nutrient supply, modulation of endogenous phytohormones, activation of antioxidant defense systems, and improvement of photosynthetic efficiency [7, 9, 10].

Although the experimental design was appropriate for controlled greenhouse screening, the study was conducted during a single growing season, with one safflower cultivar and three replicates per treatment. These factors should be considered when interpreting the broader agronomic relevance of the findings. Field-based trials across contrasting soils, seasons, and environments are therefore required to confirm the stability, reproducibility, and practical value of the observed responses under real farming conditions [13, 14].

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that soil-applied Limnospira platensis and Cladophora glomerata (particularly in combination) can significantly improve safflower growth, photosynthetic pigment accumulation, and yield-related traits under fertilizer-free and high-temperature conditions. The combined application of 1.25% L. platensis and 1% C. glomerata was the most effective treatment for biomass and yield formation, increasing seed yield per plant, 1000-seed weight, whole-plant fresh weight, and root fresh weight compared with the untreated control. These responses suggest that the two algal materials may act through complementary mechanisms involving nutrient supply, bioactive compounds, improved root development, and enhanced photosynthetic performance.

The results highlight the potential of locally sourced algal biomass as a low-input, environmentally sound biostimulant for safflower production. However, the study was limited to greenhouse conditions, a single growing season, and a single cultivar. Multi-year and multi-location field trials, together with assessments of biomass production, processing logistics, application efficiency, and economic feasibility, are needed before practical recommendations can be developed for low-input, organic, and semi-arid farming systems.
