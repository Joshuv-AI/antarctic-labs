Livestock guardian dogs create safer landscapes for sheep by reducing fox predation risk attributes
Authors: Ana Paola Yusti-Muñoz, Darío Moreira-Arce, Diego A Peñaranda, Pablo M Vergara, Javier A Simonetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLivestock predation by carnivores drives global human-carnivore conflict. Non-lethal strategies like Livestock Guardian Dogs (LGDs) prove effective, yet their interactions with biophysical factors (e.g., habitats) and ecological variables (e.g., carnivore/prey abundance) remain poorly understood. This study evaluated whether LGD presence reduces sheep predation by Lycalopex foxes and how LGDs interact with biophysical/ecological variables shaping predation risk patterns, using Chilean Patagonia ranches as a model system. Using 2017–2018 predation data, we identified key landscape and species variables influencing risk (vegetation composition, nearest distance to road/farmstead, fox/hare abundance, LGD presence) via logistic regression on kill and random points from five ranches (two with LGDs, three without). We analyzed 94 fresh sheep carcasses killed by foxes: 26 in LGD ranches (0.77 sheep/ha) vs. 68 without LGDs (1.34 sheep/ha). Fox predation was lower with LGDs (χ2 = 4.90, p = 0.026). Models showed negative shrubland cover association and LGD × bareland × hare abundance interactions. With LGDs, risk declined with bareland and hare abundance; without LGDs, risk was unrelated to bareland but increased sharply with hare abundance. Mean predation probability was 0.26 (± 0.001), peaking at 0.39 (± 0.07) without LGDs. Introduction

Globally, livestock predation by carnivores is a major cause of human-wildlife conflict1. Livestock predation may be explained by modifications to carnivore habitats2, as well as by changes in wild prey abundance and the introduction of domestic animals that exhibit less anti-predatory behavior, and occur in larger densities compared to wild prey, thereby increasing the likelihood of predation1. Economic losses from livestock predation often lead to the persecution and killing of carnivores, with retaliatory hunting currently causing population declines and local extinctions of at least 32% of carnivore species worldwide3.

The first step to mitigate conflict with carnivores is to reduce livestock predation. Direct and non-lethal modifications to rangelands, such as the use of barns, fences, shepherds, and guardian animals (e.g., dogs and donkeys), have proven to be more effective than lethal control methods in reducing livestock predation worldwide4, 5. Additionally, spatially explicit predation risk models that provide quantitative and visual guidance on locations where carnivores are more or less likely to prey on livestock, have emerged as a useful tool. When applied at large spatial scales, these models can detect and anticipate predation problems, thereby enhancing risk management planning in livestock-raising areas, including the implementation and allocation of predation control strategies6. Predation risk assessments, based on optimal foraging theory7 and spatial ecology approaches such as the landscape of fear model8, elucidate the relationship between sites where livestock are preyed upon and the environmental and ecological attributes associated with these sites9. These ecological attributes may include habitat characteristics (e.g., vegetation cover and structure), proximity to human settlements, livestock type and abundance, and wild prey availability10.

The use of livestock guardian dogs (hereafter LGDs) is one of the most effective non-lethal strategies to protect different livestock species from a suite of wild carnivores, particularly mesocarnivores4. The ability of guardian dogs to act as top predators and intimidate carnivore species can increase carnivores’ perceived or actual predation risk and/or risk of injury, thereby reducing predation losses11, 12. By safeguarding livestock without entirely excluding carnivores, LGDs represent a “green” predation control strategy, that would aid the conservation of native carnivores in livestock-oriented landscapes13. However, LGD effectiveness varies widely (11–100%)5, highlighting the need to evaluate how different attributes of livestock systems influence their capacity to protect livestock. The success of guardian dogs in reducing livestock predation may depend on habitat, management, and predator-related factors that affect predation risk. For instance, high densities of carnivores and livestock increase the frequency of encounters between these species, and heighten predation probabilities14, 15. Furthermore, livestock with access to forest stands or shrublands are more vulnerable, as these areas provide refuge for carnivores and hinder livestock escape16, 17. Livestock rearing in remote areas, distant from human settlements and low-use roads, also reduces protection against carnivores15. Finally, wild prey abundance could also affect predation risk10, but its effect is debated. Several studies show reductions in wild prey abundance increase domestic predation by large and medium-sized carnivores18, 19, while others found that high abundances of wild prey, such as ungulates, result in increased cattle predation by wolves (Canis lupus) and snow leopards (Panthera uncia)20, 21. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of LGD effectiveness requires examining how the risk of livestock predation in the presence of LGDs is influenced by habitat conditions, the availability of alternative prey, and management prescriptions22.

Global demand for meat and dairy products is projected to increase by at least 15% over the next decade, driving growth in livestock production, including sheep23. This expansion might intensify human–carnivore conflicts worldwide24. Therefore, to foster the use of LGDs as a non-lethal tool for controlling livestock predation by carnivores, this research aims to assess whether the presence of LGDs reduces sheep predation by native mesocarnivores, and how their presence interacts with different attributes of grazing systems (e.g., vegetation composition, distance to human infrastructure, wild species abundance) and predation risk. We also seek to enhance understanding of the variables that increase livestock predation risk by mesocarnivore species, factors that are largely ignored in global livestock predation risk evaluations despite the richness and ecological diversity of mesocarnivores and their increasing involvement in human–carnivore conflicts3, 25, 26. We rely on sheep ranches in Chilean Patagonia as a model system, where sheep losses are largely attributed to predation by culpeo (Lycalopex culpaeus) and chilla (L. griseus) foxes.

Chilean Patagonia accounts for 92% of the nation’s sheep production27, with perceived annual losses attributed to both fox species ranging from 3% to 29% of total holdings28, 29. Although both fox species are protected under Chilean Hunting Law (Law 19.473, Ministry of Agriculture), lethal control remains common, particularly of culpeo foxes29. A previous study indicates that 91% of ranchers believe LGDs reduce sheep predation29. However, ecological and anthropogenic factors influencing LGDs effectiveness remain poorly understood, hindering their wider adoption. To address these gaps, we predict that LGDs reduce the probability of sheep predation by foxes, and mediate the number and influence of multiple habitat features, management practices, and alternative prey availability on predation risk. To evaluate these predictions, a natural experiment was conducted on sheep ranches differing in their use of LGDs to prevent fox predation. Ultimately, this research aims to inform predation control strategies and promote coexistence between carnivores and sheep ranchers through LGD use.

Methods

Study landscapes

Research was conducted across five sheep ranches from Isla Riesco, Magallanes Region, Chile. Prior permission was obtained from the landowners before entering the ranches to conduct carcass searches and camera-trap installation. In Isla Riesco, as well as throughout the Magallanes region, sheep farming is extensive30. Ranches tended to hold similar sheep densities (ca. 1 sheep/ha) but differed in the presence of LGDs. Estancia Anita Beatriz (52° 51′ 59″ S, 71° 33′ 16″ W, 730 ha) and Estancia Adela (52° 51′ 00″ S, 71° 30′ 38″ W, 1562 ha), kept approximately 1500 and 1800 sheep, respectively, and used Pyrenean Mountain Dogs and Pyrenees Mastiffs, with two and three dogs, respectively. At the time of this study, LGDs had been used for about 10 years. In contrast, Fundo Ankel (52° 49′ 57″ S, 71° 25′ 30″ W, 1673 ha) with approximately 2100 sheep, Caledonia (52° 50′ 21″ S, 71° 29′ 54″ W, 1481 ha) with about 1800 sheep, and Emiliana (52° 49′ 55″ S, 71° 28′ 25″ W, 1781 ha) with approximately 1200 sheep did not use LGDs. All ranches shared a vegetation composed of Magellanic subpolar forest, mainly with Nothofagus species, shrublands dominated by calafate (Berberis microphylla) and romerillo (Baccharis magellanica), peatlands, and grasslands for livestock use. Ranches differed in the proportion of area covered by forest (χ2 = 256.98, df = 4, p < 0.001), grasslands (χ2 = 469.91, df = 4, p < 0.001) and shrublands (χ2 = 182.62, df = 4, p < 0.001).

Sheep predation data

Sheep predation assessments were conducted between mid-September 2017 and mid-May 2018, spanning from early austral spring to mid-autumn. According to local sheep ranchers, most predation events take place from the lambing season (approximately October-November) to the postpartum period (approximately November to March). Therefore, the sampling period coincided with the regional sheep predation peak. Sheep and lamb carcasses were systematically searched for along transects, each visited once, with transects spaced every 500 m to cover the full width of each ranch. Kill sites were established only from fresh carcasses (1–2 days old) to accurately determine fox predation according to Guarda et al.31. As it was not possible to differentiate between chilla and culpeo fox attacks, the kills were treated as Lycalopex fox attacks in general. A case-control approach was used to compare habitat conditions around the detected carcasses (e.g., kill sites) with those around randomly selected points (see below)6. This sampling design implied that for each kill site, one random control point was located 200 m away from a fresh carcass, in a randomly chosen direction, to avoid possible points and buffer overlap. Each selected control point was visited and non-predation was confirmed. All kill and random sites were georeferenced using a Garmin GPSMAP 64s.

Habitat, management and predator data

At each kill and random site, a set of biophysical and ecological variables potentially affecting the probability of sheep predation by foxes was quantified. These included biophysical landscape variables such as vegetation composition and structure, and distance to nearest water sources, as well as variables related to human presence, including distance to nearest human infrastructure (roads and farmsteads). Regarding carnivore and livestock ecology, the abundance of foxes, their wild prey, and sheep were assessed. Finally, in terms of predation control management, the presence or absence of livestock guardian dogs (LGDs) within the ranch was considered (Table 1). Variables related to habitat composition and species abundance were measured within a 100-m radius buffer as empirical data indicate that guarded sheep are more vigilant when LGDs stay within this range32, and that most LGD-carnivore agonistic interactions occur ca. 100 m from livestock33.

Area covered by each vegetation type was obtained from the 2017 land cover map of Isla Riesco (30-m resolution). Additionally, a structural vegetation complexity index (VCI) was calculated as , where horizontal vegetation cover (Hcv) is the length (m) of shrubs/tree trunks intercepted along 5-m transects on each side of camera locations (10 m total), and vertical vegetation cover (Vcv) is the vertical projection height (m) of trees/shrubs at 1-m intervals along each transect32. VCI (m) is a geometric index reflecting structural complexity/rugosity at kill and random points. Values near 0 indicate scarce/no tall vegetation (grasslands/barelands); values near 7 reflect maximum complexity (transects fully covered by tall shrubs/trees). Values of 3–5 suggest moderate coverage, such as shrublands.

The abundance of sheep, foxes, and wild prey (represented by the European hare Lepus europaeus), was quantified using RAI (Relative Abundance Index; RAI) which represents the detections rate of each species in camera traps34. This index was used as a surrogate of abundance and activity as species with higher densities tend to be more often detected and recorded than less abundant ones34, 35, 36. Although gregariousness can bias RAI37, our study species (foxes, hares) are predominantly solitary outside the breeding season, minimizing this problem. The European hare was used as a surrogate for wild prey, as it could be a major dietary item for foxes inhabiting areas at high latitudes, low altitudes, and anthropogenic landscapes, such as those from Patagonia sheep ranches38. In fact, European hares contribute up to 30% of the biomass consumed by foxes in Patagonia28.

To sample foxes, hares and sheep, fifty-five camera traps were installed for species photo capture (Bushnell Trophy Cam 8MP, Model 119436), from August 2016 to February 2017 (winter to austral summer) across five ranches: 18 cameras in ranches with LGDs (Anita Beatriz = 7, Adela = 11) and 37 in ranches without LGDs (Ankel = 11, Caledonia = 9, Emiliana = 17). An additional set of 58 cameras were installed from December 2017 to June 2018 (austral summer and autumn): 29 in Anita Beatriz (with LGDs) and 29 in Ankel (without LGDs). Camera stations were spaced 750 m apart to minimize overlapping detections and maximize sample coverage, increasing the likelihood of detecting foxes. Cameras were set to capture three photos per trigger, with a 5-minute delay. To avoid overestimations of species frequency, only detections of a species on a camera separated by an interval of 60 min were considered for constructing a frequency matrix. Based on this matrix, a kernel density map was generated. The kernel map was intersected with a 100-m radius buffer around each kill and random site to assign mean values of fox and prey frequency to each buffer. The kernel was constructed using the “Heatmap” tool in QGIS, while mean values were assessed using the “Zonal Statistics” plugin in QGIS.

Statistical analysis

A test of proportions was firstly used to compare the number of sheep killed by foxes on ranches with and without LGDs, relative to the total number of animals and the size of each ranch. In other preliminary analyses, we assessed differences in vegetation structure between kill and random points using a Mann–Whitney U test. Then, we used mixed effects logistic regression from the lm4 R package to evaluate the effects of biophysical landscape variables (area covered by forest, shrublands, grasslands, and barelands, nearest distance to water sources), biophysical landscape/human presence variables (nearest distance to road and farmstead), management variables (presence of LGDs), livestock variables (sheep abundance), and carnivore variables (hare and fox abundance) on sheep predation probabilities by foxes (Table 1). The dummy response variable took the value of 1 at kill sites and 0 at random points. The presence or absence of LGDs on each ranch was included as a covariate. To determine how the effects of the variables changed in the presence of LGDs, we incorporated interaction terms between the LGDs and these variables into the candidate models. Ranches were included as a random factor to control for variability related to ranch-specific attributes and management practices other than LGD use. All continuous covariates were standardized to z-scores to improve interpretation and model convergence. To avoid collinearity and model overfitting, only non-correlated variables (− 0.70 < rs > 0.70, p < 0.05) were included in the same models. Thus, the area of grassland, which was positively correlated with the area of bareland (rs = 0.70, p < 0.001), and negatively correlated with area covered by forest (rs = − 0.75, p < 0.001), was not included in the same models as these latter variables. Additionally, we did not incorporate variables significantly associated with the presence or absence of LGDs into the same model.

We tested for spatial autocorrelation among kill sites using Moran’s I index, which was calculated using the ‘HotSpot’ plugin in QGIS. This analysis revealed no spatial correlation among kill sites in the study area (Moran’s I = 0.112, Z = 0.474, p = 0.635). The dredge function from the MuMin package was used to rank the candidate models that were built using all possible combinations of variables. Models were ranked based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Models with an AIC difference (ΔAIC) of < 2 were considered to be the best models, and model averaging was conducted on variables present in these models. To assess the model’s goodness of fit, the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and the Area Under the Curve (AUC) were calculated. AUC values 0.70–0.80 indicate acceptable model performance; values from 0.80 to 0.90—good performance, and from 0.90 to 1—excellent performance39. The effect size of predictor variables was evaluated using model-averaged parameters, odds-ratio and error estimates, which derived from weighted averages of these values across the set of candidate models (ΔAIC < 2)40, 41. We interpreted each variable having a strong effect on sheep predation if its confidence interval did not overlap zero. Statistical analysis was performed with packages “nlme”, “MASS”, “mgcv”, “car” and “glmmTMB”, using R version 4.4.2 (R Core Team, 2024).

To visualize predation risk patterns, we generated a “risk map” from the best-supported model (lowest AIC). The study area was divided into 100 × 100 m pixels, and for each pixel we calculated the linear predictor using measured covariate values (e.g., shrub cover, bareland, hare abundance) and their model coefficients. Pixels were classified by risk score and mapped using a continuous color gradient (higher risk = warmer tones), identifying predation hotspots across ranch landscapes. All spatial analysis were assessed using QGIS 3.16 Hannover.

Results

Kill and random sites

A total of 94 fresh sheep carcasses resulting from fox attacks were detected, alongside 94 random points sampled over seven months, with one week of sampling per month. Twenty-six carcasses were found in ranches with LGDs (Anita Beatriz = 10; Adela = 16), yielding a mean proportion of predation of 0.77 sheep/ha (SE = 0.11) over the 7-month sampling period. In ranches without LGDs, 68 carcasses were found (Caledonia = 5, Emiliana = 21, Ankel = 42), with a mean proportion of predation of 1.34 sheep/ha (SE = 0.54) during sampling (Fig. 1). A two-sample test of proportions indicated that the proportion of sheep killed by foxes was lower in ranches with the presence of LGDs than in those where LGDs were not used (χ2 = 4.90, df = 1, p = 0.026). Most kills occurred during summer (57.1%), followed by late winter (25.3%), spring (13.1%), and autumn (4.3%).

Drivers of sheep predation

The probability of sheep predation was explained by the effects of the area covered by shrublands and the interactions between LGDs and bareland and between LGDs and hares, as revealed by the best-supported logistic models (Table 2; full model selection table is provided in Supplementary Table S3). The effect of shrubland cover on the probability of sheep being predated was positive (β = 0.52, SE = 0.16, p = 0.001), indicating that sheep are at a higher risk in areas that are extensively covered by shrubs (Fig. 4A). In ranches with LGDs, the risk of sheep predation by foxes decreases as the area of bareland increases (β = − 1.12, SE = 0.46, p = 0.015; Fig. 4B). In contrast, the risk of sheep predation did not vary with the amount of bareland in ranches without LGDs (Fig. 4B). The odds ratio indicated that sheep in bareland habitats had 79% less chance to be predated in presence of LGD than in ranches without LGD (Table 3; Fig. 5).

Discussion

Livestock guardian dogs are among the most effective tools to prevent carnivore attacks on livestock4, 5. However, no studies to date have assessed how the presence of guardian dogs influences predation risk beyond the number of animals killed. Nor is it known how their use might mediate the importance of various habitat and ecological attributes that shape livestock predation by carnivores. The initial prediction was partially supported, as guardian dogs did not reduce the number of attributes associated with sheep predation by foxes, but they effectively and significantly decreased the influence of some of them on predation risk (bareland cover and hare abundance). Hence, despite our study included few sheep ranches, it provides empirical support for the effectiveness of guardian dogs in reducing livestock predation by mesocarnivores over a large spatial extent4, and for their ability to create safer landscapes for sheep by reducing predation risk in areas that, without them, might be more vulnerable to predation by carnivores such as chilla and culpeo foxes.

Sheep vulnerability to fox attacks increases with the extent of shrub cover. This pattern is consistent with global trends regarding livestock predation risk15. Structurally complex habitats such as shrublands and forests increase livestock predation risk, including sheep, by limiting visibility and escape routes42, 43. Additionally, for opportunistic predators like Lycalopex foxes, which prefer nocturnal attacks44, thick cover facilitates inconspicuous attacks on prey45. Among Patagonian ranchers, this pattern of increased predation in shrublands is also widely recognized. To reduce sheep predation, ranchers often prune shrubs. However, shrublands are key ecosystems in Patagonian, supporting high diversity of plants, vertebrates and invertebrates46. Moreover, due to their deeper root systems and lower growth rates compared to grasses, shrubs are more resistant to current droughts, helping buffer the negative effects of drought on both ecosystem functioning and livestock husbandry47. They also provide shelter for livestock during strong winds and snowfall. Therefore, to prevent predation in shrubland areas without removing it, ranchers could avoid using paddocks with high shrub coverage in lambing season, when more vulnerable individuals like lambs are present48.

Bareland cover significantly influenced sheep predation by foxes. In the absence of livestock guardian dogs, predation risk remained relatively constant across the bareland gradient. Sheep naturally concentrate on open habitats such as grasslands49; however, the quality of grasslands can affect herd size and proximity among individuals50, potentially influencing individual mortality risk. In areas with low nutritional quality, like the degraded grasslands that compose much of the bareland in the study area, sheep often form large but dispersed groups50. This social structure may increase predation risk, as seen in ranches without dog protection, since in gregarious species like sheep, individuals isolated in open areas are particularly vulnerable to predation48. In contrast, sheep accompanied by guardian dogs experience a considerable (79%) lower predation risk in open areas with extensive bareland. This pattern likely results from several factors. Livestock guardian dogs can promote larger, more cohesive sheep groups that stay closer together and are more vigilant32, which is especially effective in open areas where grouping enhances predator detection and response. Furthermore, the presence of guardian dogs’ whole-body odor, a complex olfactory signal, reduces fox detection on camera traps, increases fox fear behavior, and decreases their food intake51. Thus, the reduction in sheep predation on ranches with guardian dogs may be explained by behavioral changes in both sheep and foxes, mediated by dogs. These changes may cause spatial segregation between species and promote antipredator behaviors in sheep, ultimately reducing the likelihood of fox attacks.

Wild prey abundance is key for carnivore populations52. High wild prey abundance, such as ungulates, has been associated with increased livestock predation by large carnivores in subsistence and extensive livestock systems20, 21, a pattern explained by apparent competition, where one prey species negatively affects the other due to carnivore influence53. In Patagonian sheep ranches, hares might drive this indirect interaction, as sheep predation by foxes increased with higher frequencies of the exotic European hare in ranches without livestock guardian dogs. Lagomorphs arriving in a new habitat rapidly establish strong interactions with extant predators54. In the study region, hares, consumed by at least eight native carnivores, including Lycalopex foxes, caused significant apparent competition with native herbivores, mainly small mammals55. This finding could have implications for livestock rearing as abundant hares may increase fox populations and aggregations, heightening encounters with sheep and subsequent predation risks15, 55. Furthermore, while lagomorph populations fluctuate55, 56, sheep populations remain relatively stable due to management practices, allowing predators to get all-year round access to livestock increasing spatial predation risk.

In the presence of livestock guardian dogs, predation probabilities remain low even at high frequency of hares. This negative interaction could be explained by hare avoidance of areas patrolled by dogs. As is the case with foxes, hare visitation rates to experimental stations with presence of guardian dog’s whole-body odor decrease compared to unscented sites57. Additionally, hares at scented stations displayed increased vigilance, a behavior linked to fear. Therefore, fear and avoidance of dogs may lead to lower hare detection around the guarded sheep, reducing their attractiveness to foxes and likely decreasing predation risk on sheep. Nevertheless, the relationship between hare and fox abundance requires further investigation in Patagonia, especially in sheep ranches, due to its possible implications in conflict dynamics. Based on this research and on Ugarte et al.57, livestock guardian dogs could be used not only as deterrents against carnivores but also to limit the interaction between livestock and wild herbivores that can serve as disease vectors13, and compete with livestock both directly and indirectly58, as found here with the exotic European hare.

Livestock guardian dogs reduce sheep predation by foxes in otherwise riskier areas, creating safer landscapes for sheep and ultimately reducing economic losses. The behavioral changes guardian dogs induce in native carnivores, such as foxes, as well as in their wild and domestic prey32, 51, 57, partly explain their effectiveness as a predation control method. Moreover, as attributes related to predation risk decrease in importance in the presence of guardian dogs, predation management for ranchers dealing with medium-sized predators may become simpler. The deterrent effect of livestock guardian dogs could reduce producers’ willingness to kill carnivores in retaliation, supporting biodiversity conservation in livestock landscapes. However, it is crucial to recognize that implementing any predation control strategy, lethal or non-lethal, within a single ranch can alter local carnivore populations and predation patterns. Such changes may cause predators to shift their activity to neighboring properties, potentially intensifying conflicts in adjacent areas59, 60. Consequently, while guardian dogs and similar methods can mitigate predation locally, they might inadvertently displace the problem rather than eliminate it entirely. This possibility highlights the need for further research and suggests that predation control measures should be coordinated at a larger, landscape scale to ensure more uniform risk reduction across neighboring ranches.

Although livestock guardian dogs are not entirely harmless to wildlife, lethal encounters remain rare33, 61; in this context, their role as a ‘wildlife-friendly’ strategy is well established. Therefore, its success regarding wild species conservation depends on rigorous training, supervision, proper feeding, and consistent correction of undesirable hunting behaviors62. Given the current need for a paradigm shift from carnivore control to coexistence to minimize human impacts on native species, guardian dogs emerge as a promising tool for transforming livestock landscapes from areas of co-occurrence to landscapes of coexistence with wild carnivores.
