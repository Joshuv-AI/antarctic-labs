A Manually Annotated and Curated Mediterranean Plant Image Dataset of Native Species from Lebanon
Authors: Yasmine Taki, Dany Abou Jaoude, Ibrahim Issa, Salma N Talhouk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe FloraLebanon (FL) is a curated field dataset of 24,944 plant images from 102 Mediterranean species native to Lebanon. University student volunteers took images during field expeditions to the Al Shouf Biosphere Reserve in 2024. The volunteers followed a structured image-capture protocol developed for the project to document whole plants, leaves, stems, flowers, fruits, and habitats. Image pre-processing involved the manual removal of redundant and poor-quality images, followed by a second cleaning technical validation. Species identification was validated by examining voucher specimens taken from the field and confirmed by two independent taxonomic experts. The FloraLebanon (FL) dataset includes plant images, a metadata sheet of plant nomenclature, and scans of voucher specimens. The FloraLebanon (FL) dataset is open access, collected and curated to support machine learning and biodiversity monitoring. Our goal is to expand the dataset to cover Lebanon’s flora, estimated at 2,600 species.

Background & Summary

Collecting field data on plant species presence and distribution is essential for monitoring the impacts of climate change and habitat loss, and for taking informed conservation measures1–3. This process, however, is constrained by time, resources, and by the “taxonomic impediment” which is a global decline in the number of taxonomists4,5. As such, researchers have designed projects that engage non-expert citizen scientists in gathering sufficient quantities of data from the field6. Machine learning (ML) supports such projects by helping overcome the “taxonomic impediment”, enabling the identification of plant images collected in the field by non-experts2,3,5.

Many training datasets for ML consist of images captured in laboratory settings and are used for algorithm development or for highlighting key plant morphological traits. Such datasets consist of idealized plant images that do not represent complex, variable, and cluttered settings in which plants naturally occur7–9. Model robustness is thus reduced when applied to large image data collected by non-experts in the field3,9,10.

The images of plants taken in their natural habitat differ from those taken in controlled environments, in that they occur in a complex setting with variations in lighting, the presence of other plant species, noise in backgrounds, and other factors. While more challenging to produce and work with, such plant datasets are a robust resource for training and validating algorithms that can be reliably used for species identification of images collected by non-experts8,11,12.

Our goal is to promote the use of AI to enable citizen science projects for biodiversity conservation in Lebanon. This paper introduces FloraLebanon (FL) and explains how the data was collected and organized to support the training of machine learning models for accurate identification of native plant species in their natural habitats in Lebanon. The paper presents the first batch of manually curated and annotated field image dataset of wild species collected during 2024 following 27 field visits to different sites in the Al Shouf Biosphere Reserve. To date, the dataset consists of over 24,944 plant images representing 102 plant species.

Methods

Site description

Lebanon lies within the Mediterranean Basin, a global biodiversity hotspot characterized by high endemism and extensive habitat loss13,14. The country hosts an estimated 2,600 plant species across an area of 10,452 km² 15–17.

Mediterranean plant species images were taken in the Al Shouf Biosphere Reserve, Lebanon’s largest protected area, which plays a vital role in conserving the country’s natural heritage, particularly its iconic cedar forests (Cedrus libani). Covering about 500 km², roughly 5% of the area of the of Lebanon’s total land area, it spans longitudes 35°28′–35°47′ E and latitudes 33°32′–35°48′ N, with elevations ranging from 1,200 to 1,980 m above sea level18–22.

Field expeditions

Twenty-seven field visits were conducted in the Al Shouf Biosphere Reserve between April and December 2024 to collect images of species across different seasons. Data collection sites were selected in consultation with the reserve managers who pointed out biodiversity-rich areas as well as suitable trails and paths in terms of accessibility and habitat diversity. Common species were typically accessible along short and easy hiking trails less than 3 km, while moderate to difficult trails longer than 7 km were conducted to capture species that are less common. Permissions for sample and image collection in the natural reserve were secured from the Al Shouf Biosphere Reserve management team.

Plant image capture methodology

A plant image capture methodology was developed to guide non-expert volunteers in systematically and comprehensively documenting each species. The protocol focus was on key morphological traits crucial for identification, and details were captured from multiple angles and perspectives. Using a variety of devices, including iPhones, Samsung, and Tecno models, allowed volunteers to contribute images with diverse resolutions and qualities. For each plant specimen, at least one photograph was taken of the attributes shown in Fig. 1. Volunteers were asked to photograph the full plant silhouette, document leaf arrangement, show leaf details, capture flower and fruit stages, take photos of plants in groups, and include one image showing the habitat in which they are found.

Data organization: Annotation

Volunteers assigned a species field code to all images collected within a 20 m quadrat and to a corresponding voucher specimen, which was placed in a plant press for later taxonomic validation and herbarium archiving. The field coding system comprised: a species sequence number (e.g., 1, 2, 3…), the date of identification (DDMMYYYY), the collector’s initials (e.g., YT for Yasmina Taki), a letter referring to the plant press (e.g., A, B…), and a site number (a numeral indicating the site location). For example, the code “1-23082024_YTA1” indicates the first species collected on August 23, 2024, by Yasmina Taki, using plant-press A, at site 1. This field code served as an internal organizational system during fieldwork. It does not appear in the final dataset, which instead contains image folders named according to the scientific names of taxonomically identified species.

Data organization: Preprocessing

A manual cleaning process was carried out to remove duplicates and poor-quality pictures, optimizing the dataset for potential machine learning applications. The curated images (details in validation section) and their metadata were organized in an Excel file.

Data Overview

The first batch of the FloraLebanon (FL) manually curated and annotated field image dataset, collected in 2024, includes 24,944 images representing 102 manually identified native plant species from Lebanon, representing around 4% of Lebanon’s flora estimated at 2,600 species and nearly 10% of the 1,054 plant species reported in the Al Shouf Biosphere Reserve. The goal is to expand data to capture all of Lebanon’s flora.

Figure 4 shows that the total number of images per species was highly variable image counts per species ranging from fewer than 50 to more than 750.

Bar graph showing the total number of images per species in the FloraLebanon (FL) dataset.

This variability in image count per species is primarily attributed to species morphology. Species with relatively simple morphology (e.g., Allium rotundum) present fewer features than those with more complex forms (e.g., Valeriana erotica), which require a larger number of images to capture all characteristics. Another factor influencing the number of images per species is population size and distribution. When the population is small and not widespread (e.g., Himantoglossum caprinum and Iris susiana), opportunities for repeated documentation across different quadrats are limited. Seasonal growth dynamics also play a role, as some species undergo rapid developmental transitions (e.g., Anacamptis pyramidalis and Gladiolus italicus) making it difficult to obtain additional images during subsequent site visits. A fourth factor relates to the variation in image quality, as volunteers had differing photography skills and devices. Consequently, some species required extensive image curation, with many low-quality images removed to maintain dataset standards (e.g., Achillea falcata). Despite class imbalance in the dataset, the majority of species (80 out of 102) fall within the 50–300 image-count range (Fig. 5).

Technical Validation

Species identification

Species identification was performed by consulting with images and voucher specimens and using taxonomic keys and field guides (Post herbarium at the American University of Beirut, Tohmé and Tohmé16, and Plants of the World Online24. To ensure the accuracy of these identifications, a cross-validation process was implemented, involving two additional independent taxonomic experts (Moustapha Itani and Mohamad Al Zein), both with specialized expertise in the region’s plant life. These experts reviewed a subset of the images and voucher specimens to verify the initial identifications.

Image curation

Curation of the FloraLebanon (FL) dataset involved a multi-stage process. During the first stage, all images that were blurry, that presented redundant perspectives and angles, or that did not clearly show the target specimen were deleted. This was followed by a second cleaning to ensure the dataset met the quality requirements for machine learning applications.

Usage Notes

The FloraLebanon (FL) is a raw dataset publicly accessible via AUBScholar Works repository. The repository contains raw data image files, the metadata file in excel format, and pdf scan for voucher specimens. While this dataset is not organized into train-test splits, it is primarily intended for training and evaluating artificial intelligence models for automated plant species identification. There is also a plan to develop a user-friendly mobile application that can assist both the public and conservationists in identifying and monitoring plant diversity in Lebanon.

Limitations

The FloraLebanon (FL) dataset in its current version has some limitations including: (1) Class imbalances whereby the number of images per species varies with species morphology, rarity, distribution, and seasonal phenology. (2) Background noise is present in some images where non-plant elements appear in the background. (3) Image quality is variable depending on collector’s photographic skills and mobile devices. (4) The dataset does not provide full phenological coverage due to resource and time constraints.
