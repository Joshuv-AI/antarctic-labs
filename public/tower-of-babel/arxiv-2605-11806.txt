Adaptive Kernel Ridge Regression with Linear Structure: Sharp Oracle Inequalities and Minimax Optimality
Xin Bing Chao Wang

Abstract
Kernel ridge regression (KRR) is a widely used nonparametric method due to its strong theoretical guarantees and computational convenience. However, standard KRR does not distinguish between linear and nonlinear components in the signal, instead applying a single functional regularization to the entire function. This may lead to unnecessary shrinkage of linear structure and consequently suboptimal prediction performance. In this paper, we propose a modified regression procedure that augments KRR with an explicit linear component. The proposed method has the same computational complexity as standard KRR and introduces no additional tuning parameters. Theoretically, we establish a sharp oracle inequality for the proposed estimator and show that it adaptively captures both linear and nonlinear structure, achieving minimax optimal prediction risk under general kernels. Compared with standard KRR, the proposed method improves both the bias and approximation error at the expense of only an additional parametric variance term, which is negligible in low- and moderate-dimensional settings. In high-dimensional regimes, incorporating ridge regularization for the linear component yields a procedure that performs uniformly no worse than KRR. Extensive simulation studies support the theoretical findings.

Introduction

Kernel ridge regression (KRR) is a canonical tool for nonparametric regression, offering a flexible framework for estimating complex functions through regularization in a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS). Due to its strong theoretical guarantees and computational convenience, it has been widely used across statistics and machine learning, including many recent applications such as causal inference
(

)
, image recognition
(

)
, reinforcement learning
(

(

Let

In many applications, however, the regression function comprises both linear and nonlinear components. Standard KRR does not distinguish between these parts, instead penalizing the entire function through the single RKHS norm in (
2

In particular, KRR does not explicitly exploit the parametric structure of the linear component and may therefore estimate it less efficiently than methods tailored to linear models. An exception arises in classical smoothing spline models, where the linear component lies in the null space of the associated spline penalty, such as the cubic smoothing spline in one dimension or thin-plate spline models in higher dimensions (see, e.g.,
33

12

35

Motivated by this observation, we propose a modified kernel ridge regression framework that augments the RKHS component with an explicit linear term of the form

\widehat{f}(x)=x^{\top}\widehat{\alpha}+\widehat{g}(x)\qquad\forall~x\in\mathcal{X},

(3)

where
(\widehat{\alpha},\widehat{g})
are jointly optimized by solving

\displaystyle\min_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{d},g\in\mathcal{H}_{K}}\Big\{~\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(Y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\alpha-g(X_{i})\bigl)^{2}+\lambda\|g\|_{K}^{2}\Big\}.

(4)

This formulation allows the linear signal to be estimated without shrinkage from the RKHS penalty, while retaining the flexibility of kernel methods for modeling the nonlinear component. The joint optimization also distinguishes this procedure from a naive two-step algorithm that first regresses
Y
onto
X
and then applies standard KRR to the residuals. We refer to
Remark

1
of
Section

2
for a detailed discussion of such comparison.
In
Section

2
we further show that the optimization (
4
) admits a closed-form solution and can be solved with the same computational complexity as solving the standard KRR.

Our main theoretical contribution is to analyze the prediction risk of the proposed predictor in
Section

3
and to demonstrate its advantages over standard KRR. For ease of presentation, we focus on the in-sample prediction risk under both fixed and random designs, as it already illustrates the main ideas. Similar results can be extended to out-of-sample prediction risk, albeit with more cumbersome analysis. For any measurable function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
that may depend on the data
\mathcal{D}
, we define its (in-sample) prediction risk as

\mathcal{R}(f-f^{*}):={1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[(f-f^{*})(X_{i})^{2}\right]={1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[(f(X_{i})-f^{*}(X_{i}))^{2}\right].

(5)

To use standard KRR as a benchmark, we first present in
Theorem

1
an oracle inequality for its prediction error, a result that, to the best of our knowledge, is not available in the existing literature.
For a fixed
\mathcal{H}_{K}
and regularization parameter
\lambda>0
,
Theorem

1
shows that the prediction risk of
\widetilde{g}
in (
2
) decomposes into three terms:

\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{g}-f^{*})~\leq~\textsf{Var}_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}(\lambda)+\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{K}}\Bigl\{\lambda\|f\|_{K}^{2}+\mathcal{R}(f-f^{*})\Bigr\}.

(6)

On the right-hand side, the first term represents the variance of
\widetilde{g}
, which depends on the complexity of
\mathcal{H}_{K}
and the regularization parameter
\lambda
. The remaining two terms capture the bias induced by regularization and the approximation error arising from restricting the search to
\mathcal{H}_{K}
. Notably, KRR effectively selects the function
f\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
that balances bias and approximation, which gives rise to a sharp oracle inequality. As the regularization parameter
\lambda
increases, we observe the classical bias–variance tradeoff: the variance decreases while the bias increases.

Compared to the above rate, our theory in
Theorem

2
for fixed design and
Theorem

3
for random design shows that the proposed
\widehat{f}
in (
3
) satisfies

\mathcal{R}(\widehat{f}-f^{*})~\leq~\sigma^{2}{d\over n}+\textsf{Var}_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}(\lambda)+\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{d},f\in\mathcal{H}_{K}}\Bigl\{\lambda\|f\|_{K}^{2}+\mathcal{R}(\langle\alpha,\rangle+f-f^{*})\Bigr\}.

(7)

The benefit of incorporating the linear term
x^{\top}\widehat{\alpha}
in (
3
) is evident from the resulting bias and approximation error: by optimally balancing the linear and nonlinear components, the proposed predictor achieves smaller bias and approximation error than KRR. Consequently, it allows for a larger choice of
\lambda
to better reduce the variance, leading to a smaller overall prediction error. To illustrate this, consider the case where
f^{*}
is linear: by setting
f\equiv 0
and taking
\lambda\to\infty
in (
7
), the proposed estimator
\widehat{f}
recovers the risk
\sigma^{2}d/n
, which holds for general
\mathcal{H}_{K}
and thus extends classical results for specific kernels used in smoothing spline models. We emphasize that this reduction to the parametric rate under a linear model does not generally hold for KRR, even with an optimal choice of
\lambda
in (
6
). On the other hand, when there is no linear signal, a setting favorable to KRR, taking
\alpha=0
in (
7
) shows that the proposed
\widehat{f}
is no worse than (
6
), up to the term
\sigma^{2}d/n
. As detailed in
Remark

7
, this term can be negligible in general settings with small or moderate
d
. Finally, the advantage of
\widehat{f}
over
\widetilde{g}
persists more broadly whenever a nontrivial linear component is present in
f^{*}
; see
Remark

4
.

When
d
is relatively large, we show in
Section

4
that a ridge-type linear estimator can be used in place of OLS to further reduce the term
\sigma^{2}d/n
. Although this introduces an additional regularization parameter, optimizing over both the ridge regularization parameter and the RKHS regularization parameter yields a risk bound that adapts to the presence of both linear and nonlinear signals, and is uniformly better than that of standard KRR; see
Theorems

4
and
5
.

Developing procedures that adapt to the intrinsic complexity of the underlying signal has long been a central theme in statistical learning. A large body of work on adaptive estimation, model selection, and oracle inequalities has emphasized the importance of procedures that adapt to unknown structural complexity and automatically balance approximation and estimation errors
(
4
;
7
;
32
)
. In particular,
17
highlighted the distinction between “practically parametric” and “practically nonparametric” problems and argued that statistical procedures should adapt to latent low-complexity structure whenever possible. Our goal in this paper, however, is to develop a modification of KRR that adapts to latent linear structure while preserving the flexibility of general RKHS methods.

Our approach is also related to partially linear models (PLS)
(
13
)
, in which the regression function is decomposed into a linear component
X_{1}^{\top}\beta
and a nonlinear component
g(X_{2})
where
(X_{1},X_{2})
form a partition of the observed features
X
.
In analyzing PLS, it is often indispensable to assume some identifiability condition to separate the linear and nonlinear signals. For instance, in
22
where a joint estimation for both linear and nonlinear components is adopted, a condition that the smallest eigenvalue of
\mathbb{E}[(X_{1}-\mathbb{E}[X_{1}|X_{2}])(X_{1}-\mathbb{E}[X_{1}|X_{2}])^{\top}]
is bounded away from zero is required.
A similar treatment was also considered in the subsequent works, see for instance
37
, and recently
16
. Alternatively,
39
;
38
consider a three-step procedure. First, the response and each coordinate of
X_{1}
are separately regressed onto the nonlinear feature
X_{2}
. Second, the residuals of the response are linearly regressed onto the residuals of the coordinates of
X_{1}
to obtain an estimator
\widehat{\beta}
of
\beta
. Finally,
Y-X_{1}^{\top}\widehat{\beta}
is regressed onto
X_{2}
to estimate the nonlinear function
g
.
Their analysis relies on the same identifiability condition
as in
22
.
By contrast, our formulation in (
4
) decomposes the prediction into a linear component and an RKHS component based on the
same input

X
. The identifiability condition commonly used in the PLS literature is clearly not applicable in our context. Additive models
(
35
)
offer another approach for capturing both linear and nonlinear structure; however, they rely on a coordinate-wise decomposition of the nonlinear component, which can be restrictive in practice.

We contrast our proposed procedure with a natural two-step procedure that first regresses the responses onto

The residuals of predicting

\widehat{g}_{\text{two}}=\argmin_{g\in\mathcal{H}_{K}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\big\|Q_{\mathbb{X}}\mathbb{Y}-g(\mathbb{X})\big\|^{2}+\lambda\|g\|_{K}^{2}\right\},

which, due to the representer theorem, has a solution with its explicit form given in (
40
).
The corresponding fit of such two-step procedure is

\widehat{f}_{\text{two}}(\mathbb{X})=\mathbb{X}\widehat{\alpha}_{\text{two}}+\widehat{g}_{\text{two}}(\mathbb{X})=P_{\mathbb{X}}\mathbb{Y}+\mathbb{K}(\mathbb{K}+\lambda\mathbb{I}_{n})^{-1}Q_{\mathbb{X}}\mathbb{Y}.

This distinction can also be understood from an iterative perspective: initializing with

The first term on the right-hand side of (

\widetilde{g}

As
\lambda

\lambda

The derivation of the oracle inequality in

1

is finite-dimensional,

and the boundedness of the kernel function.

We proceed to state the risk bound of the proposed predictor.
To facilitate understanding, we start by decomposing the regression function

\displaystyle f^{*}(x)=x^{\top}\alpha+(f^{*}(x)-x^{\top}\alpha)=:x^{\top}\alpha+f_{\alpha}(x)

Recover the parametric rate under linear setting.

3.2
Prediction risk bounds under the random design

The risk bounds in

1
and
2

\mathbb{X}
and the kernel matrix

To quantify the randomness related with

Assumption 2
.

The kernel function

A key element in our analysis is the integral operator
L_{K}:\mathcal{L}_{2}(\rho)\to\mathcal{L}_{2}(\rho)

\displaystyle L_{K}f:=\int_{\mathcal{X}}K_{x}f(x)~{\rm d}\rho(x).

We assume a standard condition that

can be eigen-decomposed.

Assumption 3
.

There exist a sequence of eigenvalues
\{\mu_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
arranged in non-increasing order and corresponding eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
that form an
orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\rho)

L_{K}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mu_{j}\langle\phi_{j},\cdot\rangle_{\rho}~\phi_{j}.

3

(
18

when

is compact and
2

Under Assumption
3

the kernel function

\displaystyle K(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mu_{j}\phi_{j}(x)\phi_{j}(x^{\prime}),\qquad\forall~x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}.

(19)

In the case
\mu_{j}>0
for all

can be equivalently written as

\mathcal{H}_{K}=\{f=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{j}\phi_{j}:\ \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{\gamma_{j}^{2}}/{\mu_{j}}<\infty\}.

If for some
k\in\mathbb{N}

\mu_{j}>0

\mu_{j}=0

In view of the risk bounds in

1
and

f_{\lambda,\mu}~=~P_{\mu,\lambda}f^{*}(\mathbb{X}),

so that

\displaystyle\big\|Q_{\mu}Q_{\mu,\lambda}f^{*}(\mathbb{X})\big\|^{2}~=~

\displaystyle\left\|Q_{\mu}f^{*}(\mathbb{X})-Q_{\mu}P_{\mu,\lambda}f^{*}(\mathbb{X})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle~=~

\displaystyle\left\|Q_{\mu}f^{*}(\mathbb{X})-Q_{\mu}f_{\lambda,\mu}(\mathbb{X})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle~\leq~

\displaystyle\left\|Q_{\mu}^{1/2}f^{*}(\mathbb{X})-Q_{\mu}^{1/2}f_{\lambda,\mu}(\mathbb{X})\right\|^{2}

by
\|Q_{\mu}\|_{\rm op}\leq 1

\displaystyle~\leq~

\displaystyle\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{K}}\left\{{1\over n}\left\|Q_{\mu}^{1/2}(f(\mathbb{X})-f^{*}(\mathbb{X}))\right\|^{2}+\lambda\|f\|_{K}^{2}\right\}

by (
52
)

\displaystyle~=~

\displaystyle\argmin_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n},f\in\mathcal{H}_{K}}\left\{{1\over n}\left\|\mathbb{X}^{\top}\alpha+f(\mathbb{X})-f^{*}(\mathbb{X}))\right\|^{2}+\mu\|\alpha\|^{2}+\lambda\|f\|_{K}^{2}\right\},

where the last step holds by
following the same argument in
Lemma

6
with
f^{*}(\mathbb{X})
in place of
\mathbb{Y}
. This gives the upper bound of
\mathrm{II}
.
Collecting the upper bounds of
\mathrm{I},\mathrm{II}
completes the proof.
∎

C.3
Proof of
Theorem

5

Proof.

By noting that
Q_{\mu}\mathbb{K}Q_{\mu}~\preceq~\mathbb{K}
and
using the same argument in the proof of
Lemma

2
with
Q_{\mu}
in place of
Q_{\mathbb{X}}
gives

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\widetilde{\nu}_{i}\over\widetilde{\nu}_{i}+\lambda\right)^{2}~\leq~\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\widehat{\mu}_{i}\over\widehat{\mu}_{i}+\lambda\right)^{2},\qquad\forall~\lambda>0.

Then in view of the argument of proving
Theorem

3
, we have

\mathbb{E}{\rm Tr}\left(\widetilde{\mathbb{V}}^{2}~\widetilde{\mathbb{V}}_{\lambda}^{-2}\right)~\leq~\mathbb{E}{\rm Tr}\left(\mathbb{K}\mathbb{K}_{\lambda}^{-1}\right),

so that it remains to derive an upper bound for

\mathbb{E}[{\rm Tr}(P_{\mu}^{2})]
.

To this end, note that

\widehat{\Sigma}={1\over n}\mathbb{X}^{\top}\mathbb{X}={1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}.

We find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\rm Tr}(P_{\mu}^{2})]~\leq~\|P_{\mu}\|_{\rm op}~\mathbb{E}~{\rm Tr}(P_{\mu})~\leq~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}~{\rm Tr}(P_{\mu})

by
\|P_{\mu}\|_{\rm op}\leq 1

\displaystyle~=~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm Tr}(\mathbb{X}(\mathbb{X}^{\top}\mathbb{X}+n\mu\mathbb{I}_{d})^{-1}\mathbb{X}^{\top})\right]

\displaystyle~=~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm Tr}(\widehat{\Sigma}~(\widehat{\Sigma}+\mu\mathbb{I}_{d})^{-1})\right]

\displaystyle~\leq~

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\left(\mathbb{E}\widehat{\Sigma}~(\mathbb{E}\widehat{\Sigma}+\mu\mathbb{I}_{d})^{-1}\right)

by Jensen’s inequality

\displaystyle~=~

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}(\Sigma~(\Sigma+\mu\mathbb{I}_{d})^{-1}).

The proof is complete.
