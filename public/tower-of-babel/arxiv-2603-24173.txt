On the dynamical degree of surjective endomorphisms Thanks: MS 2010 classification : 14E05, 37F10, 14J26 Thanks: Key words : surjective rational map, del Pezzo surface, dynamical degree
Ilya Karzhemanov

Abstract
. We establish a couple of dynamical properties of surjective rational maps f : X ⇢ X f:X\dashrightarrow X for smooth projective surfaces X X . We also give a numerical characterization of regular f f in the case when X X is a del Pezzo surface. Some explicit constructions and calculations, related to the topological entropy of f f , are provided.



1.
Introduction

1.1
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective complex surface. In the paper

[
11
]
, we have initiated the study of the geometry of
surjective endomorphisms
f:X\dashrightarrow X
, that is
rational maps satisfying the property
f(X\setminus Z)=X
for
some finite subset
Z\subset X
(cf.
[
9
]
,

[
10
]
and
[
8
]
for statistical, physical and
computational aspects of the story). The aim of the present note
is to highlight some
dynamical
sides of such maps
f
—
again in the case when the divisor
-K_{X}
is ample (i.e. when
X

is a
del Pezzo surface
).
1)
1)

1)

We refer to
[
5
, Ch.
8]
for the basic properties of such
X
.

Consider the Néron–Severi group
\text{Pic}(X)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}=N^{1}(X)
. It follows from
[
11
,
2.1]

that
f
induces a linear map
f^{*}:N^{1}(X)\to N^{1}(X)
with the
property

(f^{n})^{*}=(f^{*})^{n}\ \forall\ n\in\mathbb{N}\quad\text{for the
corresponding iterates}.

The maps with this
algebraic stability
behavior had
acquired a great deal of studies in rational dynamics (see

[
3
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
15
]
). In this regard, one
important numerical characteristic of
f
is the
(first)
dynamical degree

\lambda(f)
, defined as follows. Let
H\in\text{Pic}(X)
be an ample divisor. Then

\lambda(f):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{((f^{n})^{*}(H)\cdot H)}.

This definition does not actually depend on the choice of
H
.

Here is a (classical) relation between
\lambda(f)
and the
spectral radius
\rho(f^{*})
of the linear operator
f^{*}
:

Proposition 1.2
.

\lambda(f)=\rho(f^{*})
and there
is a (
Perron
) vector
v\in N^{1}(X)
, which is a nef class
on
X
, satisfying
f^{*}(v)=\lambda(f)v
.

This claim is very well-known to specialists of course. Yet we
include its proof for transparency and in order to adapt to our
present setting (see Section
2
below).

Remark 1.3
.

Proposition
1.2
implies that

\lambda(f)
is an
algebraic integer
because
f^{*}

preserves the (full) lattice
\text{Pic}(X)
. However, in the case
of an arbitrary rational dominant map
X\dashrightarrow X
,
already when
X=\mathbb{P}^{2}
, its dynamical degree can be

transcendental
(see
[
2
]
). Note also that
Proposition
1.2
holds for any surjective
map
f
and any surface
X
with irregularity
q(X)=0
(see the
arguments in Section
2
).

1.4
.

In Section
3
, we establish a relation between

\lambda(f)
and the (topological) degree
\deg f
of
f
, namely
that
\lambda(f)^{2}\geq\deg f
. Again, this is a classical

log-concavity property
of the dynamical degrees, which we
prove via algebro-geometric means. Next, following the steps of
the proof, we obtain a
numerical
characterization of
regularity (in the current setting):

Theorem 1.5
.

The map
f
is
regular
iff

\lambda(f)^{2}=\deg f
.

Note that the implication “
f
is regular
\Longrightarrow\lambda(f)^{2}=\deg f
” is evident via the projection formula

f_{*}f^{*}=(\deg f)\text{Id}
. In turn, the opposite direction
requires some extra reasoning, which seems to has not been done
previously.

Remark 1.6
.

The case of monomial endomorphisms of

\mathbb{P}^{2}
shows that Theorem
1.5

does not
hold
for arbitrary rational dominant maps
X\dashrightarrow X
(see

[
6
, Section 3]
).

The paper closes with Section
4
, where we discuss
some basic properties of the entropy of
f
, which we further
support by explicit calculations for a wide class of surjective
endomorphisms. We also consider a seemingly interesting class of
rank
1
maps (see Definition
4.5
) and
discuss their structure.

2.
Spectral interpretation (revisited)

2.1
.

Let
V
be a finite-dimensional real normed space and
K\subset V
a closed cone with nonempty interior. Suppose
T:V\longrightarrow V
is
a linear map such that
T(K)\subseteq K
. Denote by
\rho(T)
the
spectral radius of
T
.

Let us recall the following general result (see
[
13
]
):

Theorem 2.2

(Krein–Rutman)
.

In the previous setting,
\rho(T)
is
an eigenvalue of
T
and there exists an eigenvector
v\in K,v\neq 0
, such that
T(v)=\rho(T)v
.

2.3
.

We retain the notations from Introduction. Take
V:=N^{1}(X)
,
T:=f^{*}
and
K:=
the nef cone of
X
(cf.
[
11
, Lemma
2.5]
), so that Theorem
2.2

provides a nef
\mathbb{R}
-class
v
on
X
with
f^{*}(v)=\rho(f^{*})v
.

Choose the divisor
H
from
1.1
above in the
form
v+H^{\prime}
for some ample
H^{\prime}\in N^{1}(X)
.

Lemma 2.4
.

There exists an absolute constant
C

such that

C^{-1}\rho(f^{*})^{n}\leq((f^{*})^{n}(H)\cdot H)\leq C\rho(f^{*})^{n}.

Proof.

The upper bound follows from the operator norm growth. In turn,
the lower bound follows from the fact that
v
is an eigenvector
of
f^{*}
and that the intersections
H\cdot v,H\cdot H^{\prime}
are
positive.
∎

In order to prove Proposition
1.2
it
suffices to take the
n^{\text{th}}
roots of the inequalities in
Lemma
2.4
and then pass to the limit
n\to\infty
.

Remark 2.5
.

Note that the class
v

is not
necessarily big
(i.e. it need not satisfy
(v^{2})>0
). As an
example we consider
X:=\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\bf t}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\bf w}
and
its (regular) endomorphism

f([t_{0}:t_{1}]\times[w_{0}:w_{1}]):=[t^{d}_{0}:t^{d}_{1}]\times[w_{0}:w_{1}]

for some
d\geq 2
. In the basis of
N^{1}(X)
, corresponding to two
rulings on
X
, the operator
f^{*}
is represented by the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}d&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
. Then we have

\rho(f^{*})=d
and
(v^{2})=0
.

3.
Relation between
\lambda
and
\deg

3.1
.

Let us see first that
\lambda(f)^{2}\geq\deg f
in our setting.
Consider a resolution diagram for
f
:

\textstyle{Y\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{g}
\scriptstyle{h}
\textstyle{X\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f}
\textstyle{X.}

Here
g
is a composition of blow-ups and
h
is a regular
morphism. Then for
H
from
1.1
we have
f^{*}(H)=g_{*}(h^{*}(H))
and so

h^{*}(H)=g^{*}(f^{*}(H))-E,

where
E:=\displaystyle\sum_{i}a_{i}E_{i}
,
E_{i}
are

g
-exceptional curves and
a_{i}\geq 0
are some coefficients. It
follows that

(f^{*}(H)^{2})=(h^{*}(H)^{2})-E^{2}=(\deg f)(H^{2})-E^{2}\geq(\deg f)(H^{2})

because the intersection matrix
\{(E_{i}\cdot E_{j})\}_{i,j}
is
negative definite.

Further, since both classes
f^{*}(H)
and
H
are nef, the Hodge
Index Theorem gives

(f^{*}(H)\cdot H)^{2}\geq(f^{*}(H)^{2})(H^{2}).

Thus we get

(f^{*}(H)\cdot H)^{2}\geq(\deg f)(H^{2})^{2}.

Finally, replacing
f
with
f^{n}
and using
\deg f^{n}=(\deg f)^{n}
, we get

\sqrt[n]{((f^{n})^{*}(H)\cdot H)^{2}}\geq(\deg f)\sqrt[n]{(H^{2})^{2}},

which for
n\to\infty
becomes
\lambda(f)^{2}\geq\deg f
, as
claimed.

3.2
.

We now turn to the proof of Theorem
1.5
. Let

\lambda(f)^{2}=\deg f
and suppose the map
f
is

non-regular
. The latter means that the resolution
g:Y\longrightarrow X
in
3.1
is a non-trivial birational
contraction.

Consider the Perron class
v
from
Proposition
1.2
. We have
(v^{2})\geq 0

because
v
is nef.

Lemma 3.3
.

The class
v
can not be big
(
\Longleftrightarrow
can not satisfy
(v^{2})>0
).

Proof.

Suppose the contrary. Then
v
equals an
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
-linear
combination of big divisors
v_{j}
on
X
(see
[
14
, 2.2.B]
).
After taking a multiple one may assume that each
v_{j}
is
represented by some curve. All
v_{j}
can also be chosen to contain
an indeterminacy point of
f
.

Now put (formally)
H:=v
in
3.1
. Then it
follows that
E\neq 0
and

(f^{*}(v)^{2})=(h^{*}(v)^{2})-E^{2}>(\deg f)(v^{2}).

On the other hand, we have
(f^{*}(v)^{2})=(\lambda(f)^{2})(v^{2})
and

(v^{2})>0
by assumption, which contradicts the initial equality

\lambda(f)^{2}=\deg f
.
∎

According to Lemma
3.3
it remains to exclude
the remaining case when
(v^{2})=0
(cf.
Remark
2.5
). Here we will crucially rely on
the fact that
X
is a del Pezzo surface.

Namely, the Mori cone of
X
is rational polyhedral, and
v

corresponds to one of its faces (cf.
[
11
,
2.9]
).
Furthermore, this face is
contractible
, i.e. there exists a
morphism
X\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
given by (a multiple of)
v
. Then similar
arguments as in the proof of Lemma
3.3
, with

H:=v
and
E\neq 0
, yield

0=(f^{*}(v)^{2})=(h^{*}(v)^{2})-E^{2}>(\deg f)(v^{2})=0,

which is absurd. This concludes the proof of
Theorem
1.5
.

4.
Examples and discussion

4.1
.

Let again the setting be as in
1.1
above. With
the map
f
are associated two kinds of (
algebraic
)
“entropies”:
\log\lambda(f)
and
\log\deg f
. Both numbers
measure certain rate of volume growth of algebraic cycles on
X
.
For
\log\lambda(f)
, we have seen this in
Proposition
1.2
(see also the initial
definition of
\lambda(f)
), whereas
\log\deg f
can be described
as follows (cf.
[
12
,
1.1]
). Let
\omega
be a
positive closed
(1,1)
-form on
X
(e.g. the restriction of the
Fubini–Studi form). Then

\log\deg f=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\int_{X}f^{n*}(\omega\wedge\omega).

Note that the usual (
topological
) entropy
h(f)
of
f

satisfies
h(f)\leq\max\{\log\lambda(f),\log\deg f\}
(see

[
4
, Théorème 1]
).

We proceed with specific examples.

Example 4.2

(see
[
12
, Example 1.6]
)
.

The map
f=[f_{0}:f_{1}:f_{2}]:\mathbb{P}^{2}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2}
with quadratic components

f_{0}:=xz+y^{2},\ f_{1}:=yz+x^{2},\ f_{2}:=x^{2}+y^{2}

has
\lambda(f)=2

and
\deg f=3
. Thus
h(f)\leq\log 3
.

Example 4.3

(see
[
8
, Proposition 3.2]
)
.

The map
f=[f_{0}:f_{1}:f_{2}]:\mathbb{P}^{2}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2}
with cubic components

f_{0}:=x^{2}y+y^{2}z,\ f_{1}:=xyz,\ f_{2}:=x^{2}y+xy^{2}+2y^{2}z+z^{2}(x+y)

has

\lambda(f)=3
and
\deg f=4
. Thus
h(f)\leq\log 4
.

4.4
.

Let us give the following:

Definition 4.5
.

The map
f:X\dashrightarrow X
is
said to be
of rank
1
if
f^{*}
has rank
1
.

The preceding examples are particular instances of rank
1
maps.
Furthermore, according to
[
7
, Theorem 1.6]
there is a
surjective map
\varphi:\mathbb{P}^{2}\dashrightarrow X
, as is actually
the case for
any rational surface

X
. Then, composing this

\varphi
with a linear projection
\psi:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2}
, yields a
surjective endomorphism
f=\varphi\circ\psi
of
X
. By
construction the linear map
f^{*}
has rank
1
because it factors
through
\varphi^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Example 4.6

(see
[
11
, Remark 2.3]
)
.

Consider
X:=\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
equipped with product coordinates
[t_{0}:t_{1}]\times[w_{0}:w_{1}]
. Then Segre embedding maps
X
(isomorphically) onto a
quadric in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
given by the equation
x_{0}x_{3}-x_{1}x_{2}=0
in
projective coordinates
x_{0}:=t_{0}w_{0},x_{1}:=t_{0}w_{1}
, etc. Define
(surjective) map
\varphi:\mathbb{P}^{2}\dashrightarrow X
by

\varphi([x:y:z])=[xy:x^{2}-y^{2}]\times[xz:x^{2}-z^{2}]

and take
\psi:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2}
to be the projection from the point

[1:0:0:1]\in\mathbb{P}^{3}
. Then we have

f([t_{0}:t_{1}]\times[w_{0}:w_{1}])=[t_{0}t_{1}w_{0}w_{1}:t_{0}^{2}w_{1}^{2}-t_{1}^{2}w_{0}^{2}]\times[t_{0}w_{1}(t_{0}w_{0}-t_{1}w_{1}):t_{0}^{2}w_{1}^{2}-(t_{0}w_{0}-t_{1}w_{1})^{2}].

The matrix of the operator
f^{*}
— in the basis as in
Remark
2.5
— is
\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\
2&2\end{pmatrix}
. It follows that
\lambda(f)=4
. Note also that

\deg f=8
. Thus
h(f)\leq\log 8
.

4.7
.

Every rank
1
surjective map
f
satisfies
\lambda(f)=
the
trace of
f^{*}
(cf. Proposition
1.2
).
Hence we have
\lambda(f)\in\mathbb{Z}
. At the same time, as
Remark
2.5
shows, the property
\lambda(f)\in\mathbb{Z}

does not
characterize rank
1
endomorphisms.
This abundance of “degenerate” surjective endomorphisms for del
Pezzo surfaces should be contrasted with the more restrictive

non-degenerate
case (see
[
11
, Theorem 1.2]
).

In order to get a grasp of rank
1
maps one may wonder: does any
such
f
factor through
\mathbb{P}^{2}
via some surjective maps
\varphi

and
\psi
as above? It turns out that it
need not
. To see
this let us modify the map
f
from
Example
4.6
as follows:

f_{\varepsilon}([t_{0}:t_{1}]\times[w_{0}:w_{1}])=[t_{0}t_{1}w_{0}w_{1}:t_{0}^{2}w_{1}^{2}-\varepsilon t_{1}^{2}w_{0}^{2}]\times[t_{0}w_{1}(t_{0}w_{0}-\varepsilon t_{1}w_{1}):t_{0}^{2}w_{1}^{2}-(t_{0}w_{0}-t_{1}w_{1})^{2}]

for the parameter
\varepsilon
close to
1
. In other words, we
are considering a family of rational maps
f_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{P}^{1}_{{\bf t}}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}_{{\bf w}}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}_{{\bf t}}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}_{{\bf w}}
, written shortly as

{\bf t}\times{\bf w}\mapsto f_{\varepsilon}({\bf t})\times f_{\varepsilon}({\bf w})

(compare with
[
12
,
1.3]
).

The indeterminacy locus
Z_{f_{\varepsilon}}
of
f_{\varepsilon}

consists of two components
(f_{\varepsilon}({\bf t})=0)
and

(f_{\varepsilon}({\bf w})=0)
. It follows that

Z_{f_{\varepsilon}}
is
finite
, of total length
16
, for
all
\varepsilon
. Hence the schemes
Z_{f_{\varepsilon}}
form a

flat family
. Now, since
f_{1}=f
is surjective, the proof
of
[
12
, Theorem 1.10]
applies verbatim and gives
surjectivity of the general map
f_{\varepsilon}
as well. But for
the latter following holds:

Proposition 4.8
.

If
\varepsilon\neq 1
is generic, then we
have
f_{\varepsilon}\neq\varphi\circ\psi
for any surjective
maps
\varphi,\psi
as above.

Proof.

Suppose the contrary. Then, since
f^{*}_{\varepsilon}=f^{*}_{1}
for
all
\varepsilon
(cf. Example
4.6
),
it follows that
\psi:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2}
must necessarily be a

regular
projection. Hence there is an involution
\iota
,
acting biregularly on
X
, such that
X/\penalty\iota=\mathbb{P}^{2}
and

f_{\varepsilon}\circ\iota=f_{\varepsilon}
.

Put
t:=t_{0}/\penalty t_{1}
and
w:=w_{0}/\penalty w_{1}
. It is easy to
see that
\iota
acts as follows:

(t,w)\mapsto(A(w),A^{-1}(t))

for some
A\in\mathrm{PGL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. We also have

f_{\varepsilon}(t,w)=\big(\frac{tw}{t^{2}-\varepsilon w^{2}},\frac{t(tw-\varepsilon)}{t^{2}-(tw-1)^{2}}\big).

The first component of
f_{\varepsilon}
depends only on the ratio

s:=t/\penalty w
. Then the property
f_{\varepsilon}\circ\iota=f_{\varepsilon}
forces
s^{\prime}:=A(w)/\penalty A^{-1}(t)
to satisfy
either
s^{\prime}=s
or
s^{\prime}=-\varepsilon/\penalty s
. This, in turn,
gives the following options:

•

A(w)=K/\penalty w
for some
K\in\mathbb{C}
;

•

A(w)=Lw
for
L^{2}=-\varepsilon
.

It is now immediate that the second component

\displaystyle\frac{t(tw-\varepsilon)}{t^{2}-(tw-1)^{2}}
of

f_{\varepsilon}
fails to be
\iota
-invariant. This contradiction
finishes the proof of Proposition
4.8
.
∎

Thus we see that even for “simple” surfaces the structure of
their surjective endomorphisms is quite tricky. It would be
interesting to obtain a rough, at least, description of such
endomorphisms
f
— perhaps in terms of a “statistics” of
numbers
\lambda(f)/\penalty\deg f
(compare with
[
1
, Lecture
3]
).
