Affinity- and epitope-dependent pathogenicity of GM-CSF autoantibodies in patients with autoimmune pulmonary alveolar proteinosis
Authors: Shinji Futami, Chie Kawai, Jun-ichi Kishikawa, Masaki Hirose, Hiroshi Kida, Po-hung Wang, Kazuo Yamashita, Fumihiko Ishikawa, Takayuki Kato, Yoshikazu Inoue, Atsushi Kumanogoh, Tomohiro Kurosaki, Takeshi Inoue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autoimmune pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (aPAP) is a rare lung disease caused by autoantibodies targeting granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF). Although serum GM-CSF autoantibody levels are markedly increased in aPAP patients, total antibody titer does not correlate with disease severity. Here, we characterize 186 monoclonal anti-GM-CSF autoantibodies derived from GM-CSF-specific B cells in 28 aPAP patients with varying disease severity, including three longitudinal cohorts, to determine whether epitope specificity and affinity of the autoantibodies contribute to disease pathophysiology. We classify these antibodies into two groups based on their epitopes: class 1 (targeting A, BD, or D epitopes) and class 2 (targeting B or C epitopes). In class 1 antibodies, affinity strongly correlates with neutralization activity, whereas this relationship is weak or absent in class 2 antibodies. High-affinity class 1 antibodies are present at higher levels in patients with more severe disease and are sufficient to induce PAP symptoms in a humanized mouse model. Thus, these findings identify epitope specificity and affinity as key determinants of pathogenicity and provide a mechanistic framework for understanding why total serum autoantibody levels fail to reflect disease severity in aPAP.

Introduction

Pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (PAP) is a rare lung disease that results in respiratory failure owing to an accumulation of pulmonary surfactant1–3. The observation of similar phenotypes in granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF)- or GM-CSF receptor-deficient mice revealed that the disease arises from the dysfunction of alveolar macrophages as a consequence of impaired GM-CSF signalling, which is normally responsible for the clearance of excessive surfactant lipids and proteins in the lung4–7.

In autoimmune PAP (aPAP), autoantibodies to GM-CSF have been found in both peripheral blood and alveolar lavage fluid8, and a serum GM-CSF autoantibody test has become a reliable clinical diagnostic tool for aPAP9. Indeed, transfer of affinity-purified polyclonal GM-CSF autoantibodies from aPAP patients has been demonstrated to be sufficient to develop the disease in nonhuman primates10. However, the serum concentrations of GM-CSF antibodies do not reliably correlate with disease severity, prognosis, or other biomarkers of PAP11,12.

The action mechanism of autoantibodies on aPAP pathogenesis has been proposed by two non-mutually exclusive models by human monoclonal antibody (mAb) analyses13,14. First, based on the data that almost all autoantibodies tested neutralized the GM-CSF activity, their pathogenicity could be due to blocking GM-CSF binding to its cognate receptor13. Alternatively, binding of polyclonal autoantibodies to GM-CSF reduces its biologically active concentration by irreversible sequestration of GM-CSF into high molecular weight immune complexes and degradation of immune complexes in an Fc-dependent manner14. However, neither study included samples spanning a wide range of disease severity. Therefore, the reason for the discrepancy between serum anti-GM-CSF concentrations and patient disease severity remains to be determined.

Here, we investigate whether epitope specificity and antibody affinity determine the pathogenic relevance of GM-CSF autoantibodies in aPAP. By analyzing GM-CSF-specific B cells from aPAP patients with varying degrees of disease severity, including longitudinal samples, we identify distinct antibody classes with different relationships between affinity and neutralizing activity and show that high-affinity autoantibodies targeting receptor-interfering epitopes are enriched in more severe disease. Furthermore, we demonstrate that such antibodies are sufficient to induce pathology in a humanized mouse model. Thus, these findings indicate that the pathogenicity of GM-CSF autoantibodies is shaped not simply by their abundance, but by their qualitative properties. More broadly, this study provides a framework for understanding how epitope specificity and affinity determine the disease relevance of cytokine autoantibodies and may inform improved biomarker and therapeutic strategies.

Results

Study design and patient characteristics

A total of 28 aPAP patients, including three patients with longitudinal sampling at a 1.5-year interval, were enrolled in this study. The distribution of sex and age in our cohort reflects the general distribution of Japanese aPAP patients (Fig. 1a)15. Notably, our cohort covers patients with a broad range of disease severity, determined by a disease severity score (DSS)16, which is defined by the presence of symptoms and the degree of reduction in arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) at the time of diagnosis (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Data 1). As expected, all the clinical parameters measured were significantly correlated with the DSS (Fig. 1b), except for the plasma anti-GM-CSF antibody levels (Fig. 1c), consistent with the notion that the serum GM-CSF autoantibody levels are not correlated with disease severity11. Identification of GM-CSF-specific B cells in aPAP patients

To address the question of how GM-CSF autoantibodies contribute to aPAP pathology, we developed a single B cell analysis pipeline that enables unbiased and efficient characterization of the antigen-specific B cell repertoire and mAbs (Fig. 2a)17. As the frequencies of GM-CSF-specific B cells in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were found to be very rare, the staining with antigen probe that had been conjugated to two different fluorophores (BV421 and APC) allowed us for accurate identification of antigen-specific B cells as double positives (CD19+, BV421-GM-CSF+/APC-GM-CSF+) by flow cytometry (Fig. 2b). We identified GM-CSF-specific B cells with a large variance in number in aPAP patients, in contrast to undetectable or only a limited numbers in healthy controls (Fig. 2b). The majority of GM-CSF-specific B cells expressed IgG1 (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Data 2), consistent with the notion that GM-CSF autoantibodies in aPAP patients are composed predominantly of IgG1 and IgG2 isotypes8,9. To examine the functional properties of antibodies encoded by GM-CSF-specific B cells, we cloned and characterized mAbs from single cell-sorted GM-CSF-specific B cells by PCR amplification of immunoglobulin heavy- and light-chain V(D)J fragments followed by our antibody cloning pipeline (Fig. 2a)17. More than 90% of mAbs generated successfully bound to GM-CSF, validating the reliability of our methodology. In total, we cloned and produced 181 anti-GM-CSF mAbs recombinantly as IgG1/Igκ or IgG1/Igλ, including 2 from healthy controls and 179 from aPAP patients (Supplementary Data 2). More than 70% of these mAbs were derived from three longitudinally followed patients (Pt9, Pt10, and Pt11). We also synthetically produced five previously reported anti-GM-CSF mAbs (Supplementary Data 2)13. Comparison of antibody repertoires across patients showed that, although certain VH genes were preferentially used in some patients, such as IGHV1-3 in Pt10, we did not identify any “public” VH genes, i.e., ones commonly used across patients, in our cohort (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Data 2). Repertoire analysis of mAbs from longitudinal patients (Pt9, Pt10, and Pt11) revealed preferential VH gene usage shared between initial and follow-up samples within each patient, but not across different patients (Fig. 2d).

Correlation of autoantibody affinity with disease severity

The absence of public clonotypes and the diverse clonality of GM-CSF autoantibodies prompted us to comprehensively assess their binding capacity to GM-CSF by our established high-throughput bead-based flow cytometric binding assay, in which FACS compensation beads coated with each mAb were mixed with fluorescently-labelled GM-CSF probe and analysed by flow cytometry17,18. We assigned a geometric mean fluorescence intensity (gMFI) value to each antibody as an affinity index. Somatic hypermutation (SHM) analysis of all the antibody sequences revealed that the total number of nucleic acid mutations in the heavy- and light-chains was significantly higher in the high-affinity antibodies than in the low-affinity ones (Fig. 3a). The contribution of SHM to the antibody affinity was further confirmed by generating unmutated common ancestor (UCA) variants of the antibody in which SHMs in both the VH and VL regions were reverted to the germline sequence. All four UCA antibodies tested by biolayer interferometry (BLI) showed more than 100-fold lower affinity than their corresponding mutated antibodies (Fig. 3d). Although total serum GM-CSF antibody levels have been reported not to correlate with disease severity, our data indicate that the number of SHMs in GM-CSF-reactive peripheral blood B cells is associated with disease severity (Fig. 3b, c). In fact, high-affinity anti-GM-CSF antibody concentration in plasma, selectively measured by ELISA after removal of low-affinity antibodies by guanidine treatment, was significantly higher in patients with severe disease than in those with mild disease (Fig. 3e).

We next analyzed longitudinally collected samples to observe any correlation between disease status and the property of GM-CSF-specific B cells within individual patients. We focused on two patients (Pt10 and Pt9) for detailed analysis. In Pt10, an initial blood sample was obtained during a severe disease stage, and then a follow-up sample was taken 1.5 years later, when the clinical condition had spontaneously improved without treatment during the intervening period (Fig. 4a). We found that the frequency of GM-CSF-specific B cells was decreased in the follow-up sample (Fig. 4b). Concomitantly, SHM numbers were significantly fewer and the affinity to GM-CSF was significantly lower in mAbs using VH1-3/VK1-5 genes from the follow-up sampling (Fig. 4c). In contrast, in Pt9, whose disease status was severe at the initial sampling and had worsened by the follow-up time point (Fig. 4e), such a decrease in the frequency of GM-CSF-specific B cells was not observed (Fig. 4f). Furthermore, SHM levels and the affinities of mAbs using VH7-4-1/VK1-39, VH3-66/VK1-39, and VH1-69/VK4-1 genes remained largely unchanged during this period (Fig. 4g). Collectively, these data suggest a strong correlation between disease severity, the number of SHM, and the affinity of autoantibodies, thereby emphasizing the importance of GC reactions in generating pathogenic GM-CSF-reactive B cells.

Epitope dependency on the neutralization potency of autoantibodies

To examine whether epitope specificity might also contribute to disease pathogenicity, we conducted competitive binding experiments using BLI for epitope specification (Fig. 5a and b). Based on the competitive binding properties of mAbs, we identified at least six epitopes on GM-CSF antigen; five sterically non-overlapping epitopes (“A”-“E” epitopes) and one epitope that overlaps with both B and D epitopes (“BD” epitope) (Fig. 5b). No specific epitope was preferentially enriched among antibodies derived from patients with severe disease. The ability of autoantibodies to neutralize the bioactivity of GM-CSF was assessed by measuring their inhibition of human GM-CSF-dependent proliferation and survival of the TF-1 human cell line in vitro13,14,19,20. Selected mAbs showed a range of neutralization activity, with IC50 values ranging from 1 to 20,000 ng/mL (Supplementary Data 2). As expected, there was a general correlation between the affinity (KD, determined by BLI) and the IC50 of mAbs, albeit with some outliers (Fig. 5d). When analyzed by epitope, the strength of this correlation appeared to be epitope-dependent: mAbs targeting the A, BD, and D epitopes exhibited a strong correlation between KD and IC50 values; those targeting the C epitope showed only a weak correlation; and B epitope-targeting mAbs displayed no detectable neutralization activity regardless of their affinity (Fig. 5e). Furthermore, mAbs against the A and BD epitopes showed a significant correlation between IC50 values and the number of SHM, whereas C epitope-targeting mAbs did not (Fig. 5f). Notably, high-affinity, potently neutralizing autoantibodies, defined as KD < 10−8 M and IC50 < 100 ng/mL, were almost exclusively derived from severe (DSS3-5) patients (20/23 clones, 87%). In contrast, low-affinity, weakly neutralizing Abs, defined as KD > 10−8 M and IC50 > 1,000 ng/mL, were found in both severe and mild (DSS1-2) cases (10/20 clones, 50%) (Fig. 5e and g). These findings suggest that both epitope specificity and affinity maturation contribute to the pathogenic potential of the autoantibodies.

Supporting this view, the C epitope was the dominant target in one mild case (Pt4; DSS2), and most A or BD epitope-targeting antibodies in another mild case (Pt11; DSS2) exhibited low neutralizing activity (Fig. 5h). In contrast, all mAbs from one severe case (Pt10; DSS4) targeted the A epitope, including those with high neutralizing activity. In another severe case (Pt9; DSS4/5), the Abs showed broad epitope diversity and a range of neutralizing activities, including potently neutralizing BD and D epitope Abs. Taken together, these results led us to hypothesize that Abs targeting the A, BD, or D epitopes with strong neutralizing activity play a dominant role in severe pathogenicity, whereas Abs targeting B or C epitopes contribute only marginally to disease severity. We therefore classified these Abs as class 1 (A, BD, and D epitopes) and class 2 (B and C epitopes), respectively. Importantly, the plasma concentration of high-affinity class 1 anti-GM-CSF antibodies was significantly higher in patients with severe disease than in those with mild disease (Fig. 5i).

Epitope mapping of BD and C epitope autoantibodies

The epitope of a high-affinity and potently neutralizing A epitope antibody had been identified by X-ray crystallography in previous studies (“F1 antibody” in refs. 13,21), revealing that this antibody interferes with the binding of GM-CSF to its receptor alpha subunit. To obtain more structural insights into the pathogenic mechanism of autoantibodies, we performed epitope mapping of selected mAbs with BD and C epitope specificities by cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM). To overcome the general difficulty in structure determination of small-sized proteins such as GM-CSF ( < 30 kD), which have limited three-dimensional features for cryo-EM analysis, we adopted a strategy of preparing the GM-CSF antigen complexed with two non-competing Fab fragments; the A epitope F1 Fab and either a BD or C epitope Fab. The GM-CSF:A Fab:BD Fab complex and the GM-CSF:A Fab:C Fab complex structures were determined by a cryo-EM single-particle analysis at 4.0 Å and 3.7 Å resolution, respectively. The workflows of the image processing are summarized in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2. The previously reported X-ray structure of the GM-CSF:A Fab complex fitted well into both cryo-EM maps (Supplementary Fig. 3). Besides this, the general structure of the Fab fragment also fitted to the additional densities in each map, allowing these densities to be assigned to the BD and C Fabs, respectively (Fig. 6a and c). For the BD Fab, due to insufficient resolution to build an atomic model, a general Fab atomic model was used to estimate its epitope region. In the case of the GM-CSF:A Fab:C Fab complex, local refinement focusing on the interfaces among GM-CSF and two Fabs improved the quality of the map and allowed us to build an atomic model (Supplementary Fig. 4a), which showed specific interactions between GM-CSF and the C Fab (Supplementary Fig. 4b–d). These structural analyses suggested the GM-CSF epitope regions for BD and C antibodies (K74-Q86 for BD and L25-A33 for C). To functionally validate the proposed BD epitope, we introduced alanine substitutions into the predicted epitope on GM-CSF and measured binding to the BD Fab by BLI. Induction of aPAP pathogenesis in humanized mice by single monoclonal autoantibodies

The pathogenicity of GM-CSF antibodies in animal models has only been evaluated in non-human primates because of the lack of cross-species reactivity of GM-CSF between mice and humans10,22,23. In fact, homozygous hIL-3/GM-CSF knock-in (KI) mice, in which mouse IL-3 and GM-CSF are replaced by their human counterparts, develop PAP due to the impaired differentiation of mouse alveolar macrophages24, as is also the case with GM-CSF-deficient mice25. It has been shown that transplantation of human cord blood hematopoietic cells into hIL-3/GM-CSF KI mice on an immunodeficient background (Rag2−/− Il2rg−/−) supports human alveolar macrophage development and can rescue the PAP phenotype24. In our study, hIL-3/GM-CSFKI/KI Rag2−/− Il2rg−/− mice were transplanted intravenously with CD34+ human hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPC) one to two days after birth, as previously described26 (Fig. 7a). Six weeks post-transplantation, once human cell engraftment had been confirmed in peripheral blood, we administered high-affinity class 1 (A epitope (Pt10-21) or BD epitope (Pt9-2-28)) or class 2 (B epitope (Pt9-2-58)) GM-CSF mAbs intravenously every two weeks to assess their pathogenic potential in vivo. Thirteen weeks later, mice engrafted with human HSPCs showed lower total protein levels in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid compared to non-engrafted controls, as expected (Fig. 7b). Notably, administration of class 1, but not class 2, autoantibodies significantly increased BAL protein levels (Fig. 7b). Consistent with these findings, lung histopathology revealed accumulation of foamy macrophages in the alveolar spaces in mice injected with the class 1 mAbs (Fig. 7c). Discussion

Our study identifies two types of autoantibodies distinguished by the correlation between their affinity for GM-CSF and their neutralizing activity: class 1 Abs (targeting A, BD, and D epitopes), which show a strong correlation, and class 2 Abs (targeting B, and C epitopes), which exhibit a weaker or no correlation. Structural analyses of BD and C epitope Abs in this study, together with the previously reported structure of an A epitope Ab13,21, provide mechanistic insights into the functional differences between class 1 and class 2 Abs. A and BD epitope Abs (class 1) bind to GM-CSF in a manner that directly interferes with its interaction with the GM-CSF receptor alpha- and beta-subunits, respectively. In contrast, C epitope Abs (class 2) do not directly block the receptor-binding interface, although some may still indirectly interfere with such interaction.

The reasons why cytokine autoantibodies do or do not cause pathology remain unclear. However, three lines of evidence from our study support the classical model in which autoantibodies with sufficiently high affinity can neutralize the biological activity of a cytokine simply by binding to it and blocking its interaction with the cognate receptor. First, the presence of memory B cells encoding class 1 high-affinity Abs was associated with disease severity. Indeed, the highest affinity of class 1 Abs for GM-CSF (KD in the ~ 10−10 M range) was comparable to the affinity of GM-CSF for its receptor (KD ≈ 2 × 10−10 M)27. Second, our longitudinal analysis suggested that clinical improvement in aPAP symptoms is more closely related to reduced affinity maturation and/or a reduced abundance of autoreactive B cells, rather than an obvious shift in epitope class. Finally, the injection of independent class 1, but not class 2, mAbs (KD ~ 2 × 10−10 M) was sufficient to induce pathology in a humanized GM-CSF/GM-CSF receptor mouse model.

Apart from the aforementioned neutralization mechanism, several studies of toxins and cytokines have demonstrated synergistic effects between Abs recognizing distinct epitopes on the same molecules14,28,29, suggesting that, in some settings, the generation of autoantibodies targeting multiple epitopes on the same antigen may lead to the formation of immune complexes that are subsequently cleared in vivo. Indeed, in aPAP, it has been proposed that such immune complex formation results in sequestration of GM-CSF into high-molecular-weight complexes and/or Fc-dependent degradation of GM-CSF in vivo. Through such a scavenging mechanism, GM-CSF is effectively cleared, leading to a reduction in its biologically active concentration. Thus, both neutralization and clearance mechanisms are likely to function in a complementary manner in the context of polyclonal responses by autoantibodies. Nevertheless, our data suggest that the generation of high-affinity class 1 autoantibodies is a critical determinant of severe disease progression. In this context, it could be of significant importance to understand how B cell epitope spreading occurs on GM-CSF, as has been described in diseases such as SLE, multiple sclerosis, and pemphigus30.

Overall, we observed a strong correlation between the number of SHMs in GM-CSF autoantibodies, their affinity, and disease severity (Fig. 3), suggesting the importance of sustained GC reactions in the progression of aPAP. Moreover, affinity and neutralizing activity were well-correlated among certain class 1 autoantibodies. Although the precise mechanism underlying the breakdown of immune tolerance that leads to the emergence of GM-CSF-reactive B cells remains unclear, a genome-wide association study has identified a genetic variant in the MHC class II region that is significantly associated with increased disease risk in aPAP31. Given the essential role of CD4+ T follicular helper cells in maintaining GCs, T cell tolerance break potentially may contribute to persistent GC reactions and promote the development of high-affinity, autoreactive B cells, ultimately driving aPAP pathogenesis.

For the treatment of aPAP, a replacement therapy with GM-CSF inhalants has been conducted as a commercially available medication, because the disease is based on abnormal GM-CSF signaling. However, in severe cases, invasive interventions such as whole-lung lavage to physically remove surfactant deposits in the alveolar space have become the standard of care. Therefore, methods to transmit GM-CSF signals while suppressing the function of pathogenic autoantibodies may be considered as a therapeutic option in the future. For example, although technically challenging, the development of GM-CSF variants with sequence modifications to escape class 1 Ab recognition while maintaining the ability to bind to its receptor for transducing a proper signal might contribute to improving the pharmacological therapy of inhaled recombinant GM-CSF32. Alternatively, the development of novel agonists capable of activating GM-CSF receptor signaling may also be worth exploring. In addition, quantification of circulating high-affinity antibodies directed to class 1 Ab epitopes may improve the diagnosis and clinical assessment of aPAP, as this parameter appears to more closely reflect the disease severity.

The primary limitation of this study is that we focused mainly on class 1 antibodies, and therefore the contribution of class 2 antibodies to disease pathogenicity has not been fully addressed. In fact, high-affinity class 2 antibodies were also identified in some severe patients. Alternative mechanisms, including non-neutralizing, Fc-dependent effector functions of class 2 antibodies, and their synergistic effects with class 1 antibodies, may also contribute to disease pathogenesis. It should also be noted that all samples analyzed in this study were obtained from Japanese patients, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Future study investigating potential changes in the autoantibody repertoire after inhaled recombinant GM-CSF therapy will be of clinical importance.

In conclusion, our data, obtained from aPAP patients across a broad range of disease severity, demonstrate the presence of circulating GC-CSF-specific B cells that produce autoantibodies with diverse affinities and epitope specificities. Affinity maturation of B cells recognizing receptor-interfering epitopes appears to enhance pathogenic potential. These findings provide important insights into the immunopathogenesis of aPAP and implications for the development of improved diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Patient samples and study approval

Patients were enrolled through public recruitment at NHO Kinki Chuo Chest Medical Center and the University of Osaka from July 2017 to October 2021, with the clinical laboratory information related to the disease severity measured by the insurance coverage (Supplementary Data 1). Plasma and PBMCs were isolated from whole blood using Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation (#GE17-1440-03; GE Healthcare) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the cells were stored in CELLBANKER 1 (#11910; Takara Bio) at −80°C. Commercially available PBMCs were used as healthy controls (Precision for Medicine). The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of NHO Kinki Chuo Chest Medical Center (permission #559) and the University of Osaka (permission #781-4). All volunteers provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. No financial compensation was provided to participants.

Information on participants’ sex was obtained from their clinical medical records. Patients with aPAP are more common in males than in females, but the severity of the disease is independent of sex. A detailed summary of the clinical characteristics of participants is presented in Supplementary Data 1.

Recombinant GM-CSF protein expression and purification

Human GM-CSF cDNA fused with C-terminal Avi-tag and 6×His-tag was cloned into the pcDNA3.4 vector (#A14697; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Recombinant GM-CSF protein was expressed and purified using the Expi293 Expression System (#A14635; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and TALON metal affinity resin (#635504; Clontech) as described17,33. Biotinylation of GM-CSF was performed by co-transfection of a BirA expression plasmid(#64395; Addgene) and expression in culture with 100 μM biotin (#B4501; Sigma-Aldrich).

Flow cytometry and cell sorting

Single-cell suspensions of human PBMCs were analyzed and sorted on a FACSAria Ⅱ (BD). Cryopreserved PBMCs were thawed at 37°C and immediately washed with PBS containing 2% FBS, followed by staining with fluorescently labeled GM-CSF probes and antibodies in PBS with 2% FBS for 20 min at 4°C. FITC anti-human CD38 (#11-0388-42), PE anti-human IgL (#12-9990-42), APC Streptavidin (#SA1005), and anti-human Fc Receptor binding inhibitor (#14-9161-73) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. PE-Cy7 anti-human IgD (#348209), APC-Cy7 anti-human CD27 (#356424), BV785 anti-human CD19 (#302240), and BV421 Streptavidin (#405225) were purchased from BioLegend. BV510 anti-human IgG (#563247) was purchased from BD. Propidium iodide (#537060) (as a viability dye) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software v10.5.3 (BD). Representative gating strategy can be found in Supplementary Fig. 6. Antibodies used for flow cytometry are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

BCR cloning and antibody expression

BCR cloning and monoclonal antibody expression were performed as described previously17. GM-CSF-specific B cells from human PBMCs were single-cell sorted into 96-well plates containing 4 μL/well of ice-cold 0.5 × PBS with 10 mM dithiothreitol (#707265 ML; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1.6 U RNasin Plus RNase Inhibitor (#N261B; Promega). cDNA was synthesized in a total volume of 10 μL/well in the original 96-well plates containing 100 ng random primer (pd(N)6; #11034731001; Sigma-Aldrich), 0.5 μL of 10 mM each dNTPs (#201912; QIAGEN), 0.33% (v/v) NP-40 (#28324; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 5 U SuperScript Ⅳ reverse transcriptase (#18090050; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 1× RT buffer by incubating at 23°C for 10 min, 50°C for 10 min and then 80°C for 10 min. PCR-amplified Igγ and Igκ or λ V(D)J transcripts were cloned into the human Igγ1/ Igκ (pVITRO1-dV-IgG1/κ, #52213; Addgene) or Igγ1/ Igλ-expression vector (pVITRO1-dV-IgG1/λ, #52214; Addgene)34, respectively, using the SLiCE method35. Monoclonal antibodies were expressed using the Expi293 Expression System and purified from the culture supernatants of Expi293F cells by Protein G Sepharose (#P3296; Sigma-Aldrich) or Protein G Mag Sepharose (#28-9513-79; Cytiva). Fab fragments of antibodies fused with C-terminal His-tag were expressed and purified using the Expi293 Expression System and TALON metal affinity resin.

Bead-based flow cytometric binding assay

OneComp eBeads compensation beads (#01-1111-42; Thermo Fisher Scientific) were incubated with mouse anti-human IgG (#555784; BD), followed by coating with 100 ng monoclonal antibodies (hIgG1/hIgκ or hIgλ) to be tested. After washing with PBS containing 2% FBS, particles were incubated with a PE-conjugated GM-CSF probe for 20 minutes at room temperature. The binding capacity of antibodies to the GM-CSF probe was assessed by flow cytometry and gMFI values of > 15,000 were defined as high-affinity.

Biolayer interferometry

The kinetics of mAb binding to GM-CSF were assessed using an OctetRED96e system (ForteBio) at 30°C with shaking at 1,000 rpm. Anti-human Fc (AHC) biosensors (#18-5060; Sartorius) were loaded with 10 μg/mL mAbs for 600 s and incubated with serially diluted recombinant GM-CSF (100 nM, 33 nM, 11 nM, and 0 nM) for 600 s, followed by immersion in 1 × Kinetic Buffer (0.1% BSA, 0.002% Tween20 in PBS) for 1,800 s of dissociation time. Alternatively, streptavidin biosensors were loaded with 5 μg/mL biotin-GM-CSF mutants and incubated with 33 nM Fab BD. The association rate constant (kon), the dissociation rate constant (koff), and the dissociation constant (KD) values were calculated using Octet Data Analysis software (ForteBio). For competitive binding experiments, AHC biosensors were loaded with 10 μg/mL of the first mAb (mAb1; Pt10-77 for A epitope, Pt9-2-87 for B epitope, Pt9-2-12 for C epitope, Pt9-2-38 for D epitope, Pt11-65 for E epitope, and Pt11-16 for BD epitope) for 600 s and were incubated with 100 nM GM-CSF for 300 s. The sensors were then immersed in 10 μg/mL of the second mAb (mAb2) to be assessed for 300 s.

In vitro neutralization assay

The neutralization activity of mAbs was evaluated by their ability to inhibit GM-CSF-dependent proliferation of the TF-1 cell line (#CRL-2003; ATCC) as reported previously8,13,14,19. Cells were cultured at 2,500 cells/100 μL in RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS on 96-well microplates at 37 °C in the presence of 200 pg/mL hGM-CSF and serially diluted mAbs. After 3 days, cell proliferation was quantified by a Cell Counting Kit-8 (#CK04; Dojindo Laboratories) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

ELISA

Micro-ELISA plates (#442404; Nunc MaxiSorp, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were coated with 1 μg/mL hGM-CSF. After blocking with BlockingOne reagent (#03953-95; Nacalai Tesque), the plates were incubated with serially diluted plasma or mAbs. For the measurement of high-affinity antibody concentration, the plates were then incubated with 2 M guanidine hydrochloride at room temperature for 30 min to remove low-affinity antibodies. GM-CSF-specific IgG antibodies were detected using horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-human IgG (#2044-05; Southern Biotech) and SureBlue TMB substrate (#5120-0074; KPL). The absorbance at 450 nm was measured with a microplate reader (ARVO X3, PerkinElmer).

Class 1 antibody concentrations were determined by pre-incubating plasma with mouse IgG1/κ-harboring mAbs specific for A epitope (Pt10-21) and D epitope (Pt22-26), whereas class 2 antibody concentrations were determined using mAbs specific for B epitope (Pt9-2-58) and C epitope (Pt22-14). Class-specific antibody concentrations were then calculated by subtracting the residual antibody concentration after pre-incubation from the total antibody concentration.

Cryo-EM acquisition

Recombinant human GM-CSF was mixed with two non-competing Fab fragments of anti-GM-CSF antibodies for 30 min on ice. The solution was centrifuged at 14,000 × g on a 100 kDa ultrafiltration spin column (#UFC5100; Merck) and the supernatant was collected. To prepare the cryo-EM grid for the complex of GM-CSF and two Fab fragments, 2.7 μL of the prepared sample solution was loaded onto a glow-discharge Quantifoil Cu R1.2/1.3 300 mesh and blotted for 3 s by Vitrobot IV followed by vitrification with liquid ethane. Cryo-EM movies were automatically collected using the Titan Krios equipped with a K3 BioQuantum camera using SerialEM software36. The conditions of cryo-EM acquisition were a nominal magnification: 105,000, a pixel size: 0.675 Å/pix, a total electron dose: 65 e-/Å, the corrected spherical abbreviation: 0.032 mm for the complex with BD and C Fabs and 0.054 mm for the complex with A and C Fabs.

Image processing

The image processing was performed using CryoSPARC v4.0.037. The collected movies were subjected to motion correction and contrast transfer function estimation. The preprocessed Cryo-EM images were selected by manual inspection and curation. The selected images, 9,697 images for the complex with A and BD Fabs and 3,467 images for the complex with A and C Fabs, were used for further processing. Particles initially picked using blob picking were subjected to several rounds of 2D Classification. The particles belonging to the good classes from the 2D Classification results were then used as the training dataset to create the model with Topaz, a machine-learning-based particle picker38. This model was subsequently used for the final particle picking.

For the complex of GM-CSF:A Fab:BD Fab, the picked 939,335 particles were subjected to several rounds of 2D classification to select the good particles. After obtaining the initial reference map by Ab initio reconstruction job, the selected good particles were selected by several rounds of Heterogenous refinement job. The selected 50,310 particles were subjected to Non-uniform refinement. This refinement resulted in a density map for the complex with a resolution of 4.0 Å. For the complex of GM-CSF:A Fab:C Fab, 601,878 particles were picked and subjected to 2D classification and Heterogenous refinement, the same as in the case of the complex with A and BD Fabs. The selected 189,815 particles were subjected to Homogenous refinement. The refinement provided a density map of the complex at 3.7 Å resolution.

Model building and refinement

For the complex of GM-CSF:A Fab:BD Fab, since the density map was not adequate to build the atomic model, the previously reported model of GM-CSF and A Fab (PDB: 6BFQ) was fitted into the map as a rigid body. In addition, for epitope estimation, a general structure of Fab was fitted rigidly into the density corresponding BD Fab using UCSF ChimeraX software39. To build the atomic model of the GM-CSF:A Fab:C Fab complex, the atomic model of the complex of GM-CSF and A Fab was used as the initial model (PDB: 6BFQ)21. The model was fitted into the density map as a rigid body, and then manually refined using COOT software40. The manually corrected model was refined using iSOLDE and Servalcat software41,42. The model quality was assessed by MolProbity scores43. The statistics of maps and models presented here are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. All figures were prepared using UCSF ChimeraX.

Mice

Rag2tm1.1Flv Csf2/Il3tm1.1(CSF2,IL3)Flv Il2rgtm1.1Flv/J mice were purchased from Jackson laboratory (strain #014595). Mice were bred and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions in a controlled environment (22 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 10% humidity) with a 12-h light/dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water. Experimental and control animals were housed separately. Both male and female mice were used in this study, as no clear sex-based differences were observed in our mouse model. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Research Committee of Immunology Frontier Research Center, The University of Osaka (R1-02-1) and conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the committee.

Within 48 hrs after birth, 3 × 104 human CD34+ HSPCs (#ST-70008-1; STEMCELL Techno) were injected intravenously via the superficial temporal vein. Six weeks later, the engraftment of human leukocytes was confirmed by the existence of human CD45+ mouse CD45− cells by flow cytometry. Two hundred μg of patient-derived GM-CSF mAbs was injected intravenously every 2 weeks for a total of seven injections, and bronchoalveolar lavage was performed at week 13. After mice were sacrificed by CO2 inhalation, a 22-gauge Surflo Catheter (#SR-FS2232; TERUMO) was inserted into the trachea. Then, the stylet hub was removed, and the catheter and trachea were firmly tied together with a nylon string. A 1 mL syringe containing 1 mL PBS was used to inject and aspirate the PBS 3 times, and then the syringe was removed from the catheter. Protein concentration in the recovered solution was quantified using the BCA protein assay kit (#T9300A; TAKARA).

Histopathology

Following the same treatment used for bronchoalveolar lavage, lungs were immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Image acquisition and evaluation of alveolar areas were performed using a BZ-X700 fluorescence microscope (Keyence).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Prism 11 (GraphPad). Comparisons between two groups were performed using two-tailed unpaired t-test with Welch’s correction, two-tailed Student’s t-test, or two-tailed Mann–Whitney test as appropriate. Comparisons among three or more groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
