The effect of olanzapine on diabetes related index in humans: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Ridong You, Mohammad Safargar, Kousalya Prabahar, Hamed Kord-Varkaneh, Junyi Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimOlanzapine is a widely used second-generation antipsychotic with well-established efficacy in the treatment of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and related psychiatric conditions. However, its use has been consistently linked to metabolic adverse effects, particularly disturbances in glucose regulation. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to quantitatively evaluate the effects of olanzapine on key diabetes-related biomarkers, fasting blood sugar (FBS), fasting insulin, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), in human populations, using evidence derived exclusively from randomized controlled trials (RCTs).MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase from inception to July 1, 2025, without language or date restrictions. Eligible studies were RCTs involving human participants that compared olanzapine with placebo or no treatment and reported pre- and post-intervention data for FBS, fasting insulin, or HbA1c. Study quality was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool, and the certainty of evidence was evaluated using the GRADE framework. Pooled effect estimates were calculated as weighted mean differences (WMDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using random-effects models. Subgroup, sensitivity, and publication bias analyses were performed to explore heterogeneity and assess result robustness.ResultsFifteen RCTs comprising 18 comparison arms were included in the meta-analysis. Olanzapine treatment was associated with a statistically significant increase in FBS (WMD: 2.618 mg/dL; 95% CI: 0.283 to 4.954) and fasting insulin levels (WMD: 3.636 µIU/mL; 95% CI: 1.964 to 5.307). In contrast, no significant change was observed in HbA1c levels (WMD: −0.029%; 95% CI: −0.284 to 0.226). Subgroup analyses suggested larger glycemic changes with higher doses (≥ 10 mg/day), longer treatment durations (≥ 12 weeks), and lower baseline body mass index, although these trends were not consistently statistically significant. Substantial heterogeneity was observed for FBS outcomes, whereas fasting insulin results were highly consistent across studies.ConclusionOlanzapine use is associated with modest but significant elevations in FBS and insulin levels, indicating early disturbances in glucose homeostasis and a potential risk of insulin resistance, while effects on HbA1c remain inconclusive. Clinically, this highlights the need for routine monitoring of glucose and insulin levels, early lifestyle interventions, and consideration of pharmacologic strategies in high-risk patients to prevent progression to diabetes. Introduction

Olanzapine is a second-generation atypical antipsychotic that is extensively used in the treatment of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and other severe psychiatric illnesses because of its strong effectiveness in alleviating psychotic symptoms and stabilizing mood [1]. However, alongside its clinical efficacy, growing evidence has raised concerns about its unfavorable metabolic profile, particularly its effects on glucose regulation and the increased risk of diabetes mellitus [2, 3]. These metabolic complications are of substantial clinical relevance, as they can heighten cardiovascular risk and negatively affect long-term health outcomes in individuals receiving antipsychotic medications [4–6]. Consequently, a clear understanding of how olanzapine influences key diabetes-related biomarkers, such as fasting blood sugar (FBS), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and fasting insulin concentrations, is essential for improving therapeutic decision-making and reducing metabolic harm.

The biological mechanisms responsible for olanzapine-associated metabolic disturbances are complex and involve multiple pathways. Olanzapine has been strongly linked to weight gain, increased visceral fat accumulation, and disruptions in lipid metabolism, all of which contribute to the development of insulin resistance [7–9]. Experimental evidence further indicates that olanzapine may directly impair pancreatic β-cell activity, interfere with insulin signaling cascades, and alter appetite-regulating neuropeptides, thereby facilitating hyperglycemia and compensatory elevations in insulin secretion [10]. In clinical settings, these mechanisms translate into heterogeneous effects on glycemic measures. While several studies have documented notable increases in FBS and insulin levels [11–13], others have reported modest, negligible, or inconsistent changes in HbA1c values [14, 15]. Such discrepancies likely reflect differences in study methodologies, participant characteristics, treatment dosages, and duration of exposure, underscoring the importance of systematically synthesizing the available evidence.

Although prior narrative reviews and meta-analyses have contributed valuable perspectives on antipsychotic-related metabolic effects, many are constrained by limited sample sizes, heterogeneous study designs, or the absence of pooled quantitative analyses focusing specifically on glycemic outcomes [16, 17]. To date, a comprehensive meta-analysis restricted to randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that precisely quantifies the impact of olanzapine on diabetes-related biomarkers in human populations, while accounting for variations in dosage, treatment length, and baseline metabolic profiles, remains lacking. Filling this gap is particularly important for informing clinical practice in vulnerable groups, including children and adolescents, older adults, and patients with existing metabolic abnormalities.

Against this background, the present study undertook a systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs to quantitatively assess the effects of olanzapine on core diabetes-related biomarkers in humans, specifically FBS, fasting insulin, and HbA1c. Although previous reviews have broadly addressed metabolic adverse effects associated with antipsychotic therapy, focused meta-analytic evaluation of RCT evidence examining olanzapine-induced changes in glycemic parameters is still limited. By adhering strictly to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, this study seeks to generate reliable, evidence-based estimates of olanzapine’s influence on glucose homeostasis and to explore how treatment duration, dosing strategies, and baseline metabolic status may modify these effects.

Materials and methods

This meta-analysis was conducted in adherence to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [18].

Search strategy

A systematic and exhaustive literature search was performed across multiple major electronic databases, including Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and PubMed/MEDLINE, to identify all relevant studies published up to July 1, 2025. The search strategy incorporated a combination of MeSH and non-MeSH terms and employed the following keywords: ((olanzapine[MeSH Terms]) OR (olanzapine[tiab])) AND (((((“Clinical Trials as Topic“[Mesh] OR “Cross-Over Studies“[Mesh] OR “Double-Blind Method“[Mesh] OR “Single-Blind Method“[Mesh] OR “Random Allocation“[Mesh] OR RCT[Title/Abstract] OR “Clinical Trial” [Publication Type] OR “Controlled Clinical Trials as Topic“[Mesh] OR “Intervention Studies“[Title/Abstract] OR “intervention“[Title/Abstract] OR Trial[Title/Abstract] OR “controlled trial“[Title/Abstract] OR “randomized“[Title/Abstract] OR “randomised“[Title/Abstract] OR “random“[Title/Abstract] OR “randomly“[Title/Abstract] OR “placebo“[Title/Abstract] OR “assignment“[Title/Abstract]))))) (see Supplementary Table 1 for the complete search syntax). No limitations were imposed on language or publication date to maximize the breadth and inclusiveness of the retrieved evidence. Additionally, the reference lists of all eligible articles were manually screened to identify any further studies that met the inclusion criteria.

Inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria

Study selection followed the PICOS framework: Population (P): Human participants only. Intervention (I): Studies must have evaluated Olanzapine as the primary intervention. Comparison (C): Inclusion required a comparator group receiving either placebo or no treatment. Outcome (O): Studies were required to report the mean and standard deviation (SD) for at least one component of the diabetes biomarkers, FBS; HbA1c; Insulin, both before and after the intervention. Study Design (S): Only RCTs were considered eligible. Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) were classified as editorials, letters, or review articles; (2) lacked a control group for comparison; or (3) did not report diabetes-related outcomes.

Data extraction

Key data were extracted from each included study, covering both study-level and outcome-specific variables. Extracted information included: first author’s name, total sample size, distribution of participants by group, mean age, body mass index (BMI), treatment duration, geographical location, study design, olanzapine dosage, and year of publication. Lipid outcome data, including mean and SD values for FBS; HbA1c; Insulin, were recorded, along with information on the participants’ health status. Data extraction was independently performed by two reviewers to ensure consistency and minimize bias. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and when consensus could not be reached, a senior author made the final decision.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies were evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (ROB2) tool [18, 19], This tool assesses several key domains: the randomization process, adherence to intended interventions, completeness of outcome data, accuracy of outcome measurements, and the potential for selective reporting. Based on these criteria, each study was categorized as having either a low risk of bias, some concerns, or a high risk of bias. To further assess the strength and reliability of the evidence, the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach was applied. This framework rates the certainty of evidence across studies as very low, low, moderate, or high, based on factors such as study limitations, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias [19].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 15 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Pooled effect estimates were calculated using the DerSimonian and Laird random-effects model, expressed as weighted mean differences (WMD) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) [20, 21]. where R denotes the correlation coefficient. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using Higgins’ I² statistic and the Cochran’s Q test (χ²). An I² value was interpreted as follows: Low heterogeneity: I² < 30%, Moderate heterogeneity: I² = 31%–74%, High heterogeneity: I² > 75%. Substantial heterogeneity was indicated by a p-value < 0.10 from the χ² test. Sensitivity analyses were also carried out by sequentially excluding each individual study to test the robustness and stability of the overall findings [22]. To identify potential sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were performed based on predefined study characteristics. Publication bias was assessed using both the Egger’s regression test and visual inspection of funnel plots. Where evidence of bias was present, the trim-and-fill method was employed to estimate the impact of potentially missing studies on the pooled results [23].

Results

Study selection

The study selection process followed the PRISMA guidelines and is summarized in Fig. 1. A total of 17,832 records were identified through comprehensive searches of the PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and EMBASE databases. After removal of 3,976 duplicate records, 13,856 unique articles remained and were screened based on titles and abstracts. Of these, 13,822 articles were excluded for failing to meet the predefined inclusion criteria. The full texts of 24 potentially eligible articles were retrieved and assessed in detail. Nine studies were subsequently excluded for the following reasons: inappropriate study design (n = 1), absence of a control group (n = 3), and insufficient outcome data (n = 5). Ultimately, 15 studies comprising 18 comparison arms met the eligibility criteria and were included in the meta-analysis. These included 16 arms reporting FBS, 6 arms reporting insulin outcomes, and 2 arms reporting HbA1c [24–38].

Characteristics of the included studies

The key characteristics of the included studies are presented in Table 1. A total of 18 trials were ultimately included, with sample sizes ranging from 318 to 1,583 participants. The studies were conducted across a wide range of geographic settings, including the United States, Canada, China, Iran, Japan, and India, and encompassed diverse populations such as healthy volunteers, pediatric patients with cancer, individuals with psychotic depression in remission, and patients with psychiatric disorders including schizophrenia, bipolar I disorder, borderline personality disorder, and eating disorders. The mean age of participants varied considerably, from 8.5 years in pediatric cohorts to approximately 55 years among older adults with psychotic depression. Intervention durations also differed markedly, spanning from as short as 3 days to as long as 36 weeks. When reported, baseline BMI values were generally within the normal to overweight range, ranging from 16.9 to 25.4 kg/m². Daily olanzapine doses across the included studies ranged from 10 to 20 mg. Most trials assessed one or more glycemic outcomes, including FBS, fasting insulin levels, and HbA1c, while some evaluated only a subset of these measures based on study design and clinical objectives. Collectively, the included trials provided a heterogeneous yet comprehensive body of evidence, allowing for a robust assessment of the metabolic effects of olanzapine across different age groups, clinical populations, and treatment durations. The number of participants contributing to each outcome differed because not all included trials measured or reported fasting insulin and HbA1c. FBS is routinely collected as part of standard safety monitoring in olanzapine trials, whereas insulin and HbA1c are less consistently assessed, particularly in short-term studies, and in several trials quantitative data (mean ± SD) were unavailable for pooling. A detailed evaluation of risk of bias, conducted using the Cochrane Risk of Bias framework, is presented in Supplementary Table 2. The overall certainty of evidence assessed using the GRADE framework is presented in Supplementary Table 3. Evidence certainty was rated as moderate for FBS and fasting insulin, and low for HbA1c, primarily due to inconsistency and imprecision.

Meta-analysis findings

Effect of olanzapine on FBS

A pooled analysis of 14 study arms involving 1,583 participants (711 in the placebo group and 891 treated with olanzapine) demonstrated a statistically significant increase in FBS levels associated with olanzapine use (weighted mean difference: 2.618 mg/dL; 95% CI: 0.283 to 4.954; P = 0.028). Notably, substantial heterogeneity was observed among the included studies (I² = 73.5%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Subgroup analyses suggested a dose-related trend. Higher olanzapine doses (≥ 10 mg/day) were associated with a greater, though not statistically significant, increase in FBS levels (WMD: 2.995 mg/dL; 95% CI: −0.315 to 6.305; P = 0.076), whereas lower doses (< 10 mg/day) did not result in a statistically significant change (WMD: 1.739 mg/dL; 95% CI: −0.279 to 3.756; P = 0.091).

Forest plot of the randomized controlled trials investigating the effects of olanzapine on FBS. WMD, weighted mean difference. CI, confidence intervals. FBS, Fasting Blood Sugar

With respect to treatment duration, shorter interventions (< 12 weeks) were linked to a non-significant increase in FBS (WMD: 1.928 mg/dL; 95% CI: −1.869 to 5.724; P = 0.320), while longer durations (≥ 12 weeks) showed a larger, yet still non-significant, increase (WMD: 2.815 mg/dL; 95% CI: −0.257 to 5.888; P = 0.073).

Baseline BMI also appeared to influence outcomes. Participants with a BMI < 25 kg/m² experienced a greater, though not statistically significant, rise in FBS levels (WMD: 3.739 mg/dL; 95% CI: −1.139 to 8.617; P = 0.133) compared with those with a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² (WMD: 1.351 mg/dL; 95% CI: −1.730 to 4.431; P = 0.390) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Effect of olanzapine on fasting insulin

A meta-analysis of five study arms including 359 participants (183 in the placebo group and 206 receiving olanzapine) demonstrated a modest yet statistically significant increase in fasting insulin levels associated with olanzapine treatment (WMD: 3.636 µIU/mL; 95% CI: 1.964 to 5.307; P < 0.001). No significant heterogeneity was observed across the included studies (I² = 0.0%, P = 0.464) (Fig. 3).

Effect of olanzapine on HbA1c

A pooled analysis of two study arms involving 318 participants (127 in the placebo group and 191 receiving olanzapine) showed no statistically significant change in HbA1c levels (WMD: −0.029%; 95% CI: −0.284 to 0.226; P = 0.825). This analysis was characterized by substantial between-study heterogeneity (I² = 67.6%, P = 0.079) and was based on a very limited number of studies, thereby substantially limiting the precision and reliability of the pooled estimate (Fig. 4).

Sensitivity analyses and publication bias

Sensitivity analyses, performed by sequentially excluding individual studies, confirmed the robustness of the findings, as the pooled effect estimates remained largely unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 2). Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s test, with no evidence of bias detected for FBS, fasting insulin, or HbA1c outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Discussion

This meta-analysis offers a thorough assessment of the effects of olanzapine on major diabetes-related biomarkers, including FBS, fasting insulin, and HbA1c, across a wide range of human populations. The pooled results indicate that olanzapine treatment is associated with small yet statistically significant increases in FBS and fasting insulin concentrations, whereas no significant change was observed in HbA1c levels. These findings are consistent with accumulating evidence that olanzapine directly influences glucose metabolism, beyond its well-documented propensity to induce weight gain [39, 40].

Importantly, these results should be interpreted in the context of the elevated baseline metabolic risk inherent to patients with schizophrenia and bipolar disorder. Independent of antipsychotic treatment, individuals with severe mental illness exhibit higher rates of insulin resistance, impaired glucose tolerance, and cardiometabolic disease, driven by a complex interplay of genetic susceptibility, neuroendocrine dysregulation, lifestyle factors, and illness-related behaviors [41]. Consequently, the metabolic alterations observed in this meta-analysis likely reflect an interaction between olanzapine’s pharmacological effects and an already vulnerable metabolic milieu, rather than metabolic dysfunction attributable solely to medication exposure.

The observed elevation in FBS (WMD: 2.618 mg/dL) supports the recognized diabetogenic potential of olanzapine and reinforces the notion that its metabolic effects are not solely mediated by increased body weight [42–44]. From a mechanistic perspective, olanzapine has been shown to disrupt insulin signaling pathways, impair glucose uptake in peripheral tissues, and diminish pancreatic β-cell function, thereby contributing to elevated blood glucose levels [45–48]. The parallel increase in fasting insulin (WMD: 3.636 µIU/mL) points to compensatory hyperinsulinemia, suggesting the early development of insulin resistance. This metabolic pattern implies that olanzapine may place stress on glucose regulatory systems, leading to increased insulin secretion in an attempt to preserve normoglycemia, particularly during short- to intermediate-term exposure [49, 50].

Although the absolute increase in FBS observed in this meta-analysis was modest (WMD: 2.618 mg/dL), its clinical relevance should be interpreted in context. In isolation, a change of this magnitude is unlikely to trigger diagnostic reclassification or immediate clinical intervention in otherwise metabolically healthy individuals. However, even small upward shifts in FBS may be clinically meaningful in populations already at elevated metabolic risk, such as patients with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder, who have a higher baseline prevalence of insulin resistance, impaired glucose tolerance, and cardiometabolic disease. In such individuals, incremental increases in FBS may contribute to progression toward prediabetes or diabetes over time, particularly with prolonged exposure, higher doses, or concomitant weight gain. Similarly, the pooled effect on HbA1c (WMD: −0.029%) was negligible, reflecting the short duration of most included trials and the delayed responsiveness of HbA1c to early glycemic changes. Together, these findings suggest that early disturbances in glucose regulation, indicated by FBS and fasting insulin elevations, may precede detectable changes in HbA1c, highlighting the importance of proactive and regular metabolic monitoring. From a clinical perspective, these findings support current guideline recommendations advocating early and ongoing assessment of glycemic parameters in patients treated with olanzapine, even when short-term changes appear quantitatively modest.

These findings are broadly consistent with prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses evaluating metabolic adverse effects across antipsychotic agents. Comparative analyses have consistently demonstrated that second-generation antipsychotics differ substantially in their metabolic liability, with olanzapine, alongside clozapine, associated with a higher risk of weight gain, insulin resistance, and dysglycemia compared with agents such as risperidone, quetiapine, ziprasidone, or aripiprazole. In this context, the magnitude and direction of changes observed in FBS and insulin in the present meta-analysis align with the established classification of olanzapine as a metabolically high-risk antipsychotic. These findings further reinforce evidence that its effects on glucose regulation extend beyond weight-mediated mechanisms alone [51]. A recent meta-analysis of observational studies reported that olanzapine is associated with a risk of new-onset diabetes that is either lower than or comparable to that of clozapine [52]. However, the overall certainty of this evidence is limited, because most included studies relied on indirect comparisons and observational data, which are susceptible to confounding factors. Nonetheless, these findings support the broader literature indicating that among second-generation antipsychotics, olanzapine and clozapine are associated with the highest metabolic risk, while other agents, such as risperidone, quetiapine, and aripiprazole, generally exhibit a lower propensity for glucose dysregulation.

While several antipsychotics have been associated with modest alterations in glucose metabolism, olanzapine appears to exert a comparatively more pronounced effect on insulin sensitivity and diabetes risk than many other second-generation agents, a pattern reflected in network meta-analyses showing greater glucose increases with olanzapine compared with ziprasidone, lurasidone, and risperidone, and in population-based studies demonstrating stronger associations with incident type 2 diabetes for olanzapine than for many other SGAs. [53].

Subgroup analyses identified meaningful trends related to dosage, duration of treatment, and baseline BMI. Greater increases in FBS were observed among individuals receiving higher doses of olanzapine (≥ 10 mg/day) and those undergoing longer treatment periods (≥ 12 weeks), although these effects did not consistently reach statistical significance. These findings suggest a possible dose- and time-dependent influence on glycemic control. Moreover, participants with lower baseline BMI (< 25 kg/m²) exhibited comparatively larger increases in FBS than those with higher BMI, potentially reflecting distinct metabolic adaptations between lean and overweight individuals [54]. Together, these observations highlight the heterogeneity of metabolic responses to olanzapine and underscore the importance of individualized risk assessment.

The absence of a statistically significant change in HbA1c should be interpreted with particular caution. Only two study arms contributed to the HbA1c analysis, resulting in a small and methodologically limited evidence base with substantial heterogeneity. Given that HbA1c reflects average glycemic exposure over approximately two to three months, the relatively short duration of several included trials, combined with heterogeneous study populations and clinical contexts, likely reduced the sensitivity of this outcome to detect olanzapine-related effects. Consequently, the pooled HbA1c estimate is subject to considerable uncertainty and should not be interpreted as evidence that olanzapine has no effect on longer-term glycemic control. Rather, the findings suggest that early metabolic disturbances induced by olanzapine, such as elevations in FBS and insulin, may precede detectable changes in HbA1c, particularly in short- to intermediate-term studies [15, 55].

The substantial heterogeneity observed in FBS outcomes (I² = 73.5%) reflects the influence of diverse study characteristics, including differences in treatment duration, dosing regimens, psychiatric diagnoses, and baseline metabolic profiles. By contrast, fasting insulin outcomes demonstrated no heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%), indicating a more uniform insulin response to olanzapine across study populations. This contrast suggests that while the degree of glucose elevation may vary, the development of compensatory hyperinsulinemia is a consistent pharmacological effect of olanzapine.

Overall, the findings of this meta-analysis provide compelling evidence that olanzapine induces quantifiable disturbances in glucose regulation and insulin dynamics. The combination of elevated FBS and insulin levels is indicative of early insulin resistance and supports the view that olanzapine’s metabolic effects arise from both direct pharmacologic actions and indirect pathways involving weight gain and adiposity [50, 56, 57]. Recognizing and characterizing these effects is essential for accurately interpreting metabolic changes during antipsychotic treatment and for guiding future investigations into preventive and therapeutic strategies.

Clinical implications

Healthcare providers prescribing olanzapine should be cognizant of its capacity to disrupt glucose metabolism, particularly in patients with pre-existing metabolic risk factors. The observed increases in FBS and compensatory hyperinsulinemia suggest that insulin resistance may emerge early in treatment, often preceding detectable changes in HbA1c. Accordingly, regular metabolic monitoring, including FBS and, where feasible, fasting insulin, may be particularly informative during the initial phases of treatment and following dose escalation.

Preventive strategies play a central role in mitigating olanzapine-associated metabolic risk. Lifestyle interventions focusing on dietary counseling, physical activity, and weight management initiated at the onset of treatment may help attenuate early metabolic changes. Individualized risk assessment based on baseline BMI, family history of diabetes, and anticipated treatment duration may further guide the intensity of monitoring and preventive efforts.

In patients who exhibit early signs of insulin resistance or progressive metabolic deterioration, pharmacological interventions may be considered. Adjunctive metformin has demonstrated efficacy in improving insulin sensitivity and reducing antipsychotic-associated weight gain and may be particularly beneficial in high-risk individuals [58]. While switching to antipsychotic agents with a lower metabolic risk profile may be appropriate for some patients, this decision must be carefully balanced against psychiatric stability. Emerging therapies, such as glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, show promise but require further validation before routine clinical use. Overall, prompt identification and proactive management of metabolic abnormalities may reduce the risk of progression to type 2 diabetes and long-term cardiovascular complications, thereby optimizing both metabolic and psychiatric outcomes.

Strengths

This study has several notable strengths. First, the exclusive inclusion of RCTs reduces the risk of bias inherent in observational research. Second, the extensive search strategy across multiple databases, without restrictions on language or publication date, improves the representativeness and completeness of the evidence base. Third, the application of rigorous statistical approaches—including random-effects modeling, sensitivity analyses, and evaluation of publication bias—enhances the robustness of the findings. Finally, subgroup analyses offer clinically meaningful insights into how dosage, treatment duration, and baseline BMI may influence glycemic outcomes.

Limitations

Despite the strengths of this analysis, several limitations should be acknowledged. Considerable heterogeneity in FBS outcomes likely reflects variations in study populations, psychiatric diagnoses, and trial methodologies. The relatively small number of studies reporting HbA1c and fasting insulin limited the precision of these pooled estimates. Treatment durations varied substantially, and many trials were of short duration, which may have led to underestimation of long-term metabolic effects. Furthermore, limited information on potential confounders, such as dietary intake, physical activity, and concomitant medications, may have influenced glycemic outcomes. Although trends suggest dose- and duration-related effects, the available data do not allow for definitive causal inferences or clear recommendations regarding optimal monitoring intervals. In addition, the small number of trials reporting fasting insulin and HbA1c limits the power of funnel plots and Egger’s regression to detect publication bias, and the absence of observed bias for these outcomes should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of no small-study effects.

Conclusion

Olanzapine treatment is associated with significant increases in FBS and fasting insulin levels, suggesting an elevated risk of insulin resistance, while effects on HbA1c remain uncertain. These results emphasize the importance of proactive metabolic surveillance and individualized risk assessment in patients receiving olanzapine, particularly those predisposed to diabetes. Clinicians must carefully weigh the therapeutic advantages of olanzapine against its metabolic liabilities and implement early interventions, including lifestyle modification or pharmacologic measures, when appropriate. Future well-designed, long-term RCTs are needed to clarify the mechanisms driving olanzapine-related metabolic dysfunction, define dose–response relationships, and identify effective strategies to mitigate these adverse effects. Integrating systematic metabolic monitoring into routine care may help optimize the safety and effectiveness of olanzapine therapy, ultimately improving both mental health outcomes and overall patient well-being.
