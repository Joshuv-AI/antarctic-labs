Weak and Strong Fibrations of Functors and Motion Planning
Isaac Carcacía-Campos, Enrique Macías-Virgós, David Mosquera-Lois

Abstract
. We develop a homotopical framework for small categories that extends classical invariants of algebraic topology to the categorical setting. Our approach is based on the construction of a genuine path category, obtained through a zig–zag and localization procedure, which allows us to define strong and weak fibrations for functors. We establish their basic properties, introduce a fibrant replacement for functors, and extend homotopical invariants such as the Švarc genus and sectional category to small categories. Finally, we apply this framework to motion planning in small categories, providing categorical analogues of Farber’s topological complexity while removing finiteness constraints typical of existing approaches.

Introduction

The study of homotopical invariants has played a central role in algebraic topology since its origins.
Classical notions such as the Lusternik–Schnirelmann category (LS-category)
[
7
]
, topological complexity
[
3
]
, the Švarc genus and sectional category
[
12
]
, and the more recent notion of homotopic distance
[
10
]
, provide quantitative tools for measuring the complexity of spaces and maps.
These invariants are particularly relevant in applications such as motion planning in robotics, where their values for a configuration space determine the intrinsic difficulty of designing a continuous motion planning algorithm
[
8
]
.

In recent years, several authors have explored categorical analogues of these invariants.
Tanaka studied LS-category for small categories
[
13
]
.
Subsequently, the two last authors of present paper introduced the notions of topological complexity and homotopic distance for functors
[
9
]
.
More recently, these ideas have been extended and refined in various directions
[
2
,
1
]
.

All these works build on Lee’s approach to homotopies in categories
[
6
]
and on Grothendieck’s theory of fibrations
[
14
]
.
Nevertheless, these approaches do not fully capture the homotopical behaviour required in the categorical setting, which limits the study of motion planning invariants for small categories.

The aim of this paper is to overcome these limitations by developing a systematic homotopical framework for small categories, extending classical invariants to this combinatorial setting.
Our starting point is Hoff’s approach to homotopy theory in categories
[
5
]
, based on the construction of a
path category
.
However, Hoff’s construction proves too rigid for our purposes.
This motivates our definition of a novel genuine
path category
associated with a small category, obtained via a zig–zag construction followed by a localization procedure.
This provides a flexible and well-behaved model for categorical paths, which in turn allows us to introduce appropriate notions of fibration.

The main contributions of this work may be summarized as follows:

•

We introduce a path category
\mathrm{P}\mathcal{C}
for any small category
\mathcal{C}
, together with an endpoint functor that plays the role of the classical path fibration (Theorem
4.1
).

•

We define notions of
strong
and
weak
fibrations for functors, inspired by the classical lifting properties in topology, and establish their first properties, including relations between them (Theorem
2.8
), and their behaviour under pullbacks and composition.

•

We construct the
fibrant replacement
of a functor (Theorem
3.1
), showing that any functor factors as a weak homotopy equivalence followed by a fibration.

•

Using these tools, we extend classical invariants such as the Švarc genus, sectional category, LS-category, categorical complexity, and homotopic distance to the setting of small categories, introducing both strong and weak versions.

•

Finally, we apply this framework to
motion planning in small categories
, obtaining categorical analogues of Farber’s topological complexity and proving relations among the different invariants (Theorem
4.10
).

Furthermore, by working in the setting of small categories we are able to overcome the finiteness condition that is required for fibrations in the simplicial context
[
4
]
.
This yields a more flexible and general theory, extending the scope of homotopical invariants beyond the restrictions of the simplicial framework.

1.1.
Definition of the path category

For each natural number

0
to

if

if

is odd.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category. A

is a functor
I:\mathbb{I}_{m}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
. For a path of length
m

I:\mathbb{I}_{m}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
we refer to the object
I(0)
as the initial point or object of the path
I
and to
I(m)
as the end point or object of the path. As an abuse of notation, we use the term

to not specify the length. A category
\mathcal{C}
is
connected
if for every pair of objects
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
there is path such that the initial object is
\mathcal{C}
and the end point is
\mathcal{D}
.

We define

as the category whose objects are paths and a morphism between two paths
I\colon\mathbb{I}_{m}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
and
J\colon\mathbb{I}_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
is a pair
(F,\alpha)\colon I\rightarrow J
where

•

F\colon\mathbb{I}_{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{I}_{n}
is a functor such that
F(0)=0
and
F(m)=n

•

and
\alpha
is a natural transformation
\alpha\colon I\implies J\circ F
.

The category

has (finite) paths as objects. However, it has some issues that we need to fix. For example, two constant paths with the same constant value but different length are different, but from a homotopic point of view, they must be the same since one may be seen as a reparameterization of the other. We solve this difficulty by localizing.

We define the set
W\subset\mathrm{Mor}(\mathcal{P}\mathcal{C})
as the set of all morphisms between paths such that the natural transformation is the identity, i.e. where
I=J\circ F
and therefore the morphism has the form
(F,1_{I})
. With this we can define our path category using localization
[
11
]
.

1.2.
Functoriality of the path construction

In this subsection we establish that the path construction behaves functorially. This will allow us to treat the passage from a category to its path category as a genuine functor between categories, and to understand how path categories interact with categorical products.

Lemma 1.3
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a small category, then its path category
\mathrm{P}\mathcal{C}
is also small.

Proof.

We begin by proving that
\mathrm{P}\mathcal{C}
is a small category. Recall that the objects of
\mathrm{P}\mathcal{C}
are functors
\mathbb{I}_{m}\to C
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Let us begin by fixing
m
. Giving a functor
\mathbb{I}_{m}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
amounts to choosing a finite tuple of objects and a finite tuple of morphisms from the sets
\mathrm{Ob}(C)
and
\mathrm{Mor}(C)
(respectively). Therefore, for each
m
, the collection

\mathrm{Fun}(\mathbb{I}_{m},\mathcal{C})
of functors is a set.
Finally, the class of all paths is obtained as a countable union

\mathrm{Ob}(\mathrm{P}\mathcal{C})\;=\;\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\mathrm{Fun}(\mathbb{I}_{m},\mathcal{C}),

which is still a set. Hence the objects of
\mathrm{P}\mathcal{C}
form a set.

Morphisms in
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{C}
are pairs
(F,\alpha)
, where
F:I_{m}\to I_{n}
is a
functor between finite zig–zag categories and
\alpha:I\Rightarrow J\circ F

is a natural transformation. These data are again specified by finitely many
objects and morphisms of
C
, so
\mathrm{Mor}(PC)
is a set. Thus
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{C}
is small.

Finally, the localization
\mathrm{P}\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{P}\mathcal{C}[W^{-1}]
has the same set of
objects as
\mathrm{P}\mathcal{C}
, and morphisms are represented by finite zig–zags of arrows in

PC
with arrows in
W
reversed. Since
\mathrm{P}\mathcal{C}
is small, the collection of such
zig–zags is also a set. Therefore
\mathrm{P}\mathcal{C}
is small.
∎

Proposition 1.4
.

There is a functor
\mathrm{P}\colon\textbf{cat}\rightarrow\textbf{cat}
that takes a category
\mathcal{C}
to the category
\mathrm{P}\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

Suppose that we have a functor
G\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
we have to define a functor
\mathrm{P}F\colon\mathrm{P}\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathrm{P}\mathcal{D}
. Take a path
I\colon\mathbb{I}_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
we have an object
\mathrm{P}G(I)=G\circ I
. Now suppose that you have a morphism of paths in
\mathcal{C}
given by
(F,\alpha)\colon I_{1}\rightarrow I_{2}
we have a morphism of paths in
\mathcal{D}
given by
(F,1_{G}\alpha)
where
1_{G}\alpha
denotes the horizontal composition of natural transformations. This defines a functor between the localizing categories since every morphism in
W_{\mathcal{C}}
is of the form
(F,1_{I})
and it goes to an element
(F,1_{G}1_{I})=(F,1_{G\circ I})\in W_{\mathcal{D}}
.
∎

To simplify the treatment of the product of categories later, we state the following lemma, which follows from the properties of localization of categories.

Lemma 1.5
.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
be two categories. There is an equivalence between
\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{B}_{1}\times\mathcal{B}_{2})
and
\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{B}_{1})\times\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{B}_{2})
.

The path fibration and auxiliary lemmas

From the construction of the path category we can also build two functors that relate the path category with the original one.

Let
\mathcal{C}

2.
Fibrations

2.1.
Homotopy between functors

We introduce two notions of homotopy between functors. We begin by recalling the notion of homotopy (we refer to it as
strong homotopy
) in small categories introduced by Lee (
[
6
]
). Then we introduce a novel and weaker notion of homotopy (we refer to it as
weak homotopy
) inspired on the one introduced by Hoff
[
5
]
but with the difference that we work with a different category of paths and explained in Example
1.2
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
F,G\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
be two functors between small categories. We say that
F
and
G
are
strong homotopic
, denoted by
F\simeq G
, if, for some
m\geq 0
, there exists a functor
H\colon\mathcal{C}\times\mathbb{I}_{m}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
, called a homotopy (of length
m
), such that
H(-,0)=F
and
H(-,m)=G
. We will denote the fact that two functors
F
and
G
are strong homotopic with
F\simeq G
.

Strong homotopy between functors proves to be too rigid for developing motion planning in small categories since with this we do not have the usual equivalence between homotopic sections to
\Delta\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
and sections to the path fibration
\pi\colon\mathrm{P}\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
. That is why we introduce weak homotopies in the spirit of
[
5
]
and
[
4
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
F,G\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
be two functors between small categories. The functors
F
and
G
are weak homotopic, denoted
F\simeq_{w}G
, (we also say there is a weak homotopy between them) if there exists a functor
H\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathrm{P}\mathcal{D}
such that
\pi_{0}\circ H=F
and
\pi_{1}\circ H=G
.

Observe that being strong or weak homotopic defines an equivalence relation among functors.

Definition 2.3
.

Two small categories
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
are
strong
(respectively
weak
) homotopy equivalent or
strong
(respectively
weak
) homotopic if there are exist functors
F\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
and
G\colon\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
such that
G\circ F\simeq\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{C}}
and
F\circ G\simeq\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{D}}
(respectively
G\circ F\simeq_{w}\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{C}}
and
F\circ G\simeq_{w}\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{D}}
). The small category
\mathcal{C}
is
contractible
(respectively
weak contractible
) if it is strong homotopic (respectively weak homotopic) to the trivial category
\bullet
with only one object and one morphism.

Proposition 2.4
.

(1)

If two functors are strong homotopic then they are also weak homotopic.

(2)

As a consequence, if two categories are homotopic then they are also weak homotopic.

Proof.

Suppose that
F,G\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
are strong homotopic and
H\colon\mathcal{C}\times\mathbb{I}_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
is a homotopy between then. We define the weak homotopy
H^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathrm{P}\mathcal{D}
defined as
H^{\prime}(c)=H(c,-)
.
∎

The converse claim does not hold in general, as the following example shows.

.

Let us consider the category

Proposition 2.6
.

Let

be two weak homotopic functors between small categories. If
\mathcal{C}
is finite (the set of morphisms is finite), then

and

are strong homotopic.

Proof.

Suppose that we have a weak homotopy

such that
\pi_{0}\circ H=F
and
\pi_{1}\circ H=G
. We have that since
\mathcal{C}
have a finite number of objects and morphism there is maximum lenght
m
for the paths

and the intermediate path that appears in the morphism between them. We can then define a strong homotopy by taking

to be a functor defined by taking some adequate equivalent paths for each object
c
in
\mathcal{C}
.
∎

Let

(1)

(2)

given by

is the natural transformation appearing in

and such that

for every onject

in
\mathcal{C}
.

For each object

For each

We now define a functor

are generated by those of the form

with

\mathbb{I}_{M}

•

For

we put

.

•

Let

\mathcal{C}

\mathrm{P}\mathcal{B}
, the arrow

3.1.

A central technique in homotopical settings is to replace a given object by a fibrant one. Here we adapt this idea to functors between small categories, constructing a factorization that provides a weak homotopy equivalence followed by a fibration.

n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that there is a geometric cover
\{\mathcal{U}_{0},...,\mathcal{U}_{n}\}
of
\mathcal{C}
verifying that for every
i\in\{0,...,n\}
the inclusion
\iota_{i}\colon\mathcal{U}_{i}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
is weak (resp. strong) homotopic to a constant functor.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category. We define the diagonal functor
\Delta\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
as the functor that takes each object
c
to
\Delta(c)=(c,c)
and each morphism
f
to
\Delta(f)=(f,f)
.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a a small category. A subcategory
\mathcal{U}
of
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
is a
weak (resp. strong) Farber subcategory
if there is a functor
F\colon\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
such that
\Delta\circ F
is weak (resp. strong) homotopic to
\iota
where
\iota\colon\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
is the inclusion.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category, the
weak (resp. strong) categorical complexity
of
\mathcal{C}
, denoted by
\mathop{\mathrm{cTC}}(\mathcal{C})
(resp.
\mathop{\mathrm{cTC}}_{w}(\mathcal{C})
), is the least non-negative integer
n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that there is a geometric cover
\{\mathcal{U}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{U}_{n}\}
of
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
by Farber subcategories.

Definition 4.6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
be two small categories and let
F,G\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
be two functors. The
weak (resp. strong) homotopic distance

\mathrm{cD}(F,G)
(
\mathrm{cD}_{w}(F,G)
) between
F
and
G
is the least non-negative integer
n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that there is a geometric cover
\{\mathcal{U}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{U}_{n}\}
such that
F|_{\mathcal{U}_{i}}\simeq_{w}G|_{\mathcal{U}_{i}}
(
F|_{\mathcal{U}_{i}}\simeq G|_{\mathcal{U}_{i}}
)for every
0\leq i\leq n
.

The last two authors of the present paper proved (see
[
9
]
):

(1)

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a connected category and
c\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
be a constant functor. Then

\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}(\mathcal{C})=\mathrm{cD}(c,1_{\mathcal{C}})

where
1_{\mathcal{C}}
is the identity.

(2)

The strong categorical complexity of a small category
\mathcal{C}
is the strong homotopic distance between the two projections
p_{1},p_{2}\colon\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
, that is,
\mathop{\mathrm{cTC}}(C)=\mathrm{cD}(p_{1},p_{2}).

The analogous result for the weak Lusternik-Schnirelmann category follows immediately from the definition:

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a connected category and
c\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
be the constant functor with value the object
c
in
\mathcal{C}
. We have that:

\mathrm{ccat}_{w}(\mathcal{C})=\mathrm{cD}_{w}(c,1_{\mathcal{C}})

where
1_{\mathcal{C}}
is the identity.

We prove the analogous result for the weak categorical complexity.

Proposition 4.8
.

The weak categorical complexity of
\mathcal{C}
is the weak homotopic distance between the two projections
p_{1},p_{2}\colon\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
, that is,

\mathrm{cTC}_{w}(C)=\mathrm{cD}_{w}(p_{1},p_{2}).

Proof.

Take a Farber subcategory
\mathcal{U}
with inclusion
\iota\colon\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
. Since
\mathcal{U}
is Farber there is a functor
F\colon\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
and a weak homotopy
H\colon\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathrm{P}(\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C})
between
\iota
and
\Delta\circ F
, i.e.
\pi_{0}\circ H=\iota
and
\pi_{1}\circ H=\Delta\circ F
. We have to define a weak homotopy
H^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathrm{P}\mathcal{C}
between
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, which is the same as giving a functorial form of assigning a path between
c
and
d
for any object
(c,d)
in
\mathcal{U}
.

For each object
(c,d)
in
\mathcal{U}
we have that
H(c)
is a path between
(c,d)
and
\Delta\circ F(c,d)=(F(c,d),F(c,d))
and this is the same as a path from
I_{c}\colon\mathbb{I}_{m}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
from
c
to
F(c,d)
and a path
J_{D}\colon\mathbb{I}_{m}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
from
d
to
F(c,d)
. We can take the composition of
I
with the inverse of
J
to obtain the wanted path. Moreover, this is functorial since for each morphism
(f,g)\colon(c,d)\rightarrow(a,b)
we can define a morphism between them using the fact that there is a path morphism
H(f,g)\colon H(c,d)\rightarrow H(a,b)
with means that there a morphism between
I_{c}
and
I_{a}
and between
J_{d}
and
J_{b}
that induces our wanted morphism between
H^{\prime}(c,d)
and
H^{\prime}(a,b)
.

Now take a subcategory
\mathcal{U}
with a weak homotopy
H\colon\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathrm{P}\mathcal{C}
between
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
. We can show that
\mathcal{U}
is a Farber subcategory with the functor
F\colon\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
given by
p_{1}
, i.e.
\iota\simeq_{w}\Delta\circ p_{1}
. A homotopy
H^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{U}\rightarrow
between
\iota
and
\Delta\circ p_{1}
is a functorial assignment that takes any object
(c,d)
to a path between
(c,d)
and
\Delta\circ p_{1}(c,d)=(c,c)
. This can be done by using the fact that a path in
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
is the same as two paths in
\mathcal{C}
. Hence we can take the path between
c
and
d
defined by
H(c,d)
and the constant path between
c
and
c
with the same length as
H(c,d)
. This is functorial since
H
is a functor.
∎

Remark 2
.

Observe that as a consequence of Proposition
2.4
and Proposition
2.6
, for each pair of functors
F,G\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
,
\mathrm{cD}_{w}(F,G)\leq\mathrm{cD}(F,G).
Therefore,
\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}_{w}(\mathcal{C})\leq\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}(\mathcal{C})
and
\mathop{\mathrm{cTC}}_{w}(\mathcal{C})\leq\mathop{\mathrm{cTC}}(\mathcal{C})
. Furthermore, all inequalities are equalities if
\mathcal{C}
is finite by Proposition
2.6
.

4.3.
Relations between Svarc Genus, sectional category and other homotopy invariants

We prove a result, which may be seen as a counterpart of
[
10
, Theorem 2.8]
in the context of small categories, relating the previous homotopic invariants.

Proposition 4.9
.

For every pullback of small categories:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}

we have that:

\mathrm{secat}(P^{\prime})\leq\mathrm{secat}(P).

In particular, if
P
is a weak (resp. strong) fibration, then

\mathrm{SG}_{w}(P^{\prime})\leq\mathrm{SG}_{w}(P)
(resp.
\mathrm{SG}_{s}(P)\leq\mathrm{SG}_{s}(P^{\prime})
).

Proof.

Suppose that we have a geometric cover
\{\mathcal{U}_{0},\dots\mathcal{U}_{n}\}
of
\mathcal{B}
with local sections
s_{i}\colon\mathcal{U}_{i}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}
. For each
0\leq i\leq n
, we define
G^{-1}(\mathcal{U}_{i})
as the subcategory of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
with objects
b
and morphisms
f
in
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
such that
G(b)
and
G(f)
are objects and morphisms in
\mathcal{U}_{i}
. Then
\{G^{-1}(\mathcal{U}_{0}),\dots,G^{-1}(\mathcal{U}_{n})\}
is a geometric cover of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
.

We define local sections
s^{\prime}_{i}\colon G^{-1}(\mathcal{U}_{i})\rightarrow\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
as
s_{i}^{\prime}(b)=(b,s_{i}(G(b))
. This is well defined since
P(s(G(b)))=G(b)
and it is clearly a local section using the fact that
P^{\prime}(b,s_{i}(G(b))=b
.

If
P
is a weak (strong) fibration then
P^{\prime}
is also a weak (strong) fibration (Proposition
2.9
) and
\mathrm{secat}(P)=\mathrm{SG}_{w}(P)(\mathrm{resp.}\,\mathrm{secat}(P)=\mathrm{SG}(P))
and
\mathrm{secat}(P^{\prime})=\mathrm{SG}_{w}(P^{\prime})(\mathrm{resp.}\,\mathrm{secat}(P^{\prime})=\mathrm{SG}_{(}P^{\prime}))
(Proposition
3.6
). ∎

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
F,G\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
be two funtors and let
\pi\colon\mathrm{P}\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}
be the path fibration. Consider the pullback:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{P}\mathcal{D}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(F,G)}

Then,
\mathrm{cD}_{w}(F,G)=\mathrm{secat}(q)
and
\mathrm{secat}(q)\leq\mathrm{cD}(F,G)
. Moreover, if
\mathcal{C}
is finite,
\mathrm{secat}(q)=\mathrm{cD}(F,G).

Proof.

We begin by proving that
\mathrm{secat}(q)=\mathrm{cD}_{w}(F,G)
. First,
\mathrm{secat}(q)=\mathrm{SG}_{w}(q)=\mathrm{SG}(q)
by Proposition
2.9
, Proposition
3.6
and Theorem
4.1
. Hence, it is enough to prove the inequalities
\mathrm{secat}_{w}(q)\leq\mathrm{cD}_{w}(F,G)
and
\mathrm{cD}_{w}(F,G)\leq\mathrm{SG}(q)
.

We begin by proving the first inequality. Suppose that we have a geometric cover
\{U_{0},\dots,U_{n}\}
such that
F|_{\mathcal{U}_{i}}\simeq_{w}G|_{\mathcal{U}_{i}}
for every
0\leq i\leq n
. We define the local sections
s_{i}\colon\mathcal{U}_{i}\rightarrow P
as:

s_{i}(c)=(c,H(c))

where
H\colon\mathcal{U}_{i}\rightarrow\mathrm{P}\mathcal{D}
is the weak homotopy between
F|_{\mathcal{U}_{i}}
and
G|_{\mathcal{U}_{i}}
.

Conversely, suppose that we have a geometric cover
\{\mathcal{U}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{U}_{n}\}
with local sections
s_{i}\colon U_{i}\rightarrow P
for every
0\leq i\leq n
. Take
i
such that
0\leq i\leq n
we must define a homotopy between
F|_{\mathcal{U}_{i}}
and
G|_{\mathcal{U}_{i}}
but this can easily be done usign
s_{i}
. Indeed, we define
H
as
p\circ s_{i}
. We claim that this give us the wanted homotopy since we have that
\pi\circ H=\pi\circ p\circ s_{i}=(F,G)\circ q\circ s_{i}=(F,G)\circ\iota_{i}
. If we compose with the natural projections
p_{1},p_{2}\colon\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
we have that
\pi_{j}\circ H=p_{j}\circ\pi\circ H=p_{j}\circ(F,G)\circ\iota_{i}
that equals
F|_{\mathcal{U}_{i}}
if
j=1
and
G|_{\mathcal{U}_{i}}
if
j=2
.

The last inequality and the equality under finiteness hypothesis follows from Remark
2
.
∎

Corollary 4.11
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small category and
\pi\colon\mathrm{P}\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
be the path fibration. Then

\mathrm{cTC}_{w}(\mathcal{C})=\mathrm{secat}(\pi)
and
\mathrm{secat}(\pi)\leq\mathrm{cTC}(\mathcal{C})
with a equality if
\mathcal{C}
is finite.

Proof.

The morphism
(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})\colon\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
is the identity of
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
. So we have that

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{P}\mathcal{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{P}\mathcal{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1_{\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}}}

and therefore applying Proposition
4.10
and Proposition
4.8
we obtain
\mathrm{cTC}_{w}(\mathcal{C})=\mathrm{cD}_{w}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})=\mathrm{secat}(\pi)
. The last part is also a consequence of Remark
2
.
∎

Corollary 4.12
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small connected category and let
c\colon\bullet\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
be a constant functor. Then

\mathrm{ccat}_{w}(\mathcal{C})=\mathrm{SG}_{w}(c)

and
\mathrm{ccat}_{w}(\mathcal{C})\leq\mathrm{cTC}_{w}(\mathcal{C})
.

Proof.

Applying Theorem
4.10
and Proposition
4.7
we obtain that
\mathrm{ccat}_{w}(\mathcal{C})=\mathrm{cD}_{w}(c,1_{\mathcal{C}})=\mathrm{secat}(q)
where
q
is the morphism that appears in the pullback

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{P}\mathcal{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(c,1_{\mathcal{C}})}

We claim that
P
is precisely (with the notation of Theorem
3.1
)
\mathrm{E}_{c}
where
c
, by an abuse of notation, denotes the constant functor associated to the object
c
. Indeed, the objects of
P
are pairs
(x,I)
where
x
is an object in
\mathcal{C}
and

is a path between

and

. Using the notation of Theorem

of

as the homotopy equivalence and

we can check that

. Hence,
\mathrm{SG}_{w}(c)=\mathrm{SG}_{w}(c_{1}\circ c_{2})=\mathrm{SG}_{w}(c_{2})=\mathrm{SG}_{w}(q)=\mathrm{secat}(q)=\mathrm{cD}_{w}(c,1_{\mathcal{C}})
.
The inequality

.
∎

Proposition 4.13
.

Let
P\colon\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
be a surjective weak fibration. Then
\mathrm{secat}(P)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}_{w}(\mathcal{B})

Proof.

Suppose that you have a geometric cover
\{U_{0},\dots,U_{n}\}
of
\mathcal{B}
such that
\iota_{i}\simeq_{w}c
where
c
is a constant functor. We can define
s_{i}\colon\mathcal{U}_{i}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}
as a constant functor
e
with
e
such that
P(e)=b
. Then we have that
s_{i}
is a local section since
P\circ s_{i}=c\simeq_{w}\iota_{i}
.
∎

Corollary 4.14
.

Let
F,G\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
be a functor between small categories. Then:

(1)

\mathop{\mathrm{cTC}}(\mathcal{C})_{w}\leq\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}_{w}(\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C})

(2)

\mathrm{cD}_{w}(F,G)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}_{w}(\mathcal{C})

Proof.

We prove (1). We know that
\mathop{\mathrm{cTC}}(\mathcal{C})_{w}=\mathrm{secat}(\pi)
by Proposition
4.11
. Applying Proposition
4.13
we obtain
\mathrm{secat}(\pi)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}_{w}(\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C})
.
We prove (2).
\mathrm{cD}_{w}(F,G)=\mathrm{secat}(q)
for

the morphism defined by the pullback of Theorem

. Applying Proposition
4.11
we obtain the inequality since
\mathrm{secat}(q)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{ccat}}_{w}(\mathcal{C})
.
∎
