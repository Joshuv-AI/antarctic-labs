Health and economic burdens of emerging infectious diseases in the USA, US territories and globally
Authors: J P Sevilla, Daria Burnes, Mona Birjandi, Joseph S Knee, Julie M Villanueva, David P Durham, Scott V Nystrom, Courtney D Petro, Jennifer Lumpkins, Deborah Schneider, David E Bloom, Richard C White, Robert A Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEmerging infectious diseases (EIDs) cause significant health and economic burdens in the USA and globally. Existing methods and analyses fall short of what is required to prioritise diseases for health technology research and development (R&D), including for medical countermeasure (MCM) development within rapid response frameworks by the Center for Biomedical Advanced Research and Development Authority, part of the Administration for Strategic Preparedness and Response within the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services.MethodsWe developed a method for quantifying and ranking health and economic disease burdens (‘full burdens’) and applied it to 15 high-priority EIDs for 223 countries and territories, including the USA and US territories, historically from 2000 to 2022 and prospectively from 2025 to 2034. Health burdens consisted of disability-adjusted life-year losses, converted into monetary values using the value of a statistical life-year. Economic burdens consisted of direct and indirect costs during the acute stage of illness for hospitalised cases. We computed unweighted and weighted burden measures, the latter controlling for global disparities in ability-to-pay to avoid EID burdens. We projected future disease burdens using Monte Carlo simulation.ResultsPandemics caused the largest historical and projected unweighted and weighted full burdens in the USA and globally. Among non-pandemics, across unweighted and weighted burdens, dengue and cholera imposed the largest historical and projected full burdens globally; West Nile Virus imposed the largest historical and projected full burdens in the USA, and dengue imposed the largest historical full burdens in the US territories. Weighted full burdens exceeded five times the unweighted ones. Regionally, the Americas and Africa faced the largest per capita weighted burdens while the Western Pacific region faced the smallest.ConclusionR&D priority-setting, including MCM development, depends on multiple criteria, including disease burdens. Our full burden quantification methods and results, along with other such criteria, can inform optimal priority-setting.Keywords: Global Health; Health economics; Public Health; Infections, diseases, disorders, injuries

Introduction

Emerging infectious diseases (EIDs) are ‘infections that have newly appeared in a population, or have existed but are rapidly increasing in incidence or geographic range’.1 Most such diseases are zoonotic. Others result from pathogens with pre-existing footholds in human populations that develop variants with heightened transmissibility, severity or resistance to existing medical countermeasures (MCMs)2; spread to new geographic areas3; or re-emerge in areas undergoing changes.2

EIDs cause significant health and economic burdens globally. For example, zoonotic EIDs include:(a) all seven respiratory pandemics since 1889,4 including the 1918 Spanish influenza pandemic (with an estimated 17.4 million deaths5 and 6% gross domestic product (GDP) losses,6(b) both pandemics since 2000 (the 2009 influenza A subtype H1N1 and 2020 SARS-CoV-2 pandemics, the latter associated with an estimated 18 million deaths7 and US$5.5 trillion in GDP losses8),(c) six of seven WHO Public Health Emergencies of International Concern since 2005 (including the 2014 West African Ebola epidemic, which resulted in an estimated 11 000 deaths9 and between US$2.8 and US$32.6 billion in GDP losses10) and (d) all diseases on the WHO 2018 priority pathogen list of diseases that pose the greatest epidemic and pandemic threats.11

Addressing EIDs is therefore a high global health priority. An important question is how to rank EIDs according to their burdens to support priority-setting with respect to research and development (R&D) into MCMs. The relative ranking of EIDs by disease burden can inform their relative R&D priority. A ranking method should account for both the health and economic burdens of these diseases, and for disparities in the distribution of such burdens. This question is faced by both commercial and non-commercial funders of such R&D, including the U.S. Center for Biomedical Advanced Research and Development Authority (BARDA). BARDA is part of the U.S. Administration for Strategic Preparedness and Response, established in 2006 under the U.S. Pandemic and All-Hazards Preparedness Act. BARDA’s mission is to develop MCMs addressing the public health and medical consequences of chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear threats, pandemic influenza and EIDs. To facilitate programmatic decisions and complement other ongoing efforts regarding the future direction of its EID portfolio, BARDA needs an approach to enable more direct comparisons of health and economic burdens among EIDs.

While many studies have reported the health burdens of individual diseases (eg, Guo et al12 for dengue; Zheng et al13 for cholera), their varying methodologies make disease comparisons difficult. Of studies estimating global burdens of multiple infectious diseases, few measured health burdens using summary health measures like disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) or quality-adjusted life-years (exceptions include Di Bari et al14; IHME15), hampering joint consideration of mortality and morbidity burdens. Few studies derived monetary values for health burdens (exceptions include Sevilla et al8 for the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic and Smith et al16 for Lassa fever), hampering economic evaluations and assessments of funding needs and commitments. Few studies aggregated health and economic burdens, which is critical to understand the full burden of EIDs.812,16

Similarly, there are a few projections regarding future burdens,17 and most focused on single diseases and only health burdens. The Global Burden of Disease Study18 projects health burdens, but it does not compute monetary values or include economic burdens, and it excludes relevant historical information and projections related to pandemics. No studies of global economic burdens accounted for insensitivity to ability-to-pay (ATP) of standard economic evaluation methods.

To address this gap and support priority setting, we developed a systematic approach to measuring weighted full burdens that allowed for consistent comparisons across EIDs and applied this approach to calculate the comprehensive health and economic burden of 15 representative EIDs in the USA, the US territories and globally over the historical period from 2000 to 2022 and a projection period from 2025 to 2034. We focused on many infectious diseases and countries to generate burden measures that reflect the global scale of EIDs as a whole and to allow for comparisons among diseases and geographies. We measured both health and economic burdens to account for the full burdens these diseases impose on society and generated monetary values for these burdens to facilitate economic evaluation. We used weights to account for disparities in the global distributions of such burdens. Our method can be considered an application of weighted cost-benefit analysis (wCBA), where full health and economic burdens are assigned monetary values reflecting individuals’ willingness-to-pay (WTP) to avoid these burdens, and weights reflecting diminishing marginal utility of income are used to account for global disparities in ATP. Results from this study will facilitate BARDA’s selection of pathogens for targeted MCM development. Our results and methods can also inform general global health priority-setting.

Selection of study EIDs

We quantified the health and economic burdens of 15 high-priority EIDs that have emerged since 2000 globally across 223 countries, territories and other locations (hereafter, ‘countries’), including the USA and the US territories. (Online supplemental table S1 shows the sample of countries.) We quantified these burdens historically, from 2000 to 2022 (the ‘historical period’), and prospectively, from 2025 to 2034 (the ‘projection period’; data constraints prevented us from projecting US territory burdens). The 15 diseases are chikungunya, cholera, SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19), Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever (CCHF), dengue, Ebola virus disease (Ebola), Lassa fever (Lassa), Marburg virus disease (Marburg), Middle East respiratory syndrome (MERS), Nipah and Henipaviral disease (Nipah), pandemic influenza A subtype H1N1/09 (H1N1), SARS, Rift Valley fever (RVF), West Nile virus disease (West Nile) and Zika virus disease (Zika).

Though disease X is on the WHO priority list, we cannot incorporate it in our historical analysis since it is, by definition, a placeholder name for a future disease with the potential to become a serious international epidemic or pandemic.24 We incorporate it in our projections by simulating possible pandemics from coronavirus and influenza virus (see Projection model), which have been considered the pathogen families most likely to yield disease X.24,26 We excluded non-resistant forms of HIV/AIDS, malaria and tuberculosis, which did not emerge or reemerge within our historical window as defined by inclusion in the UKHSA guidance, WHO priority list or BARDA strategic plan. Our study diseases include no antimicrobial-resistant (AMR) pathogens. Resistant pathogens can, in principle, satisfy the definition of EIDs (see the Introduction section), but only one such pathogen emerged in our historical window 2000–2022 as defined by inclusion in the UKHSA guidance, WHO priority list or BARDA strategic plan. This is multidrug-resistant (MDR) malaria, which emerged in Myanmar in 2008.22 However, data for MDR malaria were not available from our data sources, rendering its inclusion in our analysis infeasible.

Health, economic and full burdens

We defined full burdens as the sum of health and economic burdens from EID cases (see Sevilla et al8 for a derivation of this value formula). We defined a ‘case’ as a symptomatic case of any severity level (see online supplemental table S2). We ignore asymptomatic cases, which incur no burdens and whose contribution to transmission is accounted for in the burdens of the resulting symptomatic cases.We measured health burdens across all symptomatic infections, but measured economic burdens only among hospitalised cases.

Health burdens consisted of DALY losses, which in turn consisted of years of life lost and years lived with disability (YLDs). See online supplemental table S3 for YLD derivations. We converted health burdens into monetary values by valuing each DALY by the value of a statistical life-year (VSLY), an estimate of individuals’ WTP for health.

We adopted a societal perspective to measuring economic burdens, which we defined as the sum of direct and indirect costs. Direct costs were treatment costs regardless of payer. Indirect costs equalled the value of lost paid and unpaid work time. We adopted a human capital approach to indirect costs, measuring costs at the patient level and ignoring labour market dynamics, including worker replacement. We measured direct and indirect costs only during the acute stage of illness and for cases requiring hospitalisation, ignoring outpatient and critical care costs. We assumed direct and indirect costs were the same for fatal and non-fatal cases. For simplicity, we ignored other economic burdens faced by the macroeconomy, governments, firms, communities, caregivers and households.

Because WTP is constrained by ATP, standard WTP-centric economic valuation can undervalue burdens and benefits faced by developing nations.27 For example, valuing mortality risks using the country-specific value of a statistical life (VSL) can undervalue developing-country risks since VSL rises with income.27 To address this, we measured full burdens using both standard WTP methodology, not adjusting for global ATP disparities (calling these ‘full burdens’ or ‘unweighted full burdens’) and a weighting methodology adjusting for such disparities (calling these ‘weighted full burdens’). The latter roughly measures what full burdens would be if ATP were everywhere the same as in the USA in 2022. We adopted conservative assumptions under which weighting involved multiplying full burdens in a country-year by the ratio of per capita gross national income (PCGNI) in the USA in 2022 to PCGNI in that country-year.

Monetary values are in 2022 US dollars (USD). Online supplemental file 1 describes currency conversions.

Historical analysis

We obtained and estimated health burdens from data from GBD,23 the WHO,28,31 U.S. Centers for Disease Prevention and Control,932,34 Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention,35 36 European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control,37,41 Our World in Data,42 United Nations World Population Prospects Life Tables,43 Pan American Health Organization,44 Government of Canada,45 National Center for Vector Borne Diseases Control, India46 and other published literature.47,54 See online supplemental table S2 for details.

We computed VSLY using methodology from Robinson et al,27 and a VSL of US$11.4 million.55

Direct costs per case equalled the product of the disease-specific hospitalisation rate (see online supplemental table S4), the disease-specific hospitalisation length of stay (LOS) (see online supplemental table S5) and the country-specific cost per hospital bed day (from WHO-CHOICE).56 Indirect costs per case equalled the product of the probability of a hospitalised case (proxied by hospitalisation rates), the number of workdays lost (proxied by LOS), the country-specific hours per day spent on paid and unpaid work from Charmes57 and the country- and year-specific average hourly wage derived using data from the International Labour Organization.58 We discounted health and economic burdens at 3% per year, following WHO recommendations.59

We applied imputation and extrapolation methods where data were unavailable (see online supplemental file 1 for details).

We estimated health burdens, economic burdens and unweighted and weighted full burdens for every disease-country-year combination. We computed these burdens per capita and population-wide. Aggregating population-wide burdens to WHO regions and globally follows naturally.

Projection model

We projected future burdens over the years 2025–2034 using Monte Carlo simulation. In this simulation, we generated multiple possible futures (henceforth ‘iterations’) from a probabilistic model of the future, and computed summary statistics of patterns across those iterations.

We built our model using EID- and country-specific probability distributions (PDs) over per capita unweighted and weighted full burdens. We derived PDs using the results of our historical analysis, which showed how EID-specific burdens varied across historical years in each country. We combined historical information about the 2009 H1N1 pandemic with longer-term information about influenza pandemics from 1889 onwards (including the 1918 Spanish influenza pandemic) to generate a PD for all pandemic influenza diseases. We likewise combined historical information about SARS, MERS and COVID-19 to generate a PD for all coronavirus diseases. We take pandemic influenza and coronavirus projections to constitute projections of the two most plausible viral families from which disease X will emerge.24 We also accounted for the long-term historical frequency of large-scale Ebola and Zika outbreaks. See online supplemental tables S6–S8 for illustrations of aspects of our method for cholera, pandemic influenza and coronavirus, respectively.

We drew 5000 iterations (see online supplemental table S9 for justification). Each iteration resulted in an estimate for unweighted and weighted per capita full burdens for every EID-country-projection-year combination, or 13×223×10=28 990 estimates. We adjusted these estimates for population growth, economic growth and discounting. We further adjusted the estimates for dengue and certain neglected tropical diseases to incorporate GBD’s forecasts of these diseases. The summary statistics across iterations reflect both likely future outcomes and uncertainty over those outcomes.

Results

Historical analysis

Table 1 summarises the results of our historical analysis (results for the US territories are in online supplemental table S10), and figure 2 shows annual per capita full burdens and weighted full burdens by region. (Results by country, year and disease are in online supplemental file 3).

During the historical period across all EIDs (when discussing results, ‘all EIDs’ correspond to the 15 study EIDs), cases were 6.1 billion globally, 145 million in the USA and 3 million in the US territories. The corresponding fatalities were 21.0 million globally, 1.2 million in the USA and 7497 in the US territories.

Full burdens were US$17.6 trillion globally (health and economic burdens of US$17.5 trillion and US$104.8 billion, respectively), US$7.8 trillion in the USA (health and economic burdens of US$7.8 trillion and US$11.5 billion, respectively) and US$18.9 billion in the USA territories (health and economic burdens of US$17.2 and US$1.6 billion, respectively). Weighted full burdens were US$88.1 trillion globally (health and economic burdens of US$86.7 trillion and US$1.4 trillion, respectively), US$7.9 trillion in the USA (health and economic burdens of US$7.9 trillion and US$11.9 billion, respectively) and US$59.4 billion in the US territories (health and economic burdens of US$54.0 billion and US$5.4 billion, respectively). Global weighted full burdens exceeded five times unweighted full burdens, reflecting the considerable burdens borne by low- and middle-income countries. This suggests the scale of global EID burdens can be masked by the relatively low incomes of countries affected. Full burdens per case were US$2901 globally, US$53 668 in the USA and US$6452 in the US territories. Weighted full burdens per case were US$14 525 globally, US$54 917 in the USA and US$20 323 in the US territories. Annual per capita full burdens were US$108 globally, US$1080 in the USA and US$206 in the US territories; and annual per capita weighted full burdens were US$542 globally, US$1105 in the USA and US$648 in the US territories.

Looking at full burdens per capita per year, whether controlling for ATP or not, the largest such burdens were found in the Americas. Controlling for ATP, the second most affected region was Africa, followed closely by Europe, South-East Asia and the Eastern Mediterranean. The least affected region was the Western Pacific. Controlling for ATP, the largest population-wide burdens were in South-East Asia, which contains large low- and middle-income countries like India, Indonesia and Bangladesh. The Americas followed, with only slightly smaller burdens. Africa and Europe faced intermediate-size burdens, and the Eastern Mediterranean and Western Pacific shared similar, smaller burdens.

We disaggregated full burdens by cost category. Fatality-related health burdens constituted almost 88% of full burdens, disability-related health burdens constituted almost 12% of full burdens, and direct and indirect costs combined constituted less than 1% of the full burden. Thus, deaths were the largest contributor to burdens and a simple but powerful summary indicator of their scale.

COVID-19 had the largest unweighted and weighted full burdens globally and across all WHO regions, with one exception. Globally, COVID-19 had a full burden of US$16.0 trillion and a weighted full burden of US$63.3 trillion. In the USA, it had a full burden of US$7.5 trillion and a weighted full burden of US$7.6 trillion. In the US territories, it had a full burden of US$12.8 billion and a weighted full burden of US$39.5 billion. Full burdens and weighted full burdens of COVID-19 were, respectively, US$251.2 billion and US$6.8 trillion in Africa; US$9.2 trillion and US$19.0 trillion in the Americas; US$393.0 billion and US$5.4 trillion in the Eastern Mediterranean; US$4.9 trillion and US$13.1 trillion in Europe; US$520.0 billion and US$15.9 trillion in South-East Asia; and US$718.4 billion and US$3.0 trillion in Western Pacific. The exception to this rule is that cholera had a larger weighted full burden, US$7.4 trillion, than COVID-19 in Africa.

Globally, the EIDs with the next largest burdens were pandemic H1N1, dengue and cholera, whose full burdens were in the hundreds of billions (US$720 billion for pandemic H1N1, US$430 billion for dengue and US$380 billion for cholera), and whose weighted full burdens were in the trillions (US$12.2 trillion for cholera, US$8.0 trillion for dengue and US$4.4 trillion for pandemic H1N1). Significantly below these in terms of burdens were West Nile, with a full burden of US$18.8 billion, and Ebola, with a weighted full burden of US$93.2 billion. All other EIDs had full burdens below US$2.4 billion and weighted full burdens below US$8 billion.

Global disease rankings were relatively similar whether based on unweighted or weighted full burdens. The most important consequence of weighting on the global disease ranking was for Ebola, ranked eighth according to unweighted full burdens but fifth according to weighted full burdens, reflecting the poverty of the countries affected by the 2014–2016 West African Ebola epidemic.

In the USA, pandemic H1N1 had the second highest full burden, at US$235.1 billion. There was a significant drop in the burdens of the remaining EIDs. The EID with the third highest burden was West Nile, with a full burden of US$18.2 billion. Dengue had the fourth highest full burden, at US$220 million. Ebola had the fifth highest burden, at US$11 million. All other EIDs had full burdens near or below US$1 million.

In the US territories, dengue had the second highest full burden, at US$4.4 billion. Cholera had the third highest full burden, at US$1.6 billion. Zika had the fourth highest full burden, at US$139 million. Chikungunya had the fifth highest burden, at US$21 million. Data on cases and deaths required for burden calculations were unavailable for all other EIDs in the US territories.

In Africa, pandemic H1N1 (full burdens and weighted full burdens of US$24.3 billion and US$774.6 billion, respectively), dengue (full burdens and weighted full burdens of US$5.0 billion and US$164.0 billion, respectively), and Ebola (full burdens and weighted full burdens of US$1.1 billion and US$93.2 billion, respectively) had the next largest burdens in that order. The remaining diseases had significantly smaller burdens.

In the Americas, pandemic H1N1 and dengue have the second and third largest full burdens (US$287.9 billion and US$172.4 billion, respectively), though their rankings switch according to weighted full burdens (US$658.4 billion and US$1.8 trillion, respectively). The diseases with the fourth through seventh largest burdens were cholera (full burdens and weighted full burdens of US$39.8 billion and US$439.8 billion, respectively), West Nile (full burdens and weighted full burdens of US$18.2 billion and US$20.3 billion, respectively), Zika (full burdens and weighted full burdens of US$759.4 million and US$7.7 billion, respectively) and chikungunya (full burdens and weighted full burdens of US$435.9 million and US$3.6 billion, respectively). The remaining diseases had significantly smaller burdens.

In the Eastern Mediterranean, the diseases with the second through fifth largest burdens were cholera (full burdens and weighted full burdens of US$22.0 billion and US$913.1 billion, respectively), pandemic H1N1 (full burdens and weighted full burdens of US$20.6 billion and US$345.9 billion, respectively), dengue (full burdens and weighted full burdens of US$5.2 billion and US$216.0 billion, respectively) and MERS (full burdens and weighted full burdens of US$2.3 billion and US$6.6 billion, respectively). The remaining diseases had burdens lower than US$1 billion.

In Europe, the diseases with the second through fourth largest burdens were pandemic H1N1 (full burdens and weighted full burdens of US$240.4 billion and US$666.6 billion, respectively), cholera (full burdens and weighted full burdens of US$3.9 billion and US$65.6 billion, respectively) and West Nile (full burdens and weighted full burdens of US$568.2 million and US$2.1 billion, respectively). The remaining diseases had burdens lower than US$70 million.

In South-East Asia, the diseases with the second through fourth largest burdens were dengue (full burdens and weighted full burdens of US$148.8 billion and US$5.2 trillion, respectively), cholera (full burdens and weighted full burdens of US$73.2 billion and US$2.7 trillion, respectively), and pandemic H1N1 (full burdens and weighted full burdens of US$26.5 billion and US$1.2 trillion, respectively). The remaining diseases had much lower burdens.

In the Western Pacific, the disease with the second largest burdens was pandemic H1N1 (full burdens and weighted full burdens of US$120.3 billion and US$739.8 billion, respectively). Dengue and cholera have the third and fourth largest full burdens (US$98.8 billion and US$25.6 billion, respectively), though their rankings switch according to weighted full burdens (US$695.2 billion and US$698.7 billion, respectively). The remaining diseases had much lower burdens.

Our historical period saw two pandemics, the 2009 H1N1 pandemic and the 2020–2023 COVID-19 pandemic, which jointly accounted for the largest share of unweighted and weighted full burdens: annual pandemic full burdens were US$727 billion globally, US$336 billion in the USA and US$556 million in the US territories; and annual weighted full burdens were US$2.9 trillion globally, US$343 billion in the USA and US$1.7 billion in the US territories. Excluding these pandemics, annual full burdens across all other EIDs averaged US$36 billion globally, US$802 million in the USA and US$264 million in the US territories; and annual weighted full burdens across all other EIDs averaged US$887 billion globally, US$894 million in the USA and US$867 million in the US territories.

Table 3 shows that projected average annual per capita full burdens across all EIDs in the USA (without territories) had an expected value across 5000 iterations of US$461, a median value of US$4, a 90% CI of US$2 to US$4098, a minimum of US$1 and a maximum of US$9148. Pandemic influenza accounted for 68% of the expected burdens, coronavirus for 31% and the remaining EIDs collectively accounted for about 1%. Thus, our single best estimate of the expected annual per capita full burden in the USA across all EIDs was US$461. The actual annual per capita full burden tended to be small, less than or around US$4, reflecting the relatively low burden of non-pandemic EIDs in the USA. But with a small annual probability, which we estimated from historical incidence, of about 1.5%, a large influenza or coronavirus pandemic could strike, causing enormous burdens. Thus, future EID risks in the USA were associated with high probabilities of low burdens and low probabilities of enormous burdens, and these low probability high burdens were associated with large pandemics.

Globally, the average annual per capita full burden across iterations was US$49, the median was US$7, the 90% CI was US$6 to US$383, the minimum was US$5 and the maximum was US$827. The average annual per capita weighted full burden was US$370, the median was US$178, the 90% CI was US$146 to US$1865, the minimum was US$123 and the maximum was US$3833. In contrast to the USA, when we accounted for ATP, we found that the relative importance of non-pandemic to pandemic EIDs rose globally: cholera’s and dengue’s shares of the weighted full burdens were 26% and 19%, respectively (as compared with less than 1% in the USA), pandemic influenza’s share was 39% (compared with 68% in the USA) and coronavirus’s share was 16% (compared with 31% in the USA).

Three factors help explain US-global differences. First, the globe has lower ATP than the USA, making the gap in unweighted full burdens larger than that in weighted full burdens. Second, the USA seems disproportionately affected by large pandemics (our COVID-19 historical analysis found that US and global deaths per capita were 0.0036 and 0.0021, respectively), explaining why maximum and 95th percentile values of per capita weighted full burdens were much lower globally than in the USA. Third, controlling for ATP, non-pandemic EIDs, specifically cholera and dengue, imposed much larger burdens globally than in the USA, explaining why median, 5th percentile and minimum per capita weighted full burdens were much higher globally than in the USA.

Thus, high probability non-pandemic EIDs imposed larger per capita burdens globally than in the USA, while low probability pandemics imposed relatively larger burdens per capita in the USA than globally. The latter effect dominated the former, so the USA on average faced a larger burden across all EIDs taken together.

Looking at regional expected values, across unweighted and weighted full burdens, the Americas region tended to have among the highest per capita burdens, and the Western Pacific was the region with the lowest. Looking at unweighted per capita burdens, Europe had the second largest values, reflecting its high ATP, but looking at weighted per capita burdens, Africa and South-East Asia had significant per capita burdens, reflecting concentrations of cases and deaths in those regions. These regional patterns roughly mirror those of the historical analysis.

Looking at regional variations in relative disease burdens, Africa’s projected burdens are largest for cholera, followed by pandemic influenza and coronavirus; projected burdens in the Americas are largest for pandemic influenza, coronavirus and dengue; projected burdens in the Eastern Mediterranean and Western Pacific are largest for pandemic influenza, followed by coronavirus and cholera (though cholera has a higher weighted full burden than coronavirus); projected burdens in Europe are highest for pandemic influenza followed by coronavirus; projected burdens in South-East Asia are dominated by dengue and pandemic influenza, followed by coronavirus and cholera (though cholera has a higher weighted full burden than coronavirus).

We combined per capita burden projections with population projections to compute population-wide burdens. We estimated an average population across the projection period of 351 million in the USA and 8.5 billion globally. In the USA, we found that the annual weighted full burden from all EIDs had an expected value of US$166.1 billion, but could be as high as US$3.3 trillion and as low as US$702 million. Globally, we found that the same burden had an expected value of US$3.1 trillion but could be as high as US$32.6 trillion and as low as US$1.05 trillion.

Discussion

We developed a general wCBA-based method for quantifying and ranking the unweighted and weighted health and economic burdens of disease. We used this method to measure the global burden of 15 representative EIDs spanning diverse transmission modes, fatality rates, outbreak frequencies and endemic regions.

We found that pandemics caused the largest historical and projected unweighted and weighted full burdens among the 15 EIDs, both in the USA and globally. COVID-19 accounted for the vast share of historical burdens from 2000 to 2022, but pandemic influenza, which is a potential disease X, accounted for most of the projected burdens from 2025 to 2034. Among non-pandemic EIDs, across historical and projected analyses, and across unweighted and weighted full burdens, dengue and cholera had the largest burdens globally, and West Nile had the largest full burden in the USA. Our projections suggest that the time trends of EID burdens will be dominated by the dynamics of pandemics. But our historical analysis suggests that abstracting away from pandemics and accounting for ATP, there is a downward trend in global non-pandemic EID burdens driven largely by declining burdens from cholera.

Regardless of weighting, our historical analysis and projections showed that the Americas region tended to face the largest regional per capita burdens while the Western Pacific region faced the smallest. Controlling for ATP, the Africa and South-East Asia regions had among the highest per capita burdens, reflecting concentrations of cases and deaths in those regions, while South-East Asia was the region with the largest population-wide burdens given its large population. Notably, the USA accounted for large shares of population-wide burdens and had one of the world’s highest per capita unweighted full burdens.

Our method supports ranking and comparing diseases by their unweighted or weighted full burdens, which can inform global health priority-setting and R&D prioritisation as one input among many. Our method for burden quantification and ranking is general and can be applied across disease areas, countries and R&D funders. R&D priorities generally reflect multiple factors. For example, in the U.S. Government context, BARDA’s preparedness and response strategy is two-pronged and involves advanced development of both MCMs targeted at priority pathogens and flexible platform technologies for accelerated response to multiple known and unknown pathogens.62 Within both prongs, priority-setting—given constrained resources—is informed by health and economic disease burdens along with other factors such as national security threats, the existing R&D landscape (including R&D opportunities and products with varying extents of development, regulatory approval, manufacturing scale and accessibility to those in need), demand or market potential and potential for funding partnerships.63 64 BARDA and other organisations (including global health funders) can combine our burden estimates and rankings with information on other relevant priority-setting criteria to optimise their response to critical public health needs.

limitations

Study limitations include

First, our estimates are conservative:(a) we only analysed a subset of EIDs;(b) we calculated economic burdens only for hospitalised cases and during the acute stage;(c) data limitations prevented us from measuring the health burden of long-term sequelae, such as from congenital Zika syndrome (CZS) (though in the case of CZS, accounting for an estimated 3591 cases and 30 027 DALY losses in Brazil from 2015 to 202065 would not change our main conclusions);(d) we did not incorporate public health costs (eg, surveillance and outbreak response); and (e) we did not incorporate the macroeconomic impacts of severe outbreaks, an important limitation given a recent finding that the macroeconomic costs of COVID-19 roughly equal its combined health burdens and direct and indirect cost burdens.8

Second, given the difficulties of predicting the future, for many EIDs, our projections did not model possible future changes to the health, policy, socioeconomic and demographic landscape that could alter future disease trajectories from historical ones. However, our projections accommodated these various landscape features to the extent they shaped historical patterns.

Third, data limitations required us to impute data across countries, diseases and time periods. Data also suffer from measurement error. For example, inadequate disease surveillance systems can result in under-reporting of disease cases and deaths. GBD adjusts its case and death estimates for under-reporting, so this under-reporting risk is mitigated where we rely on their estimates, which are for cholera, COVID-19, dengue and Zika globally; and Ebola for the Democratic Republic of Congo. Some data sources are unclear regarding whether ‘cases’ include asymptomatic infections, which required us to make educated guesses regarding whether they do. Resource and time constraints prevented us from exhaustively searching for EID cases and deaths data.

Table 2 shows significant differences in burdens between pandemic and non-pandemic EIDs, and between dengue and cholera on the one hand and the remaining EIDs on the other. These significant differences suggest that study limitations regarding burden measurement for these remaining EIDs are unlikely to affect their rank relative to pandemics, dengue and cholera.

Fourth, our Monte Carlo projections involved simplifying assumptions. To a significant extent and for many EIDs, our projections largely reflected historical patterns adjusted for population growth, economic growth and discounting. We accounted for additional disease-specific information only for diseases for which GBD provided forecasts: dengue and other EIDs that fall within the GBD category of ‘Other Neglected Tropical Diseases’. (GBD has COVID-19 forecasts showing a transition to endemicity, which we did not use because our projection involves the entire family of coronaviruses.) We assumed that large influenza pandemics do not coincide with large coronavirus pandemics, and that the former supersede the latter when they might otherwise coincide. This assumption tended to raise pandemic influenza’s share of projected burdens and lower pandemic coronavirus’s share more than other equally reasonable assumptions might.

Fifth, our focus on EIDs implies our analysis does not shed light on the priority of other major global infectious disease challenges, such as HIV, malaria, tuberculosis and AMR.

Conclusions

EIDs have been and will continue to be important causes of health and economic burdens in the USA and globally. EID-related R&D priority-setting, including for MCM development by BARDA, generally reflects multiple criteria, including relative disease burdens, current R&D opportunities and pipeline products, demand and market potential, and other health security risks. Our methods for quantifying weighted health and economic burdens can, in combination with other such criteria, support these priority-setting decisions.
