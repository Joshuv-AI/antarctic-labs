Perturbations of measures and sets having curves in d d directions
Jakub Takáč

Abstract
We show that whenever a separable subset S S of a complete metric space X X admits a d d -dimensional weak tangent field, the set S S is close to being d d -dimensional in the following sense. Whenever μ \mu is a Borel finite measure on X X supported on S S , then a typical 1 1 -Lispchitz map (in the sense of Baire category) into a Euclidean space maps μ \mu -almost all of S S into a set of Hausdorff dimension at most d d . When taking d = 0 d=0 , this implies that any 1 1 -purely unrectifiable set is typically carried into a Hausdorff 0 0 -dimensional set up to a μ \mu -null set. We show that the result is sharp in Euclidean spaces and, more generally, in strictly convex Banach spaces of finite dimension.



1
Introduction

Recall that a set is called
n
-rectifiable, if it can be covered, up to an
\mathcal{H}^{n}
-null set, by a countable number of Lipschitz images of subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. On the other hand a set
S
is called
n
-purely unrectifiable, if for every
n
-rectifiable set
E
, one has
\mathcal{H}^{n}(S\cap E)=0
. Here and hereafter
\mathcal{H}^{n}
is the
n
-dimensional Hausdorff measure. In this paper, we study the geometric idea that
n
-purely unrectifiable sets are, in some sense, close to being at most
(n-1)
-dimensional.

This is motivated by the previous work of Bate and Li
[
7
]
and Bate and Weigt
[
8
]
. It follows from their result that an
n
-purely unrectifiable set
S
of
finite
Hausdorff measure essentially admits a so-called
(n-1)
-dimensional weak tangent field. This can be used to show that an arbitrarily small
1
-Lipschitz perturbation of the identity may be found, say
f
, satisfying
\mathcal{H}^{n}(f(S))=0

[
10
, Theorem 1.1]
. In this sense, the set
S
is close to being
\mathcal{H}^{n}
-null. However, since the argument crucially relies on the fact that
S
has an
(n-1)
-dimensional weak tangent field, a natural question is whether one can produce a perturbation
f
as above with the stronger property
\dim_{H}(f(S))\leq n-1
, where
\dim_{H}
stands for the Hausdorff dimension.

The condition that
S
be of finite Hausdorff measure is, in all of the aforementioned results, crucial. Therefore, it is also of interest to see what kind of a result, say in the spirit of the said perturbation result of Bate
[
10
]
, can be obtained for general purely unrectifiable sets
S
.

1.1
A geometric property of planar purely unrectifiable sets

To further motivate our work, we begin with a simple result that can be obtained from the classical Besicovitch projection theorem.

Proposition 1.1
.

If
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a compact,
1
-purely unrectifiable set with
\mathcal{H}^{1}(S)<\infty
, then there exists a sequence of
1
-Lipschitz maps
f_{i}
converging to the identity uniformly such that
f_{i}(S)
is finite for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Recall the assertion of the Besicovitch projection theorem which states that for almost every direction
\theta
, the orthogonal projection to the line through the origin in the direction of
\theta
, say
p_{\theta}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to L_{\theta}
, satisfies

\mathcal{H}^{1}(p_{\theta}(S))=0.

Thus, it is without loss of generality to assume that the orthogonal projections to the two canonical vectors, say
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, carry
S
into a set of zero length.

By the definition of
\mathcal{H}^{1}
, for any
\varepsilon>0
, each of the sets
p_{i}(S)
can be covered by a countable number of intervals such that the total sum of their lengths does not exceed
\varepsilon
. Since
S
is compact, this collection can also be taken to be finite.
Say we denote the union of these intervals by
G_{i}
. We now let
f_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be given by

f_{i}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\chi_{\mathbb{R}\setminus G_{i}}.

Note that
f_{i}
carries
p_{i}(S)
into a finite set.
It follows that the map

f\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2};\quad f(x)=(f_{1}(p_{1}(x)),f_{2}(p_{2}(x))),

carries
S
into a finite set, is
1
-Lipschitz and satisfies

\lVert f-\operatorname{Id}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{2}\varepsilon
.
∎

1.2
Perturbations in metric spaces

The previous proposition verifies a particular special instance of the idea discussed above, namely that
1
-purely unrectifiable sets of finite length are close to being
0
-dimensional (even finite). However, same method cannot be applied to
n
-purely unrectifiable sets with
n>1
and, more crucially, to sets having infinite Hausdorff measure. To obtain results in these more general settings, we rely on different techniques based on
metric
notions. The perturbation result of Bate serves as an entry point to our approach and therefore we now continue by presenting the result explicitly.

Given a metric space
X
, the symbol
\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(X,\mathbb{R}^{m})
stands for the space of
1
-Lipschitz and bounded maps from
X
to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We equip the space with the supremum norm, making it into a
complete
metric space.

Recall that a subset
H
of a metric space is called
residual
if it contains an intersection of a countable number of dense open sets. The Baire category theorem asserts that in a complete metric space, residual sets are dense. Therefore, residual sets are a suitable notion of “large” sets. In particular, if we wish to assert that a Lipschitz map
f
may be approximated with maps having a given property (cf. Proposition
1.1
), this immediatelly follows if we are able to show that the set of maps possessing the given property is residual. We shall say that a
typical
element of the space
H
satisfies the given property, say
(P)
, if the set of elements satisfying
(P)
is residual.

The following theorem of Bate
[
10
]
originally required an additional assumption, but it can be removed by applying the recent result of Bate and Weigt
[
8
]
obtaining thereby the formulation we state below.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
10
,
8
]
)
.

Let
X
be a complete metric space.

(i)

If
S\subset X
is purely
n
-purely unrectifiable and
\mathcal{H}^{n}(S)<\infty
,
then a typical
f\in\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(X,\mathbb{R}^{m})
satisfies
\mathcal{H}^{n}(f(S))=0
.

(ii)

If
E\subset X
is
n
-rectifiable,
\mathcal{H}^{n}(E)>0
and
m\geq n
, then the set of maps
f\in\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(X,\mathbb{R}^{m})
satisfying
\mathcal{H}^{n}(f(E))>0
is open and dense.

Thus in particular, amongst sets of finite
\mathcal{H}^{n}
measure, the
n
-purely unrectifiable ones are characterised by the fact that they can be perturbed, with a Lipschitz map, into an
\mathcal{H}^{n}
-null set. In this sense,
n
-purely unrectifiable sets of finite measure are close to being
\mathcal{H}^{n}
-null.

Let us now examine closely the requirement of
\mathcal{H}^{n}(S)<\infty
in the first item of the theorem. Clearly, it may weakened to only requiring that
\mathcal{H}^{n}_{|S}
be
\sigma
-finite. On the other hand, it may not be removed entirely, as can be seen by the following example. Suppose
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the Koch curve. Then any Lipschitz (even continuous) map
f\colon S\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
which is sufficiently close, in the supremum norm, to the identity, maps
S
into a
path-connected
set containing more than
1
point. The image therefore must have positive length. Since
S
is
1
-purely unrectifiable, it follows that Theorem
1.2
fails without the finite measure assumption.

Therefore the natural question is whether some analogous statement can be formulated, which would work without the finite measure assumption. The content of our main result is precisely such a statement, taking into consideration also the previously discussed idea of
n
-purely unrectifiable sets being “nearly”
(n-1)
-dimensional. We begin by stating the special case for
1
-purely unrectifiable sets.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
X
be a complete metric space and suppose
S\subset X
is separable. If
S
is purely
1
-unrectifiable, then for any finite Borel measure
\mu
on
S
and any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the set

\{f\in\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(X,\mathbb{R}^{m}):\exists N\subset S,\mu(N)=0\;\text{and}\;\dim_{H}f(S\setminus N)=0\}

is residual in
\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(X,\mathbb{R}^{m})
.

If this result is applied to a natural measure on the Koch curve (e.g. the one obtained from the self-similar construction, or the Hausdorff measure of the correct dimension) we can see that while almost all of the curve is typically mapped into a
0
-dimensional set, the full image is typically a non-trivial continuous curve. Thus, a typical
1
-Lipschitz map sends
\mu
-large sets into Hausdorff-small sets and
\mu
-small sets into Hausdorff-large sets.

We continue with the presentation of the higher-dimensional version of Theorem
1.3
. Firstly, the notion of
n
-pure unrectifiability has to be replaced with a more rigid notion relying on weak tangent fields.
Given a subset
S
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we say that
S
admits a
d
-dimensional weak tangent field if there is a map
\tau
from
S
to the Grassmanian of
d
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
with the following property.
For every Lipschitz curve
\gamma
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
and for
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
x\in\gamma\cap S
,

\gamma^{\prime}(\gamma^{-1}(x))\in\tau(x).

For example, an embedded
d
-dimensional
C^{1}
-surface
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
admits a
d
-dimensional tangent field - this can be taken as the classical tangent field of the surface itself.
Also, it follows immediately from the definitions that
S
is
1
-purely unrectifiable if and only if
S
admits a
0
-dimensional weak tangent field. We remark that this notion can be made sense of in a general metric space by considering mappings into Euclidean spaces of higher dimension - see Definition
2.18
.

For a set
S
with
\mathcal{H}^{n}(S)<\infty
it follows from the aforementioned results
[
7
,
8
]
that
S
is purely
n
-unrectifiable if and only if
S
admits an
(n-1)
-dimensional weak tangent field up to an
\mathcal{H}^{n}
-null set. Thus, we argue that in the absence of the finite measure assumption, the correct condition to impose on
S
is a dimensionality of a weak tangent field as opposed to pure unrectifiability.

Our main result follows. Notice that by taking
d=0
below, one recovers Theorem
1.3

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
X
be a complete metric space and
S\subset X
a separable subset which admits a
d
-dimensional weak tangent field. Then for any finite Borel measure
\mu
on
S
and any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the set

\{f\in\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(X,\mathbb{R}^{m}):\exists N\subset S,\mu(N)=0\;\text{and}\;\dim_{H}(f(S\setminus N))\leq d\}

is residual in
\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(X,\mathbb{R}^{m})
.

Next, we are concerned with the sharpness of Theorem
1.4
. Firstly, we can now see that the Hausdorff dimension is not stable under perturations. Recall that given a finite Borel measure
\mu
on some metric space
X
, its
Hausdorff dimension
,
\dim_{H}\mu
, is the infimum of the set of those
s\in\mathbb{R}
for which there exists a
\mu
-null set
N
with

\mathcal{H}^{s}(X\setminus N)<\infty.

Since the Hausdorff dimension is not stable under perturbations, we may “forcefully” stabilize it via the relaxation:

\begin{split}\dim_{H}^{\textnormal{stable}}(\mu)&=\max_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\inf\{s\in[0,\infty]:\exists f_{i}\in\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{k},\mathbb{R}^{k})\\
&f_{i}\to\operatorname{Id}\;\text{uniformly on bounded sets and}\dim_{H}({f_{i}}_{\#}\mu)\leq s\},\end{split}

(1.1)

for a finite Borel measure
\mu
on the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. This may be modified for the metric setting via

\begin{split}\dim_{H}^{\textnormal{stable}}(\mu)&=\max_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\inf\{s\in[0,\infty]:\{f\in\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(X,\mathbb{R}^{m}):\dim_{H}({f}_{\#}\mu)\leq s\}\\
&\text{is dense in $\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(X,\mathbb{R}^{m})$}\},\end{split}

(1.2)

for a finite Borel measure
\mu
on the complete metric space
X
.
The coincidence of the two definitions whenever
X
is Euclidean is neither obvious, nor crucial for our argument. It does follow from the theory developed in the rest of this paper, see Corollaries
5.22
and
5.20
and the discussion at the end of Section
5
.

Theorem
1.5
gives a one-sided estimate on the quantity
\dim_{H}^{\textnormal{stable}}(\mu)
in terms of the weak tangent field. Namely, for a subset
S
of a metric space
X
, we define its
tangential dimension
,
\dim_{T}S
, by

\dim_{T}(S)=\min\{d\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}:\textnormal{$S$ admits a $d$-dimensional weak tangent field}\},

where we convene that the minimum of the empty set is
\infty
. For a finite Borel measure
\mu
on the complete metric space
X
, we let its
tangential dimension
,
\dim_{T}\mu
, be given by

\dim_{T}\mu=\min\{d\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}:\exists N\subset X,\mu(N)=0\;\textnormal{and}\;\dim_{T}(X\setminus N)\leq d\}.

Equipped with this notation, Theorem
1.4
in particular implies that

\dim_{H}^{\textnormal{stable}}(\mu)\leq\dim_{T}\mu.

In the case when
X
is a Euclidean space, we are able to also obtain the converse inequality.
The principal intermediate result which we prove is that for measures in the Euclidean space (or, more generally, strictly convex finite dimensional Banach spaces) the tangential dimension is preserved under small perturbations.

Theorem 1.5
.

Suppose
\mu
is a finite Borel measure in the strictly convex finite dimensional Banach space
X
.
Then there is a compact convex set
C\subset X
and
\varepsilon>0
such that whenever a
1
-Lipschitz map
f\colon X\to X
satisfies
\lVert f-\operatorname{Id}\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}(C)}\leq\varepsilon
, then

\dim_{T}(f_{\#}\mu)\geq\dim_{T}\mu.

By relying on the theory of so-called Alberti representations, which we discuss extensively in Subsection
2.4
, it follows that the number of independent Alberti representations a measure has is preserved under perturbations. Thus, available results in literature
[
12
,
6
,
4
]
almost immediately yield the partial converse to Theorem
1.4
.

Corollary 1.6
.

Suppose
\mu
is a Borel finite measure in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
with
\dim_{T}(\mu)\geq d\leq m
. Then the set

\{f\in\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{k},\mathbb{R}^{m}):\dim_{H}(f_{\#}\mu)\geq d\}

has non-empty interior.

The combination of Theorem
1.4
and the preceding Corollary
1.6
implies the equality

\dim^{\textnormal{stable}}_{H}\mu=\dim_{T}\mu

for any Borel finite measure
\mu
on a Euclidean space. A trivial, yet remarkable consequence of this equality is that
\dim_{H}^{\textnormal{stable}}(\mu)
is an integer. In particular
\dim_{H}^{\textnormal{stable}}(\mu)
is at most the integer part of
\dim_{H}(\mu)
.

1.3
On some related results

The aforementioned Alberti representations of a finite Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
are used in the work of Alberti and Marchese
[
1
]
to construct the
decomposability bundle

V_{\mu}
which is a
\mu
-measurable map on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
taking values in the Grassmanian of linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. This bundle can be seen as a kind of tangent field of
\mu
and one of the main results of the paper
[
1
]
is a characterisation of
\mu
-a.e. differentiability of Lipschitz functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
in terms of
V_{\mu}
. A seemingly different construction of a kind of tangent field of a measure is carried out by Bouchitté et al
[
11
]
, which can be shown to be equivalent to the bundle of Alberti and Marchese
[
5
]
.

Alberti and Marchese
[
1
]
need to construct Lipschitz mappings, which are not differentiable in a prescribed direction and on a prescribed set. At this point in time, the kind of construction they rely on is fairly well established in the literature. The main geometric point is that Lipschitz functions may be constructed that have “bad behaviour” in those direction in which there are no Lipschitz curves. Similar type of a construction is used, for example, in the aforementioned work of Bate
[
10
]
, the paper of Schioppa
[
16
]
on derivations, the earlier work of Bate
[
9
]
on Lipschitz differentiability spaces or the paper of Aliaga et al
[
2
]
on
1
-purely unrectifiable spaces.

In order to prove our Theorem
1.4
we also rely on this type of construction, namely we explicitly make use of the form of this construction found in Bate’s paper
[
10
, Proposition 3.5]
. The main point in our case is not a construction of a Lipschitz map which is not differentiable (in a prescribed direction) but rather a map that “squashes” nearby points together (also only in a prescribed direction).
The existence of these maps that squash points together is what allows us to construct (different) maps that effectively concentrate measure into sets that have small Hausdorff content of the given dimension. This is the core idea behind Theorem
1.4
and is carried out rigorously in Section
3
.

Finally, we discuss a connection of Marstrand’s projection theorem
[
14
,
13
]
to Theorem
1.4
. To make our point clear, let us first recall the Besicovitch–Federer projection theorem. The theorem states that for a purely
n
-unrectifiable subset
S
of a Euclidean space, of finite measure, almost every orthogonal projection to an
n
-dimensional subspace carries
S
into a null set. If
S
is
n
-rectifiable and has positive measure, than the opposite conclusion holds.
Therefore, if one simply takes the Besicovitch–Federer theorem and replaces “almost every projection” with “a typical
1
-Lipschitz map”, the resulting statement is true; in fact it is a special case of Theorem
1.2
above due to Bate
[
10
]
. Therefore the natural question is whether the same can be done for a variant of the Marstrand’s projection theorem.

One formulation of said theorem found in the book of Mattila
[
15
, Corollary 9.8]
implies in particular that if a set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
has positive Hausdorff
s
-measure, then almost every projection of the set to an
m
-dimensional subspace with
m<s
has Hausdorff dimension at least
m
.
If, on the other hand,
\dim E<m
, then the dimension of almost every projection to an
m
-dimensional subspace is at least
\dim E

[
15
, Corollary 9.4]
. In this sense almost all projections preserve the Hausdorff dimension.
This is contrary to our results, which state that if typical
1
-Lipschitz maps are considered (as opposed to almost all projections) then preservation of Hausdorff dimension cannot be expected.

To illustrate our point on a specific example, if one considers once again the Koch curve in the plane equipped with the Hausdorff measure of the correct dimension, then almost every projection to a
1
-dimensional subspace has dimension one (in fact, the pushforward of the measure is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure and its density lies in
L^{2}

[
13
]
), while a typical
1
-Lipschitz pushforward of the measure is Hausdorff
0
-dimensional.

1.4
Structure of the paper

Section
2
contains preliminaries needed in the rest of the paper. Sections
3
and
4
are dedicated to proving Theorem
1.4
together with some additional results concerning certain cases when the target space
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is replaced by a finite dimensional

space. We also provide the result in several forms, each essentially equivalent to every other. Section

. Finally, Section

is concerned with proving Theorem
1.5
and Corollary
1.6
.

Preliminaries

In this section we collect some basic preliminary facts needed to carry out the arguments in the rest of the paper. We rely largely on existing literature, however, there are several basic results concerning
measurability
of certain constructions that are not available and we must develop the theory ourselves .

2.1
Basic measure theory

In the following we recall some basic measure theory in general metric spaces, which we will use without further reference.
We call a metric space
X
which is complete and separable a
Polish metric space
.
If
X
is a set and we say that
\mu
is a measure on
X
, we mean that
\mu
is an outer measure. If
X
is a set and
\Sigma
is a
\sigma
-algebra on
X
and we say that
\mu
is a measure on
(X,\Sigma)
, we mean that
\mu
is an outer measure on
X
and each set in
\Sigma
is
\mu
-meausrable. If the
\sigma
-algebra with which we are working is clear from the context, we shall omit specifying it.
We say that a measure
\mu
on the metric space
X
is Borel, if the
\sigma
-algebra of
\mu
-measurable sets contains the Borel
\sigma
-algebra. Recall that if
X
is a Polish metric space and
\mu
is a
\sigma
-finite Borel measure on
X
, then
\mu
is
inner regular
, i.e. for every Borel set
B\subset X
there exists a sequence of compact sets
K_{i}\subset B
such that

\mu(B\setminus\bigcup_{i}K_{i})=0.

In particular, if
\mu(B)<\infty
, then to each
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a compact set
K\subset B
such that
\mu(B\setminus K)<\varepsilon
.

We say that a measure
\mu
on a space
X
is supported on a
\mu
-measurable set
E\subset X
if
\mu(X\setminus E)=0
. We let
\operatorname{spt}\mu
be the least closed set
F\subset X
such that
\mu(X\setminus F)=0
.
If
X
is a complete metric space and
\mu
is a Borel
\sigma
-finite measure on
X
which is supported on a separable set, then
\mu
is inner regular. Indeed, if
\mu
is supported on the separable set
S
, then
\operatorname{spt}\mu\subset\overline{S}
and
\overline{S}
is a Polish metric space.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X
be a metric space and
s\in[0,\infty)
. For a subset
E\subset X
and
\delta\in(0,\infty]
, we define the
(
s
-dimensional) Hausdorff
\delta
-content
of
E
by

\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\delta}(E)=\inf\{\sum_{i}\operatorname{diam}(E_{i})^{s}:E\subset\bigcup_{i}E_{i};\;E_{i}\subset X\}.

We call
\mathcal{H}^{k}_{\infty}(E)
the
(
s
-dimensional) Hausdorff content
of
E
.
The
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
of
E
is defined as

\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)=\sup_{\delta>0}\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\delta}(E).

We let
\dim_{H}(X)
be the infimum of those values of
s
for which
\mathcal{H}^{s}(X)=0
. If no such
s
exists, we let
\dim_{H}(X)=\infty
. The quantity
\dim_{H}(X)
is called the Hausdorff dimension of
X
.

Given a measure space
(R,\mu)
, and
f_{i},f
measurable functions valued in some metric space
(X,d)
, we say that
f_{i}\to f
in measure, if for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\mu(\{x\in R:d(f_{i}(x),f(x))\geq\varepsilon\})\to 0.

We recall the basic fact that if
\mu
is
\sigma
-finite, then this notion of convergence is induced by a metric.
The following averaging lemma is the main tool we use to show convergence in measure.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose
(R,\mu)
is a probability space and
A\in[0,1]
. Whenever
\xi\colon R\to\mathbb{R}
a measurable function such that

\xi\leq 1\quad\text{a.e.}\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{R}\xi\mathrm{d}\mu\geq 1-A,

then

\mu\{(x\in R:\xi(x)>1-\sqrt{A})\}\geq 1-\sqrt{A}.

Proof.

Let us denote
\lambda=\mu\{(x\in R:\xi(x)>1-\sqrt{A})\}
. Then we have

1-A\leq\int_{R}\xi\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\{(x\in R:\xi(x)>1-\sqrt{A})\}}\xi\mathrm{d}\mu+\int_{\{(x\in R:\xi(x)\leq 1-\sqrt{A})\}}\xi\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\lambda+(1-\lambda)(1-\sqrt{A}).

Using elementary arithmetic yields

\lambda\geq 1-\sqrt{A}.

∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(R,\Sigma)
be a measurable space and suppose
\mu
and
\nu
are finite measures on
R
with
\mu\ll\nu
.
To each
\alpha>0
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that whenever
\tilde{\nu}
is another finite measure on
R
with
\tilde{\nu}\leq\nu
and
(\nu-\tilde{\nu})(R)\leq\varepsilon
, then there is a measurable set
E\subset R
such that
\mu(R\setminus E)\leq\alpha
and
\mu_{|E}\ll\tilde{\nu}
.

Proof.

Let
g=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\in L^{1}(\nu)
. For any
r>0
, there exists
t_{0}>0
such that if
G\subset R
is measurable and
\nu(G)\leq t_{0}
, then

\mu(G)=\int_{G}g\mathrm{d}\nu\leq\alpha.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be such that
\sqrt{\varepsilon\nu(R)}<t_{0}
. Let
f=\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{\nu}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}
. Then, by our assumption,

f\leq 1\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{\nu(R)}\int f\mathrm{d}\nu\geq 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{\nu(R)}.

Thus, by Lemma
2.2
, the set
E=\{x\in R:f(x)\geq 1-\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\nu(R)}}\}
satisfies

\frac{1}{\nu(R)}\nu(E)\geq 1-\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\nu(R)}},

i.e.

\nu(R\setminus E)\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon\nu(R)}.

Whence, by our assumption on
\varepsilon
,
\mu(R\setminus E)\leq\alpha
. On the other hand, since
f
is bounded away from zero on
E
, it is the case that
\nu_{|E}\ll\tilde{\nu}_{|E}
, which implies
\mu_{|E}\ll\tilde{\nu}
.
∎

2.2
Hausdorff distance

Let
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
be metric spaces. On the product
X\times Y
we shall convene to define the maximum product metric:

d_{X\times Y}((x_{0},y_{0}),(x_{1},y_{1}))=d((x_{0},y_{0}),(x_{1},y_{1}))=\max\{d_{X}(x_{0},x_{1}),d_{Y}(y_{0},y_{1})\}.

For a subset
E\subset X
and
r\geq 0
, we let

B_{X}(E,r)=B(E,r)=\{x\in X:\operatorname{{dist}}(x,y)\leq r\}.

For a pair of non-empty subsets
E,F\subset X
, we define their
Hausdorff distance
by

d_{H(X)}(E,F)=\max\{\inf\{r\geq 0:E\subset B(F,r)\},\inf\{r\geq 0:F\subset B(E,r)\}\}.

By
H(X)
we denote the collection of non-empty closed
bounded
subsets of
X
. With this notation,
(H(X),d_{H(X)})
is a metric space. If
X
is complete, separable or compact, respectively, so is
H(X)

[
3
]
. The following lemma follows immediately from the definition of the maximum product metric.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose
X,Y
are metric spaces and let
p_{X}\colon X\times Y\to X
and
p_{Y}\colon X\times Y\to Y
be the coordinate projections. Let
E,F\subset X\times Y
be non-empty and suppose
r\geq 0
is such that

\begin{split}&B_{X}(p_{X}(E),r)\supset p_{X}(F);\\
&B_{Y}(p_{Y}(E),r)\supset p_{Y}(F).\end{split}

Then
B_{X\times Y}(E,r)\supset F
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Suppose
X,Y
are metric spaces.
The identity map

\operatorname{Id}\colon H(X)\times H(Y)\to H(X\times Y)

given by

\operatorname{Id}(E,F)=E\times F\quad\text{for $(E,F)\in H(X)\times H(Y)$}

is an isometry.

Proof.

Let
E,G\in H(X)
and
F,A\in H(Y)
. From Lemma
2.4
, it follows that

d_{H(X\times Y)}(E\times F,G\times A)\leq\max\{d_{H(X)}(E,G),d_{H(Y)}(F,A)\}.

The converse inequality is an immediate consequence of the fact that the projection maps
p_{X}
and
p_{Y}
are
1
-Lipschitz, i.e.

d_{X}(p_{X}(x_{0},y_{0}),p_{X}(x_{1},y_{1}))\leq d_{X\times Y}((x_{0},y_{0}),(x_{1},y_{1})),

and similarly for
p_{Y}
.
∎

2.3
Spaces of Lipschitz maps and residuality

Suppose
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
are metric spaces and
f\colon X\to Y
is a map. We call the least number
L
such that

d_{Y}(f(x_{0}),f(x_{1}))\leq Ld_{X}(x_{0},x_{1})\quad\text{for all $x_{0},x_{1}\in X$,}

the
Lipschitz constant
of
f
and denote it by
\textnormal{Lip}(f)
. If
\textnormal{Lip}(f)
is finite, we call
f
Lipschitz and for
L\in[0,\infty)
, we say that
f
is
L
-Lipschitz if
\textnormal{Lip}(f)\leq L
.

Recall the McShane extension theorem which asserts that whenever
X
is a metric space,
E\subset X
and
f\colon E\to\mathbb{R}
is
L
-Lipschitz, then there exists an extension of
f
to
X
which is also
L
-Lipschitz.

Suppose now
X
is a metric space and
(Y,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Y})
is a normed linear space. For
L\in[0,\infty)
we define the metric space

\textnormal{Lip}_{L}(X,Y)=\{f\colon X\to Y:f\;\text{is $L$-Lipschitz and bounded}\}

equipped with the supremum metric

d(f,g)=\lVert f-g\rVert_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in X}\lVert g(x)-f(x)\rVert_{Y}.

If
Y
is complete, then so is
\textnormal{Lip}_{L}(X,Y)
.

Given a metric space
X
a set
H\subset X
is called
residual
, if it contains a countable intersection of dense and open sets. Recall the Baire category theorem, which asserts that if
X
is complete and
H\subset X
is residual, then
H
is dense. In particular, if
Y
is complete, then residual subsets of
\textnormal{Lip}_{L}(X,Y)
are dense.

2.4
Curve fragments and Alberti representations

Let us fix a metric space
X
.
We let

\Gamma(X)=\{\gamma:\operatorname{Dom}\gamma\to X:\gamma\in\textnormal{Lip}(\operatorname{Dom}\gamma,X);\;\operatorname{Dom}\gamma\subset[0,1]\}.

The elements of the set
\Gamma(X)
are called
Lipschitz curve fragments
. Note that formally, by the set-theoretic definition of a function, each
\gamma\in\Gamma(X)
is a closed subset of
X\times[0,1]
. Thus, we equip
\Gamma(X)
with the
Hausdorff metric
inherited from
H(X\times[0,1])
. We denote the metric by
d_{\Gamma(X)}
.
We define the space

P(X)=\{(\gamma,t)\in\Gamma(X)\times[0,1]:t\in\operatorname{Dom}\gamma\}.

and equip it with the product metric inherited from
\Gamma(X)\times[0,1]
.

The notion of a curve fragment will later allow us to introduce Alberti representations and weak tangent fields, which are geometric notions which form the basis of the present paper. However, more set-theoretical aspects of the theory need to be developed first. This is made imperative by our efforts is Section
5
, where the geometric idea of what we do is relatively clear (and essentially contained in Subsection
5.1
), however, the rigorous proofs require us to show measurability of certain operations. This is by no means obvious and requires the theory which we introduce in the remainder of this subsection and the subsequent subsection.

The main point being that given a measure
\eta
on the space
\gamma
, we shall need to do a kind of restriction of
\eta
-a.e.
\gamma
to some subset of
\operatorname{Dom}(\gamma)
. The problem is that the particular subset to which we restrict could, in principle, vary wildly from one curve fragment to another. The two main tools we use are the following lemma and the “natural” embedding of
\Gamma(X)
into
P(\Gamma(X))
introduced below.

Lemma 2.6

(Lemma 3.6
[
8
]
)
.

Suppose
X
is a Polish metric space and
K\subset X
is compact. Let

\Gamma_{K}(X)=\{\gamma\in\Gamma(X):\gamma^{-1}(K)\not=\emptyset\}.

Then
\Gamma_{K}(X)
is closed and the map
R_{K}\colon\Gamma_{K}(X)\to\Gamma(X)
given by

R_{K}(\gamma)=\gamma_{|\gamma^{-1}(K)}

is a Borel map. In particular, given any compact set
C\supset K
, the map
R_{K}\colon\Gamma_{K}(X)\to\Gamma(C)
is Borel.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
X
be a complete and separable metric space. The function
\mathcal{H}^{1}\colon\Gamma(X)\to[0,\infty)
given by
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma)=\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma(\operatorname{Dom}\gamma))
is Borel. In fact, it is a pointwise limit of upper semi-continuous maps.

Proof.

Firstly, recall that for any metric space
M
, any set
A\subset M
, and any
\delta>0
one may alternatively define the Hausdorff content via

\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\delta}(A)=\inf\{\sum_{i}\operatorname{diam}A_{i}:A\subset\bigcup_{i}A_{i},\;\text{$A_{i}$ are open}\}.

Thus, the map

\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\delta}\colon H(M)\to[0,\infty]

is upper semi-continuous at each compact
A\in H(M)
. Indeed, let
A_{i}
be an admissible cover of
A
. Then, by compactness, there is a finite subcover
A_{i_{1}},\dots,A_{i_{n}}
. It follows that there is some
r>0
such that
B(A,r)\subset\bigcup_{i}A_{i}
. Thus, if
B\subset M
has Hausdorff distance from
A
at most
r
, it follows that
A_{i}
also forms an admissable cover of
B
. In particular, if
B_{k}
is a sequence of elements of
H(M)
converging to
A
, it holds that

\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\delta}(A)\geq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\delta}(B_{k}).

Suppose now
\gamma_{k},\gamma\in\Gamma(X)
and
\gamma_{k}\to\gamma
in
\Gamma(X)
. Then by definition also
\gamma_{k}(\operatorname{Dom}\gamma_{k})\to\gamma(\operatorname{Dom}\gamma)
in
H(X)
. Thus, using the above paragraph for
M=X
, it follows that

\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\delta}(\gamma(\operatorname{Dom}(\gamma)))\geq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\delta}(\gamma_{k}(\operatorname{Dom}\gamma_{k})).

That is,
\gamma\mapsto\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\delta}(\gamma(\operatorname{Dom}(\gamma)))
is upper semi-continuous on
\Gamma(X)
. It follows that
\mathcal{H}^{1}
, defined as above, is a pointwise limit of upper semi-continuous maps and therefore it is Borel.
∎

Combination of the two previous lemmas implies that for any compact set
K\subset X
, the functional

\gamma\mapsto\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma\cap K)

is Borel.

Definition 2.8
.

Let

.

One can verify that for any Borel set

, the map

\gamma\mapsto\int_{\gamma^{-1}(E)}|\gamma^{\prime}|\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}

is
\eta
-measurable and the formula

B(\eta)(E)=\int_{\Gamma(X)}\int_{\gamma^{-1}(E)}|\gamma^{\prime}|\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}\mathrm{d}\eta(\gamma),\quad\text{for any Borel set $E\subset X$,}

(2.1)

holds. The formula (

Definition 2.9
.

Let

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
X
be a metric space and consider the map
E\colon\Gamma(X)\to\Gamma(P(X))
given by

\begin{split}&\operatorname{Dom}(E(\gamma))=\operatorname{Dom}\gamma\quad\text{for $\gamma\in\Gamma(X)$},\\
&E(\gamma)(t)=(\gamma,t)\in P(X)\quad\text{for $\gamma\in\Gamma(X)$ and $t\in\operatorname{Dom}(E(\gamma))$.}\end{split}

The map
E
is an isometry.

Proof.

Using Lemma
2.5
and the definition of
E
, for any
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}\in\Gamma(X)
, we have

\begin{split}&d_{\Gamma(P(X))}(E(\gamma_{0}),E(\gamma_{1}))\\
&=d_{H(P(X)\times[0,1])}(\{\gamma_{0}\}\times\operatorname{Dom}(\gamma_{0})\times\operatorname{Dom}(\gamma_{0}),\{\gamma_{1}\}\times\operatorname{Dom}(\gamma_{1})\times\operatorname{Dom}(\gamma_{1}))\\
&=\max\{d_{H(P(X))}(\{\gamma_{0}\}\times\operatorname{Dom}(\gamma_{0}),\{\gamma_{1}\}\times\operatorname{Dom}{\gamma_{1}}),d_{H([0,1])}(\operatorname{Dom}(\gamma_{0}),\operatorname{Dom}(\gamma_{1}))\}\end{split}

However, since
P(X)
is a subset of
\Gamma(X)\times[0,1]
, it follows from Lemma
2.5
, that the last expression above is equal to

\begin{split}&\max\{d_{H(\Gamma(X))}(\{\gamma_{0}\},\{\gamma_{1}\}),d_{H([0,1])}(\operatorname{Dom}(\gamma_{0}),\operatorname{Dom}(\gamma_{1})),d_{H([0,1])}(\operatorname{Dom}(\gamma_{0}),\operatorname{Dom}(\gamma_{1}))\}\\
&=\max\{d_{\Gamma(X)}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}),d_{H([0,1])}(\operatorname{Dom}(\gamma_{0}),\operatorname{Dom}(\gamma_{1}))\}=d_{\Gamma(X)}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}),\end{split}

where the first inequality uses the fact that the Hausdorff distance of singletons agrees with the distance of their elements and the second equality uses the definition of distance in
\Gamma(X)
.
∎

Suppose
X
is a metric space and
(Y,d)
is another metric space,
E\subset X
is closed and
\mathcal{E}_{i},\mathcal{E}\colon P(X)\to Y
are Borel measurable maps. We say that the sequence
\mathcal{E}_{i}

m
-converges to

on

, and write
\mathcal{E}_{i}\overset{m}{\to}\mathcal{E}
on

, if for every

, the functions
\mathcal{E}_{i}(\gamma,\cdot)
converge in measure to
\mathcal{E}(\gamma,\cdot)
on

.

Lemma 2.11
.

Suppose
E\subset X
is closed and
\mathcal{E}_{i},\mathcal{E}\colon P(X)\to Y
are Borel measurable maps with
\mathcal{E}_{i}\overset{m}{\to}\mathcal{E}
on
E
. Let
\eta
be a finite Borel measure on
\Gamma(X)
which is supported on
\Gamma(E)
. Then
\mathcal{E}_{i}\to\mathcal{E}
in measure on
P(X)
with respect to the measure
(\eta\times\mathcal{H}^{1}_{[0,1]})_{|P(X)}
.

Proof.

Fix
\delta>0
. Consider for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
the function
F^{i}_{\delta}\colon\Gamma(X)\to\mathbb{R}
given by

F^{i}_{\delta}(\gamma)=\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{t\in\operatorname{Dom}\gamma:|\mathcal{E}_{i}(\gamma,t)-\mathcal{E}(\gamma,t)|\geq\delta\}).

Since each
F^{i}_{\delta}
is the measure of a slice of the Borel measurable set
(|\mathcal{E}_{i}-\mathcal{E}|)^{-1}((-\infty,-\delta]\cup[\delta,\infty))
, Fubini’s theorem implies that each
F^{i}_{\delta}
is
\eta
-measurable and, moreover,

(\eta\times\mathcal{H}^{1}_{[0,1]})(\{(\gamma,t):|\mathcal{E}_{i}(\gamma,t)-\mathcal{E}(\gamma,t)|\geq\delta\})=\int_{\Gamma(X)}F^{i}_{\delta}(\gamma)\mathrm{d}\eta(\gamma).

(2.2)

Since
\mathcal{E}_{i}\overset{m}{\to}\mathcal{E}
on
E
,
F^{i}_{\delta}\to 0
pointwise on
\Gamma(E)
. By Egorov’s theorem, since
\eta
is supported on
\Gamma(E)
, to each
\varepsilon>0
, we find some Borel set
\mathcal{U}\subset\Gamma(E)
such that

\eta(\Gamma(X)\setminus\mathcal{U})<\varepsilon

(2.3)

and
F^{i}_{\delta}\to 0
uniformly on
\mathcal{U}
. Thus, for every
r>0
, we find some
i_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
i\geq i_{0}
,

F^{i}_{\delta}(\gamma)\leq r\quad\text{for all $\gamma\in\mathcal{U}$}.

(2.4)

Using (
2.2
), we have

\begin{split}(\eta\times\mathcal{H}^{1}_{[0,1]})(\{(\gamma,t):&|\mathcal{E}_{i}(\gamma,t)-\mathcal{E}(\gamma,t)|\geq\delta\})=\int_{\mathcal{U}}F^{i}_{\delta}\mathrm{d}\eta+\int_{\Gamma(X)\setminus\mathcal{U}}F^{i}_{\delta}\mathrm{d}\eta\\
&\overset{\eqref{E:curve-uniform},\eqref{E:curve-small-set}}{\leq}r\eta(\mathcal{U})+\eta(\Gamma(X)\setminus\mathcal{U})\leq r\eta(\Gamma(X))+\varepsilon.\end{split}

Since

was arbitrary and

is finite, the statement follows by sending

∎

2.5
Restriction operators

Definition 2.12
.

Let
X
be a metric space and
\mathcal{U}\subset\Gamma(X)
. A map
\mathfrak{R}\colon\mathcal{U}\to\Gamma(X)
is called a
restriction on
\mathcal{U}
, if for every
\gamma\in\mathcal{U}
,
\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{R}(\gamma))\subset\operatorname{Dom}(\gamma)
and for each
t\in\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{R}(\gamma))
it holds that
\mathfrak{R}(\gamma)(t)=\gamma(t)
. The restriction
\mathfrak{R}
is called Borel if both
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathfrak{R}\colon\mathcal{U}\to\Gamma(X)
are Borel.
The
relative density
of the restriction
\mathfrak{R}
is the map
\mathcal{D}\colon\mathcal{U}\to[0,1]
given by

\mathcal{D}(\gamma)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathfrak{R(\gamma)})}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma)}&\text{if $\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma)>0$},\\[12.0pt]
1&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

Theorem 2.13
.

Suppose
X
is a complete and separable metric space and
\mathfrak{R}\colon\mathcal{U}\to\Gamma(X)
is a Borel restriction. Then the relative density
\mathcal{D}
of
\mathfrak{R}
is Borel. Suppose
\eta
is a finite Borel measure on
\Gamma(X)
, supported inside
\mathcal{U}
. Then

\begin{split}&B(\mathfrak{R}_{\#}\eta)\leq B(\eta);\\
&(B(\eta)-B(\mathfrak{R}_{\#}\eta))(X)=\int_{\mathcal{U}}(1-\mathcal{D}(\gamma))\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma)\mathrm{d}\eta(\gamma).\end{split}

Proof.

The fact that the relative density is Borel follows from the fact that it is a composition of
\mathfrak{R}
and
\mathcal{H}^{1}
, which are both Borel (by Lemma
2.7
).
Since curve fragments
\gamma
with
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma)=0
contribute nothing to the barycenter, it is without loss of generality to assume that
\mathcal{U}
contains only curve fragments with positive length. Then
B(\mathfrak{R}_{\#}\eta)\leq B(\eta)
follows immediately from the definition of restriction. Moreover,

B(\mathfrak{R}_{\#}(\eta))(X)=\int_{\Gamma(X)}\int_{\gamma}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{R}_{\#}(\eta)(\gamma)=\int_{\mathcal{U}}\int_{\mathfrak{R}(\gamma)}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}\mathrm{d}\eta(\gamma),

and, since
\eta
is suported inside
\mathcal{U}
,

B(\eta)(X)=\int_{\mathcal{U}}\int_{\gamma}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}\mathrm{d}\eta(\gamma).

Substracting these quantities yields

(B(\eta)-B(\mathfrak{R}_{\#}(\eta)))(X)=\int_{\mathcal{U}}(\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma)-\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathfrak{R}(\gamma)))\mathrm{d}\eta(\gamma)=\int_{\mathcal{U}}(1-\mathcal{D}(\gamma))\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma)\mathrm{d}\eta(\gamma).

∎

Corollary 2.14
.

Suppose
X
is a complete and separable metric space and
\mathfrak{R}_{i}\colon\mathcal{U}\to\Gamma(X)
is a sequence of Borel restrictions whose relative densities
\mathcal{D}_{i}
converge to
1
pointwise in
\mathcal{U}
. Suppose
\eta
is a finite Borel measure in
\Gamma(X)
supported on
\mathcal{U}
. Suppose further that every
\gamma\in\mathcal{U}
satisfies
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma)>0
and that there is some
L<\infty
such that every
\gamma\in\mathcal{U}
is
L
-Lipschitz. Then
(B((\mathfrak{R}_{i})_{\#}\eta)-B(\eta))(X)\to 0
.

Proof.

Since the domain of each
\gamma\in\mathcal{U}
is contained in
[0,1]
, it follows that
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma)\leq L
for every
\gamma\in\mathcal{U}
. Thus, the sequence
(1-\mathcal{D}_{i})\mathcal{H}^{1}(\cdot)
, where each
\mathcal{D}_{i}
is the relative density of
\mathfrak{R}_{i}
, is a uniformly bounded sequence of Borel maps converging pointwise to
0
on
\mathcal{U}
. Since
\eta
is a finite Borel measure, by dominated convergence theorem, we have

\int_{\mathcal{U}}(1-\mathcal{D}_{i}(\gamma))\mathcal{H}^{1}(\gamma)\mathrm{d}\eta(\gamma)\to 0,

and so the statement follows from Theorem
2.13
.
∎

Finally, we shall introduce a way to obtain restriction maps which are guaranteed to be Borel. This is obtained by considering restrictions which arise as
slices
of compact sets in
P(X)
.

Theorem 2.15
.

Suppose
X
is a complete separable metric space and
\mathcal{K}\subset P(X)
is a compact set. Let
p\colon P(X)\to\Gamma(X)
be the projection map
p(\gamma,t)=\gamma
.
The set
\mathcal{U}=p(\mathcal{K})
is a compact subset of
\Gamma(X)
.
The map

\mathfrak{R}\colon\mathcal{U}\to\Gamma(X)

given by

\mathfrak{R}(\gamma)=\gamma_{|\{t\in\operatorname{Dom}\gamma:(\gamma,t)\in\mathcal{K}\}}

is a well defined, Borel restriction on
\mathcal{U}
.

Proof.

The validity of the definition follows from the fact that since
\mathcal{K}
is compact, also any set of the form

\{t\in\operatorname{Dom}\gamma:(\gamma,t)\in\mathcal{K}\}

is compact. Since the map
p
is continuous,
\mathcal{U}
is a continuous image of a compact set and thus compact. We have

\mathfrak{R}=E^{-1}\circ R\circ E,

where
E\colon\Gamma(X)\to\Gamma(P(X))
is defined in Lemma
2.10
and
R
is given by

R(\Phi)=\Phi\cap\mathcal{K}=\Phi_{|\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{K})}\quad\text{for $\Phi\in\Gamma(P(X))$ with $\Phi\cap\mathcal{K}\not=\emptyset$.}

By Lemma
2.6
, the map
R
is Borel. Since
E
is an isometry and
R
maps

into

, it follows that
\mathfrak{R}
is Borel.
∎

2.6
Cones and independence

Definition 2.16
.

Let
V
be a finite dimensional Banach space. For

3
Perturbations of measures to sets of low dimension

The purpose of this section is to prove the density statement in Theorem
1.4
(recall that when showing a set is residual, one always needs to prove
density
and
openness
of certain sets).
That is, given a measure
\mu
on a metric space
X
, we show that any map on
X
can be approximated with maps that squash a very large
\mu
-proportion of
X
into a small set.

The first subsection concerns a construction of a map that perturbs a measure on the real line. In Subsection
3.2
, this is combined with Proposition
2.26
to obtain mappings of measures on general metric spaces. Explicitly, we use the aforementioned proposition to obtain a map
f
from the metric space
X
into
\mathbb{R}
and then perturb the measure
f_{\#}\mu
via a map
g\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. The particular properties of the two constructions result in a map
g\circ f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
which “squashes” most of
\mu
and is
1
-Lipschitz.

In Subsection
3.3
, we work with maps into finite dimensional Banach spaces “coordinate by coordinate”.
By using the results of the previous subsections, we obtain maps that squash a small piece of
\mu
(i.e. we obtain
local
results). Finally, in Subsection
3.4
we use a particular “glueing” technique from
[
10
]
to glue the local results into global ones.

It is essential that the map
g\circ f
obtained in the first two subsections is 1-Lipschitz.
This is to ensure that function constructed in the latter subsections lies in
\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(X,\mathbb{R}^{m})
.
A very subtle point of our construction is that the map
g
is
not
1-Lipschitz.
Indeed, for sometime we sought a proof for which
g
is 1-Lipschitz, so that
g\circ f
is automatically 1-Lipschitz.
However, it is not clear to us how to obtain such a
g
.
Instead, in the construction presented below, we permit
g
to expand the distance between points, but only when this stretching is compensated for by
f
.

3.1
Concentrating measure in the real line

We begin with a construction that concentrates measure on the real line into a finite set, with a function that approximates the identity and which is required to be
1
-Lipschitz on scales which are not too small.
On the other hand, we allow the Lipschitz constant to be very large locally, but we require that the size of the neighbourhoods on which this happens is arbitrarily small.

Suppose
R\in(0,\infty)
,
N\in\mathbb{N},N\geq 2
,
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
are given. Define, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, the intervals

\begin{split}&I_{k}=I_{k}(R,N,t_{0})=[t_{0}+kR,t_{0}+kR+\tfrac{R}{N}),\\
&J_{k}=J_{k}(R,N,t_{0})=[t_{0}+kR+\tfrac{R}{N},t_{0}+(k+1)R).\end{split}

These intervals form a partition of
\mathbb{R}
as we vary
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Furthermore, if
R
and
N
are fixed, then the intervals

I_{k}(R,N,i\tfrac{R}{N}),\quad i\in\{0,\dots,N-1\},k\in\mathbb{Z}

form a partition of
\mathbb{R}
. Consequently,
given any finite Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
and any
R\in(0,\infty)
,
N\geq 2
, there exists
t_{0}\in[0,R)
such that

\mu(\bigcup_{k}I_{k}(R,N,t_{0}))\leq\frac{1}{N}\mu(\mathbb{R}).

(3.1)

We define

\varphi_{R,N,t_{0}}(t)=\begin{cases}N&\text{if $t\in I_{k}(R,N,t_{0})$ for some $k\in\mathbb{Z}$},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and we let

g_{R,N,t_{0}}(t)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\varphi(s)\mathrm{d}s.

We will write
\varphi
instead of
\varphi_{R,N,t_{0}}
and
g
instead of
g_{R,N,t_{0}}
whenever the relevant parameters are clear from the context.
Note that
g
maps
\bigcup_{k}J_{k}
into a countable set.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
R\in(0,\infty)
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
and all
t\in\mathbb{R}
it holds that

|g(t)-t|\leq|t_{0}|+R.

(3.2)

Proof.

For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
k\geq 0
we have

I_{k}\cup J_{k}=[t_{0}+kR,t_{0}+(k+1)R)

and for
t\in I_{k}\cup J_{k}
, we have,
by definition,

kR=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+kR}\varphi(s)\mathrm{d}s\leq g(t)\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+(k+1)R}\varphi(s)\mathrm{d}s=(k+1)R.

That is,
g(t)\in[kR,(k+1)R]
, which implies (
3.2
). The case
k<0
is proven analogously.
∎

Observation 3.2
.

For any
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
R\in(0,\infty)
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the function
g
is
N
-Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
R\in(0,\infty)
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
and any
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
with
|s-t|\geq 2R
, it holds that

\frac{|g(s)-g(t)|}{|s-t|}\leq\frac{\lfloor\frac{1}{R}|s-t|\rfloor+1}{\lfloor\frac{1}{R}|s-t|\rfloor-1}

(3.3)

Proof.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\geq 2
and let
k_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
satisfy
k_{1}-k_{0}=n
. Suppose
s\in I_{k_{0}}\cup J_{k_{0}}
,
t\in I_{k_{1}}\cup J_{k_{1}}
.
Now we consider what happens at the endpoints of the intervals.
We have
s<t
and

s\leq t_{0}+(k_{0}+1)R\quad\text{and}\quad t\geq t_{0}+k_{1}R,

altogether:

|s-t|\geq|t_{0}+k_{1}R-t_{0}+(k_{0}+1)R|=nR-R.

Similarly, using the fact that
g
is non-decreasing,

|g(s)-g(t)|\leq|g(t_{0}+(k_{1}+1)R)-g(t_{0}+k_{0}R)|=nR+R.

Thus,

\frac{|g(s)-g(t)|}{|s-t|}\leq\frac{nR+R}{nR-R}=\frac{n+1}{n-1}.

(3.4)

For
t\in\mathbb{R}
, define
k(t)\in\mathbb{Z}
to be the unique integer for which
t\in I_{k(t)}\cup J_{k(t)}
.
For
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
, let
n(s,t)=|k(t)-k(s)|
. Observe that if
|t-s|\geq 2R
, then
n(s,t)\geq 2
. Thus, (
3.4
) implies that, whenever
|s-r|\geq 2R
,

\frac{|g(s)-g(t)|}{|s-t|}\leq\frac{n(s,t)+1}{n(s,t)-1}.

(3.5)

As the length of each
I_{k}\cup J_{k}
is
R
, we have

n(s,t)\geq\lfloor\tfrac{1}{R}|s-t|\rfloor.

Thus, by (
3.5
), using also the fact that
n\mapsto\frac{n+1}{n-1}
is decreasing for
n\geq 2
, (
3.3
) holds.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

For any fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}
with
N\geq 2
and
r>0
, one has

\lim_{R\to 0_{+}}\sup_{|s-t|\geq r}\frac{|g_{R,N,t_{0}}(s)-g_{R,N,t_{0}}(t)|}{|s-t|}=1,

the expression being independent of
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

The inequality “
\leq
” follows from Lemma
3.3
. For the converse, suppose
R\leq r
and consider
s=t_{0}
,
t=t_{0}+R
. Then
g(s)=0
and
g(t)=R
, whence

\sup_{|s-t|\geq r}\frac{|g_{R,N,t_{0}}(s)-g_{R,N,t_{0}}(t)|}{|s-t|}\geq 1.

∎

Theorem 3.5
.

For any
\eta>0
, there exists
L\in[1,\infty)
such that the following holds. Given any
D>0
,
r>0
, any Borel probability measure
\mu
on
[-D,D]
and any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a map

such that

(i)

\lVert h-\operatorname{Id}\rVert_{\infty}<\varepsilon
,

(ii)

is

-Lipschitz,

(iii)

|h(s)-h(t)|\leq|s-t|
whenever

satisfy

,

(iv)

there is some compact set

such that
\mu(E)\geq 1-\eta
and

is finite.

Proof.

Find
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\frac{1}{N}\leq\eta
and
let
L=N
.
Given any

, if
|g(t)-t|<\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon
for

, then

|(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})g(t)-t|<\varepsilon\quad\text{for all $t\in[-D,D]$.}

(3.6)

By Lemma

, there is some
R>0
such that

\sup_{|s-t|\geq r}\frac{|g_{R,N,t_{0}}(s)-g_{R,N,t_{0}}(t)|}{|s-t|}<\frac{1}{1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}},

(3.7)

for any

. Let

be such that (

) holds and

.
Let

be such that (

) holds. Denote

and

. Using Lemma

, we have

so that via (

) we have

Using the estimate (

), we have for

By definition of

is finite, and therefore also

While

might not be compact, we can simply take

, which is closed (as

The fact that

It remains to show

∎

3.2
Construction of a real-valued perturbation that concentrates measure into finite sets

In the previous subsection we constructed a map which, while
1
-Lipschitz on scales which are not too small, can have a very large Lipschitz constant due to points at small scales. In order to utilize this construction, we combine it with Proposition
2.26
.
We next show that that the good properties of the aforementioned proposition can be preserved after extending the relevant maps.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
X
be a metric space,
\delta>0
and
\varphi\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
a Lipschitz map. For every
\varepsilon>0
, there is
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for any set
S\subset X
and
g\colon S\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

|g(y)-g(z)|\leq|\varphi(y)-\varphi(z)|+\delta d(y,z),

(3.8)

for all
y,z\in S
and

|g(x)-\varphi(x)|<\varepsilon_{0},

(3.9)

for all
x\in S
,
there exists an extension of
g
(denoted again by
g
) to
X
such that
(
3.8
) holds for all
y,z\in X
and (
3.9
) holds for all
x\in X
upon replacing
\varepsilon_{0}
with
\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Define

\mathfrak{d}(x,y)=|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|+\delta d(x,y),\quad\text{for $x,y\in X$.}

The map
\mathfrak{d}
is a metric on
X
.
There exist
\Delta>0
and
\varepsilon>\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that both

\varepsilon_{0}\leq\delta\Delta

(3.10)

and

\displaystyle\Delta(2\textnormal{Lip}(\varphi)+\delta)+\varepsilon_{0}<\varepsilon

(3.11)

hold.
Now let
g
satisfy (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) and define

G(x)=\begin{cases}g(x)&\text{if $x\in S$},\\
\varphi(x)&\text{if $x\in X\setminus B(S,\Delta)$}.\end{cases}

Take
x\in S
and
y\in X\setminus B(S,\Delta)
. Then, as (
3.10
) holds and
\Delta\leq d(x,y)
, (
3.9
) gives

|G(x)-G(y)|\leq|g(x)-\varphi(x)|+|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|\leq\varepsilon_{0}+|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|\leq\delta d(x,y)+|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|.

In other words,
G
is
1
-Lipschitz on its domain equipped with the metric
\mathfrak{d}
. Using McShane’s extension theorem, we may extend
G
to
X
(the extension being denoted again by
G
) in such a way that

|G(x)-G(y)|\leq|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|+\delta d(x,y)\quad\text{for all $x,y\in X$}.

Note that
G
is also an extension of
g
and so it suffices to show that

|G(x)-\varphi(x)|<\varepsilon\quad\text{for all $x\in X$.}

If
x\in S
, this follows by
\varepsilon>\varepsilon_{0}
, while if
x\in X\setminus B(S,\Delta)
, this is immediate since
G(x)=\varphi(x)
. Therefore, it remains to consider the case when
x\in B(S,\Delta)\setminus S
. Let
\eta>0
be such that

(\eta+\Delta)(2\textnormal{Lip}(f)+\delta)+\varepsilon_{0}<\varepsilon.

The existence of such an
\eta
is guaranteed by (
3.11
). Find

such that
d(x,y)<\Delta+\eta
. Then

\begin{split}|G(x)-\varphi(x)|&\leq|G(x)-G(y)|+|G(y)-\varphi(y)|+|\varphi(y)-\varphi(x)|\\
&\leq\delta d(x,y)+|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|+\varepsilon_{0}+|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|\\
&\leq\delta(\Delta+\eta)+\textnormal{Lip}(\varphi)(\Delta+\eta)+\varepsilon_{0}+\textnormal{Lip}(\varphi)(\Delta+\eta)\\
&\leq(\Delta+\eta)(2\textnormal{Lip}(\varphi)+\delta)+\varepsilon_{0}<\varepsilon.\end{split}

∎

Given two subsets
E,F
of a metric space
(X,d)
we use the notation

\operatorname{{dist}}(E,F)=\inf_{x\in E,y\in F}d(x,y).

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
S
be a compact metric space and,
\rho>0
,
\delta>0
and suppose that
f\colon S\to\mathbb{R}

3.3
Obtaining local vector-valued perturbations from the real valued perturbations

In this subsection we follow, to the word, the definitions and conventions of
[
10
, Section 4]
. The approach to obtain vector-valued perturbations given well-behaved real-valued perturbations is the same as in the aforementioned paper. We omit some of the details and justifications, as they can all be found therein.

Given a finite-dimensional vector space
V
and a basis
b_{1},\dots,b_{m}
of
V
consisting of unit vectors, we denote by
b^{*}_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
the
i
-th coordinate functional:

b^{*}_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}b_{j}\right)=\lambda_{i}.

We let
K_{u}
be the minimal constant
K
such that the inequality

\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{m}l_{i}b_{i}^{*}(x)b_{i}\rVert\leq K\lVert l\rVert_{\infty}\lVert x\rVert_{V}

holds for every
x\in V
and
l\in\ell^{m}_{\infty}
. The quantity
K_{u}
is always finite and
K_{u}=1
if
V=\ell_{p}^{m}
for any
p\in[1,\infty]
.

Definition 3.9
.

For
V
a finite dimensional Banach space and an integer
d\geq 0
, we let
\tilde{K}(V,d)
be the least
K\geq 1
for which the following is true:
There exists
K_{d},K_{p}>0
and, for any
d
-dimensional subspace
W
of
V
, a basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}
of
V
consisting of unit vectors and projections
P\colon V\to W
and
Q\colon V\to\ker P
such that:

(i)

P(x)+Q(x)=x
for all
x\in V
;

(ii)

\|b_{i}^{*}\|\leq K_{p}
for each
1\leq i\leq m
;

(iii)

\|P\|,\|Q\|\leq K_{d}
;

(iv)

For each
x\in V
and
l\in\ell_{\infty}^{m}
with
\|l\|_{\infty}\leq 1
,

\left\|P(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}l_{i}b_{i}^{*}(Q(x))b_{i}\right\|\leq K\|x\|.

We remark that one always has

\tilde{K}(V,d)\leq K_{u}(2\sqrt{d}+1),

therefore the quantity
\tilde{K}(V,d)
is always finite and in the case of
V=\ell^{m}_{p}
, the quantity is independent of the dimension
m
.

Observation 3.10

(
[
10
]
, Observation 4.2)
.

For any

and

,

\tilde{K}(\ell^{m}_{2},d)=1.

Observation 3.11

(
[
10
]
, Observation 4.3)
.

Suppose

admits a basis such that

. Then

\tilde{K}(V,0)=1.

In particular,

\tilde{K}(\ell^{p}_{m},0)=1,

for every
p\in[0,\infty]
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

For the remainder of this subsection, unless stated otherwise, we consider
V
to be a fixed finite-dimensional Banach space. We also fix
d\geq 0
and a
d
-dimensional subspace
W\subset V
. We assume that
K_{d},K_{p}>0
,
P
,
Q
,
b_{1},\dots,b_{m}
are as in Definition
3.9
.
We let
T_{i}=b_{i}^{*}\circ Q\colon V\to\mathbb{R}
.
The following is essentially the main content of
[
10
, Lemma 4.5]
.

Lemma 3.12
.

There is some
C_{V}>0
, depending only on

, such that the following is true.
Suppose

is a metric space and

.
Suppose that for each

In the proof of

, the function

is defined using a particular instance of functions

\mathcal{G}=\bigcap_{\varepsilon,\delta>0,\eta\in(0,1)}\mathcal{G}_{\delta,\eta,\varepsilon}.

The inclusion from left to right is immediate, we prove the converse inclusion. To that end, let
\delta>0
and assume

for every
\eta\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon>0
. Then, by definition, for every

, there exists a compact set

such that

\mu(S\setminus E(n,i,k))<\frac{1}{i}2^{-n}
,

(ii)

\mathcal{H}^{d+\delta}_{\infty}(f(E(n,i,k)))<2^{-i}\frac{1}{k}
.

We let

E_{\delta}=\bigcap_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}E(n,i,k).

Then
(i)
and
(ii)
imply

and

(b)

\mathcal{H}^{d+\delta}_{\infty}(f(E_{\delta}))=0

It follows that

, whence also

yields

Next, we show that for every
\delta,\varepsilon>0
,
\eta\in(0,1)
, the set

is open and dense in
\textnormal{Lip}_{L}(X,V)

Firstly, density. Fix

and suppose

. Since

is an
\tilde{A}(f,d)

\tilde{K}(V,d)=1
, for any

, we have from Theorem

(and Observation

) a function

and a compact set

This implies that

for every

and

. As

is arbitrary, this shows density of

in
\textnormal{Lip}_{L}(X,V)
.

To show openness, we write
\mathcal{K}(S)
for the family of compact subsets of

and

\mathcal{G}_{\delta,\eta,\varepsilon}=\bigcup_{E\in\mathcal{K}(X);\mu(E)\geq 1-\eta}\{f\in\textnormal{Lip}_{L}(X,V):\mathcal{H}^{d+\delta}_{\infty}(f(E))<\varepsilon\}.

Each of the sets inside the union on the right hand side is open due to Lemma
4.1
. Therefore, the set
\mathcal{G}_{\delta,\eta,\varepsilon}
is open.
Since we may write

\mathcal{G}=\bigcap_{\delta,\varepsilon>0,\eta\in(0,1)}\mathcal{G}_{\delta,\eta,\varepsilon}=\bigcap_{n,k,i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{G}_{\frac{1}{n},\frac{1}{k},\frac{1}{i}},

the set

is a countable intersection of open dense sets.

In the general case, unless
\mu
is trivial, in which case there is nothing to prove, it follows from our assumptions, that there exists a set
N\subset\tilde{N}\subset S
and
compact

\tilde{A}(F,d)
sets

such that

(1)

\mu(\tilde{N})=0
,

(2)

0<\mu(S_{i})<\infty
and

(3)

S\setminus\tilde{N}=\bigcup_{i}S_{i}
.

Denote
\mu_{i}=\frac{1}{\mu(S_{i})}\mu_{|S_{i}}
. As each
S_{i}
is compact and each
\mu_{i}
is a probability measure on

, we have already shown that the sets

\mathcal{F}_{i}=\{f\in\textnormal{Lip}_{L}(X,V):\dim_{H}(f_{\#}\mu_{i})\leq d\}

are residual

and thus, also the set

\mathcal{F}=\bigcap_{i}\mathcal{F}_{i}

is residual

. It remains to show

\{f\in\textnormal{Lip}_{L}(X,V):\dim_{H}(f_{\#}\mu)\leq d\}=\mathcal{F}.

The inclusion from left to right is immediate. Suppose

. Then, for each

, there is a

with

and such that

(4.1)

Let

∎

5
Stability of the number of Alberti representations under Lipschitz perturbations

The purpose of this section is to prove Theorem
1.5
. This is allows us to obtain the converse to Corollary
4.5
whenever the ambient space is Euclidean, namely Corollary
1.6
. Unfortunately, we are not able to do this in the setting of general metric spaces. The issue that arises is explained below in Subsection
5.1
. We obtain results for Euclidean spaces and also some results in the similar spirit for strictly convex finite dimensional Banach spaces.

5.1
On perturbations of Lipschitz curve fragments

In this subsection we assert a particular property of (small) perturbations of Lipschitz curve fragments. Roughly speaking, the property we require is that the perturbed curve goes in almost the same direction as the original curve for “most of the time” provided the perturbation is small enough.
The problem is that this particular property is not satisfied in general metric spaces. Geometrically, the crucial point is the following. Suppose
\gamma
is a unit speed curve in some normed linear space
X
with a uniformly convex unit ball. Suppose
\xi
is a
1
-Lipschitz curve, which is uniformly very close to
\gamma
(and having the same domain). Then on a large portion of the domain,
\gamma^{\prime}-\xi^{\prime}
is small. This fails without the convexity assumption. For example, in the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\infty}
equipped with the supremum norm, we can consider the pair of curves
\gamma
and
\xi
as in Figure
1
.

\gamma
\xi
\varepsilon

Figure 1:
The curve
\gamma
goes in the direction of
e_{1}
in the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\infty}
equipped with supremum norm.
The curve
\xi
is very close to
\gamma
uniformly, but the
directions
of
\gamma
and
\xi
disagree by a lot on their entire domain.
By refining the “frequency” of
\xi
, one may achieve
\lVert\gamma-\xi\rVert_{\infty}<\varepsilon
for arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
, but one always has
\gamma^{\prime}=e_{1}
and
\xi^{\prime}=e_{1}\pm e_{2}
a.e.

The reason this problem is “allowed” to happen is that the curve
\xi
in fact has length equal to the length of
\gamma
(note that the projection from the image of
\xi
to the image of
\gamma
is an isometry). Such a problem cannot happen in normed spaces whose unit ball is strictly convex.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
(X,|\cdot|)
be a finite dimensional Banach space.
Let
I=[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
be a non-degenerate interval and suppose
\varepsilon>0
. Suppose
\gamma\colon I\to X
is a Lipschitz curve with unit speed and let

u=\frac{\gamma(b)-\gamma(a)}{b-a}.

Let
f\colon\gamma\to X
be
1
-Lipschitz with
\lVert f-\operatorname{Id}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon
. Suppose
\varphi
is a linear functional on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of norm
1
satisfying also
\varphi(u)=|u|
.
Let

A=1-|u|+\frac{2\varepsilon}{b-a}.

Then

\frac{1}{b-a}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{t\in I:\varphi((f\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t))>1-\sqrt{A}\})\geq 1-\sqrt{A}.

Proof.

By the fundamental theorem of calculus, and using also the estimate on the uniform distance of
f
to the identity, we obtain

\int_{I}(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(t)\in B((b-a)u,2\varepsilon).

Thus, applying the linear map
\varphi
to both sides, using also linearity of integration and the fact that
\varphi(u)=|u|
, we arrive at

\int_{I}\varphi((f\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t))\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(t)\geq(b-a)|u|-2\varepsilon.

Thus, denoting
\xi(t)=\varphi((f\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t))
and dividing both sides by
b-a
, we obtain

\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{I}\xi\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}\geq|u|-\frac{2\varepsilon}{b-a}=1-A.

Since
\varphi
is of norm
1
,
\xi\leq 1

\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e. Therefore the required inequality follows from Lemma
2.2
.
∎

Definition 5.2
.

Let
(X,|\cdot|)
be a normed linear space.
Given a non-degenerate interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
and a Lipschitz curve
\gamma\colon I\to X
of unit speed, we call the number

\left|\frac{\gamma(b)-\gamma(a)}{b-a}\right|

the
slope
of
\gamma
.

We only define slope for curves of unit speed. Moreover if the slope of
\gamma
is equal to
1
, it is necessary that
\gamma
is a geodesic. Thus, if
X
has unique geodesics and
\gamma
has slope
1
, then
\gamma
must be a line segment.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
(X,|\cdot|)
be a finite dimensional Banach space.
Let
I=[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
be a non-degenerate interval. Suppose
\gamma\colon I\to X
is a
C^{1}
curve with unit speed and let
s<1
,
\varepsilon>0
. Then there exists a partition
a=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{k}=b
of
I
such that for every
i=1,\dots,n
, the slope of
\gamma_{|[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}
is greater than
s
and, moreover

|\gamma^{\prime}(t)-\frac{\gamma(t_{i})-\gamma(t_{i-1})}{t_{i}-t_{i-1}}|<\varepsilon\quad\text{for every $t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i}]$}

Proof.

Since the function
\gamma^{\prime}\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is continuous and
I
is compact,
\gamma^{\prime}
is uniformly continuous. It follows that for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a partition
a=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{k}=b
of
I
such that

\sup_{t,s\in[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}|\gamma^{\prime}(t)-\gamma^{\prime}(s)|\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{for each $i=1,\dots,n$.}

Let us denote

S_{i}=\frac{1}{t_{i}-t_{i-1}}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\gamma^{\prime}(t)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(t).

Then, by the fundamental theorem of calculus,
|S_{i}|
is the slope of
\gamma_{|[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}
.
We can estimate

|S_{i}-\gamma^{\prime}(t_{i})|=\left|\frac{1}{t_{i}-t_{i-1}}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\gamma^{\prime}(t)-\gamma^{\prime}(t_{i})\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(t)\right|\leq\frac{1}{t_{i}-t_{i-1}}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}|\gamma^{\prime}(t)-\gamma^{\prime}(t_{i})|\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(t)\leq\varepsilon.

Since
\gamma
has unit speed, it follows that
|\gamma^{\prime}(t_{i})|=1
and therefore, by the triangle inequality,
|S_{i}|\geq 1-\varepsilon
. Thus, the statement follows by possibly reducing
\varepsilon
so that
\varepsilon\leq 1-s
.
∎

Recall that a normed linear space
(X,\lVert\cdot\rVert)
, its unit ball denoted by
B_{X}
, is called
uniformly convex
if for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists some
\delta>0
such that whenever
x,y\in B_{X}
satisfy
\lVert x-y\rVert\geq\varepsilon
, then

\left\lVert\frac{x+y}{2}\right\rVert\leq 1-\delta.

The space
X
is called
strictly convex
if the unit sphere of
X
contains no line segments. If the space
X
is not strictly convex, then by taking for
x
and
y
any pair of distinct points on any line segment in the unit sphere, it follows that
X
is also not uniformly convex. In other words, uniformly convex spaces are strictly convex.

The following lemma has a standard proof relying on compactness of the unit ball in finite dimensional spaces.

Lemma 5.4
.

A finite dimensional normed linear space
X
is strictly convex if and only if it is uniformly convex.

Proposition 5.5
.

Suppose a normed linear space
X
is uniformly convex. Then to each
u
in
\partial B_{X}
, the unit sphere of
X
, a unit linear functional
\varphi_{u}\in X^{*}
can be found in such a way that the following holds. To each
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that whenever
u\in\partial B_{X}
,
v\in B_{X}
and
\varphi_{u}(u-v)<\delta
, then
\lVert u-v\rVert<\varepsilon
.

Proof.

By Hahn-Banach, for each
u\in\partial B_{X}
there exists a
\varphi_{u}\in X^{*}
such that
\varphi_{u}(u)=1
and
\lVert\varphi\rVert=1
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
and suppose
\delta>0
is from the definition of uniform convexity. That is, for any
u,v\in B_{X}
with
\lVert u-v\rVert\geq\varepsilon
, it holds that

\left\lVert\frac{u+v}{2}\right\rVert\leq 1-\delta.

(5.1)

Now let
u\in\partial B_{X}
and
v\in B_{X}
be such that

\varphi_{u}(u-v)<\delta.

(5.2)

For contradiction, let us also assume that
\lVert u-v\rVert\geq\varepsilon
. We may then use (
5.1
) together with
\lVert\varphi\rVert=1
to assert that

\varphi_{u}(u)+\varphi_{u}(v)\leq 2-2\delta.

(5.3)

Adding the inequalities (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) yields

2\varphi_{u}(u)<2-\delta.

This contradicts the fact that
\varphi_{u}(u)=1
.
∎

It can be immediately verified, that if
X
has finite dimension and satisfies the property in the conclusion of Proposition
5.5
, then it must be strictly convex, and therefore, by Lemma
5.4
, also uniformly convex.

Remark 5.6
.

The arguments in the remainder of this section, concerning the tangents of Lipschitz curves and directions of Alberti representations are carried out in the setting of strictly convex,
finite dimensional
Banach spaces
X
. In these spaces, the conclusion of Proposition
5.5
holds, which is of principal importance.
Since any uniformly convex Banach space
X
possesses the Radon-Nikodým property, an appropriate notion of a tangent to a Lipschitz curve
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to X
can be defined (and exist
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.). Moreover, Proposition
5.5
does
not
assume finite dimension of

. Therefore, it would also be possible to work in infinite dimensional uniformly convex Banach spaces. This extra generality, however, is not of so much importance and would require significant additional preliminaries, which is why we only work in the finite dimensional setting.

Theorem 5.7
.

Suppose
(X,|\cdot|)
is a finite dimensional strictly convex Banach space. Let
I=[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
be a non-degenerate interval and
\gamma\colon I\to X
a
C^{1}
curve of unit speed. Then, to each
\delta>0
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that the following holds. If
f\colon\gamma\to X
is
1
-Lipschitz and
\lVert f-\operatorname{Id}\rVert_{\infty}<\varepsilon
, then

\frac{1}{b-a}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{t\in I:|(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t)-\gamma^{\prime}(t)|\geq\delta\})\leq\delta.

(5.4)

Proof.

Let us denote by
B
the unit ball of
X
.
For
u\in\partial B
let
\varphi_{u}
be from Proposition
5.5
. There exists some
\delta\geq A>0
such that for any
u\in\partial B
and any
v\in B

\varphi_{u}(u-v)\leq\sqrt{A}\implies|u-v|\leq\frac{\delta}{3}.

Note that in particular, since
\varphi_{u}(u)=1
, we have

\varphi_{u}(v)\geq 1-\sqrt{A}\implies|u-v|\leq\frac{\delta}{3}.

(5.5)

Now let
s<1
be such that
s>1-A
and
1-s\leq\frac{\delta}{3}
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, using Lemma
5.3
, we can find a partition
a=t_{0}<\dots,<t_{k}=b
of
I
such that the slope of each
\gamma_{|[t_{i-1},t_{i}]}
is at least
s
and, moreover, if we denote

w_{i}=\frac{\gamma(t_{i})-\gamma(t_{i-1})}{t_{i}-t_{i-1}};\quad A_{i}=1-|w_{i}|+\frac{2\varepsilon}{t_{i}-t_{i-1}},

then

|w_{i}-\gamma^{\prime}(t)|\leq\frac{\delta}{3}\quad\text{for all $t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i}]$.}

(5.6)

Note that the condition on slopes is equivalent to
|w_{i}|\geq s
.
Let

r=\min_{i=1,\dots,k}t_{i}-t_{i-1}.

Finally, we choose the
\varepsilon>0
which is defined by

A=1-s+\frac{2\varepsilon}{r}.

Then
A_{i}\leq A
.
Let
u_{i}=\frac{w_{i}}{|w_{i}|}
and
\varphi_{i}=\varphi_{u_{i}}
. Then
\varphi_{i}(w_{i})=|w_{i}|
and thus we may use Lemma

to obtain

\frac{1}{t_{i}-t_{i-1}}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i}]:\varphi_{i}((f\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t))>1-\sqrt{A_{i}}\})\geq 1-\sqrt{A_{i}}.

Using (
5.5
),
A_{i}\leq A
and
\sqrt{A}\leq\delta
, this implies

\frac{1}{t_{i}-t_{i-1}}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i}]:|(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t)-u_{i}|\leq\frac{\delta}{3}\})\geq 1-\sqrt{A_{i}}\geq 1-\sqrt{A}\geq 1-\delta.

Thus, passing to the complement,

\frac{1}{t_{i}-t_{i-1}}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i}]:|(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t)-u_{i}|>\frac{\delta}{3}\})\leq\delta.

Now let

be such that
|(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t)-u_{i}|>\frac{\delta}{3}
. Then using the estimate (

),

and the triangle inequality yields

|(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t)-\gamma^{\prime}(t)|\leq|(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t)-u_{i}|+|u_{i}-w_{i}|+|w_{i}-\gamma^{\prime}(t)|\leq\frac{\delta}{3}+|1-s|+\frac{\delta}{3}\leq\delta.

Thus, combining the last two estimates, we arrive at

\frac{1}{t_{i}-t_{i-1}}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i}]:|(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(t)-\gamma^{\prime}(t)|>\delta\})\leq\delta.

The required inequality (
5.4
) follows by summing over

.
∎

Lemma 5.8
.

Suppose
K
is a compact subset of a Banach space
X
and
C\subset X
is a convex set containing an open neighbourhood of
K
. Then
C
also contains an open neighbourhood of
\operatorname{conv}K
.

Proof.

Let
G
be an open neighbourhood of
K
contained in
C
. Since
K
is compact, there exists some
\delta>0
such that
B(K,\delta)\subset G
. In particular, for every

and

with
\lVert v\rVert\leq\delta
, it holds that
x+v\in G
.

Fix

. To show that
C
contains on open neighbourhood of
\operatorname{conv}K
, it suffices to show that

whenever
\lVert v\rVert\leq\delta
. By definition, there are
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N}\in[0,1]
with
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}=1
and
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in K
such that

. Then it holds that

x+v=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}(x_{i}+v).

It follows that
x+v\in\operatorname{conv}G\subset\operatorname{conv}C=C
.
∎

Theorem 5.9
.

Suppose
(X,|\cdot|)
is a finite dimensional strictly convex Banach space and let

\{f\in\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(D,X):\dim_{H}f_{\#}\mu\geq d\}.

Corollary 5.20
.

Let
\mu
be a Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
with
\dim_{T}\mu\geq d
. Let
m\geq d
. Then the set

\{f\in\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{k},\mathbb{R}^{m}):\dim_{H}f_{\#}\mu\geq d\}

(5.13)

has non-empty interior. For any compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that
\dim_{T}\mu_{|K}\geq d
, any
F\in\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{k},\mathbb{R}^{m})
satisfying
F_{|C}=I_{|C}
for some isometry
I\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a convex set
C
containing an open neighbourhood of
K
, is in the interior of the set in (
5.13
).

Remark 5.21
.

One can use the deep result of DePhilippis and Rindler

where the set

5.20
.

Finally, we collect a corollary which provides a characterisation of the stabilized Hausdorff dimension in Euclidean spaces, which was discussed in the introduction. This follows from the combination Corollary
4.5
and the previous Corollary
5.20
.

We remark that one may state this type of a result in many equivalent ways such as replacing “residual” with “dense” or requiring instead of density only approximation of the identity (cf. the different definitions (
1.2
) and (

)). Since one may pass from each of these types of statements to any other, we only state the result in one form, the one which we find the most convenient for the reader.

Corollary 5.22
.

Suppose
X
is a complete and separable metric space and
\mu
is a Borel measure on
X

\begin{split}\dim_{T}\mu\geq\inf\{s\in[0,\infty]&:\{f\in\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(X,\mathbb{R}^{m}):\dim_{H}(f_{\#}\mu)\leq s\}\;\text{is residual in $\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(X,\mathbb{R}^{m})$}\\
&\text{for every $m\in\mathbb{N}$.}\}.\end{split}

If, moreover,
X=\mathbb{R}^{k}
for some

\begin{split}\dim_{T}\mu=\inf\{s\in[0,\infty]&:\{f\in\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{k},\mathbb{R}^{m}):\dim_{H}(f_{\#}\mu)\leq s\}\;\text{is residual in $\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{k},\mathbb{R}^{m})$}\\
&\text{for every $m\in\mathbb{N}$.}\}.\end{split}

In particular, the quantity on the right hand side is an integer.

\geq
” is Corollary

.
