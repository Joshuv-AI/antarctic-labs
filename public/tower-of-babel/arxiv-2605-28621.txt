Non-Kähler metrics on complex manifolds of LVMB type
Federico Thiella

Abstract
LVMB manifolds are a class of non-Kähler compact complex manifolds with a remarkably rich geometry: in many cases they admit a holomorphic bundle structure over a compact toric manifold. In fact, such a bundle is determined by an algebro-combinatoric datum encapsulating a simplicial fan. This is reflected in a close relationship between the geometry of an LVMB manifold and that of its toric base space. Throughout this paper, we restrict to this subclass of LVMB manifolds. We provide a formula for the characteristic class of the bundle in terms of the original LVMB datum. Subsequently, this expression is employed to address the existence of Hermitian metrics satisfying “special” conditions. We consider balanced and SKT metrics, showing that the former are obstructed in most cases. Moreover, within the LVMB class, we construct a new example of a manifold admitting a balanced metric. Finally, restricting ourselves to the subclass of LVM manifolds, we characterize those admitting an SKT metric.

Introduction

LVMB manifolds are a class of compact non-Kähler manifolds whose structure is controlled by an algebro-combinatoric datum. Some of them are very well-known examples in the literature: complex tori, Hopf manifolds and Calabi–Eckmann manifolds all belong to the LVMB class. They were first introduced by López de Medrano and Verjovsky
[
50
]
, and then generalized by Meersseman
[
55
]
. A further generalization was then provided by Bosio
[
11
]
. More references for LVMB manifolds and their geometry include
[
56
,
19
,
61
,
67
,
8
,
6
,
7
,
42
,
44
,
43
,
52
,
60
]
.

The algebro-combinatoric nature of LVMB manifolds makes them one of the few systematic constructions of non-Kähler manifolds in the landscape of complex manifolds. Although complex geometry aims to describe complex manifolds in their full generality, constructing and studying new complex manifolds plays a leading role in contemporary research. In fact, very few results are known to hold in full generality, while many others are proved just for restricted classes of complex manifolds. This gap motivates the study of new examples of complex manifolds: understanding their properties may either disprove a resilient conjecture in general, or extend the range of cases in which it holds.

This paper investigates the geometry of LVMB manifolds and, in particular, establishes conditions that ensure the existence of
canonical
Hermitian metrics. The existence of these metrics is a central problem in complex geometry, aiming to extend the Uniformization Theorem to higher dimensional complex manifolds. Such generalization has a twofold nature. Indeed, a canonical Hermitian metric can be characterized either by a curvature property of some Hermitian connection (like being Einstein or with constant scalar curvature), or by a cohomological property of its fundamental form.

In this sense, the Kähler setting is very rigid. Indeed, as Kähler metrics are precisely the ones for which the Levi–Civita connection is Hermitian, this connection is the most natural choice in the Kähler setting. The problem of classifying space forms—i.e. complete manifolds with constant curvature—is solved even in the Riemannian setting; similarly, complete Kähler manifolds with constant holomorphic sectional curvature have just three models: the projective space, the complex space, and the open unit ball
[
45
, §IX.7]
. Thus, finding canonical metrics on a Kähler manifold essentially reduces to two classes: Kähler–Einstein metrics and cscK metrics. The existence of the former on General Type and Calabi–Yau compact manifolds was proved in the seminal works of Yau and Aubin
[
66
]
, while for Fano manifolds this problem is more delicate. Recently, the existence of cscK metrics has also been fully characterized
[
15
]
.

Addressing the same problem in a generic Hermitian setting is much more involved. Indeed, a non-Kähler manifold admits several Hermitian connections that can be considered when a curvature condition is imposed; since they all have torsion, some technicalities need to be introduced. On the other hand, the Kähler condition can be relaxed as well: this is done by identifying weaker cohomological properties for the fundamental form. Yet several choices emerge naturally, depending on the context
[
35
,
31
]
. This leads to introducing many different
“special” Hermitian metrics
.

Although the equations defining such metrics appear deceptively simple (we will discuss two of them in
??
), examples appear either in very special classes, or sporadically: this leaves many questions and conjectures unanswered. Indeed, it remains unclear to what extent admitting a special metric restricts a complex manifold, and how two different such metrics interact. For example, it is conjectured by A. Fino and L. Vezzoni in
[
29
]
that the coexistence of a balanced metric with an SKT one (see below) forces the underlying manifold to be Kähler. This holds for a large class of quotients of Lie groups carrying a complex structure, but since examples lack in the first place, the general picture has not yet been fully understood.

Many of these problems have been investigated on quotients of Lie groups where explicit computations are often possible
[
1
,
28
]
, while outside this class our knowledge is significantly more limited
[
23
]
. This is our main motivation to address this kind of problem for LVMB manifolds. On one hand, their structure is stiff enough to make computations feasible; on the other hand, they are very abundant as any compact toric manifold admits multiple “companion” LVMB manifolds. Moreover, their construction is not related to Lie groups, hence we expect to encounter genuinely different behaviours.

In the geometric structure of LVMB manifolds we can find a holomorphic foliation
[
48
,
55
]
. Under a certain rationality condition on the initial datum, the leaves are complex tori and the leaf space is a compact toric variety—either smooth, or endowed with finite quotient singularities
[
56
]
. We restrict to smooth base-spaces; this identifies a subclass—which we call
regular
—of LVMB manifolds that are holomorphic principal torus bundles. This suggests that their geometry could be determined (at least partially) by the characteristic class of the associated torus bundle and hence by the generating algebro-combinatoric datum itself. The relations between this datum, the LVMB manifold it generates, and the bundle structure, are explored in
??
of this paper. This closer description of the structure of regular LVMB manifolds will be crucial to apply some general results by D. Grantcharov, G. Grantcharov and Y. S. Poon
[
34
]
to address the existence of “special” Hermitian metrics on this class of manifolds.

In order to investigate the bundle structure of regular LVMB manifolds, we adopt Bosio’s approach
[
11
]
and the construction described in
[
6
]
. From their perspective, any complete simplicial fan can be embedded—often by suitably adding some vectors called
ghosts
—into a so-called
triangulated vector configuration

[
49
]
. For rational smooth fans, such an object is given by a pair
(V_{\Sigma},\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma})
, where
V_{\Sigma}
is a list containing all primitive vectors of a fan
\Sigma
, plus the ghosts, while
\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}
is the combinatorial datum of
\Sigma
. We will call these triangulated configurations
regular
.

From this, by choosing an ordered basis
(f_{0},\dots,f_{2m})
for the space of linear relations among the vectors in
V_{\Sigma}
, and then applying the so-called
Gale transform
as in
[
6
]
, we obtain a point configuration in a complex affine space. Together with the fan combinatorial datum, this gives rise to an LVMB manifold
N
, which then is the total space of a principal holomorphic torus bundle over the compact toric manifold
X_{\Sigma}
.

This structure clearly emerges when this construction is compared with the well-known Audin–Cox construction of toric manifolds. Exploiting this, in this paper we describe how the choice of a Gale dual configuration determines a splitting of
\pi:N\to X
into
S^{1}
-principal sub-bundles. This point of view is crucial for our discussion: indeed each of them can be represented as a complex line bundle over
X
, and since the base space is a toric manifold, their first Chern class can be algebraically described in terms of the simplicial fan corresponding to
X
. This traces a clear link between the characteristic class of the holomorphic torus bundle
\pi:N\to X_{\Sigma}
associated to a regular LVMB manifold, and the initial triangulated vector configuration for which a Gale dual configuration has been chosen. We provide an explicit formula in the theorem:

Theorem
??
.

Let
(V_{\Sigma},\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma})
be a regular triangulated configuration with underlying fan
\Sigma
. Let
(f_{0},f_{1},\dots,f_{2m})
be any Gale dual configuration for this datum and
K
be the lattice spanned by these vectors. Then, with
N
denoting the corresponding LVMB manifold, the characteristic class of the principal torus bundle

is

\gamma(\pi)=\sum_{j=1}^{2m}\iota^{\vee}(f_{j}^{*})\otimes f_{j}\in K_{\Sigma}^{\vee}\otimes K,

where
\iota:K_{\Sigma}\hookrightarrow K
is the inclusion of the sublattice
K_{\Sigma}
of linear relations among non-ghost vectors, and
\iota^{\vee}
its adjoint map.

We use this to provide a bigraded model for the algebra of differential forms of a regular LVMB manifold. As an application, we then prove that a regular LVMB manifold satisfying the
\partial{\bar{\partial}}
-lemma must be a complex torus. These facts were already known for a subclass of LVMB manifolds called

[
61
]
. Leveraging some results in
[
40
]
, we can further exploit the bigraded model to explore the Borel spectral sequence induced by the holomorphic torus bundle
\pi:N\to X_{\Sigma}
. This yields a neat expression for the first Chern and Bott–Chern classes of a regular LVMB manifold.

It has been clear since the early works on LVMB manifolds, that those admitting a Kähler metric are only complex tori
[
50
,
55
,
11
]
. Yet, the existence of other special metrics on manifolds in the LVMB class remained unexplored before Faucard
[
21
]
addressed the existence of
Locally Conformally Kähler
metrics (LCK for brevity). His answer is mostly negative: it is proven that, apart from Hopf manifolds, LCK metrics on LVMB manifolds are obstructed at the level of the group of automorphisms. See also
[
22
]
for LCK metrics on coverings of LVMB manifolds.

As an application of

and of its cohomological implications, we explore the existence of two other types of special metrics on LVMB manifolds:
balanced
metrics and
SKT
metrics, of which we recall the definitions in

Theorem
??
.

Let
(V,\mathcal{T})
be a regular vector configuration containing at least one ghost vector. Then, any corresponding LVMB manifold admits a balanced metric if and only if it is a complex torus.

This does not hold for configurations without ghost vectors. Indeed, in

we explicitly construct an LVMB manifold

of complex dimension
4
supporting a balanced metric. This is, to the best of our knowledge, a new example. Indeed, employing the results developed so far, we show that it does not belong to any known class of compact complex manifolds admitting a balanced metric. This construction can be generalized to provide a family of examples of the form
N_{\ell}\to(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{1})^{\ell}
with complex dimension
\left\lfloor\frac{3}{2}\ell\right\rfloor
.

Finally, we discuss the existence of SKT metrics on regular LVMB manifolds. Employing our formula for the characteristic class

such that

is projective, this is indeed a characterization.

Theorem

Let
\pi:N\to X_{\Sigma}
be the principal torus bundle associated to a regular LVMB manifold

\gamma(\pi)=\sum_{j=1}^{2m}\iota^{\vee}(f_{j}^{*})\otimes f_{j}

The projectivity of the base space is not a very restrictive assumption: this is indeed the case considered by Meersseman and Verjovsky in

Theorem

Let

1
The structure of LVMB manifolds

In their full generality, LVMB manifolds can be obtained as leaf spaces of a holomorphic group action; by construction, their geometry is controlled by a datum
(\Lambda,\mathcal{E})
which we will call
LVMB datum

[
55
,
11
]
. This is
Gale dual
to a so-called
triangulated vector configuration

(V,\mathcal{T})

[
49
]
; however, the way the latter determines the former depends on some choices. This means that the same triangulated configuration produces several LVMB data, hence many LVMB manifolds. In turn, any triangulated configuration contains a simplicial fan embedded in it, thus considering such configurations instead of LVMB data highlights the relation between LVMB and toric manifolds.

This is the setting we consider throughout this paper: when we restrict to the regular case—i.e. to considering triangulated vector configurations
(V,\mathcal{T})
encapsulating a
smooth
complete simplicial fan
\Sigma
—the LVMB manifolds we obtain are the total spaces of holomorphic torus bundles over the toric manifold associated to
\Sigma

[
56
]
. This motivates the smoothness assumption we make throughout this paper. However, in the more general case where
\Sigma
is just a rational simplicial fan, these bundles get only mild singularities
[
56
]
, so we conjecture that the techniques we describe here could be generalized.

1.1
Construction of LVMB manifolds

In the following, we adopt the approach of
[
11
]
and
[
6
]
.

Definition 1
.

Let
E
be a
d
-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{R}
.
A
triangulated vector configuration
is a pair
(V,\mathcal{T})
where

•

V=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
is a list of vectors in
E
, admitting repetitions. We assume that
E=\left\langle v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
.

•

We call
\tau\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
a
simplex
if
\{v_{j}:j\in\tau\}
is a set of linearly independent vectors. A simplicial
cone
over
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
is the convex set
\cone(\tau)=\{\sum_{j\in\tau}\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}v_{j}\}
. Then,
\mathcal{T}
is an abstract simplicial complex of these simplexes such that:

–

\cone(\tau)\cap\cone(\tau^{\prime})=\cone(\tau\cap\tau^{\prime})
for all
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
,

–

\bigcup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\cone(\tau)\supseteq\cone(V)
.

In particular
\cone(\emptyset)=\{0_{E}\}
, while, by convention,
\cone(V)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{R}_{\geq}v_{j}
.

A triangulated vector configuration
(V,\mathcal{T})
is
rational
if there exists a full-rank lattice
L
in
E
such that
v_{i}\in L
for all
i=1,\dots,n
. Notice that, in this case,

\Rel(V)=\left\{c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}v_{j}=0\right\}

is a rational subspace in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
—that is, it admits a basis of vectors in
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
. We say that
(V,\mathcal{T})
is
regular
, if, for any
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
such that
|\tau|=d
, the set
\{v_{j}:j\in\tau\}
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
L\subset E
.

A configuration
(V,\mathcal{T})
is called
odd
if
V
has odd cardinality, and
balanced
if
\sum_{i}v_{i}=0
.

Remark 1.1
.

This definition allows some vectors in
V
not to belong to any simplex of
\mathcal{T}
. These will be called
ghost vectors
, and up to a change of indices, they can be put at the head of
V
as
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
.

For the construction of LVMB manifolds we will need to consider odd and balanced configurations satisfying
n-d=2m+1
for some positive integer
m
. This is not very restrictive as, just by adding ghost vectors, we can turn any configuration
(V,\mathcal{T})
into a new one satisfying the conditions above. In fact, balancedness can be achieved by prepending
-\sum_{i}v_{i}
to
V
and increasing
n
by 1; oddness, instead, can be obtained just by prepending one or two copies of
0
to
V
and correspondingly increasing
n
.

1.1.1
Simplicial fans as triangulated vector configurations

Polyhedral fans are the cornerstones of toric geometry. For an exhaustive presentation of this subject, we refer to Cox, Little and Schenck’s book
[
18
]
. Here we recall some definitions.

Definition 2
.

Let
\Sigma
be a polyhedral fan in
E
, we denote its
j
-dimensional skeleton
\Sigma^{(j)}
:

•

\Sigma
is called
simplicial
when the minimal generators of each of its maximal cones are linearly independent over
\mathbb{R}
,

•

\Sigma
is called
complete
if its support
|\Sigma|
coincides with
E
,

•

\Sigma
is called
rational
if there exists a full-rank lattice
L
in
E
that has nonempty intersection with each one-dimensional cone in the fan,

•

\Sigma
is called
smooth
if the minimal generators of maximal cones in
\Sigma
generate
L
as
\mathbb{Z}
-module.

Thus, to any rational fan there naturally corresponds the set of minimal generators of the one-dimensional cones; these last are called
rays
. If the fan is smooth, this set is a set of
\mathbb{Z}
-generators of the lattice.

Any
smooth
fan
\Sigma
can be encoded in a
regualar
triangulated vector configuration
(V_{\Sigma},\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma})
as follows. We consider
\tilde{v}_{1},\dots,\tilde{v}_{s}
as the minimal generators of
\Sigma^{(1)}
, then we set
V_{\Sigma}=(\tilde{v}_{1},\dots,\tilde{v}_{s})
; on the other hand, for the combinatorial datum
\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}
, we just copy the combinatorics of
\Sigma
. More precisely,
\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma}
if and only if
\{\tilde{v}_{j}:j\in\tau\}
spans a cone in
\Sigma^{(|\tau|)}
. As we started with a smooth fan,
(V_{\Sigma},\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma})
is well-defined, rational and, moreover, regular.

The configuration
(V_{\Sigma},\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma})
obtained this way is not necessarily odd and balanced; however it can be embedded into such a one by adding an appropriate number of ghost vectors as described above. In order to preserve rationality, these must lie in the lattice with respect to which
\Sigma
is rational. At the end of this process, we get a new rational vector configuration

V_{\Sigma}^{\prime}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{k},v_{k+1}=\tilde{v}_{1},\dots,v_{k+s}=\tilde{v}_{s}).

When no confusion is feared, we will not distinguish
V_{\Sigma}
and
V_{\Sigma}^{\prime}
. Hence, we will often use the notation
(V_{\Sigma},\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma})
to denote an odd and balanced triangulated configuration encapsulating a complete simplicial fan
\Sigma
; when needed, ghost vectors will be specified in the context.

Remark 1.2
.

A complete simplicial fan, whether rational or not, yields a whole family of toric data
[
62
]
. In turn, each such datum can be embedded into infinitely many triangulated vector configurations, all odd and balanced
[
6
]
. In this paper, we restrict our attention to the smooth case, see
??
for comments about the general setting.

Note
.

Any simplicial fan
\Sigma
embedded in a balanced triangulated configuration
(V_{\Sigma},\mathcal{T}_{\Sigma})
must be complete. Indeed, since
\sum_{v\in V_{\Sigma}}v=0
, it holds
E=\cone(V_{\Sigma})\subseteq\bigcup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\cone(\tau)
. As these are precisely the cones of
\Sigma
, it is complete.

Since each

.

Remark 1.3
.

As in the rest of the paper, the
(2m+1)
-tuple
(f_{0},\dots,f_{2m})
will play a major role; we will often refer to it as a Gale dual configuration of some

1.1.3
The construction

Now we show how a genuine LVMB datum
(\Lambda,\mathcal{E})
can be obtained from a triangulated vector configuration
(V,\mathcal{T})
, for which a Gale transform

has been chosen. We do so by selecting a

\Pi=[P|I_{m}]\in\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times 2m}(\mathbb{C})

\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2m}

\Lambda^{\mathbb{R}}

1.3

2
The characteristic class of a regular LVMB manifold

We are now able to study
N
as a holomorphic principal torus bundle over a toric manifold. Standard ideas in algebraic topology (see e.g.
[
54
,
58
]
) prove that these bundles can be given a characteristic class in cohomology which classifies them. On the other hand, LVMB manifolds are realizations of the algebro-combinatoric datum
(V,\mathcal{T})
, so we expect to be able to extract this kind of topological information directly from it. Indeed, we provide an explicit formula for the characteristic class of the torus bundle underlying a regular LVMB manifold.

We start by recalling how the characteristic class of a principal torus bundle is constructed in general. These general properties will be applied to the LVMB case in
??
.

2.1
The characteristic class of a principal torus bundle

The characteristic class of a principal
G
-bundle
\pi:P\to M
can be essentially defined in two ways: the first uses Chern–Weil theory to associate suitable cohomology classes to the curvature of a principal connection, while the second involves algebraic topology and, in particular, the theory of classifying spaces. Here, we will be using the second approach, so we will denote as
\pi_{G}:EG\to BG
the classifying bundle of the generic topological group
G
. As a consequence of the Eckmann–Hilton duality, we have a weak homotopical equivalence between
G
and the loopspace of
BG
. This fact reads as

\pi_{n}(G)\cong\pi_{n+1}(BG)

for any
n\geq 0
.

If
G=T^{k}
is a real torus, it can be written as the quotient
\mathbb{R}^{k}/L
for some full rank lattice
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
; immediately, we conclude that
\pi_{1}(T^{k})=L
, as loops can be canonically identified with its generators. Since all the other homotopy groups are trivial, we have that
BT^{k}
is an Eilenberg–MacLane space
K(L,2)
, hence
H_{2}(BT^{k})\cong\pi_{2}(BT^{k})\cong L
because of Hurewicz theorem, and so
H^{*}(BT^{k};\mathbb{Z})
is isomorphic to the graded group algebra
\mathbb{Z}(L^{\vee})
generated in degree 2. If
L=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{Z}\lambda_{i}
and
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
is the dual basis,
\mathbb{Z}(L^{\vee})
is nothing but the polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]
where
\deg x_{j}=2
. Therefore, a principal
T^{k}
-bundle
\pi:P\to M
determines a continuous map
\Gamma:M\to BT^{k}
, unique up to homotopy equivalences; in turn, this provides a graded ring homomorphism
\Gamma^{*}:\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]\to H^{*}(M;\mathbb{Z})
. Being graded, such map is completely determined by the restriction
\Gamma^{*}|_{2}:\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{Z}x_{i}=\Hom(L,\mathbb{Z})\to H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z})
. Finally, as
L
is free and finitely generated,
\Hom(\Hom(L,\mathbb{Z}),H^{2}(M,\mathbb{Z}))\cong L\otimes H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z})
, whence we conclude that torus bundles over
M
are classified by this group. More details can be found in
[
40
]
where this result is discussed via Čech cohomology instead.

Definition 4
.

We denote the image of
\Gamma^{*}|_{2}
under this identification as
\gamma(\pi)\in L\otimes H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z})
. We refer to this object as the
characteristic class
of
\pi:P\to M
.

Remark 2.1
.

If
H_{1}(M)
has no torsion (which is always the case of compact toric manifolds that are indeed simply connected
[
18
, Thm. 12.1.10]
), the Universal Coefficients Theorem gives the isomorphism
H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z})\cong\Hom(H_{2}(M),\mathbb{Z})
of finitely generated Abelian groups. Thus,
L\otimes H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z})
can be identified in three ways, namely

\Hom(L^{\vee},H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z}))\cong L\otimes H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z})\cong\Hom(H_{2}(M),L);

(6)

notice that the first is the one we used to pass from
\Gamma^{*}|_{2}
to
\gamma(\pi)
. As these identifications are canonical,
\gamma(\pi)
is associated to three
apparently
different objects. However, to keep the notation light, we will not distinguish the images of
\gamma(\pi)
via the isomorphisms above, thus referring to all of them as “characteristic class of
\pi
”. Indeed, no confusion has to be feared as the identification we are subtending will be always clear from the context.

The expression of
\gamma(\pi)
can be written explicitly by means of the Chern classes of the components of
\pi
. Indeed, the choice of a basis for
L
induces
T^{k}\cong\mathrm{U}(1)^{k}
as Lie groups, and
\mathrm{U}(1)^{k}
naturally embeds in
\mathrm{U}(k)
as maximal torus with the natural inclusion
i:\mathrm{U}(1)^{k}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{U}(k)
.

As
B
functorially maps groups to topological spaces,
i
induces a continuous map on classifying spaces
Bi:B(\mathrm{U}(1))^{k}\to B\mathrm{U}(k)
, and a ring homomorphism in cohomology

Bi^{*}:H^{*}(B\mathrm{U}(k);\mathbb{Z})\to H^{*}(B(\mathrm{U}(1)^{k});\mathbb{Z}).

As
B
commutes with products
[
54
, 129]
, by the Künneth Formula we have

H^{*}(B(\mathrm{U}(1)^{k});\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{*}((B\mathrm{U}(1))^{k};\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{*}(B\mathrm{U}(1);\mathbb{Z})^{\otimes k}\cong\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}].

On the other hand,
B\mathrm{U}(k)
is the infinite dimensional complex Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}(k,\mathbb{C}^{\infty})
of rank
k
. It is a standard result that
H^{*}(B\mathrm{U}(k);\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}[c_{1},\dots,c_{k}]
(see e.g.
[
59
]
), where
c_{j}
is the
j
-th Chern class of the tautological bundle of
\operatorname{Gr}(k,\mathbb{C}^{\infty})
; in particular,
\deg c_{j}=2j
. As an equivalent formulation of the splitting principle for complex vector bundles, we have that
Bi^{*}(c_{j})=\sigma_{j}
, where
\sigma_{j}
denotes the invariant polynomial of degree
j
in the variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
. Therefore,

c_{1}(\pi)=(\Gamma^{*}\circ Bi^{*})(c_{1})=\Gamma^{*}(\sigma_{1})=\Gamma^{*}\left(\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{j}\right).

(7)

This Chern class can be related to the Chern classes of each component: indeed, since
BT^{k}\cong(B\mathrm{U}(1))^{k}
, the classifying map
\Gamma:M\to BT^{k}
splits into the product of
k
maps
\Gamma_{j}:M\to B\mathrm{U}(1)
. More precisely, if
\Delta:M\to\prod_{j=1}^{k}M
denotes the diagonal morphism, we have

\Gamma=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}\Gamma_{j}\right)\circ\Delta=(\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{k}),

hence, by duality,
\Gamma^{*}=\Delta^{*}\circ\bigoplus_{j=1}^{k}\Gamma_{j}^{*}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Gamma_{j}^{*}
, so
??
can be read as

c_{1}(\pi)=\Gamma^{*}\left(\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{j}\right)=\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Gamma_{j}^{*}(x_{j})=\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{1}(\pi_{j}),

where
\pi_{j}
is the
j
-th component of
\pi
determined by the splitting we chose for
T^{k}
. This observation makes also explicit the expression of
\gamma(\pi)
: indeed, if
\iota_{j}:\mathbb{Z}[x_{j}]\hookrightarrow\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]
denotes the
j
-th canonical coprojection of the polynomial ring, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
the pairing between
L
and
L^{\vee}
, we have
c_{1}(\pi_{j})=\Gamma_{j}^{*}(x_{j})=(\Gamma^{*}\circ\iota_{j})(x_{j})=\left\langle x_{j},\gamma(\pi)\right\rangle
; therefore

\gamma(\pi)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{1}(\pi_{j})\otimes\lambda_{j}\in H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z})\otimes L,

(8)

and, in particular,
c_{1}(\pi)=\left\langle\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{j},\gamma(\pi)\right\rangle
.

2.3
Chern classes of regular LVMB manifolds

For a compact Kähler manifold, the vanishing of its first Chern class is equivalent to existence of a Kähler Ricci-flat metric on it
[
71
]
. Kähler manifolds admitting such metrics are called Calabi–Yau manifolds. Geometrically, they are characterized by the existence of an étale covering with trivial canonical bundle and, in particular, their holonomy group reduces to
\mathrm{SU}(n)
.

In the non-Kähler setting, things become more involved as the vanishing of the first Chern class no longer implies the holomorphic triviality of the canonical bundle: this is what happens for Calabi–Eckmann manifolds, for example. Indeed, whenever the
\partial{\bar{\partial}}
-lemma fails, like in many non-Kähler manifolds, the role of the de Rham cohomology is played by other two cohomologies: the Bott–Chern cohomology, and the Aeppli cohomology. If
(\mathcal{A}_{M}^{p,q},\partial,{\bar{\partial}})
is the bidifferential bicomplex of
\mathbb{C}
-valued smooth forms on
M
, they are respectively defined in bidegree
(p,q)
as

H^{p,q}_{\text{BC}}(M)=\frac{\ker\partial\cap\ker{\bar{\partial}}\cap\mathcal{A}_{M}^{p,q}}{\Ima(\partial{\bar{\partial}})\cap\mathcal{A}_{M}^{p,q}},\qquad H^{p,q}_{\text{Ae}}(M)=\frac{\ker\partial{\bar{\partial}}\cap\mathcal{A}_{M}^{p,q}}{(\Ima\partial+\Ima{\bar{\partial}})\cap\mathcal{A}_{M}^{p,q}}.

On the other hand, a non-Kähler manifold also admits multiple Hermitian connections; among these, a distinguished role is played by the
Chern connection
and the
first canonical connection

[
47
]
. Combining these notions, it is possible to generalize the topological first Chern class of a holomorphic line bundle
L\to M
so that some of the good properties of the Kähler case are retained. Namely, for any Hermitian metric
h
on
L
, the curvature form
\Theta^{h}=-i\partial{\bar{\partial}}\log h
of the Chern connection is
\partial
and
{\bar{\partial}}
-closed, hence it defines three different first Chern classes: the
first Bott–Chern class

c_{1}^{\text{BC}}(L)\in H^{1,1}_{\text{BC}}(M)
, the
first Aeppli–Chern class

c_{1}^{\text{Ae}}(L)\in H^{1,1}_{\text{Ae}}(M)
and the (topological) first Chern class
c_{1}(L)\in H^{2}_{\text{dR}}(M)
. As these are represented by the same real
(1,1)
-form, they are mapped onto each other by the natural morphisms

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{1,1}_{\text{BC}}(M)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2}_{\text{dR}}(M;\mathbb{C})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{1,1}_{\text{Ae}}(M)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c_{1}^{\text{BC}}(L)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c_{1}(L)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c_{1}^{\text{Ae}}(L)}

If

denotes the anticanonical bundle of
M
, we define

as
c_{1}^{\diamond}(\mathcal{K}^{-1}_{M})
, where

is a placeholder used to denote either “BC” or “Ae” superscripts.

The first Bott–Chern class appears in

]
where the author proves that its vanishing is equivalent to the existence of a Chern Ricci-flat metric; this gives a sort of weak non-Kähler version of the Calabi–Yau theorem. On the other hand,

is represented by the first Ricci form of the first canonical connection
[

]
, hence Levi–Civita Ricci-flat metrics exist only when this class vanishes. However, no analogue of the Calabi–Yau Theorem exists for these metrics.

This motivates our interest in computing these classes for LVMB manifolds. In the following, we use the characteristic class

to denote the first Chern class in any of the above cohomologies.

Toric geometry can be exploited once again to compute combinatorially the Chern classes of a toric manifold. If

are the Poincaré-dual classes associated to the invariant divisors of a smooth toric manifold

,

shifts to describing

and

Before addressing this, we need the following well-known fact regarding the Chern class of smooth toric varieties.

Let

be a smooth compact toric variety associated to a smooth complete simplicial fan

if and only if

.

Proof.

Let

be the dimension of the ambient space of
\Sigma
; when considering a rational fan
\Sigma
, we are implicitly assuming that
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is endowed with a full rank lattice

with respect to which
\Sigma
is rational.

Let

be the primitive generators of the rays in
\Sigma^{(1)}
and
\{D_{i}\}_{i}
the corresponding torus-invariant divisors. Because of the expression of

, this vanishes if and only if
\sum_{i}D_{i}
is linearly equivalent to a principal divisor. As we discussed in

, such divisors correspond to linear functionals in

via the map there defined as

. Hence, any principal divisor can be written as

with

is a basis for the class group,

2.3.2

The Leray–Serre spectral sequence for a principal

converges to the singular cohomology of the total space at its third page. Whenever the monodromy action of

3
Special metrics on regular LVMB manifolds

In this section we will investigate the existence of
balanced
and
SKT
metrics on regular LVMB manifolds.

We recall that balanced metrics, sometimes also called semi-Kähler or co-Kähler metrics, are Hermitian metrics whose fundamental form is co-closed. They were introduced by Michelsohn in
[
57
]
, where she also characterizes their existence on compact complex manifolds. Like the LCK metrics we mentioned in the introduction, balanced ones have good geometrical properties as well. Indeed, they are stable under holomorphic modifications
[
2
]
: on complex surfaces, this fact translates to the well-known stability of Kähler metrics under both blow-ups and blow-downs.

On the other hand, SKT metrics, often referred to as
pluriclosed
metrics, are the ones whose fundamental form satisfies
\partial{\bar{\partial}}\omega=0
. The acronym “SKT”, standing for
Strong Kähler with Torsion
, recalls that the corresponding Bismut connection (sometimes called
Kähler with Torsion
, or
Strominger
connection) has closed torsion
[
10
]
. In that paper, Bismut shows how the SKT condition naturally appears in an index theorem for non-Kähler complex manifolds. SKT metrics appear ubiquitously on complex surfaces: this is a direct consequence of the celebrated
“Théorème de l’excentricité nulle”
of Gauduchon
[
32
]
.

3.1
Submersion metrics on holomorphic principal torus bundles

In general, the space of Hermitian metrics on a complex manifold is huge: this makes it difficult to explore without explicit construction techniques which are capable of detecting special metrics. Nevertheless, on an LVMB manifold, we can exploit its holomorphic torus bundle structure to “assemble” a Hermitian metric on the total space by lifting a metric defined on the base, and then completing it to a positive definite tensor.

Metrics constructed this way are called
submersion metrics
and, since they detect the bundle structure, in case of a holomorphic principal torus bundle, those that are special can be explicitly characterized
[
34
]
. As we will discuss in this section, this is particularly remarkable for LVMB manifolds. Indeed, their bundle structure is determined by the characteristic class
\gamma(\pi)
, and we showed in
??
that this is encoded in the algebro-combinatoric datum, which the LVMB manifold is associated with.

Definition 5
.

Let
\pi:M\to X
be a holomorphic fiber bundle. We say that a Hermitian metric

is an isomorphism of Hermitian vector bundles.

A holomorphic principal torus bundle

are coupled together by the complex structure

(14)

Twistor spaces are a natural construction associating a complex manifold to a

Quotients of Lie groups

Many examples of compact Hermitian manifolds arise as quotients of Lie groups by co-compact lattices. Those admitting an invariant metric are precisely the holomorphically parallelisable manifolds

is a K3 surface; in this case
b_{2}(S\times H)=b_{2}(S)+b_{2}(H)=b_{2}(S)+22
, while
b_{2}(N_{3})=1
by Hurewicz Theorem.

Similarly, we can show that
N_{3}
is not a toric suspension. Indeed, the Puppe sequence of any toric suspension splits as

0\to\pi_{1}(M\times\mathbb{C}^{2k})\to\pi_{1}\left(\frac{M\times\mathbb{C}^{2k}}{\mathbb{Z}^{2k}}\right)\to\pi_{0}(\mathbb{Z}^{2k})\to 0,

thus non-trivial toric suspensions are never simply connected.

It remains to exclude all examples related to Lie groups. Of course,
N_{3}
has not the homotopy type of a quotient
\Gamma\backslash G
of a Lie group by a co-compact lattice. Indeed, as
\pi_{2}(G)=0
for any Lie group, also
\pi_{2}(\Gamma\backslash G)=0
. Similarly, we can see that
N_{3}
does not have the homotopy type of a generalized flag manifold. Indeed, Schubert stratification shows that these are homeomorphic to the CW-complex obtained attaching their Schubert cells (see
[
13
]
for a self-contained presentation of Schubert calculus and references therein). As these are all isomorphic to complex affine spaces, their CW-complex structure is generated by cells of even real dimension; in particular, they have positive Euler characteristic. Contrarily,
\chi(N_{3})
vanishes since, by
??
,
c_{4}(N_{3})=0
and the Gauss–Bonnet–Chern Theorem forces
\chi(N_{3})=\int_{N}c_{4}(N_{3})=0
.
∎

Higher dimensional examples

Similar considerations can be performed to construct higher dimensional examples. Indeed, we can consider
(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{1})^{2d+1}
as base toric variety, if
\Sigma
is its associated fan in
\mathbb{R}^{2d+1}

\mathbb{R}^{2d+1}

\left(\sum_{j=1}^{4d+2}\epsilon_{j},\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2},\dots,\epsilon_{4d-1}+\epsilon_{4d}\right),

\mathbb{R}^{4d+2}

Thus, the corresponding action over

.

be an integer, and let

. Moreover,
N_{\ell}

\ell

\ell

is a holomorphic principal bundle.
