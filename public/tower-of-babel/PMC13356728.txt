Outcomes of patients with post-traumatic stress disorder following arthroscopic rotator cuff repair
Authors: Akin A. Adio, Muhammad Hamza Ilyas, Asim Handy, Andrew Horn, Joseph A. Abboud, Hafiz F. Kassam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRotator cuff repair (RCR) is commonly performed to treat symptomatic rotator cuff tears, but post-operative recovery can be influenced by patient-level factors beyond structural pathology. Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), a prevalent mental health condition associated with altered pain perception and substance use, may affect recovery following RCR.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using the TriNetX Research Network. Adult patients undergoing RCR with PTSD were matched 1:1 to patients without PTSD based on demographics and comorbidities. Ninety-day and 2-year post-operative outcomes were evaluated, and relative risks (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated.ResultsA total of 2,262 matched patients were included per cohort. At 90 days, there were no differences in medical complications between groups. However, patients with PTSD demonstrated increased emergency department (ED) visits (16.58% vs. 11.16%, RR: 1.49, P < .001) and higher rates of sleep disorders (16.22% vs. 12.76%, RR: 1.27, P = .002). The mean number of opioid prescriptions was similar (2.87 ± 2.97 vs. 2.69 ± 2.39, P = .132). Additionally, patients with PTSD were more likely to develop cannabis use disorder at 2 years (3.48% vs. 1.21%, RR: 2.89, P < .001).ConclusionPTSD is associated with sleep disorders, ED utilization, and cannabis use disorders. These findings highlight the importance of addressing psychosocial factors in post-operative recovery.

Rotator cuff tears are a leading cause of shoulder pain and disability, and their prevalence increases steadily with age.2 Population studies estimate that cuff tears are present in over 20% of adults over the age of 60 even in the absence of acute trauma.33 Rotator cuff repair (RCR) is commonly performed to reduce pain, restore strength, and improve overall shoulder function.10 However, a consistent subset of patients report persistent pain stiffness or dissatisfaction despite technically intact repairs on imaging.14 Prior investigations have focused on structural and technical predictors such as tear size tendon retraction muscle atrophy and repair configuration to explain outcome differences.17 Although these variables are important, they do not fully account for the variability in post-operative pain, opioid use, and revision surgery observed in clinical practice, which suggests that patient-level psychosocial factors may meaningfully influence recovery.3

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a psychiatric disorder characterized by intrusive recollections, hyperarousal avoidance behaviors, and dysregulation of stress response systems.1 In orthopedic trauma populations, the prevalence of PTSD has been reported to range from approximately 10% to 30%, depending on injury severity and timing of assessment.21 PTSD is prevalent among trauma-exposed individuals and military veterans, and these populations are frequently encountered in orthopedic settings. Emerging data demonstrate that patients with PTSD exhibit altered pain modulation, increased central sensitization, and higher baseline pain intensity compared with individuals without PTSD.29 PTSD is also associated with increased prescription opioid exposure and greater risk of prolonged opioid use.27 In orthopedic surgery more broadly, mental health disorders have been linked to increased post-operative opioid consumption, higher healthcare utilization, and worse patient-reported outcomes.19 PTSD represents a distinct neurobiological condition with specific alterations in stress circuitry and behavioral coping patterns that may uniquely influence post-operative recovery. Despite this, the relationship between PTSD and outcomes following RCR remains poorly defined.

Given prior evidence linking PTSD to altered pain processing and opioid exposure, we sought to determine whether this condition confers additional risk in the setting of RCR. The purpose of this study is to evaluate the association between PTSD and post-operative outcomes after RCR using a large propensity-matched national cohort. We compared 90-day medical complications, as well as 2-year complications. We hypothesized that PTSD would be associated with increased opioid use and sleep disturbance after surgery.

Methods

This is a retrospective cohort study designed to evaluate the risk of 90-day perioperative complications and 2-year outcomes in patients undergoing RCR with PTSD. The study aims to compare these patients to a matched control group without PTSD. We used TriNetX, an aggregated database of deidentified electronic medical records from healthcare organizations within the United States collaborative network. The query for deidentified patient data was executed on March 1, 2024. Patients undergoing arthroscopic RCR from March 1, 2005, to March 1, 2024, were included. Because this study solely used deidentified patient records and the absence of individually identifiable data, institutional review board approval was not required.

Cohort selection

Patients who underwent arthroscopic RCR were identified using Current Procedural Terminology codes corresponding to arthroscopic RCR. Patients with a diagnosis of PTSD were identified using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification diagnosis codes for PTSD (F43.1). The PTSD cohort was compared with a control group consisting of patients without a documented diagnosis of PTSD prior to surgery. Inclusion criteria encompassed adult patients over 18 years of age at the time of surgery. Only patients who had visited a TriNetX-affiliated facility at least 2 years after their index procedure were included. The cohort construction process is illustrated in Figure 1. Propensity match

The TriNetX platform was used to conduct 1:1 propensity score matching employing logistic regression. The platform integrates nearest-neighbor matching and ensures that the difference between propensity scores is P > .05 for each covariate after matching. Propensity matching was performed to balance demographic characteristics (age, sex, race, and ethnicity) as well as relevant clinical factors including hypertensive diseases, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, ischemic heart diseases, diseases of the liver, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diseases of arteries, arterioles, and capillaries, alcohol-related disorders, cerebrovascular diseases, chronic kidney disease, heart failure, and prior cerebral infarction. Additional musculoskeletal covariates included complete rotator cuff tear or rupture not specified as traumatic and incomplete rotator cuff tear or rupture not specified as traumatic. Psychiatric and behavioral health covariates included chronic depression, depressive episode, sleep disorders, anxiety disorders, cannabis-related disorders, opioid-related disorders, body mass index, and hemoglobin A1c. Medication-related covariates included antidepressant and antipsychotic use.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes included 90-day medical and post-operative outcomes following RCR. These outcomes included infection, acute kidney injury, venous thromboembolism, sleep disturbances, opioid prescriptions, acute postprocedural pain, orthopedic aftercare, physical therapy utilization, and emergency department visits. Secondary outcomes evaluated at 2 years included revision surgery, lysis of adhesions or manipulation under anesthesia, shoulder stiffness, and conversion to shoulder arthroplasty. The development of opioid use disorder and cannabis use disorder was also assessed. For these outcomes, patients with a prior diagnosis at any time before the index procedure were excluded using the TriNetX platform to ensure that only new post-operative diagnoses were captured. Opioid use was defined on the basis of opioid prescription records within TriNetX rather than confirmed patient consumption. All outcomes were identified using standardized International Classification of Diseases and Current Procedural Terminology codes available within the TriNetX platform.

Statistical analysis

Relative risks (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals and associated P values were calculated for all outcomes using the TriNetX analytical platform. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests, while continuous variables were analyzed using Student t-tests. Statistical significance was defined as a 2-tailed P value less than .05. To account for multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied, with adjusted significance thresholds of P < .0056 for 90-day outcomes and P < .0083 for 2-year outcomes.

Results

Ninetyday outcomes

After Bonferroni correction (adjusted significance threshold P < .0056), patients with PTSD had significantly higher rates of sleep disturbances (16.22% vs. 12.76%, RR: 1.27, P = .002) and emergency department visits (16.58% vs. 11.16%, RR: 1.49, P < .001) compared with controls. Opioid use was higher in the PTSD cohort but did not remain significant after adjustment (53.46% vs. 49.69%, RR: 1.08, P = .019). The mean number of opioid prescriptions was similar between groups (2.87 ± 2.97 vs. 2.69 ± 2.39, P = .132). There were no significant differences between groups in infection (P = .827), venous thromboembolism (P = .350), acute postprocedural pain (P = .336), orthopedic aftercare (P = .861), or physical therapy utilization (P = .512). Acute kidney injury occurred in fewer than 10 patients in both cohorts and was not analyzed due to database reporting limitations. Discussion

In this analysis, PTSD among patients undergoing RCR was associated with higher rates of sleep disturbance and emergency department visits in the early post-operative period. At longer-term follow-up, PTSD was associated with an increased risk of developing cannabis use disorder. These findings suggest that PTSD may adversely influence post-operative recovery and behavioral outcomes following RCR.

The clinical relevance of PTSD in orthopedic surgery is likely to increase as populations are exposed to trauma. Veterans from early 2000s conflicts are aging into groups at higher risk for degenerative shoulder pathology.7,9,28,30 The lifetime prevalence of PTSD among US military veterans is approximately 10%, with Iraq and Afghanistan war veterans showing even higher rates approaching 25%.30,32 Many of these patients are now entering their fourth and fifth decades of life, when the incidence of rotator cuff disease and surgical intervention rises.12 As a result, orthopedic surgeons are increasingly likely to encounter patients with concomitant PTSD and rotator cuff pathology.8

The increased substance use observed in our study aligns with prior literature linking psychological trauma to heightened pain sensitivity. This effect may be related to increased anxiety and altered pain processing.16,18,22 Dysregulation of stress response pathways in PTSD may further amplify pain perception and reduce tolerance to post-operative discomfort.25,31 Consistent with these mechanisms, Katakam et al demonstrated that patients with PTSD undergoing shoulder arthroplasty have an increased risk of higher post-operative substance use.13 Similarly, Rozet et al reported that PTSD was associated with an increased risk of prolonged chronic post-operative pain following elective orthopedic surgery in military veterans.26 The increased rate of cannabis use disorder found in our study further suggests a pattern of maladaptive coping and substance reliance.20 Collectively, these observations underscore the importance of tailored pain management strategies and mental health screenings in this population. This includes multimodal analgesia approaches, pre-operative psychological optimization, and trauma-focused therapy.13,21

Patients with PTSD also demonstrated higher rates of post-operative sleep disturbances. Sleep disturbance is a recognized risk factor for inferior functional outcomes following RCR.34 Importantly, sleep disruption is highly prevalent in patients with PTSD, with studies reporting that 70–90% of individuals with PTSD experience clinically significant sleep disturbances, including insomnia and trauma-related nightmares.15 These disturbances are driven by underlying hyperarousal and altered sleep architecture and are considered a core feature of the disorder rather than a secondary symptom.6 Poor sleep quality has been associated with increased pain sensitivity, impaired recovery, and worse patient-reported outcomes following RCR.4 Accordingly, strategies aimed at improving perioperative sleep in this population may be beneficial. Perez et al have demonstrated that melatonin supplementation following arthroscopic RCR can improve sleep quality and reduce post-operative narcotic use.23 Given the bidirectional relationship between sleep disturbance and pain perception, improving perioperative sleep may not only enhance recovery and functional outcomes but also help reduce reliance on opioid analgesics following RCR.

Patients with PTSD in the present study also demonstrated significantly higher rates of emergency department utilization following RCR. Increased healthcare utilization among patients with PTSD has been consistently demonstrated across both medical and surgical populations and is thought to reflect heightened pain and difficulty coping with post-operative stressors.11 PTSD is characterized by persistent hyperarousal and exaggerated threat perception, which may lower the threshold for seeking acute medical evaluation following surgery. Additionally, PTSD frequently coexists with depression, sleep disturbance, and substance use disorders, all of which may contribute to fragmented outpatient follow-up and increased reliance on emergency care services.5 Importantly, increased emergency department utilization in this population may not necessarily indicate higher rates of structural surgical complications, but rather differences in post-operative recovery experience and psychosocial support needs.24 These findings highlight the importance of early post-operative follow-up and multidisciplinary support strategies in patients with PTSD undergoing RCR.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the data were derived from a large administrative database and therefore may be subject to coding errors or misclassification. For example, the database does not provide granular data on tobacco use (eg, pack-years) or quantitative alcohol consumption, precluding adjustment for these factors, which are known to influence rotator cuff healing and opioid use. The identification of outcomes relied on diagnostic and procedural codes. As a result, disease severity may not be fully captured. Additionally, important rotator cuff–specific variables such as tear size, tendon quality, chronicity of the tear, surgical technique, and fixation construct were not available in the database, and the TriNetX platform does not capture validated patient-reported outcome measures such as the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score or the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand score, limiting our ability to assess functional recovery. Opioid exposure was determined from prescription records, which may not reflect actual patient consumption, and prior emergency department utilization could not be incorporated into matching as a potential confounder of post-operative emergency department visits. Lastly, we could not determine the severity of PTSD symptoms or access to psychiatric treatment or counseling. This limits the ability to evaluate how differences in mental health management may influence post-operative outcomes.

Conclusion

PTSD represents an important psychosocial factor influencing post-operative recovery following RCR, with associations observed in sleep disturbance, cannabis use disorder, and ED utilization. These findings highlight the potential value of optimizing pain control and sleep quality in patients with PTSD undergoing RCR.
