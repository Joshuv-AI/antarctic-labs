Replicating lipid micelles: a feasible precursor to the origin of life and the earliest appearance of genomes
Authors: Daniel W Nebert, Zijuan Liu, Vasilis Vasiliou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The most commonly accepted scenario of early Earth includes: creation of the universe around 13.8 Ga (Giga-annus; or 109 years ago); establishment of our solar system ~ 4.60 Ga; and formation of Earth ~ 4.54 Ga. The earliest life forms on our planet so far observed to have existed, are microbes that left signals of their presence in rocks ~ 3.6 Ga — suggesting that Life forms existed within the first 940 million years after Earth’s formation. However, an intriguing recent publication [1] infers that the last universal common ancestor (LUCA) likely existed by 4.2 Ga, and that the inferred LUCA had a genome of at least 2.5 Mb of DNA, encoding around 2,600 proteins; this suggests that sophisticated Life might have existed within the first 340 million years after Earth was formed. The commonly accepted geological history of early Earth suggests that the turbulent Hadean Eon lasted until 4.0 Ga, with the Late Heavy Bombardment (LHB) period occurring around 4.1 to 3.8 Ga. If Earth during the Hadean exhibited a molten surface, intense volcanic activity, and constant bombardment by asteroids and comets — how were sensitive molecules (e.g., nucleic acids, proteins) able to survive? Considering the “Lipid First” hypothesis [2], we propose that replicating lipid micelles are feasible candidates for having populated much of Earth’s deep hydrothermal vents and turbulent surface within the first 340 million years of Earth’s existence. These lipid micelles could therefore have provided a plausible form of “protective capsules” inside which early Life’s sensitive molecules were able to evolve.

The purpose of this invited review is to remind Readers of this journal that “genomes” have existed since the beginning of Life on Earth. This journal accepts papers on the topics of evolutionary biology and evolutionary genomics — which would include evolution of genomes during the beginning of Life on early Earth. This ‘Origin-of-Life’ article should help Human Genomics readers realize and appreciate that our human genome today is the ultimate culmination of more than 4 billion years of evolution — including thousands of gene families that have arisen via gene duplications. What is the underlying reason for the process of evolution? To improve pathways for species survival: finding food, avoiding predators, and breeding successfully — almost always provoked by constantly changing environmental pressures. First, we provide a brief overview of the most commonly-accepted scenario of early Earth’s history.

In the beginning, the theoretical “Big Bang” occurred around 13.8 Ga, and our solar system is believed to have originated ~ 6.0 Ga. About 4.54 Ga, Earth was first formed and completely unrecognizable — having coalesced together from a cloud of dust. Figure 1A illustrates the first two eons of Earth’s history. The Hadean Eon is the oldest geologic eon in Earth’s history, from the moment of our planet’s formation, ~ 4.54 Ga until 4.0 Ga. The severe environmental conditions of the Hadean have been described as “being similar to Hades,” — hence, the name.

The Hadean was characterized by a molten lava surface, intense volcanic activity, constant bombardment by asteroids and comets, and formation of the Moon. The Hadean Eon represents the time before formation of the earliest known rocks, and (until recently) was believed to be a time before any possible emergence of living organisms. Key events included formation of Earth’s core and early crust, and creation of our Moon.

The Moon formed 4.30±0.21 Ga (Fig. 1B), shortly after Earth formed, when debris ejected from a Mars-sized protoplanet (named Theia) collided with early Earth, resulting in the material eventually coalescing into our Moon. Recent research [3, 4], however, suggests our Moon might have formed even earlier (i.e., ~ 4.51–4.52 Ga), with formation of the Moon occurring shortly after birth of the Solar System (~ 4.6±0.05 Ga).

The Late Heavy Bombardment (LHB) period (Fig. 1B) is a theoretical period occurring around 4.1 Ga to 3.8 Ga, during which time the inner solar system experienced a surge in asteroid and comet impacts — creating the heavily cratered surfaces seen today on the Moon, Earth, Mars, and Mercury. This event, perhaps generated by giant planet migration, drastically shaped the orbits of the early planets, and potentially delivered water. However, the timing of this event challenges the timeline for Life’s emergence, because recent studies question the intensity and timing of the LHB period — due to new evidence suggesting substantially earlier water and evolution of Life [vide infra].

When did life first evolve?

Physical evidence of Life observed in rocks — dates back to 3.7 to 3.5 Ga (Fig. 1B). Microfossils and stromatolites in Australia and Greenland suggest microbes existed early on, whereas even older clues (e.g., carbon isotopes in ~ 4.1-Ga zircon grains) have hinted at Life’s potential origins around 4.1 Ga [5], although direct fossil proof starts about half a billion years later. Carbon isotopes (specifically, a high 12C/13C ratio, or negative δ13C), found in 4.1-billion-year-old graphite inclusions within Jack Hills zircons are interpreted as potential evidence of life, suggesting that a terrestrial biosphere existed by 4.1 Ga — roughly 300 million years earlier than previously thought. The graphite trapped inside these zircons shows a high concentration of 12C relative to 13C (a low δ13C value), which is a common signature of biological processes including photosynthesis.

The Archean (4.0 Ga to 2.5 Ga; Fig. 1A) is Earth’s second geologic eon, which includes evidence for emergence of the first life (anaerobic microbes) in an oxygen-poor atmosphere, formation of early continents from microcontinents, and the beginning of significant rock and fossil records — setting the stage for complex life. Named after the Greek word for “beginning,” the Archean precedes the Proterozoic Eon and featured a different atmospheric composition with methane and CO2, kept warm by the young Sun.

The first era of the Archean Eon of the Earth’s geologic record — spanning ~ 400 million years, from the end of the Hadean Eon (~ 4.0 Ga) — is the Eoarchean Era — which ended 3.6 Ga (Fig. 1A). During the Eoarchean Era, Earth’s crust is believed to have solidified, “substantial land” began to form, the first life in the form of simple prokaryotes is believed to have originated, and initial cooling and precipitation of water led to the formation of oceans. Furthermore, it was a time of intense volcano activity — which contributed to creation of early island arcs and greenstone belts. As noted above, the first widespread signs of life have been found in ancient rocks close to the end of the Eoarchean Era (Fig. 1B).

The Eoarchean Era is followed by the Paleoarchean (3.6 to 3.2 Ga), the Mesoarchean (3.2 to 2.8 Ga), and the Neoarchean (2.8 to 2.5 Ga) eras (Fig. 1A). The first photoynthetic organism is proposed to have evolved during the Paleoarchean Era [6]. The origin of modern-style plate subduction, based on geologic evidence from the Pilbara Craton in western Australia, is believed to have occurred during the Mesoarchean Era. The Neoarchean Era ended 2.5 Ga (Fig. 1A) — noted as the time when significant shifts in Earth’s tectonic processes and the composition of atmospheric gases occurred; this marked the transition from the Archean Eon to the Proterozoic Eon. Very recent chemical evidence from ancient crystals, however, suggests that Earth’s tectonic plates might have already started moving by 3.3 Ga [7].

As stated above, the earliest life forms so far observed to exist are microscopic organisms that left signals of their presence in rocks 3.6±0.1 Ga. However, the 2024 publication by Moody et al. [1] shows credible evidence inferring that the last universal common ancestor (LUCA) likely existed by ~ 4.2±0.1 Ga — which is only 340±110 million years after Earth first formed and which occurs before the end of the Hadean Eon. Moreover, Moody and coworkers suggest that the LUCA was highly complex — with a genome of ~ 2.5 million base-pairs (range 2.49–2.99 Mb), encoding around 2,600 proteins.

If this estimate (1) is true, what sort of “compartmentalization” — to protect vulnerable nucleic acids, proteins, and other sensitive molecules from the harsh environment of early Earth — must have already been in place? If the “Lipid First” hypothesis (2) is correct, then replicating lipid micelles might be the ideal candidates for having populated deep hydrothermal vents and much of Earth’s surface — well within the first 340 million years after Earth’s formation. The remainder of this Review expands on this premise.

The “last universal common ancestor”

“Intracellular Life” includes biological processes, organisms, or pathogens that exist, function, or replicate — within the boundary of a cell (either a host cell or a self-contained unit). Common ancestry of all existing “intracellular Life on Earth” would likely need to contain: [a] the universal genetic code, [b] use of ATP (or something similar) as a common energy source, [c] equipment for protein synthesis, and [d] shared chirality (i.e., property of a molecule that is non-superimposable on its mirror image) of the (almost-universal) set of 20 amino acids. Moreover, the LUCA must have been able to avoid or fight off predators, find food, and reproduce. LUCA is the node on the Darwinian tree-of-life (Fig. 2) from which the fundamental prokaryotic domains (Archaea and Bacteria) diverged. Life’s evolutionary timescale has typically been calibrated to the oldest fossil occurrences — which should influence our understanding of early evolution of life on Earth.

What kind of environment did LUCA inhabit and when? Was LUCA a simple or a complex organism? Previous estimates of LUCA by various laboratories have been in a state of confusion — either due to conceptual disagreement about what LUCA is, or because of different methodological approaches and data. Published analyses differ among each scientist’s inferences of LUCA’s genome size — from conservative estimates of 80 orthologous proteins, up to 1,529 different potential gene families [1]. Interpretations range from little beyond an information-processing and metabolic core, all the way to a prokaryote-grade organism with much of the gene repertoire of modern Archaea and Bacteria. Moody et al. used [a] molecular clock methodology, [b] horizontal gene-transfer-aware phylogenetic reconciliation, and [c] existing biogeochemical models — to address questions about LUCA’s inferred age, gene content, metabolism, and impact on the early Earth system [1].

Molecular-clock estimations of the origin age of LUCA

Life’s evolutionary timescale has been typically calibrated to the oldest fossil occurrences, as mentioned above. However, the veracity of fossil discoveries — even from the early Archaean period — has been challenged because of Earth’s violent environment at that time. Relaxed Bayesian node-calibrated-molecular-clock approaches have provided a better means of integrating the scarce fossil and geochemical record of early life with the information provided by recent molecular data. Yet, restricting LUCA’s age is thought-provoking — because of limited prokaryote fossil calibrations, as well as the uncertainty of their assignment on the tree-of-life phylogeny (Fig. 2). Furthermore, molecular clock estimates of LUCA have previously relied upon conserved universal single-copy marker genes within phylogenies for which LUCA represents the root [1].

Dating the root of a tree is not easy, because errors propagate — as one goes from the tips to the root of the dated phylogeny; in addition, information is not yet available to estimate the rate of evolution for the branch incident on the root node. In an attempt to remove some of this uncertainty, Moody and coworkers thus chose to accept only genes that had duplicated before LUCA — with two (or more) copies in LUCA’s genome [1]. Using dated gene-duplication methodology to infer evolutionary age — is similar to the independent efforts of James A Lake [8] and Johan Peter Gogarten [9] “to date origins by gene duplications,” 20 to 35 years ago, although today the methods are much more sophisticated (i.e., hundreds of Archaea and Bacteria genomes sequenced and available on the internet).

The root in these gene trees therefore represents these duplications that precede LUCA, whereas LUCA is represented by two descendant nodes. Use of these universal paralogs also has the advantage that the same calibrations can be applied at least twice. After duplication, the same species divergences are represented on both sides of the gene tree and thus can be assumed to have the same age; this considerably reduces the uncertainty when genetic distance (branch length) is resolved into absolute time and rate. On the other hand, when a shared node is assigned a fossil calibration — such cross-bracing also serves to double the number of calibrations on the phylogeny — thus improving divergence-time estimates. For dating early Earth’s history, the authors therefore adjusted their molecular clock analyses, which included thirteen data sets (Table 1), i.e., analyzing genes that had duplicated before LUCA — with two (or more) copies in LUCA’s genome [1]. This information was then extrapolated and subsequently used to infer the date of origin of the LUCA.

Moody et al. used previously established dates for four fossil calibrations and included nine additional calibrations — with justifications provided below. Five of the newly defined calibrations (i.e., total group Eukarya for the archaeal lineage, total group Eukarya for the mitochondrial lineage, Embryophyta, Archaeplastida, and Eumetazoa) have been revised to follow the latest geochronological updates and literature as of December 2023, and so differ from those used in previous studies [12, 16]. Note that the root of a phylogeny requires constraining in molecular clock analyses. Moody et al. (Table 1) controlled the root (representing the duplication of pre-LUCA paralogs) with a hard upper boundary equal to the age of the moon-forming impact [1].

Calibration on the root of the Darwin tree-of-life is of particular importance. Some previous studies have suggested the unlikelihood of an earlier age of LUCA — based on the assumption that Life could not have survived the Late Heavy Bombardment period (~ 3.95±0.15 Ga). However, the LHB hypothesis is extrapolated and scaled from the Moon’s impact record — and that interpretation has been questioned in terms of the intensity, duration, and even the accuracy of time when a possible LHB episode occurred [vide supra]. Thus, the LHB hypothesis should not be considered as a credible limitation on inferring an earlier age of LUCA.

Moody and coworkers used soft-uniform boundaries (Table 1), with the maximum-age boundary based on the time of the Moon-forming impact (~ 4.30±0.21 Ga), which would have effectively sterilized Earth’s precursors, Tellus and Theia. The minimum boundary on the age of LUCA was based on low-δ98Mo isotope values (indicative of Mn oxidation, compatible with oxygenic photosynthesis) — dated minimally to ~ 2,945 Ma (Table 1). This geological time falls within the Archean Eon — at the boundary between the Mesoarchean and Neoarchean eras (Fig. 1A).

The authors’ estimates for LUCA’s age [1] were also inferred from a concatenated and a partitioned dataset — both consisting of five pre-LUCA paralogs: (catalytic and non-catalytic subunits from ATP synthases; elongation factors Tu and G; signal recognition protein and signal recognition particle receptor; tyrosyl-tRNA and tryptophanyl-tRNA synthetases; and leucyl- and valyl-tRNA synthetases). Marginal densities fell within calibration densities, when topologically adjacent calibrations did not overlap temporally, but the marginal densities may differ when they overlap — to ensure the relative age relationships between ancestor-descendant nodes.

The authors considered those marginal densities as a reasonable interpretation of the calibration evidence — given the phylogeny. The duplicated LUCA node-age estimates (which the authors obtained under the autocorrelated rates and independent-rates log-normal relaxed-clock models with the authors’ partitioned dataset) fell within their composite age estimate for LUCA — ranging from 3347 Ma to 4520 Ma (Table 1) — and are comparable to three previous studies [12, 17, 18]. Dating analyses based on single genes, or on concatenations that excluded each gene in turn, also resulted in comparable timescales [1].

Biochemical estimations of the origin age of LUCA

To estimate the metabolic pathways of LUCA, Moody et al. first inferred an updated microbial phylogeny from 57 phylogenetic marker genes from 700 genomes — comprising 350 Archaea and 350 Bacteria genomes. This tree was in good agreement with recent phylogenies of the archaeal and bacterial domains of Life.

Moody et al. [1] used the probabilistic gene- and species-tree reconciliation algorithm “ALE” to infer the evolution of gene family trees for each sampled entry in the KEGG Orthology (KO) database on their species tree. [ALE infers the history of gene duplications, horizontal gene transfers, and losses — based on comparisons between the distribution of bootstrapped gene trees and the reference species tree, allowing authors to estimate the probability that the gene family was present at a node in the tree.] This approach has several advantages for drawing inferences about LUCA. Most gene families have experienced gene transfer since the time of LUCA; therefore, explicitly modeling transfers — enables authors to include many more gene families in the analysis than had been possible using previous approaches. Because this analysis is probabilistic, authors can also account for uncertainty in gene-family origins and evolutionary history by averaging over different scenarios using the reconciliation model. Using this approach, Moody et al. then estimated the probability that each KEGG gene family KO Orthology was present in LUCA, and then they used the resulting probabilities to construct a hypothetical model of LUCA’s gene content, metabolic potential, and environmental context [1].

Using the KEGG annotation is beneficial — because it allows the authors to connect their inferences to curated functional annotations; however, it has the drawback that some widespread gene families that were likely present in LUCA are divided into multiple KEGG Orthology (KO) families that individually appear to be restricted to taxonomic groups and are inferred to have arisen later. To account for this limitation, authors also performed an analysis of “Clusters of Orthologous Genes” (COG) gene families (which correspond to more coarse-grained functional annotations).

By using modern prokaryotic genomes as training data, the authors [1] thus used a predictive model to estimate the genome size and the number of protein families encoded by LUCA — based on the relationship between the number of KEGG gene families, and the total number of proteins encoded by modern prokaryote genomes. On the basis of the prokaryotic proteins for the KO gene families, authors identified a conservative subset of 399 KO gene families that were likely to be present in LUCA (probability with prokaryotic proteins ≥ 0.75, and found in both Archaea and Bacteria); these families formed the basis of the authors’ metabolic reconstructions. Furthermore, by integrating over the inferred prokaryotic proteins of all KO gene families — including those with low probabilities — the authors also estimated LUCA’s genome size. Their predictive model estimated a genome size of 2.75 Mb (range 2.49–2.99 Mb), encoding ~ 2,657 (range = 2,451–2,855) proteins [1].

A list of 298 universal marker genes was identified — by creating a non-redundant list of markers used in previous studies on archaeal and bacterial phylogenies [1]. Although authors can estimate the number of genes in LUCA’s genome, it is more difficult to identify the specific gene families that might have already been present in LUCA — based on the genomes of modern Archaea and Bacteria (it is likely that the modern version of these pathways would be considered incomplete, based on LUCA’s gene content, through subsequent evolutionary changes). The authors should therefore expect reconstructions of metabolic pathways to be incomplete — due to this phylogenetic noise and other limitations of the analysis pipeline. For example, when looking at genes and pathways that can unequivocally be mapped to LUCA, (e.g., the ribosome and aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases for implementing the genetic code), Moody et al. [1] found that they mapped many (but not all) of the key components to LUCA. The authors interpreted this to mean that their reconstruction is probably incomplete — but that their interpretation of LUCA’s metabolism relies on their inference of pathways, not individual genes.

Was LUCA likely an aerobe or anaerobe?

Aerobes require oxygen for growth and energy metabolism, typically using O2 as a terminal electron acceptor in respiration. On the other hand, anaerobes do not require oxygen, which is often toxic to them; instead, anaerobes rely on fermentation or other anaerobic respiration pathways to generate energy. The inferred gene content of LUCA therefore suggests it was an anaerobe — because authors did not find support for the presence of terminal oxidases. Instead, Moody et al. [1] identified almost all genes encoding proteins of the archaeal (and most of the eubacterial) versions of the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway (WLP) (prokaryotic proteins PPs > 0.7), indicating that LUCA had the potential for acetogenic growth (i.e., forming acetate, or acetic acid, as a product of metabolism) and/or carbon fixation. LUCA encoded some Ni-, Fe-hydrogenase subunits, which may have enabled growth in H2. (Enzyme complexes involved in methanogenesis — such as methyl-coenzyme M reductase and tetrahydromethanopterin S-methyltransferase — were inferred to be absent, suggesting that LUCA was unlikely to function as a modern methanogen.)

The authors [1] found strong support for some components of the TCA cycle (including subunits of oxoglutarate/2-oxoacid ferredoxin oxidoreductase, succinate dehydrogenase, and homocitrate synthase) — although some steps were missing. LUCA was probably capable of gluconeogenesis/glycolysis in that they found support for most subunits of enzymes involved in these pathways. Considering the presence of the WLP, this may indicate that LUCA had the ability to grow organoheterotrophically and potentially also autotrophically. [Heterotrophs cannot produce their own food and must consume other organisms or organic carbon for energy, whereas autotrophs create their own organic food using sunlight (photosynthesis) or chemical energy (chemosynthesis), forming the base of food chains.] Gluconeogenesis would have been important in linking carbon fixation to nucleotide biosynthesis via the pentose phosphate pathway — most enzymes of which seemed to be present in LUCA. The authors [1] found no evidence that LUCA was photosynthetic — determining low P-values for almost all components of oxygenic and anoxygenic photosystems.

Interestingly, Moody et al. also found support for the presence of 19 class 1 CRISPR-Cas effector protein families in the genome of LUCA — including types I and III, Cas10, and Cas7 [1]. The absence of Cas1 and Cas2 might suggest that LUCA encoded an early Cas system with the means to deliver an RNA-based immune response by cutting (Cas6/Cas3) and binding (CSM/Cas10) RNA, while lacking the full immune-system-site CRISPR. This finding would support the suggestion that the effector stage of CRISPR–Cas immunity evolved from RNA-sensing for signal transduction, based on the similarities in RNA-binding modules of the proteins. (This conclusion would be consistent with the idea that cellular life was already involved in an “arms race” with viruses at the time of LUCA.) Intriguingly, the results of this study indicate that an early Cas system was an ancestral immune system of extant cellular life.

Altogether, these metabolic reconstructions suggest that LUCA was a relatively complex organism, similar to today’s Archaea and Bacteria. On the basis of ancient duplications of the selenocysteine synthase and ATP synthase genes before LUCA, along with highly significant P-values for key components of those systems (e.g., membrane-bound ATP synthase subunits, genes involved in peptidoglycan synthesis, and the cytoskeletal actin-like protein, MreB) — it is highly likely that LUCA possessed the core cellular apparatus of modern prokaryotic life. This also might include the basic constituents of a phospholipid membrane, although the authors’ analysis did not conclusively establish its composition. In particular, Moody and coworkers [1] recovered the following enzymes involved in the synthesis of ether and ester lipids: alkyldihydroxyacetonephosphate synthase, glycerol 3-phosphate, and glycerol 1-phosphate, and components of the mevalonate pathway (mevalonate 5-phosphate dehydratase, hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA reductase, mevalonate kinase, and hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA synthase).

Compared with previous estimates of LUCA’s gene content — the authors [1] found 81 overlapping COG (clusters of orthologous genes) gene families with the consensus dataset of Ref [19]., and 69 overlapping KEGG Orthology genes with the dataset of Ref [20]. Key points of agreement between the present study [1] and previous studies include: presence of the signal recognition particle protein, ffh [homolog of the mammalian Signal Recognition Particle 54 (SRP54) protein] used in targeting and delivery of proteins to the plasma membrane, a high number of aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases for amino acid synthesis, and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis bacterial enzymes.

Where on early earth was LUCA most likely to be located?

Moody et al. [1] thus inferred LUCA to be a thermophilic anaerobic autotroph that used the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway (WLP) for carbon fixation — based on the presence of a single enzyme [carbon monoxide dehydrogenase (CODH)] — and likewise suggested that LUCA was capable of nitrogen fixation using a nitrogenase. The authors reconstruction-mapped many more genes to LUCA — albeit each with lower probability — than previous analyses and yielded an estimate of LUCA’s genome size that is within the range of modern prokaryotes. The result is an incomplete picture of a cellular organism that was prokaryote-grade — rather than progenotic (i.e., a hypothetical primitive evolutionary stage of life that preceded modern prokaryotic cells) and that, similarly to prokaryotes today, probably existed as part of an ecosystem.

As the common ancestor of sampled, already-existing prokaryotic life, LUCA is the oldest node on the species tree that can be reconstructed via phylogenomics, but it was already the product of a highly innovative period in evolutionary history — during which most of the core components of cells were established. Moody et al. [1] were unable to reconstruct LUCA’s contemporaries using phylogenomics, but they could propose hypotheses about their physiologies, based on the reconstructed LUCA whose features immediately suggest the potential for interactions with other prokaryotic metabolic pathways.

The inference that LUCA used the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway (WLP) helps limit the environment in which it could have lived. Modern acetogens can grow autotrophically on H2 (as well as CO2), or heterotrophically on a wide range of alternative electron donors — including alcohols, sugars, and carboxylic acids. This metabolic flexibility would be key to their modern ecological success. Acetogenesis — whether autotrophic or heterotrophic — has a low energy-yield and low growth-efficiency (although use of the reductive acetyl-CoA pathway for both energy production and biosynthesis would decrease the energy cost of biosynthesis); this would be consistent with an energy-limited early Earth biosphere [1].

If LUCA functioned as an organoheterotrophic acetogen, it was necessarily part of an ecosystem containing autotrophs providing a source of organic compounds (because the abiotic source flux of organic molecules would have been minimal on early Earth). Alternatively, if LUCA functioned as a chemoautotrophic acetogen, it could (in principle) have lived independently off an abiotic source of H2 (and CO2). However, it is implausible that LUCA would have existed in isolation — because the by-products of its chemoautotrophic metabolism would have created a niche for a consortium of other metabolisms (as found today in modern sediments). This would include the potential for LUCA itself to grow as an organoheterotroph [1].

A chemoautotrophic acetogenic LUCA could have occupied two major potential habitats: the first (and perhaps more likely) is the deep ocean floor, where hydrothermal vents and serpentinization of sea-floor would have provided a source of H2. Consistent with this, authors [1] found support for the presence of reverse gyrase (a hallmark enzyme of hyperthermophilic prokaryotes) — which would not be expected if early Life existed on the ocean surface.

The second habitat is the ocean surface, where the atmosphere would have provided a source of H2 derived from volcanoes and metamorphism (the process in which rocks are transformed into new rocks with different mineral compositions or textures due to factors such as heat, pressure, and chemical reactions with fluids). Indeed, the authors [1] detected the presence of spore photoproduct lyase, which, in surviving organisms, repairs methylene-bridged thymine dimers occurring in spore DNA, because of damage induced via UV radiation.

Previous studies have often favored a deep-ocean environment for LUCA, because early Life would have been better protected there from an episode of Late Heavy Bombardment. However, if the LHB was less intense than initially proposed, or just a sampling artefact — then these arguments are diminished. Another possibility may be that LUCA inhabited a shallow hydrothermal vent or a hot spring [1].

Based on thermodynamic considerations — methane (CH4) and carbon dioxide (CO2) are expected to be the eventual metabolic end-products of the resulting ecosystem, with a small fraction of the initial H2 consumption buried as organic matter; the resulting CH4 flux into the atmosphere would fuel photochemical H2 regeneration and associated productivity on the ocean surface. Existing models suggest that the resulting global H2-recycling system is highly effective, such that the supply flux of H2 to the surface could have exceeded the volcanic input of H2 to the atmosphere — by at least one order of magnitude — in turn, implying that the productivity of such a biosphere was boosted by a comparable factor. Photochemical recycling to carbon monoxide (CO) would also be a process that would support a surface niche for organisms consuming CO [1].

In deep-ocean habitats, there could have been some localized recycling of electrons, but a quantitative loss of highly insoluble H2 and CH4 to the atmosphere — and minimal return after photochemical conversion of CH4 to H2 — would mean global recycling to any substantive ocean depth would be minimal. Hence, the surface environment for LUCA could have become dominant. The global net primary productivity of an early chemoautotrophic biosphere — including acetogenic LUCA and methanogens — could have been about three orders of magnitude less than today. Further, lightning could have provided a source of nitrite and nitrate — consistent with LUCA’s inferred pathways of nitrite and (possibly) nitrate reduction [1].

In conclusion, by treating gene presence probabilistically, the authors’ reconstruction techniques mapped many more genes (2,657 genes) to LUCA than previous analyses, and the authors estimated the size of LUCA’s genome (2.75 Mb), which is within the range of modern prokaryotes. Moody et al. [1] cannot use phylogenetics to reconstruct other members of this early ecosystem, but they felt that they could infer their metabolic networks, based on the metabolic inputs and outputs of LUCA. How evolution proceeded from the origin of life — to early communities at the time of LUCA — remains an open question, but the inferred age of LUCA (4.2±0.1 Ga; within 340 million years after Earth’s formation), compared with that of the origin of Earth and Moon, suggests that the process required a surprisingly short interval of geologic time.

History of origin-of-life research

Around 25 years ago, estimates of LUCA’s age had ranged from 3.5 to 3.8 Ga, within the Paleoarchean and Eoarchean Eras (Fig. 1B). The Moody et al. study [1] proposes a substantially earlier origin of LUCA — 4.1 to 4.3 Ga — which [vide supra], appears to precede the theoretical Late Heavy Bombardment period (Fig. 1B). Given such a violent environment of early Earth’s molten surface, intense volcanic activity, constant bombardment by asteroids and comets, and the time before formation of the earliest known rocks — how could heat-labile nucleotides such as nucleic acids and other proteins critical for Life, survive and evolve?

In the 1960s, scientists — including Carl Woese [21], Francis Crick [22], and Leslie Orgel [23] — had independently suggested that early life forms might have relied on RNA for both genetic information storage and catalytic functions (i.e., RNA formation before the evolution of DNA and proteins). In 1962, Alexander Rich [24] proposed that RNA could have had a phenotype beyond its informational role, suggesting RNA’s catalytic capabilities. The discovery of ribozymes (RNA molecules that act as catalysts) in the early 1980s by Tom Cech [25] and Sidney Altman [26] — provided strong experimental support for RNA preceding DNA in the origin of life. In 1986, Walter Gilbert [27] was the first to coin the term “RNA World” in a commentary on the catalytic properties of RNA.

Research over the past several decades has confirmed the useful properties of random RNA sequences (i.e., the production of active RNA ligases from accidental sequences of RNA); however, this is not enough to prove the “RNA World” hypothesis. Based on what is known about organisms before 2015, it is not possible to conclude that a self-replicating molecule was the primary source of catalytic mechanisms fundamental to living systems. Research on the “RNA World” hypothesis is ongoing, although recent findings threaten to disprove this hypothesis. In a 1996 report, NASA specified that the “significant difficulties” surrounding the “RNA World” concept would include the extreme chemical fragility of RNA and its narrow range of catalytic activities [28]. Some “RNA World” proponents have noted that many viruses are made of RNA, not DNA; whether the genesis of viruses might have occurred before or after the LUCA — as well as the diversity of extant viruses and their hosts — remains a subject of investigation.

The “Lipid First” hypothesis

Whereas the prevailing scientific narrative has been that of a “polymers first” approach or an “RNA world,” Doron Lancet’s group at the Weizmann Institute of Science in Israel, have presented an intriguing alternative; They proposed a “Lipid World” scenario as a plausible origin for life, in which lipid micelles (structures simpler than cell-like vesicles) could have been nanoscopic protocell precursors. Central to this theory is that these micelles could exhibit thermal resistance, catalysis, and compositional self-reproduction — passing information through a mixture of their lipid components — rather than through the self-replication of sequence polynucleotides [2].

For decades, the prevailing scientific narratives (dubbed the “RNA World” or “Polymers First” or “Concatemers First” hypotheses) — had suggested that Life began when complex, self-replicating molecules somehow emerged from the primordial soup. These molecules were thought to have been the crucial starting point — later becoming enclosed within membranes to form rudimentary cells. However, this traditional view faces significant challenges. Lancet and coworkers [2] have explained how totally impossible it would be that complex polymers, such as RNA molecules, might spontaneously form with the necessary specific sequences and structures needed for replication — in the midst of the chaos, extreme heat, and disorder of the early Earth environment [vide infra].

Furthermore, no single molecule — not even RNA or DNA — can self-reproduce in isolation, because they rely on the intricate metabolic network machinery of an entire cell. This raises the question: what if prebiotic chemicals could possess capabilities beyond those of modern biomolecules? Instead of “Concatemers First” or “RNA World” or “DNA World,” the Lancet group in Rehovot, Israel, therefore advanced an alternative perspective (called the “Lipid World” or the “Lipid First” hypothesis), in which they propose a “more economical, or frugal, beginning” of Life on early Earth [2]. Instead of starting with complex sequence information-carrying polymers, their hypothesis proposes that Life emerged from much simpler, spontaneously self-assembling and replicating supramolecular structures composed of diverse lipid-like amphiphilic molecules that were precursors to Life.

Also known as amphiphiles or surfactants, these lipids are molecules with both a polar (hydrophilic) head, and an apolar (hydrophobic) tail (Fig. 3). These compounds are proposed to have become very abundant on early Earth — as evidenced by analyses of meteorites, as well as chemical reactions known to occur in suboceanic hydrothermal vents [2].

It is well known that a key property of lipids is their ability to self-assemble spontaneously in aqueous environments, energetically driven by the tendency of their hydrophobic tails to cluster away from the water phase. This self-assembly can create various structures — including nanoscopic spheres called micelles, as well as larger spheres with a watery core called vesicles. Unlike highly constrained biological polymers, lipid self-assembly is known to be promiscuous, meaning that most types of amphiphiles (biological, synthetic in the lab, or found in primordial soup in high diversity) can combine to form these supramolecular structures that are mutually attracted by noncovalent bonds [2].

With their enclosed water-filled core, vesicles have long been considered prime candidates for early protocells during evolution, due to their resemblance to modern cells. Nevertheless, research from the Lancet lab highlights the compelling possibility that nanoscopic lipid micelles could have been an early precursor to Life [2]. Micelles are simpler than vesicles — typically comprising only a few hundred lipid molecules arranged in a single layer, with their hydrophobic tails facing inwards and the polar heads facing outwards into the aqueous phase; these micelles thus lack the water-containing center of vesicles (Fig. 3).

Despite their simplicity, micelles would possess several advantageous features in the earliest stages of early Earth evolution: [a] their nanoscopic size would mean that they contain much fewer molecules, rendering their composition more sensitive to change, and potentially facilitating interactions between components; [b] micelles would exhibit a high surface-to-volume ratio; [c] they would possess enhanced reactant concentrations; [d] they would encourage all chemicals against all molecular networking; and [e] they would represent a large diversity of compositions, because their environmental molecular repertoire is suspected to be much larger than the molecule count in the assembly.

For any entity to be considered a precursor to Life, it must be able to facilitate chemical reactions via mutual catalysis. Lipid molecules have been shown to be simple catalysts (“lipozymes”), because they can have headgroups such as diverse amino acids or small peptides. Micelles therefore would provide a unique milieu for catalysis, significantly amplifying reaction rates — due to their concentration, surface effects, and polarity gradients [2]. These catalytic capabilities thus indicate that micelles would effectively harbor mutually-catalytic networks. In such a network, molecules within the micelle would actually catalyze the entry, formation, or modification of other molecules, helping to maintain and grow the entire structure (Fig. 3).

A critical characteristic of Life is self-reproduction. Micelles can grow by incorporating more lipid molecules from their environment (termed “accretion”), or by synthesizing and/or modifying lipids internally. Upon growth, micelles will often split into two or more parts, resulting in “offspring assemblies.” Meaningful reproduction, however, requires that new assemblies will inherit characteristics from the parent. Because micelles lack a genetic sequence (such as RNA, or DNA), the proposed “Lipid First” solution can only pass information through composition. Reproduction in this context implies that the micelle would grow and divide, while maintaining stable internal concentration relations (homeostatic growth) — which is then transferred to the ‘progeny’ assemblies upon fission (Fig. 3). This compositional information in the primordial soup would be similar to non-genetic (i.e., epigenetic) inheritance in present-day prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.

The process of micelle and vesicle formation represents an endothermic reaction — which is a chemical or physical process that absorbs heat from its surroundings, resulting in a decreased temperature of the immediate environment. Thermodynamically, it has been shown that micelles pursue their structural stability in water over an extended range of temperatures, from 0° C to more than 90° C [32]. In terms of kinetics, chemical reactions become more rapid as temperature increases, i.e., molecules gain kinetic energy, causing them to move faster and collide more frequently. Thus, even at higher temperatures, simple micelles will retain their properties to establish catalytic networks and self-reproduction, much better than evolutionarily later complex entities, e.g., RNA and proteins, which are well known to be highly sensitive to increased temperatures.

In summary, the process of proposed micellar reproduction (“Lipid First” hypothesis) would be more favorable in the very hot environment of early Earth — both thermodynamically and kinetically. Because hydrothermal vents on the deep ocean floor, and hot springs on the surface of early Earth, would likely have approached 90° C or higher, hyperthermophilic prokaryotes thrive even today — it is therefore feasible that the Lancet laboratory’s proposed “Lipid First” micelles and vesicles arose and became precursors to early Life on Earth.

Formation of “composomes” as dynamic attractors

Quantitatively, to explore catalytic networks and reproduction, the Lancet laboratory further developed the Graded Autocatalysis Replication Domain (GARD) model [29]. These additional studies are based on the idea that the kinetic behavior of the micellar mutually-.

catalytic network — constitutes a physicochemical dynamic system. A key finding of this analysis is that those uncommon compositions that exhibit self-reproduction (composomes) represent attractor dynamic states [31].

Although a random composition is unlikely to be a composome — additional simulations reveal something crucial: reproducing composomal states appear much more frequent than expected. “Attractor dynamics” connotes that a system tends to settle into stable non-equilibrium states. In the GARD model, this means that — from practically all initial random compositions, after a finite number of growth and fission events — the assembly will reach a stable composome. Simulations also indicate that repertoire diminution would occur, as assemblies approach attractors: the number of molecule types decreases, resulting in potent catalytic values within the network — in a manner analogous to modern cells that use a relatively small subset of their vast repertoire of chemical compounds available [31].

Obviously, the capacity for compositional reproduction in micelles, could lead ultimately to compositional mutations. Exponential growth of micellar counts having similar composition with some variation would constitute “proto-species.” This, in turn, would allow natural selection, providing a foundation for “primeval Darwinian evolutionary attributes.” If micelles with certain compositions would reproduce more successfully than other micelles that do not (e.g., due to more effective catalytic networks), then their proto-species would become more predominant. This scenario would differ from the view that “Darwinian evolution would always require complex biopolymers such as DNA or RNA” (i.e., primordial evolution could have begun at a much simpler stage in early Earth’s history).

This scenario would also suggest a plausible path for increasing complexity. Once reproducing micellar entities exist, they can evolve in many diverse directions. Such evolution could drive the gradual emergence of more complex catalytic networks (e.g., the beginnings of metabolism). Such micellar catalysis could also facilitate the synthesis of more complex molecules — for example, construction of amino acids into peptides, or of nucleic acids into nucleotides, to increasingly complex proteins and longer-in-length RNA molecules. Some lipid modifications could lead to structural changes — potentially triggering the transition from micelles to more complex vesicular forms.

Transition from simple micelles to the more cell-like vesicle protocells would also be a necessary step towards present-day life on early Earth. For example, sufficiently endogenously catalytic modification of one-chain lipids into two-chain molecules within a micelle — could induce a shift to vesicular structure [2], as exemplified in Fig. 3.

How would the larger vesicles acquire the complex catalytic networks needed for reproduction and evolution? This would require crucial synchrony between membrane growth and growth of the internal aqueous content. The “Lipid First” hypothesis offers a solution (Fig. 3). Reproducing micelles (which already contain functional catalytic networks), could fuse with existing non-reproducing vesicular membranes; this fusion would then transfer network components and catalytic capabilities from the micelle to the vesicle and allow its membrane to reproduce. This process — of transferring compositional information via fusion — might be viewed as analogous to horizontal gene transfer, or to epigenetic inheritance, in modern-day biology.

At this point, any molecule in the watery milieu inside a vesicle would not be coupled to the membranal network (Fig. 3). However, gradual events (mutations) in the membrane could lead to increasing control of catalytic molecules in the membrane — which would begin to control the composition of the inner volume. These membrane mutations might eventually allow synchronous compositional reproduction that encompasses both membrane and the internal metabolism, a well-known challenge in the field. This stepwise progression (starting with simple, spontaneously-forming, reproducing micelles) that gradually build complexity towards vesicular protocells both at the membrane level, and at the intracellular content level) — would present a more plausible chemical path from chaotic non-living matter to (seemingly more ordered) Life — rather than scenarios relying on the sudden appearance in the early Earth milieu of complex biopolymers, such as RNA and/or DNA [2].

The Lancet lab’s hypothesis would portray the first steps of Life on early Earth — not as a singular, improbable event involving the perfect assembly of complex machinery — but as an emergent property of dynamic, self-organizing chemical systems [2]. Their model suggests that the fundamental attributes of Life (i.e., catalysis, reproduction, and containment) would have been integrated from the very beginning of chemical reactions in early Earth’s primordial soup within simple micellar structures.

Subsequent evolution toward today’s sophisticated, biopolymer-based Life (included in the central dogma of biology, and ultimately the formation of the inferred primitive LUCA that preceded archaebacteria) could then be seen as “more plausible,” because of this initial, more primitive form of primeval Darwinian evolution. Whereas the journey, from these early protocells to complex modern cells, still represents a vast expanse of hypothetical research — focusing on the self-reproduction and evolutionary potential of simple, dynamic, supramolecular lipid assemblies — this attractive “Lipid First” hypothesis would provide a tangible and scientifically rigorous path forward [2] in deciphering the origins of Life on early Earth.

Conclusions

The earliest Life forms so far proven to exist are microscopic organisms that left signals of their presence in rocks determined to be ~ 3.6±0.1 Ga old. Given that Earth first formed ~ 4.54 Ga, this indicates that Life would have begun within the first 840 million years after formation of the planet. However, the Moody et al. publication [1] — described in meticulous detail in the first half of the present review — shows data inferring that the last universal common ancestor (LUCA) might have existed as early as 4.2 + 0.1 Ga (i.e., 4,200 million Ga, which is just 340 million years after Earth was first formed). Considering that generation-times for modern-day prokaryotes are in the range of 30 min, the number of 30-min intervals in 340 million years would be 6.0 × 1012 (or 5.9568e + 12), implying that this could be a reasonable amount of time for LUCA to have arisen.

This figure illustrates how spontaneously-forming lipid micelles — capable of compositional self-reproduction through mutual catalytic networks — could have populated early Earth during the Hadean Eon. Through processes described by the GARD model, these nanoscopic assemblies could have evolved toward increasingly compositional complexity, eventually transitioning into vesicle-like protocells capable of internal metabolism and metabolic pathways. Such structures would provide a protective and catalytic environment enabling the more reasonable gradual emergence of nucleic acids, proteins, and molecules involved in energy metabolism. Within this framework, the relatively early appearance of LUCA around ~ 4.2 Ga would become more plausible, because micellar assemblies could have served as the earliest evolutionary platform for progenotic chemical selection and protometabolic networks.
