Abstract
. This paper studies the validity of Stokes’ theorem for differential subcomplexes naturally adapted to the noncommutative geometry of positively graded Lie groups, with particular emphasis on Carnot groups. We introduce geometric conditions under which Stokes-type formulae hold for the Rumin complex and for a new family of spectral complexes associated with the homogeneous weight filtration of the de Rham complex. In particular, the spectral complexes allow us to recover the validity of Stokes’ theorem on locally smooth intrinsic graphs. This is achieved by showing that the corresponding Stokes’ formulae are governed entirely by the degree of the underlying submanifolds. Our approach also reveals that both the Rumin complex and the spectral complexes can be interpreted directly in terms of the classical de Rham complex through the Leibniz rule and integration over suitable classes of submanifolds, namely R R -manifolds and spectral manifolds, respectively. Finally, motivated by this interaction between homogeneous weights and degrees of submanifolds, we propose a notion of currents naturally adapted to these subcomplexes.

1.
Introduction

Stokes’ theorem represents one of the most important results in integration theory on oriented manifolds. It expresses a fundamental principle in differential geometry: global geometric information can be recovered from local differential data via boundary interactions. By relating the integral of a differential form over a manifold to the integral of the form itself over its boundary, it provides a unifying framework that generalises classical results such as the fundamental theorem of calculus, Green’s theorem, and the divergence theorem, and represents one of the central bridges between analysis, geometry, and topology.

The importance of Stokes’ theorem goes far beyond mathematics, with applications in several areas of theoretical physics. It underlies the passage from macroscopic integral laws to local differential equations. It allows global balance principles, formulated as fluxes across the boundary of a region, to be expressed in terms of pointwise differential relations, thus linking conservation laws with their differential counterparts, as in the classical formulation of Maxwell’s equations and fluid dynamics.

In the broad context of differential geometry, Stokes’ theorem underpins the topological properties of the de Rham cohomology of smooth manifolds. Indeed, it implies that the integration of differential forms over singular chains vanishes on boundaries and depends only on cohomology classes, thereby inducing a natural isomorphism between the de Rham cohomology of a smooth manifold and its singular homology with real coefficients. Stokes’ theorem also implies the homotopy invariance of de Rham cohomology: homotopies produce chains whose boundary measures the difference between the endpoints, so the pullbacks of a closed form along homotopic maps define the same cohomology class and therefore have the same integrals over corresponding cycles.

In the context of de Rham currents, Stokes’ theorem is the basic principle for the definition of the boundary operator. Viewed as a generalised
k
-dimensional manifold, a
k
-current
T
, or de Rham current, is a continuous linear functional defined on any smooth, compactly supported
k
-form
\alpha
. Its boundary is precisely introduced assuming the validity of Stokes’ theorem
\partial T(\alpha)=T(d\alpha)
. Any smooth, oriented,
k
-dimensional submanifold
\Sigma
with boundary induces a
k
-current
[[\Sigma]]
, and Stokes’ theorem shows that the boundary current is defined by the geometric boundary
\partial\Sigma
of
\Sigma
along with its induced orientation:

\partial[[\Sigma]](\alpha)=\int_{\Sigma}d\alpha=\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha=[[\partial\Sigma]](\alpha)\,.

The abstract notion of boundary allows one to formulate the celebrated Plateau problem, namely the existence of a mass minimising current among all currents with prescribed boundary. The solution of this problem is a foundational result of geometric measure theory in Euclidean spaces; see, for instance,
[
12
,
41
]
.

In the context of geometric measure theory in Carnot groups the situation is considerably subtler. Finding the proper geometric integration requires the
right domains of integration as intrinsic smooth objects with respect to the
geometry of the group. Since the first developments of geometric measure theory in
Heisenberg groups, it appeared that the right smooth objects are
intrinsic graphs, as the building blocks for the notion of intrinsic rectifiability

[
18
,
20
]
. An intrinsic graph is a set associated with a pair
(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
of complementary subgroups (Definition
5.1
). It is a set of the form
\{w\phi(w):w\in A\}
, where
A\subset\mathbb{W}
is an open set and
\phi:A\to\mathbb{V}
is the defining mapping.
Intrinsic graphs, introduced and studied in
[
15
,
17
]
, are intrinsically regular sets when their defining mapping is continuously intrinsically differentiable. They also appear as regular sets arising from an intrinsic differential calculus in Carnot groups, using the notion of Pansu differentiability,
[
35
]
. In sum, when the ambient space is a Carnot group, or more generally a positively graded group, the geometry of regular subsets is much more rigid, and intrinsic graphs with intrinsically differentiable defining mapping become the proper class of intrinsically
C^{1}
smooth submanifolds.
Crucially, not every differential form of the de Rham complex interacts well with smooth intrinsic graphs from the point of view of integration. By contrast, the forms that appear in the Rumin complex
(E_{0}^{\bullet},d_{c})
are compatible with the underlying sub-Riemannian structure, given their fundamental role in the definition of intrinsic graphs, as shown in
[
17
]
.

This observation naturally leads to the question of whether the Rumin differential
d_{c}
admits a Stokes-type theorem analogous to the classical one. Beyond the fact that simple Rumin forms are in a one-to-one correspondence with intrinsic graphs (as observed explicitly in Section
5
), the Rumin complex plays a central role in the sub-Riemannian setting, by providing a differential complex whose operators reflect the grading of the underlying Lie algebra while retaining many of the structural properties of the de Rham complex. Our approach to establishing conditions under which a Stokes’ formula holds for the Rumin complex on an orientable
C^{1}
manifold
\Sigma
with boundary
\partial\Sigma
is based on relating the operator
d_{c}

to the exterior derivative
d
, thereby allowing us to exploit the validity of the classical Stokes’ theorem for differential forms.

Since the Rumin differential is defined as
d_{c}=\Pi_{0}d\Pi_{E}\alpha
, one of the main difficulties in deriving a Stokes-type formula from the classical one lies in the presence of the projection operators
\Pi_{0}
and
\Pi_{E}
. A natural strategy is therefore to identify a class of manifolds on which the contribution of this projection becomes unnecessary or can be controlled directly.

The key ingredient underlying our approach is the characterisation of the space of smooth differential forms
\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
on a positively graded group
\mathbb{G}
though the algebraic differential
d_{0}
and its adjoint
\delta_{0}
. The latter is defined once a suitable scalar product on
\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
is fixed (see Subsection
2.1
). In particular, the associated algebraic Laplacian
\Box_{0}:=d_{0}\delta_{0}+\delta_{0}d_{0}
yields a Hodge-type orthogonal decomposition of
\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
(as shown in Proposition
2.16
), so that

\displaystyle\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})=\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\oplus\ker\Box_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\,.

This decomposition allows us to isolate the component of a differential form that effectively contributes to the Rumin differential and, crucially, to identify those whose vanishing renders the projection operators irrelevant in the context of integration. More precisely,

•

E_{0}^{\bullet}=\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
;

•

for every
\alpha\in E_{0}^{\bullet}
, one has
\Pi_{E}\alpha=\alpha+\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
;

•

for every
\alpha\in E_{0}^{\bullet}
,
d\Pi_{E}\alpha\in\big(\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\big)^{\perp}\cap\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})=\big(\ker\Box_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\big)\cap\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
.

These observations suggest introducing a class of submanifolds on which forms belonging to
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
integrate to zero, thereby eliminating the contribution of the projection operators in Stokes-type formulas for the Rumin complex.
Indeed, if this is the case, we obtain Stokes’ theorem for the Rumin differential
d_{c}
directly from the classical Stoke’s formula, since

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha\underbrace{=}_{(\ast)}\int_{\partial\Sigma}\Pi_{E}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}d\Pi_{E}\alpha\underbrace{=}_{(\ast)}\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\alpha\,,

where by
(\ast)
we denote the equalities that follow from the fact that the integrals of forms in
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
over
\partial\Sigma
and
\Sigma
vanish.

Definition A

(
R
-manifolds, Definition
3.10
)
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{G}
be an oriented
k
-dimensional
C^{1}
-manifold, with or without boundary. We say that
\Sigma
is an
R
-manifold if

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\eta=0\qquad\text{for every }\eta\in\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})\cap\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}.

Theorem A

(Stokes’ theorem on
R
-manifolds, Theorem
3.11
)
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{G}
be a
k
-dimensional
R
-manifold whose boundary
\partial\Sigma
is also an
R
-manifold. Then

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\alpha\qquad\text{for all }\alpha\in E_{0}^{k-1}.

In view of Theorem
A
, the Rumin complex, and in particular the action of the Rumin differential, admits an interpretation through Stokes’ theorem. From this perspective, the Rumin complex can be understood not as a genuinely new differential complex, but rather as the de Rham complex viewed through integration along suitable classes of manifolds. More precisely, the differential
d_{c}

arises from the exterior derivative once forms are tested only against manifolds for which the contributions coming from
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
vanish. Section
4
makes this viewpoint precise by showing that the Rumin complex can be reconstructed from the de Rham complex using only the Leibniz rule for the exterior derivative together with integration over appropriate manifolds, such as
R
-manifolds.

A distinctive feature of the Heisenberg groups is that this reconstruction does not require selecting preferred representatives in
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}

when applying the Leibniz rule, reflecting the intrinsic nature of the Rumin complex in this setting (see Subsection
4.1
). In contrast, already for
\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
(see Subsection
4.2
), recovering the Rumin complex from the Leibniz rule and integration necessarily involves a choice of representatives in
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
. This highlights a phenomenon already well known for the Rumin complex on arbitrary Carnot groups, namely that its construction depends explicitly on the choice of scalar product introduced in Subsection
2.1
.

As observed in Proposition
3.13
, requiring both
\Sigma
and
\partial\Sigma
to be
R
-manifolds is a rather restrictive assumption, and one that is not always necessary. In particular, this requirement can be avoided whenever

\Pi_{E}\alpha=\alpha\ \text{ or }\ d\Pi_{E}\alpha\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\ \text{ for a given }\ \alpha\in E_{0}^{\bullet}\,.

This phenomenon already appears in the simple setting of Heisenberg groups
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Indeed, it is well known that Stokes’ theorem for the Rumin complex is valid on what we define as
locally smooth intrinsic graphs with boundary
, that is
C^{1}
orientable manifolds
\Sigma
with boundary
\partial\Sigma
, where both
\Sigma
and
\partial\Sigma
are, up to a negligible set, locally the images of a
C^{2}
intrinsic graph (see Definition
5.15
). We also recall that the same conclusion also holds for
C^{1}_{\mathbb{H}}
-regular submanifolds with boundary by means of smooth approximation arguments
[
21
,
38
]
. However, as we show in Proposition
5.16
, there is a substantial difference between smooth intrinsic graphs of low dimension and those of low codimension. More precisely, in the
(2n+1)
-dimensional Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{n}
,

•

if
0<k\leq n
, smooth intrinsic graphs are
R
-manifolds;

•

if
n+1\leq k<2n
, smooth intrinsic graphs are generally
not

R
-manifolds.

Nevertheless, the special properties of the Rumin complex in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
compensates for this failure. Indeed, for every
\alpha\in E_{0}^{k}
, one has
\Pi_{E}\alpha=\alpha
for
k\geq n+1
, while
d\Pi_{E}\alpha\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{k+1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
for
k\geq n
.
These two properties are sufficient to recover the validity of Stokes’ theorem on locally smooth intrinsic graphs with boundary also in the high-dimensional regime.

The situation changes drastically for general positively graded groups, and even for Carnot groups. In Proposition
5.18
, we explicitly show that Stokes’ theorem for the Rumin complex may fail on locally smooth intrinsic graphs with boundary in the four-dimensional Carnot group
\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
.

A remarkable feature of our approach is that all the smooth intrinsic graphs that are shown in this paper to be
R
-manifolds are characterised solely through their degree. This concept appears in
[
32
]
to study explicit integration formulas to compute the spherical measure constructed from a homogeneous distance of the group when it is restriced to smooth submanifolds. Area formulas for the spherical measures in Carnot groups or general positively graded group show that the degree of a submanifold coincides with its Hausdorff dimension with respect to
any fixed homogeneous distance of the group.

Indeed, Corollary
5.14
shows that integration over a smooth intrinsic graph annihilates all differential forms whose weight exceeds the degree of the graphing subgroup. Consequently, the problem of determining whether a given locally smooth intrinsic graph is an
R
-manifold reduces to comparing the degree of the of the submanifold with the possible weights of forms appearing in
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
. In particular, the validity of Stokes-type formulas for these classes of locally smooth intrinsic graphs with boundary is governed entirely by the grading structure of the group and by the degree of the submanifold, rather than by finer geometric properties of the graph itself.

This degree-related perspective underlying the notion of
R
-manifold naturally extends beyond the Rumin complex and leads to the study of a new family of differential subcomplexes, namely the spectral complexes

\{(E_{j,l}^{\bullet,\bullet},\Delta_{j})\}_{j\in I_{\bullet,\bullet}}

introduced in
[
45
]
. These complexes arise by interpreting the de Rham complex of a positively graded Lie group as a truncated multicomplex and considering the spectral sequence associated with the filtration by homogeneous weight. In this framework, the exterior differential decomposes according to the grading (
2.3
) and the corresponding spectral sequence isolates, page by page, the components of the differential responsible for specific jumps in weight.

To describe these spectral complexes, one introduces the spaces
Z_{r}^{p,\bullet}
and
B_{r}^{p,\bullet}
, which provide explicit representatives for the pages of the spectral sequence and admit a natural reinterpretation in terms of Rumin forms and the Rumin differential. More precisely, the decomposition

\displaystyle d_{c}=\sum_{j\in I_{p,k}}d_{c}^{j}

of the Rumin differential into homogeneous operators of bidegree
(j,1-j)
gives rise to a corresponding family of spectral differentials

\Delta_{j}\colon E_{j,l}^{p,\bullet}\longrightarrow E_{m,j}^{p+j,\bullet}

each encoding the contribution of the exterior derivative associated with a weight increase of exactly
j
. The resulting spectral complexes therefore refine the Rumin complex by separating the different homogeneous components of the differential structure.

Similarly to what happens for the Rumin complex, these operators admit a direct interpretation through Stokes’ theorem. Indeed, we show that each differential
\Delta_{j}
coincides, up to exact forms and terms of sufficiently high weight, with the classical exterior derivative. Consequently, when integrating over an orientable
C^{1}
manifold
\Sigma
with boundary such that
\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)-\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma)=j
, all irrelevant terms vanish for purely degree-theoretic reasons, and one obtains a Stokes’ formula associated specifically with the spectral differential
\Delta_{j}
. In this way, the geometry of the manifold determines which component of the spectral decomposition governs the integration process. More precisely, this can be rephrased as follows.

Theorem B

(Stokes’ theorem for spectral complexes, Theorem
6.9
)
.

Fix a bidegree
(p,k-1)
for which the operator

\Delta_{j}\colon E_{j,l}^{p,k-1-p}\longrightarrow E_{m,j}^{p+j,k-p-j}

is nonzero, for some admissible choice of
l,m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{G}
be an orientable
k
-dimensional
C^{1}
manifold such that
\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)=p+j
, and assume that
\partial\Sigma
is also an orientable
C^{1}
manifold with
\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma)=p
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}\Delta_{j}\alpha\ \text{ for every }\alpha\in E_{j,l}^{p,k-1-p}\,.

In contrast with the Rumin complex, the validity of Stokes’ theorem for spectral complexes depends crucially on the degree of the underlying submanifold. In Remark
6.13
, we show that the operators
\Delta_{j}\colon E_{j,l}^{p,\bullet}\longrightarrow E_{m,j}^{p+j,\bullet}
do not, in general, satisfy a Stokes’ formula on arbitrary
R
-manifolds. The reason is that the representatives defining the action of
\Delta_{j}
are only determined modulo higher-weight terms and
d
-exact forms whose primitives need not vanish under integration on
R
-manifolds as soon as
j\geq 2
. This shows that the correct framework for Stokes’ theorem for spectral complexes is instead dictated by degree compatibility.
This phenomenon naturally motivates the introduction of distinguished classes of submanifolds adapted to spectral complexes, for which a Stokes-type theorem for the operators
\Delta_{j}

becomes valid.

Definition B

(Spectral manifolds, Definition
6.14
)
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{G}
be a
k
-dimensional orientable
C^{1}
manifold with boundary
\partial\Sigma
. Denote by
P_{h}
the set of weights of nontrivial Rumin forms
E_{0}^{h}
in degree
h
. We say that
\Sigma
is a
spectral manifold with boundary
if

\deg(\Sigma)\in P_{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\deg(\partial\Sigma)\in P_{k-1}.

In case
\Sigma
has no boundary, so it is orientable and only the condition
\deg(\Sigma)\in P_{k}
is satisfied, we say that
\Sigma
is a spectral manifold.

Crucially, by Lemma
5.10
, for a spectral manifold with boundary, we have necessarily that
\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma)<\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)
. This means that, by construction of the spectral complexes, there exists some choices of
l,m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
so that

\Delta_{j}\colon E_{j,l}^{\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma),k-1-\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma)}\longrightarrow E_{m,j}^{\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma),k-\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)}

since
j=\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)-\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma)\geq 1
. This property directly implies that locally smooth intrinsic graphs are indeed spectral manifolds with boundary.

Theorem C

(Stokes’ theorem on locally smooth intrinsic graphs with boundary, Corollary
6.10
)
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{G}
be an orientable
k
-dimensional locally smooth intrinsic graph with boundary
\partial\Sigma
, then

\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}\Delta_{j}\alpha\quad\text{for every }\alpha\in E_{j,l}^{p,k-1-p}\,,

where
p=\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)
,
j=\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)-\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma)
, and some
l\in\mathbb{N}
such that
E_{j,l}^{p,k-1-p}\neq 0
.

The fact that locally smooth intrinsic graphs with boundary satisfy this degree condition is a direct consequence of Proposition
5.5
, so that for
\operatorname{graph}(\phi)
a
k
-dimensional
(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
-graph, we have that
\operatorname{deg}(\operatorname{graph}(\phi))=\operatorname{deg}(\mathbb{W})\in P_{k}
for each degree.

In Section
7
, we reinterpret the spectral complexes
\{(E_{j,l}^{\bullet,\bullet},\Delta_{j})\}_{j\in I_{\bullet,\bullet}}
directly in terms of the de Rham complex and Theorem
B
. More precisely, we show that the action of the spectral differentials
\Delta_{j}
can be recovered from the exterior derivative through the Leibniz rule together with integration over suitable spectral manifolds with boundary. In contrast with the situation for the Rumin complex, no choice of preferred representatives is required when applying the Leibniz rule. The construction is entirely determined by the homogenous weight of the forms involved and the de Rham differential itself, highlighting the fact that even though the spaces of forms
E_{j,l}^{p,\bullet}=Z_{j}^{p,\bullet}\cap\big(B_{l}^{p,\bullet}\big)^{\perp}
are defined using an appropriate scalar product, the action of the spectral differential does not depend on it.

Spectral manifolds, spectral complexes, and Stokes’ theorem also serve as our guiding ideas to find the associated theory of currents. In a few words, as the de Rham currents are associated with the de Rham complex, in positively graded groups one should expect to define currents by duality from suitable subcomplexes of the de Rham complex.

In Heisenberg groups, currents were studied in
[
20
]
, as dual of the Rumin complex, using intrinsic
\mathbb{H}
-regular submanifolds as the intrinsically smooth domains of integration. Then a number of interesting results on Heisenberg currents subsequently appeared, with applications to geometric measure theory, in
[
21
,
20
,
4
,
46
,
26
,
11
,
16
]
. A notion of Rumin currents in Carnot groups has also been proposed and studied in
[

]
.

In Section

, we define spectral currents as duals of spectral complexes.
The fact that the boundary is also a spectral current is not an obvious fact and requires careful considerations on the defining properties of the forms appearing in these subcomplexes, viewed as quotient spaces (see Remark
8.1
). Just like in the definition of spectral manifold, the degree, or weight, of the current plays a crucial role in their construction and their properties. This idea is encoded in the concept of weighted comass (see Definition

) and allows us to define the mass of a
(p+j,k,m,j)
-spectral current
T\colon\mathscr{D}_{m,j}^{p+j,k-p-j}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Note that the boundary
\partial^{j}T
of a
(p+j,k,m,j)
-spectral current

turns out to be a
(p,k-1,j,l)
-spectral current.

In the case of an oriented

C^{1}
spectral manifold
\Sigma
with boundary

, then by Theorem

, we get

\displaystyle[[\partial^{j}\Sigma]]_{p}(\omega)=\int_{\partial\Sigma}\omega=\int_{\Sigma}\Delta_{j}\omega=[[\Sigma]]_{p+j}(\Delta_{j}\omega)\ \text{ for any }\omega\in\mathscr{D}_{j,l}^{p,k-1-p}\,,

where
\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma)=p
and
\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)=p+j
.

This appears to be a unifying view including the well known theories of currents in Heisenberg groups.

2.
The de Rham complex on positively graded Lie groups

In this paper, we will be studying the validity of Stokes’ theorem for both the Rumin complex as well as the spectral complexes introduced in
[
45
]
. In order to make this exposition as self-contained as possible, we provide a brief overview of the main properties of the de Rham complex
(\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G}),d)
on a given connected, simply-connected positively gradable Lie group
\mathbb{G}
. Crucially, we will show that the de Rham complex is a truncated multicomplex, which is the critical property needed for constructing spectral complexes.

Let us start by fixing some notation. Given an
n
-dimensional real Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
with basis
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
,
we will denote by
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
the dual space, and by
(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
the dual basis, namely
\theta_{i}(X_{j})=\delta_{ij}
.
Real-valued linear functionals on
\mathfrak{g}
, that are elements of
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
, are also known as 1-covectors.

Definition 2.1

(Homogeneous and positively graded Lie groups)
.

If
\mathfrak{g}
admits a family
\delta_{\lambda}\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
of automorphisms for
\lambda>0
, we say that the connected, simply-connected Lie group
\mathbb{G}
with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is
homogeneous
. These automorphisms are called

dilations
, and have the form
\delta_{\lambda}=\operatorname{Exp}(A\ln\lambda)
, where
A
is a diagonalisable operator on
\mathfrak{g}
with positive eigenvalues and
\operatorname{Exp}
is the exponential on all the endomorphisms of the vector space
\mathfrak{g}
.

Let
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
be a basis of eigenvectors of
A
. In this basis,
\delta_{\lambda}
is represented by the diagonal matrix
\operatorname{Mat}(\delta_{\lambda})=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda^{\nu_{1}},\ldots,\lambda^{\nu_{n}})
. The eigenvalues repeated according with their multiplicity are
\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
. The distinct eigenvalues
w_{1},\ldots,w_{s}
are called the
weights
of the dilations and are labelled in increasing order
0<w_{1}<\cdots<w_{s}
. We set
\mathfrak{g}_{w_{j}}
as the
w_{j}
-eigenspace for
A
. Then
\mathfrak{g}
decomposes into a direct sum

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{s}\mathfrak{g}_{w_{j}}\ \text{ such that }[\mathfrak{g}_{w_{i}},\mathfrak{g}_{w_{j}}]\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{w_{i}+w_{j}}\ ,\ 1\leq i,j\leq s\,.

This direct sum decomposition is often referred to as a homogeneous structure.

When the weights of the homogenous structure (
2.1
) are positive integers, the group
\mathbb{G}
is said to be
positively graded
. This is equivalent to requiring the weights to be positive rationals, see
[
13
, Lemma 3.1.9]
.

If in addition one can take
w_{1}=1
, with
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
generating the whole Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
via iterated brackets, the Lie group
\mathbb{G}
is called
stratifiable
and we refer to the corresponding direct sum decomposition (
2.1
) as the stratification (given a stratifiable group
\mathbb{G}
, the choice of stratification is unique up to Lie algebra automorphisms
[
29
, Proposition 9.2.9]
).

As a straightforward consequence of the fact that the direct sum decomposition (
2.1
) is finite, we get that
\mathfrak{g}
(and hence also the homogeneous Lie group
\mathbb{G}
with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
) is nilpotent. Note that a given nilpotent Lie group
\mathbb{G}
may admit several non-equivalent homogeneous structures
[
24
]
.

Since the positively graded group
\mathbb{G}
we are considering is connected, simply-connected and nilpotent, its exponential map
\exp\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{G}
is a bijection and a global diffeomorphism. This then allows us to extend the dilations to the group. We will keep the same notation for the dilations on the group, so that
\delta_{\lambda}\colon\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{G}
with
\delta_{\lambda}(\exp X)=\exp(\delta_{\lambda}X)
for any
X\in\mathfrak{g}
.

Definition 2.2

(Carnot groups)
.

A stratifiable Lie group
\mathbb{G}
is
Carnot
if it is equipped with a stratification and a scalar product on
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
. As such, Carnot groups represent the simplest examples of sub-Riemannian manifolds.

Although Carnot groups are the most important setting for the applications of the results proved in this paper, the subcomplexes we will be constructing require a suitable scalar product to be defined on the whole Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
(see Subsection
2.1
). In the case of a non-stratifiable positively gradable Lie group, it is standard to work with a Riemannian metric on
\mathbb{G}
adapted to the homogeneous structure (
2.1
). However, in the case of a Carnot group, where the scalar product is only defined on the first layer
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
, one should extend it to a scalar product on the whole of
\mathfrak{g}
by taking a Riemannian metric on
\mathbb{G}
that
tames
the given sub-Riemannian one
[
40
, Chapter 1]
. There are of course an infinite amount of such Riemannian metrics, but for our purposes it is sufficient to fix one which is adapted to the homogeneous structure (the stratification in this case).

The dilations
\delta_{\lambda}\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
with
\lambda>0
naturally extend to the exterior algebra of covectors
\bigwedge^{\bullet}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
and to the space of smooth forms
\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
via the formula

\displaystyle\left(\delta_{\lambda}\alpha\right)_{x}(V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}):=\alpha_{{\delta_{\lambda}x}}(\delta_{\lambda}V_{1},\ldots,\delta_{\lambda}V_{k})\ \text{ where }\alpha\in\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G}),\,x\in\mathbb{G}\text{ and }V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}\in\Gamma(T\mathbb{G})\big|_{x}\cong\mathfrak{g}\,.

We keep the same notation
\delta_{\lambda}
for these extensions. One can check that the
\delta_{\lambda}
s respect the wedge product, that is

\displaystyle\delta_{\lambda}(\alpha_{1}\wedge\alpha_{2})=(\delta_{\lambda}\alpha_{1})\wedge(\delta_{\lambda}\alpha_{2})\ \text{ for all }\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\,.

Definition 2.3

(Weights of forms)
.

We say that a form
\alpha\in\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
is
homogeneous
if there exists a
p\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
\delta_{\lambda}\alpha=\lambda^{p}\alpha
for all
\lambda>0
. We refer to the number
p
as the
weight
of
\alpha
and we will use the shorthand notation
w(\alpha)=p
. An alternative way of expressing that a form
\alpha
is such that
w(\alpha)=p
is to say that
\alpha
has
pure weight
p
.

Notice that 0-forms have weight
0
, while the volume form
\operatorname{vol}
has weight
Q
equal to the homogeneous dimension
\sum_{j=1}^{s}w_{j}\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{g}_{w_{j}}
of the group
\mathbb{G}
. Such dimension precisely coincides with the Hausdorff dimension of with respect to a fixed homogeneous distance, according to the next definition.

Definition 2.4
.

Given a positively graded group
\mathbb{G}
, a distance
\rho
on
\mathbb{G}
is
homogeneous
if

\displaystyle\rho(xp_{1},xp_{2})=\rho(p_{1},p_{2})\ \text{ and }\ \rho(\delta_{\lambda}(p_{1}),\delta_{\lambda}(p_{2}))=\lambda\rho(p_{1},p_{2})\ \text{ for all }x,p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathbb{G}\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda>0.

Homogeneous distances can be constructed in any homogeneous group,
[
25
]
, and also other general constructions are available,
[
19
]
. In H-type groups, the Cygan-Koranyi distance is another well known example of homogeneous distance,
[
9
]
.

If
\mathbb{G}
is stratified and equipped with a left invariant sub-Riemannian metric, then the well-known
Carnot-Carathéodory distance
can be defined and it is another example of homogeneous distance.

We will denote by
\mathcal{H}^{d}
and
\mathcal{S}^{d}
the
d
-dimensional Hausdorff and spherical Hausdorff measures induced by a fixed homogenous distance
\rho
, respectively. We will denote by
Q
the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathbb{G}
with respect to
\rho
(which always coincides with the weight of the volume form of
\mathbb{G}
).

Remark 2.5
.

When dealing with a Lie group
\mathbb{G}
, one can consider the subcomplex of the de Rham complex consisting of left-invariant differential forms
\Omega^{\bullet}_{L}(\mathbb{G})
. A left-invariant
k
-form is uniquely determined by its value at the identity, where it defines a linear map
\bigwedge^{k}\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{R}
, by identifying the tangent space at the identity with the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, and so
\Omega^{\bullet}_{L}(\mathbb{G})=\bigwedge^{\bullet}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
.

Moreover, in the case of a homogeneous Lie group
\mathbb{G}
, we can identify the tangent space
T_{x}\mathbb{G}
to
\mathbb{G}
at any point
x\in\mathbb{G}
with
\mathfrak{g}\cong T_{e}\mathbb{G}
by means of the isomorphism
dL_{x}
, where
L_{x}
denotes the left-translation by
x\in\mathbb{G}
. For
\xi\in\bigwedge^{k}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
and
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})
, we can regard
f\otimes\xi
as a smooth
k
-form by
(f\otimes\xi)_{x}=f(x)(L_{x^{-1}})^{*}\xi
, giving rise to the isomorphism

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\bigwedge\nolimits^{k}\mathfrak{g},C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})\right)\cong C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})\otimes\bigwedge\nolimits^{k}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}\to\Gamma\left(\bigwedge\nolimits^{k}(T^{*}\mathbb{G})\right)=\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})\,.

Without loss of generality, one can consider a
graded basis

(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
, namely a basis of
\mathfrak{g}
adapted to the direct sum decomposition (
2.1
):

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}_{w_{1}}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{m_{1}}\}\ \text{ and }\ \mathfrak{g}_{w_{i}}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{m_{i-1}+1},\ldots,X_{m_{i}}\}\ \text{ for }2\leq i\leq s\,.

Furthermore, its dual basis
(\theta_{1},\ldots\theta_{n})
also reflects the direct sum decomposition in terms of weights, since
w(\theta_{i})=p
if and only if
X_{i}\in\mathfrak{g}_{p}
.

Lemma 2.6

(Forms of different weight are linearly independent)
.

Let us consider
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})
two arbitrary smooth
k
-forms. If they are both homogeneous with
w(\alpha_{1})\neq w(\alpha_{2})
, then they are linearly independent.

Proof.

Let us first prove the claim for two left-invariant 1-forms
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
. If
w(\theta_{1})=p_{1}\neq w(\theta_{2})=p_{2}
, by definition of weights we have that
\theta_{1}\in\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}_{p_{1}}
and
\theta_{2}\in\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}_{p_{2}}
and they are therefore linearly independent by the direct sum decomposition (
2.1
).

Given
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\bigwedge^{k}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
with
k>1
, if
w(\xi_{1})\neq w(\xi_{2})
then without loss of generality we can assume
\xi_{1}=\theta_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\theta_{i_{k}}
and
\xi_{2}=\theta_{j_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\theta_{j_{k}}
with

\displaystyle w(\xi_{1})=w(\theta_{i_{1}})+\cdots+w(\theta_{i_{k}})\neq w(\xi_{2})=w(\theta_{j_{1}})+\cdots+w(\theta_{j_{k}})\,.

This means that there exists at least one
l\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
such that
w(\theta_{i_{l}})=p_{1}\neq w(\theta_{j_{l}})=p_{2}
, i.e.
\theta_{i_{l}}\in\mathfrak{g}_{p_{1}}^{\ast}
and
\theta_{j_{l}}\in\mathfrak{g}_{p_{2}}^{\ast}
belong to different subspaces of the direct sum decomposition.

Finally, the claim follows from the fact that a smooth form
\alpha\in\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})
is homogeneous of weight
p
if
\alpha=\sum_{j}f_{j}\otimes\xi_{j}
where
f_{j}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})
and
w(\xi_{j})=p
for each
p
.
∎

As a direct consequence, the space of smooth forms
\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})
admits a direct sum decomposition given by the weights. Throughout this paper, we will express this decomposition using the following notation

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})=\Gamma\left(\bigwedge\nolimits^{k}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}\right)=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}p+q=k\\
0\leq p\leq Q\end{subarray}}\Gamma\left(\bigwedge\nolimits^{p,q}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}\right)=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}p+q=k\\
0\leq p\leq Q\end{subarray}}C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})\otimes\bigwedge\nolimits^{p,q}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}p+q=k\\
0\leq p\leq Q\end{subarray}}\Omega^{p,q}(\mathbb{G})\,,

where
\bigwedge^{p,q}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
and
\Omega^{p,q}(\mathbb{G})
denote respectively the space of left-invariant and smooth forms of weight
p
and degree
p+q=k
.

Given a smooth form
\alpha\in\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
which is
not
homogeneous, that is
\alpha=\sum_{j=1}^{\kappa}\alpha_{j}
with each summand having pure weight
w(\alpha_{j})=p_{j}
, we will denote by
(\alpha)_{p_{j}}
the projection of
\alpha
onto
\Omega^{p_{j},\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
, that is
(\alpha)_{p_{j}}=\alpha_{j}
.

Remark 2.7
.

When working with differential forms on Carnot groups,
k
-forms of weight
p
are typically denoted by
\Omega^{k,p}(\mathbb{G})
. However, since spectral sequence techniques are needed to define the spectral complexes used later in the paper, we adopt the notation that is standard in that context.

Lemma 2.8
.

Given a positively graded group
\mathbb{G}
, the direct sum decomposition (
2.1
) of its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
induces a decomposition of the exterior differential
d
acting on smooth forms, which can be easily expressed in terms of the weight increase. For an arbitrary
\alpha\in\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{G})
of pure weight
p
and degree
k
, one can write

(2.3)

\displaystyle d\alpha=d_{0}\alpha+d_{w_{1}}\alpha+\cdots+d_{w_{s}}\alpha

where
d_{0}\alpha\in\Omega^{p,k+1-p}(\mathbb{G})
and
d_{w_{i}}\alpha\in\Omega^{p+w_{i},k+1-w_{i}-p}(\mathbb{G})
for each
i=1,\ldots,s
.

Proof.

Given an arbitrary
k
-form of weight
p
, we have
\alpha=\sum_{j}f_{j}\otimes\xi_{j}
with each
f_{j}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})
and
\xi_{j}\in\bigwedge^{p,k-p}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
. The exterior differential applied to
\alpha
has the following expression

\displaystyle\begin{split}d\bigg(\sum_{j}f_{j}\otimes\xi_{j}\bigg)\left(V_{1},\ldots,V_{k+1}\right)=&\sum_{j}\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}(-1)^{i-1}V_{i}f_{j}\otimes\xi_{j}(V_{1},\ldots,\hat{V}_{i},\ldots,V_{k+1})+\\
&+\sum_{j}\sum_{1\leq i<l\leq k+1}(-1)^{i+l}f_{j}\otimes\xi_{j}\left([V_{i},V_{l}],V_{1},\ldots,\hat{V}_{i},\ldots,\hat{V}_{l},\ldots,V_{k+1}\right)\end{split}

for any
V_{1},\ldots,V_{k+1}\in\Gamma(T\mathbb{G})
.

Using the more streamlined notation
\alpha=\sum_{j}f_{j}\xi_{j}
to express the
k
-form, we get the formula

\displaystyle d\bigg(\sum_{j}f_{j}\xi_{j}\bigg)=\sum_{j}\left(df_{j}\wedge\xi_{j}+f_{j}d\xi_{j}\right)=\sum_{j}df_{j}\wedge\xi_{j}+\sum_{j}f_{j}d\xi_{j}\,.

If we consider a graded basis
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
with dual basis
(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
, we obtain a very explicit expression for the first summand, that is

\displaystyle\sum_{j}df_{j}\wedge\xi_{j}=\sum_{j}\sum_{l=1}^{n}X_{l}f_{j}\theta_{l}\wedge\xi_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{X_{l}\in\mathfrak{g}_{w_{i}}}\sum_{j}X_{l}f_{j}\theta_{l}\wedge\xi_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}d_{w_{i}}\alpha\,.

For each
i=1,\ldots,s
, we see that

\displaystyle d_{w_{i}}\alpha=\sum_{X_{l}\in\mathfrak{g}_{w_{i}}}\sum_{j}X_{l}f_{j}\theta_{l}\wedge\xi_{j}=\big(d\alpha\big)_{p+w_{i}}\in\Omega^{p+w_{i},k+1-p-w_{i}}(\mathbb{G})

since each
X_{l}\in\mathfrak{g}_{w_{i}}
and so
w(\theta_{l})=w_{i}
.

Regarding the second summand, one needs to show that unless
d\xi_{j}
vanishes, we have
w(d\xi_{j})=w(\xi_{j})=p
, i.e. it keeps the weight constant. In the case of homogeneous Lie groups, one can show this by using the group’s dilations. Indeed, this second summand coincides with the action of the exterior derivative on left-invariant forms, which can be seen as

\displaystyle d\xi_{j}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k+1})=\sum_{1\leq i<l\leq k+1}(-1)^{i+l}\xi_{j}\left([X_{i},X_{l}],X_{1},\ldots,\hat{X}_{i},\ldots,\hat{X}_{l},\ldots,X_{k+1}\right)\text{ for all }X_{i}\in\mathfrak{g}\,.

This formula, combined with the fact that dilations
\delta_{\lambda}
are automorphisms of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, readily implies that
d
applied to
\bigwedge^{k}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
commutes with the dilations. More explicitly, given a left-invariant 1-form
\theta_{j}\in\mathfrak{g}_{w_{i}}
, we have that for any
X_{i_{1}},X_{i_{2}}\in\mathfrak{g}

\displaystyle\delta_{\lambda}\left(d\theta_{j}\right)(X_{i_{1}},X_{i_{2}})=

\displaystyle d\theta_{j}(\delta_{\lambda}X_{i_{1}},\delta_{\lambda}X_{i_{2}})=-\theta_{j}\left([\delta_{\lambda}X_{i_{1}},\delta_{\lambda}X_{i_{2}}]\right)=-\theta_{j}\left(\delta_{\lambda}[X_{i_{1}},X_{i_{2}}]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\delta_{\lambda}\theta_{j}([X_{i_{1}},X_{i_{2}}])=d(\delta_{\lambda}\theta_{j})(X_{i_{1}},X_{i_{2}})\,.

Proceeding by induction, if we assume that
d(\delta_{\lambda}\xi_{j})=\delta_{\lambda}(d\xi_{j})
for a given left-invariant
k
-form
\xi_{j}\in\bigwedge^{k}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
, by the Leibniz rule we have

\displaystyle\delta_{\lambda}(d(\theta_{j}\wedge\xi_{j}))=

\displaystyle\delta_{\lambda}(d\theta_{j}\wedge\xi_{j}-\theta_{j}\wedge d\xi_{j})=\delta_{\lambda}(d\theta_{j})\wedge\delta_{\lambda}\xi_{j}-\delta_{\lambda}\theta_{j}\wedge\delta_{\lambda}(d\xi_{j})=d(\delta_{\lambda}\theta_{j})\wedge\delta_{\lambda}\xi_{j}-\delta_{\lambda}\theta_{j}\wedge d(\delta_{\lambda}\xi_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\left(\delta_{\lambda}\theta_{j}\wedge\delta_{\lambda}\xi_{j}\right)=d(\delta_{\lambda}(\theta_{j}\wedge\xi_{j}))\,.

This commutativity then implies that
w(d\xi_{j})=w(\xi_{j})=p
and so

d_{0}\alpha=\sum_{j}f_{j}d\xi_{j}\in\Omega^{p,k+1-p}(\mathbb{G})
.
∎

Before being able to construct spectral complexes in this setting, we first need to show that the cochain complex that we are working on, namely the de Rham complex, is indeed a truncated multicomplex.

Definition 2.9

(Definition 2.1 in
[
30
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a commutative unital ground ring. An
s
-multicomplex (also known as twisted chain complex) is a
(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Z})
-graded
\mathcal{K}
module
C
equipped with maps
d_{i}\colon C\to C
for
i\geq 0
of bidegree
|d_{i}|=(i,1-i)
such that

(2.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{i+j=n}d_{i}d_{j}=0\ \text{ for all }n\geq 0\text{ and }d_{k}=0\text{ for all }k>s\,.

We should also mention that in Definition
2.9
we are choosing a cohomological sign and degree convention for the differentials
d_{i}
.

Proposition 2.10
.

The de Rham complex
(\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G}),d)
on a positively graded group with direct sum decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{w_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{w_{s}}
is a truncated
w_{s}
-multicomplex.

Proof.

The direct sum decomposition according to weights given in (
2.1
) endows the space of smooth forms
\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
with a bigrading. In Lemma
2.6
, we established the existence of differential maps
d_{j}\colon\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\to\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
of bidegree
|d_{j}|=(j,1-j)
. Note that, except for
d_{0}
which is a
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})
-linear map, the differential maps
d_{w_{i}}
with
i=1,\ldots,s
are
\mathbb{R}
-linear. For this reason, even though each
\Omega^{p,q}(\mathbb{G})
is a
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})
-module of bidegree
(p,q)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}
, in this setting
\mathcal{K}=\mathbb{R}
. This fact will play a role when constructing the subspaces of forms
Z_{r}^{\bullet,\bullet}
and
B_{r}^{\bullet,\bullet}
introduced in Definition
6.1
, which will turn out to be
\mathbb{R}
-submodules of
\Omega^{\bullet,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
.

We are left to prove that the formulae in (
2.4
) also hold.
These follow directly from the fact that
(\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G}),d)
is a complex, i.e.
d^{2}=0
, and that forms of different weight are linearly independent (Lemma
2.6
).
Let us first express the exterior derivative as
d=d_{0}+d_{1}+\cdots+d_{w_{s}}
, where for each
j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}

d_{j}
is the zero map unless
j=w_{i}
for some
i=1,\ldots,s
.
Indeed, given a
k
-form
\alpha\in\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{G})
, if we expand the expression for
d^{2}\alpha
according to the weight, we get

\displaystyle d^{2}\alpha=

\displaystyle d\left(d_{0}\alpha+d_{1}\alpha+\cdots+d_{w_{s}}\alpha\right)=(d_{0}+d_{1}+\cdots+d_{w_{s}})\left(d_{0}\alpha+d_{1}\alpha+\cdots+d_{w_{s}}\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{0}^{2}\alpha+\left(d_{0}d_{1}+d_{1}d_{0}\right)\alpha+\left(d_{0}d_{2}+d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}d_{0}\right)\alpha+\cdots+d_{w_{s}}^{2}\alpha=\sum_{n=0}^{2w_{s}}\sum_{i+j=n}d_{j}d_{i}\alpha=0\,.

Each summand
\sum_{i+j=n}d_{j}d_{i}\alpha
has weight
p+n
, that is

\displaystyle\sum_{i+j=n}d_{j}d_{i}\alpha\in\Omega^{p+n,k+1-p-n}(\mathbb{G})\ \text{ for each }n=0,\ldots,2w_{s}\,.

By Lemma
2.6
, we get that each summand must be zero.
∎

Finally, in the context of the de Rham complex on positively graded groups, weights of differential forms are always non-negative. In other words, the space of smooth forms
\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
is an
(\mathbb{Z}^{+},\mathbb{Z})
-graded
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})
-module. Not only this, but since the group
\mathbb{G}
is finite-dimensional, the range for the weights is finite and takes integer values between 0 and the weight
Q
of the volume form.

2.1.
Introducing a scalar product on
\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})

The Rumin complex
(E_{0}^{\bullet},d_{c})
(as presented in Proposition
3.5
) is defined as subspaces of the space of smooth forms
E_{0}^{\bullet}\subset\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
. These
E_{0}^{\bullet}
are isomorphic to the cohomology of the complex
(\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G}),d_{0})
, however since we want to consider them as subspaces of smooth forms and not as quotients, we require a way of identifying complements of the subspace
\operatorname{Im}d_{0}
. This can be easily obtained by introducing a scalar product on
\mathfrak{g}
adapted to the homogeneous structure (
2.1
), which canonically extends to the space of left-invariant forms
\Omega^{\bullet}_{L}(\mathbb{G})=\bigwedge^{\bullet}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
. Since the scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{k}
defined on each
\bigwedge^{k}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
is adapted to the positive grading, we have that forms of different weight are orthogonal, i.e. for each degree
k

(2.5)

\displaystyle\text{ given }\theta\in\bigwedge\nolimits^{p_{1},k-p_{1}}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}\ \text{ and }\ \xi\in\bigwedge\nolimits^{p_{2},k-p_{2}}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}\,,\ \text{ if }p_{1}\neq p_{2}\text{ then }\langle\theta,\xi\rangle_{k}=0\,.

It is then possible to define the Hodge-
\star
operator as the linear map acting on
\bigwedge^{\bullet}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
as

\displaystyle\star\colon\bigwedge\nolimits^{k}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}\xrightarrow{\cong}\bigwedge\nolimits^{n-k}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}\ ,\ \theta\wedge\star\xi:=\langle\theta,\xi\rangle_{k}\operatorname{vol}.

The scalar product can be further extended to the space of smooth forms
\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
viewed as a
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})
-module generated by
\bigwedge^{\bullet}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
. In particular, when considering the subspace
\Omega^{\bullet}_{c}(\mathbb{G})
of compactly supported smooth forms, one can introduce a scalar product, the so-called
L^{2}
-inner product on forms, defined as

\displaystyle\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G}))}:=\int_{\mathbb{G}}\alpha\wedge\star\beta\,.

Here the Hodge-
\star
operator is naturally extended to a
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})
-linear map
\star\colon\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})\longrightarrow\Omega^{n-k}(\mathbb{G})
by viewing the space of smooth forms simply as a
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})
-module generated by
\bigwedge^{\bullet}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
.
It then follows directly from the definition of the
L^{2}
-inner product on forms and (
2.5
) that also in the case of smooth differential forms, forms of different weight are orthogonal.

To reduce the notation used throughout the paper, we will keep
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{k}
to also denote the
L^{2}
-inner product between two smooth differential forms of degree
k
.
Finally, given a subspace
S
of
\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
, we will denote the orthogonal projection onto
S
by
\operatorname{pr}_{S}
.

Definition 2.11

(The adjoint of
d_{0}
)
.

We can use the scalar product just introduced to define the formal transpose (adjoint) of
d_{0}
, which we will denote by
\delta_{0}
. In other words, the map
\delta_{0}\colon\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{G})\to\Omega^{p,k-1-p}(\mathbb{G})
is defined by imposing

\displaystyle\langle d_{0}\alpha,\beta\rangle_{k+1}=\langle\alpha,\delta_{0}\beta\rangle_{k}\ \text{ for any }\alpha\in\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})\text{ and }\beta\in\Omega^{k+1}(\mathbb{G})\,.

The fact that
\delta_{0}
keeps the weight of forms constant, i.e.
w(\delta_{0}\alpha)=w(\alpha)
, is a direct consequence of the fact that elements of different weight are orthogonal and that
d_{0}
keeps the weight of forms constant.

Another crucial operator that can be defined from
d_{0}
using the scalar product is its partial inverse. This operator will be central to the construction of the so-called Rumin differential
d_{c}
.

Definition 2.12

(The partial inverse
d_{0}^{-1}
)
.

The map
d_{0}
being
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})
-linear implies that it acts as a bijection from
\left(\ker d_{0}\right)^{\perp}=\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
onto
\operatorname{Im}d_{0}
. Following Rumin’s construction, it is customary to use the shorthand notation
d_{0}^{-1}
to denote the linear map given by

d_{0}^{-1}:=d_{0}^{-1}\operatorname{pr}_{\operatorname{Im}d_{0}}\colon\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{G})\longrightarrow\Omega^{p,k-1-p}(\mathbb{G})\,.

In particular, it readily follows that
(d_{0}^{-1})^{2}=0
and
\ker d_{0}^{-1}=\ker\delta_{0}=\left(\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\right)^{\perp}
.

Before defining the space of Rumin forms, let us stress that the map
d_{0}
is an extension of the Chevalley-Eilenberg differential to the space of smooth forms, so in particular it is a linear map between finite dimensional spaces (we refer to
[
14
]
for a more thorough explanation of this fact). In particular, this implies that the map
d_{0}\colon\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{G})\to\Omega^{p,k-1-p}(\mathbb{G})
has closed range, a critical property that will be used when working with the algebraic Laplacian associated with
d_{0}
.

Definition 2.13

(The space of Rumin forms)
.

As shown in Proposition
2.10
, the de Rham complex
\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
on a positively graded group
\mathbb{G}
with differential
d=d_{0}+d_{w_{1}}+\cdots+d_{w_{s}}
is a truncated multicomplex. In particular, equation (
2.4
) for
n=0
gives that
d_{0}^{2}=0
, that is
(\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G}),d_{0})
is also a complex. Therefore, it is possible to compute its cohomology. As already mentioned, we are interested in defining subspaces of forms and not quotients. Using the
L^{2}
-scalar product introduced in Subsection
2.1
, one can define the space of Rumin forms
E_{0}^{\bullet}
associated with the truncated multicomplex
(\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G}),d=d_{0}+d_{w_{1}}+\cdots+d_{w_{s}})
as

(2.6)

\displaystyle E_{0}^{k}=\ker d_{0}\cap\left(\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\right)^{\perp}\cap\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})=\ker d_{0}\cap\ker\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})\,,

the last equality following from the closed range theorem.

Definition 2.14

(Orthogonal projection onto
E_{0}^{\bullet}
)
.

Let us consider the operator

\displaystyle\Pi_{0}:=\operatorname{Id}-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}-d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}\colon\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{G})\to\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{G})\,.

From the definition of the partial inverse map
d_{0}^{-1}
, we have that

•

\Pi_{0}
maps elements of
\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{G})
onto elements of
\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{G})
, i.e. it preserves both the weight and the degree of forms;

•

d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}=\operatorname{pr}_{\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}}
and
d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}=\operatorname{pr}_{\operatorname{Im}d_{0}}
;

•

\Pi_{0}^{2}=(\operatorname{Id}-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}-d_{0}^{-1}d_{0})(\operatorname{Id}-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}-d_{0}^{-1}d_{0})=\operatorname{Id}-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}-d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}=\Pi_{0}
.

Therefore, the map
\Pi_{0}
is an orthogonal projection onto the subspace

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}\Pi_{0}=\ker d_{0}\cap\ker d_{0}^{-1}=\ker d_{0}\cap\ker\delta_{0}=E_{0}\,,

that is
\Pi_{0}=\operatorname{pr}_{E_{0}}
.

A useful characterisation of the space of Rumin forms is as the “harmonic forms” of the algebraic Laplacian defined using the operator
d_{0}
and its adjoint
\delta_{0}
. Moreover, such a Laplacian admits a Hodge-decomposition thanks to the fact that
d_{0}
has closed range.

Definition 2.15

(The algebraic Laplacian
\Box_{0}
)
.

Let us consider the Laplacian operator defined using the algebraic differential
d_{0}
and its adjoint
\delta_{0}
, that is

\displaystyle\Box_{0}:=d_{0}\delta_{0}+\delta_{0}d_{0}\colon\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{G})\longrightarrow\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{G})\,.

Just like the projection
\Pi_{0}
,
\Box_{0}
preserves both the weight and the degree of forms. It is a symmetric operator since
\langle\Box_{0}\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\rangle_{k}=\langle\alpha_{1},\Box_{0}\alpha_{2}\rangle_{k}
for any
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})
, and its kernel coincides with the space of Rumin forms

\displaystyle\ker\Box_{0}=\ker d_{0}\cap\ker\delta_{0}=E_{0}\,.

The inclusion
\ker d_{0}\cap\ker\delta_{0}\subset\ker\Box_{0}
is direct, while the converse one follows from the fact that if
\alpha\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})
then

\displaystyle 0=\langle\Box_{0}\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{k}=\langle d_{0}\delta_{0}\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{k}+\langle\delta_{0}d_{0}\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{k}=|d_{0}\alpha|^{2}_{k}+|\delta_{0}\alpha|^{2}_{k}\ \Longrightarrow\ d_{0}\alpha=\delta_{0}\alpha=0\,.

Proposition 2.16

(Hodge decomposition for
\Box_{0}
)
.

The space of smooth forms
\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
admits an orthogonal direct sum (or a Hodge) decomposition in terms of
\Box_{0}
,
d_{0}
and its adjoint
\delta_{0}
. More explicitly,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})=\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\oplus\ker\Box_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\,.

Proof.

The statement follows from the fact that the orthogonal complement of the kernel of a map with closed range coincides with the range of its adjoint: for each degree
k
we have

\displaystyle\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})=

\displaystyle\ker d_{0}\oplus\left(\ker d_{0}\right)^{\perp}=\ker d_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ker d_{0}\cap\ker\delta_{0}\oplus\ker d_{0}\cap\left(\ker\delta_{0}\right)^{\perp}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ker\Box_{0}\oplus\ker d_{0}\cap\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}=\ker\Box_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\,.

∎

The presentation of the Rumin complex adopted here follows the approach developed in

Lemma 3.1
.

If
B\colon\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\to\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
is an operator that strictly increases the weight of forms so that

\displaystyle\text{given }\alpha\in\Omega^{p,\bullet}(\mathbb{G}),\ \text{ we have that }B\alpha\in\Omega^{p^{\prime},\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\text{ with }p^{\prime}>p\,,

then
B
is nilpotent, i.e. there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
independent of
B
such that
B^{N}\equiv 0
.

Proof.

The nilpotency of any operator that strictly increases the weight of forms follows from the assumption that the range of possible weights is bounded, from 0 up to the weight of the volume form
Q=w(\operatorname{vol})
. In particular, the statement holds for any such operator
B
, regardless of how
B
acts on the degree of forms.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
A_{0}\colon\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\to\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
be an invertible linear map that preserves the weights, while
B\colon\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\to\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
is an operator that strictly increases the weights. Then the operator
A:=A_{0}-B\colon\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\to\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
is invertible and its inverse
A^{-1}\colon\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\to\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
has the form

\displaystyle A^{-1}=A_{0}^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\left(BA_{0}^{-1}\right)^{j}=\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\left(A_{0}^{-1}B\right)^{j}\bigg)A_{0}^{-1}\,,

with
N\in\mathbb{N}
the nilpotency exponent of
B
, as discussed in Lemma
3.1
.

Proof.

By construction, we have
A=A_{0}-B=\left(\operatorname{Id}-BA_{0}^{-1}\right)A_{0}=A_{0}\left(\operatorname{Id}-A_{0}^{-1}B\right)
.

The operators
BA_{0}^{-1}
and
A_{0}^{-1}B
act on
\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
by strictly increasing weights, hence they are both nilpotent by Lemma
3.1
. Consequently, we get invertibility via Neumann series: both
\operatorname{Id}-BA_{0}^{-1}
and
\operatorname{Id}-A_{0}^{-1}B
are invertible with

\displaystyle\left(\operatorname{Id}-BA_{0}^{-1}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\left(BA_{0}^{-1}\right)^{j}\ \text{ and }\ \left(\operatorname{Id}-A_{0}^{-1}B\right)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\left(A_{0}^{-1}B\right)^{j}\,.

The claim follows directly from the fact that
A^{-1}=A_{0}^{-1}\left(\operatorname{Id}-BA_{0}^{-1}\right)^{-1}=\left(\operatorname{Id}-A_{0}^{-1}B\right)^{-1}A_{0}^{-1}
. Again, just like in Lemma
3.1
, the statement holds for any such operators
A_{0}
and
B
, regardless of how they act on the degree of forms.
∎

Using these two lemmata, we are now able to construct the necessary projection maps to define the Rumin differential
d_{c}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let us consider the operator
b:=d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}-d_{0}^{-1}d=-d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})
acting on the space of smooth forms
\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
.

(1)

The map
b\colon\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\longrightarrow\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
preserves the degree, it is nilpotent and hence
\operatorname{Id}-b
is invertible.

(2)

The operator

(3.1)

\displaystyle\Pi:=\left(\operatorname{Id}-b\right)^{-1}d_{0}^{-1}d+d\left(\operatorname{Id}-b\right)^{-1}d_{0}^{-1}

is the projection of
\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
onto

\displaystyle F:=\operatorname{Im}d_{0}^{-1}+\operatorname{Im}dd_{0}^{-1}=\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}+\operatorname{Im}d\delta_{0}

along

\displaystyle E:=\ker d_{0}^{-1}\cap\ker d_{0}^{-1}d=\ker\delta_{0}\cap\ker\delta_{0}d\,.

Proof.

(1)

By definition, the operator
b
strictly increases the weight and so by Lemma
3.1
it is nilpotent, i.e. there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
b^{N}=0
. We can now apply Lemma
3.2
taking
A_{0}=\operatorname{Id}
and
B=b
, so the operator
\operatorname{Id}-b
is invertible and its inverse is given by

(3.2)

\displaystyle\left(\operatorname{Id}-b\right)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}b^{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\left[-d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})\right]^{j}\,.

This also implies that
\Pi
as defined in (
3.1
) is a well-defined operator on
\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
.

(2)

By (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), we have that the image of
\Pi
is included in the subspace
F\subset\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
defined in the statement. Moreover, using the properties of
d
,
d_{0}
and
d_{0}^{-1}
we get that

\displaystyle\Pi d_{0}^{-1}=d_{0}^{-1}\ \text{ and }\ \Pi dd_{0}^{-1}=dd_{0}^{-1}

so that
\Pi=\operatorname{Id}
on
F
and
\Pi^{2}=\Pi
, i.e.
\Pi
is a projection onto
\operatorname{Im}\Pi=F
along
\ker\Pi
. Finally,
\ker\Pi
contains the subspace
E
introduced in the statement. Since we also have the equalities

\displaystyle d_{0}^{-1}\Pi=d_{0}^{-1}\ \text{ and }\ d_{0}^{-1}d\Pi=d_{0}^{-1}d\,,

we obtain that
\ker\Pi=E
.

∎

Following Rumin’s notation, we denote the two projections onto
F
and
E
respectively as

\displaystyle\Pi_{F}:=\Pi\ \text{ and }\ \Pi_{E}:=\operatorname{Id}-\Pi\,,

where
\Pi
is the projection defined in (
3.1
).

Lemma 3.4
.

The projector operators
\Pi_{E}
,
\Pi_{F}
, and
\Pi_{0}
enjoy the following properties:

(1)

d_{0}^{-1}\Pi_{E}=\Pi_{E}d_{0}^{-1}=0
;

(2)

d\Pi_{F}=\Pi_{F}d
and
d\Pi_{E}=\Pi_{E}d
;

(3)

\Pi_{0}\Pi_{E}\Pi_{0}=\Pi_{0}
and
\Pi_{E}\Pi_{0}\Pi_{E}=\Pi_{E}
.

Proof.

The equalities in (1) follow from the fact that
d_{0}^{-1}\Pi=\Pi d_{0}^{-1}
, while those in (2) can easily be checked by carrying out the explicit computations that show
d\Pi=d(\operatorname{Id}-b)^{-1}d_{0}^{-1}d=\Pi d
.

Finally, to get the equalities in (3), we need to apply the fact that
\operatorname{Im}\Pi\subset\operatorname{Im}d_{0}^{-1}\subset\left(\ker\Box_{0}\right)^{\perp}
and the equalities in (1), so that

\displaystyle\Pi_{E}\Pi_{0}\Pi_{E}=\Pi_{E}^{2}-\Pi_{E}\left(d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}+d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}\right)\Pi_{E}=\Pi_{E}\ \text{ and }\ \Pi_{0}\Pi_{E}\Pi_{0}=\Pi_{0}^{2}-\Pi_{0}\Pi\,\Pi_{0}=\Pi_{0}\,.

∎

As a consequence of Lemma
3.4
, we get the following result.

Proposition 3.5

(Theorem 2.6 in
[
43
]
)
.

The de Rham complex
(\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G}),d=d_{0}+d_{w_{1}}+\cdots+d_{w_{s}})
splits into two subcomplexes
(E^{\bullet},d)
and
(F^{\bullet},d)
. Moreover, the following operator

(3.3)

\displaystyle d_{c}:=\Pi_{0}d\Pi_{E}\colon E_{0}^{\bullet}\subset\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})\longrightarrow E_{0}^{\bullet}\subset\Omega^{k+1}(\mathbb{G})

satisfies
d_{c}^{2}=0
, the complex
(E_{0}^{\bullet},d_{c})
computes the same cohomology as the initial de Rham complex
(\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G}),d)
and it is known as the Rumin complex.

Proof.

The fact that
(E_{0}^{\bullet},d_{c})
is a complex follows from applying properties (2) and (3) of Lemma
3.4
to the explicit formula (
3.3
) together with the fact that
d^{2}=0
. By property (2) in Lemma
3.4
, we get that
\Pi_{E}
is a homotopical equivalence between the de Rham complex
(\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G}),d)
and the subcomplex
(E^{\bullet},d)
. Finally, property (3) in Lemma
3.4
further implies that
E
and
E_{0}
are in bijection, and that
\Pi_{E}
restricted to
E_{0}
and
\Pi_{0}
restricted to

are inverse maps of each other. Hence, the complex
(E^{\bullet},d)
is conjugated to
(E_{0}^{\bullet},d_{c})
with
d_{c}=\Pi_{0}d\Pi_{E}
, as defined in the statement.
∎

In order to study the properties of the Rumin differential needed to justify the introduction of special manifolds on which Stokes’ theorem holds for
(E_{0}^{\bullet},d_{c})

. Throughout the computations, we will also adopt the shorthand notation introduced in

Definition 3.6
.

Given the maps
d_{0},\,d_{1},\ldots,d_{w_{s}}
that make up the exterior derivative
d
as expressed in (
2.3
) and satisfy the multicomplex relations (
2.4
), together with the partial inverse
d_{0}^{-1}
introduced in Definition
2.12
, we are going to define by induction the following maps on
\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
:

(3.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{1}=d_{1}\ \text{ and }\ \partial_{r}=d_{r}-\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}d_{r-j}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{j}\ \text{ for }r\geq 2\,.

For clarity, let us see the explicit expression of the operator
\partial_{4}
:

\displaystyle\partial_{4}=

\displaystyle d_{4}-\sum_{j=1}^{3}d_{3-j}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{j}=d_{4}-d_{3}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{1}-d_{2}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{2}-d_{1}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{4}-d_{3}d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}-d_{2}d_{0}^{-1}\left(d_{2}-d_{1}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{1}\right)-d_{1}d_{0}^{-1}\left(d_{3}-d_{2}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{1}-d_{1}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{4}-d_{3}d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}-d_{2}d_{0}^{-1}\left(d_{2}-d_{1}d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\right)-d_{1}d_{0}^{-1}\left(d_{3}-d_{2}d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}-d_{1}d_{0}^{-1}d_{2}+d_{1}d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\right)

Note that by definition, each differential operator
\partial_{r}
increases the weight of a form by
r
and its degree by 1. This can be more compactly expressed by stating that
\partial_{r}
has bidegree
|\partial_{r}|=(r,1-r)
, that is:

\displaystyle\text{ for any }\alpha\in\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{G})\ \text{ we have }\ \partial_{r}\alpha\in\Omega^{p+r,k+1-p-r}(\mathbb{G})\,.

Lemma 3.7

(Expressing
d_{c}
on
E_{0}
)
.

Using the operators introduced in Definition
3.6
, one can express the operator
d_{c}
acting on
E_{0}^{\bullet}
as follows:

(3.5)

\displaystyle d_{c}\alpha=\Pi_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha=\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha-d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha\ \text{ for any }\alpha\in E_{0}^{\bullet}\,.

Here is
N\in\mathbb{N}
taken so that
\partial_{N}\alpha=0
for every
\alpha\in E_{0}^{\bullet}
.

Proof.

The existence of some
N\in\mathbb{N}
for which
\partial_{N}\alpha=0
for every
\alpha\in E_{0}^{\bullet}
is guaranteed by the fact that the range of possible weights is bounded. Note that, in general, there will be no relationship between
w_{s}
and
N
. Depending on the group
\mathbb{G}
(and hence the multicomplex structure of the de Rham complex), but also on the weight and degree of the form
\alpha\in\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
, one could have
w_{s}
bigger, smaller, or equal to
N
. The only thing that is certain for any given positively gradable group
\mathbb{G}
is that both
w_{s}
and
N
are finite positive integers.

Let us now focus on the action of
\Pi
on elements of
E_{0}
as defined in (
3.1
). If
\alpha\in E_{0}^{\bullet}
, then
\alpha\in\ker d_{0}\cap\ker\delta_{0}=\ker d_{0}\cap\ker d_{0}^{-1}
, and so the action of
\Pi
simplifies to

\displaystyle\Pi\alpha=

\displaystyle\left(\operatorname{Id}-b\right)^{-1}d_{0}^{-1}d\alpha=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\left[-d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})\right]^{j}d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})\alpha=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}(-1)^{j-1}\left[d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})\right]^{j}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})\alpha-\left[d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})\right]^{2}\alpha+\left[d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})\right]^{3}\alpha+\cdots+(-1)^{N-2}\left[d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})\right]^{N-1}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}}_{d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{1}}\alpha+\underbrace{d_{0}^{-1}(d_{2}-d_{1}d_{0}^{-1}d_{1})}_{d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{2}}\alpha+\underbrace{d_{0}^{-1}\left(d_{3}-d_{2}d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}-d_{1}d_{0}^{-1}d_{2}+d_{1}d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\right)}_{d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{3}}\alpha+\cdots

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{r}\alpha

so that

\displaystyle d\Pi_{E}\alpha=

\displaystyle d\bigg(\alpha-\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{r}\alpha\bigg)=(d-d_{0})\alpha-(d-d_{0})d_{0}^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{w_{s}}d_{j}\alpha-\sum_{j=1}^{w_{s}}d_{j}d_{0}^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{1}\alpha+\cdots+d_{w_{s}}\alpha-d_{1}d_{0}^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha-d_{2}d_{0}^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha-\cdots-d_{w_{s}}d_{0}^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{1}\alpha+\underbrace{d_{2}\alpha-d_{1}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{1}\alpha}_{\partial_{2}\alpha}+\underbrace{d_{3}\alpha-d_{2}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{1}\alpha-d_{1}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{2}\alpha}_{\partial_{3}\alpha}+\cdots+\partial_{N-1}\alpha-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha=\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha-\operatorname{pr}_{\operatorname{Im}d_{0}}\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha=\operatorname{pr}_{(\operatorname{Im}d_{0})^{\perp}}\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha

Finally, since
d_{0}^{-1}
acts trivially on
(\operatorname{Im}d_{0})^{\perp}
and we have just shown that
d\Pi_{E}\alpha\in\ker\Box_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
, we get

\displaystyle\Pi_{0}d\Pi_{E}\alpha=

\displaystyle\left(\operatorname{Id}-d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}\right)d\Pi_{E}\alpha=\left(\operatorname{Id}-d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}\right)\bigg(\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha-d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha\,.

∎

Remark 3.8
.

The fact that
d\Pi_{E}\alpha
belongs to
\left(\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\right)^{\perp}=\ker\Box_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
also follows from the fact that
d\Pi_{E}\alpha=\Pi_{E}d\alpha\in E\subset\ker\delta_{0}=\left(\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\right)^{\perp}
by Lemma
3.3
. This property will be fundamental in the definition of the appropriate manifolds over which we should integrate in order to obtain Stokes’ theorem for
d_{c}
(see Definition
3.10
).

Since
d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}
and
d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}
are two projections of bidegree
(0,0)
, i.e. they keep both the weight and the degree constant, the operator
d_{c}
consists of a sum of operators
\partial_{r}-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{r}-d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}\partial_{r}
of bidegree
|\partial_{r}|=(r,1-r)
. Since we will be interested in each one of such operators, we introduce the following notation

Definition 3.9
.

Given the Rumin differential
d_{c}
whose explicit expression is given in (

), we introduce the notation

\displaystyle d_{c}^{r}=\partial_{r}-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{r}-d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}\partial_{r}\ \text{ for each }r=1,\ldots,N-1

to denote each summand of
d_{c}
of bidegree
|d_{c}^{r}|=(r,1-r)
.

Using the properties of the Rumin differential
d_{c}
, and in particular the fact that
E\subset(\operatorname{Im}d_{0})^{\perp}=\ker\Box_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
, we can now introduce the concept of
R
-manifolds, that is manifolds for which Stokes’ theorem holds for the Rumin complex
(E_{0}^{\bullet},d_{c})
.

Definition 3.10

(
R
-manifolds)
.

Consider
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{G}
an oriented
k
-dimensional
C^{1}
-manifold (with or without boundary). We say that
\Sigma
is an
R
-manifold if

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\eta=0\ \text{ for any }\eta\in\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})\cap\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\,.

Since our results throughout the paper rely on being able to apply the classical Stokes’ theorem, we require sufficient Euclidean regularity of the manifold with boundary we are considering.

Theorem 3.11

(Stokes’ theorem on
R
-manifolds)
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{G}
be a
k
-dimensional
R
-manifold whose boundary
\partial\Sigma
is also an
R
-manifold, then Stokes’ theorem holds for the Rumin complex
(E_{0}^{\bullet},d_{c})
, that is

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\alpha\ \text{ for all }\alpha\in E_{0}^{k-1}\,.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in E_{0}^{k-1}
be Rumin form of degree
k-1
, then on one hand

\displaystyle\Pi_{E}\alpha=\alpha-\Pi\alpha=\alpha-d_{0}^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{N-1}\partial_{r}\alpha\in\ker\Box_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}

while on the other

\displaystyle d\Pi_{E}\alpha=d\Pi_{E}\alpha-d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}d\Pi_{E}\alpha+d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}d\Pi_{E}\alpha=d_{c}\alpha+d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}d\Pi_{E}\alpha\in\ker\Box_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\,.

Therefore, the claim directly follows from the classical Stokes’ theorem since

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha\underbrace{=}_{(\ast)}\int_{\partial\Sigma}\Pi_{E}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}d\Pi_{E}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}\left(d_{c}\alpha+d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}d\Pi_{E}\alpha\right)\underbrace{=}_{(\ast)}\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\alpha\,,

where by
(\ast)
we denote the equalities that follow from
\partial\Sigma
and
\Sigma
being
R
-manifolds.
∎

Remark 3.12
.

As a side note, we remark the same statement can be reached by using the properties listed in Lemma
3.4
since

d\Pi_{E}\alpha=\Pi_{E}d\alpha=\Pi_{E}\Pi_{0}\Pi_{E}d\alpha=\Pi_{E}\Pi_{0}d\Pi_{E}\alpha=\Pi_{E}d_{c}\alpha=d_{c}\alpha+\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\,.

This is the idea behind the definition of the boundary operator
\partial_{c}
introduced in
[
27
]
.

Note that the condition that a manifold
\Sigma
be an
R
-manifold, as in Definition
3.10
, is stronger than what is actually required for the validity of Stokes’ theorem for the Rumin complex. Indeed, when dealing with the boundary term, the assumption that
\partial\Sigma
is an
R
-manifold is unnecessary whenever
\Pi_{E}\alpha=\alpha
. Similarly, the requirement that
\Sigma
itself be an
R
-manifold can be dropped whenever
d\Pi_{E}\alpha\in\ker\Box_{0}
.
Both situations admit natural characterisations in terms of the homogeneous weights of the forms involved.

Proposition 3.13
.

Given
\mathbb{G}
a positively graded group, let us consider a Rumin form
\alpha\in E_{0}^{k-1}
of homogeneous weight
w(\alpha)=p
, then

(1)

if
p=\max\{w(\beta)\mid\beta\in\Omega^{k-1}(\mathbb{G})\}
, then
\Pi_{E}\alpha=\alpha
;

(2)

if
d\Pi_{E}\alpha=(d\Pi_{E}\alpha)_{p+j_{1}}+\cdots+(d\Pi_{E}\alpha)_{p+j_{s}}\in\oplus_{i=1}^{s}\Omega^{p+j_{i},k-p-j_{i}}(\mathbb{G})
then
\left(d\Pi_{E}\alpha\right)_{p+j_{1}}\in E_{0}^{k}
.

Before proceeding with the proof of this Proposition, we need a technical lemma regarding the behaviour of the differentials
\partial_{r}
(see also
[
45
, Lemma 3.3]
).

Lemma 3.14
.

Given an arbitrary
k
-form
\alpha\in\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{G})
with
d_{0}\alpha=0
, i.e.
\alpha\in\ker d_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle d_{0}\partial_{1}\alpha=0\ \text{ and }\ d_{0}\partial_{r}\alpha=-\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}d_{i}\left(\partial_{r-i}-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{r-i}\right)\alpha\ \text{ for any }r\geq 2\,.

Proof.

The first claim readily follows from the fact that
\alpha\in\ker d_{0}
and the multicomplex relations (
2.4
) for the maps
d_{i}
since

\displaystyle d_{0}\partial_{1}\alpha=d_{0}d_{1}\alpha=-d_{1}d_{0}\alpha=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \partial_{1}\alpha\in\ker d_{0}\,.

Proceeding similarly, if
r=2
, we get

\displaystyle d_{0}\partial_{2}\alpha=d_{0}(d_{2}-d_{1}d_{0}^{-1}d_{1})\alpha=-d_{1}^{2}\alpha-d_{2}d_{0}\alpha+d_{1}d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\alpha=-d_{1}(d_{1}-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}d_{1})\alpha=-d_{1}(\partial_{1}-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{1})\alpha\,.

In general, for
r\geq 2
, the multicomplex relations (
2.4
) can be re-expressed as

(3.6)

\displaystyle d_{0}d_{r}=-\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}d_{i}d_{r-i}-d_{r}d_{0}

and so

\displaystyle d_{0}\partial_{r}\alpha=

\displaystyle d_{0}\bigg(d_{r}-\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}d_{r-i}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{i}\bigg)\alpha=-\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}d_{i}d_{r-i}\alpha-d_{r}d_{0}\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\sum_{j=1}^{r-i-1}d_{j}d_{r-i-j}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{i}\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}d_{r-i}d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{i}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}d_{j}d_{r-j}\alpha+\sum_{j=1}^{r-2}\sum_{i=1}^{r-j-1}d_{j}d_{r-i-j}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{i}\alpha+\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}d_{j}d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{r-j}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-d_{r-1}d_{1}\alpha+d_{r-1}d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{1}\alpha-\sum_{j=1}^{r-2}d_{j}\bigg(d_{r-j}-\sum_{i=1}^{r-j-1}d_{(r-j)-i}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{i}\bigg)\alpha+\sum_{j=1}^{r-2}d_{j}d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{r-j}\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-d_{r-1}(\partial_{1}-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{1})\alpha-\sum_{j=1}^{r-2}d_{j}\left(\partial_{r-j}-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{r-j}\right)\alpha=-\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}d_{j}\left(\partial_{r-j}-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{r-j}\right)\alpha\,.

∎

Proof of Proposition
3.13
.

The first property follows from the assumption that
\alpha
is a form of maximum weight amongst all forms in degree
k-1
. Indeed, since
\Omega^{w,k-1-w}(\mathbb{G})=0
for every
w>p=w(\alpha)
, we have

\displaystyle\Pi_{E}\alpha=\alpha-\underbrace{d_{0}^{-1}\sum_{r\geq 1}\partial_{r}\alpha}_{\text{weight }\geq p+1}=\alpha\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{k-1}(\mathbb{G})\,.

The second property requires applying the formulae found in Lemma
3.14
to prove, as a first step, that if
\left(d\Pi_{E}\alpha\right)_{p+l}=0
for each
l=1,\ldots,j_{1}-1
, then
\partial_{j_{1}}\alpha\in\ker d_{0}\cap\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})
. This can be quickly seen using the equality
\left(d\Pi_{E}\alpha\right)_{r}=\partial_{r}\alpha-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{r}\alpha
for each
r\in\mathbb{N}
.

If
j_{1}=1
, then by Lemma
3.14
we directly have that
d_{1}\alpha\in\ker d_{0}\cap\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})
. For
j_{1}>1
, assuming
\left(d\Pi_{E}\alpha\right)_{p+l}=0
for each
l=1,\ldots,j_{1}-1
means that
\partial_{l}\alpha-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{l}\alpha=0
, and so Lemma
3.14
gives

\displaystyle d_{0}\partial_{j_{1}}\alpha=-\sum_{l=1}^{j_{1}-1}d_{j_{1}-l}\left(\partial_{l}-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{l}\right)\alpha=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \partial_{j_{1}}\alpha\in\ker d_{0}\,.

Given
\alpha\in E_{0}^{k-1}
of homogeneous weight
p
, assuming that

\displaystyle d\Pi_{E}\alpha=\left(d\Pi_{E}\alpha\right)_{p+j_{1}}+\left(d\Pi_{E}\alpha\right)_{p+j_{2}}+\cdots+\left(d\Pi_{E}\alpha\right)_{p+j_{s}}\in\bigoplus_{i=1}^{s}\Omega^{p+j_{i},k-p-j_{i}}(\mathbb{G})\,.

implies that the non-trivial component of lowest weight is
\left(d\Pi_{E}\alpha\right)_{p+j_{1}}
, that is
\left(d\Pi_{E}\alpha\right)_{p+l}=0
for every
l=1,\ldots,j_{1}
. This might either happen because
\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{p+l,k-p-l}(\mathbb{G})=0
,
d_{c}^{l}\alpha=0
, or a combination of the two, for each
l=1,\ldots,j_{1}-1
.

By the previous computations, we get
\partial_{j_{1}}\alpha\in\ker d_{0}\cap\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})
, and so
\left(d\Pi_{E}\alpha\right)_{p+j_{1}}=\partial_{j_{1}}\alpha-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{j_{1}}\alpha\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})
.

As a side note, once we know that
\left(d\Pi_{E}\alpha\right)_{p+j_{1}}\neq 0
, there is no way of controlling whether
(d\Pi_{E}\alpha)_{p+i}
belongs to
\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})
for higher weights. Indeed, in general,
(d\Pi_{E}\alpha)_{p+i}\in\big(\ker\Box_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\big)\cap\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})
for any
i>j_{1}
(see Subsection
4.2
for an explicit example where this holds true).
∎

4.
Re-expressing the Rumin complex in view of Stokes’ theorem

In view of Theorem
3.11
, it is possible to reinterpret the Rumin complex, and in particular the action of the Rumin differential, in terms of Stokes’ theorem. The aim of this section is to show that the Rumin complex can be recovered from the de Rham complex solely through the Leibniz rule for the exterior derivative together with integration along suitable manifolds, such as
R
-manifolds.

A key feature of the Heisenberg groups is that no choice of “special representatives” in
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
is required when applying the Leibniz rule. This highlights the naturality of the Rumin complex in this setting (see Subsection
4.1
). By contrast, already in the case of
\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
(see Subsection
4.2
), recovering the Rumin complex from the Leibniz rule and integration does require choosing representatives in
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
. Consequently, the construction depends explicitly on the choice of scalar product introduced in Subsection
2.1
.

The general form of this construction on positively graded groups is stated in Proposition
4.1
.

4.1.
The Rumin complex on
\mathbb{H}^{n}

Let us start by considering the 3-dimensional Heisenberg group, denoting by
(X,Y,T)
the orthonormal basis of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}^{1}
with non-trivial bracket relation
[X,Y]=T
and by
(dx,dy,\tau)
its dual basis. It is well-known that
\mathbb{H}^{1}
is stratifiable with a 2-step stratification
\mathfrak{h}^{1}=V_{1}\oplus V_{2}
given by
V_{1}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X,Y\}
and
V_{2}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{T\}
. Let us study the space of smooth forms
\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{H}^{1})
in terms of the Hodge decomposition (
2.7
) in each degree

•

\Omega^{0}(\mathbb{H}^{1})=C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{1})=\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{0}(\mathbb{H}^{1})
;

•

\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{1})=(\ker\Box_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0})\cap\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{1})
, where
\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{1})=\Omega^{1,0}(\mathbb{H}^{1})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{1})}\{dx,dy\}
,
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{1})=\Omega^{2,-1}(\mathbb{H}^{1})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{1})}\{\tau\}
, and
\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\cap\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{1})=0
;

•

\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{1})=(\ker\Box_{0}\cap\operatorname{Im}d_{0})\cap\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{1})
, where
\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{1})=\Omega^{3,-1}(\mathbb{H}^{1})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{1})}\{dx\wedge\tau,dy\wedge\tau\}
,
\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\cap\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{1})=\Omega^{2,0}(\mathbb{H}^{1})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{1})}\{dx\wedge dy\}
, and
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{1})=0
;

•

\Omega^{3}(\mathbb{H}^{1})=\Omega^{4,-1}(\mathbb{H}^{1})=\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{3}(\mathbb{H}^{1})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{1})}\{dx\wedge dy\wedge\tau\}
.

Let us consider an arbitrary Rumin 1-form
\alpha=f_{1}dx+f_{2}dy\in E_{0}^{1}
, then

\displaystyle d\alpha=

\displaystyle(Xf_{2}-Yf_{1})dx\wedge dy-Tf_{1}dx\wedge\tau-Tf_{2}dy\wedge\tau=-(Xf_{2}-Yf_{1})d\tau-Tf_{1}dx\wedge\tau-Tf_{2}dy\wedge\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-d\big[(Xf_{2}-Yf_{1})\tau\big]+d(Xf_{2}-Yf_{1})\wedge\tau-Tf_{1}dx\wedge\tau-Tf_{2}dy\wedge\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-d\big[(Xf_{2}-Yf_{1})\tau\big]+\big[X(Xf_{2}-Yf_{1})-Tf_{1}\big]dx\wedge\tau+\big[Y(Xf_{2}-Yf_{1})-Tf_{2}\big]dy\wedge\tau

where we only used that fact that
dx\wedge dy\in\operatorname{Im}d_{0}
and

(Xf_{2}-Yf_{1})dx\wedge dy=-d_{0}\big[(Xf_{2}-Yf_{1})\tau\big]=-(Xf_{2}-Yf_{1})d_{0}\tau=-(Xf_{2}-Yf_{1})d\tau\,.

It is therefore sufficient to use the Leibniz rule, namely
gd\tau=d(g\tau)-dg\wedge\tau
for any
g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{1})
.

Rearranging the final formula, we get

\displaystyle d\big[f_{1}dx+f_{2}dy+(Xf_{2}-Yf_{1})\tau\big]=\big[X(Xf_{2}-Yf_{1})-Tf_{1}\big]dx\wedge\tau+\big[Y(Xf_{2}-Yf_{1})-Tf_{2}\big]dy\wedge\tau\,.

This can easily be re-expressed in terms of the Rumin differential, since by the Leibniz rule we have

\displaystyle gd\tau=

\displaystyle d(g\tau)-dg\wedge\tau=d\big[gd_{0}^{-1}d\tau\big]-dg\wedge d_{0}^{-1}d\tau=d\big[d_{0}^{-1}(gd\tau)\big]-(d-d_{0})d_{0}^{-1}(gd\tau)

and so

\displaystyle d\alpha=\underbrace{(Xf_{2}-Yf_{1})dx\wedge dy}_{d_{1}\alpha}\underbrace{-Tf_{1}dx\wedge\tau-Tf_{2}dy\wedge\tau}_{d_{2}\alpha}=d\big[d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\alpha\big]-(d-d_{0})d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\alpha+d_{2}\alpha\,,

that is

\displaystyle d\big[\underbrace{\alpha-d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\alpha}_{\Pi_{E}\alpha}\big]=d_{2}\alpha-d_{1}d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\alpha\longleftrightarrow d\Pi_{E}\alpha=d_{c}\alpha\,.

In other words, up to an exact form
d\eta
with
\eta\in\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\subset\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{1})
, the Rumin differential
d_{c}
coincides with the exterior derivative
d
. Therefore, when integrating over an
R
-manifold
\Sigma
whose boundary
\partial\Sigma
is also an
R
-manifold, Theorem
3.11
follows directly from the classical Stokes’ theorem for the exterior derivative
d
.

The same phenomenon holds more generally on any
\mathbb{H}^{n}
; see Proposition
5.16
for a detailed discussion of the validity of Stokes’ theorem in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
.

It is important to note that, in some situations, it is essential that the initial form
\alpha\in E_{0}^{k-1}
be a Rumin form. To illustrate this phenomenon, it suffices to consider the case of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, although analogous considerations extend readily to every
\mathbb{H}^{n}
with
n\geq 1
. Let us denote by
(X_{1},X_{2},Y_{1},Y_{2},T)
the orthonormal basis of the 5-dimensional Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}^{2}
with non-trivial bracket relations
[X_{1},Y_{1}]=[X_{2},Y_{2}]=T
and by
(dx_{1},dx_{2},dy_{1},dy_{2},\tau)
its dual basis. Then
\mathbb{H}^{2}
is stratifiable with a 2-step stratification
\mathfrak{h}^{2}=V_{1}\oplus V_{2}
given by
V_{1}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{1},X_{2},Y_{1},Y_{2}\}
and
V_{2}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{T\}
. Then the direct sum decomposition (
2.3
) in this case is as follows:

•

\Omega^{0}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{0}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
;

•

\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=(\ker\Box_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0})\cap\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
, where
\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=\Omega^{1,0}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{2})}\{dx_{1},dx_{2},dy_{1},dy_{2}\}
,
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=\Omega^{2,-1}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{2})}\{\tau\}
, and
\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\cap\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=0
;

•

\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=(\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\oplus\ker\Box_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0})\cap\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
, where
\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{2})}\{dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2},dx_{1}\wedge dy_{2},dx_{2}\wedge dy_{1},dy_{1}\wedge dy_{2},dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}-dy_{1}\wedge dy_{2}\}
,
\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\cap\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{2})}\{d\tau\}\subset\Omega^{2,0}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
, and
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=\Omega^{3,-1}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{2})}\{dx_{1}\wedge\tau,dx_{2}\wedge\tau,dy_{1}\wedge\tau,dy_{2}\wedge\tau\}
;

•

\Omega^{3}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=(\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\oplus\ker\Box_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0})\cap\Omega^{3}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
, where
\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{3}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{2})}\{dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}\wedge\tau,dx_{1}\wedge dy_{2}\wedge\tau,dx_{2}\wedge dy_{1}\wedge\tau,dy_{1}\wedge dy_{2}\wedge\tau,(dx_{1}\wedge dy_{1}-dx_{2}\wedge dy_{2})\wedge\tau\}\subset\Omega^{4,-1}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
,
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{3}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{2})}\{d\tau\wedge\tau\}
, and
\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\cap\Omega^{3}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=\Omega^{3,0}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{2})}\{dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}\wedge dy_{1},dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}\wedge dy_{2},dx_{1}\wedge dy_{1}\wedge dy_{2},dx_{2}\wedge dy_{1}\wedge dy_{2}\}
;

•

\Omega^{4}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=(\ker\Box_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}d_{0})\cap\Omega^{4}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
, where
\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{4}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=\Omega^{5,-1}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{2})}\{dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}\wedge dy_{1}\wedge\tau,dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}\wedge dy_{2}\wedge\tau,dx_{1}\wedge dy_{1}\wedge dy_{2}\wedge\tau,dx_{2}\wedge dy_{1}\wedge dy_{2}\wedge\tau\}
,
\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\cap\Omega^{4}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=\Omega^{4,0}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{2})}\{dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}\wedge dy_{1}\wedge dy_{2}\}
, and
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{4}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=0
;

•

\Omega^{5}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{5}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=\Omega^{6,-1}(\mathbb{H}^{2})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{2})}\{dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}\wedge dy_{1}\wedge dy_{2}\wedge\tau\}
.

Let us consider an arbitrary horizontal
2
-form

\alpha=fd\tau+\overline{\alpha}\in\ker d_{0}\ \text{ where }\ \overline{\alpha}=\Pi_{0}\alpha\in E_{0}^{2}\,.

Using reasoning analogous to the one above, we obtain

\displaystyle d\alpha

\displaystyle=d(fd\tau)+d\overline{\alpha}=d\big[d(f\tau)-df\wedge\tau\big]+d\big[d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\overline{\alpha}\big]+d_{c}\overline{\alpha}=d\big(d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\overline{\alpha}-df\wedge\tau\big)+d_{c}\overline{\alpha}

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow d\Big(\alpha-\underbrace{d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\overline{\alpha}+df\wedge\tau}_{\in\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}}\Big)=d_{c}\overline{\alpha}\,.

The crucial point is that the correction term belongs to
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
. Consequently, when integrating over an
R
-manifold
\Sigma
whose boundary
\partial\Sigma
is also an
R
-manifold, this term does not contribute and we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha=\int_{\partial\Sigma}(fd\tau+\overline{\alpha})=\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\overline{\alpha}=\int_{\partial\Sigma}\overline{\alpha}.

Consider now an arbitrary
3
-form
\alpha\in\ker d_{0}\cap\Omega^{3}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
,
for instance

\alpha=fdx_{1}\wedge d\tau+\overline{\alpha}\ \text{ where }\ \overline{\alpha}=\Pi_{0}\alpha\in E_{0}^{3}\subset\Omega^{4,-1}(\mathbb{H}^{2})\ \text{ and }\ fdx_{1}\wedge d\tau\in\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\subset\Omega^{3,0}(\mathbb{H}^{2}).

Then

\displaystyle d\alpha

\displaystyle=d(fdx_{1}\wedge d\tau)+d\overline{\alpha}=d\big[d(fdx_{1}\wedge\tau)-df\wedge dx_{1}\wedge\tau\big]+d_{c}\overline{\alpha}=d\big(df\wedge dx_{1}\wedge\tau\big)+d_{c}\overline{\alpha}

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow d\Big(\alpha+\underbrace{df\wedge dx_{1}\wedge\tau}_{\notin\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}}\Big)=d_{c}\overline{\alpha}\,.

In this case, the previous argument breaks down since, in general,

df\wedge dx_{1}\wedge\tau\in\Omega^{4,-1}(\mathbb{H}^{2})

does not belong to
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
. Hence its integral over
\partial\Sigma
does not vanish in general. Therefore, for an arbitrary
R
-manifold
\Sigma
whose boundary
\partial\Sigma
is also an
R
-manifold, one obtains

\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha-\int_{\partial\Sigma}df\wedge dx_{1}\wedge\tau=\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\overline{\alpha}=\int_{\partial\Sigma}\overline{\alpha}\neq\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha\,.

In this sense, when studying Stokes’ theorem on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, it becomes necessary to restrict to Rumin forms when considering degrees at least 3.

The same reasoning applies more generally on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. For
k\leq n+1
, given an arbitrary form

\alpha\in\ker d_{0}\cap\Omega^{k-1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})\ \text{ with }\ \alpha=\Pi_{0}\alpha+\operatorname{Im}d_{0},

one obtains

\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\Pi_{0}\alpha=\int_{\partial\Sigma}\Pi_{0}\alpha,

provided that both
\Sigma
and
\partial\Sigma
are
R
-manifolds.

Equivalently, up to an exact form whose primitive belongs to
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
, the exterior derivative of
\alpha\in\ker d_{0}\cap\Omega^{k-1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})

coincides with the Rumin differential
d_{c}
of
\Pi_{0}\alpha\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{k-1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
.

By contrast, when
k>n+1
, one must restrict to Rumin forms

\alpha\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{k-1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})

in order to obtain Stokes’ theorem for
d_{c}
.

4.2.
The Rumin complex on
\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}

Let us consider the 4-dimensional connected, simply-connected nilpotent Lie group
\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
, whose Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
is spanned by the orthonormal basis
(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4})
with only one non-trivial Lie bracket
[X_{1},X_{2}]=X_{4}
. For completeness, we also recall the non-commutative group law
(\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R},\ast)
, together with the corresponding left-invariant vector fields and 1-forms (see also
[
28
]
). Given
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})
and
y=(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4})\in\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
, one has

\displaystyle(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\ast(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4})=\big(x_{1}+y_{1},x_{2}+y_{2},x_{3}+y_{3},x_{4}+y_{4}+\tfrac{x_{1}y_{2}-x_{2}y_{1}}{2}\big)

and

\displaystyle X_{1}=

\displaystyle\partial_{1}-\frac{x_{2}}{2}\partial_{4}\ ,\ X_{2}=\partial_{2}+\frac{x_{1}}{2}\partial_{4}\ ,\ X_{3}=\partial_{3}\ ,\ X_{4}=\partial_{4}

\displaystyle\theta_{1}=dx_{1}

\displaystyle\ ,\ \theta_{2}=dx_{2}\ ,\ \theta_{3}=dx_{3}\ ,\ \theta_{4}=dx_{4}-\frac{x_{1}}{2}dx_{2}+\frac{x_{2}}{2}dx_{1}\,.

Using the fact that
d\theta_{1}=d\theta_{2}=d\theta_{3}=0
, while
d\theta_{4}=-\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}
, one readily obtains an explicit description of the space of smooth forms through the direct sum decomposition (
2.7
) in each degree. To simplify the otherwise cumbersome notation, we will denote the group
\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
simply by
\mathbb{G}
thoughout the remainder of this subsection.

•

\Omega^{0}(\mathbb{G})=C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})=\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{0}(\mathbb{G})
;

•

\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{G})=(\ker\Box_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0})\cap\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{G})
, where
\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{G})=\Omega^{1,0}(\mathbb{G})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})}\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3}\}
,
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{G})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})}\{\theta_{4}\}
, and
\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\cap\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{G})=0
;

•

\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{G})=(\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\oplus\ker\Box_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0})\cap\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{G})
, where
\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{G})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})}\{\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{3},\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3},\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{4},\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{4}\}\subset\Omega^{2,0}(\mathbb{G})\oplus\Omega^{3,-1}(\mathbb{G})
,
\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\cap\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{G})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})}\{\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}\}\subset\Omega^{2,0}(\mathbb{G})
, and
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{G})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})}\{\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}\}\subset\Omega^{3,-1}(\mathbb{G})
;

•

\Omega^{3}(\mathbb{G})=(\ker\Box_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}d_{0})\cap\Omega^{3}(\mathbb{G})
, where
\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{3}(\mathbb{G})=\Omega^{4,-1}(\mathbb{G})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})}\{\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{4},\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4},\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}\}
,
\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\cap\Omega^{3}(\mathbb{G})=\Omega^{3,0}(\mathbb{G})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})}\{\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3}\}
, and
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{3}(\mathbb{G})=0
;

•

\Omega^{4}(\mathbb{G})=\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{4}(\mathbb{G})=\Omega^{5,-1}(\mathbb{G})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})}\{\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}\}
.

Similarly to what was done in the case of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, let us first consider an arbitrary horizontal 1-form
\alpha=f_{1}\theta_{1}+f_{2}\theta_{2}+f_{3}\theta_{3}\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{G})
:

\displaystyle d\alpha=

\displaystyle(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}+(X_{1}f_{3}-X_{3}f_{1})\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{3}+(X_{2}f_{3}-X_{3}f_{2})\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3}+

\displaystyle-X_{4}f_{1}\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{4}-X_{4}f_{2}\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{4}-X_{4}f_{3}\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})d\theta_{4}+(X_{1}f_{3}-X_{3}f_{1})\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{3}+(X_{2}f_{3}-X_{3}f_{2})\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3}+

\displaystyle-X_{4}f_{1}\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{4}-X_{4}f_{2}\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{4}-X_{4}f_{3}\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-d\big[(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})\theta_{4}\big]+d(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})\wedge\theta_{4}+(X_{1}f_{3}-X_{3}f_{1})\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{3}+(X_{2}f_{3}-X_{3}f_{2})\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3}+

\displaystyle-X_{4}f_{1}\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{4}-X_{4}f_{2}\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{4}-X_{4}f_{3}\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-d\big[(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})\theta_{4}\big]+(X_{1}f_{3}-X_{3}f_{1})\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{3}+(X_{2}f_{3}-X_{3}f_{2})\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3}+

\displaystyle+\big[X_{1}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})-X_{4}f_{1}\big]\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{4}+\big[X_{2}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})-X_{4}f_{2}\big]\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{4}+

\displaystyle+\big[X_{3}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})-X_{4}f_{3}\big]\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}

so that by rearranging the expression we get

\displaystyle d\big[\alpha+\underbrace{(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})\theta_{4}}_{\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}}\big]=d\big[\underbrace{\alpha-d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\alpha}_{\Pi_{E}\alpha}\big]=

\displaystyle d_{1}\alpha-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\alpha+(d_{2}-d_{1}d_{0}^{-1}d_{1})\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{c}^{1}\alpha+d_{c}^{2}\alpha+\underbrace{\big[X_{3}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})-X_{4}f_{3}\big]\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}}_{\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{c}\alpha+\underbrace{\big[X_{3}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})-X_{4}f_{3}\big]\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}}_{\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}}\,.

This identity is not as straightforward as the one obtained for the Rumin differential on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. In the present setting, the exterior derivative of
\alpha
does not coincide with the Rumin differential
d_{c}\alpha
modulo an exact form whose primitive belongs to
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
. Instead, one has

d\alpha=d_{c}\alpha+\zeta\ \mod d(\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0})\ ,\ \text{ where }\zeta\in\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\,.

Nevertheless, similarly to the previous situation, both correction terms vanish after integrating on
R
-manifolds. Consequently, provided that both
\Sigma
and
\partial\Sigma
are
R
-manifolds, the Stokes’ theorem of Theorem
3.11
again follows directly from the classical Stokes’ theorem for the exterior derivative
d
.

Interestingly, the situation becomes even more delicate when dealing with
2
-forms. Let us consider a horizontal Rumin
2
-form

\alpha=f_{1}\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{3}+f_{2}\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3}\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{2,0}(\mathbb{G}).

A direct computation gives

\displaystyle d\alpha={}

\displaystyle(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3}+X_{4}f_{1}\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}+X_{4}f_{2}\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}\,.

Let us focus on the term
\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3}\in\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\cap\Omega^{3}(\mathbb{G})
.
Since
d_{0}^{-1}(\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3})=\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}\in\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{G})
,
one could repeat the same reasoning as before and write

(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})d(\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4})=(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3}\,,

before applying the Leibniz rule to recover the expression of the Rumin differential up to the exact form

d\big[(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}\big].

However, unlike the case of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, there is no canonical reason to choose the primitive belonging to
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
. Indeed, for every
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3}

\displaystyle=d(\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4})=d(c_{1}\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{4}+c_{2}\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{4}+\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4})

since
\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{4},\,\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{4}\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{3,-1}(\mathbb{G})
.

Notice that choosing an arbitrary horizontal
2
-form would generate a recursive chain of corrections, which is precisely the obstruction avoided by the definition of the projection
\Pi_{E}
. By contrast, choosing a primitive in
\ker d_{0}\cap\Omega^{3,-1}(\mathbb{G})
is perfectly admissible and leads to genuinely different expressions.

Indeed, proceeding in this way yields

\displaystyle d\alpha={}

\displaystyle(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})d(c_{1}\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{4}+c_{2}\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{4}+\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4})+X_{4}f_{1}\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}+X_{4}f_{2}\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle d\Big[(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})(c_{1}\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{4}+c_{2}\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{4}+\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4})\Big]

\displaystyle-d(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})\wedge(c_{1}\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{4}+c_{2}\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{4}+\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4})+X_{4}f_{1}\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}+X_{4}f_{2}\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle d\Big[(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})(c_{1}\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{4}+c_{2}\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{4}+\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4})\Big]

\displaystyle+\big[c_{1}X_{2}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})-c_{2}X_{1}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})\big]\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{4}

\displaystyle+\big[X_{4}f_{1}-c_{1}X_{3}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})+X_{1}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})\big]\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}

\displaystyle+\big[X_{4}f_{2}-c_{2}X_{3}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})+X_{2}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})\big]\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}\,.

Rewriting the previous identity in terms of the projected representative
\Pi_{E}\alpha
, we obtain

\displaystyle d\Big[\Pi_{E}\alpha+(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})

\displaystyle\underbrace{(c_{1}\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{4}+c_{2}\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{4})}_{\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{3,-1}(\mathbb{G})}\Big]={}d_{c}\alpha

\displaystyle+\big[c_{1}X_{2}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})-c_{2}X_{1}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})\big]\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{4}

\displaystyle-c_{1}X_{3}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}-c_{2}X_{3}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}\,.

In particular, the resulting expression depends explicitly on the choice of primitive for the
\operatorname{Im}d_{0}
-component. This phenomenon sharply contrasts with the situation in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, where the Leibniz rule canonically recovers the Rumin differential.

In other words, even if both
\Sigma
and
\partial\Sigma
are
R
-manifolds, integrating the previous identity gives

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial\Sigma}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})(c_{1}\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{4}+c_{2}\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{4})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\alpha+\int_{\Sigma}\big[c_{1}X_{2}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})-c_{2}X_{1}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})\big]\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{4}

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\Sigma}c_{1}X_{3}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}-\int_{\Sigma}c_{2}X_{3}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}.

Therefore, in general, the Stokes’ theorem of Theorem
3.11
is recovered only for the distinguished choice
c_{1}=c_{2}=0
, namely the choice of primitive lying in
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
.

Finally, as in the case of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
in degrees
k>n
, one sees that it is necessary to start with a Rumin form

\alpha\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{k-1}(\mathbb{G})

in each degree (see also Subsection
5.2
). For instance, let us consider an arbitrary
2
-form

\alpha=f\,d\theta_{4}+\overline{\alpha}_{2}+\overline{\alpha}_{3}\in\ker d_{0}\cap\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{G}),

where

\overline{\alpha}_{2}=(\Pi_{0}\alpha)_{2}\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{2,0}(\mathbb{G})\ \text{ and }\ \overline{\alpha}_{3}=(\Pi_{0}\alpha)_{3}\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{3,-1}(\mathbb{G})\,.

Then

\displaystyle d\alpha

\displaystyle=d(f\,d\theta_{4})+d\overline{\alpha}_{2}+d\overline{\alpha}_{3}=d\big[d(f\theta_{4})-df\wedge\theta_{4}\big]+d\big[d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\overline{\alpha}_{2}\big]+d_{c}^{2}\overline{\alpha}_{2}+d_{c}^{1}\overline{\alpha}_{3}.

Equivalently,

\displaystyle d\Big[\alpha

\displaystyle-\underbrace{d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\overline{\alpha}_{2}+X_{3}f\,\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}}_{\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}}+\underbrace{X_{1}f\,\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{4}+X_{2}f\,\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{4}}_{\ker\Box_{0}}\Big]=d_{c}\overline{\alpha},

where
\overline{\alpha}=\Pi_{0}\alpha=\overline{\alpha}_{2}+\overline{\alpha}_{3}
.

Thus, even if both
\Sigma
and
\partial\Sigma
are
R
-manifolds, the
\ker\Box_{0}
-component does not disappear in general, and

\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha+\int_{\partial\Sigma}\Pi_{0}(df\wedge\theta_{4})=\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\overline{\alpha}=\int_{\partial\Sigma}\overline{\alpha}\neq\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha\,.

This shows that, in order to recover Stokes’ theorem for the Rumin differential, one must restrict to Rumin forms rather than arbitrary
d_{0}
-closed representatives.

4.3.
The Rumin differential from the Leibniz rule on arbitrary Carnot groups

The computations carried out in the previous subsections for
\mathbb{H}^{n}
and
\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
show that the Rumin differential can be recovered recursively from the classical exterior derivative by repeatedly applying the Leibniz rule and separating the resulting terms according to the Hodge decomposition (
2.7
) induced by
d_{0}
,
\delta_{0}
, and
\Box_{0}
.

In the case of Heisenberg groups, this procedure is canonical and naturally reproduces the structure of the Rumin complex. By contrast, the example of
\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
highlights that, on more general positively graded groups, the reconstruction process may depend on the choice of primitives for the
\operatorname{Im}d_{0}
-components, and that the role of the projection onto
\ker\Box_{0}
becomes essential.

The aim of this subsection is to formalise this phenomenon in full generality. More precisely, we show that, starting from a Rumin form
\alpha\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{k-1}(\mathbb{G})
, the whole expression of the Rumin differential
d_{c}\alpha
can be reconstructed recursively from the Leibniz rule, modulo terms belonging to
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
.

Proposition 4.1

(The Rumin complex from the Leibniz rule)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a positively gradable Lie group and let
\alpha\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{k-1}(\mathbb{G})
be a Rumin form. Then one can retrieve the whole expression of the Rumin differential from applying the Leibniz rule recursively, up to forms in
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{k-1}(\mathbb{G})
, then

\displaystyle d\alpha=

\displaystyle d_{1}\alpha+d_{2}\alpha+\cdots+d_{w_{s}}\alpha=d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})\alpha+\Pi_{0}(d-d_{0})\alpha+d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}(d-d_{0})\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\big[d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})\alpha\big]-(d-d_{0})d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})\alpha+\Pi_{0}(d-d_{0})\alpha+d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}(d-d_{0})\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\big[d_{0}^{-1}d\alpha\big]-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\big[(d-d_{0})d_{0}^{-1}d\big]\alpha+\Pi_{0}\big[d\alpha-(d-d_{0})d_{0}^{-1}d\alpha\big]+d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}\big[d\alpha-(d-d_{0})d_{0}^{-1}d\alpha\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\big[d_{0}^{-1}d\alpha-d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})d_{0}^{-1}d\alpha\big]+(d-d_{0})d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})d_{0}^{-1}d\alpha+\Pi_{0}\big[d\alpha-(d-d_{0})d_{0}^{-1}d\alpha\big]+

\displaystyle+d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}\big[d\alpha-(d-d_{0})d_{0}^{-1}d\alpha\big]=\cdots=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\big[\sum_{i=0}^{N}(-1)^{i}[d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})]^{i+1}\alpha\big]+\Pi_{0}(d-d_{0})\big[\sum_{i=0}^{N}(-1)^{i}[d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})]^{i}\big]\alpha+

\displaystyle+d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}(d-d_{0})\big[\sum_{i=0}^{N}(-1)^{i}[d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})]^{i}\big]\alpha

where the fact that there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
[d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})]^{N+1}=0
is due to the fact that the range of possible weights of forms in each degree is finite. From the explicit expressions of
\Pi_{E}=\operatorname{Id}-\Pi
as expressed in (
3.2
), we get that

\displaystyle\alpha-\sum_{i=0}^{N}(-1)^{i}[d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})]^{i+1}\alpha=\Pi_{E}\alpha

while from Lemma
3.7
we get that

(d-d_{0})\big[\sum_{i=0}^{N}(-1)^{i}[d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})]^{i}\big]\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{i}\alpha

so that, once we rearrange the whole formula we get

\displaystyle d\big[\Pi_{E}\alpha\big]=

\displaystyle d\bigg[\alpha-\underbrace{\sum_{i=0}^{N}(-1)^{i}[d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})]^{i+1}\alpha}_{\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pi_{0}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{i}\alpha\bigg)+d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{i}\alpha\bigg)=d_{c}\alpha+\underbrace{d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{i}\alpha\bigg)}_{\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}}\,.

∎

Remark 4.2
.

Observe that in the proof of Proposition
4.1
we crucially used the identity

\displaystyle d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\xi=d\big[d_{0}^{-1}\xi\big]-(d-d_{0})d_{0}^{-1}\xi\ \text{ for every }\xi\in\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})\,.

However, the choice of the primitive
d_{0}^{-1}\xi
is not unique. Indeed, for every
\beta\in\ker d_{0}
, one also has

\displaystyle d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\xi=d_{0}(\beta+d_{0}^{-1}\xi)=d\big(\beta+d_{0}^{-1}\xi\big)-(d-d_{0})\big(\beta+d_{0}^{-1}\xi\big).

Consequently, the recursive reconstruction of the exterior derivative in terms of the Rumin differential depends essentially on the distinguished choice of primitive given by
d_{0}^{-1}
. In general, arbitrary choices of
\beta
may introduce additional terms for which the recursive procedure is no longer controlled, even if one assumes
w(\beta)\geq w(\xi)
, which is the condition ensuring the nilpotency of the operator
d_{0}^{-1}(d-d_{0})
.

5.
Intrinsic graphs

In this section, we present some properties of oriented integration on submanifolds, with a special emphasis on the integration of homogeneous differential forms on smooth intrinsic graphs. Oriented integration can also be represented using a Riemannian metric and the corresponding Riemannian surface measure. However, the idea of developing a theory of currents as duals of the Rumin complex leads one to consider a surface measure modelled on the weights of the complex,
[
11
,
46
,
4
,
20
,
33
]
.
The spherical (or the Hausdorff) measure constructed from a homogeneous distance of the group naturally takes into account the weights of the Rumin forms. Actually, this was one of the motivations to find explicit area formulas for the spherical measure of submanifolds, that is strictly related to the degree (or weight) of the submanifold, as previously formulated in
[
34
]
.

The notion of
degree
as introduced in
[
32
]
turns out to be crucial (see also
[
36
,
37
,
7
]
for further developments). As we will see in Definition
5.9
, the notion of degree of a homogeneous
k
-vector is perfectly analogous to the notion of weight of a homogeneous differential form as presented in Definition
2.3
. For this reason, the degree of a
k
-vector and its weight will be understood as being the same, and the two terms will be used interchangeably, as long as the terminology is clear from the context.

The aim of this section is to study the relationship between smooth intrinsic graphs and the
R
-manifolds introduced above. To this end, we first recall the definition of an intrinsic graph, its main properties and the notion of the degree of a submanifold. This will allow us to determine whether a given smooth intrinsic graph can be an
R
-manifold.
To introduce intrinsic graphs, we need to refer to a specific factorisation of
\mathbb{G}
.

Definition 5.1

(Complementary subgroups)
.

Given a positively graded group
\mathbb{G}
together with two homogeneous subgroups
\mathbb{W}
and
\mathbb{V}
of
\mathbb{G}
, we say that
\mathbb{W}
and
\mathbb{V}
are
complementary subgroups
of
\mathbb{G}
if

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}\cap\mathbb{V}=\{e\}\ \text{ and }\ \mathbb{G}=\mathbb{W}\mathbb{V}.

To emphasize the order of factorisation, we may say that
(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
is a
pair of complementary subgroups
.

In equivalent terms,
\mathbb{W}
and
\mathbb{V}
are complementary subgroups if and only if for each
p\in\mathbb{G}
there exists a unique pair
(w,v)\in\mathbb{W}\times\mathbb{V}
such that
p=wv
.

Definition 5.2

(Group projections)
.

If
(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
is a pair of complementary subgroups, we know that for any
p\in\mathbb{G}
there exists a unique pair
(w,v)\in\mathbb{W}\times\mathbb{V}
such that
wv=g
. Consequently the following group projections are well-defined

(5.1)

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathbb{W}}\colon\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{W}\ ,\ \pi_{\mathbb{W}}(wv)=w\ \text{ and }\ \pi_{\mathbb{V}}\colon\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{V}\ ,\ \pi_{\mathbb{V}}(wv)=v.

Notice that the order in the choice of
\mathbb{W}
and
\mathbb{V}
matters. Indeed, if
\mathbb{W}
and
\mathbb{V}
are complementary subgroups in
\mathbb{G}
, i.e.
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{W}\mathbb{V}
, then it is also true that
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{V}\mathbb{W}
. However the non-commutative group operation yields two different group projections. For this reason, indicating the ordered pair
(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
of complementary subgroups uniquely determines the group projections of Definition
5.2
.

We are now in the position to introduce the notion of intrinsic graph.

Definition 5.3

(Intrinsic graphs)
.

Let
(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
be a pair of complementary subgroups of
\mathbb{G}
, and let
A\subset\mathbb{W}
. We define the intrinsic graph of
\phi\colon A\to\mathbb{V}
as the set

\displaystyle\operatorname{graph}(\phi):=\{w\phi(w)\mid w\in A\}\subset\mathbb{G}\,.

The
graph map

\Phi\colon A\to\mathbb{G}
of

is defined as
\Phi(w):=w\phi(w)
for all

.

Intrinsic graphs are quite natural objects in the development of geometric measure theory of Carnot groups. They originally appeared in finding the proper version of the De Giorgi’s rectifiability theorem in Heisenberg groups,
[
18
]
. Subsequently, intrinsic graphs in Carnot groups have been thoroughly studied in
[

,
17

. The intrinsic regularity of an intrinsic graphs comes from the intrinsic differentiability of the defining mapping. Actually, in many cases this request is equivalent to require that the uniformly intrinsically differentiable intrinsic graph is locally the non-critical level set of a Pansu differentiable mapping,
[

,
10
,
1
,

]
, although the problem is still open for arbitrary uniformly intrinsically differentiable graphs.

The relationship between simple left-invariant Rumin forms and pairs of complementary subgroups was already studied in Section 2 of
[

]
. We will include a shorter version here, in order to make this presentation more self-contained.

Lemma 5.4

(Proposition 2.9 in
[
17
]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a Lie group of dimension
n
and denote by
\mathfrak{g}
the Lie algebra of left invariant vector fields on
\mathbb{G}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that a scalar product is fixed in
\mathfrak{g}
. Let
\mathfrak{w}
be a
k
-dimensional subspace of
\mathfrak{g}
, and let
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n-k}
be a basis of
\mathfrak{w}^{\perp}
. If we set
\theta_{i}:=Z_{i}^{\ast}
for
i=1,\ldots,n-k
and
\theta:=\theta_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\theta_{n-k}
, then we have

i.

\mathfrak{w}=\{X\in\mathfrak{g}\mid\iota_{X}\theta=0\}=\{X\in\mathfrak{g}\mid\star\theta\wedge X^{\ast}=0\}
.

ii.

If
\mathfrak{w}
is a Lie subalgebra, then there exists
\eta\in\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
such that
d\theta=\eta\wedge\theta
.

iii.

Conversely, if
\theta=\theta_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\theta_{n-k}\in\bigwedge^{n-k}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
is a simple left invariant form such that
d\theta=\eta\wedge\theta
for some
\eta\in\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
, then

\mathfrak{w}:=\{X\in\mathfrak{g}\mid\iota_{X}\theta=0\}
is a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Here
\iota_{X}
denotes the interior product. Notice that if
\mathfrak{g}
is nilpotent, then
\mathfrak{w}
is a Lie subalgebra if and only if
d\theta=0
.

The previous lemma gives the following proposition, also taken from
[
17
]
.

Proposition 5.5

(Theorem 2.10 in
[
17
]
)
.

Given two

and

two simple left invariant Rumin forms with

such that

, then

\displaystyle\mathfrak{w}:=\{X\in\mathfrak{g}\mid\iota_{X}\theta=0\}\ \text{ and }\ \mathfrak{v}:=\{X\in\mathfrak{g}\mid\iota_{X}\xi=0\}

are both Lie subalgebras of
\mathfrak{g}
. Moreover,
\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{w}=k
,
\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{v}=n-k
, and
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{w}\oplus\mathfrak{v}
. If, in addition,
\xi=\xi_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\xi_{k}
and
\theta=\theta_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\theta_{n-k}
, where all the
\xi_{i}
s and the

and

respectively, then both

and
\mathfrak{v}
are homogeneous Lie subalgebras of

, and so if we set

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}:=\exp(\mathfrak{w})\ \text{ and }\ \mathbb{V}:=\exp(\mathfrak{v})

then

and

are complementary subgroups. In particular, since
\star E_{0}^{k}=E_{0}^{n-k}
, if

, and in this case
\mathfrak{w}
and
\mathfrak{v}
are orthogonal.

Conversely, suppose
\mathfrak{w}
and
\mathfrak{v}
are homogeneous Lie subalgebras of

such that

and
\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{v}=n-k

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
\mathfrak{g}
,
\xi\in E_{0}^{k}
, and

such that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{w}:=\{X\in\mathfrak{g}\mid\iota_{X}\theta=0\}\ \text{ and }\ \mathfrak{v}:=\{X\in\mathfrak{g}\mid\iota_{X}\xi=0\}\,.

Remark 5.6
.

Proposition
5.5
highlights a clear correspondence between the space of homogeneous simple Rumin forms and complementary subgroups.
A natural question is whether one can always find a basis of Rumin forms made of homogeneous simple left-invariant forms. Indeed, this is not the case. Take for example the 9-dimensional 2-step nilpotent Lie group
\mathbb{G}
whose Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
has the following non-trivial brackets:

\displaystyle[X_{1},X_{2}]=[X_{3},X_{4}]=T_{1}\ ,\ [X_{1},X_{3}]=T_{2}\ ,\ [X_{1},X_{4}]=T_{3}\ ,\ [X_{2},X_{3}]=T_{4}\ ,\ [X_{2},X_{4}]=T_{5}\,.

Then, if
(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4},T_{1},T_{2},T_{3},T_{4},T_{5})
is taken as an orthonormal basis of
\mathfrak{g}
and we use the notation
dx_{i}=X_{i}^{\ast}
and
\tau_{j}=T_{j}
to denote the dual orthonormal basis of
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
, straightforward computations show that

\displaystyle E_{0}^{2}=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})}\{dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}-dx_{3}\wedge dx_{4}\}\oplus\Theta^{3,-1}_{16}

where
\Theta^{3,-1}_{16}
denotes a 16-dimensional subspace of
\Omega^{3,-1}(\mathbb{G})
, the space of smooth 2-forms of weight 3. In this case, it is clear that one cannot find a basis of simple covectors to span the 1-dimensional subspace of Rumin 2-forms of weight 2.

To prove the statements needed to study whether smooth intrinsic graphs are
R
-manifolds, we will first introduce some crucial concepts. We refer for instance to
[
36
,
6
]
for more information.

Definition 5.7

(Definition 2.1 in
[
32
]
)
.

Let
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
be a basis of
\mathfrak{g}
adapted to the grading (
2.1
). The degree
\operatorname{deg}(j)
of
X_{j}
is the unique integer
p
such that
X_{j}\in V_{p}
. Moreover, given
X_{J}:=X_{j_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge X_{j_{k}}
be a simple
k
-vector of
\bigwedge^{k}\mathfrak{g}
, then the degree of
X_{J}
is the integer
\operatorname{deg}(J)
defined as the sum
\operatorname{deg}(j_{1})+\cdots+\operatorname{deg}(j_{k})
.

By comparing this concept of degree of a simple
k
-vector with Definition
2.3
, it is clear that the two concepts coincide.

Let us now fix a graded metric
g
on
\mathbb{G}
, namely a left-invariant Riemannian metric on
\mathbb{G}
such that the subspaces
V_{w_{i}}
in (
2.1
) are orthogonal. It is easy to observe that all left-invariant Riemannian metrics such that the basis
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
adapted to the grading is orthogonal are graded metrics and the family of
\{X_{J_{k}}=X_{j_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge X_{j_{k}}\}_{J_{k}}
forms an orthonormal basis of
\bigwedge^{k}\mathfrak{g}
with respect to the induced metric. The norm induced by
g
on
\bigwedge^{k}\mathfrak{g}
will be simply denoted by
|\cdot|
.

Definition 5.8

(Definition 2.3 in
[
32
]
)
.

Let
\tau\in\bigwedge^{k}\mathfrak{g}
be a simple
k
-vector and let
1\leq p\leq Q
be a natural number. Let
\tau=\sum_{J_{k}}\tau_{J_{k}}X_{J_{k}}
be expressed in terms of a fixed orthonormal basis
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
adapted to the grading. Similarly to the notation introduced in the case of covectors (and forms), the projection of
\tau
to the component of degree
r
is defined as

\displaystyle\big(\tau\big)_{r}=\sum_{d(J_{k})=r}\tau_{J_{k}}X_{J_{k}}\,.

The
degree
of
\tau
is then defined as the maximum degree, that is

\displaystyle\operatorname{deg}(\tau)=\max\{r\in\mathbb{N}\mid(\tau)_{r}\neq 0\}\,.

The same concept can be expressed in terms of weights, that is, given a non-homogeneous
k
-vector
\tau
expressed as a linear combination of simple
k
-vectors, the degree of
\tau
is understood as the maximal homogeneous weight of the basis
k
-vectors with non-zero coefficients.

We are now ready to define the degree of a submanifold.

Definition 5.9

(Degree of a submanifold)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a
C^{1}

k
-dimensional submanifold of a positively graded Lie group
\mathbb{G}
. For
x\in\Sigma
, let
\tau_{\Sigma}(x)\in\bigwedge^{k}\mathfrak{g}
be any unit tangent
k
-vector to
\Sigma
at
x\in\Sigma
with respect to an arbitrary auxiliary Riemannian metric
\tilde{g}
, i.e.
|\tau_{\Sigma}(x)|_{\tilde{g}}=1
. Then the degree of the point
x\in\Sigma
is defined as

\displaystyle\operatorname{deg}_{\Sigma}(x)=\operatorname{deg}(\tau_{\Sigma}(x))\,,

while the degree of the submanifold
\Sigma
is defined as

\displaystyle\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)=\max\{\operatorname{deg}_{\Sigma}(x)\mid x\in\Sigma\}\,.

We will say that the point
x\in\Sigma
has maximum degree if
\operatorname{deg}_{\Sigma}(x)=\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)
.

It is not difficult to show that this definition is independent of the choice of the auxiliary metric
\tilde{g}
, since the degree is invariant under multiplication by non-zero scalars. Moreover, it is independent of the choice of the fixed adapted basis
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
and depends only on the tangent spaces
T_{x}\Sigma
and the homogeneous structure (
2.1
) of
\mathfrak{g}
, i.e. on the homogeneous weight/degree of
\tau_{\Sigma}(x)
.

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{G}
be a
C^{1}
oriented manifold with boundary. Then
\deg(\partial\Sigma)<\deg(\Sigma)
.

Proof.

Let
k
be the dimension of
\Sigma
. Let us consider
p\in\partial\Sigma
such that
d_{\partial\Sigma}(p)=\deg(\partial\Sigma)
, let
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{k})
be a basis of
T_{p}\Sigma
, such that
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{k-1})
is a basis of
T_{p}(\partial\Sigma)
. Whenever
1\leq k^{\prime}\leq n
, we set

I(k^{\prime},n)=\{(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k^{\prime}})\in(\mathbb{Z}^{+})^{k^{\prime}}\mid 1\leq j_{1}<j_{1}<\cdots<j_{k^{\prime}}\leq n\}

and observe that there exists
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k-1})\in I(k-1,n)
such that
\deg(\beta)=\deg(\partial\Sigma)
.
Consider now the matrix

\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}c_{1}^{1}&c_{2}^{1}&\cdots&c_{k}^{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
c_{1}^{n}&c_{2}^{n}&\cdots&c_{k}^{n}\end{array}\right)=C,

where
e_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i}^{j}X_{j}(p)
. We consider the
k
-tangent vector

\tau_{\Sigma}(p)=\sum_{\gamma\in I(k,n)}\tau_{\gamma}^{\Sigma}\;X_{\gamma}=\sum_{\gamma\in I_{\beta}(k,n)}\tau_{\gamma}^{\Sigma}X_{\gamma}+\sum_{\gamma\in I(k,n)\setminus I_{\beta}(k,n)}\tau_{\gamma}^{\Sigma}X_{\gamma}\in\Lambda^{k}(T_{p}\Sigma)

and we have defined

I_{\beta}(k,n)=\left\{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{k})\in(\mathbb{N}^{+})^{k}\;\middle|\;1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{k}\leq n,\;\{\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k-1}\}\subset\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\}\right\}.

By contradiction, if we have

\tau_{\gamma}^{\bar{\Sigma}}=0\qquad\text{for all }\gamma\in I_{\beta}(k,n),

then all rows of
C
are linear combinations of the rows

(c_{1}^{\beta_{1}},\ldots,c_{k}^{\beta_{1}}),\;(c_{1}^{\beta_{2}},\ldots,c_{k}^{\beta_{2}}),\;\ldots,(c_{1}^{\beta_{k-1}},\ldots,c_{k}^{\beta_{k-1}})

of
C
, therefore proving that the rank of
C
is less than
k
.
This conflicts with the fact that
\tau_{\Sigma}(p)\neq 0
.
We have proved that there exists
\alpha\in I_{\beta}(k,n)
such that

\tau^{\Sigma}_{\alpha}\neq 0
, hence

d(\Sigma)\geq\deg(\alpha)>\deg(\beta)=\deg(\partial\Sigma),

where the strict inequality holds since
\alpha
contains all indexes of
\beta
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
\alpha\in\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{G})
be a
k
-form of homogeneous weight
w(\alpha)=p
and let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{G}
be an oriented
k
-dimensional compact submanifold.
If
p>\deg(\Sigma)
, then
\int_{\Sigma}\alpha=0
.

Proof.

We set
N=\deg(\Sigma)
. By assumption,
w(\alpha)=p
and so we may write
\alpha=\sum_{I\colon w(\theta_{I})=p}f_{I}\theta_{I}
. We can orient
\Sigma
by a unit tangent
k
-vector field
\tau
on
\Sigma
, where
\tau=\sum_{J\colon w(X_{J})\leq N}\tau_{J}X_{J}
. Here, both
f_{I}
and
\tau_{J}
are smooth functions on
\Sigma
. Then we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}\alpha(\tau)\,d\mathrm{vol}_{k}=\sum_{I:\,w(\theta_{I})=p}\sum_{J:\,w(X_{J})\leq N}\int_{\Sigma}f_{I}\tau_{J}\,\theta_{I}(X_{J})\,d\mathrm{vol}_{k}

where
\operatorname{vol}_{k}
is the
k
-dimensional Riemannian surface measure on
\Sigma
, and
\{\theta_{I}\}
is the dual basis to
\{X_{I}\}
. Since
w(\theta_{I})=p>w(X_{J})
for each
J
by the assumption
p>N
, we readily get that
I\neq J
and hence
\theta_{I}(X_{J})=0
for every such pair
I,J
by Lemma
2.6
. Thus the claim holds true.
∎

Remark 5.12
.

Let
\mathbb{W}\subset\mathbb{G}
be any homogeneous subgroup of a positively graded group
\mathbb{G}
. Since
\mathbb{W}
is invariant under the group dilations of
\mathbb{G}
, it inherits a natural structure of homogeneous positively graded Lie group. In particular, if the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{w}
of
\mathbb{W}
admits the graded decomposition
\mathfrak{w}=W_{w_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus W_{w_{s}}
, then it is straightforward to notice that the Jacobian of the dilations
\{\delta_{\lambda}|_{\mathbb{W}}\}_{\lambda>0}
is
\lambda^{Q_{\mathbb{W}}}
, where

Q_{\mathbb{W}}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}w_{i}\,\dim W_{w_{i}}

is the
homogeneous dimension
of
\mathbb{W}
.
If we endowed
\mathbb{W}
with a homogeneous distance
\rho
, then the uniqueness of the Haar measure yields
C>0
such that

\mathcal{S}^{Q_{\mathbb{W}}}\big(B_{\mathbb{W}}(w,r)\big)=C\,r^{Q_{\mathbb{W}}},

where
B_{\mathbb{W}}(w,r)
denotes the metric ball in
\mathbb{W}
of center
w
and radius
r
with respect to
\rho
,
and
\mathcal{S}^{Q_{\mathbb{W}}}
is the
Q_{\mathbb{W}}
-dimensional spherical measure constructed from
\rho
.
In particular, the Hausdorff dimension
\operatorname{dim}_{H}(\mathbb{W})
of
\mathbb{W}
with respect to
\rho
satisfies

\dim_{H}(\mathbb{W})=Q_{\mathbb{W}}\,.

Moreover, we choose a graded basis
(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k})
of
\mathfrak{w}
, where

k=\operatorname{dim}(\mathbb{W})
and
\operatorname{dim}(\mathbb{W})
denotes the topological dimension of
\mathbb{W}
. Due to the left invariance of
Y_{j}
, we have

\deg\big(Y_{1}(w)\wedge\cdots\wedge Y_{k}(w)\big)=Q_{\mathbb{W}}=\dim_{H}\mathbb{W}

for every
w\in\mathbb{W}
. We may finally conclude that

\deg(\mathbb{W})=Q_{\mathbb{W}}=\dim_{H}(\mathbb{W}).

The next proposition proves the constancy of the pointwise degree on a smooth intrinsically differentiable intrinsic graph
\Sigma
. It mainly relies on two area formulas for the spherical measure of a submanifold. In particular the equality
\dim_{H}\Sigma=\dim_{H}\mathbb{W}
will arise as an important tool. Here
\mathbb{W}
denotes the first factor of the factorising pair
(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
.

Proposition 5.13
.

Let
\Sigma=\operatorname{graph}(\phi)\subset\mathbb{G}
be a
(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
-graph, with
\phi\colon A\to\mathbb{V}
,
A\subset\mathbb{W}
open, and
\phi
of class
C^{2}
. If
\phi
is also continuously intrinsically differentiable, then
\operatorname{deg}_{\Sigma}(x)=\deg(\mathbb{W})
for every
x\in\Sigma
.

Proof.

Let
\Phi:A\subset W\to\mathbb{G}
be the intrinsic graph map defined by
\Phi(w)=w\phi(w)
.
We set
N=\deg(\mathbb{W})
.
Since
\phi
is continuously intrinsically differentiable, we can apply the area formula for intrinsic graphs (see
[
5
, Theorem 1.2]
). Moreover, by
[
5
, Proposition 7.2]
, the intrinsic Jacobian
J\Phi
is continuous and strictly positive on
A
.

Therefore, for every open, bounded, and sufficiently small set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{G}
such that
\Sigma_{\Omega}:=\Sigma\cap\Omega\neq\emptyset
, we have
0<\mathcal{S}^{N}(\Sigma_{\Omega})<\infty
,
where
N
is the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathbb{W}
.

Let
N_{0}:=\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)
be the degree of
\Sigma
, and define
\Sigma_{0}:=\{x\in\Sigma:\operatorname{deg}_{\Sigma}(x)=N_{0}\}
,
which is a nonempty open subset of
\Sigma
. Set

A_{0}:=\Phi^{-1}(\Sigma_{0})\subset A
,
which is a nonempty open subset of
A
.

Since
\Phi
is a
C^{1}
diffeomorphism onto
\Sigma
, it provides a global parametrization of
\Sigma
. Hence, for every open, bounded, and sufficiently small set
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{G}
such that
\emptyset\neq\Sigma_{\Omega^{\prime}}:=\Sigma\cap\Omega^{\prime}\subset\Sigma_{0}
, we can apply the area formula for submanifolds (see
[
32
, (1.4)]
) and obtain
0<\mathcal{S}^{N_{0}}(\Sigma_{\Omega^{\prime}})<\infty
.
Since a set cannot have finite positive spherical measure in two different dimensions, it follows that
N=N_{0}
.

Next, combining the intrinsic area formula with the classical Euclidean area formula, we obtain that there exists a constant
C>0
such that for every open set
A^{\prime}\subset A_{0}
,

\displaystyle\int_{A^{\prime}}J\Phi(w)\,d\mathcal{H}^{k}_{|\cdot|}=\int_{\Phi(A^{\prime})}\frac{J\Phi(\Phi^{-1}(x))}{J_{g}\Phi(\Phi^{-1}(x))}\,d\mathrm{vol}_{k}(x)\leq C\int_{\Phi(A^{\prime})}|\tau_{\Sigma}^{N}(x)|\,d\mathrm{vol}_{k}(x),

where
J_{g}\Phi
denotes the classical
k
-dimensional Jacobian of
\Phi
with respect to the fixed scalar product
g
. Thus, taking the averaged integrals on
\Sigma_{0}\cap B_{r}(x)
with respect to
\mathrm{vol}_{k}
and letting
r\to 0^{+}
, we get
J\Phi(\Phi^{-1}(x))\leq C|\tau_{\Sigma}^{N}(x)|\cdot J_{g}\Phi(\Phi^{-1}(x))
.
Since both
J\Phi
and
J_{g}\Phi
are continuous and strictly positive on
A
, it follows that

|\tau_{\Sigma}^{N}(\Phi(w))|>0\quad\text{for all }w\in A_{0}
.

We now prove that
A_{0}=A
. Assume by contradiction that
A_{0}\subsetneq A
. Since
A
is open and connected and
A_{0}
is nonempty and open, there exists a point
w_{0}\in\partial A_{0}\cap A
.
Let
x_{0}:=\Phi(w_{0})\in\Sigma
. Since
w_{0}\notin A_{0}
, we have
x_{0}\notin\Sigma_{0}
, hence

\operatorname{deg}_{\Sigma}(x_{0})<N=N_{0}\ \Longrightarrow\ |\tau_{\Sigma}^{N}(x_{0})|=0\,.

On the other hand, taking a sequence
w_{j}\in A_{0}
such that
w_{j}\to w_{0}
, and using the continuity of all terms, we can pass to the limit in
J\Phi(w_{j})\leq C\,|\tau_{\Sigma}^{N}(\Phi(w_{j}))|\,J_{g}\Phi(w_{j})

to obtain

J\Phi(w_{0})\leq C\,|\tau_{\Sigma}^{N}(x_{0})|\,J_{g}\Phi(w_{0})=0,

which contradicts the fact that
J\Phi(w_{0})>0
.
Therefore,
A_{0}=A
, and so every point of
\Sigma
has degree
N
.
∎

As a direct consequence of Lemma
5.11
together with Proposition

, we get the following result.

Corollary 5.14
.

Let
\Sigma=\operatorname{graph}(\phi)\subset\mathbb{G}
be a
(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
-graph,

open, and
\phi\colon A\to\mathbb{V}

Due to the importance of intrinsic graphs in geometric measure theory on Carnot groups, we introduce a class of surface that are at an intermediate step between smooth surfaces and intrinsically regular surfaces.

Definition 5.15

(Locally smooth intrinsic graphs)
.

We say that
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{G}
, an orientable

if, for any point

outside a negligible set, there exists a neighbourhood

of

such that

is a

intrinsic graph.

We say that an orientable

is a

if both
\Sigma
and
\partial\Sigma
are locally smooth intrinsic graphs.

5.1.
Locally smooth intrinsic graphs vs.
R
-manifolds on Heisenberg groups

Let us first study the simple case of
\mathbb{G}
being the
2n+1
-dimensional Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. It is well-known that Stokes’ theorem for the Rumin complex holds on locally smooth intrinsic graphs with boundary given by a smooth intrinsic graph, as also shown in
[
21
,
17
,
11
]
.

In this subsection, we will rephrase the same result in a slightly different way: we will first show that locally smooth intrinsic graphs of topological dimension

are indeed
R
-manifolds, while those of topological dimension
k>n
in general are not. Crucially, Stokes’ theorem also holds in all dimensions, because for
k>n
we are precisely in case (1) of Proposition

, and for each

the Rumin differential is homogeneous, so we are also in case (2) of Proposition
3.13

Proposition 5.16

(Stokes’ theorem for the Rumin complex on locally intrinsic graphs in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
)
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
be a
k
-dimensional locally smooth intrinsic graph
\Sigma
with boundary
\partial\Sigma
, then

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\alpha\ \text{ for any }\alpha\in E_{0}^{k-1}\,.

In the case of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, there is a straightforward characterisation of the spaces of Rumin forms
E_{0}^{k}
in terms of their weight (being horizontal or vertical). Moreover, the weight of forms will also determine which space in the direct sum decomposition (

) they belong to.

Let us denote by
(X_{i},Y_{i},T)_{i=1}^{n}
denote the orthonormal basis of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}^{n}
with non-trivial bracket relations
[X_{i},Y_{i}]=T
for each
i=1,\ldots,n
. We will use
(dx_{i},dy_{i},\tau)_{i=1}^{n}
for its dual basis. It is well-known that
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is stratifiable with a 2-step stratification
\mathfrak{h}^{n}=V_{1}\oplus V_{2}
given by
V_{1}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{i},Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
V_{2}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{T\}
, so that for each degree
k=1,\ldots,2n
the space of smooth forms splits into

and
vertical
forms, that is
\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})=\Omega^{k,0}(\mathbb{G})\oplus\Omega^{k+1,-1}(\mathbb{G})
. In degree
k=0
, the space of 0-forms is made of smooth functions that only have weight 0, while in top degree the volume form has weight
2n+2
. Furthermore

•

for
1\leq k\leq n
,
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}=\Omega^{k+1,-1}(\mathbb{G})
;

•

for
k=1
,
\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\cap\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{G})=0
,
\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{G})=\Omega^{1,0}(\mathbb{G})
, and
\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{G})\cap\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}=\Omega^{2,-1}(\mathbb{G})
;

•

for
1<k\leq n
,
\Omega^{k,0}(\mathbb{G})=\big(\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\oplus\ker\Box_{0}\big)\cap\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})
, while
\Omega^{k+1,-1}(\mathbb{G})=\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})
;

•

for
n+1\leq k\leq 2n
,
\Omega^{k,0}(\mathbb{G})=\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\cap\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})
, and
\Omega^{k+1,-1}(\mathbb{G})=\big(\ker\Box_{0}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\big)\cap\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})
;

•

for
k=2n
,
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{2n}(\mathbb{G})=0
,
\Omega^{2n,0}(\mathbb{G})=\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\cap\Omega^{2n}(\mathbb{G})
, and
\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{2n}(\mathbb{G})=\Omega^{2n+1,-1}(\mathbb{G})
.

Crucially, in each degree the space
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})
is always a subspace of the space of vertical forms
\Omega^{k+1,-1}(\mathbb{G})
, that is the space of
k
-forms of weight
k+1
.

On the other hand, intrinsic graphs on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
of low dimension are always “horizontal” submanifolds, that is given
\operatorname{graph}(\phi)\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
a
(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
-graph with
\operatorname{dim}\mathbb{W}=k\leq n
, then
\operatorname{deg}(\operatorname{graph}(\phi))=\deg(\mathbb{W})=k
. By Corollary
5.14
, we obtain that, as long as
1\leq k\leq n
,

\displaystyle\int_{\operatorname{graph}(\phi)}\eta=0\ \text{ for any }\eta\in\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})

since
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})=\Omega^{k+1,-1}(\mathbb{G})
if
k\leq n
.
As a direct consequence, we have that for a locally smooth intrinsic graph
\Sigma
of dimension
1\leq k\leq n
, we have
\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)=k
, since the degree of
\Sigma
is the maximum amongst all possible degrees, which are necessarily all equal to
k
. By Corollary
5.14
, this implies that such low-dimensional locally smooth intrinsic graphs
\Sigma
are indeed
R
-manifolds as defined in Definition
3.10
. By Theorem
3.11
, this implies that for
1\leq k\leq n
, given
\Sigma
an orientable
k
-dimensional locally smooth intrinsic graph with boundary
\partial\Sigma
, Stokes’ theorem holds and

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\alpha\ \text{ for all }\alpha\in E_{0}^{k-1}\ \text{ with }0\leq k<n\,.

In the high-dimensional case (or, equivalently, low codimension as usually expressed in the literature), this is not the case. Indeed, for
n<k<2n
, we have that
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{k}(\mathbb{G})
is a non-trivial subspace of
\Omega^{k+1,-1}(\mathbb{G})
, and the integration of these forms on intrinsic smooth submanifolds of low codimension might not vanishing.

We first notice that given
\operatorname{graph}(\phi)\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
a
(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
-graph with
\operatorname{dim}\mathbb{W}=k\geq n+1
, then
\operatorname{deg}(\mathbb{W})=k+1
and so Corollary
5.14
does not apply. Indeed, already in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, one can easily come up with an example of a smooth intrinsic graph of dimension
3
over which the integral of the form
d\tau\wedge\tau\in\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
does not vanish.

It suffices to consider the trivial smooth and continuously intrinsically differentiable intrinsic graph

\Sigma_{0}=\{(x_{1},0,x_{2},0,t):x_{1},x_{2},t\in\mathbb{R}\}

with
\phi\equiv 0
and coordinates
(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2},t)
.
We have that, since the 3-form
\tau\wedge d\tau\in\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
can be expressed explicitly as

\displaystyle\tau\wedge d\tau=

\displaystyle\tau\wedge(dy_{1}\wedge dx_{1}+dy_{2}\wedge dx_{2})=dt\wedge dy_{1}\wedge dx_{1}+dt\wedge dy_{2}\wedge dx_{2}+

\displaystyle+\frac{y_{1}dx_{1}\wedge dy_{2}\wedge dx_{2}-x_{1}dy_{1}\wedge dy_{2}\wedge dx_{2}+y_{2}dx_{2}\wedge dy_{1}\wedge dx_{1}-x_{2}dy_{2}\wedge dy_{1}\wedge dx_{1}}{2}\,,

it is straightforward to check that for every nonnegative and nonvanishing
\varphi\in C_{c}^{0}(\Sigma_{0})
, one has

\int_{\Sigma_{0}}\varphi\,\tau\wedge d\tau=\int_{\operatorname{supp}\varphi}\varphi\,dt\wedge dx_{3}\wedge dx_{1}\neq 0.

In other words, in low codimension smooth and continuously intrinsically differentiable graphs
need not be

R
-manifolds, although Stokes’ theorem for the Rumin complex still holds for them. This is due to the following two facts: for forms of degree
k>n
we have

•

E_{0}^{k}\subset\Omega^{k+1,-1}(\mathbb{G})
, that is Rumin forms have maximal weight, and so
\Pi_{E}\alpha=\alpha
for every
\alpha\in E_{0}^{k}
by (1) of Proposition
3.13
;

•

given
\alpha\in E_{0}^{k}\subset\Omega^{k+1,-1}(\mathbb{G})
, we have
d\Pi_{E}\alpha=\big(d\Pi_{E}\alpha\big)_{k+2}
and so
d\Pi_{E}\alpha\in\ker\Box_{0}
by (2) of Proposition
3.13
, i.e.
d_{c}\alpha=d\Pi_{E}\alpha
(in this specific case, since
\Pi_{E}\alpha=\alpha
, we have a further simplification, as
d_{c}\alpha=d\alpha
).

Hence, given a locally smooth intrinsic graph
\Sigma
with boundary
\partial\Sigma
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}d\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\alpha\ \text{ for all }\alpha\in E_{0}^{k-1}\text{ with }n<k\leq 2n+1\,,

and both integrals are non-trivial since
\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma)=k
and
\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)=k+1
, and we are integrating vertical forms
\alpha
and
d_{c}\alpha
on them respectively (has
\alpha
and
d_{c}\alpha
been horizontal form, both integrals would vanish by Corollary
5.14
).

Finally, the case of degree
n
, where the Rumin differential
d_{c}\colon E_{0}^{n}\to E_{0}^{n+1}
has differential order 2, is a mixture of both cases. Let
\Sigma
be an
(n+1)
-dimensional locally smooth intrinsic graph with boundary
\partial\Sigma
. Since
\alpha\in E_{0}^{n}\subset\Omega^{n,0}(\mathbb{G})
, we have
\Pi_{E}\alpha\neq\alpha
, however
\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma)=n
, and so
\partial\Sigma
is an
R
-manifold. The
n+1
-dimensional
\Sigma
is not since
\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)=n+2
. However,
d\Pi_{E}\alpha=(d\Pi_{E}\alpha)_{n+2}
for any
\alpha\in E_{0}^{n}
, and so by (2) in Proposition
3.13
we have that
d\Pi_{E}\alpha\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{n+1}(\mathbb{G})
and
d_{c}\alpha=d\Pi_{E}\alpha
. We can therefore directly apply Stokes’ theorem to get

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha\underbrace{=}_{\partial\Sigma\;R\text{-mfld}}\int_{\partial\Sigma}\Pi_{E}\alpha\underbrace{=}_{\text{Stokes' thm}}\int_{\Sigma}d\Pi_{E}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\alpha\ \text{ for all }\alpha\in E_{0}^{n}\,.

5.2.
Locally smooth intrinsic graphs vs.
R
-manifolds on the group
\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}

Let us consider the 4-dimensional connected, simply-connected nilpotent Lie group
\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
introduced in Subsection
4.2
.
As already stated in Proposition
5.5
, the splittings of the Lie algebra are in a one-to-one correspondence with the space of simple Rumin forms. Let us use the computations already carried out in Subsection
4.2
to study whether locally smooth intrinsic graphs in
\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
are
R
-manifolds.

\operatorname{dim}(\mathbb{W})=1

In degree 1, the space of Rumin 1-forms is 3-dimensional and spanned by
\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3}\}
, 3 simple horizontal 1-forms which correspond to the following three choices of complementary subgroups for smooth intrinsic
(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
-graphs:

•

(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
with
\exp(\mathbb{W})=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{1}\}
and
\exp(\mathbb{V})=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{2},X_{3},X_{4}\}
;

•

(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
with
\exp(\mathbb{W})=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{2}\}
and
\exp(\mathbb{V})=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{1},X_{3},X_{4}\}
;

•

(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
with
\exp(\mathbb{W})=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{3}\}
and
\exp(\mathbb{V})=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{1},X_{2},X_{4}\}
.

In each case,
\operatorname{deg}(\mathbb{W})=1
, while
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R})=\Omega^{2,-1}(\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R})
, and so by Corollary
5.14
we get that for any 1-dimensional smooth intrinsic graph

\displaystyle\int_{\operatorname{graph}(\phi)}\eta=0\ \text{ for any }\eta\in\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R})

by Corollary
5.14
, since
w(\eta)=2>1=\operatorname{deg}(\operatorname{graph}(\phi))
.
Therefore, every 1-dimensional locally smooth intrinsic graph is an
R
-manifold.

\operatorname{dim}(\mathbb{W})=3

Using Hodge-
\star
duality considerations, The case of 3-dimensional smooth intrinsic graphs is complementary to the 1-dimensional one, so that we get the following three choices of complementary subgroups for
(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
-graphs:

•

(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
with
\exp(\mathbb{W})=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{1},X_{2},X_{4}\}
and
\exp(\mathbb{V})=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{3}\}
;

•

(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
with
\exp(\mathbb{W})=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{1},X_{3},X_{4}\}
and
\exp(\mathbb{V})=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{2}\}
;

•

(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
with
\exp(\mathbb{W})=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{2},X_{3},X_{4}\}
and
\exp(\mathbb{V})=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{1}\}
.

Interestingly,
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{3}(\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R})=0
so 3-dimensional smooth intrinsic graphs are trivially
R
-manifolds for any choice of complementary subgroups.

\operatorname{dim}(\mathbb{W})=2

The space of Rumin 2-forms is spanned by the following simple forms
\{\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{3},\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3},\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{4},\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{4}\}\subset\Omega^{2,0}(\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R})\oplus\Omega^{3,-1}(\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R})
, which correspond to the following four choices of complementary subgroups for
(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
-graphs:

(1)

(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
with
\exp(\mathbb{W})=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{1},X_{3}\}
and
\exp(\mathbb{V})=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{2},X_{4}\}
;

(2)

(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
with
\exp(\mathbb{W})=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{2},X_{3}\}
and
\exp(\mathbb{V})=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{1},X_{4}\}
;

(3)

(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
with
\exp(\mathbb{W})=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{1},X_{4}\}
and
\exp(\mathbb{V})=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{2},X_{3}\}
;

(4)

(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
with
\exp(\mathbb{W})=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{2},X_{4}\}
and
\exp(\mathbb{V})=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{X_{1},X_{3}\}
.

Note that for the first two choices of complementary subgroups we have that
\operatorname{deg}(\mathbb{W})=2
, while for the final two
\operatorname{deg}(\mathbb{W})=3
. Moreover, since
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R})\subset\Omega^{3,-1}(\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R})
, directly from Corollary
5.14
, we get that any locally smooth intrinsic graph of degree 2, i.e. constructed using the complementary subgroups (1) or (2), is an
R
-manifold. The same reasoning does not apply to locally smooth intrinsic graphs of degree 3 constructed using the complementary subgroups (3) and (4). It is not hard to construct examples of smooth intrinsic graphs of constant degree 3 that are
not

R
-manifolds.

Remark 5.17
.

Let us consider the pair of complementary subgroups
(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
in (3) as above. We are interested in showing that one can find an associated smooth intrinsic graph
\Phi(w)=w\phi(w)
which
is not
an
R
-manifold. For this claim to hold, it suffices to find a smooth and continuously intrinsically differentiable function
\phi\colon U\to\mathbb{V}
,
where
U\subset\mathbb{W}
is any open bounded set, such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Phi(U)}\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}\neq 0\ \text{ since }\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\cap\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R})=\operatorname{span}_{C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R})}\{\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}\}\,.

By the specific choice (3) of complementary subgroups, we get that

\displaystyle\Phi(w)=w\phi(w)=\big(w_{1},0,0,w_{4}\big)\,\big(0,\phi_{2}(w),\phi_{3}(w),0\big)=\big(w_{1},\phi_{2}(w),\phi_{3}(w),w_{4}+w_{1}\phi_{2}(w)/2\big)\,.

Then, using the explicit expression of the left-invariant 1-forms
\theta_{i}
in Subsection
4.2
, we get that

\displaystyle\int_{\Phi(U)}\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}=

\displaystyle\int_{U}\Phi^{\ast}\big(\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}\big)=\int_{U}\Phi^{\ast}\big(dx_{3}\wedge dx_{4}+\tfrac{x_{1}}{2}dx_{2}\wedge dx_{3}-\tfrac{x_{2}}{2}dx_{1}\wedge dx_{3}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{U}\big(d\Phi_{3}\wedge d\Phi_{4}+\tfrac{\Phi_{1}}{2}d\Phi_{2}\wedge d\Phi_{3}-\tfrac{\Phi_{2}}{2}d\Phi_{1}\wedge d\Phi_{3}\big)=\int_{U}\big(\partial_{1}\phi_{3}(w)-{\phi_{2}(w)}{}\partial_{4}\phi_{3}(w)\big)dx_{1}\wedge dx_{4}\,.

A simple example of a smooth intrinsically differentiable map
\phi\colon U\subset\mathbb{W}\to\mathbb{V}
for which the integral
\int_{\Phi(U)}\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}
does not vanish is given by
\phi(w)=(0,w_{1})
, namely
\phi_{2}(w)\equiv 0
and
\phi_{3}(w_{1},w_{4})=w_{1}
, so that
\partial_{1}\phi_{3}(w)-\phi_{2}(w)\partial_{4}\phi_{3}(w)=1
. Thus, the integral does not vanish.
The continuous intrinsic differentiability of
\phi\colon U\to\mathbb{V}
could be checked directly, or observing that
\Sigma=\Phi(U)
is also a non-critical level set of a continuously Pansu differentiable function and then apply
[
8
, Theorem 4.3.7]
. We observe that

\operatorname{graph}(\phi)=f^{-1}(0)=\{(w_{1},0,w_{1},w_{4}):(w_{1},w_{4})\in U\}\ ,\text{ where }\ f(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},w_{4})=(w_{2},w_{3}-w_{1})

and
f:E\to\mathbb{V}\approx\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with
E=\{(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}):(w_{1},w_{4})\in U,w_{2},w_{3}\in\mathbb{R}\}
.

The previous remark shows that 2-dimensional, smooth intrinsic graphs of degree 3 in
\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
are not
R
-manifolds in general. However, unlike the case of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, we are not able to leverage extra properties of the Rumin complex. In fact, we will see that Stokes’ theorem for the Rumin complex will not hold on them.

Proposition 5.18

(Stokes’ theorem for the Rumin complex on locally smooth intrinsic graphs in
\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
)
.

Let
\alpha\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R})
be a Rumin
k
-form with
k\geq 1
and let
\Sigma
be a
(k+1)
-dimensional locally smooth intrinsic graph with boundary
\partial\Sigma
, then

k=1
:

if
\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)=2
, then
\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\alpha
;

k=1
:

if
\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)=3
, then
\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha\neq\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\alpha
;

k=2
:

if
\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma)=p=w(\alpha)
, then
\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\alpha
;

k=2
:

if
\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma)<p=w(\alpha)
, then
\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha\neq\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\alpha
.

Proof.

Let us first consider the case of
\alpha\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R})
. We have already shown that 1-dimensional locally smooth intrinsic graphs and 2-dimensional locally smooth intrinsic graphs of degree 2 are
R
-manifolds, hence the first claim directly follows from Theorem
3.11
.

Let us consider the case where
\partial\Sigma
is a 1-dimensional smooth intrinsic graph, while
\Sigma
is a 2-dimensional locally smooth intrinsic graph of degree 3. Then, taking an arbitrary Rumin 1-form
\alpha=f_{1}\theta_{1}+f_{2}\theta_{2}+f_{3}\theta_{3}
gives

\displaystyle d\Pi_{E}\alpha=

\displaystyle d\big(\alpha-d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\alpha\big)=d\big(\alpha+(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})\theta_{4}\big)=d_{c}\alpha+[X_{3}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})-X_{4}f_{3}]\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}\,.

Since in general
\int_{\Sigma}\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}\neq 0
(see Remark
5.17
), we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha=\int_{\partial\Sigma}\Pi_{E}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}d\Pi_{E}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\alpha+[X_{3}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})-X_{4}f_{3}]\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}\neq\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\alpha

for an appropriate choice of the horizontal 1-form
\alpha
for which
X_{3}(X_{1}f_{2}-X_{2}f_{1})-X_{4}f_{3}\neq 0
.

The case of
\alpha\in E_{0}^{2}
and
\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma)=w(\alpha)
covers exactly two cases: when
w(\alpha)=2
and
\partial\Sigma
is a locally smooth intrinsic graph of degree 2, and when
w(\alpha)=3
and
\partial\Sigma
is a locally smooth intrinsic graph of degree 3. In the first case, since both
\partial\Sigma
and
\Sigma
are
R
-manifold, the claim follows directly from Theorem
3.11
. On the other hand, if
w(\alpha)=3
, we have that
\partial\Sigma
is not an
R
-manifold, however since
3
is the maximal possible weight of forms in degree 2, we have that property (1) of Proposition
3.13
applies, so that Stokes’ theorem holds true since
\Pi_{E}\alpha=\alpha
.

Finally, if
w(\alpha)=2
while
\partial\Sigma
is a locally smooth intrinsic graph of degree 3 (which is the only possibility for
w(\alpha)<\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma)
to be true), then
\Pi_{E}\alpha=\alpha+\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}\neq\alpha
and since in general
\int_{\partial\Sigma}\theta_{3}\wedge\theta_{4}\neq 0
we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha\neq\int_{\partial\Sigma}\Pi_{E}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}d\Pi_{E}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\alpha

the last equality following from the fact that
\Sigma
is an
R
-manifold.
∎

To recap, in this section we have shown that in general locally smooth intrinsic graphs are not
R
-manifolds. In some cases, (for example in the case of Heisenberg groups) one can rely on some extra properties of the Rumin complex to obtain Stokes’ theorem on locally smooth intrinsic graphs
\Sigma
with boundary
\partial\Sigma
, i.e.
\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\alpha=\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha
. In the case where such properties do not hold, however, one is not able to recover the same formula.

6.
Stokes’ Theorem for Spectral Complexes

In this section, similarly to what we have done for the Rumin complex, we study the validity of Stokes’ theorem for the spectral complexes
\{(E_{j,l}^{\bullet,\bullet},\Delta_{j})\}_{j\in I_{\bullet,\bullet}}
introduced in
[
45
]
. These subcomplexes arise from the de Rham complex when the latter is viewed as a truncated multicomplex (see Proposition
2.10
).

We begin by describing the main structural properties of these subcomplexes via the graded
\mathbb{R}
-modules
Z_{r}^{p,\bullet}
and
B_{r}^{p,\bullet}
, introduced in
[
30
]
. These subspaces of forms represent an alternative method used to describe the quotient spaces
E_{r}^{p,\bullet}
arising at each page of the spectral sequence associated with the filtration given by homogeneous weight. We then show that these spaces admit an equivalent formulation in terms of Rumin forms and the Rumin differential
d_{c}
.
Finally, once the main properties are covered, the validity of Stokes’ theorem on smooth intrinsic graphs for the spectral complexes
\{(E_{j,l}^{\bullet,\bullet},\Delta_{j})\}_{j\in I_{\bullet,\bullet}}
follows as a direct consequence of Corollary
5.14
.

Let us quickly recall the definitions of the modules
Z_{r}^{p,\bullet}
and
B_{r}^{p,\bullet}
, together with the induced differentials
\Delta_{r}\colon Z_{r}^{p,\bullet}/B_{r}^{p,\bullet}\to Z_{r}^{p+r,\bullet}/B_{r}^{p+r,\bullet}
. Here we adopt the indexing and notation consistent with the bidegree convention used in Definition
2.9
. For a more thorough presentation of such submodules we refer to
[
45
]
.

Definition 6.1

(Definition 2.6 in
[
30
]
)
.

Let
\alpha\in\Omega^{p,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
and let
r\geq 1
. We define the graded
\mathbb{R}
-submodules
Z_{r}^{p,\bullet}
and
B_{r}^{p,\bullet}
of
\ker d_{0}\cap\Omega^{p,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
as follows.

\displaystyle\alpha\in Z_{r}^{p,\bullet}\ \Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\ \text{for }1\leq j\leq r-1\,,\text{ there exists }z_{p+j}\in\Omega^{p+j,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\text{ such that}

\displaystyle\ d_{0}\alpha=0\text{ and }d_{n}\alpha=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}d_{i}z_{p+n-i}\text{ for all }1\leq n\leq r-1\,.

\displaystyle\alpha\in B_{r}^{p,\bullet}\ \Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\ \text{for }0\leq k\leq r-1\text{ there exists }c_{p-k}\in\Omega^{p-k,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\text{ such that}

\displaystyle\ \alpha=\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}d_{k}c_{p-k}\text{ and }0=\sum_{k=l}^{r-1}d_{k-l}c_{p-k}\text{ for }1\leq l\leq r-1\,.

Proposition 6.2

(Theorem 2.10 in
[
30
]
)
.

The
r^{th}
differential of the spectral sequence corresponds to the map

\displaystyle\Delta_{r}\colon Z_{r}^{p,\bullet}/B_{r}^{p,\bullet}\longrightarrow Z_{r}^{p+r,\bullet}/B_{r}^{p+r,\bullet}\ ,\ \Delta_{r}\big([x]\big)=\bigg[d_{r}x-\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}d_{i}z_{p+r-i}\bigg]

where

and the family

is chosen so as to satisfy the relations of Definition
6.1
.

Remark 6.3
.

It easily follows from the definitions of both
Z_{r}^{\bullet,\bullet}
and
B_{r}^{\bullet,\bullet}
that

\displaystyle\alpha\in B_{r}^{p,\bullet}\Longleftrightarrow\exists\,c_{p-r+1}\in Z_{r-1}^{p-r+1,\bullet}\text{ such that }d(c_{p-r+1}+c_{p-r+2}+\cdots+c_{p})=\alpha+\Omega^{\geq p+1,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})

where the forms
c_{p-i}\in\Omega^{p-i,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
for
i=0,\ldots,r-2
satisfy the equations in Definition
6.1
, and
\Omega^{\geq p+1,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
denotes all the forms of weight at least
p+1
.

This characterisation will play a central role throughout this section and the next one, in particular in the analysis of the validity of Stokes’ theorem. With a slight abuse of notation, we summarise it by writing

\displaystyle B_{r}^{p,\bullet}=dZ_{r-1}^{p-r+1,\bullet}+\Omega^{\geq p+1,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\,.

Similarly, for any
\alpha\in Z_{r}^{p,\bullet}
, one has

\displaystyle\Delta_{r}\alpha=d(\alpha-z_{p+1}-\cdots-z_{p+r-1})+B_{r}^{p+r,\bullet}+\Omega^{\geq p+r+1,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\,,

where the family
\{z_{p+j}\}_{1\leq j\leq r-1}
is as in Definition
6.1
.

Remark 6.4
.

Using Definition
6.1
, one readily verifies that for
r=1
,

\displaystyle\alpha\in Z_{1}^{p,\bullet}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ d_{0}\alpha=0\ \text{ and }\ \alpha\in B_{1}^{p,\bullet}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \exists\,\beta\in\Omega^{p,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\ \text{ such that }d_{0}\beta=\alpha\,.

In particular,

Z_{1}^{p,\bullet}=\ker d_{0}\cap\Omega^{p,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\ \text{ and }\ B_{1}^{p,\bullet}=\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\cap\Omega^{p,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})

so there is a natural isomorphism between the space of Rumin forms
E_{0}^{\bullet}
and the first page of the spectral sequence,

E_{0}^{\bullet}\cong\bigoplus_{p=0}^{Q}Z_{1}^{p,\bullet}/B_{1}^{p,\bullet}\,.

Moreover, using the scalar product introduced in Subsection
2.1
, we can express

\displaystyle E_{0}^{k}=\bigoplus_{p=0}^{Q}\,Z_{1}^{p,k-1}\cap\big(B_{1}^{p,k-p}\big)^{\perp}\,.

A classical property underlying the construction of spectral complexes is the chain of inclusions

\displaystyle B_{1}^{p,\bullet}\subseteq B_{2}^{p,\bullet}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq B_{\infty}^{p,\bullet}\subseteq Z_{\infty}^{p,\bullet}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq Z_{2}^{p,\bullet}\subseteq Z_{1}^{p,\bullet}\ \text{ for every }p\in\mathbb{N}\,.

Notice that, when the multicomplex is given by the de Rham complex of a positively graded Lie group
(\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G}),d=d_{0}+d_{w_{1}}+\cdots+d_{w_{s}})
, the associated spectral sequence collapses after finitely many steps, i.e. there is exists a positive integer
M\leq Q=w(\operatorname{vol})
such that
B_{\infty}^{p,k-p}=B_{M}^{p,k-p}
and
Z_{\infty}^{p,k-p}=Z_{M}^{p,k-p}
for every
p=0,\ldots,Q
and every
k=0,\ldots,\operatorname{dim}(\mathbb{G})
. Moreover, for every
k=1,\ldots,\operatorname{dim}(\mathbb{G})
we also have that
B_{\infty}^{p,k-p}=Z_{\infty}^{p,k-p}
at each weight.

As shown in
[
45
]
, the spaces
Z_{r}^{p,\bullet}
and
B_{r}^{p,\bullet}
, as well as the differentials
\Delta_{r}
, admit explicit descriptions in terms of Rumin forms and the Rumin differential.

Proposition 6.5

(Propositions 3.4, 3.6 and 3.8 in
[
45
]
)
.

Let
Z_{r}^{p,\bullet},B_{r}^{p,\bullet}\subset\Omega^{p,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
be as in Definition
6.1
.

One has
Z_{1}^{p,\bullet}=\ker d_{0}\cap\Omega^{p,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
. For
r\geq 2
, a form
\alpha\in\Omega^{p,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
belongs to
Z_{r}^{p,\bullet}
if and only if
\alpha\in\ker d_{0}\cap\Omega^{p,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
and there exist forms
{\omega}_{p+i}\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{p+i,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
with
i=1,\ldots,r-2
such that

\displaystyle d_{c}^{i}\Pi_{0}\alpha=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}d_{c}^{i-j}\omega_{p+j}\ \text{ for each }i=1,\ldots,r-1\,.

On the other hand,
B_{1}^{p,\bullet}=\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\cap\Omega^{p,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
. For
r\geq 2
, a form
\alpha\in\Omega^{p,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
belongs to
B_{r}^{p,\bullet}
if and only if there exist
c_{p-r+1}\in\ker d_{0}\cap\Omega^{p-r+1,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
as well as
\omega_{p-r+i}\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{p-r+i,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
for

such that

\displaystyle d_{c}^{i}\Pi_{0}c_{p-r+1}=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}d_{c}^{i-j}\omega_{p-r+1+j}\ \text{ for each }i=1,\ldots,r-2

and

\displaystyle\Pi_{0}\alpha=d_{c}^{r-1}\Pi_{0}c_{p-r+1}-\sum_{i=1}^{r-2}d_{c}^{r-1-i}\omega_{p-r+1+i}\,.

Finally, for any
r\geq 1
and

, one has

\displaystyle\Delta_{r}\big([\alpha]\big)=\bigg[d_{c}^{r}\Pi_{0}\alpha-\sum_{i=2}^{r-1}d_{c}^{i}\omega_{p+r-i}\bigg]\,,

where the
\omega_{p+r-i}\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{p+r-i,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
are chosen so that
d_{c}^{j}\Pi_{0}\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}d_{c}^{i}\omega_{p+j-i}
for
j=1,\ldots,r-1
.

As already proved explicitly in Lemma
3.7
, in general, given an arbitrary choice of bidegree
(p,k)
, the Rumin differential
d_{c}
applied to
E_{0}^{k}\cap\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{G})
will be given by a sum of several operators. Let us introduce the following notation: for a given choice of bidegree
(p,k)
, there exists
I_{p,k}=\{\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{N}\}
with each
\nu_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\nu_{1}<\cdots<\nu_{N}
such that

\displaystyle d_{c}=\sum_{j\in I_{p,k}}d_{c}^{j}\colon E_{0}^{k}\cap\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{G})\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j\in I_{p,k}}E_{0}^{k+1}\cap\Omega^{p+j,k+1-p-j}(\mathbb{G})

where each
d_{c}^{j}
has bidegree
(j,1-j)
. Equivalently, this can also be expressed by saying that at each page
j\in I_{p,k}
, the differentials
\Delta_{j}
are non-trivial maps

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}\colon Z_{j}^{p,k-p}/B_{j}^{p,k-p}\longrightarrow Z_{j}^{p+j,k+1-p-j}/B_{j}^{p+j,k+1-p-j}\,.

As shown explicitly in Lemma
3.7
, for a fixed bidegree
(p,k)
, the restriction of the Rumin differential
d_{c}
to
E_{0}^{k}\cap\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{G})

may decompose as a sum of several homogeneous components. More precisely, there exists a finite set

I_{p,k}=\{\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{N}\}\subset\mathbb{N},\qquad\nu_{1}<\cdots<\nu_{N},

such that

d_{c}=\sum_{j\in I_{p,k}}d_{c}^{j}\colon E_{0}^{k}\cap\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{G})\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j\in I_{p,k}}E_{0}^{k+1}\cap\Omega^{p+j,k+1-p-j}(\mathbb{G}),

where each component
d_{c}^{j}
has bidegree
(j,1-j)
.

In terms of the spectral sequence, these components correspond to the only possibly nonzero differentials

\Delta_{j}\colon Z_{j}^{p,k-p}/B_{j}^{p,k-p}\longrightarrow Z_{j}^{p+j,k+1-p-j}/B_{j}^{p+j,k+1-p-j}\ ,\quad j\in I_{p,k}.

If there exists
\alpha\in Z_{j}^{p,\bullet}
such that
d_{c}^{j}\Pi_{0}\alpha\notin B_{j}^{p+j,\bullet}
(modulo lower-order terms), then the differential
\Delta_{j}
is nonzero.

Proposition 6.6
.

[Proposition 4.2 in

] Fix a bidegree

Then, for each

\displaystyle Z_{j}^{p,k-p}/B_{m_{1}}^{p,k-p}\xrightarrow{\Delta_{j}}Z_{l}^{p+j,k+1-p-j}/B_{j}^{p+j,k+1-p-j}\xrightarrow{\Delta_{l}}Z_{m_{2}}^{p+l+j,k+2-p-l-j}/B_{l}^{p+l+j,k+2-p-l-j}

.

Notice how in Proposition

each non-trivial map

determines the page of the

Here

may be chosen arbitrarily. However, when constructing a complex,

is determined by the bidegree
(m_{1},1-m_{1})
of the incoming differential

, while
m_{2}
is determined by the bidegree
(m_{2},1-m_{2})
of the outgoing differential
\Delta_{m_{2}}

.

In this way, for each nontrivial space

, one obtains as many possible subcomplexes as there are elements

.

Since we want to realise these complexes as subspaces of differential forms, rather than as quotient spaces as in (

), we use the scalar product introduced in Subsection

.

Definition 6.7
.

[Definition 4.3 in

Given the de Rham complex
(\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G}),d=d_{0}+d_{w_{1}}+\cdots+d_{w_{s}})

Then, for each

and

(6.2)

\displaystyle E_{j,l}^{p,k-p}:=Z_{j}^{p,k-p}\cap\big(B_{l}^{p,k-p}\big)^{\perp}\subset\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{G})\,.

Moreover, for each

\displaystyle E_{l,m_{1}}^{p-l,k-1-p+l}\xrightarrow{\Delta_{l}}E_{j,l}^{p,k-p}\xrightarrow{\Delta_{j}}E_{i,j}^{p+j,k+1-p-j}\xrightarrow{\Delta_{i}}E_{m_{2},i}^{p+j+i,k+2-p-j-i}

E_{j,l}^{\bullet,\bullet}

\Delta_{j}
of bidegree
(j,1-j)
, thus forming a collection of complexes. We refer to this collection as the
spectral complexes
associated with the de Rham complex
(\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G}),d)
of the positively gradable Lie group
\mathbb{G}
and denote it by

\displaystyle\big\{\big(E_{j,l}^{\bullet,\bullet},\Delta_{j}\big)\big\}_{j\in I_{\bullet,\bullet}}\,.

Remark 6.8
.

When we write

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}\colon E_{j,l}^{p,k-p}\longrightarrow E_{m,j}^{p+j,k+1-p-j}

we mean that the operator defined in (
6.1
) is composed with the projection onto the subspace
E_{m,j}^{p+j,k+1-p-j}
, i.e. onto the orthogonal complement of
B_{j}^{p+j,k+1-p-j}
.

Unlike in the case of the Rumin complex, where the operator
\Pi_{0}
in
d_{c}=\Pi_{0}d\Pi_{E}
is an orthogonal projection onto
\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{\bullet,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
, here the projection is realised by modifying representatives through the addition or subtraction of exact terms. More precisely, given a representative of
\Delta_{j}([\alpha])
, one replaces it by an equivalent form modulo
B_{j}^{p+j,k+1-p-j}
by adding terms of the form

d(c_{p+1}+\cdots+c_{p+j})
,
where
c_{p+i}\in\Omega^{p+i,k-p-i}(\mathbb{G})
are chosen as in Definition
6.1
.

Theorem 6.9

(Stokes’ theorem for spectral complexes)
.

Fix a bidegree
(p,k-1)
for which the operator

\Delta_{j}\colon E_{j,l}^{p,k-1-p}\longrightarrow E_{m,j}^{p+j,k-p-j}

is nonzero, for some admissible choice of
l,m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{G}
be an orientable
k
-dimensional
C^{1}
manifold such that
\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)=p+j
, and assume that
\partial\Sigma
is also an orientable
C^{1}
manifold with
\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma)=p
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}\Delta_{j}\alpha\ \text{ for every }\alpha\in E_{j,l}^{p,k-1-p}\,.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in E_{j,l}^{p,k-1-p}\subset E_{0}^{k-1}\cap\Omega^{p,k-1-p}(\mathbb{G})
.
By Definition
6.7
and formula (
6.1
), we can represent
\Delta_{j}\alpha
as

\Delta_{j}\alpha=d(\alpha-z_{p+1}-\cdots-z_{p+j-1})+B_{j}^{p+j,k-p-j}+\Omega^{\geq p+j+1,\bullet}(\mathbb{G}),

where
z_{p+i}\in\Omega^{p+i,k-1-p-i}(\mathbb{G})
,
i=1,\ldots,j-1
,
are determined by the condition
\alpha\in Z_{j}^{p,k-1-p}
. Moreover, using the characterisation

B_{j}^{p+j,k-p-j}=dZ_{j-1}^{p+1,k-p-2}+\Omega^{\geq p+j+1,\bullet}(\mathbb{G}),

we obtain that
\Delta_{j}\alpha
differs from
d(\alpha-z_{p+1}-\cdots-z_{p+j-1})

by terms that either belong to
dZ_{j-1}^{p+1,k-p-2}
or have weight at least
p+j+1
. Finally, since
\deg(\partial\Sigma)=p
, the forms
z_{p+i}
, as well as those in
Z^{p+1,k-2-p}_{j-1}
, have strictly larger weight and therefore do not contribute to the integral over
\partial\Sigma
.

This can be more compactly rephrased using a slight abuse of notation as follows

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}\big(\alpha-z_{p+1}-\cdots-z_{p+j-1}\big)+Z_{j-1}^{p+1,k-2-p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}d\big(\alpha-z_{p+1}-\cdots-z_{p+j-1}\big)+dZ_{j-1}^{p+1,k-2-p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}d\big(\alpha-z_{p+1}-\cdots-z_{p+j-1}\big)+dZ_{j-1}^{p+1,k-2-p}+\Omega^{\geq p+j+1,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})=\int_{\Sigma}\Delta_{j}\alpha\,.

and the claim follows directly from Corollary
5.14
: the additional terms in the expression of
\Delta_{j}\alpha
do not contribute to the integral over
\Sigma
, since

•

the terms in
dZ_{j-1}^{p+1,k-p-2}
integrate to zero by Stokes’ theorem and
\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma)=p
;

•

the terms in
\Omega^{\geq p+j+1,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
vanish on
\Sigma
because
\deg(\Sigma)=p+j
.

∎

Corollary 6.10

(Stokes’ theorem on locally smooth intrinsic graphs with boundary)
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{G}
be an orientable
k
-dimensional locally smooth intrinsic graph with boundary
\partial\Sigma
, then

\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}\Delta_{j}\alpha\quad\text{for every }\alpha\in E_{j,l}^{p,k-1-p}\,,

where
p=\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)
,
j=\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)-\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma)
, and some
l\in\mathbb{N}
such that
E_{j,l}^{p,k-1-p}\neq 0
.

Proof.

It is enough to show that the assumptions that
\Sigma
is a locally smooth intrinsic graph with boundary
\partial\Sigma
ensures the existence of indices
j\in I_{p,k-1}
with
p=\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma)
and
l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that

E_{j,l}^{p,k-1-p}\neq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta_{j}\colon E_{j,l}^{p,k-1-p}\longrightarrow E_{m,j}^{p+j,k-p-j}

is nonzero for some
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.

Since
\Sigma
is a locally smooth intrinsic graph of degree
p+j
, there exists at least a point
p\in\Sigma
such that a neighbourhood of
p
in
\Sigma
is a
(\mathbb{W},\mathbb{V})
-graph with
\operatorname{deg}(\mathbb{W})=p+j
. Therefore, by Lemma
5.4
, we have
E_{0}^{k}\cap\Omega^{p+j,k-p-j}(\mathbb{G})\neq 0
. Similarly, the condition
\deg(\partial\Sigma)=p
implies

E_{0}^{k-1}\cap\Omega^{p,k-1-p}(\mathbb{G})\neq 0
.

Finally, by Lemma
5.10
, we have that
j\geq 1
necessarily.

By the decomposition of the Rumin differential, this implies that there exists at least one
j\in I_{p,k-1}
such that the component

d_{c}^{j}\colon E_{0}^{k-1}\cap\Omega^{p,k-1-p}(\mathbb{G})\longrightarrow E_{0}^{k}\cap\Omega^{p+j,k-p-j}(\mathbb{G})

is nonzero. This then implies that there exists a corresponding spectral differential
\Delta_{j}
which is nontrivial on a suitable subspace
E_{j,l}^{p,k-1-p}
.

The claim then follows from Theorem
6.9
.
∎

Remark 6.11

(Geometric interpretation)
.

The previous corollary shows that the degree of the intrinsic graph determines which component of the spectral complex governs the Stokes’ formula.

More precisely, the decomposition of the Rumin differential

d_{c}=\sum_{j\in I_{p,k-1}}d_{c}^{j}

splits the Rumin differential into components of different homogeneous weights. The condition
\deg(\partial\Sigma)=p
and
\deg(\Sigma)=p+j
selects exactly the component
d_{c}^{j}
that produces a nontrivial contribution when passing from
\partial\Sigma
to
\Sigma
.

Equivalently, among all the spectral differentials
\{\Delta_{j}\}_{j\in I_{p,k-1}}
, only those compatible with the degree jump between
\partial\Sigma
and
\Sigma
can contribute to the Stokes’ formula. In this sense, the geometry of the submanifold determines which subcomplex
(E_{j,l}^{\bullet,\bullet},\Delta_{j})
is relevant.

Example 6.12

(
\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
and spectral Stokes phenomena)
.

We consider the
4
-dimensional nilpotent Lie group
\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
.
Using the explicit description of Rumin forms and complementary subgroups given in Subsection
4.2
, we analyse how the spectral Stokes theorem behaves for smooth intrinsic graphs.

The space of Rumin 1-forms coincides with the space of horizontal 1-forms, so every 1-dimensional intrinsic graph must have degree 1. However, the space of Rumin
2
-forms contains components in both

\Omega^{2,0}(\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R})
and
\Omega^{3,-1}(\mathbb{H}^{1}\times\mathbb{R})
.
Consequently, the Rumin differential decomposes as
d_{c}=d_{c}^{1}+d_{c}^{2}
,
and both components may contribute nontrivially.

As a result, for
2
-dimensional smooth intrinsic graphs
\Sigma
, the spectral Stokes’ theorem may involve either
\Delta_{1}
or
\Delta_{2}
, depending on the choice of complementary subgroup (equivalently, on the corresponding simple Rumin form). In particular, higher-order contributions can arise, reflecting the presence of non-horizontal directions.

This example shows that, even in a low-dimensional setting, different geometric configurations of intrinsic graphs can lead to different spectral complexes used for Stokes’ formula. In particular, the degree of the submanifold determines which component
\Delta_{j}
appears.

Remark 6.13
.

Stokes’ theorem does not, in general, hold for the operators

\Delta_{j}\colon E_{j,l}^{p,k-1-p}\longrightarrow E_{m,j}^{p+j,k-p-j}

on arbitrary
R
-manifolds when
j\geq 2
.

In the case when
j=2
, given
\alpha\in Z_{2}^{p,k-1-p}\subset\ker d_{0}\cap\Omega^{p,k-1-p}(\mathbb{G})
, there exists
z_{p+1}\in\Omega^{p+1,k-2-p}(\mathbb{G})
such that
d_{1}\alpha=d_{0}z_{p+1}

and therefore

\Delta_{2}\alpha=d(\alpha-z_{p+1})+B_{2}^{p+2,k-p-2}\,.

The condition above determines uniquely the component of
z_{p+1}
in
\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
, namely
\operatorname{pr}_{\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}}z_{p+1}=d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\alpha
.
Hence one can choose
z_{p+1}
so that its
\ker d_{0}
-component vanishes. In that case, if
\partial\Sigma
is an
R
-manifold, one has

\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha=\int_{\partial\Sigma}(\alpha-z_{p+1}).

However, the operator
\Delta_{2}
is only defined up to addition of elements in

B_{2}^{p+2,k-p-2}=dZ_{1}^{p+1,k-2-p}+\Omega^{\geq p+3,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
,
and the forms in
Z_{1}^{p+1,k-2-p}=\ker d_{0}\cap\Omega^{p+1,k-2-p}(\mathbb{G})
are not, in general, annihilated by integration on
\partial\Sigma
, even if
\partial\Sigma
is an
R
-manifold. Consequently, one cannot expect

\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}\Delta_{2}\alpha

to hold in general.

In the case when
j\geq 3
, if
\alpha\in Z_{j}^{p,k-1-p}
with
j\geq 3
, the characterisation in terms of the Rumin differential (see Proposition
6.5
) implies the existence of forms
\omega_{p+i}\in\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{p+i,k-1-p-i}(\mathbb{G})
with
i\geq 1
,
such that

d_{c}^{1}\Pi_{0}\alpha=0,\qquad d_{c}^{2}\Pi_{0}\alpha=d_{c}^{1}\omega_{p+1}.

In particular, unless
d_{c}^{2}\Pi_{0}\alpha=0
, the form
\omega_{p+1}
is a nontrivial Rumin form of weight strictly larger than
p
.

As a consequence, even if
\partial\Sigma
is an
R
-manifold, one cannot remove the contribution of such terms when passing from
\alpha
to a representative of
\Delta_{j}\alpha
. Therefore, in general,

\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha\neq\int_{\Sigma}\Delta_{j}\alpha,\qquad j\geq 3.

We further remark that the condition
\Sigma
being an
R
-manifold is never needed when dealing with spectral complexes
\Delta_{j}\colon E_{j,l}^{p,\bullet}\longrightarrow E_{m,j}^{p+j,\bullet}
, as long as
\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)=p+j
since for any
j\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle dZ_{j}^{p,\bullet}=B_{j+1}^{p+j,\bullet}\subset Z_{1}^{p+j,\bullet}=\ker d_{0}\cap\Omega^{p+j,\bullet}\,.

The assumptions
\deg(\Sigma)=p+j
and
\deg(\partial\Sigma)=p
in Theorem
6.9
are essential. It ensures that the additional terms appearing in the definition of
\Delta_{j}\alpha
either vanish on
\Sigma
(by weight considerations) or integrate to zero (being exact).

Finally, if either
\deg(\partial\Sigma)<p
or
\deg(\Sigma)<p+j
, then the degree compatibility is lost, and Stokes’ theorem for the operator
\Delta_{j}
fails, similarly to the phenomenon observed in Proposition
5.18
.

The validity of Stokes’ theorem for spectral complexes being so inextricably linked to the degree of the submanifold naturally motivates the introduction of a distinguished class of submanifolds for which the analogue of Stokes’ theorem associated with the spectral complex holds.

Definition 6.14

(Spectral manifolds)
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{G}
be a
k
-dimensional orientable
C^{1}
manifold and denote by
P_{h}
the set of weights of nontrivial Rumin forms
E_{0}^{h}
in degree
h
. We say that
\Sigma
is a
spectral manifold
if
\deg(\Sigma)\in P_{k}
.

We say that
\Sigma
is a
spectral manifold with boundary
if
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{G}
is a
k
-dimensional orientable
C^{1}
manifold with boundary
\partial\Sigma
and

\deg(\Sigma)\in P_{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\deg(\partial\Sigma)\in P_{k-1}.

By Lemma
5.10
, we know that necessarily

\deg(\partial\Sigma)<\deg(\Sigma)
, hence if
\Sigma
is a spectral manifold, there exists a nontrivial spectral complex whose differential connects forms of weight
\deg(\partial\Sigma)
in degree
k-1
to forms of weight
\deg(\Sigma)
in degree
k
. Consequently, Theorem
6.9
applies to
\Sigma
and
\partial\Sigma
through the corresponding differential
\Delta_{j}
, where
j=\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)-\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma)
.

In this sense, spectral manifolds provide the natural geometric setting where the spectral complex behaves as a genuine boundary operator from the point of view of integration theory (see also Section
8
). This is because the defining condition for a spectral manifold guarantees that the degrees of the manifold and of its boundary are compatible with the weights appearing in the spectral complexes, so that Stokes’ theorem can be realised through a suitable spectral differential.

As already observed in Corollary
6.10
, the class of spectral manifolds contains, in particular, locally smooth intrinsic graphs with boundary. However, Remark
5.6
suggests that this class might be strictly larger. Indeed, one can find subspaces of
\ker\Box_{0}\cap\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
spanned by non-simple Rumin forms, whereas smooth intrinsic graphs are in one-to-one correspondence with simple Rumin forms. Therefore, spectral manifolds capture geometric configurations that cannot be represented by intrinsic graphs alone.

In the cases where the Rumin differential coincides with some differential appearing in the spectral complexes, we get that Stokes’ theorem holds for the Rumin complex by applying directly Theorem
6.9
. Notice that this is the case whenever we consider the Rumin differential
d_{c}
acting between Rumin
(k-1)
-forms of maximal weight and Rumin
k
-forms of minimal weight, i.e. if
N_{k-1}=\max\{w(\alpha)\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\mid\alpha\in E_{0}^{k-1}\}
and
n_{k}=\min\{w(\alpha)\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\mid\alpha\in E_{0}^{k}\}
, then

\displaystyle d_{c}\colon E_{0}^{k-1}\cap\Omega^{N_{k-1},k-1-N_{k-1}}(\mathbb{G})\longrightarrow E_{0}^{k}\cap\Omega^{n_{k},k-n_{k}}(\mathbb{G})

coincides with the differential

\displaystyle\Delta_{n_{k}-N_{k-1}}\colon Z_{1}^{N_{k-1},k-1-N_{k-1}}\cap\big(B_{m}^{N_{k-1},k-1-N_{k-1}}\big)^{\perp}\longrightarrow Z_{l}^{n_{k},k-n_{k}}\cap\big(B_{n_{k}-N_{k-1}}^{n_{k},k-n_{k}}\big)^{\perp}\ \text{ for any }m,l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\,.

In some special Carnot groups, such as Heisenberg groups and the 5-dimensional Cartan group, this happens in each degree since for each
k=0,\ldots,\operatorname{dim}(\mathbb{G})
there exists only one
p=p(k)
such that
E_{0}^{k}\cap\Omega^{p,k-p}(\mathbb{G})\neq 0
(see Remark 4.4 in
[
45
]
for an explicit proof of this fact). In particular, this implies that Proposition
5.16
can be viewed as a direct consequence of Corollary
6.10
in the case where
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{H}^{n}
.

Corollary 6.15

(Stokes’ theorem for the Rumin complex on locally smooth intrinsic graphs in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
and the Cartan group)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be either the
(2n+1)
-dimensional Heisenberg group or the 5-dimensional free nilpotent Cartan group. Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{G}
be a
k
-dimensional locally smooth intrinsic graph with boundary
\partial\Sigma
, then

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha=\int_{\Sigma}d_{c}\alpha\ \text{ for any }\alpha\in E_{0}^{k-1}\,.

7.
Re-expressing spectral complexes in view of Stokes’ Theorem

It is possible to reinterpret the spectral complexes
\{(E_{j,l}^{\bullet,\bullet},\Delta_{j})\}_{j\in I_{\bullet,\bullet}}
, and in particular the action of the differentials

\Delta_{j}\colon E_{j,l}^{p,\bullet}\longrightarrow E_{m,j}^{p+j,\bullet},

in terms of Stokes’ theorem. The aim of this discussion is to emphasise the naturality of the spectral complexes in the context of positively graded Lie groups. For this reason, unlike in Section
4
, the considerations below are carried out in full generality and apply to spectral complexes defined on any positively gradable Lie group
\mathbb{G}
.

To simplify the notation in the following computations, we introduce a decomposition adapted to the algebraic Hodge Laplacian
\Box_{0}=d_{0}\delta_{0}+\delta_{0}d_{0}
, following the Hodge decomposition described in (
2.7
).

Definition 7.1
.

Let
\alpha\in\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
. We write

\alpha=\check{\alpha}+\overline{\alpha}+\hat{\alpha},

where

•

\check{\alpha}=\operatorname{pr}_{\operatorname{Im}d_{0}}\alpha=d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\alpha\in\operatorname{Im}d_{0}
;

•

\hat{\alpha}=\operatorname{pr}_{\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}}\alpha=d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}\alpha\in\operatorname{Im}\delta_{0}
;

•

\overline{\alpha}=\Pi_{0}\alpha=\alpha-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}\alpha-d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}\alpha\in\ker\Box_{0}
.

In particular,
\overline{\alpha}
is the Rumin component of
\alpha
.
When
\alpha\in\Omega^{p,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
and
\check{\alpha}=d_{0}\beta_{p}
, we will denote by
\beta_{p}
a primitive of
\check{\alpha}
, in order to keep track of the weight of the corresponding term.

To avoid excessively cumbersome notation, we do not carry out the computations in full generality for the differential
\Delta_{j}
acting on a form
\alpha\in Z_{j}^{p,\bullet}
for arbitrary
j\geq 1
. Instead, we focus on the cases
j=1,2,3
, which already illustrate the underlying mechanism and the role of the Leibniz rule in the general case.

Recall that, on a positively gradable Lie group
\mathbb{G}
, the exterior differential can be written as

d=d_{0}+d_{1}+\cdots+d_{w_{s}},

where
d_{j}
is the zero operator unless
j=w_{i}
for some
i=1,\ldots,s
(see Lemma
2.8
).

j=1
.
In this case, we consider
\alpha\in Z_{1}^{p,\bullet}=\ker d_{0}\cap\Omega^{p,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
. Using the Hodge decomposition, we write

\alpha=\check{\alpha}+\overline{\alpha}=d_{0}\beta_{p}+\overline{\alpha}\,,

so that

\displaystyle d\alpha=

\displaystyle d_{0}\alpha+d_{1}\alpha+\cdots+d_{w_{s}}\alpha=d_{1}(d_{0}\beta_{p}+\overline{\alpha})+\Omega^{\geq p+2,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})=d_{1}\overline{\alpha}-d_{0}d_{1}\beta_{p}+\Omega^{\geq p+2,\bullet}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{1}\overline{\alpha}-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\overline{\alpha}+d_{0}\big(d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\overline{\alpha}-d_{1}\beta_{p}\big)+\Omega^{\geq p+2,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})=d_{c}^{1}\overline{\alpha}+\underbrace{d_{0}\big(d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\overline{\alpha}-d_{1}\beta_{p}\big)}_{\in B_{1}^{p+1,\bullet}}+\Omega^{\geq p+2,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{c}^{1}\overline{\alpha}+d\big(d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\overline{\alpha}-d_{1}\beta_{p}\big)-(d-d_{0})\big(d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\overline{\alpha}-d_{1}\beta_{p}\big)+\Omega^{\geq p+2,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{c}^{1}\overline{\alpha}+d\big(d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\overline{\alpha}-d_{1}\beta_{p}\big)+\Omega^{\geq p+2,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\,.

Rearranging the formula, we obtain

\displaystyle d\big(\alpha-d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\overline{\alpha}+d_{1}\beta_{p}\big)=d_{c}^{1}\overline{\alpha}+\Omega^{\geq p+2,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})

In other words, the differential

\Delta_{1}\colon Z_{1}^{p,\bullet}\longrightarrow E_{m,1}^{p+1,\bullet}

coincides with the exterior derivative
d
, up to an exact form and terms of weight
\geq p+2
.

Therefore, for any oriented
C^{1}
manifold
\Sigma
with
\deg(\Sigma)=p+1
and boundary
\partial\Sigma
with
\deg(\partial\Sigma)=p
, the spectral Stokes’ theorem reduces to the classical Stokes’ theorem for
d
.

j=2
.
In this case, we have

\displaystyle\alpha\in Z_{2}^{p,\bullet}\ \longleftrightarrow\ d_{0}\alpha=0\ \text{ and there exists a }z_{p+1}\in\Omega^{p+1,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\text{ such that }d_{1}\alpha=d_{0}z_{p+1}\,.

Notice that the second condition, univocally determines
\hat{z}_{p+1}=d_{0}^{-1}d_{0}z_{p+1}
, but we have no control over
\operatorname{pr}_{\ker d_{0}}z_{p+1}
. This is because

\displaystyle d_{1}\alpha=d_{1}(d_{0}\beta_{p}+\overline{\alpha})=-d_{0}d_{1}\beta_{p}+d_{1}\overline{\alpha}-d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\overline{\alpha}+d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\overline{\alpha}=d_{c}^{1}\overline{\alpha}+d_{0}d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\overline{\alpha}-d_{0}d_{1}\beta_{p}=d_{0}z_{p+1}

so on one hand this implies that
d_{c}^{1}\overline{\alpha}=0
, but also that,
\hat{z}_{p+1}=d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\overline{\alpha}-d_{1}\beta_{p}
. Therefore
d_{1}\alpha=d_{0}z_{p+1}
as long as
\hat{z}_{p+1}=d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\overline{\alpha}-d_{1}\beta_{p}
, for any
\check{z}_{p+1}+\overline{z}_{p+1}\in\ker d_{0}\cap\Omega^{p+1,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
. Then

\displaystyle d\alpha=

\displaystyle d_{0}\alpha+d_{1}\alpha+d_{2}\alpha+\Omega^{\geq p+3,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})=d_{0}\hat{z}_{p+1}+d_{2}\alpha+\Omega^{\geq p+3,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{0}(\check{z}_{p+1}+\overline{z}_{p+1}+\hat{z}_{p+1})+d_{2}\alpha+\Omega^{\geq p+3,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle dz_{p+1}-(d-d_{0})(\check{z}_{p+1}+\overline{z}_{p+1}+\hat{z}_{p+1})+d_{2}\alpha+\Omega^{\geq p+3,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle dz_{p+1}-d_{1}(\check{z}_{p+1}+\overline{z}_{p+1})-d_{1}(d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\overline{\alpha}-d_{1}\beta_{p})+d_{2}d_{0}\beta_{p}+d_{2}\overline{\alpha}+\Omega^{\geq p+3,\bullet}(\mathbb{G}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle dz_{p+1}+d_{c}^{2}\overline{\alpha}-\underbrace{d_{1}(\check{z}_{p+1}+\overline{z}_{p+1})+d_{0}\big(d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{2}\overline{\alpha}-d_{2}\beta_{p}\big)}_{\in B_{2}^{p+2,\bullet}}+\Omega^{\geq p+3,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle dz_{p+1}+d_{c}^{2}\overline{\alpha}-d\big[\check{z}_{p+1}+\overline{z}_{p+1}-d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{2}\overline{\alpha}+d_{2}\beta_{p}\big]+(d-d_{1}-d_{0})(\check{z}_{p+1}+\overline{z}_{p+1})+

\displaystyle+(d-d_{0})(-d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{2}\overline{\alpha}+d_{2}\beta_{p})+\Omega^{\geq p+3,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle dz_{p+1}+d_{c}^{2}\overline{\alpha}-d\big[\check{z}_{p+1}+\overline{z}_{p+1}-d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{2}\overline{\alpha}+d_{2}\beta_{p}\big]+(d-d_{1}-d_{0})(\check{z}_{p+1}+\overline{z}_{p+1})+\Omega^{\geq p+3,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})

and so by rearranging we get

\displaystyle d(\alpha-\hat{z}_{p+1}+d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{2}\overline{\alpha}-d_{2}\beta_{p})=d_{c}^{2}\overline{\alpha}+\Omega^{\geq p+3,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\,.

Note that in this case
d_{1}(\check{z}_{p+1}+\overline{z}_{p+1})+d_{0}\big(d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{2}\overline{\alpha}-d_{2}\beta_{p}\big)
belongs to
B_{2}^{p+2,\bullet}=dZ_{1}^{p+1,\bullet}+\Omega^{\geq p+3,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
simply because
\check{z}_{p+1}+\overline{z}_{p+1}\in\ker d_{0}\cap\Omega^{p+1,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})=Z_{1}^{p+1,\bullet}
.

Furthermore, let us highlight the fact that in general for a given
\alpha\in Z_{2}^{p,\bullet}
, we won’t have that
d_{c}^{2}\overline{\alpha}
belongs to
B_{3}^{p+2,\bullet}\cap(B_{2}^{p+2,\bullet})^{\perp}
, that is

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}\alpha=d_{c}^{2}\overline{\alpha}+\xi_{p+2}\ \text{ for some }\xi_{p+2}\in B_{2}^{p+2,\bullet}=dZ_{1}^{p+1,\bullet}+\Omega^{\geq p+3,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\,,

so one can simply apply again the Leibniz rule just as we did above, since
\xi_{p+2}=d\theta_{p+1}+\Omega^{\geq p+3,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
for some
\theta_{p+1}\in Z_{1}^{p+1,\bullet}
. We then finally obtain the following simplified expression

\displaystyle d(\alpha-\hat{z}_{p+1}+d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{2}\overline{\alpha}-d_{2}\beta_{p}-\theta_{p+1})=\Delta_{2}\alpha+\Omega^{\geq p+3,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\,.

In other words, the differential
\Delta_{2}\colon Z_{2}^{p,\bullet}\longrightarrow E_{m,2}^{p+2,\bullet}
is the same as the exterior derivative
d
, up to an exact form exact form
d\eta
with
\eta\in\Omega^{p+1,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\oplus\Omega^{p+2,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
and forms of weight
\geq p+3
. When taking the integral over a
C^{1}
manifold
\Sigma
for which
\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)=p+2
whose boundary is a
C^{1}
manifold
\partial\Sigma
for which
\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma)=p
, we get exactly the Stokes’ theorem of Theorem
6.9
directly from the classical Stokes’ theorem together with the Leibniz rule.

j=3
. In this case, we have

\displaystyle\alpha\in Z_{3}^{p,\bullet}\longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle d_{0}\alpha=0\text{ and there exist }z_{p+i}\in\Omega^{p+i,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\text{ with }i=1,2\text{ such that }

\displaystyle d_{1}\alpha=d_{0}z_{p+1}\text{ and }d_{2}\alpha=d_{1}z_{p+1}+d_{0}z_{p+2}\,.

From the previous steps, we already know that

•

\alpha=d_{0}\beta_{p}+\overline{\alpha}
;

•

d_{c}^{1}\overline{\alpha}=0
;

•

z_{p+1}=\check{z}_{p+1}+\overline{z}_{p+1}+\hat{z}_{p+1}
with
\hat{z}_{p+1}=d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\overline{\alpha}-d_{1}\beta_{p}
and
\check{z}_{p+1}=d_{0}\beta_{p+1}
.

The additional condition then reads as

\displaystyle d_{2}\alpha=d_{2}(d_{0}\beta_{p}+\overline{\alpha})=-d_{1}^{2}\beta_{p}-d_{0}d_{2}\beta_{p}+d_{2}\overline{\alpha}=d_{1}(d_{0}\beta_{p+1}+\overline{z}_{p+1}+d_{0}^{-1}d_{1}\overline{\alpha}-d_{1}\beta_{p})+d_{0}z_{p+2}\,.

By re-arranging, this gives

\displaystyle\partial_{2}\overline{\alpha}-\partial_{1}\overline{z}_{p+1}=d_{0}\big(z_{p+2}+d_{2}\beta_{p}-d_{1}\beta_{p+1}\big)

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow d_{c}^{2}\overline{\alpha}-d_{c}^{1}\overline{z}_{p+1}=d_{0}(z_{p+2}+d_{2}\beta_{p}-d_{1}\beta_{p+1}-d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{2}\overline{\alpha}+d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{1}\overline{z}_{p+1})

which implies that
d_{c}^{2}\overline{\alpha}=d_{c}^{1}\overline{z}_{p+1}
but also that
\hat{z}_{p+2}=d_{1}\beta_{p+1}-d_{2}\beta_{p}+d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{2}\overline{\alpha}-d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{1}\overline{z}_{p+1}
. This also means that for any other choices of
\check{\zeta}_{p+1}+\overline{\zeta}_{p+1}\in\ker d_{0}\cap\ker d_{c}^{1}\cap\Omega^{p+1,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
, we have that
{y}_{p+1}:=\check{z}_{p+1}+\check{\zeta}_{p+1}+\overline{z}_{p+1}+\overline{\zeta}_{p+1}+\hat{z}_{p+1}
also satisfies the equations
d_{1}\alpha=d_{0}{y}_{p+1}
and
d_{2}\alpha=d_{1}y_{p+1}+d_{0}y_{p+2}
with
\hat{y}_{p+2}=\hat{z}_{p+2}+d_{1}\gamma_{p+1}-d_{0}^{-1}\partial_{1}\overline{\zeta}_{p+1}
, where
\check{\zeta}_{p+1}=d_{0}\gamma_{p+1}
. Moreover, one can also consider
z_{p+2}=\check{z}_{p+2}+\overline{z}_{p+2}+\hat{z}_{p+2}
for any choice of
\check{z}_{p+2}+\overline{z}_{p+2}\in\ker d_{0}\cap\Omega^{p+2,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle d\alpha=

\displaystyle d_{0}\alpha+d_{1}\alpha+d_{2}\alpha+d_{3}\alpha+\Omega^{\geq p+4,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{0}y_{p+1}+d_{1}y_{p+1}+d_{0}\hat{y}_{p+2}+d_{3}\alpha+\Omega^{\geq p+4,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d(y_{p+1})-d_{2}y_{p+1}+dy_{p+2}-d_{1}y_{p+2}+d_{3}\alpha+\Omega^{\geq p+4,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d(y_{p+1}+y_{p+2})+\partial_{3}\overline{\alpha}-\partial_{2}(\overline{z}_{p+1}+\overline{\zeta}_{p+1})-d_{1}\overline{z}_{p+2}+d_{0}\big[d_{1}\beta_{p+2}+d_{2}(\beta_{p+1}+\gamma_{p+1})-d_{3}\beta_{p}\big]+\Omega^{\geq p+4,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d(y_{p+1}+y_{p+2})+\partial_{3}\overline{\alpha}-\partial_{2}\overline{z}_{p+1}-\underbrace{\partial_{2}\overline{\zeta}_{p+1}-d_{1}\overline{z}_{p+2}+\operatorname{Im}d_{0}\cap\Omega^{p+3,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})}_{\in B_{3}^{p+3,\bullet}}+\Omega^{\geq p+4,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d(y_{p+1}+y_{p+2})+\Delta_{3}\alpha+B_{3}^{p+3,\bullet}+\Omega^{\geq p+4,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\,.

Using the fact that
B_{3}^{p+3,\bullet}=dZ_{2}^{p+1}+\Omega^{\geq p+4,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
, one can use the Leibniz rule again to show that

\displaystyle d(\alpha-y_{p+1}-y_{p+2}-y_{p+3})=\Delta_{3}\alpha+\Omega^{\geq p+4,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\,,

that is, the differential
\Delta_{3}\colon Z_{3}^{p,\bullet}\longrightarrow E_{m,3}^{p+3,\bullet}
is the same as the exterior derivative
d
, up to an exact form
d\eta
with
\eta\in\Omega^{p+1,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\oplus\Omega^{p+2,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})\oplus\Omega^{p+3,\bullet}(\mathbb{G})
and forms of weight
\geq p+4
. When taking the integral over a
C^{1}
manifold
\Sigma
for which
\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)=p+3
whose boundary is a
C^{1}
manifold
\partial\Sigma
for which
\operatorname{deg}(\partial\Sigma)=p
, we get exactly the Stokes’ theorem of Theorem
6.9
directly from the classical Stokes’ theorem together with the Leibniz rule.

8.
Currents in positively graded groups

The purpose of this section is to show how Stokes’ theorem for spectral complexes automatically introduces the natural setting for a general theory of currents in positively graded groups.
It is, in fact, quite natural to replace a spectral manifold with a continuous operator acting on the smooth compactly supported forms of the spectral complexes
\{(E_{j,l}^{\bullet,\bullet},\Delta_{j})\}_{j\in I_{\bullet,\bullet}}
.

In this section,
\mathbb{G}
will denote a positively graded group and
U\subset\mathbb{G}
an open subset. We also introduce the spaces
{\mathscr{D}}^{p,k-p}_{j,l}(U)
of compactly supported smooth forms of
E^{p,k-p}_{j,l}(U)
, viewed as quotient spaces. Before introducing the precise definition of such quotient spaces, the following remark is needed.

Remark 8.1

(Viewing
Z_{r}^{\bullet,\bullet}
and
B_{r}^{\bullet,\bullet}
as quotients)
.

Let us consider the spaces of
r
-cocycles and
r
-boundaries that are traditionally used to define the quotients
E_{r}^{\bullet,\bullet}
of spectral sequences (see e.g.
[
39
,
30
]
). Given
U\subseteq\mathbb{G}
an open subset, for
r\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{r}^{p,\bullet}(U):=

\displaystyle\{\alpha\in\Omega^{\geq p,\bullet}(U)\mid d\alpha\in\Omega^{\geq p+r,\bullet}(U)\}\ \text{ and }\ \mathcal{B}_{r}^{p,\bullet}(U):=\mathcal{Z}_{r-1}^{p+1,\bullet}(U)+d\mathcal{Z}_{r-1}^{p-r+1,\bullet}(U)\,.

Then it is straightforward to show the following isomorphisms: for each
r\geq 1
,

(8.1)

\displaystyle Z_{r}^{p,\bullet}(U)\cong\mathcal{Z}_{r}^{p,\bullet}(U)/\mathcal{Z}_{r-1}^{p+1,\bullet}(U)\ \text{ and }\ B_{r}^{p,\bullet}(U)\cong\mathcal{B}_{r}^{p,\bullet}(U)/\mathcal{Z}_{r-1}^{p+1,\bullet}(U)\,,

where
Z_{r}^{p,\bullet}(U)
and
B_{r}^{p,\bullet}(U)
are the
\mathbb{R}
-submodules of
\Omega^{\bullet}(U)
constructed according to Definition
6.1
.

For a thorough exposition of these properties, we refer to
[
31
]
.

Definition 8.2
.

Given
U\subseteq\mathbb{G}
an open subset, for any
j,l\geq 1
and
p=0,\ldots,Q
, we define

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{j,l}^{p,\bullet}(U):={\{\alpha\in Z_{j}^{p,\bullet}(U)\cap\Omega_{c}^{\bullet}(U)\}}\ /\ \overline{\{\beta\in B_{l}^{p,\bullet}(U)\cap\Omega_{c}^{\bullet}(U)\}}\,.

The closure here is taken in order to have the isomorphism between these quotients
\mathscr{D}_{j,l}^{\bullet,\bullet}(U)
and the subspaces of compactly supported forms in
E_{j,l}^{\bullet,\bullet}(U)
, that is

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{j,l}^{p,\bullet}(U)\cong\{\alpha\in Z_{j}^{p,\bullet}(U)\cap\Omega_{c}^{\bullet}(U)\}\cap\big(\{\beta\in B_{l}^{p,\bullet}(U)\cap\Omega_{c}^{\bullet}(U)\}\big)^{\perp}\,.

We remark that the closure is taken with respect to the
L^{2}
-norm, but the
\mathbb{R}
-modules
Z_{j}^{p,\bullet}(U)
and
B_{l}^{p,\bullet}(U)
are viewed via the isomorphism (
8.1
). In other words, we do not view a form in either
Z_{j}^{p,\bullet}(U)
or
B_{l}^{p,\bullet}(U)
as purely its representative of weight
p
, as this would also prevent the subspaces
Z_{j}^{p,\bullet}(U)
from being closed.

Using this characterisation of
\mathscr{D}_{j,l}^{\bullet,\bullet}(U)
, as well as the submodules
Z_{r}^{\bullet,\bullet}(U)
and
B_{r}^{\bullet,\bullet}(U)
, as quotients, there is a natural way of extending norms from the space of compactly supported forms. Indeed, given a norm
\|\cdot\|_{X}
on a Banach space
X
, we define the norm
\|\cdot\|_{X/Y}
on the quotient
X/Y
as follows

(8.2)

\displaystyle\|[x]\|_{X/Y}:=\inf_{y\in Y}\|x-y\|_{X}\ \text{ for any }x\in X\,.

Definition 8.3

(Seminorms on
\mathscr{D}_{j,l}^{p,\bullet}
)
.

For any compactly supported
\alpha\in Z_{r}^{p,\bullet}(U)\cap\Omega_{c}^{\bullet}(U)
and
\beta\in B_{r}^{p,\bullet}(U)\cap\Omega_{c}^{\bullet}(U)
, we can use the isomorphisms (
8.1
) and formula (
8.2
) to define the seminorms

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{K,i}([\alpha]):=\inf\{p_{K,i}(\alpha+\tilde{\alpha})\mid\tilde{\alpha}\in\mathcal{Z}_{r-1}^{p+1,\bullet}(U)\}\ \text{ for any }[\alpha]\in\mathcal{Z}_{r}^{p,\bullet}(U)/\mathcal{Z}_{r-1}^{p+1,\bullet}(U)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{K,i}([\beta]):=\inf\{p_{K,i}(\beta+\tilde{\beta})\mid\tilde{\beta}\in\mathcal{Z}_{r-1}^{p+1,\bullet}(U)\}\ \text{ for any }[\beta]\in\mathcal{B}_{r}^{p,\bullet}(U)/\mathcal{Z}_{r-1}^{p+1,\bullet}(U)\,,

where
p_{K,i}
denotes the seminorm on compactly supported forms
\omega\in\Omega^{k}_{c}(U)

p_{K,i}(\omega)=\max\big\{\|\partial^{\alpha}\omega_{I}\|_{C^{0}(K)}\mid\omega=\sum_{I}\omega_{I}\,\theta_{I},\;I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in I(k,n),\,|\alpha|\leq i\big\}

and
\theta_{I}=\theta_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\theta_{i_{k}}\in\bigwedge^{k}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
.

Finally, for any
\omega\in\mathscr{D}_{j,l}^{p,\bullet}(U)=Z_{j}^{p,\bullet}(U)\cap\Omega_{c}^{\bullet}(U)/\,\overline{B_{l}^{p,\bullet}(U)\cap\Omega_{c}^{\bullet}(U)}
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{K,i}(\omega):=\inf\{\mathfrak{p}_{K,i}(\omega+\tilde{\omega})\mid\tilde{\omega}\in\overline{B_{l}^{p,\bullet}(U)\cap\Omega^{\bullet}_{c}(U)}\}\,,

hence allowing to construct the associated
strict inductive limit topology
on
\mathscr{D}_{j,l}^{p,\bullet}(U)
.

Definition 8.4

(Spectral current and its boundary)
.

We say that
T:\mathscr{D}^{p,k-p}_{j,l}(U)\to\mathbb{R}
is a
(p,k,j,l)
-
spectral current in
U
, or simply a
spectral current in
U
, if it is linear and continuous with respect to
the strict inductive limit topology on
\mathscr{D}^{p,k-p}_{j,l}(U)
.
The
boundary
of
T
is naturally defined as

\partial^{l}T(\omega)=T(\Delta_{l}\omega)\quad\text{for each}\;\omega\in\mathscr{D}^{p-l,k-1-p+l}_{l,m}(U)

where we have set the spectral differential

\Delta_{l}\colon E_{l,m}^{p-l,k-1-p+l}\longrightarrow E_{j,l}^{p,k-p}\,,

for an appropriate choice of
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.

The continuity of the boundary of
T
follows directly from the characterisation of the differential
\Delta_{j}
via the exterior derivative (see Proposition
6.2
) and the well-posedness of the seminorms
\tilde{p}_{K,i}
defined on
\mathscr{D}_{j,l}^{p,\bullet}(U)
. In other words, the properties of the boundary operator
\partial^{j}
follow directly from the properties of the boundary operator
\partial
of a classical current, given by the exterior derivative.

Therefore, given
T
a
(p+j,k,m,j)
-spectral current in
U
, we have that
\partial^{j}T
is a
(p,k-1,j,l)
-spectral current. Again, the choices of the indices
m,l\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
are not uniquely defined, and are chosen depending on the spectral complex so that both
E_{j,l}^{p,k-1-p}
and
E_{m,j}^{p+j,k-p-j}
are nontrivial.

If
\Sigma
is an oriented
k
-dimensional
spectral manifold
of class
C^{1}
, then we set

[[\Sigma]]_{p}(\omega):=\int_{\Sigma}\omega\ \text{ for any }\omega\in\mathscr{D}_{j,l}^{p,k-p}

to denote the
(p,k,j,l)
-spectral current associated with the spectral manifold
\Sigma
of degree
p
. Here we use the notation
[[\Sigma]]_{p}
is to highlight the fact that
\operatorname{deg}(\Sigma)=p
.

In the case of an oriented
k
-dimensional
C^{1}
spectral manifold
\Sigma
with boundary
\partial\Sigma
, then by Stokes’ theorem for spectral complexes (Theorem
6.9
), we get

\displaystyle[[\partial^{j}\Sigma]]_{p}(\omega)=\int_{\partial\Sigma}\omega=\int_{\Sigma}\Delta_{j}\omega=[[\Sigma]]_{p+j}(\Delta_{j}\omega)\ \text{ for any }\omega\in\mathscr{D}_{j,l}^{p,k-1-p}\,.

Inspired by the classical definition of comass in
[
12
]
, we define a modified notion of comass adapted to the bigraded structure of
\Omega^{\bullet}_{c}(\mathbb{G})
(see Definition
2.3
). This modification is designed to capture the additional geometric information encoded by the homogeneous weight decomposition.

Definition 8.5

(Weighted comass of a differential form)
.

Let
\theta\in\bigwedge^{k}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
and let
W_{k}
denote all the possible weights of covectors in degree
k
, then we can introduce a weighted-comass as

\displaystyle\|\theta\|_{p}:=\sup\{\theta(v)\mid v\in\bigwedge\nolimits^{k}\mathfrak{g},\,v\text{ is simple and of weight/degree }p,\text{ and }|v|\leq 1\}\,.

For each
p\in W_{k}
, we will refer to
\|\theta\|_{p}
as the
p
-comass of
\theta\in\bigwedge^{k}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
.

We note that in the case where
\theta\in\bigwedge^{k}\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
, is a homogenous
k
-covector of weight
p\in W_{k}
, its comass
\|\theta\|
in the classical sense (see e.g.
[
12
, Subection 1.8.1]
) coincides with
\|\theta\|_{p}
by the orthogonality of covectors of different weight (
2.5
).

This definition readily extends to arbitrary compactly supported forms, for that, given
\omega\in\Omega^{k}_{c}(U)
, we define its
p
-comass as

\displaystyle\|\omega\|_{p}:=\sup\{\|\omega(x)\|_{p}\mid x\in U\}\ \text{ for a given }p\in W_{k}\,.

Definition 8.6

(Comass of a form in
Z_{j}^{p,k}
)
.

Let
\omega\in Z_{j}^{p,k}(U)\cap\Omega^{\bullet}_{c}(U)
. Then by the isomorphism
8.1
, we can view the form
\omega
as a linear combination
\omega-z_{p+1}-\cdots-z_{p+j-1}\in\oplus_{i=0}^{j-1}\Omega^{p+i,k-p-i}_{c}(U)
, where forms
z_{p+i}\in\Omega^{p+i,k-p-i}_{c}(U)
are uniquely determined up to elements in
\mathcal{Z}_{j-1}^{p+1}(U)\cap\Omega_{c}^{\bullet}(G)
. We then define its comass as the
p
-comass of the form
\omega-z_{p+1}-\cdots-z_{p+j-1}
, that is

\displaystyle\|\omega\|=\|\omega\|_{p}=\|\omega-z_{p+1}-\cdots-z_{p+j-1}\|_{p}=\|\omega-z_{p+1}-\cdots-z_{p+j-1}+\mathcal{Z}_{j-1}^{p+1}(U)\cap\Omega_{c}^{\bullet}(G)\|_{p}\,.

The first equality follows from the fact that
\omega
is a homogeneous form of weight
p
, while the others are due to the fact that the
p
-comass of forms of weight strictly greater than
p
vanish identically.

Remark 8.7
.

Note that this definition mimics exactly the simplifications needed in Theorem
6.9
in order to obtain the validity of Stokes’ theorem when integrating over a manifold of a given fixed degree
p
equal to the weight of the form
\omega\in Z_{j}^{p,\bullet}
considered.

Definition 8.8

(Mass of a spectral current)
.

Let
T\colon\mathscr{D}_{j,l}^{p,k-p}(U)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a
(p,k,j,l)
-spectral current in
U
, we define its
p
-mass as

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(T):=\sup\{T(\omega)\mid\omega\in\mathscr{D}_{j,l}^{p,k-p}\ \text{ and }\|\omega\|\leq 1\}\,.

Since we have shown that, given
T
a
(p+j,k,m,j)
-current in
U
, its boundary
\partial^{j}T
is a
(p,k-1,j,l)
-current in
U
, and so it is possible to compute its mass to. Similarly to what has been done in the literature, we propose the definition of
normal spectral currents
as those for which both
T
and
\partial^{j}T
have finite mass, so that
\mathbf{N}(T)=\mathbf{M}(T)+\mathbf{M}(\partial^{j}T)
.
