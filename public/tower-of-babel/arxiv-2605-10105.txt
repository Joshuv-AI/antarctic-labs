Application of Machine Learning to 21 cm Cosmology
Hayato Shimabukuro

Abstract
In this chapter, the use of machine learning (ML) in redshifted 21 cm cosmology is discussed, especially for the cosmic dawn, the Epoch of Reionization, and the scientific program of SKA-Low. The 21 cm signal is useful because it can directly probe diffuse neutral hydrogen. At the same time, it is a difficult signal, since the observable depends on density, ionization, heating, radiation backgrounds, and instrumental response in a nonlinear way. The first part of this chapter reviews the basic physical ingredients needed for the later discussion, including the global signal, spatial fluctuations, morphology-aware summaries, and the 21 cm forest. The next part describes the main difficulties for realistic analysis, such as bright foregrounds, radio-frequency interference, ionospheric and calibration effects, incomplete sampling, and the cost of forward modeling in large parameter spaces. Based on this background, ML applications are grouped by their role in the analysis pipeline. Observation-domain methods work on contaminated data products; theory-domain methods accelerate or compress forward modeling; and inference-domain methods connect observables to astrophysical and cosmological constraints. The 21 cm forest is also discussed as a case where one-dimensional spectra, small-scale information, and uncertain source populations make ML useful.



0.1
Introduction

Before the first stars and galaxies formed, the Universe did not contain luminous sources. This stage is usually called the Dark Ages. It ended gradually when the first generations of stars and galaxies started to illuminate, heat, and ionize the surrounding intergalactic medium. The cosmic dawn and the Epoch of Reionization (EoR) describe this broad transition from a cold and mostly neutral Universe to the ionized state observed at later times. Figure
1
places these epochs in the standard cosmic timeline. To determine when this transformation occurred and what kinds of sources drove it is still one of the central goals of observational cosmology. High-redshift galaxies and quasars already give important clues, but they do not directly map the thermal and ionization state of diffuse hydrogen over cosmological volumes.

The redshifted 21 cm line is the most direct probe of this diffuse neutral gas
(
2006PhR…433..181F
;
2012RPPh…75h6901P
;
2023PASJ…75S…1S
)
. Global-signal experiments measure the sky-averaged brightness temperature as a function of observing frequency, while interferometers such as LOFAR, MWA, HERA, and SKA-Low target spatial fluctuations. Thus, the scientific motivation is clear. At the same time, the practical difficulty is also clear: the expected signal is faint, the foregrounds are bright, and the data analysis must handle instrumental, astrophysical, and computational uncertainties at the same time. In the SKA era, sensitivity alone will not be the only limiting issue. Calibration, foreground control, data volume, and high-dimensional inference have to be treated as parts of one analysis problem
(
2020PASP..132f2001L
;
Kern & Liu 2021
)
.

This chapter does not aim to give a complete introduction to 21 cm astrophysics. Instead, it gives the physics needed to understand where ML can help, where it can be risky, and what kind of validation is required.

0.2
Physics of the 21 cm Signal

For the following discussion, only the basic relations are needed. More detailed derivations can be found in
(
2006PhR…433..181F
;
2012RPPh…75h6901P
;
2020PASP..132f2001L
;
2023PASJ…75S…1S
)
. Neutral hydrogen produces the 21 cm line through the hyperfine transition of its ground state. The relative abundance of the triplet and singlet states is described by the spin temperature
T_{\rm s}
,

\frac{n_{1}}{n_{0}}=3\exp\left(-\frac{T_{\star}}{T_{\rm s}}\right),

(1)

where
n_{1}
and
n_{0}
are the triplet and singlet populations, and
T_{\star}=h\nu_{21}/k_{\rm B}\simeq 0.068\ {\rm K}
is the temperature corresponding to the hyperfine energy splitting.
If the spin temperature is equal to the cosmic microwave background (CMB) temperature,
T_{\rm s}=T_{\gamma}
, no differential 21 cm signal is observed. The signal appears when physical processes make
T_{\rm s}
different from
T_{\gamma}
. Collisions, Lyman-
\alpha
coupling, and X-ray heating are the most important processes during the epochs considered here:

T_{\rm s}^{-1}=\frac{T_{\gamma}^{-1}+x_{\rm c}T_{\rm K}^{-1}+x_{\alpha}T_{\rm K}^{-1}}{1+x_{\rm c}+x_{\alpha}},

(2)

where
x_{\rm c}
and
x_{\alpha}
denote the collisional and Lyman-
\alpha
coupling coefficients, and
T_{\rm K}
is the kinetic temperature of the gas.

A commonly used form of the differential brightness temperature is

\displaystyle\delta T_{\rm b}(\mathbf{x},z)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle 27\ {\rm mK}\ x_{\rm HI}(\mathbf{x},z)\left(1+\delta_{\rm b}(\mathbf{x},z)\right)\left(1-\frac{T_{\gamma}(z)}{T_{\rm s}(\mathbf{x},z)}\right)

(3)

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{1+z}{10}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{0.15}{\Omega_{\rm m}h^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{\Omega_{\rm b}h^{2}}{0.023}\right).

The sign of the signal is mainly determined by the ratio between
T_{\rm s}
and
T_{\gamma}
: absorption corresponds to
T_{\rm s}<T_{\gamma}
, while emission corresponds to
T_{\rm s}>T_{\gamma}
. The above expression omits the line-of-sight velocity-gradient factor and finite-optical-depth corrections. These terms are needed in precision modeling, but the qualitative point can already be seen: the observable mixes density, ionization, temperature, and radiation backgrounds in a nonlinear way
(
2006PhR…433..181F
;
2012RPPh…75h6901P
;
2020PASP..132f2001L
)
. Because of this coupling, the same 21 cm observable can be consistent with several different source models.

0.2.1
21 cm global signal

The sky-averaged 21 cm signal, usually called the global signal, is

\overline{\delta T}_{21}(z)=\langle\delta T_{\rm b}(\mathbf{x},z)\rangle.

(4)

It gives a compact summary of the thermal and ionization history. Its evolution is often described by three broad transitions: the onset of Lyman-
\alpha
coupling, the absorption trough before substantial X-ray heating, and the later transition toward emission once the gas is heated above the CMB temperature. Figure
2
shows this sequence schematically. At very high redshift,
T_{\rm s}
remains close to
T_{\gamma}
and the signal is weak. During cosmic dawn, radiative coupling links
T_{\rm s}
to the colder gas temperature, producing a negative signal. X-ray heating then raises the gas temperature and can turn the signal positive. Finally, as reionization removes neutral hydrogen, the 21 cm signal fades away.

The global signal is powerful because it directly traces the integrated history of early radiation backgrounds. The weak point is that it is only one sky-averaged quantity. Different combinations of star-formation efficiency, escape fraction, X-ray efficiency, and source spectra can produce similar global histories. Therefore, spatial fluctuations, higher-order statistics, and complementary observables are necessary for breaking degeneracies.

Schematic redshift evolution of the sky-averaged 21 cm brightness temperature (the 21 cm global signal) through the Dark Ages, cosmic dawn, and the Epoch of Reionization. The schematic highlights the absorption trough during cosmic dawn, the transition to emission after X-ray heating, and the eventual disappearance of the signal as reionization progresses.

0.2.2
Spatial fluctuations

Spatial fluctuations contain the information lost by averaging over the sky. They respond to the three-dimensional distribution of matter, radiation sources, heated regions, and ionized bubbles. A standard way to summarize these fluctuations is to Fourier transform the brightness-temperature field
\delta T_{\rm b}(\mathbf{x},z)
and compute its variance as a function of wavenumber. The 21 cm power spectrum is

P_{21}(k,z)=\left\langle\big|\widetilde{\delta T_{\rm b}}(\mathbf{k},z)\big|^{2}\right\rangle,

(5)

which measures the variance on different spatial scales. Observational results are often expressed using the dimensionless form

\Delta^{2}_{21}(k,z)=\frac{k^{3}}{2\pi^{2}}P_{21}(k,z).

(6)

This is the quantity shown in Fig.
3
.

The redshift evolution of the power spectrum reflects the sequence of Lyman-
\alpha
coupling, heating, and ionized-bubble growth. Figure
3
compares theoretical expectations for
\Delta^{2}_{21}
with current upper limits. Current limits from MWA, LOFAR, HERA, and related pathfinder experiments remain above many fiducial predictions, but they already rule out or disfavor some extreme astrophysical scenarios and provide practical guidance for future surveys.

A power spectrum is not, however, a complete description of the 21 cm field. Reionization is highly inhomogeneous, and the resulting brightness-temperature field can be strongly non-Gaussian. For this reason, analyses often combine the power spectrum with one-point summaries, higher-order statistics, and morphology-sensitive descriptors such as skewness, bispectra, bubble statistics, or topological quantities

Watkinson & Pritchard 2015

;

;

)
.

Interferometers do not observe
\delta T_{\rm b}
directly. They measure visibilities: Fourier components of the beam-weighted sky. Because the beam and baseline response vary with frequency, smooth-spectrum foregrounds can leak into Fourier modes that would otherwise be used for the cosmological signal. This motivates analyses in
(k_{\perp},k_{\parallel})
space and the familiar foreground-wedge language
(

;

;
Liu et al. 2014
)
.

0.2.3
Beyond the power spectrum: morphology-aware summaries

The discussion above may give an impression that the power spectrum is the final observable for 21 cm cosmology. This is not correct. The power spectrum is very important, and it should remain a reference statistic. It is compact, it is directly connected to interferometric measurements, and current observational limits are usually given in this form. However, it is still a two-point statistic. It keeps information about the variance at each scale, but it does not keep the full phase information of the field. This limitation is harmless only when the field is close to Gaussian. The 21 cm field during the cosmic dawn and reionization is not usually in this situation.

The reason is physical. Ionized regions grow around biased sources, X-ray heating can be patchy, and Lyman-
\alpha
coupling is also inhomogeneous. These processes make a field with bubbles, tunnels, isolated cold regions, heated patches, and connected neutral structures. Two simulated maps may have almost the same power spectrum but different morphology. In that case, using only
P_{21}(k)
means that some useful information has already been discarded. Therefore, higher-order and morphology-sensitive summaries are not only technical extensions. They are needed when the field is far from a Gaussian random field.

The simplest extension is one-point statistics. The probability distribution function, variance, skewness, and kurtosis use the distribution of brightness-temperature values in the map. They do not know where each pixel is located, but they are sensitive to the mixture of neutral, ionized, cold, and heated regions. Earlier studies used the 21 cm PDF and related moments to follow the reionization history and to distinguish reionization topologies which can be partly degenerate in the power spectrum
(
Ichikawa et al. 2010
;
Watkinson & Pritchard 2014
;
2015MNRAS.451..467S
;
2016PASJ…68…61K
)
. Their advantage is simplicity. Their weakness is also clear. They compress the image strongly, and rare pixels or residual noise can affect high-order moments.

A more direct higher-order statistic in Fourier space is the bispectrum. It measures correlations among three Fourier modes and therefore keeps part of the phase-coupling information which is absent in the power spectrum. The 21 cm bispectrum has been used to study non-Gaussianity from X-ray heating, ionized bubbles, light-cone effects, and reionization morphology
(
2016MNRAS.458.3003S
;
2018MNRAS.476.4007M
;
Hutter et al. 2020
;
Mondal et al. 2021
;
Watkinson et al. 2022
)
. This is a natural statistic when the analysis stays in Fourier space. At the same time, the bispectrum is a large data vector, and its covariance and foreground response are not simple. It can add information, but it is not a free improvement over the power spectrum.

Bubble-size distributions give another view. They try to describe the characteristic scale of ionized or neutral regions more directly. This is physically attractive because the growth and merging of ionized bubbles is one of the main pictures of reionization. Several definitions can be used, such as spherical-average, mean-free-path, or friends-of-friends methods, and these definitions do not always give the same answer
(
Giri et al. 2018
;
Doussot & Semelin 2022
)
. For this reason, a bubble-size distribution should not be treated as an observable without convention. The definition of a bubble, the thresholding method, beam smoothing, and foreground filtering all affect the result.

Topological summaries are useful because they do not require choosing one bubble radius. Minkowski functionals measure geometrical and topological properties of excursion sets, such as volume, surface area, curvature, and Euler characteristic. In the 21 cm context,
Yoshiura et al. 2017
applied 3D Minkowski functionals to the brightness-temperature field and showed that they can trace non-Gaussian morphology caused by spin-temperature fluctuations and ionized bubbles. Related work using contour Minkowski tensors also studied the shape and size anisotropy of ionized regions
(
Kapahtia et al. 2018
)
. More recently, reionization-parameter inference with 3D Minkowski functionals has been examined with SKA-Low-like observational effects
(
Diao et al. 2024
)
. These papers are the more direct references for Minkowski-based 21 cm morphology than general ML papers.

Betti numbers and persistent homology describe topology in a different but related language. Betti numbers count connected components, tunnels, and cavities. Persistent homology follows how such structures appear and disappear when a threshold is changed, and it gives a hierarchy of topological features rather than a single number at one threshold. This approach has been developed for the topology of reionization and for mock 21 cm observations
(
Giri & Mellema 2021
;
Elbers & van de Weygaert 2019
;
Elbers & van de Weygaert 2023
)
. The useful point is that topology can describe percolation and connectivity, which are not cleanly captured by the angle-averaged power spectrum.

Wavelet-based summaries give an intermediate case between handcrafted statistics and deep neural networks. The wavelet scattering transform uses fixed wavelet filters and nonlinear operations to extract multiscale, non-Gaussian information. It is not as transparent as the power spectrum, but it is much less flexible than a trained neural network. For 21 cm images, the WST has been proposed and tested as a way to extract non-Gaussian information and improve astrophysical constraints
(
Greig et al. 2022
;
Greig et al. 2023
;
Hothi et al. 2024
;
2025arXiv251200402S
)
. Comparisons among different summary statistics also show that the question is not simply whether a statistic is new, but how much information it keeps after noise, foreground treatment, and parameter degeneracies are included
(
Prelogović & Mesinger 2024
)
.

There is a useful way to understand these summaries. The power spectrum asks how large the fluctuations are at a given scale. The PDF asks how often each brightness temperature appears. The bispectrum asks whether structures at different scales are correlated in phase. Bubble-size statistics ask how large the ionized or neutral regions are under a chosen definition. Minkowski functionals and Betti numbers ask how regions are connected when the threshold is changed. Wavelet summaries ask how structures at several scales are coupled. These questions are different. Therefore, disagreement among these summaries is not automatically a problem. It can be a sign that the models differ in a way hidden by one particular compression.

For ML applications, this point is important. A neural network trained on maps or light-cones can in principle use all morphology information, including information that is difficult to write as an analytic statistic. This is one reason why convolutional neural networks can give strong parameter constraints in idealized tests. However, this strength also creates a weakness. If the network is too flexible, it may use features which are not physically robust. For example, it may learn the resolution of the simulation, the exact smoothing scale, the noise pattern, or an artifact of the foreground treatment. Then the apparent accuracy on test simulations can be misleading.

Handcrafted morphology summaries have a different weakness. They are more interpretable, but they may not be optimal. A Betti curve or a Minkowski functional compresses the field in a particular way. The choice is physically motivated, but it still discards information. A learned summary may keep more information, but its meaning is less clear. For this reason, the best use is often not to replace one by the other. A practical strategy is to compare power spectra, physical morphology summaries, and learned summaries on the same simulations. If they prefer the same region of parameter space, the inference is more credible. If they do not agree, the disagreement should be studied instead of being averaged away.

These methods are especially relevant for SKA-Low because the final data products will not be ideal three-dimensional maps. Real analysis may involve masked Fourier modes, anisotropic noise, light-cone evolution, and foreground avoidance. A morphology statistic that works only on clean simulation boxes may fail after observational effects are applied. Therefore, every morphology-aware summary needs an observation-level test. It should be measured not only on the intrinsic brightness-temperature field, but also after beam smoothing, thermal noise, foreground filtering, and the same masks used in the data analysis.

The main lesson is simple. The power spectrum should remain a reference statistic, because it is well understood and directly connected to current upper limits. But it should not be treated as the only scientifically meaningful statistic. The 21 cm signal is a field, not only a curve in
k
. ML is useful partly because it can use field-level information. Still, this use is safe only when the learned information is compared with interpretable summaries and tested under realistic observational changes.

0.2.4
21 cm forest

The 21 cm forest offers a different route to the high-redshift neutral Universe. Instead of measuring diffuse emission or absorption over the sky, it searches for narrow absorption features in the spectra of high-redshift radio-loud background sources. These features are produced by intervening neutral hydrogen. The method is especially sensitive to cold gas and small-scale structure, and is therefore most informative before strong X-ray heating or in environments where neutral absorbers remain abundant
(
Carilli et al. 2002
;
Furlanetto & Loeb 2002
;
Furlanetto 2006
;
Xu et al. 2009
;
Xu et al. 2011
;
Mack & Wyithe 2012
;
Ewall-Wice et al. 2014
;
Šoltinský et al. 2021
;
Šoltinský et al. 2025
)
.

Because forest absorption probes small line-of-sight scales, it is sensitive to information that is difficult to access with direct imaging alone. The 21 cm forest has therefore been discussed as a probe of the thermal state of the neutral intergalactic medium, small-scale cosmological structure, and dark-matter microphysics. Recent work has expanded the analysis beyond individual absorption features to one-dimensional power spectra, halo-based modeling, learned summaries, and topological statistics, while continuing to examine whether suitable background sources and instrumental sensitivity will be available
(
Shimabukuro et al. 2014
;
Shimabukuro et al. 2020a
;
Shimabukuro et al. 2020b
;
2023PhRvD.107l3520S
;
Shao et al. 2023
;
Shao et al. 2025
;
Shimabukuro et al. 2025
;
Shimabukuro 2026
;
Šoltinský et al. 2025
)
.

0.3
Challenges in interpreting 21 cm data

The difficulty of 21 cm cosmology is not caused by a single obstacle. Three problems appear together: the signal is intrinsically non-Gaussian, the measurements are contaminated by severe foregrounds and instrumental effects, and the inference problem is computationally expensive.

0.3.1
Physical and computational challenges

The brightness temperature depends on several coupled fields: density, ionization fraction, kinetic and spin temperatures, and radiation backgrounds. These fields evolve nonlinearly and are driven by source populations whose properties are still poorly constrained. As a result, different astrophysical models can generate similar global signals or power spectra
(
Mesinger et al. 2011
;
Greig & Mesinger 2015
;
Greig & Mesinger 2017
;
Greig & Mesinger 2018
;
Park et al. 2019
;
Sitwell et al. 2014
;
Mesinger et al. 2014
;
Shao et al. 2023
;
Shimabukuro et al. 2025
)
.

These degeneracies are not limited to parameter values. Information is spread across redshift, scale, and morphology. Two models may agree in the global signal or in an angle-averaged power spectrum, but still differ in ionized-bubble topology, higher-order statistics, or light-cone anisotropy. This is the motivation for using multiple summaries or even field-level information, rather than compressing the data too early.

Computation creates a second bottleneck. Robust inference requires many forward simulations and careful propagation of uncertainty through large data vectors. Semi-numerical simulations are much faster than full radiation-hydrodynamic calculations, but Bayesian exploration can still become expensive when repeated over large astrophysical parameter spaces. The difficulty increases further once realistic covariance, foreground choices, and instrumental nuisance parameters are included
(
Prelogović & Mesinger 2024
)
.

The nuisance space is not a technical afterthought. Calibration residuals, foreground modeling assumptions, beam uncertainties, and data-selection choices can all affect the recovered signal. Treating these effects as fixed choices can make the final constraints appear artificially precise. A more defensible analysis must either marginalize over them or demonstrate that the result is insensitive to them.

Simulator error also has to be handled explicitly. Covariance matrices estimated from a finite simulation suite are noisy, and semi-numerical models do not reproduce every feature of higher-fidelity calculations. If such differences are ignored, they can bias parameter recovery. This is why emulator error, simulator mismatch, and model discrepancy are increasingly treated as parts of the inference problem, not as small corrections to be added at the end
(
Bevins et al. 2025
)
.

0.3.2
Observational complexity

Foreground emission is brighter than the cosmological signal by many orders of magnitude. Its intrinsic spectral smoothness helps. However, the frequency-dependent response of an interferometer mixes foreground power into the modes of interest and produces the foreground wedge
(
Chapman & Jelić 2019
;
Datta et al. 2010
;
Parsons et al. 2012
;
Liu et al. 2014
;
Liu et al. 2014
;
2020PASP..132f2001L
)
. Radio-frequency interference (RFI), ionospheric variations, gain-calibration errors, polarization leakage, and incomplete
uv
sampling give further complications
(
Offringa et al. 2010
;
Offringa et al. 2015
;
Mevius et al. 2016
;
de Gasperin et al. 2018
;
Trott & Wayth 2016
;
Mertens et al. 2020
;
Kern & Liu 2021
;
Brackenhoff et al. 2024
;
Mertens et al. 2024
)
.

The visibility domain is a natural place to understand these effects. An interferometer does not measure the brightness-temperature field itself, but Fourier modes of the beam-weighted sky. Since both the primary beam and the projected baselines depend on frequency, foregrounds that are spectrally smooth on the sky are no longer perfectly smooth after passing through the instrument. This is the physical origin of leakage into higher
k_{\parallel}
modes.

Calibration is also difficult for the same reason. Small gain errors, beam-model inaccuracies, direction-dependent effects, and polarization leakage can leave chromatic residuals. These residuals do not have to look like the original foreground. They can mimic part of the spectral structure expected from the 21 cm signal. Heavy RFI flagging creates another problem, because it makes the sampling in time and frequency irregular. Unless the window function and error propagation are handled carefully, this irregularity can broaden mode mixing.

In practice, current analyses usually do not rely on only one foreground strategy. Wedge avoidance discards contaminated regions of Fourier space, and it is relatively conservative. Foreground subtraction or reconstruction tries to recover more information. However, it depends more strongly on modeling assumptions. For SKA-level work, a combination of avoidance, subtraction, and visibility-level validation is likely more reliable than one method alone.

0.3.3
Additional SKA-era challenges

SKA-Low changes the scale of this problem. Data volume, near-real-time processing, reproducibility, and uncertainty propagation across calibration, imaging, foreground treatment, and inference become part of the scientific analysis. Therefore, pipeline architecture is not only engineering. It also affects what can be inferred. This motivates ML methods which are fast, auditable, uncertainty-aware, and robust to distribution shift
(
Gagnon-Hartman et al. 2021
;
Kennedy et al. 2024
;
Sooknunan et al. 2024
)
.

At this scale, data logistics and analysis choices cannot be separated clearly. Continuous visibility streams, large calibration state spaces, and repeated imaging and inference cycles make manually tuned offline workflows not sufficient. A reliable SKA pipeline will need automated quality control, deterministic reprocessing, and provenance tracking. Then a result can be reproduced after software, calibration models, or hardware assumptions change.

Domain shift is likely one of the main failure modes for ML. Training data must come from simulations. On the other hand, real data contain changing ionospheric conditions, imperfect beam models, hardware drift, source-subtraction residuals, and sky complexity that simulations cannot capture perfectly. Therefore, a high-capacity network can give a confident answer for the wrong reason. Out-of-distribution tests, uncertainty calibration, and stress tests against alternative simulators are not optional safeguards. They are part of the scientific validation.

Therefore, the most realistic SKA strategy is hybrid. Physical models provide interpretability and constraints on what the pipeline can do. ML components can accelerate expensive steps, identify structured residuals, or compress information that is hard to summarize by hand. The aim should not be to replace the whole traditional pipeline. It should be to build an end-to-end analysis in which the approximations remain visible.

0.4
Machine learning approaches to 21 cm signal analysis

ML applications in 21 cm cosmology have rapidly increased in recent years
(
Moriwaki et al. 2023
)
. However, listing architectures one after another does not give a very useful picture of the field. A CNN used for RFI flagging, a Gaussian-process model used for foreground covariance, an emulator for the power spectrum, and a neural posterior estimator for astrophysical parameters are all called “ML”. However, they solve different problems and have different risks.

For this reason, the literature is organized by function rather than by algorithm. Observation-domain methods act on measured or partially processed data. Theory-domain methods speed up or compress forward modeling. Inference-domain methods connect data products to astrophysical and cosmological parameters. This division is not exact, because many methods cross the boundaries. However, it makes the scientific role of each ML component easier to judge. Figure
4
summarizes this workflow.

Figure 4:

Schematic overview of ML workflows in 21 cm cosmology.
The arrows indicate the main forward flow emphasized in this review: ML can enter at the observation stage to clean or reconstruct contaminated data products, at the theory stage to emulate or compress forward models, and at the inference stage to map observables to astrophysical or cosmological constraints.
In practice, validation and iterative refinement can also pass information in the reverse direction.

The workflow in Fig.
4
should not be read as a fixed sequence. Cleaned data may be reduced to conventional summaries or passed through learned feature extractors. Theory-side surrogates may be used both to create training sets and to accelerate posterior sampling. Posterior checks may also reveal that the simulations or preprocessing were inadequate. The figure is useful mainly because it gives a simple question for each ML method: is it preserving the signal, accelerating the model, or estimating the parameters?

0.4.1
Observation-domain methods

Observation-domain ML works before the final cosmological inference. Its inputs may be visibilities, time–frequency data, dirty maps, image cubes, or intermediate products. Its output is not a posterior. It is a data product which later analysis will use. This makes the approach useful for SKA-Low, because manual inspection and hand-tuned rules will not scale. At the same time, it makes the validation demanding. A method that improves the visual appearance or reduces variance may still remove part of the cosmological signal.

RFI mitigation is the clearest example for operation. Supervised classifiers and anomaly-detection methods can flag contaminated time–frequency samples more consistently and at larger scale than manual inspection or simple thresholding
(
Akeret et al. 2017
;
Kerrigan et al. 2019
;
Vafaei Sadr et al. 2020
)
. The scientific consequence is not only cleaner data. Flagging changes the sampling window that enters map-making and power-spectrum estimation, so false positives and false negatives propagate into leakage and error estimates.

After contaminated samples are removed, the remaining sampling becomes irregular. Hard flagging leaves gaps in time–frequency space, and these gaps can broaden Fourier leakage. Therefore, learned inpainting or reconstruction has been proposed as a way to fill missing regions with statistically consistent values before later processing
(
Pagano et al. 2023
)
. Such methods are useful only when they behave like controlled interpolation. If they generate plausible-looking structures without preserving the relevant statistics, they create a new systematic error rather than solving the old one.

Foreground mitigation is another major target. Because the instrument mixes foreground power into cosmological modes, the task is not only to subtract a smooth spectral component. The model must describe the joint spatial–spectral structure of the contamination while preserving the 21 cm signal. Neural methods have been used to reconstruct wedge-filtered light-cones and to recover information from contaminated tomographic data products
(
Gagnon-Hartman et al. 2021
;
Kennedy et al. 2024
)
. In parallel, Gaussian-process foreground pipelines with ML-enhanced covariance kernels have been developed for more flexible separation, including applications to LOFAR EoR analyses
(
Acharya et al. 2024a
;
Mertens et al. 2024
;
Acharya et al. 2024b
)
. These examples are important because they move ML away from unconstrained denoising and toward statistical models for which residual covariance and signal loss can be inspected.

Calibration and ionospheric effects form a related class of problems. Small gain errors, beam mismatch, direction-dependent effects, and variable propagation through the ionosphere can leave coherent spectral structures. These residuals are often too complicated for a very low-dimensional analytic model. At the same time, they are too structured to be treated as white noise. Therefore, data-driven corrections and adaptive covariance models have been explored to reduce leakage and stabilize recovered power spectra
(
Kern & Liu 2021
;
Brackenhoff et al. 2024
;
Mertens et al. 2024
)
. For global-signal radiometers, calibration has a slightly different form but the logic is similar. A machine-learning calibration framework has been tested for radiometer systems, where receiver reflections and impedance mismatch can otherwise introduce spectral structure that is dangerous for a sky-averaged 21 cm search
(
Leeney et al. 2025
)
.

ML can also enter before the data are taken. Recent work using conditional neural Bayes ratio estimation shows how a neural ratio estimator can be conditioned on experimental-design parameters and used to scan a continuous design space
(
Leeney et al. 2026
)
. This is still an early direction, but it is conceptually important. In this case, ML is not cleaning data or estimating parameters from a fixed experiment. It is used to decide which experimental configuration has higher discovery probability.

Image-domain ML is also becoming more common. Segmentation networks can identify ionized and neutral regions in noisy or foreground-degraded maps, enabling analyses based on bubble sizes, morphology, or topology
(
Bianco et al. 2021
;
Bianco et al. 2024
)
. Recent work has moved from segmentation to partial recovery of the 21 cm morphology itself from foreground-contaminated observations
(
Bianco et al. 2025
)
. This direction is scientifically interesting because reionization is not a Gaussian random field. Much of the information is morphological. The danger is that a visually convincing reconstruction can still fail in the statistics important for inference. Therefore, pixel-level agreement is a weak benchmark unless it is accompanied by tests using power spectra, topology, morphology, and downstream parameter recovery.

Another observation-domain use is cross-domain translation using external tracers. Conditional generative adversarial networks have been used to predict 21 cm maps from Lyman-
\alpha
emitter distributions, and related image-to-image methods have been applied in line-intensity mapping to separate confused components and reconstruct three-dimensional fields
(
Yoshiura et al. 2021
;
Moriwaki et al. 2020
;
Moriwaki et al. 2021a
;
Moriwaki & Yoshida 2021b
)
. These methods should be understood as conditioning tools or cross-probe fusion tools. They can give informative priors or morphology-aware summaries, but they do not replace radio observations.

The maturity of these methods is not uniform. RFI classifiers and covariance-based foreground models are relatively close to operational use, because their effect can be evaluated inside existing visibility-domain or LOFAR-style workflows. Segmentation and image-recovery networks are less mature. They remain mostly validated by simulations, and their test distribution can change substantially when beam errors, foreground complexity, calibration residuals, and missing-data patterns are included. For this branch of the literature, the most relevant benchmark is not how good the images look. It is whether downstream summaries and inferred parameters improve without hidden signal loss
(
Akeret et al. 2017
;
Acharya et al. 2024a
;
Acharya et al. 2024b
;
Bianco et al. 2024
;
Bianco et al. 2025
)
.

Therefore, observation-domain ML is both useful and risky. It is useful because it addresses practical bottlenecks which will otherwise limit SKA analysis. It is risky because every preprocessing choice changes the data used later for inference. The evaluation criteria should be signal preservation, residual-error calibration, and robustness to changed foreground or instrument conditions, not only visual quality.

0.4.2
Theory-domain methods

On the theory side, the main reason for using ML is repeated forward modeling. The question is not whether a network can recognize a pattern. The question is which expensive calculation can be replaced or accelerated without losing the relevant physics. In 21 cm cosmology, the most established answer is summary-statistic emulation.

An emulator replaces many calls to a forward model with a fast surrogate trained on a suite of simulations. Early studies used neural networks and Gaussian processes to interpolate the 21 cm power spectrum across astrophysical parameter space, allowing Bayesian exploration to proceed much faster than brute-force simulation
(
Kern et al. 2017
;
Schmit & Pritchard 2018
;
Jennings et al. 2019
)
. This is still one of the clearest uses of ML in the field. Its advantage is speed. Its limitation is also clear: an emulator of a weak summary cannot recover information that the summary has already discarded.

Global-signal emulation has become a clear subfield. The 21cmGEM emulator of
Cohen et al. 2020
was an early neural-network emulator of the global signal from cosmic dawn and reionization. The later
globalemu
framework used redshift as an input variable and gave a fast emulator for the sky-averaged signal and neutral-fraction history
(
Bevins et al. 2021
)
. Related work then explored different neural representations, including 21cmVAE, recurrent long short-term memory networks, and Kolmogorov–Arnold networks
(
Bye et al. 2022
;
Dorigo Jones et al. 2024
;
Dorigo Jones et al. 2025
)
. The lesson is simple but useful. The global signal is a one-dimensional curve, but it is not just a list of unrelated frequency channels. The order of the frequency or redshift bins contains physical information, so the architecture of the emulator can matter.

Emulation is also no longer limited to one observable.
Breitman et al. 2024
developed 21cmEMU to emulate several 21cmFAST summary observables, including the neutral fraction, power spectrum, spin temperature, global signal, UV luminosity functions, and CMB optical depth. Such tools are useful because real constraints do not usually come from one measurement alone. They also connect naturally to analyses of HERA power-spectrum limits, where forward modeling and statistical validation are already part of the interpretation of upper limits
(
Abdurashidova et al. 2023
)
.

A deeper issue is compression. The power spectrum is widely used because it is well defined, relatively low dimensional, and directly connected to current upper-limit analyses. However, when the 21 cm field becomes non-Gaussian, the choice of summary becomes part of the scientific model. One response is to enrich the handcrafted summary set, for example, by combining multiple redshifts, higher-order statistics, or morphology-sensitive quantities. Another response is to learn compact representations from simulated maps or light-cones, and then to test whether they improve parameter recovery. Recent studies comparing multiple summaries within a common inference framework suggest that neither traditional nor learned compression should be preferred in advance. The useful criterion is retained parameter information at fixed computational cost
(
Prelogović & Mesinger 2024
;
Semelin et al. 2025
)
.

This is why light-cone and map-based neural studies are important even when they are presented as inference papers. They show that morphology, redshift ordering, and non-Gaussian texture carry information beyond low-dimensional summaries
(
Gillet et al. 2019
;
Prelogović et al. 2022
;
Neutsch et al. 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2022a
;
Zhao et al. 2022b
)
. In this sense, they give theorists guidance about what should be emulated next. The target may not be only the power spectrum. It may be a representation which preserves the physically relevant structure of the field.

Fully field-level surrogates and generative field models remain less developed than power-spectrum emulators in EoR 21 cm work. This imbalance is an important point. Interpolating a low-dimensional observable is much easier than generating three-dimensional fields with correct topology, redshift evolution, and parameter dependence. Cross-domain image-to-image studies and related generative models indicate possible directions. However, they are not yet substitutes for physical simulators
(
Yoshiura et al. 2021
;
Moriwaki et al. 2020
;
Moriwaki et al. 2021a
;
Moriwaki & Yoshida 2021b
)
. Visual realism is not sufficient. A useful field-level surrogate must preserve non-Gaussian observables and must remain reliable when astrophysical parameters or conditioning variables move away from the training set.

Another promising, but still less mature, route is operator-level acceleration. Instead of replacing the whole simulator, a costly substep is emulated, or a residual correction around a physical approximation is learned. This is useful because it localizes the approximation and can make error accounting easier than in one monolithic generator. However, in 21 cm cosmology, this approach is still not as developed as whole-observable emulation. It should not be hidden under the broad phrase “ML surrogate modeling” without specifying what is actually emulated.

Therefore, uncertainty-aware emulation is essential. A fast mean prediction is not sufficient. Emulator error must be estimated and propagated into the final inference. Otherwise, the surrogate can produce constraints which are fast, precise, and biased. Recent work has started to quantify how emulator inaccuracy changes the recovered posterior, rather than treating emulator accuracy as only a root-mean-square error number
(
Bevins et al. 2025
)
. This point is important for both theory-domain and inference-domain applications. The safest theory-domain applications of ML are those where the physical forward model remains visible and the surrogate discrepancy is treated as part of the error budget.

0.4.3
Inference-domain methods

Inference-domain ML uses simulated observables for inferring astrophysical and cosmological parameters. A useful distinction is between deterministic models, which return parameters or labels directly, and probabilistic methods, which try to return a calibrated posterior or likelihood-related quantity.

The earliest 21 cm ML applications were mainly classifiers and regressors. These studies showed that maps and light-cones contain information beyond conventional two-point summaries. CNN classifiers could distinguish, for example, between AGN-driven and galaxy-driven reionization from morphology alone
(
Hassan et al. 2019
)
. Regression models then recovered astrophysical parameters from power spectra, images, and light-cones with encouraging accuracy in idealized settings
(
Shimabukuro & Semelin 2017
;
Gillet et al. 2019
;
Choudhury et al. 2022
)
. Later work extended this approach to contaminated light-cones, alternative architectures, and joint inference of astrophysics with dark-matter physics
(
Prelogović et al. 2022
;
Neutsch et al. 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2022a
;
Zhao et al. 2022b
;
Saxena et al. 2023
)
. Related artificial neural network studies showed that derived quantities such as bubble-size statistics and the global 21 cm signal can be reconstructed from other summaries
(
Shimabukuro et al. 2022
;
Shimabukuro 2025
)
.

Global-signal inference is a related but different problem. The data vector is low dimensional, but foreground and instrumental nuisance terms dominate the analysis
(
Chapman & Jelić 2019
;
2020PASP..132f2001L
)
. In this case, ML is more naturally used as a flexible emulator for the physical signal, as a learned model for nuisance terms, or as an amortized engine for joint inference over astrophysical and instrumental parameters
(
Cohen et al. 2020
;
Bevins et al. 2021
;
Bye et al. 2022
;
Dorigo Jones et al. 2024
;
Dorigo Jones et al. 2025
)
. The risk is that features learned from idealized training sets may look precise, although in real data they may be dominated by beam chromaticity, calibration drift, or foreground-model error.

Direct regression is still useful, but it has a clear limitation. A deterministic point estimate is not the same as a posterior. It can show that a data representation contains useful information, and it can serve as a fast compressor. However, it does not solve uncertainty quantification by itself. This limitation is one reason why simulation-based inference (SBI) and related likelihood-free approaches have become increasingly important
(
Prelogović & Mesinger 2024
;
Semelin et al. 2025
;
Alsing et al. 2019
;
Cranmer et al. 2020
)
. Depending on the method, SBI learns an approximation to the posterior, the likelihood, or a likelihood ratio from simulations. After training, the inference model can be reused for many mock or observed data vectors.

A typical SBI workflow draws parameters from a prior, runs the simulator, computes observables, and trains a conditional density estimator using the resulting parameter–data pairs. Neural posterior estimation, neural likelihood estimation, and neural ratio estimation differ in the object that they approximate. However, all of them can use flexible density models such as normalizing flows or autoregressive networks
(
Rezende & Mohamed 2015
;
Papamakarios et al. 2017
)
. Figure
5
shows this logic schematically for 21 cm cosmology.

Normalizing flows and related neural density estimators are useful when several data sets must be analysed together.
Bevins et al. 2024
combined SARAS3 global-signal information with HERA power-spectrum limits. More recent work used neural-density-estimation-accelerated Bayesian analysis to combine 21 cm power-spectrum upper limits with non-21 cm probes, including CMB and Lyman-line constraints
(
Sims et al. 2025
)
. These examples are important because they are closer to how the field will actually constrain the first galaxies. A final parameter constraint will probably not come from one summary statistic or from one telescope alone.

One strength of SBI is that it does not require choosing a single summary statistic in advance. It can condition on a power spectrum, several redshift slices, learned embeddings of maps or light-cones, or a combination of handcrafted and learned summaries. Recent work has shown that combining summaries within an SBI framework can tighten constraints relative to using one summary alone
(
Semelin et al. 2025
)
. This is expected because the 21 cm signal stores information across scale, redshift, and morphology, and no single low-dimensional summary is guaranteed to contain all of it.

Recent comparison studies also give a useful warning. Inference performance depends on the simulator, the summary representation, and the probabilistic estimator. A powerful density estimator cannot recover information removed by a poor summary. Also, a learned summary cannot correct a biased simulator. Therefore, comparisons based on common simulation databases shift attention away from architecture rankings and toward calibration, coverage, and robustness under shared test conditions
(
Mériot et al. 2025
;
Semelin et al. 2025
)
.

Therefore, direct regression and SBI should be seen as complementary rather than mutually exclusive. A neural network may first compress a map into informative features, and a probabilistic stage may then convert those features into calibrated constraints. The methodological question is not only whether ML beats the power spectrum. It is whether learned representations and probabilistic inference can be combined without sacrificing interpretability or uncertainty control.

Likelihood-free methods are also starting to move beyond the standard EoR power-spectrum problem. Recent 21 cm forest studies use learned summaries and latent encodings of noisy sightline spectra to recover information about thermal history and dark-matter physics more efficiently than power-spectrum-only approaches in low-SNR regimes
(
Sun et al. 2025
;
Patil et al. 2026
)
. This is a useful test case because the forest is not naturally described by a simple two-point statistic.

The main caveat is calibration. SBI can produce smooth and structured posteriors even if the simulator or the emulator is wrong. Therefore, recent SKA-oriented work treats simulator mismatch, domain shift, coverage failure, and emulator-induced posterior bias as central issues
(
Sooknunan et al. 2024
;
Bevins et al. 2025
)
. The necessary diagnostics include coverage tests, simulation-based calibration, posterior predictive checks, and cross-simulator stress tests
(
Talts et al. 2018
)
. Transfer-learning studies between idealized simulations and more realistic mock observations point to the same conclusion: performance often degrades first at the simulator–observation interface, not inside the density estimator itself
(
Neutsch et al. 2022
)
. For inference-domain ML, the serious question is therefore not whether a network can recover parameters from held-out simulations. The question is under which perturbations the resulting posteriors remain trustworthy.

0.4.4
Machine learning for the 21 cm forest

The 21 cm forest gives a different problem for ML from the usual EoR imaging or power-spectrum analysis. The data are basically one-dimensional spectra along the line of sight to bright background radio sources. The signal is a set of absorption features, not a three-dimensional diffuse map. This difference sounds small, but it changes the statistical problem. In forest studies, the important information can be in narrow troughs, in the distribution of optical depths, or in the correlation of absorption features along and between sightlines. Therefore, a method which is good for two-dimensional images is not automatically the best method for forest spectra.

One direct use of ML is feature finding. In a simple case, the task is to distinguish real absorption features from thermal noise and instrumental residuals. A conventional matched filter is a natural baseline, and any neural method should be compared with it. ML may still help because the shape of a forest spectrum is not always a sum of isolated lines. Absorption can be blended, the continuum can be uncertain, and the same sightline can include IGM absorption and absorption by denser structures. Convolutional networks, recurrent models, or transformer-like models can be trained to identify such patterns, but the training set must include realistic continuum errors and instrumental effects. Otherwise the network may only learn an ideal line catalogue.

A second use is compression. A forest spectrum can contain many frequency channels, while the number of physical parameters of interest is smaller. Learned latent variables, autoencoders, or scattering-type summaries can compress the spectrum before inference. This is useful when a direct likelihood for the full spectrum is not available. It is also useful when the signal-to-noise ratio is low and individual absorption lines are not robust. In that situation, the information may be statistical. It may be present in many weak features rather than in one clear detection. Recent work using learned summaries and latent encodings has shown that forest spectra can contain information about the thermal history and dark-matter physics which is not efficiently captured by a simple one-dimensional power spectrum
(
Sun et al. 2025
;
Patil et al. 2026
)
.

The 21 cm forest is also closely connected to small-scale physics. Warm dark matter, small-scale matter suppression, gas temperature, X-ray heating, and the abundance of small neutral structures can all affect the absorption pattern
(
Shimabukuro et al. 2014
;
Shimabukuro et al. 2020a
;
Shimabukuro et al. 2020b
;
2023PhRvD.107l3520S
;
Shimabukuro et al. 2025
)
. This makes the forest scientifically attractive, but it also makes the inference problem degenerate. For example, less small-scale structure and stronger heating can both reduce strong absorption features. A network trained only to regress one parameter may give a precise answer even when the spectrum is actually consistent with several physical explanations. Probabilistic inference is therefore more appropriate than point prediction.

Topological and morphology-based summaries are useful in this context because a forest spectrum is not only an amplitude field. It has a structure along frequency or comoving distance. The birth and merging of absorption troughs under a threshold change can be used to describe how absorption systems are organized. This kind of description is different from measuring only the variance. It is also relatively interpretable, because one can ask which troughs survive a persistence cut and which features disappear as noise. Recent applications of persistent homology to the 21 cm forest indicate that topological summaries can give information complementary to amplitude-based statistics, especially when thermal history and dark-matter effects are partially degenerate
(
Shimabukuro 2026
)
.

However, the most serious uncertainty for the 21 cm forest is not only the analysis method. It is the source population. The existence, number density, spectra, and variability of sufficiently bright high-redshift radio sources are still uncertain
(
Šoltinský et al. 2021
;
Šoltinský et al. 2025
)
. This point should not be hidden by ML. If the background source population is wrong in the simulation, a supervised model may learn a problem which the real survey does not have. For example, the network may be trained on spectra with smooth power-law continua, while real sources may have spectral curvature, intrinsic absorption, or time variability. In that case, good performance on mock spectra is not a proof of observational feasibility.

For this reason, ML for the 21 cm forest should be tested in two directions. The first direction is astrophysical variation. The training and test sets should vary the source continuum, source flux, gas temperature, ionizing background, X-ray heating, and small-scale structure. The second direction is observational variation. The same method should be tested with different noise levels, frequency resolution, flagging patterns, bandpass errors, and foreground residuals. A method which works only for one ideal observing setup is not yet a forest search strategy.

There is also a possible role for ML in survey design. If a catalogue of candidate high-redshift radio sources is available, ML can rank targets by expected forest detectability after combining source flux, spectral smoothness, redshift, observing frequency, and expected thermal noise. This is not the same as detecting the forest itself. It is a decision problem before observation. The danger is that the ranking will be dominated by assumptions about unknown source populations. Therefore, such a target-selection model should be used as a sensitivity study rather than as an automatic selection rule.

The 21 cm forest is therefore a useful example of both the promise and the risk of ML. The data are simple in dimension, but not simple in interpretation. ML can find weak and non-Gaussian patterns in spectra. It can also compress information more efficiently than a fixed power spectrum. But the method is only credible if it is tested against continuum uncertainty, source-population uncertainty, and simulator mismatch. For the forest, these uncertainties are not small corrections. They are part of the central scientific problem.

0.5
Summary

This chapter reviewed ML applications in 21 cm cosmology by considering where each method enters the analysis pipeline. Observation-domain methods are closest to direct operational use for tasks such as RFI mitigation, foreground and covariance modeling, calibration support, and morphology-aware reconstruction. Theory-domain methods are strongest when they accelerate a specified forward model, especially by emulating summary statistics. Inference-domain methods range from fast deterministic regressors to SBI frameworks which aim to return calibrated posteriors for non-Gaussian observables.

A repeated point is that the 21 cm signal should not be reduced too quickly to one statistic. The power spectrum remains a central observable, but the cosmic dawn and reionization fields contain non-Gaussian and morphological information. PDFs, higher-order statistics, topological summaries, wavelet-based summaries, and learned representations can therefore be useful complements. The 21 cm forest gives another example. Its one-dimensional spectra are simple in format, but they carry small-scale information and are affected by uncertain source populations. This makes ML attractive, but also makes validation more demanding.

The overall picture is mixed, and this point is important. The evidence is strongest for targeted ML components which have well-defined inputs, outputs, and validation tests. It is weaker for broad claims that end-to-end networks or generative models can already replace major parts of the physical analysis pipeline. For SKA-Low, the most defensible strategy is a hybrid strategy: ML should be used where it makes a difficult subproblem faster or better conditioned, but the physical model, uncertainty budget, and validation tests should remain visible. A learned compression or reconstruction is useful only if it preserves the information needed for inference and if its uncertainty remains calibrated under realistic changes in foregrounds, instruments, and simulations.
