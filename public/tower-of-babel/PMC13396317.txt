Formulation and characterization of nano biopesticides via nanosuspension technology and its packaging designs
Authors: XiaoPeng Niu, Yue Shen, Pei Wang, Kousar Rasheed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPesticides have long been essential in agriculture for controlling pests, diseases, and weeds; however, their extensive use has led to serious concerns regarding resistance development, environmental contamination, and human health risks. Nano-biopesticides have emerged as a sustainable alternative, offering enhanced efficacy, reduced toxicity, improved target specificity, and controlled release behaviour. These formulations are typically developed either by employing nanomaterials with intrinsic pesticidal activity or by encapsulating active ingredients within nanoscale carriers to improve stability, solubility, and bioavailability. This review focuses on the formulation and characterization of nano-biopesticides via nanosuspension technology, highlighting preparation methods, key components, and mechanisms of action. In addition, special emphasis is placed on the role of advanced packaging design in maintaining physicochemical stability, preventing degradation, and extending shelf-life through the use of biodegradable polymers, UV-protective materials, and smart controlled-release systems. Despite their potential, challenges related to toxicity, environmental fate, and regulatory frameworks remain. Further research is essential to ensure the safe and scalable application of nano-biopesticides in sustainable agriculture.Keywords: Green synthesis., Nanosuspensions, Nano-biopesticides., Eco-friendly., Packaging design., Smart delivery systems.

Introduction

The organophosphates, chlorinated hydrocarbons, carbamates, and urea derivatives, are pesticides used to control pests and prevent plant diseases [1]. These compounds are widely used in veterinary medicine, agriculture, and healthcare, and are dangerous to humans and animals by contact with the skin, inhalation, and assimilation, in addition to contaminating the environment. The wrong application of pesticides in agriculture may interfere with the antioxidant and oxidant enzyme activity in the human body, which may lead to the diseases caused by the oxidative stress [2].

The traditional compounds of pesticides have a number of weaknesses including high concentration of organic solvents, dust drift, low dispersibility, long soil life, and other similar weakness [3]. This limitation means that most of the applied pesticides are released to the atmosphere, while only a small portion is retained on the surfaces. Such low efficiency is also one of the causes of pollution. Therefore, it is necessary to reduce pesticides waste, minimize the costs of production, and decrease the environmental emissions and, at the same time, increase the lifespan of pesticides upon crop activities [4].

Other pest control researchers have focused on coming up with alternatives to synthetic chemicals which are not toxic to the management of pests and diseases. A possible alternative is biopesticides, which are also known as biological pesticides. Biopesticides are ‘naturally occurring substances, microorganisms, and plant-produced substances that control pests’ as per the definition provided by the US Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA) [5]. These naturally occurring compounds have been used in the place of chemical pesticides in the control of the plant pests. The advantages of using biopesticides as natural agents over the old pesticides are: reduced toxicity; specificity; extreme efficacy with little dosage; rapid degradation; also, low toxicity resulting in practically non-toxic emissions [6].

Application of nanotechnology into biopesticides has aroused controversy, with new properties to the traditional materials. Nano-biopesticides can be considered as a subset of biopesticides that incorporate nanomaterials to make them more effective. Nano-biopesticides are advanced biopesticides that utilize nanotechnology to design efficient delivery platforms for biopesticides. The unique characteristics of nanomaterials, such as their size, surface area, and reactivity, make them useful in pest control while minimizing their effects on non-target species. This evolution from traditional pesticides to nano-biopesticides represents a major advancement in the practice of pest management and agriculture [7].

It is clear that the use of nanomaterials in pesticides preparations has been occupying an unprecedented growth in the recent years. Two major classes of nano-biopesticide systems are carrier-based nanoformulations and intrinsically active nanomaterials. In carrier-based nanoformulations, nanostructures are employed as carriers to encapsulate or load pesticide active ingredients for enhanced stability, controlled release, and solubilizing properties to reduce environmental and non-target exposure [8]. Nanocarriers materials are likely to be used to ensure active ingredients (AIs) do not degrade and promote a regulated release, which are the most important aspects of the pesticide technology developments [9]. On the other hand, intrinsically active nanomaterials are used as the active agent for pest control in the form of a nanomaterial (for example, metal or metal oxide nanoparticles) that has inherent toxicity or inhibitory activity against the pest or pathogen [10]. These two methods extend the scope of nanotechnology in agriculture as a means of both improving the delivery of actives ingredients and using nanomaterials for direct pest control.

Biopesticides can be formulated using different precursors including microbes, fungi and plants. The one that based on botanic are made out of natural plant-based bioactive complexes. They provide a competitive advantage against artificial pesticides because they degrade very quickly and are environmentally friendly. Most of these include plant extracts and essential oils which have undergone a lot of scrutiny. They include different compounds that are toxic to a diverse species of insects [11].

Moringa oleifera is a plant that possesses numerous useful qualities and has the bio pesticidal potential. Its phytochemical compositions are tannins, flavonoids, alkaloids and saponins. It is worth noting that one of the triterpenoid compounds, saponins, exhibit antimicrobial, insecticidal, and nematocidal as well as antioxidant properties [12]. The use of Moringa oleifera as a biopesticide against a number of pests such as wheat berry aphids, moth larvae, yellow fever mosquitoes, flea beetles, spotted cucumber beetles, and melon fruit flies is possible [13].

Despite significant advances in nano-biopesticide formulation, limited attention has been given to their packaging and storage systems, which are equally critical for maintaining stability and efficacy during transportation and field application. Nanoformulations are highly sensitive to environmental factors such as light, temperature, humidity, and oxygen, which can lead to particle aggregation, degradation of active ingredients, and reduced bioactivity [14–16]. Recent research emphasizes the importance of functional packaging materials, including UV-protective coatings, oxygen-barrier films, and biodegradable polymer-based containers, to preserve nano-scale properties and ensure controlled release behaviour. Moreover, the integration of nanotechnology into packaging, often referred to as “nano-enabled packaging” has shown promising results in enhancing shelf-life, preventing contamination, and improving application efficiency in agricultural systems [17, 18].

Agrochemicals: a massive threat for biodiversity

The widespread use of agrochemicals during the present decades is possibly associated with soil deprivation, which is one of the serious impacts of conservative agriculture [19]. Agronomic practices were more environmentally friendly before the industrial revolution. The internal resources were also dependent on organic matter recycling and crop revolution, which allowed preservation of soil nutrients, biotic pest control, and development of buffer zones at the periphery of the farm. These methods of farming ensured a natural balance between farming and the environment leading to few cases of environmental destruction [20].

But with the advancement of agriculture, there was no understanding of the environmental principles and this disturbed the harmony between nature and agricultural. Although automation in agriculture has been seen to increase productivity, it has been associated with environmental degradation in most cases. Agrochemicals are used widely, and this leads to a decrease in the plant diversity in the agro-ecosystems [21]. The selective use of some plant species contributes to increased competition between plants, which changes accessibility to the soil and light in the same ecosystem.

Agrochemicals cause imbalances in nutrient availability to various plant species by increasing biomass. The presence of certain plants due to the promoted biomass denies sunlight to those plants that could not grow to the same height as well as reduces the nutrient supply [22]. To overcome these issues, there is a need to learn more about the ecological principles and move towards sustainable practices like organic farming that would reduce the negative impact of intensive agriculture on the environment. Balancing between more agricultural production and preserving the environment is one of the tasks that is of great importance to the contemporary agriculture.

Agrochemicals’ effects on climate changes

Environmental changes such as increase in temperature, shift in precipitation, and drought are directly related with alteration in the behaviour of the pests and pathogens, which finally affects the yield of crops. The combination of climate variables and the use of agrochemicals has a major impact on the populations of pests and their intensity. There is a higher dependence on pesticides, which may accidentally damage the valuable insect species. Secondly, pests and diseases vary, and this reduces the effectiveness of pesticides. The increase in herbicide use implies the development of weed resistance and decrease in the impact of herbicides [23].

Research in the U.S. and Korea [24] has indicated high volatility rates of organic chlorine pesticides in hot weather with the resultant air pollution. A past study in the U.S. also pointed to the impact of weather factors such as the temperature and the amount of rainfall on the rates of application and the amount of pesticides leached into the field. With increase in temperature, degradation of pesticides increases, although this can vary through drought conditions which inhibit the decomposition of pesticides. The interaction of the multiple aspects and their overall effect on pesticide leaching is still a complicated issue, which may be different in various parts of the world.

Practices such as introduction of new cultivar, change in land use and changes in practices in crop revolution can disrupt pesticide requirements. Moreover, an increase in temperature and humidity will increase the activity of pests and change the flora of the weed, which may increase the use of herbicides and pesticides [25].

These dynamics highlight the complexity of interplay between environmental changes, agricultural activities, and management of pests and the need to find adaptive measures in various geographical areas so as to deal with this predicament.

Agrochemicals impacting the environment

Agrochemical have been proved to provide negative impacts on the organisms and their environment. They pollute surface water, groundwater, and the atmosphere, decrease enzyme activity, decrease the population of useful insects and other winged creatures, reduce biodiversity, and increase soil salinity and contaminants [26]. The manufactured substances are capable of interfering with the ecosystem and disturbing the ecological balance. In sea water, artificial substances that include inorganic phosphates and nitrates help in eutrophication and specimen demise. The residues of pesticide in the soil, shallow water, and ground water are very dangerous to ecology and non-target organism.

The very important natural pollinators such as honeybees and butterflies are highly sensitive to pesticides. Not only do pesticides poison bees but they also cause species extinction that depends on the pollination of plants, especially via the Colony Collapse Disorder in which worker bees disappear abruptly in hives or colonies. The Honeybees, which are vital in pollination, are killed by the pesticides applied on crops during their flowering period [27]. The USDA and USFWS project their estimates to cost US farmers at least $210 million every year because of economical pollination in agriculture by pesticides. Pesticides have been seen to destroy about 1/5 of honeybee colonies in the America, and were seen to further destroy 16% in their effects [28].

Although agricultural chemicals provide a fast, easy and relatively cost-effective solution to regulatory pests, ailments and weeds, they are equally perilous to human health and the atmosphere [29]. Therefore, there is a need to seek and impart alternatives to the already available agrochemical solutions with an aim of protecting the atmosphere and human health.

Agrochemicals impacting the food

The retention of pesticide functional groups in crops and fruits depends on the interaction between the environmental conditions and the functional group of pesticides. The presence of physiochemical properties of both the pesticide molecules and the food itself plays a big role in the pesticide retention [30]. The composition of pesticides has a half-life ranging between hours and years implying their permanency in crop.

Pesticides are degraded by oxidation-reduction, metabolic, hydrolysis, and affected in change of pH and temperature. Such chemicals are used at various stages of fruits and vegetables germination, flowering, fruiting, and fruits after harvest resulting in different amounts of pesticide residues [31]. Some of them are left on the surface after application and others get into the flesh of the produce.

Investigations, such as [32] have indicated that the concentration of pesticides varies in fruits and vegetables; the concentration of pesticides in the epidermis than the flesh has been found to be higher. On the same note, the concentrations of residue vary among the outer layers of vegetables and the lower leaf surfaces. Fruits and vegetables can be washed with or special disinfectants to get rid of many of the pesticide residues present. Nevertheless, the effectiveness of the washing process can be decreased when some insecticides such as pyrethroids are present on the surfaces of fruits.

In cereal grains, continuous pesticide application is made to bran and germ layers and a minimal percentage of the lipophilic pesticides reach the germ and the bran layers. Pulses on the other hand are more likely to store more concentration of pesticides in their seed covers.

The different routes of application and properties of the pesticides highlight the complications of pesticide residue distribution in crops and why there is a need to understand these relationships in order to implement effective cleaning and management approaches [33].

Types of pesticides

The World Health Organization (WHO) draws on pesticides as a major threat to the atmosphere and the health of people. In turn, this phenomenon paves the way to the creation of novel pesticides that contain new natural and synthetic components. They are designed to be less toxic, specific to pests, work with reduced doses, and they break down faster than the conventional pesticides. The new pesticides are divided into two list such as biopesticides and nano-pesticides [34].

Biopesticides

Among pest-control solution is biopesticides which are products of microorganisms, plants, and animals. Though synthetic pesticide is quite effective in ensuring the protection of agricultural products, prolonged application of synthetic pesticides is associated with carcinogenicity, environmental persistence, and other negative effects. The need to deal with these problems required the emergence of new pesticides. A new and safer ecologically friendly alternative was introduced in the form of biopesticides [6].

Biopesticide applications can be traced back to the late 1800s when the use of fungal spores was used in the control of insect pests. Agostine Bassi confirmed in 1836 the protection abilities of spores of silver muscadine mould [9] (Beauveria bassiana) against giant silkworm. This was one of the first documented cases of the use of biopesticides. Biopesticides have continued to have a steady role in contemporary farming systems since, though on a smaller scale than the traditional practices of conservatism in crop safety. The main dissimilarity between biopesticides and synthetic pesticides is their mode of action. Although the majority of synthetic pesticides are mostly neurotoxic to pests, many biopesticides use alternative modes of action, such as anti-feeding, disruption of mating patterns, desiccation, and suffocation.

According to the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), there are 3 different categories of biopesticides, as reflected in Fig. 1, which include biochemical, microbial, and plant-incorporated protectants (PIPs).

Microbial pesticides

Another possible alternative to chemical insecticides is the biopesticides that are made by microorganisms including viruses, bacteria, yeast, fungi and protozoa. They are more targeted and their efficiency in fighting against pests differs across the species [35]. The pathogenicity of microbial pesticides to the target pest is triggered by the penetration of the pathogen in the skin or gut of the insect, which results in the development of the pathogen and subsequent death of the host insect.

There is an important role played by insecticidal toxins which are critical in the pathogenic process of these pathogens. The majority of the pollutants that are generated by bacterial pathogens are categorized as peptides, but their structure and toxicity levels may differ greatly.

Microbial pesticides are effective and safe, which benefits people and non-target organisms leaving little to no traces on foods. These pathogens provide ecological security by being able to reduce the natural pest fears thus promoting better biodiversity in the controlled ecologies. They are highly specific to a range of targeted pests and the favourable insects are able to proliferate in the presence of crops that have been preserved. This particularity has led to the adoption of microbial insecticides as biological agents of control within the past 3 decades [36].

Various microorganisms (bacteria, viruses, fungi or protozoa) are used as active ingredients in the control of different pests. Both of the active ingredients show some relative specificity to the target pest. An example would be the fungi that specialize in the control of the weeds and the ones that are specific to insects [37]. Indeed, Bacillus thuringiensis toxin may be useful against Aedes aegypti, but the sphaericus strain proves useful in contradiction to an array of insects, including Culex quinquefasciatus.

Biochemical pesticides

Biochemical pesticides are substances that are naturally found in the atmosphere, but which use non-toxic methods to deal with pests. As opposed to conventional pesticides which have direct effects and affect their targets by killing them, biochemical pesticides act indirectly. They interrupt the normal processes of their targets whereby in most cases they interfere with their sexual processes which is an example of their mechanism of action.

They have been derived using the natural plant-derived compounds such as terpene, phenolics, alkaloids and other secondary chemicals [38]. Pesticidal properties have also been demonstrated by some vegetable oils like the canola oil. This difference in mode of action defines the biochemical pesticides as an alternative method of managing pests as opposed to direct effects of the traditional pesticides.

Plant-incorporated protectants (PIPs)

PIPs or plant- incorporated protectants include pest-controlling substances synthesized by plants and the hereditary material that a plant requires to make its substances. The most common types of pesticides that are currently being used are artificial organic compounds, but with low molecular weights (LMWs) and usually lower than 501 g/mol or 0.51 kDa [39].

There has been massive research on the environmental and analytical chemistry of these synthetic pesticides and their potential effects on human and ecological health.

Even though synthetic LMW pesticides have dominated the market, biopesticides are increasingly becoming significant in the entire market of pesticides. Particularly, PIPs are biopesticides, which are declared in the tissues of the genetically modified (GM) yields, and which are designed to protect them against pests such as insects and viruses. As the use of insecticidal PIPs has become widespread across the globe and new kinds of PIPs are being developed against the insect pests, the centrifugal focus in this respect lies on the PIPs that are being developed to deal with the insects [40].

The insect pests that ingest transgenic crop tissues absorb PIPs, including the Cry proteins and double-stranded ribonucleic acid (dsRNA) which affect insect development or kill them in many ways. Cry protein PIPs interact with the particular receptors of epithelial cell of the insect midgut, which result in the development of transmembrane pores, cell lysis and eventual pest death [41].

Different Cry proteins are also specific in their structures and toxicity in relation to specific insect orders. On the example, Cry1 proteins are toxic to Lepidoptera such as the corn borer and Cry3 proteins to Coleoptera, such as the corn rootworm.

Nano‑pesticides

Nano-pesticides are the latest trend in the fight against pests that use engineered pesticide-carrying nanoparticles. These nanoparticles have been applied or can be applied in innovative methods of transporting pesticides. Nanosuspensions have emerged as a versatile and scalable delivery platform among the different nano-delivery systems studied extensively and will be discussed in this review. These formulations seek to enhance the performance of existing pesticides of active ingredients (AI) or to enhance its environmental protection performance, or both at the same time.

Advantages of nano pesticides

Nano pesticides are also coming in as a measure to counter the shortfalls of the conventional pesticides or to develop effective substitutes to agronomic applications. Studies [42] are also being conducted on the potential of nanotechnology in the application of insecticides with the aim of gaining insight into its future projections and trends.

NPts have a number of benefits on traditional pesticides. They are said to be more accurate in applications and thus result in a better crop and plant health a major milestone in the history of agriculture. To illustrate, the example of NPts is more targeting with the 32.7% efficacy and less poisonous toward non-target organisms with the 42.2% of conventional pesticides [43]. Also, they reduce unnecessary loss of active ingredients through premature release or environmental factors, and enhance leaf adhesion as well as crop production. In terms of these factors, NPts offer a favourable growth within the agricultural industry.

With the increase of the living standards, the security of the agricultural products and their quality in terms of nutrients becomes a crucial concern. There is also increased awareness of the risks associated with the use of traditional pesticides and the need to protect the environment [44]. This increased awareness underlines the dire need of agricultural developments that aim at inventing non-polluting and safe pesticides. NPts provide an effective opportunity to consider all these issues and meet the changing requirements of agricultural sustainability and safety.

In 2017, Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) [45] expanded the definition of biopesticides to include natural animal, bacterial, plant-derived or mineral-derived pesticides to control pests. One such instance is the antifungal component of Bacillus which inhibits the growth of manuport oryzae which also demonstrates the same level of performance like carbendazim. The fact that this strain can be used as per a biopesticide implies that the manuport oryzae may be controlled biologically using this strain.

Studies have been done on the incorporation of nanotechnology into biopesticides. Scientists are exploring the application of green and non-toxic nanomaterials to package pesticides, in order to produce eco-friendly biopesticides. The idea is to introduce a new solution of using non-toxic natural or synthetic nanoparticles in conjunction with biopesticides to form novel and environmentally-friendly formulations of pesticides. This is an initiative that is in line with the formulation of green, sustainable pest management in agriculture solutions [46] (Table 1).

Composition of nano pesticides

Nanoparticles (NPs) serving as active ingredients (AIs) carriers can be divided into 2 major groups [34]. This review emphasizes nanosuspension based formulations where an existing conventional formulation (i.e. artificial insecticide/fungicide) is converted into nano particles to enhance its performance. In this system, nanosuspensions act as carriers and aid the efficacy of the pesticide by enhancing its availability, adhesion to plant leaf surfaces, and controlled release at the target site. Carrier components like stabilizer, polymer and other additives merely play supportive role to the delivery of the formulation while no pesticidal activity is associated with themselves, unlike intrinsically active nanomaterials like metal and metal oxide nanoparticles which kill the pest because of their inherent toxicity and hence do not fall in the scope of this review.

In order to provide complete information, it is necessary to differentiate between intrinsically active nanomaterials like silver, copper, and zinc oxide nanoparticles and carrier based nanosuspensions. The intrinsically active nanomaterials act as pesticides themselves. Thus, the mode of action in case of intrinsically active nanomaterials is oxidative stress, toxicity due to toxic metal ion release, and physical damage to the cell membrane. Hence, it becomes clear that intrinsically active nanomaterials have a different mechanism than carrier based nanosuspensions, wherein the nanomaterial merely serves as a carrier of the active compound of the pesticide.

Active ingredients

In the context of the nanosuspension carriers, the active substance of the particular pesticide, either a synthesized chemical substance (insecticide/fungicide) or plant-based material, exists in a nano-scale solid state form. The other components that go into the formation of the carrier system consist of such elements as stabilizers, polymers, wetting agents, and surfactants, serve no other function except for that of aiding purposes. They work towards the stabilization of the particles, inhibit their agglomeration, help adhere to the leaf surface, enhance dispersal in the solution used for spraying, and control the release of the active substance. However, the action mechanism of the pesticide itself is not connected to the carrier elements [35].

Natural essential oils naturally present in plants, including cinnamon, eugenol,, thyme, eucalyptol and rosemary are frequently used in most organic products. These extracts have been proven to be effective as biopesticides in agriculture, and have toxicity in parasitic nematodes, bacteria, viral pathogens, fungi, and crop pests. Nevertheless, their physicological and chemical nature such as low solubility in water and solubility, restrict their application and usage.

These challenges can be overcome greatly by the addition of proper surfactants such as plant essential oils to these lipophilic compounds. They are dispersed into nanoemulsions under the aid of the surfactants which decreases interfacial tension, forms a protective coating around the drop of oil, and causes the droplets not to merge due to electrostatic repulsion. The end result of this process is the creation of stable water-in-oil (W/O) or oil-in-water (O/W) particles that are between 101 nm and 600 nm in diameter which are stable over a long period of time [56].

The surfactants can be classified into cationic, nonionic, anionic, and amphoteric. The use of nonionic surfactants that have superior physicochemical properties is very important in the process of nanoemulsion. They lead to controlled delivery, accuracy in targeting and less dependence on dosage in nano-pesticides as illustrated in Fig. 2. Some of the most common nonionic surfactants in nanoemulsion preparations include sorbitol ester, polysorbates (Tween 81), Triton X-100, polyoxyethylene castor oil ether, EL-20, alkyl glucosides, and so forth. These surfactants help to stabilize and make nanoemulsion-based formulations effective in agricultural use [57].

Stabilizers

Nanosuspensions do not exhibit good agglomeration, particularly with active pharmaceutical substances with high log P values and enthalpy. In response to this, steric hindrance, and electrostatic repulsion are mandatory to stabilization. Stabilizers are important in inhibiting the ripening of Ostwald since the stabilizers could cause the coalescence of the particles due to an increase in the surface energy in the absence of stabilizers.

The stabilizers in nanosuspensions are crucial and should be carefully selected to ensure the physical stability of the suspension as well as in- vivo appearance. Instead of using a single stabilizer, the mixture of stabilizers is usually desirable to provide the formation of a stable nanosuspension. Some stabilizers used in nanosuspension formulation are poloxamers, povidone, polysorbate, lecithin, and cellulose derivatives. All these stabilizers help keep the nanosuspension together and allow it to sustain the suspension [59].

Poloxamers

Poloxamers are FDA-approved as generally recognized as safe (GRAS) compounds applied in the parenteral, oral, and topical route. Mostly, popular stabilizers are poloxamer 188 and poloxamer 407. The continued stability of nanosuspension and reduction of the process of ripening of Ostwald considerably rely on variables such as hydrophilic-lipophilic ratio, functional groups, and polymer shape in this category [60].

Amino acid-based stabilizers

Stabilizers such as amino acid-based, such as leucine copolymer, in aqueous environments are added into the stable nanosuspensions. Lecithin, which survives the sterilizing procedures is a stabilizer in the formulation of parenteral nanosuspension. Albumin in a concentration of 0.003 to 5% helps in stabilizing the surface and targeting drugs in the production of nanosuspensions. Types of amino acids and stabilizers are: arginine, proline and transferrin [61].

Additional agents

Other agents have a role in stabilisation of nanosuspensions. Polyethylene glycol succinate which is a vitamin E derivative is low toxicity and outstanding physical stability, which can be used in ocular applications, parenteral and oral nanosuspensions. A BASF innovated stabilizer known as Soluplus is made of a copolymer of polyvinyl acetate, polyvinyl chloride and PEG which improves stability, bioavailability and dissolution of the planned nanosuspensions [62].

Common stabilizers include polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP), and PEGylated chitosan which have been known to enhance stability along with dissolution rates.

Surfactants and co-surfactants

The use of emulsion or microemulsion methods depends on the use of surfactants and co-surfactants. The phase behaviour and drug loading is independent of the surfactant-to-co-surfactant ratio. It is effectively utilized in the development of good nanosuspensions with the use of sodium dodecyl sulphate, Tweens, co-surfactants such as Transcutol, bile salts, Glycofurol, ethanol, dipotassium glycyrrhizinates, and isopropyl alcohol [63].

Organic solvent

Nanosuspensions are usually prepared using organic solvents by the precipitation technique. In the selection of these solvents, factors like toxicity, removability and availability is of great importance. The use of class III organic solvents such as alcohol, ethyl acetate, ethyl formate, butanol, ethyl ether, methyl acetate, triacetin, and methyl ethyl ketone are safer as they are perceived to have less risks on human health [64].

In the case of creation of nanosuspensions based on an emulsion as a template, water-miscible organic solvents are used. These solvents provide the formation of nanosuspensions with the help of an emulsion-based process and help to stabilize and make the process more efficient.

Other excipients

Indeed, β-cyclodextrin as a complexing agent is vital in enhancing bioavailability and dissolution rate of the active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) in nanosuspensions. Besides this agent, other excipients are used in a formulation depending on the needs of a given formulation and also based on the type, route and the physicochemical properties of the API.

Techniques for formulation of nanosuspension

Nanosuspension formulation has been realized in 2 main directions which include bottom-up and top-down technologies. An example of methods used in writing nanosuspensions has been discussed in Fig. 3 [65]. Some of these methods include marinization principles in media milling or jet milling that is usually used in the preparation of nanosuspension. There are a number of benefits and drawbacks of marinization. As an example, it increases the rate of dissolution of the drug, but it can affect the saturation solubility of the drug. Bottom-up technique

The bottom-up method of creating nanosuspensions is by creation of a supersaturated solution of the active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) and then the solution is transformed to nanosuspension through precipitation. This method, as the name suggests, begins at the molecular level, and proceeds to form particles in the nano scale.

This is a slow process of reducing the level of solvent in order to initiate development of the API to nanosized particles. Alternatively, the dissolution of the drug substance in solvent can also be performed and followed by the addition of an anti-solvent that would lead to the precipitation of the drug substance to nanosized particles. An anti-solvent is a liquid in which the drug substance does not dissolve [66].

The bottom-up method encompasses several methods that encompass solvent anti-solvent method, supercritical fluid method and emulsification-solvent evaporation method [67]. The methods rely on variations in the solvent-anti-solvent interaction or a carefully managed precipitation to get the sought nanosize structures of the final nanosuspension formulation.

Solvent-antisolvent method

The method described, known as the precipitation method, involves the precipitation of a solute by mixing an anti-solvent. Typically, a suitable solvent, often water, is used to dissolve the drug substance. An anti-solvent, miscible with the first solvent but insoluble with the drug substance, is then added in the presence of a surfactant. Rapid mixing of the anti-solvent and drug solution triggers rapid supersaturation of the drug in the mixture, as illustrated in Fig. 4.

Formulation of nanosuspension by the solvent-antisolvent method [59]

This process generates either amorphous or crystalline ultrafine API. It progresses through two main phases: nuclei formation followed by crystal growth.

In the case of nanosuspensions, high nuclei growth and low crystal growth is desired, to formulate the nanosuspensions. The two stages are sensitive to temperature, which requires optimization of the temperature to develop effective nanosuspension. The pros of this technique are that it is simple, scalable, cost effective, but suffers drawbacks: the drug must essentially dissolve in at least 1 solvent, and growth of crystals outside the nano-range must be evaluated by the means of a suitable solvent [68].

Supercritical fluid method

Supercritical fluid method entails the application of a fluid whose temperature and pressure are above normal values. The density of such a fluid is similar to that of liquids and gas diffusivity. The formation of nanosuspensions is possible by such techniques as Rapid Expansion of Supercritical Solution (RESS), Supercritical anti-solvent, and Precipitation with Compressed Anti-solvent (PCA). In the methods, the supercritical fluid holding the API in an appropriate solvent is sprayed through a jet, which forces the solvent to evaporate and deposits the API as nanoparticles.

The precipitation with compressed anti-solvent is a technique in which supercritical carbon dioxide is used to prepare nanoparticles. In this case, the drug solution is injected into a compressed chamber in which CO2 is put as a result of which a nanoprecipitation is achieved by the removal of the solvent. The advantages associated with PCA include the low use of organic solvents and ease of purifying the precipitated nanoparticles [69].

Top-down technique

The top-down approach is based on mass reduction strategy to decrease large particles into nanoscale particles. The top-down technique includes such techniques as media milling, high-pressure homogenization, and emulsification by melts and diffusion of emulsions.

Media milling

Media Milling was introduced by Liversidge et al. in 1992 and is patented by Nano Systems which is now with Elan drug delivery and it minimises particle size by high shear rates. The important element in this process is precise temperature control because the high shear rates may produce heat which may harm heat-sensitive drug substances and excipients. Media milling is done using equipment like the “High shear media mill” or the Pearl mill. The mill consists of a milling chamber, milling shaft and recirculation chamber. Reduction of particles in the pearl mill is through impaction, which basically reduces the particles to nanoscale. The relation between the mill and the recirculation chamber will enable production to be continuous and this will provide both batch and continuous production as illustrated in Fig. 5. The media mill can also be used to perform operations with a duration of 30 to 60 min with a reduction in particle size to below 200 nm [70].

It is the process that Skype Pharmaceutical is now patented on; initially discovered by R. H. Muller, the patent was first owned by DDS Gmbh. Three homogenizers are generally used in high-pressure homogenization namely, the APV Micron Lab 40, piston gap homogenizer, and the Stansted homogenizer.

High-pressure homogenization works under the pressures of 100 to 1500 in transforming particle size between the microns and nanoscale using collision process. The processing parameters are pressure and homogenization cycles that are strictly manipulated to get the optimal size of the particles in the nanosuspension. This has been done in two forms i.e. batch or continuous form with a capacity ranging between small volumes of only a few millilitres to big capacity of hundreds of litres. Moreover, the approach falls into different categories: Dissocubes, in which high-pressure homogenization is used in H2O; Nanopore, where high-pressure homogenization is used in non-aqueous media; and Nano edge, in which a combination of precipitation and high-pressure homogenization methods is used. Dissocubes

Indeed, the piston-gap high-pressure homogenizer is especially appropriate when it comes to the production of nanosuspensions of drugs insoluble in water. The drug and the surfactant are firstly prepared as a micro-sized suspension in an appropriate dispersion medium. The suspension is then forced through the small hole of valve of the homogenizer under high pressure.

The main concept of such a process is based on cavitation in water. The strong cavitation forces inside homogenizer are so strong to convert the particles that are microns to nanoparticles. The outstanding weakness of this process, however, is that the preparation of a micro suspension must be done prior to the feeding of the homogenizer and as such, an additional preparation process must be included to the process.

Characterization techniques of nanosuspensions

It is important to assess nano-materials, such as nanosuspension, in order to know their unique characteristics and uses. These evaluation techniques make it easy to compare formulations and help in coming up with optimized products. Nevertheless, all the techniques have their advantages and disadvantages, which provokes a problem in choosing the most appropriate methods of characterization. In order to conquer these obstacles, methods that are reproducible and reliable must be used. There are various evaluation techniques and they can also be utilized successfully to produce better products.

Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX)

Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) is a technique that is mostly applied to determine the elemental structure of substances. It has the principle of varying materials releasing varying X-rays in the process of interacting with electromagnetic radiations. The lower energy electrons are excited by focusing an obsessive beam of electrons or protons upon a sample and change to higher energy shells. The net effect of this movement is the creation of electron holes that are occupied by the electrons of higher energy thus releasing energy as X-rays. The emitted X-rays are measured using a spectrometer which gives the opportunity to estimate the elements in the sample by evaluating the energies of the X-rays emitted.

Nevertheless, EDX possesses certain drawbacks limiting its use in the sample characterization. The elements such as hydrogen, helium and lithium, do not have sufficient electrons to generate typical X-rays and hence they cannot be detected using the EDX analysis. Moreover, low spectral resolution is also a problem as it creates overlapping peaks in EDX spectra which are difficult to determine its actual elements [72].

Particle size analyzer

The size of particles in the nano-meter scale, especially of the nanosuspensions, is a very important aspect to determine. This is most preferred by particle size analyzers such as the Malvern zeta series. These are analysers with high sensitivity in the detection of small sizes of particles even at low levels of concentration based on the use of backscatter optics. A well-known method that tries to measure dispersed nanoparticles in nanosuspensions is known as Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS). But there is a problem of measuring the presence of aggregates in the sample. Such a restriction complicates the direct correlation of size fractions with a particular composition. Knowledge of the refractive index of the solvent is also another requirement of DLS. Moreover, in a situation where a sample is a mixture of nanoparticles, the research will prefer other refractive nanoparticles. Further, the intensity-based usual size of the particles may also be affected by the bigger or contaminating particles within the sample.

Zeta potential

Zeta potential is a useful parameter that is applied to measure the surface charge characteristics of dispersed nanoparticles in nanosuspensions. It is essential in the comprehension of the interaction of these particles in the biological settings and the electrostatic interactions between these particles and bioactive complexes. This possible is the voltage difference between the surface of solid particles in a conductive fluid and the material of the liquid, and is expressed in volts (V) or millivolts (mV). Zeta potential analysis can be used to predict stability of disseminated nanoparticles as regards to their colloidal stability or storing stability. It can also provide a clue into the type of material which is enclosed on the surface of the particle.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM)

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) is a high-resolution technique that is applied in the determination of particle sizes. It entails physical scanning of samples with probe tip in submicron size range. The technique gives topographical information that is constructed around the forces between the sample surface and the tip. Dependent on the characteristics of the sample analysis may be performed in contact or not contact mode. Contact mode involves pattering the probe on the surface of the sample in order to determine surface belongings, whereas in not contact mode the probe is raised above the conducting surface. This technique is especially beneficial in the characterisation of non-conductive samples such as polymeric nanomaterials and micro-crystals.

However, AFM has limitations. It is unable to scan images as fast as a Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) and can take some minutes to scan. The images may be affected by image artifacts and nonlinearities, hysteresis and creep of piezoelectric material. Also, AFM images reflect the contact of probe to the sample surface but not the actual sample surface.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) is a well-known technique of the analysis of particles and molecules of small size. It is useful in offering useful 3D information on structures acquired through X-ray diffraction. Under FTIR, the frequencies of vibrations of a complex can be predicted in certain regions depending on the nature of the atoms and chemical bonds present in it. The method is widely used in the characterization of biomolecules, and has become a potent method of evaluation of microbial samples, facilitated by inexpensive minicomputers and sophisticated procedures of determination of patterns and statistical analysis.

FTIR has also been known to be applied in the identification and differentiation of bacteria using infrared spectrophotometry. It has demonstrated great efficacy in the analysis of biological macromolecules, cells as well as tissues in the past decade. One shortcoming of FTIR is however that FTIR does not measure spectra as such but instead measures interferograms, which are often difficult to interpret unless they are converted into spectra. The equipment is expensive initially and in case of maintenance, this may also be a problem especially since the value of an FTIR spectrum diminishes more rapidly because of the wrong settings of the equipment unlike in a grating spectrum.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) is an effective technique of characterising nanosuspensions specially to study the morphology and surface characteristics of the particles. To perform the SEM analysis, the samples are dried into a dry form by taking the nanoparticles of the nanosuspension and straddling them on a conductive sample support (usually covered with a metal of similar composition such as gold) and are viewed in a high vacuum environment. SEM produces high-resolution images of a sample surface, and the details of the particles as thin as 5 nm can be observed. The electrons are emitting secondary electrons off the surface of the specimen, by a highly focused electron beam, which provides information about the surface structure of the specimen. This device operates quickly with the capability to perform Secondary Electron Detector (SED) and X-Ray Detector (EDS) analyses within a period of less than 5 min with high chances of providing digital data.

SEM is, however, a costly tool that needs maintenance through constant flow of cooling water and constant voltage to maintain the stability of its electromagnetic coils since, at its time of inception, coils were not invented with electromagnets. The use of the SEM and the sample preparation require specific training. In addition, SEM can only be applied to solid inorganic samples, which can fit in the vacuum chamber and can withstand the needed vacuum pressure. Although contact with radiation is minimally possible, the relevant tool does not include data on size distribution.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Another potent technique that is employed to characterize samples in high magnification is Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM). TEM does not work similarly to SEM but offers similar data. It has been used to image materials at the nano-meter scale making use of bright field and dark field modes. TEM is based on a fine electron beam passing through a small portion of the specimen which scatters and the electrons will spread through the object. It then employs the lenses to enlarge and project the image on a fluorescence screen to give high-quality images with details on the structure of the images.

TEM however, has drawbacks in terms of size, cost and time-consuming sample preparation such that it makes ultra-thin sections of the specimen. It gives black and white pictures, does not have colour and requires the same maintenance as SEM such as voltage and current control of the coils and circulation of cooling water. TEM can only be applied to samples that are electron-transparent and can withstand vacuum conditions and fit within the chamber. Handling of this instrument also requires specialized training.

Ultraviolet (UV) spectroscopy

The ultraviolet (UV) spectroscopy such as the Ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy is widely used in the evaluation of substances at the nano-range. It is based on the law of Lambert-Beer, which states that the intensity of the light ray is going to reduce in direct proportion to the thickness of the translucent medium, as the ray crosses through it.

UV spectroscopy is a simple and useful technique of characterization, especially of nanoparticles that exhibit optical characteristics that are sensitive to agglomeration, size, shape, concentration, and surface-related refractive index. It works by measuring the level of radiation reflected off the sample to that of radiation traversing a reference substance.

The UV spectroscopy however has drawbacks namely its wide spectrum, which can make it difficult to do specific chemical analysis of unknown sample with the method. It works best in the analysis of concentration in a low concentration range. Also, it takes a lot of time to initiate analyses.

Action mechanism of nanosuspension as biopesticides

Most of the benefits that result from the use of nanosuspensions in formulation can be attributed to the capability of the system to deliver, retain, and increase the availability of the active substance within the target site. This is based on improved physiochemical properties, controlled release rate, and high bioavailability. The reduction of pesticides to the nanometer range will result in an increase in the surface area of the substance, making the suspension capable of staying in suspension for a longer period without settling in the spray tank. It also facilitates easier wetting and spreadability on the surface of the leaves, especially on waxy leaves because of decreased interfacial tension.

Another very important property of the nanosuspensions is the controlled release. Stabilizers, including polymers and surfactants, are responsible for controlling the rate of dissolution or release of the active substance. Coatings based on the use of polymer materials contribute to the delay of the dissolution, causing an extended period of action of the drug and minimizing the loss of substances due to evaporation, which is crucial for the delivery of volatile substances such as essential oils. Controlled adhesion and desorption also enhance rain-fastness by preventing wash-off from plants. Therefore, nanosuspensions are generally effective in pest management applications using smaller amounts of active ingredients than their conventional counterparts, due to better coverage, less loss, and prolonged exposure to the active ingredient. It should be pointed out that this increased effectiveness is accomplished without altering the natural toxicological function of the pesticide because the nanosuspension only serves as a vehicle for the active ingredient.

Application of nanosuspension as biopesticides

Carbofuran nanosuspension

The nanosuspension of Carbofuran is an insecticide that has been produced with the aim of improving its efficacy for crop protection. This product is principally used for controlling economically important agricultural pests, including the diamondback moth (Plutella xylostella L.), which damages cruciferous crop areas particularly in cabbage. The Carbofuran nanosuspension formulation also provides effective control of soil and foliar insects. These nanosuspension produces extremely fine particles that allow for greater spreading on plant surfaces, increased contact with target insects, and improved uptake efficiency. Consequently, carbofuran nanosuspension provides a higher level of pest control at reduced application rates than conventionally used formulations, with 13.5% increase in efficacy against diamondback moth larvae. Carbofuran nanosuspension retains reasonable physical and chemical stability of almost 2 years throughout storage and field use, thereby ensuring that there is little differentiation of performance within or between storage [73].

Chlorantraniliprole nanosuspension

For controlling a major rice pest, the leaffolder (Cnaphalocrocis medinalis), chlorantraniliprole can be used in a nanosuspension. In this active ingredient, chlorantraniliprole, is formulated into very fine particles having average size of approximately 56 nm. Due to the small size of the particles, they offer improved performance in the field. Smaller particle sizes decrease dispersion within spray solutions, thus allowing for more uniform distribution of the product on plant surfaces. Additionally, this formulation improves the ability of the pesticide to adhere to leaves and to remain on the leaves (i.e., retention), so that more of the active ingredient will stay on the target foliage and not be lost to runoff or poor adhesion. The chlorantraniliprole nanosuspension will, therefore, provide equivalent to or greater control of the pest compared to traditional formulations, but with 40% less product being applied. Thus, the use of nanosuspensions for insecticides will improve the efficiency of application, improve the effectiveness in the field, and reduce the overall quantity of pesticide applied [74].

Lambda-cyhalothrin nanosuspension

The lamda-cyhalothrin nanosuspension is a type of nano-formulated pesticide that is used as a foliar application in controlling lepidopterous pests in agriculture. The lambda-cyhalothrin insecticide is created as very fine particulate matter to improve its distribution on the leaves of plants. In comparison to conventional emulsifiable concentrates (EC), the nanosuspension will have superior adhesion to the leaf surface therefore, more pesticide will stay on plants rather than being lost after application. It provides significantly increased resistance to wash-off from rain, thereby allowing it to maintain efficacy when applied in fields. In addition, because of their small size, the particles in the nanosuspension can make contact with and act on target pests much more rapidly, resulting in quicker knockdown times. Thus it increases the efficiency of pesticide [75].

Bacillus subtilis nanosuspension

The usage of Bacillus subtilis potato isolate has been studied as a biopesticide for controlling the bacterium Xanthomonas oryzae, responsible for bacterial leaf blight of rice. This biopesticide in nanosuspension form is modified to a very fine and stable liquid formulation that improves its application and distribution for superior contact with target pathogens under field conditions. This formulation has allowed for a more practical and stable form of delivery of this microbial biopesticides. Therefore, the results of this study demonstrate that nanosuspension formulations should be considered as potential delivery systems for microbial biopesticides, improving their viability and assisting in controlling plant diseases within agriculture [76].

Spinetoram nanosuspension

Spinosad nanosuspension has been developed to work against the diamondback moth (Plutella xylostella L.), which is a significant pest of brassica crops and may result in considerable yield losses. The active ingredient is ground to a particle size of less than 100 nanometers via advanced milling, improving its efficacy compared to standard formulations. The nanosuspension also increases the insecticide’s ability to spread out and adhere to the leaf surface and, as a result, allow for more effective attachment to and penetration of the plant tissue. The increased interaction between the insecticide and leaf reduces run-off losses due to poor wettability, which is a frequent limitation of other formulations. As a result, spinetoram nanosuspension exhibit significantly greater toxicity against the target pest larvae, even at substantially lower active ingredient concentrations. This increases the overall efficiency of the formulation and its potential impact on the environment [77].

In conclusion, nanosuspension of biopesticides formulations are capable of improving its delivery, increasing biopesticides performance, efficacy, reducing the amount of dosage required and decreasing the potential for environmental contamination. Thus they present a viable option for sustainable pest management.

Packaging design of nano-biopesticides

Importance of packaging in nano-biopesticides

Packaging is a crucial yet often overlooked component in the development of nano-biopesticides. Unlike conventional formulations, nano-sized particles possess high surface energy, making them prone to aggregation, sedimentation, and physicochemical instability during storage. Improper packaging can result in loss of nano-characteristics, reduced bioavailability, and diminished pesticidal efficacy. Therefore, packaging systems must be designed to maintain colloidal stability, prevent contamination, and protect formulations from environmental stressors such as UV radiation, oxidation, and temperature fluctuations [78, 79].

Types of packaging materials

Polymeric materials such as polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP), and polyethylene terephthalate (PET) are widely used due to their chemical resistance and mechanical strength. However, advanced biodegradable polymers like polylactic acid (PLA), polycaprolactone (PCL), and chitosan are gaining attention for sustainable nano-biopesticide packaging. These materials provide controlled permeability, reduced environmental impact, and compatibility with nan formulations. Similarly, another class of biopolymer-based packaging derived from natural sources (e.g., starch, cellulose, chitosan) offers eco-friendly alternatives [80]. Chitosan-based films, for instance, exhibit antimicrobial properties and can stabilize nano-biopesticides by forming protective matrices around active ingredients [81].

Smart and nano-enabled packaging

Emerging technologies involve “smart packaging” systems that respond to environmental stimuli such as pH, temperature, or humidity. These systems can regulate the release of nano-biopesticides in response to field conditions. Nano-sensors embedded in packaging materials can also monitor degradation or contamination, offering real-time quality control [82].

Despite of these advancements, challenges remain in scaling up nano-enabled packaging systems due to cost, regulatory concerns, and environmental safety. Future research should focus on developing cost-effective biodegradable packaging materials, standardizing storage conditions, and evaluating long-term environmental impacts.

Factors effecting the field performance of biopesticides and nano-biopesticides

Sprayability

The efficiency of biopesticides is significantly affected by application technologies and spray factors which play a crucial role in ensuring that biopesticides efficiently control pests. Improved technologies in biopesticides application, such as controlled droplet application, variable rate technology, intelligent spraying technology, sensor technology, air-assisted sprayers, robotics, automatic targeting technology, thermal fogging, and aerial spraying, have significantly improved the efficiency of biopesticides. Improved technologies have enhanced droplet size uniformity and velocity, which has improved the efficiency of biopesticides by deposition of droplets on target surfaces while minimizing drift to non-target surfaces. Precision and automation also allow for real-time detection of pests, hence ensuring that biopesticides are targeted correctly [5]. Overall, improved spray technologies increase efficiency in coverage, reduce biopesticides needed, minimize costs of application, and reduce environmental impacts, thus playing a key role in optimizing biopesticides’ performance in the field [83].

Soil and water conditions

The performance of nanopesticides is affected by the behaviour of the nanoparticles in the soil and water. The nanopesticides do not dissolve instead, they remain as small particles. They may aggregate and adhere to other organic matter as the organic matter can cause the nanopesticides to aggregate and adhere to each other or remain constant. The nanopesticides can also get coated by the biological matter that effect their interaction with soil and water. When the nanopesticides aggregate and adhere to each other, they become denser and settle faster. This makes them travel less distance. The nanopesticides can also be affected by the water composition. The composition may include factors like calcium and magnesium. They can cause the nanopesticides to aggregate and adhere to each other [84].

Moreover, if the soil is not fully wet, the soil particles may be stuck in certain spaces which may prevent nanobiopesticides from moving freely. This means the nanopesticides may remain stuck in one place. In addition, the soil may not be evenly distributed; therefore, the nanopesticides may react differently. This may cause them to be distributed unevenly. Moreover, the composition of the nanopesticides may influence their performance. Some nanopesticides may be stuck to the soil due to their composition. In other cases, the nanopesticides may move freely through the soil. It implies that the performance of nanopesticides is not constant, rather it depends on the environment [85].

Climate condition and retention ability

Environmental stability and retention of pesticide is an important factor to determine its efficiency. Environmental instability can occur as a result of the degradation of biopesticides via transformation to inactive metabolites, immobilization of pesticides in soils or dispersion into aquatic systems damaging the availability and performance of biopesticides for their respective targets. Moreover, climatic conditions including temperature, relative humidity, and UV radiation have a dramatic impact on the rate of breakdown of biopesticides, particularly with regard to microbial and plant based biopesticides [86].

Retention of biopesticides on plant surfaces is critical for effective pest control because many biopesticides can be easily degraded by environmental factors, such as sun and heat degradation and washed off by rain or irrigation. Factors that affect retention of biopesticides on foliage include the cuticle thickness, waxiness, and surface texture of the plant [87]. Designing suitable formulations is essential to overcoming these issues and enhancing their performance and retention on leaves. Stable formulations help to maintain biopesticides activity over the course of their intended use. Use of surfactants and stickers to improve adhesion to plant surfaces can reduce wash-off losses from rain or irrigation. The use of advanced technology, such as nano-encapsulation will further enhance the stability of active compounds and allow them to be released slowly and continuously over time. In addition, the use of stabilizing agents, including UV protectants and antioxidants will greatly benefit the longevity of biopesticides in the field [88].

Persistence of pesticides

Persistence is another critical aspect of biopesticides. Biopesticides must typically remain viable for several days to allow for microbial colonization, metabolic activation, and interaction with plant tissues or the rhizosphere, therefore, if they do not remain active long enough, they will not be effective. The amount of the applied product that actually reaches the target site is affected, to some degree, by the environmental conditions to which it is exposed like availability in water, soil movement and runoff/drift. As the scale of application grows, the importance of factors such as dispersal behaviour, ecosystem interactions, and negative effects on non-target organisms also increases [89].

Photo-degradation

Sunlight, especially UV Radiation, has a significant impact on the effectiveness of biopesticides because UV radiation can break down their active ingredients rapidly. Many biopesticides are very sensitive to light, so if the biopesticide does not have proper protection from light shortly after application, they can lose their efficacy. Most of the nanobiopesticides have overcome this problem because of Nanotechnology is being applied to them. Various types of nanomaterials e.g., ceramic, metal oxides, carbon based graphene, carbon nanotubes, carbon quantum dots, MoS₂ can be selected to either shield biopesticides from UV exposure or to control their light response. The presence of these nanomaterials can slow the rate at which biopesticides degrade due to exposure to UV light hence, increasing their period to remain active. Other engineering techniques such as surface modifications may also enhance biopesticide performance by lowering the amount of their lost due to UV radiation degradation. The overall importance of reducing photo-degradation is to enhance the efficacy of biopesticides, thus formation of nanobiopesticides represent viable means of enhancing their stability and longevity [90].

Volatilization

Volatilization leads to a decrease in the activity of biopesticides due to the active ingredients being lost through evaporation rather retaining on plants or in the soil. This is especially critical for volatile biopesticides like plant essential oils (PEOs) that are commonly utilized as bioinsecticides and biofungicides. Volatilization decreases their efficacy and leads to decreased field longevity. The rate of volatilization can be accelerated by environmental conditions, including elevated temperatures, low humidity, and wind velocity, immediately following application of the biopesticides [91].

Several formulation strategies are used to improve the stability and retention of biopesticides and one effective approach is to encapsulate biopesticides within materials like polymers or nano carriers. These materials assist in controlling the evaporation rate of the active ingredient by regulating when and how fast the active ingredient is released from the matrix into the environment over time. The controlled release of active ingredients from encapsulated biopesticides reduces the amount lost to volatilization therefore their biological activity last longer [92].

Leaching

Leaching is the process whereby pesticides, including biopesticides and nano-biopesticides, can lose their effectiveness of pest control by leaching the active substances away from the target area of application by transporting them downwards in the soil. As the pesticide product leaches further into the soil, it has decreased availability at the root zone or soil surface, which results in decreased effectiveness for controlling pests. Leaching of biopesticides from the target zone is more likely to occur with water-soluble products and also occurs more frequently in agricultural soils with high water permeability, little organic material, or acidic or alkaline environments. Therefore, active substances from pesticide products will often not effectively control target pest populations because they have leached into greater depths of the soil profile or into groundwater, leading to a reduction in the overall biological effectiveness of the biopesticides [93].

Additionally, the rain conditions and other modifying agents may reduce the ability of the soil to bind to the pesticides making the pesticide ingredients very mobile and allowing them to move downward more rapidly. Because of these limitations, the use of nanotechnology-based formulations such as nanocapsules e.g., chitosan based systems has been employed to provide slow release of active ingredients in soil. These types of systems hold the biopesticide in the area it was applied for longer periods of time thus reducing amount lost due to leaching and increasing overall efficiency by maintaining sufficient concentrations needed for pest control where needed [94].

Environmental impact of biopesticides and nano biopesticides

Nano biopesticides generally exhibit lower environmental persistence and reduced toxicity to non-target organisms due to their natural origin and rapid biodegradability. Nanobiopesticides further enhance environmental safety through controlled release and targeted delivery, minimizing chemical runoff and ecological contamination, although their long-term environmental fate still requires careful evaluation. Conclusion

Nanosuspension-based nano-biopesticides offer a promising approach for sustainable agriculture by improving solubility, stability, target specificity, and controlled release of active ingredients. This review highlights the fundamental aspects of formulation and characterization of nano-biopesticides, including top-down and bottom-up fabrication approaches, selection of stabilizers and excipients, and their role in enhancing stability, efficacy, and controlled release behavior. The integration of nanotechnology into biopesticide systems significantly improves target specificity, reduces required dosages, and minimizes environmental impact. Importantly, the role of packaging design has emerged as a critical factor in preserving the physicochemical stability and functional performance of nano-biopesticides. Advanced packaging systems based on biodegradable polymers, UV-protective materials, and smart delivery platforms can effectively prevent degradation, aggregation, and premature release of active ingredients, thereby extending shelf-life and ensuring consistent field performance. Despite these advancements, challenges related to large-scale production, cost-effectiveness, regulatory approval, and comprehensive assessment of environmental and toxicological impacts remain. Therefore, future research should focus on the development of eco-friendly, scalable formulation and packaging strategies, along with standardized safety evaluations, to facilitate the practical implementation of nano-biopesticides in sustainable agriculture.
