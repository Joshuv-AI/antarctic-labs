 ABSTRACT
Small and medium-sized commercial buildings (SMCBs) are most of the US commercial building stock,
yet most have no automation system and run on standalone thermostats and fixed schedules. This report
documents the design and laboratory validation of a low-cost building energy management system (BEMS)
developed at Oak Ridge National Laboratory and configured for an occupied medical clinic in Yuma,
Arizona. The BEMS replaces the standalone thermostats on 11 single-stage heat-pump rooftop units, adds
circuit-level power metering, and is configured rather than hard-coded for each building. The complete
device set—11 thermostats, 5 power meters, and network equipment—was validated in the laboratory.
Every configured device returned its data points; all 879 remote commands were confirmed by reading
the values back at the device; occupant changes at a keypad paused and released automated control; and
each commanded change energized the correct control output. The configuration is written once per
device type, and the effort grows with the variety of equipment rather than with the number of devices.
Additionally, on-site installation needs only local electricians and HVAC technicians. Collectively, these
make the BEMS deployment practical for SMCBs that conventional systems cannot serve affordably.

 1. INTRODUCTION
1.1 BACKGROUND
Commercial buildings account for approximately 20% of total energy consumption in the United States,
and about 87% of them are small and medium-sized commercial buildings (SMCBs) (U.S. Energy
Information Administration 2021). More than 85% of SMCBs have no building automation system (BAS)
to coordinate equipment (Trenbath et al. 2022; Maisha, Trenbath, and Meyer 2023). These buildings are
typically conditioned by packaged rooftop units (RTUs), and each unit has its own standalone, hardwired
thermostat, programmed with a fixed schedule and manually adjusted (Kim and Braun 2018). As a
result, these buildings run their HVAC systems longer than necessary—set back poorly or not at all when
unoccupied—and have little or no ability to respond to grid conditions during the demand peak period.
These shortcomings stem from the absence of automated control rather than from the equipment itself
(Vandenbogaerde, Verbeke, and Audenaert 2023). Studies have shown that managing an RTU’s heating
and cooling set points, schedules, setbacks, and optimal start times can address the 20%–25% excess
energy use that a lack of proper energy management causes in these buildings (Katipamula, Lutes, and
Huang 2023), while also enabling demand response to lower operating costs and ease peak demand
on the grid (Nutaro et al. 2015; Kim and Braun 2022). However, capturing this opportunity at scale is
constrained by cost and by system integration effort: conventional solutions are engineered per building,
require separated configuration across multiple vendor-specific tools, and cost about $56/m2 ($5.20/ft2 ) to
establish, with roughly half of it for labor (Png et al. 2019; Guo et al. 2025). A low-cost, interoperable, and
user-centric building energy management system (BEMS) is therefore needed to make advanced control
affordable and enable building-to-grid integration in SMCBs.
To address this gap, Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) has developed an open-source, scalable,
configuration-driven BEMS for SMCBs. It is built on VOLTTRON, the open-source distributed, agent-based,
sensing-and-control middleware developed under US Department of Energy (DOE) sponsorship (Katipamula
et al. 2016), which provides the message bus, device drivers, and agent execution environment. The
deployment approach follows the supervisory controller kit framework that ORNL developed with
Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) for SMCBs (Katipamula, Lutes, and Huang 2023). A
supervisory computer, retrofitted into a building with no BAS, hosts the centralized control applications
and communicates with field devices over industry-standard protocols, specifically BACnet and Modbus.

 1

The kit is designed to be low-cost, interoperable across equipment vendors, operable without engineering
expertise, and able to deliver energy savings and grid service (Goodman et al. 2023).
Building on the development, this project aims to apply that BEMS to a real, occupied office building
rather than to a representative case, validating it against that building’s own equipment and configuration.
As a preliminary study, this work demonstrates the feasibility of the system design for this project building
without substantial installation cost and effort.

1.2 PROJECT OBJECTIVE AND SCOPE
This Cooperative Research and Development Agreement (CRADA) project advances the mission of
DOE’s Building Technologies Office (BTO), which is to accelerate the adoption of cost-effective,
high-performing, and demand-flexible buildings among the commercial buildings hardest to reach: the
SMCBs, which lack automation systems that advanced control requires (Cruz Rios et al. 2023). The
project aims to accelerate the transition from a laboratory demonstration to real-world adoption across
SMCBs by applying an automation and advanced control capability already developed at ORNL through
a real estate partner that owns small medical office buildings. The wide adoption of the proposed approach
would result in a 20%–25% reduction in the more than 10,000 trillion Btu of source energy these buildings
consume nationally (Goodman et al. 2023; Katipamula, Lutes, and Huang 2023).
This report documents the design and full-scale laboratory validation of the developed BEMS as configured
for the CRADA partner’s small commercial medical building in Yuma, Arizona, including required
thermostats, power meters, network devices, and computer. Specifically, it describes the project building
and its existing controls, the system’s hardware and software architecture, the full-scale laboratory
integration and validation performed at ORNL, and the evidence the system operates as intended, from
the building control application to the equipment terminals.

 2. BUILDING AND OPERATIONAL CONTEXT
2.1 BUILDING DESCRIPTION
The building selected in this project is a small commercial medical clinic in Yuma, Arizona. The
single-story outpatient facility of approximately 14,000 ft2 was constructed in 2013 and is operated by
a regional healthcare provider. The project building is located in a hot, dry desert climate (ASHRAE
climate zone 2B) and is therefore cooling-dominated for most of the year, with peak electrical demand
set by compressor operation in the packaged rooftop units. Those 11 units are the building’s principal
controllable electrical load.
The building is fully occupied during operating hours, Monday through Friday from 8:00 a.m. to 6:00 p.m.
It is therefore occupied for 50 of the 168 hours in a week and unoccupied for the other 118, or 70% of the
week. The longest unoccupied period is 62 hours, from Friday evening to Monday morning. This typical
business-hours schedule offers a significant energy-saving opportunity through a setback control strategy
that requires no new mechanical equipment, only the ability to change a set point schedule across all 11
zones and to confirm each change was applied at the devices. Two constraints shaped how the system could
be designed. The first is occupant comfort: the clinic’s set point schedule, set by the building manager,
has to keep the building comfortable throughout operating hours, but comfort preferences may differ from
occupant to occupant, so a manual adjustment made at a thermostat has to be allowed to stand. The second
is security: the field devices have to operate on an isolated local network, separate from the public network
and out of reach of building occupants. Both are reflected in the system design described in Section 3.
Table 1 lists the characteristics of the project building and its existing equipment and controls. The
building is representative of the SMCBs this project targets: its floor area is under 50,000 ft2 , and its
heating and cooling are provided by packaged rooftop equipment under local thermostatic control. Like
more than 85% of SMCBs, it has no supervisory control layer of any kind. The detailed system design
described in Section 3 was therefore made for the typical SMCB rather than for this site alone.

2.2 EXISTING HVAC AND CONTROLS
The project building is conditioned by 11 Trane single-stage heat-pump rooftop units (RTUs), one per
zone, ranging from 3 to 5 nominal tons and totaling 48 tons. Each unit delivers supply and return air to
its zone and provides manual outside-air control. The units are single-stage, so each provides a single level
of heating and cooling capacity; there is no variable capacity or variable-speed fan control to modulate.
The 11 units are distributed across 5 electrical rooms; each room carries the electrical panels of 2–3 units
(Section 3.1). Figure 1 shows the schematic single-story floor plan of the building, with the thermostats
and RTUs serving each zone.
The RTUs are controlled locally by standalone, hardwired programmable zone-sensor thermostats
(Trane BAYSENS019C), each requiring a five-conductor control wire to its unit. The building has no
supervisory controller or BAS: the units are operated on fixed local schedules and set points, with no
centralized monitoring, no remote adjustment, and no capability to coordinate across zones or respond to
grid conditions. Any schedule or set point change requires a person to walk to each of the 11 thermostats
in turn, which is why systematic setback control for energy savings is difficult to sustain in this type of
building.
Therefore, per-unit local control with no visibility, no remote access, and no coordination is what the
BEMS is designed to address in this project.

 3. SYSTEM DESIGN AND ARCHITECTURE
This section describes the system as designed for the selected small commercial building. The main
purposes of this system design are to replace the existing standalone thermostats with networked thermostats
and to add zone-level electrical power meters, both coordinated by the BEMS, which provides centralized
monitoring and remote control of all 11 RTUs. The system is organized in three layers, each discussed in
the following sections: the field devices that sense and actuate (Section 3.1); the two networks the system
communicates over, one inside the building for the field devices and one cellular for access from off site
(Section 3.2); and the containerized supervisory software that acquires data, applies control, and serves a
user interface (Section 3.3). Figure 2 shows the equipment in each layer and the wired and wireless links
that connect them.

3.2 COMMUNICATIONS AND REMOTE ACCESS
The system communicates with field devices over an industry-standard communication protocol, BACnet/IP.
Each thermostat and each power meter is reached at a static address on the local network. Which objects
the BEMS reads and writes at each device are set by the configuration authored for the building: each
device’s address, the voltage and sensor channels each metered circuit is wired to, the object instance of
every point, and, for the thermostat points, the object type and whether the point is written. Inside the
software, the drivers publish and subscribe on the middleware message bus.
Internet connectivity is supplied by a cellular Wi-Fi router with a Verizon subscriber identity module
(SIM) card inserted for service on a commercial cellular network. The BEMS has its own path to the
internet and does not use the building’s network. That independence is deliberate: the building’s network
is not a route into the BEMS, and an outage on it cannot stop the BEMS. The router holds a static address
and forwards a single inbound port to the laptop. Remote access from off site ends there.
The BEMS runs on two networks, and keeping them apart is the principal security measure. Both are
shown in Figure 2. The cellular network is the one the cellular router provides, and it carries the only
connection to the internet. The device network, addressed as 192.168.68.0/24, is the one the mesh
system carries. The thermostats and power meters join this device network over Wi-Fi, and the laptop
joins it by wire. Wireless access requires a WPA2-PSK key. The network does not broadcast its SSID and

 7

therefore does not appear in a scan for nearby Wi-Fi networks. Nothing on the internet can reach the field
devices.
The laptop is the only host on both networks, and it reaches each over its own wired Ethernet connection
rather than over Wi-Fi. Two physical links keep the remote-access path and the field-device path separate,
giving each a connection with reliability that does not depend on wireless coverage inside the building.
The mesh system takes its connection from the laptop rather than from the cellular router. The device
network has no path to the public network except through the laptop; thus, the laptop is the boundary
between the two networks.
Because the software runs on the laptop rather than in a cloud service, a loss of the cellular link does not
stop the system. The thermostats and power meters remain reachable on the device network, schedules
and setbacks continue to be applied, and measurements continue to be recorded. Only remote access from
off site is lost, and only until the link returns. If control depended on that link, the system would stop
applying schedules and setbacks whenever the cellular connection failed, and no one at the building would
be watching for it.

3.3 MONITORING AND CONTROL SOFTWARE
The BEMS runs on the open-source middleware, VOLTTRON, and is deployed as a set of Docker
containers with dedicated driver containers for each device type (Schneider thermostats and eGauge power
meters). Each software component runs in its own container together with the required libraries. Through
configuration-driven deployment, minimal effort and implementation time are needed to install the
complete software on the laptop. Alongside the device drivers, the developed BEMS includes a message
bus, a historian time-series database, and a web server and client that present a graphical user interface
for operators (Chung, Huang, Lian, et al. 2026; Chung, Huang, Liu, et al. 2026). Figure 4 shows how the
containers interact with each other and connect to the field devices.

 Figure 4. Software architecture of the developed building energy management system.

3.3.1 Configuration-Driven Deployment
The BEMS is adapted to a building by writing configuration files, not by changing system code. Figure 5
shows the configuration each driver reads and what that driver connects to. A deployment is described in
two kinds of information, both read by the drivers at startup:
 • Device information lists the devices installed and how each one is reached. For each of the 11
 thermostats, the configuration file records the network address and the BACnet device instance. For
 each of the 11 zone circuits, it records the address of the power meter serving that circuit (5 meters
 in all) and the voltage and sensor channels the circuit is wired to.

 8

 • Point information specifies what data the BEMS exchanges with each kind of device. For the
 thermostat, it covers 12 points for room conditions, set points, and operating mode, nine of which
 the BEMS writes. For the power meter, it covers registers for line voltages and per-circuit sensor
 channels.
Through configuration-driven deployment, the required effort and time do not grow as devices are added
because one set of point information covers each device type rather than each installed device. The
same software can serve another building with the same device types. The devices are reached over the
industry-standard BACnet/IP protocol rather than over a vendor-specific one. Adding a rooftop unit,
renaming a zone, or changing which points are exchanged is an edit to a configuration file, not to system
code.

 management system.

3.3.2 Control Application and Occupant Override Handling
The control application implemented for this phase of the project performs data acquisition and remote
schedule and set point (setback) control: it continuously collects thermostat and meter data, applies the
occupied and unoccupied setback schedule to every zone automatically, and allows that schedule and its set
points to be adjusted remotely.
Because the project building is occupied during operating hours, occupant-override detection was
developed for the BEMS. It identifies a manual adjustment made at the thermostat and gives that setting
priority. Each accepted supervisory command is recorded as a per-zone baseline (heating and cooling set
points, system mode, and fan mode). The BEMS compares the set points it reads from each thermostat
against that baseline and infers a manual override when one of them differs from the baseline by more
than a deadband (1◦ F by default), or when the system mode or fan mode changes. On detecting an
override, automated control to that zone is suspended for a pause period (90 min by default), during which
BEMS commands to the zone are dropped and logged so that the occupant’s setting stands. When the
pause expires, automated control resumes, and the next scheduled write restores the scheduled set point.
Commanded set points are additionally clamped to the range supported by the device (55◦ F–90◦ F), so that
a mistaken command cannot drive the rooftop units to an extreme set point. The paused state is exposed
through the interface so that operators can see which zones are currently under occupant control. Figure 6

 9

illustrates the concept of the occupant-override detection, including the deadband and the pause period for
one zone over time.

 Figure 6. Concept diagram of occupant-override detection.

Giving the occupant’s setting priority is a deliberate choice, not a shortcoming. Comfort preferences differ
from person to person and from zone to zone, and a centralized system for an occupied building has to
accommodate that for practical implementation (Hong et al. 2017). The pause lets an occupant’s manual
adjustment stand for a bounded period, after which the schedule resumes.

 4. LABORATORY VALIDATION METHOD
This section describes the test environment and the test procedure. The system was installed and validated
at full scale in the laboratory at ORNL so that what remained to be done on site would be mounting,
wiring, and powering equipment whose behavior had already been demonstrated.

4.1 LABORATORY TEST BED
The test bed was prepared at the operating scale of the deployment: a cellular router, a mesh Wi-Fi node,
11 thermostats, and 5 power meters, configured from the device and point information specified for the
project building, as shown in Figure 7. Testing at full scale runs that configuration at its operating size,
which could reveal problems of device communication and data acquisition.
As designed in Section 3.1, the laptop ran the containerized software components and polled each device at
a static address on the local network through its configured BACnet/IP driver (Appendix A). The cellular
router and Wi-Fi mesh node provide the building’s independent internet path to enable remote access
from off site. The rooftop units, the CT sensors, the uninterruptible power supply, and the remaining mesh
Wi-Fi nodes were not part of the test bed. A thermostat’s real output is a set of switched control signals
to equipment absent in the laboratory. To close this gap, thermostat control wiring was terminated on
a display board with an indicator for each of the five outputs: auxiliary heat, dehumidification, heating,
cooling, and fan (Figure 7). Connected to one thermostat at a time, it showed which output a commanded
change energized, confirming that the thermostat drove the correct terminal, not just that it accepted the
command.

4.2 TEST PROCEDURE
Three tests were conducted on September 3, 2026: data acquisition, remote set point and mode control,
and occupant override.
 1. Test A (device communication and data acquisition). Every configured device was polled through
 its device driver at a 2 min interval for 6 h without intervention, using the building’s own device
 and point information, with no device-by-device changes. The outcome and duration of every poll
 were logged. A device counted as communicating only when the poll returned its configured points;
 connecting without returning them was considered as a failure with every device on one subnet. The
 test also exercised the addressing scheme against collision and contention. The setback schedule ran
 throughout so that the schedule-based control was observed over the same period rather than as a
 separate exercise.
 2. Test B (remote set point and mode control). Heating set point, cooling set point, system mode and
 fan mode were written to all 11 thermostats over 20 cycles, each carrying all four command types.
 Every write was followed by a logged read of the device, and a write counted as confirmed only
 when the read-back matched the command. The display board (Figure 7) indicated which of the five
 outputs was energized. The thermostat had to energize the expected output and no other—and switch
 the output off when the command was withdrawn. The logged read showed what the thermostat
 reported; the board showed what reached the terminals.
 3. Test C (occupant-override handling). Set point, system-mode, and fan-mode changes were
 made by hand at the thermostat keypad. Ten cases were defined in advance, each with its expected
 outcome recorded before it was run. They follow the parameters of the override handling described
 in Section 3.3.2, and each group varies one thing: the size of the change, the kind of change, or its

5.3 OCCUPANT OVERRIDE HANDLING
The occupant-override handling test was conducted against the detection and pause logic described in
Section 3.3.2, configured for these tests with a 60 min pause period. A set point change made by hand at
a thermostat, larger than the 1◦ F deadband, paused automated control of that zone. A change of system
mode or fan mode also paused it, with no deadband applying. Figure 11 shows one recorded event, case
C4a. The override cases were run against a constant set point rather than against the two-period schedule
of Section 5.2, so the pause behavior could be observed without a scheduled transition within the pause
period. The dashed line is the set point the schedule commanded, 74◦ F throughout; the solid line is the
set point read back from the thermostat. The occupant raised the set point at the thermostat to 76◦ F, the
change was detected 1.2 s later, and automated control of the zone stopped for the configured 60 min.
Twelve set point commands with 75◦ F were sent to the zone during the pause and were refused; none
was delivered, and each is marked with a cross in the figure. At 8:15 a.m., the pause expired, the next set
point command was delivered, and the zone went to 75◦ F. That is marked with a filled circle in the figure.
The setback schedule wrote the zone back to 74.0◦ F 21 min later. What happens at the end of the pause
matches Section 3.3.2 in two respects: the pause logic stops refusing at the instant the pause period ends
because the next write reached the device, and it does not itself restore the set point because the zone held
the test’s value until the schedule wrote again.

 6. DISCUSSION
Most SMCBs have little automation and are run by standalone thermostats on fixed schedules that nobody
can change without walking to each one in turn. A centralized BEMS that would hold a setback schedule
across an SMCB already exists; what blocks it is the per-building engineering cost that building owners
cannot afford. The system reported here answers that cost: commercially available devices, open-source
software, a single small computer rather than dedicated controls hardware, and a streamlined deployment.
The whole system takes 78 authored entries, which the drivers expand into 352 device-instance bindings
at start-up. At full building scale—11 thermostats and 5 power meters—the system reached every device,
confirmed all 879 commands at the device, held and released an occupant override, and drove the correct
equipment output.
A few limitations bound what this report claims. This report does not quantify energy consumption,
peak demand, or savings, and no finding here supports the energy saving that the BEMS could achieve
at the project building. Field M&V requires the system to run in the occupied building against its
prior fixed-schedule baseline, and that comparison does not exist because the system was not installed.
Installation was not performed because the CRADA partner’s business plans for the building changed
during the period of performance. One further gap belongs to the equipment: because the test bed had no
rooftop units, the testing could not demonstrate whether a rooftop unit accepts conventional thermostat
control in place of its serial zone sensor. Converting any rooftop unit on site would have shown that.
This project delivers a validated system, a configuration, a documented installation procedure, and
guidance for applying the approach to another SMCB. The full device set was assembled and validated at
ORNL, and the software was running on a local computer reachable from off site. The procedure requires
HVAC technicians to replace the zone sensor at each rooftop unit, a local electrical contractor to install
the five power meters, and ORNL staff to start the software. No part of the procedure requires a technical
specialist to integrate control systems, and a typical building owner could procure the work locally. The
proposed approach suits packaged rooftop units under single-stage heat-pump control, one per zone,
with standalone thermostats and no BAS. Four practices would change on a different building: assign
every device a unique BACnet device instance because discovery cannot distinguish devices that share
one; establish the electrical service before the meter registry is written because the channel names must
match the electrical wiring the building actually has; assemble the full device complement before any site
visit because a defect in remote thermostat control appeared only at full scale; and check how a candidate
thermostat accepts BEMS commands because an occupant must still be able to change the set point at the
wall.
Although this project has no field result, it produced the three major outcomes an M&V study for SMCBs
most needs and can least afford: the zone-level metering, the confirmed write path, and the archived
configuration that can be applied again. Beyond this project building, the collaboration aims for the
CRADA partner to apply the approach across its other similar building properties without the laboratory
repeating this work. Applying it to an occupied building, with updated configurations, is the step this
report leaves open. Extending the validated control to peak-demand strategies, and adding a full range of
installation costs to the replication guidance, would support the low-cost and energy-saving claims with
evidence rather than only design intent.

 7. CONCLUSIONS
The main purpose of this project is to demonstrate the feasibility of a low-cost BEMS deployment and
bring advanced control strategies to SMCBs at a lower cost to configure and install. We developed that
system and configured it for an occupied medical clinic in Yuma, Arizona, replacing the standalone
thermostats on 11 single-stage heat-pump rooftop units and adding circuit-level electrical power metering.
The complete equipment set for the project building was then assembled and validated in the laboratory.
Every configured device was polled throughout the run, and no poll that returned took longer than 6 s;
all 879 remote commands issued across 4 command types were verified by reading the values back
from the device; a manual override at a thermostat keypad stopped automated control of that zone and
released it when the pause expired; and each commanded change energized the correct terminal on a wired
display board for the rooftop unit. These outcomes describe the system the project building would have
run because the devices, their network addresses, and their sensor or control point information were all
specified for that building.
The value of the project’s outcomes lies in what it costs to reproduce them. A deployment is described
once per equipment type rather than once per installed unit; the description a person writes therefore
grows with the variety of equipment in a building and not with its size. The physical work can be done
by licensed electricians and HVAC technicians that a typical building owner can hire locally. A later
effort would begin with the metering and the verified control path already in place, which is where most
of the cost of an M&V study in such a building falls. Because the CRADA partner’s business plans
for the building changed, the on-site installation was cancelled, and the field M&V that a full system
demonstration requires could not be carried out. For the next steps, the same approach should be applied
to another SMCB, with its own configuration description. Measured and verified energy savings, together
with installation costs, would turn a validated design into a complete system demonstration.
