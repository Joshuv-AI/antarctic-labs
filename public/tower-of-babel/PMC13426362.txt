Hitting the bullseye: PSMA-targeted small molecule drug conjugate for prostate cancer treatment
Authors: Dhanashree Murugan, Loganathan Rangasamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) is a significantly overexpressed receptor in prostate cancer, making it an ideal target. Hence, targeted delivery of a payload via a small molecule to PSMA would facilitate localized cytotoxicity, thereby eradicating the tumor. Herein, we describe a small-molecule drug conjugate (DUPA-SS-Exa, 10) comprising 2-[3-(1,3-dicarboxy propyl)-ureido]pentanedioic acid (DUPA), a PSMA binding ligand, conjugated to exatecan (8) payload via a glutathione-sensitive disulfide traceless linker. Exatecan (8) has been extensively delivered via antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) and has shown favourable results. The DUPA-SS-Exa (10) conjugate was computationally evaluated to assess PSMA binding affinity in a dynamic environment, and ΔGbind was found to be −115.85 ± 4.52 kcal mol−1. An in vitro cell cytotoxicity assay performed against the PSMA-positive LNCaP cell line showed an IC50 of 117.01 ± 6.67 nM for conjugate (10). DUPA-SS-Exa (10) demonstrated high specificity, showing negligible cytotoxicity against PSMA-negative PC3 cells and non-cancerous HEK293 cells. Furthermore, DUPA-SS-Exa (10) is stable at acidic pH, in PBS, and in serum. Beyond its stability, the conjugate proved significantly safer than free exatecan (8) in B16F10 melanoma models, while exatecan (8) caused severe toxicity, leading to significant weight loss and mortality in mice models. These results establish DUPA-SS-Exa (10) as a stable, highly specific, and potent therapeutic strategy for PSMA-expressing tumors with a favorable safety profile.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is the 2nd most frequently diagnosed cancer in India as well as worldwide.1,2 The incidence of prostate cancer usually occurs in men over the age of 50 years. Unfortunately, 43% of prostate cancers were diagnosed at the metastatic stage.1 The standardized 5-year overall survival rate was 35%.3 Hence, it is necessary to improve existing treatment strategies and identify novel approaches.

One of the innovative strategies that delivers the drugs precisely to the desired target site of action is Targeted Drug Delivery (TDD). With this approach, the drug is released at the site without damaging surrounding non-cancerous tissues. Because of this strategy, these drugs were developed to remain structurally and chemically inert throughout their circulation.4 These features enable TDD to be superior to systemic drug delivery approaches. TDD administration improves the therapeutic window, decreases adverse effects, lowers dosage, and enhances therapeutic potential.4,5

One approach to targeted drug delivery is to engineer a ligand that binds to overexpressed extracellular receptors. This ligand will be conjugated to the potent cytotoxic drug; hence, it delivers the attached cytotoxic agent to receptor-overexpressing tumor cells. Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) is one of the receptors that is overexpressed in prostate cancer.6 PSMA is a transmembrane glutamate carboxypeptidase II (GCPII). It has an extracellular domain, a short transmembrane domain, and a cytoplasmic tail. One of the roles of PSMA is to enable folate and glutamate uptake and assist in their metabolism.7 The physiological function of PSMA remains unexplored; however, it has been observed to be involved in various biochemical pathways, including the androgen receptor, DNA damage response, and PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathways.7 Hence, it is presumed that PSMA plays a significant role in cancer-related angiogenesis, extracellular matrix degradation, tumor invasion, and integrin signal transduction.7,8 Previously, it was believed that PSMA expression is higher only in prostate cancer.9 However, various clinical case reports reveal that the expression is also found in glioblastoma,10 salivary gland cancer,11 thyroid cancer,12 breast cancer,13 lung cancer,14 hepatocellular cancer,15 and renal cell carcinoma.16 Hence, PSMA can be an excellent biomarker for targeting various types of cancer.

PSMA-based therapies are being extensively researched and studied in various modalities such as PSMA-based small-molecule drug conjugates (SMDCs) for treatment, PSMA-based near-infrared fluorescent imaging agents for fluorescence-guided surgery (FGS), PSMA-based radioimaging agents for positron emission tomography (PET) or single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) applications,17 PSMA-based CAR T cell applications,18 and PSMA-based gene delivery applications.19 FDA-approved DUPA-conjugated lutetium 177Lu radiotherapeutic agent (PLUVICTO) to treat metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer, and it shows 49% tumor shrinkage and/or disappearance.20 Similarly, Locametz is also an FDA-approved DUPA-conjugated gallium 68Ga radiodiagnostic agent that is used for PET scanning in suspected prostate cancer patients.20,21

PSMA-based SMDCs have provided promising results and have the potential to replace conventional cancer therapies. Some PSMA-based SMDCs, along with their IC50 values, are shown in Fig. 1.21–24 From these conjugates, EC1167 has completed the phase 1 clinical trial.25 Many researchers have used dipeptide-containing, glycosidase-cleavable, or phosphatase-cleavable linkers to deliver various cytotoxic payloads, such as mertansine (DM1),26 camptothecin derivative indotecan,27 duocarmycin 28, paclitaxel,29 MMAE,30 and docetaxel.31

The highlighted conjugate is the target candidate under investigation in this study.21–24

Similarly, OTL78 is a PSMA-targeted NIR imaging agent that is currently in phase 2a clinical trials. This conjugate enables real-time imaging during robot-assisted radical prostatectomy, providing critical insights into the location and extent of prostate cancer.32 Various PSMA-based radiotracers, such as [18F]FDCFyL, [18F]PSMA-1007, [18F]PSMA-JK-7, [68Ga]PSMA, [177Lu]PSMA-617, and [99mTc]PSMA, provide an innovative diagnostic approach for monitoring prostate cancer recurrences in patients.17 Apart from diagnosis and monitoring, PET/CT scan using [18F]PSMA-1007 shows high accuracy in primary prostate cancer staging and lymph node staging in the case of lymph node metastatic prostate cancer.33 Alzubi J et al. reported that a combination of PSMA-directed CAR T cells and low-dose docetaxel showed complete tumor regression in prostate cancer xenograft models.18

Encouraged by the above research findings, we have designed a bioconjugate using the DUPA ligand (2-[3-(1,3-dicarboxypropyl)-ureido] pentanedioic acid) to deliver exatecan (8). The DUPA-conjugated exatecan (10) cytotoxic payload comprises three components. The first is the DUPA ligand, which selectively binds only to the PSMA. The S–S cleavable linker is cleaved at the intracellular glutathione concentration within the endolysosomal complex, enabling the delivery of cytotoxic agents into the cytoplasm. The exatecan (8) is a camptothecin derivative that inhibits the function of topoisomerase I. The synthesis, biological evaluation, and preclinical studies in the tumor model are described below.

DUPA-SS-Exa (10) showed a binding affinity score of −9.95 kcal mol−1. The conjugate is bound to PSMA in the tunnel such that (10) forms a bond with Zn metal ions at one end, and the other end (exatecan) is exposed to solvent‐exposed regions. Similar to DUPA-SS-Exa (10) docking, other PSMA-targeting SMDCs docked with PSMA (PDB: 9HLW) (Fig. 3 and Table 1). From the observation, it is understood that the most essential binding is the Zn metal coordination bond, which provides greater stability between PSMA and the conjugates. Apart from the Zn metal bond, strong hydrogen bonds in the S1′ pocket anchoring play a major role in the stability (Table 1). These interactions are observed in all conjugates; however, the DUPA-SS-Exa (10) and PSMA_Abi conjugates show exceptionally high binding affinities, i.e., −10.3 and −12.2 kcal mol−1, respectively. One of the reasons for such high stability is extensive π-cloud stabilization through geometric interlocking between the ligand and the hydrophobic and aromatic pockets of PSMA (Fig. 3 and Table 1). Initial in silico docking trials of the high-molecular-weight PSMA-targeted drug conjugates CT2274 and EC1169 (Fig. 1) using AutoDock Vina yielded physically unrealistic positive binding affinities. This thermodynamic anomaly was attributed to a severe combinatorial explosion of the conformational search space, driven by the presence of more than 32 active rotatable bonds (TORSDOF > 32). Under these unconstrained conditions, the standard docking algorithm introduced significant steric strain, non-physical structural distortions, and an inability to resolve a physically meaningful docked pose for CT2274 and EC1169.

Molecular docking of various PSMA-targeting SMDCs with PSMA (PDB ID: 9HLW).

Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulation was performed to evaluate the dynamic stability and binding of DUPA-SS-Exa (10) with PSMA. The structural integrity of the complex was monitored via Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD), which revealed two distinct stability phases (Fig. 4A). Phase 1 (0–16 ns) indicates an initial equilibration period, during which the PSMA-conjugate (10) complex remained stable, with minor structural fluctuations around an average RMSD of 2.0 Å. The second phase shows a significant structural conformational change (a peak jump after 16 ns). After 16.5 ns, the system reaches a new plateau, indicating a new stability following the conformational change, probably due to rearrangement of the Zn coordination. This type of rearrangement is known as the induced-fit mechanism, in which the receptor undergoes localized structural changes to optimize its fit with the ligand. The Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) was employed to assess residue-level flexibility. According to the RMSF, peaks show pronounced fluctuations, indicating that these protein residues are highly flexible. The residues above 0.3 nm fluctuation were Glu78, Gly80, Arg135, Phe280, Ser281, Phe431, Glu656, Val659, and Ala690 (Fig. 4B). All these residues were loop bends, which are exposed to water molecules, and these amino acids were not involved in the binding pocket. Hence, none of these fluctuating residues were involved in ligand binding. The residues between 313–380 and 500–530, which are in the core protein, show high stability with fluctuations less than 0.1 nm. Finally, the intermolecular stability between PSMA and conjugate (10) was validated by hydrogen-bond analysis (Fig. 4C).

In silico analysis of DUPA-SS-Exa (10) interaction with PSMA by MD simulation. (A) RMSD, (B) RMSF, (C) number of H-bonds formed, and (D) potential energy of the interaction.

The conjugate (10) interacts with PSMA on average through 5 hydrogen bonds. The high H-bond occupancy indicates that polar interactions act as a driving force for stabilizing the conjugate (10) within the binding pocket. DUPA-SS-Exa (10) has 3 carboxylic acid groups and 2 secondary amines, which form polar interactions with neighbouring atoms on the receptor. The binding free energy was calculated by extracting 200 frames from the production trajectory using the gmx MMPBSA tool. The ΔGbind was evaluated by considering ΔGVDW (van der Waals interactions), ΔGEEL (Electrostatic energy), ΔGEGB (Polar solvation energy), and ΔGESURF (non-polar solvation energy). The average ΔGbind was −115.85 ± 4.52 kcal mol−1. Even though a structural transition occurred at 16 ns in the RMSD graph, the hydrogen-bonding network and binding affinity remained consistent throughout the 20 ns, indicating a high stability of DUPA-SS-Exa (10) binding with the PSMA (Fig. 4C and D).

ADME properties prediction for DUPA-SS-Exa (10)

To predict the ADME properties of DUPA-SS-Exa (10), the ADMETlab 3.0 online tool was used. DUPA-SS-Exa (10) exhibited low intestinal absorption (HIA) and lower Caco-2/MDCK permeability. This highlights that the conjugate is suitable for intravenous delivery rather than the oral route. Additionally, high plasma protein binding (97.34%), low unbound fraction (Fu = 1.73%), and low volume of distribution (VDss = −0.841 L kg−1) restrict off-target tissue accumulation by keeping the conjugate securely sequestered within the blood. It is also predicted to be impermeable to the BBB, and it displays an inert nature when interacting with the cytochrome P450 enzymes, which significantly eliminates the risk of CNS toxicities and metabolic drug–drug interactions. A steady clearance rate (CLplasma = 0.904 mL min−1 kg−1) and a favorable elimination half-life (T1/2 = 2.33 h) were observed. From the safety perspective, the conjugate (10) showed lower hERG cardiotoxicity and was negative for Ames mutagenicity. Furthermore, it high probability of p53 pathway activation, indicating exatecan's mode of action (Table 2).

Synthesis and characterization of DUPA conjugates

DUPA precursor was synthesized using the previously reported protocol by Sengupta et al.37 (Fig. 5). To synthesize the DUPA-SS-Exa (10) conjugate, the DUPA precursor was first converted to DUPA-cysteine (4) by solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) to obtain the DUPA-SH group (Fig. S1). The chromatogram showed that the synthesized DUPA-cysteine (4) had a purity of 96.33% (Fig. S2 and S3). Furthermore, the nitro-pyridyldisulfanyl compound was generated using 2-(pyridine-2-disulfyl)ethan-1-ol (5) and 4-nitrophenyl carbonochloride (6) to obtain the 4-nitrophenyl 2-(pyridin-2-yldisulfanyl)ethyl carbonate (7).38 This synthesized compound was evaluated using 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and HRMS (Fig. S4–S6). The linker was further reacted with exatecan mesylate (8) to obtain a nitro-pyridyldisulfanyl-activated exatecan (9), which readily attaches to the SH group of DUPA-cysteine (4). The DUPA-SS-Exa (10) conjugate was evaluated using HRMS (Fig. S7). The final conjugate was purified and analyzed to obtain pure DUPA-SS-Exa (10) conjugate (Fig. S8). This disulfide-based chemistry is crucial for exatecan (8) to be released from the bioconjugate. This linker acts as a traceless, cleavable linker, enabling unhindered release of exatecan (8) inside tumor cells.38

Schematic representation of DUPA-conjugated cleavable exatecan (DUPA-SS-Exa) (10).

Furthermore, DUPA-glucosamine (DUPA-glu) (12) was synthesized by the formation of the amide bond between DUPA ligand and glucosamine in the presence of EDC (Fig. S9). DUPA-glu (12) was purified using semi-preparative HPLC with a Refractive Index Detector (RID). The analytical chromatogram shows a single refractive index peak at 2.307 min, corresponding to DUPA-glu (12) (Fig. S10). The purified DUPA-glu (12) was evaluated using HRMS (Fig. S11).

DUPA-FITC (16) was synthesized using the BOC-protected DUPA precursor (2) and ethylenediamine (13) to form DUPA-EDA (14) (Fig. S12 and S13). Later, DUPA-EDA was conjugated with FITC isocyanate (15) to obtain DUPA-FITC (16). DUPA-FITC (16) was purified by RP-HPLC and characterized by HRMS (Fig. S14 and S15). This conjugate (16) will be used for internalization studies and to determine binding affinity in LNCaP cells.

Stability of DUPA-SS-Exa conjugate (10)

The stability of the conjugate (10) was evaluated under two different conditions, i.e., at pH 4.5 and in the presence of FBS (50%). It is essential to determine whether the conjugate (10) is stable at pH 4.5 (an acidic pH in the tumor microenvironment). Under acidic conditions, no significant difference was observed until 24 h of treatment with DUPA-SS-Exa (10) or exatecan (8). After 24 h of incubation, the DUPA-SS-Exa (10) conjugate showed peak splitting, which may be due to the release of exatecan (8). The peak percentage of DUPA-SS-Exa (10) was 84.30% (Fig. 6A). The decrease in stability may be due to the presence of acids that can oxidize the disulfide bond, reducing it to thiol groups.39

Graph illustrating the stability of the DUPA-SS-Exa (10) and exatecan (8) (A) at pH 4.5, (B) in the presence of 50% of FBS, and (C) at pH 7.2 under different time intervals.

Another important aspect is determining whether the conjugate will withstand the enzymes and proteins present in serum during circulation under physiological conditions. These assays revealed that the conjugate DUPA-SS-Exa (10) is more stable than the unconjugated exatecan (8). No significant difference in stability was observed until 6 h of interaction with FBS; however, some degradation occurred after 12 h of interaction with DUPA-SS-Exa (10). At 12 h, 67.45% of the conjugate was present, while 51.05% of exatecan (8) was present intact. At 24 h, 29.85% of the conjugate was stable, while only 11.61% of exatecan (8) was stable (Fig. 6B). This indicates DUPA-SS-Exa (10) is much more stable than exatecan (8) under 50% FBS. The probable reason for this degradation may be the presence of glutathione in FBS.40 The conjugate, as well as exatecan (8), did not show any changes at pH 7.2 (Fig. 6C).

DUPA-SS-Exa (10) shows enhanced anti-tumor activity in in vitro and preclinical tumor models

The cytotoxicity of DUPA-SS-Exa (10) was evaluated in PSMA-positive LNCaP cells, PSMA-negative PC-3 cells, as well as non-cancerous HEK293 cells (Fig. 7). DUPA-SS-Exa (10) showed cytotoxicity towards PSMA-positive LNCaP cells with an IC50 value of 117.01 ± 6.67 nM at 24 h and 86.75 ± 5.82 nM at 48 h (Fig. 7 and Table 3). The targeted cytotoxicity was further confirmed by testing DUPA-SS-Exa (10) against PSMA-negative PC-3 cells, which showed an IC50 > 100 µM. This precisely demonstrates the ability of DUPA-SS-Exa (10) to distinguish PSMA-positive from PSMA-negative cells. The IC50 value of DUPA-SS-Exa (10) was significantly lower than that of its exatecan (8) counterpart in both PSMA-positive and PSMA-negative cell lines. As expected, when LNCaP cells were previously treated with DUPA-glu (12) (a PSMA-blocking agent), then treated with DUPA-SS-Exa (10), no cytotoxicity was observed. This further confirms the receptor-mediated uptake of DUPA-SS-Exa (10). Similarly, a cytotoxicity assay against non-cancerous HEK 293 cells revealed that the absence of PSMA in these cells rendered DUPA-SS-Exa (10) non-cytotoxic. This shows the ability of DUPA-SS-Exa (10) to deliver the payload to the targeted tumor cells, thereby reducing the bystander effect on surrounding healthy cells. Exatecan (8) is a non-selective cytotoxic payload that acts independently of PSMA expression and exhibits high toxicity toward normal cells, as demonstrated by IC50 values of 7.86 ± 0.42 nM at 24 h and 7.27 ± 0.22 pM at 48 h.41 There is a 1000-fold decrease in cytotoxicity when exatecan (8) is treated with HEK 293 cells. According to the mechanism of action of exatecan (8), it binds to and temporarily forms TOP1-DNA covalent cleavage complexes.42 Cytotoxicity is triggered only when cells are actively undergoing DNA replication, i.e., the S-phase of the cell cycle. HEK 293 cells have a doubling period of 20–24 h. Hence, during the 24 h of exatecan (8) exposure, few cells might be in S-phase; thus, the cells tolerate the presence of exatecan (8). However, this scenario changes over 48 h of exatecan (8) treatment, during which the active cells might have undergone at least one cell division.43,44 This maximizes the fatality of the HEK 293 cells; thus, the IC50 decreases drastically.45,46

The efficacy of DUPA-SS-Exa (10) was evaluated in a female syngeneic mouse model of subcutaneous melanoma. B16F10 cells and the Geltrix mixture were injected subcutaneously into the left flank of C57BL/6 black mice, and the tumor was allowed to grow to 150 mm3 of tumor volume. There were four different study groups: control (tumor-induced mice), exatecan (8)-injected group (treatment group), DUPA-glu (12) + DUPA-SS-Exa (10) injected group (blocking group), and DUPA-SS-Exa-injected group (treatment group). When the tumor reached 200 mm3 of volume, conjugates were injected into the respective groups, and their body weight and tumor growth were monitored. A total of 6 injections were administered at 2 day intervals (Fig. 11A). The control group received a saline injection, which served as a negative control. In the blocking group, DUPA-glu (12) was injected (100-fold) 1 h before the injection of DUPA-SS-Exa (10) such that it would block the PSMA receptors. Throughout the study period, there was no significant change in body weight in the control, blocking, and DUPA-SS-Exa-treated groups (Fig. 11B and C). However, severe weight loss was observed in the unconjugated exatecan (8)-treated group. It was observed that the body weight of exatecan-treated mice was severely reduced within 4 injections, indicating that exatecan (8) showed severe toxicity to the mice. However, this group of mice showed reduced tumor volume and, due to greater body weight loss, the mice were sacrificed. This also indicates that DUPA-SS-Exa (10) significantly reduces toxicity to mice. However, there was no significant decrease in tumor volume in DUPA-SS-Exa (10)-treated mice (Fig. 11B and D). Later, by evaluating PSMA expression, it was confirmed that B16F10 cells, a melanoma-origin cell line, did not exhibit substantial PSMA expression (Fig. 11E).47 Our experimental findings confirm that the specific B16F10 cell line utilized in our study lacked sufficient surface PSMA expression to facilitate receptor-mediated endocytosis for DUPA-SS-Exa (10). However, a few studies show that there is a presence of PSMA expression in melanoma cell lines.48 As expected, tumor growth occurred in the PBS-treated control and blocking groups. Furthermore, the future direction of this study is to evaluate DUPA-SS-Exa (10) in a humanized prostate cancer-induced tumor mice model (LNCaP cell-induced tumor mice model), which might provide more insight into its potential to reduce tumors. Similarly, the translational potential of DUPA-SS-Exa (10) can be evaluated by comparing it with clinical-stage PSMA-SMDCs.

Experimental section

In silico analysis of DUPA-SS-Exa conjugate

Molecular docking

The crystal structure of the PSMA was obtained from the RSCB Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 9HLW). The receptor was prepared for docking by removing the heteroatoms and water molecules. The box dimensions were 103.423, 86.164, 112.351, and the grid size was maintained at 40 × 40 × 40. The conjugate (10) and other PSMA-SMDCs were prepared using AutoDock Tools and saved in PDBQT format. Autodock Vina (v.1.1.2) was used to perform the molecular docking study.49 The best docked pose with the lowest binding free energy conformer was analyzed using PyMOL Molecular Graphics System (v. 1.3) (https://pymol.org) and Drug Discovery Studio (https://www.3dsbiovia.com). This complex was further simulated in GROMACS for 20 ns.

System set-up and molecular dynamics simulation

The complex from docking was prepared for simulation using GROMACS.50,51 The ligand was prepared using ACPYPE with General Amber Force Field (GAFF) parameters, and the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field was applied to the PSMA (PDB ID: 9HLW).52,53 The complex was solvated in a dodecahedron box of 21 665 water molecules and ionized with two Na+ counterions. Further, the energy minimization step was performed using the steepest descent algorithm until a maximum force of <1000 kJ mol−1 nm−1 was reached. Then, the system was equilibrated in the NVT ensemble (at 298 K) and NPT ensemble (at 1 psi) using the V-rescale thermostat and Parrinello–Rahman barostat, respectively. After the final production run, the trajectory was analyzed by evaluating RMSD, RMSF, H-bond generation, and binding free energy calculation using MM/PBSA.54

Synthesis of DUPA-conjugated exatecan (DUPA-SS-Exa) (10)

Synthesis of 2-[3-(1,3-dicarboxy propyl)-ureido]pentanedioic acid (DUPA) ligand

In an argon atmosphere and maintaining the temperature at −50 °C using dry ice, triphosgene (0.050 g), a highly reactive nucleophile, was dissolved in dry DCM (dichloromethane). In a separate vial, Bis(tert-butyl)-l-glutamate hydrochloride (1) (1 eq., 0.150 g) was dissolved in dry DCM. Furthermore, this mixture was added to trisphogene while stirring. Similarly, triethylamine (TEA) was added and the mixture was allowed to react for 90 minutes at −50 °C, followed by another 90 minutes at room temperature (RT). Later, l-glutamic-γ-benzyl-α-tert-butyl hydrochloride (0.159 g) and triethylamine (0.14 mL) were dissolved in dry DCM and added to the reacting mixture. The reaction was monitored by TLC using a 1 : 3 ethyl acetate: hexane mixture. Furthermore, the reaction was continued for 24 hours at RT. The reaction mixture was concentrated using rotary evaporation under reduced pressure. The resultant was diluted with ethyl acetate and water, and a brine solution was used to wash. The organic layer was separated, dried using anhydrous Na2SO4, and filtered. Again, the solvent was evaporated, and the crude product was purified by column chromatography on 100–200 mesh silica gel, eluting with a 25% ethyl acetate in hexane mixture.37 The purified benzyl tris(tert-butoxy) protected DUPA precursor (2) was obtained. Benzyl tris(tert-butoxy) protected DUPA precursor: yield (0.557 g, 71.69%), colour: white powder, melting point = 144 °C. 1H NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3) δ 5.00 (s, 1H), 4.64 (s, 1H), 4.26 (s, 1H), 2.24 (s, 1H), 1.99 (s, 1H), 1.62 (s, 1H), (s, 2H), (s, 10H), 1.18 (s, 2H), 0.81 (s, 1H) (Fig. S1).

Synthesis of DUPA-cysteine (4) ligand via solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS)

In a peptide synthesis vessel, H-Cys(Trt)-2-Cl-Trt-resin (3) (200 mg, 0.1002 mmol, 1 eq.) was swelled with dichloromethane (DCM) and dimethylformamide (DMF) for 45 min each. After swelling, the first amino acid Fmoc-Phe-OH solution (77.69 mg, 0.2004 mmol, 2 eq.) and Hünig's base (129.75 mg, 1.002 mmol, 10 eq.) were introduced to the resin, followed by PyBOP (104.28 mg, 0.2004 mmol, 2 eq.). The reaction was allowed to proceed for 4 h, the coupling solution was drained, and the resin was washed with DMF (3 × 10 mL) to remove unreacted amino acids. Kaiser tests were performed to assess reaction completion. Fmoc deprotection was carried out using 20% piperidine in DMF (3 × 10 mL) before each amino acid coupling. The sequence of addition was Fmoc-Phe-OH (77.69 mg, 0.2004 mmol, 2 eq.), N-Fmoc-8-aminooctanoic acid (Fmoc-AOC-OH) (77.69 mg, 0.2004 mmol, 2 eq.), and the DUPA ligand. To finish the synthesis, the DUPA-cys ligand (4) (97.90 mg, 0.2004 mmol, 2 eq.) was added following the same coupling, washing, and deprotection steps described above. DUPA-cys (12) peptide was cleaved from the resin using a cleavage mixture consisting of 92.5% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), 2.5% water, 2.5% ethanedithiol (EDT), and 2.5% triisopropylsilane (TIS). The cleavage mixture was added, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 1.5 h. The cleavage mixture was drained into a clean flask. The resin was washed an additional 3 times with the cleavage mixture. The combined mixture was concentrated under reduced pressure to a smaller volume (∼5 mL) and precipitated in cold diethyl ether. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation, washed 3 times with ethyl ether, and dried under high vacuum. The product was evaluated by HRMS, where the calculated m/z [M + H] was 858.9610; found were m/z [M + H] = 859.3971 and [M + Na] = 881.3829, (Fig. S2). Later, the cleaved DUPA-cys (4) was subjected to purity analysis by RP-HPLC, and the percent purity was found to be 96.33% (Fig. S3). DUPA-cys obtained from SPPS was used for the next step of the synthesis37,57 (Fig. 5).

Synthesis of 4-nitrophenyl 2-(pyridin-2-yldisulfanyl)ethyl carbonate activated exatecan (9)

2-(Pyridyldithio) ethyl alcohol (5) (168 mg, 0.897 mmol, 1 eq.) was dissolved in DCM, and pyridine (141.94 mg, 1.794 mmol, 2 eq.) was added to it under stirring conditions. Furthermore, DMAP (108.71 mg, 0.8972 mmol, 1 eq.) was separately dissolved in DCM and added to this mixture; similarly, 4-nitrophenyl chloroformate (6) (361.69 mg, 1.794 mmol, 2 eq.) was added under stirring conditions. The reaction was carried out for 12 hours at room temperature. The reaction mixture was washed with 2 M HCl and brine, then dried over MgSO4. The solvent was removed by rotary evaporation, and the product was purified by silica column chromatography using hexane/ethyl acetate (A = hexane, 90% to 50%; B = ethyl acetate).58 The cleavable linker was analyzed through 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy. Furthermore, this linker (7) (152 mg, 0.4314 mmol, 1.1 eq.) was dissolved in DMSO, and TEA (40 mg, 0.3921 mmol, 1 eq.) was gradually added to the solution containing exatecan mesylate (8) (208.42 mg, 0.3921 mmol, 1 eq.), which was separately dissolved in DMSO. The mixture was stirred for 20 h to obtain the activated exatecan (9). It was evaluated by HRMS, where the calculated m/z [M + H] was 648.7244, the observed mass m/z [M + H] was 650.1709, and m/z [M/2 + H] was 325.0890 (Fig. S6).

Conjugation of DUPA-cysteine (4) and nitro-pyridyldisulfanyl activated exatecan (9)

DUPA-cys (4) (30 mg, 0.0349 mmol, 1 eq.) and Hünig's base (22.64 mg, 0.1745 mmol, 5 eq.) were dissolved separately in DMSO and added to a solution of activated-exatecan (9) (22.64 mg, 0.0349 mmol, 1 eq.). The reaction was allowed to occur for 24 hours (Fig. 5). The product obtained was evaluated by HRMS, and the calculated m/z [M + H] was 1396.5234, and m/z [M/2 + H] was 698.2617; the observed m/z [M + H] was 1396.5865, and m/z [M/2 + H] was 698.7747 (Fig. S7). Later, to measure the purity of the conjugate, it was injected into an analytical RP-HPLC system, and a single peak was observed at 1.205 min.

Synthesis of DUPA-glucosamine (DUPA-glu) (12)

BOC-protected DUPA ligand (2) was dissolved in methanol. 1-Ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC) and Triethylamine (TEA) were added, and the mixture was stirred for 1 h. To this stirring mixture, N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) was added, and the mixture was stirred for 6 h. Glucosamine (11) was added, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 48 h to obtain the BOC-protected DUPA. To remove BOC groups, 10% TFA was added to the reaction mixture, which was then stirred for an additional 12 h (Fig. S4). The solvent was evaporated using the rotary evaporator. The compound obtained was a yellow-colored oil, which was further purified by RP-HPLC. The method used was A = methanol and B = 0.01% TFA in water, with an 80 : 20 isocratic gradient. It was run for 20 min with a flow rate of 7 mL min−1. The analytical chromatogram of DUPA-glu (12) showed a single peak at 2.307 min. Later, the conjugate was characterized by HRMS; the calculated m/z [M–H] was 481.4110, and found m/z [M–H] peak was found at 480.3490 (Fig. S11).

Synthesis of DUPA-EDA

BOC-protected DUPA ligand (2) was dissolved in methanol. 1-Ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC) (2 eq.) and triethylamine (TEA) (2 eq.) were added, and the mixture was stirred for 1 h. Synthesis of DUPA-FITC

FITC (15) (1.2 eq.) was dissolved in methanol under stirring conditions and purged in an Argon atmosphere for 45 min. DUPA-EDA (14) (30 mg, 1 eq.) and Hunig's base (5 eq.) were mixed and added to the stirring solution of FITC (15). The reaction was allowed to react for 16 h. The solution was further purified via RP-HPLC (A = acetonitrile and B = water), 80 : 20 isocratic method was followed for 20 min with a flow rate of 7 mL min−1. The analytical chromatogram revealed a single peak of the DUPA-FITC (16) (Fig. S14). Serum stability assay

The stability of the DUPA conjugate (10) was evaluated in 50% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 0, 6, 12, and 24 h, with incubation at 37 °C. The experiments were performed in duplicate. After incubation, aliquots of each sample were quenched with 3 volumes of HPLC solvents, vortexed, and stored at −20 °C for 5 min, then centrifuged at 15 000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was taken and then centrifuged again. The final supernatant was analyzed by RP-HPLC.59 The method used was A = acetonitrile and B = 0.01% TFA in water; an isocratic 70 : 30 ratio was maintained. The conjugates were run for 10 min on an analytical HPLC (Shimadzu) at a flow rate of 1 mL min−1. The column used was a C18 Shim-pack GIST column (particle size 5 Å, dimensions were 20 × 250). The same column was used throughout the studies. The percentage stability was relatively calculated by comparing the area of the peaks at baseline time-zero injection.

Stability assay at acidic pH

The stability of the DUPA-SS-Exa (10) conjugate and exatecan (8) was evaluated at pH 4.5. The experiment was conducted in duplicate at different time intervals. Each sample was incubated at its respective pH and at RT. The samples were diluted 3× with the HPLC solvents. The method used was A = acetonitrile and B = 0.01% TFA in water; an isocratic 70 : 30 ratio was maintained. The conjugates were run for 10 min on an analytical HPLC system at a flow rate of 1 mL min−1. The percentage stability was relatively calculated by comparing the area of the peaks at baseline time-zero injection.

Cytotoxicity of exatecan conjugates

Cytotoxicity of the DUPA-SS-Exa (10) and exatecan (8) was evaluated through an MTT assay in LNCaP cell lines (DUPA receptor-positive cell line), PC-3 (DUPA receptor-negative cell line), and HEK 293 cells (non-cancerous cell line) (Table 2 and Fig. 7). LNCaP cells were grown in RPMI-1640 media; PC3 and HEK293 cells were grown in high-glucose DMEM media. All cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2, and the media were supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cells (5 × 103 cells per well in 200 µL of media) were allowed to grow for 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2 in 96-well plates. Cells were treated with increasing concentrations of compounds for 24 h and 48 h. DUPA-glu (12) was added 1 h before the addition of the DUPA-SS-Exa (10) conjugate; it was used to block the cell-surface DUPA receptors, and exatecan (8) was used as a control. After 24 and 48 h, 20 µl of MTT reagent (5 mg mL−1 in sterile distilled water) was added to each well, and the plates were wrapped in aluminium foil and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. The purple formazan product was dissolved by adding 100 µL DMSO to each well. The absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (TECAN INFINITE M Plex, Switzerland) at 595 nm. The study was conducted in triplicate.

Apoptosis assay using annexin V and PI

PSMA-positive LNCaP cells were cultured in 6-well plates. Each well was treated with DUPA-SS-Exa (10) conjugate and exatecan (8) at their IC50 values for 24 hours. After treatment, the floating dead cells were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 2 minutes and collected. Later, the adherent cells were trypsinized and collected into the same tube. These pellets were resuspended thoroughly with 400 µl of DPBS and 2 drops of annexin V-FITC as per the manufacturer's protocol (Thermofisher Scientific) and 2 µl propidium iodide (1 mg mL−1). These cells were analyzed using a CytoFLEX Flow Cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA). The results were obtained using CytExpert software.60

Cellular uptake assay

PSMA-positive LNCaP and PSMA-negative PC-3 (1.5 × 105 cells per mL) were seeded in 24-well plates and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. After the 24 h incubation, the old media were carefully removed, and fresh media containing 25 nM of DUPA-FITC (16) was added. The cells were immediately visualized using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus, CLSM – Fluoview Fv3000, Japan) at 40× magnification at 1 min, 15 min, 30 min, and 1 h.

Binding affinity assessment using flow cytometry

PSMA-positive LNCaP cells were cultured in 6-well plates at a seeding density of 3 × 105 cells per well. These cells were treated with DUPA-FITC (16) at concentrations ranging from 250 pM to 1000 nM. The cells were incubated at 4 °C for 1 h. These cells were later washed and trypsinized to remove unbound DUPA-FITC (16). The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of these cells was measured by flow cytometry. To determine the specific binding affinity, saturation binding assays were conducted across an increasing concentration gradient (0 to 250 nM). The resulting fluorescence intensity values were fitted to a hyperbolic binding isotherm (Bmaxx)/(Kd + x), utilizing non-linear regression analysis in GraphPad Prism.

Preclinical evaluation of DUPA conjugate in a syngeneic tumor mice model

All animal studies were conducted with the approval of the Institutional Animal Ethical Committee (IAEC) of Vellore Institute of Technology (VIT) (VIT/IAEC-26/16 Apr/24/16). 1 × 107 cells of B16F10 melanoma cells were mixed with Geltrix in a 1 : 2 ratio and were immediately injected subcutaneously (s.c.) into the left flank of female C57BL/6 mice. Female mice were selected since female mice have higher tumor susceptibility in tumor generation.61 The tumor volume was measured until it reached 200 mm3, and the DUPA conjugate was administered on the 20th day of tumor implantation. Mice were distributed randomly into 4 groups, each having 5 mice for Control (saline-treated), DUPA-SS-Exa (10) (7.5 mg kg−1) + DUPA-glu (12) (10 times the excess 75 mg kg−1) (blocking studies), DUPA-SS-Exa (10) (7.5 mg kg−1) (treatment group), and exatecan (8) (7.5 mg kg−1) (treatment group). For blocking studies, DUPA-glu (12) was injected one hour before DUPA-SS-Exa (10), allowing DUPA-glu (12) to block the active PSMA receptors. The dose of the treatment was selected from the literature.62 The conjugate was administered in 3 doses per week for 2 weeks. The tumor volume was calculated by measuring the length and width of the tumor using a Vernier caliper and calculating using the formula (V = 0.5 × L × W2). The changes in tumor volume and body weight were measured.

PSMA expression assessment in cell lines

LNCaP, PC-3, and B16F10 cell lines were grown in appropriate conditions and harvested. The cell pellets were washed and resuspended in RIPA buffer containing a protease inhibitor cocktail. The lysates were incubated on ice for 45 minutes with intermittent vortexing, followed by centrifugation at 12 000×g for 15 minutes at 4 °C. The supernatant containing total protein was collected and quantified by BCA assay. The IRIS11 pre-stained protein ladder (BIOHELIX, India) from the 200 kDa to 15 kDa range was run along with the cell lysate proteins. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were cut horizontally and incubated separately overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody against PSMA (PSMA/FOLH1 Rabbit mAb, Cat: A22211, Abclonal) and an internal control antibody (GAPDH) (GAPDH (14C10) Rabbit mAb, Cell Signalling Technology), followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (HRP Goat Anti-rabbit IgG, Cat A21020, Abbkine) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected by 1 second exposure using enhanced chemiluminescence in the Amersham™ ImageQuant™ 800 western blot imaging system (Cytiva, USA) and visualized using ImageJ software.

Conclusion

PSMA-based therapies have been developing tremendously in the pharmaceutical industry. Several companies have PSMA-based therapies in their pipelines, and many of these conjugates are currently in clinical trials. For instance, Pluvicto, developed by Novartis, is the first FDA-approved PSMA-targeted radioligand used for treating metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer.63 Similarly, AbbVie has engineered a dual-variable IgG1 drug conjugate targeting PSMA and STEAP1 (six-transmembrane epithelial antigen of prostate 1). This IgG1 antibody is conjugated with a topoisomerase 1 inhibitor (Top1i), and the resulting conjugate is currently in phase I clinical trials.64 These illustrate the significance of PSMA-based therapies.

In this research, we have described the generation of tumor-targeted small-molecule drug conjugates (SMDCs) delivered through the PSMA. DUPA-SS-Exa (10) exhibits high binding affinity to the PSMA in in silico analysis and shows excellent anti-cancer activity in in vitro studies. It is evident that SMDCs exhibit higher therapeutic activity than ADCs, owing to their smaller size, greater cell permeability in solid tumors, greater stability in the physiological environment, a manageable synthesis process, and non-immunogenic nature.65 This finding is further supported by a comparison between DUPA-SS-Exa (10) and ADCT-241, in which DUPA-SS-Exa (10) demonstrated enhanced anticancer activity against LNCaP cells. ADCT-241 is an IgG1 antibody targeted to PSMA, linked via a valine–alanine linker to exatecan (8). ADCT-241 had an ED50 value of 996.9 ng mL−1, while DUPA-SS-Exa (10) had an IC50 value of 163.33 ng mL−1 (117.01 ± 6.67 nM). This indicates that DUPA-SS-Exa (10) shows 6-fold higher anti-tumor activity than the ADC counterpart.66 DUPA-SS-Exa (10) shows excellent stability in the presence of acid and serum. DUPA-FITC (16) was internalized within 1 h of treatment; hence, similar behaviour could be expected from DUPA-SS-Exa (10). Apoptosis analysis also proved that DUPA-SS-Exa (10) efficiently killed the PSMA-positive LNCaP cells. Although DUPA-SS-Exa (10) was unable to treat melanoma cells due to the absence of PSMA expression, it demonstrated its potential in vitro in the LNCaP cell line. This provides various opportunities to evaluate the conjugate in the LNCaP-induced immunodeficient xenograft tumor mice model.
