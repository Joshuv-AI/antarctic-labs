Mathematical modeling and optimization of a three-echelon supply chain management by incorporating circular consumption, cooperative advertisement cost, and selling price decisions
Authors: Mohammed AlKahtani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study develops a centralized three echelon open loop supply chain optimization model that integrates circular input adoption with coordinated pricing and cooperative advertising decisions. In the proposed setting, recycled materials and usable components are supplied through an external recycling market and become available for procurement by supply chain members as circular inputs. Circularity is represented through echelon specific circular input utilization decision variables at the supplier, manufacturer, and retailer levels, allowing the model to capture the economic and demand side effects of circular input adoption under market availability constraints. Customer demand is formulated as a function of selling price, cooperative advertising effort, and average circular input utilization, thereby linking market response with sustainability oriented operational decisions. Key cost parameters are modeled as triangular fuzzy numbers and transformed into crisp equivalents using the signed distance method. The resulting nonlinear constrained optimization problem maximizes total supply chain profit subject to advertising budget, inventory capacity, recycled input availability, and circularity related feasibility constraints. Sequential Quadratic Programming is used as the main solution approach and is compared with genetic algorithm, pattern search, and minimax methods. The numerical results show that the SQP based solution achieves the highest profit while maintaining feasible and balanced decision values across pricing, advertising, and circular input utilization variables. Sensitivity analysis further indicates that profitability is most affected by recycled input availability and circular cost adjustment, while incentive strength provides a secondary but consistent improvement. The proposed framework provides a quantitative decision support tool for firms seeking to coordinate pricing, advertising, and circular input adoption decisions while maintaining profitability under realistic market and policy conditions.

Introduction

Escalating resource scarcity, climate change, and tightening environmental regulations have pushed firms to reconsider the traditional linear model of “take, make, dispose” and to move toward circular-economy-based operations. Circular economy thinking focuses on retaining value through reuse, remanufacturing, recycling, and product life extension, so that materials circulate in technical and biological loops instead of ending as waste1. Although recent trend in circular economy is increasing but Circularity Gap Report estimates that the global share of secondary materials in total material use declined from 9.1% in 2018 to 7.2% in 2023, indicating that global production systems remain heavily dependent on virgin inputs despite increased circular economy activity2. In parallel, environmental impacts are strongly supply-chain dominated, CDP reports that corporates’ Scope 3 supply-chain emissions are, on average, 26 times larger than emissions from direct operations, reinforcing the need for multi-echelon interventions spanning upstream supply, manufacturing, and downstream retail operations. Recent work at the interface of circular economy and supply chain management shows that supply chain processes are central to enabling this transition, since sourcing, production, distribution, and reverse logistics collectively determine how effectively materials are recovered and recirculated3.

From a managerial perspective, the shift to circular supply chains is not purely normative. It is increasingly viewed as a strategic opportunity that links profitability with sustainability. Systematic reviews, such as those by Govindan and Hasanagic, highlight that firms pursue circular practices to reduce costs, secure access to critical materials, and improve their market position. Still, they also face considerable barriers related to economics, regulation, and technological readiness4. These barriers imply that the feasible level of circularity is constrained by capital availability, recovery and recycling infrastructure, and the ability to coordinate actors across multiple echelons.

At the same time, demand in many markets is no longer exogenous. Customers respond to product price, advertising effort, and perceived environmental attributes, including recycled materials or circular packaging. The global advertising expenditure projected to reach approximately USD 1.08 trillion in 2024, which highlights why cooperative advertising structures can meaningfully affect profitability in retailer-facing demand models5. A model is proposed by6 sustainable supply chains increasingly model demand as a function of price, advertising, and a “green” or “circular” quality level, showing that sustainability can be a demand driver rather than only a cost burden7. This creates a complex set of trade-offs, as firms must coordinate on prices, advertising budgets, and circular consumption levels while coping with practical constraints in accessing and qualifying recycled inputs that are supplied through external recyclers and facilitated by third party logistics providers in an open loop setting.

The present research responds to this context by developing a three echelon mathematical model for an open loop circular input adoption structure consisting of a supplier, a manufacturer, and downstream retailers. In this setting, the focal supply chain does not directly control customer return flows, collection routing, disassembly, or remanufacturing operations. Instead, recycled materials and usable components are assumed to be supplied through an external recycling market, supported by independent recyclers and recovery actors. The supplier, manufacturer, and retailer can procure these circular inputs according to their respective utilization decisions. Circularity is therefore represented by echelon level circular input utilization proportions rather than by explicitly modeled reverse network flows. Demand at the retailer is modelled as a variable quantity that depends simultaneously on selling price, cooperative advertising effort, and the average circular input utilization level across the supply chain. The model also incorporates market availability limits for recycled inputs, budget restrictions, capacity constraints, incentive subsidies, and circularity related penalty costs. This structure enables the study to quantify how pricing, advertising, and circular input adoption interact in a coordinated three echelon supply chain without claiming to optimize a complete closed loop recovery system.

Literature review

Early work on the circular economy emphasized broad principles and macro-level transitions, but more recent research has explicitly connected these ideas to supply chain design. Hazen and co-authors discuss how traditional supply chain management practices can be reconfigured to support circular economy outcomes, highlighting the role of sourcing, production, distribution, and recovery systems in closing resource loops8. Bibliometric and conceptual studies demonstrate that circular supply chain management has emerged as a distinct field concerned with designing network structures that enable reuse, remanufacturing, recycling, and cascading use of materials9.

From an operational viewpoint, extensive reviews of reverse logistics and closed-loop supply chains have been conducted, identifying key configurations for collection, inspection, remanufacturing, and recycling and summarizing how these are integrated with forward flows10. The circularity at the supply chain level relies on the coexistence of forward and reverse channels, which significantly complicates planning decisions. Complementing this, systematically mapping the drivers, barriers, and practices that influence circular economy implementation from a supply chain perspective has been conducted. It is concluded that economic and financial constraints, along with regulatory uncertainty, are among the most critical obstacles to higher circularity4,11. These studies collectively underscore the need for quantitative models that do not treat circularity as a purely conceptual goal but explicitly account for investment limits, capacity constraints, and financial trade-offs when designing circular supply chain strategies.

In parallel, it is also observed that pricing and advertising decisions in supply chains under price-sensitive or promotion-sensitive demand. The demand may increase or decrease with retail price and may depend on trade credit or promotional decisions12. A recent research analyzes the joint optimization of pricing, ordering, and advertising in a single manufacturer single retailer supply chain, showing how advertising effort shapes demand and interacts with wholesale and retail prices13. The effects of price and advertising budget6 and advertising cost7 on demand have been studied, and inventory models have been proposed to adjust to fluctuating demand levels. Within green supply chains, several authors extend these ideas by introducing demand functions that depend on price, advertising, and a greenness level. Liu investigates pricing policies in a green supply chain where demand is influenced by targeted advertising and the product’s green degree, and demonstrates how higher greenness can stimulate demand under suitable pricing and promotion strategies7. Demand-oriented sustainability models show that consumer preferences increasingly respond to environmental attributes, advertising effort, and pricing decisions14,15. More recently, studies on green supply network design consider demand that depends on both price and environmental attributes, and integrate these relationships into facility location and flow optimization to jointly address economic and ecological objectives16. These contributions show that modelling demand as a function of price, advertising, and sustainability attributes yields more realistic insights. However, they typically refer to a general “green degree” or environmental performance rather than explicitly treating circular consumption of materials and products as a demand driver along multiple echelons in the chain.

Given the distributed nature of supply chains, researchers have long studied coordination mechanisms such as advertising cost-sharing contracts17, revenue-sharing schemes18, and sustainability-oriented incentive contracts19. Supply-chain partners can enhance overall profitability by collaborating to stimulate market demand through coordinated operational and marketing decisions20–23. Demonstrate18 and24 that cooperation among suppliers, manufacturers, and retailers, particularly through joint advertising and aligned pricing policies, can significantly improve performance compared to decentralized decision-making. Later studies consider contracts that combine revenue sharing with advertising cost sharing, showing that when pricing and advertising are jointly optimized under such agreements, full coordination can be achieved under certain conditions24. The costs at each level, supplier, manufacturer, and retailers, are essential to consider while optimizing the revenue model. Basic cost structures commonly include setup, production, ordering, holding, and transportation costs at different stages of the supply chain25. Recent extensions incorporate additional elements such as advertising expenditure26 and carbon-related costs27 to reflect sustainability considerations and market-driven demand dynamics28. In supplier-centric models, costs typically include setup, production, and advertising expenses, whereas manufacturers incur setup, manufacturing, ordering, and advertising costs. Retailers, in turn, are generally modeled with ordering and advertising costs that directly influence consumer demand29. The inclusion of carbon and advertising costs alongside traditional operational costs enhances model realism by linking economic performance with environmental responsibility and market responsiveness, thereby providing a more reliable foundation for coordinated supply-chain decision-making.

Recent three echelon supply chain studies have also examined pricing, marketing effort, warranty services, return policies, delayed payment structures, social responsibility, and sustainability oriented coordination mechanisms. For example, previous studies have analyzed three level supply chains with warranty, pricing, and marketing decisions30, price and marketing effort dependent demand with recycling savings in closed loop settings31, price, quality, effort level, and return policy decisions in three level closed loop supply chains32, lead time aggregation in three echelon systems33, upstream advance and downstream delayed payment structures34, raw material pricing, manufacturing, and inventory policies35, social responsibility and product returns36, sustainable three tier supply chains under stochastic consumption and channel power structures37, and climate effects, carbon emissions, and delayed payments in sustainable three echelon systems38. These studies reinforce the importance of coordinated economic and sustainability decisions in multi echelon supply chains, but they mainly focus on closed loop returns, payment structures, warranty policies, channel power, or general sustainability measures rather than open loop circular input adoption with cooperative advertising, incentive support, and recycled input availability constraints.

In the context of green and circular supply chains, more recent research introduces contracts that incorporate product greenness, green investment, and cost or revenue sharing. A study conducted on two part tariff and cost sharing contracts for green supply chains and show that these can coordinate pricing and greenness decisions while improving environmental outcomes39.

Chen et al.40, examined a dual channel green-supply chain where demand depends on price, sales effort, and green degree, and investigate how coordination contracts can align the manufacturer and retailer. Saha et al.41 focus on optimal strategies for green investment and cost or revenue sharing in sustainable supply chains, emphasizing the importance of contract design to link investment decisions with economic performance. Despite this progress, most of these studies center on greenness or emission reduction and do not explicitly model circular consumption decisions across multiple echelons, nor do they integrate penalty cost structures that reflect failure to meet circularity targets alongside incentives for exceeding them.

A substantial stream of research has developed closed-loop supply chain and circular network design models. These works typically determine optimal facility locations, capacities, and material flows for forward and reverse channels, sometimes under multi-period or multi-product settings. The integration of circularity, pricing, advertising, and multi-echelon coordination naturally leads to complex, nonlinear, and mixed-integer formulations. To tackle such models, many authors have turned to metaheuristics42. Metaheuristics such as genetic algorithms43–45, simulated annealing46, tabu search47, and particle swarm optimization48 are now widely used in circular supply chain optimization due to their flexibility and ability to handle multiple objectives and nonconvex landscapes. In some studies, many algorithms are compared to assess their effectiveness under specific conditions. Three algorithms, genetic algorithms, simulated annealing, and a new Keshtel algorithm, are compared for a closed-loop design and pricing problem49. Genetic algorithms, simulated annealing, and particle swarm optimization are compared for the design of a circular closed-loop network. A circular closed-loop supply chain network is designed. It employs three metaheuristics, namely simulated annealing, genetic algorithms, and particle swarm optimization, tuned via the Taguchi method, to optimize configuration under cost and environmental criteria50. Recent contributions propose management optimization strategies for closed-loop supply chains using a genetic simulated annealing algorithm, further reinforcing the role of hybrid metaheuristics in this area51. These studies collectively justify the use of metaheuristics such as GA and simulated annealing for solving integrated circular supply chain models where exact methods may be computationally prohibitive.

However, three gaps remain. First, there is limited work that explicitly models variable demand driven by price, advertising, and circular consumption simultaneously in a three-echelon supply chain where circular decisions are taken at multiple stages rather than at a single firm. Second, while several studies incorporate contracts for greenness or emission reduction, few embed penalty and incentive mechanisms that directly tie circular consumption targets to capital investment limits and profit allocation across supply chain members. Third, although metaheuristics and nonlinear programming methods are widely used in closed loop and circular supply chain models, limited attention has been given to open loop circular input adoption models that integrate recycled input utilization, cooperative advertising, pricing, incentive subsidies, and market availability limits within a single profit maximizing framework.

This study contributes to the circular supply chain literature by developing a centralized three echelon open loop optimization model that jointly integrates circular input adoption, cooperative advertising, and selling price decisions within a unified profit maximization framework. Unlike conventional closed loop supply chain models that explicitly optimize customer returns, collection, inspection, remanufacturing, or recycling flows, the proposed model focuses on an open loop setting in which recycled materials and usable components are supplied through an external recycling market and adopted by supply chain members as circular inputs. Circularity is modeled through echelon specific utilization decisions at the supplier, manufacturer, and retailer levels, allowing the model to capture how circular input adoption influences both cost structures and customer demand. The model further incorporates recycled input availability constraints, circularity related penalty costs, incentive based subsidies, cooperative advertising effects, selling price decisions, and fuzzy cost uncertainty. This integrated formulation produces a nonlinear and non-separable decision structure that supports coordinated supply chain planning under realistic market, policy, and circular input availability conditions. Thus, the main contribution of this work lies in providing a quantitative decision support framework for evaluating the economic tradeoffs among pricing, advertising, circular input adoption, and external recycled input availability in a three echelon supply chain.

SCM mathematical model

Circularity in the supply chain management model

In this study, circularity is represented from an open loop input adoption perspective. The model does not include an internally controlled reverse logistics network, customer return collection, disassembly, reuse, or remanufacturing flow decisions. Instead, it assumes that recycled materials and usable components are supplied by external recyclers in the market and become available for procurement by the supplier, manufacturer, and retailer. Therefore, the circularity decision in this model refers to the proportion of externally available circular input used at each echelon. This interpretation is narrower than a full closed loop supply chain model but is consistent with industrial settings where firms purchase certified recycled inputs from external recovery markets rather than managing the entire reverse channel themselves.

Circularity is modeled independently at each level, with each player managing their own us, um, and ur. Demand increases are shared because they rely on the average circularity; any player’s improvements benefit the entire supply chain. The profit function covers the whole chain (total profit) and is calculated as: Profit = Revenue + Total Incentives Subsidy - TC. The objective is to maximize the supply chain’s total profit, accounting for the total incentive subsidy received by supply chain players for circular input adoption. The incentive term represents an exogenous subsidy provided by a regulator (or external agency) proportional to circular input adoption. It is not an internal transfer among supply chain members (Fig. 1).

There are no specific rules for sharing costs or profits from circularity investments, neither the supplier nor the manufacturer directly subsidizes each other’s circularity efforts. Each stage bears its own setup costs and penalties related to circularity. The goal of optimization is to maximize total profit through full coordination, such as a centrally managed or perfectly unified supply chain, rather than decentralized negotiations. The overall supply chain’s average circularity is computed as a simple average, shown in the Eq. (1).

Circular cost adjustment (CrCAd) is an important parameter that depends on the product type and the maturity of the circular supply chain system in the market. Initially, circular costs are higher than linear costs due to the unavailability of supply chain drivers and infrastructure, such as recyclers, collectors, sorting firms, and recovery units. But, with respect to time and as a result of improvements in external recycling infrastructure, sorting quality, supplier qualification, and circular input market maturity, supported by stakeholders, e.g., local government, individual recyclers, original equipment manufacturers, and other service providers, the circular cost becomes cheaper. We consider it -5, indicating that the circular product is 5 dollars less expensive than the virgin product.

A convex penalty is a cost that increases as the circular fraction rises, and is also incorporated into the supply chain model. This reflects the practical realities that pushing circular usage to very high levels can become progressively more expensive, for example, due to diminishing returns, technical limits, or rising operational complexity, which are common challenges in circular supply chains. At moderate levels, circularity can be efficient, but above a threshold, operations may face technical challenges or require significant investment, resulting in higher marginal costs. In the proposed model, c2 is the quadratic cost penalty coefficient for each chain member: supplier (c2s), manufacturer (c2m), and retailers (c2r), which is set to30,40, and10. These numbers indicate that increasing circularity is most expensive for the supplier, less so for the manufacturer, and least for the retailer. Suppliers often face the most significant technical and economic challenges in integrating recycled inputs due to raw material standards, regulatory barriers, or a lack of recycled supply. Manufacturers have intermediate difficulty; they may need new processes or technologies to handle more recycled content. Retailers are usually least affected, as their main changes are logistics, marketing, or packaging. These coefficients can be set based on expert judgment, industry benchmarks, or scenario testing. Demand depending on the circular consumption, selling price, and advertising cost

Demand is a function of advertising costs and the product’s selling price. Representation of demand as a function of the cost of advertising and selling price as decision variables has been done in the study by52, where different scaling factors and parameters reflect aspects of local and international markets with respect to these variables. To reflect realistic market behavior and prevent unbounded demand growth, the effect of advertising on demand is modeled using a concave saturation function with diminishing marginal returns. This captures the fact that initial advertising yields higher incremental demand, while additional spending produces progressively smaller gains. Accordingly, the advertising terms are introduced through ln (1+⋅), ensuring stable demand response and improved numerical behavior of the optimization model, as shown in Eq. (9).

The signed distance method was selected because it provides a simple and computationally efficient way to transform triangular fuzzy numbers into crisp representative values while preserving the influence of the lower, modal, and upper estimates. Compared with ranking based or α-cut based fuzzy methods, the signed distance approach requires fewer computational steps and can be directly embedded into nonlinear optimization models without introducing additional nested uncertainty constraints. This is suitable for the present study because the main objective is to evaluate coordinated pricing, advertising, and circular input utilization decisions under fuzzy cost uncertainty, rather than to develop a separate fuzzy optimization algorithm. However, α-cut based methods may provide a more detailed representation of uncertainty intervals and can be considered in future extensions.

SCM model formulation

Coordination among the supplier, manufacturer, and retailers is modeled through a co-operative advertising arrangement in which the supplier and manufacturer jointly bear part of each retailer’s advertising expenditure to stimulate product demand. Under this collaboration, the supply chain members align their decisions to improve overall profitability by strengthening demand at the retail level. The proposed supply chain management model quantifies both routine and policy-driven cost components incurred across the three tiers. These costs are grouped into fixed and variable categories. Core operational costs include production, setup, holding, ordering, and transportation. In addition, carbon-related costs and advertising expenditures are incorporated to better represent economic and environmental performance. Because inventory and production related costs are affected by volatile conditions such as inflation, the ongoing energy situation, and fluctuations in oil prices, these parameters are treated as uncertain. Neglecting such variability can lead to decisions that are not robust in practice. Accordingly, the principal cost terms for the supplier, manufacturer, and retailers are modeled using fuzzy representations. The resulting fuzzy parameter sets are then handled using the signed distance method. For clarity, the model is organized into four parts: demand formulation, echelon level cost functions, revenue and incentive terms, and operational constraints. These components are then combined in the centralized profit maximization objective.

The circularity related parameters used in the numerical example were selected to represent plausible managerial and policy conditions rather than universal industry constants. The circular cost adjustment reflects the net unit cost difference between circular and virgin inputs and may vary across industries depending on recycling infrastructure, input quality, certification requirements, and supplier maturity. A negative value indicates that circular inputs are economically favorable relative to virgin inputs, while a positive value would represent additional cost burden. The penalty coefficients represent the increasing marginal difficulty of achieving higher circular input utilization at each echelon. Higher penalty values at the supplier level reflect stricter raw material quality requirements and technical compatibility constraints, whereas lower retailer level penalties reflect comparatively simpler changes in packaging, storage, and market handling. The incentive rate and incentive gain represent external policy support, such as subsidies, tax credits, procurement incentives, or circular economy promotion schemes. In practical applications, these parameters can be estimated from industry data, expert judgment, policy documents, or calibrated through scenario analysis.

The data is further analyzed using a non-linear optimization technique to optimize both the product’s selling price and the advertising costs among supply chain partners under a co-op advertising collaboration policy.

Solution algorithms

The three-tier supply chain management model is designed and analyzed to maximize profit, incorporating a co-op advertising policy where supply chain partners evenly share advertising costs. The model is based on a nonlinear optimization problem with multiple constraints and variables. This constrained problem can be transformed into an unconstrained optimization problem and solved by searching for the global optimum. However, traditional methods have been found less effective and are now replaced by approaches based on the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions, which are necessary for optimizing constrained problems. These conditions also facilitate the solution of many nonlinear problems by directly computing Lagrange multipliers, often via SQP methods. The SQP technique finds smooth, uniform, and appropriate solutions for all the decision variables in an optimal and smooth way. The solutions obtained using SQP techniques do not result in extreme points and hence display stable convergence, thereby showing proper alignment between production, inventory, and operation under the constrained conditions. Such an outcome establishes that SQP technique is appropriate for solving constrained and nonlinear supply chain problems related to optimization.

Computational settings

To ensure a fair and reproducible comparison, all solution methods were evaluated using the same objective function, variable bounds, nonlinear constraints, stopping criteria, and feasibility tolerance. SQP was executed using the same initial point and termination tolerances across all test scenarios. SQP convergence was assessed using the solver termination criteria, including objective function tolerance, step size tolerance, and maximum constraint violation. The final SQP solution satisfied the nonlinear constraints within the feasibility tolerance ε = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{10}^{-4}$$\end{document}, indicating stable convergence for the tested model. Since genetic algorithm (GA) based search is stochastic, GA was independently executed for 20 runs with different random seeds, and the best, worst, mean, and standard deviation of the objective value were recorded. Pattern Search and MiniMax were executed as deterministic comparison methods using the same model structure and feasibility requirements.

The feasibility tolerance was set to ε = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{10}^{-4}$$\end{document}. Feasibility was assessed using the maximum constraint violation across all variable bounds and nonlinear constraints. For each run, a solution was considered feasible if all constraints were satisfied within this tolerance. It is important to note that a decision variable located at or near its lower or upper bound was not treated as infeasible, provided that the specified bound and all other constraints were satisfied within ε. Therefore, bound active values, such as circularity variables equal or close to 1, were interpreted as feasible active bound solutions rather than constraint violations.

GA was implemented as a stochastic benchmark and was executed for 20 independent runs using different random seeds. The GA settings, including population size, crossover probability, mutation probability, elitism, and maximum generation limit, were kept fixed across all runs. Constraint handling was performed using a penalty based feasibility strategy, in which infeasible solutions were penalized according to their maximum constraint violation. The resulting best, worst, mean, and standard deviation values were reported to account for the stochastic nature of GA.

The feasibility rate, defined as the percentage of runs that satisfied all constraints at termination, was reported for each method and computed as in Eq. (36).

Numerical results and discussion

This section presents and discusses the results obtained from the numerical experiments conducted on the proposed supply chain optimization model. The performance of the model was assessed by comparing SQP with GA, Pattern Search, and MiniMax in terms of decision variables, objective function outcomes, and computational efficiency. The best values for decision variables across these methods are shown in Table 4. In particular, solutions from the Genetic Algorithm vary significantly across variables, with many showing scattered or suboptimal values, suggesting sensitivity to initialization and constraint handling in the high-dimensional search space or limited local refinement. Pattern Search and MiniMax tend to push several variables toward their limits, resulting in corner solutions that meet constraints but may overlook the supply chain system’s economic trade-offs, especially for inventory and flow-related decisions in Table 5.

SQP generates medium values with good balance, indicating tradeoff between risk management and profits. Both Pattern Search Methods and MiniMax tend to produce extreme values, indicating risk aversion/protection with poor profit management at times. Genetic Algorithm generates medium but unstable values for dealing with uncertainty as well as economic performance.

The continuous decision variables u, um, and ur are close to 1 (0.9917, 0.8593, and 0.7144), indicating high utilization or satisfaction levels for the related echelons or service metrics. This suggests effective coordination of production, inventory, and distribution decisions across the chain. Overall, the SQP solution strikes a favorable balance between profit maximization and constraint satisfaction, maintaining decision variables within practical ranges that can be implemented with minimal disruption.

Sensitivity analysis

This subsection evaluates the robustness of the proposed open loop circular supply chain model under changes in key market, cost, policy, and uncertainty parameters. Sensitivity analysis is included to (i) verify the stability of the optimal solution under realistic variability and (ii) identify dominant drivers of profitability and circular input adoption decisions (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{u}_{s},\:{u}_{m},\:{u}_{r}$$\end{document}). The baseline solution corresponds to the numerical experiment reported in Tables 4 and 5, and all sensitivity runs are solved using the same solver settings and feasibility tolerance = 10−4.

Managerial insights

The numerical results show that coordinated decisions on pricing, advertising, and circular input utilization can materially improve overall profitability while maintaining feasible and implementable operating levels. Managers can use the SQP solution as a benchmark policy for setting stable price and advertising levels and for selecting circular input utilization targets us, um, and ur that remain within practical ranges and avoid extreme corner solutions.

Because the proposed system follows an open loop circular input adoption structure, managers should not interpret the model as a tool for designing a complete reverse logistics or remanufacturing network. Instead, the model supports decisions on how much externally available recycled input should be adopted at each echelon while maintaining profitability and feasibility. Managers should therefore monitor recycled input availability, supplier certification, input quality, and price stability in the external recycling market. When recycled input availability is low, the model suggests that circularity targets should be moderated or supported through incentives. When recycled input availability and cost conditions are favorable, higher circular input utilization can improve both market response and profitability.

Further, the results suggest several practical actions. Managers should treat recycled input availability as a strategic procurement constraint, develop reliable links with certified recyclers, and set different circularity targets for suppliers, manufacturers, and retailers according to their operational burden. Cooperative advertising should also be aligned with circularity claims so that customers are aware of recycled input adoption. Policy makers can support adoption through subsidies or tax incentives, especially when circular inputs are costly or operational penalties are high. Managers should also avoid forcing circularity to its maximum level without considering marginal penalty costs.

Finally, the model can be used as a decision support tool to quantify tradeoffs between higher circular input utilization and the associated cost and demand effects captured in the objective and constraints. This supports practical managerial decisions such as setting circular procurement targets, allocating advertising budgets to improve market acceptance, and selecting price levels that sustain demand while meeting circularity goals under realistic market availability limits.

Conclusions

In this work, a mathematical optimization model is presented for a three-level supply chain network involving a supplier, a manufacturer, and several retailers. In fact, its principal contribution lies within its framework where it integrates variables of circular economy decisions. This model is distinct since it considers variables such as the circular economy ratio, costs of advertising, as well as prices as crucial variables within its framework.

The model analyzes intricately the interaction patterns between forward and circular flows and analyzes the impact of circularity choice on efficiency and economic performance. By incorporating circularity ratio into the objective function and constraints, it helps analyze trade-offs between R, R*, and R** processes and costs associated with reverse logistics. Thus, there is a balanced representation of present-day supply chains that consider profit and sustainability-driven objectives.”

Numerical experiments reveal the superiority of SQP over GA, Pattern Search, as well as MiniMax algorithms for solving this nonlinear constrained optimization problem. The solutions from SQP not only perform better regarding total profit value as well as the stability of decision variables with comparable computational time but also indicate smooth production/shipment plans, realistic levels of inventory, as well as equitable allocation of resources with good coordination among the supply chain partners.

Most importantly, the results verify that joint decisions regarding advertising, selling price, and circular input utilization can enhance supply chain performance. Advertising improves market awareness and demand response, while pricing decisions support revenue optimization. At the same time, circular input utilization can reduce dependence on virgin inputs and strengthen the environmental positioning of the product, provided that recycled inputs are available at economically feasible cost and quality levels. The profit improvement observed in the numerical results therefore arises from coordinated pricing, cooperative advertising, incentive support, and optimized circular input adoption rather than from internally modeled reuse or remanufacturing operations.

From a management perspective, the model can be seen as a decision support model for the design and management of circular supply chain networks. It can help a manager determine the optimal levels of advertising expenditures, pricing, and the amount of circular activity as well as examine the impact of enhanced levels of the latter on capacity and profit allocation. The framework also guides strategic planning by pinpointing the optimal circularity level that maximizes economic benefits while controlling circular input adoption and processing costs.

In conclusion, applying the circularity variables in the coordinated three-level supply chain model results in economically feasible and optimal solutions. The findings provide conclusive evidence of effective and coordinated circular supply chain practices and strategies through pricing and marketing for overall sustainability and profit maximization. Future research may extend the present open loop circular input adoption model in several directions. First, the model can be expanded into a full closed loop supply chain framework by explicitly incorporating customer returns, collection centers, inspection, disassembly, remanufacturing, and recycling flow decisions. Second, stochastic or robust optimization can be used to represent uncertainty in demand, recycled input availability, input quality, and advertising effectiveness. Third, a multi period and multi product extension can capture dynamic circular input availability, changing customer preferences, and long term investment decisions. Finally, future studies may integrate environmental performance indicators, such as carbon emissions, waste reduction, and life cycle impacts, to develop a multi objective framework that jointly optimizes economic and environmental performance.
