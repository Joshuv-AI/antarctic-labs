Cadherin regulation of endoplasmic reticulum-plasma membrane contact sites
Authors: Sonam Lhamo, Navaneetha Krishnan Bharathan, William Giang, Kandice R Levental, Cory L Simpson, Stephanie E Zimmer, Thomas Saba, Julie Beaucousin, Mélina L Heuzé, Andrew P Kowalczyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYThe spatial organization and dynamics of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) govern when and where ER tubules engage with other organelles and the plasma membrane. We previously found that ER tubules are closely associated with desmosomes, but the mechanisms of ER recruitment to these adhesive intercellular junctions were unclear. Here, we demonstrate that recruitment of ER tubules to intercellular junctions is dependent upon E-cadherin association with α-catenin. During junction formation, adherens junctions and ER tubules appear nearly simultaneously at nascent cell–cell contacts, followed by desmosome formation. ER recruitment allows the formation of ER-plasma membrane contact sites (ER-PMCSs) and an assembly comprising adherens junctions, ER-PMCS, and desmosomes. Ablating adherens junctions disrupts this tripartite assembly and perturbs global lipid levels. Collectively, our findings identify cadherins as key organizers of ER-PMCS positioning and suggest that the cell–cell adhesion-organelle unit integrates cellular mechanical elements with plasma membrane homeostasis.

INTRODUCTION

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is a multifunctional organelle that regulates proteostasis, lipid homeostasis, and calcium signaling1,2 The ER performs many of these functions at specialized membrane contact sites where the ER is positioned within 10–25 nm of adjacent organelles or the plasma membrane to allow communication between cellular compartments.1–4 ER morphology and spatial distribution influence its functions. For example, ER sheets in the perinuclear region are sites of protein synthesis and trafficking, whereas the peripheral tubular ER network forms contact sites with various organelles and the plasma membrane. ER-plasma membrane contact sites (ER–PMCS) influence cell migration, calcium signaling, and lipid metabolism.5–8 However, the cellular mechanisms governing the assembly and spatial distribution of ER-PMCS are poorly understood.

Recent studies indicate that ER tubules associate with both cell–cell and cell-substrate adhesive interfaces.5,7,9 We previously identified close physical and functional associations between the ER and the desmosome, an adhesive cell–cell junction.10,11 Desmosomes are robust cell–cell adhesion complexes that are anchored to intermediate filaments and confer mechanical integrity to epithelial tissues and cardiac muscle.12–14 Volume electron microscopy (EM) of epithelial cells revealed ER tubules in close proximity to desmosomes, often making physical contacts with both keratin filaments and the outer dense plaque of desmosomes.10 Importantly, desmosomes and keratin filaments influence ER tubule organization and dynamics.10 However, disrupting desmosomes did not abolish ER localization at cell–cell interfaces, prompting us to investigate additional mechanisms that influence ER organization at intercellular contacts. Furthermore, it is currently unknown whether desmosomes or other adhesive cell–cell junctions influence contacts between the ER and the plasma membrane.

Adherens junctions are adhesion complexes that maintain tissue architecture and regulate epithelial cell morphology and differentiation.15,16 These junctions require classical cadherins, such as E-cadherin, and are coupled to the actin cytoskeleton through α- and β-catenin.17 Using a panel of A431 epithelial cells depleted of various adherens junction proteins, we show that E-cadherin association with α-catenin is both necessary and sufficient to recruit ER tubules to cell–cell interfaces. Furthermore, ER recruitment to cell–cell interfaces promotes the formation of ER-PMCS in close spatial and temporal proximity to both adherens junctions and desmosomes. High-resolution imaging indicates that adherens junctions, desmosomes, and ER form an adhesion-organelle assembly that organizes ER contacts across the plasma membrane. Interestingly, disrupting this complex by ablating adherens junctions altered global cellular lipid content. These findings reveal an unrecognized role for cadherins in organizing ER-PMCS, providing a means for localized regulation of plasma membrane lipids and architecture.

RESULTS

E-cadherin is required for ER localization to cell–cell interfaces

We recently determined that peripheral ER tubules form stable associations with desmosomes, and that disrupting desmosomes alters ER tubule dynamics.10 However, ER tubules persist at cell–cell interfaces in desmosome-deficient cells,10 suggesting that other intercellular complexes, such as adherens junctions regulate ER organization at these sites. To determine if adherens junctions regulate ER localization at cell–cell interfaces, we generated A431 epithelial cells stably expressing a fluorescently tagged pH-stable ER luminal marker, ER-StayGold, in both wild-type (WT) A431 and E/P-cad KO cells in which both E– and P-cadherin genes were ablated.18 We incubated cells with fluorescently tagged wheat germ agglutin (WGA-640R) to visualize the plasma membrane and assessed ER localization by live-cell spinning disk confocal microscopy. In WT cells, ER tubules fully extended to cell–cell interfaces (Figure 1A, top left) but remained more distant from the periphery at the outer edges of multi-cellular colonies (Figure 1A, arrow). In E/P-cad KO cells, ER tubules failed to extend to cell–cell interfaces in contrast to WT cells (Figure 1A, top right). Re-expression of WT Tag-red fluorescent protein (RFP)-tagged E-cadherin in the E/P-cad KO cells (E/P-cadKO+E-cad-WT) restored ER localization to cell–cell interfaces (Figure 1A, bottom). We quantified ER distribution by measuring the mean fluorescence intensity of ER-StayGold along a radial axis from the nuclear envelope to the cell edge. In all three cell lines, ER density was highest around the nucleus, where the ER is stacked into sheets. ER density progressively decreased toward the cell periphery, where the ER becomes more tubular. However, in E/P-cad KO cells, this decrease in peripheral ER tubules was more pronounced, and the phenotype was rescued by re-expression of WT E-cadherin (E/P-cad-KO+E-cad-WT) (Figure 1B). In the most peripheral region of the cell, E/P-cad KO cells exhibited a 2-fold reduction in ER density compared to WT cells (Figure 1C). These findings demonstrate that a classical cadherin is required for ER recruitment to cell–cell interfaces.

We postulated that E-cadherin recruits ER tubules to cell–cell interfaces through association with the microtubule or actin cytoskeleton since both of these networks influence ER organization.19–22 Adherens junctions associate with microtubules through p120-catenin23 and with actin filaments through α-catenin.24,25 We tested the role of p120-catenin and α-/β-catenin in ER recruitment using E-cadherin mutants deficient in p120-catenin binding (E-cad-GGG-RFP) or β-catenin binding (E-cad-ΔBCBS-RFP). Interestingly, the expression of E-cad-GGG in E/P-cad KO cells restored ER localization at cell–cell interfaces comparable to E-cad-WT, despite its lower expression level due to increased turnover of the cadherin in the absence of p120-catenin binding26–29 (Figures 1D and 1E). Moreover, the E-cad-GGG mutant associated with both β- and α-catenin and recruited these actin adaptor proteins to cell–cell interfaces (Figures S1B and S1C). In contrast to E-cad-GGG, E-cad-ΔBCBS, which cannot bind β- and α-catenin, failed to localize properly at cell–cell interfaces. Instead, E-cad-ΔBCBS often accumulated as cytoplasmic puncta (Figure S1), consistent with previous reports of trafficking defects in E-cadherin mutants unable to bind β-catenin.30 We confirmed by immunofluorescence the lack of p120-catenin and β-catenin association with E-cadherin in E-cad-GGG and E-cad-ΔBCBS cells, respectively (Figures S1A and S1B). Quantification of peripheral ER density indicated that E-cadherin accumulation at cell–cell contacts is required for ER recruitment to cell–cell interfaces and that E-cadherin interactions with p120-catenin are dispensable for this function (Figure 1F).

Adherens junctions recruit ER to cell–cell interfaces through E-cadherin association with α-catenin

α-catenin stabilizes adherens junctions against mechanical forces by linking E-cadherin to the actin cytoskeleton.31 To test whether α-catenin is required for ER recruitment to cell–cell interfaces, we imaged ER distribution in A431 cells lacking α-catenin (α-cat KO). Similar to our observations in E/P-cad KO cells, ER tubules were distal from cell–cell interfaces in the α-cat KO cells (Figure 2A), though E-cadherin and p120-catenin still localized to cell–cell interfaces, albeit at a lower level than in WT A431 cells (Figure S2A). Next, we directly tested the role of E-cadherin association with α-catenin in ER recruitment by expressing an E-cadherin-α-catenin-RFP chimeric protein that lacks the binding sites for both p120-catenin and β-catenin (E-cad-α-cat).32 In addition, we used a variant of this protein, E-cad-αcat-VBSm-RFP, which harbors an α-catenin deletion that eliminates the vinculin binding site. Both α-catenin and vinculin mediate interactions between the cadherin tail and the actin cytoskeleton, with vinculin being particularly important for the ability of adherens junctions to resist mechanical stress.32,33 We confirmed that vinculin was recruited to adherens junctions with the WT α-catenin chimera and that this interaction was absent in the E-cad-α-cat-VBSm cells (Figure S2B, arrows). Interestingly, the E-cad-αcat chimera restored ER localization to cell–cell interfaces in E/P-cad KO cells to a level comparable to E-cad-WT (Figures 2B–2D). In contrast, E-cad-α-cat-VBSm failed to fully restore peripheral ER localization. These results suggest that adherens junctions recruit ER to cell–cell interfaces through E-cadherin and α-catenin association, and that this process is partially dependent on vinculin.

Adherens junctions and ER tubules appear simultaneously at nascent cell–cell contacts, followed by desmosome formation

To define the temporal hierarchy of ER recruitment to cell–cell interfaces relative to adherens junctions and desmosomes, we performed a calcium switch assay in A431 cells stably expressing E-cad-HaloTag7 (adherens junction marker), mApple-VAPB (ER marker), and desmoplakin-EGFP (DP-EGFP, desmosome marker). Cells were initially cultured in low calcium (0.03 mM) overnight to ablate calcium-dependent adherens junctions and desmosomes. Cells were subsequently switched to high calcium (2.7 mM) to initiate junction assembly. Immediately after exposure to high calcium, cells were imaged every 60 s over a 1.5 h time course. Initially, ER tubules were distal from the plasma membrane (Figure 3A–0 min, Video S1). Upon cell–cell contact formation, E-cadherin puncta appeared, followed by extension of ER tubules to E-cadherin puncta within a few minutes (Figures 3A–4 min, 3B). In almost all instances, an ER tubule from the adjacent cell extended to the nascent E-cadherin puncta within five minutes, forming a mirror-image-like arrangement of ER tubules (Figure 3B). These events were followed by desmoplakin clustering near E-cadherin-positive puncta (Figure 3A–29 min). Similar observations were made in cells expressing fluorescently tagged α-catenin as an alternative adherens junction marker (Figure S3, Video S2). These results support previous studies demonstrating that desmosome assembly occurs after adherens junction formation.34–38 To resolve the timing of E-cadherin and α-catenin appearance at nascent cell–cell contacts, we imaged A431 cells expressing E-cadherin-mEmerald, mChilada-α-catenin, and HaloTag7-VAPB during calcium-induced junction assembly. In most instances, E-cadherin and α-catenin clustered together at nascent cell–cell contacts (Figure 3C–22 min, Video S3). The first ER tubules at newly formed cell–cell contacts appeared two minutes after E-cadherin and α-catenin (Figure 3C–26 min, 3D). Often, ER tubules in one cell appeared before ER tubules in the adjacent cell (Figure 3D). Collectively, these findings indicate that adherens junction assembly is followed closely by ER tubule recruitment. Desmosome assembly is subsequently initiated near adherens junctions at domains where ER tubules have formed paired arrangements at cell–cell contacts.

ER tubules contact the plasma membrane at cell–cell interfaces after adherens junctions assemble

The data above suggest that adherens junctions recruit ER tubules to cell–cell interfaces. However, it is not well understood if ER forms contact sites with the plasma membrane at cell–cell adhesive junctions. We used two complementary approaches to determine if ER tubules form contacts with the plasma membrane at cell–cell junctions. First, we analyzed 3D focused ion beam scanning electron microscopy (FIB-SEM) data of A431 cells with 4 nm isotropic voxel sizes.10 Using a deep learning tool (3D U-Net), we segmented the ER, plasma membrane, and desmosomes (Figures 4A–4E). We defined ER-PMCS as plasma membrane regions where the ER membrane is within 28 nm39. ER-PMCS were frequently observed adjacent to desmosomes at cell–cell interfaces, as well as at apical regions (Figures 4F–4L, Video S4), with half of all ER-PMCS being within ~480 nm of desmosomes (Figure 4M). In addition, ER-PMCS within 1 μm of desmosomes was larger than those located more distal to desmosomes (Figure 4N). These data demonstrate that bona fide ER-PMCS localize to plasma membrane domains adjacent to desmosomes at cell–cell interfaces.

In addition to volume EM analysis, we performed live-cell imaging of A431 cells to define the organization of ER and ER-PMCS at cell–cell junctions. We first examined the localization of ER tubules relative to both adherens junctions and desmosomes in A431 cells using super-resolution spinning-disk confocal microscopy. Imaging of cells co-expressing mChilada-α-catenin, HaloTag7-VAPB, and DP-EGFP revealed ER tubule tips associated with α-catenin-positive puncta at cell–cell interfaces (Figure 5A, arrows). Moreover, α-catenin and ER tubules often flanked the edges of desmosomes (Figure 5A, arrowheads). To determine if ER tubules near α-catenin represent ER-PMCS, we used mApple-tagged MAPPER, a well-characterized reporter of ER-PMCS.39 MAPPER consists of the transmembrane domain of the luminal ER calcium sensor STIM1, which anchors it to the ER membrane, and the polybasic motif of the G-protein Rit, which mediates binding to the plasma membrane when the ER is within 10–25 nm. At cell–cell interfaces, MAPPER organized into both punctate and elongated structures, similar to the morphologies of ER-PMCS observed by volume EM (Figure 4). Importantly, MAPPER localized adjacent to α-catenin and desmoplakin (Figure 5B). These data indicate that adherens junctions, desmosomes, and ER-PMCS organize into a tripartite assembly at cell–cell interfaces.

To assess the hierarchy of ER-PMCS formation relative to the assembly of adherens junctions and desmosomes, we conducted calcium switch experiments and live-cell imaging of A431 cells co-expressing HaloTag7-α-catenin, mApple-MAPPER, and DP-EGFP (Video S5). Before cell–cell contact formation, MAPPER puncta were located distal from the leading edge of cells and were predominantly at the cell-substrate interface (Figure 5C–45 min). Adherens junctions formed first, as defined by the appearance of α-catenin puncta at nascent cell–cell contacts (Figure 5C–47 min, arrows), followed by the appearance of MAPPER (Figure 5C–48 min, arrows). In ~66% of instances (n = 12/18 cell–cell contacts), MAPPER puncta appeared prior to desmoplakin clustering (Figure 5C–48 min; Figure 5D). However, in ~33% of instances (n = 6/18 cell–cell contacts), MAPPER appeared after desmoplakin puncta formation (Figure 5E). Together, these results indicate that adherens junctions assemble first, followed by ER tubule recruitment and subsequent and near-simultaneous formation of ER-PMCS and desmosomes.

Adherens junctions are required for ER-PMCS formation at cell–cell interfaces and regulate cellular lipid levels

The assembly hierarchy of adherens junctions, desmosomes, and ER-PMCS suggests that adherens junctions might govern the formation of ER-PMCS. To investigate this possibility, we performed live-cell confocal imaging of mApple-MAPPER in WT and E/P-cad KO cells. In WT cells, numerous MAPPER puncta were present at cell–cell interfaces (3.46/μm border ±0.36). In contrast, E/P-cad KO cells had fewer MAPPER puncta at cell–cell interfaces (1.65/μm border ±0.08) (Figures 6A and 6B). Quantitative analysis also confirmed a significant reduction in the average MAPPER puncta size at cell–cell interfaces in the E/P-cad KO cells (0.090 μm2 ± 0.0095) compared to WT cells (0.151 μm2 ± 0.022) (Figure 6C). These results indicate that adherens junctions are essential for the proper formation of ER-PMCS at cell–cell interfaces.

ER-PMCS comprise multiple proteins that tether the ER to the plasma membrane, including extended synaptotagmins (E-syts) and OSBP-related proteins (ORPs).39–41 To determine if these proteins mediate ER-PMCS formation at cell–cell interfaces, we expressed fluorescently tagged E-syt1, E-syt3, and ORP5 in E/P-cad KO cells stably expressing Ecad-RFP. Notably, all three proteins enriched near E-cadherin at cell–cell interfaces, albeit to varying degrees (Figures S4A and S4B). ORP5, an ER-PMCS lipid transport protein (LTP), showed the highest enrichment at cell–cell interfaces. These data indicate that bona fide ER-PMCS proteins localize at cell–cell interfaces.

Calmin is an ER resident protein that was recently reported to couple the ER to actin filaments near focal adhesions to regulate ER-PMCS spatial distribution at the cell-matrix interface.7 In A431 cells, endogenous calmin is primarily enriched at cell–cell interfaces and colocalized with E-cadherin (Figure 6D). To determine if calmin localization is dependent on adherens junctions, we localized calmin in WT, E/P-cad KO, and α-cat KO cells. In WT cells, calmin was enriched at cell–cell junctions (Figure 6E; n = 148/160 cells). In contrast, calmin was largely absent from cell–cell borders in E/P-cad KO and α-cat KO cells (Figure 6E; n = 16/80 E/P-cad KO cells; n = 0/97 α-cat KO cells). Moreover, calmin puncta at cell–cell borders in E/P-cad KO cells were much smaller and fewer than those in WT cells. Re-expression of E-cad-WT, E-cad-α-cat, or E-cad-α-cat-VBSm in E/P-cad KO cells restored junctional calmin localization (Figure 6F; n = 47/83, n = 53/60, and n = 22/27 cells, respectively). Together, these results indicate that both E-cadherin and α-catenin are necessary and sufficient to localize calmin to cell–cell junctions. We confirmed these findings using fluorescently tagged exogenous calmin in E/P-cad KO and E-cad KO cells rescued with E-cadherin. In E-cad-positive cells, mCherry-calmin localized to ER tubules and also concentrated at cell–cell interfaces (Figure S4C, arrows). By contrast, in E/P-cad KO cells, mCherry-calmin localized to the ER but failed to enrich at cell–cell interfaces (Figure S5C, arrowheads). We also observed calmin co-localization with E-cadherin in Caco-2 cells, a human colon adenocarcinoma cell line (Figure S4D, arrows).

Cells rely on both vesicular transport and non-vesicular transport at ER-membrane contact sites to deliver lipids to organelles and the plasma membrane.1,4 At the plasma membrane, ER-PMCS enable direct exchange of lipids between the ER and plasma membrane. Given that E/P-cad KO cells exhibited fewer and smaller ER-PMCS at cell–cell interfaces compared to WT cells (Figures 6A–6C), we next determined whether these differences result in altered cellular lipid composition. We performed mass spectrometry-based lipidomic analysis on whole cell lysates from WT, E/P-cad KO, and E/P-cad KO + E-cad-WT cells. Compared to WT cells, E/P-cad KO cells showed a marked decrease in several plasma membrane-enriched lipids, such as cholesterol, phosphatidylserine, and sphingolipids/glycosphingolipids. Re-expression of E-cad-WT in E/P-cad KO cells restored these lipid levels to varying degrees (Figure 6H; Table S1). Conversely, storage lipids, such as cholesterol esters, were significantly elevated in the E/P-cad KO cells, and this change was rescued by re-expression of WT E-cadherin (A431 = 3.70%, E/P-cad KO = 10.76%, E/P-cad KO + E-cad-WT = 6.34%). The biosensor is localized at the plasma membrane and cell–cell contacts. However, in the E/P-null cells, localization at cell–cell contacts was reduced.

We further assessed membrane lipids using genetically encoded fluorescently tagged lipid biosensors in A431 WT and E/P-cad KO cells. EGFP-GRAM-W, an accessible cholesterol sensor,42 enriched at cell–cell contacts relative to the basal membrane in A431 WT cells, and this enrichment was reduced in E/P-cad KO cells (Figures S5A and S5B). A phosphatidylserine biosensor, mRFP-Lact-c2,43 also accumulated at cell–cell contacts relative to the basal membrane. However, there was no significant difference in the phosphatidylserine biosensor enrichment at cell–cell contacts between WT and E/P-cad KO cells (Figures S5C and S5D). Collectively, these findings suggest that loss of adherens junctions perturbs cellular lipid content by impairing ER-PMCS formation and positioning.

ER-PMCS form predominantly at sites of cell–cell and cell-substrate adhesion

Epithelial cells have polarized plasma membrane domains where the cells adhere to the extracellular matrix at the base of the cell and form cell–cell junctions at the lateral plasma membrane domains. To assess how adherens junctions influence the spatial distribution of ER-PMCS, we quantified the axial position of individual MAPPER puncta across the entire height of the cell. In z stack images of mApple-MAPPER-expressing cells labeled with the plasma membrane marker WGA-640R, we classified MAPPER puncta into three categories: “Basal MAPPER” were defined as those located within 0.9 μm of the base of the cell; “Lateral MAPPER” were defined as those that colocalized with WGA signal at cell–cell interfaces; and “Apical MAPPER” were defined as those located near the top of the cell. In WT cells, basal MAPPER puncta were relatively round and smaller compared to lateral MAPPER puncta, which were larger and often appeared as linear streaks arranged in a pairwise fashion at both sides of adjacent cells (Figure 7A, arrows). In contrast to WT cells, E/P-cad KO cells displayed more basal MAPPER and fewer lateral or apical MAPPER puncta at cell–cell interfaces (Figure 7A, arrowheads). In both WT and E/P-cad KO cells, apical MAPPER puncta were substantially less abundant than those at cell-substrate and cell–cell interfaces. We assessed the distribution of MAPPER across plasma membrane regions in each cell line by calculating the percentage of MAPPER at lateral and apical domains as a proportion of total MAPPER puncta (Figures 7B and 7C). In WT cells, 67% ± 2.8 of MAPPER puncta were lateral/apical, whereas in E/P-cad KO cells, only 30% ± 6.2 of MAPPER puncta were lateral/apical (Figure 7C). These data indicate that ER-PMCS preferentially form at cell–cell interfaces when adherens junctions are present. In contrast, loss of desmosomes in A431 cells in which desmoglein-2 is ablated using CRISPR-Cas9 (Dsg KO) did not impair MAPPER puncta formation nor their distribution (Figure S6). Together, these findings demonstrate that adherens junctions regulate the polarized distribution of ER-PMCS across plasma membrane regions.

(F) Working model of the stepwise process of adherens junction assembly, ER tubule recruitment, ER-PMCS formation, and desmosome assembly at nascent cell–cell contacts. Adherens junctions recruit ER to newly formed cell–cell contacts and promote the formation of ER-PMCS. Desmosomes assemble adjacent to adherens junctions at sites of paired ER arrangement. Together, adherens junctions, ER, ER-PMCS, and desmosomes mature into an integrated junctions-organelle assembly.

Vinculin localizes to adherens junctions under tension and reinforces cadherin-actin linkages.33,44 Further, actomyosin tension influences ER localization at the plasma membrane.45,46 Given that the E-cad-α-cat-VBSm chimera protein, which is unable to associate with vinculin, failed to fully rescue ER localization to cell–cell interfaces (Figures 2B–2D), we asked if ER-PMCS preferentially localize to plasma membrane domains enriched in vinculin. In mApple-MAPPER expressing A431 cells, vinculin was prominently localized at the basal membrane and apico-lateral cell–cell interfaces (Figure S7). In contrast, MAPPER puncta were primarily located at basal and lateral cell–cell interfaces (Figure S7). These data suggest that ER and ER-PMCS preferentially localize at cell–cell interface regions under low tension.

Our data in 2D cell culture systems indicate that ER-PMCS primarily form at regions of the plasma membrane involved in cell–cell and cell-substrate adhesion. We tested this possibility further using an organotypic model of stratifying TERT-immortalized human epidermal keratinocytes (N/TERT-2G).47 We transduced these keratinocytes with mApple-MAPPER and Dsg1-GFP to assess ER-PMCS and desmosome localization, respectively. In 3D organotypic cultures, MAPPER localized adjacent to desmosomes at cell–cell interfaces within the same epidermal layer (Figure 7E, bottom row) as well as between basal and suprabasal keratinocytes (Figure 7E, top row, arrows). Similar to our observations in 2D cell culture, mirror image-like arrangements of MAPPER were also observed at lateral cell–cell interfaces (Figure 7E, bottom, arrow heads). Collectively, these data reveal a structural relationship between adhesive cell–cell junctions and the ER, whereby adherens junctions govern the formation and positioning of contact sites between the ER and the plasma membrane.

DISCUSSION

The spatial organization and dynamics of ER membranes dictate where and when the ER contacts other organelles and the plasma membrane. In various cell types, ER contacts with the plasma membrane regulate lipid and calcium flux to influence a wide range of physiological processes, such as cell migration, cell-matrix adhesion, and plasma membrane repair.5–8 We demonstrate that adherens junctions are essential for recruiting the ER to cell–cell interfaces, driven by associations between E-cadherin and α-catenin. ER recruitment to cell–cell interfaces by adherens junctions promotes interactions between the ER and the plasma membrane, resulting in a tripartite assembly comprising ER-PMCS, adherens junctions, and desmosomes. Disruption of adherens junctions destabilizes the organelle-junction assembly and perturbs cellular lipid homeostasis.

Previous EM analysis of hepatocytes revealed extensive ER tubule association with cell–cell interfaces, a feature conserved across several epithelial cell models and implicated in regulating hepatocyte differentiation.9 These findings are consistent with our volume EM studies demonstrating an intimate association of ER tubules with desmosomes.10 However, the mechanisms regulating ER positioning at cell–cell interfaces remain poorly understood. We show that the E-cadherin-α-catenin complex is necessary and sufficient to recruit ER tubules to cell–cell interfaces, whereas cadherin association with p120-catenin is dispensable (Figures 1 and 2). α-catenin interaction with vinculin further enhances ER recruitment to adhesive cell junctions. For example, an E-cadherin-α-catenin chimeric protein lacking both the p120 binding site and the β-catenin binding domain was sufficient to restore ER localization to cell–cell interfaces in E/P-cadherin KO cells. However, a variant of this chimeric protein that is deficient in vinculin binding was not fully effective in restoring ER localization to cell–cell interfaces (Figure 2B). These observations suggest that the link between E-cadherin, the actin cytoskeleton, and actomyosin-generated tension is crucial for ER positioning at cell–cell interfaces. These findings are consistent with a previous study suggesting that ER targeting to adherens junctions depends on local actomyosin tension at adherens junctions.45

To further understand the relationship between peripheral ER tubule localization and intercellular junction formation, we determined the spatiotemporal hierarchy of ER recruitment to nascent cell–cell junctions. Prior to cell–cell contact formation, ER tubules are positioned distally from lamellipodia (Figure 3A), similar to ER organization in migrating cells.7 Upon cell–cell contact formation, ER tubules are rapidly recruited to nascent junctions, often appearing simultaneously with adherens junctions but before desmosomes (Figure 3). As intercellular junctions mature and increase in number, ER tubules progressively accumulate at cell–cell interfaces. Lamellipodia contain densely packed and branched actin filaments. Once cells make contact, adherens junctions associate with and reorganize actin filaments through α-catenin.24,48,49 Actin reorganization during intercellular junction formation could facilitate ER tubule localization to cell–cell interfaces.

The recruitment of ER tubules to nascent cell–cell interfaces raised the possibility that these tubules interact with plasma membrane domains proximal to adhesive cell junctions. We utilized two complementary approaches to assess ER-PMCS localization. Volume EM revealed that ER-PMCS predominantly form adjacent to desmosomes, and those contact sites were larger in size than ER-PMCS distal to desmosomes (Figure 4). Similarly, MAPPER fluorescent puncta were frequently observed adjacent to both adherens junctions and desmosomes (Figure 5B). Live-cell imaging indicated that ER forms contacts with the plasma membrane after adherens junction assembly, followed closely by the appearance of desmoplakin puncta (Figures 5C–5G). As junction assembly progresses, adherens junction and desmosome components segregate into mature adhesion complexes adjacent to ER-PMCS, resulting in a tripartite assembly that mediates both cell–cell adhesion and ER-plasma membrane communication (Figure 7F).

This study identifies adhesive cell junctions as a regulator of both the formation and positioning of ER-PMCS. WT A431 cells exhibited more and larger MAPPER puncta at cell–cell interfaces than E/P-cad KO cells lacking adherens junctions (Figures 6A–6C). The majority of MAPPER puncta in WT cells appeared at cell–cell interfaces, whereas in E/P-cad KO cells, the majority of MAPPER puncta appeared at cell-matrix interfaces (Figure 7C). These results indicate that ER-PMCS preferentially form at plasma membrane regions that contain adhesive cell–cell or cell-matrix contacts. Similarly, ER-PMCS formed at cell–cell interfaces between cells of the basal and suprabasal layers in 3D organotypic epidermal cultures of stratified keratinocytes (Figure 7J). These findings indicate that cell–cell and cell-substrate structures are important regulators of ER contact with the plasma membrane.

Multiple proteins tether the ER to the plasma membrane. We have found that E-syt1, E-syt3, and ORP5 all localize adjacent to E-cadherin at cell–cell interfaces, albeit to varying degrees (Figure S4). Notably, we find that calmin, a protein recently shown to link ER to the actin cytoskeleton at focal adhesions, co-localizes with E-cadherin at cell–cell interfaces (Figure 6D). Calmin enrichment at cell–cell contacts was dependent on E-cadherin and α-catenin (Figures 6E and 6F). Additional studies are needed to catalog the full repertoire of ER-PMCS proteins that localize to cell–cell junctions and the molecular interactions that mediate co-assembly of these complexes.

ER-PMCS maintain calcium and membrane lipid homeostasis.3,50 Lipidomic analysis of whole cell membranes from WT and E/P-cad KO cells revealed that several lipid species, including phosphatidylserine, cholesterol, and sphingolipids, were downregulated in E/P-cad KO cells (Figures 6D and 6E; Table S1). Reduced cholesterol levels were also observed in an α-catenin KO epithelial cell line.51 Interestingly, these lipids are enriched in the plasma membrane and are delivered to the plasma membrane at ER-PMCS.52,53 Using a fluorescently tagged, genetically encoded sensor for cholesterol, we find that cholesterol accumulates at cell–cell interfaces relative to the basal plasma membrane and that this enrichment is reduced in E/P-cad KO cells (Figure S5). The levels of storage lipids, such as cholesterol esters, were elevated in E/P-cad KO cells. When cholesterol accumulates in the ER, cells store the excess cholesterol as cholesterol esters inside lipid droplets.54,55 The increase in cholesterol esters in E/P-cad KO cells suggests an accumulation of cholesterol in the ER, which could be due to defects in cholesterol trafficking from the ER to the plasma membrane upon loss of ER-PMCS at cell–cell interfaces. These results suggest that adherens junctions regulate cellular lipid homeostasis by determining the positioning and size of ER-PMCS. However, it is also plausible that adherens junctions influence lipid metabolism through mechanisms independent from ER-PMCS.

Desmosome assembly is synchronized with ER-PMCS formation, as revealed by coordinated MAPPER and desmoplakin appearance at nascent cell–cell interfaces (Figures 5C and 5D). Furthermore, ER-PMCS were observed to be adjacent to desmosomes in 3D organotypic epidermal cultures (Figure 7E). Both plasma membrane lipids and calcium signaling regulate cell–cell junction formation,51,56–61 highlighting a plausible role of ER-PMCS in coordinating junction formation and epithelial architecture, including in stratified tissues. Notably, deficiency of the ER calcium pump sarco-ER calcium ATPase 2 (SERCA2) leads to desmosome defects that result in skin fragility.62 Studies have demonstrated that loss of SERCA2 function impairs desmosome formation by perturbing desmosomal protein trafficking, desmosomal gene expression, and protein kinase C-α signaling.60,63–65 Our study raises the possibility that the defect in desmosomes associated with SERCA2 mutations could also be explained by the physical proximity of ER tubules and ER-PMCS with desmosomes and adherens junctions. Furthermore, we recently reported that disrupting desmosomes results in an ER stress response in keratinocytes.10,11 Collectively, these findings suggest that ER association with the plasma membrane at adhesive junctions integrates cellular mechanics, stress signaling, and lipid metabolism to influence plasma membrane physiology and tissue homeostasis.

Limitations of the study

Our analysis of the temporal sequence of adherens junction, ER, and desmosome appearance at cell–cell interfaces was conducted with relatively limited temporal resolution. In particular, the experimental design limited the resolution of ER-PMCS and desmosome formation kinetics. Our studies revealed a role for adherens junctions in regulating both ER-PMCS formation and plasma membrane-enriched lipid levels, and we observed that several LTPs accumulate at cell–cell interfaces. However, our studies do not rule out the possibility that adherens junctions influence other lipid regulatory mechanisms, such as vesicular transport or metabolic pathways that might also influence membrane lipid homeostasis. Additional studies are needed to reveal molecular interactions that mediate associations between the cadherin-catenin complex and ER membranes, as well as the broader pathophysiological roles for this association.

Organotypic epidermal culture

Human organotypic epidermal cultures were generated as previously described.76 Organotypic cultures were generated from N/TERT-2G47 cells virally transduced with human Dsg1-GFP and mApple-MAPPER. For virus transduction, N/TERT-2G cells were cultured in virus-containing medium containing 4 μg/mL polybrene for 60 min at 37°C. Cells were then rinsed in PBS and refed in their native medium and passaged upon reaching 70%–80% confluency. Transduced cells were differentiated at an air-medium interface using CnT-Prime Epithelial 3D Airlift Medium (CELLnTEC #cnT-PR-3D) atop J2-3T3 fibroblast-populated type 1 collagen rafts.

To make fibroblast-collagen rafts, J2-3T3 fibroblasts were trypsinized, re-suspended in DMEM+10% FBS, counted, centrifuged for 5 min at 200 g, and the supernatant was aspirated. The cell pellet was re-suspended in 1/10 the final volume required (2 mL per organotypic culture) using 10X reconstitution buffer (1.1 g of NaHCO3 plus 2.39 g of HEPES in 50 mL of 0.05 N NaOH) and 1/10 the final volume of 10X DMEM was then added. High-concentration rat tail type 1 collagen was added to achieve a final concentration of 4 mg/mL with sterile water added to adjust to the final volume. The collagen/fibroblast slurry was mixed by several inversions, then pipetted into the top chamber of a transwell insert housed within a deep 6-well culture plate. The slurry was allowed to solidify in a tissue culture incubator at 37°C for 60 min. The polymerized fibroblast rafts were submerged in DMEM+10% FBS and incubated at 37°C for at least 24 h.

Next, N/TERT-2G cells were trypsinized and re-suspended in DMEM+10% FBS. Cells were counted and the required volume was centrifuged at 200 g for 5 min. The supernatant was aspirated, and the cell pellet was re-suspended in DMEM+10% FBS to a final volume of 2 mL per organotypic culture. The medium was aspirated from both the upper and lower chambers of the transwell without disturbing the collagen rafts. N/TERT-2G cells were seeded atop the raft (2 million cells) and DMEM+10% FBS was added to the bottom chamber to submerge the insert and raft; cultures then were incubated at 37°C for 24–48 h at which point the medium was removed from both chambers. Finally, 10 mL of 3D Airlift Medium was added only to the bottom chamber of the transwell, reaching the underside of the raft and exposing the overlying N/TERT-2G cell monolayer to the air. Cultures were fed 3D Airlift Medium every other day for 7 days prior to performing live spinning disk confocal imaging.

METHOD DETAILS

Plasmid transfection

Plasmid transfection was performed using DharmaFECT kb DNA transfection reagent. Cells were seeded on #1.5H coverslips in 4-well plate or 8-well #1.5H chambered cover glass (for live-cell imaging) the day prior to transfection. For 4-well plates, 0.2 μg plasmid and 1.5 μg DharmaFECT kb were each diluted separately in 50 μL serum-free media (SFM), then combined and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. The resulting mixture was further diluted with 500 μL culture media and added dropwise to cells. For 8-well plates, plasmid DNA and DharmaFECT KB were each diluted in 25 μL of SFM instead of 50 μL. The culture medium was replaced at 16 h after transfection.

Indirect immunofluorescence

Cells were grown to at least 50% confluency on #1.5H glass coverslips in 4-well plates. For WGA labeling, cells were incubated with 2 μg/mL WGA-640R in Hanks’ balanced salt solution containing calcium and magnesium (HBSS+) at 37°C for 10 min and then washed with 1X HBSS+. Subsequent steps were performed at room temperature (RT). Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS with calcium and magnesium (PBS+) for 10 min and washed 3X with 1X PBS+. Cells were permeabilized and blocked in 1X PBS+ containing 0.1% Triton and 1% BSA (blocking buffer) for 15 min, incubated in primary antibodies in blocking buffer for 1 h, followed by 3 washes in 0.1% Triton in 1X PBS+ (wash buffer). Cells were then incubated in secondary antibodies in blocking buffer for 45 min, followed by 3 washes in wash buffer. Coverslips were mounted onto glass microscope slides using ProLong Glass mounting medium and cured overnight.

The following antibody dilutions were used: anti-E-cadherin (1:1000), anti-p120 catenin (1:250), anti-β-catenin (1:1000), anti-α -catenin (1:1000), and anti-vinculin (1:300), Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit (1:1000), Alexa Fluor 647 donkey anti-rabbit (1:1000), Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse (1:1000), and Alexa Fluor 647 goat anti-mouse (1:1000).

Live-cell imaging

Cells were seeded on 8-well chambered #1.5H cover glass. For WGA labeling, cells were incubated with 2 μg/mL WGA-640R in HBSS+ or HBSS without calcium and magnesium (HBSS−; for low to high calcium switch assay) at 37°C for 10 min. Cells were then washed with HBSS+ or HBSS−. Cell were imaged in DMEM with high glucose and HEPES and without phenol red (live-cell imaging media) with 10% FBS.

Nikon 100x/1.49 NA Apo TIRF oil immersion objective with its correction collar optimized for imaging at 37°C was used. Images were taken in 12-bit with high gain (12-bit sensitive’) and with ‘Standard’ readout mode. Coarse alignment for simultaneous dual-channel imaging was achieved using spinning disk pinhole alignment until pixel-perfect overlap was achieved in the center of field of view for the two cameras. Physiological temperature and CO2 levels (5%) were achieved with Tokai Hit stage top incubation system (model STXF-WELSX-SET).

For confocal imaging of live organotypic epidermal cultures, N/TERT-2G transduced with fluorophore-tagged constructs were seeded onto fibroblast-collagen rafts. After 7 days, stratified cultures were prepared for imaging by removing the entire culture from the transwell using a scalpel and transferring into a 35 mm glass-bottom dish. Images of live organotypic epidermis were acquired on a Hamamatsu ORCA-FusionBT sCMOS camera using a Yokogawa CSU-W1 spinning disk confocal system on a Nikon Ti2 microscope. Samples were illuminated using 488, 561, 640 nm laser excitation lines, and fluorescence was detected using a 60 ×1.2 NA water objective (Nikon) with standard emission filters. Multi-color z stack images were acquired using a piezo motor with a step size of 200 nm.

Calcium switch assay

A431 cells expressing various fluorescent reporters were seeded on 8-well chambered #1.5H cover glass at a density of ~20,000 cells/well in DMEM (high glucose) with 10% TET-tested FBS and 1X antibiotic-antimycotic solution. Around 24 h prior to imaging, media was aspirated and cells were washed once with 1X HBSS- (without calcium and magnesium). Cells were then incubated in calcium-free DMEM supplemented with 200 mM L-glutamine, 10% Chelexed TET-tested FBS, 1X antibiotic-antimycotic solution, and 30 μM calcium chloride (“low calcium media”) for 18-24 h to induce junction disassembly. For DP-EGFP-expressing cells, the low calcium media was additionally supplemented with 4 μg/ml doxycycline at this time to induce expression of DP-EGFP, which is under the control of a Tet-On promoter. The next day, low calcium media was aspirated, and cells were incubated in high calcium imaging media (phenol red-free DMEM with high glucose and HEPES, 10% TET-tested FBS, 1X antibiotic-antimycotic solution, 2.7 mM calcium chloride) supplemented with a HaloTag ligand for 15 min at 37°C. High calcium imaging media was prepared by increasing the calcium concentration of DMEM from 1.8 mM to 2.7 mM using 2.5 M calcium chloride stock solution. For experiments in Figures 3A, 3C, and 5C, cells were incubated with 13 nM JFX650 HaloTag ligand (Promega). For experiments in Figure S3, cells were incubated with either 13 nM JFX650 HaloTag ligand (Promega) or 250 nM JF657 HaloTag ligand (Janelia). During this 15 min incubation step, the dish was mounted onto the spinning disk confocal microscope and scanned for regions of interest where two or more cells were positioned adjacent to each other. Once multipoint coordinates were set, time-lapse imaging was initiated.

Microscopy

Live-cell imaging was performed using a Nikon spinning disk confocal microscope. Fixed-cell imaging was performed using either a Nikon spinning disk confocal microscope or Zeiss AxioObserver.Z1 microscope. The Nikon Ti2-E microscope is equipped with a Yokogawa CSU-X1 spinning disk confocal unit, LUNF XL laser unit, Nikon Perfect Focus System, Z piezo stage, motorized XY stage, two sCMOS cameras (ORCA-Fusion BT, Hamamatsu Corp.), and two fast filter wheels with elements controlled through hardware-triggering through Nikon’s National Instruments Breakout Box. The acquisition software was NIS-Elements (v5.30.02, v5.30.04, v5.30.06, and 5.42.03). The polychroic mirror within the Yokogawa CSU-X1 unit is a Semrock Di01-T405/488/568/647. Single-emission filters (Chroma ET525/36m, Chroma ET605/52m, and Chroma ET705/62m) were used with the 488 nm, 561 nm, and 640 nm lasers. The Zeiss AxioObserver.Z1 microscope is equipped with ZEN Blue acquisition software (v2.3). Images were acquired using Zeiss Plan-Apochromat 63x/1.4 NA oil DIC immersion objective and an X-Cite 120 LED Boost with the following filters: Zeiss filter set 43 HE (excitation: BP 550/25 (HE), beamsplitter: FT 570 (HE), emission: BP 605/70 (HE)) and Zeiss filter set 50 (excitation: BP 640/30, beamsplitter: FT 660, emission: BP 690/50). Images were captured with an Axiocam 506 monochrome camera.

Super-resolution imaging system

Super-resolution live-cell images of α-catenin, VAPB, and desmoplakin were acquired on a Nikon Ti2-E microscope equipped with a Yokogawa CSU-W1 spinning disk unit in super-resolution (SoRa) mode. Cells were maintained at 37°C and 5% CO2 with the Tokai Hit stage top incubation system. Images were taken with a 60X/1.49 NA Apo TIRF oil objective with 2.8× increased magnification and were deconvolved in NIS-Elements.

Image processing

Live-cell snapshots of ER-StayGold and mApple-MAPPER were denoised using Denoise.ai software on NIS-Elements. Calcium switch images were denoised using Noise2Void v0.2.1 (N2V), a self-supervised deep learning algorithm. Denoising was performed on single channel and single timepoint images. After re-combining into a hyperstack, datasets were corrected for lateral chromatic aberration using NanoJ’s channel registration. N2V was used with a TensorFlow-DirectML on a Windows10 workstation with an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU.

ImageJ was used to process images, adjust for brightness and contrast using lookup tables (LUTs), split by channels and timepoints, and displayed as montages.

Quantification of ER distribution

Quantification of ER distribution was done on maximum intensity projection images. Images were flat field corrected to remove non-uniform illumination. Regions of interest (ROIs) were manually drawn along the plasma membrane at cell edges and nuclear borders based on WGA-640R and ER-StayGold labeling, respectively. Each cell was divided into 24 concentric rings from the nucleus to the plasma membrane using a modified version of Andrew Moore’s ImageJ macro for radial interpolation (https://github.com/andmoo91/Radial-Intensity-Analysis). Data plotting was performed using seaborn (0.13.2) and matplotlib (3.8.3). To measure ER distribution in cells, the mean fluorescence intensity of ER-StayGold in each ring was divided by the mean fluorescence intensity of ER-StayGold in the perinuclear ring to normalize for differences in ER-StayGold intensities between different cells and biological replicates. To measure peripheral ER density, the ratio of the mean fluorescence intensity of ER-StayGold in the outermost ring (ring #24) to the perinuclear ring (ring #2) was calculated.

Quantitative analysis of MAPPER puncta

Quantification of the number and the area of individual MAPPER punctum at cell–cell borders was done on maximum intensity projection images that were denoised using Denoise.ai software on NIS-Elements. MAPPER images were segmented using the Pixel Classification workflow in ilastik. Briefly, features were drawn to distinguish background from the foreground. The resulting binary images were overlaid with the corresponding WGA-640R fluorescence images. ROIs were manually drawn on WGA-labeled cell–cell borders. An in-house ImageJ macro was used to quantify the number and area of MAPPER puncta within the ROIs, and the maximum Feret diameter (a proxy for length) of individual ROIs. The number of MAPPER puncta at cell–cell borders was calculated by dividing the total number of MAPPER puncta in each ROI by the length of the ROI. The mean area of MAPPER puncta was calculated by dividing the total area of all MAPPER puncta in each ROI by the number of MAPPER puncta within that ROI.

To measure the axial/z axis position of MAPPER puncta, z stack images of MAPPER were segmented using the 3D Pixel Classification workflow in ilastik and processed by an in-house ImageJ macro. 26-connectivity was used to create separate 3D objects, and centroid positions were measured using MorphoLibJ (v1.6.4).

Quantitative analysis of lipid probe localization

Quantification of EGFP-GRAM-W and mRFP-Lact-c2 localization were done on live-cell confocal images. Cells with similar expression levels of the constructs were used for analysis. A segmented ROI line was drawn at a cell–cell border z-plane, and another line was drawn at the basal membrane. After subtracting the camera offset value from the images, the ratio of the mean intensity of the construct at the cell–cell border to the mean intensity at the basal membrane was used to determine the cell–cell border enrichment of the lipid probes.

ER–PMCS analysis in FIB-SEM data

ER–PMCS analysis was done on previously acquired FIB-SEM data on A431 epithelial cells with 4 × 4 × 4 nm3 voxels.10 Analysis was performed primarily at regions containing cell–cell interfaces. 3D segmentations of ER, plasma membrane, and desmosomes were performed using a 3D U-Net, configured by nnU-Net v2.2 combined with human proofreading.74 Defining an ER–PMCS as a region of the PM where the ER is within 28 nm, we dilated the ER segmentation and applied a 26-connected component analysis to the union of PM and dilated ER to generate distinct objects using Dragonfly software, Version 2024.1 for Windows.

Lipidomic of whole cell membranes

Cells were cultured to 95% confluency and washed 3x with cold 1X PBS without Ca2+ and Mg2+ (PBS −). All the subsequent steps for homogenizing cells were done on ice or at 4°C. Cells were scraped in 1mL 1X PBS- using cell scraper and collected into Eppendorf tubes. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 200 rcf for 5 min, and pellets were washed with 1X PBS−. Centrifugation and wash were repeated 1X. The cell pellet was resuspended in 300 μL 1X PBS- and the cell number was counted using hemocytometer to obtain similar number of cells for lipid comparisons between different cell lines. Cell concentration of 3000 cells/μL were prepared in 400 μL of 1X PBS-. Cells were then homogenized with 25 strokes of 25G 5/8 needle, fast-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and their detailed lipid compositions were analyzed by shotgun electron spray ionization with tandem MS-MS (ESI-MS/MS) by Lipotype, GmbH (Dresden, Germany) and detailed below.

Lipid extraction for mass spectrometry lipidomic

Mass spectrometry-based lipid analysis was performed by Lipotype GmbH (Dresden, Germany) as described.77 Lipids were extracted using a two-step chloroform/methanol procedure. Gangliosides were extracted from the water phase of the preceding chloroform/methanol extraction with a solid phase extraction protocol. Samples were spiked with internal lipid standard mixture containing: cardiolipin 14:0/14:0/14:0/14:0 (CL), ceramide 18:1;2/17:0 (Cer), diacylglycerol 17:0/17:0 (DAG), hexosylceramide 18:1;2/12:0 (HexCer), dihexosylceramide 18:1;2/12:0 (DiHexCer), Globoside 3 18:1;2/17:0 (Gb3), GM3-D3 18:1;2/18:0 (GM3), GM1-D3 18:1;2/18:0 (GM1), lyso-phosphatidate 17:0 (LPA), lyso-phosphatidylcholine 12:0 (LPC), lyso-phosphatidylethanolamine 17:1 (LPE), lyso-phosphatidylglycerol 17:1 (LPG), lyso-phosphatidylinositol 17:1 (LPI), lyso-phosphatidylserine 17:1 (LPS), phosphatidate 17:0/17:0 (PA), phosphatidylcholine 15:0/18:1 D7 (PC), phosphatidylethanolamine 17:0/17:0 (PE), phosphatidylglycerol 17:0/17:0 (PG), phosphatidylinositol 16:0/16:0 (PI), phosphatidylserine 17:0/17:0 (PS), cholesterol ester 16:0 D7 (CE), sphingomyelin 18:1;2/ 12:0;0 (SM), sulfatide d18:1;2/12:0;0 (Sulf), triacylglycerol 17:0/17:0/17:0 (TAG) and cholesterol D6 (Chol). Gb4 was estimated semi-quantitatively, using the Gb3 standard. After extraction, the organic phase was transferred to an infusion plate and dried in a speed vacuum concentrator. 1st step dry extract was re-suspended in 7.5 mM ammonium acetate in chloroform/methanol/propanol (1:2:4; V:V:V) and 2nd step dry extract in 33% ethanol solution of methylamine in chloroform/methanol (0.003:5:1; V:V:V). All liquid handling steps were performed using Hamilton Robotics STARlet robotic platform with the Anti Droplet Control feature for organic solvents pipetting.

MS data acquisition

Samples were analyzed by direct infusion on a QExactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) equipped with a TriVersa NanoMate ion source (Advion Biosciences). Samples were analyzed in both positive and negative ion modes with a resolution of Rm/z=200 = 280000 for MS and Rm/z=200 = 17500 for MSMS experiments, in a single acquisition. MSMS was triggered by an inclusion list encompassing corresponding MS mass ranges scanned in 1 Da increments. Both MS and MSMS data were combined to monitor CE, Chol, DAG and TAG ions as ammonium adducts; LPC, LPC O-, PC, PC O-, as formiate adducts; and CL, LPS, PA, PE, PE O-, PG, PI, and PS as deprotonated anions. MS only was used to monitor LPA, LPE, LPE O-, LPG, and LPI as deprotonated anions; Cer, HexCer, and SM as formiate adducts and cholesterol as ammonium adduct of an acetylated derivative.

MS data analysis and post-processing

Data were analyzed with in-house developed lipid identification software based on LipidXplorer. Data post-processing and normalization were performed using an in-house developed data management system. Only lipid identifications with a signal-to-noise ratio >5 and a signal intensity 5-fold higher than in corresponding blank samples were considered for further data analysis.

The following lipid names and abbreviations are used: ceramide (Cer), cholesterol, sphingolipids (SL), glycosphingolipids (GSL), diacylglycerol (DAG), phosphatidic acid (PA), phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylinositol (PI), phosphatidylserine (PS), and their respective lysospecies (lysoPA, lysoPC, lysoPE, lysoPI, and lysoPS) and ether derivatives (PC O-, PE O-, LPC O-, and LPE O-).

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Quantifications were performed using ImageJ macros, and statistical analyses were calculated using GraphPad Prism (v11.0.0). Statistical tests, sample sizes, and p-values are plotted in figures or figure legends. The sample size (n) represents the number of cells, cell–cell borders, or cell–cell contact formations, as indicated in figure legends. Experimental means from 3 independent experiments were used for statistical analysis. One-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test or unpaired Welch’s t-tests were used to calculate significance.
