Direct microbiota profiling of apheresis-associated products for microbiological insights in cell therapy
Authors: Dong Woo Shin, Sujin Oh, Yun Ji Hong, Kyoung Un Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cellular therapies require rigorous prevention of bacterial contamination during cell collection, manufacturing, and infusion. We characterized 16 S rRNA profiles in blood-derived specimens obtained during leukapheresis. Leukapheresis donors provided five specimen types: buffy coats (BCs), whole-blood plasma (WBP), apheresis plasma stored at room temperature for 24 h (AP24) and 72 h (AP72), and saliva. Species-level identification was performed using next-generation sequencing-based 16 S rRNA analysis and a database-weighted method. In total, 40 samples from eight donors were analyzed. Plasma specimens (WBP, AP24, and AP72) exhibited higher alpha diversity than saliva (Shannon index, p < 0.05). Beta diversity analysis identified three distinct clusters corresponding to BC, plasma specimens, and saliva (permutational multivariate analysis of variance, p = 0.001). Streptococcus oralis subsp. tigurinus was predominant across all specimens types, Bifidobacterium kashiwanohense predominated in blood-derived specimens, and Enhydrobacter aerosaccus was observed exclusively in plasma specimens. Skin swab culture performed before and after venipuncture site disinfection exhibited no bacterial growth post-disinfection, suggesting that skin-derived carryover is unlikely to fully explain the detected microbial DNA signals. This study provides microbial DNA profiles of various blood-derived specimens obtained during leukapheresis. These findings provide preliminary reference information that may assist interpretation of molecular microbial signals in cellular therapy manufacturing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57771-0.

Introduction

Blood-derived cellular products are widely used in clinical practice across a range of therapeutic modalities, including transfusion, the use of human cell, tissue, and cellular and tissue-based products (HCT/Ps) such as hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and approaches based on advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs) including chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy1,2. To ensure the safety and efficacy of these interventions, stringent measures must be implemented to prevent the transmission of infectious agents throughout the entire continuum of cell collection, processing, and administration3,4. Unlike viral screening, which is conducted on donor whole blood, bacterial contamination testing is performed on blood products5. This strategy enables the assessment of the actual product designated for infusion, facilitates the detection of contamination introduced during manufacturing, and improves the sensitivity of bacterial detection compared to whole-blood-based testing.

Traditional culture-based methods are time-intensive, potentially delaying treatment, and exhibit limited ability to detect fastidious or non-culturable microorganisms6. Broad molecular detection approaches, including pan-bacterial and pan-fungal PCR assays, have been explored as complementary or alternative strategies for sterility testing7. However, interpretation of molecular signals remains challenging because these methods may detect DNA derived from non-viable organisms as well as background microbial DNA originating from reagents, consumables, or intrinsic low-biomass biological signals8. In this context, establishing reference microbial DNA profiles from non-contaminated starting materials may facilitate differentiation between baseline molecular signals and findings that may represent clinically meaningful contamination in cell therapy manufacturing environments. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables comprehensive characterization of microbial communities, including taxa that are difficult or impossible to detect using conventional culture techniques6; however, appropriate interpretation requires sufficient taxonomic resolution to contextualize detected organisms in relation to existing knowledge on bacterial contamination of blood products3,4. Improved species-level classification may also support development of standardized validation materials, such as mock microbial communities, for molecular-based microbial profiling in cell therapy products.

Considering its advantages, NGS has been increasingly used to characterize the microbial composition of various body environments. Prior studies investigating the microbiomes of distinct anatomical sites, particularly saliva and blood, emphasize the need for further studies into the significance of microorganisms detected in blood-derived specimens. The oral microbiome, has been associated with a range of systemic diseases, including cardiovascular disease and certain malignancies9,10. Comparative analyses of microbiomes across different body sites, such as saliva and blood, have indicated potential inter-site microbial interactions10,11. A notable study suggests that there is no distinct or resident microbiome in human blood in the strict ecological sense of a stable, interacting microbial community. However, this conclusion followed extensive computational and statistical decontamination to distinguish biological signal from background noise, and the study acknowledged that bacteria from other body sites may transiently circulate in the bloodstream12. When microbial DNA is detected in blood-derived cellular products, it becomes essential to discern whether these findings reflect clinically significant microbial contamination or transient/background molecular signals detectable in blood-derived specimens. To facilitate this distinction, the establishment of reference microbial DNA profiles from non-contaminated blood-derived products is imperative.

We characterized the microbiome commonly present in various blood-derived samples collected during the manufacturing process of cell therapy products and offered insights into microbiological considerations relevant to cell therapy.

Methods

Participants

This study was conducted as part of a larger research initiative focused on the standardization of leukapheresis for cell therapy product manufacturing. Donor eligibility criteria included individuals aged 20–39 years who were non-smokers, had abstained from alcohol consumption on the day prior to donation, and were not taking any medications at the time of evaluation. On donation day, vital signs were required to meet: systolic blood pressure 90–180 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure < 100 mmHg, body temperature ≤ 37.5℃, and heart rate 50–99 beats per min. A complete blood count (CBC) was performed using an automated hematology analyzer (XN-1000; Sysmex, Kobe, Japan). Leukapheresis was initiated after confirming a hemoglobin concentration of ≥ 12.0 g/dL.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (approval number: B-2210-787-301; 4 Oct 2022). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant for study participation and data publication. This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Specimens

Leukapheresis was performed using the Spectra Optia Apheresis System (Terumo BCT, Lakewood, CO, USA). Approximately 100 mL of mononuclear cells and 50 mL of plasma were collected for the broader study on standardizing leukapheresis. Of this, 10 mL of apheresis plasma (AP) was used for the present study. To investigate microbial changes during room temperature storage, the AP was divided into two aliquots and stored under conditions identical to those used for platelet components13. One aliquot was stored in a platelet agitator at 20–24 ℃ for 24 h (AP24) and the other for 72 h (AP72).

Whole blood was collected via a three-way stopcock connected to the venipunctured apheresis kit, following disinfection of the stopcock hub surface. Buffy coats (BCs) and whole-blood plasma (WBP) were separated by centrifugation: 300 g for 20 min, then 2,330 g for 10 min. For infectious marker screening, serum was collected in serum-separating tubes through a diversion pouch and tested for the following infectious markers: hepatitis B surface antigen, anti-hepatitis C virus antibodies, anti-human immunodeficiency virus antibodies, anti-human T-lymphotropic virus antibodies, and venereal disease research laboratory test.

Saliva was collected by passive drooling into a sterile tube after > 30 min fasting.

16S rRNA-targeted sequencing

16S rRNA gene sequencing was conducted by the PHiCS Institute (Seoul, Republic of Korea). Microbial DNA was extracted from 1 mL of each specimen using the QIAamp DNA Microbiome Kit (QIAGEN, Venlo, The Netherlands). DNA concentration and purity were assessed using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the 16 S rRNA gene were amplified by PCR using KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix PCR kits (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The primers used for amplification were 519 F (5′-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Nextera XT DNA library preparation kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and pooled to a final loading concentration of 8 pM. Paired-end (2 × 300 bp) sequencing was performed using the MiSeq platform (Illumina).

Species-level taxonomic classification

Demultiplexed reads were processed using a pipeline based on the Divisive Amplicon Denoising Algorithm-2 (DADA-2), implemented within the QIIME2 platform14,15. Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were generated through a series of steps including quality filtering and trimming, deduplication, ASV inference, paired-end merging, and chimera removal. For taxonomic profiling, ASVs were classified against the SILVA rRNA database (99% identity threshold) using a pre-trained scikit-learn naïve Bayes classifier from the QIIME2 q2-feature-classifier plugin16,17. To enhance species-level classification, which is often challenging with 16 S V3–V4 sequences due to insufficient resolution, environment-specific taxonomic weights were incorporated. These weights were computed using the q2-clawback plugin18, based on microbial abundance data derived from blood and saliva samples obtained from healthy individuals in a previous study conducted by our group19. The taxonomic classifier was retrained separately for each sample type: blood-specific weights were applied to BC, WBP, and AP samples, whereas saliva-specific weights were used for saliva samples. This approach allowed for more accurate and context-specific species-level classification of microbial communities.

Diversity analysis

Data analyses not previously described were conducted using R software (version 4.1.2; R Development Core Team, Vienna, Austria). QIIME2 artifacts were imported into the R environment via the qiime2R package and transformed into phyloseq objects using the phyloseq package20,21. To mitigate artifactual biases, feature tables were normalized by rarefaction prior to diversity analyses. Alpha and beta diversity metrics were calculated using phyloseq21. Alpha diversity was assessed using observed feature counts, Shannon entropy, and Pielou’s evenness. Beta diversity, reflecting inter-sample differences in microbial composition, was quantified using the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity index. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was performed to visualize patterns of microbial community dissimilarity across samples. To evaluate the influence of specimen type on microbial community structure, permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was conducted using the vegan package22. Permutations were constrained within individual subjects to account for the paired study design.

Differential abundance testing

For differential abundance analysis, we applied the Analysis of Compositions of Microbiomes with Bias Correction 2 (ANCOM-BC2), which uses a log-linear regression framework to estimate and adjust for unknown sampling fractions, correcting compositional biases23. To account for the paired study design, where multiple specimen types were collected from the same individuals, subject-specific effects were incorporated into the model formula to control for within-subject variability. Taxa were considered differentially abundant between specimen types based on a false discovery rate threshold of < 0.05.

Statistical analysis

Relative abundance was calculated to visualize the distribution of microbial taxa across samples. Species comprising < 2% of the total microbial community were aggregated and collectively designated as “Others (< 2%)”, with the combined proportion representing their cumulative relative abundance. Associations between categorical variables in contingency tables were assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. To compare alpha diversity metrics between specimen types, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied due to the non-parametric nature of the data. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. To facilitate visualization of inter-donor and intra-donor variability across specimen types, a heatmap of species-level relative abundance was generated.

Skin swab culture

To assess skin disinfection efficacy, swabs were taken from the antecubital area using cotton swabs moistened with 0.9% sterile saline, before and after 2% chlorhexidine disinfection. Each swab was immersed in 500 µL of saline to elute microorganisms, and the suspension was spread onto two blood agar plates and two chocolate agar plates. One set was incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 3 days; the other anaerobically at 37 °C for 5 days. Microbial identification was performed using matrix assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; Sirius, Bruker corporation, Billerica, MA, USA).

Results

Microbial diversity analysis

In total, eight male participants (D1–D8) aged 24–33 years were included. All had CBC values within the normal range and tested negative for infectious disease markers (Table 1). Five different specimen types were collected from each subject for 16 S rRNA gene sequencing and subsequent microbial diversity analysis. In the alpha diversity analysis, plasma specimens (WBP, AP24, and AP72) exhibited significantly higher Shannon indices compared to saliva (p = 0.008, 0.008, and 0.016, respectively), whereas the increase observed in BC was not statistically significant (p = 0.195) (Fig. 1a). Beta diversity analysis revealed three distinct clusters corresponding to BC, plasma specimens, and saliva (PERMANOVA R2 = 0.244 and p = 0.001; Fig. 1b). Saliva differed significantly from all other specimen types (p = 0.001 for comparisons with BC, WBP, and AP24; p = 0.003 for AP72). BC samples differed significantly from AP24 (p = 0.002) and AP72 (p = 0.003), whereas the comparison with WBP approached statistical significance (p = 0.07). No significant differences were observed among the plasma specimens (WBP, AP24, and AP72; all pairwise p-values > 0.05).

16S rRNA profiles across donors and specimen types

Relative abundance patterns across donors and specimen types were examined using a species-level heatmap (Fig. 2) together with genus- and species-level stacked bar plots (Supplementary Fig. S1 and S2). Streptococcus oralis subsp. tigurinus was detected in all participants and across most specimen types, with particularly high relative abundance in saliva specimens (mean 33.0%, range 8.8–51.0%). Bifidobacterium kashiwanohense was consistently detected across most participants in blood-derived specimens (BC, WBP, AP24, and AP72), ranging from 2.3% to 8.4%, indicating inter-donor similarity. Enhydrobacter aerosaccus showed marked inter-donor variability and was characteristically enriched in plasma specimens (WBP, AP24, and AP72), where high relative abundance was observed in participants D6–8 (10.2–33.4%), while remaining low or undetectable in other participants. In saliva samples, five species were predominant: Streptococcus oralis subsp. tigurinus, Neisseria flavescens, Neisseria subflava, Porphyromonas sp., and Streptococcus salivarius subsp. salivarius. While Streptococcus oralis subsp. tigurinus showed consistently high relative abundance across donors, the remaining four species exhibited inter-donor variability in relative abundance. Collectively, these patterns indicate intra-donor variability in microbial composition across specimen types.

To determine the predominant bacterial species in each specimen type, taxa with a relative abundance of ≥ 2% in at least three out of the eight individuals were selected for analysis (Table 2). Streptococcus oralis subsp. tigurinus was prevalent across all specimen types and was detected in all eight individuals in the AP24, AP72, and saliva samples. Although Bifidobacterium kashiwanohense and Pseudomonas sp. were most frequently observed in BC and were prevalent in blood-derived samples (BC, WBP, AP24, and AP72), they were absent from saliva. Porphyromonas sp. was predominantly detected in saliva samples. Enhydrobacter aerosaccus was exclusively observed in plasma specimens. Because inclusion in Table 2 was based on a predefined relative abundance threshold (≥ 2%) and the number of individuals meeting this criterion, changes in detection frequency across time points (AP24 to AP72) do not necessarily reflect temporal changes in absolute microbial load. Such variation may reflect biological phenomena, including microbial community restructuring, but may also arise from threshold effects whereby shifts in relative abundance across taxa alter the number of donors exceeding the predefined criterion.

Microbial dynamics during room temperature storage

Using normalized feature count analysis, we assessed whether specific microorganisms proliferate over time under storage conditions mimicking those of platelet products, namely, maintenance at room temperature (20–24 °C) with continuous agitation13. Normalized feature count analysis did not demonstrate consistent temporal increases in microbial DNA signal across taxa during room-temperature storage of plasma specimens. Although Neisseria subflava was below the detection limit in WBP and AP24 and showed increases at AP72 (9, 7, and 4 normalized feature counts in three donors, respectively), these observations were insufficient to support a meaningful temporal increase.

Effectiveness of skin disinfection in preserving sample integrity

Pre-disinfection skin swab cultures revealed the presence of diverse bacterial taxa, including Staphylococcus hominis, Micrococcus luteus, and Bacillus megaterium (Table 3). Following disinfection, no bacterial growth was observed in any subjects, emphasizing the effectiveness of procedure in eliminating skin flora and preventing contamination of blood-derived specimens.

Discussion

Our study offers valuable insights into the microbial composition of blood-derived specimens that may serve as source materials for cellular therapy products. By employing NGS of the 16 S rRNA gene across multiple blood-derived specimens and saliva, we observed distinct microbial diversity patterns associated with different specimen types. Although species-level resolution using 16 S rRNA sequencing is inherently constrained, the application of a weighting strategy improved taxonomic classification, facilitating meaningful comparisons with existing literature and broader contextual interpretation. A previous study has indicated that peripheral blood does not harbor a microbiome in the strict sense of a stable, self-sustaining microbial community12. Notably, this conclusion was reached following rigorous computational and statistical decontamination approaches, although the study also acknowledges that microorganisms from other body sites can temporarily appear in the bloodstream, a finding that has been repeatedly documented in the literature24–27. In contrast, the present study focused on comparative characterization of microbial DNA signal patterns across leukapheresis-derived specimen types under standardized analytical conditions, rather than inferring a core blood microbiome. In our study, skin swab cultures obtained prior to disinfection revealed the presence of common skin commensals, which are recognized as typical contaminants in blood culture collections28. Conversely, no bacterial growth was observed in any post-disinfection skin swab samples (Table 3), demonstrating the effectiveness of disinfection procedures. These observations support the inference that microorganisms detected in blood-derived specimens collected after proper skin disinfection are less likely to be explained solely by skin-derived carryover. Our data suggest the presence of reproducible baseline microbial DNA signal patterns across donors and specimen types. Several taxa, including Streptococcus oralis subsp. tigurinus and Bifidobacterium kashiwanohense, were consistently detected in multiple donors, indicating structured background signal patterns rather than purely stochastic noise. Specimen-type-associated differences were also observed, such as enrichment of Enhydrobacter aerosaccus in plasma specimens. Although viable and non-viable organisms cannot be distinguished by this approach, these findings suggest that microbial DNA signal profiles may partly reflect baseline biological or procedural characteristics. Together, these data provide preliminary reference information that may assist interpretation of molecular sterility testing results.

Unexpectedly, alpha diversity was higher in blood-derived specimens than in saliva (Fig. 1a). This observation may partly reflect differences in the distribution of relative abundances, as saliva samples in the present study were dominated by a limited set of highly abundant oral taxa. However, because blood-derived specimens represent low-biomass samples, the contribution of technical factors such as background DNA contamination from reagents or stochastic amplification effects cannot be excluded8. Further studies incorporating appropriate negative controls or other quality control strategies may help to clarify the biological relevance of these findings.

Infections associated with the infusion of blood-derived cellular products, including platelet components, hematopoietic stem cells, and CAR T-cells, are of critical concern and are subject to stringent regulatory oversight29–31. Platelet components are stored at 20–24 °C to preserve cellular viability and functional integrity. However, this temperature range supports bacterial proliferation, rendering platelet products more susceptible to transfusion-transmitted bacterial infections compared to red blood cells stored at 4 °C5,13. Consequently, the shelf life of platelet components is limited to 5–7 days, and robust contamination control measures are mandated. These include culture-based testing, rapid detection assays, and pathogen reduction techniques29. In a similar vein, sterility testing is being conducted to detect bacterial contamination in hematopoietic stem cell products30. CAR T-cell therapies are also subject to rigorous quality control under Current Good Manufacturing Practice regulations to prevent microbial contamination during the manufacturing process31. Traditionally, concerns regarding microbial contamination during the collection of cellular products have centered on the inadvertent introduction of skin commensals, whereas our study offers a novel perspective by emphasizing potential endogenous or non-skin-derived microbial sources. In transfusion and cell therapy settings, bacterial contaminants of concern most commonly include skin commensals such as Staphylococcus, Cutibacterium, and Bacillus species, as well as Gram-negative organisms, particularly members of the order Enterobacterales3. In the present study, the taxa identified showed limited overlap with these commonly reported contaminants, which may in part reflect effective skin disinfection prior to specimen collection. The implications of this limited overlap require further investigation to clarify the relevance of these molecular findings in the context of transfusion medicine and cell therapy manufacturing.

Based on prior studies, the bacterial species identified in Table 2 are predominantly associated with specific anatomical or environmental habitats, including gut-associated taxa (Bacteroides spp., Bifidobacterium kashiwanohense, Dialister invisus, Escherichia coli, Okadaella gastrococcus, Parabacteroides spp., and Ruminococcus sp.)32–38, oral cavity inhabitants (Neisseria subflava, Porphyromonas sp., and Streptococcus spp.)39–42, skin-associated species (Enhydrobacter aerosaccus)43, and the environmentally associated Pseudomonas sp44., with some degree of habitat overlap among taxa. These findings are consistent with a previous study suggesting that bacteria detected in the bloodstream are likely transient and derived from commensal communities in other body sites12. These observations provide ecological context for interpreting taxa detected in blood-derived specimens from healthy donors.

Neisseria subflava has been identified as a microbial species present in the blood of healthy individuals, with evidence of DNA replication activity within the bloodstream12 and documented involvement in cases of bacteremia45–47. In our study, Neisseria subflava was frequently observed in plasma specimens as well as in saliva (Table 2). Neisseria subflava is generally regarded as a transient presence in the blood of healthy individuals, likely originating from the oropharygeal microbiota12. Neisseria subflava and other non-pathogenic Neisseria species can harbor and exchange antimicrobial resistance genes in the oropharynx, raising concerns about the potential transfer of resistance traits to pathogenic species such as Neisseria gonorrhoeae39,40. Further studies are needed to clarify the biological and clinical relevance of this species in blood-derived materials used for cellular therapies.

Our study has several limitations. First, a notable limitation arises from the methodological differences between culture-based analysis and NGS-based 16 S rRNA sequencing. Although culture-based studies were conducted on skin swabs to validate decontamination, culture analyses were not extended to other sample types analyzed by NGS. Conversely, NGS was not performed on skin swab samples, creating a methodological gap. This reciprocal limitation restricts the ability to fully integrate NGS data with the results from culturable microorganisms.

Second, NGS-based 16 S rRNA analysis is unable to differentiate between live and dead bacteria. Although potential proliferation was assessed using normalized feature counts in our study, additional methods, such as RNA-based detection or viability-specific staining followed by flow cytometry, may be required48. These approaches would provide a more accurate assessment of bacterial viability, which is particularly critical when evaluating the clinical implications of microbial contamination in cell therapy products.

Third, because blood-derived specimens represent low-biomass samples, background microbial DNA originating from reagents, extraction kits, or laboratory environments may influence sequencing results. In the present study, extraction blanks and no-template PCR controls were not included, and formal computational contaminant identification approaches were not applied. Therefore, the contribution of reagent- or kit-derived background signals cannot be fully excluded. To partially mitigate the influence of low-abundance background taxa, analyses focused on species exceeding a predefined relative abundance threshold (≥ 2%), representing a pragmatic strategy conceptually similar to previously described abundance threshold-based filtering approaches49. Furthermore, the taxa commonly detected in our study were not consistent with the profiles typically reported for reagent-derived “kitome” contaminants8, suggesting that the detected signals cannot be explained solely by such contamination. Nevertheless, future studies incorporating negative controls and statistical decontamination strategies would further strengthen biological interpretation of microbial signals detected in blood-derived specimens.

Finally, the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of our findings. The study included samples from eight donors, which may appear limited initially. However, with five different specimen types collected from each individual, a total of 40 samples were analyzed. The strength of our study lies in this comprehensive analysis of multiple specimen types, including BCs, WBP, AP collected longitudinally, and saliva. This approach facilitated a detailed investigation of microbial profile variations across different specimen types within the same individual, mitigating the confounding effects of inter-individual variability and bolstering the robustness of our specimen-specific observations. These findings provide a valuable foundation for future studies involving larger, more diverse cohorts.

In conclusion, NGS-based 16 S rRNA analysis characterized microbial DNA detected in multiple blood-derived specimen types obtained during leukapheresis. These findings provide preliminary reference information that may assist interpretation of molecular microbial signals in cellular therapy manufacturing.
