Effect of early analgesia with erector spinae plane block to reduce ventilation after severe chest trauma: the multicentre randomised controlled TARGET study protocol
Authors: Pierre Bouzat, Anais Adolle, Tobias Gauss, Bruno Perreira, Jean-Michel Constantin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBlunt chest trauma is frequently associated with rib fractures, and inadequate pain control may lead to respiratory complications and prolonged ventilatory support. In spontaneously breathing patients, pain impairs coughing and secretion clearance, promoting atelectasis and respiratory failure, while in mechanically ventilated patients, it may delay weaning. Current French guidelines recommend intravenous multimodal analgesia as first-line therapy followed by thoracic epidural or paravertebral block if pain remains uncontrolled. However, these techniques have limited applicability in the trauma setting. The erector spinae plane (ESP) block is a simpler ultrasound-guided regional analgesia technique with fewer contraindications. Although retrospective data suggest potential respiratory benefits, its efficacy has not been evaluated in randomised controlled trials. This study aims to assess whether early initiation of a continuous ESP block reduces ventilatory support duration after blunt chest trauma.Methods and analysisThe EffecT of eARly analGesia with ErecTor spinae plane block (TARGET) trial is a multicentre, superiority, randomised controlled trial including adult patients (≥18 years) with blunt chest trauma and at least three rib fractures on thoracic CT scan requiring intensive care unit admission. Participants are randomised to receive either early continuous ESP block initiated within 6 hours of admission or standard intravenous multimodal analgesia with escalation to thoracic epidural or paravertebral block if indicated. The primary outcome is the number of days alive and free from invasive or non-invasive curative ventilation within 30 days after randomisation or hospital discharge. Randomisation is stratified by centre and age (<65 or ≥65 years). Investigators and outcome assessors are not blinded. Introduction

Blunt chest trauma is a common pattern of severe trauma patients and previous studies report a mortality rate of up to 25% following severe thoracic trauma.1 Chest wall injuries such as rib fractures or flail chest are common lesions in this context. The diagnosis of these injuries relies on the use of thoracic CT scanning included in the whole-body CT scan. Patients with fractured ribs may require invasive or non-invasive ventilation (NIV) during their intensive care unit (ICU) stay and are at risk for several complications including pneumonia, atelectasis and acute respiratory distress syndrome.2 Older age, greater number of fractured ribs, concomitant injuries and chronic lung disease are the main documented risk factors for complications.3

Pain control is one of the key goals for the management of chest trauma. Pain limits coughing efficiency and secretion clearance, thereby potentially leading to progressive atelectasis, loss of functional residual capacity and, ultimately, respiratory distress. Pain control can improve ventilatory function and prevent respiratory complications. Diverse strategies have been proposed to alleviate pain after chest trauma. Recent French guidelines underline the benefit of regional analgesia with epidural analgesia (EA) or paravertebral block (PVB) in association with intravenous multimodal analgesia within the first 12 hours.4 However, these techniques necessitate experienced anaesthetic medical staff and are often contraindicated in case of post-traumatic coagulopathy. As a result, only few patients benefit from these blocks in the early phase of chest trauma management and pain relief often consists of intravenous multimodal analgesia. In a recent French survey (Blondonnet et al5), for 70% of the respondents, patients with trauma with a theoretical indication of regional anaesthesia did not receive EA or PVB for the following reasons: the ICU had no experience of regional anaesthesia (62%), no anaesthesiologist-intensivist working in the ICU (46%), contraindications (27%), ignorance of the French guidelines (19%) and no protocol available (13%).5 As a result, there is a need for a simpler regional anaesthesia that may improve pain relief and may influence outcome such as the duration of mechanical/NIV in the ICU.

Erector spinae plane (ESP) block is an alternative technique to control pain after thoracic surgery.6 It consists in depositing the local anaesthetic in the fascial plane, deeper than the erector spinae muscle at the tip of the transverse process of the vertebra. It can be done continuously with a dedicated catheter that allows intermittent boli with a pump. It relies on a high-volume, low concentration anaesthetic, usually ropivacaine 2 mg/mL (from 20 to 40 mL). The ESP block works at the origin of the spinal nerves, based on cadaveric and contrast studies. Cadaveric studies demonstrated that block at T5–T7 level is sufficient to have unilateral sensory block from T1 to L3.7 It can be also done bilaterally in case of bilateral injuries. This block is less invasive with fewer contraindications as compared with EA or PVBs. In particular, compared with PVB, there is no risk of pleural injury and compared with EA, it can be performed with coagulation disorders.8

This technique has gained interest to control postoperative pain after cardiothoracic9 or breast surgeries.10 After chest trauma, only a few studies have assessed the efficacy11 and the safety8 of ESP block. Recently, in 79 patients with traumatic rib fractures, ESP block restored respiratory capacity thanks to efficient analgesia without major complication.12 In this study, 77% of the patients received an ESP block catheter. The catheter remained sited until the acute pain team deemed the pain could be managed orally (mean duration, 3.7 days; range 0.6–9.3 days). Incentive spirometry volumes doubled from baseline during the first 24 hours with a mean increase of 545 mL. This effect was maintained over 72 hours. Pain scores were also significantly reduced. The serratus anterior plane block (SPB) is another fascial plane technique that has been studied in rib fractures; however, its analgesic coverage is limited to the lateral chest wall (T2–T9) and it does not reliably cover posterior or medial rib fractures. In a prospective randomised trial comparing ESP block versus SPB in multiple rib fractures, ESP block provided superior analgesia and greater improvement in diaphragmatic excursion. Given this evidence and the broader anatomical coverage of the ESP block, the TARGET trial uses the ESP block as the experimental intervention. Nevertheless, there is no multicentre randomised controlled trial regarding the effect of early continuous ESP block on clinically relevant outcomes such as ventilator-free days (VFD).

We hypothesise that patients with rib fractures after chest trauma may benefit from early continuous ESP block in the trauma bay to decrease the use of invasive or NIV. The targeted population is the category of chest patients with trauma at risk of pulmonary complications: adult patients with three or more rib fractures with an indication of ICU admission.4 We will not include patients with an extrapulmonary indication of mechanical ventilation such as patients with traumatic brain injury or spinal cord injury at the cervical or thoracic levels. We expect the early ESP block strategy to be superior to the standard strategy according to French recommendations regarding the number of VFD including non-invasive and invasive ventilation. From a public health standpoint, the use of early continuous ESP block may change current guidelines regarding chest trauma management.

This manuscript was written in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines and the trial methodology follows recommendations from the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.13

Trial design

TARGET is an open, pragmatic, multicentre, superiority randomised controlled trial and is conducted in 14 ICUs of different French hospitals. The participating centres are: CHU Grenoble Alpes (Grenoble), Hôpital Lariboisière (Paris), Hôpital de Beaujon (Paris), Hôpital La Pitié-Salpêtrière (Paris), Hôpital Européen George Pompidou (Paris), CH Annecy Genevois, CHU Bordeaux, CHU de Clermont-Ferrand, Hôpital Lyon Sud (Pierre-Bénite), Hôpital d’instruction des armées Percy (Clamart), Hôpital d'Instruction des Armées Sainte Anne (Toulon), CHU de Lille, CHU Toulouse -Rangueil, CHU Toulouse—Purpan and CHRU Tours. No financial or other incentives are offered to participants for taking part in this study.

Randomisation

Patients will be screened for enrolment as soon as possible after eligibility has been confirmed. Screening is performed by the on-call anaesthetist or intensivist present at the time of trauma bay admission, who reviews the CT scan findings and clinical status to assess inclusion and exclusion criteria. All consecutive eligible patients admitted during the study period will be screened and a screening log is maintained at each centre to record all screened patients and reasons for non-inclusion. To promote adequate enrolment, the coordinating team at CHU Grenoble Alpes monitors inclusion rates monthly across all sites. Centres with lower-than-expected recruitment are contacted by the clinical research coordinator for case-by-case review. Regular steering committee meetings (at least every 6 months) review enrolment progress and address any site-specific barriers to recruitment. No additional recruitment incentives (financial or otherwise) are offered to sites or participants. Enrolled patients will be randomised on a 1:1 basis in permuted blocks (variable block sizes of 2, 4 and 6) by local investigators using a dedicated, password-protected, SSL-encrypted website (CSOnline, Clinsight) accessible 24 hours a day, 7 days a week to allow immediate and concealed allocation. The centralised randomisation system automatically conceals allocation until the moment of enrolment, ensuring adequate allocation concealment. The randomisation sequence is generated and held by the data management team at CHU Clermont-Ferrand; local investigators and clinical staff are unaware of block sizes. Following enrolment in the TARGET Trial, each participant will be given a unique trial number and a case report form (CRF) will be completed by the local team. The randomisation sequence will be stratified (1) per centre and (2) according to age (<65 or ≥65 years). As the need to randomise patients during a short time-frame mandates that the randomisation is as simple as possible, we have elected not to use minimisation or to stratify on additional potential confounders. However, thoracic injury pattern (bilateral injury, flail chest, number of ribs) and ventilation status at enrolment are prespecified covariates in the adjusted analyses. The statisticians conducting the primary analysis will remain blinded to treatment allocation until the statistical analysis plan is finalised and the database is locked.

Trial intervention

Patients in the control group will be managed according to the French recommendations for chest trauma management.4 Patients will receive intravenous multimodal analgesia in the trauma bay. Thoracic EA or continuous PVB will be performed as soon as possible within the first 12 hours postadmission if the pain is not controlled by intravenous multimodal analgesia according to our national guidelines.

Patients in the experimental group will have a continuous ESP block within the first 6 hours postadmission. If necessary, patients will receive intravenous multimodal analgesia in the trauma bay. ESP catheter will be used from the trauma bay to the ICU as long as necessary with a dedicated infusion pump (CADD-Solis infusion pump, SMITH Medical with a bolus mode). If chest wall injuries are bilateral, bilateral ESP catheters can be done. In case of continuous ESP block failure at the early phase (defined by more than three attempts within the first 6 hours postadmission, incidence <5% of the total experimental group), patients will be switched to the control group. In case of accidental catheter removal during ICU stay, a new introduction of ESP catheter is allowed if pain is not controlled by intravenous multimodal analgesia. All protocol deviations, including crossovers and treatment switches, will be systematically documented in the eCRF with the date, reason and clinical circumstances. Crossover from experimental to control is anticipated to be infrequent (<5%), based on prior observational data; however, it will be accounted for in the per-protocol analysis. In the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis, patients will be analysed according to their randomised allocation regardless of actual treatment received, which may dilute the observed treatment effect but preserves the integrity of the comparison. The trial is not expected to maintain complete treatment separation, as crossover is an inherent feature of pragmatic clinical trials; this is acknowledged as a limitation and will be addressed in sensitivity analyses. In the control arm, patients may receive ESP block or other regional techniques as rescue analgesia if standard therapy is insufficient, per institutional practice; this will also be documented.

ESP block procedure

After patients are positioned optimally (sitting or lateral decubitus), the affected area is identified along with the target transverse process. Given that local anaesthetic spreads cranially and caudally from the point of injection, this is usually the transverse process most central to the affected rib levels. The ultrasound transducer is placed in a longitudinal parasagittal orientation, about 3 cm lateral to the spinous processes, allowing for visualisation of adjacent transverse processes (TP) in an in-plane approach. These are recognisable as flat, squared-off acoustic shadows with only a very faint image of the pleura visible.

After correct TP identification, an 18-gauge echogenic needle is inserted using an in-plane, cranial-to-caudal approach to contact the bony shadow of the TP with the tip deep to the fascial plane of the erector spinae muscle. The correct location of the needle tip is confirmed by injecting 0.5–1 mL of normal saline 0.9% and observing linear fluid spread lifting the erector spinae muscle off the tip of the TP. Once the fascial plane is recognised, the needle is removed and the catheter is inserted through the needle sheath. Correct catheter location is confirmed by bolusing 2–3 mL of normal saline 0.9%. Following confirmation of correct catheter tip location, 30 mL of ropivacaine 2% is injected and cranial and caudal spread of local anaesthetic can be visualised. In case of bilateral ESP catheters, initial bolus is only 25 mL by catheter. After initial bolus, a dedicated infusion pump (CADD-Solis infusion pump, SMITH Medical with a bolus mode) is used to provide a continuous infusion of ropivacaine (2 mg/mL) 1 mL/hour with a 25 mL bolus every 6 hours.

The ESP catheter is kept in place as long as it is providing analgesic benefit. Factors such as respiratory status, ambulation, oral intake of medications and chest tube removal are taken into account when deciding when to remove the ESP catheter. The catheter may also be removed if the site becomes infected, local anaesthetic leaks or at the patient’s request.

ESP training

Centres have been selected because of their experience in managing patients with severe trauma. All participating centres are level I trauma centres (centres de traumatologie de niveau 1, labelled by French health authorities) with an anaesthesiologist designated as trauma team leader available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. Each centre has confirmed a minimum annual caseload of severe trauma admissions sufficient to recruit the allocated number of participants. In case of inexperienced medical staff for this technique, the anaesthesiologist in charge of the trauma bay can be trained by a local anaesthesiologist involved in thoracic surgery. Outcomes

Primary outcome

The primary endpoint is alive and VFD within the first 30 days or hospital discharge, whichever occurred first. Ventilation includes invasive and non-invasive curative ventilation but does not consist of prophylactic NIV, that is, NIV in the absence of acute respiratory failure defined as the presence and persistence for more than 30 minutes of hypoxaemia (PaO2 <60 mm Hg when breathing room air or PaO2 <80 mm Hg when breathing 15 L/min of oxygen or SpO2 <90% when breathing room air), plus either a respiratory rate higher than 30 breaths/min or clinical signs suggestive of intense respiratory muscle work and/or laboured breathing, such as use of accessory respiratory muscles, paradoxical motion of the abdomen or intercostal retraction.14 The primary endpoint is a commonly reported composite outcome measure in acute respiratory failure trials.15 We believe that this outcome is relevant since the tested intervention affects both the need for ventilation support and ventilator duration in the same direction as mortality. After chest trauma, VFDs appropriately capture the combined effect of continuous ESP block on both mortality and the duration of invasive/NIV given that inadequate analgesia is associated with pulmonary complications that induce morbimortality.

Statistical analysis

All analyses will be performed with the use of Stata software (V.15, StataCorp, College Station) before the breaking of the randomisation code, according to International Conference on Harmonization-Good Clinical Practice guidelines. All analyses will be detailed in a separate statistical analysis plan, which will be published before the randomisation of the last participant.

The primary analysis will be conducted in the ITT population being all randomised patients except those who withdraw consent for data use. We will perform a per-protocol analysis on the primary outcome. The criteria for including patients in the ITT and in the per-protocol populations, respectively, are provided below.

Baseline variables will be reported as numbers and percentages for categorical variables and medians with IQRs for continuous variables. According to the CONSORT 2010 statement, group differences in baseline variables will not be compared using significance testing unless specifically requested by peer reviewers.

Primary analysis

To assess the effect of early analgesia with ESP block after chest trauma on the number of days alive and without invasive or NIV, the primary analysis will be performed by non-parametric Mann-Whitney according to Yehya et al.15 Non-survivors will be assigned the worst possible number of days alive and without invasive or NIV (ie, 0 days). Results will be expressed as median difference and 95% CI.

Secondary analyses

Multiple linear regression analysis will be used to identify relevant baseline covariates in the ITT population with anticipated relationship with the primary outcome (criterion for entering variables will be p<0.10), in addition to the stratification variables (age) and prespecified baseline covariates such as thoracic injury pattern (bilateral/flail chest/number of ribs), associated lesions (ISS<>15) and respiratory comorbidity. Adjusted analyses will be performed using random-effects linear regression model to take into account within and between centre variability with centre as random-effect. If appropriate, a logarithmic transformation will be envisaged to achieve normality of dependent outcome. Significance of the intervention will be assessed based on p values and regression coefficient with 95% CIs calculated from this regression.

Preplanned subgroup analyses

Planned subgroup analyses will assess heterogeneity of the effect of early analgesia with ESP block after chest trauma on the primary outcome measure in prespecified subgroups of patients with thoracic injury pattern (bilateral/flail chest/number of ribs), ventilation status (non-invasive/invasive ventilation), type of surgery (thoracic/other), respiratory comorbidity (no/yes), age (<>65 years old) and associated lesions (ISS<>15).

According to usual recommendations, the interactions between group randomisation and subgroups will be evaluated in regression models (ie, random-effects linear model).

The primary outcome will be evaluated in the subgroup analyses without adjustments for multiplicity.

Significance

A two-sided p value of less than 0.05 will be considered for statistical significance of all analyses. Because of the potential for type 1 error due to multiple comparisons, findings for analyses of secondary endpoints will be interpreted as exploratory.

Interim analysis

No interim analysis will be conducted.

Method for missing data

We do not expect missing data on the primary outcome measure and a complete case analysis will be performed.

We will use an eCRF with a pragmatic design and incentive strategies to maximise complete registration in order to minimise the occurrence of missing data.

If less than 5% of the data are missing on any secondary outcomes, a complete case analysis without input of missing values will be performed. If the frequency of missing data is >5%, an additional analysis will be performed using the multiple imputation method (Stata command mi). We will use outcomes and the most important baseline characteristics in the multiple imputation. The exact variables to be used to estimate the missing values will be outlined in the detailed statistical analysis plan; if multiple imputation is used, then the primary result of the trial will be based on the imputed data.

To put significant results into perspective, a sensitivity analysis will be conducted by defining a worst-case scenario in which patients with missing data do not react to the study treatment (whatever it may be). Also, if multiple imputation is used, we will in addition provide a best–worst worst–best case scenario as a sensitivity analysis to assess the potential impact of any pattern of missing data. In the best–worst case scenario, it is assumed that patients lost to follow-up in the experimental group have had a beneficial outcome and those with missing outcomes in the control group have had a harmful outcome. Conversely, in the worst best–case scenario, it is assumed that patients who will be lost to follow-up in the experimental group have had a harmful outcome and that those lost to follow-up in the control group have had a beneficial outcome.

Discussion

Adequate pain management is a cornerstone of care in patients with blunt chest trauma, as uncontrolled pain contributes to respiratory complications, prolonged ventilatory support and increased morbidity. Despite guideline recommendations, the use of thoracic EA or PVB in the acute trauma setting remains limited because of technical complexity, contraindications and potential complications. The TARGET trial has been designed to address an important gap in the evidence by evaluating whether early initiation of a continuous ESP block can improve clinically relevant respiratory outcomes in this population.

The ESP block is an attractive alternative regional analgesia technique in patients with trauma. It is ultrasound-guided, relatively easy to perform, and associated with a favourable safety profile compared with neuraxial techniques. Its anatomical distance from the neuraxis and pleura reduces the risk of severe complications and allows use in patients with post-traumatic coagulopathy, a frequent limitation to epidural or paravertebral analgesia. Furthermore, the possibility of maintaining a continuous infusion via a catheter makes it suitable for prolonged analgesia from the trauma bay to the ICU. Retrospective studies and observational data have suggested potential benefits on respiratory mechanics and oxygenation, but robust prospective evidence has been lacking.

By focusing on VFDs as the primary outcome, this study integrates both survival and duration of invasive and non-invasive ventilatory support, providing a patient-centred and clinically meaningful endpoint. This outcome is particularly relevant in chest trauma, where the main therapeutic objective of analgesia is to preserve spontaneous breathing, facilitate effective coughing and secretion clearance and allow early liberation from mechanical ventilation. The pragmatic design of the trial, conducted across multiple centres and embedded in routine trauma care pathways, is expected to enhance the generalisability of the findings.

Several limitations inherent to the study design should be acknowledged. Blinding of clinicians and investigators is not feasible given the nature of the intervention, which may introduce performance bias. However, the primary outcome is objective, prospectively defined and less susceptible to subjective assessment. In addition, crossover from the experimental group to standard analgesia may occur in case of ESP block failure, potentially attenuating the observed treatment effect, although this reflects real-world practice and will be accounted for in the analysis strategy. The open-label design also introduces the risk of differential cointervention bias, whereby clinicians in the experimental group may modify analgesic or physiotherapy regimens based on their belief in the ESP block’s efficacy. This is mitigated by the objective nature of the primary endpoint. Variability in cointerventions such as ventilatory strategies or physiotherapy practices between centres may also influence outcomes, although randomisation and stratification are expected to minimise imbalance. The sample size was calculated on the basis of available regional registry data (TRENAU registry) and a published observational cohort, which carry uncertainty; however, the 80% power and two-sided alpha of 5% provide a robust statistical framework. The absence of a preceding feasibility RCT is acknowledged; the decision to proceed directly to the definitive trial was based on high observed technical feasibility of the ESP block across participating trauma centres and the availability of dedicated funding. Pain assessment in non-verbal or intubated patients relies on the BPS rather than self-report, introducing some imprecision in analgesic outcome measurement. These limitations will be transparently reported in the final publication.

If early continuous ESP block is shown to reduce ventilatory support duration, this trial could support a shift in analgesic strategies for blunt chest trauma, promoting earlier use of regional analgesia in the trauma bay. Such findings would have important implications for clinical practice, resource utilisation and patient outcomes and could inform future national and international guidelines on chest trauma management.
