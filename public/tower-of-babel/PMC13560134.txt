Agreement between random upper arm circumference and standard mid-upper arm circumference for identifying acute malnutrition among children aged 6–59 months in Southwest Ethiopia
Authors: Anuwar Husen, Anteneh Berhane, Melese Sinaga Teshome
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMid-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC) is a widely used, rapid, and low-cost anthropometric tool for community-based screening of acute malnutrition among children. However, accurate MUAC assessment requires identification of the midpoint of the upper arm and proper tape placement, which may contribute to inter-observer variability and inconsistencies in field measurements. Random Upper Arm Circumference (RUAC) has been proposed as a simpler alternative for community screening. Nevertheless, evidence regarding the agreement and validity of RUAC compared with standard MUAC for identifying wasting among Ethiopian children remains limited. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the concordance and validity of RUAC compared with MUAC in identifying wasting among children aged 6–59 months in Southwest Ethiopia.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 591 children aged 6–59 months in Southwest Ethiopia from June 2 to June 30, 2023. Participants were selected using simple random sampling from child registration lists used as the sampling frame. Paired RUAC and standard MUAC measurements were obtained from each child to assess agreement between the two methods. Children were classified as wasted using the MUAC cut-off value of < 12.5 cm, and RUAC was categorized using the same threshold. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26. Agreement between RUAC and MUAC was evaluated using Cohen’s Kappa for categorical measures and Bland-Altman analysis for continuous measurements. Diagnostic performance was assessed using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Assumptions for statistical analyses were checked before analysis.ResultsAmong the participants, 61 (10.4%) children were classified as wasted using standard MUAC measurements. Significant agreement was observed between RUAC and standard MUAC classifications (κ = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.60–0.79; p < 0.001). Using standard MUAC as the reference method, RUAC demonstrated a sensitivity of 81.6% (95% CI: 71.9%-89.0%) and specificity of 95.2% (95% CI: 93.1%-96.9%) for identifying wasting among children aged 6–59 months. The positive predictive value and negative predictive value of RUAC were 66.2% (95% CI: 55.0%-76.2%) and 97.8% (95% CI: 96.1%-98.9%), respectively. RUAC also demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance, with an area under the curve of 0.99 (95% CI: 0.98–0.99) for severe acute malnutrition and 0.94 (95% CI: 0.93–0.96) for moderate acute malnutrition.ConclusionRUAC showed substantial agreement with standard MUAC and acceptable diagnostic performance for identifying wasting among children aged 6–59 months in Southwest Ethiopia. However, the evidence is limited to a single-site cross-sectional study, therefore, generalization beyond the study population is not justified. Further validation across different Ethiopian settings is recommended.

Introduction

Malnutrition refers to deficiencies, excesses, or imbalances in energy and/or nutrient intake [1, 2]. It is broadly categorized into undernutrition and overnutrition. Undernutrition includes wasting (acute malnutrition), stunting, underweight, and micronutrient deficiencies, while overnutrition includes overweight and obesity. Among these, wasting represents the most severe form of acute undernutrition and is strongly associated with increased risk of morbidity and mortality among children under five years of age [3–5]. Children with wasting are substantially more likely to die than well-nourished children, and severe acute malnutrition remains a major contributor to under-five mortality globally [4, 6].

Undernutrition in early childhood is driven by a combination of inadequate dietary intake, infectious diseases, suboptimal infant and young child feeding practices, and underlying socioeconomic deprivation [3, 7]. These determinants frequently coexist in low- and middle-income countries, including Ethiopia, where food insecurity and recurrent infections continue to contribute to persistent child undernutrition [8, 9].

Globally, approximately 45 million children under five were affected by wasting in 2022, with the highest burden concentrated in Asia and sub-Saharan Africa [2, 10]. Sub-Saharan Africa accounts for a substantial proportion of the global burden of undernutrition [11, 12]. In Ethiopia, wasting remains a major public health concern, with national prevalence estimates of approximately 11% and substantial regional variation reported across recent surveys [13, 14].

The continued burden of acute malnutrition has prompted implementation of national and global response strategies aimed at improving early detection and timely treatment. In Ethiopia, the National Nutrition Program and the Community-Based Management of Acute Malnutrition (CMAM) strategy emphasize community-level identification and management of acute malnutrition among children under five years of age [15–17]. Effective implementation of these programs depends on the availability of simple, reliable, and scalable screening tools suitable for routine use in community and resource-limited settings [18]. Inaccurate classification during screening may result in delayed treatment of affected children or unnecessary referral of non-malnourished children, potentially affecting both clinical outcomes and program efficiency. Several anthropometric methods are used to assess nutritional status in children, including weight-for-height z-score, body mass index-for-age, and MUAC. Although weight-for-height assessment is widely used, it requires calibrated equipment, accurate age determination, and trained personnel, which may limit its applicability in community-based settings. Consequently, MUAC has become one of the most widely used screening tools for identifying acute malnutrition among children aged 6–59 months because of its simplicity, low cost, rapid application, and strong association with mortality risk [19]. Standard MUAC measurement requires identification of the midpoint between the acromion and olecranon processes of the upper arm; however, inconsistency in midpoint localization and measurement technique may contribute to inter-observer variability in field conditions [20]. RUAC has been proposed as a simplified anthropometric approach that measures upper arm circumference without strict reliance on midpoint localization [21]. By reducing dependence on precise anatomical identification, RUAC may simplify field measurement procedures. However, evidence regarding the diagnostic performance and agreement of RUAC compared with standard MUAC remains limited, particularly in Ethiopian settings [22]. In Ethiopia, and specifically in Southwest Ethiopia, limited evidence is available regarding whether RUAC can reliably reproduce MUAC-based classification of acute malnutrition in community screening programs. Lack of agreement between RUAC and standard MUAC could result in misclassification of nutritional status, potentially leading to missed identification of children requiring treatment or inappropriate referral of non-malnourished children.

Children aged 6–59 months constitute the primary target population for community-based screening programs because they are particularly vulnerable to growth faltering, infectious disease-related weight loss, and mortality associated with acute malnutrition [17, 19]. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests variation in MUAC performance across age subgroups within this range, particularly between children aged 6–23 months and those aged 24–59 months, which may influence evaluation of alternative anthropometric approaches [23]. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the diagnostic performance and agreement of RUAC compared with standard MUAC for identifying acute malnutrition among children aged 6–59 months in Southwest Ethiopia. The findings are expected to inform clinicians, community health workers, researchers, and policymakers regarding the potential applicability of RUAC in similar resource-constrained settings.

Methods

Study area, design, and period

The study was conducted in Masha Woreda, located in the Southwest Ethiopia. Masha is situated approximately 676 km southwest of Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. The woreda is predominantly rural, and the main livelihoods include mixed farming and coffee production. Commonly consumed foods include maize, enset, teff, legumes, and locally available vegetables. Health services in the district are provided through health centers, health posts, and community-based health extension programs that support maternal and child health and nutritional screening services. A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from June 2 to June 30, 2023.

Study population and eligibility criteria

The study population consisted of children aged 6–59 months who were randomly selected from six kebeles in Masha Woreda. Children who had resided in the study area for at least six months and whose upper arms were accessible and suitable for anthropometric assessment were included in the study. Children who were seriously ill during data collection, those with upper limb deformities or skin conditions such as dermatitis or ulcers, and children with bilateral pitting edema were excluded because these conditions could interfere with accurate anthropometric measurements.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

The sample size was determined using Buderer’s formula for diagnostic test studies based on sensitivity estimation. Assuming a sensitivity of 90%, a margin of error of 5%, and a wasting prevalence of 22.6% from comparable settings [24] the calculated sample size was adjusted by 10% for possible non-response, resulting in a final sample size of 591.

Six kebeles were selected randomly from Masha Woreda. A list of eligible children obtained from health extension worker registration records served as the sampling frame. Participants were selected using simple random sampling with proportional allocation across kebeles. In households with more than one eligible child, one child was selected using a lottery method.

Data collection procedures

Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire and standardized anthropometric measurements. The questionnaire was developed after reviewing relevant literature [25–27]. Information on socio-demographic characteristics was obtained from mothers or caregivers. Child age was determined using immunization cards when available; otherwise, locally adapted event calendars were used.

Anthropometric measurements were performed following World Health Organization-recommended procedures using standardized equipment. MUAC was measured on the left arm using a non-stretchable color-banded measuring tape after identifying and marking the midpoint between the acromion and olecranon processes. RUAC was measured using the same tape without identifying the anatomical midpoint. Measurements were recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm, and the average of three readings was used for analysis.

Weight was measured using a calibrated digital weighing scale with children wearing minimal clothing and no shoes. Length was measured for children younger than 24 months using a length board, while standing height was measured for children aged 24–59 months using a stadiometer. Bilateral pitting edema was assessed using standard clinical procedures.

Operational definition

Moderate acute malnutrition (MAM) was defined as MUAC ≥ 11.5 cm and < 12.5 cm. Severe acute malnutrition (SAM) was defined as MUAC < 11.5 cm. Global acute malnutrition (GAM) included both MAM and SAM classifications [25].

Data quality control

The questionnaire was prepared in English, translated into Amharic, and back-translated to ensure consistency. Training was provided for data collectors and supervisors before data collection. Anthropometric standardization exercises and inter-observer reliability assessments were conducted during training. To minimize measurement bias, RUAC measurements were obtained before MUAC measurements [25]. Each anthropometric measurement was taken three times, and the average value was used for analysis. Data completeness and consistency were checked daily by supervisors and the principal investigator. To minimize anthropometric measurement error, the percentage of technical error of measurement (%TEM) was calculated, and the results were compared against acceptable thresholds. Where variability exceeded acceptable limits, retraining and corrective feedback were provided to data collectors before continuing data collection.

Data processing and analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using SPSS version [insert version]. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. Agreement between RUAC and MUAC classifications was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient and Bland-Altman analysis.

Diagnostic performance of RUAC was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), likelihood ratios, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis using MUAC as the reference standard. The optimal RUAC cut-off point was determined using the Youden index. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated to assess the linear relationship between RUAC and MUAC measurements. In addition, a chi-square test was performed to assess the association between classifications generated by RUAC and standard MUAC. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants

Of the 591 sampled children, 585 children aged 6–59 months participated in the study, yielding a response rate of 98.9%. The mean (± SD) age was 23.3 (± 14.3) months. Male children accounted for 51.3% (n = 300) of participants. Most households were male-headed (92.3%), and the predominant religion was Protestant (74.2%). Nutritional status of the study participants

Based on standard MUAC measurements, 61 (10.4%) children were classified as wasted. Of these, 3.2% had severe acute malnutrition and 6.9% had moderate acute malnutrition. Using RUAC measurements, 12.7% of children were classified as wasted, including 3.7% with severe acute malnutrition and 8.8% with moderate acute malnutrition. The mean (± SD) MUAC and RUAC measurements were 13.99 (± 1.23) cm and 13.96 (± 1.23) cm, respectively (Table 2).

Agreement between RUAC and MUAC

Substantial agreement was observed between RUAC and standard MUAC classifications for identifying wasting among children aged 6–59 months (κ = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.60–0.79; p < 0.001). A statistically significant association was also observed between RUAC and MUAC classifications based on chi-square analysis (χ² = 15.4, p < 0.001).

Agreement varied across subgroups. Kappa agreement was higher among males (κ = 0.79) than females (κ = 0.59). Across age categories, agreement ranged from moderate to substantial. Agreement was also higher for severe acute malnutrition than for moderate acute malnutrition (Table 3).

Bland-altman agreement analysis

Bland-Altman analysis demonstrated a mean difference (bias) of 0.025 cm between RUAC and MUAC measurements, with 95% limits of agreement ranging from − 0.27 cm to 0.32 cm. Regression analysis showed no evidence of proportional bias (p = 0.60) (Fig. 1).

Diagnostic performance of RUAC

Using standard MUAC as the reference method, RUAC demonstrated a sensitivity of 81.6% (95% CI: 71.9%-89.0%) and a specificity of 95.2% (95% CI: 93.1%-96.9%) for identifying wasting among children aged 6–59 months. The positive predictive value was 66.2% (95% CI: 55.0%-76.2%), while the negative predictive value was 97.8% (95% CI: 96.1%-98.9%). The positive likelihood ratio was 17.15 (95% CI: 11.49–25.6), and the negative likelihood ratio was 0.19 (95% CI: 0.11–0.30) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study evaluated the agreement and diagnostic performance of RUAC compared with standard MUAC for identifying acute malnutrition among children aged 6–59 months in Southwest Ethiopia. The findings showed substantial agreement between RUAC and MUAC-based classification, indicating a moderate level of consistency between the two anthropometric approaches in this setting. However, these findings should be interpreted as agreement with an existing screening tool rather than validation against a diagnostic gold standard, and therefore do not support claims of clinical equivalence or interchangeability [19, 28].

The observed differences in prevalence estimates between RUAC and MUAC suggest that classification outcomes are sensitive to small methodological variations in anthropometric measurement, particularly around fixed diagnostic thresholds. Such differences are expected in field-based nutritional assessments, where minor variations in measurement technique, positioning, or landmark identification may result in misclassification near cut-off points. Previous anthropometric studies have similarly demonstrated that MUAC-based classification is particularly sensitive to measurement error in borderline nutritional status categories [29, 30].

The level of agreement observed in this study is broadly consistent with evidence from low- and middle-income country settings evaluating simplified anthropometric screening approaches against standard MUAC measurement. These studies generally report moderate-to-substantial agreement, although the magnitude varies depending on population characteristics, age distribution, and measurement protocols [29, 31]. However, agreement statistics such as Cohen’s kappa reflect classification consistency rather than diagnostic validity. Therefore, the observed agreement does not imply that RUAC is interchangeable with MUAC for clinical or programmatic decision-making.

RUAC also demonstrated relatively high sensitivity and specificity when compared with MUAC-based classification. However, these diagnostic accuracy estimates should be interpreted cautiously because MUAC was used as the reference standard. Since MUAC itself is a screening tool rather than a definitive diagnostic measure such as weight-for-height z-score or clinical assessment, the analysis is subject to incorporation bias. This may lead to overestimation of diagnostic performance, a recognized limitation in diagnostic accuracy studies where the index and reference tests are not independent [28, 32].

Variation in agreement across age groups was observed, with lower agreement among younger children. This finding is consistent with established evidence that MUAC varies systematically with age and body composition during early childhood, which affects classification stability when fixed cut-off values are applied across the full 6–59 month age range [33, 34]. However, subgroup analyses in this study were exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously, as the study was not powered for formal interaction testing.

The RUAC cut-off values derived in this study demonstrated strong internal performance. However, because these thresholds were generated and evaluated within the same dataset, there is a risk of optimism bias and overfitting. This is a known limitation in diagnostic studies when cut-point derivation and performance evaluation are not externally separated [35]. Therefore, these cut-offs require validation in independent populations before they can be considered for operational use.

From a public health perspective, simplified anthropometric tools such as RUAC may offer potential operational advantages in community-based screening programs where trained personnel and time constraints limit the feasibility of standard MUAC measurement. However, evidence from implementation research indicates that simplicity alone does not guarantee diagnostic robustness or stability across diverse populations. Anthropometric indicators may vary by age structure, population characteristics, and contextual factors, which limits direct generalization of findings beyond the study setting [34, 36]. Therefore, RUAC should be considered investigational pending further validation.

The findings suggest that RUAC shows acceptable agreement and diagnostic performance relative to MUAC for identifying acute malnutrition in this study setting. From a programmatic perspective, this indicates that RUAC may be considered as an alternative anthropometric screening approach for further evaluation within community nutrition programs, particularly in settings where MUAC implementation is constrained by training demands or measurement variability. However, given that MUAC was used as the reference standard rather than a diagnostic gold standard, these results are not sufficient to support immediate replacement of MUAC in national or subnational protocols. Any consideration of RUAC in policy should therefore proceed through phased validation, operational feasibility testing, and external validation across diverse Ethiopian populations before integration into CMAM or community screening guidelines.

This study has several strengths, including a relatively large community-based sample, standardized anthropometric measurement procedures, and the use of multiple complementary statistical approaches (agreement analysis, diagnostic accuracy metrics, and ROC analysis), which together strengthen internal consistency of findings. In addition, paired measurement of RUAC and MUAC in the same individuals reduces between-subject variability and improves comparability. However, important limitations should be acknowledged. First, the use of MUAC as the reference standard introduces incorporation bias, as MUAC is itself a screening indicator rather than a diagnostic gold standard such as weight-for-height z-score or clinical outcome assessment. This likely inflates estimates of diagnostic performance. Second, the cross-sectional design limits inference on predictive validity or clinical outcomes over time. Third, cut-off optimization was performed within the same dataset, introducing a risk of overfitting and limiting generalizability of derived thresholds. Finally, findings are geographically restricted to a single district in Southwest Ethiopia and may not be fully generalizable to populations with different genetic, socioeconomic, or environmental profiles.

Conclusion

RUAC demonstrated substantial agreement with MUAC and acceptable diagnostic performance for identifying acute malnutrition among children aged 6–59 months in this study setting. However, these findings reflect concordance with an existing screening tool rather than validation against a diagnostic gold standard, and therefore should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical equivalence or interchangeability. Given the cross-sectional design, use of MUAC as the reference standard, and the single district study setting, the results should be considered preliminary. RUAC may have potential as a complementary screening approach in community settings, but further external validation across diverse Ethiopian populations is required before any programmatic or policy-level application can be recommended.
