Using United States medicare program claims to identify reliable measures of low value services influenced by primary care physician decisions
Authors: Chris Fleming, Dmitriy Poznyak, Rhea Powell, Claire Erba, Eunhae Shin, Rui Wang, Claire Burkhart, Arkadipta Ghosh, Eugene C Rich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLow-value services (LVS) are those that “… provide little to no benefit to patients….”1 Among an existing set of 31 LVS are invasive procedures not typically performed by primary care physicians (PCPs), and other LVS that may be commonly provided by specialists. To facilitate research on the role of primary care physicians in low-value care, we sought to identify the reliability of two subsets of LVS measures that are more directly subject to PCPs’ decisions. We refer to these as primary care adjacent (“PCA”) LVS measures.MethodsRetrospective observational cohort study to identify which of a 31-item LVS measure set were most commonly performed, ordered, or referred by PCPs and examine the reliability of subsets of PCA-LVS measures. Setting was primary care practices in the Comprehensive Primary Care Plus (CPC+) model evaluation. 5,741,061 Medicare fee-for-service (FFS) beneficiaries attributed to 11,910 practices and 40,981 physicians in 2018 were observed. Based on percentages of LVS commonly performed, ordered, or referred by PCPs, we defined a 4-item and an 11-item PCA-LVS measure. We compared the reliability of the 4-item, 11-item, and 31-item LVS measure sets using Spearman ρ (rho) rank-order correlation and the Intra-Class correlation coefficient (ICC). Limitations included that PCP performing, ordering, and referring data are not available in all claims. Claims-based analyses of diagnosis codes may misclassify some appropriate services as PCA-LVS.ResultsThe 4-item PCA-LVS measure has a significantly higher mean reliability estimate than the original 31-item measure in identifying PCPs who provide low versus high rates of LVS. The 11-item PCA-LVS measure also had significantly higher reliability than the 31-item measure but lower reliability than the 4-item measure.ConclusionsWe identified two LVS subsets reflecting LVS events most influenced by PCPs. Further work will be required to ascertain how best to use this or related PCA-LVS measures to enhance the quality of primary care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14983-4.Keywords: Low-value care, Low-value services, Overuse, Primary care, Medicare, Quality

Introduction

Low-value services (LVS) in health care provide little to no benefit to patients and have potential to cause patient harm or result in unnecessary costs [1]. Measurement of LVS may be helpful to identify and address examples of such low-value care. Schwartz and colleagues identified 31 services detectable in Medicare claims to examine variation in provision of LVS among Medicare beneficiaries [2, 3]. To address changes in diagnosis coding and clinical practice since 2009 not reflected in the work by Schwartz and colleagues, we previously updated the list of 31 claims-based LVS) and demonstrated the updated LVS measure reliably detected variations in care across primary care practice sites observed in the Comprehensive Primary Care Plus (CPC+) initiative [4, 5].

Many of these examples of low-value care are invasive procedures not typically performed by primary care physicians (PCPs), and other LVS within the competencies of PCPs may be commonly provided by specialists. Therefore, to identify PCP-relevant measures, Schwartz and colleagues selected a subset of the original 31 low-value care examples “whose use could be influenced substantially by a primary care physician’s treatment or referral decisions.”3 They reported similar variations among Medicare beneficiaries using measure subsets of 17, 12, and 8 low-value services. Baum and colleagues used Medicare claims analysis of the 31 LVS measures to quantify the share of Medicare spending directly attributable to PCPs through 2014 [6]. They found PCP ordering or performing claims accounted for a median of 8.3% of attributed patients’ costs for LVS. They also estimated PCP referrals accounted for a median of 15.4% of their patients’ costs for low-value care. Their data confirm that some LVS (e.g., pulmonary artery catheterization) were rarely performed by PCPs, while others (e.g., total or free T3 level testing for patients with hypothyroidism) were more commonly ordered or performed by PCPs than specialists.

While these studies confirmed some LVS were more commonly ordered or performed by PCPs, these studies used older LVS measures and did not assess the reliability of potential LVS measure subsets. To facilitate research on contemporary variations in low-value primary care, we sought to identify a subset of the updated LVS measures that are more directly influenced by current decisions of PCPs. To identify these “primary care-adjacent” services, we analyzed 2018 Medicare claims for beneficiaries observed during the CPC+ evaluation. Specifically, we sought to understand which of the updated 31-item LVS measure set were most commonly performed, ordered, or referred by PCPs and examined the reliability of two proposed subsets of primary care adjacent LVS measures.

Methods

Data source and CPC+ background

For this work, we used claims data associated with Medicare beneficiaries attributed to practices participating in the CPC+ Model, a Traditional Fee-for Service Medicare Alternative Payment Model launched by CMS on January 1, 2017. The CPC+ Model ran for five years in 26 regions of the country (additional details provided in Appendix A). To identify which of the 31 updated LVS measures (see Table 1) were more often performed or referred by PCPs (hereafter referred to as “primary-care adjacent” or PCA-LVS), we used fee-for-service (FFS) claims data for 5,741,061 Traditional Medicare beneficiaries attributed to 11,910 practices in 2018 that were either participating in CPC + or were chosen as comparison practices for the evaluation.

Primary care practices that participated in CPC+ could participate in either Track 1 or Track 2, with Track 2 offering more enhanced reimbursement but imposing advanced primary care practice requirements. Therefore the types of practices electing Tracks 1 and 2 were different, with comparison practices selected by Track with similar characteristics to the CPC+ practices before the start of the initiative (see Appendix A for more details regarding Track 1 and Track 2 practices).

Identifying PCA-LVS measures

We used a step-wise approach to identify subsets of PCA-LVS measures. First, building off the work of Baum and colleagues, we analyzed Medicare FFS claims to observe how often LVS were ordered/referred, or performed by PCPs [6]. As noted by Baum et al., PCP Performed services “indicates claims for which a beneficiary’s PCP was the performing physician on the Medicare claim; ” PCP Referred services reflect those where the PCP’s National Provider Identifier (NPI) was entered on the Medicare claim as the physician referring the patient for the service. The term “LVS events” denotes claims for LVS-associated HCPCS codes observed at the claim-line level among beneficiaries attributed to Track 1 or Track 2 practices. The programming code for the LVS measures searches Medicare FFS Carrier, Outpatient, and Inpatient claims files for relevant services (see Appendix A for more information about the programming code and our work updating it).

As CMS allows for documentation of clinician performing, ordering, or referring information as well as clinician specialty information in claims, we searched relevant claims variable fields to identify 2018 claims associated with possible LVS events [7, 8].

To identify PCPs, we utilized provider specialty codes in claim line items associated with LVS services. For LVS claims, we identified PCPs via the Line CMS Provider Specialty Code variable. The following codes indicate a PCP in our analyses: 01 (general practice physician), 08 (family practice physician), 11 (internal medicine physician), 37 (pediatric medicine physician), and 38 (geriatric medicine physician). Because it is often difficult to identify physician assistants and nurse practitioners in these settings who are chiefly collaborating with specialized physicians, we excluded claims linked to those types of clinicians. We then finalized our coding scheme by creating a binary indicator which identified whether a LVS event was linked to a PCP ordering, referring, or performing the relevant service as reflected in claims.

Once our coding scheme was in place, for each of the 31 LVS measures we calculated percentages of each service that were PCP-referred or were performed by a PCP, i.e., the percentage that could be categorized as a PCA-LVS event, such as a PCP performing bone mineral density testing within two years of a prior bone mineral density test for patients with established osteoporosis diagnosis. We calculated these percentages across all LVS measures, where denominators were set equal to all LVS in 2018, and the numerators were set to the number of events to which a PCP was linked. We then identified individual LVS measures for which PCPs performed or referred LVS at rates higher than the average such rate across all 31 LVS measures. We refer to groups of such measures as PCA-LVS measure subsets, which we identified based on the distribution of percentages of PCP involvement across LVS events. We report our results by CPC+ track (see Appendix A for more information about how we calculated LVS events).

Approach to testing reliability of PCA-LVS measures

Our second step involved conducting tests of measure reliability in detecting physician-specific performance, both for the overall LVS measure set (31 LVS measures) and for the PCA-LVS subsets we identified. To do this we conducted reliability analysis using the data from beneficiaries attributed to physicians at CPC + and comparison practices. Reliability is defined as the extent to which the measure scores can be replicated over repeat measurements [9]. Higher values of reliability at the physician level also indicate a greater ability to differentiate physicians from one another on that measure [10].

In our previous work, we used the split-half method in addition to the test-retest method to measure reliability [4]. Since the split-half method can, under some conditions, over- or under- estimate reliability because of chance variations in the measure results caused by sampling, we only assessed stability of the measure scores using the test-retest method, which involves comparing the measure scores for the same physicians within the same measurement period. Since we only had one year of data, we opted to use bootstrap resampling to generate independent samples of patients within the same physicians. By creating multiple repeated samples and then averaging the results, the bootstrap method cancels out the chance variations in the split-half design and avoids biased sampling, maintains the original sample size, and allows estimation of confidence intervals for the reliability estimates. We drew 2,000 independent samples of patient records from each physician, maintaining the same number of patients as in the original sample. These random samples from a given physician are assumed to reflect an independent set of re-measurement of the LVS rates for a physician. We then grouped these 2,000 samples into 1,000 pairs, with each pair consisting of two consecutive bootstrap draws. Then, we calculated the correlations between the LVS measure rates within each pair of samples. This process allowed us to generate a distribution of correlation coefficients, from which we estimated the average correlation and the 95% confidence interval in 1,000 paired samples.

We estimated measure reliability via Spearman ρ (rho) rank-order correlation and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The Spearman’s ρ measures statistical dependence of the physician’s score rank in sample A relative to their rank in sample B. The Spearman ρ ranges between − 1 and 1. The ICC, which ranges between 0 and 1, captures the degree of correlation and agreement between measurements and is represented as a ratio of the variance in the measure scores between physicians over the sum of the variances between and within physicians, where the variance in the measure scores within physicians was estimated via bootstrap resampling. The ICC is a measure of how similar the outcomes for the same physicians would be if they treated different samples of patients, relative to other physicians. Hence, the smaller the disagreement between the measure scores for each physician in different samples, the larger the ICC. Conceptually, calculating the ICC using bootstrap resampling allowed us to assess how similar the LVS scores for the same physicians would be in different samples of patients, relative to all other physicians. Since reliability can be affected by low numbers of patients for some physicians, we restricted the sample to only include physicians with at least 20 patients. For more information on the reliability methods, see Appendix B.

Reliability analysis

We assessed reliability of the 4-item and the 11-item PCA subsets of LVS measures, relative to the previously validated 31-item LVS measure. As we did in a previous paper, we conducted bootstrap resampling to assess reliability of the 4-item and 11-item PCA subsets, and the 31-item LVS measure set (results presented in Table 4) [4].

For the bootstrap resampling analysis, among both Track 1 physicians (N = 20,920) with at least 20 beneficiaries (N = 3,090,020) as well as Track 2 physicians (N = 20,061) with at least 20 beneficiaries (N = 2,884,399), the 4-item and 11-item PCA-LVS subsets had higher reliability relative to the 31-item LVS set, using the Spearman ρ (rho) rank-order correlation. The mean N of patients for clinicians with > = 20 patients was 148 patients among Track 1 physicians and 144 patients among Track 2 physicians.

Comparing results between the 4-item and 11-item PCA-LVS subsets, we found the 4-item subset to be more reliable, as evidenced by the higher correlations across the 1,000 pairs of bootstrap samples. Mean Spearman ρ correlation values for the 4-item subset across the 1,000 pairs of bootstrap samples were 0.768 (95% C.I.: 0.762–0.774) and 0.750 (95% C.I.: 0.762–0.774) among Track 1 and Track 2 physicians, respectively, compared to 0.754 (95% C.I.: 0.748–0.760) and 0.733 (95% C.I.: 0.727–0.739) for the 11-item subset and 0.750 (95% C.I.: 0.744–0.756) and 0.731 (95% C.I.: 0.725–0.737) for the full 31-item LVS set (Table 4).

The 4-item and 11-item PCA-LVS subsets also demonstrated slightly higher reliability than the 31-item LVS set according to the ICC statistics. Among Track 1 physicians mean ICCs were 0.842 (95% C.I.: 0.835–0.850), 0.834 (95% C.I. 0.828–0.841), and 0.803 (95% C.I. 0.796–0.809) for the 4-item and 11-item PCA-LVS subsets and 31-item LVS set, respectively (Table 5). Among Track 2 physicians, mean ICCs were 0.803 (95% C.I.: 0.791–0.815), 0.796 (95% C.I. 0.788–0.805), and 0.776 (95% C.I. 0.768–0.783) for the 4-item and 11-item PCA-LVS subsets and 31-item LVS set, respectively (Table 5). Following Portney and Watkins (2000), ICC values above 0.75 indicate good reliability [11]. According to the National Quality Forum guidelines, ICC coefficients above 0.6 indicate adequate test-retest reliability [12].

Discussion

Using Medicare claims data, we identified two subsets of PCA-LVS reflecting low-value service events most influenced by PCPs. Our analyses demonstrate that the 4-item PCA-LVS measure has a significantly higher mean reliability estimate than the original 31-item measure (or the 11 item PCA-LVS measure) in identifying those PCPs who provide low versus high rates of low-value services (Tables 4, 5 and 6).

The four services included in the 4-item PCA-LVS measure (colorectal cancer screening for adults over age 85; PSA testing for men age 75 and over with no prostate cancer history; total or free T3 testing for patients with hypothyroidism; and 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D testing in the absence of hypercalcemia or decreased kidney function) reflect examples of two different types of low-value primary care decisions: cancer screening and diagnostic testing. For each of the four low-value services, PCPs accounted for over 50% of the LVS events. Collectively, these 4 services constituted 16% of the LVS events experienced by CPC+ Medicare beneficiaries in 2018. For entities (e.g., payers, health systems, or individual primary care practices) considering initiatives that assess LVS provision by PCPs, the 4-item measure set is statistically more reliable than the 11-item or 31-item measure sets. Although the increase in reliability for the 4-item measure is modest, even small improvements in precision of estimates can often have significant implications for inferences about the quality of care [14, 15]. Reducing physician-specific error estimates decreases the likelihood of misinterpreting a physician’s performance due to noise and lowers the risk of misclassifying providers into incorrect performance categories. Additionally, enhanced reliability improves the ability to detect small changes in performance over time, facilitating more effective quality improvement efforts and better tracking of progress.

For each of these 4 PCA-LVS there are opportunities for some PCP practices to generate extra Medicare FFS revenue by providing these services. In some organizations the physicians (or their practice sites) may receive the revenue but in other circumstances (such as integrated delivery systems) another cost center within the larger organization may receive the revenue. Interestingly, recent research using the 31-item LVS measure found that PCPs working in health systems had lower 31-item LVS scores [5]. These significantly lower rates of LVS were concentrated among a small subset of the 31 services, including colorectal and prostate cancer screening, and T3 level testing for patients with hypothyroidism. While it is possible the lack of physician-level financial incentives for low-value testing might have contributed to this result, other work has found LVS also occur in settings lacking PCP FFS incentives for overuse [16]. Analyzing a larger set of low-value services, Ganguli and colleagues (2021) showed some health system characteristics were associated with higher rates of low-value care, including systems with a smaller proportion of primary care physicians and those serving areas with higher levels of health care spending [17]. In their analyses of a larger set of “primary care influenced” LVS measures, Schwartz and colleagues (2019) found “extensive physician-level variation not attributable to patient, organizational, or regional factors.”3 Further research using the 4-item PCA-LVS measure may prove useful in better understanding the physician incentives, practice settings, organizational affiliations, and patient and community factors that contribute most strongly to such low-value primary care. Such information may be informative to professional associations and provider organizations seeking to facilitate reductions in rate of PCP use of such low-value care.

Other potential applications of such PCA-LVS measures are in interventions such as PCP feedback or pay for performance. Various feedback programs aimed at PCPs have proven successful in reducing some examples of low-value care [18, 19]. Research has been more mixed regarding the benefits of pay for performance initiatives on low-value care [20]. While the simplicity and primary care salience of the 4-item PCA-LVS measure may facilitate its application, various policy researchers have noted the risks of PCP-directed pay-for-performance plans that focus on narrow areas of clinical care and the potential for sophisticated health care systems to manage such isolated metrics without addressing more patient-centered outcomes [21, 22].

Applying the 4- or 11-item PCA-LVS measures to track LVS provision at primary care practices may incentivize some primary care practices to continue to provide other LVS not included in these metrics. Limiting the number of measures tracked to those most pertinent to PCP decision making, however, would allow practices to more effectively target resources to ensure patients are receiving quality care. Alternatively, the 11-item measure is also a reliable measure of variations in PCP LVS and might highlight additional opportunities for PCPs to reduce low-value care. With either metric, as we found in previous work, initiatives to apply these measures in clinical settings would require monitoring and updating as clinical evidence, payer responses, and practice patterns change [4].

In conclusion, we have identified a 4-item measure of Primary Care Adjacent LVS which reliably identifies high LVS vs. low LVS PCPs. Further work will be required to ascertain how best to use this or related PCA-LVS measures to enhance the quality of primary care practice.

Limitations

Though we sought to reduce the number of appropriate services misclassified as “low-value” (using the methods of Schwartz and colleagues [2]), these limitations are difficult to avoid in a strictly claims-based analysis lacking the clinical nuance available from medical records. Conversely, it’s quite likely this approach also underestimates the number of LVS delivered. See Appendix A for more on the methods and the programming code.

As mentioned, our analyses were restricted to Medicare claims that contained referring and performing provider data as well as provider specialty information. In an analysis of 2018 and 2019 Medicare FFS claims, we found that 95% of Medicare FFS Carrier claims contained referring National Provider Identifier (NPI) and related specialty information while 70% of Carrier claims contained performing NPI and specialty information.

As with any analysis of Traditional Medicare beneficiaries, the low value services studied apply to an older age population with insurance benefits determined under Medicare regulations.

Despite these limitations, we have identified a 4-item measure of Primary Care Adjacent LVS which reliably identifies high LVS vs. low LVS PCPs. The environment and context for applying measurement to improve primary care practice has become even more complex since 2018 [23, 24]. Accordingly, further work will be required to ascertain how best to use this or related PCA-LVS measures to enhance the quality of primary care practices.
