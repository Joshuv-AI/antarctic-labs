The role of polygenic risk in Alzheimer's disease prediction for African Americans
Authors: Christina G Hutten, Todd Beck, Denis Evans, Kumar B Rajan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAfrican Americans (AAs) face a higher risk of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) than European Americans (EUs), yet the utility of polygenic risk scores (PRS) in AAs remains underexplored.METHODSA standardized dementia PRS was evaluated in the Chicago Health and Aging Project (n = 4336; 61% AA) for associations with ADRD and cognitive trajectories over 8.4 years.RESULTSPRS predicted ADRD more strongly in EUs (c‐index 0.86) than AAs (0.77); however, it conferred higher risk in AAs (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.36, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.04–1.78) compared to EU (HR = 1.13, 95% CI: 0.88–1.44). The PRS remained predictive among AAs after apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 adjustment (HR = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.03–2.27). Higher PRS associated with lower baseline cognition and faster decline (p < 0.05).DISCUSSIONPRS conferred greater ADRD risk in AAs and comparable rates of cognitive decline, despite stronger discrimination in EUs, adding to the limited knowledge of genetic contributions to ADRD risk among AAs beyond APOE ε4 alleles.Keywords: admixture, Alzheimer's disease and related dementias, cognitive decline, diverse populations, polygenic risk score, risk stratification

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) and related dementias (ADRD) affect nearly 7 million people in the United States over the age of 65 and are the fifth leading cause of death; furthermore, with the baby boomer generation advancing in age, by 2060, the prevalence of ADRD is expected to double. 1 The reverberation of these progressive conditions across the health‐care landscape will be significant, imposing strain on the health‐care system and society as a whole, while creating substantial economic and psychological burden for both patients and caregivers. 2 These challenges will magnify existing inequities in populations at higher risk for ADRD. Non‐Hispanic/Latino (NH) African Americans (AAs) face a disproportionate burden of disease, experiencing higher incidence, earlier onset, and faster rates of cognitive decline compared to NH White adults. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Early identification of those at risk would be beneficial for implementing preventive measures and assessing interventions.

Twin and family studies show that the genetic heritability of late‐onset ADRD is about 58% to 80%. 7 Carrying two copies of the apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele represents the strongest known genetic risk factor for late‐onset AD and is present in approximately one third of ADRD cases; 8 , 9 , 10 however, the risk associated with APOE ε4 alleles has been shown to vary by global and local genetic ancestry. 11 , 12 , 13 Polygenic risk scores (PRS) may capture additional ADRD risk by aggregating effect sizes from thousands of common variants associated with dementia identified by genome‐wide association studies (GWASs). However, PRS performance in AAs remains underexplored due to the limited representation of AA participants in GWAS analyses. 14 Whether a PRS associated with dementia differentially modifies the effect of APOE ε4 across diverse populations remains unclear. Using the biracial, longitudinal Chicago Health and Aging Project (CHAP), we examined associations between dementia PRS and incident ADRD and measures of cognition and cognitive decline, while accounting for APOE ε4 alleles.

METHODS

Study population

CHAP, conducted from 1993 to 2012, is a biracial, population‐based, longitudinal cohort study of adults aged ≥ 65 years residing in four adjacent urban communities on Chicago's South Side. 15 Approximately 79% of all adults > 65 in the communities were enrolled, with new enrollment occurring in subsequent years after residents reached 65 years of age. 15 Participants were followed every 3 years for up to six cycles and completed in‐home cognitive assessments and informational interviews. 16 A stratified random sample, based on age, race, sex, and cognitive level, was selected from each cycle for clinical evaluation of incident ADRD, and blood samples were collected for biomarker assessment and DNA extraction. 16 , 17 Additional details regarding the CHAP study design and sample processing have been reported elsewhere. 3 , 18 , 19

Measures of cognition

A battery of four cognitive exams (two episodic memory scores for immediate and delayed story recall, 20 , 21 one for perceptual speed, 22 and one for the Mini‐Mental State Examination [MMSE]) 23 were administered during in‐home interviews at baseline and at 3‐year intervals. Cognitive domains were constructed using a weighted composite of individual test scores, following a standardized approach previously validated within the CHAP cohort. To ensure that these domains maintain factorial invariance across racial groups, we used factor loadings derived from longitudinal models that have been shown to perform consistently across African American and European American (EU) participants. 3 , 15 The approach minimizes measurement bias and ensures the PRS reflects true biological and clinical divergence rather than psychometric differences between groups. A global measure of cognition was derived by averaging the four cognitive tests, which were each centered and scaled to their respective baseline mean and standard deviation for the full cohort. 18 , 24 , 25 , 26 The individual standardized scores for memory, processing speed, and MMSE were also assessed and recorded separately. 18 Incident ADRD was based on guidelines for a history of cognitive decline and impairment in at least two cognitive domains. 18 , 27

Genotyping, imputation, and quality control

Samples were genotyped in five batches using single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)‐based arrays; EU samples (EU‐1, n = 624) and AA samples (AA‐1, n = 2322) were genotyped using the Illumina Human1M SNP array; EU‐2 (n = 353) and AA‐2 (n = 347) were genotyped using Illumina MEGAEX SNP array; and EU‐3 (n = 790) were genotyped using Affymetrix Genome‐Wide Human SNP Array 6.0. To mitigate potential technical variability arising from the different genotyping batches and arrays, we implemented the following multi‐state, quality control (QC) and harmonization protocol. Each batch was processed through QC using the Will Rayner toolbox to check strand direction, alleles, positions, reference and alternate allele assignments, and frequency differences compared to the 1000 Genomes project (Phase_3), and to remove minor allele frequencies (MAFs) > 0.4. 28 After initial QC, all data were imputed to a common reference panel, ensuring a consistent set of genetic variants across the entire sample. Each unphased batch was submitted separately to the Trans‐Omics in Precision Medicine (TOPMed) Imputation Server using Minimac4, LiftOver, phased by Eagle v2.4, and imputed to the version TOPMed‐r3 reference panel, freeze 9. 29 , 30 After imputation, all variants were filtered to remove any with MAF < 0.01, imputation quality (R 2) < 0.3, missingness > 0.05, or non‐biallelic variants. APOE genotyping of rs7412 and rs429358 SNPs was conducted separately by the Broad Institute at Harvard University (Cambridge, MA) using the hME Sequenom MassARRAY and dichotomized to those who carry any APOE ε4 allele and those with no copies of the APOE ε4 allele. 31

The first 20 principal components (PCs) of unrelated CHAP participants (n = 4336) were computed using PLINK. Adjustment of the PCs was included as a covariate in all regression models to capture and adjust for any residual technical variance or population stratification. Population labels from 1000 Genomes (Hg38) were used to label PC and Admixture plots to confirm that genetic ancestry of the cohort aligned with self‐reported race. Population‐labeled PC plots were created for each genotyped and imputed subset using pgsc_calc “–run_ancestry” function. 32 Admixture plots were created for the unrelated sample using the “ADMIXTURE” package 33 and the k‐number of ancestral populations was chosen by analyzing the lowest cross‐validation error when comparing k = 2 through k = 5 and by comparing the population proportion provided by the pgsc_calc “–run_ancestry” function.

PRS selection and construction

The PGS catalog (pgscatalog.org) 34 , 35 was searched for any PRS constructed from variants associated with “Dementia” (MONDO_0001627). PGS003958 was selected for its inclusion of GWAS conducted in a proportion of AA participants and for its evaluation in the presence and absence of the APOE gene region on chromosome 19. 36 The PRSsum score was developed by combining summary statistics from five separate GWASs of AD PRS for dementia as an unweighted sum. The “psgc_calc” package was downloaded to a local server for PRS calculation. 32 Approximately 96% of variants from PGS003958 matched those in the CHAP cohort (n = 13,545/14,109). Two SNPs (rs769449 and rs7412) located within the APOE gene region (19:44,905,796‐19:44,909,393) were omitted from PRS construction for the main analysis to minimize confounding from the strong effect of APOE on ADRD risk. The PRS analysis with the two APOE‐region SNPs included is provided in the supporting information. PRSs were standardized to ancestry subsets, AA and EU separately, so that each would have a mean of zero and a variance of one to account for differences in PRS distributions across populations and to facilitate comparison of effect sizes. A sensitivity analysis was conducted using full‐sample standardized PRSs to have a mean of zero and a variance of one (see supporting information), which retains between‐ancestry differences in PRS distribution and may influence the magnitude of estimated effects.

Covariates

Demographic characteristics were collected at baseline in‐home interviews, including age in years centered at 75, biological sex (male/female), education as number of years of school centered at 12, and self‐reported race (AA/EU) using the 1990 US Census questionnaire. 24 Other risk factors for ADRD (heart disease, diabetes, hypertension, and stroke) were collected using self‐reported responses to the Established Populations for Epidemiologic Studies of the Elderly. 37 Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were reported as means and standard deviations (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables, medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for skewed continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and presented across ancestry‐specific tertiles of PRS.

Linear mixed‐effects regression models with random intercepts and slopes were used to assess the association between PRS and the annual rate of each cognitive outcome (global cognition, memory scores, processing speed, and MMSE) and adjusted for age; sex; education; interactions between time and age, time and sex, and time and education; presence of any APOE ε4; and the first five PCs. Interactions between PRS and time were assessed, and results were stratified by self‐reported race. Cox proportional hazards models were estimated for ADRD risk in those who underwent cognitive evaluation, weighted to the full CHAP population. Models were adjusted for age, sex, time, presence of any APOE ε4 allele, and the first five PCs, and stratified by self‐reported race.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics did not significantly vary across tertiles of ancestry‐standardized PRS. Cognitive measures (global cognition, memory score, and MMSE) declined significantly across PRS tertiles. Processing speed did not significantly decrease across PRS tertiles. The proportion of APOE ε4 alleles increased significantly across PRS tertiles.

When PRS was standardized to the full sample, the proportion of participants rose significantly across tertiles of PRS for those who were AAs, had diabetes, had hypertension, incident ADRD, or had any APOE ε4 allele (Table S1 in supporting information). BMI also increased significantly across PRS tertiles while each cognitive measure (global cognition, memory score, processing speed, and MMSE) and age decreased across PRS tertiles. PRS discrimination and association with incident ADRD

The dementia‐related PRS without two APOE‐region SNPs had improved discrimination for incident ADRD in the CHAP sample in the EU subset versus the AA subset when adjusted for age, sex, education, and the first five PCs (Table 2A) according to concordance statistics (0.86 versus 0.77, respectively). However, the risk of incident ADRD was not significantly increased for the EU subset (hazard ratio [HR]: 1.13, 95% confidence interval [CI: 0.88–1.44]) while there was a significant 1.4 times higher risk for incident ADRD in the AA subset (HR [95% CI]: 1.36 [1.04–1.78]). When accounting for carriers of any APOE ε4 alleles in the model adjustment, discrimination increased slightly in the EU subset and decreased slightly in the AA subset. While the association between PRS and incident ADRD remained insignificant for the EU subset, every one SD increase of PRS in the AA subset was associated with a significant 1.6 times higher risk for ADRD (HR [95% CI]: 1.57 [1.03–2.27]; Table 2A). Including the two APOE‐region SNPs in the PRS did not appreciably change discrimination or the association of the PRS with incident ADRD in either subset (Table 2B), nor did standardization of the PRS to the full sample (Table S2A–B in supporting information). The full sample‐standardized PRS did strengthen the effect size for AAs and was similar after adjustment for APOE ε4 allele carriers (Table S2A–B).

PRS associations with cognitive score outcomes

The ancestry‐standardized PRS was associated with deficits in global cognition at baseline in the EU, with a coefficient of –0.03 (P = 0.01). For the memory score and MMSE in both the AA and EU subsets, baseline deficits were more pronounced in the EU than in the AA subset (Figure 3A). After adjustment for any APOE ε4 alleles, these associations were nullified in both AA and EU subsets (Figure 3B). For the association of PRS with measures of decline, PRS was significantly associated with a faster decline in global cognition, memory score, and processing speed, all by −0.01 (P < 0.001), with no difference in the rate of decline for each measure between the EU and AA subsets (Figure 3A). When adjusted for any APOE ε4 alleles, decline in memory score and processing speed were no longer significantly associated. Global cognition remained significant with a small decline of –0.005 (P < 0.05) per one SD increase in ancestry‐standardized PRS for both EU and AA subsets (Figure 3B). PRS was significantly associated with a decline in MMSE, which was more pronounced in EUs by −0.02 (P < 0.001) than in AAs, with an estimate of –0.01 (P < 0.001; Figure 3A). When adjusted for any APOE ε4 alleles, the magnitude of decline in MMSE for EUs was attenuated and equal to that of As, with a decline of –0.01 per one SD increase in PRS, which remained significant (P < 0.02; Figure 3B). In the sensitivity analysis, the full sample–standardized PRS showed the same patterns and magnitudes of association with cognitive measures as the ancestry‐standardized PRS (Figure S5 in supporting information). Including two SNPs within the APOE region in the PRS did not appreciably change the results (Figure S6 in supporting information).

Association of tertiles of PRS and measures of cognitive decline

For all cognitive measures, the association with ancestry‐standardized PRS showed a stepwise acceleration in decline, with those in the third tertiles of PRS showing greater decline than those in the second tertile, compared to those in the first tertile (Figure 4A). The increase in acceleration between the second and third tertile was more pronounced in the EU subset than the AA subset for all measures (Figure 4A). Adjustment for APOE ε4 alleles attenuated these associations (Figure 4B). For the EU subset, those in the third tertile of PRS showed significant decline in global cognition by −0.014 (P = 0.01), MMSE by –0.024 (P = 0.006), and processing speed by −0.013 (P = 0.03). In the models of ancestry‐standardized PRS adjusted for APOE ε4 alleles, the AA subset showed little differentiation between tertiles of PRS and each cognitive measure and only remained significant for those in the third tertile of PRS for global cognition by –0.009 (P = 0.03) and for those in the second tertile of PRS for processing speed by –0.008 (P = 0.03; Figure 4B). In the sensitivity analysis, using the full‐sample standardized PRS and adjusted for APOE ε4 alleles, those in the second and third tertile of PRS showed a significant decline in global cognition and MMSE in the EU subset compared to the lowest tertile (Figure S7 in supporting information). The full‐sample standardized PRS was also significant for those in the third tertile of PRS in the AA subset for global cognition and MMSE. Including two SNPs within the APOE region in the PRS did not appreciably change the results (Figure S8 in supporting information).

DISCUSSION

In this cohort, PRS showed improved performance in predicting incident ADRD among EUs compared to AAs; however, predicted risk for ADRD was higher among AAs than among EUs when accounting for APOE ε4 alleles. While no significant differences were identified in PRS associations with baseline cognition after accounting for APOE ε4 alleles, PRS was associated with faster MMSE decline and faster global cognitive decline among EUs and AAs. The findings help elucidate differences in genetic contributions to ADRD risk and cognitive decline between EU and AA subsets beyond risk conferred by APOE ε4 alleles.

Precision medicine offers the opportunity to improve risk prediction by identifying individuals who may be at higher genetic risk for disease and tailoring treatments and interventions, using genetic data earlier than common risk factors develop. 39 , 40 However, most PRS to date lack data on performance in AA populations. 41 , 42 The reduced discriminatory performance of PRS in AAs has important practical implications for equitable implementation of genomic risk prediction. 14 Lower predictive accuracy may lead to under‐identification of high‐risk AA individuals and less effective targeting of preventive interventions, thereby limiting the clinical utility of PRS in this population. 14 , 43 This stems from the lack of GWASs that include large enough proportions of participants with AFR ancestry to provide meaningful variant associations and effect sizes. 14 In contrast, EU populations may derive greater benefit from current PRS models because these scores are largely trained on European‐centric GWAS datasets. If used without ancestry‐specific refinement, current PRS frameworks risk exacerbating existing disparities in risk assessment and precision medicine. 14 , 44 These findings underscore the urgent need for more diverse GWAS representation and development of ancestry‐inclusive PRS models to ensure equitable genomic health care across populations. 39

Furthermore, when PRS, derived from GWAS performed with EU populations, are tested for performance in AA populations, the performance is inadequate compared to EU populations regardless of the disease of interest; the same is seen in highly admixed populations such as Hispanics/Latinos. 44 , 45 , 46 This is because variants identified as in linkage disequilibrium (LD) with causal variants within the EU population may not be representative of those in LD in AA populations, given the longer evolutionary timeframe and greater recombination. 46 Additionally, there are differences in allele frequencies and genetic architecture between EU and AA populations. 14 , 43 Significant focus has been turned to PRS methods development to improve performance among non‐European populations, including using varying clumping and thresholding (C+T) parameters and/or Bayesian approaches (PRS‐CS, PRS‐CSx) and varying numbers of SNPs. 45 , 46 , 48 , 49

Previous studies have identified that AAs are at higher risk for incident ADRD with earlier onset, and faster rates of cognitive decline compared to NH White adults. 3 , 5 , 6 , 31 APOE ε4 is a major risk factor for ADRD. Multiple studies have found that PRS modifies the APOE ε4–related incidence of ADRD, 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 but few have assessed differences between EU and AA populations. Here, we ascertained that the dementia‐related PRS in AAs was associated with a 1.4‐fold higher risk of incident ADRD and a 1.6‐fold higher risk when also accounting for APOE ε4 allele carriers. The proportion of risk attributable to PRS provides significant additional risk information for AAs but not for EU, suggesting that APOE ε4 confers greater risk to EU subsets than to AAs. This is consistent with reports that the effect of APOE ε4 is less pronounced in AA populations. 54 , 55 , 56 , 57 While some have suggested that this may be related to differences in gene–environment interactions between EU and AA populations, here we provide evidence that additional genetic risk may contribute to ADRD incidence in AA populations.

Sofer et al., 36 assessing the same PRS and its association with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) in the Study of Latinos‐Investigation of Neurocognitive Aging, found faster decline in global cognition (“G‐factor”) and the Brief Spanish English Verbal Learning Tests (B‐SEVLT); however, they found associations were stronger when models were not adjusted for APOE alleles. In the current analysis, we found the PRS to be associated with faster decline in cognitive measures, including global cognition and MMSE, with differences between AA and EU in the magnitude of MMSE decline. Additionally, we found the associations were stronger when models were not adjusted for APOE alleles in the EU sample. Still, adjustments for APOE alleles did not have as much of an impact on effect sizes in the AA subset. It has previously been suggested that subtle differences in cognitive measures may emerge in mid‐life among individuals with high genetic risk. 57 These differences may extend to subtle differences between AA and EU populations in the development of cognitive decline, based on high genetic risk and APOE allele status.

The 1.6‐fold increased risk of ADRD per SD of PRS in our AA participants, independent of APOE, likely reflects a combination of differences in genetic architecture, enrichment of vascular and inflammatory pathways, and gene–environment interactions. While amyloid precursor protein/amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau pathways were prominent in a large, NH White rare variant meta‐analyses, these pathways were not identified as top disease‐associated pathways in AAs. 53 In contrast, prior work suggests that ADRD risk in AA populations may be more strongly influenced by cardiometabolic and vascular processes, 54 , 59 , 60 , 61 which may interact with polygenic risk to activate neurodegenerative pathways. Consistent with this hypothesis, we observed differences in cardiometabolic risk factors, including diabetes and hypertension, across PRS tertiles under full‐sample standardization in AA participants, suggesting that the PRS may capture cardiometabolic‐related biology that clusters with these risk factors. However, these patterns should be interpreted cautiously, as they may also reflect differences in PRS distribution across ancestries. Differences in effect size across standardization approaches likely reflect between‐ancestry differences in PRS distribution rather than differences in underlying biological association. Differences in linkage disequilibrium structure and allele frequencies 14 , 43 , 47 may result in PRS capturing distinct biological mechanisms across ancestries, including immune and lipid‐related pathways not fully captured by APOE. 62 , 63 , 64 Social and environmental exposures may also modify the penetrance of genetic risk, contributing to observed differences in effect size.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several notable strengths. First, our biracial cohort is particularly unique for its large sample of AA participants with available genomic data. Given past disparities in genetic research in diverse populations, our study helps add to the literature of available findings surrounding genetic risk for AAs of ADRD and cognitive decline. These findings may be more generalizable to highly admixed populations with significant proportions of AFR and EUR ancestry than to studies that include only participants with EUR ancestry. Second, our cohort had a mean follow‐up of 8.4 years, allowing us to follow trajectories of cognitive decline and development of incident ADRD. Despite the relatively large sample, our study may have limited power to detect significant associations in all subsets.

CONCLUSION

The dementia‐related PRS provided better risk stratification beyond APOE for AAs and highlighted differences in cognitive decline between the EU and AA subsets, as reflected in MMSE and global cognition. Overall, the differences highlighted by PRS‐associated risk between EU and AA populations suggest differences in biological pathways influencing the onset of ADRD and cognitive decline. Further work should include larger GWAS samples of AFR ancestry and subsequent PRS evaluations to continue reducing health disparities for AA populations. Additional studies should investigate the specific variants included in the PRS and integrate multi‐omic technologies, such as epigenomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics, to tease out differences in the mechanisms driving ADRD risk and cognitive decline in AA compared to EU populations.
