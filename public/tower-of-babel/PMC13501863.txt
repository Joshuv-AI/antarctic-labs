Forced migrant fathers’ experiences of pregnancy and childbirth: a hermeneutic phenomenological study
Authors: Andy Mprah, Melanie Haith-Cooper, Fiona Meddings
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThere is an increasing body of literature exploring migrant (including forced migrant) women’s experiences of maternity care in their host country. However, there is a paucity of research around the perspective of migrant fathers.ObjectiveThis study aims to explore the experiences of forced migrant fathers during the pregnancy and childbirth of their partners, examining the challenges and emotional dynamics encountered in these transitional periods.MethodA hermeneutic phenomenological approach was employed to gain a deep understanding of the fathers’ lived experiences. In-depth, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 15 forced migrant fathers who had recently experienced the birth of a child. The data were analyzed using framework analysis to identify and interpret the core themes emerging from their narratives.ResultsThe analysis identified several key themes, including being sidelined, alien NHS maternity journey and living in the asylum system. Despite these challenges, many fathers expressed a strong desire to support their partners and play an active role in the upbringing of their children. Forced migrant fathers often felt marginalised in the healthcare system due to cultural misunderstandings and language barriers. Their narratives highlighted the need for more inclusive practices that recognize and support the unique challenges they face. Emotional resilience and adaptive strategies were commonly observed, demonstrating their commitment to their families amidst challenges.Key conclusion and implications for practiceThe study underscores the importance of culturally sensitive and inclusive healthcare practices to support forced migrant fathers during pregnancy and childbirth. Healthcare providers should be trained to recognize the specific needs of this population, ensuring that fathers are actively involved and supported. Improving communication and providing tailored resources can enhance the overall well-being of forced migrant families and contribute to positive childbirth experiences. By acknowledging and addressing the unique challenges faced by forced migrant fathers, healthcare systems can foster more equitable and supportive environments for all families.

Background

Men play an important role in supporting women during their maternity journey [1–3] and their input can significantly improve women’s maternity experiences, health and wellbeing [4]. However, fathers have their own wellbeing needs which is important to consider when providing maternity care [5, 6]. Fathers who have a negative perception of their maternity journey can experience a deterioration of their mental health and wellbeing [7], because of the complex nature of their migration experience. There is an increased expectation in high-income countries that fathers provide ongoing support to their partners as childbirth companions during the maternity journey [8] and their experiences of this has attracted increased research interest [4, 5, 9–14]. However, research has mainly focused on the experiences of the majority population [15] and not much is known about migrant (especially forced migrant) fathers’ experiences of the maternity journey [16, 17].

Most high-income countries are experiencing significant levels of migration [18, 19], with live births to UK based migrant mothers more than doubling from 11.7% to 28.7% [20]. The resulting ethnic and cultural diversity from increased migration can add to challenges in delivering healthcare to service users, most particularly maternity care [21]. Migrants are a heterogenous group with some people choosing to move to a new country, for example for work or study and others, including asylum seekers and refugees, being forced to flee home. It is important that the different life experiences of migrants, and there resulting needs are considered within maternity services.

Migrant women often have difficulties accessing maternity care in their host country and experience poorer pregnancy outcomes than non-migrant women in high-income countries [22–25]. In the UK, almost a quarter of women who died between 2015 and 2017 from all causes were migrants born outside the UK (23%). 42% of maternal deaths were to women who did not have UK citizenship (including forced migrants) [26]. Difficulties in accessing maternity care are exacerbated for forced migrant women, who have poorer physical and mental health, are more likely to live in poverty [27] where pregnancy can exacerbate poor health, poverty, and deprivation [28].

There is an increasing body of literature exploring migrant (including forced migrant) women’s experiences of maternity care in their host country [29, 30], however there is a paucity of research around the perspective of migrant fathers [31–33]. Migrant men’s previous experiences supporting their partners in childbirth in their home country may be significantly different to their host country due to different healthcare systems and expectations on their roles in the host country. Additionally, how long the migrant father has lived in the new country may also affect the experience due to the process of acculturation [34, 35].

Forced migrant fathers may experience the same difficulties as other migrant fathers, but in addition they have distinct/specific needs, having the added burden of trauma from home country and on their journey to the new country [36, 37]. Factors impacting their adaptation to migration include negative discourse around migration and in the UK, and their life being controlled by the asylum system [38–40]. They can have poor underlying mental health [41], and experiencing a negative maternity journey could exacerbate poor mental health which can impact their abilities to provide support to their partners. This is an important topic worthy of further research; therefore, a consultation exercise informed by co-production was undertaken with forced migrant women attending a voluntary sector group, a non-governmental organisation (NGO) which comprises of forced migrant mothers supervised by staff. National Institute for Health and Care Research (2021) describes co-producing a research project as an approach in which researchers, practitioners, and members of the public work together by sharing power and responsibility from start to end of a project, including the generation of Knowledge [41].

They re-enforced the need for more research exploring their partners experiences of the maternity journey. Those who had partners in the UK believed that men experienced barriers to accessing maternity care with their partners which needed addressing. Therefore, the aim of this study is to explore the lived experiences of men from refugee and/or asylum-seeking backgrounds (forced migrant fathers) when they support their partners through maternity care in the United Kingdom in order that interventions can be implemented to assist them (and therefore their partners) in this journey. Ultimately, this would help the men in their role in supporting their partners and also contribute to improving their wellbeing, in a population that can have poor underlying mental health [42, 43].

Design

The ontology adopted in this research was the social constructionist framework. A social constructionist theoretical position can be traced in part to an interpretive approach to thinking [44]. The epistemology adopted was a hermeneutic phenomenological approach using semi-structured individual interviews. This was considered to understand forced migrant fathers’ experiences of the maternity journey in the UK, a hermeneutic phenomenological approach using semi-structured individual interviews was considered most appropriate. Hermeneutic phenomenology aims to explore people’s understanding of their lived experience and their relationship to a phenomenon [45] in this case the maternity journey in the UK, and how their lived experience has been influenced by their constructions of reality influenced by their sociocultural background and their life in the UK [46]. The study is reported in accordance with COREQ (Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research) [47].

Setting

Potential participants were identified through non-governmental organisations, which in the UK are the main providers of social support to forced migrant families. In particular, organisations that support pregnant women and those with young children were approached including the Refugee Council, the Maternity Stream charity, and the wider City of Sanctuary Network. AM attended a meeting with forced migrant mothers and made a presentation about the study and the interest in recruiting their partners/husbands. Gatekeepers who were staff in these organisations were provided information sheets to share and were supportive in approaching the women’s partners/ husbands through the women. Interested fathers contact details were passed onto AM to follow up. Due to the COVID pandemic, the plan to meet face to face with interested fathers to enable recruitment had to be changed. Consequently, they were approached via telephone.

Sampling and data collection procedures

Purposive sampling was undertaken to recruit forced migrant fathers who had experienced NHS maternity care with their partners in the UK [48–50]. The inclusion criteria included men aged over 18 who were the biological father to the baby. Provision was made for the research participants to be interviewed in their preferred language by providing appropriate voluntary sector interpreters identified through the gatekeepers when needed. Men were given £10 following the interview four out of pocket expenses.

The plan to conduct face to face semi-structured interviews had to be changed due to the restrictions from the COVID-19 pandemic. Consequently, audio recorded interviews were conducted by AM through telephone (n = 5), WhatsApp video (n = 1), WhatsApp audio (n = 5) or zoom (n = 4) depending on the participants preferences [51, 52]. As a result, there was no limit on where in the UK men were living. Informed consent was audio recorded and transcribed as part of the interview, through an interpreter when required. In all, the services of 5 interpreters for 5 participants who could not speak and understand English were adopted. These interpreters were family friends, professionals from the voluntary sector and forced migrants who had regularized their stay in the UK. Participants were concerned with their personal information being shared with the Home Office, therefore during the consent processes and throughout the study confidentiality and anonymity was maintained [53]. A total of 14 men were interviewed in 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic as part of a PhD study. A smaller number of participants are generally required for hermeneutic phenomenological research to facilitate a depth of data to clearly portray their experience [54]. This number allowed for a narrative overview of sample characteristics based on ethnicity, home country, language, and fathers with maternity experience from both their home country and the UK.

In line with semi-structured interviews, the interview guide (see Table 1) included both questions with probes [55] and meaningful prompts [56] that sought deeper understanding of the forced migrant fathers’ experiences. The questions were checked by voluntary sector workers who suggested rewording some questions to simplify them. The questions were then piloted with two fathers. Two questions were found to retrieve similar responses. As such, one was deleted to avoid duplication. In addition, some questions were rephrased to ensure they encouraged conversation about men’s experiences rather than a yes/ no answer. The two men were re-approached to provide more detail in their responses using the amended questions. Piloting the interview guide also enabled AM to be aware of some linguistically and culturally sensitive words [57, 58] and as part of the conversation, ensure men explored their traditional beliefs about the father’s role on the maternity journey. Men were offered a debrief at the end of the interview and the opportunity for signposting for further psychological support if needed.

Interview guide

AM maintained a reflexive diary while undertaking the fieldwork. This enabled his self-awareness of how his background could influence the different aspects of the research [59, 60]. Such reflexive engagements at every stage of research have been encouraged in hermeneutic phenomenology [61].

Author reflexivity

AM, as a migrant father himself brought with him his own perspectives which could had influenced the analysis and interpretation of data. Through the reflexive journal, he was able to articulate and acknowledge these perspectives and his positionality as an insider/outsider. However, scholars have attempted to move away from the insider-outsider dichotomy of positionality to emphasise ‘the space between’ [62, 63]. AM’s research positionality could shift at any time during the research process as aas ablack student, economic migrant in the UK, with experience of the maternity journey in the USA.

This is in line with hermeneutic phenomenology. When an interview occurs virtually, the traditional ways of creating rapport and trust may not always work [64, 65]. Since interviewing is a personal method of data collection, there is the need to establish a certain degree of trust between the interviewer and the interviewees [66]. As such, in the virtual semi-structured interviews with the fathers, AM sought to gain this trust by sharing his own experience of the maternity journey in the USA as a migrant father. This enabled the forced migrant fathers to freely share their experiences of the maternity journey knowing the researcher could understand their unique situation.

Data management and data analysis

The audio recordings were transcribed verbatim, and a sample checked by MC and FM to ensure accuracy. The nine out of 14 participants who could read English were sent respective copies of the interview transcripts for accuracy and resonance with their experiences [67]. They were given a week to recompensate their answers to the interview. However, none of these participants sought to change any part of their interview transcript. The other five participants were not sent copies because they required interpreters and could not read English. Each interview transcript was uploaded to NVivo 12 software. Using NVivo has been reported to improve the transparency of framework analysis because analytic decisions and interpretations can easily be traced back to the raw data [68]. The five stages of framework analysis were undertaken [68, 69]. (See Table 2).

Figure 1 shows the original static model (first version of conceptual framework which does not reflect coding but shows initial understanding of relationships, themes and concepts) of all preliminary themes using NVivo [71]. From this, 29 preliminary codes were noted. Stage two involved developing framework categories from the a priori and emergent issues to organise the data. Thirdly the data were indexed into the framework categories by revisiting each transcript and extracting the preliminary codes. These were then grouped together to illuminate the most important and relevant aspects of the data. Not all codes fitted into a particular framework category so another framework category ‘other’ was developed which facilitated ideas for changing or adding more framework categories. The fourth stage was charting; summarising the indexed data in each framework category by participant and then organising in a chart format. Each summary was then linked to the relevant text in each transcript in NVivo [71]. This ensured all the important data in the transcript were captured to improve rigour. Finally, the data were mapped and interpreted to understand the dataset as a whole. The key characteristics in the charted data were examined for emergent patterns which were used to develop themes to capture forced migrant fathers’ experiences of the maternity journey with their partners in the UK. All stages of data analysis were discussed as a research team to increase credibility and rigour. This analysis, peer-debriefing and scrutiny reflects processes in other literature [68, 72].

Results

Fourteen men participated in the study. They were living in Yorkshire, North-East England, and Wales. Their ages ranged from 29 to 52 and six were first time fathers (see Table 4). Nine men were refugees and five men were asylum seekers, originating from seven different countries. Five of the men required an interpreter. (See Table 4 for the demographics of the fathers). Three themes emerged from the data: being sidelined, adapting to alien NHS maternity services, and living in the asylum system. (See Table 5 for themes and sub-themes from this study). Being sidelined

Most of the participants discussed how they experienced being in the background when engaging with NHS maternity care with the focus of care being on their partners. Two of the fathers were present with their partners and acted as interpreters and once the interpreting was done, they both felt excluded. One father discussed his experience when his partner needed a caesarean section:

“I think they side-lined me because I was expecting to attend the operation myself. But they used me only for interpreting and as soon as they make everything ready, they said, yeah, you need to go, you need to go, I said, I’m the father. They said, you’re not one we need inside the operation, you need to go out” (Abdul).

“I think they side-lined me because I was expecting to attend the operation myself. But they used me only for interpreting and as soon as they make everything ready, they said, yeah, you need to go, you need to go, I said, I’m the father. They said, you’re not one we need inside the operation, you need to go out” (Abdul).

This father described how he had to be assertive after the baby was born to ensure he was not excluded from caring for his newborn baby:

“I waited, I think, like half an hour. Yeah, and then they came back, took me to the baby. And I’ve seen the baby, not the mum, because the mum was in the operation. Yeah, after I’ve seen the baby as well, they said I need to go. But I refused, I said, “No, the baby’s alone, I need to be with her” (Abdul).

“I waited, I think, like half an hour. Yeah, and then they came back, took me to the baby. And I’ve seen the baby, not the mum, because the mum was in the operation. Yeah, after I’ve seen the baby as well, they said I need to go. But I refused, I said, “No, the baby’s alone, I need to be with her” (Abdul).

However, other fathers felt included in maternity care, especially being present during the labour. One father reported how being used as an interpreter for his wife made him feel valued and involved in the maternity care:

“My wife did not understand the kind of Arabic from the interpreter and so the midwife called me to interpret to my wife about the pregnancy. Later, I stayed and felt very important and involved” (Ishak).

“My wife did not understand the kind of Arabic from the interpreter and so the midwife called me to interpret to my wife about the pregnancy. Later, I stayed and felt very important and involved” (Ishak).

Two fathers described how they felt excluded when they were at the hospital and were refused food at a time when their partner was fed. One man reported:

“They brought sandwich and tea for only my wife. They said the food was only for my wife. I was hungry and did not have money for food, so I ate the sandwich and when the midwife came, she laughed at me that the food was for my wife. I was embarrassed” (Faizel).

“They brought sandwich and tea for only my wife. They said the food was only for my wife. I was hungry and did not have money for food, so I ate the sandwich and when the midwife came, she laughed at me that the food was for my wife. I was embarrassed” (Faizel).

Fathers who had previously experienced the maternity journey in home country shared varied experiences of this theme. For one father, he did not attend any of the maternity appointments with his wife in Syria and did not expect to be involved in his wife’s maternity journey in the UK. However, another father, with experience of the maternity journey in Iran experienced a clash of cultures when he expected to be asked for consent before anything happened to his wife in labour. Instead, his wife was asked for her consent, leaving him feeling excluded:

“In Iran, I went to labour ward with my wife and the doctors always confirmed from me to say yes before the operation on my wife to take out the baby. In the UK it is opposite” (Zebu).

“In Iran, I went to labour ward with my wife and the doctors always confirmed from me to say yes before the operation on my wife to take out the baby. In the UK it is opposite” (Zebu).

Six of the participants felt sidelined as they could not accompany their partner to the hospital due to having to care for their other children. They had no family in the UK and could not afford to pay for childcare. One participant describe the contrast between this situation and in home country where he did not want to engage with maternity care:

“In Syria, taking care of the other children was not the reason for my non-involvement. Her sisters took care of the children and attended the hospital with her. Here in the UK, I could not be involved because I had to stay back and take care of the children at home” (Aziz).

“In Syria, taking care of the other children was not the reason for my non-involvement. Her sisters took care of the children and attended the hospital with her. Here in the UK, I could not be involved because I had to stay back and take care of the children at home” (Aziz).

Alien NHS maternity journey

Participants discussed how their experiences of the maternity journey with their partners were influenced by their unfamiliarity with NHS maternity services. They described how they didn’t understand the services available and what was expected of them as fathers when they attended appointment and labour ward with their partners:

“…from knowing the maternity services in my country inside-out and now confused with the maternity practice in the UK without really knowing if my role is a father or just a helper, that’s the big worry for me, it was, yeah” (Ishak).

“…from knowing the maternity services in my country inside-out and now confused with the maternity practice in the UK without really knowing if my role is a father or just a helper, that’s the big worry for me, it was, yeah” (Ishak).

Some fathers reported how the alien maternity journey was exacerbated by language barriers and a lack of interpreters. They could not understand the explanations for care provided by the health professionals. This led to a sense of loss of control:

“Back home in Iran, it was my native dialect so I was confident and felt like a man who knows what will happen to my wife’s pregnancy at all times” (Zebu).

“Back home in Iran, it was my native dialect so I was confident and felt like a man who knows what will happen to my wife’s pregnancy at all times” (Zebu).

A lack of interpreters was exacerbated during the COVID pandemic leading to one participant taking action to ensure he understood the maternity care his partner was receiving:

“…there is no-one from my country and I can’t bring any interpreter from my country… and then at the same time because of this Corona everything is closed…they don’t bring interpreter, so I had to pay for train ticket for my friend to come help” (Faizel).

“…there is no-one from my country and I can’t bring any interpreter from my country… and then at the same time because of this Corona everything is closed…they don’t bring interpreter, so I had to pay for train ticket for my friend to come help” (Faizel).

Even in the absence of a language barrier, some men reported a lack of information or conflicting information from health professionals which did not help demystify the alien environment. One participant discussed how conflicting information led to him and his partner preparing for a caesarean section when it became apparent it was not necessary. Another father reported how information about NHS maternity seemed contradictory when they were referred to another hospital:

“Yeah, because they said it is more specialised in complicated pregnancy and they said to us, you might deliver a child there rather than here. But when we went there, in Leeds the doctor said it was not a big deal and we need to go back (to original hospital)” (Ishak).

“Yeah, because they said it is more specialised in complicated pregnancy and they said to us, you might deliver a child there rather than here. But when we went there, in Leeds the doctor said it was not a big deal and we need to go back (to original hospital)” (Ishak).

However, three participants felt they had adequate information about the maternity journey, and they understood what was going on at most stages of the process and this made them feel in control of what was going to happen. One participant reported:

“Our midwife was very good, and she took her time to make us understand every stage of my partner’s pregnancy and what to expect from the hospital” (Saad).

“Our midwife was very good, and she took her time to make us understand every stage of my partner’s pregnancy and what to expect from the hospital” (Saad).

Some men experienced an alien environment due to differences in maternity care practices in the UK compared to home country. They discussed how NHS normal maternity care is mainly led by female midwives whereas in home country, they were led by male doctors. This influenced their experience when accessing care:

“When it comes to decisions we have to make, the midwife directs every discussion to my wife and sometimes my wife had to be the one to say can I ask my husband first about this? In Iran, I feel important in the room because I am the one paying and so the doctor speaks directly to me even though my wife is the one pregnant” (Zebu).

“When it comes to decisions we have to make, the midwife directs every discussion to my wife and sometimes my wife had to be the one to say can I ask my husband first about this? In Iran, I feel important in the room because I am the one paying and so the doctor speaks directly to me even though my wife is the one pregnant” (Zebu).

Four participants raised concerns about the management of post-mature pregnancies in the UK and the perceived liberal approach allowing women’s pregnancies to continue beyond their expected date of delivery. Fathers suggested that there were no delays in home country when pregnancy was overdue due to the fear of the baby dying:

“In Syria, when time is up for the baby to come, the doctors don’t delay at all. They delivered the baby quickly. In the UK, they do not see it as an emergency, and I was worried” (Abdul).

“In Syria, when time is up for the baby to come, the doctors don’t delay at all. They delivered the baby quickly. In the UK, they do not see it as an emergency, and I was worried” (Abdul).

Similarly, fathers reported how even the ‘waters’ breaking, which to them indicated signs of labour, was not treated with the urgency that would have been attached to this in home country. One participant discussed the anxiety and worry caused by this difference in practice:

“The water broke at 04:00 am in the morning. And because this was the first time in the UK, I was very worried and felt helpless. So, we waited for one hour and called 111 and told them what had happened. They told us to wait for signs of labour instead of coming to the hospital… (in home country) If the water breaks and there is delay, even if the baby survives, it is believed the baby will be a sick baby with many health issues like blindness” (Saad).

“The water broke at 04:00 am in the morning. And because this was the first time in the UK, I was very worried and felt helpless. So, we waited for one hour and called 111 and told them what had happened. They told us to wait for signs of labour instead of coming to the hospital… (in home country) If the water breaks and there is delay, even if the baby survives, it is believed the baby will be a sick baby with many health issues like blindness” (Saad).

Living in the asylum system

Participants who were asylum seekers discussed how the asylum system had a negative effect on their experiences of the maternity journey in the UK. This included a lack of financial support from the Home Office leading to living in poverty and one participant described building debt to access maternity appointments:

“I just borrowed money from my friends… because we were moved to London at the time…and I will borrow the Oyster as well…and from the Oyster I used to travel with my wife in the buses to the hospital for maternity appointments…” (Nitin).

“I just borrowed money from my friends… because we were moved to London at the time…and I will borrow the Oyster as well…and from the Oyster I used to travel with my wife in the buses to the hospital for maternity appointments…” (Nitin).

Some fathers shared experiences of being billed for maternity services because they had no recourse to public funds and the effect of this on their maternity journey:

“…they sent a bill for £13,238 to me. So, they ask our details…even I talk to them, I argued to the NHS because they are given the paperwork and we filled the form…the management told to us, they are going to send this paperwork to the Home Office… if they agreed we will take it further, otherwise we will charge you and this made me feel anxious about the situation…… whatever application in future is made to the Home Office will be rejected automatically…so I was really shocked and nervous and this was very stressful” (Nitin).

“…they sent a bill for £13,238 to me. So, they ask our details…even I talk to them, I argued to the NHS because they are given the paperwork and we filled the form…the management told to us, they are going to send this paperwork to the Home Office… if they agreed we will take it further, otherwise we will charge you and this made me feel anxious about the situation…… whatever application in future is made to the Home Office will be rejected automatically…so I was really shocked and nervous and this was very stressful” (Nitin).

One father reflected on how the situation would have been different if he knew he was going to be charged for maternity care:

“… if they had told me to pay earlier, I would ask my wife to abort this child because I can’t pay …” (Aryan).

“… if they had told me to pay earlier, I would ask my wife to abort this child because I can’t pay …” (Aryan).

All fathers discussed how the asylum system prevented them from receiving support during the maternity journey. Female family and friends, who provided support to pregnant women in home country, were unable to travel to the UK to support the current maternity journey. Therefore, the men were the only support for their partner.

“Back home in Iran, we had support from not only our mothers and sisters. Even female family friends and neighbours helped take my wife for maternity appointments, offered to cook for us and came to bath the baby. In the UK, none of our female relatives were here to help” (Zebu).

“Back home in Iran, we had support from not only our mothers and sisters. Even female family friends and neighbours helped take my wife for maternity appointments, offered to cook for us and came to bath the baby. In the UK, none of our female relatives were here to help” (Zebu).

Some participants created alternative networks of friends to help them and their partners through the maternity journey which provided practical support.

“…and because of the community we started to receive food, clothes, and some little money, and some nappies, everything they helped us” (Nitin).

“…and because of the community we started to receive food, clothes, and some little money, and some nappies, everything they helped us” (Nitin).

However, in some cases this support was described as inadequate due to people having other responsibilities and lacking time. One participant discussed how his network was not Iranian and therefore had a different cultural background to the father and his partner, and didn’t necessarily understand their needs:

“And he mentioned his friend and wife who were from Iraqi community who sometimes helped. When they went into labour, to the hospital, they stayed with their kids at home” (Zebu).

“And he mentioned his friend and wife who were from Iraqi community who sometimes helped. When they went into labour, to the hospital, they stayed with their kids at home” (Zebu).

Some fathers discussed how the asylum system in the UK led to him and his partner suddenly being dispersed; being moved by the Home Office to another area of the country. This caused an interruption in their access to NHS services and led to a negative experience of the maternity journey:

“Oh yeah, you know, at that time I have a little bit problem, you know, I was living in a town and then we got moved to another and this affected all our appointments with the midwife.” (Razak).

“Oh yeah, you know, at that time I have a little bit problem, you know, I was living in a town and then we got moved to another and this affected all our appointments with the midwife.” (Razak).

Another father described being dispersed to a new area and being housed in poor quality accommodation which affected the wellbeing of the family:

“The hostel was very bad and with my wife pregnant, we were all in the same room sharing the bath and toilet in a single room. It was very bad with the second baby.” (Saad).

“The hostel was very bad and with my wife pregnant, we were all in the same room sharing the bath and toilet in a single room. It was very bad with the second baby.” (Saad).

One participant highlighted how maternity services differed between areas. This was confusing when dispersed to a new location:

“…about the red book…when we got moved to the new place, the new midwife asked if I have smartphone, I said yes. She said…the application for the maternity red book will be put on your smartphone …he must show us how to use it.” (Faizel).

“…about the red book…when we got moved to the new place, the new midwife asked if I have smartphone, I said yes. She said…the application for the maternity red book will be put on your smartphone …he must show us how to use it.” (Faizel).

Despite some forced migrant fathers being dispersed with their pregnant partners to new locations as part of the asylum-seeking process, one father, who was a refugee had a positive experience:

“No, no, no. Nearly four years I have been here. I don’t move. Never! My wife got pregnant in Wakefield and gave birth in Wakefield” (Aziz).

“No, no, no. Nearly four years I have been here. I don’t move. Never! My wife got pregnant in Wakefield and gave birth in Wakefield” (Aziz).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to explore the lived experiences of forced migrant fathers regarding their maternity journey in the UK. Some fathers experienced being sidelined when their partners were receiving maternity care. This was exacerbated by a lack of support in caring for other children in the family. Fathers reported difficulties negotiating NHS maternity services as they felt alien with entirely different maternity practices to their experiences in home country. Some fathers also experienced difficulties with the English language and received inadequate and often conflicting information about the maternity journey. The fathers reported issues around living in an asylum system where they experienced difficulties maintaining their role as head of the family. They experienced financial difficulties throughout the maternity journey, did not have family and friends to support them and some received inadequate support in their communities. They also reported how being dispersed to new locations during the pregnancy was stressful, with trying to access maternity care for their partner in a new place and feeling helpless due to the new accommodation they were provided not being suitable for their family.

Our findings revealed that participants’ roles varied in the UK compared to the previous roles they took in their home countries. These forced migrant fathers would have been the heads of their household, taking responsibility for the lives of their family members including financially, a role they tried to maintain in the UK. Some forced migrant fathers in our study reported that they would not usually be involved in the maternity journey in their home country as it was the cultural tradition that childbirth was ‘women’s business’. Other men reported that in home country that they would be in control of the maternity journey, paying for their partners maternity care and consequently making care decisions with male doctors. These findings reflect previous Australian studies exploring the experiences of maternity care in migrant men from the Middle East, Africa and South East Asia [31, 73]. They found that men had to renegotiate their role as a father in their new country due to a cultural clash with the expectations on them in home country. Similarly, men in our study had to renegotiate their levels of engagement in the maternity journey. Men who were not traditionally involved had to support their wife as there was no other source of support. Fathers who had been in control had to relinquish this control to their wives who were expected to make the decision about their care in the UK.

There is a lack of previous research around forced migrant fathers’ experiences of the maternity journey with their partners. However, some of the findings resonate with previous studies that have explored forced migrant women’s experiences of maternity care. Like the fathers in this study, women experienced difficulties navigating the NHS system due to a lack of understanding and information provided about health care provision in the UK [74, 75]. Also, women experienced barriers created by the asylum system and sudden dispersal, including poverty, a lack of safe housing and having no support with caring for other children [74–76]. Previous research also found language barriers created challenges when women tried to access care [75]. Some findings from this study also resonate with the wider literature around fathers and the matenity journey more generally. Like the men in this study, native fathers’ have reported feeling sidelined [31, 73] when accessing maternity care, feeling that health professionals focus purely on the mother during maternity encounters. However, for men in this study, the sense of feeling sidelined was exacerbated by language and cultural barriers and a lack of understanding of the maternity system.

In our findings, men reported difficulties in negotiating NHS maternity services due to the cultural differences in maternity practices in home country. In particular, the gender of the care giver was an issue to some men, discussing the peculiarities of midwifery as a female dominated profession leading normal maternity care. They compared this to in home country where male doctors controlled maternity care. For many of the fathers, the gender of the health professionals was critical to how they engaged with maternity services and impacted on how comfortably they disclosed their concerns. Conversely, some men preferred female health professionals for religious reasons, to examine their partners, reflecting previous research that showed Afghan migrant women engaging with female maternity carers were more comfortable in disclosing their health and family concerns [77].

It is well documented in the maternity literature that persistent language barriers during maternity interactions can influence the experience of women and the quality of care received [78–83]. Our findings highlighted similar issues with forced migrant fathers’ experiences being influenced by language. Language barriers exacerbated the sense of an alien environment and feeling a lack of control over maternity interactions. In addition, some men who spoke English felt their only role was as an interpreter for their partner. This contributed to them feeling like outsiders in their maternity journey with the focus being on their partners and how they articulated their experiences. However, some forced migrant fathers found interpreting for their partner a positive experience, feeling valuable when supporting their partner. It is well documented that only professional, trained interpreters should be used in health care [84, 85] to reduce the risk of interpreting errors from informal interpreting [86]. Professional interpreters lead to greater satisfaction and best communication outcomes for patients [87]. However, in reality, professional interpreters are not always available, and this study has highlighted how this can affect the maternity experience of the father as well as the mother.

Even in the absence of a language barrier, men in this study reported that the sense of accessing alien maternity services was exacerbated by a lack of information or misinformation being provided to them and their partners by staff. This reflects studies examining information provision by health professionals to migrants more generally to enhance effective communication between both parties using audiovisual and web-based materials [88–91]. The importance of tailoring information to meet the needs of forced migrant families with cultural and language barriers is important [83].

The effects of living in the asylum system with reliance on the Home Office for housing and subsistence support to take care of their families were particularly evident in the accounts of the forced migrant fathers. This affected the maternity journey in the UK with their partners and more especially their roles as breadwinners for their family. In home country, these fathers were independent with adequate support from family during their partners’ pregnancy, had enough funds to take care of the pregnancy and had stable accommodation. However, in the UK, the restrictions in the asylum system made it difficult for fathers to fulfil their role as breadwinner. Part of this role was managing household bills and the fathers in this study felt responsible when their partners were presented with a bill for the maternity care they had received and felt fearful about being charged, even when they were entitled to health care. In some cases, this led to a hesitation to seek care with their partners, reflecting previous research which found that the fear of being charged can deter migrants from accessing health care, only attending emergency care when health conditions deteriorate [92].

The sudden dispersal of forced migrant families that comes with the asylum process in the UK was evident in the accounts of the fathers in this study. Suddenly moving forced migrant fathers and their families to new areas of the country was often done routinely by the Home Office very late in pregnancy and in some cases soon after delivery. This contravenes the Home Office’s own policy on Healthcare Needs and Pregnancy Dispersal of asylum seekers [93] and led to an interruption in maternity care. The impact of dispersal on the maternity journey has been raised in a number of studies exploring forced migrant women’s experiences [94–96]. Like the men in this study, women have previously reported how being suddenly moved from one location in the UK to another disrupted their maternity care, removed their social networks and led them to live in often unsuitable accommodation [96]. In the case of forced migrant fathers, these issues were exacerbated by the father perceiving that as the head of the family, he needed to take responsibility for the family starting all over again. The fear of being dispersed led to the men feeling a loss of control over their lives, which in turn affected the partners wellbeing. This loss of control of the family impacted on the men’s sense of responsibility in this study.

Inadequate social support in society for forced migrant fathers was evident in their accounts of their experience of the maternity journey in the UK, especially those fathers that had not been living in the UK for very long. Seminal work by Johnsen [97] describes social support as a tangible social network that offers psychological benefits and resources available within the network to help individuals cope. The restrictions attached to the asylum system in the UK left migrant fathers and their families without family and friends, who were in home country and also a lack of social support due to living in an unfamiliar community. Consequently, the fathers had to adjust their roles in the UK and care for their partners in every aspect of the maternity journey, the opposite of the situation in home country. The need for forced migrant women to have adequate social support during the maternity journey is well established [98–101]. This study has highlighted how essential social support is as well for the father to reduce social isolation and improve mental health [99].

Our findings have implications for maternity care providers and the voluntary sector (non-governmental organisations) who provide much of the community support for forced migrant families. Care and support on the maternity journey needs to consider the social and cultural context of the family including the man’s traditional role in the family and how he has to renegotiate this to provide support and care for partners in the UK. The man who has traditionally been the breadwinner in home country and who is not able to maintain this in the UK due to the poverty and unstable housing caused by the asylum process, may feel undermined and for a group of people who already can have poor underlying mental health [102], this could further erode mental wellbeing. An understanding of the role of the father in forced migrant families is important for midwives and other maternity care providers to ensure adequate support is received by the family and not just the pregnant woman.

Family centred approaches to healthcare are being adopted in other areas of healthcare for example in the neonatal intensive care unit with sick babies [103]. However, to overcome the feeling of being an ‘outsider’ in forced migrant men, a family centred approach could be adopted in maternity care. This would build on current care models which centre women in care decisions such as personalised care [104] and continuity of care [105]. Adopting a family centred approach could ensure fathers are positioned centrally along with their partners, leading to feeling included in care provision.

Men’s experiences of the maternity journey resonate with those of their partners [106–108], but as head of the family, these experiences could be more profound with fathers not feeling able to fulfil their role and support their family. Maternity care providers need to consider men’s experiences when providing care. This relates to addressing language barriers, providing information about care but also acknowledging that the experiences in home country may be very different to the UK and information must be tailored to reflect that the father may have either not been involved in maternity care before or may have been in control of it in home country, making decisions for their partners. If fathers are taking on a caring role for their partner in the postnatal period, then antenatal preparation for this role through education classes would enable them to fulfil this role. The implication for the voluntary sector is the need for community support on the maternity journey for the forced migrant fathers as well as women. One approach could be to link families with others in the local community who share a common language, cultural background, and geographical heritage.

Strength and limitations of study

This study used an interpretative phenomenological approach which has the strength of exploring deeper insights into forced migrant fathers’ lived experience. Although the sample was small, the heterogeneity of the group of fathers in terms of country of origin, time in the UK and previous experience of the maternity journey was a strength of the study. In addition, non-English speakers were included in the study, a group often excluded from research due to communication difficulties [109, 110]. Including this group ensured issues relevant to language barriers enriched the dataset and increased the transferability of this study to other forced migrant fathers. The main researcher AM was a migrant father who had experienced maternity care in the UK. It is possible that his pre-existing understanding, and beliefs influenced the line of questioning in the interviews. However, this was mitigated as a strength by using a conversational style, encouraging the exploration of lived experience of the participant. Also, AM maintained a reflexive journal and noted that being an insider helped to build a rapport with participants who can lack trust in authority in the UK [111] leading to more in depth responses to questions. Additionally, verbatim quotations are included to demonstrate that the narrative reflects what was actually said by the interviewees, including the credibility of the findings.

Conclusion

The findings show that the perceptions of forced migrant men experiencing the maternity journey in the UK is influenced by their sociocultural perceptions of the father’s role in home country. It is important that both maternity staff and non-Governmental organisations understand this and intervene to meet the needs of these men, so they can effectively support their partners during the maternity journey. These needs include improving access to services by overcoming language and cultural barriers, receiving accurate, tailored information about the maternity journey and importantly, ensuring support for fathers who in home country would have their families to provide support to pregnant women. Men who traditionally have been the breadwinner for the family need support to continue in that role in a restrictive asylum system that can render men feeling powerless. The finding from this study resonate with the evidence around forced migrant women’s experiences of maternity care, however as the head of the household, the father has additional needs which must be considered within maternity care in order that he can provide the best possible support to his partner throughout the maternity journey. This support is essential in this context where the family are experiencing an alien environment and a lack of wider support from family through the maternity journey.
