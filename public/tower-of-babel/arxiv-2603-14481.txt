Convergence of Two Time-Scale Stochastic Approximation: A Martingale Approach
Mathukumalli Vidyasagar

Abstract
In this paper, we analyze the two time-scale stochastic approximation (TTSSA) algorithm introduced in [ 1 ] using a martingale approach. Until now, almost all of the analysis has been based on the ODE approach. The martingale approach leads to simple sufficient conditions for the iterations to be bounded almost surely, as well as estimates on the rate of convergence of the mean-squared error of the TTSSA algorithm to zero. Thus the boundedness of the iterations is a conclusion and not a hypothesis. Our theory is applicable to nonlinear equations, in contrast to many papers in the TTSSA literature which assume that the equations are linear. The convergence of TTSSA is proved in the “almost sure” sense, in contrast to earlier papers on TTSSA that establish convergence in distribution, convergence in the mean, and the like. Moreover, in this paper we establish different rates of convergence for the fast and the slow subsystems, perhaps for the first time. Finally, all of the above results to continue to hold in the case where the two measurement errors have nonzero conditional mean, and/or have conditional variances that grow without bound as the iterations proceed. This is in contrast to previous papers which assumed that the errors form a martingale difference sequence with uniformly bounded conditional variance. It is shown that when the measurement errors have zero conditional mean and the conditional variance remains bounded, the mean-squared error of the iterations converges to zero at a rate of o ⁡ ( t − η ) o(t^{-\eta}) for all η ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \eta\in(0,1) . This improves upon the rate of O ( t − 2 / 3 ) O(t^{-2/3}) proved in [ 2 ] (which is the best bound available to date). Our bound is virtually the same as the rate of O ⁡ ( t − 1 ) O(t^{-1}) proved in [ 3 ] , but for a Polyak-Ruppert averaged version of TTSSA, and not directly. Rates of convergence are also established for the case where the errors have nonzero conditional mean and/or unbounded conditional variance.

Introduction

1.1
Problem Statement

In this paper, we study the problem of solving
coupled

nonlinear equations of the form

{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})={\bf 0},\quad{\bf g}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})={\bf 0},

(1)

where
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{l}
, and
{\bf f}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{l}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

{\bf g}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{l}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{l}
.
To achieve this objective, we have available only
noise-corrupted

measurements of
{\bf f}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
{\bf g}(\cdot,\cdot)
.
Specifically, let
{\bf y}_{t+1}
denote a noisy measurement of
{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t})
,
and let
{\bf z}_{t+1}
denote a noisy measurement of
{\bf g}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t})
.
Then the iterations proceed as follows:

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+\alpha_{t}{\bf y}_{t+1},\quad{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}={\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t}+\beta_{t}{\bf z}_{t+1},

(2)

where
\alpha_{t},\beta_{t}
denote the step sizes.
This approach is known in the literature as
Two Time-Scale
Stochastic Approximation (TTSSA)
.
It was first introduced in
[
1
]
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{0}
,
and the measurement sequences
{\bf y}_{1}^{t}:=({\bf y}_{1},\cdots,{\bf y}_{t})
,

{\bf z}_{1}^{t}:=({\bf z}_{1},\cdots,{\bf z}_{t})
.
Note that there are no
{\bf y}_{0}
and
{\bf z}_{0}
.
For convenience, given a random variable
X

E_{t}(X)
denote the
conditional expectation
E(X|{\mathcal{F}}_{t})
.
To characterize the nature of the noisy measurements, define

{\bf x}_{t}:=E_{t}({\bf y}_{t+1}-{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t})),\quad{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}:={\bf y}_{t+1}-{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t})-{\bf x}_{t},

(3)

and similarly, define

{\bf w}_{t}:=E_{t}({\bf z}_{t+1}-{\bf g}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t})),\quad{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1}:={\bf z}_{t+1}-{\bf g}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t})-{\bf w}_{t}.

(4)

From the “tower” property of conditional expectations

[
4
, Sec. 9.7, p. 88]
, it follows that

E_{t}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1})={\bf 0},\quad E_{t}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1})={\bf 0}.

(5)

Thus one can think of
{\bf x}_{t}
and
{\bf w}_{t}
as the “biases” of the two
measurements
{\bf y}_{t+1}
and
{\bf z}_{t+1}
respectively,
and of
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}
and
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1}
as the “unpredictable” part of the errors.
Note that, as a result of the above definitions, it follows that

E_{t}(\|{\bf y}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2})=\|{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t}))+{\bf x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+E_{t}(\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}),

(6)

E_{t}(\|{\bf z}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2})=\|{\bf g}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t}))+{\bf w}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+E_{t}(\|{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}).

(7)

With these definitions, we can write

{\bf y}_{t+1}={\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t})+{\bf x}_{t}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1},\quad{\bf z}_{t+1}={\bf g}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t})+{\bf w}_{t}+{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1}.

(8)

The objective of Two Time-Scale Stochastic Approximation (TTSSA)
is to determine conditions under which the iterations in (
2
)
converge to a solution of (
1
), and if possible, obtain
estimate of the
rate
at which convergence takes place.
The objective of this paper is to present the best results thus far
on the convergence and behavior of TTSSA.

1.3
Introduction to TTSSA

Now let us return to the coupled system of equations (
1
).
If the ODE

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\dot{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\\
\dot{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\\
{\bf g}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\end{array}\right]

is globally asymptotically stable, then (
1
) can be solved
using conventional SA applied to the map
({\bf f},{\bf g}):{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{l}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{l}
.
However, there are several applications where this stability property
does not hold.
One such example arises in Actor-Critic methods of Reinforcement Learning (RL),
in which the above ODE is not stable; however, a
singularly perturbed

version of it in the form

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\dot{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\\
\epsilon\dot{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\\
{\bf g}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\end{array}\right]

(13)

is globally asymptotically stable for
all sufficiently small
\epsilon
.
In such a case, the update equations take the form (
2
).
Note that the
single step size
in (
10
) is replaced by a

pair of step sizes
in (
2
).
For this reason, (
2
) is referred to as Two Time-Scale Stochastic
Approximation (TTSSA).

Overview of the Paper

2.1
Contributions of the Paper

The main objective of this paper
is to extend the martingale approach of
[
22
]

to the two time-scale stochastic approximation (TTSSA) algorithm
in (
2
).
Some of the substantial outcomes of this extension are the following:

•

The theory is applicable to
nonlinear

contrast to many papers in the TTSSA literature which assume that the equations
are linear.

•

Our analysis applies to the case where the noisy measurements

grow without bound as the iterations proceed.
So far as we are aware, no other paper studies this case.

•

The convergence is proved in the “almost sure” sense,
in contrast to earlier papers on TTSSA that establish convergence
in distribution, convergence in the mean, and the like.
Since every run of an iterative algorithm results in
exactly one

it is highly desirable that

to the desired limit.
We also provide estimates of the
rate of convergence
to the
solution of (
1
).

•

Throughout it is assumed that the singularly perturbed system
(

) is globally exponentially stable.
When the measurements are unbiased and the errors have bounded variance

{\bf x}_{t}={\bf 0}
,
{\bf w}_{t}={\bf 0}
, and
E_{t}(\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2})

and
E_{t}(\|{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2})
are both uniformly bounded with respect to
t
),
it is shown that the mean-squared error of the
TTSSA algorithm converges to zero
at a rate of
o(t^{-\eta})
for all
\eta\in(0,1)
.
In contrast, in
[
2
]
(which is the best bound available to date),
the rate of convergence is
t^{-2/3}
, which is worse than the above.
In
[
3
]
, a convergence rate of
O(t^{-1})
is
established; however, it is for a
Polyak-Ruppert averaged
version of
(
2
), and not for (
2
) directly.
In contrast, our results are for the original TTSSA of (
2
).
Note that there is virtually no difference between

and

o(t^{-\eta})
for all
\eta\in(0,1)
.
Moreover, in this paper we establish different rates of convergence for

Convergence rates are also established for the case where the

Most papers do not even study this situation.

Throughout, all random variables are assumed to be defined on some

Thus
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}_{t+1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}

Given a random variable

For background material on conditional expectations and their properties,

Theorem 1
.

(Robbins-Siegmund Theorem
[
23
]
)
Suppose
\{z_{t}\},\{f_{t}\},\{g_{t}\},\{h_{t}\}
are
stochastic processes taking values in
[0,\infty)
, adapted to some
filtration
\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}
, satisfying

E_{t}(z_{t+1})\leq(1+f_{t})z_{t}+g_{t}-h_{t}\mbox{ a.s.},\;\forall t,

(14)

where, as before,
E_{t}(z_{t+1})
is a shorthand for
E(z_{t+1}|{\mathcal{F}}_{t})
.
Then, on the set

\Omega_{0}:=\{\omega\in\Omega:\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}f_{t}(\omega)<\infty\}\cap\{\omega:\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}g_{t}(\omega)<\infty\},

we have that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}z_{t}
exists, and in addition,

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}h_{t}(\omega)<\infty
.
In particular, if
P(\Omega_{0})=1
, then
\{z_{t}\}
is bounded
almost surely and converges almost surely to some random variable
X
,
and in addition,
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}h_{t}(\omega)<\infty
almost surely.

Theorem
1
gives a sufficient condition for convergence,
but does not say anything about the

The concept of almost sure convergence does not lend itself naturally

For this purpose, we introduce the following definition, inspired by

[
34
]
:

Definition 1
.

Suppose
\{Y_{t}\}
is a stochastic process, and
\{f_{t}\}

is a sequence of positive numbers.
We say that

1.

Y_{t}=O(f_{t})
if
\{Y_{t}/f_{t}\}
is bounded almost surely.

2.

Y_{t}=\Omega(f_{t})
if
Y_{t}
is positive almost surely, and

\{f_{t}/Y_{t}\}
is bounded almost surely.

3.

Y_{t}=\Theta(f_{t})
if
Y_{t}
is both
O(f_{t})
and
\Omega(f_{t})
.

4.

Y_{t}=o(f_{t})
if
Y_{t}/f_{t}\rightarrow 0
almost surely as

.

The next theorem builds on Theorem
1
and provides bounds
on the rate of convergence.

Theorem 2
.

(See
[
8
, Theorem 5.2]

Suppose
\{z_{t}\},\{f_{t}\},\{g_{t}\},\{\alpha_{t}\}

,

, adapted to some

\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}
.
Suppose further that

where

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}f_{t}(\omega)<\infty,\quad\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}g_{t}(\omega)<\infty,\quad\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}(\omega)=\infty.

Then

(16)

3.2
Assumptions on the Singularly Perturbed System

In this subsection, we state the various assumptions made on the singularly
perturbed system (
13
).
At a first glance, it might appear that there are quite a few assumptions.
However,
all
of these assumptions hold naturally for

linear
singularly perturbed systems of the form

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\dot{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\\
\epsilon\dot{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\\
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\end{array}\right].

(18)

The standard assumptions for this problem are that the matrices

A_{22}
and
A_{r}:=A_{11}-A_{12}A_{22}^{-1}A_{21}
are both
Hurwitz, i.e., all of their eigenvalues have negative real parts.
With these assumptions, standard results guarantee that (
18
)
is globally exponentially stable for
all sufficiently small

\epsilon
.
This in turn guarantees the existence of suitable quadratic Lyapunov
functions.

Though there are a great many assumptions below, they are natural
“nonlinear” generalizations of the above simple assumptions; they are
taken from
[
32
]
.
We use the suffix and/or subscript
F
to denote the
“fast” subsystem, and
S
to denote the “slow” subsystem.
Note that
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(t)
is the “fast” variable while
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)

is the “slow” variable.

Throughout, we use the abbreviation
GLC
to denote the set of
globally Lipschitz functions, with the domain and range being evident
from the context.
Further, if
{\bf h}\in GLC
, then
L_{\bf h}
denotes its global Lipschitz constant.

The first set of assumptions have to do with the existence of equilibria.

(F-G)

Both
{\bf f}
and
{\bf g}
belong to
GLC

(with Lipschitz constants
L_{\bf f}
and
L_{\bf g}
respectively).

(A1)

There exists a
GLC
function
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{l}
such that

{\bf g}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))={\bf 0},\;\forall{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

(19)

so that, for each
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the vector
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{*}={\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})

is an equilibrium of the “fast” ODE

\epsilon\dot{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}={\bf g}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}).

(A2)

We have that

{\bf f}({\bf 0},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\bf 0}))={\bf 0},

(20)

so that
{\bf 0}
is an equilibrium of the “slow” ODE

\dot{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}={\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})).

The next set of assumptions have to do with the existence of suitable
Lyapunov functions for both the slow and the fast systems.

(VS1)

There exists a
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
function
V_{S}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

and positive constants
a_{S},b_{S},c_{S}
such that

a_{S}\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq V_{S}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\leq b_{S}\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}^{2},\;\forall{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

(21)

\langle\nabla V_{S}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}),{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))\rangle\leq-c_{S}\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}^{2},\;\forall{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

(22)

(VS2)

\nabla V_{S}(\cdot)
belongs to
GLC
, and

\nabla V_{S}({\bf 0})={\bf 0}
.

(VF1)

There exists a
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
function

and constants

(23)

(VF2)

GLC

Further, (i)

, and (ii)

, for all

For brevity, we denote the Lipschitz constants

3.3
Stability of the Singularly Perturbed System

In this subsection, it is shown that the assumptions in Section

3.2
imply the global exponential stability of the singularly
perturbed system (
13
) for
sufficiently small

values of
\epsilon
.
The contents of this subsection mimic
[
32
]
, and
form the starting point for the analysis of the TTSSA algorithm of
(
2
).

Lemma 1
.

Suppose the assumptions in Section
3.2
hold.
Then there exist constants
D_{1},D_{2},D_{3}
such that

\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}V_{F}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}),{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\rangle\leq D_{1}\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}-{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}^{2}+D_{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}\cdot\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}-{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2},

(27)

\langle\nabla V_{S}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}),{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})-{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\rangle\leq D_{3}\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}\cdot\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}-{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}.

(28)

Remark:

The inequalities (
27
) and (
28
)
are
assumed to hold
in
[
32
]
.
However, it is shown here that these need not be assumed – they are a
consequence of the assumptions already made.

Proof.

Recall that
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}V_{F}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))={\bf 0}
.
Therefore

\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}V_{F}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\|_{2}=\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}V_{F}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}V_{F}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))\|_{2}\leq L_{F}\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}-{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}.

Next,

{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})={\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))+[{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})-{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))].

Hence

\|{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\|_{2}\leq\|{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))\|_{2}+\|{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})-{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))\|_{2}.

Next, note that, for
{\bf u}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\bf v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{l}
, we have that

\|({\bf u},{\bf v})\|_{2}\leq\|{\bf u}\|_{2}+\|{\bf v}\|_{2}.

Consequently

\|{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))\|_{2}\leq L_{\bf f}(\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}+\|{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2})\leq L_{\bf f}(1+L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}.

Next

\|{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})-{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))\|_{2}\leq L_{\bf f}\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}-{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2},

Combining these bounds shows that

\|{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\|_{2}\leq L_{\bf f}(1+L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}+L_{\bf f}\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}-{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}.

Thus we have bounds for the norms of both the terms in the inner product
in (
18
).
Now applying Schwarz’ inequality gives (
18
) with

D_{1}=L_{F}L_{\bf f},D_{2}=L_{F}L_{\bf f}(1+L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}).

To derive (
18
), observe that

\|\nabla V({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}\leq L_{S}\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}.

Also, as shown above

\|{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})-{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))\|_{2}\leq L_{\bf f}\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}-{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}.

Combining all these bounds and applying Schwarz’ inequality leads to
(
28
) with

D_{3}=L_{S}L_{\bf f}(1+L_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}).

This completes the proof.
∎

With this preliminary result out of the way, we follow

[
32
]
to construct a Lyapunov function
for the singularly perturbed system (
13
) in the form

V_{d}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}):=(1-d)V_{S}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})+dV_{F}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}),

(29)

where
d\in(0,1)
is a constant chosen by the user.

Theorem 3
.

Under the stated assumptions, we have that

\dot{V}_{d}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\leq-{\bf u}^{\top}M(\epsilon){\bf u},

(30)

where

{\bf u}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}\\
\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}-{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}\end{array}\right],

(31)

M(\epsilon):=\left[\begin{array}[]{ll}(1-d)C_{S}&-dD_{3}\\
-(1-d)D_{2}&(dC_{F}D_{1})/\epsilon\end{array}\right].

(32)

Moreover, the matrix
[M(\epsilon)+M^{\top}(\epsilon)]/2
is positive definite,
and the system (
13
) is globally exponentially stable, whenever

\epsilon<\epsilon^{*}(d):=\frac{4d(1-d)C_{S}C_{F}D_{1}}{[(1-d)D_{2}+dD_{3}]^{2}}.

(33)

Proof.

The proof consists of computing the time derivative
\dot{V}_{d}
, via

\dot{V}_{d}=(1-d)\langle\nabla V_{S}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}),{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\rangle+d\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}V_{F}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}),{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}(,{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\rangle+\frac{d}{\epsilon}\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}V_{F}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}),{\bf g}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}(,{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\rangle.

We will analyze each of the three terms on the right side (without the
constants
1-d
and
d
), using Lemma
1
.
First

\displaystyle\langle\nabla V_{S}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}),{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\nabla V_{S}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}),{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))\rangle+\langle\nabla V_{S}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}),{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})-{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-C_{S}\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}^{2}+D_{3}\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}\cdot\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}-{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}.

Second

\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}V_{F}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}),{\bf f}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\rangle\leq D_{1}\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}-{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}^{2}+D_{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}\cdot\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}-{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}.

Third

\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}V_{F}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}),{\bf g}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}(,{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\rangle\leq-C_{F}\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}-{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}^{2}.

Combining these three bounds, and using the notation
{\bf u}
defined in
(
31
) gives (
30
) with
M(\epsilon)
defined as in (
32
).
For the final step, note that

[M(\epsilon)+M^{\top}(\epsilon)]/2=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}(1-d)C_{S}&-[(1-d)D_{2}+dD_{3}]/2\\
-[(1-d)D_{2}+dD_{3}]/2&(dC_{F}D_{1})/\epsilon\end{array}\right].

The trace of this matrix is always positive, while the determinant is
positive whenever (
33
) holds.
∎

Note that if we actually know the various constants in
M(\epsilon)
, we can
try to find an “optimal” choice of
d
so as to maximize
\epsilon^{*}(d)
.
However, in TTSSA, the fixed constant
\epsilon
is replaced by a

sequence

\{\epsilon_{t}\}
that converges to zero.
Therefore there is no gain in trying to optimize the choice of
d
,
and we might as well takd
d=0.5
.
However, we do not do this, just in case some future researcher may
wish to use a different choice of
d
.

4.2.1
Proof of Theorem
4

The proof of Theorem
4
is based on Theorem
1
.
We use the Lyapunov function
V_{d}
of (
29
), and show
that it satisfies (
14
) with
z_{t}=V_{d}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t})
,
for suitable choices of the terms
f_{t},g_{t}
and

, and
\alpha_{t}

as above.
This is accomplished by obtaining suitable upper bounds for

V_{d}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1})
.
Because

V_{d}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1})=(1-d)V_{S}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1})+dV_{F}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}),

we can find bounds for each term separately and combine the bounds.

Finding these upper bounds is facilitated by the following fact,
which is stated as
[
35
, Eq. (2.4)]
.
It states the following:
Suppose
J:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
, and that
\nabla J(\cdot)

is
GLC
with Lipschitz constant
L
.
Then

J({\bf x}+{\bf y})\leq J({\bf x})+\langle\nabla J({\bf x}),{\bf y}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|{\bf y}\|_{2}^{2}.

(50)

Note that the above bound is also the upper bound given in

[
36
, Eq. 2.1.9]
.
The other part (the lower bound) implies that, if the above bound
holds with the inequality reversed, then
J(\cdot)
is convex.
However, no such assumption is made here.

Now we begin by finding an upper bound for
V_{S}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1})
.
Using (
50
) gives

\displaystyle V_{S}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{S}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+\alpha_{t}{\bf y}_{t+1})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle V_{S}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})+\alpha_{t}\langle\nabla V_{S}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}),{\bf y}_{t+1}\rangle+\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{2}L_{S}\|{\bf y}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}.

Applying the operation
E_{t}(\cdot)
to both sides, invoking (
3

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we have analyzed
the two time-scale stochastic approximation (TTSSA) algorithm
introduced in
[
1
]
using a martingale approach.
This approach leads to simple sufficient conditions for the iterations
to be bounded almost surely, as well as estimates on the rate of convergence
of the mean-squared error of the TTSSA algorithm to zero.
Our theory is applicable to
nonlinear
equations, in
contrast to many papers in the TTSSA literature which assume that the equations
are linear.
The convergence of TTSSA is proved in the “almost sure” sense,
in contrast to earlier papers on TTSSA that establish convergence
in distribution, convergence in the mean, and the like.
Moreover, in this paper we establish different rates of convergence for
the fast and the slow subsystems, perhaps for the first time.
Finally, all of the above results to continue to hold in the case where
the two measurement errors have nonzero conditional
mean, and/or have conditional variances that grow without bound as the
iterations proceed.
This is in contrast to previous papers which assumed that the errors form
a martingale difference sequence with uniformly bounded conditional variance.

It is shown that when the measurement errors have zero conditional
mean and the conditional variance remains bounded, the mean-squared
error of the iterations converges to zero
at a rate of
o(t^{-\eta})
for all
\eta\in(0,1)
.
This improves upon the rate of
O(t^{-2/3})
proved in

[
2
]
(which is the best bound available to date).
Our bound is virtually the same as the rate of
O(t^{-1})
proved in

[
3
]
, but for a
Polyak-Ruppert averaged
version of
TTSSA, and not directly.
Rates of convergence are also established for the case where the
errors have nonzero conditional mean and/or unbounded conditional variance.

Realizing the “optimal” convergence rate of

o(t^{-\eta})
for all
\eta\in(0,1)
requires the choice

\alpha_{t}=O(t^{-1}),\quad\beta_{t}=O(t^{-(1-\Delta)}),

where
\Delta
is arbitrarily small.
With this choice, we have that
\alpha_{t}/\beta_{t}=O(t^{-\Delta})
.
Thus
\beta_{t}
decays at “almost” the same rate as
\alpha_{t}
,
chich is somewhat counter-intuitive.
A good topic for future research is to find good rates of convergence for
TTSSA when
\beta_{t}
decays much faster than
\alpha_{t}
.
