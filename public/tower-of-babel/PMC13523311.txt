Background

Mycoplasma pneumoniae-induced rash and mucositis (MIRM) is an extrapulmonary complication of a respiratory tract infection characterized by prominent mucositis with or without vesiculobullous and targetoid skin eruptions. For many years, it was considered among the spectrum of erythema multiforme (EM), often also identified as atypical Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS) or toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN). However, the syndrome presents some evident distinct epidemiological, clinical, and histological features; therefore, since 2014, it has been considered a separate medical entity [1–4]. It is most commonly observed in young patients in winter. Similarly to EM, there is a male predilection. Prodromal symptoms, including fever, malaise, and cough, typically precede mucocutaneous symptoms by an average of 7–10 days [5, 6]. The disease course is milder, with lower rates of sequelae and mortality compared to EM, SJS, and TEN [6–8]. The hallmark of the disease is the involvement of mucosal surfaces [9–11]. The oral cavity is involved in the majority of patients, with symptoms ranging from erosions and ulcers to denuded tissue. Ocular involvement is the second most common extrapulmonary finding in MIRS, presenting as purulent bilateral conjunctivitis, photophobia, pseudomembrane formation, ulceration, and eyelid edema [2, 7, 12]. It is increasingly evident that respiratory pathogens other than Mycoplasma can cause a picture similar to MIRM, and other infectious etiologic factors have been described. Therefore, the term reactive infectious mucocutaneous eruption (RIME) has recently emerged to encompass a broader spectrum of pathogens, including Chlamydophila pneumoniae, SARS-CoV-2, and influenza B, which can act alone or in conjunction with Mycoplasma pneumoniae to cause mucocutaneous disease [6, 11, 13–16]. The pathophysiology of MIRM is not fully understood but may be distinct from that of erythema multiforme or Stevens–Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis. B-cell involvement, resulting in cutaneous immune complex deposition and complement formation, or molecular mimicry between mycoplasma’s adhesion molecules and keratinocytes, has been suggested [5, 9, 17].

MIRM is a rare disease; the literature reports on this topic are still very scarce, and we found only one report describing the case of a Polish patient with a rare salivary gland involvement [18]. We report a case of a 5-year-old boy from Poland with acute Mycoplasma pneumoniae respiratory tract infection presenting severe mucocutaneous lesions.

Case presentation

A 5-year-old Polish boy was initially admitted to the acute admissions unit of the Children’s Health Centre in Poznan, Poland, due to prolonged fever and erosive-ulcerative lesions on the oral mucosa and lips, and slightly elevated, pink, rash-like skin eruptions in June 2024. After 2 days, he was transferred to the department of observational and communicable diseases at this facility, where he remained until discharge.

During the 5 days prior to admission, a child had had an increased body temperature at a level of 38.5–39 °C, accompanied by a productive, paroxysmal cough. He was seen by a pediatrician who, based on the examination, diagnosed pneumonia and prescribed antibiotics (amoxicillin) and expectorants (erdosteine). The treatment duration was 5 days. The oral and labial lesions developed after the second dose of the antibiotic was administered. It was accompanied by the redness of the conjunctivae. Two days later, skin lesions on the trunk and limbs erupted. Vesicles and wheals with erythematous borders were present on the trunk and limbs, while elevated, pink, itchy, rash-like lesions appeared on the arms. The patient had difficulties in eating, presented loss of appetite, and had not defecated for 7 days before admission to the hospital. He did not vomit. The diuresis was initially normal, then became slightly reduced.

Upon admission, the boy presented with a fever and was weakened. The air passage was preserved, and the blood pressure was 105/58 mmHg. Upon examination, the redness of the conjunctivae and target skin lesions, accompanied by polymorphic skin eruptions including patches, papules, and vesicles, was revealed on the trunk and limbs. In the oral examination, hemorrhagic crusts and bleeding were observed on the lips, while erosions and ulcers with gelatinous coating were found intraorally. The tongue and throat were massively coated. Figures 1, 2 and 3 illustrate the oral presentation of the disease, including visible labial crusting, gelatinous blister covers, and erythema. Electrocardiography and echocardiography did not reveal heart insufficiency; the heart dimensions and contractility, as assessed by the pericardium, were normal, and the coronary arteries were undilated. A minor mitral valve incompetence was revealed. The chest X-ray showed a slightly increased peribronchial pattern in the parahilar region of the lower lung fields. The lung fields were free from focal lesions; the costophrenic angles were clear. Blood examinations revealed insignificantly increased erythrocyte count (5.33 × 1012/L) and increased leucocyte count (15.9 × 109/L), accompanied by lymphocytopenia (18.4%), neutrophilia (71.8%), decreased MCV (68.5 fL), and thrombocytosis (632 g/L). C-reactive protein (23.3 mg/L), ferritin (116.2 ng/mL), and IgM (2.44 g/L) were elevated. Other parameters, including serum glucose, troponin I, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), Anti-streptolysin-O (ASO), Na, K, IgA, and IgG, were within the range of the norm. Urinalysis did not show any abnormalities. Serologic tests for Epstein–Barr virus yielded negative results for IgM, while IgG was reactive. It was positive for Mycoplasma pneumoniae in both classes (IgM and IgG). The Mycoplasma pneumoniae genetic material was detected in the pneumonia panel (PCR).

The initial diagnosis on admission to the hospital was Kawasaki disease. Based on the results of clinical examination and accessory investigations, a final diagnosis of MIRM was established.

Treatment included systemic and local steroids (hydrocortisone, prednisolone), antibiotics (gentamicin), mucolytics (ambroxol), analgesics (paracetamol), and local antifungal agents (nystatin). The drugs were administered for 2 weeks until the patient was discharged from the hospital. Due to feeding disturbances in the acute phase of the disease, there was also an attempt at tube-feeding during hospital stay, though ineffective.

The patient was discharged after 2 weeks in generally good condition, with the oral lesions in remission, though not fully healed. Some crusting and occasional bleeding from the lips were still present. A follow-up visit took place in the Department of Dental Surgery, Periodontology, and Oral Mucosa Diseases at Poznan University of Medical Sciences 2 months later. The boy showed no systemic signs or symptoms. Skin eruptions were fully healed. The dental examination revealed tender, erythematous patches on the buccal mucosa and upper lip. Mycologic tests were negative. A mouth rinse containing neomycin, nystatin, hydrocortisone, glycerin, and Anaesthesin was prescribed for 1 week; the patient did not return for further follow-up.

Discussion and conclusions

Mycoplasma pneumoniae-induced rash and mucositis (MIRM) is characterized by prominent mucositis with limited skin involvement [7, 17, 19]. Urogenital lesions are also quite common. It is predominantly observed in children and adolescents [2, 7]. Diagnostic criteria for MIRM proposed by Canavan et al. include: minimal skin involvement (< 10% total body surface area), vesiculobullous or scattered atypical target lesions, two or more mucosal sites involved, and clinical and laboratory evidence of M. pneumoniae infection. The last criterion should be supported by clinical findings of atypical pneumonia, including an increase in specific IgM antibodies and/or the detection of the microbial genome by PCR [1, 20]. Distinguishing among the clinical spectrum of erythema multiforme/Stevens–Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis, as well as MIRM, is also based on the evaluation of skin lesions, which in MIRM are milder [2, 7, 17, 21]. The patient described in our case report presented signs and symptoms typical of MIRM. That included a young age of onset, a history of an upper respiratory infection, prominent mucositis, and atypical cutaneous target lesions. Positive Mycoplasma pneumoniae immunoglobulin M antibodies and positive PCR were also revealed. However, based on our knowledge, there was only oral mucosal involvement, while the genital mucosa was free from pathological eruptions. Other mucosal regions affected included the conjunctiva and the pharyngolaryngeal region.

Histologic findings of mucocutaneous lesions in MIRM include cutaneous leukocytoclastic vasculitis, characterized by neutrophilic perivascular infiltration at lesional sites and keratinocyte apoptosis [7, 17, 18]. However, there are no evident pathognomonic histopathologic determinants in MIRM. Many overlapping features with both SJS and EM, such as the above-mentioned apoptotic keratinocytes, perivascular dermal infiltrate, and full-thickness epidermal necrosis and detachment, make the diagnosis often challenging. The distinct biopsy features enabling histopathological differentiation between these diseases remain controversial. No significant, consistent histologic distinctions in specimens from erythema multiforme, SJS, and TEN were revealed in Rzany et al.’s study [22]. Meanwhile, Wetter and Camilleri identified histopathologic features in drug-induced SJS that were absent in MIRM or immunization-induced SJS in some examined specimens. According to the authors, SJS may exhibit more necrotic keratinocytes, denser dermal infiltrates, microscopic red blood cell extravasation, pigment incontinence, and parakeratosis compared to MIRM [23]. Some characteristic histologic findings help distinguish EM from SJS-TEN. As indicated in the study by Amode et al., a predominantly inflammatory pattern with lichenoid infiltrates and basal epidermal necrosis is typical of EM, whereas a predominantly necrotic pattern with major epidermal necrosis and minimal inflammatory infiltration is characteristic of SJS [24]. Meanwhile, MIRM exhibits a distinct histologic pattern, resembling TEN, characterized by widespread epidermal necrosis and apoptotic keratinocytes at the periphery, accompanied by a minimal inflammatory infiltrate [24].

Differential diagnosis in this case should, therefore, include EM, SJS, TEN, and Kawasaki disease [25]. As the oral lesions erupted shortly after the introduction of the first antibiotic, followed by the development of skin and ocular symptoms, a drug-induced reaction could also be considered. However, typical targetoid skin lesions on acral surfaces and confirmation of Mycoplasma pneumoniae as the source of the infection support a diagnosis of MIRM.

Differentiation between MIRM, EM, and SJS/TEN is essential for establishing treatment and prognosis. Identifying Mycoplasma pneumoniae as a causative factor helps prevent misdiagnosis of drug allergies. Once incorrectly recognized, it can adversely affect future treatment options. Serologic testing (immunoglobulin M), culture, or PCR is used to diagnose MIRM [7, 25, 26].

EM is often self-limiting. In more severe cases, it can be treated with topical or systemic corticosteroids. Antivirals (acyclovir) are often useful in resistant cases, as the most common cause is human herpesvirus 1 (HHV-1). Other treatment options include nonsteroidal drugs like dapsone, rituximab, and apremilast. Supportive care of the oral mucosa and pain management is required [7, 27].

The treatment algorithm for MIRM has not yet been clearly established, and it has not been widely studied in randomized controlled trials. Causative therapy based on antibiotics directed against M. pneumoniae is most commonly used, along with topical or systemic steroids, anesthetics, coating agents, and intravenous hydration and immunoglobulins [6, 7, 9, 12, 26, 27]. MIRM has an overall good prognosis. The majority of patients fully recover, and the recurrences are rare [12, 28]. As the ophthalmic manifestations are common, follow-up with an ophthalmologist is recommended [1].

Meanwhile, a significant risk of complications, including sepsis, gastrointestinal bleeding, pulmonary and cardiac problems, and death, must be considered in case of SJS/TEN. The treatment includes withdrawal of offending agents and comprehensive supportive care. Most of the cases should be managed in an intensive care unit or burn units [7, 27].

We recommend that pneumonia accompanied by mucocutaneous eruptions, especially in young patients, should raise clinical suspicion of MIRM. Additional case reports are needed to enhance our understanding of the characteristics of MIRM and its response to therapy.
