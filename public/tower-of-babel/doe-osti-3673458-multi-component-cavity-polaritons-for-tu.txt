 Executive Summary
In this report I summarize the accomplishments achieved during the project period for award DE-SC0022134.
The members of our research team aimed to use this project to understand how quantum entanglement
persists and evolves temporally due to the formation of hybrid states of light and matter known as cav-
ity polaritons. These excitations form when one confines light spatially on length scales close to optical
wavelengths in the presence of molecules whose frontier electrons are capable of interacting strongly with
photons. While entanglement remains a critical resource for quantum information science (QIS), the com-
munity’s understanding of the connections between polariton properties and photon-electron entanglement
remain incomplete.
 To attain this understanding, the lead principal investigator (PI), Aaron S. Rury, organized a research
team to help produce this understanding, as shown on the previous page. The research team identified four
research thrusts for the project: 1) develop extended definitions of multipartite entanglement using algebraic
approaches, 2) establish spectroscopic observables whose dynamics carry information on entanglement in
strongly coupled multi-component systems, 3) develop multi-cavity and multi-chromophore polariton sam-
ples suitable for generating and characterizing light-matter entanglement, and 4) understand the signatures of
intermolecular exciton delocalization in light-absorbing macromolecules towards manipulating light-matter
entanglement in multi-chromophore polariton samples. The successful achievement of these aims leveraged
the respective team members’ expertise in fundamental mathematics, applied mathematics, physics theory,
theoretical and experimental optical and magnetic spectroscopy, and nano-fabrication. The lead PI matched
investigators’ expertise with specific thrust areas to form four sub-teams: math, spectroscopy theory, spec-
troscopy experiment, and exciton delocalization.
 The research team has able to generate important results in each of these thrust area to help push for-
ward the community’s understanding entanglement generation and characterization in hybrid light-matter
systems. The math sub-team members used algebraic methods and standard definitions of entanglement
generation to produce rules for specific invariant quantities related to quantum correlations in mixed states
of mutliple qubits. The spectroscopy theory sub-team used advanced dynamical models to show how classi-
cal light fields produce quantum entanglement between photon pairs and between photons and qubits within
an electromagnetic cavity. The spectroscopy experiment sub-team was able to demonstrate cavity polariton
formation using multiple chromophores and multiple cavities. Furthermore, precision spectroscopic mea-
surements enabled the spectroscopy sub-team to demonstrate the conditions under which cavity polariton
formation suppresses disorder in molecular ensembles and the spectroscopic signatures of that suppression.
Lastly, the exciton delocalization sub-team was able to demonstrate quantum-based rules for determining
how singlet and triplet molecular excitations spread over the constituents of macromolecular chromophores.
By ligating ions carrying unpaired electrons, the exciton delocalization sub-team was able to show that spin
polarized states delocalize in rigid molecular structures. When combined, these results will push forward the
understanding of how molecules can be applied in QIS technologies when leveraging collective light-matter
interactions.

Aim 1: Develop extended definitions of multipartite entanglement using algebraic approaches.
The essential challenge in describing multi-partite entanglement stems from the lack of straightforward
analogies of the bipartite case to well-known linear algebraic methods. PIs Candelori, Chernyak and Klein
are developing new approaches to study multi-partite entanglement of mixed states from an algebraic per-
spective. The main research problem studied has been the calculation of the ring of polynomial invariants
of a mixed state of two qubits. There are two reasons why we focus on mixed states of two qubits. First,
in chemical systems, e.g., Fabry-Pérot optical cavities, the qubits, represented by molecules, are inevitably
coupled to the environment/bath and therefore are in mixed states. Second, the number of qubits in chemical
systems is generally very large, so that efficient study of entanglement can be performed by analyzing the
entanglement of just a few qubits at a time, starting with two-qubit subsystems, obtained via tracing out the
degrees of freedom of the remaining cubits, as well as the bath, if present.
 While much work has been done towards the aforementioned classification, a complete understanding
of the results of the approach defined above remains unknown. More precisely, we consider the action of the
product of unitary groups G = U (2) × U (2) on the Liouville space L(V ) of density matrices representing
a mixed state of two qubits in V = C2 ⊗ C2 . We would like to compute the ring of polynomial functions on
this space that are invariant under the G-action. Such transformations are referred to as local unitary (LU)
transformations, whereas the equivalence classes with respect to LU transformations are called orbits. The
subgroup H ⊂ G that does not move a representative of an equivalence class is called an orbit stabilizer ,
so that the orbit as a space of LU-equivalent states is canonically isomorphic to a quotient space G/H. The
orbits are naturally represented by the fibers of the projection map L(V ) → L(V )/G.
 The PIs produced equations for L(V ) as a semi-algebraic variety (that is, as the locus in RN described
by polynomial inequalities); in order to calculate the ring of invariants, the first reduction is to pass to the
(larger) real vector space of Hermitian matrices H(V ) with trace 1, which is a purely algebraic object, not
subject to inequalities. Once the calculation of the ring of invariants is done here, the polynomial inequalities
defining L(V ) inside H(V ) can then be applied. The real vector space V is of dimension 15, with an action
of G that passes through an action of SO(3) × SO(3). Some results are present in the literature about
this case, but the full ring of invariants is not known, and it seems beyond the computational ability of
widely available software/hardware. The members of the math sub-team constructed a set of nine complete
(in the sense they are capable of distinguishing between any two generic orbits) invariants for this case.
They have also constructed additional invariants capable of distinguishing between arbitrary, not necessarily
generic orbits, as well as some relations between them. The PIs proved that the invariants only involving
2-point correlation functions form a polynomial ring in three variables. This corresponds to the case of
SO(3) × SO(3) acting on R3 ⊗ R3 , a nine-dimensional problem, significantly more tractable than the
15-dimensional problem.
 For the full problem, the PIs investigated the possibility of first complexifying the action, thus resulting
in the action of SL(2) × SL(2) on the space End(V ) of all matrices (with trace 1), and then recover
the ring of real invariants on H(V ) by taking the fixed points of the real structure induced by function
A ↦→ A∗ on matrices. This seems to be a new approach to the problem. The complex invariants might be
simpler to compute, even though preliminary results suggest that the calculation must be done with a high-
performance computing grid. The PIs are currently writing software to run the calculation on Wayne State’s
high-performance computing grid. The PIs also developed an alternative approach to treat the full problem.
It has been shown that one can associate with any directed graph (quiver), with the vertices of bi-valence
(0, 1), (1, 0), (1, 1), (0, 3), and (3, 0), a polynomial LU invariant on the Liouville space L(V ). Moreover,
the PIs have shown that these invariants generate the whole ring of polynomial invariants. To study the
relations between the invariants, associated with the graphs, the research team associated a polynomial of 9
variables with each such invariant to characterize each one completely. The members of the math sub-team
also worked to connect these findings to spectroscopic theory.

 The members of the math sub-team laid the foundations for the study of rational functions on the Liou-
ville space of mixed states of two qubits that are invariant under the action of local unitary operators. This
approach to the classification problem of entanglement was novel. All previous work by the PIs and other
authors was focused on the case of polynomial invariants, rather than rational invariants. More precisely,
let L be the space of Hermitian, trace one, linear transformations on a two-qubit Hilbert space, of the form
V = V1 ⊗ V2 . Density matrices ρ ∈ L represent mixed states of two qubits. Quantum unitary evolution by
a unitary gate U is given by matrix conjugation, ρ ↦→ U † ρU . The ‘local unitary operators’ are the unitary
gates of the form U = U1 ⊗ U2 , where Ui is a one-qubit gate on Vi , i = 1, 2. They form a subgroup G of the
full unitary group of V = V1 ⊗ V2 . If V1 and V2 are considered to be qubits owned by two different parties A
and B, local unitary operators represent quantum evolution that can be done locally and independently by A
and B. The level of entanglement of a quantum state ρ cannot therefore be altered by local unitary operators.
For this reason, the classification of entanglement can be approached by studying subsets of states (called
‘orbits’) that can be obtained from one another by application of local unitary operators. To understand the
orbits, in turn, members of the math sub-team studied functions f : L → R on Liouville space L that are
invariant under the action of local unitary operators. Formally, these relations are written,

                                      f (U1† ⊗ U2† · ρ · U1 ⊗ U2 ) = f (ρ).

Much of previous work has been focused on classifying such invariant functions f that are polynomial in the
coordinates of ρ. In this context, previous work showed that polynomial invariants can be generated by 15
fundamental invariants, which satisfy an intricate web of 63 polynomial relations. This complicated structure
makes the use of these invariants difficult in practice, and it also suggests that the study of polynomial
invariants of three or more mixed qubits is likely intractable.
 By contrast, the PIs in this project worked with a supported postdoctoral fellow to study the case when
f is a rational function: a ratio of polynomials. This classification of rational invariants simplifies the study
of polynomial. invariants thanks to the availability of methods from algebraic geometry. For the case of two
mixed qubits, if we denote by R(L)G the field of all rational invariants, the PIs showed that

                                           R(L)G = R(f1 , . . . , f9 ),

so that every rational invariant can be expressed as a rational function of nine fundamental, algebraically
independent invariants f1 , . . . , f9 . Again, this treatment provides a simpler structure than the polynomial
case and can be extended to the case of multiple qubits. Moreover, as part of this investigation, the PIs
found that these nine invariants separate orbits in general positions so that one can completely determine
the entanglement level of a generic state. This finding is an important breakthrough in the classification of
multi-partite entanglement and can be used to assess entanglement generation in a specific class of mixed
quantum states: X-states.
 The notion of X-states, as introduced by the PIs, generalizes many well-known families of entangled
two-qubit states, including Bell states, Werner states, isotropic states, and maximally entangled mixed states.
Previous approaches to defining X-states depend on choices of basis sets for the underlying Hilbert spaces.
Unfortunately, a definition which crucially depends on the choice of basis sets does readily not lend itself
to in-depth mathematical study. The PIs proposed a “coordinate-free” definition of X-states, which is not
dependent on the choice of basis sets. This definition is given by taking the set of solutions of a finite
collection of polynomial equations. The set of solutions of a finite set of polynomial equations is known
as an algebraic variety and is a fundamental object in the field of algebraic geometry. By recasting the
definition of X-states in algebraic geometry language, we are able to employ a wealth of techniques native
to the subject. The method developed by the math sub-team gives rise to the description of all the invariant
rational functions on the space of X-states as five fundamental polynomial invariants p1 , · · · , p5 , which

Aim 2: Establish spectroscopic observables whose dynamics carry information on entangle-
ment in strongly coupled multi-component systems.
The spectroscopy theory sub-teams have developed physical and spectroscopic models capable of bridging
the knowledge gap between mathematical formalisms of quantum information such as entanglement and the
characteristic parameters of cavity polaritons: the Hopfield coefficients. To establish how polaritons encode
this information, spectroscopy theory sub-teams applied novel methods to solvable models and developed
new spectroscopic approaches to understand light-matter entanglement in the collective limit.
 Challenges in describing entanglement physically stem from the lack of simple systems that can serve
as models for testing ideas and methods. Members of the physics theory sub-team (PIs Chernyak and Sinit-
syn) have been further developing theoretical techniques for computation and analysis of the final states
in integrable dynamical models, such as the BCS and driven Tavis-Cummings models, with the latter de-
scribing a mechanism of creating entanglement via transient cavity polariton formation. These PIs have
developed closed-form expressions for the phases of the scattering matrices in a general multi-level inte-
grable Landau-Zener (LZ) model. The physics theory sub-team have applied these formalisms to the BCS
and Tavis-Cummings models. These applications show the final states of the BCS model have been explic-
itly identified for the case of the most symmetric (Bell-like) initial correlated states. The reduced 2-qubit
invariants have been analyzed; it has been shown that in the thermodynamic limit (Ns → ∞) the correspond-
ing orbits are substantially non-generic and show interesting entanglement features. The team discovered
that the BCS model also plays a role of a link between the disordered and completely degenerate Tavis-
Cummings models, which allowed members of the physics model sub-team to obtain explicit the scattering
matrix for the degenerate model. This finding also allowed the physics theory sub-team to understand the
symmetry properties of scattering in the disordered model in terms of the degenerate counterpart, which
contains much less data.
 The sub-team applied these results to the problem of quantum correlations of spins in cavity QED and
interacting atomic-molecular systems. As a highly nontrivial toy model, members of the physics theory
sub-team derived a fully integrable model of a stimulated chemical reaction between ultra cold atomic and
molecular Bose-Einstein condensates. The results show that such reactions lead to macroscopic quantum
cat-states, whose properties are suitable for further application in quantum sensing.
 In addition to elucidating these direct connections between physical models and the mathematical for-
malism of multi-partite entanglement, the spectroscopy theory sub-team led by co-PI Mukamel aims to
determine how the number of individual molecules of a particular species coupled to photons of a cavity
mode affects the nature of light-matter entanglement. In the absence of spectroscopic methods capable of
assessing the cooperativity using sources of classical light, co-PI Mukamel has been developing theoretical
predictions of the spectroscopic signals of cavity polariton systems using classical and entangled photon
pairs formed within simple resonator structures.
 To address a problem of creating entanglement in a material system using classical optical sources the
research team members considered an example of a single-mode optical cavity with two non-interacting
two-level systems/qubits, coupled to the optical cavity mode. Members of the spectroscopy theory sub-team
showed that if one initializes the system with a non-entangled state of the qubit and a Fock state |n⟩ of the
cavity mode, then the system evolution generates entanglement between the qubits. This result stems from
connections between the maximal concurrence possible in a physical system and the X-states studied by the
math sub-team. Using this connection, the spectroscopy theory sub-team showed that the entanglement of

 As noted in the publication detailing these results,
‘The research team considered the generation of entanglement between two qubits by using a classical light
source and a quantized cavity mode and showed how two qubits can be entangled by exchanging quanta
with a third party, which in this case is the photons in a cavity mode. Quanta can be pumped into the system
through an external driving by a classical light source coupled to the cavity mode, with no direct driving
of the qubits. The studied cavity-qubits system is a useful testbed for fundamental quantum properties of
light-matter interaction and entanglement. The presented framework enables selecting specific operations
on the joint cavity-qubits state by an appropriate pulse shaping of an external classical light. The set of
all possible operations accessible by the subcycle or the quasistatic driving and how each operation can
be activated or suppressed with prescription for a high fidelity can be used for a laser-based experimental
generation and control of the entanglement between noninteracting systems.’

 To leverage their expertise in high energy spectroscopic methods, PIs Chernyak and Mukamel studied
the effects of polariton formation in optical cavities on the photoelectron spectroscopy and photochemistry,
in particular charge migration between non-interacting directly photo-ionized systems. Polaritonic effects
in photochemistry, in particular conical point assisted photoisomerization. It was demonstrated that strong
polariton coupling leads to substantial changes in the rates of chemical reactions. In their theoretical study
of the quantum dynamics of the photoionized 5-Bromo-1-pentene, this sub-team showed that the charge
migration process can be induced by placing the molecule in an optical cavity, and be monitored by time-
resolved photoelectron spectroscopy. Upon studying the collective nature of the polariton formation induced
charge migration, the research team members found out that, unlike spectroscopic observables, molecular
charge dynamics in a cavity remain local and do not occur collectively. In particular, as opposed to the
Rabi splitting, increasing the molecule numbers in cavity cannot enhance the rates of charge migration.

 While the formation of cavity polaritons from molecular chromophores whose excitons possess large
binding energies opens avenues towards novel room temperature applications in quantum information sci-
ences, the highly disordered nature of molecular materials also imposes limit on the extent of coherence
believed to be realizable in these systems. To overcome this obstacle, lead PI Rury and his group have been
assessing polariton-mediated coherent averaging over the energetic disorder present in molecular ensembles
in amorphous media. Through these studies, the team demonstrated control over the relaxation rates of
cavity polariton states formed from molecular excitons under ambient conditions can be achieved through
a rational approach. The delocalized nature of cavity polariton states enables dynamical averaging over
the energetic disorder of molecular chromophores embedded in amorphous polymer matrices inside these
structures. This dynamical averaging leads to a reduced width of the peaks of UP and LP states measured
in the transmission spectra of polariton samples formed from different porphyrin macromolecular species.
Models capture this dynamical averaging using the same physical theory implemented to explain ‘motional
narrowing’ of the LP peak formed by strongly coupling photons to excitons in III-V semiconductor quantum
wells at low temperatures. In addition, this averaging possesses four important characteristics. First, unlike
previous studies examining the dispersion of LP peaks in the photoluminescence spectra of cavity polariton
systems, we use well-designed transmission to show the delocalized nature of the photons in micro-resonator
modes enables narrowing of the UP peak, which had not been observed previously. Second, suppression
of energetic disorder amongst the ensemble of excitons in micro-resonators depends on the collective light-
matter coupling, g c , which can be proportional to collective vacuum Rabi splitting (ℏΩR ) between the UP
and LP states and had not been observed previously. Third, models capable of explaining the experimen-
tal results necessitate our using a constant, α, that reduces σ phenomenologically. Lastly, differences in the
quantitative value of α between the distinct samples correspond to differences in the microscopic delocaliza-
tion of excitons in our chromophores. These results suggest researchers can utilize specific rules to average
over the energetic disorder characteristic of molecular materials at room temperature and enable relatively
increased spatial and temporal coherence in molecule-based excitations through cavity polariton formation.

As noted previously the Soret absorption features of Cu-M and F20 Cu-M peak at a higher energies and
narrow relative to those of their respective homo-dimers. Based on the inability of dimers to aggregate
due to steric hindrance caused by the dihedral angle between the planes of the two porphyrin macrocycles,
these relative changes to the absorption spectrum of Cu-M and F20 Cu-M indicate that delocalized excitons
form in aggregates of these chromophores. The aggregation of these Cu-porphyrin molecules in face-to-face
orientations leads to H-aggregate states that form due to interactions between the Soret absorption transition
dipole moments on neighboring molecules. The dotted lines in Figure 11(a) show that we can reproduce the
experimental absorption spectra using an intermolecular exciton model developed by Spano and applied to
Cu-porphyrins recently. These model results predict that the collective intermolecular coupling strength, Jc ,
is at least 24.5 meV and 49.5 meV for aggregates of Cu-M and F20 Cu-M, respectively. Most importantly,
since the ligated Cu+2 cation contains an unpaired d-electron, the delocalized excitons result from formal
doublet states that participate in the chromophores’ Soret transition due to exchange interactions between
transition metal and frontier molecular orbitals. As shown in the PI, this electron exchange also affects the
light emission spectra of F20 Cu-M and F30 Cu-D in ways consistent with delocalized exciton formation.

The radiative relaxation of the |TB ⟩ state to |GU ⟩ must preserve the spins of the Cu cations since there is
no spin-flip operator in the light-matter interaction. Based on this consideration, the team proposed that
the spins of electrons in the 2 B and ground states must point in the opposite direction, which supports
the hypothesis of anti-ferromagnetic coupling in the models of the dominant DAS in the tr-EPR spectrum
F20 Cu-M. The delocalized trip-doublet exciton also help to explain the dynamics of the ‘low-field’ signal
that dominates the DAS of F20 Cu-M.
    Previously, the research team proposed that light emission from the |TB ⟩ state observed experimentally
peaks at delays relative to an excitation pulse due to thermal equilibration with the dark state that lies ∼70
µeV below the bright state. This delayed emission mechanism justifies a kinetic model that accounts for
the number of sites over which the trip-doublet exciton delocalizes, which was called NT . Based on this
proposal, one expects that the radiative relaxation rate of the delocalized trip-doublet excitation will increase
relative to that of an individual molecule, kr , by a factor of NT . If the intermolecular spin polarization
forms via radiative relaxation, then it’s dynamics should appear as the depletion of the |TB ⟩ state during
the enhanced radiative relaxation, i.e 1-IT (t) for a trip-doublet light emission function IT (t). To test this
hypothesis, the dynamics of both time resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) and the DAS contribution to the
tr-EPR of F20 Cu-M were fit to kinetics consistent with this physical model.
 The comparisons between experimental and model results shown in Figure 13(c) and Figure 13(d) show
that the dynamics of both the trip-doublet light emission and the spin polarized state match using model
of the delocalized trip-doublet exciton. Accounting for the sample temperature through the Bose-Einstein
distribution that describes the vibrational populations that cause non-radiative relaxation allows the same
kinetic model to explain the dynamics of both the decay of the trip-doublet light emission and the rise of the
‘low-field’ DAS signal resulting from our SVD analysis. These comparisons suggest that the intermolecular,
coupled spin state consistent with the model EPR spectrum shown in 13(a) forms due to collective light
emission from the delocalized trip-doublet state, |TB ⟩, to a antiferromagnetically coupled pair of electron
spins located on the Cu+2 cations of neighboring porphyrin macrocycles. The electronic structure of the
Cu-porphyrins helps explain why the ‘low-field’ signal plateaus at the longest time delays we consider in
the tr-EPR dynamics of F20 Cu-M and decays for the case of Cu-M.
 As noted above, substitution of the meso positions on the macrocycle with penta-fluorinated phenyl
groups leads to the generation of a HOMO state comprised primarily of the a1u porphyrin MO. This state
contains minimal electron density on the interior N atoms of the macrocycle, which inhibits the exchange
interactions between the electrons in the MO with the unpair electron of the Cu2+ cation. Minimization of
this exchange interaction reduces probability that any spin polarization relaxes following formation due to
photo-excitation. Based on this physical logic, the significant, non-zero ‘low-field‘ signal plateaus at finite
intensity and lives for over 30 µs due to the electron’s inability to relax non-radiatively through exchange
interactions with the surrounds molecular orbitals. In contrast, the a2u HOMO state of Cu-M contains
substantial electron density on the interior N atoms of the porphyrin macrocycle. If any intermolecular
spin polarization forms in aggregates of this Cu-porphyrin, then that electron density can interact with the
Cu+2 unpaired electron to create an effective non-radiative relaxation pathway that reduces the population
differences between the states of the strongly coupled spin pair. The intermolecular spin polarization formed
via photo-excitation of Cu-M would then decay rapidly on time scales proportional to the inverse of the
electron exchange rate, which is estimated to be less than 1 ps.
 These findings and our interpretation provide evidence that intermolecular spin polarization can be
pumped deterministically through state-selective, enhanced radiative emission caused by aggregate forma-
tion among fluorinated derivatives of meso-substituted Cu-porphyrins. This state-selective process mani-
fests itself as a ‘low-field’ signal in the transient EPR spectra measured following excitation. We model
this ‘low-field’ behavior adequately using simple models of population residing in the lowest energy state
of a anti-ferromagnetically coupled spin manifold, the singlet state. Faster exchange interactions between
