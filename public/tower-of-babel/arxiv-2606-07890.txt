Partially Performative Prediction
Jaewook Lee, Tijana Zrnic

Abstract
Performative prediction studies feedback loops that arise when predictive models are deployed in consequential domains. In these settings, deploying a model can change the population whose patterns the model aims to predict, inducing a distribution shift that is endogenous to the learning system. This perspective departs from classical treatments of distribution shift, where shifts are typically modeled as exogenous changes in the data-generating process. Yet, in practice, distribution shift is rarely one or the other. Predictive models may influence future data through the decisions they support, while the world itself continues to drift for reasons beyond the learner’s control. We study partially performative prediction , a framework that captures both endogenous and exogenous sources of distribution shift. The framework generalizes performative prediction by allowing the data distribution to evolve both in response to the deployed model and according to an external, time-varying process. We extend the central notions of performative stability and performative optimality to this setting by defining their online analogues that track the evolving partially performative environment. We analyze practical learning heuristics, including repeated retraining, and characterize when they successfully adapt to partially performative environments.



1
Introduction

When prediction informs decision-making, predictive models do not merely describe a population; they can also shape it. Disease forecasts guide interventions that slow disease spread; recommendation systems influence user preferences; stock and home price forecasts affect the prices that ultimately materialize. Performative prediction
[
27
]
formalizes this phenomenon by studying learning problems in which deploying a model changes the distribution of future observations.

The core idea is that deploying a model
\theta
induces a model-dependent distribution over observations, denoted by
{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)
. The learner’s objective is to minimize the expected loss under the induced distribution, called the
performative risk
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{PR}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)}[\ell(Z,\theta)],

(1)

where
Z
denotes an observation, typically a feature–label pair
Z=(X,Y)
. The central object
{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)
is called the
distribution map
. Thus, the goal is not simply to choose a model that performs well on the population as currently observed, but one that performs well after the model’s impact on the population has been realized. Existing work studies two central solution concepts:
performative stability
and
performative optimality
, and analyzes when retraining procedures converge to such equilibria. In particular, these procedures generate a sequence of model updates
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots
, whose limit is either a performatively stable or a performatively optimal point.

Performativity, therefore, surfaces as distribution shift: each model update may induce a new data distribution. What distinguishes performative distribution shift is that it is
endogenous
: it arises from the learner’s own actions. This contrasts with classical models of distribution shift in machine learning, which typically treat the shift as driven by
exogenous
factors. For example, online learning studies algorithms for adapting a sequence of actions
\theta_{t}
to an arbitrary sequence of distributions
P_{t}
.

The starting point of this work is the observation that, in realistic performative environments, distribution shift is rarely purely endogenous. Deployed predictive systems are embedded in changing populations, markets, and platforms. Their predictions may influence future data, but so too may seasonal effects, policy changes, market forces, and strategic behavior outside the learner’s control.

To capture this interplay, we introduce
partially performative prediction
, an online model that combines endogenous and exogenous sources of distribution shift. At round
t
, the data distribution is

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha_{t}){\mathcal{D}}(\theta)+\alpha_{t}P_{t},

where
{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)
captures the performative response to the deployed model
\theta
,
P_{t}
captures an exogenous distribution shift, and
\alpha_{t}\in[0,1]
controls their relative strength. This framework strictly generalizes the classical performative setting, recovered when
\alpha_{t}=0
, and approaches a purely exogenous-shift setting when
\alpha_{t}=1
. Note also that this model allows for a fixed non-performative component, obtained when
P_{t}
is constant across
t
.

Partially performative models require a reconsideration of the learner’s objective. Since the environment is fundamentally non-stationary, the goal can no longer simply be convergence to a fixed model, as in performative stability or performative optimality. Instead, the learner must contend with a moving target whose moves are only partly induced by the learner’s own decisions. At the same time, the model highlights several new questions about the interaction between endogenous and exogenous shifts. Is a smaller value of
\alpha_{t}
always more favorable to the learner, or can a large exogenous component help? How does the rate of change of
P_{t}
affect the performance of learning algorithms? And how do the conditions under which retraining heuristics converge, or fail to converge, change relative to the classical performative setting (
\alpha_{t}=0
)?

1.1
Our contributions

We study all the above questions and more in the partially performative setting. Our first contribution is to extend the classical solution concepts of performative prediction to non-stationary environments. In the standard setting, performative stability and performative optimality are fixed models. Under partial performativity, the relevant stable and optimal points may change over time. We therefore introduce regret-based analogues, which we call
performative stability regret
and
performative optimality regret
. These quantities compare the learner’s sequence of decisions to the time-varying sequence of performatively stable points

\theta^{\mathrm{PS}}_{1},\theta^{\mathrm{PS}}_{2},\dots

and performatively optimal points

\theta^{\mathrm{PO}}_{1},\theta^{\mathrm{PO}}_{2},\dots
,
respectively. We then analyze several natural learning algorithms, including repeated risk minimization
[
27
]
and gradient-based methods, under these regret notions.

A key quantity in our bounds is the
path length
: the cumulative movement of the comparator sequence. For performative stability and performative optimality, we denote the one-step changes by

\displaystyle\Delta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}=\|\theta^{\mathrm{PS}}_{t}-\theta^{\mathrm{PS}}_{t+1}\|\quad\text{ and }\quad\Delta_{t}^{\mathrm{PO}}=\|\theta^{\mathrm{PO}}_{t}-\theta^{\mathrm{PO}}_{t+1}\|.

The path lengths
\sumop\displaylimits_{t}\Delta_{t}^{(\cdot)}
measure the degree of non-stationarity in the partially performative problem. Slowly varying exogenous distributions
P_{t}
lead to small path length, while rapidly changing
P_{t}
can lead to large path length.

Our first main result bounds the stability regret of repeated risk minimization (RRM), an idealized form of retraining given by

\theta_{t+1}=\argmin_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta_{t})}[\ell(Z,\theta)].

Let
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{RRM}}
denote the RRM algorithm.

Theorem 1.1

(Informal)
.

Suppose that the loss
\ell(z,\theta)
is
\beta_{z}
-smooth in
z
and
\mu
-strongly convex in
\theta
, and that the distribution map
{\mathcal{D}}
is
\epsilon
-Lispchitz with
\epsilon<\frac{\mu}{(1-\alpha_{\min})\beta_{z}}
, where
\alpha_{\min}=\min_{t\in[T]}\alpha_{t}
. Then, the performative stability regret of repeated risk minimization after
T
steps satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}_{T}^{\mathrm{PS}}({\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{RRM}})

\displaystyle={\mathcal{O}}\left(1+\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T-1}\Delta^{\mathrm{PS}}_{t}\right).

(2)

This result makes explicit a tradeoff between endogenous and exogenous shifts. When the shifts are mostly performative (
\alpha_{t}\approx 0,\forall\,t
), the regret has no dependence on
T
because the path length vanishes. The condition on performativity reduces to the standard condition
\epsilon<\frac{\mu}{\beta_{z}}
from
[
27
]
. Conversely, when the shifts are mostly exogenous (
\alpha_{t}\approx 1,\forall\,t
), there is no condition on the strength of performativity, but the regret depends on the non-stationarity of
P_{t}
. If
P_{t}
is near-constant, the regret is constant too; if
P_{t}
fluctuates rapidly, the regret could be large. Thus, retraining on the most recent data is effective under mild distribution shift, but ineffective when shifts are unpredictable.

We also prove analogous stability regret bounds for gradient-based algorithms, including those with “greedy” and “lazy” deployment schedules
[
20
]
.

Our second main contribution concerns performative optimality. We study algorithms that explicitly anticipate performativity by using zeroth-order gradient estimation techniques. Such methods perturb deployed models to estimate how changes in the model affect the induced distribution, and prior work has shown that related procedures can converge to performatively optimal points in the classical setting
[
21
,
11
,
19
,
18
,
3
]
. We show that, in partially performative environments, these ideas yield regret guarantees relative to the time-varying sequence of performatively optimal points.

Theorem 1.2

(Informal)
.

Suppose that the performative risk at step
t
is convex for all
t
. Then, there exists an algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
that after
T
steps achieves performative optimality regret of

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}^{\mathrm{PO}}_{T}({\mathcal{A}})

\displaystyle={\mathcal{O}}\left(d\,\sqrt{T}\cdot\left(1+\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T-1}\Delta^{\mathrm{PO}}_{t}\right)\right),

where
d
is the dimension of
\theta
.

Again, we see path length as a key quantity in the bound. Like in the previous result, we can again observe a tradeoff between having a large endogenous and a large exogenous component. A large exogenous component might make the path length large. At the same time, a larger endogenous component can make the performative risk nonconvex, even when the loss
\ell(z,\theta)
is convex. If
\alpha_{t}\approx 1
, the performative component is weak; consequently, convexity of the loss directly implies convexity of the performative risk. Thus, exogenous variation can make the target harder to track, while endogenous feedback can make the target harder to optimize.

1.2
Related work

Our work contributes to the growing literature on performative prediction
[
27
]
, which formalizes settings in which deploying a model changes the future data distribution. A large body of work studies algorithms for finding performatively stable points
[
20
,
4
,
22
,
30
,
24
,
26
,
5
]
and performatively optimal points
[
21
,
11
,
16
,
24
,
31
,
18
]
, under progressively weaker assumptions. See
8
and
15
for recent surveys of the area.

Most relevant to our work are extensions with stateful observation models
[
2
,
28
,
12
,
17
,
19
]
, which allow prior deployments to influence the current data distribution. These models capture memory, delayed responses, and other forms of endogenous dynamics. However, they still pursue the classical notions of performative stability and performative optimality; the underlying environment is fully determined by the history of deployments. In contrast, our model allows exogenous components of the data-generating process to evolve over time, so the relevant stable and optimal points may drift.

Another closely related thread studies performative regret minimization
[
14
,
3
,
32
,
25
]
, where the learner deploys models sequentially and seeks regret bounds on the accumulated performative risk. In these works, the performative risk is static: the distribution map
{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)
is fixed, and regret is measured against a fixed baseline. In our setting, the distribution map
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta)
may vary with time. Accordingly, we study dynamic notions of performative regret, benchmarking the learner against time-varying sequences of performatively stable or performatively optimal models.

At a technical level, our model of partially performative prediction resembles the model of algorithmic collective action
[
7
]
in the presence of selfish agents by
37
. They similarly show a faster convergence rate of repeated risk minimization in the presence of a fixed, non-performative distributional component, which corresponds to the behavior of a coordinated collective in their case. They do not, however, analyze regret guarantees or non-stationary environments.

Our analysis builds on tools from online learning, in particular online convex optimization
[
38
,
9
]
and zeroth-order optimization
[
6
,
1
]
. We study dynamic regret, which measures performance relative to a changing sequence of optima rather than a single best baseline in hindsight. Dynamic regret originated in tracking changing experts and comparators
[
10
,
38
]
and was later sharpened through path and loss variation
[
13
,
23
,
33
,
35
]
. Some of our results rely on zeroth-order optimization, where path-length-dependent dynamic regret guarantees are known
[
36
]
, though existing analyses do not accommodate performative feedback.

2
Preliminaries

We begin by reviewing the formal setup of performative prediction, with a focus on the partially performative setting studied in this work. The learner chooses model parameters
\theta\in\Theta
, where
\Theta
is a convex, compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of diameter
D_{\Theta}
. We write
\Pi_{\Theta}
for the Euclidean projection onto
\Theta
. Performance is measured by a loss function
\ell(z,\theta)
, where
z
denotes a data point; thus,
\ell(z,\theta)
is the loss incurred by model
\theta
on instance
z
.

In partially performative prediction, the data distribution after deploying model
\theta
at time
t
is

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta)=(1-\alpha_{t}){\mathcal{D}}(\theta)+\alpha_{t}P_{t},

(3)

where
\alpha_{t}\in[0,1]
is a fixed sequence of mixture coefficients and
P_{t}
is an exogenous distribution that is not affected by the deployed model. The component
{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)
captures the performative response to model
\theta
, while
P_{t}
captures time-varying external shift. Setting
\alpha_{t}=0
recovers the standard performative prediction model
[
27
]
. When the deployed model at time
t
is
\theta_{t}
, we sometimes use the shorthand
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}\coloneqq{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta_{t})
.

The partially performative setting admits natural analogues of the central concepts from performative prediction. We define the
performative risk
and
decoupled performative risk
at time
t
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{PR}_{t}(\theta)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta)}[\ell(Z,\theta)],\quad\mathrm{DPR}_{t}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta^{\prime})}[\ell(Z,\theta)].

The performative risk evaluates a model on the distribution that it induces. The decoupled performative risk separates the model being evaluated,
\theta
, from the model that induces the distribution,
\theta^{\prime}
.

The
performatively stable point
at time
t
is defined as the point
\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}\in\Theta
satisfying

\displaystyle\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}\coloneqq\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathrm{DPR}_{t}(\theta,\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}).

Thus, once
\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}
is deployed and induces the distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}})
, it minimizes the expected loss on that induced distribution. The
performatively optimal point
at time
t
is the point

\displaystyle\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PO}}\coloneqq\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathrm{PR}_{t}(\theta).

Performative optimality jointly accounts for the loss incurred by predicting with
\theta
and the distributional response induced by deploying
\theta
. In general, stable and optimal points do not coincide
[
27
]
.

Because the stable and optimal points may vary with time, a natural objective is to achieve low regret relative to the sequences
\{\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}\}_{t\in[T]}
and
\{\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PO}}\}_{t\in[T]}
. This leads to our definitions of
performative stability regret
and
performative optimality regret
.
Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be an algorithm that outputs a possibly stochastic sequence of iterates
\{\theta_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
. We define the performative stability regret and the performative optimality regret of
{\mathcal{A}}
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}_{T}^{\mathrm{PS}}({\mathcal{A}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{PR}_{t}(\theta_{t})]-\mathrm{PR}_{t}(\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}})\right),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}_{T}^{\mathrm{PO}}({\mathcal{A}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{PR}_{t}(\theta_{t})]-\mathrm{PR}_{t}(\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PO}})\right).

The expectation is taken over any randomness in the algorithm.

Several previous works have studied regret guarantees in performative prediction
[
14
,
32
,
3
,
5
]
. Their guarantees are given in terms of
static
regret, which compares the learner’s realized loss to that of a single fixed
\theta\in\Theta
. In the standard performative prediction model, this is natural, since the stable and optimal points do not change over time. In the partially performative setting, however, the exogenous component can make these points time-varying. We therefore study
dynamic
regret, which compares the learner’s performance to a sequence of models. This distinction parallels the classical difference between static and dynamic regret in online learning; see, for example,
[
9
]
.

Finally, we invoke several common assumptions from the performative prediction literature, including (strong) convexity and smoothness conditions on the loss
\ell(z,\theta)
.
We say that a continuously differentiable function
\ell:{\mathcal{Z}}\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
is

\mu
-strongly convex in
\theta
if

\displaystyle\ell(z,\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\ell(z,\theta)+\langle\nabla_{\theta}\ell(z,\theta),\theta^{\prime}-\theta\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|^{2},\qquad\forall\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta,\,z\in{\mathcal{Z}},

(4)

and convex in
\theta
if the above holds with
\mu=0
.

We say that
\ell
is
\beta_{\theta}
-smooth in
\theta
if

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\theta}\ell(z,\theta)-\nabla_{\theta}\ell(z,\theta^{\prime})\|

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{\theta}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|,\qquad\forall\,\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta\,,z\in\mathcal{Z},

(5)

and
\beta_{z}
-smooth in
z
if
1
1

1

Note that
\beta_{z}
-smoothness is also defined using the gradient with respect to
\theta
.

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\theta}\ell(z,\theta)-\nabla_{\theta}\ell(z^{\prime},\theta)\|

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{z}\|z-z^{\prime}\|,\qquad\forall\,z,z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z},\,\theta\in\Theta.

(6)

We say that
\mathrm{PR}_{t}(\theta)
is
L
-Lipschitz if

\displaystyle|\mathrm{PR}_{t}(\theta)-\mathrm{PR}_{t}(\theta^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq L\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|,\qquad\forall\,\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta.

(7)

Here we also state one assumption specific to performative settings: sensitivity of the distribution map.
We say that
{\mathcal{D}}
is
\epsilon
-sensitive if

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}_{1}\bigl({\mathcal{D}}(\theta),{\mathcal{D}}(\theta^{\prime})\bigr)\leq\epsilon\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|,\qquad\forall\,\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta,

(8)

where
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
denotes the Wasserstein-
1
distance. This condition requires the performative response to vary smoothly with the deployed model. Intuitively,
\epsilon
measures the strength of performative effects.

Remark 2.1
.

When we make assumptions on the loss as a function of
z
, we require the assumptions to hold for all
\theta
; for example,
z\mapsto\ell(z,\theta)
is
\beta_{z}
-smooth for all
\theta
.
For the assumptions on the loss as a function of
\theta
, however, our results hold under a weaker assumption that the
expected
risk
\mathrm{R}_{D}(\theta)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim D}[\ell(Z,\theta)]
is
\mu
-strongly convex,
\beta_{\theta}
-smooth, etc, for all admissible distributions that could be generated by
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta_{t})
. For simplicity we state the assumptions for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, as in
Eqs.

4
,
5
and
7
, however all the proofs go through if we consider the expected risk.

Making assumptions on the expected risk is strictly weaker, especially if we have additional structure on the distributions that can be generated by
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta_{t})
.
For example, consider a simple one-dimensional coin-flip setting with a single feature where the random variable
Z=(X,Y)\in\{-1,+1\}^{2}
has a fixed distribution of
X\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\{-1,+1\})
and only the conditional law of
Y\mid X
can change due to performative and exogenous factors.
Consider

\displaystyle\ell(z,\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\theta-y)^{2}-x\theta^{2}.

We have that
\theta\mapsto\ell(z,\theta)
is nonconvex in
\theta
for
x=1
.
However, if we average the loss over the distribution of
(X,Y)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}_{D}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim D}\left[\frac{1}{2}(\theta-Y)^{2}-X\theta^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim D}\left[\frac{1}{2}(\theta-Y)^{2}\right],

which is
1
-strongly convex in
\theta
.
Similarly, we can also consider

\displaystyle\ell(z,\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\theta-y)^{2}-x|\theta|,

which is non-smooth at
\theta=0
, but admits a
1
-smooth
\mathrm{R}_{D}(\theta)
under the same setup.

3
Repeated risk minimization under partial performativity

We begin by studying repeated risk minimization (RRM)
[
27
]
. Given an initial
\theta_{1}\in\Theta
, RRM sets

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta_{t})}[\ell(Z,\theta)].

(RRM)

We use
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{RRM}}
to denote this algorithm.

We start from a key technical lemma that characterizes one-step RRM updates.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)
is
\epsilon
-sensitive (
8
)

\mu
-strongly convex in
\theta
(
4
) and
\beta_{z}
-smooth in

(

).
Then, the iterates of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{RRM}}
satisfy

\displaystyle\lVert\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1-\alpha_{t})\epsilon\beta_{z}}{\mu}\|\theta_{t}-\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}\|.

We defer the proof of
Lemma

3.1
to
Section

B.1
.
We note that
Lemma

3.1

[

]

\alpha_{t}=0
and
\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}\equiv\theta^{\mathrm{PS}}
for all
t
. Building on this lemma, we obtain the following stability regret for RRM.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)
is
\epsilon
-sensitive (

),
\ell(z,\theta)
is
\mu
-strongly convex in
\theta
(
4
)
and
\beta_{z}
-smooth in

(

),
and
\mathrm{PR}_{t}(\theta)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}_{T}^{\mathrm{PS}}({\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{RRM}})

Remark 3.3
.

Although we state all (strong) convexity and smoothness conditions directly in terms of the loss
\ell
for simplicity, all results in the paper also hold under the corresponding conditions on the
expected
loss; see
Remark

2.1
for details.
Also, we show in
Lemma

A.5
that
\ell
being Lipschitz in both
z
and
\theta
implies that
\mathrm{PR}_{t}(\theta)
is Lipschitz.

In the standard performative setting with
\alpha_{t}\equiv 0
, convergence of RRM to stability required that the contraction factor
\gamma=\frac{\epsilon\beta_{z}}{\mu}
be strictly less than one.
Under the partially performative setting with possible
\alpha_{t}>0
, the contraction factor
\gamma
gets smaller by a factor of
(1-\alpha_{t})
, and thus we only need a weaker sensitivity condition
\gamma=\frac{(1-\alpha_{\min})\epsilon\beta_{z}}{\mu}<1
, or equivalently

\epsilon<\frac{\mu}{(1-\alpha_{\min})\beta_{z}}
. At the same time, large exogenous shifts may lead to large gaps between subsequent stable points
\Delta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}
.
To provide more intuition for
\Delta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}
, we state an upper bound on
\Delta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}
as a function of the mixture coefficient
\alpha_{t}
, how fast the distribution
P_{t}
varies, and the properties of the loss function.

Proposition 3.4
.

Fix
t\in\mathbb{N}
.
Suppose that
{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)
is
\epsilon
-sensitive (
8
)
and
\ell(z,\theta)
is
\mu
-strongly convex in
\theta
(
4
) and
\beta_{z}
-smooth in
z
(
6
).
Further, suppose that
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}({\mathcal{D}}(\theta_{t+1}^{\mathrm{PS}}),P_{t+1})\leq C
for some
C<\infty
. Then, if
\epsilon<\frac{\mu}{(1-\alpha_{t})\beta_{z}}
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}\leq\frac{\beta_{z}}{\mu-\beta_{z}(1-\alpha_{t})\epsilon}\left(\alpha_{t}{\mathcal{W}}_{1}(P_{t},P_{t+1})+\lvert\alpha_{t}-\alpha_{t+1}\rvert C\right).

We defer the proof of
Proposition

3.4
to
Section

B.3
.
We observe that
\Delta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}
is driven by (i) how fast the exogenous component drifts,
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}(P_{t},P_{t+1})
; (ii) the contribution of the exogenous component
\alpha_{t}
; and (iii) the local fluctuations in the mixture coefficient,
|\alpha_{t}-\alpha_{t+1}|
. In particular,
\Delta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}
is small whenever
\alpha_{t}
is consistently small or the distributions

do not vary significantly.

As a special case, suppose that
\alpha_{t}\leq ct^{-b}
, for some
c,b>0

{\mathcal{W}}_{1}(P_{t},P_{t+1})
is uniformly bounded for all
t

\epsilon<\mu/\beta_{z}
, we can conclude

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}_{T}^{\mathrm{PS}}({\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{RRM}})=\mathcal{O}\left(1+\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T-1}\alpha_{t}\right).

For the case
b\in(0,1)
, we have
\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T-1}\alpha_{t}={\mathcal{O}}(T^{1-b})
, and therefore
\mathrm{Reg}_{T}^{\mathrm{PS}}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{RRM}})={\mathcal{O}}(T^{1-b})
.
Similarly, for
b=1
we have
\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{T-1}\alpha_{t}=\mathcal{O}(\log T)

5
Achieving optimality under partial performativity

We now turn our attention to studying performative optimality regret. Performative optimality is harder to achieve than stability because computing the gradients of
\mathrm{PR}_{t}(\theta)
is rarely feasible, due to the (typically complex) dependence of
\mathrm{PR}_{t}(\theta)
on
\theta
through
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta)
. We thus turn to zeroth-order algorithms, which only require querying
\mathrm{PR}_{t}(\theta)
, not its gradient.

Throughout, we make a couple of mild assumptions: that
|\ell(z,\theta)|\leq F
for some finite
F
, for all
z,\theta
, and that
\Theta
contains a ball of radius
r
centered at
0

r>0
.

To prove that the regret vanishes, we will need
\mathrm{PR}_{t}(\theta)
to be convex or strongly convex. This is not guaranteed by the convexity of
\ell(z,\theta)
alone. Miller et al.
[
21
]
identify conditions that ensure convexity of the performative risk, the most important one being a so-called mixture dominance condition. Miller et al. show that this condition is satisfied by location-scale families. In the partially performative setting, convexity is easier to ensure, since the
\alpha_{t}
-fraction of the objective corresponding to the exogenous component is convex whenever

is convex. We formalize this below.

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)
is
\epsilon
-sensitive (
8
), and
\ell(z,\theta)
is
\mu
-strongly convex in
\theta
(
4
) and
\beta_{z}
-smooth in

(
6

Assume also the mixture dominance condition
[
21
]

Then,

is
(\mu-2(1-\alpha_{t})\epsilon\beta_{z})

If
\mu-2(1-\alpha_{t})\epsilon\beta_{z}
is positive, then
\mathrm{PR}_{t}(\theta)
is

convex by that amount.
If
\mu-2(1-\alpha_{t})\epsilon\beta_{z}
is negative, then

is

convex by that amount, meaning that adding a quadratic regularizer of that magnitude makes

Experiments

We conduct experiments evaluating the stability regret of the retraining algorithms considered in the paper. We include several main results in this section and defer further empirical results and additional experimental details to Appendix

We use the credit scoring dataset from
[

]
, in particular the processed version from
[

We introduce an exogenous component

with the same covariance matrix as the performative component, which models a time-varying population of non-strategic individuals that do not optimize their predicted score.
To capture “hard” and “easy” exogenous shifts, we consider two cases: in the first,

7
Conclusion

We introduce partially performative prediction—a novel model for learning under distribution shifts that are both induced by performative feedback and exogenous variations. In this setting, we measure performance via regret relative to a sequence of performatively stable and performatively optimal points, rather than a single stable or optimal point, as in the standard performative prediction model. We prove dynamic regret bounds for several standard algorithms, including repeated risk minimization and variants of stochastic gradient descent.

Several questions remain unresolved, such as matching regret lower bounds or tighter upper bounds. As we briefly discussed, the regret rate of greedy SGD is worse than the rate of the other studied algorithms, and we do now know if this gap is fundamental. It would also be valuable to study performative stability regret and performative optimality regret beyond the presented model of partial performativity. These regret notions can be defined with respect to any time-varying map
{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta)
, thus allowing natural extensions to models beyond linear mixtures. Finally, our regularity assumptions on the loss (such as convexity) and the distribution map (such as sensitivity) are still fairly restrictive; finding ways to relax those assumptions remains an open question.

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{t+t_{0}}\right)S(t+t_{0})^{-m}+S(\alpha-m)(t+t_{0})^{-1}\cdot(t+t_{0})^{-m}

\displaystyle=S\left(1-\frac{m}{t+t_{0}}\right)(t+t_{0})^{-m}.

Since Bernoulli’s inequality (for
m\geq 0
and
\frac{1}{t+t_{0}}>-1
) implies

\displaystyle 1-\frac{m}{t+t_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{t+t_{0}}\right)^{-m},

we have

\displaystyle F_{t+1}

\displaystyle\leq S\left(1+\frac{1}{t+t_{0}}\right)^{-m}(t+t_{0})^{-m}=S(t+t_{0}+1)^{-m}

as desired.
∎

Lemma A.3

(Lemma B.5,

.

Lemma A.4
.

Let

is convex,

\displaystyle\leq|b-a|\,{\mathcal{W}}_{1}(Q_{2},R_{2}).

without loss of generality, this holds because the distributions

and
(1-b)Q_{2}+bR_{2}
share a mass of at least
1-b
from
Q_{2}
and
a
from

.

to
R_{2}

Lemma A.5
.

Suppose that the map
{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)
is
\epsilon
-sensitive (

\mathrm{PR}_{t}(\theta)=\mathrm{R}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta)}(\theta)

B.1
Proof of
Lemma

3.1

Here we prove
Lemma

3.1
, restated below for the sake of readability.

See
3.1

Proof.

(The proof is similar to that of Theorem 3.5 of
[
27
]
.
For completeness, we provide the full proof adapted to our settings.)

Define
g(\varphi)=\mathrm{R}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta)}(\varphi)
and
g^{\prime}(\varphi)=\mathrm{R}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta^{\prime})}(\varphi)
for fixed
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
.
Let
{\mathcal{G}}(\theta)\coloneqq\argmin_{\varphi\in\Theta}g(\varphi)
and
{\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})\coloneqq\argmin_{\varphi\in\Theta}g^{\prime}(\varphi)
.
Then the first-order optimality conditions yield

\displaystyle\nabla g({\mathcal{G}}(\theta))^{\top}\big({\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})-{\mathcal{G}}(\theta)\big)

\displaystyle\geq 0,\qquad\nabla g^{\prime}({\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime}))^{\top}\big({\mathcal{G}}(\theta)-{\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})\big)\geq 0.

By strong convexity, we have

\displaystyle g({\mathcal{G}}(\theta))-g({\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\geq\nabla g({\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime}))^{\top}\big({\mathcal{G}}(\theta)-{\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})\big)+\frac{\mu}{2}\|{\mathcal{G}}(\theta)-{\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})\|^{2},

\displaystyle g({\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime}))-g({\mathcal{G}}(\theta))

\displaystyle\geq\nabla g({\mathcal{G}}(\theta))^{\top}\big({\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})-{\mathcal{G}}(\theta)\big)+\frac{\mu}{2}\|{\mathcal{G}}(\theta)-{\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mu}{2}\|{\mathcal{G}}(\theta)-{\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})\|^{2},

where the last inequality comes from the first-order optimality condition of
{\mathcal{G}}(\theta)=\argmin_{\varphi\in\Theta}g(\varphi)
.
Therefore we have

\displaystyle-\nabla g({\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime}))^{\top}\big({\mathcal{G}}(\theta)-{\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle\geq\mu\|{\mathcal{G}}(\theta)-{\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})\|^{2}.

Note that

\displaystyle-\nabla g({\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime}))^{\top}\big({\mathcal{G}}(\theta)-{\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle\leq-\nabla g({\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime}))^{\top}\big({\mathcal{G}}(\theta)-{\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})\big)+\nabla g^{\prime}({\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime}))^{\top}\big({\mathcal{G}}(\theta)-{\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla g({\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime}))-\nabla g^{\prime}({\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime}))\|\cdot\|{\mathcal{G}}(\theta)-{\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})\|,

where the first line comes from the first-order optimality condition of
{\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})=\argmin_{\varphi\in\Theta}g^{\prime}(\varphi)
.

Since
\nabla g(\varphi)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta)}[\nabla_{\theta}\ell(Z,\varphi)]
and
\nabla g^{\prime}(\varphi)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta^{\prime})}[\nabla_{\theta}\ell(Z,\varphi)]
, we can observe that
\beta_{z}
-smoothness and the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality of the
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
distance implies that

\displaystyle\|\nabla g({\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime}))-\nabla g^{\prime}({\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime}))\|

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta)}[\nabla_{\theta}\ell(Z,{\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime}))]-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta^{\prime})}[\nabla_{\theta}\ell(Z,{\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime}))]\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{z}\cdot{\mathcal{W}}_{1}\big({\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta),{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta^{\prime})\big)

and by
\epsilon

{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}_{1}({\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta),{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta^{\prime}))

\displaystyle={\mathcal{W}}_{1}\left((1-\alpha_{t}){\mathcal{D}}(\theta)+\alpha_{t}P_{t},(1-\alpha_{t}){\mathcal{D}}(\theta^{\prime})+\alpha_{t}P_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{t}){\mathcal{W}}_{1}\left({\mathcal{D}}(\theta),{\mathcal{D}}(\theta^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{t})\epsilon\lVert\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rVert.

Therefore we have

\displaystyle\mu\|{\mathcal{G}}(\theta)-{\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-\nabla g({\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime}))^{\top}\big({\mathcal{G}}(\theta)-{\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{t})\epsilon\beta_{z}\cdot\lVert\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rVert\cdot\|{\mathcal{G}}(\theta)-{\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})\|.

If
{\mathcal{G}}(\theta)={\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})
, the desired inequality is trivial.
Otherwise, dividing by
\|{\mathcal{G}}(\theta)-{\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})\|
gives

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{G}}(\theta)-{\mathcal{G}}(\theta^{\prime})\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1-\alpha_{t})\epsilon\beta_{z}}{\mu}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|,

for any
\theta,\theta^{\prime}
.
This immediately implies

\displaystyle\lVert\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1-\alpha_{t})\epsilon\beta_{z}}{\mu}\lVert\theta_{t}-\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}\rVert,

as desired, as
\theta_{t+1}=\mathcal{G}(\theta_{t})
and
\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}
is defined such that
\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}=\mathcal{G}(\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}})
.
∎

&\phantom{{}\geq{}}{}+{}\left(\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}}[\nabla_{\theta}\ell(Z,\theta_{t})]-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}}[\nabla_{\theta}\ell(Z,\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}})]\right)^{\top}(\theta_{t}-\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}).\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\nabla_{\theta}\ell(z,\theta_{t})^{\top}(\theta_{t}-\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}),

which is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left(\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{t}}[\nabla_{\theta}\ell(Z,\theta_{t})]-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}}[\nabla_{\theta}\ell(Z,\theta_{t})]\right)^{\top}(\theta_{t}-\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}})&=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{t}}[\xi_{t}(Z)]-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}}[\xi_{t}(Z)]\\

Plugging (

E.1

Here we prove

Lemma E.1

The logistic loss is
\lambda
-strongly convex in
\theta

if
\|z\|\leq R_{z}

\|\theta\|\leq R_{\theta}
for some finite
R_{z},R_{\theta}

\lambda_{z}+\frac{R_{z}^{2}}{4}

Since Gaussian distributions technically have unbounded support, when plotting the theoretical upper bounds later we use slightly conservative constants as a high-probability surrogate for both the smoothness constants above and the Lipschitz constants.

We can also compute

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}_{1}\bigl({\mathcal{D}}(\theta),{\mathcal{D}}(\theta^{\prime})\bigr)=\|A(\theta-\theta^{\prime})\|\leq 0.1\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|.

Consequently, the sensitivity condition is satisfied.

Computing the stable points.

To approximate
\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}
empirically, we solve the fixed-point problem

\displaystyle\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}=\argmin_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}})}[\ell(Z,\theta^{\prime})]

by a bilevel fixed-point iteration heuristic.
At each outer step, the inner fixed-distribution risk minimization is approximated by projected gradient descent using common random numbers.
The experiment uses
20
outer iterations,
60
inner gradient steps, and inner step size
0.2
for the fixed point iteration to compute
\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}
, and use
512
Monte Carlo samples to estimate the gradient.
Risk evaluations for plotting use
16384
common random samples, and these are shared across algorithms and comparator evaluations for a consistent comparison.

G.2
Further results on synthetic data

Setup.

We also report an experiment on synthetic Gaussian data.
We minimize the squared loss
\ell(z,\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\|z-\theta\|^{2}
over
\Theta=[-B,B]^{d}
with
B=5
.
We use a partially performative setting similar to that of
Section

G.1
:

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{t}(\theta_{t})=(1-\alpha_{t}){\mathcal{D}}(\theta_{t})+\alpha_{t}P_{t},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{N}}(A\theta+m,\Sigma),\qquad P_{t}={\mathcal{N}}(m_{t},\Sigma_{P}).

We use
\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\epsilon=0.3
,
\Sigma=\sigma^{2}I
,
\Sigma_{P}=\sigma_{P}^{2}I
, and
\sigma=\sigma_{P}=0.5
.
The vector
m
is sampled once from
[-1,1]^{d}
, and the matrix
A
is sampled once and rescaled to have operator norm
\epsilon
.
The
\alpha_{t}
grid consists of the constant schedule
\alpha_{t}=1
and polynomial decays
\alpha_{t}\propto t^{-b}
with
b\in\{0.5,0.75,1.0,1.5,2.0\}
.
For the exogenous sequence, we report both a shared i.i.d. randomly varying sequence in the unit Euclidean ball and the stationary sequence
m_{t}\equiv m_{0}
, where
m_{0}
is drawn once from the same bounded construction.
The experiments use
d\in\{2,10\}
,
T_{\mathrm{stab}}=100000
for the algorithms
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{RRM}},\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{RGD}}
, and
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{SGD\text{-}greedy}}
and
T_{\mathrm{lazy}}=1000
for
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{SGD\text{-}lazy}}
.

The quadratic loss is
1
-strongly convex in
\theta
and
1
-smooth in both
\theta
and
z
.
Since
{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)
and
{\mathcal{D}}(\theta^{\prime})
have the same covariance for any
\theta,\theta^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}_{1}\bigl({\mathcal{D}}(\theta),{\mathcal{D}}(\theta^{\prime})\bigr)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{W}}_{1}\bigl({\mathcal{N}}(A\theta+m,\Sigma),{\mathcal{N}}(A\theta+m^{\prime},\Sigma)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\|A(\theta-\theta^{\prime})\|\leq\epsilon\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|.

Thus
{\mathcal{D}}
is
\epsilon
-sensitive, and the partially performative map has sensitivity at most
(1-\alpha_{t})\epsilon
.
Since
\epsilon=0.3<1=\mu/L_{z}
, the stability condition is satisfied.

As in the previous experiment, because Gaussian distributions have unbounded support, we use slightly conservative constants as a high-probability surrogate for the Lipschitz constants that are only used to compute the theoretical upper bounds.

Computing the stable points.

For the stable point, the minimizer of the squared loss under a fixed distribution is its mean.
Hence
\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}
solves

\displaystyle\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha_{t})(A\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}+m)+\alpha_{t}m_{t},

and therefore can be evaluated in closed form as the solution to the linear system:

\displaystyle\theta_{t}^{\mathrm{PS}}=\bigl(I-(1-\alpha_{t})A\bigr)^{-1}\bigl((1-\alpha_{t})m+\alpha_{t}m_{t}\bigr).
