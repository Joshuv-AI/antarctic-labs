Knowledge, Attitudes, and Perceptions of Neurodevelopmental Disorders Among Pregnant Women in Resource‐Limited Settings: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Biazin Yenealem Mekuriaw, Tezerash Abeje, Anteneh Gashaw, Bereket Tesfaye, Getu Belay, Chalachew Kassaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsChildren with neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) are at risk for developmental, cognitive, and psychosocial challenges. This study aimed to assess the level of knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions of pregnant women toward NDDs and to identify factors associated with this.MethodsAn institution‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted. The level of knowledge, attitudes, and perception of pregnant women toward NDDs was assessed using the knowledge, attitudes, and perception domain questionnaires. The data were analyzed using SPSS version 27. Linear regression analysis was performed to examine significant factors associated with knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions of NDDs among pregnant women. The association between variables was assessed using the adjusted unstandardized (β) coefficient with a 95% confidence interval, and variables with a p‐value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant factors associated with the outcome variables.ResultsA total of 404 pregnant women participated. The mean knowledge, attitude, and perception scores were 18.76 ± 3.74, 13.62 ± 4.08, and 26.14 ± 3.07, respectively. When converted to percentages of the maximum possible scores, these corresponded to 43.6%, 38.5%, and 72.8%, respectively, indicating inadequate knowledge, poor attitudes, and good perception toward neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs). Lower educational status and a history of substance use were significantly associated with a lower knowledge scores regarding NDDs, whereas good social support was associated with higher knowledge scores. Having a diploma or higher educational status and strong social support were positively associated with favorable attitudes and perceptions. Additionally, a family history of psychiatric illness was associated with less favorable attitudes toward NDDs.ConclusionThis study highlights a positive perception toward NDDs but reveals significant gaps in knowledge and attitudes, especially among women with limited education and psychosocial vulnerability. Maternal education at the diploma level and higher and strong social support are positively associated with favourable attitudes and perceptions toward NDDs. A history of psychiatric illness was a factor associated with poor attitudes and perceptions regarding NDDs. Therefore, boost community education and reliable health information access; integrate NDD education into routine antenatal care; enhance social support, such as partners and target interventions for women with substance use or psychiatric history.Keywords: ANC, attitude, knowledge, neurodevelopmental disorders, perception, pregnant women

Introduction

Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) are a complex and heterogeneous group of chronic conditions with onset in the child's developmental period, usually in utero or during early childhood [1, 2]. These conditions are accompanied by deficits in the processes of foundation and social functions like reasoning, emotional responses, movement, and interaction, often persisting for the individual's lifetime [2]. NDDs include autism spectrum disorder (ASD), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), intellectual disability, communication impairment, and learning difficulties [2]. The prevalence of NDDs is quite considerable from an international perspective, impacting millions of children and their families while straining health services, especially in low‐resource countries [1]. Even though the causative factors are still not completely understood and multi‐faceted, there are strong causes, including genetic, socio‐environmental, dietary, and prenatal influences, that play a role in their onset [1, 2].

Globally, especially in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), there is inadequate knowledge and understanding of developmental disorders [3]. Some studies pointed out that those misconceptions, stigma, and a lack of adequate information contribute to the delays in the recognition and management of these disorders [3]. Expectant women constitute a pivotal population in the prevention and early detection of NDDs, as antenatal care utilization, maternal health behaviors, and proactive engagement with healthcare services are strongly associated with optimal neurodevelopmental outcomes in children [4, 5]. Instructing mothers on the risk factors of NDDs, such as infections, inadequate diet, vitamin deficiency, poisoning, substance abuse, and psychosocial violence during pregnancy, is crucial for minimizing the occurrence and impact of these disorders [4, 5].

In low‐resource settings, antenatal care is often prioritized toward reducing maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality, frequently at the expense of other critical domains such as child neurodevelopmental health [6]. Therefore, chances for counseling, health promotion, and risk mitigation for neurodevelopmental impairments during pregnancy are negligible [7]. Additionally, deeply rooted sociocultural beliefs coupled with stigma about children with developmental delays tend to result in underutilized services and increased social isolation for the child and family, leading to a reduced quality of life [8].

Identifying pregnant women's existing gaps concerning NDDs is important to designing appropriate, sociocultural relevant health education programs and interventions [9]. These programs may enhance maternal practices during pregnancy, help in identifying vulnerable newborns, and facilitate prompt referrals for necessary specialized care [10]. Also, intervening in such gaps would alleviate the overwhelming social stigma and discrimination encountered by families with children suffering from NDDs, promote social inclusiveness, and strengthen community support [10]. In some contexts where there is limited access to healthcare resources and other mental health services, providing the appropriate information to mothers can positively impact maternal and child health [9, 10]. This study assesses the knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions of NDDs among pregnant women in resource‐limited settings. By examining awareness levels, prevalent misconceptions, and underlying cultural beliefs, this study provides evidence to inform targeted public health interventions and maternal health policy development. Furthermore, it advocates for integrating neurodevelopmental education into routine antenatal care. Ultimately, these findings aim to bridge existing knowledge gaps and support maternal health strategies that prioritize the long‐term developmental well‐being of future generations.

Methods and Materials

Study Area and Design

An institution‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted at Dilla University Teaching Hospital, located in Dilla Town, Ethiopia. Dilla town is the capital of the Gedeo Zone, in southern Ethiopia. Dilla town is 360 km from Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia. The hospital provides a wide range of maternal and child health services, making it an appropriate setting for assessing pregnant women's knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions regarding neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs). During the data collection period, an average of 718 pregnant women attended antenatal care services monthly. The study was conducted from August 1 to November 30, 2024.

Participants

The study included all pregnant women aged 18 years and older who sought antenatal care at Dilla University Referral Hospital. Individuals with severe mental disorders or debilitating physical illnesses were excluded.

Sample Size Determination

The sample size was calculated using the single population proportion formula with a 95% CI, a 5% margin of error, and an estimated proportion (p) of 50%. After adding a 10% non‐response rate, the final sample size was 384 + 38 = 422.

Sampling Procedure

Participants were selected using a systematic random sampling technique using a total list of participants from the hospital. Initially, the number of participants to be recruited from the hospital was allocated based on the total number of participants. The sampling interval (K) was determined by dividing the total number of pregnant women in the ANC sampling frame by the required sample size. The calculated sampling interval was 1.7. Based on this, participants were selected from every other woman from the sampling frame until the total sample size allocated for the hospital was reached.

Data Collection

Data were collected through face‐to‐face interviews using an Amharic version of a structured questionnaire. This instrument assessed socio‐demographic characteristics, psychosocial and obstetric history, clinical and behavioral factors, and participants' knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions. The knowledge, attitude, and perception domains of neurodevelopmental disorders were assessed using an adapted version of the questionnaire of previous studies [4]. The knowledge domain consists of 8 items, attitude 7 items, and perception 6 items. Those participants who scored above 50% for each domain scale were classified as good, and those below 50% were classified as poor. The internal validity of those domains of items was 0.80% [4].

Knowledge Item Scale

These domain‐scale questionnaires are employed to evaluate awareness and recognition of NDD risk factors during pregnancy [4]. It has eight questionnaires; these questionnaires address awareness of the importance of antenatal check‐ups and vaccinations and the impact of injuries during delivery, which can lead to mental disorders in children, speech difficulty, and physical disability. Each correct response was scored as 1, and incorrect or “don't know” responses were scored as 0.

Attitude Item Scale

This item scale assesses awareness of various factors impacting neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) in children [4]. It indicates maternal disposition toward recognised causes and symptoms of NDDs. This item scale has seven questions focused on understanding pregnancy‐related complications, and it covers prenatal risk factors leading to neurodevelopmental disorders, including infections, substance use, maternal health conditions, and age. It also explores knowledge of postnatal signs such as delayed crying, premature birth, and developmental milestones like neck control and sitting. Moreover, the awareness of behavioural indicators like lethargy or hyperactivity is important for the early identification of developmental and health.

Perception Item Scale

This six‐item scale assesses beliefs and the application of knowledge regarding the prevention of neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) during pregnancy, focusing on critical measures such as maternal health, nutrition, vitamin B12 intake, early symptom detection, and parental counseling [4]. Items were scored on a Likert scale and summed to calculate total perception scores, with higher scores reflecting better perception of NDDs.

Operational Definitions

Good knowledge, attitude, or perception is defined as answering 50% or more of the respective questions correctly, while “poor” status is assigned to those scoring below 50%. These operational definitions ensure a standardized evaluation of how effectively participants understand and perceive preventive strategies for adverse developmental events [4].

Data Quality Control and Procedures

The questionnaire was adapted from validated studies and translated into Amharic and Gedeoffa. A 2‐day training was provided to collectors, and a pretest was conducted on 5% of the sample to ensure consistency and clarity. During the data collection period, study participants were informed about the purpose and the importance of the study. Three psychiatry nurses and three midwife nurses were recruited and trained for 2 days as data collectors at each selected hospital. Two supervisors (MSc in psychiatry) were involved during data collection time to supervise the overall data collection procedures. The pretest was conducted on 5% of the total sample size (n = 21) at the antenatal care unit of Yirga Chaffee Primary Hospital to assess the consistency and validity of the data collection tool. Some modifications were made based on the pretest findings prior to the actual data collection. The collected data were checked for completeness and accuracy.

Data Processing and Analysis

The data were first checked for completeness, consistency, and coding. The coded data were entered and cleaned using KoboCollect5 software version 7.3, and then exported into SPSS statistical software version 27 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were presented using text, frequencies, tables, charts, and figures.

Knowledge, attitude, and perception (KAP) toward NDDs were measured using structured questionnaires. Knowledge items were scored as 1 for correct responses and 0 for incorrect responses, while attitude and perception items were measured using Likert scales. Total scores were calculated by summing individual item responses. For descriptive purposes, participants scoring above the 50% for each KAP domain were considered to have better knowledge, positive attitudes, or better perception, while those scoring below 50% were considered to have lower knowledge, less favorable attitudes, or poorer perception.

After confirming that all relevant statistical assumptions were met, simple linear regression analyses were performed to assess the association between each independent variable and the continuous outcome variables (knowledge, attitude, and perception). Variables with p‐values < 0.2 in the bivariate analysis were considered for inclusion in the multiple linear regression model to control for potential confounding factors. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The strength of associations was assessed using adjusted unstandardized β coefficients, with 95% confidence intervals reported to indicate the precision and direction of the associations. Model fit was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R 2).

The majority (40.3%) of the age range was 26–30. The majority (36.4%) of the participants were educated in college and above. About 39.2% of the participants work in the private sector (Table 1).

Knowledge of the Respondents About Neurodevelopmental Disorders

The mean score of knowledge item questions was 18.76 ± 3.74 with a 95% CI of 16.92–20.88. The mean percentage score of the knowledge score was 43.6%. These indicated that the majority of pregnant women had a low level of knowledge. More than half of the participants (51.2%) demonstrated inadequate understanding of the term neurodevelopmental disabilities. The majority (98.0%) of participants recognized the importance of antenatal check‐ups and pregnancy vaccinations, while 2% of the participants lacked this awareness. Furthermore, 90.8% of the participants acknowledged the potential for birth injuries to contribute to childhood mental adversities. More than half (68.1%) of the participants were unaware that delayed speech development (i.e., inability to speak by 7 months) could indicate an NDD. While 29.2% of participants identified prenatal risk factors for NDDs, the majority (70.8%) lacked an understanding of key risks such as maternal infections, toxin exposure, and nutritional deficiencies (Figure 1).

Attitude of the Respondents Toward Their Neurodevelopmental Disorder

The mean score of attitude item questions was 13.62 ± 4.08 (95% CI: 11.01–15.09). The mean percentage score of attitude score was 38.5%, indicating that the majority of pregnant women exhibited a negative attitude. The majority (90.8%) of the participants had an awareness of the potential teratogenic effects of TORCH infections, drug use, alcohol consumption, and smoking during pregnancy. More than half (72.5%) of the participants recognized the association between untreated maternal hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and thyroid disorders and the development of NDDs in offspring. More than half (76.0%) of the participants lacked awareness regarding advanced maternal age as a risk factor for NDDs. The majority (71.0%) of participants failed to recognize delayed attainment of motor milestones, specifically neck control and independent sitting beyond 6 months, as a potential sign of NDDs (Figure 2).

Distribution of attitude levels regarding neurodevelopmental disorders among pregnant women attending antenatal care in Dilla teaching hospital, southern Ethiopia, 2025 (n = 404).

Perception of the Respondents Toward Their Neurodevelopmental Disorder

The mean score of perception item questions was 26.14 ± 3.07 (95% CI of 25.37–28.24). The mean percentage score of perception score was 72.8%. This indicated that the majority of pregnant women had a good level of perception. In terms of intervention strategies, nearly all participants (96.5%) recognized that early and accurate diagnosis is critical for optimizing child outcomes. Furthermore, a substantial majority (83.2%) identified maternal deficiencies in vitamin B12 and folic acid as potential etiological contributors to neurodevelopmental disorders (Figure 3).

Factors Associated With Pregnant Women's Knowledge Regarding NDDs

In the present study, simple linear regression analysis identified urban residence, higher educational status (diploma and above), lifetime substance use, history of past psychiatric illness, and strong social support as factors associated with knowledge of NDDs (p < 0.2). These variables were subsequently entered into the multiple linear regression model. After adjusting for potential confounders, higher educational status (diploma and above), lifetime substance use, and strong social support remained significantly associated with knowledge of NDDs (p < 0.05).

The level of knowledge regarding NDDs among pregnant women who were unable to read and write was lower by 2.21 units (95% CI: (−2.08, −0.46)) compared with those who had a diploma or higher educational qualification. The knowledge regarding NDDs among pregnant women with strong social support was higher by 2.84 units (95% CI: 1.68–3.99) compared with those with poor social support. The knowledge score regarding NDDs was lower by 0.48 units (95% CI: −1.24, −0.63) among pregnant women with a history of lifetime substance use compared with those without such a history (Table 4).

Factors Associated With Pregnant Women's Attitude Regarding NDDs

The simple linear regression analysis indicated that government employment, higher educational attainment (diploma and above), being in the third trimester of pregnancy, a family history of psychiatric illness, and strong social support were factors associated with attitudes toward neurodevelopmental disorders (p < 0.2). The multiple linear regression analysis indicated that higher educational status (diploma and above) and strong social support were positively associated with attitudes toward NDDs. Pregnant women having a family history of psychiatric illness were negatively associated with attitude toward NDDs (p < 0.05). Pregnant women with a diploma or higher educational qualification exhibited an attitude score regarding neurodevelopmental disorders that was 3.13 units higher than that of women with lower educational levels. (95% CI: 1.94, 4.36) compared with those who were unable to read and write. Attitude regarding NDDs among pregnant women with strong social support was higher by 4.24 units (95% CI: 3.23, 5.47) compared with those with poor social support. In contrast, the knowledge score regarding NDDs was lower by 2.45 units (95% CI: (−3.56, −1.25)) among pregnant women with a family history of psychiatric illness compared with those without such a history (Table 5).

Factors Associated With Pregnant Women's Perception Regarding NDDs

The simple linear regression analysis indicated that urban residence, the presence of a known chronic medical illness, higher educational status (diploma and above), third‐trimester pregnancy, past psychiatric illness, and strong social support were factors associated with the perception of NDDs (p‐value < 0.2). The multiple linear regression analysis indicated that higher educational status (diploma and above) was a factor positively associated with perception regarding NDDs. Having a history of psychiatric illness was a factor negatively associated with perception at a p‐value < 0.05. The level of perception regarding NDDs among pregnant women with a diploma and above educational qualification was higher by 1.84 units (95% CI: 1.15–2.95) compared with those who were unable to read and write. Perception regarding NDDs among pregnant women with strong social support was higher by 4.24 units (95% CI: 3.23, 5.47) compared with those with poor social support. In contrast, the perception score regarding NDDs was lower by 3.87 units (95% CI: −5.46, −2.38) among pregnant women with a history of psychiatric illness compared with those without such a history (Table 6).

Discussion

This study evaluated the level of knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions (KAP) of pregnant women regarding neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs). Our findings showed a significant disparity between awareness and perception: while a majority of respondents (72.8%) maintained positive perceptions of NDDs, only 43.6% possessed adequate knowledge, and a concerningly low 38.5% exhibited favorable attitudes. These results highlight a critical public health gap; maternal awareness and disposition are foundational to early detection and timely intervention, both of which are essential for optimizing long‐term developmental outcomes for children [11, 12].

In this study, 43.6% of pregnant women demonstrated adequate knowledge of NDDs, a finding that aligns with previous research conducted in Nigeria [13]. This consistency suggests a pervasive lack of awareness across similar healthcare systems, likely attributable to the inadequate integration of child neurodevelopmental education into routine Antenatal Care (ANC) protocols. In this study, the moderate level of knowledge observed aligns with similar findings in resource‐constrained contexts. For instance, a study conducted in Nigeria reported that less than half of the surveyed women had sufficient knowledge of common NDDs such as autism spectrum disorder and ADHD [14]. Limited awareness in such settings may be attributed to multiple factors, including low access to mental health services, inadequate integration of developmental health in antenatal care, and cultural perceptions that often conflate neurodevelopmental issues with supernatural or behavioral problems [15, 16].

Our results also align with the Health Belief Model [17], which posits that individuals are more likely to engage in health‐seeking behaviors when they perceive a threat and recognize the benefits of action. Pregnant women with higher education or urban residence demonstrated greater knowledge, perhaps due to better access to health communication and a stronger perception of risk.

The low positive attitude rate (28.5%) is particularly concerning. Attitudes toward children with NDDs are often shaped by deep‐seated cultural beliefs and stigma. Negative attitude may manifest as fear, shame, or rejection, thereby contributing to the marginalization of affected individuals and their families [18]. This is compounded by inadequate maternal health education, which fails to address developmental health as a priority. The findings reinforce the need for antenatal programs to include not only biomedical information but also stigma‐reduction strategies that foster empathy, acceptance, and inclusion [19].

Low level of the knowledge and unfavorable attitude, 72.8% of participants expressed a positive perception of NDDs. This discrepancy indicates that while many women recognize the significance of neurodevelopmental health, they may not have accurate or actionable information. Such a gap between perception and knowledge has been documented in previous studies and is often explained by the influence of general awareness campaigns that raise concern but fall short of providing detailed, usable information [20]. These findings highlight a significant opportunity for health promotion programs to refine existing knowledge frameworks and implement targeted interventions that enhance both public understanding and health‐seeking behaviors.

The second objective of this study was to identify factors associated with KAP of NDDs among pregnant women. This finding indicates that pregnant women with lower educational status (below college level) had significantly lower knowledge of neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs). Low level of education attributable to reduced health literacy, limited access to reliable health information, and fewer opportunities for meaningful engagement with structured health education during antenatal care. Women with lower levels of education may face challenges in understanding complex developmental concepts, interpreting health messages, and recognizing early developmental deviations, which collectively contribute to poorer awareness of NDDs. Previous studies have demonstrated that maternal education is a key determinant of health literacy and plays a critical role in recognizing developmental milestones and early warning signs of neurodevelopmental disorders [21, 22, 23]. Lower educational attainment has been repeatedly associated with inadequate recognition of developmental and behavioral problems in children, underscoring the pivotal role of education in shaping maternal awareness and informed health‐seeking behavior [24].

Empirical evidence from diverse settings further supports this relationship. For example, a study conducted in Saudi Arabia reported that women with lower educational backgrounds were significantly less likely to identify early signs of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) compared with women who had attained higher levels of education [25]. Similarly, other studies have shown that maternal education influences the timely identification of developmental concerns such as autism and attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), with lower educational levels linked to delayed recognition and postponed initiation of appropriate interventions [26].

Knowledge regarding neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) was significantly lower among pregnant women who reported a family history of psychiatric illness. This finding may be explained by the pervasive stigma surrounding mental illness, which is particularly pronounced in many low‐income and culturally traditional settings. Families affected by psychiatric disorders often experience internalized shame, fear of labeling, and anticipated discrimination, which may discourage open discussion and proactive engagement with mental health–related information. Rather than facilitating awareness, familial exposure to psychiatric illness may promote secrecy, denial, and avoidance behaviors. Evidence from stigma research indicates that personal or family experience with mental illness does not necessarily translate into improved knowledge; instead, it may contribute to misinformation and reduced mental health literacy [27, 28].

Substance use was associated with lower levels of knowledge regarding NDDs among pregnant women. This finding is consistent with previous studies reported that substance use is associated with reduced health‐seeking behavior, limited engagement with preventive health services, and lower exposure to structured health education [29]. Substance use may also impair cognitive functioning and psychosocial stability, which can hinder the acquisition and retention of health‐related information. Furthermore, women with a history of substance use often face stigma, social marginalization, and weakened social support networks, factors that may further restrict their participation in antenatal care and educational interventions addressing fetal and child neurodevelopment. The previous evidence indicates that substance‐using women have lower awareness of the adverse effects of alcohol and other substances on fetal brain development, including the increased risk of neurodevelopmental disorders such as attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder and learning disabilities [30].

Pregnant women with a diploma or higher educational attainment demonstrated significantly better attitudes and more favorable perceptions toward neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) compared with women who were unable to read and write. Maternal education is a well‐established determinant of health literacy, cognitive awareness, and engagement with health information. Consistent with this finding, a study conducted in Australia reported that higher maternal educational attainment strongly predicts knowledge of child development and neurodevelopmental conditions [31]. Evidence from low‐ and middle‐income countries similarly indicates that higher maternal education enhances access to reliable health information, improves comprehension of antenatal counseling, and increases utilization of formal healthcare services, all of which contribute to improved awareness and perception of NDDs [31, 32]. Educated women are more likely to access mass media, digital platforms, and evidence‐based sources of information, facilitating early recognition of developmental delays and associated risk factors. Moreover, higher educational attainment is associated with reduced misconceptions and fatalistic beliefs regarding developmental and psychiatric conditions, thereby fostering more positive and accepting attitudes toward children with NDDs. Previous studies conducted in low‐ and middle‐income settings have consistently reported that women with secondary or higher education exhibit more favorable attitudes toward childhood developmental disorders, including autism spectrum disorder and intellectual disability, compared with women with no formal education [33, 34]. Education also increases exposure to antenatal counseling and structured health messaging, which further shapes perceptions and attitudes related to early child development and neurodevelopmental functioning. A study from India reported that awareness and perception of NDDs among pregnant women were notably low and largely attributable to limited formal education, with only 24% having ever heard of NDDs and merely 13% understanding their meaning [4]. Collectively, these findings reinforce prior evidence that higher educational status enhances health literacy, awareness, and the capacity to access and interpret maternal and child mental health information. Studies across low‐ and middle‐income countries have repeatedly identified maternal education as a strong predictor of awareness and perception of childhood neurodevelopmental disorders [35], likely through increased exposure to formal healthcare systems, media, and digital information platforms that promote understanding of early neurodevelopmental risks and functions.

Pregnant women with strong social support demonstrated significantly higher levels of knowledge, more favorable attitudes, and better perceptions regarding neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) compared with those who reported poor social support. This association is well supported by behavioral and social science frameworks that emphasize the central role of interpersonal networks in shaping maternal health literacy and beliefs. Pregnant women with strong social support had higher knowledge scores regarding NDDs than their counterparts with limited support. Social support from partners, family members, peers, and community structures facilitates the sharing of health‐related information, emotional reassurance, and collective problem‐solving, all of which are particularly critical during pregnancy [36]. Women embedded in supportive social environments are more likely to receive guidance on child development, attend antenatal care visits, and participate in maternal education programs, thereby enhancing their understanding of neurodevelopmental outcomes [37, 38]. Similarly, pregnant women with strong social support exhibited substantially more positive attitudes toward NDDs. This finding is consistent with stress‐buffering and social learning theories, which posit that supportive social networks promote adaptive attitudes by providing emotional reassurance, modeling positive health behaviors, and facilitating the exchange of accurate information. Previous studies have shown that social support positively influences maternal attitudes and beliefs about child health and developmental disorders by reducing fear, stigma, and perceived burden [39]. Women within supportive family and community contexts may also have greater opportunities to openly discuss child development concerns, which helps normalize NDDs and promotes acceptance.

Furthermore, strong social support was associated with a markedly higher perception score regarding NDDs. This finding aligns with social ecological and stress‐buffering models, which highlight the influence of social networks on health‐related knowledge, beliefs, and coping capacities. Evidence from studies among pregnant women and caregivers indicates that support from partners, family, and the wider community enhances maternal engagement with health services and facilitates the exchange of information related to child development and mental health [40]. Through shared experiences, peer discussions, and encouragement to seek care and information, women with strong social support are more likely to develop accurate and positive perceptions of NDDs. In addition, supportive social networks have been shown to enhance psychological well‐being and health awareness among pregnant women. Research on antenatal cognitive and mental health outcomes similarly demonstrates that strong social support protects against psychological distress and contributes to healthier cognitive perceptions and functioning [41].

Pregnant women with a history of psychiatric illness demonstrated significantly lower perception scores regarding neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs). Although direct evidence linking maternal psychiatric history to reduced perception of NDDs is limited, a substantial body of antenatal mental health literature indicates that pre‐existing mental health conditions adversely affect cognitive processing, health‐related appraisal, and information retention. Studies have consistently shown that women with a history of mental illness have higher odds of experiencing antenatal psychiatric morbidity, which is frequently associated with reduced engagement in health education activities and lower levels of health literacy [42]. Moreover, individuals living with mental illness may encounter barriers such as impaired cognitive engagement, internalized stigma, and limited access to accurate and timely health information [27]. Psychiatric symptoms, particularly depression and anxiety, are known to compromise concentration, motivation, and learning capacity, thereby reducing the ability to seek, comprehend, and retain information related to child neurodevelopment. Consistent with this explanation, prior maternal mental health studies have reported that psychiatric morbidity is associated with lower maternal awareness and suboptimal preventive health behaviors [43].

Implication of the Study

These findings offer critical insights for public health policy, maternal education, and early childhood intervention, particularly in low‐resource settings. The identified deficits in maternal knowledge (43.6%) and positive attitudes (28.5%) regarding neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) underscore an urgent need for targeted antenatal education. By strengthening maternal awareness, healthcare systems can facilitate earlier detection and intervention, mitigating the long‐term impact on families. Furthermore, the correlation between rural residence, occupational status, and awareness levels suggests that interventions must be context‐specific. Integrating NDD education into routine antenatal care will improve health literacy and empower women, ultimately fostering superior developmental outcomes for children.

Limitations

First, this study relied on self‐reported data from pregnant women regarding their knowledge and attitudes toward NDDs, which may be subject to social desirability bias or recall inaccuracies. While participants may express positive perceptions during an interview, these may not fully reflect their real‐world health‐seeking behaviors or the depth of their clinical understanding. Future research should consider longitudinal designs or qualitative interviews to capture more detailed maternal behaviors. Second, the study was conducted within a specific limited‐resource clinical setting, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to women in private healthcare or those in more urbanized regions with better access to information. Third, although the survey instrument was adapted, translated into the local language, and pre‐tested to ensure clarity, it has not undergone formal psychometric validation within this specific population. While these steps enhanced the tool's utility, we acknowledge that full linguistic and cultural reliability requires further rigorous validation studies. Finally, this study did not account for institutional variations between different healthcare facilities, which may influence the quality of health education provided during antenatal care.

Conclusions

This study reveals that while pregnant women in this resource‐limited setting maintain a relatively high perception of neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), their level of knowledge and attitudes remains low. Specifically, over half of the participants demonstrated poor NDD knowledge, and only a quarter held positive attitudes. Therefore, to improve NDD awareness, attitude, and perception among pregnant women, provide tailored maternal health education for working women to improve NDD attitude, integrate assessment of NDDs into routine antenatal care to enable early identification and intervention. Implement targeted education programs explaining NDD types, early signs, risk factors, and the role of early interventions, and develop targeted health education interventions for pregnant women with a history of substance use or psychiatric illness to address misinformation and stigma related to neurodevelopmental conditions. Integrate NDD education into routine antenatal care to improve maternal knowledge, with special emphasis on women with low educational attainment. Strengthen social support mechanisms, including partner involvement and community‐based maternal support groups, to enhance positive attitudes and perceptions toward NDDs. For future researchers, conduct qualitative and longitudinal research to gain deeper insights into pregnant women's NDD knowledge, attitude, and perceptions.
