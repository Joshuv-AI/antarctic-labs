Benefit of Psyllium with Weight Loss Diet in Preventing Liver Dysfunction in Overweight or Obese Adults with Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD)
Authors: Zahra Akbarzadeh, Mojgan Nourian, Gholamreza Askari, Mohammad R. Maracy, Reza Amani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:MASLD is the revised nomenclature of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) which is the most common chronic liver disease worldwide and it significantly impacts the burden of the disease. Individuals who are overweight or obese have three times higher risk of developing MASLD. Losing body weight is, therefore, recommended to manage MASLD. This study was performed to assess the effect of psyllium on liver function in overweight or obese adults with (MASLD).Methods:We assessed 80 overweight or obese MASLD patients diagnosed by elastography. They were divided into intervention and placebo groups through randomized block design. Intervention group was given 10 grams of psyllium and the control group was given 10 grams of ground wheat as supplement. Weight loss diet and physical activity (PA) recommendations were followed by both groups for 10 weeks. Serum liver enzymes, liver function (measured by fibroscan), PA, and dietary intake were assessed pre- and post-intervention.Results:Totally, 78 participants finished the RCT. After controlling confounding variables, intake of calorie (P = 0.02), carbohydrate (P = 0.01), alanine aminotransferase (P = 0.006), waist circumference (P = 0.006), BMI (P = 0.006), and liver fibrosis score (0.001) were lowered in the intervention group compared with the controls. However, serum aspartate aminotransferase reduction was not significant (P = 0.98).Conclusions:Psyllium can be introduced as a dietary supplement that exerts beneficial role in gut health, weight loss, and preventing MASLD.

Introduction

Because NAFLD does not accurately reflect the current understanding of disease drivers, it is revised to MASLD.[1]

MASLD is the most common chronic liver disease worldwide and it significantly affects the burden of disease.[2] When the accumulation of fat (mainly triglycerides) occurs in more than 5% of the liver cells, fatty liver appears. Because of the limited number of pain sensors in the liver, liver diseases are often not painful, so a chronic liver disease can remain undiagnosed for a long time.[2]

The prevalence of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease in Western countries is 30%–40%.[3] A meta-analysis study showed that the global prevalence of MASLD is estimated at 30%. Latin America had the highest prevalence of MASLD (i.e., 44.37%).[4]

Individuals who are overweight or obese have a three times higher risk of developing MASLD than individuals who are normal weight.[56789] Since type 2 diabetes and obesity are increasing among youth, the prevalence of MASLD in children and young adults (ages 6–29 years) has increased (40%).[10] Development of MASLD is influenced by some genes (GCKR, TM6SF2, PNPLA3, MBOAT7, and HSD17B13), gut dysbiosis, race, and environmental factors such as insufficient access to affordable nutrient-dense foods and/or safe areas to do physical activity as well as areas with high levels of air pollution.[56711] Notably, MASLD has a significant economic burden.[12] It is possible to prevent or postpone its progression, optimize the management of early cirrhosis, and decrease the risk of developing cardiovascular disease (CVD) or diabetes by diagnosing and treating liver disease early.[13]

Psyllium (Plantago ovata) is a kind of fiber that has been used in traditional medicine in some countries.[14] Psyllium is water-soluble and form a viscous colloidal solution.[15] The ratio of soluble to insoluble dietary fiber in psyllium is approximately 7:3.[16] The fibers are also related to a lower postprandial glucose response and decreased blood cholesterol concentration.[17]

Psyllium is a kind of Soluble Non-Starch Carbohydrates (NSP) that can have lipid-lowering, anti-diabetic, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory effects on human health.[181920] Consumption of prebiotic fiber like psyllium has direct and indirect mechanisms on the intestinal microbiota.[21] A meta-analysis showed that psyllium can effectively reduce body weight and waist circumference.[22] One study showed that psyllium is more effective than an anti-obesity drug (orlistat) in reducing serum triglycerides, lipopolysaccharides, and liver cholesterol via increasing the abundance of Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, Bacteroides, and Coprobacillus.[23] It is suggested that the gut microbiome may play an important role in the pathogenesis of MASLD.[24]

According to our knowledge, there have been studies that reported inverse correlations between dietary fiber intake and MASLD.[25262728] One study by Yu Zhu et al. showed that adding dietary fiber to nutritional advice has significant benefits in preventing MASLD.[28] The results of the study conducted by Quetglas-Llabrés et al.[29] in 40 MASLD adult patients who followed a 24-month lifestyle intervention based on the Mediterranean diet showed that antioxidants and inflammatory biomarkers were improved post-intervention. Identifying potential modifiable factors that can prevent the development of MASLD is necessary because no effective medical treatment for MASLD has been reported.[26] Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effect of Psyllium on the improvement of liver function in overweight or obese adults with MASLD.

Methods and Materials

Totally, 80 overweight or obese adults (43 men and 37 women) with BMI ≥25 kg/m2 (mean age = 44.9 years) were enrolled in a placebo-controlled, single-blind, parallel randomized clinical trial study December 2015–March 2015 at the Department of Internal Medicine, Shariati Hospital, Isfahan, Iran. A block design was used to randomize the selection.

Inclusion criteria were overweight and obese adults with MASLD diagnosed by Physical examination and/or laboratory tests like serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) more than 40 IU/L (14) and/or elastometry values greater than 4 kPa in Fibroscan (FibroScan 402, Echosens, France).

The exclusion criteria involved chronic liver disorders (such as cirrhosis, hepatitis, and liver cancer), lactation or pregnancy, taking corticosteroids, anticonvulsants, and antiepileptic drugs, any cancers, using a weight loss diet over the past 3 months and/or preference to not continue the study. To enter the study, all patients filled out the written consent form. The Medical Ethics Committee at the Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (NO: 394327) approved the study protocol. The sample size was determined 35 individuals in each group using a power level of 0.8 and a 2-sided confidence level of 0.05. The total sample size for each group was finally calculated at 40, considering a 10% dropout estimate. For blinding, all participants were given the same color packet with the same instruction.

Finally, 38 individuals (13 males and 25 females) were given 10 grams of psyllium and 37 individuals (15 females and 22 males) took 10 grams of crushed wheat as the placebo. Packets labeled A and B by a different person. At the end of the study, the packets were counted to determine adherence to the study.

Each pack (5 gr) was consumed at least 30 minutes before breakfast and dinner with 240 mL water twice a day. No sugar or artificial sweeteners were added. The study lasted 10 weeks. Patients were followed with weekly calls and monthly visits to ensure true consumption. Participants who failed to consume more than 10% of their packets, were excluded from the study.

Demographic information, physical activity levels, and dietary intake were assessed by demographic, physical activity, and 3-day food record questionnaires, respectively.

All biochemical assessments were accomplished in the same laboratory using standard laboratory methods. According to standard protocol, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were measured by enzymatic Photometric assay (Parsazmoun, Tehran, Iran) at the baseline and end of week 10. Dietary modification advice including increased consumption of vegetables, whole grains, and low-fat dairy products was given to all patients who were enrolled.

Both groups followed regular exercise, with a minimum of 30 minutes per session, at least three times a week. Weight loss diets were given to all participants based on their energy requirements. Total daily energy intake was reduced by 300–500 kcal from each participant’s estimated energy requirement. Total dietary fat content was restricted to less than 30%. Saturated fats were less than 10% while carbohydrate intake made up 40%–54% of the total calorie intake. The recommended amount of weight loss was 0.5–1.5 kg per week. Higher weight loss (generally >1.6 kg per week) may lead to inflammation with NASH and was not suggested.[30]

A 3-day food record was used to assess nutrition status. All participants recorded the foods and beverages they ate and drank in 3 days.

Analysis of food consumption was conducted by using a customized version of Nutritionist IV software. At weeks 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10, the metabolic equivalent task (MET) questionnaire was applied to assess the patient’s physical activity.

Liver stiffness was assessed using Fibro scan-402 with 3. 5 MHz frequency by an experienced gastroenterologist (R.R) in all patients pre and post-intervention. Patients were in a dorsal decubitus position, with their right arm in maximum abduction, above their head. The probe was placed perpendicular to the skin surface over one of the intercostal spaces near the right lobe of the liver. This method is very simple and non-invasive. The participant took a deep breath while lying on his back. In this method, ultrasound waves are radiated to the liver in three different positions, and its return time is reported in one to three minutes, after that, the doctor can determine the stiffness of the liver based on the number obtained from the fibroscan. The stage of liver fibrosis is classified as F0-F4.

The normal distribution of data was verified using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. At the end of the study, data was analyzed using SPSS software version 18 (SPSS Chicago, IL, USA). A p value less than 0.05 means that the difference is statistically significant. The mean ± SD was used to report continuous variables. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Demographic variables were analyzed using the Chi-squared test. Student’s t-test and paired t-test were used for comparing variables within and between groups, respectively. In case of non-parametric data, results were compared using Wilcoxon and Mann-Whitney tests, accordingly. Differences between the groups from the baseline to week 10 were assessed by analysis of the covariance test.

Results

A total of 75 of the 80 volunteers completed the study, and the mean age was 44.6 years. Two participants of intervention groups could not tolerate psyllium so they were excluded from the study. In placebo groups, 5 persons were excluded; 3 of them for immigration and 2 persons for lack of cooperation. There was no significant difference between the groups in terms of height, age, waist and hip circumferences, waist-to-hip ratio, physical activity level, and serum ALT and AST levels. [Table 1]. As shown in Table 2, there was a significant difference between calorie (P = 0.02), and carbohydrate (P = 0.01) intakes, after controlling confounding variables (gender, age, smoking, literacy level, energy intake, BMI) between the groups. According to Table 3, after adjusting the above confounding variables, waist circumference and waist/hip ratio were decreased significantly in both groups (P = 0.05). Weight loss and BMI reduction were observed in both groups (P = 0.03 and P = 0.006, respectively).

Discussion

The present study showed that the daily administration of 10 grams of psyllium along with a plus weight loss diet and other dietary recommendations for 10 weeks improved anthropometric indices, especially abdominal obesity, and fibrosis score, and decreased the serum level of ALT. These changes may subsequently delay liver damage and prevent its progress in MASLD patients.

Previous studies have shown after significant weight loss, improvement was seen in MASLD liver enzymes.[31323334] Comparative studies in various settings, including registries, primary care settings, community cohorts, and population studies, have noted that MASLD overlaps entirely with the NAFLD population.[13536]

Weight loss was linked to remission of MASLD in participants regardless of their type of metabolic dysfunction.[37] The aim of nutrition therapy in MASLD patients is to decrease energy intake by 500–1000 calories per day to attain a 7–10 percent weight reduction.[2383940]

To date, several studies have reported an inverse relationship between dietary fiber intake and MASLD.[25262728] By increasing nutritional fiber intake, patients with MASLD can improve their hepatic steatosis, reduce serum Zonulin levels (a marker of intestinal permeability), lower liver enzymes, and prevent hepatic steatosis.[41] A recent study of 9,254 American participants aged 18-80 years demonstrated that the relationship between dietary fiber and MASLD was inverse, and the dose-response curves showed that a higher intake of dietary fiber could have even more benefits in protecting against MASLD.[28] A cross-sectional study with 6,613 adult participants with MASLD reported that increasing dietary fiber intake from 38 mg/kg/d to 117 mg/kg/d was found to reduce 60% the likelihood of MASLD.[26]

When comparing those who consumed the highest amount of dietary fiber with those of the lowest amount, observational data suggests a 30% decrease in obesity incidence.[42] Psyllium husk, which is classified as a food, is a safe choice for weight control.[23] In one animal study, while orlistat is commonly used for weight loss, psyllium husk was reported as a more cost-effective option to treat hypercholesterolemia and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease caused by a high-fat diet, according to Deng et al. study.[23] The viscous gel of psyllium reduces blood cholesterol levels by binding to bile acids in the gut and eliminating them through the stool.[23] It was also reported that obese mice fed a high-fat diet (HFD) experienced an increase in food intake after taking orlistat, compared to psyllium which resulted in an increase in water intake, decreased serum triglyceride levels, and decreased glucose tolerance.[23]

According to the results of the study on 56 adult male Wister rats, hepatic protection and improvement of liver enzyme disorders and lipid profiles in MASLD patients may be caused by the antioxidant properties of psyllium extract.[43]

Dietary fibers can positively function to control appetite and body weight. One meta-analysis study reported that psyllium supplementation does not reduce body weight, and waist circumference significantly.[44] According to a review, it was suggested that psyllium supplements could help control weight in individuals with overweight and obesity.[45] In another systematic review consuming psyllium-based cereals for breakfast can have a positive impact on BMI and lower the risk of being overweight or obese.[46]

In most cases, MASLD develops when there are alterations in glucose and lipid metabolism. Insulin resistance (IR) at the level of the liver, and adipose tissue causes ectopic fat accumulation, worsening of IR, excess free fatty acids (FFAs), and lipotoxicity. Excess FFA levels can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, and increased oxidative stress, which triggers a fibrogenic response in hepatocytes cells that can contribute to the progression of NASH and cirrhosis.[4748]

Dietary fiber is fermented by intestinal microorganisms to produce short-chain fatty acids (propionic acid, butyric acid, etc.), which regulate hepatic lipid metabolism, improve insulin sensitivity, and affect the permeability of the intestinal barrier.[2627] SCFAs can reduce the risk of MASLD by promoting energy expenditure and lipid oxidation in an adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase AMPK-dependent manner.[28] Also, SCFAs can activate G-protein-coupled receptors (GPR41 and GPR43) and suppress appetite, ultimately preventing weight gain.[28] In a study, it was found that psyllium supplementation hurt participants’ hunger and desire to eat, especially in higher doses.[49] Consuming this soluble fiber can cause a delay in gastric emptying[4450] and a slowdown in carbohydrate absorption. In humans, it has been demonstrated that psyllium can improve glucose and insulin responses.[41] Psyllium husk affects the composition of the microbiota, changes the fecal water content and alters the transit time of fecal mass.[51]

In MASLD patients, there was an increase in the abundance of the Lactobacillus genus and a decrease in the Ruminococcaceae family.[5253] It seems that psyllium can be beneficial in MASLD patients through changes in intestinal flora.

Strengths of this study

Visiting patients every two weeks and prescribing a diet that was adjusted for each person according to their economic conditions and usual eating habits are considered as the study strengths.

Limitations of this study

First, the design of our study was cross-sectional and could not define the causal relationship between nonalcoholic fatty liver disease and dietary fiber intake. Second, the relatively small sample size and short intervention time with no follow-up period after the end of the intervention should be considered.

Conclusions

It seems that taking psyllium supplements along with weight loss diet and increased physical activity can improve anthropometric indices, especially abdominal obesity, and decrease the serum level of alanine aminotransferase enzyme and more importantly, the fibrosis score. These positive changes can subsequently delay liver damage and prevent its progression in MASLD patients. Psyllium has the potential to be a food supplement that improves gut health and decreases the risk of MASLD.
