Clinicopathological Features of Lupus Erythematosus Panniculitis (LEP): A Retrospective Analysis of 54 Cases
Authors: Maryam Nasimi, Narjes Ramezani, Kambiz Kamyab, Alireza Abdshah, Maryam Ghiasi, Sahar Montazeri, Hanie Babaie, Fatemeh Saberi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLupus Erythematosus Panniculitis (LEP) is a rare subtype of Cutaneous Lupus Erythematosus. It is a challenging disease to diagnose and treat due to limited understanding of its clinical and histopathological characteristics.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective review of 54 biopsy‐proven LEP cases evaluated at a dermatology referral center from 2016 to 2022. Demographic, clinical, laboratory, and treatment data were extracted from medical records, and histopathologic features were reassessed when slides required further characterization. Direct immunofluorescence and serologic results were recorded when available. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (v26), with significance defined as p < 0.05.ResultsFifty‐four patients with LEP (female‐to‐male ratio 5:1; mean age 38.8 ± 14.7 years) were included. Coexistent systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) was present in 24.1% of cases. Delayed diagnosis was significantly associated with lipoatrophy (p < 0.001). Histopathologic findings most commonly included lymphocytic vasculitis (72.2%), histologic features of discoid lupus erythematosus (68.5%), and lobular panniculitis (63%). ANA, anti‐double‐stranded DNA, and direct immunofluorescence were positive in 24.1%, 16.6%, and 72.2% of patients, respectively. Overall, 85.2% of patients improved clinically. Hydroxychloroquine combined with corticosteroids and mycophenolate mofetil was associated with the highest improvement rates, whereas intralesional corticosteroid therapy appeared least effective, although differences were not statistically significant.ConclusionIt is essential to evaluate the clinical and histopathologic features of patients accurately before diagnosis. Delay in diagnosis may be more common in patients without associated SLE or DLE, although these associations were not statistically significant in our cohort. Hydroxychloroquine‐based combination therapy, particularly with corticosteroids and mycophenolate mofetil, was associated with favorable clinical outcomes, although prospective studies are needed to establish optimal treatment strategies.

Introduction

Lupus erythematosus panniculitis (LEP), also known as lupus profundus, is an uncommon subtype of cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE) that accounts for only 1%–3% of cases [1, 2, 3]. This subtype may present in isolation or in conjunction with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) or discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE) [1]. Lupus panniculitis can affect individuals of any age and gender, but it is more commonly observed in women between the ages of 30 and 60 years [3].

Clinically, LEP manifests in subcutaneous tissue as recurring tender, indurated plaques and nodules; over time, these lesions may resolve with lipoatrophy, dermal fibrosis, deep scarring, and contour deformities that markedly impact quality of life [4, 5, 6].

While several case series have described LEP, the literature remains limited by small cohorts, variable reporting of histopathologic findings, and inconsistent assessment of factors such as diagnostic delay, risk of disfigurement, and association with SLE or DLE [3, 4, 7]. Recent retrospective studies have further characterized disease course and therapeutic responses but continue to highlight the rarity and heterogeneity of LEP [3, 4, 7, 8]. One of the largest recent series reviewed 61 patients and found substantial diagnostic latency and a higher than historically reported SLE association [3]. Yet even this study highlighted heterogeneous features and did not comprehensively integrate clinical, laboratory, and histologic parameters in one large cohort. Consequently, a clear knowledge gap persists in the characterization of LEP; specifically, the frequency and interactions of hallmark clinical features, the predictors of diagnostic delay, or poor outcome, and histopathologic correlations.

In this context, we present a retrospective analysis of 54 biopsy‐confirmed cases of LEP, representing the largest single‐center series from our region. Specifically, this study aimed to: (1) characterize the demographic, clinical, laboratory, and histopathologic features of LEP; (2) evaluate associations between clinical characteristics, including diagnostic delay and lipoatrophy; and (3) describe treatment patterns and clinical outcomes in this cohort. By addressing these objectives, we sought to improve understanding of the clinicopathologic spectrum of LEP, highlight the potential clinical consequences of diagnostic delay, and provide observational data regarding treatment patterns and outcomes that may guide future prospective studies.

Methods

This retrospective study included 54 patients with a clinicopathologic diagnosis of LEP who were evaluated at Razi Hospital, a tertiary dermatology referral center, between 2016 and 2022. Cases were identified through a review of hospital medical records from the study period. Patients were eligible if they had a clinicopathologic diagnosis of LEP supported by compatible clinical findings and histopathologic examination of skin biopsy specimens. Patients without histopathologic confirmation, those with insufficient clinical, laboratory, or histopathologic records to confirm the diagnosis, or those in whom an alternative diagnosis was established after clinicopathologic review were excluded.

Demographic, clinical, laboratory, and histopathologic data, together with treatment modalities and clinical outcomes, were extracted from the medical records. Variables assessed included age, sex, lesion morphology and distribution, associated SLE or DLE, laboratory findings, histopathologic characteristics, treatment regimens, clinical outcomes, and time to diagnosis (defined as the interval between patient‐reported symptom onset and the establishment of the clinicopathologic diagnosis of LEP). Associations between time to diagnosis and relevant demographic and clinicopathologic variables were evaluated. When additional histopathologic characterization was required, biopsy slides were re‐cut and re‐evaluated. Histopathologic features assessed included lobular or septal panniculitis, hyaline fat degeneration, nodular lymphoid structures in the deep dermis and subcutaneous fat, hyperkeratosis, hydropic degeneration of the basal layer, perivascular lymphocytic infiltration, and increased mucin deposition. Missing data were handled using available‐case analysis, and no imputation was performed.

This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee. Patients had provided consent at the time of their initial visit for the future use of de‐identified clinical photographs for research and publication purposes.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software version 26. Quantitative variables were summarized using means and standard deviations, whereas categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using the χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Comparisons between groups for quantitative variables were performed using the independent‐samples t‐test for normally distributed data and the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test for non‐normally distributed data. A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 54 patients with biopsy‐confirmed LEP were included, comprising 45 (83.3%) women and 9 (16.7%) men. The mean age at diagnosis was 38.8 years (SD = 14.7). Although the mean age was higher in men than in women (46.4 vs. 37.4 years), the difference was not statistically significant. Thirteen patients (24.1%), including 1 man and 12 women, had a prior diagnosis of SLE. No significant association was observed between sex and the presence of SLE (p = 0.428).

The mean time to diagnosis was 22.1 months (SD = 33.3). Patients with a history of SLE had a shorter mean time to diagnosis than those without SLE (14.4 vs. 24.5 months), although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.193).

Tenderness was reported in 40 patients (74.1%), and pruritus in 16 (29.6%). Plaques were the most common lesion morphology, occurring in 33 patients (61.1%), followed by nodules in 20 (37.0%), papules in 2 (3.7%), and patches in 2 (3.7%). Erythema of the overlying skin was observed in 43 patients (79.6%), while indurated subcutaneous nodules or plaques were present in 39 (72.2%).

Lipoatrophy was observed in 11 patients (20.4%), and ulceration in 7 (13.0%) (Figure 1). Patients with lipoatrophy had a significantly longer mean time to diagnosis than those without lipoatrophy (32.5 vs. 5.6 months, p < 0.001).

The distribution of lesions was as follows: proximal upper extremities (40.7%), head (29.6%), trunk (29.6%), proximal lower extremities (22.2%), distal upper extremities (14.8%), and distal lower extremities (13.0%). No significant associations were observed between the anatomical distribution of lesions and a history of systemic lupus erythematosus (all p > 0.05).

Clinical features of DLE, including dyspigmentation, scarring alopecia, and scaly plaques, were present in 12 patients (22.2%). Five of these patients (41.7%) also had SLE; however, no significant association was observed between clinical DLE features and SLE (p = 0.134). Overall, 46 patients (85.2%) reported systemic symptoms, including pain, fever, myalgia, arthralgia, and fatigue. Among these patients, 11 (23.9%) had a history of SLE, with no significant association between systemic symptoms and SLE (p = 1.000).

Treatment and Clinical Outcomes

Patients were treated with hydroxychloroquine (52 patients), oral corticosteroids (33), mycophenolate mofetil (25), topical corticosteroids (13), and intralesional corticosteroid injections (16). Overall, 46 patients (85.2%) experienced clinical improvement, 6 (11.1%) had no change in symptoms, and 2 (3.7%) experienced worsening disease.

Among the 52 patients treated with hydroxychloroquine, 27 (51.9%) also received corticosteroids, of whom 22 (81.4%) demonstrated clinical improvement. Three patients (5.7%) received hydroxychloroquine in combination with mycophenolate mofetil, and 2 (66.7%) improved clinically. An additional 21 patients (40.3%) received hydroxychloroquine, corticosteroids, and mycophenolate mofetil, with 19 (90.4%) demonstrating clinical improvement. No statistically significant differences in clinical improvement were observed among treatment regimens.

Among patients receiving hydroxychloroquine in combination with corticosteroids, the proportion of patients with clinical improvement was lowest in those treated with intralesional corticosteroid injections (68.8%), compared with more than 90% among those receiving oral or topical corticosteroids; however, this difference was not statistically significant.

No significant associations were identified between age, sex, history of SLE, history of DLE, and either clinical improvement or time to diagnosis.

Laboratory Findings

ANA was positive in 13 patients (24.1%), of whom 11 had a history of SLE. Anti‐dsDNA antibodies were positive in 9 patients (16.7%), including 8 with SLE. Positive ANA and anti‐dsDNA antibodies were both significantly associated with the presence of SLE (both p < 0.001), with strong strengths of association (Cramer's V = 0.797 and 0.678, respectively).

Direct immunofluorescence (DIF) was positive in 39 patients (72.2%), including 9 patients with SLE and 30 without SLE. No significant association was observed between DIF positivity and SLE (p = 0.782; Cramer's V = 0.038), indicating a negligible strength of association.

Subcutaneous involvement. Lobular panniculitis was present in 34 patients (63.0%), and septal panniculitis in 28 (51.9%). Inflammatory infiltrates within the subcutaneous fat included plasma cells (46.3%), histiocytes (25.9%), eosinophils (11.1%), and neutrophils (1.9%). Hyaline fat necrosis was observed in 21 patients (38.9%), while subcutaneous necrosis and fat fibrosis were each identified in one patient (1.9%). Lymphoid follicle formation with germinal centers was present in 6 patients (11.1%).

Dermal involvement. Lymphocytic infiltration of the deep dermis was observed in 32 patients (59.3%), mucin deposition in 27 (50.0%), dermal fibrosis in 17 (31.5%), dermal necrosis in 1 (1.9%), follicular plugging in 1 (1.9%), perivascular lymphocytic infiltration in 36 (66.7%), peri‐adnexal inflammation in 7 (13.0%), and calcification in 4 (7.4%). Lymphocytic vasculitis, characterized by lymphocytic infiltration of and around blood vessels with erythrocyte extravasation, was present in 39 patients (72.2%).

Epidermal involvement. Interface dermatitis was observed in 20 patients (37.0%), acanthosis in 16 (29.6%), hyperkeratosis in 14 (25.9%), dyskeratosis in 4 (7.4%), basement membrane thickening in 3 (5.6%), and epidermal atrophy in 8 (14.8%).

Overall, histopathologic features of DLE, including interface dermatitis, epidermal atrophy, hyperkeratosis, follicular plugging, basement membrane thickening, and perivascular or peri‐adnexal inflammation, were identified in 37 patients (68.5%). Among patients with SLE, 8 (61.5%) demonstrated these histopathologic features, compared with 29 (70.7%) of those without SLE. This difference was not statistically significant (Table 2).

Discussion

In this retrospective study of 54 patients with biopsy‐confirmed LEP, we characterized the demographic, clinical, laboratory, and histopathologic features of one of the largest single‐center cohorts reported from our region. LEP predominantly affected women, frequently presented with tender plaques or nodules involving the proximal extremities, and demonstrated characteristic histopathologic findings, including lymphocytic vasculitis, histologic features of discoid lupus erythematosus, and lobular panniculitis. We also observed that longer time to diagnosis was significantly associated with lipoatrophy, while hydroxychloroquine‐based combination therapy was associated with favorable clinical outcomes.

According to prior studies conducted in Western populations, middle‐aged women (40–50 years) seem to be most affected by LEP [9, 10], whereas studies from Asian communities have shown that LEP seems to affect a slightly younger age group of between 20 and 40 years old [1, 11]. In our study, the average age of patients was 38.8, with a female/male ratio of 5:1, which correlates well with results from Asian populations. The mean age was 46.6 years in men and 37.36 years in women. Although this difference was not statistically significant, the fact that multiple studies indicate men tend to develop SLE and CLE at an older age than women [12, 13, 14] suggests the need for epidemiological research to explore the sex‐based differences in this disease.

The reported prevalence of coexistent SLE in patients with LEP ranges from 10% to 42% [5]. In a retrospective series of 61 patients, Rangel et al. reported SLE in 21% of cases [3]. Similarly, 24.1% of patients in our cohort had coexistent SLE. Although patients with SLE had a shorter time to diagnosis than those without SLE, this difference did not reach statistical significance.

LEP can cause various clinical features, ranging from painful indurated plaques and nodules with overlying erythematous skin (the most common manifestation) to ulceration, atrophy, and features of DLE [3]. Skin ulceration is observed in 28% of all LEP patients, as reported by Martens et al. [9]. Lipoatrophy and ulceration occurred in a subset of patients, consistent with previous reports. Importantly, patients with lipoatrophy experienced significantly longer times to diagnosis, suggesting that prolonged disease before diagnosis may contribute to irreversible tissue damage.

The most common sites of involvement are the proximal extremities, head, upper trunk, breasts, and buttocks [5]. Consistent with previous studies, plaques and nodules involving the proximal extremities, head, and trunk represented the predominant clinical presentation.

In contrast to the findings of a case series involving 74 LEP patients [15], which suggested an association between male gender, history of DLE, and lower limb involvement with the presence of SLE, our data did not reveal such a correlation between the presence of SLE and gender, DLE history, or lower limb involvement. These contrasting results may be due to variations in sample size, demographics, or methodology between the two studies. Further research with larger sample sizes and diverse populations is needed to explore other potential risk factors for the development of SLE in these patients.

Although histopathological examination plays an important role in the diagnosis of LEP, there is controversy about the definitive histopathological findings of this disease. LEP is known as mostly lobular panniculitis [16]; however, according to Black [17], LEP is a mixed panniculitis (both septal and lobular). Our histopathological results were more consistent with the mixed panniculitis pattern, as lobular panniculitis and septal panniculitis were observed in 63% and 51.9% of cases, respectively.

Histopathologic features of DLE can be present with or without clinical symptoms of DLE [11, 18]. A series of 12 LEP cases revealed DLE epidermal changes in 67%, with only 33% of them having clinical DLE lesions [1]. In this study, 68.5% of patients had DLE histologic changes, including interface dermatitis, epidermal atrophy, hyperkeratosis, follicular plugging, basement membrane thickening, and perivascular inflammation, while 22.2% of cases showed cutaneous findings of DLE. No association was observed between SLE history and DLE histopathological signs.

Infiltration of immune cells in the subcutaneous fat is mostly lymphocytic, but plasma cells, histiocytes, neutrophils, and eosinophils can also be seen [19, 20]. Although eosinophils are not a particularly notable finding in LEP, they may aid in diagnosis since they are often seen in LEP and not in other forms of cutaneous lupus erythematosus [5, 19]. Eosinophil infiltration was detected in 11.1% of our patients.

The presence of lymphoid follicles in the subcutaneous fat is a hallmark of LEP and can be found in 45%–78% of cases, but since they may also be present in instances of deep morphea, dermatomyositis panniculitis, erythema nodosum, and erythema induratum of Bazin, they are not pathognomonic [2, 19, 21]. We observed lymphoid follicle formation in the subcutaneous tissue with a germinal center in only 11.1% of our cases, which is lower in comparison with previous studies. Other characteristic findings that support the diagnosis are interface dermatitis, mucin deposition, hyaline fat necrosis, and lymphocytic nuclear dust.

Our findings reinforce previous reports that lymphocytic vasculitis (LV), DLE‐associated epidermal changes, and mixed panniculitis represent the predominant histopathologic hallmarks of LEP. Interestingly, lymphocytic vasculitis was observed more frequently in our cohort than in several previous series, where LV had been considered a characteristic and constant feature of LEP by many authors [1, 5]. Ng Pei Lin and colleagues [1] reported lymphocytic vasculitis in 33% of their cases.

ANA was detected in 24.1% of our cases, which is similar to the findings reported by Ng Pei Lin et al. [1] (27% of 11 patients). Anti‐dsDNA antibodies were positive in 16.6% of our cases as well. The reported proportion exceeds the 5% described by Parodi et al. [20]; however, it aligns with the findings given by Rangel et al. [3], who revealed a proportion of 25%. A correlation was observed between positive ANA, anti‐dsDNA antibodies, and the presence of SLE, suggesting that ANA positivity is more reflective of concomitant systemic autoimmunity than LEP itself. The percentage of positive DIF was 72.2% in our series, which is comparable to the 70% rate reported by Sanchez et al. [18].

Most often, the treatment starts with a combination of potent topical steroids and a systemic treatment. Antimalarials such as hydroxychloroquine or chloroquine are preferred first‐line treatments [4, 5]. Oral corticosteroids are helpful in severe cases by regulating inflammation and alleviating symptoms [4, 21]. Recent retrospective cohort data have similarly supported antimalarial‐based regimens as the cornerstone of LEP management while highlighting the need for standardized treatment algorithms and long‐term outcome studies [8].

When first‐line therapy is not effective, thalidomide, mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine, cyclosporine, dapsone, intravenous immunoglobulins, rituximab, or methotrexate can be used as alternative treatments; however, the outcomes might vary [4]. Lesions may leave scars, calcification, or atrophy after healing. Some authors recommend cosmetic procedures like dermal fillers, surgical removal, or fat grafts for the improvement of disfigurement [5, 22], but these should be considered cautiously because trauma, including injection, appears to be a risk factor in the development or worsening of lupus lesions [17].

Hydroxychloroquine was the cornerstone of treatment in our cohort and was most commonly used in combination with corticosteroids, with or without mycophenolate mofetil. Overall, most patients experienced clinical improvement, and the highest improvement rates were observed among patients receiving combination therapy. Although intralesional corticosteroid injections appeared less effective, treatment comparisons should be interpreted cautiously, given the retrospective design and heterogeneous treatment regimens. This aligns with a review by Fraga and García‐Díez, who noted that intralesional corticosteroids are typically not effective and may worsen the atrophy [5]. Overall, favorable clinical outcomes were observed in most patients. Despite the fact that older age is linked to a higher treatment response in SLE patients [23], the factors influencing treatment response in LEP patients have not yet been investigated extensively. There was no discernible correlation between clinical improvement and age, gender, or a history of SLE or DLE in our study.

Subcutaneous panniculitis‐like T‐cell lymphoma (SPTCL) is one of the most challenging differential diagnoses of LEP. SPTCL and LEP resemble each other clinically, as they both can present with plaques and nodules on the proximal extremities, trunk, and buttocks [22]. In 20% of cases, SPTCL is associated with another autoimmune disease, such as SLE [24]. Adipocyte rimming, in which abnormal lymphocytes encircle adipocytes, monoclonality in the T cell receptor's gene rearrangement, and atypical lymphocytes are mostly observed in SPTCL, while vacuolar interface dermatitis and lymphoid follicles with germinal centers favor LEP, whereas all these features could be found in both diseases [2, 22, 24, 25]. Recent clinicopathologic studies have further emphasized the substantial overlap between LEP and panniculitic T‐cell lymphoproliferative disorders, suggesting that some cases may exist along a biologic continuum rather than representing entirely distinct entities [22, 26]. Magro et al. [22] proposed the idea of “subcuticular T‐cell lymphoid dyscrasia,” to refer to atypical instances of LEP, SPTCL, and indeterminate lymphocytic lobular panniculitis. Close follow‐up, multiple biopsies, immunohistochemistry tests, and analysis of T‐cell receptor gene rearrangement can help with a precise diagnosis in suspicious or resistant treatment cases [2, 25].

This study has several strengths, including one of the larger reported cohorts of biopsy‐confirmed LEP patients and a comprehensive clinicopathologic evaluation incorporating clinical, serologic, immunopathologic, and histologic data. Importantly, we identified a significant association between delayed diagnosis and lipoatrophy, suggesting that reducing diagnostic delay may help decrease irreversible tissue damage. Nevertheless, our findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations, including the retrospective single‐center design, potential referral bias, heterogeneous treatment regimens, and limited long‐term follow‐up. Future prospective multicenter studies incorporating longitudinal follow‐up, standardized outcome measures, and molecular or immunologic profiling may help identify biomarkers of disease activity, improve diagnostic accuracy, and facilitate the development of more targeted therapeutic approaches for LEP.

Conclusion

LEP poses unique challenges in terms of diagnosis and management due to its diverse and non‐specific clinical presentations and potential for disfigurement. It has several differential diagnoses, ranging from other connective tissue diseases to malignancies. For an accurate diagnosis, clinical, immunofluorescence, and histopathologic features should all be taken into consideration. Longer time to diagnosis was associated with an increased risk of lipoatrophy and may contribute to long‐term scarring and cosmetic disfigurement. Although patients without associated SLE or DLE tended to have longer times to diagnosis, these associations were not statistically significant in our cohort.

Expanding our understanding of this disease helps with easier diagnosis and developing more targeted and personalized treatments. Our results underscore the importance of early recognition and provide new insights regarding treatment. The combination of hydroxychloroquine with corticosteroids and mycophenolate mofetil was associated with clinical improvement in this cohort. Further research, particularly prospective studies with larger patient cohorts, is needed to better understand the disease and refine treatment strategies.
