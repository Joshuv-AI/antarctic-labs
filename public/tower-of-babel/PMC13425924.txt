Detection of rheumatoid arthritis-associated interstitial lung disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shangwen Qi, Yutong Jiang, Huan Li, Xiaohong Gong, Qin Li, Xueqin Zhou, Songwei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRheumatoid arthritis–associated interstitial lung disease (RA-ILD) often has an insidious onset with few or no respiratory symptoms, so early disease may be overlooked. Timely diagnosis and monitoring are therefore crucial. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) is the reference standard for RA-ILD, but cost and radiation limit its use as a routine screening tool. Several lower-cost modalities—such as serum biomarkers, machine learning models, and lung ultrasound (LUS)—have been investigated, but their diagnostic value has not been systematically appraised.ObjectiveTo evaluate the accuracy of serum biomarkers, LUS, and biomarker-based prediction models, most of which were based on logistic regression, for early RA-ILD diagnosis, and to explore their roles as adjunctive screening tools in HRCT-based diagnosis.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Embase and Web of Science for studies assessing the diagnostic accuracy of serum biomarkers, machine learning models, or LUS in RA-ILD. Risk of bias was assessed using QUADAS-2. A bivariate mixed-effects model was used to pool sensitivity, specificity and construct summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curves.ResultsTwenty-six studies involving 4,544 participants were included. Among individual biomarkers, KL-6 showed pooled sensitivity and specificity of 0.82 (95% CI 0.68–0.91) and 0.82 (95% CI 0.71–0.89), respectively. LUS showed pooled sensitivity and specificity of 0.96 (95% CI 0.72–0.99) and 0.97 (95% CI 0.66–1.00), respectively. Biomarker-based prediction models, most of which were based on logistic regression, showed pooled AUCs of 0.837 (95% CI 0.795–0.880) in training cohorts and 0.815 (95% CI 0.759–0.871) in validation cohorts. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously due to variations in study design, diagnostic thresholds, reference standards, and risk of bias, and a lack of sufficient external validation for several prediction models.ConclusionsSerum biomarkers, biomarker-based prediction models, and LUS may provide useful auxiliary information for early RA-ILD detection. KL-6 demonstrated the most consistent diagnostic performance among individual biomarkers. The high pooled estimates for LUS should be interpreted cautiously because they were derived from a limited number of studies with heterogeneity. At present, these tools should be regarded as adjunctive tools for risk stratification and triage rather than replacements for HRCT. They may help identify patients with RA who are more likely to require confirmatory HRCT and closer follow-up.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04417-6.Keywords: Rheumatoid arthritis, Interstitial lung disease, Lung ultrasound, Biomarkers, Meta-analysis, Diagnostic accuracy

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis-associated interstitial lung disease (RA-ILD) is one of the most frequent and severe extra-articular complications of RA. RA has a global prevalence of approximately 1% [1], and ILD has been reported in 11.0%−20.6% of patients with RA [2, 3]. Due to an insidious onset and few specific symptoms at an early stage, RA-ILD is often prone to missed diagnosis in routine clinical practice. Once progressive fibrosis develops, prognosis worsens substantially, making RA-ILD an important cause of death in patients with RA [4–6]. Therefore, earlier detection of RA-ILD is important for prompt evaluation and subsequent management.

High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) is currently the main diagnostic means for RA-ILD and is widely regarded as the reference imaging tool for ILD [7–9]. It plays a central role in identifying interstitial abnormalities, characterizing fibrotic imaging patterns, and supporting updated radiologic classification. It can also help exclude other pulmonary conditions, such as infection, drug-induced lung toxicity, and malignancy [8]. Usual interstitial pneumonia and nonspecific interstitial pneumonia are common imaging patterns, while lung biopsy may be required in selected atypical cases. However, interpretation of atypical imaging presentations may vary [10], subtle or early abnormalities may still require multidisciplinary contextual interpretation, HRCT is relatively costly, and repeated imaging raises concerns about radiation exposure [9]. As a result, HRCT is not ideal for routine screening in all patients with RA. Moreover, invasive pathological confirmation also carries a risk of tissue injury. These limitations have increased interest in lower-cost and less invasive tools that may assist with earlier detection of RA-ILD.

Within this context, different auxiliary approaches have been explored, with different roles in clinical practice. Serum biomarkers may be useful as screening or adjunctive diagnostic tools because they are relatively accessible and may reflect alveolar epithelial injury, inflammation, or fibrotic activity. A previous systematic review summarized biomarkers that may help predict RA-ILD, but there were few available studies for meta-analysis. Among serum biomarkers, MUC5B mutations showed potential predictive value. However, the generalizability of these findings remains uncertain because of regional and ethnic variations [11–13]. In addition, several serum biomarkers have shown promise in RA-ILD diagnosis. For example, serum KL-6 [14] and sCD25 [15] are elevated in patients with RA-ILD, suggesting that individual biomarkers may have value in disease screening and assessment. More recently, researchers have attempted to combine clinical variables and laboratory indicators into prediction models to improve diagnostic efficiency. In addition to traditional logistic regression, machine learning methods have also been explored for the early identification and differential diagnosis of RA-ILD in recent studies. However, the current evidence remains heterogeneous and still requires further validation [16–18].

Lung ultrasound (LUS) has also been investigated as a potential screening tool for RA-ILD because of its accessibility, non-invasiveness, and ease of use [19]. Abnormal B-lines, subpleural changes, and pleural line abnormalities may help identify interstitial lung involvement. In some clinical settings, especially where HRCT is not readily available, biomarkers or LUS may provide useful supplementary information for selecting patients who require further imaging evaluation. A normal result of LUS below a defined cutoff may also help reduce unnecessary HRCT and the resulting radiation exposure [20]. However, these methods should be regarded as possible complements to HRCT rather than direct replacements.

Although the value of biomarkers, prediction models, and LUS in RA-ILD has all been studied, systematic evidence on their diagnostic performance remains limited. In particular, these methods are often discussed together, but their value should be interpreted within a unified early-detection pathway, with biomarkers and LUS mainly used as adjunctive tools and prediction models used to improve risk discrimination. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the accuracy of serum biomarkers, biomarker-based prediction models, and LUS for RA-ILD diagnosis, and to compare their potential advantages in supporting earlier detection.

This study was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) [21]. The protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD42025641298).

Data sources and search strategy

A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library from their inception to December 1, 2024. The search strategy combined subject headings and free-text terms related to RA, ILD, biomarkers, LUS, prediction models, and machine learning. No restrictions were applied regarding country or year of publication. Only English-language studies were included. The full search strategies for all databases, including search strings and filters, are provided in Table S1.

Study selection and data extraction

All retrieved records were imported into EndNote for duplicate removal. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible studies. Disagreements were resolved by consultation with a third reviewer.

Two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized spreadsheet. The extracted information included first author, year of publication, country, study design, patient source, sample size, number of RA-ILD cases, age, sex, index test category, detailed test method, diagnostic threshold or cut-off value when available, reference standard used for RA-ILD diagnosis, and measures of diagnostic performance. For prediction model studies, results from training and validation cohorts were extracted separately when reported. Because the reference standard for RA-ILD varied across the included studies, the diagnostic definition used in each study was recorded in detail during data extraction, including HRCT alone, HRCT combined with clinical findings or biomarkers, and classification-based approaches.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies were assessed independently by two reviewers. This review focused on diagnostic accuracy, so the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) was used as the primary assessment tool [22]. QUADAS-2 covers four domains (patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing), and also assesses concerns regarding applicability in the first three domains. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. In addition, an NOS-based summary of the observational study characteristics was retained in the Supplementary Materials for descriptive reference [23].

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

For studies conducting single diagnostic tests, meta-analyses were performed on sensitivity and specificity using a bivariate mixed-effects model based on diagnostic 2 × 2 tables. Pooled sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratio, negative likelihood ratio, diagnostic odds ratio, and area under the summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curve with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.

When diagnostic 2 × 2 tables were not directly reported, they were reconstructed using the available data on diagnostic accuracy whenever possible. The number of RA-ILD and non-RA-ILD participants was combined with the reported sensitivity and specificity to estimate true positives, false negatives, true negatives, and false positives. If diagnostic metrics (e.g., sensitivity, specificity, precision, accuracy, Youden index) were insufficient, reconstruction could be performed only when sufficient information was available [24]. Studies without sufficient information for reconstruction were excluded from the quantitative synthesis. As reconstructed 2 × 2 tables may introduce rounding error and additional uncertainty, the resulting pooled estimates should be interpreted cautiously.

Serum biomarkers, LUS, and biomarker-based prediction models were included because they have all been proposed as lower-cost or less invasive tools for earlier RA-ILD identification, risk stratification, or triage before confirmatory HRCT. However, they belong to different diagnostic classes and were therefore synthesized separately. Biomarker-based prediction models were analyzed separately from serum biomarkers and LUS. Logistic regression-based models were described as prediction models rather than being equated with modern machine learning. No overall pooled estimate was calculated across these fundamentally different approaches.

For biomarkers pooled from more than four studies, potential threshold effects were explored using Spearman correlation analyses and SROC curve analyses. Due to the limited number of studies in most pooled analyses and the substantial variations in diagnostic thresholds, reference standards, and study design, formal meta-regression and publication bias analyses were not performed. We also conducted sensitivity analyses only for studies using HRCT alone as the reference standard or for studies at lower risk of bias. However, formal meta-analyses of sensitivity were not performed because the number of eligible studies within several diagnostic categories became too small after restriction, particularly for LUS and prediction models. This would have produced statistically unstable estimates. Instead, heterogeneity of reference standards and risk of bias were explicitly described and incorporated into the interpretation of the results. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata 16.0.

Results

Search results

Initially, 7199 studies were retrieved. After 1503 duplicate publications were eliminated, the title or abstract of the remainder was read to exclude non-relevant studies (n = 5651). Then the full text was examined, and non-eligible studies were further excluded (n = 9 as conference abstracts, n = 10 due to a lack of outcome metrics). Finally, 26 studies [11, 20, 25–48] were included (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flowchart

Study characteristics

All the included studies, with 4544 cases including 2083 RA-ILD cases, were published in 2011–2024, and they were conducted in ten countries: China (n = 13), USA (n = 3), and Argentina (n = 3). They were case–control studies (n = 11) and cohort studies (n = 15). The data were sourced from a single center in 17 studies and multiple centers in the others. Biomarkers were reported in 13 studies, biomarker-based models in nine studies, and LUS in four studies (Table S2). The reference standards used for RA-ILD diagnosis included HRCT alone, HRCT combined with clinical findings or biomarkers, and classification-based approaches, but they were heterogeneous across studies. This heterogeneity should be considered when interpreting the pooled diagnostic estimates.

Quality assessment results

The QUADAS-2 tool showed moderate overall methodological quality across the 26 included studies, with the bias mainly arising from patient selection and index test. For the patient selection, three studies were rated as low risk and 13 as high risk, mainly because of insufficient reporting of consecutive or random enrollment and inappropriate exclusions. For the index test, 20 studies were judged to be at high risk, largely because diagnostic thresholds were determined post hoc from ROC analyses rather than being pre-specified. For the reference standard, 10 studies were rated as low risk, three as high risk, and 10 as unclear because of insufficient reporting of blinding. For the flow and timing, five studies were rated as low risk and 10 as high risk, mainly owing to unclear intervals between the index test and the reference standard and exclusion of patients from the final analysis. To sum up, the pooled diagnostic estimates should be interpreted cautiously (Fig. 2, 3, Table 1).

Meta-analyses

Diagnostic accuracy of biomarkers for RA-ILD

The diagnostic accuracy of 35 biomarkers for RA-ILD was reported. Meta-analyses revealed that for RA-ILD diagnosis, KL-6 had a C-index of 0.843 (95% CI 0.776–0.910), a sensitivity of 0.82 (95% CI 0.68–0.91), and a specificity of 0.82 (95% CI 0.71–0.89) (Fig. 4). The Spearman correlation coefficient was −0.20, suggesting that no substantial threshold effect was present and threshold-related heterogeneity was likely limited. sCD25 had a C-index of 0.726 (95% CI 0.663–0.789), a sensitivity of 0.09–0.89, and a specificity of 0.47–0.77. Diagnostic accuracy of biomarkers for RA-ILD

Logistic regression (LR)

The included studies mainly involved LR models established based on multiple biomarkers. In the training set, LR had an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.837 (95% CI 0.795–0.880) for its diagnostic accuracy for RA-ILD, with specificity and sensitivity of 0.86 (95% CI 0.81–0.91) and 0.79 (95% CI 0.75–0.83) (Fig. 5). The Spearman correlation coefficient was 0.10, suggesting that any threshold effect was minimal and may account for only a very small proportion of the observed heterogeneity. In the validation set, LR had an AUC of 0.815 (95% CI 0.759–0.871), with specificity and sensitivity of 0.621–0.852 and 0.731–0.914. Most of the prediction models were based on logistic regression and were developed in limited datasets, with only a few undergoing external validation. Therefore, these pooled estimates should be regarded as exploratory rather than definitive evidence of model performance. Further validation in independent, multicenter cohorts is needed for these models before being recommended for routine clinical use.

LUS

Several studies have explored LUS for RA-ILD diagnosis. Dina S. Fotoh [39] calculated the number of ultrasound B-lines in 14 intercostal spaces to assess the extent and severity of pulmonary fibrosis. Specifically, a segment was considered positive for ILD in the case of ≥ three B-lines in a single segment or > five B-lines in adjacent spaces, and 10 B-lines were considered all-white patterns. Moreover, a new scoring system, the Pulmonary Very Short index, based on the specific intercostal spaces in most relation to HRCT was adopted by Natalia Mena-Vázquez [49], and it exhibited a high specificity for patients with < five B-lines detected, effectively ruling out ILD and avoiding the need for HRCT. M. Di Carlo [43] utilized a simplified scoring method involving 14 intercostal spaces to assess the presence of ultrasound B-lines and recorded the quantitative cutoffs of nine B-lines, which displayed a sensitivity of 70% and a specificity of 97% for significant RA-ILD detected by thoracic HRCT. R. Tanten Zabaleta [20] also incorporated pleural irregularity (PI) in assessing B-lines in 14 intercostal spaces and found that the optimal cutoffs for ILD detection are eight B-lines and seven PIs. By evaluating B-lines, PIs, and A-line deletion in 14 thoracic regions, Maria Otaola [48] identified the positive criterion for ILD as a total B-line score ≥ five in LUS.

Meta-analyses revealed that for RA-ILD diagnosis, LUS had a C-index of 0.839 (95% CI 0.800–0.877), a sensitivity of 0.96 (95% CI 0.72–0.99), and a specificity of 0.97 (95% CI 0.66–1.00) (Fig. 6). The Spearman correlation coefficient was 0.54, suggesting that a possible threshold effect was present and threshold-related heterogeneity may have contributed to the pooled estimates. However, these estimates were derived from a small number of studies with different scoring systems, cutoffs, and reference standards, and most studies were observational or single-center in design. Therefore, the pooled estimates for LUS should be regarded as exploratory evidence. Although LUS appears to be a potential screening or triage tool in selected settings, the current evidence remains insufficient to support its use as a replacement for HRCT.

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the value of biomarkers alone, biomarker-based prediction models, and LUS in RA-ILD diagnosis. Among individual biomarkers, KL-6 showed relatively stable diagnostic performance, with pooled specificity and sensitivity of 0.82 (95% CI 0.71–0.89) and 0.82 (95% CI 0.68–0.91), respectively. However, the evidence for sCD25 remained limited, with a specificity of 0.475–0.773 and a sensitivity of 0.0917–0.889. Biomarker-based prediction models, most of which were based on logistic regression, showed moderate diagnostic performance, with a sensitivity of 0.731–0.914 and a specificity of 0.621–0.852 in the validation sets. LUS had pooled specificity and sensitivity of 0.97 (95% CI 0.66–1.00) and 0.96 (95% CI 0.72–0.99), respectively. Overall, the available evidence suggests that several lower-cost or less invasive tools may have potential value in supporting early RA-ILD identification. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the included studies differed substantially in diagnostic thresholds, reference standards, study design, and risk of bias. Therefore, the results should be regarded as supportive and exploratory rather than definitive evidence for their use as replacements for HRCT. However, the evidence for most biomarkers and prediction models remains limited, and the apparently stronger diagnostic performance of LUS should be interpreted cautiously since it was derived from a small number of heterogeneous studies.

RA-ILD-related markers have been reviewed previously. A systematic review (Guo) found great differences in biomarkers between the disease and non-disease populations [12]. However, the real value of these markers in RA-ILD diagnosis has not been fully explored, and only their differences have been analyzed without considering whether they are independent factors. Wang [4] probed into risk factors for RA-ILD with a large number of included studies but did not discuss the predictive effect of these factors on RA-ILD. In contrast, this study further reviewed the predictive value of these factors to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of their diagnostic value.

Among the evaluated biomarkers, KL-6 showed the most consistent diagnostic signal, and KL-6 and sCD25 represented the two biomarkers with the most promising potential for identifying RA-ILD in this study. Previous studies have supported KL-6 as a serum marker for RA-ILD [33]. As a mucin-like glycoprotein that is strongly expressed on type II pneumocytes, KL-6 reflects alveolar epithelial injury and fibrotic activity and has been reported to exert pro-fibrotic and anti-apoptotic effects on lung fibroblasts. A retrospective study [14] suggested that KL-6 may serve not only as a diagnostic marker for RA-ILD but also as an indicator of disease severity and prognosis. In patients with ILD, serum KL-6 levels increase and are positively correlated with CT grade but negatively correlated with forced vital capacity and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide [50]. Elevated KL-6 levels have also been associated with short-term progression and survival in RA-associated usual interstitial pneumonia [51]. These findings verified the view that higher serum KL-6 levels may reflect worsening lung involvement and impaired pulmonary function. In this study, KL-6 showed balanced pooled sensitivity and specificity, suggesting that it may be useful as an adjunctive tool for RA-ILD screening or risk stratification. In addition, a serum KL-6 cutoff of 277.5 U/mL has been reported to be able to distinguish ILD from non-ILD [39].

sCD25, also known as the soluble interleukin-2 receptor alpha chain, is generated by protein hydrolysis and is mainly derived from the cell membrane of activated T cells [52]. Previous studies have shown that serum sCD25 concentrations are higher in RA-ILD patients with consolidation on HRCT, whereas no significant elevation has been observed in relation to other HRCT features. This further suggests an association between sCD25 and RA-ILD [15]. In this study, however, the diagnostic performance of sCD25 was less robust, and the observed ranges of sensitivity and specificity were wide. This variability may reflect differences in patient selection, disease stage, assay methods, HRCT manifestations, and threshold determination across studies. Therefore, although KL-6 and sCD25 appear promising and support the potential value of serum biomarkers alone in RA-ILD screening and assessment [53], they cannot yet be considered standalone diagnostic tools, and their clinical value still requires further validation in larger and more standardized studies.

In addition to their potential role in early identification, some biomarkers may also have value in disease monitoring. Research has suggested the association of KL-6 with not only RA-ILD but also CT extent, pulmonary function impairment, and short-term outcome. This indicates that serial measurement may help reflect changes in disease activity or progression [14, 50, 51]. Similarly, the reported correlation between LUS B-lines and serum KL-6 raises the possibility that these tools may complement each other during follow-up, particularly when repeated HRCT is undesirable [39, 54, 55]. By contrast, the evidence for sCD25 in monitoring remains limited, and its current value appears to be more supportive than established. Overall, biomarkers and LUS may have potential utility beyond screening, including longitudinal assessment and treatment monitoring, but this remains insufficiently validated and should be confirmed in prospective studies with repeated measurements.

Although biomarkers alone showed potential diagnostic value, biomarker-based prediction models may further improve discrimination by integrating multiple variables. In this study, prediction models showed moderate diagnostic performance, but the relevant evidence was driven mainly by logistic regression-based models, including those based on serum biomarkers, clinical variables, or combined indicators [29, 35, 38, 42, 46, 47]. Logistic regression has good interpretability and remains useful for clinical prediction, but it should be distinguished from broader machine learning approaches. Recent studies have explored machine learning classifiers and multi-biomarker machine learning approaches for RA-ILD identification, but evidence from these methods remains limited [16–18, 56]. In addition, many included models were developed in single-center or limited datasets, and external validation was insufficient [35, 38, 42, 46, 47]. Therefore, the current results should be interpreted as preliminary evidence for model-based risk stratification rather than as confirmation of ready-to-use diagnostic models.

LUS is of particular interest because it is simple, non-invasive, radiation-free, and potentially suitable for screening or triage in selected clinical settings. Since HRCT is relatively costly and not ideal for routine use in all patients with RA, LUS may serve as a practical supplement to the early identification of interstitial lung involvement, especially when repeated radiation exposure is undesirable [20]. Previous studies have shown that B-lines, pleural irregularities, and related simplified scoring systems may help identify early signs of RA-ILD [20, 43, 48]. In clinical practice, LUS can be performed at 14 thoracic sites, including bilateral anterior and posterior chest regions, which has been reported to improve feasibility and shorten examination time while maintaining satisfactory sensitivity [48]. M. Di Carlo [43] reported that a cutoff of nine B-lines in HRCT yielded a sensitivity of 70% and a specificity of 97% for severe RA-ILD, supporting the value of B-line quantification in improving the interpretability of LUS findings. R. Tanten Zabaleta [20] further incorporated pleural irregularities into LUS assessment and found that cutoff values of eight B-lines and seven pleural irregularities were associated with ILD, with a sensitivity above 80% and a specificity close to 80%. These findings suggest that both B-lines and pleural abnormalities may contribute to the diagnostic performance of LUS. However, the included studies used different scoring systems, cutoffs, and study populations, and most were single-center observational studies. In several studies, ultrasound thresholds were derived post hoc on the basis of ROC analyses, which may have inflated the reported diagnostic accuracy. Moreover, the apparently high pooled sensitivity and specificity may partly reflect spectrum bias and selective reporting, and missed diagnosis may still occur in some RA-ILD patients [43]. In addition, the small number of included studies on LUS restricted further subgroup or sensitivity analyses. Therefore, the high pooled sensitivity and specificity of LUS should be regarded as exploratory evidence. LUS may be useful for screening or triage in selected clinical settings, but it cannot currently replace HRCT for RA-ILD diagnosis.

From a clinical implementation perspective, the value of these tools lies not only in diagnostic discrimination but also in their potential contribution to more efficient screening. HRCT remains the reference imaging modality for RA-ILD, but its use as a routine screening test for all patients with RA is restricted by cost, access, and radiation exposure. In contrast, serum biomarkers are relatively accessible and, in some settings, may already be part of routine laboratory assessment, while LUS is radiation-free and can be performed at the bedside. These methods may therefore help identify patients who warrant confirmatory HRCT or closer surveillance, potentially reducing unnecessary imaging and improving the allocation of diagnostic resources. A pragmatic clinical pathway may involve first-line risk stratification using accessible serum biomarkers and/or bedside LUS, followed by HRCT in patients with abnormal findings or persistent clinical suspicion. However, a formal cost-effectiveness analysis was not performed in this study, and the practical value of such screening pathways still requires prospective evaluation in real-world settings.

Furthermore, the combination of serum KL-6 and radiation-free LUS remains clinically interesting as a potential auxiliary strategy for RA-ILD assessment. In patients with RA-ILD, LUS B-lines have been reported to correlate more strongly with serum KL-6 than with HRCT findings. The combination of LUS B-lines and serum KL-6 may therefore help identify early pulmonary changes in RA-ILD, such as early alveolitis [57], and may also contribute to severity assessment and prognostic evaluation [54, 58]. However, the current evidence remains limited and does not support the replacement of this combination for thoracic HRCT. Future studies should therefore focus on several aspects. Large prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate the diagnostic performance of KL-6, LUS, and other promising biomarkers in more representative RA populations. Greater consistency in reference standards, threshold reporting, and study design will also improve comparability across studies. For prediction models, future work should move beyond conventional logistic regression and compare multiple modern machine learning approaches in terms of discrimination, calibration, interpretability, and clinical feasibility. Models integrating biomarkers, LUS parameters, and key clinical variables may be particularly valuable for developing more robust auxiliary diagnostic strategies for RA-ILD.

Advantages and limitations

This study provides, to our knowledge, the first systematic comparison of the value of biomarkers alone, biomarker-based prediction models, and LUS in RA-ILD diagnosis, offering a structured overview of potential auxiliary diagnostic strategies. The review was prospectively registered, and study quality was assessed using QUADAS-2, allowing a more appropriate evaluation of risk of bias in diagnostic accuracy research. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, substantial heterogeneity was present across the included studies in patient selection, diagnostic thresholds, reference standards (HRCT alone, HRCT combined with clinical findings or biomarkers, and classification-based approaches), index tests, and statistical methods, which may have affected the comparability of diagnostic accuracy estimates. Second, many studies were observational, retrospective, or single-center studies, and some were case–control studies. Therefore, selection bias and spectrum bias may have been introduced, restricting the generalizability of the findings to broader RA populations. Third, QUADAS-2 assessment showed high or unclear risk of bias in several domains, especially patient selection, index test, and flow and timing. In particular, several diagnostic thresholds were determined post hoc using ROC analyses rather than being prespecified, which may have inflated diagnostic accuracy. Fourth, the evidence for several metrics was limited by the small number of available studies. In particular, the pooled estimates for LUS were based on only a few studies with different scoring systems and cutoffs. Therefore, the high pooled sensitivity and specificity of LUS should be interpreted cautiously. Fifth, most biomarker-based prediction models were based on logistic regression and lacked external validation. These models should not be equated with broader machine learning approaches, and their clinical applicability remains uncertain until they are validated in independent multicenter cohorts. Sixth, a considerable number of the included studies were conducted in Chinese populations. This may have introduced regional or population-related bias, restricting the generalizability of the findings across ethnic, geographic, or healthcare settings.

Therefore, the findings of this review that were generated by the hypothesis should be considered exploratory. Serum biomarkers, biomarker-based prediction models, and LUS may help support early identification, risk stratification, or triage of RA-ILD, but they should still be used as complements to HRCT until further evidence is obtained from large prospective multicenter studies with standardized thresholds and reference standards.

Conclusion

Serum biomarkers, biomarker-based prediction models, and LUS may be used to support early RA-ILD identification, particularly as adjunctive tools for risk stratification and triage. Among individual biomarkers, KL-6 showed the most consistent diagnostic performance, whereas evidence for sCD25 and other biomarkers remained limited. Biomarker-based prediction models had moderate diagnostic value, but they were mostly based on logistic regression and lacked sufficient external validation. Although the pooled diagnostic estimates for LUS were high, they were based on a limited number of studies with heterogeneity and thus should be interpreted cautiously. Overall, these approaches should be regarded as adjunctive tools rather than replacements for HRCT, and larger prospective multicenter studies with standardized thresholds and reference standards, and external validation are still needed.
