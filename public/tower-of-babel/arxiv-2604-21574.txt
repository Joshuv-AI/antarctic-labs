Generalized stochastic spin-wave theory for open quantum spin systems
Zejian Li, Anna Delmonte, Rosario Fazio

Abstract
We propose a semiclassical framework for solving open quantum dynamics in driven-dissipative spin systems. Our method consists of generalized spin-wave approximations tailored to describing quantum trajectories unravelled from the master equation, and generically applies to regimes beyond the reach of conventional spin-wave theories, including short-range interactions and local quantum jumps, enabling the efficient simulation of large-scale interacting spins. We illustrate the versatility of our framework by studying a variable-range driven-dissipative Ising model on a 2D lattice. When the dissipation acts along the drive axis, we find a continuous phase transition breaking the ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} symmetry, and demonstrate that the interaction range, when tuned from fully-connected to nearest-neighbour, profoundly alters the universality class of the criticality. With the dissipation along the interaction axis, we show the emergence of a first-order transition. Demonstrated with both state-diffusion and quantum-jump types of trajectory dynamics, our framework provides a powerful toolbox for the efficient semiclassical description of non-equilibrium dynamics and many-body phases in spin systems.

Introduction

A central frontier in modern physics is the study of open quantum many-body systems

;
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
, where an exciting variety of out-of-equilibrium phases and critical phenomena occur due to the rich interplay between unitary quantum dynamics and the dissipative environment.
Along with their importance for quantum technologies

;

, the recent decades have witnessed impressive development in experimental platforms

;

;

;

;
Underwood et al. 2012
;
Safavi-Naini et al. 2018
;
Cole et al. 2021
;
Leghtas et al. 2015
. They offer a versatile playground for exploring nonequilibrium quantum dynamics

;
Pieczarka et al. 2020
;
Castellanos et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2022
;
Mei et al. 2022
, and, in turn, sparked intense theoretical activities
Sieberer et al. 2016
;
Eisert et al. 2015
;
Camacho et al. 2022
;
Vasseur and Moore 2016
;
Bernard and Doyon 2016
.

The investigation of open quantum many-body systems is, in general, a formidable challenge. Besides the rare cases of analytically solvable models, see e.g.

;
Paz and Maghrebi 2021
;
Bartolo et al. 2016
, one generally needs to resort to numerical simulations or approximate approaches. Approximation methods in particular, exploit prior knowledge on the physical system and reduce the dynamics to a much smaller effective manifold via clever design of the state representation.

In systems with long-range magnetic ordering, the

approximation proved to be useful in many different contexts

;

;
Van Kranendonk and Van Vleck 1958
, based on a semiclassical representation of spins via the Holstein-Primakoff transformation
Bloch 1932
;

. To the lowest order, this can be regarded as approximating the quantum fluctuations on top of a classical state as a set of harmonic oscillators. In equilibrium settings, the spin-wave theory has been highly successful in capturing the magnetic properties of a wide range of materials, showing good accordance with experimental data
Van Kranendonk and Van Vleck 1958
. It has also been extended to the time-dependent regime for both unitary and dissipative systems
Rückriegel et al. 2012
;
Lerose et al. 2019
;
Seetharam et al. 2022
to describe non-equilibrium dynamics

.

More recently, the spin-wave framework has been generalized by us to describe quantum trajectories in driven-dissipative long-range systems, which was formulated in the context of heterodyne monitoring of a collective decay channel
Li et al. 2025a
and shown to provide a sound approximation of the monitored dynamics. A distinct advantage comes from the stochastic nature of the approach: while the physical state
along the quantum trajectory
is approximated by a Gaussian ansatz
Verstraelen and Wouters 2018
;
Delmonte et al. 2026
, which captures quantum correlations on the quadratic level, the trajectory-averaged state, consisting of a mixture of Gaussians, provides a much more accurate representation of the Lindblad dynamics compared to deterministic semiclassical methods targeting directly the density matrix
Seetharam et al. 2022
, allowing us to capture highly non-Gaussian mixed states that are by construction lost in the latter.

In this work, we reformulate and further generalize the framework of spin-wave quantum trajectories (SWQT) introduced in
Li et al. 2025a
, making it a generic semiclassical approach to capturing the driven-dissipative spin dynamics, applicable to regimes including local quantum jumps and short-range interactions, where the conventional spin-wave approaches typically break down. This is achieved via generalized spin-wave approximations along quantum trajectories, featuring two essential new ingredients:

•

we bosonize each individual spin in its
local
comoving frame with a higher-order Holstein-Primakoff expansion, and the bosonic state is approximated with a Gaussian ansatz;

•

we introduce a non-singular parametrization of the comoving frames via
quaternions
, resolving the common pathology of spherical coordinate singularities affecting all time-dependent spin-wave theories
Rückriegel et al. 2012
;
Lerose et al. 2019
;
Seetharam et al. 2022
.

The resulting spin-wave dynamics can be elegantly cast in a stochastic differential equation, enabling efficient simulation of the dynamics, for both
state-diffusion
and
quantum-jump
types of monitoring. The method is carefully benchmarked, and we show that it is exact in the non-interacting limit, consistent with the semiclassical assumptions. To demonstrate the wide applicability of the framework, we study a two-dimensional (2D) transverse-field Ising model subjected to local dissipation, with a power-law interaction profile allowing the interaction range to be tuned from fully-connected to nearest-neighbor. When the dissipation acts along the drive axis, the steady state admits a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry-breaking phase transition, and we show that the interaction range profoundly alters the dissipative criticality in this system, with the critical exponent moving from the mean-field value to that of the 2D Ising universality class. We also consider a variant of the model with the dissipation acting along the interaction direction, and show the emergence of a first-order phase transition.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. In Sec.

V

II
Quantum trajectories in dissipative spin lattices

We consider a lattice of interacting spins, whose dissipative dynamics can be cast in the form of a Lindblad master equation

\displaystyle\dfrac{{\rm{d}}\hat{\rho}}{{\rm{d}}t}

\displaystyle=-{\rm{i}}[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}]+\sum_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{L}_{i}\hat{\rho}\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{i}-\dfrac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{L}_{i},\hat{\rho}}}\right\}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\hat{\rho}\,,

While the formalism of our spin-wave framework will not depend on the particular form of the model, we expect the theory to be best applicable to systems with “semiclassical” behavior, such as macroscopic collective ordering, as is the case in many long-range interacting systems. \displaystyle\mathrm{Model\penalty\ I:\penalty\ }\hat{H}^{z-xx}=h\sum_{i}\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{i}-\sum_{i\neq j}J^{(\alpha)}_{ij}\hat{\sigma}^{x}_{i}\hat{\sigma}^{x}_{j}\,,

The second model we consider has a modified Hamiltonian, with the interaction and driving axes interchanged,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Model\penalty\ II:\penalty\ }\hat{H}^{x-zz}=h\sum_{i}\hat{\sigma}^{x}_{i}+\sum_{i\neq j}J^{(\alpha)}_{ij}\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\hat{\sigma}^{z}_{j}\,,

(5)

As a key ingredient of our framework, we
unravel
the master equation (
1

This unraveling process corresponds to an alternative description of open quantum dynamics by modeling the dissipative environment as an observer that continuously monitors the system with weak measurements, resulting in stochastic dynamics of the system state. In this work, we will consider two typical unravelings usually considered in the quantum optics community, the

II.1
Heterodyne

The first unraveling we will consider in this work describes the measurement process of heterodyne detection, which is given by the following stochastic equation,

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}\hat{\rho}={\rm{d}}t\mathcal{L}\hat{\rho}+{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}\hat{\rho}\,,

(6)

where the second term encodes the stochastic noise arising from the monitoring,

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}\hat{\rho}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{i}\mathcal{H}[{\rm{d}}Z^{*}_{i}\hat{L}_{i}]\hat{\rho}

(7)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\rm{d}}Z_{i}^{*}(\hat{L}_{i}-\langle\hat{L}_{i}\rangle)\hat{\rho}+\mathrm{H.c.}}}\right]\,,

with
{\rm{d}}Z_{i}
the complex Wiener process defined via its second moment,

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}Z_{i}{\rm{d}}Z_{j}=0\,,\quad{\rm{d}}Z_{i}^{*}{\rm{d}}Z_{j}=\delta_{ij}{\rm{d}}t\,,

(8)

and
\langle\bullet\rangle\equiv\Tr[\hat{\rho}\bullet]
is the
single-trajectory
expectation value, whose dependence on the current state
\hat{\rho}
makes the dynamics nonlinear, accounting for the measurement back action.
The heterodyne dynamics is continuous in time and describes a quantum state diffusion process.

II.2
Quantum jump

We will also consider the quantum-jump unraveling, which models the experimental process of counting photons emitted from the system, whose dynamics can be cast in the stochastic master equation as follows,

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}\hat{\rho}

\displaystyle={\rm{d}}t\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{-{\rm{i}}[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}]-\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\mathcal{H}[\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{L}_{i}]\hat{\rho}}}\right\}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i}{\rm{d}}M_{i}(t)\mathcal{J}[\hat{L}_{i}]\hat{\rho}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}[\hat{A}]\hat{\rho}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{A}-\langle\hat{A}\rangle}}\right)\hat{\rho}+\hat{\rho}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{A}^{\dagger}-\langle\hat{A}^{\dagger}\rangle}}\right)\,,

(10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}[\hat{L}]\hat{\rho}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\hat{L}\hat{\rho}\hat{L}^{\dagger}}{\langle\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{L}\rangle}-\hat{\rho}\,,

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}M_{i}(t){\rm{d}}M_{j}(t)

\displaystyle={\rm{d}}M_{i}(t)\delta_{ij}\,,

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}[{\rm{d}}M_{i}(t)]

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{L}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{i}\rangle(t){\rm{d}}t\,.

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}

\displaystyle=\hat{H}-\dfrac{{\rm{i}}}{2}\sum_{i}\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{L}_{i}\,,

III
Generalized stochastic spin-wave theory along quantum trajectories

In this section, we formulate the generalized framework of spin-wave quantum trajectories (SWQT), a semiclassical approximation method tailored for describing driven-dissipative spin dynamics, which further extends the stochastic spin-wave theory developed in our previous work
Li et al. 2025a
.
In Sec.
III.1
, we first bosonize spin operators in
local
comoving frames for each individual spin, which, a priori, admit independent orientations, as graphically represented in Fig.
1
(b). This accommodates most generic spin systems with local spin damping and no longer relies on the assumption that the system state always exhibits a well-defined collective polarization along the trajectory dynamics.

Secondly, we present in Sec.
III.2
the quaternion formalism for parameterizing the comoving frames (in which the spins are bosonized), thereby resolving the common pathology of coordinate singularities shared by all previous time-dependent spin-wave theories. In a conventional spin-wave approach, one typically performs the bosonization of the spin operators in a comoving frame parametrized by angle coordinates of the Bloch sphere
(\theta,\phi)
with predefined local frame orientations, which inevitably results in coordinate singularities – a direct consequence of the
hairy ball theorem

Brouwer 1912
which states that one cannot have a nonvanishing continuous tangent vector field (in this case, the local frames are defined by tangent unit vectors) on a 2-sphere. In practice, this hinders the efficient solution of the dynamics if the spin polarization approaches a coordinate singularity. We completely remove this obstacle by introducing a non-singular parametrization of the local frames using quaternions and by parallelly transporting the comoving frame along the dynamics [Fig.
1
(c)]. The resulting spin-wave dynamics can then be formulated as a stochastic differential equation, allowing for efficient simulations of the quantum trajectories.

III.1
Local spin-wave approximations

Our semiclassical approach builds on the assumption that each individual spin in the system admits a well-defined classical magnetization direction (which is, for example, the case of spin coherent states) along the trajectory dynamics
2
2

2

Note that this condition can be more easily satisfied along trajectories than on the level of the average mixed state.
. This local spin orientation can be parametrized by the rotation operator
\hat{Q}_{i}\equiv{\rm{e}}^{-{\rm{i}}\theta_{i}{\bm{n}}_{i}\cdot\hat{\bm{s}}_{i}}
, for some generic rotation angle
\theta_{i}
and rotation axis
\bm{n}_{i}
, and
\bm{\hat{s}}_{i}=(\hat{s}^{x}_{i},\hat{s}^{y}_{i},\hat{s}^{z}_{i})
denotes the spin operators (in the lab frame
Oxyz
) on the site
i
, which satisfy the standard
\mathfrak{su}(2)
algebra
[\hat{s}_{i}^{\mu},\hat{s}^{\nu}_{j}]={\rm{i}}\delta_{ij}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda}\hat{s}^{\lambda}_{i}
. For spin
s=1/2
, they are related to the Pauli matrices via
\hat{s}_{i}^{\mu}=\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{\mu}/2
. This rotation allows us to define a
comoving frame

O{\tilde{x}}_{i}{\tilde{y}}_{i}{\tilde{z}}_{i}
[see Fig.
1
(b)] aligned with the spin orientation, in which the comoving spin operators are defined via the passive transformation
\hat{s}^{\tilde{\alpha}}_{i}=\hat{Q}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{s}_{i}^{\alpha}\hat{Q}_{i}=\sum_{\beta}Q^{i}_{{\tilde{\alpha}}\beta}\hat{s}_{i}^{\beta}
, where we denote the matrix representing the rotation
\hat{Q}_{i}
as
Q^{i}_{{\tilde{\alpha}}\beta}
when there is no ambiguity. For the moment, we leave the rotation
\hat{Q}_{i}
in its most general form, and only assume that it satisfies the alignment condition that
\langle\hat{s}_{i}^{\tilde{x}}\rangle=\langle\hat{s}_{i}^{\tilde{y}}\rangle=0
, i.e. the comoving
{\tilde{z}}
axis be parallel to the local magnetization vector
\langle\bm{\hat{s}}\rangle
. The comoving spin operators are then bosonized via the Holstein-Primakoff transformation,

\displaystyle\hat{s}_{i}^{\widetilde{+}}=\sqrt{2s}f({\hat{n}})\hat{b}_{i}\,,\penalty\ \hat{s}_{i}^{\widetilde{-}}=\sqrt{2s}\hat{b}^{\dagger}f({\hat{n}}_{i})\,,\penalty\ \hat{s}_{i}^{\tilde{z}}=s-\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{b}_{i}\,,

(14)

where
\hat{s}_{i}^{\widetilde{\pm}}\equiv\hat{s}_{i}^{\tilde{x}}\pm{\rm{i}}\hat{s}^{\tilde{y}}_{i}
,
\hat{b}_{i}
are bosonic operators satisfying
[\hat{b}_{i},\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{j}]=\delta_{ij}
,
{\hat{n}}_{i}\equiv\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{b}_{i}
, and the bosonic operator function is defined as

\displaystyle f({\hat{n}})=\sqrt{1-\dfrac{{\hat{n}}}{2s}}\,.

(15)

While this mapping is exact, one typically needs to expand the square root as a series and truncate it at a certain order to make practical use of this formalism. The lowest order yields
f_{0}=1
, which defines the regime of the standard linear spin-wave theory, valid for regimes where
\langle{\hat{n}}\rangle/2s\ll 1
. In this work, we will consider the next-to-leading-order truncation in a Newton series expansion

,

\displaystyle f({\hat{n}})\simeq 1-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\sqrt{1-\dfrac{1}{2s}}}}\right){\hat{n}}\,.

(16)

In the case of
s=1/2
, this mapping is exact and represents the hard-core boson limit,

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{\widetilde{+}}

\displaystyle=2(1-\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{b}_{i})\hat{b}_{i}\,,

(17)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{\widetilde{-}}

\displaystyle=2\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{i}(1-\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{b}_{i})\,,

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{\tilde{z}}

\displaystyle=1-2\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{b}_{i}\,.

The bosonic modes are then approximated with a Gaussian ansatz, whose validity requires our above construction of the comoving frame: despite the exact spin-to-boson mapping (
17
), the resulting bosonic states are, in general, highly non-classical, due to the fact that the local spin basis states
\{\ket{\uparrow}_{\tilde{z}},\ket{\downarrow}_{\tilde{z}}\}
are mapped to Fock states
\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}
. By aligning the
{\tilde{z}}
axis with the spin’s orientation, we effectively redefine the vacuum of the bosonic mode, such that the remaining fluctuations can be approximated on the quadratic level with the Gaussian ansatz. A control parameter of the approximation can then be naturally defined as

\displaystyle\epsilon\equiv\dfrac{1}{Ns}\sum_{i}\langle\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{b}_{i}\rangle=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\sqrt{\Sigma_{\tilde{\mu}}(\langle\hat{s}_{i}^{\tilde{\mu}}\rangle/s)^{2}}}}\right)\,,

(18)

which measures the deviation from the bosonic vacuum as witnessed by the average number of excitations, i.e., the
spin-wave density
,
and can be regarded as the average deficit in the length of the normalized spin magnetization vector.

This Gaussian approximation allows us to uniquely specify the state of the entire system with the following
O(N^{2})
variational parameters:

•

orientation of the comoving frames —
\hat{Q}_{i}
,

•

first moments —
\beta_{i}\equiv\langle\hat{b}_{i}\rangle
,

•

second moments —
u_{ij}\equiv\langle\hat{\delta}_{i}\hat{\delta}_{j}\rangle\,,v_{ij}\equiv\langle\hat{\delta}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{\delta}_{j}\rangle
,

where
\hat{\delta}_{i}\equiv\hat{b}_{i}-\beta_{i}
. Note that the construction of the comoving frame and the Gaussian approximation implies that
\beta_{i}=0
for a
static
state, yet this quantity plays a central role in determining the dynamics of the frame
\hat{Q}_{i}
, via the procedure we explain below.

After initializing the variational parameters such that the frames
\hat{Q}_{i}
are aligned with each spin (i.e.,
\beta_{i}=0
), our algorithm can be conceptually understood as a stroboscopic procedure repeating the two following operations in every discretized time step
\delta t
, until the desired final time
t
is reached:

1.

Calculate the infinitesimal increments for the Gaussian parameters
\delta\beta_{i}
,
\delta u_{ij}
, and
\delta v_{ij}
using the stochastic master equation, and update these quantities with the increments. Note that the frame
\hat{Q}_{i}
is kept constant within this step, which implies that the local
{\tilde{z}}_{i}
axes no longer align with the individual spins after the update as
\delta\beta_{i}\neq 0
in general.

2.

Update local frames
\hat{Q}_{i}
each along a
geodesic
rotation [see Fig.
1
(c)] such that
\beta_{i}=0
after the rotation. This can be achieved self-consistently by considering the evolution of the bosonic mode
\hat{b}_{i}
generated by the (passive) rotation of the frame alone. Finally, increase the time by
\delta t
and start a new iteration.

From the procedure described above, we note that the first moments
\beta_{i}
are dummy variables since they are always zero at the end of each iteration, and are used only to compute the dynamics of the frame parameters
Q_{i}
. The first step is standard as in usual Gaussian approximation methods
Verstraelen and Wouters 2018
;
Li et al. 2025a
, where the expectation value of any higher moment is decomposed into first and second moments using the Wick theorem, allowing us to close the equations on the Gaussian level, and we derive their expressions in the Appendix
B
. In what follows, we elaborate on the second step, which crucially depends on the parametrization of the frame
\hat{Q}_{i}
. We show that the quaternion formalism naturally provides a non-singular parametrization of the comoving frame, and the above procedure results in a stochastic differential equation for the variational parameters in the limit of
\delta t\to 0
.

III.2
Non-singular parametrization of the comoving frame with quaternions

We now construct the comoving frame
O{\tilde{x}}_{i}{\tilde{y}}_{i}{\tilde{z}}_{i}
. As illustrated in Fig.
1
(b), any local frame with
{\tilde{z}}
aligned with the spin vector is a valid choice, which leaves the orientation within the
O{\tilde{x}}{\tilde{y}}
plane a gauge freedom with no physical consequence. Instead of fixing a gauge, which is equivalent to defining a unit vector field on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and will hence lead to discontinuity, we leave it as a dynamical degree of freedom that evolves naturally with the state.

Let us consider the local frame of the
i-
th spin parametrized by the generic rotation

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{i}(\theta_{i},{\bm{n}}_{i})\equiv{\rm{e}}^{-{\rm{i}}\theta_{i}{\bm{n}}_{i}\cdot\hat{\bm{s}}_{i}}\,,

(19)

and derive its dynamics that preserves the alignment with the local spin magnetization. In the rest of this derivation, we drop the site index
i
for ease of notation, as the same procedure applies to each individual site
i
independently. An efficient and non-singular way to encode this rotation is via the unit
quaternion

\displaystyle Q\equiv\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cos\dfrac{\theta}{2},{\bm{n}}\sin\dfrac{\theta}{2}}}\right)\,,

(20)

which we also denote by
Q
since they form a representation of the rotation group
SO(3)
under quaternion multiplication. This is manifest from the representation of quaternions via Pauli matrices,

\displaystyle q=(q_{r},q_{x},q_{y},q_{z})\mapsto q_{r}\hat{\mathds{1}}-{\rm{i}}\bm{q}\cdot\bm{\hat{\sigma}}\,,

(21)

with the short-hand notation
\bm{q}\equiv(q_{x},q_{y},q_{z})
. The unit quaternion

\displaystyle Q\mapsto\cos\dfrac{\theta}{2}\hat{\mathds{1}}-{\rm{i}}\sin\dfrac{\theta}{2}{\bm{n}}\cdot\bm{\hat{\sigma}}={\rm{e}}^{-{\rm{i}}\frac{\theta}{2}{\bm{n}}\cdot\bm{\hat{\sigma}}}\,.

(22)

In particular, the non-commutative quaternion product
Q_{1}Q_{2}
corresponds to the rotation
\hat{Q}_{1}\hat{Q}_{2}
.

To find the dynamics of
Q
, we proceed in the same way as in our previous work
Li et al. 2025a
. After an infinitesimal time evolution of the state in the static frame
Q(t)
, we pause the physical dynamics to find the rotation
R({\rm{d}}t):Q(t)\mapsto Q(t+{\rm{d}}t)
that restores the alignment, as graphically represented in Fig.

\langle\hat{b}\rangle
evolved from 0 to
\beta

\hat{R}(\phi,{\bm{m}})={\rm{e}}^{-{\rm{i}}\phi{\bm{m}}\cdot\hat{\bm{s}}}
, for some angle
\phi
and unit vector axis
{\bm{m}}
to be determined. When the angle evolves with
{\bm{m}}
fixed, the fictitious dynamics induced on the frame-dependent operator
\hat{b}(\phi)

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{RF}}

\hat{Q}(t+{\rm{d}}t)=\hat{R}(\phi,{\bm{m}})\hat{Q}(t)\equiv\hat{Q}(\phi)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{RF}}

\displaystyle=-{\rm{i}}\dfrac{{\rm{d}}\hat{Q}^{\dagger}(\phi)}{{\rm{d}}\phi}\hat{Q}(\phi)

(23)

\displaystyle=-{\rm{i}}\hat{Q}^{\dagger}(t)\dfrac{{\rm{d}}\hat{R}^{\dagger}}{{\rm{d}}\phi}\hat{R}\hat{Q}(t)

\displaystyle=\hat{Q}^{\dagger}(\phi)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\bm{m}}\cdot\hat{\bm{s}}}}\right)\hat{Q}(\phi)

\displaystyle={\bm{m}}\cdot\tilde{\bm{s}}(\phi)\,,

where

This results in

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{RF}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\dfrac{s}{2}}(m_{x}+{\rm{i}}m_{y})\hat{b}^{\dagger}+\sqrt{\dfrac{s}{2}}(m_{x}-{\rm{i}}m_{y})\hat{b}

(24)

\displaystyle+m_{z}(s-\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b})\,,

which is quadratic in
\hat{b}
.
It is then straightforward to solve for the dynamics of the comoving operator
\hat{b}

\displaystyle\dfrac{{\rm{d}}\hat{b}}{{\rm{d}}\phi}={\rm{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\hat{H}_{\mathrm{RF}},\hat{b}}}\right]=-{\rm{i}}\sqrt{\dfrac{s}{2}}(m_{x}+{\rm{i}}m_{y})+{\rm{i}}m_{z}\hat{b}\,.

(25)

Our goal is to find
{\bm{m}}
and
\phi

\beta

\langle\hat{b}(\phi)\rangle=0

\displaystyle\hat{b}(\phi)-\hat{b}(0)=-{\rm{i}}\sqrt{\dfrac{s}{2}}(m_{x}+{\rm{i}}m_{y})\phi\,.

(26)

This shows that the rotating-frame evolution gives a displacement to the bosonic operator
\hat{b}
(to the leading order of the Holstein-Primakoff transformation), and therefore the covariances are not affected by the rotation.
Our solution for the angle and the axis is therefore

\displaystyle\phi=|{\tilde{\beta}}|\,,\quad{\bm{m}}=\dfrac{1}{|{\tilde{\beta}}|}({\imaginary{\tilde{\beta}}},-\real{\tilde{\beta}},0)\,,

(27)

with
{\tilde{\beta}}\equiv\beta\sqrt{2/s}

\hat{R}
as a function of
\beta

\displaystyle R(\beta)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cos\dfrac{\phi}{2},{\bm{m}}\sin\dfrac{\phi}{2}}}\right)

(28)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cos\dfrac{|{\tilde{\beta}}|}{2},\dfrac{1}{2}\imaginary{\tilde{\beta}}\,\mathrm{sinc}\dfrac{|{\tilde{\beta}}|}{2},-\dfrac{1}{2}\real{\tilde{\beta}}\,\mathrm{sinc}\dfrac{|{\tilde{\beta}}|}{2},0}}\right)\,,

where
\mathrm{sinc}(x)\equiv\sin(x)/x

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}Q=(R-1)Q\,,

. Otherwise,

\displaystyle\cos\dfrac{|{\rm{d}}{\tilde{\beta}}|}{2}

\displaystyle=1-\dfrac{|{\rm{d}}{\tilde{\beta}}|^{2}}{8}+O({\rm{d}}t^{2})\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{sinc}\dfrac{|{\rm{d}}{\tilde{\beta}}|}{2}

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}A=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\dfrac{|{\rm{d}}{\tilde{\beta}}|^{2}}{8},\dfrac{1}{2}\imaginary{\rm{d}}{\tilde{\beta}},-\dfrac{1}{2}\real{\rm{d}}{\tilde{\beta}},0}}\right)\,,

III.3
Summary of the method

We now briefly recap the key operations performed in our algorithm. We first obtain the expressions of
{\rm{d}}\beta_{i}
,
{\rm{d}}u_{ij}
and
{\rm{d}}v_{ij}
using the stochastic master equation (see Appendix
B
where we derive them for both the heterodyne and the quantum-jump unravelings), and then derive
{\rm{d}}Q_{i}
from
{\rm{d}}\beta_{i}
using Eq. (
32
) (for the continuous evolution) or Eq. (
29
) (for the discontinuous jump), thus eliminating the dummy variables
{\rm{d}}\beta_{i}
and arriving at a closed set of stochastic differential equations for
Q_{i}(t)
,
u_{ij}(t)
and
v_{ij}(t)
, which fully determines the dynamics of the state within our semiclassical approximations.

Finally, let us remark that our construction of the
local
spin-wave approximations and the inclusion of higher-order corrections in the bosonization significantly enlarges the domain of applicability of spin-wave quantum trajectories, beyond the conventional regime of large-
s
or long-range interacting systems: in fact, it is exact in representing quantum trajectories of a single spin-
1/2
(where the standard spin-wave theory completely breaks down), and provides a reasonably good approximation even at weakly coupled few-body systems with nearest-neighbor interactions [while the conventional spin-wave theory typically relies on the long-range (or high-dimensional) many-body limit], as we will benchmark in the next section. Strengthened by the singularity-free parametrization via the quaternion formalism, our framework enables the efficient simulation of large-scale spin systems, which we now explore below.

IV.1.2
Symmetry breaking along trajectories

Let us now apply the generalized SWQT framework to larger system sizes and study the temporal trajectory dynamics in the power-law spin system. As described in Sec.
II
, the Liouvillian of the model I admits a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry due to its invariance under the transformation
\hat{s}^{x,y}_{i}\mapsto-\hat{s}^{x,y}
. This implies that at any finite sizes, the (unique) steady state exhibits the same symmetry and therefore always has
m^{x,y}=0
, where we define the notation for the average magnetization
m^{\mu}\equiv\sum_{i}\langle\hat{\sigma}^{\mu}_{i}\rangle/N=0
. In contrast, the stochastic master equation for the heterodyne unraveling (
6
) does not share this symmetry due to the presence of terms of the form
\hat{L}_{i}\hat{\rho}
and
\hat{\rho}\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{i}
, thus offering a perfect playground for observing the symmetry-breaking dynamics in real time. Fig.
4
(a) shows a single SWQT heterodyne trajectory for
N=6\times 6
with
h=J
,
\gamma=0.6J
and
\alpha=1
corresponding to the mean-field symmetry-broken regime, where the
x-
and
y-
site-averaged spin components switch between two metastable branches, with a switching time on the order of the inverse Liouvillian gap
Minganti 2018
.
Fig.
4
(b) shows the distribution of
m^{x}
at long times along trajectories of different values of
\gamma
, revealing the bimodality of the trajectory ensemble: the average state can be viewed as a symmetric mixture of states with opposite
x-
(and
y-
) magnetization in the ferromagnetic phase. This bimodality disappears when the system enters the disordered phase with larger values of
\gamma
.

A few remarks are in order. The above bimodality implies that the average mixed state is highly non-Gaussian and therefore is very difficult to capture with semiclassical approximations operating on the level of the mixed density matrix. The SWQT framework successfully reproduces this essential feature in symmetry-breaking phase transitions, thanks to two crucial facts, that 1) the stochastic sampling of spin-wave trajectories effectively constructs a mixture of Gaussian states, resulting in a larger family of states that can be reached on the level of the average density matrix, and that 2) the choice of the heterodyne unraveling explicitly breaks the symmetry on the level of the quantum trajectory, such that our semiclassical state representation does not need to struggle in trying to approximate a symmetric bimodal state. This will no longer be the case if one chooses a symmetry-preserving trajectory dynamics, such as the quantum-jump unraveling (since
\hat{L}_{i}
and
\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{i}
always appear together, and that symmetry transformation simply flips the sign of
\hat{L}_{i}=\sqrt{\gamma}\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{i}
, leaving the stochastic equation invariant). The true state along the quantum-jump dynamics therefore remains (or quickly relaxes to be) already bimodal and resembles a Schrödinger cat state
Minganti 2018
(as it is also a pure state, i.e., the bimodality must result from a coherent superposition), which is highly quantum and falls beyond the reach of semiclassical approximations. Nevertheless, our spin-wave framework can be applied to the quantum-jump unraveling in the absence of this symmetry, which we demonstrate on the
\hat{H}^{x-zz}
model in Sec.
IV.2
.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we have proposed a semiclassical approximation framework based on a generalized stochastic spin-wave theory along quantum trajectories of driven-dissipative spin systems. Our results highlight several features of the methodology that can be summarized as follows:

•

The method significantly widens the applicability of spin-wave approximations, via the construction of local comoving frames, the inclusion of higher-order corrections, and the quaternion parametrization of the frames, which also completely resolved the coordinate singularity issues commonly present in time-dependent spin-wave theories.

•

Applying this framework to the 2D variable-range driven-dissipative spin model with a spontaneous
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry-breaking phase transition, we unveil a crossover of universality class from mean-field to 2D Ising when the interaction range shortens. Remarkably, the method accurately recovers the non-mean-field critical exponent
\beta=1/8
in the nearest-neighbor interacting limit.

•

The spin-wave framework is further generalized to incorporate local quantum-jump dynamics, with careful benchmarks showing its accuracy, and is showcased by capturing a first-order transition in a 2D nearest-neighbor interacting spin lattice without
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry.

Beyond these results demonstrating the broad applicability of our method, several exciting directions are already manifest for future explorations. For example, the framework can be readily extended to include bosonic modes interacting with the spins, where the same Gaussian approximation is applied to the bosons, as demonstrated in our previous work
Li et al. 2025a
. This allows us to explore a large class of light-matter interaction models, such as the Tavis-Cummings model
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
;
Delmonte et al. 2025
, highly relevant for current synthetic quantum platforms.
Moreover, as our method construction is insensitive to the spatial geometry of the physical system, we expect it to work equally well in any dimension, allowing us to probe the interplay between the interaction range and the dimensionality in the critical dynamics, which is a fundamental problem in statistical physics.
Furthermore, the framework can be readily combined with the recently developed variational phase-space approaches
Tosca et al. 2025
;
Tosca et al. 2026
, to provide a beyond-semiclassical description of monitored dynamics in the deep-quantum regime. Finally, given the generality of our formalism, the proposed SWQT framework sets a natural and scalable playground for investigating the rich physics in open many-body spin systems, paving the way to the exploration of novel collective phenomena in non-equilibrium quantum matter.

Appendix A
Mean-field equations for the spin models

We derive in this appendix the mean-field equations for the spin models considered in this work. Consider the following form of the Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle\hat{H}=h\sum_{i}\hat{\sigma}^{{\bm{n}}_{1}}_{i}+\sum_{i\neq j}J_{ij}^{(\alpha)}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{{\bm{n}}_{2}}\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{{\bm{n}}_{2}}\,,

(35)

with
\hat{\sigma}^{{\bm{n}}}\equiv{\bm{n}}\cdot\bm{\hat{\sigma}}
, and the Lindblad jump operator
\hat{L}_{i}=\sqrt{\gamma}\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{i}
, which covers both models defined in the main text. In the mean-field approximation, the system state is described by a factorized ansatz,

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{MF}}=\bigotimes_{i}\hat{\rho}_{i}\,,

(36)

and we further impose
\hat{\rho}_{i}=\hat{\rho}
,
\forall i
, due to the translational symmetry of the Liouvillian. The dynamics can then be solved in terms of the single-body state
\hat{\rho}
,

\displaystyle\dfrac{{\rm{d}}\hat{\rho}}{{\rm{d}}t}

\displaystyle=-{\rm{i}}[\hat{H}^{\mathrm{MF}}(\hat{\rho}),\hat{\rho}]+\gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{\sigma}^{+}\hat{\rho}\hat{\sigma}^{-}-\dfrac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\hat{\sigma}^{+}\hat{\sigma}^{-},\hat{\rho}}}\right\}}}\right)

(37)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{MF}}\hat{\rho}\,,

where the mean-field Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\mathrm{MF}}(\hat{\rho})=h\hat{\sigma}^{{\bm{n}}_{1}}+2J\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{\rho}\hat{\sigma}^{{\bm{n}}_{2}}]\hat{\sigma}^{{\bm{n}}_{2}}\,,

(38)

and we recall that
\sum_{j\neq i_{0}}J_{i_{0},j}^{(\alpha)}=J
,
\forall i_{0}
, due to the Kac normalization.
In particular, the mean-field steady state can be obtained by solving
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{MF}}\hat{\rho}=0
. The above derivation shows that the mean-field approximation ignores any quantum correlation between different sites, and that it is blind to the interaction range
\alpha
.

Appendix B
Unravelling-specific spin-wave equations

In this appendix, we derive the spin-wave equations for the two unravelings considered in the main text.

B.1
Heterodyne dynamics

Within the Gaussian approximation, the heterodyne equation (
6
) translates into the stochastic dynamics of the variational parameters, which are conveniently described by the adjoint stochastic master equation.
For a time-independent operator
\hat{O}
, its expectation value along the heterodyne dynamics evolves as follows,

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}\langle\hat{O}\rangle={\rm{d}}t\langle\mathcal{L}^{\ddagger}\hat{O}\rangle+\langle{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{\ddagger}\hat{O}\rangle\,,

(39)

where the adjoint superoperators are defined as follows,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\ddagger}\hat{O}\equiv

\displaystyle{\rm{\penalty\displaystyle i}}[\hat{H},\hat{O}]+\sum_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{O}\hat{L}_{i}-\dfrac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{L}_{i},\hat{O}}}\right\}}}\right)\,,

(40)

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{\ddagger}\hat{O}\equiv

\displaystyle\penalty\displaystyle\sum_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\rm{d}}Z_{i}^{*}\hat{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{L}_{i}-\langle\hat{L}_{i}\rangle}}\right)+}}\right.

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.{\rm{d}}Z_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{L}_{i}^{\dagger}-\langle\hat{L}_{i}^{\dagger}\rangle}}\right)\hat{O}}}\right]\,.

The first moment
\beta_{i}=\langle\hat{b}_{i}\rangle
is the expectation value of a time-independent operator (since the frame remains static in this step), hence one can use directly the adjoint stochastic master equation (
39
) to obtain

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}\beta_{i}={\rm{d}}t\langle\mathcal{L}^{\ddagger}\hat{b}_{i}\rangle+\langle{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{\ddagger}\hat{b}_{i}\rangle\,.

(41)

The second moments are expectations of time-dependent operators by definition, of the form
\hat{\delta}_{A}\hat{\delta}_{B}=\hat{A}\hat{B}-\langle\hat{A}\rangle\langle\hat{B}\rangle
for some time-independent operators
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
, and their increments should be evaluated using stochastic differentiation rules. Let us first note that the adjoint superoperator
\mathcal{L}^{\ddagger}
is linear, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\ddagger}(\hat{A}+\lambda\hat{B})=\mathcal{L}^{\ddagger}\hat{A}+\lambda\mathcal{L}^{\ddagger}\hat{B}\,,

(42)

and that adding multiples of identity does not contribute to the expectation value, i.e.,
\langle\mathcal{L}^{\ddagger}(c\hat{\mathds{1}})\rangle=0
,
\forall\lambda,c\in\mathbb{C}
, and the exact same properties are satisfied by the superoperator
{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}
. This allows us to derive,

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}\langle\hat{\delta}_{A}\hat{\delta}_{B}\rangle

\displaystyle={\rm{d}}\langle\hat{A}\hat{B}\rangle-{\rm{d}}(\langle\hat{A}\rangle\langle\hat{B}\rangle)

(43)

\displaystyle={\rm{d}}\langle\hat{A}\hat{B}\rangle-({\rm{d}}\langle\hat{A}\rangle)\langle\hat{B}\rangle-\langle\hat{A}\rangle{\rm{d}}\langle\hat{B}\rangle-{\rm{d}}\langle\hat{A}\rangle{\rm{d}}\langle\hat{B}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\mathcal{L}^{\ddagger}+{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{\ddagger})(\hat{A}\hat{B})\rangle-\langle(\mathcal{L}^{\ddagger}+{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{\ddagger})\hat{A}\rangle\langle\hat{B}\rangle

\displaystyle-\langle\hat{A}\rangle\langle(\mathcal{L}^{\ddagger}+{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{\ddagger})\hat{B}\rangle-\underbrace{\langle(\mathcal{L}^{\ddagger}+{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{\ddagger})(\langle\hat{A}\rangle\langle\hat{B}\rangle)\hat{\mathds{1}}\rangle}_{=0}

\displaystyle-\langle{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{\ddagger}\hat{A}\rangle\langle{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{\ddagger}\hat{B}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\mathcal{L}^{\ddagger}+{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{\ddagger})(\hat{\delta}_{A}\hat{\delta}_{B}))-\langle{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{\ddagger}\hat{A}\rangle\langle{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{\ddagger}\hat{B}\rangle\,,

where the last term is proportional to
{\rm{d}}t
and arises from the Wiener noise properties
{\rm{d}}Z_{i}^{*}{\rm{d}}Z_{j}=\delta_{ij}{\rm{d}}t
. This gives the increments for the second Gaussian moments,

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}u_{ij}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{L}^{\ddagger}(\hat{\delta}_{i}\hat{\delta}_{j})\rangle+\langle{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{\ddagger}(\hat{\delta}_{i}\hat{\delta}_{j})\rangle

(44)

\displaystyle-\langle{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{\ddagger}\hat{b}_{i}\rangle\langle{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{\ddagger}\hat{b}_{j}\rangle\,,

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}v_{ij}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{L}^{\ddagger}(\hat{\delta}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{\delta}_{j})\rangle+\langle{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{\ddagger}(\hat{\delta}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{\delta}_{j})\rangle

\displaystyle-\langle{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{\ddagger}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{i}\rangle\langle{\rm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{\ddagger}\hat{b}_{j}\rangle\,.

B.2
Quantum-jump dynamics

Similar to our previous treatment, to find the stochastic dynamics governing the Gaussian variational parameters, we proceed by first recasting the quantum-jump equation (

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}\langle\hat{O}\rangle

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}\beta_{i}

B.2.1
Implementation with the waiting-time algorithm

In practice, we follow the standard implementations of the quantum-jump dynamics by sampling the waiting-time distribution between jumps instead of generating
{\rm{d}}M_{i}
at every time step
Wiseman and Milburn 2009
. To determine the time for each jump event, we first draw a random number
r
uniformly between 0 and 1, and then integrate the
ordinary
differential equation governing the smooth no-jump evolution,

\displaystyle\dfrac{{\rm{d}}\langle\hat{O}\rangle}{{\rm{d}}t}

\displaystyle={\rm{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle[\hat{H},\hat{O}]}}\right\rangle-\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\mathcal{H}^{\ddagger}[\hat{L}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{i}]\hat{O}}}\right\rangle\,,

(50)

which is equivalent to considering a time evolution generated by the effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonian (
12
).
This directly translates into an ordinary differential equation for our dynamical variational parameters
Q_{i}(t)
[using Eq. (
32
)],
u_{ij}(t)
and
v_{ij}(t)
using the equations derived above.
The wavefunction norm
\Lambda
, initialized with value
1
, decays along the non-unitary evolution according to

\displaystyle\dfrac{{\rm{d}}\Lambda}{{\rm{d}}t}=-\sum_{i}\langle\hat{L}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{i}\rangle\Lambda\,,

(51)

and the norm value
\Lambda(t)
equals the probability of such a no-jump evolution up to time
t
to occur [see Eq. (
11
)]. The correct waiting-time distribution is therefore generated by interrupting this evolution at the time where
\Lambda(t)=r
. A jump is then applied, updating the state as

\displaystyle\langle\hat{O}\rangle\to\dfrac{\langle\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{O}\hat{L}_{k}\rangle}{\langle\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{k}\rangle}\,,

(52)

where a single jump channel
k
is selected with probability proportional to
\langle\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{k}\rangle
. In terms of the Gaussian parameters of the state ansatz, this update reads [see Eq. (
49
)],

\displaystyle\beta_{i}

\displaystyle\to\beta_{i}^{J}\equiv\dfrac{\langle\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{b}_{i}\hat{L}_{k}\rangle}{\langle\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{L}_{k}\rangle}\,,

(53)

\displaystyle u_{ij}

\displaystyle\to\dfrac{\langle\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{b}_{i}\hat{b}_{j}\hat{L}_{k}\rangle}{\langle\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{L}_{k}\rangle}-\beta_{i}^{J}\beta_{j}^{J}\,,

\displaystyle v_{ij}

\displaystyle\to\dfrac{\langle\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{b}_{j}\hat{L}_{k}\rangle}{\langle\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{L}_{k}\rangle}-\beta_{i}^{J*}\beta_{j}^{J}\,,

\displaystyle Q_{i}

\displaystyle\to R(\beta^{J}_{i})Q_{i}\,,

where the last equation for the quaternion
Q_{i}
uses the finite (non-infinitesimal) version of the rotation defined in Eq. (
28
), which updates the local comoving frames and resets the dummy variables
\beta_{i}
to 0. This completes the jump update, after which we reset the norm to
\Lambda=1
and start over with a new iteration of the above waiting-time sampling procedure, until the desired final time of the evolution is reached.

B.2.2
Implementation of local jumps

The above formulation of quantum-jump spin-wave dynamics, despite being formally generic, yields only a rough approximation if implemented naively. For example, the jump operator
\hat{L}_{i}=\sqrt{\gamma}\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{i}
, which sends any local spin state to the down state, inevitably generates a large displacement
\beta_{i}^{J}
in the bosonized dynamics (and hence an uncontrolled spin-wave density) when a jump occurs, eventually breaking the validity of the spin-wave approximation. Luckily, since the post-jump state (for this specific jump process) of the site corresponding to the jump channel is known, namely the
\ket{\downarrow}_{z}
state disentangled from the rest of the system, one can encode this information directly in the jump update. This results in a correction to the update rules (
53
): assuming
k
is the selected jump channel, we set all covariances involving site
k
, i.e. those of the form
u_{ki}
,
u_{ik}
,
v_{ki}
,
v_{ik}
, to zero, and update
Q_{k}
to a quaternion corresponding to the down-pointing local frame, for example
Q_{k}\to(0,1,0,0)
. In weakly entangled systems, this locally exact correction significantly improves the validity of the spin-wave approximations.
