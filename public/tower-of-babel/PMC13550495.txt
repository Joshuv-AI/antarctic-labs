A unified constitutive model for yielding single-lining tunnel supports with resistance limiters under large deformation
Authors: Muhammad Zohaib, Qiu Wenge, Muhammad Awais Hussain, Weipeng Pan, Muhammad Akbar, Tarek Salem Abdennaji, Aymen Flah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLarge deformation in soft rock tunnels remains a critical challenge in mining and underground engineering, with conventional rigid supports prone to overstressing and failure due to limited deformation compatibility. Although yielding supports with resistance limiters such as inflatable airbags and circular steel bellows (CSBs) provide improved adaptability, current design approaches remain largely empirical or overly simplified. As a result, they lack a unified constitutive framework that can quantitatively describe their multi-stage mechanical behavior and effectively link it to performance-based design. To address this gap, this study develops and experimentally validates a novel constitutive model that characterizes yielding supports through three explicit phases: elastic yielding (initial stiffness ko = 0.18–0.25 kN/mm), post-yield hardening (hardening stiffness kh = 0.42–0.68 kN/mm), and locking (ultimate resistance FL = 2.0–2.4 kN). To enable quantitative performance assessment, eight dimensionless indices are introduced, including the yielding ductility ratio (μ), deformation capacity envelope (Ω), plasticity index (ψ), strain localization index (LƐ), strain uniformity index (UƐ), support adaptability index (SAI), and energy-reaction coupling coefficient (η). Large-scale model tests demonstrate that airbag limiters reduce vertical deformation by approximately 35% relative to rigid supports and exhibit superior early-stage adaptability (Ω ≈ 0.46), whereas CSBs provide higher energy dissipation (Ed = 50–55 J), greater strain uniformity (Uε ≈ 0.79), and enhanced stability through post-yield hardening. The proposed framework further integrates these indices into practical design tools, including stability maps based on stiffness ratios (α = ko/kr, β = kh/kr) and the Yield-Hardening Design Surface (YHDS), enabling performance-based selection and optimization of yielding supports under squeezing conditions. This work provides a validated, mechanics-based, and metrics-driven approach to mining and tunnel support design, effectively bridging the gap between constitutive modeling and practical engineering application.Keywords: Yielding tunnel support; Constitutive model; Strain uniformity index; Mining and underground engineering, Support adaptability index; Energy-reaction coupling coefficient; Squeezing groundSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Tunnelling through soft, fractured, or squeezing rock masses under high in situ stress continues to pose significant challenges in underground engineering1–4. In conventional rigid support systems, large deformations, often exceeding a few percent of the tunnel width, can generate excessive stresses that lead to cracking, spalling, and, in severe cases, structural collapse5–8. The primary limitation of traditional rigid supports, such as steel sets, thick concrete linings, and systematic bolting, lies in their inability to accommodate ground convergence while maintaining sufficient load bearing capacity9–11. To address this limitation, yielding or deformable support systems have been developed to allow controlled ground movement while dissipating energy and redistributing stress12–14.

Various yielding support concepts have been proposed and investigated over recent decades, including compressible concrete or lattice girders in primary support15,16, yielding steel arches with sliding or hinging mechanisms17,18, ductile rock bolts19–22, and compressible liners23. More recently, innovative resistance limiters, such as circular steel bellows (CSBs) and inflatable airbags, have been introduced to provide staged resistance through regulated compression24. Experimental studies indicate that such systems can reduce lining strains by 60–75% compared to rigid supports25, representing a significant advancement in single-lining systems for squeezing ground conditions. These systems operate based on the stiffness matching principle, in which a low initial stiffness permits early deformation accommodation, followed by progressive stiffening to ensure confinement and stability26,27.

Despite these advances, the analytical and constitutive modeling of yielding support systems remains limited. Most existing tunnel-support interaction models represent the support response using simplified linear or perfectly plastic assumptions. This includes the widely used convergence-confinement technique28,29 and its extensions for elastoplastic rock30,31. Such simplifications fail to capture the inherently nonlinear and multi-stage mechanical behavior of modern yielding limiters, particularly their stiffness evolution, energy dissipation characteristics, and post-yield hardening behavior32,33. In addition, although advanced constitutive models exist for individual materials34, cyclic plasticity for metals35–38, and damage models for concrete39, these models are typically developed and validated independently and are not integrated into a unified tunnel–rock–support interaction framework.

This leads to a critical research gap: a unified constitutive framework that integrates the staged mechanical response of yielding resistance limiters, including their deformation phases, energy dissipation mechanisms, and load-transfer behavior, into a comprehensive tunnel-support interaction model is currently lacking. Existing studies primarily focus on empirical performance comparisons40,41 or numerical simulations that treat yielding contacts as simplified boundary conditions14,42. Consequently, there are limited experimentally validated models capable of quantitatively describing the force–displacement response of yielding supports, which restricts parameter optimization and accurate performance prediction in design. The absence of such physics-based tools limits the practical implementation of these advanced support systems in large-deformation tunnelling conditions43,44.

To address this gap, a unified constitutive model for single-lining tunnel supports incorporating resistance limiters is developed and experimentally validated in this study. The proposed model characterizes the mechanical response through three distinct stages, namely elastic yielding, post-yield hardening, and locking. In addition, a set of dimensionless performance indices is introduced to quantify deformation capacity, energy dissipation, and system stability, providing a systematic basis for performance evaluation and design optimization.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section "Constitutive Framework" presents the constitutive model formulation and mechanical idealization. Section "Experimental Methodology" details the large-scale physical model tests, including the experimental setup, material properties and monitoring scheme for rigid, airbag- and CSB-supported tunnels. Section "Experimental Results" presents and discusses the results, covering force–displacement response, stiffness evolution, energy dissipation, strain redistribution and stability analysis. Section "Sensitivity Analysis of Constitutive Parameters" concludes with key findings, design implications and recommendations for implementation in squeezing ground conditions.

Constitutive framework

This section introduces a unified constitutive framework for single-lining tunnel supports with resistance limiters. The aim is to clearly describe the complex nonlinear behavior observed in large-deformation tunnel support systems. To do this, the framework combines a simple mechanical representation, a clear description of different response stages, basic mathematical relationships, and dimensionless indices that help evaluate performance in a practical way.

By comparing the mechanical idealization with experimental force–displacement curves, all test cases had a coefficient of determination R2 > 0.9745. This strong connection demonstrates how well the nonlinear radial spring model captures the primary deformation mechanisms while ignoring ancillary factors that have a negligible impact on the overall force–displacement response.

Staged mechanical response

Experimental observations reveal three distinct deformation stages that characterize the mechanical behavior of yielding tunnel supports with resistance limiters:

Stage I: Elastic yielding

The initial stiffness k0 controls the quasi-linear elastic nature of the initial reaction46–48. In order to accommodate early ground convergence, the resistance limiter experiences recoverable deformation during this phase. Depending on the limiter setup, the yield displacement uy, which normally ranges from 1.0 to 1.2 mm, marks the changeover to Stage II.

Stage II: Post-yield hardening

The system shows progressive stiffening after yield initiation, which is indicated by the hardening stiffness kh. 50–60% of the overall energy absorption occurs during this period, which is the principal energy dissipation phase49,50. Stress concentration and localized failure are avoided while regulated load transmission to the lining is made possible by the hardening reaction.

Stage III: Locking

When the resistance limiter reaches the locking displacement uL (3.5–3.8 mm), it fully activates and the surrounding rock and lining become rigidly connected51. The ultimate resistance (FL) controls this last stage. This maximizes confinement while limiting excessive deformation. The framework is especially appropriate for rate-independent geological materials. Because the transition between phases is controlled by displacement thresholds rather than time-dependent processes. These three phases are schematically shown in Fig. 2, which emphasizes the distinctive patterns of force mobilization and stiffness evolution.

It should be clarified that the locking stage represents a stabilized resistance plateau rather than a peak strength followed by softening. The parameter FL​ corresponds to the fully mobilized load-bearing capacity of the support system after complete activation of the resistance limiter. Post-peak softening behavior is not explicitly considered in the present model, as the focus is on support-controlled response under large deformation conditions. This assumption is consistent with the design objective of maintaining sustained confinement and avoiding sudden loss of support capacity.

Dimensionless performance indices

This paper presents eight dimensionless performance indices to facilitate systematic performance measurement, comparison, and design optimization of yielding tunnel support systems under high deformation. These indicators create a clear, quantitative connection between the support system’s functional behavior and quantifiable mechanical factors including stiffness, displacement, strain, and energy. The suggested framework provides a unified foundation for performance-based design and selection in squeezing ground conditions by normalizing response characteristics, which enables objective evaluation of deformation capacity, energy dissipation efficiency, strain redistribution, and system stability across various limiter configurations. This constitutive framework establishes a direct link between measurable mechanical parameters and quantitative performance metrics, enabling systematic evaluation and optimization of yielding support systems for large deformation tunnel applications.

Although the proposed model represents post-yield hardening in a unified manner, the underlying physical mechanisms differ between resistance limiter types. In CSB systems, hardening primarily arises from plastic deformation and geometric effects of the steel bellows, whereas in airbag systems, stiffness evolution is governed by gas compression, which may introduce pressure-dependent and rate-dependent behavior. The present formulation captures these effects at a macroscopic level through equivalent stiffness parameters; however, these differences should be considered when interpreting results, particularly for time-dependent or dynamic loading conditions.

Experimental methodology

This section describes the experimental program carried out to validate the proposed constitutive framework and to investigate the mechanical behavior of yielding single-lining tunnel supports with resistance limiters under large deformation conditions. A series of controlled laboratory model tests were conducted to replicate the complex nonlinear interaction between the support system and the surrounding ground.

Model test setup

Scaling principles

In accordance with accepted similitude standards for geotechnical model testing, the experimental program used a geometric scale ratio of 1:10. Scaling laws for important parameters were derived using Buckingham’s π theorem54:

where λ denotes the scale factor for length (λL​), stress (λσ​), elastic modulus (λE​), and strain (λε​). These scaling relationships ensure that stress and strain fields in the model accurately represent prototype conditions while maintaining dimensional consistency.

Test chamber configuration

As shown in Fig., tests were carried out in a rigid steel test chamber with interior measurements of 3.0 m (length) × 1.85 m (width) × 3.0 m (height). Third. The chamber features a reinforced reaction frame, hydraulic loading system, and polycarbonate observation windows for visual monitoring. Boundary conditions were designed to minimize edge effects, with a clearance distance of 1.22 m from the tunnel centerline to lateral boundaries, exceeding the minimum requirement of one tunnel diameter55.

To replicate a prototype burial depth of 20 m, the tunnel model was placed in the middle of the room with 1.5 m of soil covering it. Base reflection effects were avoided and symmetric boundary conditions were achieved by an extra 1.5 m of foundation dirt beneath the tunnel invert. To ensure that there was no lateral strain during testing, load cells were installed on the stiff base plate and sidewalls of the test chamber (Fig. 3).

1 = Reaction frame, 2 = Fixed spring, 3 = Reaction column, 4 = Fixing bolts, 5 = Observation window, 6 = Base plate, 7 = Wire rope, 8 = Reinforcing strip, 9 = Side plate, 10 = Jack.

Loading system

Two synchronized hydraulic jacks (HJS-30, maximum capacity 294 kN each) applied uniform surface loading through a rigid distribution beam. The loading system provided displacement-controlled monotonic loading at a constant rate of 0.5 mm/min, corresponding to a prototype loading rate of 5.0 mm/min after scaling considerations. This loading rate ensured quasi-static conditions while preventing dynamic effects that could influence the force–displacement response.

Materials and specimen preparation

Surrounding rock simulant

The surrounding rock material was designed to simulate Class IV rock mass conditions according to the Chinese Code for Design of Railway Tunnels56. The mixture comprised barite powder (40% by weight), river sand (30%), quartz sand (15%), engine oil (10%), and rosin (5%), formulated to achieve target mechanical properties through extensive preliminary testing. The simulant exhibited a unit weight γ = 22 kN/m3, elastic modulus E = 0.04 GPa, cohesion c = 0.006 MPa, and internal friction angle ϕ = 27°, satisfying the scaling relationships with prototype values of Ep = 1.6 and cp = 0.24 MPa (Table 1). The material demonstrated strain-softening behavior with a post-peak strength reduction of 30%, accurately representing the brittle-ductile transition characteristics of squeezing rock masses.

Tunnel lining model

The tunnel lining was constructed using gypsum-water mixture (ratio 1.05:1 by weight) reinforced with 0.6 mm diameter steel wire mesh at 20 mm spacing. This composition yielded an elastic modulus of 0.84 GPa, compressive strength of 0.57 MPa, and Poisson’s ratio of 0.22, corresponding to scaled properties of C30 concrete (Ep = 33.6 GPa, fcp = 23.1 MPa). The lining had an inner diameter of 50 cm and thickness of 2 cm, with a length of 85 cm to ensure plane strain conditions in the central measurement section.

Instrumentation scheme

A comprehensive instrumentation array was deployed to capture multi-parameter response data:

Strain measurement

Sixty-four foil strain gauges (BF-120, gauge factor 2.08 ± 1%, resistance 120 Ω ± 0.3%) were installed on the lining exterior at three monitoring sections spaced 15 cm apart (Fig. 7). Each section included 16 gauges positioned at the crown, invert, shoulders, and sidewalls in both longitudinal and circumferential orientations to capture strain gradients and bending moments.

Experimental equipment.

Displacement monitoring

Eight linear variable displacement transducers (LVDTs, Omega WDS-200, range ± 10 mm, accuracy ± 0.1% FS) measured radial convergence at the crown, invert, and sidewalls. Additionally, four LVDTs monitored vertical settlement of the ground surface above the tunnel.

Pressure distribution

Eight earth pressure cells (KYB-100, range 0–1 MPa, accuracy ± 0.5% FS) were installed at the interface between the lining and surrounding ground at crown, invert, and sidewall locations. The pressure cells featured flexible diaphragms to minimize stress disturbance and ensure accurate measurement of contact pressures.

Data acquisition system

A 24-bit data acquisition system sampling at 100 Hz was linked to each sensor. To maximize the signal-to-noise ratio, the system included programmable signal conditioning with tunable gain and filtering. Hardware triggering was used to establish data synchronization, guaranteeing synchronous measurement across all channels.

Loading protocol

The experimental loading protocol comprised four sequential phases designed to replicate in-situ stress evolution during tunnel excavation:

Phase I: Initial stress state: K0 conditions (K0 = 0.5), which correspond to the pre-excavation stress field, are established by applying 0.5 MPa vertical pressure. To enable creep stability and stress redistribution, this phase was sustained for a whole day.

It should be noted that the experimental program adopts a hydrostatic stress condition (k₀ = 1.0) to isolate the influence of stress magnitude on support response. While this assumption enables controlled evaluation of deformation and constitutive behavior, it does not account for anisotropic in situ stress conditions that may induce shear-dominated failure mechanisms in practice. Therefore, the applicability of the results to strongly anisotropic stress environments should be interpreted with caution.

Deformation under different load.

The traditional rigid lining (Case 1) shows the largest settlement, reaching a maximum vertical deformation of about − 5.83 mm at a load of approximately 0.083–0.085 MPa (Fig. 11a). Cracking initiates at the invert at around 0.0375–0.0395 MPa and then propagates toward the sidewalls (Fig. 11b). This early cracking and concentrated deformation reflect the high stiffness and limited deformation capacity of rigid supports, which leads to stress concentration and brittle failure58. Such behavior is consistent with previous studies on rigid linings under high stress conditions.

Introducing a yielding interface significantly improves the response. In Case 2 (airbag limiter), deformation is clearly reduced and more evenly distributed (Fig. 11c). The maximum vertical settlement decreases to about − 3.82 mm, representing a reduction of roughly 35–38% compared to Case 1. The deformation curve is smooth and continuous, indicating stable load transfer and effective interaction between the surrounding rock, airbag layer, and lining. This behavior agrees with earlier findings that airbag systems provide a flexible, energy-absorbing interface that enhances stress redistribution and limits deformation9.

The CSB system (Case 3) shows comparable or slightly better performance. The maximum vertical deformation stabilizes between − 3.60 and − 3.70 mm (Fig. 11d). Design implications and engineering guidance

Applicability domains of support systems

Because of its high initial stiffness k0​, the rigid support system produces a high normalized pressure at very small convergence (less than about 1.5%). This allows rapid mobilization of support reaction, but it also limits the ability to accommodate deformation. As loading increases, stress concentration develops, leading to early cracking. For this reason, rigid support is most suitable for tunnels in relatively competent ground with low expected convergence.

At moderate convergence levels (about 1.5–3%), the airbag limiter regime becomes dominant. The low initial stiffness and relatively large yield displacement uy​ of the airbag system allow better deformation compatibility between the surrounding rock and the lining. In this stage, pressure is redistributed more effectively, and the normalized pressure either stabilizes or slightly decreases. This behavior reflects controlled yielding, reduced strain localization, and the high deformation capacity observed in experiments.

At higher convergence levels (greater than about 3–4%), the system transitions to the CSB limiter regime. Here, the response is governed by the large locking displacement uL​ and post-yield hardening stiffness kh​ of the CSB system. As a result, the normalized pressure begins to increase again, but in a gradual and controlled manner. Unlike rigid supports, this increase is accompanied by sustained energy dissipation and improved stability. This defines a high-deformation stability domain suitable for severe squeezing conditions.

Figure 29 illustrates a clear transition in support behavior: from stiffness-controlled response (rigid support), to deformation-controlled yielding (airbags), and finally to hardening-controlled stability (CSBs). This progression aligns with the staged mechanical response defined in the proposed unified constitutive model and provides practical guidance for performance-based tunnel support design.

It is derived directly from the experimental energy balance and normalized constitutive parameters. This plane establishes a constitutive admissibility boundary, over which controlled yielding with stable energy dissipation is attained and below which stiffness-dominated behavior causes early instability and strain localization. As a result, the criterion offers a quantitative differentiation between unstable, marginal, and stable yielding regimes that cannot be adequately described by criteria based solely on stiffness or strength.

The design implications are further highlighted by the placements of various support systems that have been empirically measured. The low-, low-kh/k0​ region is where conventional stiff supports cluster, indicating their limited deformation tolerance and propensity for brittle reaction. Because of their large early-stage deformation capacity and inadequate post-yield hardening, inflatable airbag limiters fall into an intermediate domain that explains their decreased stability at big convergence. By combining significant deformation accommodation with strong post-yield hardening and great energy dissipation efficiency, CSB systems, on the other hand, plot well over the stability boundary and provide sustained confinement under extreme squeezing circumstances.

These results show that the linked evolution of deformation, hardening, and energy dissipation, rather than any one parameter, controls stable tunnel support performance. In order to bridge the gap between laboratory-derived constitutive behavior and real tunnel support design, the YHDS provides a physically grounded design tool for choosing and optimizing yielding support systems based on predicted convergence demand and needed stability level.

Practical design recommendations

Specific recommendations are developed for the efficient deployment of yielding tunnel supports with resistance limiters in squeezing ground based on the integrated experimental, constitutive, and parametric findings of this work. The primary deformation control goal should guide the choice between inflatable airbag and circular steel bellows (CSB) systems. Because of their large deformation capacity envelope (Ω ≈ 0.46) and low initial stiffness (k0 = 0.18 kN/mm), airbag limiters are ideal for tunnels where early-stage deformation accommodation is crucial. Because of this, they are appropriate for applications requiring temporary support, heterogeneous ground requiring stress redistribution, and moderate squeezing conditions with projected convergence below 3%. On the other hand, CSB limiters should be specified in situations requiring strong energy dissipation (ψ ≈ 1.63) and robust post-yield confinement (kh = 0.68 kN/mm), such as in rockburst-prone locations or under high overburden pressures, or in severe squeezing conditions where convergence is anticipated to reach 4%. The Yield-Hardening Design Surface (YHDS) stability criterion (Γ = (kh/k0) ⋅ (u/uL) ⋅ ψ > 1.0) provides a key testing tool to assure regulated yielding and avoid premature locking or instability.

A methodical design and monitoring procedure is necessary to put these ideas into practice. Engineers should use the performance-based selection charts (Figs. 29–31) to determine the proper limiter system after calibrating the constitutive model using site-specific ground characteristics. Real-time monitoring of the strain uniformity index (Uε) and Support Adaptability Index (SAI) during construction enables adaptive management; actions like pressure adjustments or additional support should be triggered when performance deviates more than 20% from expectations. Moreover, yielding limiters must be incorporated into a comprehensive support system, frequently in conjunction with systematic bolting, in which the bolts regulate local rock mass behavior and the limiters oversee global convergence.

For long-term performance and durability, a strict quality control regime after installation is essential. This includes factory testing of limiter units, non-destructive installation verification, and routine inspections. Conclusions

From this experimental research the following conclusions are drawn,

Experimental validation confirms the three-stage constitutive model accurately represents yielding support behavior. The model’s piecewise force–displacement formulation characterized by initial stiffness k0​ (0.18–0.25 kN/mm for airbags, higher for rigid supports), hardening stiffness kh (0.42–0.68 kN/mm for CSBs), and locking displacement uL (3.5–3.8 mm) showed excellent agreement with measured data (R2 > 0.97). Airbag systems exhibited lower initial stiffness and larger yield displacement uy, while CSBs demonstrated stronger post-yield hardening.

Based on the experimental findings and model limitations, future research should focus on: (1) Time-dependent validation incorporating creep effects observed during Phase I loading stabilization (24-h hold periods); (2) Cyclic loading tests to assess performance under unloading–reloading conditions simulated in Phase III; (3) Scale effects investigation using the established 1:10 scaling relationships to validate prototype predictions.
