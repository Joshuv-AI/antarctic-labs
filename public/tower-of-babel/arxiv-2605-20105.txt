Optimal Representation Size: High-Dimensional Analysis of Pretraining and Linear Probing
Valentina Njaradi, Clémentine Dominé, Rachel Swanson, Marco Mondelli †, Andrew Saxe †

Abstract
Learning to generalise from limited data is a fundamental challenge for both artificial and biological systems. A common strategy is to extract reusable structure from abundant unlabelled data, enabling efficient adaptation to new tasks from limited labelled data. This two-stage paradigm is now standard in modern training pipelines, where pretraining is followed by fine-tuning or linear probing. We provide an analytical model of this process: structure extraction is formalized as principal component analysis on unlabelled data, and downstream learning as linear regression on a separate labelled dataset. In the high-dimensional regime, we derive exact expressions for training and generalisation error showcasing their dependence on representation dimensionality, unlabelled and labelled sample sizes, and task alignment. Our results show that pretrained representations strongly influence downstream generalisation, and we characterize the optimal representation size as a function of task parameters: with abundant pretraining data but scarce downstream data, maximally compressed representations are optimal, whereas with limited pretraining data, higher-dimensional representations generalise better. Furthermore, we establish an exact trade-off between pretraining and supervision, quantifying how much unlabelled data is required to replace a single labelled sample. Beyond our idealised model, we observe similar phenomenology in autoencoders and pretrained LLMs. Altogether, we highlight that optimising representation size is critical, giving conditions for when compression during pretraining improves generalisation.

Introduction

Large language models, vision transformers, and related architectures have demonstrated language, reasoning and mathematical capabilities widely considered out of reach just a decade ago
[
65
,
41
,
90
,
44
]
. This progress has been made possible by multi-stage training pipelines capable of leveraging massive amounts of unlabelled data:
models are first pretrained on a large corpus of unlabelled data, then fine-tuned on labelled tasks and finally refined, e.g., via reinforcement learning from human feedback
[
26
,
66
]
, low-rank adaptation
[
46
]
or test-time training
[
82
,
58
,
3
]
. Such a paradigm unlocks remarkable sample efficiency, as large pretrained models generalise on new tasks where they have access only to thousands (or even hundreds) of samples
[
2
,
45
]
.

At their core, these training pipelines rely on a key fact: pretraining on large datasets yields rich representations that effectively transfer to downstream tasks. This idea is not new, as greedy layer-wise training and stacked autoencoders were designed to learn reusable representations in pretraining a decade before modern transformers
[
18
,
33
,
89
]
. However, different pretraining objectives emphasize different aspects of the data, and it remains unclear which features of a representation are most useful for downstream learning.

Characterizing these representations, how they support computation, transfer to new tasks and enable adaptive behaviour in artificial and biological systems, has been a longstanding focus in machine learning, as well as cognitive science and neuroscience
[
17
,
60
,
35
]
. In machine learning, structured representations and prior knowledge can accelerate learning and enhance generalisation, but may also hinder adaptation, as exemplified by catastrophic interference in continual and reversal learning
[
68
,
54
,
96
,
25
]
. Similar trade-offs arise in biological systems, where representations must support flexible and efficient adaptation
[
10
]
. A crucial property underlying these effects is the size (or dimensionality) of representations. Both high and low-dimensional representations have been observed across a range of brain regions and tasks
[
24
,
27
,
62
,
64
]
as well as in machine learning models
[
2
,
87
]
, yet a principled account of how dimensionality interacts with task and environment structure to shape learning performance and sample efficiency remains missing.

Here, we study a two-stage learning setting in which representations are first learned via principal component analysis (PCA) from unlabelled samples drawn from a spiked covariance model, and then reused by linear regression on an independent labelled dataset. The covariance structure plays a central role: it produces both a tractable model for analysis and controlled alignment between the pretraining and supervised training stages, thus enabling us to isolate how pretrained representations influence downstream generalisation. More precisely, our contributions are as follows:

•

We derive exact asymptotic expressions for estimation, generalisation and training error (Theorem
1
,
2
and
3
, respectively). From this, we establish the improvement in generalisation coming from tuning the representation size, as well as the improvement due to adding more pretraining and finetuning data. For optimally-tuned errors, we identify regions where unlabelled samples are more valuable than labelled samples (Figure
2
).

•

We characterize when either
(i)
low-dimensional representations are useful for generalisation on downstream tasks, or
(ii)
tasks benefit from detailed
high-dimensional representations (Figure
3
). In particular, we identify phase transitions where compression becomes useful (Corollaries
1
,
2
), and describe their behaviour for an infinite number of pretraining samples (Corollary

).

•

We finally show that the theoretical predictions derived in our idealized setting extend to trained autoencoders when the number of samples is larger than the number of features, and further demonstrate that the insights unveiled by our theory persist in more realistic settings through linear probing of pretrained transformers (Figure

Related work

High-dimensional regression.
High-dimensional regression, in the regime where number of features and samples grow proportionally, has been the subject of intense investigation, leading to the precise characterization of the test error
[
32
,
85
,
75
,
92
]
and phenomena such as benign overfitting
[
15
]
and double descent
[
16
,
1
]
.
This line of work further characterizes the distribution of the empirical risk minimiser
[
43
]
, the impact of spurious correlations
[
22
]
, performative learning
[
29
]
, as well as training on multiple or surrogate data sources
[
74
,
47
,
55
,
48
]
.
Principal component regression (PCR), where regression is performed on principal components (PCs) of the sample covariance, provides a natural way to restrict regression to a low-dimensional subspace. Its performance depends critically on the number of retained components, which governs the bias–variance trade-off
[
52
,
93
,
42
,
61
]
, and has been characterized both non-asymptotically
[
31
]
and in proportional asymptotics for population and sample PCs
[
95
,
93
,
42
]
. In contrast, we study pretrained regression (PR), with PCs formed during a pretraining phase and reused for a downstream task, separating the sample sets for PCA and subsequent regression. As a result, both the asymptotic risk and the optimal number of principal components for PR differ fundamentally from the classical PCR setting.

Pretraining and transfer learning.

More broadly, the reuse of pretrained representations across tasks has been extensively studied in transfer learning, primarily in supervised settings where generalisation depends on task similarity or alignment with pretrained features
[
67
,
98
,
39
,
56
,
83
,
94
,
79
]
. These approaches typically do not model representations learned from unlabelled data. Complementary work establishes benefits of unsupervised and self-supervised pretraining
[
57
,
7
,
6
,
97
]
. Closest to our paper, recent work analyses PCA-based pretraining in spiked models
[
53
]
, but considers fixed representations without addressing the role of representation dimensionality in generalisation.

Representation dimensionality.

A growing body of work highlights that pretrained representations often lie in low-dimensional subspaces, as evidenced by studies of intrinsic dimensionality
[
2
,
87
]
, low-rank adaptation
[
46
]
, and linear probing
[
4
]
. Recent theoretical work has begun to formalise aspects of this structure
[
91
,
5
]
. While these papers study the structure of pretrained features and how they support downstream prediction, they do not provide a closed-form characterisation of how the optimal retained dimension depends jointly on pretraining, downstream data, and task structure. Addressing this question through an analytically tractable theory is the focus of our work.

Spiked covariance models.

The spiked covariance model
[
51
]
provides a tractable framework for studying low-dimensional structure in high-dimensional data. A key feature is the BBP phase transition
[
50
,
59
,
11
]
: above this threshold, empirical principal components align with the population spike, while below it they are asymptotically uninformative
[
50
,
13
,
69
,
12
]
. This transition makes the spiked models well-suited for analysing how representation quality depends on sample size and signal strength. Accordingly, they have been used to analyse kernel methods, neural networks and feature learning
[

,
40
,
63
]
, as well as the emergence of low-rank structure during training
[
8
,
28
,
30
,
80
]
. Finally, spiked covariances have been
used to analyse PCR, showing how the projection onto empirical PCs has a regularising effect
[
37
]
. Our setting differs from these works: the signal subspace is estimated from an independent unlabelled pretraining sample and then used for a separate labelled task, allowing us to characterise the optimal representation dimension.

Setup

We study two-stage learning with unlabelled pretraining followed by supervised learning:

1.

In the pretraining stage, we observe inputs
\mathbf{X}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}\times p}
whose rows are i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma})
, forming the
unlabelled
dataset
\mathcal{D}_{u}
. From this data, the model extracts a representation via PCA: it computes the top
m
principal components of
\mathbf{X}_{u}
, stacks them into
\mathbf{U}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times m}
, and defines a projection of retained directions
\mathcal{P}_{m}=\mathbf{U}_{m}\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}
with a discarded complement
\mathcal{P}_{\perp}=I-\mathcal{P}_{m}=\mathbf{U}_{\perp}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}
. If
m>n_{u}
, the PCA subspace is completed with uniformly random orthonormal vectors.

2.

In the downstream stage, we observe a
labelled
dataset
\mathcal{D}_{l}=\{\mathbf{X}_{l},\mathbf{y}_{l}\}
, where
\mathbf{y}_{l}=\mathbf{X}_{l}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}+\mathbf{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}}
, the rows of
\mathbf{X}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}\times p}
are i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma})
and independent of
\mathbf{X}_{u}
,
{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the ground-truth signal and
\mathbf{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}}
is Gaussian noise with i.i.d. entries of variance
\sigma^{2}
.

The pretrained regression model uses the representation learned from
\mathcal{D}_{u}
to fit the downstream task by regressing on the projected inputs (see Figure
1
(a)
for a schematic representation):

{\mathbf{\hat{w}}}=(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}_{l}=(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{l}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}+(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{\xi},

(1)

where
(\cdot)^{\dagger}
denotes the pseudoinverse. We denote by
\Pi
the projection onto the row space of
\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m}
, i.e., the subspace spanned by the projected training inputs. This setup follows
[
42
]
, with the key difference that the projection subspace is learned from independent unlabelled data rather than the downstream dataset itself.
The quantities of interest are estimation, generalisation and training error:

\displaystyle E^{\mathrm{est}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\xi}}\!\left[\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-{\mathbf{\hat{w}}}\|^{2}\right],\quad E^{\mathrm{gen}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},y,\mathbf{\xi}}\!\left[(y-\mathbf{x}^{\top}{\mathbf{\hat{w}}})^{2}\right],\quad E^{\mathrm{train}}=\frac{1}{n_{l}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\xi}}\!\left[\|\mathbf{y}_{l}-\mathbf{X}_{l}{\mathbf{\hat{w}}}\|^{2}\right]

where the test point
(\mathbf{x},y)
in
E^{\mathrm{gen}}
is drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{new}})
.
We work in the high-dimensional regime where
p/n_{l}\to\gamma_{l}\in(0,\infty)
,
p/n_{u}\to\gamma_{u}\in(0,\infty)
,
m/p\to\alpha\in[0,1]
with
\alpha
the fraction of dimensions to keep, and
\gamma_{u},\gamma_{l}
the aspect ratios for the datasets
\mathcal{D}_{u},\mathcal{D}_{l}
.
Due to the projection of the downstream inputs, we define the effective aspect ratio
\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}:=\frac{m}{n_{l}}\to\alpha\gamma_{l}
and assume
\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}\neq 1
.

The population covariance follows a rank-one spiked model
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{I}_{p}+(\lambda-1)\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\top}
, where
\lambda>1
and the unit vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are fixed. The alignment of
{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}
with the spike eigenvector of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is captured by
({\mathbf{w^{\star}}}^{\top}\mathbf{v})^{2}/\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}\to\eta
, and the mass spectrum of
{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}
on the eigenbasis of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is
\eta\delta_{\lambda}+(1-\eta)\delta_{1}
. The test population covariance follows a spiked model with
T
spike directions
\{\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}\}_{i=1}^{T}
and eigenvalues
\{\nu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{new}}=\mathbf{I}_{p}+\sum_{i=1}^{T}(\nu_{i}-1)\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}^{\top}
.

. We further study how

Theorem 1

(Asymptotic estimation error)
.

As
n_{l},n_{u},p,m\to\infty
with
p/n_{l}\to\gamma_{l}
,

,
m/p\to\alpha

\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}\neq 1

(3)

where
\bar{w}^{\ast}
is the deterministic limit of

,
\bar{\lambda}=1+(\lambda-1)(1-\bar{p}_{\perp,\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}})

\bar{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\bar{p}_{\perp,{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}}+\frac{\lambda-1}{\bar{\lambda}}\bar{p}_{\perp,{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}}^{2}.

The expression in (
3

{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}
through the subspace induced by
\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m}
, and the error reflects how
{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}
decomposes relative to this subspace. By decomposing into two components, aligned or orthogonal to
\mathbf{U}_{m}
,
{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}={\mathbf{w^{\star}}}_{\parallel}+{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}_{\perp}
, the error splits into three contributions:
(a)
the
missing signal

(b)
the
leaked signal
, and
(c)
the
variance
term.

(a)

The term
1-\bar{\pi}_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}}
captures the
missing signal
, i.e. directions that lie in the retained subspace but cannot be reliably estimated from finite samples.

(b)

The key non-trivial effect is the
leak
. In an isotropic model, the components
\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m}
and
\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{\perp}
are independent, so
{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}_{\perp}
is completely invisible to the estimator. Here, the spike in the covariance couples these two parts of the data, so directions outside the retained subspace become correlated with it. As a result, part of
{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}_{\perp}
leaks into
\Pi

\mathbf{v}
, producing the bias term
\frac{(\lambda-1)^{2}}{\bar{\lambda}^{2}}\bar{p}_{\perp,{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}}^{2}\,\bar{\pi}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}}

6
in Appendix
B

(c)

The remaining part of
{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}_{\perp}
does not couple back into the learned subspace.
Together with label noise, this contributes to the
variance
, which exhibits a double descent
[
16
]
controlled by
\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}=\alpha\gamma_{l}

Theorem 2

(Asymptotic generalisation error)
.

Under the same regime, let
\rho_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}}
be the deterministic limit of
\frac{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}}{\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|}
. The generalisation error converges a.s. to

E^{\mathrm{gen}}_{\infty}=E^{\mathrm{est}}_{\infty}+\bar{w}^{\ast}\sum_{i=1}^{T}(\nu_{i}-1)\left(\underbrace{\rho_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}}-\bar{\pi}_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}}}_{\text{missing signal}}-\underbrace{\frac{\lambda-1}{\bar{\lambda}}\bar{p}_{\perp,{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}}\,\bar{\pi}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}}}_{\text{leaked signal}}\right)^{2}+\sigma^{2}.

The calculation of the generalisation error builds on that of the estimation error, with an additional contribution measuring how the prediction error
{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-{\mathbf{\hat{w}}}
projects onto the spikes
\{\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}\}_{i=1}^{T}
in the test population covariance. Each direction contributes proportionally to its strength
(\nu_{i}-1)
. For each
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}
, the error depends on the mismatch between the true alignment
\rho_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}}
and the alignment recovered by the estimator. As in the estimation error, this mismatch decomposes into a
missing signal
term and a
leaked signal
term, arising from finite-sample limitations and the coupling induced by the spike. The final
\sigma^{2}
term reflects the irreducible error due to label noise.
For additional discussions and a breakdown of different terms in the generalisation error, see Figure
7
in Appendix
B

Theorem 3

(Asymptotic training error)
.

Under the same regime, the training error converges a.s. to

E^{\mathrm{train}}_{\infty}=\bigl(\bar{w}^{\ast}\bar{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathrm{eff}}+\sigma^{2}\bigr)\max\!\left(0,\,1-\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}\right).

This expression has the standard interpolation form. When

In this section, we evaluate the extent to which theoretical predictions are valid beyond the idealised setting employed in their derivation. We first consider a controlled synthetic experiment in which the representation learned by an autoencoder approximates PCA. We then relax the assumptions of linearity and spiked isotropic covariance, allowing for a more general covariance structure. Finally, we show that our predictions extend to representations learned by modern neural networks.

5.1
Autoencoders as learned representations

We train the autoencoder on a pretraining task and use the learned representations for regression on downstream tasks. This approach is motivated by the fact that, in several regimes, autoencoders with a bottleneck dimension
m
recover the subspace spanned by the top-
m
principal components of the pretraining data
[
73
,
14
,
70
,
23
]
. We discuss below the regimes under which this correspondence holds.

Spiked isotropic covariance.
We first train autoencoders on the spiked identity covariance structures considered in the theory. For linear autoencoders, the optimal bottleneck size, defined as the value minimising the generalisation error on a downstream task, closely follows our theoretical results (compare Figure
4
(a)
with Figure
3
(a)
). We highlight that the boundaries of the phase transitions in the heatmaps precisely match the predictions from Section
4.2
. Nonlinear autoencoders exhibit the same qualitative behaviour, except when the number of pretraining samples is small, see Figure
4
(b)
. This discrepancy is explained by a data-dependent transition: both linear and nonlinear autoencoders recover PCA only once sufficient pretraining data is available, see Figure
15
in Appendix
C.2
. In the low-sample regime, autoencoders instead tend to memorise training samples
[
34
,
49
,
71
]
. Despite this, when
n_{u}<p
, higher-dimensional representations still yield lower errors on downstream tasks.

General covariance structure.

For a spiked general covariance, the same qualitative behaviour persists. In fact, while phase boundaries shift, the overall structure is preserved: no compression when pretraining data is limited; small bottlenecks for large pretraining and small downstream datasets; and intermediate-sized bottlenecks for large pretraining datasets. This is illustrated in Figure
4
(c,d)

13
in Appendix
C.2
. Furthermore, when optimising for training error, the results closely match the spiked identity case (Figure

in Appendix
C.2
).
At lower signal-to-noise ratios, the regime where compression is optimal expands (Figure
14
in Appendix
C.2
), in agreement with the theoretical analysis. Nonlinear autoencoders again exhibit deviations from PCA at low pretraining sample sizes.

Overall, our empirical results show that the theoretical predictions derived in the idealised setting extend to trained linear and nonlinear autoencoders when enough pretraining data is available, and that the qualitative phase structure persists under general covariance spectra.

5.2
Linear probing transformers

We next examine whether representations learned by modern architectures exhibit similar dimensionality effects under linear probing. We use models from the Pythia family
[
21
]
, which provide access to intermediate checkpoints throughout pretraining. As these models are trained for
\sim
1.5 epochs on the Pile dataset
[
36
,
20
]
, the training step serves as a proxy for the number of pretraining samples
n_{u}
. The learned representations exhibit a spiked covariance structure, with a clear bulk and a small number of outlying eigenvalues (Figure
4
(f)
), and the spikes become more pronounced as training progresses, see Figure
18
in Appendix
C.3
. For each checkpoint, we project representations onto the top-
m
principal components and train linear probes on a downstream sentiment classification task (SST-2 from GLUE
[
90
]
), selecting

to optimise validation or training accuracy.

The dependence on dataset size is consistent with theoretical predictions, see Figure
4
(e)
. For small downstream datasets, models that are pretrained longer benefit from stronger compression, see Figure

in Appendix

in Appendix

in Appendix

.

In summary, these results show that real-world representations exhibit a theoretically predictable qualitative phase structure when optimising dimensionality for downstream generalisation.

6
Discussion

We present a theoretical model quantifying how representation dimensionality affects training and generalisation error on downstream tasks. We demonstrate that
(i)
tuning this dimensionality significantly improves generalisation compared to full representations or naive fixed dimensionality reduction, and that
(ii)
the optimal dimensionality is determined by the amount of pretraining and downstream data, the task signal-to-noise ratio, the strength of structure in the inputs, and the alignment between task-relevant signals and dominant covariance directions.
In particular, when pretraining data is abundant and downstream data is limited, the optimal representations are maximally compressed. Furthermore, we unveil a trade-off between pretraining and supervision, identifying regimes where unlabelled data yields larger gains than labelled samples.
These results provide a principled explanation for why large models trained on extensive data and exhibiting low intrinsic dimensionality
[
2
]
, are especially effective for fine-tuning and linear probing.

The question of optimal representations is central to machine learning as well as neuroscience, where internal representations support categorisation, reasoning, planning, navigation, and decision-making
[
81
,
38
,
78
]
. A key property of these representations is their dimensionality. High-dimensional representations may support flexible readout across tasks
[
72
,
84
,
76
,
19
]
, whereas low-dimensional representations are associated with abstraction, efficient coding, or structured prior knowledge
[
86
,
64
]
. Our results provide a quantitative characterization of how task structure and data availability determine the optimal representation tradeoff in a simplified setting, offering predictions for how biological systems might organize representations for effective learning.

Several limitations motivate future work: relaxing the shared-spike assumption to capture data mismatch, extending the analysis to ridge-regularised learning, and considering richer covariance models. Another important direction is to replace the closed-form regression estimator with gradient-based training, enabling the study of optimisation-induced bias and its dependence on initialisation from pretraining. Finally, extending the framework beyond PCA-based projections to learned feature representations would allow for a closer connection to empirical observations in deep networks.

For each

\displaystyle f_{\gamma}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\lambda_{+}

The key idea of each proof is to decompose the model’s estimator

{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}
outside the relevant subspace, and two variance terms arising from noise and model mismatch. The Gaussian structure of the data allows us to express the component of
\mathbf{X}_{l}
orthogonal to the prior subspace

\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathbf{U}_{m}

Conditional Gaussian decomposition under prior subspace projections.

Let

Then, conditioning on
\mathbf{U}_{m}

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}&\mathbf{\Sigma}_{m\perp}\\
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp m}&\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp\perp}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{U}_{m}&\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}\\
\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{U}_{m}&\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}.

Moreover, due to the conditional distribution,
\mathbf{X}_{\perp}

\mathbf{X}_{m}

\mathbf{X}_{\perp}=\mathbf{X}_{m}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{m\perp}+\mathbf{E},

(6)

where

and its rows are i.i.d. with distribution

Under the spiked covariance model of Assumption

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}^{-1}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}_{\perp}}_{\text{variance from model mismatch}}+\underbrace{(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\xi}_{\text{variance from label noise}},

\mathbf{X}_{m}
and an independent noise matrix
\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}\times(p-m)}
(see Section
A.2
). We then average over
\mathbf{E}
conditioned on
\mathbf{X}_{m}
and
\mathbf{U}_{m}
, then average over
\mathbf{X}_{m}
conditioned on
\mathbf{U}_{m}
, and finally over
\mathbf{U}_{m}
.

Step 1: Decomposition of the error.

The estimation error splits into bias and variance:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-{\mathbf{\hat{w}}}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\left\|\left(\mathbf{I}-(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{l}\right){\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\right\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\xi^{\top}(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger\top}(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\xi\right]

\displaystyle-2\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\xi^{\top}(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger\top}\left(\mathbf{I}-(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{l}\right){\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\left\|\left(\mathbf{I}-(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{l}\right){\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\right\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\left[\xi\xi^{\top}(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger\top}(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\left\|\left(\mathbf{I}-(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{l}\right){\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\right\|^{2}+\sigma^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{X}_{l}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\right].

Substituting the result in (
13
) further decomposes the bias:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}}\left\|\left(\mathbf{I}-(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{l}\right){\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}}\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-\Pi{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-A\Pi\mathbf{v}-(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}}\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-\Pi{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-A\Pi\mathbf{v}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\bar{w}^{\ast}}\left\|(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle-2\underbrace{\frac{1}{\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}}({\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-\Pi{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-A\Pi\mathbf{v})^{\top}(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}}_{\text{cross term }T}.

Step 2:
\mathbf{E}
-independent term.

The first term
\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-\Pi{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-A\Pi\mathbf{v}\|^{2}
does not depend on
\mathbf{E}
, so its conditional expectation on
\mathbf{E}
is itself. We expand using the property of the projection matrix
\Pi
that
\Pi^{2}=\Pi
:

\frac{1}{\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}}\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-\Pi{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-A\Pi\mathbf{v}\|^{2}=1-\frac{\|\Pi{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}}{\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}}+\frac{A^{2}}{\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}}\|\Pi\mathbf{v}\|^{2},

(14)

where the cross term
-2A{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}^{\top}\Pi\mathbf{v}+2A{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}^{\top}\Pi\Pi\mathbf{v}=0
. Substituting
A=\frac{\lambda-1}{\tilde{\lambda}}(\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathcal{P}_{\perp}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}})
gives

1-\frac{\|\Pi{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}}{\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}}+\frac{(\lambda-1)^{2}}{\tilde{\lambda}^{2}}\frac{(\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathcal{P}_{\perp}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}})^{2}}{\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}}\|\Pi\mathbf{v}\|^{2}\Rightarrow 1-\bar{\pi}_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}}+\frac{(\lambda-1)^{2}}{\bar{\lambda}^{2}}\bar{p}_{\perp,{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}}^{2}\bar{\pi}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}}\,\,\text{almost surely},

(15)

which matches the first two terms of the bias. The projections
\frac{\|\Pi{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}}{\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}}
and
\|\Pi\mathbf{v}\|^{2}
converge almost surely to
\bar{\pi}_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}},\bar{\pi}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}}
in Equation (
39
) according to Lemma
6
. The quantity
\frac{\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathcal{P}_{\perp}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}}{\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|}
converges almost surely to
\bar{p}_{\perp,{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}}
in Equation (
34
) according to Lemma
5
, and
\tilde{\lambda}=1+(\lambda-1)\|\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{v}\|^{2}
converges to
\bar{\lambda}
almost surely by the continuous mapping theorem and the almost sure convergence of
\|\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{v}\|^{2}\to\bar{p}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}}
from Equation (
30
) (see Lemma
4
). By the continuous mapping theorem, the combined expression in (
15
) converges almost surely.

Step 3: Cross term linear in
\mathbf{E}
.

We write the cross term as

T=\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{d},

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|}(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger\top}({\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-\Pi{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-A\Pi\mathbf{v})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-m}.

Conditioned on
(\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{U}_{m})
, the vectors
\mathbf{c}
and
\mathbf{d}
are deterministic and independent of
\mathbf{E}
. Since the rows of
\mathbf{E}
are i.i.d. Gaussian with covariance
\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
, it follows that

T\mid(\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{U}_{m})\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\,(\mathbf{d}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbf{d})\,\|\mathbf{c}\|^{2}\right).

We now bound the variance. First,

\mathbf{d}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbf{d}=\frac{\|\mathcal{P}_{\perp}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}}{\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}}+\frac{\lambda-1}{\tilde{\lambda}}\frac{({\mathbf{w^{\star}}}^{\top}\mathcal{P}_{\perp}\mathbf{v})^{2}}{\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}},

which by the almost sure limits of
\|\mathcal{P}_{\perp}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}
,
\tilde{\lambda}
, and
{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}^{\top}\mathcal{P}_{\perp}\mathbf{v}
, converges almost surely to

\bar{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\bar{p}_{\perp,{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}}+\frac{\lambda-1}{\bar{\lambda}}\bar{p}_{\perp,{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}}^{2}.

In particular, there exists a deterministic constant
C_{1}
such that for all sufficiently large
p
,

\mathbf{d}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbf{d}\leq C_{1}\quad\text{almost surely.}

It therefore remains to control
\|\mathbf{c}\|^{2}
. We have

\|\mathbf{c}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}}\frac{\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-\Pi{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-A\Pi\mathbf{v}\|^{2}}{\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{X}_{m})^{2}}.

By Step 2, the numerator is bounded almost surely for all sufficiently large
p
. For the denominator, writing
\mathbf{X}_{m}=\mathbf{G}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}^{1/2}
(see Lemma
1
) gives

\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{X}_{m})\geq\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{G})\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}^{1/2}).

Note that
\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}^{1/2})=\min\{1,\sqrt{\tilde{\lambda}}\}
for any
p
. By the singular value inequality for Gaussian random matrices (see e.g. Exercise 7.13 in
[
88
]
), for all
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{G})\leq|\sqrt{n_{l}}-\sqrt{m}|-t\right)\leq 2e^{-t^{2}/2}.

Since
\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}=m/n_{l}\neq 1
, we have

|\sqrt{n_{l}}-\sqrt{m}|=\sqrt{p}\frac{|1-\sqrt{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}}|}{\sqrt{\gamma_{l}}}=\sqrt{p}\,c_{\gamma}.

Choosing
t=\tfrac{1}{2}c_{\gamma}\sqrt{p}
and setting
c=c_{\gamma}^{2}/8
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{G})\leq\frac{1}{2}c_{\gamma}\sqrt{p}\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{c_{\gamma}^{2}}{8}p\right).

Noting that
\tilde{\lambda}\leq\lambda
, and setting
a_{\gamma}:=\frac{1}{4}c_{\gamma}^{2}\min\{1,\lambda\}
,
b_{\gamma}:=\frac{1}{8}c_{\gamma}^{2}
,
we have

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{X}_{m})^{2}\leq a_{\gamma}p\right)\leq 2e^{-b_{\gamma}p}.

Therefore, there exists
C_{2}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\|\mathbf{c}\|^{2}\leq\frac{C_{2}}{p}\right)\geq 1-2e^{-b_{\gamma}p}.

Define the event

\mathcal{E}_{p}:=\left\{\|\mathbf{c}\|^{2}\leq\frac{C_{2}}{p}\right\}.

Then

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{p}^{c})\leq 2e^{-b_{\gamma}p}.

For any
\delta>0
,

\mathbb{P}(|T|\geq\delta)=\mathbb{P}(|T|\geq\delta,\mathcal{E}_{p}^{c})+\mathbb{P}(|T|\geq\delta,\mathcal{E}_{p})\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{p}^{c})+\mathbb{P}(|T|\geq\delta,\mathcal{E}_{p}).

Noting that there exists
C_{1}
s.t.
\mathbf{d}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbf{d}\leq C_{1}
for any large
p
, on
\mathcal{E}_{p}
,

\mathrm{Var}(T\mid\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{U}_{m})\leq\frac{C_{1}C_{2}}{p}.

Thus, on
\mathcal{E}_{p}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\!\left(|T|\geq\delta\mid\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{U}_{m}\right)\leq 2\exp(-c_{\delta}p),

and since this holds for any
\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{U}_{m}
, by the law of total probability, we have

\mathbb{P}(|T|\geq\delta,\mathcal{E}_{p})\leq 2e^{-c_{\delta}p}.

Therefore,

\mathbb{P}(|T|\geq\delta)\leq 2e^{-b_{\gamma}p}+2e^{-c_{\delta}p}=Ce^{-cp}.

Since
\sum_{p}Ce^{-cp}<\infty
, the Borel–Cantelli lemma implies

T\xrightarrow{a.s.}0.

Step 4: Average of the trace terms.

The variance term contains

\mathrm{Tr}[(\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{X}_{l}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}]=\text{Tr}[(\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{m})^{\dagger}].

We note that the term
\mathrm{Tr}[(\mathbf{X}_{m}\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top})^{\dagger}]
is equivalent to the above, since the non-zero eigenvalues of
\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{m}
and
\mathbf{X}_{m}\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top}
are the same. By Lemma
2
, the trace term has a limiting value
\frac{\min\{1,\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}\}}{|\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1|}
almost surely.

Step 5: Quadratic term in
\mathbf{E}
.

Define

Q:={\mathbf{w^{\star}}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}\mathbf{E}^{\top}(\mathbf{X}_{m}\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top})^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}.

Let

\mathbf{a}:=\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\qquad\mathbf{A}:=(\mathbf{X}_{m}\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top})^{\dagger}.

Conditioned on
(\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{U}_{m})
, the vector
\mathbf{a}
and the matrix
\mathbf{A}

are fixed and independent of
\mathbf{E}
. Since the rows of
\mathbf{E}
are i.i.d. Gaussian with covariance
\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
, the vector

\mathbf{c}:=\mathbf{E}\mathbf{a}

is Gaussian with

\mathbf{c}\mid(\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{U}_{m})\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\,(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbf{a})\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}}\right).

Therefore

Q=\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{c}.

First, the conditional expectation is

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{E}}[Q\mid\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{U}_{m}]=(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbf{a})\text{Tr}(\mathbf{A}).

Here

\mathbf{a}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbf{a}={\mathbf{w^{\star}}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}=\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}.

By the deterministic equivalents used above,

\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\bar{w}^{\ast}\bar{\sigma}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}.

Moreover, by the pseudo-inverse trace computation in Step 4,

\text{Tr}\!\left[(\mathbf{X}_{m}\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top})^{\dagger}\right]\xrightarrow{a.s.}\frac{\min\{1,\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}\}}{|\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1|}.

Thus

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{E}}[Q\mid\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{U}_{m}]\xrightarrow{a.s.}\bar{w}^{\ast}\bar{\sigma}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\frac{\min\{1,\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}\}}{|\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1|}.

It remains to show concentration around this conditional expectation. Write

\mathbf{c}=\sqrt{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbf{a}}\;\mathbf{z},\qquad\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}}),

conditionally on
(\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{U}_{m})
. Then

Q-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{E}}[Q\mid\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{U}_{m}]=(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbf{a})\left(\mathbf{z}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}-\text{Tr}(\mathbf{A})\right).

By the Hanson–Wright inequality, for every fixed
\delta>0
,

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{E}}\!\left(\left|Q-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{E}}[Q\mid\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{U}_{m}]\right|\geq\delta\mid\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{U}_{m}\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left[-c\min\left(\frac{\delta^{2}}{(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbf{a})^{2}\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}^{2}},\frac{\delta}{(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbf{a})\|\mathbf{A}\|}\right)\right].

We now bound the quantities appearing in this inequality. As above,

\mathbf{a}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbf{a}=\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}

is bounded for all sufficiently large
p
almost surely. Furthermore, since

\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}\neq 1
, the nonzero spectrum of
\frac{1}{n_{l}}\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{m}
is bounded away from zero almost surely. Equivalently, the nonzero singular values of
\mathbf{X}_{m}
are of order
\sqrt{p}
(see argument in Step 3). Hence

\|\mathbf{A}\|=\|(\mathbf{X}_{m}\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top})^{\dagger}\|=O\!\left(\frac{1}{p}\right),

and

\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i:\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{X}_{m})>0}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{X}_{m})^{4}}=O\!\left(\frac{1}{p}\right).

Consequently, for all sufficiently large
p
,

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{E}}\!\left(\left|Q-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{E}}[Q\mid\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{U}_{m}]\right|\geq\delta\mid\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{U}_{m}\right)\leq 2e^{-c_{\delta}p}.

The right-hand side is summable in
p
, so Borel–Cantelli gives

Q-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{E}}[Q\mid\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{U}_{m}]\xrightarrow{a.s.}0.

Combining this concentration statement with the limit of the conditional
expectation yields

Q\xrightarrow{a.s.}\bar{w}^{\ast}\bar{\sigma}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\frac{\min\{1,\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}\}}{|\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1|}.

Combining.

Summing the contributions from Steps 1–5 and applying the continuity mapping theorem concludes the proof.

∎

A.4
Proof for the generalisation error

Proof of Theorem
2
.

We decompose the error and take averages over
\mathbf{E}
,
\mathbf{X}_{m}
,
\mathbf{U}_{m}
.

Step 1: Decomposition of the generalisation error.

We start from the definition of the generalisation error

E^{\mathrm{gen}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},y,\xi}\!\left[\|y-\mathbf{x}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{w}}\|^{2}\mid\mathbf{w}^{\star},\mathbf{X}_{l},\mathbf{X}_{u}\right].

(16)

Using the data model
y=\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{w}^{\star}+\xi
and independence of the test noise, this becomes

\displaystyle E^{\mathrm{gen}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\xi}\!\left[(\mathbf{w}^{\star}-\hat{\mathbf{w}})^{\top}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}(\mathbf{w}^{\star}-\hat{\mathbf{w}})\mid\mathbf{w}^{\star},\mathbf{X}_{l},\mathbf{X}_{u}\right]+\sigma^{2}

(17)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\!\left[(\mathbf{w}^{\star}-\hat{\mathbf{w}})^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}(\mathbf{w}^{\star}-\hat{\mathbf{w}})\right]+\sigma^{2}.

Using the spiked covariance model
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{new}}=\mathbf{I}_{p}+\sum_{i}(\nu_{i}-1)\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}^{\top}
, we obtain

E^{\mathrm{gen}}=E^{\mathrm{est}}+\sum_{i}(\nu_{i}-1)\,\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\!\left[\left((\mathbf{w}^{\star}-\hat{\mathbf{w}})^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}\right)^{2}\right]+\sigma^{2}.

(18)

Substituting
\hat{\mathbf{w}}=(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathbf{w}^{\star}+(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\xi
and expanding the quadratic form, the cross term vanishes in
\xi
expectation and we obtain a bias contribution

\left(({\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{l}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}})^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}\right)^{2}

(19)

and a variance contribution proportional to

\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}^{\top}(\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{X}_{l}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}.

(20)

Using the same decomposition and conditional Gaussian argument as in Equation (
13
),
(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{l}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}=\Pi{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}+A\Pi\mathbf{v}+(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}_{\perp}
, we further decompose the bias

({\mathbf{w^{\star}}}^{\top}-(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{l}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}})^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}=\mathbf{w}^{\star}{}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}-\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}^{\top}\Pi{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-A\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}^{\top}\Pi\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}^{\top}(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}_{\perp},

(21)

with the squared term being:

\displaystyle\underbrace{({\mathbf{w^{\star}}}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}-\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}^{\top}\Pi{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-\frac{\lambda-1}{\tilde{\lambda}}({\mathbf{w^{\star}}}^{\top}\mathcal{P}_{\perp}\mathbf{v})(\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}^{\top}\Pi\mathbf{v}))^{2}}_{\mathbf{E}\text{--independent}}+\underbrace{(\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}^{\top}(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}_{\perp})^{2}}_{\text{quadratic in }\mathbf{E}}

\displaystyle-2\underbrace{({\mathbf{w^{\star}}}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}-\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}^{\top}\Pi{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-\frac{\lambda-1}{\tilde{\lambda}}({\mathbf{w^{\star}}}^{\top}\mathcal{P}_{\perp}\mathbf{v})(\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}^{\top}\Pi\mathbf{v}))(\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}^{\top}(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}_{\perp})}_{\text{cross term}}.

Step 2:
\mathbf{E}
-independent term.

The term linear in
\mathbf{E}
is made up of terms that have almost sure limits. In particular:

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}^{\top}\Pi{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\sqrt{\bar{w}^{\ast}}\bar{\pi}_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}}\qquad

by Lemma
7

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}^{\top}\Pi\mathbf{v}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\bar{\pi}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}}\qquad

by Lemma
7

\displaystyle{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}^{\top}\mathcal{P}_{\perp}\mathbf{v}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\sqrt{\bar{w}^{\ast}}\bar{p}_{\perp,{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}}\qquad

by Lemma
5

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\bar{\lambda}\qquad

by Lemma
4

\displaystyle{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\sqrt{\bar{w}^{\ast}}\rho_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}}\qquad

by model assumptions

Thus, by the continuous mapping theorem, we have the almost sure convergence of the entire
\mathbf{E}
-independent term to

\bar{w}^{\ast}\left(\rho_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}}-\bar{\pi}_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}}-\frac{\lambda-1}{\bar{\lambda}}\bar{p}_{\perp,{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}}\bar{\pi}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}}\right)^{2}.

Step 3: Cross term linear in
\mathbf{E}
.

The linear
\mathbf{E}
term has the form

T=\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{d},

where

\mathbf{c}=C\,(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger\top}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i},\qquad\mathbf{d}={\mathbf{w^{\star}}}_{\perp},

and
C
is the deterministic prefactor defined in Step 2, which converges almost surely to an
O(1)
limit.

This term has the same structure as the term linear in
\mathbf{E}
in
Theorem
1
, Step 3, with the vector

{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-\Pi{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}-A\Pi\mathbf{v}

replaced by the unit vector
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}
and multiplied by the scalar
C=O(1)
.
The same arguments apply and we conclude that

T\xrightarrow{a.s.}0.

Step 4: Quadratic term in
\mathbf{E}
.

Define

Q:={\mathbf{w^{\star}}}_{\perp}^{\top}\mathbf{E}^{\top}(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger\top}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}^{\top}(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}_{\perp}.

Let

\mathbf{a}:={\mathbf{w^{\star}}}_{\perp},\qquad\mathbf{A}:=(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger\top}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}^{\top}(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}.

Conditioned on
(\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{U}_{m})
, the vector
\mathbf{a}
and matrix
\mathbf{A}
are independent of
\mathbf{E}
, so
Q=\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{E}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{a}
is a quadratic Gaussian form.

This is exactly the same object as in Theorem
1
, Step 5, with a different choice of
\mathbf{A}
. The concentration argument therefore carries over verbatim once we verify that the same norm bounds hold.

First, as in Theorem
1
, Step 3,

\mathbf{a}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbf{a}=O(1)\qquad\text{almost surely.}

Next, since
\mathbf{A}
is rank one,

\|\mathbf{A}\|=\|(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}\|^{2},\qquad\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}^{2}=\|\mathbf{A}\|^{2}.

Using the bounds on
\|(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
established in Theorem
1
, Step 3,
together with
\|\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}\|=1
, we obtain

\|\mathbf{A}\|=O\!\left(\frac{1}{p}\right),\qquad\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}^{2}=O\!\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right),\qquad\text{almost surely.}

The conditions required for the Hanson–Wright concentration used in Theorem
1
, Step 5 are satisfied. Therefore, by the same argument as in Theorem
1
, Step 5,

Q-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{E}}[Q\mid\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{U}_{m}]\xrightarrow{a.s.}0.

Finally, the conditional expectation is

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{E}}[Q\mid\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{U}_{m}]=(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbf{a})\text{Tr}(\mathbf{A})=O\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)\xrightarrow{a.s.}0

Step 5: Variance contribution.

Finally, we consider the term from Equation (
20
) and note it is the same as the norm of the matrix
\mathbf{A}
from Step 4:

\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}^{\top}(\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{X}_{l}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}=\|(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{A}\|=O\left(\frac{1}{p}\right).

Thus, this term converges to 0:

\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}^{\top}(\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{X}_{l}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}\xrightarrow{a.s.}0.

Combining.

Thus, in the high-dimensional limit the only additional contribution relative to the estimation error is the spike bias term, and the generalisation error converges to

E^{\mathrm{gen}}_{\infty}=E^{\mathrm{est}}_{\infty}+\bar{w}^{\ast}\sum_{i}(\nu_{i}-1)\left(\rho_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}}-\bar{\pi}_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}}-\frac{\lambda-1}{\bar{\lambda}}\bar{p}_{\perp,{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}}\bar{\pi}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}}\right)^{2}+\sigma^{2}.

∎

A.5
Proof for the training error

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Let

\mathbf{H}=(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}

and recall that the target vector is
\mathbf{y}_{d}=\mathbf{X}_{l}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}+\mathbf{\xi}
. Then, the residual vector is:

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{d}-\mathbf{X}_{l}{\mathbf{\hat{w}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{y}_{d}-(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}_{d}-(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{\perp})(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}_{d}

(22)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{y}_{d}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}}-\mathbf{H})(\mathbf{X}_{l}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}+\mathbf{\xi}),

where we note that the term multiplying two projections is 0:

(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{\perp})(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m})^{\dagger}=\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}\underbrace{\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{m}}_{0}(\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathbf{U}_{m})^{\dagger}=0.

The training error splits into two terms since the noise
\mathbf{\xi}
is independent and zero-mean:

\displaystyle E^{\mathrm{train}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{l}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\xi}}\left[\left\|(\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{X}_{l}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}+(\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{\xi}\right\|^{2}\right]

(23)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{n_{l}}\left\|(\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{X}_{l}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\right\|^{2}}_{\text{Bias}^{2}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{n_{l}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\xi}}\left[\left\|(\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{\xi}\right\|^{2}\right]}_{\text{Variance}}.

We start by computing the bias.
Using the decomposition
\mathbf{X}_{l}=\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m}+\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{\perp}
and the fact that
(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{m}=0
, the bias simplifies to:

\text{Bias}^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{l}}\left\|(\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{\perp}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\right\|^{2}.

(24)

Substituting the conditional Gaussian decomposition, we have

\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{\perp}=\mathbf{X}_{m}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{m\perp}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}+\mathbf{E}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top},

where
\mathbf{E}
is independent of
\mathbf{X}_{m}
. Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle(\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathcal{P}_{\perp}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{X}_{m}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{m\perp}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}+(\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{E}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}

(25)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{E}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}_{\perp},

where we used
(\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{X}_{m}=0
and defined
{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}_{\perp}=\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}
. Note that the entries of
\mathbf{E}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}_{\perp}
are i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2})
, with

\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}=\frac{\|\mathcal{P}_{\perp}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}}{\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}}+\frac{\lambda-1}{\tilde{\lambda}}\frac{(\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathcal{P}_{\perp}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}})^{2}}{\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}}.

(26)

Conditioned on
\mathbf{X}_{m}
,
\mathbf{H}
is deterministic, and the term

\|(\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{E}{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}_{\perp}\|^{2}

is a quadratic form
z^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}}-\mathbf{H})z
where

z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}})
. Hence, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{E}}\left[\text{Bias}^{2}\mid\mathbf{X}_{m}\right]=\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}}{n_{l}}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}}-\mathbf{H})=\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\left(1-\frac{\min(m,n_{l})}{n_{l}}\right),

(27)

where the last equality holds almost surely (w.r.t. the randomness in
\mathbf{X}_{m}
).

Next, to compute the variance, recall that the label noise
\mathbf{\xi}
is isotropic
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}})
. Thus, we have

\frac{1}{n_{l}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\xi}}\left[\mathbf{\xi}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{\xi}\right]=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n_{l}}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{I}_{n_{l}}-\mathbf{H})=\sigma^{2}\left(1-\frac{\min(m,n_{l})}{n_{l}}\right),

(28)

where the last equality again holds almost surely (w.r.t. the randomness in
\mathbf{X}_{m}
).
Note that

1-\frac{\min(m,n_{l})}{n_{l}}=\max(0,1-\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}).

To conclude, it suffices to recall from the proof of Theorem
1
that
\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\bar{\sigma}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
, and the argument is complete.
∎

A.6
Auxiliary lemmas

Lemma 1

(Limiting spectral distribution of
\mathbf{X}_{m}
)
.

Consider the setup of Section
3
, and define

\mathbf{X}_{m}:=\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathbf{U}_{m}.

Then the empirical spectral measure of
\frac{1}{n_{l}}\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{m}
converges weakly almost surely to the Marchenko–Pastur law with aspect ratio
\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}
, i.e.,

\hat{F}^{\frac{1}{n_{l}}\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{m}}\Rightarrow F_{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}}\qquad\text{almost surely.}

Proof.

Fix a realization of the full sequence
(\mathbf{U}_{m})_{p\geq 1}
. Conditional on this realization, the rows of
\mathbf{X}_{m}=\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathbf{U}_{m}
are i.i.d. Gaussian in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with covariance

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}:=\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{U}_{m}.

Using the form of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
from Assumption
2
, we obtain

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}=\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}\Bigl(\mathbf{I}_{p}+(\lambda-1)\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\Bigr)\mathbf{U}_{m}=\mathbf{I}_{m}+(\lambda-1)(\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{v})(\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{v})^{\top}.

Hence,
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}-\mathbf{I}_{m}
has rank at most one. Since
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}\succ 0
, define
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}\times m}
and
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
by

\mathbf{G}:=\mathbf{X}_{m}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}^{-1/2}\qquad\mathbf{W}:=\frac{1}{n_{l}}\mathbf{G}^{\top}\mathbf{G}.

Conditional on
\mathbf{U}_{m}
,
\mathbf{G}
has i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
entries. The sample covariance can be rewritten:

\frac{1}{n_{l}}\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{m}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{n_{l}}\mathbf{G}^{\top}\mathbf{G}\right)\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}^{1/2}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}^{1/2}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}^{1/2}.

Since
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}=\mathbf{I}_{m}+\mathbf{R}
with
\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{R})\leq 1
, the spectral decomposition shows that
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}^{1/2}-\mathbf{I}_{m}
also has rank at most one. Consequently,

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}^{1/2}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}^{1/2}-\mathbf{W}=(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}^{1/2}-\mathbf{I}_{m})\mathbf{W}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}^{1/2}+\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}^{1/2}-\mathbf{I}_{m}),

and thus

\operatorname{rank}\!\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}^{1/2}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}^{1/2}-\mathbf{W}\right)\leq 2.

Let
\hat{F}^{\mathbf{A}}
denote the empirical spectral distribution function of a real symmetric matrix
\mathbf{A}
. By the rank inequality (e.g., see Theorem A.43 in
[
11
]
),

\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\hat{F}^{\frac{1}{n_{l}}\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{m}}(\theta)-\hat{F}^{\mathbf{W}}(\theta)\right|\leq\frac{2}{m}\underset{p\to\infty}{\to}0.

Since
\mathbf{W}
is a standard Wishart matrix of size
m\times m
with aspect ratio
\frac{m}{n_{l}}\to\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}
, by the Marchenko–Pastur theorem
[
59
]
,

\hat{F}^{\mathbf{W}}\Rightarrow F_{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}}\qquad\text{almost surely, conditional on }(\mathbf{U}_{m})_{p\geq 1}.

Let
A:=\left\{F^{\frac{1}{n_{l}}\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{m}}\Rightarrow F_{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}}\right\}.
Taken together, the arguments above shows that:

\mathbb{P}\!\left(A\,\middle|\,(\mathbf{U}_{m})_{p\geq 1}\right)=1.

We now remove the conditioning on
(\mathbf{U}_{m})_{p\geq 1}
. By the law of total probability,

\mathbb{P}(A)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{P}\!\left(A\,\middle|\,(\mathbf{U}_{m})_{p\geq 1}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}[1]=1.

This proves that

\hat{F}^{\frac{1}{n_{l}}\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{m}}\Rightarrow F_{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}}\qquad\text{almost surely.}

∎

Lemma 2

(Deterministic equivalent for the pseudoinverse trace)
.

Consider the setup of Section
3
, and define

\mathbf{X}_{m}:=\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathbf{U}_{m}.

Then

\mathrm{Tr}\!\big[(\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{m})^{\dagger}\big]\xrightarrow{a.s.}\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}}{1-\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}},&\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}<1,\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1},&\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}>1.\end{cases}

Proof.

We have

\text{Tr}\!\big[(\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{m})^{\dagger}\big]=\frac{1}{n_{l}}\text{Tr}\Bigl((\frac{1}{n_{l}}\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{m})^{\dagger}\Bigr)=\frac{m}{n_{l}}\cdot\frac{1}{m}\text{Tr}\Bigl((\frac{1}{n_{l}}\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{m})^{\dagger}\Bigr).

(29)

By Lemma
1
, the empirical spectral distribution of
\frac{1}{n_{l}}\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{m}
converges weakly almost surely to the Marcenko–Pastur law with aspect ratio
\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}
. If
\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}<1
, its support is bounded away from zero, and the claim follows by continuity of
x\mapsto 1/x
. If
\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}>1
, there is an additional atom at zero; however, the pseudo-inverse removes this null space contribution, and the nonzero spectrum remains bounded away from zero, so the same argument applies on the effective support. Therefore,

\frac{1}{m}\text{Tr}\Bigl(\frac{1}{n_{l}}(\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{m})^{\dagger}\Bigr)=\int\frac{1}{x}\,dF^{\frac{1}{n_{l}}\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{m}}(x)\xrightarrow{a.s.}\int\frac{1}{x}\,dF_{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}}(x).

Recall that

\int\frac{1}{x}\,dF_{\gamma}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\gamma},&\gamma<1,\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma(\gamma-1)},&\gamma>1.\end{cases}

Substituting into (
29
) and using
\gamma=\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}
gives

\text{Tr}\!\big[(\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{m})^{\dagger}\big]\xrightarrow{a.s.}\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}}{1-\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}},&\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}<1,\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1},&\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}>1,\end{cases}

which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4

(Deterministic limit of
\|\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{a}\|^{2},\|\mathcal{P}_{\perp}\mathbf{a}\|^{2}
)
.

Consider the setup of Section
3
, and let
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a fixed vector with population mass distribution
G_{\mathbf{a}}
on the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{m}
be the projection onto the top
m
eigenvectors of the prior sample covariance
\mathbf{S}_{u}
, with
m/p\to\alpha
.
Let
G^{\mathrm{sample}}_{\mathbf{a}}
denote the deterministic limiting weighted spectral measure defined in Lemma
3
. Let
\lambda_{t}
be the deterministic bulk threshold corresponding to retaining an asymptotic fraction
\alpha
of principal components

F_{\gamma_{u}}(\lambda_{t})=1-\alpha.

Then, almost surely,

\frac{\|\mathcal{P}_{\perp}\mathbf{a}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}}\;\xrightarrow{a.s.}\;\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{\lambda_{-}}^{\lambda_{t}}g_{\mathbf{a}}^{\mathrm{sample}}(\lambda),&\gamma_{u}<1,\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle G_{\mathbf{a}}^{\mathrm{sample}}(\{0\})+\int_{\lambda_{-}}^{\lambda_{t}}g_{\mathbf{a}}^{\mathrm{sample}}(\lambda),&\gamma_{u}>1,\;\alpha<\tfrac{1}{\gamma_{u}},\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\frac{1}{\gamma_{u}}}\,G_{\mathbf{a}}^{\mathrm{sample}}(\{0\}),&\gamma_{u}>1,\;\alpha\geq\tfrac{1}{\gamma_{u}},\end{cases}

(30)

where
\lambda_{-}=(1-\sqrt{\gamma_{u}})^{2}
is the infimum of the support of the Marcenko-Pastur distribution as defined in Equation (
5
) and

\|\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{a}\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}-\|\mathcal{P}_{\perp}\mathbf{a}\|^{2}.

(31)

Proof.

Let

\hat{G}_{\mathbf{a}}^{\mathrm{sample}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{p}(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{i})^{2}\,\mathbf{1}\{\theta\leq\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{S}_{u})\}

be the weighted empirical spectral measure of
\mathbf{S}_{u}
. By Lemma
3
,
\hat{G}_{\mathbf{a}}^{\mathrm{sample}}
converges almost surely to the deterministic measure
G_{\mathbf{a}}^{\mathrm{sample}}
.

Since
\|\mathcal{P}_{\perp}\mathbf{a}\|^{2}/\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}
is exactly the mass of
\hat{G}_{\mathbf{a}}^{\mathrm{sample}}
on the discarded eigenspaces, its limit is obtained by integrating
G_{\mathbf{a}}^{\mathrm{sample}}
over the discarded part of the spectrum.

If
\gamma_{u}<1
, there is no nullspace, so only bulk directions are discarded. This gives

\frac{\|\mathcal{P}_{\perp}\mathbf{a}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\int_{\lambda_{-}}^{\lambda_{t}}g_{\mathbf{a}}^{\mathrm{sample}}(\lambda).

If
\gamma_{u}>1
and
\alpha<1/\gamma_{u}
, fewer than all positive-eigenvalue directions are retained, so the entire nullspace is discarded as well. Hence

\frac{\|\mathcal{P}_{\perp}\mathbf{a}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}}\xrightarrow{a.s.}G_{\mathbf{a}}^{\mathrm{sample}}(\{0\})+\int_{\lambda_{-}}^{\lambda_{t}}g_{\mathbf{a}}^{\mathrm{sample}}(\lambda).

Finally, if
\gamma_{u}>1
and
\alpha\geq 1/\gamma_{u}
, all positive-eigenvalue directions are retained, and the retained subspace is completed by adding

m-\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{S}_{u})=m-n_{u}

orthonormal directions chosen uniformly at random in the nullspace, independently of
\mathbf{a}
. Therefore only the fraction

\frac{p-m}{p-n_{u}}\;\xrightarrow{a.s.}\;\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\frac{1}{\gamma_{u}}}

of the nullspace remains discarded. Since the total asymptotic mass of
\mathbf{a}
in the nullspace is
G_{\mathbf{a}}^{\mathrm{sample}}(\{0\})
, we obtain

\frac{\|\mathcal{P}_{\perp}\mathbf{a}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\frac{1}{\gamma_{u}}}\,G_{\mathbf{a}}^{\mathrm{sample}}(\{0\}).

The identity
\|\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{a}\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}-\|\mathcal{P}_{\perp}\mathbf{a}\|^{2}
is immediate.
∎

Lemma 6

(Deterministic limit of the effective projection
\|\Pi\mathbf{a}\|^{2}

.

Consider the setup of Section
3

\mathbf{X}_{m}=\mathbf{X}_{l}\mathbf{U}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}\times m}

\Pi_{m}:=\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\dagger}\mathbf{X}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},\qquad\Pi:=\mathbf{U}_{m}\Pi_{m}\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}.

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}
be
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}:=\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{U}_{m}
with
(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i},\tilde{\lambda}_{i})
denoting the eigenpairs. For any fixed vector
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
with
\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{a}\neq 0
, define

\tilde{\mathbf{a}}:=\frac{\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{a}\|}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

and let
\tilde{G}_{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}}
be the mass distribution of
\tilde{\mathbf{a}}
onto the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}
:

\tilde{G}_{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}}(\tilde{\tau})=\sum_{i}(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{i})^{2}\,\mathbf{1}\{\tilde{\lambda}_{i}\leq\tilde{\tau}\}.

Then, it holds almost surely

\tilde{G}_{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}}(\tau)\Rightarrow\left(1-\frac{\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}}^{2}}{\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}}\bar{p}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}}}\right)\delta_{1}+\frac{\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}}^{2}}{\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}}\bar{p}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}}}\delta_{\bar{\lambda}},

and

\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Pi\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}}\cdot\begin{cases}1,&\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}<1,\\[6.0pt]
1-\displaystyle\int\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1}{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1+\tilde{\tau}}\,d\tilde{G}_{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}}(\tilde{\tau}),&\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}>1.\end{cases}

(39)

with
\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}},\bar{p}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}},\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}}
defined via Equations (
30
) and (
34
).

Proof.

We start by computing explicitly
\tilde{G}_{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}}
. Recall that

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}=\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{U}_{m}=\mathbf{I}_{m}+(\lambda-1)(\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{v})(\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{v})^{\top}.

Then,
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}
has
m-1
eigenvalues equal to
1
and the remaining one is

\tilde{\lambda}=1+(\lambda-1)\|\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{v}\|^{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\bar{\lambda}=1+(\lambda-1)\bar{p}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}},

where the convergence occurs almost surely. The corresponding unit eigenvector is
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}=\frac{\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{v}\|}=\frac{\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{v}\|}.

Therefore the spectral measure
\tilde{G}_{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}}
of

\tilde{\mathbf{a}}=\frac{\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{a}\|}

with respect to the eigenbasis of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}
is supported on
\{1,\tilde{\lambda}\}
, and hence

\tilde{G}_{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}}=\bigl(1-(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{v}})^{2}\bigr)\delta_{1}+(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{v}})^{2}\delta_{\tilde{\lambda}},

with

(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{v}})^{2}=\frac{((\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{a})^{\top}(\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{v}))^{2}}{\|\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{a}\|^{2}\,\|\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{v}\|^{2}}=\frac{(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{m}\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{v})^{2}}{\|\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{a}\|^{2}\,\|\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{v}\|^{2}}=\frac{(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{v})^{2}}{\|\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{a}\|^{2}\,\|\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{v}\|^{2}}.

Thus,
\tilde{G}_{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}}
is a two-point measure, with the original atom at
\lambda
replaced by an atom at
\tilde{\lambda}
, and with weight determined by the projected overlap. Almost surely, it holds that:

\tilde{G}_{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}}(\tau)\Rightarrow\left(1-\frac{\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}}^{2}}{\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}}\bar{p}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}}}\right)\delta_{1}+\frac{\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}}^{2}}{\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}}\bar{p}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}}}\delta_{\bar{\lambda}}.

Case
m<n_{l}
.

In this regime,
\mathbf{X}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}\times m}
has full column rank almost surely, so
\Pi_{m}=\mathbf{I}_{m}
. Hence
\tilde{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\Pi_{m}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}=1
, giving

\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Pi\mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}},

as
\frac{\|\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{a}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}}
from Lemma
4
.

Case
m>n_{l}
.

In this regime,
\mathbf{X}_{m}
has a nullspace of dimension
m-n_{l}
, and

\tilde{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\Pi_{m}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}=1-\tilde{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\Pi_{m,\mathrm{null}}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}.

Conditioned on
\mathbf{U}_{m}
, the rows of
\mathbf{X}_{m}
are Gaussian with covariance
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{mm}=\mathbf{U}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{U}_{m}
. Therefore, by the nullspace mass formula from Lemma
3
applied to the
m
-dimensional model with aspect ratio

\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}=m/n_{l}>1
,

\tilde{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\Pi_{m,\mathrm{null}}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\int\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1}{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1+\tilde{\tau}}\,d\tilde{G}_{\tilde{\mathbf{a}}}(\tilde{\tau}).

(40)

Finally, noting that
\frac{\|\mathcal{P}_{m}\mathbf{a}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}}
according to Lemma
4
and combining the two terms via the continuous mapping theorem gives the required result.

∎

Lemma 7

with
\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}},\bar{p}_{\mathbf{b},\mathbf{b}},\bar{p}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}},\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}},\bar{p}_{\mathbf{b},\mathbf{v}}

. The mass distribution

\mathbf{a}

\mathbf{v}

For bilinear quantities, the signs of the corresponding

Define the limiting value of the population and sample spike eigenvector overlap

\alpha=1

\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{eff}}=0
, thus simplifying

\left.\frac{d}{d\alpha}E^{\mathrm{gen}}_{\infty}(\alpha)\right|_{\alpha=1^{-}}=\frac{\gamma_{l}\sigma^{2}}{(1-\gamma_{l})^{2}}-\frac{\bar{w}^{\ast}}{1-\gamma_{l}}D_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}}.

Consequently,
\alpha=1
is locally stable iff

\left.\frac{d}{d\alpha}E^{\mathrm{gen}}_{\infty}(\alpha)\right|_{\alpha=1^{-}}\leq 0,

or equivalently

\gamma_{l}\leq\frac{SD_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}}}{1+SD_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}}},\qquad S:=\frac{\bar{w}^{\ast}}{\sigma^{2}}.

The equality gives the local stability boundary of the full representation:

D_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}}=\begin{cases}(1-\eta)+\eta\displaystyle\frac{\lambda\gamma_{u}}{(\lambda-1+\sqrt{\gamma_{u}})^{2}},&\gamma_{u}<1,\\[12.0pt]
(1-\eta)+\eta\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{u}}{\gamma_{u}-1+\lambda},&\gamma_{u}>1,\end{cases}

\boxed{\gamma_{l}^{\mathrm{crit}}=\frac{S\left[(1-\eta)+\eta D_{\mathbf{v}}\right]}{1+S\left[(1-\eta)+\eta D_{\mathbf{v}}\right]},}

where

D_{\mathbf{v}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\lambda\gamma_{u}}{(\lambda-1+\sqrt{\gamma_{u}})^{2}},&\gamma_{u}<1,\\[12.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{u}}{\gamma_{u}-1+\lambda},&\gamma_{u}>1.\end{cases}

A.9
Infinite data limits

We consider what happens to the errors and optimal
\alpha
when
\gamma_{u}\to 0
or
\gamma_{l}\to 0
.

Prior data limit
\gamma_{u}\to 0
.

In this limit, the empirical spike aligns with the population spike, and the retained subspace contains the spike direction together with an
\alpha
-fraction of the isotropic bulk.

For any fixed vectors
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}
, we have

\displaystyle\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}}

\displaystyle\to\eta_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}}+(1-\eta_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}})\alpha,

\displaystyle\bar{p}_{\perp,\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}}

\displaystyle\to(1-\eta_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}})(1-\alpha),

\displaystyle\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}

\displaystyle\to\rho_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}}\rho_{\mathbf{b},\mathbf{v}}+\alpha\left(\rho_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}-\rho_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}}\rho_{\mathbf{b},\mathbf{v}}\right),

\displaystyle\bar{p}_{\perp,\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}

\displaystyle\to(1-\alpha)\left(\rho_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}-\rho_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}}\rho_{\mathbf{b},\mathbf{v}}\right).

In particular,

\bar{p}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}}\to 1,\qquad\bar{p}_{\perp,\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}}\to 0,\qquad\bar{\lambda}\to\lambda.

For the effective projections
\bar{\pi}
, the behaviour depends on
\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}
.

If
\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}<1
, then

\bar{\pi}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}}\to\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}},\qquad\bar{\pi}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}\to\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}.

If
\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}>1
, define

A_{\lambda}:=\frac{\lambda}{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1+\lambda},\qquad A_{1}:=\frac{1}{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}},

then

\displaystyle\bar{\pi}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}}

\displaystyle\to\eta_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}}A_{\lambda}+(1-\eta_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}})\alpha A_{1},

\displaystyle\bar{\pi}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}

\displaystyle\to\rho_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}}\rho_{\mathbf{b},\mathbf{v}}A_{\lambda}+\alpha\left(\rho_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}-\rho_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{v}}\rho_{\mathbf{b},\mathbf{v}}\right)A_{1}.

Errors in the limit
\gamma_{u}\to 0
.

In the infinite-prior-data limit, the empirical spike is recovered exactly. Hence

\bar{p}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}}\to 1,\qquad\bar{p}_{\perp,{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}}\to 0,\qquad\bar{\lambda}\to\lambda,

and

\bar{p}_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}}\to\eta+(1-\eta)\alpha,\qquad\bar{p}_{\perp,{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}}\to(1-\eta)(1-\alpha).

Therefore

\bar{\sigma}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\to(1-\eta)(1-\alpha).

Since the leak term vanishes,

\boxed{E^{\mathrm{est}}_{\infty}\to\bar{w}^{\ast}\left(1-\bar{\pi}_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}}\right)+\frac{\min\{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}},1\}}{|\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1|}\left[\bar{w}^{\ast}(1-\eta)(1-\alpha)+\sigma^{2}\right],}

where

\bar{\pi}_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}}=\begin{cases}\eta+(1-\eta)\alpha,&\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}<1,\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\eta\frac{\lambda}{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1+\lambda}+(1-\eta)\frac{1}{\gamma_{l}},&\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}>1.\end{cases}

Moreover, taking
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{new}}=\mathbf{\Sigma}
, we obtain

\boxed{E^{\mathrm{gen}}_{\infty}\to E^{\mathrm{est}}_{\infty}+\bar{w}^{\ast}(\lambda-1)\eta\left(\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1}{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1+\lambda}\right)^{2}+\sigma^{2}.}

Finally, the training error satisfies

\boxed{E^{\mathrm{train}}_{\infty}\to\left[\sigma^{2}+\bar{w}^{\ast}(1-\eta)(1-\alpha)\right]\max\{0,1-\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}\}.}

Downstream-data limit
\gamma_{l}\to 0
.

For any fixed
\alpha>0
, we have
\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}=\alpha\gamma_{l}\to 0
, hence

\bar{\pi}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}}\to\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}},\qquad\bar{\pi}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}\to\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}.

Moreover, the variance factor satisfies

\frac{\min\{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}},1\}}{|\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1|}=\frac{\alpha\gamma_{l}}{1-\alpha\gamma_{l}}\to 0,

so all downstream finite-sample variance contributions vanish.

The pretraining projections
\bar{p}
remain determined by
\gamma_{u}
.

At
\alpha=1
,

\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}}=1,\qquad\bar{p}_{\perp,\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}}=0,\qquad\bar{\pi}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}}\to 1,

and

\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}=\rho_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}},\qquad\bar{p}_{\perp,\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}=0,\qquad\bar{\pi}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}\to\rho_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}.

At the spike-retaining endpoint
\alpha\to 0
,

\bar{\pi}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}}(0)\to\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}}(0),\qquad\bar{\pi}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}(0)\to\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}(0),

with
\bar{p}
determined by the prior overlap
c(\gamma_{u})
.

Errors in the limit
\gamma_{l}\to 0
.

For fixed
\alpha>0
,
\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}=\alpha\gamma_{l}\to 0
. Hence

\bar{\pi}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}}\to\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}},\qquad\bar{\pi}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}\to\bar{p}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}},

and

\frac{\min\{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}},1\}}{|\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1|}\to 0.

Therefore the estimation error satisfies

\boxed{E^{\mathrm{est}}_{\infty}\to\bar{w}^{\ast}\left[1-\bar{p}_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}}+\frac{(\lambda-1)^{2}}{\bar{\lambda}^{2}}\bar{p}_{\perp,{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}}^{\,2}\bar{p}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}}\right].}

The generalisation error satisfies

\boxed{E^{\mathrm{gen}}_{\infty}\to E^{\mathrm{est}}_{\infty}+\bar{w}^{\ast}\sum_{i=1}^{T}(\nu_{i}-1)\left(\rho_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}}-\bar{p}_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}}-\frac{\lambda-1}{\bar{\lambda}}\bar{p}_{\perp,{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}}\bar{p}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new},i}}\right)^{2}+\sigma^{2}.}

(43)

The training error satisfies

\boxed{E^{\mathrm{train}}_{\infty}\to\sigma^{2}+\bar{w}^{\ast}\left[\bar{p}_{\perp,{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}}+\frac{\lambda-1}{\bar{\lambda}}\bar{p}_{\perp,{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}}^{\,2}\right].}

(44)

Specialization to
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\textrm{new}}=\mathbf{\Sigma}
.
We further specialize the result in the limit to the setting where the validation data is the same as pretraining and downstream data.

Corollary 3
.

Assume
\gamma_{u}=p/n_{u}\to 0
, so that the empirical spike direction converges to the population spike
\mathbf{v}
. Let
\eta=\eta_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},\mathbf{v}},\;\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}=\alpha\gamma_{l},\;S=\bar{w}^{\ast}/\sigma^{2}
. Then the estimation error becomes

E^{\mathrm{est}}_{\infty}\to\bar{w}^{\ast}(1-\bar{\pi}_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}})+\frac{\min\{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}},1\}}{|\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1|}\left[\bar{w}^{\ast}(1-\eta)(1-\alpha)+\sigma^{2}\right],

where

\bar{\pi}_{{\mathbf{w^{\star}}},{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}}=\begin{cases}\eta+(1-\eta)\alpha,&\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}<1,\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\eta\frac{\lambda}{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1+\lambda}+(1-\eta)\frac{\alpha}{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}},&\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}>1.\end{cases}

For matched test covariance
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{new}}=\mathbf{\Sigma}
,

E^{\mathrm{gen}}_{\infty}\to E^{\mathrm{est}}_{\infty}+\bar{w}^{\ast}(\lambda-1)\eta\begin{cases}0,&\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}<1,\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\left(\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1}{\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}-1+\lambda}\right)^{2},&\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}>1,\end{cases}+\sigma^{2}.

Finally,

E^{\mathrm{train}}_{\infty}\to\left[\sigma^{2}+\bar{w}^{\ast}(1-\eta)(1-\alpha)\right]\max\{0,1-\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}\}.

Consequently, the phase transitions in this limit can either be derived by taking derivatives and limiting values
\alpha
, or by substituting the limits directly in Corollaries
1
and
2
. In this regime, the phase boundaries become independent of
\gamma_{u}
and depend only on
\eta
,
\mathrm{SNR}
,
\lambda
, and
\gamma_{l}
.

Corollary 4

(Phase transitions for
\gamma_{u}\to 0
)
.

In the limit
\gamma_{u}\to 0
, the spike direction is recovered exactly. Hence,

c(\gamma_{u})\to 1
and
D_{\mathbf{v}}(\gamma_{u})\to 0
. Thus, the transition curve when

\gamma_{l}>1
simplifies to

(1-\eta)\frac{1}{\gamma_{l}}=\eta\frac{\lambda\gamma_{l}(\gamma_{l}-1)}{(\gamma_{l}-1+\lambda)^{2}}+\frac{1}{S(\gamma_{l}-1)}.

Furthermore, when
\gamma_{l}<1
, the stability boundary reduces to

\gamma_{l}^{\mathrm{crit}}=\frac{S(1-\eta)}{1+S(1-\eta)}.

We compare three approaches: standard regression, pretrained regression (PR) where PCA is learned on prior data and transferred to the downstream task, and principal component regression (PCR), where PCA is learned directly on the downstream data.

n_{l}

Data generation.

Both covariances use
p=200
features, noise standard deviation
\sigma=1

\mathrm{SNR}=9,\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|=3
.

•

Spiked identity (
\lambda=5.0
)
:
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{I}_{p}+(\lambda-1)\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\top}
; true weight vector aligned with the spike
{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}=\|{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}\|\mathbf{v}
, where
\mathbf{v}

\mathbf{\Sigma}

•

Spiked Toeplitz
(
\lambda=5.0

\mathbf{\Sigma}
is a Toeplitz matrix with
\rho=0.5
, with
\lambda
added to the top eigenvalue;
{\mathbf{w^{\star}}}
again aligned with the top eigenvector.

Each grid point
(n_{u},n_{l})
averages five independent replications, with
n_{u},n_{l}\in[1,1200]
each swept over
20
uniform values.

Models.

For each
(n_{u},n_{l})
pair,
\alpha\in\{0.01,0.05,\ldots,1.0\}
(20 values) sets the latent dimension
m=\lfloor\alpha p\rfloor
. The three models below are evaluated.

1.

PCA + Regression (PR)
: Eigenvectors of
\mathbf{S}_{u}=\mathbf{X}_{u}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{u}/n_{u}
are computed;
\mathbf{X}_{l}
is projected onto the top-
m
eigenvectors and OLS is applied in the latent space.

2.

Linear AE
: Encoder (
\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
) and linear decoder (
\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
) trained by minimizing MSE on
\mathbf{X}_{u}
; the frozen encoder maps
\mathbf{X}_{l}
to features for OLS.

3.

Nonlinear AE
: Same as the linear AE but with a sigmoid nonlinearity applied at the encoder,
\mathbf{z}=\sigma(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{enc}}\,\mathbf{x})
.

All autoencoders are implemented in

and trained with Adam (
\mathrm{lr}=10^{-3}

D.5
Compute

Theory curves.

Theory curves were computed on a single CPU (Intel Xeon E5-2680 v4 @ 2.40 GHz,
no GPU required). Each configuration sweeps a
100\times 100
grid of

(n_{u},n_{l})
values over 30 values of
\alpha
, amounting to approximately

300{,}000
evaluations of closed-form expressions. The full sweep covers
24 configurations,

\lambda^{*}\in\{1.1,2.0,5.0,10.0\}\quad\times\quad\mathrm{SNR}\in\{0.1,0.5,1.0,3.0,5.0,9.0\},
with alignment
\eta=1.0
, totaling approximately
7.2
million evaluations.
When executed in parallel, the full sweep completes in approximately 2h. Peak RAM usage was approximately 2 GB per configuration.

Simulations.

Simulations were distributed over a SLURM cluster. Each job was allocated
4 CPUs and 30 GB of RAM. The full grid

6\ \mathrm{SNR}\times 4\ \lambda_{1}\times 4\ \lambda_{2}\times 30\ n_{b}

produced 2,880 SLURM jobs, each processing 30 values of
n_{l}
with 100
independent replications at
p=500
. Each replication involves an
eigendecomposition and pseudoinverse of a
p\times p
matrix for each of the 30 values of
\alpha
. When executed in parallel, the full sweep completes in approximately 10h.

Autoencoder experiment.

Simulations were distributed over a SLURM cluster. Each job was allocated
1 GPU, 8 CPUs, and 16 GB of RAM. The full grid

20\ n_{\mathrm{base}}\times 20\ n_{\mathrm{task}}

produced 400 SLURM jobs, each processing 20 values of
\alpha
with 5
independent replications at
p=200
. Each replication trains a linear and a
nonlinear autoencoder for 20,000 epochs for each value of
\alpha
, followed by
OLS regression in the learned latent space. The individual jobs completed in 10-50min depending on parameters.

LLM probe experiment.

Simulations were distributed over a SLURM cluster. Each job was allocated
1 GPU, 16 CPUs, and 32 GB of RAM. Two sets of 10 SLURM jobs were submitted:
one per checkpoint of Pythia-70M-deduped for the downstream-PCA condition and
one for the pretrain-PCA condition. Each job sweeps over 10 training-set sizes
and 3 seeds, fitting a linear probe for up to 10,000 epochs with early stopping
across up to 14 PCA dimensionalities
m
.
