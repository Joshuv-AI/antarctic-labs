Advances in nano-TCM for Alzheimer’s disease: lipid-based carriers integrated with innovative delivery strategies
Authors: LiLi Deng, Huan Li, ShaSha Yang, FengMing Chen, JiaXin Qiao, HuiZhong Bao, XiaoMeng Wang, ShiChao Fang, Bing Xu, ZhiShu Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a multifactorial neurodegenerative disorder characterized by β-amyloid (Aβ) plaque deposition, tau hyperphosphorylation, neuroinflammation, and oxidative stress. However, current therapies remain largely symptomatic. Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM)-derived monomers exhibit considerable anti-AD potential owing to their multitarget neuroprotective activities. However, their therapeutic translation is severely limited by poor stability, low bioavailability, and restricted brain delivery across the blood-brain barrier (BBB). This review summarizes the pathological basis of AD, the neuroprotective mechanisms of representative TCM-derived monomers, and the major BBB-related barriers that hinder effective brain delivery. Particular emphasis is placed on lipid-based nanocarriers, including exosomes, liposomes, solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs), and nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs), as platforms for improving drug stability, BBB transport, and brain accumulation. We further highlight innovative delivery strategies that integrate ligand-mediated targeting with biomimetic modification, particularly cell membrane camouflage and exosome-inspired engineering. These approaches may confer immune evasion, prolonged circulation, enhanced biocompatibility, and improved lesion-oriented delivery. Finally, we discuss the challenges facing the clinical translation of lipid-based nanocarriers, including large-scale production, quality control, regulatory considerations, and long-term safety. Collectively, these lipid-based nanoplatforms provide a promising framework for advancing next-generation nano-TCM therapeutics for AD. Future progress will depend on optimized carrier design, rigorous mechanistic validation, comprehensive long-term safety assessment, and clinically relevant translational studies.Graphical abstract

Introduction

AD is a common neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive cognitive decline and memory impairment. As the leading cause of dementia, AD disproportionately affects women. With the acceleration of global aging, the prevalence of AD continues to rise, posing significant public health and socioeconomic challenges. According to a 2022 global dementia prevalence study published in The Lancet Public Health, the number of individuals worldwide living with AD or other forms of dementia is projected to reach approximately 116 million by 2040 and 153 million by 2050 [1]. Despite extensive research into pathogenesis and treatment over the past decades, there remains a critical unmet medical need for therapies capable of effectively slowing or reversing disease progression.

As a complex neurodegenerative disorder, AD involves multiple pathological processes, including Aβ plaque deposition, tau hyperphosphorylation, neuroinflammation, and oxidative stress [2]. These pathological changes lead to gradual neuronal degeneration and death, triggering memory loss and progressive cognitive decline [3, 4]. Although the pathological mechanisms of AD have been studied for decades, its precise etiology remains unclear [5, 6]. Studies suggest that the onset of AD may be associated with multiple factors, including genetic susceptibility, environmental influences, and lifestyle-related factors [7]. Current clinical therapies, such as acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, NMDA receptor antagonists, and Aβ-targeting monoclonal antibodies, primarily provide symptomatic relief and have not effectively delayed disease progression or reversed the disease course [8–11]. These limitations indicate that single-target therapeutic strategies struggle to effectively address the multifaceted pathophysiology of AD, highlighting the need for novel therapeutic approaches.

Within the theoretical framework of TCM, AD is associated with the concepts of “dementia” and “forgetfulness”. For millennia, Chinese herbal medicines have been utilized to treat neurological disorders, with commonly employed herbs including Panax ginseng, Astragalus membranaceus, and Acorus tatarinowii rhizomes [12–15]. In recent years, the multi-target and multi-pathway regulatory properties of TCM have attracted increasing attention as promising complementary or alternative therapeutic strategies for neurodegenerative diseases. Preclinical studies suggest that TCM-derived formulas, extracts, and monomers are of particular interest in AD research because they can simultaneously modulate multiple interconnected pathogenic processes, including Aβ deposition, tau hyperphosphorylation, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuronal apoptosis [16–21]. This multitarget mode of action is especially relevant to AD, a disorder driven by complex and interacting pathogenic cascades rather than a single dominant lesion [22]. Several representative studies support this perspective. Danggui Buxue Formula modulates TNF/NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways, inhibits neuroinflammation-driven neuronal apoptosis, and significantly improves cognitive impairment and anxiety-like behaviors in AD mouse models [23]. The Chuanxiong-Ginseng herb pair exerts neuroprotective effects through dual mechanisms, including inhibition of PKC-α/NF-κB-mediated neuroinflammation and restoration of gut microbiota homeostasis, with synergistic effects observed upon PKC-α inhibition [24]. Lycium barbarum extract activates the WNT signaling pathway and inhibits tau phosphorylation, thereby alleviating cognitive deterioration in AD mouse models [25]. Ginkgo biloba leaf extract, widely used in cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, has been shown to reduce cognitive deficits in APP/PS1 transgenic mice by improving cerebral vascular structure and function and enhancing cerebral blood flow [26]. Furthermore, compared with conventional small-molecule anti-AD drugs, many TCM-derived monomers demonstrate promising efficacy in delaying AD progression through pleiotropic mechanisms, including suppression of pro-inflammatory pathways, enhancement of anti-inflammatory responses, promotion of Aβ clearance, and attenuation of oxidative stress [27, 28]. Ginsenosides Rb1 and Rg1 significantly reverse hippocampal neuronal loss and suppress astrocytic and microglial activation in AD mouse models [29]. Berberine, derived from Coptis chinensis, inhibits the mTOR signaling pathway, promotes M2 microglial polarization, enhances Aβ plaque clearance, and significantly improves cognitive dysfunction in APP/PS1 transgenic mice [30]. Clinically, TCM exhibits profound translational significance; herbal medicines and their derived compounds have long been utilized in neurological and age-related disorders, providing viable complementary therapeutic options for patients who exhibit sub-optimal responses to current single-target interventions [31]. Therefore, TCM-based strategies may offer an attractive framework for developing multi-mechanistic interventions for AD.

Despite its therapeutic promise, the clinical translation of TCM in AD remains constrained by several important obstacles. These include the intrinsic complexity of TCM, the difficulty of effective brain delivery in central nervous system (CNS) disorders, and the heterogeneous clinical evidence supporting TCM-based interventions, all of which limit its broader clinical application [32–34]. The intrinsic limitations of TCM can be summarized into three main aspects. First, many herbal medicines and TCM-derived monomers exhibit substantial compositional complexity, which complicates the identification of definitive active constituents and their pharmacodynamic contributions [35]. Second, batch-to-batch variability and insufficient standardization may compromise reproducibility and quality control [36]. Third, many TCM-derived monomers suffer from poor aqueous solubility, rapid metabolism, low oral bioavailability, and insufficient brain exposure [37]. Beyond these intrinsic limitations, barriers to brain delivery further constrain the clinical application of TCM in AD [38]. The BBB is a major barrier to effective AD therapy because it severely restricts drug delivery to the CNS. This challenge is particularly pronounced for TCM-derived monomers. As a result, although many of these compounds demonstrate clear neuroprotective potential in experimental studies, they frequently exhibit poor physicochemical stability and limited BBB permeability, thereby making it difficult for them to reach therapeutic concentrations in brain tissue [39]. In recent years, advances in nanotechnology have provided promising strategies to overcome these barriers. Nanocarriers, characterized by low toxicity, high surface-to-volume ratios, tunable degradation kinetics, versatile drug-loading capacity, and flexible surface functionalization, can address several limitations of conventional therapeutic approaches [40]. By exploiting receptor-mediated transcytosis (RMT) and related physiological transport mechanisms, nanocarriers can traverse the BBB, thereby enhancing brain-targeted delivery, promoting drug accumulation at pathological sites, and ultimately improving therapeutic efficacy [41].

Lipid-based nanoparticles, including exosomes, liposomes, SLNs, and NLCs, have attracted considerable attention due to their favorable biocompatibility, cellular permeability, and drug-loading efficiency, making them particularly suitable carriers for brain delivery of TCM-derived monomers [42]. Exosomes are naturally occurring nanovesicles enclosed by a lipid bilayer and containing diverse biomolecules, including proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, and metabolites. In neurodegenerative diseases such as AD, exosomes exhibit distinct advantages as drug delivery vehicles owing to their low immunogenicity, minimal toxicity, and excellent biocompatibility [43]. Importantly, exosomes can efficiently cross the BBB and protect encapsulated cargos from degradation, highlighting their potential for brain-targeted delivery in AD therapy [44]. As endogenous vesicles, exosomes can evade immune surveillance and exhibit prolonged systemic circulation [45]. Liposomes are widely used in clinical applications due to their efficient drug encapsulation and delivery capabilities; however, they are often recognized as foreign particles in vivo and rapidly cleared by the immune system. To address this limitation, various surface modification strategies have been explored. PEGylation can prolong circulation time but may induce hypersensitivity reactions in certain cases [46]. SLNs and NLCs further improve drug stability and bioavailability and have shown promise in neurological drug delivery. More recently, biomimetic modification strategies have been applied to lipid-based nanocarriers by incorporating extracellular membranes or natural vesicles, thereby conferring “self-identity” signals that enable immune evasion. For example, coating liposomes with erythrocyte or macrophage membranes allows the presentation of CD47-mediated “do-not-eat-me” signals, which interact with signal regulatory protein-α (SIRPα) on phagocytes, effectively suppressing macrophage-mediated clearance and complement activation [47]. Red blood cell (RBC) membrane-camouflaged nanoparticles exhibit markedly prolonged circulation times, with reported plasma half-lives of up to 39.6 h, compared with approximately 15.8 h for conventional PEGylated nanoparticles [47]. Furthermore, liposomes mimicking exosomal or macrophage membranes incorporate native lipids and membrane proteins via membrane fusion, achieving immune camouflage while conferring enhanced targeting specificity and lysosomal escape capability, thereby enabling site-specific drug release in inflammatory or pathological microenvironments [48]. Collectively, these biomimetic lipid-based nanocarriers demonstrate improved biocompatibility and superior potential for BBB penetration and brain delivery of TCM-derived monomers.

This review provides a systematic overview of AD pathogenesis, the multi-pathway and multi-target mechanisms by which TCM-derived monomers modulate AD pathology, the physiological structure of the BBB, the major delivery challenges associated with TCM-based AD therapy, and lipid-based nano-delivery technologies together with their targeting and biomimetic modification strategies. Unlike previous reviews that have primarily focused on isolated aspects of this field, such as the pharmacological mechanisms of TCM in AD, nano-delivery systems, BBB-targeting strategies, or biomimetic modification technologies, this review specifically focuses on the integrated application of TCM-derived monomers and nanodrug delivery engineering in the context of AD therapy. Specifically, it underscores lipid-based nanocarriers encapsulating TCM-derived monomers, while highlighting innovative delivery strategies incorporating ligand-mediated targeting or biomimetic modification. In addition to outlining the delivery advantages of lipid-based nanocarriers, this review also addresses the key barriers that limit their clinical translation, including scalable manufacturing, complex regulatory requirements, and long-term safety and immunogenicity. Looking ahead, continued advances in emerging technologies are expected to help address current limitations in the scalable manufacturing, quality control, and regulatory feasibility of TCM-loaded lipid-based nano-delivery systems, thereby facilitating their clinical translation for AD therapy.

AD pathology and modulation by TCM-derived monomers

Pathogenesis of AD

AD progresses along a clinical continuum encompassing three major stages: preclinical AD, mild cognitive impairment due to AD, and dementia due to AD [49]. Although the pathophysiology is intricate, AD is characterized by hallmark neuropathologies, including extracellular plaques composed of Aβ, intracellular neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) formed by hyperphosphorylated tau, synaptic dysfunction due to altered neurotransmission, and chronic neuroinflammation [50, 51]. Under AD conditions, Aβ production is upregulated, while clearance is delayed; monomeric Aβ assembles into soluble oligomers, which further assemble into insoluble β-sheet-rich fibrils and ultimately mature plaques [52]. Soluble Aβ oligomers are considered the principal neurotoxic species: by binding to specific receptors on neuronal membranes, they induce neuronal hyperexcitability and synaptic dysfunction, thereby accelerating neurodegeneration [53, 54]. Tau, a microtubule-associated protein, stabilizes microtubules and sustains axonal transport of nutrients and neurotransmitter precursors. When hyperphosphorylated, tau detaches from microtubules, misfolds, and aggregates into insoluble assemblies that form neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs), leading to synaptic failure and neuronal injury and contributing to cognitive decline in AD [55, 56].

Multiple interacting factors drive AD progression. Aβ acts upstream of tau, initiating the shift of tau from physiological to toxic states; toxic tau in turn amplifies Aβ neurotoxicity, creating a vicious cycle [57, 58]. Pathogenic Aβ and tau also trigger excessive activation of microglia and astrocytes, sustaining chronic neuroinflammation and exacerbating neuronal damage [59]. Moreover, plaques and smaller Aβ aggregates can impair neurons by disrupting synaptic communication [60], while tau tangles impede axonal transport of nutrients essential for neuronal function and survival.

In addition to amyloid and tau pathology, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and ferroptosis are now recognized as major contributors to AD progression [61–63]. Excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) disrupts cellular redox homeostasis and damages lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids [64]. These changes further aggravate neuronal dysfunction. Mitochondria are particularly vulnerable in AD. Mitochondrial impairment can reduce ATP production, disturb calcium homeostasis, increase ROS generation, and activate intrinsic apoptotic pathways. Such abnormalities not only directly injure neurons, but also amplify Aβ toxicity, tau pathology, and neuroinflammatory responses [65, 66]. Emerging evidence also suggests that ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death characterized by lipid peroxidation, contributes to neuronal injury in AD [67, 68]. Oxidative imbalance, mitochondrial dysfunction, and disordered iron metabolism may interact to promote ferroptotic damage, thereby further exacerbating neurodegeneration [69]. Moreover, persistent oxidative damage, mitochondrial failure, and ferroptosis-related injury may accelerate synaptic degeneration and cognitive decline [70, 71].

Taken together, these findings indicate that AD is a multifactorial pathogenic network involving amyloid accumulation, tau dysregulation, chronic neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, ferroptosis-related injury, and progressive neuronal loss. This mechanistic complexity also provides a rationale for therapeutic strategies that can simultaneously modulate multiple pathological processes. At the molecular level, these pathological processes are closely associated with dysregulation of several signaling pathways, including NF-κB-mediated inflammatory activation [72], impaired Nrf2-dependent antioxidant defense [73], aberrant AKT/GSK-3β signaling associated with tau pathology [74], and ferroptosis-related pathways involved in iron homeostasis and lipid peroxidation [75]. Therapeutic mechanisms of TCM-derived monomers in AD

Given the multifactorial nature of AD, increasing evidence suggests that TCM-derived monomers exert therapeutic effects against AD by modulating multiple interconnected pathological processes [76]. Figure 2 schematically illustrates the multimodal neuroprotective mechanisms by which representative TCM-derived monomers exert therapeutic effects in AD. These monomers exert neuroprotective effects by inhibiting Aβ aggregation, reducing tau hyperphosphorylation, attenuating neuroinflammation, improving mitochondrial function, counteracting oxidative stress, and suppressing ferroptosis [76]. For example, quercetin attenuates neuroinflammation by inhibiting NF-κB and MAPK signaling, while also counteracting oxidative stress through activation of the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway [77]. Resveratrol regulates NF-κB signaling, thereby reducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines and attenuating microglial and astrocytic activation. It also prevents the further assembly of low-order Aβ oligomers into higher-order aggregates and disrupts pre-formed aggregates, thereby markedly reducing cerebral Aβ burden [78]. Curcumin exerts multimodal neuroprotective effects by suppressing NF-κB signaling. Through this mechanism, it modulates TREM2-dependent microglial activation and attenuates neuroinflammatory cascades, downregulates BACE1 to limit Aβ generation and aggregation, and decreases NF-κB-driven SET expression to reduce tau hyperphosphorylation in AD brains [79]. Berberine exerts neuroprotective effects through multiple mechanisms, including the reduction of Aβ deposition and the suppression of ferroptosis-mediated injury through regulation of ferroptosis-associated genes, such as TFR1, ACSL4, DMT1, IREB2, FTH1, and SLC7A11 [80, 81]. Despite these neuroprotective properties, TCM-derived monomers still exhibit poor physicochemical stability, including susceptibility to degradation, rapid metabolism, poor absorption, and short half-lives [82]. Additionally, the selective permeability of the BBB and the complexity of the AD microenvironment hinder the attainment of therapeutic concentrations in brain tissue, particularly for macromolecules and hydrophilic agents, thereby limiting efficacy. Nanotechnology, as a personalized drug-delivery strategy, may overcome these barriers by enhancing the delivery of neuroprotective agents to the brain and maximizing therapeutic benefit [83].

BBB structure and brain delivery strategies

Structure and function of the BBB

The BBB is a critical interface between the cerebral microenvironment and the systemic circulation. It maintains brain homeostasis by regulating molecular exchange between the blood and the brain. The BBB is composed of brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMECs), pericytes, astrocytes, and the basal lamina [89]. The primary function of the BBB is to maintain neural stability. It prevents the entry of harmful substances into the CNS [90]. At the same time, it allows essential nutrients and metabolites to cross. This occurs through passive diffusion or active transport mechanisms. BMECs form continuous monolayers, sealed by tight junctions (TJs). Junctional proteins such as occludin, claudin-5, and zonula occludens scaffolds ZO-1 and ZO-2 create intercellular seals that generate high transendothelial electrical resistance, limit paracellular diffusion, and thereby uphold BBB integrity [91–93]. The exchange of substances across the BBB is tightly regulated by a coordinated network of transporters and ion channels [94]. The functional expression of these transport systems within distinct structural compartments of the BBB governs both the influx of essential endogenous nutrients and the efflux of potentially harmful exogenous compounds, thereby maintaining CNS homeostasis. At the molecular level, the majority of BBB transporters belong to either the solute carrier (SLC) superfamily or the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) superfamily. For instance, glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), a representative SLC transporter, mediates glucose uptake into the brain and is essential for sustaining cerebral energy metabolism [95]. In contrast, efflux transporters such as P-glycoprotein (P-gp; ATP-binding cassette subfamily B member 1, ABCB1) utilize ATP hydrolysis to actively export a broad range of endogenous and xenobiotic substrates from endothelial cells into the circulation, thereby limiting the accumulation of neurotoxic substances within the brain [96]. In addition to transporter-mediated processes, drug passage across the BBB also involves receptor-mediated endocytic pathways. Receptors such as the transferrin receptor (TfR) and the insulin receptor (INSR) facilitate ligand-specific transcytosis through endothelial cells and represent important routes for the delivery of macromolecules into the brain parenchyma [97].

Conventional strategies for brain-targeted delivery

Passive transport

Passive transport across the BBB is a non-specific, energy-independent, and concentration-dependent process that is generally limited to small molecules and occurs via either paracellular transport or passive transcellular diffusion. Paracellular transport, which involves the movement of molecules between adjacent cells, is driven by local concentration gradients and is tightly constrained by TJs [98]. Passive transcellular diffusion represents a second route: in addition to O₂ and CO₂, various lipophilic molecules can diffuse into the brain depending on lipid solubility and molecular size [99]. Because the BBB is formed by lipid bilayers, lipophilic small molecules with a molecular weight < 400–500 Da and fewer than 8–10 hydrogen bonds can traverse the BBB via lipid-mediated free diffusion [100]. By contrast, many constituents of TCM are relatively large and highly polar, which substantially limits their entry via passive transcellular diffusion.

Active transport

Active mechanisms include carrier-mediated transport (CMT), RMT, adsorption-mediated transcytosis (AMT), and cell-mediated transport. CMT is a well-established pathway through which specific transporters mediate the gradient-dependent influx of essential nutrients, including glucose, amino acids, and peptides, from the vascular lumen into the brain parenchyma [101]. For example, GLUT1 is highly expressed on BMECs and mediates glucose uptake [102]. RMT is a principal pathway for macromolecules to traverse the BBB and is essential for the homeostatic delivery of iron, insulin, and leptin [103]. RMT is initiated by ligand binding to specific receptors on the luminal surface of BMECs and proceeds through multiple vesicular trafficking steps to deliver cargo into the parenchyma [104]. AMT exploits the negatively charged endothelial surface: cationic molecules adhere electrostatically to the endothelium, thereby facilitating passage across the BBB [105]. Cell-mediated transport typically involves immune cells recruited to inflamed brain regions by cytokines; for example, neutrophils can act as “Trojan horses” to ferry therapeutics into the brain [106].

In drug delivery, nanomaterials can leverage cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs), receptor-mediated pathways, and cell-mediated transport to cross the BBB [107]. Drug-loaded nanoparticles can be surface-functionalized with targeting ligands such as transferrin (Tf), lactoferrin (Lf), and apolipoprotein E (ApoE). By engaging their respective receptors, these ligands promote efficient BBB translocation and entry into the brain parenchyma [108]. Delivery challenges for TCM-derived monomers in AD

Beyond the intrinsic clinical limitations of TCM-derived monomers, effective brain delivery is further hindered by the BBB and the pathological microenvironment of AD.

BBB-related limitations of TCM-derived monomers

As a crucial protective structure of the CNS, the BBB is a selectively permeable vascular network that protects the brain from blood-borne insults while severely restricting therapeutic delivery, thereby representing a major bottleneck in CNS treatment [109]. Aside from gases (e.g., O₂, CO₂) and lipophilic small molecules, passive paracellular diffusion of larger drugs is limited; indeed, fewer than 2% of small-molecule drugs are able to cross the BBB [110]. Most bioactive constituents of TCM, including saponins and alkaloids, are relatively large and exhibit poor permeability, which limits their ability to cross the intact BBB. As a result, their distribution within brain tissue is restricted, thereby constraining their clinical utility.

Furthermore, TCM components that enter endothelial cells encounter active efflux mediated by the membrane transporter P-gp. Localized to the luminal membrane of BBB endothelial cells, P-gp uses ATP to pump therapeutic agents back into the circulation, thereby directly reducing drug accumulation in brain tissue [111, 112]. Due to the BBB’s stringent selectivity, TCM preparations and their active constituents that fall outside preferred ranges of molecular size and physicochemical properties are largely excluded. This limitation significantly reduces the clinical efficacy of conventional TCM formulations for CNS disorders [113].

AD microenvironment-associated barriers to drug delivery

The progression of AD is accompanied by pronounced disruption of the BBB, primarily driven by vascular degeneration, dysregulated molecular transport, impaired angiogenesis, and sustained inflammatory responses [114]. Under pathological AD conditions, BBB dysfunction not only compromises the clearance of Aβ but also facilitates the abnormal accumulation of pathological aggregates, including Aβ plaques and tau tangles [115]. These processes ultimately contribute to neuronal injury and cognitive impairment through amplified neuroinflammation and direct neurotoxic effects [116]. Concomitantly, both the structural integrity and functional properties of the BBB undergo pathological remodeling, thereby markedly influencing the efficiency of drug delivery to the CNS as well as intraparenchymal drug distribution [99]. Collectively, the principal mechanisms by which BBB dysfunction affects CNS drug delivery can be categorized into three interrelated processes: (1) alterations in BBB permeability; (2) dysregulation of transporter protein expression and activity; and (3) reactive glial proliferation leading to the formation of a secondary physical barrier.

Altered BBB permeability represents a central contributor to BBB dysfunction in AD [117]. Enhanced neuroinflammatory signaling, pericyte loss, and reduced expression of tight junction proteins act synergistically to increase the paracellular permeability of the BBB, as shown in Fig. 4 [118]. Accumulating evidence indicates that aberrant astrocyte activation plays a pivotal role in BBB structural compromise. Specifically, Aβ₁₋₄₂ induces excessive astrocytic activation, stimulates the release of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and activates endothelial matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9). This cascade results in the downregulation of tight junction proteins, such as claudin-5, thereby further exacerbating BBB permeability disruption [119].

Beyond tight junction damage, BBB dysfunction in AD is closely linked to extensive remodeling of key transporter proteins and their transport capacity, as shown in Fig. 5 [120]. Persistent neuroinflammation and pathological stressors profoundly reshape the transporter expression profile of BBB endothelial cells. This remodeling is characterized by reduced expression of GLUT1, low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1), and P-gp, accompanied by upregulation of the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE). Together, these changes impair BBB transport homeostasis, consequently altering drug distribution and therapeutic efficacy within the brain [121, 122]. Notably, impaired cerebral glucose transport is an early hallmark of AD and is strongly associated with reduced GLUT1 expression at the BBB. Experimental studies demonstrate that GLUT1 deficiency promotes early cerebral microvascular degeneration, decreases cerebral blood flow, and induces vascular dysfunction, ultimately damaging BBB structure. These alterations markedly diminish Aβ clearance, suppress neuronal activity, and accelerate neuronal loss and neurodegeneration [123]. Moreover, GLUT1 dysfunction has been shown to downregulate LRP1 expression, further compounding defects in Aβ transport and clearance [124]. BBB transporter proteins, including LRP1, P-gp, and RAGE, cooperatively regulate transendothelial Aβ trafficking and play an essential role in maintaining cerebral Aβ homeostasis. Dysregulation of these transport systems is therefore intimately associated with AD pathogenesis [116]. Functionally, LRP1 primarily mediates Aβ efflux from the brain into the peripheral circulation, whereas RAGE facilitates the influx of circulating Aβ into the CNS. Imbalance between these opposing transport pathways destabilizes BBB junctional integrity, disrupts barrier function, and significantly compromises the efficiency of CNS drug delivery [125]. In parallel, both the expression and transport activity of P-gp are consistently reduced in AD. As a major efflux transporter at the BBB, impaired P-gp function not only limits the clearance of endogenous substrates such as Aβ but also modulates the export of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines. Furthermore, because many therapeutic agents are P-gp substrates, its dysfunction adds an additional layer of complexity to the design and optimization of AD drug delivery strategies [126].

In addition to molecular transport abnormalities, reactive glial proliferation constitutes a significant physical barrier that further restricts drug penetration across the BBB. Under AD pathological conditions, astrocytes undergo pronounced reactive gliosis, characterized by elevated expression of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), cellular hypertrophy, and increased cell density. These changes lead to the formation of a dense perivascular glial sheath, which further impedes the diffusion of therapeutic agents into the brain parenchyma [127].

Despite the frequent observation of BBB dysfunction in AD, such alterations are typically heterogeneous and region-specific rather than globally uniform. Consequently, for most therapeutic agents, passive diffusion resulting from increased BBB permeability alone is insufficient to ensure effective brain delivery. Instead, precise and controllable trans-BBB transport strategies are required. Importantly, certain endothelial receptors and transport pathways remain relatively preserved or retain functional plasticity under pathological conditions. For instance, transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) expression remains relatively stable in the brain microvascular endothelium of AD mouse models, and Aβ-associated stimuli do not significantly alter TfR1 expression in human brain microvascular endothelial cell systems. This stability underscores the potential of TfR1 as a viable drug-delivery target even in disease settings [125, 128]. Accordingly, rational design of nanocarrier surface modifications that selectively exploit preserved BBB transport pathways represents a promising strategy to enhance brain drug exposure and improve therapeutic efficacy in AD.

Types of lipid-based nanocarriers

Given the combined constraints imposed by BBB selectivity and the AD pathological microenvironment, conventional formulations of TCM-derived monomers are often insufficient to achieve effective brain delivery. Consequently, nanotechnology-enabled delivery systems have been increasingly explored to address these challenges. Lipid-based nanocarriers, including exosomes, liposomes, SLNs, and NLCs, are composed of phospholipid monolayers or bilayers and exhibit high biocompatibility, efficient cellular uptake, and substantial drug-loading capacity. They can enhance BBB permeability and enable targeted delivery to brain regions, attracting broad interest for CNS drug delivery [42, 129]. Exosomes

Exosomes are small extracellular vesicles derived from the endosomal system of eukaryotic cells, with diameters ranging from 30 to 150 nm [130]. In the context of AD therapy, exosomes are particularly attractive as delivery vehicles for TCM-derived monomers. They can protect encapsulated cargos from degradation, improve drug exposure in the brain, and facilitate BBB transport [131]. In addition, they may enhance lesion-oriented delivery while maintaining favorable biocompatibility [132]. Therefore, exosomes represent a promising platform for improving the brain delivery of natural compounds with poor pharmacokinetic properties. Exosomes can be obtained from various sources, including mesenchymal stem cells, immune cells (e.g., macrophages, microglia), and neurons [133]. Studies have shown that exosomes respond to inflammation-induced chemotactic cues, enabling directed migration and targeted accumulation at diseased neuronal sites [134]. Exosome-mediated delivery offers low toxicity, reduced immunogenicity, and high engineering potential [135].

Effective exosome delivery depends on the selection of the parental cell type, surface functionalization, and drug-loading capacity. Exosomes possess intrinsic BBB-crossing potential; tetraspanins on their surface (e.g., CD63, CD9, CD81) facilitate targeting and selective uptake by recipient cells [136]. Proteins inherited from donor cells further promote fusion with specific targets [137]. For instance, neural stem cell-derived exosomes can recognize heparan sulfate proteoglycans on BBB endothelial cells [138], whereas macrophage-derived exosomes efficiently traverse the BBB through interactions between lymphocyte function-associated antigen-1 (LFA-1) and intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) [139]. In addition, blood-derived exosomes can selectively deliver drugs to the brain via TfR-mediated interactions [140]. Exosomes are readily functionalized; decorating their membranes with targeting ligands enables active delivery with enhanced specificity and affinity [141, 142].

Owing to their endogenous origin and favorable biocompatibility, exosomes have emerged as versatile platforms for delivering therapeutic cargos. Recent advances in exosome engineering, particularly in surface functionalization and cargo-loading strategies, have focused on improving targeting specificity and therapeutic performance, as shown in Fig. 7 [143]. To maximize drug-loading capacity, a range of loading approaches have been developed and can be broadly classified into passive and active methods. Passive loading techniques include simple incubation, freeze-thaw cycling, and surfactant-assisted incorporation, whereas active loading methods involve electroporation, sonication, and transfection [144]. These protocols differ substantially in encapsulation efficiency, cargo stability, and their impact on membrane integrity. Targeting precision is commonly enhanced through surface modification, typically by conjugating receptor-binding ligands to the exosomal membrane. For example, exosomes functionalized with brain-homing peptides, such as rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG29) or TfR ligands, have been used to deliver neuroprotective agents in models of CNS disorders, including AD [145–147]. Accordingly, systematic optimization of preparation and loading workflows remains a key challenge for advancing effective and translatable exosome-based therapies. Future advancements in receptor targeting and ligand optimization will be crucial for improving drug delivery efficiency and ensuring successful clinical translation.

Liposomes

Liposomes consist of an outer bilayer formed by amphiphilic phospholipids that encloses an aqueous core, allowing co-encapsulation of hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs [148, 149]. Liposomes typically range in size from approximately 20 to 1000 nm and they are composed of natural or synthetic phospholipids, cholesterol, and other charged lipid derivatives [150]. The unsaturation of phospholipids, the length of fatty-acyl chains, and the phase-transition temperature are key factors that determine bilayer stability, whereas cholesterol confers rigidity and increases mechanical strength [150, 151]. Owing to their membrane mimicry, liposomes can deliver cargo via membrane fusion, thereby bypassing relatively slow and inefficient endocytosis [152]. Phospholipids are susceptible to degradation, and in vivo stability is influenced by intrinsic properties (e.g., size, surface charge, lipid composition) and environmental factors (e.g., pH, temperature) [153]. To prolong circulation and enhance brain-targeted delivery, macromolecular coatings such as polymers, polysaccharides, peptides, antibodies, and aptamers are commonly introduced [154]. For example, “stealth” coatings such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) prevent vesicle–vesicle fusion and attenuate plasma protein adsorption, thereby prolonging systemic circulation time [155]. Brain targeting can be further improved by conjugating bioactive ligands (e.g., peptides, antibodies, aptamers) to the liposomal surface or to PEG termini to engage receptors overexpressed on brain endothelial cells [154].

An expanding body of evidence supports the advantages of liposomes for CNS delivery [156, 157]. Liposomes can carry drugs via noninvasive routes and, owing to their biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and structural stability, they can also extend systemic exposure and increase intraparenchymal retention [156]. For AD therapy, liposomes are well-suited carriers for TCM-derived monomers. They can improve the solubility of poorly water-soluble compounds, reduce premature degradation, and prolong systemic circulation [158]. Their structural versatility also enables surface modification for BBB targeting and brain delivery [159]. These properties make liposomes an effective platform for enhancing the therapeutic potential of TCM-derived monomers in AD.

Solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs)

SLNs are submicron spherical nanoparticles composed of a solid lipid core that encapsulates the drug, typically ranging from 40 to 1000 nm in diameter [160]. They combine the advantages of polymeric nanoparticles and liposomes, offering distinct advantages for drug delivery [161]. The solid lipid fraction (0.1%–30%) may include triglycerides, free fatty acids, steroids, and waxes. Lipophilic drugs incorporated into the core are protected from degradation and can be released in a controlled manner. Surfactants, typically accounting for approximately 0.5% to 5% of the formulation, such as phospholipids, soybean lecithin, and egg lecithin, stabilize the dispersion by forming a barrier around the outer lipid matrix [160, 162, 163]. Common preparation methods include high-shear/high-speed homogenization with ultrasonication, high-/low-temperature high-pressure homogenization, solvent evaporation, double-emulsion techniques, and spray drying [164]. Both hydrophobic and hydrophilic drugs can be encapsulated; hydrophobic drugs load readily due to their affinity for the lipid matrix, whereas hydrophilic drugs typically require additional processing, often using the double-emulsion technique [165].

SLNs feature low cytotoxicity, high biocompatibility, improved drug stability, controllable release, and relatively simple manufacturing processes. They are also amenable to surface functionalization for brain-targeted delivery, making them promising vehicles for drug delivery [165, 166]. SLNs have been used to load active compounds such as β-elemene, curcumin, and Res [167–169], thereby enhancing bioavailability and BBB penetration for brain delivery, which represents a promising strategy for the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases. SLNs are also valuable carriers for the delivery of TCM-derived monomers in AD. Their solid lipid matrix can improve formulation stability, protect encapsulated compounds from degradation, and enhance bioavailability [42]. In addition, SLNs can be engineered to facilitate BBB transport and increase brain accumulation [170]. These properties are particularly relevant for TCM-derived monomers that exhibit limited stability or poor delivery efficiency in conventional formulations.

Nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs)

NLCs, often termed second-generation lipid nanoparticles, share a generally spherical morphology with SLNs but differ fundamentally in core architecture. NLCs are formulated by combining liquid lipids, solid lipids, and surfactants at defined ratios within an aqueous dispersion medium. Unlike SLNs, they incorporate liquid lipids into the solid lipid phase, which enhances material versatility. NLCs offer several advantages, including low cytotoxicity, high biocompatibility, biodegradability, enhanced drug stability, controlled release, and the ability to be manufactured without the use of organic solvents [171]. The structural design of NLCs creates a more “loose/defective” crystalline lattice, which provides additional free volume for drug inclusion, promoting dispersion of drug molecules as monomers and amorphous clusters within the matrix, thereby enhancing drug loading and encapsulation efficiency, reducing drug leakage, and achieving smoother release profiles [172]. NLCs represent another useful platform for the delivery of TCM-derived monomers in AD. Compared with SLNs, their less ordered lipid matrix may improve drug loading and reduce drug leakage [173]. NLCs can also enhance formulation stability and facilitate brain delivery [174]. These advantages make them particularly suitable for poorly water-soluble natural compounds used in AD therapy.

Although this review focuses primarily on lipid-based nanocarriers, other nanoplatforms have also been extensively investigated for the delivery of TCM-derived monomers in AD and other CNS disorders. These include metal nanoparticles, carbon-based nanoparticles, mesoporous silica nanoparticles, polymeric nanoparticles, and albumin nanoparticles [175–177]. Such systems offer several complementary advantages, including structural tunability, responsiveness to external stimuli, and strong cargo stability.

Innovative delivery strategies

To further enhance brain-targeting specificity and nanocarrier stability, two complementary approaches are widely used. First, decorating nanocarrier surfaces with targeting ligands (e.g., peptides) improves selectivity, thereby enhancing BBB translocation and neuron-specific delivery. Second, coating nanoparticles with exogenous cell membranes or exosomes (e.g., derived from erythrocytes, stem cells, or neurons) generates biomimetic systems that promote immune evasion and prolong circulation time [133, 178]. Recent investigations suggest that combining two complementary surface modification strategies markedly improves the brain-delivery efficacy of nanoparticles. In particular, nanoparticles concurrently modified with targeting ligands (such as CPPs or Tf) and biomimetic coatings have been shown to traverse the BBB and deliver therapeutic cargo to neural tissue [179]. Ligand functionalization strategies

Functional ligands enhance BBB penetration and targeting by binding specific receptors on the endothelium and are therefore central to drug-delivery designs for CNS disorders [180]. Neves et al. prepared transferrin-modified SLNs and NLCs loaded with curcumin; in a human cerebral microvascular endothelial cell line (hCMEC/D3) monolayer BBB model, transferrin functionalization increased apparent permeability by approximately 1.5-fold relative to non-functionalized counterparts [181]. In a separate study, ApoE-decorated SLNs loaded with Res exhibited approximately 1.8-fold higher BBB permeability than non-functionalized SLNs in the same hCMEC/D3 model [182]. Common functional ligands for BBB penetration: targets, mechanisms of action, and advantages

Despite the clear advantages of ligand-mediated functionalization in enhancing BBB translocation and targeting specificity, ligand decoration alone may not fully address other critical delivery barriers, including rapid systemic clearance, opsonization, and immune recognition. These limitations highlight the need for complementary strategies that can endow nanocarriers with prolonged circulation, immune evasion, and improved stability in vivo. In this context, biomimetic modification approaches, particularly those based on natural cell membranes or extracellular vesicles, have emerged as effective solutions to further optimize brain-targeted delivery performance.

Biomimetic modification strategies

Prolonging a drug’s circulatory half-life and promoting its accumulation at cerebral lesions are critical to achieving therapeutic benefit in brain disorders. In recent years, biomimetic approaches that integrate natural cell membranes or exosomes with nanotechnology have enabled the development of membrane-engineered nanoparticles. Due to their biocompatibility and low immunogenicity, these constructs show considerable potential for the treatment of brain diseases by enhancing BBB penetration and targeted delivery within the CNS [195–197].

Cell membrane-coated nanoparticles

Rapid opsonization and clearance by the immune system present significant challenges to nanoparticle-based delivery. Cloaking nanoparticles with cell membranes is a promising strategy to evade immune clearance [198]. These cell membrane-coated nanoparticles typically exhibit a core-shell architecture, in which a therapeutic payload-carrying core is enclosed within a membrane shell that facilitates targeted delivery. Membrane coating can introduce specific transport mechanisms and new biological functions, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy. By inheriting key properties of the source cells, including biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, inflammation homing, immune evasion, homotypic targeting, and prolonged circulation, these systems have attracted considerable attention [199]. Inflammation homing refers to recruitment by signals released from inflamed tissue; for example, macrophage membranes endow nanoparticles with tropism toward inflammatory sites [200]. Immune evasion and homotypic targeting are closely linked to membrane proteins: surface proteins on the camouflaging membrane can minimize immune recognition and prolong blood residence [201].

Common membrane sources, key features, and advantages for biomimetic coating are summarized in Table 3. Commonly used membrane sources include RBC membranes, cancer cell membranes, and immune cell membranes. Among these, RBC membranes are readily obtainable at scale and protect bioactivity while prolonging circulation and minimizing immunogenicity [202]; they have been broadly applied to fabricate nanoparticles with innate biocompatibility, biodegradability, and minimal immunostimulation. The long-circulation behavior of RBC membranes is mediated by a set of membrane proteins, with CD47 playing a pivotal role by interacting with macrophage SIRPα to evade immune clearance, enabling prolonged in vivo persistence [203]. Likewise, membranes derived from immune cells (e.g., neutrophils and macrophages) can confer immune evasion and facilitate the delivery of nanoparticles to inflamed sites [197].

Researchers have also explored hybrid membranes derived from different cell types. Compared to single-source coatings, hybrid membranes retain complementary physicochemical and biological traits from multiple cell types [204]. Some hybrid membranes display whole-cell antigenic characteristics; for example, nanoparticles coated with RBC–microglial hybrid membranes exhibit enhanced evasion of macrophage phagocytosis and homotypic targeting toward microglia. Li et al. prepared aspirin/curcumin co-loaded nanoparticles wrapped with an RBC–microglia hybrid membrane [205]. This formulation targeted chronically activated microglia in the AD brain, prolonged systemic circulation, improved cognitive performance in AD model mice, reduced cerebral Aβ deposition, and decreased M1 microglial polarization. These effects were attributed to the structural stability, biocompatibility, and selective targeting conferred by the biomimetic membrane coating. Building upon membrane camouflage, investigators also employ targeting peptides to further enhance brain tropism. Peptides such as T7, TGN, and TAT can increase BBB affinity and deliver drugs preferentially to CNS lesions [206]. For example, as shown in Fig. 9A, Liu et al. used RVG29-modified RBC membranes to cloak curcumin nanoparticles, thereby prolonging systemic circulation and enhancing brain targeting [207]. RVG29-RBCm/Cur-NCs showed improved in vivo circulation and retention in the brain compared with Cur and RBCm/Cur-NCs, with a 1.19-fold longer blood circulation half-life than RBCm/Cur-NCs (15.236 ± 1.340 h vs. 12.849 ± 1.115 h). Sun et al. further wrapped self-assembled nanoparticles with T7-modified macrophage membranes to improve BBB penetration [208]. Gu et al. developed TGN-functionalized, RBC membrane-coated curcumin nanoparticles that achieved efficient BBB transport, improved spatial learning and memory in AD mice, and suppressed microglial and astrocytic activation, thereby alleviating cognitive deficits [209]. Consistently, in an in vitro coculture model, fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled TGN-RBC-NPs reached a transport rate of 29.64% at 12 h, which was markedly higher than that of NPs-FITC (5.44%) and RBC-NPs-FITC (3.72%), demonstrating the strong BBB-penetrating capability of TGN-RBC-NPs. In addition, as shown in Fig. 9B, Liu et al. developed dual-peptide-functionalized, RBC membrane-coated PLGA nanoparticles (TGN/RAP12-RBC-NPs@Ltn), in which TGN promoted BBB translocation and RAP12 enabled mitochondrial targeting in microglia [210]. UPLC-MS/MS analysis of brain biodistribution after tail-vein injection further demonstrated that TGN/RAP12-conjugated nanoparticles achieved significantly greater brain accumulation of Ltn than free Ltn, supporting their enhanced brain-targeting capability. This TGN/RAP12-RBC-NPs@Ltn platform prolonged circulation, improved cognitive performance in APP/PS1 mice, and alleviated neuroinflammation by restoring mitochondrial metabolic homeostasis.

Exosome-coated nanoparticles

Exosomes can facilitate brain-targeted delivery in CNS disorders through surface-associated molecules that mediate multiple BBB transport pathways [221]. By integrating exosomes with biomaterials such as liposomes or polymers, researchers have developed engineered vesicular systems commonly referred to as “hybrid exosomes” [143]. These constructs are designed to preserve the endogenous targeting and biological functions of exosomes while overcoming major limitations of either synthetic nanocarriers or native vesicles, including rapid systemic clearance, premature drug leakage, and suboptimal delivery efficiency. Hybridization integrates the intrinsic biocompatibility of exosomes with the structural tunability and multifunctionality of synthetic nanocarriers, thereby improving drug-loading efficiency and delivery performance while maintaining a favorable safety profile [222]. To date, numerous studies have generated hybrids of synthetic nanoparticles, primarily liposomes, and cell-derived exosomes using diverse membrane fusion strategies to improve therapeutic performance and translational potential [223]. Figure 10A schematically illustrates the generation of exosome-liposome hybrids using multiple membrane fusion strategies [224], with representative workflows based on freeze-thaw and extrusion shown in Fig. 10B and C, respectively [225, 226]. Hill et al. developed a biomimetic nanoparticle platform by coating nanoparticles with exosomes derived from U-87 glioblastoma cells using a hybrid extrusion method through a 200-nm membrane [227]. This formulation retained natural exosome surface markers, which facilitated BBB penetration, improved brain targeting specificity, reduced off-target distribution, and enhanced therapeutic efficacy.

Although exosomes are enriched in transmembrane proteins (e.g., CD81 and CD9) and integrins that provide intrinsic targeting capacity [228], their native targeting specificity may remain insufficient for efficient brain delivery. Surface engineering can enhance brain tropism, commonly through genetic modification or bioorthogonal click chemistry. Khongkow et al. generated neuron-derived exosomes expressing RVG-Lamp2b and used them to coat gold nanoparticles by serial extrusion. The resulting RVG-exosome-coated nanoparticles showed enhanced BBB transit and lesional accumulation, with superior brain-targeting performance confirmed in both in vitro and in vivo BBB models [229]. Haroon et al. functionalized exosome surfaces with RVG29 via bioorthogonal click chemistry, which significantly enhanced neuronal targeting compared with unmodified exosomes in both in vitro and in vivo models [230]. Ex vivo imaging of major organs further showed that RVG-Exo exhibited a 2-fold higher fluorescence signal in the brain than unmodified exosomes at 48 h, while biodistribution analysis demonstrated sustained brain retention for up to 48 h before gradual clearance, highlighting the improved brain-targeting efficiency of RVG-modified exosomes.

Applications of lipid-based nano-TCM in AD therapy

Exosome-mediated nano-TCM

As shown in Fig. 11A, Wang et al. prepared exosome-curcumin (Exo-Cur) using RAW264.7 macrophage-derived exosomes that retained LFA-1 [231]. By specifically engaging ICAM-1 on BMECs, Exo-Cur exhibited active targeting and traversed the BBB via RMT, thereby enhancing the cerebral bioavailability of Cur. This formulation inhibited tau phosphorylation by activating the AKT/GSK-3β signaling pathway, thereby limiting neuronal death and alleviating AD-related symptoms. In addition, as shown in Fig. 11B, Qi et al. developed plasma exosomes (Exo) loaded with quercetin (Exo-Que). Exo-Que enhanced drug bioavailability, improved quercetin’s brain targeting, and effectively alleviated cognitive dysfunction in AD mice induced by okadaic acid (OA) [131]. In a separate study, Yang et al. loaded Cur and methylene blue (MB) into RAW264.7-derived exosomes to generate Exo-Cur + MB. Exo-Cur + MB suppressed tau phosphorylation through AKT/GSK-3β activation and reversed OA-induced cognitive deficits in AD mice by reducing apoptosis [232]. Zhao et al. utilized microglia-derived exosomes as a dual-drug carrier for berberine (Ber) and palmatine (Pal) [233]. The resulting Exos-Ber/Pal combined homotypic tropism toward microglia with synergistic pharmacological effects. In APP/PS1 mice, Exos-Ber/Pal improved learning and memory, markedly reduced microglial activation and plaque burden, decreased pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β), and increased anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-4, IL-10), demonstrating substantial attenuation of neuroinflammation. Liposome-mediated nano-TCM

Salidroside (Sal) and icariin (Ica) exhibit neuroprotective activity. Zhang et al. developed Angiopep-2-modified, dual-drug-loaded liposomes (Ang-Sal/Ica-Lip) [237]. In vitro, Ang-Sal/Ica-Lip demonstrated the highest transmembrane transport efficiency, and in APP/PS1 mice, sustained brain fluorescence persisted for up to 12 h. By traversing the BBB and targeting affected brain regions, these liposomes attenuated neuronal apoptosis, suppressed inflammation, and enhanced antioxidant defenses, thereby significantly improving cognitive function in AD model mice. A transferrin-modified liposomal formulation of osthole (Tf-Ost-Lip) was developed to overcome the compound’s poor solubility, limited bioavailability, and restricted BBB permeability [238]. The system enhanced cellular uptake and BBB translocation in vitro, prolonged systemic circulation and increased brain accumulation in vivo, and ultimately improved the neuroprotective efficacy of osthole in APP/PS1 AD models, supporting its potential as a targeted delivery strategy for AD therapy. As shown in Fig. 12A, Chen et al. developed an ApoE-modified liposomal co-delivery system encapsulating resveratrol and salidroside (ApoE-Res/Sal-Lips) to overcome the poor solubility, limited specificity, and insufficient BBB penetration of the free compounds [239]. The formulation enhanced cellular uptake and BBB translocation in vitro and improved brain targeting in vivo. In APP/PS1 mouse models, ApoE-Res/Sal-Lips attenuated AD-related pathological features, ameliorated learning and memory deficits, and modulated oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and apoptosis-related pathways, highlighting the therapeutic potential of receptor-targeted liposomal co-delivery for AD treatment. In addition, Wei et al. designed a dual-targeted, acid-responsive nanoregulator, Res@TcMNP/ASO, to treat neuroinflammation in AD [240]. The cationic liposomal platform co-encapsulated a TREM2-lowering antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) and resveratrol (Res), and its surface was functionalized with D-T7 peptide and MG1 peptide to target the BBB and microglia, respectively. Res@TcMNP/ASO crossed the BBB via D-T7 and was specifically delivered to M1-type microglia via MG1, enabling synchronized delivery of both agents to activated microglia. This “immunomodulation + antioxidation” strategy restored microglial immune function through the ASO, while resveratrol mitigated ROS-driven stimulation; together, these effects suppressed chronic microglial activation and alleviated neuroinflammation in AD. SLN-mediated nano-TCM

Loureiro et al. developed SLNs loaded with resveratrol for AD therapy, functionalizing their surfaces with an anti-TfR monoclonal antibody (OX26 mAb) [248]. In an in vitro BBB model, OX26-modified SLNs exhibited enhanced endothelial uptake and facilitated RMT across the barrier compared to SLNs modified with an alternative antibody (LB509) or unmodified SLNs. In another study, Pinheiro et al. engineered RVG29-functionalized SLNs loaded with quercetin. These nanoparticles traversed the BBB via RMT, inhibited Aβ fibrillization, and conferred neuroprotection [249]. In the hCMEC/D3 monolayer BBB model, RVG29 functionalization increased apparent permeability by 1.5-fold relative to non-functionalized counterparts.

NLC-mediated nano-TCM

Raju et al. prepared NLCs loaded with berberine and evaluated therapeutic efficacy in an AlCl₃-induced AD rat model [250]. The formulation exerted neuroprotection by multiple mechanisms, including inhibition of acetylcholinesterase activity, reduction of Aβ levels, mitigation of oxidative stress, and reduction of cholesterol levels. Khodabandelou et al. produced silibinin-loaded NLCs that improved cognition and behavior in Aβ-induced AD rats. Astaxanthin (AST), a natural antioxidant with neuroprotective, anti-amyloidogenic, anti-apoptotic, and anti-inflammatory activities, has also been incorporated into NLC systems [251]. Shehata et al. formulated dual-loaded NLCs containing donepezil (DPL) and AST (DPL/AST-NLCs) that ameliorated cognitive and behavioral deficits in AlCl₃-induced AD rats [252]. DPL/AST-NLCs showed pronounced anti-amyloidogenic, antioxidant, anti-acetylcholinesterase, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic effects, significantly improving cortical and hippocampal pathology. Pinheiro et al. also prepared transferrin-decorated SLNs and NLCs loaded with quercetin to traverse the BBB by engaging overexpressed TfR on brain endothelial cells [253]. The particles measured approximately 200 nm with zeta potentials below − 30 mV. In the hCMEC/D3 BBB model, NLCs achieved greater permeability than SLNs; in vitro assays further demonstrated inhibition of Aβ protofibril formation by both nanoparticle types.

Dual-functionalized and biomimetically modified lipid-based nano-TCM

This section focuses on dual-functionalized lipid-based nano-TCM systems that integrate biomimetic modification with ligand-mediated targeting for enhanced brain delivery in AD.

Yang et al. developed a macrophage-membrane-coated SLN system and further modified the membrane with RVG29 and triphenylphosphonium (TPP), yielding dual-functional RVG/TPP-MASLNs designed to enable mitochondrial delivery of an antioxidant to neurons [254]. The macrophage membrane provided immune evasion, reducing recognition by the mononuclear phagocyte system and prolonging circulation. RVG29 bound nicotinic acetylcholine receptors on neurons, facilitating BBB traversal and neuron targeting, whereas the cationic TPP moiety harnessed the mitochondrial membrane potential to drive subcellular localization. Using genistein (GS) as a model antioxidant payload, both in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated suppression of AD-related pathology, including reduced Aβ aggregation, mitigation of hippocampal oxidative injury, and decreased aberrant glial activation.

As shown in Fig. 13A, Han et al. engineered NLCs coated with RBC membranes and dually functionalized the coating with RVG29 and TPP to enable mitochondrial targeting in central neurons [255]. Resveratrol served as the model drug. RVG29 targeted neuronal nicotinic acetylcholine receptors to enable brain delivery, whereas TPP directed the cargo to mitochondria. The nanocarrier exhibited stable and sustained release, reduced Aβ-related mitochondrial oxidative stress, and alleviated AD pathology, resulting in significant improvements in memory deficits in APP/PS1 transgenic mice.

As shown in Fig. 13B, Zhou et al. developed transferrin-modified hybrid extracellular vesicles (Tf-hEVs-Ber/Pal) for the coordinated delivery of berberine (Ber) and palmatine (Pal) [256]. The Tf-hEVs-Ber/Pal hybrid nanovesicles were prepared via a two-step process: first, Ber-loaded liposomes (Tf-Lip-Ber) were synthesized using the thin-film dispersion method. Subsequently, Tf-Lip-Ber, microglia-derived exosomes, and Pal were mixed in specific proportions to facilitate the formation of the hybrid vesicles through the water-bath method. Tf-hEVs-Ber/Pal crossed the BBB via TfR-mediated transport and targeted microglia through the homotypic tropism of microglial exosomes. In AD mouse models, Tf-hEVs-Ber/Pal reduced aberrant Aβ aggregation, attenuated core pathological damage, and selectively modulated the neuroinflammatory microenvironment. Specifically, it decreased the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and TNF-α, while increasing the levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-4 and IL-10. The system promoted microglial polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 to the anti-inflammatory M2 state, resulting in marked improvements in cognitive and behavioral performance.

Clinical translation challenges

Despite encouraging progress in preclinical studies, the clinical translation of lipid-based nano-TCM systems remains challenging [257]. Beyond therapeutic efficacy, these platforms must also meet the practical requirements of scalable manufacturing, formulation reproducibility, robust quality control, regulatory feasibility, and long-term biosafety. Major challenges in large-scale manufacturing, quality control, and batch-to-batch consistency remain unresolved. Moreover, the lack of standardized regulatory guidelines and the limited availability of clinical trial data continue to hinder regulatory approval and broader clinical application [258]. These issues are particularly relevant for multifunctional and biomimetically modified lipid-based nanocarriers, whose increased structural and compositional complexity may further complicate standardization, batch consistency, and safety evaluation. Therefore, from the perspective of clinical translation, scalable manufacturability, regulatory feasibility, and long-term safety should be incorporated as essential considerations throughout the entire process of formulation design and optimization.

Manufacturing, scalability, and regulatory considerations

Most current studies remain limited to laboratory-scale preparation and short-term efficacy evaluation. However, successful translation requires not only therapeutic efficacy but also reproducible manufacturing and stable formulation performance. A major challenge lies in large-scale production. Although many lipid-based nanocarriers can be prepared successfully at the laboratory scale, their physicochemical properties may change during industrial scale-up [259]. Key parameters, including particle size distribution, zeta potential, encapsulation efficiency, membrane integrity, and surface ligand density, must remain consistent across batches. This issue is particularly critical for biomimetically modified lipid-based nanocarriers, as their structural complexity increases the risk of batch-to-batch heterogeneity [260].

For example, the clinical translation of cell membrane-modified lipid-based nanocarriers remains constrained by multiple factors, including membrane source selection, scalable membrane production, nanoparticle stability, controlled drug release, biocompatibility and safety evaluation, immunogenicity, and the broader technical complexity of clinical implementation [261]. In addition, multistep preparation procedures, such as membrane extraction, surface conjugation, hybridization, and post-loading modification, may further reduce process reproducibility and complicate standardization [262]. From a quality-control perspective, a robust framework of quantifiable and reproducible evaluation criteria is needed, ranging from source-level cell bank management to the monitoring of membrane protein composition and functional activity during membrane extraction. At the same time, efficient purification methods for membrane isolation and a standardized quality-control system remain essential for clinically relevant development [263]. Storage stability is another practical concern. Lipid-based nanocarriers may undergo aggregation, fusion, cargo leakage, or chemical degradation during storage [264]. These changes can compromise physicochemical integrity and impair biological performance.

The clinical translation of TCM-loaded lipid-based nanocarriers is also closely constrained by regulatory requirements. Regulatory agencies such as the FDA require comprehensive evaluation of nanomaterial properties, including biodistribution, toxicity, immunogenicity, and long-term safety [265]. At the same time, nanoparticle manufacturing presents major regulatory challenges, particularly in relation to reproducibility and batch-to-batch consistency, because even subtle variations in particle size, morphology, or surface characteristics may alter biological behavior and, consequently, affect regulatory assessment [266]. Furthermore, regulatory evaluation is substantially more complex for multifunctional lipid-based nano-TCM systems than for conventional nanomedicines. This challenge is especially evident in platforms that combine TCM-derived monomers with targeting ligands, natural cell membranes, or exosomes, owing to the complexity of their manufacturing processes and the associated limitations in quality control. In such systems, regulatory assessment must extend beyond the encapsulated therapeutic agents to also consider carrier composition, source materials, surface engineering, manufacturing procedures, and process-related impurities [267, 268]. Major challenges include scalable production, batch-to-batch reproducibility, membrane source standardization, cargo heterogeneity, storage stability, and robust physicochemical characterization [261]. In addition, source variability, biological heterogeneity, and the lack of clearly defined release specifications make it difficult to establish uniform standards.

In response to these challenges, current efforts to advance the clinical translation of biomimetically modified lipid-based nanocarriers are increasingly focused on scalable manufacturing and quality control. For example, emerging microfluidic platforms may enable more integrated and automated workflows spanning cell culture, exosome isolation, and cargo loading [269, 270]. From a quality-control perspective, advanced analytical approaches, including nano-flow cytometry and single-exosome proteomic analysis, are creating new opportunities for more rigorous characterization, consistency assessment, and functional quality evaluation of exosome-based systems [271].

Therefore, overcoming the bottlenecks in clinical translation will require greater emphasis on reproducible preparation processes, standardized release criteria, and analytically validated characterization methods. Such methods should accurately capture both the physicochemical properties of lipid-based nanocarriers and the functional attributes conferred by biomimetic components. From a translational perspective, regulatory feasibility, manufacturing simplicity, and quality assurance should be incorporated at an early stage of formulation design. In parallel, optimization of nanoparticle preparation and of cell membrane and exosome extraction procedures will be essential for establishing a comprehensive quality-control system spanning raw materials, intermediates, and final products, thereby improving regulatory acceptability and facilitating clinical translation.

Long-term safety and immunogenicity

In addition to manufacturing and regulatory challenges, long-term safety also requires careful evaluation. Although lipid-based nanocarriers are generally considered biocompatible, their long-term safety is influenced by multiple formulation parameters and physicochemical properties, including lipid composition, surfactants, particle size, surface charge, and PEGylation [172, 272]. Among these factors, composition is a major determinant of safety. Certain lipid components may trigger complement activation and innate immune responses during prolonged administration, thereby increasing the risk of delayed toxicity [259]. PEGylation has been widely used to extend circulation time through surface engineering of lipid-based nanocarriers. However, PEGylated systems may also introduce additional safety concerns, particularly accelerated blood clearance after repeated dosing and hypersensitivity reactions associated with complement activation [273]. Mechanistically, both pre-existing and treatment-induced anti-PEG antibodies can bind to the nanoparticle surface during repeated administration and initiate complement activation. This process may compromise nanoparticle integrity, resulting in premature drug release, altered biodistribution, accelerated systemic clearance, and an increased risk of hypersensitivity reactions [274]. In addition, surfactants are commonly incorporated into SLNs and NLCs to improve dispersion stability and reduce interfacial tension. However, their long-term use cannot be assumed to be biologically inert. Available evidence indicates that NLCs formulated with mixed surfactants, such as polysorbate 80 and poloxamer 188, may exhibit certain cytotoxic effects [172].

The long-term safety of lipid-based nanocarriers is determined not only by their composition but also by their physiological fate after administration. Once exposed to the physiological milieu, their surfaces rapidly adsorb plasma proteins and form a complex protein corona [275, 276]. This dynamic biointerface reshapes the biological identity of the carrier and promotes recognition and clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system. For example, fibrinogen adsorbed onto nanoparticles can enhance macrophage uptake through interactions with integrin receptors [277]. Under repeated dosing conditions, sustained uptake by the mononuclear phagocyte system and preferential distribution to reticuloendothelial system organs, particularly the liver and spleen, may substantially increase the risk of long-term organ accumulation [278].

For biomimetically modified lipid-based nanocarriers, these safety issues are often more complex. Biologically derived membrane components may further affect immune recognition, complement-related reactions, and in vivo clearance behavior [135, 197, 279]. Moreover, cell membrane-derived materials are intrinsically heterogeneous. In addition to diverse membrane proteins and lipids, they may retain residual biological impurities, which could further increase the likelihood of host immune activation and local inflammatory responses [280, 281].

In chronic neurodegenerative disorders such as AD, therapeutic interventions often require long-term or repeated administration. Therefore, these safety issues deserve particular attention during clinical translation. Long-term biosafety evaluation should not be limited to acute biocompatibility, but should also include repeated-dose toxicity, immunogenicity, complement-mediated reactions, organ accumulation, and delayed histopathological changes. For lipid-based nano-TCM systems, especially those incorporating biomimetic modification, systematic assessment of long-term safety and immunogenicity should be regarded as a core requirement in future development.

Conclusions and perspectives

Conclusions

As the most common neurodegenerative disorder, AD lacks disease-modifying therapies that halt or reverse disease progression. TCM holds promise in modulating multiple pathological processes associated with AD. However, the instability of many TCM-derived monomers, coupled with the restrictive nature of the BBB, markedly limit their achievable concentrations in the brain and thus their therapeutic efficacy.

In this context, lipid-based nanocarriers have emerged as highly promising delivery platforms for AD therapy. Exosomes, liposomes, SLNs, and NLCs each provide distinct yet complementary advantages, including favorable biocompatibility, flexible cargo encapsulation, membrane affinity, and amenability to surface engineering. Importantly, these systems can be rationally designed to address multiple delivery barriers simultaneously, not only by improving drug stability and circulation behavior, but also by enhancing BBB transport, brain accumulation, and lesion-specific distribution. Beyond conventional ligand-mediated targeting, biomimetic strategies based on cell membranes or extracellular vesicles further expand the functional repertoire of lipid-based nanocarriers by conferring immune evasion, prolonged circulation, inflammation homing, and disease-relevant biological interactions. Emerging dual-functionalized systems that integrate receptor-targeting ligands with biomimetic camouflage represent a particularly compelling direction, as they more closely match the complex neurovascular and inflammatory microenvironment of AD.

Collectively, current evidence supports the view that nano-enabled delivery can substantially improve the therapeutic performance of TCM-derived monomers in AD models. More broadly, the convergence of TCM pharmacology and innovative delivery engineering offers a credible framework for overcoming one of the central obstacles in AD treatment: the mismatch between pharmacological promise and effective brain exposure. Accordingly, lipid-based nano-TCM systems should be regarded not merely as formulation upgrades, but as enabling technologies for the development of next-generation, mechanism-informed therapeutics for AD.

Perspectives

Despite encouraging progress, several major barriers still limit the clinical translation of lipid-based nano-TCM systems. First, BBB targeting efficiency remains suboptimal and highly context-dependent. Because BBB dysfunction in AD is spatially heterogeneous and dynamically regulated during disease progression, delivery strategies that rely on a single receptor or transport route may not provide consistent efficacy across disease stages or patient populations. Future work should therefore move beyond static targeting concepts and instead emphasize pathology-adaptive design, including systems that respond to inflammatory, oxidative, enzymatic, or metabolic cues within the diseased neurovascular unit.

Second, a more rigorous structure-function understanding is needed for lipid-based nanocarriers. At present, improvements in brain delivery are often reported phenomenologically, whereas the relative contributions of carrier composition, membrane mechanics, ligand density, surface protein orientation, cargo localization, and intracellular trafficking remain insufficiently defined. Establishing these relationships will be essential for the rational optimization of delivery efficiency, endosomal escape, cell-type selectivity, and controlled release behavior. In particular, future studies should distinguish more clearly between enhanced BBB passage, improved brain retention, and true target-cell engagement within the brain parenchyma.

Third, the translation of biomimetic systems is still constrained by manufacturing complexity and quality control. Challenges include scalable production, batch-to-batch reproducibility, membrane source standardization, cargo heterogeneity, storage stability, and robust physicochemical characterization. For clinically relevant development, future studies should place greater emphasis on reproducible preparation workflows, standardized release criteria, and analytically validated characterization methods capable of capturing both nanocarrier properties and biomimetic functionality.

Finally, the therapeutic logic of nano-TCM platforms should be further aligned with the multifactorial biology of AD. Because disease progression is driven by interconnected pathogenic cascades rather than a single dominant lesion, future platforms may benefit from multifunctional or combination designs capable of co-delivering anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-amyloid, tau-modulating, or metabolic-regulating agents. In this regard, TCM-derived monomers are particularly attractive because many already possess multi-target pharmacological profiles; nanocarrier engineering may therefore serve as a means to amplify and spatially direct these inherent pleiotropic effects rather than simply increasing exposure.

In summary, the next stage of development in this field should move from proof-of-concept delivery toward mechanistically validated, scalable, and clinically translatable nanotherapeutic systems. Through the deep integration of TCM-derived actives with lipid-based nanocarriers, active targeting, and biomimetic engineering, it may be possible to develop safer and more precise interventions capable of addressing the biological complexity of AD. Such progress will require not only improvements in nanocarrier design, but also closer alignment among pharmacology, neurobiology, materials science, and translational medicine.
