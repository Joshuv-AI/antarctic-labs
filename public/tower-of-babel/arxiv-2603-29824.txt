Curvature-Guided LoRA: Steering in the pretrained NTK subspace
Frédéric Zheng, Alexandre Proutière

Abstract
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods such as LoRA enable efficient adaptation of large pretrained models but often fall short of full fine-tuning performance. Existing approaches focus on aligning parameter updates, which only indirectly control model predictions. In this work, we introduce the prediction alignment problem, aiming to match the predictor obtained via PEFT to that of full fine-tuning at the level of outputs. We show that this objective naturally leads to a curvature-aware, second-order formulation, where optimal low-rank updates correspond to a Newton-like, curvature-whitened gradient. Based on this insight, we propose Curvature-Guided LoRA (CG-LoRA), which selects and scales adaptation directions using local curvature information. Our method is computationally efficient and avoids explicit second-order matrix construction. Preliminary experiments on standard natural language understanding benchmarks demonstrate improved performance and faster convergence compared to existing LoRA variants.

Introduction

Fine-tuning large-scale pretrained models has become a cornerstone of modern machine learning, enabling adaptation to a wide range of downstream tasks. However, full fine-tuning remains computationally expensive, motivating the development of parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods such as Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). By restricting updates to low-rank subspaces, LoRA significantly reduces memory and compute requirements, while retaining much of the performance of full fine-tuning. Despite these advantages, a persistent gap remains between LoRA and full fine-tuning in both convergence speed and final performance. Recent work has sought to bridge this gap by improving the initialization of low-rank adapters, typically by aligning the initial gradient or parameter updates of LoRA with those of full fine-tuning. While effective, these approaches operate in parameter space and therefore only indirectly control the quantity of primary interest: the model’s predictions.

In this paper, we take a different perspective and formulate the prediction alignment problem, whose goal is to ensure that the predictor obtained after LoRA fine-tuning matches as closely as possible that obtained via full fine-tuning. Rather than aligning parameter updates, we directly target alignment in function space, i.e., at the level of model outputs. This shift is crucial, as distinct parameter updates can induce similar predictions, while small discrepancies in parameters may lead to large functional differences. Our main contribution is to show that this prediction alignment objective naturally leads to a curvature-aware, second-order formulation. Specifically, we demonstrate that achieving alignment with full fine-tuning requires incorporating the local geometry of the loss landscape, captured through a curvature approximation of the pretrained model. This results in a Newton-like initialization of low-rank adapters, where the optimal subspaces are obtained by whitening the gradient using curvature information. In contrast to prior work relying solely on first-order gradients, our method leverages second-order structure to identify the directions that most strongly impact predictions.

Building on this insight, we introduce Curvature-Guided LoRA (CG-LoRA), a novel PEFT method that constructs low-rank adapters aligned with the prediction dynamics of full fine-tuning. Our approach selects and scales low-rank directions based on a curvature-guided objective, leading to updates that accurately track the functional behavior of full fine-tuning while preserving parameter efficiency. We further show that this method can be implemented efficiently, without explicitly forming large curvature matrices, by leveraging structured approximations. Empirically, our preliminary results show that CG-LoRA achieves faster convergence and improved performance compared to existing LoRA variants across standard benchmarks, highlighting the importance of function-space alignment and second-order information in parameter-efficient adaptation.

Related work

Low-rank adaptation.

Following the introduction of LoRA
11
, a substantial body of work has studied how to improve its empirical performance and clarify its theoretical behavior.
On the optimization side, LoRA
+

10
argues that using different learning rates for the two low-rank factors is preferable to tying them.
In a related direction, rank-stabilized LoRA (rsLoRA)
14
studies the rank-dependent scaling used in LoRA and proposes a modified scaling.

A complementary line of work investigates how the chosen rank controls the expressive power of LoRA updates and when LoRA can match full fine-tuning.

26
provides theoretical results on the expressive power of LoRA in neural networks.
In the neural tangent kernel regime,
13
derives sufficient rank conditions under which the LoRA objective can recover solutions comparable to full fine-tuning.

The main motivation of our work is the recent focus on
initialization
of low-rank adapters.
The original LoRA initialization sets the initial update to zero (by initializing one factor at zero and the other randomly), which ensures the fine-tuning trajectory starts exactly at the pre-trained solution.
Beyond this baseline,
17
argues that allowing non-zero initializations can improve robustness.
Several works then propose to leverage information in the pre-trained weights and/or the downstream task to construct data- or geometry-informed initial adapters.
PiSSA
20
initializes adapters using the SVD of the pre-trained weight matrix, effectively seeding the update in directions aligned with dominant singular components of
W_{0}
.
Other approaches
25
;
29
instead construct SVD-based initializations from loss-gradient information evaluated at
W_{0}

In our work, we also build an SVD-based initialization from gradient information, but we first apply projection matrices induced by curvature of the pre-trained network, yielding an initialization that is explicitly curvature-aware.

The above works can be viewed as refinements of the original LoRA recipe (e.g., learning-rate choice, scaling, rank, initialization) while preserving the basic low-rank update parameterization.
For completeness, we also highlight methods that modify the allocation or the parameterization itself.
AdaLoRA
28
adaptively allocates rank (i.e., parameter budget) across layers during training.
Finally, spectral-space parameterizations propose to fine-tune within a singular-subspace representation of
W_{0}
rather than via an additive low-rank update; for instance,
27
learns orthogonal transformations of leading singular subspaces.

The neural tangent kernel (NTK).

The NTK has become a central tool to understand gradient descent dynamics in wide neural networks through linearization around initialization,
12
;
16
. By analyzing the NTK, a rich literature has been developped on deriving generalization bounds for overparametrized neural networks, see
2
;
4
;
23
and references herein.
While classical results focus on random initialization for the layer weight matrices,
18
provides justification and evidence that kernel-like behavior can persist when fine-tuning from a
pre-trained
initialization.
Studying LoRA through this lens has yielded insight into rank requirements and optimization landscape properties; in particular,
13
analyzes LoRA training in the NTK regime and derives sufficient conditions on rank for favorable loss landscape optimization.
In our work, we also leverage NTK-style predictions, but use them to motivate an initialization procedure that remains informative even when the network is far from linear in practice.

Kronecker-factored curvature (K-FAC).

Second-order geometry, and in particular the Fisher information matrix, provides a principled view of curvature underlying natural-gradient methods
3
.
Since exact Fisher (or natural-gradient) computations are typically intractable at scale, K-FAC
19
introduces a structured Kronecker-factor approximation that enables efficient approximate natural-gradient updates and has proven competitive empirically.
Subsequent work refines or analyzes these approximations, including EKFAC
9
and mean-field analyses of Fisher statistics
15
.
More recently, K-FAC-style methods have been revisited for modern architectures (including weight-sharing settings arising in transformers and GNNs)
8
.
In our work, we adopt the K-FAC assumption as a tractable curvature model and use it to construct curvature-induced projection matrices that guide curvature-aware initialization of low-rank adapters.

Model and Objective

We consider a pretrained model
f
that we wish to fine-tune using the dataset
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Specifically, we focus on fine-tuning the weight matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}
of a given layer, and we denote by
f(W;x)\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
the model output as a function of the input
x
and this matrix. We begin by introducing notation that will be used throughout the remainder of the paper. The output of the model can be written component-wise as

f(W;\,x):=\bigl(f^{(1)}(W;\,x),\;\ldots,\;f^{(C)}(W;\,x)\bigr)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}.
We define the Jacobian of
f
at
(W,\,x)
as

J_{f,W,x}:=(\operatorname{vec}\nabla_{W}f^{(1)}(W;\,x),\;\ldots,\operatorname{vec}\nabla_{W}f^{(C)}(W;\,x))^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times d_{\mathrm{out}}d_{\mathrm{in}}},

as well as the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) linearization of
f
at
W
in direction
\Delta W\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}
as
f(W+\Delta W;\,x)\approx f(W;\,x)+J_{f,W,x}\operatorname{vec}(\Delta W)
. This approximation has been shown to hold throughout the fine-tuning procedure for the squared loss in infinite-width networks with random initialization
12
;
16
, uniformly over time (see Theorem 2.1 in
16
). For pretrained LLMs used as initialization, this approximation has also been supported empirically
18
;
1
. In the following, we refer to the
NTK regime
as the setting in which this linear approximation holds.

To simplify the notation, we introduce
X:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{\top}
and

Y:=(y_{1}^{\top},\ldots,y_{n}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{nC}
, and define

f(W;\,X):=\bigl(f(W;\,x_{1})^{\top},\;\ldots,\;f(W;\,x_{n})^{\top}\bigr)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{nC}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad J_{f,W,X}:=(J_{f,W,x_{1}}^{\top}\ \cdots J_{f,W,x_{n}}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{(nC)\times(d_{\mathrm{in}}d_{\mathrm{out}})}.

For a given loss function
\ell
and training points
X=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{\top}
and

Y=(y_{1}^{\top},\ldots,y_{n}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{nC}
, we define the empirical loss of predictors
f(W;X)\in\mathbb{R}^{nC}
as following:

\mathcal{L}_{n}(f(W;X)):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f(W;x_{i}),y_{i}).

3.1
Parameter-Efficient Finetuning

Assume that the model starts with pretrained matrix
W_{0}
. Fine tuning consists in optimizing
W=W_{0}+\Delta W
around
W_{0}
to minimize the empirical loss. When no constrains are imposed on
\Delta W
, we talked about Full Fine-Tuning (FFT):

\textrm{min}_{\Delta W\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}}\;\mathcal{L}_{n}(f(W_{0}+\Delta W;\,X)).

(1)

In the NTK regime, this optimization problem becomes:

\displaystyle\textrm{min}_{\Delta W\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}}\;\mathcal{L}_{n}\Big(f(W_{0};X)+J_{f,W_{0},X}\operatorname{vec}(\Delta W)\Big).

(Full Fine-Tuning)

We denote by
\Delta W^{\star}
its solution, i.e., the parameter update obtained after convergence of a full fine-tuning procedure. It follows that at convergence, the fine-tuned model can be written as
f(W_{0};x)++J_{f,W_{0},x}\operatorname{vec}(\Delta W^{\star})
(see Theorem 3.2 in
5
for a precise formulation).

LoRA constrains the weight perturbation
\Delta W
to take the form
\eta BA
, where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r}
. Here,
r
denotes the rank of the perturbation and is assumed to be much smaller than the input and output dimensions, i.e.,
r\ll\min(d_{\mathrm{in}},d_{\mathrm{out}})
. The parameter
\eta>0
is a fixed scaling factor that controls the magnitude of the adaptation
11
.

Reparameterization around a fixed LoRA initialization
(A_{0},B_{0})
.

In the default initialization of LoRA
11
,
A_{0}
is sampled using Kaiming uniform initialization while
B_{0}=0
. This implies that
B_{0}A_{0}=0
, ensuring that the initial weight matrix remains equal to the pretrained matrix
W_{0}
. In this work, we investigate improved initialization strategies and allow for choices such that
B_{0}A_{0}\neq 0
. To this end, we write
A=A_{0}+\Delta A
and
B=B_{0}+\Delta B
. With this notation, the product
BA
can be approximated as
BA\approx B_{0}A_{0}+B_{0}\Delta A+\Delta BA_{0}
, where we neglect the second-order term
\Delta B\Delta A
. Fine-tuning then begins from
\Delta A=0
and
\Delta B=0
. Some recent works explicitly control the initialization offset. For instance, in LoRA-GA
25
, the authors ensure that fine-tuning starts exactly from the pretrained matrix
W_{0}
by shifting the latter to
W_{0}-\eta B_{0}A_{0}
. In contrast, other approaches such as LoRA-One
29
propose to exploit the flexibility offered by the initialization and deliberately modify the starting point of the weights through an appropriate choice of
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
, so that
W_{0}+\eta B_{0}A_{0}
provides a better starting point than
W_{0}
itself.

We adopt the same framework as that used in LoRA-GA, and search for a fine-tuned weight matrix of the form
W+\Delta W=W_{0}+\eta(B_{0}\Delta A+\Delta BA_{0})
.
It allows for a simpler, more intuitive analysis. In Section
5
, we show how we can additionally incorporate the shift influence in our algorithmic design. Our experimental results in Section
6
validate our methodology for both the LoRA-GA and LoRA-One configurations.

The resulting LoRA fine-tuning procedure can therefore be written as follows:

\textrm{min}_{\Delta A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\mathrm{in}}},\Delta B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r}}\;\mathcal{L}_{n}(f(W_{0}+\eta(B_{0}\Delta A+\Delta BA_{0});X))

(2)

In the NTK regime, this optimization problem becomes:

\displaystyle\textrm{min}_{\Delta A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\mathrm{in}}},\Delta B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r}}\;\mathcal{L}_{n}\Big(f(W_{0};X)+\eta J_{f,W_{0},X}\operatorname{vec}(\Delta BA_{0}+B_{0}\Delta A)\Big).

(LoRA)

We denote by
\Delta A^{\star},\Delta B^{\star}
its solution, i.e., the parameter update obtained after convergence of a LoRA fine-tuning procedure.

3.2
Objective: the Prediction Alignment Problem

We aim to design a LoRA fine-tuning procedure that yields a model as close as possible to that obtained via full fine-tuning. Prior work
25
focuses on selecting
A_{0},B_{0}
so as to align the first-step parameter updates between LoRA and full fine-tuning. For gradient descent, this is equivalent to matching the initial loss gradients. Under additional assumptions on the data distribution—namely that
y
is generated by a linear or single-layer ReLU function of isotropic inputs
x
—
29
extends this analysis beyond the first step and characterizes the alignment of iterative gradient updates within a subspace, using a similar SVD-based initialization of the loss gradient. However, for highly nonlinear architectures such as large language models, even small parameter perturbations can lead to significant discrepancies between the full fine-tuning and LoRA losses, making gradient alignment an unreliable global surrogate.

To address this limitation, we instead seek to choose
A_{0},B_{0}
such that, after fine-tuning, the resulting predictor closely matches that obtained via full fine-tuning. Specifically, we consider the optimization problem:

\min_{A_{0},\,B_{0}}\left\lVert f\!\left(W_{0}+{\Delta W}^{\star};\,X\right)-f\!\left(W_{0}+\eta({\Delta B}^{\star}A_{0}+B_{0}{\Delta A}^{\star});\,X\right)\right\rVert_{2}.

(3)

Remark 3.1
.

To motivate the objective function (
3
), observe that when the loss function
\ell
is Lipschitz in the prediction argument (such a condition is satisfied both the squared loss and the cross-entropy loss), an upper bound on
\left\lVert f\!\left(W_{0}+{\Delta W}^{\star};\,X\right)-f\!\left(W_{0}+\eta({\Delta B}^{\star}A_{0}+B_{0}{\Delta A}^{\star});\,X\right)\right\rVert_{2}
directly (linearly) translate to an upper bound for the losses

\left|\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(f(W_{0}+{\Delta W}^{\star};X)\right)-\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(f(W_{0}+\eta({\Delta B}^{\star}A_{0}+B_{0}{\Delta A}^{\star}));X\right)\right|
(this can be seen by simply applying Cauchy-Schwartz and the triangle inequalities). Hence, any
(A_{0},B_{0})
minimizing the prediction discrepancy
in (
3
) simultaneously minimizes an upper bound on the empirical
loss gap between full fine-tuning and LoRA, without any structural assumption on
the data distribution or the network architecture.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that in the NTK regime:

f\!\left(W_{0}+{\Delta W}^{\star};\,X\right)-f\!\left(W_{0}+\eta({\Delta B}^{\star}A_{0}+B_{0}{\Delta A}^{\star});\,X\right)\;=\;J_{f,W_{0},X}\!\operatorname{vec}\left({\Delta W}^{\star}-\eta({\Delta B}^{\star}A_{0}+B_{0}{\Delta A}^{\star})\right).

Minimizing the prediction norm therefore penalizes only the component of the parameter mismatch that lies in the row space of
J_{f,W_{0},X}
, that is, precisely the directions that affect the model outputs. This yields a strictly tighter criterion than the Frobenius-norm alignment used in prior work, which treats all directions in parameter space equally. Our objective thus subsumes gradient alignment as a special case, while remaining well motivated beyond the NTK regime via the Lipschitz bound above.

Solving the Prediction Alignment Problem

In this section, we derive key results for solving the alignment problem in (
3
). For clarity of exposition, we focus on the squared loss; the extension to cross-entropy loss for classification is discussed in Appendix
A
.

4.1
NTK loss minimizers

Proposition 4.1
.

Consider the squared loss
\ell(f(x),y)=\frac{1}{2}\|y-f(x)\|_{2}^{2}
. Let
Z=Y-f(W_{0};X)
denote the squared-loss finetuning residuals.

(i) Full Fine-tuning (FFT). The minimum-norm solution
{\Delta W}^{\star}=\arg\min_{\Delta W}\,\mathcal{L}_{n}\left(f(W_{0};X)+J_{f,W_{0},X}\operatorname{vec}(\Delta W\right))
is given by

\operatorname{vec}(\Delta W^{\star})=J_{f,W_{0},X}^{\dagger}Z.

(4)

(ii) LoRA. Define the LoRA Jacobian as

J_{f,W_{0},A_{0},B_{0},X}=J_{f,W_{0},X}(I_{d_{\mathrm{in}}}\otimes B_{0}\quad A_{0}^{\top}\otimes I_{d_{\mathrm{out}}}).

The minimum-norm solution
\bigl(\Delta A^{\star},\,\Delta B^{\star}\bigr)=\arg\min_{\Delta A,\,\Delta B}\,\mathcal{L}_{n}(f(W_{0};X)+\eta J_{f,W_{0},X}\operatorname{vec}(\Delta BA_{0}+B_{0}\Delta A))
is given by

(\operatorname{vec}(\Delta A^{\star})^{\top}\operatorname{vec}(\Delta B^{\star})^{\top})^{\top}=\frac{1}{\eta}J_{f,W_{0},A_{0},B_{0},X}^{\dagger}Z.

(5)

Corollary 4.2
.

The objective function in (
3
) is equal to
\|(\Pi_{J_{f,W_{0},X}}-\Pi_{J_{f,W_{0},A_{0},B_{0},X}})Z\|_{2}
for any
A_{0},B_{0}
. Hence (
3
) is equivalent to

\min_{A_{0},\,B_{0}}\|(\Pi_{J_{f,W_{0},X}}-\Pi_{J_{f,W_{0},A_{0},B_{0},X}})Z\|_{2}.

(6)

Interestingly, our formulation does not depend on the choice of
\eta
. Therefore, our results are compatible with commonly chosen values
\eta=\frac{1}{r}
or its rank stabilized version
14

\eta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}
.

4.2
The KFAC basis

The LoRA Jacobian shows that the initialization matrices
(A_{0},B_{0})
affect predictions through the right singular subspace of
J_{f,W_{0},X}
. This subspace coincides with the eigen-subspace of
J_{f,W_{0},X}^{\top}J_{f,W_{0},X}
which is the Hessian of the linearized squared loss w.r.t.
W
. This quantity is well-known for linear layers and we briefly recall its computation.

Denote by
h_{i}
and
u_{i}
the input/output of layer
W
w.r.t. example
x_{i}
. Assume first no weight sharing, i.e.,
h_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}}
and
u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}
, then by chain rule, one has
\nabla_{W}f^{(c)}(W_{0};x_{i})=\nabla_{u_{i}}f^{(c)}(W_{0};x_{i})h_{i}^{\top}=\delta_{i}^{(c)}h_{i}^{\top}
,
where
\delta_{i}^{(c)}:=\nabla_{u_{i}}f^{(c)}(W_{0};x_{i})
is a pre-activation derivative. Let
\delta_{i}=(\delta_{i}^{(1)}\ \cdots\delta_{i}^{(C)})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times C}
. Then one can write:

J_{f,W_{0},X}^{\top}J_{f,W_{0},X}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(h_{i}h_{i}^{\top})\otimes(\delta_{i}\delta_{i}^{\top}).

Computing this quantity can be challenging for neural networks. A recent line of work proposed simplifications to speed up computations, the most well-known being the
K-FAC
approximation
19
;
9
;
7
which essentially replaces the sum of Kronecker products by a Kronecker product of two sums. We adopt this approximation, formalized in the following assumption.

Assumption 4.3
.

The loss Hessian satisfies the following
K-FAC
approximation

J_{f,W_{0},X}^{\top}J_{f,W_{0},X}=S\otimes T

(7)

where the definition of
S
and
T
depends on whether we apply weight sharing.

(i) Without weight sharing:
S=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}h_{i}^{\top}
and
T=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\delta_{i}^{\top}
.

(ii) With weight sharing: we consider the
K-FAC-reduce
approximation
8
, which first does pooling over the tokens, keeping the computational overhead low w.r.t. maximum sequence length
\Omega
. Let
(h_{i,\omega})_{\omega=1}^{\Omega}
be the input tokens and
(\delta_{i,\omega})_{\omega=1}^{\Omega}
the pre-activation derivative tokens, associated to example
x_{i}
at layer
W
. Then

S=\frac{1}{n\Omega^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\sum_{\omega=1}^{\Omega}h_{i,\omega})(\sum_{\omega=1}^{\Omega}h_{i,\omega})^{\top},\quad T=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\sum_{\omega=1}^{\Omega}\delta_{i,\omega})(\sum_{\omega=1}^{\Omega}\delta_{i,\omega})^{\top}.

4.3
The whitened gradient and optimal curvature-guided initialization

We introduce the whitened gradient constructed based on the thin SVD of
S
and
T
, and show that it plays a crucial role in the solution of the alignment problem.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
r_{S}=\mathrm{rank}(S),r_{T}=\mathrm{rank}(T)
, and let
S=U_{S}D_{S}U_{S}^{\top}
and
T=U_{T}D_{T}U_{T}^{\top}
where
U_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times r_{S}},D_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{S}\times r_{S}}
and
U_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r_{T}},D_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{T}\times r_{T}}
. Define the
whitened
gradient

F:=D_{T}^{-1/2}U_{T}^{\top}\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L}_{n}(f(W;X))|_{W_{0}}U_{S}D_{S}^{-1/2}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{t}\times r_{s}}.

(8)

For
r\leq\min(r_{s},r_{t})
, let
F_{r}=U_{F,r}D_{F,r}V_{F,r}^{\top},
denote the best rank-
r
approximation of
F
. The corresponding full column rank left and right modes are
L_{r}=U_{T}D_{T}^{-1/2}U_{F,r}
and
R_{r}=U_{S}D_{S}^{-1/2}V_{F,r}
, respectively.

We adopt the word "whitening" to convey the normalization aspect since
F
is unit-less from a "natural physics" perspective, and less prone to noise as we will verify experimentally in Section
6
.

Input
h\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times n}
, pre-activation derivative

Remark 4.6
.

(a) The use of the whitened gradient

can be interpreted as a Newton-like method. Our results hence suggest that minimizing the differences between the predictions obtained by full fine-tuning and by LoRA requires at least a second-order quantity while standard gradient alignment would not be sufficient.

(b) We can derive the following bounds on the spectrum of

\forall i=1,\ldots,\min(r_{S},r_{T}),\qquad\frac{s_{i}(\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L}_{n}(f(W;X))|_{W_{0}})}{\sqrt{s_{1}(J_{f,W_{0},X}^{\top}J_{f,W_{0},X})}}\ \leq\ s_{i}(F)\ \leq\ \frac{s_{i}(\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L}_{n}(f(W;X))|_{W_{0}})}{\sqrt{s_{\min}^{+}(J^{\top}_{f,W_{0},X}J_{f,W_{0},X})}}.

(11)

Therefore, the expressivity of LoRA also depends on the minimum singular value of the NTK. In particular, Eq. (

to

To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to connect the NTK spectrum to LoRA expressivity to this level of precision. We believe that this connection opens up avenues for designing strong, theoretically grounded rank-selection rules, by leveraging existing results
21
;
22
;
6
on the NTK spectrum for deep neural networks. We leave this for future work.

5
The CG-LoRA algorithm

From the previous analysis, we have identified two subspaces in which
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
should lie in order to optimize the prediction alignment achieved by full fine-tuning or by LoRA. Nonetheless, several degrees of freedom remain. In particular: (1) the relative scaling of the columns of
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
, governed by the choice of
(Q_{A},Q_{B})
; and (2) the overall scaling of
\|A_{0}\|
and
\|B_{0}\|
under an appropriate norm. We discuss these algorithmic choices in detail in this section and then provide a complete description of the CG-LoRA algorithm (see pseudo-code in Algorithms
1
and
2
).

5.1
Relative column and absolute scalings

Recall that applying the shift
W_{0}+\eta B_{0}A_{0}
to the pretrained weights allows
B_{0}A_{0}
to be interpreted as a first-step gradient, as advocated in LoRA-One. In particular, a natural choice is to align
B_{0}A_{0}
with the direction of the negative loss gradient. This motivates the following optimization problem for
(Q_{A},Q_{B})
:

\min_{(Q_{A},Q_{B})\in GL_{r}}\|B_{0}A_{0}+\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L}_{n}(f(W;X))|_{W_{0}}\|_{F}

(12)

Since
B_{0}A_{0}=L_{r}QR_{r}
with
Q
invertible, this is a usual low-rank minimization problem which admits a closed form solution.

Proposition 5.1
.

The minimization problem (
12
) admits as solutions any invertible
Q=Q_{B}Q_{A}^{\top}
such that

L_{r}QR_{r}=M:=-\Pi_{L_{r}}\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L}_{n}(f(W;X))|_{W_{0}}\Pi_{R_{r}}.

(13)

In particular, since
L_{r}
and
R_{r}
are full column rank, there is a unique invertible minimizer

Q^{\star}=-L_{r}^{\dagger}\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L}_{n}(f(W;X))|_{W_{0}}(R_{r}^{\top})^{\dagger}=-(U_{F,r}^{\top}D_{T}^{-1}U_{F,r})^{-1}D_{F,r}(V_{F,r}^{\top}D_{S}^{-1}V_{F,r})^{-1}.

There remains an infinite number of pairs
(Q_{A},Q_{B})
such that
Q_{B}Q_{A}^{\top}=Q^{\star}
. To resolve this non-uniqueness, we consider balanced realizations.

Definition 5.2

(Balanced realizations)
.

Let
M=-\Pi_{L_{r}}\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L}_{n}(f(W;X))|_{W_{0}}\Pi_{R_{r}}
with thin SVD
M=U_{M}D_{M}V_{M}^{\top}
. Then
B_{0}=U_{M}D_{M}^{1/2},A_{0}^{\top}=V_{M}D_{M}^{1/2}

is called a
balanced
realization.

By construction,
\operatorname{col}(B_{0})=\operatorname{col}(M)=\operatorname{col}(L_{r})
since
Q^{\star}
is invertible. Similarly
\operatorname{col}(A_{0}^{\top})=\operatorname{col}(R_{r})
. Hence, these choices are compatible with the results of Proposition
4.5
.

So far, we have provided a balanced choice of
A_{0}\in\mathcal{G}_{A}
and
B_{0}\in\mathcal{G}_{B}
. However, prior work
25
;
17
has shown that an additional global scaling can improve forward and backward stability (see Theorem 3.2 in
25
). In conjunction with
\eta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}
, we adopt the following scaling, inspired by these works:

\|A_{0}\|_{2}=\|B_{0}\|_{2}=\frac{d_{\mathrm{out}}^{1/4}}{\gamma},

where
\gamma
is a hyperparameter.

5.3
Algorithmic complexity

CG-LoRA (Algorithm
2
) consists of the following components: (1) computing the curvature subspaces via Algorithm
1
, (2) computing the whitened gradient
F
, (3) computing
Q^{\star}
, and (4) computing a top-
r
SVD of
M
. Importantly, none of these steps requires computing or storing any
(d,d)
matrix for
d\in\{d_{\mathrm{in}},d_{\mathrm{out}}\}
, in contrast to LoRA-GA, which stores the full loss gradients.

For the first two components, we rely on matrix–vector products. Algorithm
1
, also considered in
24
, uses a standard randomized routine combining sketching, QR decomposition, and Rayleigh–Ritz projection, optionally with power iterations, to compute
U_{S},\Sigma_{S}
and
U_{T},\Sigma_{T}
. Crucially, these methods avoid forming the full matrices
S
and
T
.

Similarly for
F
, we do not compute the full loss gradient. Instead, for linear layers, it can be written as (with
\Omega=1
when there is no weight-sharing):

\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L}_{n}(f(W;X))|_{W_{0}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\omega=1}^{\Omega}h_{i,\omega}\mu_{i,\omega}^{\top},

where
\mu_{i,\omega}
is the pre-activation token derivative of the loss gradient (as opposed to previously defined
\delta_{i,\omega}
which is associated to the output signal). As a consequence, we have:

F=D_{T}^{-1/2}[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\omega=1}^{\Omega}(U_{T}^{\top}h_{i,\omega})(U_{S}^{\top}\mu_{i,\omega})^{\top}]D_{S}^{-1/2}.

For computing
Q^{\star}
, let us define
J_{L_{r}}=L_{r}^{\top}L_{r}=U_{F,r}^{\top}D_{T}^{-1}U_{F,r}
and
J_{R_{r}}=R_{r}^{\top}R_{r}=V_{F,r}^{\top}D_{S}^{-1}V_{F,r}.

Let
\operatorname{chol}(J_{L_{r}})
,
\operatorname{chol}(J_{R_{r}})
represent the lower triangular Cholesky factors such that

J_{L_{r}}=\operatorname{chol}(J_{L_{r}})\operatorname{chol}(J_{L_{r}})^{\top},\quad J_{R_{r}}=\operatorname{chol}(J_{R_{r}})\operatorname{chol}(J_{R_{r}})^{\top}.

Then both
J_{L_{r}}s=D_{F,r}
and
Q^{\star}J_{R_{r}}=s
can be efficiently solved using these Cholesky factors.
For computing
M
let us observe that

M=L_{r}Q^{\star}R_{r}=L_{r}\operatorname{chol}(J_{L_{r}})^{-T}[\operatorname{chol}(J_{L_{r}})^{\top}Q^{\star}\operatorname{chol}(J_{R_{r}})^{\top}]\operatorname{chol}(J_{R_{r}})^{-1}R

Let
K=\operatorname{chol}(J_{L_{r}})^{\top}Q^{\star}\operatorname{chol}(J_{R_{r}})
with SVD
K=U_{K}D_{K}V_{K}^{\top}
then

M=[L_{r}\operatorname{chol}(J_{L_{r}})^{-\top}U_{K}]D_{K}[R\operatorname{chol}(J_{R_{r}})^{-\top}V_{K}]^{\top}

is a valid SVD since
(L_{r}\operatorname{chol}(J_{L_{r}})^{-\top}U_{K})^{\top}L_{r}\operatorname{chol}(J_{L_{r}})^{-\top}U_{K}=U_{K}^{\top}\operatorname{chol}(J_{L_{r}})^{-1}L_{r}^{\top}L_{r}\operatorname{chol}(J_{L_{r}})^{-\top}U_{K}=U_{K}^{\top}U_{K}=I
, and similarly for
R
.

From the above analysis, we can conclude that the computational complexity of our initialization algorithm is negligible compared to the iterative fine-tuning procedure. In practice, only pre-activation derivatives are required, and we show in Appendix
B
how to compute them efficiently using a Hutchinson trace estimator. In terms of memory, our computations are performed layerwise. We employ a hooking mechanism to compute the required quantities for each layer and discard them before moving to the next one, thereby keeping the memory footprint minimal.

6
Experiments

We perform a preliminary evaluation of the performance of CG-LoRA on standard natural language understanding tasks. To ensure a fair comparison, all algorithms are run under identical conditions, controlling for sources of variability such as random seeds, learning rates, and input tokenization. As a result, the performance of baseline methods may differ from that reported in prior work, since we do not rely on their original implementations. Our experiments were conducted using an NVIDIA A40 GPU. All our results are fully reproducible and will be made available in a final version.

6.1
Implementation details

Baselines.
Our results are compared against full finetuning and several variants of LoRA, namely 1) LoRA-GA, 2) LoRA-One, 3) rsLoRA. We decide to focus on these particular variants which are the most related, current state-of-the-art. We used the available implementation of LoRA-GA, which can directly be attached to any LoRA model from Hugging Face. For LoRA-One, we re-implemented their exact algorithm by reusing loss gradients computed by LoRA-GA. We also reused their global scaling parameters provided in their Appendix G.4.

Models and common LoRA parameters.

We consider finetuning T5-base and RoBERTa-base models with various LoRA methods applied to the same linear layers. We fix the LoRA rank
r=8
and consider the rsLoRA scaling for all methods
\eta={2\sqrt{r}}
. To keep the computational overhead low, all non-zero based initialization methods CG-LoRA, LoRA-GA, LoRA-One are run with
B_{\mathrm{init}}=16
and we accumulate them 32 times yielding an effective dataset of size
N=512
.

Datasets and metrics.
We consider several GLUE datasets and evaluate accuracy on a separate test dataset. For a text to text architecture such as T5-base, the input sentences are further preprocessed to be task-specific ("classify the grammaticality of {sentence}" for CoLA). We report standard multiclass accuracy values using predicted probabilities. For T5-base, we compute the true multi-token class probability while LoRA-GA considers only the first token class probability.

CG-LoRA hyperparameters.
Our proposed algorithm requires three hyperparameters: 1) the oversampling factor
m
for the sketching matrix, 2) the number of power iterations
q
. 3) the scaling factor
\gamma
. To demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach, we consider low values
m=r,q=1
. We also choose
\gamma=16
, as considered in
25
.

Shifted initialization.
We investigate empirically the effect of starting from the same pre-trained model
W_{0}
, denoted by CG-LoRA (shift), versus starting from the shifted initialization
W_{0}+\eta B_{0}A_{0}
, denoted by CG-LoRA (no shift).

Training.
We use the standard Hugging Face optimizer with AdamW for all methods. We use a cosine learning rate scheduler and find the learning rate by grid search. Candidate values are chosen from
\{2e-5,5e-5,1e-4,2e-4,5e-4,8e-4\}
, and they are compared using a separate validation dataset.
We fix the training batch size to
B_{\mathrm{train}}=32
samples and perform a single full pass on the training dataset. The evaluation batch size is fixed at
B_{\mathrm{eval}}=64
. We run finetuning using three different seeds and report average values as well as standard deviations.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a principled prediction alignment problem for parameter-efficient fine-tuning that focuses on aligning model outputs rather than parameter updates. We showed that this objective naturally leads to a curvature-aware, second-order formulation, and proposed CG-LoRA, which leverages curvature-whitened gradients to construct low-rank updates aligned with full fine-tuning. Despite being derived in the NTK regime, our method demonstrates strong empirical performance in practical settings, highlighting the importance of second-order information and function-space alignment for closing the gap with full fine-tuning.

As future work, we plan to conduct more extensive experiments across a wider range of models and tasks, including large language models for language generation, to further illustrate and quantify the gains of our approach, as well as explore improved curvature approximations and extensions beyond the NTK regime.

Appendix B
Obtaining activation signals

For a linear layer with batched input
h\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}
and output
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{\mathrm{out}}}
, consider for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times C}

\displaystyle\phi=\nabla_{u}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{c=1}^{C}z_{i,c}f^{(c)}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{c=1}^{C}z_{i,c}\nabla_{u_{1}}f^{(c)}(x_{i})\\
\cdots\\
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{c=1}^{C}z_{i,c}\nabla_{u_{N}}f^{(c)}(x_{i})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\sum_{c=1}^{C}z_{1,c}\delta^{(c)}_{1}\\
\cdots\\
\sum_{c=1}^{C}z_{n,c}\delta^{(c)}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{\mathrm{out}}}

where the last equality assumes no cross interaction between examples.

1.

If
C=1
, we backpropagate the tensor
z=(1,...,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
since we then have

\phi=\delta

2.

For
C>1
, we propose a Hutchison estimator. For any random variable
z_{i}
with
\mathbb{E}(z_{i}z_{i}^{\top})=I
, we have

\mathbb{E}(\phi^{\top}\phi)=\mathbb{E}(\sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_{i}\phi_{i}^{\top})=\mathbb{E}(\sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_{i}z_{i}z_{i}^{\top}\delta_{i}^{\top})=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_{i}\delta_{i}^{\top}

To estimate that expectation, we consider random Rademacher random variables
(z^{(1)},..,z^{(P)})
with corresponding
\phi_{1},...,\phi_{p}
, and compute

Y_{T}=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}\phi_{p}^{\top}(\phi_{p}\Omega_{T}).

In practice, we do a one-shot estimation with
P=1
which already yields good results.

3.

To compute the centered version
\tilde{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}(I-\frac{1}{C}11^{\top})\delta_{i}^{\top}
, we center each random variable as following

\tilde{z}_{i}=Pz_{i}=(I-\frac{1}{C}11^{\top})z_{i}=z_{i}-(\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}z_{i,c})1

hence

\mathbb{E}(\phi^{\top}\phi)=\mathbb{E}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}Pz_{i}z_{i}^{\top}P\delta_{i}^{\top})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}P\delta_{i}^{\top}

4.

To compute
O=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\Lambda_{i}\delta_{i}
, we use a more involved argument.
Let
g_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{C})
and

z_{i}=(I-p_{i}1^{\top})\operatorname{Diag}(\sqrt{p}_{i})g_{i}

therefore
z_{i}
is itself Gaussian with

\operatorname{Cov}(z_{i})=(I-p_{i}1^{\top})\operatorname{Diag}(p_{i})(I-1p_{i}^{\top})

Note that
(I-p_{i}1^{\top})\operatorname{Diag}(p_{i})=\operatorname{Diag}(p_{i})-p_{i}p_{i}^{\top}
since
1^{\top}\operatorname{Diag}(p_{i})=p_{i}^{\top}
.

And

(\operatorname{Diag}(p_{i})-p_{i}p_{i}^{\top})(I-1p_{i}^{\top})=\operatorname{Diag}(p_{i})-p_{i}p_{i}^{\top}-p_{i}p_{i}^{\top}+p_{i}(p_{i}^{\top}1)p_{i}^{\top}=\operatorname{Diag}(p_{i})-p_{i}p_{i}^{\top}=\Lambda_{i}

Therefore, the Hutchinson estimator gives

\mathbb{E}(\phi^{\top}\phi)=\mathbb{E}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}z_{i}z_{i}^{\top}\delta_{i}^{\top})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\operatorname{Cov}(z_{i})\delta_{i}^{\top}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\Lambda_{i}\delta_{i}^{\top}

U_{S}^{\top}R_{A}=U_{T}^{\top}R_{B}=0
. Let

\begin{cases}A_{0}^{\top}=U_{S}D_{S}^{-1/2}V_{F,\mathcal{I}}Q_{A}+R_{A}\\
B_{0}=U_{T}D_{T}^{-1/2}U_{F,\mathcal{J}}Q_{B}+R_{B}\end{cases}

Then,

\|(I_{r_{t}}-\Pi_{D_{T}^{1/2}U_{T}^{\top}B_{0}})F(I_{r_{s}}-\Pi_{D_{S}^{1/2}U_{S}^{\top}A_{0}^{\top}})\|_{F}=\|(I_{r_{t}}-\Pi_{U_{F,\mathcal{J}}})F(I_{r_{s}}-\Pi_{V_{F,\mathcal{I}}})\|_{F}=(\sum_{i\in(\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{J})^{c}}s_{i}^{2}(F))^{1/2}

Proposition
4.5
is then obtained by taking
I=J=[1,...,r]
and
R_{A}=R_{B}=0
.
∎

C.4
Proof of Proposition
5.1

The optimization problem can be rewritten as a standard orthogonal projection since

\min_{(Q_{A},Q_{B})\in GL_{r}}\|B_{0}A_{0}+\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L}_{n}(f(W;X))|_{W_{0}}\|_{F}=\min_{M\in\mathcal{Q}}\|M-(-\nabla)\|_{F},

where
\mathcal{Q}:=\operatorname{range}\{L_{r}QR_{r},Q\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}\}
.

We will show that
M^{\star}=-\Pi_{L_{r}}\nabla\Pi_{R_{r}}
is the orthogonal projection of
-\nabla
onto
\mathcal{Q}
and therefore
Q^{\star}=-L_{r}^{\dagger}\nabla R_{r}^{\dagger}
is indeed a minimizer of our objective. Then, we will conclude by showing that
Q^{\star}
is unique and invertible.

For the first point, observe that

-\nabla-M^{\star}=-(I-\Pi_{L_{r}})\nabla-\Pi_{L_{r}}\nabla(I-\Pi_{R_{r}}),

therefore for any
Q
, we have

<-\nabla-M^{\star},L_{r}QR_{r}>=-<(I-\Pi_{L_{r}})\nabla,L_{r}QR_{r}>-<\Pi_{L_{r}}\nabla(I-\Pi_{R_{r}}),L_{r}QR_{r}>=0.

Hence
M^{\star}
is the orthogonal projection of
-\nabla
, which implies that
Q^{\star}
is a solution of the original problem.

Since
L_{r},R_{r}
are full column rank, we can also write

\displaystyle Q^{\star}

\displaystyle=(L_{r}^{\top}L_{r})^{-1}L_{r}^{\top}\nabla R_{r}^{\top}(R_{r}^{\top}R_{r})^{-1}

\displaystyle=(U_{F,r}^{\top}D_{T}^{-1}U_{F,r})^{-1}U_{F,r}D_{T}^{-1/2}U_{T}^{\top}\nabla U_{S}D_{S}^{-1/2}V_{F,r}^{\top}(V_{F,r}^{\top}D_{S}^{-1}V_{F,r})^{-1}

\displaystyle=(U_{F,r}^{\top}D_{T}^{-1}U_{F,r})^{-1}D_{F,r}(V_{F,r}^{\top}D_{S}^{-1}V_{F,r})^{-1}.

The last expression of
Q^{\star}
proves its invertibility.

Finally, uniqueness follows from the injectivity of
Q\rightarrow L_{r}QR_{r}
since
L_{r}QR_{r}^{\top}=0\implies L_{r}^{\dagger}L_{r}QR_{r}^{\top}(R_{r}^{\top})^{\dagger}=0\implies Q=0
.

4.5

D.1
Proof of Proposition
A.3

To show invertibility of
\Phi
, it is equivalent to show that
O
is positive definite on
\operatorname{Im}(T)
. Indeed, let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{t}}
then

x^{\top}\Phi x=(U_{T}D_{T}^{-1/2}x)^{\top}O(U_{T}D_{T}^{-1/2}x)

where
U_{T}D_{T}^{-1/2}x\in\operatorname{Im}(T)
.

Now, let
\Delta=(\delta_{1},...,\delta_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times nC}
then
T=\Delta\Delta^{\top}
and
O=\Delta\mathrm{Diag}(p_{1}(1-p_{1}),...,p_{n}(1-p_{n}))\Delta^{\top}
.
In particular since each
0<p_{i}<1
then
\operatorname{Im}(O)=\operatorname{Im}(T)
.

Therefore,
z\in\operatorname{Im}(T)=\operatorname{Im}(O)\perp\operatorname{Ker}(O^{\top})=\operatorname{Ker}(0)
hence
O
is positive definite on
\operatorname{Im}(T)
and
\Phi
is invertible.

D.2
Proof of Proposition
A.5

Proof.

As for the squared-loss case, the proof is decomposed into two steps:

1.

Rewrite the expressions of
\hat{f}(X)
and
\hat{f}_{A_{0},B_{0}}(X)
as orthogonal projections of the finetuning residuals.

2.

Compute the difference between the projections.

Step 1: Let us introduce whitened coordinates to make appear orthogonal projections which are simpler to manipulate.

Let
J=UDV^{\top}
be a thin SVD. Let
\Gamma=\mathcal{C}_{0}^{1/2}J
. Since
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is invertible, we can write

\hat{f}(X)=f(W_{0};X)+\mathcal{C}_{0}^{-1/2}\Gamma(\Gamma^{\top}\Gamma)^{\dagger}\Gamma^{\top}\mathcal{C}_{0}^{-1/2}(Y-p_{0})

\Pi_{\Gamma}=\Gamma(\Gamma^{\top}\Gamma)^{\dagger}\Gamma^{\top}=\Gamma\Gamma^{\dagger}
is simply the projection over
\operatorname{col}(\Gamma)=\operatorname{col}(\mathcal{C}_{0}^{1/2}U)
. Hence, we can also rewrite

\Pi_{\Gamma}=\Pi_{\mathcal{C}_{0}^{1/2}U}=\mathcal{C}_{0}^{1/2}U(U^{\top}\mathcal{C}_{0}U)^{\dagger}U^{\top}\mathcal{C}_{0}^{1/2}.

Let
G=U^{\top}\mathcal{C}_{0}U
then
x^{\top}Gx=(Ux)^{\top}\mathcal{C}_{0}(Ux)>0
since
\mathcal{C}_{0}\succ 0
. This implies that
G
is non singular and we can write

\hat{f}(X)=f(W_{0};X)+UG^{-1}U^{\top}(Y-p_{0})

For LoRA, we obtain instead

\hat{f}_{A_{0},B_{0}}(X)=f(W_{0};X)+\mathcal{C}_{0}^{-1/2}\Pi_{\Gamma_{r}}\mathcal{C}_{0}^{-1/2}(Y-p_{0})

where
\Gamma_{r}=\mathcal{C}_{0}^{1/2}JH
. In particular
\operatorname{col}(\Gamma_{r})=\operatorname{col}(\mathcal{C}_{0}^{1/2}UP)
where
P=DV^{\top}H
.
Using the same calculus as above, this implies

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{A_{0},B_{0}}(X)

\displaystyle=f(W_{0};X)+UP((UP)^{\top}\mathcal{C}_{0}UP)(UP)^{\top}(Y-p_{0})

\displaystyle=f(W_{0};X)+UP(P^{\top}GP)P^{\top}U^{\top}(Y-p_{0})

\displaystyle=f(W_{0};X)+UG^{-1/2}G^{1/2}P(P^{\top}GP)(G^{1/2}P)^{\top}G^{-1/2}U^{\top}(Y-p_{0})

\displaystyle=f(W_{0};X)+UG^{-1/2}\Pi_{Q}G^{-1/2}U^{\top}(Y-p_{0})

where
Q:=G^{1/2}P
.
Taking the difference, we obtain

\|\hat{f}(X)-\hat{f}_{A_{0},B_{0}}(X)\|=\|UG^{-1/2}(I-\Pi_{Q})G^{-1/2}U^{\top}(Y-p_{0})\|_{2}

Finally, recall that

U^{\top}(Y-p_{0})=(D_{S}^{-1/2}U_{S}^{\top}\otimes D_{T}^{-1/2}U_{T}^{\top})J^{\top}(Y-p_{0})=-(D_{S}^{-1/2}U_{S}^{\top}\otimes D_{T}^{-1/2}U_{T}^{\top})\operatorname{vec}(\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L}_{n}(f(W;X))|_{W_{0}}),

which leads to our intermediate value

\|\hat{f}(X)-\hat{f}_{A_{0},B_{0}}(X)\|=\|G^{-1/2}(I-\Pi_{Q})G^{-1/2}(D_{S}^{-1/2}U_{S}^{\top}\otimes D_{T}^{-1/2}U_{T}^{\top})\operatorname{vec}(\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L}_{n}(f(W;X))|_{W_{0}})\|_{2}

Step 2:
We need now to characterize the column space of
Q
. First observe that

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=U^{\top}\mathcal{C}_{0}U

\displaystyle=D^{-1}V^{\top}(J^{\top}\mathcal{C}_{0}J)VD^{-1}

\displaystyle=(D_{S}^{-1/2}U_{S}^{\top}\otimes D_{T}^{-1/2}U_{T}^{\top})(J^{\top}\mathcal{C}_{0}J)(U_{S}D_{S}^{-1/2}\otimes U_{T}D_{T}^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=(D_{S}^{-1/2}U_{S}^{\top}\otimes D_{T}^{-1/2}U_{T}^{\top})(S\otimes O)(U_{S}D_{S}^{-1/2}\otimes U_{T}D_{T}^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=I_{r_{\mathrm{r_{s}}}}\otimes(D_{T}^{-1/2}U_{T}^{\top}OU_{T}D_{T}^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=I_{r_{s}}\otimes\Phi.

hence by usual properties of the Kronecker product, we also have
G^{1/2}=I_{r_{s}}\otimes\Phi^{1/2}
. This gives

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=G^{1/2}(D_{S}^{1/2}U_{S}^{\top}\otimes D_{T}^{1/2}U_{T}^{\top})(I_{d_{\mathrm{in}}}\otimes B_{0}\quad A_{0}^{\top}\otimes I_{d_{\mathrm{out}}})

\displaystyle=(I_{r_{s}}\otimes\Phi^{1/2})(D_{S}^{1/2}U_{S}^{\top}\otimes D_{T}^{1/2}U_{T}^{\top}B_{0}\quad D_{S}^{1/2}U_{S}^{\top}A_{0}^{\top}\otimes D_{T}^{1/2}U_{T}^{\top})

\displaystyle=(D_{S}^{1/2}U_{S}^{\top}\otimes\Phi^{1/2}D_{T}^{1/2}U_{T}^{\top}B_{0}\quad D_{S}^{1/2}U_{S}^{\top}A_{0}^{\top}\otimes\Phi^{1/2}D_{T}^{1/2}U_{T}^{\top})

therefore its column space is the sum of the two columns spaces:

•
