Same-session percutaneous closure of iatrogenic sinus of Valsalva rupture during transseptal puncture for balloon mitral valvotomy: a case report
Authors: I Made Junior Rina Artha, Ni Made Ayu Wulan Sari, Made Satria Yudha Dewangga, David Yobel, Gusti Ngurah Prana Jagannatha, Edoardo Zancanaro, Alexandru Patrascu’ Duygu Kocyigit Burunkaya, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBalloon mitral valvotomy (BMV) is a primary treatment for rheumatic mitral stenosis (MS), although rare procedural complications may occur. Iatrogenic sinus of Valsalva (SoV) rupture related to transseptal puncture is exceptionally uncommon, with limited reports describing immediate transcatheter management.Case summaryA 50-year-old woman with severe rheumatic MS, marked left atrial enlargement, and atrial fibrillation underwent elective BMV following successful anticoagulation therapy and resolution of a left atrial appendage thrombus. During transseptal puncture, she developed a sudden reduction in aortic diastolic pressure. Immediate angiography and transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) confirmed a focal rupture at the non-coronary cusp region, directly attributable to needle/dilator trauma. The patient remained stable, allowing immediate percutaneous closure via femoral arterial access using a 6–4 mm multi-fenestrated septal occluder device. Successful closure was confirmed intra-procedurally by TEE, demonstrating no residual shunt or worsening of aortic regurgitation. Balloon mitral valvotomy was safely completed in the same session. Serial echocardiographic follow-up at 1, 6, and 12 months demonstrated stable device placement, preserved ventricular function, and moderate residual MS without further complications.DiscussionSame-session percutaneous closure is a feasible and effective strategy for managing transseptal puncture-related SoV rupture during BMV in haemodynamically stable patients. This case highlights the importance of meticulous transseptal technique, multimodality imaging guidance, and procedural adaptability in complex rheumatic interventions.Keywords: Balloon valvuloplasty, Case report, Rheumatic heart disease, Sinus of Valsalva, Transseptal puncture

Background

Rheumatic mitral stenosis (MS) remains a major cause of cardiovascular morbidity, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.1 Balloon mitral valvotomy (BMV) is the preferred interventional treatment for anatomically suitable valves, providing symptomatic and haemodynamic improvement with a favourable safety profile.2 However, procedural complications may occur, including cardiac tamponade, severe mitral regurgitation (MR), and injury to adjacent structures such as the sinus of Valsalva (SoV).3

Summary figure

Transseptal puncture, routinely used to access the left atrium in BMV, is generally safe but may rarely cause serious complications involving adjacent cardiac structures, including the aortic root.4 Sinus of Valsalva rupture is an exceedingly rare but catastrophic complication of catheter-based interventions.5 Although not classically associated with BMV, rheumatic heart disease (RHD) may significantly distort cardiac anatomy. Chronic left atrial (LA) enlargement and fibrotic remodelling may shift the interatrial septum (IAS) closer to the non-coronary cusp (NCC), increasing vulnerability during transseptal puncture. In addition, aortic root calcification in advanced RHD may further weaken tissue integrity and predispose to injury.6,7

Historically, surgical repair has been the standard treatment for ruptured SoV, but transcatheter closure has emerged as an effective alternative in selected stable patients with comparable outcomes and shorter hospitalization.8,9 However, reports of immediate closure during transseptal puncture remain scarce. We report a rare case of SoV rupture during transseptal puncture for BMV successfully managed with same-session percutaneous closure.

Case illustration

A 50-year-old woman with RHD and valvular atrial fibrillation (AF) presented with progressive exertional dyspnoea. Examination revealed an irregular pulse and mid-diastolic murmur. ECG showed AF with controlled ventricular response. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated severe MS (mitral valve area [MVA] 0.9 cm2, mean gradient 14 mmHg), marked LA enlargement, trivial MR, and preserved left ventricular (LV) function (EF 58%). Transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) confirmed severe rheumatic morphology with dense spontaneous echo contrast and an incidental LA appendage thrombus. Balloon mitral valvotomy was deferred, and warfarin was initiated (target INR 2–3).

After 3 months, repeat TEE showed thrombus resolution with persistent severe MS. Pre-procedural imaging (Figure 1A) demonstrated calcification involving the right coronary cusp (RCC) extending towards the NCC, mild aortic regurgitation (AR), and persistent LA enlargement. Elective BMV using the Inoue balloon technique was planned under fluoroscopic and TEE guidance, with transseptal puncture subsequently performed under the same multimodality imaging guidance. After femoral venous access and transseptal sheath positioning, puncture of the IAS was performed. Immediately after dilator advancement, a sudden drop in aortic diastolic pressure occurred, raising concern for acute AR or aorto-cardiac communication. Concurrent TEE revealed a new continuous flow signal suggestive of abnormal shunting.

Multimodality imaging demonstrating preprocedural anatomy and detection of sinus of Valsalva (SoV) rupture. (B) Aortography showing contrast leakage adjacent to the non-coronary cusp, consistent with an iatrogenic rupture of the SoV.

Urgent aortography (Figure 1B; Supplementary material online, Video S1) demonstrated contrast extravasation near the NCC, consistent with acute SoV rupture. Intraprocedural TEE (Figure 2A; Supplementary material online, Video S2) confirmed continuous flow from the NCC region. The transseptal sheath was seen adjacent to the rupture site (Figure 2B), suggesting mechanical injury from the needle or dilator. Despite this, the patient remained haemodynamically stable without tamponade or severe AR.

Transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) during the procedure. (A) Standard 2D TEE showing a perforation at the non-coronary cusp (NCC) of the sinus of Valsalva, demonstrated by continuous colour Doppler flow. (B) 3D TEE image illustrating the delivery sheath crossing the NCC at the site of perforation.

Given the focal defect and stability, immediate percutaneous closure was performed via femoral arterial access using a 6–4 mm Amplatzer™ multi-fenestrated septal occluder (MFO). Device position was confirmed under fluoroscopic and TEE guidance (Figure 3A–C; Supplementary material online, Video S3), with complete sealing of the defect and no residual shunt. Subsequently, BMV was cautiously completed, achieving adequate commissural splitting (Figure 4A–C; Supplementary material online, Video S4). Post-procedural TTE showed moderate residual MS (MVA 1.4 cm2; mean gradient 6 mmHg), preserved LV function, and no significant MR. The device remained stable without interference with the aortic valve. The patient was discharged after 48 h on warfarin and guideline-directed therapy.

(C) Short-axis transoesophageal echocardiography confirming stable device position with no residual shunt or significant aortic regurgitation.

Final balloon dilation and echocardiographic confirmation post-mitral valvotomy. (A) Fluoroscopy showing Inoue balloon inflation across the mitral valve after multi-fenestrated septal occluder device placement, completing the balloon mitral valvotomy procedure. (B) Transoesophageal echocardiography demonstrates successful commissural splitting of the mitral valve with the device in a stable position. (C) Biplane and 3D colour Doppler confirming preserved transmitral flow.

At 1 month, TTE showed a stable device position and preserved right ventricular (RV) function (TAPSE 2.0 cm). At 6 and 12 months (Figure 5A–D), imaging demonstrated persistent moderate MS, stable occluder position, mild AR, preserved LV function (EF 56.7%), and mildly reduced RV function (TAPSE 1.4 cm). Clinically, she remained in New York Heart Association class I–II without new symptoms.

Discussion

Sinus of Valsalva rupture is an uncommon but potentially catastrophic complication, most often associated with congenital aneurysm, trauma, or infective endocarditis. Iatrogenic rupture is rare and has been reported during catheter-based cardiac interventions.5,10 In this case, rupture occurred immediately after transseptal puncture, strongly suggesting direct mechanical injury from the needle or dilator rather than balloon-related trauma. The mechanism was likely multifactorial, driven by rheumatic anatomical distortion combined with procedural factors. Chronic RHD leads to severe LA enlargement and fibrotic remodelling, altering the spatial relationship between the IAS and aortic root. In our case, marked LA dilation likely displaced the fossa ovalis towards the NCC, reducing the safety margin and resulting in an overly anterior puncture trajectory with unintended NCC engagement.8

Pre-procedural imaging also demonstrated RCC calcification extending towards the NCC, suggesting increased tissue fragility.6,7 Although not directly causal, such calcification may reflect underlying structural vulnerability. Intra-procedural findings showed proximity of the sheath to the rupture site, supporting a needle/dilator injury mechanism. However, limitations of single-plane TEE may have reduced precision in tracking needle orientation.11 In addition, early advancement of the dilator before full confirmation of septal position may have contributed to propagation of injury.12

Compared with previously reported iatrogenic SoV ruptures during catheter-based interventions, this case is unique in its occurrence during transseptal puncture.5,10 The focal nature of the defect and preserved haemodynamic status enabled immediate percutaneous closure, avoiding surgery. Prior studies have shown comparable outcomes between surgical and transcatheter closure of ruptured SoV lesions, particularly in stable patients.8,9 Our case supports this approach, demonstrating durable device stability and preserved cardiac function at follow-up. The AmplatzerTM MFO was selected due to its dual-disc configuration, allowing effective sealing of irregular defects while minimizing interference with aortic valve function.13,14 This was crucial in avoiding worsening AR.

This case underscores the importance of precise transseptal puncture guidance. Multimodality imaging, particularly biplane TEE or intracardiac echocardiography, may improve spatial orientation relative to the aortic root. Confirmation of needle position in two orthogonal imaging planes and real-time verification of a posterior–inferior puncture site prior to septal crossing are essential, especially in patients with marked LA enlargement or aortic cusp calcification. Any deviation, resistance, or unexpected imaging findings should prompt immediate reassessment before advancing equipment.

According to ESC guidelines, BMV is indicated in symptomatic severe rheumatic MS without contraindications such as LA thrombus.15 In this case, adherence to guideline-directed anticoagulation and delayed intervention after thrombus resolution was essential prior to proceeding with BMV. Moreover, structured follow-up confirmed stable device position and preserved function over 12 months, supporting the feasibility of same-session closure as a bailout strategy. To ensure long-term safety, we therefore propose clinical evaluation and TTE at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months, followed by annual echocardiographic assessment thereafter, with additional cardiac CT or MRI reserved for cases with clinical or echocardiographic suspicion of pericardial effusion, device migration, device erosion, or progressive AR.

This report has limitations, including limited spatial resolution of TEE and a lack of intracardiac echocardiography. Pre-procedural 3D CT reconstruction was not performed, and no follow-up cardiac CT was obtained to assess subclinical complications. Finally, this is a single case report with follow-up based primarily on echocardiography without cross-sectional imaging.

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare complication of BMV, SoV rupture during transseptal puncture. Rapid recognition and immediate transcatheter closure enabled successful management without surgical conversion. Follow-up confirmed stable device positioning and preserved cardiac function, supporting same-session percutaneous closure as a feasible bailout strategy.

Patient perspective

The patient reported significant improvement in exercise tolerance following the procedure and expressed relief that the complication could be managed without open-heart surgery. During follow-up visits, she remained clinically stable and was able to return to daily activities with minimal limitation.
