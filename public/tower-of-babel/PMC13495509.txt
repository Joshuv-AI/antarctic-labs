What type of exercise prescription is best for improving cognitive flexibility in children and adolescents? A network meta-analysis
Authors: Zixuan Yang, Ke Zhao, Yuanye Hu, Quan Zhang, Qinhan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeCognitive flexibility is defined as the capacity to adjust thoughts and behaviors in accordance with goals and changing situational demands. Across all included studies, it was operationalized using neuropsychological tasks, such as the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, Dimensional Change Card Sort, and Flanker/More-Odd Shifting Task. This study aims to investigate the distinct effects of different exercise prescription parameters (i.e., duration, frequency, period, intensity, and type) on cognitive flexibility in children and adolescents, with the ultimate goal of providing evidence to inform the development of targeted interventions for enhancing cognitive flexibility in this population.MethodsThis systematic review and network meta-analysis was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024539167). Following the PICOS principle, we established inclusion and exclusion criteria and searched the EBSCO, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane databases through November 15, 2025. Eligible studies enrolled children and adolescents aged 4–18 years, compared any exercise intervention with usual physical activity, and reported cognitive flexibility outcomes. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, extracted data, and assessed the risk of bias. Network meta-analysis was performed using Stata 17.0, and SUCRA values were used to rank the effect sizes of different exercise prescription parameters.ResultsA total of 21 articles involving 3099 participants were included in the study. The network meta-analysis revealed that interventions lasting 31–50 min were significantly more effective than those lasting 10–30 min [SMD=-0.79, 95% CI (-1.55, -0.03)] and those lasting ≥ 71 min [SMD=-1.30, 95% CI (-2.35, -0.25)]. Single aerobic exercise [SMD = 0.67, 95% CI (0.09, 1.25)] and cognitive+exercise interventions [SMD = 0.64, 95% CI (0.09, 1.18)] were significantly more effective than the control group. Additionally, interventions lasting 5–8 weeks were significantly more effective than those lasting ≥ 20 weeks [SMD=-0.79, 95% CI (-1.55, -0.03)]. Among the various intervention characteristics examined, cumulative ranking probability analysis (SUCRA) suggested that sessions lasting 31–50 min (SUCRA = 98.3%), administered twice weekly (SUCRA = 98.4%), implemented over 5–8 weeks (SUCRA = 91.4%), at low intensity (SUCRA = 89.3%), and involving ball games (SUCRA = 98.4%) may hold the greatest probability of being the most effective approach for improving cognitive flexibility in children and adolescents.ConclusionSUCRA rankings suggest that exercise sessions of 31–50 min, delivered twice per week for 5–8 weeks at low intensity and incorporating ball games, are most likely to yield the greatest benefits for cognitive flexibility. However, this reflects ranking probability rather than effect size magnitude. Direct pairwise comparisons indicated only modest effect sizes, highlighting the need for additional randomized controlled trials to confirm both the relative superiority of these parameters and their clinical significance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04972-x.

Introduction

Cognitive flexibility, a core component of executive function, refers to an individual’s ability to adjust thoughts and behaviors in accordance with goals and the ever-changing demands of the situation [1]. Research on the development of cognitive flexibility suggests that infants as young as two years old demonstrate initial signs of cognitive flexibility, with the ability becoming more formalized between the ages of 3 and 4. By the age of 6, children enter a critical period of rapid development, showing more advanced task-switching capabilities [2]. The enhancement of cognitive flexibility progresses with age, reaching maturity during adolescence [3]. Research indicates that cognitive flexibility is intricately linked to various aspects of children and adolescents’ cognitive development, reading proficiency, learning efficacy, problem-solving aptitude, psychological well-being, social engagement, and innovative potential [4, 5]. The advancement of cognitive flexibility in children not only impacts their academic achievement [6], literacy skills [7], and creative abilities [8], but also influences their emotional regulation skills [9] and psychological well-being throughout childhood and adolescence [10]. Cognitive flexibility in children and adolescents can be operationally measured with neuropsychological tasks such as the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, Dimensional Change Card Sort, or Flanker/More-odd Shifting Task.

Research has demonstrated that physical exercise intervention is an effective strategy for improving cognitive flexibility in children and adolescents [11, 12]. Long-term engagement in physical activity, as opposed to sporadic participation, has been shown to not only positively influence neurophysiological functions but also result in structural changes in the brain [13], thereby more effectively fostering the development of cognitive flexibility in children and adolescents. Numerous studies have suggested that sustained involvement in sports activities can have a beneficial impact on children’s cognitive flexibility. For instance, Pan demonstrated that a 12-week table tennis program led to a notable enhancement in the cognitive flexibility of children and adolescents [14]. Similarly, Vander implemented a 22-week moderate-intensity exercise intervention in children, resulting in a significant improvement in cognitive flexibility pre- and post-intervention [15]. Additionally, Benzing reported a substantial increase in cognitive flexibility among children and adolescents following an 8-week exercise intervention [16]. Studies on exercise interventions and cognitive flexibility in children and adolescents show inconsistent effectiveness, with some reporting insignificant outcomes [17, 18]. This heterogeneity highlights the need for further research. Systematic reviews confirm open-skill sports and aerobic exercise are more effective [19, 20], However, these reviews focused exclusively on individual exercise modalities and did not comprehensively examine all exercise prescription elements (i.e., type, duration, session length, frequency, and intensity), nor did they synthesize the evidence through meta-analysis. Notably, previous studies lack unified standards for these core dimensions, causing heterogeneity. Practitioners cannot identify optimal parameters or explain ineffective interventions, leaving the optimal prescription unclear and hindering effective practice. Thus, fragmented and incomplete existing research demands a systematic study on full prescription elements. Integrating these core variables may resolve existing heterogeneity, clarify the optimal “exercise dose,” and provide actionable guidance for practitioners—objectives that motivate the present study.

Network meta-analysis enables the indirect comparison of multiple interventions by linking treatment effects through a common comparator even in the absence of direct comparative evidence; compared with traditional meta-analysis, it allows for quantitative ranking of intervention effects, thereby identifying optimal and suboptimal treatment options and providing a clear hierarchy of priorities for exercise practice and guideline formulation [21, 22]. However, which specific combination of exercise parameters (type, intensity, frequency, session duration, and intervention period) can maximally enhance cognitive flexibility in children and adolescents aged 4–18 years remains to be systematically elucidated. Therefore, addressing the specific research question “What exercise prescription is best?“, this study systematically searches for and synthesizes randomized controlled trials, employs network meta-analysis to quantify the relative effects of different exercise parameter configurations on cognitive flexibility, utilizes SUCRA curves to rank the efficacy of these combinations, and aims to provide clear, actionable, evidence-based recommendations for selecting and formulating optimal exercise intervention strategies.

Materials and methods

This research adhered rigorously to the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [23] and was officially registered on the PROSPERO platform with the registration number CRD42024539167.

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was carried out across five prominent databases, namely EBSCO, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane, covering the period from the inception of each database to November 15, 2025. The search strategy utilized a triad of term sets in order to maximize the scope of the investigation: ①"exercise” “physical exercise” “physical activity” “physical education” “sports” “game” “fitness” “physical training” “resistance training” “endurance training” “aerobic training”; ②"adolescent” “child” “kid” “pupil” “primary school student” “youth” “juvenile” “youngster” “school children” “school-age population” “young” “schoolers” “teenager”; ③"mental agility” “cognitive flexibility” “cognitive adaptability” “mental flexibility” “cognitive agility” “cognitive adaptiveness” “flexible thinking” “adaptive cognition” “cognitive fluidity” “cognitive task” “executive function” “shifting.” Boolean logic operators were used to connect the three sets of terms with “AND” as the connector.

Eligibility criteria for study selection

In accordance with the PICOS principle we established the inclusion and exclusion criteria for the literature [24]. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1)Participants were children and adolescents aged 4–18 years. This age span captures the critical developmental window of rapid cognitive-flexibility maturation, enabling a single analysis to compare exercise effects across childhood and adolescence; (2)The experimental group received a long-term exercise intervention (≥ 4 weeks). Numerous studies have demonstrated that 4 weeks constitutes the minimum effective duration [25–27]; (3)The control group received exercise intervention, daily life intervention, or no intervention, The experimental group received specific types of exercise interventions (e.g., single aerobic exercise, Cognitive+exercise, combined exercise, or ball games); (4) The outcome measure was the level of cognitive flexibility, assessed with validated neuropsychological tasks (e.g., the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, the Dimensional Change Card Sort, or the Flanker/More-odd shifting). These instruments have undergone rigorous reliability and validity testing and are appropriate for assessing children and adolescents aged 4–18 years; (5)The study design was randomized controlled trials (RCTs); (6)Officially published English papers. The exclusion criteria included: (1)Cross-sectional studies, case-control studies, and other descriptive studies; (2)Reviews, abstracts, letters, and comments lacking a clear description of the study design; (3)Incomplete data in the literature; (4)Unpublished papers; (5)Non-English papers.

Data extraction

Two team members, well-versed in evidence-based methodology and possessing significant expertise in researching the promotion of children and adolescents’ physical health, independently conducted literature screening and data extraction. In instances of discord, they would seek guidance from a third team member who also had substantial expertise in researching the promotion of children and adolescents’ physical health, in order to arrive at a consensus. The primary data extracted encompassed details such as the study’s primary author, publication year, country of origin, number of participants, intervention type, intervention duration, intervention frequency, intervention period, intervention intensity, and primary outcome measures. If multiple measurement methods for the same outcome indicator existed within a single article, team members would discuss and select the indicator that most effectively reflected cognitive flexibility in children and adolescents for data extraction. The outcome selection followed a simple priority rule: time-based measures (e.g., reaction time, completion time) were used whenever available; if absent, accuracy-based measures (e.g., correct response rate, error rate) were adopted instead.

Risk of bias assessment

The quality of each finally included RCT was evaluated using the “Risk of Bias” assessment tool RoB 2.0 recommended by the Cochrane Collaboration [28]. RoB 2.0 comprises five domains: Randomization process, Deviations from intended interventions, Missing outcome data, Measurement of the outcome, and Selection of the reported result. The assessment results are shown in Figs. 1 and 2. The overall quality of the included literature was relatively low. It should be noted that this study has certain methodological limitations and bias risks. Some of the included studies had moderate to high bias risks due to the difficulty in implementing blinding for exercise interventions in children and adolescents and inadequate reporting of allocation concealment. Furthermore, variability in control group interventions could represent another potential source of heterogeneity.

Statistical methods

The study utilized Stata 17.0 software for data analysis. Given the continuous nature of outcome measures and the variability in assessment tools and units among individual studies, the standardized mean difference (SMD) was selected as the effect size metric for calculating pooled effect sizes. Network meta-analysis, as a methodological approach, combines direct and indirect evidence to enable the concurrent evaluation of the effects of multiple interventions. Within the network plot, individual nodes symbolize specific interventions, with their respective sizes denoting the sample sizes of associated studies. The thickness of connecting lines between nodes signifies the quantity of studies encompassed by each intervention. To ascertain the hierarchy of interventions, the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) can be used to rank interventions; a higher SUCRA value indicates a more pronounced effect of the intervention [29].

Results

Literature search outcomes

A total of 4880 articles were initially retrieved from multiple databases and sources. Following deduplication, 2086 articles were eliminated, resulting in 2797 articles for subsequent evaluation. Ultimately, 21 articles met the inclusion criteria, encompassing a total of 3099 participants. The screening process is visually represented in Fig. 3.

Basic characteristics of the included literature

Within the 21 studies analyzed, the exercise interventions implemented in the experimental groups predominantly consisted of single aerobic exercise, Cognitive+exercise, combined exercises(a combination of two or more different types of exercise modalities), and ball sports. The duration of these interventions varied across periods of 5–8 weeks, 10–12 weeks, and exceeding 20 weeks. The frequency of interventions ranged from twice, thrice, five times, to more than six times per week. Additionally, the duration of each intervention session spanned from 10 to 30 min, 31–50 min, 51–70 min, to more than 71 min. The intervention intensity ranged from low to Moderate-high intensity levels, For the included studies, the exercise intensity classification (high, moderate, low) was directly adopted if clearly defined in the study; if not, intensity was determined based on the available heart rate data using the following criteria: low intensity ≤ 50% of maximum heart rate (MHR), moderate intensity = 65%–75% MHR, moderate-high intensity = 75%–85% MHR, and high intensity ≥ 85% of MHR. Inconsistency test, transitivity test, heterogeneity test

Under the network meta-analysis framework, both global and local inconsistency tests were conducted to evaluate the consistency between direct and indirect comparison results. The global inconsistency tests for exercise type, duration, frequency, period, and intensity yielded P-values of 0.091, 0.821, 0.871, 0.535, and 0.236, respectively, all exceeding the 0.05 threshold and indicating satisfactory global consistency. Further examination of local consistency within the closed loops for exercise type revealed a P-value of 0.100, with a 95% confidence interval of [0.00, 2.69]. Notably, the P-value was greater than 0.05, and the lower bound of the inconsistency factor encompassed 0, demonstrating good local consistency within the closed loops for exercise type. Consequently, a consistency model was employed for the analysis.

Heterogeneity testing revealed significant statistical heterogeneity among the included studies (I2 = 83.9%); therefore, a random-effects model was employed. To further explore the sources of heterogeneity, meta-regression was conducted with sample size, publication year, and age as covariates. The results indicated that publication year (P = 0.016) might be a potential source of heterogeneity.

In network meta-analysis, the assumption of transitivity requires a balanced distribution of effect modifiers across different direct comparisons. This study selected three key effect modifiers that might influence the outcome indicators—sample size, age, and publication year—to validate transitivity. Box plots (Fig. 4) were constructed to assess the distribution of these effect modifiers across direct comparison groups. The results demonstrated extensive overlap of the box plots for all effect modifiers across the direct comparison groups, indicating no significant violation of transitivity in this study.

Pairwise meta-analysis results

Based on the findings presented in Table 2, it is evident that single aerobic exercise [SMD = 0.67, 95% CI (0.09, 1.25)] and cognitive+exercise interventions [SMD = 0.64, 95% CI (0.09, 1.18)] both significantly outperformed the control group. Interventions lasting 31–50 min [SMD = 1.20, 95% CI (0.61, 1.80)] were significantly more effective than the control group and also outperformed both 10–30 min [SMD=–0.79, 95% CI (–1.55, − 0.03)] and ≥ 71 min [SMD=–1.30, 95% CI (–2.35, − 0.25)], with the differences being statistically significant. A twice-weekly frequency [SMD=–1.09, 95% CI (–1.60, − 0.58)] not only outperformed the control group but also surpassed both five times per week [SMD=–0.88, 95% CI (–1.66, − 0.10)] and six times per week [SMD =–1.16, 95% CI (–2.13, − 0.18)], with the differences being statistically significant. Low intensity [SMD = 1.30, 95% CI (0.21, 2.39)] and moderate intensity [SMD = 0.93, 95% CI (0.22, 1.63)] interventions both significantly outperformed the control group, with the differences being statistically significant. Intervention periods of 5–8 weeks [SMD = 0.96, 95% CI (0.40, 1.51)] and 10–12 weeks [SMD = 0.65, 95% CI (0.02, 1.27)] both significantly outperformed the control group, with the 5–8-week period also surpassing ≥ 20 weeks [SMD=–0.79, 95% CI (–1.55, − 0.03)]. All these differences were statistically significant, whereas no other pairwise comparisons reached significance.

The results indicate that removing any single study did not significantly affect the overall combined effect size. This suggests that the analysis results of this study are quite robust, and the combined effect size is minimally influenced by any individual study.

Publication bias test

The symmetrical nature of the funnel plots depicted in Fig. 9, with the majority of data points concentrated in the upper portion of the funnel and only a small number of points lying outside its boundaries, indicates a minimal presence of publication bias in the study. Egger’s test was also used to assess publication bias. The p-value was 0.064, which is greater than 0.05, indicating that there is no significant publication bias. While these findings suggest a low likelihood of bias, caution should still be exercised in interpreting the results.

Discussion

Numerous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have examined the influence of exercise on children and adolescents’ cognitive flexibility in recent literature [46–48]. However, our network meta-analysis is the first study to investigate the effect of exercise on children and adolescents’ cognitive flexibility while considering various components of exercise prescription. Additionally, our study ranks the impact of different exercise types, intensities, frequencies, durations, and periods on children and adolescents’ cognitive flexibility, providing valuable insights for determining optimal exercise intervention prescriptions in pediatric populations. This data are essential for the formulation of effective interventions.

The network meta-analysis identified ball sports as potentially the most effective exercise modality for enhancing cognitive flexibility in children and adolescents. The optimal intervention parameters appeared to be a duration of 31–50 min, a frequency of twice weekly, a period of 5–8 weeks, and low intensity.

The findings suggest that engaging in ball sports may be among the most effective ways to promote cognitive flexibility in children and adolescents. This finding can be explained by transfer of training theory, which posits that the cognitive processes required in physical activities, such as object tracking, movement coordination, and rule retention, are consistently practiced and utilized, leading to enhancements in cognitive functioning among children and adolescents [49]. This phenomenon is attributed to the significant role of the cerebellar system, which not only governs motor control but also influences various neurocognitive functions [50]. The structure of movements in ball sports encompasses a diverse array of cognitive operational components, including the retention of rules, adaptation of posture, and focused attention, all of which are intricately linked to the operations of the cerebellar system. Furthermore, the intricate and ever-changing nature of ball sports, coupled with the simultaneous engagement of visual, auditory, and proprioceptive senses, renders it particularly effective in enhancing the activation patterns of multiple brain systems [51], ultimately bolstering children and adolescents’ cognitive flexibility. Physical exercise—including combined exercise, cognition-integrated exercise, and aerobic training—up-regulates brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), recruits hippocampal and prefrontal neuroplasticity, and strengthens synaptic efficacy and gray-matter architecture, thereby enhancing cognitive flexibility in children and adolescents. Concurrently, exercise increases cerebral blood flow and oxygen delivery, optimizes neurotransmitter balance, and attenuates neuroinflammation, providing a neurophysiological foundation for improved cognitive flexibility [52]. However, it should be noted that this ranking advantage did not reach statistical significance. Therefore, the conclusion that ball sports are the most effective for enhancing cognitive flexibility in children and adolescents should be interpreted with caution.

Based on the findings of this study, an intervention duration of 31–50 min is most likely to produce the greatest gains in cognitive flexibility among children and adolescents. Previous research has shown that aerobic exercise sessions lasting 40–60 min are necessary to fully optimize the enhancement of cognitive flexibility in children and adolescents, as shorter intervention times may result in decreased exercise efficacy [53, 54]. This is consistent with the transient hypofrontality hypothesis [55], which posits that sustained physical activity leads to competition for the brain’s limited metabolic resources. As physical activity persists, there is a decrease in activity in the prefrontal cortices, potentially leading to reduced performance in tasks that rely heavily on frontal lobe function. This is due to the strong positive relationship between the prefrontal cortices and executive functions. It is important to note that interventions that are too brief may have a negative impact on children’s cognitive flexibility [56, 57]. Studies have demonstrated that the initial 10 min of exercise have minimal effects on cognitive function, but after 20 min, there is a noticeable improvement in cognitive performance [58]. These findings indicate that exercise must reach a minimum duration threshold to produce meaningful benefits for cognitive function. This study found that the effect of exercise intervention with a duration of 10–30 min on cognitive flexibility in children and adolescents showed no significant difference compared with the control group. The possible reason is that the large span of this duration range makes it difficult to accurately observe the minimum effective threshold of exercise intervention duration for eliciting positive effects on cognitive flexibility. Additionally, prolonged exercise leading to fatigue and dehydration may shift focus from cognitive tasks to bodily discomfort, thereby impairing children and adolescents’ cognitive functions [59]. These findings suggest that an intervention duration of 31–50 min is optimal for enhancing children and adolescents’ cognitive flexibility [60]. Nevertheless, further investigation is needed to explore the phenomenon of the “optimal effect inflection point“ [61].

Based on the findings of this study, a twice-weekly intervention frequency has the highest probability of being the optimal choice. Previous research has suggested that conducting interventions twice a week is highly effective in improving children and adolescents’ executive functions [62]. The “activity-stat” hypothesis offers a plausible rationale, suggesting that there exists a fixed threshold for children and adolescents’ energy expenditure, leading them to potentially decrease physical activity on non-intervention days to maintain balance [63, 64]. According to this theoretical framework, it can be deduced that children’s regular participation in daily physical activities may already approach the optimal threshold for cognitive flexibility. Therefore, increasing the frequency of additional physical activities beyond this threshold may have a detrimental effect on children and adolescents’ cognitive flexibility. Consequently, a lower-frequency intervention may be more effective for improving cognitive flexibility.

This study indicates that an intervention period of 5–8 weeks has the highest probability of being most effective for enhancing cognitive flexibility in children and adolescents. Prior research has shown that maintaining an exercise intervention for 6 to 8 weeks results in the greatest enhancement in cognitive flexibility among children and adolescents; however, extending the intervention beyond 8 weeks may diminish its effectiveness [65]. This could be attributed to prolonged intervention periods causing impaired task self-perception and decreased arousal levels, ultimately impacting children and adolescents’ cognitive flexibility.

Compared with moderate intensity, low intensity exercise has the highest probability of conferring the greatest benefit for cognitive flexibility in children and adolescents. This finding deviates from the inverted-U hypothesis—which posits an inverted-U relationship between exercise intensity and task performance. According to this hypothesis, engaging in exercise at either excessively low or high intensities can lead to arousal levels that are suboptimal for improving task performance. It is suggested that only moderate-intensity exercise aligns with the optimal level of arousal [66]. This phenomenon may be attributed to the neurological benefits of low to moderate-intensity exercise in enhancing cognitive function when compared to vigorous exercise [67]. A study indicated that engaging in exercise at 40% of moderate intensity is particularly effective in enhancing brain structure and function [68]. Additionally, low-intensity exercise has been found to have the most significant impact on cognitive improvement, as higher exercise intensity levels can lead to increased neural ‘noise’ that may have adverse effects on cognitive function [69].

The present findings suggest that the inverted-U hypothesis may not fully apply to the relationship between exercise intensity and cognitive flexibility in children and adolescents, suggesting that the hypothesis is limited in its subjects and scope of application. In addition, the effect of intervention duration on cognitive flexibility in this population may exhibit an “optimal-effect inflection point”; however, this point remains speculative and awaits verification through further high-quality studies.

The findings of this network meta-analysis provide valuable guidance for selecting exercise interventions to improve cognitive flexibility in children and adolescents. Our results indicate that a regimen of 31–50 min ball-sport sessions, performed twice per week for 5–8 weeks at low to moderate intensity, has the highest probability of being the optimal protocol. These evidence-based insights can inform exercise-guideline development by educators and health professionals. Future studies should also consider the unique developmental characteristics of children and adolescents when tailoring individualized intervention protocols.

Although this study is the first network meta-analysis to review the impact of exercise interventions on children and adolescents’ cognitive flexibility from the perspective of the components of an exercise prescription in randomized controlled trials, it has some limitations that should be considered. (1) There was a bias caused by the repeated use of control group sample sizes. (2) The number of literatures included in the network meta-analysis on exercise intensity was relatively small. (3) There were significant differences in the measurement methods of outcome indicators, which may lead to heterogeneity. In addition, some reported SMD values were relatively large with wide confidence intervals, indicating a potential lack of precision. (4) The conclusion that low-intensity exercise is “most beneficial” may not fully account for potential confounding factors. (5) Subgroup analysis was not performed by age or developmental stage. (6) Inherent publication bias in this research field.

Conclusion

This study synthesized data from 21 trials and performed a network meta-analysis to compare the impact of various exercise regimens on enhancing cognitive flexibility in children and adolescents. The findings suggest that an exercise prescription of 31–50 min per session, twice per week, for 5–8 weeks, at low intensity and centered on ball sports, is most likely to yield the optimal intervention effect. These results have substantial implications for guiding children and adolescents in selecting the most beneficial exercise regimen. However, SUCRA rankings provide only a probability-based exploration, and the findings are constrained by the limited number of included studies; thus, the results should be interpreted cautiously. Future work must address these limitations to enhance the comprehensiveness and reliability of the evidence.

Cognitive flexibility was assessed through various neuropsychological tasks, and different tasks measured distinct cognitive dimensions (such as set-shifting, dimensional switching, and inhibitory control), leading to considerable heterogeneity in outcome indicators. In addition, the 4–18 year old sample spanned multiple developmental stages, and differences in neural plasticity and cognitive maturity may have moderated the effects of exercise interventions. Future research should use standardized, age-appropriate measurement tools and report intervention characteristics in detail to reduce heterogeneity and improve comparability.
