 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The University of Utah, in partnership with Idaho National Laboratory (INL), evaluated the
viability of Sorption-Enhanced Gasification (SEG) as a transformational technology for
producing carbon-negative hydrogen. By integrating gasification, water-gas shift, and in-situ
carbon capture within a dual fluidized bed reactor, SEG can be a process intensification strategy
that for clean hydrogen production. The project addressed the primary challenges of biomass
utilization—feedstock heterogeneity and process complexity—through a series of targeted
technical tasks, summarized below.

Feedstock engineering: To ensure reliable operation in fluidized bed systems and to
accommodate makeup of limestone required for the SEG process, a novel fuel form was
developed by co-pelletizing biomass with limestone sorbent. Testing of diverse feedstocks,
including loblolly pine and corn stover, revealed that a specific limestone particle size (16x60
mesh) was critical for maintaining structural integrity during thermal cycling. This innovation
simplifies the feeding process while ensuring intimate contact between the fuel and the capture
agent.

Reaction optimization: Kinetic studies utilizing pressurized thermogravimetry identified that
while high-ash biomass chars are quite reactive, they are susceptible to reaction inhibition by
product gases hydrogen and carbon monoxide. This finding drove the development of optimized
steam-to-biomass operating ratios. Examination of CO2 sorbent capacities and rates of uptake
and release showed that low-cost limestone is a suitable material for SEG and benefits from
activation in a high-steam environment. Furthermore, fundamental studies addressed the issue of
tar formation by developing an iron-promoted dolomite catalyst. In lab-scale reforming tests, this
material achieved over 75% conversion of tar surrogates, significantly reducing the burden on
downstream purification units.

Process validation and economics: The technical feasibility of the concept was evaluated based
on a SEG system configured similarly to the process development unit (PDU) system operated at
the University of Utah. Reactor modeling incorporated kinetics of biomass gasification evaluated
at lab scale and was used to examine how reactor configuration and operating conditions affect
system performance. Techno-economic analysis indicates the levelized cost of hydrogen in this
bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS) pathway is most sensitive to feed moisture
and hydrogen yield, and that when leveraged with appropriate tax credits can offer a hydrogen
production solution competitive with other renewable pathways, with the benefit that it
simultaneously enables carbon-negative operation.

The project results establish the potential of SEG as a robust and economically promising
technology. Future efforts should focus on long-duration pilot testing to verify sorbent life over
extended operation, scale-up testing in larger reactors integrated with downstream gas handling,
and more detailed techno-economic analysis.

 INTRODUCTION

This project investigated sorption-enhanced gasification of biomass as a means of producing a
high-hydrogen product stream while optionally capturing biogenic CO2 for sequestration in a
high process-intensified scheme that reduces the number of steps required for conversion of
biomass to hydrogen.

Hydrogen is a versatile energy carrier. Not only does it have a very high mass energy density,
but it is also carbon-free, so its use does not result in CO2 emissions. Worldwide annual
production of hydrogen today is about 120 million tons per year, corresponding to 540 GW of
energy. About 63% of the hydrogen is in pure form and is used equally for refining and ammonia
production. The remaining 37% is mixed with other gases and is used for production of methanol
and reduction of iron ore, among other uses [1]. About three-quarters of hydrogen is produced by
reforming natural gas (205 billion cubic meters), and the remaining one-quarter is produced from
coal (107 million tons). Consumption of these fossil fuels for hydrogen production results in
global emissions of 830 million tons of CO2, equivalent to one-fifth of total U.S. CO2 emissions.
There is strong incentive to reduce the carbon footprint associated with hydrogen production.

Biomass is a renewable resource. Not only does it regrow when harvested, but it is a CO2-neutral
energy source, meaning that the amount of CO2 released when a tree is burned exactly matches
the CO2 required to grow an identical replacement tree. Moreover, if one is able to capture and
permanently store the CO2 resulting from biomass combustion or gasification, the result is
negative CO2 emissions: carbon dioxide in the atmosphere is absorbed by the plant, which is then
processed to yield energy, and the resulting CO2 is sequestered resulting in an overall removal of
CO2 from the atmosphere.

Gasification has long been recognized as a promising method to convert biomass to higher value
products such as biofuels or hydrogen [2,3]. Over the past few decades, hundreds of biomass
gasification technologies have been developed worldwide, nearly all employing some sort of
fixed- or fluidized-bed reactor. However, those types of gasifiers have several well-documented
technical challenges, including problems feeding non-uniform and inhomogeneous biomass
particles, poor carbon conversion resulting from relatively low bed temperatures, risk of
fluidized bed agglomeration and collapse if the temperature becomes too high, and production of
high concentrations of condensable polyaromatic “tars” that foul downstream systems and waste
carbon that could otherwise be part of the product gas [4,5]. Few commercial biomass gasifiers
are operating today, and those that are in service are generally small air-blown systems in which
the nitrogen-diluted product gas is combusted for heat or to produce biopower in a reciprocating
engine. In the U.S., there are several ambitious projects underway to produce biofuels via
gasification. The gasifiers in those systems are not commercially proven, and the configuration
of those plants is very complex, with many sub-systems to accommodate feedstock
inhomogeneity, low carbon conversion, tar removal, syngas conditioning, catalytic synthesis, and
refining of the final liquid product to the desired biofuel fraction.

 1

What is needed is a robust gasification-based solution that can reliably produce hydrogen-rich
syngas from a range of available biomass types. The technology should leverage process
intensification and modularization to simplify the overall biomass-to-hydrogen process and
minimize capital and operating costs. The ideal gasifier would be flexible, easy to operate, and
would produce clean syngas very high hydrogen content, thus minimizing or eliminating the
need for downstream cleanup and conditioning.

The technology investigated in this project is sorption-enhanced gasification (SEG) of biomass in
a dual fluidized bed reactor, used as a means of producing clean syngas that is very high in
hydrogen. The solution incorporates low-cost catalysts to promote hydrogen production and to
minimize tar production. Consideration is also given to a variant of SEG, oxy-sorption-enhanced
gasification, which offers the opportunity to capture of nearly all the biomass carbon as a high-
purity CO2 stream that can be sequestered or stored to achieve negative CO2 emissions.

Dual fluidized bed gasification. The basis of the technology is dual fluidized bed (DFB)
gasification, an established process consisting of two interconnected fluidized beds, one (gasifier)
fluidized with steam and the other (combustor) fluidized with air (Figure 1, top). The fluidized
bed particles (e.g. sand) circulate between the reactors and act the heat carrier for the gasifier.
Biomass is fed into the gasifier reactor, where it devolatilizes to form gas and solid char. The
carbon in the char is gasified by the steam to produce hydrogen (H2) and carbon monoxide (CO):

           C (s) + H2O (g) → H2 (g) + CO(g)                                            (Reaction 1)

In DFB gasification systems, the gasifier operates at 800-860°C (1470-1580°F) and
approximately 80-85% of the carbon in the biomass is converted to syngas. The remaining
carbon in the form of char flows with the bed material to the combustor, where is burns and heats
up the bed material. Typically, the combustor operates 100-150°C hotter than the gasifier.

An attractive feature of dual fluidized bed gasification is that it is self-regulating. If biomass
conversion in the gasifier worsens due to a decrease in temperature, the result is that more char is
carried to the combustor, which results in an increase in its temperature and hotter bed particles
flowing to the gasifier, thus increasing its temperature and conversion. Alternately, if gasifier
conversion increases, then less char is carried to the combustor, so the system temperature
decreases and biomass conversion in the gasifier is reduced. This self-regulating feature of DFB
gasifiers is well-documented and results in naturally stable operation [6,7].

DFB gasification of biomass is not new. As early as the 1970s, Battelle was developing its “High
Throughput Gasification System” and in the 1990s and early 2000s a commercial demonstration
of the technology (then owned by FERCO and renamed SilvaGas™) was in operation in
Burlington, Vermont [8,9]. The gasifier was generally technologically successful, but challenges
with other parts of the gas turbine-based IGCC system and overall economics led to it being shut
down. More recently, several commercial 8 to 15 MWth DFB gasifiers based on technology from
Vienna University of Technology and licensed by REPOTEC were constructed and operated for
many years in Güssing Austria, Oberwart Austria, Villach Austria, and Senden Germany [10].

Those gasifiers produced syngas that was combusted in a reciprocating engine to produce
electric power. In 2014 a larger 32 MWth REPOTEC gasifier was started up in Gothenburg,
Sweden, as part of the GoBiGas demonstration project, which made pipeline-quality substitute
natural gas (SNG) from biomass. All of those gasifiers were technologically successful but have
since been shut down due to economic or political reasons.

Sorption-enhanced gasification. Sorption-enhanced gasification (SEG, Figure 1, middle) is, in
its most simple form, just DFB gasification with limestone added to the bed. It is a very easy and
low-cost modification that significantly improves production and purity of hydrogen. The key is
the ability of calcined limestone (i.e. calcium oxide, CaO) to absorb CO2. That reaction, which is
reversible, is shown below.

 CaCO3 (s) + heat ↔ CaO (s) + CO2 (g) (Reaction 2)

 3

In the combustor, which is hotter than the gasifier, the reaction runs in the forward direction to
produce CaO and release CO2. The CaO is carried with the bed material into the gasifier, which
is cooler, and the reaction runs in the reverse direction with CaO scavenging CO2 in the gasifier.
That does two things: (1) it reduces the concentration of CO2 in the syngas, thus increasing the
concentration of hydrogen, and (2) it drives the important water-gas shift reaction (Reaction 3,
below) to the right by reducing the partial pressure of the product gas (La Chatelier’s principle).

           H2O (g) + CO (g) ↔ H2 (g) + CO2 (g)                                        (Reaction 3)

The overall result is conversion of CO to CO2 by the water-gas shift reaction and removal of CO2
by CaO, yielding a syngas that is very high in H2. The gasifier’s steam-rich environment also
enhances hydrogen production, and catalytic bed materials can drive the water-gas shift reaction
to near equilibrium even at the relatively low bed temperatures of SEG. The process therefore
achieves process intensification by separating carbon from hydrogen in situ, minimizing the need
for complex and expensive downstream separation equipment.

Oxy-Sorption-Enhanced Gasification. An exciting variant of SEG is oxy-sorption-enhanced
gasification, in which the combustor is not fluidized with air, but is fluidized with a mixture of
oxygen and CO2 recycled from the combustor flue gas to function as an oxy-combustor (Figure 1,
bottom). This has two advantages: (1) like conventional oxy-combustion for carbon capture, the
flue gas is nearly pure CO2, which with minimal processing can be sequestered/stored to achieve
negative CO2 emissions, and (2) the combustor gains a degree of controllability since the O2/CO2
ratio of the fluidizing gas can be independently controlled. Oxy-SEG does require a source of
oxygen, but integrated solutions such as using the O2 byproduct from water electrolysis-based H2
production offer low-cost solutions that further achieve process intensification.

Previous research. Calcium oxide-based SEG was originally proposed in the 1990s as a means
of removing CO2 from coal gasification in single fluidized bed reactors [11]. Initially, limestone
was considered a sacrificial once-through additive that would be removed with the coal ash. In
the early 2000s, the potential for applying SEG to biomass for non-fossil hydrogen production,
as well as the possibility of having a continuous system with built-in regeneration of the CaO in
the combustor of a dual fluidized bed system, was recognized. Since that time, several studies
including process modeling and lab- and bench-scale experiments have proven the viability of
the process [12]. Specifically, the experimental units ranging from 20 kW to 200 kW reported
70-80 vol.% of hydrogen concentration in the product gas [13]. The general trend is that smaller
units typically saw higher hydrogen concentrations. In fact, an 8 MW commercial dual fluidized
bed (DFB) gasifier for combined heat and power (CHP) in Güssing, Austria was retrofitted to
demonstrate the SEG process, while it generated only 50 vol.% of hydrogen, far below the >70
vol.% concentrations from pilot units [14]. Two possible explanations were proposed, including
a low steam-to-carbon ratio and excess temperatures for the calcination of limestone sorbents.
This discrepancy highlights the research needs to understand the scale-up effects for process
optimization.

The CaO sorbents used for SEG displayed a decent tar destruction capability even under the low
operating temperatures. The 8 MWth SEG running on wood chips reported 1 g/Nm3 tar content in

 4

the product gas, and a 30 kWth bubbling fluidized bed SEG converting municipal solid waste in
Spain generated 7 g/Nm3 of tar [12]. The natural minerals that have been tested, such as
limestone and calcite, delivered encouraging results regarding syngas composition and tar yield
but suffered from gradually deteriorating performance over the carbonation/decarbonation cycles.
Particularly, limestones tend to undergo attrition and fragmentation, which would lead to high
dust load in the raw product gas and additional cost of sorbent makeup. Furthermore, the gradual
deactivation of sorbents is mainly caused by the sintering of carbonate, the high diffusion
resistance of the CaCO3 product layer on the surface, and the loss of pore volume. Synthetic CO2
sorbents were mostly tested in small-scale experiments for proof-of-concept and fundamental
studies without a proven mass production strategy. It has been shown that the CO2 uptake and
release can be enhanced by using proper calcium precursors such as calcium D-gluconate
monohydrate [15], introducing structure stabilizers like MgO to make the sorbent more sintering
resistant [16], creating a porous structure through templated synthesis or the Kirkendall effect
[17], and steam addition [18]. Furthermore, the doping of Ni, Ce, Fe, Co species may improve
the performance of CaO-based sorbents for SEG. The thermodynamics of SEG are well
understood, but fundamentally, it is useful to measure and compare the kinetics of different
sorbents for an extended period of time to better design the reactors.

Bed material circulation rate has two contrary effects on the hydrogen concentration in the
product gas. Under autothermal conditions, a higher solid circulation rate would carry more heat
from the combustor to the gasifier, which increases the temperature in the gasifier and thus
hinders the CaO carbonation reaction, reducing the hydrogen concentration (negative effect). On
the other hand, a higher solid circulation rate also means a larger amount of CaO coming from
the combustor to the gasifier, which is supposed to enhance the CO2 capture and boost the
hydrogen concentration (positive effect). The results from the 100 kWth advanced pilot plant at
TU Wien indicate that the temperature-associated negative effect outweighs the positive effect;
the gasifier temperature increased from 625°C to 700°C as the circulation rate was raised from
5 h-1 to 20 h-1 [19]. When it comes to the S/C ratio (mol/mol), which was typically varied from 1
to 2 for the pilot plants, the impact is rather clear: an increased S/C ratio causes stronger water-
gas shift, char-steam, and tar reforming reactions, leading to a higher H2/CO ratio. But during
realistic autothermal operation, increasing the S/C ratio will consume energy and may cause a
substantial temperature drop in the gasifier if the inlet temperature of steam is not high enough.

Under this project, the University of Utah, in collaboration with Idaho National Lab (INL)
evaluated SEG using specially pre-processed biomass as a promising method of process
intensification that can overcome several shortfalls of conventional biomass gasification-based
hydrogen production. A single self-regulating reactor system can produce a clean, high-
concentration H2 stream that reduces downstream processing needs, and the in-situ CO2 removal
can be leveraged—especially with oxy-SEG—to produce a high-purity stream of CO2 that can be
permanently stored to achieve negative CO2 emissions. SEG technology has shown promise in
fundamental and lab-scale studies in Japan, China and Europe. This project extends that work
with actual biomass materials studied under industrially-relevant conditions with fundamental
analysis and modeling of conversion to advance the technology towards demonstration and
commercial deployment.

Subtask 2.2 Prepare and Pelletize Biomass
This subtask focused on densifying the selected feedstocks to ensure reliable feeding and
consistent residence times within the fluidized bed reactor. The work was divided into an initial
bench-scale screening phase and a subsequent large-scale production campaign for the selected
materials.

 7

One-half of each material obtained from Subtask 2.1 was initially processed. To optimize pellet
quality, the moisture content of each sample was adjusted to 20 wt% using deionized water prior
to densification, as previous experimental data indicated that moist biomass pellets increase the
durability of pellets compared to dry feedstock.

The conditioned materials were pelletized to a ¼-inch diameter using a bench-scale flat die pellet
mill. Following densification, the pellets were dried in an oven at 105°C for 24 hours. To meet
the specific particle size requirements for the screening tests, the dried pellets were subsequently
ground to less than 1 mm using a laboratory-scale knife mill (Wiley, Thomas 4).

A representative sample was collected from each pelletized material for ultimate and proximate
analyses at INL. Consistent with Subtask 2.1, all operational data and physical samples were
archived in the Biomass Feedstock Library (BFL) at INL. Table 5 details the operational
metadata for these samples, and Table 6 presents their chemical characteristics.

The diverse physical nature of raw biomass presents significant challenges for reliable feeding
into pressurized gasification systems. To mitigate these issues and support larger volume testing,
INL utilized a ring die-style pellet mill to densify down-selected feedstocks into uniform, high-
density pellets. This process improves flowability, increases energy density, and ensures
consistent residence time within the fluidized bed reactor.

The pelletization was executed at INL using a specialized ring die-style pellet mill. Processing
conditions were standardized for each feedstock, targeting standard US wood pellet industry
specifications to produce durable pellets with a diameter of 0.25 inches (approx. 6 mm). This
size was selected to optimize the balance between feeding reliability in the screw feeder and
fluidization characteristics within the PDU reactor.

The initial large-scale production focused on baseline woody biomass. In April 2023, INL
processed and delivered approximately 1,450 pounds of clean Loblolly pine pellets to the
University of Utah (Figure 2). This delivery satisfied a key project milestone (Milestone 2.2) and
enabled the commencement of initial PDU shakedown and parametric testing.

Task 3. Fundamental Studies of Sorption-Enhanced Gasification
Fundamental experimental investigations were conducted at the University of Utah to understand
the chemical mechanisms and reaction kinetics underlying the SEG performance. These studies
provided critical kinetic parameters for process modeling and highlighted the differences in
reactivity among the selected feedstocks.

Subtask 3.1 Characterize Gasification Rates of Prepared Fuels
The primary objective of Subtask 3.1 was to establish the intrinsic reactivity of selected biomass
feedstocks and to quantitatively characterize the inhibitory effects of product gases, specifically
hydrogen (H2) on steam gasification rates. By deriving kinetic parameters through
thermogravimetric analysis, this study provides the foundational data required for the process
modeling and reactor design optimization carried out in subsequent project tasks. The University
of Utah TGA has been used for this subtask. The schematic design is shown in Figure 8.

To ensure the relevance of laboratory findings to pilot-scale application, experimental parameters
were selected to reflect the projected environment within an SEG gasifier. Based on the CO2
absorption profiles of calcined limestone (detailed in Subtask 3.2), optimal sorption occurs
between 650°C and 700°C. Sorbent sintering and activity loss become pronounced above 850°C.
Consequently, TGA experiments were performed within a range of 650-750°C.

The range of hydrogen concentrations (0%, 5%, and 10%) was determined based on preliminary
experimental results. As observed from Figure 10, at 700°C, even without the presence of
hydrogen, the biomass did not gasify to total conversion. From the simulation results (detailed in
Subtask 5.1), it is likely to produce more than 50 vol% of hydrogen from dry flue gas exited
from gasifier. However, H2 is well-known inhibitor of char steam gasification and with a suitable
temperature range for CO2 sorption, the resulting reaction rate would be significantly slow with
50 vol% of H2.

A gasification period of up to 300 minutes was selected. While typical reactor residence times
are shorter, this extended duration was necessary to observe the conversion of the char under
inhibitory conditions. In high-hydrogen environments, the reaction rate was observed to be
significantly slow; for example, at 10% H2, only 0.4% of the original mass was gasified between
150 and 300 minutes (Table 9). Quantifying this near-asymptotic behavior is essential for
identifying the operational limits even if residence time is impractical for industrial scale-up
systems.

summary, the intrinsic reactivity order of the materials is established as HALP >> CS > LP.
Conversely, the sensitivity to H₂ inhibition follows the same trend, with the most reactive char
(HALP) being the most severely inhibited and there is a threshold value for the amount of
hydrogen to affect the gasification rate.

The dramatic differences between the char types highlight the role of char structure and
composition. The superior reactivity of the HALP char is likely attributable to a higher
concentration of catalytically active mineral matter, specifically alkali and alkaline earth metals
(AAEMs), which is consistent with its significantly higher ash content (14.15 wt%) compared to
the other samples. The extreme inhibition observed for HALP suggests that the active sites
responsible for its high reactivity, likely the catalytic sites, also have a high affinity for hydrogen
adsorption (large adsorption equilibrium constant, K3) as shown in Table 11. This implies that
the catalytic mechanism is particularly susceptible to being blocked by H2, leading to the
observed balance between high reactivity and high inhibition.

Zhang et al. [20] also observed with increased catalytic material or ash content, the reaction rate
increased compared to ash free char. They concluded that the catalytic materials are providing
more active sites for reactants and the reaction mechanism even changes from separative active
site to common active sites, which promotes a competition between H2O and H2 for active
carbon sites. Also, with the fast hydrogen adsorption and slow desorption, the C(H) or C(H2)
complexes could be present during the experiment and occupying the carbon active sites. This
adsorption not only decreases the number of active sites associated with the carbon but also
reduce the active catalytic sites [21]. The initial high-rate phase could be driven by finely
dispersed alkali and alkaline earth metallic (AAEM) species [22].

Subtask 3.2 Lab-scale Sorption-Enhanced Gasification Studies
The technical core of the SEG process lies in the multifunctional performance of the calcium
oxide (CaO) sorbent. This section provides a detailed analysis of the two primary roles of
limestone-derived CaO: the in-situ capture of CO2 and the catalytic decomposition of biomass-
derived tars.

CO2 Sorption Studies

The primary objective of this section is to determine the optimum operating conditions for CO2
sorption using limestone (CaCO3) to enhance hydrogen production in sorption-enhanced
gasification (SEG) processes. It aims to identify the ideal temperature for CO2 absorption,
evaluate the performance of various bed materials including limestone and olivine, and
understand the complex carbonation kinetics occurring in the presence of steam and syngas
components such as CO and H2. Furthermore, the investigation seeks to analyze the multicyclic
stability and decay of the sorbent to establish its long-term viability in industrial applications.

The experimental investigations were conducted using a lab-scale fluidized bed reactor (FBR)
setup, illustrated in Figure 16. The system is centered around a vertical FBR tube placed within
an electric furnace, which provides the necessary thermal environment for both high-temperature
calcination and controlled carbonation. Gas delivery is precisely managed by multiple Mass
Flow Controllers (MFCs) that regulate the individual and mixed flows of N2, CO2, CO, and H2.
To ensure accurate thermal monitoring, a thermocouple is integrated with a temperature
controller to maintain specific bed temperatures throughout the reaction cycles. Real-time gas
analysis is performed using a CO2 analyzer that monitors the flue gas concentration, with data
transmitted to a computer for integration and subsequent sorption analysis. High-purity limestone
(CaCO3) serves as the primary sorbent, with a standard sample mass of approximately 40 g
utilized for each individual test run.

Before each carbonation test, the limestone underwent a calcination step to convert the CaCO3
into reactive CaO. This process began by heating the reactor at a steady rate of 10°C/min until it
reaches a set calcination temperature of 950°C. Throughout this heating phase, a constant flow of
1.2 to 1.6 SLPM of N2 was maintained to facilitate the desorption of CO2 from the sorbent. The
progress of the reaction was monitored via the CO2 analyzer, and calcination was considered
complete once the CO2 concentration in the flue gas returns to a zero baseline, indicating that all
carbonate has been converted to oxide.

 24

 Figure 16. Schematic of system for investigating calcination and carbonation of limestone.

Following the completion of calcination, the reactor was cooled to the desired carbonation
temperature, typically ranging between 600°C and 700°C. Baseline tests were generally
conducted using a gas mixture composed of 20 vol% CO2 and balance N2, standardizing to flows
of 0.3 SLPM and 1.2 SLPM, respectively. To ensure accurate measurement, dry runs were
performed with an empty reactor under identical conditions to establish a baseline CO2
concentration profile. The actual amount of CO2 absorbed by the limestone was then determined
by integrating the area between the CO2 concentration curves of the dry run and the experimental
run.

As seen in Figure 17 (bottom), there is a competing effect of carbonation and calcination of the
limestone (CaCO3 ↔ CaO + CO2). At the same time, limestone undergoes calcination, and
calcined limestone absorbs CO2, reaching equilibrium. Therefore, determining the amount of
CO2 absorbed during the carbonation step at 750°C remains elusive. Figure 18 shows on a per
mass basis the CO2 absorption of limestone at different carbonation temperatures. It is observed
that with increasing the temperature, CO2 absorption of limestone increases gradually until
750°C and after that, no further carbonation takes place because, at higher temperatures, there
will be calcination of limestone rather than carbonation of calcined limestone [23]. Our
observations concur with existing literature where it is reported that due to the chemical
equilibrium of the CO2 absorption reaction, the gasification temperature is limited to
temperatures below 720°C [24]. It is to be noted that sorption capacity of limestone on a per
mass basis at 650°C and 700°C is similar (Figure 18). Moreover, Fuchs, et al. reported the
impact of gasification temperature on the process characteristics of sorption-enhanced reforming
of biomass, where it is found that at a gasification temperature between 600 to 700°C, the vol%
of H2 in the main product gas from the gasifier would be maximized, vol% of CO2 would be
minimized, and the ratio of H2/CO would be maximized [25]. Considering our experimental
results and similar findings from the literature, we recommend using 650°C and 700°C as a
target gasifier temperature, which will then be used as the gasification temperature.

CO is a well-known poison for many catalysts, and its presence can significantly inhibit catalytic
reactions. This is primarily due to CO's strong adsorption on catalyst surfaces and to compete

with other reactants for active sites on the catalyst. CO may not react and consume CaO in this

As steam is also another reactant present in the gasifier for SEG process, the interaction between
steam and limestone has been investigated. The overall effect of steam on the sorbent
performance of CaO is still up for debate. Several authors have claimed that steam injection
during carbonation has little or no effect on CO2 carrying capacity [27,28], other researchers
have found that it has a positive impact [29,30].

To explore this further, we conducted carbonation with steam (in-situ) and ex-situ steam-
hydrated calcined limestone. It is generally accepted that the extension of carbonation can be
improved by using CaO hydration to obtain Ca(OH)2. Some researchers have proposed that the
provisional production of Ca(OH)2 is the cause of the improved conversion since it is more
reactive toward CO2 than CaO [28,30]. It is questionable if this process still holds true at
temperatures beyond 500°C when the formation of Ca(OH)2 is no longer thermodynamically
favored, even though it makes sense at intermediate temperatures [31]. In these situations, the

presence of steam. As a result, we also conducted the ex-situ steam hydration at a lower

balance N2 and then subsequent carbonation at 650°C with 20 vol% CO2 and 80 vol% N2. The
comparison between three methods is shown in Figure 28.

CaCO3 for all cyclic tests and observed a sorbent decay profile similar to that shown in Figure

decay after multicyclic carbonation of limestone follows a similar trend. The impact of limestone
or dolomite type on carbonation capacity has been discussed. Research indicates that different
types of limestone can significantly influence the efficiency of CaO in capturing SO2 from
combustion gases to form CaSO4 [37]. Various limestones exhibit distinct textures upon
calcination, leading to diverse sulfation patterns and maximum levels of sulfation [38]. However,
it's important to note that the mechanism differs between sulfation and carbonation reactions.
While pore blockage due to the higher molar volume of CaSO4 compared to CaCO3 or CaO
precursor plays a significant role in sulfation, it's less pronounced in carbonation [39]. Although
some instances of pore blockage during carbonation have been observed under specific
conditions, previous studies have shown similar decay trends among different limestones for
various carbonates [31,36,40], albeit with a limited number of cycles and high-purity carbonates.
Therefore, similar to previous studies, based on our experimental findings, we can conclude that
the sorbent decay profile at different carbonation temperatures is sorbent-specific.

As shown in Figure 32C, steam hydration or reactivation of calcined limestone (CaO) before
carbonation enhances CO2 sorption capacity during the carbonation stage. This is consistent with
previous literature where it is reported that Ca(OH)2 sorbent, formed during steam hydration of
calcined CaO, has a better performance for CO2 absorption than CaO only [32,33]. In both cases,
steam enhances the CO2 sorption capacity of CaO when compared to dry carbonation of CaO
(Figure 32C). The role of steam addition during carbonation can be better understood by its
ability to create micropores and large cracks, thereby creating an easier diffusion channel for
CO2 to diffuse and react to the unreacted CaO at the core of the particle. It is well known that
pore plugging plays a crucial role in sorbent decay over a multicycle. When CaO reacts with CO2,
the small pores, especially those located near the sorbent particle surface, are clogged, and this
closure of pores close to the particle surface prevents CO2 diffusion toward the partially reacted
core [35]. Introducing steam during carbonation can increase the CO2 diffusion extent inside the
pore, which could lead to an increase in carbonation conversion. Furthermore, it should be noted
that although steam doesn't have any significant role in the fast kinetic controlled regime of
carbonation, it significantly affects the diffusion-controlled regime by enhancing solid-state
diffusion of CO2 towards the unreacted core, which is evident from the apparent decrease in the
reaction time in the diffusion-controlled regime (Figure 33).

The carbonation process reveals two distinct phases, as discussed in the literature, [41] shown in
Figure 33. Initially, rapid carbonation occurs at the surface of CaO particles, characterized by a
reaction-controlled phase. Subsequently, a considerably slower phase follows, during which CO2
diffuses through the CaCO3 layer formed on the CaO surface in the preceding stage. Therefore,
as seen in Figure 33, CO2 absorption mainly occurs during the fast reaction-controlled phase. It
may be seen that when steam is introduced into the carbonation atmosphere, the outlet CO2

 39

concentration profile reaches that of the inlet CO2 concentration profile earlier than that of the N2
carbonation atmosphere, resulting in an increased carbonation rate in the diffusion-controlled
regime. Steam increases the carbonation rate during the slow diffusion-controlled regime while
not affecting the carbonation rate during the first rapid reaction-controlled regime [18,41]. This
enhanced diffusion rate can be viewed as a result of cracking and increased pore volume, making
an easier diffusion channel for CO2 through the CaCO3 layer [30].

On the contrary, when CO/H2 is introduced during carbonation, the extent of diffusion becomes
slower, which is evident from an increase in carbonation time in the slow diffusion-controlled
regime. We hypothesized that CO and H2 may also interact with CaO active sites, leading to a
competitive adsorption for the available reactive sites on the CaO surface, which can reduce CO2
absorption. If CO and H2 adsorb with CaO more readily and strongly than CO2 or occupy active
sites on the CaO surface, they can inhibit CO2 absorption. Moreover, carbon deposition is found
in the presence of CO, which can be attributed to the Boudouard reaction (2CO ↔ C + CO2). [42]
We hypothesized that this reaction could result in carbon fouling (deposition of soot or coke) on
CaO surfaces and block the CaO active sites, which are otherwise available for CO2, thereby
reducing the sorption capacity of CaO.

Tar Cracking Studies

A defining technical barrier in thermochemical biomass conversion is the formation of
condensable heavy hydrocarbons, collectively termed "tars." These species not only represent a
loss in carbon conversion efficiency but also pose significant operational risks due to
downstream fouling and catalyst poisoning.

The selection of an optimum catalyst for tar cracking is a critical factor in enhancing the
efficiency of biomass gasification. Historically, research has focused on three primary classes of
catalysts: naturally occurring minerals (dolomite, olivine) and supported metallic catalysts (Fe
and Ni-based).

 • Dolomite CaMg(CO3)2: Dolomite is a widely studied, inexpensive mineral that exhibits
 significant activity toward tar reduction once calcined. However, its implementation is
 hindered by its mechanical fragility; it is inherently soft and prone to severe attrition,
 leading to high solids carryover and downstream fouling [44]. Research by Orio et al. [45]
 demonstrated that tar cracking activity varies with chemical composition, with increased
 performance linked to larger pore diameters and higher Fe2O3 content.
 • Metallic and Bimetallic Catalysts: Supported iron and nickel catalysts are highly effective
 for tar gasification and can favorably alter the gaseous product distribution [46]. Recent
 studies by Sun et al. [47] synthesized bimetallic dolomite catalysts (Fe and Ni supported
 on dolomite), which showed high activity for naphthalene cracking and improved
 mechanical hardness. While nickel-based catalysts are particularly potent [48,49], they
 suffer from rapid deactivation due to coking. Furthermore, the toxicity of nickel
 compounds presents environmental challenges regarding the disposal of spent materials.
 • Olivine: As an alternative to dolomite, olivine is a mineral containing MgO, Fe2O3, and
 SiO2, which is favored for its superior attrition resistance. Although untreated olivine
 typically exhibits lower activity than dolomite [44], its performance can be enhanced via
 thermal pretreatment or metal impregnation. For instance, air-pretreated olivine shows
 significantly higher naphthalene conversion [50]. Metallic iron is an active species for the
 destruction of aromatic hydrocarbons; Fe-impregnated olivine has demonstrated a
 threefold increase in toluene conversion compared to raw mineral [51], with performance
 levels comparable to Ni-impregnated variants [52].

Considering the trade-offs between catalytic activity, mechanical durability, and cost, the present
study utilizes olivine as a foundational bed material. The objective is to evaluate the inherent
catalytic capabilities of limestone-derived CaO for tar model compounds and subsequently
compare these results against modified catalysts featuring metal additives.

Subtask 3.3 Evaluate Methods to Maximize Hydrogen Production
Considering the experimental results from Subtasks 3.1 and 3.2, a comprehensive strategy to
maximize hydrogen yield in the dual fluidized bed (DFB) SEG process can be done. This

 47

strategy focuses on optimizing reactor architecture, operational parameters, and sorbent
management to overcome the inherent kinetic limitations of biomass gasification and CO2
sorption.

Reactor Design and Scale-Up Strategy. The Circulating Fluidized Bed (CFB) configuration
would be recommended. It is better to the bubbling bed designs for industrial-scale SEG
deployment. While bubbling beds offer simplicity, they are constrained by limited practical bed
heights that restrict gas residence time, thereby limiting the extent of the water-gas shift (WGS)
reaction. The CFB reactor utilizes taller reactor risers, providing the sufficient residence time
required for gas-phase reactions to approach kinetic equilibrium. Furthermore, the CFB design is
more readily scalable to the targeted 5-50 MW range. Scaling a bubbling bed typically involves
increasing the diameter, which introduces significant challenges in maintaining uniform biomass
feeding and gas-solid mixing, issues that are effectively bypassed by the CFB system.

Optimization of Steam Throughput and Inhibition Mitigation. Maximizing steam flow to the
gasifier is a critical factor for driving hydrogen production and maintaining high gasification
rates. High steam partial pressure not only drives reforming and WGS reactions forward but also
serves as a vital tool for mitigating the inhibitory effects of product gases. High-pressure TGA
studies in Subtask 3.1 established that H2 is a potent inhibitor of char steam gasification; for
instance, at 700°C with 10 vol% H2, the reaction rate is significantly slowed. Higher steam
throughput dilutes the concentration of H2 and CO, reducing competitive adsorption on active
char sites. This is particularly critical for reactive but inhibition-prone feedstocks like High-Ash
Loblolly Pine (HALP), which demonstrated the highest intrinsic reactivity (HALP >> CS > LP)
but also the most severe sensitivity to H2 inhibition. To maintain adequate throughput, the system
should operate at the upper end of the stable sorbent temperature window, approximately 750°C.

Sorbent Management and Carbonation Kinetics. Maximizing H2 production requires a high
limestone-to-biomass ratio to ensure an excess of CaO sites for instantaneous CO2 capture.
Experimental investigations in Subtask 3.2 identified that optimal CO2 sorption occurs between
650°C and 700°C, with capacity decreasing significantly above 730°C due to the onset of
calcination. While syngas components like CO and H2 can inhibit carbonation by competing for
active sites in dry conditions, extending residence times by up to 20 minutes, the presence of
steam acts as a dominant promotional mechanism. Steam creates micropores and large cracks in
the CaO structure, which provides an easier diffusion channel for CO2 to reach the unreacted
core, thereby enhancing the sorption capacity by approximately 25% compared to dry conditions.

Operational Strategy and Downstream Integration Because fundamental studies revealed that the
calcination/carbonation cycling of limestone is relatively slow, with only an estimated 10-30% of
the bed material actively participating in any given pass, the total limestone inventory must be
significantly higher than the stoichiometric requirement. The project recommends a high
circulation rate to ensure that fresh, calcined sorbent is continuously supplied to the gasifier from
the combustor. Finally, while the SEG process significantly enhances in-situ H2 yield, achieving
fuel-cell grade purity requires a downstream configuration that integrates the DFB gasifier with
an external water-gas shift reactor for residual CO conversion and a Pressure Swing Adsorption
(PSA) unit for final gas separation.

The design of incorporating a recycle loop around the gasifier reactor and having an overflow
line to return material to the combustor has three advantages:

 1. The gasifier-recycle acts as a cushion, or buffer. If the gasifier reactor cyclone outlet is
 connected directly to the combustor (via a loop seal), the two reactors would be very
 tightly coupled so that any change in flow rates in either the combustor reactor or gasifier
 reactor would perpetuate through the entire system, decreasing operation stability.

 2. The configuration provides two “knobs” to adjust conditions in the reactors. The flow
 rate of air into the combustor reactor (fluidizing velocity) dictates the overall circulation
 rate of the bed material between the reactors. The flow rate of fluidizing gas in the
 gasifier reactor has limited influence on overall circulation. However, the balance of total
 bed material inventory in each of the reactors can be adjusted by changing the gasifier
 reactor fluidizing gas flow rate. By adjusting the combustor reactor and gasifier reactor
 flow rates simultaneously it is possible, for example, to achieve a particular solids
 circulation rate while maintaining 1/3 of the total bed material in the combustor reactor
 and 2/3 in the gasifier reactor.

 3. Because the overflow line from the gasifier to the combustor is low in the bed, it is
 possible to operate the gasifier as a bubbling bed by keeping the fluidizing velocity low.
 This is particularly useful during heat up to reduce velocities and overall severity of the
 environment and to minimize material loss through the gasifier reactor cyclone.

The SEG process is characterized by interconnected fluidized beds operating under varying
fluidization conditions. SEG incorporates continuous feeding of biomass and steam along with
solid sorbents, primarily calcium oxide, which facilitates in situ capture of CO2 produced during
gasification, thus significantly improving hydrogen production efficiency. The focus of the
modeling is on the gasifier. The combustor is not directly modeled.

The developed simulation is a one-dimensional (1D) numerical model comprising ordinary
differential equations (ODEs) that represent mass and energy balances in a fluidized-bed reactor
designed explicitly for sorption-enhanced gasification (SEG). The model is formulated along the

reactor. Thermal and concentration-driven diffusion effects are assumed to be negligible to
simplify numerical computations. The reactor is discretized vertically into a finite number of
ideally mixed control volumes using the finite volume method (FVM) as shown in Figure 56.

In the top part of Figure 64, the rise in volumetric flow (red dashed line) further supports the
intensification of thermal cracking and the endothermic steam reforming of hydrocarbons at
elevated temperatures, which increases total molar output at the expense of hydrogen selectivity.

The middle plot shows the influence of the Steam-to-Carbon (S/C) ratio, a key factor for
controlling the gasification environment. High S/C ratios theoretically favor H2 production by
providing the necessary reactant for both the steam gasification and WGS reactions. However,
the modeled data suggests a plateau in gas composition beyond an S/C ratio of approximately 1.5.
This indicates that once the stoichiometric requirements for maximizing the WGS reaction are
met, further increases in steam partial pressure yield diminishing returns on gas quality. While
excess steam can suppress soot formation and drive the reforming of heavier tars, it also
introduces a significant demand of heat load, requiring more energy to maintain the reactor
temperature. The relative stability of the CH4 and CO fractions across the 1.5 to 3.0 range
suggests that the sorbent's ability to remove CO2 is the primary bottleneck rather than steam
availability.

Finally, the sensitivity analysis regarding the CaCO3 (Limestone) to Biomass ratio demonstrates
the effectiveness of the sorbent loop in a steady-state regime. The bottom plot shows that even at
relatively low ratios (0.1lb/lb), the reactor achieves a high H2 volume percentage, which remains
remarkably stable as the sorbent loading increases. This suggests that the system is operating in a
regime where the kinetics of CO2 capture are sufficiently fast, or the sorbent is present in
significant excess of the equilibrium requirements at the specified temperature. However, it is
important to note that these results likely reflect a simplified assumption of ideal sorbent
performance. In practice, the lack of distinctive sensitivity to the limestone/biomass ratio may be
attributed to the exclusion of sorbent deactivation mechanisms, such as sintering and attrition,
which progressively reduce the effective surface area and carbonation capacity over multiple
cycles.

Incorporating a deactivation model would likely reveal a "critical threshold" for the sorbent-to-
fuel ratio, below which the system would fail to maintain the H2 enrichment observed here. This
distinction is vital for moving from a theoretical equilibrium analysis to a practical process
design that accounts for the cycle of the bed material.

The integration of an Oxy-Sorption Enhanced Gasification (Oxy-SEG) configuration, as depicted
in Figure 65, represents an improvement in achieving carbon-negative hydrogen production
through Bioenergy with Carbon Capture and Storage (BECCS). The system achieves
autothermal operation at a combustor temperature of 1147°C by utilizing 46.4 kg of O2 per
kilogram of biomass, which facilitates the high-temperature calcination required to regenerate
the sorbent. The simulated yield of 0.154 kg H2 per kilogram of biomass, paired with a net
process efficiency of 68% (LHV), underlines the thermodynamic viability of this approach for
high-purity syngas generation without the significant energy drawbacks typically associated with
post-combustion processes.

The implications of the Oxy-SEG setup are particularly relevant for negative-emission

produces a hydrogen-rich syngas comparable to conventional gasification with downstream
capture but does so while maintaining energy efficiency and enabling autothermal behavior.
Such results verify that the SEG system is a promising candidate for industrial-scale process
intensification, implementing cost-effective energy production.

different factors affect the levelized cost of hydrogen (LCOH). In this analysis, actual dollar

and operating costs with any degree of certainty. Instead, values were input when known (for
example, an input cost per ton of biomass), and approximations were provided by the Aspen Plus
modeling software for other components such as capital, labor and utility costs. The assumption
is that even if all the input numbers are uncertain, a sensitivity analysis to explore the impact of

changes in critical process parameters on LCOH will at least indicate directionality and which
have the largest influence.

The sensitivity analysis, visualized through the chart in Figure 66, identifies H2 yield and

from the autothermal efficiency achieved in the combustor. This implies that pre-treatment and
drying technologies are not merely supplementary but are fundamental to maintaining the 68%

compared to the costs of raw materials. This implies that research efforts should be prioritized
toward maximizing the carbonation-calcination efficiency and syngas selectivity rather than
purely seeking cheaper sorbents or feedstocks. The non-distinctive results for the limestone-to-
biomass ratio further suggest that once a stoichiometric threshold is met, the economic focus
shifts entirely toward heat integration and yield optimization.

established and economically viable framework for hydrogen production. By achieving
autothermal operation and effectively achieving desired H2 purity from total volumetric flow
through in-situ CO2 capture, the system eliminates the need for complex downstream processing
of gas separation. When scaled to a large facility processing many hundreds of tons of biomass,
the process has the potential to produce hydrogen at costs competitive with other renewable
approaches, pending more detailed techno-economic analysis.

 SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

The "Process Intensification of Hydrogen Production through Sorption-Enhanced Gasification of
Biomass" project successfully advanced the technological readiness of sorption-enhanced
gasification (SEG) through a multi-faceted R&D program. By integrating fundamental kinetic
studies, advanced feedstock preparation, pilot-scale demonstration, and comprehensive process
modeling, the project team validated the feasibility of this innovative pathway for clean
hydrogen production.

Key Technical Conclusions:

 1. Feedstock Flexibility and Optimization: The project successfully demonstrated that
 diverse biomass feedstocks, ranging from clean wood (Loblolly Pine) to high-ash
 agricultural residues (Corn Stover) and hardwoods (Sweet Gum), can be effectively
 pelletized and utilized in a pressurized feeding system. The development of co-pelletized
 biomass-limestone fuel represents a significant process intensification achievement.
 Experimental results confirmed that a 16x60 mesh limestone particle size offers superior
 structural integrity during calcination/carbonation cycles compared to finer fractions,
 minimizing attrition and ensuring stable fluidization.

 2. Fundamental Kinetics and Hydrogen Inhibition: Pressurized TGA studies revealed
 distinct reactivity profiles among the feedstocks, establishing the order: High-Ash
 Loblolly Pine (HALP) >> Corn Stover (CS) > Clean Loblolly Pine (LP). The superior
 reactivity of HALP is attributed to the catalytic effect of its high alkali metal content.
 However, a critical trade-off was identified: highly reactive chars are also the most
 susceptible to inhibition by product gases. The kinetic modeling quantified a severe
 inhibition effect from hydrogen, where even 5% H_2 concentration significantly retarded
 the gasification rate. This finding underscores the necessity of high steam throughput in
 the gasifier to dilute product gases and maintain reaction kinetics.

 3. Advanced Tar Mitigation Strategies: Lab-scale reforming studies provided a robust
 solution for the persistent challenge of tar formation. While thermal cracking alone was
 insufficient at typical SEG temperatures (700°C), the use of catalytic bed materials
 proved highly effective. The project developed an iron-promoted dolomite catalyst (10wt%
 Fe) that achieved 76% naphthalene conversion at 700°C, significantly outperforming
 plain dolomite and olivine. This catalytic enhancement is crucial for producing a clean
 syngas suitable for downstream applications without extensive secondary cleanup.

 4. Reactor Design for Scale-Up: The comprehensive modeling and PDU testing confirmed
 that a Circulating Fluidized Bed (CFB) configuration is the optimal design for
 commercial-scale SEG. Unlike bubbling beds, the CFB architecture allows for taller
 risers that provide the necessary gas residence time for the water-gas shift reaction to
 approach equilibrium. Furthermore, the 1-D reactor model validated that high solids

 78

 circulation rates are essential to supply sufficient fresh CaO to the gasifier, compensating
 for the relatively slow carbonation kinetics and partial utilization of the sorbent inventory.

 5. Economic Viability and Pathway to Market: Techno-economic analysis indicates that the
 baseline levelized cost of hydrogen (LCOH) has the potential to be competitive with
 other renewable pathways, notably because the oxy-SEG variant allows one to take
 advantage of tax credits for CO2 capture and storage for negative emissions. Sensitivity
 analysis identified feedstock cost and plant capacity as the primary cost drivers.

This project has investigated sorption-enhanced gasification of biomass as a promising, process-
intensified technology for carbon-negative hydrogen production. The data and models generated
provide a solid foundation for the next stage of development, which should be the design,
operation, and thorough performance evaluation of a continuously operating pilot plant to
demonstrate long-term reliability and sorbent durability.
