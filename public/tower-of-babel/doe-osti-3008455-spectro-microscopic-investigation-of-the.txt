EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This project investigated physico-chemical characteristics of atmospheric particles collected
during DOE ARM field campaigns and how they are involved in the hydrological cycle. Our
efforts were focused on multi-modal chemical imaging and molecular characterization of
atmospheric particles and snow samples collected during the Surface-Atmosphere Integrated
Field Laboratory (SAIL) campaign and particle samples from the Tracking Aerosol Convection
Interactions ExpeRiment (TRACER) campaign. Complementary small-scale laboratory and field
studies were conducted to test, refine, and advance emerging chemical characterization
methodologies aimed at investigating light-absorbing (brown carbon) aerosol particles, including
their molecular composition and key physical properties such as viscosity and volatility. We
examined elemental and molecular composition, mixing state, size, and morphology for
individual particles, along with molecular-level characterization of complex light-absorbing
organic constituents in bulk aerosol and snows samples. By combining these analyses with
meteorological, cloud, and in-situ aerosol field measurements, we investigate relevant aerosol
characteristics, aerosol-precipitation interactions, and changes in surface energy balance induced
by snowpack deposits. We investigated the contribution of natural and anthropogenic sources to
populations of atmospheric particles during representative atmospheric conditions; characterized
particle composition and assessed their cloud-forming propensity; correlated typical particle-type
and mixing state characteristics with real-time measurements in the areas of studies. We used
multi-modal spectro-microscopy techniques to examine the elemental and molecular
composition, mixing state, size, and morphology of individual atmospheric particles. Computer-
controlled scanning electron microscopy coupled with X-ray microanalysis enabled quantitative
determination of particle elemental composition, while synchrotron-based scanning transmission
X-ray microscopy was used to obtain quantitative insights into particle mixing state and the
molecular bonding environments of carbon. Obtained particle measurements were integrated
with real-time observations of aerosol size distributions, cloud condensation nuclei
concentrations, hygroscopicity, and bulk aerosol composition, enabling a comprehensive
characterization of aerosol properties and processes. We applied molecular-level characterization
techniques to identify composition-specific sources and transformation pathways of atmospheric
brown carbon aerosols, thereby advancing mechanistic understanding of these chemically
complex atmospheric mixtures. Our measurements generated practical, composition-resolved
aerosol metrics that are essential for informing and improving future atmospheric modeling
efforts.

PROJECT ACTIVITIES

 We began the project in August 2021. However, due to restrictions on travel, field activities,
and user-facility operations during the COVID-19 pandemic, the research progressed differently
than originally planned. Our field operations at SAIL commenced only in April 2022, after
Purdue University lifted travel restrictions for its personnel, and the TRACER campaign was
likewise postponed to the summer of 2022. In the interim, we conducted a series of ad hoc
laboratory studies using various aerosol and snow samples previously collected by our group and
our external collaborators, selected to be analogous to those anticipated from the planned field
deployments. These studies enabled us to establish, refine, and validate new analytical
methodologies and data-interpretation workflows ahead of full field-campaign operations.
 1. Chemical composition and optical properties of organic carbon (OC) in snow samples.
Using methodologies previously established in our group, we conducted a series of laboratory
studies to systematically analyze OC components in snowpack samples collected in China. This
work served as a case study, aimed at developing and validating analytical protocols that would
streamline the forthcoming analysis of SAIL snow samples. The study relied on a set of
snowpack samples brought to our laboratory by visiting scholars from China. We performed
detailed chemical characterization of brown carbon (BrC) associated with the water-soluble
organic carbon fraction using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) coupled with a
photodiode array (PDA) detector and high-resolution mass spectrometry. The identified
chromophores were grouped into five chemically distinct classes. We demonstrated that the
identification of BrC chromophores, along with quantitative analysis of their optical properties,
provides important insights into the role of BrC in snow radiative balance and photochemical
processes. The results of this work were published in Zhou et al., 2022 and Zhou et al., 2025. We
also investigated the photolytic degradation of water-soluble organic carbon (WSOC) in
snowmelt and assessed how ultraviolet irradiation alters its molecular composition, BrC
constituents, and associated effects on snow albedo. Our results showed that WSOC and its BrC
components in samples from remote regions were comparatively less susceptible to UV-induced
changes than those collected in urban environments. Samples influenced by soil and vegetation
exhibited markedly higher photoactivity: most chromophores were lost upon irradiation,
accompanied by substantial formation of nitrogen- and sulfur-containing organic compounds.
These findings demonstrated that photo-induced processes can significantly modify both the
molecular composition and radiative properties of WSOC in snowpack, with important
implications for mountainous regions where seasonal snow serves as the primary freshwater
resource. The results of this work were published in Zhou et al., 2024.
 At the SAIL field site, we collected snow samples impacted by long-range transported
atmospheric aerosol during pollution events on February 21–23, 2022, and April 11, 2023. The
chemical composition and optical properties of light-absorbing components in these samples
have been investigated in laboratory studies using methodologies developed in our group.
Chemical imaging (CI) and molecular characterization analyses indicated that the deposited
light-absorbing contaminants (LACs) were dominated by mineral dust and WSOC BrC, with
minor contributions from insoluble BC particles. The results from this work will be presented
and discussed in our forthcoming publication, Sharpe et al., 2026(a).
 2. Chemical Composition of Atmospheric Brown Carbon. We demonstrated a novel
approach for analyzing organic aerosol (OA) samples collected by an aethalometer, enabling
simultaneous determination of optical properties and molecular-level characterization of OA

composition and volatility. This dual-capability method offers a valuable new tool for advancing
aerosol chemistry research, particularly in the study of BrC. In an initial pilot study, we analyzed
the chemical composition of BrC in aethalometer-collected aerosol samples from a suburban site
in the northwestern Indo-Gangetic Plain, brought to our laboratory by visiting scholars from
India. Pronounced compositional differences were observed between urban-influenced and rural-
influenced samples. Additionally, molecular analysis of BrC revealed unexpected near-infrared
absorption arising from long-chain organic acids, leading to systematic overestimation of BC and
underestimation of BrC concentrations in aethalometer-derived optical data. These findings
highlight the need to reassess common assumptions used to apportion BrC and BC contributions
in aerosol optical measurements. The results of this work were published in Chaudhary et al.,
2025.
 At the SAIL field site, we deployed and operated an AE33 aethalometer from April 2022 to
October 2023 to obtain continuous measurements of aerosol light absorption by both black
carbon and brown carbon, while simultaneously collecting bulk PM2.5 filter samples. The optical
records were used to identify filters exhibiting distinct absorption characteristics, which were
subsequently analyzed using our molecular characterization methodologies. Results from this
work will be presented and discussed in our forthcoming publication, Sharpe et al., 2026(b).
 3. Chemical imaging of individual atmospheric particles. To investigate the correlation
between our chemical imaging (CI) measurements of substrate-collected particles and in situ
optical properties recorded at field sites, we collaborated with the ASR-funded group of R.
Chakrabarty at Washington University in St. Louis (WUStL) ahead of the delayed TRACER
campaign. Together, we designed and conducted a controlled laboratory study in which BrC
aerosols with varying optical properties were generated in a smoke chamber, followed by real-
time optical measurements, sample collection, and subsequent CI analysis. Selected biomass
fuels were burnt to produce aerosols, which were collected using a cascade impactor. Individual
particles were then examined using CI and bulk samples were analyzed using molecular
characterization methods to determine particle types, quantify their external and internal mixing
states, while bulk aerosol samples were used and classify BrC chemical types. Parameterized
mixing-state metrics were correlated with the corresponding online optical measurements
recorded for each burn experiment. The results revealed substantial fuel-dependent differences in
particle composition and optical properties, underscoring the importance of source-specific
characterization for interpreting ambient BrC measurements. The molecular characterization
results of this work were published in Siemens et al., 2024. The CI results from this work will be
presented and discussed in our forthcoming publication, Rivera-Adorno et al., 2026.
 Furthermore, the method-development components of these studies are reported in Rivera-
Adorno et al., 2024, and in the forthcoming publication Sharpe et al., 2026(c). Rivera-Adorno et
al., 2024 introduced an application of SEM imaging at a tilted angle to infer the viscosity of
individual particles from their measured height-to-width ratios, providing a simple and rapid
means to distinguish among three broad viscosity regimes: “solid,” “viscous,” and “liquid-like.”
This imaging-based approach offers a practical and unique opportunity to assess viscosities of
individual real-world OA particles without relying on specialized or complex instrumentation.
Sharpe et al., 2026(c) presents a new X-ray absorption spectral deconvolution method capable of
differentiating BrC and BC components within individual particles, delivering critical
information needed to improve representation of aerosol optical properties in atmospheric
models.

 We applied our CI methodologies for single-particle characterization to investigate the
composition and evolution of biomass-burning organic aerosol from North American wildfires
(Rivera-Adorno et al., 2025). In a separate study (Sharpe et al, 2025, we demonstrated that
wildfire emissions induce a persistent shift in aerosol composition within the upper troposphere
and lower stratosphere. Biomass-burning organic aerosol transported by pyroconvective systems
contributes substantially to the organic enrichment of the stratospheric aerosol layer. We also
contributed to a study of a regional-scale biomass-burning event in the Mediterranean, which
released substantial amounts of particles and gaseous precursors, significantly enhancing the
growth of pre-existing ultrafine particles and promoting new particle formation (Ajith et al,
2024).
 During July–August 2022, the WUStL group collected ambient particle samples at the AMF1
site during the TRACER campaign. Samples were classified and prioritized for CI analysis based
on real-time aerosol optical property measurements recorded at the field site, along with
compositional and meteorological datasets obtained from ARM core instruments. The CI
experiments required access to the EMSL/PNNL and ALS/LBNL user facilities. Experiments
began in 2023, with additional instrument time allocated to our group in 2024–2025. At the time
of this report, CI data acquisition has been completed and is now entering the analysis and
interpretation phase.
 4. Cloud-forming propensity of atmospheric particles. We collaborated with other ASR-
funded colleagues to provide particle-characterization datasets for broader studies of cloud-
forming particle properties. Using CI techniques, we characterized individual ice-nucleating
particles (INPs) within large ambient particle populations. Working with the group of Prof. D.
Knopf, optical microscopy was used to identify INPs active in immersion-freezing and
deposition modes on collected substrates, after which micro-spectroscopy CI methods were
applied to determine their composition, mixing state, and particle-type classes. From these multi-
modal datasets, a practical INP parameterization was developed to improve predictions of
atmospheric ice-crystal formation. Results from this collaboration are presented in Alpert et al.,
2022; Knopf et al., 2023; and Knopf et al., 2026.
 5. Topical Review Manuscripts. We conducted a comprehensive review of the scientific
literature and published two invited review articles summarizing recent advances in both of our
core research areas: chemical imaging of individual field-collected atmospheric particles and
molecular-level characterization of atmospheric BrC.
 Li et al., 2024 reviewed recent advances in microscopic characterization of soot and organic
particles from fossil-fuel combustion and biomass burning. The article highlighted state-of-the-
art imaging techniques and the use of fractal dimension (Df) and mixing-state metrics to quantify
soot morphology and aging, which are incorporated now into models to improve predictions of
aerosol optical properties and radiative impacts. The review emphasized the complexity of soot
and organic particle properties, their dependence on sources and atmospheric aging, and the
diverse phase behavior of soluble organics. Primary carbonaceous particles such as tar balls and
soot were noted for their strong light absorption and potential health impacts. Key research needs
include improved atmospheric measurements and modeling of particle mixing structures and
their effects.
 Laskin et al., 2025 provided a comprehensive synthesis of current knowledge on the
molecular composition and optical properties of BrC chromophores, reviewing recent molecular-
level characterization studies that identify composition-specific sources and transformation
4

pathways of BrC. The insights gained from these investigations are critical for assessing BrC
optical behavior and for developing practical composition metrics that inform and improve
atmospheric models, ultimately enabling more accurate predictions of BrC evolution, radiative
effects, and impacts on atmospheric processes and visibility.
