Invariants of Legendrian knots in thickened convex surfaces
Nancy Mae Eagles, Zijian Rong

Abstract
. We define a differential graded algebra associated to Legendrian knots in thickened convex surfaces Σ × R \Sigma\times\mathbb{R} . The algebra is defined in the same spirit as the Chekanov-Eliashberg DGA for Legendrians in R 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} , but makes use of the data of the dividing set Γ \Gamma of Σ \Sigma . The algebra is generated by countably many Reeb chords of the Legendrian Λ \Lambda , and its differential counts certain immersed polygons in the projection π : Σ × R → Σ × { 0 } \pi:\Sigma\times\mathbb{R}\to\Sigma\times\{0\} with boundary on π ⁡ ( Λ ) ∪ Γ \pi(\Lambda)\cup\Gamma . We show that the differential squares to zero and that the stable tame isomorphism type of the DGA is invariant under Legendrian isotopy. Finally, we compute several examples and use the invariant to distinguish Legendrian knots in thickened convex surfaces that cannot be distinguished by the classical invariants.



1.
Introduction

Let
(M,\xi)
be a contact
3
-manifold with contact form
\lambda
. An embedding
\Lambda:S^{1}\hookrightarrow M
is a
Legendrian knot
if
T_{p}\Lambda\subset\xi_{p}
for all

. That is,
\Lambda
is everywhere tangent to the contact structure. A smooth map
\varphi:S^{1}\times\mathbb{R}\to M
is a
Legendrian isotopy
if for each

,
\varphi(\cdot,t):S^{1}\to M
is a Legendrian knot. A powerful tool to study Legendrian knots in certain contact manifolds is Legendrian contact homology, developed by Chekanov in
[
4
]
and independently by Eliashberg, Givental, and Hofer in
[
11
]
. The homology theory falls under the ever-expanding framework of symplectic field theory, which comprises invariants built out of pseudoholomorphic curves in certain non-compact symplectic manifolds like the symplectization
\mathbb{R}\times M
. As initially proposed in
[
11
]
, under suitable circumstances one can associate to a knot
\Lambda\subset M
a differential graded algebra
A(\Lambda)
generated by
Reeb chords
of the Legendrian knot. Its differential counts certain J-holomorphic boundary punctured disks in
\mathbb{R}\times M
with asymptotic ends at Reeb chords. Some analytical care must be taken to assure that one can actually
count
such disks. Following the standard setup of Floer theory, one hopes to describe the collection of
J
-holomorphic disks as a
0
-dimensional compact manifold, seen as the solution space of some appropriate Cauchy-Riemann equation with Lagrangian boundary conditions. In the event that the homology theory is well-defined, though the DGA itself is not a Legendrian isotopy invariant, its
stable tame isomorphism class
is. Consequently, the
Legendrian contact homology

H_{*}(A(\Lambda),\partial)
is a Legendrian isotopy invariant up to isomorphism.

Well-definedness and computability of Legendrian contact homology have been achieved in several settings, the first and simplest of which is in the standard contact
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Legendrian contact homology has also been defined in circle bundles over Riemann surfaces with negative curvature
[
25
]
, in
J^{1}(S^{1})

[
22
]
, in
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}

[
6
]
, in
P\times\mathbb{R}
where

is an exact symplectic manifold
[
7
]

[
2
]
, in lens spaces
[
21
]
, in Seifert fibered spaces with a transverse contact structure
[
20
]
, in connected sums of

viewed as boundaries of subcritical Weinstein
4

[
9

, and in open book decompositions with once-punctured torus pages
[
3

. As by its original definition in
[
4

Under such a projection, one may identify Reeb chords with double points of the knot, and holomorphic disks with certain rigid polygons. This is proved in

2.1.
Convex surface theory

We assume familiarity with the standard definitions of contact geometry. We recall some of them here for convenience, but refer the reader to
[
15

[
16

Let
\Sigma

(M,\xi)
be a contact
3

\Sigma\hookrightarrow M
. The surface
\Sigma

of a convex surface

\Gamma=\{p\in\Sigma\ |\ \alpha_{p}(v_{p})=0\}.

Equivalently, it consists of points where the vector field
v
lies in the contact plane
\xi

Foundational work of Giroux
[
18

says that a surface
\Sigma
is convex if and only if its
characteristic foliation

\Sigma_{\xi}:=\xi\cap T\Sigma
is divided by a collection of embedded circles into
\Sigma_{+}
and
\Sigma_{-}
, in the sense that there exists a volume form
\omega
on
\Sigma
and a vector field

on
\Sigma

\Sigma_{\xi}
pointing from
\Sigma_{+}
to
\Sigma_{-}
such that

\Sigma_{\pm}
. This collection of embedded circles is the dividing set
\Gamma
of
\Sigma
with respect to some contact vector field

The dividing set
\Gamma
determines a germ of the contact structure on a neighborhood of
\Sigma
in
M

is some function, and

. The contact condition

\beta\wedge\text{d}u+u\text{d}\beta>0.

We can find a contact form

away from a neighborhood of
\Gamma
. This is (a version of) the

\mathbb{R}
-invariant contact structure

\Sigma\times\mathbb{R}

-invariant contact structure, along with some chosen dividing set

Stable tame automorphisms for countably generated DGAs

In this subsection we review the notions of infinite composition of elementary automorphisms and infinite stabilization introduced by

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a semi-free DGA over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
generated by
c_{1},c_{2}\dots
. An ordering
c_{\sigma(1)}<c_{\sigma(2)}<\dots
of the generators of
\mathcal{A}
(where
\sigma
is a permutation) induces a filtration

\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\mathcal{F}^{0}\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{F}^{1}\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{F}^{2}\mathcal{A}\subset\dots

by
\mathcal{F}_{i}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\langle c_{\sigma(1)},\dots,c_{\sigma(i)}\rangle
. An
elementary
automorphism of
\mathcal{A}
is a DGA automorphism from
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
such that there exists a generator
c_{\sigma(i)}
with

\displaystyle c_{\sigma(i)}

\displaystyle\mapsto c_{\sigma(i)}+v

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle\mapsto c\text{ if }c\neq c_{\sigma(i)},

where
v\in\mathcal{F}^{i-1}\mathcal{A}
. An “infinite composition of elementary automorphisms” is a DGA automorphism such that there exist generators
c_{\sigma(i_{1})},\dots,c_{\sigma(i_{n})}
such that for all
1\leq k\leq n
,

\displaystyle c_{\sigma(i_{k})}

\displaystyle\mapsto c_{\sigma(i_{k})}+v_{k}

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle\mapsto c\text{ if }c\neq c_{\sigma(i_{k})},

where
v_{i_{k}}\in\mathcal{F}^{i_{k}-1}\mathcal{A}
. (Note that the elementary automorphism only depends on the existence of such a filtration and the particular choice of filtration is irrelevant.) A
tame
automorphism of
\mathcal{A}
is a finite composition of “infinite compositions of elementary automorphisms”. Note that the orderings for the infinite compositions of elementary automorphisms need not be the same.

Remark 2.2
.

An elementary automorphism in the sense of
[
9
]
is a possibly infinite composition of elementary automorphisms in the sense of
[
4
]
, where a finite composition of elementary automorphisms is called a tame automorphism. A tame automorphism in the sense of
[
9
]
is a finite composition of elementary automorphisms in the sense of
[
9
]
. In this paper we call an elementary automorphism in the sense of
[
4
]
elementary and an elementary or tame automorphism in the sense of
[
9
]
tame.

Finally the notion of countably many stabilization operations extends naturally from the finite case.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
E=T(\{a_{i},b_{i}\}_{i\in I})
, where

or

,

, and

3.
Legendrian knots in thickened convex surfaces

In this section we study Legendrian knots in
\Sigma\times\mathbb{R}
and Legendrian Reidemeister moves for the convex surface projection
\pi:\Sigma\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\Sigma\times\{0\}
. Proofs will be given in a future paper.

Let
(\Sigma,\Gamma)
be a convex surface with dividing curve
\Gamma
. We equip
\Sigma\times\mathbb{R}
with the contact structure
\xi_{\Gamma}:=\ker(\alpha_{0})

\Lambda

First we investigate the question of lifting an immersed loop
K

\Sigma

\Sigma\times\mathbb{R}
. A necessary condition is that
TK|_{\Gamma}\subset\ker\beta
. The following lemma shows that it is also sufficient.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
K:S^{1}\hookrightarrow\Sigma
be an immersion. Then
K
lifts to a Legendrian arc
\Lambda\subset\Sigma\times\mathbb{R}
if and only if
TK|_{\Gamma}\subset\ker\beta
, i.e.,

is tangent to the characteristic foliation along
\Gamma

Proof.
Let
f:[0,1]\to\Sigma
be a parametrization of
K
. We divide

into sub-arcs

by cutting along
\Gamma
, and denote the induced parametrization of

on each
K_{j}

. Note that
u(f_{j}(i))=0
for

. First, suppose that

TK\subset\ker(\alpha)\implies(\beta+u\text{d}t)_{f_{j}(i)}(f_{j}^{\prime}(i))=0\implies\beta_{f_{j}(i)}(f_{j}^{\prime}(i))=0.

Now suppose that we only know
TK|_{\Gamma}\subset\ker(\beta)
, i.e. that
\beta_{f_{j}(i)}(f_{j}^{\prime}(i))=0
. We’d like to build a lift of each arc
f_{j}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}

\Sigma

such that

(1)

\tilde{f}_{j}(1)=\tilde{f}_{j+1}(0)

To accomplish (1), fix some

Then

In this section we prove that

are DGA maps and thus

[

. First we prove
\widehat{\partial}^{2}=0
assuming that
\Phi_{\pm}
are DGA maps.

Theorem 6.1
.

\widehat{\partial}^{2}=0
.

Proof.
Note that

\displaystyle\widehat{\partial}^{2}(c_{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=\widehat{\partial}(\Omega(\partial_{\Gamma}c_{\Gamma})+\Phi_{+}(c_{\Gamma})+\Phi_{-}(c_{\Gamma}))

\displaystyle=(\widehat{\partial}\circ\Omega+\Phi_{+}+\Phi_{-})(\partial_{\Gamma}c_{\Gamma}).

We claim that for all
w\in\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}
,

(\widehat{\partial}\circ\Omega+\Phi_{+}+\Phi_{-})(w)=\Omega\circ\partial_{\Gamma}(w).

Since
\widehat{\partial},\Gamma,\Phi_{\pm}
are additive, it suffices to prove this when
w
is a monomial. Now we use induction on the length of
w
. For a base case, let
a
be a generator of
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
. Note that

\displaystyle(\widehat{\partial}\circ\Omega+\Phi_{+}+\Phi_{-})(a)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\partial}(a)+\Phi_{+}(a)+\Phi_{-}(a)

\displaystyle=\Omega(\partial_{\Gamma}a)+\Phi_{+}(a)+\Phi_{-}(a)+\Phi_{+}(a)+\Phi_{-}(a)

\displaystyle=\Omega(\partial_{\Gamma}a).

Assume the statement holds for all
w
of length
n-1
. Then

\displaystyle(\widehat{\partial}\circ\Omega+\Phi_{+}+\Phi_{-})(a_{1}\dots a_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{\partial}(\Omega(a_{1}\dots a_{n-1})\Phi_{-}(a_{n})+\Phi_{+}(a_{1}\dots a_{n-1})\widehat{a}_{n})+\Phi_{+}(a_{1}\dots a_{n})+\Phi_{-}(a_{1}\dots a_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{\partial}\circ\Omega(a_{1}\dots a_{n-1})\Phi_{-}(a_{n})+\Omega(a_{1}\dots a_{n-1})\Phi_{-}(\partial_{\Gamma}a_{n})+\Phi_{+}(\partial_{\Gamma}(a_{1}\dots a_{n-1}))\widehat{a}_{n}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\Phi_{+}(a_{1}\dots a_{n-1})(\Omega(\partial_{\Gamma}a_{n})+\Phi_{+}(a_{n})+\Phi_{-}(a_{n}))+\Phi_{+}(a_{1}\dots a_{n})+\Phi_{-}(a_{1}\dots a_{n}).

By the induction hypothesis,

\widehat{\partial}\circ\Omega(a_{1}\dots a_{n-1})=\Omega(\partial_{\Gamma}(a_{1}\dots a_{n-1}))+\Phi_{+}(a_{1}\dots a_{n-1})+\Phi_{-}(a_{1}\dots a_{n-1}).

Hence,

\displaystyle(\widehat{\partial}\circ\Omega+\Phi_{+}+\Phi_{-})(a_{1}\dots a_{n})

\displaystyle=
