COVID‐19's Shadow: The Effect of COVID‐19 on Cytopathology Specimens Received at an Academic Laboratory in Central South Africa
Authors: Courtney T. A. Bonato, Greta Neethling, Liska Budding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe COVID‐19 pandemic severely disrupted healthcare delivery worldwide, including cytopathology services essential for timely diagnosis and management of malignancies. This study evaluated the pandemic's impact on cytopathology specimen trends at the National Health Laboratory Service (NHLS) Universitas Academic Hospital laboratory in central South Africa.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on adult non‐gynaecological cytology specimens from March 2018 to April 2024, divided into pre‐COVID‐19, COVID‐19, and post‐COVID‐19 eras. Specimens were categorised into fine needle aspirates (FNAs) and other non‐gynaecological specimens, and stratified by anatomical site and diagnosis (benign, malignant, and suspicious for malignancy). Unsatisfactory, paediatric, and inadequate samples were excluded. Time series analysis was used for comparisons.ResultsSpecimen volumes declined significantly during the COVID‐19 era (p < 0.001) and did not recover in the post‐pandemic era. Although the proportion of FNAs remained consistent, specific trends were observed, including a marked decline in breast FNAs and benign diagnoses, with a concurrent increase in malignant and cases that were suspicious for malignancy. Respiratory tract FNAs increased during the COVID‐19 era, reflecting a shift in clinical priorities.ConclusionCytopathology services in central South Africa experienced sustained reductions in the volume of specimens received during and after COVID‐19. Increased malignant diagnoses may reflect delayed presentations and diagnostic backlogs created by the lockdown periods. These findings highlight the importance of resilient diagnostic services during health crises, as well as the importance of relying on more cost‐effective and faster diagnostic tools, such as cytopathology.

Introduction

The effect of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2) has been far‐reaching. SARS‐CoV2 is the causative agent of COVID‐19, a disease that has affected healthcare facilities globally [1]. The first case of COVID‐19 was reported in late December 2019 in China and the disease spread rapidly on an international scale [2]. COVID‐19 occurred in multiple waves and, as a result, many lockdowns of varying intensity were implemented both in South Africa and internationally [3, 4]. The healthcare sector had been severely affected by COVID‐19, with the effects of the pandemic being on‐going and extending beyond the national lockdown.

Cytopathology is an essential service that has been adversely affected by COVID‐19. Lockdown restrictions minimised the availability of this service. It is therefore important to ascertain to what extent COVID‐19 affected cytopathology services in central South Africa to ensure that, should future lockdowns be imposed, this important service is not disrupted [5, 6].

Lockdown Levels

During the initial lockdown period (26 March 2020 to 30 April 2020), severe restrictions were imposed on the movement of people and goods [2]. The initial lockdown (national state of disaster alert level 5) had the most devastating effects on the South African health sector [2, 3] and affected health‐seeking behaviours due to decreased availability and access to healthcare services [8, 9]. Healthcare was restricted to emergency services only, with many elective services suspended and/or restricted [2]. Cytopathology was one of the healthcare services adversely affected by the COVID‐19 pandemic.

Cytopathology in the South African Context

Although some healthcare professionals mistakenly regard cytopathology as a non‐essential service, it is an important field in the diagnosis and management of patients with malignancies and tumours [10, 11]. The discipline of cytopathology has advanced considerably in recent years and is rapidly evolving [12, 13]. Cytopathology currently plays an integral role in the diagnosis and management of tumours in almost all organ systems. Some reasons why cytopathology is an appealing diagnostic investigation include its cost‐effectiveness, rapid turnaround time, and minimally invasive nature [11].

Cytological specimens are broadly categorised as gynaecological or non‐gynaecological. Non‐gynaecological specimens can be obtained either as fine needle aspirations (FNAs) or exfoliative cytology. Cytological specimens are useful as cells can be coagulated to create cell blocks on which ancillary testing can be performed. Immunohistochemical, histochemical, and molecular testing are some of the ancillary tests currently being performed on cytological material preserved in cell blocks [11, 13]. Not only is cytopathology key to the diagnosis of malignancy, it has also been used extensively in the diagnosis of infectious diseases [11]. When used appropriately, cytopathology is a valuable tool for diagnosing disease, guiding treatment and prognosis, and its role in healthcare is becoming more prominent.

The cytopathology laboratory in the Department of Anatomical Pathology of the National Health Laboratory Service (NHLS), Universitas Academic Hospital, in Bloemfontein, South Africa receives approximately 4700 FNAs and other non‐gynaecological specimens per year from the Free State, North West and parts of the Northern Cape provinces. Each specimen is screened primarily by a medical technologist and then checked by a second medical technologist. All FNAs are then investigated by a consultant anatomical pathologist. All other non‐gynaecological specimens showing malignancy are also reviewed by a consultant anatomical pathologist prior to being signed out and findings reported to the requesting physician.

Our study aimed to assess the effect of COVID‐19 on specimens received at the cytopathology laboratory to determine whether a significant difference occurred between pre‐COVID‐19, COVID‐19, and post‐COVID‐19 eras in specimen volumes received at the laboratory. We also investigated how the types of specimens and their diagnoses differed during the COVID‐19 eras.

Methods

Study Design

A retrospective, observational study was conducted by analysing a 6‐year database of monthly statistical reports at the cytopathology laboratory. The volume of FNAs and other non‐gynaecological specimens, as well as the diagnoses of these specimens, were extracted from this database. The data were used to describe the effects of COVID‐19 on the volume of FNAs and other non‐gynaecological specimens received, the types of specimens received during the pre‐COVID‐19, COVID‐19, and post‐COVID‐19 eras, and the effect COVID‐19 had on the diagnoses of FNAs and other non‐gynaecological specimens received for investigation.

The rationale behind the dates for these eras was that the COVID‐19 national state of disaster in South Africa extended over a period of 2 years [2]. Therefore, for an accurate comparison, 2 years before and 2 years after the national state of disaster in South Africa were selected for our study.

Inclusion Criteria

All non‐gynaecological cytopathology specimens received at the laboratory from 1 March 2018 until 30 April 2024 were included in this study. These specimens represented all FNAs (lymph node, breast, thyroid, respiratory tract, salivary gland, and others) and all other non‐gynaecological specimens (serous fluids, cerebrospinal fluid, sputum, urine, bronchial alveolar washings/brushings, and others).

Data Collection and Statistical Analysis

Data were retrieved as an Excel (Microsoft Corporation; Redmond, WA, USA) document from TrackCare, the electronic laboratory records and reporting system for all FNAs and other non‐gynaecological specimens received at the cytopathology laboratory. To ensure patient confidentiality, a unique study number was allocated to each specimen and stored on a pseudo‐anonymisation list. The study numbers were only accessible to the principal investigator and were not to be disclosed to third parties. Data collection and analysis were performed using the unique study number to protect patients' identities.

The Excel spreadsheet was submitted to a biostatistician for data analysis once all data meeting the exclusion criteria were removed. The data were analysed using SAS Version 9.2 (SAS Inc.; Cary, NC, USA). Two main categories, FNAs and other non‐gynaecological specimens, represented the samples investigated. The two main groups of data were further subdivided into the six most common specimen types received for that specific group. For each group, the remaining specimen types were all grouped under “other”. This was done for each of the designated COVID‐19 eras. The “FNA” group as well as the “other non‐gynaecological specimen” group were expressed as a percentage of the total number of specimens received per era. These percentages were then compared using time series analysis. Every diagnosis in the data was divided into one of three groups: “benign”, “malignant” and “suspicious for malignancy”. These findings were expressed as a percentage of the total number of specimens received for each era and compared for each era using time series analysis.

Results

Over the 6‐year period, a total of 28,049 FNAs and other non‐gynaecological specimens were received. As illustrated in Figure 2, the total number of specimens received by the laboratory declined by approximately one third (n = 3941; 32.7%) between pre‐COVID‐19 and the COVID‐19 era. A further decline, although nearly negligible (n = 180; 2.2%), occurred in the post‐COVID‐19 era. The overall decline was statistically significant, with p < 0.001.

The majority of specimens received during all three COVID‐19 eras were of a non‐gynaecological nature. In total, 7455 (26.6%) specimens were FNAs, while the remaining 20,594 (73.4%) were other non‐gynaecological specimens. Figure 3 represents the distribution of FNAs and non‐gynaecological specimens over the COVID‐19 eras expressed as a percentage of the total. Non‐gynaecological specimens increased from 70.0% to 78.0%, compared to FNAs showing a decrease from 30.0% to 22.0% between the pre‐ and post‐COVID‐19 eras.

Discussion

The COVID‐19 pandemic had a far‐reaching and long‐lasting global impact on healthcare services. Internationally, a pandemic of this extent had not been experienced in decades. It is thus important to study the impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic on our healthcare systems in order to prevent such devastating consequences in the future. Across the world, a decrease in the number of cytology specimens during and shortly after COVID‐19 has been reported.

Volume of Specimens Received During the COVID‐19 Eras

The study revealed a significant decline in the number of specimens received at the cytopathology laboratory during the COVID‐19 era, which appears to not have recovered during the post‐COVID‐19 era. This decrease in specimen volume during the COVID‐19 era was echoed in many other international studies [4, 14, 15, 16]. The reasons for this trend can be multifactorial, including clinics and hospitals not having cytology services available, as well as the fear patients had of these institutions as high‐risk areas for contracting COVID‐19. Furthermore, any pre‐existing clinic or hospital appointments for cytological testing that were made pre‐COVID‐19 would have been lost to follow‐up during the COVID‐19 lockdown. Due to the COVID‐19 lockdown, most, if not all, cytological programmes and services offered by hospitals and clinics were halted. Limited access to public transport during the initial lockdown would have affected patients' ability to access health institutions where cytological services were available.

The fact that the number of specimens received by the cytopathology laboratory did not recover to pre‐COVID‐19 levels may be due to patients' persistent fear of contracting COVID‐19 from health institutions. The major impact that COVID‐19 had on many patients' finances meant that they might not have been able to afford transport to travel to these institutions to access cytology services. The anxiety caused by the time lost to follow‐up might have allowed relatively treatable conditions to have become more serious or untreatable, which also could have contributed to the low post‐COVID‐19 specimen volumes.

The drastic decrease in the volume of cytological specimens received was noted especially during the initial lockdown period of COVID‐19, and for much of the period thereafter [5, 17]. Vigliar et al. [5], van Not et al. [17] and Tetteh et al. [9] shared similar opinions as to why this may have occurred. Firstly, the significant decline in the availability of outpatient care, as well as the postponement of screening and diagnostic programmes, were considerable contributors [9]. Additionally, some laboratories had concerns regarding the presence of the SARS‐CoV‐2 in the specimens themselves. This led to the sifting of specimens when received by the laboratory. Specimens from either suspected or confirmed COVID‐19‐positive patients were kept separate from other specimens and prepared in a dedicated area with more precautions taken to prevent infecting staff working with these specimens. Furthermore, triage of specimens was initiated and certain specimen types took preference over others; for example, specimens from known oncology patients or patients with a strong suspicion of malignancy took precedence [9]. Downscaling of oncological care in general was implemented, with oncology patients actively discouraged by their healthcare providers from seeking healthcare assistance in hospitals in fear of contracting COVID‐19 from exposure to the hospital environment [18].

Awel et al. suggested that patients suffering from chronic conditions and comorbidities should avoid seeking medical care during the COVID‐19 era [19]. Patients with malignancies were included in this group of chronically ill patients [19]. The reason for this behaviour could have been concern that healthcare‐providing institutions might have had a high concentration of COVID‐19‐positive patients seeking healthcare [18]. It was also argued that patients avoided healthcare as it was assumed that these facilities would be closed due to the lockdown [18]. A less severe decline in the volume of cytological specimens received at laboratories was observed with every subsequent COVID‐19 lockdown level, although it has not recovered back to pre‐COVID‐19 levels [5]. A similar trend was noted in all oncological pre‐diagnostic areas, for example, radiological services [6].

The findings of this study, with regard to specimen volumes, are congruent with similar international studies conducted [3, 11], as well as a local study undertaken in the Western Cape that investigated the volume of histological specimens [20]. However, a number of studies predicted a rebound in the number of cytology specimens in the near future, and that cytology laboratories need to be prepared to manage this increase in volume [14, 21].

Types of Specimens Received During COVID‐19 Eras

The percentage of FNAs and other non‐gynaecological specimens received by the cytopathology laboratory remained relatively unchanged over the three COVID‐19 eras, as shown in Figure 2. There was a marked decrease in the number of breast FNAs received during the COVID‐19 era, with this trend continuing into the post‐COVID‐19 era. This observation could possibly be attributed to the fact that the large breast cancer screening programmes and awareness drives that were in place before COVID‐19 were halted completely during the COVID‐19 era. These activities have yet to recover since their re‐instatement in the post‐COVID‐19 era.

It is noteworthy that respiratory tract FNAs were the only type of FNA that increased during the COVID‐19 era. This could be due to the fact that COVID‐19 is a respiratory disease and therefore, people took action and sought healthcare services more rapidly for any respiratory‐related symptoms.

As shown in Table 2, serous fluids represented the majority of specimens across all three eras. It was surprising that sputum specimens increased by only 1% from pre‐COVID‐19 to during the COVID‐19 era. It would be expected that due to the possible increase in the number of patients seeking healthcare for respiratory symptoms, the volume of sputum specimens received would show a more substantial increase. The number of serous fluids, cerebrospinal fluid, sputum, bronchial washings/brushings, and urine specimens all remained fairly stable across the COVID‐19 eras.

Type of Diagnoses (Malignant, Benign and Suspicious for Malignancy)

The number of benign cases diagnosed decreased in the post‐COVID‐19 era. The percentage of “malignant” diagnoses remained stable across the three eras, while “suspicious for malignancy” cases diagnosed increased with every consecutive era from 0.2% to 1.9% of the total specimens. It is possible that the delay in receiving timeous diagnostic services in the form of cytology led to pre‐malignant lesions or malignancies at an earlier, more treatable stage, progressing to malignant or metastatic lesions, respectively.

Another factor that might have impacted the types of diagnoses was that FNAs were performed by less experienced clinicians during the COVID‐19 era, which could implicate that malignant lesions might have been missed or not sampled adequately. Health institutions being back to normal operations after the COVID‐19 era, and clinicians with more experience in FNAs once again performing these procedures might have contributed to the increase in “suspicious for malignancy” cases. The financial effects of COVD‐19 on most of the patient population could imply that cytological services were obtained only when the pathology became severe to the extent of causing discomfort, or affected the patients' ability to function normally or contribute within their household.

Although there was a decline in the volume of cytological specimens received at cytopathology laboratories, the proportion of malignant cases diagnosed was also higher [19, 22]. There seems to be consensus across literature that this was due primarily to the successful triaging of cases [15, 22]. However, this finding could also be due to a delay in seeking medical care by a large majority of the population [22]. Furthermore, a delay from diagnosis to initiation of treatment was also noted as a result of downscaling of oncological care [17].

The increase in “suspicious for malignancy” cases is concerning, and further studies need to be conducted to ascertain whether these numbers stabilise. This finding was also reported by international studies [15, 19, 22]. If these numbers fail to stabilise, given time to recover from the effects of COVID‐19, interventions need to be initiated to address this situation.

Limitations

The fact that some specimen request forms were inadequately completed by the clinician regarding the site of the specimen led to the high number of “other” types of specimens. Evaluating and comparing the adequacy rates of specimens received by the cytopathology laboratory over each era would have been a valuable addition to this study.

Recommendations

More effort should be made to implement more effective, more rapid, and more focused diagnostic and screening programmes due to the time lost during the COVID‐19 lockdown. It would also be beneficial to do further research investigating why cytology specimen values have not returned to pre‐pandemic numbers more than 2 years after the COVID‐19 national state of disaster had ended.

More research is required to determine the worrying finding of increased malignancy and suspicious for malignancy diagnoses made on cytology post‐COVID‐19. Education and training of cytology laboratory staff should be promoted, especially due to the loss of this vital tool during the COVID‐19 era [14, 16]. Virk et.al suggested using virtual images and training as an essential tool, especially during health crises such as the COVID‐19 pandemic [16].

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that COVID‐19 had an over‐reaching effect on the cytopathology laboratory, which continued beyond the national lockdown time frame. The cytopathology laboratory is, unfortunately, still in the shadows of COVID‐19. COVID‐19 altered patients' health‐seeking behaviour and healthcare practitioners' regular practice of sending specimens for cytopathological examination. There is hope, however, and it has been predicted that the volume of cytological specimens will revert back to pre‐COVID‐19 levels [14, 21].

Cytology is a fast and cost‐effective diagnostic tool and needs to be used more frequently in the resource‐limited health system in South Africa. Cytology has also been shown to be a valuable tool that could be of significant importance during current and future health crises [23].
