Unusual foreign body in the digestive tract: Endoscopic removal of a toothbrush in a pediatric patient

Introduction

Foreign body ingestion in pediatrics is one of the most frequent emergencies [1]. The most commonly found foreign bodies worldwide are coins, followed by magnets, button batteries, pins, and organic foreign bodies [2,3]. To date, the first reported case of toothbrush ingestion was in 1882, and of all the cases reported in subsequent years, none involved spontaneous expulsion [4]. Patients with mental illnesses or eating disorders are the most prone to the accidental ingestion of unusual objects. In these patients, ingestion may be inadvertent and therefore untreated, increasing the risk of complications [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Diagnosis can be made through medical history and radiological tests, which allow detection of the location, size, shape, and quantity of foreign bodies. Although sometimes they may not be visible as they appear as radiolucent images [3]. While endoscopy is a safe and effective technique for foreign body removal, in some cases it is not the first option [2]. We report the first case in our health unit of a 13-year-old male patient who ingested a toothbrush, which was successfully removed via upper gastrointestinal endoscopy.

Patient information

A 13-year-old patient was admitted to the emergency department with mild abdominal pain and nausea, without vomiting, after ingesting a toothbrush 3 hours prior to admission. On physical examination, his heart rate was 97 beats/minute, temperature 35.2°C, blood pressure 100/58 mmHg, and oxygen saturation 98%. His abdomen was distended, nontender, without any palpable masses, and slightly tender to palpation in the left hypochondrium. During re-interviewing, the patient was uncommunicative and easily choked. He reported having an eating disorder for the past 4 months, as he had previously induced vomiting with his toothbrush.

Clinical findings

An abdominal X-ray was performed in the upright and lateral views (Fig. 1), revealing a radiopaque image below the left costal margin, apparently located in the stomach, consistent with the bristles of the toothbrush. Since a specialist was unavailable that evening and the patient presented no abdominal alarm symptoms, the patient was admitted and kept NPO (nothing by mouth) until evaluation by gastroenterology the following morning.

Upright abdominal X-ray showing a foreign body.

Course of action

Given the patient's condition, the unusual nature of the object, and the absence of abdominal alarm symptoms, the patient was evaluated by gastroenterology, which decided to perform an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy with the possibility of conversion to an exploratory laparotomy.

Diagnosis and evaluation

An upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was initially performed since the patient did not present with any alarming abdominal symptoms and was asymptomatic at the time of reassessment. The findings revealed the toothbrush handle in the lower third of the esophagus, extending into the gastric body, with the head embedded in the gastric mucosa. This was due to its rigid shape, which hindered its complete passage into the stomach, and because it was a plastic object and appeared radiolucent on the radiograph, its exact location could not be determined (Fig. 2). A polypectomy snare was introduced, as its large surface area for grasping objects allowed it to completely encircle the toothbrush. After 2 attempts, the handle was successfully captured, allowing its extraction into the mouth. Due to the rigidity of the foreign body, it was not completely removed with the polypectomy snare. Since it was located in the pharynx, a direct laryngoscopy was performed, and the brush was extracted without complications using Magyll forceps (Fig. 3). The stomach was examined, revealing confluent erosions at the brush insertion site and in the upper and middle thirds of the esophagus.

Foreign body (toothbrush) 19 cm long.

Follow-up and results

The patient remained hospitalized for 3 days after the procedure. During this period, he was evaluated by Psychiatry and Psychology due to a history of induced vomiting, which revealed a depressive background of several years’ duration. Thanks to the endoscopic removal of the toothbrush, the patient experienced minimal trauma, low economic cost, and had a good postoperative recovery, being discharged without complications.

Discussion

Foreign body ingestion in pediatrics is one of the most frequent emergencies. It occurs most often in children under 3 years of age, with a variable predominance, although it is more common in boys. Most foreign bodies are eliminated spontaneously in the stool, but in some cases, surgical intervention is necessary, either due to digestive symptoms or to treat complications arising from ingestion [1].

The most common foreign bodies worldwide are coins, followed by magnets, button batteries, pins, and organic foreign bodies. In our case, the ingested foreign body was a 19 cm toothbrush, which is extremely rare, with only about 40 cases reported worldwide. There have been other reported unusual cases of foreign body ingestion, such as the ingestion of leeches, which can cause airway obstruction. This can be fatal, and early diagnosis and timely treatment are crucial, predominantly achieved through direct laryngoscopy for visualization of these parasites. These cases are more frequent in tropical and subtropical climates, meaning they are more commonly observed in rural areas of Africa, Asia, and South America. Children living in these regions are particularly susceptible, largely due to the regular consumption of unfiltered water [8].

While most ingested foreign bodies pass through the gastrointestinal tract without complications (80%-90%), some become lodged in points of natural narrowing (upper, middle, and lower esophagus, pylorus, ileocecal valve, and rectosigmoid junction), as occurred in our patient, in whom the toothbrush became lodged in the esophagogastric junction [2,3]. Diagnosis is based on the clinical history, physical examination, and radiological tests, which allow for the detection of the location, size, shape, and number of foreign bodies. These may appear as radiopaque images. However, in some cases (35%-40%), they may not be visible, as in our case. Since it was a toothbrush, its exact position could not be visualized, as only the bristles were visible in the abdominal radiograph [3].

Patients with mental illness or eating disorders, as in the case presented, are more prone to ingesting unusual objects, such as a toothbrush. In these patients, ingestion may go unnoticed and therefore untreated, increasing the risk of complications such as ulcers and perforations. Consultation with specialists to reduce this risk, as in the case of our patient, is essential to prevent further ingestion. Additionally, follow-up after the endoscopic surgical procedure is necessary to treat the underlying pathology, which should be done in conjunction with psychiatry and psychology to address it pharmacologically or behaviorally [[4], [5], [6], [7]].

To date, the first reported case of toothbrush ingestion dates back to 1882, and of all reported cases, none involved spontaneous expulsion, as occurred in our clinical case. Due to the curvature of the duodenum and the straightness of the object, it remained at the esophagogastric junction, which can increase the possibility of complications [4].

Most reported cases were resolved by laparotomy due to complications. One case involved colonic perforation, and another, gastric perforation, resulted in death. This occurred in 1889 [6]. While endoscopy is a safe and effective technique for foreign body removal, in some cases, especially when the foreign body has traveled beyond the duodenum or cannot be removed using this technique due to complications, it is not the first option. When endoscopy is used, it is performed under general anesthesia, with the patient intubated to protect the airway, as in our case. The foreign body is removed using instruments, in this case a polypectomy snare, with the assistance of direct laryngoscopy and a Magyll forceps [2].

Patient perspective

In the case presented, the patient had a history of self-induced vomiting with a toothbrush and, therefore, went to the emergency department. An abdominal X-ray was performed, which revealed the foreign body apparently in the stomach. As in most cases described in the literature, the toothbrush did not reach the duodenum and, therefore, was not spontaneously expelled in the stool. Endoscopic extraction was performed, which contributed to the patient experiencing no complications and resuming oral intake with good tolerance. The patient was discharged without incident.

Conclusion

Toothbrush ingestion is infrequent, and spontaneous elimination through the gastrointestinal tract is unlikely. Therefore, preventing the ingestion of unusual foreign bodies in pediatric patients is essential to reducing the incidence of these events. Mental health assessment and management in pediatrics are especially relevant when a behavioral disorder or disturbance is present, as it may not be an isolated accidental event but rather the manifestation of repetitive behaviors, self-harm, and difficulties in risk perception. A multidisciplinary approach allows for addressing the underlying causes. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy is safe and effective for removing foreign bodies lodged in the upper gastrointestinal tract, and early intervention can prevent complications.
