 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report documents the microstructural characterization and hardness testing of a post-irradiation
annealed high-fluence baffle-former bolt (BFB) harvested from a commercial pressurized water reactor
(PWR) after nearly four decades of service, as well as development of grips to enable future in-situ
mechanical testing in NSUF facilities, particularly through access to the High Energy X-Ray Microscopy
(HEXM) beamline at the Advanced Photon Source (APS) provided by the NSUF facility, the Activated
Materials Laboratory (AML). Using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and electron backscatter
diffraction (EBSD) and hardness testing, the study assessed the microstructures, misorientation
characteristics, and hardness after annealing at temperatures up to 1000 °C. The results revealed significant
differences in the as-irradiated condition between the sections of the bolt exposed to 15-29 dpa, including
differences in recrystallization extent and deformation structures, and oxidation morphology. After
annealing, the extent of misorientation evolved with temperature with peaks at 550 °C and 1000 °C. This
evolution coincided with the large drop of hardness above 550 °C and a slight uptick in hardness at 1000 °C.
These findings present intriguing behavior for recovery of radiation damage in nuclear reactor materials
and present opportunities for future work at NSUF facilities. Ultimately, the work sets the baseline for
improved mechanistic understanding and predictive capability regarding the long-term performance of
reactor core internal components and thermal aging recovery of radiation-induced degradation.

 1. INTRODUCTION
This Nuclear Science User Facilities (NSUF) project seeks to characterize the microstructure and
degradation mechanisms of in-service baffle former bolts (BFBs) using capabilities only available at NSUF
facilities to develop a mechanistic understanding for strain localization and irradiation assisted stress
corrosion cracking (IASCC) in operation conditions. Core internal components in pressurized water
reactors like BFBs are exposed to many concurrent extreme environment conditions like radiation, elevated
temperature, mechanical stress, and corrosive primary coolant water. Understanding how microstructures
evolve under these conditions is paramount to probe the mechanisms for IASCC, radiation hardening, and
embrittlement and developing mitigation strategies for these in-service phenomena. The main thrust of this
project is to do a first of its kind experiment on highly radioactive material through access to the High
Energy X-Ray Microscopy (HEXM) beamline at the Advanced Photon Source (APS) at Argonne National
Laboratory (ANL) provided by the new Activated Materials Laboratory (AML) at ANL, an NSUF facility.
This report presents the characterization results using scanning electron microscopy (SEM)/electron
backscatter diffraction (EBSD) performed at the Low Activation Materials Development and Analysis
(LAMDA) lab at ORNL of the cut pieces of the BFBs after annealing at multiple elevated temperatures
between 400 and 1000 ℃. Annealing at these temperatures was done as results from annealing at ~1050 ℃
at the HEXM beamline at APS from previously characterized BFB material showed an unexpected powder
diffraction pattern indicative of a strongly refined grain structure. The results presented here provide initial
SEM/EBSD data of the altered microstructure after post-irradiation annealing to inform and compare with
the HEXM data collection at APS and the crystal plasticity finite element modeling being done as part of
this project. In addition, this report presents the grip design to enable in-situ mechanical testing of
miniaturized highly irradiated/ activated samples in the HEXM beamline at APS growing the NSUF
capabilities for future research projects.
2. MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1 Harvested baffle-former bolts, specimen cutting, and preparation
History of the harvesting of these bolts can be found in prior DOE reports [1-6]. The specimen machining
scheme and specimens’ IDs are shown in Figure 1. The two BFBs were in the reactor for about 40 years,
spanning over 35 cycles. The maximum dose (in the bolt head end or collar slice) was 41 displacements per
atom (dpa) for BFB #4412 and 29 dpa for BFB #4416. For each BFB, four bend-bar specimens and seven
thin-slice specimens were machined at the Irradiated Materials Examination and Testing (IMET) at ORNL.
The bend-bar specimens were used in the fracture toughness and fatigue crack growth rate studies [4],
whereas the thin-slice specimens were designated for microstructural analyses and advanced mechanical
testing like those described in the literature [3, 7-9]. The specimens presented in this report are from all
three sections of the #4416 BFB. The slices from each section were further cut down to ~2×1×1 mm pieces,
which were then annealed at temperatures of 400, 550, 700, 850, and 1000 °C for one hour followed by air
cooling. The annealed pieces were then mounted in conductive epoxy, ground and polished to a mirror
finish, and electropolished to produce an EBSD quality surface finish. In the previous report [10], annealing
at 1050 °C was meant to provide a comparable lattice and grain structure similar to an unirradiated reference
material, as there is unfortunately no reference material from the same heat for these bolts. However, that
annealing treatment resulted in a powder diffraction pattern in the HEXM beamline experiments, suggesting
a greatly refined grain structure. The BFB #4412 CS and #4416 CS sections that were annealed at 1050 °C
have been received back at ORNL from ANL but have not yet been removed from the Kapton and thus
have not been characterized yet.

The annealed samples of BFB #4416 were characterized by SEM-electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD)
using a Tescan Mira3 SEM equipped with an Oxford Instruments Symmetry S2 high speed EBSD detector.
The SEM/EBSD analysis and mapping were performed using the Oxford Instruments Aztec Crystal
software. The microstructure of polished cross-section in the bulk and near the oxide surface was
characterized by EBSD with inverse pole figure (IPF) maps, kernel average misorientation (KAM) maps,
and grain reference orientation deviation (GROD) maps. The latter two provide an estimate for the extent
of dislocations and strain within the material – specifically geometrically necessary dislocations.
After EBSD characterization, Vickers microhardness testing was performed on hot-mounted, polished
specimens using a Nanovea PB-1000 micro-Vickers tester. The system has a 20 N max load cell and Vickers
diamond pyramid indenter. Each day's session started with an indent on a calibration block to confirm the
instrument was reading within its expected range before proceeding with sample indents. Indentation used
a target load of 4.903 N (500 gf), load/unload rates of 9.806 N/min, and a 10 s dwell at peak load. Prior to
each 3×3 array, a single trial indent established the maximum indentation depth for that sample, which was
used to set the array spacing of 150 µm (usually > 10 × depth). Hardness (HV) was calculated from the
optically measured diagonal lengths of each indent.
3. RESULTS
3.1 Characterization of BFB #4416 after post-irradiation annealing
Figure 2 shows relatively low magnification images of the microstructure of the BFB #4416 bolt in all three
sections in the as-irradiated condition. Interestingly, the BS and MS sections have similar grain structures
consisting of equiaxed grains with a high density of annealing twins, while the CS section has a bimodal
grain structure distribution. As discussed in the previous report [10], this microstructure is characteristic of
partially recrystallized steels subjected to insufficient annealing time at elevated temperatures. The variation
in grain structure along the length of the same bolt suggests a significant gradient in thermal exposure not
only during manufacture but also potentially while in-service in the reactor. The estimated peak variation
during operation is about 50 °C (~290-340 °C) [12]; based on this, the grain variation is likely a result of
the fabrication process prior to insertion into the reactor.

 2

While the grain structure in the IPF maps in Figure 2 is similar between the BS and MS sections of the bolt,
the in-grain misorientation near grain boundaries is significantly higher in the BS section than in the MS
section as seen in the KAM maps. The misorientation is also significant in the CS section. The difference
in misorientation likely leads to a difference in response to post-irradiation annealing, particularly at the
highest annealing temperatures as evidenced in Figure 3 for the BS and MS sections after annealing at
1000 °C. The BS section has significant, though incomplete, grain refinement through grain fragmentation
of the parent grains, while the MS section has minimal grain refinement under the same conditions. This is
potentially a result of the difference in strain or misorientation values in the as-irradiated state.
Figure 4 presents higher magnification SEM/EBSD IPF and KAM maps of the BS section after annealing
at each temperature in this study. Based on the IPF maps, the microstructure does not qualitatively change
much with annealing up to 850 °C but does change significantly at 1000 °C. However, the KAM maps
show a different story with misorientation decreasing at 400 °C, increasing at 550 °C and 700, decreasing
again at 850 °C, and finally increasing at 1000 °C. This suggests changes within the material beyond the
grain size and morphology is changing within the material creating internal strains. These could include
Ni/Si-rich precipitate growth and dissolution; dislocation loop growth, climb, and annihilation; and gas-
filled cavity evolution. The evolution of these features occurs at different rates and likely depends on the
as-irradiated state. The annealing induced localized deformation, particularly observed at 1000 °C, may be
a result of gas-filled cavities growing and emitting dislocations [13, 14]. The localized deformation
channels are less apparent in the smaller fragmented grains, which suggests the localized deformation
induced by annealing leads to the grain refinement/ fragmentation at elevated temperatures instead of grain
growth or recrystallization. No phase transformations were observed in any of the samples after annealing.
Figure 5 presents higher magnification SEM/EBSD IPF and KAM maps of the MS section after annealing
at each temperature in this study. Based on the IPF maps, the microstructure does not appear to change
significantly at all regardless of annealing temperature up to 1000 °C. Like the BS section, misorientation
in the KAM maps seems to peak at 550 °C and then reduce again with increased temperature. Unlike the
BS section, it is difficult to differentiate the extent of misorientation at any of the higher temperatures. At
1000 °C, there is no obvious grain fragmentation across most of the sample; however, there are a couple of
regions where there are refined grains. Perhaps a longer annealing time or higher temperature could induce
the microstructure to behave like the BS section.
Figure 6 presents higher magnification SEM/EBSD IPF and KAM maps of the CS section in the as-
irradiated condition and after annealing at 400 °C. Mapping of the higher temperature anneals will be
conducted as part of ongoing future work in this study. However, with respect to the data collected to date,
annealing at 400 °C suggests that relief of internal misorientation and recrystallization occurred in this
section of the bolt. However, this is difficult to believe as the 400 °C annealing for only one hour typically
may only remove some dislocation loops but would not be enough for grain growth. Therefore, it is more
likely that there may be significant region-to-region variation as each cut piece is at least 2 mm away from
each other. This variation is likely related to the manufacturing processes performed in the 1960s and 1970s.
In addition to understanding the effects of annealing on microstructure, it is also important to understand
the effects of annealing on properties. The first property to be tested is hardness as it is a quick method to
gain insights of annealing on mechanical behavior. The Vickers’ hardness is plotted in Figure 7 as a function
of post-irradiation annealing temperature for each section of BFB #4416. The Vickers hardness for
unirradiated stainless steel is typically 150-220 HV depending on the heat treatment and cold work level.
The irradiated BFB #4416 shows significant hardening to 420-450 HV in the as-irradiated condition in all
three sections of the bolt, with CS > MS > BS. With annealing, the hardness is essentially retained up to an
annealing temperature of 550 °C with a slight reduction of ~4% in the BS section and ~12% in the MS
section. However, at 700 °C, the hardness drops significantly to the range of an unirradiated 316 stainless
steel. Most of the radiation defect recovery occurs in the 550-700 °C range, likely due to dissolution of
Ni/Si-rich precipitates and annihilation of dislocation loops. There is continued mild softening to 850°C to
a HV ~170-185 (the minimum in the dataset). Finally, there is a modest uptick at 1000°C (HV ~205-235)

3.2 Next steps for HEXM characterization and grip design for in-situ HEXM characterization
The CS section of both BFB #4412 and #4416 have been characterized at the HEXM beamline in the as-
irradiated condition with a focus on the surface oxide region but also including analysis hundreds of microns
from the surface to reflect the bulk of that region of the bolts. Thus, the next steps both within this project
and for future NSUF projects at the HEXM beamline for these materials are two-fold: 1) ex-situ
characterization of the other regions of the bolts as the structure and properties vary along the length of the
bolts or characterization of bolts/ PWR internal components with different compositions, such as 304
stainless steel baffle plates or 347 stainless steel BFBs; and 2) in-situ testing in the HEXM beamline to
include mechanical testing and annealing experiments on highly irradiated materials such as these BFBs.
For the first part, new samples from different sections of the BFBs will be shipped to the AML before the
end of FY27 Q1. For the second part, small-scale specimen geometries [15] (Figure 8) and grips (Figure 9)
have been designed for doing in-situ mechanical testing experiments on highly irradiated materials. The
sub-scale tensile specimens, such as the SS-Teeny (SST), enable the testing of bulk-like behavior while
reducing sample dimensions and thus radiation dose, and thus expanding the in-situ experimentation
capabilities such as at the HEXM beamline or in SEMs. It also increases the number of tensile specimens
and conditions that can be tested for a finite amount of valuable, irradiated material available to researchers.
This geometry has been successfully used in SEMs and will be used at the HEXM beamline at ANL and at
the Spallation Neutron Source at ORNL for in-situ neutron diffraction experiments (outside the scope of
this work). The grips in Figure 9 have been specifically designed for the HEXM beamline with a large open
angle to maximize the amount of diffracted X-rays that can be captured. This angle is limited by the strength
of the grip material to hold the shoulder-loaded specimen. The grips will be fabricated for use at the HEXM
beamline at ANL for use in FY27 Q1.

4. SUMMARY AND FUTURE WORK
This study provides SEM/EBSD microstructure characterization and Vickers hardness testing of post-
irradiation annealing effects of high-fluence BFBs harvested from a commercial PWR after ~40 years of
service, as well as next steps for characterization of these irradiated materials at the HEXM beamline at
ANL. The SEM/EBSD results showed that the as-irradiated microstructure had large region-to-region
variability in both grain size but also in misorientation within the grains, which may be one of the reasons
there is strong variability in post-irradiation annealing microstructure, particularly at the highest annealing
temperatures. Annealing at 1000 °C induced grain fragmentation in the thread section (BS) of the bolt which
was not observed in the mid-shank section (MS). However, this did not coincide with a large variation in
hardness as a function of annealing temperature between the two sections. There was a large radiation
hardening recovery after annealing above 550 °C, which was followed by an uptick in hardness at 1000 °C.
The mechanisms for this behavior are not entirely known but is likely due to the dissolution of Ni/Si-rich
precipitates followed by evolution of hydrogen and/or helium at elevated temperatures driving localized
deformation and subsequent grain fragmentation.
New in-situ mechanical testing grips were also designed to expand NSUF capabilities at the HEXM
beamline at ANL. These new grips will enable the testing of sub-size specimens in-situ while also reducing
the overall dose of the sample. This reduction in sample size benefits the safety of the operators and
transportation personnel but also enables the testing of more samples from limited irradiated material.
The future work for this project includes:
 • Complete SEM/EBSD and Vickers hardness testing of BFB #4416 CS section after post-irradiation
 annealing at all the temperatures in this report.
 • Ship BFB samples cut into SST geometry to the AML at ANL for the last HEXM beamline time
 allocated for this project in FY27
 • Fabricate grips for SST geometry for use at the HEXM beamline at ANL.
