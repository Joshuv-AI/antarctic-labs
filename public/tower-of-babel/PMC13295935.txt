Culturally and regionally relevant physical activity for public health promotion in a global context: nature of the evidence and definition development from a narrative review
Authors: Tetine L Sentell, Catherine M Pirkle, Leah Grout, Alban Ylli, Daniel P Heil, Simone Maria Schmid, Julia Finn, Saionara M A Câmara, Esme Yokooji, Mele A Look
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA strong and growing evidence base across multiple dimensions of health and wellness supports the value of physical activity with cultural or regional relevance, such as ethnic dance and traditional games. Greater inclusion of culturally and regionally relevant physical activity (CURE-PA) into health promotion is concordant with international public health goals, yet CURE-PAs have traditionally been underused in health and physical activity promotion especially at the population level. The specificity of deep community and cultural context of these activities, an important strength for public health promotion, can obscure communalities in approaches, synergies and outcomes across locations, programmes and populations. Following a narrative review method by a multinational expert team as preparation for the development of formal search protocols in this vast, multidisciplinary literature, this paper: (1) provides a preliminary assessment of the potential size and nature of relevant research, (2) develops a definition of CURE-PA and (3) delineates relevant characteristics supporting inclusion of CURE-PA in population level health promotion. Numerous articles and scientific disciplines with vital evidence on CURE-PA were identified, including nature and health, sport(s), dance, Indigenous health, physical literacy, sociology, political science, youth leadership development, resilience models, community design, ecology, leisure studies, cultural revitalisation and decolonialisation in sports. In formal searches, 100 000s of relevant articles would be found, highlighting the importance of clarifying CURE-PA boundaries, definitions and conceptual models in advance of formal search protocols. A synthesising literature about CURE-PA from a global health promotion perspective can guide future work and support engagement, planning, study, uptake, funding and implementation of CURE-PA in population-level public health. Greater inclusion of CURE-PA in health promotion can help to address physical inactivity as well as chronic disease, mental health concerns, loneliness and other global public health challenges, building from community strengths, preferences and knowledge systems.

Background

Public health promotion that supports, engages and leverages community strengths and assets has traditionally been underused in global health promotion, which often takes a deficit focused perspective. The importance of public health promotion approaches that prioritise cultural strengths and community values is now increasingly recognised as critical by national and international organisations to achieve improved health outcomes as well as health equity.1 2 In a recent report, the WHO noted both the importance of cultural insights for better health and the fact that this promising area is underexplored and underused globally.3

Culturally and regionally relevant physical activity (CURE-PA), such as ethnic dance or traditional games, has a strong and growing evidence base to support its value across multiple dimensions of health and wellness. CURE-PA has the capacity to address physical inactivity, which impacts an estimated 80% of adolescents and 27% of adults globally.4 Physical inactivity is a critical public health problem with extensive health, economic, environmental and social consequences.5 An estimated 6–10% of worldwide deaths from non-communicable diseases can be attributed to insufficient physical activity (PA).6 The benefits of PA are well established and include not only a reduced risk of non-communicable diseases and cancer, but also lower levels of stress and anxiety, delayed onset of dementia, maintenance of a healthy weight and increased quality of life.5

CURE-PA also shows promise in addressing other aspects of well-being, including health equity.7 Across global settings, populations who experience health and social inequities, including higher non-communicable disease burdens, are often less likely to be physically active.8 This is particularly true when the focus is on leisure-time PA, which is the PA shown to have the most health benefits.9 It is critical to include an equity frame when considering health promotion to address PA and chronic disease risk factors. The benefits of culturally relevant interventions can also reach beyond physical health improvements to the sociocultural aspects that are associated with collective well-being.10

Despite the relevance for health equity and the strong and growing evidence base, CURE-PAs are infrequently incorporated in global health promotion efforts, especially at the population level. The full scale of the existing evidence to support these practices in health promotion can be hidden by the local specificity of these activities as well as the scope and interdisciplinary diversity of connected research and practice. The deep engagement with cultural/community context, including appropriate terms for practices in Indigenous and local languages, are important strengths of CURE-PAs. Yet given this specific context, scientific literature searches with traditional methods may not readily identify commonalities in approaches, values and outcomes across locations, programmes and populations.

Additionally, activities and practices that could be considered CURE-PA have often been marginalised under colonialist and other oppressive histories and may thus be left invisible to many, especially from a national or global perspective.11 PA health promotion activities often focus broadly on getting people to walk or bike more or engage in resistance training as healthful practices. When there is encouragement of sports, these tend to ultimately descend from European colonisation, such as efforts to engage adolescents in soccer/football.11 Traditional practices may be valued as heritage activities by governmental and institutional systems, but not viewed as options for inclusion in efforts to promote ‘sport’ or ‘PA’ broadly for public health practice or funding.

There are significant local, regional and community efforts to challenge these circumstances and narratives, many notably from Indigenous perspectives and collaboratives.12 Still, there are few international PA campaigns to generally promote activities relevant to specific cultural and community contexts nor to support precolonial leisure-time PAs, many of which are widely practiced (eg, hula, Taekwondo). In fact, the prevalence of engagement in CURE-PAs may also be deeply undercounted in both specific locations and generally as, due to their local specificity, they are often not included in harmonised public health systems surveillance tools that provide background data to demonstrate existing engagement, track progress and support implementation at scale of PA initiatives across the multiple relevant settings.7 CURE-PA practices may seem historical rather than current in public health surveillance data, even when they are actively engaged in by many.

Thus, despite extensive and innovative research demonstrating the importance of this work using multiple methods and across many communities globally,12 the full utility, efficacy and value of CURE-PA is under-recognised. Additionally, the full scale of the interest and engagement in these activities can be hidden. If the full scope of the connected evidence base about this asset-based work is not visible to those in health promotion research and practice, it limits the ability to successfully advocate for these activities in population level health promotion, to justify funding for these activities to be implemented in community and clinical settings and at scale, and to consider extensions to this work and synergies across communities. Such a gap may also result in an underestimation of PA in certain groups and contribute to misdirected efforts to reduce chronic disease in these populations that focus on PA promotion rather than more fundamental causes.

The goal of this paper is to support health promotion and population-level public health efforts of engagement, planning, uptake and study of CURE-PA by collecting and connecting this evidence base from a global perspective.

Methods

This paper was developed over a multiyear process by a multinational expert team and was designed to (1) provide a preliminary assessment of the potential size and nature of relevant research to inform a future review and other activities, (2) develop a definition of CURE-PA and (3) delineate relevant characteristics supporting inclusion of CURE-PA in population level health promotion.

Authorship team

Our authorship team includes academic, applied and cultural expertise from around the globe. This includes diversity by location of residence across four WHO regions (past and current; including Albania, Brazil, Bahamas, Germany, New Zealand, Spain, Switzerland, USA, West Africa and South Africa); academic expertise across disciplines (including political science; maternal and child health; life course epidemiology; environmental health; complementary and integrative health; clinical medicine; kinesiology; exercise science; occupational therapy; ethnic studies; intervention research; community engagement and Indigenous health); professional experiences (including Fulbright Scholar; service on WHO committees; state-level, regional-level and national-level public health policy development, implementation and evaluation; school health programming and physical education development) and deep engagement in practices that could be considered CURE-PA (including hula and other dances, ecological restoration, pilgrimage, paddling, surfing and distance running). Authors identify across many identities including Indigenous scholar, immigrant, Asian American, ethnic minority, public health graduate student, college athlete, recent postdoctoral fellow, senior-level researcher and national public health leader.

Narrative review methods

Given the diverse range of sources, disciplines, knowledge-bases that needed to be included into this work, we followed a phased, narrative review13 methodology. This narrative review assesses the potential size and nature of the evidence base for this work, and draws on the expertise of our international authors around the utility of this work for public health promotion specifically.13 This work was not designed as a systematic literature review, but as a type of literature synthesis to provide evidence, frameworks and definitions to inform a future formal literature review that will synthesise more details about study methods, locations, intervention types, outcomes and measures. Work was conducted in iterative phases over several years, including feedback from multiple sources and communities of practice. Detailed information about the process and phases can be found in the online supplemental file 1.

This groundwork follows best-practices for the initial phase of a scoping review in which the preliminary search confirms the breadth and scope of a review, information about what exists already, relevant keywords, Medical Subject Headings, databases and inclusion and exclusion criteria as well as the relevant members of a suitably expert interdisciplinary team.14 These methods also align with the ‘scoping the literature phase’ of the meta-narrative review process of the RAMSES (Realist and Meta-narrative Evidence Syntheses: Evolving Standard) protocols.15

In all phases, we searched PubMed for relevant studies written in English with no restrictions on date of publication using search terms we felt would be relevant to this topic. Initial article searches included “exercise” or “physical activity” and at least one of the following terms: “culturally relevant”, “traditional”, “folk”, “Indigenous” or “ethnic.” We then searched for specific activities known to be culturally relevant and include PA, including the following: “ethnic dance”, “hula”, “hunting”, “fishing”, “spearfishing”, “surfing” and “canoeing.” Phase 2 of the process also included a preliminary search of grey and popular literature using similar methods because important health promotion evidence in general, and specifically in these types of practices, would not always appear in peer-reviewed literature. Google was searched using the same terms as above and following potentially relevant links. Additional articles and resources, including web resources, were identified through backward citation searching and updated searches. Zotero was used to manage references, and Covidence was used to manage the narrative review extraction. Article titles and abstracts were screened by one investigator to determine relevance to the review, as part of our process has included piloting extraction items and methods for future formal searches. We included journal articles of any type (eg, think piece, empirical, commentary). We did not appraise articles for quality. More details about the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the search are provided in online supplemental file 2.

From this synthesis of the literature and drawing from their cross-national and interdisciplinary expertise, our multinational team of experts then developed a consensus definition for ‘culturally or regionally relevant physical activities’ through an iterative process involving several key steps: (1) identification of the broader concept of interest; (2) conceptualisation of a preliminary definition; (3) refinement of the definition based on literature review, study team expertise and feedback; and (4) operationalising the definition to test clarity, applicability and utility in different scenarios. Examples to showcase the boundaries of what we considered CURE-PA can be found in online supplemental file 2. Members of the authorship team came to consensus for this article’s contents across multiple meetings in person and virtually across a 4-year period as described in more detail in online supplemental file 1.

As this study is a synthesis of previously published studies it did not include human subjects and did not require institutional board review.

Patient and public involvement

This project has been deeply informed by ongoing conversations and community input across settings in Hawai‘i and globally over many years. The project has a specific and explicit goal of dissemination generally and specifically intends this preliminary literature synthesis as one mechanism to share the value of—and ongoing activities in—CURE-PA across the globe for community engagement and empowerment. Events in which feedback was given include formal presentation to community as well as scientific audiences, steering committee consultations and informal conversations at events. Examples of some feedback we have received and how this was relevant to, and included in, the process phases can be found in online supplemental file 1.

Results

Size and nature of the evidence base

This preliminary narrative review made clear that the breadth of the evidence is large, with numerous articles identified across a number of different areas of science, including nature and health, resilience, sport(s), dance, youth empowerment, Indigenous health, physical literacy, leadership, political science, sociology and strengths-based approaches for community well-being and addressing equity.16 Not all of the evidence fell into the ‘physical activity’ and health/public health literature. Additional relevant work was found in research across many topics including youth leadership development, resilience models, community design and cultural revitalisation that included language and lifestyle programmes (eg, to address substance use, pain and/or chronic disease). There is also an interdisciplinary body of relevant literature related to historical and political factors and decolonialisation in sports. The body of evidence includes qualitative findings, programme evaluation, energy expenditure studies, cultural histories, injury reports and intervention. There are many personal stories and popular media with photographs to provide context.

The phased methods and diverse search strategies were not designed to precisely quantify the number of articles and other materials reviewed across years and coauthors, but to assess scope and scale. However, online supplemental file 1 provides some specific search benchmarks by phases, including insights from performing more than 50 pilot data extractions. The process has included the identification of 100 000s of relevant articles that will be found in formal searches using traditional public health databases and our expanded search terms. This speaks to the importance of clarifying CURE-PA boundaries and definitions in advance of formal search protocols.

The body of evidence confirms that many CURE-PAs are popular with general and at-risk populations7 and, thus, have potential to be implemented at scale for a variety of health promotion goals. This preliminary narrative review also revealed that innovative work is being done on this topic; many of the CURE-PA studies are some of the first of their kind for that type of activity.17 The review also highlighted the importance of Indigenous scholarship, stewardship and leadership in this area.

Definition of culturally or regionally relevant physical activity

From this narrative review, we identified a need to more clearly define CURE-PA to be clear what this evidence base may include and why. Following a review of relevant definitions and discussion in person and in writing with the authorship group, we thus define ‘culturally and regionally relevant physical activity’ as PA based on a population’s cultural customs.8 Details of this definition are described below and summarised in table 1. Culturally or regionally relevant

In our definition, cultural or regional relevance indicates customs that are based in a shared, collective, historical context. This includes Indigenous practices (eg, hula, powwow, aboriginal sports) and traditional dances, sports and games that have been classified as ‘heritage’ or ‘folk dance’. To be included in our definition of CURE-PA for health promotion, the activity only has to be culturally or regionally relevant. By this definition, CURE-PA can have collective engagement and recognition across a wide span of cultural expertise and depth.

CURE-PA can be engaged in by those for whom the activity is traditional, as well as by others. Participation in CURE-PA can occur in the location of origin (eg, hula in Hawai‘i) or by those for whom the activity is culturally relevant but who engage in the activity elsewhere (eg, hula by Native Hawaiians in the diaspora on the continental USA). CURE-PA can also include a more recent history, in which there is collective engagement in the activities in locations where they are not indigenous, but where they have become widespread and popular. For example, while surfing is indigenous to Polynesia and Hawai‘i, there is a recent history of collective engagement in Australia, where surfing is now the second-most practised water-based sport and is among the top five nature-based PAs in the country,18 as well as in Brazil, Portugal and many other countries.19 Considering the relevance of these factors and distinct cultural histories for health, especially given the interest in these activities, are valuable areas for future research and practice.

Given this complexity and the expectation that health impacts, outcomes, project goals and evaluation may vary by these domains, we developed a typology for CURE-PA based on location and the population engaging in the activity. This is described in more detail in the subsection ‘CURE-PA Typology’ below.

As used in our definition, ‘cultural relevance’ can be associated with, but should be distinguished from, several other similar terms. A continuum of approaches exists for developing health interventions that can be considered ‘culturally responsive’, from cultural adaptations to culturally grounded interventions.1 Health promotion to support CURE-PA, when implemented in and with a relevant community and following best practice approaches,1 can be considered a culturally responsive approach. A culturally responsive approach could be culturally adapted (eg, replacing a PA with a CURE-PA in an existing intervention to tailor the intervention to a particular community). Cultural adaptations, when made for either interventions or community-wide initiatives, can enhance acceptability.20 Another health promotion approach could be culturally grounded (ie, in which a new protocol was developed from scratch over time from cultural protocols).1

In keeping with identifying opportunities for valuable, strengths-based health promotion at scale across various communities and populations, this definition of CURE-PA is designed to be inclusive. By this definition, connections between culture and PA can be rooted in ethno-cultural identity, but also can include aspects of non-ethno-national, global and local cultural belonging, presuming a shared, collective, historical context.21

Physical activity

An important unifying piece of this definition is that the practice includes actual PA. CURE-PA includes a variety of types of PAs at various levels of metabolic equivalents (METs), including ethnic or folk dance; activities such as canoeing, hunting, fishing, foraging and gardening; as well as various sports, including those that may fall under an umbrella classification of ‘traditional’ (eg, ‘traditional Chinese sports’)22 or ‘heritage’.23

The WHO recommends that adults engage in a minimum of 150 min of moderate-intensity, or 75 min of vigorous-intensity aerobic PA, or an equivalent combination each week, as well as muscle strengthening two times per week.24 However, even light-intensity PAs may have significant health benefits over sedentary activities.25 Therefore, we have not included a specific MET threshold or cut-off for our definition of CURE-PA. However, consideration of activity intensity and METs for CURE-PAs may be needed prior to inclusion in health promotion interventions in light of goals and the focal population of the project.

Some health promotion initiatives and interventions include framing around a CURE-PA (eg, a canoe metaphor has been used as a framing example for a substance use programme for tribal youth in two Pacific Northwest tribes).26 While this is an important area for research, it does not constitute participation in CURE-PA. However, if, in this example, the tribal youth were participating in canoeing as part of the substance use programme and the canoeing held cultural relevance for them, then it would fall under this definition of CURE-PA.

Some studies will fit into more than one category. Therefore, multiple responses are possible when this typology is applied to the body of evidence. This distinction can be relevant for considering where, how and for whom to scale up health promotions initiatives and interventions, differences in outcomes, evaluations and project goals, and will be critical for future research and hypothesis testing in this research area. This can help create asset-driven health interventions for populations including immigrants, refugees and Indigenous populations in their homeland and across a diaspora.

Practical as well as conceptual challenges are inherent in determining the precise cultural and regional origins of certain activities, especially those that have evolved or been widely adopted beyond their initial context. Activities may also be delivered in ‘places’ that are virtual. Furthermore, consideration of hybrid or multicultural identities among participants may provide additional nuance and depth to the typology. This typology provides a lens to consider these issues for deeper consideration and discussion but is not intended to be restrictive in terms of cultural relevance or location of delivery.

Discussion

This work highlights the deep, widespread value of CURE-PA and the broad literature that already exists to support this across many types of evidence. Those who practice and promote CURE-PAs already know that these activities are valuable across multiple levels of health and well-being and have known this for centuries.28 Communities and cultures have shared this knowledge through many methods, including histories and stories that would only be shared from person to person. This work is designed to support, collect and amplify this knowledge to support health promotion of these practices towards goals of public health and health equity in a specific type of evidence gathering. In many cases this could support activities already occurring in communities from a grassroots, cultural or historical perspective that would not be considered ‘physical activity’ or ‘public health’ but can help to support and build public health from existing community interests, ongoing grassroots efforts and strengths in multidimensional ways.

Indigenous health knowledge systems, stewardship, leadership and holistic well-being frameworks provide fundamental grounding towards these goals in research, policy and practice.29 Indigenous scholarship is foundational to this field and literature syntheses bridging across both the distinct and collective findings in the area should include critical appraisals of gaps and needs in this scholarship centred from Indigenous perspectives using frameworks, models and systems centred on Indigenous values, processes and worldviews.30

This preliminary narrative review identified a wide breadth of published evidence, but further synthesis is needed to showcase the existing evidence, to evaluate population level interventions and hypothesis-driven research, and to identify gaps in the research both in terms of locations and populations of focus to build health equity. This is a preliminary narrative review from a specific perspective and is not intended to be a comprehensive summary of the full array of knowledge and history in this area.

Our definition of CURE-PA is intentionally broad and is designed to provide a connecting thread to support global public health promotion of these activities, not to diminish or co-opt existing community-based work across many areas of study and practice. The lack of a MET threshold allows the consideration of lighter activities that might be highly beneficial for reasons beyond just engagement in PA. For instance, gardening traditional fruits or vegetables, which may be a light intensity PA, may also be culturally important and linked to dietary or nutritional benefits. Lighter intensity PAs may also be more accessible than more vigorous activities for certain groups (eg, older adults, those with disabilities).

CURE-PA provides a way to focus on community strengths. Tailoring interventions to the unique cultural values and contexts of the target population has potential to increase acceptability and uptake and improve targeted behaviours such as PA.20

Implementation of these activities in research, policy and practice should follow best practices1 and, while relevant globally, also need tailoring locally. Some activities traditionally would only be practised by certain groups, at certain times and with appropriate cultural protocols and training in place.12 Failing to recognise this in health promotion or ignoring the legacy of colonisation and other harms could cause new harms. Equitable research and practice environments with shared decision-making authority, equal access to financial resources and opportunity to integrate community wisdom with the scientific method to culturally adapt the intervention should be present. Cultural expertise about the target population’s values, practices and other contextual factors as well as how to best deliver and evaluate health promotion using measures important to the target population and that fully demonstrate collective as well as individual value are important.31

Innovative, effective and culturally relevant PA interventions are needed to increase community well-being. Greater inclusion of CURE-PA is concordant with recently articulated international health promotion goals as well as local, regional and national goals towards health equity. Cultural relevance is particularly important for engagement of Indigenous populations in public health interventions.28

This work was intended to inform future formal review protocols and project planning. Future research in this area will require additional searches for specific activities, populations and locations in order to fully capture the relevant published evidence. Additional experts would need to be included in further review of the literature, including research librarians, Indigenous scholars and others with knowledge and wisdom across locations. For example, an individual with expertise in global dance could assist in developing a comprehensive list of search terms relevant to different ethnic dance styles. Existing compendiums, such as dances classified by UNESCO under definitions for ‘heritage’ activities (eg, K’cimi dancing)32 may be fruitful resources to consider.

Limitations

This study focused on a particular goal of synthesising this literature broadly from a global health promotion perspective for public health research and practice. There are limitations in our review’s methodology, scope and potential biases. Future research using other methods can answer additional, detailed questions from this body of literature in a more systematic way, including: (1) what types of articles exist in the literature on CURE-PA? (2) In what locations and in what populations is this work occurring? (2) What CURE-PAs are being studied? (3) What health conditions are being studied? (4) What are the research questions? (5) What outcome measures are being used? (5) What are the main empirical findings? We also acknowledge that review of published literature across formal repositories will not necessarily capture evidence shared through oral tradition or other methods and that the broad approach to showcase common elements must be cautious not to obscure critical, specific details. Engagement with local communities will inevitably shed further light on the value and practice of certain CURE-PAs and is needed in planning to ensure relevance. While we have strong global and disciplinary representation, we do lack representation on our authorship team from some areas that would be relevant and important to future work, including Asia and the Middle East. Also, scholarship from many languages besides English has important knowledge and evidence and must be included in this effort. This article focuses on conceptual boundaries, examples and synthesis, but does not synthesise the characteristics of a specific group of papers nor does it review the articles for quality and shares limitations with narrative reviews generally.33 We believe this article can provide grounding for future work to resolve these gaps.

Conclusions

This definitional paper and evidence synthesis was designed to support and inspire work in this area, given the high relevance of CURE-PAs as strengths-based public health approaches with known impact and value in communities for health and well-being as well as strong justification across multiple types of science and knowledge.
