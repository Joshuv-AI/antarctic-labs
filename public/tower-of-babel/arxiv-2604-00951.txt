Quantum Statistical Bootstrap
Yongkai Chen, Ping Ma, Wenxuan Zhong

Abstract
The bootstrap is a foundational tool in statistical inference, but its classical implementation relies on Monte Carlo resampling, introducing approximation error and incurring high computational cost—especially for large datasets and complex models. We present the Quantum Bootstrap (QBOOT), a quantum algorithm that computes the ideal bootstrap estimate exactly by encoding all possible resamples in quantum superposition, evaluating the target statistic in parallel, and extracting the aggregate via quantum amplitude estimation. Under mild circuit efficiency assumptions, QBOOT achieves a near-quadratic speedup over the classical bootstrap in approximating the ideal estimator, independent of the statistic or underlying distribution. We provide a rigorous theoretical analysis of its statistical error properties—addressing a gap in the quantum algorithms literature—and validate our results through experiments on the IBM quantum simulator for the sample mean problem. Our findings demonstrate that QBOOT preserves the asymptotic properties of the ideal bootstrap while substantially improving computational efficiency and precision, establishing a scalable and principled framework for quantum statistical inference.

Results

II.1
Preliminary

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
denote the sample size, let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space, and let
\bm{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\sim\mathbb{P}^{\otimes n}
be i.i.d. observations. A (real-valued) statistic is a measurable map
f:\Omega^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
; we write

\hat{\theta}_{n}\;=\;f(\bm{X})

for the estimator of an underlying parameter
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. For a threshold
z\in\mathbb{R}
, define the indicator

g(z,\mathbf{x})\;=\;\mathbf{1}\!\{\,f(\mathbf{x})\leq z\,\},\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\Omega^{n}.

The sampling distribution function (cdf) of
\hat{\theta}_{n}
under
\mathbb{P}
is then

H_{\mathbb{P}}(z)\;=\;\mathbb{P}^{\otimes n}\!\big(f(\bm{X})\leq z\big)\;=\;E_{\mathbb{P}^{\otimes n}}\!\big[\,g(z,\bm{X})\,\big].

(1)

II.1.1
Ideal nonparametric bootstrap.

The empirical distribution of
\bm{X}
is
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
, where
\delta_{X}
denotes a point mass at
X
. A
bootstrap resample

\bm{X}^{*}=(X_{1}^{*},\ldots,X_{n}^{*})
is drawn i.i.d. from
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
, written
\bm{X}^{*}\sim\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\otimes n}
. We use
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{*}(\cdot\mid\bm{X})
and
E_{*}(\cdot\mid\bm{X})
for probability and expectation with respect to the resampling mechanism, conditional on the observed data
\bm{X}
. The
ideal bootstrap value
is

H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)\;=\;\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits_{*}\!\big(f(\bm{X}^{*})\leq z\,\big|\,\bm{X}\big)\;=\;E_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\otimes n}}\!\big[\,g(z,\bm{X}^{*})\,\big|\,\bm{X}\big].

(2)

If we enumerate resamples by index vectors
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})\in[n]^{n}
(each
i_{j}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
), then each index-resample has probability
n^{-n}
being drawn and

H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)\;=\;\frac{1}{n^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in[n]^{n}}g\!\big(z,(X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{n}})\big).

(3)

Eq. (
3
) is exact and does not require distinct observed values; duplicates are naturally accounted for by their multiplicities in
[n]^{n}
. Evaluating (
3
) is intractable for even moderate
n
because the sum has
n^{n}
terms.

II.1.2
Monte Carlo (classical) bootstrap approximation.

In practice,
H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)
is approximated by independently drawing
B
bootstrap resamples
\tilde{\bm{X}}_{1}^{*},\ldots,\tilde{\bm{X}}_{B}^{*}\sim\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\otimes n}
and computing

H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}^{(B)}(z)\;=\;\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}g\big(z,\tilde{\bm{X}}_{b}^{*}\big).

(4)

Because
g\in\{0,1\}
, the Monte Carlo estimator is unbiased:

E_{*}\!\big[\,H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}^{(B)}(z)\,\big|\,\bm{X}\big]\;=\;H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z),

and has conditional variance

\mbox{Var}_{*}\!\big(H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}^{(B)}(z)\,\big|\,\bm{X}\big)\;=\;\frac{H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)\{1-H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)\}}{B}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{4B}.

Thus the root-mean-square error is of order
B^{-1/2}
, and achieving accuracy
\epsilon
(in RMSE) requires
B=\Theta(\epsilon^{-2})
Monte Carlo replicates. We refer to this Monte Carlo approximation as the classical bootstrap (CBOOT) to contrast it with the quantum bootstrap presented in this work.

II.1.3
Computational complexity of the classical bootstrap.

Let
C_{g}(n)
denote the classical time required to evaluate
g(z,\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{1}\{f(\mathbf{x})\leq z\}
once on a size-
n
sample (this includes the cost of computing
f
). Generating one bootstrap resample by sampling indices with replacement costs
\Theta(n)
random draws; hence the total cost of (
4
) is

\Theta\!\big(B\,C_{g}(n)\,+\,B\,n\big),

where the dominant term is typically
B\,C_{g}(n)
. These baseline facts will be used in Section
II.3
to compare classical and quantum complexities for a fixed target accuracy.

II.2
Quantum Bootstrap (QBOOT)

QBOOT computes the ideal bootstrap value
H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)

without
Monte Carlo approximation by (i) encoding the empirical resampling measure
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\otimes n}
as a quantum superposition, (ii) evaluating the indicator
g(z,\bm{X}^{*})=\mathbf{1}\{f(\bm{X}^{*})\leq z\}
coherently on every resample, and (iii) recovering the resulting amplitude by quantum amplitude estimation (QAE). Throughout,
n
is the sample size,
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
is the empirical distribution from Section
II.1
, and
g
is the indicator associated with the statistic
f(\bm{X})
in (
1
).

II.2.1
State encoding.

To represent the empirical resampling distribution, we index observations by
\{1,\ldots,n\}
and use
index encoding
on
m=\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil
qubits: observation
X_{i}
is associated with the orthonormal basis state
\ket{i-1}
2
2

2

If direct access to the numerical values
\{X_{i}\}
is needed inside the circuit that computes
g
, these values are loaded into a small workspace register on demand; the index encoding keeps the resampling step simple and exact.
. A single application of a preparation unitary
\mathbf{U}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}
creates the uniform superposition

\ket{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}\;=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ket{i-1}.

Placing
n
such registers in parallel yields the exact quantum representation of the bootstrap product measure,

\ket{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\otimes n}}\;=\;\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}\ket{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}\;=\;\frac{1}{n^{n/2}}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in[n]^{n}}\ket{\mathbf{i}},

(5)

where
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})
and
\ket{\mathbf{i}}=\ket{i_{1}-1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{i_{n}-1}

5

II.2.2
Coherent evaluation of the indicator.

Given
z\in\mathbb{R}
, QBOOT applies a unitary

\mathbf{U}_{g}(z):\quad\ket{\mathbf{i}}\ket{0}\ \longmapsto\ \ket{\mathbf{i}}\,\ket{g\!\big(z,\bm{X}^{*}(\mathbf{i})\big)},

which computes
g\!\big(z,\bm{X}^{*}(\mathbf{i})\big)
on the index-specified resample
\bm{X}^{*}(\mathbf{i})=(X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{n}})
and writes the result to a single
label qubit
. The label qubit, together with the resample (seed) register, decomposes the post-evaluation state into orthogonal “mean” and “contrast” subspaces. Applying
\mathbf{U}_{g}(z)
to (
5
) yields a two-component superposition

\ket{\phi(z)}\;=\;\sqrt{H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)}\,\ket{\phi_{1}}\;+\;\sqrt{1-H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)}\,\ket{\phi_{0}},

(6)

where
\ket{\phi_{1}}
(resp.
\ket{\phi_{0}}
) is supported on label-qubit state
\ket{1}
(resp.
\ket{0}
) and
\braket{\phi_{1}|\phi_{0}}=0
. Existence of such a unitary (and a constructive block-diagonal realization) follows from standard arguments and is detailed in supplementary material
S.2
. In practice,
\mathbf{U}_{g}(z)
mirrors the classical routine that evaluates
f(\cdot)
and compares it with
z
, implemented reversibly so the workspace can be uncomputed; see supplementary material Fig.
S.2
for the realization of
\mathbf{U}_{g}(z)
for the sample-mean case.

II.2.3
Amplitude estimation and read‑out.

With
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}_{g}(z)\big(\mathbf{U}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\otimes n}}\otimes I\big)
denoting the joint preparation of (
6
) from the all‑zero state, QAE constructs the Grover iterate

\mathbf{Q}\;=\;\mathbf{A}\,S_{0}\,\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}\,S_{\phi_{1}},

where
S_{0}=I-2\ket{\mathbf{0}}\bra{\mathbf{0}}
flips the phase of the all‑zero computational state and
S_{\phi_{1}}
flips the phase on the “mean” subspace spanned by
\ket{\phi_{1}}
. The operator
\mathbf{Q}
has eigenvectors
(\ket{\phi_{1}}\pm i\ket{\phi_{0}})/\sqrt{2}
with eigenvalues
e^{\pm i\,2\tau_{z}}
, where
\sin^{2}\tau_{z}=H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)

T\in\mathbb{N}

precision qubits
produces an integer outcome

, from which the QBOOT estimator is

H_{\mathrm{QBOOT}}(z)\;=\;\sin^{2}\!\left(\frac{\pi Y}{2^{T}}\right).

(7)

Standard QAE accuracy guarantees imply that the stochastic error
|H_{\mathrm{QBOOT}}(z)-H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)|
is
\mathcal{O}_{p}(2^{-T})
uniformly in
z
(see Theorem
II.2
).

II.2.4
Multiple bootstrap CDF values via parallelism.

The above procedure estimates

for a single

Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{g}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{D})\big(\ket{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\otimes n}}\ket{0}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{n/2}}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in[n]^{n}}\ket{\mathbf{i}}\,\ket{\,D-\textstyle\sum_{d=1}^{D}g(z_{d},\bm{X}^{*}(\mathbf{i}))\,}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z_{d^{\prime}})}\,\ket{\phi^{(d^{\prime})}_{1}}\;+\;\sqrt{1-H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z_{d^{\prime}})}\,\ket{\phi^{(d^{\prime})}_{0}},

where, for each threshold

\displaystyle\ket{\phi^{(d^{\prime})}_{1}}=

\displaystyle\big[H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z_{d^{\prime}})\,n^{n}\big]^{-1/2}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in[n]^{n}}\mathbf{1}\!\left\{D-\sum_{d=1}^{D}g(z_{d},\bm{X}^{*}(\mathbf{i}))<d^{\prime}\right\}\ket{\mathbf{i}}

\displaystyle\,\ket{\,D-\textstyle\sum_{d=1}^{D}g(z_{d},\bm{X}^{*}(\mathbf{i}))\,},

\displaystyle\big[(1-H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z_{d^{\prime}}))\,n^{n}\big]^{-1/2}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in[n]^{n}}\mathbf{1}\!\left\{D-\sum_{d=1}^{D}g(z_{d},\bm{X}^{*}(\mathbf{i}))\geq d^{\prime}\right\}\ket{\mathbf{i}}

\displaystyle\,\ket{\,D-\textstyle\sum_{d=1}^{D}g(z_{d},\bm{X}^{*}(\mathbf{i}))\,}.

Hence, we can simultaneously estimate

for all

II.3
Theoretical Results

Let
H_{\mathbb{P}}
denote the target sampling distribution in (
1
), let
H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}
be the ideal nonparametric bootstrap distribution in (
2
), and let
H_{\mathrm{QBOOT}}
be the QBOOT estimator defined in (
7
). For c.d.f.s
F
and
G
, write

K(F,G)=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}|F(z)-G(z)|

for the Kolmogorov distance. The total error decomposes as

K\!\big(H_{\mathrm{QBOOT}},H_{\mathbb{P}}\big)\;\leq\;\underbrace{K\!\big(H_{\mathrm{QBOOT}},H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}\big)}_{\text{quantum error }\Delta_{Q}}\;+\;\underbrace{K\!\big(H_{\mathrm{BOOT}},H_{\mathbb{P}}\big)}_{\text{bootstrap error }\Delta_{B}},

(8)

where
\Delta_{B}
is the bootstrap error and
\Delta_{Q}
is the quantum (amplitude‑estimation) error. The stochastic orders below are taken jointly over the data
\bm{X}\sim\mathbb{P}^{\otimes n}
and, when relevant, over the internal measurement randomness of QAE.
We first characterize the quantum error
\Delta_{Q}
and then review the bootstrap error
\Delta_{B}
. The balance between these two terms is a critical guide for quantum circuit design and resource allocation: choosing
T
too small makes
\Delta_{Q}
dominate, while choosing
T
too large wastes quantum resources beyond what the bootstrap accuracy requires.

II.3.1
Quantum error.

Theorem II.1
(Exact distribution of QBOOT)

Fix
T\in\mathbb{N}
and
z\in\mathbb{R}
.
Conditional on
H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)
(equivalently, on the observed data), the QBOOT estimator admits the exact representation

H_{\mathrm{QBOOT}}(z)\;\overset{d}{=}\;\sin^{2}\!\left(\frac{\pi Y_{z}}{2^{T}}\right),

(9)

where
Y_{z}\in\{0,1,\dots,2^{T}-1\}
has probability mass function

\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(Y_{z}=l)=\frac{\sin^{2}(2^{T}\tau_{z})}{2^{2T+1}}\left[\csc^{2}\!\left(\tau_{z}-\frac{l\pi}{2^{T}}\right)+\csc^{2}\!\left(\tau_{z}+\frac{l\pi}{2^{T}}\right)\right],\qquad l=0,1,\dots,2^{T}-1,

(10)

with
\tau_{z}=\arcsin\sqrt{H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)}

for
\tau_{z}\notin\{\frac{m\pi}{2^{T}}:m\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, with (
10
) understood by continuous extension at those grid points.
In particular, if
2^{T}\tau_{z}/\pi\in\mathbb{Z}
(equivalently
p_{z}=\sin^{2}(m\pi/2^{T})
for some integer
m
), then
Y_{z}
is degenerate and

\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits\!\left(H_{\mathrm{QBOOT}}(z)=H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)\right)=1.

Since
\sin^{2}
is
1

Theorem II.2
(Quantum error stochastic bound)

Let
T
be the number of precision qubits used by QAE. Then

\Delta_{Q}\;=\;K\!\big(H_{\mathrm{QBOOT}},H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}\big)\;=\;\mathcal{O}_{p}(2^{-T}),

for
\forall z\in\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem II.3
(Bias and MSE)

For any fixed
z\in\mathbb{R}
and
T\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\big[H_{\mathrm{QBOOT}}(z)\,\big|\,\bm{X}\big]-H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)

\displaystyle=2^{-2T}\sin^{2}(2^{T}\tau_{z})\sum_{l=0}^{2^{T}-1}\frac{\sin(2^{-T}l\pi+\tau_{z})}{\sin(2^{-T}l\pi-\tau_{z})},

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\Big[\!\big(H_{\mathrm{QBOOT}}(z)-H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)\big)^{2}\,\Big|\,\bm{X}\Big]

\displaystyle=2^{-2T}\sin^{2}(2^{T}\tau_{z})\sum_{l=0}^{2^{T}-1}\sin^{2}(2^{-T}l\pi+\tau_{z}).

(12)

The bias and MSE depend on

\tau_{z}
and exhibit a mild oscillatory pattern with exact zeros when

; see Fig.

. These oscillations explain the non‑monotone but

Corollary II.4
(Uniform asymptotics)

As

\big|\mathrm{Bias}\big|\;=\;\mathcal{O}(2^{-T}),\qquad\mathrm{MSE}\;=\;\mathcal{O}(2^{-T}).

II.3.2
Work (gate) complexity.

Let
Q_{g}(n)
be the gate complexity of a single call to the unitary
\mathbf{U}_{g}(z)
that evaluates
g(z,\bm{X}^{*})=\mathbf{1}\{f(\bm{X}^{*})\leq z\}
(Section
II.2
); let
Q_{\mathrm{enc}}(n)
be the cost of preparing the
n
resampling registers (index encoding), which is
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
gates, as discussed in detail in Section
S.3
of the supplementary material. One run of QAE with
T
precision qubits uses
\Theta(2^{T})
controlled applications of the Grover iterate
\mathbf{Q}
, and each
\mathbf{Q}
invokes
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}
once (hence calls
\mathbf{U}_{g}(z)
and
\mathbf{U}_{g}(z)^{\dagger}
once). Therefore, to achieve the accuracy in Theorem

, the work complexity is

\text{Work}(\text{QBOOT},\epsilon\asymp 2^{-T})\;=\;\mathcal{O}\!\big(2^{T}\,\big(Q_{g}(n)\,\vee\,n\log n(n)\big)\big),

(13)

where

. For the classical Monte Carlo bootstrap (CBOOT), attaining error
\epsilon
requires
B=\Theta(\epsilon^{-2})
resamples, at cost

\text{Work}(\text{CBOOT},\epsilon)\;=\;\mathcal{O}\!\big(\epsilon^{-2}\,\big(C_{g}(n)\,\vee\,n\log n\big)\big),

(14)

where
C_{g}(n)
is the classical cost of evaluating

once and

In theory,

. For many algorithms, the cost can even be much lower. See the supplementary material Table
3

The following theorem presents the gate complexity of QBOOT.

Theorem II.5
(QBOOT gate complexity)

To achieve
\Delta_{Q}=\mathcal{O}_{p}(2^{-T})
, QBOOT requires
\mathcal{O}\!\big(2^{T}\,(Q_{g}(n)\vee n\log n)\big)
gates. In terms of a target tolerance
\epsilon\asymp 2^{-T}
, this equals
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1}\,(Q_{g}\vee n\log n))
, whereas CBOOT requires
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2}\,(C_{g}\vee n\log n))
operations.

II.3.3
Qubit complexity of QBOOT.

Having established the queries to
\mathbf{U}_{g}(z)
and gate complexity, we now explicitly analyze the total qubit count.

The QBOOT circuit has four distinct qubit components: (1) The
bootstrap register
uses
n\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil
qubits to
hold
n
index registers of
\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil
qubits each.
(2) The
statistic register
stores the computed value of
f(\bm{X}^{*})
.
When
f
is a discrete statistic taking at most
V
distinct values
(e.g., the sample mean of
n
draws from
\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
has

V=n(n-1)+1

\lceil\log_{2}(V+1)\rceil
qubits.
For a continuous statistic standardized to
[0,1]
, one could discretize to
allow a rounding error of at most
1/V
;
choosing
V\gg n
keeps this rounding error negligible compared to the bootstrap error
\Delta_{B}
.
(3) The
label qubit
is a single qubit that stores the binary indicator
g(z,\bm{X}^{*})\in\{0,1\}
after the unitary
\mathbf{U}_{g}(z)
is applied.
(4) The
QAE register
contains
T
precision qubits used by the quantum phase estimation step of QAE to estimate

\sqrt{H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)}
to accuracy
\mathcal{O}(2^{-T})
.

Combining all components, the QBOOT circuit with
T
precision qubits
requires

N_{\mathrm{qubit}}\;=\;\underbrace{n\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil}_{\text{bootstrap register}}+\,\underbrace{\lceil\log_{2}(V+1)\rceil}_{\text{statistic register}}+\,\underbrace{1}_{\text{label qubit}}+\,\underbrace{T}_{\text{QAE register}}+\,W_{f},

(15)

where
W_{f}

The dominant term in the qubit complexity,

resamples.

.

II.3.4
Balancing stochastic errors and quantum resource allocation.

Under standard regularity for the

of

\Delta_{B}\;=\;\mathcal{O}_{p}(n^{-1}).

(16)

This bound is uniform in

on continuity points of

.
Combining (

K(H_{\mathrm{QBOOT}},H_{\mathbb{P}})=\mathcal{O}_{p}(2^{-T})+\mathcal{O}_{p}(n^{-1}).

Choosing

2^{-T}\asymp n^{-1}
(so

\mathcal{O}_{p}(n^{-1})
with quantum work

\text{Work}(\text{QBOOT})\;=\;\mathcal{O}\!\big(n\,(Q_{g}(n)\vee n\log n(n))\big),

whereas CBOOT requires

\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\,(C_{g}(n)\vee n\log n))

asymptotic speedup at matched statistical accuracy.

For

(

In that case, balancing with

II.4.1
Data and statistic.

II.4.2
Ground truth by enumeration.

For

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(1.25)\;

The

III
Discussion

QBOOT offers a principled route to
quantum‑powered
resampling–based inference.
By preparing a coherent superposition over all
n^{n}
bootstrap resamples and evaluating the indicator
g(z,\bm{X}^{*})=\mathbf{1}\{\!f(\bm{X}^{*})\leq z\!\}
in parallel, QBOOT removes Monte Carlo approximation from the bootstrap step and replaces it with a controllable quantum estimation error governed by the number
T
of precision qubits.
The analysis in Section
II.3
shows that the quantum error obeys
K(H_{\mathrm{QBOOT}},H_{\mathrm{BOOT}})=\mathcal{O}_{p}(2^{-T})
(Theorem
II.2
), while the total computational cost scales as
\mathcal{O}\!\big(2^{T}(Q_{g}\vee Q_{\mathrm{enc}})\big)
(Theorem
II.5
).
Balancing this with the classical bootstrap error suggests the practical choice
2^{-T}\asymp n^{-1}
, which yields overall cost
\mathcal{O}(n\,Q_{g})
and at least a linear‑in‑
n
advantage over the classical bootstrap when
Q_{g}\lesssim C_{g}
.
In our experiments on the sample‑mean problem, QBOOT exhibits the predicted
O(2^{-T})
decay in absolute error and dominates the classical bootstrap (CBOOT) in mean and median absolute error for matched cost; modest repetition with median aggregation suppresses the occasional large QAE errors and restores
\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}}
to the
O(2^{-T})
trend. Table
2
provides a detailed theoretical and practical comparison of QBOOT and CBOOT.

There are two important distinctions from classical practice.
First, QBOOT targets the
ideal
bootstrap quantity
H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)
rather than a Monte Carlo approximation
H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}^{(B)}(z)
.
Second, the accuracy–cost trade‑off is explicit and tunable: halving the quantum error requires one additional precision qubit
T\mapsto T+1
(i.e., a constant‑factor increase in queries), whereas the classical Monte Carlo error requires a four‑fold increase in the number of resamples
B
.

The performance guarantees rely on standard but explicit assumptions:
(i) an orthogonal encoding of the observed values into computational basis states so that resamples are perfectly distinguishable;
(ii) a unitary implementation of the statistic oracle
\mathbf{U}_{g}(z)
with gate complexity
Q_{g}
that is comparable to the classical evaluation cost
C_{g}
; and
(iii) state preparation with cost
n\log n=\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
using index encoding (Section
II.2
).
These assumptions hold for many statistics that can be computed with reversible arithmetic (e.g., sums, ranks, comparisons) and can be tightened further when quantum subroutines offer genuine savings (e.g., Grover‑type routines for order statistics or search, quantum kernels for certain similarity measures).
As with any amplitude‑estimation method, finite‑precision effects induce an oscillatory error profile; our empirical results show that a small number of repeated QAE runs with median aggregation largely removes their impact on MSE without altering the asymptotic rate.

Canonical QAE utilizes controlled powers of a Grover iterate and an inverse QFT, which can be demanding on noisy devices.
In near‑term settings, it is natural to replace the phase‑estimation primitive by shallower amplitude‑estimation variants and to use error‑mitigated circuits for the arithmetic and comparator blocks.
Our study utilizes a simulator to isolate algorithmic behavior; hardware deployment will benefit from depth-reduction strategies (e.g., iterative amplitude estimation, decomposition of comparators into low-T gates) and the robust aggregation scheme we have evaluated.

QBOOT’s design is modular at the resampling, statistic, and estimation layers.
Alternative resampling schemes—parametric, smoothed, Bayesian,
m
‑out‑of‑
n
—amount to changing the state‑preparation unitary.
The same framework extends beyond distributional estimation to hypothesis testing (quantum permutation tests) and model assessment (quantum cross‑validation), where the label qubit encodes the relevant indicator (or loss threshold) and QAE provides the aggregate.
When multiple thresholds
z
are needed, one can reuse the bootstrap superposition and evaluate multiple comparators sequentially, amortizing the state-preparation cost across the grid.

In summary, QBOOT preserves the asymptotic properties of the ideal bootstrap while delivering a clear computational advantage in the accuracy–cost trade‑off.
Although current NISQ devices limit problem sizes, rapid improvements in quantum volume and error correction should expand the feasible regime.
We anticipate that QBOOT and related quantum resampling algorithms will become practical tools for large‑scale, model‑free statistical inference, especially for statistics whose classical evaluation is costly and for workflows that require many distributional queries
z
.

Dirac notation and registers.

The computational basis for a single qubit is
\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}

\ket{\psi}=\alpha_{0}\ket{0}+\alpha_{1}\ket{1}

We use

\ket{\psi}

A linear map

U^{\dagger}U=I

\ket{1}

A projective measurement is a collection of orthogonal projectors
\{P_{i}\}

\sum_{i}P_{i}=I

For an

(S.25)

where
\ket{e_{\tau_{z}}}=\frac{1}{2^{T}}\underset{k,l=0}{\overset{2^{T}-1}{\sum}}e^{\bm{i}\left(\tau_{z}(2k+1)-\pi/2-\frac{2\pi kl}{2^{T}}\right)}\ket{l}
and

\ket{e_{-\tau_{z}}}=\frac{1}{2^{T}}\underset{k,l=0}{\overset{2^{T}-1}{\sum}}e^{-\bm{i}\left(\tau_{z}(2k+1)+\pi/2+\frac{2\pi kl}{2^{T}}\right)}\ket{l}
.

Step 4
. Measure the measurement units. The measurements of the measurement units are used to estimate
\tau_{z}
.

Appendix S.2
Formal QBOOT Construction

S.2.1
Encoding the empirical bootstrap measure

Let
\bm{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
denote the observed sample, and let
m=\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil
.
We use
index encoding
on
m
qubits with computational basis

\{\ket{0},\ldots,\ket{n-1}\}
so that observation
X_{i}
is associated with
\ket{i-1}
.
The empirical distribution
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
is encoded by

\ket{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}\;=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ket{i-1},

so a projective measurement in the index basis returns index
i
with probability
1/n
.
Placing
n
such registers in parallel gives the exact quantum representation of the bootstrap product:

\ket{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\otimes n}}\;=\;\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}\ket{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}\;=\;\frac{1}{n^{n/2}}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in[n]^{n}}\ket{\mathbf{i}},

(S.26)

where
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})
and
\ket{\mathbf{i}}=\ket{i_{1}-1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{i_{n}-1}
.
Each seed
\mathbf{i}
deterministically specifies the resampled dataset
\bm{X}^{*}(\mathbf{i})=(X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{n}})
.

S.2.1.1
Measurement–probability correspondence.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{P}}=\mathrm{span}\{\ket{i-1}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and

\mathcal{P}_{n}=\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\delta_{X_{i}}:p_{i}\geq 0,\ \sum_{i}p_{i}=1\}
.
For any
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}\ket{i-1}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{P}}
, define the classical outcome map

\mathsf{Pr}(\ket{\psi})\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}|b_{i}|^{2}\,\delta_{X_{i}}\ \in\ \mathcal{P}_{n},

(S.27)

which extends tensorially:
\mathsf{Pr}(\ket{\psi}\otimes\ket{\psi^{\prime}})=\mathsf{Pr}(\ket{\psi})\otimes\mathsf{Pr}(\ket{\psi^{\prime}})
.
Applying (
S.27
) to (
S.26
) recovers the classical product measure
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\otimes n}
over resamples
\bm{X}^{*}(\mathbf{i})
.

S.2.2
The indicator observable and its unitary realization

Let
f:\Omega^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a statistic and
g(z,\bm{X}^{*})=\mathbf{1}\{f(\bm{X}^{*})\leq z\}
.
Define the projector onto the “accept” subspace in the seed basis:

\mathbf{M}_{g}(z)\;=\;\sum_{\mathbf{i}:\,g(z,\bm{X}^{*}(\mathbf{i}))=1}\ \ket{\mathbf{i}}\bra{\mathbf{i}}.

By construction,

\bra{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\otimes n}}\mathbf{M}_{g}(z)\ket{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\otimes n}}\;=\;H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z).

To embed
g
coherently, introduce a
label qubit
and define a unitary
\mathbf{U}_{g}(z)
acting as

\mathbf{U}_{g}(z)\big(\ket{\mathbf{i}}\ket{0}\big)\;=\;\ket{\mathbf{i}}\,\ket{g\!\big(z,\bm{X}^{*}(\mathbf{i})\big)}.

(S.28)

A block-diagonal construction in the orthonormal seed basis
\{\ket{\mathbf{i}}\}_{\mathbf{i}\in[n]^{n}}
ensures the existence of
\mathbf{U}_{g}(z)
; a constructive realization follows by reversibly evaluating
f(\bm{X}^{*}(\mathbf{i}))
, comparing to
z
, and uncomputing any workspace.

S.2.3
State for amplitude estimation and eigenstructure

Let
\mathbf{U}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}
be any state-preparation unitary with
\mathbf{U}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}\ket{\mathbf{0}}_{m}=\ket{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}
, and define

\mathbf{U}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\otimes n}}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{U}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}},\qquad\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}_{g}(z)\,(\mathbf{U}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\otimes n}}\otimes I).

Acting on
\ket{\mathbf{0}}^{\otimes nm}\ket{0}
,

\mathbf{A}\ket{\mathbf{0}}\;=\;\sqrt{H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)}\,\ket{\phi_{1}}+\sqrt{1-H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)}\,\ket{\phi_{0}},\qquad\braket{\phi_{1}|\phi_{0}}=0,

so the amplitude of
\ket{\phi_{1}}
equals
\sqrt{H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)}
.
Let
S_{0}=I-2\ket{\mathbf{0}}\bra{\mathbf{0}}
(on all non-precision registers) and let
S_{\phi_{1}}
flip the phase of
\ket{\phi_{1}}
.
The Grover iterate
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{A}\,S_{0}\,\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}\,S_{\phi_{1}}
has eigenvectors

\ket{\phi_{\pm}}=\frac{\ket{\phi_{1}}\pm i\ket{\phi_{0}}}{\sqrt{2}},\qquad\mathbf{Q}\ket{\phi_{\pm}}=e^{\pm i\,2\tau_{z}}\ket{\phi_{\pm}},

with
\sin^{2}\tau_{z}=H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)
.
Applying QAE with
T
precision qubits to
\mathbf{Q}
yields the estimator

H_{\mathrm{QBOOT}}(z)=\sin^{2}\!\left(\frac{\pi Y}{2^{T}}\right),\qquad Y\in\{0,\ldots,2^{T}-1\}.

Appendix S.3
Implementation Details

State preparation (bootstrap encoding unit).

With
index encoding
,
\ket{\psi_{X_{i}}}=\ket{i-1}
on
m=\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil
qubits and
\ket{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}
is prepared by a uniform superposition, followed by a classical table that maps indices to data values when needed.
When downstream arithmetic must act on the values themselves, one may use
value encodings
(e.g., basis, angle, or amplitude encodings) in a data register linked to the index register.
Equation (
S.26
) is then prepared by
n
parallel copies of
\mathbf{U}_{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}
.

Statistic evaluation (estimator unit).

A practical
\mathbf{U}_{g}(z)
mirrors the classical computation of
g
with reversible arithmetic:
accumulate (in a statistic register) the quantity required by
f
, compare to
z
, write the comparison result to the label qubit, and uncompute the accumulator.
For the sample mean, the circuit implements a reversible adder to compute
\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}^{*}
, a comparator with threshold
nz
, a controlled
X
on the label qubit, and then the inverse adder to return all ancillae to
\ket{0}
, as shown in Figure
S.2
.
This realisation typically yields quantum gate complexity
Q_{g}
comparable to the classical cost
C_{g}
; specific
g
may admit quantum subroutines with
Q_{g}\ll C_{g}
(e.g., Grover‑style routines for order statistics).

Appendix S.4
Proof Sketches Underpinning the Main Results

Let
H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)=\sin^{2}\tau_{z}
.

Measurement outcome distribution.

A standard calculation for phase estimation applied to
\mathbf{Q}
gives, for
l\in\{0,\ldots,2^{T}-1\}
,

\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(l_{z}=l)\;=\;\frac{\sin^{2}(2^{T}\tau_{z})}{2^{2T+1}}\left[\csc^{2}\!\left(\tau_{z}-\frac{l\pi}{2^{T}}\right)+\csc^{2}\!\left(\tau_{z}+\frac{l\pi}{2^{T}}\right)\right].

(S.29)

The mass concentrates near
l
such that
2^{-T}l\pi\approx\tau_{z}
or
\pi-\tau_{z}
.
The example of probability distribution of QAE outcomes
\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(l_{z}=l)
for
\tau_{z}=0.5
and
T\in\{4,8,12\}
is shown in Figure
S.1
.

Quantum error bound (Theorem
II.2
).

Using (
S.29
), one bounds the probability that
Y
falls outside the nearest integers to
2^{T}\tau_{z}/\pi
; mapping
Y\mapsto\sin^{2}(\pi Y/2^{T})
then yields

K(H_{\mathrm{QBOOT}},H_{\mathrm{BOOT}})=\mathcal{O}_{p}(2^{-T})
.

Bias and MSE (Theorem
II.3
).

Taking expectations of
H_{\mathrm{QBOOT}}(z)-H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)
and its square with respect to (
S.29
) gives the exact expressions

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[H_{\mathrm{QBOOT}}(z)]-H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)

\displaystyle=2^{-2T}\sin^{2}(2^{T}\tau_{z})\sum_{l=0}^{2^{T}-1}\frac{\sin(2^{-T}l\pi+\tau_{z})}{\sin(2^{-T}l\pi-\tau_{z})},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[(H_{\mathrm{QBOOT}}(z)-H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=2^{-2T}\sin^{2}(2^{T}\tau_{z})\sum_{l=0}^{2^{T}-1}\sin^{2}(2^{-T}l\pi+\tau_{z}).

Approximating the sums by integrals shows both the bias and the MSE are
\mathcal{O}(2^{-T})
uniformly in
z
(Corollary
II.4
), and explains the observed oscillations as functions of
H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)
.

Appendix S.5
Proof of the Median-of-
M
Theorem

Single-run error bound(Lemma
II.6
).

Fix
z\in\mathbb{R}
and write
\tau_{z}=\arcsin\sqrt{H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)}\in[0,\pi/2]
.
Let
l^{*}=\lfloor 2^{T}\tau_{z}/\pi\rfloor
and
\phi=2^{T}\tau_{z}/\pi-l^{*}\in[0,1)
.
Because
\sin^{2}(\pi l/2^{T})=\sin^{2}(\pi(2^{T}-l)/2^{T})
,

\{|H_{\mathrm{QBOOT}}(z)-H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)|\leq\varepsilon_{T}\}

contains four outcomes:

\mathcal{S}_{z}\;=\;\{l^{*},\;l^{*}+1,\;2^{T}-l^{*}-1,\;2^{T}-l^{*}\}.

We consider

\mathcal{S}^{+}=\{l^{*},l^{*}+1\}
and
\mathcal{S}^{-}=\{2^{T}-l^{*}-1,2^{T}-l^{*}\}
.

For
\mathcal{S}^{+}
,
using
\csc^{2}(x)\geq 1/x^{2}
(since
\sin x\leq x
) and Theorem
II.1
, we have

\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(Y_{z}\in\mathcal{S}^{+})\;\geq\;\frac{\sin^{2}(\phi\pi)}{2^{2T+1}}\left[\frac{2^{2T}}{\phi^{2}\pi^{2}}+\frac{2^{2T}}{(1-\phi)^{2}\pi^{2}}\right]\;=\;\frac{\sin^{2}(\phi\pi)}{2\pi^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{\phi^{2}}+\frac{1}{(1-\phi)^{2}}\right].

(S.30)

Similarly,

\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(Y_{z}\in\mathcal{S}^{-})\;\geq\;\frac{\sin^{2}(\phi\pi)}{2\pi^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{(1-\phi)^{2}}+\frac{1}{\phi^{2}}\right].

(S.31)

Adding (
S.30
) and (
S.31
):

q_{z}\;\geq\;\frac{\sin^{2}(\phi\pi)}{\pi^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{\phi^{2}}+\frac{1}{(1-\phi)^{2}}\right]\;=:\;h(\phi).

Evaluating at the minimum of
h(\phi)
, we have

h(\phi)\geq h(1/2)=\sin^{2}(\pi/2)\cdot[4+4]/\pi^{2}=8/\pi^{2}
.
Therefore

q_{z}\;\geq\;\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}\;>\;\frac{1}{2},

(S.32)

with equality when
\phi=1/2
, i.e., when
\tau_{z}
falls exactly halfway
between two adjacent grid points.

Median-of-
M
error bound(Theorem
II.7
).

Let
X_{m}=\mathbf{1}\{|H_{\mathrm{QBOOT}}^{(m)}(z)-H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)|>\varepsilon_{T}\}

be i.i.d. Bernoulli with success parameter
1-q_{z}\leq 1-8/\pi^{2}<1/2
.
Set
S=\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{m}\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(M,1-q_{z})
.
The median fails only if
S\geq\lceil M/2\rceil
.
For any
t>0
:

\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(S\geq M/2)\;\leq\;e^{-tM/2}\,\mathbb{E}[e^{tS}]\;=\;e^{-tM/2}\,(1-q_{z}e^{t}+q_{z})^{M}.

Minimizing over
t>0
by setting the derivative to zero gives

e^{t^{*}}=q_{z}/1-q_{z}
, and substituting:

\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits(S\geq M/2)\;\leq\;\left(2\sqrt{1-q_{z}\,q_{z}}\right)^{M}\;=\;\bigl(4(1-q_{z})q_{z}\bigr)^{M/2}.

(S.33)

Since

4(1-q_{z})q_{z}\;=\;4\!\left(1-\tfrac{8}{\pi^{2}}\right)\tfrac{8}{\pi^{2}}\;\approx\;0.614.

Combining the results, we have

\mathop{\rm Pr}\nolimits\!\left(|H_{\mathrm{QBOOT}}^{(M)}(z)-H_{\mathrm{BOOT}}(z)|>\varepsilon_{T}\right)\;\leq\;\rho^{M/2}.

Setting
\rho^{M/2}=\delta
, we have

M\geq 2\log(1/\delta)/\log(1/\rho_{0})\approx 4.11\log(1/\delta)
.
