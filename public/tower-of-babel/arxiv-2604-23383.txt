Probabilistic analysis of dual decomposition on two-stage stochastic integer programs
Santanu S. Dey, Marco Molinaro, Jingye Xu

Abstract
Two-stage stochastic integer programs provide a powerful framework for modeling decision-making under uncertainty, but they are notoriously difficult to solve at scale due to their high dimensionality and intrinsic nonconvexity. Decomposition-based algorithms such as Benders methods and Branch-and-Price (related dual decomposition methods) have become standard computational approaches for such problems and demonstrate excellent empirical performance in practice. Despite their widespread use, however, existing theoretical guarantees are almost exclusively based on worst-case analyses, which predict exponential convergence behavior in the problem dimension and fail to explain the strong performance observed in practice. In this paper, we present the first average-case analysis of Branch-and-Price for a broad class of two-stage stochastic binary integer programs. We study a stochastic-input model in which objective coefficients and constraint matrices are drawn at random and right-hand-side vectors scale with the decision dimension, while the number of constraints per scenario is fixed. Under this model, we prove that, with high probability, Branch-and-Price explores at most n O ⁡ ( log ⁡ s ) n^{O(\log s)} nodes, yielding a quasi-polynomial bound on the size of the search tree in typical instances, where n n denotes the decision dimension and s s the number of scenarios. A key ingredient of our analysis is an average-case bound on the integrality gap of the natural linear programming (LP) relaxation. We show that this gap shrinks at rate O ⁡ ( log ⁡ s ​ log 2 ​ n n ) O\!\left(\frac{\log s\log^{2}n}{n}\right) with high probability. This result is of independent interest, as it implies that the integrality gap grows only logarithmically with the number of scenarios on average. This helps explain why LP-based heuristics work well in practice, even in large-scale stochastic settings with an enormous number of scenarios, where LP relaxations with a large number of constraints are usually believed to be weak.

Introduction

Consider the following problem:

\displaystyle\textup{OPT}(\mathbf{b}):=\max_{\mathbf{x}}\

\displaystyle A^{(0)}\mathbf{x}^{(0)}+A^{(i)}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\leq\mathbf{b}^{(i)},\quad\forall i\in[s],

Problem (

In this paper, we focus on the setting in which all decision variables are binary

In practice, uncertainty is typically approximated by a finite set of scenarios

Under this approximation, problem (

Although the dimension of (

In the worst case, memory requirements, solution time, and solver performance

Decomposition methods are therefore particularly appealing for solving

By decomposing the problem into smaller, more tractable subproblems, these methods
reduce memory usage, improve computational efficiency, and enable scalable solution

Two general decomposition paradigms are most commonly used in the literature for
two-stage stochastic integer programs:
Benders decomposition
and

dual decomposition
. Benders decomposition is a cutting-plane approach that separates inequalities through
value-function approximations of the second-stage problem, whereas dual

For comprehensive surveys, we refer the reader to

Despite their strong empirical performance, there remains a substantial gap
between the practical success of decomposition methods and the current theoretical
understanding of their convergence behavior for two-stage stochastic integer
programs.
Existing convergence guarantees are almost exclusively derived from worst-case
analyses, which yield exponential complexity bounds of order
\Theta(2^{n})

[

,
23
]

Such bounds typically rely on the observation that there are at most
2^{n}
possible

eliminates at least one suboptimal candidate.
However, this pessimistic worst-case behavior is rarely observed in practice.
Extensive computational studies indicate that decomposition methods often converge
in only a small number of iterations, and in many cases even a single iteration
suffices to identify a high-quality or optimal solution
[
14
]
.
This pronounced discrepancy motivates the need for a refined, average-case
analysis of the convergence behavior of decomposition methods.

In this paper, we focus on the dual decomposition approach.
Because problem (
1

duality gap between (
1

nonanticipativity reformulation. As a result, additional branching is required in practice to close this gap and
recover an integral optimal solution.
This leads to algorithmic frameworks commonly referred to as

,
column generation
, or

To move beyond worst-case analysis, we adopt the common perspective of considering random input instances and focus on average-case performance.

2
Dual Decomposition and Branch-and-Price Algorithm

In this section, we provide a detailed description of a standard Branch-and-Price algorithm
[
3
,
17
,
10
]
. Similar to the standard Branch-and-Bound algorithm, which relies on LP relaxations, Branch-and-Price can be viewed as an enumeration procedure guided by a convex relaxation, where each node solves a dual decomposition problem (also known as column generation, and as “Lagrangian relaxation” in the stochastic programming literature).

Since in later sections we will also consider Lagrangian relaxations of linear programs, we adopt the term
dual decomposition relaxation
here to clearly distinguish between these two types of relaxations.

Dual decomposition relaxation.

This continuous relaxation is obtained by convexifying each combination of the first-stage block and a single scenario block separately (whereas the original problem is equivalent to jointly convexifying the first-stage block together with all scenario blocks). More precisely, it is formally defined as

\displaystyle\textup{DUAL}:=\max_{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle s\cdot\left\langle{\mathbf{c}^{(0)}},{\mathbf{x}^{(0)}}\right\rangle+\sum_{i\in[s]}\left\langle{\mathbf{c}^{(i)}},{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}}\right\rangle

(2)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{x}^{(0)},\mathbf{x}^{(i)})\in\conv(\mathcal{X}^{(i)}),\quad\forall i\in[s],

where

\mathcal{X}^{(i)}:=\left\{(\mathbf{y}^{(0)},\mathbf{y}^{(i)})\;\middle|\;\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{}}A^{(0)}\mathbf{y}^{(0)}+A^{(i)}\mathbf{y}^{(i)}\leq\mathbf{b}^{(i)},\\
A^{(0)}\mathbf{y}^{(0)}\leq\mathbf{b}^{(j)},\quad\forall j\in[s],\\
\mathbf{y}^{(0)},\mathbf{y}^{(i)}\text{ binary}.\end{array}\right\}.

We note that solving such a relaxation can be accomplished via standard column generation by solving multiple integer linear programs (ILPs) whose size is independent of the number of scenarios (more precisely, each has
2n
variables and
2m
linear constraints). Additionally, the Branch-and-Price algorithm only needs the optimal objective value of this relaxation, which can also be obtained by performing a Lagrangian relaxation of the nonanticipativity constraints. This latter method, which also uses multiple solves of ILPs whose size is independent of
s
, is usually the one employed in practice. See, for example,
[
4
]
for more details.

Branch-and-Price.

We consider the Branch-and-Price algorithm based on the dual decomposition relaxation. More precisely, we instantiate the elements of the Branch-and-Price framework as follows (see
[
17
,
10
]
for a general description of Branch-and-Price):

1.

Node relaxation:
In each node
N
of the Branch-and-Price tree, we use the value
\textup{DUAL}(N)
of the dual decomposition relaxation (
2
) associated with that node (i.e., with the appropriate variables
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}
fixed to
0/1
).

2.

Branching rule:
Branch on any
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}
variable that has not yet been fixed at the node; it does not need to be fractional.

3.

Node selection rule:
“Best-bound,” i.e., select the unpruned node with the largest computed dual value.

4.

Finding an incumbent solution:
Whenever we are at a node
N
where all variables
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}
are fixed to
0/1
(call the fixing
\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}
), we compute a solution
\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)},\ldots,\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(s)}
such that
\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)},\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)},\ldots,\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(s)}
is feasible for the original problem and has value at least
\textup{DUAL}(N)
.

3
Size of the Branch-and-Price Tree

In this section, we prove Lemma
1.2
, namely, that as long as the integrality gap of problem (
1
) is

O_{m}\!\left(\frac{s\log s\log^{2}n}{n}\right)
with probability at least
1-\delta
, then with probability at least
1-\delta-\frac{1}{n}
, the Branch-and-Price algorithm solves (
1
) after exploring at most
n^{O_{m}(\log s)}
nodes. (In Section
4
, we prove this assumption on the integrality gap, thereby obtaining an unconditional bound on the size of the Branch-and-Price tree.)

The high-level idea for proving this result is as follows:

1.

We consider the Lagrangian formulation (using an optimal dual solution) of the standard LP relaxation, and show that the primal optimal solution associated with each node in our Branch-and-Price (BP) tree has Lagrangian value at least
OPT
(Lemma
3.1
). Intuitively, this holds because the “best-bound” rule guarantees that we never explore nodes with dual value below
OPT
, and the Lagrangian value is at least as high as our dual bound from (
2
).

2.

We can associate with each node in our BP tree a
binary
vector with Lagrangian value at least
OPT
, and at most
n
internal nodes are associated with the same vector. This implies an upper bound on the size of our BP tree based on the number of such binary vectors with high Lagrangian value (Lemma
3.2
).

This is accomplished by appropriately rounding the primal optimal solution at each node, using the fact that the Lagrangian value is linear once the Lagrangian dual has been fixed.

3.

We prove that, with high probability, there are only
n^{O_{m}(\log s)}
binary vectors with such high Lagrangian value, as long as the integrality gap is small.

While the overall argument is similar to that of
[
11
]
, the key
difference is that the original bound is exponential in the number of
constraints. In our setting, this number is
m\cdot s
, which would lead to an
exponential dependence on the number of scenarios. By contrast, our bound
scales as
n^{O_{m}(\log s)}
.
To obtain this improved dependence on
s
, we work only with the projection onto
the first-stage variables
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}
, that is, we essentially restrict
attention to the matrix
A^{(0)}
, which has only
m
rows. This approach
exploits the fact that the relaxation (
2
) convexifies each
scenario jointly with the first-stage variables, and is therefore stronger than
the LP relaxation considered in
[
11
]
.

To formalize this argument, consider the standard LP relaxation of the full problem:

\displaystyle\textup{OPT}_{\text{LP}}:=\max

\displaystyle s\cdot\left\langle{\mathbf{c}^{(0)}},{\mathbf{x}^{(0)}}\right\rangle+\sum_{i\in[s]}\left\langle{\mathbf{c}^{(i)}},{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}}\right\rangle

(3a)

s.t.

\displaystyle A^{(0)}\mathbf{x}^{(0)}+A^{(i)}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\leq\mathbf{b}^{(i)},\quad\forall i\in[s],

(3b)

\displaystyle\textbf{0}\leq\mathbf{x}\leq\textbf{1}.

(3c)

Define the integrality gap by

\Delta:=\textup{OPT}_{\text{LP}}-\textup{OPT}.

Consider the Lagrangian relaxation of this linear program:

\displaystyle\textup{OPT}_{\text{LP}}=

\displaystyle\min_{\mu\geq 0}\max_{0\leq\mathbf{x}\leq 1}\left(s\cdot\left\langle{\mathbf{c}^{(0)}},{\mathbf{x}^{(0)}}\right\rangle+\sum_{r\in[s]}\left\langle{\mathbf{c}^{(r)}},{\mathbf{x}^{(r)}}\right\rangle\right)+\left(\sum_{r\in[s]}\left\langle{\mu^{(r)}},{\mathbf{b}^{(r)}-A^{(0)}\mathbf{x}^{(0)}-A^{(r)}\mathbf{x}^{(r)}}\right\rangle\right)

(4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\mu\geq 0}\max_{0\leq\mathbf{x}\leq 1}s\cdot\left\langle{\mathbf{c}^{(0)}-(A^{(0)})^{\top}\bigg(\frac{1}{s}\sum_{r}\mu^{(r)}\bigg)},{\mathbf{x}^{(0)}}\right\rangle+\sum_{r\in[s]}\left\langle{\mathbf{c}^{(r)}-(A^{(r)})^{\top}\mu^{(r)}},{\mathbf{x}^{(r)}}\right\rangle+\sum_{r\in[s]}\left\langle{\mu^{(r)}},{\mathbf{b}^{(r)}}\right\rangle.

Let
(\mathbf{x}_{*},\mu_{*})
be the saddle point of this Lagrangian. To simplify the notation, let
\overline{\mu}_{*}:=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{i\in[s]}\mu^{(i)}_{*}
. Given any first-stage point
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}
, let

\Gamma(\mathbf{x}^{(0)}):=s\cdot\left\langle{\mathbf{c}^{(0)}-(A^{(0)})^{\top}\overline{\mu}_{*}},{\mathbf{x}^{(0)}}\right\rangle+\underbrace{\sum_{r\in[s]}\left\langle{\mathbf{c}^{(r)}-(A^{(r)})^{\top}\mu_{*}^{(r)}},{\mathbf{x}^{(r)}_{*}}\right\rangle+\sum_{r\in[s]}\left\langle{\mu_{*}^{(r)}},{\mathbf{b}^{(r)}}\right\rangle}_{\text{constant}}

be the “Lagrangian value” of this point; note that we are using the optimal dual
\mu_{*}
and also the optimal primal
\mathbf{x}^{(r)}_{*}
for all the scenarios. Define the set of first-stage binary solutions with
\Gamma
-value at least
OPT
as (recall that
OPT
is the integer optimum)

\displaystyle\Omega:=\{\mathbf{x}^{(0)}\in\{0,1\}^{n}:\Gamma(\mathbf{x}^{(0)})\geq\textup{OPT}\}.

The first claim is that for every node in the Branch-and-Price tree, the primal optimal solution for the relaxation at that node has
\Gamma
-value at least
OPT
.

Lemma 3.1

Consider any internal node in the Branch-and-Price tree, and let
\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)},\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)},\ldots,\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(s)}
be the optimal primal solution of the relaxation (
2
) at this node. Then
\Gamma(\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)})\geq\textup{OPT}
.

Proof:
Let
\textup{DUAL}(N)
denote the exact optimal value of the relaxation
(
2
) associated with node
N
in the BP tree.
During the execution of BP, the value
\textup{DUAL}(N)
is used as the
node-selection criterion under the best-bound rule.

We claim that every internal node
N
explored by BP satisfies

\textup{DUAL}(N)\geq\textup{OPT}.

By contradiction, suppose there exists a node
N
with

\textup{DUAL}(N)<\textup{OPT}
that is selected for branching. By the best-bound
rule, this implies that for every other open node
N^{\prime}
in the BP tree,
\textup{DUAL}(N^{\prime})\leq\textup{DUAL}(N)<\textup{OPT}
.
On the other hand, there exists at least one node
N^{*}
(open or pruned) whose
relaxation contains the globally optimal integral solution of the original
problem. For such a node, we have

\textup{OPT}\leq\textup{DUAL}(N^{*}).

Therefore,
N^{*}
cannot be an open node at the time
N
is selected, since all
open nodes have
\textup{DUAL}(\cdot)<\textup{OPT}
. Hence,
N^{*}
must already be
pruned.
If
N^{*}
was pruned by integrality, then an incumbent solution of value
OPT

has already been found. If
N^{*}
was pruned by bounds, then an incumbent of
value at least
OPT
must have been identified prior to pruning. In either
case, the current incumbent value is
OPT
.
However, since
\textup{DUAL}(N)<\textup{OPT}
, node
N
would be pruned by the
bounding rule and thus could not be selected for branching, contradicting our
assumption. We conclude that every internal node of the BP tree satisfies

\textup{DUAL}(N)\geq\textup{OPT}
.

To conclude the proof, we show that for any node
N
in the BP tree, the optimal
primal solution

\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)},\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)},\ldots,\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(s)}

of its relaxation (
2
) has
\Gamma
-value at least
\textup{DUAL}(N)
, namely,

\displaystyle\Gamma(\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)})\;\geq\;s\cdot\left\langle{\mathbf{c}^{(0)}},{\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}}\right\rangle+\sum_{r\in[s]}\left\langle{\mathbf{c}^{(r)}},{\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(r)}}\right\rangle.

(5)

To prove this, we expand the definition of
\Gamma
and use the
fact that
\mathbf{x}_{*}^{(r)}
maximizes

\left\langle{\mathbf{c}^{(r)}-(A^{(r)})^{\top}\mu_{*}^{(r)}},{\mathbf{x}^{(r)}}\right\rangle
to obtain

\displaystyle\Gamma(\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)})

\displaystyle\geq s\cdot\left\langle{\mathbf{c}^{(0)}-(A^{(0)})^{\top}\overline{\mu}_{*}},{\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}}\right\rangle+\sum_{r\in[s]}\left\langle{\mathbf{c}^{(r)}-(A^{(r)})^{\top}\mu_{*}^{(r)}},{\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(r)}}\right\rangle+\sum_{r\in[s]}\left\langle{\mu_{*}^{(r)}},{\mathbf{b}^{(r)}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=s\cdot\left\langle{\mathbf{c}^{(0)}},{\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}}\right\rangle+\sum_{r\in[s]}\left\langle{\mathbf{c}^{(r)}},{\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(r)}}\right\rangle-\sum_{r\in[s]}\left\langle{\mu_{*}^{(r)}},{A^{(0)}\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}+A^{(r)}\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(r)}}\right\rangle+\sum_{r\in[s]}\left\langle{\mu_{*}^{(r)}},{\mathbf{b}^{(r)}}\right\rangle.

(6)

Since the solution

\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)},\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)},\ldots,\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(s)}

is feasible for the original constraints, i.e.,

A^{(0)}\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}+A^{(r)}\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(r)}\leq\mathbf{b}^{(r)}\quad\text{for all }r\in[s],

and
\mu_{*}^{(r)}\geq 0
, the last two terms of (
6
) sum to a nonnegative value. Therefore,

\Gamma(\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)})\geq s\cdot\left\langle{\mathbf{c}^{(0)}},{\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}}\right\rangle+\sum_{r\in[s]}\left\langle{\mathbf{c}^{(r)}},{\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(r)}}\right\rangle,

which proves (
5
). This completes the proof of the lemma.

\Box

Given this result, the point
\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}
in the lemma would belong to the set
\Omega
if
\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}
had only
0/1
coordinates. However, this point is only an optimal solution of the relaxation (
2
) (at some node) and thus may have fractional coordinates. Since
\Gamma(\cdot)
is a linear function, we can round this point to a
0/1
point with at least as large a
\Gamma
-value. More precisely, for a node
N
of our BP tree, define
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}_{N}
as the argmax

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{(0)}_{N}:=\argmax\Big\{\Gamma(\mathbf{x}^{(0)})\,:\,\mathbf{x}^{(0)}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\textrm{ and $\mathbf{x}^{(0)}$ satisfies the variable fixings of the BP tree at $N$}\Big\}\,.

We now have that these solutions
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}_{N}
belong to
\Omega
, and using this we can bound the size of our BP tree by the size of
\Omega
.

Lemma 3.2

For every internal node
N
in the BP tree,
\mathbf{x}_{N}^{(0)}\in\Omega
. Moreover, for each
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}\in\Omega
, there are at most
n
internal nodes
N
in the BP tree such that
\mathbf{x}_{N}^{(0)}=\mathbf{x}^{(0)}
. In particular, the size of the BP tree is at most
2n\cdot|\Omega|+1
.

Proof:
For the first part: for an internal node
N
in the BP tree, the solution
\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}
from Lemma
3.1
is feasible for the argmax defining
\mathbf{x}_{N}^{(0)}
, which implies
\Gamma(\mathbf{x}_{N}^{(0)})\geq\Gamma(\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)})\geq\textup{OPT}
, and so
\mathbf{x}_{N}^{(0)}\in\Omega
.

For the second property: if two internal nodes
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
are not on the same path of the BP tree, then by looking at the common ancestor of these nodes we observe that there is some coordinate
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}_{j}
that is fixed to
0
in

To bound the additive integrality gap

\Pr\bigg(\Delta>\Omega_{m}\bigg(\frac{{sf}\log^{2}n}{n}\bigg)~\bigg|~Good\bigg)\leq{\frac{(s+1)}{n^{2}}+(s+1)\pi^{f}}.

\displaystyle E2:~~\begin{array}[]{l}\textrm{$\forall i\in[s]{\cup\{0\}},\|\mu^{(i)}_{\diamond}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{2\alpha}$ but}\textrm{$\exists i\in[s]{\cup\{0\}}$ such that $\|\mu^{(i)}_{\diamond}\|_{1}\geq\frac{f\log n}{2\alpha n}$ and $\big|\mathcal{N}_{0}^{(i)}\big|<\frac{n}{2^{m+{2}}}\cdot\min\Big\{\frac{1}{4}\big\lVert\mu^{(i)}_{\diamond}\big\rVert_{1},1\Big\}$}.\end{array}

The next lemma bounds the probability of the first event

Lemma 4.3

With probability at least

for all blocks

The second event

Lemma 4.4

Under the condition of Theorem

,

Proof of Lemma

Recall that the fact that

\textup{OPT}_{\text{LP}}(\mathbf{b}_{\diamond})=\max_{0\leq\mathbf{x}\leq 1}\min_{\mu\geq 0}L^{\mathrm{std}}_{\mathbf{b}_{\diamond}}(\mathbf{x},\mu)=\max_{0\leq\mathbf{x}\leq 1}L^{\mathrm{std}}_{\mathbf{b}_{\diamond}}(\mathbf{x},\mu_{\diamond})=\min_{\mu\geq 0}L^{\mathrm{std}}_{\mathbf{b}_{\diamond}}(\mathbf{x}_{\diamond},\mu),

that is,
\mathbf{x}_{\diamond}
and
\mu_{\diamond}
are optimal primal and dual
solutions, respectively.
Since
L^{\mathrm{std}}
is bilinear and the feasible regions are defined by box
constraints, the following complementary slackness conditions hold.

(cs.i)

For all
i\in[s]
, we have
\langle{\mu_{\diamond}^{(i)}},{\mathbf{b}^{(i)}_{\diamond}-A^{(0)}\mathbf{x}^{(0)}_{\diamond}-A^{(i)}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{\diamond}}\rangle=0
(if we had “
<
” then
\textup{OPT}_{\text{LP}}(\mathbf{b}_{\diamond})=\min_{\mu\geq 0}L^{std}_{\mathbf{b}_{\diamond}}(\mathbf{x}_{\diamond},\mu)
would be
-\infty

(cs.ii)

For every

\mathbf{x}_{\diamond j}^{(i)}=1
(both by optimality of
\mathbf{x}_{\diamond j}^{(i)}
) .

Recall that the integrality gap is

\Delta:=\textup{OPT}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{b})-\textup{OPT}(\mathbf{b}).

The LP optimum can be upper bounded using the dual vector
\mu_{\diamond}
as

\textup{OPT}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{b})\leq\max_{0\leq\mathbf{x}\leq 1}L^{\mathrm{std}}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{x},\mu_{\diamond}).

Moreover,
\mathbf{x}_{\diamond}
remains an optimal maximizer for this problem with

\mathbf{b}
, since
(cs.ii)
shows that the optimal choice of
\mathbf{x}

depends only on the reduced costs and is therefore independent of the

Hence,

\textup{OPT}_{\mathrm{LP}}(\mathbf{b})\leq L^{\mathrm{std}}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{x}_{\diamond},\mu_{\diamond}).

On the other hand, the integer optimum

is lower bounded by the value

s\langle\mathbf{c}^{(0)},\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}\rangle+\sum_{i\in[s]}\langle\mathbf{c}^{(i)},\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}\rangle

of the feasible integral solution
\bar{\mathbf{x}}
.

Combining these bounds and adding and subtracting

\sum_{i\in[s]}\langle\mu^{(i)}_{\diamond},A^{(0)}\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}+A^{(i)}\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}\rangle
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta\leq\;

\displaystyle s\cdot\big\langle\mathbf{c}^{(0)}-(\mu^{(0)}_{\diamond})^{\top}A^{(0)},\mathbf{x}^{(0)}_{\diamond}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}\big\rangle+\sum_{i\in[s]}\big\langle\mathbf{c}^{(i)}-(\mu^{(i)}_{\diamond})^{\top}A^{(i)},\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{\diamond}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}\big\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i\in[s]}\big\langle\mu^{(i)}_{\diamond},\mathbf{b}^{(i)}-A^{(0)}\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}-A^{(i)}\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}\big\rangle.

(13)

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathbf{c}^{(0)}-(\mu^{(0)}_{\diamond})^{\top}A^{(0)}\,},{\,\mathbf{x}^{(0)}_{\diamond}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\Big(c^{(0)}_{j}-\left\langle{\mu_{\diamond}^{(0)}},{\mathbf{a}^{(0)}_{j}}\right\rangle\Big)\cdot(\mathbf{x}^{(0)}_{\diamond j}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}_{j})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j:\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}_{j}>\mathbf{x}^{(0)}_{\diamond j}}\Big(\langle\mu^{(0)}_{\diamond},\mathbf{a}^{(0)}_{j}\rangle-c^{(0)}_{j}\Big),

(14)

where we justify the contribution of each term

\mathbf{x}^{(0)}_{\diamond j}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}_{j}=0
and the term

(cs.ii)
discussed above gives that

\displaystyle\left\langle{c^{(i)}-(\mu^{(i)}_{\diamond})^{\top}A^{(i)}\,},{\,\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{\diamond}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}}\right\rangle\leq\sum_{j:\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}_{j}>\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{\diamond j}}\Big(\langle\mu^{(i)}_{\diamond},\mathbf{a}^{(i)}_{j}\rangle-c^{(i)}_{j}\Big).

\displaystyle~~~+\sum_{i\in[s]}\Big\|\mu^{(i)}_{\diamond}\Big\|_{1}\cdot\Big\|d{\bm{1}}-A^{(i)}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}-\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{\diamond})\Big\|_{\infty}.

4.3
Bounding the Size of the Optimal Dual (Proof of Lemma

To simplify the notation, let
\xi:=\frac{2}{4\beta_{\min}-1}=\frac{1}{2\alpha}>1

1-\frac{1}{n^{2}}

\|\mu^{(i)}_{\diamond}\|_{\infty}\leq\xi
for all blocks

comes for free: this is because
\mu^{(0)}_{\diamond}
is defined as the average
\mu^{(0)}_{\diamond}=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\mu^{(i)}_{\diamond}
and, since norms are convex, by Jensen’s inequality we have
\|\mu^{(0)}_{\diamond}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{s}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\|\mu^{(i)}_{\diamond}\|_{\infty}
.

To prove the desired result, we say that an instance of our problem is

if every row

satisfies the following:

1.

\sum_{j\in[n]}A^{(0)}_{r,j}\leq\frac{n}{2}+\sqrt{n\ln snm}

i\in[s]

Notice that, for a fixed row

\mathbb{E}\sum_{j\in[n]}A^{(0)}_{r,j}=\frac{n}{2}
; moreover, since
\mathbf{c}^{(i)}_{j}
is also uniformly distributed in
[0,1]
, the probability that
\mathbf{c}^{(i)}_{j}\geq\xi\cdot A^{(i)}_{r,j}
can be seen to be
\frac{1}{2\xi}
(this uses the fact

\frac{n}{2\xi}
of the
j

Claim 4.2

If
sm\geq 2

1-\frac{1}{n^{2}}
the instance is

.

Proof:
Fix a row
r\in[m]

\Pr\bigg(\sum_{j\in[n]}A^{(0)}_{r,j}>\frac{n}{2}+\sqrt{n\ln snm}\bigg)\,\leq\,e^{-2\ln snm}=\frac{1}{(snm)^{2}}.

Taking a union bound over all rows, Item 1 in the definition of a typical instance holds with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{(sn)^{2}m}
. Also, fix a row
r\in[m]
and a block
i\in[s]
, and let

be the indicator that

5
Conclusion

This paper provides a first rigorous average-case analysis of decomposition-based methods for two-stage stochastic integer programs, with a particular focus on dual decomposition and the Branch-and-Price framework. Under a natural stochastic-input model, we show that the Branch-and-Price search tree has quasi-polynomial size with high probability, substantially improving upon the classical worst-case exponential bounds. A key ingredient in the analysis is an average-case bound on the integrality gap of the natural LP relaxation, which shows that the gap is small even when the number of scenarios grows.
An important future direction is to develop a comparable average-case analysis for Benders-type methods. While this paper focuses on Branch-and-Price and related dual decomposition schemes, Benders decomposition is another widely used approach for two-stage stochastic integer programs. Establishing average-case guarantees for Benders-type algorithms would be an important step toward a more unified theoretical understanding of why decomposition methods perform so well in practice.
