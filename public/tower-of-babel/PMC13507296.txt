The influence of students’ perceptions of teacher criticism styles on their intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior in physical education: A structural equation model based on academic emotion mechanisms
Authors: Jing Bai, Qiaoni Shen, Kai Huang, Yingchen C Bi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDisruptive classroom behavior is prevalent in physical education classes. Teachers typically respond to such behaviors with criticism, but different criticism styles may evoke distinct emotional responses in students. The present study examines how students’ perceptions of constructive and destructive criticism used by physical education teachers in response to disruptive classroom behavior influence students’ intention to improve such behavior through the mediating roles of classroom anger, classroom anxiety, and classroom helplessness. Questionnaire data were collected from 583 Chinese middle school students, and the mediation effects in the model were estimated using the product-of-coefficients approach with bias-corrected Monte Carlo bootstrapping. (1) The indirect effects of constructive criticism through classroom anger and classroom helplessness were significant, whereas the indirect effect through classroom anxiety was not significant. (2) The indirect effects of destructive criticism through classroom anger, classroom anxiety, and classroom helplessness were all significant. (3) Constructive criticism is significantly more effective than destructive criticism in enhancing students’ intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior. In the context of physical education classes in China, students’ disruptive classroom behavior may elicit different types of teacher criticism, and students’ perceptions of constructive and destructive criticism from teachers further influence their intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior through academic emotions. Constructive criticism is more likely than destructive criticism to promote students’ behavioral regulation and improvement, and physical education teachers should use specific and improvement-oriented constructive criticism more frequently.Keywords: Constructive criticism, Destructive criticism, Disruptive classroom behavior, Academic emotionsSubject terms: Education, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Reducing disruptive classroom behavior has long been a central concern in primary and secondary education research. Disruptive classroom behavior in physical education refers to student behavior that violates classroom norms, interferes with the instructional process, and reduces instructional effectiveness1,2. Because of features such as the openness of the physical education setting, numerous sources of distraction, a high degree of freedom of movement, and frequent teacher–student interaction, physical education classes constitute a setting in which disruptive behavior frequently occurs3. A recent study found that 89% of secondary school teachers reported varying degrees of disruptive behavior in their classrooms4. Such behaviors include both minor classroom disruptions (e.g., giggling and speaking out of turn) and more serious forms of misconduct (e.g., bullying)5. Disruptive classroom behavior not only disrupts classroom order but also affects classroom climate and learning outcomes6,7.

Teachers commonly use criticism as a classroom management strategy to address disruptive behavior8,9. Teacher criticism helps students recognize inappropriate behavior and correct it, but its effectiveness largely depends on how it is delivered10,11. Criticism can be categorized into constructive criticism and destructive criticism: constructive criticism is based on respect and support and emphasizes specific problems and feasible suggestions for improvement, whereas destructive criticism is often accompanied by blame or negation and lacks clear behavioral guidance and improvement suggestions12. While teacher criticism serves a behavior-correcting function, it also elicits complex emotional responses in students13,14. In classroom contexts, constructive criticism points out specific problematic behaviors and provides feasible suggestions, making students more likely to interpret teachers’ feedback as behavioral guidance rather than a denial of their personal value, thereby alleviating classroom anger, anxiety, and helplessness. In contrast, destructive criticism often involves blame, negative evaluation, or humiliation, which may lead students to perceive such criticism as a negation of their own competence and personal worth, thereby intensifying classroom anger, anxiety, and helplessness. Teachers’ criticism styles are not only associated with students’ academic emotions but also may be related to their intention to improve behavior through academic emotions15,16.

A large body of research has examined disruptive classroom behavior and teacher criticism; however, several limitations remain. First, prior studies have largely treated teacher criticism as a single classroom management strategy and have lacked systematic comparison and in-depth analysis of constructive criticism and destructive criticism. Second, in studies concerning disruptive classroom behavior and teacher feedback, academic emotions have often been regarded as background factors influencing disruptive behavior or as secondary outcome variables, and have not been examined as distinct variables in research designs. Accordingly, the present study proposes and tests a parallel mediation model in which disruptive classroom behavior influences students’ classroom anger, classroom anxiety, and classroom helplessness through two pathways—constructive criticism and destructive criticism—which in turn influence students’ intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior. By testing the mechanisms of different criticism styles, this study aims to deepen understanding of the mechanisms underlying teacher feedback in physical education and to provide theoretical and practical implications for classroom behavior intervention.

Theoretical framework: control–value theory

Control–value theory provides a theoretical basis for understanding how teachers’ criticism styles influence students’ academic emotions. According to this theory, students’ emotions in classroom settings are primarily determined by their perceived control over the classroom situation and their value appraisals17. Perceived control refers to students’ subjective judgments about whether they are capable of completing learning tasks17. Value refers to students’ perceptions of the importance and usefulness of learning tasks, activities, or outcomes17,18. Different combinations of control and value appraisals may elicit distinct emotional responses in students17. Specifically, classroom anger is often associated with relatively high perceived control and low value appraisal. When students believe that they can complete classroom tasks or adjust their behavior but feel dissatisfied with classroom activities or evaluation methods, they are more likely to feel classroom anger. Classroom anxiety is often associated with high value appraisal and uncertain or insufficient perceived control. When students attach great importance to classroom tasks or outcomes but are uncertain whether they can successfully complete these tasks, they are more likely to feel classroom anxiety. Classroom helplessness is often associated with low perceived control and high value appraisal. When students value classroom tasks or outcomes but believe that they are unable to complete these tasks through their own effort, they are more likely to feel classroom helplessness17.

In physical education settings, constructive criticism may enhance students’ control and value appraisals by emphasizing the significance of classroom tasks and providing specific directions for improvement, thereby alleviating negative academic emotions such as classroom anxiety to some extent. In contrast, destructive criticism often makes students feel denied or blamed, weakens their perceived control over behavioral improvement, and may consequently trigger negative academic emotions such as classroom anger. These academic emotions not only influence how students understand and accept teachers’ criticism but also further affect their willingness to improve disruptive classroom behavior15,18. Therefore, control–value theory provides a theoretical foundation for explaining how students’ perceptions of teachers’ criticism styles are associated with students’ intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior through academic emotions.

The influence of constructive criticism on students’ intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior

Common disruptive behaviors in physical education classes include failure to follow directions, distraction or disturbance of others, low engagement or irresponsibility, aggression, and poor self-management6. When confronted with students’ disruptive classroom behavior, teachers often respond with criticism, the core purpose of which is to encourage students to reflect on and improve such behavior, thereby facilitating the restoration of classroom order and the continuation of instructional activities19. Although criticism can help students reflect on and correct inappropriate behavior, it may also elicit academic emotions.

Academic emotions refer to students’ emotional experiences in learning-related activities20. Previous research has shown that academic emotions play an important role in the learning process, as they not only influence students’ learning motivation21, cognitive processing22, and classroom engagement23, but also affect learning outcomes and academic performance24. In educational contexts, students may experience negative academic emotions such as boredom, anger, and anxiety, as well as positive academic emotions such as pride, hope, and enjoyment17,25. Because teacher criticism is more likely to elicit negative academic emotions, the present study focuses on the influence of teachers’ criticism styles on three negative academic emotions: classroom anger, classroom anxiety, and classroom helplessness.

Classroom anger refers to an emotional experience characterized primarily by anger and irritation that arises in the classroom when students feel rejected or dissatisfied with the manner and content of teacher criticism18,26. In interactive instructional settings such as physical education classes, classroom anger is likely to arise when teacher criticism is interpreted by students as negative or accusatory feedback. In contrast, when teachers use constructive criticism to address disruptive classroom behavior, they clearly identify the problem in a respectful and supportive manner and provide specific and feasible suggestions for improvement, thereby reducing students’ perceived threat of criticism27. Even when feedback itself is critical in nature, students are likely to interpret it as behavioral guidance rather than as a negative evaluation of themselves when teachers express it in a gentle and respectful manner28. Therefore, constructive criticism helps alleviate classroom anger. As classroom anger decreases, students become more receptive to teachers’ feedback, which in turn makes it easier for them to understand classroom norms and strengthens their intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1a. When students perceive teachers’ responses to disruptive classroom behavior as constructive criticism, their classroom anger will be lower, which will be associated with stronger intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior.

Classroom anxiety refers to an emotional experience characterized by tension and uneasiness that arises in the classroom when students worry about failure, fear criticism, or face uncertain or ambiguous tasks29,30. According to control–value theory, classroom anxiety originates from the interaction between students’ perceived control and value appraisals in classroom contexts. When students perceive a learning task as highly valuable but experience low perceived control, anxiety is more likely to arise31. Research indicates that constructive feedback may help reduce students’ concern about failure by emphasizing effort and possibilities for improvement, thereby alleviating classroom anxiety12,27. Students’ perceptions of teachers’ supportive feedback and step-by-step guidance may also contribute to enhancing students’ perceived control and alleviating anxiety28. Further empirical research has also shown that, in courses such as health education, task-oriented and specific feedback may reduce students’ doubts about their competence and may alleviate anxiety by providing clear suggestions for improvement, thereby further promoting their engagement in subsequent learning32. When students perceive teachers’ responses to disruptive classroom behavior as constructive criticism, such criticism may not only reduce students’ perceived threat from criticism but also help alleviate classroom anxiety by enhancing students’ perceived control. As anxiety decreases, students are more likely to respond to teachers’ behavioral expectations in a rational and goal-directed manner, thereby strengthening their intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1b. When students perceive teachers’ responses to disruptive classroom behavior as constructive criticism, their classroom anxiety will be lower, which will be associated with stronger intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior.

Classroom helplessness refers to a negative emotional experience characterized by reduced self-efficacy and a tendency to give up after repeated frustration; it is manifested in beliefs that effort is unlikely to change outcomes, a lack of perceived control, limited positive expectations about learning, and feelings of helplessness and motivational exhaustion33. In the context of teacher feedback, the development of classroom helplessness is consistent with learned helplessness theory. When students repeatedly experience academic failure or frequently receive negative evaluations, their self-efficacy gradually diminishes, leading to a sense of powerlessness toward learning tasks. If teachers’ feedback includes excessive corrective suggestions or lacks a clear pathway for improvement, students often perceive themselves as unable to meet teachers’ expectations, leading to classroom helplessness34. Constructive criticism can enhance students’ self-efficacy and perceived control by providing specific and feasible suggestions and emphasizing the possibility of improvement, thereby effectively alleviating classroom helplessness. As classroom helplessness decreases, students are more likely to respond positively to teachers’ expectations, thereby strengthening their intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1c. When students perceive teachers’ responses to disruptive classroom behavior as constructive criticism, their classroom helplessness will be lower, which will be associated with stronger intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior.

The influence of destructive criticism on students’ intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior

Although constructive criticism is regarded as an important strategy for promoting improvement in students’ disruptive classroom behavior, in physical education classes teachers may still respond to students’ disruptive classroom behavior in a punitive manner characterized by reprimand and negative evaluation. Such criticism constitutes destructive criticism, whose core features include the absence of specific and feasible suggestions for improvement, negative content, a focus on the individual rather than the behavior itself, and emotionally charged expressions35. Destructive criticism may suppress inappropriate behavior in the short term but often fails to promote sustained behavioral improvement36. More importantly, such criticism may also elicit other maladaptive behaviors, such as avoidance behavior37 and increased aggression38, and may even exacerbate disruptive classroom behavior39.

When teachers’ criticism contains elements of blame, disapproval, or public humiliation, students may perceive it as a threat to their competence or self-worth and feel anger. Anger not only reduces students’ receptiveness to feedback but also increases the likelihood of future disruptive behavior16,40,41. Therefore, when students exhibit disruptive classroom behavior, teachers’ destructive criticism such as blame and humiliation is likely to elicit classroom anger. Such anger not only weakens students’ receptiveness to teachers’ feedback but may further provoke oppositional behavior, which exacerbates disruptive classroom behavior. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H2a. When students perceive teachers’ responses to disruptive classroom behavior as destructive criticism, their classroom anger will be higher, which will be associated with weaker intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior.

In physical education classes, destructive criticism not only elicits anger but is also likely to elicit classroom anxiety. Self-worth theory further suggests that teacher evaluations characterized by negative evaluation and blame threaten students’ self-worth, leading them to protect themselves through avoidance or withdrawal, thereby developing classroom anxiety42. In the highly visible instructional environment of physical education classes, where peer comparison is salient, such criticism further intensifies students’ concerns about failure and increases their sensitivity to negative evaluation, thereby inducing classroom anxiety. Previous research has shown that classroom anxiety consumes individuals’ cognitive resources and interferes with attentional control, thereby reducing information processing efficiency43. In educational contexts, this emotional state further weakens learning motivation and task engagement and makes students more likely to adopt coping strategies such as avoidance, withdrawal, or passive participation rather than actively improving their behavior. Therefore, in situations involving disruptive classroom behavior, destructive criticism characterized by highly negative content or emotional expression may significantly increase students’ classroom anxiety, thereby reducing their intention to comply with classroom norms and improve disruptive classroom behavior. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H2b. When students perceive teachers’ responses to disruptive classroom behavior as destructive criticism, their classroom anxiety will be higher, which will be associated with weaker intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior.

With continued exposure to destructive criticism and repeated failure, students’ perceived control in the classroom context and self-efficacy gradually weaken, and their emotional experience may shift from temporary tension and worry to classroom helplessness. According to learned helplessness theory, when students repeatedly experience failure, are unable to meet teachers’ expectations, or continually receive negative feedback, they are likely to develop reduced self-efficacy and a tendency to give up, which can lead to classroom helplessness. Previous research has indicated that when teachers use negative, punitive, or publicly humiliating feedback when responding to students’ disruptive classroom behavior, students perceive that effort cannot bring about improvement, thereby leading to learned helplessness44. In this context, students’ engagement in classroom activities decreases significantly, and their intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior correspondingly decreases, as they generally believe that sustained effort is unlikely to produce meaningful behavioral change16,31. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H2c. When students perceive teachers’ responses to disruptive classroom behavior as destructive criticism, their classroom helplessness will be higher, which will be associated with weaker intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior.

Comparison of the effects of different criticism styles on students’ intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior

The ultimate goal of criticism is to encourage students to reduce disruptive behavior; therefore, examining which criticism style is more effective in promoting improvement in students’ behavior is important. Constructive criticism emphasizes the possibility of improvement and the temporary and controllable nature of disruptive behavior, thereby alleviating students’ negative academic emotions and enhancing their intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior12,15. In contrast, destructive criticism may, through teachers’ authority and external constraints, lead students to temporarily suppress disruptive classroom behavior in the short term due to fear of punishment; however, its mechanism primarily relies on external control and fails to effectively reduce disruptive classroom behavior in the long term45.

Relevant empirical studies have also shown that, compared with students who receive constructive criticism or no feedback, those exposed to destructive criticism are more likely to experience negative emotions such as anger and anxiety and tend to adopt confrontational or avoidant strategies rather than cooperative or conciliatory strategies in subsequent interactions or conflicts with the teacher35. Further research indicates that, compared with constructive criticism, destructive criticism is more likely to elicit multiple negative academic emotions16,35. In the highly visible and group-based context of physical education classes, the negative effects of destructive criticism are further amplified by the presence of peers46. When students are publicly reprimanded in front of peers, they are prone to feelings of humiliation, isolation, and rejection, which not only intensify their negative emotions but may also trigger avoidance, resistance, or even oppositional behavior, and consequently reduce their intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H3. Constructive criticism and destructive criticism will differ in their effects on disruptive classroom behavior and students’ intention to improve such behavior, and constructive criticism is significantly more effective than destructive criticism in promoting students’ intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior.

In summary, disruptive classroom behavior influences students’ classroom anger, classroom anxiety, and classroom helplessness through two feedback pathways—constructive criticism and destructive criticism—which in turn influence their intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior. Methods

Participants and procedures

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and was approved by Ocean University of China (OUCPHECC2025070901). Before asking for permission to participate in the study, parents and adolescents read a research information form in which the study aims, rights and ethical aspects were explained. Adolescents and their parents gave their written informed consent to participate in this study. This study employed a questionnaire survey among Chinese junior high school students. Because the data were collected between August and September 2025, first-year students had limited contact with their physical education teachers and classroom interaction experience. Therefore, only second- and third-year students were included to enhance the reliability of the data. A total of 641 questionnaires were distributed, and all were returned. After data screening and removal of invalid responses, 583 valid questionnaires were retained, yielding an effective response rate of 90.95%. Among the participants, 304 were second-year students and 279 were third-year students; 303 were female students and 280 were male students.

Measures

The scales used in this study were adapted from well-established instruments in the relevant literature, and all variables were measured using a 7-point Likert scale. Given that some of the scales were originally developed in English, these English-language scales were translated and culturally adapted before formal data collection to ensure their suitability for the context of Chinese physical education classes. Specifically, two bilingual researchers with relevant academic backgrounds independently translated the English scales and then discussed their translations to develop an initial Chinese version. Subsequently, another bilingual researcher back-translated the initial Chinese version to examine its semantic consistency with the original scales. For items that were ambiguous or did not fit the context of Chinese physical education classes, the research team revised them based on suggestions from three middle school physical education teachers and one educational psychology expert. Finally, a small-scale pilot test was conducted to further examine the comprehensibility, clarity, and cultural appropriateness of the scale items.

Disruptive classroom behavior scale

Disruptive classroom behavior was measured using the Physical Education Classroom Instrument (PECI). The scale was developed by Krech et al.47 and was later validated and revised by Granero-Gallegos and Baena-Extremera6. The scale consists of 17 items and includes five dimensions: fails to follow directions (4 items; example item: “Have you ever failed to follow instructions in physical education class?“), distracts or disturbs others (4 items; example item: “Have you ever left your group without permission during physical education activities?“), low engagement or irresponsibility (4 items; example item: “Have you ever behaved in a lazy manner in physical education class?“), aggressive behavior (2 items; example item: “Have you ever spoken negatively about or disparaged others behind their backs in physical education class?“), and poor self-management (3 items; example item: “Have you ever mocked or ridiculed others in physical education class?“). Higher scores indicate higher levels of disruptive classroom behavior in physical education classes. In the present study, Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale was 0.922.

Students’ perceptions of constructive and destructive criticism scales

Students’ perceptions of constructive criticism and destructive criticism were measured using the Questionnaire on Teachers’ Use of Critical Verbal Behaviors developed by Chinese scholar Wang et al.10. The scale consists of 13 items, including 8 items assessing destructive criticism and 5 items assessing constructive criticism. A sample item for destructive criticism is: “The physical education teacher uses joking remarks to mock or ridicule us.” In the present study, Cronbach’s α coefficient for the destructive criticism subscale was 0.934. A sample item for constructive criticism is: “The physical education teacher provides constructive suggestions.” In the present study, Cronbach’s α coefficient for the constructive criticism subscale was 0.952.

Classroom anger, classroom anxiety and classroom helplessness scales

Classroom anger, classroom anxiety, and classroom helplessness were measured using the Achievement Emotions Questionnaire for Physical Education (AEQ-PE) which was adapted from the instrument developed by Fierro-Suero, Almagro, and Sáenz-López48. The questionnaire is applicable to the context of physical education among Chinese students and has demonstrated good reliability and validity49. Sample items include: “In physical education class, I often feel anger”; “I worry that the tasks in physical education class will be too difficult for me to complete”; and “I would rather not attend physical education class because I simply cannot perform those exercises.” In the present study, Cronbach’s α coefficients for the three variables were 0.910, 0.892, and 0.903, respectively.

Intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior scale

Intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior was adapted from the behavioral intention items in Ajzen’s Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) questionnaire guidelines50. The scale consists of three items. A sample item is: “I am willing to correct my disruptive classroom behavior in future physical education classes.” In the present study, Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale was 0.959.

Statistical analysis

Before conducting structural equation modeling, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed on the measurement model to evaluate the construct validity of the scales. Given the large number of items in the disruptive classroom behavior scale, and following the recommendations of Wu and Wen51, this study adopted an item parceling approach, in which items were aggregated according to their respective dimensions and the dimensions were used as indicators of latent variables, thereby improving model parsimony and stability. Subsequently, SPSS 24.0 was used to conduct descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation analyses to examine the data distribution and the relationships among variables. Because all research variables were measured using self-report questionnaires, participants’ responses may have been influenced by social desirability, potentially resulting in common method bias. Therefore, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to assess common method bias.

On this basis, AMOS 24.0 was used to test the structural equation model using maximum likelihood estimation, following the SEM procedure described by Byrne et al.. For the estimation of mediation effects, indirect effects were calculated as the product of the relevant path coefficients, and the mediation effects were further examined using the bias-corrected Monte Carlo bootstrapping method proposed by Preacher and Selig53. Finally, the significance of the mediation effects was determined by whether the bias-corrected 95% confidence interval (95% CI) included zero; if the confidence interval did not include zero, the mediation effect was considered significant.

Results

Common method bias test

Common method bias was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test. Except for demographic variables, all measurement items were subjected to an unrotated exploratory factor analysis. The results showed that a total of 10 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, and the first factor accounted for 39.22% of the variance, below the recommended 40% threshold. Therefore, the data did not exhibit serious common method bias.

Confirmatory factor analysis

Before conducting structural equation modeling, a confirmatory factor analysis was performed on the measurement model. During the CFA process, model refinement followed the methodological recommendations of Kline54. The CFA results showed that the squared multiple correlation (SMC) value of the aggressive behavior dimension within the disruptive classroom behavior construct was excessively low, indicating that this dimension did not provide a stable measure of the latent construct in the current sample. Therefore, this dimension was removed from the final measurement model. This removal does not deny the theoretical relevance of aggressive behavior to disruptive classroom behavior; rather, it reflects a measurement-model refinement based on the empirical results. Accordingly, the final operationalized construct of disruptive classroom behavior in this study mainly represents non-aggressive forms of classroom disruption. Model fit indices

Because the sample size exceeded 200, the chi-square statistic was likely to be inflated, which could make the model fit appear poorer; therefore, a bootstrap procedure was used for correction57. Following the Bollen–Stine bootstrap correction, the following fit indices were obtained: x² = 1248.929, x²/df = 2.757, GFI = 0.923, AGFI = 0.907, CFI = 0.949, IFI = 0.949, TLI = 0.944, and RMSEA = 0.055. All fit indices reached the recommended thresholds, indicating that the overall model fit was acceptable.

Path coefficient analysis

On the basis of satisfactory model fit, the structural paths among the latent variables were further examined, and the path coefficients are presented in Fig. 2. Disruptive classroom behavior negatively predicted constructive criticism (γ = −1.383, p < 0.001) and positively predicted destructive criticism (γ = 1.088, p < 0.001). Constructive criticism negatively predicted classroom anger (γ = −0.071, p < 0.05), whereas destructive criticism positively predicted classroom anger (γ = 0.498, p < 0.001). Constructive criticism did not significantly predict classroom anxiety (γ = −0.030, p > 0.05), whereas destructive criticism positively predicted classroom anxiety (γ = 0.607, p < 0.001). Constructive criticism negatively predicted classroom helplessness (γ = −0.085, p < 0.01), whereas destructive criticism positively predicted classroom helplessness (γ = 0.667, p < 0.001). Further analyses indicated that classroom anger negatively predicted intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior (γ = −0.332, p < 0.001), classroom anxiety negatively predicted intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior (γ = −0.315, p < 0.001), and classroom helplessness negatively predicted intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior (γ = −0.413, p < 0.001) (see Table 3).

Mediating effect analysis

The indirect effects were tested using Monte Carlo bootstrapping with 5,000 samples, and the results are presented in Table 4. The indirect effect through the constructive criticism path was significant (Z = − 2.238, p < 0.05). Specifically, the H1a path (DCB→CC→CA→IIDCB) was significant (Z = − 1.778, p < 0.05), the H1b path (DCB→CC→CAX→IIDCB) was not significant (Z = − 0.867, p > 0.05), and the H1c path (DCB→CC→CH→IIDCB) was significant (Z = − 2.333, p < 0.01). The indirect path through the destructive criticism path was also significant (Z = − 8.696, p < 0.001). Specifically, the H2a path (DCB→DC→CA→IIDCB) was significant (Z = − 4.091, p < 0.001), the H2b path (DCB→DC→CAX→IIDCB) was significant (Z = − 4.000, p < 0.001), and the H2c path (DCB→DC→CH→IIDCB) was significant (Z = − 5.263, p < 0.001). Further comparison revealed a significant difference between the two pathways in predicting students’ intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior (Z = 5.990, p < 0.001). Specifically, the destructive criticism pathway showed a stronger negative indirect effect on students’ intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior, whereas the constructive criticism pathway showed a weaker negative indirect effect. This result indicates that destructive criticism may be more detrimental to students’ intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior; by contrast, constructive criticism may be more favorable for the formation of this intention.

Discussion

The influence of constructive criticism on intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior

The findings of the present study indicate that, in Chinese middle school physical education classes, when students perceive teachers’ responses to disruptive classroom behavior as constructive criticism, such criticism can enhance students’ intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior by alleviating classroom anger and classroom helplessness, thereby supporting Hypotheses H1a and H1c. Classroom anger often arises when students feel disrespected, unrecognized, or unfairly treated in the classroom. Constructive criticism points out problems in a respectful and objective manner, helping students recognize that the teacher aims to correct classroom behavior rather than denying their personal ability or value12,58. By providing clear directions for behavioral improvement, this form of criticism enhances students’ perceived control over the classroom situation and allows criticism to be interpreted as help and guidance, thereby reducing classroom anger28. Reduced classroom anger may weaken students’ defensive responses to teacher feedback, making them more willing to accept teachers’ behavioral guidance and thereby strengthening their intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior.

Classroom helplessness arises when students develop the belief that “effort is ineffective” or that “effort cannot improve the current situation” after repeated failure experiences and negative evaluations, which leads them to lose confidence in behavioral improvement59. According to control–value theory, when constructive criticism points out students’ disruptive behavior while also providing clear suggestions for improvement, it can enhance students’ perceived control over classroom tasks, reduce their sense of helplessness in learning situations, and thereby alleviate classroom helplessness. A reduction in classroom helplessness means that students perceive their behavior as controllable and outcomes as improvable, making them more willing to actively correct disruptive classroom behavior60.

The findings of the present study indicate that, in Chinese middle school physical education classes, when teachers respond to students’ disruptive classroom behavior with constructive criticism, such criticism does not influence students’ intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior through classroom anxiety. Therefore, Hypothesis H1b was not supported. Classroom anxiety primarily arises from individuals’ overall cognitive appraisal of multiple factors, including task difficulty, self-competence, and evaluative situations, rather than being determined solely by the nature of the feedback itself18,61,62. First, from the perspective of state anxiety, classroom anxiety is a transient emotional response triggered by the immediate evaluative context. When disruptive behavior is pointed out by the teacher, students often become aware that they are being observed and evaluated, which in turn evokes tension and unease. Even when teachers use constructive criticism, students may still worry about violating rules again or being criticized publicly, thereby maintaining a relatively high level of anxiety. Second, the effect of constructive criticism on classroom anxiety may not be stable, and this variation may be related to differences in how students interpret teacher feedback63–65. Even when teachers provide feedback that is supportive and instructional, students may still experience different levels of anxiety. At the same time, within the Chinese cultural context, students tend to be more sensitive to public evaluation, and this cultural characteristic may, to some extent, weaken the anxiety-alleviating effect of constructive criticism. Relevant research has also shown that Chinese students report greater face concern than their European American counterparts66. In a sociocultural context that places strong emphasis on collective evaluation and face maintenance, students are more likely to experience negative emotions such as shame, embarrassment, and anxiety when they lose face in front of teachers and peers66,67. Therefore, for Chinese adolescents, constructive criticism alone may be insufficient to effectively alleviate their anxiety and enhance their intention to improve their behavior.

The influence of destructive criticism on intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior

The findings indicate that, in Chinese middle school physical education classes, when students perceive teachers’ responses to disruptive classroom behavior as destructive criticism, students’ classroom anger, classroom anxiety, and classroom helplessness increase, thereby reducing their intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior. H2a, H2b, and H2c were all supported. First, destructive criticism is often accompanied by teachers’ anger and is typically manifested as highly negative language such as scolding, humiliation, or sarcasm27. According to emotional contagion theory, teachers’ anger can be transmitted to students during interaction, thereby eliciting students’ anger41. Second, destructive criticism often lacks specific and actionable suggestions for improvement, and the feedback tends to be vague, making it difficult for students to determine how to adjust their behavior, thereby increasing classroom anxiety. Finally, destructive criticism tends to attribute classroom problems to students’ stable and uncontrollable traits, such as limited ability or personality traits. This may lead students to develop the belief that “I cannot change,” thereby inducing classroom helplessness68.

Control–value theory posits that students’ academic emotions result from the combined effects of their perceived control and value appraisals17,18. Destructive criticism is typically expressed through negative evaluation, blame, or humiliation and does not provide specific suggestions for improvement. As a result, it may weaken students’ perceived control over behavioral improvement in the classroom context while increasing the perceived importance of evaluation outcomes. In this process, students may become dissatisfied with teachers’ evaluations and resistant to them, thereby experiencing classroom anger. At the same time, under the combined influence of low perceived control and high value appraisal, students may become more concerned about their classroom performance and evaluation outcomes and increasingly worry about failing to meet teachers’ expectations, thereby intensifying classroom anxiety. Repeated destructive criticism may further undermine students’ perceived control over behavioral improvement, leading them to develop the belief that “even effort cannot change the situation,” thereby exacerbating classroom helplessness60.

In physical education classes, classroom anger, classroom anxiety, and classroom helplessness elicited by teachers’ destructive criticism further reduce students’ intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior. Previous research has indicated that emotions are important factors influencing students’ motivation, behavioral regulation, and classroom engagement in physical education. Classroom anger tends to provoke resistance, hostility, or reactance, thereby hindering students’ acceptance of teacher feedback. Classroom anxiety places students in a state of heightened tension and vigilance, shifting their attention from behavioral improvement to self-protection and thereby weakening their intention to adjust disruptive behavior. Classroom helplessness leads students to develop the belief that “no matter how hard I try, change is unlikely,” resulting in avoidance, withdrawal, or giving up on improvement efforts. Therefore, in physical education classes, destructive criticism in response to students’ disruptive classroom behavior may increase students’ classroom anger, classroom anxiety, and classroom helplessness, thereby reducing their intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior.

Comparison of the effects of different criticism styles on the intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior

The present study found that, in Chinese middle school physical education classes, when responding to students’ disruptive classroom behavior, destructive criticism may exert a stronger negative effect on students’ intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior by intensifying classroom anger, classroom anxiety, and classroom helplessness. In contrast, the negative indirect effect of constructive criticism appeared to be relatively weaker. H3 was supported, and this finding is consistent with previous research69. Constructive criticism and destructive criticism differ substantially in how feedback is delivered. Constructive criticism not only identifies problems but also provides clear directions for improvement, and is therefore more likely to be perceived by students as helpful and instructive feedback. In contrast, destructive criticism is often characterized by denial, blame, and suppression, and is thus more likely to be perceived by students as a negation of their self-worth and competence10. Previous research has shown that feedback is more likely to be accepted by students when it is less threatening and includes clear and specific suggestions for improvement27,70. Constructive criticism emphasizes guidance rather than negation, helping students understand teachers’ expectations and classroom behavioral norms, thereby alleviating negative academic emotions and enhancing their intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior. By contrast, because destructive criticism lacks the necessary guidance and support, it is more likely to make students feel rejected and blamed in the classroom, thereby eliciting negative academic emotions. Under such circumstances, even when students appear to improve their behavior, such change is more likely to reflect passive compliance with teacher authority and external pressure rather than active adjustment grounded in internal acceptance. Therefore, compared with destructive criticism, constructive criticism is more likely to strengthen students’ intention to improve their behavior by alleviating negative academic emotions.

Conclusion

In Chinese physical education classes, teachers’ criticism styles influence students’ intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior through their academic emotions. Specifically, constructive criticism is significantly more effective than destructive criticism in enhancing students’ intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior. Constructive criticism strengthens students’ intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior by alleviating classroom anger and classroom helplessness, but it does not influence their intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior through classroom anxiety. In contrast, destructive criticism intensifies students’ classroom anger, classroom anxiety, and classroom helplessness, thereby reducing their intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior. Therefore, teacher criticism is not only a classroom management strategy but also an important factor influencing students’ academic emotions and behavioral adjustment.

The present study offers the following implications for teaching practice and teacher development in Chinese physical education classes. First, when addressing students’ disruptive classroom behavior, physical education teachers should prioritize constructive criticism by identifying problematic behavior and providing clear and feasible suggestions for improvement. Second, teachers should avoid destructive criticism characterized by blame, humiliation, or emotionally charged expressions, and minimize the use of public reprimands, sarcastic remarks, or demeaning evaluations, as such practices may provoke students’ negative emotions and weaken their intention to improve disruptive classroom behavior. Finally, schools and educational administrators should incorporate classroom feedback-related content into teacher training programs to help teachers master effective feedback methods and classroom communication strategies, thereby improving the quality of classroom interaction.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations that should be addressed in future research. First, regarding the selection of emotional variables, the present study focused only on three types of negative academic emotions—classroom anxiety, classroom anger, and classroom helplessness. Future studies may incorporate additional categories of academic emotions to enable more systematic and comprehensive comparative analyses. Second, this study was conducted within the context of Chinese physical education classes. The specific patterns of teacher–student interaction and collectivistic cultural orientation may, to some extent, limit the applicability of the findings to other cultural or educational settings. Future research could further examine the generalizability of the proposed model through comparative studies across cultures, classroom types, or age groups. Third, data on all core variables were collected using self-report questionnaires. Although Harman’s single-factor test indicated no serious common method bias, this test cannot fully rule out such bias. Therefore, future studies should collect multi-source data, such as teacher reports, peer evaluations, or classroom observations, to further validate these findings. Fourth, this study employed a cross-sectional questionnaire design, which limits the ability to draw causal conclusions. Future studies should use longitudinal, experimental, or intervention designs to further examine the causal mechanisms underlying these relationships.
