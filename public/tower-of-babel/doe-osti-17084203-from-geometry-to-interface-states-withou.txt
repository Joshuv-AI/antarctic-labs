ABSTRACT
Topological materials provide a route to robust electronic, photonic, and quantum states whose
existence is protected by global properties of the underlying system. Traditional approaches for
identifying such topology typically rely on Bloch band structures, translational symmetry, and
momentum-space invariants, which limits their applicability to disordered, aperiodic, nonlinear,
non-Hermitian, and strongly inhomogeneous materials. This LDRD developed and applied real-
space and operator-theoretic methods for classifying material topology without requiring a band
structure. The central technical framework is based on numerical 𝐾-theory, Clifford and quadratic
pseudospectra, and the spectral localizer, which combine a system’s Hamiltonian or wave operator
with position and symmetry data to produce local topological markers and quantitative measures
of topological protection.
Over the course of the project, this framework was advanced in several directions. We developed
real-space approaches for nonlinear topological materials, local Chern classification in artificial
quantum materials, parity-based Z2 topology, fragile topology protected by crystalline symme-
tries, and dimensional crossovers between topological phases. We also extended the mathematical
foundations of the approach through studies of Clifford spectra in 𝐶 ∗ -algebras and developed a
pseudospectral approach to band unfolding that remains meaningful for systems lacking exact
periodicity. These methods were applied to experimentally relevant platforms including artifi-
cial graphene, exciton-polariton lattices, Shiba lattices, semiconductor meta-graphene, photonic
crystals, and quasicrystalline or disordered systems.
Together, these results establish a broad real-space framework for predicting and diagnosing topo-
logical phenomena in realistic materials and metamaterials. By avoiding reliance on Bloch band
theory, the methods developed here enable topology to be studied in finite samples, heterostruc-
tures, disordered systems, multi-scale materials, nonlinear media, and continuum simulations. This
capability provides a foundation for future discovery of topological materials and for the design of
robust devices for low-power microelectronics, photonics, and quantum information science.

1. INTRODUCTION

Topological materials have emerged as an important class of systems for both fundamental science
and potential technology. Their defining feature is the existence of boundary- or interface-localized
states whose presence is guaranteed by a topological distinction between two regions of a material,
such as a topological insulator and a trivial insulator or vacuum. In electronic systems, these states
can support robust transport channels with suppressed backscattering, making topological materials
promising for low-power microelectronics, spintronics, and quantum information applications.
Analogous topological phenomena have also been realized in photonic, acoustic, mechanical, and
other artificial materials, where topology provides a general principle for designing robust wave
propagation.
Despite this promise, the standard theoretical tools for identifying topological materials have impor-
tant limitations. Conventional topological classification is usually formulated in momentum space:
one computes Bloch eigenstates, evaluates band-theoretic invariants such as Chern numbers or Z2
indices, and then invokes bulk-boundary correspondence to infer the presence of protected interface
states. This approach has been extraordinarily successful for crystalline materials, but it relies on
assumptions that are absent in many systems of current interest. Aperiodic materials, disordered
materials, finite heterostructures, moiré systems at incommensurate twist angles, nonlinear media,
non-Hermitian systems, and strongly spatially inhomogeneous devices may not possess a useful
Brillouin zone or a clean bulk band gap. Even in periodic systems, topological features may be
obscured by disorder, multi-scale structure, continuum degrees of freedom, or the need to use very
large unit cells. As a result, important classes of materials and devices remain difficult to classify
using traditional band-structure-based methods.
The overarching goal of this LDRD was to develop and apply a real-space approach to material
topology that does not require a band structure. The project was motivated by the observation that
topology can be formulated in terms of whether a system’s Hamiltonian and position operators can
be continuously deformed to an atomic limit while preserving the relevant symmetries and without
closing the appropriate local gap. This perspective naturally connects topological materials to
numerical 𝐾-theory and operator algebras. In this setting, topology is diagnosed by constructing
matrices from the Hamiltonian, position operators, and symmetry data, and then computing local
invariants from those matrices. The resulting quantities are defined directly in position space and
are therefore applicable to finite samples, disordered systems, heterostructures, and continuum
discretizations.
A central tool developed and used throughout this project is the spectral localizer. In its simplest
form, the spectral localizer combines the Hamiltonian with position operators using a represen-
tation of the Clifford algebra. The resulting Hermitian matrix encodes both spectral and spatial
information, allowing one to determine whether a state or region of a system is simultaneously
localized near a target energy and position. Topological invariants can then be extracted from the

 9

localizer, for example through its signature, determinant, or Pfaffian, depending on the system’s
symmetry class and physical dimension. Importantly, the spectral localizer also provides a localizer
gap: a quantitative measure of local topological protection that is distinct from, and often more
useful than, a conventional bulk spectral gap. This feature is essential for studying realistic systems
in which disorder, finite-size effects, or environmental degrees of freedom obscure a clean band
gap.

The original motivation for this project was to develop a real-space framework capable of classi-
fying topological electronic materials directly from structural or real-space Hamiltonian data, with
particular interest in systems that are inaccessible to conventional band-theoretic methods. These
include quasicrystals, magnetic materials, disordered systems, and strongly interacting systems.
During the course of the project, this goal broadened into a general research program on real-space
topology and multi-operator pseudospectral methods. Rather than producing a single classification
algorithm for one class of electronic materials, the project developed a family of related mathemat-
ical and computational tools that apply across electronic, photonic, polaritonic, superconducting,
nonlinear, non-Hermitian, and artificial quantum material platforms.

The chapters of this report summarize the major outcomes of that program. Chapter 2 describes
the extension of numerical 𝐾-theory and the spectral localizer to nonlinear topological materials
described by Gross-Pitaevskii-like equations. This work establishes a framework for classifying
topology in systems where the effective Hamiltonian depends on the occupation, and where nonlin-
earity can create spatially inhomogeneous topological phases and topological interfaces. Chapter
3 applies local Chern markers derived from the spectral localizer to artificial quantum materials,
demonstrating that real-space topology can identify experimentally relevant topological features in
artificial graphene even when disorder removes clean spectral gaps. Chapter 4 develops the math-
ematical theory of multivariable pseudospectra and Clifford spectra in 𝐶 ∗ -algebras, expanding the
operator-theoretic foundation of the project beyond finite-dimensional numerical models. Chapter
5 addresses dynamically reconfigurable topological routing in nonlinear photonic systems. There,
a nonlinear response induced by optical pumping creates spatially reconfigurable Chern domains,
enabling topological interfaces and chiral routing paths to be changed after fabrication. Chapter
6 presents a position-space classification of fragile topology using matrix homotopy, providing a
route to diagnosing topological phases that are not stable under the addition of trivial atomic bands
and that are difficult to characterize outside momentum space. Chapter 7 studies dimensional
crossovers in class D systems, using local real-space invariants to track how topology evolves as
a Shiba lattice changes between two-dimensional and one-dimensional limits. Chapter 8 applies
related ideas to semiconductor meta-graphene and valleytronics, analyzing the robustness of valley
Hall edge states in artificial hexagonal boron nitride-like structures subject to realistic disorder.

The final technical chapters present two additional methodological advances. Chapter 9 develops
an efficient local classification approach for parity-based material topology, including Z2 invariants
such as those relevant to quantum spin Hall systems. This work is especially important because
it avoids dense projectors and instead uses sparse-matrix computations, making it suitable for
large-scale and continuum models. Chapter 10 introduces a band-unfolding framework based on
the quadratic pseudospectrum. This method provides a band-like description for systems in which
exact Bloch theory is unavailable or inconvenient, while giving quantitative guarantees about the
existence of approximate joint eigenstates localized in both energy and momentum.

 10

Taken together, these results demonstrate that topology can be detected, quantified, and used for
design in settings far beyond the traditional band-structure paradigm. The project established
real-space and pseudospectral tools for studying topology in nonlinear, non-Hermitian, disordered,
aperiodic, finite, multi-scale, and continuum systems. These advances support the long-term goal
of discovering and engineering topological materials for robust microelectronics, photonics, and
quantum technologies, while also providing new mathematical foundations for classifying material
topology in realistic environments.

2. PROBING TOPOLOGY IN NONLINEAR TOPOLOGICAL
 MATERIALS USING NUMERICAL 𝐾 -THEORY

Over the past two decades, the discovery of topological materials has revolutionized a broad range
of materials research through the prediction and observation of fundamentally new classes of states
that are robust against defects and imperfections. In non-interacting systems, the possible types of
topology that a material can exhibit are determined by its discrete symmetries (or lack-thereof),
yielding a periodic table of material topology rooted in the ten Altland-Zirnbauer classes. This
topological classification framework has subsequently been expanded to include crystalline sym-
metries and semimetals. For all of these cases, a non-interacting material’s topology is traditionally
determined through the calculation of invariants built from the system’s band structure and Bloch
eigenstates, and thus the topological invariants are global properties of the bulk material.
However, in many systems, interactions are both unavoidable and potentially highly desirable, as
they can result in emergent phenomena. For example, topologically ordered phases of matter
can support quasiparticles with fractional charge and anyonic statistics. More recently, studies
of interacting bosons in the mean-field limit have led to the discovery of new physics, such as
the appearance of topological bulk and edge solitons, the observation of nonlinearly induced
topological phase transitions, and the concept of multi-wave mixing with topological states. Such
bosonic systems are usually described using the nonlinear Schrödinger equation, a Gross-Pitaevskii-
like equation, in which the nonlinear response is parameterized by the strength of the inter-particle
interactions and mediated by an ambient medium such as a Kerr medium in photonic systems
or with nonlinear building block elements in acoustic platforms. Unfortunately, nonlinearities
present a substantial challenge for our understanding of material topology as they demand a shift
in perspective. Whereas non-interacting (linear) systems are topologically classified using only
a system’s single-particle Hamiltonian, classifying the topology of a nonlinear system requires
knowing its occupation. Moreover, as nonlinear effects are intrinsically local, they can break the
fundamental assumption of band theory (which demands spatial periodicity). Altogether, these
challenges have so far prohibited the development of a broadly applicable theory for classifying the
topology of nonlinear systems and their possible occupations.
In this study [12], we present a general framework for probing the topology of nonlinear materials
described by a Gross-Pitaevskii-like equation using numerical 𝐾-theory. Our framework classifies
a system’s topology in real-space using local markers, and as such directly accommodates spatial
inhomogeneities in the system’s occupation and the material’s response. To do so, we first deter-
mine the system’s Hamiltonian accounting for its occupation, and then combine it with the system’s
position operators using a non-trivial Clifford representation. This yields the system’s spectral
localizer, which defines local markers for all ten Altland-Zirnbauer classes in any physical dimen-
sion, as well as a local measure of topological protection. Using this classification framework, we
develop a rigorous definition of topological nonlinear modes distinguished by the appearance of

 13

nonlinearity-induced topological interfaces surrounding them; the topological robustness of these
nonlinear states guarantees their existence over some range of finite perturbations (i.e., a solution to
the nonlinear Hamiltonian is guaranteed). Moreover, our framework allows for probing topological
dynamics, and one can numerically observe the transport of a nonlinearity-induced topological
phase with the propagation of the nonlinear state that creates it. Overall, our classification frame-
work provides a general approach for the characterization of stationary and dynamical topological
phenomena in nonlinear materials in any discrete symmetry class and in any physical dimension.
We anticipate that our nonlinear spectral localizer framework will prove useful for the design and
development of a broad range of novel nonlinear topological phenomena such as in pump-probe-
like systems and topological frequency combs, which require simultaneous local information at
different positions and energies.

3. TOPOLOGICAL PHENOMENA IN ARTIFICIAL QUANTUM
 MATERIALS REVEALED BY LOCAL CHERN MARKERS

Over the last half-century, few physical systems have been studied as intently as two-dimensional
electron gasses subjected to a perpendicularly oriented magnetic field. By itself, this configuration
yields the integer quantum Hall effect, whose defining feature is a quantized conductivity stemming
from protected edge-localized transport channels. However, when a periodic electrostatic potential
is applied, the system becomes frustrated—the applied magnetic field 𝐵 is attempting to √︁ drive
the system to exhibit degenerate Landau levels, characterized by the magnetic length 𝑙 𝐵 = ℏ/𝑒𝐵,
while the periodic potential tries to force the system to exhibit extended Bloch modes, characterized
by the lattice constant 𝑎. This competition results in each band being split into subbands separated
by minigaps that form a fractal as a function of the applied magnetic field and Fermi energy,
Hofstadter’s butterfly. The fractal structure for a spectrally isolated underlying band is periodic in
Φ/Φ0 , where Φ = 𝐵𝐴 is the magnetic flux through a unit cell with area 𝐴 and Φ0 = ℎ/𝑒 is the
magnetic flux quantum. Hofstadter’s butterfly has been observed in artificial quantum materials
and more recently in graphene superlattices.
Despite substantial progress in achieving system periodicities large enough to enter the parameter
regime where Hofstadter’s butterfly manifests, Φ ∼ Φ0 , it remains a formidable challenge to predict
the Chern numbers associated with each minigap in experimentally realizable systems. For low-
energy models with limited degrees of freedom, there are a few different approaches to predicting
a minigap’s Chern number: via a Diophantine equation, Středa’s formula, semi-classical analysis,
bulk-boundary correspondence, or direct calculation using the occupied states. However, these
methods are impossible or impractical to apply to many experimental platforms, stymied either by
prohibitively large computational costs in the absence of a low-energy description, the lack of a
bulk spectral gap due to disorder, or the need for system-specific knowledge. In such cases, the last
resort is direct simulation of a system’s quantum transport, requiring the specification of a device
geometry and still yielding a costly computational endeavor for realistic systems. Moreover, such
an approach will miss bulk-embedded phenomena that do not contribute to the chosen transport
channels.
In our study [9], we theoretically demonstrate and experimentally validate the spectral localizer
framework for predicting a quantum material’s local Chern topology and associated boundary-
localized states. Moreover, we show how the spectral localizer framework can be used to reveal
distinct material topology at different length scales of multi-scale systems. Using this framework
in artificial graphene subjected to an out-of-plane magnetic field and described by a continuum
model without a low-energy approximation, we demonstrate quantitative agreement between the
Chern marker and the experimentally observed Hall conductivity, while inherently accounting
for fabrication disorder that is strong enough to remove the system’s spectral gaps. Moreover,
by spatially resolving local changes in the system’s topology, we show that many of the pinned

 15

states that populate the gaps between the bulk Landau levels are topological, stemming from the
distinct topology of the antidots relative to the unpatterned bulk for many magnetic field strengths.
Taking advantage of the spectral localizer’s ability to efficiently operate without a low-energy
approximation, we numerically observe the formation of Hofstadter’s butterfly from an unpatterned
system. Finally, we predict the opening of a topological band gap in artificial graphene at the
critical magnetic field due to long-range couplings, yielding a topological insulator at modest
magnetic fields. Looking forward, we anticipate that the spectral localizer framework’s application
to realistic multi-scale systems will both yield a novel approach to material classification and enable
a broad array of fundamental inquiries, such as those into the formation of Hofstadter’s butterfly
in twisted materials, as well as aid in the search for materials that exhibit the quantum anomalous
Hall effect.

5. DYNAMICALLY RECONFIGURABLE TOPOLOGICAL ROUTING IN
 NONLINEAR PHOTONIC SYSTEMS

Over the past decade, topological photonics has emerged as a promising collection of physical
principles for controlling the flow of light. For example, there is significant interest in harnessing
topological phenomena for potential applications and integration into nanophotonic systems to
realize robust photon routing and create lasers with increased coherence. In particular, the chiral
edge modes supported by photonic Chern insulators are an especially enticing class of states for
designing next-generation optical devices, as these states both enable non-reciprocal transport and
are robust against fabrication imperfections. Prior studies have observed chiral edge modes in
a variety of photonic platforms, including photonic crystals with gyro-optical materials, shifted
ring-resonator arrays, helical waveguide arrays, and more recently exciton-polariton lattices.
However, the interface localization of a photonic Chern heterostructure’s chiral edge modes also
presents a substantial limitation on device design: these states’ propagation path is fixed by the
system’s geometry and cannot be readily altered after the device is fabricated. Although there are
previous proposals for reconfigurable topological systems, they are predominantly in the microwave
regime with slow reconfigurability time scales in the range of milliseconds, and are thus not
technologically relevant for integrated nanotechnologies. At telecommunication wavelengths, prior
work on reconfigurable photonics has either been realized through dynamically controlling each
lattice element, an approach that is challenging to scale, or through tunable spatially non-uniform
non-Hermiticity, which requires introducing large material absorptivities. As such, photonic
systems rooted in linear topology are best suited to devices tailored for a single, static function,
but are poor candidates for applications requiring dynamic behavior, such as routing. In addition,
while many studies in nonlinear systems have considered topological solitons that can be injected
at different lattice sites, these states are still constrained to move along the lattice’s structural
boundary or remain confined in its bulk, and thus yield similar design constraints as linear systems.
Altogether, an ideal reconfigurable topological platform would instead exhibit a scalable, fast,
and dynamic method for changing the system’s local Chern phase without introducing additional
propagation losses, so that a reconfigurable router can take full advantage of a chiral edge mode’s
inherent reflectionless propagation and robustness against dephasing.
In this study [10], we propose an approach for realizing fast reconfigurable local Chern topology
in driven-dissipative photonic systems and numerically illustrate our method in a non-resonantly
pumped exciton-polariton platform operating at telecommunication wavelengths. In particular,
starting with a topologically non-trivial polariton lattice [Fig. ??(a)], a spatially non-uniform
incident pump can locally change the system’s topology to be trivial through a nonlinear matter-
matter interaction between the exciton reservoir and the polaritons. Thus, an incident signal
propagating in a chiral edge state is guaranteed to follow the new topological boundary determined
by the non-resonant pump pattern [Fig. ??(b)]. As the exciton reservoir is dissipative, if the

 19

non-resonant pump is turned off the full lattice returns to its original topological phase after a
characteristic decay time, but this dissipation does not strongly influence the polaritons such that
their propagation remains relatively loss-less. Moreover, by superposing the reconfigurable Chern
topology features using multiple non-resonant pump patterns and pump amplitudes, we realize
reconfigurable multi-channel topologically protected routing via different topological interfaces
in different energy ranges. To characterize the local topological dynamics we observe in our
system, we develop a nonlinear non-Hermitian generalization of the spectral localizer framework
to use with a continuum model of the exciton-polariton lattice based on the driven-dissipative
Gross-Pitaevskii equations for experimentally realizable parameters. Given the possible tiny extent
of a pumped region, the non-linear non-Hermitian local Chern marker we establish provides a
rigorous and quantitative understanding of the lattice’s local topology. Overall, while there has
been growing interest in inducing topological phase changes via optical pumping without relying on
external magnetic fields, our results show that it is possible to dynamically reconfigure a system’s
topological interfaces post-fabrication by leveraging a nonlinear response, a phenomenon that
should be available in a variety of systems featuring nonlinear interactions and may be of practical
use for multitasking devices while retaining the robustness of topological protected edge modes.

6. CLASSIFICATION OF FRAGILE TOPOLOGY ENABLED BY
 MATRIX HOMOTOPY

Since their discovery, topological phases of matter have served as pivotal physical properties
that broaden the frontiers of functional materials, both through their protection of boundary-
localized states and their prediction of delocalized single-particle wavefunctions that can precipitate
correlated phases. Representative examples include Chern insulators and topological insulators,
which host gapless edge states protected by associated topological invariants and persisting as long
as their internal symmetries are preserved and the corresponding spectral gap remains open. Such
classes of topology are now referred to as strong and exhibit a bulk-boundary correspondence,
linking the presence of protected boundary states to the nonzero topological indices of the bulk
bands. Likewise, in conjunction with certain types of interactions, the delocalized electrons
in topological bands can give rise to correlated phases such as fractional Chern insulators and
superconductivity. Within a 𝐾-theoretic framework, classification schemes rooted in vector bundles
can systematically assign topological indices to band gaps by summing up the topological invariants
of each occupied band.

Recently, the emergence of crystalline-symmetry protected topological phases has revealed a richer
variety of phenomena including weaker forms of topology. Distinct from strong topology, crys-
talline topology has a nuanced bulk-boundary correspondence, stimulating the development of new
theoretical frameworks such as topological quantum chemistry, symmetry indicators, and Wilson
loop approaches for classifying these subtle phases. Among these weak topological phases, the
concept of fragile topology has been found as a contrasting case to stable topology, where the
topological features of certain bands can be trivialized through the addition of atomic limits. Im-
portantly, the moiré flat bands of small-angle twisted bilayer graphene (TBG) have been identified
as possessing such fragile topology, indicating the delocalized nature of its single-particle wave-
functions and attracting significant attention due to potential connections to correlated phases and
superconductivity. This new class of topology also appears relevant to debates regarding the weak
topological protection of the interface states observed in various photonic crystal platforms, as
well as to potential applications of corner states and some types of edge states. However, exist-
ing studies of fragile topology have focused on momentum-space classifications, prohibiting the
application of these approaches to aperiodic and disordered materials. In particular, the lack of a
position-space approach for classifying fragile phases that works with finite regions of a material
inhibits the ability to study the manifestation of such topology for twist angles near a magic angle.
Such an approach could provide new insight into how delocalization of a system’s single-particle
wavefunctions occurs, and thus how correlated phases appear.

In this study [6], we derive and demonstrate a position-space approach for classifying fragile
topology and find an associated measure of robustness. Specifically, we show how the physical

 21

symmetries that protect fragile topology can give rise to matrix symmetries in a system’s Hamil-
tonian and position operators when expressed in an atypical basis. These matrix symmetries can
then be used to define a homotopic invariant that distinguishes 2D systems based on which atomic
limit they can be continued to, yielding an energy-resolved Z2 topological marker. Moreover, this
approach implicitly introduces a quantitative measure of topological protection that remains valid
under a variety of conditions, including finite system size, disorder, and environmental perturba-
tions. We apply our approach to a disordered 𝐶2 T -symmetric TBG model and a two-dimensional
(2D) photonic crystal embedded in an air background, showing that fragile topology can persist even
under strong disorder and when a heterostructure lacks a bulk spectral gap. Our disordered simula-
tions also reveal a disorder-induced re-entrant transition to a fragile phase with increasing disorder
strength. Altogether, as our work shows how to characterize fragile topology beyond momentum-
space descriptions, it may offer insights into both natural and metamaterial aperiodic and moiré
systems, as well as correlated phenomena and superconductivity. In addition, the arguments we
present here provide guidance on how to develop further position-space topological classification
approaches for predicting forms of material topology associated with delocalization.

7. DIMENSIONAL CROSSOVER OF CLASS D REAL-SPACE
 TOPOLOGICAL INVARIANTS

The possible topological properties of non-interacting Hamiltonians are dictated by their spatial
dimension, and discrete and crystal symmetries. In particular, changing the dimension of a system
while keeping its discrete symmetries unaltered generally yields a topological phase transition,
following the Altland-Zirnbauer classes. Examples in condensed matter systems include transitions
from a trivial insulator to a quantum spin Hall (QSH) phase in HgTe quantum wells, and transitions
from three-dimensional topological insulators with two-dimensional surface states to QSH states
with one-dimensional helical states.

Such topological transitions due to dimensional shifts have been previously theoretically charac-
terized by analyzing edge states in finite-size systems, the topological invariants based on band
representations, and the transport properties of a material. However, a real-space framework capa-
ble of illuminating how a system’s topology changes during a dimensional shift has traditionally
been missing due to the dearth of local topological markers needed to probe the topology of systems
lacking translational symmetry across all of the possible symmetry classes. Nevertheless, such a
theory could be useful to guide the design of materials and next-generation devices; for example,
to identify how thin a slab can be while maintaining its three-dimensional topological properties,
or how thick a nanowire can be while maintaining its end Majorana modes.

In this paper, we introduce a scheme to study topological phase transitions induced by the change in
a system’s spatial dimensions via local topological markers. We will focus on the symmetry class
D in the Altland-Zirnbauer classification, a class that contains some topological superconductors,
and which exhibits non-trivial invariants in both 1D and 2D. Specifically, Hamiltonians in this class
have particle-hole symmetry C, and broken time-reversal and chiral symmetries. In one dimension
(1D), the topological invariant corresponds to Z2 , while in two dimensions (2D) to Z. Our aim
is to study the evolution of the Z invariant into Z2 as one of the dimensions of the target material
embedded in an insulating environment is reduced.

In particular, we consider magnet-superconductor hybrid systems, Shiba lattices, consisting of time-
reversal-symmetry-breaking magnetic atoms placed on the surface of a superconductor arranged as
1D chains or in 2D lattices. Shiba lattices can be assembled with high precision in the laboratory
and have shown signatures of topological superconductivity. 1D Shiba chains have been fabricated
using ferromagnetic transition metal atoms (such as iron, chromium, manganese, and cobalt) placed
on the surface of lead, rhenium, niobium or 𝛽-Bi2 Pd. In 2D, Shiba lattices have been realized using
iron adatoms on rhenium, flakes of chromium tribromide placed on niobium diselenide, and cobalt-
silicon islands on Si(111) covered by lead. Additionally, arrays of chromium atoms on niobium
have shown signatures of mirror symmetry-protected topological superconductivity.

 23

The task of tracking the topological properties of the Shiba lattice across spatial dimensions
is well suited to the spectral localizer framework, a real-space numeric 𝐾-theory approach that
characterizes the local topological properties of finite-size systems, without requiring any notion
of lattice periodicity. The spectral localizer approach has been employed to identify topological
phenomena in gapless systems, nonlinear materials, non-Hermitian systems, photonic systems,
quasicrystals, time-dependent systems, disordered superconductors and heterostructures. While
most of these studies have looked at 1D and 2D systems, the framework has also been applied to a
3D system and a 4D system.
For the present study [8], we utilize two local topological markers derived from the spectral
localizer, a Chern number, and a Z2 invariant that characterizes two- and one-dimensional Shiba
lattices embedded in two-dimensional superconductors. Additionally, we also considered the one-
to-zero-dimensional crossover in a BDI class model, showing the generality of our approach. As
this framework does not rely on projecting to an occupied subspace, it is endowed with a measure of
the corresponding local topological protection related to the local spectral gap. The local nature of
the markers permits us to track their changes and corresponding local protection as the dimension of
the Shiba lattice changes from two to one dimension. The real-space topology in 2D Shiba lattices
has been characterized using a global position-space invariant, and Z2 local markers for nonchiral
phases in odd dimensions have been recently introduced. However, these approaches cannot
diagnose the topology of 1D Shiba lattices embedded in a two-dimensional superconductor.

8. SEMICONDUCTOR META-GRAPHENE AND VALLEYTRONICS

Topological insulators (TIs) are materials characterized by an insulating bulk and conducting surface
or edge states. These dissipationless edge modes arise from the contrast between the topological
invariants of a TI’s electronic structure and the trivial topology of the vacuum. This promise
of efficient energy transfer mechanisms has generated significant interest in understanding how
topological defects influence transport channels. One such defect occurs when a two-dimensional
(2D) material hosts a one-dimensional (1D) domain wall, characterized by a sharp discontinuity in
a topological invariant across the interface. A key example of such an invariant is the valley Chern
number, a quantity associated with Berry curvature localized at a single valley of an electronic band
structure. The resulting valley Hall effect that arises from the interplay of valley Chern numbers
across a fabricated domain wall has been observed in various 2D atomic-scale electronic devices,
photonic crystals, and acoustic crystals. As the effect seems to be universal to both quantum and
classical systems, it offers a promising avenue for practical energy transfer applications.
The performance of valley Hall systems based on traditional materials can, however, be challenged
by the presence of disorder, as has been recently demonstrated in the context of photonic topological
interfaces. One approach to address this issue involves implementing novel, tunable materials. In
this context, we propose an innovative mechanism for realizing valleytronics on the surface of a
semiconductor.
First, we consider artificial graphene (AG) as our primary quantum material. AG can be con-
structed by engineering a 2D electron gas (2DEG) in a semiconductor heterostructure (such as
AlSb/InAs/AlSb), and introducing a triangular antidot lattice via interferometric lithography. The
antidots repel electrons into regions of low potential, resulting in an effective honeycomb lattice
where pseudo-C atoms are located. Because changing the nanopattern design is relatively straight-
forward, AG offers greater versatility than natural graphene, as its lattice constant and depth of the
patterned holes may be strategically chosen. Then, we introduce secondary holes in the pattern
to open a bulk band gap in AG. As this mimics the structure of hexagonal boron nitride (hBN),
we have dubbed our metamaterial ‘artificial hexagonal Boron Nitride’ (AhBN). Opening a gap in
this manner in traditional graphene reveals valley Chern numbers. Therefore, in this study [7] we
aimed to understand the role of band topology and the effects of disorder on our AhBN system.
To investigate these effects, we first consider the introduction of disorder in the form of a fluctuating
potential induced by hypothetical charge puddles. As a semiconductor heterostructure contains
nearby charge impurities or similar defects, analyzing the effect of this type of disorder is important
for understanding how the valley Hall effect may survive under realistic conditions. Then, we
consider a second type of disorder resulting from the geometrical variation in the nanopatterned
holes in the 2DEG that produce the AG/AhBN. Both types of disorder are relatively short-range,
meaning that the relevant disorder size is not significantly larger than the inter-pseudoatomic
distance 𝐿; therefore, valley Hall topological protection may be less effective, as it may not

 25

completely suppress inter-valley scattering. We analyze the impact of these disorder types as a
function of the disorder strength, on both the AhBN bulk band gap as well as on the edge states
near a domain-wall.
Because the valley Chern number is not quantized and the edge states are one-dimensional, they
are susceptible to Anderson localization. We aim to understand the interplay between disorder-
induced localization and the valley Hall effect, which provides some level of protection against
localization. In particular, we seek to quantify the localization length of these wavefunctions to
accurately determine whether our system can demonstrate the valley Hall effect over length scales
relevant to nanoscale devices. For realistic disorder strengths, we find that the localization length of
the edge states spans several microns, i.e. it is more than an order of magnitude larger than the AG
periodicity 𝐿 and significantly larger than the typical mean free path (< 1 μm) of the underlying
2DEG. Thus, the valley Hall edge states, combined with the wide tunability of semiconducting
superlattices, provide a promising platform for future applications in electronic devices with low
power dissipation.

9. EFFICIENT LOCAL CLASSIFICATION OF PARITY-BASED
 MATERIAL TOPOLOGY

Topological insulators are a class of materials characterized by non-trivial topological invariants
and are guaranteed through bulk-boundary correspondence to exhibit robust boundary states pro-
tected against perturbations. This robustness is the key to enabling new technological domains,
such as spintronics, where spin-momentum locking may be useful for low-power, high-speed elec-
tronics, as well as fault-tolerant routes to quantum computing through topological superconductors.
Topological insulators can also serve as precursors to correlated phases at low temperatures when
electron interactions become strong, revealing novel forms of quantum order. In crystalline systems,
topological classification is traditionally performed using approaches rooted in band theory, where
the system’s Bloch wavefunctions are used to define invariants such as Chern numbers, the Z2 in-
dex, winding numbers, and multipole moments. Modern band-theoretic classification frameworks
based on elementary band representations can be applied across databases of crystalline materials,
resulting in the prediction of a wide variety of topological phases.
In contrast, the investigation of topology in non-periodic systems remains challenging, as the lack
of translational symmetry renders momentum-space classification formulations inapplicable. To
address this need, real-space approaches to material classification have been developed, either based
on projected position operators such as the Bott index, the Kitaev or the Bianco-Resta markers,
or rooted in the spectral localizer. These theories of real-space markers have been successfully
applied to identify Chern- and Weyl-based topology in quasicrystalline, amorphous, and disordered
systems. For non-periodic Z2 topological insulators in class AII of the Altland-Zirnbauer (AZ)
scheme that exhibit the quantum spin Hall effect (QSHE), specific real-space methods have also
been developed, such as approaches based on twisted boundary conditions, scattering matrices,
non-commutative geometry, and the spin Bott index. However, these methods for aperiodic class
AII systems face significant limitations. In particular, these existing local markers assume the
presence of a spectral gap or mobility gap in the Hamiltonian and sometimes also a gap in a spin
projection, and thus can fail in gapless systems that commonly arise in realistic models of disordered
or aperiodic topological phases. Moreover, these methods often lack an independent measure of
topological protection, i.e., beyond any spectral gap, making it difficult to quantify the robustness
of any edge states. Computationally, the reliance on projectors in these approaches also forces
the consideration of dense matrices, limiting these frameworks to low-energy approximations of
a material and thus requiring that such a low-energy approximation first be found. Yet, aperiodic
materials represent a crucial outstanding class of materials for broad topological classification that
are becoming even more relevant with the advent of twisted van der Waals systems that can exhibit
a variety of topological and correlated phases, and in which incommensurate twist angles yield
quasiperiodic systems.
In this part of the project [13], we develop a numerically efficient approach rooted in the spectral

 27

localizer framework to classify Z2 parity-based topology across a range of different symmetry
classes and demonstrate its application to realistic aperiodic systems without needing a low-energy
approximation. As this framework does not rely on projecting into an occupied subspace, our
approach can identify the quantum spin Hall effect in aperiodic class AII systems without requiring
assumptions about whether the material exhibits a spectral gap or sufficiently separated spin sectors.
In particular, the material’s local topology is diagnosed through the sign of the Pfaffian of a skew-
symmetric matrix derived from the spectral localizer, and a key aspect of this study is the release
of an efficient scalable algorithm for directly computing a Pfaffian’s sign for arbitrary sparse skew-
symmetric matrices. In other words, our algorithm can handle materials and heterostructures in
arbitrary dimensions described by wave equations, not just tight-binding models. We illustrate the
utility of our approach by both locally classifying the QSHE in a tight-binding quasicrystalline
heterostructure and identifying the fragile topology of a 𝐶2 T -symmetric 2D photonic quasicrystal.
More broadly, our numerical approach allows for the efficient computation of Z2 parity-based
invariants in many other AZ classes, both enabling the prediction of boundary-localized protected
states in natural, photonic, and acoustic materials, as well as identifying candidate materials that
may exhibit novel correlated phases. Moreover, while our algorithm does not directly compute the
Pfaffian of a sparse skew-symmetric matrix to find its sign, it can be combined with algorithms
 √︁ to find
a skew-symmetric matrix’s determinant to yield the Pfaffian, as Pf(𝑀) = sign(Pf(𝑀)) det(𝑀).

10. BAND UNFOLDING VIA THE QUADRATIC PSEUDOSPECTRUM

Band theory is one of the foundational tools in the study of natural and artificial materials, from
which a wide variety of material properties can be determined, including transport, optical re-
sponses, and material topology. However, this reliance upon band theory also presents a challenge
for understanding materials with large unit cells, such as those found in substitutional alloys and
twisted van der Waals materials, as well as materials that lack crystalline symmetry altogether, such
as quasicrystals. In systems that are periodic on a long length scale, the Brillouin zone becomes
correspondingly small and the resulting electronic bands are densely folded, complicating the band
structure’s interpretation and presenting challenges for predicting material properties. In such
systems, an unfolded band-like structure can be constructed by projecting the Bloch eigenstates
of the supercell |𝜓ksc ⟩ onto Bloch states associated with a reference primitive cell |𝜓kpc ⟩, yielding
spectral weights |⟨𝜓ksc |𝜓kpc ⟩| 2 that reveal the system’s momentum-resolved character, but requires
specifying a primitive unit cell for comparison and forces aperiodic systems to be approximated
as periodic. The spectral function 𝐴(k, 𝐸) = − 𝜋1 Im[⟨k|𝐺 (𝐸)|k⟩], defined through the system’s
Green’s function projected into a plane wave basis |k⟩, provides a complementary description of
band-like features and remains well-defined in aperiodic systems, but does not imply that the system
exhibits a state, or approximate state, at k.

Thus, despite their utility, both spectral weights and spectral functions are fundamentally indirect:
they reveal band-like features, but neither guarantees the existence of states that are simultaneously
localized in both energy and momentum. Mathematically, this limitation is rooted in the all-
or-nothing nature of whether two operators share an eigenbasis. If a system’s Hamiltonian 𝐻
commutes with some set of unitary translation operators 𝑇 𝑗 , [𝐻, 𝑇 𝑗 ] = 0, its band structure is
the joint spectrum of these operators 𝐻|𝜓𝑛,k ⟩ = 𝐸 |𝜓𝑛,k ⟩ and 𝑇 𝑗 |𝜓𝑛,k ⟩ = 𝑒𝑖k·a 𝑗 |𝜓𝑛,k ⟩, where |𝜓𝑛,k ⟩
is a shared eigenvector, i.e., the wavefunction of the 𝑛th band with wavevector k and a 𝑗 is the
lattice vector associated with 𝑇 𝑗 . Instead, if [𝐻, 𝑇 𝑗 ] ≠ 0, no such joint spectrum can be defined
as the two operators do not share an eigenbasis. Yet, recently, the mathematical field of operator
algebras has developed the concept of multi-operator pseudospectra, which enables the definition of
approximate joint eigenvectors between almost-commuting operators. In other words, if operators
𝐴 and 𝐵 satisfy ∥ [ 𝐴, 𝐵] ∥ ≤ 𝛿 for some constant 𝛿, where ∥ · ∥ is the ℓ 2 operator norm, then
approximate joint eigenvectors |𝜙⟩ can be found such that 𝐴|𝜙⟩ ≈ 𝜆 𝐴 |𝜙⟩ and 𝐵|𝜙⟩ ≈ 𝜆 𝐵 |𝜙⟩, and
the possible degree of exactness of these approximations is set by 𝛿. Thus, these techniques present
the possibility of defining unfolded band-like structures that can smoothly interpolate between both
periodic and aperiodic materials.

In this portion of the project [1], we develop a direct generalization of dispersion relations using the
quadratic pseudospectrum and derive bounds that quantify the extent to which the system exhibits an
approximate joint eigenstate that is localized in energy and crystal momentum. Moreover, we show
that by incorporating both a system’s unitary translation operators and its position operators into

 29

this multi-operator pseudospectrum, we can apply our framework to systems with open boundaries
while suppressing edge effects, enabling its direct application to disordered, quasicrystalline, and
other aperiodic systems without any other approximations. To demonstrate this band-unfolding
approach’s broad applicability, we show unfolded approximate band structures for both a Fibonacci
chain and the 2D breathing honeycomb lattice. Looking forward, our approach rooted in multi-
operator pseudospectral methods enables the prediction of the momentum-space properties of
materials regardless of the presence of sparse defects, incommensurate twist angles, magnetic
fields, or other weak perturbations that break a system’s periodicity.

11. CONCLUSION

This LDRD set out to develop a real-space approach to material topology that does not rely on
a band structure. The motivation was that many materials and devices of current interest fall
outside the scope of conventional momentum-space classification methods. Disorder, aperiodicity,
finite sample geometry, nonlinear response, non-Hermiticity, strong spatial inhomogeneity, and
multi-scale structure can all obscure or eliminate the assumptions needed to define Bloch bands
and associated topological invariants. The central outcome of this project is the development
and application of a broad operator-theoretic framework for diagnosing topology directly from
real-space data.

The primary technical foundation of the project is the spectral localizer and its relationship to
numerical 𝐾-theory, Clifford spectra, and pseudospectral methods. By combining Hamiltonians
or wave operators with position operators and symmetry data, the spectral localizer provides local
topological markers that can be evaluated in finite systems without periodic boundary conditions.
In addition to producing topological indices, this framework yields a localizer gap that quantifies
the robustness of the topological classification. This measure of protection is particularly valuable
in realistic settings where a conventional bulk spectral gap may be absent, obscured by disorder, or
not meaningful because the system is nonlinear, non-Hermitian, or spatially inhomogeneous.

Several major advances were achieved. First, the project extended real-space topological classifica-
tion to nonlinear systems. In nonlinear materials, the effective Hamiltonian depends on the system’s
occupation, and nonlinear effects can locally change the topology of a material. The nonlinear
spectral localizer framework developed here provides a method for identifying nonlinearity-induced
topological interfaces and for characterizing the robustness of nonlinear topological modes. This
result expands topological classification beyond the usual linear single-particle setting and provides
a route to understanding topological solitons, pump-induced topological phases, and dynamically
evolving topological structures.

Second, the project demonstrated the power of local real-space topology in artificial quantum mate-
rials. By applying local Chern markers to artificial graphene in magnetic fields, the project showed
that the spectral localizer can recover experimentally relevant Hall topology while accounting for
realistic disorder and continuum degrees of freedom. This work also revealed topological states
associated with antidot structures and provided a framework for interpreting Hofstadter physics and
Chern phases in multi-scale materials without relying on simplified low-energy models.

Third, the project advanced the mathematical foundations of the spectral localizer through the
study of Clifford spectra and pseudospectra in 𝐶 ∗ -algebras. This work clarified how the finite-
dimensional numerical methods used in quantum material models relate to infinite-dimensional
operator theory. It also identified examples in which infinite-dimensional Clifford spectra differ

 31

qualitatively from their finite-dimensional analogues, thereby strengthening the theoretical basis for
applying pseudospectral and localizer methods to realistic continuum and large-scale systems.
Fourth, the project established new directions for dynamically reconfigurable topological devices.
In nonlinear exciton-polariton systems, a spatially patterned pump can locally alter the Chern
topology of a lattice and thereby move the interface along which chiral edge states propagate. This
mechanism enables topological routing paths to be changed after fabrication, addressing a key
limitation of conventional topological photonic devices whose edge-state pathways are fixed by
their static geometry. The same general principle may be applicable to other nonlinear photonic,
acoustic, or electronic platforms in which external fields can locally tune the material response.
Fifth, the project developed real-space approaches to weak and crystalline topology, including
fragile topology. Fragile topological phases are difficult to characterize using traditional stable
𝐾-theory because they can be trivialized by adding atomic bands. By using matrix homotopy
and symmetry-induced matrix constraints, this project produced a position-space diagnostic for
fragile topology and an associated robustness measure. This capability is important for studying
disordered or aperiodic systems, photonic crystals embedded in gapless environments, and moiré
materials where fragile topology is believed to be connected to delocalized wavefunctions and
correlated electronic phases.
Sixth, the project demonstrated that real-space markers can track dimensional crossovers between
topological classifications. In class D Shiba lattices, local invariants derived from the spectral
localizer were used to follow the evolution from two-dimensional Z topology to one-dimensional
Z2 topology as one spatial dimension is reduced. This provides a way to determine when a finite-
width material behaves as a genuinely higher-dimensional topological phase and when it crosses
over to a lower-dimensional one. Such information is directly relevant to nanowires, thin films,
superconducting heterostructures, and other reduced-dimensional devices.
Seventh, the project connected real-space topology to semiconductor artificial materials and val-
leytronics. The study of artificial hexagonal boron nitride-like semiconductor meta-graphene
showed that valley Hall edge states can persist over device-relevant length scales even in the pres-
ence of realistic disorder. Although valley Chern numbers are not protected in the same way
as strong topological invariants, the analysis indicated that semiconductor superlattices provide
a tunable platform in which valley Hall transport may remain sufficiently robust for nanoscale
applications.
Eighth, the project produced an efficient sparse-matrix approach for parity-based Z2 topology.
By determining the sign of the Pfaffian of a skew-symmetric matrix derived from the spectral
localizer, this method enables local classification of quantum spin Hall and related parity-based
phases without computing dense spectral projectors. This computational advance is significant
because it allows real-space Z2 classification to be applied to large tight-binding systems, continuum
discretizations, heterostructures, and aperiodic systems where existing methods are impractical or
require assumptions about spectral or spin gaps.
Finally, the project developed a band-unfolding approach based on the quadratic pseudospectrum.
This method generalizes the concept of a dispersion relation to systems in which the Hamiltonian
does not exactly commute with translation operators. Instead of requiring exact Bloch eigenstates,
the quadratic pseudospectrum identifies approximate joint eigenstates localized in energy and

 32

momentum and provides bounds on the accuracy of that localization. This approach creates a
bridge between conventional band theory and real-space descriptions of disordered, quasicrystalline,
weakly perturbed, or large-supercell systems.
Overall, the project achieved its central scientific objective: it demonstrated that real-space operator
methods can identify and quantify topological phenomena in systems that are inaccessible or poorly
suited to conventional band-theoretic approaches. These advances now provide the mathematical
and computational infrastructure needed to pursue that original long-term goal more effectively,
while also opening new research directions in nonlinear topology, reconfigurable photonics, arti-
ficial quantum materials, fragile topology, sparse topological algorithms, and pseudospectral band
analysis. In addition, this project contributed to the development of two reviews on the spectral
localizer framework [3, 11], and additional studies in nonlinear and interacting systems [5, 4].
The broader impact of this work is the establishment of a flexible framework for topology-driven
materials discovery and device design. Because the methods operate directly in real space, they
are compatible with finite geometries, interfaces, disorder, continuum models, and large-scale
numerical simulations. They can therefore be used not only to classify idealized bulk materials,
but also to predict whether a specific fabricated or simulated device will support robust interface-
localized states. This capability is essential for translating topological materials from abstract band
classifications to practical technologies.
In summary, this LDRD established Sandia capabilities in real-space and pseudospectral approaches
to material topology. The resulting framework advances both the theoretical understanding of topo-
logical phases and the practical ability to identify robust states in realistic materials and devices.
These developments support future efforts in accelerated materials discovery, low-power micro-
electronics, photonics, and quantum information science, and provide a foundation for continued
work at the intersection of topology, operator theory, and materials design.
