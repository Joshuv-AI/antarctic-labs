Islam Eldifrawi, Shengrui Wang, Amine Trabelsi

Abstract
With the vast amount of content uploaded every hour, along with the AI generated content that can include hallucinations, Automated Fact-Checking (AFC) has become increasingly vital, as it is infeasible for human fact-checkers to manually verify the sheer volume of information generated online. Professional fact-checkers have identified several gaps in existing AFC systems, noting a misalignment between how these systems operate and how fact-checking is performed in practice. In this paper, we introduce CAAFC (Chronological Actionable Automated Fact-Checker), a framework designed to bridge these gaps. It surpasses SOTA AFC and hallucination detection systems across multiple benchmark datasets. CAAFC operates on claims, conversations, and dialogues, enabling it not only to detect factual errors and hallucinations, but also to correct them by providing actionable justifications supported by primary information sources. Furthermore, CAAFC can update evidence and knowledge bases by incorporating recent and contextual information when necessary, thereby enhancing the reliability of fact verification.

Introduction

Researchers have been investigating methods to produce AFC and hallucination detection systems, leveraging approaches from NLP, machine learning, knowledge representation, and database systems to predict the veracity of claims

AFC pipelines use LLMs that start by detecting “check-worthy” claims, then they retrieve relevant evidence (information used as reference to judge the claim veracity) from trusted sources for each claim, then each claim is deemed true, false, or unverifiable, generating a human-readable explanation as Shown in Figure

However, many challenges are still present in both fields. We summarize the most pressing of these challenges in the form of the following four research questions:

RQ1:
How can we obtain up-to-date evidence to judge claim veracity? Can we use this new evidence and update the knowledge of our databases?

RQ2:
Professional fact-checkers follow different processes from most AFC systems. Does explicitly following fact-checkers’ methodologies lead to improved fact-checking performance?

RQ3:
Can the tasks of non-factual hallucination detection and automated fact-checking be unified, enabling the use of one detection and correction system for both tasks?

RQ4:
Given access to up-to-date evidence, is the use of large-scale frontier LLMs as fact-checkers still necessary? Alternatively, can AFC be decomposed into smaller sub-tasks that can be addressed by smaller quantized LLMs while preserving the performance?

RQ5:
While most existing fact-checking systems focus primarily on claim verification, their generated justifications often lack explicit generation structure or rules. How can we generate high-quality, structured justifications that enhance transparency and foster user trust?

18
summarize the key differences between how professional fact-checkers conduct their work and how most AFC systems operate. One of the most significant distinctions lies in evidence retrieval, as professional fact-checkers prioritize primary / original sources such as official statements, and photos or videos documenting an alleged event. In contrast, most AFC systems rely heavily on secondary sources, such as media outlets reporting on an event, introducing interpretative bias rather than presenting raw evidence. In addition, professional fact-checkers provide explicit justifications for their choice of information sources as part of a robust editorial process. They also emphasize the importance of temporal grounding, ensuring that all retrieved evidence is accompanied by a timestamp, as factual correctness is inherently chronological.

Another key distinction lies in how the veracity of claims is justified. For professional fact-checkers, the most critical property of an explanation is traceability
18
. This requires the inclusion of direct links to the evidence sources. The importance of providing supporting links for actionable explanations has also been emphasized by

Actionability was evaluated by
4

Specifically, actionable explanations are those that identify factual errors in false claims, provide explicit corrections, and cite the evidence sources that support these corrections. In this work, we generate actionable explanations using feedback from FinGrAct as an evaluation framework to assess the explanations quality.

C1:

To address RQ1

Using CAAFC, we introduce a novel method for detecting outdated or missing knowledge in databases, and for correcting this knowledge based on newly retrieved, chronological evidence. C2:

To address RQ2
, CAAFC follows the professional fact-checkers workflow in obtaining the evidence from its original primary source. For instance, consider a claim in which Trump tweeted that the the American economy state is great. Trump’s Twitter account is preferred over news outlets reporting this event, as it serves as the primary source. By prioritizing the

C3:

To address RQ3

C4:

To address RQ4
, CAAFC outperforms SOTA models (like GPT-4, GPT-5.2, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and LLAMA3.1-70B) across multiple benchmark datasets in both tasks, with quantized Gemma3-27B demonstrating that dividing the complex task of misinformation detection and correction into simpler sub-tasks leads to decent fact verification.

Related Work

In this section, we highlight other existing fact-checking systems and pipelines.

15
pipeline (INFACT) starts by enriching the claim with metadata (author and source URL) and prompting an LLM to reformulate it accordingly. INFACT then generates ten questions using manually curated in-context examples. For each question, the system retrieves evidence. Each retrieved document is embedded using the gte-base-en-v1.5 model and ranked by semantic similarity. INFACT iteratively evaluates the results, prompting the LLM to answer the question. After processing all questions, the LLM determines the claim’s veracity. They achieved 0.72 accuracy on AVeriTeC.

The Loki
10
pipeline comprises five components: a Decomposer that extracts individual claims, a Checkworthiness Identifier that filters non-worthy content, a Query Generator that formulates retrieval queries, an Evidence Retriever that collects supporting information, and a Claim Verifier that assesses the evidence and presents relevant snippets. This pipeline is optimized to minimize the number of steps and model calls, and is released as an open-source system that can operate with any LLM as its underlying model.

Most of the previous work primarily focuses on analyzing the strengths and weaknesses of LLMs across a broad range of tasks, without addressing the lack of chronological reasoning in the retrieved evidence. In particular, their reliance on SERP- or Serper-based is susceptible to retrieving evidence from secondary sources that add their own interpretation, their retrieval does not account for temporal consistency, nor do they tackle the challenge of generating high-quality justifications.

Methodology

CAAFC is a module based frame-work with six modules using quantized LLMs as shown in Figure

The Extractor Segmentor:
The Extractor Segmentor is responsible for claim extraction and decomposing long and complex claims or conversations into simpler atomic claims that are easier to process and verify. This step is particularly important for claims containing multiple statements. For example, the claim “Earth is a blue, flat star” can be decomposed into three atomic claims;Earth is blue, Earth is a star, and Earth is round.
12
demonstrated that using atomic claims leads to more precise identification of inaccuracies, and greater transparency during evidence retrieval. Therefore, we base our prompt adopting their approach to segment claims into atomic units.
We extract all possible claims, unlike what happens in the classical AFC pipelines, which only focus on check-worthy claims.

The Primary Chronological Evidence Retriever:
This module processes the list of atomic claims, together with their corresponding date, to retrieve reliable and time-relevant information.
Unlike classical AFC, we direct our evidence retrieval towards the primary sources that should be checked to verify the list of the atomic claims.
Fact-checkers don’t cite, for example, media citing source, they try to find the most primary, most original source of data
18
. For example, consider the claim made by Donald Trump in 2025 that the Biden administration spent millions on making mice transgender. Secondary sources such as CNN (often characterized as left-leaning), IMDB, and other outlets reported that the accusation was false. In contrast, primary sources, including official expenditure records published on WhiteHouse.gov, were interpreted as supporting the claim. To mitigate the risk of retrieving evidence from potentially biased sources, we introduce directive search, which guides retrieval toward primary-source evidence.
The module searches for chronological knowledge encompassing both the time when the claim was made and the present using the fetched primary sources, thereby providing as much contextual evidence as possible which is different from classical AFC.

The Actionable Justifier:
This module is new to the AFC pipeline and integrates the claim, the retrieved evidence, and the atomic claims with their respective justifications to generate a combined actionable justification. In the context of AFC, actionability refers to producing an explanation that not only highlights the specific errors present in the claim but also provides corrections for those errors and its sources. The prompt used in this module is shown in Figure
7
in Appendix
A.3
.

The Actionability Evaluator:
To ensure that the generated justification is actionable, this module evaluates its quality using FinGrAct
4
. The interaction between this module, the justification revisory module, and the actionable justifier module ensures actionability is produced within the justifications. In FinGrAct, a score of zero mostly indicates that the claim is true and does not require an actionable explanation. FinGrAct evaluates the actionability of a justification based on three criteria. The first criterion assesses whether the justification highlights all factual errors in the claim: a score of 0 indicates that it does not detect any errors, 1 indicates partial detection, and 2 indicates that all errors in the claim are correctly identified. The second criterion evaluates whether the justification corrects the identified errors. A score of 0 means no corrections are provided, 1 means partial correction, and 2 means that all errors are fully corrected. The third criterion measures whether the information sources cited in the evidence are functional, relevant, and supportive of the justification. A score of 0 indicates that the links are non-functional, 1 that they are functional, 2 that they are relevant, and 3 that they fully support the justification. The total FinGrAct score is obtained by summing the scores from these criteria, resulting in a maximum score of 7 points.

Therefore, if the justification’s actionability score is at least 4 out of 7 points, the justification is considered to have an acceptable degree of actionability. If the justification fails to meet this threshold, the module identifies its weaknesses and provides feedback to the justification revisory, prompting an enhanced and more actionable revision. This refinement process aims to achieve a score of 4 points or more on the actionability scale.

Justification Revisory:
This new module refines the generated justification by incorporating feedback from FinGrAct, with the goal of improving its actionability. Specifically, it enhances the justification by adding missing corrections and highlighting previously unaddressed factual errors. We mainly employ Gemma 3–27B in this module to reduce computational overhead while maintaining performance. The prompt for the justification revisory module is in Figure

4
Datasets

To evaluate the performance of CAAFC, we used four benchmark datasets. For each dataset, we relied solely on the test set, since our approach is based on zero-shot CoT prompting, therefore, does not require access to the training data.

The first dataset, AVeriTeC
17
, contains 500 real-world claims in its test set. Each claim is accompanied a fine-grained veracity label (122 supported claims, 305 refuted, 35 not enough evidence, and 38 conflicting evidence).We merged the two classes "not enough evidence" and "conflicting evidence" into a single class labeled "unverifiable". Dataset labels aggregation is to unify the output of CAAFC, however, CAAFC justifications provide fine-grained details on its classification. More details on aggregation and its effect are in Appendix
A.10
.

The second dataset, CoverBench
8
, contains 733 claims in the testset (330 labeled as false and 403 as true). Each entry includes the claim, its unprocessed evidence, the label, the claim’s source, and its domain. The primary purpose of this dataset in our evaluation is to assess the robustness of our framework in handling complex, and domain-diverse claim verification.

The third dataset, FACTors
2
, contains a collection of recent claims spanning a broad chronological range. Notably, 196 of these claims emerged after the release dates of models such as Llama3.3-70B and Gemma3-27B, making this dataset particularly suitable for evaluating our framework’s ability to verify claims that the underlying LLMs have not been previously exposed to as shown in Appendix
A.9
. The FACTors test set comprises 5000 claims, categorized as follows: 221 true, 3735 false, 632 misleading, 264 partially true, and 148 unverifiable claims. Following our classification strategy, we merged the misleading, and partially true categories into the false class.

The final dataset is DiaHalu
3
, a challenging dialogue-based benchmark featuring longer conversations and multiple categories of hallucinations. We focus exclusively on the factual and non-factual dialogues; we have 122 and 123 conversations respectively. We also experimented with HaluEval dataset that focuses on non-factual hallucination detection in dialogues without any sampling in Appendix
A.11
.

Experiments and Results

5.1
Evidence Evaluation

In this experiment,
we aim to evaluate the quality of the evidence retrieved from Google.
In the literature, the proxy-reference evaluation method
7
;
1
is commonly used for this purpose. Their approach assesses evidence based on two criteria: (1) the veracity verdict, where a correct verdict implies that the retrieved evidence is valid, and (2) the similarity between the retrieved evidence and the reference evidence. However, this method implicitly assumes that the reference evidence is always optimal, overlooking the possibility that it may be outdated or lacking contextual information that was unavailable at the time it was collected.

To identify instances where the retrieved evidence may surpass the dataset evidence-for example, when the latter is outdated or lacks sufficient context-we prompted three LLMs to generate independent veracity verdicts. Specifically, we used Llama 3.3-70B, Gemma 3-27B, and GPT-OSS-120B. When all LLMs produced the same veracity verdict that contradicted the dataset’s label given the retrieved evidence (e.g., a claim labeled as true in the dataset but predicted as false by all LLMs), we conduct a comparative analysis between our retrieved evidence and the evidence provided in the dataset. The three LLMs are just used as a candidate indicator of problematic instances that are thoroughly analyzed in Appendix
A.6
.

We analyzed all the instances where LLMs agree among themselves on a certain veracity label but they contradict with the dataset label. We found that this contradiction is either because of outdated information, or missing context in the dataset evidence information.

In this experiment, the Actionable Justifier, Justification Revisory, and Actionability Evaluator modules were disabled. The pipeline therefore consisted of only three components: the extractor-segmentor, the chronological evidence retriever, and the fact-checker. Each claim required three calls—one per active module. On average, the inference time for the extractor-segmentor using Gemma 3–27B was 1.5 seconds. The chronological evidence retrieval, implemented via Selenium on Google AI Mode, required approximately 3 seconds. The fact-checker module required an additional 1.5 seconds, resulting in a total average processing time of 6 seconds per claim. By comparison, GPT-5.2, implemented following the framework of

6
Conclusion

We introduce CAAFC, a framework designed to breakdown AFC into simpler subtasks. CAAFC consistently outperforms SOTA models across multiple datasets, and can be applied to detect and correct hallucinations in LLMs generations. In addition, CAAFC produces actionable, and chronological justifications, supported by reliable sources, thereby enhancing transparency, and explainability.

Limitations

1.

Language limitation:
CAAFC is currently designed and evaluated exclusively on English-language content. Its performance on claims, conversations, or evidence in other languages remains unexplored and may require multilingual reasoning capabilities. In future work, we will explore the usage of multi-linguage LLMs and perform evaluations on multi-lingual datasets.

2.

Risks of automatically updating knowledge bases from web evidence:
A further concern arises from the political and ideological skew that may be implicitly encoded in search engines and web-scale language models, which CAAFC leverages as its primary evidence source. Because ranking algorithms and training data can over-represent state or corporate media, particular geopolitical blocs, or high-SEO partisan outlets, the retrieved evidence may systematically privilege certain narratives over others, especially on contentious topics such as elections, pandemics, or international conflicts.

3.

Computational overhead:
The framework may require multiple LLM calls per instance, which can increase inference latency. However, it offers a substantial reduction in GPU memory usage. CAAFC requires only 17 GB of GPU RAM using the quantized Gemma3-27B LLM, whereas GPT-5.2 would require significantly more. In future work, we plan to explore fast APIs for the evidence retrieval component and upgrade our hardware for higher compute power to further reduce overall response time. In addition, our experiments were conducted using an NVIDIA Quadro RTX 8000 GPU. Since the hardware specifications used by OpenAI for GPT-5.2 is not publicly disclosed, a direct comparison of inference times may not be entirely equitable.

A.1
FinGrAct Scoring

A score of 0 indicates that the claim is true, and no supporting links were provided by the Google. When the score equals 3, three main scenarios may occur: (1) supporting links for a true claim are provided, (2) the error detection score is 2 while the error correction score is 1 with no links provided, or (3) the error correction score is 2 while the error detection score is 1 with no links provided. For scores above 3, the link score remains 3, and the increase arises from improvements in error detection and/or error correction. This observation justifies setting our acceptance threshold at 4.

A.2
Ablation studies

The comparisons between CAAFC and the aforementioned state-of-the-art models are based on accuracy and macro F1-score of the predicted verdicts or labels. Therefore, removing modules designed to enhance justification quality—such as the actionable justifier, justification revisory, and actionability evaluator—would not substantially affect overall classification performance. Nevertheless, these modules remain important for producing high-quality justifications.

The Fact-Checker module is central to the AFC process and therefore indispensable. Moreover, the extractor–segmentor produces outputs that feed into the Fact-Checker, the final verdict algorithm, and the actionable justifier; removing it would require substantial redesign of CAAFC’s architecture. Importantly, the extractor–segmentor gives CAAFC an advantage over some frontier LLMs by extracting claims that —including those that present subjective opinions as facts and those inferred from paragraphs—as demonstrated in Appendix
A.8
.

We evaluated the impact of the evidence retrieval component by removing it and measuring CAAFC’s performance in its absence in experiment
5.2
. In addition, we evaluated the impact of removing the primary source fetcher by measuring the resulting accuracy and F1-score across the datasets. No significant changes were observed in either metric. However, we argue that this component remains essential, as Google search may rely on secondary sources such as blogs or platforms like Reddit, which can contain biased or unverified information. Therefore, we retain this module to ensure greater evidence reliability and robustness. Furthermore, this component enhances the reproducibility of the evidence when primary sources exist, since the retrieved evidence originates from the same source.

It is worth noting that incorporating chronological evidence retrieved from Google led to consistent accuracy improvements across all datasets between Experiment
5.2
after data cleaning and Experiment
5.3
using Gemma3-27B in CAAFC. On AVeriTeC , accuracy increased from 0.880 to 0.8940; on CoverBench, from 0.810 to 0.829; and on FACTors, from 0.914 to 0.919. These results indicate that incorporating chronology into the evidence retrieval process positively impacts fact-verification accuracy. FACTors is the most recent dataset among the three, and therefore likely required fewer evidence updates compared to AVeriTeC or CoverBench.

How does Google rank search results and how does AI on Google search work

Google ranks websites in search results by using complex algorithms that sort through its index based on several hundred signals to provide the most relevant and helpful results for a user’s query. The primary goal is to match the user’s intent with high-quality, usable, and authoritative content.
2
2

2

https://www.semrush.com/blog/Google-search-algorithm/

The ranking process involves three main stages:

1.

Crawling: Google uses automated programs called "crawlers" or "bots" to discover new and updated pages on the web by following links and reading sitemaps.

2.

Indexing: The information gathered by the crawlers is analyzed, and the content is stored in Google’s massive index (a digital library). This process involves understanding the page’s content, images, and key elements like title tags and headings.
3
3

3

https://blog.photobiz.com/blog-post/breaking-down-Googles-search-algorithm

3.

Ranking: When a user enters a search query, Google’s ranking systems sort through the indexed pages and order them based on relevance and quality to that specific query and user context.
4
4

4

https://developers.Google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/how-search-works

Key Ranking signals

While Google uses a vast number of signals, the most important signals generally fall into these categories:

1.

Relevance and Search Intent: Google first establishes the intent behind a user’s query (e.g., informational, navigational, transactional) and prioritizes pages that are most likely to satisfy that intent. The content should use relevant keywords naturally in headings, body text, and title tags.

2.

Content Quality and E-E-A-T: High-quality, original, and helpful content is crucial. Google emphasizes E-E-A-T (Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, and Trustworthiness), especially for sensitive topics ("Your Money or Your Life" topics). Content that offers unique insights, is well-researched, and regularly updated tends to rank higher.

3.

Backlinks (Authority): Backlinks, or links from other websites to a page, act as "votes of confidence." Links from established, high-authority websites significantly boost a page’s perceived credibility and authority.

4.

Context and Personalization: Results are customized based on the user’s location, language, device type, and search history to provide the most relevant information.

AI on Google Search summarizes search engine results. Google has integrated Gemini 3 Pro into its search experience to summarize complex information.

In this paper Google AI is solely used for the summarization for Google results fetched from primary sources.

We did not use commercial scraping services such as Serper for two main reasons. First, these services require paid subscriptions. Second, they typically return a list of links related to the query, which necessitates additional steps—namely scraping the linked pages, filtering for relevant content, and then summarizing the extracted information. Each of these stages (scraping, relevance filtering, and summarization) introduces potential sources of error and increases system complexity.

In contrast, the public Google AI Mode directly provides a synthesized summary of the most relevant content, eliminating the need for separate scraping and summarization steps. This reduces error propagation and simplifies the pipeline, which motivated our decision to use it in combination with Selenium. However, this choice introduces higher latency compared to directly interacting with structured APIs. The steps carried out by Google to validate its synthesized summary is mentioned in Appendix
A.7
.

Regarding the reproducibility of using Google AI Mode or its alternatives (e.g., Serper or Serp), search engine results continuously change and are updated as new information becomes available. This dynamic nature highlights the importance of chronology: any changes in the world’s knowledge over time can be captured through chronologically ordered retrieved evidence.

A.6.5
How does errors propagate in CAAFC pipeline

Errors in the Actionable Justifier are mitigated through the refinement cycle involving FinGrAct and the Justification Revisory modules. In contrast, errors in the extractor-segmentor, primary chronological evidence retrieval, or fact-checker modules are not self-correcting and therefore have a direct impact on the final verdict. In the following points we will show examples of errors occurring in these three modules:

Errors in the extractor-segmentor:
In case an error happened in the subclaims extraction, for example the claim stating that
"From 8th October the UK government will combine weekly flu and covid reports."
, Gemma3-27B produced the following sub-claims
["The UK government combined weekly flu and covid reports from 8th October.","The information was combined together from 8th October."]
, and clearly the second sub-claim is the error as it is not mentioned in the original claim. Google evidence is:
"The official body that produced these reports, Public Health England (PHE), began publishing a combined report on that date for the 2020 to 2021 season. However, there are important details to clarify: The reports were combined, not the data. Separate surveillance data on COVID-19 and influenza cases were published within a single document for the convenience of the public and health officials."
and it is clearly addressing both sub-claims. Based on this the fact-checker output is:
{
’subclaims’: [
{
’text’: ’The UK government combined weekly flu and covid reports from 8th October.’, ’label’: ’true’, ’justification’: ’The evidence states that Public Health England, a UK government body, began publishing a combined report on October 8, 2020.’
}
,
{
’text’: ’The information was combined together from 8th October.’, ’label’: ’false’, ’justification’: ’The evidence clarifies that the reports were combined, but not the data itself; separate surveillance data was published within a single document.’
}
]
}
. This type of errors is not automatically detectable. This constitutes a misinterpretation error (the second error pattern) by Gemma3–27B, which inferred a hidden implication that the data had been combined. However, this implication is not stated by the claim itself.

If Google retrieves non-related evidence (e.g., due to hallucination or retrieval errors), the claim is marked as unverifiable during the fact-checking stage, and the generated explanation explicitly states that the evidence is unrelated to the claim. A.10
Concern about the aggregation of data labels in fine-grained datasets

Because CAAFC is evaluated on datasets that adopt heterogeneous labeling schemes, label aggregation is necessary to produce a unified and consistent output space across benchmarks. For example, labels such as misleading, partially true, and false are consolidated into a single false category. This harmonization is a methodological requirement rather than a design choice intended to favor CAAFC.

We acknowledge the concern that such aggregation might appear to create an unfair comparison with state-of-the-art (SOTA) baselines evaluated under the original fine-grained labels. However, the opposite scenario also occurs. In the case of CoverBench and the sampled DiaHalu—both contains only two labels (true or false)—CAAFC is capable of producing an additional unverifiable prediction. This introduces a structural disadvantage for CAAFC, as any unverifiable output is effectively penalized under the binary evaluation scheme.

Despite this clear disadvantage, CAAFC still surpasses the SOTA baselines on CoverBench and the sampled DiaHalu (2 out of 4 datasets). This demonstrates that the observed performance gains cannot be attributed to label aggregation.

Furthermore, we provide detailed justifications explaining why each claim is deemed true, false, or unverifiable, compensating for the lack of fine-grained classification.

A.12
Human comparison between dataset evidence and Google evidence

For evidence-quality evaluation, the framework uses three LLMs (Llama 3.3, Gemma 3-27B, GPT-OSS-120B) to decide when dataset labels/evidence are wrong and to prefer Google AI evidence. Then the same set of models is used as the fact-checker inside CAAFC, and performance is reported against the cleaned datasets that were curated using those LLMs. This may create a circular evaluation: LLMs are both judge and system under test. To mitigate this effect we perform an external human audit of the removed instances.

Three human annotators, MSc students familiar with NLP tasks, aged between 22 and 30 years, were asked to determine whether the Google AI–retrieved evidence is better than the original dataset evidence, following the criteria and guidelines shown in Figure
10
. The annotators didn’t know the sources of evidences or any related metadata. The annotators didn’t receive any training. There are around 375 chronological mismatch instances in total across the datasets. The evaluation was conducted on 100 randomly selected examples exhibiting evidence–chronology mismatch patterns across the four combined datasets. The 100 examples include claims that were unanimously deemed by the 3 LLMs as true while the dataset label is false, and vice versa. One of these two evidences support the claim while the other refutes it.

Annotators are instructed to assign the veracity label based solely on their selected preferred evidence. This requirement is intended to ensure careful attention and logical consistency throughout the annotation process. For example, if annotators select the first evidence as the preferred evidence because it supports the claim, yet assign a false veracity label, this indicates an internal inconsistency in their evaluation. Such a discrepancy suggests that the evidence assessment and the veracity judgment were not aligned, likely reflecting insufficient attention to the example.

In total, Annotator 1 exhibited 2 such inconsistencies, Annotator 2 exhibited 11, and Annotator 3 exhibited 7. Table
8
shows the Pearson correlation between the annotators. Since Annotator 2 exhibited the highest number of discrepancies, this annotator also shows the lowest correlation with the other two annotators.

You are asked to perform two sequential tasks:

1.

Evidence Comparison Task:
Compare two pieces of evidence (Evidence 0 and Evidence 1) related to the same claim and determine which one is better based on specific evaluation criteria mentioned later.

2.

Claim Veracity Evaluation Task:
Using the preferred evidence from Task 1, determine the veracity of the claim.

You must complete Task 1 first, then proceed to Task 2.

Task 1 Evidence Comparison

You will receive a claim, evidence 0, and evidence 1. Your goal is to determine which evidence is stronger and more reliable for fact-checking the claim

•

Step 1: Evaluate Each Evidence Independently based on how recent and temporally appropriate the evidence is relative to the claim, how directly and sufficiently the evidence addresses the claim, and if it includes all the necessary details.

•

Step 2: Choose your preferred evidence (evidence 0 or evidence 1)

Task 2 Claim Veracity Evaluation

Use only the preferred evidence to determine the claim veracity (true, false):

•

True – The evidence clearly supports the claim.

•

False – The evidence contradicts the claim.

Figure 10:
Instructions provided to the human annotators

We also assess agreement among human annotators using inter-annotator agreement Krippendorff’s Alpha for multi-rater reliability which reports 0.891. This high agreement is expected due to the following factors:

1.

Clear Annotation Guidelines – annotators have strict, well-defined rules. In addition, there is a direct channel (emails) for them to send any questions regarding the instructions.

2.

Objective or Easy-to-Classify Data – Tasks with minimal ambiguity (e.g., labeling with ’0, or ’ labels like "true and false") often lead to high agreement.

3.

Annotators with similar backgrounds tend to agree more than crowd-sourced annotators.

A recruitment email was sent to postgraduate students and three annotators volunteered for this task. Given this, we can assert that their annotations were conducted solely based on their understanding of the provided instructions.

Next, we evaluate agreement between the majority vote of the human annotations and the unanimous labels produced by the LLMs. A majority vote across the three annotators is used to obtain a single human reference label, and agreement between this majority label and the LLM decision is measured using Cohen’s Kappa.

The Cohen’s Kappa for preferred evidence selection between the LLMs and the majority vote of the human annotators is 0.979, indicating significant agreement. For the veracity labels, Cohen’s Kappa is 0.946, which also reflects a very high level of agreement. The slight difference between these scores is mainly attributable to discrepancies and internal inconsistencies among the human annotators, particularly in cases where an annotator selected a piece of preferred evidence but assigned a contradictory veracity label.
