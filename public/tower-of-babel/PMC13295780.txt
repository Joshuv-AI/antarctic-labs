Supporting nurses to improve clinical trial discussions with patients with lung cancer using the Lung I-ACT tool: a mixed methods pilot study
Authors: Benjamin Lond, Christopher Dodd, Zoe Davey, Iain Richard Williamson, Janette Rawlinson, John McPhelim, Liz Darlison, Catherine Henshall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmpowering nurses to discuss clinical trials with patients can increase recruitment rates. Clinical trials are essential for improving lung cancer patient outcomes; in the short term, they provide individuals access to new treatments and care regimens which, longer term, have the potential to advance clinical care. However, recruitment into lung cancer clinical trials is as low as 5%, and lung cancer nurses feel ill-equipped to signpost clinical trial opportunities to patients. This study aimed to pilot and assess the acceptability and utility of the ‘Lung I-ACT tool’: a newly developed resource comprising a patient-facing leaflet and a nurse-facing poster, designed to help lung cancer nurses initiate clinical trial discussions with patients.MethodsA 6-month, mixed methods, pilot study was conducted at seven NHS hospital sites in the UK; four were intervention sites, three were control sites. Data on self-efficacy, knowledge, confidence, awareness and current practice were collected using online surveys for nurses at baseline, 3 and 6 months, and online interviews with lung cancer nurses and patients from intervention sites. Survey data were analysed using descriptive and inferential statistics, and interview data were thematically analysed via Framework Method.ResultsThirty-four nurse respondents were recruited to the survey. Survey findings indicated increased frequency of clinical trial discussions at the intervention sites, and increased nurse awareness, confidence and knowledge to navigate these discussions. Eleven nurses and four patients were interviewed; these data comprised five themes regarding lung cancer nurses’ and patients’ views of the Lung I-ACT tool: (1) content and format of the tool, (2) application and utility of the tool in practice, (3) perceived changes to practice, (4) knowledge, awareness and confidence to discuss clinical trials, (5) contextual and situational factors impacting the presentation of clinical trials.ConclusionThe Lung I-ACT tool improves nurses’ confidence in discussing trials with patients through increasing their awareness of clinical trial opportunities and helping them to structure conversations.

Introduction

Globally, lung cancer incidence remains among the highest of all cancers diagnosed, with the highest mortality rate.1 In the UK, it is the third most diagnosed cancer but has the highest mortality rate.2

Smoking is the most common cause of lung cancer; other causes include air pollution, secondhand smoke, industrial substances and radon, family history and previous lung disease.3 Treatments for lung cancer include surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy and targeted immunotherapy; treatment offered depends on tumour histology, size and grading, whether metastatic disease is present as well as individual health and fitness.3

Lung cancer clinical trials underpin the UK Government’s plan for improving lung cancer patients’ outcomes. They can provide access to new and existing innovative treatments and can lead to advances in their effectiveness and application, benefiting the health economy through prosperous relationships between the government and health and life sciences industry.4 The benefits of high levels of enrolment in clinical trials are apparent at individual, population, policy and economic levels. Empowering nurses to have discussions with patients about clinical trials can increase recruitment rates. Around 95% of patients in the UK report being open to considering participation in cancer clinical trials if eligible.5 Despite this apparent enthusiasm, the proportion of lung cancer patients enrolling in clinical trials remains low globally at 5%–13%.68 Reasons for this low conversion rate include the way in which clinical trial information is presented to patients, strict eligibility criteria and geographical variation in clinical trial availability, in addition to patients’ misconceptions of research and fears of being treated as a ‘guineapig’.9 A recent survey found that less than half of patients with lung cancer recalled discussing the option of clinical trial participation with a healthcare professional.10 A way of improving on this is for healthcare professionals to be better supported to have clinical trial discussions with patients to improve recruitment.11 In the UK, lung cancer nurses are commonly based in hospitals and are a consistent part of the care pathway, from diagnosis onwards. As such, they are well placed to have these discussions with patients. Nurses have more contact time with patients than other healthcare professionals, and the trusting relationships they build with patients positions them to positively influence the number and quality of discussions they have with them about clinical trial opportunities.

Despite clear advantages of discussing clinical trial opportunities with patients, many lung cancer nurses are reluctant to do so, citing a lack of knowledge, confidence and time.12 13 Educational resources can help overcome these challenges by empowering healthcare professionals to adopt desired change behaviours14 and are effective in supporting them to lead informed discussions with patients about their care pathway and decision-making priorities.15 16 The Lung I-ACT tool has been designed, through rigorous methodological and theoretical processes, to support lung cancer nurses to have discussions with patients about clinical trial opportunities.13 In line with the Medical Research Council’s Framework for the Development of Complex Interventions,17 its interactive design was informed by a review of the international literature9 and focus groups and consultation meetings with lung cancer patients, carers and healthcare professionals.13 The tool was theoretically underpinned by the COM-B model of behavioural change, which proposes three necessary components—capability, opportunity and motivation—for any behaviour to occur, enabling targeted interventions to be developed to facilitate effective change.14

The Lung I-ACT tool has two elements: (1) a patient-facing information leaflet and (2) a nurse-facing poster (figure 1). The former contains basic trial and ‘myth busting’ information, including a brief description of what to expect if participating in a trial and a glossary of common trial-related terms. The leaflet acts as a structured, interactive guide and was designed for use by nurses to aid discussions with patients about clinical trials. The leaflet acts not only as a self-directed source of information for patients but also as an aid for nurses to introduce the topic of trials with patients at an appropriate time. The nurse-facing poster has been designed for use in non-patient-facing areas, acting as a reminder for nurses to discuss trials with patients. It includes space for locally available lung cancer clinical trials information to be added to it, promoting interactions between lung cancer nurses and research delivery nurses. Lung I-ACT is a novel and practical solution to a known problem facing clinical trial recruitment more broadly. This paper reports on a pilot study undertaken to test the acceptability of implementing Lung I-ACT across several NHS sites in the UK.

Methods

Aim

This study aimed to test the acceptability of implementing the Lung I-ACT tool across NHS lung cancer settings in the UK and was informed by the following interlinked objectives:

To examine nurses’ knowledge, awareness and confidence to discuss clinical trials with patients across seven UK NHS intervention and control sites.

To examine nurses’ knowledge, awareness and confidence to discuss clinical trials with patients across seven UK NHS intervention and control sites.

To compare any changes in knowledge, awareness and confidence between lung cancer nurses at intervention and control sites.

To explore lung cancer nurses’ and patients’ perspectives on the Lung I-ACT tool and its impact on clinical trial discussions.

Study design and setup

This mixed methods pilot study, consisting of online surveys and interviews, was conducted at seven NHS sites (four interventions and three controls) across England and Scotland. Intervention and control sites included both larger tertiary or teaching hospitals and smaller district general hospitals.

Intervention sites received printed and digital copies of the Lung I-ACT tool (poster and leaflet). The Lung I-ACT tool was developed as two paper-based resources with input from nurses and patients, who expressed preferences for tangible, hard copies that were easy to access and more prominent in clinical areas than digital options.13 Nurses at intervention sites attended online training sessions delivered by the research team to familiarise them with the tool; its aim and purpose and to encourage them to use the tool flexibly, as part of their routine clinical practice and usual interactions with patients. Control sites delivered usual clinical care and did not receive any training or access to the Lung I-ACT tool. Training was provided to intervention sites after collecting baseline data to promote intervention and control site parity.

Patient and public involvement

A former patient with lung cancer and clinical trial participant (JR) acted as a research collaborator, contributing to the pilot’s design, materials, and implementation, and the manuscript draft.

Participant recruitment and data collection

Participants were recruited between August 2024 and March 2025. Registered lung cancer nurses involved in frontline lung cancer patient care for at least 30% of their role, employed at participating sites were eligible to participate in the surveys and interviews. Patients with lung cancer aged 18 years or over who had been treated at one of the intervention sites and who had consented to a trial since the pilot had started were eligible to participate in the interviews. Nurses and patients were identified by local principal investigators and given participant information sheets and written informed consent was obtained.

Nurses at the intervention and control sites completed an online survey via the Qualtrics (2006) platform at baseline, 3 months and 6 months. The survey was circulated via email by members of the local research team at each site and reminder emails were also sent to prompt nurses to complete the survey. The survey included the 10 item ‘General Self-Efficacy Scale’ (GSE).18 Scores ranged from 10 to 40, with a higher score indicating greater perceived self-efficacy. Seven 5-point Likert-style questions assessing lung cancer nurses’ knowledge, awareness and confidence of clinical trials were also included (strongly agree=1 to strongly disagree=5). Current practice was assessed by asking nurses how many patients they had discussed clinical trials within the last 3 months and how many patients went on to enrol on a clinical trial in the same period. A mix of open and closed questions asked nurses to list any challenges and facilitators to having clinical trial discussions (online supplemental file 1).

Towards the end of the pilot, lung cancer nurses and patients from each of the four intervention sites were invited to take part in semistructured online interviews lasting approximately 30 min to gather views and experience of the Lung I-ACT tool. Nurses and patients from control sites were not interviewed as they had not seen the tool and were unable to comment on its use. Eligible individuals were invited to participate in person and via email by members of the local research team at each site. Interviews were conducted by members of the core research team (BL, CD and ZD) with previous training and experience of qualitative data collection, analysis and reporting. Separate topic guides for nurses and patients comprising opening/ice breaker questions and follow-on questions were utilised to facilitate interviewer consistency across the different interviews, build rapport and ensure that a range of domains were covered. The topic guide was used iteratively and supplemental questions were added as interviews progressed. Interviews were audio recorded and transcribed and deidentified by a local transcription company. Though not a criterion for completion of data collection, data saturation was thought to have been achieved by the research team who agreed that no new ideas or insights were being shared during the latter interviews (online supplemental file 1). Data analysis

Differences in GSE, current practice, and knowledge, awareness and confidence at baseline, 3 months and 6 months in both the control and intervention groups were assessed using the non-parametric Friedman’s test. A Bonferroni correction, with an adjusted alpha of 0.004, was applied to account for the impact of multiple comparisons on the family wise error rate. Participants lost to follow-up were excluded from the analysis. Statistical analysis was carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics V.29.

Open-text survey responses were analysed using a conventional approach to Content Analysis with code categories derived directly and inductively.19 Two research team members (BL, CD) conducted the initial coding in Microsoft Word, prior to further developing the analytic process through interpretive discussions with other core research team members (CH, ZD).

Interview data were analysed using the Framework Method.20 Core research team members (BL, CD, ZD, CH) individually read and inductively coded selected nurse and patient transcripts before meeting to develop two ‘working analytical frameworks’: one for nurse participant and one for patient participant transcripts. The remaining transcripts were subsequently coded by two team members (BL, CD). The analytical frameworks were used flexibly and iteratively, to allow new codes to be added as relevant, and regular online meetings were held for the core research team (BL, CD, CH, ZD) to review the ongoing coding process. Data and codes were then charted into each framework matrix, using Microsoft Excel. The matrices were used to organise and compare codes and participant quotes across the dataset; this facilitated the systematic clustering of codes and data into interpretive themes and subthemes and captured similarities and differences in perspectives across nurse and patient participants.

Interview themes and supporting quotes

Theme 1: content and format of the tool

Nurses and patients provided positive feedback about the content and format of the Lung I-ACT tool. They reported that they thought the leaflet provided patients with accessible information about clinical trials, clearly explained concepts via lay language and simple sentence structure and avoided dense words or phrases. Individual participants commented that they liked the overall presentation style, use of colour and images and commented that the tool would be easy to navigate by a range of users with different reading abilities and learning styles.

Theme 2: application and utility of the tool in practice

Participants at most sites said they were able to use the tool to varying degrees throughout the pilot. At the two teaching hospitals, posters were placed in nursing offices and were used to track local trials; leaflets were included within the information packs given to new lung cancer patients. At one District General Hospital, the poster was put in a patient waiting area but was not used to track local trials; the leaflet was only shared with patients who asked about clinical trials. At another District General Hospital, neither the poster nor leaflet was used as part of their routine practice.

Despite these differences, nurses generally reported finding the tool easy to use and had embedded them into clinical practice; they expressed their intent to continue to use the tool after the pilot ended. However, nurse participants at one site said they felt anxious and unconfident about using the Lung I-ACT tool due to fears of not knowing how to answer patients’ follow-on questions about trials.

Theme 3: perceived changes to practice

Most nurses stated that the tool had helped to prompt, guide and structure discussions about clinical trials with patients. While these conversations were often brief and would ideally need to be revisited, nurses viewed the increasing number of discussions they were having positively, providing a way for them to proactively inform patients about alternative treatment options. They felt this helped to empower patients to explore clinical trials further with themselves or other multidisciplinary team members. Some nurses also cited positive instances of offering the Lung I-ACT leaflet to patients and family members in response to impromptu discussions with them about clinical trials.

Nurses at most sites reported an increase in communications and improvements in relationships with research delivery team colleagues about clinical trials, due to making contact to identify local research activity that they could track via the poster’s clinical trial table.

Theme 4: knowledge, awareness and confidence to discuss clinical trials

Most nurses stated that while they did not feel their knowledge of clinical trials had increased, they did feel more able to facilitate discussions about trials by using the leaflet as a guide to convey essential trials information to patients.

Nurses generally reported that their awareness of clinical trials had increased and attributed this to the prominent placement of the tool in information packs and clinic spaces, prompting them to think about local clinical trials that might be available and signpost their patients to research delivery teams for more information. Some nurses also expressed increased awareness of the importance of discussing clinical research with all patients as part of their extended professional roles, to promote informed decision-making.

Most nurses reported that their confidence to discuss clinical trials had increased. Several nurses described feeling more confident to inform patients that they did not always have the answers to their questions about clinical trials, affirming their role boundaries, but that they could signpost them to other multidisciplinary team members to aid the progression of research discussions.

Theme 5: contextual and situational factors impacting the presentation of clinical trials

Nurses reported that a chronic lack of time and resources consistently affected their ability to discuss clinical trials with patients, with competing role priorities often compounded by staffing issues. Low staffing was reported at all sites and was particularly acute at two sites, where a combination of long-term sick leave and maternity leave had led to depleted nursing numbers.

Additional barriers were linked to nurses’ gatekeeping roles, including assessments about patients’ capacity to engage with or absorb trial information, concern about giving patients false hope and beliefs about whether they thought it was likely patients would be eligible to enrol in trials generally.

While patient participants confirmed that the barriers identified by nurses were real, they consistently agreed that they wanted opportunities to access information about trials. Patients had mixed views as to when the best time to be introduced to clinical trials information was. One patient felt that time was needed to process their diagnosis first and that trials information should follow later; others felt trials information should be given as early as possible and revisited regularly.

Open-text survey findings

While the open-text survey questions and findings did not explicitly relate to the Lung I-ACT tool, the data generated did address broader contexts that influence clinical trial discussions. These data are summarised here for completeness and transparency purposes. Primarily, when asked to list the main barriers or challenges to discussing clinical trials with patients, nurse participants from all sites reported a lack of knowledge, time, staffing and resources, and patient-related factors such as poor health, informational and emotional overload, and language. Conversely, when asked to list what they felt could improve discussions around clinical trials with patients, nurse participants consistently reported more knowledge, time, staffing and resources. When asked if they were more likely to discuss trials with particular types of patients, nurse participants stated they were more likely to discuss trials with younger, healthier and more highly educated patients, and less likely to discuss trials with patients who were older, less healthy or who they perceived lacked social support. Further detail on the data generated from the open-text questions is contained within online supplemental file 2.

Discussion

The Lung I-ACT tool was developed using rigorous and collaborative methods including continuous engagement with lung cancer nurses, healthcare professionals and lung cancer patients13 in accordance with the UK Medical Research Council’s updated guidance for developing complex interventions.17 Increasing the number and quality of patient-facing discussions about clinical trial opportunities, through the use of tools such as Lung I-ACT, is central to improving outcomes for patients with cancer and growing the life science industry.4 11

Data from interview and open-text survey data indicated that nurses and patients who accessed the Lung I-ACT tool found them to be informative, visually engaging and easy to navigate. Several nurses commented that the tool was easy to embed in practice and that they were committed to using them beyond the pilot study. The tool was found to help nurses initiate and structure discussions about trials, increasing their awareness and confidence in this as well as in their ability to signpost patients to relevant trial information, conduct more frequent discussions about trials and effectively communicate with multidisciplinary and research delivery team members. Although survey data did not demonstrate statistically significant findings between intervention and control groups, likely due to the study’s small sample size, notable trends were observed, which aligned with our open-text survey and interview findings.

Our findings have demonstrated Lung I-ACT’s acceptability and applicability in lung cancer care settings. Nurses reported feeling more able, aware and confident in discussing clinical trial opportunities with patients and increases in the number of discussions taking place, highlighting the potential widespread utility of Lung I-ACT in contributing to positive changes in the recruitment of patients to clinical trials.

The Lung I-ACT tool aimed to support nurses to discuss trial opportunities early and often with patients; this was embedded in practice by the inclusion of the patient-facing leaflet in information packs provided to patients during initial consultations. As well as acknowledging the need for increasing the quality and quantity of discussions about clinical trial opportunities with patients, it is recommended that these discussions take place early in the care pathway to allow patients adequate time to consider trial participation.11 The Lung I-ACT tool promotes opportunities for nurses to play a key part in this, empowering them to act as patient advocates, while utilising their central role as key care coordinators and primary liaison between patients and other multidisciplinary team members.21 This mechanism acknowledges nurses’ role boundaries and their understanding of when it is appropriate to refer on, seek advice and signpost to other colleagues and services to maximise opportunities for patients to access the right care at the right time and promoting informed decision-making.

Our findings showed that the Lung I-ACT tool led to increased confidence for nurses in terms of initiating discussions and signposting clinical trials information to patients; increased confidence can act as a significant facilitator to enriching these discussions.13 22 Moreover, the Lung I-ACT tool resulted in the development of better working relationships and enhanced communications between research delivery team members and lung cancer nurses. The more joined up clinical trial processes among staff promoted by Lung I-ACT have the potential to increase access and availability of clinical trial information for patients. Improved multidisciplinary team communication has been shown to positively impact patient outcomes23 and may also serve to break down perceptions of research as something separate from core clinical practices.

Nurses naturally act as expert conduits through which different specialisms can be channelled,24 and Lung I-ACT was coproduced with nurses to complement their current role within the multidisciplinary team.13 The Lung I-ACT tool can be easily embedded and applied in clinical practice settings as it does not demand any substantial changes to nurses’ role requirements or impact on limited time pressures.12 This ease of implementation in practice indicates that the resource is a viable and sustainable option for use in practice settings in the longer term.

Implications for practice

The Lung I-ACT tool is acceptable and applicable for use in practice, as demonstrated by the study’s qualitative interview findings. While the quantitative and qualitative survey data did not provide evidence of the tool’s efficacy, they did not contradict this contention. The Lung I-ACT tool has the potential to increase the quantity and quality of discussions between nurses and patients about clinical trial opportunities and is an important part of the trials recruitment jigsaw. It can be used alongside other interventions and strategies to help unlock opportunities for patients to consider clinical trials as viable and realistic options alongside other cancer treatments. A model of best practice provides lung cancer nurses with the tools they need to signpost patients to trial opportunities, while developing strong links with research delivery teams for the provision of more detailed trial information can be developed from the study findings.

Strengths and limitations

The Lung I-ACT pilot took place across a broad geographical area and included both teaching and district general hospitals, demonstrating the transferability of our findings across a wide range of settings and the applicability of the tool across different contexts and clinical practice environments. However, the study findings are limited by the pilot study’s small sample size. While trends were observed, this limited the power and complexity of the quantitative analysis and did not allow for effectiveness of the tool to be evaluated. The small sample size of the pilot was further compounded by the attrition of several nurse participants. Moreover, the number of patient participants (n=4) was especially low due to three patients withdrawing due to ill health, with insufficient time to recruit others. The purpose of interviewing patients was to provide a complementary perspective to nurses’; however, the small number of patient participants limited the richness of data in this area. Finally, the most nurse and patient participants identified as White-British. While at least 60% of lung cancer cases in the UK are diagnosed in White-British populations,25 the findings may still not be wholly representative of the patient with lung cancer and caregiver population in the UK. Future research should aim to evaluate the Lung I-ACT tool in larger, more diverse samples, to allow for the effect of the intervention on trial recruitment outcomes to be examined.

Future research

Our findings indicate that the use of tools such as Lung I-ACT can help healthcare professionals have discussions with patients about clinical trials opportunities; future research can build on this preliminary success. There is potential for transferability of many of the findings, including the use of concise accessible patient-facing leaflets to help healthcare professionals have discussions about trials and demystify key trials information, and using a nurse-facing poster to facilitate better relationships between clinical and research delivery staff. Future research should seek to build on this and adapt, implement and evaluate the Lung I-ACT tool in other health and care settings and across other cancer sites. In addition, exploratory work to examine the tool’s applicability and deliverability across underserved groups would increase the generalisability and transferability of the tool to the broader lung cancer patient population in the UK and internationally. This can promote its inclusive use across a range of settings and contexts.

Conclusion

The Lung I-ACT pilot has confirmed the tool’s value in supporting and initiating clinical trial discussions between nurses and patients as shown from our qualitative interview findings. While the tool’s effectiveness could not be examined quantitatively due to sample size limitations, positive trends against key outcomes were observed. The Lung I-ACT tool has clearly and positively impacted nurses’ confidence, increasing trial awareness and helping to structure and shape trial discussions. Lung I-ACT can make an important contribution to increasing awareness of clinical trials among patients with lung cancer, promoting informed decision-making. Ultimately, this has the potential to increase recruitment, leading to breakthroughs in cancer treatment and care for the benefit of patients, healthcare organisations, industry leaders, policy and practice and enacting widespread benefits for the health and care ecosystem.
