Suicidality-Related Emergency Department Visits Among US Children and Adolescents, 2016-2022
Authors: Ayse Kubra Coskun, Mohan Krishna Palakollu, Danielle B Dilsaver, Reva Siva, Madeleine Horan, Rajesh R Tampi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine national trends in suicidality-related (intentional self-harm, self-inflicted injury, and suicide attempt) emergency department (ED) visits among US children and adolescents between 2016 and 2022 and describe demographic and psychiatric correlates across age groups.MethodData were obtained from the 2016 to 2022 Nationwide Emergency Department Sample (NEDS) for patients aged 5 to 19 years with suicidality-related ICD-10-CM codes. Weighted national estimates, demographics, and psychiatric comorbidities were compared across age groups (5-9, 10-14, and 15-19 years). Temporal trends were evaluated using Daniel’s test for trend, with p < .05 considered significant.ResultsThere were an estimated 1,100,785 suicidality-related ED visits (intentional self-harm 61%, self-inflicted injury 39%, suicide attempt 9%). The number of visits increased significantly among age group 10 to 14 for all diagnoses (p ≤ .01) and among age group 15 to 19 for suicide attempts only (p = .003). Female patients (74.7%) accounted for most visits. Depression (45.3%) and anxiety (21.6%) were the most common comorbidities.ConclusionSuicidality-related ED visits among US youth remain alarmingly high, especially among young adolescents. The predominance of depression, anxiety, and female patient representation underscores the need for early, community-based mental health interventions and post-ED follow-up care.Clinical guidance• Screen earlier and repeatedly. Incorporate routine suicide risk screening beginning in late elementary school (ages 9–10), especially during well-child visits, school physicals, and emergency department encounters, even when presenting complaints are nonpsychiatric.• Treat self-harm as a high-risk signal. Any self-inflicted injury—regardless of stated intent—should trigger a structured suicide risk assessment, safety planning, and close follow-up, as self-harm often precedes suicide attempts.• Address comorbid depression, anxiety, and trauma aggressively. Early identification and evidence-based treatment (eg, cognitive behavioral therapy, dialectical behavior therapy-informed skills, trauma-focused care) should be prioritized, with coordinated family involvement when developmentally appropriate.• Strengthen transitions of care after emergency department visits. Ensure rapid outpatient follow-up (within 7 days), clear communication with caregivers, and linkage to school- and community-based supports, particularly for preteens and adolescents from low-income households.Diversity & Inclusion StatementOne or more of the authors of this paper self-identifies as a member of one or more historically underrepresented racial and/or ethnic groups in science. We actively worked to promote inclusion of historically underrepresented racial and/or ethnic groups in science in our author group. Suicide, the act of developing and implementing plans to end one’s life, remains a leading cause of death among children in the United States. In 2023, 2,643 children aged 0 to 19 died by suicide.1 Of these, 481 deaths occurred among children aged 10 to 14, and 2,156 occurred among adolescents aged 15 to 19.1 Beyond its devastating human toll, pediatric suicidality places a substantial financial burden on the health care system. A recent analysis found that suicide attempts (SAs) among youth 8 to 21 years old generated direct medical costs totaling $869 million in 2021, with prevention of intentional overdoses alone potentially saving $530 million annually.2

Recent evidence suggests a continued increase in suicide rates among pediatric and adolescent populations. Between 2008 and 2021, suicide rates among children aged 8 to 12 years rose by approximately 8% annually.3 In 2021, suicide ranked as the third leading cause of death among US high school students aged 14 to 18 years.4 Importantly, this rise in suicide rates is not confined to adolescents—suicidality has also been increasingly reported among elementary school–aged children.5 Despite their substantial contribution to our understanding of pediatric and adolescent suicidality, these studies tend to focus on aggregated trends or specific subpopulations, leaving gaps in understanding of temporal changes in SA among children and adolescents. In a more recent comprehensive review, Burstein et al.6 reported that between 2007 and 2015, suicide-related emergency department (ED) visits doubled among children aged 10 to 18, underscoring the urgent need for enhanced mental health resources and structured postdischarge planning. However, that study excluded children younger than 10 years of age.

Importantly, as highlighted in prior literature, studying suicidality at the population level presents important methodological challenges, particularly when relying on administrative datasets. In the absence of direct clinical assessments, researchers must rely on diagnostic coding to approximate suicidality-related behaviors. Within large national datasets such as the Nationwide Emergency Department Sample (NEDS), ICD-10-CM codes provide the most feasible and standardized approach for identifying suicidality-related encounters, including suicidal ideation (SI), self-harm, and SA. Although these codes have limitations due to variability in provider documentation and the lack of universally standardized operational definition, they remain the most widely used and practical method for capturing suicide-related presentations on a scale. Accordingly, ICD-10-CM–based classifications serve as a proxy for suicidality-related behaviors in epidemiological studies of ED utilization.7

Using this approach, we analyzed NEDS data from 2016 to 2022 to identify recent trends in suicidality-related ED visits among children and adolescents, including children as young as 5 years old. Evaluating these temporal patterns by age group and associated biopsychosocial factors may help guide targeted developmentally informed preventive strategies and resource allocation.

Method

Data Source

We used data from the 2016 to 2022 NEDS, part of the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project sponsored by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality.8 NEDS represents the largest all-payer ED database in the United States, encompassing approximately 137 million ED visits annually.7 The database is de-identified, compliant with Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, and publicly available. This analysis was determined to be not human subjects research by the Creighton University Institutional Review Board (InfoEd Record Number: 2005429).

Cohort Identification

We included ED visits for patients aged 5 to 19 years. Visits were selected if they contained at least 1 suicidality-related ICD-10-CM diagnosis code (intentional self-harm, self-inflicted injury, or SA). These codes were selected because within the ICD-10-CM system, providers often use these classifications interchangeably when evaluating patients presenting to the ED with suspected SA, and standardized operational definitions for these categories are lacking.7 However, these diagnostic categories are not mutually exclusive, and individual encounters may include multiple suicidality-related codes.

Demographic data extracted included biological sex (male, female), race (White, Black, Hispanic, other), primary payer (Medicaid, Medicare, private, other), income quartile, hospital region (Northeast, Midwest, South, West), hospital location/teaching status (metropolitan/nonteaching, metropolitan/teaching, nonmetropolitan), and psychiatric comorbidities. In addition to psychiatric comorbidities, we also identified history of SI and history of SA. All diagnoses were identified via ICD-10-CM codes8; codes were adapted from Zipursky et al.9 and are provided in Table S1, available online.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the yearly rate of suicidality-related ED visits per 10,000 US population. Suicidality-related visits were defined as encounters with at least 1 ICD-10-CM diagnosis code for SI, intentional self-harm, self-inflicted injury, or SA. In addition, we examined ED discharge disposition as a secondary outcome.

Statistical Analysis

Year-over-year trends were assessed using Daniel’s test for trend.10 Daniel’s test for trend, also known as Spearman rank correlation, is a nonparametric statistical test to detect trend in a time series. Daniel’s test is used when one of the variables is chronological, which in this case was year. To account for yearly population changes, the rate of suicidality-related ED visits was estimated per 10,000 US population.11,12 More specifically, the NEDS-estimated number of yearly suicidality-related ED visits was divided by the yearly US population based on the July 1 estimate provided by the US Census Bureau and multiplied by 10,000. Year-over-year trends in the rate of suicidality-related ED visits were evaluated overall and by age group: children (5-9 years), young adolescents (10-14 years), and adolescents (15-19 years).

US Census Bureau nationwide population estimates were used to account for fluctuations in the US population appropriately. Relatedly, age group classifications were informed by the US Census Bureau age ranges. Descriptive characteristics were stratified by age group and compared via χ2 tests. Two-tailed p < .05 indicated statistical significance. All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.).

Results

Between 2016 and 2022, there were an estimated 1,100,785 ED visits among children aged 5 to 19 with a suicidality-related diagnosis (unweighted N = 253,255; 95% CI, 1,046,030-1,155,539). Of those, an estimated 674,446 ED visits were for intentional self-harm (unweighted N = 155,431; 95% CI, 637,144-711,748), 433,951 ED visits were for self-inflicted injury (unweighted N = 99,452; 95% CI, 411,852-456,050), and 95,606 ED visits were for SA (unweighted N = 21,867; 95% CI, 89,895-101,318). Notably, 9.02% (weighted N = 99,242; 95% CI, 94,036-104,463) of ED visits involved patients with more than 1 suicidality-related diagnosis.

Demographics

Descriptive characteristics by age group are provided in Table 1. ED visits for intentional self-harm were most frequent in adolescents (63.37%) and young adolescents (57.83%) compared with children (19.12%) (p < .001). In contrast, ED visits for self-inflicted injury were the most frequent in children (72.90%), followed by young adolescents (42.52%) and adolescents (37.58%) (p < .001). For young adolescents and adolescents, 8.86% and 8.46%, respectively, of suicidality-related ED visits were for SA, whereas 20.84% of suicidality-related ED visits for children were for SA (p < .001). Medicaid was the most frequent insurance across age groups (children: 69.86%; young adolescents: 53.74%; adolescents: 45.81%), followed by private insurance (children: 22.58%; young adolescents: 38.64%, adolescents: 43.49%). More than half of suicidality-related ED visits were patients living in a low-income area, according to income quartile by patient zip code (ie, lowest 2 income quartiles) (children: 63.03%; young adolescents: 53.54%; adolescents: 52.72%). Suicidality-related ED visits frequently occurred in the South (children: 38.92%; young adolescents: 35.27%; adolescents: 34.08%) and Midwest (children: 25.31%; young adolescents: 27.78%; adolescents: 28.33%). Most suicidality-related ED visits occurred at metropolitan teaching facilities (children: 68.96%; young adolescents: 67.40%; adolescents: 64.93%). Notably, for adolescents, 21.36% of suicidality-related ED visits were patients with a history of SI, and 13.14% were patients with a history of SA. Similarly, for young adolescents, 23.66% of suicidality-related ED visits were patients with a history of SI, and 12.91% were patients with a history of SA. In suicidality-related ED visits involving children, 12.56% had a history of SI, and 5.45% had a history of SA.

Figure S1, available online, illustrates the year-over-year trend in the rate of all pediatric (5-19 years), young adolescent (10-14 years), and adolescent (15-19 years) suicidality ED visits in which the patient had a history of SA or SI. For all pediatric patients, the year-over-year trend in the rate of suicidality-related ED visits in which the patient had a history of SI was statistically significant (ptrend < .001). This significant trend was also seen specifically in young adolescents (ptrend < .001) and adolescents (ptrend = .014). Interestingly, in adolescents, the yearly trend in the rate of visits with a history of SA was statistically significant (ptrend = .003). However, this trend was not statistically significant in young adolescents (ptrend = .148) or the general pediatric population (ptrend = .052).

Psychiatric Comorbidities

In general, depression and anxiety disorders were the most frequent comorbidities (45.28% and 21.58%, respectively). Of suicidality-related ED visits involving young adolescents and adolescents, 44.11% and 46.19%, respectively, of patients had a diagnosis of a depressive disorder compared with 14.22% of visits involving children (p < .001). Likewise, in 19.70% and 22.62% of suicidality-related ED visits involving young adolescents and adolescents, patients had a diagnosis for an anxiety disorder compared with only 8.25% of visits involving children (p < .001). Other common psychiatric comorbidities that varied in frequency by age group included bipolar disorder; trauma- and stressor-related disorders; schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders; attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorders; sleep-wake disorders; disruptive, impulse-control, and conduct disorders; autism spectrum disorder; and intellectual disabilities (Table 1).

Discharge Disposition

Routine discharge (discharge to home) was most common for children; 62.29% of children (5-9 years old) had routine discharge compared with only 39.62% of young adolescents (10-14 years old) and 37.51% of adolescents (16-19 years old). Discharge to home health care or transfer to another inpatient hospital, skilled nursing facility, or inpatient rehabilitation facility was more common in young adolescents and adolescents at 43.23% and 40.33%, respectively. Notably, 21.19% of adolescents were admitted to the treatment facility.

Sample Characteristics in Relation to Broader US ED Population

Prior studies suggest higher ED utilization among female patients, patients with lower income, and uninsured patients.13 Although our findings were consistent with respect to gender and income, differences were observed in payer status and race.

Discussion

Our findings reflect trends in ED utilization for suicidality-related diagnoses rather than direct measures of population-level suicidality. ED-based data are influenced by factors such as health care access, help-seeking behavior, and coding practices and therefore should be interpreted within this context.

These data demonstrate a substantial burden of suicidality-related ED visits among US youth, with particularly notable increases in preadolescence and early adolescence. Between 2016 and 2022, there were an estimated 1,100,785 ED visits among children and adolescents aged 5 to 19 years that involved a suicidality-related diagnosis. Of these, 674,446 (61%) were for intentional self-harm, 433,951 (39%) for self-inflicted injury, and 95,606 (9%) for SA. These findings reflect a continuation of a troubling national pattern observed in prior research. Burstein et al.6 reported that between 2007 and 2015, annual ED visits for suicide among youth aged 5 to 18 years nearly doubled—from 580,000 to 1.12 million. Similarly, Bommersbach et al.14 found that from 2011 to 2020, the proportion of pediatric ED visits attributable to suicide-related concerns rose from 0.9% to 4.2% (23.1% average annual percent change) (p < .001). Collectively, these data underscore that despite growing awareness and expansion of mental health resources,15 suicidal behavior leading to ED visits among children and adolescents remains a significant public health concern, warranting continued efforts in early detection, intervention, and systems-level prevention.

The study period includes the onset and progression of the COVID-19 pandemic, which has been associated with significant disruptions in youth mental health and health care utilization. Prior studies have demonstrated increases in ED visits for self-harm and SAs during the pandemic, particularly among adolescent girls.16 Although our study was not designed to isolate pandemic-specific effects, these broader contextual factors likely contributed to observed trends and should be considered when interpreting findings.

Children (5-9 Years Old)

Suicidality remains relatively rare but not absent in this age group. In our analysis, there were no statistically significant trends in suicidality-related behaviors among children aged 5 to 9 years. However, the scarcity of research focused specifically on this developmental period underscores the need for more age-targeted studies. Bridge et al.17 reported stable suicide rates among children aged 5 to 11 years between 2008 and 2012; However, during this period, the suicide rates for Black children increased from 1.36 to 2.54 per 1 million, whereas for White children, rates decreased from 1.14 to 0.77 per 1 million during that period. Because NEDS data did not include race and ethnicity before 2019, racial disparities could not be evaluated in this study. Additionally, Geoffroy et al.18 found a pooled prevalence of 7.5% for SI among 98,044 children younger than 12 years across 28 studies. Collectively, whereas suicide remains uncommon in early childhood, these findings highlight that suicidal thoughts and behaviors do occur and warrant early recognition, culturally responsive prevention strategies, and further research into developmental risk factors.

Young Adolescents (10-14 Years Old)

In our analysis, young adolescents aged 10 to 14 years demonstrated a statistically significant rise in suicide-related ED visits across all studied diagnoses. This trend aligns with national data showing that suicide rates in this age group tripled from 0.9 to 2.9 per 100,000 between 2007 and 2018.19 By 2020, suicide had become the second leading cause of death among youth aged 10 to 14 years.20

Global data reveal similar patterns. Michalek et al.21 reported that between 1990 and 2019, overall suicide-related mortality rate among children worldwide decreased from 2.33 to 1.30 per 100,000. However, the proportion of deaths attributable to suicide within the 10- to 14-year age group slightly increased from 2.76% to 2.78%, particularly in countries with higher sociodemographic index scores, which account for high per capita income, high educational attainment, and low total fertility rates. The study found a positive association between higher sociodemographic index score and the contribution of suicide to all-cause mortality, suggesting that social and environmental pressures in more developed regions may heighten vulnerability to suicidality among young adolescents.21 Understanding these contextual factors is essential for developing tailored prevention strategies.

Self-harm also emerged as a significant suicidality-related diagnosis within this group. Farkas et al.22 identified a marked increase in self-injurious behavior from 1998 to 2018, though the trend plateaued between 2015 and 2018. More recently, Wong et al.23 found that ED visits for self-harm nearly doubled during the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic, representing a higher proportion of all psychiatric presentations. Continued research and implementation of early, skills-based prevention programs remain critical for this vulnerable developmental stage.

Adolescents (15-19 Years Old)

Among adolescents aged 15 to 19 years, only the diagnosis of SA demonstrated a statistically significant upward trend; other suicidality-related diagnoses, including self-harm and SI, did not show significant changes during the study period. Historically, suicide trends in this age group have fluctuated. National data indicate that suicide rates remained stable from 2000 to 2007, increased between 2007 and 2014, and then rose even more sharply from 2014 to 2017.24 During the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, ED visits for SA among adolescents aged 12 to 17 years increased by 50.6% in girls and 3.7% in boys.24 Smaller cohort studies have similarly shown that most suicide-related ED visits occur within the 15- to 17-year age range.25 Multiple, interrelated risk factors likely contribute to these patterns, including psychiatric comorbidities such as depression and anxiety, exposure to adverse childhood experiences, social isolation, self-harm behaviors, and sociocultural influences such as racism and social media use.20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26 Understanding how these risk factors interact—and how existing prevention and intervention programs can be adapted to address them—is essential. Future studies focusing on differential risk by gender, culture, and psychiatric profile may help identify the most effective, developmentally appropriate prevention strategies for older adolescents.

Gender and Psychiatric Comorbidity

Gender Differences

Gender remains a major factor influencing suicide risk and suicidal behavior. Across all suicidality-related diagnoses in our analysis, a higher proportion of ED visits occurred among female patients compared with male patients. This pattern is consistent with national data showing that girls have experienced a steeper rise in suicide death rates than boys over recent decades. Between 1975 and 2016, suicide mortality increased by 12.7% among girls aged 10 to 14 years compared with 7.1% among boys and by 7.9% among girls aged 15 to 19 years compared with 3.5% among boys.24, 25, 26, 27 Although SAs are more common among female patients, completed suicides continue to be more frequent among male patients, primarily due to the use of more lethal methods.20,28 Several factors may underlie the narrowing gender gap in suicidality, including differences in coping styles, exposure to psychosocial stressors, and rising rates of self-harm among girls. Although our dataset did not allow for differentiation between nonsuicidal and suicidal self-harm, prior research indicates that female patients are at higher risk for self-injurious behaviors, which in turn increase the likelihood of SA.29 Further investigation into the gender-specific pathways of suicidality, particularly the interactions between social, cultural, and biological factors, will be essential for informing prevention and tailoring interventions. Gender-responsive approaches, such as emotion regulation training and early screening in primary care and school settings, may help reduce the growing burden of suicidality among adolescent girls, while addressing access to lethal means among boys.

Psychiatric Comorbidity

From the perspective of comorbid psychiatric disorders, our data showed that depression was the most common comorbidity among all patients (45.3%), followed by anxiety disorders (21.6%), trauma-related disorders (10.6%), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (9.2%), substance use and addictive disorders (8.8%), and bipolar disorder (5.6%). These findings are consistent with prior studies indicating that mood and anxiety disorders are strongly associated with suicidality in youth.30

Interestingly, research suggests that the psychiatric conditions associated with SI may differ from conditions that predispose individuals to SA. Nock et al.31 found that the most common comorbidities among adolescents with SI included major depressive disorder (MDD)/dysthymia, specific phobia, oppositional defiant disorder, intermittent explosive disorder, substance abuse, and conduct disorder, whereas posttraumatic stress disorder, MDD/dysthymia, oppositional defiant disorder, eating disorders, and bipolar disorder were more strongly associated with subsequent SA. Similarly, Moller et al.32 and Hua et al.20 identified MDD, bipolar disorder, and trauma-related disorders as the most common psychiatric diagnoses among suicidal youth. Recognizing these diagnostic patterns is crucial for clinical risk assessment and treatment planning. Early identification and management of underlying psychiatric conditions, especially depression, anxiety, and trauma, can facilitate timely interventions, improve family and provider awareness, and potentially reduce recurrence and escalation of suicidality-related ED visits. Integrating suicide risk screening within mental health and primary care settings remains a key step toward comprehensive prevention.

This study has several limitations. First, our findings reflect trends in ED utilization for suicidality-related diagnoses and may not directly represent population-level changes in suicidality. Second, reliance on ICD-10-CM codes, which are primarily designed for billing, may have resulted in misclassification and underreporting of suicidality-related encounters. The coding categories of intentional self-harm, self-inflicted injury, and SA are not uniformly defined or applied across providers, and prior work suggests that a substantial proportion of suicidality-related visits may not be captured by these codes.7 In addition, providers may preferentially document underlying psychiatric diagnoses rather than suicide-specific codes, further contributing to potential underestimation. The lack of clear differentiation between suicidal and nonsuicidal self-harm may also introduce heterogeneity in the data. Third, race and ethnicity data were unavailable before 2019, limiting the ability to assess longitudinal analyses of disparities. Fourth, the NEDS dataset does not include follow-up outcomes or mortality data and therefore excludes completed suicides, leading to an underestimation of the overall burden of suicidality. Finally, the study period includes the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, which was associated with significant disruptions in youth mental health and health care utilization. Because our analysis was not designed to isolate pandemic-specific effects, observed trends may partially reflect these broader contextual changes.

Future studies linking administrative and clinical datasets as well as incorporating more recent data will be essential for improving coding accuracy, assessing long-term outcomes, and strengthening suicide prevention strategies among children and adolescents. Between 2016 and 2022, suicidality-related ED visits among US children and adolescents remained alarmingly high, with the steepest increases observed among youth aged 10 to 14 years. Depression and anxiety were the most common psychiatric comorbidities, and girls accounted for a disproportionate share of visits. These findings underscore the urgent need for early, developmentally appropriate, and culturally sensitive prevention programs—beginning in elementary and middle school years—and for expanded access to evidence-based mental health care. Integrating ED-based screening with community follow-up, strengthening school-based interventions, and addressing gender and socioeconomic disparities may be essential steps in reducing the escalating pediatric suicide crisis.
