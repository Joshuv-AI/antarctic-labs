Obesity Attenuates the Association Between the Modified Five-Item Frailty Score and Mortality in Septic Surgical Patients
Authors: Bradley Butsch, Maryn Montgomery, Shachi Srivatsa, Courtney Collins, Patrick J Kennedy, Brett Tracy, John Loftus, Jon Wisler, Anahita Jalilvand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:Obesity is an independent predictor of mortality in septic surgical patients, likely associated with underlying physiologic frailty, measured by the modified 5-item frailty index (mFI-5). This study aimed to determine the prevalence of frailty among obese and nonobese surgical patients and evaluate the impact of frailty on mortality in these populations.Methods:We performed a single-center retrospective cohort study of surgical intensive care unit patients admitted with sepsis (sequential organ failure assessment, SOFA ≥2). Individual mFI-5 scores (range 0–5) were calculated and the prevalence of prefrailty (mFI <2), moderate-frailty (mFI = 2), and severe-frailty (mFI ≥3) across body mass index (BMI) categories (underweight, nonobese, class I-III obesity) was compared. BMI <18.5 kg/m2 was excluded. Characteristics associated with mFI ≥2 were evaluated. Factors independently associated with frailty, incorporating demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical variables were identified. Four cohorts were made: obese (BMI ≥30 kg/m2, n = 766) and nonobese (18.5–29.9 kg/m2, n = 574), further divided into frail (mFI≥2) and prefrail (mFI <2). In-hospital mortality, cumulative 90-day mortality, and 90-day survival were compared across groups.Results:Severe frailty was highly associated with increasing BMI class, occurring in 37% of morbidly obese patients versus 19% of nonobese patients (P < 0.005). Increasing BMI independently predicted moderate-to-severe frailty (odds ratio [OR]: 1.04, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.02–1.05). Frailty significantly increased 90-day mortality in nonobese patients (17.4% versus 36.4%, P < 0.005) but not in obese patients (34% versus 35%). Nonfrail nonobese patients demonstrated superior 90-day-survival.Conclusions:Obesity is highly associated with higher mFI-5 scores, though its prognostic impact appears attenuated in obese versus nonobese cohorts, suggesting the need for more discriminatory frailty tools in the obese population.Keywords: Acute care surgery, Frailty, Obesity, Sepsis, Surgical sepsis

Introduction

The validity of the obesity paradox within septic surgical patients is controversial. Although there is literature supporting a modest improvement in sepsis survival for patients with class I obesity, most patients included in these studies were from nonsurgical intensive care units.1 To this point, we have sought to elucidate how this paradox may be applicable to surgical patients. We have previously published that within surgical patients with sepsis, those with obesity exhibit a bimodal distribution of survival, including a very early survival benefit (<7 d) followed by a steep rise in overall 90-day mortality compared to their nonobese counterparts.2 However, in a subsequent analysis, we identified that patients with obesity who had undergone bariatric surgery prior to their sepsis admission exhibited a significant survival improvement compared to patients with obesity who had not had bariatric surgery.3 In fact, their survival approached that of the nonobese cohort. We hypothesized that the observed survival improvement in the bariatric cohort was partly driven by reductions in prehospitalization comorbidity burden, as measured by the Charlson comorbidity index (CCI).

These data suggested that the association between body mass index (BMI) and outcomes following sepsis is not uniform. In fact, significant heterogeneity may exist in outcome trajectories for different obesity phenotypes.4 To this point, we sought to evaluate how medical frailty may modulate the interplay between weight status and outcomes following sepsis. Frailty can be underrecognized in obesity, as it is primarily thought of as a wasting syndrome in underweight patients.5 Although there are many different constructs that exist to measure frailty, including assessment of physical deficits, physiological dysfunction, and sarcopenia, the comorbidity driven-clinical scores are often utilized to facilitate risk stratification in larger datasets. The mFI-5, derived from the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP), is a pragmatic tool to estimate frailty that has been shown to predict mortality, hospital length of stay, and readmission rates.6–9 It incorporates five clinical variables: hypertension requiring medication, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), dependent functional status, congestive heart failure (CHF), and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).

However, there is significant overlap between the mFI-5 and obesity-related comorbidities. This may be partially explained by shared pathophysiology between the two disease states, as obesity is a risk factor for the development of frailty.5 Alternatively, this relationship may also result from shared comorbidity burden rather than overlapping frailty phenotypes. Therefore, it is unclear how the mFI-5 modulates outcomes within patients with obesity. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to determine the association between frailty, as measured by the mFI-5, and obesity within surgical patients with sepsis. Secondarily, we sought to explore the impact of this frailty metric on in-hospital and cumulative 90-day mortality for obese and nonobese septic surgical patients. We hypothesized that (1) obesity would be highly associated with the frailty classifications of the mFI-5, and that (2) frailty would contribute to our previously reported disparity in mortality between obese and nonobese septic surgical patients.

Methods

Patients and study design

All patients admitted to a large quaternary surgical intensive care unit (ICU) were retrospectively reviewed from July 2011 to July 2019 maintained and collected in an institutionally approved and reviewed database under the institutional review board (IRB) # 2024H0244. Informed consent was waived per institutional review board approval. Inclusion criteria were (1) surgical patients admitted to the surgical ICU, (2) age ≥18 y, and (3) diagnosis of sepsis as defined by SEPSIS III guidelines with a sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) of ≥ 2.10 Patients were excluded if they were prisoners, pregnant, or did not have complete mortality data up to 90 d from index admission. In addition, readmissions were excluded from the analysis, as their mortality might be reasonably influenced by their index stay. A 5-item modified frailty index (mFI-5) score (0–5) was calculated for each patient, based on the presence of hypertension requiring medication, COPD, dependent functional status, CHF, and T2DM, with each condition weighted equally. Functional dependence was categorized based on the definition from the National Quality Improvement Program.

To determine the association between obesity and mFI-5, the prevalence of frailty (nonfrail/prefrail: mFI-5<2, moderately frail: mFI-5 = 2, severely frail: mFI-5 >2) was compared across BMI categories (underweight: BMI <18.5 kg/m2; nonobese: BMI 18.5–29.9 kg/m2; class I obesity: BMI 30–34.9 kg/m2; class II obesity: BMI 35–39.9; and class III obesity: BMI 40.0 and above.) The definitions of frailty utilized in this study were based on well-established cutoffs utilized in previous publications.11 ,12 Next, baseline demographic, comorbidity, and clinical data were compared between frail (mFI-5 ≥ 2) and nonfrail (mFI-5<2) patients.

The second objective of our study was to determine how frailty status impacted our previously documented findings regarding obesity and sepsis mortality.2 For this analysis, the same study population was divided into obese (BMI≥ 30 kg/m2) and nonobese (BMI 18.5–29.9 kg/m2) cohorts and then further categorized by the presence of frailty (prefrail/nonfrail (mFI-5<2) and frail (mFI-5 ≥ 2)). This created four cohorts, across which in-hospital death, cumulative 90-day mortality, and 90-day survival were compared. Additional outcomes evaluation included respiratory failure requiring invasive mechanical ventilation, renal failure, discharge disposition, and length of overall and ICU stay. For this analysis, underweight patients (BMI <18.5 kg/m2) were excluded from this analysis to remain consistent with our prior methodology.2,3 Overall, descriptive statistics and survival curve analyses were conducted using BMI categories to facilitate clinical translatability of the data. A sensitivity analysis was performed by modeling BMI as a continuous variable to reduce error attributable to categorization and increase the rigor of reported results.

Data collection and outcome measures

All variables reported were pulled using the electronic medical record. Our institutional (electronic medical record) is linked to most hospitals in the state, enabling us to capture data for patients transferred from outside facilities. This feature was used to record admission laboratory data and diagnosis for hospital transfers. Baseline characteristics captured included demographic data (age, sex, race [non-Hispanic White, Black, Other, or Unknown]), transfer status (yes/no), Area Deprivation Index (ADI), Distressed Communities Index (DCI), and BMI. Selected prehospitalization comorbidities were recorded based on admission documentation and included CHF, T2DM, moderate/severe liver disease, COPD, obesity (class I-III), stage III and above chronic kidney disease, metastatic cancer, and CCI. We utilized a binary classification of high distress (>74th percentile) for both ADI and DCI, as this is consistent with our prior published literature.13

Admission laboratory results and sepsis data were obtained from the patient’s initial presentation. Included were median SOFA score, creatinine (mg/dL), white blood cell count (K/uL, WBC), hemoglobin (g/dL), lactate (mmol/L), and any vasopressor use on admission. The initial source of sepsis was determined based on in-depth chart review. The source of infection was categorized into: (1) line-associated, (2) respiratory, (3) intra-abdominal (hepatobiliary, foregut, small, and large bowel), (4) skin and soft tissue (burn and soft tissue infection), (5) urologic, (6) orthopedic, (7) head and neck, and (8) thoracic. We also specified the index procedure for all patients in this study (exploratory laparotomy, soft tissue debridement, and other). Finally, outcomes captured included ICU length of stay (days), overall length of stay (days), respiratory failure, days on the ventilator (days), renal failure, days on renal replacement therapy (days), mortality (in-house, 90-day), and discharge disposition (home, skilled nursing facility/long-term acute care hospital, hospice).

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata/SE 18.1 (StataCorp, TX). Normally distributed values were compared using Student’s t-test, and nonparametric continuous variables were compared using Mann–Whitney U. Chi-squared or Fisher Exact tests were utilized to compare categorical variables, depending on the sample size. A multiple logistic regression was performed to determine patient characteristics associated with an mFI-5 score ≥2. Variables selected for this model were based on significant characteristics on bivariate analysis or were deemed clinically relevant. No backwards or forwards selection was done on the final model presented. A Brier score was reported to assess model performance. A 90-day Kaplan–Meier survival curve was calculated based on the obesity and frailty cohorts. Finally, we investigated the effect modification and concern for multicollinearity between obesity and frailty. An interaction term between obesity and frailty was included in a multivariable logistic regression assessing 90-day mortality, which adjusted for SOFA, age, and transfer status. The significance of this interaction term was tested using a Wald χ2 test. This was further elucidated with adjusted mortality probabilities across all four cohorts (obese/nonFrail, obese/frail, nonobese/nonfrail, and nonobese/frail). Finally, variance inflation factors were calculated from multiple linear regression models for 90-day mortality to assess for multicollinearity between BMI and frailty, along with the remaining predictors. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Distribution of frailty categories by BMI category

Of the 1401 patients included in this study, 51 (3.7%) were considered underweight, 574 (41.3%) were nonobese, and the remaining met criteria for obesity (54.7%). Within the obesity cohort, 34.9% (n = 293) had class I obesity, 187 patients had class II obesity (35.0%), and class III obesity was represented by 37.3% or 286 patients. When evaluating the prevalence of frailty overall, 641 (45.8%) of the study cohort were considered prefrail (mFI<2), 409 (29.2%) were classified as moderately frail (mFI = 2), and the remaining 25.1% (n = 351) were severely frail. The distribution of frailty categories across BMI categories is shown in Figure 1. The prevalence of frailty categories was statistically different across BMI classes (P < 0.005). The most notable differences were seen when comparing the distribution of prefrail and severely frail between nonobese/underweight and class III obesity cohorts. Patients with class III obesity demonstrated the highest proportion of severely frail patients (37%) compared to underweight (14%) and nonobese (19%) patients. Frailty classifications were comparable between class I and class II obesity.

Baseline characteristics by frailty status

Of the 1401 patients, 641 (45.8%) were classified as nonfrail/prefrail, and 760 (54.2%) as frail. Compared to the prefrail cohort, frail patients were older (median 64.5 (43–68) versus 58 (43–68) P < 0.005) and had higher BMI (34.5 ± 11.6 kg/m2 versus 31.3 ± 10.1 kg/m2, P < 0.005). Of the comorbidities not represented by the mFI-5, frail patients had a higher prevalence of stage III or greater chronic kidney disease (16.1% versus 4.8%, P < 0.0005), and a higher proportion had a prior history of cerebrovascular accident (14.6% versus 6.4%, P < 0.005). In addition, they were more likely to have a cancer diagnosis on admission (either solitary or metastatic) compared to the prefrail or nonfrail cohort (31.3% versus 24.2%, P = 0.003). No significant differences were observed in sex, race, transfer status, socioeconomic indices (ADI and DCI), history of liver disease, metastatic cancer, or Charlson comorbidity index (Table 1).

The model exhibited moderate predictive ability (Brier score: 0.196).

Outcomes by obesity and frailty status

A comparison of hospital outcomes by frailty category and obesity status is summarized in Table 4. There was no difference in median ICU length of stay or overall hospital length of stay between cohorts. The incidence of respiratory failure was statistically higher when comparing obese versus nonobese cohorts in either the frail or nonfrail groups (P = 0.02). Although frailty was associated with a modest increase in the incidence of renal failure requiring renal replacement therapy within nonobese patients (13.8% versus 9.5%, P = 0.11), obesity was associated with a statistically significant increase in rates of renal failure requiring renal replacement therapy regardless of frailty status (P < 0.005). Within the nonfrail cohort, obesity was associated with a significant increase in both in-house (28.1% versus 10.8%, P < 0.005) and 90-day mortality (33.9% versus 17.4%, P < 0.005). Within the frail cohorts, both nonobese and obese cohorts had comparable mortality data for in-hospital. Similarly, within the obesity cohorts, in-hospital (28.1% versus 28.8%) and 90-day mortality (33.9% versus 34.9%) were comparable regardless of frailty status.

Investigating the interaction between frailty classification and obesity status on 90-day survival and mortality

Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to assess 90-day survival for obese and nonobese cohorts based on frailty status in Figure 2. In Figure 2A, we compared survival for frail (mFI ≥ 2) and prefrail (mFI <2) across obese and nonobese cohorts. The nonobese nonfrail (NONF) group had the highest survival rate (82.6%), significantly outperforming all other groups. The obese, nonfrail (ONF) group had a lower survival rate (66.4%), while both frail groups―nonobese frail (NOF, 63.6%) and obese frail (65.7%)―exhibited similar but diminished survival. Severely frail subgroups had the poorest outcomes: nonobese severely frail (NOSF) at 62.0% and obese severely frail (OSF) at 59.5%.

(A and B) 90-day survival by BMI and frailty cohort.

The interaction between obesity and frailty on 90-day mortality was assessed in Tables 5–7. In addition to both obesity and frailty being independently associated with 90-day mortality, a significant interaction was observed between obesity and an mFI ≥ 2 (OR: 0.42, 95% CI 0.25–0.70; χ2 = 11.1, P = 0.0009) (Table 5). We confirmed this effect modulation with a sensitivity analysis modeling BMI as a continuous variable (OR: 0.973, 95% CI: 0.95–0.99), highlighting a 2.7% decrease in frailty’s effect with each unit increase in BMI. This suggests that increasing BMI and obesity status significantly reduces the association between this frailty metric and mortality. Calculated adjusted mortality probabilities across all four cohorts further highlight this concept (Table 6). Although frailty was associated with a 57% increase in 90-day mortality in the nonobese cohorts (33% versus 21%), it demonstrated minimal association with adjusted mortality in the obese group (32% versus 36%) (Table 6). Finally, given the association between the mFI-5 and obesity described in Table 3, we investigated the concern for collinearity between these variables. The variance inflation factors summarized in Table 7 underscore that there is minimal statistical collinearity between any of the covariates presented in this section (all variance inflation factor <1.5).

Discussion

This is the first study to our knowledge that characterizes the prevalence of frailty by obesity class and elucidates how the modified five item frailty score modulates outcomes within septic surgical patients. The data reported here supports our first hypothesis that obesity is highly associated with moderate to severe frailty as measured by the mFI-5. In fact, increasing BMI was an independent predictor of an mFI-5 score ≥ 2, even after adjusting for baseline demographics not included in the score. Secondly, we hypothesized that frailty within the obese cohort would contribute significantly to the increased mortality noted in our previous studies. Surprisingly, higher frailty classifications had a much more significant impact on mortality within the nonobese cohort, with frail nonobese patients having similar in-hospital and 90-day mortality to frail obese patients. For the obese cohort, only severe classifications of frailty were associated with a demonstrable difference in 90-day mortality or survival, whereas moderate frailty had a more marginal impact. Finally, outcomes such as hospital or ICU length of stay, respiratory, and renal failure did not appear to be significantly altered by the presence of moderate to severe frailty when comparing outcomes for obese and nonobese patients. In other words, differences in these outcomes remained mostly driven by obesity status in this study.

Obesity is highly associated with increasing mFI-5 scores

In this study, over 61% of patients with obesity met criteria for moderate frailty based on the mFI-5. Notably, severe frailty accounted for almost 40% of patients with class III obesity, which was approximately twice the prevalence of the nonobese cohort. The high association between increasing mFI-5 scores and obesity may not be surprising at face value, given that three out of the five aspects of this frailty metric are known obesity-related complications, including hypertension requiring medications, CHF, and type II diabetes. In fact, reductions in functional status can also be seen in severe obesity.14 This suggests that the pathophysiology of obesity may share common immunologic and metabolic pathways seen in the progression of frailty. Many recent studies have shown that visceral fat deposition and changes in adiposity, as seen in sarcopenic obesity, are highly correlative with the development of frailty.15,16 In addition, chronic adipose tissue inflammation, which is fundamental to mediating the downstream effects of obesity, promotes frailty by instigating inflammaging, immunosenescence, and muscular breakdown.17,18 Therefore, our data fit into a growing narrative regarding obesity’s role in accelerating the development of frailty.19–21 However, this is not to suggest that obesity is the only pathway to frailty or that all patients with obesity are frail. These data highlight how comorbid the two diagnoses are and underscore the importance of considering frailty as a downstream complication from obesity. In the future, we hope to investigate how radiographic sarcopenia is associated with the mFI-5 and how it modulates outcomes in septic surgical patients.

The impact of frailty scores on mortality for obese and nonobese septic surgical patients

Unexpectedly, our data demonstrated that obesity significantly attenuated the impact of mFI-5 scoring on mortality. This is further reflected in the survival curves and adjusted mortality rates. Although the addition of frailty in nonobese patients increased their mortality dramatically, it had minimal effect on the cohorts with obesity. Based on our analysis, it is unlikely that this effect modulation is explained by statistical collinearity. Rather, the overlap between obesity and frailty may reduce this score’s discriminatory ability within the obese cohort. One potential reason for this phenomenon may be the contribution of risk saturation, whereby obesity is associated with such a significant increase in mortality that it reduces the effect size associated with the mFI-5 classification. Otherwise stated, a “ceiling effect” is observed when obesity is introduced as a risk factor in our mortality models.22 Conversely, in the nonobese cohort, frailty exerts a significant impact on outcomes.

Another important area for future investigation is whether the mFI-5 score is an accurate metric to capture true physiologic frailty in patients with obesity. Because it relies on comorbidities alone, the mFI-5 does not differentiate between metabolically driven versus sarcopenic phenotypes of frailty. Sarcopenia is strongly associated with frailty and worsened mortality in obesity,23 and it is not incorporated into the mFI-5. This metric may be sufficient for nonobese patients, where the included comorbidities are most closely associated with frailty. In the obese cohort, this becomes more ambiguous, as both disease processes generate similar organ dysfunction with different clinical implications. This likely contributes to the mFI-5’s reduced sensitivity in patients with obesity, who may present with a distinct frailty phenotype from their nonobese counterparts. Our outcomes data in this study further support this theory, as there were minimal differences in renal and respiratory failure between frail and nonfrail patients with obesity.

Currently, the mFI-5 is widely utilized in models for surgical morbidity and mortality. This study highlights potential concerns about globally applying this metric to a highly prevalent and vulnerable population. Clinicians and researchers should consider that the mFI-5’s sensitivity may be dampened in populations with obesity. To this point, recent promising studies have demonstrated that a modified metabolic score, which combines visceral fat burden, metabolic parameters, and anthropometric values, may be more accurate in predicting frailty in adults who are overweight or obese.24 Future efforts should focus on validating obesity-specific frailty scores to identify at-risk patients. Not only could this facilitate more nuanced operative decision-making in the emergent setting, but it may also highlight opportunities for targeted interventions in the postoperative period.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, our analysis was retrospective and single center in design. As a result, there is potential for selection bias and unmeasured confounding variables. The patient population may not be fully representative of all sepsis patients, particularly those in medical ICUs or nonsurgical settings. Caution is therefore warranted in generalizing our findings to other institutions or patient cohorts. Second, frailty was quantified using the modified 5-item frailty index derived from comorbidity and functional status data. Although the mFI-5 is a validated metric in surgical populations, it is inherently limited by the reliance on documented comorbid conditions and a binary functional assessment. It may not capture all dimensions of frailty (e.g., cognitive impairment, nutritional status, or subtle functional decline). Some patients classified as nonfrail by mFI-5 might still have had elements of frailty not reflected in the index, and misclassification is possible if comorbidities were underreported. Using administrative data for frailty also cannot substitute for a comprehensive geriatric assessment; thus, our frailty measure is a surrogate with imperfect sensitivity. Third, we did not account for body composition or fat distribution. Finally, outcome measures were focused on in-hospital and 90-day mortality. We did not evaluate longer-term outcomes such as functional status or quality of life in survivors, which are highly relevant in frail populations. It is known that many frail sepsis survivors experience significant disability or require long-term care.

Conclusions

Despite the above limitations, our study shows that classifications of frailty as determined by the mFI-5 were highly comorbid with increasing BMI in this cohort of septic surgical patients. Although increasing frailty significantly modulated 90-day mortality in nonobese septic surgical patients, its impact was blunted in the cohort with obesity, with only severe frailty having an observable effect. These findings highlight potential issues with utilizing the modified five item frailty score in obese populations, while also underscoring the profound impact obesity has on mortality discrepancies in septic surgical patients.
