SimpleGPT: Improving GPT via A Simple Normalization Strategy
Marco Chen, Xianbiao Qi, Yelin He, Jiaquan Ye, Rong Xiao

Abstract
In this work, we revisit Transformer optimization through the lens of second-order geometry and establish a direct connection between architectural design, activation scale, the Hessian matrix, and the maximum tolerable learning rate. We introduce a simple normalization strategy, termed SimpleNorm, which stabilizes intermediate activation scales by construction. Then, by analyzing the Hessian of the loss with respect to network activations, we theoretically show that SimpleNorm significantly reduces the spectral norm of the Hessian, thereby permitting larger stable learning rates. We validate our theoretical findings through extensive experiments on large GPT models at parameter scales 1B, 1.4B, 7B and 8B. Empirically, SimpleGPT, our SimpleNorm-based network, tolerates learning rates 3 × \times -10 × \times larger than standard convention, consistently demonstrates strong optimization stability, and achieves substantially better performance than well-established baselines. Specifically, when training 7B-scale models for 60K steps, SimpleGPT achieves a training loss that is 0.08 lower than that of LLaMA2 with QKNorm, reducing the loss from 2.290 to 2.208. Our source code will be released at https://github.com/Ocram7/SimpleGPT .

Classical optimization theory

Standard results imply that gradient descent is stable only when

\eta\leq\frac{2}{\beta}=\frac{2}{\sup_{\bm{x}}\,\|\bm{H}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}\|_{2}},

establishing the Hessian spectral norm as the fundamental quantity governing admissible learning rates and convergence behavior.

By grounding Transformer design in classical optimization principles

Related Work

Normalization methods.

Normalization has long been a central tool for stabilizing optimization and improving convergence in deep networks. Batch Normalization (BN)
(
14
)
normalizes activations using mini-batch statistics and has been widely successful in convolutional architectures, but its behavior can depend on batch size and distributed synchronization. Layer Normalization (LN)
(
1
)
and its variants remove batch dependence by computing statistics across features within each sample and have become the standard in Transformers. Related methods such as Instance Normalization (IN)
(
40
)
, Group Normalization (GN)
(
44
)
, RMSNorm
(
46
)
, and nGPT
(
19
)
further tailor normalization to specific architectural or efficiency constraints.

Normalization Placement in Transformers.

Beyond the choice of normalization operator, its
placement
within Transformer blocks plays a critical role in optimization stability. The original Transformer architecture adopted post-normalization (PostNorm), in which normalization follows residual addition
(
41
)
. Subsequent large-scale practice shifted toward pre-normalization (PreNorm), placing normalization before attention and MLP sublayers to improve trainability in deep networks
(
43
)
.

Recent work further systematizes normalization placement and explores additional insertion points. Deeply Normalized Transformer (DNT)
(
28
)
categorizes multiple strategies—including InputNorm, PreNorm, MidNorm, PostNorm, and QKNorm—and motivates them through a Jacobian- and gradient-stability analysis. DNT ultimately combines InputNorm, PreNorm, MidNorm, and QKNorm, while avoiding PostNorm due to its potential training instabilities. Among these placements, QK normalization (QKNorm)
(
12
)
specifically targets the attention mechanism, stabilizing the geometry of query–key interactions and mitigating softmax saturation.

By treating normalization as a design space over both operator and location, these works emphasize that stability and conditioning can be targeted at specific architectural subcomponents, rather than only at block outputs. As model depth increases, normalization also interacts with residual pathways and initialization. DeepNorm
(
42
)
, for example, modifies residual scaling and initialization to bound parameter updates and control dynamical growth with depth, complementing normalization-placement strategies.

Normalization-free Transformers.

Motivated by the cost/complexity of normalization and the desire for simpler training dynamics, recent work questions whether explicit normalization is necessary in Transformers.

Transformers without Normalization
shows that replacing normalization layers with a simple point-wise nonlinearity, Dynamic Tanh (DyT)
(
48
)
, can match normalized baselines across tasks, suggesting that an appropriate bounded nonlinearity can provide much of the stability typically attributed to LN/RMSNorm.
Building on this,
Stronger Normalization-Free Transformers

(
5
)
studies the design of point-wise functions more broadly and reports improved normalization-free performance via a searched function family (e.g.,
\mathrm{Derf}
), outperforming LN/RMSNorm/DyT across multiple domains. Despite being framed as normalization-free, these approaches fundamentally operate by controlling the norm of activations through bounded transformations, and can therefore be viewed as a form of implicit normalization.

Positioning of our work.

While our method can be viewed as a study of normalization placement in Transformers, its key distinction lies in explicitly linking architectural design to second-order optimization geometry. Rather than motivating normalization heuristically or empirically, we analyze how local normalization immediately following linear mappings stabilizes activation scale and, in turn, constrains the spectral norm of the Hessian and leads to a smoother optimization landscape. This perspective yields a principled characterization of the maximum tolerable learning rate and provides a unified theoretical explanation for optimization stability in large Transformer models.

Preliminaries

We consider the unconstrained convex optimization problem

\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\bm{x}),

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
differentiable

3.1
Convex and Smoothed Optimization

Lipschitz gradient smoothness.

If
f
is twice differentiable, its second-order Taylor expansion at point
\bm{x}
is

\displaystyle f(\bm{y})\approx f(\bm{x})

\displaystyle+\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{y}-\bm{x}\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}(\bm{y}-\bm{x})^{\top}\nabla^{2}f(\bm{x})(\bm{y}-\bm{x}).

The second-order term captures the local curvature.

Definition 3.1

(
\beta
-smoothness)
.

The function
f
is said to be
\beta
-smooth if

\|\nabla f(\bm{y})-\nabla f(\bm{x})\|_{2}\leq\beta\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\|_{2},\forall\bm{y},\bm{x}.

For convex and differentiable functions,
\beta
-smoothness is equivalent to the following quadratic upper bound:

f(\bm{y})\leq f(\bm{x})+\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{y}-\bm{x}\rangle+\frac{\beta}{2}\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2},\forall\bm{y},\bm{x}.

This inequality plays a central role in step-size (learning rate) selection for optimization methods.

Gradient descent and learning rate.

Consider the standard gradient descent iteration

\bm{x}_{k+1}=\bm{x}_{k}-\eta\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k}),

where
\eta>0
is the learning rate. We wish to understand how the choice of
\eta
depends on the smoothness constant
\beta
, and how this choice affects convergence.

Descent condition.

Evaluating the quadratic upper bound with

\bm{y}=\bm{x}_{k+1}=\bm{x}_{k}-\eta\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})
and
\bm{x}=\bm{x}_{k}
, we obtain

f(\bm{x}_{k+1})\leq f(\bm{x}_{k})-\eta\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\beta\eta^{2}}{2}\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})\|_{2}^{2}.

Rearranging terms gives

f(\bm{x}_{k+1})\leq f(\bm{x}_{k})-\left(\eta-\frac{\beta\eta^{2}}{2}\right)\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})\|_{2}^{2}.

A sufficient condition for monotone decrease of the objective is therefore

0<\eta\leq\frac{2}{\beta}.

(1)

This bound characterizes the
stability region
of gradient descent for convex
\beta
-smooth functions.
\frac{2}{\beta}
is usually known as the maximum tolerable learning
rate.

For convex
\beta
-smooth problems, among all fixed learning rates that ensure descent, the canonical choice is typically
\eta=\frac{1}{\beta}
. This choice balances progress and stability and leads to the sharpest worst-case guarantees.

4.1
SimpleNorm: A Unified Normalization Strategy

Definition of SimpleNorm.

We define
SimpleNorm
as placing a normalization operator
immediately
after a linear mapping.
Given an input vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and a linear transformation
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
, we abstract SimpleNorm as a primitive operator

\bm{\Psi}(\bm{x})=\operatorname{Norm}({\bm{W}\bm{x}}),\qquad

(3)

where
\operatorname{Norm}(\cdot)
is a normalization operator such as LayerNorm or RMSNorm. SimpleNorm is motivated by a simple yet effective
placement
strategy, rather than algebraic complexity. In contrast to existing normalization techniques that typically operate at the level of residual blocks,
hidden states, or parameter reparameterization,
SimpleNorm enforces normalization
locally and immediately
after linear mapping, treating “linear mapping immediately followed by a normalization” as a single, unified operator.

As illustrated in

SimpleGPT
uses
SimpleNorm as a fundamental building block.
SimpleNorm is systematically inserted wherever a linear layer appears,
including MLP projections, attention projections (Q, K, V),
output projections, and gating or memory-related modules. Take

\bm{W}_{q}

\bm{W}_{k}

Instantiating SimpleNorm with RMSNorm

In this work, we instantiate

with RMSNorm

\bm{\Psi}(\bm{x};\bm{W},\bm{\gamma})=\bm{\gamma}\odot\sqrt{d}\,\frac{\bm{W}\bm{x}}{\left\lVert\bm{W}\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}},\qquad

(4)

where
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}

\bm{W},\bm{\gamma}

For later analysis, we define the intermediate variables

\displaystyle\bm{z}

\displaystyle=\bm{W}\bm{x},

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\|\bm{z}\|_{2},

\displaystyle\bm{u}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{z}}{s},

\displaystyle\bm{P}

\displaystyle=\bm{I}-\bm{u}\bm{u}^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{D}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Diag}(\bm{\gamma}),

\bm{\Psi}(\bm{x};\bm{W},\bm{\gamma})=\sqrt{d}\,\bm{D}\bm{u}

Core Properties of SimpleNorm

The following sections prove two important mechanisms of SimpleNorm: SimpleNorm directly stabilizes the scale of activations to be on the order of
\sqrt{d}
, and SimpleNorm constrains the spectral norm of the Hessian of the loss w.r.t. the activations, smoothing the loss landscape and enabling larger learning rates. Moreover, we present a hypothesis for why, in addition to the predicted optimization stability, SimpleNorm also exhibits strong empirical performance.

4.2
Mechanism I: Stable Activation Scale

By construction, SimpleNorm stabilizes the scale of intermediate activations by normalizing
immediately
after each linear mapping. Recall that

\bm{\Psi}(\bm{x};\bm{W},\bm{\gamma})=\sqrt{d}\,\bm{D}\bm{u}\qquad\text{ with }\|\bm{u}\|_{2}=1.

Then

\|\bm{\Psi}(\bm{x};\bm{W},\bm{\gamma})\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}\,\|\bm{D}\bm{u}\|_{2}.

In particular, letting
\gamma_{\min}=\min_{i}|\gamma_{i}|
and
\gamma_{\max}=\max_{i}|\gamma_{i}|
, we have the bound

\gamma_{\min}\sqrt{d}\;\leq\;\|\bm{\Psi}(\bm{x})\|_{2}\;\leq\;\gamma_{\max}\sqrt{d}.

Thus, up to the learned per-dimension scaling
\bm{\gamma}
, each projection is rescaled to have norm on the order of
\sqrt{d}
. Consequently, SimpleNorm prevents intermediate representation norms from drifting with depth or weight growth, eliminating a common source of activation explosion. Mechanism II shows how, in addition to activations, SimpleNorm also stabilizes curvature via the gradient Lipschitz constant.

4.3
Mechanism II: Smoother Loss Landscape

Smoothness and the Hessian.

The smoothness of the objective directly constrains optimization stability: larger curvature implies smaller safe learning rates. Given a twice-differentiable
\beta
-smooth objective
\ell(x)
, we quantify local curvature by the activation Hessian
\bm{H}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}=\nabla^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}\ell
. Specifically, the supremum of the spectral norm of the Hessian upper bounds local curvature and governs gradient stability:

\beta\;=\;\sup_{\bm{x}}\|\bm{H}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}(\bm{x})\|_{2}.

To show SimpleNorm yields a smoother landscape, we prove two results: (i) the SimpleNorm Hessian decomposes as
\bm{H}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}=\bm{L}+\bm{C}
and in high dimension
\|\bm{C}\|_{2}\ll\|\bm{L}\|_{2}
; (ii) compared to a linear projection whose curvature scales as
\|\bm{W}\|_{2}^{2}
, the SimpleNorm curvature is scale-invariant with respect to
\|\bm{W}\|_{2}
. Combined, these imply
\|\bm{H}^{\mathrm{sn}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}\|_{2}\ll\|\bm{H}^{\mathrm{lin}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}\|_{2}
since
\|\bm{W}\|_{2}
generally grows during training.

SimpleNorm Derivatives.

First, we compute the first-order gradient and second-order Hessian of
\ell
with respect to
\bm{x}
.

Given
\bm{y}=\sqrt{d}\,\bm{D}\bm{u}
, let

\ell=\ell(\bm{y}),\qquad\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}:=\nabla_{\bm{y}}\ell,\qquad\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}:=\nabla^{2}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\ell.

First-order derivative.

The Jacobian of the normalization satisfies

\frac{\partial\bm{u}}{\partial\bm{z}}=\frac{1}{s}\bm{P},

which yields the Jacobian of
\bm{y}
with respect to
\bm{x}
:

\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}:=\frac{\partial\bm{y}}{\partial\bm{x}}=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{s}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{P}\,\bm{W}.

Applying the chain rule, the gradient is

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ell={\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}}^{\top}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{s}\,\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{P}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}.

(5)

Second-order derivative.

Differentiating the gradient leads to the standard decomposition

\bm{H}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}=\nabla^{2}_{\bm{x}}\ell=\underbrace{{\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}}^{\top}\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\,\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}}_{\text{Gauss--Newton term}}\;+\;\underbrace{\bm{C}}_{\text{curvature term}},

(6)

where the first term is the Gauss–Newton component

{\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}}^{\top}\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}=\frac{d}{s^{2}}\,\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{P}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{P}\,\bm{W},

(7)

and the second term is from the curvature of the normalization,

\bm{C}=-\frac{\sqrt{d}}{s^{2}}\,\bm{W}^{\top}\Bigl(\bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\bm{u}^{\top}+\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\bm{P}+\bm{u}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{P}\Bigr)\bm{W}.

(8)

Gauss–Newton Term Dominates in High Dimension.

Next, we show that under standard high-dimensional and non-pathological conditions,
the Gauss–Newton term dominates the curvature induced by normalization.

Theorem 4.1

(Gauss–Newton dominance for SimpleNorm)
.

Let
\bm{H}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}=\nabla^{2}_{\bm{x}}\ell
denote the activation Hessian induced by
SimpleNorm for a twice-differentiable objective
\ell(\bm{y})
. Then, the Hessian decomposes as

\bm{H}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}=\bm{L}+\bm{C},\qquad\bm{L}=(\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}})^{\top}\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}},

where
\bm{C}
is the curvature term induced by normalization.

Assume
\|\bm{x}\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
,
\bm{D}=\bm{I}
,
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
has high effective rank

\|\bm{W}\|_{F}^{2}/\|\bm{W}\|_{2}^{2}\geq c\,d
, and the input and loss derivatives are not
pathologically aligned with
\bm{W}
. Define

\kappa:=\left\|\frac{\sqrt{d}\,\bm{W}}{\|\bm{W}\bm{x}\|_{2}}\right\|_{2}.

Then
\kappa=\Theta(1)
with high probability, and there exists a constant

\tau=\Theta(1)
such that

\|\bm{L}\|_{2}=\tau\,\kappa^{2}\,\|\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\|_{2},\qquad\|\bm{C}\|_{2}\leq\frac{3\kappa^{2}}{\sqrt{d}}\,\|\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\|_{2}.

In particular, if
\|\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\|_{2}=O(\|\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\|_{2})
, then

\|\bm{C}\|_{2}\ll\|\bm{L}\|_{2}

so the Gauss–Newton term dominates the Hessian w.h.p.

A complete proof is given in
Appendix D
.

SimpleNorm Hessian is Weight Scale-Invariant.

Finally, we compare the SimpleNorm Hessian’s magnitude to that of a plain linear projection. We show that linear curvature grows quadratically with the weight matrix spectral norm
\|\bm{W}\|_{2}
, whereas SimpleNorm removes this dependence.

Theorem 4.2

(Linear curvature scales with
\|\bm{W}\|_{2}^{2}
while SimpleNorm does not)
.

Let
\ell=\ell(\bm{y})
be twice differentiable, with

\bm{H}_{yy}=\nabla^{2}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\ell
and
\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}=\nabla_{\bm{y}}\ell
.

Consider the linear mapping with its Hessian

\bm{y}_{1}=\bm{W}_{1}\bm{x},\qquad\bm{H}^{\mathrm{lin}}_{xx}=\bm{W}_{1}^{\top}\bm{H}_{yy}\bm{W}_{1},

and the SimpleNorm mapping with its Hessian

\bm{y}_{2}=\bm{D}\frac{\sqrt{d}\,\bm{W}_{2}\bm{x}}{\|\bm{W}_{2}\bm{x}\|_{2}},\qquad\bm{H}^{\mathrm{sn}}_{xx}=\bm{L}+\bm{C}.

Assume
\bm{H}_{y_{1}y_{1}}=\bm{H}_{y_{2}y_{2}}:=\bm{H}_{yy}
,
\bm{W}_{1}=\bm{W}_{2}:=\bm{W}
, and that the conditions of
Theorem 4.1
hold, such that
\|\bm{L}\|_{2}\gg\|\bm{C}\|_{2}
. Then, with high-probability,

\|\bm{H}^{\mathrm{sn}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}\|_{2}=\Theta\!\left(\kappa^{2}\,\|\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\|_{2}\right),\qquad\kappa^{2}=\frac{d}{\|\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}}=\Theta(1),

where
\widetilde{\bm{W}}=\bm{W}/\|\bm{W}\|_{2}
.

Moreover, if the range of
\widetilde{\bm{W}}
is not adversarially aligned with the leading eigenspace of
\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}
, then there
exists a constant
c_{\mathrm{lin}}=\Theta(1)
such that

\|\bm{H}^{\mathrm{lin}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}\|_{2}=\|\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\bm{W}\|_{2}\;\geq\;c_{\mathrm{lin}}\,\|\bm{W}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\|_{2}.

Consequently, as
\|\bm{W}\|_{2}
grows during training,

\|\bm{H}^{\mathrm{lin}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}\|_{2}\;\gg\;\|\bm{H}^{\mathrm{sn}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}\|_{2}\qquad\text{(with high probability)}.

Intuitively, SimpleNorm removes the dependence of curvature on weight scale by normalizing activations, whereas a linear projection amplifies curvature as
\|\bm{W}\|_{2}
grows. We provide a proof for
Theorem 4.2
in
Appendix E
.

SimpleNorm Enables Larger Learning Rates.

For a twice-differentiable
\beta
-smooth objective,
the maximum stable learning rate of gradient descent is inversely proportional to
\beta
, the Lipschitz constant of the gradient, which is equivalent to the supremum of the spectral norm of the Hessian:

\eta\;\leq\;\frac{2}{\beta}
where
\beta=\sup_{\bm{x}}\|\bm{H}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}(\bm{x})\|_{2}

Theorem 4.1
and
Theorem 4.2
establish that, under standard high-dimensional and non-pathological conditions, the SimpleNorm Hessian is invariant to the spectral norm of the weight matrix whereas the Hessian of a linear projection scales quadratically with the weight norm.

Consequently, since the weight spectral norm generally grows throughout training, the SimpleNorm-based SimpleGPT architecture has a smoother loss landscape that can tolerate significantly larger learning rates compared to methods based on direct linear projections.

4.4
Interpretation: Beyond Optimization Stability

We have established two core properties of SimpleNorm:
(i) it stabilizes activation scale at
\Theta(\sqrt{d})
, and
(ii) it smooths the loss landscape by constraining the spectral norm of the
activation Hessian, enabling larger and more stable learning rates.
Although these properties explain the improved optimization stability of SimpleNorm,
they do not fully account for the strong empirical performance observed in
Section 5
.

We hypothesize that SimpleNorm provides additional benefits at a more
global
representational level. By normalizing immediately after each linear projection,
SimpleNorm ensures that every layer induces a genuinely nonlinear transformation,
even in regimes where the surrounding network would otherwise behave nearly
linearly. This effectively increases the depth of nonlinear interactions and
enhances expressive capacity without increasing parameter count.

Under this view, SimpleNorm improves performance through a dual effect:
locally, by improving optimization geometry via reduced curvature variability;
and globally, by increasing expressiveness through pervasive
normalization-induced nonlinearity. We believe this combination explains why
SimpleNorm yields consistent empirical gains beyond what would be expected from
learning-rate stability alone.

4.5
Use
torch.compile
to Speedup Training

Normalization layers are memory-bound and frequently executed, making them a potential bottleneck. By fusing reduction and pointwise operations and leveraging
torch.compile
, SimpleNorm’s increased normalization overhead is largely amortized, resulting in around a 3% training-time increase compared to GPT with QKNorm.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we revisit Transformer optimization from a second-order perspective and establish a direct connection between architectural design, the Hessian matrix, and optimization stability. By introducing SimpleNorm and analyzing its induced Hessian structure, we show that reducing the Hessian norm of the activation with respect to the loss enables substantially larger admissible learning rates. The resulting model,
SimpleGPT
, reliably supports learning rates up to 3
\times
-10
\times
larger than strong baselines while maintaining stable optimization. Across extensive experiments on
nanoGPT
-,
Llama2
and
Llama3
-style models, spanning parameter scales from 1B to 8B, our method consistently achieves substantially stronger performance than GPT with QKNorm. Importantly, these gains are obtained with minimal additional computational overhead.

Appendix A
Derivatives of
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ell
and
\nabla_{\bm{x}}^{2}\ell
for SimpleNorm

We consider the mapping

\bm{y}=\bm{\gamma}\odot\sqrt{d}\,\frac{\bm{W}\bm{x}}{\|\bm{W}\bm{x}\|_{2}},

where
\bm{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\odot
denotes elementwise multiplication and
\ell=\ell(\bm{y})
is a scalar loss.

As before, let us define some intermediate variables,

\bm{z}=\bm{W}\bm{x},\qquad s=\|\bm{z}\|_{2},\qquad\bm{u}=\frac{\bm{z}}{s},\qquad\bm{P}=\bm{I}-\bm{u}\bm{u}^{\top},\qquad\bm{D}=\operatorname{Diag}(\bm{\gamma}),

where
\bm{P},\bm{D}
are symmetric.

Let us define the normalized and scaled output as

\bm{y}=\sqrt{d}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{u},

and let

\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}:=\nabla_{\bm{y}}\ell,\qquad\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}:=\nabla^{2}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\ell.

Let us define the Jacobian
\bm{A}
of the normalization map
\bm{u}
with respect to
\bm{z}
as

\bm{A}:=\frac{\partial\bm{u}}{\partial\bm{z}}=\frac{1}{s}\,\bm{P},

and consequently

\frac{\partial\bm{y}}{\partial\bm{z}}=\sqrt{d}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{A}=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{s}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{P}.

We will repeatedly use the chain rule through the path

\bm{W}\to\bm{z}\to\bm{u}\to\bm{y}\to\ell,\qquad\text{and similarly}\qquad\bm{\gamma}\to\bm{D}\to\bm{y}\to\ell.

(1) First-order derivative.

Since
\bm{z}=\bm{W}\bm{x}
, we have

d\bm{z}=\bm{W}\,d\bm{x}.

From
\bm{u}=\bm{z}/s
and
\bm{A}=\partial\bm{u}/\partial\bm{z}
, it follows that

d\bm{u}=\bm{A}\,d\bm{z}=\frac{1}{s}\,\bm{P}\,d\bm{z}.

Therefore,

d\bm{y}=\sqrt{d}\,\bm{D}\,d\bm{u}=\sqrt{d}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{A}\,\bm{W}\,d\bm{x},

so the Jacobian is

\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}:=\frac{\partial\bm{y}}{\partial\bm{x}}=\sqrt{d}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{A}\,\bm{W}=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{s}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{P}\,\bm{W}.

Applying the chain rule gives

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ell={\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}}^{\top}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}=\sqrt{d}\,\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{D}^{\top}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}.

Using
\bm{A}^{\top}=\bm{A}
and
\bm{D}^{\top}=\bm{D}
, we obtain

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ell=\sqrt{d}\,\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{A}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{s}\,\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{P}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}.

(9)

(2) Second-order derivative.

Differentiating
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ell=\sqrt{d}\,\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{A}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}
yields

d(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\ell)=\sqrt{d}\,\bm{W}^{\top}\Bigl(\bm{A}\,\bm{D}\,d\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}+d\bm{A}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\Bigr).

This induces the standard decomposition

\nabla_{\bm{x}}^{2}\ell={\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}}^{\top}\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}+\bm{C},

where the two terms are computed below.

(A) Linear (Gauss–Newton) term.

Since
d\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}=\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\,d\bm{y}
, and
d\bm{y}=\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}\,d\bm{x}
, we have

d\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}=\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\,\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}\,d\bm{x}.

Substituting into
\sqrt{d}\,\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{A}\,\bm{D}\,d\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}
gives

\sqrt{d}\,\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{A}\,\bm{D}\,d\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}=\sqrt{d}\,\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{A}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\,\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}\,d\bm{x}={\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}}^{\top}\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}\,d\bm{x}.

Using
\bm{A}=\bm{P}/s
and
\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}=(\sqrt{d}/s)\bm{D}\bm{P}\bm{W}
, we obtain the explicit form

{\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}}^{\top}\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}=\frac{d}{s^{2}}\,\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{P}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{P}\,\bm{W}.

(B) Curvature (normalization) term.

Recall
\bm{A}=\frac{1}{s}\bm{P}
.
Using
d\bm{u}=\bm{A}\,d\bm{z}
and
ds=d\|\bm{z}\|_{2}=\bm{u}^{\top}d\bm{z}
, one obtains

d\bm{A}=-\frac{1}{s^{2}}\Bigl((\bm{u}^{\top}d\bm{z})\bm{P}+\bm{P}\,d\bm{z}\,\bm{u}^{\top}+\bm{u}\,d\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{P}\Bigr).

Right-multiplying by
\bm{D}\,\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}
gives

d\bm{A}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}=-\frac{1}{s^{2}}\Bigl((\bm{u}^{\top}d\bm{z})\bm{P}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}+\bm{P}\,d\bm{z}\,\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{D}\,\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}+\bm{u}\,d\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{P}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\Bigr).

Equivalently, pulling the scalar contractions to the left yields the linear map in
d\bm{z}
:

d\bm{A}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}=-\frac{1}{s^{2}}\Bigl(\bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\bm{u}^{\top}+\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\bm{P}+\bm{u}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{P}\Bigr)\,d\bm{z}.

Substituting
d\bm{z}=\bm{W}\,d\bm{x}
and left-multiplying by
\sqrt{d}\,\bm{W}^{\top}
gives the curvature term

\bm{C}=-\frac{\sqrt{d}}{s^{2}}\,\bm{W}^{\top}\Bigl(\bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\bm{u}^{\top}+\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\bm{P}+\bm{u}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{P}\Bigr)\bm{W}.

(If desired, define
\bm{g}_{1}:=\bm{D}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}
to simplify the expression, but we keep the figure’s symbols explicit.)

Combining the linear and curvature terms, the Hessian with respect to
\bm{x}
is

\bm{H}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}=\nabla_{\bm{x}}^{2}\ell=\frac{d}{s^{2}}\,\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{P}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{P}\,\bm{W}\;-\;\frac{\sqrt{d}}{s^{2}}\,\bm{W}^{\top}\Bigl(\bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\bm{u}^{\top}+\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\bm{P}+\bm{u}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{P}\Bigr)\bm{W},

(10)

where
\bm{z}=\bm{W}\bm{x},s=\|\bm{z}\|_{2},\bm{u}=\frac{\bm{z}}{s},\bm{P}=\bm{I}-\bm{u}\bm{u}^{\top},\bm{D}=\operatorname{Diag}(\bm{\gamma}).

Derivatives of
\nabla_{\bm{W}}\ell
and
\nabla_{\operatorname{vec}(\bm{W})}^{2}\ell
for
\bm{y}=\bm{W}\bm{x}

We consider the linear mapping

\bm{y}=\bm{W}\bm{x},

where
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
,
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
\ell=\ell(\bm{y})
be a scalar-valued loss function.

We define the first- and second-order derivatives of the loss
l

with respect to
\bm{y}
as

\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\;:=\;\nabla_{\bm{y}}\ell(\bm{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\;:=\;\nabla_{\bm{y}}^{2}\ell(\bm{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.

\bm{W}
.

Since
\bm{y}
depends linearly on
\bm{W}

d\bm{y}=d\bm{W}\,\bm{x}.

Applying the chain rule,

d\ell=\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}^{\top}d\bm{y}=\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}^{\top}(d\bm{W}\,\bm{x})=\langle\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\bm{x}^{\top},\,d\bm{W}\rangle_{F}.

Matching coefficients under the Frobenius inner product yields the gradient

\nabla_{\bm{W}}\ell\;=\;\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\,\bm{x}^{\top}.

(11)

(2) Second-order derivative with respect to
\bm{W}
.

To express second-order derivatives, it is convenient to vectorize
\bm{W}
.
Using the standard identity for matrix–vector products,

\bm{y}=(\bm{x}^{\top}\otimes\bm{I}_{d})\,\operatorname{vec}(\bm{W}),

\bm{y}
on
\operatorname{vec}(\bm{W})

Jacobian with respect to
\operatorname{vec}(\bm{W})
.

From the above expression, the Jacobian of
\bm{y}
with respect to

\operatorname{vec}(\bm{W})

\bm{J}_{\operatorname{vec}(\bm{W})}^{\bm{y}}\;\triangleq\;\frac{\partial\bm{y}}{\partial\operatorname{vec}(\bm{W})}=\bm{x}^{\top}\otimes\bm{I}_{d}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{d\times(md)}.

\operatorname{vec}(\bm{W})

Because the mapping
\bm{W}\mapsto\bm{y}

Hence, by the second-order chain rule, the Hessian of the loss with respect to

\nabla_{\operatorname{vec}(\bm{W})}^{2}\ell={\bm{J}_{\operatorname{vec}(\bm{W})}^{\bm{y}}}^{\top}\,\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\,\bm{J}_{\operatorname{vec}(\bm{W})}^{\bm{y}}.

\bm{H}_{\bm{z}\bm{z}}=\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{y}}{\partial\bm{z}}\Big)^{\!\top}\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{y}}{\partial\bm{z}}\Big)+\sum_{k=1}^{d}(\bm{g}_{\bm{y}})_{k}\,\nabla^{2}_{\bm{z}\bm{z}}y_{k}.

The first term is the Gauss–Newton part:

\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{y}}{\partial\bm{z}}\Big)^{\!\top}\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\Big(\frac{\partial\bm{y}}{\partial\bm{z}}\Big)=\frac{d}{s^{2}}\,\bm{P}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{P}.

For the second term, we use the bilinear form of the normalization Hessian:

\nabla^{2}\bm{u}(\bm{z})[\bm{a},\bm{b}]=-\frac{1}{s^{2}}\Big((\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{a})\,\bm{P}\bm{b}+(\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{b})\,\bm{P}\bm{a}+(\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{P}\bm{b})\,\bm{u}\Big).

Since
\bm{y}=\sqrt{d}\bm{D}\bm{u}
,

\sum_{k=1}^{d}(\bm{g}_{\bm{y}})_{k}\,\nabla^{2}y_{k}[\bm{a},\bm{b}]=\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}^{\top}\nabla^{2}\bm{y}[\bm{a},\bm{b}]=\sqrt{d}\,(\bm{D}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}})^{\top}\nabla^{2}\bm{u}[\bm{a},\bm{b}].

Substituting gives

\sum_{k=1}^{d}(\bm{g}_{\bm{y}})_{k}\,\nabla^{2}y_{k}[\bm{a},\bm{b}]=-\frac{\sqrt{d}}{s^{2}}\Big((\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{a})(\bm{D}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}})^{\top}\bm{P}\bm{b}+(\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{b})(\bm{D}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}})^{\top}\bm{P}\bm{a}+(\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}})\,\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{P}\bm{b}\Big).

Equivalently, the associated matrix form is

\sum_{k=1}^{d}(\bm{g}_{\bm{y}})_{k}\,\nabla^{2}_{\bm{z}\bm{z}}y_{k}=-\frac{\sqrt{d}}{s^{2}}\Big(\bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\bm{u}^{\top}+\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\bm{P}+\bm{u}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{P}\Big).

Combining both terms,

\bm{H}_{\bm{z}\bm{z}}=\frac{d}{s^{2}}\,\bm{P}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{P}-\frac{\sqrt{d}}{s^{2}}\Big(\bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\bm{u}^{\top}+\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\bm{P}+\bm{u}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{P}\Big),\qquad s=\|\bm{W}\bm{x}\|_{2}.

Using
\operatorname{vec}(\bm{z})=(\bm{x}^{\top}\otimes\bm{I})\operatorname{vec}(\bm{W})
,

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{\operatorname{vec}(\bm{W})\operatorname{vec}(\bm{W})}

\displaystyle=(\bm{x}\bm{x}^{\top})\otimes\bm{H}_{\bm{z}\bm{z}}

(16)

\displaystyle=(\bm{x}\bm{x}^{\top})\otimes\left[\frac{d}{s^{2}}\,\bm{P}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{P}-\frac{\sqrt{d}}{s^{2}}\Big(\bm{P}\bm{D}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\bm{u}^{\top}+\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\bm{P}+\bm{u}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}^{\top}\bm{D}\bm{P}\Big)\right].

Appendix D
Proof of
Theorem 4.1

Proof.

Recall SimpleNorm:

\bm{y}=\bm{\gamma}\odot\sqrt{d}\,\frac{\bm{W}\bm{x}}{\|\bm{W}\bm{x}\|_{2}},\qquad\bm{z}=\bm{W}\bm{x},\qquad s:=\|\bm{z}\|_{2},\qquad\bm{u}:=\frac{\bm{z}}{s},\qquad\bm{P}:=\bm{I}-\bm{u}\bm{u}^{\top}.

Assume
\bm{D}=\operatorname{Diag}(\bm{\gamma})=\bm{I}
. Let
\ell=\ell(\bm{y})
and define

\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}:=\nabla_{\bm{y}}\ell(\bm{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}:=\nabla^{2}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\ell(\bm{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.

By the chain rule, we have the decomposition:

\bm{H}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}=\nabla^{2}_{\bm{x}}\ell=\underbrace{{\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}}^{\top}\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\,\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}}_{\text{Gauss--Newton term}}\;+\;\underbrace{\bm{C}}_{\text{curvature term}}

(17)

Here the Jacobian is

\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{s}\,\bm{D}\bm{P}\bm{W}=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{s}\,\bm{P}\bm{W},

and therefore the Gauss–Newton term is

\bm{L}=(\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}})^{\top}\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}}=\frac{d}{s^{2}}\,\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{P}\,\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\,\bm{P}\,\bm{W}.

(18)

The curvature term (with the condition
\bm{D}=\bm{I}
) can be written as

\bm{C}=-\frac{\sqrt{d}}{s^{2}}\,\bm{W}^{\top}\Bigl((\bm{P}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}})\bm{u}^{\top}+(\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}})\,\bm{P}+\bm{u}(\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}^{\top}\bm{P})\Bigr)\bm{W},

(19)

where
\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}
is a scalar.

Bounding the Gauss–Newton term.

Define

\kappa:=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\|\bm{W}\bm{x}\|_{2}}\,\|\bm{W}\|_{2}

We show that
\kappa=\Theta(1)
under high effective rank and typical input.
Let
\bm{x}=\sqrt{d}\,\xi
where
\xi
is isotropic (e.g., uniform on the sphere or subgaussian). Then

\mathbb{E}\|\bm{W}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}=d\,\mathbb{E}\,\xi^{\top}\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{W}\xi=\|\bm{W}\|_{F}^{2},

and by standard concentration for quadratic forms,

\|\bm{W}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}\asymp\|\bm{W}\|_{F}^{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\|\bm{W}\bm{x}\|_{2}\asymp\|\bm{W}\|_{F}\qquad\text{with high probability}.

Thus

\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\|\bm{W}\bm{x}\|_{2}}\,\|\bm{W}\|_{2}\asymp\sqrt{d}\,\frac{\|\bm{W}\|_{2}}{\|\bm{W}\|_{F}}=\sqrt{\frac{d}{r_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{W})}},\qquad r_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{W}):=\frac{\|\bm{W}\|_{F}^{2}}{\|\bm{W}\|_{2}^{2}}.

If
r_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{W})\asymp cd
, then
\kappa\asymp 1/\sqrt{c}=\Theta(1)
with high probability.

Additionally, define

\tau:=\frac{\big\|\widetilde{\bm{W}}^{\top}\bm{P}\,\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\,\bm{P}\,\widetilde{\bm{W}}\big\|_{2}}{\|\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\|_{2}}\in[0,1],\qquad\widetilde{\bm{W}}:=\frac{\bm{W}}{\|\bm{W}\|_{2}}.

Under the theorem’s “non-pathological alignment” assumption (i.e., the dominant spectral modes of
\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}
are not eliminated by projecting out
\mathrm{span}(\bm{u})
and are represented in the range of
\bm{W}
, which has high effective rank), we have
\tau=\Theta(1)
.

Now, it follows that

\bm{L}=\frac{d}{\|\bm{W}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}}\,\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{P}\,\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\,\bm{P}\,\bm{W}=\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\|\bm{W}\|_{2}^{2}}\,\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{P}\,\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\,\bm{P}\,\bm{W}=\kappa^{2}\,\widetilde{\bm{W}}^{\top}\bm{P}\,\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\,\bm{P}\,\widetilde{\bm{W}}

and

\|\bm{L}\|_{2}=\tau\,\kappa^{2}\,\|\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\|_{2}\qquad\text{where }\tau=\Theta(1)\text{ and }\kappa=\Theta(1)\quad[\text{w.h.p}]

(20)

Bounding the curvature term.

From
Equation 19
and submultiplicativity,

\|\bm{C}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{d}}{s^{2}}\,\|\bm{W}\|_{2}^{2}\,\Big\|(\bm{P}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}})\bm{u}^{\top}+(\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}})\,\bm{P}+\bm{u}(\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}^{\top}\bm{P})\Big\|_{2}.

Now use
\|\bm{u}\|_{2}=1
,
\|\bm{P}\|_{2}=1
, and
\|\bm{P}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\|_{2}\leq\|\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\|_{2}
:

\|(\bm{P}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}})\bm{u}^{\top}\|_{2}=\|\bm{P}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\|_{2}\|\bm{u}\|_{2}\leq\|\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\|_{2},

\|(\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}})\,\bm{P}\|_{2}=|\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}|\,\|\bm{P}\|_{2}\leq\|\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\|_{2},

\|\bm{u}(\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}^{\top}\bm{P})\|_{2}=\|\bm{u}\|_{2}\,\|\bm{P}\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\|_{2}\leq\|\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\|_{2}.

By triangle inequality, the middle norm is at most
3\|\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\|_{2}
, hence

\|\bm{C}\|_{2}\leq\frac{3\sqrt{d}}{\|\bm{W}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}}\,\|\bm{W}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\|_{2}=\frac{3\kappa^{2}}{\sqrt{d}}\,\|\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\|_{2}.

(21)

Dominance of
\bm{L}
over
\bm{C}
.

Combining (
20
) and (
21
),

\frac{\|\bm{C}\|_{2}}{\|\bm{L}\|_{2}}\leq\frac{3}{\tau\sqrt{d}}\,\frac{\|\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\|_{2}}{\|\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\|_{2}}.

Assuming
\tau=\Theta(1)
(non-pathological alignment) and
\|\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\|_{2}/\|\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\|_{2}=O(1)
(bounded gradient-to-curvature ratio, as stated in the theorem assumptions), we obtain

\frac{\|\bm{C}\|_{2}}{\|\bm{L}\|_{2}}=O(d^{-1/2}),

so in high dimension
\|\bm{L}\|_{2}\gg\|\bm{C}\|_{2}
with high probability. Therefore the Gauss–Newton term dominates
\bm{H}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}
in typical non-pathological regimes.

This completes the proof of
Theorem 4.1
.
∎

Appendix E
Proof of
Theorem 4.2

Proof.

Provided
\ell=\ell(\bm{y})
is twice differentiable. Denote

\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}:=\nabla_{\bm{y}}\ell,\qquad\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}:=\nabla^{2}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\ell,

We have the two mappings:

Linear:
\bm{y}_{1}=\bm{W}_{1}\bm{x}
,
\bm{H}^{\mathrm{lin}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}=\bm{W}_{1}^{\top}\bm{H}_{\bm{y}_{1}\bm{y}_{1}}\bm{W}_{1}
.

SimpleNorm:
\bm{y}_{2}=\bm{D}\frac{\sqrt{d}\,\bm{W}_{2}\bm{x}}{\|\bm{W}_{2}\bm{x}\|_{2}}
,
where
\bm{D}=\mathrm{Diag}(\bm{\gamma})
and define

\bm{z}=\bm{W}_{2}\bm{x},\quad s=\|\bm{z}\|_{2},\quad\bm{u}=\bm{z}/s,\quad\bm{P}=\bm{I}-\bm{u}\bm{u}^{\top}.

The Hessian w.r.t.
\bm{x}
admits the standard decomposition

\bm{H}^{\mathrm{sn}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}\;=\;\bm{L}+\bm{C},\qquad\bm{L}={\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}_{2}}}^{\top}\bm{H}_{\bm{y}_{2}\bm{y}_{2}}{\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}_{2}}},

where
\bm{J}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{y}_{2}}
is the Jacobian of
\bm{y}_{2}
w.r.t.
\bm{x}
, and
\bm{C}
is the
curvature term induced by the normalization.

Assuming the high-dimensional conditions stated in
Theorem 4.1
hold
(
\|\bm{x}\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
,
\bm{D}=\bm{I}
,
\|\bm{P}\|_{2}=1
,
\bm{W}_{2}
has high effective rank, no pathological alignment, and
\|\bm{g}_{\bm{y}}\|_{2}/\|\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\|_{2}=O(1)
),
Theorem 4.1
shows that the Gauss-Newton term dominates the curvature term, i.e.,
\|\bm{L}\|_{2}\gg\|\bm{C}\|_{2}
. Therefore, we obtain the approximation
\|\bm{H}^{\mathrm{sn}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}\|_{2}\;\approx\|\bm{L}\|_{2}
.

We now show an integral result: the spectral norm of
\bm{H}^{\mathrm{lin}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}
is directly proportional to the spectral norm of the weight matrix
\bm{W}
whereas the spectral norm of
\bm{H}^{\mathrm{sn}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}
is independent of the spectral norm of the weight matrix. In the following, we assume
\bm{W}=\bm{W}_{1}=\bm{W}_{2}
and
\bm{H}_{\bm{y}_{1}\bm{y}_{1}}\,=\,\bm{H}_{\bm{y}_{2}\bm{y}_{2}}\,:=\,\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}
.

Define
\widetilde{\bm{W}}\;:=\;\frac{\bm{W}}{\alpha}
where
\alpha\;:=\;\|\bm{W}\|_{2}
. Consequently,
\|\widetilde{\bm{W}}\|_{2}=1
and
\bm{W}=\alpha\widetilde{\bm{W}}
. Now, treating
\bm{H}^{\mathrm{lin}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}
and
\bm{H}^{\mathrm{sn}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}
as functions of
\alpha
, it follows that:

\|\bm{H}^{\mathrm{lin}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}(\alpha)\|_{2}=\|\bm{W}_{1}^{\top}\bm{H}_{\bm{y}_{1}\bm{y}_{1}}\bm{W}_{1}\|_{2}=\alpha^{2}\|\widetilde{\bm{W}}^{\top}\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\widetilde{\bm{W}}\|_{2}

(22)

and

\|\bm{H}^{\mathrm{sn}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}(\alpha)\|_{2}\;\approx\;\|\bm{L}(\alpha)\|_{2}=\frac{d}{\|\bm{W}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}}\,\|\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{P}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\,\bm{D}\,\bm{P}\,\bm{W}\|_{2}=\frac{d}{\|\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}}\,\|\widetilde{\bm{W}}^{\top}\,\bm{P}\,\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\,\bm{P}\,\widetilde{\bm{W}}\|_{2}

(23)

Note that
\bm{P}=\bm{I}-\bm{u}\bm{u}^{\top}
is independent of
\alpha
:

\bm{u}(\alpha)=\frac{\bm{W}\bm{x}}{\|\bm{W}\bm{x}\|_{2}}=\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{x}}{\|\alpha\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{x}\|_{2}}=\frac{\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{x}}{\|\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{x}\|_{2}},\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\bm{P}(\alpha)=\bm{I}-\bm{u}(\alpha)\bm{u}(\alpha)^{\top}=\bm{P}(1).

Therefore,
\|\bm{H}^{\mathrm{lin}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}(\alpha)\|_{2}
depends quadratically on
\alpha
whereas
\|\bm{H}^{\mathrm{sn}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}(\alpha)\|_{2}
is
scale-invariant
in
\alpha
.

Next, we compare the magnitudes of the two Hessians. First, recall the definition of
\kappa
provided in
Theorem 4.1
:

\kappa\;:=\;\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\|\bm{W}\bm{x}\|_{2}}\|{\bm{W}}\|_{2}=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\alpha\|\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{x}\|_{2}}\alpha\|{\widetilde{\bm{W}}}\|_{2}=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\|\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{x}\|_{2}}

By definition,
\kappa^{2}=\frac{d}{\|\widetilde{\bm{W}}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}}
, and
Theorem 4.1
implies
\kappa^{2}=\Theta(1)
w.h.p.

Moreover, since
\|\widetilde{\bm{W}}\|_{2}=\|\bm{P}\|_{2}=1
,

\|\bm{H}^{\mathrm{sn}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}(\alpha)\|_{2}\approx\|\bm{L}\|_{2}=\kappa^{2}\,\|\widetilde{\bm{W}}^{\top}\bm{P}\,\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\,\bm{P}\,\widetilde{\bm{W}}\|_{2}\leq\kappa^{2}\,\|\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\|_{2}

(24)

For the linear module, there exists a constant
c_{\mathrm{lin}}>0
such that

\|\bm{H}^{\mathrm{lin}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}(\alpha)\|_{2}=\alpha^{2}\|\widetilde{\bm{W}}^{\top}\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\widetilde{\bm{W}}\|_{2}\geq\alpha^{2}c_{\mathrm{lin}}\,\|\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\|_{2}.

We assume
\mathrm{Range}(\widetilde{\bm{W}})
is not adversarially aligned with the leading eigenspace of
\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}
; in particular the overlap is constant-order, so the visibility constant satisfies
c_{\mathrm{lin}}=\Theta(1)
and does not decay with
d
.

Combining all the aforementioned results, the final ratio between the two Hessians satisfies:

\frac{\|\bm{H}^{\mathrm{lin}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}(\alpha)\|_{2}}{\|\bm{H}^{\mathrm{sn}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}(\alpha)\|_{2}}\;\geq\;\frac{\alpha^{2}\|\widetilde{\bm{W}}^{\top}\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\widetilde{\bm{W}}\|_{2}}{\kappa^{2}\,\|\bm{H}_{\bm{y}\bm{y}}\ \|_{2}}\geq\alpha^{2}\frac{c_{\text{lin}}}{\kappa^{2}}

Empirically,
\alpha=\|\bm{W}\|_{2}
typically grows to tens or hundreds during training,
so
\alpha^{2}c_{\text{lin}}\gg\kappa^{2}
(recall
\kappa=\Theta(1)
w.h.p.). Hence:

\|\bm{H}^{\mathrm{lin}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}\|_{2}\gg\|\bm{H}^{\mathrm{sn}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}\|_{2}\qquad\text{(with high probability)}.

In summary, in non-pathological cases, the gradient Lipschitz constant of
\|\bm{H}^{\mathrm{lin}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}\|_{2}
is much larger than the gradient Lipschitz constant of
\|\bm{H}^{\mathrm{sn}}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}\|_{2}
.

This completes the proof of
Theorem 4.2
.

∎
