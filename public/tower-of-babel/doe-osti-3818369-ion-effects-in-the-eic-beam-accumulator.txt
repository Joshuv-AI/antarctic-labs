Introduction
The Electron-Ion Collider (EIC) is currently under development at Brookhaven National Laboratory. As part of the
EIC injector chain, the Beam Accumulator Ring (BAR), located between the 750 MeV linac and the 5–18 GeV Rapid
Cycling Synchrotron (RCS), plays a critical role in accumulating charge from multiple linac pulses to produce the
high-intensity polarized electron beam required for injection into the RCS. To meet the EIC specifications, BAR will
successively accumulate approximately 30 linac bunches of 1.1 nC each at a repetition rate of 30 Hz, achieving a
nominal single-bunch charge of 28 nC (with a design maximum of 35 nC to provide margin for beam losses during
acceleration and extraction). The high single-bunch charge required by the EIC presents one of the primary design
challenges for BAR. The interaction of such an intense beam with the residual gas can drive ion accumulation and
ion-driven instabilities. Although this effect is self-saturating and usually does not result in beam loss, a significant
blow-up of the transverse emittance can affect the injection efficiency into the RCS.

Numerical simulations of ion effects
For more accurate results, we carried out numerical multi-particle simulations using the ELEGANT code [7]. The
IONEFFECTS elements were inserted after each quadrupole magnet; the pressure profile was read from the data
generated by the SynRad/Molflow codes. The Poisson solver method was used for more accurate simulation of the
ion-induced fields; multiple ionization of trapped ions was taken into account. The electron beam was simulated by a
Gaussian distribution of 10000 macroparticles with the equilibrium bunch length, energy spread, and transverse
emittances. As a worst-case scenario, the single-bunch charge of 35 nC was assumed. Four cases with the integrated
beam current of 1 A·h, 100 A·h, 1000 A·h, and 10000 A·h without baking out of the vacuum chamber were analyzed.

Since the ion-driven instability results in the particle oscillation amplitudes exceeding the vacuum chamber aperture,
a significant particle loss is observed in the 1 A·h case corresponding to the early-stage commissioning. Although a
continuous increase of the vertical beam size is observed in the 1000 A·h case, a time of about 20-30 ms, with an
acceptable beam size, may be sufficient to rotate the longitudinal phase space by RF modulation and extract the beam.
No ion-driven instability is observed in the 10000 A·h case.

Conclusion
Ion accumulation is likely in the Beam Accumulator Ring, especially during machine commissioning and early
operation. The studies were done for the worst-case scenario of the accumulated beam before extraction, with a
maximum charge of 35 nC. Note that a few effects, such as ion neutralization and gradual accumulation of the electron
beam, were not taken into account. We hope the effect is reduced with the gradual accumulation, not considered in
these studies.
We expect the ion effects to subside after the integrated beam current exceeds 1000 Ah (about a year of continuous
operation with a 1 Hz repetition rate). Note that the NSLS VUV ring reached the residual gas pressure equilibrium
after 1500 Ah, with no significant improvement in pressure or lifetime after that [1,8,9].
Further increasing the pumping efficiency would be incremental at best due to the space constraints of the lattice
design. The Synrad simulations were done with Electron-Stimulated Desorption and scattering included, so the results
are conservative as well. The simulations also show that adding distributed pumping inboard of the beam channel is
not very effective and not worth the added complexity.

 8

The ion-cleaning electrodes are included in the design to suppress ion accumulation and prevent beam instability. We
also plan on pre-baking all vacuum chambers and backfilling with nitrogen, which has been shown to reduce
commissioning time.
