A skin-deep mystery in a patient with chronic granulomatous disease
Authors: Sarah A. Commaroto, Laurie A. Temiz, Wei-Shen Chen, Lilia Correa-Selm
Published: 2026

Introduction

Chronic granulomatous disease (CGD) is a rare inherited immunodeficiency caused by mutations in genes encoding the phagocyte NADPH oxidase complex. This enzyme deficiency impairs the production of reactive oxygen species and leads to recurrent bacterial and fungal infections with granuloma formation.1 The most common infections in patients with CGD are caused by Staphylococcus aureus, Nocardia species, and Aspergillus species.1 Although prophylactic therapy has increased overall survival and reduced the frequency and severity of infections, CGD patients continue to experience 1 severe infection every 3 to 4 years.2 Here, we report a rare case of disseminated blastomycosis with cutaneous involvement in a patient with CGD.

Case report

Our patient is a 65-year-old male who was diagnosed with CGD at age 12. The patient was taking prophylactic Bactrim (800-160 mg/d), azithromycin (500 mg/d), and itraconazole (100 mg 2×/d) for his CGD. He initially presented to an outside clinic with a 1 to 2 cm well-circumscribed hyperkeratotic, almost verrucous red-purple plaque with peripheral erythema and scaling on his left lateral lower leg (Fig 1, A). The lesion was asymptomatic but had grown gradually over the past 3 years. Shave biopsy revealed infectious granulomatous dermatitis. Despite negative tissue Gram-, PASF, AFB, and Fite special stains, an infectious etiology was favored. The patient returned for follow-up, and a punch biopsy was obtained from the previously sampled plaque. Biopsy result was a CGD cutaneous reaction. PCR testing (limited fungal coverage) and pathergy test were negative. At this time, differential diagnoses included infectious etiologies (including Leishmania, Mycoplasma, Blastomyces, and Histoplasma), cutaneous CGD, granulomatous pyoderma gangrenosum and squamous cell carcinoma.

The patient was seen 5 months later in our department by a different physician. The left leg plaque had become increasingly painful, scaly, and enlarged (Fig 1, B). The patient was prescribed ketoconazole 2% cream and re-evaluated 2 weeks later, during which time he was prescribed mupirocin ointment and received an intralesional Kenalog (ILK) injection (3 mg/2.8 mL), for suspected granulomatous pyoderma gangrenosum.

The patient was then referred to our team. The left lower leg plaque was unresponsive to the ketoconazole 2% cream and mupirocin ointment and worsened following the ILK injection (Fig 1, C). Three days after the ILK injection and 1 week after starting montelukast, he developed a progressive pruritic and painful eruption on the abdomen, back, groin, and bilateral upper thighs (Fig 2). Patient denied any new exposures or recent travel. HSV1/2 DNA and Varicella Zoster Virus swabs were negative. A punch biopsy was taken of the left lower abdomen, and triamcinolone 0.1% ointment was prescribed. Pathologists favored a cutaneous manifestation of CGD, provided an infectious etiology was sufficiently excluded.Fig 2Scattered, scaly, erythematous plaques with overlying pustules on the abdomen.

The patient was started on apremilast 1 week later by his internist, favoring generalized pustular psoriasis triggered by the ILK injection. The eruption on the abdomen and thighs improved minimally with triamcinolone 0.1% ointment but nearly resolved with apremilast.

Two months later, the patient presented to an outside clinic and underwent a punch biopsy of the left lower leg plaque, which had grown from 15 mm to 55 × 50 mm since his initial visit (Fig 1, D). Histopathologic evaluation (Fig 3, A-C), Grocott’s methenamine silver (GMS) and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS-F) staining (Fig 3, D) demonstrated findings concerning for blastomycosis or cryptococcus. Repeat PCR testing was negative; however, the panel was not a comprehensive pan-fungal assay due to outside clinic limitations. At this visit, the patient’s eruption on his abdomen, back and forearms had recurred after the near-complete resolution with apremilast.

The patient was then referred to infectious disease (ID). Computed tomography (CT) of his chest revealed reticulonodular and alveolar disease, along with infectious/inflammatory right upper lobe bronchitis. Despite negative urine Blastomyces antigen and serum antibody testing, ID made a presumptive diagnosis of disseminated blastomycosis given his clinical presentation, CT results, and biopsy findings. Further confirmatory testing was not achievable due to clinical urgency.

The patient was admitted to receive a 2-week course of IV Amphotericin B (475 mg/d). Urine blastomycosis antigen and serum antibodies were negative. A subsequent CT chest showed improvement of diffuse bronchial wall thickening and patch ground glass opacities. A PICC line was placed, and the patient was discharged home to continue IV therapy.

Seven days after therapy initiation, the patient returned to the hospital due to acute kidney injury secondary to amphotericin B use without proper IV fluid hydration. He completed the remainder of his 14-day therapy and was discharged with itraconazole (200 mg 2×/d, increased from prior dose of 100 mg 2×/d) and resumed Bactrim prophylaxis.

Discussion

Blastomycosis is typically diagnosed through a combination of histopathology, culture, and adjunctive testing. Fungal culture remains the diagnostic gold standard, with reported sensitivity of approximately 85% to 92% in pulmonary disease; however, results may take 1 to 4 weeks, and yield is significantly reduced following antifungal exposure.3 In this case, fungal culture from a skin biopsy obtained prior to amphotericin initiation was negative, although chronic itraconazole suppression may have reduced yield. Fungal PCR offers high specificity and rapid turnaround (24-48 hours); however, sensitivity in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue is variable due to DNA degradation.3 Importantly, the PCR assay performed in this case utilized a limited multiplex panel that did not include endemic fungi such as Blastomyces, representing a key diagnostic limitation. Definitive species identification requires DNA probe (AccuProbe), matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry, or sequencing of the isolate, none of which were performed due to clinical limitations.3

Initial biopsies were negative, likely due to the shave technique limiting sampling depth and low organism burden early in disease. While final histopathologic evaluation demonstrated fungal organisms on GMS and PAS-F staining with morphology consistent with Blastomyces, this represents a presumptive rather than definitive diagnosis. GMS is a pan-fungal stain and does not provide species-level identification. Morphologic mimics, such as Cryptococcus, were considered, however they were excluded based on histopathologic features in conjunction with the broader clinical picture. Urine Blastomyces antigen testing (MVista enzyme immunoassay (EIA)), demonstrates high sensitivity in disseminated disease (∼92% to 93%) but lower sensitivity in isolated pulmonary infection (∼79%), with specificity limited by cross-reactivity with Histoplasma and other endemic fungi.3 In our patient, both urine antigen and serum antibody testing were negative, highlighting the limitations of assays in certain clinical contexts. Collectively, these findings underscore that negative culture, serology, and limited PCR do not exclude blastomycosis, particularly in immunocompromised patients receiving chronic antifungal therapy.

Clinically, blastomycosis presents with nonspecific flu-like symptoms, and over 50% of infections remain undiagnosed due to asymptomatic presentation.4 Cutaneous blastomycosis is among the most common extrapulmonary manifestations, occurring in up to 40% to 80% of disseminated cases.3 Patients often present with papulopustules or well-demarcated verrucous plaques with scale-crust, peripheral pustules, and central ulceration. Given its nonspecific clinical presentation, blastomycosis is often overlooked unless patients exhibit persistent skin lesions, fail to respond to antibacterial therapy, or have known risk factors for exposure.

Patients with CGD are predisposed to invasive fungal infections, most commonly Aspergillus fumigatus and A. nidulans.5 While cutaneous fungal infections are common in CGD, infections due to endemic dimorphic fungi such as Blastomyces are not well characterized and may be underrecognized. The pathogenesis of blastomycosis in CGD can be understood through several immunologic defects. Loss of NADPH oxidase activity impairs reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, eliminating a critical mechanism of intracellular fungal killing.2 Blastomyces yeast can survive within macrophages, and its virulence factor BAD1 suppresses TNF-α production and T-cell activation, further impairing host defense.6 Granuloma formation in CGD is functionally deficient, allowing persistence of viable organisms. Additionally, impaired neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation and attenuated Th1/Th17 responses further compromise antifungal immunity.7 Together, these defects create a permissive environment for intracellular survival of dimorphic fungi.

This patient developed disseminated blastomycosis despite itraconazole suppressive therapy. Several mechanisms may explain this breakthrough infection. First, itraconazole exhibits highly variable oral bioavailability, particularly in capsule form, which requires an acidic gastric environment for absorption.8 Therapeutic drug monitoring was not performed in this case, and subtherapeutic levels cannot be excluded. Second, itraconazole is fungistatic rather than fungicidal against Blastomyces. In immunocompetent hosts, antifungal suppression is complemented by intact immune-mediated killing; however, in CGD, the absence of oxidative killing eliminates this synergy, allowing persistence of viable organisms. Third, suppressive therapy does not prevent new infection following environmental exposure. Although no clear exposure was identified, this remains a plausible contributing factor. Fourth, antifungal resistance, while rare, has been reported following prolonged azole exposure. Antifungal susceptibility testing was not performed and represents an additional limitation. The observed clinical improvement following escalation to itraconazole 200 mg twice daily suggests that prior dosing (100 mg twice daily) may have been subtherapeutic.

This case has several limitations. Diagnostic evaluation was constrained by fragmented care between our academic center and outside community-based facilities, limiting early access to high-yield testing, including pan-fungal PCR and comprehensive fungal culture. Confirmatory diagnostic modalities, including Blastomyces-specific PCR, immunohistochemistry, antifungal susceptibility testing, and itraconazole therapeutic drug monitoring were not performed. These limitations collectively contributed to diagnostic uncertainty and delay. However, the overall clinical course, CT findings, histopathology, and response to amphotericin B supports a presumptive diagnosis of disseminated blastomycosis.

Conclusions

Overall, this case highlights a rare and previously unreported presentation of disseminated blastomycosis with cutaneous involvement in a patient with CGD. This case broadens the known spectrum of fungal infections in CGD beyond Aspergillus and Candida species and challenges prior assumptions that dimorphic fungi like B dermatitidis typically do not occur in this population. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for atypical fungal pathogens in CGD patients with persistent skin or pulmonary findings, even in the absence of positive serology or culture, to promote timely diagnosis and initiation of appropriate therapy.
