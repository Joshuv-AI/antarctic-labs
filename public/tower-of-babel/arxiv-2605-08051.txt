Inferring Asteroseismic Parameters from Short Observations Using Deep Learning: Application to TESS and K2 Red Giants
Nipun Ghanghas, Siddharth Dhanpal, Shravan Hanasoge, Praneeth Netrapalli, Karthikeyan Shanmugam

Abstract
Asteroseismology is the study of resonant oscillations of stars to infer their internal structure and dynamics. It is also a powerful tool for precisely determining stellar parameters such as mass, radius, surface gravity, and age. The ongoing TESS mission, with its nearly complete sky coverage, presents a unique opportunity to uniformly probe stellar populations across the Milky Way. TESS is estimated to have observed more than 300,000 oscillating red giants, most of which have one to two months of observations. Given the scale of this dataset, we need a fast, efficient, and robust way to analyse the data. In this work, our objective is to develop a machine learning (ML) based method to infer asteroseismic parameters from short-duration observations. Specifically, we focus on two global seismic parameters, the large frequency separation ( Δ ​ ν \Delta\nu ) and the frequency at maximum power ( ν max \nu_{\mathrm{max}} ), from one-month-long TESS observations of red giants. Meanwhile, for K2 data, our focus extends to inferring the period spacings of dipolar gravity modes ( Δ ​ Π 1 \Delta\Pi_{1} ), in addition to Δ ​ ν \Delta\nu and ν max \nu_{\mathrm{max}} . Our findings demonstrate that our machine learning algorithm can accurately infer Δ ​ ν \Delta\nu and ν max \nu_{\mathrm{max}} for approximately 50% of samples created by taking one-month Kepler and K2 observations. For TESS one sector data however, we recover reliable Δ ​ ν \Delta\nu for only about 23% of the stars. Additionally, we get reliable Δ ​ Π 1 \Delta\Pi_{1} inferences for about 200 young red-giants from K2. For these Δ ​ Π 1 \Delta\Pi_{1} inferences, we see a good match with the well known Δ ​ ν − Δ ​ Π 1 \Delta\nu-\Delta\Pi_{1} degenerate sequence observed in Kepler red-giants.

II
Data

II.1
TESS data

We use light-curves detrended using the co-trending basis vector (CBV) correction method implemented in the TASOC (TESS Asteroseismic Science Operations Center) pipeline developed by the coordinated activity TESS Data for Asteroseismology (T’DA) within the TESS Asteroseismic Science Consortium (TASC)
(
28
;
51
)
. We cross-matched the red giants identified by
34
and used light-curves recorded at a cadence of 30 minutes for all the available stars. Instead of raw QLP data, which was used by
34
, we use the detrended data from TASOC pipeline because it is optimized for asteroseismology and preserves the stellar oscillation signal better that QLP, which is optimized for planet searches and removes stellar variability over long timescales while performing corrections. These detrended lightcurves from TASOC pipeline are useful for inferring
\Delta\nu
from one-month TESS observations. We perform sigma clipping to reject flux values with more than 5-
\sigma
discrepancy. Following a similar procedure to
86
, we fill all gaps smaller than 1.5 hours in length using linear interpolation to minimize the impact of windowing
23
. Finally, we reject all the stars with greater than 30% empty timestamps at this stage.
We use the lightkurve package
(
49
)
to download light curves hosted at Mikulski Archive for Space Telescopes (MAST) and compute Lomb-Scargle periodograms
(
50
;
79
;
72
)
. To make a uniform size sample, we use the same frequency range to calculate the power spectral density (PSD) for all the stars, which is to be fed into the neural network for analysis.
In total, we have 30,720 TESS red-giants for seismic analysis.

II.2
K2 data

We use detrended lightcurves form the
K2SFF
pipeline
(
90
)
which performs corrections for trends related to spacecraft pointing and other systematics. We take the long cadence (
\sim
30 minutes) data for red-giant stars listed in K2 GAP DR3
(
94
)
and high-pass filter each lightcurve with a boxcar filter of width 4 days. Next, we perform sigma clipping to reject flux values with more than 5-
\sigma
discrepancy. Finally, we fill gaps shorter than 1.5 hours using linear interpolation similar to TESS data (
II.1
).
Again, we use the lightkurve package to download light-curves from MAST and compute Lomb-Scargle periodograms for all these stars with the same frequency range.
In total, we have 18,560 K2 red-giants with
\Delta\nu
in the range
1-19~\mu\rm Hz
, out of which 2,176 lie within
\Delta\nu
range of
9-19~\mu\rm Hz
.

II.3
Training sets

II.3.1
For TESS Model

We combine red giant datasets from the Kepler and K2 missions observations to train a model designed for application to one-month TESS observations; we hereafter refer to this as the
TESS-model
. For K2, we use data from K2 GAP DR3
(
94
)
, which provided asteroseismic measurements of
\Delta\nu
and
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
for 19,417 red giants. For Kepler, we use observations of 21,144 red giants, identified by
(
17
)
, who have also provided measurements of
\Delta\nu
and
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
for these stars.
To create training samples comparable in resolution to TESS 1-sector data, we divided each 4-year (in case of Kepler) and 3-month (in case of K2) light-curve into multiple, non-overlapping, 27-day segments. Due to the stochastic excitation of oscillations, each of these shorter-duration lightcurves has a unique noise profile. This stochastic behaviour may also lead to some of these shorter light-curves not showing solar-like oscillations.

Again, we follow the same procedure as described in
II.1
, filling gaps smaller than 1.5 hrs in duration using linear interpolation and discarding samples having more than 30% empty data points at the end.
Following this, we have 822,274 samples in total, 768,267 from Kepler and 53,980 from K2. We use 70% of these data for training, 15% for validation and the remaining 15% as a test set.

The decision to use observations rather than synthetic data for training is motivated by the well-established links between granulation timescales, oscillation amplitudes, and the location of oscillation power within the PSD
(
43
;
39
;
45
;
56
;
12
;
92
)
. This was also emphasized by
37
, who leveraged binned 2D images of the PSD to detect oscillations and estimate
\nu_{\text{max}}
. Incorporating these empirical relationships can potentially help the neural network better identify oscillation signatures, especially in short-duration datasets like TESS, where poor frequency resolution and low signal-to-noise ratios pose additional challenges.
We also experimented with training a model on synthetic data, but it significantly underperformed compared to the model trained on observations when evaluated on the Kepler-as-TESS test set. This highlights key differences between synthetic and real data, particularly at low resolution and SNR.
However, training on observations carries the risk of the model internalizing
\Delta\nu
–
\nu_{\text{max}}
,
\tau_{\mathrm{conv}}
–
\nu_{\text{max}}
, and amplitude-scaling relations that may not hold perfectly in the TESS sample. However, as shown by
87
, the overall offsets between Kepler and TESS measurements of
\nu_{\max}
and
\Delta\nu
are small when averaged over the population. Nevertheless, there are mild evolutionary-state–dependent trends, with TESS
\nu_{\max}
values being typically 2–3% lower for red clump stars near
\nu_{\max}\sim 40,\mu
Hz, and indications of a small positive bias at high
\nu_{\max}
for red-giant–branch stars. However, the training set remains broadly representative for our purposes, apart from instrument-specific effects such as Earthshine and other systematics.

III
Methods

We train deep neural networks to infer seismic parameters directly from PSD profiles—using one-month TESS observations for the
TESS Model
, and three-month K2 observations for
K2 Model-1
and
Model-2
, collectively referred to as the
K2 Models
(see Section
III.4
for details). Inputs to all the models are 1-D arrays of normalized power from the PSDs, without any background correction or feature extraction, and outputs are probability distributions for each parameter (see section
III.1
). All the relevant features are learned by the neural networks on their own from the full PSD.
Although the
TESS Model
and
K2 Models
differ in terms of kernel sizes and number of output parameters, the overall architecture is the same for all the models (see section
III.2
).

III.1
Converting regression problem to classification

Identifying parameters corresponding to the PSD of a given star constitutes a regression problem. However, in our approach, we divided the continuous range of each parameter into a fixed number of bins and trained the model to classify the correct bin number based on the given PSD, effectively recasting the regression task as a classification problem. The model then outputs an array of probabilities, representing the likelihood that each parameter falls into a specific bin.

There are several scientific and practical reasons for this choice. First, classification can improve predictive accuracy, particularly when the relationship between inputs and outputs is highly non-linear
(
88
)
or when the output parameters span many orders of magnitude (as is often the case in asteroseismology). Second, classification inherently respects physical constraints — for example, it avoids predicting unphysical negative values for quantities like
\Delta\nu
or
\Delta\Pi_{1}
, which sometimes arise in unconstrained regression models. Lastly, but most importantly, formulating the inference as a classification problem allows us to recover a probabilistic distribution over the parameter space, giving us access not just to a point prediction but to a meaningful measure of the uncertainty and possible multimodality in the inferred values.

As established by
75
, neural network classifiers trained with one-hot encoded target vectors and a cross-entropy loss function yield outputs that estimate Bayesian posterior probabilities. In this context, the output probability distribution over the bins can be interpreted as a quantized approximation to the posterior distribution of the parameter, conditioned on the observed data and the learned model. To validate this capability of the ML models we have shown a comparison of output probabilities from these models with the posteriors obtained using MCMC for a Kepler red giant at K2 resolution (
\sim 88
days observations) in section
V.1
.

While ML output represents a Bayesian posterior, it is not the same posterior that can be obtained using methods such as Markov-Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). The uncertainties derived from the present distribution are systematically limited due to the finite sizes of the bins, number of training samples used and network complexity. Consequently, MCMC fits could generally provide more accurate values and more precise uncertainties for these stars.

Nonetheless, this method effectively provides an empirical distribution over the parameter range, from which we can extract not only the most probable value (the mode) but also meaningful uncertainty estimates (which are reasonably well calibrated– as reflected by the empirical 1
\sigma
fractions close to the ideal value of 0.683 in subsequent sections). This offers a significant advantage over standard regression based neural networks, which typically do not provide any probability distribution directly. Although techniques like k-fold cross-validation can be employed to estimate uncertainties, this approach requires training multiple instances of the model, substantially increasing training time. Furthermore, this approach often fails to fully capture complex multi-modal behavior, which presents a significant limitation in our specific application because the asteroseismic parameters, especially
\Delta\Pi_{1}
, can exhibit multimodal distributions. Alternatively, mixture density networks
(
10
;
33
)
can be used to obtain probability distributions, but these necessitate a predefined number of modes and are susceptible to mode collapse. Again, a comprehensive comparison of these different methods is beyond the scope of the present effort.

III.2
Model architecture

In our initial tests, we explored a range of neural network architectures, including a vanilla CNN (Convolutional Neural Network), CNN-LSTM (combining CNNs with Long Short-Term Memory layers), ResNet (Residual Networks), and Vision Transformers (ViT). These architectures were chosen to represent both traditional convolution-based models and more recent attention-based models, allowing us to assess their relative strengths for the task of inferring seismic parameters from photometric power spectra.

Among these, ResNet, which augments CNNs with skip (residual) connections between layers, consistently outperformed the other architectures in both validation loss and stability. We attribute this to ResNet’s ability to mitigate vanishing gradient problems and efficiently capture both local and global patterns in the PSD, which proved particularly useful given the multi-scale features present in the seismic signals. Based on this performance, we selected ResNet as the backbone architecture for both the
TESS Model
and the
K2 Models
, and further optimized it through hyperparameter tuning (see next section).

The final architecture of the adopted ResNet model is shown in Figure
2a
.

III.3
Fine-tuning model hyperparameters

To further optimize model performance, we tuned key hyperparameters such as kernel size, learning rate, number of ResNet blocks, and dropout rate.

Based on the results from a grid search, for the
TESS Model
, we used a smaller kernel size of 21 (compared to 51 for the
K2 Models
) which account for the lower frequency resolution in the one-month TESS datasets. This choice allows the model to focus on narrower spectral features without oversmoothing. We also found that a higher dropout rate of 0.4 was necessary for the
TESS Model
(compared to 0.1 for the
K2 Models
) to mitigate overfitting, due to significant limitations on training data available for TESS. Additionally, the number of ResNet blocks was tuned to balance model complexity against overfitting risk, leading to the selection of a slightly shallower configuration.

The learning rate and batch size were held constant across models (see Section
III.4
) after confirming that these settings provided stable convergence in all cases. Throughout this tuning process, the final hyperparameter choices were determined by the configuration that yielded the lowest validation loss while maintaining consistent performance across multiple training runs.

These design and tuning decisions were guided by empirical performance on the validation set and repeated testing, ensuring that the final models are robust, well-calibrated, and capable of generalizing effectively to unseen data.

III.4
Training

We feed the normalized power from the PSD as input to the model, along with the corresponding one-hot-encoded vectors (bin numbers) for the target output parameters (
\Delta\nu
and
\nu_{\rm max}
for the
TESS Model
;
\Delta\nu
,
\nu_{\rm max}
,
\Delta\Pi_{1}
, and
q
for the
K2 Models
). The models are trained as supervised classifiers with the objective of minimizing the categorical cross-entropy loss across all output parameters.

For optimization, we use the ADAM optimizer, which we selected for its robustness and efficiency in handling sparse gradients and adaptive learning rates. To improve convergence, we apply a cosine decay with restarts schedule on the learning rate, which periodically resets the learning rate to avoid local minima and encourage exploration during training.

Training is conducted on 2 GPUs, with an effective initial learning rate of
1.5\times 10^{-4}/2
(scaled by the number of GPUs) and a batch size of 64 per GPU, balancing memory constraints with stable gradient estimates. To ensure the model achieves optimal convergence while mitigating overfitting, we implement early stopping based on the validation loss. Training is halted if the validation loss fails to improve for five consecutive epochs (patience = 5), allowing the model sufficient opportunity to learn meaningful patterns without overtraining on noise or spurious correlations. Throughout training, the validation loss is continuously monitored, and the model state corresponding to the lowest observed validation loss is saved as a checkpoint. This checkpointed model—representing the best generalization performance on unseen data—is then used for inference on both the test dataset and subsequent application samples.

TESS Model

We trained the
TESS Model
using a one-month Kepler/K2-as-TESS dataset to predict
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
and
\Delta\nu

III.4.2
K2 Models

For K2 data, we trained two separate models, each tailored to different parameter ranges, but with identical architectures. Both models output
\nu_{\text{max}}
,
\Delta\nu
,
\Delta\Pi_{1}
, and

The bin sizes for all the

The need for different models arises from the difficulty of inferring
\Delta\Pi_{1}
from just three months of observations. While
18
demonstrated that all four parameters can be inferred from a single model using 4-year Kepler data, shorter observations pose significant challenges. In particular, measuring
\Delta\Pi_{1}
for evolved red giants and red-clump stars is complicated by the decreasing spacing between successive radial orders and increased granulation noise at low frequencies. Additionally, as stars evolve, mixed-mode inertia increases, reducing mode detectability
(
27
)
, and mixed mode density decreases with the onset of helium burning
(
25
)
, further complicating period spacing measurements.

Initially, we attempted to infer
\Delta\Pi_{1}
across the full
\Delta\nu
range of
1-19~\mu\mathrm{Hz}
, but useful results were obtained only for stars with
\Delta\nu>9~\mu\mathrm{Hz}
. This led to training a separate model specifically for the
9-19~\mu\mathrm{Hz}
range, which proved more effective in inferring
\Delta\Pi_{1}
within this domain. Therefore, all
\Delta\Pi_{1}
inferences presented here are derived exclusively from
K2 Model-2
, which targets young red giants.

IV
Results

In this section, we first compare the performance of all models on their respective test sets to evaluate and validate prediction accuracy. Subsequently, we apply the models to the target observational datasets—TESS data for the
TESS Model
and K2 data for the
K2 Models
—and present the results of reliable inferences in the form of tables, intended for prospective users.

For these tests, we report the mode of the output probability distributions as the predicted value for each parameter. We choose the mode because the predicted distributions can be broad and could also exhibit multimodal behaviour, particularly for
\Delta\Pi_{1}
. In such cases, the mode — as the most probable class — offers a more representative estimate than the mean or median, both of which can lie in low-probability regions or fall between distinct peaks. Moreover, due to the typically peaked nature of the softmax outputs in most cases, the mode and median often coincide.

To quantify uncertainty, we compute the difference between the values at the 16th and 84th percentiles relative to the 50th percentile (median) of the output distribution. The total uncertainty is then defined as the absolute sum of these two differences. This approach provides both a sharp point prediction (via the mode) and a meaningful measure of the spread and asymmetry in the predicted distribution (via the percentiles). Furthermore, to assess the calibration and reliability of the reported uncertainties, we use normalized residual, which is defined as:

\text{Normalized residual}=\begin{cases}\frac{\hat{y}-y}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\text{high}}^{2}+\sigma_{\text{ref}}^{2}}},&\text{if }\hat{y}<y\\
\frac{\hat{y}-y}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\text{low}}^{2}+\sigma_{\text{ref}}^{2}}},&,\text{if }\hat{y}\geq y,\end{cases}

where
\hat{y}
is the predicted value,
y
is the reference value,
\sigma_{\text{low}},\sigma_{\text{high}}
represent the lower and upper uncertainties on the prediction and
\sigma_{\text{ref}}
the uncertainty on reference value. This definition assumes that the uncertainties in the predictions and reference values are independent, which is a reasonable approximation here since they are derived from different methods, and the reference uncertainties are typically much smaller, being based on substantially longer observations. For a well calibrated model, the empirical 1
\sigma
fraction – defined as the fraction of stars with absolute normalized residuals less than 1 – should be approximately 0.683, corresponding to the 68.3% confidence interval for normally distributed errors. Values higher than 0.683 indicate that the reported uncertainties are too large (the model is under-confident), while values lower than 0.683 indicate that the uncertainties are underestimated (the model is over-confident).

Since our ML models generate predictions for all processed stars without prior vetting, we apply empirically defined uncertainty thresholds to filter and retain only the most reliable inferences. These thresholds vary across models and are determined based on test set performance, by evaluating how closely the model predictions align with reference values. We analyze how this agreement degrades with increasing uncertainty and select cutoff values—typically between 5% and 20%—that preserve predictive accuracy while minimizing the number of outliers.

For example, in the Kepler/K2-as-TESS test set, we find that using a
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
uncertainty threshold of 20% retains approximately 85% of the sample, for which 99.1% of predictions have relative errors below 20%. Reducing the threshold to 15% lowers the yield to 78%, while improving accuracy only marginally to 99.4%. Given this trade-off, we adopt the 20% threshold in this case.

Thus, the selected cuts strike a balance between maximizing the usable sample size and maintaining high confidence in the reported parameters.

For TESS

IV.1.1
Kepler/K2-as-TESS test set

We first validate the performance of the
TESS Model
using a test set composed of one-month segments from Kepler/K2 red-giant observations. This dataset was excluded from both training and validation, making it an unbiased benchmark. The set constitutes approximately 15% of the full Kepler/K2-as-TESS dataset, totalling 112,640 samples. However, we note that, the values reported by
17
for Kepler red giants are derived using a similar machine learning technique and it is desirable to compare the present inferences with classical methods. Hence, we make use of the
92
catalogue for Kepler giants, where the authors used the SYD pipeline to determine
\nu_{\text{max}}
and
\Delta\nu
and visually verified the results for approximately 16,000 red giant stars. For K2 red giants, we use values from K2 GAP DR3
(
94
)
, which combines results from multiple pipelines including A2Z
(
55
)
, BAM
(
95
)
, CAN
(
42
)
, COR
(
60
)
and SYD
(
41
)
. Of the 112,640 segment-based samples, we find only 84,480 in these two catalogues, which makes our final test set for this comparison.

To identify reliable inferences, we select stars with predicted
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
uncertainties below 20%, resulting in a filtered subset of 79,421 stars (about 94% of the total). Figure
3a
shows a comparison between model predictions and reference values from classical catalogues mentioned above, derived from time series that are 3 times loner for K2 and 48 times for Kepler (which make majority of the sample). Among the filtered stars, 78.4% agree to within uncertainties and 99.6% have relative errors in
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
below 20% (see fig
3b
), demonstrating that the
TESS Model
can accurately infer
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
from one-month Kepler/K2 observations. Furthermore, the empirical
1\sigma
fraction is 0.683, indicating that the
\nu_{\max}
uncertainties are perfectly calibrated for this reliable set.

To validate the
\Delta\nu
performance, we apply an additional uncertainty threshold: only stars with
\Delta\nu
uncertainties below 10% are retained. This further narrows the subset to 67,424 stars, which is roughly 79.8% of the original test set. In this subset, 84.5% of stars agree with the reference values within uncertainties, and 98.3% exhibit relative errors in
\Delta\nu
below 10% (see fig
4b
). The empirical
1\sigma
fraction for this set is 0.772, indicating that the reported
\Delta\nu
uncertainties are slightly overestimated on average, and therefore conservative. This confirms the model’s ability to recover
\Delta\nu
reliably from one-month observations within this more restricted subset and give realistic estimates of uncertainty. Figure
4a
also displays this comparison, with predictions color-coded by
confidence
, defined as the maximum value of the output probability distribution for a given parameter and star.

The increased scatter in fractional residuals for stars with
\Delta\nu\lesssim 5~\mu
Hz is expected: small misclassifications (e.g., by one or two bins) in this low-frequency regime lead to larger relative errors compared to the high-frequency regime. This effect is compounded by the reduced frequency spacing between radial modes at low
\Delta\nu
, where peaks lie closer together and convective background noise becomes more dominant. These challenges are exacerbated by the limited frequency resolution of one-month-long light curves.
The higher average confidence in this low-
\Delta\nu
regime is also explained by the stricter filtering imposed by the same 5% uncertainty threshold in the low-frequency regime, which retains only high-certainty cases in this range. Taken together, these two effects may give the visual impression that stars with higher prediction confidence have larger fractional residuals. However, statistical analysis reveals the opposite: the Pearson correlation between confidence and absolute fractional residual is -0.263 (p
<
0.0001), and the Spearman correlation is -0.365 (p
<
0.0001), indicating a statistically significant inverse relationship. However, the correlation is weak to moderate, suggesting that the relationship between confidence and absolute fractional residual might not be strictly linear. Nevertheless, it suggests that, on average, model predictions with higher confidence have lower absolute fractional residual.

Additionally, in appendix
C
we show that
\Delta\nu
values returned by the model are not simply derived based on the
\Delta\nu~-\nu_{\text{max}}
relation for solar-like oscillators
(
85
)
, which is inherent in the training sample made of Kepler and K2 red giants. In fact, the model is inferring
\Delta\nu
with much higher precision than is expected when relying on the
\Delta\nu-\nu_{\rm max}
scaling. We therefore argue that this is achieved using the underlying pattern of frequency spacing in the PSD. In order to explicitly test and verify that the model can indeed infer
\Delta\nu
without
\nu_{\rm max}
inputs, we train a
\Delta\nu
-only model. The predictions of this
\Delta\nu
-only model match with the values returned by
TESS Model
(which has both
\Delta\nu
and
\nu_{\rm max}
), see appendix
C
for comparison.

IV.1.2
TESS red giants

Now that we have validated the performance of the
TESS Model
on one-month segments of Kepler/K2 red-giant observations, we apply it to a new dataset comprising 30,720 red giants observed by TESS. These stars are drawn from the catalog by
34
, with data processed using the TASOC pipeline.

To identify reliable inferences from the
TESS Model
, we adopt the same selection criteria used previously—uncertainties less than 20% in
\nu_{\rm max}
and less than 10% in
\Delta\nu
. To independently assess the robustness of our
\nu_{\rm max}
predictions from TESS observations, we compare them with the values reported by
34
. Even without applying any quality cuts, 77.5% of the predictions agree within the uncertainty bounds of their published values.

Applying the
\nu_{\rm max}
quality cut yields reliable inferences for 17,374 stars (approximately 55% of the original set). Among these, 90% exhibit relative differences within 20%, and 86% fall within the combined uncertainty range. The empirical
1\sigma
fraction for this subset is 0.79, indicating that the uncertainties are slightly overestimated on average and are therefore conservative and safe to use. Furthermore, fewer than only 1% of stars show discrepancies greater than 35% when compared to the
34
catalog.

Figure
5a
shows the density plot comparing the two sets of
\nu_{\rm max}
values. While most predictions lie along the 1:1 diagonal line, a small feature slightly offset from the diagonal due to these
\sim
1% outliers is noticeable. Several factors could contribute to this deviation: (i)
34
employ a different machine learning approach that operates on binned 128
\times
128 two-dimensional images of the power spectrum density (PSD), in contrast to our method, which uses the full one-dimensional power spectrum as input to a 1D convolutional neural network; (ii) the data originate from different pipelines (QLP vs. TASOC); and (iii) they rely on raw, uncorrected light curves, whereas our analysis uses detrended time series. These distinctions can lead to systematic offsets in the derived seismic parameters.

Figure
5b
presents the histogram of relative differences in
\nu_{\rm max}
for the selected 17,374 stars. The majority fall below the 20% error threshold, with the long tail corresponding mostly to stars affected by systematic artifacts in the power spectrum. A small number of cases show incorrect predictions from our model—where noise features were mistakenly identified as
\nu_{\rm max}
—and vice versa for the reference values.

For
\Delta\nu
, we apply the same reliability threshold of 10% uncertainty, which in our earlier validation tests was shown to yield relative errors under 10% for 98.3% of the selected stars from one-month-long Kepler/K2 test set. This results in a high-confidence subset of 7,001 stars (
\sim
23% of the original set) for which we report confident
\Delta\nu
estimates from one-sector TESS data.

The final set of reliable
\nu_{\rm max}
and
\Delta\nu
predictions is listed in Table
2
. We additionally flag stars where our
\nu_{\rm max}
estimate differs by more than 35% from
34
using a binary
mismatch
indicator. We caution users against relying on these mismatched values, as such discrepancies are often associated with spurious peaks caused by systematics in the PSD. For instance,
84
report that, in QLP data, faint stars often show spurious power excesses at harmonics of one cycle per day (11.57
\mu
Hz), attributed to incomplete correction of Earthshine effects
(
32
)
.

Note the significant difference in the yield of reliable inferences between the Kepler/K2-as-TESS test set and TESS, particularly for
\Delta\nu
, 80 % vs 23 %. This lower yield for TESS red giants has also been noted in previous studies; for example,
87
reported that
\Delta\nu
could be measured for only about 14% of stars with one sector of data, even among bright red giants (limited to those with Kepler magnitude
<
13). One possible explanation for this discrepancy is the lower oscillation amplitude observed for the same stars in TESS compared to Kepler, due to TESS’s redder passband. Theoretically, the oscillation amplitude in TESS is expected to be about 0.68 times that in Kepler
(
53
)
, a prediction recently confirmed by
84
through a direct comparison of TESS and Kepler observations. Additionally, the overall photometric quality of TESS is lower than that of Kepler, with reduced precision and greater susceptibility to crowding effects
(
89
)
. Furthermore, spacecraft systematics unique to TESS, which are absent in Kepler lightcurves and therefore not reflected in the training dataset could also increase the mismatch. Additionally, helium-core-burning red clump (RC) stars dominate TESS seismic detections
(
34
;
87
)
, with
\nu_{\text{max}}
in the 30-40
\mu
Hz range. Since extracting
\Delta\nu
from RC stars is particularly challenging, this may also contribute to the lower yield.

Comparison with K2 GAP DR3

To validate the performance of
K2 Model-1
, which is trained to infer

and

across the full

range of 1–19

(

. To identify reliable inferences, we adopt quality thresholds of less than 20% uncertainty in
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
and less than 10% in
\Delta\nu

Out of 18,560 K2 GAP DR3 stars, 17,133 (91%) meet the reliability criterion for
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
, and 14,809 (80%) additionally satisfy the
\Delta\nu
threshold. The empirical
1\sigma
fractions are 0.554 for
\nu_{\max}
and 0.632 for
\Delta\nu
in their respective reliable samples, indicating that the uncertainties are slightly under-calibrated, particularly for

. This is reflected in the normalized residuals for

94
(1.017 for RGB and 1.008 for RC) to obtain approximate pre-scaled
\nu_{\max}
values. As this correction is only approximate—since the original scaling was pipeline- and star-dependent—residual differences can propagate into the normalized residuals and may explain the observed offset. However, the relative-error histograms in Figures
6c
and
6d
do not show a significant systematic shift, indicating that the predictive values themselves are not strongly biased, and that the effect is primarily associated with uncertainty calibration rather than the inferred
\nu_{\max}
values.

Figures
6a
and
6b
show the comparisons between the model-inferred
\nu_{\max}
and
\Delta\nu
values and those from K2 GAP DR3, using the approximately unscaled
\nu_{\max}
values to enable a more direct, like-for-like comparison. Despite some scatter, the majority of predictions fall within 20% relative error for
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
(92%) and 10% for
\Delta\nu
(95%). The remaining off-diagonal scatter is minimal, with only 5% and 2.5% of stars exceeding a 35% relative error in
\nu_{\rm max}
and
\Delta\nu
, respectively.

Part of the residual discrepancies may be attributed to differences in the data processing pipelines—K2 GAP DR3 uses EVEREST light curves, while this work relies on K2SFF. A noticeable feature is a concentration of outliers around
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}\sim 50\ \mu
Hz and
\Delta\nu\sim 5\ \mu
Hz, which aligns with known systematics in K2 data caused by spacecraft thruster firings. These firings, occurring approximately every 6 hours, introduce spurious peaks near harmonics of 47.19
\mu
Hz in the power spectrum, occasionally leading to incorrect parameter identification (see the K2 Data Handbook for details).

Overall, these results demonstrate that
K2 Model-1
can reliably infer
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
and
\Delta\nu
from three-month-long K2 light curves, provided appropriate uncertainty-based filtering is applied.

.

As also noted previously, the short observational baseline of three months inherently limits the reliability of
\Delta\Pi_{1}
estimates for many stars, since only a small number of dipole mixed modes are typically detectable—even for targets with relatively high signal-to-noise ratios.

To isolate reliable predictions, we apply quality cuts based on fractional uncertainties: specifically, we retain stars with
\Delta\Pi_{1}
uncertainties below 10% and
q
uncertainties below 0.12. We further exclude predictions in the lowest and highest
\Delta\Pi_{1}
bins, as these often reflect boundary effects—cases where the model assigns values at the edges of the training range, thus being limited by the imposed bounds rather than the data. Additionally, we discard stars with

, as predictions in this regime are particularly prone to failure.

This lower threshold on
q
is motivated by the findings of
18
, who showed that both machine learning and MCMC-based inferences become increasingly unreliable when
q
is very small. Furthermore,
69
demonstrated that when the spacing between gravity modes exceeds the width of the avoided crossing (which shrinks as
q
decreases), the mixed modes no longer adequately sample the stretching function. This results in poor constraints on the coupling strength, and might also reduce the reliability of
\Delta\Pi_{1}
estimates for such stars.

The uncertainty thresholds were determined empirically by testing model performance on a this test set of three-month Kepler observations. These cuts were optimized to balance sample size and reliability, ensuring that the final set of retained stars exhibits high prediction accuracy and a low fraction of unidentified outliers. This filtering is essential to extract meaningful
\Delta\Pi_{1}
inferences from three-month datasets with limited resolution. Full details on determining the
\Delta\Pi_{1}
selection threshold are presented in appendix
D
.

Out of the 59,904 samples (where multiple PSDs originate from different three-month segments of the same star), 13,297 have matches in
46
. These 13,297 samples correspond to 775 unique red-giant stars. Selecting at most one PSD per star and applying the quality cuts yields a final sample of 191 stars. Figure
7a
shows comparisons of
\Delta\Pi_{1}
for these 191 stars, with values inferred by the
K2 Model-2
from three-month segments of Kepler observations on the y-axis and values reported by
46
based on analyses of full four-year long Kepler observations on the x-axis. We observe a good agreement in the inferred
\Delta\Pi_{1}
values, except for 8 stars, which are showing significant discrepancies. Figure
7b
shows the histogram of relative errors for non-anomalous stars in this sample,
\sim

8
illustrates the
\Delta\nu
–
\Delta\Pi_{1}
distribution for these stars, where most follow the well-established degenerate sequence
(
91
;
15
)
. Furthermore, the empirical
1\sigma
fraction is 0.78 for this subset, which goes to show that the reported uncertainties are, on average, conservative and therefore reliable. The mean and median normalized residuals for this set are 0.043 and -0.031, respectively, indicating that there is no overall bias, and reinforcing the reliability of our
\Delta\Pi_{1}

V.1
MCMC and ML Comparison

To validate the capability of our ML models to approximate Bayesian posterior distributions, we compare the network outputs with posteriors obtained using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analysis for the Kepler red giant KIC 11912315, truncated to K2-like resolution (
\sim
88 days of observation). We employ the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm
(
57
;
30
)
to sample the posterior, assuming a likelihood based on
\chi^{2}
with 2 degrees of freedom, given the observed power spectral density (PSD) and a synthetic model
M(\nu,\Theta)
. This methodology follows the approach summarized in
18
, based on the formalism described in
7
. We refer the reader to these works for further details on the MCMC implementation.
Fig.
10
displays the best-fit MCMC model (red) overlaid on the smoothed data (black); the PSD obtained from the full 4-year Kepler observation is shown in gray for reference. Fig.
11
presents the comparison between the MCMC posteriors and the
K2 Model
outputs for
\nu_{\rm max}
,
\Delta\nu
,
\Delta\Pi_{1}
and
q
. To facilitate visual comparison, the MCMC posteriors have been normalized to match the peak probability of the ML outputs. We observe that the ML outputs and MCMC posteriors overlap and agree well within uncertainties for all parameters. Notably, the ML output for
\nu_{\rm max}
exhibits a bimodal structure with peaks roughly separated by
\Delta\nu
. This suggests the model detects an ambiguity in the
\nu_{\rm max}
inference, identifying two different sets of modes for potential Gaussian envelope for the oscillation signal separated by one radial order. To better illustrate the model’s capability to approximate the underlying
\nu_{\rm max}
posteriors, Appendix
G
presents direct MCMC comparisons for six additional stars.

Beyond the
\nu_{\rm max}
multi-modality, the ML distribution for all parameters except
\Delta\nu
are broader than the MCMC posterior. This difference in posterior width is not unexpected. The MCMC algorithm uses significant computational resources (thousands of steps) to map the likelihood surface for a given target, allowing for a highly optimized exploration of the parameter space. In contrast, the ML model provides an instantaneous, generalized inference. The exact difference between the posteriors depends on many factors such as bin sizes for models, training set size, network complexities, and number of steps in MCMC and its convergence criteria. Therefore, the systematically broader ML distributions are a natural consequence of these methodological differences, and the ML uncertainties may be interpreted as being more conservative. A detailed comparison disentangling these effects is beyond the scope of the current work.

V.2
Bin Sizes and Uncertainties

While the probabilistic outputs of our neural networks are interpreted as Bayesian posteriors following
75
, we note an important theoretical nuance regarding the choice of bin sizes explored during our model optimization. Fundamentally, there exists a trade-off in bin sizing between strict theoretical correctness and empirical precision.

For bin convergence exercises (appendix
B
), we utilized one-hot encoding across all models. While this approach is straightforward for models with larger bin sizes, utilizing one-hot encoding to describe the PSD parameters at the finest bin sizes presents a theoretical challenge. This issue arises because the intrinsic noise in the PSD limits the precision achievable by an ideal theoretical sampler, effectively broadening the posterior. Consequently, the “ideal” theoretical bin size would need to vary dynamically from target to target depending on the local signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). However, fixing a conservatively large bin size globally to accommodate the lowest-SNR targets would be counterproductive, as it would artificially degrade the predictive resolution for the high-SNR targets that are of primary scientific interest.

To assess the extent of this theoretical limitation across our datasets, we note the approximate intrinsic precision limits dictated by the observational baselines. For 3-month K2 data, these theoretical limits are roughly 0.68
\mu
Hz for
\nu_{\rm max}
, 0.04
\mu
Hz for
\Delta\nu
, 0.1 seconds for
\Delta\Pi_{1}
, and 0.01 for
q
. For shorter 1-month TESS observations, the intrinsic noise floor is naturally higher, with precision limits of roughly 1.1
\mu
Hz for
\nu_{\rm max}
and 0.07
\mu
Hz for
\Delta\nu
. When utilizing bin sizes smaller than these thresholds for noise-dominated spectra, representing the target with a single one-hot bin strains the strict mathematical preconditions required for pure Bayesian interpretation.

To address this, we rely on tested machine learning practice and prioritize high-resolution precision. For models trained on synthetic data (e.g., the
K2 Models
), the true underlying parameters are known exactly. In such cases, while treating the target as a strict one-hot
\delta
-function is a formal theoretical approximation, empirical evidence across astrophysical machine learning demonstrates that prioritizing high-resolution mapping is a proven, effective strategy
(
81
;
71
;
78
, e.g.,)
. Rather than artificially widening the bins, the network implicitly learns the posterior width dynamically from the variance of the training ensemble. During training, the model encounters many similar noisy input spectra corresponding to slightly different exact ground-truth labels. This forces the global minimum to naturally distribute the softmax probability mass across multiple adjacent bins.

The empirical behavior of the network under this approximation is demonstrated in our convergence exercises (Appendix
B
; Tables
3
and
4
), where model performance metrics (e.g., normalized residuals, median uncertainties and 1-sigma fractions) transition smoothly without abrupt changes or qualitative degradation, even as bin sizes are reduced below the aforementioned intrinsic precision limits dictated by the noise. While this empirical robustness does not substitute for formal Bayesian correctness at extreme bin sizes sizes, it underscores the network’s predictive stability when pushed beyond its theoretical constraints.

Crucially, we emphasize that this theoretical limitation primarily affects the extreme lower bounds of our convergence exercises. As shown in Appendix
B
, the Median Squared Error (MdnSE) for all parameters — in both the
K2
and
TESS
models — converges at bin sizes that are safely
larger
than these intrinsic precision limits (see Figures
12
and
13
, and Tables
3
and
4
). As the network achieves optimal performance before reaching the average noise floor, the final asteroseismic parameters reported in this work are derived from models utilizing bin sizes that are larger than the intrinsic precision limits of the PSDs. Consequently, the outputs from our final models are not limited due to this theoretical training constraint and remain robustly interpretable as Bayesian posterior probabilities.

Additionally, for models trained directly on observational data (e.g., the
TESS Model
), the “ground truth” labels were derived from cataloged central values and implemented as one-hot encoded targets, effectively collapsing the known uncertainties of the reference catalogs into single discrete bins. Treating cataloged central values as exact training targets is a widely used practice in astrophysical machine learning
(
47
;
20
, e.g.,)
. Specifically within asteroseismology, deep neural networks have been successfully used in regression tasks for continuous parameters by utilizing central catalog values as exact ground-truth labels
(
37
;
34
, e.g.,)
. Thus, while framing this specific task as a discretized classification problem introduces an artificial source of symmetric label noise, deep neural networks are fundamentally mathematically robust to such noise when trained across large ensembles
(
77
)
.

Nevertheless, we acknowledge that this discretization inherently acts as an artificial source of label noise. Consequently, the network may struggle to infer posterior widths that are narrower than the intrinsic uncertainties present in the original training catalogs. Our training data contains heterogeneous precision; for instance, the subset from
17
is inherently resolution-limited to
0.1\,\mu
Hz in
\Delta\nu
and
2\,\mu
Hz in
\nu_{\rm max}
, whereas the K2 GAP DR3 catalog is not. However, as established during our model validation, the network optimizes its weights using the higher-precision K2 data to generalize beyond the limits of the coarser subsets. When benchmarked against high-precision test sets (Table
3
), the model achieves median absolute errors of
0.04\,\mu
Hz for
\Delta\nu
and
1.5\,\mu
Hz for
\nu_{\rm max}
. These predictive errors are significantly smaller than the resolution limits of the training subset used by
17
, confirming that the model successfully internalizes the highest available precision and is not bottlenecked by heterogeneous label quality.

Finally, in practical terms, this theoretical constraint is negligible for our specific application. Because the
TESS Model
is applied to short, one-month observations but was trained on significantly more precise values derived from longer-baseline data, the intrinsic observational noise of the 1-month TESS spectra naturally dominates over the variance floor inherited from the training labels.

VI
Summary and Conclusions

We have developed deep learning–based methods to infer key asteroseismic parameters from short-duration photometric observations. These models take normalized power spectral densities (PSDs) as input and output probability distributions for each parameter, learning relevant features directly from the data—without requiring background fitting or mode identification. Specifically, we target the inference of
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
and
\Delta\nu
from one-sector TESS-like observations, and
\Delta\Pi_{1}
, in addition to these two, from three-month K2 light curves.

The
TESS Model
is trained on one-month PSD segments derived from Kepler and K2 red giant light curves. For the
K2 Models
, we generate synthetic datasets to support training across a wide range of evolutionary stages. We validate each model on its respective test sets using observations and apply empirically derived quality-based selection criteria to isolate reliable predictions. In addition, we assess the robustness of the
K2 Models
on a separate synthetic test set using the same filtering strategy.

Across the various comparison sets, the empirical
1\sigma
fraction typically ranges from 0.63 to 0.80. This suggests that the reported uncertainties are, in most cases, conservatively estimated or reasonably well calibrated.

Appendix A
Generating synthetics

The detailed formulation for generating synthetic spectra can be found in
17
, which is implemented in the Spectra Simulator code developed by Dr. Othman Benomar
9
. However, while
17
assumes that all oscillation modes are resolved, we have modified the simulator to account for unresolved modes, improving its applicability to our target systems. Additionally, we adopt a different formalism for computing the heights and widths of mixed modes, following the approaches outlined in
27
and
62
. Furthermore, we are passing all the small spacing values separately, namely
d_{01},d_{02}~\&~d_{03}
, unlike
17
where these values are calculated internally as some fraction of a single parameter
d_{0,\ell}
. For the sake of continuity, we provide below a brief summary of the overall methodology for generating synthetic spectra, while also highlighting the key differences introduced in our implementation.

A.1
Mode frequencies

The asymptotic theory of oscillations of p modes in red giants may be expressed as
(
63
)
,

\frac{\nu_{n_{p},l}}{\Delta\nu}=n_{p}+\frac{l}{2}+\epsilon_{p}-d_{0l}(\Delta\nu)+\frac{\alpha_{l}}{2}\left[n_{p}-\frac{\nu_{\text{max}}}{\Delta\nu}\right]^{2},

(A1)

where
\Delta\nu
is large separation,
\nu_{\text{max}}
is the frequency at maximum power,
n_{p}
is the p-mode radial order,
\ell
is the angular degree,
\epsilon_{p}
is the phase offset,
d_{0\ell}
is the small frequency separation and
\alpha_{\ell}
term accounts for the curvature of p-modes.

We calculate
\nu_{\text{max}}
based on
\Delta\nu
using the relation for solar-like oscillators given in
85
as given below and add a 10% spread on top (see table
1
):

\nu_{\rm{max,scaled}}=(\Delta\nu/0.263)^{1/0.77}

(A2)

For
\delta_{02}~\&~\delta_{03}
we use the
\delta_{0\ell}-\Delta\nu
relation for Kepler red giants based on
40
and add a 20% spread on top (see table
1
):

\delta_{02,\rm{scaled}}=0.121~\Delta\nu+0.047

(A3)

\delta_{03,\rm{scaled}}=0.282~\Delta\nu+0.16

(A4)

Meanwhile for
\delta_{01}
we take uniform values from (-0.6 to 0.6) based on the same study. Note that we need to divide these values of small spacings by
\Delta\nu
to account for the difference in the definition of
\delta_{0\ell}
in
40
and in equation
A1
(see table
1
).

Mixed-mode frequencies for red giants are given by an implicit equation
(
65
)
,

\mathrm{tan}~\pi\frac{\nu-\nu_{p}}{\Delta\nu}=q~\mathrm{tan}\frac{\pi}{\Delta\Pi_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{\nu}-\frac{1}{\nu_{g}}\right),

(A5)

where
\nu_{g}
is the asymptotic frequency of pure
g
modes.
For dipole modes
\nu_{g}=1/(-n_{g}+\epsilon_{g})~\Delta\Pi_{1}
, where
n_{g}
is the radial order and
\epsilon_{g}
is the offset parameter for
g
modes.

We solve equation
A1
to determine frequencies of
\ell
=0, 2 and 3 modes, which are treated as pure pressure modes in our synthetics.
\ell
=2 and 3 are in principle mixed modes, however, as the coupling between g-mode and p-mode cavity for these modes is very weak, only the p-dominated modes are visible. To determine the frequencies of dipole mixed modes, we take solutions of the implicit equation
A5
in a range of 1.2 times
\Delta\nu
for each pure
\ell
= 1 p mode.

A.2
Rotational splittings

Rotation breaks the spherical symmetry of the star and lifts the degeneracy in
m
, splitting each mode of degree
\ell
into
2\ell+1
azimuthal components. The frequency of each of these components is given by
\nu_{n,\ell,m}=\nu_{n,\ell}+\delta\nu_{n,\ell}
, where
\delta\nu_{n,\ell}
is the rotational splitting. For p-modes in solar-like stars, the dependence of rotational splitting on
(n,\ell)
is weak within the observed frequency range
(
52
)
. Rotational splittings for p-modes may be approximated as,

\nu_{n,\ell,m}=\nu_{n,\ell}-m~\delta\nu_{n,\ell}~~,

(A6)

where
\delta\nu_{n,\ell}=\Omega/2\pi
is a function only of average internal rotation rate
\Omega
. Furthermore, owing to the large envelopes in red giants and the high sensitivity of p-modes rotational kernels to the outer layers of the star, it is common to approximate average rotation as
\Omega\simeq\Omega_{env}
and hence
\delta\nu_{n,l}\simeq\Omega_{env}/2\pi

(
26
)
.

Mixed modes on the other hand are influenced by both the core and the envelope. For
\ell=1
mixed modes in red giants and early subgiants, a two-zone model of rotation can be used to estimate the rotational splittings as shown by
26
. Moreover, they also demonstrate that the contribution from core and envelope to the rotational splitting depends upon the ratio
\zeta(\nu)
of kinetic energy of the modes in g-cavity and the total kinetic energy of modes as,

\delta\nu_{rot}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\Omega_{core}}{2\pi}\zeta(\nu)+\frac{\Omega_{env}}{2\pi}(1-\zeta(\nu)).

(A7)

Additionally,
\zeta(\nu)
is well approximated by the properties of pure p and g modes as
(
14
;
64
)
,

\zeta(\nu)=\left[1+\frac{1}{q}\frac{\nu^{2}\Delta\Pi_{1}}{\Delta\nu}\frac{cos^{2}\pi\frac{1}{\Delta\Pi_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{\nu}-\frac{1}{\nu_{g}}\right)}{cos^{2}\pi\frac{\nu-\nu_{p}}{\Delta\nu}}\right]^{-1}.

(A8)

However, this first-order description of rotation does not account for near-degeneracy effects (NDE), which arise when frequency separation between consecutive mixed modes becomes comparable or lower than the rotational splitting
16
. NDE leads to avoided crossings of each multiplet component at different age, causing asymmetries in the rotational splittings. There are two formalisms to account for these effects, one is based on decomposing mixed modes in the basis of pure pressure modes (
\pi
modes) and pure gravity modes (
\gamma
modes)
(
67
)
in which the rotational operator is diagonal
66
and one can solve the eigenvalue problem to get the frequencies
68
. Second approach as discussed in
48
applies rotational perturbations to the asymptotic frequencies of pure p- and g-modes and then solve equation
A5
separately for each multiplet. Furthermore, authors argue that this approach is similar to the one proposed by
68
and adequately accounts for NDE. Thus, for stars showing non-negligible NDE the first-order description of rotation is inadequate and could lead to potential biases in inferences of period spacings and coupling factor. However,
48
noted that they found consistent results for
\Delta\Pi_{1}
,
q
, and other parameters using these two approaches, with significant differences appearing only in the inference of the envelope rotation rate, which we do not attempt to infer here. We also compared the reliable non-anomalous
\Delta\Pi_{1}
inferences from our model for 997 stars in common with
48
. We obtain consistent results for 99.3% of these stars (see appendix
E
), which confirms that the first-order rotational formalism is generally sufficient to obtain accurate
\Delta\Pi_{1}
inferences, at least for stars within our reliable set (inferences satisfying quality thresholds).

Finally, we found the inclusion of this first-order rotational description to be very effective in comparison to not including any rotation at all, as is shown in appendix
E
.

A.3
Heights and Widths of modes

Each oscillation mode is modelled as a Lorentzian centred at
\nu(n,\ell,m)
with height
H(n,\ell,m)
and linewidth
\Gamma(n,\ell,m)
. To obtain realistic mode heights and widths for
\ell=0,2
and
3

p
modes, we adopt the heights and linewidths of radial modes derived from templates of red giants and sub-giants observed by Kepler (KIC 10147635, KIC 11414712, KIC 12508433, KIC 6144777, KIC 8026226, KIC 11026764, KIC 11771760, KIC 2437976 and KIC 6370489), rescaling them based on
\nu_{\text{max}}
and
\Delta\nu
, and applying the appropriate geometrical factors for the
\ell=2~\&~3
modes, following a technique similar to
44
(see also
17
for an example). For the dipole mixed modes, we compute amplitudes and linewidths by scaling the radial modes using the ratio of kinetic energy trapped in the g-mode cavity to the total mode kinetic energy,
\zeta(\nu)
. We use the formalism highlighted in
62
; specifically,
8
estimate the width of mixed modes as
\Gamma_{1}(\nu)=\Gamma_{0}(1-\zeta(\nu))
, and
6
have shown that the amplitude for resolved dipole mixed modes is
A_{1}^{2}(\nu)=A_{0}^{2}(1-\zeta(\nu))
. As noted in
62
, when the geometrical factor is omitted, these amplitudes correspond to similar heights for resolved dipolar mixed modes since
A^{2}=\pi\Gamma H/2
.

We treat any two modes with a frequency separation smaller than
\delta f_{\rm{res}}
as duplicates and retain only one of them in the final calculations, where
\delta f_{\rm{res}}
is the frequency resolution of the PSD, defined as the inverse of the light curve’s total observation time. Further, we set a minimum observable linewidth (
\Gamma_{min}=\frac{2~\delta f_{\rm{res}}}{\pi}
) for all the modes i.e
\Gamma_{n}=\rm{max(\Gamma_{n},\Gamma_{min})}
. For all unresolved modes, i.e., when
\Gamma_{n}
was less
\Gamma_{min}
, we also apply a dilution factor to the heights
(
19
)
, which expresses,

\tilde{H}_{n}=\frac{\pi}{2~\delta f_{res}}\Gamma_{n}~H_{n}

(A9)

where
H_{n}
and
\tilde{H}_{n}
is the mode height before and after applying the dilution factor. Such the total power of the mode is conserved.
Accounting for geometrical and visibility factor, the final heights
H(n,\ell,m)
of modes are determined as

H(n,\ell,m)=r^{2}_{\ell,m}(\iota)V(\ell)\tilde{H}_{n},

(A10)

where
V(\ell)
is the mode visibility,
\tilde{H}_{n}
is the height for the mode of radial order
n
(without geometrical and visibility factor) and
r^{2}_{\ell,m}(\iota)
is the geometrical factor which depends on the inclination angle
\iota
, determined according to

r^{2}_{\ell,m}(\iota)=\frac{(\ell-|m|)!}{(\ell+|m|)!}[P_{\ell}^{|m|}(cos\iota)]^{2},

(A11)

where
P_{\ell}^{|m|}
is the associated Legendre polynomial.

A.4
Noise Model

The noise model comprises two components, a Harvey-like profile generated by surface granulation and white noise which is frequency independent. At low frequencies, the granulation component dominates and at high frequencies, white noise is the primary contributor. We do not consider additional facular signatures in our synthetics.

The noise model is given by

B(\nu)=\frac{H_{g}}{1+(\tau\nu)^{\mathrm{p}}}+N_{0},

(A12)

where the first term is the Harvey-like component which depends on the characteristic granulation amplitude
H_{g}
, the granulation timescale
\tau
, and power-law exponent
p
. The second term,
N_{0}
, represents white noise.

Furthermore, granulation amplitude and timescale have been observed to vary with
\nu_{\text{max}}

(
43
;
56
;
12
)
. Hence, the granulation amplitude and timescale are modeled as,

H_{g}=A_{g}\nu_{\text{max}}^{B_{g}}~+~C_{g}~,~~\tau=A_{\tau}\nu_{\text{max}}^{B_{\tau}}~+~C_{\tau},

(A13)

where (
A_{g},B_{g},C_{g}
) and (
A_{\tau},B_{\tau},C_{\tau}
) are free parameters which control the granulation amplitude and timescale respectively.

A.5
Power Spectrum Model

The power spectrum model comprises two parts, the oscillation signal
S
and noise profile
B
. The oscillation signal is a sum of Lorentzians centred around the respective mode frequencies, given by,

S(\nu)=\sum_{n}\sum_{\ell=0}^{3}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}~\frac{H~(n,\ell,m)}{1+4\left(\frac{\nu-\nu_{n\ell m}}{\Gamma(n,\ell,m)}\right)^{2}}.

(A14)

Mode frequencies
\nu_{n\ell m}
, height
H(n,\ell,m)
and width
\Gamma(n,\ell,m)
of Lorentzian peaks are determined as explained previously.

For a given set of parameters, the the power spectrum model is calculated by adding signal and noise profile part as,

M(\nu)=S(\nu)~+~B(\nu).

(A15)

We then multiply a random realization of chi-squared noise with two degrees of freedom to the resulting model to obtain the synthetic PSD profile.

Appendix B
Choosing bin size

To determine the optimal bin sizes for our network architectures, we conducted convergence exercises by training multiple model instances with progressively finer bins. We compared performance across several diagnostics: Median Squared Error (MdnSE), empirical 1
\sigma
fraction, mean/median and standard deviation of normalized residuals, median predicted uncertainty, and the reliable fraction (the number of confident inferences). Together, these metrics capture the effects of binning resolution on model accuracy, precision, calibration, and outlier behavior. We utilized MdnSE rather than Mean Squared Error because it is significantly more robust to extreme outliers, thereby more accurately reflecting the model’s baseline performance across diverse bin configurations.

While strictly varying one parameter at a time across our entire multi-dimensional grid constitutes an ideal controlled experiment. Our convergence exercises primarily utilize a multi-variable sweep to efficiently map the parameter space without training approximately 60 separate models, supplemented by targeted single-parameter control models for most sensitive parameters (discussed below) to demonstrate empirical robustness of this approach.

Finally, as detailed in Section
V.2
, we note that utilizing strict one-hot encoding at finest bin sizes stretches the theoretical preconditions for interpreting model outputs when the bin width falls below the intrinsic noise limit of the data. We refer the reader to Section
V.2
for a comprehensive discussion on this trade-off between empirical precision and formal mathematical correctness.

Appendix C
Large-spacing inferences from TESS Model

Our training sample for the
TESS Model
is based on
\Delta\nu
and
\nu_{\text{max}}
measurements of red giants observed by Kepler and K2, as detailed in section
II.3.1
. Hence, this training set codifies the
\Delta\nu~-\nu_{\text{max}}
relation for solar-like oscillators
(
85
)
(
\Delta\nu\ \propto\nu_{\text{max}}^{0.77}
) enabling determination of
\Delta\nu
to 10-15% accuracy given
\nu_{\rm max}
. The machine-learning model could potentially leverage this and predict
\Delta\nu
based solely on this relation instead of identifying the pattern associated with the large spacings. To verify this, we create three distinct sets of synthetics with 10, 20 and 30 percent spread in
\nu_{\rm max}
on top of this relation, keeping other parameters the same as in table
1
(see appendix
A
for full details on generating synthetics). Based on the
\Delta\nu-\nu_{\rm max}
relation, 10, 20 and 30 percent spread in
\nu_{\rm max}
would correspond to 7.7, 15.4 and 23.4 percent spread in
\Delta\nu
, respectively. Figure
14
shows the density plot for true vs predicted
\Delta\nu
for reliable inferences on the synthetic set with 20 percent spread in
\nu_{\rm max}
and fig
15
shows the histogram of relative errors for reliable predictions for all three synthetic sets (using the same quality thresholds as described in section
IV.1
). The shaded regions highlight the
\Delta\nu
spread expected from simple scaling using the
\Delta\nu-\nu_{\rm max}
relation. Predictions based solely on this scaling relation would result in
\Delta\nu
values being uniformly distributed over the corresponding range. However, the relative-error values in figure
15
indicate
\Delta\nu
to be much more tightly constrained, peaking at zero, for all three sets. Based on this, we conclude that the
TESS Model
is not simply using the scaling relation to determine
\Delta\nu
- rather, it is predicting
\Delta\nu
based on the underlying frequency-separation pattern. However, we note that the yield for reliable inferences drops with increasing spread in
\Delta\nu-\nu_{\rm max}
values. This drop is likely due to increasing differences in the of training sets (based on Kepler/K2 one-month segments, where most of the stars have under 10% spread in
\Delta\nu-\nu_{\rm max}
) and synthetic test sets (with 10, 20 and 30% spread). Nonetheless, the accuracy and precision for the reliable inferences does not changing appreciably for synthetic sets with higher spreads (fig
15
).

Furthermore, to explicitly verify that the model can learn and predict
\Delta\nu
without
\nu_{\text{max}}
in the inputs, we trained a model with a single output,
\Delta\nu
. Training data for this model were the same as those used for the
TESS Model

. Figure

IV.1.1
for details). Nearly all inferences lie along the 1:1 line, which shows that the
TESS Model
can identify the large-spacings pattern and predicts
\Delta\nu
independently of
\nu_{\text{max}}

Appendix D
Period Spacings Selection Cut

To determine the optimal selection threshold for reliable
\Delta\Pi_{1}
inferences, we evaluate model performance on the Kepler-as-K2 test set for a range of threshold values. Specifically, we vary the threshold—defined as the maximum allowed percentage error in the model prediction—from 1–30% in steps of 1%, and assess the resulting model accuracy, coverage, and outlier behaviour. No cuts based on
q
or any other parameters are applied, ensuring that this analysis remains independent of other model outputs.

Here, coverage denotes the fraction of stars from the full test set that satisfy the selection cut; accuracy refers to the fraction of stars within this selected subset whose
\Delta\Pi_{1}
predictions agree with the reference values within 5%; and outliers represent those outside this 5% tolerance. We also track the anomalous fraction—the proportion of stars within each selected subset flagged as anomalous predictions based on the criteria defined in Section
IV.2.2
. The difference between the outlier and anomalous fractions therefore indicates the number of unidentified outliers that remain within the selected subset.
The optimal threshold should maximize coverage while maintaining high accuracy and a low fraction of unidentified outliers.
Figure
17
shows these quantities for the sample of 13,297 Kepler-as-K2 red-giant samples, made of 775 unique stars with
\Delta\Pi_{1}
values reported in
46
(see section
IV.2.2
for details on this test set). Based on this analysis, we adopt a conservative

Appendix E
Effectiveness of first-order rotation formalism

To verify the effectiveness of our currently implemented first-order rotation formalism (see appendix
A
), we created a synthetic dataset with core and envelope rotation rates set to zero, while keeping all other parameters ranges the same as in table
1
. We trained an instance of K2 Model, termed
K2 Model Norot
, on this dataset to compare its performance with a model trained on synthetics with rotation included. We use the Kepler-as-K2 test set (see section
IV.2.2
) to access model performance on observations directly. We evaluate model accuracy, coverage, outlier and anomalous fraction for this model as is done for
K2 Model
in appendix
D
to have a full comparison of model performance. Figure
17
shows these values for
K2 Model Norot
at different
\Delta\Pi_{1}
percentage error thresholds. In comparison to
K2 Model
, fig
17
, the accuracy for
K2 Model Norot
drops rapidly with increasing percentage error threshold and correspondingly the outlier fraction rises with the same rate. Furthermore, the number of unidentified outliers – which is the difference between outliers and anomalous fraction – is non-negligible even for stars with very low uncertainties and becomes very significant with increasing values of percentage error threshold. All the gains in coverage by
K2 Model Norot
are offset by the steep decline in accuracy.

The poor performance of
K2 Model Norot
compared to
K2 Model
could be attributed to differences between the synthetic dataset with no rotational splittings and observations. Thus, the implementation of a first-order description of rotation, even though it does not account for near-degeneracy effects, helps machine learning models more accurately infer
\Delta\Pi_{1}
values.
Lastly, 99.9% of the common reliable non-anomalous inferences by the two models are in agreement. This goes to show that for majority of the stars within the reliable set, inclusion of first-order formalism of rotation which does not take into account near-degeneracy effects does not change
\Delta\Pi_{1}
inferences.

In order to validate the reliable non-anomalous inferences from
K2 Model
and
K2 Model Norot
, we compared the inferred
\Delta\Pi_{1}
values with those reported by
(
48
)
, who employed a rotational formalism that accounts for near-degeneracy effects. Figure
19a
shows the comparison of
\Delta\Pi_{1}
inferred by the
K2 Model
with
(
48
)
for 997 stars in common. Of these, 797 stars have measured core rotation rates and are color-coded accordingly, while stars without rotation measurements are shown in grey.
We find excellent agreement between the two studies, with 99.3% of the stars exhibiting
\Delta\Pi_{1}
differences below 5%, as illustrated by the relative error histogram in Figure
19b
. These results demonstrate that, at least for the reliable subset of stars (i.e., inferences satisfying the quality threshold), a first-order rotation formalism is generally sufficient to accurately infer
\Delta\Pi_{1}

We also compared the confident non-anomalous
\Delta\Pi_{1}
inferences from the
K2 Model Norot
with those reported by
(
48
)
for 1219 stars in common (not shown here for brevity), of which 950 have measured rotation values in their catalogue. We again find good agreement, with 98.5% of the sample exhibiting relative errors below 5%.
This result demonstrates that even a model trained on synthetic spectra without rotation (
K2 Model Norot
) can accurately infer
\Delta\Pi_{1}
for the majority of observations that include rotational splittings. However, we observe a slightly higher number of outliers in this case, which further highlights the importance of incorporating rotation — even at the level of a first-order approximation — into the synthetic training set to reduce outliers and obtain more reliable
\Delta\Pi_{1}
