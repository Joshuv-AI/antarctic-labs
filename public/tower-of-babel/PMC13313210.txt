Serum trace metals across environmental-pressure strata in Campania, Italy (SPES): a population-based cross-sectional biomonitoring study
Authors: Pellegrino Cerino, Antonio Pizzolante, Carlo Buonerba, Paolo Montuori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesWe characterized fine-scale geographic variation in serum trace metals across Campania (southern Italy).Materials and MethodsIn 2016–2017, the SPES programme sampled residents aged 20–49 years from municipal registries across Campania. Municipalities were stratified using an environmental-pressure index into three broad impact strata: high-impact, medium-impact and low-impact. For higher spatial resolution, municipalities were also grouped into 21 predefined municipal clusters. We measured 20 serum trace metals by ICP-MS and estimated age- and sex-adjusted geometric mean ratios (GMRs) comparing each stratum or cluster with the study-wide geometric mean.ResultsOf 4205 enrolled participants, 4188 were included. Medium-impact areas showed higher mercury (GMR 1.87, 95% CI 1.73–2.02) and antimony (1.95, 1.71–2.23) relative to the cohort average. At the municipal-cluster scale, the Irno Valley clusters around the Fonderie Pisano foundry (Baronissi-Pellezzano and Salerno) had about six-fold higher mercury (GMR 5.71 and 6.10) and about three-fold higher antimony (3.45 and 2.93), whereas the Valle del Sabato cluster showed about ten-fold higher antimony (GMR 10.5) and about seven-fold higher nickel (7.16) and cadmium (7.67).ConclusionsOverall serum metal concentrations were broadly comparable with European general-population ranges, but pronounced small-area heterogeneity indicates localized hotspots that provincial or regional averages can obscure.Keywords: Human biomonitoring, trace metals, environmental exposure, geospatial analysis, Campania

Introduction

Pollution and chemical exposures remain a leading, preventable driver of morbidity and premature mortality globally and in Europe, and their impacts are often concentrated where environmental and social stressors co-occur [1–2]. Among chemical hazards, metals and metalloids warrant sustained public health attention because several are persistent in environmental media, may bioaccumulate through food chains, and are linked to cardiovascular, renal, neurological, reproductive, and carcinogenic outcomes [3–4]. Recent ecotoxicological literature also shows that cadmium, lead, and agrochemical contaminants can accumulate in aquatic organisms and produce measurable growth, behavioral, haematological, biochemical, and morphological effects in sentinel fish species, reinforcing the broader environmental relevance of persistent metal and contaminant pollution [5–7]. In Europe, emissions of selected heavy metals have declined over recent decades, yet legacy contamination, diffuse sources, industrial point sources, and complex exposure pathways can sustain population exposure and community concern [8].

A central challenge for prevention is that environmental monitoring-while essential for source identification-does not quantify the internal dose that results from combined routes of exposure and individual behaviors. Human biomonitoring (HBM) directly measures chemicals or biomarkers in human specimens, integrating inhalation, diet, occupation, lifestyle, and other pathways into a biologically relevant metric for surveillance, risk assessment, and evaluation of interventions [9]. Europe has invested in advancing and harmonizing HBM through WHO/Europe’s work and coordinated initiatives such as HBM4EU, which demonstrated the feasibility and value of generating comparable exposure data across countries and populations [10–11]. However, large, population-based HBM datasets remain limited in settings where community concern is high, exposures are heterogeneous, and risk narratives are contested.

The Campania region in southern Italy illustrates this challenge. For decades, parts of Campania-particularly in the provinces of Naples and Caserta-have been associated with the “Land of Fires” (Terra dei Fuochi) phenomenon, encompassing illegal trafficking, dumping, and burning of waste alongside pressures from urbanization and industrial activities [12–13]. Epidemiological surveillance and ecological analyses have reported modest but recurring excesses in selected outcomes in some areas, but causal attribution remains difficult because contamination is spatially heterogeneous, exposures occur as complex mixtures, and behavioral and occupational factors can confound or modify associations [14]. Previous biomonitoring efforts in Campania provided important signals while also underscoring the need for broader, population-based follow-up capable of supporting geospatial contrasts and adjustment for individual determinants of exposure [15–16].

To address these gaps, the Exposure Study on Susceptible People (SPES) was designed as a population-based HBM survey embedded in an explicit “source-pathway-target” framework and informed by multi-matrix environmental monitoring in Campania [17–19]. Municipalities were geo-stratified using a pressure index derived from a comparative risk assessment model combining georeferenced information on contamination sources and environmental matrices, and then grouped into clusters (domains) intended to enable interpretable comparisons of internal dose across gradients of environmental pressure [18,20]. SPES recruitment was designed around random sampling from municipal registries of residents aged 20–49 years, with collection of biospecimens and structured information on residential history, occupation, lifestyle, and diet, including use of validated dietary assessment instruments aligned with EPIC methodology [19,21]. SPES was promoted and sponsored by the Istituto Zooprofilattico Sperimentale del Mezzogiorno (IZSM) and was implemented as a monocentric investigation coordinated and conducted by the Istituto Nazionale Tumori IRCCS “Fondazione G. Pascale” (Naples) under the scientific leadership of the late principal investigator Dr Maurizio Montella [17,19]. Under a regional agreement, the University of Naples Federico II (Department of Public Health) provided technical and methodological support for selected data-processing and statistical components, but did not take an active role in participant investigation [20]. The principal dissemination output to date has been an online technical report (grey literature) that did not undergo full journal peer review [20].

Against a backdrop of long-standing community concern about environmental contamination in parts of Campania, we conducted a secondary analysis of the SPES serum trace-metals module using a prespecified, fully scripted pipeline and a de-identified analytical dataset. Our objectives were to quantify geographic differences in serum trace metals across impact-area strata and municipal clusters while appropriately handling < LOQ values, assay nonperformance, and covariate missingness; provide a parsimonious, interpretable set of contrasts adjusted for age group and sex to support future analyses; and provide a transparent, fully scripted analysis and missing-data pipeline with prespecified QC checks. Although SPES fieldwork was conducted in 2016–2017, dissemination has been delayed by organizational and system-level disruptions, including reprioritisation during the COVID-19 period.

We prespecified the following analytic targets: the primary objective was to estimate differences in serum trace-metal concentrations across impact-area strata (high, medium, and low) and municipal clusters. Primary endpoints were serum trace-metal concentrations (20 analyte channels), summarized as geometric means and compared using geometric mean ratios (GMRs) with 95% CIs from log-linear models adjusted for age group and sex. The point-source proximity analysis using residential distance and direction was exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting, and participants

We conducted a secondary cross-sectional analysis of data from the SPES population-based human biomonitoring programme implemented in the Campania region (southern Italy) in 2016–2017 within the “Campania Trasparente” monitoring framework [16–20]. This report follows the STROBE Statement and the STROBE-ME extension; statistical reporting follows SAMPL recommendations [22–24]. Field operations (sampling and recruitment, informed consent, clinical examination, biospecimen collection and storage, and laboratory determinations) were completed within the parent SPES programme under a prospectively approved protocol; the present work used a curated, de-identified, frozen analytical extract and involved no new participant contact, specimen collection, or laboratory testing [17,19,20].

Municipalities were geo-stratified using the Municipality Index of Environmental Pressure (MIEP), previously developed by some of the coauthors of this manuscript, and aggregated into municipal clusters. MIEP is a municipality-level composite geostratification tool rather than an individual exposure metric. It was developed from the Campania Trasparente comparative-risk framework by combining georeferenced information on potential contamination sources, territorial pressures, and environmental matrices at the municipality scale [18,20]. Its purpose in this analysis was to support sampling and broad stratum-level contrasts; it does not encode participant-specific residential history, time-activity patterns, diet, occupation, smoking, proximity to local sources, or metal-specific toxicokinetics. Consequently, high-, medium-, and low-impact strata should be interpreted as contextual pressure categories, not as ordered individual-dose categories expected to show monotonic gradients for every serum metal.

Recruitment targets were allocated across high-, medium-, and low-impact strata in a 4:2:1 ratio [18,20]. Participants were randomly sampled from municipal registries using study software and invited via mailed contact with call-center follow-up, supported by territorial recruitment units and communication campaigns [17,20]. The protocol eligibility criteria were age 20–49 years, both sexes, negative history for HCV/HBV/HIV, and residence in the municipality for ≥ 10 continuous years; later SPES outputs describe closely related criteria (20–50 years and ≥ 5 years residence) [17,19,20]. The analytical extract included a small number of enrolled participants outside the protocol age range (observed 19–55 years; Web Appendix Table S3).

Ethics approvals and written informed consent were obtained within the parent SPES programme (Ethics Committee, IRCCS “Fondazione G. Pascale”, Commissioner Deliberation n.590, 03 Aug 2016). This secondary analysis was conducted under SPES data-governance procedures [19–20].

The parent SPES programme enrolled 4,205 participants. Recruitment, clinical examinations, and biospecimen collection were conducted between November 2016 and August 2017 [17,20]. For this manuscript, 17 participants recruited in a municipality outside the study inclusion area were excluded, yielding an analytic dataset of n = 4188 participants assigned to 21 municipal clusters [20]. Clusters 20–21 correspond to the Irno Valley area around the Fonderie Pisano foundry; in the dataset used here, cluster assignment is based on municipality of residence (cluster 20: Baronissi and Pellezzano; cluster 21: Salerno). The planning-stage protocol describes 15 territorial domains; the implemented SPES dataset and technical report define the 21 clusters used here. The analysis sample size was fixed by the parent programme; no separate power calculation was undertaken for this reanalysis. Participant inclusion is summarized in Web Appendix Figure S1.

Fifty-one participants provided biospecimens but did not complete questionnaire/diet modules (“non-responders”). These records were retained; multiple-imputation model fitting was restricted to responders (n = 4137), with non-responders carried in prediction-only mode (Web Appendix Figure S2). Participants were not excluded for missing covariate data (Figures 1 and 2).

An exploratory point-source subanalysis evaluated gradients in serum metal concentrations with residential distance and direction among participants with geocoded residential coordinates within a 10 km buffer around the Pisano foundry centroid (WGS84: 40.7097° N, 14.7785°E). Of 398 addresses in the buffer, 360 participants were retained for modeling after geocoding and availability checks (Web Appendix Figure S3). This buffer was used only for the exploratory distance and direction models and did not affect municipality-based cluster assignment in the main analyses.

Data sources, variables, and laboratory procedures

Questionnaire/clinical data and laboratory results were captured in the SPES web-based data-management platform and linked via a unique participant identifier [19–20]. A de-identified analytical extract linking questionnaire and clinical modules and laboratory results was provided with documentation of variable definitions and conventions; the extract was frozen before analysis. Dataset construction and derivations were fully scripted to provide an audit trail and did not overwrite original laboratory concentrations (Web Appendix Supplementary Methods Sections 1.1, 1.3–1.4, and 1.9; Tables S2, S17, S22, and S27–S28).

Primary geographic predictors were impact-area stratum (high, medium, and low environmental pressure) and municipal-cluster membership (21 clusters) [17,18,20]. Municipality identifiers were retained for descriptive mapping. Primary endpoints were serum concentrations of 20 trace-metal analyte channels measured by ICP-MS; endpoints are referred to by element name in the main text (Li, Be, Cd, As, Cr, Co, Cu, Fe [two channels], Hg, Mn, Mo, Ni, Pb, Sb, Se, Sr, Tl, V, Zn). Concentrations were recorded in ug/L, except Fe and Zn (mg/L), as provided in the analytical extract. Isotopic channel labels, measurement units, distributional family assignments, working scales, and left-censoring imputation models are listed in Web Appendix Table S17.

Biospecimens were collected and processed using standardized SPES procedures [17,19–20]. In brief, blood was collected and serum and plasma were separated by refrigerated centrifugation; serum was aliquoted, stored at −80 degrees C, and tracked in the IZSM biobank under continuous temperature monitoring. Before analysis, samples were thawed at room temperature, mixed, diluted 1:10 in nitric acid, and analyzed in duplicate by ICP-MS (NexION 350, PerkinElmer) using 89Y, 103Rh, and 175Lu as internal standards; the mean of duplicate measurements was used for statistical analyses. Calibration curves met R2 > 0.99, and quality control included internal controls and certified reference materials, following SPES documentation [20].

Detection-limit reporting in this secondary analysis is based on the analyte-specific LOQ fields contained in the frozen analytical extract; instrument-level limits of detection were not available in the archived dataset. For each metal, the extract includes indicators for assay performance, whether values were below the analyte-specific LOQ, the LOQ value, and prespecified QC flags. Metal-specific assay nonperformance and < LOQ counts/proportions are reported in Web Appendix Tables S7 and S20. Values < LOQ were retained and treated as left-censored [25–26] at the LOQ for censoring-aware imputation, rather than being replaced by zero or LOQ/2. Using standard analytical-method terminology [27,28], the available documentation supports single-laboratory QA/QC reporting but does not provide an inter-laboratory comparison or proficiency-testing validation report for the serum trace-metals module.

Because metal distributions are typically right-skewed, descriptive summaries use the original concentration scale, whereas inferential models use natural-log concentrations; standardized z-score log concentrations were also analyzed for cross-metal comparability. Primary downstream models were purposefully parsimonious and included age group and sex as individual-level covariates, together with the relevant geographic factor in each model route. A broader prespecified set of socioeconomic, lifestyle, occupational, anthropometric, residential-history, and dietary variables was available and was used for descriptive summaries and as auxiliary information in the imputation stage (Web Appendix Table S2), but these variables were not included in the primary geographic contrast models.

Main statistical analysis

Participant characteristics are summarized using counts (%) and mean (SD) or median (IQR). For metals, we report geometric means with 95% confidence intervals (computed on the log scale), alongside medians and IQRs. Sampling weights and post-stratification (raking) weights from the parent programme were not available in the de-identified analytical extract; therefore, descriptive summaries are unweighted and describe the analytic sample. To reflect the sampling framework in inferential analyses, we report results by impact-area stratum and include either impact-area stratum (AREA route) or municipal-cluster indicators (CLUSTER route) as design variables in regression model [19–20].

Potential sources of bias include differential participation across strata, questionnaire nonresponse, and selection into the geocoded sub-cohort. Detailed counts of individuals invited or approached and reasons for nonparticipation were not available in the de-identified extract; we therefore report the available module-completion counts (Web Appendix Figure S1) and geocoding workflow (Web Appendix Figure S3) and apply regression adjustment and multiple imputation under the prespecified missing-data framework. Deterministic preprocessing preserved questionnaire skip logic (explicit “Unknown” and, where applicable, “Not applicable” levels), parsed numeric fields, and enforced coherent handling of < LOQ values; the corresponding recoding rules, HI20 plausibility thresholds/counts, and metal measurement/censoring summaries are documented in Web Appendix Tables S7, S19, S20, and S22.

Primary inferential analyses quantified differences in serum metal concentrations across impact-area strata and municipal clusters, adjusting for age group and sex. These models were designed to estimate interpretable spatial contrasts rather than fully adjusted source-specific causal effects; therefore, additional determinants of internal metal dose, such as diet, smoking, occupation, and socioeconomic context, were not included in the main contrast layer. Because clusters are nested within strata by design, we fit two parallel model routes rather than including both factors simultaneously:

Adjusted geometric means and geometric mean ratios were obtained from log-linear models using estimated marginal means. For each categorical factor, each level was contrasted against the model-based overall mean (computed with proportional weights reflecting the observed sample distribution). Estimates were pooled across imputations using Rubin’s rules [29]. False discovery rate was controlled within each modeling route using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (target FDR 5%); both nominal p values and FDR-adjusted q values are reported [30].

For the point-source sub-analysis, eligible addresses were geocoded (automated forward geocoding [31] with confidence-based acceptance followed by manual verification when needed; Web Appendix Figure S3). Distance to the foundry centroid and initial bearing were computed on WGS84; bearings were discretized into eight 45-degree sectors (N as reference), and distance was scaled per kilometer. Within each imputed dataset, we fit metal-specific linear regressions with log concentration as outcome and age group, sex, distance, and direction sector as covariates; estimates were pooled across imputations. The joint direction effect was assessed with a multivariate Wald test for pooled MI estimates. Multiplicity across the 20 metals in this exploratory analysis was addressed using Benjamini-Hochberg FDR.

Missing data and software

Missingness was classified by mechanism: left-censoring (<LOQ), assay nonperformance, prespecified plausibility-based outlier flags (HI20; Web Appendix Table S19), and missingness in nonmetal laboratory covariates and questionnaire and diet variables (including structural skip patterns). Questionnaire and diet variables subject to skip logic were not multiply imputed; missing values were recoded deterministically to explicit categories. Routine clinical laboratory variables with low missingness were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (30 imputations) implemented in mice, with responder-only model fitting and prespecified eligibility masks (Web Appendix Figure S2) [32]. The imputation models used an expanded set of auxiliary predictors from the analytical extract (including recoded questionnaire and diet variables) to support the missing-at-random assumption and preserve geographic contrasts; age, sex, impact-area stratum, and municipal cluster were forced into every imputation model. Downstream inferential models were intentionally parsimonious and adjusted only for age group and sex (plus impact-area stratum or municipal cluster). This separation was intended to preserve comparability across 20 metals and two spatial scales, avoid overadjustment for variables that could lie on exposure pathways, and ensure that auxiliary variables affected the imputation stage rather than the primary geographic contrast estimands. Values < LOQ were handled using censoring-aware imputation within the chained-equations system (Web Appendix Supplementary Methods Sections 1.2 and 1.7; Tables S17, S20, and S27–S28). Assay nonperformance was imputed only for metals with ≤ 5% nonperformance among responders; mercury and nickel missing exams were not imputed by design and were analyzed on the measured subset. Metal-specific working scales (natural-log, and prespecified Box-Cox transformations for selected analytes) were specified a priori in the imputation stage (Web Appendix Table S17) [33]. Analyses were conducted in R using fully scripted workflows with recorded software versions and run metadata (Web Appendix Supplementary Methods Section 1.9). The Web Appendix comprises Supplementary Methods Sections 1.1–1.10, Supplementary Results Sections 2.1–2.5, Tables S1–S33, and Figures S1–S5.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted a matched-set sensitivity analysis to assess whether the primary geographic contrasts were sensitive to the handling of values below the limit of quantification (LOQ) and to the limited assay nonperformance imputed in the primary analysis. The analysis was designed as a like-for-like comparison between two analyses applied, metal by metal, to the same participant set: a measured-only censoring-likelihood sensitivity analysis and a matched rebuild of the primary-analysis contrasts. This design separated the effect of the alternative censoring specification from differences in participant composition or downstream contrast estimation.

For each metal, we first defined a measured-only analytic set. Participants were eligible if the assay for that metal had been performed, the observation was not flagged as a prespecified QC-high outlier, and the result was either a finite positive quantified concentration or a valid left-censored observation with a finite positive LOQ. Participants were also required to have a participant identifier, age group, sex, impact-area stratum, and municipal-cluster assignment. Both geographic variables were required for all sensitivity analyses, even when only one geographic route was being fitted, so that each metal had a single unambiguous matched participant set for both the impact-area and municipal-cluster comparisons. Assay nonperformance was not imputed in this sensitivity analysis; therefore, unperformed assays, including the limited assay nonperformance imputed in the primary analysis for metals meeting the ≤ 5% criterion, were excluded from the corresponding measured-only set. Metal-specific measured-only denominators, censoring fractions, and censoring-likelihood fit QC are reported in Web Appendix Table S29.

Within each measured-only set, quantified concentrations were retained unchanged. Values below the LOQ were treated as left-censored observations and repeatedly sampled from fitted metal-specific conditional distributions below an effective censoring threshold. This threshold was defined as LOQ* = LOQ − min{max(1 × 10−9, 1 × 10−6 × |LOQ|), 0.5 × LOQ}. LOQ* lies just below the reported LOQ and was used as the operative censoring bound in model fitting and simulation to ensure that generated censored values remained strictly below the reported censoring boundary. This approach is consistent with likelihood-based methods for environmental and biomonitoring data with detection limits [25,26].

Censoring-likelihood models were fitted separately for each metal and geographic model route, using the full prespecified formula with age group, sex, and either impact-area stratum or municipal cluster. The same metal-specific working scales and distributional families used in the primary imputation framework were retained: Gaussian Tobit models on the natural-log scale, Gaussian Tobit models on fixed Box-Cox scales, or interval-censored Gamma models on the original concentration scale, depending on the metal-specific family assignment. No simplified fallback formula was used.

For each metal-route combination, 60 completed sensitivity datasets were generated. This number was specified as a Monte Carlo precision parameter, exceeded the 30 imputations used in the primary analysis, and was intended to reduce random variation in pooled GMRs, standard errors, p values, and false-discovery-rate classifications, particularly for metals with substantial left-censoring [29,34,35]. In these completed sensitivity datasets, only left-censored concentrations were replaced by simulated values. Participant identifiers, covariates, geographic indicators, and uncensored quantified concentrations were copied forward unchanged. Coefficient uncertainty in the censoring model was propagated through repeated coefficient draws where supported by the fitted variance-covariance matrix; scale or dispersion parameters were kept at their fitted values for generation of censored draws. Simulated censored concentrations were constrained to remain positive and below LOQ*.

Each completed sensitivity dataset was then analyzed using the same downstream model used for the primary geospatial analysis. The outcome was the natural-log concentration, and fixed effects were age group, sex, and either impact-area stratum or municipal cluster. Adjusted marginal means were estimated with proportional weights, and each factor level was contrasted against the model-based overall mean rather than against a reference category. Repeated-analysis estimates were pooled on the log scale using Rubin-style scalar pooling, with small-sample degrees of freedom handled as in the primary multiple-imputation analysis [29,36]. Pooled adjusted means and contrasts were exponentiated to obtain adjusted geometric means and geometric mean ratios (GMRs) with 95% confidence intervals. False discovery rate was controlled separately within each geographic modeling route using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure [30].

To provide the matched primary-analysis comparator, the primary-analysis contrast layer was rebuilt on the exact same metal-specific participant IDs used in the measured-only sensitivity analysis. This rebuild used the archived 30 completed datasets from the primary multiple-imputation analysis; the primary imputation models themselves were not re-estimated. Within each completed primary dataset, observations were restricted to the matched measured-only participant set for the corresponding metal, the same age-group, sex, impact-area, and municipal-cluster recoding was applied, and participants were required to have a finite positive metal concentration. The same log-linear model, proportional-weight estimated marginal means, level-versus-overall contrasts, scalar pooling procedure, and route-wise false-discovery-rate adjustment were then applied to the 30 completed primary datasets. Stage-specific repeated-completion metadata and matched primary-analysis rebuild QC are reported in Web Appendix Table S30.

The formal comparison was restricted to matched geographic contrasts. Impact-area contrasts from the impact-area route were compared only with impact-area contrasts, and municipal-cluster contrasts from the municipal-cluster route were compared only with municipal-cluster contrasts. Agreement was summarized by direction relative to the model-based overall mean, the ratio of the sensitivity-analysis GMR to the matched primary-analysis GMR, and preservation of route-wise q ≤ 0.05 status. Direction was defined by the sign of log(GMR), with GMRs above 1 interpreted as higher than the model-based overall mean and GMRs below 1 as lower than the model-based overall mean. Magnitude agreement was classified using prespecified ratio-of-ratios thresholds: close agreement within 1.25-fold, moderate shift >1.25-fold to ≤1.50-fold, and large shift >1.50-fold. Agreement summaries were calculated across all geographic contrasts and, separately, among geographic contrasts significant in the matched primary-analysis rebuild, to distinguish stability of the primary geographic signals from variation in null or near-null contrasts. Detailed implementation rules are provided in Web Appendix Supplementary Methods Section 1.10; metal-specific matched-set denominators and quality-control checks are provided in Web Appendix Tables S29–S30. Route-level agreement and contrast-level comparison outputs are reported in Web Appendix Tables S31–S33.

Results

Of 4205 participants enrolled in SPES, 17 recruited in Battipaglia were outside the study inclusion criteria and were excluded, leaving 4,188 participants in the analytic dataset (2395 [57.2%] in the high-impact area, 1,196 [28.6%] in the medium-impact area, and 597 [14.3%] in the low-impact area). Mean age was 34.9 years (SD 8.8) and 2,258 (53.9%) participants were women; age-group distribution was <30 years: 1,389 (33.2%), 30–39 years: 1,293 (30.9%), and ≥ 40 years: 1,506 (36.0%). Questionnaire and dietary data were available for 4,137 (98.8%) responders (Table 1). Stage-specific reasons for nonparticipation at recruitment stages were not available in the de-identified analytical extract.

Data completeness and missingness

Assay nonperformance was ≤5% for 18 of 20 analyte channels but substantial for mercury (785 [18.8%] unperformed assays) and nickel (1,861 [44.4%] unperformed assays); analyses for these metals used the measured subsets (mercury n = 3403; nickel n = 2327; Web Appendix Table S7). Among performed assays, left-censoring at the LOQ ranged from 0.02% (copper, selenium, strontium, and zinc) to 71.0% (lead), with high censoring also for beryllium (69.2%), cadmium (44.7%), chromium (41.1%), and antimony (36.2%) (Web Appendix Table S20). Because mercury and nickel were analyzed on measured subsets, estimates for these metals apply to participants with performed assays; differential assay nonperformance by batch or calendar time cannot be excluded. Multiple-imputation diagnostics are summarized in Web Appendix Tables S23–S28 and Figures S4–S5.

These measurement features have different implications for inference. High proportions of < LOQ results mainly reflect left-censoring, as in Pb-208, Be-9, Cd-111, Cr-52, and Sb-121, whereas Hg-202 and Ni-60 were chiefly affected by assay nonperformance and were therefore analyzed on measured subsets. For highly censored analytes, estimated geometric means and GMRs are necessarily more dependent on the assumed censored-data distribution and should be interpreted with greater caution, particularly when contrasts are small, borderline, or isolated.

Overall metal distributions

Metal concentrations were right-skewed and varied over several orders of magnitude (Web Appendix Table S8). Units vary by analyte (iron and zinc in mg/L; all other analytes in ug/L; Web Appendix Table S17). As exemplars (all in ug/L), overall geometric means were 0.0294 for lead (71.0% <LOQ), 0.00637 for cadmium (44.7% <LOQ), 0.761 for mercury (n = 3403), and 984 for copper (Web Appendix Table S8); metal-specific censoring fractions are summarized in Web Appendix Table S20.

Age-related differences were modest, with higher copper and strontium and lower Fe in participants aged ≥ 40 years (Web Appendix Table S8; adjusted estimates in Web Appendix Table S9). Sex differences were evident for several metals, with higher lithium, iron, selenium, zinc, arsenic, and mercury in men and higher copper, cobalt, and molybdenum in women (Web Appendix Table S8; adjusted estimates in Web Appendix Table S9).

Primary geospatial contrasts

Crude geometric means by impact-area stratum are summarized in Web Appendix Table S8. In the adjusted AREA-route model, the most pronounced differences involved mercury and antimony: medium-impact areas were higher and high-impact areas lower relative to the cohort overall (mercury: medium GMR 1.87 [95% CI 1.73–2.02] vs. high 0.714 [0.682–0.747]; antimony: medium 1.95 [1.71–2.23] vs. high 0.681 [0.634–0.732]; both q < 0.001; Table 2; Figure 2). Cadmium also differed by impact area: medium-impact areas were higher (GMR 1.33 [95% CI 1.17–1.51]) and high-impact areas lower (0.843 [0.783–0.908]) relative to the cohort overall (q < 0.001; Table 2). Low-impact areas had higher manganese (GMR 1.47 [1.28–1.70]) and molybdenum (1.51 [1.28–1.77]) and modestly higher lead (1.18 [1.04–1.34]) (q ≤ 0.05; Table 2). Full AREA-route estimates for all metals are provided in Web Appendix Table S9. Cluster-level contrasts showed substantial heterogeneity beyond the three-area classification (Table 3; Web Appendix Table S10). Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis was completed for all 20 metals in both primary geographic model routes. Specifically, the measured-only censoring likelihood was fitted once for each metal within each geographic model route—impact area and municipal cluster—yielding 40 metal-route fits. All 40 fits passed prespecified quality-control checks, and 60 completed sensitivity datasets were generated for each metal-route combination, yielding 2,400 completed sensitivity datasets in total. Across 20 metals, two geographic model routes, and 30 primary imputations, all 1,200 same-participant primary re-analysis checks matched the measured-only participant sets exactly, confirming that the comparison was not affected by differences in analytic population. This measured-only design also addressed the only analyte with non-trivial assay nonperformance below the primary-analysis imputation threshold: Zn-66 had 180 unperformed assays in the full cohort (4.3%) but entered the sensitivity analysis only through the 4,008 participants with performed zinc assays, of whom 4,007 were uncensored and one was < LOQ. Metal-specific measured-only sample sizes, censoring fractions, and fit QC are shown in Web Appendix Table S29; repeated-completion and matched-rebuild QC are shown in Web Appendix Table S30.

The geographic pattern was highly stable. Across all geographic contrasts, direction relative to the model-based overall mean was preserved for 59 of 60 impact-area contrasts and 412 of 420 municipal-cluster contrasts. Among contrasts significant in the corresponding same-participant primary re-analysis, direction was preserved for all 28 impact-area contrasts and all 102 municipal-cluster contrasts. Magnitude agreement was also high: 27 of 28 significant impact-area contrasts and 92 of 102 significant municipal-cluster contrasts differed by no more than 1.25-fold on the GMR scale. The q ≤ 0.05 classification was retained for 24 of 28 and 92 of 102 significant contrasts, respectively. Direction reversals were confined to null or near-null contrasts, whereas the non-close shifts among significant contrasts were confined to antimony, indicating greater sensitivity of antimony magnitude estimates—particularly at cluster level—to the alternative censoring specification. The substantive hotspot pattern was nevertheless unchanged: medium-impact areas remained elevated for mercury and antimony, cluster 19 remained the strongest hotspot for antimony, nickel, and cadmium, and clusters 20–21 in the Irno Valley remained the principal mercury/antimony hotspots. Zinc geographic contrasts were essentially unchanged. Route-level agreement is summarized in Web Appendix Table S31; contrast-level AREA and CLUSTER comparisons are provided in Web Appendix Tables S32 and S33.

Taken together, the measured-only censoring analysis provided an empirical check on reliability under high left-censoring and measured-only inference, without removing the information loss inherent to very high < LOQ fractions. The preservation of direction for all significant geographic contrasts supports the main spatial conclusions, while the concentration of non-close shifts in antimony identifies this analyte as the main source of uncertainty in effect-size magnitude rather than as evidence of a different spatial interpretation.

Exploratory Point-source subanalysis

In an exploratory geocoding sub-study (Web Appendix Figure S3), 362 of 398 (91.0%) addresses within the 10 km study buffer around the foundry centroid were geocoded after automated and manual quality control, and 360 participants had metal measures for distance and direction models (Web Appendix Table S11 and Table 4). Residential distance from the point source ranged from 0.21 km to 8.35 km (median 2.08 km [IQR 1.19–2.79]) (Table 4). Direction-sector counts were imbalanced and the NE sector contained no observations (Web Appendix Table S12), so direction effects were evaluated over the six estimable direction coefficients (Table 5). After false-discovery-rate correction across the 20 analyte channels, distance was associated with 11 elements (12 analyte channels, counting Fe-56 and Fe-57 separately; q ≤ 0.05; Table 5), with strong inverse gradients for chromium (ratio per 1 km 0.413 [95% CI 0.296–0.576]), manganese (0.450 [0.356–0.569]), and molybdenum (0.508 [0.403–0.641]) and similar inverse gradients for nickel and antimony; beryllium showed a positive gradient (Table 5; Web Appendix Table S14). Global direction effects did not survive false-discovery-rate correction for any metal (all q ≥ 0.064; Table 5; Web Appendix Table S13).

Discussion

In this population-based survey of 4,188 adults from Campania, we found marked small-area heterogeneity in serum trace metals that was not apparent from broad geographic aggregation. Although overall concentrations were broadly within the ranges reported in European general-population biomonitoring [37], several metals displayed pronounced spatial contrasts at multiple scales—impact-area strata, municipal clusters, and, exploratorily, within a 10 km buffer around a foundry. The key message is therefore not a single “most affected” area, but rather that internal exposure profiles vary locally and in metal-specific ways, so that regional or provincial averages—and even composite environmental-pressure categories—can mask distinct exposure patterns.

For policy interpretation, population reference context and health-based safety thresholds should be distinguished. The statement that overall SPES concentrations were broadly comparable with European general-population biomonitoring ranges [37] is a population-level comparison, not evidence that all measured values are clinically negligible. Direct comparison with established safety thresholds is limited because biomonitoring reference values, clinical decision limits, occupational biological exposure indices, and health-based HBM guidance values differ by analyte, biological matrix, population group, and derivation method. For several SPES metals, accepted health-based interpretive values are not available for serum in the general adult population or are more commonly defined for whole blood or urine rather than serum [3,38]. Therefore, the present results should be interpreted as spatial surveillance signals and as priorities for environmental and exposure-pathway follow-up, not as individual clinical diagnoses or direct exceedance assessments.

At the broadest scale, adjusted area-stratified comparisons showed a selective and non-monotonic pattern across metals. Medium-impact areas had higher mercury and antimony, while high-impact areas had lower concentrations for mercury, antimony, cadmium, and manganese; low-impact areas had higher manganese and molybdenum and modestly higher lead. These findings underscore that the SPES environmental-pressure index is best viewed as a tool for geostratified sampling and hypothesis generation rather than a direct surrogate for internal dose for every metal. If the index were interpreted as an individual-level, metal-specific exposure score, such non-monotonicity would be consistent with exposure misclassification. In the present analysis, however, the index was intentionally used as a municipality-level contextual pressure classification, and several mechanisms could explain its imperfect correspondence with serum biomarkers. First, serum concentrations integrate multiple pathways, including dietary intake, smoking, occupation, residential history, local source proximity, and background environmental sources, which may not align with the environmental matrices used to construct the pressure index. Second, metals differ in source profiles, environmental mobility, toxicokinetics, and in the extent to which serum reflects recent versus longer-term exposure. Third, essential elements are under physiological control, and serum levels can reflect nutritional status, regulation, and inflammation as much as external contamination. The non-monotonicity therefore should not be interpreted as paradoxical, but as evidence that composite environmental-pressure classifications cannot be assumed to map uniformly onto real-world internal exposure across a multi-metal panel.

The cluster-level models revealed additional heterogeneity beyond the three-area classification and showed that spatial contrasts were concentrated in a subset of municipal clusters, with different metals highlighting different hotspots. For example, cluster 19 showed extreme elevations in antimony and nickel and substantially higher cadmium, whereas clusters 20–21 (medium impact; municipalities in the Salerno-Irno Valley area) showed the most pronounced elevations for mercury (approximately six-fold) and antimony (approximately three-fold) relative to the cohort overall. Other clusters also demonstrated distinct profiles (e.g., elevations in lead in cluster 18), and several clusters showed systematically lower concentrations for selected metals. This mixture of high and low deviations across clusters indicates that local determinants of internal exposure are heterogeneous and likely reflect a combination of source proximity, urban and traffic-related factors, occupational patterns, and behavioral determinants that vary across municipalities. Importantly, the cluster estimates identify where multi-metal deviations occur; they do not, on their own, identify the specific source or pathway.

The exploratory point-source analysis adds a complementary, within-area perspective. Among participants residing within 10 km of the foundry centroid, residential distance was inversely associated with multiple metals after false-discovery-rate control, with the steepest gradients for chromium, manganese, and molybdenum, and additional inverse gradients for nickel and antimony. Thallium also met the Benjamini-Hochberg threshold for distance (q = 0.038), but the pooled distance coefficient had relatively low multiple-imputation degrees of freedom (df = 45.9) and imputation diagnostics indicated substantial ridge regularization for Tl-205 (Web Appendix Tables S15 and S23), so this finding should be interpreted cautiously. By contrast, directional effects did not survive multiplicity adjustment for any metal, which could reflect limited sample size and uneven sector coverage within the buffer, as well as temporal variability in wind patterns not captured by a cross-sectional residential metric. Notably, the metals with the strongest between-cluster elevations (e.g., mercury in clusters 20–21) were not identical to those with the clearest within-buffer distance gradients (e.g., chromium, manganese and molybdenum). This discordance is not necessarily contradictory: the cluster models quantify between-municipality contrasts across the entire study region, whereas the point-source models quantify within-buffer gradients in a restricted subset and on a different spatial scale. Moreover, some metals-particularly mercury-have multiple dominant exposure pathways in general populations and may therefore show weaker or less coherent distance gradients than metals more tightly linked to particulate emissions and deposition. Measurement features may also contribute: mercury had substantial assay nonperformance and appreciable left-censoring, which could attenuate spatial gradients. Taken together, the point-source results are best interpreted as exploratory evidence that some metals show proximity gradients compatible with local contributions (or correlated near-source exposures), while other metals show stronger contrasts at municipality scale, consistent with multiple pathways and broader contextual differences.

This study has several strengths. It leverages a large, population-based human biomonitoring dataset with geostratified recruitment, quantifies a broad panel of metals using a consistent laboratory platform, and applies prespecified methods to handle left-censoring and missingness. The large sample size supported relatively precise estimation of broad-area and cluster-level contrasts, but sample size does not eliminate the possibility of residual confounding. The analytic strategy-using parsimonious downstream adjustment (age and sex) while incorporating additional auxiliary information in the imputation stage-prioritises transparency and comparability across metals and spatial scales, and reduces the risk of overadjustment for variables that might lie on exposure pathways. Nonetheless, several limitations should be considered. The curated analytical extract included a small number of participants outside the protocol age range (19–55 vs. 20–49 years; Web Appendix Table S3); we retained them to preserve the frozen extract. The design is cross-sectional and cannot establish temporal ordering or causality. Downstream models did not adjust for detailed dietary, behavioral, occupational, or socioeconomic covariates, so residual confounding by diet, smoking, occupation, socioeconomic position, or other factors could partly explain some spatial contrasts. Accordingly, the geographic GMRs should be interpreted as age- and sex-adjusted spatial contrasts rather than source-specific causal effects. Dedicated follow-up analyses will evaluate individual-level determinants of metal concentrations, including diet, smoking, occupational exposures, and socioeconomic indicators.

The temporal scope of the data is also important. SPES recruitment, biospecimen collection, and laboratory measurements were conducted between November 2016 and August 2017; the results therefore represent a historical internal-dose snapshot from that period. The cross-sectional design cannot determine whether the same patterns persist today. Exposure patterns may have changed because of remediation, industrial activity, environmental controls, waste-management practices, dietary or behavioral changes, or residential mobility, while some metals and environmental reservoirs may persist over longer time frames. The findings remain relevant as a documented baseline and as a tool for prioritizing follow-up, but only a repeated biomonitoring campaign using comparable laboratory methods and geographic definitions could determine whether current serum concentrations have persisted, attenuated, or shifted geographically.

Assay nonperformance was substantial for mercury and nickel, and high left-censoring for several metals, especially lead and beryllium and also cadmium, chromium, and antimony, increases uncertainty despite censoring-aware imputation. In these analytes, the observed data provide limited information about the exact distribution below the LOQ; therefore, estimates are more model-dependent than for analytes with little censoring. Censoring-aware imputation is preferable to deletion or simple substitution because it respects the known censoring boundary, but it cannot recover all information lost by high censoring. Consequently, highly censored analyte contrasts should be interpreted most cautiously when differences are small, borderline after FDR correction, or not reproduced in sensitivity analysis.

Cluster-level estimates are more granular than the three-area estimates and may be less stable for small-amplitude or isolated contrasts. Although cluster sample sizes were broadly similar, statistical stability also depends on censoring, within-cluster variability, and the number of simultaneous contrasts. We therefore emphasize 95% CIs, FDR-adjusted q values, cross-metal coherence, and matched-set sensitivity results rather than isolated point estimates. Confidence is strongest for large-amplitude, multi-metal, directionally consistent contrasts that persisted in sensitivity analysis; near-null or borderline cluster contrasts should be treated as hypothesis-generating. Laboratory documentation was limited to the published SPES methods and the frozen analytical extract; although these sources report duplicate analysis, calibration linearity, internal controls, and certified reference materials, they did not include an independent inter-laboratory comparison or proficiency-testing validation report, so between-laboratory reproducibility could not be evaluated. The point-source analysis was restricted to a geocoded subset and did not incorporate meteorological data, time-activity patterns, or historical residential mobility, which likely contribute to exposure variability.

The findings have clear implications for surveillance and follow-up. First, they support the value of integrating biomonitoring with fine-scale spatial approaches: broad area strata provided an initial overview, but cluster-level contrasts and the exploratory proximity analysis revealed additional heterogeneity that would be missed by regional averages. Second, prioritization for follow-up should be driven by consistent, multi-metal signals rather than by a single metal or a single geographic label. This includes targeted environmental investigation (e.g., environmental sampling and source characterization) in municipalities or subareas showing coherent elevations, and community-engaged risk communication tailored to local exposure pathways. Finally, dedicated secondary analyses should evaluate individual-level determinants of trace-metal concentrations, including diet, smoking, occupational exposures, socioeconomic context, and source-related determinants, and future work should extend these analyses to repeated biomonitoring and health-relevant endpoints alongside harmonized reporting in line with MIR-HBM guidance [38], to facilitate comparability and translation to policy.

Conclusions

Serum trace-metal concentrations in this population-based Campania cohort provide a 2016–2017 biomonitoring baseline. Overall concentrations were broadly comparable with European general-population ranges, but pronounced small-area heterogeneity identified localized hotspots that were obscured by broader geographic summaries. The municipal-cluster analysis was more informative than coarse impact-area stratification for highlighting these signals. Because direct safety-threshold interpretation is limited for several serum metals, the findings should be used for population-level surveillance, spatial prioritization, and exposure-pathway investigation rather than as individual clinical exceedance assessments. Taken together, the findings support targeted environmental follow-up, context-specific risk communication, and repeated biomonitoring to determine whether these historical exposure patterns persist, have attenuated, or have shifted geographically.
