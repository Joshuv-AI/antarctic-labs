Language Acceptors with a Pushdown: Characterizations and Complexity Thanks: Electronic version of an article published as International Journal of Foundations of Computer Science , Vol. 36, No. 03, 2025, pp. 345–370 © copyright World Scientific Publishing Company .
Oscar H. Ibarra, Ian McQuillan

Abstract
We study one-way nondeterministic pushdown automata ( 𝖭𝖯𝖣𝖠 {\mathsf{NPDA}} ), optionally with reversal-bounded counters. Finite-turn pushdown automata are pushdown automata with a bound on the number of switches between pushing and popping. We give new characterizations for finite-turn pushdown automata, and for finite-turn pushdown automata augmented with reversal-bounded counters. The first is in terms of multi-tape nondeterministic finite automata ( 𝖭𝖥𝖠 {\mathsf{NFA}} ), and the second is in terms of multi-tape 𝖭𝖥𝖠 {\mathsf{NFA}} with reversal-bounded counters. We then use the characterizations to determine the complexity of the languages defined by these automata. In particular, we show that languages accepted by finite-turn 𝖭𝖯𝖣𝖠 {\mathsf{NPDA}} augmented with reversal-bounded counters are in 𝖭𝖫𝖮𝖦 {\mathsf{NLOG}} . For the non-finite-turn case, the languages are in 𝖣𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 ⁡ ( log 2 ⁡ n ) {\mathsf{DSPACE}}(\log^{2}n) and in 𝖯 {\mathsf{P}} . We also look at the space complexity of languages accepted by two-way machines. In particular, we show that every language accepted by a two-way 𝖭𝖯𝖣𝖠 {\mathsf{NPDA}} with reversal-bounded counters that makes a polynomial (resp., exponential) number of input head reversals is in 𝖣𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 ⁡ ( log 2 ⁡ n ) {\mathsf{DSPACE}}(\log^{2}n) (resp., 𝖣𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 ⁡ ( n 2 ) {\mathsf{DSPACE}}(n^{2}) ). This remains true if the pushdown can flip its contents a bounded number of times.

One-way nondeterministic pushdown automata, denoted by
{\mathsf{NPDA}}
, are one of the most well-studied classes of machines.
This class of automata is quite practical to use. For example, it is well known that all languages accepted by pushdown automata are in
{\mathsf{P}}

[
15
]
and also in

14

t

{\mathsf{NPDA}}
(respectively finite-turn

t

[
8
]

It is known that all languages accepted by finite-turn pushdown automata are in
{\mathsf{NSPACE}}(\log n)={\mathsf{NLOG}}

[
22
]
(they can be accepted in
\log n
space by a nondeterministic Turing machine, which are all in
{\mathsf{P}}

Another type of machine model that has been extensively studied are one-way nondeterministic reversal-bounded multicounter machines. A one-way
k
-counter machine has a one-way read-only input tape, with
k

15
]

r
(respectively a finite number). It is also possible to combine different types of stores. The class of one-way reversal-bounded multicounter machines is denoted by
{\mathsf{NCM}}

[
2
,
18
]
. In
[
18
]
,
one-way nondeterministic pushdown automata augmented with reversal-bounded counters were also studied, denoted here by
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
.

{\mathsf{NPCM}}
is strictly more powerful than either
{\mathsf{NPDA}}
or
{\mathsf{NCM}}
, and it has an
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete non-emptiness problem (“given

[
10
]

It was recently shown that
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
has a decidable (in fact

-complete) boundedness problem (“given

, is

a bounded language?”
[
3
]
).
Also recently, it was shown that the membership problem for two-way
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
(where the input tape head can move in both directions) is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete
[
17
]
.

In this paper, we give new characterizations in terms of multi-tape acceptors.
We define a
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
as being
finite-turn
if the pushdown is finite-turn.
The characterizations which are in terms of multi-tape
versions of nondeterministic finite automata (
{\mathsf{NFA}}
) and
{\mathsf{NCM}}

are as follows:

1.

finite-turn
{\mathsf{NPDA}}
in terms of multi-tape
{\mathsf{NFA}}
,

2.

finite-turn
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
in terms of multi-tape
{\mathsf{NCM}}

As by-product of the characterizations, we show that
all languages accepted by finite-turn
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
are in
{\mathsf{NLOG}}
and that
that all languages accepted by
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
can be accepted by

\log^{2}(n)
space-bounded deterministic Turing machines.
We also investigate the space complexity of languages accepted by
two-way machines. In particular, we show that every language accepted by a
two-way
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
that makes a polynomal (in the length of the input)
number of input head reversals is in
{\mathsf{DSPACE}}(\log^{2}n)
(which generalizes to another machine model where the pushdown can flip its contents a bounded number of times).
This result matches the space complexity of
one-way
{\mathsf{NPDA}}
languages (equal to the context-free languages)
[
21
,
14
]
.
We also show that all
{\mathsf{NPCM}}

since the membership problem for two-way

is
{\mathsf{NP}}

[
17

The results in this paper significantly expand the knowledge regarding the time and space required to simulate generalizations and restrictions of one- and two-way pushdown automata with deterministic Turing machines.

First, we see the following generalization from

Proposition 5.1

For

Next, we study bounds on the number of changes of direction on the two-way input tape.
We say a function

is

if there is a
{\mathsf{DTM}}
that on an input of length
n

a string of length at least
\log_{2}R(n)+1
on the worktape using at most

R(n)

we say a

is

if every input of length

(note that when

We call a

if the input head reverses occur only at the end markers.
We call a

is some constant, and we use

Lemma 5.3
.

Every
2{\mathsf{NPCM}}

M
can be converted to an equivalent sweeping

2{\mathsf{NPCM}}
.

Proof 5.4
.

The pushdown stack can be used to enforce the turns take place at the end markers. If the turn occurs at the end marker, there is no problem. If a turn occurs before the end marker it records on the top of the stack (using a different symbol) to count (by pushing) the number of moves until it reaches the end marker to which it was moving. When it reaches the end marker, it can reposition the head back to the location where it wanted to make a turn by popping the stack. Note, that if the number of reversals on the input is unbounded (finite), the number of added turns on the stack will be unbounded (finite).

In the proof of the next result, we introduce a technique that allows us
to use complexity results for one-way machines to prove complexity
results for two-way machines.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let

be a logspace-constructible function.
All

-reversal

{\mathsf{DSPACE}}(\log^{2}(nR(n)))

.

Remark 5.7

In the proposition above, we used the standard definition of space complexity of Turing machines, as in

.

2.

For any logspace-constructible function
R(n)
, all languages accepted by
R(n)
-reversal
finite-turn sweeping
2{\mathsf{NPCM}}
are in

{\mathsf{NSPACE}}(\log(nR(n)))
.

The result above is a generalization of the known result that
finite-turn
{\mathsf{NPDA}}

(i.e., one-way
{\mathsf{NPDA}}
whose stack makes at most

k
turns from some

) languages are in
{\mathsf{NLOG}}

[
22
]
.
In particular, item 2 shows that
all languages accepted by poly-reversal
finite-turn sweeping
2{\mathsf{NPCM}}
are in
{\mathsf{NLOG}}
(without the sweeping restriction, this simulation causes the pushdown to no longer be finite-turn).

It was also shown in
[
22
]

that finite-turn
{\mathsf{DPDA}}
(i.e., deterministic) languages
are in
{\mathsf{DLOG}}
(=
{\mathsf{DSPACE}}(log~n))
. Using this result and similar
techniques as above, we have (where
2{\mathsf{DPDA}}
are two-way deterministic pushdown automata):

Proposition 5.10
.

The following are true:

1.

All languages accepted by finite-reversal
finite-turn
2{\mathsf{DPDA}}
are in
{\mathsf{DLOG}}
.

2.

For any logspace-constructible function
R(n)
, all languages accepted by
R(n)
-reversal
finite-turn sweeping
2{\mathsf{DPDA}}
are
in
{\mathsf{DSPACE}}(\log(nR(n)))
.

We do not know if Proposition
5.10
is true when
2{\mathsf{DPDA}}
is
replaced by
2{\mathsf{DPCM}}
, as we do not know at this time whether the languages
accepted by finite-turn
{\mathsf{DPCM}}
are in
{\mathsf{DLOG}}
.

Similarly, we note the following regarding time complexity. We say a function
T(n)
is
time constructible
if a
{\mathsf{DTM}}

can compute it in
T(n)
time.

Proposition 5.11
.

Let
T(n)
be a time-constructible function such that all
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
(respectively
{\mathsf{NPDA}}
) languages are in
{\mathsf{DTIME}}(T(n))
.
Then every finite-reversal
2{\mathsf{NPCM}}
(respectively finite-reversal
2{\mathsf{NPDA}}
) language is in
{\mathsf{DTIME}}(T(n))
.
Further, all poly-reversal
2{\mathsf{NPCM}}
(respectively poly-reversal
2{\mathsf{NPDA}}
) languages are in
{\mathsf{P}}
.

Proof 5.12
.

Let
M
be
k
-reversal.
As in the proof of Proposition
5.5
, let
Z
be a
T(n)
-time-bounded deterministic Turing machine accepting
L(M^{\prime})
.
We construct another
{\mathsf{DTM}}

Z^{\prime}
which starts by computing
T(n)
. Then,
given input
x_{0}
, it writes on a read/write tape the string

w=x_{0}^{(k)}
. Then
Z^{\prime}
simulates
Z
on
w
while stopping after
T(n)
steps. It is evident that

Z^{\prime}
has the same time complexity as
Z
.

For the second statement, if
M
is
n^{c}
-reversal for some constant
c
, then following the same proof of Proposition
5.5
,
the one-way machine
M^{\prime}
accepts a language in
{\mathsf{P}}
by Proposition
4.9
. So and so a Turing machine
Z

can be built running in
O(n^{k})
time for some constant

accepting
L(M^{\prime})
. From
Z
, another Turing machine
Z^{\prime}
can be built that on input

x_{0}
, puts
x_{0}^{(n^{c})}
on a worktape in
O(n\cdot n^{c})
time, and then simulates
Z
that runs in time complexity
O(n^{(c+1)k})
.

While typically membership for
{\mathsf{NPDA}}
is decided on a random access machine (and is faster than
O(n^{3})
), it runs in
n^{3}
time on a
{\mathsf{DTM}}

[
14
]
.

Corollary 5.13
.

Every language accepted by a finite-reversal
2{\mathsf{NPDA}}
can be accepted by a
{\mathsf{DTM}}
in
n^{3}
time.

The two-way to one-way reduction technique in the proof of Proposition
5.5

can be used to prove other complexity results involving two-way machines.
For example, in
[
4
]
, Chan studied

{\mathsf{NPDA}}
and
2{\mathsf{NPDA}}
with counters that are
c(n)
-reversal-bounded (i.e., the
number of changes between sequences of non-increasing and non-decreasing in each counter is at most
c(n)
on an input of size
n
), generalizing

{\mathsf{NPCM}}
and
2{\mathsf{NPCM}}
, respectively. He showed the following, where
c(n)
is time constructible:

1.

An
{\mathsf{NPDA}}
with
c(n)
-reversal-bounded counters can be simulated by an
{\mathsf{NTM}}

(i.e., a nondeterministic multi-tape TM) within time
p(n+c(n))
for some
polynomial
p
.

2.

A
2{\mathsf{NPDA}}
with
c(n)
-reversal-bounded counters can be simulated by an
{\mathsf{NTM}}

within time
p(n^{n^{2}}+c(n))
for some polynomial
p
.

Chan asked whether the
{\mathsf{NTM}}
simulation time above can be lowered. He noted
that in
[
9
]
,
2{\mathsf{NCM}}
(i.e.,
2{\mathsf{NPCM}}
without reversal-bounded counters) accept only languages
in
{\mathsf{NLOG}}

and hence in
{\mathsf{P}}
, but from (2) above, the
{\mathsf{NTM}}
simulation time for the same
machines augmented with a pushdown store is still

(constant
c
) for
some
polynomial

. We will see that a better simulation time can be obtained
when
the number of turns the two-way input head makes is bounded.

Consider an
R(n)
-reversal
2{\mathsf{NPDA}}

M
with
c(n)
-reversal-bounded counters. Then, we can use item (1) above
and the proof technique in Proposition
5.5
to show the following:

Proposition 5.14
.

Let
R(n)
and
c(n)
be time constructible. An
R(n)
-reversal
2{\mathsf{NPDA}}

M
with

-reversal-bounded counters can be simulated by an
{\mathsf{NTM}}
within time

p(nR(n)+c(n))
for some polynomial
p
.

The reason the term
c(n)
above does not change is that in converting the

2{\mathsf{NPDA}}

M
to an
{\mathsf{NPDA}}

M^{\prime}
using the technique of Proposition
5.5
, the
number of reversals each counter of

makes is the same as those of

,
since the simulation of

by

is faithful, as noted in the proof of
Proposition

.

Thus, for
R(n)
-reversal
2{\mathsf{NPDA}}
, we
can get smaller time bounds than in (2) when

is small. For example:

Corollary 5.15
.

The following are true:

1.

A poly-reversal
2{\mathsf{NPDA}}

within time

for some polynomial

.

2.

An

-reversal

-reversal-bounded counters (for

within time

for

.

6
Finite-Flip
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
(Finite-Flip
2{\mathsf{NPCM}}
)

In this section, we will show that the space complexity results
we obtained for the machines in the previous sections
generalize to when the machines can reverse its pushdown
a finite number of times during the computation. This generalization of
{\mathsf{NPDA}}
has been previously studied
[
13
,
12
]
.

A flip-
{\mathsf{NPDA}}

M
is an
{\mathsf{NPDA}}
which has the ability to
flip its pushdown at various times during its computation
by entering one of a designated set of flip states. Thus,
when the pushdown contains
Z_{0}\alpha
(where
Z_{0}
is the bottom-of-stack marker which is never replaced) and
M
enters a
flip state, the stack becomes
Z_{0}\alpha^{R}
. A machine
M
is

k
-flip (respectively finite-flip) if every accepted string can be accepted in a computation
where
M
makes at most
k
flips (respectively a finite number of flips) of the stack. In fact,
without loss of generality, we will assume
that every input is accepted with the machine using
exactly
k
flips of the stack (instead of at most
k

flips). This is because the state can count the number
of flips the machine has made and can perform additional dummy
flips before accepting to make the number of flips
exactly
k
.
Note that a 0-flip
{\mathsf{NPDA}}
is just an
{\mathsf{NPDA}}
.
We refer the reader to

[
13
,
12
]
for formal definition.

We can then also define
k
-flip
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
, finite-flip
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
,
finite-flip
2{\mathsf{NPCM}}
, finite-turn finite-flip
2{\mathsf{NPCM}}

(which is a finite-flip
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
that makes a finite number
of turns on it pushdown between flips),
R(n)
-reversal finite-flip

2{\mathsf{NPCM}}
(which is a finite-flip
2{\mathsf{NPCM}}
that makes at
most
R(n)
input head reversals), etc. Thus the models in
the previous sections are generalized by replacing ‘
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
’
and ‘
2{\mathsf{NPCM}}
’ by ‘finite-flip
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
’ and ‘finite-flip
2{\mathsf{NPCM}}
’,
respectively.

The next lemma follows from Theorem 5 in
[
13
]
, which does not use the
\$
symbol, but it can be added.

Lemma 6.1
.

[
13
]

Let
k\geq 0
. A language
L
can be accepted by a (
k+1
)-flip
{\mathsf{NPDA}}

M=(Q,\Sigma,\Gamma,q_{0},F,\delta)
that makes exactly
k+1
on every accepting computation if and only if
the language

L_{R}=\{u\$v^{R}\mid\begin{array}[t]{l}(q_{0},u,Z_{0})\vdash^{*}(q_{1},\lambda,Z_{0}\gamma)\mbox{~with~}k\mbox{~flips, there is a flip from~}q_{1}\mbox{~to~}q_{2},\mbox{~and}\\
(q_{2},v,Z_{0}\gamma^{R})\vdash^{*}(q_{3},\lambda,Z_{0}),q_{3}\in F\},\end{array}

is accepted by a
k
-flip
{\mathsf{NPDA}}

M^{\prime}
.
Moreover, if
M

is finite-turn, then
M^{\prime}
is also finite-turn.

The above result holds when the
{\mathsf{NPDA}}
is replaced by

{\mathsf{NPCM}}
, as we show next.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
k\geq 0
. A language
L
can be accepted by a (
k+1
)-flip
{\mathsf{NPCM}}

M=(Q,\Sigma,\Gamma,q_{0},F,\delta)
that makes exactly
k+1
on every accepting computation if and only if
the language

L_{R}=\{u\$v^{R}\mid\begin{array}[t]{l}(q_{0},u,Z_{0})\vdash^{*}(q_{1},\lambda,Z_{0}\gamma)\mbox{~with~}k\mbox{~flips, there is a flip from~}q_{1}\mbox{~to~}q_{2},\mbox{~and}\\
(q_{2},v,Z_{0}\gamma^{R})\vdash^{*}(q_{3},\lambda,Z_{0}),q_{3}\in F\},\end{array}

is accepted by a
k
-flip
{\mathsf{NPCM}}

M^{\prime}
.
Moreover, if
M

is finite-turn, then
M^{\prime}
is also finite-turn.

Proof 6.3
.

Let
\Sigma
be the input alphabet of
M
, and
C_{1},\ldots,C_{m}
be
its monotonic counters, where
m
is an even positive integer. Construct an
{\mathsf{NPDA}}

M_{1}
with input alphabet

\Delta=\Sigma\cup\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}\}
, where the
c_{i}
’s are new
symbols corresponding to the counters. When given an input

x\in\Delta^{*}
,
M_{1}
simulates the computation of
M
but reads
symbol
c_{i}
whenever
M
increments counter
C_{i}
.

By Lemma
6.1
, we can construct a flip

{\mathsf{NPDA}}

M_{1}^{\prime}
which makes exactly
k
flips that accepts
L(M_{1})_{R}
. Note that the input alphabet
of
M_{1}^{\prime}
is
\Delta
. We can then construct a flip
{\mathsf{NPCM}}

M^{\prime}
with counters
C_{1},\ldots,C_{m}
to simulate
M_{1}^{\prime}
, but with input
\Sigma
. So in the simulation,
M^{\prime}
instead
of reading a letter
c_{i}
, increments counter
C_{i}
. In this way, the places on
the input where it is supposed to read
c_{i}
are nondeterministically guessed.
Then
M^{\prime}
accepts
L_{R}
.

Proposition 6.4
.

Every language accepted by a finite-flip
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
is in

{\mathsf{DSPACE}}(\log^{2}n)
.

Proof 6.5
.

The construction is by induction on the number of flips
k
. We know already that
0
-flip
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
are equal to
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
, which are in

{\mathsf{DSPACE}}(\log^{2}n)
by Proposition
4.6
.
Assume by induction that all
k
-flip
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
are in
{\mathsf{DSPACE}}(\log^{2}n)
.
Let
M
be an
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
which makes exactly
k+1
flips.
From Proposition
6.2
, we can construct a flip
{\mathsf{NPCM}}

M^{\prime}
which makes exactly
k
flips such that

L(M^{\prime})=L(M)_{R}
.
By induction,
L(M^{\prime})
can be accepted by a
{\mathsf{DTM}}

Z^{\prime}
in
\log^{2}n
space.

We construct a
{\mathsf{DTM}}

Z
to accept
L(M)
which, when given an input
w

systematically splits the input into
u
and
v
such that
w=uv
in every possible way, starting with
|u|=0
. Then
Z
simulates the
{\mathsf{DTM}}

Z^{\prime}
on
u\$v^{R}
(it needs only
\log~n
space to remember the location of the split). Clearly, the simulating
{\mathsf{DTM}}

Z
is also
\log^{2}n
space-bounded.

We will prove
L(Z)=L(M)
.
First
Let
w\in L(M)
. Then
w=uv
where
u
is the part that is read up until the
k^{\mbox{th}}
flip, it flips the final time, then it reads
v
and accepts. Because of that,

u\$v^{R}
is in
L(M)_{R}=L(Z^{\prime})
. Then in
Z
, on input
w
, it will eventually try the partition
w=uv
and check that
u\$v^{R}
is in
L(Z^{\prime})
and then accept
w
. Conversely, let
w\in L(Z)
. Then on input
w
, there must be some split
w=uv
such that
u\$v^{R}\in L(M)_{R}
. By the construction of
L(M)_{R}
, this means
uv
accepts in
M
.

By the same ideas as above, we can also show the following:

Proposition 6.6
.

Every language accepted by a finite-turn finite-flip
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
is in

{\mathsf{NLOG}}
.

Using Propositions
6.4
and
6.6
, we can then
show (using the same proof techniques) that all the results
in Sections 4 and 5 hold when ‘
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
’ and ‘
2{\mathsf{NPCM}}
’ are
replaced by ‘finite-flip
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
’ and ‘finite-flip
2{\mathsf{NPCM}}
’ respectively.
In particular, we have:

Proposition 6.7
.

The following are true:

1.

For logspace-constructible
R(n)
,
every language accepted by an
R(n)
-reversal finite-flip
2{\mathsf{NPCM}}

is in
{\mathsf{DSPACE}}(\log^{2}(nR(n)))
.

2.

Every language accepted by a poly-reversal finite-flip
2{\mathsf{NPCM}}

is in
{\mathsf{DSPACE}}(\log^{2}n)
.

3.

Every language accepted by a finite-reversal finite-turn finite-flip
2{\mathsf{NPCM}}

is in
{\mathsf{NLOG}}
.

For the time complexity of finite-flip
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
languages, we have:

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
T(n)
be a time-constructible function. If every
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
language is accepted by a
{\mathsf{DTM}}
in

T(n)
time, then every
k
-flip
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
language can be accepted
by a
{\mathsf{DTM}}
in
n^{k}T(n)
time.

Proof 6.9
.

The proof is again an induction on
k
. This is obviously
true for
k=0
. Now assume that every
k
-flip
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
language
can be accepted by a
{\mathsf{DTM}}
in
n^{k}T(n)
time. Let
M
be
(k+1)
-flip
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
. We construct a
k
-flip
{\mathsf{NPCM}}

M^{\prime}
as in the proof of Proposition
6.4
.
Then, by the induction hypothesis,
L(M^{\prime})
can be accepted by a
{\mathsf{DTM}}

Z
in
n^{k}T(n)
time. From
Z
, we construct a
{\mathsf{DTM}}

Z^{\prime}
to accept

L(M)
as in that proof. Since there are
n
possible positions in
the input
w
to make the split, systematically checking each of these for a “valid” split will add a factor of
n
in the complexity. It follows that
Z^{\prime}
will have time complexity

n^{k+1}T(n)
.

Since we know that every
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
language is in
{\mathsf{P}}

by Proposition
4.9
, we have:

Corollary 6.10
.

Every language accepted by a finite-flip
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
is in
{\mathsf{P}}
.

We can then generalize the above result (using the two-way to one-way conversion technique) to:

Corollary 6.11
.

Every language accepted by a poly-reversal finite-flip
2{\mathsf{NPCM}}
is in
{\mathsf{P}}
.

Clearly, the proposition above holds for
k
-flip NPDA languages. Since every
{\mathsf{NPDA}}
language (
{\mathsf{CFL}}
) can be accepted by a
{\mathsf{DTM}}
in

O(n^{3})
time
[
14
]
, we have:

Corollary 6.12
.

Every language accepted by a
k
-flip
{\mathsf{NPDA}}
can be
accepted by a
{\mathsf{DTM}}
in
n^{k+3}
time.

This generalizes to:

Corollary 6.13
.

Every language accepted by a finite-reversal

k
-flip
2{\mathsf{NPDA}}
can be accepted by a
{\mathsf{DTM}}
in
n^{k+3}
time.

7
Conclusions

In this paper, we provided characterizations of one-way nondeterministic finite-turn pushdown automata and finite-turn pushdown automata augmented with reversal-bounded counters (
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
). The first characterization uses a restriction of multi-tape nondeterministic finite automata, and the second uses multi-tape reversal-bounded multi-counter machines. This result generalizes a known characterization for linear context-free grammars.

We then studied the complexity of the languages accepted by
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
. We showed that
finite-turn
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
languages are in
{\mathsf{NLOG}}
. For (unrestricted)
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
, the languages they accept are in
{\mathsf{P}}
, and they also can be accepted by
\log^{2}n
-space-bounded deterministic Turing machines, matching the space complexity of
the languages accepted by pushdown automata.
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
is an interesting class as it accepts more languages than both reversal-bounded multicounter machines and pushdown automata, but also has a
{\mathsf{coNP}}
-complete boundedness and emptiness problems.
We then studied extensions to one- and two-way
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
, where the pushdown can flip a finite number of times.

1
.
In
[
17
]
, the complexity of some of the same machine models studied here was also studied. The decision problems that were analyzed included the non-emptiness problem and the general membership problem. The general membership problem (sometimes called the uniform membership problem in the literature) is, “given both the machine
M
and
w
as inputs, determine whether
M
accepts
w
?”. In
[
17
]
,
it was shown that for any type of machine model, the complexity of general membership for the two-way machine model is polynomial-time equivalent to the complexity of general membership for the one-way machine model which is in turn polynomial-time equivalent to the non-emptiness problem for the one-way machine model. This allows us to fill in the complexity for the general membership problem for most machine models studied by using the complexity for the non-emptiness problem. and
{\mathsf{NPCM}}
