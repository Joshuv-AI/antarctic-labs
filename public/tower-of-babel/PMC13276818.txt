Navigating the diagnostic ‘gray zone’: prospective evaluation of an integrated MRI-Biomarker model for renal allograft triage
Authors: Zhouyan Liao, Siyuan Ma, Ziyi Liu, Hao Tang, Baodi Deng, Jie Zhang, Ting Yin, Wei Chen, Omar Darwish, Qiuxia Wang, Gen Chen, Jeong Min Lee, Zhen Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA critical dilemma in post-transplant surveillance is determining when minor functional changes (subtle serum creatinine fluctuations or slow renal function decline) or ambiguous ultrasound findings (including mildly elevated resistive index or cortical echogenicity) warrant invasive biopsy and treatment modification. We aimed to develop a non-invasive integrated tool combining advanced diffusion MRI with functional biomarkers to stratify patients within this diagnostic gray zone.Materials and MethodsIn this prospective study, 120 renal transplant recipients underwent multi-b-value diffusion-weighted imaging at 3.0 T within 48 h prior to an allograft biopsy. Quantitative parameters of six diffusion MRI models were extracted from renal cortex and medulla. Clinical indicators, including serum HCO3-, were recorded. To establish a clear endpoint, patients were categorized into a Treatment-Changed group (presence of actionable active pathology, requiring modified therapies) and a Treatment-Unchanged group (normal or solely chronic/non-reversible lesions). Multivariable logistic regression identified independent predictors. Diagnostic performance was assessed using area under the curve (AUC) using repeated stratified 5-fold cross validation with 10 repeats and bootstrap resampling. Kaplan–Meier analysis assessed the association between model-based risk and subsequent graft functional decline, defined as a 30% or greater decrease in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) from baseline.ResultsThe cortical distributed diffusion coefficient (DDC) and serum HCO3-, emerged as independent predictors of treatment-changed group. The integrated model achieved an AUC of 0.941, significantly outperforming the standard clinical reference model (eGFR, proteinuria, and ultrasound; AUC 0.563). Net reclassification improvement (0.479) demonstrated the model correctly reclassified 45.6% of patients. Furthermore, model-derived high-risk status significantly predicted long-term graft functional decline (log-rank p = 0.005).ConclusionThis integrated multiparametric protocol provides promising preliminary decision support for renal allograft surveillance. By noninvasively identifying the potential presence of actionable active pathology, this preliminary model may help optimize biopsy referrals and stratify patients who potentially require treatment modification. However, given the single-centre design, external validation is required before clinical implementation.Keywords: Kidney transplantation, clinical decision support, diffusion-weighted imaging, stretched exponential model, triage

Introduction

The long-term success of a kidney transplant hinges on vigilant surveillance [1], yet clinicians are frequently confronted by a common and consequential dilemma: how to act when routine monitoring yields ambiguous results. A slowly rising serum creatinine paired with an unremarkable ultrasound creates a state of diagnostic uncertainty [2], forcing a choice between the risks of delayed intervention for progressive injury and the burdens of unnecessary invasive biopsy [3].

Non-invasive functional MRI has emerged to bridge this diagnostic gap, with Diffusion-Weighted Imaging (DWI) serving as the most promising candidate. We specifically prioritized DWI because, among the spectrum of functional modalities, it represents the most mature and clinically accessible sequence [4–6]. Unlike experimental techniques that require specialized hardware, DWI is universally available on standard clinical scanners and offers superior reproducibility, making it uniquely suited for widespread adoption. In particular, DWI [7] enables the simultaneous assessment of tissue microstructure and the local functional environment[8], directly aligning with the pathophysiology of allograft injury (e.g. rejection, fibrosis [9]). Recent investigations, including those employing sophisticated multi-parametric models [10], have successfully characterized microstructural heterogeneity at a cellular level [11] and validated its utility for grading fibrosis [12].

However, despite its technical maturity and biological validity, a critical translational barrier remains: prior research has predominantly focused on retrospective histologic correlation [13–15]. Although this step is necessary for biological validation, it is insufficient for driving clinical adoption. The pressing unmet need extends beyond merely finding a better imaging correlate of tissue pathology, the field requires a practical tool that directly informs subsequent clinical action by identifying conditions that necessitate an immediate change in management. To achieve clinical viability, such a tool must combine diagnostic accuracy with a streamlined workflow suitable for decision-making under uncertainty.

To address this unmet clinical need, we envision a non-invasive triage tool for broader allograft surveillance that serves as a gatekeeper prior to biopsy referral. For the present prospective study, we enrolled recipients with unexplained graft dysfunction who were already scheduled for a biopsy to ensure a rigorous histological reference. Our primary objective was to develop a decision support model integrating advanced DWI parameters and routine clinical biomarkers. Specifically, we aimed to predict the potential presence of actionable active pathology that necessitates an immediate modification of baseline therapies. By exploring the capacity to differentiate patients who potentially require therapeutic intervention from those without acute actionable findings, we seek to provide a preliminary framework to help optimize future biopsy referrals and guide clinical management within this diagnostic gray zone.

Materials and methods

Participants

This prospective study complied with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (approval number: TJ-IRB202308123). Each participant has signed the informed consent form. From September 2023 to October 2024, 120 patients were initially recruited. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) signed informed consent; (3) a clinical indication for renal allograft biopsy, as determined solely by the treating clinician and independent of this research, due to unexplained graft dysfunction or the presence of donor-specific antibodies; (4) willingness to undergo MRI examination.

Nineteen patients were excluded (15 declined biopsy and 4 had incomplete MRI examinations), leaving 101 patients for the final analysis (Figure 1). Clinical data from our hospital medical record system were recorded, including sex, age, etc.

Flowchart of the study population.

Functional stratification and sonographic assessment

Patients were descriptively stratified into superior graft function (SGF; eGFR ≥43.7 mL/min/1.73m2) and inferior graft function (IGF; eGFR <43.7 mL/min/1.73m2) groups. The threshold of 43.7 mL/min/1.73m2 was referenced from a recent publication [16], as an indicator associated with graft prognosis. Importantly, to preserve statistical power and avoid information loss, eGFR was modeled as a continuous variable in all primary inferential analyses.

Ultrasound findings were operationalized as a binary variable based on established criteria from prior transplant imaging literature [17]. Examinations were classified as ‘Normal’ only in the presence of preserved corticomedullary differentiation, normal cortical echogenicity, and a resistive index (RI) within the physiological range (≤0.8). Conversely, the ‘Abnormal’ category encompassed any structural or hemodynamic deviation, including increased cortical echogenicity, loss of differentiation, pelvicalyceal dilatation, perirenal collections, or an RI >0.8. This binary classification reflects the high-sensitivity “red flag” approach used in routine clinical triage. While this broad designation inevitably captures non-specific or chronic changes rather than definitively actionable injury, it accurately represents the real-world threshold that prompts an allograft biopsy. To overcome this inherent diagnostic limitation of ultrasound, our integrated model was evaluated against strict, pathology-driven endpoints.

Histologic ground truth and primary endpoint

Renal allograft biopsies were performed within 48 h of the MRI examination. Histological evaluation was conducted independently by an experienced renal pathologist blinded to all clinical and MRI data, adhering strictly to the Banff 2019 classification [18]. Importantly, to prevent empirical medical interventions from confounding the diffusion imaging signals or masking histological features, all MRI examinations and subsequent biopsies were performed strictly prior to any acute escalation of immunosuppression. Attending physicians, who were blinded to the MRI data, determined whether to modify the clinical treatment plan based solely on the biopsy findings. To eliminate any ambiguity in endpoint adjudication, this clinical classification was determined by multidisciplinary transplant team consensus, strictly applying predefined institutional clinical management rules to the definitive biopsy diagnoses. To reflect real-world clinical decision-making and establish a reproducible primary endpoint, participants were categorized into two groups based on the presence of actionable pathology requiring immediate therapeutic intervention.

The Treatment-Changed group (n = 57) consisted of patients whose biopsy revealed actionable active lesions that necessitated a modification of their baseline immunosuppressive or medical therapies. These active pathologies included acute rejection, active polyomavirus nephropathy, acute tubular injury, and active glomerulonephritis. Conversely, the Treatment-Unchanged group (n = 44) comprised patients whose biopsy revealed either no significant specific abnormalities or solely chronic non-reversible lesions that did not warrant an acute change or escalation in their ongoing management plans. MRI techniques

All participants underwent renal allograft MRI on a 3-Tesla scanner (MAGNETOM Skyra, Siemens Healthcare) with a body coil (18-channel). To ensure imaging quality and safety, all patients undergoing MRI examinations were required to fast for 8 h and refrain from drinking for 4 h before the examination and received education on MRI safety precautions and breathing training.

After routine coronal and axial T2-weighted imaging, DWI was acquired using a reduced field-of-view (rFOV) single-shot echo-planar imaging sequence (ZOOMit). This technique was specifically selected to minimize susceptibility artifacts and optimize signal homogeneity. Acquisition parameters included: repetition time/echo time = 7700/71 ms; field of view = 288 × 288 mm; matrix size = 120 × 120; slice thickness = 4.0 mm; slice = 36; acceleration factor = 2; direction = 4; total scan time = 10 min 16 s. Eleven b-values were applied (0, 20, 50, 80, 100, 200, 500, 800, 1000, 1500, 2000s/mm2) covering a broad diffusion spectrum. To standardize ROI placement and enhance reproducibility, a single axial slice at the renal hilum (largest parenchymal cross-section) was selected for analysis. The acquisition and ROI analysis protocols were consistent with the PARENCHIMA [4] recommendations and Quantitative Imaging Biomarker Alliance (QIBA) requirements [19] for renal diffusion MRI standardization.

Image post-processing and ROI segmentation

Diffusion-weighted images were post-processed using the BoDiLab module (MIWP-MR workstation V1.0, Chengdu Zhongying Medical Technology Co., Ltd., China) to generate parametric maps on the conventional monoexponential model (to derive ADC), as well as advanced models including intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM), diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI), stretched exponential model (SEM), fractional order calculus (FROC), and continuous-time random walk (CTRW) models. The precise equations and parameter derivations for each model are detailed in Supplementary Note S1 [12].

To ensure spatial alignment and maximize methodological reproducibility, image segmentation was performed using the well-established open-source software ITK-SNAP (version 4.2.0, www.itksnap.org) strictly after the generation of all diffusion parametric maps. Two experienced radiologists (with 2 and 9 years of experience, respectively) independently placed regions of interest (ROIs) on the axial slice with the largest renal hilum. A freehand ROI encompassing the entire cortex and three oval ROIs (>30 pixels each) within medullary pyramids were drawn, avoiding artifacts. The readers strictly avoided macroscopic focal lesions, visible blood vessels, cysts, and areas with severe susceptibility artifacts. To guarantee absolute spatial consistency across all complex diffusion models, these exact ROI masks were subsequently overlaid onto all other generated parametric maps to extract the functional metrics. Interobserver agreement was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs).

Statistical analysis

SPSS (version 26.0, IBM) and R (version 4.4.2) were used for statistical analysis and GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.1, GraphPad Software Inc.) for visualization of the results. Differences between the Treatment-Changed group and the Treatment-Unchanged group were compared using the Student-t test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the Chi-square test for categorical variables.

Comparison of diffusion models

In a dedicated analysis to identify the optimal biophysical framework, we systematically evaluated six diffusion models: mono-exponential ADC, IVIM, DKI, SEM, FROC, and CTRW. The discriminative ability of each model was individually assessed using AUC. The DeLong test was employed to statistically compare the AUCs of different diffusion models to determine if the improvement in performance was statistically significant.

Variable selection and model development

Candidate predictors included all clinical, laboratory, and ultrasound variables with established or exploratory relevance to allograft injury. To avoid overly restrictive screening, we first performed univariable logistic regression using a standard threshold of p < 0.05 to minimize the risk of missing potentially important confounders. Variables meeting this criterion were entered into a multivariable backward stepwise logistic regression using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF < 5)

A Clinical Reference Model representing the current standard of care per KDIGO and ACR guidelines (the KDIGO 2024 guideline [20] and the ACR Appropriateness Criteria 2024 [21]), was constructed a priori using eGFR, 24-hour urinary protein (24h-Upro), and ultrasonographic findings.

Model performance and internal validation

The final integrated model included variables retained after stepwise selection. The full model equation, including intercept and regression coefficients, is reported. Continuous predictors were entered without transformation, and effect sizes were expressed per clinically meaningful increment to improve interpretability. Model discrimination was assessed using AUC with 95% confidence intervals. Calibration was primarily evaluated using calibration plots, with the Hosmer–Lemeshow test reported as a supplementary metric. Clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis across a range of threshold probabilities.

Given the sample size, extensive internal validation was performed to provide an honest assessment of model performance and to rigorously correct for potential overfitting. First, we performed 1000 bootstrap resamples. Crucially, to account for the entire model building procedure, the whole variable selection process including the initial univariable screening at p < 0.05 and the subsequent backward stepwise elimination was completely repeated from scratch within each independent bootstrap sample. The selection frequency across the 1000 resamples was formally recorded to empirically assess the stability of the stepwise procedure. The optimism corrected AUC was calculated as the difference between the apparent AUC and the average optimism estimated from the bootstrap.

Second, repeated stratified 5-fold cross-validation with 10 repeats, corresponding to 50 train test splits was conducted to estimate the average diagnostic performance metrics. Finally, calibration was assessed using calibration curves across the 1000 bootstrap resamples and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test to evaluate the agreement between predicted and observed risks. Clinical utility was quantified via decision curve analysis (DCA) across a continuous range of threshold probabilities.

Incremental value and prognostic assessment

The incremental value of the final model over the Clinical Reference Model was quantified in two steps: (1) Comparison of AUCs using the DeLong test; (2) Calculation of the Categorical Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) at the optimal threshold, decomposing it into Event NRI (improved detection of actionable injury) and Non-Event NRI (improved identification of stable patients).

The model’s prognostic value was evaluated by stratifying patients into high- and low-risk groups based on its optimal cutoff. Kaplan–Meier analysis with the log-rank test was used to compare the cumulative probability of subsequent graft functional decline (≥30% eGFR drop from baseline [22]) between groups. This was compared against stratification by baseline graft function (SGF vs. IGF).

Results

Clinicopathologic characteristics of participants

A total of 101 kidney transplant recipients were included, with 57 patients in the Treatment-Changed group and 44 patients in the Treatment-Unchanged group. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. The two groups were well balanced for most variables, including age, sex, BMI, eGFR, serum creatinine, blood pressure, and the proportion of patients with hypertension or diabetes (all p > 0.05). In addition, no significant differences were observed in the type or proportion of baseline immunosuppressive regimens between the two groups.

Among the evaluated MRI parameters (Table 3), the Treatment-Changed group consistently showed lower diffusion coefficients and related metrics compared with the Treatment-Unchanged group, particularly in the renal cortex. Significant differences were observed for D-CTRWcortex, D-FROCcortex, μ-FROCcortex, DDC-SEMcortex, MD-DKIcortex and ADCcortex. Similar but less pronounced differences were seen in the medulla. Interobserver reliability assessment

The ICCs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the selected SEM model parameters were as follows: α-SEMcortex (ICC = 0.947, 95% CI: 0.921–0.964); DDC-SEMcortex (ICC = 0.915, 95% CI: 0.874–0.943); α-SEMmedulla (ICC = 0.949, 95% CI: 0.924–0.965); DDC-SEMmedulla (ICC = 0.919, 95% CI: 0.880–0.945). These results indicated excellent interobserver agreement and good measurement repeatability. As the software automatically computed the mean values of MRI parameters within each region of interest, ICC analysis was performed on representative parameters from the SEM model to reflect consistency across measurements.

Comparison of diffusion models

The diagnostic performance of the six diffusion models in discriminating the Treatment-Changed group was evaluated and compared based on their AUC values. Among all models, the SEM demonstrated superior performance with an AUC of 0.912. The FROC model ranked second with an AUC of 0.741, followed by the CTRW model (AUC = 0.701). The conventional models, including IVIM (AUC = 0.684), DKI (AUC = 0.680), and mono-exponential model (AUC = 0.667), showed comparatively lower discriminative ability (Figure 4(A)).

In the multivariable analysis, only HCO3- (OR, 0.658; 95% CI: 0.532–0.813; p < 0.001) and DDC-SEMcortex (OR, 0.003; 95% CI: 0–0.053; p < 0.001) remained independent predictors of the Treatment-Changed group. The final integrated diagnostic model is explicitly defined by the following logistic regression equation: Logit (P) = 21.908 - (5.938 ×DDC-SEMcortex) - (0.418 ×HCO3-). Where 21.908 represents the model intercept, −5.938 is the adjusted regression coefficient for cortical DDC-SEM, and −0.418 is the adjusted regression coefficient for serum HCO3-. This indicates that every 0.1 × 10−3 mm2/s improvement in cortical DDC-SEM decreases the odds of requiring a treatment change by 45%.

The optimal cut-off value determined by the Youden Index yielded a sensitivity of 70.2%, a specificity of 95.5%, and an overall accuracy of 81.2% (Table 6). The positive and negative predictive values were 95.2% and 71.2%, respectively, indicating stable diagnostic performance for identifying patients requiring treatment modification.

Internal validation results

Model calibration was good, confirmed visually by calibration plots and statistically by a non significant Hosmer–Lemeshow test (p = 0.684), indicating a high degree of agreement between predicted and observed risks. Across these resamples, DDC-SEMcortex was selected in 99.3% and serum HCO3- in 96.3% of bootstrap samples, confirming that the two predictors are highly stable despite the stepwise algorithm (Table S1). Bootstrap internal validation that repeated the entire stepwise selection process in 1000 bootstrap resamples yielded an apparent AUC of 0.941, and an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.87 (Figure 5(A)). The average optimism was 0.071, indicating a moderate degree of overfitting.

Internal validation and stability assessment of the logistic regression model.

(A) Density distribution of Area Under the Curve (AUC) across 1000 bootstrap resamples. This plot illustrates the rigorous internal validation process utilized to quantify and subsequently correct for model overoptimism. The Apparent AUC distribution (blue curve) represents the diagnostic performance when the model is both trained and evaluated on the identical bootstrap sample. This distribution predictably shifts towards artificially high values, visually capturing the algorithmic overfitting inherent to data driven stepwise selection in limited samples. Conversely, the Test AUC distribution (red curve) represents the true diagnostic performance when that exact same bootstrap derived model is evaluated against the original complete patient cohort. The spatial shift between the peaks of these two distributions quantifies the statistical optimism. By calculating this difference and subtracting it from the apparent performance of our original model, we derived the final optimism-corrected AUC, ensuring a transparent and objective assessment of out-of-sample generalizability.

The repeated stratified 5-fold cross-validation (50 folds in total) yielded a mean AUC of 0.938 ± 0.048 (Figure 5(B)), with mean sensitivity of 0.867 ± 0.105 and mean specificity of 0.850 ± 0.118. The decision curve analysis showed a consistent net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities (Figure 5(C)). Survival analysis of predicting graft deterioration

Over the designated study period, the median follow-up duration for the entire patient cohort was 14 months (Interquartile Range: 6 to 19 months). The overall cumulative incidence of the primary outcome (event proportion) across the study population was 27.7%. Patients who did not experience the primary endpoint by the end of the study period, or those who were lost to routine clinical monitoring, were administratively censored at the date of their last documented clinical encounter. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated a significant divergence in survival-free-from-functional-decline between the high- and low-risk groups as defined by our model (Log-rank p = 0.005; Figure 6(A)). In contrast, stratification by baseline graft function (SGF vs. IGF groups) showed no significant association with functional decline (Log-rank p = 0.554; Figure 6(B)).

Discussion

This study developed a preliminary non-invasive triage model designed to assist in identifying renal transplant recipients who potentially require immediate management modification. By anchoring our analysis to “the Treatment-Changed group” rather than isolated histologic correlation, our integrated model (cortical DDC-SEM and HCO3-) achieved strong discriminative ability (AUC 0.941). Functioning as a potential clinical decision-support filter, this tool aims to optimize the diagnostic pathway by assisting in prioritizing biopsy decisions for high-risk cases while supporting routine patient management for low-risk patients.

The selection of cortical DDC-SEM as the optimal imaging biomarker is grounded in its unique ability to quantify the microstructural heterogeneity characteristic of injured renal allografts. Unlike native kidneys, injured allografts typically exhibit a complex admixture of edema, inflammation, and fibrosis [23]. Our systematic comparison confirmed that the SEM is practical and superior to other methods for this specific application. It captures a continuous distribution of diffusion rates, providing a more reliable measure of early structural disorder than mono-exponential DWI [12,24], which oversimplifies the tissue environment. Furthermore, SEM demonstrated greater stability than IVIM, as the distinction between perfusion and diffusion in allografts is frequently obscured by significant tissue edema [6,25]. Additionally, SEM proved more resilient to noise than DKI [26] and avoided the overfitting risks associated with higher-order models such as FROC and CTRW [14,27].

The independent selection of serum HCO3- highlights its unique value in identifying early allograft stress. Unlike serum creatinine, which reflects global filtration, a decline in HCO3- serves as a sensitive sentinel for renal tubular acidosis, detecting subtle tubular dysfunction that often precedes glomerular filtration decline [28]. Furthermore, this acidosis actively exacerbates injury by promoting pro-fibrotic and inflammatory pathways [29,30]. Therefore, the inclusion of HCO3- allows our model to detect the initial functional derangement that precedes and contributes to the structural damage quantified by DDC [31], thereby offering a more proactive window for intervention [32].

To address the potential for overfitting in this single-center cohort, model stability was evaluated using repeated cross-validation and extensive bootstrap resampling. DDC-SEMcortex and HCO3- were retained as independent predictors in nearly all bootstrap iterations, confirming favorable algorithmic stability. Although our internal validation quantified a moderate degree of optimism expected in such exploratory studies, the adjusted discriminative ability remained stable even after stringent optimism correction. The model also demonstrated good calibration, reflecting reliable agreement between predicted and observed clinical risks. Furthermore, decision curve analysis indicated that utilizing this preliminary model to guide biopsy decisions would offer a higher net clinical benefit than standard reference strategies across a wide range of clinically relevant thresholds. While these internal validation results are highly encouraging, independent external validation is still required before routine clinical application.

The value of this framework extends beyond immediate diagnosis to long-term prognostication. The risk stratification defined by our model was significantly associated with subsequent graft functional decline, a potential predictive capability often absent in conventional static functional metrics (such as delayed graft function). This suggests that our model captures active, progressive pathophysiological processes rather than static functional states. The ability to identify this high-risk phenotype early in the disease course could offer a valuable opportunity for preemptive intervention and optimized resource allocation [12,33].

The ultimate utility of any new tool is measured against current practice. Our preliminary model demonstrated substantial incremental value over the standard clinical-imaging workup (AUC 0.941 vs. 0.563). Net reclassification improvement index (0.479) confirmed that it correctly reclassified a significant proportion of patients, primarily by better identifying patients who potentially require intervention. Therefore, this tool is best positioned not as a replacement for first-line surveillance (e.g. ultrasound, eGFR), but as a targeted, second step problem-solving instrument for the common diagnostic gray zone. Pending future external validation, its strategic integration into existing care pathways could ultimately enhance the precision of long term surveillance, optimize biopsy yields, and support routine patient management while remaining fully aligned with established clinical guidelines.

Limitations

Several important limitations should be acknowledged. First, our analysis was based on a biopsy referred cohort, meaning all included patients presented with prior clinical indications that warranted further invasive assessment. While this approach ensures a highly relevant clinical sample, we recognize the inherent potential for spectrum bias associated with this specific study design. Consequently, the applicability of our integrated model to completely asymptomatic outpatient populations remains to be prospectively confirmed. Second, we note that serum bicarbonate levels may be influenced by oral alkali supplementation or diuretic use, and these medication details were not systematically recorded in this prospective cohort. Nevertheless, a post hoc sensitivity analysis excluding bicarbonate from the model yielded a DDC-SEMcortex based AUC of 0.892, confirming that the imaging biomarker provides incremental predictive value independent of acid base status. Future prospective studies should consider blinded collection of laboratory data to further mitigate any potential circularity concerns.

In addition to these design considerations, several statistical limitations merit attention. Despite rigorous internal bootstrap validation, residual model optimism and statistical instability arising from extreme values in sparse predictors remain potential concerns. Most importantly, the absence of external validation in independent multi institutional cohorts represents a major limitation of the present work. Before this promising preliminary model can be considered for clinical translation, its generalizability and diagnostic accuracy must be confirmed in external populations. While this single representative slice strategy was carefully selected as a practical compromise to minimize scan time and motion artifacts in this preliminary cohort, it is inherently acknowledged as a limitation relative to multi slice or whole graft volumetric analysis, which should be considered in future external validation studies.

Conclusion

This study introduces a preliminary integrated multiparametric diffusion MRI model to support routine surveillance in renal transplant recipients. By focusing on clinically actionable interventions rather than histologic correlations alone, the model shows reasonable diagnostic and prognostic performance. It may serve as a potential triage adjunct to assist in prioritizing biopsy decisions in cases of diagnostic uncertainty. However, these findings highlight the strict necessity for external validation in independent cohorts before any routine clinical implementation can be considered. Nevertheless, this approach offers a practical step toward more informed allograft management and timely therapeutic decisions.
