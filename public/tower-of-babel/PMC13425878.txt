Evolutionary characteristics and implementation effects of China’s low-carbon policy system: a quantitative analysis based on policy texts
Authors: Liu Xuexin, Jiang Lihua, Kong Xiaoxu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
China’s low-carbon policy system is a key institutional arrangement for advancing carbon disclosure, promoting green transformation, and supporting the achievement of the “dual-carbon”goals. However, existing studies have paid limited attention to the policy system itself as an integrated and evolving object of analysis. To address this gap, this paper develops a three-dimensional analytical framework of policy instruments, policy objectives, and policy effectiveness, and applies content analysis and temporal interval analysis to 1008 coded policy documents issued between 2007 and 2024. The results show that first, policy instruments are structurally imbalanced, with environment-type instruments dominating the policy mix, while supply-side and demand-side instruments remain relatively insufficient. Second, policy objectives are primarily oriented toward the national “dual-carbon”strategy, with comparatively limited attention to corporate sustainable development and investors’ low-carbon information needs. Third, policy effectiveness is unevenly distributed, as most policy texts are concentrated in provincial-level guiding documents, whereas high-authority policy documents remain limited. Fourth, China’s low-carbon policy system exhibits a clear three-stage evolution, moving from initial exploration to system construction and deepening, and then to adaptation and innovation. Finally, broader trend-based evidence suggests that changes in carbon emissions, green finance, and firm-level green transformation are broadly aligned with the direction of China’s low-carbon policy development. By shifting the analytical focus from isolated policy measures to the policy system itself, this study provides a more systematic basis for understanding the internal logic and evolutionary dynamics of China’s low-carbon governance, while also offering policy implications for China and institutional reference for other developing countries.

Introduction

Amid the escalating global climate crisis, carbon emissions and their environmental impacts have become a focal point for the international community [1, 2]. To address this challenge, nations worldwide have successively announced carbon neutrality targets and progressively been refining carbon disclosure policies to accelerate the green transformation of businesses and society [3]. As the world’s largest carbon emitter, China formally committed in 2020 to achieving "carbon peaking by 2030 and carbon neutrality by 2060"—a strategic milestone underscoring its decisive steps toward climate mitigation and sustainable development. Against this backdrop, China’s low-carbon policies, as pivotal instruments for realizing the "dual-carbon" goals, have garnered increasing attention from policymakers and academia.

In recent years, the Chinese government has introduced a series of low-carbon regulations, including The Interim Regulations on Carbon Emission Trading Management and The Measures for the Mandatory Disclosure of Enterprise Environmental Information. These policies aim to standardize corporate carbon disclosure practices to enhance their transparency. Following the 20th Central Committee’s Third Plenary Session, China has further clarified its green development roadmap by proposing to: China will build a circular economy with low-carbon energy systems, restructure industrial and energy sectors, and strengthen the "dual-control" mechanism for total carbon emissions and intensity. These measures not only establish a legal foundation for carbon disclosure but also provide actionable guidance for enterprises and stakeholders to participate in emission reduction efforts [4, 5]. Carbon disclosure is not only a critical reflection of corporate environmental responsibility but also serves as a key basis for national macro-level decision-making. By disclosing carbon-related information, enterprises can enhance the transparency of their carbon footprints, provide foundational data for carbon trading, regulation, and verification, and thereby advance carbon emission reduction and sustainable development [6, 7]. However, despite the continuous improvement of the policy framework, carbon disclosure still faces severe operational challenges in practice, such as inconsistent reporting standards [8], corporate greenwashing, and insufficient voluntary participation [9]. More importantly, these persistent problems suggest that the key issue lies not merely in disclosure behavior at the firm level, but in whether the low-carbon policy system itself has formed a coherent and effective institutional arrangement capable of coordinating policy instruments, policy objectives, and implementation authority.

From a theoretical perspective, a critical gap remains in the existing literature on China’s low-carbon governance. Although a growing body of research has examined China’s low-carbon development, most studies have focused on three aspects: the classification and distribution of policy tools, the implementation paths of low-carbon policies, and the micro-level effects of such policies on corporate behavior, industrial upgrading, energy efficiency, and emission reduction [10]. By contrast, insufficient attention has been paid to the low-carbon policy system as an integrated object of analysis. In particular, existing studies have not yet adequately explained how the policy framework itself is internally structured, how its different elements interact, and how it has evolved over time. As a result, the policy system is often treated as a static institutional background rather than as an evolving governance structure in its own right. This theoretical gap limits a deeper understanding of the internal logic and coordination mechanisms of China’s low-carbon governance.

From a practical perspective, this gap is far from trivial. Over nearly two decades, China’s carbon disclosure and low-carbon policies have been issued incrementally by multiple central ministries, local governments, and other policy actors, resulting in a policy framework that is broad in scope but also highly layered and potentially fragmented. While the number of policy documents has expanded rapidly, persistent problems remain in practice, including inconsistent disclosure standards, uneven implementation, corporate greenwashing, and insufficient voluntary participation. These challenges suggest that the problem does not lie solely at the firm level, but may also stem from structural weaknesses within the policy system itself. If the internal configuration of the policy system remains unclear, policymakers will find it difficult to identify where institutional coordination is insufficient, where policy priorities are imbalanced, and why implementation gaps continue to persist. In this sense, studying the policy system itself is of clear practical importance for improving governance effectiveness and supporting the achievement of the dual-carbon goals. Therefore, it is necessary to move beyond general discussions of policy importance and conduct a systematic analysis of China’s low-carbon policy system, so as to clarify its internal structure, evolutionary characteristics, and practical effectiveness.

To bridge these theoretical and practical gaps, this study addresses the following three research questions:How is China’s low-carbon policy system structured, and how has it evolved over time in terms of policy instruments, policy goals, and formal policy authority?What major structural imbalances and coordination problems exist within the current policy framework, particularly in the allocation of policy instruments, the prioritization of policy goals, and the distribution of formal policy effectiveness?What do these structural characteristics and evolutionary patterns imply for the future optimization of China’s low-carbon governance, and to what extent can they provide useful institutional reference for other developing countries facing similar transition pressures?

These questions require a research design that takes the policy system itself, rather than isolated policy outcomes, as the primary object of analysis. The object of this study is not a single policy measure, a specific region, or one dimension of firm behavior, but a national policy system composed of heterogeneous policy documents issued by different authorities over a long period of time. Such an object is inherently structural, layered, and dynamic. Precisely because the research object is a policy system, a policy-text-based analytical approach is necessary: only by examining policy documents as the direct carriers of governmental intent can the study identify how institutional supply is configured, where structural imbalances are embedded, and how the policy system has evolved historically. A fragmented approach focusing only on downstream outcomes would make it difficult to reveal these internal coordination problems. Accordingly, by systematically evaluating 1008 policy documents issued between 2007 and 2024, this study seeks to clarify the internal logic, structural characteristics, and evolutionary trajectory of China’s low-carbon policy system. In doing so, it aims not only to advance the theoretical understanding of low-carbon governance as a policy system, but also to provide a more reliable diagnostic basis for continuous policy refinement. The marginal contributions of this study are threefold:

First, a three-dimensional analytical framework integrating “Policy Instruments—Policy Goals—Policy Effectiveness” has been constructed to systematically diagnose the structural configuration of China’s low-carbon policy system. Unlike prior studies that predominantly examine either the classification of policy tools or the downstream effects of policy implementation in isolation, this framework enables a holistic and multi-dimensional evaluation of the policy architecture. By simultaneously capturing the interrelationships among instruments, objectives, and effectiveness, it extends the analytical boundaries of existing research and offers a novel theoretical lens and replicable methodological template for future policy studies.

Second, this study systematically traces the evolutionary trajectory of China’s low-carbon policies, uncovering their phased characteristics and underlying developmental logic. Through quantitative content analysis of 1008 policy documents spanning 2007–2024, the research delineates three distinct developmental phases: the Initial Exploration Period (2007–2012), characterized by foundational policy formulation and pilot initiatives; the System Construction and Deepening Period (2013–2020), marked by institutionalization and the integration of market-based mechanisms such as carbon trading; and the Comprehensive Advancement Period (2021–present), distinguished by an emphasis on technological innovation and alignment with global climate governance frameworks. By meticulously examining the shifting policy priorities and instrument choices across these phases, this study offers a clear roadmap for understanding the dynamic evolution of the policy system.

Third, the study identifies specific structural imbalances within the current policy framework and proposes targeted optimization pathways. The findings reveal, for instance, a pronounced overreliance on environmental regulatory instruments (e.g., command-and-control measures) coupled with an underdevelopment of supply-side incentives (e.g., R&D subsidies, talent cultivation) and demand-side tools (e.g., green procurement, consumer incentives). Additionally, a misalignment between stated policy goals and the instruments deployed is identified. Based on these diagnostic insights, the study outlines actionable recommendations for rebalancing the policy instrument portfolio, enhancing goal-instrument coherence, and improving overall policy effectiveness. These insights not only contribute to the continuous refinement of China’s carbon disclosure mechanisms but also offer a replicable “Chinese Solution” for other developing countries confronting similar climate governance challenges.

Literature review

China’s low-carbon policy system serves as the core institutional arrangement for promoting economic green transformation, with its theoretical foundation traceable to environmental economics and sustainable development theory. China primarily implements low-carbon policies through energy conservation and emission reduction, green technology research, as well as fiscal incentives [11]. Carbon information disclosure, as a crucial means of facilitating the effective operation of a low-carbon economy, has been highly valued by many governments [12]. By making carbon information transparent through disclosure, it provides basic information for policy implementation and reinforces corporate responsibility for emission reduction. Currently, the Carbon Disclosure Project (CDP) is a globally influential platform that provides information disclosure services for investors, enterprises, and others, and is widely recognized as the "gold standard" for environmental reporting [13].

The research on low-carbon policies abroad began in the 1990s. The signing of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) in 1992 initiated the systematic process of global climate governance, while the Kyoto Protocol in 1997 first established clear and quantitative emission reduction targets. Subsequently, the implementation of the European Union Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) provided a practical model for regional low-carbon policies. In academic research, relevant studies have primarily focused on the following four areas: Firstly, research evaluated disclosure practices across different countries. Using global firm-level data, Huang et al. shows that the relationship between firms’ underlying carbon performance and disclosure differs markedly across developed and developing economies, suggesting that greenhouse gas disclosure is shaped by heterogeneous regulatory and institutional environments rather than following a uniform pattern worldwide [14, 15]. In addition, the OECD’s Global Corporate Sustainability Report 2024 shows substantial cross-country variation in the disclosure and assurance of GHG information, further indicating that reporting practices remain uneven across jurisdictions. Secondly, more recent research has examined how climate-related regulation influences market operation and disclosure governance. Existing studies suggest that mandatory climate disclosure rules can affect firms’ climate commitments, improve the credibility of disclosed information, and strengthen the role of capital markets in supporting low-carbon transition [16–18]. Thirdly, recent studies have examined the drivers of carbon-related disclosure from a more diversified perspective. Existing evidence suggests that disclosure behavior is shaped jointly by government attention, investor pressure, and ownership-related governance mechanisms, rather than by a single external force alone. For example, Zhu et al. find that government environmental attention can enhance firms’ ESG performance through channels including environmental governance information disclosure while [14, 15, 19] shows that green investors significantly promote corporate carbon disclosure. Grassi et al. [20, 21] further demonstrate that ownership structure also plays an important role in climate-related corporate reporting. Fourthly, more recent research has examined how carbon-related disclosure affects firms’ financial value and market response. Existing studies show that both voluntary and mandatory climate disclosures can shape investors’ perceptions and have measurable valuation effects. For example, Flammer et al. [22] find that firms with more voluntary climate action disclosures receive more positive stock market reactions, while Alsaifi et al. [23] shows that mandatory carbon disclosure announcements can also trigger differentiated market responses depending on firms’ carbon intensity and ownership structure. Related research further suggests that climate disclosure may lower the cost of capital and improve firm value by enhancing transparency and strengthening market confidence [24, 25]. Ganda studied the impact of carbon emission reporting on the financial value of South African companies from 2010 to 2015 and found that carbon emission disclosure was positively correlated with ROA (an accounting-based metric) but negatively correlated with MVA (a market-based metric) [26].

Domestic research on low-carbon policies commenced in the early twenty-first century, with early studies emphasizing energy conservation and efficiency enhancement, as well as exploring the localization of international experiences [27, 28]. These studies promoted low-carbon development through management measures such as energy efficiency and emission standards in diverse industries and the imposition of carbon taxes [29, 30]. Policy focus has been gradually shifted towards low-carbon development, technology, and green economy [31, 32], forming a vertical policy system encompassing overall strategies, basic policies, and specific policies, as well as an interconnected horizontal policy system, to actively deal with global warming [33]. In later stages, China established a timeline for peaking carbon emissions and achieving carbon neutrality, setting new constraints for green and low-carbon policies. During this period, scholars conducted quantitative analyses of China’s "dual carbon" constraints by studying the relationship between carbon information disclosure policies and enterprises. Research has shown that carbon information disclosure plays a crucial role in helping the government reasonably to control pollution [34, 35], to strengthen law enforcement and supervision of enterprises [36, 37], to reduce capital and financing costs [38, 39], and to assist investors in assessing corporate carbon performance [40, 41].

Although low-carbon policies are of great significance in promoting enterprises’ low-carbon transformation and addressing climate change, current related research still has some deficiencies. Firstly, there are limitations in the scopes of research. Existing studies mostly focus on cases in specific regions or individual enterprises, lacking comprehensive and systematic research on low-carbon policies from a national-level and policy perspective. Secondly, policy and theoretical research are insufficient. In-depth analysis of low-carbon policies is lacking, and a systematic theoretical framework is absent, especially in research on the theoretical foundation, policy tools, policy objectives, and policy effectiveness of carbon information disclosure. Finally, there is a lack of evaluation of policy trends and effects. The academic community has relatively insufficient research on the trends of national and local low-carbon policies, and there is a significant shortage in the evaluation of the effectiveness of existing policies and the prediction of future policy directions. Based on this, this paper constructs a three-dimensional analysis framework of "policy tools—policy objectives—policy effectiveness", adopts content analysis and social network analysis methods, and uses NVivo12 software to conduct a quantitative analysis of China’s carbon information disclosure policy documents. It systematically combs through the development processes of China’s carbon information disclosure policies, reveals their stage characteristics and internal logic, deeply explores the implementation effects of low-carbon policies, and puts forward suggestions for optimizing future policy development. The aim is to provide theoretical support and practical guidance for improving China’s low-carbon policy system.

Materials and method

Data sources of China’s low-carbon policies

The data in this paper are primarily sourced from DRCnet, the database platform of the Development Research Center of the State Council, together with the New Think Tank Service Platform1. All the low-carbon policies promulgated by China’s central government and provinces from June 2007 to May 2024 are selected as the analysis texts. The screening of policy documents is divided into three steps: Firstly, the domestic central policies and local policies are collected through the 30·60 Dual Carbon Strategy Research and Decision-making Support Platform; Secondly, the policies are screened based on objectives and themes. Policy documents containing keywords such as "carbon information", "carbon disclosure", "carbon emission", "carbon footprint", "carbon peak", "carbon neutrality", and "carbon market" are searched, and texts irrelevant to the research theme are eliminated. Some policies only mention "green and low-carbon" without further elaboration, and these texts are also excluded; Finally, in order to conduct statistical analysis more effectively, the policy data in this paper are classified according to the voluntariness of disclosure (voluntary or involuntary) and the type of disclosure (government-led or market-driven). After the above screening, a total of 1347 policy documents are finally obtained, including forms such as notices, opinions, and plans. The 1347 documents constituted the initial policy corpus used for descriptive statistics and word-frequency analysis. Before the formal coding stage, all documents were further subjected to a full-text relevance review based on the three-dimensional analytical framework of policy instruments, policy objectives, and policy effectiveness, in order to identify the final coding sample. Among them, a total of 144 national policies issued by the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China, the State Council, and various ministries and commissions account for 10.7%; The policies issued by provinces are mainly specific measures to implement national decisions, with a total of 1,203 items, accounting for 89.3%.

A word frequency analysis has been conducted on 1347 low-carbon policy texts, and a word cloud has been generated after removing meaningless single characters and numbers (see Fig. 1). The word cloud graph intuitively displays the main concerns of the policy texts, including carbon information, carbon disclosure, carbon emissions, carbon footprint, carbon peak, carbon neutrality, carbon market, and carbon trading, etc. This indicates that the collected textual materials are highly consistent with the research theme of this study.Fig. 1Word cloud of China’s low-carbon policies

Analysis of the subjects of China’s low-carbon policies

By further analyzing the issuing subjects of the 1347 policy documents issued by the central government and national ministries and commissions, it is found that a small number of policies are jointly issued by multiple policy subjects, while the majority of policies are independently issued by a single subject. Specifically, low-carbon policies involve multiple government agencies such as the National Development and Reform Commission, the Ministry of Ecology and Environment, the Ministry of Finance, and provincial and municipal governments. Due to the large number of policy documents, this paper divides them into national policies and local policies. Among them, there are 144 national policies and 1,203 local policies in total.

Among the 144 national policy documents, 103 policies were issued independently, accounting for 71.5%; and 41 policies were jointly issued, accounting for 28.5%. Among the national policies issued independently, the Ministry of Ecology and Environment issued 30 policies, which accounted for the highest proportion; followed by the National Development and Reform Commission issuing 25 policies, the State Council issuing 9 policies, the Ministry of Finance issuing 6 policies, the Ministry of Education issuing 4 policies. Departments such as the China National Light Industry Council, the National Energy Administration, and the State Administration for Market Regulation issued relatively few policies, with a total of 29 policies combined. However, it must be explicitly clarified that the China National Light Industry Council is an industry association rather than a formal governmental administrative body equivalent to national ministries. To prevent substantive misclassification and ensure the rigorousness of the policy effectiveness evaluation (Dimension Z), documents issued by such non-administrative industry associations were strictly excluded from the final analytical sample during the subsequent screening process (as detailed in the exclusion criteria in Sect. "Encoding of policy texts").

Among the 1,203 local policy documents, 971 policies were issued independently, accounting for 80.7%; and 232 policies were jointly issued, accounting for 19.3%. Among the local policies issued independently, provincial and municipal governments issued 402 policies, which accounted for the highest proportion; followed by provincial and municipal Development and Reform Commissions issuing 238 policies, provincial and municipal Ecological Environment Bureaus issuing 131 policies, provincial and municipal Departments of Housing and Urban–rural Development issuing 18 policies, provincial and municipal Departments of Industry and Information Technology issuing 14 policies. Departments such as Provincial Forestry Bureaus, government offices for Official Business Management, and Market Supervision Bureaus issued relatively few policies, with a total of 168 policies combined.

Construction of the three-dimensional analysis framework of China’s low-carbon policies

This study builds upon Howlett’s typology of policy instruments—namely supply-side, demand-side, and environmental instruments—to delineate the policy tool dimension (2005). It further incorporates Peng Jisheng’s analytical approach to assessing policy effectiveness to define the effectiveness dimension (2008). To address the limitation of existing analytical frameworks that tend to “underscore instruments and effectiveness while underemphasizing policy goals,” this study introduces an additional dimension—policy goals—and thereby develops a three-dimensional framework for the systematic evaluation of carbon disclosure policies (see Fig. 2).Fig. 2Three-dimensional analytical framework for China’s low-carbon policy texts

The proposed framework comprises three interrelated dimensions: policy instruments, policy goals, and policy effectiveness. The policy instrument dimension (X-axis) includes the three categories of supply-side, demand-side, and environmental tools. The policy goal dimension (Y-axis) centers on three fundamental objectives: aligning with China’s national “dual-carbon” strategy, meeting investors’ demand for low-carbon information, and fostering firms’ sustainable development. The policy effectiveness dimension (Z-axis) classifies the actual impact of policy implementation into five levels. The following section provides a detailed exposition of the analytical framework and its constituent elements.

X dimension: policy tools dimension

Based on the policy tools theory and referring to [42], as well as [43], this study incorporates supply-type tools, demand-type tools, and environment-type tools into the analysis framework of low-carbon policies, and takes them as the three aspects of investigation in the X dimension respectively. Demand-type policy tools refer to the policies issued by the government in the face of demands for carbon emission reduction and achievements of carbon neutrality, targeting various subjects such as the government, enterprises, universities, scientific research institutions, users, and the public. Y dimension: policy objectives dimension

Theoretically, this study draws upon a three-tier analytical framework encompassing the state-market-firm levels. Corresponding to the macro-, meso-, and micro-level logics, the policy goal dimension (Y-axis) is further decomposed into three core objectives: aligning with the national “dual-carbon” strategy, responding to investors’ low-carbon information needs, and promoting corporate sustainable development. This multi-level structure enables a more comprehensive assessment of the directional role of carbon disclosure policies.

At the state (macro) level, the objective of aligning with the national “dual-carbon” strategy refers to leveraging carbon disclosure policies to facilitate the achievement of China’s carbon-peaking and carbon-neutrality goals. This goal underscores the macro-level function of policy instruments in guiding enterprises and society toward emission reduction.

At the market (meso) level, responding to investors’ low-carbon information needs aims to enhance transparency so that investors obtain sufficient, reliable carbon-related data to support investment decisions consistent with the low-carbon transition.

At the firm (micro) level, promoting corporate sustainable development highlights the role of policy in incentivizing enterprises to strengthen their carbon management capacity, thereby achieving coordinated economic, environmental, and social benefits.

Z dimension: policy effectiveness dimension

Peng Jisheng et al.’s framework incorporates multiple dimensions of policy characteristics, including policy intensity, policy scope, and policy timeliness (2008). In the context of carbon disclosure policies, this study uses administrative hierarchy primarily as a proxy for formal policy authority and institutional scope. National-level policies generally possess broader jurisdiction and stronger formal binding force, whereas provincial-level policies are limited to subnational application. Therefore, administrative hierarchy provides a relatively stable and comparable basis for classifying the formal strength of policy texts in a large-scale national sample.

At the same time, this study does not assume that higher-level documents always generate stronger implementation effects in practice. In some cases, provincial or local policies may contain more specific enforcement requirements, clearer responsibilities, or stricter implementation arrangements than higher-level national framework documents. Therefore, the hierarchy-based Z-dimension should be understood as capturing the formal authority and potential institutional force of a policy, rather than serving as a complete measure of actual implementation effectiveness.

Despite this limitation, the hierarchy-based classification remains appropriate for the present study because it ensures coding consistency and cross-document comparability across a large number of policy texts issued by different authorities over time. It is particularly suitable for identifying the formal distribution of policy strength within the national policy system. Moreover, the completeness of alternative dimensions is limited in subnational carbon disclosure documents: only 15% explicitly specify policy timeliness, and merely 30% mention the scope of application. Such data constraints hinder the reliable quantification of these dimensions. Results and analysis

Encoding of policy texts

This study examines China’s carbon disclosure policies and systematically collects a total of 1347 policy documents issued at both the central and local levels from authoritative government sources to ensure data reliability and rigor. Based on the pre-established three-dimensional analytical framework—comprising policy instruments, policy goals, and policy effectiveness—the research team conducted a close reading of all documents and identified 1008 policies that are highly relevant to the research topic as the final analytical sample. Specifically, 339 documents were excluded after the full-text relevance review. For transparency and reproducibility, each excluded document was assigned to one primary exclusion category according to its main reason for removal. Among them, 165 documents were excluded because they contained only broad references to “green” or “low-carbon” development without substantive provisions related to carbon information disclosure, carbon governance, or implementation mechanisms. This category also included notices issued by industry associations or other non-administrative organizations that mentioned low-carbon development only in general terms but did not provide substantive and codable policy content, such as the notice issued by the China National Light Industry Council. In addition, 130 documents were excluded because they were procedural, forwarding, or duplicate notices that largely repeated higher-level policy content without introducing new actionable policy instruments or regulatory requirements. The remaining 44 documents were excluded because, although they mentioned relevant keywords, they did not contain analyzable text units that could be meaningfully coded under any of the three dimensions of the analytical framework. Therefore, only documents containing substantive and codable policy content were retained in the final coding sample of 1008 documents.

The core methodological approach is content analysis. To ensure scientific rigor and coding reliability, a coding team was formed, consisting of two doctoral candidates specializing in environmental management and one associate professor in public policy. The coding procedure followed a strict multi-step protocol. First, a detailed Coding Manual was developed to standardize all procedures. The Coding Manual specified not only the conceptual definitions of each category, but also the operational procedures for identifying coding units, assigning codes, and resolving overlaps across categories. For each category, the manual included definition statements, inclusion and exclusion criteria, representative keywords, and illustrative examples extracted from the policy texts. To improve coding transparency, explicit decision rules were established for policy sentences that could plausibly fit more than one category. In the policy instrument dimension, coders identified the dominant policy function of a sentence rather than relying solely on surface wording. Provisions primarily aimed at creating an external institutional environment through planning, regulation, taxation, or public services were coded as environmental instruments. Provisions mainly providing direct resource inputs—such as funding, infrastructure, platforms, talent, or technological support—were coded as supply-side instruments. Provisions mainly intended to stimulate market demand or actor participation through procurement, demonstration, guidance, or cooperation were coded as demand-side instruments. When a sentence contained more than one policy function, a primary code was assigned according to the most direct policy action, while a secondary code was added only when the functions were substantively independent and equally salient. Similar principles were applied to the policy goal dimension, where each sentence was classified according to its most immediate target at the macro, meso, or micro level. Specifically, sentences were coded as serving the national “dual-carbon” strategy when their primary purpose was macro-level emission governance, as meeting investors’ low-carbon information needs when they mainly enhanced information transparency for capital markets, and as promoting corporate sustainable development when they primarily aimed to strengthen firms’ internal green management and long-term sustainability. By contrast, the policy effectiveness dimension was coded solely according to the issuing authority and legal-administrative rank of the document; therefore, multiple coding was not permitted in this dimension. Second, an independent pilot coding was conducted on 10% of the sample (101 documents), followed by the calculation of Krippendorff’s alpha to assess intercoder reliability. The results indicate high reliability across all dimensions—policy instruments (α = 0.87), policy goals (α = 0.85), and policy effectiveness (α = 0.91)—all exceeding the acceptable threshold of 0.80, thereby confirming the robustness of the coding results. Third, upon confirming reliability, the full sample was coded. Disagreements were resolved through a two-step adjudication process. The two doctoral coders first re-examined disputed cases against the Coding Manual and attempted to reach consensus. If disagreement persisted, the associate professor reviewed the case and made the final decision. Resolved boundary cases were subsequently incorporated into the Coding Manual as reference examples for the remaining coding process. Coding was performed in accordance with the rules of “policy number–article–sub-article” and “policy number–article.”For example, when a policy sentence simultaneously referred to target-setting and financial support, coders distinguished between institutional orientation and direct resource provision. If the core clause emphasized planning requirements or regulatory expectations, the sentence was coded primarily as an environmental instrument. If it focused on subsidies, earmarked funds, or direct support arrangements, it was coded primarily as a supply-side instrument. Dual coding was retained only when both functions were explicitly stated and operated independently within the same provision.

After coding all relevant provisions, each coding unit was mapped onto the three-dimensional analytical framework and categorized accordingly. If a policy provision corresponded to more than one coding unit, additional coding layers were assigned. This process resulted in the construction of a comprehensive coding matrix for China’s carbon disclosure policy texts. In particular, the excessive use of environment-type policy tools and the serious shortage of supply-type policy tools indicate that the policy tools have not fully exerted their complementary functions and the synergy among them is not strong, thus affecting the overall development of China’s low-carbon policies.

To deeply understand the root cause of this structural imbalance—where environmental tools dominate (52%) while supply-side tools are relatively scarce (15.5%)—it is essential to contextualize this phenomenon within China’s specific governance structure. This imbalance can be largely attributed to the institutional logic of China’s top-down ‘Target Responsibility System’ for environmental governance. Under this mechanism, the central government assigns rigid decarbonization targets, and local governments face strict performance evaluations. To swiftly meet these political mandates, local authorities exhibit a strong institutional preference for environmental tools (such as setting emission limits and administrative regulations). These regulatory tools are administratively efficient to issue and can immediately transfer the compliance pressure to enterprises without requiring upfront governmental expenditure. Conversely, supply-side tools—such as direct financial subsidies, tax incentives, and infrastructure investments—impose substantial fiscal burdens. Given the widespread constraints on local fiscal budgets and the prolonged feedback cycles of supply-side investments, policymakers are naturally incentivized to rely on low-cost administrative regulations rather than fiscally demanding supply-side support. This underlying governance structural feature fundamentally explains the deficit of market-enabling tools in the current policy mix.

Discussion

To further deepen the understanding of China’s carbon disclosure policies and ensure analytical continuity with the preceding sections, this study integrates the subsequent analysis into a coherent research loop consisting of static feature identification, dynamic evolution tracking, and empirical effectiveness assessment. The earlier section employed the three-dimensional analytical framework to systematically depict the “static” distributional characteristics of China’s carbon disclosure policies across the dimensions of policy instruments, policy goals, and policy effectiveness.

Building on this foundation, the present section shifts the focus to the “dynamic” evolution of the policy system and its “practical” implementation outcomes. While the static analysis offers a snapshot of the policy system’s current configuration, examining its dynamic evolution helps uncover developmental patterns and future trajectories. Effectiveness analysis, in turn, provides a critical test of the policy’s actual impact. Only by integrating these three analytical perspectives—static assessment, dynamic evolution, and effectiveness verification—can a comprehensive evaluation of the policy system be achieved. Such an integrated approach generates more complete evidence base for policy refinement, one that simultaneously incorporates current conditions, developmental trends, and real-world performance.

The evolutionary trends of China’s low-carbon policies

To accurately trace the temporal evolution of themes within China’s carbon disclosure policy system, this study applies a time-interval analytical approach grounded in the theory of literature growth [44]. Drawing on the quantitative results derived from the three-dimensional analytical framework presented earlier, the development of China’s carbon disclosure policies is divided into three stages: an Initial Development Stage (2006–2010), a System-building and Deepening Stage (2011–2020), and an Adaptation and Innovation Stage (2021-present). (1) Initial Development Stage (2006–2010). During this nascent phase, the number of policy documents remained limited (16 in total), indicating an embryonic policy system. (2) System-building and Deepening Stage (2011–2020). With a sharp increase in policy issuances (603 documents), the carbon disclosure policy system entered a phase of rapid construction and refinement. (3) Adaptation and Innovation Stage (2021-present). As the number of newly issued policies stabilized (728 documents), the system transitioned into a new phase characterized by higher-quality development. The specific evolutionary pattern is illustrated in Fig. 7.Fig. 7The distribution and development stage division of China’s low-carbon policies from 2008 to 2024

The distribution and development stage division of China’s low-carbon policies from 2008 to 2024

Initial development period (2006–2010)

During the initial development period, with the increasingly prominent issue of global climate change, China began to recognize the profound impact of carbon emissions and climate change on the sustainable development of the economy and society. In this period, China started to attach importance to the public disclosure of environmental information and regarded it as a key measure to promote environmental protection and social supervision. In 2008, the State Environmental Protection Administration (now the Ministry of Ecology and Environment) issued the Measures for the Disclosure of Environmental Information (for Trial Implementation). This was the first time in China that the specific requirements and procedures for the disclosure of environmental information were clearly defined in the form of departmental regulations. The introduction of these measures marked an important milestone in the process of China’s environmental governance. It not only enhanced the public’s right to know about the environmental situation but also improved the transparency of enterprises’ environmental behaviors and their sense of social responsibility.

During this stage, the central and local governments worked closely together to jointly promote the construction of a policy system for environmental and social sustainable development. According to statistics, between 2006 and 2010, a total of 16 relevant policy documents were issued at both the central and local levels, covering a wide and diverse range of environmental protection. The themes of these policy documents can be summarized into three aspects: ①Energy conservation and emission reduction: By setting long-term goals, efforts were made to promote the improvement of energy efficiency and the control of greenhouse gas emissions; ②Policy guidance and incentives: Some regions took the lead in exploring pilot carbon emission trading programs, using market mechanisms to encourage enterprises to reduce emissions; ③The initial establishment of regulatory standards: Legal support was provided for energy conservation and emission reduction to ensure the effective implementation of various energy conservation measures.

System construction and deepening period (2011–2020)

During the system construction and deepening period, the severity of the global climate change issue has prompted China to have a more profound understanding of carbon emissions and their impacts on the sustainable development of the economy and society. In this stage, the Chinese government not only strengthened the supervision of carbon emissions but also actively built a comprehensive national low-carbon policy framework. The aim is to effectively control and reduce carbon emissions through institutional means and promote the transformation of the economy and society towards a green and low-carbon direction. Compared with the initial development period (2006–2010), policy activities in this stage were significantly more active. The central and local governments issued a total of 603 relevant policy documents.

The policy themes during the system construction and deepening period are diversified and in-depth, mainly including the following four aspects: ①Energy conservation and emission reduction: By introducing advanced technologies, energy conservation and emission reduction measures are implemented in key fields, and the industrial structure is promoted to be adjusted towards the direction of low consumption and low emissions; ②Intensification of policy guidance and incentives: Not only have multiple carbon emission pilot areas been established, but also the popularization and education of the public’s low-carbon awareness have been strengthened, forming a good atmosphere in which all societal entities jointly participate in it; ③Establishment of the national carbon emission trading market: A national total amount setting and quota allocation plan for carbon emission trading has been formulated, carbon emission accounting and reporting standards for enterprises in key industries have been issued, and the functions of the carbon trading registration and settlement system have been continuously improved, providing institutional guarantees and technical support for the market-oriented trading of carbon emission rights; ④Research, development and application of green technologies: China actively promotes the research, development and application of green technologies such as carbon capture, utilization and storage (CCUS), injecting new vitality into China’s green and low-carbon development.

Adaptation and innovation period (since 2021)

With China’s increasingly important status in global climate governance, the Chinese government officially put forward the "30·60" dual carbon goals at the United Nations Climate Summit, that is, it committed to achieving carbon peak before 2030 and carbon neutrality before 2060. The proposal of this goal is not only a clear plan for China’s own sustainable development path but also an important contribution to global actions to address climate change. In order to achieve this grand goal, the Chinese government has taken carbon information disclosure as one of the key means and continuously deepened and improved the relevant policy system. Up to May 2024, the Chinese government has issued a cumulative total of 728 relevant policy documents in this stage, covering multiple fields such as carbon emission management, green finance, and market mechanisms, demonstrating the Chinese government’s firm determination and practical actions in promoting the green and low-carbon transformation.

The policy themes during the adaptation and innovation period focus on five dimensions: ① China coordinately promotes energy conservation and emission reduction, pollution reduction, and carbon reduction: China is committed to developing a low-carbon, environmentally friendly, and circular economy, and manages to achieve a win–win situation between economic development and environmental protection through technological innovation and industrial upgrading; ②China comprehensively implements the action plans for carbon peak and carbon neutrality: China has clearly defined the goals and tasks at each stage to ensure the steady realization of the dual carbon goals; ③Major banks actively respond to the call of green finance: Through financial instruments such as green credit and green bonds, banks provide strong financial support for green and low-carbon projects and facilitate the green transformation of the economy; ④China improves and expands the carbon emission trading market: China strengthens the transparency and accuracy of carbon information disclosure and provides market participants with clear and reliable carbon price signals; ⑤China further strengthens the construction of laws, regulations, and supervision systems: The government has increased the supervision of enterprises’ carbon information disclosure, clearly requiring enterprises to disclose carbon information in accordance with the established regulations. For enterprises that fail to disclose carbon information as required or have false reports, the regulatory authorities will impose penalties in accordance with the law to ensure the effective implementation of the carbon information disclosure system.

The characteristics and main issues of the evolution of China’s low-carbon policies

The evolutionary characteristics of China’s low-carbon policies are mainly reflected in three aspects: the evolution of policy objectives, the enhancement of policy implementation capabilities, as well as the growth and stability of the number of policies (see Fig. 8 for details).Fig. 8Stage-based evolution of China’s low-carbon policy system

Stage-based evolution of China’s low-carbon policy system

The evolution of policy objectives

The objectives of China’s low-carbon policies have undergone a transformation from isolated points to all-inclusive whole. During the initial development period (2006–2010), the policy emphasis was mainly on reducing energy consumption and pollutant emissions to address the increasingly severe environmental pollution problem. However, with the intensification of the global climate change issue, China gradually realized that single energy conservation and emission reduction measures could no longer meet the requirements of sustainable development. Therefore, the policy objectives have gradually shifted towards more comprehensive low-carbon governance, emphasizing that while reducing carbon emissions, the control of other pollutants should also be taken into account, so as to achieve the coordinated development of the economy, society, and the environment. This evolutionary process not only reflects policymakers’ profound understanding of the balance between environmental protection and economic growth but also lays a theoretical foundation for the subsequent deepening and improvement of policies.

The enhancement of policy implementation strength

The implementation strength of China’s low-carbon policies has shown a remarkable change from weak to strong. During the initial development period (2006–2010), policies mainly focused on formulating standards and frameworks, providing basic guidance for carbon information management, but the actual implementation strength was relatively limited. When entering the system construction and deepening period (2011–2020), policies began to pay attention to the actual implementation effects. Through measures such as setting up carbon emission trading pilots and formulating industry standards, enterprises were urged to implement the goals of energy conservation and emission reduction. In the adaptation and innovation period (since 2021), the policy implementation strength has been further reinforced. The government has not only increased the supervision of carbon information disclosure but also ensured the effective implementation of policies through legislative means. For example, enterprises are clearly required to disclose carbon information in accordance with the regulations, and penalties are imposed on violations to ensure the authenticity and reliability of carbon information. This transformation indicates that policymakers not only focus on the top-level design of policies but also attach great importance to the practical implementation of policies and the evaluation of their actual effects.

The growth and stability of the number of policies

In terms of the number of policies, the development of low-carbon policies has undergone changes in three stages. During the initial development period (2006–2010), as carbon information management was still in its infancy, the number of relevant policies was relatively small, mainly focusing on the construction of the basic framework. When entering the system construction and deepening period (2011–2020), with the gradual deepening of carbon information management, the number of policies showed a rapid growth trend. The central and local governments issued a large number of relevant policy documents, covering multiple fields such as carbon emission management, green finance, and market mechanisms. After entering the adaptation and innovation period (since 2021), the number of policies has stabilized at a relatively high level. This indicates that policymakers are paying increasing attention to low-carbon governance work. At the same time, they are constantly optimizing and refining existing policies to ensure their effectiveness and sustainability. The growth and stability of the number of policies not only reflect the gradual maturity of the carbon information governance system but also demonstrate policymakers’ long-term commitment and strategic determination towards the goal of low-carbon development.

Main issues

Building upon the preceding quantitative analysis, this section synthesizes the major structural imbalances identified within China’s low-carbon policy system. These findings are derived directly from the empirical patterns documented in the earlier sections.

First, a multi-layered imbalance exists in the configuration of policy instruments. The coding results reveal that environment-type instruments account for 638 items, representing approximately 52% of the total 1226 coded policy tool applications, while supply-type tools (190 items, 15.5%)—such as specialized funding, talent development, and technology promotion—remain comparatively underdeveloped. This pattern indicates a structural preference for regulatory and goal-setting approaches over capacity-building strategies. Moreover, within the environment-type category itself, a notable internal asymmetry is evident: target planning (355 items, 55.6%) and regulatory control (183 items, 28.7%) constitute the dominant share, whereas market-based incentives such as tax preferences (7 items, 3%) and financial support mechanisms (19 items, 11.6%) are remarkably scarce. This dual imbalance—between environment-type and supply-type tools, and between regulatory and market-based instruments within the environment-type category—suggests that the policy system relies heavily on administrative enforcement while underutilizing fiscal and financial incentives to stimulate voluntary corporate participation. Prior research has observed that an excessive dependence on command-and-control instruments, without corresponding market-based facilitation, tends to generate compliance burdens rather than intrinsic motivation for enterprises, thereby contributing to insufficient voluntariness in carbon disclosure and persistent greenwashing behaviors [9, 34].

Second, the distribution of policy-issuing authorities exhibits a high degree of concentration, which may constrain implementation coordination. The analysis reveals that 79% of the sampled policies were issued by a single dominant authority category, while other issuing bodies account for substantially smaller shares (16.5%, 3.6%, 0.5%, and 0.4% respectively). This concentration pattern suggests that China’s low-carbon policy system remains heavily centralized at certain administrative levels, with limited participation from other governmental actors. While centralized policy-making can ensure strategic coherence at the national level, it may also create coordination challenges: lower-level governments may lack sufficient policy-making autonomy or targeted instruments adapted to regional circumstances, potentially leading to implementation gaps and uneven enforcement outcomes across localities [36]. The supervision difficulties widely acknowledged by practitioners may, in part, stem from this structural mismatch between centralized policy formulation and decentralized implementation requirements.

Third, the policy system’s capacity to address firm-level and investor-level needs remains insufficient. Although the overall number of policies has grown substantially, the preceding goal distribution analysis indicates that policy objectives are predominantly oriented toward macro-level strategic targets—such as carbon peaking, energy structure adjustment, and industrial upgrading—while relatively fewer policies explicitly address firm-level compliance facilitation, investor guidance, or the establishment of independent third-party verification mechanisms. This orientation gap may partially explain why, despite the proliferation of policy documents, persistent implementation challenges remain in practice, including inconsistent disclosure standards, insufficient corporate voluntariness, and the absence of reliable third-party auditing systems. When enterprises—particularly small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs)—lack targeted policy support and clear operational guidance, their capacity and willingness to participate in carbon disclosure are inevitably constrained [4].

In sum, the three structural imbalances identified above—the multi-layered asymmetry in policy instrument configuration, the concentration of policy-issuing authority, and the insufficient orientation toward firm-level and investor-level support—constitute the core challenges embedded within China’s current low-carbon policy framework. These structural features not only constrain the policy system’s internal coherence but also contribute to the downstream implementation difficulties observed in practice. Addressing these imbalances will require a more balanced deployment of policy instruments with greater emphasis on market-based incentives and demand-side tools, broader participation of diverse policy-issuing bodies to enhance local adaptability, and a strategic reorientation of policy goals toward creating enabling conditions for enterprise-level compliance and stakeholder engagement.

Trend-based evidence related to the development of China’s low-carbon policies

This section extends the analysis from policy texts to the broader empirical context in which China’s low-carbon policies have evolved. By examining changes in carbon emissions, green finance, and firm-level green transformation, it provides supplementary evidence for understanding the practical implications of policy evolution. Through this comparison, the section helps assess whether the direction of real-world trends is broadly consistent with the orientation of China’s low-carbon policy development.

Conclusions and policy recommendations

Conclusions

This paper systematically constructs a three-dimensional analysis framework of "policy tools -policy objectives-policy effectiveness". Through text analysis, it evaluates the development and effectiveness of China’s low-carbon policies, and uses the temporal interval method to reveal the evolutionary trends of low-carbon policies. The main research conclusions are as follows:

First, the use of policy tools is unbalanced. The research finds that there are significant differences in the usage proportions of the three types of policy tools, namely supply-side, demand-side, and environmental policy tools. Specifically, environmental policy tools, such as regulatory controls and target planning, have the highest frequency of use among policy tools, indicating that policymakers prefer to use indirect means to promote carbon information disclosure. In contrast, the use of supply-side and demand-side policy tools is relatively scarce, which limits the full exertion of policy impetus. In addition, the internal structure of policy tools also shows an imbalance. To be specific, supply-side policy tools mainly focus on technology promotion, while insufficient attention is paid to infrastructure construction and human resources support. Demand-side policies place more emphasis on demonstration projects and encouragement and guidance, but the roles of tools such as government procurement and communication and cooperation have not been fully demonstrated. Environmental policies mainly focus on target planning and regulatory controls, but there is insufficient support in terms of financial support and tax incentives.

Second, there are differences in the priority of policy objectives and potential gaps. The study finds that policy objectives that are consistent with the national "dual carbon" strategy dominate among all policy objectives, which reflects the government’s firm determination in achieving the goals of carbon peak and carbon neutrality. However, compared with the objectives that conform to the national strategic goals, the number of policy objectives aimed at promoting the sustainable development of enterprises and meeting the needs of investors is relatively small. Although these objectives are of crucial importance for promoting the green transformation of the economy and attracting low-carbon investment, their relatively low frequency of appearance in policy documents indicates that these objectives have not been given sufficient attention in the current policy framework. That is, in the process of formulating policy objectives, key objectives have not received the attention and resource allocation commensurate with their importance.

Third, the distribution of policy effectiveness is uneven. The research reveals that most of the policy documents concentrate on effectiveness level 2, that is, the policy documents on carbon information disclosure are mainly composed of opinions and notices issued by provincial Party committees and provincial governments. Although such policy documents have an advantage in quantity, they are inferior to policy documents at higher levels in terms of compulsoriness and implementation strength. Moreover, insufficient attention is paid to policy documents at effectiveness levels 1, 3, 4, and 5. The neglect of policy documents at high effectiveness levels will affect the comprehensive implementation and effectiveness of policies, thus limiting the overall effectiveness of the policy system.

Fourth, the cross-dimensional analysis reveals several structural mismatches within the policy system. Through the cross-analysis between the X dimension (policy tools) and the Y dimension (policy objectives), as well as the cross-analysis between the X dimension and the Z dimension (policy effectiveness), the study finds that, in terms of the policy objective promoting the sustainable development of enterprises, the technology promotion among the supply-side policy tools has a relatively strong correlation with the policy objective. In terms of the policy objective conforming to the national "dual carbon" strategy, the target planning and regulatory control tools among the environmental policy tools are more prominent. The demand-side policy tools are active in meeting the low-carbon needs of investors. In particular, the demonstration project and encouragement and guidance tools play a key role in enhancing the transparency of enterprises’ carbon information disclosure and investors’ confidence. In terms of policy effectiveness, supply-side policy tools appear more frequently in documents at high effectiveness levels, indicating that they may be more effective in ensuring policy implementation and supervision. Environmental policy tools dominate in policy documents at effectiveness level 2, reflecting their advantages in forming a broad market and public consensus. The results show that the deployment of policy instruments is not always well aligned with policy goals or formal policy authority. In particular, regulatory and planning tools are overused, while support-oriented and market-oriented instruments remain underdeveloped. At the same time, the policy system still provides limited support for firm-level and investor-level needs. These patterns point to persistent challenges in instrument coordination, goal balance, and institutional integration.

Fifth, China’s low-carbon policy system exhibits a clear three-stage evolution. From 2007 to 2024, the system moved through an initial exploration stage, a system construction and deepening stage, and a stage of adaptation and innovation. Across these stages, policy instruments became more diversified, policy goals shifted from relatively singular to more comprehensive arrangements, and the number of policies expanded rapidly before stabilizing at a relatively high level. This trajectory reflects the transformation of China’s low-carbon governance from policy initiation to system building and adaptive refinement.

Sixth, broader trend-based evidence suggests that the evolution of several macro- and firm-level indicators was broadly aligned with the direction of China’s low-carbon policy development. Over the sample period, carbon emissions showed a turning trend after reaching their peak around 2010, and the pace of decline became more visible after 2016. The green finance index also increased substantially, indicating a continued strengthening of financial attention to and participation in low-carbon sectors. At the firm level, listed companies exhibited noticeable improvements in environmental information disclosure, environmental performance, and environmental protection investment. Taken together, these trends suggest that the progression of China’s low-carbon policy system unfolded alongside broader changes in emissions control, green financial development, and corporate green transformation. These trends provide a broader empirical context for understanding the practical relevance of policy evolution.

Policy recommendations

First, China should optimize the allocation of policy instruments and improve the coordination among supply-side, demand-side, and environment-type tools. The empirical results show that environment-type instruments are excessively dominant, while supply-side instruments remain insufficient and demand-side tools have not yet fully played their market-activating role. This suggests that the current policy system is relatively strong in regulatory and planning functions, but comparatively weak in substantive support and market activation. To address this problem, future policy design should increase support-oriented instruments in areas such as infrastructure construction, technical service platforms, and professional talent development, especially for sectors and regions with weaker disclosure capacity. At the same time, demand-side instruments should be further strengthened by linking government procurement, green supply-chain evaluation, and demonstration projects with firms’ disclosure performance. In addition, a coordinated mechanism that combines regulatory requirements with fiscal, tax, and financial incentives should be established, so as to gradually form a more balanced and mutually reinforcing policy-tool portfolio.

Second, China should improve the balance of policy objectives and strengthen the connection between macro-strategic goals and the concrete needs of enterprises and investors. The analysis of the policy-goal dimension indicates that most policy documents are strongly oriented toward the national “dual-carbon” strategy, while comparatively less attention is given to investors’ low-carbon information needs and especially to firms’ sustainable development. Therefore, future policy design should not only maintain the macro-level strategic orientation, but also refine meso- and micro-level policy goals. In practice, this may be achieved by clarifying core disclosure indicators, reporting frequency, industry-specific disclosure templates, and forward-looking risk disclosure requirements, while also providing differentiated guidance for different types of firms—especially small and medium-sized enterprises—to improve policy operability and reduce compliance uncertainty. For investors, disclosure rules should place greater emphasis on comparability, risk relevance, and decision usefulness, so that carbon-related information can be more effectively integrated into financing, investment, and valuation processes.

Third, China should strengthen the formal authority and binding force of high-effectiveness policy documents. The empirical findings reveal that most policy texts are concentrated in provincial-level guiding documents, while high-authority policy documents remain relatively limited. This suggests that the current policy framework has achieved broad coverage, but still lacks sufficient top-level institutional consolidation. To address this issue, key disclosure requirements should be gradually incorporated into higher-level regulatory documents, and the legal status of core carbon disclosure rules should be strengthened. At the same time, the division of responsibilities among environmental, financial, industrial, and market-regulatory authorities should be further clarified, so as to improve policy consistency and reduce fragmentation across administrative levels. By enhancing the authority, stability, and coherence of the policy framework, China can provide a stronger institutional basis for the long-term development of its low-carbon governance system.

Fourth, China should promote differentiated policy improvement according to the evolutionary stage of the low-carbon policy system. The study shows that China’s low-carbon policy system has evolved through three stages: initial development, system construction and deepening, and adaptation and innovation. This implies that future policy optimization should avoid a one-size-fits-all approach and instead respond to the characteristics of the current stage. In the present period, greater emphasis should be placed on moving from policy expansion to policy refinement—that is, from increasing the number of policy texts to improving their integration, precision, and enforceability. In particular, more attention should be given to aligning carbon disclosure rules with broader developments in green finance, carbon markets, and sustainability governance, so as to improve the adaptability and forward-looking capacity of the policy system under changing domestic and international conditions.

Fifth, China should strengthen the supporting implementation infrastructure of the low-carbon policy system and enhance the international alignment of carbon disclosure standards. The discussion of the manuscript suggests that, beyond policy design itself, the practical operation of the policy system also depends on the gradual strengthening of supporting governance mechanisms. Therefore, future efforts should focus on improving the implementation infrastructure through pilot third-party verification mechanisms, digital monitoring and data-sharing platforms, regular policy evaluation arrangements, and more standardized disclosure guidance. These measures would help enhance the comparability, credibility, and usability of carbon-related information, while also reducing implementation uncertainty for firms and improving the effectiveness of supervision.

At the same time, China should further strengthen the international alignment of its carbon disclosure standards. By drawing on advanced international practices and promoting greater comparability between domestic disclosure rules and major global sustainability reporting frameworks, China can improve the transparency, credibility, and international recognition of its low-carbon policy system. Such efforts would not only support China’s deeper participation in global climate governance, but also provide useful institutional experience for other developing countries seeking to improve their carbon disclosure systems.

Overall, the optimization of China’s low-carbon policy system should proceed along five interconnected directions: rebalancing policy instruments, broadening and refining policy objectives, strengthening high-level institutional authority, improving stage-specific policy adaptation, and consolidating implementation support while promoting the international alignment of carbon disclosure standards. Only in this way can China further enhance the practical effectiveness of its low-carbon governance and provide more solid institutional support for achieving the “dual-carbon” goals.

Limitations

This study has three main limitations. First, the policy effectiveness dimension is measured mainly by the administrative rank and formal authority of the issuing body. Although this approach improves coding consistency and comparability, it cannot fully capture actual implementation strength, local enforcement capacity, or firm-level compliance. Therefore, the Z-dimension should be interpreted as a proxy for formal policy authority rather than as a complete measure of realized policy effectiveness. Second, although this study develops a coding manual and conducts reliability tests, policy text coding still involves a certain degree of interpretive judgment. Some policy clauses may contain multiple functions or relate to more than one policy objective, which may lead to boundary cases in classification. Third, the analysis of policy implementation implications is descriptive rather than causal. Trends in carbon emissions, green finance, and firm-level green transformation may also be influenced by macroeconomic conditions, industrial structure, technological progress, energy prices, and market factors. Therefore, these trends should be understood as indicative evidence rather than causal proof of policy effectiveness. Overall, this study provides a structural and text-based diagnosis of China’s low-carbon policy system, but it does not offer a complete evaluation of actual implementation outcomes. Future research could combine policy text analysis with regional data, firm-level evidence, or causal identification methods to further examine the practical effects of low-carbon policies.
