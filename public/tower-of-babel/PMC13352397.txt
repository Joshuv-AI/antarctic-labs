A machine learning pipeline identifies non-motor symptoms as key contributors to sarcopenia in Parkinson's disease
Authors: Minkyeong Kim, Myung Jun Lee, Dahun Kim, Dukjoong Kim, Seoung Hyeon Je, Soo-Kyoung Kim, Heeyoung Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesSarcopenia, characterized by the loss of skeletal muscle mass and physical function, is more prevalent among patients with Parkinson's disease (PD) and is linked to poor clinical outcomes. This study aimed to investigate clinical features associated with sarcopenia in PD, which was further validated using a two-stage modeling pipeline.MethodsClinical and demographic data were collected and compared between the sarcopenia and non-sarcopenia groups. The dataset was split into a training and a hold-out test set (70:30). Following an initial variable screening in the training set based on clinical judgment and inter-variable correlations, a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression was applied to identify six key predictors. These variables were then used to train a logistic regression model selected based on the highest Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUROC) in stratified cross-validation. Finally, the model was evaluated on the independent hold-out test set.ResultsPatients with PD and sarcopenia exhibited longer disease duration, higher Hoehn and Yahr stages, and a greater non-motor burden. The final logistic regression model revealed that body mass index, body fat ratio, and non-motor features including cognitive decline, autonomic dysfunction, fatigue, and mood disturbance were the most influential variables. The model achieved an AUROC of 0.793, recall of 0.500, and specificity of 0.902.ConclusionPatients with PD and sarcopenia exhibit distinct clinical characteristics defined by both anthropometric and non-motor features. These findings suggest that a comprehensive clinical assessment may be warranted for the early identification of sarcopenia in PD.

Introduction

Sarcopenia is characterized by the loss of skeletal muscle mass and deterioration of physical function. [1] Globally, it affects a substantial number of individuals, and its prevalence is projected to rise. Ageing-related mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, poor nutrition, physical inactivity, and comorbidities contribute to muscle wasting. Although pharmacological therapies are currently limited, exercise and nutritional interventions have demonstrated promising results.

Sarcopenia is more common among patients with Parkinson's disease (PD) than in the general population and is significantly associated with PD progression. [2], [3] Reduced physical activity, malnutrition, and sleep disturbance are known contributors to sarcopenia in PD. Additionally, α-synuclein deposition in skeletal muscle and alterations in the neuromuscular junction suggest that sarcopenia in PD likely involves both central and peripheral mechanisms. [4], [5]

Given that sarcopenia becomes more prevalent as PD progresses and contributes to functional disabilities such as falls, [6] early diagnosis and intervention are warranted. In this study, we aimed to identify clinical features that are most relevant to sarcopenia, which was validated using a two-stage modeling approach.

Methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Gyeongsang National University Hospital, Jinju, Korea (approval No. 2020–09-007 and 2023–07-037). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Acquisition of clinical data

Patients with PD, fulfilling the Movement Disorder Society (MDS) clinical diagnostic criteria [7] and regularly attending the movement disorder clinic at Gyeongsang National University Hospital, were prospectively enrolled from May 2022 to December 2024. Patients with medical conditions causing muscle wasting, such as cancer, active tuberculosis, advanced liver cirrhosis (Child-Pugh class B or C), or renal disease requiring hemodialysis, were excluded.

Clinical data included sarcopenia profile, demographics, comorbidities, motor and non-motor symptoms, bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) parameters, and lifestyle factors. Based on the updated AWGS 2019 criteria [8], sarcopenia was defined as low appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI; <7.0 kg/m2 for men, <5.7 kg/m2 for women) combined with either low handgrip strength (<28 kg for men, <18 kg for women) or reduced 6-m walking velocity (<1.0 m/s).

Motor symptoms were evaluated during the “on” state using the MDS-Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale III (MDS-UPDRS III) and modified Hoehn and Yahr (HY) stage. The levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD) was calculated. Non-motor symptoms were assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Global Deterioration Scale (GDS), Non-Motor Symptom Scale (NMSS), Korean version of the Parkinson Fatigue Scale (PFS), and Parkinson's disease questionnaire (PDQ-8). Anthropometric measures were obtained from BIA. Social history and exercise habits (International Physical Activity Questionnaire) were also recorded.

Data preprocessing

To prevent data leakage, the dataset was first partitioned into a 70% training set (n = 121) and a 30% hold-out test set (n = 53) using stratified sampling. All subsequent feature selection steps were performed exclusively within the training set, reserving the hold-out test set only for final model evaluation.

Variables directly defining sarcopenia (ASMI, handgrip strength, and 6-m walking velocity) and those conceptually identical (ASM, SMI, muscle mass, skeletal muscle) were excluded a priori. Comorbidities were selected a priori based on established pathophysiological links to sarcopenia: hypertension (vascular dysfunction leading to reduced muscle perfusion), diabetes mellitus (insulin resistance-induced muscle wasting), dyslipidemia (intramuscular lipid infiltration), thyroid disease (direct effect on muscle metabolism), osteoporosis (osteosarcopenia) and pulmonary disease (chronic systemic inflammation). This resulted in 53 variables.

Within the training set, correlation analysis assessed multicollinearity (Supplementary Material 1). For pairs with a correlation coefficient | r | > 0.7, one variable was removed based on clinical importance, resulting in 24 continuous and 11 categorical variables for Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression. Prior to model training, all continuous variables were z-score standardized, and categorical variables were converted using one-hot encoding.

Feature selection via LASSO regression

To identify the most relevant clinical variables associated with sarcopenia, LASSO regression with L1 regularization was applied to the training set. To address class imbalance, class weights were adjusted inversely proportional to class frequencies. A stratified 3-fold cross-validation grid search was conducted to determine the optimal regularization parameter (C, searched over [10–4,10–1]), optimized for the F2 score (recall weighted twice as much as precision) to minimize missed cases. Variables with non-zero coefficients were selected for model development.

Model validation and interpretation

Selected features were used to develop the final predictive model by comparing five representative algorithms: logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting, support vector machine, and K-Nearest Neighbors. The decision threshold was optimized by maximizing the F1 score across three cross-validation folds to address class imbalance. Model comparison was performed using stratified 3-fold cross-validation within the training set, prioritizing Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUROC).

The finalized model was evaluated on the independent 30% hold-out test set, using the AUROC, F1-score, Precision, Recall, Specificity, and Brier score. Bootstrap resampling (n = 1000) was used to estimate 95% confidence intervals. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were calculated to quantify feature impact.

Statistical analysis

Clinical characteristics were compared between the sarcopenia and non-sarcopenia groups using Welch's t-test, Mann–Whitney U test, or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. All statistical analyses were performed using Python (3.12.13) with the scikit-learn (1.6.1), and SHAP (0.51.0) libraries.

Results

Clinical comparison of sarcopenia and non-sarcopenia groups

A total of 174 patients were included, of whom 41 (23.6%) were diagnosed with sarcopenia. Supplementary Material 1 presents the sarcopenia profile along with clinical characteristics. Consistent with the diagnostic criteria of the AWGS 2019, the sarcopenia group exhibited significantly lower ASMI values, handgrip strength, and 6-m walking velocity compared with the non-sarcopenia group. The sarcopenia group included a higher proportion of female patients (78.0% vs. 55.6%) and demonstrated older age at disease onset and fewer years of education. Hypertension was more frequent in the sarcopenia group.

While total MDS-UPDRS III scores and LEDD did not differ significantly, the sarcopenia group had significantly higher axial subscores and HY stage. Regarding cognitive function, the sarcopenia group showed significantly greater impairment, with lower MoCA and higher GDS scores. They also exhibited significantly higher scores on cardiovascular, sleep, mood, intellectual, and miscellaneous domains of the NMSS, as well as on the PFS and the PDQ-8, suggesting greater non-motor burden. BIA revealed significant group differences across most parameters, except for absolute fat mass and abdominal fat ratio. Additionally, patients with sarcopenia were less likely to consume coffee daily and reported less moderate or vigorous physical activity.

Among the five candidate algorithms evaluated, logistic regression achieved the highest AUROC (Supplementary material 3). On the independent hold-out test set, the final logistic regression model achieved an AUROC of 0.793 (95% CI: 0.648–0.915), recall of 0.500 (95% CI: 0.250–0.750), specificity of 0.902 (95% CI: 0.805–0.976), F1-score of 0.546 (95% CI: 0.285–0.769), precision of 0.600 (95% CI: 0.333–0.875), and a Brier score of 0.176 (Supplementary Materials 4 and 5). The AUROC curve supported the model's discriminative ability (Supplementary Material 6A), and the precision-recall curve demonstrated superior performance over random prediction, illustrating a trade-off between precision and recall (Supplementary Material 6B).

Discussion

In this study, we compared the clinical characteristics of patients with PD with and without sarcopenia and identified clinical variables associated with sarcopenia. We validated these findings through a logistic regression model using a two-stage modeling pipeline. Notably, the model's discriminative ability was primarily driven by a combination of anthropometric measures (BMI, body fat ratio) and non-motor features, including cognitive decline, autonomic dysfunction, fatigue, and mood disturbance.

The use of a two-stage modeling pipeline―specifically, LASSO-based feature selection followed by logistic regression―offered methodological advantages over conventional regression. LASSO's L1 regularization enabled variable selection, effectively achieving a more parsimonious set of six variables. The concise 6-variable model may serve as a basis for a practical screening tool. Furthermore, logistic regression outperformed other candidate algorithms in cross-validation, supporting its suitability for this dataset.

BMI emerged as the most influential predictor in the final model. This aligns with previous studies reporting that patients with PD generally have lower BMI than age- and sex-matched controls, [9] and that low BMI is a significant risk factor for sarcopenia. Studies suggested a link between low BMI and underlying PD pathology, such as reduced dopamine transporter activity. [10]

Although BMI is a convenient index, our model also identified body fat ratio as the second most critical feature, highlighting the importance of body composition. Previous studies on body composition in PD have yielded heterogeneous results, with some showing increased visceral fat often associated with levodopa-induced dyskinesia and others reporting progressive fat loss. [9], [11] Our final model retained both variables with opposing coefficients: a higher BMI was associated with decreased risk of sarcopenia, whereas a higher body fat ratio with increased risk. The correlation coefficient r = 0.569 in the training set falls below the conventional multicollinearity threshold (> 0.7), indicating that these two variables capture distinct information for sarcopenia identification. Nevertheless, as BMI and body fat ratio remain conceptually related to sarcopenia itself, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Notably, non-motor features were significant contributors to sarcopenia identification. This is consistent with previously reported associations between sarcopenia and non-motor symptoms in PD. Cognitive decline, autonomic dysfunction, fatigue, and depressive mood may be associated with impaired health literacy, reduced physical activity, and worsened nutritional status, which could contribute to sarcopenia risk. [12] Conversely, sarcopenia may be associated with greater non-motor burden.

Daily coffee consumption was significantly less frequent in the sarcopenia group. This finding should be interpreted with caution as it is confounded by sex, age, income, occupation, or health behaviors. However, given that coffee is a well-known protective factor in PD, further research in a larger cohort is needed to clarify whether coffee consumption influences sarcopenia risk in PD or reflects shared underlying pathophysiology. Although female sex has been reported as a risk factor for sarcopenia in PD, its contribution in our model was minimal. Female sex showed a strong negative correlation with both ASMI and handgrip strength (r = −0.73 and − 0.75, respectively). As these are core diagnostic components of sarcopenia, including sex resulted in limited additional discriminatory value. Additionally, MDS-UPDRS III scores and LEDD did not differ significantly between groups. This may be due to assessments being performed during the “on” state and the inclusion of only patients capable of safely completing a 6-m walk. Including a broader range of disease severity in the “off” state might have revealed a stronger contribution of motor symptoms.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, resulting in a limited events-per-variable ratio of 6.83. Although L1 regularization and stratified cross-validation were applied to mitigate overfitting, and the model demonstrated consistent performance across cross-validation and the hold-out test set, our results should be validated in a larger cohort. Second, despite acceptable discriminative performance, the recall remains relatively modest (0.50), indicating that a substantial proportion of sarcopenia cases may not be detected by the model. Accordingly, clinical application should be treated with caution unless the class imbalance issue is further addressed in future studies. Third, as this study has a cross-sectional design, the observed associations should be interpreted as such rather than implying causality. Lastly, while internal validation was performed using a hold-out test set, external validation using an independent dataset is required. Thus, findings in this study should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating until generalizability is established in future multicenter longitudinal studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identified key clinical features associated with sarcopenia in PD using a machine learning approach. Both anthropometric measures and non-motor symptoms-including cognitive decline, autonomic dysfunction, fatigue, and mood disturbance-were core features associated with sarcopenia identification. Our findings suggest a comprehensive assessment may be warranted for early identification of sarcopenia in this vulnerable population.
