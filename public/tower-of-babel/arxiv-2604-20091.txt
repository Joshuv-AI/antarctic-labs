1.
Introduction

Our aim in this paper is to give a construction of curves with minimal

a
-number in the context of Artin–Schreier covers.

1.1.
Background

Let
k
be a perfect field of characteristic
p
with algebraic
closure
{\overline{k}}
, and let
Z
be a smooth, irreducible, projective
curve of genus
g_{Z}
over
k
. There is an additive,
p^{-1}
-linear
map called the Cartier operator

\mathcal{C}:H^{0}(Z,\Omega^{1}_{Z})\to H^{0}(Z,\Omega^{1}_{Z})

defined as follows. Choose
x\in k(Z)\setminus k(Z)^{p}
. Then

\{1,x,\dots,x^{p-1}\}
is a basis of
k(Z)
as a vector space over
the subfield
k(Z)^{p}
. Given
\omega\in H^{0}(Z,\Omega^{1}_{Z})
, write

\omega=\left(f_{0}+f_{1}x+\cdots+f_{p-1}x^{p-1}\right)\,dx,

with
f_{0},\dots,f_{p-1}\in k(Z)^{p}
. Then
\mathcal{C}
is defined by

\mathcal{C}(\omega)=f_{p-1}^{1/p}\,dx.

Clearly
\mathcal{C}
is additive in
\omega
. The phrase “
p^{-1}
-linear”
means that
\mathcal{C}(f\omega)=f^{1/p}\mathcal{C}(\omega)
for
f\in k(Z)^{p}
. See

[
5
]
for the original definition and
[
2
]

for a careful account of representing semi-linear operators by
matrices in this and related contexts.

The
a
-number
of
Z
is defined as

a_{Z}:=\dim_{k}\ker\left(\mathcal{C}:H^{0}(Z,\Omega^{1}_{Z})\to H^{0}(Z,\Omega^{1}_{Z})\right).

The terminology comes from the fact that
a_{Z}
is the largest integer

a
such that there is an injection of group schemes over
k

\left(\alpha_{p}\right)^{a}\hookrightarrow J_{Z}[p],

where
J_{Z}[p]
is the
p
-torsion group scheme of the Jacobian of
Z
.
The integer
a_{Z}
is also a measure of the complexity of
J_{Z}[p]
in
that it is the minimal number of generators of the local-local part of
the Dieudonné module of
J_{Z}[p]
over the Dieudonné ring. Clearly

0\leq a_{Z}\leq g_{Z}
, and it is known that
a_{Z}=0
if and only if
Z

satisfies
J_{Z}[p]({\overline{k}})=(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{g_{Z}}
, and
a_{Z}=g_{Z}
if and only if

J_{Z}
is isomorphic over
{\overline{k}}
to the product of
g_{Z}
copies of a
supersingular elliptic curve.
We omit details as neither of these connections will be needed in this
article, but see
[
7
]
for a quick introduction to these and
related results.

1.2.
Results

Let
X=\mathbb{P}^{1}
over
k
with coordinate
x
. Let
d
be a positive
integer not divisible by
p
, and let
A\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{d+1}
be the open
subset of tuples
(a_{0},\dots,a_{d})
with
a_{d}\neq 0
. For

a=(a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in A(k)
let

f_{a}(x)=a_{0}+a_{1}x+\cdots+a_{d}x^{d}\in k[x].

Let
\pi:Y_{a}\to X
be the Artin–Schreier cover of
X
defined by

Y=Y_{a}:\qquad y^{p}-y=f_{a}(x).

It is well known (see, e.g.,
[
9
, Prop. 6.4.1]
) that
the genus of
Y
is
(p-1)(d-1)/2
.

Booher and Cais gave lower and upper bounds on the
a
-number of
Y

in
[
3
]
. These upper and lower bounds coincide when

p=2
, so both are sharp. Thus we may assume from now on that
p>2
.
The Booher–Cais lower bound was simplified by Booher, Pries and
several collaborators in
[
1
]
(initiated at PROMYS
2019), and they proved that the lower bound is optimal for
p=3
and

p=5
. In
[
8
]
, Shi gives further examples of Artin–Schreier
covers with minimal
a
-number for every
p
.

Our main result shows that the Booher–Cais bound is optimal for every

p
and every pole order
d
. We write
\lfloor x\rfloor
for the
largest integer
\leq x
, and for
d
prime to
p
we define an integer

L(d):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{(p-1)/2}\left\lfloor\frac{(p-\ell)d}{p}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{((p^{2}+1-2\ell p)d}{2p^{2}}\right\rfloor.

Theorem 1.3
.

There is a non-empty Zariski open subset
U
of
A
such that if
a\in U(k)
, then the
a
-number of
Y
is
L(d)
.

(This is, up to a simple change of variables, the bound

L_{(p+1)/2}(d)
given in
[
1
]
.)

Our result also has consequences for Artin–Schreier covers of general
ordinary curves
X
, i.e., curves with
a_{X}=0
. Let
X
be a (smooth,
irreducible, projective) ordinary curve over
k
, and
let
\pi:Y\to X
be a ramified Artin–Schreier cover of
X
defined by

Y:y^{p}-y=f,\quad\text{for $f\in k(X)$}.

If
P_{1},\dots,P_{B}
are the branch points of
\pi
, let
d_{1},\dots,d_{B}

be the ramification invariants of
\pi
, i.e., let
d_{b}
be the
unique break in the lower ramification filtration of
\gal(Y/X)
at

P_{b}
.
The integers
d_{b}
are necessarily prime to
p
.

Corollary 1.4
.

Suppose that
k
is algebraically closed and
X
is ordinary, and
choose an integer
B\geq 1
, points
P_{1},\dots,P_{B}\in X(k)
, and positive
integers
d_{1},\dots,d_{B}
all relatively prime to
p
. Then there
exists an Artin–Schreier cover
\pi:Y\to X
ramified precisely at

P_{1},\dots,P_{B}
with ramification invariants
d_{1},\dots,d_{B}
such
that
a_{Y}
is minimal among all covers ramified at
B
points with
invariants
d_{1},\dots,d_{B}
, namely

a_{Y}=\sum_{b=1}^{B}L(d_{b}).

This is exactly
[
1
, Thm. 1.2]
plus our
Theorem
1.3
.

1.5.
Strategy

The structure of the formula in Theorem
1.3
reflects our
strategy of proof, namely the use of a certain filtration on

H=H^{0}(Y,\Omega^{1}_{Y})
. In Section
2
, we define subspaces

0\subseteq H^{\leq 1}\subseteq H^{\leq 2}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq H^{\leq p-1}=H,

and in Theorem
2.5
we compute the dimension of the
kernel of
\mathcal{C}
restricted to each
H^{\leq J}
(with
a\in U(k)
for a certain
non-empty open
U\subseteq A\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{d+1}
). The proof of
Theorem
2.5
is reduced to Theorem
2.8
which
asserts the surjectivity or injectivity of certain maps between
subquotients of
H
. To establish Theorem
2.8
, we regard
the coefficients
a_{0},\dots,a_{d}
as variables and prove that a certain
determinant is a non-zero polynomial in these variables, and so the
set where it vanishes is a proper Zariski closed subset of the space
of coefficients.

In the end, we find more generally that for
J\leq(p+1)/2
, and
generic
a
,

\dim_{k}\ker\left(\mathcal{C}:H^{\leq J}\to H\right)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{J}\left\lfloor\frac{(p-\ell)d}{p}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{(p(J+1-\ell)-J)d}{p^{2}}\right\rfloor,

(1.1)

and that for
J\geq(p-1)/2
,

\ker\mathcal{C}|_{H^{\leq J}}=\ker\mathcal{C}|_{H^{\leq(p-1)/2}}.

The main theorem is an immediate consequence.
A posteriori
, we
see that the right-hand side of (
1.1
) is the quantity

L_{J}(d)
studied in
[
1
]
.

Recall that
k

. Let

over

Definition 2.1

For

and
0<i<(p-j)d/p

\omega_{i,j}=y^{j-1}x^{i-1}\,dx.

\displaystyle H^{\leq J}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left.\omega_{i,j}\right|0<j\leq J,0<i<(p-j)d/p\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left.\omega_{i,j}\right|j=J,0<i<(p-j)d/p\right\rangle

\displaystyle{}^{p}H^{J}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left.\omega_{i,j}\right|j=J,0<i<(p-j)d/p,p|i\right\rangle

\displaystyle{}^{\prime}H^{J}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left.\omega_{i,j}\right|j=J,0<i<(p-j)d/p,p\mathrel{\mathchoice{\not|}{\not|}{\kern-1.99997pt\not\kern 1.99997pt|}{\kern-1.99997pt\not\kern 1.99997pt|}}i\right\rangle.

It is clear that

Theorem 2.5
.

There is a non-empty Zariski open subset
U\subseteq A
such that if
a\in U(k)
, and
Y=Y_{a}
, we have:

(1)

For
0<J\leq(p+1)/2
, the restriction of
\mathcal{C}
to

H^{\leq J}\overset{\mathcal{C}}{\longrightarrow}\bigoplus_{\ell=1}^{J}H(\ell,J).

Proof that Theorem
2.5
implies
Theorem
1.3
.

Part (2) of Theorem
2.5
says that the kernel of
\mathcal{C}
on

H
is contained in
H^{\leq(p-1)/2}
. Using
Definitions
2.1
and

, we have

\dim\left(H^{\leq(p-1)/2}\right)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{(p-1)/2}\left\lfloor\frac{(p-\ell)d}{p}\right\rfloor.

The case

of part (1) of Theorem

implies
that the dimension of the image (i.e., the codimension of the kernel)
of
\mathcal{C}
on

is

\dim\left(\bigoplus_{\ell=1}^{(p-1)/2}H(\ell,(p-1)/2)\right).

The summands on the right have dimensions

\left\lfloor\frac{(p^{2}+1-2\ell p)d}{2p^{2}}\right\rfloor
, so the
dimension of the kernel of
\mathcal{C}
is

\sum_{\ell=1}^{(p-1)/2}\left\lfloor\frac{(p-\ell)d}{p}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{(p^{2}+1-2\ell p)d}{2p^{2}}\right\rfloor,

as desired.
∎

We continue by refining the statement of Theorem
2.5
.

Proposition 2.6
.

For
0<J<p
and
a\in A(k)
, we have

(1)

\mathcal{C}\left(H^{J}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{C}\left(H^{\leq J}\right)\subseteq\bigoplus_{\ell=1}^{J}H(\ell,J).

(2)

\mathcal{C}\left({}^{\prime}H^{J}\right)\subseteq\bigoplus_{\ell=1}^{J-1}H(\ell,J).

(3)

The map
{}^{p}H^{J}\overset{\mathcal{C}}{\to}\mathcal{C}({}^{p}H^{J})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{C}(H^{J})
composed with
the inclusions in (1) and the projection onto the factor
\ell=J

induces an isomorphism

{}^{p}H^{J}\,\tilde{\to}\,H(J,J).

Proof.

Let
\omega_{i,j}
be a basis element of
H^{\leq J}
, so
j\leq J
and

0<i<(p-j)d/p
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\omega_{i,j})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}\left(y^{j-1}x^{i-1}\,dx\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}\left((y^{p}-f)^{j-1}x^{i-1}\,dx\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\binom{j-1}{\ell-1}(-1)^{j-\ell}\mathcal{C}\left(y^{p(\ell-1)}f^{j-\ell}x^{i-1}\,dx\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\binom{j-1}{\ell-1}(-1)^{j-\ell}y^{\ell-1}\mathcal{C}\left(f^{j-\ell}x^{i-1}\,dx\right).

(2.1)

The
\ell
-th term of the sum
lies in
H^{\ell}
and since the degree of
f^{j-\ell}x^{i}
is at
most
(j-\ell)d+(p-j)d/p
, we have that

\mathcal{C}\left(f^{j-\ell}x^{i-1}\,dx\right)
is
g(x)x^{-1}\,dx
where

g
has degree at most

\frac{(j-\ell)d+(p-j)d/p}{p}=\frac{(p(j+1-\ell)-j)d}{p^{2}},

so the
\ell
-th term of the sum
lies in
H(\ell,j)\subseteq H(\ell,J)
. This completes the proof of
part (1).

For part (2), note that the
\ell=j
term of the sum
in equation (
2.1
) is
y^{j-1}\mathcal{C}\left(x^{i-1}\,dx\right)
, and
this is zero if

. Thus if
\omega_{i,j}\in{}^{\prime}H^{J}
, the part
of
\mathcal{C}(\omega_{i,j})
in the

vanishes. This establishes part (2).

Part (3) follows by similar reasoning: if

,

\mathcal{C}\left(y^{j-1}x^{i-1}\,dx\right)=y^{j-1}x^{i/p-1}\,dx+\cdots

where the omitted terms involve lower powers of

under consideration sends
\omega_{i,j}
to
\omega_{i/p,j}
. Let

Since the differentials

follows that as

runs through the basis

\{\omega_{p,j},\omega_{2p,j},\dots,\omega_{pI,j}\}

For

Theorem 2.8

If

. We first prove by

To prove part (2) of Theorem

Proposition 3.2

(1)

If

|R_{J}|\leq|C_{J}|

(2)

Proof.

(1), Supposing

. If
(i,\lambda)\in R_{J}
, then from the definition of

i<\frac{((p(\lambda+1)-J))d}{p^{2}}\leq\frac{(pJ-J)d}{p^{2}}\leq\frac{(p^{2}-1)d}{2p^{2}}<d/2.

Rewriting the inequalities defining
R_{J}
, we have

-\frac{(J-1)d}{p}<pi-\lambda d<\frac{(p-J)d}{p},

and since

the lower bound is
\geq-(p-J)d/p

0<\lambda<p
, so

\lambda
is prime to

; this is
non-zero, and we choose the sign so that

. Then we have that

0<i^{\prime}<(p-J)d/p
and

,

. This defines a map

. If both
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
map to
i^{\prime}
, then

. But
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
are
<d/2
, so this
is possible only if
i_{1}=i_{2}
. Thus the map
R_{J}\to C_{J}
is
injective.

(2) Supposing
(p+1)/2\leq J<p
, we write down an injective map of
sets
C_{J}\hookrightarrow R_{J}
. Choose
i^{\prime}\in C_{J}
and note that
i^{\prime}<d/2
.
Let
\iota
be the unique integer in
(0,d)
with

, and let
\rho=(p\iota-i^{\prime})/d
. By the

,
p\mathrel{\mathchoice{\not|}{\not|}{\kern-1.99997pt\not\kern 1.99997pt|}{\kern-1.99997pt\not\kern 1.99997pt|}}i^{\prime}
, so we have
0<\rho
, and since

\iota<d
, we have
\rho<p
. If
\rho<J
we claim that

(i=\iota,\lambda=\rho)
is an element of
R_{J}
. Indeed, we have

0<pi-\lambda d=i^{\prime}<\frac{(p-J)d}{p},

and
0<\lambda<J
as required. If
\rho\geq J
, we claim that

(i=d-\iota,\lambda=p-\rho)
is an element of
R_{J}
. Indeed,

-\frac{(J-1)d}{p}\leq-\frac{(p-J)d}{p}<-i^{\prime}=pi-\lambda d<0,

and

as required. Thus we have defined a map

.
If
i^{\prime}_{1}
and

both map to

, and since
i_{1}^{\prime}

i_{2}^{\prime}

,

is

∎

Now take

, let

in

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\omega_{i^{\prime},J})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{J}\binom{J-1}{\ell-1}(-1)^{J-\ell}y^{\ell-1}\mathcal{C}\left(f^{J-\ell}x^{i^{\prime}-1}\,dx\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda=0}^{J-1}\binom{J-1}{\lambda}(-1)^{\lambda}y^{J-\lambda-1}\mathcal{C}\left(f^{\lambda}x^{i^{\prime}-1}\,dx\right),

where the second expression is the result of substituting

\left(\binom{J-1}{\lambda}(-1)^{\lambda}b_{\lambda,pi-i^{\prime}}^{1/p}\right)_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i,\lambda)\in R_{J}\\
i^{\prime}\in C_{J}\end{subarray}}

Now

((i,\lambda),i^{\prime})

Thus, to prove Theorem

i^{\prime}\in C_{J}\end{subarray}}

We change point of view and make calculations in the polynomial ring

(We have delayed doing so until now due to the exponent

Proposition 3.4

(1)

(2)

(2) It suffices to check this for the maximum value of

\displaystyle pi>\frac{(\lambda p-J+1)d}{p}

Let

.

(2)

The values of

(3)

The values of

for a fixed

(1) and (2) are obvious from the definition of

.

(3) Since

, the values of

, and

We now consider

. If

Let
R_{N}\subseteq R_{J}
be the indices of the rows of
N
and let
C_{N}\subseteq C_{J}
be the indices of the columns of
N

We consider the permutation expansion of the determinant of

More precisely, for each bijection
\sigma:R_{N}\to C_{N}
, we form the

Pr(\sigma):=\prod_{i\in R_{N}}N_{i,\sigma(i)}

\sigma

.

We now introduce a monomial ordering and prove that there is a unique
permutation
\sigma
such that the leading monomial of
Pr(\sigma)
is
maximal. This monomial cannot by cancelled by any other monomial in

Define a monomial order on

by declaring that

a_{0}^{e_{0}}\cdots a_{d}^{e_{d}}<a_{0}^{f_{0}}\cdots a_{d}^{f_{d}}

.
Thus
a_{d}
is larger than any monomial in

Define a grading on
S
by declaring that
a_{i}
has weight
i
. Then

is homogeneous of degree
\lambda
and has weight

.

For

for the leading monomial of

with respect to the order above.

Recall that every
(i,\lambda)\in R_{N}
is uniquely determined by

.
To ease notation, for the rest of the proof we always denote elements
of
R_{N}
by

(rather than by

). In particular, a pair

will always denote an element of

.

For a bijection
\sigma:R_{N}\to C_{N}

\Gamma(\sigma)=\left\{(i,\sigma(i))\mid i\in R_{N}\right\}\subseteq R_{N}\times C_{N}.

The following result establishes that
\det(N)\in S
is non-zero, and
thus completes the proof of Theorem
2.8
.

Theorem 3.6
.

(1)

If
(i,i^{\prime})\in T_{N}
, then

LM(N_{i,i^{\prime}})=LM(b_{\lambda,pi-i^{\prime}})=a_{\phi(i,i^{\prime})}a_{d}^{\lambda-1}.

(2)

Pr(\sigma)\neq 0
if and only if
\Gamma(\sigma)\subseteq T_{N}
.

(3)

If
\Gamma(\sigma)\subseteq T_{N}
, then

LM(Pr(\sigma))=a_{0}^{e_{0}}a_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots a_{d-1}^{e_{d-1}}a_{d}^{e_{d}+\Lambda}

where for
1\leq\ell\leq d
,

e_{\ell}=\left|\left\{i\in R_{N}|\phi(i,\sigma(i))=\ell\right\}\right|,

and

\Lambda=\sum_{i\in R_{N}}(\lambda-1).

(
Recall that each index

in
R_{N}
determines a unique

\lambda
.
)

(4)

There exists a bijection
\sigma_{0}:R_{N}\to C_{N}
such that

\Gamma(\sigma_{0})\subseteq T_{N}
and such that if

\sigma\neq\sigma_{0}
is another bijection
\sigma:R_{N}\to C_{N}
with

\Gamma(\sigma)\subseteq T_{N}
, then

LM(Pr(\sigma_{0}))>LM(Pr(\sigma))
.

(5)

\det(N)\in R
is non-zero.

Proof.

(1) Note that
b_{\lambda,pi-i^{\prime}}
is homogeneous of degree
\lambda

and has weight

. Clearly

a_{\phi(i,i^{\prime})}a_{d}^{\lambda-1}
is the largest monomial of degree

\lambda
and weight

, and it appears in

with coefficient
\lambda
.

(2) The product
Pr(\sigma)
is non-zero if and only if each of the factors is
non-zero if and only if
\Gamma(\sigma)\subseteq T_{N}
.

(3) If
\Gamma(\sigma)\subseteq T_{N}
, then

LM(Pr(\sigma))=\prod_{i\in R}LM(N_{i,\sigma(i)}),

and the result then follows from part (1).

(4) We construct
\sigma_{0}
with a greedy algorithm with steps
indexed by
d,d-1,\dots,1
. (See below for an example.) At step

, consider

\mathsf{R}_{d}:=\left\{i\in R_{N}\mid\text{there is an $i^{\prime}\in C_{N}$ with
$\phi(i,i^{\prime})=d$}\right\}.

For each

3.5

guarantees that there is a unique

such that
\phi(i,i^{\prime})=d
, and
we set
\sigma_{0}(i)=i^{\prime}
. This defines a map
\sigma_{0}:\mathsf{R}_{d}\to C_{N}
.
Part (1) of Proposition
3.5
guarantees that
\sigma_{0}
is
injective. Let
\mathsf{C}_{d}=\sigma_{0}(\mathsf{R}_{d})
. (If
\mathsf{R}_{d}
happens to be

and proceed to the next step of the

occur in the same rows as entries where

.

In step

\sigma_{0}(i)=i^{\prime}
. (If

(5) Part (4) shows that

Remark 3.7

It is clear that the open set
