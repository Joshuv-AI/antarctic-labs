Framework-templated gas lattices in metal-organic frameworks
Authors: Younghun Kim, Dohoon Kim, Seungwoo Kim, Yunsung Lim, Jihan Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA gas lattice is a crystalline-like arrangement of adsorbates stabilized inside a porous host by confinement and periodic binding, providing a structurally defined adsorption state that links uptake and transport. Here, we present an end-to-end pipeline to discover and design framework-templated gas lattices in metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) using Xenon as a proof-of-concept guest. High-throughput screening with a lattice-aware descriptor identified Co-CAU-36 as a host that stabilizes a pore-spanning Xenon lattice, while isotherm analysis resolved shell formation, pore filling, and cooperative lattice establishment under application-relevant conditions. In Xenon/Krypton mixtures, Co-CAU-36 exhibited strong pore-scale core–shell segregation, and NEB calculations indicated a low-barrier core diffusion pathway for Krypton, implying separation relevance. Finally, ML-guided genetic optimization over a pillared-MOF space yielded candidates with Xenon lattice signatures. Here, we show that framework-templated gas lattices constitute a distinct adsorbed phase, characterized by long-range periodic order, collective transport pathways, and non-additive mixture behavior.Subject terms: Computational methods, Metal-organic frameworks

Introduction

Adsorption in porous materials, such as zeolites, activated carbons, and metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) underpins a wide range of separation and storage technologies, including carbon capture, catalysis, and sensing1–5. Accordingly, adsorption design has largely been pursued from an adsorbent-centric perspective, where pore geometry, chemical functionality, and framework flexibility are tuned to optimize performance metrics such as Henry coefficients, isosteric heats, and gas selectivity derived from isotherms or site-based models6–10. For instance, MOF design for separations and capture commonly focuses on strengthening host–guest interactions through open metal sites or polar functional groups, and on tuning pore size and topology to balance capacity and size/shape discrimination11–13. While these approaches have delivered powerful design rules, they often compress the adsorbed phase into averaged scalar and implicitly assume weak or short-ranged correlations among adsorbates. As a result, adsorption regimes governed by collective behavior, where guest–guest correlations and cooperative reorganizations control selectivity and hysteresis, remain difficult to predict and to design using adsorbent-only heuristics14.

A complementary perspective is to treat the adsorbed phase itself as a design target. A growing body of work has shown that confinement in MOFs can drive cooperative pore-filling transitions and adsorption–desorption hysteresis, producing stepwise or S-shaped isotherms that are attractive in practice since they can yield switching-like responses and large working capacities within targeted pressure windows15–17. At the same time, these macroscopic signatures can arise from multiple underlying mechanisms under confinement, and the corresponding microscopic states may be heterogeneous or pathway dependent, complicating predictive design and transferability across conditions18. This motivates moving beyond describing phase-change-like behavior solely through averaged observables and instead seeking structurally explicit targets that connect microscopic organization to functional response.

In this context, framework-templated gas lattices, where adsorbates assemble into crystalline-like sublattices commensurate with the pore architecture, provide a particularly well-defined and quantifiable limit of confinement-driven reorganization. Rather than viewing lattice formation as incidental condensation, this picture emphasizes the framework’s role as a geometric and energetic template that can register adsorption sites into a periodic binding landscape and promote long-range guest–guest packing into an extended, pore-spanning lattice. Previous studies have reported localized phase transitions and solid-like adsorbate motifs in porous environments, especially for the transition of gas to the liquid phase of water under strong confinement and solid-bubble formation of noble gases19–21. These observations demonstrate that pores can stabilize adsorbate morphologies distinct from their usual behavior, and they suggest that commensurate packing may enable dense and spatially uniform pore filling relative to amorphous arrangements under comparable conditions18. However, reported ordered motifs have often been limited in spatial extent, restricted to specific adsorption sites or local pockets, and identified in an ad hoc manner for individual materials or conditions. Consequently, it remains unclear whether extended, framework-templated gas lattices can be stabilized systematically, which host structural features promote their formation, and how persistently they are maintained across conditions.

Herein, we address these questions by advancing an adsorbate phase-aware design framework in which ordering and framework-templated gas lattice formation are treated as explicit objectives (Fig. 1). MOFs provide an ideal platform for this approach since their modular chemistry and structural diversity offer a vast, systematically tunable space in which pore size, shape, functionality, and flexibility can be engineered22–24. Using this tunability, we adopt a coupled host–guest and guest–guest perspective, treating the adsorbate lattice as a co-designed component and exploiting its size and propensity for commensurate packing to favor specific adsorbed-phase behaviors. We therefore move beyond optimizing adsorption properties alone and instead target control over how molecules organize within pores and identify the emergent adsorbed phase that forms, thereby positioning framework-templated gas lattices as a designable adsorption mode that can be mapped, screened, and engineered across the MOF landscape.

Conceptual shift from host-only adsorption design to framework-templated gas lattice design.

Conventional MOF design is framed primarily as selecting metal nodes and organic linkers to optimize host–guest interactions, with the adsorbed phase typically treated as disordered and evaluated through averaged descriptors, such as uptake and selectivity. Adsorbate-phase design expands the design variables to include the emergent gas lattice, explicitly accounting for guest–guest correlations and commensurate packing within the pore to stabilize a crystalline-like, structure-defined adsorbed phase and enable more tunable, application-directed adsorption responses.

Results

Our end-to-end workflow for discovering, characterizing, and designing framework-templated gas lattice in MOFs is shown in Fig. 2. Using xenon as a proof-of-concept guest, we developed a screening approach that searches MOF space by quantifying gas lattice signatures from grand canonical Monte Carlo (GCMC) configurations and applied it to identify candidate MOF structures with a high propensity for lattice formation. For the leading candidate, we then complemented the snapshot-based metrics with isotherm analysis, which links lattice formation to adsorption thermodynamics by resolving sequential regimes of (1) shell occupancy, (2) pore filling, and (3) cooperative lattice formation. We further probed the lattice-forming mechanism in the candidates by conducting lattice-site analysis with energy grid, which identifies how xenon populates specific MOF sites to build up the gas lattice. To elucidate the origin and robustness of the phenomenon, we systematically vary gas identities for gas lattice formation and show the roles of guest size and guest–guest packing in stabilizing the gas lattice. Extending beyond single-component adsorption, we conducted multicomponent GCMC simulations to show that gas lattice can persist under competitive adsorption in binary gas adsorption and reorganize into spatially heterogeneous arrangements, including core–shell motifs relevant to challenging separations, such as xenon/krypton. Finally, guided by these mechanistic insights, we pursue inverse design of MOFs with enhanced lattice propensity by constructing and optimizing a family of pillared MOFs (pMOFs), where pillar length and 2D layer identity serve as modular handles to tune pore periodicity and commensurate packing.

Overall schematics of exploring the MOF space for ordered adsorption through gas lattice formation.

Searching through a gas lattice in MOF follows the following pipeline: database screening, adsorbent analysis, adsorbate analysis, binary-gas adsorption, and inverse design.

Database screening and adsorbent analysis

To enable high-throughput discovery of gas lattice, we first established a gas lattice recognition descriptor that links a target crystalline adsorbate motif to adsorption configurations obtained from molecular simulation (Fig. 3A). As a target gas lattice, we adopted a face-centered-cubic (FCC) and body-centered-cubic (BCC) xenon lattice as an idealized reference environment, motivated by the simplicity of using a well-defined crystalline local coordination as a template25,26. To search for the most stable FCC and BCC phases of xenon, we performed an interaction energy analysis of the xenon lattice to pinpoint an optimal xenon lattice (Supplementary Fig. 1). We then compared this reference xenon lattice with xenon snapshots sampled in GCMC simulations within candidate MOFs. In these simulations, we employed the universal force field (UFF) as a practical framework force field for large-scale screening, consistent with its widespread use in prior high-throughput MOF studies27,28. As a sanity check on this choice, we compared the simulated adsorption behavior against available experimental benchmarks, including representative xenon isotherms and xenon/krypton selectivity trends, and found reasonable agreement at the trend level (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Our screening strategy combines a global lattice-likelihood descriptor with a local structural identification step. To probe the onset of gas lattice under minimally favorable conditions, we carried out GCMC simulations at 10 K and 1 bar conditions and analyzed the resulting adsorption configurations. For each MOF, we computed radial distribution functions (RDFs) from GCMC snapshots and quantified the extent to which the snapshot RDF reproduces the characteristic peak positions and intensities of the RDFs of FCC and BCC xenon lattice obtained from interaction energy analysis (4.34, 6.13, 7.525 Å peaks for FCC and 4.23, 4.89, 6.925 Å peaks for BCC) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Since adsorption in a gas lattice exhibits both enhanced short-range structure and longer-range periodic correlations, this RDF-based comparison provides a robust, simulation-ready proxy for crystallinity that is less sensitive to instantaneous fluctuations than pointwise geometric measures. We note that HCP is also a relevant competing motif for a Lennard–Jones-like adsorbate. However, because the present screening relies on an RDF-based neighbor-count descriptor, it is more sensitive to the emergence of ordered packing in general than to subtle discrimination between closely related close-packed motifs, such as FCC and HCP, which have very similar local coordination shells (FCC peaks: 4.34, 6.13, and 7.53 Å, HCP peaks: 4.32, 6.16, and 7.03 Å). To translate these structural signatures into an interpretable packing metric, we further defined a coordination-like descriptor by integrating the RDF over the first-neighbor shell and scaling by the adsorbate number density in Eq. 2. Specifically, we defined the neighbor count (NC) as a representation of the expected number of xenon neighbors within a distance window around the first-shell peak, thereby coupling peak sharpness/strength in RDFs with how densely xenon populates the pore. This combined descriptor helps distinguish genuine crystalline-like gas lattice from merely strong adsorption at isolated sites, since uptake alone does not necessarily produce a well-defined first-neighbor environment.

To make the search tractable over large databases, we applied a geometry-based pre-filter to the CoREMOF 25 database29, a computationally-ready database for MOFs, before evaluating gas lattice descriptors. Specifically, we selected structures with the largest cavity diameter (LCD) exceeding the characteristic length scale needed to accommodate the target xenon cluster of both BCC and FCC lattices (7.0 Å), while requiring a pore-limiting diameter (PLD) that remains accessible to xenon to avoid kinetic exclusion (4.0 Å). Out of 12,493 structures, 3390 MOF remained after this geometrical elimination. The resulting 3390 candidates were ranked by the combined neighbor-count score, and the top 3 structures were examined in detail (Supplementary Fig. 3). In practice, the high-ranked FCC candidates did not exhibit a clear long-range lattice in their snapshots, whereas the BCC-based screening yielded a MOF with pronounced crystalline-like organization (Supplementary Fig. 3). Among the top three BCC neighbor-count candidates, one MOF, Co-CAU-36 (2018[NiCo][scu]3[ASR]4)30, exhibited markedly higher lattice signatures than the others, and showed strong crystalline-like features in both NCs and real-space configurations (Fig. 3B). Although a BCC phase has never been observed under the xenon bulk conditions, it is noteworthy that confinement within screened MOF can induce and stabilize a BCC-like gas lattice of the adsorbate26. To further verify that the observed xenon gas lattice is not an artifact of the UFF description, we repeated the analysis with different force fields, Dreiding, and confirmed that the same qualitative gas lattice formation is preserved (Supplementary Fig. 4).

NC is used here as an initial screening metric that quantifies how closely the local adsorption environment resembles the target ordered reference at the level of first-neighbor coordination. In this sense, a high NC indicates that the adsorbed phase is developing a locally lattice-like environment, but it does not by itself establish the persistence, uniformity, or long-range registry of the ordered state throughout the pore. For this reason, NC-based screening is followed by additional structural validation. To verify that the gas lattice metrics correspond to genuine crystalline-like local environments rather than coincidental density fluctuations, we performed polyhedral template matching (PTM)31 on GCMC snapshots for the Co-CAU-36. PTM compares the local neighborhood around each xenon particle against ideal reference polyhedra and assigns the best-matching crystalline environment when present, using BCC clusters as templates. Snapshot of Co-CAU-36 yielded clear, repeated BCC template matches that coincided with visibly ordered xenon arrangements throughout the pore network (Fig. 3B). The repeated appearance of BCC-matched local environments across multiple pores and independent snapshots indicates that xenon forms a framework-templated gas lattice under confinement, rather than sporadic, transient nucleation, and confirms that the BCC signal corresponds to a physically meaningful crystalline-like adsorbate structure. To exclude the possibility that the observed BCC-like xenon lattice is a finite-size artifact, we repeated the simulations using larger supercells and found that the same ordered gas-lattice structure is retained (Supplementary Fig. 5).

To test the reproducibility of the gas lattice across independent configurations, we averaged the last 100 statistically independent xenon snapshots of Co-CAU-36 from the 10,000-production cycle and constructed a spatial density map (Fig. 3C). The density is averaged along the axis to yield a two-dimensional cross-sectional view. The resulting distribution shows well-defined, periodically localized xenon probability maxima in Co-CAU-36 for both the z-axis view and the x-axis, and provides an ensemble-level signature of a persistent lattice. In contrast, the corresponding density map for IRMOF-1 is comparatively diffuse and does not exhibit the periodically localized maxima characteristic of a framework-templated xenon lattice.

Then, to connect these structural signatures to adsorption thermodynamics under more realistic conditions, we next analyzed the xenon isotherm in Co-CAU-36 at 200 K up to 1 bar (Fig. 3D). This temperature–pressure window was selected from the broader 100–300 K range examined in this study as an application-relevant regime in which xenon remains strongly adsorbed and the MOF still supports the onset of lattice formation (Supplementary Fig. 6). The low-pressure region, form 0 to 0.2 bar, was sampled with a finer pressure grid to resolve the subtle curvature change and more clearly capture the incipient S-shaped character of the isotherm. The 200 K density maps in Fig. 3D differ in appearance from the strongly localized 10 K lattice signature in Fig. 3C: at elevated temperature, the xenon probability distribution is broadened, and the central maximum is less pronounced, reflecting thermal smearing and increased configurational fluctuations. Nevertheless, the 200 K maps still exhibit a symmetry-consistent localization pattern that emerges at high loading and indicates that lattice formation persists as a statistically preferred motif. The uptake curve can be naturally partitioned into three regimes that mirror the evolution of the density maps: an initial shell-occupancy regime in which xenon preferentially populates the strongest binding sites, a subsequent pore-filling regime where uptake spreads into the remaining accessible volume, and a final lattice formation regime in which the confined adsorbate reorganizes into a more crystalline-like arrangement. This analysis supports a mechanism in which the MOF does not merely provide capacity but furnishes a templating environment that enables site anchoring, intermediate filling, and the emergence of a gas lattice of xenon. We also observe that following shell occupancy at 0.1 bar, the isotherm exhibits a subtle convex-to-concave curvature change, indicative of an incipient S-shaped character consistent with cooperative filling (Fig. 3D).

Together, the neighbor-count ranking, local PTM identification, ensemble-averaged density maps, and isotherm analysis establish a consistent picture: a subset of MOFs can stabilize xenon in an ordered, BCC-like gas lattice, and the proposed workflow can efficiently identify such candidates from a large structural database. Importantly, we do not suggest that framework-templated gas-lattice formation necessarily yields the highest adsorption capacity relative to existing MOFs in all cases. Rather, its significance lies in enabling more controllable adsorption thermodynamics and spatial organization within the pore. This behavior suggests that adsorbate-phase design could help concentrate working capacity into a user-defined operating window and provide a potentially useful handle for gas capture applications that benefit from sharp uptake steps and controllable switching-like adsorption responses.

Geometrical and energy grid analysis of the adsorbent

Next, we describe in detail the mechanisms that lead to gas lattice formation of xenon in Co-CAU-36. Structurally, Co-CAU-36 is a porous MOF built from Co–phosphonate inorganic chains and a porphyrin-based tetraphosphonate linker that assemble into a 3D MOF with 12.5 Å pore spanning in a 1D channel30. It was originally reported as a model system for accurate structure determination and pore-guest localization using continuous-rotation electron diffraction, highlighting its well-defined pore environment. Building on its identification as the lead xenon lattice-forming MOF, we next examined how the framework stabilizes the BCC-like adsorbate motif at a higher level of theory. We carried out DFT geometric optimizations of the Co-CAU-36 plus xenon configuration, which preserved the xenon lattice arrangement and yielded a stable, well-defined xenon orientation within the channel (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. 7). To further confirm that the xenon adsorption sites are not force-field-specific, we compared the DFT-optimized xenon positions with those obtained from force-field calculations. The representative adsorption sites were consistently identified at both levels of theory, and their relative energetic preference was also preserved (Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 1). These agreements support that the force field reliably captures the key xenon adsorption environments in Co-CAU-36 and that the predicted xenon lattice arrangement reflects a robust MOF-stabilized configuration rather than a force-field artifact.

We next probed why Co-CAU-36 can sustain an extended BCC-like xenon lattice by first examining its geometric registry. Co-CAU-36 comprises repeating structural layers along the confined channel direction, and it has a periodic pore environment in which equivalent adsorption environments recur with a well-defined axial spacing. This layered repeat is proportionate with the periodicity required to stack a BCC xenon lattice along a 1D channel and suggests that the framework geometry itself provides a registry condition for propagating BCC-like packing beyond a single local pocket.

Building on this geometric picture, we conducted an energy-grid analysis of Co-CAU-36 to determine how the MOF’s binding landscape templates the BCC-like xenon lattice (Fig. 3E). Mapping the single-particle xenon adsorption energy throughout the pore reveals a periodic network of low-energy minima that defines the preferred adsorption sites. These minima exhibit a repeat spacing of approximately 10.4 Å across the pore and 9.0 Å along the channel direction, which closely matches the high-occupancy shell positions extracted from isotherm GCMC snapshots at 0.1 bar (10.0 and 8.8 Å, respectively). Both length scales are also near the characteristic lattice constant of the 2 × 2 BCC xenon reference (9.78 Å × 9.78 Å), indicating that the lattice density pattern is pinned by MOF-imposed adsorption wells rather than arising from entropic packing alone. In effect, Co-CAU-36 provides geometric registry and a periodic energetic template that guides xenon into BCC-like local environments, while xenon–xenon cohesion completes the remaining positions and propagates the lattice motif along the 1D channel.

However, the binding-site spacings and the ideal BCC lattice constant are not identical and imply a small degree of geometric mismatch. This mismatch can be accommodated by the finite compressibility of the confined xenon assembly and by attractive xenon–xenon interactions, which stabilize a crystal-like arrangement within the channel. More broadly, these observations suggest a clear design principle: MOFs with binding-site periodicities that more closely match the target lattice could further strengthen the BCC lattice and may even enable alternative lattice motifs, such as FCC, under appropriate confinement and interaction conditions.

Adsorbate analysis for gas lattice

Having established a xenon lattice in the Co-CAU-36, we next evaluated whether the BCC motif in Co-CAU-36 reflects a confinement-stabilized state or a fragile, kinetically trapped configuration. To do so, we performed a molecular dynamics melt–quench simulation at fixed xenon loading. The temperatures in this protocol are not meant to represent equilibrium adsorption conditions but rather provide a controlled way to disrupt the adsorbate packing and test whether the gas lattice motif can recover. Starting from a lattice configuration, we heated the adsorbate to 500 K to melt the packing and then quenched to 300 and 100 K to check the framework and lattice flexibility under thermal perturbation (Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Fig. 9). Xenon disorders upon heating but progressively reforms the BCC-like arrangement on cooling and demonstrates a reversible, MOF-templated recovery and supporting that the lattice state is a stable confinement-driven preference (Supplementary Fig. 9).

With this stability established, we then asked how general the gas lattice tendency is and how it depends on gas properties. We systematically compared one-component gases by relating the observed phase to their probe size and dispersive interaction parameters (Fig. 4), using the same screening thermodynamic conditions (10 K, 1 bar) to enable direct comparison across guests.

We performed GCMC simulations for a set of one-body gases spanning wide ranges of size and dispersive interaction strength (argon, krypton, methane, and xenon) and evaluated their degree of ordering to quantify their tendency of forming gas lattice using the same density profile analysis (Fig. 4). These gases span a broad range of probe sizes and dispersive interaction strengths, as reflected by their Lennard–Jones parameters (Supplementary Table 2). The results reveal a clear gas-dependent trend: argon and krypton did not develop appreciable gas lattice under the conditions tested, whereas methane and xenon exhibit progressively stronger signatures of gas lattice. In practical terms, gases with weak dispersive interactions and/or small effective sizes, such as argon and krypton, failed to stabilize the correlated first-neighbor environment needed for a crystalline-like phase, while more interactive and larger guests like methane and xenon can support cooperative packing. Consistent with this interpretation, the observed gas lattice trend is naturally explained by the interplay of MOF pore environment and the size of the gases: the correct coherence between MOF binding sites and gas size determines whether the adsorbed phase remains amorphous-like or crosses into a gas lattice regime.

Extension to molecular adsorbates

Beyond guest size, the same framework-templated gas-lattice concept can also be extended to molecular adsorbates. To test this possibility, we applied the same screening workflow to ethane and searched for MOF hosts that stabilize an ordered adsorbed motif under confinement (Fig. 5). Unlike xenon, however, ethane is a non-spherical molecular adsorbate and therefore was not evaluated using simple monatomic lattice references, such as FCC, BCC, or HCP, in the same sense as a one-body gas. Instead, we constructed the ethane metric from the center-of-mass (COM) packing motif of a bulk crystalline BCC ethane phase and ranked candidate MOFs by the similarity of their adsorption snapshots to this ethane-specific ordered reference. This distinction is important because the ordered xenon lattice is effectively characterized by a relatively regular square-like registry in the projected pore plane, which makes both the design target and the local structural assignment amenable to standardized descriptors, such as NC and PTM motif analysis. By contrast, the ethane COM arrangement is anisotropic and rectangular rather than square, and the full molecular packing further depends on orientational degrees of freedom. As a result, the same workflow can still identify ordered molecular motifs at the COM level, but the design target is less naturally standardized than in the xenon case and cannot be captured as completely by the same symmetry-based metrics alone.

Using this procedure, we first ranked candidate MOFs by the ethane COM-based NC score, then selected the most strongly ordered candidates based on their snapshot regularity. By this procedure, we identified MOF hosts, Ln2Cu3(IDA)632, in which confined ethane develops a clear ordered arrangement. Ln2Cu3(IDA)6 does not reproduce the bulk crystalline ethane structure exactly, but it formed a spatially regular cluster with similar COM distance characteristics and ordering tendency, indicating that the framework can still template an ordered molecular packing even when the confined arrangement deviates from the exact bulk crystal structure. Detailed comparison between the bulk ethane crystal and ethane snapshots can be found in the Supplementary Fig. 10.

The ethane results further demonstrate that framework-templated gas lattice can extend beyond one-body gases to molecular adsorbates. Whereas xenon is naturally described by translational packing and local coordination, ethane introduces additional structural richness through its anisotropic COM arrangement and molecular character. Nevertheless, the same snapshot-based workflow successfully identified an ordered ethane motif under confinement through COM-based structural matching. More broadly, because our screening framework quantifies ordering directly from adsorption configurations, it can be generalized to other gases by adopting an appropriate ordered reference and applying the same analysis across MOF space.

Gas lattice of binary gas adsorption

We further examined whether the gas lattice persists, and how it reorganizes, under competitive adsorption conditions relevant to separations by performing multicomponent GCMC for xenon/krypton. We first assessed temperature dependence by tracking composition-resolved occupancy densities in the xenon/krypton mixture. For assessing the xenon/krypton mixtures, density maps are shown for a single unit cell centered so that the middle of each map corresponds to the pore center for better visualization. Since Co-CAU-36 forms a periodically repeating 1D channel, the reported densities are time-averaged along the channel axis and yielded a two-dimensional cross-sectional view where the axes represent lateral positions across the pore. We averaged 500 snapshots throughout the 50,000 production cycles to capture the steady-state core–shell organization of the xenon/krypton mixture, as slow xenon–krypton exchange events make equilibration more challenging. At low temperature region (50 and 100 K), the xenon fraction map is sharply localized and indicated strong pore-scale partitioning, whereas increasing temperature (150 and 200 K) progressively broadens the composition profile and reduces contrast in the xenon-rich domain, consistent with thermal disruption of registry and mixing of the co-adsorbed phase (Fig. 6A). Among the temperatures examined, 100 K exhibited the clearest spatial separation and highest contrast between xenon-rich and krypton-rich regions, and we therefore select 100 K as the representative condition for subsequent structural and mechanistic analysis. Similar pore partitioning was observed in the various pressure regions with xenon/krypton co-adsorption (Supplementary Fig. 11).

For xenon/krypton in Co-CAU-36, the adsorbates remain highly structured and become compositionally heterogeneous: xenon preferentially occupies the most strongly templated regions of the pore, while krypton is displaced into the complementary free volume defined by the xenon sublattice, yielding a pronounced pore-scale partitioning consistent with a core–shell-like motif (Fig. 6B). The xenon-fraction density maps show high-contrast spatial variation and indicate that the mixture is not uniformly mixed but instead segregates within the pore according to the xenon gas lattice registry.

To assess whether this partitioning is unique to the gas lattice-forming host environment, we repeated the same xenon/krypton simulations in a set of representative MOFs chosen to satisfy broadly similar pore-accessibility criteria, including IRMOF-1, UIO-68, ZIF-10, and MgMOF-74 (Fig. 6C). In these MOFs, both snapshots and xenon-fraction density maps show substantially weaker spatial segregation and little evidence of the high-contrast, pore-registered partitioning observed in Co-CAU-36. Instead, the mixtures tend to be krypton-rich with xenon more diffusely distributed, consistent with the smaller krypton probe size allowing it to occupy a wider range of available pore regions and interstitial free volume under competitive conditions. Even in MgMOF-74, which provides a 1D channel geometry, xenon/krypton does not develop the same templated registry, indicating that 1D confinement alone is insufficient. By contrast, Co-CAU-36 remains comparatively xenon-rich and exhibits strong pore-scale partitioning, suggesting that its binding-site pattern and geometric periodicity selectively stabilize a xenon-anchored sublattice. Together, these comparisons highlight that pore-scale partitioning requires not only accessible confinement but also the right registry between binding-site periodicity, pore geometry, and adsorbate packing.

Its noteworthy that, although krypton shows only a weak gas lattice in the single-component case, it becomes markedly more structured when co-adsorbed with xenon in Co-CAU-36, indicating that the mixture gas lattice is not a simple superposition of two independent single-gas behaviors. Instead, xenon stabilizes a crystalline-like sublattice under confinement that effectively partitions the channel into a strongly templated shell near the binding sites and a more open core along the pore axis. Krypton is consequently displaced from the xenon-rich shell and preferentially occupies xenon-defined interstitial and core regions. It inherits the spatial registry imposed by the xenon template and produces an apparent co-adsorption-induced structuring of krypton. This segregation also has direct transport implications. To quantify the effect of shell versus core occupation on mobility along the channel, we performed nudged elastic band (NEB) calculations along the repeating adsorption-site pathway (Supplementary Note 3 and Fig. 7). The NEB was conducted on a partial lattice pathway from the 100 K GCMC snapshot in the previous analysis (Fig. 7). We selected two NEB diffusion pathways to match the distinct transport environments created by shell-core segregation: krypton migrating between shell sites, and krypton migrating along the channel core (Fig. 7A). These sites were chosen because xenon primarily occupies the strongly templated shell near binding sites and because co-adsorption with xenon redistributes krypton into the more open core, where long-axis transport is expected to be least hindered. These two scenarios isolate the effects of species identity and spatial pathway on the migration barrier, directly linking adsorption-driven partitioning to the emergence of a low-resistance diffusion corridor for krypton. The computed migration barriers decrease from krypton in the shell (6.41 kcal/mol) to krypton in the core (1.46 kcal/mol) (Fig. 7B).

These results provide a proof-of-concept that the ordered adsorbed phase can persist and reorganize under competitive adsorption, generating distinct thermodynamic and kinetic regions within the pore. Once xenon saturates the shell, Krypton is funneled into a low-barrier core diffusion pathway, whereas xenon remains trapped in the more strongly bound shell. In this sense, the gas lattice creates a coupled thermodynamic–kinetic separation mechanism that extends beyond site-affinity arguments alone.

Inducing gas lattice through inverse design

The Co-CAU-36 case study suggests that framework-templated gas lattices emerge when the host provides a strongly confining pore environment that promotes commensurate packing and long-range guest–guest correlations. Because this type of quasi–1D channel architecture is difficult to access systematically through conventional MOF discovery, we next pursued an inverse-design strategy to search MOF space directly for pore environments with suitable 1D channels. Motivated by experimental demonstrations that pillaring 2D MOF sheets can generate well-defined channels33–35, we constructed a large pillared-MOF (pMOF) design space by combining 2D MOF layers36 and organic pillars37 drawn from existing building-block databases (Fig. 8A). Because these candidates were assembled from reported building blocks and known 2D MOF motifs, rather than unconstrained hypothetical components, the resulting design space remains at least partially anchored to experimentally accessible chemistry. In addition, we evaluated the generated structures using MOFChecker38 and retained only candidates that passed these validity checks for further analysis. This modular representation makes the pore geometry programmable through discrete choices of layer and pillar and creates a natural search space for inverse design.

To enable efficient optimization over this combinatorial space, we used the neighbor-count descriptor as the objective and trained a surrogate model to predict ordering propensity from pMOF components. We first generated labeled training data by randomly sampling ~3000 pMOFs for each of three representative 2D-layer topologies. For every sampled pMOF, we ran xenon GCMC at 10 K and 1 bar and computed NCs from production snapshots. These topology labels were then used to train a machine-learning model that maps the component-level representation to predicted NC, enabling rapid evaluation of ordering propensity without explicit simulation for each new candidate (Supplementary Fig. 12). Using this surrogate as the fitness function, we applied a genetic algorithm (GA) for 20 generations across all three topologies, evolving pMOF genomes through selection and mutation to enrich structures predicted to stabilize gas lattices (Supplementary Fig. 13). We then inspected the 30 top-ranked candidates from each topology using explicit GCMC validation and structural analyses. An extended gas lattice was consistently observed only for sql topology-based layers, whereas the best candidates from the other topologies did not yield a comparable gas lattice under the same conditions (Supplementary Fig. 14). Performing the inverse-design loop separately for BCC and FCC targets yielded distinct high-scoring candidates for each lattice type, including sql+NiINPc+Zn+P51 (BCC) and sql+NiTNPc+Cu+P158 (FCC) (Fig. 8B).

The GA-selected pMOFs that emerged from inverse design share a common structural motif: both are built from naphthalocyanine (NPc) based 2D layers that assemble into the sql topology, with the channel geometry tuned by the choice of metal nodes and pillar (Note S4). To probe how much lattice formation depends on metal identity versus the underlying 2D-layer–pillar architecture, we performed metal-substitution tests in these pMOFs (Supplementary Fig. 15). Varying the metal nodes in the 2D layer had little effect on the BCC candidate, consistent with the BCC lattice being primarily dictated by the more rigid/commensurate channel template imposed by the sql layer plus pillar. In contrast, the FCC candidate exhibited substantially greater sensitivity to the choice of 2D-layer metal: substitutions, such as copper, palladium, and zinc, maintained higher lattice signatures, consistent with their stronger preference for square-planar coordination environments that better preserve the intended sql-layer geometry, whereas other metals disrupted the registry needed to stabilize the FCC-like arrangement39–41. By comparison, changing the metal centered in the NPc macrocycle produced only minor differences in lattice formation trends (Supplementary Fig. 15). These tests suggest that gas-lattice propensity is governed primarily by the 2D-layer and pillar architecture, where the channel size and periodic binding template decide the gas lattice propensity.

Explicit GCMC validation confirmed that these GA-selected pMOFs can stabilize extended, framework-templated xenon lattices with long-range registry. For the BCC-targeted framework (sql+NiINPc+Zn+P51), real-space snapshots show a highly regular lattice that propagates along the channel, and density maps display sharply localized maxima at repeatable periodic positions, consistent with a robust, pore-spanning BCC-like gas lattice (Fig. 8B). The FCC-targeted framework (sql+NiTNPc+Cu+P158) also develops a lattice-like motif, but the ordering is less uniform, with visibly larger positional variance across the channel cross-section (Fig. 8B). This contrast matches the broader screening trend that FCC-like matches are rarer and less definitive than BCC-like matches, indicating a narrower structural tolerance for stabilizing an FCC-type lattice.

Energy-grid analysis provides a mechanistic basis for this difference. In both pMOFs, the single-particle energy grid defines a strongly periodic binding landscape that pins the shell sites that seed lattice formation (Fig. 8C). However, the degree of commensurability differs: for the BCC case, the ideal xenon lattice length scale (14.67 Å) is closely approached by the framework’s energy-grid spacing (13.89 Å), yielding a near-commensurate template that supports sharp density maxima and low configurational variance. In the FCC case, the mismatch is larger when the ideal FCC length scale (18.39 Å) exceeds the framework spacing (16.89 Å), introducing greater geometric frustration that manifests as broadened density peaks and reduced registry in snapshots. This length-scale matching also suggests that gas-lattice formation should not be viewed as a purely energetic effect or as a simple consequence of the MOF periodicity aligning with an integer multiple of an ideal BCC xenon lattice. Although ordering reduces configurational freedom, the associated entropic penalty is mitigated under strong confinement, where pore geometry and binding-site topology already restrict the accessible disordered configurations of the adsorbed phase. Accordingly, the degree of ordering is governed not only by adsorption energetics, but also by how closely the framework spacing matches the characteristic xenon lattice length scale, together with the degree of geometric frustration that this mismatch introduces. Overall, the snapshot/density evidence together with energy-grid commensurability demonstrates that ML-guided inverse design over a modular pMOF space can deliberately generate hosts that template BCC-like and FCC-like xenon gas lattices.

Beyond identifying inverse-designed pMOFs that promote xenon lattice formation, these results highlight a useful distinction between the present approach and conventional performance-guided MOF design in the choice of design target. In most MOF discovery workflows, the framework is optimized to maximize bulk metrics, such as adsorption capacity, selectivity, or heat of adsorption. By contrast, our approach treats the adsorbed phase itself as an explicit structural target. Rather than asking which framework gives the highest average performance, we ask which framework geometry and pore topology can template a desired guest arrangement under confinement. In this sense, the framework is designed not only as a passive host but as a scaffold that stabilizes a specific correlated adsorption state. We view this as complementary to conventional MOF screening: performance-based metrics remain essential for evaluating application relevance, while adsorbate-phase design offers an additional route to uncover adsorption mechanisms, cooperative filling behavior, and spatially organized mixed-gas states that may not be apparent from scalar performance descriptors alone.

Discussion

In this study, we developed an end-to-end workflow to discover and design framework-templated gas lattices in MOFs using xenon as a model guest. Database screening identified Co-CAU-36, where PTM and density maps confirmed a pore-spanning BCC-like xenon lattice, supported by DFT, melt–quench recovery, and isotherm/energy-grid analyses that link periodic binding templates to cooperative filling. Probe studies revealed a gas-dependent gas lattice, and xenon/krypton mixtures showed strong pore-scale partitioning with xenon-induced krypton structuring. NEB calculations further indicated a low-barrier core diffusion pathway for krypton, highlighting separation relevance. Finally, ML-guided GA over a large pMOF space yielded candidates that stabilize BCC- and FCC-like lattice signatures in explicit validation simulations.

From a practical perspective, framework-templated gas lattices influence adsorption behavior by introducing more cooperative and spatially organized filling than in conventional disordered adsorption. Rather than universally maximizing total uptake, such ordering can help concentrate adsorption into a narrower operating window, produce sharper uptake responses, and stabilize distinct filling regimes within the pore. In mixed-gas systems, the same framework-templated ordering can also have implications for separation by promoting species-dependent spatial partitioning and differentiating thermodynamic and kinetic environments. As illustrated here for xenon/krypton co-adsorption, one component can occupy the templated shell region while the other is redistributed into a more weakly hindered core region, suggesting a separation mechanism that extends beyond site-affinity differences alone.

One reflection made in this pipeline is that the neighbor-count objective is not an absolute or complete metric for gas-lattice formation; rather, it provides a practical, early-stage screening signal that enriches lattice-prone candidates at scale. Future work can improve robustness by incorporating complementary order considerations (e.g., symmetry-aware local order parameters or structure-factor-based descriptors) that better distinguish true extended lattices from partial or local structuring. We also noticed that while inverse design successfully identified pMOF candidates that promote lattice-like configurations, the resulting top structures were not guaranteed to be experimentally stable. Integrating feasibility constraints directly into the optimization would enable co-optimization of gas-lattice propensity and chemical plausibility. While demonstrated here with noble gases and ethane, the same lattice-templating principles are expected to be even more consequential for anisotropic and polarizable molecules, such as CO2 and H2O. And as such, the foundation built in this work can be seen as a first step towards engineering structure-defined adsorbate states for application-directed separations and storage.

Methods

Grand canonical Monte Carlo (GCMC) simulations

All GCMC simulations were performed using RASPA 2.042. A Lennard–Jones cutoff of 12.5 Å was applied, and long-range corrections were handled according to the default/standard implementation in RASPA. Framework–adsorbate and adsorbate–adsorbate nonbonded interactions were described using the UFF force field27 for the MOF atoms and established TraPPE models43 with previously reported parameters for the adsorbates16,44.

For high-throughput database screening of MOF database, pMOF screening, and detailed gas lattice analyses of selected candidates, 10,000 initial cycles and 10,000 production cycles were used to ensure improved equilibration and more robust statistics. For the multicomponent GCMC, 1000 initial cycles and 50,000 production cycles were used to account for the relatively slower xenon–krypton swap events. All xenon/krypton multicomponent GCMC was conducted with a mol fraction of 0.2 xenon and 0.8 krypton according to previously reported ratios45. In all cases, structural configurations from the production stage were saved for post-processing analyses, RDF, NCs, and local structural classification, as described below.

Geometrical property calculations and snapshot analysis

Geometrical descriptors of MOFs (LCD and PLD) were computed using Zeo++46. These properties were used as pre-screening filters (e.g., pore accessibility). To quantify the adsorbate lattice from GCMC outputs, RDFs g(r) were computed from xenon snapshots using MDAnalysis47. The RDF-based gas lattice signatures were then converted into an interpretable packing descriptor, NC, by integrating the RDF in a peak-centered window and scaling by the adsorbate number density:

Where ri denotes the target peak position (e.g., first-neighbor shell) and Δ is the half-width of the integration window. This definition couples structural correlations (peak sharpness/intensity in g(r)) with packing density, enabling direct ranking of candidate MOFs by the propensity to form crystalline-like local environments.

Polyhedral template matching

All PTM31 calculations were conducted in OVITO software48. PTM compares the local neighborhood around each xenon particle against ideal reference polyhedra and assigns the best-matching crystalline environment when present, using BCC clusters as templates31. A cutoff of 0.11 was used following the default setting.

Energy grid calculations

All energy grid calculations were conducted with GRIDAY software49. Xenon parameters from previously reported parameter was used44.

Construction of pMOFs

The 3D pMOF library was constructed by stacking 2D MOF monolayers and inserting organic pillars using a custom Python workflow built on the Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE)50. The organic pillar library was derived from a previously reported MOF-constructor/linker database36,37. Linkers from the linker database were converted into an N-donor pillar format where applicable, and their chemical existence/identity was cross-checked using PubChem51.

To ensure consistent and sterically reasonable insertion, a pillar in-plane orientation was defined in a local node reference direction. The pillar orientation was determined via principal component analysis over its hydrogen (or backbone) coordinates to obtain a principal axis. The node reference direction was defined by a local vector constructed from framework atoms adjacent to the coordinating group (e.g., near carboxylate, N-heterocycle, or thioether donors). Pillars were then inserted between stacked layers and rotated to align their principal axis with the node direction. Donor-specific rotational offsets (e.g., 90° for certain O/N donor geometries) were applied to reduce steric clashes and enforce chemically reasonable coordination geometries. The resulting structures were subsequently subjected to geometry cleanup (energy minimization) before adsorption screening.

Labeled training data was generated by randomly sampling ~3000 pMOFs for each of three representative 2D-layer topologies. For every sampled pMOF, xenon GCMC at 10 K and 1 bar and NCs from production snapshots were calculated. These data were further used as a machine learning dataset for inverse design.

Machine learning and genetic algorithm

The MOFNet model was used to predict the NC from categorical representations of pMOFs37. Each MOF was encoded using indices representing the topology, organic linkers, metals, and pillars, which were transformed into continuous feature vectors using learnable embedding layers with a fixed embedding dimension.

The embedded features were concatenated and projected into a latent feature space through a fully connected layer, followed by batch normalization and ReLU activation52. The resulting representations were then processed by a two-layer multilayer perceptron, with each layer consisting of a fully connected transformation, batch normalization, an activation function, and dropout. A final linear layer mapped the learned representation to a single scalar prediction. The model was trained for 100 epochs with a batch size of 32 and a learning rate of 3.0 × 10−4. A dropout rate of 0.1 was applied.

A GA was implemented to explore candidate compositions of pMOFs. Each candidate was encoded as a chromosome consisting of discrete genes representing organic linkers, metals, and pillars according to the topology specification. The initial population comprised 100 candidates generated by randomly sampling valid building blocks for each gene from predefined candidate pools. Selection was performed using tournament selection. New candidates were generated through crossover and mutation, while the top 30 candidates were retained via elitism to form the next generation. After the final generation, the 30 highest-fitness candidates were selected as the final GA outputs.
