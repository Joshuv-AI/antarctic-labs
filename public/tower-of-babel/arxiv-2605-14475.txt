GeoVista: Visually Grounded Active Perception for Ultra-High-Resolution Remote Sensing Understanding
Jiashun Zhu, Ronghao Fu, Jiasen Hu, Nachuan Xing, Xu Na, Xiao Yang, Zhiwen Lin, Weipeng Zhang, Lang Sun, Zhiheng Xue, Haoran Liu, Weijie Zhang, Bo Yang, College of Computer Science, Technology, Jilin University, China, [-3pt] Key Laboratory of Symbolic Computation, Knowledge Engineering of Ministry of Education {zhujs25,hujs5522}@mails.jlu.edu.cn , {furh,ybo}@jlu.edu.cn

Abstract
Interpreting ultra-high-resolution (UHR) remote sensing images requires models to search for sparse and tiny visual evidence across large-scale scenes. Existing remote sensing vision-language models can inspect local regions with zooming and cropping tools, but most exploration strategies follow either a one-shot focus or a single sequential trajectory. Such single-path exploration can lose global context, leave scattered regions unvisited, and revisit or count the same evidence multiple times. To this end, we propose GeoVista , a planning-driven active perception framework for UHR remote sensing interpretation. Instead of committing to one zooming path, GeoVista first builds a global exploration plan, then verifies multiple candidate regions through branch-wise local inspection, while maintaining an explicit evidence state for cross-region aggregation and de-duplication. To enable this behavior, we introduce APEX-GRO , a cold-start supervised trajectory corpus that reformulates diverse UHR tasks as Global-Region-Object interactive reasoning processes with a unified, scale-invariant spatial representation. We further design an Observe-Plan-Track mechanism for global observation, adaptive region inspection, and evidence tracking, and align the model with a GRPO-based strategy using step-wise rewards for planning, localization, and final answer correctness. Experiments on RSHR-Bench, XLRS-Bench, and LRS-VQA show that GeoVista achieves state-of-the-art performance. Code and dataset are available at GeoVista .

Introduction

Ultra-high-resolution (UHR) remote sensing images typically cover large geographic areas
(
6
;
16
)
, while task-relevant evidence may appear as sparse, tiny, and spatially scattered regions. This mismatch between scene scale and evidence scale makes UHR interpretation different from natural image understanding. A model is expected not only to recognize local visual patterns, but also to decide where to search, which regions warrant closer inspection, and how to integrate evidence observed at different scales. Therefore, UHR remote sensing tasks naturally call for multi-scale observation, progressive evidence acquisition, and sequential decision-making
(
22
;
35
)
.

Existing studies mainly address this challenge from two directions. One line of work reduces visual redundancy through one-shot region selection or static visual pruning, including text-guided visual pruning methods, GeoLLaVA-8K
(
34
)
, and C2F
(
22
)
. These methods improve efficiency by selecting compact visual evidence, but their focusing decisions are usually made before reasoning proceeds and cannot be easily revised. Another line of work equips models with interactive observation operations, such as cropping, resolution adjustment, and local zooming, as in ZoomEarth
(
19
)
and GeoEyes
(
35
)
. These methods enable fine-grained inspection during inference, but their exploration is often organized as a fixed or single-path process, making it difficult to coordinate multiple candidate regions under complex scene layouts.

As illustrated in Figure

, this design leads to a recurring limitation that we refer to as
single-trajectory bias
. Single-focus approaches, such as ZoomEarth
(
19
)
, perform one-shot cropping to enlarge local details, but they may discard global context and miss spatially scattered targets. Repeated-focus approaches, such as GeoEyes
(

)
, support iterative observation through multi-step zooming, but their search process often follows a depth-first trajectory toward a single local region. Once the model commits to an incomplete or incorrect path, other relevant regions may remain unexplored. Moreover, without an explicit evidence state, the model cannot reliably distinguish newly discovered evidence from regions that have already been verified, resulting in missed detections or duplicated counting. As shown in Figure

This motivates a central question:
how can a model decide where to look, at what scale to inspect, and when to stop, while coordinating global search, local verification, and evidence aggregation?
To answer this question, we propose
GeoVista
, a planning-driven active perception framework for UHR remote sensing understanding. Given a task and a global observation, GeoVista first generates a structured exploration plan over multiple candidate regions of interest. It then performs branch-wise verification, adaptively re-focuses uncertain regions, and updates an explicit evidence state that records inspected locations, verified findings, and their spatial references. The final answer is produced by aggregating evidence across branches, which expands search coverage while preserving global context and reducing duplicated evidence.

Training such a framework is challenging because standard VQA-style supervision provides only final answers and does not specify how a model should search across scales. We therefore construct
APEX-GRO
, a cold-start supervised trajectory corpus that teaches the model to express UHR tasks as cross-scale interactive reasoning processes with a discrete, scale-invariant spatial syntax across Global, Region, and Object levels. Based on this initialization, we introduce an
Observe-Plan-Track
mechanism to coordinate global observation, region planning, branch-wise inspection, and evidence tracking. Finally, we apply a GRPO-based alignment strategy with step-wise rewards for planning quality, localization progress, and final answer correctness. As shown in Figure
2
, GeoVista achieves the highest mean accuracy on XLRS-Bench. Tool-Interleaved Visual Reasoning.

Beyond static image-text understanding, recent work has explored tool-interleaved visual reasoning, where models dynamically acquire visual evidence during the reasoning process. The “Thinking with Images” paradigm

However, these works are mainly designed for natural images, documents, medical images, or synthetic reasoning tasks, and their data distributions are not directly aligned with UHR remote sensing imagery, where task-relevant evidence is often tiny, sparse, and distributed across large geographic areas. Meanwhile, traditional RS VQA datasets such as RSVQA
(
20
)
and EarthVQA
(
37
)
mainly provide single-turn question-answer supervision, offering limited guidance on how a model should search, zoom, and verify evidence across scales.

Active Perception for UHR Remote Sensing.

Active perception methods attempt to address the resolution bottleneck by enabling models to select regions, adjust resolutions, or zoom into local details during inference. In remote sensing, methods such as ZoomEarth
(
19
)
, and GeoEyes
(
35
)
demonstrate the effectiveness of interactive observation for UHR image interpretation. Related trajectory datasets, including LRS-GRO
(
19
)
and UHR-CoZ
(
35
)
, further introduce multi-level annotations or interleaved reasoning trajectories for active perception. Nevertheless, existing approaches usually organize exploration as a single sequential path, which limits their ability to compare multiple Regions of Interest (ROIs), maintain global context, track verified evidence, and avoid missed or duplicated detections. In contrast, GeoVista introduces planning-driven multi-branch exploration through an Observe-Plan-Track mechanism, enabling global planning, recursive local verification, and evidence aggregation for UHR remote sensing understanding.

3
Methodology

Given an UHR remote sensing image
I
and a user query
Q
, our goal is to generate an answer
A
by actively acquiring and aggregating visual evidence across multiple scales. Instead of encoding the entire image in a single forward pass or following a single zooming trajectory,
GeoVista
formulates UHR interpretation as a planning-driven active perception process. The model first observes a global view, then plans multiple candidate ROIs, verifies them through branch-wise local inspection, and finally aggregates the verified evidence for prediction.

This section introduces the three key components of GeoVista. First, we construct
APEX-GRO
, a supervised trajectory corpus that teaches the model to conduct cross-scale interactive reasoning over Global, Region, and Object levels. Second, we present the
Observe-Plan-Track
inference framework, which enables global planning, recursive local verification, and explicit evidence-state tracking. Third, we describe a two-stage training pipeline that combines supervised fine-tuning with GRPO-based alignment for long-horizon active perception.

3.1
APEX-GRO: Cross-Scale Interleaved Trajectory Construction

UHR remote sensing interpretation often requires more than a single
image-question-answer pair: a model must decide where to inspect, invoke visual
tools, update intermediate evidence, and continue exploration when local
regions remain ambiguous. However, most existing RS VQA datasets provide only
final-answer supervision and do not specify how cross-scale evidence should be
acquired. To provide a cold start for this behavior, we construct

APEX-GRO
(
A

Trajectory Format.

Each trajectory in APEX-GRO is represented as a sequence of reasoning
states, tool actions, visual observations, and evidence updates.

\tau=(z_{0},a_{0},o_{1},z_{1},a_{1},o_{2},\dots,z_{T},A),

where
z_{t}
is the textual reasoning state,
a_{t}
is a visual action such as
cropping or zooming,
o_{t}
is the returned visual observation, and
A
is the
final answer.
We define each visual action as
a_{t}=\texttt{crop}(b_{t},\ell_{t})
, where
b_{t}

is the selected ROI and
\ell_{t}\in\{L_{0},L_{1},L_{2}\}
denotes the global,
region, or object level.
In practice, trajectories are serialized with functional fields such as

<tool_call>
,
<think>
, and
<answer>
. Within

<think>
, sub-steps such as
[Observation]
,
[Plan]
,
and
[Track]
are used to update evidence and monitor progress. This
format provides supervision for both tool use and evidence aggregation.

, APEX-GRO is synthesized

The checklist only guides task decomposition; exact oracle coordinates and final answers are hidden from the teacher model, reserved for automatic validation and human verification. Third, a teacher VLM (
gemini-3.1-flash-lite-preview
) generates tool-interleaved trajectories by alternating among reasoning, planning, crop-based inspection, and evidence tracking. Finally, we apply quality control to remove trajectories with coordinate leakage, invalid tool syntax, malformed spatial references, or inconsistent final answers, and drop evaluation benchmark overlaps.

Global-Region-Object Hierarchy.

APEX-GRO organizes UHR perception into a three-level hierarchy. At the

Global
level (
L_{0}
), the model observes a downsampled global view

V^{0}_{\text{global}}
and produces a macroscopic exploration plan. At the

Region
level (
L_{1}
), it inspects candidate ROIs
V^{1}_{\text{crop}}

to verify structural patterns and narrow down task-relevant areas. At the

Object
level (
L_{2}
), it performs fine-grained inspection

V^{2}_{\text{crop}}
for small targets, object attributes, and precise
localization. This hierarchy encourages global coverage before local evidence
acquisition and reduces unnecessary inspection of irrelevant regions.

Scale-Invariant Spatial Representation.

A central challenge in UHR reasoning is that the same object may appear at
different pixel scales across crop levels. Absolute pixel coordinates do not
generalize well across image sizes, while unrestricted continuous coordinates
are difficult to serialize reliably in language-model outputs. We therefore
adopt a discrete relative coordinate system. For a crop ROI

b_{i}=(x_{\min},y_{\min},w,h)
, the forward is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(x,y|b_{i})

\displaystyle=\left(\mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{x-x_{\min}}{w}1000\right),\mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{y-y_{\min}}{h}1000\right)\right).

(1)

For a bounding box
B=(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2})
, the transform is applied to its two
corner points, i.e.,

\mathcal{T}(B|b_{i})=(\mathcal{T}(x_{1},y_{1}|b_{i}),\mathcal{T}(x_{2},y_{2}|b_{i}))
.
This representation provides a nominal relative resolution of
0.1\%
within
each ROI and decouples spatial outputs from the absolute image resolution.

3.2
GeoVista: Observe-Plan-Track Active Perception Framework

GeoVista follows the same action space and spatial syntax as APEX-GRO during inference. Given an UHR image
I
and a query
Q
, it avoids greedily committing to a single zooming path. Instead, it first constructs a global plan over multiple ROIs, and then verifies these ROIs with local observations while maintaining a structured evidence state. Although all branches are decoded sequentially in one autoregressive trace, they are conditioned on a shared global plan and correspond to different planned ROIs. We therefore refer to this process as
multi-branch exploration
.

Observe: Global Scene Observation.

GeoVista first obtains a downsampled global view
V^{0}_{\text{global}}
of the UHR image. This view provides broad spatial coverage and supports reasoning about scene layout, potential target distribution, and task-relevant search regions. The global view is not expected to resolve all fine-grained details; instead, it provides the context for exploration planning.

Plan: Multi-ROI Exploration Planning.

Conditioned on
Q
and
V^{0}_{\text{global}}
, the policy
\pi_{\theta}
generates a structured plan
P^{0}=(p_{1}^{0},\dots,p_{K}^{0})
, where each item
p_{i}^{0}=(r_{i}^{0},g_{i}^{0},s_{i}^{0})
records an ROI
r_{i}^{0}
, a verification goal
g_{i}^{0}
, and its current status
s_{i}^{0}
. Here
K
is constrained by the available tool-call budget. The plan specifies which regions should be inspected and what evidence should be verified in each region, enabling targeted exploration instead of unstructured zooming.

Track: Branch-wise Verification and Evidence Update.

For each pending ROI
r_{i}^{d}
at depth
d
, GeoVista extracts a high-resolution crop
v_{i}^{d}=\mathrm{Crop}(I,r_{i}^{d})
from the original image and decodes a branch trajectory
\tau_{i}^{d}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid Q,V^{0}_{\text{global}},P^{d},E,r_{i}^{d},v_{i}^{d})
. After each branch, the evidence state is updated as
E\leftarrow\operatorname{Update}(E,\tau_{i}^{d})
. Each evidence entry records the inspected ROI, global coordinates, verification status, local observations, detected objects or counts, and unresolved sub-goals. Local crop coordinates are mapped back to the global image frame before being written into
E
, which helps reduce redundant exploration and duplicated counting. If a crop still contains dense or ambiguous content, GeoVista can append a sub-plan
P_{i}^{d+1}
and continue inspection at a finer scale. The process terminates when all planned ROIs are verified, the maximum depth is reached, or the tool-call budget is exhausted.

Evidence Aggregation.

After branch-wise verification, GeoVista aggregates the evidence state into a
final prediction
A\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid Q,V^{0}_{\text{global}},P,E)
.
The aggregation step maps local findings back to their corresponding nodes in
the global plan, forming a hierarchical evidence tree with verified
observations, rejected hypotheses, and unresolved cases. The final answer is
therefore generated from organized spatial evidence rather than an unstructured
long reasoning history.

3.3
Training Pipeline: Supervised Cold-Start and GRPO Alignment

Figure 4:
Training pipeline of GeoVista.
Stage I performs supervised fine-tuning on APEX-GRO trajectories. Stage II applies GRPO-based alignment on verifiable grounding and counting tasks. The plan reward in the figure is a simplified illustration; the exact state-machine formulation is described in the text.

As illustrated in Figure
4
, GeoVista is trained in two stages. The first stage performs supervised fine-tuning on APEX-GRO to initialize the model with tool syntax, scale-invariant spatial grounding, and cross-scale reasoning patterns. The second stage applies GRPO-based alignment to improve long-horizon active perception on verifiable tasks.

Stage I: Supervised Fine-Tuning.

Given the trajectory corpus
\mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}}=\{(I,Q,\tau)\}
, we optimize the model to imitate the interleaved reasoning trajectories:

\small\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}=-\sum_{(I,Q,\tau)\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}}}\sum_{t=1}^{|\tau|}\log\pi_{\theta}(z_{t}\mid I,Q,z_{<t}).

(2)

Here,
z_{t}
denotes the serialized textual token at step
t
, including reasoning text, plan items, tool calls, evidence updates, and final answers. This stage teaches the model how to produce parseable trajectories and how to decompose large-scale visual reasoning into global observation, regional inspection, and object-level verification.

Stage II: GRPO-Based Agentic Alignment.

SFT teaches the model how to imitate reference trajectories, but it does not directly optimize exploration strategies in unseen scenes. To further align GeoVista with task success, we apply GRPO
(
26
)
on grounding and counting tasks, whose outputs can be automatically verified with bounding boxes or object counts.

For each training instance
x=(I,Q)
, we sample a group of
G
trajectories
\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}
from the current policy and compute a scalar reward
R(o_{i})
for each trajectory. The policy is optimized with the clipped GRPO objective:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|o_{i}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_{i}|}\min\Big(r_{i,t}\hat{A}_{i},\operatorname{clip}(r_{i,t},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_{i}\Big)-\beta_{\text{KL}}D_{\text{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}\right)\Bigg],

(3)

where
r_{i,t}=\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,t}\mid x,o_{i,<t})/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i,t}\mid x,o_{i,<t}).
The reference model
\pi_{\text{ref}}
is initialized from the SFT checkpoint.

Reward Design.

For a trajectory output
o
, the total reward combines format validity, task accuracy, localization quality, and plan-following quality:

R(o)=\begin{cases}\omega_{1}r_{\text{fmt}}+\omega_{2}r_{\text{acc}}+\omega_{3}r_{\text{iou}}+\omega_{4}r_{\text{plan}},&\text{if }r_{\text{fmt}}=1,\\
-\gamma_{\text{fmt}},&\text{if }r_{\text{fmt}}=0.\end{cases}

(4)

Invalid formats receive a fixed negative reward because malformed outputs cannot be parsed into valid tool actions, spatial references, or evidence states. Beyond standard format (
r_{\text{fmt}}
) rewards, we introduce three core components:

Accuracy Reward (
r_{\text{acc}}
).

The accuracy reward evaluates the final task output. For counting tasks, we use a smoothed relative-error score, defined as
r_{\text{acc}}^{\text{count}}=1-\tanh(|\hat{y}-y|/\max(y,1))
, where
\hat{y}
and
y
denote the predicted and ground-truth counts. For grounding tasks, we measure the normalized distance between the predicted and ground-truth box centers, i.e.,
r_{\text{acc}}^{\text{ground}}=1-\tanh(\|c(\hat{B})-c(B)\|_{2}/1000)
, where
\hat{B}
and
B
are the predicted and ground-truth boxes in the normalized coordinate space, and
c(\cdot)
returns the box center. This term rewards final task correctness, while the IoU reward below provides a stricter spatial overlap signal.

IoU Reward (
r_{\text{iou}}
).

For single-target grounding, localization quality is measured by the standard IoU, i.e.,
r_{\text{iou}}^{\text{ground}}=\operatorname{IoU}(\hat{B},B)
. For counting tasks with predicted candidate boxes
\mathcal{P}
, we compute
r_{\text{iou}}^{\text{count}}=|\mathcal{P}|^{-1}\sum_{\hat{B}_{i}\in\mathcal{P}}s(\hat{B}_{i})
, where
s(\hat{B}_{i})
is a strict inclusion score that rewards boxes covering matched ground-truth targets and penalizes artificially enlarged regions. This term encourages the model to localize counted objects rather than relying on coarse or over-expanded regions.

State-Aware Dynamic Plan Reward (
r_{\text{plan}}
).

The plan reward encourages the model to plan before acting, follow the plan during exploration, and update the evidence state consistently. We first compute a task-dependent difficulty coefficient
\mathcal{D}\in(0,1]
: for counting,
\mathcal{D}=1-\exp(-\lambda_{c}y)
increases with the number of targets; for grounding,
\mathcal{D}=\exp(-\lambda_{g}|B|/10^{6})
is larger for smaller targets in the normalized
1000\times 1000
space.

We evaluate plan execution using a lightweight state machine where items are either pending or completed. Valid transitions require completing a pending item after inspection or expanding it into a sub-plan. Based on the trajectory, we assign a score
Q_{\text{plan}}\in\{1,0,-1\}
:
1
for valid execution,
0
for bypassing verification, and
-1
for state violations (e.g., skipping unvisited regions or inconsistent states). The final plan reward is defined as:

r_{\text{plan}}=\begin{cases}\alpha\mathcal{D},&\text{if }Q_{\text{plan}}=1,\\
-\beta\mathcal{D},&\text{if }Q_{\text{plan}}=0,\\
-\gamma_{\text{plan}},&\text{if }Q_{\text{plan}}=-1.\end{cases}

(5)

Thus, structured exploration is rewarded more strongly on difficult examples, while shallow reasoning on simple examples is not over-penalized; severe state-machine violations still receive a fixed negative reward to preserve execution fidelity.

4.3
Fine-Grained Analysis on XLRS-Bench

As detailed in Table
2
, GeoVista consistently achieves the best results across all perception sub-categories, including Counting (43.62), Scene Classification (53.08), Object Spatial Relationship (42.25), and Object Properties (57.58). Compared with the strongest baseline in each category, these correspond to improvements of 2.37, 1.41, 8.05, and 16.86 points, respectively. These gains indicate that concurrent ROI exploration effectively improves fine-grained evidence acquisition in UHR scenes.

In addition to accuracy, GeoVista exhibits a distinct inference pattern. It uses more tool calls per question than prior zoom-in methods (
3.82
Tools/Q), reflecting its broader multi-ROI exploration strategy. At the same time, it maintains a lower token consumption per reasoning turn (164.96 Tok/Turn) than ZoomEarth (274.30) and GeoEyes (263.15). 5
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce GeoVista, an active perception framework for UHR remote sensing imagery. By replacing isolated single-path scanning with Planning-Driven Parallel Multi-Hop Exploration, it achieves seamless global-local integration with high precision. Furthermore, we constructed APEX-GRO, a rich interleaved reasoning training set that provides the crucial scale-invariant spatial syntax for initial supervised fine-tuning. We further aligned the agent using a customized GRPO pipeline. By applying multi-dimensional constraints and dynamic rewards, we forced the agent to "think" before acting, successfully internalizing a strict "Observe-Plan-Track" process. Extensive evaluations across RSHR-Bench, XLRS-Bench, and LRS-VQA demonstrate that GeoVista achieves state-of-the-art performance, conclusively proving that structured parallel reasoning and strict step-by-step adherence are the keys to unlocking complex scene understanding in expansive visual environments.

To generate high-quality, leakage-free, end-to-end reasoning trajectories, APEX-GRO employs an automated and human-in-the-loop pipeline comprising four core modules.This pipeline aims to generate the Agent-Zoom-SFT dataset for model fine-tuning through systematic constraints and validation.The specific construction process is as follows:

1. Data Collection & Difficulty Assessment

The pipeline first collects samples from multi-source remote sensing datasets (e.g., DOTA-v1.5, FAIR1M, EarthVQA), covering diverse tasks such as counting, visual grounding, route planning, classification, and spatial relationships. To ensure data quality, the system introduces a Difficulty Assessment mechanism, categorizing the generated questions and images into two types:

•

Hard Cases:
100% manual labeling is adopted to ensure the absolute correctness of multi-level parallel zooming reasoning trajectories in complex scenarios.

•

Simple Cases:
Rapidly processed through an Automated Labeling pipeline to extract target areas (VLM Regions).

2. Context Engine & Constraints Injection

After acquiring the foundational data, the context engine is responsible for generating structured prompts. This engine fuses three major input streams:

•

Trajectory Compiler:
Converts raw coordinates into a Nested Checklist to provide structured targets.

•

Task-Specific SOP Template:
Strictly defines the output format of the CoT.

•

Constraint Injection:
Compulsorily injects behavioral constraints (Hide Oracle Knowledge) to ensure a Human Visual Simulation exploration process.

Appendix F
Limitations and Future Work

Although GeoVista has shown significant improvements in UHR remote sensing understanding, several limitations remain for future exploration. First, due to computational constraints, the reinforcement learning phase was limited to two epochs. More extensive training with a larger exploration budget may yield further performance gains and deeper emergent agentic behaviors. Second, while our multi-dimensional reward design effectively guides the active perception process, we did not conduct an exhaustive ablation study to quantitatively isolate the exact contribution of each group reward component. Third, our current RL alignment focuses exclusively on verifiable reasoning tasks (Reasoning RL) such as grounding and counting. We have not explored yet a two-stage reinforcement learning pipeline that incorporates General RL for broader open-ended semantic alignment. Finally, our framework utilizes the standard GRPO formulation; future research could investigate derivative algorithms of GRPO to further enhance sample efficiency and training stability.

Appendix G
Broader Impacts

This work advances ultra-high-resolution remote sensing understanding, offering significant positive societal impacts in domains such as disaster response, urban planning, environmental monitoring, and agricultural assessment. However, as with any advanced remote sensing analysis technology, there are potential dual-use risks, including unauthorized surveillance or potential privacy infringements. To mitigate these risks, our GeoVista framework and the APEX-GRO dataset are developed using publicly available, anonymized satellite and aerial imagery. We strongly advocate for the responsible, transparent, and regulated deployment of these technologies in strict compliance with international privacy standards and ethical guidelines.
