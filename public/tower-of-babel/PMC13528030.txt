Integrating serological markers of Helicobacter pylori and pepsinogen to improve detection of gastric precancerous conditions
Authors: Zetian Chen, Hongxin Huang, Huimin Tao, Rui Wu, Zekuan Xu, Qiang Zhan, Zheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastric cancer (GC) remains a significant global health burden due to its high incidence and mortality, especially in China. This study aimed to evaluate the performance of combined serological assessment of pepsinogen (PG) and Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) antibodies for identifying gastric precancerous conditions.MethodsFrom November 2016 to September 2019, an observational cohort study was performed to evaluate serum levels of PG and H. pylori antibodies. Among 19,879 participants, four groups were defined based on serological status: Group A (PG–/ H. pylori–), Group B (PG–/ H. pylori+), Group C (PG+/ H. pylori+), and Group D (PG+/ H. pylori–). Participants from Groups C and D were included in the analysis, while individuals from Groups A and B were randomly selected at a 3:1 ratio relative to Groups D and C, respectively. In total, 1,152 subjects underwent endoscopic and histopathological assessment.ResultsGroup C demonstrated significantly elevated odds of atrophic gastritis (AG) (OR = 1.98; 95% CI: 1.17–3.26) and intestinal metaplasia (IM) (OR = 1.40; 95% CI: 0.73–2.67) compared to Group A. In the gastric corpus, the odds of AG and IM were 5.0 and 6.8 times higher, respectively, in Group C versus Group A.ConclusionCombined serological assessment of PG and anti- H. pylori IgG improves the identification of gastric precancerous conditions, with particularly enhanced performance in the gastric corpus.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-04992-2.Keywords: Atrophic gastritis, ABC classification, Helicobacter pylori, Pepsinogen assay, Gastric cancer

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) ranks fifth in terms of incidence and mortality rates globally [1]. In 2022, there were approximately 968,350 new cases and 659,853 deaths due to GC worldwide [1], with nearly 40% of these cases occurring in China [2]. Alarmingly, over 60% of patients present with advanced disease at the time of diagnosis, primarily due to the inadequate detection rate of atrophic gastritis (AG), intestinal metaplasia (IM), and early-stage GC [3].

AG and IM are well-recognized precursors to GC. The incidence rates of AG (25.8% [4]) and IM (16.79% to 38.56% [5]) are high in the general population in China. Previous study indicated an incidence rate of 20.8% for these conditions [6], with affected individuals demonstrating a significantly higher risk of developing GC [7]. Early diagnosis and intervention in patients with AG or IM can substantially reduce the incidence of GC [8, 9], presenting a feasible preventive strategy. Despite the presence of a national GC screening program in Korea, only 56.3% of the eligible population underwent screening via gastroscopy [10]. In contrast, the availability of professionals and facilities for gastroscopy in China is relatively limited [6], resulting in lower screening rates. This underscores the urgent need for simpler, safer, and more efficient screening methods for individuals with precancerous lesions [11].

The ABC method, which combines serum pepsinogen (PG) level measurements with the detection of anti-Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) antibodies, was initially proposed to assess gastric health [12]. PG is secreted by the gastric mucosa and is classified into PGI and PGII. Serum PG levels serve as a marker for AG, reflecting the condition of the gastric mucosa [13], while H. pylori infection is closely associated with gastric precancerous lesions [14, 15]. Several studies have demonstrated the diagnostic utility of this method for identifying gastric precancerous lesions [16]. However, Huang et al. [17] reported that there exists limited data on the sensitivity and discrimination of these serologic markers.

Given the high incidence rates of gastric precancerous lesions and GC in China, coupled with the pressing need for an effective screening program, there has been no large-scale population study utilizing the ABC method in this context [11]. Therefore, this study was conducted to investigate the diagnostic value of ABC screening in the Chinese population.

Methods

Study population

This observational cohort study, conducted from November 2016 to September 2019, integrated serological testing for PG and H. pylori. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuxi People’s Hospital (KS201511). All procedures adhered to the ethical principles of the institutional and national research committees, the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its subsequent revisions, or comparable ethical standards. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians.

A total of 50,063 individuals aged 40 to 69 years from seven geographically diverse communities in Xinwu District, Wuxi City, Jiangsu Province, China, were invited to participate [18]. These communities were randomly selected from 68 available areas to ensure representativeness. Among them, 31,508 agreed to take part. Exclusion criteria included a history of cancer, gastric surgery (including endoscopic submucosal dissection or mucosal resection), coagulopathy, severe cardiopulmonary, hepatic, renal, or psychiatric conditions, and failure to provide informed consent. To avoid potential interference with PG measurements, participants confirmed that they had not taken acid suppressants, gastroprotective agents, or antiplatelet drugs (e.g., aspirin) within the preceding two weeks.

Ultimately, 19,879 eligible participants were included. Fasting blood samples were collected to measure serum levels of PGI, PGII, the PGI/II ratio (PGR), and anti-H. pylori IgG. PG quantification was performed using commercial kits (Wuxi Jiangyuan Industrial Technology and Trade, China), following the manufacturers’ instructions. According to the kit criteria, atrophic serum PG (PG+) was defined as PGI < 60 ng/mL and PGR < 6.0. H. pylori IgG antibodies were detected using a commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Assure Tech, China), with seropositivity defined as antibody concentration ≥ 10 U/mL, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Detailed serological methods have been described previously [11].

Participants were categorized into four groups based on PG and H. pylori serology: Group A: PG–/H. pylori–, Group B: PG–/H. pylori+, Group C: PG+/H. pylori+, Group D: PG+/H. pylori–. Information regarding prior H. pylori eradication therapy was not systematically collected, and thus Group D (PG+/H. pylori–) may include both individuals with spontaneous seroreversion due to advanced atrophy and those with a history of successful eradication therapy.

All individuals in Groups C and D were included in the endoscopic sub-study. Participants from Groups A and B were randomly selected at a 3:1 ratio relative to Groups D and C, respectively (Fig. 1). In total, 1,152 subjects underwent endoscopic and histopathological evaluation.

Process of inclusion for study population. 50,063 participants aged 40 to 69 years were recruited from seven communities in the Xinwu District, and 31,508 were willing to participate in the study. Of these, participants with a history of developed cancer; gastric surgery, including endoscopic submucosal dissection or endoscopic mucosal resection; coagulopathy; and serious cardiopulmonary, liver, kidney, or psychiatric disorders; or those failed to complete the questionnaire were excluded from the study. Ultimately, 19,879 participants met the inclusion criteria. People in group C and D were all recruited for the study, and those in group A and B were recruited randomly at a ratio of 3:1 to group D and C, respectively. Through the telephone invitation, 1,174 people intended to participant in the endoscopy screening program. On that day, 22 people refused to underdo the endoscopy, and in the end, 1,152 participants underwent endoscopy and histopathological assessment

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data were collected via self-administered questionnaires. Variables included sex, age, height (cm), weight (kg), education level (high school or below vs. above high school), high-salt diet (yes/no), frequency of fruit and vegetable intake (< 3 times/week vs. ≥3 times/week), smoking status (current or former vs. never), alcohol use (current or former vs. never), tea consumption (yes/no), presence of gastrointestinal symptoms (yes/no), and family history of GC. A positive family history was defined as GC in a first-degree relative.

Endoscopy and histopathological assessment

Endoscopic procedures were performed by 20 experienced gastroenterologists, each with over five years of expertise. Biopsy samples were taken from both the gastric antrum and corpus, with at least one sample per site. Gastric mucosal biopsies were assessed in accordance with the standard criteria from the World Health Organization for the classification of GC and the updated Sydney System for grading and classification of gastritis by two expert gastrointestinal pathologists [19]. Each participant received a diagnosis regarding the presence of AG, IM, or GC.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 21.0 (IBM Corp, USA). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means ± standard deviations. Group comparisons were made using the chi-square test for categorical variables and analysis of variance for continuous measures. Logistic regression models were applied to evaluate associations with AG and IM, calculating odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of participants in four groups

A total of 1,152 patients were included, comprising 533 in group A, 310 in group B, 85 in group C, and 224 in group D. There were no significant differences were found in age (P = 0.773), BMI (P = 0.596), education level (P = 0.444), high-salt diet (P = 0.454), intake of fruit and vegetables (P = 0.977), drinking (P = 0.212), tea consumption (P = 0.506), gastrointestinal symptom (P = 0.578), and family history (P = 0.514) among the four groups (Table 1). In this study, the number of males in groups B and C was significantly higher than in groups A and D, which is related to the higher prevalence of H. pylori infection among males in the population [20].

Participants with AG and IM in four groups

In this study, 98 (18.4%), 73 (23.5%), 26 (30.6%), and 57 (25.4%) participants were detected with AG in group A, B, C, and D, respectively. Meanwhile, 61 (11.1%), 54 (17.4%), 13 (15.3%), and 41 (18.3%) participants were suffered from IM in four groups, respectively. Participants in group C were at two-fold increased odds of having AG compared to participants in group A (OR = 1.956; 95%CI, 1.174-3.260). Meanwhile, the prevalence rates of AG in group B (OR = 1.367; 95%CI, 0.971-1.924) and group D (OR = 1.515; 95%CI, 1.044-2.198) were also higher than group A. The prevalence rates of AG in group B, C, and D were higher than group A when the confounding factors were adjusted (Table 2). Furthermore, the prevalence rates of IM in group B, C, and D were higher than group A (group B: OR = 1.632, 95%CI, 1.098-2.427; group C: OR = 1.397, 95%CI, 0.731-2.671; group D: OR = 1.734, 95%CI, 1.127-2.668), and the same conclusion was drawn when the confounding factors were adjusted (Table 2).

Discussion

Our study suggests that combined serum PG concentrations and serum anti-H. pylori IgG antibody can significantly increase the detection rate of AG and IM in people. Meanwhile, the AG and IM in gastric corpus can be diagnosed via ABC method effectively. Furthermore, more GC was detected via ABC method. Therefore, this method is feasible to detect the precursors of GC in clinical application.

The prevalence rates of AG and IM in China are reported to be 25.8% and 23.6%, respectively [4, 21]. In our previous study, the prevalence rates of AG and IM in this district were 20.8% [6, 11]. In the current study, we found that 22.0% of individuals suffered from AG, while 14.7% had IM among the 1,152 participants, which is consistent with our earlier findings.

Currently, there is limited research on the large-scale application of the ABC method for diagnosing AG and IM within the Chinese population. Zagari et al. [13] reported that the combination of PG, anti-H. pylori antibodies, and gastrin-17 serological assays appears to be a reliable tool for the diagnosis of AG (sensitivity was 74.7%, 95% CI, 62.0-84.3; specificity was 95.6%, 95%CI, 92.6-97.4). In our large-scale population screening study, this method significantly improves the detection rate of AG (Group C vs. Group A: OR = 1.956; 95%CI, 1.174-3.260) and IM (Group C vs. Group A: OR = 1.397; 95%CI, 0.731-2.671). Notably, we are the first to report the detection rates of this method in the gastric antrum and corpus. According to our results, ABC method could detect the AG (Group C vs. Group A: OR = 4.990; 95%CI, 2.291-10.869) and IM (Group C vs. Group A: OR = 6.818; 95%CI, 2.486-18.697) in gastric corpus efficiently. A three-phase, multicentre study involving 5,248 subjects from Singapore and Korea suggested that the area under the curve (AUC) for H. pylori serology, PG I/II, and the ABC method in diagnosing GC were 0.635, 0.641, and 0.647, respectively [22]. Furthermore, Watabe et al. [23] reported that the annual incidence of GC was 0.04%, 0.06%, 0.35%, and 0.60% in groups A, B, C, and D, respectively. Our study found that the GC detection rates in groups B, C, and D were 2- to 3-fold higher than in group A. This underscores the efficiency of the ABC method in identifying high-risk individuals with gastric premalignant lesions and GC during large-scale population screenings [24].

Shimoyama et al. [25]reported that the applicability of the ABC method should be evaluated before use in the screening of population with a high prevalence of H. pylori infection for poor sensitivity and specificity. Therefore, concerning the recent ABC studies, advanced decision criteria should be proposed to decrease the false-negative evaluation of AG and IM and GC risk. Murphy et al. [26] used pepsinogen and H. pylori to construct a model for predicting GC incidence, achieving improved accuracy. Hirai et al. [27] pointed out that combining serological antibody tests with endoscopy can more effectively detect precancerous lesions and GC, which is similar to our study findings and the scheme of Liao et al. [28]. The original ABC classification system was established in Japanese populations using different cut-off values (PGI ≤ 70 ng/mL and PGR ≤ 3.0) and assay platforms. While our study employed a PG+ definition of PGI < 60 ng/mL and PGR < 6.0 based on the manufacturer’s instructions for the Chinese population, the overall risk stratification principle—combining PG and H. pylori serology—remains consistent. The differences in cut-off values should be considered when comparing our findings with Japanese studies, as they may influence the sensitivity and specificity for detecting precancerous lesions across populations.

In contrast to the corpus, no significant differences in the detection rates of AG or IM in the gastric antrum were observed across the four serological groups. While atrophic changes typically progress from the antrum to the corpus, the absence of significant antral differences may be explained by several factors. First, antrum-restricted atrophy is common in general populations, particularly in regions with high H. pylori prevalence, which may attenuate group-specific differences. Second, the ABC classification system is primarily designed to identify corpus-predominant atrophy, as corpus atrophy correlates more strongly with serological markers. Third, the stratified sampling design—with selective inclusion of Groups A and B—may have influenced the distribution of antral lesions across groups. Collectively, these factors may account for the observed discordance between antral and corpus findings.

In the Xinwu District, our team has been running community-based GC prevention programs for several years, engaging over ten thousand participants. Patients with severe gastric atrophy or high-risk metaplasia were advised to undergo endoscopic surveillance. Consequently, the detection rates of precancerous lesions and GC in this study may have been influenced by prior participant awareness and targeted surveillance recommendations, which should be considered when interpreting the generalizability of our findings to populations without such screening programs. In addition, in this general population-based screening cohort, the overall GC detection rate was low, limiting statistical power to detect differential cancer risk across serological groups. Consequently, although Group C is theoretically expected to have the highest risk, the small absolute number of cancer cases may have introduced bias. Additionally, we did not collect information on prior H. pylori eradication therapy; therefore, Group D may represent a heterogeneous population comprising both individuals with naturally cleared infection due to severe atrophy and those with a history of successful eradication, which could influence the observed risk associations. Furthermore, the diagnostic performance of the ABC method for IM was limited in our study. Although Group C (PG+/H. pylori +) showed a higher odds of IM compared to Group A, the difference did not reach statistical significance (OR = 1.397; 95% CI: 0.731–2.671). This suggests that the ABC method has inherent limitations in identifying IM.

In conclusion, combination of serum PG concentrations and serum anti-H. pylori IgG antibodies significantly enhances the detection rates of gastric premalignant lesions and GC, particularly in the gastric corpus.
