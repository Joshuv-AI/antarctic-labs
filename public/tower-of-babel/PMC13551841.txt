How does Chinese culture influence nursing students’ perceptions of schizophrenia? an exploratory qualitative study
Authors: Xi Chen, Jiayin Ruan, Yan Li, Sau Fong Leung, Daniel Thomas Bressington
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStigmatizing attitudes toward mental illness are common among nursing students, which can create barriers to treatment and recovery for people with schizophrenia. Chinese culture, which emphasizes face-saving and Confucianism, differs from Western culture in its understanding of stigma. This results in different approaches to dealing with stigma, leading to diverse outcomes. This study aims to explore how Chinese culture influences nursing students’ perceptions of schizophrenia. A qualitative descriptive approach was adopted for this study. Purposive and snowball sampling were used to recruit nursing students between July 25, 2022, and September 30, 2022 from a hospital in mainland China. Online focus group interviews were conducted with nursing students. Data were analyzed using conventional content analysis with NVivo 12. Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently. Thirty nursing students participated in the focus group interviews, which ranged from 74 to 102 min in duration. The participants were aged 19 to 22 years, and included 25 females and five males. Five themes were derived from the interviews: knowledge gaps regarding schizophrenia; misconceptions about schizophrenia, impact of cultural perceptions on attitudes towards schizophrenia, nursing students’ intentional behavior toward schizophrenia, and pity and awareness of injustice towards people with schizophrenia. Cultural factors influencing nursing students’ perspectives on schizophrenia include beliefs and stigma surrounding mental illness, family and community attitudes, religious interpretations, and exposure to or engagement with mental health services. Introduction

Schizophrenia is considered one of the most severe and stigmatized mental disorders [1]. Between 1990 and 2019, the global burden of schizophrenia saw notable increases in raw numbers: prevalence rose from 14.2 million to 23.6 million, incidence increased from 941,000 to 1.3 million, and Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) grew from 9.1 million to 15.1 million, marking rises of over 65%, 37%, and 65%, respectively [2]. Stigma contributes to these impairments because it poses a great barrier for people with schizophrenia to receive treatment [3] and recovery [4].

Background

Qualitative methods are well-placed to understand the lived experiences and views of people regarding mental illness [5].

The qualitative study collects data from a subjective perspective of an event or experience, generating data from three dimensions: who, what, and where [6]. A qualitative study can explore the subjective nature of questions and the diverse experiences of participants. This exploration can provide an in-depth understanding of nursing students’ perspectives toward schizophrenia [7]. Some qualitative studies have explored the perspectives of health professional students toward mental illness, but cultural factors have largely been ignored [8–10]. Schizophrenia, being the most severe mental illness, needs to be explored specifically.

Nurses, as the largest occupational group in the health professional system, play an essential role in caring for and managing people with schizophrenia [11]. Nursing students, who are future nurses, should be prepared to play important roles in caring for individuals with schizophrenia. A qualitative study conducted in the USA with 19 nursing students suggests that nursing students lack mental health knowledge [8], a finding that contrasts with another qualitative study conducted in Canada with 18 health professional students [8]. Nursing students often hold negative attitudes and exhibit distancing behavior toward people with mental illness [8–10]. However, these studies also report that participants express some positive attitudes toward people with mental illness [8–10]. While the importance of acknowledging cultural uniqueness in decreasing mental illness stigma has been raised, further exploration is lacking [8]. These studies were conducted in the USA, Canada, and Spain, with no studies conducted in China. Studies also reported that nursing students in China hold stigmatized views towards people with mental illness [12, 13]. Therefore, it is necessary to explore nursing students’ perspectives toward people with schizophrenia in China.

Mental illness stigma is influenced by multiple factors [14], with cultural ones [15] playing a significant role. This influence manifests in perceptions. For example, in Asian societies, mental illness is often viewed as personal weakness or self-control failure, influenced by the concept of “face,” and seen as bringing shame to the family [16]. In some African cultures, schizophrenia may be attributed to spiritual or supernatural explanations, which may influence stigma and reluctance to seek help [17]. In Arab societies, mental illness is viewed as divine punishment, with religious beliefs worsening prejudice and delaying treatment [18]. In Western societies, stigma stems from misconceptions that those with mental health conditions are dangerous or unpredictable, resulting in social exclusion and discrimination despite growing recognition of mental illness as a health issue [19].

The interpretation, practices, and impacts of stigma can vary across cultures [20]. People from different cultures have varying understandings of stigma and treat mental illness differently, leading to different outcomes for individuals with mental illness [20]. Chinese culture is deeply influenced by Confucianism, Taoism, and Buddhism, which may cause Chinese people to respond differently to schizophrenia [21]. For example, a study that explored the application of mental illness stigma theory to Chinese societies reported that people with schizophrenia were deemed morally bankrupt, and both these people and their families were morally wrong [20]. Another study explored the concept of ‘Face’ and the embodiment of stigma in China, identifying the belief that schizophrenia resulted from ancestors’ faults or immoral behavior, and that individuals with schizophrenia were thought to have been immoral in their previous lives [22]. Due to the ‘face-saving’ culture, people in China may be hesitant to disclose their own or their family members’ mental illness [23]. Chinese people’s perspective on mental illness extends beyond viewing it as just an illness. A book introducing Chinese culture discusses this perspective, suggesting that Chinese people believe mental illness is caused by black magic or by being possessed or disturbed by spirits, whether gods or devils [24].

Cultural factors should not be overlooked in this exploration process. However, very few researchers have acknowledged the potential role of cultural factors in the formation and development of schizophrenia stigma. Moreover, mental illness stigma, specifically schizophrenia stigma, may present differently in various countries due to cultural differences. To our knowledge, few studies have focused on exploring nursing students’ perspectives toward people with schizophrenia under the influence of Chinese culture. The perspectives of nursing students toward people with schizophrenia may significantly influence their choice of nursing department and affect their effectiveness in caring for individuals with schizophrenia, ultimately impacting the recovery of those with the condition. This study aims to explore how Chinese culture influences nursing students’ perceptions of schizophrenia.

Methods

This study report adheres to the reporting guidelines of the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative studies (COREQ): the 32-item checklist [25].

Ethical issues and data safety

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of The Hong Kong Polytechnic University (HSEARS20220127002 on 22 February 2022) and the Research Ethics Committee of the Xiangya Hospital (KE202203129 on 18 March 2022). Informed consent was obtained from every participant in this study. This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The participants were furnished with an information sheet and reminded of the voluntary nature of the research. Each participant was offered 50 Chinese yuan to compensate for their time participating in the focus group interviews.

Theoretical framework underpinning of this study

In this study, the Socio-Ecological Model was used to guide the interpretation of findings [26]. The model posits that the individual behavior both influences and is influenced by the surrounding environment. The model typically includes the interplay of four levels: individual, relationship, community and societal. Nursing students’ perceptions of schizophrenia are shaped by multi-level influences, including cultural norms, family expectations, educational exposure, and social stigma. Consistent with the interpretivist approach, the axiological stance of this study recognizes that values—particularly cultural beliefs about schizophrenia—play a central role in shaping participants’ perceptions.

Ontology, epistemology, axiology, and methodology of the study

Ontology

This study adopted a relativist ontology, assuming that multiple realities existed and were shaped by sociocultural contexts.

Epistemology

An interpretivist epistemology was employed, whereby knowledge was co-constructed through interactions between researchers and participants.

Axiology

The study acknowledged that researchers’ values and cultural perspectives might influence data interpretation; therefore, reflexivity was maintained throughout the research process.

Methodology

Grounded in an interpretivist paradigm, the study employed a qualitative descriptive design to explore culturally embedded perceptions of schizophrenia.

Study design and setting

A qualitative descriptive design was deemed a suitable approach for this study. The qualitative descriptive approach acknowledges the subjective nature of the problem and the participants’ diverse experiences, presenting the findings of the initial research question through direct reflection or the use of similar terminology [27]. The study took place at a hospital in mainland China that provides clinical training to more than 350 fourth-year nursing students annually from various parts of the country. Data collection occurred through focus group interviews conducted on an online interview platform (Tencent video conference platform).

Focus groups, which involve conducting in-depth interviews within a specific population, have been utilised to create or adapt pertinent intervention strategies [28]. Focus group interviews were conducted for data collection from July 25, 2022, to September 30, 2022. Before officially starting the interviews, we invited some fourth-year nursing students to provide suggestions to improve the study design and implementation.

Sample size

Participants were split into separate focus groups, each consisting of six participants [29]. Data collection needed to be efficiently managed to ensure that data saturation determined the sample size [30]. The exact target sample size prior to the focus group interviews could not be ascertained due to the reliance on data saturation as a determinant [31]. Concurrent data analysis was implemented during the interview period.

Data collection

A discussion guide for focus group interviews was prepared based on a literature review and group deliberations. Preliminary interviews were conducted to refine the questions for nursing students. The interview guide was adjusted, and specific questions are listed in Appendix 1. The lead researcher introduced the research topic before each interview. A sociodemographic questionnaire was used to capture participant details (Table 1). The efficacy of focus group interviews in generating comprehensive data is well-acknowledged [32]. The research team gathered insights through interactive exchanges, exploring cultural impacts [28]. A male second-year PhD candidate led focus group interviews, also the lead researcher, who had undergone postgraduate research training. Simultaneously, a research assistant documented non-verbal cues and emotional states of all participants through field notes. Participants had flexibility in choosing a comfortable setting. The lead researcher introduced himself and explained the reasons and interests of this research topic before each interview. Introductory questions were used to establish rapport relationship and each interviews lasted 1 to 1.5 h, with recordings and transcriptions completed. Transcripts were cross-referenced with videos to ensure accuracy. Final transcripts were shared with participants for feedback and clarification, including documenting non-verbal responses [32].

Data analysis

Qualitative content analysis is a widely used method in qualitative research to interpret meaning from textual data [33]. It falls within the naturalistic paradigm, emphasizing the interpretation of text data [34]. There are three types of qualitative content analysis: conventional, directed, and summative [34]. In this study, conventional content analysis was chosen due to its suitability for exploring the research question and the researchers’ prior experience with this method. NVivo 12 software (Lumivero, Denver, CO, USA) was used for data management, following the five stages of qualitative content analysis: data preparation, reading and memoing, data coding, category generation, and presentation of descriptions and themes. First, the two coders (XC and JR) read and re-read each participant’s interview transcript several times to immerse themselves in the data and develop a comprehensive understanding of nursing students’ perceptions of schizophrenia within the context of Chinese cultural influence. Subsequently, the coders conducted line-by-line coding in NVivo 12 independently to identify potential coding units related to these perceptions, based on actual words or phrases used by the participants. Then, the coders compared and discussed their generated coding units for the individual transcripts until they reached a consensus. Following this, the coders condensed the coding units based on shared characteristics. Additional coding units of a higher abstract level were extracted and clustered into subthemes and themes through an iterative and inductive process.

An experienced qualitative researcher independently coded the transcripts, and the results were compared for consistency. Research findings were shared with participants for feedback through member checking, enhancing the trustworthiness of the results. No disagreeing comments were received. The study findings were translated into English to ensure accuracy, with discussions held in English and assistance from an English teacher for translation verification. Data analysis performed after each focus group interview, with transcripts and coding completed before the next interview. Descriptive statistics were used to describe participant demographics, including means, standard deviations, and percentages where applicable.

Issues of data trustworthiness

Credibility, dependability, transferability, and confirmability constitute the four criteria that establish the trustworthiness of a robust qualitative study [35]. Peer-debriefing, member-checking, and transcription of all interview data into Chinese were carried out by an independent research assistant. Following data analysis, different categories and subcategories were developed, and the research findings were translated into English. This approach aimed to reduce the loss of meaning during translation, thereby bolstering the credibility of the findings [36]. To ensure dependability, a comprehensive audit trail and analytical memos were maintained, documenting each step of the study. Detailed data regarding demographic and clinical attributes, as well as the study context, were provided to enhance the transferability of the findings [37]. The researcher engaged in self-reflection to determine whether any unintentional cues were given to participants that could have been misleading. Simultaneously, a research assistant scrutinized video recordings of the interviews to identify potential instances where the researcher may have influenced the participants’ answers. Peer-debriefing was utilised to evaluate the data analysis process. Two proficient research assistants reviewed the discussions and analysis methods, enhancing the data’s confirmability [38].

Reflexivity to address researcher bias

To address potential researchers’ bias, reflexivity was actively maintained throughout the research process. Guided by Mezirow’s theory of reflectivity [39], critical reflection was incorporated at different stages of the study, including data collection, coding, and theme development. Strategies such as use of reflexive journals, conducting regular team discussions, and engaging in iterative comparison of interpretations were employed to minimize subjective bias and enhance the credibility of the findings.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants

The length of the focus group interviews varied from 74 min to 102 min, with a median duration of 83 min. The participants’ ages ranged from 19 to 22 years, 25 participants were female and five were male. The participants represented six universities or colleges, and only two participants belonged to ethnic minorities (Tujia and Dong nationality). Further details of the participants’ demographic characteristics are displayed in Table 1.

As the nursing students were undergoing clinical placements in hospitals, they were supervised by nurses with varying work schedules, making it difficult to fix the number of participants for simultaneous participation in the focus group interviews. Consequently, these focus group interviews consisted of two groups with five participants, one group with six participants, and two groups with seven participants. No new information, codes, or themes emerged during the fifth focus group interview, indicating that data saturation had been reached [40]. Specifically, saturation was assessed through: repetition of codes across focus groups; stability of the thematic framework; no emergence of new conceptual categories; sufficient depth and variation in existing themes.

Additionally, no participants withdrew from this study, suggesting their interest in the study and belief in its significance.

Five themes and six sub-themes were derived from the focus group interviews. Since each focus group interview provided sufficient information about the research topic, no repeated interviews were conducted. All participants were provided with the study findings after the data analysis was completed and the coding tree was constructed, and no disagreements were indicated in the feedback received. Theme 1. Knowledge gaps regarding schizophrenia

The level of knowledge regarding schizophrenia among participants in this study was limited. Responses from the interviews suggested that participants lacked adequate understanding of schizophrenia.

The nursing students expressed knowledge deficit about schizophrenia. For example, they mentioned:

Even as an intern nursing student, I didn’t know that schizophrenia can be cured. (Participant 7).

I read the textbook and memorised this knowledge before the exam. But after the exam, I forget it completely. Now I don’t know what medicine people with schizophrenia should take. (Participant 1)

All of the above information indicates that the nursing students had knowledge gaps regarding schizophrenia.

Theme 2. Misconceptions about schizophrenia

The recognition of schizophrenia among participants in this study was poor. Participants held misconceptions about schizophrenia, for example:

From what I read in literary and artistic novels in high school, I know that people with schizophrenia have 22 personalities. (Participant 11)

The symptoms of schizophrenia are temporary, and patients can be cured by themselves even without medical treatment. (Participant 9)

From these findings, it is evident that participants in this study held misconceptions about schizophrenia, which may contribute to the stigma towards individuals with this condition.

Theme 3. Impact of cultural perceptions on attitudes towards schizophrenia

Sub-theme 1. Multiple attitudes: fear, curious, sympathy

The nursing students expressed various attitudes towards schizophrenia, for example:

I feel fear towards individuals with mental illnesses because I feel people with schizophrenia may appear different from other normal people. (Participant 5)

Without prior understanding of caring for individuals with schizophrenia, I may be curious to engage and interact with them in a caregiving role. (Participant 27)

I can sympathise with people with schizophrenia, as there is little interaction with them. I can only offer some encouraging words to them online. When I encounter them in the real world, I will not treat them as a special group but rather as ordinary individuals. I will not view them as freaks. (Participant 8)

This information indicates that participants hold diverse attitudes towards people with schizophrenia.

Sub-theme 2. Influence of culture on attitude formation

The nursing students stated that their attitudes towards schizophrenia were culturally influenced, manifesting as specific perceptions. For example:

The attitude towards people with schizophrenia to some extent is influenced by our culture. Looking back 20–30 years, in rural areas, people despised individuals with mental illnesses because they didn’t consider it an illness. In the feudal context of rural areas, it was believed to be possession by ghosts. Mental illnesses are intertwined with China’s ghost and deity culture. (Participant 1)

Monsters and demons are related to Taoism. The ancients emphasised the concept of “three souls and seven spirits”, which also stems from Taoist ideology. The way the ancients perceived individuals with schizophrenia could be described as losing their souls and spirits. In a normal person, the harmony and unity of the three souls and seven spirits exist within us. When the three souls and seven spirits are in a state of disorder, it can manifest as that kind of mental abnormality, even what we call schizophrenia. (Participant 10)

The above information shows that participants acknowledged the influence of culture on the formation of their attitudes towards people with schizophrenia.

Theme 4. Nursing students’ intentional behavior toward schizophrenia Sub-theme 1. Contributing towards public stigma towards schizophrenia

The nursing students mentioned that stigmatizing behavior exists towards people with schizophrenia. For instance:

For example, when we are out on the street and encounter someone who appears to have mental health issues, we tend to say, “Keep some distance”. (Participant 22)

It’s also out of self-protection, uh, that kind of mentality, because they will feel that when they encounter people with mental illness problems, they have to stay away, and I want to stay away from people with mental illness. It is also a self-protection mentality. (Participant 8)

The participants, who were nursing students likely to provide services to people with schizophrenia after graduation, also demonstrated coping strategies for encountering individuals with the condition. However, these strategies could also be interpreted as another form of stigmatization.

Sub-theme 2. Coping with schizophrenia within the context of traditional culture

The nursing students mentioned that their coping strategies towards schizophrenia were influenced by traditional Chinese culture. This influence manifests particularly when the affected individuals are their parents. Despite harboring stigma towards those with schizophrenia, the strong influence of filial piety often leads participants to prioritize the care of their family members over stigmatization. For example: ‘Traditional culture can influence our perception of schizophrenia. Filial piety is highly valued, and if my own parents were diagnosed with schizophrenia, regardless of their condition, I would take care of them.’ (Participant 19).

Personally, I would definitely not have any discriminatory mindset towards individuals with schizophrenia, especially if they were my closest loved ones. (Participant 1)

For the sake of clan honour, family members with schizophrenia will not be allowed to contact outsiders and will be hidden for fear of losing face. (Participant 7)

The above information demonstrates how nursing students’ intentional behavior towards people with schizophrenia may be influenced by traditional Chinese culture, particularly in relation to coping if a family member was diagnosed.

Theme 5. Pity and awareness of injustice towards people with schizophrenia

Sub-theme 1. Understanding the suffering

The nursing students mentioned that they empathized with the pain experienced by people with schizophrenia. For example:

Because I am certain that regardless of the illness, anyone who is diagnosed with a condition will undoubtedly experience hardship. (Participant 6)

I feel like he must be in a lot of excruciation. It’s like his expression shows that he is aware that his previous behaviour was wrong, but the damage has already been done. (Participant 18)

The above information demonstrates that nursing students empathize with and understand the pain experienced by people with schizophrenia. This high level of pity is expressed despite these views being predicated upon stigmatized views of schizophrenia, such as that people with schizophrenia have done something wrong, or that they cannot function in society.

Sub-theme 2. Indications of unfair treatment

The nursing students mentioned that they were aware of and empathized with the unfair treatment faced by people with schizophrenia. For example:

After schizophrenia has been treated, I think they can work normally. Otherwise, it is impossible, just because he has this kind of mental illness, society abandons him! (Participant 9)

Many people with schizophrenia lived in the psychiatry department for more than 10 years, 20 years, or even 40 years, and the longest was apparently 42 years. Is there not a little bit of a chance for their reintegration into society? Actually, I think it’s because of our stereotypes towards them that we treat them differently. (Participant 7)

From the perspective of the participants in this study, it appears that they empathize with people with schizophrenia. They comprehend the suffering of those with schizophrenia, while also expressing animosity towards individuals who bully them. This empathy stems from their ability to envision themselves in a similar situation and consider the potential of experiencing such bullying themselves.

Discussion

The study aims to explore how Chinese culture influences nursing students’ perceptions of schizophrenia. Eleven participants (36.7%) had friends or family members with mental illness, and 12 participants (40%) had interacted with people with schizophrenia.

A previous study reported that individuals with personal or close contacts’ experience of mental illness demonstrated more respectful and accepting attitudes toward people with mental illness, characterized by reduced blame, anger, and social distance [41]. This may have influenced participants’ perceptions in the present study and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Theme one revealed that nursing students lacked sufficient knowledge about schizophrenia, consistent with previous research conducted in Burkina Faso, Africa, where 36% (45/125) of nursing students exhibited a general deficiency in mental health knowledge [42]. Similarly, a study conducted in Australia with 114 nursing students reported that nearly 40% of the students felt they lacked sufficient understanding of mental health [43]. Theme two indicated that nursing students held misconceptions about schizophrenia. Misconceptions regarding schizophrenia are not uncommon, as similar findings have been reported in a prior study [44]. The main finding of our study is that misconceptions about schizophrenia were influenced by traditional Chinese culture, manifesting as specific perceptions. According to a study conducted in Lebanon involving 2,289 participants, there is a positive association between attitudes towards mental disorders and mental health knowledge [45]. Furthermore, supportive and open-minded behavior is likely to follow a positive attitude [45]. Our study, along with previous research, highlights the importance of emphasizing mental health education. Improving nursing students’ knowledge of mental health may foster positive attitudes and behaviors towards individuals with mental illness [46].

Theme three of this study revealed that nursing students held diverse attitudes towards schizophrenia, such as curiosity and sympathy, which have been reported in previous studies [47–49]. These findings indicate that nursing students’ attitudes towards schizophrenia are not strictly negative or positive. Instead, their attitudes are multifaceted. A negative attitude among nursing students towards individuals with schizophrenia may impact the quality of care for this population in the future [49]. It is crucial to address how to transform negative attitudes into positive ones and also essential that the students are made aware of these biases and their potential impacts. Curiosity could be leveraged to motivate nursing students to acquire a more in-depth understanding of schizophrenia, and heightened awareness of mental health could aid in mitigating negative attitudes and stigma towards individuals with mental illness [50]. Considering that nurses themselves are among the groups facing mental health challenges from caring for people with mental illness with little optimism, fostering a positive attitude towards mental illness among nursing students could benefit their future nursing careers. It could make them more willing to seek help when they encounter mental health issues [51]. Meanwhile, our study found that nursing students’ attitudes towards schizophrenia were influenced by culture, and this influence manifests as specific perceptions. These cultural beliefs may influence nursing students’ attitudes toward individuals with schizophrenia. For example, if nursing students believe that people with schizophrenia are possessed by ghosts, they may keep their distance, and this negative attitude could be difficult to change. On the other hand, if nursing students adhere to Taoist beliefs, they might think that schizophrenia is related to a disorder of the ‘three souls and seven spirits’ and that the condition will disappear if these spirits are restored to their normal order. This belief may lead to a more positive attitude, as they would see schizophrenia as treatable and unrelated to the individual’s morality or behavior. Following the findings of Theme three, it is essential to consider the culture-specific perceptions that shape nursing students’ attitudes.

Theme four uncovered that nursing students’ intentional behavior toward schizophrenia. Their coping strategy for schizophrenia is creating distance towards people with schizophrenia. The finding aligns with a previous study that reported noticeable social distance among nursing students towards people with schizophrenia [46]. Similar reluctance to work in psychiatric settings has been observed in previous study [52], indicating that this issue is prevalent in mainland China and deserves attention. Additionally, our study revealed that nursing students exhibit diverse intentional behaviors towards people with schizophrenia, particularly when the condition affects their own families. Nursing students may also harbor significant stigma towards their family members with schizophrenia. However, when nursing students are confronted with mental illness within their own families, their sense of duty to support their family members, stemming from Chinese tradition, outweighs the impact of public stigma. Influenced by traditional Chinese culture, this influence manifests as a perception; nursing students tend to transcend the stigma they harbor and become more inclined to care for their family members, aligning with ‘Filial piety,’ a prevailing social value in Chinese society [53]. This phenomenon is closely related to the concept of filial piety, a core principle in Chinese Confucianism [54]. Future interventions to reduce mental illness stigma could employ metaphors that prompt participants to imagine individuals with schizophrenia as their own family members, potentially lessening the stigma associated with mental illness.

Despite nursing students’ willingness to care for family members with schizophrenia, they may be more inclined to conceal them to avoid ‘losing face’ when others become aware of the condition. This behavior serves as a coping mechanism within the traditional culture of Chinese nursing students. When nursing students have real contact with people recovering from schizophrenia and nurses working in psychiatry departments, their stereotyping or stigmatization of people with schizophrenia, as well as their willingness to work in psychiatry departments, could be changed [55]. Providing nursing students with metaphors that help them consider the possibility that individuals with schizophrenia could be anyone, including family members, may be a way to decrease the stigma nursing students hold towards individuals with schizophrenia.

Theme five emphasized that nursing students possess pity and awareness of injustice towards people with schizophrenia. Notably, the nursing students demonstrated an understanding of the suffering experienced by people with schizophrenia, indicating their pity towards them. Additionally, the nursing students acknowledged the unfair treatment or stigma faced by individuals with schizophrenia, further highlighting their pity. They emphasized that people with schizophrenia receive unfair treatment and annoyance to those who implement these unfair treatments to people with schizophrenia.

These findings are consistent with earlier studies that have reported nursing students’ empathy towards individuals with schizophrenia [56, 57]. A study conducted in Germany also found that increased levels of empathetic emotions resulted in decreased stigma surrounding mental health, fostering more positive attitudes towards individuals with mental illness [58]. This may suggest that empathetic emotions can stimulate reflective cognitive processes, and the ability to empathize reduces stigma towards individuals with mental illness.

The above themes also indicated important factors neglected by previous studies that culture plays an essential role in stigma in stigmatizing attitudes and experiences. Stigma was viewed as a universal phenomenon across different cultures and societies by Link [59]. Thus, culture is an important factor deserving systematic study because of its interplay with the mechanisms of stigma. Therefore, future studies should take cultural considerations into account when designing interventions aimed at reducing mental illness stigma among nursing students.

Potential interventions based on participant feedback

The outcomes of this study suggest potential intervention directions for future research. This study highlights the significant role of cultural factors in shaping nursing students’ knowledge, attitudes, and intentional behaviours toward schizophrenia. Based on the findings, several interventions may be considered to reduce schizophrenia-related stigma among nursing students. First, mental health education should be enhanced to address knowledge gaps and misconceptions. Second, contact-based interventions may help reduce stereotyping and social distance. Third, culturally tailored strategies should be incorporated, including the use of culturally relevant metaphors (e.g., framing individuals with schizophrenia as one’s own family members) to leverage values such as filial piety and promote empathy. Fourth, interventions should explicitly address culturally rooted beliefs (e.g., supernatural or moral attributions) to facilitate a more accurate biomedical understanding of schizophrenia. Additionally, interventions should aim to raise awareness of unconscious, culturally rooted biases and their influence on caring behaviours, stigma, and recovery outcomes in people with schizophrenia. Finally, these interventions may inform the development of longitudinal and comparative studies, as well as the design and implementation of culture-specific interventions to reduce schizophrenia-related stigma among nursing students and healthcare professionals.

Limitations

There were some limitations in this study. First, the low representation of ethnic minorities (only two participants) and individuals with religious beliefs (only one participant) in the study sample limits the ability to fully understand the influence of nationality and religious beliefs on nursing students’ stigma towards people with schizophrenia, limiting the transferability to other Chinese settings. The lack of religious diversity may limit the exploration of how spiritual or religious beliefs influence perceptions of schizophrenia. In contexts where religion plays a stronger role, interpretations of mental illness may differ (e.g., spiritual attribution versus biomedical understanding); and this should be explored in future research. The use of only one group of Chinese students residing in China may also result in a lack of transferability to settings outside China. A larger and more diverse sample would provide a more comprehensive understanding of these factors and improve the transferability of findings. Second, conducting interviews online introduced technical issues and disruptions due to unstable internet connections, resulting in interruptions and potentially affecting the quality of audio recordings. Future research could aim to mitigate technical issues associated with online interviews to improve the quality of data collection. Online interviews may have affected participant engagement and the analysis of non-verbal communication. To address this, we used field notes to capture tone and contextual cues, encouraged participants to elaborate on their responses, and employed probing questions to enhance depth. Third, there exists potential for social desirability bias, which may have curtailed students’ sharing of potentially contentious ideas. Fourth, exploring the views of fourth-year nursing students on schizophrenia might yield different insights compared to their earlier years of training. Understanding these issues earlier in their education could be more beneficial than waiting until they are in clinical placements. Fifth, while gender diversity was considered during recruitment, the final sample was predominantly female due to the gender distribution within the nursing student population and recruitment difficulty. Sixth, the interview guide did not explicitly explore culturally appropriate intervention strategies (e.g., what specific cultural elements should be addressed in an intervention). Future studies should include targeted questions to examine this issue in greater depth. Seventh, question 11 in the interview guide may be subject to social desirability bias, potentially yielding socially acceptable rather than candid responses; future studies should consider reframing it (e.g., “What concerns or hesitations do you have about working in psychiatric nursing?”) to elicit more authentic perspectives.

Conclusions

This study aims to explore how Chinese culture influences nursing students’ perceptions of schizophrenia. The interviews revealed that Chinese nursing students sometimes hold unconscious biases toward schizophrenia, which may be influenced by traditional cultural beliefs. It is important that these students are made aware of these beliefs because they will influence their caring behaviors and may further promote public stigma towards people with schizophrenia. Therefore, culturally specific interventions should be justified for further research to decrease nursing students’ stigma towards schizophrenia.

Relevance for clinical practice

This study finds the critical role of culture in shaping nursing students’ perceptions and behaviors toward individuals with schizophrenia. By identifying cultural misconceptions and knowledge gaps, the research underscores the necessity for culturally tailored interventions in mental health education. Addressing these cultural factors in the interventions can help reduce schizophrenia stigma, improve empathy, and enhance care quality for people with schizophrenia. Clinicians and educators can leverage these insights to develop targeted interventions that transform negative attitudes and promote more compassionate, stigma-free mental health care.
