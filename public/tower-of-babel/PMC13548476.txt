Exploring heterogeneity in ICU nurses’ perceived intrahospital transport safety: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Wen Zhao, Ao Li, Li Tang, Zhengrong Cai, Shuaishuai Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntrahospital transport (IHT) of critically ill patients is a high-risk process in intensive care units (ICUs), with nurses playing a pivotal role in ensuring patient safety throughout transport. Despite the critical importance of nurses’ contributions to safe IHT, little is known about the heterogeneity of ICU nurses’ perceptions of IHT safety. Therefore, this study aimed to identify latent profiles of perceived IHT safety among ICU nurses and to examine the sociodemographic, professional, and IHT-related factors associated with profile membership.DesignA cross-sectional, exploratory study.MethodsA total of 653 ICU nurses from 10 tertiary hospitals in Hunan Province, China, completed the Chinese version of the Intrahospital Transport Safety Scale (IHTSS) and a structured questionnaire. Latent profile analysis (LPA) was performed to identify latent profiles of perceived IHT safety. Multinomial logistic regression was subsequently used to examine sociodemographic, professional, and IHT-related factors associated with profile membership.ResultsThree latent profiles of perceived IHT safety were identified: low (19.4%), moderate (57.3%), and high (23.3%) perceived IHT safety. The profiles differed mainly in overall levels of perceived IHT safety rather than in dimension-specific patterns. Non-use of an IHT checklist was associated with increased odds of membership in both the low-profile (OR = 4.08, 95% CI: 2.17–7.67) and moderate-profile groups (OR = 2.29, 95% CI: 1.32–3.96), compared with the high-profile group. Conversely, greater confidence in performing IHT-related tasks was associated with reduced odds of membership in the low-profile (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.49–0.70) and moderate-profile groups (OR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.61–0.84).ConclusionsICU nurses exhibited ordered heterogeneity in perceived IHT safety. Non-use of an IHT checklist and lower confidence in IHT-related tasks were associated with lower perceived safety. These findings may inform organisational assessment and targeted education, although causality cannot be established and objective transport risk was not assessed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15007-x.

Introduction

Patient safety remains a major concern in intensive care units (ICUs), where critically ill patients often present with complex clinical conditions, rapidly evolving physiological states, and the need for time-sensitive decision-making [1]. In this highly dynamic environment, safe care depends not only on the competence of individual healthcare professionals but also on effective multidisciplinary teamwork, reliable equipment, clear communication, and well-designed organisational processes. Consequently, patient safety in the ICU is shaped by both the complexity of patient care and the broader healthcare system in which that care is delivered [2].

Intrahospital transport (IHT) represents a particularly challenging clinical process in which these system-level vulnerabilities may become especially apparent. IHT of critically ill patients is a routine yet high-risk component of ICU care. During transport, patients temporarily leave the closely monitored and highly controlled ICU environment to undergo diagnostic or therapeutic procedures in locations such as radiology departments, operating rooms, or interventional suites. Safe IHT requires the coordinated functioning of multiple system components, including appropriately trained personnel, monitoring and transport equipment, transport routes, effective communication, and preparedness for emergencies [3]. Failures or disruptions in any of these elements may increase the risk of physiological deterioration, delayed recognition of clinical instability, equipment-related incidents, or communication breakdowns. As such, IHT represents a critical process in which patient vulnerability intersects with the complexity of the healthcare work system.

Importantly, perceived IHT safety should be conceptually distinguished from objectively measured IHT-related outcomes. In this study, perceived IHT safety refers to ICU nurses’ cognitive and emotional appraisal of whether transport-related work conditions are safe and adequately supported, encompassing organisational coordination, equipment readiness, environmental constraints, teamwork, communication, and individual preparedness [4, 5]. It reflects nurses’ professional judgment regarding the safety of the transport process, rather than the actual occurrence of transport-related errors, adverse events, or patient harm. While these perceptions can provide valuable insights into work-system vulnerabilities and areas needing organisational support, they should not be interpreted as direct measures of objective IHT safety.

Existing research has advanced understanding of IHT safety from multiple perspectives. Observational studies and reviews have primarily focused on the incidence, nature, and risk factors of IHT-related adverse events, demonstrating that critically ill patients may experience physiological deterioration, equipment-related issues, and communication failures during transport [6–8]. Methodological and guideline-oriented studies have further highlighted the importance of pre-transport risk assessment, staff preparation, equipment readiness, continuous monitoring, standardised communication, and checklist-based procedures [9–11]. More recent nursing research has begun to examine nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and experiences regarding IHT, indicating that transport is often perceived as stressful, workload-intensive, and dependent on adequate training and organisational support [12]. However, this body of evidence remains largely focused on adverse events, protocols, checklist implementation, and general attitudes, while less is known about perceived IHT safety as a multidimensional appraisal of the work system among ICU nurses [13]. In particular, it remains unclear whether ICU nurses form distinct subgroups with different patterns of perceived IHT safety, and whether these patterns can serve as a descriptive basis for identifying areas requiring educational or organisational support.

Given this gap, a person-centred analytical approach may provide a more nuanced understanding of ICU nurses’ perceived IHT safety. Transport-related work conditions—including training exposure, checklist integration, equipment readiness, workload, teamwork, and organisational support—are unlikely to be distributed uniformly across ICU nurses. Consequently, nurses may differ not only in their overall level of perceived IHT safety but also in how they appraise organisational arrangements, technical resources, environmental constraints, and task-related collaboration. Unlike conventional variable-centred analyses, which focus on mean levels or associations between variables, latent profile analysis (LPA) can identify subgroups of nurses exhibiting similar response patterns across multiple indicators. Applied to the Intrahospital Transport Safety Scale (IHTSS), LPA may uncover ordered or multidimensional patterns of perceived IHT safety that would otherwise be obscured in mean-level analyses. Identifying such latent profiles may offer a more refined basis for organisational needs assessment, targeted educational planning, and further evaluation of transport-related work systems. Accordingly, this study aimed to identify latent profiles of ICU nurses’ perceived IHT safety and to examine sociodemographic, professional, and IHT-related characteristics associated with profile membership.

Methods

Aim

This study aimed to investigate heterogeneity in ICU nurses’ perceived IHT safety using a person-centred analytical approach. Specifically, the objectives were to: [1] identify latent profiles of perceived IHT safety among ICU nurses via latent profile analysis (LPA); and [2] examine sociodemographic, professional, and IHT-related characteristics associated with profile membership.

Design

This exploratory cross-sectional survey applied LPA to item-level scores on the IHTSS to identify subgroups of ICU nurses with similar patterns of perceived IHT safety. Sociodemographic, professional, and IHT-related characteristics associated with profile membership were subsequently examined using an exploratory, adjusted analysis.

Setting

This study was conducted in the ICUs of 10 tertiary teaching hospitals located in four cities of Hunan Province, China (Changsha, Hengyang, Xiangtan, and Shaoyang). These hospitals routinely provide advanced critical care services and regularly perform IHT of critically ill patients for diagnostic and therapeutic procedures, including computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), interventional procedures, and emergency surgery. The participating units comprised medical, surgical, emergency, and mixed general ICUs. ICU nurses were actively involved in transport-related activities, including pre-transport assessment, preparation of monitoring and life-support equipment, patient monitoring during transport, communication with receiving departments, and post-transport patient stabilisation.

Participants and sampling

Participants were ICU nurses employed at the selected tertiary hospitals. Inclusion criteria were: [1] current employment in an ICU; [2] participation in IHT of critically ill patients within the preceding 6 months. Nurses who were on leave during the survey period or lacked recent IHT experience were excluded.

A hospital-level cluster sampling strategy was employed. The sampling units were tertiary hospitals in Hunan Province that routinely performed IHT of critically ill patients. Ten hospitals were randomly selected from the eligible list using a computer-generated random number procedure. Following hospital selection, all ICU nurses meeting the eligibility criteria within medical, surgical, emergency, and mixed general ICUs were invited to participate. Randomisation was therefore conducted at the hospital-cluster level rather than at the individual nurse level.

Data were collected through an anonymous online questionnaire distributed via WeChat between October and December 2022. Participation was voluntary. A total of 773 questionnaires were returned, of which 653 passed data-quality screening and were retained for analysis.

No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. As this was an exploratory latent profile analysis, all eligible ICU nurses within the selected hospital clusters were invited to participate. The final sample size was evaluated in relation to the number of IHTSS indicators and the stability of the latent profile solution. The IHTSS included 24 items, and the final sample of 653 participants exceeded the commonly used pragmatic benchmark of 5–10 participants per indicator. Model adequacy was further assessed using convergence, replication of the best log-likelihood, profile size, entropy and average posterior classification probabilities.

Measures

Sociodemographic and professional characteristics

Sociodemographic and professional characteristics were collected using a structured questionnaire developed for this study. Variables included age, gender, educational attainment, years of ICU work experience, professional title, ICU type and specialist nurse status. IHT-related variables included previous participation in IHT-related training and use of an IHT checklist.

IHT-related fear and confidence in performing IHT-related tasks were assessed using single items rated on an 11-point numerical rating scale ranging from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating greater fear or confidence, respectively. These single-item measures were used for exploratory purposes and had not undergone formal psychometric validation.

Intrahospital Transport Safety Scale (IHTSS)

Perceived intrahospital transport safety was measured using the Chinese version of the IHTSS [4]. The Chinese version of the IHTSS has previously undergone translation, cultural adaptation, and psychometric evaluation among ICU nurses in China. The instrument consists of 24 items across four dimensions: organisation, tools and techniques, environment, and IHT-related tasks and collaboration. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Total scores ranged from 24 to 120, with higher scores indicating greater perceived IHT safety. The scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.97).

Statistical analysis

Preliminary analysis and descriptive statistics

Descriptive analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25.0. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means (standard deviations) or medians (interquartile ranges), as appropriate. Differences across LPA-derived profiles were examined using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and ANOVA or non-parametric tests for continuous variables. Effect sizes were reported as Cramer’s V and η², respectively. Two-sided p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Data-quality screening and handling of missing data

During data cleaning, returned questionnaires were screened for data-quality problems, including substantial missing responses, systematic missing-response patterns, patterned responses and logical inconsistencies. A standardised z-score based on the number of missing responses per questionnaire was used as an auxiliary screening indicator. Questionnaires with an absolute z-score ≥ 2.58 were flagged for manual review rather than excluded solely on the basis of the z-score. Questionnaires were excluded when review confirmed abnormal missing-response patterns, patterned responses or logical inconsistencies. A total of 120 questionnaires were excluded after data-quality screening because of abnormal missing-response patterns, patterned responses or logical inconsistencies.

Among the retained questionnaires, item-level missingness was below 5% for all IHTSS items and continuous IHT-related variables. Complete-case analysis was therefore applied and no imputation was performed. Because the missing-data mechanism could not be fully verified, possible bias related to complete-case analysis was considered when interpreting the findings.

Latent profile analysis

Latent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted using the 24 item-level IHTSS scores to examine heterogeneity in ICU nurses’ perceived IHT safety. Analyses were performed in Mplus version 8.3 with robust maximum likelihood estimation. For each model, 500 random sets of starting values were generated, and the 100 best solutions were retained for final-stage optimisation. Model stability was evaluated by verifying replication of the best log-likelihood and convergence without warnings.

Models with increasing numbers of profiles were compared using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC), entropy, bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), and Lo–Mendell–Rubin test (LMRT). Lower AIC, BIC, and aBIC values indicate better fit. Entropy values > 0.80 indicate adequate classification precision. Significant BLRT and LMRT results suggest that a model with k profiles fits better than one with k − 1 profiles. Profile size, model parsimony, and substantive interpretability were also considered. Profiles comprising < 5% of the sample were considered potentially unstable unless substantively meaningful.

Exploratory analysis of factors associated with profile membership

After identification of the optimal latent profile solution, multinomial logistic regression was used to explore factors associated with profile membership. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariable analyses and those considered clinically relevant were entered into the multivariable model. The high perceived IHT safety profile served as the reference category. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The most likely profile assignment derived from the LPA was used as the dependent variable. This exploratory analysis did not explicitly account for uncertainty in profile classification.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of participants

A total of 653 ICU nurses were included in the analysis. The majority were female (84.7%), held a bachelor’s degree (86.2%), had participated in IHT-related training (87.0%), and reported using an IHT checklist (73.8%). Nurses were drawn from general, surgical, medical, and emergency ICUs, with variation in age, years of ICU experience, professional title, and specialist nurse status. Latent profile analysis

LPA was conducted using the 24 item-level IHTSS scores. Models with one to five profiles were estimated and compared using model fit indices, classification quality, profile size, and substantive interpretability (Table 2). The three-profile solution was selected as the final model. Compared with the two-profile solution, it showed lower AIC, BIC, and aBIC values, high entropy (0.981), and significant LMRT (p = 0.027) and BLRT (p < 0.001) results. Although the four- and five-profile solutions yielded lower information criteria, the LMRT results were non-significant and the additional profiles offered limited interpretability. The five-profile solution also contained a very small profile (approximately 1% of the sample), raising concerns regarding stability. Overall, the three-profile solution was considered the most parsimonious and interpretable model.

The profiles primarily differed in overall levels of perceived IHT safety rather than in dimension-specific patterns. Class 1 comprised 127 nurses (19.4%) and was characterised by consistently lower item scores across all four dimensions, and was labelled the low perceived IHT safety profile. Class 2 comprised 374 nurses (57.3%) and showed intermediate scores, and was labelled the moderate perceived IHT safety profile. Class 3 comprised 152 nurses (23.3%) and was characterised by consistently higher scores, and was labelled the high perceived IHT safety profile. Item-response patterns across the three profiles are presented in Fig. 1.

Average posterior probabilities ranged from 0.985 to 0.997, indicating excellent classification quality (Table 3). Descriptive distributions of IHTSS total scores across the three profiles are provided in Supplementary Table S2. These comparisons are presented descriptively because the profiles were derived from item-level IHTSS responses.

Factors associated with perceived IHT safety profile membership

An exploratory multinomial logistic regression analysis was performed to examine factors associated with perceived IHT safety profile membership after adjustment for sociodemographic, professional, and IHT-related characteristics. The high perceived IHT safety profile served as the reference category. The overall model was statistically significant (χ² = 126.87, df = 30, p < 0.001; R² = 0.206).

Non-use of an IHT checklist was associated with increased odds of membership in both the low perceived IHT safety profile (OR = 4.08, 95% CI: 2.17–7.67, p < 0.001) and the moderate perceived IHT safety profile (OR = 2.29, 95% CI: 1.32–3.96, p = 0.003), relative to the high perceived IHT safety profile. Higher confidence in performing IHT-related tasks was associated with reduced odds of membership in the low profile (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.49–0.70, p < 0.001) and the moderate profile (OR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.61–0.84, p < 0.001).

Male nurses had higher odds of belonging to the low perceived IHT safety profile (OR = 2.60, 95% CI: 1.26–5.36, p = 0.010). Compared with a bachelor’s degree, junior college education was associated with lower odds of membership in both the low profile (OR = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.06–0.82, p = 0.025) and the moderate profile (OR = 0.22, 95% CI: 0.08–0.57, p = 0.002). These findings should be interpreted cautiously because the junior college subgroup was relatively small.

Age, years of ICU experience, ICU type, specialist nurse status, previous IHT-related training, and IHT-related fear were not independently associated with profile membership after adjustment. Detailed results are presented in Table 4.

Discussion

This study used a person-centred analytical approach to examine heterogeneity in ICU nurses’ perceived intrahospital transport (IHT) safety. Three latent profiles were identified, representing low, moderate, and high levels of perceived IHT safety. The profiles primarily differed in their overall level of perceived safety rather than in distinct dimension-specific patterns. In the exploratory adjusted analysis, non-use of an IHT checklist and lower confidence in performing IHT-related tasks were consistently associated with membership in the low and moderate perceived IHT safety profiles. These findings provide a descriptive understanding of variation in ICU nurses’ perceptions of IHT-related work systems and may help identify areas requiring further organisational assessment and educational support. Given that intrahospital transport of critically ill patients is a high-risk process associated with physiological instability, equipment-related problems, and adverse events, understanding how frontline nurses perceive transport safety may contribute to efforts to improve patient safety during transport.

Heterogeneity in perceived intrahospital transport safety among ICU nurses

The three latent profiles indicate that perceived IHT safety was heterogeneous among ICU nurses. Approximately one-fifth of participants were classified into the low perceived IHT safety profile, while more than half were classified into the moderate profile, suggesting that a substantial proportion of ICU nurses perceived room for improvement in the conditions supporting safe transport. Prior research has shown that safety perceptions often vary among healthcare professionals within the same organisation, reflecting differences in local work environments, resource availability, and safety culture [14, 15]. The observed heterogeneity likely reflects meaningful variation in how nurses experience and evaluate transport-related work systems.

Importantly, the profiles primarily reflected ordered differences in overall perceived IHT safety. Item-level trajectories were broadly parallel across the organisation, tools and techniques, environment, and IHT-related tasks and collaboration dimensions, rather than exhibiting qualitatively distinct patterns. Consequently, the profiles should not be interpreted as discrete clinical phenotypes or objective risk categories [16]. Their value lies in capturing meaningful variation in nurses’ appraisal of transport-related work systems that may be obscured when only mean scores are reported. Similar ordered latent profiles have been observed in studies of patient safety culture, burnout, and work engagement, where subgroups differ mainly in severity or intensity rather than in qualitatively distinct patterns [16, 17]. These findings suggest that safety perceptions exist along a continuum rather than as categorically distinct states.

This interpretation also highlights the contribution of the person-centred approach. Conventional variable-centred analyses describe average scores or associations across the entire sample but may overlook nurses who consistently report lower perceived safety across multiple aspects of the transport process. Identifying ordered profiles provides a more nuanced understanding of within-population heterogeneity and may help identify groups for whom further assessment of organisational support, preparation, and workflow conditions is particularly relevant [18].

A work-system interpretation of perceived IHT safety

The present findings support the view that intrahospital transport (IHT) should be understood as a complex sociotechnical process rather than a purely technical procedure. The Systems Engineering Initiative for Patient Safety (SEIPS) framework emphasises that safety-related processes emerge from interactions among people, tasks, tools and technologies, organisational conditions, and care environments [2, 19]. This framework therefore provides a useful lens through which to interpret the observed latent profiles. Previous studies of intrahospital transport have likewise highlighted that adverse events rarely result from a single technical failure; rather, they arise from interactions among staffing, communication, equipment, environmental constraints, and workflow design [8, 20].

At the person level, confidence in performing IHT-related tasks remained independently associated with profile membership after adjustment. Higher confidence was associated with lower odds of membership in both the low and moderate perceived IHT safety profiles. Confidence should not be viewed solely as an individual psychological characteristic. Rather, it may also reflect familiarity with transport procedures, previous exposure to complex transport situations, perceived role clarity, access to support, and the extent to which nurses feel prepared to respond to patient deterioration outside the ICU environment. This interpretation is consistent with Bandura’s theory of self-efficacy, which proposes that confidence in task performance is shaped by mastery experiences, social support, and environmental resources, and in turn influences how individuals perceive challenges and risks [21]. In critical care settings, higher self-efficacy has been associated with better clinical performance, greater preparedness, and more positive perceptions of patient safety.

At the level of tools and work processes, non-use of an IHT checklist was associated with higher odds of membership in the low and moderate perceived IHT safety profiles. This finding is consistent with previous research emphasising the importance of structured preparation, equipment readiness, continuous monitoring, and effective communication during the transport of critically ill patients [22, 23]. More broadly, checklist-based interventions have been shown to reduce omissions, improve team coordination, and strengthen adherence to safety-critical processes across a range of healthcare settings. A checklist may contribute to safety not merely by documenting task completion, but also by clarifying responsibilities, reducing the likelihood of omissions, and fostering a shared mental model among team members before transport. Nevertheless, the present cross-sectional findings cannot determine whether checklist use improves perceived safety or whether nurses working in better-supported environments are more likely to use checklists consistently.

Previous IHT-related training and fear differed across profiles in the unadjusted analyses but were not independently associated with profile membership after adjustment. This attenuation suggests that their apparent associations may partially overlap with checklist use, confidence, or other contextual factors. Exposure to training alone may be insufficient if it is not integrated into routine workflows, reinforced through practice, and supported by organisational structures. Evidence from implementation science suggests that educational interventions are generally more effective when combined with system-level supports, feedback mechanisms, and opportunities for experiential learning [24]. Similarly, fear may reflect a broader appraisal of uncertainty, workload, or insufficient support rather than an isolated individual response. Previous studies have shown that perceptions of risk and psychological strain among healthcare professionals are strongly influenced by organisational climate, staffing adequacy, and workload pressures [25].

Organisational and educational implications

The identified profiles may provide a useful starting point for organisational needs assessment; however, they should not be used as direct criteria for screening, performance evaluation, or intervention allocation. Nurses classified within the low perceived IHT safety profile may benefit from further assessment to determine whether their concerns are primarily related to equipment preparation, checklist integration, communication, role clarity, staffing adequacy, or environmental constraints. This recommendation is consistent with contemporary patient safety frameworks, which emphasise understanding system conditions and work-as-done rather than attributing safety concerns solely to individual performance.

The adjusted findings suggest several practical priorities. First, IHT checklists should be embedded within routine clinical workflows rather than treated merely as documentation tools. Potential implementation strategies include clear assignment of responsibilities, pre-transport briefings, bedside verification procedures, and post-transport feedback. Second, educational programmes should extend beyond knowledge transfer and incorporate simulation-based training, scenario-based rehearsal, and management of unexpected patient deterioration during transport. Simulation-based education has been shown to enhance clinical competence, teamwork, and confidence in high-risk clinical situations [26]. Third, communication between sending and receiving departments should be strengthened through standardised handover processes and clearly defined escalation pathways. Effective communication and structured handovers are widely recognised as essential components of safe transitions of care and have been associated with reductions in preventable adverse events [27].

These implications should be interpreted in light of the study design. The observed associations do not demonstrate that checklist implementation or confidence-enhancing interventions will improve objective transport safety. Future intervention studies should evaluate whether organisational and educational strategies influence both subjective outcomes, such as perceived safety and confidence, and objective outcomes, including checklist adherence, physiological instability, transport-related incidents, and patient-level adverse events. Such an approach is consistent with recommendations from patient safety researchers advocating the integration of perceptual measures and objective outcome indicators to provide a more comprehensive assessment of safety performance [28].

Alternative explanations should also be considered. Differences in perceived IHT safety may be influenced by institutional safety culture, staffing arrangements, workload, burnout, ICU case mix, and local transport protocols. Previous evidence suggests that staffing adequacy, workload, and burnout are closely associated with both safety perceptions and patient outcomes in critical care settings [29]. The present study was not designed to disentangle these contextual influences. Future research should incorporate hospital-level and unit-level factors to better understand how organisational conditions shape perceived IHT safety.

Strengths, limitations and future research directions

This study has several strengths. First, it applied a person-centred analytical approach to examine heterogeneity in ICU nurses’ perceived IHT safety, extending prior research that relied on overall scores or variable-centred comparisons. This approach allowed identification of distinct subgroups and provided a nuanced understanding of within-population variation. Second, the study included nurses from multiple tertiary hospitals and ICU types, capturing variation across regional critical care settings. Third, perceived IHT safety was measured using the Chinese version of the IHTSS, a multidimensional instrument assessing organisational, technical, environmental, and task-collaboration factors. Finally, the study distinguished perceived safety from objectively measured transport outcomes, reducing the risk of overinterpreting perception-based findings.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and observed associations between profile membership and checklist use, confidence, or other factors should be interpreted as exploratory. Data were collected via voluntary self-report at a single time point, introducing potential recall bias, social desirability bias, and common method variance. Objective indicators of transport safety—such as checklist adherence, physiological instability, incident reports, adverse events, or patient outcomes—were not included, so it is unclear whether perceived profiles correspond to actual differences in performance or patient safety. No formal a priori sample size calculation was conducted; although classification quality and model stability were acceptable, replication in independent samples is needed. The multinomial logistic regression analysis used the most likely profile assignment without accounting for classification uncertainty, and nurses were nested within hospitals without modelling hospital-level clustering, which may have conflated institutional and individual effects.

Finally, the study was conducted among ICU nurses in tertiary hospitals from a single Chinese province, which may limit generalisability. Future research should recruit more diverse samples, assess reproducibility of the identified profiles, incorporate multilevel and longitudinal designs, and explore links between perceived safety and objective IHT outcomes. Intervention studies are also needed to determine whether workflow-integrated checklists, simulation-based training, and enhanced interprofessional coordination can improve both perceived and objective dimensions of IHT safety, thereby contributing to safer intrahospital transport and other high-risk transitions in critical care.

Conclusions

This study identified three ordered profiles of ICU nurses’ perceived IHT safety, characterised by low, moderate, and high levels of perceived safety. In exploratory adjusted analyses, non-use of an IHT checklist and lower confidence in performing IHT-related tasks were associated with membership in the lower perceived safety profiles. While these findings may help inform organisational needs assessments and the development of targeted educational strategies, they should not be interpreted as evidence of causal relationships or objective transport risk. Future studies are needed to examine the reproducibility of these profiles in more diverse settings and to explore their associations with objective indicators of IHT practice and patient outcomes.
