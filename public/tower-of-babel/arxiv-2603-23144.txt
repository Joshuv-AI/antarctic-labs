The Persistent Radio Sources and Multi-wavelength Counterparts of Fast Radio Bursts in Massive Binary Systems
Z. Y. Zhao, F. Y. Wang, Z. G. Dai

Abstract
Fast radio bursts (FRBs) are millisecond-duration pulses originating from cosmological distances. Multi-wavelength counterparts associated with FRBs are important for unveiling their physical origins. Recent observations provide strong evidence that the sources of some active FRBs are residing in massive star binaries. In this letter, we study the electromagnetic counterparts of FRBs, including the persistent radio sources (PRSs) and the bow shock radiation from wind collisions for FRBs residing in magnetar - massive star binaries. We find that the PRSs with luminosity 10 38 − 10 39 10^{38}-10^{39} erg s -1 can be generated by young magnetar wind nebulae (MWN). The age of magnetars is a few decades. The observed long-term variation of flux density for PRSs can be explained by the internal magnetic field decay of magnetars. The bow shock radiation can account for the less luminous PRS of FRB 20201124A. The multi-wavelength emission arising from synchrotron radiation and inverse-Compton scattering in the bow shock can be the electromagnetic counterpart of FRBs. The emission at keV, GeV and TeV bands from the binary model can be detected at the distances of ∼ 10 − 100 \sim 10-100 Mpc, ∼ 1 − 10 \sim 1-10 Mpc and ∼ 0.1 \sim 0.1 Mpc by current instruments, respectively.

Model description

II.1
Energy Injection

In the context of MWN models, the compact PRSs are powered by the rotation or magnetic energy injection from the young magnetar. The initial rotational energy of a magnetar with initial spin period
P_{\mathrm{i}}
is

E_{\mathrm{rot}}=\frac{1}{2}I\Omega_{\mathrm{i}}^{2}\approx 2\times 10^{52}P_{\mathrm{i},-3}^{-2}\mathrm{erg},

(1)

where
I=10^{45}\mathrm{~g}\mathrm{~cm}^{2}
is the moment of inertia, and
\Omega_{\mathrm{i}}=2\pi/P_{\mathrm{i}}
is the initial spin angular frequency. The spin-down luminosity of a magnetar is

L_{\mathrm{sd}}\approx 7.2\times 10^{47}\mathrm{erg}\mathrm{s}^{-1}B_{\mathrm{d},14}^{2}P_{\mathrm{i},-3}^{-4}\left(1+\frac{t}{t_{\mathrm{sd}}}\right)^{-2},

(2)

where
B_{\mathrm{d}}
is the dipolar magnetic field and
t_{\mathrm{sd}}\approx 3\times 10^{4}\mathrm{s}~P_{\mathrm{i},-3}^{2}B_{\mathrm{d},14}^{-2}
is the spin-down timescale.

The magnetar’s internal magnetic field may also power the MWN via energetic outflows. The initial internal magnetic energy of a magnetar with an initial internal magnetic field
B_{\mathrm{int}}
is

E_{\mathrm{B,i}}=\frac{1}{6}B_{\mathrm{int}}^{2}R_{\mathrm{NS}}^{3}\approx 2\times 10^{49}B_{\mathrm{int},16}^{2}\mathrm{~erg},

(3)

where
R_{\mathrm{NS}}
is the magnetar radius. If the source is active enough, the magnetic energy release process can be regarded as continuous, which is a good approximation for a young magnetar.
The internal magnetic field decay luminosity of a magnetar is
(
27
;
8
;
62
)

L_{\mathrm{B}}(t)=L_{\mathrm{B},0}\left(1+\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{B}}t}{t_{\mathrm{d}}}\right)^{-\frac{2}{\alpha_{\mathrm{B}}}-1}.

(4)

Following
62
, the decay index
\alpha_{\mathrm{B}}=1
is taken throughout in our calculation. In this case, the magnetic field decay timescale is
t_{\mathrm{d}}\approx 10^{3}t_{0,3}B_{\mathrm{int},16}^{-\alpha_{\mathrm{B}}}~\mathrm{yr}

t_{0,3}=t_{0}/\left(10^{3}~\mathrm{yr}\right)
is the fiducial decay timescale for
B_{\mathrm{int}}=10^{16}
G. The initial magnetic field luminosity is

L_{\mathrm{B},0}=\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{B}}E_{\mathrm{B},0}}{t_{\mathrm{d}}}\approx 5.3\times 10^{38}t_{\mathrm{d},3}^{-1}B_{\mathrm{int},16}^{2+\alpha_{\mathrm{B}}}\mathrm{~erg}\mathrm{~s}^{-1}.

(5)

The timescale for the decay of the fiducial magnetic field is highly uncertain. Research focusing on anomalous X-ray pulsars (AXPs) indicates that the magnetic field undergoes substantial decay over a typical period ranging from

III.1
Dynamics and the Nebular Magnetic Field Evolution

The inner density structure of the unshocked SNe ejecta can be described as a power-law profile
(
40
)

\rho_{\mathrm{ej}}(r,t)=\frac{(3-\delta)}{4\pi}\frac{M_{\mathrm{ej}}}{R_{\mathrm{ej}}(t)^{3}}\left(\frac{r}{R_{\mathrm{ej}}(t)}\right)^{-\delta},

(15)

where
M_{\mathrm{ej}}
is the ejecta mass, and
R_{\mathrm{ej}}=v_{\mathrm{ej}}t
is the radius of the ejecta. The ejesta density profile is usually very flat (with index
\delta=0-1
) due to the mixture of material
(
23
)
, and we set
\delta=1
in this work. In the free expansion (FE) phase, the ejecta expands with a nearly constant velocity of
v_{\mathrm{ej}}=\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{SN}}/M_{\mathrm{ej}}}\simeq 10^{4}\mathrm{~km}\mathrm{~s}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{SN}}/10^{51}\mathrm{erg}\right)^{1/2}\left(M_{\mathrm{ej}}/M_{\odot}\right)^{-1/2}
, where
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{SN}}
is the supernova explosion energy. The MWN radius
R_{\mathrm{n}}
is given by
(
60
)

\frac{\mathrm{d}R_{\mathrm{n}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\sqrt{\frac{7}{6(3-\delta)}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{inj}}}{M_{\mathrm{ej}}}\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{n}}}{R_{\mathrm{ej}}}\right)^{\delta-3}}+\frac{R_{\mathrm{n}}}{t}

(16)

for
R_{\mathrm{n}}<R_{\mathrm{ej}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{inj}}=\int\dot{E}dt
is the energy injected into the nebula from the magnetar. Nearly all the rotational energy will be released within a short time
t_{\mathrm{sd}}
for a magnetar. When the rotational energy exceeds the supernova explosion energy (
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{inj}}>\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{SN}}
), the nebula will be accelerated significantly and the nebula may catch up with the expanding ejecta. In this case, the nebula and ejecta radius will evolve together

\frac{\mathrm{d}R_{\mathrm{ej}}}{\mathrm{~d}t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}R_{\mathrm{n}}}{\mathrm{~d}t}=v_{\mathrm{ej},\mathrm{f}},

(17)

where the final velocity is

\displaystyle v_{\mathrm{ej,f}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\left(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{inj}}+\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{SN}}\right)/M_{\mathrm{ej}}}

(18)

\displaystyle\approx 0.1c~\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{rot,52}}^{1/2} M_{\mathrm{ej,1M_{\odot}}}^{-1/2}.

The evolution of magnetic energy in the nebula depends on the adiabatic expansion and the energy injection from the central engine
(
53
)

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{B}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{\dot{R}_{\mathrm{n}}}{R_{\mathrm{n}}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{B}}+\frac{\sigma}{1+\sigma}\dot{E},

(19)

where
\sigma
is the magnetization parameter of the injected magnetar wind. The magnetic energy in the nebula is
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{B}}=B_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}/8\pi\cdot V_{\mathrm{n}}
, where
B_{\mathrm{n}}
and
V_{\mathrm{n}}
are the average magnetic field and the volume of nebula.

III.2
The Evolution of Particle Distribution

The emission from MWN can be obtained by solving the electron continuity equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}n_{\mathrm{e},\gamma}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}}\left(\dot{\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}}n_{\mathrm{e},\gamma}\right)=\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{e},\gamma},

(20)

where
n_{\mathrm{e},\gamma}d\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}
is the electron number density between Lorentz factors
\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}
and
\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}+d\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}
. The cooling term
\dot{\gamma}_{\mathrm{e}}
of electrons includes the radiation cooling (synchrotron radiation and IC scattering) and adiabatic expansion

\dot{\gamma}_{\mathrm{e}}(\gamma_{\mathrm{e}},t)=\dot{\gamma}_{\mathrm{e,syn}}(\gamma_{\mathrm{e}},t)+\dot{\gamma}_{\mathrm{e,ic}}(\gamma_{\mathrm{e}},t)+\dot{\gamma}_{\mathrm{e,ad}}(\gamma_{\mathrm{e}},t).

(21)

The energy loss due to synchrotron radiation is
(
79
)

\dot{\gamma}_{\mathrm{e,syn}}=-\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}B_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}^{2}}{6\pi m_{\mathrm{e}}c},

(22)

where
\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}
and
m_{\mathrm{e}}
are the Thomson cross-section and electron mass, respectively. Additionally, the electrons will also lose energy through the IC scattering of synchrotron photons or stellar photons. The detailed SSC and EIC emission process is given in Appendix
C
. From the constraints provided by VLBI observations, the scale of PRS is estimated to be approximately on the order of parsecs or sub-parsecs
(
52
;
9
)
. The EIC energy-loss can be ignored at such a distance. The energy loss due to adiabatic cooling is given by

\dot{\gamma}_{\mathrm{e},\mathrm{ad}}=-\frac{1}{3}\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}\frac{d\ln V_{\mathrm{n}}}{dt}=-\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}\frac{\dot{R}_{\mathrm{n}}}{R_{\mathrm{n}}},

(23)

The injected particles are assumed to be a broken power-law distribution
(
87
)

\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{e},\gamma}(t)=\begin{cases}Q_{0}(t)\left(\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}/\gamma_{\mathrm{b}}\right)^{-p_{1}}&\gamma_{\min}\leqslant\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}\leqslant\gamma_{\mathrm{b}}\\
Q_{0}(t)\left(\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}/\gamma_{\mathrm{b}}\right)^{-p_{2}}&\gamma_{\mathrm{b}}\leqslant\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}\leqslant\gamma_{\mathrm{max}}\end{cases},

(24)

where
\gamma_{\min},\gamma_{\mathrm{b}}
, and
\gamma_{\max}
are the minimum, break, and maximum Lorentz factors of the injected electrons. The power-law indices for low- and high-energy particles are found to be
p_{1}<2
and
p_{2}>2

(
87
)
. At any given time

The emission from the wind interactions

IV.1
Electron distribution in bow shocks

The unshocked electrons in the pulsar wind will be accelerated to ultra-relativistic speeds in the termination shock. The injected electron is usually assumed to follow a power-law distribution
\dot{Q}\left(\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}\right)\sim\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}^{-p}
for
\gamma_{\mathrm{e},\min}<\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}<\gamma_{\mathrm{e},\max}
, where
p
is the electron spectrum index. The minimum Lorentz factor of the shocked electrons is determined by
(
44
)

\gamma_{\mathrm{e},\min}=\Gamma\frac{p-2}{p-1},

(27)

where
\Gamma
is the Lorentz factor of the pre-shock pulsar/magnetar wind. The pulsar wind is primarily composed of ultra-relativistic (
\Gamma\sim 10^{5}-10^{6}
) electron-positron pairs. The giant flares of a magnetar are usually baryon-loaded. Most of the energy in the magnetar outflow is carried by the mildly relativistic ejecta, e.g.,
\Gamma m_{\mathrm{e}}c^{2}\sim 0.16-10
GeV from the radio emission of SGR 1806
-
20
(
71
;
33
)
.
The balance between the acceleration and the cooling process determines the maximum Lorentz factor

\gamma_{\mathrm{e},\max}=\sqrt{\frac{6\pi e}{\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}B_{2}(1+Y)}},

(28)

where the Compton parameter
Y
is given in Section
B
, considering both SSC and EIC processes. For small magnetized pre-shocked magnetar winds (
\sigma\ll 1
), the downstream magnetic field is given by
(
42
;
43
)

B_{2}=3(1-4\sigma)\left[\frac{\dot{E}\sigma}{r_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}c(1+\sigma)}\right]^{1/2}.

(29)

The cooling timescale of the electrons is
t_{\text{cool }}=\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}m_{\mathrm{e}}c^{2}/P_{\mathrm{rad}}
, where
P_{\mathrm{rad}}=(4/3)\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}c\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}^{2}\left(B_{2}^{2}/8\pi\right)(1+Y)
is the radiation power. The cooling is effective for the electrons with the Lorentz factor above the critical Lorentz factor
\gamma_{\mathrm{e},\mathrm{c}}
, given by equating the cooling timescale to the dynamic timescale
(
82
)

\gamma_{\mathrm{e},\mathrm{c}}=\frac{6\pi m_{\mathrm{e}}c}{\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}B_{2}^{2}\tau_{\mathrm{dyn}}(1+Y)}.

(30)

The dynamic timescale of electrons streaming with the post-shock velocity
v_{\mathrm{f}}\simeq c/3

(
42
)
is
\tau_{\mathrm{dyn}}=\xi r_{\mathrm{s}}/v_{\mathrm{f}}
with the coefficient
\xi\sim 3

(
88
)
.

The evolution of the electron distribution is determined by the continuity equation (
20
). However, the cooling timescale is usually much smaller than the orbital period of the massive binary systems. The time-dependent term in equation (
20
) can be neglected (
\partial n_{\gamma}/\partial t=0
). Under the above steady-state approximation, the electron distribution is

n_{\gamma}\propto\begin{cases}\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}^{-2},&\gamma_{\mathrm{e},\mathrm{c}}\leq\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}<\gamma_{\mathrm{e},\min}\\
\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}^{-(p+1)},&\gamma_{\mathrm{e},\min}\leq\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}\leq\gamma_{\mathrm{e},\max}\end{cases}

(31)

in the fast-cooling case and

n_{\gamma}\propto\begin{cases}\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}^{-p},&\gamma_{\mathrm{e},\min}\leq\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}\leq\gamma_{\mathrm{e},\mathrm{c}}\\
\gamma_{\mathrm{e},\mathrm{c}}\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}^{-(p+1)},&\gamma_{\mathrm{e},\mathrm{c}}<\gamma_{\mathrm{e}}\leq\gamma_{\mathrm{e},\max}\end{cases}

(32)

in the slow-cooling case. The injected electron number is

N_{\mathrm{e}}=\frac{\dot{E}\tau_{\mathrm{d}}}{\Gamma m_{\mathrm{e}}c^{2}(1-\sigma)}.

(33)

The electron number density in the bow shock is
n_{\mathrm{e}}=\int n_{\gamma}d\gamma=N_{\mathrm{e}}/V_{\mathrm{b}}
, where
V_{\mathrm{b}}=4\pi x^{2}D
the volume of the emission region. The shocked pulsar wind flow will be bent due to the Coriolis force, forming a spiral-shaped cavity. The pressure balance between the pulsar wind and stellar wind gives the radius of the cavity

\frac{\dot{E}}{4\pi cx^{2}}=P_{\perp}=\rho_{\mathrm{w}}\left(\frac{4\pi}{P_{\mathrm{orb}}}\right)^{2}x^{2},

(34)

where
\rho_{\mathrm{w}}(r)=\rho_{\mathrm{w},0}(r/R_{\star})^{-2}
. The balance location is usually much larger than the orbital distance

x=\left(\frac{\dot{E}v_{\mathrm{w}}P_{\mathrm{orb}}^{2}}{(4\pi)^{2}c\dot{M}_{\mathrm{w}}}\right)^{1/2}

(35)

VII
Conclusions

The persistent radio counterparts have been detected for some active repeating FRBs. The long-term evolution of the DM and RM of these FRBs is diverse. One-zone models encounter challenges in accounting for intricate DM and RM variations. Recently,
90
proposed a unified model, the magnetar-massive-star binary embedded in a SNR, which can explain PRS, DM and RM observations. In this work, we investigate how the observed spectra and light curves of PRSs can be explained within the framework of a binary system. In addition, we have also provided the multi-wavelength counterparts from the wind interaction between the magnetar and the massive star. Our conclusions are as follows:

•

For a young magnetar to drive FRBs and bright PRSs, the magnetar wind is usually stronger than the stellar wind. In the direction perpendicular to the orbital plane, the magnetar wind will propagate freely, leading to the formation of the MWN during the interaction between the magnetar’s outflow and the SN ejecta. In the orbital plane, the bow shock will form at the standoff distance determined by the pressure balance of the magnetar wind and the stellar wind.

•

Considering the radiation of the bow shock is governed by the IC process, the emission is mainly at high energy band. Based on the estimation of the emission efficiency, we find that the bow shock radiation can only account for some faint PRSs with luminosity
\nu L_{\nu}<10^{38}
erg s
-1
.

•

The bright PRS may originate from the synchrotron radiation from the MWN at a distance much greater than the orbit separation. The MWN may be powered by the rotational energy or the internal magnetic energy. The spindown timescale of a magnetar is very short, resulting in the faster decay of the luminosity. The rapid spin-down limits the luminosity of PRS to only reach above
10^{29}
erg/s/Hz when they are extremely young (e.g.,
t_{\mathrm{age}}<10
yr, see Figure
3
(1b)). However, the SNR is expected to be optically thick for radio signals at an age
\lesssim 10
yr due to the free-free absorption of SN ejecta
(
59
;
109
)
.

•

Compared to rotational energy, the internal magnetic energy of magnetars has a longer decay timescale, which allows the luminosity of MWNs to remain stable over a period
1-100
yr. The observed spectra of PRSs associated with FRB 20121102A, FRB 20190520B and FRB 20240114A can be explained by the synchrotron radiation from the magnetic-powered nebula with internal magnetic field
\sim 10^{16}
G. The ages of FRB 20121102A, FRB 20190520B and FRB 20240114A are a
10-20
yr, which is consistent with the study of DM and RM variations
(
90
)
.

•

The PRS luminosity is stable for FRB 20121102A during the seven-year monitoring
(
10
)
, while it is decreasing over the period of
\sim 4
yr for FRB 20190520B
(
106
;
7
)
.
For FRB 20121102A, the magnetic field decay timescale is 100 yr. When the age is much smaller than the decay timescale, the luminosity can remain stable. FRB 20190520B has a shorter decay timescale (
t_{\mathrm{d}}=50
yr), the luminosity is decreasing when
t_{\mathrm{age}}\sim t_{\mathrm{d}}
.

•

Both PWN, MWN, and binary models can explain the observed spectrum of the PRS associated with FRB 20201124A. We present predictions for the light curves, which can be tested by future observations.
In the case of rotation-powered nebula models, the flux of the PRS will decay rapidly over time. While in the case of magnetic-powered nebula models, the flux of the PRS can remain stable for a considerable period. The radiation from the bow shock will oscillate with the orbital phase.

•

Magnetars associated with active FRBs are younger and more active. Therefore, if they are in massive binary systems, the wind interaction will produce much stronger multi-wavelength radiation than that of Galactic gamma-ray binaries. The synchrotron keV radiation can be detected by Chandra and XMM-Newton at the distance of
\sim 10
Mpc. The SSC and EIC scattering radiation is mainly at the GeV band, which can be detected by Fermi-LAT at the distance of
\sim 1
Mpc. The VHE TeV
\gamma

\sim 0.1
