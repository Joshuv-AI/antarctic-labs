RibXcar with Dots Point Technique: The Science of Fracture as a Safety Principle
Authors: Raúl M. Manzaneda Cipriani, Héctor Duran, Gerardo A. Adrianzen, Emmanuel Flores, Enrique Romero Algara, Jose David Salazar, Hassan Ben Moussa, Boris Fogel, Richard Chumbiauca Munares, Gustavo Enrique Gamarra Quispe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Minimally invasive percutaneous aesthetic rib reshaping (RibXcar) aims to reduce waist circumference through rib angulation produced by controlled monocortical fractures. Achieving a precise fracture path while avoiding bicorticality is essential to minimize complications. This study compares 2 corticotomy orientations used for rib remodeling: perpendicular corticotomy (point-to-point, dots point) and parallel scraping (no dots point).Methods:Fifty porcine ribs from 24-kg Yorkshire pigs were randomly assigned according to corticotomy orientation. Rib dimensions were recorded using standardized instruments. The force and time required to generate monocortical and bicortical fractures were measured with a calibrated digital dynamometer. Data were collected in a standardized format and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 25 (IBM Corp.).Results:Rib weight and size were comparable between groups. Perpendicular corticotomy required significantly less force than parallel scraping to achieve monocortical fractures (6.67 ± 1.07 N versus 14.54 ± 1.27 N; P < 0.001) and bicortical fractures (10.0 ± 1.0 N versus 17.83 ± 0.83 N; P < 0.001). Monocortical fractures were obtained faster with the perpendicular technique (5.43 ± 0.86 s versus 6.19 ± 0.54 s), whereas bicortical fractures occurred more rapidly with the parallel scraping approach (3.63 ± 0.63 s versus 7.90 ± 0.71 s).Conclusions:In this ex vivo model, perpendicular corticotomy demonstrated biomechanical advantages by requiring less force and allowing better control to achieve safe monocortical fractures, whereas parallel scraping favored earlier bicorticality. These findings suggest that perpendicular corticotomy may provide a safer and more predictable strategy for rib remodeling.

Takeaways

Question: Does the perpendicular (dots technique) provide biomechanical and safety advantages over parallel scraping (no dots technique) for rib fracture tracing in RibXcar?

Findings: The dots technique required less force and achieved monocortical fractures faster, producing a controlled and stable fracture line. In contrast, the no dots technique required greater force and showed a higher tendency toward early bicorticality with a more irregular fracture pattern.

Meaning: The dots technique appears safer and more predictable, reducing mechanical stress and improving fracture control, whereas the no dots technique may increase the risk of inner cortex penetration and related complications.

INTRODUCTION

Minimally invasive rib reshaping for aesthetic waist reduction using ultrasound-guided percutaneous puncture (RibXcar) achieves rib angulation through a controlled monocortical fracture. Therefore, performing an adequate corticotomy is essential to ensure effective and safe angulation. This requires a thorough understanding of rib anatomy and fracture mechanics, as a precise monocortical fracture defines the angulation pathway while preventing bicortical injury or fracture displacement, which are major complications associated with pneumothorax or hemothorax.1,2

The fracture line should enable the procedure to be performed easily, allowing angulation to be achieved without intense force being applied, which could damage the pleura or cause vascular injury.1 This step is one of the most important, as it determines where the rib fracture should occur to avoid altering the rib anatomy with spiculations or irregularities. These can cause bulges or protrusions, asymmetries, or discomfort to patients.3–6

With the rise in popularity of minimally invasive coastal remodeling involving percutaneous fracture, the question arises as to how the fracture should be performed. Different approaches to this procedure have emerged in response to this question, so it is important to evaluate the characteristics, advantages, and potential complications of the two most widely described models.1,7

The objective of this study is to investigate the biomechanical differences between perpendicular corticotomy and the parallel approach as distinct monocortical fracture patterns. We evaluate and compare their biomechanical characteristics to better understand their structural behavior. This may help identify the most predictable and controlled fracture pattern for rib remodeling procedures.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was conducted by extracting 50 ribs from 2 pigs from an ex vivo animal model for human consumption one hour after death. These procedures were performed in a controlled environment, in accordance with health and biological waste regulations issued by local regulatory agencies. The characteristics of the ribs used corresponded to the joints of the true, false, and floating ribs. Rib pairs 1 and 2 were excluded because they had more pronounced angles or characteristics that differed from the usual characteristics in these procedures.

The pigs used were obtained from a local center selling meat for human consumption. The breed of these specimens was Yorkshire, and they weighed 24 kg each. Suppliers were asked to ensure that the specimens were properly eviscerated and sanitized for rib resection.

REMOVAL OF RIBS

A team of plastic surgeons disarticulated each rib from its anterior (chondrocostal) and posterior (costovertebral) joints to remove the ribs. Next, the bones were stripped of soft tissue, such as ligaments, muscles, and fatty tissue. This process was carried out for each specimen. Each rib was then labeled with a specific number, considering its weight and size characteristics, to enable adequate reference to comparisons and similarities (Fig. 1).

Nomenclature and numbering adjustments for each rib.

Weight was measured using a TRUPER Digital Scale Model BASE-5EC (year 2025), a compact scale with 5 kg capacity, 15-cm tempered glass plate, high-precision sensor, TARE function, auto-zero and auto-off features, overload indicator, measurement units of kg/g/oz/lb, and resolution of 1 g, whereas rib length was measured using an EINHELL/kwb Class II standardized steel tape measure (3 m/300 cm) to ensure accurate morphometric assessment (Figs. 2 and 3).

Weight measurements for each rib.

Length measurements for each rib.

Based on these measurements, ribs were randomized into the corresponding groups according to their weight and size. Each rib was then attached to a rigid wooden structure weighing approximately 24 kg to simulate the weight of the animal specimen, using 0.6–0.9 g zinc-galvanized steel alloy nails, with positioning structured to replicate the anatomical orientation of the specimen. Subsequently, a 1.2-mm cavity was created in the distal region of each rib, through which a 1.0-mm high-strength monofilament nylon thread was passed and secured with a knot; a second distal knot was tied to serve as the anchor point for tension measurement (Fig. 4).

Placement of nylon and knotting for measurement.

The tensile force required to produce monocortical and bicortical fractures was then evaluated using a Shahe AMF-2 digital dynamometer, capable of measuring thrust and traction forces up to 20 N and 2 kg. This device applied controlled downward tensile force to induce rib angulation and generate the corresponding fracture patterns. All procedures were performed by a single evaluator to ensure methodological consistency. All data were entered into Microsoft Excel 2019 statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), applying independent samples t test or Mann-Whitney U test according to data distribution, with normality assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

The study used nonliving porcine rib specimens obtained from a licensed distributor of animals intended for human consumption, under routine veterinary and sanitary oversight. Because no identifiable human data were collected and no live animals were used, the project did not constitute human-subjects research or live-animal experimentation. The protocol was reviewed by the Ethics Committee of the Peruvian Society of Plastic Surgery, which issued a written waiver (exemption) of ethics approval. All procedures complied with applicable national regulations governing the use of biological materials and with institutional biosafety and biosecurity standards. The Declaration of Helsinki pertains to research involving living human participants and, accordingly, was not applicable to this investigation.9–11

RESULTS

Fifty pork ribs were analyzed, divided into 2 groups according to the fracture technique used (perpendicular osteotomy [dots point approach] and parallel scraping [no dots point approach]) and subdivided into monocortical and bicortical fractures.

In the monocortical fracture group, the mean weight of the ribs treated with perpendicular technique was 12.0 ± 1.91 g, whereas the mean weight of the ribs in parallel scraping group was 10.54 ± 1.94 g. This difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.0704). In terms of length, the ribs in the perpendicular group measured an average of 12.0 ± 0.71 cm, compared to 12.0 ± 1.0 cm in the parallel scraping group, with no significant differences observed (P = 1.0).

In bicortical fractures, the average weight of the ribs in the perpendicular group was 10.69 ± 2.53 g, compared to 9.92 ± 1.93 g in the parallel scraping group (P = 0.3957). In terms of length, the ribs in the perpendicular group measured an average of 11.11 ± 0.93 cm, whereas those in the parallel scraping group measured 11.00 ± 0.63 cm; again, there were no statistically significant differences (P = 0.802). Overall, the weight and length values were comparable between the 2 techniques (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

Minimally invasive percutaneous rib remodeling (RibXcar) is a well-described procedure for aesthetic waist reduction. Its main advantage is the absence of scars, as a needle-guided piezotome is used to create a controlled monocortical fracture, allowing precise rib angulation and harmonious waist contouring.1,2

The safety and effectiveness of this procedure depends on precise corticotomy technique. A controlled monocortical fracture is created on the rib surface to define the angulation limit while preserving the internal cortex as a structural support. Understanding the appropriate force and corticotomy pattern is essential to achieve controlled rib angulation and minimize the risk of unintended complete fracture.8,12,13

The perpendicular (dots point) maneuver creates a controlled weakening of the rib to safely guide the fracture while preserving the inner cortex as a structural support. This point-by-point vertical approach allows precise control of penetration depth, direction, and applied force, enabling a controlled corticotomy with less force and reducing the risk of bicortical injury and damage to cancellous bone and intercostal vessels. Through tactile feedback and clear anatomical control, it provides a safer and more predictable method for rib remodeling. In contrast, the parallel approach may result in less precise anatomical limits due to outer cortex irregularities, increasing the risk of overpenetration into the intercostal space and potential injury to the intercostal neurovascular bundle, as no anatomical stop is present.14–16

One of the most important aspects of this maneuver is the presence of small residual cortical unions, or “bone bridges,” which provide support between the fracture line components. These connections stabilize and organize the corticotomy, promoting controlled progression of the fracture. This preserves adequate adhesion of the rib fragments and prevents displacement and unwanted bicorticalization. As described in biomechanical studies of bone fractures and experimental models of corticotomy17,18 these cortical bridges provide mechanical stability and contribute to the controlled propagation of the fracture line.

The parallel scraping (no dots point) maneuver involves making continuous back-and-forth cuts to create a continuous solution on the outer cortex. By relying on movements rather than the surgeon’s proprioception, the cortex can be broken more quickly, regardless of depth limitations. Consequently, the fracture line may become irregular, affecting the outer cortex, cancellous bone and inner cortex.7

The main complication of this method is the difficulty in accurately identifying the outer cortex boundaries, which may compromise controlled fracture. If the corticotomy extends too deeply toward the cancellous bone or inner cortex, it increases the risk of bicortical fracture or bone spicule formation, potentially leading to serious complications, such as pneumothorax or hemothorax.7

This experimental study compared 2 rib fracture techniques in a porcine model, evaluating force, time, and baseline characteristics. Rib weight and size were homogeneous, with no significant differences between groups. This uniformity ensured that biomechanical differences observed were attributable to the techniques rather than anatomical variability (Table 1).

Regarding the force required to induce a fracture, the results revealed significant differences (P < 0.001). Considerably less force was required for monocortical (6.67 ± 1.07 N) and bicortical (10.0 ± 1.0 N) fractures using the dots point technique than for the no dots point technique (parallel scraping) (14.54 ± 1.27 and 17.83 ± 0.83 N, respectively).

This suggests that perpendicular technique is a more efficient procedure from a mechanical point of view, as it reduces the effort required to produce the fracture. In a clinical context, this finding is relevant as it could allow for greater surgical control and reduce the risk of inadvertent injury to adjacent structures (Table 2).

Our work showed that the fracture line affects the amount of force required for a proper monocortical fracture. To achieve an effective fracture without complications, it is not necessary to apply a high-intensity force vector; rather, a subtle force that builds on the work of the fracture line is required. The RibXcar technique with perpendicular approach best structures this process.17,18

A relevant biomechanical aspect is how energy is transmitted depending on the approach to the fracture line. In the dots point technique, the line is made vertically and perpendicular to the rib axis, concentrating the energy on the outer cortex in a controlled manner. This pattern favors regulated fracture progression and reduces force propagation toward the inner cortex.

By contrast, the no dots point technique involves making a stroke parallel to the costal axis, generating continuous energy transmission along the rib. This increases the likelihood that the force will extend toward the inner cortex, promoting bicorticality and reducing control over the fracture.

Regarding the time required to achieve fracture, divergent patterns were revealed between the 2 techniques. Monocortical fracture was achieved more quickly with the dots point (perpendicular) technique than with the no dots point (parallel scraping) technique (4.68 ± 0.89 versus 6.20 ± 0.56 s; P < 0.001). However, the no dots point technique (parallel scraping) was significantly faster in the case of bicorticality (3.52 ± 0.68 versus 8.08 ± 0.89 s; P < 0.001). These results suggest that the perpendicular technique offers greater control in achieving monocortical fracture, whereas the parallel scraping technique tends to favor early bicorticalization, which could be problematic in procedures where this is to be avoided (Table 3).

Significant differences were observed in the force required for the transition from a monocortical to a bicortical fracture between the 2 techniques. In the perpendicular group, the transition from monocortical to bicortical fracture required an increase in force of approximately 49.9% (from 6.67 to 10.00 N). By contrast, moving from monocortical (14.54 N) to bicortical (17.83 N) in the parallel scraping group required only 22.6% more force (Table 2).

These results suggest that the perpendicular corticotomy offers a greater margin of safety in terms of applied force and proprioception. In other words, the boundary between monocorticalization and bicorticalization is narrower with the the parallel scraping technique, which increases the risk of exceeding the limit of monocorticalization inadvertently during the learning curve. Therefore, the perpendicular technique could be considered safer for trainees, as it offers a greater margin of tolerance in the application of controlled force.

Overall, the results show that perpendicular technique requires less force and allows greater control over the time taken to generate monocortical fractures. In contrast, the parallel scraping technique achieves bicorticality more quickly; however, this may pose a risk from a clinical point of view, as it increases the likelihood of damaging the inner cortex, which has implications for surgical safety.19–22

The present study was conducted using an ex vivo experimental model, which allowed precise evaluation of the biomechanical behavior associated with different corticotomy orientations under controlled conditions, without the influence of soft tissue variables. Although this model does not replicate the complete clinical environment, it provides important mechanistic insight into the structural response of the rib to perpendicular and parallel corticotomy patterns. These findings may help explain the clinical predictability observed with perpendicular corticotomy approaches. However, clinical extrapolation should be made with caution, and further in vivo and clinical studies are required to confirm the translational relevance of these biomechanical observations.

CONCLUSIONS

The procedure demonstrated that the dots point technique (perpendicular approach) enables rib fractures to be induced with less force and greater monocortical control than the no dots point technique (parallel approach). The latter requires greater effort and favors early bicorticalization. These results suggest that the dots point technique has significant biomechanical advantages, making it a safer and more predictable alternative for rib remodeling. However, further studies are needed to validate its clinical application.
