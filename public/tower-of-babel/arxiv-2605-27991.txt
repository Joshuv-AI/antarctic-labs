Gradient-Flow Optimization as Dynamic Random-Effects Inference: Testing and Early Stopping with Applications to Deep Learning
Minhao Yao, Ruoyu Wang, Xihong Lin, Lin Liu, Zhonghua Liu

Abstract
Gradient-flow optimization is usually viewed as an algorithmic procedure for minimizing empirical loss, with training duration selected by validation or heuristic early-stopping rules. We develop a statistical inference framework for the gradient-flow training trajectory itself. The central object is fixed-operator squared-error gradient flow: whenever the fitted value evolves through a time-invariant positive semidefinite training operator, the trained model output at each training time is exactly equivalent to the best linear unbiased predictor, or empirical-Bayes posterior mean, under a corresponding random-effects model. Under this representation, training time becomes a variance-component parameter governing how variance is reallocated from residual noise to structured signal. This turns two basic training decisions into inferential problems. First, whether training is needed is formulated as a variance-component test for signal beyond initialization. Second, how long to train is formulated as restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of the training-time variance component. The resulting REML-guided early stopping rule has a spectral interpretation: it selects the training time at which optimized spectral losses become empirically decorrelated from the eigenvalues of the training operator, yielding an effective degrees-of-freedom measure for the evolving trained model. We establish asymptotic prediction optimality for fixed-design in-sample risk and, under additional kernel regularity conditions, random-design out-of-sample risk. Deep learning models in fixed-kernel gradient regimes provide canonical modern-AI instantiations of the theory. Numerical experiments and a UK Biobank proteomics application show that the proposed inferential approach attains competitive prediction accuracy while reducing the reliance on validation splits and repeated checkpoint evaluation.

Deep learning has achieved remarkable success across a wide range of scientific applications

Recent statistical overviews have clarified many aspects of approximation, optimization, over-parameterization, and generalization

, but a statistical interpretation of the training trajectory remains less developed. This limitation is central to the interpretability of deep learning models as statistical procedures and is closely related to the broader concern that deep learning often behaves as black-box models
(

A substantial statistical literature studies deep learning models as flexible nonparametric regression estimators, focusing on how network architecture, function-class complexity, and structural assumptions on the target function or covariate distribution determine the accuracy of empirical-risk-minimizing predictors
(
60

8
;

However, they provide an incomplete account of the fitted predictor produced by actual training, since they typically focus on the global empirical-risk minimizer, which may not be attainable in practice because of the complicated loss landscape of neural network training
(
2
)
. The final predictor is therefore shaped not only by the function class and loss, but also by the optimization trajectory and the time at which training is stopped
(
27
)
. Understanding the statistical role of training itself consequently requires a complementary theory of the training path and stopping time.

This leaves both a conceptual and a methodological gap. Conceptually, we lack a statistical characterization of how optimization reallocates variation from residual noise to structured signal along the training path. Methodologically, we lack a corresponding statistical framework that turns the decisions of whether to train and when to stop into explicit inferential problems, rather than relying solely on validation-based or heuristic early-stopping rules
(
52
;
78
;
27
)
.
The central question of this paper is whether the training path itself can be given an inferential interpretation. In particular, we ask whether training time can be treated not merely as an external tuning parameter, but as an estimable statistical parameter governing signal extraction and model complexity.

We address this question by developing a general optimization–inference duality for fixed-operator squared-error gradient flow. Let
f_{t}({\bm{X}})
denote the vector of fitted values on
n
training samples
{\bm{X}}=({\bm{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\bm{x}}_{n})^{\top}
at training time
t

{\bm{y}}
denote the response vector. Suppose that
f_{t}({\bm{X}})
evolves according to

\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{t}({\bm{X}})}{\mathrm{d}t}=-{\bm{H}}\{f_{t}({\bm{X}})-{\bm{y}}\},

where
{\bm{H}}
is a time-invariant positive semidefinite training operator induced by a fixed positive semidefinite kernel
h(\cdot,\cdot)

{\bm{H}}_{ij}=h({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}}_{j})

1\leq i,j\leq n

{\bm{H}}

(
4

(
61
)
, linearized predictor, and, as a canonical deep learning instance, infinite-width neural networks in the neural tangent kernel (NTK) regime
(
31

. Within our framework, the gradient-flow trajectory is exactly equivalent, at every training time
t
, to the best linear unbiased predictor (BLUP), or empirical Bayes posterior mean, under a corresponding working random-effects model
(
30
;
22
)
. Thus, algorithmically, gradient flow is an optimization path; statistically, the same path is a random-effects inference path in which training time acts as a variance-component parameter governing how variation is reallocated from residual noise to structured signal.

We emphasize that the scope of our contribution is broader than the NTK regime. The NTK provides a well-established fixed-kernel description of neural-network training in suitable infinite-width regimes
(
31
;
21
;
38
;
6
;
50
;
75
)
, and serves here as a canonical deep-learning instantiation of our theory. Importantly, this fixed-kernel description is not limited to fully connected networks: NTK training dynamics have been shown to be architecturally universal for broad classes of neural network architectures, including convolutional, recurrent, residual, graph, and attention-based architectures
(
75
)
. More recently, NTK-based and linearized quantities have also been used in modern AI systems, including transformer fine-tuning and large language model adaptation
(
45
;
3
;
72
;
40
)
, further illustrating the practical relevance of fixed-kernel and linearized perspectives.

The optimization–inference duality developed in this paper, however, is not an NTK-specific phenomenon. It is a property of fixed-operator squared-error gradient flow more generally. This distinction is also important from the perspective of classical statistics. Although mixed-model representations of kernel ridge regression and smoothing splines are classical, those formulations typically start from a fixed kernel or an explicit quadratic penalty and lead to a
static
ridge-type regularization parameter
(
70
;
65
;
58
;
43
)
. The random-effects representation developed here is fundamentally different: the covariance
\exp(t\bm{H})-\bm{I}
is determined by the residual-decay operator
\exp(-t\bm{H})
of the gradient-flow trajectory itself, and the resulting
dynamic
BLUP at each training time
t
reproduces the corresponding early-stopped predictor. Thus, the novelty is not a random-effects representation of a kernel smoother, but a trajectory-indexed random-effects representation of the optimization path, in which training time
t
becomes an estimable variance-component parameter.

Our dynamic representation turns two basic training decisions into statistical inference problems. First, whether to train becomes a variance-component testing problem: the null hypothesis of no useful training corresponds to the absence of the training-induced random effect, leading to a score test for signal beyond initialization
(
42
)
. Second, conditional on training being warranted, the stopping time can be estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML)
(
30
;
17
)
. In this formulation, early stopping is recast as estimation of a variance component controlling model complexity, rather than as a purely validation-based or heuristic tuning rule.

The REML-guided stopping rule also has an interpretable spectral characterization. In the eigenbasis of the fixed training operator, gradient flow activates directions at different rates: larger-eigenvalue directions are fitted earlier, whereas smaller-eigenvalue directions retain more of their initial residual until later in training. The REML estimating equation selects the training time at which optimized spectral losses become empirically decorrelated from the eigenvalues of the training operator. This spectral loss decorrelation condition yields a natural effective degrees-of-freedom measure for the evolving predictor. We further show that, although the REML criterion is derived from a Gaussian working random-effects model, the resulting prediction guarantee does not require Gaussian errors or correct specification of that working model. Instead, under suitable regularity conditions on the actual data-generating process, the REML-guided stopping time achieves oracle-type guarantees for fixed-design in-sample risk and, under additional kernel regularity conditions, for random-design out-of-sample risk.

We complement the theory with numerical experiments and an application to UK Biobank proteomics data
(
67
)
. Across simulated and real-data examples, the proposed testing procedure detects when training extracts signal beyond initialization, and the REML-guided stopping rule achieves prediction accuracy comparable to validation-based early stopping while avoiding a separate validation split and reducing repeated checkpoint evaluation. These results illustrate the practical role of the proposed framework: it provides a statistically interpretable, sample-preserving, and computationally efficient principle for testing and early stopping in settings where (approximately) fixed-operator training dynamics are appropriate. To facilitate adoption, we also provide a computationally efficient and user-friendly Python package,
GF-REML
, implementing the proposed testing and REML-guided early-stopping procedures.

2
Gradient-Flow Optimization with a Fixed Training Operator

This section formulates the optimization setting studied in the paper. We first introduce the data model and notation for prediction functions on the training sample. We then describe squared-error gradient flow directly at the level of training-sample predictions through a positive semidefinite training operator. This formulation is intentionally general: it is not tied to a particular model class, and includes linear regression
(
4
)
, fixed-kernel methods
(
61
;
28
)
, linearized predictors, and neural networks in the neural tangent kernel regime
(
31
;
38
)
as special cases. The central optimization object is the fixed-operator gradient-flow trajectory, whose closed-form solution provides the basis for the statistical inference developed in Section
3
.

2.1
Data Model and Notation

Let
\{({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be the training data, generated from

y_{i}=f_{\star}({\bm{x}}_{i})+\varepsilon_{i},

(1)

where
f_{\star}
is the ground truth function,
{\bm{x}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an input feature vector,
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is the continuous response variable, and
\varepsilon_{i}
are independent and identically distributed error terms with mean zero and variance
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}
. Let

{\bm{X}}=({\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
,

{\bm{y}}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{\top}
, and

\bm{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})^{\top}

be the design matrix, response vector, and error vector of the training data, respectively. For any scalar function
f
, we write

f({\bm{X}})=\{f({\bm{x}}_{1}),\ldots,f({\bm{x}}_{n})\}^{\top}
. Unless otherwise stated, we condition on the design matrix
{\bm{X}}
and treat the input features as fixed; the random-design setting is considered separately in Corollary
1
.
For notational simplicity, we omit hats for intermediate quantities along the training trajectory and reserve the hat notation for final estimators or estimated quantities when distinction is needed.

2.2
Squared-Error Gradient Flow and Prediction

We formulate the training dynamics directly through the prediction vector on the training sample. For any scalar function
f
, we write
f(\bm{X})=(f(\bm{x}_{1}),\ldots,f(\bm{x}_{n}))^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e., the vector of predictions obtained by applying
f
row-wise to the design matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. Let
f_{t}
denote the predictor at training time
t
, and define the prediction vector
\bm{f}_{t}:=f_{t}(\bm{X})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Consider the unnormalized squared-error loss

L(f)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-f(\bm{x}_{i}))^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{y}-f(\bm{X})\|^{2},

(2)

where
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Gradient flow on this loss induces the following dynamics on the prediction vector:

\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{t}({\bm{X}})}{\mathrm{d}t}=-{\bm{H}}(t)\{f_{t}({\bm{X}})-{\bm{y}}\},\qquad{\bm{H}}(t)={\bm{H}}(t)^{\top}\succeq 0,

(3)

where
{\bm{H}}(t)
is a time-dependent positive semidefinite (PSD) training operator and defines the linear map from the residual to the derivative of the predictions.

Let
\mathcal{T}(t)
denote the residual-evolution operator satisfying

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{T}(t)=-{\bm{H}}(t)\mathcal{T}(t)
and

\mathcal{T}(0)={\bm{I}}
. Then the solution of (
3
)
with initial condition
f_{t}({\bm{X}})|_{t=0}=f_{0}({\bm{X}})
is

f_{t}({\bm{X}})=\bm{f}_{0}(\bm{X})+\{\bm{I}-\mathcal{T}(t)\}\{{\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}})\}
.
If
{\bm{H}}(s){\bm{H}}(r)={\bm{H}}(r){\bm{H}}(s)
for all
r,s\in[0,t]
, that is, if the time-dependent training operators are pairwise commuting on
[0,t]
, then

\mathcal{T}(t)=\exp\left\{-\int_{0}^{t}{\bm{H}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right\}=\exp\{-t\bar{{\bm{H}}}(t)\},\qquad\bar{{\bm{H}}}(t)=t^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}{\bm{H}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

(4)

Here
\bar{{\bm{H}}}(t)
is the time-averaged training operator. The pairwise commutativity condition ensures that the cumulative effect of the time-varying operators can be represented by a single matrix exponential; without this condition, the residual-evolution operator generally cannot be simplified to this form.

Inspired by continuous-time analyses of early stopping in least-squares regression
(
4
)
and fixed-kernel gradient-flow limits in deep learning
(
38
)
, we focus on the time-invariant case
{\bm{H}}(t)\equiv{\bm{H}}
. In this case, the residual-evolution operator reduces to
\mathcal{T}(t)=\exp(-t{\bm{H}})
, yielding

\widehat{f}_{t}^{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{X}})=f_{0}({\bm{X}})+\{{\bm{I}}-\exp(-t{\bm{H}})\}\{{\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}})\}.

(5)

When a cross-operator
h({\bm{x}},{\bm{X}})
is available for a new input
{\bm{x}}
, the corresponding out-of-sample extension of (
5
) is

\widehat{f}_{t}^{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{x}})=f_{0}({\bm{x}})+h({\bm{x}},{\bm{X}}){\bm{H}}^{\dagger}\{{\bm{I}}-\exp(-t{\bm{H}})\}\{{\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}})\},

(6)

where
{\bm{H}}^{\dagger}
denotes the Moore–Penrose inverse
(
51
)
. If
{\bm{H}}
is positive definite, then
{\bm{H}}^{\dagger}={\bm{H}}^{-1}
. The in-sample expression (
5
) is recovered from (
6
) by evaluating
{\bm{x}}
at the training points, in which case
h({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{X}})
is the
i
th row of
{\bm{H}}
. We now give three concrete examples of fixed operators
\bm{H}
that arise in practice.

Example 1

(Linear regression)
.

For linear regression, the predictor is
f(\bm{x})=\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\beta}
with
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The prediction vector on the training set is
\bm{f}=\bm{X}\bm{\beta}
, where
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
is the design matrix. The Jacobian with respect to
\bm{\beta}
is
\bm{X}
, so the fixed training operator is
\bm{H}=\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}
.

Example 2

(Kernel regression (unpenalized))
.

For kernel regression in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) with kernel
k(\cdot,\cdot)
, the kernel Gram matrix is
\bm{K}_{ij}=k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})

(
61
)
. Consider the unpenalized objective

\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{K}}\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{y}-f(\bm{X})\|^{2}.

The gradient flow fixed operator
\bm{H}=\bm{K}
yields the early-stopped predictor
\widehat{\bm{f}}_{t}=(\bm{I}-e^{-t\bm{K}})\bm{y}
under zero initialization
f_{0}(\bm{X})=\bm{0}
. No explicit penalty term is added; regularization arises solely from early stopping at time
t
. This is fundamentally different from kernel ridge regression, which adds an explicit penalty term
\rho\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}^{2}
to the objective function
(
43
)
.

Example 3

(Deep learning architectures in the NTK regime)
.

For a neural network architecture in the infinite-width NTK regime, the neural tangent kernel (NTK) converges to a deterministic kernel
h_{\infty}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})

(
31
;
38
)
. This fixed-kernel description is not limited to fully connected networks; NTK training dynamics have been shown to be architecturally universal for broad classes of neural network architectures expressible through tensor programs
(
76
;
75
)
, including modern architectures such as convolutional, recurrent, residual
(
29
)
, graph, and attention-based networks
(
69
)
. The NTK Gram matrix on the training set is
\bm{H}_{\infty}=(h_{\infty}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j}))_{i,j=1}^{n}
. In this regime, the matrix remains constant during training, so the gradient-flow operator is
\bm{H}=\bm{H}_{\infty}
, and the network is trained without any explicit penalty term.

3
Random-Effects Representation of Fixed-Operator Training

This section develops the random-effects inference framework for fixed-operator gradient flow. We first establish the exact equivalence between the gradient-flow trajectory and the BLUP under a working random-effects model. We then use this equivalence to construct a variance-component test for training-induced signal and a REML-guided estimator of the stopping time. The section concludes with the spectral interpretation of the stopping rule, the resulting effective degrees-of-freedom measure, and oracle-type prediction guarantees.

3.1
Fixed-Operator Gradient Flow as BLUP

We now show that the fixed-operator gradient-flow trajectory in (
3
) with a fixed
{\bm{H}}
admits an exact random-effects representation. This result is stated for a general fixed positive semidefinite training operator
{\bm{H}}
; the canonical NTK case is recovered by taking
{\bm{H}}=\bm{H}_{\infty}
in the deep learning training setting. Let
\mathsf{Tr}({\bm{A}})
denote the trace of a matrix
{\bm{A}}
,
\bm{I}
denote the
n\times n
dimensional identity matrix, and
\gamma_{t}=n^{-1}\mathsf{Tr}\{\exp(t{\bm{H}})\}
be the average eigenvalue of
\exp(t{\bm{H}})
.

The following theorem formalizes this optimization–inference equivalence by showing that, for any fixed positive semidefinite training operator
\bm{H}
, the entire fixed-operator gradient-flow trajectory can be represented exactly as a BLUP under a time-indexed working random-effects model; the proof is given in the Supplementary Materials.

Theorem 1

(Equivalence between fixed-operator gradient flow and BLUP)
.

For each training time
t\geq 0
, the fitted-value trajectory
\widehat{f}_{t}^{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{X}})
in (
5
) satisfies

\widehat{f}_{t}^{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{X}})=f_{0}({\bm{X}})+\widehat{\bm{u}}_{t},

where
\widehat{\bm{u}}_{t}
is the BLUP
(
30
)
of the random effects
\bm{u}_{t}
in the following working random-effects model:

\begin{split}{\bm{y}}&=f_{0}({\bm{X}})+\bm{u}_{t}+\bm{\varepsilon}_{t},\\
\bm{u}_{t}&=(u_{1,t},\cdots,u_{n,t})^{\top}\sim N\left(\bm{0},\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}\{\exp(t{\bm{H}})-\bm{I}\}\right),\\
\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}&=(\varepsilon_{t,1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{t,n})^{\top}\sim N(\bm{0},\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}\bm{I}),\end{split}

(7)

where
\bm{u}_{t}\perp\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}
and

\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}=\gamma_{t}^{-1}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}

for some constant
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}>0
.
In the canonical NTK case,
{\bm{H}}=\bm{H}_{\infty}
, and the result gives the random-effects representation of the NTK gradient-flow output
\widehat{f}^{{\bm{H}}_{\infty}}_{t}({\bm{X}})
.

(Comparison with explicit quadratic-penalty estimators)
.

\bm{C}_{t}=\exp(t\bm{H})-\bm{I}

Remark 2

(Time-varying training operators)
.

Theorem
1
is stated for a fixed operator
{\bm{H}}
, but the time-varying formulation in (
4
) suggests a natural cumulative-operator extension.
Suppose the time-varying positive semidefinite training operators are pairwise commuting over time, that is,
{\bm{H}}(s){\bm{H}}(r)={\bm{H}}(r){\bm{H}}(s)
for all
r,s\in[0,t]
.
The gradient-flow solution becomes

\widehat{f}_{t}(\bm{X})=f_{0}(\bm{X})+\bigl(\bm{I}-\bm{\mathcal{T}}(t)\bigr)\bigl\{\bm{y}-f_{0}(\bm{X})\bigr\}
,
where
\bm{\mathcal{T}}(t)=\!\exp\!\bigl\{-\int_{0}^{t}{\bm{H}}(s)\,ds\bigr\}
. The random-effects representation in (
7
) holds with
\exp\{t{\bm{H}}\}
replaced by
\!\exp\!\bigl\{\int_{0}^{t}{\bm{H}}(s)\,ds\bigr\}
.

Empirical Bayes interpretation.

The random-effects representation also gives an empirical Bayes interpretation of fixed-operator
gradient-flow training. Conditional on
t
and
\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}
, the latent
training-induced signal
{\bm{u}}_{t}
is assigned the implicit dynamic Gaussian prior

{\bm{u}}_{t}\mid t,\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}\sim N\left(0,\,\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}\{\exp(t{\bm{H}})-\bm{I}\}\right)
,
and the observation model is

{\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}})={\bm{u}}_{t}+\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}
and

\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}\sim N(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}\bm{I})
.
The posterior mean of
{\bm{u}}_{t}
, equivalently its BLUP, is

\mathbb{E}\left({\bm{u}}_{t}\mid{\bm{y}},t,\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}\right)=\{\bm{I}-\exp(-t{\bm{H}})\}\{{\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}})\}
.
Thus, the fixed-operator gradient-flow predictor is the empirical Bayes posterior mean of the latent signal,
with
t
playing the role of a covariance hyperparameter. Estimating
t
by REML therefore turns early stopping into empirical Bayes estimation of the prior covariance rather than a tuning-parameter selection problem.

This differs from the empirical-Bayes interpretation of classical quadratic-penalty estimators, such as penalized splines
(
58
)
or kernel ridge regression
(
43
)
, where the penalty is imposed explicitly and determines a static Gaussian prior covariance. Here, no explicit penalty is added; the Gaussian prior covariance
\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}\{\exp(t{\bm{H}})-\bm{I}\}
is induced dynamically by the gradient-flow residual-decay operator and evolves with training time
t
.

In the remainder of this section, all testing, REML, spectral, and prediction results are stated for a general fixed positive semidefinite training operator
{\bm{H}}
. The NTK case is obtained as the special case
{\bm{H}}={\bm{H}}_{\infty}
, with cross-operator
h({\bm{x}},{\bm{X}})=h_{\infty}({\bm{x}},{\bm{X}})
.

3.2
Testing for Training-Induced Signal

A key question in gradient flow optimization is whether training extracts predictive structure in
y
from
{\bm{X}}
beyond initialization, which can guide our assessment of whether training is useful. This inferential problem can be handled via the random-effects model (
7
). Under the working random-effects model (
7
), the null hypothesis that training induces no additional signal is
\bm{u}_{t}=0
, equivalently
{\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}})=\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}
.
Under the data-generating model (
1
), a no-signal null is naturally expressed as
f_{\star}({\bm{X}})=0
after centering.
These two null formulations need not coincide when the initialized prediction
f_{0}({\bm{X}})
is nonzero.

To obtain a test that removes the initialized prediction and controls the null behavior under both formulations, we propose to remove the initialized prediction
f_{0}({\bm{X}})
using the projection matrix
\bm{\Pi}=\bm{I}-(f_{0}(\bm{X})^{\top}f_{0}(\bm{X}))^{-1}f_{0}(\bm{X})f_{0}(\bm{X})^{\top}
onto the subspace orthogonal to the initialized prediction
f_{0}(\bm{X})
and construct a score test based on the residual likelihood. Specifically, note that
\bm{\Pi}
is the projection matrix onto an
n-1
dimensional space. It has
n-1
eigenvalues equal to one. Let
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(n-1)}
be a matrix that has the
(n-1)
eigenvectors of
\bm{\Pi}
associated with the eigenvalue one as its columns. Then,
\bm{\Pi}=\bm{M}\bm{M}^{\top}
,
\bm{I}_{n-1}=\bm{M}^{\top}\bm{M}
, and the random-effects model (
7
) yields the orthogonally transformed model

\widetilde{\bm{y}}=\bm{M}^{\top}\bm{u}_{t}+\bm{M}^{\top}\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}
,
\mathsf{Cov}(\widetilde{\bm{y}})=\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon,t}\bm{M}^{\top}\exp(t\bm{H})\bm{M}
. Thus, the initialized prediction
f_{0}({\bm{X}})
is removed, and inference can focus on whether training reveals a predictive signal beyond initialization.

The residual log-likelihood function after removing the initialized prediction is

\displaystyle\widetilde{\ell}_{R}(t,\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\log\det\left[\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}\bm{M}^{\top}\exp(t\bm{H})\bm{M}\right]-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}}\widetilde{{\bm{y}}}^{\top}\left(\bm{M}^{\top}\exp(t\bm{H})\bm{M}\right)^{-1}\widetilde{{\bm{y}}}.

(8)

Then, testing whether training is statistically necessary can be formulated as the hypothesis testing problem
H_{0}:t=0
versus
H_{1}:t>0
. Under the random-effects representation in (
7
), the null hypothesis
t=0
corresponds to the absence of the training-induced random effect, that is, no predictive structure is extracted beyond initialization. After profiling out
\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}
in the above residual log-likelihood (
8
) and omitting constants that are irrelevant to the data, we obtain the following score test statistic
(
42
)

\widetilde{T}=\frac{(n-1)\widetilde{{\bm{y}}}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{H}}\widetilde{{\bm{y}}}}{\widetilde{{\bm{y}}}^{\top}\widetilde{{\bm{y}}}}

where
\widetilde{\bm{H}}=\bm{M}^{\top}\bm{H}\bm{M}
. The
p
-value can be computed using the exact method
(
19
)
.

3.3
REML-guided Selection of Training Time

In this section, we exploit model (
7
) to motivate a new early stopping criterion.
Model (
7
) contains two unknown parameters, the training time
t
and the noise variance
\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}
. We propose to estimate them using the REML method, which provides a principled approach to determine the early stopping time. Because our target is to estimate the stopping time for the training procedure, which involves both the initialization and the gradient descent training, we consider the restricted log-likelihood function based on the original likelihood function:

\displaystyle\ell_{R}(t,\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{n}{2}\log(\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2})-\frac{1}{2}\log\det\left[\exp(t\bm{H})\right]-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}}({\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}}))^{\top}\exp(-t\bm{H})({\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}})).

For a fixed
t
, maximizing
\ell_{R}(t,\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2})
with respect to
\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}
gives

\widehat{\sigma}_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}=n^{-1}({\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}}))^{\top}\exp(-t\bm{H})({\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}}))
.
Substituting this expression back into the restricted log-likelihood shows that estimating
t
is equivalent to minimizing

Q(t)=n\log\left(({\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}}))^{\top}\exp(-t\bm{H})({\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}}))\right)+\log\det\left[\exp(t\bm{H})\right].

Let
\{\lambda_{k},\bm{v}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
be the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the fixed training operator
\bm{H}
, sorted in descending order. Define the projection coefficient of residual
({\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}}))
onto the
k
-th eigenspace as
c_{k}=\bm{v}_{k}^{\top}({\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}}))
. Note that
{\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\bm{v}_{k}
. Then, we have

Q(t)=n\log\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}^{2}\exp(-t\lambda_{k})\right)+t\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k},\quad t\geq 0.

(9)

Differentiating
Q(t)
with respect to
t
and setting
Q^{\prime}(t)=0
gives:

Q^{\prime}(t)=-n\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}c_{k}^{2}\exp(-t\lambda_{k})}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}^{2}\exp(-t\lambda_{k})}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}=0.

After some algebra, we find that the REML estimate of training time
t
is the solution to the following estimating equation

\Psi_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\lambda_{k}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\right)\left\{c_{k}^{2}\exp(-t\lambda_{k})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}^{2}\exp(-t\lambda_{j})\right\}=0.

(10)

Remark 3
.

The estimating equation (
10
) can also be understood without relying directly on the Gaussian likelihood. Let

\bm{r}_{0}={\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}})

denote the initial residual vector for the training sample of size
n
, and let
\bm{H}=\bm{V}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{V}^{\top}
,
\bm{\Lambda}=\mathsf{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
,

\bm{V}=(\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{n})
,
be the eigendecomposition of
\bm{H}
, where

\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}\geq 0
. Then

\exp(-t\bm{H})=\bm{V}\exp(-t\bm{\Lambda})\bm{V}^{\top}
,

\exp(-t\bm{\Lambda})=\mathsf{diag}\{\exp(-t\lambda_{1}),\dots,\exp(-t\lambda_{n})\}
. For a candidate training time
t
, define the vector of eigen-residuals
\mathrm{ER}(t)
as follows

\mathrm{ER}(t)=\exp(-t\bm{\Lambda}/2)\bm{V}^{\top}\bm{r}_{0},

where the
k
-th eigen-residual is

\mathrm{ER}_{k}(t)=\exp(-t\lambda_{k}/2)\bm{v}_{k}^{\top}\bm{r}_{0},k=1,\dots,n
.
Thus,
\mathrm{ER}_{k}(t)
is the residual coordinate in the
k
-th eigendirection after applying the square-root residual decay factor
\exp(-t\lambda_{k}/2)
, and
\mathrm{ER}_{k}^{2}(t)=\exp(-t\lambda_{k})\{\bm{v}_{k}^{\top}\bm{r}_{0}\}^{2}
.

The REML early stopping time is the point at which the squared eigen-residuals become empirically uncorrelated with their corresponding eigenvalues. Equivalently, it solves the moment condition

\Psi_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\lambda_{k}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\right)\left\{\mathrm{ER}_{k}^{2}(t)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{ER}_{j}^{2}(t)\right\}=0.

(11)

A positive value of
\Psi_{n}(t)
indicates that larger-eigenvalue directions still carry relatively large squared eigen-residuals. Along the training trajectory, residuals in these directions decay exponentially faster, so this pattern suggests that training has not yet sufficiently reduced the residual in the dominant eigendirections. A negative value of
\Psi_{n}(t)
indicates that larger-eigenvalue directions have relatively small squared eigen-residuals, while the remaining residual is more concentrated in smaller-eigenvalue directions, whose residuals decay more slowly. This suggests that training has moved beyond the dominant eigendirections and is increasingly fitting smaller-eigenvalue directions. The REML stopping time is therefore the balance point at which this empirical spectral covariance is zero. This provides a moment-based interpretation of the REML estimating equation and helps explain why the resulting stopping rule can remain useful beyond the Gaussian working model.

Next, we establish sufficient conditions for the existence and uniqueness of the empirical REML-guided early stopping time
\widehat{t}^{\mathrm{REML}}_{n}
, defined as a solution to the empirical estimating equation (
10
), or equivalently, as a minimizer of the restricted log-likelihood.
By construction,
\widehat{t}^{\mathrm{REML}}_{n}
is any solution to
Q^{\prime}(t)=0
.
To characterize the shape of
Q(t)
, define
p_{k}(t)=c_{k}^{2}\exp(-t\lambda_{k})/\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}^{2}\exp(-t\lambda_{j})\}
.
Then,
p_{1}(t),\ldots,p_{n}(t)
form a probability distribution
\mathcal{P}^{\lambda}_{t}
on
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and straightforward calculation yields

Q^{\prime\prime}(t)=n\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}(t)\left\{\lambda_{k}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}(t)\lambda_{j}\right\}^{2}=n\,\mathsf{var}_{\mathcal{P}^{\lambda}_{t}}(\lambda)\geq 0.

Because
Q^{\prime\prime}(t)
is non-negative,
Q(t)
is convex on
[0,\infty)
. The following proposition gives sufficient conditions for the existence and uniqueness of
\widehat{t}^{\rm REML}_{n}
.

Proposition 1

(Existence and uniqueness of the REML-guided stopping time)
.

Suppose that

(i)

Q^{\prime}(0)<0
;

(ii)

there exist
k,j\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
such that
\lambda_{k}\neq\lambda_{j}
.

Assume
\varepsilon_{i}
is a continuous random variable for
i=1,\dots,n
.
Then, with probability one, there exists a unique
\widehat{t}^{\rm REML}_{n}\in(0,\infty)
such that

Q^{\prime}(\widehat{t}^{\rm REML}_{n})=0.

Moreover,
\widehat{t}^{\rm REML}_{n}
is the unique minimizer of
Q(t)
over
t\in(0,\infty)
.

Remark 4
.

Condition (i) requires that the REML profile objective initially decreases away from
t=0
. This is equivalent to
\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}^{2}\lambda_{k}/\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}^{2}>\bar{\lambda}
which can be further written as the covariance form

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(c_{k}^{2}-\bar{c^{2}})(\lambda_{k}-\bar{\lambda})>0

with
\bar{c^{2}}=n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}^{2}
.
Condition (i) can be satisfied if the projection coefficient
c_{k}^{2}
tends to be larger for large eigenvalues. Condition (ii) ensures that the eigenspaces include at least one eigenvalue below
\bar{\lambda}
, so that
Q^{\prime}(t)
eventually becomes positive as
t\to\infty
. In addition, condition (ii) guarantees strict convexity of
Q(t)
, and hence uniqueness of the empirical REML-guided stopping time. The spectral patterns underlying conditions (i) and (ii) can be empirically assessed from the data, as illustrated later in Figure
2
(b).

Remark 5
.

We explicitly exploit the exponential covariance structure of the random effect term
\bm{u}_{t}\sim N(\bm{0},\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}(\exp(t\bm{H})-\bm{I}))
. The exponential covariance structure
\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}(\exp(t\bm{H})-\bm{I})
helps to establish the convexity of the REML objective function, which leads to the uniqueness of the REML stopping time. In contrast, REML criteria for smoothing-parameter selection in penalized spline regression may exhibit multiple local optima
(
55
)
; the exponential residual-decay structure here yields a convex REML profile objective, a property we use for uniqueness and for the prediction-risk analysis in Theorem
2
.

3.4
Spectral Loss Decorrelation in Training Dynamics

In this section, we develop a geometric and spectral interpretation of the fixed-operator gradient-flow training dynamics.
We show that, under the random-effects representation, training progressively reallocates variance from residual noise to a fixed-operator-structured random effect and acts as a coordinate-wise spectral regularization procedure in the fixed-operator eigenbasis. This interpretation leads to the notion of spectral loss decorrelation: the REML estimating equation selects the training time at which the optimized spectral losses are empirically decorrelated from the eigenvalues of
{\bm{H}}
. It also yields a natural effective degrees-of-freedom (edf) measure for the trained model, with each eigendirection of
{\bm{H}}
contributing according to its training-time-dependent activation.
Together, these results clarify how model complexity evolves during training and provide a statistical rationale for the REML-guided early-stopping criterion.

Model (
7
) uses the random effect
\bm{u}_{t}
to model the difference
f_{\star}(\bm{X})-f_{0}(\bm{X})
. The variance scale then satisfies
\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}=n\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}/\sum_{k=1}^{n}\exp(t\lambda_{k})
. Under this formulation, the total marginal variance
\sigma^{2}_{\text{Total}}=\mathsf{Tr}\{\mathsf{var}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{t})\}+\mathsf{Tr}\{\mathsf{var}(\bm{u}_{t})\}=n\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}
remains constant for all
t\geq 0
. In the random-effects model, the covariance of the random effect admits the spectral decomposition

\mathsf{var}({\bm{u}}_{t})=\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}(\exp(t\bm{H})-\bm{I})=n\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(p_{k,t}-p_{0,t})\bm{v}_{k}\bm{v}_{k}^{\top},

where
\bm{v}_{k}
is the eigenvector of
\bm{H}
corresponding to eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
,

p_{k,t}=\exp(t\lambda_{k})/\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp(t\lambda_{j})
is the softmax weight assigned to
the
k
-th eigendirection of
\bm{H}
, and

p_{0,t}=\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp(t\lambda_{j})\}^{-1}
. The above decomposition indicates that the eigenprojector
\bm{v}_{k}\bm{v}_{k}^{\top}
is prioritized in
\mathsf{var}(\bm{u}_{t})
according to the softmax value
p_{k,t}
.
As training progresses, the error variance
\mathsf{Tr}\{\mathsf{var}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{t})\}=n^{2}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}/\sum_{k=1}^{n}\exp(t\lambda_{k})
is monotonically decreasing in
t
, while the variance of the random effect,
\mathsf{Tr}\{\mathsf{var}(\bm{u}_{t})\}=n\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}-\mathsf{Tr}\{\mathsf{var}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{t})\}
, is monotonically increasing. This implies that the signal-to-noise ratio increases over time, where
\mathsf{var}(\bm{u}_{t})
represents the signal captured from the initial residual for prediction. Specifically, the proportion of the variance explained by the random effect is given by

\frac{\mathsf{Tr}\{\mathsf{var}(\bm{u}_{t})\}}{\sigma^{2}_{\text{Total}}}=1-\frac{n}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\exp(t\lambda_{k})}.

In addition to the above
variance component explanation, the random effect perspective naturally motivates interpreting fixed-operator gradient-flow training as a spectral regularization method. Note that
\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{n}
span
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the predicted random effect
\widehat{\bm{u}}_{t}
can be written as
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\widehat{a}_{k,t}\bm{v}_{k}
, where the coefficients
\{\widehat{a}_{k,t}\}_{k=1}^{n}
are characterized by the following proposition, which establishes a geometric interpretation of the gradient descent training dynamics. This result also shows the converse side of the optimization–inference duality: the BLUP induced by the random-effects model is itself the solution to a spectral regularization problem.

Proposition 2

(Spectral regularization characterization of the random-effects predictor)
.

For each
t>0
, the
\rm BLUP

\widehat{{\bm{u}}}_{t}
can be obtained by solving the following spectrally regularized optimization problem

\widehat{{\bm{u}}}_{t}=\arg\min_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\|{\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}})-\bm{u}\|^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(\bm{v}_{k}^{\top}\bm{u})^{2}}{\exp(t\lambda_{k})-1}\right\}.

The predicted random effect
\widehat{\bm{u}}_{t}
can be written as
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\widehat{a}_{k,t}\bm{v}_{k}
, where
the coefficient vector
\widehat{{\bm{a}}}_{t}=(\widehat{a}_{1,t},\dots,\widehat{a}_{n,t})^{\top}
can be obtained by solving the following regularized optimization problem

\widehat{\bm{a}}_{t}=\arg\min_{\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\|{\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}})-\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\bm{v}_{k}\|^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{\exp(t\lambda_{k})-1}\right\}

(12)

with conventions that
1/0=\infty
and
0\cdot\infty=0
.
The above objective function can be decomposed as

\|{\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}})-\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\bm{v}_{k}\|^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{\exp(t\lambda_{k})-1}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal{J}_{k}(a_{k};t),

where
\widehat{a}_{k,t}=c_{k}\left(1-\exp(-t\lambda_{k})\right)
,
\mathcal{J}_{k}(a_{k};t)=c_{k}^{2}-2c_{k}a_{k}+a_{k}^{2}/\{1-\exp(-t\lambda_{k})\}
,
and
\mathcal{J}_{k}(\widehat{a}_{k,t};t)=c_{k}^{2}\exp(-t\lambda_{k})
.

When
\lambda_{k}=0
, the corresponding term is interpreted in the limiting sense: the coefficient along that eigendirection is constrained to be zero, so that
\widehat{a}_{k,t}=0
and
\mathcal{J}_{k}(\widehat{a}_{k,t};t)=c_{k}^{2}
.
Equivalently, the displayed optimization may be written over the eigendirections with
\lambda_{k}>0
, with zero-eigenvalue directions left unactivated by training. We also note that, in many canonical NTK settings, all eigendirections are active: for feedforward networks with bias terms, at least two hidden layers, and continuous, almost-everywhere differentiable, non-polynomial activation function, the infinite-width NTK matrix
{\bm{H}}_{\infty}
is strictly positive definite for distinct inputs
(
12
)
.

Proposition
2
shows that training induces an eigenspace-specific spectral regularization mechanism in the eigenbasis of the fixed operator
{\bm{H}}
. Specifically, the estimated coefficient along the
k
th eigendirection satisfies
\widehat{a}_{k,t}=c_{k}\{1-\exp(-t\lambda_{k})\}
,
1\leq k\leq n
. Because the factor
1-\exp(-t\lambda_{k})
increases monotonically with
\lambda_{k}
, directions associated with larger eigenvalues are learned more rapidly at an exponential rate and contribute more strongly to the fitted model, whereas directions associated with smaller eigenvalues are incorporated more gradually during training and zero-eigenvalue directions are left unactivated in training. Thus, early stopping acts as a coordinate-wise spectral regularization rule, controlling how quickly different eigenspaces of
{\bm{H}}
are activated along the training trajectory. This eigenspace-specific filtering determines both how much each spectral component contributes to the fitted model and how much optimized spectral loss remains after training.

To characterize the spectral evolution of the training trajectory, we introduce the following notion of spectral loss decorrelation.

Definition 1

(Spectral loss decorrelation)
.

Let
\mathcal{J}_{k,t}=\min_{a_{k}}\mathcal{J}_{k}(a_{k};t)=\text{ER}_{k}^{2}(t)
be the optimized spectral loss on the
k
-th eigenspace, equivalently, the squared eigen-residual. We say that spectral loss decorrelation holds at time
t
if the empirical covariance between
\{\mathcal{J}_{k,t}\}_{k=1}^{n}
and
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
is zero, i.e.,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\mathcal{J}_{k,t}-\bar{\mathcal{J}}_{t})(\lambda_{k}-\bar{\lambda})=0,

where
\bar{\mathcal{J}}_{t}=n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathcal{J}_{k,t}
and
\bar{\lambda}=n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}
.

Note that
\mathcal{J}_{k,t}=c_{k}^{2}\exp(-t\lambda_{k})
according to Proposition
2
, the REML estimating equation in (
10
) can be interpreted as selecting the training time
t
such that the Empirical Spectral Covariance (ESC)
\Psi_{n}(t)
between the optimized spectral loss
\{\mathcal{J}_{k,t}\}_{k=1}^{n}
and fixed-operator eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
is zero. In this sense, the REML-guided early stopping rule selects
t
that achieves
spectral loss decorrelation
, as shown in Figure
2
(a).

Figure 2
:
(a). Empirical spectral covariance (ESC)
\Psi_{n}(t)
during the training process. The training time is displayed on the
\log_{10}
scale. (b). Scatter plot of
\{(\lambda_{k},\min_{a_{k}}\mathcal{J}_{k}(a_{k};t))\}_{k=1}^{n}
at three training times:
t=0
,
\widehat{t}_{n}^{\mathrm{REML}}
and
5\times\widehat{t}_{n}^{\mathrm{REML}}
. The horizontal axis shows the eigenvalues of the fixed operator
{\bm{H}}
, and the vertical axis shows the corresponding optimized spectral losses; both axes are displayed on the
\log_{10}
scale. For visualization, values on the vertical axis below
10^{-8}
are truncated at
10^{-8}
.

Figure
2
(b) illustrates how training time reallocates the optimized spectral losses across eigendirections of the fixed operator
{\bm{H}}
. At
t<\widehat{t}_{n}^{\rm REML}
(e.g.
t=0
), the empirical spectral covariance
\Psi_{n}(t)>0
, indicating that directions with larger eigenvalues still carry larger spectral losses; this corresponds to an underfitting regime, since even the dominant, more learnable eigendirections have not yet been sufficiently fitted. At
t=\widehat{t}_{n}^{\rm REML}
, we have
\Psi_{n}(\widehat{t}_{n}^{\rm REML})=0
, suggesting that the optimized spectral losses are balanced across eigendirections. This indicates a stopping time at which gradient flow has substantially learned the meaningful signal carried by the leading eigendirections, without yet overfitting the noisier small-eigenvalue directions. At
t>\widehat{t}_{n}^{\rm REML}
(e.g.,
t=5\times\widehat{t}_{n}^{\rm REML}
), the empirical spectral covariance
\Psi_{n}(t)<0
, meaning that the large-eigenvalue directions have already been fit aggressively while the remaining loss is concentrated in the small-eigenvalue directions; this indicates an overfitting regime in which further training primarily fits noisy directions associated with small eigenvalues.

The spectral regularization also yields a natural measure of model complexity. Let
{\bm{r}}_{0}={\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}})
. Since
\widehat{{\bm{u}}}_{t}=\left(\bm{I}-\exp\left(-t\bm{H}\right)\right)\bm{r}_{0}
, the BLUP
\widehat{{\bm{u}}}_{t}
is a linear smoother of
{\bm{r}}_{0}
with smoother matrix
{\bm{S}}_{t}=\bm{I}-\exp\left(-t\bm{H}\right)
. Following
10
,
we define the effective degrees of freedom of fixed-operator gradient flow at training time
t
as

\mathrm{edf}(t):=\mathsf{Tr}({\bm{S}}_{t})=\mathsf{Tr}\left(\bm{I}-\exp\left(-t\bm{H}\right)\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\{1-\exp(-t\lambda_{k})\}.

This time-varying quantity provides a spectral measure of model complexity along the training trajectory. Each fixed-operator eigenspace contributes exactly
1-\exp(-t\lambda_{k})
to
\mathrm{edf}(t)
, so
\mathrm{edf}(t)
aggregates the effective number of spectral directions that have been activated by training time
t
. In particular,
\mathrm{edf}(0)=0
, and
\mathrm{edf}(t)
increases monotonically with
t
, showing that model complexity grows continuously during training. Moreover, directions associated with larger eigenvalues are activated more rapidly, while directions associated with smaller eigenvalues remain suppressed for a longer period. Therefore, early stopping controls model complexity by regulating how many spectral directions of the fixed operator
{\bm{H}}
are effectively used at a given training time.

Remark 6
.

Our results provide a unified statistical interpretation of training dynamics in fixed-operator gradient flow. Specifically, the training time
t
plays four simultaneous roles: it is the stopping time of the fixed-operator gradient flow; it determines the strength of spectral regularization in the fixed-operator eigenbasis;
it quantifies model complexity through the effective degrees of freedom
\mathrm{edf}(t)
; and it can be estimated in a principled manner by REML. The REML estimating equation (
10
) therefore links training dynamics, spectral regularization, model complexity,
and statistical estimation within a single unified framework.

3.5
Prediction Optimality of REML-Guided Early Stopping

In this section, we show that the REML-guided early stopping time achieves optimal prediction performance by leveraging the specific structure of the gradient-flow dynamics in equation (
6
). Although the REML criterion is motivated by the working random-effects model in equation (
7
), the prediction risk is analyzed under the original data-generating model in equation (
1
). Thus, the optimality result is not a
direct consequence of the correct specification of the random-effects model; rather, it shows that the REML-induced stopping rule remains prediction-optimal under the actual data-generating mechanism. Let
\widehat{y}_{i,t}=\widehat{f}^{{\bm{H}}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{i})
denote the in-sample prediction for
observation
i
at training time
t\geq 0
.

Following the standard prediction-error framework for evaluating fitted predictors
(
16
;
28
)
, we first consider the following in-sample prediction risk

\mathcal{E}_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[(y_{{\rm new},i}-\widehat{y}_{i,t})^{2}],

where

y_{{\rm new},i}=f_{\star}({\bm{x}}_{i})+\varepsilon_{{\rm new},i}
,
\varepsilon_{{\rm new},i}
is independent of
{\bm{y}}
and has the same distribution as
\varepsilon_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
. In the theoretical analysis, we allow
\mathsf{var}(\varepsilon_{i})
to depend on
n
and denote it by
\sigma_{\varepsilon,n}^{2}
. This allows the theoretical results to cover the different noise regimes, including those with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{\varepsilon,n}^{2}>0
(standard-noise regime) and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{\varepsilon,n}^{2}=0
(small-noise regime)
(
11
;
41
)
. For analytical convenience, we assume that the response and the initial prediction are centered such that
\mathbb{E}[n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}]=0
and
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{0}({\bm{x}}_{i})=0
.
Next, we establish the “oracle” property of
\widehat{t}_{n}^{\rm REML}
.
Note that

{\bm{y}}-\widehat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}=\exp\left(-t\bm{H}\right)\left\{\bm{y}-f_{0}({\bm{X}})\right\},

where
\widehat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}=(\widehat{y}_{1,t},\dots,\widehat{y}_{n,t})^{\top}
,
and
\widehat{t}_{n}^{\rm REML}
is the minimizer of the following penalized weighted training error

\displaystyle V_{n}(t)=n^{-1}\exp\{n^{-1}Q(t)\}=\underbrace{n^{-1}({\bm{y}}-\widehat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})^{\top}\exp(t\bm{H})({\bm{y}}-\widehat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})}_{\text{weighted training error}}\quad\times\underbrace{\exp(t\bar{\lambda}).}_{\text{penalty for overfitting}}

In the above decomposition of the REML criterion
V_{n}(t)
, the weighted training error term
n^{-1}({\bm{y}}-\widehat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})^{\top}\exp(t\bm{H})({\bm{y}}-\widehat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})=n^{-1}({\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}}))^{\top}\exp(-t\bm{H})({\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}}))
decreases as
t
increases, thereby preventing underfitting. In contrast, the exponential penalty factor
\exp(t\bar{\lambda})
discourages overly large stopping times and hence mitigates overfitting. In addition, the specific structure of
V_{n}(t)
enables it to closely approximate the in-sample prediction risk in a neighborhood of its minimizer. Leveraging these observations, we can establish the optimality of
\widehat{t}_{n}^{\mathrm{REML}}
in terms of the in-sample prediction risk under the following conditions. Let

b_{k}={\bm{v}}_{k}^{\top}(f_{\star}({\bm{X}})-f_{0}({\bm{X}}))
.

Condition 1

(Learnability)
.

\sigma_{\varepsilon,n}^{-2}\inf_{t\geq 0}\mathcal{E}_{n}(t)\to 1
.

Condition 2

(Bounded projection)
.

n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{f_{\star}({\bm{x}}_{i})-f_{0}({\bm{x}}_{i})\}^{2}
is uniformly bounded above.

Condition 3

(Existence of small eigenvalue)
.

There is some
\alpha>0
and
\delta_{n}\to 0
such that
n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}1\{\lambda_{k}\leq\delta_{n}\bar{\lambda}\}\geq\alpha
.

Condition 4

(Sub-Gaussian error)
.

The normalized error term
\sigma_{\varepsilon,n}^{-1}\varepsilon_{i}
(
i=1,\dots,n
) is sub-Gaussian with sub-Gaussian norm uniformly bounded above.

Condition
1
assumes that the optimal prediction risk
\sigma_{\varepsilon,n}^{2}
is attainable given a sufficiently appropriate training duration. Condition
2
imposes a constraint on the initialization bias
f_{\star}({\bm{X}})-f_{0}({\bm{X}})
. Condition
3
requires that a non-negligible proportion of the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
are substantially smaller than the average eigenvalue
\bar{\lambda}
, which is typically ensured when the eigenvalues decay sufficiently fast. For instance, suppose
\lambda_{k}/\lambda_{1}
is of order
k^{-\zeta}
for some
\zeta>1
. Then
\bar{\lambda}/\lambda_{1}
is of order
n^{-1}
, and the number of eigenvalues satisfying
\lambda_{k}/\lambda_{1}\geq\delta_{n}\bar{\lambda}/\lambda_{1}
is of order
(n/\delta_{n})^{1/\zeta}
. This quantity is
o(n)
if
\delta_{n}=n^{-(\zeta-1)/2}
. Consequently,
n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}1\{\lambda_{k}\leq\delta_{n}\bar{\lambda}\}\to 1
and Condition
3
holds for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
under this choice of
\delta_{n}
.
Condition
4
is a standard assumption on the error term to establish concentration results
(
71
)
.
We are now ready to state the following theorem.

Theorem 2

(In-sample prediction optimality of REML-guided early stopping)
.

Suppose
n^{a}\sigma_{\varepsilon,n}^{2}\to\infty
for some
a\in(0,1)
,
\sigma_{\varepsilon,n}^{2}
is uniformly bounded above, and
\lambda_{n}>0
. Under the model in (
1
) and Conditions
1
–
4
, we have

\frac{\mathcal{E}_{n}(\widehat{t}_{n}^{\rm REML})}{\inf_{t\geq 0}\mathcal{E}_{n}(t)}\to 1,

in probability as
n\to\infty
.

Corollary 1

(Out-of-sample prediction optimality of REML-guided early stopping)
.

Let

\mathcal{E}_{n}^{*}(t)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\{y_{\rm new}-\widehat{f}^{{\bm{H}}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{\rm new})\right\}^{2}\right]

be the out-of-sample prediction risk under the random-design setting, where the expectation is
taken over an independent test observation and the training data. Here

y_{\rm new}=f_{\star}({\bm{x}}_{\rm new})+\varepsilon_{\rm new}
, and

{\bm{x}}_{\rm new}
and
\varepsilon_{\rm new}
are independent of
{\bm{y}}
and
{\bm{X}}
and have the same
distributions as
{\bm{x}}_{i}
and
\varepsilon_{i}
, respectively.

Assume that the conditions of Theorem
2
hold and

\sqrt{n}\sigma_{\varepsilon,n}^{2}\to\infty
. In addition, assume that
h(\cdot,\cdot)
is a bounded
Mercer kernel

(
48
;
61
)
,
\mathbb{E}\{f_{0}({\bm{x}})^{4}\}<\infty
, and regularity conditions
S5
in the Supplementary Materials
hold. Then, the REML-guided stopping time
\widehat{t}_{n}^{\rm REML}
is asymptotically optimal
for out-of-sample prediction:

\frac{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{*}(\widehat{t}_{n}^{\rm REML})}{\inf_{t\geq 0}\mathcal{E}_{n}^{*}(t)}\to 1,

in probability as
n\to\infty
.

Proofs of Theorem
2
and Corollary
1

can be found in Supplementary Materials. Theorem
2
establishes
the in-sample prediction optimality of the REML-guided early stopping time, whereas
Corollary
1
extends this guarantee to the random-design
out-of-sample prediction risk. Thus,
\widehat{t}_{n}^{\rm REML}
achieves the same
first-order prediction risk as the oracle stopping time not only on the training design
points, but also for an independently sampled test point.

Notably, although
\widehat{t}_{n}^{\rm REML}
is motivated by the Gaussian likelihood,
Theorem
2
and Corollary
1
do not
require the error term
\varepsilon_{i}
to be Gaussian. In addition, these results allow
the variance of the error
\sigma_{\varepsilon,n}^{2}
to either stay constant or go to
zero as
n\to\infty
. The variance
\sigma_{\varepsilon,n}^{2}
stays constant in the
standard regression setting, while the small-noise regime can be a suitable approximation
to scenarios where the relationship between the response
y_{i}
and the feature
{\bm{x}}_{i}

is nearly deterministic
(
41
)
, which is commonly encountered in image
classification and semantic recognition
(
36
;
14
)
. Under the
small-noise regime, the denominator of the ratios

\mathcal{E}_{n}(\widehat{t}_{n}^{\rm REML})/\inf_{t\geq 0}\mathcal{E}_{n}(t)
and

\mathcal{E}_{n}^{*}(\widehat{t}_{n}^{\rm REML})/\inf_{t\geq 0}\mathcal{E}_{n}^{*}(t)

can go to zero, while the ratios themselves still converge to one. This implies that the
REML-guided early stopping time can fully exploit the nearly deterministic relationship
and achieve prediction risks approaching zero in both the in-sample and out-of-sample
settings.

Remark 7
.

In the canonical NTK case, all results in Sections
3.2
-
3.5
apply by setting
{\bm{H}}={\bm{H}}_{\infty}
, where
{\bm{H}}_{\infty}
is the infinite-width NTK matrix on the training sample. The eigenvalues, eigendirections, empirical spectral covariance, effective degrees of freedom, score test, and REML-guided stopping time are then interpreted with respect to the NTK operator. Thus, deep learning training in the NTK regime is an important special case of the general fixed-operator random-effects inference theory developed above. We use this special case in the numerical experiments and UK Biobank application below to illustrate the proposed hypothesis testing and REML-guided early stopping procedures.

6
Discussion

The central message of this work is that fixed-operator gradient-flow training admits an optimization–inference duality. Algorithmically, gradient flow is a trajectory that reduces empirical loss. Statistically, under squared-error dynamics with a fixed positive semidefinite training operator
{\bm{H}}
, the same trajectory is a random-effects inference path: the fitted predictor is a BLUP, or empirical Bayes posterior mean, and training time acts as a variance-component parameter controlling signal extraction and model complexity. The NTK regime, obtained by taking
{\bm{H}}={\bm{H}}_{\infty}
, provides a canonical deep-learning instantiation, but the underlying representation is not specific to NTK models.

This perspective contributes to the broader effort to understand deep learning models as trained predictive procedures, rather than only as function classes or final estimators. Recent work has emphasized that deep learning theory should explain the mechanics of the learning process itself, including training dynamics, limiting regimes, model complexity, and predictive behavior
(
64
)
. The present work provides a statistical inference component of this program by showing that fixed-operator gradient flow can be interpreted as an inferential path in which training reallocates variation from residual noise to structured signal.

A direct implication is that two basic training decisions become inferential questions. First, whether to train can be tested through a variance-component score test: under the random-effects representation, the null hypothesis of no useful training corresponds to the absence of the training-induced random effect. Second, conditional on evidence that training is beneficial, the stopping time can be estimated by REML. Thus, early stopping is no longer only a computational or validation-based tuning device
(
52
;
78
;
27
)
; it becomes estimation of a variance-component parameter governing the complexity of the trained predictor.

From an empirical Bayes viewpoint
(
22
)
, the proposed procedure estimates the prior covariance of the latent training-induced signal from the data and then uses the corresponding posterior mean as the trained predictor. This interpretation clarifies the practical advantages of REML-guided early stopping. The stopping time is estimated directly from the random-effects structure induced by the training operator, rather than selected retrospectively through repeated checkpoint evaluation. The procedure also preserves the full sample for model fitting instead of reserving part of the data for validation, which can be important in moderate-sample or low-signal settings. In addition, because the stopping time is inferred within a unified statistical model, the resulting procedure provides an interpretable characterization of training duration through variance allocation, spectral regularization, and effective degrees of freedom. The BLUP representation also suggests a route toward predictive inference along the training path, since the working random-effects model provides both a conditional mean and a conditional covariance for the training-induced signal. Developing calibrated prediction intervals for new inputs, especially under model misspecification and finite-width or discrete-time training dynamics, is an important direction for future work.

The random-effects representation also clarifies the spectral mechanism of early stopping. Along a fixed-operator gradient-flow path, residuals decay exponentially in the eigendirections of
{\bm{H}}
. Larger-eigenvalue directions are fitted earlier, whereas smaller-eigenvalue directions retain more of their initial residual until later in training. The REML estimating equation selects the training time at which the optimized spectral losses become empirically decorrelated from the eigenvalues of the training operator. Proposition
2
further shows the converse side of the optimization–inference duality: the BLUP arising from the random-effects model is equivalently the solution to a spectral regularization problem. This spectral loss-decorrelation condition provides an interpretable stopping principle: before this point, dominant eigendirections remain underfit; after this point, further training increasingly targets lower-eigenvalue directions that are more likely to contain noise. The same spectral structure yields a natural effective degrees-of-freedom measure, since the fitted trajectory is a linear smoother of the initial residual with smoother matrix
{\bm{I}}-\exp(-t{\bm{H}})
. Thus, training duration, spectral regularization, and effective model complexity are connected within a single statistical framework. In the NTK case, these eigendirections, eigenvalues, and degrees of freedom are those of
{\bm{H}}_{\infty}
.

This spectral perspective also distinguishes the proposed framework from classical kernel ridge regression and kernel-machine mixed models
(
43
)
. In those settings, regularization is typically imposed statically through an explicit ridge, smoothing, or variance-ratio parameter. By contrast, early-stopped gradient flow generates regularization dynamically through the training trajectory itself. The exponential covariance structure
\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}\{\exp(t{\bm{H}})-{\bm{I}}\}
is therefore not an arbitrary modeling choice; it is the covariance structure needed for the BLUP to reproduce the gradient-flow shrinkage induced by the residual-decay operator. This dynamic covariance is the basis for the BLUP representation, the variance-component test, the REML stopping rule, and the spectral interpretation of model complexity. From this perspective, over-parameterization itself does not imply uncontrolled complexity; rather, realized complexity depends on how long the training dynamics are allowed to activate spectral directions of the training operator.

Although the REML criterion is motivated by a Gaussian working random-effects model, the prediction optimality results do not require Gaussian errors or correct specification of that working model. Under regularity conditions, the REML-guided stopping time achieves asymptotically optimal fixed-design in-sample prediction risk relative to the oracle stopping time. Under additional random-design and kernel regularity conditions, the same stopping rule also achieves asymptotically optimal out-of-sample prediction risk for an independently sampled test point. These results support the use of the random-effects representation as a working inferential model whose induced stopping rule remains prediction-optimal under broader data-generating mechanisms.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the exact equivalence developed in this paper relies on a fixed training operator and continuous-time squared-error gradient flow. In the deep learning setting, this corresponds most directly to the infinite-width fixed-kernel regime, where the tangent operator remains constant during training and the network behaves like its linearization around initialization
(
31
;
38
;
45
;
3
;
40
;
72
)
. Although this regime provides a tractable and widely used theoretical model for over-parameterized neural networks, finite-width networks trained with discrete-time or stochastic optimization may deviate from this idealized setting. In particular, they may exhibit representation learning, whereby internal features and the induced tangent operator evolve during training rather than remaining fixed
(
15
;
73
;
74
;
53
)
. The operator formulation in (
4
) suggests a natural extension to such time-varying dynamics: the relevant object would be the cumulative training operator, summarized by
\int_{0}^{t}{\bm{H}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s
or its averaged version
\bar{{\bm{H}}}(t)
. Conceptually, this would replace the exponential covariance induced by
t{\bm{H}}
with a covariance induced by the full evolution operator of the time-varying dynamics. Mean-field analyses provide one possible route
(
49
)
, as they describe training through the evolution of the parameter distribution and can view the resulting dynamics as kernel boosting with a time-varying, data-dependent kernel
(
47
;
15
)
. Another important direction is to extend the present inference framework beyond first-order gradient flow to accelerated and momentum-based optimization dynamics
(
66
)
.

Second, the current theory focuses on squared-error loss. Extending the framework to classification and other generalized response models will require new analysis because the loss geometry, likelihood structure, and induced covariance dynamics differ from the squared-error case. Recent work on infinitely wide and deep neural networks further shows that classification can exhibit behavior qualitatively different from regression, including Bayes consistency through singular-kernel limits under suitable activation functions
(
54
)
. Nevertheless, the random-effects viewpoint suggests a natural path forward through generalized linear mixed-model analogues and variance-component inference for non-Gaussian outcomes.

A related computational issue concerns the construction and use of the training operator in large-scale applications. This issue is especially relevant for biobank-scale prediction, which is the primary application focus of this paper. The exact theory is stated for a fixed positive semidefinite operator
{\bm{H}}
on the training sample, but forming and decomposing a dense sample-by-sample operator may be computationally demanding in large cohorts such as the UK Biobank. Several computational strategies may be used to improve scalability. First, a useful analogy comes from genetic-relationship-matrix (GRM)-based mixed models in statistical genetics, where cohort-scale REML and variance-component analyses are made feasible through sparse relationship matrices, iterative solvers, blockwise computation, and parallel analyses across many traits, rather than repeated dense matrix factorizations
(
77
;
44
;
79
;
32
;
46
)
. Similar ideas can be adopted in the present setting by sparsifying or approximating
{\bm{H}}
, computing matrix–vector products without explicitly storing the full operator, using randomized eigensolvers to extract leading spectral components, and reusing the same estimated operator across multiple traits or phenotypes. Second, in larger modern-AI settings such as language-model fine-tuning, reduced fixed operators may be constructed from tangent features restricted to Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) or adapter parameters, random projections of gradient features, kernel sketches based on subsamples, or Jacobian-free NTK approximations
(
45
;
3
;
72
;
40
)
. Third, these strategies are closely connected to classical scalable kernel approximations, including randomized Nyström approximation, leverage-score and projection-based sketches, fast-transform landmark constructions, and memory-efficient block–low-rank approximations
(
20
;
26
;
62
;
63
;
39
)
. These approaches are compatible with the proposed theory whenever they yield a fixed positive semidefinite training operator, or a controlled fixed approximation thereof, on the analyzed sample. Formalizing the statistical effects of operator sparsification, sketching, and mild operator evolution along the training path is an important direction for future work.

More broadly, this formulation points to a statistical agenda for understanding training dynamics. Training time is only one hyperparameter governing the trajectory of a predictive model. Other quantities, including learning rate, weight decay, initialization scale, architecture, batch size, and data augmentation, may also regulate variance allocation, spectral shrinkage, or effective model complexity. Developing inferential tools for these quantities could lead to more principled and interpretable model-selection procedures for modern machine learning.

In summary, the proposed random-effects representation provides a bridge between gradient-flow optimization and classical statistical inference. It yields a formal test for training necessity, a REML-guided rule for early stopping, a spectral interpretation of model complexity, and asymptotic optimality guarantees for prediction. Deep learning models in fixed-kernel and NTK regimes provide canonical modern-AI instantiations of this theory, but the underlying optimization–inference duality is a broader principle of fixed-operator squared-error gradient flow.

Appendix S1
Proof of Theorem
1

Throughout the proof, we use
c
and
C
to denote generic positive constants whose values may differ from place to place.

Let
\bm{r}_{0}={\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}})
. By the fixed-operator gradient-flow solution in (
5
), we have

\widehat{f}_{t}^{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{X}})=f_{0}({\bm{X}})+\{\bm{I}-\exp(-t{\bm{H}})\}\bm{r}_{0}.

We now show that the BLUP under the working random-effects model (
7
)
gives the same expression. Under (
7
), we have

\bm{r}_{0}=\bm{u}_{t}+\bm{\varepsilon}_{t},

where

\mathsf{var}(\bm{u}_{t})=\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}\{\exp(t{\bm{H}})-\bm{I}\},\qquad\mathsf{var}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{t})=\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}\bm{I},\qquad\bm{u}_{t}\perp\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}.

Therefore,

\mathsf{var}(\bm{r}_{0})=\mathsf{var}(\bm{u}_{t})+\mathsf{var}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{t})=\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}\exp(t{\bm{H}}).

Although
\exp(t{\bm{H}})-\bm{I}
may be singular when
{\bm{H}}
is only positive semidefinite,

\exp(t{\bm{H}})
is positive definite for every
t\geq 0
, since the eigenvalues of

\exp(t{\bm{H}})
are
\exp(t\lambda_{k})>0
. Hence the BLUP of
\bm{u}_{t}
is

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{u}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Cov}(\bm{u}_{t},\bm{r}_{0})\{\mathsf{var}(\bm{r}_{0})\}^{-1}\bm{r}_{0}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}\{\exp(t{\bm{H}})-\bm{I}\}\left\{\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}\exp(t{\bm{H}})\right\}^{-1}\bm{r}_{0}

\displaystyle=\{\bm{I}-\exp(-t{\bm{H}})\}\bm{r}_{0}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle f_{0}({\bm{X}})+\widehat{\bm{u}}_{t}

\displaystyle=f_{0}({\bm{X}})+\{\bm{I}-\exp(-t{\bm{H}})\}\{{\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}})\}

\displaystyle=\widehat{f}_{t}^{{\bm{H}}}({\bm{X}}),

which proves the equivalence. The above proof is valid for any positive
\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}
and we adopt
\sigma_{\varepsilon,t}^{2}=\gamma_{t}^{-1}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}
to ensure the initial risk
n^{-1}\mathbb{E}[\|{\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}})\|^{2}]
does not depend on
t
under the random effect model.

Appendix S2
Proof of Proposition
1

First, we have proven that
Q(t)
is convex on
[0,\infty)
. Because
\varepsilon_{i}
is a continuous random variable for
i=1,\dots,n
,
c_{k}\neq 0
for all
k=1,\dots,n
with probability one. Under condition (ii) in Proposition
1
, the support of the probability weights
p_{k}(t)
contains at least two distinct eigenvalues for every finite
t\geq 0
. Hence

Q^{\prime\prime}(t)=n\,\mathsf{var}_{p_{t}}(\lambda)>0,\qquad t\geq 0,

so
Q(t)
is strictly convex on
[0,\infty)
. Therefore
Q^{\prime}(t)
is strictly increasing, and any solution to
Q^{\prime}(t)=0
is unique.

It remains to show the existence of a root. Condition (i) gives
Q^{\prime}(0)<0
. Next, as
t\to\infty
, for every
k\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
,

p_{k}(t)=\frac{c_{k}^{2}\exp(-t\lambda_{k})}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}^{2}\exp(-t\lambda_{j})}

concentrates on the minimum eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}
. Consequently,

\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}(t)\lambda_{k}\longrightarrow\lambda_{n},\qquad t\to\infty.

Therefore,

Q^{\prime}(t)\longrightarrow n(\bar{\lambda}-\lambda_{n}),\qquad t\to\infty.

Condition (ii) implies that this limit is strictly positive. Since
Q^{\prime}(t)
is continuous on
[0,\infty)
, there exists at least one
t\in(0,\infty)
such that
Q^{\prime}(t)=0
. By strict convexity, this root is unique. The equivalence between
Q^{\prime}(t)=0
follows from (
10
). Since
Q(t)
is strictly convex, this unique critical point is also the unique minimizer of
Q(t)
over
[0,\infty)
.

Appendix S3
Proof of Proposition
2

Consider the proposed regularization objective function:

\mathcal{J}(\bm{u})=\|{\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}})-\bm{u}\|^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\rho_{k}(t)(\bm{v}_{k}^{\top}\bm{u})^{2},

(S1)

where
\rho_{k}(t)=\frac{\exp(-t\lambda_{k})}{1-\exp(-t\lambda_{k})}
. Let
c_{k}=\bm{v}_{k}^{\top}({\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}}))
and
u_{k}=\bm{v}_{k}^{\top}\bm{u}
be the projections of the initial residual and the optimization variable onto the eigenvector
\bm{v}_{k}
, respectively. Since the eigenvectors
\{\bm{v}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
form an orthonormal basis, we can rewrite the Euclidean norm in the eigenbasis:

\|{\bm{y}}-f_{0}({\bm{X}})-\bm{u}\|^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(c_{k}-u_{k})^{2}.

Substituting this into (
S1
), the objective function decouples into a sum of independent component-wise problems:

\mathcal{J}(\bm{u})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[(c_{k}-u_{k})^{2}+\rho_{k}(t)u_{k}^{2}\right].

To find the minimizer
\widehat{\bm{u}}_{t}
, we take the partial derivative with respect to each component
u_{k}
and set it to zero:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial u_{k}}=-2(c_{k}-u_{k})+2\rho_{k}(t)u_{k}=0\implies(1+\rho_{k}(t))u_{k}=c_{k}.

Solving for
u_{k}
, we obtain:

\widehat{u}_{k,t}=\frac{c_{k}}{1+\rho_{k}(t)}.

Substituting the definition of
\rho_{k}(t)
:

1+\rho_{k}(t)=1+\frac{\exp(-t\lambda_{k})}{1-\exp(-t\lambda_{k})}=\frac{1-\exp(-t\lambda_{k})+\exp(-t\lambda_{k})}{1-\exp(-t\lambda_{k})}=\frac{1}{1-\exp(-t\lambda_{k})}.

Therefore, the optimal solution component is:

\widehat{u}_{k,t}=c_{k}(1-\exp(-t\lambda_{k})).

This is exactly the projection of the fixed-operator gradient flow solution
\widehat{f}^{{\bm{H}}}_{t}({\bm{X}})-f_{0}({\bm{X}})
onto the
k
-th eigenvector. Thus,
\widehat{\bm{u}}_{t}
corresponds precisely to the fixed-operator gradient flow solution at time
t
.

Then, we evaluate the objective function
\mathcal{J}(\bm{u})
at the optimal solution
\widehat{\bm{u}}_{t}
. Using the relation
c_{k}-\widehat{u}_{k,t}=\rho_{k}(t)\widehat{u}_{k,t}
derived from the first-order condition, the term for the
k
-th component becomes:

\displaystyle\text{Loss}_{k}

\displaystyle=(c_{k}-\widehat{u}_{k,t})^{2}+\rho_{k}(t)\widehat{u}_{k,t}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\rho_{k}(t)\widehat{u}_{k,t})^{2}+\rho_{k}(t)\widehat{u}_{k,t}^{2}

\displaystyle=\rho_{k}(t)(1+\rho_{k}(t))\widehat{u}_{k,t}^{2}.

Substituting
\widehat{u}_{k,t}=\frac{c_{k}}{1+\rho_{k}(t)}
, we have:

\text{Loss}_{k}=\rho_{k}(t)(1+\rho_{k}(t))\left(\frac{c_{k}}{1+\rho_{k}(t)}\right)^{2}=c_{k}^{2}\frac{\rho_{k}(t)}{1+\rho_{k}(t)}.

Recalling that
\frac{\rho_{k}(t)}{1+\rho_{k}(t)}=\exp(-t\lambda_{k})
, the component-wise minimized loss simplifies to:

\text{Loss}_{k}=c_{k}^{2}\exp(-t\lambda_{k}).

Summing over all components, the total minimized regularized loss is:

\mathcal{J}(\widehat{\bm{u}}_{t})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}^{2}\exp(-t\lambda_{k}).

Condition

implies that

are uniformly sub-Gaussian. Applying the Hanson–Wright inequality

For the linear term,

for some

Combining the two bounds yields

\sigma_{\varepsilon,n}^{2}\leq\inf_{t\geq 0}\mathcal{E}_{n}^{*}(t)\leq\mathcal{E}_{n}(\widehat{t}_{n}^{\rm REML})=\sigma_{\varepsilon,n}^{2}\{1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\}
. We have

\frac{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{*}(\widehat{t}_{n}^{\rm REML})}{\inf_{t\geq 0}\mathcal{E}_{n}^{*}(t)}=\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon,n}^{2}\{1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon,n}^{2}\{1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\}}\to 1

in probability. This proves Corollary
1
.
∎
