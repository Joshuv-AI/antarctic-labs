Federated LoRA Fine-Tuning for LLMs via Collaborative Alignment
Shuaida He, Liwen Chen, Long Feng

Abstract
Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) has emerged as a powerful tool for parameter-efficient fine-tuning of large language models (LLMs). This paper studies LoRA under a federated learning setting, enabling collaborative fine-tuning across clients while preserving parameter efficiency. We focus on a highly heterogeneous regime in which clients share only partial structure and a substantial subset may be contaminated. We propose Collaborative Low-rank Alignment and Identifiable Recovery (CLAIR), a contamination-aware framework that relies only on preliminary local estimators. Its formulation applies broadly, from linear regression to neural network and LLM modules, whenever local adaptation can be represented by matrix-valued updates. CLAIR recovers the shared LoRA subspace and detects contaminated clients via a structured low-rank plus block-sparse decomposition. We prove exact recovery of the shared LoRA subspace in the noiseless case, stable recovery under preliminary estimation error, and consistent collaborative-set recovery under mild separation conditions. We further quantify the gain from CLAIR refinement: it reduces off-subspace estimation error through cross-client averaging while preserving client-specific variation within the shared LoRA subspace, thus improves over local fine-tuning whenever this oracle gain outweighs the costs of subspace estimation and benign-client heterogeneity. Empirically, we demonstrate the benefits of CLAIR by fine-tuning a Transformer architecture on a text-copying task. The results show accurate contamination detection and improved benign-client performance compared with local fine-tuning and non-robust federated averaging.



Keywords:
Low-rank representations; multi-task learning; robust aggregation; federated learning; parameter-efficient fine-tuning.

1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT
[
39
]
, Gemini
[
9
]
, and Llama
[
14
]
, have become central to modern artificial intelligence, achieving strong performance in text generation, reasoning, and decision-making
[
21
]
. Built on Transformer architectures with attention mechanisms
[
34
,
27
]
,
these models often contain millions to billions of parameters and are pretrained on massive data to learn general-purpose representations.
In contrast, local downstream adaptation typically targets narrower tasks or domains and relies on substantially smaller samples.
Full parameter fine-tuning is then computationally expensive and statistically inefficient for individual clients or organizations.
This motivates parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT), which freezes most pretrained weights and updates only a small set of task-specific parameters
[
18
,
41
]
.

Low-Rank Adaptation
[
20
, LoRA;]
is a prominent example of this principle. For a pretrained weight matrix
\mathbf{W}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p}
, LoRA parameterizes the adapted weight as

\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\Delta},\quad\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A},

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
is a low-rank update, or adapter, with
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}
,
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times p}
, and
r\ll\min(p,q)
.
By representing adaptation through low-dimensional factors, LoRA greatly reduces the number of trainable parameters while retaining substantial flexibility. The resulting adapters are compact, easy to store and transfer, and can be switched across tasks while sharing the same pretrained backbone
\mathbf{W}_{0}
.
This makes LoRA well-suited to efficient fine-tuning on small local datasets, and particularly useful in multi-task and multi-client settings.
Consequently, LoRA has become a standard approach for adapting large pretrained models and has been widely used across a broad range of downstream applications
[
18
,
24
]
.

Although LoRA was introduced as an engineering device for PEFT, it reflects a broader statistical principle: exploiting intrinsic structures, such as low-rankness, to improve statistical efficiency.
This principle underlies classical methods such as matrix completion
[
5
,
22
]
, principal component analysis (PCA) and robust PCA
[
4
]
, reduced-rank regression
[
42
]
, mixture models
[
10
]
, and tensor regression
[
44
]
.
At the same time, the factorized formulation of LoRA provides a flexible model for shared structure across related learning problems, connecting naturally to representation based multi-task learning, in which multiple tasks share a latent representation while retaining task-specific coefficients
[
11
,
26
,
32
]
; fine-tuning-based meta-learning
[
8
]
; and linear model transfer learning, where a target problem borrows structure from related source problems
[
15
]
.

Low-rank adaptation can benefit substantially from collaboration across groups or clients through federated learning
[
25
, FL, ]
, which mitigates local data scarcity by pooling information at the model level. Federated learning enables collaborative model improvement while preserving data governance, as each client trains locally on private data and shares only model updates or parameters.
Combining LoRA with federated aggregation methods such as FedAvg has shown broad applicability across downstream tasks
[
43
,
2
]
.
This paradigm is particularly attractive in sensitive or regulated domains, such as healthcare, where LLMs could be customized to analyze institution-specific medical text while complying with strict privacy requirements.

Federated deployment of LoRA raises several issues beyond the centralized PEFT.
The first is to determine which object should be shared across clients.
Specifically, for client
k
, write the locally adapted weight as
\mathbf{W}^{(k)}=\mathbf{W}_{0}+\mathbf{B}^{(k)}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}
.
Then, in centralized LoRA, only the refined estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}
is of primary interest.
In a federated system, however, aggregation must identify the component of
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}
that is transferable across clients.
For example, product-level methods construct a stacked adapter whose product represents a weighted average of the client updates
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(k)}
, thereby treating the full adapters as directly comparable matrices
[
38
]
.
Factor-level methods impose stronger structural alignment, typically by sharing, fixing, or selectively aggregating one LoRA factor, either
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}
or
\mathbf{B}^{(k)}

[
30
,
31
,
16
]
.
Expert-level methods allocate domain-specific LoRA experts and allow clients select subsets through an adaptive mixture-of-experts (MoE) mechanism, so that the transferable units are selected expert adapters
[
36
]
.
Despite their empirical success, there remains limited theoretical understanding of which shared structure is identifiable and why its aggregation, even in basic FedAvg-type procedures, improves estimation.
This motivates a formal treatment of the shared LoRA component as the collaborative structure underlying federated fine-tuning.

The second challenge is to handle statistical heterogeneity across clients.
Heterogeneity, arising from distributional shifts, task differences, or other sources, determines whether collaboration is beneficial: when clients are sufficiently aligned, methods such as FedAvg can improve efficiency, whereas under severe heterogeneity, purely local training may be preferable
[
7
]
.
This difficulty is compounded by the decentralized nature of federated learning, where some clients may be outlying, weakly related, or even adversarial
[
23
]
.
We refer to such clients as contaminated, in contrast to benign clients whose LoRA adapters share a common structure and can benefit from collaborative refinement.
In practice, even a small contaminated fraction can distort the estimated shared structure and induce negative transfer for benign clients
[
17
,
36
]
.
Thus, the central inferential task is to separate benign variation around the shared low-rank adapter structure from contamination that violates it. We formalize this task as collaborative-set recovery: adaptively identifying the benign clients for collaboration while excluding contaminated ones.

Moreover, existing federated LoRA methods typically require client updates to be represented relative to the same backbone and parameterization. This assumption is natural when the server controls a shared open backbone
\mathbf{W}_{0}
, or when clients report adapters
\{\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
relative to a common checkpoint. However, direct adapter-level aggregation is not readily applicable when the server observes only locally adapted weights or preliminary local estimators
\{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, without access to the underlying base model or the corresponding adapter decompositions.
This issue is particularly relevant when clients are initialized from similar but non-identical base models within a common parameter space, since LoRA adapters are tied to their underlying backbones
[
37
,
28
,
12
]
.
Therefore, (
1
) should be viewed as an ideal population representation rather than an observed decomposition, raising a basic identifiability question: when neither the base model nor the adapters is separately observed, what shared low-rank structure is identifiable from preliminary local estimators alone?

To address these issues, we propose a contamination-aware framework for collaborative federated fine-tuning of LLMs via low-rank adaptation. The framework accommodates settings where (i) the underlying foundation model
\mathbf{W}_{0}
is unknown or inaccessible, (ii) model structure is only partially shared across clients, and (iii) client heterogeneity and contamination are present, with the contaminated subset allowed to grow with the number of clients.
Its formulation applies broadly, from linear regression to neural network and LLM modules, whenever local adaptation can be represented by matrix-valued updates.

Conceptually, our framework converts federated LoRA fine-tuning into a structured decomposition problem, whose identifiable components encode transferable local knowledge and the client contamination pattern. This is achieved through a pairwise contrast construction that cancels the unobserved backbone while preserving client-pair information.
The resulting decomposition takes a low-rank plus block-sparse form, which resembles robust PCA
[
4
,
19
]
. On the other hand, its geometry differs fundamentally due to the constraints induced by the federated LoRA formulation.
Consequently, classical exact-recovery arguments do not apply directly. We address this challenge with a tractable convex program followed by subspace projection, which simultaneously recovers the shared structure and filters out contaminated clients, providing the basis for client-specific collaborative refinement.

Theoretically, we establish exact and stable recovery guarantees for the shared structure and prove consistency of contaminated client detection under suitable identifiability conditions.
A central contribution is an explicit characterization of when collaborative fine-tuning improves upon purely local estimation, together with a quantification of the resulting mean-squared error gain.
In contrast to existing empirical studies on federated LoRA aggregation
[
38
,
16
,
2
]
, our analysis identifies the source of client-level improvement: the refined estimator preserves the local-specific error in the shared adapter row space, while reducing the orthogonal-complement error through averaging over collaborating benign clients. This yields a transparent gain-to-cost condition under which collaborative fine-tuning outperforms local fine-tuning, and also clarifies when negative transfer may occur.

Empirically, we evaluate CLAIR in a controlled sequence-copying experiment with Transformer models locally fine-tuned via LoRA. The experiment illustrates the two statistical roles characterized by the theory: detecting a contaminated client through contrasts among locally adapted models, and improving prediction accuracy for benign clients relative to local fine-tuning and non-robust FedAvg baselines.

,

2
Problem setup

Consider regression models distributed across
K
data clients.
For client
k\in[K]
, the model is specified as

\mathbf{y}^{(k)}=f^{(k)}\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(k)};\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\bigr),

(2)

where
\mathbf{y}^{(k)}
is the response vector,
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}
the covariate vector,
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(k)}
the random noise,
f^{(k)}
a general (linear or nonlinear) function, and
\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p}
the target parameter matrix. We assume

\mathbf{W}^{(k)}=\mathbf{W}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(k)},\quad\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(k)}=\mathbf{B}^{(k)}\mathbf{A}^{(k)},

(3)

where
\mathbf{W}_{0}
is an unknown global base model shared across clients, and
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(k)}
captures client-specific heterogeneity.
Here,
\mathbf{B}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}
,
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times p}
, and rank
r<\min(p,q)
. By constraining
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(k)}
to be low-rank, model (
2
) together with (
3
) naturally leads to a federated LoRA formulation. With only the decomposition in (
3
), the pair
(\mathbf{W}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(k)})
is clearly non-identifiable. However, our aim is not to identify
(\mathbf{W}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(k)})
, but rather to refine a local estimator of
\mathbf{W}^{(k)}
and obtain an improved one, as discussed in detail later.

With a suitable choice of
f^{(k)}
, model (
2
) is highly expressive and covers a broad class of settings, including linear regression, multiple-index models with nonlinear links, and neural networks with additional trainable parameters beyond
\mathbf{W}^{(k)}
.
In particular, in modern LLM fine-tuning,
\mathbf{W}^{(k)}
denotes a locally adapted module for client
k
, obtained from the pretrained backbone
\mathbf{W}_{0}
via a low-rank adapter
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(k)}
.
Such modules typically include transformer weights, such as the query, key, and value projections in self-attention
[
34
]
.
The heterogeneity in
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(k)}
reflects differences in datasets, downstream tasks, or reasoning objectives, etc.

In federated learning, each client reports a local estimator of
\mathbf{W}^{(k)}
based on its private dataset
\mathcal{D}^{(k)}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(k)},\mathbf{y}_{i}^{(k)})\}_{i=1}^{n_{k}}
.
We call the resulting estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}
the preliminary estimator for client
k
.
It may be obtained either by local full training, such as stochastic gradient descent with a client-specific loss, or by fine-tuning from
\mathbf{W}_{0}
through estimation of
\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{(k)}
.
Since
\mathbf{W}_{0}
is often inaccessible or unidentifiable in practice, and the shared row space
\operatorname{row}(\mathbf{A})
is also unknown, our subsequent procedure relies only on the collection of preliminary estimators
\{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
.

Our goal is to develop a unified framework for collaborative fine-tuning under the federated LoRA paradigm, without imposing structural assumptions on
f^{(k)}
or requiring access to
\mathbf{W}_{0}
, and to quantify the statistical gain of the resulting estimators of
\{\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
. A natural question is therefore: which clients can collaborate, and what shared information yields mutual benefit?

We answer this question by introducing the collaborative set of benign clients.
Formally, assume there exist a subset of clients
\mathcal{C}\subseteq[K]
and a matrix
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\in\mathbb{O}_{p,r}
such that, for some small
\delta>0
,

\max_{k\in\mathcal{C}}\ \min_{\mathbf{O}\in\mathbb{O}_{r,r}}\|\mathbf{O}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}-\mathbf{A}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}\leq\delta.

This formulation accounts for the rotational non-identifiability of LoRA factorization. Indeed, for any
\mathbf{O}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{O}_{r,r}
,
\mathbf{B}^{(k)}\mathbf{A}^{(k)}=(\mathbf{B}^{(k)}\mathbf{O}^{(k)})(\mathbf{O}^{(k),\top}\mathbf{A}^{(k)})
, so
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}
is identifiable only through its row space.
We may therefore assume, without loss of generality, that
\mathbf{A}^{(k),\top}\in\mathbb{O}_{p,r}
.
For each
k\in\mathcal{C}
, after rotating
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}
by an optimizer and reusing the same notation, the preceding condition becomes

\max_{k\in\mathcal{C}}\|\mathbf{A}^{(k)}-\mathbf{A}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}\leq\delta.

(4)

Thus, clients in
\mathcal{C}
have adapter row spaces uniformly close to the common row space
\operatorname{row}(\mathbf{A})
, whereas clients in
\mathcal{C}^{c}:=[K]\setminus\mathcal{C}
are treated as contaminated.
The set
\mathcal{C}
therefore identifies the clients that share a compatible adapter structure and can benefit from collaborative estimation.
We write
\eta:=|\mathcal{C}|/K
as the collaborative proportion, with larger
\eta
indicating stronger cross-client homogeneity in the federated network.

Remark 1

(Interpretation of the shared LoRA factor)
.

The shared row space condition (
4
) places the collaborative structure in the right LoRA factor
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}
. This choice is consistent with prior federated LoRA formulations
[
16
]
and supported by empirical evidence on adapter sharing
[
36
]
, and has a natural representation-learning interpretation.
Specifically, consider a single-layer network with activation
\sigma
,

\mathbf{Y}^{(k)}=\sigma\{\mathbf{W}_{0}\mathbf{X}^{(k)}+\mathbf{B}^{(k)}(\mathbf{A}^{(k)}\mathbf{X}^{(k)})\},

where
\mathbf{X}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
denotes the input representations.
Then for a fixed LoRA factorization,
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}
maps the input to an
r
-dimensional adapter bottleneck, whereas
\mathbf{B}^{(k)}
maps this bottleneck to the output coordinates.
Thus, sharing the row space of
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}
amounts to sharing an input-side representation, while allowing client-specific output coefficients.
When
q=1
and
\mathbf{W}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
, this includes representation-based multi-task learning as a special case
[
32
]
; when
q>1
, it gives a reduced-rank or multi-response regression structure
[
42
]
.
Related shared-representation and task-specific structures also appear in adaptive multi-task and transfer learning
[
8
,
11
]
.
A related input-output asymmetry appears in domain adaptation, where domain-specific feature maps may be combined with a shared or nearly shared classifier or output head
[
3
,
33
]
.
This provides an analogue of a formulation based on shared left factors
\mathbf{B}^{(k)}
, although our main analysis focuses on the shared right-factor row space in (
4
); the dual formulation can be handled analogously.

3
Methodology

This section develops Collaborative Low-rank Alignment and Identifiable Recovery (CLAIR), a data-driven procedure for federated LoRA fine-tuning. CLAIR constructs a canonical matrix decomposition from pairwise differences of preliminary local estimators, thereby eliminating the common backbone and separating the shared row-space signal of benign clients from orthogonal contamination. This decomposition defines the estimands that underlie both active-set recovery and collaborative refinement.

3.1
The CLAIR procedure

We start by circumventing the need for accessing the shared global parameter
\mathbf{W}_{0}
while still leveraging the similar structure encoded in
\{\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
.
For distinct clients
j\neq k
, regard client
j
as a reference for client
k
.
For benign pairs
(j,k)\in\mathcal{C}
, we have the approximate relation

\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\approx\mathbf{W}^{(j)}-(\mathbf{B}^{(j)}-\mathbf{B}^{(k)})\mathbf{A},

where
\mathbf{A}
acts as the “center” of
\{\mathbf{A}_{j}:j\in\mathcal{C}\}
.
Consequently, instead of estimating the unidentified global parameter
\mathbf{W}_{0}
in (
2
), we refine
\mathbf{W}^{(k)}
by adapting the reference model
\mathbf{W}^{(j)}
with
(\mathbf{B}^{(j)}-\mathbf{B}^{(k)})\mathbf{A}
.
Aggregating such pairwise adaptations over multiple reference clients integrates local information and yields a refined estimator of
\mathbf{W}^{(k)}
.

To formalize this construction, let

\mathcal{G}:=\{(j,k)\in[K]\times[K]:1\leq j<k\leq K\}
denote the set of unordered client pairs, indexed by the convention
j<k
, with cardinality
G=|\mathcal{G}|=\binom{K}{2}
.
For
g=(j,k)\in\mathcal{G}
, we use
g
and
(j,k)
interchangeably to label the corresponding block, and define the pairwise difference of preliminary estimates by

\widehat{\mathbf{D}}^{(j,k)}:=\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p}.

(5)

Stacking all these blocks vertically in a fixed order yields the aggregated difference matrix

\widehat{\mathbf{D}}:=\bigl[\{\widehat{\mathbf{D}}^{(1,2)}\}^{\top},\ldots,\{\widehat{\mathbf{D}}^{(1,K)}\}^{\top},\{\widehat{\mathbf{D}}^{(2,3)}\}^{\top},\ldots,\{\widehat{\mathbf{D}}^{(K-1,K)}\}^{\top}\bigr]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{Gq\times p}.

(6)

This construction collects each pairwise contrast and preserves the client-pair indexing of each row block. The particular stacking order does not affect the subsequent analysis, and we fix one ordering of
\mathcal{G}
throughout.

Given
\widehat{\mathbf{D}}
, we solve the penalized convex program

(\widehat{\mathbf{L}},\widehat{\mathbf{S}})\ \in\ \arg\min_{\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\omega_{g}\|P_{g}(\widehat{\mathbf{D}}-\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{S})\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}+\lambda_{L}\|\mathbf{L}\|_{*}+\lambda_{S}\|\mathbf{S}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1},

(7)

where
\lambda_{L}>0
,
\lambda_{S}>0
are regularization parameters and
\omega_{g}>0
are block-specific weights.
While a detailed justification of (
7
) is deferred to the next subsection, we briefly note that
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}
estimates the low-rank shared signal, whereas
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}
captures blockwise sparse contamination.
We use
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}
for active-set identification and
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}
for collaborative fine-tuning.
Specifically, denote
the singular value decomposition (SVD) of
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}
and the corresponding projection onto the row space as

\widehat{\mathbf{L}}=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top},\quad P_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}:=\widehat{\mathbf{A}}\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}.

Here, we deliberately denote the right singular vectors of
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}
by
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}
rather than the conventional
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}
, to emphasize that
P_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
is an estimate of
P_{\mathbf{A}}
.
With
P_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\perp}}:=\mathbf{I}-P_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
, we further define the plug-in estimators

\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{\mathbf{A}}:=\widehat{\mathbf{D}}P_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}},\qquad\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}:=\widehat{\mathbf{D}}P_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\perp}}.

For distinct
j,k\in[K]
, write

P_{j,k}:=P_{(\min\{j,k\},\,\max\{j,k\})}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(j,k)}:=P_{j,k}(\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}})
,
so that
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(j,k)}
denotes the block associated with the unordered pair
\{j,k\}
.
The collaborative set is then estimated by

\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}:=\Bigl\{k\in[K]:\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j\neq k}{\mathrm{1}}\{\|\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(j,k)}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}\leq\tau_{n}\}\geq\alpha\Bigr\},

(8)

where tuning parameters
\tau_{n}\geq 0
and
\alpha\in[0.5,1)
.

For each
k\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}
, define the refined estimator

\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}|}\sum_{j\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}}\Bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{\mathbf{A}}^{(j,k)}\Bigr),

(9)

where, for all
j,k\in[K]
, the pairwise correction term
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{\mathbf{A}}^{(j,k)}
is defined antisymmetrically:
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{\mathbf{A}}^{(j,k)}=(\widehat{\mathbf{D}}P_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}})^{(j,k)}
if
j<k
,
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{\mathbf{A}}^{(j,k)}=-(\widehat{\mathbf{D}}P_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}})^{(k,j)}
if
j>k
, and
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{\mathbf{A}}^{(j,k)}=\mathbf{0}
if
j=k
.
Thus,
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{\mathbf{A}}^{(j,k)}
is obtained by correcting client
j
’s preliminary estimator toward client
k
.

Equation (
8
) estimates the collaborative set
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}
by a majority-voting rule. For each retained client, (
9
) transports the reference estimators toward the target client through the estimated shared component and averages these pairwise adaptations over
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}
.
The next subsection explains why this procedure targets the identifiable component of the federated LoRA signal.

3.2
Canonical estimands and oracle interpretation

We now introduce the latent population objects that motivate the preceding procedure.
For each
g=(j,k)\in\mathcal{G}
, define

\mathbf{D}^{(j,k)}:=\mathbf{W}^{(j)}-\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p}

and the stacked population contrast matrix

\mathbf{D}:=\left[\{\mathbf{D}^{(1,2)}\}^{\top},\ldots,\{\mathbf{D}^{(1,K)}\}^{\top},\{\mathbf{D}^{(2,3)}\}^{\top},\ldots\{\mathbf{D}^{(K-1,K)}\}^{\top}\right]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{Gq\times p}.

(10)

By direct algebra,

\mathbf{D}^{(j,k)}=(\mathbf{B}^{(j)}-\mathbf{B}^{(k)})\mathbf{A}+[\mathbf{B}^{(j)}(\mathbf{A}^{(j)}-\mathbf{A})-\mathbf{B}^{(k)}(\mathbf{A}^{(k)}-\mathbf{A})].

For each
g=(j,k)
, define

\mathbf{L}_{0}^{(j,k)}:=\begin{cases}(\mathbf{B}^{(j)}-\mathbf{B}^{(k)})\mathbf{A},&\text{if }j,k\in\mathcal{C},\\
\mathbf{0}_{q\times p},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\quad\mathbf{S}_{0}^{(j,k)}:=\begin{cases}\mathbf{0}_{q\times p},&\text{if }j,k\in\mathcal{C},\\
\mathbf{B}^{(j)}\mathbf{A}^{(j)}-\mathbf{B}^{(k)}\mathbf{A}^{(k)},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(11)

and

\mathbf{E}_{0}^{(j,k)}:=\begin{cases}\mathbf{B}^{(j)}(\mathbf{A}^{(j)}-\mathbf{A})-\mathbf{B}^{(k)}(\mathbf{A}^{(k)}-\mathbf{A}),&\text{if }j,k\in\mathcal{C},\\
\mathbf{0}_{q\times p},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Stacking blockwise yields the decomposition

\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{L}_{0}+\mathbf{S}_{0}+\mathbf{E}_{0},

where
\mathbf{L}_{0},\mathbf{S}_{0},\mathbf{E}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{Gq\times p}
are formed by vertically concatenating
\{\mathbf{L}_{0}^{(j,k)}\},\{\mathbf{S}_{0}^{(j,k)}\},\{\mathbf{E}_{0}^{(j,k)}\}
across
(j,k)\in\mathcal{G}
.

We analyze the structural roles of
\mathbf{L}_{0}
,
\mathbf{S}_{0}
, and
\mathbf{E}_{0}
separately.
First,
\mathbf{L}_{0}
is a low-rank matrix satisfying

\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{L}_{0})\leq r\ll\min\{Gq,p\}
as
\mathbf{A}
is of rank
r
.
Without loss of generality, we may further assume that

\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{L}_{0})=r.

(12)

This condition holds whenever the active clients exhibit sufficient heterogeneity in their left LoRA factors; for example, it is implied by the existence of a pair
(j_{0},k_{0})\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{B}^{(j_{0})}-\mathbf{B}^{(k_{0})})=r
.
Under (
12
), the row space of
\mathbf{L}_{0}
coincides with the shared LoRA row space spanned by
\mathbf{A}
, thus we have

\mathbf{L}_{0}=\mathbf{L}_{0}P_{\mathbf{A}}.

(13)

Next,
\mathbf{S}_{0}
is blockwise sparse, with support determined by the contaminated clients.
Define

\mathcal{I}:=\{(j,k)\in\mathcal{G}:j\in\mathcal{C}^{c}\ \text{or}\ k\in\mathcal{C}^{c}\}

as the index set of client pairs involving at least one contaminated client, and let
s:=|\mathcal{I}|
.
The complement
\mathcal{I}^{c}
then consists of benign pairs, for which the shared low-rank structure is retained.
Let

P_{\mathcal{I}}:=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{I}}P_{g}
,
P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}:=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{I}^{c}}P_{g}
,
and define the corresponding block-support subspace

\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}}:=\{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{Gq\times p}:P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{X}\}
.
By construction, we have
P_{g}(\mathbf{S}_{0})=\mathbf{0}
for all
g\in\mathcal{I}^{c}
and thus

P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{S}_{0})=\mathbf{S}_{0}.

(14)

For
\mathbf{E}_{0}
, a direct calculation shows that for all distinct
j,k\in[K]
, the corresponding block satisfies

\|\mathbf{E}_{0}^{(j,k)}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}\leq\delta(\|\mathbf{B}^{(j)}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}+\|\mathbf{B}^{(k)}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}})
,
where the maximal approximation error
\delta
is defined in (
4
). Thus,
\mathbf{S}_{0}
isolates structured heterogeneity associated with contaminated client pairs, whereas
\mathbf{E}_{0}
collects the residual approximation error arising from imperfect alignment of the active clients’ row-space factors.

With the constraints (
13
) and (
14
), we rewrite the decomposition as

\mathbf{D}_{0}=\mathbf{L}_{0}+\mathbf{S}_{0},\quad\mathbf{L}_{0}=\mathbf{L}_{0}P_{\mathbf{A}},\quad P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{S}_{0})=\mathbf{S}_{0}.

(15)

We emphasize the two constraints because, as shown later, the identifiable objects are only
(P_{A},\mathcal{I})
, rather than the individual components
(\mathbf{L}_{0},\mathbf{S}_{0})
. In practice, the approximation error is typically nonzero, the observed matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{D}}
introduced in (
5
) admits the following noisy decomposition:

\widehat{\mathbf{D}}=\mathbf{L}_{0}+\mathbf{S}_{0}+\mathbf{E},\quad\mathbf{L}_{0}=\mathbf{L}_{0}P_{\mathbf{A}},\quad P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{S}_{0})=\mathbf{S}_{0},\quad\mathbf{E}=\mathbf{E}_{0}+\mathbf{E}_{1}.

(16)

Here,
\mathbf{E}_{0}
is the deterministic approximation error defined above, while
\mathbf{E}_{1}
is induced by the preliminary estimation errors.
Specifically, with

\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}:=\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}-\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p},\qquad k\in[K],

the
(j,k)
th block of
\mathbf{E}_{1}
is

\mathbf{E}_{1}^{(j,k)}=\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}.

Remark 2

(Role of
\mathbf{E}_{1}
)
.

For each client
k
, the local model follows (
2
).
Given a preliminary estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}
, obtained from either a linear or nonlinear local fit, our procedure first constructs the stacked matrix of pairwise differences in (
6
). The term
\mathbf{E}_{1}
collects the resulting stochastic discrepancies in these contrasts, including finite-sample estimation error in
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}
, optimization error from local training, model misspecification in
f^{(k)}
, and observation noise.

Under standard regularity conditions, these discrepancies may have sub-Gaussian behavior at the stacked-matrix level. For example, this holds in linear models with sub-Gaussian covariates and noise, and in certain nonlinear models with bounded gradients and sub-Gaussian residual fluctuations.
The subsequent analysis does
not
require a fully specified form of
f^{(k)}
, and it only uses tail control for the residuals, such as a sub-Gaussian or, more generally, finite Orlicz-norm condition.
This formulation covers linear regression, index models, and one-layer neural adaptations commonly used in local fine-tuning.

With the decomposition in (
16
), it is natural to consider the noisy recovery problem

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}}

\displaystyle\lambda_{L}\|\mathbf{L}\|_{*}+\lambda_{S}\|\mathbf{S}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}

(17)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{L}P_{\mathbf{A}},\ P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{S})=\mathbf{S},\ \omega_{g}\|P_{g}(\widehat{\mathbf{D}}-\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{S})\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}\leq\varepsilon_{g}^{2},\ \forall g=(j,k)\in\mathcal{G},

where
\lambda_{L},\lambda_{S}>0
are regularization parameters,
\omega_{g}>0
are block-specific weights, and
\varepsilon_{g}:=\|P_{g}(\mathbf{E})\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}
is the blockwise noise level, which combines the deterministic approximation error
\mathbf{E}_{0}
and the preliminary estimation error
\mathbf{E}_{1}
for client pair
g
.

Problem (
17
) is an oracle formulation: it assumes knowledge of the row-space projector
P_{\mathbf{A}}
, the contamination support projector
P_{\mathcal{I}}
, and the blockwise noise levels
\{\varepsilon_{g}:g\in\mathcal{G}\}
.
These quantities are unavailable in practice.
Accordingly, the implemented estimator of our approach is based on the penalized program (
7
), which serves as a fully data-driven surrogate for (
17
). In the theoretical analysis, we prove that the surrogate in (
7
)
automatically yields a solution that matches the oracle problem in (
17
).
Before analyzing this surrogate, however, we first clarify what is identifiable from the underlying decomposition, even in the noiseless setting.

Although the decomposition
\mathbf{D}_{0}=\mathbf{L}_{0}+\mathbf{S}_{0}
arises naturally in the federated LoRA setting and has a clear practical interpretation, the pair
(\mathbf{L}_{0},\mathbf{S}_{0})
is generally not identifiable. The obstruction is geometric: a row-block sparse component may also lie in the shared row space, allowing part of the signal to be shifted between the low-rank and sparse components without changing their sum.
We formalize this ambiguity as follows.

Definition 1

(Shiftable subspace)
.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{A}}:=\{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{Gq\times p}:\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{X}P_{\mathbf{A}}\}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}}:=\{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{Gq\times p}:P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{X}\}
.
The shiftable subspace is defined as

\mathcal{M}:=\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}}\cap\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{A}}=\{\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{Gq\times p}:P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{M})=\mathbf{M},\ \mathbf{M}=\mathbf{M}P_{\mathbf{A}}\}.

The presence of
\mathcal{M}
creates an intrinsic non-identifiability.
Any
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{M}
can be shifted between the low-rank and sparse components without altering feasibility:

\mathbf{D}_{0}=(\mathbf{L}_{0}+\mathbf{M})+(\mathbf{S}_{0}-\mathbf{M})
.
Thus, the raw pair
(\mathbf{L}_{0},\mathbf{S}_{0})
is not the appropriate estimand.
To remove this ambiguity, we introduce a canonical representation that is invariant to shifts in
\mathcal{M}
. Specifically, write

\widehat{\mathbf{D}}=\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{A}}+\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}+\mathbf{E},\qquad\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{A}}:=\mathbf{D}_{0}P_{\mathbf{A}},\qquad\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}:=\mathbf{D}_{0}P_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}},

(18)

where
P_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}:=\mathbf{I}-P_{\mathbf{A}}
.
Then, the projected components
\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{A}}
and
\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}
are unchanged under every admissible shift in
\mathcal{M}
, thus serving as primary targets for subsequent collaborative refinement and contamination identification.

To clarify the role of
\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}
in collaborative-set recovery, observe first that
\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(j,k)}=\mathbf{0}
for every benign pairs
j,k\in\mathcal{C}
.
For pairs of contaminated clients,
j,k\in\mathcal{C}^{c}
, the corresponding block may be zero or nonzero: it can vanish when the two contaminated effects cancel, or when their difference lies entirely within the shared row space.
We therefore impose a mixed-pair separation condition
\|\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(j,k)}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}>0
for all
j\in\mathcal{C}
and
k\in\mathcal{C}^{c}
.
Under this condition, every benign client has at least
|\mathcal{C}|-1
pairwise blocks satisfying

\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(j,k)}=\mathbf{0}
, while every contaminated client has at most
|\mathcal{C}^{c}|-1
such zero blocks.
Consequently, when
|\mathcal{C}|>|\mathcal{C}^{c}|
, equivalently
\eta>1/2
, the collaborative-set is identified by the population majority zero-block rule

\mathcal{C}=\biggl\{k\in[K]:\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j\neq k}{\mathrm{1}}\Bigl\{\|\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(j,k)}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}=0\Bigr\}\geq\frac{K\eta-1}{K-1}\biggr\}.

The estimated quantities
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{\mathbf{A}}=\widehat{\mathbf{D}}P_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}=\widehat{\mathbf{D}}P_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\perp}}
introduced in the preceding subsection are precisely plug-in estimators of these canonical targets, which leads directly to the active-set rule (
8
) and the refined estimator (
9
).

4
Computation

This section describes how to solve the penalized problem (
7
). The objective admits an efficient proximal gradient implementation
[
1
,
13
]
.
Denote the loss and penalty function as

\ell(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\omega_{g}\|P_{g}(\widehat{\mathbf{D}}-\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{S})\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2},\qquad h(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}):=\lambda_{L}\|\mathbf{L}\|_{*}+\lambda_{S}\|\mathbf{S}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1},

respectively.
Define the linear operator
P_{\omega}:=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\omega_{g}P_{g}
.
Since each
P_{g}
is an orthogonal projection,
P_{\omega}
is self-adjoint and positive semi-definite.
Writing
\mathbf{R}:=\widehat{\mathbf{D}}-\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{S}
, we have

\ell(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathbf{R},\ P_{\omega}\mathbf{R}\rangle
. Direct differentiation yields

\nabla_{\mathbf{L}}\ell(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=-P_{\omega}\mathbf{R},\qquad\nabla_{\mathbf{S}}\ell(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=-P_{\omega}\mathbf{R}.

(19)

We apply the proximal gradient method in the product space equipped with the norm
\|(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})\|^{2}:=\|\mathbf{L}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}+\|\mathbf{S}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}
.
Given the
m
th iterate
(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{m},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{m})
and a step size
t_{m}>0
for
m\in[M-1]
, define the proximal function

\displaystyle\psi_{m}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}\mid\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{m},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{m})

\displaystyle:=\ell(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{m},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{m})+\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{L}}\ell(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{m},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{m}),\ \mathbf{L}-\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{m}\rangle+\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{S}}\ell(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{m},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{m}),\ \mathbf{S}-\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{m}\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2t_{m}}\Big(\|\mathbf{L}-\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{m}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}+\|\mathbf{S}-\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{m}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}\Big)+h(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}).

The next iterate is obtained from:

(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{m+1},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{m+1})=\argmin_{\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}}\ \psi_{m}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}\mid\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{m},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{m}).

(20)

Denote the residual

\mathbf{R}^{m}:=\widehat{\mathbf{D}}-\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{m}-\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{m}

and use (
19
), the problem (
20
) is then equivalent to

\min_{\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}}\ \lambda_{L}\|\mathbf{L}\|_{*}+\frac{1}{2t_{m}}\|\mathbf{L}-(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{m}+t_{m}P_{\omega}\mathbf{R}^{m})\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}+\lambda_{S}\|\mathbf{S}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}+\frac{1}{2t_{m}}\|\mathbf{S}-(\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{m}+t_{m}P_{\omega}\mathbf{R}^{m})\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2},

which separates in
\mathbf{L}
and
\mathbf{S}
.
Consequently,

\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{m+1}=\mathcal{SVT}\big(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{m}+t_{m}P_{\omega}\mathbf{R}^{m};\ t_{m}\lambda_{L}\big),\qquad\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{m+1}=\mathcal{BST}\big(\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{m}+t_{m}P_{\omega}\mathbf{R}^{m};\ t_{m}\lambda_{S}\big).

(21)

Here, the Singular Value Thresholding operator is defined by

\mathcal{SVT}(\mathbf{M};\tau)=\mathbf{U}\mathrm{diag}((\sigma_{i}-\tau)_{+})\mathbf{V}^{\top}

for
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{U}\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{i})\mathbf{V}^{\top}
, and the Block Soft Thresholding operator acts on each row-block
g
as

P_{g}\bigl(\mathcal{BST}(\mathbf{M};\tau)\bigr)=\Big(1-\frac{\tau}{\|P_{g}(\mathbf{M})\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}}\Big)_{+}P_{g}(\mathbf{M}),\quad g\in\mathcal{G}.

Since the projections
\{P_{g}\}_{g\in\mathcal{G}}
are mutually orthogonal and
P_{\omega}=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\omega_{g}P_{g}
is block-diagonal under the row-block decomposition,

\|P_{\omega}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\max_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\omega_{g}
.
The gradient of
\ell
on the product space is therefore Lipschitz with constant

L_{\ell}=2\|P_{\omega}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=2\max_{g}\omega_{g}
.
Thus, one may use any constant step size
t\leq 1/L_{\ell}
, or select
t_{m}
adaptively by backtracking.

Input:

\widehat{\mathbf{D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Gq\times p}
,
\lambda_{L}
,
\lambda_{S}
, steps
M
, step size
t
, and weights
\{\omega_{g}\}_{g\in\mathcal{G}}
, thresholds
\alpha
and
\tau_{n}
.

Initialization
:
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{0}=\mathbf{0}_{Gq\times p}
,
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{0}=\mathbf{0}_{Gq\times p}
.

for

m=0

to

M-1

do

\mathbf{R}^{m}=\widehat{\mathbf{D}}-\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{m}-\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{m}
;

\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{m+1}=\mathcal{SVT}\big(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{m}+tP_{\omega}\mathbf{R}^{m};\ t\lambda_{L}\big)
;

\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{m+1}=\mathcal{BST}\big(\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{m}+tP_{\omega}\mathbf{R}^{m};\ t\lambda_{S}\big)
;

end for

Compute
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{M}=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}
,
P_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}:=\widehat{\mathbf{A}}\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}
, and
P_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\perp}}:=\mathbf{I}-P_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
;

Compute
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{\mathbf{A}}=\widehat{\mathbf{D}}P_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}=\widehat{\mathbf{D}}P_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\perp}}
;

Compute
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}
using (
8
) and
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}
using (
9
);

Output
:
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}
and
\{\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}:k\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\}
.

Remark 3

(Choice of the majority threshold
\alpha
)
.

For collaborative-set recovery, an

in the range of

\frac{|\mathcal{C}^{c}|-1}{K-1}<\alpha\leq\frac{|\mathcal{C}|-1}{K-1}.

is sufficient to guarantee the consistency of benign-set recovery, as shown in Theorem

is large, this requirement becomes approximately

, where

5
Recovery analysis

This section studies the recovery properties of CLAIR.
We first clarify the identifiability issue and analyze exact recovery for (
15
) in the idealized noiseless setting, where the approximation error
\mathbf{E}_{0}
and preliminary estimation error
\mathbf{E}_{1}
are zero.
We then consider the practical noisy setting, where
\widehat{\mathbf{D}}
contains both
\mathbf{E}_{0}
and
\mathbf{E}_{1}
, and establish stable row-space recovery and consistent collaborative set detection.

5.1
Identifiability

Although the low-rank plus block-sparse form in (
15
) suggests a structured robust PCA-type decomposition, the inherent geometry is different.
The row-block sparse component in (
15
) may partially align with the shared LoRA row space, allowing the signal to be shifted between the low-rank and sparse components without changing their sum, as illustrated by Definition
1
.
Consequently, the raw pair
(\mathbf{L}_{0},\mathbf{S}_{0})
defined in (
11
) is generally not identifiable, even in the noiseless setting.

To formalize this obstruction, we introduce the relevant notation.
Let
\mathbf{L}_{0}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\top}
be a rank-
r
singular value decomposition of
\mathbf{L}_{0}
, where

\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{Gq\times r}
and
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
.
We write

\mathcal{U}:=\{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{X}^{\top}:\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}\}
and
\mathcal{V}:=\{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{V}^{\top}:\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{Gq\times r}\}
for the associated column and row space subspaces, respectively.
The tangent space at
\mathbf{L}_{0}
is

\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}_{0}):=\{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\top}+\mathbf{X}_{2}\mathbf{V}^{\top}:\mathbf{X}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r},\ \mathbf{X}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{Gq\times r}\},

with projection

P_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{M}):=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{M}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\top}-\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\top}

(22)

for any
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{Gq\times p}
. Let
P_{\mathcal{T}^{\perp}}
denote the projector onto the orthogonal complement
\mathcal{T}^{\perp}
.

Classical robust PCA arguments typically rely on transversality between the tangent space
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}_{0})
and the sparse support subspace
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}}
.
This condition fails in the present setting.
Indeed, if
\mathcal{I}\neq\emptyset
, take any nonzero
\mathbf{W}
satisfying
P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{W})=\mathbf{W}
and set
\mathbf{H}:=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{V}^{\top}
.
Then
\mathbf{H}\in\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}_{0})\cap\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}}
and
\mathbf{H}\neq\mathbf{0}
.
This nontrivial intersection is the geometric obstruction underlying the shift ambiguity.
Consequently, classical exact-decomposition arguments
[
4
,
6
]
do not apply directly.

We address this by handling the column and row spaces of
\mathbf{L}_{0}
separately. We impose rank-sparsity conditions through the column space
\mathcal{U}
, and handle row-space distinguishability by projecting each sparse block onto
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{V}_{\perp}
.
We first show that
\mathcal{U}
and the block-sparse subspace
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}}
are transverse under the following condition.

Lemma 1
.

Define
\rho:=\|P_{\mathcal{I}}P_{\mathbf{U}}P_{\mathcal{I}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
,
where
P_{\mathbf{U}}
denotes the orthogonal projector onto
\mathcal{U}
.
If
\rho<1
, then
\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}}=\{0\}
and
P_{\mathbf{A}}
is identifiable from the decomposition (
15
).

The condition
\rho<1
rules out overlap between the column space and the contaminated block support, but it does not by itself identify the raw pair
(\mathbf{L}_{0},\mathbf{S}_{0})
. The remaining ambiguity lies in the shiftable subspace
\mathcal{M}
.
We therefore work with the projected canonical components

(\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{A}},\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}):=(\mathbf{D}_{0}P_{\mathbf{A}},\mathbf{D}_{0}P_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}).

These components are invariant to all shifts in
\mathcal{M}
.
Indeed, for any feasible decomposition satisfying (
15
), we have
\mathbf{L}P_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}=\mathbf{0}
.
Hence,

\mathbf{S}P_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}=\mathbf{D}_{0}P_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}},\quad\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S}P_{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{D}_{0}P_{\mathbf{A}}.

Thus, the projected pair
(\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{A}},\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}})
is uniquely determined by
\mathbf{D}_{0}
, even though
(\mathbf{L}_{0},\mathbf{S}_{0})
itself may not be.

The central object is therefore the projector
P_{\mathbf{A}}
, which encodes the shared row space of the benign low-rank adapters.
Once
P_{\mathbf{A}}
is identified, the canonical decomposition (
18
) is determined.
Consequently, accurate recovery of
P_{\mathbf{A}}
from
\mathbf{D}_{0}
, or from its noisy counterpart
\widehat{\mathbf{D}}
, is essential for both contaminated-client identification and collaborative fine-tuning.
We turn to the recovery of
P_{\mathbf{A}}
in the next subsection.

5.2
Exact recovery

This section studies the ideal noiseless decomposition, which provides a benchmark for recovery analysis and isolates the role of structural conditions such as rank-sparsity incoherence.

When
\delta=0
, all benign clients share the same matrix
\mathbf{A}
, and hence
\mathbf{E}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
.
Suppose further that
\mathbf{E}_{1}=\mathbf{0}
. The observed contrast matrix then admits the noiseless decomposition (
15
).
To recover the low-rank and block-sparse components in (
15
), it is natural to consider the constrained program

\min_{\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}}\ \lambda_{L}\|\mathbf{L}\|_{*}+\lambda_{S}\|\mathbf{S}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{D}_{0},\quad\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{L}P_{\mathbf{A}},\quad P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{S})=\mathbf{S}.

(23)

The constraint
\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{L}P_{\mathbf{A}}
restricts the low-rank component to the shared row space, whereas
P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{S})=\mathbf{S}
imposes the prescribed block support.
Since both
P_{\mathbf{A}}
and
\mathcal{I}
are unknown in practice, we refer to (
23
) as the noiseless oracle problem.

The following assumption formally underlies the common row-space constraint.

Assumption 1

(Active clients share a common space
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{A}}
)
.

There exists an active client set
\mathcal{C}\subseteq[K]
with
|\mathcal{C}|=\eta K
and
\eta\in(0,1]
such that Condition (
4
) and the rank condition (
12
) hold.

Recall that the separation condition
\rho<1
ensures that contamination in the column space can be distinguished from the sparse block components.
To make this conceptual condition verifiable, we introduce concrete assumptions motivated by the federated LoRA setting, together with their practical interpretations.

Assumption 2

(No client-pair dominates
\mathcal{U}
)
.

There exists a constant
\mu\geq 1
such that

\max_{g}\|P_{g}P_{\mathbf{U}}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}\leq\frac{\mu r}{G}.

(Block-IC)

Assumption

imposes a blockwise incoherence (Block-IC) condition on the column space
\mathcal{U}
.
Since
\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\|P_{g}P_{\mathbf{U}}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}=r

Condition (

\mathcal{U}
from being overly concentrated on a small number of client-pair blocks.
Consequently, for any contaminated set
\mathcal{I}

This is a worst-case condition, since it ignores cancellations or misalignment across corrupted blocks. Combined with an upper bound on

Lemma 2

Under Assumption

In practice, both

\min_{\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}}\ \lambda_{L}\|\mathbf{L}\|_{*}+\lambda_{S}\|\mathbf{S}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{D}_{0}.

\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{A}}

Theorem 1

Assume

If

\frac{\lambda_{S}}{\lambda_{L}}\in\biggl(\frac{\sqrt{\mu r/G}}{b-(1+a)\sqrt{\mu rs/G}},\ \frac{a}{(1+a)\sqrt{s}}\biggr),

(25)

then, for
any

(\widehat{\mathbf{L}},\widehat{\mathbf{S}})

Theorem

establishes exact recovery of the shared row-space projector in a nonidentifiable low-rank plus block-sparse decomposition induced by federated LoRA contrasts. The main challenge is to show that this row-space conclusion remains valid after the oracle constraints on
P_{\mathbf{A}}
and
\mathcal{I}
are removed in program (

We address this by constructing a dual certificate at an arbitrary oracle optimizer and showing that it certifies optimality for (

Thus, the oracle problem (

The regularizer requirement (

Its upper bound keeps the correction on contaminated support within the nuclear-norm subgradient, while its lower bound makes the block penalty large enough to control leakage on uncontaminated blocks.
With such a certificate, any optimizer of (

\mathcal{M}

The separation condition rules out any shift that changes the component orthogonal to the oracle row space, and the identity

Together, these facts imply

for every optimizer, which is precisely the quantity needed for active-set detection and collaborative refinement.

5.4
Collaborative-set recovery

In this subsection, we show that the estimator

\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}:=\Bigl\{k\in[K]:\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j\neq k}{\mathrm{1}}\{\|\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(j,k)}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}\leq\tau_{n}\}\geq\alpha\Bigr\}

given by (
8
) recovers the benign client set
\mathcal{C}
under a mild signal-gap condition.
Recall that for any benign block
g=(j,k)\in\mathcal{G}
with
j,k\in\mathcal{C}
, the block
\mathbf{D}_{0}^{(g)}
lies in the row space of
\mathbf{A}
, and hence
\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(j,k)}=\mathbf{0}
for every benign pair
j\neq k
.
However, for
(j,k)
involving at least one contaminated client, the behavior of
\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(j,k)}
is not automatically determined, as discussed at the end of Section
3.2
.
For this, we define the minimum mixed-pair signal level

\beta_{\min}:=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\mathcal{C},\,k\in\mathcal{C}^{c}\end{subarray}}\|\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(j,k)}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}},

and require it to dominate the uniform projected estimation error

\varepsilon_{\sup}:=\max_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\|P_{g}(\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}-\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}})\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}.

The collaborative set recovery argument then follows.
We first establish a high probability bound for
\varepsilon_{\sup}
.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose the assumptions of Theorem
2
hold. Then

\varepsilon_{\sup}=O_{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl(\sqrt{\frac{\log K}{K}}\Bigr).

The following theorem combines this uniform error control with a mixed-pair signal gap to establish collaborative-set recovery in the noisy setting.

Theorem 3

(Collaborative-set recovery)
.

Assume
|\mathcal{C}|=\eta K
with
\eta>1/2
.
Suppose

\frac{|\mathcal{C}^{c}|-1}{K-1}<\alpha\leq\frac{|\mathcal{C}|-1}{K-1}

and
\beta_{\min}>0
.

(i) Under the assumptions of Theorem
1
, suppose further
0\leq\tau_{n}<\beta_{\min}
, then
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{C}
.

(ii) Under the assumptions of Theorem
2
, suppose further
c\sqrt{\log K/K}<\tau_{n}<\beta_{\min}-c\sqrt{\log K/K}
for sufficiently large
K
and some constant
c>0
. If
\sqrt{\log K/K}/\beta_{\min}\to 0
, then
\mathbb{P}(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{C})\to 1.

The mixed-pair separation condition
\beta_{\min}>0
is mild: it requires only that benign–contaminated pairs leave a nonzero orthogonal signal, while imposing no restriction on pairs of contaminated clients.
The majority condition
\eta>1/2
ensures that the admissible interval for
\alpha
is nonempty and prevents contaminated clients from passing the zero-block counting threshold.
As discussed earlier, this condition can be further relaxed by imposing additional assumptions on pairs of contaminated clients, such as requiring them to be sufficiently heterogeneous.

6
Error analysis

We now quantify the statistical gain of collaborative fine-tuning by analyzing the CLAIR refined estimator
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}
. Recall from (
9
) that, for each
k\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}
,

\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}|}\sum_{j\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}}\Bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{(j)}+\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{\mathbf{A}}^{(k,j)}\Bigr).

Define the local mean squared error

\mathcal{E}^{(k)}:=\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}-\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2},

and its projected counterparts in the space of
\mathbf{A}^{\perp}
and
\mathbf{A}
as

\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(k)}:=\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}P_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2},\quad\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}}^{(k)}:=\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}P_{\mathbf{A}}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}.

(29)

Further let
\theta_{k}
and
1-\theta_{k}
denote the respective ratios of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}}
to
\mathcal{E}^{(k)}
, i.e.,

\theta_{k}:=\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(k)}}{\mathcal{E}^{(k)}},\quad 1-\theta_{k}=\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}}^{(k)}}{\mathcal{E}^{(k)}}

(30)

When
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}
is evenly distributed over the space spanned by
P_{\mathbf{A}}\cup P_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}
, we have

\theta_{k}=O\left(\frac{p-r}{p}\right),\quad 1-\theta_{k}=O\left(\frac{r}{p}\right),

In later analysis, we will show that
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(k)}
corresponds to the noise reducible by CLAIR, whereas
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}}^{(k)}
is not reducible. Consequently, in the LoRA setting where
r\ll p
, CLAIR can yield substantial estimation gains.

We now introduce a quantity that measures the oracle CLAIR gain for client
k
, along with its ratio to
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(k)}
:

\mathcal{H}^{(k)}:=\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(k)}-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|^{2}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(j)},\quad\phi_{k}:=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{(k)}}{\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(k)}}.

(31)

Here, the quantity
\mathcal{H}^{(k)}
measures the oracle collaborative gain
in the orthogonal complement of the shared row space, as will be illustrated in Theorem
4
. Furthermore,
\phi_{k}
measures the fraction of the total reducible noise captured by this oracle gain; clearly,
\phi_{k}<1
.
Although
\mathcal{H}^{(k)}
and
\phi_{k}
are not necessarily positive, such as in the case of a benign client with an unusually small orthogonal complement error, they are typically on the order of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(k)}
. In particular, if
\{\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{C}}
are identically distributed, then

\mathcal{H}^{(k)}=\frac{|\mathcal{C}|-1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(k)},\quad\phi_{k}=\frac{|\mathcal{C}|-1}{|\mathcal{C}|}.

We now state the estimation gain of CLAIR under an oracle setting where
P_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}=P_{\mathbf{A}}
and
\delta=0
.
In practice, estimating
P_{\mathbf{A}}
incurs an additional cost and introduces extra misalignment error, which will be discussed in Theorem
5
and
6
.

Theorem 4

(Oracle CLAIR gain)
.

Assume the conditions of Theorem
2
hold with
\delta=0
and assume
P_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}=P_{\mathbf{A}}
.
Fix
k\in\mathcal{C}
, then the exact oracle MSE gain of the refined estimator relative to the local estimator is

\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}-\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}=(1-\phi_{k}\theta_{k})\ \mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}-\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}.

(32)

In particular, if
\{\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{C}}
are identically distributed and evenly distributed over the space
P_{\mathbf{A}}\cup P_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}
, then, for any
k\in\mathcal{C}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}-\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}=\left[1-\frac{(|\mathcal{C}|-1)(p-r)}{|\mathcal{C}|p}\right]\ \mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}-\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}.

Theorem
4
provides three insights. First, the gain comes entirely from reducible noise in the orthogonal complement of the shared row space. Second, averaging over benign clients yields a
1/|\mathcal{C}|
variance-reduction effect,
making the benefit of a larger collaborative set explicit. Third, a lower LoRA rank
r
leads to a larger CLAIR gain.

Now we consider the practical setting with an additional noisy decomposition cost. Define the cost for client
k
and its ratio to the total local risk

(33)

where

and

and
\mathbf{E}_{0}^{(k,k)}=\mathbf{0}
.
Here,
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{cost}}^{(k)}
represents the additional cost of estimating
P_{\mathbf{A}}
, consisting of a benign misalignment bias
-\widebar{\mathbf{E}}_{0}P_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}
and a subspace estimation error controlled by
P_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}-P_{\mathbf{A}}
.
We next show that improvement of the CLAIR estimator depends on a tradeoff between the collaborative gain
\mathcal{H}^{(k)}
and the noisy-decomposition cost
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{cost}}^{(k)}
.

(Noisy CLAIR gain)

]
. This controlled design allows client heterogeneity, low-rank adaptation, and client-level contamination to be directly examined.
The experiment evaluates whether CLAIR can identify contaminated clients and improve benign-client prediction through selective collaborative refinement.

8.1
Task and model

In a copying task, the input sequence
\mathbf{x}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{T})
contains a segment
\mathbf{u}^{\#}
of length
L<T/2
that appears twice.
The model must use the earlier occurrence of this segment to predict the next tokens. For example, after observing “[A], [l], [i], [c], [e]” in
\mathbf{u}^{\#}
, a model prompted with “[A], [l]” should generate “[i], [c], [e]” successively under next-token prediction.
Although simple to state, the task requires content-based retrieval and positional alignment, making it a controlled benchmark for a primitive form of algorithmic reasoning in LLMs.

In this experiment, all sequences have length
T=64
and are generated over a discrete vocabulary

\mathcal{A}=\{a,\ldots,z,A,\ldots,Z,\&\},

consisting of
52
letter tokens and one padding token “
\&
”.
Given a segment
\mathbf{u}^{\#}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{L})
sampled from a client-specific distribution
P_{x}
on
\mathcal{A}
, we construct a sequence

\mathbf{x}=(*,\ \mathbf{u}^{\#},\ *,\ \mathbf{u}^{\#},\ *),

where the two copies of
\mathbf{u}^{\#}
are placed randomly and the remaining positions
*
are filled with random tokens.
Varying the segment length
L
and sampling distribution
P_{x}
across client-specific copying tasks naturally induces heterogeneity and can create contaminated clients.

In this experiment, each client
k
is assigned a specific copying task. Following a standard parameter-efficient fine-tuning pipeline, we first pretrain an attention-based Transformer
f_{0}
on a base copying task and then fine-tune it for each client using LoRA on local sequences
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(k)}\}_{i=1}^{n_{k}}
.
The Transformer contains two attention blocks, each with projection matrices
\{\mathbf{W}_{q},\mathbf{W}_{k},\mathbf{W}_{v},\mathbf{W}_{o}\}
, and LoRA is applied to all eight projections.
A subsequent collaboration step, implemented either by a FedAvg-based procedure or by CLAIR, is applied separately to the client-specific adapted weights associated with each projection matrix.

The model is trained autoregressively by next-token prediction from prefixes.
The architecture and training protocol follow standard Transformer practice; implementation details are provided in the Appendix.

8.2
Experimental settings

We consider a federated network of nine benign clients and one contaminated client, and explore two experimental regimes: a homogeneous one where all benign clients share the same copying task, and a heterogeneous one where they follow the same copying rule but differ in token distribution
P_{x}
and copy length
L
.
In both regimes, the contaminated client outputs a model adapted from a mismatched copying task compared to benign clients.

In the homogeneous regime, all benign clients share a common power-law sampling distribution

P_{x}(X=a;t)=\frac{a^{-t}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{52}\ell^{-t}}

for all
a\in[52]
, with exponent
t=1.1
, and uses a fixed copy length
L=16
.
In the heterogeneous regime, each benign client has a client-specific power-law exponent drawn from
[0.95,1.6]
and a copy length drawn from
\{10,\ldots,26\}
independently in each replicate.
In both regimes, each client trains LoRA adapter locally on
N_{\mathrm{train}}=2000
generated sequences, using batch size
50
, one epoch, learning rate
0.001
, and LoRA rank
r=3
.

We compare CLAIR with several baselines: local LoRA, FedAvg over all clients, and oracle FedAvg over the benign client set. Importantly, oracle FedAvg relies on the true collaborative set and is therefore unavailable in practice; it is included only as an ideal contamination-free benchmark.
For CLAIR, we set
(\lambda_{L},\lambda_{S})=(0.5,0.4)
in the homogeneous regime and
(0.5,0.2)
in the heterogeneous regime, with collaborative-set detection thresholds
0.5
and
0.01
, respectively, and voting threshold
\alpha=0.5
.
Each method is evaluated using the masked next-token error, which excludes the first copy and the first three positions of the second copy, as in
[
29
]
. Specifically, on the test sequences
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{test}}:=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}=(x_{i,1},\ldots,x_{i,T})\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{test}}}
, let
m_{i}
be the starting position of the second copied segment and let
L_{i}
be its length. Given the predictive distribution
\widehat{p}_{t}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x}_{i,<t})
given by model
\widehat{f}
, define
\widehat{x}_{i,t}=\argmax_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\widehat{p}_{t}(a\mid\mathbf{x}_{i,<t}).

The masked next-token error is then

\operatorname{Err}_{\mathrm{mask}}=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{test}}}|\mathcal{M}_{i}|}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{test}}}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{M}_{i}}{\mathrm{1}}\{\widehat{x}_{i,t}\neq x_{i,t}\},\quad\mathcal{M}_{i}=\{m_{i}+3,\ldots,m_{i}+L_{i}-1\}.

Equivalently, the reported masked next-token accuracy is

\operatorname{Acc}_{\mathrm{mask}}=1-\operatorname{Err}_{\mathrm{mask}}.

9
Summary

We develop CLAIR as a contamination-aware framework for collaborative federated LoRA fine-tuning when the common backbone is unknown and only preliminary local estimators are available.
We explain why CLAIR can improve over local fine-tuning without directly averaging incompatible adapters.
The MSE analysis further provides insights that collaboration is beneficial only when the oracle variance reduction from averaging benign clients dominates the costs caused by row-space heterogeneity, subspace estimation, and noisy decomposition. Thus, the theory gives both a positive mechanism for federated LoRA gains and a diagnostic condition under which negative transfer may occur.

Future work should relax several structural restrictions.
One direction is to replace the single-majority collaborative set by multiple or overlapping collaborative groups, allowing layer-specific ranks and client-dependent adapter subspaces. This extension would connect naturally with mixture-of-experts federated LoRA, where clients may select different expert adapters rather than share one global subspace.
Another direction is to relax the current preliminary estimator assumption by deriving error conditions from the dynamics of deep learning fine-tuning, including stochastic optimization error, nonlinear representation drift, and module-wise interactions. These problems warrant further investigation.

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{S}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}\geq\|\mathbf{S}^{\prime}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}-\langle\mathbf{Q}/\lambda_{S},\boldsymbol{\Delta}\rangle+(1-b)\|P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}.

In particular, if
(\mathbf{L}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\Delta},\mathbf{S}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\Delta})
is also optimal for (24), then
P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})=0
and
P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})=0
.

Proof of Lemma
S5
.

Write
\mathbf{Q}/\lambda_{L}=\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}+\mathbf{Z}
with
\mathbf{Z}:=P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}(\mathbf{Q}/\lambda_{L})
and
\|\mathbf{Z}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq a
.
Fix any perturbation
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
. By the duality between nuclear and operator norms, there exists
\mathbf{W}\in\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}
such that

\|\mathbf{W}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=1,\quad\langle\mathbf{W},P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\rangle=\|P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{*}.

Then
\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}+\mathbf{W}\in\partial\|\mathbf{L}^{\prime}\|_{*}
.
By the subgradient inequality,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{L}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{*}

\displaystyle\geq\|\mathbf{L}^{\prime}\|_{*}+\langle\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}+\mathbf{W},\boldsymbol{\Delta}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\|\mathbf{L}^{\prime}\|_{*}+\langle\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top},\boldsymbol{\Delta}\rangle+\|P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{*}

Note that

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{Q}/\lambda_{L},\boldsymbol{\Delta}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top},\boldsymbol{\Delta}\rangle+\langle\mathbf{Z},P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\langle\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top},\boldsymbol{\Delta}\rangle+a\cdot\|P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{*}.

Plugging in gives

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{L}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{*}

\displaystyle\geq\|\mathbf{L}^{\prime}\|_{*}+\langle\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top},\boldsymbol{\Delta}\rangle+\|P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{*}

\displaystyle\geq\|\mathbf{L}^{\prime}\|_{*}+\langle\mathbf{Q}/\lambda_{L},\boldsymbol{\Delta}\rangle+(1-a)\|P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{*}.

For the sparse term, note that
\mathbf{S}^{\prime}=P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{S}^{\prime})
, so

\|\mathbf{S}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}=\|P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{S}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\Delta})\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}+\|P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}.

Since
P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{Q})/\lambda_{S}\in{\mathrm{Sign}}_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{S}^{\prime})
, convexity on the support subspace gives

\|P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{S}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\Delta})\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}\geq\|\mathbf{S}^{\prime}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}-\langle P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{Q})/\lambda_{S},P_{\mathcal{I}}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})\rangle.

Hence

\|\mathbf{S}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}\geq\|\mathbf{S}^{\prime}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}-\langle\mathbf{Q}/\lambda_{S},\boldsymbol{\Delta}\rangle+\langle P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\mathbf{Q})/\lambda_{S},P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})\rangle+\|P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}.

By block duality,

|\langle P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\mathbf{Q})/\lambda_{S},P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})\rangle|\leq\|P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\mathbf{Q})/\lambda_{S}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},\infty}\cdot\|P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}\leq b\|P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}.

Therefore

\|\mathbf{S}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}\geq\|\mathbf{S}^{\prime}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}-\langle\mathbf{Q}/\lambda_{S},\boldsymbol{\Delta}\rangle+(1-b)\|P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}.

Finally, multiplying the regularization parameters and adding two inequalities leads to

\lambda_{L}\|\mathbf{L}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{*}+\lambda_{S}\|\mathbf{S}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}\geq\lambda_{L}\|\mathbf{L}^{\prime}\|_{*}+\lambda_{S}\|\mathbf{S}^{\prime}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}+\lambda_{L}(1-a)\|P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{*}+\lambda_{S}(1-b)\|P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}.

Thus, if
(\mathbf{L}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\Delta},\mathbf{S}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\Delta})
is also optimal for (24), then objective values are equal, so the nonnegative remainder must be zero:

P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})=0,\quad P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})=0.

Proof completed.

The following lemma transfers the row-block incoherence from
\mathbf{U}_{0}

\mathbf{U}^{\prime}
on

Lemma S6

(Incoherence transfer)
.

Let
(\mathbf{L}^{\prime},\mathbf{S}^{\prime})
be an optimal solution to (23), with SVD
\mathbf{L}^{\prime}=\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}
. Let
(\mathbf{L}_{0},\mathbf{S}_{0})
be the model-induced pair, that is,
P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{L}_{0}=\mathbf{L}_{0}
,
P_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{S}_{0}=\mathbf{S}_{0}
and
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{L}_{0})=r
, with SVD
\mathbf{L}_{0}=\mathbf{U}_{0}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}\mathbf{V}_{0}^{\top}
. Then
\|P_{g}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}\leq\|P_{g}\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}
for every
g\in\mathcal{I}^{c}
.
In particular, if (Block-IC) holds for
\mathbf{U}_{0}
, then

\max_{g\in\mathcal{I}^{c}}\|P_{g}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}\leq\frac{\mu r}{G}.

Proof of Lemma
S6
.

By Lemma
S2
, there exists
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{M}
such that

\mathbf{L}^{\prime}=\mathbf{L}_{0}+\mathbf{M},\qquad\mathbf{S}^{\prime}=\mathbf{S}_{0}-\mathbf{M}.

Since
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}}
, we have
P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{0}
. Hence

P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{L}^{\prime}=P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\mathbf{L}_{0}+\mathbf{M})=P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{L}_{0}=\mathbf{L}_{0}.

Moreover,
\mathbf{L}^{\prime}=\mathbf{L}^{\prime}P_{\mathbf{A}}
and Condition (12) implies
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})\leq\mathrm{rank}(P_{\mathbf{A}})=r
, while

\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})\geq\mathrm{rank}(P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{L}^{\prime})=\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{L}_{0})=r.

Therefore
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})=r
.

Now, write the compact SVDs

\mathbf{L}^{\prime}=\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top},\qquad\mathbf{L}_{0}=\mathbf{U}_{0}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}\mathbf{V}_{0}^{\top},

so we have

(P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime})\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}=P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{L}^{\prime}=\mathbf{L}_{0}.

Since
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})=r
, the matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}
has full row rank
r
, so there exists a matrix
\mathbf{H}
satisfying
(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top})\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{I}_{r}
. Consequently,

P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}=(P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime})(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top})\mathbf{H}=(P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top})\mathbf{H},

which yields

\operatorname{col}(P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime})=\operatorname{col}(P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top})=\operatorname{col}(\mathbf{L}_{0})=\operatorname{col}(\mathbf{U}_{0}).

Thus,

P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}=\mathbf{U}_{0}\mathbf{U}_{0}^{\top}(P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime})

because
\mathbf{U}_{0}\mathbf{U}_{0}^{\top}
is the orthogonal projector onto
\operatorname{col}(\mathbf{U}_{0})
.

Now fix
g\in\mathcal{I}^{c}
. Since
P_{g}P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}=P_{g}
,

\displaystyle P_{g}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=P_{g}(P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=P_{g}(\mathbf{U}_{0}\mathbf{U}_{0}^{\top}P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=P_{g}(\mathbf{U}_{0})(\mathbf{U}_{0}^{\top}P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime})

Since

(\mathbf{U}_{0}^{\top}P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime})^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{0}^{\top}P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}=\mathbf{U}^{\prime\top}P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}=\mathbf{I}_{r}-\mathbf{U}^{\prime\top}P_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\preceq\mathbf{I}_{r},

we have
\|\mathbf{U}_{0}^{\top}P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
.
Therefore,

\|P_{g}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}\leq\|P_{g}\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}\|\mathbf{U}_{0}^{\top}P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|P_{g}\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}.

Taking the maximum over
g\in\mathcal{I}^{c}
and using (Block-IC) gives

\max_{g\in\mathcal{I}^{c}}\|P_{g}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}\leq\frac{\mu r}{G}.

∎

Lemma S7
.

Let
(\mathbf{L}^{\prime},\mathbf{S}^{\prime})
be an optimal solution to (23), with compact SVD
\mathbf{L}^{\prime}=\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}
.
Define
\rho^{\prime}:=\|P_{\mathcal{I}}P_{\mathbf{U}^{\prime}}P_{\mathcal{I}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
.
Then

\rho^{\prime}\leq\frac{\lambda_{S}}{\lambda_{L}}\sqrt{s}.

In particular, if

\frac{\lambda_{S}}{\lambda_{L}}\leq a\frac{1-\rho^{\prime}}{\sqrt{s}}

for some
a\in(0,1)
, then

\rho^{\prime}\leq\frac{a}{1+a}<1.

Proof of Lemma
S7
.

By Lemma
S3
, there exists
\mathbf{H}\in\partial\|\mathbf{S}^{\prime}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}
such that

\lambda_{L}P_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}=\lambda_{S}P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{H}P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime}}).

Right-multiplying by
\mathbf{V}^{\prime}
and then left-multiplying by
(\mathbf{U}^{\prime})^{\top}
gives

\lambda_{L}(\mathbf{U}^{\prime})^{\top}P_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}=\lambda_{S}(\mathbf{U}^{\prime})^{\top}P_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{H}P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime}}.

Taking operator norms and using
\|\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\|P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}=1
, we obtain

\rho^{\prime}=\|(\mathbf{U}^{\prime})^{\top}P_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{\lambda_{S}}{\lambda_{L}}\|(\mathbf{U}^{\prime})^{\top}P_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{H}P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{\lambda_{S}}{\lambda_{L}}\|P_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{H}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{\lambda_{S}}{\lambda_{L}}\|P_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{H}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}.

Because
\mathbf{H}\in\partial\|\mathbf{S}^{\prime}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}
, each block of
P_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{H}
has Frobenius norm at most
1
, so

\|P_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{H}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}\leq\sum_{g\in\mathcal{I}}1=s.

This proves the first claim.
If the displayed upper bound on
\lambda_{S}/\lambda_{L}
holds, then

\rho^{\prime}\leq\frac{\lambda_{S}}{\lambda_{L}}\sqrt{s}\leq a(1-\rho^{\prime}),

hence
(1+a)\rho^{\prime}\leq a
, which yields the second claim.

Lemma S8
.

Let
(\mathbf{L}^{\prime},\mathbf{S}^{\prime})
be an optimal solution to (23),with compact SVD
\mathbf{L}^{\prime}=\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}

Let

Under
\delta=0

\|(\mathbf{I}-P_{\mathcal{I}}P_{\mathbf{U}^{\prime}}P_{\mathcal{I}})^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{1}{1-\rho^{\prime}}.

Proof of Lemma
S8
.

Note that
\mathbf{D}_{0}=\mathbf{L}^{\prime}+\mathbf{S}^{\prime}=\mathbf{L}_{0}+\mathbf{S}_{0}
.
Since
P_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{S}^{\prime}=\mathbf{S}^{\prime}
implies
P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{S}^{\prime}=0

P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{L}^{\prime}=P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\mathbf{D}_{0}-\mathbf{S}^{\prime})=P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{D}_{0}

leads to
P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{D}_{0}=P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{L}_{0}=\mathbf{L}_{0}

By assumption and Lemma
S1

, so

A^{\top}A=(\mathbf{U}^{\prime})^{\top}P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\succ 0.

Therefore,

The next proposition ensures the existence of the dual certificate used in the proof of Theorem 1.

Proposition S1
.

Suppose the noiseless decomposition
\mathbf{D}_{0}=\mathbf{L}_{0}+\mathbf{S}_{0}
satisfies Assumptions 1–2 of the main paper.Let
(\mathbf{L}^{\prime},\mathbf{S}^{\prime})
be any optimal solution of the oracle problem (23), with compact SVD
\mathbf{L}^{\prime}=\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}
. Let
a,b\in(0,1)
satisfy
\sqrt{\mu rs/G}<ab/(1+a)^{2}
.
If

\frac{\lambda_{S}}{\lambda_{L}}\in\biggl(\frac{\sqrt{\mu r/G}}{b-(1+a)\sqrt{\mu rs/G}},\ \frac{a}{(1+a)\sqrt{s}}\biggr),

then there exists a matrix
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{Gq\times p}
satisfying (
S1.3
).

Proof of Proposition
S1
.

Define
\rho^{\prime}:=\|P_{\mathcal{I}}P_{\mathbf{U}^{\prime}}P_{\mathcal{I}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
and set

x:=\sqrt{\frac{\mu rs}{G}},\qquad\lambda:=\frac{\lambda_{S}}{\lambda_{L}}.

By Lemma
S7
and the assumed upper bound on
\lambda
, we have

\rho^{\prime}\leq\lambda\sqrt{s}<\frac{a}{1+a}.

Hence,

1-\rho^{\prime}>\frac{1}{1+a}.

Under the present noiseless setup, Lemma
S8
implies that
(\mathbf{I}-P_{\mathcal{I}}P_{\mathbf{U}^{\prime}}P_{\mathcal{I}})
is invertible on
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}}
, and further

\|(\mathbf{I}-P_{\mathcal{I}}P_{\mathbf{U}^{\prime}}P_{\mathcal{I}})^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{1}{1-\rho^{\prime}}<1+a.

Now we construct
\mathbf{Q}
in the form

\mathbf{Q}:=\lambda_{L}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}+P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}(\mathbf{Z}),

for some
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{Gq\times p}
satisfying
P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{Z})=\mathbf{Z}
.
By construction,

P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})}(\mathbf{Q})=\lambda_{L}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}

is satisfied.
Moreover,

P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}\mathbf{Z}=P_{\mathbf{U}^{\prime\perp}}\mathbf{Z}P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime\perp}}=P_{\mathbf{U}^{\prime\perp}}\mathbf{Z}-P_{\mathbf{U}^{\prime\perp}}\mathbf{Z}P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime}}.

Hence, once
\mathbf{Z}P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime}}=0
, we have
P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}\mathbf{Z}=P_{\mathbf{U}^{\prime\perp}}\mathbf{Z}
.

By Lemma
S3
, there exists
\mathbf{H}\in\partial\|\mathbf{S}^{\prime}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}
on
\mathcal{I}
such that

\lambda_{L}P_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}=\lambda_{S}P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{H}P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime}}).

Define

\mathbf{R}:=\lambda_{S}P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{H})-\lambda_{L}P_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}=\lambda_{S}P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{H}P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime\perp}}),

then
P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{R})=\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{R}P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime}}=\mathbf{0}
.
We therefore set

\mathbf{Z}:=(\mathbf{I}-P_{\mathcal{I}}P_{\mathbf{U}^{\prime}}P_{\mathcal{I}})^{-1}\mathbf{R}.

(S1.4)

Since the inverse operator acts only on the left, it does not change the nullspace of
\mathbf{R}
. So
\mathbf{R}P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime}}=\mathbf{0}
implies that
\mathbf{Z}P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime}}=\mathbf{0}
.
Thus indeed
P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}\mathbf{Z}=P_{\mathbf{U}^{\prime\perp}}\mathbf{Z}
.
Substituting (
S1.4
) into

\displaystyle P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{Q})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{L}P_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}+P_{\mathcal{I}}P_{\mathbf{U}^{\prime\perp}}\mathbf{Z}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{L}P_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}+(\mathbf{I}-P_{\mathcal{I}}P_{\mathbf{U}^{\prime}}P_{\mathcal{I}})\mathbf{Z}

yields

P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{Q})=\lambda_{S}P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{H}).

Because
\mathbf{H}\in\partial\|\mathbf{S}^{\prime}\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},1}
, we have
P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{H})\in{\mathrm{Sign}}_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{S}^{\prime})
, and hence the third condition in (
S1.3
) hold.

Next we verify the bound on
P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}(\mathbf{Q})
.
Since
P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}(\mathbf{Q})=P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}(\mathbf{Z})
,

\displaystyle\|P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}(\mathbf{Q})\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\|P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}(\mathbf{Z})\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{Z}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\mathbf{I}-P_{\mathcal{I}}P_{\mathbf{U}^{\prime}}P_{\mathcal{I}})^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|\mathbf{R}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}.

Also, each block of
P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{H})
has Frobenius norm at most
1
, so

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{R}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{S}\|P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{H}P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime\perp}})\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{S}\|P_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{H})\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{S}\sqrt{s},

and

\|\mathbf{Z}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}\leq(1+a)\lambda_{S}\sqrt{s}.

(S1.5)

Therefore

\|P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}(\mathbf{Q})\|_{\mathrm{op}}<(1+a)\lambda_{S}\sqrt{s}<a\lambda_{L},

where the last inequality is exactly the assumed upper bound on
\lambda
. This proves the second condition in (
S1.3
).

Finally, we bound
\|P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\mathbf{Q})\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},\infty}
.
Let

\gamma:=\max_{g\in\mathcal{I}^{c}}\|P_{g}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}.

For any
g\in\mathcal{I}^{c}
, since
P_{g}\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{0}
,

\displaystyle P_{g}\mathbf{Q}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{L}P_{g}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}+P_{g}P_{\mathbf{U}^{\prime\perp}}\mathbf{Z}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{L}P_{g}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}-P_{g}P_{\mathbf{U}^{\prime}}\mathbf{Z}.

Hence

\displaystyle\|P_{g}\mathbf{Q}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{L}\|P_{g}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}+\|P_{g}P_{\mathbf{U}^{\prime}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|\mathbf{Z}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\bigl(\lambda_{L}+\|\mathbf{Z}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}\bigr).

Using the bound (
S1.5
), we obtain

\|P_{g}\mathbf{Q}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}<\gamma\lambda_{L}+\gamma(1+a)\lambda_{S}\sqrt{s}.

Thus it suffices to show

\gamma\lambda_{L}+\gamma(1+a)\lambda_{S}\sqrt{s}<b\lambda_{S},

equivalently,

\gamma\lambda_{L}<\lambda_{S}[b-\gamma(1+a)\sqrt{s}].

By Lemma
S6
,
\gamma\leq\sqrt{\mu r/G}=x/\sqrt{s}
.
Since

x<\frac{ab}{(1+a)^{2}}<\frac{b}{1+a},

the term
b-(1+a)x
is positive.
Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{b-(1+a)\gamma\sqrt{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{x/\sqrt{s}}{b-(1+a)x}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\mu r/G}}{b-(1+a)\sqrt{\mu rs/G}}.

The assumed lower bound on
\lambda
now yields

\lambda>\frac{\gamma}{b-(1+a)\gamma\sqrt{s}},

which is equivalent to

\gamma\lambda_{L}+\gamma(1+a)\lambda_{S}\sqrt{s}<b\lambda_{S}.

Consequently,
\|P_{g}\mathbf{Q}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}<b\lambda_{S}
for every
g\in\mathcal{I}^{c}
, and hence

\|P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\mathbf{Q})\|_{{\mathrm{blk}},\infty}\leq b\lambda_{S}.

We have verified all four conditions in (
S1.3
), so the proof is complete.
∎

Proof of Theorem 1

(\mathbf{L}^{\prime},\mathbf{S}^{\prime})

P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})=0,\quad P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})=0.

From
P_{\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})^{\perp}}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})=0
, we know
\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\mathcal{T}
, so
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
can be written as

\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}^{\top}+\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}
for some
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
.
Now multiply by
P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime\perp}}
:

\boldsymbol{\Delta}P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime\perp}}=\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime\perp}}\in\mathcal{U}^{\prime},

where
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}:=\{\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mid\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}\}
.
The condition
P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})=0
implies that
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
is supported only on blocks in
\mathcal{I}
, so
\boldsymbol{\Delta}P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime\perp}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}}
.
Hence

\boldsymbol{\Delta}P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime\perp}}\in\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}}.

Let
\rho^{\prime}:=\|P_{\mathcal{I}}P_{\mathbf{U}^{\prime}}P_{\mathcal{I}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
. By Lemma
S8
,
\rho^{\prime}<1
. Applying the same argument as in Lemma 1 of the main paper with
P_{\mathbf{U}}
replaced by
P_{\mathbf{U}^{\prime}}
, we obtain

\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}}=\{0\}.

Thus

\boldsymbol{\Delta}P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime\perp}}=0,

which further leads to
\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\boldsymbol{\Delta}P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime}}
.
Therefore,

\widehat{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{L}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}=(\mathbf{L}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\Delta})P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime}}=\widehat{\mathbf{L}}P_{\mathbf{V}^{\prime}},

so

\mathrm{row}(\widehat{\mathbf{L}})\subseteq\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{V}^{\prime})=\mathrm{row}(\mathbf{L}^{\prime})=\mathrm{row}(\mathbf{A})

(S1.6)

On the other hand, using the fact that
P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\mathbf{S}^{\prime})=\mathbf{0}
and
P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\boldsymbol{\Delta})=\mathbf{0}
, we have

P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\widehat{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{0}.

By feasibility, we further have

P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\widehat{\mathbf{L}})=P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}(\mathbf{D}_{0})=\mathbf{L}_{0}.

Since
\mathbf{L}_{0}=\mathbf{L}_{0}P_{\mathbf{A}}
and Condition (12) gives
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{L}_{0})=r
, we have
\mathrm{row}(\mathbf{L}_{0})=\mathrm{row}(\mathbf{A})
.
Hence

\mathrm{row}(\mathbf{A})=\mathrm{row}(\mathbf{L}_{0})=\mathrm{row}(P_{\mathcal{I}^{c}}\widehat{\mathbf{L}})\subseteq\mathrm{row}(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}).

(S1.7)

Combine (
S1.6
) and (
S1.7
), we have

\mathrm{row}(\widehat{\mathbf{L}})=\mathrm{row}(\mathbf{A}).

Therefore, for any compact SVD
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}
,

P_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}=P_{\mathbf{A}}.

This holds for every optimizer, thus
P_{\widehat{\mathbf{A}}}
is unique.
∎

Lemma S9
.

|\mathcal{C}|=\eta K
for some
\eta\in(0,1]

For
k\in\mathcal{C}

Proof of Lemma
S9
.

Define the stacked matrix
\mathbf{C}_{\mathcal{C}}
by stacking
\mathbf{B}^{(j)}-\mathbf{B}^{(k)}

\mathbf{L}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{0}=\mathbf{L}_{\mathcal{C}}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{\mathcal{C}}

.

S2

\mathbf{L}^{\prime}=\mathbf{L}_{0}+\mathbf{M}

Since
\mathbf{L}_{0}

Lemma S11
.

Suppose
\mathbf{E}_{1}
admits the blockwise representation
\mathbf{E}_{1}^{(j,k)}=\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}
for
(j,k)\in\mathcal{G}
, where
\mathcal{G}:=\{(j,k):1\leq j<k\leq K\}
,
G:=|\mathcal{G}|
, and
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p}
.
Then,

\|\mathbf{E}_{1}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}=K\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}-\Bigl\|\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\Bigr\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}.

In particular,

\|\mathbf{E}_{1}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}\leq K\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}.

Proof of Lemma
S11
.

By the definition of the stacked pairwise error matrix,

\|\mathbf{E}_{1}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}=\sum_{(j,k)\in\mathcal{G}}\|\mathbf{E}_{1}^{(j,k)}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}=\sum_{j<k}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}.

Expanding the square gives

\sum_{j<k}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}=\sum_{j<k}\Bigl(\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}-2\langle\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j},\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\rangle\Bigr).

For the diagonal part, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j<k}\bigl(\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bigl(\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}\bigr){\mathrm{1}}\{j<k\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}\cdot\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\mathrm{1}}\{j<k\}\Bigr)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}\cdot\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{K}{\mathrm{1}}\{j<k\}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{K}(K-j)\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}(k-1)\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=(K-1)\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}.

For the cross term, use

\Bigl\|\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\Bigr\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}+2\sum_{j<k}\langle\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j},\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\rangle.

Equivalently,

2\sum_{j<k}\langle\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j},\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\rangle=\Bigl\|\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\Bigr\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}.

Substituting these two identities into the expansion yields

\sum_{j<k}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}=(K-1)\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}-\Bigl(\Bigl\|\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\Bigr\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}\Bigr),

that is,

\|\mathbf{E}_{1}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}=K\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}-\Bigl\|\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\Bigr\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}.

The inequality follows by dropping the nonnegative term

\bigl\|\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\bigr\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}
.
∎

Lemma S12
.

Under the representation in Lemma
S11
and Assumption 5 of the main paper, there exist absolute constants
c,C>0
such that, for every
k\in[K]
,

\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}\leq c\,pq\,\tau_{k}^{2},

and, for any
t>0
,

\|\mathbf{E}_{1}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}\leq C\,pq\,\tau^{2}\bigl(K^{2}+K^{3/2}\sqrt{t}+Kt\bigr)

with probability at least
1-e^{-t}
.

Proof of Lemma
S12
.

By Lemma
S11
, we have

\|\mathbf{E}_{1}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}\leq K\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}.

Now define

Z_{k}:=\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2},

then
Z_{k}
’s are independent zero mean random variables.
It suffices to seek a tight upper bound fort

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}Z_{k}.

We first bound the expectation term
\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}
.
Let

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}:={\mathrm{vec}}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{pq}

and define

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}:={\mathrm{cov}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k})=\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}^{\top}),

where the second equality uses
\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}=\mathbf{0}
.
By Assumption 5, for every
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{pq}
with
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}=1
,

\|\langle\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}\rangle\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\tau_{k}.

Using the standard sub-Gaussian moment bound, we obtain

\mathbf{u}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}\mathbf{u}=\mathbb{E}\langle\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}\rangle^{2}\leq c\tau_{k}^{2}

for every unit
\mathbf{u}
and some constant
c>0
, which implies

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}\preceq c\tau_{k}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{pq}.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}=\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})\leq cpq\,\tau_{k}^{2}.

To bound the stochastic deviation term, note that Assumption 5 with a standard
\varepsilon
-net argument for sub-Gaussian vectors yields

\bigl\|\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}\|_{2}\bigr\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq c_{2}\sqrt{pq}\tau_{k}

for constant
c_{2}>0
.
Therefore, using the standard bound
\|X^{2}\|_{\psi_{1}}\lesssim\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}
for scalar
X
,

\bigl\|\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr\|_{\psi_{1}}\leq c_{3}pq\tau_{k}^{2}

for some
c_{3}>0
.
Since
\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}\|_{2}=\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\|Z_{k}\|_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle=\bigl\|\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{F}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{F}^{2}\bigr\|_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\bigl\|\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr\|_{\psi_{1}}+\|\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{F}^{2}\|_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\bigl\|\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr\|_{\psi_{1}}+c_{4}\bigl\|\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr\|_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{5}pq\tau_{k}^{2},

where
c_{4},c_{5}>0
are constants.
Thus,
Z_{k}
are sub-Exponential.
Employing Bernstein inequality leads to

\sum_{k=1}^{K}Z_{k}\leq c_{6}\Biggl(pq\sqrt{t\sum_{k=1}^{K}\tau_{k}^{4}}+pqt\max_{1\leq k\leq K}\tau_{k}^{2}\Biggr),

with probability at least
1-e^{-t}
.

Combining the expectation term and stochastic term yields

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}cpq\tau_{k}^{2}+c_{6}\Biggl(pq\sqrt{t\sum_{k=1}^{K}\tau_{k}^{4}}+pqt\max_{1\leq k\leq K}\tau_{k}^{2}\Biggr)

\displaystyle\leq Cpq\tau^{2}(K+\sqrt{tK}+t)

for some constant
C>0
, where the last inequality employs the uniform bound
\tau_{k}\leq\tau
.
Therefore,

\|\mathbf{E}_{1}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}\leq K\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}\leq Cpq\tau^{2}(K^{2}+\sqrt{t}K^{3/2}+tK)

with probability at least
1-e^{-t}
.
∎

Lemma S14
.

Under the representation in Lemma
S11

C>0

.

S12
, we leverage the client-wise decomposition of
\mathbf{E}_{1}
through the independent mean-zero matrices
\{\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}

By linearity of the Frobenius inner product,

\langle\mathbf{A},\mathbf{E}_{1}\rangle=\sum_{(j,k)\in\mathcal{G}}\langle\mathbf{A}^{(j,k)},\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{k}\rangle.

Rewrite the sum by collecting the terms multiplying the same

\displaystyle=2(K-1)\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\|\mathbf{A}^{(g)}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}

(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
.
So it suffices to bound
1-\phi_{k}\theta_{k}
and
\varphi_{k}
separately.

Let
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j}:={\mathrm{vec}}(\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j})
.
Since
P_{\mathbf{A}}
and
P_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}
are orthogonal projectors, vectorization gives

\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j}P_{\mathbf{A}}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j}^{\top}(P_{\mathbf{A}}\otimes I_{q})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j},\qquad\|\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{j}P_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j}^{\top}(P_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}\otimes I_{q})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j}.

By Assumption 5,
\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j}=\mathbf{0}
. Hence

\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}}^{(j)}=\mathrm{tr}\{(P_{\mathbf{A}}\otimes I_{q})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j}\},\qquad\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(j)}=\mathrm{tr}\{(P_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}\otimes I_{q})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j}\}.

The projectors
P_{\mathbf{A}}\otimes I_{q}
and
P_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}\otimes I_{q}
have ranks
qr
and
q(p-r)
, respectively. Assumption 6 of the main paper therefore implies, for every
j\in\mathcal{C}
,

\kappa_{-}qr\tau^{2}\leq\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}}^{(j)}\leq\kappa_{+}qr\tau^{2},

and

\kappa_{-}q(p-r)\tau^{2}\leq\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(j)}\leq\kappa_{+}q(p-r)\tau^{2}.

In particular,
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(k)}>0
and

\kappa_{-}qp\tau^{2}\leq\mathcal{E}^{(k)}=\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}}^{(k)}+\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(k)}\leq\kappa_{+}qp\tau^{2}.

Using the notation

\theta_{k}=\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(k)}}{\mathcal{E}^{(k)}},\quad\phi_{k}=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{(k)}}{\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(k)}},\quad\varphi_{k}=\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{cost}}^{(k)}}{\mathcal{E}^{(k)}},

we have

1-\theta_{k}=\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}}^{(k)}}{\mathcal{E}^{(k)}}\leq\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}}^{(k)}}{\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(k)}}\leq\frac{\kappa_{+}}{\kappa_{-}}\frac{r}{p-r},

and

1-\phi_{k}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(k)}(\eta K)^{2}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}^{(j)}\leq\frac{\kappa_{+}}{\kappa_{-}}\frac{1}{\eta K}.

Thus, for all sufficiently large
K
,

1-\phi_{k}\theta_{k}=(1-\theta_{k})+\theta_{k}(1-\phi_{k})\leq C_{1}\left(\frac{r}{p-r}+\frac{1}{\eta K}\right)

(S4.9)

for a constant
C_{1}>0
depending only on
\kappa_{+}/\kappa_{-}
.

We next control the non-oracle cost
\varphi_{k}
. Lemma
S20
, together with
\delta=O(K^{-1/2})
and
B=O(\sqrt{qr})
, gives

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{cost}}^{(k)}\leq C_{2}\left\{\frac{qr}{K}+\frac{qr+\tau^{2}pq}{K}\right\}

for a constant
C_{2}>0
.
Dividing by the lower bound
\mathcal{E}^{(k)}\geq\kappa_{-}qp\tau^{2}
yields

\varphi_{k}\leq C_{3}\left\{\frac{r}{pK\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{K}\right\}.

Using
K\tau^{2}\asymp 1
and
\eta\leq 1
, this becomes

\varphi_{k}\leq C_{4}\left(\frac{r}{p-r}+\frac{1}{\eta K}\right),

(S4.10)

after enlarging
C_{4}
.

Combining (
S4.9
) and (
S4.10
), and (
S4.8
), we have

\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}-\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}\leq\Bigl(\frac{c_{0}}{\eta K}+\frac{c_{1}r}{p-r}\Bigr)\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{(k)}-\mathbf{W}^{(k)}\|_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}

for constants
c_{0},c_{1}>0
.

It remains to justify that Condition (34) in the main text, or equivalently,

\sqrt{1-\phi_{k}\theta_{k}}+\sqrt{\varphi_{k}}<\sqrt{1-\zeta_{k}\theta_{k}},

(S4.11)

is automatically satisfiable.
By (
S4.9
) and (
S4.10
), we have
\sqrt{1-\phi_{k}\theta_{k}}+\sqrt{\varphi_{k}}=o(1)
when
K\to\infty
and
r/(p-r)\to 0
.
For all sufficiently large
K
, set
\zeta_{k}:=(1-C_{k})/\theta_{k}
for any
(\sqrt{1-\phi_{k}\theta_{k}}+\sqrt{\varphi_{k}})^{2}<C_{k}<1
, then Condition (
S4.11
) immediately holds.
Since
C_{k}>1-\phi_{k}\theta_{k}
, we have
0<\zeta_{k}<\phi_{k}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

S5
Additional details for the sequence-copying experiment

This section provides additional details on the sequence-copying experiment in Section 8 of the main paper.

Copying tasks
. All sequences have length
T=64
and are defined over a vocabulary of size
53
, consisting of
52
letter tokens and one padding token. Given a sampled segment
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{L})
, the clean-copying task places two adjacent copies of
u
in the sequence and pads the remaining positions. The fuzzy-copying task instead embeds the two copies in sampled background tokens; the three background lengths before, between, and after the copies are determined by sampling two distinct cut points among the available non-copy positions.

The model is trained autoregressively by next-token prediction from prefixes. Following
[
29
]
, evaluation uses masked next-token accuracy: positions in the first copy and the first three positions of the second copy are excluded, so the reported accuracy focuses on retrieval after a short burn-in period. In the homogeneous regime, evaluation is conducted on the global noisy-long distribution; in the heterogeneous regime, each benign client is evaluated on its own distribution. All results are averaged over
100
Monte Carlo replicates.

Backbone pretraining and local LoRA fine-tuning
. The common backbone is a Transformer with two self-attention blocks, hidden dimension
64
, one attention head per block, rotary positional encoding, residual connections, and layer normalization. It is pretrained for
5500
Adam steps on a balanced mixture of fuzzy-copying tasks with
L\in\{5,\ldots,15\}
, power-law exponent
1.1
, batch size
64
, and learning rate
0.001
. The pretrained backbone is then frozen throughout the federated fine-tuning experiments.

In the local fine-tuning stage, each client inserts rank-
3
LoRA adapters into the eight attention projections
\{\mathbf{W}_{q},\mathbf{W}_{k},\mathbf{W}_{v},\mathbf{W}_{o}\}
from the two Transformer blocks and into the final output layer. The LoRA
\mathbf{A}
factors are initialized from the same seed across clients, and only LoRA parameters are updated locally. Each client trains on
2000
generated sequences for one epoch, using batch size
50
, learning rate
0.001
, LoRA scale
16
, and dropout
0.005
.

Federated settings
. In the homogeneous regime, all benign clients share the same fuzzy-copying distribution, with power-law exponent
1.1
and fixed segment length
L=16
. In the heterogeneous regime, each benign client’s power-law exponent is redrawn from
[0.95,1.6]
, and its fixed segment length is redrawn from
\{10,\ldots,26\}
in each replicate. In both regimes, the contaminated client contributes a model fine-tuned on a copying task mismatched to those of the benign clients.

In the refinement stage, aggregation is restricted to the eight attention matrices from the two Transformer blocks. The output layer is excluded from aggregation, although it is locally fine-tuned. CLAIR uses regularization parameters
(\lambda_{L},\lambda_{S})=(0.5,0.4)
in the homogeneous regime and
(0.5,0.2)
in the heterogeneous regime, with active-pair thresholds
0.5
and
0.01
, respectively.
