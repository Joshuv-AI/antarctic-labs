The Harrowing Status of Research and Medical Practice in Countries Affected by War
Authors: Gheorghe-Andrei Dan
Published: 2026

“When the rich wage war, it’s the poor who die” – Jean-Paul Sartre

The WHO states that health is a fundamental human right, and achieving the highest attainable standard of health requires action across social, economic and health sectors.1 The seven key principles governing the right of every human being to access health are: universal accessibility, equity, community participation, comprehensiveness and integration, affordability, intersectoral action and government responsibility.

For many decades, heart disease was considered to be mainly a problem of rich, industrialised countries. However, this picture has changed dramatically in recent decades. Today, the majority of deaths from heart disease occur in low- and middle-income countries, accounting for about 80% of all heart disease deaths worldwide.2 This shift has mainly taken place because of poverty caused by conflict or natural disasters. Poor diet, high levels of stress, lack of appropriate medical care, and the emergence of diseases that can damage the heart over time are among the major players contributing to cardiovascular disease and death in war-affected countries.

In public health, researchers use the term “epidemiological transition” (a term introduced by Omran in 1971) to describe the change a country undergoes whereby it moves from a situation where most citizens die from infections and malnutrition to one where they most often die from chronic diseases like heart disease and cancer.3 Many low-income countries nowadays face deaths from both infections and rising cardiovascular disease, and, very importantly, people in low-income countries die from heart disease at younger ages than people in high-income countries. The WHO estimates that more than three out of four premature deaths from heart disease happen in low- and middle-income countries, where there also is a striking disparity in cardiologist availability compared with developed countries.

A 2021 European Society of Cardiology report indicated that Western European countries typically have more than 60 cardiologists per million population, with some countries exceeding 80 per million.4 However, in sub-Saharan Africa, five countries (15%) lack a single trained cardiologist, and overall, the density of cardiologists is extremely low, with many countries having fewer than one cardiologist per million population.5

Sudan offers one of the most distressing examples of this problem. The country has many people suffering from heart conditions, yet it has very few cardiologists, limited hospital equipment and almost no capacity to perform heart surgery outside of the capital city. When a new wave of armed conflict broke out in April 2023, the situation became catastrophic. Hospitals were damaged or closed, doctors fled, and millions of people were forced to leave their homes, losing access to whatever medical care they had.

As of early 2024, the country has 6.8 million internally displaced people, surpassing Syria, and 1.5 million people have fled abroad. Nearly half the population (24.8 million people) require humanitarian assistance, while 18 million face acute food insecurity, including 4 million children under 5 suffering from malnutrition. The UN has described this as a “forgotten war” and one of the worst humanitarian disasters in recent history.6

In 1901, the first three civilian doctors arrived in Sudan, at a time when cardiovascular disease (CVD) was a rare occurrence.7 Afterwards, the burden of rheumatic fever was immense, particularly during the 1970s and 1980s, with very high morbidity and mortality rates. Hospital resources were over-stretched by chronic valvular disease admissions, and multivalvular disease contributed to nearly 25% of all rheumatic fever patient admissions. Hypertension was reported to affect fewer than 25% of the population; however, autopsy studies revealed hypertensive stigmata in the majority of patients with CVD.8

While ischaemic heart disease (IHD) was a rarity in Sudan at the beginning of the 20th century, recent reports have shown IHD now represents two-thirds of all hospitalised patients and is one of the leading causes of heart failure.7 A recent study showed that patients with heart failure in Africa were the youngest, illiterate, lacked health insurance and most likely to be in the most severe New York Heart Association class, compared with those from Asia, the Middle East and South America.9 Another recent study pointed to the fact that access to expensive and technical cardiac imaging is likely to remain limited in low- and middle-income countries.10

Sudan’s health system, already fragile, underfunded, understaffed and reliant on out-of-pocket payments, was severely jeopardised by the war. The conflict has dramatically worsened conditions: the WHO reports that 70% of healthcare facilities in war-affected areas had been forced to close by the end of 2023, and Federal Ministry of Health data confirms that more than 30% of public hospitals are no longer operational.6 After the war, new dimensions were added to the epidemiological picture. For communicable diseases, outbreaks of cholera, measles, dengue fever, chikungunya and rabies have surged due to displacement and collapsed sanitation. In the case of non-communicable diseases, treatment disruptions, medication shortages and power outages (e.g. affecting insulin storage) have worsened outcomes for chronic disease patients. Added to all of these, a dramatic increase in physical injuries and psychological trauma has further darkened the landscape.

In the current volume of European Cardiology Review, Eldisugi Hassan Mohammed Humida and Hussain Gadelkarim Ahmed present a systematic review describing the evolution of cardiovascular disease in Sudan, between 1970 and 2025.11 The authors should be congratulated for their careful, informative analysis, which reveals a picture dominated by difficulties in data gathering, limited diagnostic tools, reduced awareness regarding cardiovascular prevention and extremely limited healthcare resources.

Worryingly, the authors highlight the fact that CVD mortality is predicted to increase markedly by 2050, with IHD and stroke as key contributors. Despite the significant gap in data recording, as highlighted by Humida and Ahmed, the WHO reports that in 2020 Sudan experienced 43,966 fatalities attributed to CHD, accounting for 20.16% of all deaths in the country. The age-adjusted mortality rate is 231.30 per 100,000 individuals (20th worldwide).

Low-income, war-affected countries such as Sudan require international support to assist health services in their endeavours to comply with the basic principles of public health ethics.12 These include addressing the fundamental causes of disease and aiming to prevent adverse health outcomes in a way that respects the rights of individuals in the community.

Current health service inequalities in high-versus low-income, war-affected countries are Orwellian in character and a painful reminder of the disastrous consequences of war, causing long-term disruption to health systems by destroying infrastructure, decimating healthcare forces and triggering medicines shortages. The paper by Humida and Ahmed should act as a call to action to all stakeholders involved in the process.
