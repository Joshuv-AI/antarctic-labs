Time-Dependent Logarithmic Perturbation Theory for Quantum Dynamics: Formulation and Applications
J.C. del Valle 1, P. Bergold 2, K. Kropielnicka 3

Abstract
We present the time-dependent extension of logarithmic perturbation theory for non-relativistic quantum dynamics governed by the Schrödinger equation, in which the logarithm of the wave function is expanded in powers of a coupling constant. The resulting hierarchical equations for the perturbative corrections are defined via a gauge-rotated Hamiltonian of the unperturbed system and lead to closed-integral expressions for the time-dependent corrections based on Duhamel’s formula. This integral structure of the corrections, the hallmark of time-independent logarithmic perturbation theory, is preserved in the present extension. It provides, in particular, a computable expression for the instantaneous energy shifts. Furthermore, dynamic energy shifts arise naturally within this framework in the form of time-averaged expectation values of pseudopotentials and can be related, for example, to AC Stark shifts and electric polarizabilities. As an illustration, we apply the method to the harmonic oscillator and the hydrogen atom, both driven by a time-dependent laser field. The harmonic oscillator delivers a proof of principle for which the exact solution is recovered, while the hydrogen atom illustrates the method applied to an atomic system and its capability to provide analytical results. Supported by numerical simulations, we demonstrate the applicability to obtain relevant physical observables with high accuracy. The present approach offers a promising alternative for analytical studies of time-dependent multi-photon processes in the perturbative regime.



Introduction

In the non-relativistic regime, quantum dynamics is governed by the time-dependent Schrödinger equation (TDSE), which is a linear evolution problem.
In many situations, approximate yet accurate and physically relevant solutions can be calculated using time-dependent perturbation theory (TDPT), which represents a major tool for describing externally perturbed systems evolving under time-dependent interactions.
This approach is particularly effective when the coupling constant, which controls the strength of the interaction between the unperturbed system and the external perturbation, is
sufficiently small
.
For instance, in attosecond physics, second-order TDPT underlies the theoretical description of the interferometric technique known as
reconstruction of attosecond beating by interference of two-photon transitions
(RABBITT; see
33
), as well as its multi-sideband extensions
47
;
5
.
Within this framework, TDPT describes the photoelectron sideband oscillations, allowing one to extract the time delays that occur during photoionization.
In this context, the coupling constant is determined by the amplitudes of the infrared probe and the extreme-ultraviolet pump.

Standard TDPT is commonly formulated in terms of the
Dyson series
, as presented in standard quantum mechanics textbooks; see, e.g.,
39
;
40
;
26
.
Despite its wide success, the Dyson-series formulation becomes cumbersome at high orders due to explicit time ordering and the rapid growth of nested time integrals — a conceptual difficulty recognized long ago
7
.
Moreover, it offers limited scope for analytical treatments of the wave function, except for very specific perturbations (e.g., harmonic driving).
Furthermore, it typically involves infinite sums over the unperturbed spectrum of energies, whose accurate evaluation can be computationally expensive and whose convergence is not always guaranteed or is challenging to prove; see, e.g.,
41
.
A few alternative approaches to time-dependent problems are available in the literature, including
stationary perturbation theory

25
and
Floquet perturbation theory

18
;
38
.

In contrast to TDPT, there are several formulations of its time-independent counterpart, among which
Rayleigh–Schrödinger perturbation theory

26
is the textbook method par excellence.
A variety of alternative approaches have also been developed, including the methods of Dalgarno–Stewart
8
, Fernández and Castro
14
, Swenson and Danforth
42
, the
moment method

15
, and
logarithmic perturbation theory
(LPT)
1
;
2
, also known as the
non-linearization procedure

44
, among others.
We refer the interested reader to
13
for a detailed overview and discussion of the scope of the various formulations, where their advantages and limitations are also described.
Among these formulations, LPT stands out due to the following features, which arise from considering the logarithm of the wave function as the central quantity: (i) it provides corrections of arbitrary order for the energy in integral form; and (ii) the unperturbed wave function serves as the main ingredient for constructing the series of the perturbed wave function. Owing to these features,
LPT has been extensively used to study a wide range of quantum systems, including anharmonic oscillators
10
, screened Coulomb potentials
12
, the Stark
35
and Zeeman
9
effects in hydrogen, as well as various radial potentials
1
.
For a historical review of the method and its applications, we refer the reader to
4
.
To the best of the authors’ knowledge, a time-dependent extension of LPT that preserves features (i) and (ii) has not been developed so far.

In this paper, we formulate the time-dependent extension of LPT with the aim of facilitating analytical results for time-evolved wave functions, which is often outside the scope of standard TDPT.
This method, here referred to as
time-dependent logarithmic perturbation theory
TDLPT, is designed to address the time-evolved phase (here defined as the logarithm of the wave function) and offers a natural way to compute dynamic energy shifts induced by an arbitrary external time-dependent potential.
The proposed approach is well-suited to situations in which the initial condition of the TDSE corresponds to the ground state of the unperturbed Hamiltonian — a common physical scenario.
In addition to the formulation, we apply TDLPT to two physically relevant systems: (1) the one-dimensional harmonic oscillator; and (2) the hydrogen atom, both subjected to a linearly-polarized laser field.

In the case of the harmonic oscillator, we show that the exact solution of the TDSE can be fully recovered analytically within the TDLPT framework with only the first two correction terms, unlike in the standard formulation of TDPT.
Therefore, this application offers a proof of principle of the method.
In the case of the hydrogen atom, the analytical structure of the wave function at large distances can be established in the form of an asymptotic expansion.
In particular, we show that the correction terms to the wave function can be obtained by solving individual linear evolution problems, revealing that the time-evolved phase itself undergoes transitions that obey the standard selection rules.
We support this study with numerical solutions of the underlying evolution equations, which are then used to compute the AC energy shifts and the induced dipole moment.
For the latter, we benchmark against a numerical solution of the full TDSE.

The present paper is organized as follows.
In Section I, we state the problem under consideration.
In Section II, we establish the theoretical framework of the TDLPT.
This includes the derivation of the equations that dictate the evolution of the correction terms and their expectation values.
Finally, it describes the connection to LPT and dynamic energy shifts.
We then apply our method to the harmonic oscillator and the hydrogen atom in Section III.
Conclusions are presented in Section IV.

Supporting mathematical calculations and proofs are provided in the Appendices.
Atomic units (
\hbar=m_{e}=e=4\pi\varepsilon_{0}=1
) are used throughout the paper unless stated otherwise.

I
Generalities

We consider the linear TDSE

\begin{cases}\mathrm{i}\,\partial_{t}\psi(x,t)=\hat{H}(t)\,\psi(x,t),\quad x\in\Omega,\quad t\geq 0,\\
\psi(x,0)=\psi_{0}(x),\end{cases}

(I.1)

where the domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is either bounded (thus, (
I.1
) is equipped with suitable boundary conditions) or unbounded, and
\psi_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is a given initial condition.
Moreover, we assume that
\hat{H}(t)
is a time-dependent Hermitian operator, which can be written as the sum of a time-independent and a time-dependent component:

\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{\text{int}}(t).

(I.2)

In practice,
\hat{H}_{0}
is the free-system Hamiltonian taking the form

\hat{H}_{0}=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+V_{0},\qquad\Delta=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{i}}^{2},

(I.3)

where the potential
V_{0}\colon{\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}}
is a real-valued function that acts as a multiplication operator.
As in many physical situations, we assume the initial condition to be the lowest normalized eigenfunction of
\hat{H}_{0}
, i.e.,

\hat{H}_{0}\,\psi_{0}=E_{0}\,\psi_{0},\qquad\int_{\Omega}|\psi_{0}(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=1,

(I.4)

where
E_{0}
denotes the corresponding lowest energy.
Therefore,
\psi_{0}
represents the wave function of the ground state, which in particular is nodeless inside
\Omega
and can always be assumed to be real-valued
36
.

Finally, the time-dependent component of the Hamiltonian is assumed to have the form

\hat{H}_{\text{int}}(t)=\lambda\,V_{\text{int}}(x,t),

(I.5)

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is
small
and is referred to as the coupling constant.
Typically, the real-valued time-dependent potential
V_{\text{int}}
arises from the interaction of the system with an external field.

In most situations of practical interest, the TDSE (
I.1
) cannot be solved analytically, and one must therefore resort to numerical approximations.
Owing to the formidable challenges posed by this equation, the development of efficient and accurate numerical methods for approximating solutions of the TDSE remains an active area of research
3
;
24
.
The development of combined analytical and numerical results is valuable since it sheds light on the underlying physical processes that purely numerical solutions of the TDSE may overlook.

Taking advantage of the smallness of the coupling constant, perturbation theory stands out as a well-suited methodology that may provide results of analytical nature.
Hence, in the spirit of standard LPT, here we will commence with an exponential
ansatz
of the solution to (
I.1
).
As we will show, this consideration leads to various analytical results which have not been reported in the literature so far; for example, closed formulas of the corresponding correction terms and dynamic energy shifts, both in integral form.

Following the scheme of perturbation theory, we construct a solution to (

Q_{1}(x,t):=V_{\text{int}}(x,t),\qquad Q_{n}(x,t):=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\nabla\Phi_{n-k}(x,t)\cdot\nabla\Phi_{k}(x,t).

(II.11)

\psi(x,t)=\sqrt{\rho(x,t)}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}S(x,t)}

Additionally, if one equips (

16

28

(i) In our formulation, the expansion parameter is the coupling constant
\lambda

The latter is sometimes identified as the semiclassical parameter in the mathematical literature; see, e.g.,

29

(ii) In contrast to (

Finally, we point out that there are also similarities with the formulation of TDPT presented by Chung

II.1
The
n
-th Order Correction

For each
n>0
, equation (
II.9
) together with the initial condition (
II.11
) can be written in the form of an ordinary differential equation

\mathrm{i}\,\partial_{t}u(t)=\hat{\mathcal{L}}\,u(t)+f(t),\quad u(0)=0,

(II.14)

where, in our specific setup,
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
is a linear differential operator given by

\hat{\mathcal{L}}:=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta+2\nabla\Phi_{0}\cdot\nabla\right)\quad\text{and}\quad f(t):=Q_{n}(x,t).

(II.15)

As shown in Appendix
A

\hat{\mathcal{L}}
is the generator of a
C_{0}

\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\hat{\mathcal{L}}}
which takes the form of a
gauge rotation
of the semigroup generated by the shifted free Hamiltonian (
\hat{H}_{0}-E_{0}
), and plays the role of the propagator.
Explicitly, we have

\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\hat{\mathcal{L}}}=\mathrm{e}^{\phi_{0}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\,(\hat{H}_{0}-E_{0})}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\phi_{0}}.

(II.16)

Hence, a direct application of

;
21
— in the literature also known as the
variation-of-constants formula

— yields the following closed integral representation for the correction terms:

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\,t\mathcal{L}}\,\Phi_{n}(x,0)-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(t-s)\,\hat{\mathcal{L}}}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

(II.17)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\phi_{0}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(t-s)\,(\hat{H}_{0}-E_{0})}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\phi_{0}}Q_{n}(x,s)\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad n\geq 1,

(II.18)

where in the last line the initial condition (
II.11

The availability of an integral representation for the

In contrast, standard TDPT is formulated in terms of the Dyson series, with the relation between these two representations discussed in Appendix

II.2
Evolution Equation in Expectation-Value Form

In this subsection, we derive the following evolution equation for the expectation values of the correction terms and the pseudopotentials
Q_{n}
:

\mathrm{i}\,\partial_{t}\langle\Phi_{n}(x,t)\rangle_{\psi_{0}}=\langle Q_{n}(x,t)\rangle_{\psi_{0}},\quad n\geq 1.

(II.19)

As we will show, this equation is independent of the imposed initial conditions for
\Phi_{n}
and leads to the physical condition of the absence of particle current at the boundary of
\Omega
.
Furthermore, it is essential for connecting the time-dependent perturbation theory with the time-independent case.

To show (
II.19
), we multiply both sides of the general solution (
II.17
) by
\psi_{0}^{2}
and integrate over the, possibly unbounded, domain
\Omega
.
This leads us to

\int_{\Omega}\psi_{0}(x)^{2}\Phi_{n}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\psi_{0}(x)^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\,t\hat{\mathcal{L}}}\Phi_{n}(x,0)\,\mathrm{d}x-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\psi_{0}(x)^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(t-s)\,\hat{\mathcal{L}}}Q_{n}(x,s)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s.

(II.20)

Moreover, using the gauge-rotated representation of the semigroup (
II.16
) together with (
II.5
) yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\psi_{0}(x)^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(t-s)\,\hat{\mathcal{L}}}Q_{n}(x,s)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{e}^{-\phi_{0}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(t-s)\,(\hat{H}_{0}-E_{0})}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\phi_{0}}Q_{n}(x,s)\,\mathrm{d}x

(II.21)

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{0},\hat{U}(t-s)\psi_{0}\,Q_{n}(x,s)\rangle,

(II.22)

where
\hat{U}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\,t(\hat{H}_{0}-E_{0})}
is the free-evolution operator on
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Using the fact that
\hat{U}(t)
is unitary and
\hat{U}(t)\psi_{0}=\psi_{0}
(recall that
\psi_{0}
is the ground state of
\hat{H}_{0}
), the expectation value (
II.22
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{0},\hat{U}(t-s)\psi_{0}\,Q_{n}(x,s)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t-s)\psi_{0},\psi_{0}\,Q_{n}(x,s)\rangle=\langle Q_{n}(x,s)\rangle_{\psi_{0}}.

(II.23)

Using a similar procedure, one can show that

\int_{\Omega}\psi_{0}(x)^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\,t\mathcal{L}}\Phi_{n}(x,0)\,\mathrm{d}x=\langle\Phi_{n}(x,0)\rangle_{\psi_{0}}.

(II.24)

This implies that (
II.20

\langle\Phi_{n}(x,t)\rangle_{\psi_{0}}=\langle\Phi_{n}(x,0)\rangle_{\psi_{0}}-\,\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}\langle Q_{n}(x,s)\rangle_{\psi_{0}}\,\mathrm{d}s.

(II.25)

After taking the time derivative, the stated result (
II.19
) emerges.
This result has direct implications on the particle current at the boundary
\partial\Omega

Indeed, multiplying (
II.9
) by
\psi_{0}^{2}
and integrating over
\Omega
, we obtain the following relation:

\mathrm{i}\,\partial_{t}\langle\Phi_{n}(x,t)\rangle_{\psi_{0}}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\left[\psi_{0}(x)^{2}\nabla\Phi_{n}(x,t)\right]\mathrm{d}x+\langle Q_{n}(x,t)\rangle_{\psi_{0}}.

(II.26)

Consequently, (
II.19
) enforces

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\left[\psi_{0}(x)^{2}\nabla\Phi_{n}(x,t)\right]\mathrm{d}x=0.

(II.27)

In the case where
\Omega
is a bounded domain, one can apply the Gauss–Ostrogradsky Theorem to the latter equation; therefore,

\int_{\partial\Omega}\psi_{0}(x)^{2}\,\nabla\Phi_{n}(x,t)\cdot\mathbf{n}(x)\,\mathrm{d}S=0,

(II.28)

where
\mathbf{n}(x)
denotes the outward unit normal on
\partial\Omega

This implies that, for all times, the particle current is absent at the boundary.

II.3

III
Applications

In this section, we explore two examples where the previously developed formalism of TDLPT is applied.
Given their physical relevance, we consider first the harmonic oscillator subjected to an external time-dependent electric field.
This example provides a proof of principle, since the time evolution of the system can be determined exactly
22
.
We then apply the formalism to study the dynamics of the hydrogen atom under the influence of a laser pulse, focusing on the first-order correction term, the dynamic energy shifts, the induced dipole moment, and the asymptotic expansion of the wave function.

III.1
Harmonic Oscillator in an External Electric Field

Consider a one-dimensional harmonic oscillator, initially in its ground state, perturbed by a time-dependent electric field.
Thus, we take the Hamiltonian

\hat{H_{0}}=-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}x^{2},\quad-\infty<x<\infty,

(III.1)

and the time-dependent external potential

V_{\text{int}}(x,t)=x\,g(t),

(III.2)

where
g(t)
describes the temporal profile of the external electric field.
Therefore, the amplitude of the electric field plays the role of the coupling constant.
Since the initial condition is the ground state of
\hat{H}_{0}
, we have

\psi_{0}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-\phi_{0}(x)},\quad\phi_{0}(x)=\frac{x^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\ln\pi.

(III.3)

According to (
II.4

IV
Conclusions and Outlook

In this work, we have developed the time-dependent extension of LPT in which the wave function is expressed in exponential form, with its argument expanded as a power series in the coupling constant.
This extension is carried out in a systematic and rigorous manner.

Within the framework of TDLPT, the key features of the original time-independent formulation are retained, most notably the appearance of perturbative corrections in closed integral form, whose evolution is governed by a gauge-rotated free time-evolution operator.
In this approach, dynamic energy shifts arise naturally in integral form and admit a transparent interpretation as time averages calculated via pseudopotentials.
In contrast to standard formulations of TDPT based on the Dyson series, TDLPT provides a direct route to computing these shifts for general time-dependent perturbations.

We have applied the method to two physically relevant systems: the harmonic oscillator and the hydrogen atom, both subjected to a time-dependent external field.
In the former case, TDLPT reproduces the exact wave function, thereby providing a proof of principle of the method.
In the latter, the system is analyzed using both analytical and numerical methods.
Moreover, we showed that the selection rules of the hydrogen atom can be inferred from the structure of the phase corrections.
Remarkably, the same structure of the correction terms extends to systems governed by arbitrary radial potentials, such as those used within the celebrated single-active-electron approximation.

The present work establishes a basis for further investigations in perturbative regimes, including the strong-coupling regime via the Symanzik scaling.
It also opens the possibility of deriving analytical expressions for phase corrections and experimentally relevant observables — such as AC Stark shifts, polarizabilities, and photoionization time delays — in situations where standard perturbative approaches do not readily yield closed-form results.

\mathrm{i}\,\partial_{t}v(x,t)=\hat{\mathcal{K}}\,v(x,t),\quad v(x,0)=v_{0}(x):=\mathrm{e}^{-\phi_{0}(x)}u_{0}(x),

(A.5)

where, upon using (
II.6
), the Schrödinger operator
\hat{\mathcal{K}}
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{K}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left[\Delta+\Delta\phi_{0}-(\nabla\phi_{0})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+V_{0}-E_{0}

\displaystyle=\hat{H}_{0}-E_{0}.

(A.6)

Provided that the initial condition in (
A.5
) satisfies
v_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, the solution is

v(x,t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\,\hat{\mathcal{K}}}v_{0}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\,\hat{\mathcal{K}}}\mathrm{e}^{-\phi_{0}}u_{0}.

(A.7)

Consequently, multiplying both sides of (
A.4
) by
\mathrm{e}^{\phi_{0}}
yields that the solution of the original equation (
A.1
) can be written in terms of the
C_{0}
-semigroup
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\,\hat{\mathcal{K}}}
on
L^{2}(\Omega)
as

u(x,t)=\mathrm{e}^{\phi_{0}(x)}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\,\hat{\mathcal{K}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\phi_{0}(x)}u_{0}(x).

(A.8)

This suggests that the operator
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
generates a
C_{0}
-semigroup acting on the weighted space
L^{2}_{\psi_{0}^{2}}(\Omega)
and can be written according to (
A.6
) as a
gauge rotation

\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\hat{\mathcal{L}}}:=\mathrm{e}^{\phi_{0}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\,(\hat{H}_{0}-E_{0})}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\phi_{0}}.

(A.9)

The fact that
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
generates a
C_{0}
-semigroup on the weighted space can be argued as follows.
Firstly, we note that, for all functions
h\in L^{2}_{\psi_{0}^{2}}(\Omega)
, we have

\int_{\Omega}|\mathrm{e}^{-\phi_{0}(x)}h(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}|h(x)|^{2}\psi_{0}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\|h\|_{L^{2}_{\psi_{0}^{2}}(\Omega)}<\infty,

(A.10)

which shows that
\mathrm{e}^{-\phi_{0}}h\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Moreover, using that the semigroup
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\,(\hat{H}_{0}-E_{0})}
maps functions from
L^{2}(\Omega)
to
L^{2}(\Omega)
, we conclude that

\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\,(\hat{H}_{0}-E_{0})}\mathrm{e}^{-\phi_{0}}h=:g\in L^{2}(\Omega).

(A.11)

Using that
\mathrm{e}^{\phi_{0}}g\in L^{2}_{\psi_{0}^{2}}(\Omega)
since

\|\mathrm{e}^{\phi_{0}}g\|_{L^{2}_{\psi_{0}^{2}}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}|\mathrm{e}^{\phi_{0}(x)}g(x)|^{2}\psi_{0}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}<\infty,

(A.12)

we therefore obtain that the operator
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\hat{\mathcal{L}}}
maps functions from
L^{2}_{\psi_{0}^{2}}(\Omega)
to
L^{2}_{\psi_{0}^{2}}(\Omega)
.

Secondly, the fact that
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\hat{\mathcal{L}}}
is a bounded operator follows from

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\hat{\mathcal{L}}}\|_{L^{2}_{\psi_{0}^{2}}(\Omega)\to L^{2}_{\psi_{0}^{2}}(\Omega)}&=\sup\{\|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\hat{\mathcal{L}}}h\|_{L^{2}_{\psi_{0}^{2}}(\Omega)}\colon\|h\|_{L^{2}_{\psi_{0}^{2}}(\Omega)}=1\}\\
&=\sup\{\|\mathrm{e}^{-\phi_{0}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\hat{\mathcal{L}}}h\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\colon\|h\|_{L^{2}_{\psi_{0}^{2}}(\Omega)}=1\}\\
&=\sup\{\|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\,(\hat{H}_{0}-E_{0})}\mathrm{e}^{-\phi_{0}}h\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\colon\|h\|_{L^{2}_{\psi_{0}^{2}}(\Omega)}=1\}\\
&=\sup\{\|\mathrm{e}^{-\phi_{0}}h\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\colon\|h\|_{L^{2}_{\psi_{0}^{2}}(\Omega)}=1\}\\
&=1,\end{split}

(A.13)

which shows that
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\hat{\mathcal{L}}}
is actually a unitary operator on
L^{2}_{\psi_{0}^{2}}(\Omega)
.
The remaining conditions for
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\hat{\mathcal{L}}}
to be a
C_{0}
-semigroup can be readily verified by the reader using the definition of the operator in (
A.9
) together with the fact that
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\,(\hat{H}_{0}-E_{0})}
forms a semigroup on
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Appendix B
Connection to Standard TDPT

Standard TDPT is formulated in terms of the Dyson series, which can be obtained via a bootstrapping construction based on Duhamel’s formula applied to the TDSE.
Within the present approach, such an expansion is recovered by a power series of (
II.1
) in the coupling parameter
\lambda
, thereby providing a direct connection between the conventional perturbative treatment and the TDLPT framework.

According to Duhamel’s formula applied to (
I.1
), the time-evolved wave function takes the implicit form

\psi(x,t)=\psi_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}E_{0}t}-\mathrm{i}\,\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(t-s)\hat{H}_{0}}\,V_{\mathrm{int}}(x,s)\,\psi(x,s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

(B.1)

We proceed by

B.1
) into
\psi(x,s)
under the integral, which yields

(B.2)

Higher-order terms in
\lambda
are obtained by repeating this procedure and result in the following expression for the solution:

\displaystyle\psi(x,t)

\displaystyle=\psi_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}E_{0}t}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-\mathrm{i}\,\lambda)^{n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s_{1}}\cdots\int_{0}^{s_{n-1}}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(t-s_{1})\hat{H}_{0}}V_{\mathrm{int}}(x,s_{1})

In this appendix, we provide details for the calculation of the time-averaged energy shift.
Performing the time integration in equation (
