Na2CO3-assisted oxygen-alkali recyclable cellulose separation/pulping technology of straw and silicon controllable migration
Authors: Qixi Xu, Jiachuan Chen, Kai Zhang, Junchao Li, Mu Zhou, Guihua Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryA green recyclable Na2CO3-assisted oxygen-alkali (SCOA) low-temperature cellulose separation/pulping technology is explored in this work for overcoming the technical defects, such as high temperature, severe cellulose degradation, and interference from silicon elements in the conventional cellulose separation/pulping process of wheat straw. Compared with conventional oxygen-alkali (OA) technology, the unscreened and screened cellulose-based pulp yields increased by 8.2% and 5.6%, respectively, at 115°C and an alkali charge of 22% (as Na2O) with a Na2CO3 proportion of 40%, and the cellulose crystallinity of pulp, the tensile index and ring crush of hand-sheet paper increased by 10.1%, 26%, and 23%, respectively. As an important interfering factor, silicon migration was mainly influenced by temperature and pH value, and only 18.2% of the initial dissolved silicon remained in the SCOA pulping black liquor, decrease of 53.4% compared with the OA technology. SCOA technology can obtained high-quality cellulose-based pulp with stable yield.

Introduction

Cellulose paper-based plays an irreplaceable supporting role in key fields such as cultural communication and packaging logistics.1,2 However, with the intensification of global competition for wood resources and the tightening of ecological protection policies, increasing pressure on fiber raw material supply has been the topic for the papermaking industry.3 One of the effective approach to utilize the abundant agricultural waste straw resources is to separate the cellulose components of the straw for pulping and papermaking, thereby efficiently utilizing the renewable and biodegradable fiber resources and improving the human living environment,4 which can effectively reduce the dependence of the pulping and papermaking industry on wood resources, providing an important approach for the resource utilization of agricultural waste straw. Wheat straw, the main by-product of wheat harvesting, is one of the most widely distributed and promising agricultural biomass resources and exhibits distinct advantages for cellulose separation/pulping applications.5 Structurally, wheat straw possesses a loosely organized tissue with a high proportion of parenchyma cells and abundant pectic substances, which facilitates chemical penetration and fiber separation.6 Its cellulose content is comparable to that of hardwood, its lignin is predominantly alkali-soluble, and its fibers are rich in hydrophilic groups and reactive linkages, collectively conferring high reactivity under alkaline conditions.7,8,9,10

Nevertheless, the conventional technology for cellulose separation/pulping of wheat straw using high temperature and high sodium hydroxide dosage has the drawbacks of uneven chemical consumption and fluctuations in pulp quality.11 Moreover, cellulose in wheat straw is prone to excessive degradation under high-temperature and strongly alkaline conditions.12 Oxygen-alkali (OA) cellulose separation/pulping is more suitable for agricultural straw fibers such as wheat straw with loose tissue structures. Based on the synergistic action of oxygen and alkali, oxygen molecules undergo free-radical reactions with lignin, generating active oxygen species (AOs), including superoxide anion radicals (O2−·), hydroxyl radicals (HO·), and hydroperoxyl radicals (HOO·), which promote lignin degradation and dissolution, obtaining cellulose pulp with relatively high brightness.13,14 However, conventional OA cellulose separation/pulping technology uses sodium hydroxide as the sole alkali source, and strong alkalinity can cause the β-1,4-glycosidic bonds in cellulose to cleave and break, thereby reducing cellulose pulp yield and cellulose fiber quality. Furthermore, during the silica removal stage of the alkali reuse process, the dissolved silicon forms colloidal complexes, which leads to scaling in the evaporators and the agglomeration of fly ash in the recovery boiler. This phenomenon significantly reduces the alkali recovery efficiency and increases energy consumption and lime slurry pollution.15 Consequently, the conventional OA cellulose separation/pulping technology for agricultural residues urgently require improvement.

Sodium carbonate, due to its relatively mild/moderate alkalinity and chemical stability, can partially substitute/replace sodium hydroxide to regulate the alkalinity and the pH value in the oxygen-alkali cellulose separation/pulping process, thereby decreasing the non-selective degradation of cellulose and improving the selective dissolution and separation of lignin.16 In previous studies, the research group of the authors investigated the effects of OA pulping process involving sodium carbonate on the pulping and papermaking performance of bagasse and reed. The research results demonstrated that the buffering effect of sodium carbonate can effectively inhibit the dissolution and scaling of silicon, which is the main interfering factor in the alkali recovery process, increasing cellulose pulp yield, improving alkali recovery conditions, and obtaining a mild and sustainable pulping technology.17,18 Furthermore, partially replacing sodium hydroxide with sodium carbonate can establish a green recycling cellulose separation/pulping technology, enabling the recycling of the alkaline solution, and enhancing the economic and environmental performance of the cellulose separation/pulping process.19

In this work, based on the aim of enhancing the utilization value of agricultural waste wheat straw and the green recyclability of the cellulose separation/pulping process, a green recyclable cellulose separation/fiber preparation technology of sodium carbonate-assisted oxygen-alkali (SCOA) will be proposed. The efficiency of partially replacing sodium hydroxide with sodium carbonate in the oxygen-alkali cellulose separation/pulping process, and the influence of process conditions on the efficiency of cellulose separation/pulping and the papermaking performance of cellulose-based pulp will be investigated and explored, and the migration and distribution of silicon as the key influencing factor in the green recycling process of cellulose separation/pulping will be analyzed. This work will provide theoretical basis and technical support for the high-value utilization of agricultural straw resources and the industrial application of green recycling separation technology.

Results and discussion

Effects of process parameters on the performance of SCOA cellulose separation/pulping

Based on a single-factor experimental design, the effects of temperature, sodium carbonate proportion, alkali charge, and holding time on the screened cellulose pulp yield, brightness, and Kappa number during SCOA cellulose separation/pulping technology for wheat straw were investigated.

Cellulose separation/pulping temperature, as a key parameter controlling the reaction kinetics of the SCOA cellulose separation/pulping process and the evolution pathways of active oxygen species (AOs), was first examined. As shown in Figures 1A and 1B, when the temperature increased from 110°C to 130°C, the unscreened cellulose-based pulp yield, Kappa number, and viscosity continuously decreased, whereas the screened cellulose-based pulp yield and brightness reached the maximum at 115°C. This phenomenon may be attributed to the differences in the composition of AOs and their reaction selectivity within different temperature ranges. Under conditions of 110°C–115°C, O2−· may act as the dominant reactive species, exhibiting relatively high reactivity toward lignin β-O-4 and β-5 ether linkages, while its attack on cellulose β-1,4-glycosidic bonds appears to be comparatively limited.20 Consequently, a moderate increase in temperature may promote lignin fragmentation and dissolution, with only minor effects on carbohydrates.Figure 1Effects of process parameters on the performance of SCOA cellulose separation/pulpingEffects of (A and B) cellulose separation/pulping temperature, (C and D) sodium carbonate proportion, (E and F) alkali charge, (G and H) holding time on cellulose pulp yield, brightness, and Kappa number during the SCOA cellulose separation/pulping process. Data are represented as mean ± SEM (n = 2 independent experiments).

With a further increase in temperature, free-radical chain reactions may be significantly intensified, and O2−·, upon gaining additional energy, tends to convert into HOO· and even HO·.21 These highly reactive species are continuously degrading residual lignin but may also induce cleavage of hemicellulose backbones and peeling reactions in the amorphous regions of cellulose.22 As a result, simultaneous decreases in screened cellulose pulp yield and viscosity are observed, accompanied by the formation of chromophoric structures that lead to a reduction in brightness.

The adjusting/regulatory effect of sodium carbonate mass proportion on the SCOA cellulose separation/pulping performance became more evident. As shown in Figures 1C and 1D, at a fixed alkali charge, an increase of sodium carbonate proportion led to overall increases in unscreened cellulose-based pulp yield, Kappa number, and viscosity, while brightness exhibited a decreasing trend. The screened cellulose pulp yield reached its maximum at a sodium carbonate proportion of 40%. This behavior may be associated with changes in pH stability and the redox environment of the cellulose separation/pulping process induced by the buffering effect of Na2CO3. An appropriate amount of Na2CO3 may delay the protonation of O2−·, thereby increasing the O2−·/HO· ratio, enhancing the selectivity of delignification reactions, and suppressing excessive carbohydrate degradation. However, at higher sodium carbonate proportions, the redox potential of the system may decrease, inhibiting the generation of HO· and reducing delignification efficiency. Meanwhile, steric hindrance arising from residual lignin may impede effective cellulose fiber separation, ultimately resulting in decreases in screened cellulose pulp yield and brightness.23,24

Effects of alkali dosage and holding time on the properties of cellulose-based pulp exhibited pronounced synergistic regulation characteristics. With increasing alkali dosage/charge, the aryl ether bonds and some C-C bonds in lignin macromolecules will accelerate the fragmentation and degradation of lignin, causing its dissolution (Figures 1E and 1F). However, the risk of cellulose degradation in amorphous regions may also increase, leading to a trend in which the screened cellulose-based pulp yield initially increases and subsequently decreases.25 The extension of holding time may enhance the diffusion and penetration of alkali liquor and oxygen into different layers of the cellulose fiber cell wall, thereby promoting the progression of delignification reactions from the surface toward the interior. Nevertheless, excessively long reaction time may aggravate oxidative cleavage of the cellulose backbone (Figures 1G and 1H). Meanwhile, alkaline oxidative depolymerization of cellulose chains under high mass-transfer conditions may be the primary reason for the continuous decline in viscosity.26

It can be seen that during the process of the SCOA cellulose separation/pulping technology, the performance of the cellulose-based pulp was influenced by the temperature-dependent reaction pathways, the regulation of the sodium carbonate ratio on the distribution of active oxygen species, and the effect of alkali dosage, and the holding time. Under appropriate technological conditions, these factors may establish a relatively stable synergistic relationship, thereby obtaining a balance between selective delignification and cellulose fiber separation.

Comparison between SCOA and OA cellulose separation/pulping processes

Under a relatively low temperature condition of 115°C, the SCOA cellulose separation/pulping process achieved optimal comprehensive performance cellulose-based pulp at an alkali charge of 22% (as Na2O) with a molar proportion of 40% sodium carbonate, in which the screened and unscreened cellulose-based pulp yields reached 55.5% and 61.6%, respectively, with a Kappa number of 16.0 and a cellulose-based pulp viscosity of 806.5 mL g−1. OA cellulose separation/pulping was conducted at the same alkali dosage for comparison (Figure 2). Compared with the OA cellulose separation/pulping process, the unscreened cellulose-based pulp yield increased by 8.2% in the SCOA cellulose separation/pulping process, and the screened cellulose pulp yield increased by 5.6%, the Kappa number increased by 8.3 units, and cellulose-based pulp viscosity increased by 152.5 mL g−1, demonstrating obvious advantages. The superiority of the proposed process was further confirmed by the strength properties of hand-sheet paper from SCOA cellulose-based pulp with a tensile index of 92.4 N m g−1, a tear index of 3.7 mN m2 g−1, a burst index of 5.4 kPa m2 g−1, and a ring crush index of 9.0 N m g−1, compared with cellulose-based OA pulp, increased by 26%, 12%, 8%, and 23%, respectively. The significant improvement in mechanical strength properties may be attributed to the milder action of sodium carbonate in the SCOA cellulose separation/pulping technological process, which can preserve cellulose fiber dimensions and cellulose fiber quality.19 However, the OA cellulose separation/pulping process produced a higher cellulose-based pulp brightness, reaching 53.7% ISO.Figure 2Effects of SCOA and OA cellulose separation/pulping processes on cellulose pulp yield and paper strength propertiesNote: Data are represented as mean ± SEM (n = 2 independent experiments).

As shown in Figure 2, the properties and performance of the wheat straw raw material and the corresponding cellulose-based pulps were characterized. The surface of wheat straw is generally smooth but contains abundant micropores (Figure 3A). Its characteristic stomatal structures typical of gramineous plants provide effective mass-transfer pathways for alkali penetration and diffusion of dissolved components during cellulose separation/pulping. Distinct differences in cellulose fiber morphology were observed among different cellulose separation/pulping processes. Cellulose fibers obtained from the SCOA cellulose separation/pulping process exhibited smooth and full surfaces, with the lamellar structure of the cellulose fiber cell wall remaining well preserved (Figure 3B). In contrast, cellulose fibers from the OA cellulose separation/pulping process showed pronounced collapse and wrinkling, accompanied by irreversible damage to the cell wall structure (Figure 3C), directly reflecting the fundamentally different modes of cellulose fiber-alkali interaction in the two processes.Figure 3Structural and morphological characterization of wheat straw and cellulose-based pulps obtained from different cellulose separation/pulping processes(A) Wheat straw.(B) Cellulose-based SCOA pulp.(C) CELLULOSE-based OA pulp.(D) FT-IR spectra.(E) XRD patterns. These morphological differences originate from the distinct action mechanisms of sodium carbonate and sodium hydroxide within the cellulose fiber cell wall. Under the highly alkaline conditions of OA cellulose separation/pulping, excessive cellulose fiber swelling occurs, disrupting the hydrogen-bond network between cellulose microfibrils and markedly reducing the mechanical strength of the cell wall. Meanwhile, strong alkali conditions accelerate rapid lignin fragmentation and dissolution, weakening the structural support of the cell wall and ultimately leading to cellulose fiber collapse and shrinkage.27 By contrast, in the SCOA cellulose separation/pulping process, the HCO3−/CO32− buffer solution formed via sodium carbonate hydrolysis can effectively inhibit severe cellulose peeling reactions caused by excessively high pH values, and regulate/moderates excessive cellulose fibers swelling. As a result, the integrity and stability of the lamellar cell wall structure are well maintained, demonstrating the significant advantage of the SCOA process in protecting the structure of cellulose fiber.

FT-IR spectra (Figure 3D) showed that the characteristic aromatic skeletal vibration bands at 1510 and 1600 cm−1, corresponding to C=C stretching of lignin, almost completely disappeared in both cellulose-based pulps, indicating the high delignification efficiency of OA cellulose separation/pulping. The absorption band at 1735 cm−1 is attributed to the C=O stretching vibration of acetyl and carboxylic ester groups in hemicelluloses, mainly originating from acetylated xylan and pectic esters.28 This band vanished entirely in the cellulose-based OA pulp, whereas a weak residual absorption was still observed for the cellulose-based SCOA pulp at the same wavenumber, suggesting partial retention of hemicellulose. As hemicellulose acts as an inter-cellulose fiber “adhesive”, its preservation contributes to maintaining cellulose fiber bonding, which explains the smoother and more intact cellulose fiber surfaces observed in cellulose-based SCOA pulps.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Figure 3E) revealed that neither cellulose separation/pulping process altered the cellulose I crystalline structure, with characteristic diffraction peaks located at 2θ ≈ 14°, 16°, 22°, and 34.5°, corresponding to the (1–10), (110), (200), and (004) planes, respectively.29 However, it was observed that there were significant differences in the crystallinity of cellulose were observed. Compared to the crystallinity of raw wheat straw cellulose, the crystallinity of cellulose from OA pulping process increased by 10.0%, while the crystallinity of cellulose from SCOA pulping process increase of 20.1%. It can be seen that the crystallinity of the cellulose separated in the SCOA pulping process was 10.1% higher than that in the OA pulping process. This indicated that reactive oxygen species preferentially disrupted the amorphous regions of cellulose during delignification, while hydroxyl radicals (HO·) generated in the Soda-O2 system may non-selectively attack crystalline domains, thereby moderating the overall increase in crystallinity.30

Silicon migration behavior during SCOA cellulose separation/pulping process

Silicon in wheat straw is mainly present as amorphous hydrated silica (SiO2·nH2O), which is predominantly distributed on the surface of the raw material and embedded within organic matter or small amounts of other mineral phases. Part of the silicon can associate with cell-wall polysaccharides via hydrogen bonding forming organic-inorganic complexes.31 To elucidate the silicon migration mechanism during SCOA cellulose separation/pulping process, its dynamic behavior was analyzed.

As shown in Figure 4A, silicon migration during SCOA cellulose separation/pulping process can be divided into three distinct stages. During the heating stage of the cellulose separation/pulping process (0–45 min, heating to 90°C corresponding to Table 1), the process pH value of the alkali liquor decreases from 13.60 to 12.65, the silicon content in the raw wheat straw material decreased rapidly, while that in the pulping black liquor sharply increased. With increasing temperature, silicon on the surface of wheat straw reacted with the alkali liquor and rapidly hydrolyzed into soluble sodium silicate (Na2SiO3). The high alkali concentration promoted an almost unidirectional migration of silicon into the black liquor. During the late heating and early isothermal stage of the cellulose separation/pulping process (45–120 min, heating to 115°C followed by 1 h at constant temperature, corresponding to Table 1), the process pH value decreased sharply from 12.65 to 9.55, and inflection points appear in the silicon content of the cellulose-based pulp and the dissolved silicon content of the black liquor, showing trends opposite to those observed in the first stage. The rapid decrease of pH value may be mainly attributed to the degradation of lignin and hemicellulose under high-temperature alkaline conditions, generating organic acids (e.g., formic and acetic acids) that significantly consume alkali.32 The decreased alkalinity reduced the solubility of silicon in the black liquor, resulting in its precipitation as colloidal silica (SiO2·nH2O) or the formation of complex precipitates with metal ions. Meanwhile, the surface of cellulose-based pulp fibers had significant swelling under the combined effect of temperature and alkali, thereby increasing its specific surface area and providing abundant sites for the re-deposition of silicon.33 Exposed carboxyl (–COOH) and hydroxyl (–OH) groups can electrostatically adsorb positively charged silica colloids, leading to the re-adsorption of part of the dissolved silicon from the pulping black liquor onto the cellulose-based pulp surface.34 During the late isothermal stage of the cellulose separation/pulping process (120–300 min, corresponding to Table 1), the easily degradable lignin and hemicellulose were largely removed, the generation rate of organic acids decreased, and the pH value stabilized at approximately 9.23, the dissolution and precipitation/re-adsorption of silicon reached a dynamic equilibrium.Figure 4Silicon migration behavior during SCOA cellulose separation/pulping process(A) Variation of system pH and silicon migration behavior during the SCOA cellulose separation/pulping process.(B) Distribution of silicon at the endpoint for SCOA and OA cellulose separation/pulping processes. The comparison of silicon distribution after cellulose separation/pulping using SCOA and OA processes (Figure 4B) revealed significant differences. The final pH value of the SCOA process is 9.23, which was significantly lower than that of the OA process (10.21). Correspondingly, the dissolved silicon content in the black liquor from SCOA cellulose separation/pulping process is only 0.24 g, compared with 0.52 g for OA cellulose separation/pulping process, representing a decrease of 53.4%. This difference can be attributed to multiple synergistic factors: the alkalinity of sodium carbonate was between that of silicates and sodium hydroxide, its introduction systematically reduced the pH value of the black liquor, thereby decreasing the solubility of silicate ions (SiO32−). The buffering capacity of sodium carbonate suppresses local pH fluctuations, preventing the instantaneous supersaturation of silicates typically observed in strongly alkaline systems.35 The dissolved silicon in the black liquor may mainly exist as Na2SiO3, and the common-ion effect of carbonate (competition between CO32− and SiO32− for dissolution) further destabilize silicate species.15 These factors working together cause silicon to preferentially aggregate in the form of solid precipitates within the cellulose-based pulp, rather than existing in dissolved form in the recyclable alkali liquor, which significantly improved the operational environment/conditions of the alkaline recovery system.

Effect of alkali recycling on the performance of cellulose-based pulp and silicon accumulation

Based on the effective alkali concentration in the black liquor from the SCOA cellulose separation/pulping process, the black liquor was subjected to evaporation concentration, combustion, and subsequent dissolution to obtain a green alkali liquor. The conventional causticizing step in the cellulose separation/pulping process was eliminated, instead, a portion of the sodium hydroxide consumed during the fiber separation/pulping was supplemented to regenerate the alkali liquor, which was then reused in the subsequent cyclic cellulose separation/pulping process.

In the SCOA cellulose separation/pulping technology process with alkali recycling, the unscreened cellulose pulp yield exhibited a slight increase with increasing recycling cycles, while the screened cellulose-based pulp yield showed a marginal decrease, with a variation of only ∼1.1% (Figure 5A). The brightness of cellulose-based pulp decreased slightly, while the Kappa number and the viscosity of cellulose-based pulp gradually increased. The tensile index of the resulting paper sheets showed a minor decrease (Figure 5B), whereas the other mechanical properties had no obvious changes (Figure 5C). Meanwhile, the content of dissolved silicon in cellulose-based pulp and black liquor increased gradually with the increase of recycling times (Figure 5D).Figure 5Effect of alkali recycling on the performance of cellulose-based pulp and silicon accumulationChanges in (A) cellulose-based pulp yield, (B) cellulose-based pulp properties, (C) paper strength properties, and (D) silicon accumulation behavior during alkali recycling in the SCOA cellulose separation/pulping process. The gradual increase in soluble silicon in the black liquor can be mainly attributed to the partial redissolution of silicates that were not completely precipitated in the previous cycle during the evaporation and concentration stage, allowing silicon to re-enter the alkali liquor. The increase in unscreened pulp yield is likely associated with enhanced silicon deposition on cellulose fiber surfaces that hinders liquor penetration. In addition, the accumulation of silicates can consume a part of the effective alkali, leading to a decrease in the redox potential of the reaction system and suppressing the generation efficiency of highly oxidative HO· radicals during delignification, ultimately influencing screened pulp yield.

Silicon accumulation plays a key role in the evolution of cellulose-based pulp and paper properties during alkali recycling.36 Specifically, silicon deposition can consume effective alkali in the alkali liquor and reduce the reaction activity, resulting in decreased delignification efficiency and increased residual lignin content in the cellulose-based pulp, which showed as an increased of Kappa number and a decrease of brightness. Meanwhile, the reduced severity of cellulose degradation led to an increase in cellulose-based pulp viscosity. Silicon deposited on the microfibril surfaces forms a physical barrier that interferes with the formation of dense and effective hydrogen-bonding networks between cellulose fibers, weakening inter-fiber bonding and consequently reducing the tensile index.37 In contrast, the tear index is primarily governed by the intrinsic strength of individual cellulose fibers, while the burst and ring crush indices are more dependent on cellulose fiber stiffness and network integrity. Silicon deposition can partially enhance cellulose fiber rigidity and intrinsic strength, thereby compensating for the loss of bonding strength to some extent.38

Overall, after the alkali recycled five cycles, silicon accumulation was observed in both the black liquor and the cellulose-based pulp from the SCOA cellulose separation/pulping technology process. While maintaining a relatively stable cellulose-based pulp yield, the SCOA technology process can achieve sustainable operation of the alkali recovery process at the cost of only a slight decrease in the brightness and tensile strength of hand-sheet paper from cellulose-based pulp, demonstrating the feasibility and reliability of the proposed green recyclable cellulose separation/pulping technology.

In conclusion, the SCOA recyclable separation technology for the cellulose separation/pulping of wheat straw has been successfully developed, in which sodium carbonate partially replaces sodium hydroxide. Cellulose based pulp of wheat straw with high yield and excellent strength performance were obtained under low temperature and mild alkaline conditions (with a processing temperature of 115°C and a sodium carbonate content of 40% in the 22% alkaline dosage). Compared with conventional high temperature oxygen-alkali cellulose separation/pulping process, the cellulose-based pulp yield and screened pulp yield in the SCOA process were significantly increased by 8.2% and 5.6%, respectively, and cellulose crystallinity increased by 10.1%. The tensile index, tear index, burst index, and ring crush of hand-sheet paper from SCOA cellulose-based pulp increased by 26%, 12%, 8%, and 23%, respectively, compared with the conventional OA cellulose-based pulp, exhibiting optimal mechanical strength properties. As an important interfering factor affecting the operation of the cellulose separation/pulping process technology cycle for straw such as wheat straw, the migration of silicon was mainly influenced by temperature and pH value. Based on this, the dissolution of silicon has been effectively controlled by adjusting/regulating the process pH value. Silicon dissolved as sodium silicate and entered the pulping black liquor during initial high alkalinity heating stage of the cellulose separation/pulping process, dissolved silicon transformed into colloidal silica re-enriching in the cellulose-based pulp via electrostatic interactions with hydroxyl and carboxyl groups, result in only 18.2% of the initial dissolved silicon remained in the pulping black liquor, corresponding to a decrease of 53.4% compared with the OA cellulose separation/pulping process, thereby improving the efficiency of alkali recovery/recycling. The recyclable SCOA cellulose separation/pulping technology/process has been established enabling alkali recovery and recycling. After the alkali was recycled five cycles during the cellulose separation/pulping process, the yield of screened pulp with a Kappa number of 16.80, brightness of 43.6% ISO, and viscosity of 832 mL g−1 remained 54.7%, and its hand-sheet paper exhibited a tensile index of 84.3 N m g−1, tear index of 3.7 mN m2 g−1, burst index of 5.2 kPa m2 g−1, and ring crush index of 8.7 N m g−1. In conclusion, SCOA recyclable separation technology for wheat straw can efficiently utilize agricultural straw resources, providing a sustainable and economically feasible approach for energy-saving and emission reduction in the pulping and papermaking industry, demonstrating significant potential for industrial application.

Limitations of the study

The mechanism of silicon migration is mainly described by phenomena, and the dissolution kinetics, interface interaction and morphological characterization are not involved in this work.

Method details

Preparation of cellulose pulp with sodium carbonate-assisted oxygen-alkali technology

Sodium carbonate-assisted oxygen-alkali (SCOA) process for cellulose separation/pulping of wheat straw were conducted using a mixed alkali system composed of sodium carbonate and sodium hydroxide in a 15-L ZQS electrically heated rotary digester equipped with four 1-L batch reactors. 50 g (oven-dry basis) of wheat straw was placed in a polyethylene bag, followed by the addition of the prepared 300 mL alkali liquor, mixed well by kneaded for uniform impregnation. The impregnated wheat straw was then transferred into a TD3-A batch reactor. Air inside the reactor were removed by three successive pressurization–depressurization cycles, after which oxygen was introduced and the initial oxygen pressure was 0.8 MPa. The reactors were fixed in the rotary digester and subjected to oxygen-alkali (OA) cellulose separation/pulping according to the preset program. After cellulose separation/pulping, the resulting cellulose-based pulp and pulping black liquor were separated and collected for subsequent performance evaluation and compositional analysis.

The SCOA cellulose separation/pulping process parameters were optimized by single-factor method. The oxygen pressure of 0.8 MPa and liquid-to-solid ratio of 1:6 were set as the constant experimental conditions. Firstly, under the conditions of an alkali dosage of 28% (as Na2O, with the molar proportion of sodium carbonate of 40%) and a holding-temperature time of 4 h, the separation/pulping temperature within the range of 110°C–130°C was optimized. Secondly, under the optimized temperature of 115°C, a 28% alkali dosage (as Na2O) and a holding-temperature time of 4 h, the molar proportion of sodium carbonate in the range of 20%–70% was optimized. Thirdly, the alkali dosage (18%–28%, as Na2O) was optimized under the optimized temperature of 115°C, the optimized molar proportion of 40% (Equivalent to a mass ratio of 46.9%) and a holding-temperature time of 4 h. Finally, the holding-temperature time (1–5 h) was also optimized under the optimized temperature of 115°C, the optimized molar proportion of 40% sodium carbonate in the optimized 22% alkali dosage (as Na2O).

The OA cellulose separation/pulping process used as a control was conducted with an alkali charge of 22% (as Na2O), a cellulose separation/pulping temperature of 115°C, a holding time of 4 h at 115°C, an oxygen pressure of 0.8 MPa, and a liquid-to-solid ratio of 1:6.

Alkali recycling in SCOA cellulose separation/pulping process

The sodium carbonate content in the green liquor was determined according to the method specified in GB/T 4348.1–2013. A 10 mL aliquot of the pulping black liquor was taken and subjected to evaporation and concentration, followed by combustion at 800°C. The resulting solid residue was dissolved in 490 mL deionized water with thorough stirring, and the solution was filtered to obtain the green liquor. A mixed indicator of bromocresol green and methyl red was added to the green liquor, and the solution was titrated with 0.5 mol/L hydrochloric acid standard solution under magnetic stirring until the color changed to dark red, indicating the endpoint. The volume of hydrochloric acid consumed was recorded, and the concentration of sodium carbonate in the green liquor was calculated according to Equation 1.(Equation 1)CNa2CO3=McV12V2Where CNa2CO3 represents the concentration of sodium carbonate in the green liquor (g/L), M is the relative molecular mass of sodium carbonate (g/mol), c is the concentration of hydrochloric acid (mol/L), V1 is the volume of hydrochloric acid consumed (mL), and V2is the volume of the green liquor sample (mL).

Based on the titration results, the volume of black liquor required to provide 7.5 g of sodium carbonate was accurately measured. The black liquor was evaporated on a hot plate at 105°C and concentrated to a viscous state. It was then heated on a heating mantle at 325°C for thorough carbonization, removing most of the water and volatile organic matter. The carbonized residue was transferred to a ceramic crucible that had been pre-ignited to constant weight, and ignited in a muffle furnace at 800°C for 6 h to completely oxidize the organic components and fully convert the inorganic salts. After ignition, the crucible was cooled to room temperature and removed; the obtained solid consisted essentially of sodium carbonate. Then, 8.5 g of sodium hydroxide was added, and the mixture was diluted to 300 mL with deionized water. Titration verification confirmed that the total alkali content of this recycled alkali solution was equivalent to that of the initial pulping alkali liquor. The regenerated alkali solution was reused in the next SCOA cellulose separation/pulping process cycle to achieve the recycling of alkali.

Evaluation of cellulose-based pulp properties

The cellulose-based pulp from SCOA cellulose separation/pulping process, defined as unscreened cellulose-based pulp, was washed with water until neutral pH, and screened using a flat-screen classifier, and the screened cellulose-based pulp were obtained. The unscreened cellulose-based pulp yield and the screened cellulose-based pulp yield were calculated as the ratios of the oven-dry mass of unscreened cellulose-based pulp or screened cellulose-based pulp to the oven-dry mass of the raw material (50 g), respectively.

Cellulose-based pulp viscosity and Kappa number were determined in accordance with GB/T 1548–2004 and GB/T 1546–2018, respectively.

The soluble silica content in pulping black liquor and the insoluble silica content in cellulose-based pulp were measured following the method reported by Gan et al.17

Concretely, a sample of cellulose-based pulp (1 g) or vacuum-filtered black liquor (5 mL) was placed in a crucible and completely carbonized on a hot plate at 325°C. After carbonization, an appropriate amount of nitric acid and sulfuric acid was added to thoroughly wet the sample. The insoluble residue was then filtered, washed, and returned to the crucible, followed by gradual heating in a muffle furnace to 800°C and holding for 6 h to ensure complete carbonization. After cooling to room temperature, the remaining residue was silica. The soluble silica content in the black liquor and the insoluble silica content in the pulp are calculated from Equation 2 and Equation 3, respectively.(Equation 2)Thesolublesilicacontentinblackliquor=M1L0L1Where M1 represents the mass of silica obtained from the black liquor sample (g), L0 represents the total volume of the pulping solution (mL), and L1 represents the volume of the black liquor sample used for the test (mL).(Equation 3)Theinsolublesilicacontentinpulp=M2M0M3Where M2 represents the mass of silica obtained from the pulp sample (g), M0represents the total mass of pulp obtained from the pulping process (g), and M3represents the mass of the pulp sample used for the test (g).

Refining of pulp, preparation of hand-sheet paper and its physical property testing

30g (oven-dry basis) of cellulose-based pulp were diluted to a consistency of 10%, then placed in the PTI refiner (S4011, Birkenau, Germany), and beat, refined to the beating degree of 40 ± 3°SR controlled by adjusting the refining speed and disc gap. Hand-sheet paper from SCOA cellulose-based pulp with a grammage of 70 g m−2 were prepared using a standard sheet PTI former (PK-3A, Birkenau, Germany). The tensile index, tear index, ring crush index, burst index, and brightness of the hand-sheet paper from SCOA cellulose-based pulp were determined in accordance with GB/T 455.1–1989, GB/T 455–2002, GB/T 2679.8–1995, GB/T 454–2002, and GB/T 7974-2002, respectively.

Structural and morphological characterization of wheat straw and cellulose-based pulp

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was employed to analyze the crystalline structures of wheat straw and cellulose-based pulp samples. Diffraction data were deconvoluted using PeakFit software, with crystalline peaks fitted using a combination of Gaussian and Voigt functions.39 The crystallinity index (CrI) was defined as the ratio of the integrated area of all crystalline peaks to the total diffraction area (Equation 4):(Equation 4)Crl=ΣACrylΣACryl+ΣAamph×100%where ∑Acryl represents the integrated area of all crystalline peaks, and ∑Aamph is the integrated area of all amorphous peaks.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) was used to analyze functional group vibrations of wheat straw and cellulose-based pulp in the range of 400–4000 cm−1. Surface morphologies were observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Fiber dimensional parameters, including fiber length, width, aspect ratio, and fines content, were determined using an L&W Fiber Tester Plus.

All statistical analyses and figure plotting were performed using Origin. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. No formal hypothesis testing was conducted in this study.
