Multiport quantum interferometry with universal nonunitary transformations

Chaojie Wang, Yuning Zhang, Yuanyuan Chen, Xiubao Sui, Lixiang Chen

Abstract

Quantum interferometry provides a pivotal method for investigating complex photon-photon interaction, enabling various quantum information technologies that beyond the possibilities of classical physics. While such an effective interaction is mostly studied in N × N unitary transformation, m × n nonunitary transformation that relates to lossy multiport quantum interferometry requires a different approach. Here, we experimentally demonstrate a concise yet powerful tool to unravel fundamental features of multiport quantum interferometry with universal nonunitary transformations. To confirm their intriguing unitary and nonunitary properties, Hong-Ou-Mandel (HOM) interference is observed to reveal its nontrivial particle exchange phase, wherein the conventional HOM interference based on unitary transformation and the anomalous HOM interference based on nonunitary transformation can be readily harnessed. These results provide an alternative approach to investigate the quantum light-matter interaction, facilitating the use of complex multiport quantum interference for large-scale quantum information processing tasks such as quantum simulation and quantum computation.

INTRODUCTION

The exploitation of quantum interference promises to enhance the performance of various quantum technologies that beyond the possibilities of classical physics, ranging from high-precision sensors (, ) and intrinsically secure long-distance quantum communication security (–) to even providing exponential computational advantage for certain tasks (, ). Hong-Ou-Mandel (HOM) interference is a prototypical example of such a quantum phenomenon that demonstrates the fact that identical photons arriving simultaneously at opposite input ports of a beam splitter would bunch into a common output as a direct result of their bosonic nature and the unitary property of lossless beam splitter (–). Because quantum technologies aim to provide speed up in several fields, complex and multiport quantum circuits are required (, ). Among the now developed experimental platforms, unitary transformations are routinely modeled and used in optical quantum information processing, which conserves the number of photos and adhere to bosonic commutation relations. Backed by their implementation in terms of basic building blocks as phase shifters acting on individual modes and lossless beam splitter mixing two modes, any discrete unitary operator can be realized with a step-by-step implementation recipe. However, as unitarity imposes restrictions on the transformation matrix, nonunitarity may relax these restrictions and unlock fascinating opportunities for arbitrary transformations.

Because solving NP-complete problem using only unitary operators may be not feasible, nonunitary transformations have the potential to provide an alternative solution (, ). In open physical systems with lossy time evolution, nonunitary operators prove useful in modeling their interaction with real environment and the inevitable imperfections of real optical elements (). In contrast, as quantum entanglement is fragile and susceptible to decoherence, selective filtering of photonic quantum entanglement based on nonunitary optics is also developed to mitigate interaction between the quantum system and its deleterious environment (). Another reason for stepping outside the framework of unitary networks is that transformations may have an unequal number of input and output mode of interest, which is a clear indicator of nonunitarity (). Additionally, one noteworthy property of nonunitary transformation can allow for highly tunable quantum interference, and, thus, Boson sampling and quantum walks with such nonunitary transformation may potentially incorporate fermionic or anyonic behaviors that can be directly mimicked by reconfigurable multiport quantum interferometry (, ).

Up to now, the experimental implementations of these unitary operations mainly focus on 2 × 2 matrix transformation. For example, lossy beam splitter based on graphene (, ), metasurfaces (), surface plasmon polaritons (), Cr-SiN-Cr thin films (), and fully integrated waveguides with engineered dissipation () have been extensively explored and shown to exhibit many intriguing phenomena. However, multiport interferometry with universal nonunitary transformation is essential for large-scale quantum information processing applications. In particular, programmable quantum chips have the stringent requirement for dynamical control of quantum operators. This problem has merely been theoretical investigated through the realization of arbitrary linear transformations, which provides an ingenious decomposition method to construct any desired operators (, ). Thus, multiport quantum interferometry with universal nonunitary transformation has not yet been explored, particularly in terms of providing programmable functionality for integrated quantum photons.

Here, we show that any multiport interferometry with universal nonunitary transformation can be extracted from a photonic unitary circuit (, ). Because any discrete unitary operator can be theoretically designed and experimentally realized through a decomposition strategy in terms of simple building blocks (, ), the universal nonunitary operators are thus achievable in such a concise yet efficient manner. Moreover, the tunability of phase shifts and mode mixing provides precise control of individual elements of transformation matrix, resulting in the possibility of harnessing complex unitary and nonunitary operators that are necessary in programmable integrated optics. Building on the dilation from nonunitary to unitary operators and realization of any discrete unitary transformation, we use balanced beam splitters to construct a 4 × 4 unitary transformation, wherein the unitary property of contracted 2 × 2 transformation can be dynamically controlled. To confirm its intriguing particle exchange phase difference in unitary and nonunitary transformations, paired photons are incident on the 4 × 4 photonic circuit from different input modes such that the coalescence and anticoalescence of photons are determined by the contracted 2 × 2 operators. This highly tunable multiport quantum interference may provide an alternative approach to control photon-photon interaction, thereby providing a highly versatile mechanism for large-scale and complex quantum technologies.

This work validates a promising approach to use elaborate unitary circuits to extract any desired nonunitary transformation and set the stage for advanced quantum technologies to be developed on programmable photonic platforms. We envisage that incorporating such universal nonunitary transformation into the task of quantum information processing would substantially enrich the available quantum operations and quantum functionalities in photonic integrated circuits. For example, our method allows the construct of high-dimensional nonunitary transformation for multiphoton interference, which may provide an alternative route toward the experimental implementation of various quantum logic gates (, , ).

RESULTS

Let us begin by outlining the basic method of implementing any nonunitary matrix Tm×n as shown in . Because complex Hadamard matrices relate the computational basis of a discrete Hilbert space to some mutually unbiased basis, Tm×n corresponds to m input modes and n output modes, whose physical implementation has relatively little explored. To tackle this issue, we extend the nonunitary operator Tm×n to unitary operator SN×N through matrix dilation (), wherein N represents the number of input and output modes, N≥n, and N≥m. Backed by an alternative arrangement of beam splitters and phase shifters, any unitary multiport interferometer can be achievable with optimal designs that have already been widely investigated (, ). Thus, the formalism developed for unitary transformations describing lossless N×N interferometers is available to include any m×n nonunitary transformation. For example, we note that any m×n (m≤n) linear transformation can be either directly embedded within a 2n×2n unitary transformation within a scaling factor (), or, in a more compact way, implemented by two separate m×n interferometers connected to each other via phase and amplitude modulators ().

For any target scattering matrix, a singular value decomposition is performed such that three main components—U, D, and W—are yielded. Through the analogous matrix dilation, the orthogonal matrices U and W, the diagonal matrix D can be implemented in a larger-scale multiport quantum interferometry (see the Supplementary Materials for a concrete example). Specifically, if the beam splitter exhibits as a unitary scattering matrix with φpe=π, then identical photons and symmetrically entangled photons that arrive simultaneously on this unitary beam splitter from different input modes would bunch into a common output mode, which corresponds to the well-known HOM-dip as a direct result of bosonic nature. In contrast, if the beam splitter exhibits as a nonunitary scattering matrix with φpe=0, then identical photons and symmetrically entangled photons that arrive simultaneously on this nonunitary beam splitter from different input modes would antibunch into opposite output modes, which corresponds to the anomalous HOM-peak that mimics the fermonic nature. Thus, HOM interference provides an observable certification for unitary and nonunitary property of the scattering matrix. Differently, for unitary beam splitter, antisymmetrically entangled photons with φω=π would behave as HOM-peak that results from their bosonic nature and unitary property of the scattering matrix. Analogously, for nonunitary beam splitter, antisymmetrically entangled photons with φω=π would behave as HOM-peak that also mimics the fermonic nature. Therefore, the selective input and output modes and tunable phase shifts can be used to implement programmable universal nonunitary transformations and provide a versatile tool to harness highly tunable quantum interference.

DISCUSSION

By embedding any nonunitary matrix within a larger-scale unitary matrix, we present a concise yet efficient method to deal with universal m×n nonunitary transformation. The selectively choosing of input and output ports allows for flexible control over the unitary property of transformation matrix, which is accompanied by the nontrivial particle exchange phase. To confirm this intriguing quantum phenomenon, we implement a HOM experiment to observe the particle exchange statistics. With respect to the conventional unitary operator, identical photons and symmetrically entangled photons exhibit the HOM-dip, and antisymmetrically entangled photons exhibit the HOM-peak, which corresponds to the well-known HOM interference patterns as a direct result of bosonic nature. In contrast, with respect to nonunitary operators, identical photons and symmetrically entangled photons exhibit the HOM-peak, and antisymmetrically entangled photons exhibit the HOM-dip, which corresponds to the anomalous HOM interference patterns that mimic the fermonic nature.

Notably, the tunable photon-photon interaction in multiport quantum interferometry has great potential in designing novel functions and complex quantum gate in integrated photonic circuits, particularly for those programmable quantum information tasks such as quantum simulation and quantum computation. Loss-induced nonunitary operator can naturally describe the interaction between open physical systems and may be connected to non-Hermitian systems that open avenues for quantum emulation and ultrasensitive metrology. Additionally, universal m×n nonunitary transformation can provide an opportunity for harnessing multiphoton and multimode quantum information processing, which allows for great benefit from the capability to introduce sophisticated nonunitary realization into integrated quantum optics and paves the way toward large-scale and programmable quantum information platforms.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

In our experiment, we pump two mutually orthogonally oriented 2-mm-long type II periodically poled potassium titanyl phosphate crystals with a 405-nm continuous-wave grating-stabilized laser diode. The crystals provide type II collinear phase matching with the pump (p), signal (s), and idler (i) photons at center wavelengths of λp ≈ 405 nm and λs,i ≈ 810 nm at a temperature of 23.5°C. A pair of half-wave and quarter-wave plates in the pump beam are used to set the incident polarization at a diagonal state with a tunable phase shifter as (∣Hp〉+exp(iφω)∣Vp〉)/2, ensuring that both mutually orthogonal crystals are equally pumped. Consequently, the SPDC process in the first crystal yields the transformation of ∣Hp〉→∣Hω1Vω2〉 and, in the second crystal, produces the transformation of ∣Vp〉→∣Vω1Hω2〉, where H and V denote horizontal and vertical polarizations, respectively. Followed by a polarization beam splitter that routes orthogonal polarization states into distinct spatial modes, the resultant quantum state can be expressed as ∣Ψ〉=(∣ω1ω2〉+exp(iφω)∣ω2ω1〉)/2. The frequency detunings Δ=∣ω1−ω2∣ and the relative phases φω can be continuously tuned by adjusting its phase matching temperature. By adjusting its phase matching temperature, the frequency detunings Δ and the relative phases φω can be continuously tuned from ∆=0 THz at 23.5°C (symmetric entanglement with degenerate wavelength) to ∆=2.2 THz and φω=π at 42°C (antisymmetric entanglement with nondegenerate wavelengths) and ∆=4.2 THz and φω=0 at 60°C (symmetric entanglement with nondegenerate wavelengths).

Subsequently, these frequency-entangled or frequency-degenerate photons are coupled into single-mode fibers such that only the Gaussian mode can be transmitted into the 4 × 4 quantum interferometry from different input ports. We use four single-photon detectors labeled by A, B, C, and D to measure the probability of these photons appearing at four different output ports. The paired photons are detected by silicon avalanche photon diodes, and two-photon coincidence events are identified using a fast electronic AND gate when paired photons arrive at their corresponding detectors within a coincidence time window as 1 ns. As a result, these coincidence events can be analyzed from different configurations of two random detectors (i.e., AB, AC, AD, BC, BD, and CD).
