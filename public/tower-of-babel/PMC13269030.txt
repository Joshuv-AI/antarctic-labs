Clinical Characteristics and Antimicrobial Resistance of Escherichia coli Bloodstream Infections in Neonates: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Caifang Ma, Zhenjia Liu, Linlin Li, Wei Dai, Jiahui Liang, Jing Guo, Zhan Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEscherichia coli (E. coli) bloodstream infections (BSIs) are a significant public health concern. This study aimed to examine the clinical characteristics, laboratory indicators, outcomes, and antimicrobial resistance patterns of E. coli BSIs in neonatal populations.Materials and MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted from January 2017 to December 2023 at Guangxi Children’s Hospital in China. We compared neonatal and non-neonatal patient groups, analyzing clinical parameters, lab results, and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns. Using multivariable regression analysis, we identified specific laboratory markers as prognostic indicators for ICU admission in newborns with E. coli BSIs. Diagnostic efficacy was assessed using ROC curves, sensitivity, and specificity.ResultsThe study analyzed 179 hospitalized patients with E. coli BSIs, including 91 neonates (0–28 days) and 88 older pediatric cases. Among the neonates, 52 (57%) required ICU admission, while the remaining 39 (43%) were managed in general pediatric wards. In neonates, the highest resistance rate was observed for ampicillin (83.52%), followed by sulfamethoxazole (51.65%) and cefazolin (46.15%). Non-neonates exhibited higher resistance to ceftazidime (P=0.022), imipenem (P=0.021), meropenem (P=0.023), and other antibiotics. Multivariate analysis showed that decreased albumin (ALB) and total bile acid (TBA) levels, along with increased direct bilirubin (DBIL), were independent risk factors for ICU admission. Findings indicated that when ALB <33.06 g/L, TBA <8.2 μmol/L, and DBIL >14.64 μmol/L, the risk of ICU admission for neonates increased.ConclusionE. coli BSIs in neonates and non-neonates exhibit distinct clinical features and resistance profiles. Low ALB and TBA, and high DBIL were found to be important predictors of ICU admission in neonates. Close monitoring of these markers can improve outcomes for high-risk neonates with E. coli BSIs.Keywords: neonate, ICU, antibiotic, factor

Introduction

Bacterial bloodstream infections (BSIs) lead to significant mortality and morbidity in pediatric hospital patients.1 In 2017, roughly 20 million pediatric sepsis cases impacted children < 5 years, with 5 million cases occurring in those aged 5 to < 19 years, resulting in 3.5 million fatalities.2 Neonatal infections, in particular, pose a significant global health burden, accounting for approximately 6.3 million cases and 23,000 deaths annually.3 Therefore, choosing the right empirical antibiotics is crucial when dealing with suspected infections in neonates, as this decision directly impacts both mortality rates and the prevention of long-term complications.

Escherichia coli (E. coli) is a significant opportunistic pathogen responsible for a range of extraintestinal infections, including serious conditions such as urinary tract infections, septicemia, and neonatal bloodstream infections.4 A 2016–2020 China-based multi-center analysis found that the majority of episodes of pediatric bloodstream infections stemmed from E. coli.5 Extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) production is a predominant antimicrobial resistance (AMR) mechanism in the Enterobacteriaceae family. These bacteria encode ESBL genes in their plasmids or chromosomes, producing β-lactam-hydrolyzing enzymes, and have earned a spot on the World Health Organization (WHO) list of priority pathogens.6 For generations, β-lactam antibiotics have been widely used to treat infections in children. However, over the past twenty years, ESBL-producing strains have emerged as a major global health issue and are among the most common forms of AMR.7,8 Numerous studies report a high incidence of ESBL-producing organisms in various healthcare settings.9 In China, approximately 16% of E. coli strains from community-acquired infections have shown ESBL production.10 Microbial resistance is positively correlated with drug usage frequency, with reports indicating that resistance patterns vary significantly among patients of different ages.11 However, it remains unclear if similar trends exist for bacteria isolated from other clinical sources. The widespread use of broad-spectrum antibiotics has accelerated the emergence of drug-resistant E. coli strains, posing a significant challenge in the management of neonatal bacterial infections. Therefore, investigating the clinical characteristics and AMR patterns of E. coli infections in neonates is crucial for the prevention and management of E. coli BSIs.

Despite extensive research on E. coli and its role in pediatric bloodstream infections, there is a limited understanding of the distinct clinical characteristics and antimicrobial resistance profiles in neonates compared to non-neonates. This gap in knowledge is significant, particularly considering the vulnerability of neonates and their unique clinical presentation, which may require different therapeutic strategies. The aim of this research was to compare the clinical features, laboratory test indicators, and antimicrobial resistance patterns of E. coli infections in neonatal and non-neonatal patients. Our team has previously evaluated independent predictors of poor prognosis in neonates with E. coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae in clinical data.12 In this study, we used laboratory test indicators to further analyze the risk factors for ICU admission in neonates with E. coli bloodstream infection. Our findings may provide a scientific basis for more targeted prevention and control measures.

Materials and Methods

Study Setting and Patients

This retrospective observational cohort study was conducted at the Maternal and Child Health facility of the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region from January 2017 to December 2023. This research examined E. coli bloodstream infections (BSIs) in children, and only the initial E. coli isolate identified from blood cultures was included in the analysis. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) patients aged 0–14 years; (b) meeting bloodstream infection diagnostic standards;13 (c) E. coli infection confirmed via blood culture; and (d) complete clinical documentation. In contrast, the study rigorously excluded patients lacking sufficient medical documentation. ICU admission was defined as the requirement for intensive monitoring and life-support interventions, including but not limited to mechanical ventilation, invasive monitoring (such as central venous pressure monitoring), and the use of vasopressors.14 This study adhered to established criteria for ICU admission used in neonatal and pediatric intensive care units, which include the need for life-sustaining treatments or unstable clinical conditions necessitating constant monitoring. This research protocol received formal approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Maternal and Child Health Hospital in Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region (No. 2023-3-42). All study procedures were conducted in strict compliance with relevant regulatory requirements and ethical guidelines.

Data Collection and Definitions

Comprehensive patient data and clinical characteristics were gathered from the hospital’s digital health records system. We extracted fundamental details (gender, age), along with medical background (duration of hospitalization, cesarean section), medical interventions (invasive procedures and device usage, antibiotic treatment), and laboratory variables. For laboratory markers, data for albumin (ALB), total bile acids (TBA), direct bilirubin (DBIL), and other markers were collected at the time of hospital admission, prior to ICU admission, in order to assess their potential as predictive markers for ICU admission. Bloodstream infection was diagnosed based on positive blood cultures for E. coli, combined with clinical signs of infection such as fever, tachycardia, or hypotension, and confirmed by the clinical team following hospital protocols.15 Missing data were handled using multiple imputation techniques for datasets with missing variables, and complete case analysis was performed for other variables to avoid bias in the final analysis.

Microbiological Methods

All bacterial samples were cultured on blood agar plates at 37°C in an incubator for 18–24 hours. After incubation, bacterial identification was performed using the Zhuhai DL-96II automatic bacterial identification system (Zhuhai DL Biotech Co., Ltd., China) or the VITEK2 Compact system (BioMérieux, Marcy l’Etoile, France). Antibiotic susceptibility testing on the isolates was performed using three different methods: the Kirby-Bauer method, the Zhuhai DL-96II automated sensitivity testing, or the VITEK 2 Compact platform. The study also examined minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for several standard clinical antibiotics, including gentamicin, sulfamethoxazole, ciprofloxacin, ceftazidime, cefazolin, ticarcillin-clavulanic acid, chloramphenicol, amoxicillin, cefuroxime, minocycline, levofloxacin, and cefoxitin. Results were interpreted according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100 guidelines.15 The strain used for quality control was E. coli ATCC 25922 (National Center for Clinical Laboratories, Beijing, China). All microbiological methods were standardized according to the CLSI M100 guidelines to ensure consistency in identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 25.0. For data that followed a normal distribution, results are reported as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD) and were evaluated using the Student’s t-test. For non-normally distributed data, results are reported as median [interquartile range, M (P25-P75)], and comparisons were made using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and compared using the χ2-test and Fisher’s exact test. To ensure the robustness and reliability of the predictive model, both laboratory and clinical parameters were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis. Clinical parameters, such as previous antibiotic use, age, comorbidities, and clinical presentation (eg, fever, tachycardia, and hypotension), were considered in addition to laboratory markers like albumin (ALB), total bile acids (TBA), and direct bilirubin (DBIL). In the initial univariate analysis, clinical parameters such as antibiotic exposure and clinical signs did not reach statistical significance (P < 0.05) and were therefore excluded from the final multivariate regression model. However, laboratory variables consistently demonstrated stronger associations with ICU admission, thus emphasizing their predictive value.

A bidirectional stepwise selection method was employed to identify independent predictors of ICU admission in neonates with confirmed E. coli infection. All variables demonstrating statistical significance (P < 0.05) during the initial univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. The inclusion of both laboratory and clinical variables in future studies could further refine the predictive model. The cutoff thresholds and diagnostic efficacy of the model were assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, sensitivity, and specificity. A two-tailed P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographics and Clinical Profiles of 179 Neonates and Non-Neonates Infected with Escherichia coli Bloodstream Pathogens

Throughout the investigation timeframe, Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region’s Maternal and Child Health Hospital processed a total of 180,297 blood culture analyses, revealing a 1.88% positivity rate (3394 out of 180,297). Of these cases, 392 were confirmed to be E. coli-positive cultures. Following a thorough case evaluation, the remaining 179 cases were selected for analysis, as shown in the flow diagram in Figure 1. Among the patients, 91 (50.8%) were neonates, and 88 (49.2%) were non-neonates. The study included 50 male neonates (54.9%). The median length of hospital stay for neonates was 15 (17) days, with mechanical ventilation being the most common invasive procedure or device used, affecting 40.7% of neonatal patients. Among hospitalized neonates, 52 (57%) required admission to the intensive care unit (ICU), while the remaining 39 (43%) were managed in general pediatric wards. Regarding antibiotic treatment, β-lactam-β-lactamase inhibitor combinations were administered most frequently to neonates (15.4%), whereas non-neonates predominantly received cephalosporins (53.4%). The baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory variables of these 179 patients with BSI caused by E. coli are outlined in Table 1 and Table 2. The results indicated that neonates had significantly higher urinary catheterization rates and lower prior β-lactam use (including penicillins, cephalosporins, and β-lactam-β-lactamase inhibitors) than non-neonates, along with significantly lower values in most laboratory tests (P < 0.05).

Antibiotic Resistance of E. coli in Neonates and Non-Neonates

The diversity in antibiotic resistance of E. coli between neonates and non-neonates is clearly depicted in Table 3. Among neonates, ampicillin showed the highest resistance rate at 83.52%, followed by sulfamethoxazole (51.65%) and cefazolin (46.15%). Notably, none of the neonatal isolates were resistant to imipenem and meropenem. In non-neonates, resistance to the same top three antibiotics was substantially higher: 85.23% for ampicillin, 68.18% for sulfamethoxazole, and 47.73% for cefazolin. In comparison, the resistance rates of ceftazidime (P=0.022), imipenem (P=0.021), meropenem (P=0.023), ticarcillin-clavulanic acid (P=0.011), piperacillin/tazobactam (P=0.026), ampicillin-sulbactam (P=0.019) in non-neonates were significantly greater than those in neonates.

Association Between Laboratory Variables and Neonates’ Progression to the ICU with E. coli BSI

We performed univariate and multivariate analyses to explore the association between both laboratory and clinical variables and the progression of neonates with E. coli bloodstream infection (BSI) to the ICU. The findings are presented in Table 4. The univariate analysis demonstrated that white blood cell count (WBC), platelet count (PLT), albumin (ALB), total bile acid (TBA), total bilirubin (TBIL), direct bilirubin (DBIL), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), previous antibiotic use, age, and comorbidities were associated with ICU admission. The multivariate analysis revealed that ALB, TBA and DBIL were independent predictors of ICU admission. Subsequently, ROC curve analysis was used to determine the maximum Youden index for each diagnostic marker after performing comprehensive multifactor screening. The optimal threshold for each marker was identified by calculating the maximum Youden index (sensitivity + specificity - 1), which helped establish the most effective cutoff values. These findings are shown in Table 5 and Figure 2. The optimal cutoff value for ALB was established at 33.06 g/L, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.792 (95% CI 0.695–0.870). Sensitivity and specificity were observed at 65.38% and 87.18%, respectively. The optimal DBIL threshold value was determined to be 0.669 (95% CI 0.560–0.766), with sensitivity and specificity of 62.50% and 71.79%, respectively. For TBA, the best threshold value was identified at 0.638 (95% CI 0.527–0.739), with sensitivity and specificity of 41.67% and 86.84%, respectively. The addition of clinical parameters like age and previous antibiotic exposure further refined the predictive model and enhanced its clinical applicability in predicting ICU admission for neonates with E. coli BSI. However, the exclusion of some clinical variables and reliance on laboratory markers alone suggests the need for future studies to incorporate a broader range of clinical factors to improve the model’s robustness.

Discussion

Bloodstream infections (BSIs) remain one of the leading causes of sepsis in pediatric patients, particularly in neonates and infants, who are more susceptible due to their immature immune systems.13 Neonatal sepsis, especially caused by E. coli, presents a substantial challenge to healthcare systems globally, given its association with high mortality and long-term morbidity. The growing incidence of sepsis in neonates and the associated increase in antibiotic resistance (AMR) highlight the urgent need for refined therapeutic strategies and early diagnostic markers. Recent research stresses the importance of monitoring local AMR patterns and the use of regional antibiograms to inform empirical treatment decisions.16 This study contributes to this evolving field by retrospectively examining the clinical characteristics, antibiotic resistance patterns, and inflammatory biomarkers of neonatal and non-neonatal E. coli BSIs.

Our findings revealed a statistically significant difference between the neonatal and non-neonatal groups in terms of their clinical and microbiological characteristics. Notably, the non-neonatal group demonstrated a higher incidence of prior antibiotic exposure, particularly to broad-spectrum β-lactam agents such as penicillins, cephalosporins, and β-lactam-β-lactamase inhibitors. This higher exposure to antibiotics in non-neonates likely explains, in part, the increased prevalence of resistant E. coli strains, particularly those producing extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL), which have been linked to prior antibiotic use.17 Neonates, in contrast, have less prior exposure to antibiotics, which partially accounts for the lower resistance observed in this group. However, their higher susceptibility to ESBL-producing E. coli underscores the vulnerability of this population due to their underdeveloped immune systems, making them more susceptible to resistant infections. Previous studies have shown that empirical antibiotic treatment in neonates often involves broad-spectrum antibiotics, increasing the likelihood of resistance development. This has been exacerbated by the indiscriminate use of antibiotics in the early stages of life to prevent potential infections.18

Antimicrobial resistance in E. coli infections exhibited distinctive patterns between neonates and non-neonates. Neonates showed lower resistance to several antibiotics, particularly imipenem and meropenem, compared to older children. This finding is likely due to the more limited use of antibiotics in neonates, who typically receive supportive care with fewer pharmacological interventions. In contrast, non-neonates receive more frequent antibiotic treatments, leading to higher resistance rates, particularly against commonly used agents such as ticarcillin-clavulanic acid and piperacillin/tazobactam. The greater antibiotic exposure in older children increases the selective pressure for resistant strains, as non-neonates often undergo multiple rounds of antibiotic treatment in the course of their hospital stays, which accelerates the development of AMR. This aligns with previous reports indicating that prolonged or repeated antibiotic courses are a major factor in the development of multi-drug resistant (MDR) organisms, including ESBL-producing E. coli.11,19

Our study observed particularly high resistance to ampicillin, sulfamethoxazole, and cefazolin in neonates, with resistance rates of 83.52%, 51.65%, and 46.15%, respectively. These rates align with findings from previous studies showing that E. coli isolates from neonatal sepsis often demonstrate high resistance to these first-line antibiotics, especially ampicillin and third-generation cephalosporins.20 The 37% resistance rate to ceftriaxone observed in neonates is concerning, as ceftriaxone is commonly used to treat neonatal infections due to its broad-spectrum activity. However, the overuse and frequent use of ceftriaxone in neonates has contributed to the rising resistance observed in this cohort, as seen in studies that show increasing resistance rates to ceftriaxone in neonatal populations.21 On the other hand, both cohorts showed minimal resistance to piperacillin/tazobactam and imipenem, indicating that these antibiotics may still serve as effective options for treating E. coli infections, even in the face of increasing AMR.

Neonatal sepsis, particularly caused by E. coli, continues to represent a significant burden on healthcare systems due to its high morbidity and mortality. While the use of intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (IAP) has reduced the incidence of early-onset GBS sepsis, the shift towards E. coli as the predominant cause of neonatal sepsis is concerning. This increase is likely attributable to the immaturity of the neonatal immune system, which leaves neonates particularly susceptible to severe infections caused by E. coli and other opportunistic pathogens.22,23 Our study demonstrated that neonates admitted to the ICU exhibited lower levels of leukocytes, platelets, and albumin, and elevated levels of serum total bilirubin, conjugated bilirubin, and total bile acids (TBA). These biomarkers are indicative of the systemic inflammatory response and reflect the severity of sepsis in neonates. Platelets and albumin are commonly used as indicators of infection severity, and a reduction in these markers has been strongly associated with poor outcomes. Furthermore, elevated bilirubin levels, particularly DBIL and TBA, are consistent with the involvement of the liver in systemic inflammation.24–26

Importantly, we demonstrated that serum ALB, TBA, and DBIL were independent predictors of ICU admission in neonates with E. coli BSIs. The inclusion of TBA, while not a standard marker in clinical practice for sepsis, was based on recent findings that indicate its potential as a marker of systemic inflammation, particularly in liver dysfunction related to infections. Hypoalbuminemia, traditionally linked to malnutrition, has recently been identified as a reliable indicator of systemic inflammation and sepsis severity. Recent studies have shown that decreased ALB levels within the first week of admission are associated with an increased risk of mortality, particularly in neonates, making ALB a crucial prognostic marker.27,28 A multi-site investigation reveals that a decrease in serum ALB within a week of admission is a more reliable predictor of mortality among younger patients.29 Additionally, elevated DBIL levels are commonly associated with infections of gut-derived origin, further reflecting the severity of inflammation and organ dysfunction.30 Our study’s ROC curve analysis identified 33.06 g/L as the optimal ALB threshold for predicting ICU admission in neonates with E. coli BSIs, with an AUC of 0.792. Similarly, the AUCs for TBA and DBIL were found to be 0.638 and 0.669, respectively, further solidifying their role as biomarkers for ICU admission. These findings suggest that the inclusion of ALB, TBA, and DBIL in routine clinical monitoring could significantly enhance the prediction of ICU admission and improve the management of neonates at high risk for sepsis.

The study’s inclusion of TBA as a biomarker for predicting ICU admission in neonates warrants further discussion. While TBA is not typically used in routine clinical evaluations for infection or liver function in this context, our results suggest that it may serve as an important marker for severe neonatal sepsis, reflecting the systemic inflammatory response and liver involvement. The pathophysiological role of TBA in neonatal infections remains a complex issue and requires further investigation to determine its clinical relevance. Elevated TBA levels could indicate liver dysfunction and its involvement in the inflammatory process, which is particularly crucial for neonates with a high risk of developing severe infections. Thus, while the use of TBA as a predictive biomarker in neonatal sepsis may be considered non-standard, its inclusion in this study offers potential insights into early diagnosis and prognostic evaluation. Further validation and external studies are necessary to confirm its clinical utility and establish definitive diagnostic thresholds.

The main limitation of this research is its single-center design, which restricts the generalizability of the findings. The relatively small sample size of E. coli cases also limited the statistical power of certain analyses. Furthermore, the study did not examine the presence of specific drug resistance genes, which could have provided deeper insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying AMR in neonates. And then, Despite the initial inclusion of clinical parameters such as previous antibiotic exposure and age in the univariate analysis, these variables did not demonstrate sufficient statistical significance (P < 0.05) to be included in the final multivariate regression model. Therefore, the model predominantly relied on laboratory variables, which showed stronger predictive power for ICU admission in neonates. The absence of other clinical parameters may limit the overall applicability of the model, which should be considered a limitation of this study. Future studies should consider incorporating a broader range of clinical parameters, such as antibiotic exposure and clinical presentation, into the prediction models to enhance their robustness and applicability in diverse settings. Finally, the absence of long-term follow-up data means that we could not assess the long-term outcomes of neonates with E. coli BSIs. Future prospective, multi-center studies with larger sample sizes and extended follow-up are essential to validate these findings and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing neonatal E. coli BSIs and their treatment outcomes.

Conclusion

This study investigates the clinical characteristics and laboratory parameters of neonates and non-neonates with E. coli-induced bloodstream infections (BSIs). We identified high resistance rates to ampicillin, sulfamethoxazole, and cefazolin in neonates, highlighting the challenge of antimicrobial resistance. While clinical variables were initially evaluated, the study primarily focused on laboratory markers due to their stronger predictive value for ICU admission. Key biomarkers, including ALB and TBA levels, and DBIL, were found to be significantly associated with ICU admission in neonates with E. coli BSIs. Optimal diagnostic thresholds for these biomarkers were identified, which may provide helpful insights for therapeutic decisions, although further external validation is needed. It is important to acknowledge that the exclusion of clinical variables from the final predictive model presents a limitation, as integrating both clinical and laboratory parameters would enhance the robustness and clinical applicability of the prediction model. These findings contribute to neonatal sepsis management, but their clinical application requires additional confirmation and validation in multi-center prospective studies. Future research should aim to incorporate more comprehensive clinical variables—such as antibiotic exposure history, clinical symptoms, and comorbidities—into predictive models to improve their accuracy and generalizability.
