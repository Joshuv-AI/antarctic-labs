Abstract
. Fix a finite field 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} and let A ∈ 𝔽 q n × n A\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n} be a uniformly random n × n n\times n matrix over 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} . The asymptotic distribution of the determinant det ( A ) \det(A) is well-understood, but the asymptotic distribution of the permanent per ⁡ ( A ) \operatorname{per}(A) is still something of a mystery. In this paper we make a first step in this direction, proving that per ⁡ ( A ) \operatorname{per}(A) is significantly more uniform than det ⁡ ( A ) \operatorname{det}(A) .



1.
Introduction

Two important matrix parameters are the
determinant

and
permanent
: for an
n\times n
matrix
A=(a_{i,j})
,
they are defined as

(1.1)

\det(A)=\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\operatorname{sign}(\pi)\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,\pi(i)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{per}(A)=\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,\pi(i)}.

If we fix a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and let
A\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}
be a uniformly random
n\times n
matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, it is easy to compute the limiting distribution of
\det(A)
(this is essentially equivalent to computing the number of invertible matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, which goes all the way back to Galois
[
30
, p. 280]
).

Fact 1.1
.

For a prime power
q
, let

\alpha_{q}=1-\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{-i}).

Then, for a uniformly random
n\times n
matrix
A\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}
and any
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr[\det(A)=x]=\begin{cases}\alpha_{q}&\text{if }x=0\\
(1-\alpha_{q})/(q-1)&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Notably, this limiting distribution is
not
uniform over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
: the determinant is significantly more likely to take the value zero than any other value. For example, when
q=3
we have
\alpha_{q}\approx 0.44
, which is significantly larger than
1/q=1/3
. Roughly speaking, this is because the event
\{\det(A)=0\}
describes the rows of
A
being linearly dependent: if we reveal
A
row-by-row, and at some step we discover that a row lies in the span of the previous rows, this tells us that
\det(A)=0
regardless of what happens in future rows. On the other hand, if after revealing the second-last row we still haven’t discovered a linear dependence relation between the rows, then it is easy to see that the conditional distribution of
\det(A)
is uniform over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Unlike the determinant, the permanent does not seem to have any linear-algebraic meaning (unless our field has characteristic 2, in which case the determinant and permanent are equal). It is tempting to conjecture that, if
\mathbb{F}_{q}
does not have characteristic 2, then the permanent of a uniformly random matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is asymptotically uniform, as follows.

Conjecture 1.2
.

Fix a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of odd characteristic.
Then, for a uniformly random
n\times n
matrix
A\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}
,
and any
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr[\operatorname{per}(A)=x]=\frac{1}{q}.

This conjecture seems to have been floating around the community for a while, but we are not completely sure about its origins. Permanents of random matrices over finite fields have received quite some interest in the computer science community (see e.g.
[
8
,
20
,
9
,
10
,
3
]
), due to a phenomenon called
random self-reducibility
, but surprisingly we were not able to find any study of the asymptotic distribution of
\operatorname{per}(A)
in this literature. The earliest reference we could find was in the open problem session of a 2017 workshop on combinatorics at the Mathematisches Forschungsinstitut Oberwolfach
[
16
]
, in which Swastik Kopparty asked for the limiting distribution of the permanent over
\mathbb{F}_{3}
. The
\mathbb{F}_{3}
case of
Section

1
also appears explicitly in a recent paper by Scheinerman
[
29
]
(backed by quite convincing computational evidence), and the general case of
Section

1
appeared in a very recent paper of Ghasemi, Gross and Kopparty
[
11
]
. See also a related conjecture by Esperet
[
7
]
(motivated by questions in graph theory).

Note that, by symmetry (through rescaling any row),
\operatorname{per}(A)
is equally likely to take any nonzero value. Thus, in order to understand the distribution of
\operatorname{per}(A)
it suffices to understand
\Pr[\operatorname{per}(A)=0]
. More specifically, the assertion of
Section

1
is equivalent to
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr[\operatorname{per}(A)=0]=1/q
.

Although we are not able to prove
Section

1
, we are at least
able to prove that the permanent is (asymptotically)
more
uniform than the determinant,
as follows (recall that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr[\det(A)=0]=\alpha_{q}>1/q
and observe that
\alpha_{q}-1/q
has order of magnitude
1/q^{2}
).

Theorem 1.3
.

Fix a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of odd characteristic. For a uniformly random
n\times n
matrix
A\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}
,
we have

(1.2)

\Pr[\operatorname{per}(A)=0]\geq\frac{1}{q}

for all
n
, and we have the
strict
inequality

(1.3)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Pr[\operatorname{per}(A)=0]<\alpha_{q}.

Moreover, for all
n\geq 3
we have

(1.4)

\Pr[\operatorname{per}(A)=0]\leq\frac{1}{q}+\frac{C}{q^{3}}

for some absolute constant
C
.

For
n=2
one has
\Pr[\operatorname{per}(A)=0]=1/q+\Theta(1/q^{2})
, so the assumption
n\geq 3
in
Equation

1.4
cannot be omitted.

We remark that a theorem in a similar spirit was previously proved by Budrevich and Guterman
[
2
]
(see also the simpler proof in
[
1
]
, and the earlier work
[
5
]
): restated in probabilistic language, their main result was that for any finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of odd characteristic, and any
n\geq 3
, the zero-permanent probability is strictly less than the zero-determinant probability. However, the gap they established between these two probabilities tends to zero very rapidly as
n\to\infty
. Budrevich and Guterman were motivated by
Pólya’s permanent problem
(see e.g.
[
27
,
25
,
33
]
), which asks to what extent it is possible to re-express the permanent of a matrix in terms of a determinant. The gap established in
[
2
]
shows that there is no bijective “converter”
f:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}
such that
\operatorname{per}(A)=\det(f(A))
for all
A\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}
. Note that
Theorem

1.3
implies the stronger result that there is no such converter that works for
almost
all
A\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}
.

1.1.
General distributions

It turns out that
Section

1
is
universal
, in the sense that the distribution of the determinant of a random matrix
A\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}
does not depend very strongly on the distribution of the entries of
A
. For example, it is known that for
any
non-constant distribution
\mu
over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
of prime order, if
A\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n\times n}
is a random matrix with independent
\mu
-distributed entries, then
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr[\det(A)=0]=\alpha_{p}
. This seems to have been first proved in 1990 by Charlap, Rees and Robbins
[
4
]
, though similar theorems with more restrictive assumptions were proved much earlier by Kozlov
[
18
]
and Kovalenko and Levitskaja
[
17
]
, and a very general theorem over arbitrary finite fields (necessarily with a more technical statement) was later proved by Kahn and Komlós
[
15
]
. See also
[
24
,
23
,
36
,
37
,
26
,
6
,
21
]
for some more recent work on universality of various algebraic properties for random matrices over finite fields.

We can prove a theorem in the spirit of
Theorem

1.3
for arbitrary (non-constant) distributions over finite fields of prime order, as follows.

Theorem 1.4
.

Fix a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
of prime order
p\geq 3
. Then, there is
\delta_{p}>0
such that the following holds.
Let
\mu
be a probability distribution over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
supported on more than one value. If
A\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n\times n}
is a random matrix with independent
\mu
-distributed entries, then for every
z\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
we have

(1.5)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Pr[\operatorname{per}(A)=z]\leq\alpha_{p}-\delta_{p}

and

(1.6)

\frac{1}{p}-\frac{C}{p^{3}}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Pr[\operatorname{per}(A)=z]\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Pr[\operatorname{per}(A)=z]\leq\frac{1}{p}+\frac{C}{p^{3}}.

for some absolute constant
C
.

Remark
.

For simplicity we have stated
Theorem

1.4
only for prime fields, but our proof approach surely can be adapted to arbitrary finite fields, under some kind of non-degeneracy assumption on the distributions of the entries. Unfortunately, our proof approach does not seem to be compatible with the weakest non-degeneracy conditions in the literature. For example, Kahn and Komlós
[
15
]
proved that as long as
\mu
is a distribution over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
that is not supported on an affine translate of a subfield of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, then for a random matrix
A
with independent
\mu
-distributed entries we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr[\det(A)=0]=\alpha_{q}
. In this very general setting we do not even know how to prove the inequality
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Pr[\operatorname{per}(A)=x]\leq\alpha_{q}
.

Remark
.

In combinatorial random matrix theory, perhaps the most intensively studied class of random matrices is the class of random
sign matrices
. Letting
A\in\{-1,1\}^{n\times n}
be a uniformly random
n\times n
matrix with
\pm 1
entries, in spectacular recent work, Tikhomirov
[
32
]
proved that
\Pr[\det(A)=0]=(1/2+o(1))^{n}
(see also the surveys
[
34
,
35
,
12
,
28
]
). There has been quite some interest in permanents of random sign matrices (see e.g.
[
31
,
22
,
19
,
13
]
), but still much less is known about permanents than determinants. It seems quite plausible that
\operatorname{per}(A)
and
\det(A)
have very similar behaviour, but it is worth noting that
Equation

1.6
gives one of the first ways in which the distributions of
\operatorname{per}(A)
and
\det(A)

differ
: their statistics mod
p
are quite different, for all primes
p\geq 3
.

1.2.
Overview of the paper and proofs

It would be possible to give a unified proof of
Theorems

1.3
and
1.4
, but we found it to be clearer to present the proofs separately.

In
Section

2
we give the short proof of
Theorem

1.3
. We reveal the rows of our random matrix one-by-one and study the permanents of submatrices as we go, inductively showing that “nonzero permanents are at least as likely as nonzero determinants”. Then, in the final few rows we proceed slightly differently, exploiting a certain symmetry property of the permanent.

In
Section

3
we prove
Theorem

1.4
. The overall strategy is the same, but we need to additionally exploit the fact that if we add a large number of independent
\mu
-distributed random variables, the result is nearly uniform. So, if we can simultaneously keep track of the permanents of many submatrices, we can “simulate” the uniform case in the
\mu
-distributed setting. This requires a few additional ideas.

2.
Proof for the uniform case

In this section we prove
Theorem

1.3
. First, it is easy to see that the permanent enjoys an analogue of the minor expansion formula for the determinant, as we record below. This fact will allow us to study permanents recursively.

Fact 2.1
.

For any matrix
A\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times k}
, we have

\operatorname{per}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\operatorname{per}(A^{\prime}_{i})x_{i},

where
A^{\prime}_{i}
is the
(k-1)\times(k-1)
submatrix of
A
obtained by deleting the last row and the
i
th column, and
x_{i}
is the entry of
A
in the last row and the
i
th column.

Now, the following lemma shows that nonzero permanent-minors “grow
at least as well” as nonzero determinant-minors, each time we reveal an additional row (note that for a uniformly random matrix
A\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}
, when conditioning on linearly independent outcomes of the first
n-s
rows, the probability that the next row is in their span is precisely
q^{-s}
).

Definition 2.2
.

In the setting of
Theorem

1.3
, for any
0\leq s\leq n
, let
A^{\uparrow s}
be the
(n-s)\times n
submatrix
of
A
obtained by deleting the last
s
rows. Let
\mathcal{E}(s)

be the event that
A^{\uparrow s}
contains a
(n-s)\times(n-s)

submatrix with nonzero permanent.

Lemma 2.3
.

For any
s\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, and any outcome
of
A^{\uparrow s}
satisfying
\mathcal{E}(s)
, we have

\Pr[\mathcal{E}(s-1)\,|\,A^{\uparrow s}]\geq 1-q^{-s}.

Proof.

Throughout this proof, we implicitly condition on the outcome of
A^{\uparrow s}
(i.e., we treat it as a non-random object). That is to say, we only work with the randomness of the last (i.e.,
(n-s+1)
-th) row of
A^{\uparrow(s-1)}
. Denote the entries of this row by
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that the
(n-s)\times(n-s)
submatrix
of
A^{\uparrow s}
formed by the last
n-s
columns has nonzero permanent. Under this assumption, we will actually only need to use the randomness of
x_{1},\dots,x_{s}
: fix arbitrary outcomes of
x_{s+1},\dots,x_{n}
, and for the rest of the proof we also implicitly condition on these outcomes.

For
i\in\{1,\dots,s\}
, write
t_{i}
for the permanent of the
(n-s+1)\times(n-s+1)

matrix obtained from
A^{\uparrow(s-1)}
by deleting all of the first

s
columns except the
i
th. Then (recalling that we are in a conditional probability space where the only relevant source of randomness is the entries
x_{1},\dots,x_{s}
, and recalling
Section

2
), note that
t_{1},\dots,t_{s}
are all independent, and
t_{i}
is a bijective
affine-linear transformation of
x_{i}
. This means that
t_{1},\dots,t_{s}
are independent random variables that are uniform on
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The desired result follows, noting that
\mathcal{E}(s-1)
can only fail if
t_{1}=\dots=t_{s}=0
.
∎

Iterating
Section

2
shows that the permanent is
at
least
as uniform as the determinant, as follows.

Corollary 2.4
.

For any
s\in\{1,\dots,n\}
we have

\Pr[\mathcal{E}(s-1)]\geq\prod_{i=s}^{n}(1-q^{-i}),

and in particular

\Pr[\operatorname{per}(A)=0]=1-\Pr[\mathcal{E}(0)]\leq 1-\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-q^{-i})\leq\alpha_{q}.

Proof.

We have

\Pr[\mathcal{E}(s-1)]\geq\prod_{i=s}^{n}\Pr[\mathcal{E}(i-1)\,|\,\mathcal{E}(i)]\geq\prod_{i=s}^{n}(1-q^{-i}),

using
Section

2
and the fact that
\Pr[\mathcal{E}(n)]=1

(the empty matrix has permanent 1).
∎

We will also need the following elementary
lemma about
3\times 3
matrices.

Lemma 2.5
.

Fix a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of odd characteristic,
and let
B\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{3\times 3}
be a symmetric
3\times 3

matrix whose diagonal entries are all zero and whose off-diagonal
entries are all nonzero. Then
\operatorname{rank}(B)=3
.

Proof.

Write
B=(b_{i,j})
, and observe that
\det(B)=2b_{1,2}b_{1,3}b_{2,3}\neq 0
.
∎

The last ingredient we will need is Mantel’s theorem from extremal graph theory (see e.g.
[
14
, Theorem 4.6]
).

Theorem 2.6
.

If a graph on
n
vertices has more than
n^{2}/4
edges, then it contains a triangle.

Now we are ready to prove
Theorem

1.3
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

First, the desired
lower
bound in
Equation

1.2
is near-trivial. Indeed, by
Section

2
, the permanent of
A
is a linear combination of entries of
A
in the last row, where the coefficients are the permanents of the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
submatrices of
A^{\uparrow 1}
. Given an outcome of
A^{\uparrow 1}
satisfying
\mathcal{E}(1)
, at least one of the coefficients of this linear combination is nonzero. The entries of the last row of
A
are independent random variables uniform in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, so this linear combination is itself uniform in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Thus, we obtain (noting that we always have
\operatorname{per}(A)=0
if the event
\mathcal{E}(1)
does not hold)

\Pr[\operatorname{per}(A)=0]=(1/q)\Pr[\mathcal{E}(1)]+\Pr[\mathcal{E}(1)^{\mathrm{c}}]\geq 1/q.

For the rest of the proof we focus on upper-bounding
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Pr[\operatorname{per}(A)=0]
to show first
Equation

1.3
and then
Equation

1.4
. For this, we will use
Section

2
, but we will treat the last two rows slightly differently. To this end, we define a symmetric matrix
H=(h_{i,j})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}
that encodes the permanents of
(n-2)\times(n-2)
submatrices in
A^{\uparrow 2}
: for
i\neq j
, let
h_{i,j}
be the permanent
of the submatrix of
A^{\uparrow 2}
obtained by deleting columns
i
and
j
, and for each
i
let
h_{i,i}=0
. Let

\vec{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
be the last row of
A^{\uparrow 1}
(i.e., the second-last row of
A
). Then, via
Section

2
, it is not hard to see that the entries of
H\vec{x}
are the permanents of the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
submatrices in
A^{\uparrow 1}
. Thus,
\mathcal{E}(1)
holds if and only if
H\vec{x}=\vec{0}
.

If we fix an outcome of
A^{\uparrow 2}
such that
\operatorname{rank}(H)\geq 3
, then, conditionally on this outcome of
A^{\uparrow 2}
, the random vector
H\vec{x}
is uniform on some linear subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
with dimension at least 3. So,

(2.1)

\Pr[\mathcal{E}(1)\,|\,\operatorname{rank}(H)\geq 3]=\Pr[H\vec{x}=\vec{0}\,|\,\operatorname{rank}(H)\geq 3]\geq 1-q^{-3}.

Note that
Equation

2.1
gives a stronger bound than the
s=2
case of
Section

2
. The idea for the rest of the proof is to show that
\operatorname{rank}(H)\geq 3
with non-negligible probability, so we can use
Equation

2.1
to improve on the proof of
Section

2
.

Consider the graph
G
on the vertex set
\{1,\dots,n\}
where

ij
is an edge if
h_{i,j}\neq 0
. The probability that a given
pair
ij
does not form an edge of
G
is at most
\alpha_{q}
,
by
Section

2
(applied to an
(n-2)\times(n-2)
random matrix). A direct calculation shows that
\alpha_{q}<0.45
(using
q\geq 3
). Thus, by Markov’s inequality, the probability
that our graph
G
has at least
\binom{n}{2}-n^{2}/4

non-edges is at most

\frac{\alpha_{q}\cdot\binom{n}{2}}{\binom{n}{2}-n^{2}/4}=2\alpha_{q}+o(1)\leq 0.9

(assuming, as we may, that
n
is sufficiently large). If this does not occur,
then by Mantel’s theorem (
Theorem

2.6
)
G
contains a triangle. This triangle corresponds
to a
3\times 3
principal submatrix of
H
whose diagonal entries
are all zero and whose off-diagonal entries are all nonzero, so
\operatorname{rank}(H)\geq 3

by
Section

2
. In summary, letting
\mathcal{F}
be the event
that
\operatorname{rank}(H)\geq 3
, we have proved that

\Pr[\mathcal{F}]\geq 1-2\alpha_{q}-o(1)\geq 0.1.

Now, we have

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{E}(1)]

\displaystyle\geq\Pr[\mathcal{E}(1)\,|\,\mathcal{F}]\Pr[\mathcal{F}]+\Pr[\mathcal{E}(1)\,|\,\mathcal{E}(2)\setminus\mathcal{F}]\Pr[\mathcal{E}(2)\setminus\mathcal{F}]

\displaystyle\geq(1-q^{-3})\Pr[\mathcal{F}]+(1-q^{-2})(\Pr[\mathcal{E}(2)]-\Pr[\mathcal{F}])

\displaystyle\geq(1-q^{-2})\Pr[\mathcal{E}(2)]+\Pr[\mathcal{F}](q^{-2}-q^{-3})

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{i=2}^{n}(1-q^{-i})+\frac{q^{-2}}{20}.

In the second line we used
Equation

2.1
to lower-bound
\Pr[\mathcal{E}(1)\,|\,\mathcal{F}]
and we used
Section

2
to lower-bound

\Pr[\mathcal{E}(1)\,|\,\mathcal{E}(2)\setminus\mathcal{F}]
. In the last line we used
Section

2
to lower-bound
\Pr[\mathcal{E}(2)]
.

Using
Section

2
, it follows that

\Pr[\operatorname{per}(A)\neq 0]=\Pr[\mathcal{E}(0)]\geq\Pr[\mathcal{E}(0)\,|\,\mathcal{E}(1)]\Pr[\mathcal{E}(1)]\geq(1-q^{-1})\Pr[\mathcal{E}(1)]\geq\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-q^{-i})+\frac{q^{-2}}{50}\geq 1-\alpha_{q}+\frac{q^{-2}}{50}

and hence
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Pr[\operatorname{per}(A)=0]\leq\alpha_{q}-q^{-2}/50<\alpha_{q}
, proving
Equation

1.3
.

For
Equation

1.4
we use a similar approach. First, note that the probability that a given pair
ij
does not form an edge of
G
can also be bounded via
Section

2
by
1-\prod_{i=1}^{n-2}(1-q^{-i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}q^{-i}\leq 2q^{-1}
. Thus, again applying Markov’s inequality as before, the probability that
G
has at least
\binom{n}{2}-n^{2}/4

non-edges is at most

\frac{2q^{-1}\cdot\binom{n}{2}}{\binom{n}{2}-n^{2}/4}=2q^{-1}\cdot\frac{2(n-1)}{n-2}\leq 8q^{-1},

recalling the assumption
n\geq 3
. Thus, we obtain
\Pr[\mathcal{F}]\geq 1-8q^{-1}
.

From
Section

2
we furthermore obtain
\Pr[\mathcal{E}(2)]\geq\prod_{i=3}^{n}(1-q^{-i})\geq 1-\sum_{i=3}^{n}q^{-i}\geq 1-2q^{-3}
. Then, the same argument as above shows that

\Pr[\mathcal{E}(1)]\geq(1-q^{-2})\Pr[\mathcal{E}(2)]+\Pr[\mathcal{F}](q^{-2}-q^{-3})\geq(1-q^{-2})(1-2q^{-3})+(1-8q^{-1})(q^{-2}-q^{-3})\geq 1-11q^{-3}

and consequently

\Pr[\operatorname{per}(A)\neq 0]\geq(1-q^{-1})\Pr[\mathcal{E}(1)]\geq(1-q^{-1})(1-11q^{-3})\geq 1-q^{-1}-11q^{-3}.\qed

Remark
.

Here we obtained a separation between the permanent and determinant by considering the last two rows in a special way. One can improve our bounds further by considering the last
s
rows in a special way, for
s>2
. In particular, with a finite amount of casework for a given value of
s
, it seems that one can improve the error term
O(q^{-3})
to
O(q^{-s-1})
. (We worked out the details of this for
s=3
, and intend to include them in a future companion note to this paper, but the casework seems to become exponentially more complicated for larger and larger
s
.)

3.
Proof for general distributions

Now, we turn to the proof of
Theorem

1.4
. Throughout this section, we fix a prime
p\geq 3
and a probability distribution
\mu
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
supported on more than one value, and we let
A\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n\times n}
be a random matrix with independent
\mu
-distributed entries.

To prove
Theorem

1.4
, we need to adapt the proof of
Theorem

1.3
to consider
many
submatrices with nonzero permanent, simultaneously. We will consider submatrices satisfying a certain “complement-disjointness” property, as follows.

Definition 3.1
.

\Pr[t\text{ is bad}\,|\,A^{\uparrow t}]\leq\rho^{|I_{n-t}\setminus J|}\leq\rho,

and for any outcome of
A^{\uparrow t}
with
I_{t-1}\subseteq J
we have
\Pr[t\text{ is bad}\,|\,A^{\uparrow t}]=0\leq\rho
. Thus, the number of bad steps is stochastically dominated by a
\operatorname{Binomial}(T^{\prime}-T,\rho)
-distributed random variable.

Now, if
I_{n-T}\not\subseteq J
, there must be an element
h\in I_{n-T}\not\subseteq J
. This means that
I_{t}\not\subseteq J
for all
t=n-T^{\prime}+1,\dots,n-T
. Furthermore, at most
|I_{n-T^{\prime}}\setminus J|\leq|I_{n-T^{\prime}}|-|J|=T^{\prime}-2T=\lfloor\delta T\rfloor
elements outside
J
get removed during the
T^{\prime}-T
steps
n-T^{\prime}+1,\dots,n-T
. This means that at least
T^{\prime}-T-\lfloor\delta T\rfloor=T
of these steps must be bad.

Thus, writing
B\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(T^{\prime}-T,\rho)
(which has expectation
\rho(T^{\prime}-T)
and standard deviation at
most
\sqrt{T^{\prime}-T}\leq\sqrt{(1+\delta)T}
), we have

\displaystyle\Pr[I_{n-T}\not\subseteq J]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr[\text{at least }T\text{ of the steps }n-T^{\prime}+1,\dots,n-T\text{ are bad}]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr[B\geq T]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr[B\geq\rho(T^{\prime}-T)+\delta T]\leq\frac{(1+\delta)T}{\delta^{2}T^{2}}=\frac{(1+\delta)}{\delta^{2}T}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2},

noting that
\rho(T^{\prime}-T)+\delta T\leq\rho(1+\delta)T+\delta T\leq T
by definition of
T^{\prime}
and of
\delta
, and using Chebyshev’s inequality.
∎

Now we show how to deduce
Section

3
.

Proof of
Section

3
.

Let
T_{\mu}(\cdot)
be as in
Section

3.1
and let
S=T_{\mu}(\varepsilon/2)
.
Let
\ell=2\lceil n^{0.99}\rceil

and fix disjoint subsets
J_{1},\dots,J_{\ell}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}

of size
2S
. Say
J_{i}
is
good
if there is a
subset
I_{i}\subseteq J_{i}
of size
|I_{i}|=S
with
\operatorname{per}(A;I_{i})\neq 0
, and say that
J_{i}
is
bad
otherwise. Then each
J_{i}

is bad with probability at most
\varepsilon/2
by

Section

3.1
. By Markov’s inequality, the probability that more than half
of
J_{1},\dots,J_{\ell}
are bad is at most
2(\varepsilon/2)=\varepsilon
. If this does not happen, i.e. if at least
\ell/2=\lceil n^{0.99}\rceil
of the sets
J_{1},\dots,J_{\ell}
are good, then
\mathcal{E}(S,\lceil n^{0.99}\rceil)
occurs.
∎

3.2.
Approximate uniformity for linear maps

In this section we prove
Section

3
and use it to deduce
Section

3
. First, we need the following simple lemma showing anticoncentration bounds for
M\vec{x}
in terms of the rank of
M
and the entry distribution of
\vec{x}
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\rho=\max_{z\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}\mu(z)<1
, and let
\vec{x}\in F_{p}^{n}

be a vector with independent
\mu
-distributed entries. Then for any matrix
M\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{m\times n}
and any

\vec{z}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{m}
, we have

\Pr[M\vec{x}=\vec{z}]\leq\rho^{\operatorname{rank}M}.

Proof.

We can interpret
M\vec{x}=\vec{z}
as a system of linear equations. Letting
r=\operatorname{rank}M
, this system has exactly
n-r
free variables. Conditioning on any entries for the entries of
\vec{x}
corresponding to these free variables, there is at most one choice for each of the remaining
r
entries such that
M\vec{x}=\vec{z}
. For each of these entries, the probability of attaining the appropriate value is at most
\rho
, so overall
\Pr[M\vec{x}=\vec{z}]
is at most
\rho^{r}=\rho^{\operatorname{rank}M}
.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove
Section

3
.

Proof of
Section

3
.

We may assume without loss of generality
\varepsilon<1/2
. Let
\rho=\max_{z\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}\mu(z)<1
be the maximum point probability of
\mu
, and let
R\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\rho^{R}\leq p^{-r}
. Furthermore, let
Q_{\mu}(\cdot)
be as in
Section

3
, let
Q=Q_{\mu}(\varepsilon/r)
and let
K=2p^{R}Q
.

First, consider the case that
\operatorname{rank}M>R
. Then by
Section

3.2
we have

\Pr[M\vec{x}=\vec{0}]\leq\rho^{\operatorname{rank}M}\leq\rho^{R}\leq p^{-r}+\varepsilon,

as desired.

So let us from now on assume that
\operatorname{rank}(M)\leq R
. Then there is a set
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}
of size
|I|\leq R
, such that the rows indexed by
I
generate the row space of the matrix
M
. In particular, for any
i=1,\dots,K
the row space of the matrix
M[\{1,\dots,m\}\times I_{i}]
is generated by the rows indexed by
I
, and so our assumption
\operatorname{rank}M[\{1,\dots,m\}\times I_{i}]\geq r
implies
\operatorname{rank}M[I\times I_{i}]\geq r
. Thus, for each
i=1,\dots,K
, the submatrix
M[I\times I_{i}]
has
r
linearly independent columns.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{I}
be the set of “popular” vectors that appear at least
K/(2p^{R})=Q
times as columns of
M[I\times\{1,\dots,n\}]
. All but at most
p^{|I|}\cdot K/(2p^{R})\leq p^{R}\cdot K/(2p^{R})=K/2
columns of
M[I\times\{1,\dots,n\}]
are popular, so there is an index
i\in\{1,\dots,K\}
such that all columns of
M[I\times I_{i}]
are popular vectors. This means in particular that there are
r
linearly independent popular vectors
\vec{v}_{1},\dots,\vec{v}_{r}\in S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{I}
. After reordering the columns of
M
, suppose without loss of generality that the first

Q
columns of
M[I\times\{1,\dots,n\}]
are equal to
\vec{v}_{1}
, the second
Q
columns are equal to
\vec{v}_{2}
, and so on. Then, whenever we have
M\vec{x}=\vec{0}
, we obtain
M[I\times\{1,\dots,n\}]\vec{x}=\vec{0}
, and hence

(x_{1}+\dots+x_{Q})\vec{v}_{1}+\dots+(x_{(r-1)Q+1}+\dots+x_{rQ})\vec{v}_{r}+M[I\times\{rQ+1,\dots,n\}](x_{rQ+1},\dots,x_{n})^{T}=M\vec{x}=\vec{0}.

Thus, writing
\vec{z}=-M[I\times\{rQ+1,\dots,n\}](x_{rQ+1},\dots,x_{n})^{T}
and
y_{j}=x_{(j-1)Q+1}+\dots+x_{jQ}
for
j=1,\dots,r
, we obtain

\Pr[M\vec{x}=\vec{0}]\leq\Pr\!\big[M[I\times\{1,\dots,n\}]\vec{x}=\vec{0}\big]\leq\Pr[y_{1}\vec{v}_{1}+\dots+y_{r}\vec{v}_{r}=\vec{z}].

Now, since
\vec{v}_{1},\dots,\vec{v}_{r}\in F_{p}^{I}
are linearly independent, for any outcome of
\vec{z}
there is at most one possible outcome of
y_{1},\dots,y_{r}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
such that
y_{1}\vec{v}_{1}+\dots+y_{r}\vec{v}_{r}=\vec{z}
.
Note that
y_{1},\dots,y_{r}
are independent, and by
Section

3
each
y_{i}
is
(\varepsilon/r)
-almost-uniform, so we may conclude that

\Pr[M\vec{x}=\vec{0}]\leq\Pr[y_{1}\vec{v}_{1}+\dots+y_{r}\vec{v}_{r}=\vec{z}]\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{p}+\frac{\varepsilon}{r}}}\right)^{r}\leq\frac{1}{p^{r}}+\varepsilon.\qed

Now we show how to deduce
Section

3
.

Proof of
Section

3
.

For large
L
, fix an outcome of
A^{\uparrow s}
satisfying
\mathcal{E}(s,L)
, so there are disjoint size-
s
sets
I_{1},\dots,I_{L}
, each satisfying
\operatorname{per}(A;I_{i})\neq 0
. For each
j\in\{1,\dots,L\}
, let
\mathcal{F}_{j}

be the event that
\operatorname{per}(A;I_{j}\setminus\{i\})=0
for all
i\in I_{j}
,
and let
X
be the number of
j\in\{1,\dots,L\}
for which
\mathcal{F}_{j}
occurs.
Our objective is to prove that, for sufficiently large
L
, we have

\Pr[X>L-\ell]\leq\frac{1}{p^{s}}+\varepsilon.

We will do this by studying a sufficiently high moment of
X
, using
Section

3
.

Write
\vec{x}
for the
(n-s+1)
-th row of
A
. First, note that
(given our outcome of
A^{\uparrow s}
), for each
j\in\{1,\dots,L\}
,
the quantities
\operatorname{per}(A;I_{j}\setminus\{i\})
, for
i\in I_{j}
,
depend linearly on
\vec{x}
. We define an
s\times n
matrix
M_{j}

to record this linear dependence (with rows indexed by

I_{j}
, and with columns indexed by
\{1,\dots,n\}
),
as follows. For
i\in I_{j}
and
h\notin I_{j}
, write
I_{j}-i+h

to denote the size-
s
set
(I_{j}\setminus\{i\})\cup\{h\}
. Then,
for
i\in I_{j}
and
h\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, define the
(i,h)
-entry
of
M_{j}
to be

\begin{cases}\operatorname{per}(A;I_{j}-i+h)&\text{if }h\notin I_{j},\\
\operatorname{per}(A;I_{i})&\text{if }i=h,\\
0&\text{if }h\in I_{j}\setminus\{i\}.\end{cases}

It is not hard to see (using
Section

2
) that for any
i\in I_{j}
, the
i
-entry of
M_{j}\vec{x}

is precisely
\operatorname{per}(A;I_{j}\setminus\{i\})
. In particular,
\mathcal{F}_{j}
occurs if and
only if
M_{j}\vec{x}=\vec{0}
. Also note that the
s\times s
submatrix
M_{j}[I_{j}\times I_{j}]
is a diagonal matrix with all entries on the diagonal being
\operatorname{per}(A;I_{i})\neq 0
, so we have
\operatorname{rank}M_{j}[I_{j}\times I_{j}]=s
.

Now, let
K_{\mu}(\cdot,\cdot)
be as in
Section

3
and let
K=K_{\mu}(s,\varepsilon/2)
. For
each subset
J\subseteq\{1,\dots,L\}
with
|J|=K
, let
M_{J}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{(Ks)\times n}

be the matrix obtained by concatenating the matrices
M_{j}
for

j\in J
below each other (i.e. the matrix whose rows are the rows of all the matrices
M_{j}
for

j\in J
). Then,
M_{J}
satisfies the conditions of
Section

3
with
r=s
(since
\operatorname{rank}M_{J}[\{1,\dots,Ks\}\times I_{j}]\geq\operatorname{rank}M_{j}[I_{j}\times I_{j}]=s
for all
j=1,\dots,K
), and therefore

\Pr\Bigg[\bigcap_{j\in J}\mathcal{F}_{j}\Bigg]=\Pr[M_{J}\vec{x}=\vec{0}]\leq\frac{1}{p^{s}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

This implies that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\binom{X}{K}}}\right]\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{p^{s}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}}\right)\binom{L}{K},

so by Markov’s inequality,

\Pr[X>L-\ell]\leq\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\binom{X}{K}\geq\binom{L-\ell}{K}}}\right]\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{p^{s}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}}\right)\binom{L}{K}\binom{L-\ell}{K}^{-1}\leq\frac{1}{p^{s}}+\varepsilon

for sufficiently large
L
.
∎
