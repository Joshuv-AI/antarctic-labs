MOG Antibody‐Associated Optic Neuritis Masquerading as Traumatic Optic Neuropathy Following Cranial Trauma: A Case Report
Authors: Yuxian Shi, Xin Qi, Boding Tong, Jing Deng, Yating Liu, Yunping Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMyelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody‐associated disease (MOGAD) is an autoimmune disorder characterized by demyelination of the central nervous system. It can impact the optic nerves, spinal cord, brain parenchyma, and brainstem, resulting in various clinical presentations such as acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM), optic neuritis (ON), transverse myelitis, and encephalitis. The optic nerve is the most commonly affected region in MOGAD; nevertheless, some individuals may exhibit no apparent clinical indications, presenting only with visual impairment, which complicates clinical identification. Although prior research has predominantly concentrated on MOGAD as a whole, investigations into isolated MOG‐ON are scarce, especially within pediatric demographics. Additionally, the accurate diagnosis of MOG‐ON is further complicated by the difficulty of identifying the definitive cause when other potential etiologies coexist. We present the case of an 8‐year‐old previously healthy Asian girl who was admitted due to head trauma and subsequently diagnosed with MOG‐ON after positive serum MOG antibody testing. This case of isolated MOG‐ON with a history of head trauma is presented to underscore the necessity of serological confirmation and to offer clinicians new perspectives on the diagnostic difficulties associated with this illness.Keywords: MOGAD, myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody‐associated disease, optic disc edema, optic neuritis, traumatic optical neuropathy

Introduction

Myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) is a highly conserved glycoprotein, primarily expressed on oligodendrocyte membranes and myelin sheaths in the mammalian central nervous system. MOG antibody‐associated optic neuritis (MOG‐ON) has lately been recognized as a distinct diagnostic category in neuro‐ophthalmology, with its pathophysiology based on immunoglobulin G (IgG) autoantibodies against myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein. It is distinguished from other types of optic neuritis (ON) by its high recurrence rate, frequent binocular onset, and high incidence of optic disc edema. The current diagnostic criteria for MOG‐ON are still subject to debate, and the diagnosis of MOG‐ON necessitates the thorough exclusion of alternative etiologies for optic neuropathy. When confronted with atypical presentations or confounding comorbidity, diagnostic challenges persist. Unfortunately, the present paucity of documented MOG‐ON cases associated with a history of trauma has not garnered adequate professional attention, frequently resulting in misdiagnosis, underrecognition, and postponed treatment. In this report, we present a diagnostically instructive case of non‐classical MOG‐ON with a concomitant traumatic history, with the objective of offering clinical insights for comparable challenging scenarios.

Case History

An 8‐year‐old Asian girl arrived at the hospital with a 15‐day history of impaired vision in the right eye subsequent to blunt trauma to the left temporal region caused by a soccer ball. During this time, she refrained from seeking medical attention or receiving treatment. Forty‐eight hours before admission, she had a blow to the right frontal region with a basketball, resulting in acute visual loss in the right eye. Visual acuity of light perception was observed in the right eye and 20/20 in the left eye during the ophthalmology examination. A brisk right relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD) is present. Both eyes exhibited normal intraocular pressure. The anterior segment was unremarkable. Diffuse edema of the right optic disc was identified via fundoscopy (Figure 1A).

Fundus photography revealed initial optic disc edema at admission, which gradually resolved over time, ultimately resulting in residual optic disc pallor.

The patient was admitted with a preliminary suspicion of traumatic optic neuropathy (TON), and comprehensive diagnostic evaluations were conducted. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) demonstrated an increased mean retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness of 169 μm in the right eye, compared to 86 μm in the left eye. The ocular B‐scan ultrasound demonstrated a protrusion of the right optic disc, while fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) showed normal dye circulation time accompanied by increasing disc leakage in the right eye (Figure 2).

FFA demonstrated blurred optic disc margins with progressive dye leakage over the angiographic time course.

The flash visual evoked potential (F‐VEP) demonstrated a delayed P2 wave peak duration of 137 ms and a reduced amplitude of 9.5 μV. Laboratory investigations including complete blood count, liver/kidney function tests, syphilis serology, autoimmune assessments, all results within normal reference ranges. Concurrent neurological examination demonstrated preserved muscle strength (5/5 in all extremities), intact sensation, normal deep tendon reflexes, and absence of bladder/bowel dysfunction. Orbital magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) indicated thickening of the right optic nerve in both the intraorbital and canalicular segments (Figure 3A,B), consistent with abnormal F‐VEP results, while brain and spinal axis (cervical, thoracic, and lumbar regions) MRIs demonstrated no structural abnormalities or pathological enhancement.

Orbital MRI demonstrated thickening and enhancement of the right optic nerve during the acute phase, with subsequent improvement in optic nerve edema following treatment.

Methods

Differential Diagnosis

Traumatic optic neuropathy featured prominently in the initial differential diagnosis. Following critical reappraisal of the clinical trajectory spanning traumatic insults to neuro‐ophthalmic manifestations, the initial diagnostic hypothesis may be invalidated.

Multiple sclerosis‐associated optic neuritis (MS‐ON) is a prevalent demyelinating etiology of acute optic neuropathy. It generally manifests as unilateral, retrobulbar optic neuritis with minimal or absent optic disc edema, noted in just a handful of instances. Orbital MRI is a crucial instrument for differential diagnosis: MS‐ON lesions are typically short‐segment and primarily affect the posterior optic nerve, while MOG‐ON lesions are generally more extensive, frequently involving over half of the optic nerve length and preferentially impacting the anterior segment. AQP4‐NMOSD, another demyelinating disorder, shares some clinical features with MOG‐ON; however, similar to MS‐ON, optic disc edema is rarely seen in AQP4‐NMOSD. Moreover, involvement of the optic chiasm and optic tracts on MRI represents a key distinguishing feature from MOG‐ON [1].

Infectious optic neuropathies are also important differential diagnoses in acute optic neuropathy. Syphilis, Lyme disease, Bartonella infection (cat‐scratch disease), tuberculosis, and toxoplasmosis can all cause optic neuritis or neuroretinitis. In the present case, comprehensive infectious work‐up—including cerebrospinal fluid triple staining (Gram/acid‐fast/India ink), pathogen‐specific antibody testing, and microbial cultures—was entirely negative. Systemic inflammatory markers were not elevated, and the patient had no fever, rash, lymphadenopathy, or other signs of systemic infection, making an infectious etiology unlikely.

Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) is a maternally inherited mitochondrial illness marked by acute or subacute, painless, sequential bilateral central vision loss, with an interval of generally 6–8 weeks between eye involvement. Fundoscopy in the acute phase may demonstrate optic disc hyperemia, peripapillary telangiectasias, tortuous arteries, and pseudoedema of the retinal nerve fiber layer. Fluorescein angiography (FFA) generally reveals no dye leakage, a characteristic that aids in distinguishing it from genuine optic disc edema. MRI hyperintensities in LHON are frequently noted in the posterior optic nerve or the optic chiasm, which contradicts the findings in the current instance Lebe [2, 3].

Further serological testing of MOG antibody returned positive (1:100) while AQP4 antibodies were negative. Based on International MOGAD Panel criteria [4], the diagnosis was modified as MOG antibody related ON.

Treatment

Upon concluding the pertinent investigations [5, 6, 7], we initiated intravenous methylprednisolone at a dosage of 500 mg/day for 5 days, subsequently reducing the dose by 50% every 3 days (250 mg for 3 days, followed by 120 mg for 3 days), commencing on the second day of hospitalization, in alignment with prior studies and consensus guidelines. On the seventh day of intravenous methylprednisolone therapy, due to inadequate recovery with right eye visual acuity still indicating only light perception, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was delivered concurrently (52.5 g divided over 3 days). Eleven days following the commencement of intravenous therapy, the treatment was transitioned to oral prednisone at a dosage of 50 mg per day for 1 week, subsequently reduced to 40 mg per day 2 days post‐hospital discharge. The patient exhibited a positive response to treatment, and funduscopy demonstrated a significant decrease in right optic disc edema relative to baseline (Figure 1B).

One month post‐discharge follow‐up indicated additional recovery: the visual acuity in the right eye achieved 20/20, alongside a decreased mean RNFL thickness of 70 μm. Fundus examination revealed diminishing right optic disc edema accompanied by persistent pallor. (Figure 1C). Serological analysis demonstrated MOG‐IgG seropositivity (titer 1:32 by cell‐based test). After thorough conversation, the patient's family opted against follow‐up F‐VEP testing, leading to the absence of post‐treatment F‐VEP data. A follow‐up orbital MRI demonstrated reduced thickness of the right optic nerve, affecting both the intraorbital and canalicular segments, in comparison to previous imaging (Figure 3C,D). Significantly, binocular sight fields remained intact.

Conclusion and Result

The case highlights the considerable clinical overlap between MOG‐ON and TON. In patients with a history of cranial trauma, traumatic mechanisms may obscure the underlying autoimmune etiology, presenting diagnostic complications for doctors. This report provides a valuable resource for precise differential diagnosis in analogous clinical situations.

Discussion

MOG antibody‐associated disease (MOGAD) is an autoimmune condition characterized by demyelination of the central nervous system. It may affect the optic nerves, spinal cord, brain parenchyma, and brainstem, resulting in a diverse array of clinical manifestations. The optic nerve is the most commonly impacted site in MOGAD, including 54%–61% in prior research involving adults and children. Furthermore, it serves as the focal point of illness recurrence, with around half of cases exhibiting bilateral optic nerve damage [8].

The patient initially experienced blurred vision in the right eye after sustaining an impact to the left side of the head, indicating a potential initial occurrence of unilateral ON. Subsequently, after trauma to the right side of the head, the patient experienced a marked decline in visual acuity in the right eye, along with pronounced optic disc edema—a characteristic sign of MOG‐ON suggesting anterior optic nerve involvement. However, the close temporal correlation between symptom start and traumatic events, together with the lack of other characteristic symptoms of MOGAD, resulted in an initial diagnosis of optic nerve injury. Following the completion of OCT and MRI scans, together with the patient's clinical characteristics and disease severity, we began to question the initial diagnosis. The unfavorable outcomes of etiological assessments, inflammatory indicators, and systemic infectious symptoms rendered an infectious etiology improbable. After conducting a thorough exclusion workup, we were ultimately guided towards a demyelinating illness, resulting in MOG antibody testing for the patient.

Traumatic optic neuropathy is clinically defined as optic nerve damage resulting from external forces affecting the frontotemporal area above the orbit after craniomaxillofacial trauma, with reported incidence rates between 0.7% and 2.5% in the literature. Indirect TON predominantly arises from force transmission through the orbital apex, optic canal, and optic foramen to the optic nerve subsequent to frontal trauma, which may transpire without any exterior injury. It may also manifest as unilateral vision loss (including absence of light perception), visual field abnormalities, and a RAPD [9, 10]. Nevertheless, the optic disc generally appears unremarkable in the early phases of TON, perhaps advancing to optic disc pallor over time—a condition that contradicts the significant disc edema noted in this instance [11]. Furthermore, the RNFL thickness in the TON eye was significantly thinner compared to the contralateral normal eye, casting doubt on the initial diagnosis [12].

Optic disc edema refers to a nonspecific swelling of the optic disc, which is classified into true and pseudo optic disc edema [13]. True unilateral optic disc edema can occur in demyelinating optic neuritis, non‐arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy, retinal vein blockage, diabetic optic disc lesions, and localized compression. Considering the lack of notable abnormalities in the retinal vessels of the affected eye and the optic disc of the contralateral eye, coupled with the clinical manifestation of abrupt vision loss and the absence of a diabetes history, we are predisposed to diagnosing demyelinating optic neuritis [14].

MRI is the principal imaging technique for diagnosing ON, differentiating it from non‐inflammatory ocular neuropathies through the identification of optic nerve enhancement. The MRI of the patient disclosed lesions primarily situated in the intraorbital portion of the optic nerve, affecting more than 50% of the prechiasmatic optic nerve length, with T2 hyperintensity—a distinctive imaging hallmark of MOG‐ON. Furthermore, MRI ruled out structural problems in other areas, thereby guiding the subsequent therapeutic approach [4].

Likewise, OCT is a non‐invasive imaging modality that captures high‐resolution cross‐sectional pictures of the retina, facilitating quantitative evaluation of distinct retinal layers. The observed RNFL thickening in the right eye signified optic disc edema, prompting a shift in the etiological investigation towards an inflammatory or demyelinating condition instead of traditional traumatic optic neuropathy. A multicenter cross‐sectional investigation has demonstrated that the median acute‐phase retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness in MOG‐associated optic neuritis (MOG‐ON) is generally greater (164 μm) than that in multiple sclerosis‐associated optic neuritis (MS‐ON, median 103 μm). Nonetheless, RNFL thickening is not exclusive to MOG‐ON and may also be present in non‐arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy. Consequently, this OCT finding should be regarded as corroborative data that, when combined with the clinical progression and MRI results, collectively indicates the necessity for additional MOG antibody testing [15, 16].

Following confirmation of positive MOG‐IgG serology, the diagnosis was amended to MOG‐ON in accordance with the International MOGAD Panel Criteria [4]. Conventional viewpoints propose that pathogen infections may initiate MOG antibody production; nevertheless, new research suggests that head trauma could serve as a potential etiological trigger for MOG‐ON. Sandweiss [17] recorded six cases of head/orbital trauma later identified as MOG‐ON. However, all of them included single‐episode trauma and demonstrated only ipsilateral ON—contrasting with the bilateral trauma history in our patient. Moreover, the median latency interval between trauma and the beginning of ON symptoms was 5 days in recorded cases, which is incongruous with the immediate post‐traumatic presentation found in the present case. Comparable incidents have been documented in aquaporin‐4 antibody‐positive neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders (AQP4‐IgG+ NMOSD), wherein three patients experienced acute ON accompanied by visual impairment subsequent to inadvertent minor ocular trauma, devoid of fundoscopic anomalies or orbital floor fractures, ultimately substantiating AQP4‐IgG+ NMOSD. Taken together, these findings suggest that trauma may serve as a trigger for active inflammatory optic neuropathy [18]; however, additional cases and evidence are needed for confirmation. In the present case, we do not attribute the patient's optic neuritis to the history of head trauma. Rather, we consider the coincidence of the trauma with the disease course of MOG‐ON, which led to the initial misdiagnosis of traumatic optic neuropathy. This case reminds clinicians to adopt a broader differential diagnostic approach and provides a valuable reference for clinical practice.

In contrast to AQP4‐IgG+ NMOSD, MOG‐ON typically presents a more advantageous prognosis [19]. Despite acute‐phase MOG‐ON patients frequently exhibiting severe vision impairment, most attain considerable visual restoration subsequent to immunotherapy [20].
