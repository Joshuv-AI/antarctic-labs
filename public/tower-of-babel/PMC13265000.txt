Locally Advanced Invasive Mole in the Presence of Low Serum β-hCG: A Case Report

Introduction

Gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (GTN) are an uncommon group of neoplasms that arise from pregnancy-related trophoblastic tissue.1 These tumors encompass several histopathological entities, including invasive mole, choriocarcinoma, placental site trophoblastic tumor (PSTT), and epithelioid trophoblastic tumor (ETT). They originate from chorionic villi and extravillous trophoblasts and exhibit varying degrees of cellular proliferation, local invasion, and metastatic potential.2

Among the spectrum of gestational trophoblastic tumors, invasive mole represents one of the most frequently encountered forms. It is defined by the presence of edematous trophoblastic tissue and hydropic chorionic villi that infiltrate the myometrium, with or without vascular invasion or extrauterine extension.3,4 Invasive mole develops in approximately 10–15% of cases following complete hydatidiform mole.5 Histopathologically, it is characterized by deep myometrial penetration of intact chorionic villi accompanied by aggressive trophoblastic proliferation with the potential for destructive invasion.2 In some cases, the lesion may extend to the peritoneal surface or involve the vaginal vault. Although invasive mole demonstrates locally aggressive behavior, distant metastasis is relatively uncommon.6

This case report describes an invasive mole occurring after a partial hydatidiform mole, complicated by severe vaginal bleeding leading to profound anemia. The report aims to discuss the clinical management, associated complications, and prognosis of gestational trophoblastic tumors, with particular emphasis on invasive mole.

Case Illustration

A 34-year-old woman, para 2 abortus 1, presented with massive vaginal bleeding accompanied by signs of hypovolemic shock. The bleeding was profuse, soaking approximately four adult diapers per day, and was accompanied by intermittent abdominal pain. She had a history of uterine evacuation with histopathological findings consistent a partial hydatidiform mole on July 30, 2024. Pre-evacuation serum β-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) level was markedly elevated at 1183002 mIU/mL.

However, due to inactive health insurance and financial constraints, follow-up was delayed until November 2024. These socio-economic barriers contributed to delayed serial β-hCG monitoring, postponed clinical evaluation, and interruption of standard post-molar surveillance, which likely delayed the diagnosis and timely management of gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (GTN). Post-evacuation follow-up showed persistently detectable but gradually declining β-hCG levels, with measurements of 9299 mIU/mL on November 2nd 2024, 1996 mIU/mL on February 13rd 2025, and 714 mIU/mL on March 20th 2025. The recent laboratory analysis was repeated to exclude the possibility of a hook effect considering the consecutive decline in β-hCG levels. During the post-evacuation period, the patient reported continuous vaginal spotting from the time of curettage until March 24, 2025. Despite a gradual decline, β-hCG levels failed to normalize and remained persistently elevated for more than six months after evacuation.

Based on persistent β-hCG elevation beyond six months following molar evacuation and ongoing abnormal uterine bleeding, the patient was diagnosed with gestational trophoblastic neoplasia assessed by a gynecologic oncology subspecialist. The patient received the first cycle of single-agent methotrexate (MTX) (300 mg/m2) on April 18–19, 2025, with a second cycle planned. At presentation, she denied symptoms suggestive of hyperthyroidism or systemic illness, including palpitations, tremors, dyspnea, anorexia, or weight loss, and reported no history of chronic disease.

On physical examination, the patient was tachycardic with a heart rate of 127 beats per minute, blood pressure of 100/78 mmHg, respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute, and body temperature of 36.7°C. Her body mass index was 20.5 kg/m2 (weight 48 kg, height 153 cm). She appeared pale, with conjunctival pallor noted. Abdominal examination revealed a soft, non-tender abdomen. Speculum examination showed vaginal bleeding, although no active bleeding was observed at that time, and the cervix appeared smooth. Bimanual examination demonstrated a uterus of normal consistency, no adnexal masses, and a non-bulging pouch of Douglas.

Doppler ultrasonography demonstrated a uterus measuring 9.56×5.52 × 7.55 cm with markedly increased vascularity within the myometrium and prominent color flow signals occupying a substantial portion of the uterine cavity (Figure 1), findings consistent with invasive trophoblastic disease. However, a discrete measurable tumor mass could not be clearly delineated on imaging. Unfortunately, Doppler parameters including resistance index (RI) and pulsatility index (PI) were not available due to limitations in the recorded imaging data. Nevertheless, the marked hypervascularity and prominent low-resistance–appearing flow pattern strongly supported the suspicion of invasive trophoblastic disease. Laboratory investigations on May 2, 2025, showed severe anemia with hemoglobin of 6.7 g/dL and hematocrit of 21.1%, while leukocyte and platelet counts were within normal ranges (4850/µL and 354000/µL, respectively). Additional laboratory work-up including liver function tests, renal function tests, and coagulation profile revealed no significant reportable abnormalities.

Doppler ultrasonography of the uterus demonstrating diffuse marked myometrial hypervascularity with prominent color flow signals occupying a substantial portion of the uterine cavity, consistent with invasive trophoblastic disease. The uterus measured 9.56×5.52 × 7.55 cm, although no discrete measurable tumor mass could be clearly delineated on imaging.

Prior to initiation of chemotherapy, the patient’s serum β-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) level was 694.1 mIU/mL on March 24, 2025, and further decreased to 580.7 mIU/mL on April 15, 2025. Following completion of the first cycle of chemotherapy, the β-hCG level declined markedly to 74.7 mIU/mL on April 30, 2025. Chest radiography performed on April 14th 2025, revealed no evidence of pulmonary metastasis or cardiomegaly.

The patient with FIGO Stage I and FIGO prognostic score of 4 was diagnosed with vaginal bleeding secondary to stage I low-risk gestational trophoblastic tumor and admitted for gynecologic oncology care. Initial management consisted of intravenous fluid resuscitation, administration of tranexamic acid and vitamin K, and blood transfusion to maintain hemoglobin levels above 10 g/dL. Despite these measures, vaginal bleeding persisted. Uterine artery embolization was considered; however, due to ongoing life-threatening hemorrhage requiring immediate definitive bleeding control, hysterectomy was selected as the most appropriate intervention. In addition to achieving hemostasis, hysterectomy also enabled definitive histopathological evaluation of the underlying lesion. Consequently, on May 3, 2025, after obtaining informed consent, the patient underwent total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingectomy due to life-threatening hemorrhagic shock, stage I low-risk GTN, and severe anemia.

Intraoperative findings revealed an enlarged uterus equivalent to 8–10 weeks of gestation, with a smooth surface and soft consistency, without adhesions to surrounding structures. Both adnexa appeared normal (Figure 2). Estimated intraoperative blood loss was approximately 800 mL, and urine output was about 200 mL.

Intraoperative findings showing an enlarged uterus, equivalent to 8–10 weeks’ gestation, with a smooth surface and soft consistency, without adhesions to surrounding structures. Both adnexa appeared normal.

Postoperatively, the patient was managed in the intensive care unit for close observation. Treatment included intravenous fluids, broad-spectrum antibiotics, analgesics, tranexamic acid, and proton pump inhibitors. She received a total of six units of packed red blood cells, two units of platelet concentrate, and two units of fresh frozen plasma during the first two postoperative days. Gradual mobilization was initiated, and hemoglobin levels were maintained at or above 8 g/dL. Serial β-hCG monitoring was planned as part of postoperative follow-up.

Histopathological examination of the hysterectomy specimen revealed features consistent with an invasive mole, characterized by the presence of hydropic chorionic villi with marked trophoblastic proliferation infiltrating the parametrium (Figure 3). These findings confirmed the diagnosis of invasive mole and indicated locally advanced trophoblastic invasion beyond the uterine corpus.

Following confirmation of the histopathological diagnosis, the patient was scheduled for continued postoperative surveillance and further oncologic management. Serial serum β-hCG measurements were planned to assess treatment response and detect residual or recurrent disease. Given the parametrial invasion, close follow-up was emphasized to monitor for potential persistence or progression of gestational trophoblastic tumor, although no evidence of distant metastasis was identified during evaluation. Discussion

Postmolar gestational trophoblastic tumors are most commonly identified through serial serum β-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) surveillance, even in patients without overt clinical manifestations.7 Nevertheless, abnormal uterine bleeding remains a frequent clinical presentation, typically related to uterine subinvolution. The bleeding pattern may be intermittent or persistent and, in certain cases, may present abruptly as massive hemorrhage.

The diagnosis of postmolar gestational trophoblastic disease is established when one or more of the following criteria are met: (1) plateauing of β-hCG levels (±10%) across four consecutive measurements over at least three weeks (days 1, 7, 14, and 21); (2) a rise in β-hCG levels exceeding 10% across three consecutive weekly measurements over a minimum of two weeks (days 1, 7, and 14); (3) persistence of detectable β-hCG levels for six months or longer following molar evacuation; or (4) histopathological confirmation of choriocarcinoma.1,3,8 In addition, post-evacuation β-hCG trends may be evaluated using the Mochizuki regression curve.9

Once gestational trophoblastic tumor is suspected or confirmed, baseline evaluation should include serum β-hCG levels and blood group.10 Further assessment to determine the extent of local disease and metastatic spread includes liver and renal function tests, transvaginal ultrasonography, chest radiography or computed tomography (CT), and brain or abdominopelvic CT or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). In selected cases, positron emission tomography (PET) scans or cerebrospinal fluid β-hCG measurement may be utilized to detect occult metastases.9

In the present case, serum β-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) levels remained persistently detectable for more than six months following molar evacuation, fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for postmolar gestational trophoblastic tumor. Therefore, the diagnosis of gestational trophoblastic tumor could have been established earlier; however, chemotherapy was initiated several months later.

Gestational trophoblastic tumors comprise several histopathological entities, including invasive mole, choriocarcinoma, placental site trophoblastic tumor (PSTT), and epithelioid trophoblastic tumor (ETT). Among these, invasive mole and choriocarcinoma are the most prevalent and are staged according to the FIGO system with risk stratification using the WHO scoring criteria.7 On ultrasonography, invasive mole, choriocarcinoma, and PSTT typically present as nonspecific focal lesions centered within the myometrium, making differentiation challenging.11 These lesions may appear echogenic, hypoechoic, heterogeneous, or multicystic, often containing anechoic spaces representing hemorrhage, necrosis, cystic degeneration, or vascular channels.12 In advanced cases, the uterus may be heterogeneously enlarged or appear as a pelvic mass involving adjacent pelvic structures.13

An Invasive Mole is a malignant form of gestational trophoblastic neoplasia characterized by myometrial invasion by hydatidiform mole tissue, either through direct infiltration or via venous channels.14,15 All cases of invasive mole arise following a molar pregnancy.16 The incidence of invasive mole ranges from 0.5–1% after partial mole and up to 15–29% after complete mole.17 Most cases occur within six months of molar evacuation and are commonly associated with persistent vaginal bleeding and elevated serum β-hCG levels.16 Approximately 15% of invasive moles develop metastases, most frequently to the lungs or vagina.15

Histologically, invasive mole is characterized by deep myometrial invasion by intact chorionic villi accompanied by marked trophoblastic proliferation. The lesion may extend through the uterine wall to involve the peritoneum or vaginal vault.6 Despite its aggressive local behavior, widespread metastasis is uncommon. Invasive mole is often diagnosed clinically based on persistently abnormal β-hCG levels following molar evacuation, and treatment is frequently initiated without histopathological confirmation.15

In this patient, persistent irregular vaginal bleeding accompanied by sustained elevation of β-hCG led to a clinical diagnosis of invasive mole, and chemotherapy was initiated accordingly. MTX is the standard first-line chemotherapeutic agent for patients with low-risk GTN.14 In this case, chemotherapy with MTX was administered based on the established risk stratification criteria.

Importantly, although serum β-hCG levels had declined to relatively low values, this did not exclude the presence of invasive mole. The recent decline in β-hCG levels was likely influenced by the chemotherapy previously received by the patient. In addition, relatively low β-hCG levels despite aggressive local invasion may reflect the biological characteristics of invasive mole. Histologically, invasive moles retain hydropic chorionic villi with variable degrees of trophoblastic proliferation, whereas choriocarcinoma is characterized by highly proliferative trophoblastic tissue without villous structures and is typically associated with markedly elevated β-hCG levels.7 Furthermore, β-hCG production depends primarily on the amount and activity of syncytiotrophoblastic tissue rather than solely on the extent of myometrial invasion. Therefore, significant local invasion and severe uterine bleeding may still occur despite relatively low or declining serum β-hCG concentrations.7 Persistent trophoblastic invasion can still occur despite low or decreasing β-hCG levels, particularly when accompanied by ongoing uterine bleeding, as observed in this case. The possibility of a laboratory “hook effect” was also considered because extremely high β-hCG concentrations may produce falsely low values in immunometric assays due to antibody saturation.18 However, repeat laboratory analysis and serial evaluation demonstrated a consistent downward trend in β-hCG levels, making a hook effect unlikely in this case.

Consequently, hysterectomy was warranted to control life-threatening hemorrhage and manage suspected invasive disease.19 Other management options such as uterine artery embolization may be considered for bleeding control.14 However, given the need for timely intervention and the unavailability of this modality at our center, a hysterectomy was performed after informed consent had been obtained. This case highlights the importance of strict postmolar surveillance and timely evaluation of persistent abnormal uterine bleeding, even when serum β-hCG levels are relatively low or declining. Early recognition and prompt management of persistent gestational trophoblastic disease are essential to prevent severe complications, including life-threatening hemorrhage.

Patient Perspective

The patient reported significant anxiety and fear related to the persistent vaginal bleeding and repeated hospitalizations. She also expressed difficulties in accessing regular follow-up due to financial limitations. Following treatment, the patient felt relieved after the bleeding resolved and understood the importance of continued β-hCG surveillance.

Conclusion

Invasive mole should be considered in patients with persistent β-hCG levels for more than six months following molar evacuation, even when the absolute β-hCG values are low and demonstrate a declining trend. Persistent or recurrent abnormal uterine bleeding remains an important clinical warning sign of ongoing trophoblastic invasion. This case highlights that low serum β-hCG levels do not exclude the presence of invasive mole, particularly when accompanied by significant hemorrhage. Hysterectomy plays a crucial role in the management of life-threatening bleeding and may be necessary despite appropriate chemotherapy, especially in cases with locally aggressive disease. Early recognition and timely intervention are essential to prevent severe complications and optimize patient outcomes.
