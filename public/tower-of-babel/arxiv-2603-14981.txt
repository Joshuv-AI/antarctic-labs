Abstract
The standard literature on special functions contains a lot of hypergeometric identities involving products and quotients of gamma functions, but still the occurrence of such identities is a sporadic phenomenon. This is because the existence of them is constrained by severe arithmetic conditions. We demonstrate this kind of constraints by focusing on a certain data region where the essential nature of the issue comes out clearly.



1
Introduction

Let
{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta;\gamma;z)
be the Gauss hypergeometric function
and
\varGamma(w)
the Euler gamma function.
Given a sextuple of data
\lambda=(p,q,r;a,b;x)\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
with
0<x<1
,
we set
f(w;\lambda):={}_{2}F_{1}(pw+a,qw+b;rw;x)
.
As a continuation to our previous studies in
[
6
,
7
]
,
we are interested in the following problem.

Problem I

Find or characterize those data
\lambda
which give rise to
a
gamma product formula
(GPF for short)

f(w;\lambda)=C\cdot d^{w}\cdot\dfrac{\varGamma(w+u_{1})\cdots\varGamma(w+u_{m})}{\varGamma(w+v_{1})\cdots\varGamma(w+v_{n})},\qquad w\in\mathbb{C},

(1)

for some constants
C
,
d\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
and
u_{1},\dots,u_{m}
,
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
,
where
w
is a free complex parameter.

The standard literature on special functions, e.g.
[
1
,
2
,
4
,
5
,
8
]
,
contains a lot of identities of the form (
1
), but still they occur only rarely
among all data
\lambda=(p,q,r;a,b;x)
which vary continuously.
The aim of this article is to address the question of why GPFs are so
sporadic, by observing that solutions
\lambda
to Problem I must be subject to certain

arithmetic constraints
, typically (but not always) like the condition
that
p,q,r
must be integers,
a,b
must be rational numbers,
and
x
must be an algebraic number of a special form.

Figure 1.1:
Partition of the
(p,q)
-plane with a fixed
r>0
.

How GPFs come about depends strongly on the direction
of the vector
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}=(p,q,r)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which we call the
principal part
of
\lambda
.
This is a kind of Stokes phenomenon with respect to the large parameter
w
.
In
[
6
,
7
]
, with a fixed
r>0
, the
(p,q)
-plane is partitioned into nine
domains and their boundaries as indicated in Figure
1.1
.
Our main focus in
[
6
,
7
]
was on the central square
\mathcal{D}
, the shaded area
in Figure
1.1
.
In this article we turn our attention to its
boundary
.
Although the boundary is thinner and more special than the open square,
but exactly because of that,
arithmetic nature
of Problem I comes out
more vividly on the boundary.

Given
k\in\mathbb{N}:=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, the
k
-
multiple
of
\lambda=(p,q,r;a,b;x)

is defined by
k\lambda:=(kp,kq,kr;a,b;x)
.
It is obvious from the definition of
f(w;\lambda)
that
f(w;k\lambda)=f(kw;\lambda)
.
This identity and Gauss’s multiplication formula

\varGamma(kw)=(2\pi)^{(1-k)/2}\cdot k^{kw-1/2}\cdot\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\varGamma\big(w+\textstyle\frac{j}{k}\big),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}

(2)

imply that any multiple of a solution to Problem I is again a solution; see
[
7
, §1]
for details.
A solution is said to be
primitive
if it is not a multiple of any other solution.
We say that
\lambda
is
elementary
if
f(w;\lambda)

has at most finitely many poles in
\mathbb{C}_{w}
.
We can then speak of elementary or non-elementary solutions to Problem I.

The hypergeometric function
{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta;\gamma;z)
has two basic symmetries

{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta;\gamma;z)={}_{2}F_{1}(\beta,\alpha;\gamma;z),\qquad{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta;\gamma;z)=(1-z)^{\gamma-\alpha-\beta}{}_{2}F_{1}(\gamma-\alpha,\gamma-\beta;\gamma;z).

(3)

The former identity is obvious from the definition of
{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta;\gamma;z)
,
while the latter is known as Euler’s transformation.
They induce transformations of data
\lambda
which takes one solution to another,

\lambda=(p,q,r;a,b;x)\mapsto(q,p,r;b,a;x),\qquad\lambda=(p,q,r;a,b;x)\mapsto(r-p,r-q,r;-a,-b;x).

(4)

The boundary of the central square
\mathcal{D}
consists of four sides,
but a combination of symmetries (
4
) brings any side
to the
south side

\mathcal{I}
in Figure
1.1
.
The full parameter region corresponding to the south side is given by

\mathcal{I}:=\{\lambda=(p,q,r;a,b;x)\in\mathbb{R}^{6}\mid 0<p<r,\,\,q=0,\,\,0<x<1\},

which is denoted by the same symbol
\mathcal{I}
.
Our main concern in this article is the study of Problem I in this region.
As a part of our earlier result
[
6
, Theorem 2.4]
the following
constraint is already known to us.

Theorem 1.1

Let
\lambda=(p,0,r;a,b;x)\in\mathcal{I}
.

(\mathrm{1})

\lambda
is an elementary solution to Problem I if and only if
b

is zero or a negative integer, in which case
f(w;\lambda)
is a rational function of
w
.

(\mathrm{2})

If
\lambda
is a non-elementary solution to Problem I, then

p,\,r\in\mathbb{Z};\qquad p\equiv r\bmod 2;\qquad b=1/2.

(5)

In particular, any non-elementary solution is a multiple of a primitive solution.

The aim of this article is to strengthen Theorem
1.1
to a large extent
by establishing the main results stated in §
2
.
This goal is achieved by making a careful examination of contiguous relations
for hyergeometric functions and by exploiting the concepts of
duality
and
reciprocity

introduced in
[
7
, Definitions 1.2 and 1.3]
, which are the transformations of data

\lambda\mapsto\lambda^{\prime}=(p^{\prime},q^{\prime},r^{\prime};a^{\prime},b^{\prime};x^{\prime})
and

\lambda\mapsto\check{\lambda}=(\check{p},\check{q},\check{r};\check{a},\check{b};\check{x})

defined by

\displaystyle p^{\prime}:=p,\quad q^{\prime}:=q,\quad r^{\prime}:=r;\quad a^{\prime}:=1-\frac{2p}{r}-a,\quad b^{\prime}:=1-\frac{2q}{r}-b;\quad x^{\prime}:=x,

(6a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\check{p}&:=-p,\qquad\check{q}:=-q,\qquad\check{r}:=r-p-q;\qquad\check{x}:=1-x;\\[2.84526pt]
&\check{a}:=\dfrac{(r-q)(1-a)-pb}{r-p-q},\qquad\check{b}:=\dfrac{(r-p)(1-b)-qa}{r-p-q}.\end{split}

(6b)

Notice that duality defines an involution on
\mathcal{I}
, while reciprocity provides
a bijection of
\mathcal{I}
onto

\mathcal{J}:=\{\lambda=(p,q,r;a,b;x)\in\mathbb{R}^{6}\mid p<0,\,\,q=0,\,\,0<r,\,\,0<x<1\},

which corresponds to the half-line
\mathcal{J}
in Figure
1.1
.
Notice also that for
\lambda\in\mathcal{I}
if
b=1/2
then
b^{\prime}=\check{b}=1/2
.

2
Main Results

We state the main results of this article and give a couple of examples to illustrate them.

Theorem 2.1

Let
\lambda=(p,0,r;a,1/2;x)\in\mathcal{I}
be any non-elementary solution to Problem I.

(\mathrm{1})

The dual
\lambda^{\prime}=(p,0,r;a^{\prime},1/2;x)\in\mathcal{I}
to the solution
\lambda
is
also a non-elementary solution to Problem I.

(\mathrm{2})

The ratio
s:=r/p
is an integer with
s\geq 2
and
p(s-1)\equiv 0\bmod 2
.
There exist
j
,
j^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that

a=j/s,\qquad a^{\prime}=j^{\prime}/s,\qquad j+j^{\prime}=s-2.

(7)

In particular,
a
and
a^{\prime}
are rational numbers.

(\mathrm{3})

The argument
x
is the unique root in the interval
(0,\,1)
of
the algebraic equation

\varphi_{s}(z):=(s-1)^{s-1}z^{s}-s^{s}(1-z)^{s-1}=0.

(8)

In particular,
x
is an algebraic number.

Certainly, equation (
8
) has a unique root
x\in(0,\,1)
,
since
\varphi_{s}(0)=-s^{s}<0<\varphi_{s}(1)=(s-1)^{s-1}
and

\varphi_{s}^{\prime}(z)=s(s-1)\{(s-1)^{s-2}z^{s-1}+s^{s-1}(1-z)^{s-2}\}>0
for
z\in(0,\,1)
.
Theorem
2.1
implies that, once the ratio
s=r/p
is given,
there are at most
s-1
possibilities for the triple
(a,a^{\prime},x)
.
The theorem does not claim that any expression for
(a,a^{\prime})
in
(
7
) leads to a solution; some or all of those candidates
may fail to give a solution.

Theorem 2.2

Let
\lambda=(p,0,r;a,1/2;x)\in\mathcal{I}
be any non-elementary solution to Problem I.

(\mathrm{1})

There exists a factorization into two components of the right-hand side below

\prod_{i=1}^{r}(w+v_{i})\prod_{i=1}^{r}(w+v_{i}^{*})=\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\Big(w+\textstyle\frac{i+a}{p}\Big)\displaystyle\prod_{j=0}^{r-p-1}\Big(w+\textstyle\frac{j-a}{r-p}\Big)\displaystyle\prod_{j=0}^{r-1}\Big(w+\textstyle\frac{j-1/2}{r}\Big)

(9)

such that the solution
\lambda
and its dual
\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}
admit GPFs of the forms

\displaystyle f(w;\lambda)

\displaystyle=C\cdot\dfrac{\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}\varGamma(w+\frac{i}{r})}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}\varGamma(w+v_{i})},

(10a)

\displaystyle f(w;\lambda^{\prime})

\displaystyle=C^{\prime}\cdot\dfrac{\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}\varGamma(w+\frac{i}{r})}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}\varGamma(w+v_{i}^{\prime})},\qquad v_{i}^{\prime}:=1-\frac{2}{r}-v_{i}^{*},

(10b)

where
C
and
C^{\prime}
are nonzero constants.
Moreover
v_{1},\dots,v_{r}
and
v_{1}^{\prime},\dots,v_{r}^{\prime}
sum to the same value

v_{1}+\cdots+v_{r}=v_{1}^{\prime}+\cdots+v_{r}^{\prime}=(r-1)/2.

(11)

(\mathrm{2})

There exist a division relation

\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\big(w+\textstyle\frac{i+a}{p}\big)\,\Big|\,\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{r}(w+v_{i}),

(12)

which allows us to arrange
v_{1},\dots,v_{r}
so that

\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\big(w+\textstyle\frac{i+a}{p}\big)=\prod_{i=r-p+1}^{r}(w+v_{i})
.
With this convention the reciprocal
\check{\lambda}\in\mathcal{J}
to the
solution
\lambda
is also a solution to Problem I having GPF

f(w;\check{\lambda})=\check{C}\cdot\dfrac{\prod_{i=0}^{r-p-1}\varGamma\big(w+\frac{i}{r-p}\big)}{\prod_{i=1}^{r-p}\varGamma(w+\check{v}_{i})},\qquad\check{v}_{i}:=v_{i}-\frac{1/2-a}{r-p},

(13)

where
\check{C}
is a nonzero constant.

The dilation constant
d
in the general template (
1
) is just
1

in GPFs (
10
) and (
13
).
The fractions in these formulas may be reducible, that is,
they may have common factors in their numerators and denominators.

Theorem 2.3

Given any non-elementary solution
\lambda=(p,0,r;a,1/2;x)\in\mathcal{I}

to Problem I, let
\deg x
denote the degree of
x
as an algebraic number.
If the ratio
s:=r/p
is
2
then
\deg x=2
, whereas if
s\geq 3
then

\deg x>\delta(s):=\frac{(\log p_{s})(s-1)}{1+\log(s-1)},

where
p_{s}
is the least prime factor of the integer
s-1
.
In particular, once an upper bound for
\deg x
is given, there
remain only a finite number of possibilities for the ratio
s=r/p
.

3
Contiguous Relations

If
\lambda
is a solution to Problem I with GPF (
1
), then
the recursion formula
\varGamma(w+1)=w\varGamma(w)
yields

\dfrac{f(w+1;\lambda)}{f(w;\lambda)}=d\cdot\dfrac{(w+u_{1})\cdots(w+u_{m})}{(w+v_{1})\cdots(w+v_{n})},

(18)

which is a rational function of
w
.
This observation leads us to the following problem.

Problem I​I

Find or characterize those deta
\lambda
for which

f(w+1;\lambda)/f(w;\lambda)
is a rational function of
w
.

We say that a piece of data
\lambda=(p,q,r;a,b;x)
is
integral

if its principal part
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}=(p,q,r)
is an integer vector.
There is a general method to obtain integral solutions to Problem I​I by means of
contiguous relations; see
[
3
,
6
]
.
For simplicity of notation we write
{}_{2}F_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$};z):={}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta;\gamma;z)

for
\mbox{\boldmath$a$}=(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})=(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
and
\mbox{\boldmath$1$}:=(1,1,1)
.
Given an integer vector
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}=(p,q,r)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
, a repeated application
of fifteen contiguous relations of Gauss gives rise to rational functions
r(\mbox{\boldmath$a$};\mbox{\boldmath$p$};z)

and
q(\mbox{\boldmath$a$};\mbox{\boldmath$p$};z)
of
(\mbox{\boldmath$a$};z)
depending on
p
such that

{}_{2}F_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}+\mbox{\boldmath$p$};z)=r(\mbox{\boldmath$a$};\mbox{\boldmath$p$};z)\,{}_{2}F_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$};z)+q(\mbox{\boldmath$a$};\mbox{\boldmath$p$};z)\,{}_{2}F_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}+\mbox{\boldmath$1$};z).

(19)

Given a piece of integral data
\lambda=(p,q,r;a,b;x)
, we put

\widetilde{f}(w;\lambda):={}_{2}F_{1}(pw+a+1,qw+b+1;rw+1;x)
.
Substituting
\mbox{\boldmath$a$}=(pw+a,qw+b,rw)
and
z=x
into the three-term
relation (
19
), we have

f(w+1;\lambda)=R(w;\lambda)\,f(w;\lambda)+Q(w;\lambda)\,\widetilde{f}(w;\lambda),

(20)

where
R(w;\lambda)
and
Q(w;\lambda)
are rational functions of
w
depending on
\lambda
.
If
Q(w;\lambda)

happens to vanish identically
, then the three-term relation
(
20
) reduces to the two-term one
f(w+1;\lambda)/f(w;\lambda)=R(w;\lambda)
,
leading to an integral solution
\lambda
to Problem I​I.
A solution arising in this manner is said to
come from contiguous relations
.

Now we bring our attention to the region
\mathcal{I}
.
The following result is known as a part of
[
6
, Theorem 5.4]
.

Theorem 3.1

If
\lambda\in\mathcal{I}
is a solution to Problem I​I and the ratio

f(w+1;\lambda)/f(w;\lambda)
is expressed in the form (
18
),
then
\lambda
is a solution to Problem I with GPF (
1
).
Moreover in formula (
18
) we must have
m=n
and

u_{1}+\cdots+u_{m}=v_{1}+\cdots+v_{m}.

(21)

This is a consequence of asymptotic analysis of the function
f(w;\lambda)

as
w
tends to infinity.
Thanks to Theorem
3.1
, Problems I and I​I are equivalent in the region
\mathcal{I}
,
so we can speak of a solution therein without specifying to which problem it is a solution.
Recall from Theorem
1.1
that any non-elementary solution to either problem
in
\mathcal{I}
is integral.
Together with these facts, the following result is also known in
[
6
, Theorem 2.4]
.

Theorem 3.2

Every non-elementary solution in
\mathcal{I}
comes from contiguous relations.
Therefore a piece of data
\lambda=(p,0,r;a,1/2;x)\in\mathcal{I}
is a solution if and only if

\lambda
is integral and
Q(w;\lambda)
vanishes identically.

It is natural to expect that constraints for the existence of non-elementary
solutions originate from the properties of contiguous relations.
Thus we begin our discussions with a closer look at contiguous relations.

3.1
Contiguous Matrices

It is better to use the matrix version of contiguous relations.
Let
\mbox{\boldmath$F$}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}):={}^{t}({}_{2}F_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$};z),\,{}_{2}F_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}+\mbox{\boldmath$1$};z))
.
Then the three-term relation (
19
) and the one with
p
replaced by

\mbox{\boldmath$p$}+\mbox{\boldmath$1$}
form the matrix equation

\mbox{\boldmath$F$}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}+\mbox{\boldmath$p$})=A(\mbox{\boldmath$a$};\mbox{\boldmath$p$})\,\mbox{\boldmath$F$}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}),\qquad A(\mbox{\boldmath$a$};\mbox{\boldmath$p$}):=\begin{pmatrix}r(\mbox{\boldmath$a$};\mbox{\boldmath$p$};z)&q(\mbox{\boldmath$a$};\mbox{\boldmath$p$};z)\\[2.84526pt]
r(\mbox{\boldmath$a$};\mbox{\boldmath$p$}+\mbox{\boldmath$1$};z)&q(\mbox{\boldmath$a$};\mbox{\boldmath$p$}+\mbox{\boldmath$1$};z)\end{pmatrix}.

(22)

For
i=1,2,3
, we put
A_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}):=A(\mbox{\boldmath$a$};\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{i})
, where
\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}=(1,0;0)
,

\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}=(0,1,0)
and
\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{3}=(0,0;1)
.
These matrices satisfy the compatibility condition

A_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}+\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{j})\,A_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$})=A_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}+\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{i})\,A_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$})
.
Given an integer vector
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{3}
, if we take a sequence of indices

i=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in\{1,2,3\}^{k}
such that
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}=\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{i_{1}}+\cdots+\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{i_{k}}
,
then we have

A(\mbox{\boldmath$a$};\mbox{\boldmath$p$})=A_{i_{k}}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}+\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{i_{1}}+\dots+\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{i_{k-1}})\cdots A_{i_{3}}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}+\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{i_{1}}+\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{i_{2}})\,A_{i_{2}}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}+\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{i_{1}})\,A_{i_{1}}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}).

(23)

Due to compatibility condition, the matrix product on the right is independent of
the choice of
i=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
.

In this article we take
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}=(p,0,r)

5.3
Arithmetic

Let
\alpha
be any nontrivial root of
\psi_{s}(z)
and
d:=\deg\alpha

its degree as an algebraic number.
Put

M=M(\alpha):=(s+\beta_{1})\cdots(s+\beta_{d}),\qquad N=N(\alpha):=\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{d},

where
B=\{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d}\}
is the set of all conjugates to
\alpha

including itself.
Since
\alpha
is an algebraic integer,
M
and
N
are rational integers.
For
s=2
we have
d=2
and
M=N=-1
.

Lemma 5.14

For each integer
s\geq 3
there exists an integer
n=n(\alpha)
such that

|n|\geq 2,\qquad n\,|\,(s-1),\qquad M=n^{s-1},\qquad nN=(s-1)^{d}.

Proof
.
For
s=3
we have
d=1
,
M=4
,
N=1
and
n=2
, so the
lemma holds true.
Hereafter we assume
s\geq 4
.

Since
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d}
are roots of
\psi_{s}(z)
, we have

\beta_{j}^{s-1}(s+\beta_{j})=(s-1)^{s-1}
for
j=1,\dots,d
.
Product of them gives
N^{s-1}M=(s-1)^{d(s-1)}
, namely,

M=\{(s-1)^{d}/N\}^{s-1}
.
Since
M
is an integer,
n:=(s-1)^{d}/N
must also be an integer,

M=n^{s-1}
and
nN=(s-1)^{d}
.
Let
P(z)
be the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
.
Since
\alpha
is an algebraic integer,

is
a monic polynomial dividing
\psi_{s}(z)
in
\mathbb{Z}[z]
, so the integer
P(-s)

divides
\psi_{s}(-s)=-(s-1)^{s-1}
in
\mathbb{Z}
.
As
P(z)=(z-\beta_{1})\cdots(z-\beta_{d})
, we have

P(-s)=(-1)^{d}\cdot M
, so
M=n^{s-1}
divides
(s-1)^{s-1}

and hence
n
divides
s-1
in
\mathbb{Z}
.
It only remains to show
|n|\geq 2
, that is,
|n|>1
.

Unless
s
is even and
B
contains the negative root

\beta(s):=-s-c(s)
with

being given in Lemma
5.5
,
inequality (
78
) in Proposition
5.12
implies

|s+\beta_{j}|>1
for
j=1,\dots,d
, and hence

|n|^{s-1}=|M|=|s+\beta_{1}|\cdots|s+\beta_{d}|>1
, which yields
|n|>1
.
Suppose now that
s
is even and
\beta(s)\in B
.
We may put
\beta_{1}=\beta(s)
.
From Lemma
5.5
we have
c_{0}<|s+\beta_{1}|<1
and

, as the algebraic integer
\beta_{1}
is not a rational integer.
If

5.13
and

.
Finally suppose

Therefore
