Abstract
. We prove Banach, Newton-Raphson and Brouwer fixed point theorems in the framework of generalized smooth functions, a minimal extension of Colombeau’s theory (and hence of classical distribution theory) which makes it possible to model nonlinear singular problems, while at the same time sharing a number of fundamental properties with ordinary smooth functions, such as the closure with respect to composition and several non trivial classical theorems of the calculus. The proved results allows one to deal with equations of the form F ⁡ ( x ) = 0 F(x)=0 , where F F is a generalized smooth function, in particular, a Sobolev-Schwartz distribution. We consider examples with singularities that are not included in the classical version of these theorems.

1.
Introduction: solving nonlinear equations with generalized functions

An superficial assumption of Leibniz’s viewpoint
natura
non facit saltus
,
[
27
]
, immediately collapses with our present
mathematical models, which frequently consider various types of discontinuities.
For example, we could be interested in large deformations of solid
bodies or very rapid mechanical fractures (elastoplasticity),
[
38
,
6
,
8
]
,
ruptures in geophysics,
[
4
,
10
]
, micromechanical
models for granular materials,
[
7
,
3
]
, in the dynamics
of two interpenetrating fluids (multifluid flows),
[
37
,
28
]
,
discontinuities in Lagrangian optics,
[
25
]
, wave propagation
in a medium with piecewise smooth characteristics (e.g. seismic waves
in stratified media,
[
22
]
), or solutions of the Schrödinger
equation for an infinite rectangular potential well or even a rectangular
potential well with periodic barriers changing at infinite frequency,

[
11
,
39
]
. A common mathematical framework used in these
models is that of Sobolev spaces, and hence of weak derivatives, and
Sobolev-Schwartz distributions. However, the informal use of nonlinear
operations, pointwise values for generalized functions and their derivatives,
and also the useful adoption of infinitesimal or infinite quantities
to construct idealized models, are necessary tools in dealing with
singular models that cannot be formalized using the aforementioned
mathematical theories (see e.g.
[
8
,
5
]
for a general overview
and the previous citations for specific applications). Clearly, opting

only
for numerical solutions does not allow to have general
theorems studying these solutions and their properties, and hence
a broader and deeper understanding.

J.F. Colombeau’s theory of generalized functions enables all these
modeling possibilities between embedded Sobolev-Schwartz distributions,
avoiding the difficulty of the Schwartz impossibility theorem. See
e.g.
[
8
,
32
,
33
,
18
]
for an introduction with applications.
In particular, generalized smooth functions (GSF) are a minimal extension
of Colombeau’s theory (and hence of classical distribution theory)
which makes it possible to model nonlinear singular problems, while
at the same time sharing a number of fundamental properties with ordinary
smooth functions, such as the closure with respect to composition
and several non trivial classical theorems of the calculus, see
[
5
,
12
,
29
,
24
,
16
]
.
One could describe GSF as a methodological restoration of Cauchy-Dirac’s
original conception of generalized function, see
[
26
,
19
]
.
In essence, the idea of Cauchy and Dirac (but also of Poisson, Kirchhoff,
Helmholtz, Kelvin and Heaviside) was to view generalized functions
as suitable types of smooth set-theoretical maps, obtained from ordinary
smooth maps depending on suitable infinitesimal or infinite parameters.

In the present paper, we deal with Banach, Newton and Brouwer fixed
point methods in the framework of GSF. This allows us to solve equations
of the form
F(x)=0
, where
F
is a GSF, in particular, a Sobolev-Schwartz
distribution. Even if we consider only the case where
x
is a (non-Archimedean,
i.e.
x
can also be an infinitesimal or an infinite number)
n
-dimensional
point, this work is preliminary to a subsequent one where we will
deal with infinite-dimensional equations.

The paper is self-contained in the sense that it contains all the
statements required for the proofs we are going to present, all the
needed notions of GSF theory are introduced, and only an elementary
knowledge of Colombeau theory is needed.

In Sec.
2
, we provide an introduction
to generalized numbers and their topology, as well as GSF and some
of their analytical properties. Sec.
3

treats the Banach fixed point theorem in finite dimensional spaces
of generalized points. We continue in Sec.
4

to treat Newton’s method in finite dimensions and the corresponding
quadratic convergence. Then, we conclude this work with some examples
in Sec.
5
, in particular we consider examples with
singularities that are not included in the classical version of these
methods.

2.2.
Topologies on

(2.1)

Generalized smooth functions and their differential calculus

Using the ring
\mathchoice{{\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}}{}^{\scalebox{0.6}{$\rho$}}}{{\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}}{}^{\scalebox{0.56}{$\rho$}}}{{\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}}{}^{\scalebox{0.47}{$\rho$}}}{{\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}}{}^{\scalebox{0.35}{$\rho$}}}\widetilde{\mathbb{R}}

infinitesimal standard deviation. If we denote this probability density
by
f(x,\sigma)
, and set
\sigma=[\sigma_{\varepsilon}]\in\mathchoice{{\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}}{}^{\scalebox{0.6}{$\rho$}}}{{\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}}{}^{\scalebox{0.56}{$\rho$}}}{{\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}}{}^{\scalebox{0.47}{$\rho$}}}{{\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}}{}^{\scalebox{0.35}{$\rho$}}}\widetilde{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}

This is the basic idea behind the following

Definition 4

and
Y\subseteq\mathchoice{{\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}}{}^{\scalebox{0.6}{$\rho$}}}{{\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}}{}^{\scalebox{0.56}{$\rho$}}}{{\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}}{}^{\scalebox{0.47}{$\rho$}}}{{\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}}{}^{\scalebox{0.35}{$\rho$}}}\widetilde{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

be arbitrary subsets of generalized points. Then, we say that

f:X\longrightarrow Y\text{ is a \emph{generalized smooth function}}

if
f:X\longrightarrow Y
is a set theoretical map and there exists a net
f_{\varepsilon}\in\mbox{$\mathcal{C}$}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{d})

defining

Theorem 13

(Brouwer)
.

Every generalized continuous map
f\in\mathchoice{{\vphantom{\mathcal{G}}}{}^{\scalebox{0.6}{$\rho$}}}{{\vphantom{\mathcal{G}}}{}^{\scalebox{0.56}{$\rho$}}}{{\vphantom{\mathcal{G}}}{}^{\scalebox{0.47}{$\rho$}}}{{\vphantom{\mathcal{G}}}{}^{\scalebox{0.35}{$\rho$}}}\mathcal{GC}^{0}([0,1]^{d},[0,1]^{d})

has a fixed point.

Proof.

Let
f_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(U_{\varepsilon},\mathbb{R}^{d})
be continuous representatives
of
f
, so that
\left\langle U_{\varepsilon}\right\rangle\supseteq[0,1]^{d}=[[0,1]_{\mathbb{R}}^{d}]\subseteq\mathchoice{{\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}}{}^{\scalebox{0.6}{$\rho$}}}{{\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}}{}^{\scalebox{0.56}{$\rho$}}}{{\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}}{}^{\scalebox{0.47}{$\rho$}}}{{\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}}{}^{\scalebox{0.35}{$\rho$}}}\widetilde{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Classically proceeding by contradiction (see also
[
16
, Lem. 11]
),
we have
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\supseteq[0,1]_{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
for all
\varepsilon

small. This means that
f_{\varepsilon}
, which is continuous, can be restricted
to
f_{\varepsilon}|_{[0,1]^{d}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and is continuous. Now,
the case might occur, that one component of
f_{\varepsilon}^{j}
, for
j=1,2,...,d

can be greater than
1
or less than
0
up to a negligible amount.
To deal with this case, we can take new representatives by setting

\bar{f}_{\varepsilon}
as
\bar{f}_{\varepsilon}^{j}=\min(f_{\varepsilon}^{j},1)
.

We need to show that this function is continuous, but this
follows similarly to the standard case where we can define the
\min

as

\bar{f}_{\varepsilon}^{j}=\min(f_{\varepsilon}^{j},1)=-\max(-f_{\varepsilon}^{j},-1)=-(\dfrac{1}{2}(-f_{\varepsilon}^{j}-1+\lvert-f_{\varepsilon}^{j}+1\rvert)).

The last expression is a composition of continuous functions,
hence
\bar{f}_{\varepsilon}=\min(f_{\varepsilon}^{j},1)
is continuous too. The
similar procedure works considering
\max(f_{\varepsilon}^{j},1).
This
yields that the initial function can be written as
f(x)=[(\bar{f_{\varepsilon}^{1}}(x_{\varepsilon}),\dots,\bar{f_{\varepsilon}^{d}}(x_{\varepsilon})]
,
i.e. we can change representatives so that
f_{\varepsilon}
remains below
1 and above 0.

We therefore proved that we can find new representatives such that

\forall^{0}\varepsilon:\ \bar{f}_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,1]_{\mathbb{R}}^{d},[0,1]_{\mathbb{R}}^{d}).

We can hence apply the standard version of Brouwer’s Fixed
point theorem to find a fixed point
x_{\varepsilon}\in[0,1]_{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
:

\bar{f_{\varepsilon}}(x_{\varepsilon})=x_{\varepsilon}.

Setting
x:=[x_{\varepsilon}]\in[0,1]^{d}
finishes the proof.
∎

Like in the classical case, via translation this result
can be extended to any interval

Quadratic convergence of Newton method

Definition 18

We say that the sequence
