Influence of microwave excitation mode and antenna-sample orientation on rebar detection using microwave infrared thermography
Authors: Barbara Szymanik, Sam Ang Keo, Franck Brachelet, Didier Defer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the use of active infrared thermography combined with microwave excitation for identifying and assessing reinforcement bars in concrete structures. The proposed approach integrates numerical modeling and experimental validation to ensure accurate and reliable results. The first phase involves developing a detailed numerical model comprising a microwave source, a broadband antenna, and a concrete structure with specified reinforcement bar arrangements. This model simulates the interaction between microwave excitation and embedded reinforcement, analyzing temperature distributions and thermal responses on the concrete surface. The goal is to optimize the measurement methodology by evaluating parameters such as excitation mode and antenna vs. sample configuration. The second phase focuses on experimental validation of the numerical findings. An experimental setup replicates the modeled conditions to compare real-world thermal patterns with simulated predictions, ensuring consistency and reliability. By combining numerical simulations with experimental testing, this study aims to establish a robust framework for using active infrared thermography with microwave excitation in non-destructive evaluation of reinforced concrete structures. The approach seeks to provide a precise, efficient method for assessing the condition and layout of reinforcement bars in concrete, contributing to advancements in structural inspection techniques.

Introduction

Reinforced concrete has been a cornerstone of modern construction for over a century, valued for its strength, durability, and versatility. Despite its widespread use, concrete structures are prone to various forms of degradation over time, influenced by environmental exposure, material fatigue, improper maintenance, or construction flaws. This deterioration can lead to critical issues such as cracks, voids, corrosion of reinforcement bars, and moisture ingress, all of which compromise the safety and longevity of the structure. Consequently, building codes worldwide mandate regular inspections to ensure structural integrity and compliance with safety standards.

Given the importance of preserving the structural and functional integrity of concrete, these inspections must often be performed after the concrete has hardened and without causing damage to the structure itself. As a result, non-destructive testing (NDT) methods have become indispensable tools in assessing the condition of reinforced concrete. These methods allow for detailed evaluation without compromising the material or structure. The key objectives of reinforced concrete testing can be listed as follows:

Structural condition assessment and defect detection: Accurately determining the dimensions of structural components and identifying defects such as voids, cracks, delaminations, and foreign inclusions is critical. Ultrasonic testing and ground-penetrating radar (GPR) are commonly employed for this purpose1–8. These techniques are highly efficient and reliable; however, interpreting the acquired data often requires specialized expertise due to the complexity of results. Thermographic methods, both active and passive, provide additional capabilities, particularly in identifying surface-level flaws like plaster separation or minor delamination9,10. While these methods are promising, their effectiveness is typically constrained to near-surface defects.Reinforcement bar location and corrosion monitoring: Locating and assessing the condition of reinforcement bars is essential for structural diagnostics. Electromagnetic methods, such as eddy current testing or magnetic methods, offer a promising approach by enabling both the detection and characterization of embedded reinforcement11–16. Radiographic techniques, while highly precise, are less commonly used due to their operational complexity and the potential health hazards they pose17–20. Impedance tomography presents another viable option, though its requirement for direct contact with the structure can be a limitation in certain scenarios. These approaches help in identifying areas of concern, enabling targeted maintenance and repair21–23.

While current NDT methods offer substantial insights into the condition of concrete structures, they are not without limitations. Many techniques, such as ultrasonic testing and GPR, produce complex data that require advanced processing and expertise to interpret accurately. Radiographic methods, although precise, are often impractical for routine inspections due to safety and operational concerns.

Active thermography, a powerful non-destructive testing method, relies on the use of an external energy source to induce temperature contrasts within a material, enabling the detection of internal defects or features. Traditional heating methods, such as halogen lamps, flash lamps, infrared radiators, or hot air systems, often fall short when applied to large concrete structures due to their limited penetration depth and inefficiency. In response to these limitations, this study proposes the use of microwave heating as an energy source for active thermography, offering a novel approach to enhance the technique’s applicability. Microwave heating is unique in its volumetric nature, which allows the internal regions of a material to be heated directly, unlike conventional surface-heating methods24. This volumetric heating ensures rapid energy transfer, as the specific volume of the material is heated almost instantaneously. However, the heating efficiency depends not only on the thermal properties of the material but also on its electrical characteristic parameters. An intriguing effect occurs when microwaves interact with metallic elements, such as steel reinforcement bars embedded in concrete. Owing to the skin effect, microwaves possess an extremely shallow penetration depth in metals, so that heat generation is concentrated exclusively on the surface of steel reinforcement25. This phenomenon significantly influences the observable thermal patterns on the surface of the concrete and provides a unique signature for identifying and analyzing the reinforcement.

Materials and methods

In this chapter, we present the key assumptions and methodologies underlying both the numerical modeling and the experimental studies conducted in this research. The discussion is structured into two main sections: the first focuses on the computational approach, while the second provides details regarding the experimental setup and procedures.

Regarding the numerical modeling, we introduce a detailed computational model designed to simulate the investigated process using two distinct heating strategies. The first approach assumes continuous heating, where a constant power input is applied over a fixed period of time. The second approach considers heating in a modulated mode, in which the power input varies according to a predefined periodic function. The numerical simulations were performed using the COMSOL Multiphysics package, a widely used finite element analysis tool that enables accurate modeling of heat transfer and related physical phenomena.

In the second part of the chapter, we shift our focus to the experimental studies. We provide a comprehensive description of the sample used in the measurements. Additionally, we outline the measurement methodology, detailing the experimental setup, the instrumentation employed, and the procedures followed.

Numerical models

The numerical models were developed to closely represent the experimental setup while allowing for controlled parametric studies. The simulation included a broadband horn aluminum antenna as the excitation source and a concrete block embedded with eight steel reinforcing bars (Fig. 1). The choice of a broadband horn antenna was made to ensure efficient energy transmission and coverage over a wide frequency range, making it suitable for analyzing the thermal response of the embedded steel reinforcements within the concrete structure.

The dimensions of the modeled concrete block were selected to reproduce the essential features of the experimental reinforced concrete specimen while limiting the computational cost of the 3D electromagnetic–thermal simulation. Particular attention was paid to preserving the parameters most relevant to the thermal response, namely the concrete cover, rebar diameter, and rebar spacing. The adopted values (12 mm rebar diameter, 100 mm rebar spacing, and 15 mm concrete cover) correspond to the experimental specimen and are representative of practical reinforced concrete configurations.

Although the overall dimensions of the simulated concrete block (800 mm × 800 mm × 65 mm) were slightly reduced compared to the actual specimen used in the experimental studies (1000 mm × 1000 mm × 65 mm), this modification was introduced to optimize computational efficiency without compromising the fundamental physical effects being investigated. The primary objective of the numerical model was to explore the influence of multiple reinforcing bars on the temperature distribution within the concrete, particularly in the presence of metallic inclusions that can significantly affect heat conduction and dissipation. By incorporating multiple steel bars into the model, it was possible to examine the extent to which thermal variations occur due to the presence of numerous conductive elements within the otherwise insulating concrete matrix.

The modeled concrete block dimensions of 800 mm × 800 mm × 65 mm provides a realistic representation of a typical reinforced concrete slab. The center-to-center distance between adjacent rebars was 100 mm, ensuring a consistent spacing that resembles typical reinforcement layouts used in construction. The concrete cover of 15 mm was the layer of concrete above the reinforcement on the side facing the antenna.

Each of these configurations was chosen to reflect possible real-world conditions while also providing valuable insights into the role of angular positioning in energy absorption and heat propagation.

The first scenario, where the sample is positioned at 0°, represents an idealized case in which the electromagnetic waves from the antenna are directed perpendicularly to the surface of the concrete block. In this orientation, the energy transfer is maximized due to the direct alignment of the antenna’s radiation pattern with the sample. However, despite its theoretical significance, this configuration cannot be implemented experimentally because the thermal camera, which is used for temperature measurements, must have an unobstructed view of the sample’s surface. In a real experimental setup, the camera would be blocked by the antenna itself in this configuration. Nevertheless, this scenario was included in the numerical analysis to establish a reference case for evaluating the impact of angular variation on heat distribution.

The numerical models were developed using the commercial finite element computing environment COMSOL Multiphysics, specifically leveraging the Heat Transfer (HT) and Electromagnetic Waves (EMW) modules. The coupled electromagnetic-thermal model provided insights into the interaction between microwave energy and the reinforced concrete block, capturing key physical processes such as wave absorption, resistive heating, and subsequent heat transfer. The study was divided into two steps: the frequency domain solved only for EMW module, and the time domain solved only for HT module. This way, in the first step the total power dissipation would be computed, and then the time evolution of temperature within the sample would be analyzed.

To accurately simulate the experimental setup, an electromagnetic wave was generated using a port boundary condition applied to a specific wall of the waveguide. The following parameters were defined for the simulation: waveguide mode TE10 (the dominant transverse electric mode), operating frequency: 2.45 GHz (average value of 2.45 ± 0.05 GHz), which is a standard industrial, scientific, and medical (ISM) band frequency commonly used for microwave heating applications37, and heating duration was set to 300 s.

For the proposed numerical model, a finite element mesh consisting of 486,342 tetrahedral elements was generated (Fig. 5). The mesh density was carefully adjusted to ensure the accuracy and stability of the simulation, particularly in regions where electromagnetic wave propagation and heat transfer phenomena were most critical, i.e., the waveguide, antenna, and sample domains.

Experimental setup and specimens

Two experimental campaigns were conducted to evaluate the behavior of a reinforced concrete (RC) wall under microwave heating, using a setup that closely mirrored the conditions in a numerical simulation. The RC wall sample was equipped with extremities of steel bars protruding from the top of the wall, which allowed for precise identification and monitoring of the location of each steel bar during testing. This feature was crucial for understanding the behavior of the reinforcement within the concrete during the experiment.

The experimental setup is shown in Fig. 6, and as per safety regulations, the tests were carried out inside a microwave protection enclosure. This enclosure had previously been used in similar experiments, ensuring safety and consistency across tests27,28.

Fig. 6The experimental setup (from34, used with permission).

The experimental setup (from34, used with permission).

The heating system used was a 2.45 GHz microwave system, which was consistent with the configuration used in the numerical model. The RC wall sample was heated with an average power of 600 W for a duration of 300 s, utilizing only a modulation heating mode.

The concrete slab was cast with a water/cement/sand/coarse aggregate mixing ratio of 1:1.6:4.45:4.7 (by weight). The cement type I 52.5R, sand (0/4), and coarse aggregate (4/12) were used. The slab was exposed to an ambient room temperature of 20 ± 2 °C and a relative humidity of 45% for 1 month before being subjected to the tests at the age of 2 months. The wall was equipped with nine steel bars, each 12 mm in diameter, spaced 100 mm apart inside the concrete matrix. This arrangement ensured that the thermal effects on different reinforcement locations could be monitored. Additionally, the concrete cover exposed to the antenna was 15 mm, which provided a relevant scenario for investigating heat penetration through typical reinforced concrete constructions.

The electromagnetic wave generation was carried out using a magnetron at the power of 800 W (maximum power) of a commercial microwave oven. The controls ‘section of the oven was kept to adjust the desired emission’s duration and power. The microwave heating was directed at the sample using a pyramidal horn antenna (with an opening of 40°), placed at a distance of 40 cm from the surface of the wall. Two different antenna orientations were tested (Fig. 7): one at a 30° angle (denoted as direction α) and another at a 45° angle (denoted as direction β). This variation in the antenna’s positioning was intended to study the directional effects of microwave energy on the thermal response of the concrete. In order to capture the thermal distribution and behavior during the test, an infrared camera sensitive to medium waves in the range of 3–5 µm, with a 320 × 256 matrix detector in InSb (indium antimonide), was employed to record thermograms, offering a detailed view of temperature changes in real-time. The camera had a frame rate of 5 images per second, ensuring high temporal resolution for monitoring heat patterns. For each antenna orientation, the infrared camera was positioned at two distinct locations to cover the entire wall surface. When the antenna was positioned at a 30° direction (α), the camera was placed 2.11 meters away from the sample, in the 45° direction (β). In contrast, when the antenna was at a 45° direction (α), the camera was placed 2.32 m away in the 30° direction (β). These configurations allowed comprehensive coverage and accurate temperature readings from different angles. The time interval between both experimental scenarios was 2 days.

Fig. 7Visualization of the setup configurations used in the experiments (from34, used with permission).

Visualization of the setup configurations used in the experiments (from34, used with permission).

The microwave signal, detected by a sensor, was recorded as an output signal, shown in Fig. 8. The pulse train, corresponding to the average microwave power of 600 W, had a period of 30 s, with a pulse duration of 23 s. Within each pulse train, there was a modulation period of 200 ms, with individual pulses lasting 100 ms. This pulse-width modulation (PWM) cycle, with a 50% duty cycle within a 23-s pulse, was simplified in the numerical model as a continuous pulse with a corresponding power reduction.

Numerical modeling results

In this section, we present the results of the numerical modeling for all the previously introduced scenarios, carefully evaluating the outcomes and comparing them with the experimental data. The numerical results serve as a basis for understanding the behavior of reinforced concrete under microwave heating and assessing the performance of the model. First, we will present the raw numerical results, which are critical for evaluating the accuracy and reliability of the simulation. The Fig. 9 presents a comparison of the temperature distribution in the sample after 300 s of heating and a 0° angle between the antenna and the sample for both considered scenarios: continuous heating (Fig. 9a) and modulated heating (Fig. 9b). It is important to note that, for the continuous heating scenario, the power level was reduced to 77% in order to ensure that the energy supplied to the sample was comparable to that in the modulated heating case. This adjustment corresponds to a heating cycle in which the sample is subjected to 7 s of no heating during a 30-s interval, mirroring the energy delivery pattern of the modulated heating scenario.

Since the penetration depth of electromagnetic waves in steel at 2.45 GHz frequency is extremely shallow—limited to just a few micrometers (approximately 4 μm in our case)—the steel bars within the reinforced concrete wall should not experience significant direct heating from microwave exposure. Instead, most of the microwave energy is absorbed by the surrounding concrete matrix, which exhibits a much greater penetration depth for electromagnetic waves at these frequencies. This distinction is crucial when analyzing the thermal response of the material, as the heat distribution primarily results from the interaction between the microwaves and the concrete rather than direct heating of the reinforcement.

Fig. 10 provides a clear visualization of the heating pattern within the concrete block, emphasizing the impact of the embedded steel bars on the thermal distribution. It is evident from the figure that the concrete surrounding the reinforcement undergoes substantial heating, while the steel bars act as thermal barriers, restricting the spread of heat. As a result, warmer regions are observed around the bars, particularly above them, where heat accumulates due to the redistribution of thermal energy.

Fig. 10a presents the temperature distribution at a selected time point (300 s of heating) for the discussed case, where the camera is positioned at a 0° angle to the sample, and continuous heating is applied. The visualization is taken from a plane intersecting the reinforcing bars (21 mm from the sample surface), clearly demonstrating that the steel rods do not heat up directly due to microwave exposure. Additionally, Fig. 10b shows the temperature distribution on a plane above the rods (14 mm from the sample surface), revealing the significant influence of the reinforcement on the overall heat distribution. The presence of the steel bars alters the thermal flow within the concrete, creating distinct temperature variations around them. Of particular interest is the analysis of total power dissipation density, also illustrated in Fig. 10c. It distinctly highlights that energy dissipation occurs primarily within the cement matrix, reinforcing the observation that microwaves are absorbed by the concrete rather than the steel reinforcement. This finding further supports the conclusion that the heating effect results mainly from the interaction between microwaves and the dielectric properties of concrete rather than direct absorption by the steel bars.

Conclusions and further work

This study aimed to develop a comprehensive numerical methodology for analyzing the complex network of reinforcement bars embedded within a concrete structural sample. A key focus was placed on understanding the dynamics of temperature change and its interaction with the reinforcement layout. To achieve this, a systematic approach was presented, including a trend elimination strategy to mitigate large-scale heating inconsistencies and a method for accurately identifying the positions of reinforcing bars. In order to validate the numerical findings, a series of controlled experiments were performed, providing crucial experimental data for comparison.

One of the critical differences between the numerical modeling and the experimental setup was the heating approach. While the numerical simulations utilized both continuous and modulated heating, the experiments employed modulated heating. What is interesting is the numerical simulations shown, that there is no significant difference between the two proposed approaches. The results from numerical modeling enhance the interpretation of the diffraction and interference phenomena that always happen in the detection of reinforcement bar layers by microwave thermography. As a complement to the previous interpretation by experimental approaches, numerical modeling illustrated clearly the heating conduction inside the reinforced concrete wall in the heating face.

A significant challenge in analyzing the thermal images was the presence of non-uniform heating effects caused by the radiation pattern of the antenna and the orientation of the sample. To address this, Gaussian filtering was applied to the experimental data, greatly improving the clarity of the results. This filtering technique helped suppress noise and enhance the contrast between areas influenced by reinforcement and those that were not, making it easier to accurately pinpoint the locations of the steel bars. The benefits of using Gaussian filtering were particularly evident when comparing raw and processed temperature profiles. Without filtering, the temperature distributions contained fluctuations that obscured the reinforcement positions, making unambiguous identification difficult. However, after applying Gaussian filtering, the thermal signatures of the rebars became significantly clearer, ensuring reliable detection. This highlights the value of advanced post-processing techniques in refining experimental results and improving the correlation between numerical and experimental data.

The limitations and disadvantages of the study lie in only one type of concrete and only one configuration of steel bars (spacing and diameter) in the experimental campaigns. The variability of these parameters would be considered in order to validate the effect of other eventual physical phenomena during the interaction of the sample with the microwave. Future work could explore optimizing the heating parameters and further refining signal processing techniques to enhance detection accuracy across various structural configurations. The perspective of the present study would also be the application of the MIRT to the detection of corrosion of the reinforcement bars in the reinforced concrete structure.
