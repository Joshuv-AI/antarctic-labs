A new hope: locus coeruleus-norepinephrine system at the nexus of neuropsychiatric symptoms
Authors: Anu Korukonda, David Weinshenker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The earliest stages of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) are frequently characterized by neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS) such as anxiety, agitation, depression, compulsivity, appetite dysregulation, and sleep disturbances, often preceding measurable cognitive decline. Evidence from clinical and animal studies implicates hyperactivity of the locus coeruleus-norepinephrine (LC-NE) system as a mechanistic driver of these behaviors. Here, we review noradrenergic circuits that can potentially underlie psychiatric disturbances to identify therapeutic targets for preventing and delaying onset of AD. Given that this system influences attention, arousal, mood, and stress responses, LC-NE hyperactivity across circuitry involving amygdala, thalamus, hypothalamus, anterior cingulate cortex, prefrontal cortex, and olfactory areas can contribute to NPS features in early AD. Advances in neuroimaging and physiological measures of noradrenergic function have enabled in vivo tracking of LC integrity and NE transmission, offering the opportunity to detect LC-NE dysfunction early in disease progression and potentially implement targeted pharmacologic and neuromodulatory interventions to restore optimal LC-NE tone. Overall, dissection of LC-NE circuitry and its clinical translation hold promise for developing biomarker-driven, stage-specific interventions to reduce NPS burden and enhance the efficacy of disease-modifying therapies in AD.

Alzheimer’s disease

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by memory loss, language and motor deficits, executive dysfunction, and declining functional independence [1, 2]. Currently, a staggering 6.7 million individuals in the United States and 50 million people worldwide are affected, with prevalence projected to more than double by 2050 [1, 3]. AD imposes immense emotional, social, and financial burdens on patients, families, caregivers, and healthcare systems.

Neuropathologically, AD is defined by the accumulation of extracellular amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques and intracellular tau aggregates called neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) [4–6]. Longitudinal biomarker studies indicate that these pathological processes begin years to decades before the onset of cognitive deficits [5, 6]. Growing evidence suggests that the earliest signs of AD may not be purely cognitive. Psychiatric and behavioral disturbances often appear in individuals who are cognitively intact but show early AD-like neuropathology and encompass symptoms including anxiety, agitation, aggression, apathy, delusions, impulsivity, compulsive behaviors, appetite dysregulation, and sleep disturbances [7]. Historically considered risk factors for both AD and non-AD dementias, these neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS) and sleep-related disorders are now recognized as integral components of early AD [7–9].

This evolving perspective has motivated the formalization of mild behavioral impairment (MBI), characterized by persistent behavioral and affective changes, within the AD diagnostic framework [10–12]. MBI has been proposed as an intermediate clinical stage linking asymptomatic AD pathology and MCI (Fig. 1), with NPS conferring an increased risk of progression from MCI to dementia, underscoring their prognostic value in AD pathogenesis [8].

Interestingly, the earliest detectable tau pathology appears in subcortical nuclei that regulate arousal, emotion, and behavior, positioning these structures at the forefront of MBI-related research [13–15]. Among them, the brainstem locus coeruleus (LC), the primary source of norepinephrine (NE) in the central nervous system (CNS), is the first region to exhibit soluble, hyperphosphorylated ‘pretangle’ tau [16]. Through its extensive projections to the forebrain, midbrain, spinal cord, and cerebellum, the LC influences processes often disrupted in early AD, including arousal, vigilance, mood, cognition, stress reactivity, and sleep-wake regulation [17–20]. The convergence of LC tau pathology and NPS manifestations implicates LC-NE dysfunction as a key mechanism underlying MBI and associated behavioral disturbances [21, 22].

Despite recent approval of amyloid-targeting disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) such as lecanemab and donanemab, their clinical utility is limited by modest cognitive benefits, unknown effects on NPS burden, and significant safety and cost concerns [23]. These gaps emphasize the need to better understand circuit-level mechanisms contributing to the psychiatric and behavioral symptoms that may represent the earliest clinical manifestation of AD. In this review, we examine noradrenergic circuits that, when dysregulated, may contribute to MBI-related phenotypes across the AD continuum, highlighting LC-NE hyperactivity as a potential link between early tau pathology and behavioral changes. We also assess animal models of discrete MBI-relevant behaviors to understand how LC tau accumulation may drive progression from asymptomatic AD pathology to MBI, MCI, and ultimately dementia. By integrating clinical and animal studies, we aim to identify therapeutic targets and monitoring strategies focused on the behavioral manifestations of AD.

General properties of LC-NE transmission

Situated near the base of the fourth ventricle, the LC is the principal source of NE in the CNS. Despite comprising only ~30,000–50,000 neurons in humans, the LC has widespread projections to the neocortex, hippocampus, amygdala, hindbrain, and spinal cord, arising from distinct modules of NE neurons and enabling diverse functions [24]. Seminal animal studies have established that LC-NE transmission modulates states such as attention, vigilance, emotion, and sleep, as well as physiological processes including heart rate, respiration, and blood flow distribution [17, 25, 26]. The canonical view asserts that the LC operates in two firing modes, tonic or phasic, with pacemaker activity varying as a function of arousal, and novel or salient stimuli and goal-directed behaviors eliciting phasic bursts [27, 28]. This strict bimodal classification has recently been challenged by a large-scale cross-species analysis that reveals highly diverse LC firing patterns [29]. Regardless, LC firing releases NE onto metabotropic G-protein coupled adrenoreceptors (ARs). These receptors include Gq-coupled α1-ARs (α1A, α1B, α1D), Gs-couped β-ARs (β1, β2, β3), and Gi-coupled α2-ARs (α2A, α2B, α2C) [28, 30]. LC-NE output exhibits an inverted-U relationship with behavioral state, in which low tonic activity is associated with disengagement and hypovigilance, whereas moderate tonic elevations, coupled with phasic responses to salient stimuli, support task engagement. Meanwhile, excessive firing, such as during chronic stress, promotes hyperarousal and distractibility, degrading focused performance [17, 18, 25, 30].

Pathological evidence of LC-NE dysfunction in AD

Postmortem studies identify the LC as the earliest site of hyperphosphorylated tau, with pretangle condensates appearing decades before forebrain Aβ pathology [14, 16, 31]. Phosphorylated tau (pTau), detected by the AT8 antibody which recognizes the pTau residues, Ser202 and Thr205, has been shown to accumulate in proximal LC axons (Braak stage 0a), extend into the somatodendritic compartment (Braak stage 0b), and subsequently appear in other neuromodulatory nuclei, including the dorsal raphe, basal forebrain, and hypothalamus (Braak stage 0c) [16, 32]. 3-D imaging further revealed that pretangle tau first emerges in the LC core and dendro-dendritic clusters within dorsal LC processes projecting to susceptible AD regions [33]. With progression, insoluble NFTs follow a trajectory from the transentorhinal cortex (Braak stages I-II) to neocortical and association areas (Braak stages III-IV) to primary and secondary cortices (Braak stages V-VI) [16]. Spatiotemporal mapping in living subjects further supports this sequence, showing that reduced LC integrity precedes tau accumulation in the medial temporal lobe [34]. Stereological analyses show that 7.9% of LC neurons contain pretangle tau at Stage 0, which doubles by Stage I, with LC volume declining by 8.4% along the rostrocaudal axis over Braak stages, despite neuronal cell body preservation until Stage III [32, 35]. Proposed mechanisms for the selective LC vulnerability include (i) anatomic proximity to the fourth ventricle and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)-borne toxicants and inflammatory molecules, (ii) high metabolic load from pacemaker firing, (iii) long, highly branched, and unmyelinated axons, and (iv) neurotoxic NE metabolites that trigger tau pathology such as 3,4-dihydroxyphenylglycoladhedyde (DOPEGAL), although direct causal links remain to be investigated [22, 36, 37].

Clinical evidence of LC-NE contributions to neuropsychiatric symptoms in AD

Several studies connect tau pathology in the LC to NPS in early AD. Postmortem analyses combined with informant-based assessments revealed that subjects at Braak I/II have higher odds of anxiety, agitation, depression, appetite changes, and sleep disturbances [21]. One report indicates that LC tangle burden and cognition are partially mediated by social engagement, with increased tau aggregation associated with reduced social interaction, particularly among cognitively unimpaired individuals [38]. A meta-analysis of 45 clinical AD studies further implicates LC-NE dysregulation as a key contributor to agitation [39].

In vivo, neuromelanin (NM)-sensitive magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is widely used as a proxy for LC structural integrity [40]. Because LC neurons accumulate NM granules as a byproduct of catecholamine metabolism, signal intensity quantified using contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) is typically interpreted as an index of LC structural integrity rather than functional activity [41]. Reduced NM signal has been observed in individuals with amnestic MCI and daytime sleep-related dysfunction [42]. As Braak stages progress, the mid-caudal LC signal gradually diminishes, with larger losses reported at stages III-VI [43]. Ultra-high field 7T MRI, which considerably improves LC spatial resolution, similarly demonstrates lower middle-to-caudal LC integrity, measured as signal intensity relative to the pontine tegmentum, correlating with frequent nocturnal awakenings, especially in cognitively intact older adults with elevated plasma pTau231 [44]. Follow-up studies showed that pTau231, but not Aβ42/40, associates with reduced rostral LC integrity across aging and predicts cognitive decline [45].

In contrast to structural metrics, positron emission tomography (PET) approaches provide indirect indices of LC metabolic activity via MRI-guided delineation of the LC, given limited spatial resolution of PET imaging. FDG-PET studies indicate that elevated LC metabolism in preclinical AD is associated with higher CSF Aβ42 and cognitive resilience, whereas FDG uptake declines in later AD [46]. In a separate autoradiographic study using the selective NE transporter (NET) radioligand (S,S)-[18F]FMeNER-D2, reduced NET density was observed in the LC and thalamus of AD brains [47]. Although seemingly paradoxical, these findings may reflect stage-dependent alterations in LC function, wherein early compensatory increases in LC metabolic output support cognition but confer greater NPS risk. Consistent with this interpretation, NM signal may serve as a structural proxy for alterations in NE metabolism; specifically, in tau-positive individuals, higher LC CNR predicts MBI-related NPS severity, such as agitation [39, 48], impulsivity, and aggression [43].

Biochemical analyses further implicate noradrenergic dysregulation. CSF NE levels are significantly elevated in MCI and early AD but decline in later stages when LC undergoes frank degeneration [49]. Metabolism of NE by monoamine oxidase A (MAO-A) generates the toxic aldehyde DOPEGAL, which promotes pathogenic tau accumulation and cell death [36, 37, 50]. Postmortem LC tissue from AD patients shows 2.8- and 3.6-fold increases in DOPEGAL and MAO-A, respectively, compared to pathologically intact brains [50]. Further metabolism by catechol-o-methyl transferase results in 3,4-dihydroxyphenylglycol (MHPG), which also increases in AD, with higher MHPG levels correlating with NPS severity that are partially mediated by CSF pTau [51–54]. Even when LC neurons are preserved, they exhibit upregulation of genes coding for ATP metabolism, inflammation, and tyrosine hydroxylase (TH), the rate-limiting enzyme for NE synthesis, suggesting compensatory increases in NE turnover with downstream effects on AR density and neurotransmission [55–57]. For example, aggressive AD patients showed increased α1-AR density and affinity in the frontal cortex [58].

Despite clinical studies linking LC dysfunction to NPS, several methodological limitations hinder precise staging of these associations, particularly in early stages. Detecting tau pathology within the LC at Braak stage 0-I in living subjects remains technically challenging because tau PET ligands have off-target binding to NM, and most biomarker studies rely on temporal or cortical tau measures rather than quantification of brainstem involvement [59]. Furthermore, clinical phenotyping is usually centered on cognitive impairment, with most individuals presenting at the MCI stage. Few studies thus systematically stratify subjects within an MBI framework. Consequently, although there is evidence for LC tau deposition, noradrenergic dysregulation, and behavioral changes, the temporal relationship between these events remains poorly characterized.

Together, the evidence supports a non-linear, stage-dependent model in which the LC-NE system undergoes dynamic alterations across the disease continuum [22, 60] (Fig. 2). Because pathological tau accumulation has been shown to reduce cortical neuron activity, we postulate that early tau deposition within the LC induces compensatory increases in tonic firing, metabolic demand, and NE turnover [61, 62]. While such homeostatic plasticity may transiently preserve cognition, sustained hyperactivity can promote anxiety, agitation, hyperarousal, and depression-like states, phenotypes consistent with affective domains of MBI. As degeneration progresses and LC integrity declines, reduced LC-NE tone may shift symptoms towards motivational deficits, reflected as apathy, inattention, and hypovigilance (Fig. 2). Importantly, MBI is heterogeneous, with distinct domains likely reflecting different deviations from optimal LC-NE transmission. Animal models investigating LC involvement in AD

Rodent models offer powerful tools to investigate how LC disturbances affect NPS symptoms at the level of neuronal excitability, noradrenergic transmission, and post-synaptic changes across disease stages.

LC cell body hyperactivity and molecular alterations

Several tauopathy models have provided insights into intrinsic LC dysfunction, including the PS19 transgenic mouse expressing pan-neuronal P301S mutant human tau and viral-mediated selective expression of pseudophosphorylated or pathogenic P364S human tau within the LC [63, 64, 65]. At the molecular level, tau accumulation is associated with alterations in synaptic and cytoskeletal integrity, proteostasis, and catalytic activity, including upregulation of L-type calcium Cav1.2 channels and hyperpolarization-gated channel Hcn2, further suggesting elevated cellular stress and metabolic demand [63, 64]. Across these models, electrophysiological recordings from pTau-burdened LC neurons demonstrate heightened spontaneous firing [64], depolarized membrane potentials, and reduced GABA(A) α2 subunit expression, indicating neuronal hyperexcitability [65].

The TgF344-AD rat model provides additional insight into stage-dependent changes in LC physiology. Although these rats express mutant human amyloid precursor protein and presenilin-1 but lack a human tau transgene, they develop endogenous intraneural pTau that first appears in the LC, paralleling human pathology [66]. In young TgF344-AD rats with LC-restricted pTau, footshock induces exaggerated LC hyperactivity, whereas older rats with widespread Aβ and pTau exhibit stimulus-evoked LC hypoactivity [67]. Similarly, in aged PS19 mice, LC spontaneous firing and excitability decline along with reduced glutamatergic input [68].

LC terminal dysfunction and NE transmission

Tau pathology also alters NE transmission at projection regions. Selective lesioning with the noradrenergic neurotoxin, N-(2-chloroethyl)-N-ethyl-bromo-benzylamine (DSP-4) using an acute regimen that damages NE terminals while sparing LC cell bodies, results in increased NE turnover from surviving terminals [69, 70]. By contrast, DSP-4 administration using a chronic regimen induces marked LC neuronal loss and aggravates hippocampal neuroinflammation and cell death in PS19 mice, mimicking aspects of late AD [71]. Consistent with these findings, hyperphosphorylated tau inclusions in the LC can also harm noradrenergic fibers in the hippocampus and olfactory bulb (OB) [72]. Correspondingly, TgF344-AD rats exhibit reductions in hippocampal NE fibers and content as pathology progresses [66].

Postsynaptic adaptations

Alterations in presynaptic NE transmission are accompanied by compensatory changes at the postsynaptic level. Loss of LC fibers is associated with enhanced β-AR function [72], which amplifies long-term potentiation at perforant path-dentate granule cell synapses in the hippocampus [73]. These receptor-level adaptations suggest that postsynaptic mechanisms partially compensate for declining presynaptic NE input.

Behavioral consequences of LC-NE dysregulation

Across these models, alterations in LC firing and NE transmission are associated with behavioral phenotypes that parallel NPS observations. Early increases in LC activity are associated with anxiety-like behavior [64, 74], reduced sleep spindle density [64], and depressive-like phenotypes [75]. Pseudophosphorylated tau within the LC also impairs spatial and odor discrimination learning [72]. Notably, these deficits can be rescued by novelty-associated phasic LC stimulation, whereas stress-inducing high tonic activity exacerbates LC integrity and depressive-like behaviors, demonstrating the therapeutic utility of selective LC activity modulation [76].

As pathology progresses, behavioral impairments become more pronounced. Combining DSP-4-induced NE depletion with pTau worsens contextual [71] and social memory deficits [77]. Similarly, older TgF344-AD rats exhibit impaired spatial reversal learning that can be rescued by chemogenetic LC stimulation [66]. Environmental factors may further modulate noradrenergic dysfunction; for example, high-fat diet-induced obesity in younger TgF344-AD rats potentiates spatial working memory deficits and prevents compensatory elevations in hippocampal NE turnover [78].

These models reinforce the stage-dependent trajectory of LC-NE dysfunction. Tau-induced molecular and cellular changes disrupt homeostasis, thereby increasing LC tonic firing, metabolic demand, NE turnover, and postsynaptic adaptations. While this response may initially reflect compensatory processes that preserve cognition, it can push circuits toward maladaptive behaviors. With loss of LC projections and declining integrity, the LC-NE system transitions towards hypoactive states, contributing to cognitive deficits. These findings provide a mechanistic link between tau pathology, LC-NE dysfunction, and distinct behavioral phenotypes. Dissecting underlying noradrenergic circuits may identify targets to reduce NPS and modify disease trajectory.

LC-NE circuitry contributions to NPS in early AD

Arousal and sleep disturbances

Sleep-wake regulation is one of the most salient dimensions of arousal. Sleep disruption is highly prevalent in aging and neurodegenerative disorders, with polysomnography recordings revealing reductions in rapid eye movement (REM) and slow-wave sleep (SWS), which exacerbate cognitive decline [79, 80].

Early insights into LC physiology came from sleep studies in animal models, with LC tonic discharge highest during wakefulness, reduced during SWS, and nearly absent during REM [17, 81]. NE release in brainstem, hippocampus, and amygdala follows a similar state-dependent pattern [82, 83]. Fluctuations in LC firing rates can precede spontaneous sleep-wake transitions and are modulated by spindle activity during non-REM (NREM) sleep [17]. Genetic models reinforce this role; mice lacking dopamine β hydroxylase (DBH), an enzyme required for NE synthesis, exhibit impaired arousal and difficulty transitioning to wakefulness [84]. Even under salient conditions such as exposure to predator odor, amphetamine administration, or mild stressors, DBH-deficient (Dbh-/-) mice display reduced wakefulness and shorter sleep latency compared to controls, although strain-specific differences have been reported in REM duration and extended light-phase sleep [84–86].

Endogenous LC activity also fluctuates across sleep stages. LC firing decreases before spindle-rich intermediate sleep and REM sleep, which are critical for memory consolidation [87]. Optogenetic LC activation during sleep reduces spindle frequency, REM-associated theta power, and NREM delta power without altering overall architecture. Following spatial memory training, such stimulation disrupts CA1 place-field consolidation, biasing animals toward non-hippocampal strategies [88]. At finer timescales, NREM sleep is characterized by persistent infraslow (~50 s) NE oscillations in thalamic regions that couple with spindles and cardiac rhythms [89–91]. Low-frequency stimulation of LC terminals in the somatosensory thalamus, but not cortex, clusters spindles via α1/β-AR-dependent depolarizations of thalamocortical and reticular neurons. These oscillations likely support sensory vigilance during NREM, and their disruption may impair sleep stability [89]. Decreased NE oscillatory amplitude permits microarousals, forgoing spindle-rich sleep necessary for memory consolidation [91]. Elevated LC activity directly shapes sleep-wake transitions: increased firing induces cortical microarousals, while reduced activity facilitates NREM-REM transitions [92]. This is particularly relevant for glymphatic function, which involves the clearance of metabolic waste and neurotoxic proteins during sleep. LC hyperactivity may interfere with these processes by destabilizing spindle dynamics and promoting microarousals, ultimately compromising restorative NREM sleep [90].

Several additional noradrenergic circuits contribute to distinct aspects of arousal and sleep regulation. Local GABAergic interneurons modulate LC-NE output: coincident afferent input onto LC-GABA and LC-NE populations exerts inhibitory control that sharpens phasic responses, whereas non-coincident input primarily regulates tonic firing and global arousal [93]. The ventrolateral preoptic area (vlPO), a canonical hypothalamic sleep-promoting nucleus, receives noradrenergic input that paradoxically promotes wakefulness when engaged. Electrophysiological recordings identify two populations: sleep-active multipolar low-threshold spike (LTS) neurons inhibited by NE through α2-ARs, and wake-active non-LTS bipolar cells excited by NE via α1- and β-ARs [94]. Furthermore, stimulation of LC-NE projections to the POA increases microarousals during NREM and suppresses spindle activity, mimicking stress-induced sleep fragmentation [95]. Another pathway involves noradrenergic projections to the ventral periaqueductal gray (vPAG), a dopaminergic region active during wakefulness but silent during sleep [96]. NE stimulation of astrocytic α1-ARs facilitates glutamate-dependent activation of dopamine neurons, thereby promoting arousal and prolonging sleep latency [97].

Overall, the LC-NE system coordinates arousal, vigilance, and sleep-wake transitions through hypothalamic, thalamic, and brainstem regions. Disruptions to these pathways can impair sleep architecture, interfere with essential functions like glymphatic clearance and sleep spindles, and may play a role in the development of sleep-wake disorders seen in aging.

Anxiety

Anxiety is characterized by heightened apprehensiveness and threat perception and is associated with increased risk of progression to AD in cognitively asymptomatic individuals and those with MCI [98–101]. Evidence from other affective disorders further supports a role for LC dysregulation in threat-related behavioral syndromes. In post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), LC CNR correlates with hyperarousal symptom severity [102]. Functional imaging further demonstrates that responses to salient stimuli are associated with exaggerated threat reactivity, autonomic arousal, and higher LC activity, supporting a role for LC hyperactivity in hyperarousal states [103].

Stress is a potent activator of the LC, increasing tonic firing and forebrain NE output to drive hypervigilance and anxiety [104]. In rodents, the LC is required for anxiogenic behaviors following acute stressors such as restraint, shock, or predator odor [105–107]. Stress exposure produces persistent anxiety-like behavior in the elevated plus maze (EPM) and heightened freezing, accompanied by increased LC discharge and excitability lasting at least a week [105]. This is also associated with structural adaptations of LC neurons, including dendritic arborization and terminal sprouting, which may enhance afferent input and contribute to hyperactivity [108, 109].

At the circuit level, LC-amygdala interactions are essential. Footshock stimulation triggers NE release in the amygdala to 75% above baseline [110], and optogenetically-induced high-tonic LC firing (5 Hz) elicits anxiety-like behavior in the open field and conditioned aversion tasks [106]. Specifically, corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH)-positive inputs from the central nucleus of amygdala (CeA) act on LC CRHR1 to increase tonic activity, while LC projections back to the CeA also mediate anxiogenic phenotypes; selective stimulation of this pathway reproduces stress-induced anxiety in the EPM, whereas inhibition normalizes behavior [111]. Likewise, LC-basolateral amygdala (BLA) projections drive anxiety and aversive learning via β-AR signaling [112]. Anxiogenic responses induced by chronic pain are abolished by chemogenetic inhibition or intra-BLA infusions of a β-AR blocker [113]. Repeated predator stress further sensitizes α1-AR signaling in the BLA in a CRHR1-dependent manner, exaggerating startle and disrupting pre-pulse inhibition, which are hallmarks of PTSD-like states [114].

Noradrenergic signaling is also required for adaptive responses to novelty, which elicit anxiety-like behavior. The novelty-suppressed feeding (NSF) test is a hunger-anxiety conflict-based model where food-deprived animals are presented a food pellet in the center of a novel arena, with longer latencies to feed indicating novelty-induced anxiety [115]. Dbh-/- mice display neophilia, with shorter feeding latencies than controls, a phenotype that is reversed by NE restoration [116, 117]. Notably, NE-deficient mice also show blunted activation in the BLA following NSF, highlighting the role of LC-BLA transmission in novelty-related anxiety [116].

The bidirectional LC-amygdala circuit thus emerges as a critical node in stress-induced anxiety. Particularly, excessive LC-NE transmission drives anxiogenic behavior and induces physiological and structural adaptations that promote anxiety and PTSD-like states.

Depression

Depression, characterized by negative affect and feelings of worthlessness, is very common in aging. A meta-analysis reported that clinically significant depression increases the risk of AD nearly threefold [118]. Late-life major depressive disorder (MDD) is associated with reduced LC-anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) connectivity during attentional tasks, which significantly correlates with depression severity [119]. Postmortem studies further indicate synaptic noradrenergic deficits, including reduced NET levels in the caudal LC [120] and elevated TH immunoreactivity without changes in cell number, suggesting compensatory upregulation of catecholamine synthesis and reduced reuptake [121].

Animal models provide further insight. Stress paradigms, such as forced walking or tail shock, produce depressive-like phenotypes, including anhedonia and reduced locomotion, linked to increased LC burst firing and degeneration of cortical LC terminals [122, 123]. Stress-induced depression is proposed to deplete NE and reduce α2-AR autoreceptor feedback inhibition, leading to disinhibited LC activity [123]. Hyperactivation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, characterized by elevated cortisol and CRH, may further potentiate LC activity and TH expression, reinforcing depressive phenotypes [121, 124–126]. Chronic pain with spinal nerve ligation similarly increases prefrontal NE content and produces attentional deficits [127], while prolonged neuropathic pain activates LC-ACC projections and induces depressive-like behavior in the forced swim test (FST), which can be rescued by α1- and α2-AR antagonism [128]. Modest (~30%) neurotoxin-induced loss of LC neurons in mice promotes LC hyperactivity in surviving cells and depression-like behavior in the FST, while ablation of most (~70%) LC neurons does not [129]. Chronic administration of antidepressant drugs reduces LC activity in rodents, further implicating excessive NE transmission in depression [130].

Therefore, evidence from humans and animal models implicates LC hyperactivity in depression, with heightened LC-NE drive altering cortical and stress-responsive networks to promote depressive-like states.

Agitation, aggression and compulsivity

Agitation is a psychiatric syndrome encompassing irritability, aggression, restlessness, and repetitive motor behaviors affecting ~60% of MCI patients, with verbal and physical non-aggressive behaviors reported in 50 and 24%, respectively [131, 132].

LC-NE signaling is intimately involved in aggression and threat assessment. Mice with short attack latencies during agonistic encounters exhibit robust LC activation [133]. In the resident-intruder paradigm, Dbh-/- mice show minimal territorial aggression toward conspecifics and instead display defensive behaviors, highlighting the necessity of NE for offensive aggression [134]. Stress augments this system; chronic restraint induces fighting and elevates serum DBH and hypothalamic TH levels [135]. Pharmacological manipulations with MAO or reuptake inhibition increase aggression via α2- and β2-AR modulation [136–139]. Conversely, β-AR antagonism with propranolol or pindolol attenuates aggression across models, including isolation and footshock paradigms [140–143]. α2-AR modulation is more complex. Clonidine (α2 agonist) induces aggression, potentially via both postsynaptic α1 or α2-AR engagement depending on the administered dose [144, 145], while α2 antagonists have species- and dose-dependent effects, producing bell-shaped dose-response curves in rats but suppressing aggression in mice [146, 147].

Agitation can also manifest as inappropriate, repetitive coping behaviors, paralleling compulsive symptoms in obsessive-compulsive disorder, autism spectrum disorder, and attention deficit and hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). In rodents, tasks such as nestlet shredding (NS) and marble burying (MB) involve stress-induced repetitive coping behaviors, including shredding, grooming, digging, and burying [148]. NE-deficient mice show marked reductions in NS and MB, which are rescued by administration of the NE precursor L-3,4-dihydroxyphenylserine [149]. Pharmacological studies indicate that NS is β-AR-mediated, while MB involves α1-AR signaling [149]. The ACC emerges as a key region again, as NE-deficient mice show blunted c-fos induction during these tasks [149]. Other stress-related paradigms reinforce this link: rodents bury aversive shock probes in an NE-dependent fashion [150, 151], while predator odor evokes defensive digging and burying in control mice but not in mice with genetic or pharmacological suppression of DBH, which instead display excessive auto-grooming in response to threat [152, 153]. Perseverative errors on spatial tasks such as the 8-arm radial maze also implicate LC-ACC projections, as chemogenetic inhibition increases repetitive responding, suggesting impaired error correction [154].

In sum, noradrenergic signaling influences agitation by regulating both aggression and repetitive coping behaviors. Rodent models suggest that manipulating specific noradrenergic receptors and corticolimbic circuitry can shape these phenotypes.

Social behavior

Social dysfunction, including inappropriateness and withdrawal, is increasingly recognized as a component of MBI, with the elderly particularly vulnerable to known risk factors for AD, such as social isolation and loneliness [155]. Animal studies provide mechanistic insights into how LC-NE circuitry may regulate social behaviors relevant to these phenotypes.

NE is essential for social recognition memory, a process critical for appropriate interactions. In social discrimination tasks, NE depletion or genetic deletion of DBH abolishes the ability to distinguish familiar from novel conspecifics, whereas restoring NE rescues this performance [134, 156]. NE release in the mPFC further supports social recognition memory consolidation through β-AR/β-arrestin biased pathways, with local β-AR antagonism impairing consolidation and selective activation of the β-arrestin pathway restoring it [157].

In contrast, maladaptive social behavior is also linked with LC-NE hyperactivity. Chronic social isolation induces neophobia, associated with increased NE transmission, and accompanied by downregulation of δ-opioid receptor and upregulation of κ-opioid receptor in the LC [158]. Social stress further elevates LC activity and hypervigilant states, accompanied by heightened neuroimmune signaling, both of which are reversed by microglial modulation [159]. These findings suggest that excessive LC-NE signaling biases behavior toward threat sensitivity and social avoidance, phenotypes consistent with MBI.

Noradrenergic signaling within the olfactory and limbic circuits also contributes to social processing. Lesioning NE terminals in the OB impairs recognition and appropriate behavioral responses [160]. LC projections to the BLA and medial MPOA further integrate social and emotional cues, though the precise circuitry remains to be mapped [161, 162].

Collectively, these findings suggest that both loss of noradrenergic signaling and excessive LC activation can disrupt social behaviors, with hyperactivity states promoting social avoidance and inappropriate behavioral responses relevant to MBI.

Appetite

Appetite and metabolic disturbances are common but underappreciated features of AD, with patients frequently exhibiting altered hunger, weight loss, and malnutrition, which may precede cognitive decline in AD [163–165]. Although direct evidence connecting noradrenergic signaling to appetite dysregulation in AD rodent models is limited, studies conducted in normative contexts shed some light on its role in feeding.

Endogenous LC-NE activity dynamically tracks feeding state, with elevated activity during food-seeking and suppression as animals become sated [166]. Sensory-evoked LC responses are similarly reduced in fed compared to fasted states, reflecting modulation by internal energy balance. In terms of circuitry, activation of LC projections to the lateral hypothalamus (LH) attenuates feeding, likely through anxiogenic and heightened arousal effects, mirroring responses to acute stress [166]. Fear-induced cessation of feeding also occurs via co-release of NE and glutamate from a subset of LC neurons, which drives parabrachial nucleus (PBN) activation and endocannabinoid-dependent long-term depression of CeA synapses [167].

Importantly, the effects of LC-NE signaling on feeding are context- and circuit-dependent, leading to heterogeneous appetite-related behaviors. Under chronic stress conditions, LC-NE signaling promotes maladaptive feeding responses. Rodents exhibit compulsive food-seeking despite threat cues, a phenotype that depends on NE elevation in the mPFC [168]. Noradrenergic signaling in the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus also regulates appetite, with α1/β-AR activation suppressing and α2-AR agonists promoting feeding, respectively [169]. Additional neuromodulators act directly on the LC, with ghrelin, the orexinergic hormone, driving feeding and glucagon-like peptide-1 signaling inducing satiety-associated malaise and decreased food intake [170–172].

These circuits may influence appetite disturbances in AD, with increased NE content exaggerating satiety and stress responses. Increased LC-NE signaling, particularly under stress, may suppress feeding, while disruptions in noradrenergic modulation across hypothalamic and cortical circuits may contribute to maladaptive eating behaviors across disease stages.

Summary

Collectively, LC projections to subcortical and cortical regions regulate behavioral domains disrupted in MBI, with sleep disturbances and appetite dysregulation (Fig. 3). Noradrenergic biomarkers for assessing LC activity state

Accumulating evidence implicates compensatory LC-NE hyperactivity as a key contributor to MBI-related phenotypes. Noradrenergic biomarkers hold translational potential for diagnosis and clinical trial stratification, though most approaches provide indirect indices of LC activity. Neuroimaging, including ultra-high-field MRI, NM-MRI, PET-based tracers, and biochemical assays measuring CSF NE and its metabolites, has already provided critical insights into the detection of LC function in vivo, as outlined in the “Clinical evidence of LC-NE contributions to neuropsychiatric symptoms in AD” section. Over the past decade, further advances have enabled non-invasive monitoring of LC functional capacity in aging, although these approaches remain underexplored in relation to NPS heterogeneity.

Functional MRI (fMRI) provides an indirect measure of LC activity via blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signals, which reflect changes in regional blood flow and metabolism. Task-based fMRI can evaluate LC engagement and its functional connectivity (FC) with various brain networks. For example, reversal reinforcement learning task using emotional and task-related cues elicited higher LC activation in older compared to younger adults, suggesting greater LC engagement to support task performance [173]. LC BOLD signal also increases during novelty presentation, accompanied by enhanced FC between amygdala and hippocampus [174]. Notably, reduced novelty-related LC activation is associated with higher levels of Aβ accumulation [174].

Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) is a structural imaging approach that estimates axonal fiber integrity using fractional anisotropy (FA), reflecting directional water diffusion along white matter tracts. FA within the LC is higher in older adults compared to younger individuals, whereas the opposite pattern is observed in the ascending noradrenergic bundle, suggesting age-related alterations in LC microstructure and its projection pathways [175, 176]. These differences have been proposed to reflect local inflammation or increased tonic activity, which can expand neuronal volume [175]. Similarly, reduced mean and radial diffusivity within the thalamic noradrenergic projections of the central tegmental tract is associated with poorer delayed recall performance [177]. Importantly, DTI does not directly measure LC activity, but it provides valuable insight into the structural integrity of the noradrenergic ascending projections, which are known to degenerate prior to LC cell body loss in AD [22, 55].

Pupillometry remains underexplored in the context of NPS, particularly within the MBI framework, yet has emerged as a non-invasive proxy of LC activity. LC stimulation induces pupil dilation in animal models, and pupil dynamics closely track LC activity across species, including nonhuman primates and humans [178]. Pupil-linked networks overlap with salience-related brain regions, including the LC, with FC declining with healthy aging [179]. Task-evoked pupil dilation increases with age and is further exaggerated in amnestic MCI compared to cognitively intact controls; this heightened response is associated with greater LC engagement and recruitment of temporal structures on fMRI, suggesting LC elevations might be required to sustain task performance [173, 180]. Contrastingly, AD patients with diminished LC integrity and peak α frequency, an EEG marker of tonic arousal, exhibit blunted pupil responses to salient stimuli, consistent with impaired LC modulation [181].

Peripheral biomarkers also offer promising avenues. Salivary α-amylase (sAA), an enzyme released in response to sympathetic activation and regulated through β-AR pathways, increases with LC activation due to heightened sympathetic outflow and peripheral NE release [182]. Acute psychosocial stress elevates sAA reactivity, mirroring NE infusion effects in hypertensive patients [183]. Moreover, administration of the selective NE reuptake inhibitor, atomoxetine, increases sAA secretion, with positive correlations to salivary cortisol, highlighting sAA as a non-invasive, scalable marker of central LC-NE activity [184].

These emerging biomarkers, spanning structural and functional neuroimaging to physiological and peripheral measures, offer a multimodal method to track LC function in vivo. Real-world heterogeneity, including comorbidities and medication use, may further complicate interpretations. We thus propose that applying these modalities in asymptomatic and MBI populations will enable delineation of LC function across disease stages and clarify its relationship to specific NPS domains. In parallel, the development of next-generation LC biomarkers, including approaches to image tau within the LC and directly assess LC activity in vivo, will enhance their translational utility for diagnosis and stratification in clinical trials.

Modulating LC-NE transmission for therapeutics

The FDA-approved anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies, lecanemab and donanemab, have demonstrated modest slowing of cognitive decline (27 and 38.6%, respectively), corresponding to estimated delays in disease progression of ~6 months and ~4.4-7.5 months [185, 186]. Although long-term efficacy data remain to be established, amyloid-related imaging abnormalities, characterized by microhemorrhages, edema, and whole brain volumetric changes, pose significant safety concerns that often limit treatment adherence [187]. High treatment costs and the need for advanced diagnostic testing further hinder widespread adoption. There is also no evidence that these therapies alleviate AD-related NPS. These limitations underscore the urgent need for biomarker-driven treatment strategies that integrate adjuvant therapies with DMTs. Noradrenergic-based interventions represent a potential avenue in this context, though current evidence remains largely hypothesis-generating. Based on the clinical and animal studies presented in this review, we propose that LC-NE dysfunction follows a stage-dependent trajectory, with early AD phases characterized by compensatory hyperactivity that contributes to NPS, and later stages marked by NE deficiency that worsens cognitive decline [22]. Accordingly, therapeutic strategies may need to be tailored to baseline LC state, with interventions aimed at either reducing excessive tonic activity or augmenting NE transmission based on biomarker-guided stratification.

From a pharmacological perspective, selective NE (atomoxetine, FDA-approved for ADHD) or dual NE/serotonin (duloxetine, FDA-approved for affective disorders such as MDD, generalized anxiety disorder, and neuropathic pain) reuptake inhibitors have been investigated for repurposing in AD. Because NET inhibitors increase extracellular somatodendritic NE levels, they are thought to suppress LC hyperactivity by decreasing tonic LC firing via α2-AR autoreceptor activation, while preserving phasic responses and increasing the phasic-to-tonic ratio, thereby enhancing signal-to-noise [188]. In a pivotal phase IIb, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical study, atomoxetine increased CSF NE levels in patients with MCI, demonstrating robust target engagement [189]. Atomoxetine treatment was associated with reductions in AD biomarkers, including CSF total tau and pTau181, but not Aβ42, with improvements in CSF metabolic, synaptic, and inflammatory protein signatures, increased serum brain-derived neurotrophic factor levels, and enhanced temporal lobe metabolism and inter-network connectivity between the hippocampus and insula. Atomoxetine had no effect on the rate of cognitive decline in this study, as expected for the short (6-month) treatment duration [189]. Because this particular cohort exhibited few NPS at baseline or trial end, the ability of atomoxetine to alleviate these symptoms remains unknown, but it did not trigger or exacerbate them [189]. An earlier study reported that atomoxetine significantly improved cognition in a subgroup of AD participants with less impairment [190] and in a Parkinson’s disease (PD) cohort, patients with low LC integrity and impulse control dysfunction demonstrated behavioral improvement following atomoxetine treatment [191]. Interestingly, duloxetine improved verbal learning and recall tests in patients with MDD in a double-blind placebo-controlled trial, but did not affect global cognition as measured by the Mini-Mental State Examination, a common endpoint in AD trials [192]. Whether these effects translate to clinical benefit in biomarker-defined AD populations remains to be determined.

Other noradrenergic agents have shown potential for modulating LC-NE transmission, but findings remain inconsistent due to limited sample sizes and lack of systematic randomized controlled investigations in the context of neurodegeneration. Administration of an α1- (prazosin) or β-AR (propranolol) antagonist reduced agitation and aggression in probable AD patients with minimal adverse effects [193, 194]. Prazosin improved nighttime PTSD symptoms (particularly nightmares) and NREM and REM sleep quality, while propranolol marginally improved emotional reactivity and physiologic responses in chronic PTSD, albeit in a single dose study [195, 196]. Results have been mixed for α2-AR agonists; clonidine compromised sustained attention but showed beneficial effects on impulsivity and working memory in PD [197, 198]. Case reports also describe low-dose guanfacine improving disruptive behavioral symptoms in dementia [199]. Additionally, low doses of dexmedetomidine significantly decreased the incidence of delirium in ICU patients, suggesting its potential utility for suppressing hyperactive behaviors [200].

Non-pharmacological strategies can also modulate LC activity and impact cognitive reserve. Novelty exposure, which drives phasic LC responses, is linked to lower tau deposition in the medial temporal lobe and memory improvements [201, 202]. Exercise also increases endogenous NE release, as measured by sAA, and improves memory in older adults and MCI [203]. Finally, transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS) has gained popularity as a non-invasive tool. It stimulates the auricular branch of the vagus nerve, which projects to the nucleus tractus solitarius and engages the LC [204]. taVNS reliably induces pupil dilation, increases sAA levels, enhances LC resting-state FC, and improves accuracy during perceptual decision-making, demonstrating target engagement [205]. Pilot clinical studies in MCI and mild AD report improvements in working memory, language, and attentional domains, showcasing its translational feasibility [206]. Mechanistically, taVNS is hypothesized to bias LC output toward phasic, task-relevant firing, which increases pupil dilation while dampening tonic hyperactivity, improving signal-to-noise ratio [206]. These findings position taVNS as a candidate adjuvant intervention for early modulation of LC-NE hyperactivity.

Future research should prioritize biomarker-guided clinical trials to determine how LC-NE-targeted therapies influence NPS across disease stages. Key questions remain regarding how these therapies should be tailored to specific stages (i.e., targeting LC hyperactivity in early phases and noradrenergic deficiency in later stages). A critical gap is understanding how different pharmacological agents, including NET inhibitors, β-blockers, and α2 agonists, differentially modulate tonic versus phasic LC activity in humans, and whether baseline LC biomarkers can be used to stratify patients and predict treatment response. There is also a need to determine whether NE-targeting interventions improve NPS independently of cognitive outcomes, and to establish their long-term safety and tolerability in older adults with multimorbidity. Importantly, well-powered, biomarker-stratified clinical trials that incorporate MBI-based diagnosis will be essential to validate the efficacy and reproducibility of these approaches.

Conclusions

The future of the field is contingent on well-designed longitudinal studies that delineate how LC structure and function change through the course of healthy aging and maladaptive conditions. This review highlights noradrenergic circuits implicated in the emergence of NPS that closely parallel behavioral phenotypes associated with LC-NE hyperactivity. Dissecting these circuits in AD-relevant animal models will be essential to determine how hyperactivity perturbs affective and cognitive domains. Biomarker-guided modulation of LC-NE transmission and specific circuits, whether through pharmacological or neuromodulatory interventions, holds substantial potential as an adjuvant to DMTs. This strategy may not only reduce NPS burden but also slow cognitive decline in AD.
