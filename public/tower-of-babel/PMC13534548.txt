Mapping Mpox vaccine hesitancy using an integrated machine learning and structural equation modeling approach
Authors: Nahid Sultana, Mohammad Anamul Haque
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study employs an integrated computational approach to investigate Mpox vaccine intention in Bangladesh as Mpox immunisation strategies require a thorough understanding of behavioural determinants. Data from 405 participants were analysed using a structured framework that included frequentist statistics to examine demographic relationships, t-distributed Stochastic Neighbour Embedding (t-SNE) for unsupervised grouping, and structural equation modelling (SEM) to confirm underlying behavioural trends. About 77% of people were willing to get vaccinated, and this was mainly influenced by their age (p = 0.002), education (p = 0.014), and occupation (p < 0.001). The t-SNE analysis revealed two patterns of reluctance—one linked to practical barriers (mainly younger people with limited education), and another linked to more neutral or skeptical attitudes. Structural equation modelling (SEM) confirmed that perceived vaccine efficacy (vaccine knowledge) was the primary driver of intention (β = 0.346, p = 0.005). Social-structural factors significantly shaped this intent; notably, living without family was a positive predictor ( β = 0.185, p < 0.001), whereas 96% of the hesitant group resided in traditional family units. Furthermore, a strong correlation between personal intent and community advocacy (r = 0.58) suggests that prosocial motivation is a key driver in this cohort. Our results show that vaccine reluctance is not uniform. In South Asia, public health interventions need to move away from generic messages and towards customised approaches that target the particular “efficacy gaps” and structural obstacles found by this multi-method mapping.

Introduction

Mpox (previously known as monkeypox) is an infectious disease caused by the MPXV virus (an enveloped double-stranded DNA virus) that usually causes a rash and flu-like symptoms. There are 2 major types of MPXV termed as clade I and II. Clade I (previously known as Central African or Congo basin clade) is historically associated with higher virulence in the Congo Basin, and clade II (previously known as West African clade) drove the 2022 global outbreak1. The incubation period (the time between contact with the person with mpox and when the first symptoms appear) for Mpox is between 5 and 21 days. Historically, Mpox was known to have been occasionally passed on from infected animals to humans, with humans sometimes passing it on to other people that they had close contact with2. Without treatment, the majority of persons who contract Mpox will recover in a few weeks. However, some people may get serious sickness. A medical professional’s evaluation and specialised laboratory testing are necessary for a confirmed diagnosis of Mpox.

In 1970, the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) reported the first human case. Since then, several African nations have reported cases of the virus, and more recently, it has spread to several nations worldwide including cases in the subcontinent2. The 2003 epidemic in the USA also raised concerns about global health security. In 2022, the World Health Organisation (WHO) reported an increase in Mpox cases across seven African countries, with approximately 1400 cases (suspected 1392 and confirmed 44), a figure higher than the previous two decades3,4. Furthermore, 18,844 cases were reported in 29 European countries that same year. Since then, the outbreak has persisted, with over 94,000 cases confirmed worldwide5,6. Unlike previous outbreaks, the current epidemiological profile has shown a disproportionate impact on specific social networks, particularly men who have sex with men (MSM) and individuals living in high-density urban contexts7,8. On August 14, 2024, the WHO declared Mpox a public health emergency of international concern (PHEIC) due to a notable increase in Clade I cases in Central Africa and its spread to previously unaffected areas3.

The WHO’s global strategic preparation and response plan (SPRP) for 2025 is in line with Bangladesh’s national response. The incorporation of Mpox surveillance into current infectious disease networks, such as those for COVID-19 and influenza, is highlighted9. Bangladesh is still at high risk even though no Mpox cases have been found there as it is surrounded by India, where several cases have been found recently10. While therapeutic treatments are still limited, vaccination—especially the use of smallpox-based vaccines like JYNNEOS—has emerged as the primary tactic for limiting outbreaks11. However, public acceptance is a prerequisite for the success of any immunisation program, and it is currently under threat due to an increase in vaccination hesitancy worldwide12. The WHO has identified vaccine hesitancy as a public health concern13. The danger of illness and death from diseases that vaccines may be able to prevent is increased by vaccine rejection rates due to fear of vaccinations. Improving people’s perceptions of their capacity to control their vaccination behaviour is crucial, according to earlier studies14.

The WHO listed vaccine hesitancy as one of the ten threats to global health in 2019 because to its association with lower vaccination rates and an increase in the frequency of vaccine-preventable disease outbreaks across the globe15. Both internal and external factors, including as sociodemographic differences, the rate of vaccine development, and the availability of opportunities, information, resources, willpower, and skills, might have an impact on vaccine hesitancy16–18. However, for vaccinations to be implemented in any nation, there must be positive attitudes or intentions regarding vaccination as well as positive perceptions among potential recipients19. Previous research has shown that improved cognitive attitude, as opposed to emotional attitude, influences vaccination intention16.

Conventional modelling techniques, such as exploratory analysis10,20–23, univariate or multivariate logistic regression14,17,18,24–26, and structural equation model (SEM) based on elements of the theory of planned behaviour (TPB)27 can be seen with recent studies on the Mpox vaccine. TPB based current literature on Mpox vaccine intention is predominantly anchored in “clinical risk-centric” models from Western contexts explaining vaccine intention through individualistic variables: perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, and self-efficacy28,29. However, these conventional frameworks often fail to account for the unique socio-structural determinants prevalent in South Asian collectivist societies, where, health decisions are rarely individualistic; they are mediated by deep-seated structures such as family living arrangements and community-based prosocial advocacy. Furthermore, existing studies in Bangladesh30,31 have relied on frequentist statistics (multivariate regression) which treat the population as a monolith. Such methods can identify “who” is hesitant but lack the granularity to map the “hidden islands” of reluctance or distinguish between different “phenotypes” of hesitancy.

We aimed to go beyond the structure-based clinical risk questionnaire that solely focused on MSM, even if SEM was employed in previous research. To put it another way, we concentrate on social-structural factors that are specific to the South Asian setting. Even though research on Mpox vaccine intention is increasing, there is still limited understanding of how collectivist social systems in South Asia influence decision-making beyond individual health risks. Furthermore, regional studies that mainly rely on linear models may overlook the complex and non-linear patterns of vaccine hesitancy.

This study addresses these gaps by performing a sophisticated secondary analysis of a cohort of adult males in Bangladesh by moving beyond conventional statistics with a complementary dual-method strategy that utilizes unsupervised machine learning for pattern discovery followed by SEM for path verification. Specifically, this research seeks to achieve the following objectives:

First, we transition from a clinical risk-centric model to a social-structural framework, investigating how deeply embedded factors like family living structures and community advocacy roles influence health decisions in the South Asian context.Second, by utilizing t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE), we move beyond linear predictions to reveal a bifurcated structure of hesitancy, distinguishing between structural educational barriers and “soft” attitudinal skepticism.Finally, This is the first study to specifically investigate the “Family Paradox” in South Asia—where living with family, traditionally a support structure, may actually serve as a barrier to health-seeking behavior in stigmatized infectious diseases like Mpox.

Literature review and theoretical framework

The health belief model (HBM) and Mpox perception

The main framework for comprehending vaccine uptake during infectious disease epidemics is the Health Belief Model (HBM) which implies that “perception” is a combination of perceived severity (fear of the disease) and perceived susceptibility (feeling at risk) rather than a single entity32. Clinical risk, particularly in close or high-risk social groups, appears to be the primary factor influencing these perceptions for Mpox, according to recent studies conducted in Western contexts28,29, however, in South Asia, these “perceived barriers”—a fundamental HBM construct—are frequently structural (e.g., education, family influence) rather than merely clinical. Our application of a social-structural framework is justified by this.

Theory of planned behavior (TPB) and the “Intention” gap

The HBM aids in explaining why people think and feel in particular ways, while the TPB demonstrates how thoughts and feelings result in intentions33. Individual vaccine preferences, the impact of family and community, and perceived behavioural control—that is, how simple it is to receive the vaccination—are all highlighted by TPB. Although “who” is hesitant has been identified in the current work on Mpox in Bangladesh30,31, it has not mapped how subjective standards (such as living with family) truly influence the path to intention. By measuring these particular “prosocial” pathways using SEM, our study closes this gap.

The necessity of non-linear modeling (t-SNE)

A critical limitation in existing Mpox literature is the reliance on linear “frequentist” models34,35. These studies assume that vaccine intention increases at a steady rate as knowledge increases. However, behavioral data is often “noisy” and “clustered.” By introducing t-SNE, we move beyond the “monolithic” view of the population used in prior studies30,31, allowing for the discovery of distinct sub-phenotypes of hesitancy that linear regression simply cannot see.

Methodology

Study design and data collection

This study performed a secondary analysis of a cross-sectional dataset originally collected by Islam et al. (2023). The survey was conducted among the Bangladeshi adult males to assess Mpox vaccine intentions using Google survey tools from September 1, 2022, to November 30, 2022. Data were collected covering three primary domains: (1) socio-demographic characteristics, (2) perceived impact of COVID-19, and (3) knowledge and attitudes toward Mpox vaccination where out of total 419 respondent, 408 response were collected due to inaccurate information from the 11 excluded response. Adult males were in the age range from 18 to 45 years as this age is predominantly vulnerable to the Mpox virus which leads to the exclusion of females of all ages7,8,36. Participation in the survey was completely optional as well as those who participated in the survey gave their electronic approval.

While the primary data was previously collected, we validated the sampling adequacy for this secondary analysis. The study significantly exceeds the ‘10 cases per parameter’ requirement, ensuring stable and reproducible structural estimates37.

Data pre-processing and management

Both data cleaning and analysis were performed using R (version 4.3.1) where text items that were inconsistent were standardised using the stringr package. Some catagorical variables were collapsed into meaningful levels using forcats to ensure statistical power, specifically, education was divided into three levels (below primary, below graduate, and graduate & Above), while occupation was divided into student, (including service and self-employed), and others (including unemployed).

Participant flow

The data cleaning process resulted in two analytic samples tailored to the requirements of each statistical method. Starting with a screened dataset of 408 records, 3 cases were excluded due to sparse categorical distribution (2 homosexual, 1 bisexual) to prevent quasi-complete separation in the structural model, leaving N = 405 for the SEM (using Full Information Maximum Likelihood). For the t-SNE manifold learning, a complete-case analysis was performed to satisfy the requirement for a full distance matrix. After excluding 46 records with missing attitudinal values, a final sub-cohort of N = 359 was utilized for the exploratory clustering analysis. The nature of the missing data was determined to be Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) which was assessed by a sensitivity analysis (Chi-square and independent t-tests). The excluded (n = 46) and included (n = 359) cohorts across key baseline demographics (age, education, and occupation) was compared and no statistically significant differences were observed (p > 0.05), confirming that the exclusion of these incomplete records did not introduce systematic demographic bias into the downstream manifold visualization.

Descriptive and bivariate analysis

The R gtsummary package was used to summarise participant characteristics. Frequencies and percentages were used to display categorical variables with Fisher’s Exact Test to evaluate bivariate relationships between demographic characteristics and vaccine intention (classified as Hesitant, Neutral, or Willing). Fisher’s Exact Test was performed with Monte Carlo simulation (B = 5000) to account for small cell counts.

Study variables

During the survey, a 5-point Likert scale (range from 0 to 4) was used to measure vaccination intention which is the main outcome variable for the study. For reliable statistical modeling with group-based visualization, respondent replies were divided into three ordinal groups named as Willing (scores 3 and 4; agree to vaccination), Neutral (scoring 2), and Hesitant (scores 0 and 1; disagree to vaccination). This classification worked as the basis for subsequent analyses. For the latent construct indicators, this classification was used in the structural equation model as well as to distinguish participant subgroups during t-SNE dimensionality reduction.

Beside response variable vaccine intention, other variables such as age, marriage status, gender, occupation, sexual orientation, body mass index (BMI), living situation, smoking habit, details about a sexual partner, and how the COVID-19 epidemic affected their social and personal lives were considered as sociodemographic factors for the analysis.

We utilized a hybrid computational approach to overcome the limitations of frequentist monolithic modeling. First, t-SNE was employed as an unsupervised machine learning tool to uncover high-dimensional, non-linear groups within the hesitant cohort—identifying ‘phenotypes’ of resistance that are invisible to standard regression. Second, SEM was constructed based on established public health theories (e.g., Knowledge-Attitude-Practice framework).

t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE)

Participants were categorized into three distinct groups—Hesitant, Neutral, and Willing—based on their calculated mean scores from the vaccine intention scale. To visualize the multidimensional separation of these groups across demographic and attitudinal variables, we employed t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) as a non-linear dimensionality reduction technique. To explore high-dimensional patterns in vaccine hesitancy without prior bias, t-SNE was used via the Rtsne package. Before applying t-SNE, nominal variables were transformed into binary vectors. After testing multiple perplexity values (10, 30, 50, 100), the optimal visualization was selected based on the average silhouette width (ASW), which measures cohesion (within-group similarity) and separation (distance to nearest group). 95% confidence ellipses were used to demarcate the boundaries of intent groups in the 2D reduced-dimension space.

In order to account for the non-linear, high-dimensional nature of vaccination reluctance, we intentionally chose t-SNE over linear alternatives like PCA. Additionally, t-SNE preserves the local neighbourhood structures whereas PCA maximises global variance. This was essential for our study to go beyond a “monolithic” image of the population and reveal the split sub-groups of hesitancy that are frequently hidden by the global linear averages of frequentist models, such as the structural vs. attitudinal islands.

Relational mapping (Spearman correlation)

Before implementing structural equation modeling, a spearman’s rank correlation matrix was computed to screen for multicollinearity among variables as well as to identify significant bivariate drivers of vaccine intention. The objective of this step was to ensure that only variables with theoretical and statistical relevance included in the final path model.

Structural equation modeling (SEM)

To verify the mechanisms influencing the desire for vaccines, this study performed structural equation modeling (SEM) using the lavaan package in R.

The measurement model (Latent variables)

Using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) inside the SEM framework, a latent construct for vaccine intention was constructed where the participant’s self-reported agreement to receive the monkeypox vaccination, their willingness to receive vaccination, and their desire to encourage others to get vaccinated were the three observable variables that was used for this construct. To verify the construct’s convergent validity and reliability, factor loadings were evaluated.

The structural model (The hypothesized paths)

Regressions were estimated to test the influence of various variables on vaccine intention. Study variables such as knowledge for vaccination and key demographic characteristics such as living status was used to examine the direct paths between demographic/attitudinal predictors and the latent intention construct. The correlation analysis was used to detect the significant demographic/attitudinal predictors.

Estimation

For the estimation of SEM, maximum likelihood with robust standard errors (MLR) was used, given the non-normal distribution with ordinal nature of the data. Standard indices such as comparative fit index (CFI > 0.90), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI > 0.90), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA < 0.08), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) were used to assess global model fit.

Results

Demographic and socioeconomic overview

Table 1 presents the baseline demographic and socioeconomic characteristics of the study population, stratified by vaccine intention.

Age trends

A total of 405 participants were divided into various group. The grouping was done according to whether or not they planned to have the Mpox vaccine. It was discovered that 311 (77.0%) of the participants were willing to get vaccinated, 25 (6.1%) were apprehensive (hesitant), and 69 (16.9%) were neutral. Participants age was found to be a key determinant (p = 0.002) for vaccine intention where the results indicate that the vaccine intention with neutral group was dominated by youngest age category (18–25 years old, 74%), whilst the willing group displayed a more evenly distributed distribution between the 18–25 (46%) and 26–35 (41%) age ranges.

Socioeconomic and educational drivers

Vaccine intention was found to be associated with socioeconomic and educational factors, such as work level and educational achievement as well. Participants who have educational attainment with a master degree or above were found to more likely to be neutral (57%) or willing (39%) group in compared to the hesitant group (24%; p = 0.014). Moreover, vaccine intention and work level was not found to be evenly distributed (p < 0.001). 60% of respondents who were willing to get vaccinated were found to be employed or in business, whereas 52% of respondents were students who found to be neutral for vaccine intention.

Lifestyle and social factors

Meaningful correlations were seen between vaccine intention and social and lifestyle factors where living with family comprised of for 96% of the hesitant group. On the other hand, living without family was more prevalent in the willing (26%) and neutral (22%) groups (p = 0.025). Furthermore, a strong correlation also observed between intention and smoking practices (p = 0.034). Results indicate that only 20% of the hesitant group self-reported as smoker, whereas this percentage was higher (35%) for the willing group. Another factor that was seen to be associated with intention was the perceived impact of COVID-19, with 24% of the willing group and 41% of neutral respondents indicating a high impact (p = 0.016).

Non-linear grouping of vaccine intention (t-SNE)

This study employed t-SNE to understand and visualize the high-dimensional relationship between participants demographic factors, COVID-19 impact, and Mpox attitudes. For the purpose of the t-SNE analysis, the explicit “Hesitant” group and the “Neutral” group were combined because they occupied the same “spatial coordinates” in the t-SNE map. This sub-analysis was performed on the N = 359 complete-case sample. While Table 1 reports raw frequency counts using a strict definition of hesitancy (scores 0–1), the t-SNE analysis employed a broader ‘Reluctant Phenotype’ classification (scores 0–2). This adjustment was made for two reasons: (1) to ensure sufficient statistical power for the unsupervised grouping algorithm, and (2) because preliminary manifold mapping indicated that ‘Neutral’ respondents shared a high-dimensional distance matrix significantly more similar to ‘Hesitant’ respondents than to ‘Willing’ ones.

Different perplexity value was tested using the data based on visual inspection (Fig. 2 (a)) and average silhouette width (ASW)(Table 2). The highest ASW was found to be 0.12 with perplexity 30. The t-SNE algorithm was run for 1,000 iterations to ensure the visualization reached a stable global minimum, clearly separating the hesitant and willing cohort. A learning rate of 200 was applied, allowing for optimal step-size updates that preserved the local structure of the participant responses. The t-SNE analysis (Fig. 2 (b)) revealed distinct spatial grouping based on vaccine intention, where the reluctant group (orange) demonstrated unique bifurcated grouping. The use of 95% confidence ellipses confirmed that the reluctance group occupies a different multivariate space compared to those with high vaccine intention.

The t-SNE sub-group analysis (Orange islands)

In particular, the reluctant cohort of participants was found to be separated into two distinct “islands” or sub-groups (Fig. 2 (b)). While the unsupervised t-SNE analysis identified two spatial orientations within the reluctant phenotype, the ASW = 0.12 indicates a degree of group overlap. Therefore, these subgroups should be interpreted as emergent patterns rather than distinct, mutually exclusive segments. The main reluctant group representing individuals whose reluctancy is driven by common attitudinal factors shared with the neutral group. Another isolated sub-group was identified which indicate a subset of the population whose demographic profiles or specific concerns (e.g., specific combinations of occupation, residence) are mathematically distinct from the rest of the cohort. Table 3 shows the demographic comparison of the two reluctant subgroups. The “right group” is dominated by students (71.1%) who are younger and have slightly lower knowledge scores (2.33). The “left group” was characterized by a higher proportion of older professionals. Interestingly, they have higher knowledge scores (2.53) but are still hesitant.

To explore the underlying heterogeneity within the reluctant phenotype (n = 90), the cohort was partitioned into two equal subgroups (n = 45 each) based on a median split of their horizontal visual coordinates. Rather than treating this spatial boundary as a formal clustering algorithm, these subgroups were designated post-hoc to systematically profile and characterize the clear demographic and literacy divergence visible across the horizontal axis of the projection. The first group (projected on the left) showed far greater spatial dispersion, indicating a demographically diverse, professional-skewed cohort confronting complex structural obstacles. On the other hand, the second group (projected on the right) demonstrated a highly concentrated spatial density and close proximity to the neutral cohort (Fig. 2 (b)), reflecting a more homogenous student-skewed profile.

These preliminary findings, specifically the strong attitudinal correlations and the demographic bifurcation of the reluctant group—provide the empirical rationale for the subsequent SEM. The correlation matrix suggests the existence of latent constructs (like ‘Efficacy’ or ‘Altruism’), while the t-SNE results confirm that demographic covariates must be included to account for the distinct profiles of the hesitant sub-populations.

On the other hand, BMI found to be negatively correlated with vaccine intention, suggesting that health-conscious behavioral patterns may be non-linear within this cohort. These findings provide a clear roadmap for the directional paths to be tested in the SEM framework.

Discussion

The study findings offer a thorough mapping of the demographic and behavioural factors in determining what influence the Mpox vaccine intention. Three group of participants were identified labelled as Willing, Neutral, and Hesitant populations with implementing a multi-method approach that combines conventional frequentist statistics, non-linear grouping (t-SNE), and structural equation modelling (SEM).

An important insight revealed by the exploratory correlation analysis. A strong synergy found to be existed between personal vaccine intention and community advocacy (r = 0.44), however, the strongest association for vaccine intention was found with the readiness to encourage others (r = 0.58), suggesting that those willing to vaccinate do not view the action in isolation but as part of a broader community advocacy role. This finding aligns with a recent study in the United Kingdom which identifies that “altruistic concerns” and the desire to protect the community were significant drivers among high-risk groups38. This is further supported by the moderate associations with “preventing outbreaks” (r = 0.41) and “community good” (r = 0.40), indicating that prosocial motivation is a significant driver of acceptance in this cohort. This suggests that in order to make the most of the current positive social momentum, public health message in Bangladesh should change from “Protect Yourself” to “Protect Your Community”.

The study shows that willingness to get vaccinated varies across different social and demographic groups. Participants’ age was found to be a significant determinant (p = 0.002) for vaccine intention; the neutral group was dominated by the youngest category (18–25 years old), while the willing group showed a more mature, evenly distributed distribution between ages 18 and 35. Lifestyle factors also play a big role in whether people plan to get vaccinated. A notable descriptive trend was observed wherein a high proportion of the hesitant cohort lived with family but living without family was more common in the willing and neutral groups (p = 0.025); this phenomenon has been observed in the region’s COVID-19 vaccine research39. This can be termed as “intergenerational scepticism” (the transmission of vaccine concerns from older family members to younger ones) impact. Our findings suggest that in Bangladesh, independent individuals (probably students or young professionals) showed greater self-reliance and a more direct path to vaccine intention (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{\beta\:}$$\end{document} = 0.185), despite some European studies suggesting that family support generally increases health-seeking behaviour40. However, the cross-sectional nature of this study precludes any causal inference regarding the relationship between living status and vaccine hesitancy. It remains unclear whether family dynamics actively influence vaccine decision-making or if hesitant individuals are more likely to reside within family units due to broader socio-demographic factors, such as age or economic dependency. Future longitudinal research is required to disentangle these variables.

The t-SNE analysis indicates that the hesitant group did not show a monolithic construct but a distinctive bifurcated grouping. The hesitant group was divided into two separate islands which implies that hesitancy is not a single entity. In line with research in Sub-Saharan Africa, where educational inequalities continue to be a major barrier to Mpox literacy, group 2 (student-Skewed Demographic) shows demographic barriers to vaccine knowledge due to lower average age and less access to school41. Group 1 (Professional-Skewed Demographic) found to be closer to the neutral cohort but remains hesitant due to lack of trust. Traditional linear regressions would treat all these as a single hesitant group, however, our non-linear approach proves that public health interventions need to be dual-tracked: providing basic education for the “structural” group (Group 1) while addressing specific attitudinal mistrust in the “attitudinal” group (Group 2).

The “efficacy” group found in our initial correlation matrix was confirmed by the SEM where the strongest attitudinal predictor was found to be vaccination knowledge, namely the assumption that the vaccine lowers the risk of infection (β = 0.345, p = <0.001). This is in line with a 2025 meta-analysis42 that found perceived efficacy to be the most accurate measure of Mpox adoption worldwide. Believing that the vaccine reduces the risk of getting sick is the final step toward acceptance even though other social factors like age and living situation also set the stage. This reveals a crucial gap: in order to close the gap between “neutral” and “willing,” public health initiatives must go beyond increasing awareness of Mpox to present concrete evidence of the vaccine’s preventive effectiveness.

These findings suggest that public health efforts should be designed to fit these different groups: for the younger, neutral groups, emphasizing the “community good” and “outbreak prevention”—concepts they already view as synonymous (r = 0.74) may be effective. Increasing educational access for the younger hesitant segment and addressing particular attitudinal scepticism in the more “neutral-adjacent” hesitant subgroup are two strategies needed to address the split hesitant group.

Implications of the study

The findings of this study offer significant implications for understanding health behavior and improving vaccination strategies in South Asia in following ways:

Theoretical implications

Expansion of the health belief model (HBM): This study demonstrates that in collectivist societies, people’s views about vaccines are shaped not only by personal health risks but also by social and demographic factors, therefore, the findings also indicate that family and household dynamics may strongly influence how people receive and understand information about vaccine benefits. Our findings suggest a moderating effect of household dynamics which theoretically indicates that HBM frameworks in non-Western contexts must integrate familial collective efficacy as a core construct, as health decisions are negotiated within a domestic social circle rather than made in isolation.Validation of non-linear manifold learning in public health: This study also shows the value of combining machine learning methods like t-SNE with traditional statistical models such as SEM. The results shows that vaccine reluctancy is not the same for everyone, but instead includes different groups with different demographic characteristics which can help future research move beyond overall averages and better identify hidden patterns in people’s attitudes and behaviors toward vaccination. Methodologically, this study proves that vaccine hesitancy is a multidimensional manifold—bifurcated into “structural reluctance” (knowledge-dependent) and “attitudinal reluctance” (knowledge-independent).

Strengths of the study

The integration of three different analytical layers—confirmatory modelling (SEM), unsupervised machine learning (t-SNE), and frequentist statistics (Fisher’s tests) makes it possible to find hidden sub-structures (the “bifurcated” hesitant groups) that regular regression would overlook in addition to identifying broad patterns. The study goes beyond considering “hesitant” people as a monolith by employing t-SNE. A high degree of granularity for focused public health messages is provided by identifying left group (Group 1: attitudinal/soft reluctance) among higher-educated professionals and a right group (Group 2: structural/educational obstacles) among the student. The use of latent construct in SEM combining intent, expectation, and encouragement offers a more consistent and reliable evaluation of a participant’s actual behavioural disposition than many studies that rely on a single question for vaccine intent. Living with family was found to show a trend associated with increased reluctance (96%), that challenges the western belief that family support always enhances health compliance and provides a unique socio-cultural insight particularly pertinent to the Bangladeshi context.

Limitations of the study

Due to cross-sectional nature of data, the study is unable to conclusively show a long-term causal “path”. Although the SEM analysis found a link between living status and vaccine intention, this study cannot confirm whether living independently directly increases vaccine willingness or whether another factor—such as personality traits—affects both. Future long-term studies are needed to understand how changes in family and living situations influence health-related behaviors over time. This study measured people’s reported intention to get vaccinated rather than their actual vaccination behavior which may cause some participants to overstate their willingness to take the vaccine or encourage others in order to appear more socially responsible, which is known as social desirability bias.

The reliance on a purposive, web-based sample means that the findings may be skewed toward individuals with higher digital literacy and internet access. The high percentage of students and graduates in some groups suggests that participants with weaker literacy or no internet access, who may be at high risk, may be under-represented. In order to appear more prosocial, people may over-report their willingness to “encourage others” when self-reported “intention” is used instead of “actual behaviour” (vaccine uptake). This is known as social desirability bias. The low silhouette width of the t-SNE groups suggests that vaccine reluctance in Bangladesh is a fluid construct with significant demographic overlap, necessitating cautious interpretation of subgroup boundaries. Additionally, while a sensitivity analysis confirmed that the exclusion of 46 records due to missing data did not introduce systematic demographic bias (p > 0.05) and our remaining sample (N = 359) vastly exceeded t-SNE algorithmic requirements, the reliance on complete cases for non-linear manifold mapping remains a minor methodological constraint. Finally, the study’s cross-sectional design limits our ability to determine the directionality of the relationship between living arrangements and health behaviors.

Conclusions

Using the dual lenses of manifold learning (t-SNE) and structural equation modeling (SEM), this study examined the socio-demographic and attitudinal drivers of Mpox vaccine intention in Bangladesh, bridging the gap between psychological determinants and demographic characteristics. Our results indicate a split structure of reluctance that differentiates between structural educational hurdles and attitudinal scepticism, going beyond the conventional “willing versus hesitant” binary. While the t-SNE visualization suggests a potentially bifurcated structure of hesitancy—highlighting distinct pockets of educational barriers and attitudinal skepticism—these patterns require further validation in larger, more diverse cohorts. Contrary to the assumption that family units promote health-seeking behavior, our findings indicate that individuals living within a family structure exhibited significantly higher vaccine hesitancy. While exploratory, this pattern challenges Western-centric models of social support and suggests that in South Asian contexts, household hierarchies may act as a significant filter for health information.

Our analysis indicates that the perceived medical efficacy of the vaccine continues to be the final barrier to mobilisation, even though social identity and living situations set the setting for intention. These findings challenge the suitability of a “one-size-fits-all” approach in Mpox immunisation campaigns for the international scientific community. Rather, interventions need to be customised, addressing particular efficacy issues for individuals in independent living structures while utilising “prosocial advocacy” for younger neutral groups.
