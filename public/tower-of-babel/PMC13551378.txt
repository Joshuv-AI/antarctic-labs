Alkyl Glucosides in Rinse‐Off and Leave‐On Cosmetics Available on the Dutch Market
Authors: Mounir Yaâkoubi, Norbertus A Ipenburg, Anton C de Groot, Thomas Rustemeyer
Published: 2026

Alkyl glucosides (decyl, lauryl, cetearyl, coco‐, others) are nonionic surfactants and emulsifiers produced by the reaction of glucose with fatty alcohols. Their use in cosmetics has increased in recent years. These compounds are considered to have a low irritation potential and favourable ecological properties, as they are derived from renewable resources and are biodegradable. Contact allergy and allergic contact dermatitis caused by alkyl glucosides are increasingly recognized. In routine patch testing, positive reactions are reported in up to 2.4% of patients in North America and up to 1.5% in Europe. The majority of these reactions (> 80%) are currently relevant, most often due to the presence of alkyl glucosides in personal care products. Alkyl glucosides are most commonly found in shampoos and body washes marketed for infants. However, data on their presence in cosmetic products are largely limited to the United States (manuscripts under review). We therefore analysed the occurrence of alkyl glucosides in 650 cosmetic products (350 leave‐on and 300 rinse‐off) available on the Dutch market.

Methods and Results

Ingredient lists of 350 leave‐on and 300 rinse‐off cosmetics were collected from www.bol.com, the largest online retailer in the Netherlands. Details of the selection procedure and data collection can be found in the Supporting Information Materials and Methods in Data S1.

Details of the cosmetics investigated (brands, countries of origin, number of products per brand and per country) are shown in the Supporting Information Tables S1–S3 (350 leave‐on cosmetics) and Supporting Information Tables S4–S6 (300 rinse‐off cosmetics).

The presence of the various alkyl glucosides in the 350 leave‐on cosmetics is shown in Table 1. 24% of sunscreens, 22% of day creams, 17% of night creams, and 14% of eye creams contained at least one alkyl glucoside, whereas only one of 50 lip salves did. The percentage of all products with ≥ 1 glucoside was 16.9%, which increased to 19.3% when lip salves were excluded.

The most frequently identified alkyl glucoside was coco‐glucoside (101 products, 33.7%), followed by decyl glucoside (31 products, 10.3%) and lauryl glucoside (23 products, 7.7%). Few of these products contained caprylyl/capryl glucoside (n = 12), C12‐20 alkyl glucoside (n = 2), and cetearyl glucoside and glyceryl glucoside (each n = 1). Arachidyl glucoside was not present in any product in this category.

Discussion

This study shows that nearly half of the 300 rinse‐off products available in the Netherlands from a major online retailer contain one or more alkyl glucosides. The highest proportions were found in baby shampoo (62%), shower gel (62%), and baby shower gel (56%). By far the most frequently identified alkyl glucoside was coco‐glucoside, present in one‐third of all products, followed by decyl glucoside (10.3%) and lauryl glucoside (7.7%). In leave‐on cosmetics, alkyl glucosides were less common. However, when lip balms, which are fatty products that do not contain surfactants and do not require emulsification are excluded, nearly 20% of leave‐on products still contained one or more alkyl glucosides. In this category, cetearyl glucoside and decyl glucoside were most frequently identified. Cetearyl glucoside is indeed primarily used as an emulsifier in leave‐on formulations (manuscripts under review). The 650 cosmetic products analysed represent a broad range of brands and countries of origin and are available for online purchase in many countries. Our findings may therefore be representative of the situation in other European countries as well.

Since the first report of contact allergy to coco‐glucoside and lauryl glucoside in Belgium in 2003 [1], numerous case reports and case series have clearly established the allergenic potential of alkyl glucosides, particularly in cosmetics. Decyl glucoside was identified as a “hidden” allergen in the UV filter Tinosorb M and was responsible for multiple cases of allergic contact dermatitis [2]. Routine patch testing with decyl glucoside 5% pet. has yielded up to 2.4% positive reactions in North America and up to 1.5% in Europe (manuscripts under review).

In Amsterdam, between 1 November 2019 and 5 January 2026, 4343 consecutive patients were patch tested with decyl glucoside 5% pet. and lauryl glucoside 3% pet. Positive reactions were observed in 3.5% of patients to decyl glucoside and in 3.1% to lauryl glucoside (unpublished observations). Generally speaking, approximately 50% of patients allergic to decyl glucoside also react to lauryl and coco‐glucoside. Among patients reacting to lauryl glucoside, about two‐thirds also showed a positive reaction to decyl glucoside, and many also reacted to coco‐glucoside. Co‐reactions to cetearyl glucoside in decyl‐ and lauryl‐sensitized patients are less frequent (10%–20%). In contrast, at least half of patients sensitized to cetearyl glucoside also react to other alkyl glucosides (manuscripts under review).

This substantial degree of co‐reactivity, possibly reflecting cross‐reactivity, implies that patients sensitized to decyl, lauryl, or cetearyl glucoside (the three alkyl glucosides commercially available for patch testing) should avoid cosmetic products containing any alkyl glucoside, and allergic patients should be instructed accordingly. This recommendation considerably restricts the choice of suitable rinse‐off products. For further information on contact allergy and allergic contact dermatitis caused by alkyl glucosides, we refer to our review articles on this subject (manuscripts under review).
