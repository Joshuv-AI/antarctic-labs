Promoter methylation-associated brain-enriched long noncoding RNAs in glioblastoma: a multi-cohort public epigenomic re-analysis
Authors: Ye Tan, Fengyu Cheng, Pu Li, Jingxuan Xiang, Yuhong Tang, Aidong Chen, Peiying Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundGlioblastoma (GBM) is epigenetically heterogeneous, and previous long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) methylation studies have often begun from tumor-wide screens. We asked whether brain-enriched lncRNAs show promoter methylation-associated suppression in GBM.Research design and methodsBrain-enriched lncRNAs were defined using GENCODE v49 and GTEx/UCSC Xena. Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is one of the most common malignant primary brain tumors in adults and remains associated with poor survival despite maximal safe resection, radiotherapy and temozolomide-based chemotherapy [1–4]. Modern classification increasingly integrates histology with molecular information, which reflects the biological heterogeneity that cannot be captured by morphology alone [1,3].

DNA methylation is central to GBM biology. TCGA and related integrative studies have defined recurrent genomic and epigenomic subgroups, including CpG island methylator phenotype and clinically relevant MGMT promoter methylation [5–8]. More broadly, epigenetic plasticity can shape cancer cell identity and state transitions rather than simply marking downstream consequences of transformation [9].

Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) are often more tissue restricted than protein-coding genes, and the brain is one of the richest organs for lncRNA expression and regulation [10–12]. This makes brain-enriched lncRNAs an attractive entry point for studying organ-of-origin biology in GBM.

Important prior studies have already examined lncRNA methylation in glioma or GBM. Ji et al. performed a genome-wide GBM lncRNA methylation analysis; He et al. linked lncRNA methylation to glioma prognosis; Xu et al. defined epigenetically regulated lncRNAs connected to GBM heterogeneity and immune evasion; and Zhao et al. highlighted methylation-dysregulated lncRNAs in autophagy regulation [13–16]. These studies provide an important foundation, but most begin from tumor-wide lncRNA methylation or expression landscapes. Broader reviews have further summarized lncRNA and noncoding RNA dysregulation in GBM [17,18], and epigenetic mechanisms have also been implicated in brain tumor cell identity [19]. Our study differs by first restricting the candidate universe to lncRNAs enriched in normal brain tissue and then testing methylation-expression coupling within GBM.

We therefore designed a normal-brain-first public epigenomic re-analysis. We defined brain-enriched lncRNAs from GTEx and GENCODE, evaluated promoter methylation-expression coupling in matched TCGA-GBM samples, and then added external methylation, external expression, survival-direction and single-cell state layers. The intended contribution is a prioritized candidate list with multi-layer support, not a causal demonstration of direct DNA methylation-mediated regulation.

Materials and methods

Study design and workflow

This was a secondary analysis of public, de-identified multi-omics data. The workflow is summarized in Figure 1. In brief, we first defined a normal brain-enriched lncRNA candidate space, then intersected this space with available TCGA-GBM expression and methylation matrices, screened for inverse promoter methylation-expression associations, and finally evaluated the resulting candidates using independent methylation, expression, survival and single-cell data layers. The study was designed as a prioritization analysis. Candidates were required to originate from normal brain-enriched lncRNAs and to show inverse methylation-expression coupling in matched GBM samples. External analyses were used to test directional concordance and biological context rather than to establish causality.

Data sources, dataset owners and inclusion principles

Gene annotation used GENCODE human reference annotation v49 [20]. Normal-tissue expression was obtained from GTEx through UCSC Xena [21,22]. The discovery cohort used public TCGA-GBM transcriptomic, Illumina HumanMethylation450 and clinical follow-up data through UCSC Xena [5,21]. External expression and survival support used the CGGA 693 cohort [23]. External methylation validation used GEO GSE36278, and single-cell validation used GEO GSE131928 Smart-seq2 data [24–26].

GTEx, TCGA/GDC, CGGA and GEO are the dataset owners or public repositories for the datasets used here. The study used publicly available, de-identified data according to repository access policies and did not access controlled personally identifiable information. Only primary GBM samples were used in the TCGA discovery layer. When several samples existed for one patient within the same data layer, values were collapsed to the patient level using the first 12 characters of the barcode.

Definition of brain-enriched lncRnas

lncRNA-related biotypes were extracted from GENCODE, including lncRNA, lincRNA, antisense, processed_transcript, sense_intronic, sense_overlapping, bidirectional_promoter_lncRNA, macro_lncRNA, 3prime_overlapping_ncRNA and non_coding. GTEx tissues were separated into brain and non-brain groups. For each lncRNA, we calculated median brain expression, median non-brain expression, the brain/non-brain median ratio, and a tissue-specificity tau index [27].

Brain enrichment was defined a priori as median brain expression ≥1 TPM, brain/non-brain median ratio ≥2, and tau score ≥0.6. This filter was applied before tumor-centered screening to preserve organ-of-origin logic.

TCGA expression preprocessing and state-score normalization

The TCGA expression layer used the UCSC Xena TCGA-GBM STAR TPM matrix. Primary tumor samples were selected, lncRNA rows were mapped to GENCODE gene symbols, and duplicated gene symbols were resolved by retaining the row with the highest mean expression. Samples were collapsed to the patient level by averaging barcodes with the same first 12 characters. In total, 156 patients were available for expression-based analyses.

For malignant-state scores, marker genes were extracted from the same expression matrix. Each marker gene was standardized across samples by z-score transformation, and the score for each sample was the mean z-score across available marker genes. No additional batch correction was applied within the TCGA discovery layer because the expression matrix came from a single uniformly processed Xena resource. Cross-cohort expression comparisons were used qualitatively to assess concordance of expression direction rather than quantitative effect-size replication because CGGA and GTEx differ in platform, processing and cohort composition.

Promoter methylation matrix, TSS definition and patient matching

The methylation layer used public Illumina HumanMethylation450 beta values. Probes with unambiguous genomic positions in the standard 450K annotation were retained. For each lncRNA gene, a promoter-proximal region was defined as 1 kb upstream to 1 kb downstream of the GENCODE v49 gene-level transcription start site (TSS). Probes falling within this interval were assigned to the corresponding lncRNA, and the mean beta value across mapped probes represented promoter methylation.

This was a gene-level analysis. Transcript isoforms, alternative TSSs and distal enhancer-linked regulatory elements were not modeled, because the public expression and methylation matrices used here were summarized at gene or probe level and did not provide a matched isoform-aware regulatory map. This simplification may miss isoform-specific or alternative-promoter regulation and is treated as a limitation.

The methylation matrix was collapsed to the patient level and intersected with the expression matrix. After preprocessing, 140 patients had methylation data and 52 patients had both expression and methylation data for one-to-one matched correlation analysis.

Candidate screening

For each brain-enriched lncRNA entering matched TCGA analysis, Spearman rank correlation was calculated between promoter methylation and expression across matched patients. Multiple testing was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method [28]. Core methylation-associated candidates were defined as ρ ≤−0.30 and FDR < 0.05.

The term methylation-associated is used deliberately. A negative methylation-expression association supports a regulatory hypothesis but does not prove that methylation is the causal driver of lncRNA repression.

Malignant-state scoring

Three TCGA state scores were calculated: stemness, mesenchymal-like state and immunosuppression. The marker genes were SOX2, NES, PROM1, OLIG2 and MYC for stemness; CD44, CHI3L1, SERPINE1, VIM and TNFRSF1A for mesenchymal-like state; and CD274, PDCD1LG2, TGFB1, CSF1 and IL10 for immunosuppression. Marker sets and scoring rules are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Spearman correlations were calculated between core-candidate expression and state scores. In GSE131928 Smart-seq2 data, stemness and mesenchymal-like scores were calculated using the same marker sets where detectable, both at the single-cell level and after aggregation to tumor-level pseudobulk units. These analyses were interpreted in the context of prior GBM single-cell studies highlighting intratumoral heterogeneity, stem-like programs and developmental hierarchies [29–32].

Survival and covariate considerations

Exploratory, unadjusted survival analyses in TCGA and CGGA used univariable Cox proportional-hazards models with each candidate expression value entered as a continuous standardized variable [33]. These models were descriptive and should not be interpreted as independent effect estimates or as defining independent prognostic biomarkers.

We did not adjust the methylation-expression screen for age, sex, batch, molecular subtype, tumor purity, MGMT status or IDH status. Age and sex were available for part of TCGA, but the matched methylation-expression discovery layer contained only 52 patients, and several molecular or purity covariates were not uniformly available across all public data layers. This lack of covariate adjustment is an explicit limitation.

External validation and statistical analysis

GSE36278 was used to test whether core candidates showed higher promoter methylation in GBM than in non-tumor brain tissue. TCGA-mapped promoter probes were intersected with probes available in GSE36278, and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were performed at probe and gene levels with FDR correction.

CGGA 693 expression was compared with GTEx brain expression to test external downregulation direction for candidates with stable cross-cohort coverage. Because this comparison spans different cohorts and platforms, it was used qualitatively to assess concordance of expression direction rather than quantitative effect-size replication. GSE131928 Smart-seq2 data were used to assess whether detectable candidates retained state associations at tumor-cell or tumor-pseudobulk level.

All analyses were scripted in R. The candidate-level TCGA table, marker gene sets, data-source table and core evidence table are provided as supplementary files to support reproducibility [34].

Results

After intersection with TCGA-GBM expression and promoter methylation availability, 76 lncRNAs entered matched methylation-expression analysis. Seven met the core methylation-associated criteria: PTPRD-AS1, NFIB-AS1, CEROX1, IRX2-DT, MEG8, YPEL3-DT and FAM201A (Figure 2; Supplementary Table S1). This stepwise design differs from whole-tumor transcriptome screening. It first constrains the search space by normal brain enrichment and then tests whether GBM samples show methylation-expression coupling within that brain-origin candidate set.

Matched TCGA samples show inverse promoter methylation-expression coupling

Across the 52 matched TCGA patients, PTPRD-AS1 and NFIB-AS1 showed the strongest inverse coupling between promoter methylation and expression (PTPRD-AS1: ρ = −0.665, p = 2.05e-07, FDR = 0.000016; NFIB-AS1: ρ = −0.639, p = 7.27e-07, FDR = 0.000028). The other core candidates also met the predefined inverse association threshold: CEROX1 (ρ = −0.438, FDR = 0.032), IRX2-DT (ρ = −0.424, FDR = 0.036), MEG8 (ρ = −0.410, FDR = 0.042), YPEL3-DT (ρ = −0.402, FDR = 0.042) and FAM201A (ρ = −0.389, FDR = 0.047) (Figure 3; Supplementary Table S1). In the cross-cohort expression comparison, MEG8 and FAM201A were both lower in CGGA GBM than in GTEx brain (MEG8: mean log-expression difference = −1.418, FDR = 3.35e-78; FAM201A: mean log-expression difference = −0.728, FDR = 2.61e-31). Survival was therefore treated as an exploratory contextual layer and was not used to define or exclude core candidates.

Single-cell evidence is sparse but supports FAM201A at tumor-pseudobulk level

In GSE131928 Smart-seq2 data, lncRNA detection was sparse. Among the seven core candidates, FAM201A and MEG8 were the most consistently detectable, with cell-level detection rates of 3.1% and 2.5%, respectively, across 7,930 tumor cells from 127 tumor units.

At the tumor-pseudobulk level, FAM201A correlated positively with stemness score (ρ = 0.278, p = 0.0015, FDR = 0.0061) and negatively with mesenchymal-like score (ρ = −0.259, p = 0.0033, FDR = 0.0067). MEG8 also showed pseudobulk associations with mesenchymal-like and stemness scores, although its direction was less aligned with the bulk TCGA state pattern. Overall, FAM201A and MEG8 provide the clearest single-cell-detectable validation track, whereas PTPRD-AS1 and NFIB-AS1 remain the strongest matched methylation-expression candidates. Discussion

This study should be read as a candidate-prioritization analysis. Its main contribution is not a new GBM prognostic model and not a causal demonstration of direct lncRNA silencing by DNA methylation. Instead, it introduces a normal-brain-first filter and provides a ranked set of brain-enriched lncRNAs whose promoter methylation and expression are inversely associated in matched GBM samples.

The relationship to prior work is important. Genome-wide lncRNA methylation studies in glioma and GBM have already linked lncRNA methylation to prognosis, intratumoral heterogeneity, immune evasion and autophagy-related biology [13–16]. Our analysis builds on these studies but differs in three ways. First, the candidate universe is restricted before tumor analysis by normal brain enrichment. Second, the primary screen requires matched patient-level promoter methylation-expression coupling. Third, candidates are interpreted through a multi-layer evidence table rather than through a single risk score.

The seven candidates appear to occupy different evidentiary roles. PTPRD-AS1 and NFIB-AS1 are the clearest structural methylation-expression candidates because they show the strongest inverse TCGA correlations. CEROX1 is most strongly related to malignant-state scores, especially the mesenchymal-like and stemness axes. MEG8 and FAM201A show the most useful external and single-cell support and may be better suited for near-term follow-up experiments.

The results also need careful interpretation. A promoter methylation-expression correlation can reflect direct repression, but it can also arise from tumor purity, cell-state composition, lineage-associated transcriptional states, chromatin accessibility, copy-number context or broader epigenomic context. For this reason, we changed the language throughout the manuscript from direct regulatory claims to methylation-associated or promoter hypermethylation-plus-low-expression descriptions.

Several limitations remain. First, only 52 TCGA patients had matched expression and methylation data, which limits statistical power. Second, promoter methylation was summarized at the GENCODE gene-level TSS using a ± 1 kb window; alternative TSSs, isoform-specific promoters and distal enhancers were not modeled. Third, the candidate screen and survival analyses were not adjusted for age, sex, batch, subtype, tumor purity, MGMT status or IDH status. Fourth, cross-cohort comparisons between CGGA and GTEx may contain technical confounding and were therefore used qualitatively to assess concordance of expression direction rather than quantitative effect-size replication. Fifth, lncRNA annotation evolves across GENCODE releases, which may affect exact reproducibility of the candidate universe. Sixth, lncRNAs are sparsely detected in single-cell RNA-seq, limiting the single-cell layer mainly to FAM201A and MEG8. Finally, experimental perturbation is required to establish whether methylation changes directly regulate these lncRNAs or downstream malignant phenotypes.

Despite these limitations, the study provides a compact and transparent starting point for validation. Future work could prioritize MEG8 and FAM201A for joint methylation-expression profiling and perturbation experiments, while using PTPRD-AS1 and NFIB-AS1 to test the structural promoter hypermethylation-low-expression axis in GBM specimens or model systems.

Conclusions

Using GTEx/UCSC Xena, TCGA-GBM/UCSC Xena, CGGA and GEO data, this study identified a set of brain-enriched lncRNAs associated with promoter hypermethylation and lower expression in glioblastoma. The strongest matched methylation-expression candidates were PTPRD-AS1 and NFIB-AS1; CEROX1 was most closely linked to malignant-state scores; and MEG8 and FAM201A had the clearest external or single-cell support.

These findings define a brain-origin, methylation-associated lncRNA candidate set for downstream validation. They should not be interpreted as proof of direct causal regulation by DNA methylation in GBM.
