Opioid Message Fatigue in the United States: Prevalence and Associations with Stigma and Policy Support
Authors: Alyssa Harrell-Webber, Wan-Lun Chang, Naessiamba Eab-Aggrey, Bruce G Taylor, Phoebe A Lamuda, Harold A Pollack, John A Schneider, Faye S Taxman, Xiaoquan Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The opioid crisis has generated widespread public attention and messaging. However, persistent messaging may contribute to message fatigue, i.e., feelings of exhaustion and boredom due to excessive and redundant exposure. This study investigates the prevalence of opioid message fatigue among American adults, its association with individual characteristics, and its connection with opioid use disorder (OUD) stigma and policy support for OUD treatment.Method:A nationally representative cross-sectional sample of U.S. household adults aged 18+ (N = 1,022) were surveyed August 7–15, 2024. Multiple regression models examined individual characteristics associated with message fatigue, and the associations of opioid message fatigue with stigma about OUD and support for OUD-related public policies.Results:On average, participants reported a moderate level of message fatigue (M = 2.75, SD = 0.85, range = 1–5). Fatigue varied significantly by gender, education, income, political identity, and life experience. Findings suggest higher message fatigue was associated with greater stigma about OUD (B = 0.09, p < 0.001) and lower support for OUD treatment policies (B = −0.32, p < 0.001).Conclusions:Future research should continue to examine the social and policy implications of OUD message fatigue and improve public health communication by anticipating and mitigating message fatigue through strategic planning.Keywords: Message fatigue, opioid crisis, OUD, stigma, policy support

Introduction

In 2017, the United States government declared the opioid crisis a national public health emergency (Mercia, 2017). Public health initiatives (e.g., campaigns, opioid substitution programs, and changes to prescribing practices) have been implemented to address the crisis, raise public awareness of opioid misuse and opioid use disorder (OUD), and highlight evidence-informed interventions such as medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD) and harm reduction efforts such as naloxone distribution to prevent overdose deaths (Burgess et al., 2021; Furlan et al., 2018). These efforts, coupled with extensive media coverage, have made information about the opioid crisis widely available and prominent in the United States (Yang & Mackert, 2023).

Although such messages increase public awareness associated with opioid misuse, excessive exposure may also elicit message fatigue, whereby individuals feel exhausted and bored from heavy exposure to repetitive messages over time (So et al., 2017). Message fatigue has been linked to negative consequences in health communication, including information avoidance, psychological reactance, and reduced engagement in self-protective behaviors (Ball & Wozniak, 2022; Guan et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024). Beyond intrapersonal effects, message fatigue may also influence social attitudes such as stigma and policy support, which can shape public and institutional response to the opioid crisis (Taylor et al., 2021).

Despite growing research on communication issues related to the opioid crisis, the extent of accompanying message fatigue among Americans elicited by intense media coverage and public health messaging about the opioid crisis remains underexplored. To fill this gap, this study used nationally representative data to examine the prevalence of opioid message fatigue among U.S. adults. We also explored associations between message fatigue and individual characteristics, stigma toward people with OUD, and policy support for OUD treatment.

Literature review

Opioid campaigns and media attention

In a systematic review, Furlan et al. (2018) identified public health campaigns as one of the most effective approaches to mitigating opioid misuse, alongside treatment interventions such as opioid substitution programs and changes to prescribing practices. Communication campaigns have increased awareness, knowledge, and public support for crisis response and decreased opioid-related stigma (Cao & Xu, 2021; Yang & Mackert, 2023). For example, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s nationwide Rx Awareness campaign significantly reduced stigmatizing attitudes and increased empathy toward patients with OUD (Yang & Mackert, 2023). In California, awareness of the state-level campaign Unshame CA was associated with lower public stigma and greater willingness to move next door to or employ individuals with OUD (California Department of Health Care Services, 2024). Recent campaigns have featured diverse message themes and strategies, including raising awareness of OUD, promoting evidence-based practices (e.g., reduced prescribing and increased MOUD use), tailoring messages for local communities, and highlighting perceived threats of the opioid crisis such as stigma, addiction, and family struggles (Bagley & Beckham, 2021; Frkovich et al., 2022; Lefebvre et al., 2020; Stein et al., 2023).

While the positive impact of opioid campaigns has been well documented (Egan et al., 2020), unintended consequences and adverse effects of campaign messaging are possible (Scott, 2021; Siegel & Yzer, 2025). Extant work demonstrates that interventions and campaigns aimed at destigmatizing various conditions often produce unintended effects such as normalization or desensitization (Siegel & Yzer, 2025). Specifically in the context of opioids, an experiment by Parent et al. (2023) found that opioid overdose images used in police social media campaigns may inadvertently reduce individuals’ willingness to help someone experiencing an overdose. Additionally, news media often frame the opioid crisis through a criminal justice-oriented lens (Gunning et al., 2024; McGinty et al., 2016), which may reinforce stigmatized perceptions of individuals with OUD (Olsen & Sharfstein, 2014). McGinty et al. (2019), for example, analyzed 6,399 news stories and found 49% of the stories included stigmatizing language such as “opioid addict,” while only 2% used less stigmatizing terms like “person with an opioid use disorder.”

As the opioid crisis persists, efforts to mitigate OUD and reduce overdose deaths must continue (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025). Media attention to the issue is also unlikely to abate. Opioid-related intervention messages and news coverage have saturated media platforms in recent years. A study retrieved 35,758 U.S. news articles on opioid-related events from February 2018 to April 2019 alone (Hswen et al., 2020). Another study using quarterly Google searches in 2019–2020 discovered 166 opioid-related campaigns (Frkovich et al., 2022). The prevalence and intensity of opioid messaging make chronic message fatigue a possibility among the American public.

Message fatigue in health communication

Message fatigue is defined as “an aversive motivational state of being exhausted and bored by overexposure to similar, redundant messages over an extended period of time” (So et al., 2017, p. 10). So et al. (2017) identified four dimensions of message fatigue: perceived overexposure (messages appearing more frequently than preferred); perceived redundancy (messages being overlapping and repetitive); exhaustion (a feeling of burnout); and tedium (a lack of interest in the message). These negative experiences associated with message fatigue often lead to disengagement with and resistance toward the message, which in turn decrease message effectiveness (Keating & Skurka, 2024; Kim & So, 2018). A concept related to message fatigue is compassion fatigue, which describes exhaustion in response to social problems (Kinnick et al., 1996). Recent research on the opioid crisis indicates that healthcare providers and first responders experience compassion fatigue when treating large numbers of individuals with OUD and overdoses, which can contribute to reduced quality of care and increased stigma (Cernasev et al., 2021; Dowdell et al., 2022; Pike et al., 2019). Although related, message fatigue is more tightly focused on audience experience with communication and messaging, while compassion fatigue is characterized by reduced empathy or concern for suffering.

Message fatigue is often found to vary by individual characteristics. For instance, So and Alam (2019) found that women and older adults were more likely to experience fatigue from anti-obesity messages. In contrast, younger males report greater message fatigue in response to anti-tobacco messaging (So & Popova, 2018). In the context of COVID-19 messaging, females and individuals with a college or graduate degree reported higher message fatigue, whereas Democrats and those in better health reported lower levels of message fatigue (Zhao et al., 2024).

A prominent focus of existing research on message fatigue is its influence on information processing. Drawing on relevant theories of cognitive psychology and persuasion, this line of work has shown that message fatigue can lead to inattention, reactance, and other forms of biased processing and defensive message response (Kim & So, 2018; So et al., 2017). This pattern has been observed for both health campaign messaging and news coverage, even though the latter is not explicitly persuasive in nature. For example, Gurr and Metag (2022) found that excessive exposure to the same news issue could lead to audience annoyance and avoidance of future news consumption related to the issue. In a survey experiment on climate change mitigation, exposure to multiple news headlines over a short period led to message fatigue, which was in turn associated with less support for mitigation efforts (Lu, 2022).

Research has also linked message fatigue to behavioral health outcomes. For instance, Kim and So (2018) found repeated exposure to weight-management messages reduced motivation to adopt healthier behaviors. A large national study found message fatigue to be negatively associated with COVID-19 vaccine booster uptake and intentions, highlighting its potential to compromise pandemic response (Zhao et al., 2024). A meta-analysis confirmed a general link between increased message fatigue and lower intentions to adopt healthy behaviors across different contexts (Keating & Skurka, 2024).

Although message fatigue receives growing attention, it remains understudied in the context of opioid-related communication. A few studies have acknowledged the relevance of message fatigue in the opioid crisis but have not addressed it as a focal concern. Cao (2026) identified message fatigue as a potential consequence of heavy exposure to opioid risk narratives but did not specifically investigate message fatigue. Similarly, Gholamrezaei et al. (2023) limited their prescription opioid tapering intervention to 12 weeks to avoid inducing message fatigue but did not assess it as a specific outcome. Given the prevalence of opioid-related messaging, message fatigue is a realistic concern. As an initial exploration, this study seeks to document the level of opioid-related message fatigue both in the general population and among various population subgroups marked by different demographic, sociopolitical, and health-related characteristics.

Message fatigue, OUD stigma, and policy support

Understanding opioid-related message fatigue requires looking beyond individual behaviors and information processing to consider broader societal outcomes. This study considers the possibility that message fatigue may be associated with increased stigma toward people with OUD and decreased support for treatment policies. These negative effects could hinder an effective societal response to the crisis.

In many ways, the opioid crisis has rallied Americans in efforts to reduce overdose mortality and related harms. Unfortunately, public awareness has also generated widespread stigma toward individuals with OUD (Adams et al., 2021; Cheetham et al., 2022). Stigma can be broadly defined as “a simplified, standardized image of the disgrace of certain people that is held in common by a community at large” (Smith, 2007, p. 464). Stigma and controversy also surround OUD treatment methods, particularly the use of MOUD. These first-line, evidence-based treatments have been shown to reduce opioid cravings, withdrawal symptoms, and overdose risk (Food and Drug Administration, 2024). Despite such evidence, negative perceptions and stigma about MOUD create significant barriers to proper care (Cheetham et al., 2022). A common misconception is that MOUD use is simply substituting one addictive drug with another, fueling reluctance among the public, healthcare providers, and individuals with OUD (Brousseau et al., 2022). Furthermore, opioid use is frequently portrayed as a criminal behavior by the media (McGinty et al., 2016), which further exacerbates stigmatization.

Previous research notes that stigma is rooted in stereotypes and cognitive biases, functioning as a cognitively efficient thought process but one that limits critical or reflective thinking (Beer et al., 2006; Griffith & Kohrt, 2016; Link & Phelan, 2001). The level and distribution of stigma in society vary by the issue at hand. Research shows stigmatized views of OUD are more common among individuals who are older, male, White or Asian, politically conservative, and lack personal history with substance use or mental health issues (Kelly et al., 2024; Taylor et al., 2021). Failing to view OUD as a medical condition is linked to increased stigma, whereas greater familiarity with substance use disorder, having a personal relationship with someone experiencing a substance use disorder, personal experience with opioid misuse, or involvement in the justice system is associated with lower stigma (Galanis et al., 2025; Haller et al., 2026; Taylor et al., 2021).

The elaboration likelihood model (ELM; Petty & Cacioppo, 1986) provides a useful framework for understanding the potential link between message fatigue and stigma. ELM posits that when individuals are motivated and able, they tend to engage in careful, deliberate, and critical thinking. In contrast, when motivation and ability are low, individuals are more likely to rely on peripheral cues and simple decision rules to arrive at conclusions (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986). Message fatigue is a psychological state marked by low motivation and ability and a general tendency toward low elaboration (So et al., 2017). When people feel chronically fatigued due to overexposure to repeated messages, they are more likely to resort to simple heuristics and biases, such as stigmatic stereotypes, in information processing and decision making (Link & Phelan, 2001). Repeated activation of stigma will allow it to self-perpetuate, making it even more readily accessible and influential in subsequent encounters with issue-relevant messages (Higgins, 2012). As a result of this dynamic, a positive association between message fatigue and stigma is very plausible.

Beyond stigma, message fatigue may also play a role in individuals’ stance on public policies. Studies such as Kennedy-Hendricks et al. (2017) and Pyra et al. (2022) find stronger stigma is associated with increased support for punitive policies and reduced support for policies expanding access to MOUD and protecting individuals who seek help after an overdose. The dynamics between message fatigue and stigma may thus extend to the realm of policy attitudes, resulting in a negative association between message fatigue and support for policies to increase OUD treatments. In addition to stigma, message fatigue may also compromise policy support through other mechanisms. As noted earlier, message fatigue can lead to inattention, reactance, and defensive information processing (Kim & So, 2018; So et al., 2017). Consequently, message fatigue is often negatively associated with message-advocated attitudes, intentions, and behaviors (Keating & Skurka, 2024). Support for MOUD treatment is an important objective of much opioid-related messaging. If the general pattern in the literature holds, we should expect individuals experiencing greater message fatigue to be more likely to push back against such messaging and oppose treatment enhancement policies. Along similar lines, fatigue-induced disengagement with opioid-related communication may leave individuals factually uninformed about effective policy responses. This could also lead to lower policy support among those who experience higher levels of message fatigue.

Opioid-related message fatigue is underexplored, and current research has primarily focused on information processing and individual health behaviors (Keating & Skurka, 2024; So & Liu, 2023). Investigations into the broader social and policy implications of message fatigue remain scarce. To fill these gaps, this study explores the prevalence of message fatigue about the opioid crisis among the U.S. adult household population and its variation across various population subgroups. It also examines the associations of message fatigue with stigma about OUD and policy support for OUD treatment. We pose the following research questions:

Method

Overview

This study used data from the AmeriSpeak® Omnibus survey collected by NORC at the University of Chicago. AmeriSpeak® is a probability-based panel consisting of approximately 35,000 households, recruited through area probability and address-based sampling. The panel ensures a sample coverage representative of 97% of the U.S. household population. Data were collected from August 7 to 15, 2024 (N = 1,022), with a 23.5% within-panel completion rate and a 5% cumulative response rate (accounting for panel recruitment and retention). Participants completed the survey online. The survey was available in both English and Spanish, and participants received a $3 incentive for their participation. A combination of post-survey cleaning rules and panel design features was used to ensure data quality. The average completion time was approximately 15 min.

The survey was reviewed and approved by NORC’s Institutional Review Board (IRB 00000967) under its Federal-wide Assurance (FWA 00000142). During the panel registration process, participants provided informed consent and agreed to the survey’s privacy policy and terms and conditions, either online or by phone. For the current study, a request for alteration of consent and waiver of documentation of consent was approved by NORC’s IRB. Respondents were given a key information statement outlining survey topics and estimated time commitment prior to data collection. Weights were developed to align the sample with U.S. Census benchmarks, adjusting for selection probabilities and non-response.

Measures

OUD message fatigue

An eight-item scale developed by Song et al. (2023) was adapted to measure message fatigue. The opioid crisis was plugged into the items as the topic; no other changes were made to item phrasing. Sample scale items included: “At this point, I’ve heard about problems related to the opioid epidemic more than I ever needed to,” and “There are simply too many messages about the opioid epidemic nowadays.” Participants rated their agreement with scale items on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) using principal axis factoring (PAF) revealed a single factor, explaining 66.44% of the variance. The eight items were averaged into a composite score, where a higher value indicated greater OUD message fatigue (α = 0.93).

Stigma about OUD

A six-item scale assessing stigma toward OUD was adapted from previous literature (Kennedy-Hendricks et al., 2017; Taylor et al., 2021). The original items referred to addiction and pain medications in addition to OUD (Taylor et al., 2021). To maintain focus, the current scale only referred to OUD; no other changes were made. Sample statements included: “I would be willing to have a person with a current opioid use disorder start working closely with me on a job,” and “People with a current opioid use disorder are more dangerous than the general population.” Participants rated their agreement with each scale item on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). An EFA using PAF suggested two factors, explaining 44.03 and 23.15% of the variance in the data, respectively. However, the two-factor solution was largely a result of wording valence, with stigma-endorsing items loading on one factor and anti-stigma items loading on the other. Considering this pattern and following practices in previous research, we reverse coded the anti-stigma items and averaged the scale into a composite score, where a higher value indicated greater perceived stigma toward OUD (α = 0.74).

OUD policy support

Participants rated their agreement with a four-item scale assessing support for OUD treatment policies adapted from previous research (Kennedy-Hendricks et al., 2017; Taylor et al., 2021). Sample statements included: “I favor expanding Medicaid insurance benefits to provide coverage for the treatment of opioid use disorders/addiction problems,” and “I support the FDA’s approval of the first over-the-counter naloxone nasal spray (also known as “Narcan”), a medication that can quickly reverse the effects of a person experiencing an opioid overdose (i.e., taking a high dose of opioids leading to the slowing or stopping of breathing).” Responses were given on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). A “Don’t know” option was available but not selected by any respondent. An EFA using PAF revealed a single factor, explaining 59.54% of the variance in the data. The four items were averaged into an overall score, where a higher value indicated greater OUD policy support (α = 0.77). (See Supplemental Materials for complete measurement scales).

Demographic and background variables

To enable a comprehensive examination of how message fatigue may vary in society, this study collected a broad range of demographic and background information, including gender, age, education level, combined race/ethnicity, marital status, current employment status, household income, party identification, political ideology, health insurance coverage, general health condition, personal prescription opioid misuse, family/friends OUD history, and personal criminal legal involvement. All demographic and background variables were coded as categorical variables (see Table 1 for coding categories). Most of these variables were regularly updated panel characteristics available in all survey datasets from AmeriSpeak®. Others were asked in the survey specifically fielded for this study, including personal prescription opioid misuse, family/friends OUD history, and personal criminal legal involvement. While the relevance of OUD-related variables is straightforward, personal criminal legal involvement was included because of its strong association with opioid use and OUD in previous research (Berk et al., 2025; Winkelman et al., 2018).

For personal prescription opioid misuse history, participants indicated whether they “have ever taken a prescription opioid medication (e.g., fentanyl, oxycodone, etc.) in a way other than directed by a medical provider, for example, to feel good/get high or because you would get sick if you didn’t take it” (yes/no). For family or friends’ OUD history, participants were asked whether they “have any family members or close friends ever had an opioid use disorder or misused opioids (for example, used in a way other than prescribed/intended or developed a problem with them)” (yes/no). For personal criminal legal involvement, participants were asked whether they “have ever been convicted of any misdemeanor or felony crime” (yes/no) and whether they “have ever been incarcerated in jail or prison” (yes/no). A positive response to either question indicated criminal legal involvement.

Analyses

Descriptive analyses were conducted to assess sample characteristics and the distribution of OUD message fatigue within the population (RQ1). To address RQ2, we first examined the variation of message fatigue across each demographic and background variable (hereafter, individual difference variable) as described in the Measures section. A t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to ascertain statistical significance. Sidak adjustment was used for post-hoc comparisons in ANOVAs. We then conducted a multiple regression to examine the relationship between OUD message fatigue while simultaneously controlling for the same set of individual difference variables. All individual difference variables were dummy coded. The reference group for each variable was selected to best highlight social contrasts (e.g., non-Hispanic White for race and ethnicity; Democrats for party identification). To address RQ3a and b, we performed two additional multiple regression analyses using OUD message fatigue to predict stigma about OUD and OUD policy support, respectively, while again controlling for the same individual difference covariates used to address RQ2. All analyses were conducted using SPSS V.30 (IBM Inc.), applying weights to align the sample with national benchmarks. Statistical significance was determined at an alpha level of 0.05. Missing data was minimal (Max = 1.1% for ideology) and handled with listwise deletion. Diagnostic tests showed no issues with multicollinearity. The variance inflation factors (VIFs) were below 3 for all but one predictor (dummy code for age 60+, VIF = 3.41 to 3.43 across models). Residual analysis revealed no clear violation of test assumptions for multiple regression.

Results

The study included 1,022 respondents, and sample characteristics are summarized in Table 1. On average, respondents expressed a moderate level of OUD message fatigue, with notable dispersion across the scale (M = 2.75, SD = 0.85; RQ1).

RQ3a explored the association between OUD message fatigue and stigma toward OUD. The multiple regression model (Table 3) explained 16% of the variance in stigma toward OUD, F (28, 958) = 6.56, p < 0.001. OUD message fatigue was positively associated with stigma toward OUD (B = 0.09, p < 0.001). In addition, adults aged 30–44 (B = 0.20, p = 0.006), 45–59 (B = 0.30, p < 0.001), and above 60 (B = 0.31, p < 0.001) showed higher levels of stigma toward OUD than those aged 18–29. People with household incomes of $30,000–$59,999 (B = −0.20, p = 0.003) reported lower levels of stigma than those earning less than $30,000. Hispanics reported greater stigma than non-Hispanic Whites (B = 0.19, p = 0.002). Republicans showed stronger stigma than Democrats (B = 0.12, p = 0.047). Individuals with moderate (B = 0.14, p = 0.019) and conservative (B = 0.27, p < 0.001) ideology expressed greater stigma than liberals. People with a history of prescription opioid use (B = −0.19, p = 0.002) expressed lower stigma. Individuals self-rated as fair or poor in health indicated less stigma than those excellent in health (B = −0.22, p = 0.009).

Discussion

This study assessed the prevalence of OUD message fatigue among U.S. adults and its associations with OUD stigma and policy support. Using a nationally representative sample, we found a moderate level of message fatigue in the general population, with notable variations across demographic, political, and life experience subgroups. Furthermore, higher levels of message fatigue were linked to greater OUD stigma and lower policy support for evidence-informed interventions. These relationships suggest message fatigue may have broader societal implications beyond individual information processing and health behavior, as stigma and policy support can impact public health outcomes through social and institutional mechanisms, including intergroup contact, public opinion, community organizing, and legal/policy interventions (Cook et al., 2014).

Our first aim was to assess the prevalence of opioid-related message fatigue within a nationally representative sample of U.S. adults. Most published research on message fatigue has used nonprobability samples and thus could not provide accurate population estimates (Martinez Gonzalez et al., 2021; So, 2022; Zhao et al., 2024). Furthermore, past studies have not examined message fatigue in the context of opioid use. We find that many U.S. adults report fatigue from opioid-related messages. Although not extreme, such fatigue is worth attention as it may hinder public health initiatives and introduce friction into broader public response.

In both bivariate and multivariate analyses, we identify population subgroups with distinct demographic, sociopolitical, and health-related characteristics who are particularly likely to report OUD message fatigue. Consistent with other studies (e.g., So & Popova, 2018), male respondents were significantly more likely than females to experience message fatigue in our study. We also found lower message fatigue among certain higher-income groups. Despite racialized themes in public perceptions of the opioid crisis (Pyra et al., 2022), race/ethnicity was not associated with message fatigue in our data.

Past research has shown that individuals at higher risk for unhealthy behaviors tend to report greater message fatigue (e.g., Kim & So, 2018). Similarly, our study found that those with a personal history of prescription opioid misuse were more likely to report message fatigue than those without such an experience. This finding is consistent with a broad pattern in health communication where individuals most at risk tend to react more negatively to intervention messaging and other issue-relevant information, partly as a way to mitigate anxiety and perceived threat to the self (Liberman & Chaiken, 1992; Rains, 2013). Notably, individuals reporting OUD among family and friends and personal criminal legal involvement were less likely to express message fatigue. It is possible that witnessing the consequences of opioid use among family, friends, and loved ones, including experiences of vicarious stigma and facing opioid-related legal system challenges, may have increased the perceived importance of the issue and the perceived value of opioid messages, thus decreasing fatigue.

Another notable finding was the association between partisan identification and OUD message fatigue. This aligns with past research on message fatigue in the context of politically charged public health issues (So & Song, 2023; Zhao et al., 2024). In our study, Democrats reported significantly lower message fatigue than Independents and Republicans, and those with moderate or conservative ideologies experienced more fatigue than liberals. Although the opioid crisis is generally considered a bipartisan issue, the approaches to addressing it and the attributions of responsibility often diverge along partisan lines (Pyra et al., 2022). Our findings suggest political ideology may shape not only policy preferences but also feelings about opioid messaging.

Prior studies have documented widespread stigma toward OUD in American society (Cioe et al., 2020; Kennedy-Hendricks et al., 2017). OUD stigma has harmful consequences, including mental health burdens, discrimination, reduced MOUD access, and limited support for evidence-informed interventions and policies (Adams et al., 2021; Cioe et al., 2020). This study adds to this body of work by demonstrating a significant relationship between OUD stigma and message fatigue. Findings showed that individuals with greater message fatigue also reported higher levels of stigma about those with OUD. While this study does not establish a causal order between OUD message fatigue and stigma, ELM (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986) suggests that message fatigue may lead to increased reliance on stigma as a psychological shortcut in information processing and decision making, thereby reinforcing negative perceptions about OUD. Our findings are consistent with this reasoning. Furthermore, given media coverage of OUD is often stigmatizing (McGinty et al., 2019), it is possible that repeated exposure may have contributed to both message fatigue and stigma about individuals with OUD.

Public policies play an essential role in expanding access to MOUD, reducing overdose deaths, and shaping how the population addresses and mitigates the opioid crisis (Food and Drug Administration, 2024). However, policies surrounding the opioid crisis remain controversial and inconsistently supported (Pyra et al., 2022; Sylvester et al., 2022). Past research shows stigma about those with OUD is linked to lower support for treatment-focused policies in favor of punitive approaches (Kennedy-Hendricks et al., 2017; Pyra et al., 2022).

This study found that those reporting greater message fatigue were also less supportive of policies expanding OUD treatment access. Fatigue-triggered stigma may undermine support for policies promoting OUD treatment (Kennedy-Hendricks et al., 2017; Pyra et al., 2022). However, in our study, message fatigue was more strongly associated with policy support than with stigma, suggesting that its influence on policy attitudes may extend beyond stigmatizing beliefs. One possibility is that individuals experiencing higher message fatigue may reject treatment policies as a form of active resistance to opioid-related policy messaging. This may be particularly likely for policies commonly misperceived as condoning use (e.g., medication-based treatment and harm reduction policies). Another possibility is that fatigued individuals may simply have less knowledge about effective policies due to avoidance and inattention. This could also result in lower policy support. Regardless of the underlying mechanism, our findings suggest that opioid-related communication might influence public policy considerations above and beyond its content—audience experience with opioid-related messaging also matters. Overexposure to opioid-related messages and the resulting fatigue may lead the public to disengage from this issue and develop negative attitudes toward important OUD policies, thus hindering effective policy response to the opioid crisis.

Our findings suggest promising directions for theoretical advancement in message fatigue research. Previous studies have focused heavily on how message fatigue may influence individual-level information processing and behavioral decision making (Ball & Wozniak, 2022; Guan et al., 2023; Kim & So, 2018; Mao et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). Our findings also suggest broader social dynamics triggered by or involving message fatigue, such as OUD stigma and policy support, should also be part of the agenda for theoretical development. While stigma and policy support reside in individual attitudes, their impact is often more pronounced and consequential beyond the individual level, affecting societal processes and public response to important social and public health issues, such as the opioid crisis. Given that message fatigue was associated with lower policy support, integrating stigma/policy frameworks into message fatigue research can open new frontiers for theory and empirical work, fostering multi-level perspectives on the psychological, social, and structural implications of message fatigue. In the same vein, it would also be productive to engage more ecologically oriented communication theories, such as diffusion of innovation (Rogers, 2003) and media agenda-setting (McCombs, 2004), in future message fatigue research. These theories address community, media, and institutional processes of information dissemination and their impacts and are thus promising platforms to extend theorizing about message fatigue beyond the individual level.

This study has practical implications. Understanding public attitudes toward OUD messaging and monitoring the extent of message fatigue can help inform interventions, allowing adjustments in messaging strategies to mitigate fatigue (Cao, 2026; Gholamrezaei et al., 2023). Because fatigue varies across subgroups, tailored messaging is needed to alleviate fatigue within groups. Communication-based interventions should strategize the placement and flighting of messaging to reset attention among fatigue-prone subgroups. Research should explore ways to preemptively address message fatigue before it becomes problematic. Past research suggests personalizing messages, diversifying content and executional styles, and reframing as useful strategies (Guan et al., 2023; Mao et al., 2024; So et al., 2017). Interventions have already been using such strategies. For example, the HEALing Communities Study incorporated community-tailored images including local landmarks and influencers (Lefebvre et al., 2020), and the Life Unites Us campaign used hopeful, recovery-focused messaging with person-first language to reduce stigma (Bonnevie et al., 2022). Additionally, stigma reduction efforts among healthcare workers have used video-based interventions featuring patient narratives to decrease primary care clinicians’ stigma toward people with OUD (Hooker et al., 2023). In the opioid context, strategic diversification of messengers, including people with lived experience, community advocates, faith leaders, and public health authorities, might prove particularly productive in mitigating fatigue.

Given the relationship between message fatigue and OUD stigma and policy support, public health interventions should consider fatigue mitigation as part of a comprehensive strategy to counter deep-rooted stigma and promote effective public policies. Finally, the efficacy of mitigation strategies is an important question for empirical research. Rigorous testing in issue- and population-specific contexts is needed to inform decision-making in fatigue management.

Our findings must be interpreted in light of several study limitations. The cross-sectional design prevented causal interpretation of our data. We did not measure actual exposure to various types of information about the opioid crisis (e.g., news media vs. public health campaigns). For this reason, we were unable to speak on the nature or source of the messaging people received or the extent to which message fatigue tracked with such exposure. Message fatigue related to different types of messaging may be associated with social attitudes differently. This possibility was untapped in this study and should be an important topic for future research. All variables were self-reported and not immune to social desirability effects. The survey response rate was relatively low, although we deployed standard survey-weight methodologies to provide nationally representative estimates. More intensive study designs may achieve higher response rates.

Conclusion

In this survey of U.S. adults, moderate message fatigue about the opioid crisis was observed. Greater message fatigue was associated with stronger stigma about OUD and with lower support for policies to improve OUD treatment. Future research should continue to examine the social and policy implications of OUD message fatigue and improve public health communication by anticipating and mitigating message fatigue through strategic planning.
