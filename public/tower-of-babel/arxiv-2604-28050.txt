A No-Cloning Trade-off Between Black Hole No-Hair and Horizon Smoothness
Sudhanva Joshi, Sunil Kumar Mishra

Abstract
The black hole no-hair theorem is traditionally derived from the uniqueness theorems of general relativity. We show that a quantitative form follows from unitarity together with the standard semiclassical assumptions of horizon causality and interior accessibility. For a semiclassical black hole, we prove that the trace distance between exterior states corresponding to two same-charge infalling states is bounded by 2 ​ 2 ​ ε 2\sqrt{2\varepsilon} , where ε \varepsilon quantifies the diamond norm departure of the interior channel from a perfect isometry which is a quantitative measure of horizon-smoothness violation that upper-bounds 1 − F I 1-F_{I} , where F I F_{I} is the interior fidelity capturing how faithfully the infalling state is retained. Inverting this relation yields a trade-off inequality, ε ≥ D max 2 / 8 \varepsilon\geq D_{\max}^{2}/8 , between the maximum exterior distinguishability D max D_{\max} and the degree of horizon smoothness. This establishes that observable exterior quantum hair is quantitatively incompatible with exact horizon smoothness under unitary evolution: any model predicting nonzero exterior hair must violate the equivalence principle at the horizon by a quantifiable amount. Pre-existing entanglement with the infalling system is the only channel for quantum hair compatible with both unitarity and horizon smoothness.



Introduction.—
The black hole no-hair theorem is one of the most striking results in classical general relativity. Through the uniqueness theorems of
20
;
8
;
29
, it is established that a stationary black hole in Einstein-Maxwell theory is completely characterized by three externally measurable parameters, which are mass
M
, electric charge
Q
, and angular momentum
J
. All other details of the matter that formed the black hole become inaccessible to the exterior observers. The proofs for no hair theorem rely on the specific structure of the Einstein-Maxwell field equations in
3{+}1
dimensions
9
.

The quantum no-cloning theorem, which states that it is impossible to create an exact, independent copy of an arbitrary unknown quantum state using any unitary quantum operation
35
;
11
;
30
entered black hole physics through the formulation of black hole complementarity
32
, where it serves as a consistency check: information about infalling matter appears to be accessible both at the stretched horizon (to an exterior observer) and in the interior (to an infalling observer), but no single observer can access both copies, so no-cloning theorem is not violated. The firewall argument (AMPS)
2
sharpened this tension by exhibiting a scenario in which monogamy of entanglement, unitarity, and the equivalence principle appear mutually inconsistent. Harlow and Hayden
15
responded by estimating that the quantum computation required to verify the AMPS contradiction takes time exponentially in the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy
\rm S_{BH}
, rendering it physically unimplementable. The Hayden-Preskill protocol
17
showed that information thrown into a sufficiently old, rapidly scrambling black hole can, in principle, be recovered from the radiation, but only at exponential computational cost. More recently, computational complexity has been placed on a rigorous footing: Kim, Tang, and Preskill
22
showed that pseudorandom dynamics protect the interior from efficient exterior observers, and Kim and Preskill
21
proved that the black hole
S
-matrix is unitary for polynomially bounded operations under a pseudorandom assumption. Engelhardt
et al.

13
further connected pseudorandom unitaries to cosmic censorship.

In parallel, several authors have explored quantum hair, which are exterior observables beyond the classical
(M,Q,J)
that carry information about the black hole micro-states. Hawking, Perry, and Strominger
16
proposed that supertranslation charges (Soft hairs) on the horizon store such information. Calmet and Hsu
7
argued that the quantum state of the exterior graviton field can encode interior information, constituting quantum hair accessible, in principle. Raju
28
reviewed how the inclusion of quantum effects implies that the exterior observations can determine the complete quantum state, rendering the classical no-hair theorem strictly a classical statement.

In all of the above, the no-cloning theorem is used defensively: as a constraint that must not be violated. In this Letter, we reverse the logical direction. We use unitarity, together with horizon causality and interior accessibility, to derive a quantitative bound on the distinguishing power of exterior observables, and then invert this bound to obtain a trade-off inequality constraining the relationship between the exterior observability and the interior fidelity. The result is the model-independent constraint on black hole microstructure: any quantum gravity model predicting nonzero exterior hair must predict a corresponding violation of the equivalence principle at the horizon, bounded below by our inequality.

Let
\varepsilon
denote the diamond-norm distance between the actual interior channel and a perfect isometry, which is a quantitative measure of equivalence-principle violation that controls the worst-case interior fidelity via
\varepsilon\geq 1-F_{I}
, and let
D_{\max}
denote the maximum exterior distinguishability between same-charge infalling states. Then:

\varepsilon\;\geq\;\frac{D_{\max}^{2}}{8}\,.

(1)

This inequality has the logical structure of Bell’s theorem
4
: just as Bell’s inequality constrains correlations under local realism, Eq. (
1
) constrains the coexistence of quantum hair and horizon smoothness under unitarity. We note that our result is complementary to, but distinct from, the no-hiding theorem
6
, which addresses where the information goes when it leaves a subsystem; our result addresses the exterior consequences when the information remains in the interior.

Framework.—

Hilbert space and evolution.—
Consider a black hole in the semi-classical regime with Bekenstein-Hawking entropy
S_{\mathrm{BH}}=A/(4G_{N})\gg 1
. An infalling quantum system
F
has a Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}_{F}
of dimension
d
. Before infall, the black hole occupies a state
\ket{\Phi_0}_{\mathrm{BH}}
in
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{BH}}
. The total Hilbert space is
\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{H}_{F}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{BH}}
. After
F
crosses the event horizon, we adopt a tensor decomposition of
\mathscr{H}
into interior and exterior sectors,

\mathscr{H}\;=\;\mathscr{H}_{I}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{E}\,,

(2)

valid to leading order in
1/S_{\rm BH}

12
;
34
. The global state after the infall is

\ket{\Psi(\psi)}_{IE}\;=\;U\bigl(\ket{\psi}_{F}\otimes\ket{\Phi_0}_{\mathrm{BH}}\bigr)\,,

(3)

where
U
is the unitary time-evolution operator on
\mathscr{H}
that includes the gravitational interaction during horizon crossing. The reduced state on the interior and the exterior are

\begin{split}\rho_{I}(\psi)=\mathrm{Tr}_{E}\bigl[\outerproduct{\Psi(\psi)}{\Psi(\psi)}\bigr],\\
\rho_{E}(\psi)=\mathrm{Tr}_{I}\bigl[\outerproduct{\Psi(\psi)}{\Psi(\psi)}\bigr].\end{split}

(4)

Interior Fidelity.—
Let
V:\mathscr{H}_{F}\hookrightarrow\mathscr{H}_{I}
be the isometric embedding that maps the infalling system’s degrees of freedom into the interior, determined by the classical infall trajectory. We define the interior fidelity as

F_{I}(\psi)\;\equiv\;\bra{\psi}V^{\dagger}\,\rho_{I}(\psi)\,V\ket{\psi}\,.

(5)

Operationally,
F_{I}(\psi)
is the probability that an interior observer, performing the projective measurement
\{V\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}V^{\dagger},\mathbb{1}_{I}-V\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}V^{\dagger}\}
, correctly identifies the infalling state.
F_{I}=1
corresponds to perfect preservation of quantum state across the horizon (the equivalence principle at the quantum level), while
F_{I}<1
signals disruption – a firewall, fuzzball structure, or other modification of the horizon geometry.

Exterior distinguishability.—
The maximum ability of an exterior observer to distinguish two infalling states is captured by the trace distance between the corresponding exterior reduced states. We define

D_{\max}\;\equiv\;\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ket{\psi},\ket{\psi'}\in\mathscr{H}_{F}\\
\text{same }(M,Q,J)\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2}\bigl\lVert\rho_{E}(\psi)-\rho_{E}(\psi^{\prime})\bigr\rVert_{1}\,,

(6)

where the maximization is over all pairs of infalling states carrying identical conserved charges. Here
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1}
denotes the trace norm. By the Holevo-Helstrom theorem
18
,
D_{\max}
equals the maximum bias in the optimal single-shot discrimination of any two same-charge infalling states from exterior measurement alone.
D_{\max}=0
is the no-hair condition;
D_{\max}>0
indicates exterior quantum hair.

Assumptions.
—Our results require three assumptions, all standard in semiclassical black hole physics:

(A1) Unitarity.
The evolution
U
in Eq. (
3
) is unitary.

(A2) Horizon causality.
After
F
crosses the horizon, the interior-
I
is causally disconnected from the exterior-
E
: no signal propagates from
I
to
E
.

(A3) Interior accessibility.
Let
\mathscr{N}_{I}(\rho)=\mathrm{Tr}_{E}\bigl[U(\rho\otimes\outerproduct{\Phi_0}{\Phi_0}_{\mathrm{BH}})U^{\dagger}\bigr]
be the quantum channel mapping the infalling state to the interior, and let
\mathscr{V}_{I}(\rho)=V\rho V^{\dagger}
be the ideal isometric channel representing perfect classical infall. We assume:

\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl\lVert\mathscr{N}_{I}-\mathscr{V}_{I}\bigr\rVert_{\diamond}\;\leq\;\varepsilon\,,

(7)

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\diamond}
denotes the diamond norm
26
. This is the equivalence principle elevated to a rigorous operational constraint:
\varepsilon
quantifies the worst-case probability that any physical process, including one exploiting entanglement with an external reference, can distinguish the actual horizon crossing from a perfect isometry. By the Fuchs–van de Graaf inequality
14
, the diamond-norm parameter
\varepsilon
controls the worst-case interior fidelity:

\varepsilon\;\geq\;1-\min_{\ket{\psi}\in\mathscr{H}_{F}}F_{I}(\psi)\,,

(8)

so the technical parameter
\varepsilon
directly bounds the physical quantity
F_{I}
defined in Eq. (
5

\varepsilon=0
corresponds to exact horizon smoothness (
F_{I}=1
for all
\ket{\psi}
),
\varepsilon\sim e^{-S_{\mathrm{BH}}}
to nonperturbative quantum-gravitational corrections, and
\varepsilon\sim\mathscr{O}(1)
to a firewall

(see Supplemental Material

S2

Results.—

Exact no-hair lemma.—
We first establish the exact case, which contains the conceptual core of our result.

Lemma-1.
Under (A1)–(A3) with
\varepsilon=0
(i.e., perfect equivalence principle):

\rho_{E}(\psi)\;=\;\rho_{E}\quad\text{(independent of }\psi\text{)}.

(9)

Proof.—
Since
\varepsilon=0
, the interior channel
\mathscr{N}_{I}
coincides with the isometry
\mathscr{V}_{I}
, giving
\rho_{I}(\psi)=V\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}V^{\dagger}
, which is pure (See Supplemental Material
31
, Sec. (
S1
)). Because the global state
\ket{\Psi(\psi)}
is pure and its reduction to
I
is pure, the Schmidt decomposition has a single term:

\ket{\Psi(\psi)}\;=\;V\ket{\psi}_{I}\otimes\ket{\chi(\psi)}_{E}\,.

(10)

Unitarity preserves inner products:
\expectationvalue{\Psi(\psi)|\Psi(\psi')}=\expectationvalue{\psi|\psi'}
. Substituting Eq. (
10
) and using
V^{\dagger}V=\mathbb{1}_{F}
gives

\expectationvalue{\psi|\psi'}\,\expectationvalue{\chi(\psi)|\chi(\psi')}\;=\;\expectationvalue{\psi|\psi'}\,.

(11)

For any non-orthogonal pair (
\expectationvalue{\psi|\psi'}\neq 0
), dividing yields
\expectationvalue{\chi(\psi)|\chi(\psi')}=1
, so
\ket{\chi(\psi)}=\ket{\chi(\psi')}
. Extension to orthogonal pairs proceeds by choosing
\ket{\psi_+}=(\ket{\psi_0}+\ket{\psi_1})/\sqrt{2}
, which has nonzero overlap with both
\ket{\psi_0}
and
\ket{\psi_1}
, giving
\ket{\chi(\psi_0)}=\ket{\chi(\psi_+)}=\ket{\chi(\psi_1)}
. Therefore
\ket{\chi(\psi)}=\ket{\chi}
for all
\ket{\psi}
, and
\rho_{E}=\outerproduct{\chi}{\chi}
is state-independent.
\square

We note that Lemma 1 is complementary to the no-hiding theorem
6
, which establishes that information absent from a subsystem must reside in correlations. Our results address the converse situation: when information is fully retained by the interior, it is necessarily absent from the exterior.

Approximate no-hair theorem.—
In any physical setting, horizon scale quantum gravitational corrections will cause the interior channel to depart from perfect isometry. The following theorem quantifies the continuity of Lemma 1 using the information-disturbance tradeoff for quantum channels
23
.

Theorem.
Let
\mathscr{N}_{I}
and
\mathscr{V}_{I}
be as in (A3), and let
\mathscr{N}_{E}
be the complementary channel of
\mathscr{N}_{I}
, defined via the Stinespring dilation
U
. Under (A1)–(A3):

D_{\max}\;\leq\;2\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\,.

(12)

Proof Sketch
(full proof in Supplemental Material
31
, Sec (
S1
)).—We invoke the Kretschmann-Schlingemann-Werner (KSW) continuity theorem
23
, which establishes that if two quantum channels are close in diamond norm, their complementary channels are also close up to a partial isometry on the environment.

Step 1: Identify the ideal complementary channel.
The ideal interior channel
\mathscr{V}_{I}(\rho)=V\rho V^{\dagger}
is a pure isometry. Its complementary channel
\mathscr{V}_{E}
maps every input to a fixed state:

\mathscr{V}_{E}(\rho)\;=\;\outerproduct{\sigma}{\sigma}_{E_{0}}\quad\forall\;\rho\,,

(13)

since the environment of a pure isometry carries no information about the input. This is the channel-level expression of Lemma 1 at
\varepsilon=0

Step 2: Apply the KSW bound.
By assumption (A3),
\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathscr{N}_{I}-\mathscr{V}_{I}\rVert_{\diamond}\leq\varepsilon
. The KSW theorem guarantees the existence of a partial isometry
W:\mathscr{H}_{E_{0}}\to\mathscr{H}_{E}
such that

\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl\lVert\mathscr{N}_{E}-\mathscr{W}\circ\mathscr{V}_{E}\bigr\rVert_{\diamond}\;\leq\;\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\,,

(14)

where
\mathscr{W}(\cdot)=W(\cdot)W^{\dagger}
. The composite channel
\mathscr{W}\circ\mathscr{V}_{E}
maps every input to the fixed state
\tilde{\sigma}_{E}\equiv W\outerproduct{\sigma}{\sigma}W^{\dagger}
, which is independent of the infalling state
\ket{\psi}
.

Step 3: Bound the exterior distinguishability.
Since the diamond norm bounds the trace distance for any specific input
26
,
\frac{1}{2}\lVert\rho_{E}(\psi)-\tilde{\sigma}_{E}\rVert_{1}\leq\sqrt{2\varepsilon}
for every
\ket{\psi}
. For any two infalling states
\ket{\psi}
and
\ket{\psi'}
with the same conserved charges, the triangle inequality gives

\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl\lVert\rho_{E}(\psi)-\rho_{E}(\psi^{\prime})\bigr\rVert_{1}\;\leq\;2\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\,.

(15)

Maximizing over all same-charge pairs yields Eq. (
12
).
\square

Trade-off inequality.—
Inverting Eq. (
12
), we obtain the central constraint of this letter:

\boxed{\varepsilon\geq\frac{D_{\max}^{2}}{8}}

(16)

Interpretation:
Any nonzero exterior distinguishability (
D_{\max}>0
) implies that the interior channel fidelity deviates from a perfect isometry (
\varepsilon>0
). Observable quantum hair is therefore quantitatively incompatible with exact horizon smoothness.

The parameter
\varepsilon
simultaneously controls both the exterior distinguishability (via
D_{\max}\leq 2\sqrt{2\varepsilon}
) and the worst-case interior fidelity (via
F_{I}\geq 1-\varepsilon
from Eq. (
8
)). In particular,
\varepsilon=0
enforces both
D_{\max}=0
and
F_{I}=1
, while
\varepsilon>0
permits nonzero hair and reduced fidelity in a manner governed by Eq. (
16
). This is the quantitative expression of the claim that the no-cloning principle, acting through unitarity, enforces the no-hair property.
Its logical structure parallels Bell’s inequality
4
: just as a violation of Bell’s bound implies the failure of local realism, a nonzero
D_{\max}
implies, via Eq. (
16
), a quantifiable failure of exact horizon smoothness.

Consequences.-

Classical no-hair recovered.—
Setting
\varepsilon=0
in Lemma 1 yields
D_{\max}=0
exactly: an exterior observer with no prior entanglement with
F
cannot distinguish any two same-charge infalling states. The only exterior observables with non-zero distinguishing power are Gauss-law charges
(M,Q,J)
, which are excluded from our framework by construction (we restrict same-charge pairs). This recovers the classical no-hair theorem without invoking Israel-Carter-Robinson uniqueness theorems or Einstein field equations.

For semiclassical black holes with Planck-suppressed corrections
\varepsilon\sim e^{-\alpha S_{\mathrm{BH}}}
, the bound gives
D_{\max}\leq 2\sqrt{2}\,e^{-\alpha S_{\mathrm{BH}}/2}
, which is non-perturbatively small for macroscopic black holes. Classical no-hair is thus a robust consequence of unitarity, not a fine-tuned feature of the field equations.

Constraint on fuzzball and microstate geometry program.—
The fuzzball proposal
25
;
5
replaces the event horizon with horizonless microstate geometries that are, in principle, individually distinguishable from the exterior. If such microstates achieve
D_{\max}>0
, Eq.(
16
) requires

\varepsilon\;\geq\;D_{\max}^{2}/8\,.

(17)

A fuzzball that is distinguishable at level
D_{\max}
must cause a departure from perfect isometric infall of at least
\varepsilon\geq D_{\max}^{2}/8
. This is consistent with the fuzzball philosophy, which explicitly replaces the smooth horizon with structure, but Eq. (
17
) makes the trade-off
quantitative
and model-independent.

Constraint on soft-hair.—
The soft hair program
16
proposes that supertranslation charges on the horizon encode black hole microstates. If soft hair achieves
D_{\max}>0
for same-charge states without requiring pre-existing entanglement, Eq. (
16
) implies
\varepsilon>0
: the interior channel cannot be a perfect isometry. One cannot simultaneously maintain soft hair that distinguishes microstates and a smooth horizon that preserves the infalling vacuum.

Entanglement as the unique unitarity-compatible hair.—
The bound Eq. (
12
) and the trade-off inequality Eq. (
16
) apply when the infalling state
\ket{\psi}
is pure. When
F
is entangled with an exterior reference
R
prior to infall, the input to the horizon-crossing channel is mixed, and the product structure Eq. (
10
) does not follow. In this case, observables on
\mathscr{H}_{E}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{R}
can achieve
D>0
without requiring
\varepsilon>0
(see Supplemental Material
31
, Sec. (
S4
) for explicit bounds). We emphasize that this distinguishability arises from
preserved pre-existing
information about
F
on the reference
R
, information that was always external to the black hole, rather than from new exterior hair generated by horizon crossing itself; the joint
(E,R)
state in fact factorizes at
\varepsilon=0
with no infall-induced
E
–
R
correlations.

Corollary.
Pre-existing entanglement is the unique form of quantum hair that is simultaneously (i) accessible without exponential computational cost, (ii) compatible with a smooth horizon, and (iii) consistent with unitarity.

Harlow-Hayden as a no-cloning consequence.—
The Hayden-Preskill mechanism
17
shows that, after the scrambling time, infalling information is encoded in the exterior radiation in a delocalized form. Recovering this information requires exponential computational resources
15
. Our result provides an independent, information-theoretic basis: any efficient exterior protocol achieving
D_{\max}>0
with
\varepsilon\approx 0
would violate Eq. (
16
). The exponential complexity of decoding is therefore not merely a computational difficulty but a structural requirement of unitarity.

Discussion.-

From qualitative to quantitative complementarity.—
Black hole complementarity
32
argues qualitatively that no-cloning prevents simultaneous access to interior and exterior information. Our trade-off inequality Eq. (
16
) makes this quantitative: exterior distinguishability
D_{\max}
and interior-channel fidelity
\varepsilon
are constrained by an explicit inequality under unitarity. This converts the principle ”no observer sees both copies” into the operational bound ”any nonzero exterior distinguishability requires a quantifiable departure from perfect horizon smoothness.”

Universality.—
The classical no-hair theorem is specific to Einstein-Maxwell equations in
3{+}1
dimensions. Our results hold in any semiclassical theory of gravity satisfying (A1)–(A3): unitarity, horizon causality, and interior accessibility. This includes higher-dimensional black holes, black holes in string theory, and black objects with additional gauge fields. The bound is purely information-theoretic and independent of the dynamical equations governing gravity.

Scope and limitations.—
Our framework operates in the semiclassical regime where the tensor factorization
\mathscr{H}_{I}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{E}
holds to leading order in
1/S_{\rm BH}

12
;
34
, comparable to our bound for Planck-suppressed
\varepsilon
. Our theorem should therefore be understood as exact within the semiclassical regime. We emphasize that we do not address the information paradox, the Page curve, or the dynamics of Hawking evaporation. Our result concerns the
instantaneous
relationship between interior fidelity and exterior distinguishability at fixed semiclassical time after the infall, not the time-dependent retrieval of information during evaporation.

Tightness of the bound.—
The
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
scaling in Eq. (
12
) is inherited from the KSW continuity theorem
23
, which is tight for general channels. All explicit examples we have examined satisfy the stronger scaling
D_{\max}=\mathscr{O}(\varepsilon)
, indicating that the prefactor
2\sqrt{2}
can likely be improved for the specific structure of horizon-crossing channels (see Supplemental Material
31
, Sec. (
S1.5
) for discussion). The qualitative content of the trade-off—the mutual exclusivity of
D_{\max}>0
and
\varepsilon=0
—is independent of the precise constant.

Outlook.
—Several extensions are natural: (i) deriving analogous bounds for cosmological horizons in de Sitter space, where the causal structure is similar but the thermodynamics differs; (ii) connecting the entanglement-witness escape route to the asymptotic symmetry program; (iii) exploring whether the bound tightens or loosens as the black hole evaporates past the Page time. We leave these for future work.

S1.1
Purity of the interior state at

The proof of Lemma 1 in the main text relies on the statement that when assumption (A3) holds with
\varepsilon=0

Recall the diamond-norm assumption (A3):
\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathscr{N}_{I}-\mathscr{V}_{I}\rVert_{\diamond}\leq\varepsilon

\mathscr{V}_{I}(\rho)=V\rho V^{\dagger}
is the ideal isometric infall channel. When
\varepsilon=0
:

\lVert\mathscr{N}_{I}-\mathscr{V}_{I}\rVert_{\diamond}=0\,,

(S1)

which implies
\mathscr{N}_{I}=\mathscr{V}_{I}
as quantum channels. Consequently, for any pure input
\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}

\rho_{I}(\psi)\;=\;\mathscr{N}_{I}\bigl(\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}\bigr)\;=\;\mathscr{V}_{I}\bigl(\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}\bigr)\;=\;V\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}V^{\dagger}\,.

(S2)

V\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}V^{\dagger}
is rank one and pure (since
V
is an isometry,
V\ket{\psi}
is a unit vector in
\mathscr{H}_{I}
). This is the statement used in the proof of Lemma 1.

For later use we also note that, by the Fuchs–van de Graaf inequality

14
, the diamond-norm assumption controls the worst-case interior fidelity:

\varepsilon\;\geq\;\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl\lVert\mathscr{N}_{I}(\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi})-V\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}V^{\dagger}\bigr\rVert_{1}\;\geq\;1-F_{I}(\psi)\quad\forall\,\ket{\psi}\in\mathscr{H}_{F}\,,

(S3)

so that
\varepsilon\geq 1-\min_{\psi}F_{I}(\psi)
. Equation (

\varepsilon
is a
stronger
characterization of horizon smoothness than the worst-case interior fidelity
F_{I}
, in the sense that
\varepsilon=0\Rightarrow F_{I}=1
(and vice versa) but for
\varepsilon>0
the diamond norm bounds also entanglement-assisted distinguishability of the channel from a perfect isometry.

S1.2
The Kretschmann–Schlingemann–Werner theorem

We state the precise form of the continuity result we invoke. The following is Theorem 1 of
23
, specialized to our setting and stated in the convention
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\diamond}
for the completely bounded trace norm (diamond norm) of a Hermiticity-preserving super-operator.

Theorem (Kretschmann, Schlingemann, Werner, 2008).
Let
\mathscr{T}_{1},\mathscr{T}_{2}:\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H}_{A})\to\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H}_{B})
be two quantum channels with Stinespring dilations
V_{i}:\mathscr{H}_{A}\to\mathscr{H}_{B}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{E}
(
i=1,2
) sharing a common environment
\mathscr{H}_{E}
(this can always be arranged by enlarging the environment). Let
\widetilde{\mathscr{T}}_{i}:\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H}_{A})\to\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H}_{E})
, defined by
\widetilde{\mathscr{T}}_{i}(\rho)=\mathrm{Tr}_{B}[V_{i}\rho V_{i}^{\dagger}]
, denote the corresponding complementary channels. Then there exists a partial isometry
W
on
\mathscr{H}_{E}
such that

\bigl\lVert\widetilde{\mathscr{T}}_{1}-\mathrm{Ad}_{W}\circ\widetilde{\mathscr{T}}_{2}\bigr\rVert_{\diamond}\;\leq\;2\sqrt{\bigl\lVert\mathscr{T}_{1}-\mathscr{T}_{2}\bigr\rVert_{\diamond}}\,,

(S4)

where
\mathrm{Ad}_{W}(\rho)\equiv W\rho W^{\dagger}
.

The interpretation is as follows: if two channels are close in diamond norm, then their complementary channels are close up to a unitary (or partial-isometric) reshuffling of the environment. The square-root in Eq. (
S4
) is essential and reflects the cost of dualizing between channels and complementary channels via the Stinespring representation.

For our purposes, two consequences of Eq. (

S1.3
The horizon-crossing channel and its complementary channel

We now construct the actual and ideal horizon channels in a common Stinespring picture and identify their complementary channels.

Actual channel.
Let
U:\mathscr{H}_{F}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{BH}}\to\mathscr{H}_{I}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{E}
denote the unitary that implements horizon crossing in the global Hilbert space, and let
\ket{\Phi_0}_{\mathrm{BH}}
denote the initial black hole state. The Stinespring isometry of the actual interior channel is

W_{\mathscr{N}}:\mathscr{H}_{F}\to\mathscr{H}_{I}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{E}\,,\qquad W_{\mathscr{N}}\ket{\psi}\;=\;U\bigl(\ket{\psi}_{F}\otimes\ket{\Phi_0}_{\mathrm{BH}}\bigr)\,,

(S5)

which is an isometry by virtue of
U

\ket{\Phi_0}
being a fixed unit vector. The interior channel is

\mathscr{N}_{I}(\rho)\;=\;\mathrm{Tr}_{E}\bigl[W_{\mathscr{N}}\rho W_{\mathscr{N}}^{\dagger}\bigr]\,,

(S6)

and its complementary channel is

\mathscr{N}_{E}(\rho)\;=\;\mathrm{Tr}_{I}\bigl[W_{\mathscr{N}}\rho W_{\mathscr{N}}^{\dagger}\bigr]\;=\;\rho_{E}(\psi)\quad\text{for }\rho=\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}\,,

(S7)

which is the exterior reduced-state map appearing in the main text.

Ideal channel.
The ideal interior channel is the isometric embedding
\mathscr{V}_{I}(\rho)=V\rho V^{\dagger}
with
V:\mathscr{H}_{F}\hookrightarrow\mathscr{H}_{I}
. To put
\mathscr{V}_{I}
on the same Stinespring footing as
\mathscr{N}_{I}

\ket{\sigma}_{E}\in\mathscr{H}_{E}

W_{\mathscr{V}}:\mathscr{H}_{F}\to\mathscr{H}_{I}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{E}\,,\qquad W_{\mathscr{V}}\ket{\psi}\;=\;V\ket{\psi}_{I}\otimes\ket{\sigma}_{E}\,.

(S8)

This is also an isometry (since
V^{\dagger}V=\mathbb{1}_{F}
and
\expectationvalue{\sigma|\sigma}=1
). The induced interior channel is

\mathrm{Tr}_{E}\bigl[W_{\mathscr{V}}\rho W_{\mathscr{V}}^{\dagger}\bigr]\;=\;V\rho V^{\dagger}\cdot\mathrm{Tr}_{E}[\ket{\sigma}\bra{\sigma}]\;=\;V\rho V^{\dagger}\;=\;\mathscr{V}_{I}(\rho)\,,

(S9)

as required, and the complementary channel is

\mathscr{V}_{E}(\rho)\;=\;\mathrm{Tr}_{I}\bigl[W_{\mathscr{V}}\rho W_{\mathscr{V}}^{\dagger}\bigr]\;=\;\mathrm{Tr}[V\rho V^{\dagger}]\cdot\ket{\sigma}\bra{\sigma}_{E}\;=\;\mathrm{Tr}[\rho]\cdot\ket{\sigma}\bra{\sigma}_{E}\,.

(S10)

On density operators (
\mathrm{Tr}[\rho]=1
),
\mathscr{V}_{E}(\rho)=\ket{\sigma}\bra{\sigma}_{E}
for all
\rho
. This is the channel-level statement of Lemma 1: in the ideal case, the exterior carries no information about the input.

S1.5
Tightness and conjectured improvements

The bound
D_{\max}\leq 2\sqrt{2\varepsilon}
inherits its
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
scaling from the KSW continuity theorem, which is tight up to constants in the most general channel setting
23
. The constant
2\sqrt{2}
, however, is generally not tight for the specific structure of horizon-crossing channels, where the ideal channel is an exact isometry (for which the complementary channel is perfectly degenerate).

Examples.
We have examined the following families of explicit channels in which the ideal channel is the identity (an isometry from
\mathbb{C}^{d}
to
\mathbb{C}^{d}
):

1.

Depolarizing channel.

\mathscr{N}_{I}(\rho)=(1-p)\rho+p\,\mathbb{1}/d
. The diamond-norm distance from the identity is proportional to
p
, and direct computation of the complementary channel yields exterior distinguishability that scales
linearly
in
p
, hence linearly in
\varepsilon
.

2.

Dephasing channel.

\mathscr{N}_{I}(\rho)=(1-p)\rho+p\sum_{k}\ket{k}\bra{k}\rho\ket{k}\bra{k}
for an orthonormal basis
\{\ket{k}\}
. Both the diamond-norm distance from identity and the resulting exterior distinguishability are linear in
p
.

3.

Amplitude damping channel
(qubit).
\mathscr{N}_{I}
is parametrized by
\gamma\in[0,1]
and corresponds to spontaneous emission. For small
\gamma
, the diamond-norm distance from identity is
\mathscr{O}(\gamma)
, and the complementary channel produces exterior distinguishability of the same order.

In all examples, the actual scaling is
D_{\max}=\mathscr{O}(\varepsilon)
, linear in
\varepsilon
, while our proven bound is
D_{\max}=\mathscr{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
. This suggests that the prefactor
2\sqrt{2}
in our bound can be improved, possibly to a linear-in-
\varepsilon
statement, by a sharper analysis specific to the case where one channel is an exact isometry.

Open question.
Whether the linear scaling
D_{\max}=\mathscr{O}(\varepsilon)
observed in these finite-dimensional examples persists for general channels, particularly in the infinite-dimensional operator algebras relevant to quantum field theory in curved spacetime, remains an open question. The
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
scaling proven in our theorem is known to be tight for general channels in the KSW framework
23
, and we do not claim that a universal linear bound holds beyond the specific examples above. The qualitative content of the trade-off (any nonzero
D_{\max}
) requires a nonzero
\varepsilon
is independent of whether the bound can be further improved.

S2
Justification of the Assumptions

We discuss each of the three assumptions (A1)–(A3) in turn, with emphasis on the diamond-norm formulation of (A3), which is the most technical and the most important for the validity of our results in realistic black hole models.

S2.1
Assumption (A1): Unitarity

Assumption (A1) requires that the global evolution operator
U
appearing in Eq. (3) of the main text is unitary on
\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{H}_{I}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{E}
. This is a standard assumption in any quantum-mechanical treatment of black hole physics. It is required by AdS/CFT
24
, by complementarity
32
, by the AMPS argument
2
, by the
Page curve calculations of
27
;
1
, and by all modern unitary approaches to black hole evaporation. The only context in which (A1) is genuinely contested is older information-loss proposals, which we do not consider.

S2.2
Assumption (A2): Horizon causality

Assumption (A2) states that, after the infalling system
F
crosses the event horizon, the interior region
I
is causally disconnected from the exterior
E
. Operationally, no signal carrying information about the interior can be sent to the exterior at the level of semiclassical effective field theory.

This is the defining property of an event horizon in classical general relativity. Quantum-mechanically, it is the statement that, to leading order in
1/S_{\mathrm{BH}}
, the bulk effective field theory in the exterior is independent of operators acting on the interior alone. This is the form of bulk locality that survives in the semiclassical regime and is implicit in the Stinespring picture we adopt: the channel
\mathscr{N}_{I}
takes the input only into the interior factor, with no exterior dependence on
\rho
beyond what the unitary
U
produces through the entanglement structure of the output. We emphasize that (A2) is not violated by Hayden-Preskill-type information recovery from the exterior radiation
17
, which operates on a much longer (scrambling) timescale and at exponential computational complexity; (A2) is the statement of causality at the level of the single horizon-crossing event.

S2.3
Assumption (A3): Diamond-norm interior accessibility

This is the most technical and the most consequential of our assumptions. It states that the actual interior channel
\mathscr{N}_{I}
is
\varepsilon
-close, in the diamond norm, to the ideal isometric infall channel
\mathscr{V}_{I}
:

\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert\mathscr{N}_{I}-\mathscr{V}_{I}\rVert_{\diamond}\;\leq\;\varepsilon\,.

(S16)

We comment on (i) why the diamond norm is the appropriate notion of distance, (ii) the relation to the more elementary fidelity-based formulation
F_{I}\geq 1-\varepsilon
, and (iii) physical estimates of
\varepsilon
in different theoretical scenarios.

Why the diamond norm.
The diamond norm
26
;
33
is the operationally correct measure of channel distinguishability. It is defined as

\bigl\lVert\mathscr{T}\bigr\rVert_{\diamond}\;\equiv\;\sup_{d\geq 1}\;\sup_{\rho}\;\bigl\lVert(\mathscr{T}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{d})(\rho)\bigr\rVert_{1}\,,

(S17)

where the inner supremum is over all density operators
\rho
on
\mathscr{H}_{F}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
and the outer supremum is over the dimension
d
of an arbitrary auxiliary system. The supremum is achieved at
d=\dim\mathscr{H}_{F}
for finite-dimensional input spaces.

The operational meaning is the following:
\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathscr{N}_{I}-\mathscr{V}_{I}\rVert_{\diamond}
is the maximum probability with which any single-shot quantum experiment—possibly using auxiliary entanglement with an external reference—can distinguish the actual horizon-crossing channel from a perfect isometry. Adopting the diamond-norm formulation of (A3) ensures that our results are robust to entanglement-assisted protocols, which is the right notion of an operationally meaningful equivalence principle.

Relation to interior fidelity.
The state-level formulation
F_{I}(\psi)\geq 1-\varepsilon
for all pure
\ket{\psi}
is
weaker
than the diamond-norm assumption: it bounds only the fidelity on pure-state inputs and does not control the channel’s behavior on entangled inputs. By the Fuchs–van de Graaf inequality
14
,

1-F_{I}(\psi)\;\leq\;\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert\mathscr{N}_{I}(\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi})-V\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}V^{\dagger}\rVert_{1}\;\leq\;\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert\mathscr{N}_{I}-\mathscr{V}_{I}\rVert_{\diamond}\;\leq\;\varepsilon\,,

(S18)

so
\varepsilon\geq 1-F_{I}(\psi)
for every
\psi
, which is Eq. (8) of the main text. The converse implication—from a fidelity bound to a diamond-norm bound—requires an additional dimensional factor and is generally weaker. Our use of the diamond-norm formulation is therefore the conservative choice: it gives a stronger trade-off inequality at the cost of a slightly more demanding assumption on the interior channel.

Physical estimates of
\varepsilon
.
The numerical value of
\varepsilon
depends on the underlying theory of quantum gravity. We list four representative regimes:

1.

Classical general relativity (
\varepsilon=0
).
The classical description of horizon crossing involves no horizon-scale structure: a freely falling test body crosses the horizon as a geodesic, and its quantum state is unaffected. In this idealized regime
\mathscr{N}_{I}=\mathscr{V}_{I}
exactly.

2.

Semiclassical quantum field theory in curved spacetime (
\varepsilon\sim e^{-S_{\mathrm{BH}}}
).
Once quantum effects are included, the bulk effective field theory is expected to receive nonperturbative corrections of order
e^{-S_{\mathrm{BH}}}
from processes such as gravitational instantons and spacetime topology change. While we are not aware of a direct calculation establishing the size of such corrections to the interior channel, dimensional analysis and the general structure of nonperturbative bulk effects suggest that, in this regime,
\varepsilon
is parametrically small but not exactly zero. Our results in this regime should be understood as a structural statement rather than a precise estimate of the corresponding
D_{\max}
.

3.

Microstate geometries / fuzzballs (
\varepsilon=\mathscr{O}(1)
).
In the fuzzball proposal
25
, the classical horizon is replaced by horizonless microstate geometries that differ from one another at the would-be horizon scale. Different microstates can, in principle, be distinguished by exterior probes, and the infalling state experiences nontrivial interactions with the fuzzball structure. In this regime,
\varepsilon
is of order unity.

4.

Firewall scenario (
\varepsilon=\mathscr{O}(1)
).
The AMPS argument
2
suggests that, for old (post-Page-time) black holes, the equivalence principle must fail at the horizon in the form of a high-energy “firewall.” An infalling observer experiences violent disruption, and the interior channel deviates substantially from a perfect isometry. Again
\varepsilon=\mathscr{O}(1)
.

Our trade-off inequality (16) treats
\varepsilon
as a free parameter and applies in all four regimes. Its physical content is most striking in the third and fourth, where it provides a quantitative constraint linking the magnitude of horizon-scale disruption to the exterior distinguishability of microstates.

S3
Implications for Quantum Gravity Programs

In this section, we elaborate on the constraints that the trade-off inequality
\varepsilon\geq D_{\max}^{2}/8
places on three major proposals for the quantum structure of black holes.

S3.1
Microstate geometries and the fuzzball program

The fuzzball proposal
25
;
5
asserts that black hole microstates correspond to horizonless geometries constructed in supergravity. Different microstates differ from each other near the would-be horizon scale, and from the conventional black hole geometry. A key prediction of the program is that exterior observations can, at least in principle, distinguish one microstate from another, so that the macroscopic black hole carries quantum hair of order
\log N\sim S_{\mathrm{BH}}
bits.

If the microstates are individually distinguishable, then
D_{\max}
can be appreciable: pure microstates orthogonal in the microstate basis would yield
D_{\max}\to 1
. Our trade-off then forces

\varepsilon\;\geq\;\frac{D_{\max}^{2}}{8}\,.

(S19)

For
D_{\max}=\mathscr{O}(1)
, this implies
\varepsilon=\mathscr{O}(1)
: the fuzzball microstate geometry must cause a substantial deviation of the interior channel from a perfect isometry. This is consistent with the fuzzball philosophy
25
, which explicitly replaces the smooth horizon with structure, but Eq. (
S19
) elevates the qualitative “no smooth horizon” statement to a quantitative lower bound on the magnitude of the disruption.

We note that this constraint applies whether or not the microstate is distinguishable in practice. The relevant
D_{\max}
is the in-principle distinguishability of the exterior states, which is governed by the geometry and not by the available technology. Our trade-off, therefore, sharpens the prediction of the fuzzball program: any candidate microstate construction whose exterior geometries are even infinitesimally distinguishable must exhibit a corresponding deviation
\varepsilon>0
at the horizon scale.

S3.2
Soft hair

The soft hair proposal of Hawking, Perry, and Strominger
16
asserts that infinitely many supertranslation charges live on the horizon and store information about the matter that fell in. If two black holes formed from infalling matter with different values of these soft charges, but identical conserved
(M,Q,J)
are distinguishable to an asymptotic observer, then
D_{\max}>0
for the corresponding pair of infalling states.

Our trade-off then implies

\varepsilon\;\geq\;\frac{D_{\max}^{2}}{8}\;>\;0\,.

(S20)

This is a no-go for “free” soft hair: one cannot have soft supertranslation charges that distinguish infalling states without incurring a corresponding cost in the smoothness of the horizon. The familiar “soft hair complementarity” that proposes both soft hair and a smooth horizon is incompatible with the trade-off, unless the soft hair is degenerate in the sense that distinct infalling states generate identical soft charges (in which case
D_{\max}=0
, and soft hair carries no information about the microstate).

S3.3
Black hole complementarity

The complementarity hypothesis of Susskind, Thorlacius, and Uglum
32
proposes that the description of the black hole from the exterior and from the interior is complementary in the sense that no single observer can access both simultaneously. In our framework, complementarity corresponds to the regime
\varepsilon=0
,
D_{\max}=0
: a perfectly smooth horizon that admits an infalling observer to enter unscathed, with an exterior that carries no information about the infalling state.

This is consistent with our trade-off: the saturation occurs at
\varepsilon=D_{\max}=0
. Complementarity, therefore, corresponds to the “corner” of the allowed region, which we find is mathematically well-defined and self-consistent. Our result quantifies how complementarity
must
fail if either side of the trade-off is relaxed: any nonzero exterior distinguishability comes at the cost of horizon smoothness, and any nonzero horizon disruption permits up to
2\sqrt{2\varepsilon}
worth of exterior distinguishability.

S4
Entanglement Witness Analysis

In the main text, we noted that the trade-off inequality Eq. (16) applies when the infalling state
\ket{\psi}
is pure, and that the situation changes when
F
is entangled with an exterior reference
R
prior to infall. This section provides a careful analysis of the entangled case, showing that pre-existing entanglement does not generate new exterior hair through horizon crossing, but does preserve pre-existing exterior accessibility of information about
F
.

S4.1
Setup and notation

Let
R
be an external quantum system (the “reference”) with Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}_{R}
, and let
\ket{\Phi}_{FR}\in\mathscr{H}_{F}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{R}
be a pure entangled state with Schmidt decomposition

\ket{\Phi}_{FR}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\,\ket{i}_{F}\otimes\ket{i}_{R}\,,

(S21)

where
\{\ket{i}_{F}\}
and
\{\ket{i}_{R}\}
are orthonormal bases of
\mathscr{H}_{F}
and the relevant subspace of
\mathscr{H}_{R}
, and
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}=1
with
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
. The reference
R
remains causally and dynamically disconnected from the black hole throughout: the global time evolution is
U\otimes\mathbb{1}_{R}
, where
U
is the horizon-crossing unitary defined in Eq. (
S5
).

After horizon crossing, the global state is

\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm{tot}}}\;=\;\bigl(U\otimes\mathbb{1}_{R}\bigr)\bigl(\ket{\Phi}_{FR}\otimes\ket{\Phi_0}_{\mathrm{BH}}\bigr)\;=\;\sum_{i}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\,\bigl(W_{\mathscr{N}}\ket{i}\bigr)_{IE}\otimes\ket{i}_{R}\,,

(S22)

where
W_{\mathscr{N}}
is the actual Stinespring isometry of Eq. (
S5
).

S4.2
Exact case:
\varepsilon=0

When
\varepsilon=0
, Lemma 1 of the main text gives
W_{\mathscr{N}}\ket{i}=V\ket{i}_{I}\otimes\ket{\chi}_{E}
for a single,
i
-independent state
\ket{\chi}
. Substituting into
Eq. (
S22
):

\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm{tot}}}\;=\;\biggl(\sum_{i}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\,V\ket{i}_{I}\otimes\ket{i}_{R}\biggr)\otimes\ket{\chi}_{E}\;\equiv\;\ket{\Omega}_{IR}\otimes\ket{\chi}_{E}\,.

(S23)

The global state factorizes as
\ket{\Omega}_{IR}\otimes\ket{\chi}_{E}
. The exterior
E
is in a pure product state
\ket{\chi}
, completely uncorrelated with both
I
and
R
.

Tracing over the interior gives

\rho_{ER}(\Phi)\;=\;\mathrm{Tr}_{I}\bigl[\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm{tot}}}\bra{\Psi_{\mathrm{tot}}}\bigr]\;=\;\ket{\chi}\bra{\chi}_{E}\otimes\rho_{R}(\Phi)\,,

(S24)

where
\rho_{R}(\Phi)=\mathrm{Tr}_{F}[\ket{\Phi}\bra{\Phi}_{FR}]=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\ket{i}\bra{i}_{R}
is the reduced state of
R
for the original entangled state
\ket{\Phi}_{FR}
. Crucially,
\rho_{R}(\Phi)
is the
same
state
R
would have had even
without
horizon crossing: it is determined entirely by the pre-infall preparation
\ket{\Phi}_{FR}
.

Implication.
The joint
(E,R)
state factorizes as a product. The exterior carries no information about the infalling state (as in Lemma 1), and there are no
E
-
R
correlations generated by horizon crossing. The reference
R
retains its information about the original state
\ket{\Phi}_{FR}
, but this information was never inside the black hole and is unaffected by infall.

In particular, two different entangled states
\ket{\Phi}_{FR}
and
\ket{\Phi'}_{FR}
with different reduced states
\rho_{R}\neq\rho_{R}^{\prime}
can be distinguished by an observer with access to
R
alone, and hence by any observer with access to
\mathscr{H}_{E}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{R}
. This is the precise sense in which “observables on
\mathscr{H}_{E}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{R}
can achieve
D>0
without requiring
\varepsilon>0
,” as stated in the main text below Eq. (16): the
D>0
comes entirely from the
R
factor, which retains pre-existing exterior accessibility of information about
F
. No new hair is generated by horizon crossing.

S4.3
Approximate case:
\varepsilon>0

When
\varepsilon>0
, Eq. (
S23
) no longer holds exactly, and the joint
(E,R)
state need not factorize. The KSW bound applied to the channel
\mathscr{N}_{I}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{R}
versus
\mathscr{V}_{I}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{R}
(or, equivalently, the diamond-norm bound
\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathscr{N}_{E}-\mathrm{Ad}_{W}\circ\mathscr{V}_{E}\rVert_{\diamond}\leq\sqrt{2\varepsilon}
established in Sec.
S1.4
, applied to inputs in
\mathscr{H}_{F}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{R}
) gives

\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl\lVert\rho_{ER}(\Phi)-\widetilde{\sigma}_{E}\otimes\rho_{R}(\Phi)\bigr\rVert_{1}\;\leq\;\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\,.

(S25)

That is, the joint
(E,R)
state is, up to a correction of order
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, a product of the input-independent exterior state
\widetilde{\sigma}_{E}
and the reference’s reduced state. The correction permits genuine
E
-
R
correlations of order
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
to develop through horizon crossing, but these are bounded by the same parameter
\varepsilon
that governs the trade-off inequality (16).

Quantitative bound for the entangled case.
Let two entangled states
\ket{\Phi},\ket{\Phi'}\in\mathscr{H}_{F}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{R}
be given, and let
D^{ER}_{\max}(\Phi,\Phi^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\lVert\rho_{ER}(\Phi)-\rho_{ER}(\Phi^{\prime})\rVert_{1}
. By Eq. (
S25
) and the triangle inequality (using
\widetilde{\sigma}_{E}\otimes\rho_{R}(\Phi)
and
\widetilde{\sigma}_{E}\otimes\rho_{R}(\Phi^{\prime})
as pivots),

D^{ER}_{\max}(\Phi,\Phi^{\prime})\;\leq\;2\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\;+\;\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl\lVert\rho_{R}(\Phi)-\rho_{R}(\Phi^{\prime})\bigr\rVert_{1}\,,

(S26)

where we have used the identity
\lVert\widetilde{\sigma}_{E}\otimes\rho_{R}-\widetilde{\sigma}_{E}\otimes\rho_{R}^{\prime}\rVert_{1}=\lVert\widetilde{\sigma}_{E}\rVert_{1}\cdot\lVert\rho_{R}-\rho_{R}^{\prime}\rVert_{1}=\lVert\rho_{R}-\rho_{R}^{\prime}\rVert_{1}
for normalized
\widetilde{\sigma}_{E}
. The first term is the new contribution from horizon crossing, bounded by the trade-off scale
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
. The second term is the pre-existing distinguishability of the reference, which is
not
bounded by
\varepsilon
and is determined solely by the preparation of the entangled state.

Equation (
S26
) makes precise the corollary in the main text: pre-existing entanglement provides exterior access to information about
F
via the reference
R
, but this information is not amplified or modified by horizon crossing (beyond a correction of order
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
). The trade-off inequality constrains the new exterior accessibility generated by the horizon crossing, while preserved pre-existing accessibility is excluded from the constraint.

S4.4
Summary

We summarize the role of pre-existing entanglement as follows:

•

In the exact case
\varepsilon=0
, entanglement does not generate any new exterior hair: the joint
(E,R)

The classical results restrict the geometry of a stationary vacuum or electrovacuum solutions; the present work derives a quantitative bound on exterior distinguishability from quantum information theory alone, applicable in any spacetime dimension and to any quantum gravity model satisfying assumptions (A1)–(A3).

The classical results are mathematically deep statements about the moduli space of solutions to specific field equations. They make no prediction about quantum hair, do not address the role of entanglement or computational complexity, and do not extend to higher-dimensional or non-Einstein gravities. Our result, by contrast, is a statement about quantum channels and their continuity properties; it applies to any quantum theory of gravity satisfying our three assumptions and explicitly characterizes the role of pre-existing entanglement and computational complexity in modulating the no-hair statement. The two approaches are complementary: the classical proofs constrain the geometry, while ours constrain the quantum information.

S6
Logical Structure: Comparison with Bell’s Theorem

The trade-off inequality (16) and Bell’s inequality
4
share a common logical architecture: both are conditional inequalities that constrain the joint values of two observables under a set of physical assumptions, and both are violated only if at least one assumption fails.

Bell’s theorem.
Under the assumptions of (i) locality, (ii) realism, and (iii) free choice, the correlations between two spacelike-separated measurements satisfy

\bigl|\langle AB\rangle+\langle AB^{\prime}\rangle+\langle A^{\prime}B\rangle-\langle A^{\prime}B^{\prime}\rangle\bigr|\;\leq\;2\,,

(S27)

where
A,A^{\prime},B,B^{\prime}
are dichotomic observables. Quantum mechanics predicts violations of Eq. (
S27
) up to
2\sqrt{2}

Trade-off inequality.
Under the assumptions of (A1) unitarity, (A2) horizon causality, and (A3) interior accessibility in the diamond norm, the exterior distinguishability and horizon-smoothness parameter satisfy

\varepsilon\;\geq\;\frac{D_{\max}^{2}}{8}\,.

(S28)

Differences.
Bell’s inequality is a statement about correlations between distant measurements on a fixed quantum state; the trade-off inequality is a statement about distinguishability of states under a fixed channel. Bell’s theorem is empirically tested by performing the relevant measurements; the trade-off inequality is, at present, not directly testable, because we have no way to control or measure the parameters
D_{\max}
and
\varepsilon
for a realistic black hole. Both, however, share the structural property that they convert a list of seemingly innocuous physical assumptions into a sharp quantitative constraint, and force any model violating the constraint to identify which assumption it sacrifices.
