The assessment of the intake of NOVA food groups among Jordanian adults
Authors: Lana M Agraib, Buthaina Alkhatib, Asem Taher Shawabkeh, Ahmad Suleiman Aslaih
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUltra-processed foods (UPFs) are becoming increasingly common in diets worldwide, raising growing concerns about their negative impacts on both human health and the environment.AimTo assess the contribution of unprocessed or minimally processed foods (UMPFs), UPFs, processed culinary ingredients (PCIs), and processed foods (PFs) to the daily energy intake among Jordanian adults.MethodologyA cross-sectional study was conducted involving a convenience sample of 537 Jordanian adults (19–64 years), who were assessed for dietary intake using a validated Arabic food frequency questionnaire (FFQ). Food items were classified according to the NOVA system, and energy contributions from UMPFs, PCIs, PFs, and UPFs were analyzed.ResultsAmong participants, UPFs were the main source of energy (51.93%), followed by UMPFs (31.05%), PCIs (9.32%), and PFs (7.74%). Males had higher total energy intake and consumed more UPFs and PFs than females, while females consumed more UMPFs. Higher UMPF intake was associated with older age, female sex, and higher BMI, whereas higher UPF intake was linked to younger age, male sex, and lower BMI. No significant differences in PCI or PF intake were found by sex or demographics.ConclusionThese findings highlight clear sex- and age-related differences in dietary patterns, particularly in the consumption of UPF and UMPF. Over half of the energy comes from UPF in the overall sample, especially among males and younger individuals, raising nutritional concerns and underscoring the need for targeted public health strategies to promote healthier food choices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40795-026-01397-y.Keywords: NOVA, Ultra-processed food, Processed foods, Screening, Jordan

Introduction

Ultra-processed foods (UPFs) are a recent classification that is rapidly growing in popularity and making up a larger part of diets worldwide, raising concerns about their impacts on health and the environment [1]. The most common classification system that captures food processing is the NOVA classification, which defines UPFs as industrial products made primarily from substances extracted or derived from foods, with additives to enhance flavor, texture, and shelf life, and containing little or no whole foods [2, 3]. They are often energy-dense, nutrient-poor, and highly palatable, which makes them likely to replace healthier foods in the diet [4].

Consumption of UPFs is rising globally, displacing minimally processed foods (MPF), varies across countries, and accounts for about 25–60% of total daily energy intake, with higher consumption among younger people, those with lower education, and in high-income countries [5–8]. Analysis of the total energy intake among adults has found that UPFs consumption makes up almost half (45%) of the daily calories consumed by Canadian adults [7], about 57.0% among US adults [5], 48.6% in UK adults [2], 30.0% among the Brazilian population [9], and a lower consumption among Korean adults (25.1%) [8]. To our knowledge, there is a shortage of researchers studying UPF consumption in Middle Eastern countries; only two studies have covered this area. It is reported that UPFs account for about 40% of daily energy intake among Jordanian children and adolescents, with savory snacks, sweets, industrial grain products, fast foods, and sweetened beverages being the main contributors [10]. In Lebanon, UPFs contribute even more, accounting for approximately 46.7% of total energy intake among adults, with bread, breakfast cereals, fast foods, and sweets as the primary sources [11].

The consumption of UPFs is linked to poor diet quality and a higher risk of chronic diseases such as obesity, cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, cancer, depression, and all-cause mortality [5, 12, 13]. The suggested possible mechanisms include poor nutrient profile (high in free sugars, fats, salt; low in fiber, protein, and micronutrients), altered food structure, and effects of additives and packaging chemicals [3, 5, 7, 13]. Despite this, data are scarce on UPF consumption and its contribution to non-communicable diseases in the Middle East regions. Therefore, the current study aimed to evaluate the contribution of unprocessed or minimally processed foods (UMPF), UPFs, processed culinary ingredients (PCI), and processed foods (PF) to the daily energy intake of Jordanian adults, and to examine sex- and weight-related factors (body mass index (BMI)) associated with UPF consumption in the population.

Materials and methodology

Study design and data collection

A cross-sectional study was conducted with a convenience sample of 537 adult participants (274 females and 263 males) aged 19–64 years in the Middle Governates (Amman, Zarqa, Madaba, and Balqa), between February and April 2024. Participants were invited via email/calls through snowball sampling. The sample size was calculated using a 95% confidence interval, a 5% margin of error, and an assumed response distribution of 30%. Raosoft software (Raosoft, Inc., a free online tool based in Seattle, WA, USA) was used for this calculation. Based on these parameters and a target population of Jordanian adults aged 19–64, a minimum of 318 participants should be recruited. The final convenience sample comprised 627 participants (90 were excluded). Participants were eligible if they were Jordanian, non-vegetarian, and not on a reduced diet during the study period. Exclusion criteria included breastfeeding and pregnant women, vegans, and vegetarians. Those with invalid food frequency questionnaires (FFQ) or who under- or over-reported their intake were also excluded. Data was collected through face-to-face interviews by a trained nutritionist. The study received approval from The Hashemite University’s Institutional Review Board (No. 6/1/2023/2024), in line with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written consent was obtained from all participants who chose to participate in the study.

Anthropometric measurements and indices

Interviewers obtained and recorded weight (in kilograms) and height (in centimeters) measurements in the surveys using digital scales, vertical stadiometers, and a metal tape measure, respectively, under standard measurement techniques. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated, and particulates were categorized into different categories based on the World Health Organization (WHO) [14]. A BMI of less than 18.5 kg/m² is considered underweight, while a BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 kg/m² is classified as normal weight. A BMI from 25.0 to 29.9 kg/m² is categorized as overweight, and a BMI over 30 is classified as obese.

Food consumption data

For habitual food consumption (usual) over the last year, a validated Arabic version of a semi-quantitative FFQ comprising 157 items was used [15]. The Photographic Jordanian Food Atlas determined the exact amounts of food intake [16]. Additionally, composite recipes were created from photos of several meals and dishes typical of the Jordanian diet. Moreover, Lebanon Food Composition Data: Traditional Dishes, Arabic Sweets, and Market Foods [17], and Composition of Local Jordanian Food Dishes [18] were used to identify Jordanian and Arabic dishes missing from the food processor. An automated nutrition analysis program was used to input the collected food intake data (ESHA’s Food Processor, Nutrition Analysis Software, version 11.0; ESHA’s Food Processor® Nutrition Analysis Software (version 11.15.94, database structure 11.15.1).

The NOVA classification system was based on previously published supplementary Table S1 provided by Juul et al. [5]and previously published classification [1, 2]. The reported single food items and the essential ingredients of mixed dishes in FFQ was grouped into the following four groups (and subgroups within these groups): cereals, legumes, vegetables, fruits, milk, and meat are examples of UMPF; table salt, sugar, honey, vegetable oils, and butter are examples of PCI; canned vegetables in brine, salted or sugared nuts, canned fish, freshly made bread, and cheeses are examples of PF; and mass-produced packaged bread, carbonated soft drinks, confectionery, cookies, breakfast “cereals,” flavored yogurts, reconstituted meat products, ready-to-heat meals, and packaged instant soups and noodles are examples of UPFs [1, 2]. Classification was based on the degree and purpose of processing, while mixed traditional dishes were classified by their main ingredients and usual preparation methods. The complete item-level classification is provided in Supplementary Table S2. Then, the total calories consumed from each NOVA group were calculated, along with the macronutrient intake within each group. Regarding UPF intake, based on the FFQ classification, it was primarily derived from mostly come from following consumed items within this categories: industrial bakery products (e.g., packaged breads, buns, and croissants), sweet snacks and traditional desserts (e.g., biscuits, cakes, wafers, knafeh, and halawet el jibn), sugar-sweetened and artificially sweetened beverages (e.g., soft drinks, juice drinks, and energy drinks), processed meat and poultry products (e.g., nuggets, sausages, shawarma, and deli meats), breakfast cereals, and ready-to-eat or convenience foods (e.g., pizza and similar items), lastly from sauces and condiments such as mayonnaise and ketchup.

Misreporting energy intake

The Schofield equations were used to calculate the basal metabolic rate (BMR) based on weight, age, and sex for each participant [19]. The ratio of reported daily energy intake (EI) to BMR was calculated to determine the extent of underreporting. According to Black and Cole (2000), participants with an EI: BMR ratio of less than 1.2, 1.2–2.4, and greater than 2.4 were categorized as under-reporters, plausible over-reporters, and over-reporters, respectively [20, 21]. The total number of participants, including those who participated, was 537 out of 627 (90 were under- or over-reporting). However, the exclusion was applied as a data quality control step to minimize the influence of implausible reporting, and thus likely improved the internal consistency of the analyses. Nevertheless, this approach may have affected the sample’s representativeness and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0 software (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp, USA). The significance level was set at p < 0.05. Mean ± standard deviation (SD) and frequencies with percentages (%) were used for categorical descriptive statistics. The independent t-test and ANOVA were used to compare the means of continuous variables (intake quartiles for different NOVA food groups, age, sex, weight, height, and BMI). In contrast, the Chi-square (χ2) test was used to detect differences among categorical variables.

(age, sex, weight, height, BMI, the relative contribution of NOVA food groups from total energy intake for the total study population, and based on sex).

Results

Table 1 describes the characteristics of participants in general (Table 1) and across NOVA intake quartiles (Table 2). As presented in Table 1, a total of 537 adults were included, consisting of 49.0% males (n = 263) and 51.0% females (n = 274). The study sample had a mean age of 32.4 ± 12.9 years, an average height of 168.4 ± 11.6 cm, and a mean body weight of 73.4 ± 15.9 kg. The mean BMI was 25.75 ± 4.95 kg/m². Nearly half of the population is classified as normal weight (44.8%). However, a notable proportion (51.5%) were either overweight or obese. For UMPF intake (Table 2), significant differences were observed in age (p < 0.001), sex (p = 0.030), BMI (p = 0.035), and age groups (p < 0.001). Higher UMPF consumption was associated with older age (37.74 ± 14.13 years for Q4), female sex (61.9% vs. 38.1% for males, who reported a higher prevalence of low UMPF consumption), and higher BMI (26.68 ± 4.56 for Q4). Similarly, PCI intake showed significant associations with age (ranging from 30.14 ± 12.33 at Q1 to 35.00 ± 14.27 at Q4; p = 0.019) and age groups (p = 0.001), with greater intake among young adults. For UPF intake, higher intake was significantly associated with younger age (27.52y ± 9.29 vs. 39.52y ± 14.67 for Q1; p < 0.001), male sex (59.0% vs. 41.0% for females, p = 0.012), and lower BMI (24.92 ± 4.75; p = 0.006) were more prevalent among normal weight (50.7% and 52.6% for Q3 and Q4, respectively; p = 0.015). In contrast, PF intake showed no significant associations with any demographic or anthropometric variables.

Discussion

Jordan’s nutritional situation had been addressed in stage 4 (degenerative disease stage), which is characterized by increased consumption of UPFs (52% of total energy intake comes from UPFs) [22], the increased prevalence of overweight and obesity (about 75%), reflecting a clear shift toward diet-related non-communicable diseases (NCDs) [23], and Jordan’s diet has shifted from plant-based traditional foods to calorie-dense Western diets, rich in fats, sugars, and processed foods. Soft drink and salt intake exceed WHO recommendations, while fruit and vegetable consumption is inadequate [24, 25]. The current study aimed to highlight the usual intake of NOVA food groups among a convenience sample of Jordanian adults. The findings reported a surprising result that is not far from those of other regions and some low- to middle-income countries. The present findings indicated that UPFs contributed the highest caloric content (1449.00 kcal; 51.93% of total energy), followed by UMPFs, PCIs, and PFs. The relative intake of UPFs contributed most to energy intake across all groups, particularly among males (53.65%) compared with females (50.27%; p < 0.001). In contrast, females showed a higher relative intake of UMPFs (32.36%) compared to males. No significant sex differences were observed in PCI and PF relative intake (as a percentage of total calories), indicating comparable consumption patterns for these categories. Moreover, higher UMPF consumption was associated with older age, female sex, and a higher BMI. Similarly, PCI intake was higher among young adults. Higher UPF intake was significantly associated with younger age, male sex, and lower BMI (more prevalent among normal weight). In contrast, PF intake showed no significant associations with any demographic or anthropometric variables.

Consistent with the present findings, among Lebanese adults from the same Middle Eastern region, Khattar and colleagues found that UPFs contributed significantly more to total energy intake (%TEI) in males (49.1%) than in females (44.27%), with a statistically significant sex difference. However, UPFs accounted for the highest percentage of total energy intake (%TEI) in the sample (46.73%), followed by UMPFs. Additionally, younger participants (aged 18) had the highest UPF intake (53.07% of TEI), while older adults had the lowest (43.39%), also showing a significant difference across age groups. Moreover, females had a lower likelihood of having high UPF intake compared with males (OR = 0.48, 95% confidence interval (95% CI) = 0.306–0.76, and p-value = 0.002), and participants in the 31–50 age category had lower odds of having high UPF intake compared with those aged 18 years (OR = 0.27, 95% CI = 0.09–0.75, and p-value = 0.012) [11].

National surveys in the US showed even larger UPF contributions, comprising 57.9% of total energy intake, and accounting for 89.7% of the energy intake from added sugars. The content of added sugars in UPFs (21.1% of calories) was eight times higher than in PF (2.4%) and five times higher than in UMPFs and PCIs combined (3.7%) [26]. Additionally, the National Diet and Nutrition Survey (2008–2014) in the UK reported that the average energy intake was 1764 kcal/day, with 30.1% of calories coming from UMPFs, 4.2% from PCIs, 8.8% from PFs, and 56.8% from UPFs [2].

On the other hand, the average daily energy intake for the adult French population was 2111 kcal, of which 31.1% originated from UPFs. This proportion was greater among younger people and those living in urban areas, but lower among individuals with incomplete high school education and retirees [27]. Among Italian adults, the average energy from UPF was 17·3% for a total of 154·8 g/d of UPF eaten daily, 40.0% from UMPF, 24·4% from PF, and 19·6% from PCIs [28].

A conflicting result regarding the sex role in UPFs’ consumption: in line with the present findings, UPFs account for nearly 45% of the daily calorie intake for Canadian adults, with higher consumption observed among men and younger individuals [29]. Moreover, the frequency of UPFs consumption was significantly lower in females and decreased linearly with age in Brazil [30]. This was also reflected in the Swiss population, where the median energy intake from UPFs was 587 kcal/day, accounting for nearly a third of the total energy intake (28.7%). The median intake of UPFs relative to TEI was higher among young participants (< 30 years; p = 0.001) and in men than in women (28.4% vs. 29.2%) [31]. In Korea, the overall contribution of ultra-processed foods increased from 23.1% (2010–2012) to 26.1% (2016–2018). The dietary energy contribution of UPFs was high among men and urban residents and increased with education and income levels; it also peaked among adolescents and thereafter decreased with age [32]. Contrasting results were obtained in Italy and Spain, where females were reported to consume more UPFs than males. Spain’s UPF consumption increased by 10.8% overall between 1991 and 2008 (p-value < 0.001). Younger people (p-value < 0.001) and women (p-value = 0.01) consumed more UPFs [33]. In Italy, Ruggiero and colleagues found that individuals residing in Southern Italy, particularly those in urban settings, had a positive connection with UPF intake, and that lower UPF consumption was associated with males and older respondents (aged > 65 years vs. 20–40 years) [28]. The discrepancy in results across countries could be due to differences in the tools used to collect food consumption data, such as FFQs and 24-hour recalls, as well as differences in cultural and sociodemographic factors.

In line with the current findings concerning the age group, parallel results were obtained in Lebanon [11], Australia [34], France [27], Italy [28], Brazil [30]. This could be related to the fact that food preferences differ notably between younger and older generations. Younger people are generally more open to trying new, trendy, and convenient foods, while older adults tend to prefer traditional, familiar, and fresh foods. These differences are shaped by lifestyle, cultural identity, health concerns, and sensory perception [35, 36].

Implementing public health policies and campaigns to highlight the risks of UPFs is essential, as is supporting food systems to enhance the supply, affordability, and perceived value of unprocessed and minimally processed foods and freshly prepared meals. It is particularly important to limit UPF marketing and visibility among younger adults. This can be achieved through regulations banning UPF in public institutions. Additionally, front-of-package warning labels identifying ultra-processed foods, as well as regulations on formulation and processing practices, should be considered. Seriously, research should focus on actual purchasing and consumption behaviors. Conducting qualitative studies of factors influencing public eating habits may also be a valuable strategy for achieving the long-term goal of increasing access to healthier options. Based on EMRO and WHO recommendations to reduce UPF consumption in Jordan, a mix of tax, regulatory, and education policies is needed. Strengthening the existing sugar tax to include energy drinks and sweets could lead to a 20% price increase, as seen in countries such as Mexico and the UK. Jordan could also introduce clear front-of-pack labels for high-sugar and high-fat products. Regulating food environments in schools and institutions by banning UPFs and promoting healthy options is crucial. The government might also provide subsidies or vouchers for low-income families to buy fruits and vegetables. Lastly, establishing marketing restrictions to protect children and raising public awareness about UPFs can lead to healthier choices and improve overall public health [37, 38].

This study contributes to the existing literature and addresses a significant topic that has not yet been explored at the national level in Jordan. However, the reliance on memory for data collection introduces potential recall bias and inaccuracies in estimating portion sizes. To reduce this risk, trained nutritionists provided participants with visual aids to help them recall and estimate portions more accurately. Also, the FFQ cannot specify a method of bread preparation, which makes the NOVA food classification difficult (it was classified based on references [1, 2]). Another limitation is the absence of certain sociodemographic data, such as smoking habits, physical activity, marital status, medical history, illnesses, medications, and employment status, which were not collected. The exclusion of extreme energy intake values should be interpreted with caution, as the FFQ is not designed to provide precise estimates of absolute energy intake and may not fully capture total food consumption. Therefore, some individuals with true but atypical dietary patterns may have been excluded, or misclassification of implausible reporters may have occurred. In addition, the age distribution of the sample, which included a relatively small proportion of older adults, may have influenced the overall mean UPF consumption observed in this study. Caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to populations with different age structures, as younger individuals are more likely to consume UPFs, which may have led to a higher estimated mean UPF intake. Moreover, despite a large convenience sample, data collection was primarily from the Central governorates of Jordan, and the age distribution limits the generalizability of the findings to older Jordanian adults and to the entire population.

In conclusion, the study underscores a high reliance on UPFs in the usual dietary intake of Jordanian adults, contributing over half of total daily energy intake. Males had a significantly higher proportion of energy from UPFs compared to females, while females consumed more UMPFs. Patterns of consumption varied by age, sex, and BMI: younger adults and males favored UPFs, whereas older adults, females, and those with higher BMI favored UMPFs. Younger adults are more commonly consuming PCIs, and PFs showed no demographic associations. These findings reflect dietary trends in Jordan that are comparable to those observed in both regional and some low-to middle-income countries, highlighting the nutritional transition and the need for public health strategies to promote healthier eating habits.
