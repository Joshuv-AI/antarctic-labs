Epigenetics-mediated pathological alterations and their diagnostic and therapeutic potential in sepsis-associated acute kidney injury
Authors: Qian Zhang, Yuan Tan, Jingjin Tao, Boxin Yang, Zhen Xu, Shuo Yang, Liyan Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI) is a prevalent and life-threatening condition among critically ill patients. At present, studies on SA-AKI are still in the experimental stage, and comprehensively elucidating the pathogenesis of SA-AKI would effectively promote the development of diagnostic and therapeutic for SA-AKI. Emerging evidence highlights the critical role of cell-specific epigenetic dysregulation in the severity and progression of SA-AKI, particularly in monocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, endothelial cells (ECs), and renal tubular epithelial cells (RTECs). Classical epigenetic modifications, including DNA methylation and histone modifications, serve as pivotal regulators of gene transcription, influencing multiple pathological processes, including inflammatory responses, microcirculatory dysfunction, and metabolic reprogramming during SA-AKI. Therefore, this review aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of the underlying epigenetic mechanisms of SA-AKI progression and explore the potential of epigenetic markers as diagnostic tools and therapeutic targets. Finally, we propose that targeting epigenetic modifications could pave the way for novel diagnostic strategies and precision medicine to improve SA-AKI outcomes.

Introduction

Sepsis, a severe systemic inflammatory response triggered by infectious or non-infectious insults, is characterized by dysregulated host immunity and multi-organ dysfunction [1]. The kidney is the most frequently affected organ, with epidemiological studies indicating that 45–70% of sepsis patients develop acute kidney injury [2]. Notably, SA-AKI exhibits higher in-hospital mortality and worse prognosis compared to other AKI subtypes, underscoring its clinical severity [3]. The pathophysiology of SA-AKI involves complex crosstalk between immune cells (such as macrophages, monocytes, neutrophils), endothelial cells (ECs), and renal tubular epithelial cells (RTECs), driven by epigenetic dysregulation [4]. These modifications, particularly DNA methylation and histone alterations orchestrate critical pathological cascades, including sustained inflammation, microcirculatory dysfunction, and metabolic reprogramming, collectively exacerbating renal injury [2].

Critically, epigenetic modifications serve as a critical link between genetic information and external stimuli, playing a key role in regulating cellular responses to environmental challenge. Sepsis, a potent infectious stressor, contributes to widespread epigenetic alterations that aggravate kidney injury [5]. Canonical epigenetic modifications, including DNA methylation and histone modifications, not only mediate the initial cellular response to sepsis but also amplify the susceptibility of AKI to sepsis via genetic and epigenetic reprogramming [6, 7]. Importantly, the reversibility, hereditability, and environment dependence of epigenetics facilitate the understanding of phenotypic remodeling in SA-AKI. A comprehensive elucidation of these mechanisms may offer valuable therapeutic strategies for SA-AKI.

Despite sharing the same genome, immune cells, ECs, and RTECs exhibit unique epigenetic landscapes that govern their distinct functions and phenotypes during the progression of SA-AKI [8]. For instance, in LPS-activated macrophages, coordinated enhancement of glycolytic flux and one-carbon metabolism drives S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) production, thereby inducing active histone mark H3K36me3 that drives macrophage polarization toward the pro-inflammatory phenotype (M1) through chromatin remodeling [9]. Concurrently, in RTECs models mimicking sepsis, the DNA demethylase ten-eleven translocation methylcytosine dioxygenase 1 (TET1) modulates renal inflammation and oxidative stress by regulating methylation patterns at SOD1 and SOD2 promoters [10]. Based on these findings, this review provides a systematic analysis of the role of epigenetic mechanisms in SA-AKI progression, focusing on how epigenetic modifications mediate the dysfunction of immune cells, ECs, and RTECs. Further, highlighting the diagnostic and therapeutic potential of targeting epigenetic alterations in SA-AKI provides novel insights and strategies for improving clinical outcomes.

Epigenetic mechanisms in SA-AKI progression

Epigenetic modifications, particularly DNA methylation and histone post-translational modifications, maintain reversibility through dynamic regulation by “writers” and “erasers” [11], thereby precisely modulating the disease progression of SA-AKI. This highly reversible and efficient enzymatic reaction network confers remarkable plasticity to the epigenome [12], which continuously regulates chromatin accessibility and transcription factor activity to drive the gene expression related to SA-AKI (Fig. 1).

DNA methylation dynamics in SA-AKI

DNA methylation is one of the most extensively studied epigenetic modifications and plays a pivotal role in kidney dysfunction [13]. This process is attributed to DNA methyltransferases (such as DNMT1, DNMT3A, DNMT3B), by adding methyl groups to cytosines on CpG islands [14]. Generally, hypermethylation suppresses gene expression, while hypomethylation facilitates transcription. Genome-wide DNA methylation analyses have revealed extensive epigenetic remodeling in kidney tissues during sepsis, with differentially methylated regions demonstrating strong correlations with clinical features such as disease severity and hospitalization duration, underscoring the importance of DNA methylation in SA-AKI pathogenesis [15, 16]. Furthermore, DNA methylation markers have shown high diagnostic accuracy in distinguishing SA-AKI from sepsis without AKI (SN-AKI), with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.92 [17]. These findings suggest that DNA methylation not only plays a crucial role in the progression of SA-AKI, but also serve as potential biomarkers for differentially diagnosing SA-AKI, assessing disease severity, and predicting prognosis.

Histone modification dynamics in SA-AKI

Histone modifications represent a fundamental epigenetic mechanism that dynamically regulates chromatin structure and gene expression. These chemical modifications, which primarily target lysine and arginine residues on histone tails (H2A, H2B, H3 and H4), are dynamically controlled by opposing enzymatic systems: “writer” enzymes (such as HATs and HMTs) add modifications, while “eraser” enzymes (including HDACs and HDMs) remove them, creating a rapidly responsive regulatory system [18]. Classical histone modifications include histone methylation, acetylation, ubiquitination, and phosphorylation. Among these, histone acetylation and methylation have been the most extensively studied. Histone acetylation generally induces gene transcriptional activation, whereas the impact of histone methylation depends on the amino acid type and modification extent. For example, H3K4me1, H3K4me3, H3K36me3, and H3K79me1 are considered markers of active transcription, while H3K9me3, H3K27me3, and H4K20me3 are associated with transcriptional repression and silent chromatin states [19]. In the SA-AKI model induced by LPS, histone modifications are significantly altered in inflammatory and injury-related genes, including tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α), kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1), neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), and intercellular cell adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) [20]. Highlighting the pivotal role of histone modification in modulating disease-related genes.

Cell type-specific epigenetic abnormalities in SA-AKI

During the pathological progression of SA-AKI, multiple cellular components (including monocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, ECs, and RTECs) exhibit characteristic epigenetic regulatory abnormalities. These cell-specific epigenetic reprogramming processes differentially modulate critical pathophysiological mechanisms, including immune inflammatory dysregulation, metabolic reprogramming, microcirculatory dysfunction, and cellular injury, collectively driving SA-AKI progression [21]. Notably, the epigenetic regulatory networks demonstrate significant heterogeneity across different cell lineages (Fig. 2). Such cell type-dependent epigenetic signatures not only provide novel insights into elucidating the molecular mechanisms of SA-AKI but also establish a molecular foundation for developing precision therapeutic strategies targeting specific epigenetic regulators.

Epigenetic-mediated monocytes and macrophages activation in SA-AKI

Monocytes and macrophages, as key components of the innate immune system, exert a pivotal function in SA-AKI progression [22]. Evidence has confirmed that epigenetic abnormalities in macrophages fundamentally influence inflammatory responses, phenotypic switching between pro-inflammatory M1 and reparative M2 states, and metabolic states [23]. Clarifying the regulatory mechanisms of epigenetics in macrophages would be beneficial for exploring epigenetic therapy strategies targeting macrophages.

DNA methylation in monocyte and macrophage

Changes in DNA methylation are vital drivers of monocyte and macrophage dysfunction, as well as immune disorders in SA-AKI [24]. Clinical investigations reveal genome-wide methylation remodeling in circulating monocytes from septic patients, with modification magnitudes showing significant positive correlations with IL-6/IL-10 expression profiles and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores [25]. These locus-specific alterations amplify systemic inflammation through inflammatory-immune signaling axis activation, ultimately inducing renal parenchymal damage [25, 26]. In LPS-stimulated macrophages, DNMT1-mediated hypermethylation of the SOCS1 promoter disrupts JAK/STAT3 negative feedback, skewing polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. Using DNMT1 inhibitors reprograms macrophages toward an M2-reparative phenotype, facilitating functional renal recovery [27]. Therefore, targeting DNMT may represent an effective therapeutic strategy to modulate macrophage polarization and suppress inflammation. Importantly, DNA methylation exhibits intricate crosstalk with cellular metabolism in SA-AKI pathogenesis. The association between enhanced glycolysis and increased tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle activity with DNA methylation activation has been widely demonstrated to fuel the one-carbon cycle, increasing SAM availability to both establish and erase methyl marks [28]. Hypomethylation of the GABA transporter-2 promoter increases SAM bioavailability, which both upregulates OXPHOS gene expression and enhances USP16 promoter methylation to promote TLR4 ubiquitination and NF-κB pathway inhibition [29, 30]. Taken together, DNA methylation synchronizes inflammatory programming with metabolic reprogramming in monocytes and macrophages. These findings highlight that elucidation of DNA methylation dynamics in macrophages merits further exploration.

Histone modification in monocyte and macrophage

Histone modifications act as a pivotal epigenetic mechanism that orchestrates multilayered control of the inflammatory cascade in macrophages during SA-AKI. At the initiation stage, LPS stimulation recruits the histone acetyltransferase CBP to the TLR4 signalosome, where it catalyzes acetylation of the TIR domain and thereby amplifies NF-κB signaling [31]. This step is further potentiated by the histone demethylase KDM5B, which erases the H3K4me3 mark at the IκBα (Nfkbia) promoter to repress its expression and relieve the negative feedback on NF-κB [32]. Subsequently, activated NF-κB not only directly drives inflammatory gene transcription but also induces formation of a functional ribonucleoprotein complex between lincRNA-Tnfaip3 and HMGB1. This complex remodels the histone modification landscape (including dynamic changes in H3K4me3, H3K27me3, H3K9ac, and H3K27ac) at inflammatory gene loci, sustaining chromatin accessibility and perpetuating transcriptional activation of pro-inflammatory genes [33, 34]. Such epigenetic control that threads through the entire inflammatory signaling axis underpins the relentless amplification of the inflammatory response in SA-AKI. The balance of macrophage polarization in SA-AKI is coordinately regulated by histone-modifying enzymes and non-coding RNA (ncRNA) networks. Mechanistic studies demonstrate that the histone demethylase JMJD3 removes the repressive H3K27me3 mark from promoters of homeostatic genes (IRF4, Arg1 and Chil3), facilitating M2 polarization [35], whereas miR-27b restrains this process by targeting JMJD3 and sustaining H3K27me3 at the NF-κB promoter, thereby suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines and reinforcing the M2 phenotype [36]. Conversely, LPS stimulation enriches HDAC3 and H3K9ac at the miR-4767 promoter to suppress its expression, activating the cGAS/STING-mediated pyroptosis pathway [37]. The effect reversible by HDAC inhibitors (HDACi), which promote M2 reprogramming and mitigate cellular injury [38]. Thus, epigenetic inhibitors may slow the course of SA-AKI by modifying macrophage polarization. Notably, beyond microRNAs, circRNAs and lncRNAs play pivotal roles in this regulatory network. CircMAPK1 degrades KDM2B mRNA to sustain WNK1 promoter methylation, triggering NLRP3 inflammasome activation [39], whereas lncRNA GAS5 functions as a molecular sponge for miR-155-5p to upregulate the deacetylase SIRT1, thereby inhibiting HMGB1 release and promoting M2 polarization [40]. Collectively, these findings delineate the synergistic interplay between histone modifiers and ncRNAs in dictating macrophage fate and highlight the therapeutic potential of epigenetic reprogramming for mitigating renal dysfunction in SA-AKI.

It is important to note that the shift in macrophage metabolism from OXPHOS to glycolysis worsens kidney damage as sepsis progresses. This metabolic reprogramming is tightly tied to epigenetic changes, including histone acetylation and methylation. LPS-induced metabolic reprogramming in macrophages leads to an increase of acetyl-CoA levels, thereby activating ATP Citrate Lyase and enhancing citrate metabolism [41], which in turn promotes histone acetylation and inflammatory gene expression [42]. In human primary monocytes/macrophages, the histone methyltransferase SET7 adds H3K4me1 to distal enhancer regions of metabolic enzymes such as malate dehydrogenase 2 and succinate dehydrogenase B, which are involved in the TCA cycle as well as forming a feedback loop to support macrophage activation and metabolic reprogramming [43]. Moreover, studies have demonstrated that LPS-induced macrophages exhibit the increased SAM due to altered one-carbon metabolism, which further impacts DNA and histone methylation patterns [44]. Interestingly, exogenous supplementation with methyl donors like folate or choline has been shown to alleviate LPS-induced macrophage activation by enhancing SAM-mediated histone methylation [45, 46]. The above suggests that modifying aberrant epigenetic alterations may be a viable technique for correcting metabolic abnormalities in macrophages.

Overall, the dysregulated epigenetic landscape in monocytes and macrophages drives inflammatory responses, immune imbalance, and metabolic disruption, which may serve as unique therapeutic targets in SA-AKI.

Epigenetic-mediated neutrophils activation in SA-AKI

Neutrophils, as the first line of defense in immune-inflammatory response to sepsis, aggravate kidney injury by secreting neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) and inflammatory cytokines and promote vascular leakage via interacting with ECs [47, 48]. Notably, epigenetic modifications in neutrophils significantly influence their functions and further modulate the pathological process in SA-AKI.

DNA methylation in neutrophils

Neutrophil-specific DNA methylation patterns have been identified as a central molecular switch regulating the immune response in SA-AKI, with the resulting immunophenotypic shift serving as an early epigenetic biomarker for sepsis susceptibility [49]. During the initial phase of sepsis, the catalytic activity of DNA methyltransferase DNMT3A is significantly suppressed. This dysfunction of epigenetic regulation exhibits a precise temporal correlation with the rapid upregulation of key inflammatory mediators, including pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and transcriptional regulators, suggesting that DNA methylation remodeling plays a pivotal role in initiating the inflammatory cascade [50, 51]. Consistent with this, subsequent studies revealed that LPS stimulation induces hypomethylation of the NFKB1 promoter in neutrophils, thereby relieving its epigenetic silencing and sustaining activation of the NF-κB inflammatory signaling axis [52]. These findings collectively demonstrate that DNA methylation-mediated neutrophil dysfunction primarily exacerbates SA-AKI progression by amplifying the immune-inflammatory response. Therefore, precise modulation of neutrophil inflammatory signaling pathways controlled by DNA methylation may represent a critical therapeutic strategy for early mitigation of the cytokine storm and improved clinical outcomes.

Histone modification in neutrophils

The release of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) serves as a critical hallmark of neutrophil activation in SA-AKI, a process that is precisely regulated by histone modifications. Recent studies demonstrate that HDAC-mediated histone H3 deacetylation represents a crucial step for peptidylarginine deiminase 4 (PAD4)-dependent citrullination, which subsequently triggers NETs formation [53]. Excessive NETs release exacerbates renal injury through multiple mechanisms: degrading endothelial glycocalyx, activating platelets, and promoting microthrombosis, ultimately compromising microcirculation [54]. Furthermore, HDAC11 has been shown to aggravate renal damage by suppressing IL-10 secretion, activating endothelial cells, and upregulating adhesion molecules, thereby facilitating neutrophil rolling and renal infiltration [55]. Concurrently, LPS stimulation can remodel the neutrophil transcriptional network through H3K4me3 epigenetic marks, which not only regulates NET-related gene expression but also dynamically modulates neutrophil phenotypic switching among pro-inflammatory, anti-inflammatory and tissue-repair profiles [56]. Of particular significance, the histone methyltransferase SET1B enhances H3K4me3 modification at the hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) promoter to upregulate its expression [57], leading to increased lactate dehydrogenase A levels and a metabolic shift from oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to aerobic glycolysis in neutrophils. This metabolic reprogramming process not only provides energy support for NETs formation but also modulates the intensity of neutrophil inflammatory responses [58]. Targeting key epigenetic regulators including HDAC, PAD4 and SET1B has been demonstrated to coordinately modulate neutrophil NETs formation, inflammatory responses and metabolic reprogramming through synergistic epigenetic mechanisms, thereby ameliorating SA-AKI [53, 59].

These findings reveal a dynamically integrated epigenetic regulatory network encompassing DNA methylation, histone methylation, acetylation, and citrullination, which orchestrates neutrophil activation status in SA-AKI through multi-layered molecular interactions. Delineating the hierarchical relationships and temporal dynamics among these epigenetic modifications will provide crucial theoretical foundations for developing targeted therapies to reverse pathological neutrophil activation and restore immune homeostasis.

Epigenetic-mediated endothelial cells activation in SA-AKI

Sepsis-induced endothelial dysfunction is a major contributor to kidney injury, vascular leakage, leukocyte adhesion, thrombosis, and microcirculatory damage [60]. Furthermore, DNA methylation and histone modifications are vital components of ECs, and their alterations have been verified to modulate the function of ECs and the severity of SA-AKI [4].

DNA methylation in endothelial cells

A whole-genome methylation analysis has revealed significant changes in DNA methylation patterns in SA-AKI mice and EC models mimicking SA-AKI. Specifically, 1792 genes were hypermethylated, while 804 genes were hypomethylated. Most of the differentially methylated genes are involved in immune cell activation, secretion of inflammatory factors, and cell adhesion [61]. In spite of mediating modifications in downstream signaling pathways, DNA methylation is also precisely modulated by major transcription factors. In renal ECs, LPS-induced key transcription factors (such as STAT and AP1) regulate DNA methylation and the transcription of associated genes, ultimately modifying inflammatory responses, vascular reactivity, and microthrombosis formation [61, 62]. These methylation-driven gene alterations resulted in the sustained activation of renal ECs. Additionally, methylation-induced ECs metabolic shift are a major factor in aggravating SA-AKI. Hypomethylation of the vital metabolic enzyme PFKFB3 in ECs enhances glycolysis in response to metabolic reprogramming during SA-AKI [63, 64]. Furthermore, the specific methylation patterns observed in renal ECs could help distinguish SA-AKI from S-NAKI [17]. In conclusion, ECs may exhibit cell-type-specific DNA methylation, which proves essential to controlling functional alterations in these cells.

Histone modification in endothelial cells

Emerging evidence demonstrates that ECs undergo profound histone modifications during SA-AKI, characterized by dysregulation of key chromatin-modifying enzymes. In LPS-induced EC models, various histone methyltransferases show abnormal upregulation, including SET and MYND domain containing 1 (SMYD1), Enhancer of Zeste Homolog 2 (EZH2), SET domain-containing 1 (SET1), and JMJD3. Mechanistically, SMYD1 exacerbates renal injury through NF-κB pathway activation and H3K4me3-mediated transcriptional activation of IL-6 [65]. Concurrently, SET1-mediated elevation of H3K4me3 levels, coupled with EZH2- and JMJD3-regulated reduction of H3K27me3, collectively induces aberrant Notch signaling activation, triggering ECs dysfunction [66]. The epigenetic landscape is further modulated by ncRNAs: lnc-IL7R preserves H3K27me3 at the promoters of key adhesion molecules E-selectin and VCAM-1, selectively silencing their transcription while concurrently repressing IL-6 and IL-8. This dual repression interrupts endothelial-leukocyte adhesion and the ensuing inflammatory cascade, thereby negatively regulating the overall inflammatory response and averting microvascular dysfunction [67]. Meanwhile, circEXOC5 recruits EZH2 to inhibit the expression of transcription factor Nrf2, thereby promoting endothelial pyroptosis [68]. Intriguingly, HDAC family members exhibit functional dichotomy - classical HDACs degrade the glycocalyx via MMP2/9 upregulation [69]. In contrast, SIRT1 ameliorates LPS-induced endothelial hyperpermeability, suppresses ROS production, and facilitates endothelial repair [70]. Similarly, SIRT6 inhibits HIF1α/p300-mediated Ero1α (endoplasmic reticulum oxidoreductase 1 alpha) expression by deacetylating H3K9, thereby reducing inflammation and oxidative stress in ECs [71]. Although epigenetic modulators like EZH2 inhibitors show promise in preclinical models [72], significant translational challenges remain in developing these interventions for clinical SA-AKI management.

Emerging research has highlighted the pivotal interplay between metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic modifications in ECs during SA-AKI. Under septic conditions, ECs undergo a profound metabolic shift from OXPHOS to glycolytic dominance, resulting in substantial lactate accumulation. Notably, lactate serves not merely as a metabolic byproduct but also as a critical epigenetic regulator that directly remodels chromatin architecture through lysine lactylation (Kla) modifications [73]. Mechanistic studies reveal marked enrichment of H3K14la marks at promoters of ferroptosis-related genes, which directly drives endothelial ferroptosis and compromises microvascular barrier integrity [74]. Concurrently, lactate-induced H3K18la modifications at the EGR1 promoter upregulate heparanase expression, exacerbating microcirculatory dysfunction and renal parenchymal injury [75]. Complementing this pathway, mitochondrial metabolic dysregulation promotes citrate efflux, which is subsequently cleaved by ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY) to generate acetyl-CoA. This central metabolic intermediate serves dual roles as an “acetyl donor” to elevate global histone acetylation levels and as an activator of the FoxO1-H3 acetylation-c-Myc signaling axis, thereby promoting endothelial glycolipid metabolic reprogramming and vascular inflammation that amplifies organ damage [76]. At the molecular level, metabolites including lactate and acetyl-CoA reshape the chromatin landscape through histone lactylation/acetylation, rapidly translating metabolic disturbances into pro-inflammatory and pro-ferroptotic transcriptional programs. This process ultimately establishes a self-perpetuating “metabolic disorder-epigenetic remodeling-inflammatory amplification” vicious cycle within the septic microenvironment that perpetuates renal injury progression.

These findings provide a novel conceptual framework for understanding SA-AKI pathogenesis and identify promising therapeutic targets at the metabolism-epigenetics interface.

Epigenetic-mediated renal tubular epithelial cells activation in SA-AKI

The most damage-sensitive kidney cells, RTECs, are essential for SA-AKI under epigenetic control. They perform an active part in oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, circulatory malfunction, inflammatory reactions, and various forms of cell death.

DNA methylation in renal tubular epithelial cells

DNA methylation is the most persistent epigenetic alteration for ensuring appropriate RTECs function. Recently, a cohort study involving 1.5 million individuals has constructed the most comprehensive GWAS association map of kidney function, identifying approximately 878 genetic loci associated with kidney function through analyzing the genotypes and DNA methylation profiles of 443 kidneys. Of these genetic loci, 56% are directly related to kidney function impairment [16]. This study first revealed the strong impact of DNA methylation on kidney function, with tissue-specific genetic changes, and emphasized the crucial role of DNA methylation patterns in RTECs in kidney function and metabolism. The tissue- and cell-specific nature of DNA methylation may provide a potential direction for the precise diagnosis of diseases. Moreover, DNA methylation also plays a role in RTECs injury in SA-AKI. In an animal model of SA-AKI, the methylation-dependent silencing factor 2 (MBD2) binds to the PKCη CpG island, inhibiting its methylation at the promoter region. This promotes PKCη activation, triggering the p38 MAPK and ERK1/2 pathways, which lead to inflammation and apoptosis in RTECs [77]. However, the demethylase TET1 prohibits ROS accumulation, inflammation, and fibrosis by demethylating antioxidant enzymes SOD1 and SOD2 at their promoter regions, thereby improving kidney injury [10]. Moreover, RTECs-specific methylation biomarkers can effectively distinguish between SA-AKI and SN-AKI [17]. As a result, these dysregulated DNA methylations influence the activity of RTECs and coordinate the pathogenesis of SA-AKI, indicating prospective treatment targets.

Histone modification in renal tubular epithelial cells

Histone modifications are integral to the pathogenesis of RTECs in SA-AKI. Studies demonstrate that in SA-AKI mouse models, EZH2-mediated aberrant enrichment of H3K27me3 at the Sox9 promoter leads to transcriptional silencing of this gene and suppression of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, consequently inducing RTECs death, inflammatory responses, and aggravated renal injury [78]. Notably, different histone deacetylase (HDAC) subtypes exhibit distinct functional patterns in SA-AKI. Downregulation of class III HDAC members (SIRT1, SIRT3, and SIRT6) closely correlates with RTECs damage, whereas class I, II, and IV HDACs may exacerbate renal injury. Currently, HDAC-targeting therapeutic strategies have become a research focus. On one hand, upregulation of SIRT family proteins demonstrates significant protective effects: SIRT1 activates autophagy through deacetylating Beclin1 and p53 [79, 80]; SIRT3 alleviates mitochondrial damage by deacetylating YME1L1 ATPase [81]; SIRT6 inhibits inflammation, cell death, and oxidative stress through multiple pathways [82]. On the other hand, inhibitors targeting class I, II, and IV HDACs also show promising renoprotective effects. Furthermore, ncRNAs play crucial roles in regulating RTECs injury in SA-AKI. LncRNA GAS5 suppresses pyroptosis by downregulating miR-579-3p and activating the SIRT1/PGC-1α/Nrf2 pathway [83]. Elevated urinary miR-340-5p levels can predict poor prognosis in SA-AKI and modulate RTEC injury by targeting lysine (K)-specific demethylase 4 C (KDM4C) [84]. These findings collectively reveal the central position of epigenetic regulatory networks in SA-AKI and provide important targets for developing novel therapeutic strategies.

It is noteworthy that histone modifications significantly contribute to the metabolic reprogramming of RTECs during SA-AKI. The downregulation of SIRT3 leads to hyperacetylation and inactivation of the E1 subunit α of pyruvate dehydrogenase, resulting in lactate accumulation in RTECs and exacerbating kidney injury [85]. Importantly, the role of histone lactylation induced by lactate accumulation in SA-AKI has recently garnered significant attention. Studies have shown that lactate can promote histone H3K18 lactylation, activating the RhoA/Rho-associated protein kinase (ROCK)/Ezrin signaling pathway and NF-κB, thereby mediating inflammation and apoptosis and further aggravating kidney injury [86]. Interestingly, glycolysis inhibitors targeting lactate have been found to mitigate SA-AKI by reducing histone lactylation modifications and upregulating SIRT3 expression [87]. To sum up, adverse histone changes directly damage RTECs, exacerbating kidney injury in sepsis. However, correcting these harmful histone modifications can effectively alleviate kidney damage during the septic process.

Potential epigenetics in the treatment and diagnosis of SA-AKI

With the in-depth research on epigenetic modifications, some promising epigenetic targets in SA-AKI have been proposed, which opens new insights for the development of precision medicine. Of significance, specific inhibitors targeting DNA methylation and histone modification modulators such as DNMT, HDAC, EZH2 and HAT, as well as SIRT activators and other small molecule drugs, are being actively explored as potential treatments for SA-AKI.

Therapeutic targeting of DNA methylation in SA-AKI

Researchers have determined that DNA methylation disorders are closely related to inflammation, microcirculation dysfunction, and metabolism disturbances in SA-AKI. DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) inhibitors, as a well-established class of epigenetic modulators, have demonstrated proven efficacy in the treatment of hematologic malignancies. Notably, decitabine and azacitidine have received FDA approval for managing myelodysplastic syndromes, acute myeloid leukemia, and lymphoma [88, 89]. Building upon their established safety profiles and clinical experience, these agents are now emerging as promising therapeutic candidates for SA-AKI (Table 1). Preclinical studies have demonstrated that 5-aza-2’-deoxycytidine exerts renal protective effects through targeted demethylation of the klotho gene promoter region, significantly upregulating klotho protein expression. This mechanism effectively attenuates renal inflammation, inhibits apoptosis, and ameliorates histopathological damage [90]. Furthermore, this agent selectively reduces methylation levels at the Kruppel-like factor 2 (KLF2) promoter, subsequently downregulating the expression of adhesion molecules, including VCAM-1 and E-selectin. These actions collectively maintain endothelial homeostasis and mitigate microthrombosis, thereby improving renal microcirculatory dysfunction [62]. Of particular significance, decitabine has shown remarkable organoprotective effects in SA-AKI animal models through specific inhibition of NF-κB pathway activation, evidenced by reduced systemic inflammatory responses and improved 28-day survival rates [91]. These findings underscore the translational potential of DNMT inhibitors in SA-AKI management through their multitarget regulatory mechanisms, offering novel perspectives for developing precision epigenetic therapies. However, clinical translation requires addressing several critical challenges including optimizing dosing regimens to balance efficacy and safety, as well as validating clinical outcomes through standardized trials. Future investigations should prioritize establishing SA-AKI-specific epigenetic regulatory networks to provide a theoretical foundation for the precision application of DNMT inhibitors.

Therapeutic targeting of histone modification in SA-AKI

Histone-modifying enzymes HDAC I/II and SIRT play crucial roles in the pathogenesis of SA-AKI (Table 1). Currently, several drugs targeting these enzymes have been clinically approved for the treatment of other diseases, providing potential therapeutic options for SA-AKI through drug repurposing. Among HDAC inhibitors, vorinostat (Zolinza®), an FDA-approved drug for cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (CTCL), has demonstrated immunomodulatory effects that are being explored in other disease contexts [92], while romidepsin (Istodax®), approved for CTCL, has been investigated as a latency-reversing agent in HIV research. However, its direct application in SA-AKI requires further validation [93, 94]. These clinical experiences establish a foundation for applying HDAC inhibitors in SA-AKI. Both the HDAC I inhibitor MS-275 and the HDAC IIa inhibitor TMP195 have demonstrated notable renal protective effects in SA-AKI models, functioning through alleviating oxidative/endoplasmic reticulum stress and reducing renal injury biomarker expression, respectively [95, 96]. Regarding HDAC III (SIRT) activators, compounds including resveratrol (RSV), SRT1720, polydatin, and melatonin exhibit therapeutic potential: RSV and polydatin activate autophagy via deacetylation of P53 and Beclin1 to mitigate renal injury [79, 80]; SRT1720 improves endothelial barrier function and corrects microcirculatory disturbances [70]; while melatonin maintains metabolic homeostasis through SIRT3-mediated deacetylation of mitochondrial transcription factor A [97]. Additionally, the selective EZH2 inhibitor 3-deazaneplanocin A effectively suppresses inflammatory responses and apoptosis in SA-AKI by upregulating Sox9 to activate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [78]. Particularly noteworthy is dexmedetomidine, an approved sedative that alleviates SA-AKI-associated inflammation and renal injury by targeting epigenetic regulators including KDM5A and HDAC [98–100]. The established clinical safety profiles and well-defined mechanisms of action of these drugs offer new perspectives for SA-AKI treatment.

Despite the encouraging prospects of epigenetic drugs in preclinical studies, their clinical translation remains challenging. One major obstacle is the broad-spectrum activity of many epigenetic inhibitors, which can affect multiple genes and signaling pathways, leading to nonspecific regulation and an increased risk of adverse effects. Additionally, prolonged use of these agents may induce genomic instability, thereby elevating the risk of tumorigenesis. Furthermore, due to the dynamic and reversible nature of epigenetic modifications, determining the optimal therapeutic window poses a significant challenge. Fortunately, advancements in interdisciplinary fields such as high-throughput sequencing, artificial intelligence, and nanocarrier technology offer new opportunities to refine epigenetic therapies. Future research should focus on enhancing drug specificity, optimizing targeted delivery, and accelerating clinical trials to facilitate the translation of epigenetics-based precision medicine in the diagnosis and treatment of SA-AKI.

DNA methylation/histone modification biomarkers in SA-AKI

Epigenetic modifications not only play a broad role in regulating the pathogenesis of SA-AKI but also show significant promise as biomarkers for both diagnosis and prognosis. Recent evidence suggests that different organs and cell types exhibit unique DNA methylation patterns. Tissue-specific methylation signatures identified in circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) reflect the pathological state of diseases and serve as disease-specific biomarkers [112]. For example, a study conducted methylome sequencing on plasma cfDNA from patients with SA-AKI, analyzing the DNA methylation contributions of various cell types based on their specificity. This study specifically explored the correlation between DNA methylation patterns in different renal cell types and SA-AKI. The results revealed that methylation markers specific to renal tubular epithelial cells and endothelial cells of renal origin accurately distinguished SA-AKI from SN-AKI, achieving an AUC of 0.92 [17]. Further functional enrichment analysis of differential DNA methylation showed that these changes were primarily associated with pathways related to inflammation, coagulation, and cellular metabolism, all of which are consistent with the pathogenesis of sepsis-induced acute kidney injury [17]. These findings offer new insights into the understanding and identification of SA-AKI, laying the groundwork for the development of novel diagnostic tools and therapeutic strategies. Interestingly, another study also identified unique DNA methylation patterns in vascular endothelial cells from human renal tissue and evaluated these methylation markers in patient plasma cfDNA. The results indicated an elevated concentration of cfDNA in the plasma of sepsis patients, accompanied by increased endothelial-specific DNA methylation markers [113]. Given that endothelial-specific DNA methylation may reflect early vascular damage in SA-AKI, detecting these markers could potentially aid in the early diagnosis of the condition. Beyond early diagnosis, epigenetic modifications also offer valuable insights into disease severity. One study observed a significant elevation in the H3K18 lactylation level in septic patients, which was positively correlated with disease severity scores such as APACHE II, SOFA, inflammatory cytokine levels, ICU length of stay, and duration of mechanical ventilation [114]. Collectively, these findings suggest that liquid biopsies utilizing tissue- and cell-specific epigenetic markers have the potential to expand the understanding of human disease dynamics. These markers could prove crucial for improving the sensitivity and specificity of liquid biopsies targeting specific diseases. Importantly, epigenetic-based markers offer significant clinical potential: they enable minimally invasive blood-based assessments with quantitative detection and are relatively conserved across highly heterogeneous diseases. However, further research is necessary to precisely map organ- and tissue-specific epigenetic patterns and their relationships with gene expression and phenotypic outcomes.

Conclusion

Epigenetic alterations in macrophages, neutrophils, ECs, and RTECs play a crucial role in the progression of SA-AKI. These modifications selectively regulate the transcription of key genes involved in inflammation, oxidative stress, microcirculatory dysfunction, and metabolic reprogramming, thereby influencing the severity of kidney damage. Recent preclinical studies have highlighted the potential of epigenetic-targeted drugs, such as DNMT inhibitors, SIRT activators, HDAC inhibitors, and HAT inhibitors, in alleviating inflammation and mitigating cell damage, demonstrating promising prospects for SA-AKI treatment. However, the clinical safety and efficacy of these therapies require further validation. Moreover, tissue-specific biomarkers based on DNA methylation and histone modifications show tremendous potential in the diagnosis and prognosis assessment of SA-AKI, though large-scale, multi-center studies are necessary to validate their clinical applicability. In summary, epigenetic modifications are central to the pathogenesis and progression of SA-AKI, and their roles as biomarkers and therapeutic targets offer new opportunities for early diagnosis and personalized treatment.
