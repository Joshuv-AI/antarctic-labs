Prevalence and genotypic characterization of Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) harboring femA and tetM in canine and feline otitis externa
Authors: Mahdi Rezaverdinejad, Amir Tukmechi, Mojtaba Hadian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the molecular prevalence and antibiotic resistance patterns of Staphylococcus aureus in companion animals with otitis externa in Urmia, Iran. From July 2024 to May 2025, 108 ear swabs were collected from affected dogs (n = 56) and cats (n = 52). Isolates were confirmed using biochemical tests and PCR targeting 16 S rRNA and femA genes. Susceptibility testing followed CLSI standards, and PCR was used to detect mecA and tetM genes. S. aureus was identified in 18 samples (16.66%), with a higher isolation rate in dogs (19.64%) compared to cats (13.46%). Phenotypic analysis revealed alarming resistance levels, particularly in dogs against penicillin (90.91%) and tetracycline (81.8%). Molecular characterization showed that 27.77% of isolates harbored the mecA gene (MRSA), and 55.55% carried the tetM gene. A significant correlation was observed between animals aged > 4 years and the carriage of resistance genes (P < 0.05). These findings highlight a significant prevalence of MRSA and multidrug-resistant strains in the region. The high rate of beta-lactam resistance suggests that empirical therapy is no longer effective. Consequently, routine antimicrobial susceptibility testing and a One Health approach are crucial to manage these infections and mitigate zoonotic transmission risks.Keywords: Staphylococcus aureus, Methicillin Resistance (MRSA), Otitis externa, mecA gene, tetM gene, Companion animalsSubject terms: Diseases, Microbiology, Molecular biology

Introduction

Otitis externa (OE) is a predominant reason for veterinary consultation, presenting significant management challenges due to recurrence and chronicity1. Although the primary etiology of otitis externa is often allergic (e.g., atopic dermatitis, food hypersensitivity), parasitic, or anatomical, the clinical course of the disease is almost invariably complicated by secondary bacterial and fungal infections that perpetuate inflammation and hinder recovery. Among the wide range of commensal organisms occupying skin and mucosal surfaces, Staphylococcus aureus has emerged as a pathogen of concern2. Although Staphylococcus pseudintermedius is historically and frequently considered the dominant coagulase-positive staphylococcus (CoPS) in dogs, S. aureus has gained increasing importance due to its expansive repertoire of virulence factors, enhanced capacity for biofilm formation, and distinct propensity to acquire multidrug resistance genes. Unlike S. pseudintermedius, S. aureus possesses a much higher zoonotic potential, and recent epidemiological shifts suggest that this bacterium is blurring the traditional boundaries between human and animal pathogens, becoming a shared health challenge3.

The transition of S. aureus from a commensal resident to a virulent pathogen in the ear canal results from a complex interaction between host immune compromise and bacterial virulence factors4. When the ear microenvironment changes characterized by increased humidity, pH alterations, and epithelial damage S. aureus can proliferate rapidly5,6. A critical factor in the recalcitrance of these infections is the bacterium’s ability to survive the harsh environmental conditions of the ear7.

The epidemiology of MRSA in veterinary settings is concerning. MRSA, initially considered a hospital-acquired pathogen (HA-MRSA) in humans, has established successful lineages in companion animals. Studies from various geographical regions, from Europe to Asia, have reported varying prevalence rates of MRSA in dogs and cats, often reflecting the clonal types present in their human owners8,9. This parallel evolution suggests that companion animals are not merely passive victims of MRSA but active participants in the transmission cycle10. Furthermore, the emergence of MDR strains showing resistance to three or more antimicrobial classes (e.g., fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, and macrolides) in addition to beta-lactams has escalated the threat level11. Co-selection of resistance genes implies that the use of non-beta-lactam antibiotics can inadvertently lead to the selection of MRSA, creating a therapeutic dead-end for many veterinary clinicians12.

The close bond between humans and their pets creates a unique ecological niche for the exchange of resistant bacteria, necessitating a One Health approach for surveillance. Companion animals share the home environment, furniture, and often beds with their owners, facilitating the direct transmission of staphylococci through physical contact, aerosols, or shedding of dander13. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have quantified this risk, showing that MRSA colonization rates in pets are significantly higher in households where owners are infected or work in healthcare settings14,15. This phenomenon, often termed reverse zoonosis or anthroponosis, implies that humans may act as the primary reservoir of S. aureus for pets, with animals subsequently acting as vectors for reinfection or dissemination to other animals16.

Molecular epidemiological studies using techniques such as spa typing and MLST have confirmed that MRSA clones isolated from pets (e.g., ST22, ST398) are often indistinguishable from major human epidemic clones17. In cases of recurrent otitis or pyoderma in pets, failure to identify and treat colonized human contacts can lead to a cycle of reinfection that undermines veterinary therapeutic efforts. Therefore, the tracking of MRSA in a pet is not merely a veterinary issue but a sentinel event with profound implications for public health18,19.

In the era of molecular diagnostics, reliance on phenotypic susceptibility testing alone is insufficient for a comprehensive understanding of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) dynamics. While mecA detection is the gold standard for defining methicillin resistance, accurate species identification is the first critical step20,21. The femA gene (factor essential for methicillin resistance) serves as a highly specific diagnostic marker unique to S. aureus22. This gene plays a pivotal role in the synthesis of the pentaglycine interpeptide bridge of the peptidoglycan cell wall. Its detection serves a dual purpose: it acts as a robust internal control to verify species identity and ensures that the observed methicillin resistance is genuinely associated with S. aureus and not coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) or other CoPS such as S. pseudintermedius23.

Moreover, the MRSA resistance profile is rarely limited to beta-lactams. Tetracyclines, being one of the oldest and most widely used antibiotic classes in veterinary medicine due to their broad spectrum and cost-effectiveness, have exerted immense selective pressure on bacterial populations24,25. Tetracycline resistance in staphylococci is primarily mediated by ribosomal protection proteins encoded by genes such as tetM and tetO. The presence of the tetM gene holds significance beyond clinical resistance to tetracyclines. This gene is often located on conjugative transposons (e.g., the Tn916 family), which are major vehicles for horizontal gene transfer (HGT) of resistance genes between different bacterial species26,27. Therefore, tracking tetM alongside mecA is a valuable indicator for assessing co-selection potential; a phenomenon that explains how MDR clones persist in the population even in the absence of beta-lactam antibiotic use28.

Despite the vast volume of literature documenting MRSA prevalence in Europe, North America, and East Asia, data from the Middle East, and particularly Iran, remain fragmented and limited. Most existing studies in this region have focused on phenotypic characteristics or targeted S. aureus in food-producing animals, leaving a significant knowledge gap regarding the molecular epidemiology of ear infections in companion animals29. There is a distinct lack of data simultaneously assessing the presence of mecA, femA, and tetM genes in clinical isolates from dogs and cats with otitis externa. This paucity of information hinders the development of evidence-based antimicrobial stewardship guidelines adapted to local epidemiology30.

Therefore, the present study was designed to address these deficiencies by conducting a cross-sectional molecular survey. The metropolis of Urmia in northwestern Iran was selected as a sentinel surveillance site. Its strategic border location facilitates the cross-border movement of animals and trade, potentially acting as a gateway for the introduction of diverse bacterial clones into the region. Furthermore, recent shifts in urban lifestyle and increased pet ownership in this area provide a suitable model for monitoring the transmission dynamics of resistant bacteria31. The specific objectives of this research were: to determine the prevalence of S. aureus in cases of canine and feline otitis externa; to determine phenotypic resistance profiles against a panel of clinically relevant antibiotics; and to molecularly characterize the isolates for the presence of mecA, femA, and tetM genes. By correlating genotypic markers with phenotypic resistance, this study aims to provide one of the few regional molecular profiles of MRSA in the companion animal population, thereby informing veterinary treatment protocols and public health risk assessments.

Materials and methods

Study design, population, and sampling criteria

This cross-sectional study was designed to investigate the presence of Methicillin-Resistant S. aureus (MRSA) in ear exudates of cats and dogs suffering from otitis externa in the northwest region of Iran. Urmia (located at latitude 37°32’ N and longitude 45°04’ E) features a cold semi-arid climate with cold, snowy winters and hot, dry summers, classified climatically as a cold temperate region (Fig. 1). The sampling campaign was conducted over an 11-month period, from July 2024 to May 2025. This timeframe was selected to encompass seasonal variations (summer to mid-spring) that may influence the incidence of dermatological diseases in companion animals. All procedures involving animals were conducted in strict accordance with the ethical guidelines and regulations for the care and use of animals in research. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Urmia University (Approval ID: IR-UU-AEC- 3/107). Furthermore, written informed consent was obtained from all pet owners prior to the enrollment of their animals in the study.

Geographic map of the study locations in West Azerbaijan, Iran.

The study population comprised 108 companion animals, consisting of 56 dogs (Canis lupus familiaris) and 52 cats (Felis catus) referred to five distinct veterinary centers, including the university veterinary teaching hospital, private small animal clinics, and a local animal shelter. This diverse sampling strategy was employed to minimize selection bias and ensure that the isolates represented a broad range of the local companion animal population.

Animals were eligible for inclusion if they exhibited clinical signs consistent with otitis externa (OE) upon otoscopic examination. These signs included erythema (redness), edema of the ear canal, head shaking, scratching, pain upon palpation, and the presence of purulent or ceruminous discharge. Animals that had received systemic or topical antimicrobial treatment within two weeks prior to presentation were excluded to prevent false-negative culture results and to avoid masking the true resistance profile of the causative agents2. Detailed demographic data, including age, sex, breed, living conditions (indoor vs. outdoor), and history of recurrent ear infections, were recorded for each case using a standardized questionnaire.

Sample collection and transport

Sampling was performed under strict aseptic conditions to prevent contamination with environmental flora or commensals of the external ear pinna. Prior to sampling, the external ear was gently cleaned with 70% ethanol to remove surface contaminants. Sterile cotton-tipped swabs were carefully inserted into the vertical and horizontal ear canals to contact the exudate while avoiding contact with the external skin. Two swabs were collected from each affected ear: one for direct microscopic examination (Gram staining) and the other for bacterial culture. To ensure the viability of fastidious organisms during transport, culture swabs were immediately placed in Stuart transport medium (Merck, Germany)8. All samples were transported to the Microbiology Laboratory of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine in a cool box (4 °C) within 2 to 4 h of collection and processed immediately upon arrival32.

Culture and phenotypic identification of S. aureus

Upon arrival at the laboratory, the collected ear swabs were processed immediately, within 1 h of arrival, for bacterial isolation. Each swab was first incubated in 5 mL of Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB; Merck, Germany) containing 6% sodium chloride for 24 h at 37 °C for initial enrichment17. Subsequently, the bacterial suspension was cultured on Mannitol Salt Agar (MSA; Merck, Germany) and 5% Sheep Blood Agar (BA; Merck, Germany) and incubated for 24 to 48 h at 37 °C under aerobic conditions. Yellow colonies with a color change halo on MSA media were selected as suspected S. aureus colonies. Initial identification was performed using Gram staining (Gram-positive cocci), catalase test (with 3% hydrogen peroxide), and tube coagulase test (with rabbit plasma). For more precise confirmation, coagulase-positive colonies were identified using the VITEK® 2 Compact automated system (bioMérieux, USA) with Gram-Positive Cocci (GPC) cards. The standard S. aureus strain (ATCC 25923) was used as quality control in all culture stages. Confirmed isolates were stored in TSB containing 20% glycerol at -80 °C8,33.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

Antibiotic susceptibility of S. aureus isolates was assessed using the disk diffusion method (Kirby-Bauer) on Mueller-Hinton Agar (MHA; Merck, Germany) according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI, 2024) guidelines. Antibiotics tested were Cefoxitin (30 µg), Penicillin (10 units), Cefepime (30 µg), and Tetracycline (30 µg) (Mast Group, UK). Antibiotic disks were placed on the surface of the culture medium, and after incubation for 24 h at 37 °C, the diameters of inhibition zones were measured. The standard S. aureus strain (ATCC 25923) was used as quality control. For phenotypic screening of methicillin resistance (MRSA), the cefoxitin disk was used (as a surrogate for methicillin) according to CLSI standards. Isolates with an inhibition zone diameter ≤ 21 mm were considered methicillin-resistant and were subjected to PCR for detection of the mecA gene for confirmation. Antibiotic susceptibility results were reported as Susceptible (S), Resistant (R), or Intermediate (I) according to CLSI standards.

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from confirmed S. aureus isolates using a DNA extraction kit (Favorgen, Taiwan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, bacterial cells from an overnight culture in TSB were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 10 min. The bacterial pellet was resuspended in lysis buffer containing lysozyme (20 mg/mL) and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. DNA was extracted using silica columns, eluted in 100 µL of elution buffer, and stored at -20 °C. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and samples with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 were used for subsequent analyses34,35.

Molecular identification and detection of resistance genes

Conventional PCR was employed for the identification of S. aureus and confirmation of antibiotic resistance by amplifying 16 S rRNA, mecA, femA, and tetM genes36. The femA gene (factor essential for methicillin resistance) serves as a definitive species-specific molecular marker for S. aureus. Its detection was used for molecular confirmation of species identity12. The methicillin resistance gene (mecA) encodes the PBP2a protein and is the definitive marker for MRSA37. The tetracycline resistance gene (tetM) encodes a ribosomal protection protein, a common mechanism of tetracycline resistance in companion animals.

PCR reactions were performed in a volume of 25 µL, containing 5 µL template DNA, 1 µL of each primer (10 µM concentration), 12.5 µL master mix (Ampligon, Germany), and sterile distilled water to complete the volume. Gel electrophoresis

PCR products were separated by electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel prepared in 1x TBE buffer (Tris-Borate-EDTA). The gel was stained with a safe nucleic acid stain (SafeStain, SinaClon) and viewed under UV light using a Gel Doc system. A 100 bp DNA ladder was used as a molecular weight marker to verify amplicon size. The standard S. aureus strain (ATCC 43300) was used as a positive control, and sterile distilled water as a negative control36,38.

Sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

For molecular confirmation and phylogenetic analysis, a representative subset of PCR products was selected. Specifically, for each target gene (16 S rRNA, mecA, tetM, and femA), one PCR-positive isolate from a canine host and one from a feline host were randomly chosen. These selected positive PCR products were purified using a PCR purification kit (Favorgen, Taiwan) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The purified amplicons were dispatched to Pishgam Biotechnology Company (Tehran, Iran) for bidirectional DNA sequencing using the Sanger chain-termination method on an automated ABI 3730xl DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) with the forward and reverse primers listed in Table 1. The obtained chromatograms were visually inspected, and sequence quality was checked using Geneious Prime software (version 2023.2) to trim low-quality terminal bases. The consensus sequences were then subjected to the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) on the NCBI server (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) for homology analysis against reference sequences in the GenBank database. Finally, phylogenetic trees were constructed using MEGA software (version 12) via the Maximum Likelihood method with the Kimura 2-parameter model and 1000 bootstrap replicates. The confirmed nucleotide sequences were subsequently registered in the GenBank database, and accession numbers were obtained39.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to determine the frequency and percentage of isolates. The relationship between the presence of resistance genes and categorical variables (age, sex, season, and location) was evaluated using the Chi-square test (X2) or Fisher’s Exact test, where appropriate. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Bacterial isolation and prevalence

In this cross-sectional study, a total of 108 clinical samples were collected from ear exudates of companion animals with signs of otitis externa (56 dogs and 52 cats). Initial phenotypic screening revealed that 18 isolates exhibited characteristic golden-yellow colonies with mannitol fermentation on Mannitol Salt Agar (MSA) and beta-hemolysis on blood agar. These 18 isolates were biochemically confirmed as S. aureus based on being Gram-positive cocci, catalase-positive, and tube coagulase-positive, which was further verified by the VITEK 2 automated system. Subsequent molecular analysis targeting the genus-specific 16 S rRNA gene confirmed the definitive presence of S. aureus in all 18 phenotypically identified samples, showing a distinct amplicon size of 757 bp (Fig. 3A). Thus, the overall prevalence of S. aureus in the study population was determined to be 16.66% (18/108).

Species-based breakdown analysis showed that S. aureus was isolated from 11 of 56 dog samples (19.64%) and 7 of 52 cat samples (13.46%). Although a higher numerical prevalence was observed in canine isolates, this difference between the two host species was not statistically significant (P > 0.05).

Antimicrobial susceptibility profiles

Antimicrobial susceptibility test results (disk diffusion method) on the 18 S. aureus isolates revealed distinct resistance patterns between dogs and cats. Penicillin resistance assessment indicated a very high rate; 90.91% of dog isolates and 71.42% of cat isolates were resistant to this antibiotic. Regarding methicillin resistance (based on Cefoxitin disk results), the resistance phenotype (MRSA) was observed in 18.18% of dog isolates and 14.29% of cat isolates.

The rate of tetracycline resistance in this study was substantial. 81.82% of dog isolates and 42.86% of cat isolates showed a tetracycline-resistant phenotype. Furthermore, resistance to Cefepime was noticeably higher in cats (28.57%) compared to dogs (9.09%). Molecular characterization

Molecular analysis was performed to identify mecA, femA, and tetM genes, yielding the following results (representative PCR amplification products are shown in Fig. 3). The femA gene, selected for species confirmation, was identified in all positive samples (18/18, 100%). The presence of the mecA gene, the definitive indicator for MRSA identification, was confirmed in 5 out of 18 isolates (27.77%). The distribution of this gene was higher in dogs (36.36%, 4 cases) compared to cats (14.28%, 1 case). This finding aligns with phenotypic results, demonstrating a higher frequency of resistance determinants in canine isolates. The tetM gene had the highest frequency among the investigated genes, identified in 10 isolates (55.55%). The tetM gene was identified in 54.54% of dog isolates (6/11) and 57.14% of cat isolates (4/7). Comparison with phenotypic data revealed that the correlation was not exact, demonstrating distinct host-dependent discrepancies. In canine isolates, phenotypic tetracycline resistance (81.82%) exceeded tetM prevalence, suggesting the involvement of alternative resistance mechanisms (such as tetK, tetL, or efflux pumps) in the remaining tetM-negative resistant strains. Conversely, in feline isolates, tetM genotypic prevalence (57.14%) was higher than phenotypic resistance (42.86%), indicating the presence of a phenotypically silent or unexpressed resistance determinant in one isolate. Notably, only a single multidrug-resistant (MDR) S. aureus isolate (5.56%, 1/18) was identified in this study, which was recovered exclusively from a canine sample, representing 9.09% (1/11) of the total dog isolates.

Regarding seasonal distribution, isolates were most frequently recovered in spring. Regarding sampling location, the highest overall infection rate was observed in animal shelters and the lowest in veterinary clinics and hospitals, whereas the resistance gene prevalence followed the opposite trend, with clinics and hospitals exhibiting the highest frequency of resistance genes. Furthermore, comparison between animal species showed that canine hosts exhibited a higher overall prevalence of both S. aureus infection and resistance genes than felines. Overall, 27.77% of all S. aureus isolates in this study were identified as MRSA strains (carrying mecA), with dogs acting as the predominant reservoir for these resistant pathogens compared to cats.

Discussion

The alarming rise of Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR) in zoonotic pathogens, particularly coagulase-positive staphylococci, has become a major challenge for veterinary medicine and public health in the 21st century. The present study aimed to analyze the molecular epidemiology and resistance patterns of S. aureus in dogs and cats with otitis externa in the Urmia region. Our findings of a significant prevalence of Methicillin-Resistant (MRSA) strains and severe resistance to penicillin serve as a serious warning for the clinical management of ear infections and the potential risk of zoonotic transmission in this geographical area.

In the current research, the overall prevalence rate of S. aureus in clinical otitis externa samples was determined to be 16.66% (18/108). This finding is remarkably consistent with the results of Bourély et al.2 in France, who identified staphylococci as major agents of otitis in dogs, although our prevalence rate is slightly lower than reports by Youn et al.40 in Zambia. These differences can be attributed to geographical variables, differences in animal demographics, and, most importantly, the history of antibiotic usage in different regions.

Species breakdown revealed that dogs (19.64%) are significantly more affected by S. aureus infection than cats (13.46%). This observation is consistent with findings by Fessler et al.41 and Wedley et al.42. Anatomically and physiologically, the canine ear canal possesses a longer vertical and horizontal structure (L-shaped), which complicates ventilation and provides a warm, moist environment for bacterial proliferation. Furthermore, Kottler et al.43 noted that dogs are exposed to a higher bacterial load due to greater social interactions with the outdoors and other animals. In contrast, cats, with their obsessive grooming behaviors, may mechanically reduce bacterial load, although MRSA isolation from them remains a serious threat44.

One of the prominent yet concerning findings of this study was the exceptionally high rate of penicillin resistance (90.91% in dogs and 71.42% in cats). This level of resistance indicates the widespread establishment of the blaZ gene in the region’s bacterial population. This gene encodes the penicillinase (beta-lactamase) enzyme, which hydrolyzes the beta-lactam ring of classical antibiotics, rendering them inactive. Our results align with reports by Rana et al.9 in Bangladesh and Leite et al.19 in Brazil, who reported similar penicillin resistance rates. These data strongly suggest that penicillin and its beta-lactamase-sensitive derivatives should no longer be used as a first-line empirical therapy in pet otitis.

The tetM gene was identified in 55.55% of isolates. However, the phenotypic resistance to tetracycline was notably higher (81.82%). This discordance suggests the presence of other resistance determinants not investigated in this study, such as tetK or tetL, which encode active efflux pumps. While tetM mediates ribosomal protection (conferring resistance to all tetracyclines, including minocycline), efflux genes typically confer resistance only to tetracycline. The high phenotypic resistance rate highlights the necessity of screening for a broader panel of tetracycline resistance genes in future studies.

The detection of 27.77% of S. aureus isolates containing the mecA gene (including 4 cases in dogs and 1 cases in cats) is one of the pivotal findings of this research. The presence of the mecA gene, carried on the Staphylococcal Chromosomal Cassette (SCCmec), leads to the expression of modified penicillin-binding protein (PBP2a). This protein has a very low affinity for all beta-lactam antibiotics, effectively rendering treatment with penicillins, cephalosporins, and carbapenems ineffective.

The prevalence rate of MRSA in the present study is higher compared to the early study by Loeffler et al.10 in Europe and the report by Dziva et al.3 in Trinidad, which may indicate an increasing trend in the dissemination of resistant clones in recent years. An important point in our study was the relative concordance between phenotypic resistance (Cefoxitin disk) and genotypic detection (mecA). However, as discussed by Rahman et al.45, instances of discordance may occur due to the phenomenon of heteroresistance or the presence of rarer resistance mechanisms like the mecC gene, which are not detected by conventional mecA primers. The presence of these resistant strains in pets, as mentioned in the systematic review by Abdullahi et al.15, severely increases the risk of clinical treatment failure and disease chronicity.

Pets are recognized as potential reservoirs for the transmission of resistant strains to humans (and vice versa) due to close physical contact with their owners. Although sampling from owners was not performed in this study, the isolation of MRSA strains with genetic characteristics shared between humans and animals in studies by Kottler et al.43 and Gomez-Sanz et al.46 reinforces the likelihood of interspecies transmission. The study by Algammal et al.14 demonstrated that MRSA strains can circulate between humans and pets (Ping-pong transmission). This is particularly dangerous for immunocompromised individuals, children, and the elderly who are in contact with infected animals. Therefore, our results extend beyond veterinary importance and highlight the necessity of strict hand and environmental hygiene for owners after contact with animals suffering from otitis.

Our demographic analysis revealed a dual and interesting pattern: while younger animals (under 2 years) showed a higher rate of bacterial infection, older animals (over 4 years) were significantly more likely to host strains containing resistance genes. Higher infection prevalence in young animals can be attributed to an incompletely developed immune system and high-risk social behaviors. Conversely, the accumulation of resistance genes in older animals reflects cumulative selection pressure, suggesting that older animals may have a history of cumulative antibiotic exposure over their lifetime, leading to the selection and proliferation of resistant clones. Furthermore, a longer lifespan inherently increases their cumulative exposure to diverse environmental and animal-associated pathogens. This prolonged exposure provides more opportunities to encounter and acquire a wider pool of novel AMR determinants, which can subsequently be disseminated among the host’s microflora via horizontal gene transfer mechanisms.

The prevalence of 38.9% in summer can be justified by increased environmental temperature and humidity. Kaben et al.44 noted that high humidity causes maceration of the ear epidermis and changes the quality of cerumen, creating a nutritious substrate for bacterial growth. Additionally, increased animal activity outdoors during warm seasons increases the likelihood of contact with contaminated environmental sources or swimming (which increases ear moisture).

Like any scientific study, this research had limitations. The sample size (108 cases), while sufficient for a preliminary study, may not fully reflect the genetic diversity of the pathogen at the national level40. Also, the lack of investigation into biofilm formation in isolated strains is a limitation; as Stephen et al.47 demonstrated, the ability to form biofilms in staphylococci plays a key role in the failure of topical otitis treatments. Furthermore, the lack of simultaneous sampling from animal owners and the non-use of high-resolution molecular typing methods (such as MLST or spa typing) limits our definitive interpretation regarding the transmission route (human-to-animal or vice versa). However, the similarity of observed antibiotic profiles to human reports in the region strengthens the hypothesis of strain circulation between the two hosts.

For future studies, it is suggested to use advanced molecular typing methods such as spa typing or MLST to accurately determine circulating clones and their phylogenetic origins48. Simultaneous sampling of animals and their owners should be performed to definitively prove zoonotic transmission pathways. The efficacy of new antimicrobial compounds or ear cleaning products on planktonic and biofilm forms of MRSA strains isolated in Iran should be evaluated.

In summary, this study confirms the concerning presence of Methicillin-Resistant S. aureus (MRSA) and Co-resistant strains in the pet population of Urmia. The high rate of penicillin resistance challenges traditional treatment protocols and dictates the necessity of routine antibiogram testing prior to drug prescription. Identifying older animals and warm seasons as risk factors helps clinicians perform more rigorous screening for these high-risk groups. Ultimately, These findings highlight the need for interdisciplinary collaboration between veterinarians and physicians under a One Health approach to monitor and control the spread of resistant pathogens.

Conclusion

The present study provides molecular insights into S. aureus epidemiology in cases of otitis externa in companion animals in the northwest region of Iran (Urmia). Our results confirm the alarming prevalence of Methicillin-Resistant (MRSA) strains carrying the mecA gene and a very high rate of phenotypic resistance to penicillin and tetracycline in dog and cat populations. The identification of older animals (> 4 years) as more frequent carriers of resistance genes underscores the risk of cumulative antimicrobial exposure and serves as a serious warning for the emergence of co-resistant strains.

These findings indicate that empirical therapy with beta-lactam antibiotics in cases of pet otitis is no longer a reliable approach, and performing Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (Antibiogram) before prescribing medication must become a clinical standard. Furthermore, the role of pets as potential reservoirs for the transmission of resistant strains to humans highlights the necessity of adopting a One Health approach and closer cooperation between veterinarians and physicians. For future studies, continuous monitoring using advanced molecular typing methods (such as MLST) and simultaneous sampling from pet owners is suggested to more accurately understand the dynamics of zoonotic transmission.
