Clinical Recommendations for the Management of Male Infertility: A Consensus from the Korean Society for Sexual Medicine and Andrology (KSSMA) Clinical Practice Guideline Committee
Authors: Young Joon Moon, Jae Yong Jeong, Kang Hee Shim, Dong Hyuk Kang, Hae Do Jung, Dong Sup Lee, Seung-Hun Song, Hyun Jun Park, Joo Yong Lee, Korean Society for Sexual Medicine and Andrology Clinical Practice Guideline Committee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Male infertility significantly contributes to the global reproductive burden and serves as a critical indicator of overall male health. As clinical pathways have historically focused on female-factor evaluation and assisted reproductive technology (ART), there is an increasing need for etiology-directed and time-sensitive male management strategies. This Clinical Recommendation, developed by the Korean Society for Sexual Medicine and Andrology Guideline Committee, synthesizes recent evidence from major international guidelines with specific considerations for the Korean healthcare environment. Management should be couple-centered, integrating the female partner’s reproductive timeline to avoid preventable delays in effective therapy. Core recommendations emphasize addressing modifiable risk factors as foundational care, while restricting empiric pharmacotherapy to phenotype-driven, time-limited trials. Disease-specific pathways for varicocele, endocrine disorders, and obstructive/non-obstructive azoospermia are delineated, emphasizing the individualized selection between microsurgical reconstruction and surgical sperm retrieval. In the ART interface, the selective use of intracytoplasmic sperm injection is recommended, prioritizing safety and the prevention of multiple pregnancies. Furthermore, structured follow-up should prioritize patient-important outcomes, such as live birth and treatment burden, over intermediate semen parameter changes. By harmonizing international evidence with local clinical constraints, including national insurance structures and referral patterns, these recommendations aim to facilitate consistent, safe, and patient-centered care for infertile men in Korea.

INTRODUCTION

Male infertility is a major contributor to the global infertility burden, implicated in nearly half of all cases involving couples seeking fertility care. Historically, clinical pathways have been skewed toward female-factor evaluation and assisted reproductive technology (ART). This imbalance often leads to delayed identification of treatable or modifiable male conditions and missed opportunities to optimize outcomes through targeted male-focused interventions. Contemporary guidance increasingly emphasizes that male infertility is not merely a reproductive diagnosis but also a potential marker of broader health risks, necessitating a structured management strategy that integrates reproductive goals with general health optimization [12].

Over the past few years, several international societies have updated their evidence-based recommendations, including the American Urological Association/American Society for Reproductive Medicine (AUA/ASRM) 2024 guideline amendment and the European Association of Urology (EAU) 2025 update. These reflect significant advances in surgical techniques, genetic testing, adjunctive medical therapies, and the strategic integration of ART [12]. Concurrently, national and topic-specific efforts—such as the first Australian evidence-based male infertility guideline and the Canadian Urological Association (CUA) guideline on azoospermia—have provided practical, health-system–adapted guidance and Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE)-informed recommendations for high-impact clinical decision points [34]. Furthermore, the Global Andrology Forum (GAF) has produced Delphi- and GRADE-based statements for controversial areas including non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA), varicocele, and sperm DNA fragmentation (SDF), aiming to standardize care where evidence remains evolving [567].

The Korean Society for Sexual Medicine and Andrology (KSSMA) Clinical Practice Guideline Committee has a long-standing commitment to evidence synthesis in andrology. While diagnostic frameworks have been previously established to emphasize comprehensive assessment [7], the present clinical recommendation focuses exclusively on management. This document aligns treatment strategies with identified etiologies, couple-specific factors (notably female partner age and ovarian reserve), and time-to-pregnancy priorities. By separating the diagnostic work-up (“what to treat”) from the therapeutic domain, we aim to provide clearer guidance on “how to treat,” “when to escalate,” and “how to counsel” in real-world practice [128].

Management of male infertility spans a spectrum from non-invasive to invasive interventions. Non-invasive approaches include counseling on lifestyle modifications, mitigation of gonadotoxic exposures, and selective medical therapy for specific endocrine phenotypes. Invasive management encompasses microsurgical or radiologic correction of anatomic lesions (e.g., clinical varicocele), reconstructive surgery for obstructive azoospermia (OA), and surgical sperm retrieval (SSR) for both OA and NOA, often in coordination with ART. The evidence base remains heterogeneous; while some interventions (e.g., SSR and varicocelectomy) are supported by robust data, others—such as neoadjuvant hormonal manipulation in NOA—rely on expert consensus. Consequently, modern guidelines prioritize transparent grading and shared decision-making regarding benefits, harms, costs, and potential delays to ART [1234567910].

A central goal of this KSSMA recommendation is contextualization. Even with international alignment on evidence, implementation varies by healthcare system, reimbursement structure, and referral patterns. For instance, National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidance provides a model for standardizing ART escalation and counseling within a national system [11], while Australia’s guidelines demonstrate how to translate international evidence into actionable national recommendations [3]. Similarly, the CUA’s structured framework for azoospermia addresses questions directly relevant to Korean practice, such as the utility of cryopreservation after SSR and the role of hormonal pre-treatment [4].

Accordingly, this document aims to: (1) synthesize high-quality evidence and recent international guidance into concise, graded clinical recommendations for male infertility management; (2) provide practical algorithms for major management pathways; and (3) highlight Korea-specific considerations—particularly counseling points and reimbursement-related decision nodes—to facilitate consistent, safe, and patient-centered care.

METHODS

1. Guideline scope and target users

This Clinical Recommendation was developed by the KSSMA Clinical Practice Guideline Committee to provide evidence-informed, Korea-adapted recommendations for the management of male infertility. The scope intentionally excludes the diagnostic work-up, which has been addressed separately in a contemporary diagnostic framework paper [8]. The target users are urologists/andrologists, reproductive endocrinologists, fertility specialists, and allied clinicians involved in couple-based infertility care.

2. Key clinical questions and outcomes

We prespecified management-focused clinical questions using a PICO framework (Population–Intervention–Comparator–Outcomes), organized into four domains:

(1) General (non-invasive) management: lifestyle and modifiable factors, medical therapy for selected endocrine phenotypes.(2) Correctable male-factor conditions: including varicocele and ejaculatory dysfunction–related interventions.(3) Azoospermia management: obstructive vs. non-obstructive; reconstruction vs. SSR and ART integration.(4) Adjunctive and emerging areas: selective use of SDF–informed strategies.

Outcomes prioritized for recommendation-making included live birth/ongoing pregnancy, natural conception, semen parameter improvement, sperm retrieval rates (for azoospermia), procedure-related harms, time-to-pregnancy, and patient-reported outcomes (treatment burden, decision regret), with additional consideration of feasibility and resource use in the Korean healthcare environment [123456711].

3. Evidence sources and literature search strategy

Given the availability of multiple high-quality, recently updated guidelines, we adopted a hybrid approach combining (1) guideline-based evidence synthesis and (2) focused searches for key primary studies and systematic reviews. Core evidence sources were the AUA/ASRM male infertility guideline (2024 amendment), the EAU Sexual and Reproductive Health guideline (2025 update), and national guidance from Australia, Canada (CUA), NICE, and the GAF consensus series and the latest national fertility treatment registry reports in Korea [12345671112]. In addition, domestic evidence was supplemented by analyzing the latest national fertility treatment registry reports to ensure the recommendations were operational within the Korean healthcare system. To ensure up-to-date coverage, we performed focused literature searches in PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library for randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews published between January 2024 and December, 2025. Where needed, we incorporated methodological or definitional clarifications from the World Health Organization (WHO) 6th semen manual to support consistent terminology across evidence sources [1314].

4. Evidence appraisal and handling of discordant recommendations

Recommendations were informed by the certainty and direction of evidence as presented in the source guidelines. When different guidelines reached different conclusions (e.g., selective use of SDF testing; endocrine manipulation prior to SSR in NOA) or when significant evidence emerged post-publication of the source guidelines, the committee directly evaluated:

(1) differences in populations and eligibility criteria,(2) outcome prioritization (semen parameters vs. live birth),(3) the certainty of evidence and risk of bias, and(4) contextual factors affecting implementation in Korea (availability of microsurgery/SSR expertise, ART access, and reimbursement structure) [1234567].

Notably, in finalizing these Korea-specific considerations, the committee utilized clinical expert consensus and the aforementioned national registry data [12] rather than a systematic search of local bibliographic databases, given the limited number of large-scale domestic clinical trials in these specific domains. In areas with very low certainty evidence, we favored transparent, conditional recommendations emphasizing shared decision-making and explicit counseling about expected benefit, uncertainty, harms, and opportunity costs (particularly delay to ART) [456711].

5. Consensus process and formulation of recommendations

Draft recommendation statements were prepared by assigned subgroups and iteratively refined for clinical clarity and feasibility in Korean practice. For controversial settings (e.g., NOA and peri-SSR decision points), we leveraged the structured GRADE Evidence to Decision frameworks of the GAF and CUA to maintain transparency [4567]. To ensure objectivity, all committee members disclosed potential conflicts of interest (COI), and those with significant conflicts were recused from drafting or voting on specific recommendations related to those interests. Final statements were approved by committee-wide consensus.

PRINCIPLES OF MANAGEMENT

Management of male infertility should be etiology-directed, couple-centered, time-sensitive, and health-oriented. While semen parameter improvement serves as a useful intermediate outcome to guide the clinical decision between continuing conservative management and escalating to ART, the ultimate goals remain live birth/ongoing pregnancy, minimizing treatment burden, and avoiding preventable delays to effective therapy [11]. These principles are particularly important because male infertility frequently coexists with modifiable lifestyle and metabolic risk factors, and accumulating data suggest that impaired semen quality may reflect broader health vulnerability, offering a “teachable moment” for preventive care [1516]. To enhance clinical usability, these principles are summarized as draft KSSMA recommendation statements with a proposed strength of recommendation and key supporting international guideline anchors (Table 1).

1. Couple-centered care and time-to-pregnancy awareness

Male infertility management should be planned in parallel with the female partner’s evaluation and reproductive timeline. Female age and ovarian reserve strongly influence whether to prioritize (1) male-factor optimization first (e.g., reversible causes, medical therapy trials) vs. (2) early escalation to ART with/without SSR. A practical approach is to define, at the initial management visit, a time horizon (e.g., 3 to 6 months for potentially reversible interventions) after which ART escalation should be reconsidered if pregnancy has not occurred. This specific timeframe reflects the biological duration of the spermatogenic cycle (approximately 74 days) and the clinical observation that maximum semen parameter improvements, such as those following varicocelectomy or medical therapy, typically manifest within this window. This time-sensitive framework reduces opportunity costs from prolonged empiric therapy and supports shared decision-making aligned with couple preferences.

2. Address reversible and modifiable factors early

Before initiating medical or invasive interventions, clinicians should systematically address modifiable exposures and comorbidities known to impair spermatogenesis and sperm function, including smoking, obesity, and heat exposure [17181920]. For example, cigarette smoking is associated with poorer semen parameters in meta-analyses, supporting routine smoking cessation counseling as part of fertility care [17]. Obesity is linked to reproductive endocrine disruption and semen impairment, and interventional studies suggest that diet-induced weight loss can improve sperm concentration and total sperm count in men with obesity [1819]. Heat exposure and scrotal hyperthermia are associated with reversible deterioration of semen quality; common modern contributors include prolonged laptop use on the lap and other behaviors increasing scrotal temperature [2021].

3. Medical therapy should be phenotype-driven, not routine

Empiric pharmacologic therapy for all men with abnormal semen parameters is not evidence-based. Instead, medical management should be phenotype-driven, targeting specific endocrine or clinical contexts:

(1) Fertility-preserving alternatives to exogenous testosterone: In men with hypogonadal symptoms who desire fertility, selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) such as clomiphene can raise endogenous testosterone and gonadotropins without suppressing spermatogenesis; evidence from systematic reviews supports hormonal improvement and generally favorable safety profiles [22].(2) Aromatase inhibitor therapy in selected men: In men with obesity-related or biochemical evidence of elevated estradiol relative to testosterone, aromatase inhibition (e.g., letrozole) has been studied as a means to improve endocrine balance and semen parameters; randomized and controlled data suggest potential improvement in sperm concentration in selected oligozoospermic men [23].(3) Gonadotropin therapy and recovery after androgen exposure: For men with infertility related to non-prescribed anabolic-androgenic steroid (AAS) use or testosterone exposure, recovery strategies using human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) with or without follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and/or adjunctive agents have supportive clinical literature and should be discussed in a structured cessation-and-recovery plan [24].

Because high-quality evidence linking these medical therapies directly to live birth is limited in many settings, treatment should be framed as a time-limited therapeutic trial with predefined reassessment points (hormones and semen parameters) and explicit ART contingency planning.

4. Adjuncts such as antioxidants: selective, transparent counseling

Oxidative stress is commonly invoked in male infertility, but the clinical benefit of antioxidant supplementation remains uncertain. Cochrane reviews suggest possible improvement in pregnancy or live birth outcomes, yet the certainty of evidence is low/very low and studies are heterogeneous [25]. Accordingly, antioxidants should be presented as an optional adjunct rather than core therapy, with counseling about uncertain benefit, product variability, and the importance of not delaying definitive interventions when time-to-pregnancy is critical.

5. Fertility preservation and procedural planning

Management should incorporate fertility preservation when there is a foreseeable risk of declining spermatogenesis (e.g., progressive severe oligozoospermia, planned gonadotoxic therapy, or anticipated surgery affecting the reproductive tract). For azoospermia or severe spermatogenic failure, counseling should include the role of SSR with cryopreservation as a strategy to avoid repeated procedures and reduce time pressure at ART cycles, when feasible and consistent with local practice patterns [26].

6. Shared decision-making and longitudinal follow-up

Given heterogeneous evidence and strong dependence on couple-specific factors, management should be delivered through shared decision-making with explicit discussion of: expected benefits, uncertainty, harms/complications, cost and access constraints, and the potential need to transition to ART. Follow-up should include reassessment of semen parameters at clinically appropriate intervals (often aligned with a spermatogenic cycle) and monitoring of endocrine or metabolic comorbidities when present.

DISEASE-SPECIFIC MANAGEMENT

Disease-specific management of male infertility should be etiology-directed and aligned with the couple’s reproductive timeline, prioritizing interventions that are reversible, evidence-supported, and feasible without causing avoidable delays to ART [1235611]. Table 2 summarizes the proposed KSSMA disease-specific recommendation statements with suggested strength and concise guideline anchors.

1. Varicocele-associated infertility

Varicocele repair may be considered for men with clinical (palpable) varicocele, infertility, and abnormal semen parameters, particularly when the female partner has favorable reproductive potential and time allows for post-treatment reassessment [123611]. Treatment of subclinical varicocele is generally not recommended [136]. In NOA with varicocele, repair should be selective and preference-sensitive due to inconsistent evidence and potential delay to SSR/ART [14510]. However, it should be noted that a recent meta-analysis cited in the EAU 2025 update suggests improved sperm retrieval rates (SRR; odds ratio 2.65) after varicocelectomy in NOA men with clinical varicocele, although high-quality controlled data remain limited [2].

2. Endocrine disorders

For hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, induction of spermatogenesis with hCG±FSH is the standard disease-specific therapy [27]. In men desiring fertility, exogenous testosterone should be avoided because it suppresses spermatogenesis [12]. For functional hypogonadism (often obesity-related), lifestyle optimization is foundational, with phenotype-driven medical therapy considered selectively and as a time-limited trial [12223].

3. Infection/inflammation and leukocytospermia

Antibiotics should be reserved for confirmed infection or strong clinical suspicion. Routine prolonged empiric treatment solely for leukocytospermia has uncertain fertility benefit and should not delay definitive management when time-to-pregnancy is critical [111].

4. Ejaculatory dysfunction and sperm delivery disorders

Management should be tailored to etiology and may include pharmacologic therapy (e.g., for retrograde ejaculation), assisted ejaculation, sperm retrieval from urine or the reproductive tract, and ART when required [111].

5. Obstructive azoospermia: reconstruction vs. SSR+ART

Both microsurgical reconstruction and SSR with intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) are acceptable pathways in OA, with selection driven by obstruction site, female partner age/ovarian reserve, desired family size, and local expertise/cost/access [12829]. When reconstruction is planned, discussion of simultaneous sperm retrieval and cryopreservation may be reasonable in selected settings [1].

6. NOA: SSR-centered strategy

NOA management should prioritize standardized SSR planning, with microdissection testicular sperm extraction (micro-TESE) preferred where expertise is available [25]. Hormonal “pre-treatment” aimed solely at improving retrieval or live birth remains uncertain and should be considered selectively with explicit counseling on limited evidence and opportunity costs [4510]. While the committee recognizes the current lack of high-certainty evidence for routine use, in cases where medical therapy is pursued after shared decision-making, it should be administered as a strictly monitored, time-limited trial with predefined reassessment milestones to avoid delaying definitive surgical intervention. Cryopreservation is a practical strategy to reduce repeat procedures, and fresh vs. frozen-thawed testicular sperm can be used pragmatically based on center practice [430].

7. Severe male factor, SDF-informed strategies, and ART interface

Early coordination with reproductive medicine is essential for severe male factor infertility [111]. SDF testing and SDF-informed interventions may be considered selectively in scenarios such as unexplained infertility or recurrent ART failure, recognizing heterogeneity and limited evidence for some strategies [7].

ART INTERFACE

The ART interface in male infertility management should be couple-centered and time-sensitive, with early coordination between andrology and reproductive medicine to minimize opportunity costs from delayed escalation when prognosis for natural conception is low [12311]. Crucially, the medical and surgical management of male infertility and ART should not be viewed as competing strategies. Rather, they share a complementary relationship aimed at treating the underlying etiologies of infertility while maximizing the chances of a healthy pregnancy. Establishing a robust, multidisciplinary collaborative system between andrologists and reproductive endocrinologists is essential for optimizing this integrated care [31]. Practical decisions include (1) when to proceed to intrauterine insemination (IUI) vs. in vitro fertilization (IVF) vs. ICSI, (2) whether to pursue corrective male-factor interventions in parallel without delaying effective therapy, and (3) procedural planning such as sperm cryopreservation and selection of sperm source for ICSI in selected scenarios [12347]. The key decision points for the ART interface are summarized in Table 3.

1. Selecting IUI vs. IVF vs. ICSI

When considering IUI, total motile sperm count (TMSC) remains a useful predictor of success and should be integrated with the female partner’s age and ovarian reserve [1132]. When semen quality is substantially compromised, recurrent IUI failure is expected and early transition to IVF/ICSI is often more efficient within a defined time horizon [12311].

For IVF vs. ICSI, contemporary randomized trials and large datasets consistently indicate that routine ICSI does not improve live birth outcomes compared with conventional IVF in couples without severe male factor infertility, supporting a more selective approach to ICSI use [3233343536]. Accordingly, ICSI should be reserved for clear indications (e.g., severe male factor, prior fertilization failure, very low sperm numbers, or when only surgically retrieved sperm are available), consistent with guideline-based practice [123]. The shift toward selective ICSI usage emphasizes that ART should not be a surrogate for treating underlying male factors; rather, ART and male-factor optimization should be synchronized to enhance the overall quality and safety of the reproductive process.

2. Integrating male-factor treatment with ART timing

Male-factor optimization (e.g., addressing reversible exposures, endocrine correction, or varicocele repair when appropriate) can be pursued in parallel with ART planning when it does not jeopardize the couple’s reproductive timeline [123611]. A pragmatic strategy is to define an initial period for potentially reversible interventions (often one spermatogenic cycle) while simultaneously preparing ART pathways if prognosis is poor or female age/ovarian reserve mandates urgency [123].

3. Surgical sperm retrieval and cryopreservation planning

In azoospermia or severe spermatogenic failure, early discussion of SSR strategy and cryopreservation can reduce repeat procedures and avoid time pressure at the ART cycle [1234526]. Where feasible, aligning SSR and cryostorage logistics with the planned ART timeline is recommended, and frozen–thawed testicular sperm can be used pragmatically based on center practice given broadly comparable outcomes in many settings [26].

4. SDF-informed strategies and sperm source for ICSI

SDF testing and SDF-informed interventions should be considered selectively, particularly in scenarios such as recurrent ART failure, with clear counseling on assay variability and heterogeneous evidence [7]. In nonazoospermic men with persistently elevated SDF and repeated ICSI failure, use of testicular sperm for ICSI (Testi-ICSI) has been associated with improved clinical outcomes in systematic reviews/meta-analyses, but data quality is variable; therefore, this strategy should be limited to carefully selected couples with explicit counseling on uncertainty and procedural trade-offs [3738].

5. Minimizing ART-related harms and counseling on embryo transfer

While ART significantly improves the probability of conception, the hormonal therapies required for ovarian stimulation carry inherent maternal and neonatal risks [39]. Because ART choices influence maternal and neonatal risks, counseling should explicitly address prevention of multiple pregnancy but also the potential for complications such as ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS), preterm birth, and low birth weight [4041]. Embryo transfer decisions should follow contemporary evidence-based guidance emphasizing individualized embryo number selection based on prognosis and safety, aligning with international recommendations [42]. This is particularly relevant in time-sensitive couples where rapid escalation to ART is appropriate but should not increase avoidable downstream harms [12311].

FOLLOW-UP AND OUTCOME ASSESSMENT

Although the primary focus of this guideline is clinical management, accurate follow-up assessment necessitates the use of standardized diagnostic methodologies to ensure longitudinal comparability. Therefore, the following outcome evaluations are integrated with established diagnostic standards to guide therapeutic adjustments. Follow-up in male infertility should be structured, time-sensitive, and outcome-oriented, balancing (1) biologic latency of spermatogenic change, (2) the couple’s reproductive timeline (especially female age/ovarian reserve), and (3) the likelihood that additional monitoring will change management rather than simply “observe” abnormal results [1238111314]. Because semen parameters show substantial withinindividual variability, follow-up should prioritize trend assessment using standardized methods over singlepoint interpretation [13144344454647]. 2. When to repeat semen analysis: timing and interpretation

Because of biologic lag and variability, it is generally reasonable to obtain at least two semen analyses and to repeat testing at an interval that reflects a spermatogenic cycle (often ~2 to 3 months), with earlier reassessment mainly when sperm are very low/absent or when results would immediately change management [12344]. Evidence demonstrates limited reproducibility of semen analysis and supports repeat testing to avoid misclassification and inappropriate escalation/delay [4344454647]. Practical points:

(1) Use the same lab and standardized abstinence window where possible to reduce analytic noise [13144344454647].(2) Interpret changes as clinically meaningful only when they alter the couple-level plan (e.g., upgrading/downgrading IUI vs. IVF/ICSI pathways), not merely as statistical fluctuations [1231149].(3) If ART is time-critical, avoid prolonged serial retesting that does not change decisions; instead, integrate follow-up with an agreed time horizon (e.g., reassess at 8 to 12 weeks, then decide) [123].

3. Post-intervention follow-up: procedure- and therapy-specific considerations

After varicocele repair, most semen improvement is expected by around 3 months, with limited additional gains beyond 6 months in many cohorts; thus, a 3-month assessment is practical to determine whether to continue expectant management vs. escalate fertility treatment [123505152]. However, a subset may experience limited improvement or even “downgrading” of TMSC, supporting counseling that follow-up results may redirect ART planning [4953]. For adjunct oxidative/SDF-focused care, available meta-analytic data suggest SDF improvement can be observed during follow-up, but test selection and timing should remain selective and decision-linked rather than routine [754].

During medical therapy, follow-up should be phenotype-driven and focused on both efficacy and safety. For men receiving SERMs or other hormonal manipulation, schedule reassessment of symptoms, hormones, and semen parameters at predefined intervals (commonly 3 to 6 months) with explicit stop/transition criteria if targets are not met or if adverse effects occur [12355]. Emerging data suggest that “historical” milestones (e.g., 3 months) may be insufficient to capture maximal endocrine and semen response in some men treated with clomiphene; therefore, counseling should emphasize individualized timelines and ART contingencies [55].

After SSR/micro-TESE, follow-up should document sperm retrieval outcomes, cryopreservation status, and couple-level ART progress. For men considering repeat micro-TESE after failure, counseling should include realistic retrieval expectations and patient selection factors, and should be coordinated with the partner’s ART timing [548].

4. Defining success: intermediate vs. patient-important outcomes

Semen parameter improvement is a useful intermediate marker, but follow-up should explicitly track patient-important outcomes—pregnancy/live birth, time-to-pregnancy, and treatment burden—and should transparently communicate uncertainty when intermediate improvements do not translate into higher live birth probability (particularly across heterogeneous etiologies) [1234856]. ART outcome counseling may incorporate semen characteristics and paternal factors (e.g., age/body mass index) when relevant to decision-making and expectation setting, without overstating causality [56].

KOREA-SPECIFIC CONSIDERATIONS

Male infertility management in Korea should be grounded in the universal principles of international guidelines—being etiology-directed, couple-centered, and time-sensitive—while adopting implementation strategies that reflect domestic institutional, resource-based, and socio-cultural contexts [123811]. Particularly since the 2017 expansion of national insurance coverage and policy support for infertility treatments, clinical practice has increasingly demanded standardized pathways that integrate “medical optimization” with considerations of accessibility, financial burden, referral logistics, and seamless coordination with ART [125758].

Furthermore, South Korea faces unprecedented demographic challenges, characterized by an ultra-low birth rate and the highest maternal age at first childbirth among OECD countries [5960]. Given these sociodemographic realities, the minimization of “watchful waiting” is crucial. The acceptable duration of isolated male-factor treatment must be carefully weighed against the rapid decline in female reproductive potential. Specifically, in women aged ≥35 years, the acceptable duration of isolated male-factor treatment should be shortened, and early parallel ART planning is recommended. In women aged ≥40 years, male optimization should not delay ART initiation [61].

1. Healthcare reimbursement, insurance policy, and the referral pathway

In the Korean clinical landscape, infertility care tends to transition rapidly toward ART, primarily led by reproductive endocrinology and obstetrics. Therefore, clinicians must provide clear guidance on the balance between “time, cost, and success rates,” even when male factors are potentially reversible [12311]. As the utilization of ART has become nearly universal due to expanded policy support, it is essential to define a structured “male management window” at the initial visit—specifying the scope and duration of male-factor treatment to be pursued in parallel with the ART plan—to avoid unnecessary delays in the overall reproductive timeline [35758].

To support these clinical pathways, the National Health Insurance System (NHIS) facilitates the early management of male infertility by providing broad coverage for comprehensive diagnostic workups and treatment [62]. Since 2025, the “Preconception Health Care Support Project” has further enhanced accessibility by offering periodic subsidies for essential fertility screenings for men of reproductive age [63]. A landmark policy reform in November 2024 transitioned ART coverage from a “per-couple” to a “per-birth” basis—resetting treatment cycle limits for each subsequent pregnancy—while implementing a uniform 30% co-payment regardless of age and ensuring financial protection even for discontinued cycles [64].

Complementing these national efforts, several local government initiatives currently provide specialized supplementary grants for male-specific surgical interventions, including varicocelectomy and TESE. Clinicians should proactively integrate these reimbursement and support frameworks into patient counseling to prevent the premature abandonment of etiology-directed treatments due to financial barriers [65]. By leveraging these public resources, clinicians can establish evidence-based, optimal treatment strategies that maximize the overall reproductive success for the couple [66].

2. Domestic ART landscape and patient safety

According to the 2022 annual report based on national registry data, a substantial volume of IUI and IVF (both fresh and frozen) cycles are performed in Korea, with a rising trend in single embryo transfer (SET) to mitigate multi-fetal pregnancy risks [12]. Given that ART is the central pillar of infertility care in Korea, this guideline emphasizes (1) the optimal timing for ART escalation, (2) the evidence-based selective use of ICSI, and (3) shared decision-making focused on maternal and neonatal safety as indispensable elements of clinical practice [12342].

3. Legal and ethical frameworks (donation and consent)

In Korea, assisted reproduction is strictly regulated by the Bioethics and Safety Act. Consequently, counseling and documentation regarding the donation, storage, and use of gametes and embryos, as well as the principle of non-commercialization, must be standardized [67]. Specifically, regarding the use of non-partner gametes (e.g., donor sperm), it is highly recommended that institutions maintain Standard Operating Protocols (SOPs) addressing anonymity, infection screening, traceability, and informed consent to navigate ongoing socio-legal and ethical debates [68].

4. Laboratory standardization and utilization of local evidence

Adherence to the WHO 6th edition laboratory standards and the principles of repeat testing is equally critical in the Korean context [1314434445]. As the age of couples seeking fertility care rises, domestic epidemiological data have increasingly highlighted the impact of environmental factors—such as fine particulate matter (PM 2.5/10) and endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs)—on semen parameters in Korean men [697071]. These findings should be utilized as concrete evidence for lifestyle modification and environmental counseling during follow-up.

5. Socio-cultural perceptions and complementary medicine

Social stigma surrounding male infertility often leads to treatment avoidance. Initial counseling should therefore be structured to deliver a positive message: that male-factor evaluation enhances the overall success rate, safety, and efficiency of the couple’s fertility journey [1231718192021]. Furthermore, given the significant utilization of traditional Korean medicine and other complementary therapies, clinicians are encouraged to adopt a “Parallel Care Counseling Framework” that transparently discusses the level of evidence, safety profiles, and the potential risk of delaying definitive ART [57].

CONCLUSIONS

Male infertility management should be delivered as etiology-directed, couple-centered, time-sensitive, and health-oriented care, with success defined by patient-important outcomes—particularly pregnancy/live birth and reduced treatment burden—rather than semen parameter change alone [12315]. Across contemporary international guidance, several consistent themes emerge. The recommendations presented in this guideline represent practical approaches to implementing these core principles within the Korean clinical context. First, early integration of male evaluation and management with the female partner’s reproductive timeline is essential to prevent avoidable delays to effective therapy [12311]. Second, modifiable risk factors and reversible contributors should be addressed systematically, but empiric pharmacologic therapy should not be routine; medical treatment is best applied in a phenotype-driven, time-limited manner with predefined reassessment and ART contingency planning [1232225]. Third, disease-specific pathways for varicocele, endocrine disorders, and azoospermia should be individualized, with clear counseling on benefits, uncertainty, and opportunity costs—particularly for NOA where standardized SSR planning and appropriate use of micro-TESE and cryopreservation can optimize efficiency and reduce repeated procedures [245926]. Fourth, the ART interface requires selective, evidence-informed decision-making, including avoiding routine ICSI in non-severe male factor infertility and maintaining a strong focus on safety and prevention of multiple pregnancy through individualized embryo transfer strategies [3233343542].

Finally, male infertility should also be recognized as a potential marker of broader health vulnerability, creating an opportunity for preventive care and longitudinal risk modification [1516]. Future work should strengthen Korea-specific implementation by harmonizing referral pathways, laboratory standardization, outcome reporting, and national data linkage, enabling continuous refinement of recommendations as local evidence and resources evolve.
