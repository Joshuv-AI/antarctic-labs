Highly efficient production of β-caryophyllene in Saccharomyces cerevisiae using a novel synthase from Artemisia argyi
Authors: Xiaochun Xiao, Xiukun Wan, Hui Lu, Weijia Cheng, Jiajia Liu, Hui Jiang, Yao Zhi, Fangfang Chen, Jing Yang, Liying Er, Li Lu, Ge Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstractβ-Caryophyllene, a bicyclic sesquiterpene shows remarkable application potential in agriculture for plant disease prevention, pest control and green pesticide development. However, conventional production methods—relying on plant extraction or chemical synthesis—exhibit inherent limitations that hinder both the achievable yield and the commercial scalability of β-caryophyllene manufacturing. In this study, we mined and identified a highly efficient β-caryophyllene synthase, designated ArarCARS. Through molecular docking and ancestral sequence reconstruction, we screened mutants at key residues and revealed that the F117Y variant significantly enhanced production. Molecular dynamics simulations demonstrated that this mutation improved product formation through enhanced enzyme stability. Based on these results, we engineered a high-titer β-caryophyllene-producing Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain via a series of metabolic engineering strategy. Fed-batch fermentation at low temperatures achieved a final β-caryophyllene titer of 35.4 g/L, representing the highest level documented so far. This work not only provides an effective strategy for β-caryophyllene biosynthesis but also offers engineering insights for the efficient microbial production of other valuable terpenoids.Keywords: β-Caryophyllene, Enzyme directed evolution, Ancestral sequence reconstruction, Metabolic engineering

Introduction

β-Caryophyllene is a kind of sesquiterpene that emerges as a molecule of significant industrial and biomedical interest. β-Caryophyllene exhibits diverse biological activities, including anticancer, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective activities [1]. In agriculture, it serves dual roles as an eco-friendly insect repellent that disrupts pest reproduction and attracts natural predators, and as a precursor for green pesticides [2,3]. Despite its versatility, traditional extraction from plants remains inefficient due to low natural abundance and environmental variability, while chemical synthesis typically involves toxic or hazardous reagents. Recent progress in synthetic biology provides a viable alternative, with engineered microorganisms now serving as efficient cellular platforms for terpenoid biosynthesis[[4], [5], [6]].

Currently, significant progress in the microbial biosynthesis of β-caryophyllene through enzyme and metabolic engineering has been previously achieved in various microbes. (Table 1). The biosynthesis of β-caryophyllene proceeds from farnesyl diphosphate (FPP), catalyzed by β-caryophyllene synthase (CARS); FPP is supplied via either the methylerythritol phosphate (MEP) or mevalonate (MVA) pathway [7,8]. Lu et al. successfully obtained a more active variant (E353D) of CPS from Artemisia annua through directed evolution. Through co-expression of genes associated with the MVA pathway, β-alanine metabolism as well as the ATP-binding cassette transporter gene, they achieved a final β-caryophyllene production of 594.05 mg/L in engineered Saccharomyces cerevisiae (S. cerevisiae) [7]. Zhang et al. systematically modulated sterol synthesis to balance cellular resource allocation between growth and production, yielding 21.4 g/L β-caryophyllene under fed-batch fermentation [9]. Despite these achievements, current production levels remain insufficient for industrial implementation. Further enhancement—through the identification of more efficient synthases and systematic optimization of the biosynthetic pathway—is therefore essential to develop an economically viable bioprocess.

Ancestral sequence reconstruction (ASR) is an important sequence approach used to study protein evolutionary history by introducing some residues of inferred ancestral enzymes into modern enzymes [22], resulting in enzymes with better catalytic activity [23], thermal stability [24] or substrate specificity [25]. Thus, ancestral enzymes represent promising starting points for directed evolution efforts [26]. Integrating deep mutational scanning with ASR holds significant promise for improving the catalytic efficiency of β-caryophyllene synthase, thereby facilitating high-level microbial production.

In this study, we implemented a multi-module metabolic engineering strategy in S. cerevisiae to overproduce β-caryophyllene. Our approach involved screening for highly efficient β-caryophyllene synthases, constructing a β-caryophyllene synthase variants library, and optimizing MVA pathway flux. Employing fed-batch fermentation, we attained a final β-caryophyllene yield of 35.4 g/L, which represents the highest level reported to date for a sustainable S. cerevisiae–based production system.

Materials and methods

Plasmids and strains

The β-caryophyllene synthase genes were synthesized by Genscript following codon optimization for expression in S. cerevisiae (amino acid sequences provided in Table S1). Tables S2 and S3 list all strains and plasmids employed. Primer sequences (Table S4) were supplied by GeneCreate Biological Engineering Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Plasmid assemblies were generated via yeast assembly or Gibson assembly following previously reported methods [27,28]. The S. cerevisiae strain CEN.PK2-1D was engineered for β-caryophyllene production. Yeast transformations were conducted using the LiAc/SS carrier DNA/PEG method [29].

Media and culture conditions

Escherichia coli (E. coli) cultures were grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin at 37 °C. S. cerevisiae strains were cultured at 30 °C either in Yeast Extract Peptone Dextrose (YPD) or in Synthetic Complete (SC) medium. SC medium contained 0.67% yeast nitrogen base with ammonium sulfate, 2% glucose, and an appropriate amino acid dropout mixture to maintain auxotrophic selection.

Shake-flask fermentation

Seed cultures were initiated by transferring cryopreserved glycerol stocks to 5 mL of either SC or YPD medium, followed by overnight incubation at 30 °C with orbital shaking at 220 rpm. These starter cultures were subsequently diluted into 50 mL of fresh medium contained in 250 mL flasks, adjusting the initial optical density at 600 nm (OD600) to 0.1. To facilitate in situ product recovery, isopropyl myristate (IPM) was introduced at a concentration of 10% (v/v) as an organic extractive phase. The cultures were then maintained under continuous agitation at 220 rpm for a period of 72 h at 30 °C.

Bioinformatic analysis of the β-caryophyllene synthase

This study utilized publicly available genomic data for Ocimum sanctum (PRJNA267195) [30], Artemisia argyi (PRJNA804646) [31] and Liriodendron chinense (CNA0002404) [32] obtained from the National Genome Data Center. Putative terpene synthase (TPS) genes were identified by screening predicted proteomes against the Pfam database (version 28.0) for the presence of both the terpene_synth_N (PF01397) and terpene_synth_C (PF03936) domains. Gene family analysis was performed using the “Simple HMM Search” tool in TBtools [33].

Site-directed mutagenesis of β-caryophyllene synthase

The ArarCARS protein structure was predicted with AlphaFold2 [34]. Docking of 3 Mg2+-FPP molecules to ArarCARS was performed using Ledock [35] with the grid center defined based on the NtEAS structure (PDB: 3M01) [36]. Structural images were rendered with PyMOL.

Experimentally validated β-caryophyllene synthase sequences from the literature were used to identify homologous genes from 26 plant species via reciprocal best-hit analysis [37]. Coding sequences were aligned with MUSCLE [38] and a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree (1000 bootstrap replicates) was generated with IQ-TREE [39], with Taiwania cryptomerioides serving as the outgroup. Ancestral sequences were inferred with PAML following established methods[[40], [41], [42]]. Site-specific mutations were introduced by PCR-based mutagenesis according to a standard protocol [43], using primers designed to incorporate the desired nucleotide changes.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were carried out with AMBER22. The Amber ff99SB force field [44] and TIP3P water model [45] were employed for the protein and solvent, respectively. Ligand parameters were assigned with the general AMBER force field (GAFF) [46] with substrate partial charges assigned via the restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) method [47]. RESP charge calculations were performed with Gaussian16_C.01, with input files generated via REDServer-Development [48]. The tleap module in AMBER22 was used to build initial coordinate and topologies. Mutant systems were prepared following the same procedure.

Prior to production runs, systems were subjected to a standard equilibration protocol consisting of several stages: minimization of hydrogen atoms, minimization of solvent molecules, and energy minimization under harmonic positional restraints (5 kcal mol−1 Å−2 on Mg2+ ions and Cα atoms). The systems were then heated to 300 K over 60 ps (NVT ensemble) and pressure-equilibrated for 100 ps at 1 bar (NPT ensemble). Afterwards, the restraints were released stepwise through a set of 50 ps NPT simulations until fully removed. After equilibration, a 100 ns production simulation was conducted at 300 K. Throughout MD, bonds involving hydrogen were constrained with the SHAKE algorithm [49]. Structural and energetic properties—including Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD), radius of gyration, interaction energy, and backbone/ligand Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF)—were extracted from the trajectories for subsequent analyses. Molecular graphics were prepared with PyMOL (version 2.5.2).

Metabolic engineering of yeast strains

Plasmids carrying functional expression modules (promoter, terminator, gene, selective marker, and homology arm) and the backbone were subjected to restriction enzyme digestion. After gel purification to remove the backbone, the linearized expression cassettes were integrated into the yeast genome. The integrated fragments included: from plasmid p900A26, the ArarCARS gene, ERG20 gene, a LEU locus homology arm, and a LEU marker; from p908A26, the ArarCARS gene with a YPRCdelta15 homology arm and TRP marker; and from p905A26, the ArarCARS gene, tHMG1 gene, a URA3 homology arm, and HIS marker. ERG9 expression was attenuated using a CRISPR-Cas9 strategy [50,51] that deleted a 45-bp segment (positions −220 to −175) within the promoter's upstream activating sequence (UAS). For GAL80 deletion, a DNA fragment containing the GAL80 homology arm fused to the URA3 marker was generated by overlap-extension PCR and used as a template for recombination.

Fed-batch fermentation for β-caryophyllene overproduction

A cryopreserved stock was revived in YPD medium for 24 h, then used to inoculate 800 mL of Low Glucose Minimal Medium (LGM) medium at a 2% inoculum density. After approximately 16 h, the entire culture was transferred to a 1 L bioreactor. The fermentation medium contained 8 g/L KH2PO4∙7H2O, 6.2 g/L MgSO4∙7H2O, 15 g/L (NH4)2SO4 and 40 g/L glucose. Temperature was controlled at either 25 °C or 30 °C with pH maintained at 5.0. Initial conditions were established at 300 rpm agitation, 1 V/V·min aeration, and 0.02 MPa pressure. Dissolved oxygen (DO) was calibrated to 100% under these baseline conditions, with pH controlled at 5 via ammonia supplementation. Upon DO depletion below 30%, agitation and aeration were increased to 550 rpm and 2.2 V/V·min, respectively. No further adjustments were implemented following complete DO exhaustion (0%). The glucose feed rate was adjusted to maintain ethanol between 2 and 7 g/L. IPM was added at 24 h to initiate in situ product extraction.

Extraction and quantification of β-caryophyllene

Following phase separation, the IPM layer was collected and clarified via centrifugation (6000 rpm, 10 min). The organic phase was diluted with hexane prior to analysis. β-Caryophyllene was quantified by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) using a TR-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). The oven temperature program consisted of holding at 80 °C for 1 min, ramping to 220 °C at 10 °C/min, and maintaining for 15 min. The injector and transfer line were set at 230 °C and 240 °C, respectively. Identification was based on comparison of electron-ionization (EI) mass spectra with an authentic standard.

NMR detection

After column chromatography, the pure β-caryophyllene with purity ≥90% was isolated, and 10 mg of the sample was dissolved and mixed well with 400 μL of CDCl3 and then subjected to 600 MHz nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) hydrogen spectroscopy and deconvoluted to determine the molecular structure of β-caryophyllene. 1H NMR (600 MHz, CDCl3) δ 5.31 (dd, J = 10.6, 4.8 Hz), 4.94 (d, J = 1.7 Hz), 4.82 (d, J = 1.7 Hz), 2.38 – 2.33 (m), 2.33 – 2.29 (m), 2.21 (dq, J = 9.8, 4.5, 3.9 Hz), 2.09 (dt, J = 11.9, 3.6 Hz), 2.00 (qt, J = 9.0, 4.0 Hz), 1.92 (td, J = 12.3, 5.0 Hz), 1.69 (t, J = 6.5 Hz), 1.65 (dd, J = 12.3, 9.6 Hz), 1.61, 1.57 (d, J = 9.5 Hz), 1.50 (dt, J = 4.8, 2.0 Hz), 1.49 – 1.42 (m), 1.00, 0.98.

Results

Functional characterization of β-caryophyllene synthase

We previously characterized a variety of plant terpene synthases from Artemisia argyi, Ocimum tenuiflorum, and Liriodendron chinense (Fig. 1A) [[4], [5], [6]]. By co-expressing a yeast farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase (EGR20) and these terpene synthases in a terpenoid-producing yeast strain JTP04 [4], three unreported β-caryophyllene synthase genes, ArarCARS, OcsaCARS, and LichCARS were successfully identified (Fig. 1B,1C, Table S1, Fig. S1–2). The ArarCARS, OcsaCARS, and LichCARS were then integrated into the chromosome of yeast chassis JTP04 and single-copy gene-integrated strains JAM1-1, JAM2-1, and JAM3-1 were obtained accordingly (Table S2). The β-caryophyllene yields of these strains were 335.6 mg/L, 306.3 mg/L, and 33.4 mg/L in shake flask fermentation, respectively (Fig. 1D). Therefore, ArarCARS was chosen for further study as it exhibits excellent activity in the yeast chassis.

Structure-guided mutagenesis and ancestral reconstruction for enzyme optimization

To improve the catalytic activity and industrial robustness of ArarCARS, we employed directed molecular evolution by constructing a mutant library. Since amino acids surrounding the substrate-binding pocket critically influence enzyme activity through substrate interactions, we performed alanine scanning mutagenesis of these residues. We identified 23 amino acid residues located within a 5 Å radius of the substrate-binding pocket (Fig. 2A, Table S5). Two residues were already alanine, so we created 21 alanine-substituted mutants: R264A, C270A, W273A, V293A, I294A, S295A, T298A, D301A, F372A, Y376A, S401A, S402A, G403A, Y404A, M406A, L407A, R442A, D445A, M517A, Y521A, and F527A.

Reconstructed ancestral enzymes often exhibit enhanced solubility, thermal stability, and versatility compared to natural enzymes, offering significant advantages for industrial applications [52]. Therefore, we conducted ancestral sequence reconstruction (ASR) based on verified plant β-caryophyllene synthase genes (Table S6). This approach reconstructed three ancestral β-caryophyllene synthases (N1, N2, and N3) in the Artemisia genus and identified 13 key mutagenesis sites in ArarCARS: F117Y, Y134F, T140N, G160D, V161I, D174E, V176I, N189T, G232D, M418I, A503P, G536D, and S547P (Fig. 2B, Fig. S3).

We constructed a comprehensive mutant library containing all 34 variants (21 alanine mutants plus 13 ancestral mutations) and screened them using 24-well plate fermentation for β-caryophyllene production. Fifteen mutants showed significant loss of function, while five exhibited a trend toward improved production: ArarCARSC270A, ArarCARSS295A, ArarCARSF117Y, ArarCARSD174E, and ArarCARSN189T (Fig. 2C). These promising mutants were further validated in shaking flask fermentations, with ArarCARSF117Y achieving the highest yield of 409.4 mg/L—a 22% increase in yield compared to the wild-type control (Fig. 2D). This demonstrates the successful integration of structure-guided mutagenesis and ancestral reconstruction for enzyme optimization.

Molecular dynamics simulations reveal enhanced stability of the F117Y mutant

To investigate the structural mechanism underlying enhanced β-caryophyllene production in the F117Y mutant, we conducted molecular docking analyses of FPP-bound ArarCARS in both wild-type and mutant forms (Fig. 3A). Although residue F117 is located on the enzyme surface (32.3 Å from the active site) and does not directly participate in catalysis, its substitution with tyrosine introduced a hydrogen bond between the phenolic hydroxyl group of Tyr117 and the amide group of Gln90. This interaction likely enhances protein folding and structural stability, providing a plausible explanation for the mutant's improved performance.

Statistical significance was determined using Student's t-test (∗∗∗, P < 0.001).

To further evaluate the dynamic stability of the wild-type and mutant enzymes, we conducted 100 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations at 300 K for both the ArarCARS-FPP and ArarCARSF117Y-FPP complexes. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) analysis showed that the ArarCARS-FPP complex initially fluctuated around 0.5 Å before stabilizing at 1.0 Å after 40 ns, whereas the F117Y mutant remained stable at 0.5 Å throughout the simulation (Fig. 3B). This suggests that the mutant adopts a more rigid and stable conformation upon substrate binding.

To assess local flexibility, we analyzed the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), which revealed that both complexes exhibited fluctuations between 0 and 0.4 nm (Fig. 3C). However, the region surrounding residue 117 displayed reduced flexibility in the F117Y mutant, indicating enhanced local rigidity. Additionally, radius of gyration (Rg) calculations demonstrated that after an initial equilibration phase, the F117Y mutant adopted a more compact structure, with an average Rg of 24.8087 Å compared to 24.8949 Å for the wild-type enzyme (Fig. 3D).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the F117Y mutation enhances the structural stability of ArarCARS, likely through the formation of a stabilizing hydrogen bond with Gln90 and a subsequent reduction in conformational flexibility. This increased rigidity may contribute to the observed improvement in β-caryophyllene biosynthesis yield, providing valuable insights for future enzyme engineering efforts.

Metabolic engineering strategy for enhanced β-caryophyllene production

A systematic, multi-step metabolic engineering strategy was applied to the S.cerevisiae JTP04 chassis to develop strains with enhanced β-caryophyllene biosynthesis. The initial strain (JAM01) was created by integrating Module I, containing ArarCARSF117Y and ERG20 under GAL promoter. This base strain produced 409.4 mg/L β-caryophyllene. To amplify production capacity, we subsequently introduced two additional genetic modules: Module II (a second copy of ArarCARSF117Y) and Module III (a third copy of ArarCARSF117Y with the rate-limiting enzyme gene tHMG1[[53], [54], [55], [56]]) (Fig. 4A, 4B, Table S2).

A critical metabolic bottleneck was addressed through precursor flux optimization. Since ERG9 (squalene synthase) competitively consumes FPP for ergosterol biosynthesis, we knocked down ERG9 to increase FPP availability for β-caryophyllene synthesis while maintaining sufficient ergosterol production for cell viability. The resulting strain (JAM101) demonstrated a 3.6-fold improvement in production (1204.2 mg/L) compared to the initial JAM1-1 (Fig. 4A, 4B, Table S2).

To enhance industrial applicability, we further engineered the galactose induction system. By knocking out Gal80 in strain JAM102, we achieved promoter activation without galactose supplementation, thereby reducing fermentation costs. This final optimized strain reached a production titer of 1458.7 mg/L β-caryophyllene, corresponding to a 334.7% improvement relative to the initial strain and demonstrating the success of our combinatorial engineering strategy (Fig. 4A, 4B, Table S2).

High-titer β-caryophyllene production via optimized fed-batch fermentation

Fed-batch fermentation in a 1 L bioreactor was conducted to assess the industrial potential of strain JAM102. Initially, fermentation was performed at 30 °C, resulting in a β-caryophyllene titer of 26.8 g/L after 120 h (Fig. 5A). Based on reports that lower temperatures favor β-caryophyllene accumulation [57], we evaluated β-caryophyllene production across different temperatures in shake flasks and identified 25 °C as optimal (Fig. S4). Consequently, JAM102 was subjected to fed-batch fermentation at 25 °C under otherwise identical conditions. While cell growth remained unaffected, the production profile showed significantly enhanced late-stage accumulation, ultimately reaching a record titer of 35.4 g/L at 120 h—the highest reported yield to date (Fig. 5B, 5C, 5D, Table 1). Notably, although initial production was slightly slower at 25 °C compared to 30 °C, the substantial yield improvement in later stages demonstrates the effectiveness of temperature optimization for maximizing β-caryophyllene production in industrial fermentation.

Discussion

In this study, strategies involving screening for highly efficient β-caryophyllene synthases, constructing a β-caryophyllene synthase variant library, and enhancing flux through the MVA pathway were adopted to systematically optimize β-caryophyllene biosynthesis, and the β-caryophyllene titer achieved at 1458.7 mg/L in shake-flask cultures. Further scale-up in a 1 L bioreactor yielded a titer of 35.4 g/L, the highest level reported to date.

The F117Y mutation enhances product yield; however, the underlying mechanism—whether it improves protein expression, solubility, or catalytic activity—remains to be fully elucidated. Although molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations suggest that this mutation may enhance protein stability, definitive clarification of the catalytic mechanism requires systematic experimental approaches, particularly crystal structure determination. Additionally, the current mutant exhibits only a 22% increase in activity. Future efforts will focus on further improving catalytic efficiency through cumulative mutagenesis and structure-guided rational design.

Looking forward, given the demonstrated capacity of the Yarrowia lipolytica chassis to substantially enhance metabolic engineering efficiency, the implementation of ArarCARF117Y in this system warrants investigation, with the potential for significant yield improvement. Furthermore, despite the currently achieved high titers, the carbon conversion efficiency under glucose-fed conditions remains suboptimal. Future strategies will involve reprogramming carbon metabolic flux to eliminate Crabtree effect-associated ethanol formation, thereby enabling high-level biosynthesis. Consideration will also be given to downstream recovery efficiency to facilitate industrial-scale production.

In conclusion, our study establishes a platform for efficient β-caryophyllene synthesis and offers a reference for other sesquiterpene production.
