Cycle structure of random standardized permutations
Aurélien Guerder

Abstract
. In this article, we study a model of random permutations, which we call random standardized permutations, based on a sequence of i.i.d. random variables. This model generalizes others, such as the riffle-shuffle and the major-index-biased permutations. We first establish an exact result on the joint distribution of the number of cycles of given lengths, involving the notion of primitive words. From this result, we obtain various convergence results, most of which are proved using the method of moments. First we prove that the number of small cycles may have either a Poisson limit distribution, or a limit distribution given by a countable sum of independent geometric distributions. Then we establish a limit distribution for large cycles, which is the Poisson-Dirichlet process. Finally we prove a central limit theorem for the total number of cycles.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background and model

The study of random permutations is a central subject in probability theory and combinatorics. Many aspects spark interest in the field, such as inversions, descents, cycles, records, or longest increasing subsequences. The first natural model is the uniform one, for which many results are already established (see e.g.
[
ABT03
]
for cycle structure). More recent articles have shifted their attention toward non-uniform models, such as riffle-shuffle (see
[
BD92
]
), Mallows permutations (see
[
Dub24
]
) or record biased permutations (see
[
BNP24
]
). Despite this, the uniform model remains a useful reference for comparison, and it is interesting to observe that some results are identical in some non-uniform models.

Given a sequence
g=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the standardized permutation of
g
, denoted by
\mathrm{std}(g)
, is the permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
defined as follows: if the smallest value of the
g_{j},j\leq n
is
g_{j_{1}}=\dots=g_{j_{l}}=x_{0}
with
j_{1}<\dots<j_{l}
, we have
\sigma(j_{1})=1,\dots,\sigma(j_{l})=l
, then we continue counting with the second smallest value, and so on. For example, if
g=(6,1,5,3,3,1,2)
, we get
\mathrm{std}(g)=7164523
. To get a
random permutation
, we choose
G
randomly. Here we focus on the case where that the
G_{j}
are i.i.d. random variables. Since the atomless case produces a uniform random permutation (see Section
2.2.1
), we assume in all this article that the
G_{j}
take values in a countable set
\mathbb{I}
, and, for
i\in\mathbb{I}
, we write
p_{i}=\mathbb{P}(G_{j}=i)
. This model generalizes some others, such as the riffle-shuffle if the
G_{j}
are uniform in a finite set (see
[
BD92
]
or
[
Sta01
]
), or major-index-biased permutations if the
G_{j}
follow a geometric distribution (see
[
Sta01
]
or
[
Coo25
]
).

1.2.
Overview of the results

1.2.1.
Exact distribution of cycles

We first get an exact and explicit result about the distribution of cycles, by generalizing some arguments of
[
Coo25
]
. Since
\mathrm{std}
is not injective, we need to study the sequence
G
which generated the permutation
\mathrm{std}(G)
, in order to avoid loss of information. We break down the problem according to the values of the
G_{j}
variables. For a given
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{I}^{k}
, we study the number
D_{\mathbf{i}}
of
k
-cycles
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
such that
G_{x_{1}}=i_{1},\dots,G_{x_{k}}=i_{k}
. Recall that a word is primitive if it is not a power of a smaller word. It appears that the appropriate k-tuples to consider, to avoid counting a given cycle more than once, are actually the conjugacy classes of primitive words of length
k
(see Section
3.2.1
), constituting the set
\widetilde{Q}_{k}
. We then have that, for any
k\geq 1
, the number
c_{k}
of cycles of length
k
of
\mathrm{std}(G)
is given by

c_{k}=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k}}D_{\mathbf{i}}.

Define
p_{\mathbf{i}}=p_{i_{1}}\dots p_{i_{k}}
for a given
\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{I}^{k}
, and
Q_{k}
the set of all primitive words of
\mathbb{I}^{k}
. Then, our first main result is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1,\mathbf{i}_{1}\in Q_{k_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{r}\in Q_{k_{r}}
pairwise non conjugate, and
l_{1},\dots,l_{r}\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(D_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}\geq l_{1},\dots,D_{\mathbf{i}_{r}}\geq l_{r}\right)=\begin{cases}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}^{l_{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{r}}^{l_{r}}&\text{if }k_{1}l_{1}+\dots+k_{r}l_{r}\leq n;\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

This theorem characterizes the joint distribution of the
D_{\mathbf{i}}
. This result generalizes previous findings in
[
DMP95
]
concerning riffle-shuffle cycles, as well as work in
[
Coo25
]
concerning fixed points and 2-cycles of major-index biased permutations. To prove this result (Section
3
), we use a bijection between
\mathbb{I}^{n}
and
\left\{g^{\prime}\in\mathbb{I}^{n+k}\mid D_{\mathbf{i}}(g^{\prime})\geq 1\right\}
, which adds a cycle of type
\mathbf{i}\in Q_{k}
to
\mathrm{std}(g)
without changing the rest of the permutation.

1.2.2.
Asymptotic behavior of short cycles

We define the ”short cycles” as those of length smaller than or equal to a fixed
k
, as
n
tends to infinity. We note
\mathrm{Geo}_{0}(p)
the geometric distribution starting from 0, i.e. if
X
follows
\mathrm{Geo}_{0}(p)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then
\mathbb{P}(X=k)=(1-p)^{k}p
. If we let
n
tend to infinity without letting the distribution of the
G_{j}
depend on
n
, the previous theorem immediately gives us the convergence of the finite-dimensional distributions of the sequence
(D_{\mathbf{i}})_{\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{l},l\leq k}
to those of

\underset{i\in\widetilde{Q}_{l},l\leq k}{\bigotimes}\mathrm{Geo}_{0}(1-p_{\mathbf{i}}).

Since
c_{k}
is the countable sum of all the
D_{\mathbf{i}},\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k}
, to get the convergence in distribution of
c_{k}
, we prove a convergence result in the set
\ell^{1}
:

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix
k\geq 1
. Then,

(D_{\mathbf{i}})_{\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{l},l\leq k}\overset{(d)}{\longrightarrow}\underset{i\in\widetilde{Q}_{l},l\leq k}{\bigotimes}\mathrm{Geo}_{0}(1-p_{\mathbf{i}})

in
\displaystyle\ell^{1}\left(\bigcup_{l\leq k}\widetilde{Q}_{l}\right)
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
.

Corollary 1.3
.

Denote by
c_{j}
the number of cycles of
\mathrm{std}(G)
of length
j
. We have

\displaystyle\left(c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\right)\overset{(d)}{\longrightarrow}\underset{l\leq k}{\bigotimes}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{l}}X_{\mathbf{i}}\right)

where
\left(X_{\mathbf{i}}\right)_{\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{l},l\leq k}
follows the distribution
\underset{\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{l},l\leq k}{\bigotimes}\mathrm{Geo}_{0}(1-p_{\mathbf{i}})
.

Under a different hypothesis on the distribution
\mathbf{p}
, which consists on assuming that it is spreading asymptotically, we get the following result, which has already been obtained in
[
DMP95
, Proposition 5.4]
in the riffle-shuffle model:

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume that the distribution of the
G_{k}^{(n)}
satisfies

\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{i\in\mathbb{I}}p_{i}^{(n)}\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

Then, for
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

\left(c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\right)\overset{(d)}{\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{P}\left(1\right)\otimes\dots\otimes\mathcal{P}\left(\frac{1}{k}\right).

1.2.3.
Asymptotic behavior of large cycles

We define the infinite-dimensional simplex

\displaystyle\Delta^{\infty}=\left\{x\in[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}\;\big|\;x_{1}\geq x_{2}\geq\dots\text{ and }\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}x_{j}\leq 1\right\},

endowed with the topology of pointwise convergence. The study of the infinite-dimensional vector
n^{-1}\lambda^{(n)}=n^{-1}(\lambda_{1}^{(n)},\lambda_{2}^{(n)},\dots)
of
\Delta^{\infty}
, where
\lambda_{j}^{(n)}
is the size of the
j
-th greatest cycle of
\mathrm{std}(G)
, can be seen as an asymptotic definition of large cycles, since the short cycles, say the fixed points, appear at the end of the vector, and hence have no influence on the pointwise convergence of
n^{-1}\lambda^{(n)}
. Then, under a mild assumption on the distribution
\mathbf{p}
, the vector converges to the Poisson-Dirichlet process with parameter 1:

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
R\in(0;1)
, and suppose that for any
n\geq 1
and
i\in\mathbb{I}
, we have
p_{i}^{(n)}\leq R
. Then, the vector
\dfrac{\lambda^{(n)}}{n}=n^{-1}(\lambda_{1}^{(n)},\lambda_{2}^{(n)},\dots)
, where
\lambda_{j}^{(n)}
is the size of the
j
-th greatest cycle of
\mathrm{std}(G)
, converges in distribution in
\Delta^{\infty}
to the Poisson-Dirichlet process with parameter 1.

To prove this theorem, we use the result for the uniform model, and we transfer the limit distribution thanks to a criterion of convergence in distribution in the set
\Delta^{\infty}
. More precisely, the convergence in distribution in
\Delta^{\infty}
is characterized by the expectations of joint moments of functions of Poisson-Dirichlet process (see Theorem
5.3
). This value is hard to compute directly, but it is obtained by using the convergence in the uniform model. Note that this result has also already been proved in
[
DMP95
, Proposition 5.5]
in the riffle-shuffle model.

1.2.4.
Asymptotic behavior of cycle count

The last statistic that we study in this article is the total number of cycles, which is asymptotically normal (see Section
6
).

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
R\in(0;1)
, and suppose that for any
n\geq 1
and
i\in\mathbb{I}
, we have
p_{i}^{(n)}\leq R
. Then, as
n\rightarrow+\infty
,

\frac{K_{n}-\log(n)}{\sqrt{\log(n)}}\overset{(d)}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,1)

and the moments converge.

This theorem seems to be new even in the riffle-shuffle case. The proof of the theorem consists on studying the convergence of cumulants. It appears that studying the number of cycles of length smaller than
n/\log(\log(n))
is more convenient and is equivalent – this is reminiscent of the proof Erdös-Kac theorem (see
[
EK39
]
, or
[
Bil86
, Theorem 30.3]
for a more recent reference), where it is more convenient to study the number of prime divisors smaller than
n^{1/\log(\log(\log(n)))}
of a random integer, rather than the total number of divisors. Combinatorics of cumulants is also required, through the Leonov-Shiryaev formula (see Theorem
6.5
).

2.
Random standardized permutations model

2.1.
Definition

Throughout this paper, we use one-line notation for permutations, i.e. for any
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, we write
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\dots\sigma_{n}
. If
\sigma
is the
k
-cycle mapping
x_{1}
to
x_{2}
,
x_{2}
to
x_{3}
, …,
x_{k}
to
x_{1}
, we write
\sigma=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k})
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
g=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We define the permutation
\sigma:=\mathrm{std}(g)\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
by

\sigma(j)<\sigma(k)\iff g_{j}<g_{k}\text{ or }\left\{g_{j}=g_{k}\text{ and }j<k\right\}

called standardized permutation of
g
.

In other words, if the smallest value of
\left\{g_{k},k\leq n\right\}
is
g_{k_{1}}=\dots=g_{k_{l}}=x_{0}
with
k_{1}<\dots<k_{l}
, we have
\sigma(k_{1})=1,\dots,\sigma(k_{l})=l
, then we continue labeling with the second smallest value, and so on. For example, if
g=(6,1,5,3,3,1,2)
, we get
\mathrm{std}(g)=7164523
(see Figure
1
). In this paper, we consider the case where
g
is random. More precisely, we consider
G=(G_{1},\dots,G_{n})
, where the
G_{k}
are
n
i.i.d. random variables with distribution
\mu
. If the distribution of the
G_{j}
is atomless, the permutation
\mathrm{std}(G)
is uniform (see Section
2.2.1
). Throughout this text, we assume that
\mu
is a discrete distribution on
\mathbb{R}
. Specifically, we fix a countable subset
\mathbb{I}\subset\mathbb{R}
and we assume that
\mu
has support in
\mathbb{I}
. The distribution
\mu
may depend on
n
or not, when we study asymptotic behaviors. We note
p_{i}=\mu(\{i\})=\mathbb{P}(G_{k}=i)
for any
i\in\mathbb{I}
(possibly
p_{i}^{(n)}
if the distribution depends on
n
), and
\mathbf{p}=(p_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{I}}
(possibly
\mathbf{p}^{(n)}
).

2.2.
Some particular cases

The aim of this short part is to link our model to other well-known ones, that it generalizes.

2.2.1.
Atomless variables and uniform model

In this part only, we assume that the measure
\mu
has no atoms, i.e. for any
x\in\mathbb{R},\mu(\{x\})=0
. In this case, it holds that:

Proposition 2.2
.

If
\mu
has no atoms, then
\mathrm{std}(g)
is a uniform random permutation on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.

Proof.

Since
\mu
is atomless, the
G_{k}
are pairwise distinct almost surely: we consider the event
A=\{G_{k}\text{ pairwise distinct}\}
. Note that for any
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
,

A\cap(\mathrm{std}(G)=\sigma)\iff\left(G_{\sigma^{-1}(j)}\right)_{1\leq j\leq n}\text{ is increasing.}

Since the
G_{k}
are i.i.d. random variables, for any
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
,
(G_{1},\dots,G_{n})
and
\left(G_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},\dots,G_{\sigma^{-1}(n)}\right)
have the same distribution. Therefore, since
\mathbb{P}(A)=1
, the quantity

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{std}(G)=\sigma\right)=\mathbb{P}\left((\mathrm{std}(G)=\sigma)\cap A\right)\\
=\mathbb{P}\left(\left(G_{\sigma^{-1}(j)}\right)_{1\leq j\leq n}\text{ is increasing}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\left(G_{j}\right)_{1\leq j\leq n}\text{ is increasing}\right)

does not depend on
\sigma
, from which the result follows.
∎

2.2.2.
Major-index-biased permutations

In
[
Str08
]
,
[
FM12
]
, or
[
Coo25
]
, the authors deal with major-index-biased permutations. For a given
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, the major index
\mathrm{maj}(\sigma)
of
\sigma
is the sum of its descents, i.e.

\mathrm{maj}(\sigma)=\sum_{\sigma(i)>\sigma(i+1)}i.

Fix
q>0
, then a random permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is a major-index-biased permutation if it is selected with probability proportional to
q^{\mathrm{maj}(\sigma)}
(see Figure
2
for an example).

Coopman gives a random permutation generating algorithm, which appears at first sight to be different from standardized permutations, but which turns out to be equivalent.

Definition 2.3
.

[
Coo25
]
For
g=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\preceq
be an order on the pairs
(k,g_{k})
defined by

(k,g_{k})\preceq(l,g_{l})\iff g_{k}>g_{l}\text{ or }\left\{g_{k}=g_{l}\text{ et }k<l\right\}.

By ordering the pairs in increasing order and ignoring the first element of each pair, we get a permutation which we denote by
\Gamma(g)
.

A relationship exists between
\mathrm{std}
and
\Gamma
, given by

\Gamma(-g)=\mathrm{std}(g)^{-1}

for any
g=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We then get this particular case of random standardized permutation, which follows from
[
Coo25
, Theorem 1.1]
, or from
[
Sta01
, Theorem 2.1]
:

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
q\in(0,1)
, and
G=(G_{1},\dots,G_{n})
be a sequence of i.i.d. geometric random variables with parameter
1-q
. Then,
\mathrm{std}(-G)^{-1}
is a major-index-biased permutation with parameter
q
.

2.3.
Runs of a standardized permutation

Let
g\in\mathbb{I}^{n}
. For
i\in\mathbb{I}
, we define the sets of indices
\mathcal{G}_{i}=\left\{k\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket\mid g_{k}=i\right\}
, and
\mathcal{G}_{<i}=\left\{k\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket\mid g_{k}<i\right\}
. We also define the ”runs”
L_{i}:\llbracket 0,n\rrbracket\to\llbracket 0,n\rrbracket
of
\mathrm{std}(g)
by

1.
L_{i}(0)=\left|\mathcal{G}_{<i}\right|

2. for
k=1,\dots,n,

L_{i}(k)=\begin{cases}L_{i}(k-1)&\text{ if }g_{k}\neq i\\
L_{i}(k-1)+1&\text{ if }g_{k}=i.\end{cases}

If we consider the runs of
\mathrm{std}(g)
and
\mathrm{std}(g^{\prime})
for two given sequences
g
and
g^{\prime}
, we will write
L_{i}^{g}
and
L_{i}^{g^{\prime}}
. Note that the
L_{i}^{g}
function has at least one fixed point, as
L_{i}^{g}(0)\geq 0,L_{i}^{g}(n)\leq 0
and
L_{i}^{g}(x)
increases by 0 or 1 when
x
increases by 1.

The
L_{i}
are interpolations of the points of
\sigma
associated to the
g_{k}=i
, generalizing those introduced in
[
Coo25
]
. Observe that, if
\sigma=\mathrm{std}(G)
, and
g_{k}=i
, then we have
L_{i}(k)=\sigma(k)
and
L_{i}(k-1)=\sigma(k)-1
, and conversely, if
L_{i}(k)=L_{i}(k-1)+1
, then
\sigma(k)=L_{i}(k)
. Thus, the points of
\sigma
correspond to the up-steps of the
L_{i}
(see Figure

). Note that the word ”runs” usually refers to the successive rises of a permutation. Here, they can be viewed as (parts of) the usual runs of the inverse permutation.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any
i\in\mathbb{I},D_{i}
follows the distribution
\min(\mathrm{Geo}_{0}(1-p_{i}),n)
.

Proof.

Fix
i\in\mathbb{I}
and
0\leq l\leq n
, and write
A=\{g\in\mathbb{I}^{n}\mid D_{i}(g)\geq l\}
. For any
a\in\llbracket 0,n-l\rrbracket
, we write
B_{a}=\left\{g\in\mathbb{I}^{n}\mid\min\{x\mid L_{i}^{g}(x)=x\}=a\right\}
and
B^{\prime}_{a}=\left\{g\in\mathbb{I}^{n-l}\mid\min\{x\mid L_{i}^{g}(x)=x\}=a\right\}
. Note that

A=\underset{a\in\llbracket 0,n-l\rrbracket}{\bigsqcup}A\cap B_{a}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{I}^{n-l}=\underset{a\in\llbracket 0,n-l\rrbracket}{\bigsqcup}B^{\prime}_{a}.

For
a\in\llbracket 0,n-l\rrbracket
, define the following map between
A\cap B_{a}
and
B^{\prime}_{a}
:

\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}\phi_{a}&:&A\cap B_{a}&\to&B^{\prime}_{a}&\\
&&g&\mapsto&g^{\prime}&\text{where }g^{\prime}_{s}=\begin{cases}g_{s}&s\leq a;\\
g_{s+l}&s>a.\end{cases}\end{array}

In other words, we delete the first
l
values of
g
associated to a fixed point of
L_{i}^{g}
, which all equal
i
. The run
L_{i}^{g^{\prime}}
has the same first fixed point as
L_{i}^{g}
, hence
\phi_{a}(g)
is in
B^{\prime}_{a}
. We claim that
\phi_{a}
is a bijection: if
g^{\prime}=\phi_{a}(g)
, we get back
g
by inserting
l
values
i
between
g^{\prime}_{a}
and
g^{\prime}_{a+1}
. Hence, the inverse of
\phi_{a}
is

\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}\phi_{a}^{-1}&:&B^{\prime}_{a}&\to&A\cap B_{a}&\\
&&g^{\prime}&\mapsto&g&\text{where }g_{s}=\begin{cases}g^{\prime}_{s}&s\leq a;\\
i&a<s\leq a+l;\\
g^{\prime}_{s-l}&s>a+l.\end{cases}\end{array}

The above map takes values in
A\cap B_{a}
, since the first fixed point of
L_{i}^{g}
is the same as
L_{i}^{g^{\prime}}
, and since we inserted
l
values
i

. Then, for a given

, since the

are i.i.d.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(G\in A\cap B_{a})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\mathchoice{{G\,\smash{\vrule height=5.46666pt,depth=2.65723pt}}_{\,\llbracket a,a+l-1\rrbracket^{c}}}{{G\,\smash{\vrule height=5.46666pt,depth=2.65723pt}}_{\,\llbracket a,a+l-1\rrbracket^{c}}}{{G\,\smash{\vrule height=3.82668pt,depth=1.85818pt}}_{\,\llbracket a,a+l-1\rrbracket^{c}}}{{G\,\smash{\vrule height=2.73334pt,depth=2.44374pt}}_{\,\llbracket a,a+l-1\rrbracket^{c}}}\in B^{\prime}_{a},G_{s}=i\penalty\ \forall s\in\llbracket a+1,a+l\rrbracket\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\mathchoice{{G\,\smash{\vrule height=5.46666pt,depth=2.46501pt}}_{\,\llbracket 1,n-l\rrbracket}}{{G\,\smash{\vrule height=5.46666pt,depth=2.46501pt}}_{\,\llbracket 1,n-l\rrbracket}}{{G\,\smash{\vrule height=3.82668pt,depth=1.77556pt}}_{\,\llbracket 1,n-l\rrbracket}}{{G\,\smash{\vrule height=2.73334pt,depth=2.36111pt}}_{\,\llbracket 1,n-l\rrbracket}}\in B^{\prime}_{a}\right)p_{i}^{l}.

We finally sum the above expression over all the

to get

\mathbb{P}\left(D_{i}(G)\geq l\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(G\in A\right)=p_{i}^{l}\sum_{a=0}^{n-l}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathchoice{{G\,\smash{\vrule height=5.46666pt,depth=2.46501pt}}_{\,\llbracket 1,n-l\rrbracket}}{{G\,\smash{\vrule height=5.46666pt,depth=2.46501pt}}_{\,\llbracket 1,n-l\rrbracket}}{{G\,\smash{\vrule height=3.82668pt,depth=1.77556pt}}_{\,\llbracket 1,n-l\rrbracket}}{{G\,\smash{\vrule height=2.73334pt,depth=2.36111pt}}_{\,\llbracket 1,n-l\rrbracket}}\in B^{\prime}_{a}\right)=p_{i}^{l}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathchoice{{G\,\smash{\vrule height=5.46666pt,depth=2.46501pt}}_{\,\llbracket 1,n-l\rrbracket}}{{G\,\smash{\vrule height=5.46666pt,depth=2.46501pt}}_{\,\llbracket 1,n-l\rrbracket}}{{G\,\smash{\vrule height=3.82668pt,depth=1.77556pt}}_{\,\llbracket 1,n-l\rrbracket}}{{G\,\smash{\vrule height=2.73334pt,depth=2.36111pt}}_{\,\llbracket 1,n-l\rrbracket}}\in\mathbb{I}^{n-l}\right)=p_{i}^{l}

which concludes the proof.
∎

3.2.
Distribution of cycle count

We now consider the number of cycles of our model. First observe that, if we write
G_{x_{1}}=i_{1},\dots,G_{x_{k}}=i_{k}
, and if
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
is a cycle of
\mathrm{std}(G)
, then
L_{i_{1}}(x_{1})=x_{2},L_{i_{2}}(x_{2})=x_{3}\dots,L_{i_{k}}(x_{k})=x_{1}
, i.e.
x_{1}
is a fixed point of
L_{i_{k}}\circ L_{i_{k-1}}\circ\dots\circ L_{i_{1}}
, and
x_{1}-1
is also a fixed point. In what follows, we study the fixed points of
L_{i_{k}}\circ L_{i_{k-1}}\circ\dots\circ L_{i_{1}}
. Actually, the same methods as for the fixed points will be applied, with some more elements needed to generalize the result. Note that a
k
-cycle
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
of
\mathrm{std}(G)
may correspond to fixed points of compositions of runs for multiple
k
-tuples: since
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=(x_{2},\dots,x_{k},x_{1})
, it also corresponds to fixed points of
L_{i_{1}}\circ L_{i_{k}}\circ\dots\circ L_{i_{2}}
. It also corresponds to fixed points of
L_{i_{k}}\circ L_{i_{k-1}}\circ\dots\circ L_{i_{1}}\circ L_{i_{k}}\circ L_{i_{k-1}}\circ\dots\circ L_{i_{1}}
, i.e. for a
2k
-tuple, even though the cycle has order
k
rather than
2k
. To identify which
k
-tuples
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
we have to study, we need the notion of primitive words.

3.2.1.
Primitive words, conjugacy classes

Definition 3.4
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
A
a set called alphabet (
A
will be equal to
\mathbb{I}
in what follows).

•

A word (i.e. a tuple)
x\in A^{k}
is a (nontrivial) power if there exists a word
y
and an integer
l\geq 2
such that
x=y^{l}=yy\dots yy
(
l
times). The word
x
is primitive if it is not a power.

•

We say a word
x
is a conjugate of a word
y
if
x
is a cyclic shift of
y
, that is, there exist two words
u,v
such that
x=uv
and
y=vu
. The conjugacy relation is an equivalence relation.

•

We write
Q_{k}(A)
the set of primitive words of length
k
over
A
, and
\widetilde{Q}_{k}(A)
the set of all the conjugacy classes of words of
Q_{k}(A)
.

This is a standard notion in language and automaton theory; for more details, see for example
[
Sha09
]
. The set
\widetilde{Q}_{k}(A)
is also called the set of all aperiodic necklaces of length
k
over
A
. Primitive words are, in a sense, the ”building blocks” of words, since the following result holds:

Proposition 3.5
.

[
Sha09
, Theorem 2.3.4]

Every word
x
can be written uniquely as a (trivial or nontrivial) power of a primitive word
\mathbf{r}
called the root of
x
.

We now show some remarkable properties about the conjugates of a primitive word.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
x
be a primitive word. Then, if
x=uv
, where
u
and
v
are nonempty, then
y=vu
is different from
x
.

Proof.

Let us proceed by contradiction: suppose that
x=uv=vu
where
u
and
v
are nonempty. Suppose further that
u
has minimal length. Then, the word
v
begins with the word
u
: we can write
v=uv^{\prime}
. Then, we have
x=u^{2}v^{\prime}=uv^{\prime}u
. Hence, by removing the initial
u
, we get
v=uv^{\prime}=v^{\prime}u
. Repeat as long as
|v^{\prime}|\geq|u|
so that
v
can be written
v=u^{l}\tilde{v}=\tilde{v}u^{l}
, where
l\geq 1
and
|\tilde{v}|<|u|
. For
x
, we then get
x=u^{l+1}\tilde{v}=\tilde{v}u^{l+1}
. Since
u
has minimal length,
\tilde{v}
must be the empty word, and
x=u^{l+1}
, which contradicts the fact that
x
is primitive.
∎

Proposition 3.7
.

[
Sha09
, Theorem 2.4.2]

Let
w
and
x
be conjugated words. Then,
x
is a nontrivial power if and only if
w
is a nontrivial power.

Proof.

Since
w
and
x
are conjugated, there exist two words
u
and
v
such that
w=uv
and
x=vu
. Suppose that
w
is a nontrivial power, then there exists a word
y
, and
k\geq 2
such that
w=y^{k}
. Then,
y^{k}=uv
. If
|u|
is a multiple of
|y|
, then
u=y^{i}
for an integer
i
, and
v=y^{k-i}
, and hence,
x=vu=y^{k}
. Else, we write
u=y^{i}r
and
v=sy^{k-i-1}
, where
r,s
are nonempty words such that
y=rs
. We then get
x=vu=sy^{k-i-1}y^{i}r=s(rs)^{k-1}r=(sr)^{k}
, which proves that
x
is a nontrivial power.
∎

Remark that this proof also gives us that if
w
and
x
are conjugated with
w=y^{k}
, then
x=z^{k}
where
y
and
z
are conjugated.

From the propositions
3.6
and
3.7
, it follows:

Proposition 3.8
.

The conjugacy class of a primitive word of length
k
is a set of
k
distinct primitive words.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
x=x_{1}\dots x_{k}
be a primitive word. Then, its reverse word
\tilde{x}=x_{k}\dots x_{1}
is a primitive word.

Proof.

Suppose that
\tilde{x}
is a nontrivial power, and write
\tilde{x}=y^{l}
, for a
l\geq 2
. Then,
x=\tilde{y}^{l}
where
\tilde{y}
is the reverse word of
y
, and hence
x
is a nontrivial power.
∎

In what follows, we only consider words over the alphabet
\mathbb{I}
, we then note
Q_{k}
instead of
Q_{k}(\mathbb{I})
. This notion is essential for the cycle combinatorics of
\mathrm{std}(G)
, but will also complicate some arguments. While the notion of primitive words is needed to obtain exact results, it is often irrelevant in asymptotic computations. We have for example the following result:

Proposition 3.10
.

If the distribution
(p_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{I}}
on
\mathbb{I}
is non-degenerate, then

\mathbb{P}\left((G_{1}G_{2}\dots G_{k})\in Q_{k}\right)\underset{k\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}1

and more generally, for
l\geq 1
,
\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in Q_{k}}(p_{i_{1}}\dots p_{i_{k}})^{l}\underset{k\rightarrow+\infty}{\sim}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{I}^{k}}(p_{i_{1}}\dots p_{i_{k}})^{l}=\left(\sum_{i\in\mathbb{I}}p_{i}^{l}\right)^{k}=\left\lVert\mathbf{p}\right\rVert_{l}^{lk}
.

Proof.

Fix
l\geq 1
.

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{p}\right\rVert_{l}^{lk}-\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in Q_{k}}(p_{i_{1}}\dots p_{i_{k}})^{l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{I}^{k}}(p_{i_{1}}\dots p_{i_{k}})^{l}-\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in Q_{k}}(p_{i_{1}}\dots p_{i_{k}})^{l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\underset{\text{power}}{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{I}^{k}}}(p_{i_{1}}\dots p_{i_{k}})^{l}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{d\geq 2,d|k}\;\;\sum_{u_{1},\dots,u_{k/d}\in\mathbb{I}}(p_{u_{1}}\dots p_{u_{k/d}})^{dl}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{d\geq 2,d|k}\left\lVert\mathbf{p}\right\rVert_{dl}^{lk}.

Recall now that the function that maps
r\in[1,+\infty]
to
\left\lVert\mathbf{p}\right\rVert_{r}
is decreasing, since
\mathbf{p}
is non degenerate. Thus,

\left\lVert\mathbf{p}\right\rVert_{l}^{lk}-\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in Q_{k}}(p_{i_{1}}\dots p_{i_{k}})^{l}\leq\sum_{d\geq 2,d|k}\left\lVert\mathbf{p}\right\rVert_{2l}^{lk}\leq 2\sqrt{k}\left\lVert\mathbf{p}\right\rVert_{2l}^{lk}=o\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{p}\right\rVert_{l}^{lk}\right)

since
\left\lVert\mathbf{p}\right\rVert_{2l}<\left\lVert\mathbf{p}\right\rVert_{l}
.
Finally, by dividing
\left\lVert\mathbf{p}\right\rVert_{l}^{kl}
, we get the stated equivalent.
∎

3.2.2.
Application to cycle count distribution

If
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
is a cycle of
\mathrm{std}(G)
satisfying the condition
G_{x_{1}}=i_{1},\dots,G_{x_{k}}=i_{k}
, we say that
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
is a cycle of type
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
of
\mathrm{std}(G)
. Since the cycle
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
has
k
different representations, corresponding to rotations of its elements, we say that the cycle is of type
\tilde{\mathbf{i}}
, where
\tilde{\mathbf{i}}
is the conjugacy class of
\mathbf{i}
. For any
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in\mathbb{I}^{k}
, define
L_{\mathbf{i}}=L_{i_{k}}\circ L_{i_{k-1}}\circ\dots\circ L_{i_{1}}
. Remark that, as for the runs
L_{j},j\in\mathbb{I}
, we have
L_{\mathbf{i}}(x)-L_{\mathbf{i}}(x-1)\in\{0;1\}
for any
x\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
. Consequently,
L_{\mathbf{i}}
has at least one fixed point and its fixed points are consecutive. Recall that if
(x_{1}\dots x_{k})
is a cycle of type
\mathbf{i}
of
\mathrm{std}(G)
, then
x_{1}
and
x_{1}-1
are both fixed points of
L_{\mathbf{i}}
.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and let
x_{1}\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
be such that
\mathrm{std}(G)^{k}(x_{1})=x_{1}
. Define
x_{2}=\mathrm{std}(G)(x_{1}),\dots,x_{k}=\mathrm{std}(G)(x_{k-1})
, and
i_{1}=G_{x_{1}},\dots,i_{k}=G_{x_{k}}
. Then, the size
d
of the cycle of
x_{1}
in
\mathrm{std}(G)
is the length of the root of the word
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
.

In other words, if a
k
-cycle of
\mathrm{std}(G)
is of type
\mathbf{i}
, then
\mathbf{i}
is a primitive word. Recall that, since a
k
-cycle can be written in
k
ways by rotating the elements of its support, we say that this cycle is of type
\tilde{\mathbf{i}}
. For these reasons, to study the cycles of
\mathrm{std}(G)
, we will study its cycles of type
\mathbf{i}
for any
\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k}
.

Proof of Proposition
3.11
.

Write
\mathbf{i}=\mathbf{r}^{k/d^{\prime}}
where
\mathbf{r}
is the root of
\mathbf{i}
, and
d^{\prime}
is the length of
\mathbf{r}
. We then have
L_{\mathbf{i}}=L_{\mathbf{r}}^{k/d^{\prime}}
. As for any
x,L_{\mathbf{r}}(x)-L_{\mathbf{r}}(x-1)\in\{0;1\}
, the function
L_{\mathbf{r}}(x)-x
is positive, then zero, then negative. It follows that the sequence
x_{1},L_{\mathbf{r}}(x_{1}),\dots,L_{\mathbf{r}}^{k/d^{\prime}}(x_{1})
is monotone. Now,
L_{\mathbf{r}}^{k/d^{\prime}}(x_{1})=L_{\mathbf{i}}(x_{1})=x_{1}
, hence the sequence is constant. We then have in fact
L_{\mathbf{r}}(x_{1})=x_{1}
which means that the size
d
of the orbit of
x_{1}
divides
d^{\prime}
.

Suppose now that
d<d^{\prime}
. Since
x_{d+1}=x_{1}
, we would get that
\mathbf{i}
is a power of
i_{1}\dots i_{d}
, and so is
\mathbf{r}
, which is a contradiction with the fact that
\mathbf{r}
is the root of
\mathbf{i}
. Hence,
d=d^{\prime}
.
∎

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
\mathbf{i}\in Q_{k}
, and
x_{1}\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket,x_{2}=L_{i_{1}}(x_{1}),\dots,x_{k}=L_{i_{k-1}}(x_{k-1})
. Then,
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
is a
k
-cycle of
\mathrm{std}(G)
of type
\mathbf{i}
if and only if
x_{1}
and
x_{1}-1
are both fixed points of
L_{\mathbf{i}}
.

Proof.

If
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
is a
k
-cycle of
\mathrm{std}(G)
of type
\mathbf{i}
, we have already seen that
x_{1}
is a fixed point of
L_{\mathbf{i}}
. Moreover, since
G_{x_{1}}=i_{1}
,
L_{i_{1}}(x_{1}-1)=L_{i_{1}}(x_{1})-1=x_{2}-1
. We repeat for
i_{2},\dots,i_{k}
and we get
L_{\mathbf{i}}(x_{1}-1)=x_{1}-1
.

Conversely, if
x_{1}
and
x_{1}-1
are both fixed points of
L_{\mathbf{i}}
, since the
L_{i_{j}}(x)
rise by 0 or 1 at each step, we necessarily get that for any
j\geq 1,L_{i_{j}}(x_{j})=x_{j+1}
and
L_{i_{j}}(x_{j}-1)=x_{j+1}-1
, which implies that
\mathrm{std}(G)(x_{j})=x_{j+1}
for any
j
. Hence,
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
is a cycle of
\mathrm{std}(G)
.
∎

Finally, the following result shows that any primitive word with length
k
can lead to a
k
-cycle.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
\mathbf{i}\in Q_{k}
. There exists a unique
g\in\mathbb{I}^{k}
such that
\mathrm{std}(g)
is a cycle
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
of type
\mathbf{i}
.

Proof.

Define
\tilde{\mathbf{i}}=i_{k}i_{k-1}\dots i_{1}
to be the reverse word of
\mathbf{i}
and, for any
j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
,

\tilde{\mathbf{i}}^{(j)}=i_{j-1}\dots i_{1}i_{k}\dots i_{j}

the conjugate of
\tilde{\mathbf{i}}
ending with
i_{j}
. In what follows, the conjugacy class of
\tilde{\mathbf{i}}
is endowed with the lexicographical order
\prec
. From Proposition
3.9
, we know that
\tilde{\mathbf{i}}
is primitive, and hence its conjugates are pairwise distinct (Proposition
3.8
), then we can sort them in increasing order. Let
x_{j}
be the rank of
\tilde{\mathbf{i}}^{(j)}
in this sorted sequence.

Existence.
Define
g\in\mathbb{I}^{k}
by
g_{x_{j}}=i_{j}
for any
j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
. We claim that
\sigma=\mathrm{std}(g)
is the cycle
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
. Recall that, for
j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
,
\sigma(x_{j})
is the rank of
(i_{j},x_{j})
in the sequence
\left((i_{m},x_{m})\right)_{m}
sorted in increasing order according to the lexicographical order
\prec
. For
j\neq m
,

(i_{j},x_{j})\prec(i_{m},x_{m})\iff(i_{j},\tilde{\mathbf{i}}^{(j)})\prec(i_{m},\tilde{\mathbf{i}}^{(m)})\iff i_{j}<i_{m}\text{ or }\left(i_{j}=i_{m}\text{ and }\tilde{\mathbf{i}}^{(j)}\prec\tilde{\mathbf{i}}^{(m)}\right).

Now, remark that the last assertion is exactly the definition of
\tilde{\mathbf{i}}^{(j+1)}\prec\tilde{\mathbf{i}}^{(m+1)}
, which is equivalent to
x_{j+1}<x_{m+1}
(with the convention
\tilde{\mathbf{i}}^{(k+1)}=\tilde{\mathbf{i}}^{(1)}
and
x_{k+1}=x_{1}
). Hence,

(i_{j},x_{j})\prec(i_{m},x_{m})\iff x_{j+1}<x_{m+1}.

Then, for any
j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
, the rank of
(i_{j},x_{j})
in the sequence
\left((i_{m},x_{m})\right)_{m}
is
x_{j+1}
, and it follows that
\sigma(x_{j})=x_{j+1}.

Uniqueness.
Let
g^{\prime}\in\mathbb{I}^{k}
be a sequence and let
(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})
be a
k
-cycle such that
\mathrm{std}(g^{\prime})
is the cycle
(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})
, and such that for any
j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
,
g^{\prime}_{y_{j}}=i_{j}
. We will show that for any
j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket,x_{j}=y_{j}
, and the equality
g=g^{\prime}
will then follow from the relation
g^{\prime}_{y_{j}}=i_{j}=g_{x_{j}}
. Since
\mathrm{std}(g^{\prime})(y_{j})=y_{j+1}
, the number
y_{j+1}
is the rank of
(i_{j},y_{j})
in the sequence
\left((i_{m},y_{m})\right)_{m}
sorted in increasing order according to the lexicographical order
\prec
. Then, for
j\neq m
,

y_{j+1}<y_{m+1}\iff(i_{j},t_{j})\prec(i_{m},y_{m})\iff i_{j}<i_{m}\text{ or }\left(i_{j}=i_{m}\text{ and }y_{j}<y_{m}\right).

For the second case, we repeat the process with
y_{j}<y_{m}
:

y_{j}<y_{m}\iff i_{j-1}<i_{m-1}\text{ or }\left(i_{j-1}=i_{m-1}\text{ and }y_{j-1}<y_{m-1}\right).

Since
\tilde{\mathbf{i}}
is primitive, this process ends, when we treat the case of two different
i_{s}
values. Finally, we have the equivalence

y_{j+1}<y_{m+1}\iff\tilde{\mathbf{i}}^{(j+1)}\prec\tilde{\mathbf{i}}^{(m+1)}\iff x_{j+1}<x_{m+1}.

Hence,
x_{j}=y_{j}
for any
j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
.
∎

Lemma 3.18

Let

Proof.

Let

4.

4.1.
Fixed-distribution case

In this section, we consider the case where the distribution
\mathbf{p}
of the

does not depend on

tend to infinity to study the asymptotic distribution of the cycles of our model. From Theorem

, it immediately follows:

Proposition 4.1
.

•

(Fixed points) For

pairwise distinct, for
n\rightarrow+\infty
, we have

(D_{i_{1}},\dots,D_{i_{r}})\overset{(d)}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{Geo}_{0}(1-p_{i_{1}})\otimes\dots\ \otimes\mathrm{Geo}_{0}(1-p_{i_{r}})\,.

•

(Cycles) More generally, for

pairwise distinct, we have for

,

\left(D_{\mathbf{i}_{1}},\dots,D_{\mathbf{i}_{r}}\right)\overset{(d)}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{Geo}_{0}(1-p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}})\otimes\dots\ \otimes\mathrm{Geo}_{0}(1-p_{\mathbf{i}_{r}}).

A natural question is to ask how does the distribution of total number of cycles of a given length behave, without restricting to a finite number of primitive words. We are in particular interested in having a convergence result in
\ell^{1}
since this will imply the convergence of the cycle counts
c_{k}
(recall that each

writes as an infinite sum of

’s). To do this, we need a criterion for convergence in distribution in
\ell^{1}(\bigcup_{l\leq k}\widetilde{Q}_{l})
. This result may be well-known but we have not found a reference in the literature. In what follows, let
C
be a countable set (
C
will equal
\bigcup_{l\leq k}\widetilde{Q}_{l}
later).

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(X^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
X
be random variables taking values in
\ell^{1}(C)
. Assume that

\bullet
the finite-dimensional distributions of
(X^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converge to those of
X

\bullet
there exists
u\in\ell^{1}(C)
such that for any
n
and
i
,
\mathbb{E}\left(\left|X^{n}_{i}\right|\right)\leq u_{i}
.

Then,
X^{n}
converges in distribution to
X
in
\ell^{1}(C)
.

Since
C
is countable, we can prove this theorem in the particular case
C=\mathbb{N}
. In order to prove this theorem, we use the notion of tight sequences.

Definition 4.3
.

A sequence
(X^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of random variable taking values in a complete metric space
E
is tight if for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a compact subset
K
of
E
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N},\mathbb{P}\left(X_{n}\in K\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon
.

In that case, we have this useful result, following from Prokhorov theorem:

Theorem 4.4
.

[
Bil99
, Theorem 5.1]

Let
(X^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of random variables taking values in a complete separable metric space
E
, and
Z
a random variable taking values in
E
. If
X_{n}
is tight, and if any subsequence of
X_{n}
converging in distribution converges to
Z
, then
X_{n}
converges in distribution to
Z
.

As we would like to apply the previous theorem to
E=\ell^{1}(\mathbb{N})
, we need a compactness criterion on
\ell^{1}(\mathbb{N})
.

Proposition 4.5
.

A subset
K
of
\ell^{1}(\mathbb{N})
is compact if and only if
K
is closed, bounded and for any
\delta>0
, there exists
N\geq 0
such that for any
\displaystyle u\in K,\sum_{n\geq N}\left|u_{n}\right|<\delta
.

Proof.

Suppose that
K
is a compact subset of
\ell^{1}(\mathbb{N})
. Clearly,
K
is closed and bounded. For
N\geq 0
and
u\in K
, we write

F_{N}(u)=\sum_{n\geq N}|u_{n}|.

The functions
F_{N}
are continuous (and even 1-Lipschitz) on
K
: we indeed have

\left|F_{N}(u)-F_{N}(v)\right|=\left|\sum_{n\geq N}(|u_{n}|-|v_{n}|)\right|\leq\sum_{n\geq N}|u_{n}-v_{n}|\leq\left\lVert u-v\right\rVert_{1}.

Moreover, the function sequence
(F_{N})_{N\geq 0}
is non-increasing (i.e. for any
u\in K
and
N\geq 0
,
F_{N}(u)\geq F_{N+1}(u)
), and converges pointwise to 0, hence Dini’s theorem gives us that the convergence is uniform on the compact
K
. We thus have the direct implication.

Conversely, assume that
K
is closed, included in the ball
B(0,R)
, and that for any
\delta>0
, there exists
N\geq 0
such that for any
\displaystyle u\in K,\sum_{n\geq N}\left|u_{n}\right|<\delta
. We fix
\delta>0
and such an
N
. We then have that
K
is included in
H_{N}+B(0,\delta)
, where
H_{N}=\left\{u\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{N})\mid\forall n\geq N,u_{n}=0\right\}
which is finite-dimensional. The ball
\overline{B_{H_{N}}(0,\delta+R)}
is a compact subset of
H_{N}
, thus there exists
M\geq 1
and
y^{1},...,y^{M}\in H_{N}
such that

\overline{B_{H_{N}}(0,\delta+R)}\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{M}B_{H_{N}}(y^{i},\delta).

Let
x\in K
. There exists
y\in H_{N}
such that
\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_{1}<\delta
. Then, we get that
\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{1}-\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{1}<\delta
, thus
\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{1}<\delta+\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{1}<\delta+R
. Therefore,
y\in B_{H_{N}}(0,\delta+R)
, then there exists
j\in{1,...,M}
such that
\left\lVert y-y^{j}\right\rVert_{1}<\delta
. Then it holds

\left\lVert x-y^{j}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert y-y^{j}\right\rVert_{1}<2\delta.

Finally,

K\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{M}B(y^{j},2\delta).

We have proved that the set
K
is totally bounded in the complete space
\ell^{1}(\mathbb{N})
, hence it is compact (see e.g.
[
Sch97
, 19.18]
).
∎

We now turn to the proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Since
C
is countable, we assume that
C=\mathbb{N}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
. On the one hand, we have for
A>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert X^{n}\right\rVert_{1}>A\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(\left\lVert X^{n}\right\rVert_{1}\right)}{A}\leq\frac{\sum_{i\geq 0}{\mathbb{E}(|X_{i}^{n}|)}}{A}\leq\frac{\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{1}}{A}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

for
A
chosen large enough, independently of
n
. On the other hand, for
k\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i\geq N}|X^{n}_{i}|\geq\frac{1}{k}\right)\leq k\sum_{i\geq N}|u_{i}|,

hence there exists
N_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
independent of
n
such that
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i\geq N_{k}}|X^{n}_{i}|\geq\frac{1}{k}\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{k+1}}
.

Let us set

K=\left\{v\in l^{1}(\mathbb{N}),\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{1}\leq A\text{ and }\forall k\geq 1,\sum_{i\geq N_{k}}|v_{i}|\leq\frac{1}{k}\right\}

which is compact by Proposition
4.5
. Then, we have
\mathbb{P}(X^{n}\in K)\geq 1-\varepsilon
, hence the sequence
(X^{n})
is tight. The result comes from Theorem
4.4
and from the fact that the finite-dimensional distributions of the required limit characterizes its distribution.
∎

Let us return to our model of random permutations. From the previous theorem, it follows a result on the cycles of
\mathrm{std}(G)
with length smaller than any fixed number
k
as
n
tends to infinity (Theorem
1.2
). Recall that
c_{k}\left(\mathrm{std}(G)\right)=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k}}D_{\textbf{i}}.
We can now prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

First, note that
p_{i}\leq 1/2
excepted for at most one
i
. Then, if
l=1
,
\frac{p_{i}}{1-p_{i}}\leq 2p_{i}
excepted for at most one
i
, and if
l\geq 2
,
p_{\mathbf{i}}
is a product with at least two
p_{j}
distinct factors with sum less than or equal to 1, hence
p_{\mathbf{i}}\leq 1/2
(even 1/4) for any
\mathbf{i}\in Q_{l}
, then
\frac{p_{\mathbf{i}}}{1-p_{\mathbf{i}}}\leq 2p_{\mathbf{i}}
. Hence, for any
l\leq k
,
\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in Q_{l}}\frac{p_{\mathbf{i}}}{1-p_{\mathbf{i}}}<+\infty
.

We claim that
\underset{\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{l},l\leq k}{\bigotimes}\mathrm{Geo}_{0}(1-p_{\mathbf{i}})
is a distribution taking values in the set
\ell^{1}\left(\bigcup_{l\leq k}\widetilde{Q}_{l}\right)
: indeed, Fubini-Tonelli’s theorem gives for i.i.d.
X_{\mathbf{i}}
with distribution
\mathrm{Geo}_{0}(1-p_{\mathbf{i}})
,

\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{l}}X_{\mathbf{i}}\right)=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{l}}\mathbb{E}(X_{\mathbf{i}})=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{l}}\frac{p_{\mathbf{i}}}{1-p_{\mathbf{i}}}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\frac{1}{l}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in Q_{l}}\frac{p_{\mathbf{i}}}{1-p_{\mathbf{i}}}<+\infty.

Therefore,
\sum_{i\geq 0}X_{i}
is finite a.s.

Recall that it follows from Theorem
1.1
that the finite-dimensional distributions of
(D_{\mathbf{i}}^{n})_{\mathbf{i}}
converge to those of
\underset{\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{l},l\leq k}{\bigotimes}\mathrm{Geo}_{0}(1-p_{\mathbf{i}})
as
n
tends to infinity. To apply Theorem
4.2
, it is sufficient to note that for any
\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{l}
and any
n\geq 1,\mathbb{E}(D_{\mathbf{i}}^{n})\leq\mathbb{E}(X_{\mathbf{i}})
by Proposition
3.1
, and that
\bigcup_{l\leq k}\widetilde{Q}_{l}
is countable.
∎

4.2.
The small frequency case

In this part, we assume that the distribution
\mathbf{p}
varies with
n
, more precisely, we consider the case where
\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{i\in\mathbb{I}}p_{i}^{(n)}\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
. In this case, the behavior of the short cycles is different from the previous case, and we recover the same result as in the uniform model. A few technical lemmas will be useful before proving Theorem
1.4
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{p}
be random variables taking values in
\mathbb{N}
, and let
r_{1},\dots,r_{p}\geq 1
. Then,

\mathbb{E}\left(X_{1}^{r_{1}}\dots X_{p}^{r_{p}}\right)=\sum_{l_{1}\geq 1}\dots\sum_{l_{p}\geq 1}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{p}\left[l_{j}^{r_{j}}-\left(l_{j}-1\right)^{r_{j}}\right]\right)\mathbb{P}\left(X_{1}\geq l_{1},\dots,X_{p}\geq l_{p}\right).

Proof.

We have

\mathbb{E}\left(X_{1}^{r_{1}}\dots X_{p}^{r_{p}}\right)=\sum_{k_{1}\geq 1}\dots\sum_{k_{p}\geq 1}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{p}k_{j}^{r_{j}}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(X_{1}=k_{1},\dots,X_{p}=k_{p}\right).

Replace
k_{j}^{r_{j}}
by the telescoping sum
\sum_{l_{j}=1}^{k_{j}}\left[l_{j}^{r_{j}}-(l_{j}-1)^{r_{j}}\right]
, and then by expanding the product of these sums and changing the summation order, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(X_{1}^{r_{1}}\dots X_{p}^{r_{p}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l_{1}\geq 1}\dots\sum_{l_{p}\geq 1}\sum_{k_{1}\geq l_{1}}\dots\sum_{k_{p}\geq l_{p}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{p}\left[l_{j}^{r_{j}}-(l_{j}-1)^{r_{j}}\right]\right)\mathbb{P}\left(X_{1}=k_{1},\dots,X_{p}=k_{p}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l_{1}\geq 1}\dots\sum_{l_{p}\geq 1}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{p}\left[l_{j}^{r_{j}}-\left(l_{j}-1\right)^{r_{j}}\right]\right)\mathbb{P}\left(X_{1}\geq l_{1},\dots,X_{p}\geq l_{p}\right)

which is the stated result.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

If
\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{\infty}\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
, then for
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
m\geq 1
,
q_{1},\dots,q_{m}\geq 1
and
\mathbf{i}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m}
pairwise distinct classes of primitive words of respective lengths
k_{1},\dots,k_{m}\leq k
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left(D_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}^{q_{1}}\dots D_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}^{q_{m}}\right)\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{=}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}\left(1+O\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{\infty}\right)\right)

where the error is uniform in
\mathbf{i}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m}
.

Proof.

From Lemma
4.6
and Theorem
1.1
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(D_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}^{q_{1}}\dots D_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}^{q_{m}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\underset{\sum k_{i}l_{i}\leq n}{l_{1},\dots,l_{m}\geq 1}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}^{l_{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}^{l_{m}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left[l_{j}^{q_{j}}-(l_{j}-1)^{q_{j}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}\sum_{\underset{\sum k_{i}l_{i}\leq n-k_{1}-\dots-k_{m}}{l_{1},\dots,l_{m}\geq 0}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}^{l_{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}^{l_{m}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left[(l_{j}+1)^{q_{j}}-l_{j}^{q_{j}}\right]\right).

It remains to prove that the sum behaves as
1+O\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{\infty}\right)
. To do this, we bound the
p_{i}^{(n)}
by
\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{\infty}
, and the
q_{j}
by
\sum q_{j}
.

\sum_{\underset{\sum k_{i}l_{i}\leq n-k_{1}-\dots-k_{m}}{l_{1},\dots,l_{m}\geq 0}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}^{l_{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}^{l_{m}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left[(l_{j}+1)^{q_{j}}-l_{j}^{q_{j}}\right]\right)-1\\
\leq\sum_{\underset{\text{not all zero}}{l_{1},\dots,l_{m}\geq 0}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{\infty}^{l_{i}}\left[(l_{j}+1)^{\sum q_{j}}-l_{j}^{\sum q_{j}}\right]\right)

\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{\infty}m\left(\sum_{l\geq 0}\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{\infty}^{l}\left[(l+1)^{\sum q_{j}}-l^{\sum q_{j}}\right]\right)^{m-1}\left(\sum_{l\geq 1}\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{\infty}^{l-1}\left[(l+1)^{\sum q_{j}}-l^{\sum q_{j}}\right]\right).

Since
\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\dfrac{1}{2}
for
n
large enough, the above series converge, hence the above upper-bound is a
O\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{\infty}\right)
.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

If
\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{\infty}\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
, then for any
l\geq 2
,
\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{l}\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof.

It holds that

\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{l}^{l}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{I}}p_{i}^{l}\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{\infty}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{I}}p_{i}^{l-1}\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{\infty}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{I}}{p_{i}}=\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{\infty}

where the right-hand side converges to 0 by the hypothesis.
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

If
\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{\infty}\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
, then for any
k\geq 1,m\geq 1
,

\sum_{\underset{\text{pairwise distinct}}{\mathbf{i}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k}}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{k^{m}}.

Proof.

We have

1-k^{m}\sum_{\underset{\text{pairwise distinct}}{\mathbf{i}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k}}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}=\sum_{\mathbf{i}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m}\in\mathbb{I}^{k}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}-\sum_{\underset{\text{pairwise not conjugate}}{\mathbf{i}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m}\in Q_{k}}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}\\
=\sum_{(\mathbf{i}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m})\in B_{k}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}

where
B_{k}=\left(\mathbb{I}^{k}\right)^{m}\setminus\left\{\mathbf{i}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m}\in Q_{k}\text{ pairwise not conjugate}\right\}
. A tuple
\mathbf{i}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m}\in\left(\mathbb{I}^{k}\right)^{m}
is in
B_{k}
if and only if there exists
j
such that
\mathbf{i}_{j}
is a power or is conjugate to at least one of the
\mathbf{i}_{s},s<j
. Define

B_{k}=\bigsqcup_{l=1,\dots,m}B_{k}^{j}

where
B_{k}^{l}
is the set of all elements of
B_{k}
such that
j=l
is the smallest
j
such that
\mathbf{i}_{j}
is a power or is conjugate to at least one of the
\mathbf{i}_{s},s<j
. We then have

\sum_{(\mathbf{i}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m})\in B_{k}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}=\sum_{l=1}^{m}\sum_{\mathbf{i}_{1},\dots\mathbf{i}_{m}\in B_{k}^{l}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}.

The
l
-th term of this sum is

E_{l}:=\sum_{\mathbf{i}_{1}\in Q_{k}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}\left(\sum_{\underset{\text{not conjugate to }\mathbf{i}_{1}}{\mathbf{i}_{2}\in Q_{k}}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{2}}\dots\left(\sum_{\underset{\text{or conjugate to }\mathbf{i}_{1}\text{ or...or }\mathbf{i}_{l-1}}{\mathbf{i}_{l}\text{ power}}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{l}}\right)\right).

The innermost sum equals 1 since there is no condition on
i_{l+1},\dots,i_{m}
. It can be rewritten by recalling that if
\mathbf{i}
and
\mathbf{j}
are conjugates, then
p_{\mathbf{i}}=p_{\mathbf{j}}
. We then get, by bounding the sums over
Q_{k}
by the same sums over
\mathbb{I}^{k}
, that

E_{l}\leq\sum_{\mathbf{i}_{1}\in\mathbb{I}^{k}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{i}_{2}\in\mathbb{I}^{k}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{2}}\left(\dots\sum_{\mathbf{i}_{l-1}\in\mathbb{I}^{k}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{l-1}}\left(kp_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}+\dots+kp_{\mathbf{i}_{l-1}}+\sum_{\mathbf{i}_{l}\text{ power}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{l}}\right)\right)\right).

We rewrite this expression in the form

k\sum_{\mathbf{i}_{1}\in\mathbb{I}^{k}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}^{2}+...+k\sum_{\mathbf{i}_{l-1}\in\mathbb{I}^{k}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{l-1}}^{2}+\sum_{\mathbf{i}_{l}\text{ power}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{l}}\leq k(l-1)\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2k}+\sum_{d=2,d|k}^{k}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{I}^{k/d}}p_{\mathbf{i}}^{d}\\
=k(l-1)\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2k}+\sum_{d=2,d|k}^{k}\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{d}^{k}.

By summing over
1\leq l\leq m
, we then get that

1-k^{m}\sum_{\underset{\text{pairwise distinct}}{\mathbf{i}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k}}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}\leq k\frac{m(m-1)}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2k}+m\sum_{d=2,d|k}^{k}\left\lVert\mathbf{p}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{d}^{k}

which tends to 0 when
n
tends to infinity, by Lemma
4.8
, hence the result.
∎

We now turn to the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

We establish the convergence of joint moments of the variables
c_{j}
. Let
1\leq k_{1},\dots,k_{p}\leq k
be pairwise distinct, and
r_{1},\dots,r_{p}\geq 1
. We have:

\mathbb{E}\left(c_{k_{1}}^{r_{1}}\dots c_{k_{p}}^{r_{p}}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k_{1}}}D_{\mathbf{i}}\right)^{r_{1}}\dots\left(\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k_{p}}}D_{\mathbf{i}}\right)^{r_{p}}\right).

A partition
\{A_{1},\dots,A_{m}\}
of
\llbracket 1,r\rrbracket
is the partition induced by
\mathbf{i}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{r}
if the sequence
\mathbf{i}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{r}
takes exactly
m
distinct values, and if for any
s,t\in\llbracket 1,r\rrbracket
,
\mathbf{i}_{s}=\mathbf{i}_{t}
if and only if
s
and
t
are in the same block in the partition
\{A_{1},\dots,A_{m}\}
. We now expand each power, and we group the terms according to the induced partition of the indices:

\sum_{m_{1}=1}^{r_{1}}\dots\sum_{m_{p}=1}^{r_{p}}\sum_{\left\{A_{1}^{1},\dots,A_{m_{1}}^{1}\right\}}\dots\sum_{\left\{A_{1}^{p},\dots,A_{m_{p}}^{p}\right\}}\sum_{\underset{\text{pairwise distinct}}{\mathbf{i}_{1}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m_{1}}^{1}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k_{1}}}}\dots\sum_{\underset{\text{pairwise distinct}}{\mathbf{i}_{1}^{p},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m_{p}}^{p}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k_{p}}}}\mathbb{E}\left(D_{\mathbf{i}_{1}^{1}}^{\#A_{1}^{1}}\dots D_{\mathbf{i}_{m_{p}}^{p}}^{\#A_{m_{p}}^{p}}\right)

where the sums over the
A_{1}^{i},\dots,A_{m_{i}}^{i}
are over the partitions of
\llbracket 1,r_{i}\rrbracket
in
m_{i}
unordered nonempty subsets.

By Lemma
4.7
and Lemma
4.9
, we then get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(c_{k_{1}}^{r_{1}}\dots c_{k_{p}}^{r_{p}}\right)\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1}=1}^{r_{1}}\dots\sum_{m_{p}=1}^{r_{p}}\frac{1}{k_{1}^{m_{1}}\dots k_{p}^{m_{p}}}\sum_{\{A_{1}^{1},\dots,A_{m_{1}}^{1}\}}\dots\sum_{\{A_{1}^{p},\dots,A_{m_{p}}^{p}\}}1

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{1}=1}^{r_{1}}\dots\sum_{m_{p}=1}^{r_{p}}\frac{1}{k_{1}^{m_{1}}\dots k_{p}^{m_{p}}}S(r_{1},m_{1})\dots S(r_{p},m_{p})

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{m_{1}=1}^{r_{1}}\frac{1}{k_{1}^{m_{1}}}S(r_{1},m_{1})\right)\dots\left(\sum_{m_{p}=1}^{r_{p}}\frac{1}{k_{p}^{m_{p}}}S(r_{p},m_{p})\right)

where we recall that
S(r_{i},m_{i})
is the Stirling number of the second kind, corresponding to the number of partitions in
m_{i}
unordered nonempty subsets of
\llbracket 1,r_{i}\rrbracket
.

We recognize the product of the
r_{i}
th moments of Poisson distributions with parameters
\frac{1}{k_{i}}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

5.
Asymptotic distribution of large cycles

5.1.
Infinite-dimensional simplex and large cycles

Let us denote the infinite-dimensional standard simplex by
\displaystyle\Delta^{\infty}=\left\{x\in[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}\;\big|\;x_{1}\geq x_{2}\geq\dots\text{ and }\sum_{j\in\ \mathbb{N}^{*}}x_{j}\leq 1\right\}
. In this section, we study a few convergence properties in this set, which we equip with the topology of pointwise convergence.

Proposition 5.1
.

The simplex
\Delta^{\infty}
is compact.

Proof.

First, it follows from Tykhonov’s theorem that
[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
is compact. The non-increasing sequences of
[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
form a closed subset of
[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
. Indeed, we can write

\displaystyle\left\{x\in[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}\;\big|\;x_{1}\geq x_{2}\geq\dots\right\}=\bigcap_{j\geq 1}\left\{x\in[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}\;\big|\;x_{j}\geq x_{j+1}\right\}

which is an intersection of closed sets. Now, if
x^{(n)}
is a sequence of elements of
\Delta^{\infty}
which converges pointwise to
x
, Fatou’s Lemma gives us

\sum_{j}x_{j}\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\sum_{j}x_{j}^{(n)}\leq 1,

thus
x\in\Delta^{\infty}
. Hence,
\Delta^{\infty}
is a closed subset of the compact
[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
, and the result follows.
∎

For
t\geq 2
, define

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}m_{t}&:&\Delta^{\infty}&\to&\mathbb{R}\\
&&x&\mapsto&\displaystyle\sum_{j}x_{j}^{t}\end{array}

For
t=1
, we define
m_{1}
identically equal to 1.

Proposition 5.2
.

•

For
a
and
b
in
\Delta^{\infty}
, if for any
t\geq 2,m_{t}(a)=m_{t}(b)
, then
a=b.

•

The
m_{t}
for
t\geq 2
are continuous.

•

For
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a
in
\Delta^{\infty}
, if for any
t\geq 2,m_{t}(a_{n})\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}m_{t}(a)
then
a_{n}\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}a
.

Proof.

For the first point, note that
m_{t}(a)
is the
(t-1)
th moment of the probability measure
\nu_{a}=\sum a_{j}\delta_{a_{j}}+\left(1-\sum a_{j}\right)\delta_{0}
, which has bounded support, hence, it is determined by its moments. Thus, if for any
t\geq 2,m_{t}(a)=m_{t}(b)
, then
\nu_{a}=\nu_{b}
. Now, the measure
\nu_{a}
characterizes
a
: the greatest value of the sequence
a
is the greatest value
a_{1}
of the support, and appears
\nu_{a}(a_{1})/a_{1}
times in
a
, and so on.

For the second point, note that, for
x\in\Delta^{\infty}
, we have that for any
j\geq 1,x_{j}\leq\frac{1}{j}
, since the
x_{j}
are non-increasing and their sum is 1. Thus, if
a_{n}\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}a
, then
m_{t}(a_{n})\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}m_{t}(a)
by the dominated convergence theorem, with
x=\frac{1}{j^{t}}
as a dominating sequence.

The third point follows from the two previous ones, and the compactness of
\Delta^{\infty}
.
∎

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,Z
be random variables taking values in
\Delta^{\infty}
. Then
X_{n}
converges in distribution in
Z
if and only if for any
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, for any
t_{1},\dots,t_{r}\geq 2
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left(m_{t_{1}}(X_{n})\dots m_{t_{r}}(X_{n})\right)\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{E}(m_{t_{1}}(Z)\dots m_{t_{r}}(Z)).

Proof.

Denote by
\mathcal{A}
the subalgebra of
C(\Delta^{\infty})
(the set of continuous (bounded) functions on
\Delta^{\infty}
) generated by the
m_{t}
for
t\geq 1
. The direct implication follows immediately from the fact that the functions of
\mathcal{A}
are continuous and bounded. Let us now prove the converse implication. By hypothesis, we have

\forall g\in\mathcal{A},\mathbb{E}\left(g(X_{n})\right)\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{E}\left(g(Z)\right).

The algebra
\mathcal{A}
contains the constant functions, and separates the points of
\Delta^{\infty}
(Proposition
5.2
), hence, Stone-Weierstrass theorem gives us that it is dense in
C_{b}(\Delta^{\infty})
.

Let
f\in C_{b}(\Delta^{\infty})
, let
\varepsilon>0
. Then there exists a function
g
in
\mathcal{A}
such that
\left\lVert g-f\right\rVert_{\infty}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}(f(X_{n}))-\mathbb{E}(f(Z))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\mathbb{E}(f(X_{n}))-\mathbb{E}(g(X_{n}))\right|+\left|\mathbb{E}(g(X_{n}))-\mathbb{E}(g(Z))\right|+\left|\mathbb{E}(g(Z))-\mathbb{E}(f(Z))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert g-f\right\rVert_{\infty}+\left|\mathbb{E}(g(X_{n}))-\mathbb{E}(g(Z))\right|+\left\lVert g-f\right\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2}+\left|\mathbb{E}(g(X_{n}))-\mathbb{E}(g(Z))\right|+\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Since
g\in\mathcal{A}
, the second term tends to 0. Therefore,

\forall\varepsilon>0,\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\left|\mathbb{E}(f(X_{n}))-\mathbb{E}(f(Z))\right|\leq\varepsilon.

This proves the result.
∎

5.2.
Uniform model and Poisson-Dirichlet process

In the uniform model, the asymptotic distribution of the vector
(\lambda_{1}^{(n)},\lambda_{2}^{(n)},\dots)
, where
\lambda_{i}^{(n)}
is the length of the
i
th greatest cycle of a uniform random permutation of size
n
, is well known (Goncharov, 1944).

Definition 5.4
.

The Poisson-Dirichlet process (with parameter 1) is a random variable
Z
taking values in
\Delta^{\infty}
, defined by the following procedure (”Stick-breaking process”):

•

Consider i.i.d. random variables
(U_{k})_{k\geq 1}
uniform in
(0;1)
;

•

define
Y_{1}=U_{1}
and
R_{1}=1-U_{1}
(break a stick of length 1 and keep a piece of length
R_{1}
);

•

define
Y_{2}=R_{1}U_{2}
and
R_{2}=R_{1}(1-U_{2})
(break a stick of length
R_{1}
and keep a piece of length
R_{2}
);

•

iterate infinitely many times;

•

order the sequence
Y
in decreasing order to get
Z
.

See Figure
7
for a visual representation of the procedure.

0
1
Y_{1}
Y_{2}
Y_{3}
Y_{4}
Y_{5}
\dots
0
1

Z_{2}

Z_{4}
Z_{5}
\dots

Theorem 5.5
.

(e.g.
[
ABT03
, formula (1.36)]
) The vector
\dfrac{\lambda^{(n)}}{n}=n^{-1}(\lambda_{1}^{(n)},\lambda_{2}^{(n)},\dots)
, where
\lambda_{j}^{(n)}
is the length of the
j
th greatest cycle of a uniform random permutation of size
n
, converges in distribution in
\Delta^{\infty}
to the Poisson-Dirichlet process.

The idea behind this theorem is that, for a uniform random permutation
\sigma_{n}
of size
n
, if
\rho
is the cycle of
\sigma_{n}
containing 1, the length of
\rho
is uniform on
\left\{1,\dots,n\right\}
and the restriction of
\sigma_{n}
on
\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket\setminus\mathrm{supp}(\rho)
is uniform on
\mathfrak{S}_{n-k}
, conditioned to
\#\mathrm{supp}(\rho)=k
. This is not the case for
\mathrm{std}(G)
, but the result for the uniform model will allow us to recover the limit distribution for the standardized model. To achieve this, we will need the values of
\mathbb{E}(m_{t_{1}}(Z)\dots m_{t_{r}}(Z))
, in the case where
Z
is a Poisson-Dirichlet process, in order to apply Proposition
5.3
. These values are not easy to compute directly from the definition of the Poisson-Dirichlet process. However, since we know precisely the joint moments of cycles in the uniform model, we can use Proposition
5.3
and Theorem
5.5
to get the values of interest for the Poisson-Dirichlet process.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
m\geq 1
, let
t_{1},\dots,t_{r}\geq 2
, let
Z
be the Poisson-Dirichlet process. Then,

\mathbb{E}(m_{t_{1}}(Z)\dots m_{t_{r}}(Z))=\sum_{m=1}^{r}\sum_{\left\{A_{1},\dots,A_{m}\right\}}\int_{0}^{1}\dots\int_{0}^{1}x_{1}^{t_{A_{1}}-1}\dots x_{m}^{t_{A_{m}}-1}\mathds{1}_{\sum x_{i}\leq 1}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}x_{m}

where the second sum is over all the partitions of
\llbracket 1,r\rrbracket
in
m
unordered nonempty subsets, and where
t_{A}=\displaystyle\sum_{i\in A}t_{i}
.

To prove this Lemma, we will use the result from Theorem
5.5
and the following lemma:

Lemma 5.7
.

[
ABT03
, Lemma 1.1]

Let us denote
x^{[r]}=x(x-1)\dots(x-r+1)
. Then, for
l_{1},\dots,l_{n}\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(C_{j}^{(n)}\right)^{[l_{1}]}\right)=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{j^{l_{j}}}\right)\mathds{1}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}jl_{j}\leq n\right\}

where the
C_{j}^{(n)}
are the number of cycles of length
j
in a uniform random permutation of size
n
.

From this lemma, it follows the result:

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
k_{1},\dots,k_{s}\geq 1
be pairwise distinct, and let
r_{1},\dots,r_{s}\geq 1
. Then,

\mathbb{E}\left(\left(C_{k_{1}}^{(n)}\right)^{r_{1}}\cdots\left(C_{k_{s}}^{(n)}\right)^{r_{s}}\right)=\sum_{m_{1}=1}^{r_{1}}\dots\sum_{m_{s}=1}^{r_{s}}\frac{1}{k_{1}^{m_{1}}\dots k_{s}^{m_{s}}}S(r_{1},m_{1})\dots S(r_{s},m_{s})\mathds{1}_{\sum k_{j}m_{j}\leq n}

where
S(r_{i},m_{i})
is the Stirling number of the second kind, corresponding to the number of partitions in
m_{i}
unordered nonempty subsets of
\llbracket 1,r_{i}\rrbracket
.

Proof.

This follows from the polynomial formula

X^{r}=\sum_{m=0}^{r}S(r,m)X^{[m]}

in which the Stirling numbers of the second kind appear.
∎

We can now prove Lemma
5.6
.

Proof of Lemma
5.6
.

We use Lemma
5.8
to get the limit of
\mathbb{E}\left(m_{t_{1}}\left(\dfrac{\lambda^{(n)}}{n}\right)\dots m_{t_{r}}\left(\dfrac{\lambda^{(n)}}{n}\right)\right)
, which is the required value for the Poisson-Dirichlet process, since Proposition
5.3
and Theorem
5.5
hold.

Remark that we have

\lambda=(\underbrace{n,\dots,n}_{C_{n}\text{ times}},\underbrace{n-1,\dots,n-1}_{C_{n-1}\text{ times}},\dots,\underbrace{j,\dots,j}_{C_{j}\text{ times}},\dots).

We then have for
t_{1},\dots,t_{r}\geq 2
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(m_{t_{1}}\left(\dfrac{\lambda^{(n)}}{n}\right)\dots m_{t_{r}}\left(\dfrac{\lambda^{(n)}}{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{\lambda_{j}^{t_{1}}}{n^{t_{1}}}\right)\cdots\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{\lambda_{j}^{t_{r}}}{n^{t_{r}}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\frac{1}{n^{t_{1}}}\sum_{k\geq 1}C_{k}k^{t_{1}}\right)\cdots\left(\frac{1}{n^{t_{r}}}\sum_{k\geq 1}C_{k}k^{t_{r}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1}\left(\frac{k_{1}}{n}\right)^{t_{1}}\cdots\left(\frac{k_{r}}{n}\right)^{t_{r}}\mathbb{E}(C_{k_{1}}\dots C_{k_{r}}).

We now group the terms according to the partition induced by
\{k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\}
to apply Lemma
5.8
:

\quad\mathbb{E}\left(m_{t_{1}}\left(\dfrac{\lambda^{(n)}}{n}\right)\dots m_{t_{r}}\left(\dfrac{\lambda^{(n)}}{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{r}\sum_{\underset{\text{partition of }\llbracket 1,r\rrbracket}{\left\{A_{1},\dots,A_{m}\right\}}}\sum_{\underset{\text{pairwise distinct}}{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}}\left(\frac{k_{1}}{n}\right)^{t_{A_{1}}}\cdots\left(\frac{k_{m}}{n}\right)^{t_{A_{m}}}\mathbb{E}(C_{k_{1}}^{\#A_{1}}\dots C_{k_{m}}^{\#A_{m}})

=\sum_{m=1}^{r}\sum_{\underset{\text{partition of }\llbracket 1,r\rrbracket}{\left\{A_{1},\dots,A_{m}\right\}}}\sum_{\underset{\text{pairwise distinct}}{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}}\left(\frac{k_{1}}{n}\right)^{t_{A_{1}}}\cdots\left(\frac{k_{m}}{n}\right)^{t_{A_{m}}}\sum_{l_{1}=1}^{\#A_{1}}\dots{\\
}\sum_{l_{m}=1}^{\#A_{m}}\frac{1}{k_{1}^{l_{1}}\dots k_{s}^{l_{m}}}S(\#A_{1},l_{1})\dots S(\#A_{m},l_{m})\mathds{1}_{\sum k_{j}l_{j}\leq n}.

If
l_{1}=\dots=l_{m}=1
, the term

\sum_{\underset{\text{pairwise distinct}}{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}}\left(\frac{k_{1}}{n}\right)^{t_{A_{1}}}\cdots\left(\frac{k_{m}}{n}\right)^{t_{A_{m}}}\frac{1}{k_{1}\dots k_{s}}\mathds{1}_{\sum k_{j}\leq n}\\
=\frac{1}{n^{m}}\sum_{\underset{\text{pairwise distinct}}{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}}\left(\frac{k_{1}}{n}\right)^{t_{A_{1}}-1}\cdots\left(\frac{k_{m}}{n}\right)^{t_{A_{m}}-1}\mathds{1}_{\sum k_{j}\leq n}

converges as
n
tends to infinity to the limit:

\int_{0}^{1}\dots\int_{0}^{1}x_{1}^{t_{A_{1}}-1}\dots x_{1}^{t_{A_{m}}-1}\mathds{1}_{\sum x_{j}\leq 1}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}x_{m}

since we recognize a multidimensional Riemann sum (the condition over the
k_{i}
does not affect the result: we only remove a number
O\left(n^{m-1}\right)
of terms, which leads to an error in
O\left(n^{-1}\right)
).

If the
l_{i}
are not all 1, then the term is

\sum_{\underset{\text{pairwise distinct}}{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}}\left(\frac{k_{1}}{n}\right)^{t_{A_{1}}}\cdots\left(\frac{k_{m}}{n}\right)^{t_{A_{m}}}\frac{1}{k_{1}^{l_{1}}\dots k_{s}^{l_{m}}}S(\#A_{1},l_{1})\dots S(\#A_{m},l_{m})\mathds{1}_{\sum k_{j}l_{j}\leq n}\\
=\frac{1}{n^{\sum t_{j}}}\sum_{\underset{\text{pairwise distinct}}{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}}S(\#A_{1},l_{1})\dots S(\#A_{m},l_{m})\left(\frac{k_{1}}{n}\right)^{t_{A_{1}}-l_{1}}\cdots\left(\frac{k_{m}}{n}\right)^{t_{A_{m}}-l_{m}}\mathds{1}_{\sum k_{j}l_{j}\leq n}

and thus is equivalent to
\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{\sum t_{j}-m}}\int_{0}^{1}\dots\int_{0}^{1}x_{1}^{t_{A_{1}}-l_{1}}\dots x_{1}^{t_{A_{m}}-l_{m}}\mathds{1}_{\sum x_{j}l_{j}\leq 1}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}x_{m}
which tends to 0.

We therefore have that
\mathbb{E}\left(m_{t_{1}}\left(\dfrac{\lambda^{(n)}}{n}\right)\dots m_{t_{r}}\left(\dfrac{\lambda^{(n)}}{n}\right)\right)
converges to

\sum_{m=1}^{r}\sum_{\left\{A_{1},\dots,A_{m}\right\}}\int_{0}^{1}\dots\int_{0}^{1}x_{1}^{t_{A_{1}}-1}\dots x_{1}^{t_{A_{m}}-1}\mathds{1}_{\sum x_{j}\leq 1}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}x_{m}

which is indeed the claimed value for the Poisson-Dirichlet process.
∎

5.3.
Large cycles of studied model

We return to the setting of the standardized permutation model, with
\mathbf{p}
depending on
n
, and we assume that there exists
R\in(0;1)
such that for any
n\geq 1
and
i\in\mathbb{I},p_{i}\leq R
. Then, we extend the convergence to the Poisson-Dirichlet process from the uniform model to the standardized permutation model, i.e. we prove Theorem
1.5
. We first establish a few technical lemmas to prove the theorem.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, let
k_{1},\dots,k_{m}\geq\varepsilon n
, let
\mathbf{i}_{1}\in Q_{k_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m}\in Q_{k_{m}}
pairwise non-conjugate, let
r\geq 1
and let
s_{1},\dots,s_{m}\geq 1
with sum less than or equal to
r
. Then,

\mathbb{E}\left(D_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}^{s_{1}}\dots D_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}^{s_{m}}\right)=p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}\mathds{1}_{\sum k_{j}\leq n}\left(1+O\left(R^{\varepsilon n}\right)\right)

where
O
only depends on
r
and
m
(and
R
which is supposed to be fixed in this section).

Proof.

By Lemma
4.6
and Theorem
1.1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(D_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}^{s_{1}}\dots D_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}^{s_{m}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\underset{\sum k_{j}l_{j}\leq n}{l_{1},\dots,l_{m}\geq 1}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}^{l_{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}^{l_{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(l_{j}^{s_{j}}-(l_{j}-1)^{s_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}\mathds{1}_{\sum k_{i}\leq n}\sum_{\underset{\sum k_{j}l_{j}\leq n-\sum k_{j}}{l_{1},\dots,l_{m}\geq 0}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}^{l_{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}^{l_{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left((l_{j}+1)^{s_{j}}-l_{j}^{s_{j}}\right).

Now control the sum. Recall that for any
j,p_{\mathbf{i}_{j}}\leq R^{k_{j}}
. Then,

\sum_{\underset{\sum k_{j}l_{j}\leq n-\sum k_{j}}{l_{1},\dots,l_{m}\geq 0}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}^{l_{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}^{l_{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left((l_{j}+1)^{s_{j}}-l_{j}^{s_{j}}\right)-1\leq\sum_{\underset{\text{not all zero}}{l_{1},\dots,l_{m}\geq 0}}R^{\sum k_{j}l_{j}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left((l_{j}+1)^{s_{j}}-l_{j}^{s_{j}}\right)\\
\leq R^{\varepsilon n}m\left(\sum_{l>0}R^{l-1}\left((l+1)^{r}-l^{r}\right)\right)\left(\sum_{l\geq 0}R^{l}\left((l+1)^{r}-l^{r}\right)\right)^{m-1}=R^{\varepsilon n}O(1)

and hence the result holds.
∎

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, let
k_{1},\dots,k_{s}>\varepsilon n
be pairwise distinct, let
r_{1},\dots,r_{s}\geq 1
with sum less than or equal to
r
. Then,

\mathbb{E}\left(c_{k_{1}}^{r_{1}}\dots c_{k_{s}}^{r_{s}}\right)=\left(\sum_{m_{1}=1}^{r_{1}}\dots\sum_{m_{s}=1}^{r_{s}}\frac{1}{k_{1}^{m_{1}}\dots k_{s}^{m_{s}}}S(r_{1},m_{1})\dots S(r_{s},m_{s})\mathds{1}_{\sum k_{j}m_{j}\leq n}\right)\left(1+O\left(R^{\varepsilon n/4}\right)\right)

where the
O
only depends on
r
(and
R
which is supposed to be fixed).

Note that the joint moments of the cycles are asymptotically the same as for the uniform model for the ”macroscopic” cycles (with size greater than
\varepsilon n
). What remains to be shown is that only these cycles contribute to the limiting expectations of Lemma
5.6
.

Proof.

Firstly we have

\mathbb{E}\left(c_{k_{1}}^{r_{1}}\dots c_{k_{s}}^{r_{s}}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\sum_{\mathbf{i}_{1}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k_{1}}}D_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}\right)^{r_{1}}\dots\left(\sum_{\mathbf{i}_{s}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k_{s}}}D_{\mathbf{i}_{s}}\right)^{r_{s}}\right).

We expand this expression, and we group the terms according to the partition induced by the indices:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\sum_{\mathbf{i}_{1}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k_{1}}}D_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}\right)^{r_{1}}\dots\left(\sum_{\mathbf{i}_{s}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k_{s}}}D_{\mathbf{i}_{s}}\right)^{r_{s}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1}=1}^{r_{1}}\dots\sum_{m_{s}=1}^{r_{s}}\sum_{\underset{\text{partition of }\llbracket 1,r_{1}\rrbracket}{\left\{A_{1}^{1},\dots,A_{m_{1}}^{1}\right\}}}\dots\sum_{\underset{\text{partition of }\llbracket 1,r_{s}\rrbracket}{\left\{A_{1}^{s},\dots,A_{m_{s}}^{s}\right\}}}\sum_{\underset{\text{pairwise }\neq}{\mathbf{i}_{1}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m_{1}}^{1}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k_{1}}}}\dots\sum_{\underset{\text{pairwise }\neq}{\mathbf{i}_{1}^{s},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m_{s}}^{s}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k_{s}}}}\mathbb{E}\left(D_{\mathbf{i}_{1}^{1}}^{\#A_{1}^{1}}\dots D_{\mathbf{i}_{m_{s}}^{s}}^{\#A_{m_{s}}^{s}}\right)

=\left(1+O\left(R^{\varepsilon n}\right)\right)\sum_{m_{1}=1}^{r_{1}}\dots\sum_{m_{s}=1}^{r_{s}}S(r_{1},m_{1})\dots S(r_{s},m_{s})\sum_{\underset{\text{pairwise }\neq}{\mathbf{i}_{1}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m_{1}}^{1}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k_{1}}}}\dots{\\
}\sum_{\underset{\text{pairwise }\neq}{\mathbf{i}_{1}^{s},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m_{s}}^{s}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k_{s}}}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}^{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{m_{s}}^{s}}\mathds{1}_{\sum k_{j}m_{j}\leq n}

by Lemma
5.9
with
r=\sum r_{i}
. Using the calculation from the proof of Lemma
4.9
, we note that

\displaystyle\sum_{\underset{\text{pairwise }\neq}{\mathbf{i}_{1}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m_{1}}^{1}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k_{1}}}}\dots\sum_{\underset{\text{pairwise }\neq}{\mathbf{i}_{1}^{s},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m_{s}}^{s}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k_{s}}}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{1}^{1}}\dots p_{\mathbf{i}_{m_{s}}^{s}}\mathds{1}_{\sum k_{j}m_{j}\leq n}

can be replaced by
\dfrac{1}{k_{1}^{m_{1}}\dots k_{s}^{m_{s}}}
with an error
O\left(R^{\varepsilon n/4}\right)
(with the inequality
\left\lVert\mathbf{p}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{\left\lVert\mathbf{p}\right\rVert_{\infty}}
). The result follows.
∎

We can now prove Theorem
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

We show that the moments
\mathbb{E}\left(m_{t_{1}}\left(\dfrac{\lambda^{(n)}}{n}\right)\dots m_{t_{r}}\left(\dfrac{\lambda^{(n)}}{n}\right)\right)
converge to the same value as in the uniform model, which will allow us to conclude using Proposition
5.3
.

Note that we have

\lambda^{(n)}=(\underbrace{n,\dots,n}_{C_{n}\text{ times}},\underbrace{n-1,\dots,n-1}_{C_{n-1}\text{ times}},\dots,\underbrace{j,\dots,j}_{C_{j}\text{ times}},\dots).

Thus,

\mathbb{E}\left(m_{t_{1}}\left(\dfrac{\lambda^{(n)}}{n}\right)\dots m_{t_{r}}\left(\dfrac{\lambda^{(n)}}{n}\right)\right)=\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1}\left(\frac{k_{1}}{n}\right)^{t_{1}}\dots\left(\frac{k_{r}}{n}\right)^{t_{r}}\mathbb{E}(c_{k_{1}}\dots c_{k_{r}}).

Define the function
f_{n}
on
(0,1]
by

f_{n}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{r}>\varepsilon n}\left(\frac{k_{1}}{n}\right)^{t_{1}}\dots\left(\frac{k_{r}}{n}\right)^{t_{r}}\mathbb{E}(c_{k_{1}}\dots c_{k_{r}}).

We have the convergence

f_{n}(\varepsilon)\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{E}\left(m_{t_{1}}\left(\dfrac{\lambda^{(n)}}{n}\right)\dots m_{t_{r}}\left(\dfrac{\lambda^{(n)}}{n}\right)\right)

and this convergence is uniform in
n
. Indeed,

(1)

\displaystyle\left|f_{n}(\varepsilon)-\mathbb{E}\left(m_{t_{1}}\left(\dfrac{\lambda^{(n)}}{n}\right)\dots m_{t_{r}}\left(\dfrac{\lambda^{(n)}}{n}\right)\right)\right|

(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}\leq\varepsilon n\text{ or ... or }k_{r}\leq\varepsilon n}\left(\frac{k_{1}}{n}\right)^{t_{1}}\dots\left(\frac{k_{r}}{n}\right)^{t_{r}}\mathbb{E}(c_{k_{1}}\dots c_{k_{r}})

(3)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{n_{1}^{t_{1}}\dots n_{r}^{t_{r}}}r\sum_{k_{1}\leq\varepsilon n}\sum_{k_{2},\dots,k_{r}\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\left(k_{1}^{t_{1}}c_{k_{1}}\dots k_{r}^{t_{r}}c_{k_{r}}\right)

(4)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{n_{1}^{t_{1}}\dots n_{r}^{t_{r}}}r\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\sum_{k_{1}\leq\varepsilon n}k_{1}^{t_{1}}c_{k_{1}}\right)\left(\sum_{k_{2}\geq 1}k_{2}c_{k_{2}}\right)^{t_{2}}\dots\left(\sum_{k_{r}\geq 1}k_{r}c_{k_{r}}\right)^{t_{r}}\right)

(5)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\varepsilon^{t_{1}-1}r

where at step
4
, we have bounded
\sum k_{j}^{t_{j}}c_{k_{j}}
for
j\geq 2
by
\left(\sum k_{j}c_{k_{j}}\right)^{t_{j}}=n^{t_{j}}
, and we have bounded
\left(\sum_{k_{1}\leq\varepsilon n}k_{1}^{t_{1}}c_{k_{1}}\right)
by

6.
Cycle count normality

This section deals with the total number of cycles of the permutation
\mathrm{std}(G)
. We recall that, for the uniform model, a central limit type theorem holds (proved in 1944 by Goncharov):

Theorem 6.1
.

(see e.g.
[
ABT03
, (1.31)]
)
Let
K_{n}
be the number of cycles of a uniform random permutation
\sigma_{n}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Then, as
n
tends to infinity,

\frac{K_{n}-\log(n)}{\sqrt{\log(n)}}\overset{(d)}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,1)

and the moments converge.

Is is natural to ask what happens for the
\mathrm{std}(G)
model. Actually, the same result holds (Theorem
1.6
). For
n\geq 1
, define
K_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}^{(n)}
, the total number of cycles of
\mathrm{std}(G)
. To prove this theorem, we will use the convergence of cumulants to those of the limit distribution.

Definition 6.2
.

•

The joint cumulant
\kappa
of several random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{r}
with finite moments is defined by

\kappa(X_{1},\dots,X_{r})=\frac{\partial^{r}G}{\partial t_{1}\dots\partial t_{r}}(0,\dots,0)

where
G(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})=\log\mathbb{E}\left(e^{\sum_{j=1}^{r}t_{j}X_{j}}\right)
.

•

For
r\geq 1
, the
r
th cumulant of a random variable
X
is defined by

\kappa_{r}(X)=\kappa(X,\dots,X)

where
X
appears
r
times in the joint cumulant
\kappa(X,\dots,X)
.

The cumulants give us an alternative to the moments of a distribution: their convergence are equivalent but, in some cases, it can be more convenient to choose one or the other. The cumulants are a common and useful notion to study the convergence of a sequence of random variable to a normal limit. More precisely, the following common properties hold:

Proposition 6.3
.

•

The map which maps
X_{1},\dots,X_{r}
to
\kappa(X_{1},\dots,X_{r})
is
r
-linear.

•

If the family
(X_{j})_{1\leq j\leq r}
of random variables can be split into two independent families, then
\kappa(X_{1},\dots,X_{r})=0
.

•

Let
r\geq 1
and let
X_{1},\dots,X_{r}
be random variables. Then, we have a relationship between the joint cumulants and the joint moments given by

\kappa\left(X_{1},...,X_{r}\right)=\sum_{\pi}\mu(\pi,\{[r]\})\prod_{B\in\pi}\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{j\in B}X_{j}\right)

where the sum runs over all the partitions
\pi
of the set
[r]=\{1,\dots,r\}
, and where
\mu(\pi,\{[r]\})=(|\pi|-1)!(-1)^{|\pi|-1}
is the Möbius function on the set of all the partitions of
[r]
.

•

In particular,
\kappa(X_{1})=\mathbb{E}(X_{1})
and
\kappa(X_{1},X_{2})=\mathrm{Cov}(X_{1},X_{2})
.

•

Let
X_{n}
be a sequence of random variable, then the moments converge if and only if the cumulants converge. In this case, if the limits of the cumulants are the cumulants of a moment-determinate distribution
\mu
, then
X_{n}\overset{(d)}{\longrightarrow}\mu
.

•

If
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, then, for any
r\geq 1
,

\kappa_{r}(Z)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }r=2;\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

The aim of what follows is to prove that the
r
th cumulant of the random variable in Theorem
1.6
, converges to 1 if
r=2
and 0 otherwise, from which the result follows. To achieve this, we will also need the useful following formula, from Leonov and Shiryaev, giving a relation between the cumulants of random variables with repetition and the cumulants of power of random variables without repetition, which is more convenient in our case.

Definition 6.4
.

Let
r
be a positive integer, and let
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
be two partitions of
[r]=\{1,\dots,r\}
. Then, the join
\pi\vee\pi^{\prime}
of
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
is the finest partition which is coarser than
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
.

To get a different point of view,

if and only if there exists a chain

such that
j_{1}=j,j_{l}=k
and for any

,

and

belong to the same block in
\pi
or in
\pi^{\prime}
.

Theorem 6.5
.

(
[
LS59
]
, see also
[
Śni06
, Theorem 4.4]
)
Let

and let
X_{1},\dots,X_{r}
be random variables. Let
\pi_{0}
be a partition of
[r]=\{1,\dots,r\}
. Then, we have

\kappa\left(\prod_{j\in B_{0}}X_{j},B_{0}\in\pi_{0}\right)=\sum_{\pi\vee\pi_{0}=\{[r]\}}\prod_{B\in\pi}\kappa\left(X_{j},j\in B\right)

where the sum runs over all the partitions
\pi
of

is

.

Finally, since we will actually study the convergence in distribution of a modified variable, we need a criterion of convergence for the moments of the variable of interest.

Proposition 6.6
.

[
Bil86

Let

be two positive real numbers. Let

be a sequence of random variable, and

Let us get back to the model. We first show two useful lemmas before proving Theorem

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
R\in(0;1)
, and suppose that for any

and
i\in\mathbb{I},p_{i}\leq R

\mathbf{i}_{1}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k_{1}},...,\mathbf{i}_{m}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k_{m}}

\kappa\left(D_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}^{q_{1}},\dots,D_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}^{q_{m}}\right)=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{p_{\mathbf{i}_{j}}}{k_{j}}\right)O\left(R^{n/2}\right)

Proof.

By Proposition
6.3

\displaystyle\kappa\left(D_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}^{q_{1}},\dots,D_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}^{q_{m}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi}\mu(\pi,\{[r]\})\prod_{B\in\pi}\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{j\in B}D_{\mathbf{i}_{j}}^{q_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi}\mu(\pi,\{[r]\})\prod_{B\in\pi}\sum_{\underset{\sum k_{j}l_{j}\leq n}{l_{j}\geq 1,j\in B}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left[l_{j}^{q_{j}}-(l_{j}-1)^{q_{j}}\right]p_{\mathbf{i}_{j}}^{l_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi}\mu(\pi,\{[r]\})\sum_{\underset{\forall B\in\pi,\sum_{B}k_{j}l_{j}\leq n}{l_{1},\dots,l_{m}\geq 1}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left[l_{j}^{q_{j}}-(l_{j}-1)^{q_{j}}\right]p_{\mathbf{i}_{j}}^{l_{j}}

The index sets all contain the set
\displaystyle\left\{l_{1},...,l_{m}\geq 1,\sum_{j=1}^{m}k_{j}l_{j}\leq n\right\}
. All the corresponding terms of the sum appear with coefficient

. Hence, writing

\displaystyle\left|\kappa\left(D_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}^{q_{1}},\dots,D_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}^{q_{m}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{\underset{k_{j}l_{j}\leq n\forall j;\sum k_{j}l_{j}>n}{l_{1},...,l_{m}\geq 1}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left[l_{j}^{q_{j}}-(l_{j}-1)^{q_{j}}\right]p_{\mathbf{i}_{j}}^{l_{j}}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{\underset{k_{j}l_{j}\leq n\forall j;\sum k_{j}l_{j}>n}{l_{1},...,l_{m}\geq 1}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}n^{q_{j}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{j}}R^{k_{j}(l_{j}-1)}

where we use the inequalities

, and

\displaystyle\left|\kappa\left(D_{\mathbf{i}_{1}}^{q_{1}},\dots,D_{\mathbf{i}_{m}}^{q_{m}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq CR^{n-\sum k_{j}}\sum_{\underset{k_{j}l_{j}\leq n\forall j;\sum k_{j}l_{j}>n}{l_{1},...,l_{m}\geq 1}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}n^{q_{j}}p_{\mathbf{i}_{j}}

\displaystyle\leq CR^{n-mn/\log(\log(n))}n^{m}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{p_{\mathbf{i}_{j}}}{k_{j}}\right)n^{r}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{p_{\mathbf{i}_{j}}}{k_{j}}\right)O\left(R^{n/2}\right).\qed

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
R\in(0;1)
, and suppose that for any
n\geq 1
and

. Then, it holds

\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k}}p_{\mathbf{i}}\underset{k\rightarrow+\infty}{=}\frac{1}{k}\left(1+o(1)\right)

where

only depends on

Proof.

We first have

\left|1-k\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k}}p_{\mathbf{i}}\right|=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{I}^{k}}p_{\mathbf{i}}-\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in Q_{k}}p_{\mathbf{i}}=\sum_{\underset{\text{power}}{\mathbf{i}\in\mathbb{I}^{k}}}p_{\mathbf{i}}\leq\sum_{\underset{d|k}{d\geq 2}}\sum_{i_{1},...,i^{k/d}\in\mathbb{I}}\left(p_{i_{1}}\dots p_{i_{k/d}}\right)^{d}\leq\sum_{\underset{d|k}{d\geq 2}}\left\lVert\mathbf{p}\right\rVert_{d}^{k}.

Recall now that the function that maps

is decreasing, since

\kappa_{r}(\widetilde{K}_{n})=\left(1+O\left(\left(\frac{R+1}{2}\right)^{\log(\log(n))}\right)\right)\sum_{k=1}^{n/\log(\log(n))}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k}}p_{\mathbf{i}}\\
+O\left(R^{n/2}\right)\sum_{m=2}^{r}S(r,m)\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}=1}^{n/\log(\log(n))}\sum_{\underset{\text{pairwise distinct}}{\mathbf{i}_{j}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k_{j}},j\leq m}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{p_{\mathbf{i}_{j}}}{k_{j}}\right).

From Lemma
6.8
, it follows that
\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k}}p_{\mathbf{i}}\underset{k\rightarrow+\infty}{\sim}\frac{1}{k}
, and then the first term is equivalent to
\log(n)
. In the other sums in
m
, we have the bound

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}=1}^{n/\log(\log(n))}\sum_{\underset{\text{pairwise distinct}}{\mathbf{i}_{j}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k_{j}},j\leq m}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{p_{\mathbf{i}_{j}}}{k_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}=1}^{n/\log(\log(n))}\sum_{\mathbf{i}_{j}\in\widetilde{Q}_{k_{j}},j\leq m}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{p_{\mathbf{i}_{j}}}{k_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\sim}\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}=1}^{n/\log(\log(n))}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{k_{j}^{2}}\right)=O(1)

and thus the right term of eq. (
7
) is a
O(R^{n/2})
, which is negligible compared to
\log(n)
. Finally, it holds

\kappa_{r}(\widetilde{K}_{n})\sim\log(n)

which proves the stated convergence, using Proposition
6.3
.

We now show the convergence of the moments of
\frac{K_{n}-\log(n)}{\sqrt{\log(n)}}
. Define

\delta_{n}=K_{n}-\widetilde{K}_{n},\qquad A_{n}=\frac{\widetilde{K}_{n}-\log(n)}{\sqrt{\log(n)}},\qquad B_{n}=\frac{\delta_{n}}{\sqrt{\log(n)}}.

Note that
\displaystyle\frac{K_{n}-\log(n)}{\sqrt{\log(n)}}=A_{n}+B_{n}
and
B_{n}\leq\dfrac{\log(\log(n))}{\log(n)}
a.s.
Fix
r\geq 2
which we suppose to be an even number. Then, note that for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R},(x+y)^{r}\leq 2^{r-1}(x^{r}+y^{r})
(since
x\mapsto x^{r}
is a convex function). Hence,

\mathbb{E}\left(\left(A_{n}+B_{n}\right)^{r}\right)\leq 2^{r-1}\left(\mathbb{E}(A_{n}^{r})+\mathbb{E}(B_{n}^{r})\right).

Since
B_{n}\leq\dfrac{\log(\log(n))}{\log(n)}
a.s., its
r
th moment converges to 0. Moreover, we proved above that
\mathbb{E}(A_{n}^{r})
converges, thus it is bounded. Therefore, we proved that the even (absolute) moments of
\displaystyle\frac{K_{n}-\log(n)}{\sqrt{\log(n)}}
are bounded, hence the convergence of all the moments follows from Proposition
6.6
.
