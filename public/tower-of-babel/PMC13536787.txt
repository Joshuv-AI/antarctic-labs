Outcomes of cannulated screw fixation for nondisplaced femoral neck fractures in patients older than 80 years: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Sara Wahab-Zuriarrain, Angelica Millan-Billi, Javier Arenas-Romera, Mireia Gómez-Masdeu, Ion Carrera, Álvaro Toro-Aguilera, Julio De Caso-Rodríguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe optimal management of nondisplaced femoral neck fractures in very elderly patients remains controversial. Although hip arthroplasty is frequently advocated, internal fixation with cannulated screws may represent a less invasive alternative in carefully selected patients.MethodsA retrospective single-centre cohort study was conducted including patients aged 80 years or older with nondisplaced femoral neck fractures treated with cannulated screw fixation between 2010 and 2019. Primary outcomes were fixation failure and conversion to hip arthroplasty. Secondary outcomes included reoperation rate, radiographic fracture collapse, ambulatory status, weight-bearing protocol, and radiographic measurements related to screw positioning.ResultsA total of 170 patients were included, with a mean age of 86.9 years. Fixation failure occurred in 17 patients (10%). Reoperation for any cause was required in 15 patients (8.8%), and conversion to hip hemiarthroplasty was performed in 6 patients (3.5%). Radiographic fracture collapse was observed in 46 patients (30%), although only 7 patients (15.2%) required reoperation. Immediate full weight-bearing was not associated with increased fixation failure or fracture collapse and was associated with improved ambulatory status at 6 months. Greater femoral head occupancy on lateral radiographs was associated with fracture collapse, whereas tip–apex distance was not.ConclusionsIn carefully selected patients older than 80 years with nondisplaced femoral neck fractures, cannulated screw fixation was associated with low rates of fixation failure and conversion to hip arthroplasty. Immediate full weight-bearing did not increase complication rates and was associated with improved early mobility, supporting a less invasive treatment strategy in this super-aged population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13018-026-07025-6.

Introduction

Femoral neck fractures account for approximately 40 to 59% of all hip fractures and predominantly affect the elderly population. Their incidence is particularly high among super-aged patients older than 80 years, who represent up to 62% of cases. These injuries constitute a major public health concern due to their association with increased morbidity, mortality, and substantial socioeconomic burden on healthcare systems, especially in frail patients with multiple comorbidities and limited physiological reserve [1–5].

Hip arthroplasty is widely accepted as the treatment of choice for displaced femoral neck fractures in elderly patients. However, the optimal management of nondisplaced or minimally displaced fractures (Garden Classification [6] types I and II) remains controversial, particularly in very elderly individuals. Nondisplaced femoral neck fractures account for approximately approximately 11% to 16% of intracapsular fractures and are characterized by relative preservation of femoral head vascularity, making internal fixation a biologically reasonable treatment option [7].

Although internal fixation had traditionally been favored in younger and more active patients [8–10], its role in the very elderly population has been questioned due to reported complication rates, including avascular necrosis (10–45%), implant failure (8–19%), secondary fracture displacement, nonunion (10–30%), persistent pain, and inferior functional outcomes; frequently leading to reoperation and increased morbidity [7, 11, 12]. Nevertheless, these risks must be interpreted in the context of patient age, functional demand, life expectancy and perioperative risk. In super-aged patients, hip arthroplasty is associated with longer operative time, greater blood loss, increased physiological stress, and a higher incidence of perioperative medical complications, including cardiovascular events, infection, and postoperative delirium.

Fixation with CS offers several potential advantages that may be particularly relevant in this vulnerable population. This minimally invasive technique is associated with shorter operative time, reduced intraoperative blood loss, and lower surgical stress, while preserving the native hip joint. Preservation of the femoral head may also reduce the risk of prosthesis related complications such as dislocation, periprosthetic fracture, and deep infection, which can be especially devastating in patients with limited functional reserve. Importantly, available evidence does not consistently demonstrate superior functional outcomes or survival after hip arthroplasty compared with CS fixation for nondisplaced femoral neck fractures in elderly patients [12–15].

Given the growing number of super-aged patients sustaining femoral neck fractures, a better understanding of failure rates associated with less invasive fixation strategies is essential to guide treatment decisions The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the rate of fixation failure and subsequent conversion to hip arthroplasty following treatment with three cannulated screws in patients older than 80 years with nondisplaced or minimally displaced femoral neck fracture. Secondary objectives included the assessment of postoperative ambulatory status compared with prefracture walking ability, evaluation of the influence of postoperative weight-bearing protocols on fixation failure, and analysis of radiographic parameters on anteroposterior and lateral views to identify factors associated with radiographic fracture collapse.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective single-center cohort study included consecutive patients aged 80 years or older with nondisplaced femoral neck fractures treated at Level I major trauma centre between January 2010 and December 2019. Patients were identified using hospital admission records and operative logs. Demographic, clinical, surgical, and radiographic data were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records and an institutional research database (Table 1).

Preoperative fracture classification was initially performed using standard anteroposterior (AP) and lateral radiographs of the pelvis obtained at hospital admission. Fractures were classified according to Garden classification [6]. When clinical suspicion persisted despite inconclusive plain radiographs, computed tomography (CT) was obtained to confirm diagnosis.

Given that emergency department radiographs may not always provide reliable projections for assessing fracture alignment, definitive evaluation of posterior tilt and varus angulation was performed intraoperatively under fluoroscopic guidance with the patient positioned on a traction table. Posterior tilt was measured on lateral views according to the method described by Palm et al., and varus angulation was assessed on AP views relative to the femoral neck axis [16].

Inclusion criteria were: age older than 80 years, acute nondisplaced femoral neck fracture Garden type I and II (AO/OTA 31.B1.1 and 31B1.2 [17]); absence of significant varus angulation or posterior tilt greater than 20º, treatment with internal fixation using CS, and a minimum clinical follow-up of 12 months or until death.

In cases where intraoperative fluoroscopic assessment demonstrated posterior tilt greater than 20º or unacceptable varus alignment after attempted closed reduction, internal fixation was not performed and conversion to hemiarthroplasty was undertaken. In our cohort, 4 patients were converted intraoperatively based on these criteria.

Patients were excluded if they had a pre-existing ipsilateral femoral implant, pathological fracture, nonoperative management, internal fixation techniques other than CS, active hip infection or sepsis, inability to comply with postoperative follow-up. Surgical technique

All procedures were performed by either a consultant orthopaedic trauma surgeon or a senior orthopaedic registrar under consultant supervision. Patients were positioned supine on a traction table. In cases with minimal varus alignment or posterior tilt, gentle traction was applied to achieve closed reduction under fluoroscopic guidance.

Fixation was performed using three partially threaded 6.5 mm cannulated cancellous screws (TiMax® titanium screws, Zimmer Biomet; or ASNIS II stainless steel screws, Stryker). One screw was placed inferiorly along the femoral calcar and centrally on the lateral view, while the remaining two screws were positioned superiorly and parallel to the first on the AP view, with one anterior and one posterior on the lateral view, forming an inverted triangle configuration (Fig. 2).Fig. 2A Postoperative anteroposterior radiograph showing a nondisplaced femoral neck fracture fixed with three cannulated cancellous screws arranged in an inverted triangle configuration. Postoperative management

Postoperative weight-bearing recommendations were determined by operating surgeon based on bone quality, implant stability, and immediate postoperative radiographic appearance, independent of prefracture ambulatory status. Early mobilization was encouraged, beginning with bed exercises and sitting within the first 48 h, followed by standing and ambulation with assistive devices under physiotherapist supervision.

Patients were typically discharged from the orthopaedic ward within five days postoperatively in the absence of medical complications, either to home with community physiotherapy support or to a rehabilitation or nursing facility. Postoperative blood transfusion requirements were recorded.

Follow-up and outcome measures

Patients were routinely reviewed at six weeks, three months, six months and twelve months postoperatively for clinical evaluation, radiographic assessment, and ambulatory status documentation.

Follow-up completeness was assessed for both clinical and radiographic outcomes. Clinical outcomes, including ambulatory status, were available for all patients (n = 170). In cases with incomplete follow-up at specific time points, the last available ambulatory status was used for analysis. For patients who died during follow-up, the last recorded ambulatory status prior to death was included.

Radiographic outcomes required complete imaging follow-up. Patients without sufficient serial radiographic follow-up to reliably assess fracture healing and late complications were excluded from radiographic analyses but retained in clinical outcome analyses.

Overall survival was calculated from the date of surgery to the date of death or last documented clinical follow-up. Patients who remained alive were censored at the date of last contact. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method.

Primary outcome

The primary outcomes of this study were fixation failure and conversion to hip arthroplasty.

Fixation failure was defined as a composite clinical outcome including any of the following events: secondary fracture displacement, implant-related mechanical failure (screw cut-out or migration), nonunion, or symptomatic fracture site collapse. Each patient was counted only once in the composite outcome and classified according to the main clinical event leading to failure.

Secondary fracture displacement was defined as a change in fracture alignment greater than 5 mm or varus angulation greater than 10° on follow-up radiographs.

Nonunion was defined as the absence of radiographic signs of healing at six months postoperatively, associated with persistent pain or functional limitation.

Symptomatic fracture site collapse was defined as radiographic collapse at the fracture level (e.g., varus angulation, shortening, or impaction) associated with clinical symptoms.

Conversion to hip arthroplasty was analysed as a separate primary endpoint due to its clinical relevance in this elderly population.

Secondary outcome

Secondary outcomes included postoperative ambulatory status, recorded prefracture and at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively, and categorized as independent ambulation, ambulation with sticks, ambulation with a walker or frame, or wheelchair-bound/bedridden.

For ambulatory status, in cases where follow-up data at a specific time point were unavailable, the last available recorded ambulatory status was carried forward for analysis.

The association between postoperative weight-bearing protocol and fixation failure was also analysed.

Radiographic fracture site collapse was recorded as an independent radiological outcome, regardless of symptoms or need for reoperation.

Avascular necrosis (AVN) was defined as a distinct radiographic entity involving the femoral head, based on findings such as subchondral collapse, crescent sign, or progressive sclerosis, and was distinguished from fracture site collapse.

Other postoperative complications were recorded, including painful hardware, refracture, infection, ischiofemoral impingement, and postoperative bleeding. These were not considered fixation failures unless associated with mechanical or biological failure requiring reoperation.

Radiographic analysis

Postoperative anteroposterior (AP) and lateral radiographs of the hip were retrospectively reviewed using a Picture Archiving and Communication System to assess implant positioning. Screw placement was evaluated using two radiographic parameters, as illustrated in Fig. 3.Fig. 3Representative postoperative anteroposterior A and lateral B radiographs illustrating the measurements used for radiographic analysis. Femoral head diameter occupancy is shown in blue, and tip–apex distance measurements are shown in red.

Representative postoperative anteroposterior A and lateral B radiographs illustrating the measurements used for radiographic analysis. Femoral head diameter occupancy is shown in blue, and tip–apex distance measurements are shown in red.

First, the percentage of femoral head diameter occupied by the screws was calculated on both AP and lateral views by dividing the distance between the most widely separated screws by the maximum diameter of the femoral head. Second, the tip–apex distance (TAD) was measured as the distance from the tip of each screw to the apex of the femoral head on both AP and lateral radiographs. The known diameter of the cannulated screws was used to calibrate all radiographic measurements and correct for magnification.

Radiographic parameters were analysed in relation to radiographic fracture collapse and fixation failure.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparison of continuous variables among multiple groups. Continuous variables with normal distribution were compared using unpaired Student’s t tests, while the Mann–Whitney U test was used for nonparametric data. Categorical variables were compared using the X test.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association between postoperative weight-bearing status and major outcomes, adjusting for age, sex, body mass index, comorbidities, prefracture ambulatory status, ASA classification, and fracture type. Results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Prefracture ambulatory status did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.137); however, a trend toward a higher proportion of functionally dependent patients in the restricted weight-bearing groups was observed, suggesting potential confounding by indication.

According to the Garden classification, 115 fractures (67.6%) were classified as Garden type I and 55 (32.4%) as Garden type II. The mean time from admission to surgery was 3.5 ± 2.2 days (range, 0–14 days). Fifty-nine patients underwent surgery within 48 h of admission, whereas surgical delay occurred in 62 patients, primarily due to anticoagulant treatment or medical comorbidities. The mean length of hospital stay was 7.5 days (range, 2 – 28 days). During hospitalization, 26 patients (14.4%) required blood transfusion; this was not associated with preoperative oral anticoagulant use (p = 0.521).

Primary outcome measure

Fixation failure occurred in 17 patients (10%). Conversion to hip hemiarthroplasty was required in 6 patients, resulting in a conversion rate of 3.5%. Reoperation for any cause was performed in 15 patients (8.8%).

Fixation failure was driven by the following clinical events: mechanical failure (n = 4; 2.3%), nonunion (n = 2; 1.2%), and symptomatic fracture site collapse (n = 2; 1.2%). Each patient was classified according to the main clinical event leading to failure. No cases of secondary fracture displacement or avascular necrosis requiring surgical treatment were observed during the follow-up period.

Not all cases of fixation failure required surgical reintervention, as some patients remained clinically stable despite radiographic or mechanical findings.

Among reoperations, conversion to hip hemiarthroplasty was the most frequent procedure (n = 6; 3.5%), followed by hardware removal due to symptomatic hardware intolerance (n = 5; 2.9%), revision fixation (n = 2; 1.2%), minor surgical procedures (n = 1; 0.6%), and one non-surgical intervention (embolization) for postoperative bleeding (n = 1; 0.6%).

Other complications not classified as fixation failure included painful hardware (n = 5; 2.9%), distal femoral fracture (n = 2; 1.2%), ischiofemoral impingement (n = 1; 0.6%), and postoperative bleeding (n = 1; 0.6%). Radiographic outcomes

Radiographic measurements were available postoperatively for all patients; however, complete radiographic follow-up sufficient to assess fracture healing and late complications was available in 153 patients (90%). Seventeen patients did not have complete radiographic follow-up due to death or inability to attend follow-up visits, mainly related to medical comorbidities and limited mobility.

All radiographic outcomes were therefore analysed using the subgroup of patients with complete imaging follow-up (n = 153), whereas clinical outcomes were analysed in the full cohort (n = 170). Denominators are specified accordingly throughout the results.

During the follow-up period, overall mortality was 84.7% (n = 144), with 11.2% (n = 19) occurring within the first 12 months after surgery. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed a survival probability of 88.8% at 12 months. The median survival time was 1445 days (95% CI 1094–1796). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis is shown in Fig. 4.

The mean time to radiographic fracture consolidation was 5.3 ± 2.1 months. Delayed union was observed in 23 patients. Radiographic nonunion occurred in 6 of 153 patients (3.9%). Of these, only 2 cases were clinically relevant and classified as fixation failure. Among patients who developed nonunion, 80% had previously demonstrated radiographic fracture site collapse. This association was confirmed in logistic regression analysis (OR 12.57, 95% CI 1.36 – 116.22; p = 0.026).

Radiographic fracture site collapse was identified in 46 patients (46/153, 30%). In most cases, this finding was not associated with clinical symptoms. However, 7 patients with radiographic collapse (7/46, 15.2%) required reoperation, including 2 cases converted to hip hemiarthroplasty. The presence of radiographic fracture site collapse was significantly associated with reoperation (OR 9.29, 95% CI 2.32 – 37.14; p < 0.001).

No significant association was observed between fracture type (Garden I vs II) and radiographic fracture collapse (p = 0.235) or conversion to hip arthroplasty (p = 0.181).

Analysis of implant positioning showed no significant association between TAD and radiographic outcomes. The mean TAD on anteroposterior radiographs was 9.8 mm in consolidated fractures and 9.3 mm in nonunion cases (p = 0.81), while on lateral radiographs it was 8.7 mm and 9.5 mm, respectively (p = 0.74).

In contrast, a significant positive association was observed between the percentage of femoral head diameter occupied by the screws and the occurrence of fracture site collapse on lateral radiographs (p = 0.01).

Immediate FWB was not significantly associated with an increased risk of radiographic fracture site collapse (OR 0.98, 95% CI 0.39–2.45; p = 0.994). Radiographic fracture site collapse was not significantly associated with ambulatory status at final follow-up (p = 0.888).

Ambulatory status

At final follow-up, 117 patients (70%) had recovered their prefracture ambulatory status. A decline in ambulatory capacity was more frequently observed in patients who had been independently ambulatory prior to injury.

Postoperatively, 119 patients (70%) were assigned to the FWB group, 18 patients (10.6%) to the PWB group, and 33 patients (19.4%) to NWB group. Patients managed with immediate FWB showed a higher proportion of independent ambulation at 6 months in unadjusted analysis. However, this association was not confirmed in logistic regression analysis (OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.37–2.54; p = 0.636).

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association between postoperative weight-bearing status and major outcomes, adjusting for age, sex, body mass index, comorbidities, prefracture ambulatory status, ASA classification, and fracture type.

After adjustment, weight-bearing status was not significantly associated with complications, radiographic fracture site collapse, reoperation, or conversion to hip arthroplasty. In contrast, female sex was independently associated with a higher likelihood of complications (OR 0.35 for males; 95% CI 0.16–0.77; p = 0.009), radiographic fracture site collapse (OR 2.21; 95% CI 1.06–4.62; p = 0.035), reoperation (OR 3.92; 95% CI 1.33–11.56; p = 0.013), and conversion to hip arthroplasty (OR 16.58; 95% CI 1.88–146.29; p = 0.011).

No significant associations were observed for age, comorbidities, ASA classification, or fracture type across the evaluated outcomes.

Confidence intervals were wide for reoperation and arthroplasty conversion, reflecting the limited number of events and reduced statistical precision.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, fixation of nondisplaced femoral neck fractures with CS in super-aged patients older than 80 years showed a low rate of reoperation (8.8%) and conversion to hip hemiarthroplasty (3.5%). Hardware removal accounted for 2.9% of cases, whereas repeat internal fixation was required in only 1.2% of patients. These findings indicate that, in carefully selected very elderly patients, cannulated screw fixation can achieve acceptable clinical outcomes with a limited need for revision surgery.

The reoperation rate observed in our series is lower than that reported in several previous studies, in which reoperation rates after internal fixation for nondisplaced femoral neck fractures ranged from 13.3% and 30.1% [11, 12, 15, 18–21]. Importantly, in many of these reports, the most frequent indication for reoperation was removal of symptomatic hardware (33.9%), a relatively minor surgical procedure associated with lower surgical morbidity, blood loss, and recovery time compared with conversion to arthroplasty [15]. In contrast, reported rates of conversion to hip arthroplasty following internal fixation range from 8.3 to 16% [18, 22–24]. These higher conversion rates may be partially explained by differences in patient populations, as many studies included younger elderly patients, often starting from 60 years of age, with greater life expectancy and higher functional demands than those in our cohort.

The high overall mortality observed in this cohort (84.7%), with 11.2% at 12 months, reflects the frailty of this very elderly population. This may partially explain the limited availability of complete radiographic follow-up in some patients and should be considered when interpreting long-term outcomes.

Previous investigations have suggested that younger patients within the elderly population are at higher risk of revision surgery after internal fixation. Giertsen et al [8] and Zhu et al [25] reported increased revision rates in the younger subset of elderly patients, while Lagergren et al [24] demonstrated that the risk of conversion to arthroplasty was highest in women and in patients aged 70 to 79 years. These findings support the concept that age and functional demand play a relevant role in fixation failure and may help explain the favourable conversion rate observed in our cohort.

Another factor that may contribute to differences among studies is the definition of “nondisplaced” femoral neck fracture. Traditionally, this classification has relied primarily on the Garden classification system, which assesses displacement on anteroposterior radiographs. However, increasing evidence highlights the importance of sagittal plane alignment [13, 26]. Sjöholm et al [20] demonstrated that an anterior tilt greater than 10° or a posterior tilt greater than 20° is associated with a significantly increased risk of fixation failure and subsequent reoperation. In our institutional protocol, intraoperative fluoroscopic assessment is routinely performed, and if unacceptable displacement or malalignment is identified, the surgical plan is modified and hip hemiarthroplasty is performed. This stricter selection criterion may have contributed to the relatively low fixation failure and conversion rates observed in our study.

Several comparative studies have reported lower early reoperation rates and improved short-term functional outcomes after hip hemiarthroplasty compared with internal fixation for nondisplaced femoral neck fractures [9–12]. However, these potential benefits must be balanced against the increased surgical aggressiveness of arthroplasty. Hip hemiarthroplasty has consistently been associated with longer operative time, greater blood loss, larger surgical exposure, higher transfusion requirements, and longer hospital stays [11]. In our series, 14.4% of patients required postoperative blood transfusion, which is substantially lower than the transfusion rates reported in hemiarthroplasty cohorts, where up to 48% of patients have required transfusion [11, 12]. These differences are particularly relevant in very elderly patients with multiple comorbidities, in whom minimizing surgical stress may reduce medical complications and early mortality.

Radiographic measurements related to screw positioning provided additional insight into the mechanisms underlying fixation failure. In our cohort, a greater percentage of femoral head diameter occupancy was significantly associated with fracture collapse on lateral radiographs, whereas tip–apex distance was not associated with collapse or nonunion. These findings suggest that screw distribution and spatial configuration within the femoral head may be more relevant than absolute screw depth in determining construct stability in nondisplaced femoral neck fractures.

Similar concepts have been explored by Hong et al. [27], who reported that the ratio between inter-screw distance and femoral neck width on lateral radiographs was a reliable predictor of femoral head necrosis following internal fixation. Although osteonecrosis was not a primary outcome in our study, both investigations highlight the potential biomechanical importance of screw spacing and femoral head occupancy, particularly in the sagittal plane. Together, these observations support the clinical relevance of radiographic assessment of screw distribution beyond traditional parameters.

There is limited evidence regarding optimal postoperative weight-bearing protocols following cannulated screw fixation in elderly patients. Many authors recommend restricted or partial weight-bearing for at least six weeks [19], which may partly explain the superior short-term functional outcomes reported after hemiarthroplasty due to earlier unrestricted ambulation [11, 12]. In unadjusted analyses, patients allowed immediate full weight-bearing demonstrated better ambulatory status at six months. However, this association was not confirmed after multivariable adjustment, and weight-bearing status was not independently associated with clinical or radiographic outcomes. Overall, 70% of patients recovered their prefracture ambulatory status. These findings are consistent with those reported by Kim et al [28], who observed lower mortality, reduced reoperation rates, and satisfactory functional recovery following early full weight-bearing after fixation of nondisplaced femoral neck fractures.

In this study, postoperative weight-bearing protocols were determined by the operating surgeon, which may introduce confounding by indication. Although baseline characteristics were largely comparable between groups, a non-significant trend toward greater functional dependence in patients managed with restricted weight-bearing was observed, suggesting that clinical decision-making may have been influenced by patient frailty.

After adjustment for relevant clinical variables, weight-bearing status was not significantly associated with complications, radiographic fracture site collapse, reoperation, or conversion to hip arthroplasty. In contrast, female sex emerged as an independent factor associated with worse outcomes across several endpoints. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as the relatively low number of events resulted in wide confidence intervals, particularly for reoperation and arthroplasty conversion.

Therefore, the results of this study should be interpreted as reflecting associations rather than causal effects, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Future prospective or randomized studies are needed to better establish causal relationships between weight-bearing strategies and outcomes in this population.

From a clinical perspective, treatment of nondisplaced femoral neck fractures in very elderly patients should prioritize minimally invasive surgery, reduced blood loss, short hospital stays, and early mobilization to avoid the complications associated with prolonged bed rest, including thromboembolism, pneumonia, functional decline, and increased mortality. Given the limited life expectancy of patients older than 80 years, the risk of long-term complications may be lower, further supporting the use of less aggressive surgical strategies when appropriate. In this context, our findings suggest that cannulated screw fixation represents a reasonable treatment option in carefully selected super-aged patients.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design is inherently associated with selection bias and unmeasured confounding. No a priori sample size calculation was performed, particularly for secondary outcomes, which limits the ability to detect smaller associations. The study may therefore be underpowered to detect small or moderate differences between groups, particularly given the imbalance in group sizes and the relatively low number of events. Accordingly, non-significant findings should be interpreted with caution and should not be considered evidence of equivalence. Although minimum follow-up was 12 months, longer-term complications such as avascular necrosis may have been underestimated, especially in patients with limited survival. Surgical procedures were performed by multiple surgeons, introducing potential performance bias. In addition, postoperative weight-bearing recommendations were based on surgeon preference, and adherence to these instructions could not be reliably verified, potentially influencing both radiographic and functional outcomes.

Despite these limitations, the study’s strengths include a well-defined cohort of super-aged patients, strict radiographic selection criteria, and clinically meaningful primary outcomes. These results add to the growing body of evidence supporting individualized, less invasive treatment strategies for nondisplaced femoral neck fractures in very elderly patients.

Conclusion

In patients older than 80 years with nondisplaced femoral neck fractures, cannulated screw fixation was associated with low rates of reoperation and conversion to hip hemiarthroplasty. Immediate full weight-bearing did not increase fixation failure or fracture collapse and was associated with improved early ambulatory recovery. Cannulated screw fixation represents a safe and viable treatment option for carefully selected very elderly, allowing early mobilization without increasing complication rates.
