Direct Light Storage
with Controlled Dark Release
in a Bipyridine-Based Covalent Triazine Framework

Xue Wang, Bibhuti Bhusan Rath, Nicolai Schröter, Kristina Gjorgjevikj, Rohit Mann, Shun Tokuda, Yang Wang, Viola Duppel, Lorenzo Tesi, Simon Krause, Bettina V Lotsch

Abstract

Utilizing bifunctional
materials capable of direct light-to-charge
storage offers an attractive strategy to overcome solar intermittency
and enable reliable power supply under dark conditions. Reported bifunctional
materials are predominantly based on metal oxides and metal–organic
compounds, whereas fully organic systems remain rare despite their
rich structural diversity and tunability, which enable systematic
structure–property–function investigations. Here, we
reveal the light-induced, reversible electron storage properties of
a two-dimensional (2D) 2,2′-bipyridine (bpy)-based crystalline
covalent triazine framework (CTF), CTF-Bpy. Mechanistic studies suggest
that the photoreduction of CTF-Bpy primarily proceeds through a proton-coupled,
one-electron reduction of the bpy units, yielding bpyH• and bpyH2
•+ as the identified reduction
products. Photoanodes based on CTF-Bpy show a stable photoinduced
open-circuit potential (photo-OCP) of −0.92 V vs Ag/AgCl, which
is so far the most negative potential reported for an organic bifunctional
material. Under oxygen-free conditions, photogenerated electrons remain
stable and can be controllably released on-demand in the dark, either
by electrical discharge (for aqueous solar battery applications) or
by reducing O2 to H2O2. Moreover,
this work establishes CTF-Bpy as the first photochargeable covalent
organic framework (COF) with stored electrons sufficiently reducing
to drive H2 evolution in the dark in the presence of a
Pt cocatalyst, highlighting the promise of using COFs for "dark
photocatalysis".
The systematic investigation of the light-induced electron storage
in CTF-Bpy with a set of spectroscopic and (spectro-)­electrochemical
techniques provides a framework for studying optoionic materials and
guiding their rational design for tailored dark photocatalysis.

Introduction

To mitigate our over-reliance
on fossil fuels, and facilitate sustainable
energy solutions, converting solar energy into electricity and chemical
fuels via photovoltaics and artificial photosynthesis are promising
strategies. Due to the intermittent nature of solar irradiation, solar-derived
electricity or fuels typically require additional storage systems
to ensure a reliable 24 h energy supply. For example, photovoltaics is usually integrated with batteries
as two independent operating parts by electric wires, termed as photovoltaics-battery
(PV-battery) systems. Such configurations
are often bulky, inflexible, and prone to energy losses through external
wiring. In artificial photosynthesis, the produced chemical fuels,
such as H2, need to be stored separately; however, limitations
in current hydrogen-storage technologies remain a major barrier to
adopting a large-scale hydrogen economy. Alternatively, recent advances demonstrate that direct solar-to-electrochemical
storage (i.e., in a solar battery)
−

 and on-demand H2 production in the dark
,
 are possible by using
bifunctional materials capable of simultaneous light harvesting and
charge storage. Utilization of such materials can simplify wired PV-battery
devices to minimize energy losses, and alleviates challenges associated
with H2 storage.

As compared to conventional photovoltaic
materials and photocatalysts,
the key attribute of such bifunctional materials is their ability
to stably trap charges after illumination ceases for minutes or even
hours. Depending on the type of charge stored, charge-storage materials
can be classified as hole-storage or electron-storage
materials, and here, we focus on electron-storage
materials. In a typical colloidal electron storage system, the presence
of a sacrificial electron donor (SED) is generally required to scavenge
photogenerated holes and enable the accumulation of photogenerated
electrons within the system. To date,
reported materials capable of electron storage under irradiation are
largely restricted to inorganic metal oxides and metal–organic
compounds. In metal oxides such as TiO2,
,
 SnO2, MoO3, WO3,
,
 V2O5
 and their derivatives,
,
 electron storage relies on the intrinsic transition of metal redox
activity. In addition, for metal–organic
complexes
−

 and metal–organic frameworks (MOFs),
−

 electron storage can further originate from a cooperative effect
between the metal and organic ligand. More recently, zinc indium sulfide
nanocrystals and a hybrid system based
on photoactive Ru­(II) tris­(bipyridine) complexes have also been reported to store electrons under light.
Poly­(heptazine imide) (PHI)-type 2D ionic carbon nitrides represent
a prototypical carbon-based system for light-induced electron storage,
where electron stabilization is achieved via cation intercalation
(e.g., K+ in K-PHI).
,
 So far, these PHI-type
2D carbon nitrides have been used as photoanode in aqueous solar battery
applications and as photocatalyst for
time-delayed H2 and H2O2 production
in the dark.
,,
 While PHI-type 2D carbon nitrides represent a benchmark material
for light-induced electron storage and dark photocatalysis, particularly
in terms of stability, scalability and catalytic performance, their
inertness and the resulting limited chemical functionality severely
restricts their structural diversity to achieve tunable optoelectronic
and charge storage properties.

Compared to PHI-type 2D ionic
carbon nitrides, covalent organic
frameworks (COFs) offer molecular-level tunability with rich structural
diversity, making them promising candidates
for next-generation electron storage materials. For example, COFs
embedded with redox-active naphthalenediimide (NDI)
,
 and perylenetetracarboxylic diimide (PTCDI) have enabled direct solar-to-electrochemical energy storage, and
a dihydroxybenzene containing COF integrated with BiVO4 has been employed to decouple artificial photosynthesis through
photoelectrochemical energy storage. However,
to date, no COF has been reported mirroring PHI-type 2D carbon nitride
functioning both as photoanode for solar batteries and as a catalyst
for dark photocatalysis, including H2 production. A key
challenge in driving H2 evolution in the dark is that the
photoinduced open-circuit potential (photo-OCP) of the electron-storage
material needs to be more negative than the potential of the H+/H2 redox couple (−0.41 V vs NHE, at pH
7 and 25 °C). For instance, among
the reported electron storage materials, (TBA)+(NbWO6)−, Ti-MOF and K-PHI
,
 with photo-OCP of ca.
−0.50 V, −0.80 V and −0.60 V (all vs NHE) can
drive dark H2 evolution, whereas NDI-COF (−0.45
V vs NHE) cannot, likely because the
small redox potential difference (ΔE ≈ 0.04 V) is insufficient
to drive the H+/H2 reaction. Aside from this,
a more negative photo-OCP is also desired for photoanodes to deliver
sufficient charging voltages for practical solar battery applications. Note that, in this work, we tentatively use the
photo-OCP value as an approximate measure of the reducing power of
the photochargeable electron-storage materials, while acknowledging
that the measured OCP can be influenced by multiple factors beyond
the intrinsic redox potential of the material, such as the FTO substrate,
solvent properties, and recombination processes.

In this work, we selected 2D covalent triazine frameworks
(CTFs)a
subclass of COFs characterized by the triazine linkage and high chemical/thermal
stabilityto study their light-induced
electron storage properties. The nitrogen-rich, conjugated backbone
of CTFs enhances light absorption, thus
making 2D CTFs attractive candidates for time-delayed photocatalysis
in the dark. To impart the required redox-activity to the framework,
we incorporate 2,2′-bipyridine as a linker. Although 2,2′-bipyridine
is a well-established two-electron redox unit, prior studies have largely focused on its incorporation
into metal–organic systems.
,,
 The fact that molecular 2,2′-bipyridine itself
can undergo photoreduction in the presence of SEDs has remained largely
overlooked in this field.
,
 Moreover, literature
reports indicate that the one-electron reduced 2,2′-bipyridine
is more reducing than the two-electron reduced NDI, highlighting the potential of 2,2′-bipyridine-embedded
CTFs to extend solar battery charging voltages and possibly drive
dark photocatalysis (i.e., H2 evolution).

Based on
the above considerations, in this work, we investigate
a known 2,2′-bipyridine-based 2D CTFCTF-Bpyto
assess its ability for light-induced electron storage and controlled
dark release. We show that, under illumination, CTF-Bpy acts as an
electron reservoir in water in the presence of 4-methylbenzyl alcohol
(4-MBA) as SED. The photoinduced electron accumulation is reversible,
with air/O2 exposure quenching the stored electrons. Using
a suite of spectroscopic and spectro-electrochemical methods, we elucidate
that electron storage in CTF-Bpy arises from one-electron reduction
of the 2,2′-bipyridine (bpy) units via a proton-coupled process,
with bpyH• and bpyH2
•+ identified as the reduced species. Photoelectrochemical measurements
of the CTF-Bpy photoanode show a photo-OCP of −0.92 V vs Ag/AgCl
(−0.72 V vs NHE), which is more negative than the reported
value of K-PHI (−0.60 V vs NHE). The stored electrons remain
stable within the framework, allowing their controlled release in
the dark as electrical energy for aqueous solar battery applications,
or alternatively, to drive oxygen reduction to form H2O2 and proton reduction for the H2 evolution reaction
(the latter with a Pt cocatalyst) in the dark. This represents the
first example of bifunctional COFs where the stored electrons are
reductive enough to drive H2 evolution in the dark. The
systematic study of light-induced electron storage in CTF-Bpy lays
out a general protocol for the identification and rational design
of organic optoionic materials for direct solar energy conversion
and storage.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization

CTF-Bpy
was synthesized following a literature procedure by reacting 2,2′-bipyridine-5,5′-dicarboximidamide
dihydrochloride with 2,2′-bipyridine-5,5′-dicarbaldehyde
under solvothermal conditions to afford CTF-Bpy as fluffy yellow powder
(a, see Supporting Information for details).
,
 PXRD analysis of CTF-Bpy reveals
a pattern consistent with previous reports, suggesting a 2D hcb topology with AA stacking mode. The reflections at 4.7°, 8.4°, 9.8° and 26.4°
can be assigned to the {100}, {21̅0}, {200} and {001} planes,
respectively. Pawley refinement confirms a hexagonal lattice with
space group P6/m and unit cell parameters a = b = 21.944(9) Å and c = 3.537(8) Å (b). The Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrum of CTF-Bpy
exhibits the characteristic vibrations of the triazine unit at 1504
and 1335 cm–1, confirming the formation of triazine
linkages. The disappearance of the amino and aldehyde absorption indicates
near-complete reaction of the two precursors (Figure S3). The solid-state 1H magic-angle spinning
(MAS) NMR spectrum of CTF-Bpy shows a sharp signal at 8.2 ppm, corresponding
to the hydrogen atoms on the bipyridine units (Figure S4). In the 13C cross-polarization magic-angle
spinning (CP MAS) NMR spectrum, the signal at 169.7 ppm represents
the carbon atoms from the triazine ring. The signals at 157.5, 149.9,
135.3, 130.3, and 120.8 ppm can be assigned to the carbon atoms from
the bipyridine units (Figure S5). N2 sorption measurement of CTF-Bpy at 77 K yields a total pore
volume of 0.39 cm3 g–1 at P/P
0 = 0.97 and a Brunauer–Emmett–Teller
(BET) surface area of 794 m2 g–1. Fitting
the quenched solid density functional theory (QSDFT) model to the
N2 adsorption isotherm derives a pore size distribution
centered at 1.50 nm, in good agreement with the reported value (c, Figure S6). Scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) images of CTF-Bpy reveal a morphology of aggregated
particles (Figure S7). Transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) images of CTF-Bpy exhibit crystalline domains, and
the corresponding selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern
displays a lattice spacing of 1.80 nm, indexed to the {100} plane
of the CTF-Bpy structural model (Figure S8). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) reveals high thermal stability
of CTF-Bpy, with no significant mass loss observed up to 550 °C
under Ar (Figure S9).

Reversible Light-Induced Radical Formation

According to the literature, 2,2′-bipyridine (bpy) undergoes
two consecutive one-electron reductions, yielding the radical monoanion
(bpy•–) and the dianion (bpy2–), respectively.
,
 Because the two reduced states
of molecular bpy exhibit a distinct fingerprint absorption spectrum
in the visible and near-infrared (NIR) region,
,,
 we first probed the photoreduction properties
of CTF-Bpy using ultraviolet–visible-NIR (UV–vis–NIR)
spectroscopy.

The photoreduction of CTF-Bpy was explored in
an O2-free aqueous suspension in the presence of 20 mM
4-MBA as SED. The UV–vis–NIR spectrum of the CTF-Bpy
suspension exhibits an intense absorption band between 280–400
nm, corresponding to the characteristic π–π* transitions
of the triazine and pyridine rings.
−

 The optical bandgap
of CTF-Bpy is calculated to be 2.3 eV (Figure S10). Upon 365 nm UV illumination (nominal power P

nominal
 ≈ 237 mW cm–2), the suspension shows a rapid color change from yellow to black
in 1 min, accompanied by the evolution of new, broad absorption bands
in the UV–vis–NIR spectrum between 430–1370 nm.
This new absorption closely resembles that of bpy•–, as both exhibit absorption in the visible and NIR region
,,
 and can be attributed to the
singly occupied molecular orbital (SOMO)-involved low-energy transitions
of bpy•–, which are responsible for the observed
yellow-to-black color change of the suspension (a).
,
 The new band is, however, more
intense and red-shifted relative to molecular bpy•–, likely related to the extended π-conjugated framework of
CTF-Bpy. The possible coexistence of bpy2– cannot
be excluded on the basis of the UV–vis–NIR spectrum
alone owing to potential spectral overlap, as its absorption is expected
to appear at shorter wavelength as compared to bpy•–.
,
 Under continuous illumination, the new absorption
band reaches saturation after 10 min (b). In addition, the signal at 290 nm increases
slightly, which is attributed to p-tolualdehyde,
the oxidation product of 4-MBA, as confirmed by control experiments
(Figure S11). Upon air/O2 exposure,
the photoreduced CTF-Bpy is reoxidized, evident from the disappearance
of the absorption bands between 430–1370 nm, ultimately yielding
the original spectrum and the yellow color (Figure S12). The reoxidation by air/O2 is accompanied by
H2O2 formation (Figure S13), which will be discussed further below in more detail. The photoreduction
and subsequent oxidation by air/O2 can be repeated over
multiple cycles, indicating the reversibility of photoreduction and
reusability of CTF-Bpy for photocharging and subsequent charge release
(b, inset).

In addition to 4-MBA, 20 mM triethanolamine (TEOA)
and methanol
(MeOH) were also tested as SED for CTF-Bpy photoreduction in H2O. On comparing the color change and the intensity of the
new absorption band after 5 min illumination, the efficiency of the
SEDs follows the order of 4-MBA > TEOA > MeOH (Figure S14). Water can also work as a SED, but proved to be
the least effective (Figure S15). Moreover,
by conducting UV–vis–NIR studies with various 4-MBA
concentrations under illumination, the optimum concentration of 4-MBA
as the SED for CTF-Bpy is determined to be 20 mM in water (Figure S16), which was then used for subsequent
experiments.

To verify the role of redox-active bpy units for
the photoreduction
property of CTF-Bpy, the photochromic behavior of the biphenyl analogue
of CTF-Bpy, CTF-Bph, was investigated (Figure S17, S18–S21). Upon 5 min of irradiation, the absorption edge
of the UV–vis–NIR spectrum of CTF-Bph red-shifted from
approximately 430 to 480 nm. Extending the irradiation time to 30
min resulted in no additional absorption features compared to the
spectrum observed after 5 min. Notably, the light-induced UV–vis–NIR
spectral changes of CTF-Bph were irreversible and did not revert upon
exposure to air, in marked contrast to the reversible change observed
for CTF-Bpy (Figure S17). While the control
study of CTF-Bph highlights the crucial role of the bpy units in the
reversible photoreduction of CTF-Bpy, it is also important to note
that the presence of the bpy units within a COF does necessarily result
in photochromic behavior (Figure S22).

It is known that the paramagnetic one-electron reduced bpy•– gives rise to an electron paramagnetic resonance
(EPR) signal, whereas the diamagnetic two-electron reduced bpy2– is EPR silent. To identify
the reduced state of the bpy unit in CTF-Bpy upon illumination, we
conducted EPR measurements with an aqueous suspension of CTF-Bpy with
4-MBA. As shown in c, 10 min of illumination significantly increases the EPR
signal compared to the dark, resulting in a clear single peak with
a g value of 2.0030(1). This value is in agreement
with the reported value for bpy•–,
,
 supporting light-induced radical formation. After illumination is
stopped, the EPR signal intensity gradually decreases over time, possibly
due to oxygen (air) leaking into the suspension, which quenches the
radicals. Nevertheless, even after 23 h, there is still detectable
amounts of radicals (ca. 35% intensity retention as compared to the
signal after 10 min illumination), highlighting excellent radical
stability in the dark. In summary, UV–vis–NIR and EPR
results together confirm the formation of the one-electron reduced
bpy•– within CTF-Bpy under illumination,
but do not rule out the possible coexistence of bpy2–, which is EPR silent.

To check
for the possible presence of bpy2–,
we then conducted cyclic voltammetry (CV), in situ EPR- and UV–vis–NIR spectro-electrochemistry measurements.
CV measurements of CTF-Bpy were performed using a three-electrode
setup with CTF-Bpy on a fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)-coated glass
substrate as working electrode, a reference electrode and a Pt counter
electrode in O2-free electrolytes (Figure S23). The working electrode was prepared by drop-casting
a CTF-Bpy suspension in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP)
onto the FTO-coated glass substrate, yielding a uniform film with
an average thickness of ca. 2.7 μm (Figure S24). In acetonitrile containing 0.1 M tetrabutylammonium perchlorate
(TBAClO4), two redox waves at −1.9 V and −2.4
V vs ferrocene/ferrocenium (Fc/Fc+) are observed, corresponding
to the successive reductions of the bpy unit in CTF-BPy to bpy•– and bpy2– , respectively
(Figure S25). In contrast, in 0.1 M KCl
aqueous electrolyte, only one reduction wave appears at −1.1
V vs Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl), suggesting that only bpy•– is accessiblelikely due to the limited potential window
of the aqueous electrolyte (d, Figure S26). During the
cathodic scan, the CTF-Bpy film shows an electrochromic color change
from yellow to black at potentials more negative than −1.0
V and remains black even after the anodic sweep to 0 V (inset of d). The retention
of the black color, together with a substantially lower anodic peak
current (ipa) relative to the cathodic peak current (ipc), indicates an electrochemically irreversible reduction
of CTF-Bpy during the CV measurements in aqueous electrolyte. We propose
the observed irreversible reduction of CTF-Bpy here is due to protonation
of the reduced bpy species. This interpretation is supported by CV
measurements of CTF-Bpy in dry acetonitrile containing 0.1 M TBAClO4 with 0, 0.1, 0.2, or 0.5 M H2O (Figure S27). In the first CV cycle, two distinct oxidation
waves are observed following cathodic reduction in the absence of
H2O (0 M). These oxidation features progressively weaken
and nearly disappear as H2O concentration increases from
0.1 to 0.5 M, providing direct evidence that H2O promotes
the irreversible reduction of CTF-Bpy. It is plausible that H2O acts as a proton source for protonation of the reduced bpy
species, thereby stabilizing the reduced state, consistent with previous
reports that protonation of radical anions or dianions can lead to
irreversible electrochemical reduction. Nevertheless, this reduction is chemically reversible, as supported
by the CTF-Bpy film reverting from black to its original yellow color
upon air exposure after the CV measurement.

In addition to the
CV results in aqueous electrolyte, which suggest
the formation of the one-electron reduced bpy species (e.g., bpy•–) within the potential window of 0 ∼
−1.5 V, in situ EPR spectro-electrochemistry
measurements were performed to further verify that the one-electron
reduced bpy species is the sole species formed (e, Figure S28).
During the cathodic scan, no EPR signal is observed between 0 and
−0.9 V (vs Ag/AgCl, 0.5 M KCl aqueous electrolyte). Upon reaching
−1.0 V, a signal appears at g = 2.0030(1),
and the intensity of this signal continuously increases without noticeable
decrease upon further scanning to −1.5 V (e, Figure S28), corroborating the formation of sole one-electron reduced
bpy species during the CV experiments within the potential window
of 0 ∼ −1.5 V. We further performed operando UV–vis–NIR
spectro-electrochemical measurements to get a better understanding
of the redox states involved in the photoreduced CTF-Bpy (f). The pristine
CTF-Bpy film shows an absorption band between 300–500 nm centered
at 385 nm. As the cathodic sweep progresses, a new absorption band
emerges at −0.9 V between 450–1200 nm and grows continuously
as the scan proceeds to −1.5 V. This new absorption profile
matches with the radical spectrum observed under illumination (a), confirming that
it is the one-electron reduced bpy radical species formed within CTF-Bpy
under light.

Mechanism of Light-Induced
Radical Formation

To probe structural and chemical changes
of CTF-Bpy upon photoreduction,
we conducted PXRD measurements. In molecular bpy, one-electron reduction
strengthens the inter-ring bond (Cpy–Cpy, py = pyridine) and reduces the bond length from 1.48 Å to 1.43
Å. However, this change is too subtle
to be resolved by PXRD, hence the experimental and simulated patterns
of the neutral and photoreduced CTF-Bpy are indistinguishable (Figure S29).

While the spectroscopic and
spectro-electrochemical results in support the formation of one-electron reduced
bpy species, previous studies have shown that photoreduction of molecular
bpy in aqueous solution produces bpyH• and bpyH2
•+. To examine
whether proton uptake occurs during the photoreduction of CTF-Bpy,
the pH of the CTF-Bpy aqueous suspension (1 mg/mL) containing 20 mM
4-MBA was measured before and after 10 min of 365 nm irradiation,
yielding pH values of 8.60 and 8.74, respectively. This pH increase
corresponds to 0.7 × 10–6 mmol L–1 of proton loss from the aqueous medium. Because oxidation of 4-MBA
to p-tolualdehyde releases protons, the amount of p-tolualdehyde produced during irradiation was also quantitatively
determined by High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) (Figure S30). After 10 min of irradiation, the p-tolualdehyde concentration increased by 0.28 mmol L–1, corresponding to 0.56 mmol L–1 of proton release from 4-MBA oxidation. This significant discrepancy
in the supplied amount of protons and actual change in the proton
concentration (+0.56 mmol L–1 versus −0.7
× 10–6 mmol L–1) indicates
that the protons released from 4-MBA oxidation are nearly quantitatively
consumed during CTF-Bpy photoreduction, likely to compensate the negative
charges generated on the bpy moieties. Thus, these results suggest
that the reduced bpy units in photoreduced CTF-Bpy exist in the protonated
form (e.g., bpyH• and bpyH2
•+) rather than anion radicals (i.e., bpy•–), with protons being supplied by 4-MBA oxidation.

The proton-coupled
nature of CTF-Bpy reduction in aqueous medium
is further supported by CV measurements over a wide pH range (pH 1.63–13.54)
in 0.1 M KCl aqueous electrolytes (Figure S31). While one reduction wave accompanied by a corresponding oxidation
wave was observed over the entire pH range investigated, the half-wave
potential (E
1/2) shifted progressively
toward more negative potentials with increasing pH, indicating the
redox behavior of CTF-Bpy is pH-dependent. The measured E
1/2 values were subsequently used to construct the Pourbaix
diagram (b, Figure S31b). Fitting the Nernst equation to the observed data points revealed
three distinct linear regimes at pH 1.63–3.84, 3.84–6.54
and 6.54–13.54, with m/n ratios
of 1.91, 1.16 and 0.43, where m and n represent the numbers of protons and electrons involved in the corresponding
redox equilibrium. These values are roughly consistent with H+/e– ratios of 2:1, 1:1 and 1:2 in the three
pH regimes, respectively. In b, we tentatively assigned the possible half-reaction
products from neutral bpy corresponding to each m/n ratio. However, CTF-Bpy is an extended polymeric
system containing multiple redox-active bpy units in proximity, and
the derived m/n ratios do not necessarily
reflect the redox equilibrium on a respective, single bpy unit. Therefore,
the E
1/2–pH analysis only confirms
the involvement of proton transfer in the reduction process of CTF-Bpy,
and provides a rough estimate of H+/e– ratio involved in the (photo)­reduction.

As noted above, the typical photoreduction of CTF-Bpy
is conducted
at pH of 8.60, which falls within the pH regime associated with the
1H+/2e– redox stoichiometry. This indicates
that a proton-coupled, two-electron reduction redox equilibrium is
thermodynamically accessible for CTF-Bpy under the photoreduction
condition. In combination with the spectroscopic and spectro-electrochemical
results in that support the formation of one-electron-reduced bpy species,
we tentatively propose the formation of bpyH• +
bpy•– from two bpy units within CTF-Bpy (a–b). Given
the high reported pK
a of bpyH• (>14), subsequent protonation of bpy•– is
expected to be thermodynamically favorable under aqueous conditions.
Yet, we do not exclude the possibility of other redox/protonation
states, such as bpyH2
•+, bpyH–, and bpyH2, as possible photoreduction products in CTF-Bpy,
given the complexity arising from its polymeric nature.

c shows
the Raman spectra (785 nm excitation wavelength) of an aqueous CTF-Bpy
suspension with 20 mM 4-MBA, recorded as a function of illumination
time (365 nm UV light) and during subsequent air exposure. The pristine
CTF-Bpy (c,
Pristine) shows the expected bands from the triazine/pyridine rings,
whose full assignment is summarized in Figure S32 and Table S1. Upon illumination, we observe gradual emergence
of new bands at 605, 725, 972, and 1628 cm–1 as
well as less clear enhancement of Raman intensities in the range of
1000–1550 cm–1. The emergence of these new
bands is accompanied by the weakening of the major band in the pristine
CTF-Bpy at 1596 cm–1. The observed spectral changes,
except for the two main bands at 1628 and 1596 cm–1, agree well with the reported spectral changes associated with the
photoreduction of bpy to bpyH•. Exposing the suspension of the photoreduced CTF-Bpy to
air leads to the gradual recovery of the pristine CTF-Bpy spectrum,
with complete recovery after 35 min in air, indicating that the photoreduction/reoxidation
and the associated local structural changes of CTF-Bpy are fully reversible.

Raman spectra of CTF-Bpy were also recorded after 30 min of irradiation
under deuterated (MeOH-d
4 as SED/D2O as solvent) and nondeuterated conditions (MeOH/H2O) (Figure S33). Both spectra were very
similar to that obtained in the 4-MBA/H2O system (c), supporting the
role of methanol as SED for this system, consistent with our previous
observation (Figure S14). Importantly,
the most intense band at 1628 cm–1 in the photoreduced
CTF-Bpy was red-shifted to 1612 cm–1 in the deuterated
system, suggesting the presence of N-protonated species in the photoreduced
CTF-Bpy.

DFT calculations (using ORCA 6.0.1, B3LYP/def2-TZVP)
were then
performed on model molecules to simulate their Raman spectra, aiding
the assignment of the bands at 1596 and 1628 cm–1 and further elucidating the observed deuteration effect (Figure S34). As the results in support the formation of one-electron
reduced bpy species, calculations were performed for a neutral structure
model (“Neutral”) and one-electron-reduced bpy models
with different protonation states, namely, one-proton one-electron-reduced
(“1H1e”) and two-proton one-electron-reduced (“2H1e”)
species, together with their deuterated counterparts (“1D1e”
and “2D1e”). The calculated Raman spectrum of the neutral
fragment agrees well with the experimental spectrum of CTF-Bpy, showing
the validity of the DFT theory applied. In comparison to the experimental
spectra, we found that both 1H1e and 2H1e models can explain the emergent bands in the photoreduced CTF-Bpy;
the band at 972 cm–1 matches with the deformation
modes of triazine rings bonded to the reduced bpy unit (968 cm–1 in 1H1e and 972 cm–1 in 2H1e),
and the band at 725 cm–1 corresponds to the deformation
modes of reduced bpy unit in 1H1e model. Notably, these simulated
bands also appear in the deuterated models (1D1e and 2D1e), in good agreement
with the consistency of these bands in the experimental spectra toward
deuteration (Figure S33). Furthermore,
the 2H1e model explains the highest-frequency band of photoreduced
CTF-Bpy appearing at 1628 cm–1, and its deuterated
counterpart, 2D1e, as it reproduces the observed isotopic red-shift
to 1612 cm–1 (from 1595 to 1577 cm–1 in the simulated spectra). Therefore, we conclude that the majority
of Raman-active species in the photoreduced CTF-Bpy are described
as bpyH• and bpyH2
•+ (+ unreacted bpy) species, whereas their relative population remains
elusive. See Supporting Information for
the detailed assignment of the Raman bands (Table S1).

Based on the above results, here we propose a mechanism
for the
reversible photoreduction of CTF-Bpy in aqueous suspension with 4-MBA.
Above-bandgap photoexcitation induces electron–hole separation
in CTF-Bpy. The holes irreversibly oxidize 4-MBA to form p-tolualdehyde, whereas the electrons are stored and localized on
the protonated reduced bpy units within CTF-Bpy, as observed from
DFT calculations (Figure S35a). Photoreduction
of CTF-Bpy predominantly proceeds through one-electron reduction of
the bpy units. The comparison of the suspension pH before and after
irradiation, quantitative analysis of the 4-MBA oxidation product,
and pH-dependent CV measurements collectively suggest that the (photo)­reduction
of CTF-Bpy involves proton-coupled redox chemistry, consistent with
a proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) pathway. However, whether proton and electron transfer occur in
a concerted or sequential manner cannot be determined from the present
data. The protons involved in the photoreduction of CTF-Bpy are predominantly
supplied by 4-MBA oxidation. Combined with the experimental and simulated
Raman spectra, these observations support the presence of bpyH• and bpyH2
•+ as the reduction
species, although their relative populations remain unclear. The possible
coexistence of two-electron reduced bpy species (e.g., bpyH– and bpyH2) cannot be excluded at this stage.

After
photoreduction of CTF-Bpy, it retains the reduced state for
a long period of time unless it is exposed to air and being quenched
by O2. We also monitored the radical decay kinetics in
the dark under a nominally O2-free condition using UV–vis–NIR
spectroscopy (Figure S36). We observed
a mixed character of first- and second-order kinetics of the decay
rate, similar to the molecular bpy system in solution. However, a more detailed kinetic analysis is
necessary to substantiate the discharge mechanism. Nevertheless, EPR
studies shown in c suggest a high stability of the radicals formed from the photoreduction
of CTF-Bpy; in other words, a high stability of the photocharged state
of CTF-Bpy. Inspired by this, we next investigate the properties of
the light-induced electrons in CTF-Bpy and explore their applications
in aqueous solar batteries and dark photocatalysis.

(Photo)­Electrochemistry

The (photo)­electrochemical
(PEC) properties of the CTF-Bpy photoanode were assessed using the
same three-electrode setup described above, with Ag/AgCl (saturated
KCl) as the reference electrode (a). The photoanodes
were photocharged by illuminating from the front (coated) side in
an O2-free aqueous electrolyte with or without SED 4-MBA.
First, we investigate the open circuit potential (OCP) under illumination
and its dark stability in different electrolytes. As shown in b, upon 1 min illumination,
the OCP reaches −0.90 V in the electrolyte containing 4-MBA,
indicating a pronounced photovoltaic effect of CTF-Bpy (b). Under continuous illumination
for 10 min, the photo-OCP value saturates at ca. −0.92 V and
remains stable thereafter for more than 40 h in the dark, implying
highly robust electron trapping in the framework. During photocharging
in 20 mM 4-MBA aqueous electrolyte, the color of CTF-Bpy on FTO changes
from yellow to black (Figure S37), suggesting
one-electron reduction of bpy to occur, consistent with the spectro-electrochemical
experiments described above (, ). In the absence of 4-MBA,
the photo-OCP reaches −0.76 V after 10 min illumination in
water and decays much faster, signifying water as a weak SED, in line
with previous UV–vis–NIR experiments (Figure S15). This underscores the critical role of efficient
hole extraction to facilitate effective charging of the CTF-Bpy photoanode
and to stabilize the charged state. The
transient photocurrent response of the CTF-Bpy photoanode in the absence
and presence of 4-MBA are measured using chronoamperometry (CA) under
dark/light cycles. As shown in c, the photocurrent density is higher with 4-MBA than
in pure water, indicating efficient hole quenching by 4-MBA. The spikes
near the onset of photocurrent under illumination likely originate
from recombination of photogenerated charge carriers. After three dark/light cycles, the photocurrent stabilizes
at ca. 4.0 μA cm–2 and is reproducible in
subsequent cycles, indicating stable transfer of photogenerated electrons
from CTF-Bpy to the FTO upon illumination. Photocurrent responses
were further examined by applying a gradually more negative bias to
the photoanode (d). During the sweep from 0 to −1.0 V, the photocurrent remains
positive down to −0.9 V with 4-MBA and −0.7 V in pure
H2O, but becomes negative at more negative potentials,
suggesting the crossover from an electron injection from CTF-Bpy to
the FTO substrate to the reversed injection from FTO into the material
or to the electrolyte.

We then performed impedance
spectroscopy measurements
to assess
the charge transfer characteristics of CTF-Bpy in the dark and under
illumination. The experimental data collected before and after 10
min illumination were fitted using two equivalent circuit models (e, Figure S38). In the dark, the equivalent circuit consists
of a serial resistance (RS, 64.1 Ω), accounting for
electrolyte resistance, in series with a parallel combination of a
resistance RC (1.61 × 108 Ω), which
will be discussed below in more detail, with a constant-phase element
(CPE) representing the double-layer capacitance at the electrode/electrolyte
interface. After 10 min illumination,
in addition to the RS and RC–CPE parallel
elements used to fit the dark data, a charge transfer resistance (RCT, 3.68 × 103 Ω) and a Warburg impedance
(WS), assigned to ion diffusion (i.e., H+) within
the electrode material, appear in parallel with a second CPE, which
is utilized to reflect the emergence of the charge transfer process
in the photocharged state. Upon illumination, as expected, RS remains essentially unchanged (45.4 Ω), whereas RC decreases by 4 orders of magnitude (1.03 × 104 Ω),
suggesting a significant conductivity increase. Notably, the apparent
conductivity enhancement should not be interpreted as a direct measurement
of the intrinsic electronic conductivity of CTF-Bpy. Rather, the substantial
reduction in RC, together with the emergence of distinct
charge-transfer and ion-diffusion contributions under illumination,
indicates a pronounced change in the overall charge-transport properties
of the photocharged electrode. The charge trapping process corresponds
to the electronic transport channel, while ion diffusion reflects
ionic/proton transport, with interfacial charge transfer and capacitive
charging contributing throughout. Accordingly, the conductivity values
derived from RC should be interpretated as relative changes
in the resistive response of the same electrode before and after illumination.
Details of the fitted parameters of equivalent circuits in the dark
and under light are provided in Tables S2 and S3.

Solar Battery Function

Encouraged
by its light-induced electron-storage capability, we employed CTF-Bpy
as a solar battery photoanode with the three-electrode setup described
above (a).
Prior to all photocharging experiments, the photoanode was immersed
into aqueous electrolyte with 4-MBA for 1 h to stabilize the OCP (ca.
−0.20 V). After 10 min illumination under OCP conditions, the
light was switched off and the photoanodes were discharged immediately
by applying a galvanostatic discharging current (1, 1.5, 5, 10, 50,
and 100 mA g–1). The capacity increases upon decreasing
the discharge current density, and an optimum capacity of 8.2 mAh
g–1 is achieved at 1.5 mA g–1,
as shown in a. Further decreasing the discharge current
density to 1 mA g–1 causes a capacity drop to 6.7
mAh g–1, likely due to competitive charge loss via
self-discharge of the material. Extending
the illumination duration from 1 to 10 min shifts the OCP slightly
more negative (−0.89 to −0.92 V) and doubles the capacity
(4.1 mAh g–1 to 8.2 mAh g–1, at
a discharge current density of 1.5 mA g–1). Further
extending illumination to 30 min causes no OCP shift and only minor
capacity enhancement (8.6 mAh g–1), indicating saturation
of photogenerated charges by 10 min illumination (b). This behavior stems from
progressively slowed photocharging due to concomitant electron accumulation
and recombination within the material, which limits the photocharging
efficiency, as previously observed for K-PHI.
,
 This effect also partly explains the relatively low experimental
charge storage capacity of 8.6 mAh g–1, which accounts
for only 6.6% of the theoretical value (130 mAh g–1), assuming one electron is stored per bpy unit in CTF-Bpy (Figure S39). Control experiments reveal a negligible
contribution of bare FTO to electron storage (Figure S40), supporting that the observed photocharging behavior,
long-lived electron storage, and measured capacity of the CTF-Bpy
photoanodes originate primarily from CTF-Bpy rather than the FTO substrate.
Meanwhile, electrolyte-exposed FTO of CTF-Bpy photoanodes may act
as a discharge channel for photoaccumulated electrons in CTF-Bpy.
,
 Moreover, considering the narrow optical bandgap of CTF-Bpy (2.3
eV, Figure S10), photocharging was also
performed by standard solar illumination (AM 1.5 G, nominal power P
nominal ≈ 100 mW cm–2). Under solar illumination, the OCP rises rapidly from −0.20
to −0.77 V in 1 min, and saturates at −0.85 V after
2 h. Subsequent galvanostatic discharge at 1.5 mA g–1 yields a capacity of 4.7 mAh g–1 (Figure S41). Although this capacity is lower
than that achieved by 10 min UV photocharging (8.2 mAh g–1), this result demonstrates the capability of direct solar-to-electrical
energy conversion in CTF-Bpy.

Photocharging and discharging of CTF-Bpy photoanodes
were also
performed in 20 mM 4-MBA aqueous solution containing 0.1 M LiCl, NaCl
and KCl to investigate the charge-compensation mechanism. As shown
in Figure S42, neither the photocharging
behavior nor the discharging capacity exhibited an apparent dependence
on the presence of Li+, Na+ or K+, further supporting a predominantly proton-mediated charge compensation
mechanism in CTF-Bpy, consistent with our mechanistic studies above.
We then investigated the photocharging of CTF-Bpy photoanodes in aqueous
buffer solutions containing 20 mM 4-MBA at pH 3.81, 6.65, and 9.33.
As shown in Figure S43, the photo-OCP progressively
shifted toward more positive potentials with decreasing solution pH,
from −0.93 V at pH 9.33 to −0.77 V at pH 6.65 and −0.60
V at pH 3.81. This pH-dependent shift in the photo-OCP is consistent
with the observed shift of the electrochemical reduction potential
of CTF-Bpy toward more positive potentials with decreasing pH (Figure S31). The OCP of the CTF-Bpy photoanode
is constant under dark condition even in the acidic buffer solution
at pH 3.81, supporting that the observed photoreduction of CTF-Bpy
is primarily associated with the intrinsic redox properties of the
material rather than protonation effects.

To evaluate the stability
of the trapped electrons and their on-demand
release, we delayed the galvanostatic discharging (at 1.5 mA g–1) by a specified time interval after UV photocharging
at 365 nm for 10 min (c, Figure S44). After a 2 h delay,
89% of the capacity was retained, and 55% after 8 h, indicating relatively
stable electron storage in CTF-Bpy and controllable release for solar
battery applications. Even after 24 h, during which the OCP decreases
from −0.92 V to −0.83 V, 14% of the capacity is retained.
As suggested previously, Faradaic charge transfer from the partially
uncovered FTO substrate (Figure S24) to
the electrolyte may contribute to the time-dependent capacity decay.
,

We then evaluated the cycling stability of the CTF-Bpy photoanode
by repeating 1 min photocharging (365 nm) followed by galvanostatic
discharging at 10 mA g–1 for 20 cycles (d, Figure S45). Note that, at this current, the photoanode could
not be fully discharged to 0 V, owing to intrinsic resistive losses
arising from limited conductivity under high-current conditions, consistent
with previous observations for the K-PHI photoanode. Therefore, after each discharge cycle the photoanode was
removed from the electrolyte and exposed to air to complete discharging.
Even after 20 photocharge–discharge cycles, 50% capacity was
retained (d),
highlighting the relative robustness and reversibility of CTF-Bpy
as an electron reservoir for solar battery applications. Comparing
of the SEM images of the CTF-Bpy photoanode before and after cycling
reveals increased exposure of the FTO substrate (Figure S46), suggesting partial delamination of CTF-Bpy during
the cycling, which may contribute to the observed performance decay.

Dark Photocatalysis

Controlled release
of light-induced trapped charges in the dark can potentially be exploited
for dark photocatalysis, helping overcome the intermittency of solar
irradiation and challenges of chemical fuel storage (especially H2) via on-demand release. In electron-storage materials, dark
photocatalysis typically involves generating and storing photoelectrons
under illumination, followed by their release in the dark, for example
to reduce O2 to H2O2
 or H+ to H2, the latter typically
triggered by addition of a Pt cocatalyst. Motivated by the above results demonstrating the capability of CTF-Bpy
for stable electron storage in the dark, we evaluated its performance
for H2O2 production and H2 evolution
in the dark using our previously reported experimental setup (Figure S47).
,

Dark H2O2 Production

As mentioned
above, during the UV–vis–NIR experiments,
we observed H2O2 formation after quenching the
photocharged CTF-Bpy suspension by air (Figure S13), which prompted us to examine the dark H2O2 production performance of CTF-Bpy. Photocharging of CTF-Bpy
was performed by irradiating an O2-free aqueous CTF-Bpy
suspension with 20 mM 4-MBA for 36 min (365 nm), followed by O2 purging into the suspension at a flow rate of 100 mL min–1 in the dark (a). Prior to illumination,
a small amount of H2O2 (108 μmol g–1) was present in the suspension; this could be reduced
by preparing the sample in the dark under Ar (Figure S48), likely because the material is already active
under ambient light and O2. Upon O2 introduction
after the photocharging, the suspension rapidly changed from black
to the pristine yellow color (inset of a), indicating fast discharge, which is also
reflected by significant H2O2 production in
the system. The H2O2 concentration reached 1100
μmol g–1 within 12 min after O2 introduction and then remained constant over the next 2 h under
continuous O2 flow in the dark, suggesting complete consumption
of the stored electrons by O2 in 12 min. We then investigated
the dependence of dark H2O2 production on the
irradiation sources by photocharging CTF-Bpy for 36 min using 405,
505 nm LED and a full-spectrum solar simulator, and compared the results
with those obtained using a 365 nm LED. As shown in Figure S49, after switching off the light and purging with
O2 for 12 min in the dark, the amount of H2O2 produced followed the order 365 nm > 405 nm > solar
simulator,
yielding 1109, 598, and 497 μmol g–1, respectively.
In contrast, photocharging of CTF-Bpy with 505 nm irradiation was
unsuccessful. These results indicate that the 365 nm LED is the most
effective irradiation source for photocharging CTF-Bpy, likely because
its emission closely matches the absorption maximum of CTF-Bpy (Figure S49b). The cycling stability of CTF-Bpy
for dark H2O2 production was evaluated over
three consecutive cycles. As shown in Figure S50, CTF-Bpy could be repeatedly photocharged in each cycle, while the
amount of H2O2 produced remained nearly constant
across the three cycles, indicating good stability of CTF-Bpy for
repeated dark H2O2 production.

To probe whether dark H2O2 production
from
O2 reduction proceeds via a stepwise 1e– pathway in the overall 2e– reduction or via a
direct 2e– route (Figure S51), we performed a control experiment using p-benzoquinone
(p-BQ)a known O2
•–/•OOH radical scavengeras O2
•– is a characteristic intermediate of the
stepwise 1e– pathway. Although no H2O2 was formed in the presence
of p-BQ, this result alone does not confirm a stepwise
1e– pathway, as p-BQ suppresses
photocharging of CTF-Bpy by scavenging photogenerated electrons and
undergoing self-reduction to hydroquinone (Figures S52, S53). A spin-trapping experiment using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline
N-oxide (DMPO) was further performed to probe the possible formation
of O2
•– and/or its protonated
form, •OOH, as their corresponding DMPO adducts
are expected to exhibit characteristic six-line EPR spectra (Figure S54). In
a CTF-Bpy aqueous suspension containing 20 mM 4-MBA and 100 mM DMPO,
only the EPR signal of CTF-Bpy was observed after irradiation under
Ar. Upon introducing air in the dark, no six-line signal corresponding
to the DMPO-O2
•–/DMPO-•OOH adduct was observed. Instead, a characteristic four-line EPR
spectrum with an intensity ratio of approximately 1:2:2:1 was detected,
consistent with the formation of DMPO-•OH. This
detection of DMPO-•OH may suggest a stepwise 1e– pathway for the dark H2O2 formation,
as the DMPO-O2
•–/DMPO-•OOH adduct has been reported to undergo rapid decay to DMPO-•OH in aqueous media.
,

Dark Hydrogen Evolution

As shown
earlier, the photo-OCP of CTF-Bpy photoanode reaches −0.92
V vs Ag/AgCl (−0.72 V vs NHE) in 20 mM 4-MBA aqueous electrolyte
upon 10 min 365 nm UV illumination (b, b), corresponding
to the one-electron reduction of the bpy unit. Based on the H+/H2 redox potential of −0.41 V vs NHE (pH
7, 25 °C), the one-electron reduced
species is thermodynamically capable of driving the H2 evolution
reaction (with a redox potential difference of ΔE ≈ 0.31
V, corresponding to a Gibbs free energy of ΔG ≈ −60
kJ mol–1). We thus tested CTF-Bpy for H2 evolution in the dark.

Photocharging of CTF-Bpy follows the
same procedure as for dark H2O2 production (365
nm, 36 min), but instead of O2, 3 wt % Pt cocatalyst was
introduced into the suspension to drive H2 evolution in
the dark. As for the cocatalyst, we tested Pt nanoparticles (∼3
nm) and H2PtCl6 (H2PtCl6·6H2O was used) (b). In both cases, addition of the Pt cocatalyst
after switching off the light resulted in H2 evolution,
accompanied by the color reversal of the suspension from black to
pristine yellow (inset of b). Notably, the introduction of H2PtCl6 induces faster electron release and higher maximum H2 evolution as compared to Pt nanoparticles over a 3-h test
(68.1 vs 38.4 μmol g–1, corresponding to a
turnover frequency of 0.20 and 0.11 h–1, respectively).
This can be attributed to a more uniform distribution of Pt nanoparticles
(Pt0) across CTF-Bpy, enabled by in situ reduction of H2PtCl6 (Pt4+), although
the reduction itself also consumes electrons.
,
 To distinguish the amounts of stored electrons consumed by H2 evoution and H2PtCl6 reduction, inductively
coupled plasma (ICP) analysis was performed on CTF-Bpy after the dark
H2 evolution experiment. While 3 wt % of Pt, supplied by
H2PtCl6, was added to the system, ICP analysis
of the recovered CTF-Bpy revealed a Pt content of 2.16 wt %. As TEM
images show that the generated Pt nanoparticles (Pt0) remain
associated with the recovered CTF-Bpy (Figure S55), and assuming that all unreacted H2PtCl6 was removed during water washing (H2PtCl6 is highly water-soluble), the measured Pt content corresponds to
an estimated consumption of 443 μmol e– g–1 CTF-Bpy for Pt4+ reduction to Pt0.

Next, we investigated the dark H2 evolution of
CTF-Bpy
in detail with 3 wt % H2PtCl6 as the cocatalyst.
By varying the illumination time, the maximum H2 production
was achieved at 36 min illumination, with no further increase upon
extending the illumination time to 1 h (Figure S56). After the dark H2 evolution follows 1 h of
irradiation, we observed a decrease in the intensity of the first
peak ({100} plane) (Figure S57a). Since
the {100} reflection in 2D layered COFs/CTFs represents in-plane periodicity,
this decrease is most likely associated with the decrease in the stacking
order between layers under the reaction conditions (i.e., slippage
of layers), which affects the out of plane pore arrangement. It should be also noted that the {001} reflection
at around 26.4° did not change before and after the dark H2 evolution, which further indicates that the stacking distance
was maintained. Nevertheless, despite the slight decrease in crystallinity,
TEM supports the local retention of the CTF-Bpy framework structure
(Figure S58a–c), while FTIR measurements
confirm that its chemical structure is retained after the dark H2 evolution experiment (Figure S57b). The corresponding Energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis further
supports that the generated Pt nanoparticles from H2PtCl6 reduction remain associated with the material after the dark
H2 evolution experiment (Figure S58d).

Inspired by the stability of the stored electrons in the
solar
battery photoanode (c), we also tested dark H2 production by delaying
the addition of 3 wt % H2PtCl6 after 36 min
UV illumination. Delaying Pt cocatalyst addition by 12 and 36 min
yielded maximum dark H2 production of 34.1 and 23.8 μmol
g–1, corresponding to 50.1% and 34.9% of the undelayed
performance (68.1 μmol g–1), respectively
(c). While
the performance loss upon delayed Pt addition likely arises from radical
quenching via O2 (i.e., possible air leaking into the suspension)
and self-decay
,
 during the waiting period, the
delayed experiments nonetheless indicate stable charge retention,
highlighting the potential of CTF-Bpy for controlled, on-demand H2 production in the dark. Preloading Pt onto CTF-Bpy inhibits
the efficient accumulation of electrons under illumination because
the photogenerated electrons are competitively and immediately consumed
for H2 evolution, as observed both in system with H2PtCl6 (Figure S59),
and the system with Pt nanoparticles as the cocatalyst (Figure S60). CTF-Bpy represents the first example
of a COF that is capable of driving H2 evolution in the
dark (Table S4), thereby opening this rich
materials library for the rational development of more efficient COFs-based
systems for dark photocatalysis.

The Number
of Accumulated Electrons in CTF-Bpy

To estimate the fraction
of bpy units involved in light-induced
electron accumulation in CTF-Bpy, we compared the number of accumulated
electrons determined by spectroscopic methods (EPR and UV–vis)
and functional applications (solar battery and dark H2O2/H2 production) with the theoretical maximum. Quantitative
EPR measurements were performed to determine the spin concentration
of photocharged CTF-Bpy. By fitting the measured EPR intensity to
a calibration curve established using 4-hydroxy-2,2,6,6,-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl
(Tempol), the electron storage amount was determined to be 0.10 mmol
g–1 (i.e., 0.10 mmol e– per gram
of CTF) (Figures S61–S
62). Using methyl viologen (MV2+)
as an electron acceptor, the accumulated electrons in photocharged
CTF-Bpy were quantified by monitoring MV2+ reduction using
UV–vis spectroscopy, yielding an electron storage amount of
0.285 mmol g–1 (Figure S63). For the functional applications, the number of accumulated electrons
was estimated from the maximum performance achieved in each case.
Accordingly, electron-storage amounts of 0.306 mmol g–1, 2.20 mmol g–1 and 0.579 mmol g–1 were estimated from the solar battery discharge capacity (8.2 mAh
g–1), dark H2O2 production
(1100 μmol g–1), and dark H2 evolution
(68.1 μmol g–1 H2 plus 0.443 mmol
g–1 for H2PtCl6/Pt4+ reduction to Pt0), respectively. Assuming one-electron
reduction of each bpy unit in CTF-Bpy, the theoretical maximum electron-storage
capacity is 4.85 mmol g–1 (Figure S39). Thus, the electron-storage amounts determined by quantitative
EPR, MV2+ reduction, solar battery discharge, dark H2O2 production, and dark H2 evolution
correspond to 2.06%, 5.88%, 6.31%, 45.36%, and 11.94% of the theoretical
maximum, respectively. These results are summarized in Table S5 in the Supporting Information.

The relatively lower electron-storage values
estimated from MV2+ reduction, solar battery discharge,
and dark H2 evolution may be partly related to possible
air/O2 leakage. The substantially lower value obtained
from quantitative EPR may additionally reflect underestimation of
the total electron population due to EPR-silent two-electron reduced
species, whose presence cannot yet be excluded. Among the different
approaches, dark H2O2 production yielded the
highest estimated electron-storage amount, partly due to the undesired
O2 quenching in other applications directly contributed
to H2O2 formation. As the dark H2O2 production was initiated immediately after irradiation
without delay, we propose that this method provides the closest estimate
of the actual electron-storage capacity of CTF-Bpy under irradiation.
Assuming one-electron reduction per bpy unit, this value corresponds
to approximately 45% of the bpy units in CTF-Bpy participating in
electron storage upon irradiation.

Conclusion

Herein, we report and systematically
investigate the light-induced
electron storage properties of a known COF, CTF-Bpy, and explore their
utility for aqueous solar batteries and dark photocatalysis. The light-induced
electron accumulation is reversible and is quenched by exposure to
air/O2. A combination of spectroscopic, electrochemical,
and spectro-electrochemical experiments, together with DFT calculations,
reveals that the electron storage arises from the one-electron reduction
of the redox-active bpy unit via a proton-coupled process, with bpyH• and bpyH2
•+ identified
as the photoreduction product. The photogenerated electrons stored
in CTF-Bpy can be released on demand by electrical discharge in the
dark, delivering capacities up to 8.2 mAh g–1 when
employed as a photoanode for aqueous solar battery application. The
stored electrons can be released by reaction with O2 to
produce H2O2 in the dark, with a maximum production
of 1100 μmol g–1. Moreover, enabled by the
sufficiently negative photo-OCP (−0.92 V vs Ag/AgCl), the photocharged
CTF-Bpy can drive dark H2 evolution with a maximum production
of 68.1 μmol g–1 in the presence of Pt cocatalyst,
representing the first example of COFs for dark H2 evolution.
Comparison of the number of accumulated electrons estimated from different
spectroscopic methods and functional applications reveals that dark
H2O2 production yields the highest estimated
electron-storage capacity. Assuming that only one-electron reduction
of the bpy units occurs in photoreduced CTF-Bpy, this value corresponds
to approximately 45% of the bpy units undergoing one-electron reduction
under irradiation.

This systematic investigation of light-induced
electron storage
in CTF-Bpy establishes a general protocol for studying charge accumulation
in related systems and highlights the potential of structurally diverse
COFs as bifunctional materials. Moreover, the inherent molecular tunability
of COFs is advantageous to elucidate the structure–property–function
relationships for the rational design of more stable, sacrificial
reagents free, multielectron/hole storage under visible light irradiation,
advancing efficient direct solar-to-electrochemical/chemical energy
storage to mitigate solar intermittency in future energy systems.
