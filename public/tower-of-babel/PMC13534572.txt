ADHD medications and preadolescent brain structure: patterns of cortical attenuation from the ABCD study
Authors: L Nate Overholtzer, Katherine L Bottenhorn, Sarah L Karalunas, Bradley S Peterson, Megan M Herting
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAttention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is the most common neurodevelopmental disorder in the U.S. Amphetamine (AMP), methylphenidate (MPH), and nonstimulants (NS) used to treat ADHD are among the most prescribed pediatric drugs. Although pharmacotherapy is the most effective treatment, its neurostructural effects remain poorly characterized. Leveraging the ABCD Study, we used a machine learning approach to identify neuroanatomical targets of medications (N = 1306), followed by linear mixed-effects modelling to estimate the effects of ADHD status and medication use (AMP, MPH, NS) on cortical thickness, surface area, and cortical and subcortical volumes (N = 8762). ADHD was not associated with significant differences in brain structure; effects were small and bidirectional. Nevertheless, a consistent pattern emerged in which AMP and MPH effects were opposite in direction to ADHD associations (i.e., “attenuation” toward control phenotype), while NS showed a weaker pattern. AMP and MPH use were both associated with significantly reduced right banks of the superior temporal sulcus and left posterior cingulate surface area. Additionally, AMP was associated with reduced left posterior cingulate volume and MPH with increased right temporal pole volume. Introduction

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is the most prevalent neurodevelopmental disorder in the United States, with estimates of the biological prevalence ranging from 2 to 8% of children and adolescents1–3. Untreated, ADHD can substantially interfere with normal cognitive and functional pediatric development4. Among available treatments, pharmacotherapy is the first-line approach and has the most evidence for reducing academic, social, and occupational impairment, as well as improving the three core symptom domains: (1) inattention (e.g., difficulties in sustained attention and organizing tasks), (2) impulsivity (e.g., inhibitory control problems), and (3) hyperactivity (e.g., excessive motor activity)5,6. Accordingly, approximately 6% of U.S. youth ages 3 to 17 years are treated with one or more ADHD medications, making these medications some of the most common neuropsychiatric medications prescribed during childhood7,8.

ADHD medications have been hypothesized to attenuate differences in brain structure and function, rendering individuals treated with ADHD medications more phenotypically similar to Controls than unmedicated individuals9. Broadly, medications include stimulant medications (e.g., amphetamine-based [AMP] and methylphenidate-based [MPH]) as the preferred first-line agents and nonstimulant [NS] medications (e.g., norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors and alpha-2-adrenergic agonists) as second-line agents if patients fail to respond to stimulants, experience intolerable side effects, or prefer nonstimulant alternatives. Stimulant medications exert their effects primarily by increasing synaptic levels of dopamine (DA) and norepinephrine (NE) through the inhibition of transporters (e.g., DAT and NET). However, AMP and MPH differ in their additional mechanisms of action: AMP also inhibits monoamine breakdown pathways (e.g., VMAT2 and MAO), while MPH displays weak agonism of serotonergic 5-HT1A and alpha-2-adrenergic receptors9. Nonstimulant medications, which includes alpha-2-adrenergic agonists (e.g., clonidine, guanfacine) and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (e.g., atomoxetine, viloxazine), primarily modulate NE and lack the direct DA activity of stimulants. Despite stimulants being the first-line treatment, 40% of children with ADHD display a preferential response to either AMP or MPH, while 30% of children fail to respond10–12. The reasons behind variability in ADHD medication response remain unclear5,12. Understanding how these medications affect brain phenotypes may help advance precision biomarkers for predicting treatment response13.

A pediatric meta-analysis on ADHD failed to identify replicable structural brain differences, likely due to inter-study variability14. Recent literature reviews identify prefrontal cortical and basal ganglia regions as among the most frequently reported ADHD-related grey matter differences, with evidence that pediatric differences attenuate or are no longer detectable by adulthood15,16. These findings align well with the roles of DA and NE in key neural pathways of these regions. As the two primary neurotransmitters targeted by ADHD medications, these catecholamines support prefrontal executive control (via mesocortical and noradrenergic pathways), as well as striatal emotion and reward processing (via mesolimbic pathways)17. These dopaminergic pathways modulate the cognitive and affective loops of the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) circuit, where dysregulation is hypothesized to contribute to the neuroetiology of ADHD and morphological differences in these regions18,19.

Despite widespread use of ADHD medications in the U.S., research on medication effects on MRI brain phenotypes is limited. Previous studies have been constrained by small, homogeneous samples, lacking both statistical power to capture nuanced brain effects and the sample diversity for generalizability. Relatively few studies have examined structural MRI (sMRI) outcomes related to ADHD medication use, in contrast to the larger body of functional MRI literature20. Among children and adolescent sMRI studies, stimulant pharmacotherapy has been associated with attenuation in bidirectional differences between unmedicated ADHD subjects relative to Controls, including in: frontal, cingulate, and parietal-occipital cortical regions21,22, basal ganglia and cerebellar subregions23–25, and the corpus callosum and global white matter26,27. However, generalizability across these individual studies remains poor, with inconsistent and null findings20. Meta-analyses have reported associations between stimulant treatment and greater right-hemisphere basal ganglia volumes (e.g., globus pallidus and putamen) in pediatric ADHD28,29. Such findings have been interpreted as a normalization of brain structure—reduced deviation from Controls—potentially related to modulation through the CSTC pathway. However, these observations are overwhelmingly derived in cross-sectional research designs and therefore causal inferences cannot be made.

Given neurotransmitter pathway differences and regional brain variation in transporter abundance, secondary receptor interactions, and catabolic enzymes, it is plausible that stimulants and nonstimulants may achieve similar improvements in ADHD symptoms via distinct neurostructural changes (Fig. 1)9,30,31. However, no studies have directly compared the neurostructural effects of different ADHD medication classes (i.e., AMP vs. MPH vs. NS), and few have examined the brain effects of any single class using sufficiently powered samples20. Thus, our objective was first to use an explainable artificial intelligence (xAI) framework for a data-driven approach to identifying potentially medication-sensitive brain regions, and then to characterize the distinct neurostructural effects of AMP, MPH, and NS in relation to ADHD status in these regions. We leveraged a large and demographically diverse sample of preadolescents from the NIH-funded Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development℠ Study (ABCD Study®). We hypothesized that stimulants (AMP and MPH) would be associated with attenuation of ADHD-related morphological differences, particularly in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, cingulate cortex, and basal ganglia. In contrast, we expected nonstimulant effects to be more limited, reflecting primarily noradrenergic pathways and possibly affecting parietal regions.

Methods

Participants

Cross-sectional data were collected as a part of the ongoing ABCD Study and included in the ABCD 5.1 data release (doi: https://doi.org/10.15154/z563-zd24). The ABCD Study enrolled more than 11,800 children 9 and 10 years of age (mean age = 9.49; 48% female) between 2016 and 2018 for a 10-year longitudinal study32. Participants were recruited at 21 study sites across the United States, aiming to represent nationwide sociodemographic diversity33. Experimental and consent procedures are approved and overseen by the institutional review board (IRB) and human research protection programs at the University of California, San Diego (UCSD), in accordance with the United States Federal Policy for the Protection of Human Subjects (45 CFR Part 46). Participants provided written assent, and their legal guardian provided written consent to participate. The UCSD IRB acts as the IRB or record for all study sites, but institutional IRB approval is obtained by individual sites: University of Colorado—Boulder (CUB); Children’s Hospital of Los Angeles (CHLA); Florida International University (FIU); Laureate Institute for Brain Research (LIBR); Medical University of South Carolina (MUSC); Oregon Health and Science University (OHSU); University of Pittsburgh (PITT); SRI International (SRI); University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA); University of California, San Diego (UCSD); University of Florida (UFL); University of Maryland—Baltimore (UMB); University of Rochester (ROC); University of Michigan (UMICH); University of Minnesota (UMN); University of Utah (UTAH); University of Vermont (UVM); University of Wisconsin—Madison/Medical College of Wisconsin (UWM); Virginia Commonwealth University (VCU); Yale University (YALE); Washington University in St. Louis (WUSTL). See Garavan et al. (2018)33 and Volkow et al. (2018)34 for additional details. Child participant criteria included English proficiency, absence of severe sensory, neurological, medical, or intellectual limitations, and completion of an MRI scan35.

The present study analyzes a subset of participant ABCD baseline enrollment data at ages 9 and 10 years, including structural magnetic resonance imaging (sMRI), ADHD diagnostic criteria, and medication usage. For this study, we excluded participants based on sMRI quality control procedures36, incidental radiological findings37, missing covariate data, and other neurologic or psychiatric medication usage. To address within-family correlation, we semi-randomly retained one sibling per family for the LME analyses, with preference to retaining siblings with ADHD when applicable. Details of the present study’s exclusion criteria and sample size are provided via a flowchart in Supplemental Fig. 1. Sample characteristics for our study are described in Table 1.

ADHD and medication classification

Caregivers completed the computerized Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia (KSADS) for DSM-5 (KSADS-COMP)38–40 to identify participants meeting ADHD diagnostic criteria, and the Medication Inventory Survey (MIS)38,41 used to determine AMP, MPH, and/or NS medication use38. KSADS-COMP is a computerized semi-structured psychiatric interview, completed by caregivers, assessing the three core symptom domains of inattention, impulsivity, and hyperactivity based on DSM-5 criteria. From the MIS, we extracted data on prescription medication use during the two weeks preceding study visit.

All participants that were currently using at least one ADHD medication were classified as the medicated ADHD group (ADHD+RX). This approach was necessary because KSADS does not account for whether caregiver-reported symptoms reflect behavior while on or off medication, which could otherwise lead to a substantial under-classification of individuals with ADHD well-controlled by their medication. From the ADHD+RX group, we identified and removed two subjects using a NS medication for a Tic Disorder, rather than ADHD. Participants who met KSADS diagnostic criteria for ADHD (present, partial, or unspecified) and did not report ADHD medication use were labeled as the unmedicated ADHD group (ADHD-noRX). The remaining participants were labeled the non-ADHD control (Control) group. To reduce any potential bias introduced by other neuropsychiatric conditions, treatments, or comorbidities in our sample, we excluded both participants with ADHD (N = 194) and Controls (N = 110) taking antidepressant, antipsychotic, anxiolytic, antiepileptic, or other neurologic/psychiatric medications (see Supplemental Table 1).

Neuroimaging data

Scanning protocol details have been reported by Casey et al. (2018)42. A harmonized MRI protocol was used across sites, utilizing 3T scanners manufactured by Siemens, Philips, or GE. Motion compliance training and real-time, prospective motion correction were used to minimize motion distortion. T1-weighted (T1w) images consisting of 176 slices with 1 mm3 isotropic resolution were acquired using a magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence42. The ABCD Data Analysis, Informatics, and Resource Center (DAIRC) inspected individual T1w images for poor image quality (imgincl_t1w_include) and incidence of abnormal clinical findings (mrif_score). The cortical surface of participants’ T1w images was reconstructed using FreeSurfer v7.1.1 and registered to the Desikan-Killiany Atlas to quantify cortical thickness, surface area, and volume across 68 cortical brain regions of interest (ROIs) and to the Aseg Atlas to quantify volumes in 14 subcortical ROIs36.

Demographic data and covariates

The age, sex, and household size of the ABCD cohort closely match the distribution of 9- and 10-year-olds in the U.S. Census Bureau’s American Community Survey. The racial breakdown is similar, except for the underrepresentation of Non-Hispanic Asian, American Indian/Alaska Native, and Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander children in the ABCD43.

Covariates for our study included these demographic and socioeconomic variables: child’s age (in months), child’s sex at birth (male or female), race/ethnicity (Non-Hispanic white, Non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic/Latinx, Non-Hispanic Asian, or Multiracial/Other), average household income (≥ $100 K USD, ≥ $50 K and < $100 K USD, < $50 K USD, or Don’t Know/Refuse to Answer), and highest household education (Post-Graduate Degree, Bachelor’s Degree, Some College, High School Diploma/GED, or Less than High School Diploma). Race and ethnicity were assessed in the ABCD Study using two questions and are collapsed into 5 categories. The Multiracial/Other category of the combined race/ethnicity variable in ABCD includes caregiver-identified participants as American Indian/Native American, Alaska Native, Native Hawaiian, Guamanian, Samoan, other Pacific Islander, other race, or more than one race.

Analyses

Each analysis followed a two-step approach: (1) a region-of-interest (ROI) selection phase using bootstrap-enhanced elastic net (ENet) regression to identify regions potentially sensitive to medication effects (i.e., those that differ between medicated [ADHD+RX] and unmedicated [ADHD-noRX] ADHD groups), and (2) linear mixed-effects (LME) regression modelling applied to the selected ROIs to estimate the effects of ADHD status and use of specific medication classes (i.e., AMP, MPH, and NS). This workflow is depicted in Fig. 2 and was applied three times to measures of cortical thickness, surface area, and volume. All analyses were conducted in R Version 4.4.1 (R Core Team, 2024) using the glmnet version 4.1–8.1 package44 for ENet models and the lme4 version 1.1–35.5 package45 for LME models, with model diagnostics assessed using the performance version 0.12.2 package46.

Analysis 1: brain feature selection through machine learning

To identify potential neuroanatomic targets of ADHD medications, we applied bootstrap-enhanced ENet regression modelling to three sMRI metrics across 68 cortical ROIs and 14 subcortical ROI volumes to predict ADHD medication status (ADHD+RX vs. ADHD-noRX) as a logistic target (Fig. 2A). Each ML step was run separately for each sMRI metric (e.g., cortical thickness, surface area, volume). ENet regression was chosen because combining L1 and L2 regularization can accommodate the high degree of autocorrelation characteristic of MRI data. That is, it strikes a balance between sparsifying model coefficients (i.e., performing feature selection for dimensionality reduction) and retaining coefficients for correlated features (e.g., similar brain regions). This method, proposed by Bunea et al. (2011)47, involves repeatedly bootstrapping ENet regression and has been shown to improve MRI feature selection compared to traditional ENet regression.

Specifically, we performed 1,000 bootstraps of cross-validated ENet regression, ensuring each iteration preserved a stratified ratio of ADHD+RX to ADHD-noRX subjects in both the 80% training and 20% testing sets. The L1:L2 ratio, or α, was set at 0.5 to ensure models balanced sparsity and shrinkage, as noted above. Within each iteration, λ was fine-tuned via 10-fold cross-validation across the 80% training set, selecting the regularization parameter that yielded the lowest cross-validated mean squared error. Non-zero coefficients from each model iteration indicated whether an ROI was selected. To account for model quality, we weighted each selected ROI by its model’s performance on the held-out 20% testing set, using the area under the curve (AUCTest). These weighted selection scores were then averaged across bootstrap iterations to generate a feature stability weight for each ROI. Building on the approach of Bunea et al. (2011)47 and Abram et al. (2016)48 in retaining ROIs selected in 50% of bootstrap iterations, we set our feature stability threshold at 0.25—equivalent to selection in 50% of iterations weighted by a model performing at chance level (AUCTest = 0.50).

Analysis 2: linear mixed-effects (LME) modelling

Selected ROIs from Analysis 1 were included in the regression modelling stage to assess the effects of our primary dependent variables: (1) ADHD status (ADHD vs. Control), as well as medication class effects treated as dichotomous variables (2) AMP (yes/no), (3) MPH (yes/no), and (4) NS (yes/no) on the brain in these ROIs (i.e., independent variables) (Fig. 2B). Complete LME model equations are provided in Supplemental Methods. Importantly, ADHD status and each medication class were included as non-mutually exclusive dichotomous variables, allowing individuals to be coded as positive (i.e. “yes”) for multiple medication classes in cases of polypharmacy (e.g., concurrent AMP and NS use). Each regression model also included fixed effects of demographic covariates and MRI scanner manufacturer, with a random effect of MRI scanner serial number. Due to the small categorical counts, we combined the Asian and Other race/ethnicity categories into a single categorical variable (Asian/Other) for regression analysis. Inclusion of MRI scanner manufacturer (Siemens, Philips, or GE) accounts for shared differences in scanner hardware and software in analyses. Instead of an ABCD site effect, we included a random effect that accounted for the 29 unique MRI scanners using their serial numbers, which has been demonstrated to adequately model the unique scanner-related effects observed in the ABCD Study49. Additionally, we included total intracranial volume as a confounder in analyses of cortical and subcortical volumes. We removed participants with missing covariate data. To account for familial relatedness, we selected one sibling per family semi-randomly, preferentially retaining siblings with ADHD. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was performed separately for each predictor (i.e., ADHD, AMP, MPH, NS) across each set of models (i.e., thickness, surface area, volume) using the Benjamini-Hochberg method50. Effect sizes are reported as standardized beta coefficients, allowing for direct comparison across brain features. Given the limitations of relying solely on statistical significance in large population-based studies51,52, we first describe patterns in the beta coefficients—comparing the direction and average magnitude of effects for ADHD status with those of AMP, MPH, and NS—before reporting statistical significance.

Analysis 3: LME sensitivity analysis

To address potential concerns regarding ADHD misclassification based on medication use, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding participants who reported ADHD medication use but did not meet KSADS-COMP diagnostic criteria for ADHD (present, partial, unspecified, or past). This analysis serves two purposes: (1) evaluating the robustness of our findings under stricter diagnostic inclusion criteria, and (2) assessing whether methodological limitations of the KSADS-COMP may have influenced our primary results. Specifically, the KSADS-COMP relies on caregiver report, which may underestimate diagnosis in several scenarios: youth whose current symptoms have improved with pharmacotherapy, cases where caregivers have limited awareness of symptoms (inattentive presentations), and cases where caregivers underreport on symptoms outside of the household setting (e.g., school). Prescription of ADHD medications in clinical practice requires a formal diagnosis by a licensed clinician, suggesting that medication status may capture clinically diagnosed cases not detected by the research assessment alone.

Notably, 290 participants (41.5% of the ADHD+RX group) were classified as ADHD+RX despite not meeting KSADS-COMP criteria for ADHD (present, partial, unspecified, or past). We repeated the full set of LME models (as specified in Analysis 2) after excluding these 290 ADHD+RX participants. While we present these results for completeness, they should not be interpreted in isolation, and both approaches have limitations.

Results

Sample characteristics

Demographic characteristics by ADHD classification are provided in Supplemental Table 2. Consistent with prior epidemiological findings in ADHD7, our sample included a higher prevalence of male than female youth in both the medicated (Male: 73.1%; Female: 26.9%) and unmedicated ADHD groups (Male: 66.9%; Female: 32.9%), along with fewer Non-Hispanic Asian participants. Additionally, in line with prior findings7, females and Hispanic/Latinx individuals within the ADHD groups were less likely to receive ADHD medication treatment.

Analysis 1: brain feature selection through machine learning

Cortical and subcortical brain regions relevant to ADHD medication status

Under our feature stability weight threshold of 0.25, our ML approach identified nine regions for cortical thickness, two regions for cortical surface area, and twelve regions for cortical and subcortical volumes relevant for differentiating medicated (ADHD+RX) and unmedicated ADHD (ADHD-noRX) participants (Fig. 3). Regions exhibiting relevant cortical thickness differences between ADHD+RX and ADHD-noRX individuals included the right caudal anterior cingulate, right inferior temporal, left isthmus cingulate, left lateral occipital, left middle temporal, left precuneus, left postcentral, left supramarginal, and right temporal pole. The average AUCTest across bootstrap iterations was 0.51 for cortical thickness. For surface area, relevant regions included the right banks of the superior temporal sulcus and the left posterior cingulate; the average AUCTest was 0.54. For cortical and subcortical volume, relevant regions included the right banks of the superior temporal sulcus, right cuneus, left inferior parietal, right inferior temporal, left insula, right lingual, left middle temporal, left pars triangularis, left posterior cingulate, left superior parietal, right temporal pole, right accumbens area, left amygdala, and right putamen; the average AUCTest was 0.51. The average AUCs for our three bootstrap-enhanced ENet models were all near chance with poor out-of-sample discriminative performance, underscoring the limitations of standard ENet models in reliably identifying brain features. This highlights the importance of bootstrapping, which improves feature reliability metrics (e.g., our feature stability weight) by measuring feature selection frequency across resampled datasets. However, this feature stability weight cannot be conflated with predictive validity, and, thus, the identified ROI features are simply hypothesis-generating candidates for the LME phase of analysis, rather than definitive ADHD biomarkers. Post hoc, we discovered that approximately 100 bootstraps was sufficient for our data sample, as the feature stability weight leveled off around this point (Supplemental Figs. 2–4).

Exploratory: patterns in ADHD and medication effects on brain structure

For cortical thickness, individuals with ADHD exhibited non-significant decreases in cortical thickness compared to Controls in the right caudal anterior cingulate, right inferior temporal, left isthmus cingulate, left lateral occipital, left middle temporal, left postcentral, and right temporal pole (Figs. 4A and 5A). In contrast, individuals with ADHD exhibited non-significant increases in cortical thickness compared to Controls in the left precuneus and left supramarginal gyrus. AMP use showed associations opposite in direction to ADHD associations in 8 of the 9 ROIs: 7 ROIs where ADHD was negative (ADHD < Controls) and AMP positive (Use > No Use), and 1 ROI where ADHD was positive (ADHD > Control) and AMP negative (Use < No Use), except for the left precuneus (both ADHD and AMP were positive). Similarly, MPH use showed opposite directional associations to ADHD in all 9 ROIs, with MPH use showing positive associations (Use > No Use) with cortical thickness in the 7 regions where ADHD showed negative associations (ADHD < Controls), and MPH use showing a negative association (Use < No Use) with cortical thickness in both the left supramarginal gyrus and left precuneus where ADHD showed positive associations (ADHD > Controls) (Figs. 4A and 5A). Lastly, NS use showed opposite directional associations to ADHD in 7 of 9 ROIs, again showing positive associations (Use > No Use) in 5 of the 7 regions where ADHD showed negative associations (ADHD < Controls), as well as the two negative associations (Use < No Use) in both the left supramarginal gyrus and left precuneus where ADHD showed positive associations (ADHD > Controls) (Figs. 4A and 5A). Across the 9 cortical thickness ROIs, the average absolute values of the standardized betas were 0.013 ± 0.007 for ADHD, 0.015 ± 0.010 for AMP, 0.019 ± 0.011 for MPH, and 0.008 ± 0.006 for NS.

For surface area, individuals with ADHD exhibited a non-significant increase in surface area in the right banks of the superior temporal sulcus (ADHD > Control; Figs. 4B and 5B). All three medication classes showed opposite directional associations (negative) in this region (Use < No Use). In the left posterior cingulate, ADHD was associated with non-significant smaller surface area (ADHD < Controls). Only NS use showed an opposite directional association (positive) in this region (Use > No Use), while AMP and MPH showed associations in the same direction as ADHD (both negative; Use < No Use; Figs. 4B and 5B). Thus, across surface area ROIs, AMP and MPH showed opposite directional associations to ADHD in 1 of 2 ROIs, while NS showed opposite directional associations in both ROIs. For surface area ROIs, the average absolute values of the standardized beta coefficients were 0.008 ± 0.009 for ADHD, 0.047 ± 0.009 for AMP, 0.036 ± 0.007 for MPH, and 0.003 ± 0.002 for NS.

For cortical and subcortical volumes, individuals with ADHD exhibited non-significant decreases in 9 regions, including the right temporal pole, right cuneus, right inferior temporal, right lingual, left middle temporal, left pars triangularis, left superior parietal, left amygdala, and right putamen (Figs. 4C and 5C-D, Supplemental Fig. 5). In addition, ADHD was associated with non-significant increases in 5 regions, including the right banks of the superior temporal sulcus, left inferior parietal, left insula, left posterior cingulate, and right accumbens area (Figs. 4C and 5C-D, Supplemental Fig. 5). All three medication classes showed opposite directional associations to ADHD in 6 ROIs: right inferior temporal, right lingual, left middle temporal, and right temporal pole where ADHD was negative (ADHD < Controls) and AMP, MPH, and NS were all positive (Use > No Use); right banks of the superior temporal sulcus and left inferior parietal where ADHD was positive (ADHD > Controls) and AMP, MPH, and NS were all negative (Use < No Use). In 7 ROIs (right cuneus, left insula, left pars triangularis, left posterior cingulate, right accumbens area, left amygdala, and right putamen), medications showed mixed associations, where at least one medication class opposed the ADHD association and at least one aligned with it. In the left superior parietal, none of the three medication classes showed opposite directional associations to ADHD. Overall, AMP use showed opposite directional associations to ADHD in 9 of 14 ROIs, MPH use in 11 of 14 ROIs, and NS use in 9 of 14 ROIs. Compared to cortical thickness and surface area measures, the volume ROIs—particularly subcortical ROIs—displayed less consistent directional opposition between ADHD and medication associations (Figs. 4C and 5C-D, Supplemental Fig. 5). Across these cortical and subcortical volume ROIs, the average absolute standardized beta coefficients were 0.012 ± 0.007 for ADHD, 0.014 ± 0.011 for AMP, 0.016 ± 0.008 for MPH, and 0.005 ± 0.005 for NS.

Significant stimulant effects in cortical regions

After false discovery rate (FDR) correction, significant effects were observed only for AMP and MPH use in cortical regions (denoted with a star in Fig. 5). Both stimulants were associated with significantly reduced surface area in the right banks of the superior temporal sulcus (opposing the positive ADHD association) and the left posterior cingulate (Fig. 5B). Additionally, AMP use was linked to significantly decreased left posterior cingulate volume (opposing the positive ADHD association) and MPH use to significantly increased right temporal pole volume (opposing the negative ADHD association) (Fig. 5A and C). No effects of ADHD status or NS use reached significance after FDR correction. Additionally, no significant effects on subcortical volumes were observed after FDR correction.

Analysis 3: LME sensitivity analyses

After excluding ADHD+RX participants who did not meet KSADS-COMP criteria for ADHD from our LME sample, our sensitivity sample (N = 8472) included 409 ADHD+RX, 540 ADHD-noRX, and 7523 Control subjects. Within the sensitivity sample, there was once again no evidence of problematic collinearity; VIF values of 1.54 for ADHD, 1.19 for AMP, 1.35 for MPH, and 1.13 for NS in our sensitivity models.

Standardized beta coefficients with confidence intervals for ADHD, AMP, MPH, and NS from sensitivity models are plotted in Supplemental Fig. 7. Overwhelmingly, the results from the sensitivity sample were similar to the primary results. The only significant findings after FDR-correction remained the same as identified in the primary analyses: (1) decreased surface area in right banks of the superior temporal sulcus for both AMP and MPH, (2) decreased surface area in the left posterior cingulate for both AMP and MPH, (3) decreased left posterior cingulate volume for only AMP, and (4) increased volume of the right temporal pole for only MPH.

Discussion

This study is among the first to investigate associations between brain structure and ADHD medication use in a large, demographically diverse sample. By integrating machine learning to identify medication-sensitive regions with linear mixed-effects regression modelling, we observe opposing associations between ADHD status and medication use in primarily parietal, occipital, and temporal regions. Our analytic approach is further strengthened by a theoretically grounded framework that allows us to disentangle the distinct effects of different ADHD medication classes while allowing for the polypharmacy (e.g., AMP + NS or MPH + NS) observed in real-world populations.

Opposing medication and ADHD associations in cortical morphology

First, our xAI approach identified cortical features distinguishing medicated from unmedicated ADHD participants exclusively—deliberately omitting Control group data from this discovery phase. Second, we used parametric testing to measure whether those selected regions exhibited (1) ADHD-Control differences and (2) associations with the three ADHD medication classes. Because the initial ML feature identification occurred without knowledge of Control data, our finding that medication-linked regions also demonstrated opposing ADHD-Control effects do not stem from analytical circularity. Conducting ML feature identification based on ADHD vs. Control classification and then running LME models could lead to “double dipping,” increasing the risk of false positives; therefore, we did not employ this approach.

When accounting for medication use, we found no significant ADHD effects across the cortical and subcortical features examined, consistent with prior meta-analysis14. Rather than relying solely on significance, we explored directional patterns of ADHD effects in brain morphology. This revealed small, bidirectional trends across cortical features relative to Controls, highlighting the absence of a global ADHD effect at this age. These exploratory patterns provide essential context for our medication findings; focusing solely on statistical significance risks conveying the misleading impression that medications are independently associated with structural brain differences in the absence of any ADHD-related alterations.

As previously mentioned, the attenuation hypothesis posits that medication may partially remediate or normalize ADHD-related brain differences, but remains debated due to limitations such as cross-sectional designs20. Our findings showing that medication associations oppose the direction of ADHD-Control differences provide correlational support for this attenuation framework. AMP and MPH showed largely parallel effects across cortical regions for thickness, surface area, and volume, with NS demonstrating similar but weaker attenuation patterns. Crucially, while this convergence between medication associations and subthreshold ADHD-Control differences aligns with attenuation theory, our observational study design precludes causal inference. We cannot determine how medication use contributes to these structural differences; pre-existing brain characteristics may influence medication response or adherence, and unmeasured confounds may also account for these associations.

The cortical regions identified as medication-sensitive were primarily located within the parietal, occipital, and temporal lobes, with a relative paucity of findings in the frontal lobe despite long-standing hypotheses about the role of prefrontal regions in ADHD pathology15,16. One possibility is that prefrontal regions continue to exhibit ADHD-related structural differences but are not strongly associated with medication status during preadolescence; alternatively, their extended developmental trajectory into early adulthood53,54 may confer age-specific periods of sensitivity.

Directional patterns for medication associations were not observed in subcortical volumes, where medication effects appeared more variable. Prior ABCD Study research by Wu et al. (2024)55 identified differences in right insula thickness and left nucleus accumbens volume between stimulant-treated individuals with low ADHD symptoms (N = 273) and unmedicated individuals with ADHD. However, these regions were not identified as feature targets in our study, potentially due to differences in analytic approach or the broader scope of our investigation—such as disaggregating stimulant classes, including a larger number of stimulant users, and examining both stimulant and nonstimulant medication effects. Additionally, in a small ABCD Study subsample, Kaminski et al. (2024)56 reported that stimulant use was associated with altered functional connectivity between striatal regions and several large-scale brain networks, including the cingulo-opercular, salience, fronto-parietal, cingulo-parietal, somatomotor, dorsal attention, ventral attention, and visual networks. While our ML approach identified two striatal regions (the right putamen and right accumbens area), we found no significant effects of ADHD or medication use, as well as no consistent pattern in associations. Despite the basal ganglia’s frequent implication in ADHD pathogenesis15,16, we did not observe evidence of altered striatal region structure during preadolescence; alternatively, such effects may not lead to such morphological differences detectable in the present study. This may reflect individual heterogeneity in dopaminergic pathways, the prolonged developmental trajectories of subcortical regions, or limitations of current imaging modalities.

Do stimulants overcompensate?

Across medication-sensitive brain regions, the average absolute effects of AMP and MPH on cortical thickness, surface area, cortical volume, and subcortical volume were larger than those associated with ADHD. Interestingly, several cortical regions—such as the right banks of the superior temporal sulcus, right temporal pole, and the left posterior cingulate—showed significant effects of AMP and/or MPH. In the banks of the superior temporal sulcus and temporal pole, these effects were in the direction of attenuation, suggesting a reduction in non-significant ADHD-Control group differences. However, in these regions, the magnitude of stimulant-related effects exceeded those associated with ADHD diagnosis, raising the possibility of overcompensation. NS medications exhibited similar directional effects to stimulants but with smaller magnitudes, consistent with their pharmacological profile—namely, their primary action on noradrenergic pathways and downstream engagement of dopaminergic pathways57. This also aligns with clinical evidence indicating that NSs generally yield more modest improvements in ADHD symptoms5.

Bernanke et al. (2022)58, using data from the same wave of the ABCD Study, identified reduced posterior cingulate surface area as the largest ADHD-related structural difference, but they did not account for ADHD medication use. In contrast, our study found significant reductions in left posterior cingulate surface area associated with both amphetamine (AMP) and methylphenidate (MPH) use, while the effect of ADHD in this region was negative and near zero. Thus, we speculate that stimulants may be associated with de novo differences in the posterior cingulate, raising the possibility that previously reported differences may partly reflect medication effects rather than intrinsic ADHD-related neuroanatomy. However, these associations could also reflect pre-existing neurobiological ADHD differences that influence stimulant treatment selection or maintenance rather than direct medication effects (e.g., confounding by indication, selection bias). Randomized control trials and longitudinal studies are better suited to explore this question. Additionally, we observed that AMP use was associated with a significantly reduced posterior cingulate volume and ADHD with a non-significant volume increase, suggesting opposing volumetric effects may be primarily driven by surface area reduction rather than cortical thinning. Given that total surface area peaks during preadolescence, this morphometric feature may represent a particularly sensitive target for pharmacologic effects during this developmental period54.

AMP exhibited a significant negative association with posterior cingulate volume, whereas MPH exhibited a significant positive association with right temporal pole volume. This may reflect pharmacodynamic differences; some literature suggests AMP leads to greater cytosolic dopamine accumulation than MPH59. The spatial distribution of the AMP-only effect (e.g., posterior cingulate volume) aligns with a region of relatively higher dopamine transporter (DAT) density, whereas the MPH-only effect (e.g., right temporal pole) may relate to its weak serotonergic properties (i.e., 5-HT1A agonism)31. In contrast, stimulant effects observed in the superior temporal sulcus could be driven more by noradrenergic pathways, given its higher relative expression of norepinephrine transporter (NET)31. Altogether, these findings emphasize the need to account for medications as key confounding variables in future ADHD neuroimaging research.

Strengths and limitations

Our flexible LME modelling approach enabled us to isolate the unique effects of ADHD, AMP, MPH, and NS medications. Unlike conventional approaches (e.g., grouping participants into mutually exclusive medication categories, aggregating stimulants, or excluding individuals with ADHD polypharmacy), our framework accommodates overlapping medication use. This approach preserves sample size and more accurately reflects real-world treatment patterns, thereby enhancing interpretability and ecological validity. Additionally, our xAI framework used a data-driven feature selection approach to identify candidate neuroanatomical targets of medications. To our knowledge, this is the first structural MRI study to model major ADHD medication classes concurrently by disaggregating stimulant types and including nonstimulant effects, enabling identification of both common and distinct neuroanatomical associations—a critical advancement in precision psychiatric medicine.

Yet, several limitations warrant consideration. First, our cross-sectional design cannot establish temporal precedence between brain structure and medication exposure, precluding causal inference. However, the large, demographically diverse ABCD cohort offers an unparalleled opportunity to investigate medication-associated neuroanatomical variation under naturalistic conditions, including imperfect adherence. Second, our analysis focuses on a single developmental timepoint (ages 9–10 years). Given hypotheses that ADHD reflects developmental delays, longitudinal studies are needed to assess how ADHD- and medication-related neuroanatomical patterns evolve across later developmental stages. Third, our xAI approach may fail to identify ADHD-pertinent regions that are relatively medication-insensitive. Fourth, our classification captures all individuals prescribed ADHD medication, though few cases may reflect community overdiagnosis or misdiagnosis. The ABCD Study does not survey medication history prior to enrollment; therefore, lifetime exposure duration is unknown, and some ADHD-noRX participants may have prior medication exposure. We did not examine potential moderating effects of medication dosage, symptom severity, familial ADHD, or behavioral outcomes, which are key priorities for future research. Similarly, we do not consider behavioral interventions, another established ADHD treatment. Finally, due to limited sample size, we analyzed NS as a single class rather than disaggregating pharmacologically distinct alpha-agonists and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors.

Conclusions

Our study employed an innovative approach to characterizing associations between ADHD medications and preadolescent cortical and subcortical brain structure, identifying novel medication-related associations. We contribute several key findings to the field: (1) AMP and MPH were associated with patterns broadly consistent with attenuation of ADHD-related effects in the cortex, and (2) NS exhibit similar but weaker effects than those of stimulants. Additional longitudinal research is needed to investigate whether stimulants may be associated with (3) overcompensation in specific regions of the cortex, particularly in the posterior cingulate, banks of the superior temporal sulcus, and temporal pole, which may be related to their roles in dopaminergic and noradrenergic pathways.
