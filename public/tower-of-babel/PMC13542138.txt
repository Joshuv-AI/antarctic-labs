Knowledge and attitude toward chronic kidney disease prevention and associated factors among diabetic patients in Bahir Dar city, northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Bekalu Mekonen Belay, Henok Biresaw Netsere, Zemenu Addis Alem, Yirgalem Abere, Mengistu Ewunetu, Emiru Ayalew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic kidney disease is a major health and economic burden, especially in developing countries, yet evidence on diabetic patients’ knowledge and attitudes toward its prevention is limited. To assess knowledge and attitude toward chronic kidney disease prevention and their associated factors among diabetic patients. An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 386 adult diabetic patients attending public hospitals in Bahir Dar city using systematic random sampling after proportional allocation. Data were collected via face-to-face interviews, entered into EpiData for management and quality control, and analyzed using SPSS. Bi-variable and multivariable logistic regression were used to identify factors associated with knowledge and attitude toward CKD prevention, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Overall, 33.9% of participants had adequate knowledge and 49.7% had a favorable attitude toward chronic kidney disease prevention. Higher educational level (AOR = 4.50; 95% CI 2.20–9.30), longer duration of diabetes (AOR = 3.30; 95% CI 1.57–6.96), younger age (21–30 years) (AOR = 2.60; 95% CI 1.11–5.87), and heard about chronic kidney disease (AOR = 5.50; 95% CI 2.71–11.01) were significantly associated with adequate knowledge. Favorable attitude was significantly associated with higher educational level (AOR = 5.90; 95% CI 2.80–12.42), family history of chronic kidney disease (AOR = 3.00; 95% CI 1.60–5.76), and heard of chronic kidney disease (AOR = 1.70; 95% CI 1.03–2.78). Only 33.9% of diabetic patients had adequate knowledge of chronic kidney disease prevention, and 49.7% had a favorable attitude. Higher education and prior awareness of chronic kidney disease were consistently associated with better knowledge and attitude, along with clinical and demographic factors such as diabetes duration, age, and family history. The findings indicate important gaps in chronic kidney disease prevention knowledge and attitude among diabetic patients. Targeted health education within diabetic follow-up services is recommended.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a major global public health problem, characterized by a steadily rising prevalence and an increasing contribution to mortality, while elevated fasting plasma glucose remains one of the major modifiable risk factors for its development and progression1. Management focuses on early detection, prevention, and treatment of underlying causes to slow disease progression2. Preventive strategies include identifying high-risk individuals, promoting lifestyle modification, ensuring medication adherence, and early CKD detection to reduce the disease burden3–5. The CDC and expert panels recommend addressing knowledge gaps about CKD prevention6. With increasing prevalence and incidence, CKD is a growing public health concern worldwide, associated with high healthcare costs and poor outcomes7.

In Ethiopia, CKD is an emerging public health problem, with prevalence ranging from 21.8% to 35.5%8,9 and a mortality burden of 30.9% among hemodialysis patients10, showing a consistent increase in death rates11. However, most patients with renal impairment remain unaware of CKD until approximately 90% of kidney function is lost12. Patients with chronic conditions such as diabetes need adequate knowledge and positive attitudes toward CKD prevention13. lack of knowledge and unfavorable attitudes may reduce motivation for self-management behaviors critical to maintaining kidney health14. Evidence indicates substantial gaps in knowledge and attitudes toward CKD prevention among patients with diabetes mellitus (DM). In Malaysia, 77.0% of DM patients had poor knowledge and 58.2% had a poor attitude toward CKD prevention15. Similarly, in Palestine, only about half of DM patients demonstrated adequate knowledge of CKD prevention16. In Ethiopia, a study conducted in Asella reported that 61.6% of patients with type 2 DM had a good preventive attitude toward CKD17. These findings highlight considerable variability knowledge and attitudes across settings.

Despite the high burden of CKD among patients with DM in Ethiopia, evidence on patients’ knowledge and attitudes toward CKD prevention remains limited in Bahir Dar city, Northwest Ethiopia. As a major referral hub for surrounding districts, Bahir Dar serves a heterogeneous population from both urban and rural areas, enhancing the representativeness of the study. In addition, the availability of organized diabetic follow-up services provides a practical setting for systematically assessing patients and generating reliable evidence. Therefore, this study aimed to assess knowledge, attitudes, and associated factors among diabetic patients to inform targeted CKD prevention strategies for clinicians and policymakers.

Methods and materials

Study design, study area and study period

An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted among diabetic patients on follow-up care in Bahir Dar city, Northwest Ethiopia, from March 20 to April 20, 2023 GC.

Bahir Dar City is the capital of the Amhara National Regional State, located near Lake Tana at an altitude of 1840 m above sea level and approximately 565 km northwest of Addis Ababa, Ethiopia18. The city has a total of fourteen public health facilities, including eleven health centers, one primary hospital, and two specialized referral hospitals19. The hospitals are Tibebe Ghion Specialized Hospital (TGSH), Felege Hiwot Referral Hospital (FHRH), and Addis Alem Primary Hospital (AAPH).

All public health facilities provide outpatient and inpatient services, as well as chronic follow-up care, including diabetic clinics. While all facilities offer diabetic follow-up services, CKD diagnostic services—such as serum creatinine testing and urine analysis—are available in the three hospitals. However, comprehensive CKD treatment services are only provided in the two specialized referral hospitals.

Population

All DM patients in Bahir Dar City public hospitals under chronic follow-up clinics were the source population.

Whereas, selected DM patients from chronic follow-up clinics in Bahir Dar City public hospitals were the study population, all DM patients with at least three visits to chronic follow-up clinics were included in the study. Severely ill DM patients who couldn’t participate in the interview and diabetes patients with known psychiatric illnesses were excluded.

The sample size was calculated using a single population proportion formula, assuming a prevalence of adequate knowledge about CKD prevention of 36.5%20, with a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error.

Study variables

The main outcome variables were knowledge and attitude toward CKD prevention. Independent variables included socio-demographic factors (age, gender, marital status, residence, health insurance, occupation, education, and monthly income), clinical characteristics (type of diabetes, duration of treatment, comorbidities, renal function, blood glucose level, and family history of CKD), and sources of information (media, health professionals, family, and friends).

Operational definition

Adequate knowledge is defined as participant scores more than 70% on knowledge questions and Favorable attitude is defined as a participant scores more than 70% on attitude questions13,21.

The average fasting blood glucose (FBS) level ranges from 70 to 130 mg/dl referred to as good controlled, and poor controlled blood glucose is defined as an average FBS of > 130 mg/dl or < 70 mg/dl22.

Social support was assessed using the Oslo 3-item Social Support Scale (OSS-3). Scores are summed across the three items to produce a total ranging from 3 to 14, with higher scores indicating greater support. Participants were categorized as having poor3–8, moderate9,10, or strong11–13 social support24.

Data collection tools and procedures

A structured questionnaire was used to collect data. It was originally developed in English, translated into Amharic, and back-translated to English by a language expert to ensure consistency. The questionnaire included sections on socio-demographic characteristics, clinical factors, and sources of information, adapted from previous studies20,25. Social support was assessed using the Oslo Social Support Scale26. Knowledge and attitude items were adopted from the validated CKD Screening Index13,20,27. Knowledge was measured using a series of dichotomous (yes/no) questions, where each correct response was assigned a score of 1 and each incorrect response a score of 0. The total knowledge score for each participant was calculated by summing the item scores and changed percentage. Attitude was assessed using a 5-point Likert scale, with response options ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The total attitude score was obtained by summing the scores of the individual items and then changed to percentage.

Glycemic control was assessed using fasting blood glucose (FBG) measurements obtained from patients’ medical records. For each participant, the average FBG level was calculated from the three most recent consecutive FBG measurements recorded during routine follow-up visits.

Data were collected by three BSc nurses and supervised by one MSc nurse. Participants were interviewed, and their medical records were reviewed for clinical factors during medication refills or medical checkups at chronic follow-up clinics. Data collectors and supervisors received one day of training. The questionnaire was pretested on 5% of the sample (20 participants) at the University of Gondar Teaching Hospital to assess clarity and consistency. Reliability testing during the pretest yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.807 for the knowledge questionnaire and 0.762 for the attitude scale.

Statistical analysis and presentation

Collected data were checked for completeness, entered into EpiData version 4.6, and exported to SPSS version 26 for analysis. Negatively worded items (Items 608 and 611) were reverse-coded for consistency. Variables with p < 0.25 in bi-variable analysis were included in multivariable logistic regression. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and no multicollinearity was detected. Model fitness was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (p > 0.05). Multivariable logistic regression was performed using the enter method, with significance set at p < 0.05. Results were presented using tables, graphs, and descriptive statistics (percentages and means).

Factors associated with knowledge about CKD prevention

In bivariate analysis, younger age, higher education, health insurance, strong social support, prior awareness of CKD, family history of CKD, type of diabetes, and longer DM duration were associated with adequate knowledge about CKD prevention (p < 0.25), while patients with comorbidities were less likely to have adequate knowledge. In multivariable analysis, college or higher education, heard of CKD, age 21–30 years, and diabetes duration > 10 years were independent predictors (p < 0.05).

Participants with college or higher education were 4.5 times more likely to have adequate knowledge than those with no formal education (95% CI: 2.20–9.30). Those who had ever heard about CKD were 5.5 times more likely to have adequate knowledge than those who had not (95% CI: 2.71–11.01). Participants aged 21–30 years were 2.6 times more likely to have adequate knowledge than those over 50 years (95% CI: 1.11–5.87), and those with diabetes duration > 10 years were 3.3 times more likely to have adequate knowledge compared to those with < 5 years duration (95% CI: 1.57–6.96) (Table 5).

Factors associate with attitude towards CKD prevention

In bivariate analysis (p < 0.25), age, educational level, family history of CKD, heard of CKD, social support, type of diabetes, and comorbidity status were associated with favorable attitudes toward CKD prevention. In multivariable analysis (p < 0.05), higher education (college or above), family history of CKD, and prior awareness of CKD were independent predictors of a favorable attitude.

Participants with college or higher education were 5.9 times more likely to have a favorable attitude compared to those with no formal education (95% CI: 2.80–12.42). Those with a family history of CKD were 3.0 times more likely to have a favorable attitude than those without (95% CI: 1.60–5.76), and participants who had ever heard about CKD were 1.7 times more likely to have a favorable attitude than those who had not (95% CI: 1.03–2.78) (Table 6).

Discussion

In this study, 33.9% (95% CI: 29.3%–39.1%) of diabetic patients had adequate knowledge about CKD prevention. This is consistent with findings from Rwanda (35.6%)28. Conversely, the result is higher than a survey in Malaysia (23%)15. which may reflect differences in study setting. This underscores the need for targeted health education to improve CKD prevention knowledge among DM patients29.

In this study, 49.7% (95% CI: 44.6%–54.7%) of DM patients had a favorable attitude toward CKD prevention. This is higher than the study conducted in Malaysia (42.8%)15. This highlights the need to improve DM patients’ attitudes toward CKD prevention30.

Multivariable analysis showed that educational status was significantly associated with adequate knowledge about CKD prevention. Participants with college or higher education were 4.5 times more likely to have adequate knowledge than those with no formal education, consistent with studies from Tanzania31. This may be because higher education enables individuals to access health information from books, pamphlets, healthcare professionals, and social media32. This underscores the importance of targeting educational interventions to improve CKD knowledge among less-educated diabetic patients33.

The study also showed that patients with a longer duration of DM (> 10 years) were 3.3 times more likely to have adequate knowledge of CKD prevention compared with those with a shorter duration (> 5 years). This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted in Rwanda and north-eastern Ethiopia28,34. A longer duration of diabetes may increase exposure to CKD-related information through repeated clinical visits. This suggests that prolonged engagement with healthcare services and health education enhances patients’ knowledge of CKD prevention35,36.

Participants aged 21–30 years were 2.6 times more likely to have adequate knowledge than those older than 50 years, consistent with studies from Palestine16. Younger adults may have greater exposure to mass media and information technologies, such as the Internet, and are generally more receptive to new information37. This suggests that younger adults have better access to health information and higher health literacy, underscoring the need for targeted CKD prevention education among older age groups38.

This study found that participants who had ever heard about chronic kidney disease (CKD) were 5.5 times more likely to have adequate knowledge of CKD prevention compared to those who had never heard of it. To the best of our knowledge, there is limited or no prior evidence directly examining this specific association, making it difficult to draw direct comparisons. This might be because accessing health information may increase knowledge of CKD prevention. This highlights that prior awareness of CKD strongly improves preventive knowledge, emphasizing the need for broad CKD awareness and health education initiatives39,40.

Participants with a college education or higher were 5.9 times more likely to have a favorable attitude toward CKD prevention compared with those without formal education, consistent with studies in Palestine16, and Malaysia15. This may be because higher education enhances health literacy and health-seeking behaviors, leading to more favorable attitudes toward CKD prevention41,42, highlighting the need for tailored educational interventions for individuals with lower educational levels43.

This finding indicates that participants with a family history of CKD were significantly more likely to exhibit a favorable attitude toward CKD prevention compared to those without such a history. Although direct comparative studies on attitude toward CKD prevention are limited, this observation is consistent with broader evidence in CKD research44. A plausible explanation is that prior exposure to CKD within the family may increase individuals’ awareness of the disease’s severity, complications, and long-term burden, thereby fostering a more preventive-oriented mindset. Personal or familial experience with illness often enhances risk perception and motivates engagement in preventive behaviors, including lifestyle modification and adherence to medical advice45.

Furthermore, this study demonstrated that individuals who had ever heard about CKD were 1.7 times more likely to have a favorable attitude toward CKD prevention compared with those who had never heard about it. Although there is limited prior research directly examining this association, the finding is conceptually plausible. Awareness of CKD may shape individuals’ perceptions and beliefs about the disease, thereby promoting more favorable attitudes toward its prevention. This highlights the critical role of public health education and awareness campaigns in fostering positive attitudes and encouraging preventive behaviors46,47.

Strength and limitation

The study’s strengths include the use of primary data and a multicenter cross-sectional design, which enhances the representativeness of the findings. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, admitted patients with diabetes mellitus were not included, which may limit the generalizability of the results to all diabetic populations. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships between variables. In addition, the relatively novel nature of some variables assessed in this study resulted in limited availability of directly comparable literature, thereby constraining the depth of contextual comparison in the discussion section.

Conclusions

This study found that only one-third of diabetic patients had adequate knowledge of CKD prevention, and approximately half had a favorable attitude toward CKD prevention in Bahir Dar city, Northwest Ethiopia. Educational status, duration of diabetes, younger age, and prior exposure to information about CKD were significantly associated with adequate knowledge. In addition, higher educational level, family history of CKD, and having heard about CKD were significantly associated with a favorable attitude.

These findings indicate important gaps in knowledge and suboptimal attitudes toward CKD prevention among diabetic patients. Strengthening targeted health education and counseling within diabetic follow-up clinics, particularly for patients with lower educational status and limited prior exposure to CKD information, is recommended. Further longitudinal studies are needed to better understand these associations.
