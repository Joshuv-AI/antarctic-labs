Comparative genomics reveals the evolution of anthraquinone biosynthesis in Polygonaceae
Authors: Qian Cheng, Fanbo Meng, Xiuping Yang, Xiaoming Song, Wei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPolygonaceae is a globally distributed angiosperm family rich in anthraquinones (AQs), which contribute to diverse medicinal properties and species-specific chemical defenses. Despite their ecological and pharmacological significance, the evolutionary dynamics and transcriptional regulation of AQ biosynthesis genes in Polygonaceae remain largely unexplored.ResultsIn this study, we combined comparative genomics and multi-omics analyses to investigate these processes. Phylogenomic reconstruction indicated that Polygonaceae diverged from its sister family approximately 66.26 million years ago. A total of 206 polyketide synthase (PKS) genes were identified, with lineage-specific tandem duplication driving substantial expansion. Polyketide biosynthetic gene clusters with dense PKS aggregation were detected in three species, primarily shaped by tandem duplication. By integrating gene expression and synteny analyses, candidate PKS genes likely involved in AQ biosynthesis were identified. Multi-level gene regulatory networks constructed from root transcriptomes of Fallopia multiflora across different growth years revealed hierarchical regulatory relationships and divergence among candidate PKS genes. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis further identified co-expression modules and transcription factors potentially associated with AQ biosynthesis, while machine learning approaches prioritized transcription factors regulating the glycosyltransferase involved in AQ modification.ConclusionsThese results reveal the evolutionary expansion, diversification, and functional specialization of PKS genes, as well as the hierarchical transcriptional regulation underlying AQ biosynthesis in Polygonaceae. The identified candidate genes and regulatory networks provide a valuable resource for metabolic engineering and molecular breeding strategies aimed at enhancing medicinal AQ production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-13060-8.

Background

Polygonaceae is a globally distributed angiosperm family comprising approximately 50 genera and 1,200 species [1]. Members in this family are of considerable economic and ecological significance, including key grain crops (e.g., Fagopyrum esculentum) [2], natural dye plants (e.g., Persicaria tinctoria) [3], iconic alpine plants (e.g., Rheum nobile) [4, 5], arctic pioneer species (e.g., Oxyria digyna) [6], and traditional Chinese medicinal plants (e.g., Fallopia multiflora) [7]. A key class of bioactive compounds in Polygonaceae is anthraquinones (AQs), which are responsible for laxative effects [8–10]. In addition to their laxative effects, these compounds exhibit diverse pharmacological activities, such as hepatoprotective, nephroprotective, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antibacterial, and antitumor effects [8]. In plants, AQs contribute to alleviating light-induced stress [11, 12] and enhance resistance to diseases and pests [13, 14], thereby supporting adaptation to complex and heterogeneous environments.

Two biosynthetic pathways for AQs have been reported, namely the polyketide pathway producing emodin-type AQs and the shikimate pathway responsible for alizarin-type AQs [8, 15]. Polygonaceae species predominantly synthesize emodin-type AQs via the polyketide pathway, which are characterized by two hydroxyl groups at the C-1 and C-8 positions of the tricyclic aromatic ring [16]. In this pathway, type III polyketide synthases (PKSs) catalyze the condensation of acyl-coenzyme A substrates to form an octaketide chain [17]. This linear intermediate subsequently undergoes cyclization, reduction, aldol condensation, dehydration, enolization, and decarboxylation reactions to generate AQ precursors [8]. Subsequent oxidative, methylation, and glycosylation modifications are catalyzed by cytochrome P450 (CYP), O-methyltransferase (OMT) [18, 19], and UDP-dependent glycosyltransferase (UGT) [20, 21], respectively, producing structurally diverse AQ derivatives. Despite their biological and medicinal significance, the genes and regulatory mechanisms underlying AQ biosynthesis in Polygonaceae remain incompletely characterized.

Biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) are physically clustered groups of functionally related genes involved in secondary metabolite biosynthesis, which play key roles in coordinated metabolic regulation [22, 23]. While numerous BGCs have been characterized, such as the c4 BGC for momilactone biosynthesis in Oryza sativa [24], the UGT-SCPL BGC for leonurine in Leonurus japonicus [22], and the 6OMT-CYP80B-4′OMT cluster for benzylisoquinoline alkaloid in Houttuynia cordata [25], polyketide BGCs have been predominantly identified in bacteria and fungi [17]. In Polygonaceae, the only preliminary evidence of polyketide BGCs has been reported in Rheum officinale [26] and F. multiflora [27], limiting our understanding of their formation mechanisms and biological functions. Analyses of MADS-box BGCs have shown that tandem duplication (TD), nonallelic homologous recombination, selection pressure, and gene transposition are major drivers of BGCs formation [28]. The diversity of AQ composition and abundance among Polygonaceae species provides an excellent system to investigate the origin and evolution of polyketide BGCs.

Transcription factors (TFs) play central roles in regulating gene expression, influencing plant growth, stress responses, and the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites [29, 30]. In Rheum palmatum, multiple TF families involved in the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites were identified among 473 significantly expanded gene families [31]. Specifically, MYB [32, 33], bZIP [34], and WRKY [35] TFs have been proposed as regulators of AQ biosynthesis in Polygonaceae. In recent years, machine learning methods have been successfully applied to screen key TFs [36], providing powerful new tools for dissecting complex transcriptional regulatory networks underlying secondary metabolic processes. Advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies have enabled the assembly of genomes for several Polygonaceae species, including medicinal species such as F. multiflora [37], R. palmatum [31], and P. tinctoria [38]. Together with transcriptomic and metabolomic datasets, natural compounds repositories [39, 40], specialized medicinal compounds databases [41], and plant genome databases [42] provide a solid foundation for phylogenetic reconstruction, identification of secondary metabolite biosynthesis genes, and analysis of BGCs. Recent studies integrating multi-omics data at the family level, such as investigations of terpenoid BGCs in Euphorbiaceae [43] and Asteraceae [44], have established a novel research framework for Herbgenomics [45]. However, despite the increasing availability of genomic and multi-omics resources for Polygonaceae, systematic investigations of the evolutionary trajectories of AQ biosynthesis genes and their associated polyketide BGCs remain limited at the family level. Moreover, the transcriptional regulatory networks governing AQ biosynthesis and downstream modification have not yet been comprehensively explored.

In this study, we performed comparative genomic analyses using genomes of 10 representative Polygonaceae species from five genera, selected based on data quality and accessibility, aiming to reconstruct phylogenetic relationships within the family and to characterize the evolutionary patterns of PKS genes across 10 species. By integrating multi-omics data, we examined the evolutionary dynamics of polyketide BGCs and identified candidate PKS genes potentially involved in AQ biosynthesis. Additionally, we applied multi-level gene regulatory network (GRN), weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), and machine-learning models to identify genes and TF genes that may regulate AQ biosynthesis in F. multiflora. Collectively, our findings provide new insights into the evolution and transcriptional regulation of AQ biosynthesis genes in Polygonaceae and offer a valuable reference for understanding phylogenetic diversification and environmental adaptation in this family.

Materials and methods

Phylogenetic construction and divergence time estimation

Based on phylogenetic position, ecological adaptability, metabolic diversity, and data quality and availability, we selected 10 representative Polygonaceae species from five genera (Table S1). In addition, five species from Caryophyllales were included (three from Amaranthaceae, one from Cactaceae, and one from Simmondsiaceae). One superrosid species and one monocot species were further incorporated as outgroups. Single-copy orthologous groups (OGs) were identified from whole-genome assemblies of all 17 species (Table S1) using OrthoFinder (v 3.1.0) [46]. Phylogenetic trees were reconstructed based on 95 single-copy OGs using the maximum likelihood method implemented in IQ-TREE (v 3.0.1) [47]. Divergence times were estimated using MCMCtree in PAML (v 4.10.7) [48, 49]. To ensure the reliability of divergence time estimation, the following parameters were applied: burn-in = 10,000, sampling = 2, and samples = 100,000 [50]. In addition, 12 fossil calibration points (Table S2) obtained from the TimeTree (https://timetree.org/home) [51] were used for time calibration. Gene family expansion and contraction were analyzed using CAFÉ (v 5.1.0) [52] on the basis of all OGs identified by OrthoFinder. Functional divergence of expanded gene families was evaluated by Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses using the R package clusterProfiler [53], with a significance threshold of P < 0.05.

AQ biosynthesis-related gene identification and analysis

To identify key genes involved in the polyketide pathway and AQ modification (Table S3), including PKS, polyketide cyclase (PKC), UGT, caffeoyl-CoA O-methyltransferase (CCoAOMT), OMT, and CYP, hidden Markov models (HMMs) corresponding to these gene families were obtained from the InterPro database [54, 55]. Protein sequences from the 17 species were then searched against these HMMs using HMMER (v 3.3.2) [56], and candidate genes from the six gene families were identified using an E-value < 1 × 10⁻⁵. TF genes in F. multiflora were identified using the PlantTFDB (https://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/prediction.php), which classifies TF genes into 58 families. To classify candidate genes, protein sequences of previously characterized PKS, UGT, and CCoAOMT genes were collected from the UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb), TAIR (https://www.arabidopsis.org/), the UGT Nomenclature Committee (https://labs.wsu.edu/ugt/), and related publications (Tables S4-S6; Files S1-S3). Multiple sequence alignments were performed using MAFFT (v 7.505) [57] with the auto strategy, based on a combined dataset including both HMM-identified candidates and reference sequences, separately for PKS, UGT, and CCoAOMT. Poorly aligned regions were trimmed using TrimAL (v 1.4.1) [58] with the gappyout option. Phylogenetic relationships were inferred from the trimmed alignments using the maximum likelihood method implemented in IQ-TREE (v 2.1.4) [47]. Phylogenetic trees were visualized using the iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/). Gene duplication patterns, including whole-genome duplication (WGD), TD, proximal duplication (PD), transposed duplication (TRD), and dispersed duplication (DSD), were identified using DupGen_finder (v 1.0.0) [59].

Prediction and synteny analysis of polyketide BGCs

Potential polyketide BGCs were predicted from genome assemblies and annotations of 10 Polygonaceae species (Table S1) using PlantiSMASH (v 2.0.1) [60]. Homologous genes were identified using BLASTP (E-value < 1 × 10⁻⁵), and syntenic blocks were detected using WGDI (v 0.74) [61] with the -d and -kd options selected. Synteny relationships and BGC evolution were visualized using the MCScan (Python version) from the JCVI library [62].

Transcriptome analysis and AQ biosynthesis-related genes expression profiling

Transcriptome datasets of F. multiflora, F. multiflora var. angulata, R. palmatum, R. tanguticum, R. nobile, F. tataricum were obtained from the NCBI and CNCB databases (Tables S7-S16). Raw reads were quality-filtered using Trimmomatic (v 0.39) [63], and assessed using FastQC with default parameters. Clean reads were aligned to the corresponding reference genomes (Table S7) using Hisat2 (v 2.2.1) [64], and alignment files were generated using Samtools (v 1.20) [65]. Gene expression levels were quantified using FeatureCounts (v 2.1.1) [65] and normalized using the Transcripts Per Million (TPM) measurement. Expression patterns across tissues and growth years were visualized using ggplot2 in R.

Construction of a multi-level GRN based on partial correlation coefficient

The bottom-up graphic Gaussian model (bottom-up GGM) was applied to construct a multi-level GRN based on biological pathways [66]. Based on previous studies [44, 67], more stringent parameters were applied to construct the multi-level GRN. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in roots of F. multiflora across different growth years were identified using DESeq2 (Padj < 0.05, |log₂FoldChange| > 1) [68]. GO annotation was performed as described above, and GO categories containing PKS genes and “secondary metabolite biosynthetic process” were selected.

Genes in the selected pathways were assigned to the bottom layer, whereas regulatory genes were assigned to upper layers. Pearson correlation coefficients (CC) were calculated between gene pairs; pairs with CC > 0.90 were considered potentially co-regulated. Differentially expressed TF genes were introduced into upper layers. Iteratively, if partial correlation coefficient (PCC) < 0.15, the TF was considered a direct regulator, and subsequently treated as a bottom-layer gene in the next iteration. The final GRN was visualized using Cytoscape to depict hierarchical regulatory relationships.

Co-expression network analysis of AQ biosynthesis-related genes in F. multiflora

WGCNA [69] was performed on transcriptomes of roots, stems, and leaves of F. multiflora (PRJCA011391 and PRJCA022819). TF genes in F. multiflora were identified using PlantTFDB (https://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/prediction.php). Genes were retained if their cumulative TPM across all samples exceeded 10. Genes potentially involved in AQ biosynthesis, including PKS, PKC, UGT, CYP, CCoAOMT and OMT, were identified using HMMER [56].

The optimal soft-thresholding power was set to 6 based on scale-free topology. Constructed unsigned co-expression network and gene modules were identified using the blockwiseModules function in the WGCNA package (v1.73) [69], with the following parameters: power = 6, TOMType = “unsigned”, minimum module size = 30, and module merging threshold (mergeCutHeight) = 0.25. To identify gene modules highly associated with AQ compounds, CC analysis was performed between module eigengenes (MEs) and AQ compounds data.The statistical significance (P values) of CCs was calculated using the corPvalueStudent function, and the results were visualized by heatmap. Gene significance (GS) values and corresponding P values were calculated to quantify the association between each gene in the target modules and AQ compounds. Module membership (MM) values and their corresponding P values were also calculated. The relationship between GS and MM within target modules was visualized using scatter plots. Genes with high GS value (GS > 0.5 and P < 0.05) and high MM value (MM > 0.5 and P < 0.05) were defined as hub genes [36, 69, 70]. Missing values were handled using the pairwise complete observations approach (use = “p”) [71].

Identification of characteristic TF genes regulating FmuUGT106

Three methods, namely, support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE), Boruta algorithm-based random forest selection (Boruta-RF), and CC were applied to identify TFs that regulate the expression of the target gene FmuUGT106 (AYY8G000588). The detailed procedures are described below. Transcriptome datasets of F. multiflora were downloaded from the CNCB database (PRJCA011391 and PRJCA022819) and processed to generate an expression matrix. A total of 2,105 TF genes were identified using PlantTFDB (https://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/prediction.php), 1,953 TF genes with nonzero total expression were retained for subsequent analyses. Expression levels of FmuUGT106 were used as the phenotype, and those of the 1,953 TF genes were used as predictor variables.

SVM-RFE was performed using the kernlab, caret, and e1071 packages. A radial basis function kernel was applied, with the kernel width (gamma = 0.1, 1) and penalty (C = 0.1, 1) parameters optimized by 10-fold cross-validation. Feature subsets ranging from 50 features to the full set were evaluated in increments of 100 features. Model performance was evaluated using the root mean square error (RMSE). The subset with the lowest RMSE was selected as optimal, within which TF genes were ranked by 10-fold cross-validated feature importance, and the top 30 on mean ranking were retained as candidate key regulators. Boruta-RF was performed using the Boruta, caret, and randomForest packages. The Boruta algorithm was applied with 300 iterations and a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of 0.01 to identify relevant TF genes. RF models were trained using confirmed features, with the mtry parameter optimized by 10-fold cross-validation repeated five times using mean squared error (MSE) as the optimization objective. Model performance was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R²). The Boruta-RF procedure was independently repeated 10 times, and TF genes consistently identified across runs were defined as key regulatory factors. CC analysis was performed to evaluate expression correlations between the target gene and TF genes. TF genes with P < 0.05 and |CC| > 0.8 were retained. Finally, TF genes identified by three methods were intersected to improve the reliability and accuracy of the screening results, followed by comparative analysis of their expression patterns.

Results

Phylogenetic analysis and comparative genomics of Polygonaceae

To construct a phylogenetic framework for the evolution of AQ biosynthesis-related genes in Polygonaceae, we selected 17 species. These included four species from core Caryophyllales (three from Amaranthaceae and one from Cactaceae), 11 species from non-core Caryophyllales species (10 from Polygonaceae, and one from Simmondsiaceae). Additionally, one superrosid species (Vitis vinifera) and one monocot species (O. sativa) were included as outgroups (Tables S1 and S17). OrthoFinder clustering assigned 698,103 protein sequences from these species into 33,424 OG families (Table S18). Among the 10 Polygonaceae species, a total of 9,347 core gene families were identified, along with 30,797 dispensable gene families and 43,141 private gene families (Fig. 1A and S1; Table S19). The number of private gene families ranged from 1,389 to 12,929 (Fig. 1B and S1 Table S19), reflecting substantial genomic diversification within the family.

Using 95 single-copy OGs identified across the 17 species, we reconstructed the species phylogeny (Fig. 1C). The major clades and internal phylogenetic relationships within Polygonaceae were consistent with previous studies [31, 72]. Superrosids and Caryophyllales formed sister clades, and within Caryophyllales, Polygonaceae diverged first, followed by Cactaceae and Amaranthaceae. Within Polygonaceae, successive divergences gave rise to the tribes Persicarieae, Fagopyreae, Polygoneae, and Rumiceae (Fig. 1C). Divergence time estimation clarified several nodes in Polygonaceae evolution. A shared WGD event in Polygonaceae occurred ~ 67.70 million years ago (Mya) [31], close to the divergence of Polygonaceae from its sister family (~ 66.26 Mya). The genus Fagopyrum diverged from other Polygonaceae lineages ~ 57.40 Mya, with further analysis supporting a close relationship between Fagopyrum cymosum and Fagopyrum tataricum [73]. Fallopia diverged from other Polygonaceae lineages ~ 44.45 Mya, with F. multiflora and F. multiflora var. angulata diverging ~ 9.10 Mya. Oxyria digyna and Rheum diverged ~ 32.61 Mya, while the two subgenomes of P. tinctoria split ~ 8.86 Mya. These analyses established a genome-scale phylogenetic framework for five major Polygonaceae genera and provided a robust evolutionary context for investigating gene family diversification.

Gene family expansion and contraction analyses revealed evolutionary dynamics across Polygonaceae species (Fig. 1C). A total of 726 gene families showed significant expansion at the divergence node (node 22) of Polygonaceae from its sister family (Fig. 1C, Table S20). GO enrichment analysis showed that these expanded gene families were mainly involved in quercetin 4′-O-glucosyltransferase activity, S-adenosylmethionine cycle, peptidase inhibitor activity, toxin catabolic processes, and cysteine-type endopeptidase inhibitor activity (Fig. 1D, Table S21). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further revealed significant enrichment in pathways including stilbenoid, diarylheptanoid, and gingerol biosynthesis, isoflavonoid biosynthesis, anthocyanin biosynthesis, cutin, suberine, and wax biosynthesis, brassinosteroid biosynthesis, and zeatin biosynthesis (Fig. 1E, Table S22). At the species level, F. esculentum showed the largest number of expanded gene families (657), while O. digyna had the most contracted families (706) (Fig. 1C, Table S20). These changes in gene family size may be associated with long-term environmental shifts, such as the uplift of the Qinghai-Xizang Plateau and the intensification of the Asian monsoon [74]. These results indicate that gene family expansion at the divergence node of Polygonaceae from its sister family contributed to the diversification of secondary metabolites and environmental adaptation, thereby establishing a genetic foundation for the chemical diversity and ecological adaptability of extant species.

Identification and analysis of AQ biosynthesis-related genes in Polygonaceae species

As key enzymes controlling the entry step of emodin-type AQ biosynthesis, PKSs play a pivotal role in the polyketide pathway. Accordingly, we systematically investigated this gene family and identified 206 PKS genes across 10 Polygonaceae species (Table S23). Most Polygonaceae species contained fewer PKS genes than the distantly related species V. vinifera (52) and O. sativa (27), but substantially more than Arabidopsis thaliana (4) (Table S23). Compared with other Caryophyllales lineages, Polygonaceae harbored a relatively higher number of PKS genes (Table S23). Specifically, the five non-Polygonaceae species included in this study from Caryophyllales (Amaranthaceae, Cactaceae, and Simmondsiaceae) possessed 4–14 PKS genes, whereas Polygonaceae species contained 11–28 PKS genes. In addition, 86.41% of PKS genes showed significant expansion at the divergence node of Polygonaceae from its sister family (Table S24). Based on phylogenetic relationships, the PKS genes from Polygonaceae were classified into three major clades (clades 1–3) (Fig. 2A). Notably, clade 3 included a distinct subcluster primarily composed of PKS genes from Rheum nobile and Rheum tanguticum (Fig. 2A). These results indicate that the PKS gene family expanded during the divergence of Polygonaceae from other Caryophyllales, followed by lineage-specific or species-specific expansion within the family.

To explore the potential functional consequences of this expansion, we examined gene expression profiles. Polygonaceae PKS genes were broadly divided into two categories: genes expressed in multiple tissues (roots, stems, and leaves) and genes showing predominantly root-specific expression (Fig. S2-S6). Given that AQs are predominantly enriched in underground tissues (roots and rhizomes) in Polygonaceae, the root-specific PKS genes may be associated with AQ biosynthesis and accumulation. These results suggest that PKS genes in Polygonaceae exhibit expression divergence, which may reflect functional differentiation within this gene family.

To explore the mechanisms underlying PKS gene expansion, duplication patterns of PKS genes were systematically analyzed across Polygonaceae species. The contribution of different duplication modes varied markedly among lineages. Given the important roles of TD and PD in plant secondary metabolism [75, 76], subsequent analyses focused on these two modes. A high proportion of TD-derived PKS genes was observed in R. tanguticum (53.6%) and R. nobile (35.0%) (Fig. 2B; Table S25), whereas PD events predominated in F. cymosum (52.9%) and F. esculentum (39.1%) (Fig. 2B; Table S25). Chromosomal distribution analysis further revealed that PKS genes were unevenly distributed and highly enriched on specific chromosomes, particularly Chr05 in R. nobile (65.0%) and R. tanguticum (35.7%) (Fig. 2C; Table S26). Moreover, many PKS genes were organized into local gene clusters derived from TD or PD events (Fig. S7-S16). Collectively, PKS genes in certain Polygonaceae species have undergone lineage-specific expansion and exhibit chromosomal clustering, which may contribute to functional differentiation of this gene family.

In addition to PKS genes, we also identified gene families involved in AQ modification to gain a more complete view of the biosynthetic pathway. Across the 10 Polygonaceae species, a total of 1,392 UGT genes were identified (Fig. S17; Table S27). Notably, UGT73BE14 from R. palmatum and UGT73BE5 from F. tataricum, both known to catalyze the glycosylation of emodin to form emodin-6-O-glucoside (glucoemodin), belonged to the UGT group D. We also identified 115 CCoAOMT genes across these species (Fig. S18; Table S28), which may participate in methylation modifications of AQ precursors. To further pinpoint candidate genes involved in AQ modification, we reanalyzed transcriptome data from previous studies [18] and identified genes potentially involved in AQ methylation and glycosylation (Tables S29 and S30). These genes represent key genes involved in AQ methylation and glycosylation.

Synteny analysis and expression profiling of polyketide BGCs in Polygonaceae

To investigate PKS genes potentially involved in AQ biosynthesis, polyketide BGCs were identified across 10 Polygonaceae species using PlantiSMASH (Tables S1 and S17). BGCs were detected in all examined Polygonaceae genomes (Table S31). Notably, adjacent PKS gene clusters were identified on chromosome 11 of R. palmatum, chromosome 05 of R. tanguticum, and chromosome 05 of R. nobile (Fig. 3A, Tables S26 and S32). Comparative synteny analysis across Polygonaceae revealed that homologous genomic regions corresponding to these BGCs were conserved across Polygonaceae species (Fig. 3A). PKS genes within these regions were present in P. tinctoria, but occurred at reduced copy numbers. In contrast, PKS genes in the corresponding regions were lost in three Fagopyrum species, whereas they were expanded in Fallopia and Rheum (Fig. 3A). Detailed synteny analyses among R. nobile, R. tanguticum, R. palmatum, F. multiflora, and F. multiflora var. angulata showed nearly complete conservation across ~ 5-Mb genomic regions, indicating minimal large-scale chromosomal rearrangements during evolution (Fig. 3B and S19).

Based on conserved syntenic relationships, PKS genes within these regions were classified into three distinct genomic regions, Polyketide BGC1, BGC2, and BGC3 regions (Fig. 3B). The Polyketide BGC1 region was further subdivided into subclusters 1 and 2 according to the conserved positions of UGT genes (Fig. 3C). Subcluster 1 was conserved across all five species and exhibited pronounced PKS gene expansion in Rheum (Fig. 3C). Transcriptomic analyses revealed that PKS genes in this subcluster displayed predominantly root-specific expression across species (Fig. S3-S6), consistent with previously reported tissue-specific AQ accumulation profiles [16, 26]. These features indicate that subcluster 1 represents a conserved core PKS module likely involved in AQ biosynthesis. In contrast, subcluster 2 was detected in four species and contained fewer PKS genes. PKS genes in this subcluster showed broader tissue expression patterns, suggesting a secondary contribution to AQ biosynthesis.

The Polyketide BGC2 region comprised syntenic PKS gene pairs together with additional tailoring enzyme genes, including amino oxidases and CYP (Fig. 3D). However, PKS genes within this region were predominantly expressed in aerial tissues rather than roots (Fig. S3 and S4), indicating that Polyketide BGC2 is unlikely to play a major role in AQ biosynthesis.

PKS genes within the Polyketide BGC3 region showed lineage-specific expansion and root-specific expression in R. nobile but were absent or transcriptionally inactive in other species, indicating functional divergence (Fig. 3E, S3, S5 and S6). Together with the retention of conserved neighboring genes, these observations suggest lineage-specific expansion and functional divergence of PKS genes in R. nobile.

Beyond these structural and expression patterns, we propose that during evolution, the UGT genes within the Polyketide BGC1 region (Fig. 3C) and the CYP genes within the Polyketide BGC2 region (Fig. 3D) were likely integrated into the respective BGCs via gene recruitment events [77, 78]. To further validate the accuracy of our synteny‑coupled expression profiling approach, we examined independent transcriptomic data from different tissues of R. tanguticum (PRJCA037598) [79]. Notably, PKS genes within subcluster 1 of the Polyketide BGC1 region, particularly RtaG0011476, exhibited the highest expression in roots, mirroring the root‑enriched distribution of AQ compounds (Fig. S20).

These analyses indicate that conserved ancestral PKS genes underwent lineage-specific expansion and BGC formation, particularly in Fallopia and Rheum, enhancing AQ biosynthetic capacity and likely contributing to adaptive diversification in Polygonaceae.

Potential regulatory relationships of PKS genes in roots of F. multiflora at different growth years

Compared with Rheum species, F. multiflora harbors fewer PKS genes within the large-scale PKS BGC homologous regions, which reduces regulatory complexity and provides an ideal system for dissecting PKS regulatory networks. AQ accumulation in F. multiflora varies across different growth years [27], providing an opportunity for linking transcriptional regulation with metabolic variation. Accordingly, root transcriptomes from different growth years of F. multiflora were integrated to construct a multi-level GRN.

Through pairwise analysis of F. multiflora root transcriptomes from four growth years (1, 3, 5, and 10 years; PRJCA011391) (Fig. S21), we identified six sets of DEGs. The numbers of DEGs (down/up) were 4,140 (2,505/1,605), 4,854 (2,377/2,477), 4,150 (2,438/1,712), 3,650 (2,591/1,059), 2,997 (1,666/1,331), and 1,985 (1,451/534), respectively (Fig. S22 and S23; Table S33). After removing duplicate genes across the six pairwise comparisons, a total of 8,627 unique DEGs were detected across all comparisons, including 7 PKS genes and 591 TF genes (Table S34). GO enrichment analysis of the 8,627 DEGs revealed significant enrichment of GO terms associated with secondary metabolite biosynthetic process and hexosyltransferase activity (Fig. S24; Table S35). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further revealed significant enrichment for pathways involved in plant secondary metabolism, including phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, and stilbenoid, diarylheptanoid, and gingerol biosynthesis (Fig. S24; Table S36).

To investigate the regulatory mechanisms underlying PKS-mediated secondary metabolite biosynthesis, we first focused on GO terms associated with secondary metabolite biosynthetic process and further incorporated additional significantly enriched terms relevant to PKS function, including flavonoid biosynthetic process, flavonoid metabolic process, acyltransferase activity, and transferring groups other than amino-acyl groups (Padj < 0.05 and |log2FoldChange| > 1; Table S37). After the above GO-based filtering, 222 DEGs were retained from the 8,627 DEGs. Together with the 591 TF genes identified above, these genes were used to construct a multi-level GRN. Pearson correlation coefficient (CC) analysis (CC > 0.90) identified a co-expression module of 204 genes, including 7 PKS genes. Subsequent PCC analysis (PCC < 0.15) indicated that 371 TF genes were potentially associated with the regulation of 196 co-expressed genes, among which 38 TF genes showed direct associations with PKS genes. Further network analysis demonstrated strong co-expression relationships among these 38 TF genes (CC > 0.90).

Using this framework, a four-layer regulatory cascade was resolved. The GRN consisted of 7 top-layer TF genes, 69 middle-layer TF genes, 371 bottom-layer TF genes, and 196 target genes (Fig. 4; Table S38). These 447 TF genes collectively regulated genes involved in secondary metabolite biosynthesis in F. multiflora roots across different growth years. Among the 69 middle-layer TF genes, 67 showed co-expression relationships. In addition, 368 of the 371 bottom-layer TF genes exhibited co-expression patterns. It should be noted that TF assignment to hierarchical layers reflects their relative positions within the inferred network rather than exhaustive regulatory relationships. Within this network, a PKS related regulatory submodule was identified. This submodule consisted of one top-layer TF, 13 middle-layer TF genes, and 38 bottom-layer TF genes, which collectively participated in the regulation of 7 PKS genes. The inferred regulatory cascades associated with these PKS genes varied in depth, with most involving two- or three-layer cascades and only a few extending to four layers, suggesting a flexible regulatory architecture underlying PKS gene regulation (Fig. 4; Table S38). Notably, AYY3G002233 did not exhibit co-expression with other PKS genes within the network (Table S39). Moreover, AYY3G002233 and AYY3G002185 displayed distinct regulatory relationships, with no shared TF genes across any hierarchical layer in the GRN (Fig. 4 and S25; Table S38). These observations indicate that PKS genes originating from different genomic contexts may be subject to divergent transcriptional regulation, potentially reflecting functional differentiation within secondary metabolism. Collectively, the multi-level GRN provides insights into the complex regulatory architecture governing PKS genes in F. multiflora.

To evaluate the impact of threshold selection on the construction of the multi-level GRN, we compared the transcriptional regulator sets of seven PKS genes under stringent (CC > 0.90, PCC < 0.15) versus lenient (CC > 0.80, PCC < 0.30) conditions (Fig. S26). These findings confirm that threshold variation has a limited impact on the multi‑layered gene regulatory network constructed in this study.

Gene co-expression modules associated with AQ accumulation in F. multiflora

To investigate the co-expression relationships between AQ biosynthesis-related genes and TF genes, and to identify genes and TF genes highly associated with AQ accumulation, we conducted WGCNA analysis. Transcriptome datasets from roots, stems, and leaves of F. multiflora (PRJCA011391 and PRJCA022819) were collected and subjected to standardized normalization. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that samples clustered closely according to tissue type and growth year, indicating high data reliability (Fig. S27 and S28). In total, 2,105 TF genes were identified in F. multiflora, together with 574 genes putatively involved in the polyketide pathway and AQ modification (Tables S40 and S41).

WGCNA identified 12 distinct co-expression modules (Fig. S29-S31). Correlation analysis between MEs and metabolite abundance revealed a strong positive correlation between the yellow module and glucoemodin (CC = 0.83, P = 2 × 10⁻8) (Fig. 5A and S32). This module contained 201 genes, forming a co-expression network associated with glucoemodin accumulation (Table S42). The yellow module included AYY8G000588, an ortholog of UGT73BE14, which is known to catalyze the conversion of emodin to glucoemodin. In addition, several other UGT genes (including AYY5G005380, AYY10G000137, and AYY8G000553) were also present in this module, suggesting their likely involvement in the same glycosylation process (Table S42). Further analysis of GS and MM confirmed that genes with high GS for glucoemodin also exhibited high MM within the yellow module (Fig. 5B). Using thresholds of GS > 0.50 (P < 0.05) and MM > 0.50 (P < 0.05), 98 genes showing significant associations with glucoemodin were identified. Among them, AYY7G001040 (HD-ZIP), showed the highest GS (GS = 0.9768, P-GS = 1.23 × 10⁻6) together with a high MM (MM = 0.6702, P-MM = 9.54 × 10⁻5) (Table S43). Another gene, AYY10G005259 (bZIP) also exhibited high GS (GS = 0.9704, P-GS = 3.24 × 10⁻6) and MM (MM = 0.6162, P-MM = 4.79 × 10⁻4) (Table S43). Genes within the yellow module are thus strong candidates involved in glucoemodin biosynthesis.

In addition to glucoemodin, MEs-metabolite correlation analysis revealed a strong positive correlation between the yellow module and physcion (CC = 0.97, P = 3 × 10⁻18) (Fig. 5A and C). Identification and analysis of transcription factors regulating FmuUGT106 in F. multiflora

Based on the results of WGCNA, FmuUGT106 (AYY8G000588), an ortholog of UGT73BE14 involved in AQ biosynthesis, was selected for further analysis due to its role in catalyzing the conversion of emodin to glucoemodin. To investigate the potential transcriptional regulatory mechanisms regulating FmuUGT106 expression in F. multiflora, we integrated three complementary feature selection strategies including SVM-RFE, Boruta-RF, and CC analysis.

TF genes were identified using PlantTFDB, and 1,953 TF genes with nonzero cumulative expression across roots, stems, and leaves of F. multiflora were retained for subsequent analyses (Table S40). Using SVM-RFE, the top 30 ranked TF genes were selected, among which the MYB and bHLH families were the most represented. Notably, AYY2G007602, a member of the MYB family, ranked first with an average rank of 5.8 (Fig. 6A, S33 and S34; Tables S45 and S46). Boruta-RF analyses involved 10 independent random forest models, each achieving R² greater than 0.90 (Fig. S35). Across all iterations, 35 TF genes were consistently selected, with the bHLH, C2H2, ERF, and WRKY families highly represented (Fig. 6B; Tables S47 and S48). CC analysis identified 43 TF genes significantly correlated with FmuUGT106 expression (P < 0.05 and |CC| > 0.8), including 25 positively correlated and 18 negatively correlated TF genes (Fig. 6C; Table S49). Among these candidates, MYB family members were the most abundant (six TF genes) (Table S50).

Integration of all three approaches yielded 15 key TF genes consistently identified across methods (Fig. 6D). Among these, 13 TF genes showed positive correlations and two exhibited negative correlations with FmuUGT106 expression. These TF genes belong to ZF-HD, WRKY, NF-YB, NAC, MYB, G2-like, ERF, C2H2, bHLH, and ARR-B families, with two genes each from the MYB, G2-like, ERF, and C2H2 families (Fig. 6E; Table S51). These findings are consistent with previous studies [32–35], highlighting MYB, bHLH, and WRKY TF genes as key candidates regulating AQ biosynthesis in Polygonaceae.

Expression pattern analysis of the 15 key TF genes and FmuUGT106 across different tissues and roots from different growth years revealed that the 13 positively correlated TF genes were predominantly expressed in roots, particularly in 5-years roots (Fig. 6E). In contrast, two negatively correlated TF genes showed higher expression levels in stems and leaves. Integration of FmuUGT106 expression profiles with AQ compounds data (especially glucoemodin) [27] indicated that the positively correlated TF genes likely activate FmuUGT106 expression in roots, whereas the negatively correlated TF genes may repress its expression in stems and leaves. Interestingly, AYY4G006061 (ERF) and AYY2G006408 (C3H) displayed the highest expression levels in 5-years roots (Fig. 6E), suggesting a strong association with glucoemodin accumulation. However, their broad expression across multiple tissues also indicates that these TF genes may not be specific to FmuUGT106.

To further validate the screening results, we analyzed an independent transcriptomic data from root tissues of F. multiflora grown under different soil pH conditions (PRJNA1003290) [80]. The expression levels of the key glycosyltransferase gene FmuUGT106 and its 15 candidate TF genes were highly consistent with AQ accumulation profiles across samples (Fig. S36). Notably, the experimental conditions of this validation dataset (different soil pH) differed from those used for model construction, indicating that the key genes identified by machine learning and CC analysis exhibit good reproducibility and generalizability on unseen datasets.

Discussion

Recent advances in genome sequencing have led to the publication of genome assemblies for species within Polygonaceae, greatly expanding genomic resources for this family. However, due to variability in assembly quality, annotation completeness, and data accessibility among published genomes, only 10 Polygonaceae genomes with high continuity and well-annotated, publicly available datasets were selected for integrative phylogenomic and comparative analyses in this study. By leveraging these representative genomes, which collectively span five major genera, we established a genome-scale phylogenetic framework and inferred divergence times across key evolutionary nodes within Polygonaceae. Our results indicate that Polygonaceae diverged from other Caryophyllales lineages approximately 66.26 Mya, followed by a series of successive divergence events that shaped the extant diversity of Polygonaceae (Fig. 1A and C). Gene family expansion and contraction analyses revealed that 726 gene families underwent expansion at key divergence nodes within Polygonaceae. These expanded families were significantly enriched in pathways related to flavonoid and stilbenoid biosynthesis, as well as defense and detoxification associated biological processes (Fig. 1D and E). These findings suggest that ancestral Polygonaceae reinforced secondary metabolic capacity and stress-response mechanisms at the genetic level, which may have facilitated ecological adaptation during periods of pronounced environmental change and enabled the occupation of diverse ecological niches.

Despite the prominent medicinal importance of Polygonaceae species, systematic evolutionary investigations of gene families involved in AQ biosynthesis have remained limited thus far. In this study, we comprehensively identified and analyzed three gene families implicated in AQ biosynthesis, namely PKS, UGT, and CCoAOMT. It was found that the PKS gene family underwent lineage-specific expansion in Polygonaceae, with copy numbers exceeding those observed in other Caryophyllales species (Table S23). Notably, PKS genes accounted for up to 86.41% of significantly expanded genes at Polygonaceae divergence nodes, underscoring their potential contribution to lineage diversification (Table S24). Phylogenetic analyses further revealed that PKS subfamilies, including CHS and STS, exhibited intermingled branching patterns rather than forming strictly monophyletic clades (Fig. 2A). This topology suggests that functional diversification of PKS genes does not strictly mirror phylogeny, but may instead be driven by convergent evolution or subfunctionalization [81]. Importantly, a substantial proportion of PKS genes displayed root-specific expression patterns (Fig. S2-S6), which closely correspond to the specific accumulation of AQs in roots and rhizomes [16, 26, 31]. This concordance strongly supports the PKS genes as key candidates involved in AQ biosynthesis. Analyses of chromosomal distribution and duplication patterns further indicated that TD represents the primary force driving PKS gene expansion and the formation of local gene clusters in genera such as Rheum (Fig. 2B and C). In addition, the coordinated expansion of AQ-modifying enzymes, including UGTs and CCoAOMTs, likely facilitated the establishment and diversification of AQ biosynthetic capacity in Polygonaceae.

Plant BGCs have been extensively characterized in Lamiaceae [22, 23], Poaceae [24, 82], and Brassicaceae [83, 84]. However, the evolutionary dynamics of polyketide BGCs in Polygonaceae have remained largely unexplored. Through systematic identification and evolutionary trajectory analyses, we detected multiple syntenic polyketide BGCs (Fig. 3A). Microsynteny analyses demonstrated that the genomic regions harboring these BGCs are conserved within Polygonaceae, whereas the number of PKS genes and the presence of BGC vary markedly among species (Fig. 3B and E). For instance, PKS genes within Polyketide BGC1 region underwent TD and formed typical BGC in R. palmatum, R. tanguticum, and R. nobile, whereas in the closely related F. multiflora, homologous PKS genes were present but did not assemble into a canonical BGC (Fig. 3C). This evolutionary pattern contrasts with the vertically inherited and structurally conserved leonurine BGC reported in Lamiaceae [23], and instead resembles the dynamically reshaped caryophyllene BGC described in Asteraceae [44]. Based on these observations, we propose that the common ancestor of Polygonaceae did not possess a fully assembled and stably inherited polyketide BGC, but rather retained a proto-BGC region containing key PKS genes. During subsequent lineage diversification, the combined effects of TD, PD, gene recruitment of CYP and UGT genes [77, 78], and selection under evolutionary pressure may have repeatedly assembled this ancestral region into functional polyketide BGCs in a lineage-specific manner. Furthermore, although WGD events in Polygonaceae may have contributed to the expansion of gene repertoires by providing additional genetic material, our synteny-based analyses indicate that WGD is not the primary driver of polyketide BGC formation or diversification in this lineage. Instead, local duplication and gene recruitment processes appear to play more direct roles in shaping BGC architecture.

Given that BGCs are involved in AQ biosynthesis, their evolutionary dynamics may be linked to the ecological functions of these metabolites. Relevant studies indicate that AQs can help mitigate high-light stress [11, 12] and cold stress [85], both of which are characteristic of high-altitude environments. In addition, altitude-dependent variation in AQs, particularly glycosylated forms, has been observed in alpine plants [86, 87]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the formation of polyketide BGCs in Polygonaceae may be associated with adaptation to high-altitude environments.

By integrating gene expression profiles with synteny analyses, PKS genes within these regions were further categorized, enabling the identification of high-confidence candidate AQ-biosynthetic PKS genes. To elucidate their transcriptional regulation, we constructed a multi-level GRN based on root transcriptomes of F. multiflora across different growth years. GRN analysis revealed a four-layer regulatory hierarchy, comprising seven TF genes in the top layer, 69 middle-layer TF genes, 371 bottom-layer TF genes, and 196 target genes (Fig. 4). Notably, PKS genes located in different syntenic regions, such as AYY3G002233 and AYY3G002185, exhibited distinct regulatory relationships within the GRN (Fig. 4). These findings indicate that PKS genes originating from different syntenic regions may have undergone regulatory divergence under evolutionary pressure, potentially leading to functional differentiation.

To further investigate co-expression relationships between AQ biosynthetic genes and TF genes, WGCNA identified modules significantly correlated with the accumulation of glucoemodin and physcion (Fig. 5). Focusing on the key glycosyltransferase gene FmuUGT106, we integrated CC analysis with two machine learning approaches to identify TF genes potentially regulating its expression. Fifteen TF genes, including members of the MYB, bHLH, and WRKY families, were identified as candidate regulators of FmuUGT106 (Fig. 6). Collectively, these results provide new insights into the transcriptional control of AQ biosynthesis in F. multiflora and offer promising candidate TF genes for future metabolic engineering and molecular breeding efforts.

Although this study includes five genera and 10 Polygonaceae species, the current taxonomic sampling may not fully capture the evolutionary complexity of the family, potentially constraining the generalizability of certain evolutionary inferences. Future studies incorporating additional Polygonaceae genomes will be essential to refine the phylogenetic framework and enhance the robustness of evolutionary conclusions. Moreover, while the present study is primarily based on integrative bioinformatic analyses, experimental validation of the predicted results has not yet been performed, but the identified candidate genes and regulatory networks provide a valuable foundation for targeted functional validation. The inferred multi-level GRN and key candidate genes offer a conceptual framework for understanding growth year-associated regulation and AQ biosynthesis in Polygonaceae. Although our genome-wide and transcriptome-based analyses provide systematic predictions of candidate PKS genes, AQ biosynthesis-related genes, and associated transcription factors, experimental validation remains necessary to confirm their precise biological functions. Future work will focus on experimental validation of the predicted genes and their regulatory interactions to further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying these biological processes.

Conclusions

This study systematically elucidated the evolutionary trajectories of PKS genes and their associated polyketide BGCs in Polygonaceae. Our results demonstrate that TD has played a central role in driving the expansion and functional diversification of the PKS gene family, particularly by promoting the formation and lineage-specific evolution of polyketide BGCs. The identification of syntenic polyketide BGCs with conserved genomic contexts across Polygonaceae species suggests a shared evolutionary origin of these regions, whereas pronounced interspecific variation in PKS gene copy number and BGC architecture reflects dynamic evolutionary remodeling of polyketide biosynthetic pathways. Integrative expression and regulatory analyses further reveal that root-specific expressed PKS genes, together with multi-level GRN, are closely associated with AQ accumulation and may contribute to plant adaptation to environmental variation. These findings indicate that AQ biosynthesis in Polygonaceae is shaped not only by gene family expansion and BGC organization, but also by coordinated transcriptional regulation. Overall, this study advances our understanding of the evolutionary mechanisms underlying PKS genes and polyketide BGCs in Polygonaceae and provides a theoretical framework for enhancing the biosynthesis of medicinally important AQs through metabolic engineering and molecular breeding strategies.
