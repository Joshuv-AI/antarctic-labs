Binary Evolution Can Mimic the Pair-Instability Mass Gap in Black Hole Mergers
Aleksandra Olejak

Abstract
Context. The recent O4a release from the LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA collaboration, which significantly increased the number of gravitational-wave (GW) detections, reveals features with potentially important astrophysical implications. One notable example is a hint of the so-called pair-instability mass gap. In particular, the observed decline in the number of black holes (BHs) with mass above ∼ 45 ​ M ⊙ \sim 45\,M_{\odot} , together with indications of possibly higher spins for BHs above this threshold, has been interpreted by Antonini et al. and Tong et al. as evidence for pair-instability supernovae. Aims. In this work, we investigate whether mass transfer in binary systems can produce BH components’ mass distribution that mimics the pair-instability limit. Methods. We use both the population synthesis code StarTrack and a simple semi-analytical framework to highlight the impact of mass transfer efficiency on the BH masses. Results. We find that efficient mass transfer ( ≳ 50 % \gtrsim 50\% ) during the first Roche-lobe overflow, followed by a highly non-conservative second mass-transfer phase, naturally limits the mass of the first-born BH and produces a cutoff that mimics a mass gap. While the upper mass limit for the more massive BH is increased through accretion during the first mass-transfer phase and is ultimately set by the pair-instability limit, the less massive BH is limited to the stripped primary mass. As a result, the fraction of systems in which the less massive BH exceeds ∼ 45 ​ M ⊙ \sim 45\,M_{\odot} is negligible, below 10 − 5 10^{-5} in our default model. Conclusions. While the pair-instability mass gap is a plausible interpretation of current GW data, similar features can naturally arise from binary evolution. Future detections will help distinguish between these scenarios. In particular, a predominance of positive effective spins or low-spin events within the gap would challenge the pair-instability interpretation and instead support a binary-interaction origin for high-mass BHs.

Evolutionary scenario mimicking PSN mass gap

Figure 1
:
An evolutionary pathway for BBH mergers in which mass-transfer physics leads to a lower-mass first-born BH and a cutoff mimicking the presence of the PSN mass gap.

Figure
1
illustrates a schematic formation pathway for merging BBH systems that produce cutoff in less massive BH mass distribution, thereby mimicking the presence of a PSN mass gap. The initially more massive star expands first and initiates the first mass transfer phase. This phase must be relatively efficient, with more than
\sim 50\%
of the transferred mass accreted by the initially less massive companion. As a result, the accretor gains a significant fraction of mass, leading to a mass-ratio reversal. The orbital separation widens during this stage. The initially more massive star loses a substantial amount of mass through mass transfer and stellar winds, becoming stripped of its envelope. It eventually forms the first-born BH. Subsequently, the secondary star, originally the less massive component but now significantly more massive after efficient accretion, expands and fills its Roche lobe. It transfers mass onto the first-born BH; however, because the mass-transfer rate is limited to roughly the Eddington rate, this phase is highly non-conservative and contributes to the BH’s mass growth negligibly. The system thus naturally may end up with an unequal mass ratio, producing a lower-mass first-born BH. This scenario was previously presented and discussed in, e.g.,
38
;
41

3
Discussion

3.1
Efficiency of mass transfer

The proposed binary evolution scenario with mass-ratio reversal, resulting in a cutoff at
\sim 45\,M_{\odot}
in the mass distribution of the less massive BHs, requires mass transfer with an efficiency of at least
\sim 50\%
(i.e., at least half of the mass transferred by the donor during the first mass-transfer phase is accreted by the companion star). Such relatively efficient mass transfer is increasingly supported by attempts to constrain mass-transfer efficiency using various types of observed post–mass-transfer binary populations
(
58
;
47
;
33
;
48
)
.

While observations generally point toward efficient mass transfer, this assumption is not commonly adopted as a default in many binary evolution codes used to model BBH formation. Many codes rely on prescriptions with lower accretion efficiencies and significant mass loss from the system during the first Roche-lobe overflow. In particular, accretion onto the companion is often limited by the critical rotation constraint
(
19
)
, which can strongly restrict accretion and prevent mass-ratio reversal, thereby favoring the formation of more equal-mass binaries typically produced through Case A mass transfer
(
15
, e.g.,)
.
While our models favor BBH merger progenitors undergoing efficient first mass transfer in order to reproduce the GW data, in reality the mass-transfer efficiency is likely to vary across binary systems, depending on properties such as the mass ratio, orbital parameters, and metallicity, and may in some cases also favor lower efficiencies
(
48
;
40
;
61
)
.

3.2
Black Hole Spins

Another argument put forward by
4
and
56
in favor of the PSN mass gap near
45\,M_{\odot}
is a reported transition in the effective spin distribution around a similar mass threshold toward higher values. Products of second-generation mergers are expected to produce a characteristic peak in the distribution of individual spin magnitudes around
\sim 0.7

(
27
)
. Since individual spins are very poorly constrained in GW observations,
4
and
56
instead rely on the distribution of the effective spin parameter
\chi_{\mathrm{eff}}
, which is measured more accurately but represents a degenerate combination of the component masses, spins, and their tilt angles relative to the orbital angular momentum. This approach comes with important caveats.

The robustness of
\chi_{\mathrm{eff}}
measurements decreases for total binary masses
\gtrsim 50\,M_{\odot}
because the signal becomes increasingly dominated by the merger–ringdown phase, leaving only a small number of inspiral cycles where spin-dependent post-Newtonian phasing can accumulate
(
6
)
. In this regime, strong mass–ratio–spin degeneracies further limit parameter constraints, while waveform systematics become increasingly important, as modelling inaccuracies in the merger–ringdown can produce biases comparable to or larger than statistical uncertainties even at moderate signal-to-noise ratios
(
6
;
43
)
. Together, these effects make
\chi_{\mathrm{eff}}
progressively less well constrained and more model dependent at high masses. This is clearly illustrated in Fig. 5 of
4
, where massive events show very broad uncertainty ranges in
\chi_{\mathrm{eff}}
.

Moreover, the most massive BBH events detected so far tend to exhibit predominantly positive effective spins,
\chi_{\mathrm{eff}}>0
, suggestive of spin–orbit alignment and therefore an origin in isolated binary evolution, as discussed for example by
51
. By contrast, dynamical formation channels should produce a more symmetric distribution of positive and negative
\chi_{\mathrm{eff}}
values than is inferred from current GW data
(
23
, see e.g.,)
.

The origin and magnitude of BH spins remain an active subject of debate (see the recent review by
62
). One promising mechanism for producing rapidly spinning BHs through binary interactions is tidal spin-up of stripped helium stars in close post–mass-transfer binaries
(
11
;
8
)
. Moreover, as reported in previous studies, the mass-ratio reversal scenario presented here can naturally produce a more massive and more rapidly spinning BH
(
38
)
. This process also gives rise to correlations between mass, mass ratio, and effective spin
(
16
;
41
;
7
;
61
)
, which qualitatively resemble those inferred from GW observations
(
2
)
. Future work should investigate whether this binary interactions can also preferentially produce higher spins in more massive BBH mergers.

4
Summary

In this work, we present an alternative scenario to the PSN limit at
45\,M_{\odot}
proposed by
4
and
56
to interpret current GW observations. In particular, we focus on explaining the high-mass tail in the BH component distribution and the apparent mass cutoff in the distribution of the less massive BH with binary interactions. We demonstrate that BBH mergers formed via stable mass transfer channel can reproduce the observed mass distributions of both components as inferred by
56
once adopting: I) efficient mass transfer (above 50%) during the first Roche-lobe overflow onto a non-compact accretor; II) highly non-conservative accretion (nearly Eddington limited) onto the firstborn BH during the second mass transfer phase. These findings appear consistently in both population-synthesis simulations with
StarTrack
and simple semi-analytical models. This formation scenario also leaves a characteristic imprint on the BBH mass-ratio distribution: the more efficient first-phase mass transfer is the more extreme mass ratios of formed BBHs.

Although BH spin measurements for massive events are subject to large uncertainties, we postulate that the relative fraction of negative effective spins could help distinguish between the PSN interpretation and our binary-interaction scenario. If upcoming detections show a roughly balanced fraction of negative effective spins among BHs inside the PSN mass gap, this could be consistent with a dynamical origin and thus support the PSN-based interpretation. Conversely, if massive events show a clear preference for positive effective spins, isolated binary evolution would become a more plausible formation channel.

Appendix A
Semi-analytical model

A.1
Semi-analytical model

Here we construct toy semi-analytical models to further illustrate how the scenario described above operates in simple terms.

We generate
N=2\times 10^{5}
zero-age main-sequence binaries. The primary masses
m_{1}
are drawn from a power-law initial mass function with slope
\alpha=2.3
in the range
20\,M_{\odot}\leq m_{1}\leq 150\,M_{\odot}

(
32
)
. The mass ratio
q=m_{2}/m_{1}
is sampled from a uniform distribution between 0.1 and 1.0, and the secondary mass is set as
m_{2}=q\,m_{1}
. We retain only systems in which both components exceed
20\,M_{\odot}
, corresponding to potential BBH progenitors.

We assume a fixed fraction of the stellar mass contained in the helium core,
f_{\mathrm{core}}
, equal to
35\%
in our default model and
45\%
in an alternative model. Each binary is evolved through two phases of mass transfer. During the first episode, the primary transfers a fraction
f_{\mathrm{MT}}
of its envelope to the secondary. We test three cases:
f_{\mathrm{MT}}=0.5,\ 0.7,\ 0.9
. The post-transfer stellar masses are

m_{1}^{\prime}=f_{\mathrm{core}}\,m_{1},\quad m_{2}^{\prime}=m_{2}+f_{\mathrm{MT}}(m_{1}-f_{\mathrm{core}}\,m_{1}).

(1)

The second mass-transfer episode is assumed to be fully non-conservative. Therefore, we only assume that the second star loses its envelope. The final mass of the primary therefore remains unchanged, while the secondary is reduced to its core mass revised after first mass transfer,

m_{1}^{\prime\prime}=m_{1}^{\prime},\quad m_{2}^{\prime\prime}=f_{\mathrm{core}}\,m_{2}^{\prime}.

(2)

The final BH masses are assumed to be
90\%
of the final stellar masses, accounting for possible mass loss e.g., due to stellar winds and during core collapse. In practice, stellar winds may remove much larger fraction of mass from the system
(
12
, see e.g.,)
.

For each surviving system, we define the heavier and lighter BH masses as

m_{\mathrm{H}}=\max(m_{\mathrm{BH},1},m_{\mathrm{BH},2}),\quad m_{\mathrm{L}}=\min(m_{\mathrm{BH},1},m_{\mathrm{BH},2}),

(3)

and the mass ratio

q=\frac{m_{\mathrm{L}}}{m_{\mathrm{H}}}.

(4)

We impose a very high limit on PSN mass gap by excluding systems in which either BH mass falls within

80\,M_{\odot}<m_{\mathrm{BH}}<180\,M_{\odot}.

(5)

The described procedure is repeated for each value of the mass-transfer efficiency
f_{\mathrm{MT}}
, allowing us to assess its impact on the resulting BBH mass and mass-ratio distributions presented in Fig.
3
.

We note that these simplified models do not incorporate several important physical processes which would affect the resulting distributions. For example, when comparing the semi-analytical mass distribution with the population produced with
StarTrack
in Fig.
2
, the latter includes a small fraction of low-mass BHs (
<10\,M_{\odot}
) formed through successful or partially failed core-collapse supernovae according to the
26
prescription.

Despite the simplicity of the assumptions, the models reproduce several qualitative trends inferred from observed BBH populations
(
54
;
56
)
, including the
\sim 10\,M_{\odot}
peak and the apparent cutoff in the secondary-mass distribution.

We show that the mass of the more massive BH is primarily set by the amount of mass accreted during the first mass-transfer phase and, ultimately, by the assumed PSN limit. In contrast, the maximum mass of the less massive BH is determined by the mass of the first stripped stellar core of the star that only lost mass in mass transfer. For the two adopted core fractions
f_{\mathrm{core}}=0.35
and 0.45, this limit ranges from approximately
45
to
60\,M_{\odot}
. Note that some alternative recent analyses similarly suggest that the secondary-mass cutoff may occur around
\sim 60\,M_{\odot}

(
44
)
.
