Abstract
. Recent multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have begun to support Thinking with Images by invoking visual tools such as zooming and cropping during inference. Yet these systems remain brittle in fine-grained visual reasoning because they must decide where to look before they have access to the evidence needed to make that decision correctly. We identify this circular dependency as the Grounding Paradox . To address it, we propose Test-Time Scaling over Perception (TTSP) , a framework that treats perception itself as a scalable inference process. TTSP generates multiple exploratory perception traces, filters unreliable traces using entropy-based confidence estimation, distills validated observations into structured knowledge, and iteratively refines subsequent exploration toward unresolved uncertainty. Extensive experiments on high-resolution and general multimodal reasoning benchmarks show that TTSP consistently outperforms strong baselines across backbone sizes, while also exhibiting favorable scalability and token efficiency. Our results suggest that scaling perception at test time is a promising direction for robust multimodal reasoning under perceptual uncertainty. Code is available here .



1.
Introduction

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have achieved impressive progress on a wide range of vision-language tasks, including visual question answering, document understanding, and image-grounded dialogue
(
1
;
3
)
. Building on these advances, recent research has moved beyond one-shot image understanding toward the paradigm of
Thinking with Images
, wherein models are equipped with visual tools such as zooming and cropping to inspect image regions during inference
(
43
;
41
)
. This paradigm is appealing because it offers a potential path from coarse perception to targeted evidence acquisition: instead of relying exclusively on a fixed global representation of the image, the model can interact with the visual input and retrieve high-resolution information on demand.

Figure 1.
Illustration of the Grounding Paradox.
Illustration of the Grounding Paradox.

Despite this promise, however,
Thinking with Images
has yet to resolve the central challenge of fine-grained visual reasoning
(
33
)
. In practice, tool-augmented MLLMs still frequently inspect irrelevant regions, overlook critical evidence, or fail to invoke tools even when detailed information is clearly required. These failures are particularly pronounced in tasks involving small text, subtle attribute distinctions, dense symbolic content, or visually cluttered scenes. The issue, therefore, is not merely that current models lack access to high-resolution inputs. Rather, the deeper problem is that they remain unreliable at deciding where perceptual effort should be allocated in the first place. We argue that this limitation stems from a fundamental obstacle, which we term the
Grounding Paradox
.

Definition 1.1 (Grounding Paradox).

A multimodal model invokes a visual tool because its current global perceptual state
\mathcal{S}_{\text{global}}
lacks the fine-grained information needed to answer a query
Q
. However, producing an effective tool invocation—in particular, selecting the correct region
R\subset I
for further inspection—itself requires fine-grained discriminative information that is absent from
\mathcal{S}_{\text{global}}
. The model must therefore localize evidence that it cannot yet perceive.

The significance of this paradox is twofold. First, it reveals that the main bottleneck
in tool-augmented visual reasoning is not merely tool availability, but the absence of a mechanism for grounded perceptual search under uncertainty. Second, it explains why many current systems fail in a systematic rather than incidental manner: when a model does not know what detailed evidence exists or where it is located, tool use degenerates into speculative probing
(
13
;
29
)
. Mislocalized zooming, redundant inspection, and missed evidence are not isolated errors, but symptoms of a deeper circular dependency between perception and action.

This perspective also highlights a key limitation of existing approaches. Much of the recent progress in tool-augmented MLLMs has come from improving tool-call formatting, learning when to invoke tools, and enhancing grounding performance on in-domain distributions. While useful, such advances should not be conflated with genuine visual reasoning. A model that has learned to imitate tool-use patterns or exploit familiar grounding priors remains fundamentally different from one that can reason its way toward the right evidence when its initial perception is incomplete, ambiguous, or erroneous. Genuine visual reasoning requires not only acting on what is already grounded, but also recovering from insufficient grounding through deliberate exploration.

Recent work on test-time scaling offers a promising foundation for this idea
(
40
;
23
;
15
)
. In language-only domains, increasing inference-time compute by sampling and aggregating multiple reasoning trajectories has led to substantial gains in robustness and accuracy
(
32
;
20
;
39
)
. These methods rest on a simple but powerful insight: although any individual trajectory may be unreliable, the joint behavior of many trajectories can reveal consistent structure that supports better decisions. This suggests that additional computation at test time might also help address the uncertainty inherent in visual grounding
(
7
;
37
)
.

However, applying test-time scaling to visual reasoning is not straightforward. Existing methods are largely designed for text-based reasoning, where diversity across trajectories typically reflects alternative chains of logic over a fixed and fully observed input. In contrast, visual reasoning involves uncertainty not only in how to reason, but also in what has been perceived
(
27
;
18
)
. Different trajectories may inspect different regions, collect different pieces of visual evidence, and even condition subsequent reasoning on incompatible perceptual observations
(
19
;
31
)
. As a result, naively transplanting self-consistency or majority voting into multimodal settings is inadequate: without explicitly modeling the reliability, complementarity, and conflicts of perceptual evidence, aggregating trajectories can simply amplify noise.

To address this gap, we propose
Test-Time Scaling over Perception (TTSP)
, a framework that extends test-time scaling from reasoning over language to active search over visual evidence. As is shown in Figure
1
, the central idea is to treat perception itself as a scalable inference process. Rather than committing to a single tool-use trajectory, TTSP allocates test-time computation to generate multiple exploratory perception paths, applies confidence-aware selection to retain reliable observations, and distills these observations into structured intermediate knowledge that guides subsequent rounds of visual exploration. In this way, TTSP transforms tool use from one-shot guesswork into an iterative process of perceptual hypothesis testing: independent explorations probe different candidate regions, stable evidence is consolidated, conflicting observations are made explicit, and later inference is directed toward resolving the remaining uncertainty. The final prediction therefore emerges not from a single grounded trajectory, but from progressively refined perceptual consensus.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We identify the
Grounding Paradox
as a fundamental obstacle in tool-augmented visual reasoning, and show that it provides a principled explanation for the characteristic failure modes of current
Thinking with Images
systems.

•

We introduce
TTSP
, a principled framework for
test-time scaling over perception
that enables multimodal models to explore, validate, and iteratively refine visual evidence under uncertainty, rather than relying on a single potentially misgrounded trajectory.

•

We empirically demonstrate that
TTSP
consistently outperforms existing baselines across diverse visual reasoning benchmarks, providing a stronger foundation for advancing the
Thinking with Images
paradigm.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Test-time Scaling

Recent work on large language models has shown that increasing inference-time computation can substantially improve reasoning performance
(
10
;
14
)
. A major line of research achieves this through
test-time scaling
, where models generate multiple reasoning trajectories and aggregate them via self-consistency
(
32
;
20
;
2
)
, reranking
(
34
)
, verifier-based selection
(
5
)
, or search
(
39
)
. These approaches have been especially successful in language-only settings, where the input is fixed and fully observed, and where diversity across trajectories mainly reflects alternative latent reasoning paths rather than uncertainty in evidence acquisition. More broadly, this line of work has established that model performance is shaped not only by training-time optimization and model scale, but also by how computation is allocated at inference time
(
25
;
24
)
.

However, existing test-time scaling methods are largely designed for textual reasoning and do not directly address the challenges of multimodal perception. In visual reasoning, different trajectories may inspect different image regions, collect different observations, and condition subsequent reasoning on incomplete or conflicting perceptual evidence, making naive aggregation insufficient and potentially noisy. Our work builds on the central insight of test-time scaling while extending it beyond reasoning over fixed inputs to active evidence acquisition over partially observed visual scenes. In this sense, TTSP differs fundamentally from prior approaches by treating perception itself, rather than only downstream reasoning, as the object of scalable inference.

2.2.
Thinking with Images

Multimodal large language models have recently evolved from passive one-shot image understanding toward more interactive forms of visual reasoning, often described as
Thinking with Images

(
1
;
43
;
41
;
13
)
. In this paradigm, models are equipped with visual tools such as zooming, cropping, region selection, and high-resolution reinspection, allowing them to actively query image content during inference instead of relying solely on a single global visual representation
(
4
)
. This direction has improved performance on tasks that require localized evidence, including small-text recognition, chart and document understanding, dense scene interpretation, and fine-grained question answering, and has also inspired related efforts on iterative grounding, external perception modules, and agent-style multimodal tool use
(
26
;
17
;
8
;
35
;
21
)
.

Nevertheless, current approaches often focus on teaching models when and how to invoke tools, improving grounding accuracy on familiar distributions, or imitating successful tool-use behaviors, while remaining brittle when the correct evidence cannot be reliably localized from coarse initial perception
(
38
;
6
;
12
;
11
)
. As a result, models may inspect irrelevant regions, miss critical evidence, or fail to recover from early misgrounded actions. Our work differs from prior
Thinking with Images
systems by treating such failures not as incidental tool-use errors but as manifestations of a deeper inference problem. We identify the
Grounding Paradox
as the core obstacle underlying unreliable visual tool use and propose TTSP as a principled framework for multi-trajectory perceptual exploration, confidence-aware evidence selection, and iterative refinement of grounding under uncertainty.

3.
Method

3.1.
Preliminaries

We consider the visual question answering setting in which a model receives
an image
I\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}
, a natural-language
question
Q
, and optionally a set of candidate answers

\mathcal{O}=\{O_{1},\dots,O_{m}\}
, and must produce a response
A
. The
model is equipped with a visual tool

\mathcal{T}
that, given a bounding box
b=[x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2}]
, returns
a cropped and resized sub-image

\mathcal{T}(b):I\mapsto I_{\mathrm{crop}}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{H^{\prime}\times W^{\prime}\times 3}
, providing a magnified view of the specified region. A

perception trace

\tau=(r_{1},t_{1},r_{2},t_{2},\dots,r_{L})
is an
interleaved sequence of reasoning segments
r_{i}
and tool
invocations
t_{i}
(together with the returned crops), terminating in a
final segment from which the answer is extracted.

As established in Section
1
, the Grounding Paradox implies
that producing an effective tool invocation requires fine-grained
information that is available only
after
the tool has been
correctly invoked. Let
R^{*}\subset I
denote the region whose
inspection is necessary to answer
Q
. A single-pass grounding function

g_{\theta}:(I,Q)\mapsto\hat{R}
succeeds only if

\mathrm{IoU}(\hat{R},R^{*})\geq\delta
, where
\delta
is a task-specific
localization threshold. However, estimating
R^{*}
with sufficient precision
already presupposes access to the very evidence that
\mathcal{T}
is
designed to reveal. TTSP circumvents this circularity by
replacing the single estimate
\hat{R}
with a diverse set of

K
parallel hypotheses
\{\hat{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
. The probability that
at least one hypothesis covers
R^{*}
grows as

(1)

P\!\bigl(\exists\,i:\mathrm{IoU}(\hat{R}_{i},R^{*})\geq\delta\bigr)=1-\prod_{i=1}^{K}P\!\bigl(\mathrm{IoU}(\hat{R}_{i},R^{*})<\delta\bigr),

which increases with
K
as long as each hypothesis has non-negligible
success probability. The subsequent components of TTSP are
designed to identify which hypotheses succeeded and to propagate only
their evidence forward.

3.2.
Framework Overview

TTSP formulates multimodal inference as iterative search over possible
perceptions. Rather than relying on a single tool-use trajectory, it
allocates test-time computation across
N
successive rounds, each of
which improves the model’s understanding of the image through three
coupled steps. First, TTSP samples
K
perception traces in parallel,
allowing different trajectories to explore different regions and collect
different evidence. Second, it assigns each trace a reliability score
based on token-level entropy and removes low-confidence traces before
aggregation. Third, it distills the surviving traces into a structured
knowledge memory that separates stable observations from unresolved
disagreements. This memory is then fed into the next round so that later
traces can build on validated evidence while focusing their exploration
on remaining uncertainty. After the final round, TTSP predicts the
answer by reliability-weighted aggregation over all retained traces
across all rounds. The full procedure is summarized in
Figure
2
and Algorithm
1
.

3.3.
Parallel Perceptual Exploration

In each round, TTSP generates
K
perception traces
in parallel, each constituting an independent attempt to explore the
image, invoke visual tools, and arrive at an answer. Stochastic sampling
(
T_{\mathrm{gen}}>0
) induces diversity across traces, causing them
to attend to different regions and follow different reasoning paths, providing the spatial and logical diversity required to overcome the Grounding Paradox.

When structured knowledge from previous rounds is available, conditioning
every new trace on that knowledge would risk confirmation bias.
Subsequent traces might simply reinforce prior beliefs while ignoring
contradictory evidence. TTSP therefore divides the per-round sampling
budget into two complementary pools. A fraction
\alpha
of traces,
referred to as
fresh traces
, are generated without access to
prior knowledge. These traces preserve broad coverage of the visual
space and maintain the possibility of discovering evidence missed in
earlier rounds. The remaining fraction
(1-\alpha)
, referred to as

guided traces
, are conditioned on the structured knowledge memory.
They exploit accumulated evidence by building on confirmed observations
and targeting unresolved conflicts. This design yields an explicit
exploration–exploitation trade-off, analogous in spirit to strategies
used in bandits and Monte Carlo tree search, but instantiated here over
the space of perceptual hypotheses.

For every generated token
y_{t}
, TTSP records the top-
k_{\ell}

log probabilities from the model’s output distribution. These values are
available as a by-product of decoding and are used in the next stage to
estimate the reliability of each trace.

3.4.
Reliability Scoring and Filtering

Not all perception traces are equally reliable. Some may inspect
irrelevant regions, hallucinate visual details, or arrive at answers
through unstable reasoning. To prevent such traces from contaminating
downstream aggregation, TTSP employs a lightweight reliability estimator based on token-level predictive entropy.

For a trace
\tau_{i}
of length
L_{i}
, let
H_{t}
denote the Shannon
entropy of the renormalized top-
k_{\ell}
distribution at token
position
t
:

(2)

H_{t}=-\sum_{j=1}^{k_{\ell}}\hat{p}_{j}^{(t)}\log\hat{p}_{j}^{(t)},\qquad\hat{p}_{j}^{(t)}=\frac{\exp(\log p_{j}^{(t)})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{k_{\ell}}\exp(\log p_{j^{\prime}}^{(t)})}.

Averaging entropy uniformly across all tokens is not sufficiently
discriminative, since most tokens correspond to routine linguistic
content generated with near-zero uncertainty. The informative positions
are instead the trace’s highest-uncertainty decision points, such as
selecting bounding-box coordinates, interpreting subtle local evidence,
or committing to a final answer. TTSP therefore defines the reliability
score as the negative mean entropy over the
k^{\prime}
highest-entropy tokens:

(3)

\mathrm{score}(\tau_{i})=-\frac{1}{k^{\prime}}\sum_{t\in\mathrm{top}\text{-}k^{\prime}(\{H_{t}\})}H_{t}.

Higher scores, i.e., less negative values, indicate traces whose most
critical decision points were resolved with greater confidence and thus
serve as an efficient proxy for trace quality.

Within each round, traces are ranked by their reliability scores and the
bottom
\rho
fraction is discarded. The retained set
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is
used both for knowledge extraction and for final answer aggregation.
This filtering step is critical: without it, noisy or hallucinated
traces could enter the structured knowledge memory and systematically
bias later rounds.

3.5.
Knowledge Extraction and Iterative Refinement

The central mechanism through which TTSP converts additional test-time
compute into progressively better perception is its
structured
knowledge memory
, an evolving state that summarizes validated evidence
from prior rounds and directs future exploration toward unresolved
uncertainty. At the end of each round, TTSP distills the filtered traces
into a memory with two complementary components:
Confirmed
Knowledge
and
Open Conflicts
.

Confirmed Knowledge contains observations that are consistently
supported across independent retained traces and verified against the
image. These entries are concise, spatially grounded, and treated as
established facts in subsequent rounds, thereby reducing redundant
re-inspection. Open Conflicts record unresolved disagreements across
traces. Each conflict specifies the competing claims and, crucially, a
spatial directive indicating which region should be examined to resolve
the disagreement. In this way, TTSP converts abstract uncertainty into
concrete perceptual actions. This two-tier representation reflects an
important asymmetry in the value of information: confirmed facts reduce
redundant computation, whereas unresolved conflicts identify the
observations most likely to change the final prediction.

The memory is updated after each non-terminal round using a
conservative yet correctable evidence accumulation policy. Previously
confirmed facts are carried forward unless they are contradicted by the
current round’s evidence, in which case they are demoted to open
conflicts. Existing conflicts are promoted to confirmed knowledge once
the new round provides sufficient consensus. Newly observed facts are
added to the confirmed tier only when they satisfy strict criteria,
including cross-trace consistency, direct visual support, compatibility
with existing confirmed knowledge, and relevance to the question. Newly
emerging disagreements are recorded as additional open conflicts with
associated spatial directives. This conservative update strategy
prioritizes precision in the confirmed tier: falsely confirming an
incorrect fact would bias every subsequent round, making false positives
substantially more harmful than temporary omissions.

Knowledge extraction itself is implemented as a single deterministic
inference call with greedy decoding. The model is given the image, the
question, the previous memory state, and a representative subset of the
current round’s filtered traces, and is asked to synthesize them into
updated Confirmed Knowledge and Open Conflicts. Unlike stochastic trace
generation, this step is not intended to generate diverse hypotheses but
to faithfully summarize the evidence already collected.

For rounds
n>1
, the memory
\mathcal{K}_{n-1}
is injected into the
system prompt of guided traces. Confirmed Knowledge is presented as
reliable evidence that need not be re-verified, thereby preserving the
exploration budget. Open Conflicts, by contrast, are highlighted as
priority investigation targets, prompting guided traces to inspect the
relevant regions and adjudicate among competing claims. As a result,
later rounds progressively shift from undirected search toward focused
investigation of residual uncertainty.

After the final round, TTSP predicts the answer by
reliability-weighted aggregation over all retained traces

\mathcal{F}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{F}_{n}
.
Each trace
\tau_{i}
casts a vote for its predicted answer
a_{i}
with
weight

(4)

w_{i}=\exp\!\bigl(\mathrm{score}(\tau_{i})/\gamma\bigr),\qquad A^{*}=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{O}}\sum_{\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{F}:a_{i}=a}w_{i},

where
\gamma>0
is a temperature parameter controlling the sharpness
of the weighting. This formulation interpolates smoothly between uniform
voting (
\gamma\to\infty
) and winner-take-all selection
(
\gamma\to 0
), allowing TTSP to leverage the collective evidence of
all retained traces while assigning greater influence to the most
reliable ones.

4.2.
Main Results

Tables

and
2
report the main results. TTSP consistently achieves the best performance across benchmarks, backbone sizes, and evaluation settings. On the high-resolution benchmarks, TTSP improves substantially over the underlying Qwen3-VL baselines and also surpasses strong inference-time methods such as self-consistency, iterative refinement, confidence-based selection, and the concurrent approach RTWI. Similar trends are observed on MME-RealWorld-Lite, where TTSP yields gains on both perception-oriented and reasoning-oriented subsets. These results indicate that the benefits of TTSP are not merely a consequence of sampling more trajectories, but arise from how test-time computation is structured.

The gains of TTSP are especially pronounced on tasks requiring precise evidence localization and fine-grained perception. This is consistent with our central hypothesis: in multimodal reasoning, the primary bottleneck often lies not in reasoning over already observed facts, but in reliably discovering the relevant facts from the image. By combining parallel perceptual exploration, entropy-based reliability filtering, and iterative refinement through structured knowledge, TTSP increases the probability of uncovering critical evidence, suppresses unreliable trajectories, and progressively directs later rounds toward unresolved perceptual uncertainty.

Overall, these results provide strong empirical support for our central claim that scaling
perception
, rather than only downstream reasoning, is an effective and general strategy for improving multimodal visual reasoning.

w/o WA

Overall, the ablation results confirm that TTSP benefits from the
combination of diverse perceptual exploration, confidence-aware trace
selection, iterative knowledge refinement, and reliability-weighted
decision aggregation. These components work together to turn raw
test-time compute into more accurate and more stable multimodal
reasoning.

Scaling with Model Size.

Figure

(a) shows that TTSP scales favorably with model size. As the backbone becomes stronger, all methods benefit from improved base perception and reasoning ability, yet TTSP maintains a clear advantage throughout. This result suggests that TTSP is complementary to parameter scaling rather than replaced by it. Increasing model capacity alone does not resolve the core difficulty identified by the Grounding Paradox, namely how to allocate perceptual effort when the relevant evidence is not yet grounded. TTSP continues to provide gains on larger backbones because it improves the search process through which evidence is acquired, filtered, and consolidated at test time.

Scaling with Perception Width.

Figure

perception width
, i.e., the number of rollouts sampled per iteration. As the width increases, TTSP exhibits a clear upward trend and consistently outperforms alternative test-time scaling strategies. This behavior supports our motivation from the Grounding Paradox: increasing the number of parallel perception traces improves the chance that at least one trace attends to the correct evidence region. Importantly, TTSP makes better use of additional width than standard self-consistency-style approaches, because unreliable traces are filtered out before aggregation and useful evidence is carried forward through structured knowledge rather than being treated as independent one-shot samples.

Scaling with Perception Depth.

Figure
3
(c) analyzes scaling in
perception depth
, i.e., increasing the number of iterative rounds. Here TTSP also improves steadily, showing that additional rounds are beneficial when test-time computation is organized as progressive perceptual refinement rather than flat resampling. Each round consolidates validated observations and surfaces unresolved conflicts, allowing subsequent exploration to focus increasingly on the regions most relevant to the final decision. This result highlights that the effectiveness of TTSP derives not only from broader parallel exploration, but also from deeper sequential refinement of perception. Taken together, these findings support our central claim that perception itself is a scalable inference process, and that both width and depth can be exploited to improve multimodal reasoning under perceptual uncertainty.

Complexity and Efficiency

Computational Complexity Analysis

The computational cost of TTSP is dominated by perception-trace
generation. With
N
rounds and
K
traces per round, the total number
of model forward passes scales as

\mathcal{O}(N\cdot K\cdot T_{\max})
, where
T_{\max}
denotes the
maximum number of interaction turns per trace. In addition, knowledge
extraction introduces only
N-1
extra greedy inference calls, one
after each non-terminal round, which are negligible relative to the
cost of parallel trace generation. Reliability filtering is lightweight,
requiring only score computation, sorting, and thresholding, with
per-round complexity
\mathcal{O}(K\log K)
. Since
N
is small in
practice, TTSP does not introduce a qualitatively different asymptotic
complexity class compared with standard multi-sample inference; rather,
it reorganizes inference-time compute into a structured process of
exploration, filtering, and refinement.

Token Efficiency

Beyond asymptotic complexity, we also evaluate practical efficiency by
comparing TTSP with the concurrent method RTWI in terms of the total
number of generated tokens during inference. For each dataset, we repeat
the measurement five times and report the average token consumption.
Figure
4
shows that TTSP consistently requires fewer
tokens than RTWI across all evaluated settings. This result is
particularly notable because TTSP includes additional knowledge
extraction steps. Despite this extra overhead, TTSP reduces overall
token usage by explicitly consolidating shared observations into
structured knowledge, thereby avoiding redundant re-inspection of the
same regions and enabling later traces to focus on unresolved conflicts.
In other words, TTSP improves efficiency not by reducing the amount of
reasoning performed, but by allocating that reasoning more effectively.

Hyperparameter Sensitivity.

We study the sensitivity of TTSP to two key hyperparameters: the
fresh-trace ratio
\alpha
and the filtering ratio
\rho
.
Figure
5
shows that both exhibit a clear
non-monotonic trend, with the best performance achieved in an
intermediate range rather than at either extreme.
For the fresh-trace ratio, moderate values perform best across
benchmarks. Setting
\alpha
too low overemphasizes guided traces and
reduces exploratory diversity, while setting it too high weakens the
benefit of knowledge-guided refinement. This confirms the importance of
balancing exploration and exploitation in perceptual search. For the
filtering ratio, moderate filtering is likewise most effective.
Insufficient filtering allows noisy traces to contaminate aggregation
and knowledge extraction, whereas overly aggressive filtering removes
useful evidence and reduces trace diversity. In our experiments,

\alpha=0.4
and
\rho=0.4
provide a strong and robust operating
point across datasets.

5.
Conclusion

We identify the
Grounding Paradox
as a core challenge in
tool-augmented multimodal reasoning: a model must localize evidence
that it cannot yet perceive. To address this problem, we propose

Test-Time Scaling over Perception (TTSP)
, which scales
perception through parallel exploration, reliability-aware filtering,
structured knowledge refinement, and weighted aggregation.
Experiments across diverse benchmarks show that TTSP consistently
outperforms strong baselines while maintaining favorable scalability and
efficiency.

We further analyze how TTSP changes the model’s inference behavior
across rounds. In particular, we study two indicators of test-time
efficiency: the number of tool calls and the length of generated
reasoning. As shown in Figure

TTSP effectively leverages the structured knowledge accumulated from
earlier exploration.

B.1.
Analytic Study on Tool Usage

The left panel of Figure

tool calls per round. Across all evaluated benchmarks, the number of
tool calls decreases consistently as the round index increases. This
trend indicates that later rounds rely less on repeated visual
inspection and instead make more targeted use of the zoom-in tool.
Rather than re-examining the image from scratch, the model is able to
build on previously extracted knowledge and focus only on unresolved
details. This result provides direct evidence that TTSP improves the
efficiency of perceptual exploration by reducing redundant tool usage

C.2.
Evaluation Prompts

To ensure reproducibility, we provide additional details on the prompts
used in TTSP during evaluation. The prompting protocol follows the
three-stage structure of our framework: (1)
parallel perceptual
exploration
, in which the model actively inspects the image through
tool use; (2)
knowledge extraction
, in which the model
critically reviews multiple retained traces and distills them into
structured knowledge; and (3)
iterative context building
, in
which the extracted knowledge is prepended to the next round to guide
subsequent exploration.
The exact prompt templates used in our implementation are provided below.

Parallel perception prompts.

In the perceptual exploration stage, the model is instructed to behave
as a visual research assistant that answers image-grounded questions by
iteratively observing, zooming, and revising its understanding. The
prompt explicitly encourages a loop of: (i) examining the image and
identifying what is already visible, (ii) invoking the zoom-in tool to
inspect missing or uncertain details, and (iii) reviewing the returned
evidence before deciding the next action. To ensure standardized answer
extraction, the final response is required to be wrapped in

\boxed{}
. For multiple-choice tasks, the model
is instructed to output only the option letter.

The zoom-in tool is exposed as a function call, which takes as input a normalized
bounding box, a textual region label, and an image index. This tool
interface enables the model to inspect fine-grained local regions while
maintaining a consistent calling format across all benchmarks.

Knowledge extraction prompts.

After trace generation and reliability filtering, TTSP performs a
separate knowledge extraction step. In this stage, the model is prompted
as a
critical visual knowledge auditor
. It receives a set of
filtered traces from the current round, together with the previous
round’s structured knowledge state when available, and is asked to
produce an updated two-tier memory consisting of
Confirmed Knowledge and Open Conflicts. The prompt
explicitly specifies four operations: inheriting previously confirmed
facts unless contradicted, resolving previously open conflicts, adding
new confirmed facts only when they satisfy strict agreement and
grounding criteria, and flagging newly emerged conflicts relevant to the
question.

To improve precision, the prompt emphasizes conservative evidence
admission. Confirmed Knowledge is restricted to directly observable,
question-relevant facts that are consistent across traces and unlikely
to be hallucinated. Open Conflicts, in contrast, explicitly record
disagreements together with the corresponding competing claims and
spatial directives indicating where the model should inspect in the next
round.

Iterative context building.

From the second round onward, the structured knowledge extracted from
previous rounds is prepended to the system prompt of guided traces.
This context is organized into the two tiers above. Confirmed Knowledge
is presented as reliable evidence that can be used directly without
repeated verification, while Open Conflicts are highlighted as the main
targets for further investigation. The prompt therefore encourages later
rounds to focus their perceptual effort on unresolved uncertainty rather
than restarting from scratch. In contrast, fresh traces are generated
without access to prior knowledge in order to preserve exploratory
diversity.

Overall, the prompting design mirrors the core logic of TTSP: multiple
trajectories first explore the image independently, reliable evidence is
then distilled into structured intermediate knowledge, and subsequent
exploration is steered by this knowledge toward unresolved perceptual
ambiguity.

Appendix D
Limitations and Future Work

Despite its effectiveness, TTSP has several limitations. First, it is
still a test-time scaling method and therefore incurs additional
inference cost compared with single-trajectory decoding, even though our
results show that it uses computation more efficiently than competing
multi-step baselines. Second, the effectiveness of TTSP still depends on
the capabilities of the underlying multimodal backbone and visual tools:
if the base model cannot produce sufficiently diverse or partially
correct perceptual hypotheses, the benefits of multi-trajectory
exploration may be limited. Third, the current structured knowledge
mechanism is implemented through prompting and deterministic decoding,
which, while effective in practice, remains a relatively lightweight and
heuristic form of memory construction.

These limitations suggest several directions for future work. One
promising direction is to develop more adaptive allocation strategies
for test-time compute, such as dynamically adjusting exploration width,
refinement depth, or filtering strength based on the estimated
difficulty of each example. Another is to design more explicit and
learnable forms of perceptual memory and evidence consolidation, which
could improve both robustness and interpretability. Finally, it would be
valuable to extend TTSP beyond the current image-based setting to more
complex multimodal scenarios, such as multi-image reasoning, video
understanding, and embodied visual agents, where perceptual uncertainty
and tool-use planning are even more central.
