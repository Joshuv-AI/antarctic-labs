History of Presentation

An 89-year-old woman with severe degenerative aortic stenosis presented for transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) evaluation given progressive exertional dyspnea (NYHA functional class III).

Past Medical History

The patient's medical history included coronary artery disease with multiple percutaneous interventions to the proximal right coronary artery (RCA), paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, and hyperlipidemia.

Investigations

Pre-TAVR coronary angiography at the referring hospital demonstrated a patent left coronary system (Video 1). Selective engagement of the RCA was not successful because of difficulty engaging the vessel. Nonselective injections revealed an ostial stent that was placed 3 years before and was protruding into the aortic root (Video 2). Once shallowly engaged, nonselective angiography confirmed severe proximal RCA in-stent restenosis. Computed tomography before TAVR showed the ostial RCA stent extending 7.8 mm into the aortic root (Figures 1 and 2). Virtual placement of an aortic valve would have resulted in significant interaction between the stent and the valve (Figures 3 and 4). Subsequently, the patient was referred to our hospital for resolution before TAVR procedure.

Management

Given the concern for interference with TAVR deployment, percutaneous stent removal was planned. The procedure was performed through femoral arterial access. An 8-F JR4 guiding catheter was inserted into the aortic root, and a 15-mm Goose Neck snare (Medtronic) was used to capture the protruding segment of the ostial RCA stent. The Goose Neck snare captured the stent closer to the RCA ostium, and when the system was pulled as a unit, the stent was successfully removed from the coronary artery (Video 3). Given the location of Goose Neck capture, the stent folded and could not be collapsed into the guiding catheter but was successfully retrieved through the 8-F sheath (Figure 5, Figure 6, Figure 7). The entire system, consisting of removed stent, snare, and guiding catheter, was removed without complications. Repeat angiography immediately after stent removal showed TIMI flow grade 3 and moderate to severe ostial RCA stenosis without any evidence of flow-limiting dissections (Video 4). After lesion preparation with noncompliant and scoring balloons, intravascular ultrasound was performed to confirm adequate plaque modification. The lesion was treated with 2 overlapping drug-eluting stents, sized 3.5 × 20 mm and 4.0 × 16 mm, optimized with high pressure postdilation (Video 5). Final intravascular ultrasound demonstrated good expansion and precise ostial positioning without aortic protrusion (Figure 8).

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient tolerated the procedure without complication and successfully underwent TAVR 1 week later with no RCA-related issues (Video 6). She remained asymptomatic 1 year after her procedures.

Discussion

Aorto-ostial stenosis poses a unique challenge. Even in native lesions, interventions at the aorto-ostial location are technically demanding and are associated with higher complication rates, including increased risk of in-stent restenosis.1 Current practice recommends stenting these lesions with a slight overhang (typically 1-2 mm) into the aorta to ensure complete coverage of the ostium, which is of critical importance.2 However, excessive protrusion may result in major complications, including difficulty with catheter re-engagement, coronary cusp perforation, severe aortic insufficiency with pulmonary edema, stent fracture, and incomplete re-endothelialization of struts.3, 4, 5, 6

Although coronary stents protruding into the aorta are rarely reported, their management must be individualized. If the protruding stent results in mechanical complications or symptoms, percutaneous stent removal may be attempted.4 However, retrieval carries a high risk of restenosis or vessel occlusion due to intimal injury.7 Additional strategies to prevent or manage complications include flaring the proximal stent struts against the aortic wall using an Ostial Flash balloon (Verge Medical).8 This was not considered in our case for 2 reasons. First, this requires engagement of the stent and having a coronary guidewire through the true lumen of the stent and not through the side struts, both of which proved difficult. Second, although the Ostial Flash balloon is an important tool to allow flaring of an ostial stent, the length of the aortic overhang in our case was excessive. Using this balloon would not result in full flaring of the overhanging segment. In certain cases, a double-wire technique has been described to facilitate access into the true lumen of a protruding stent and allow treatment of in-stent restenosis; however, this technique may not be feasible in the setting of significant stent deformation.9

When the protruding segment is exceptionally long, removal of the stent should be explored. In our case, the risk of abrupt vessel closure during ostial stent removal was mitigated by the presence of an old stent in the proximal segment of the coronary artery. Embolization of the debris or the stent itself is another risk consideration during stent removal. This risk can be mitigated by accurate snaring techniques and the use of larger bore guide catheters and sheaths to enable complete removal of the snared stent through the sheath. Stent removal through radial artery access should be avoided to decrease the risk of stent loss in the upper extremity arteries. Our case verifies that percutaneous stentectomy using a snare is a viable bailout strategy for malpositioned coronary stents, especially when protrusion poses added risks for future interventions.
