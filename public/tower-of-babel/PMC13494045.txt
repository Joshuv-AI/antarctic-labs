A scoping review: ketamine or esketamine when used to assist psychotherapy or in combination with psychotherapy for the management of depression in adults
Authors: J. Swainson, P. Grewal, S. Polard, C. Borle, G. Gutierrez, Y. Zhang, O. Winkler, L. Burback, A. Khullar, R. S. McIntyre, E. Brietzke, C. N. Soares
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This scoping review maps literature for use of ketamine or esketamine with psychotherapy for depression or depressive symptoms. Forty-seven studies were identified including 4/47 (9%) randomized controlled trials, 4/47 (9%) open label trials, 27/47 (57%) observational or retrospective studies, and 12/47 (26%) individual case reports. Studies were categorized as Ketamine/Esketamine Combined with Psychotherapy (KCP/ES-KCP), where an altered state is not necessary for psychotherapy, or Ketamine/Esketamine Assisted Psychotherapy (KAP/ES-KAP), where an altered state and/or integration of the experience is central to the psychotherapy. The 47 included 23/47(49%) KAP, 1/47 (2%) ES-KAP, 7/47 (15%) KCP, 3/47 (6%) ES-KCP, 8/47 (17%) dual KAP/KCP and 1/47 (2%) dual ES-KAP/ES-KCP. Four studies could not be categorized. Separation into these categories was useful to more clearly report current state of the literature. Though previous reviews have suggested KAP is of benefit for depression, KAP studies were limited by lack of RCTs, lack of comparison groups without psychotherapy, and a large number of individual case reports. To date, fewer studies, but higher quality data support a KCP model, with 3 studies suggesting benefit to combining ketamine with cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT). Further, high quality research including accurate reporting and description of psychotherapy model is required.

Introduction

Psychedelics and psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy (PAP) are under intense discussion in psychiatry. Due to restrictions on psychedelic use, and the ability of ketamine to induce altered states, ketamine-assisted psychotherapy (KAP) has rapidly been promoted as a legalized alternative to PAP, ahead of the empirical evidence. Not a classical psychedelic, ketamine is an NMDA antagonist and categorized as a dissociative anesthetic. At subanesthetic doses, it has been used as an off-label pharmacotherapy to treat major depressive disorder (MDD)1–3 and bipolar depression (BD)4. Pharmacotherapy data is also emerging for the use of ketamine to treat post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)5,6, obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD)6, and substance use disorders (SUD)6,7, among others.

Ketamine (or racemic ketamine), initially used as an anesthetic agent is composed of a mixture of of two enantiomers—R-ketamine (arketamine) and S-ketamine (esketamine)8. Subsequently, both ketamine and esketamine have demonstrated potent antidepressant effects in clinical populations9. Only esketamine, however, has been approved in the United States, Canada, and much of Europe as a nasal spray to treat MDD that has not responded to traditional treatments10–12. While initially approved as an adjunctive treatment for use with an oral antidepressant10,13, intranasal (IN) esketamine has recently also been approved as monotherapy for treatment-resistant depression in the United States. Comparatively, the use of racemic ketamine remains off-label2. While it has been suggested that esketamine is a stronger blocker of the NMDA receptor than racemic ketamine and may have a milder side effect profile9,14, a 2021 meta-analysis of 24 studies found that ketamine demonstrated superior response (RR = 3.01 vs. RR = 1.38) and remission rates (RR = 3.70 vs. RR = 1.47) as well as lower drop-out rates (RR = 0.76 vs. RR = 1.37)15.

The available literature on KAP has explored the use of both ketamine and esketamine in treatment models that include psychotherapy. Within the established antidepressant dose range, ketamine and esketamine elicit transient altered states of consciousness with widely varying phenomenology, depending on dose and mode of administration, and individual factors. Typically, experiences include a sense of separation of mind from body. Other experiences include ego dissolution, time out from usual identity, out-of-body/out-of-bounded-time/out-of-bounded-place experiences, shifts from verbal to emotional or somatic processing, reductions of external sensory experiences, increased focus on internal experiences including visions, and symbolic or metaphorical representation of trauma or other unconscious material, among others16.

Within the pharmacotherapeutic context, these effects are considered as treatment emergent adverse events (TEAEs) and considered unnecessary for the medication’s antidepressant efficacy17. However, those utilizing ketamine to augment psychotherapy challenge this view as reductive, suggesting its potential utility to enhance psychotherapy18. For example, ketamine may reduce overwhelming emotions and bodily sensations, or promote perspective-taking. Symbolic or metaphorical representations, such as visual imagery, may represent traumatic experiences or unconscious material that carries significance and bears meaning. While less common than with classic psychedelics, ketamine has also been known to induce mystical experiences, which are associated with positive outcomes in PAP trials19,20. KAP often includes exploration of such experiences during integration sessions for psychological benefit.

Antidepressant effects of ketamine pharmacotherapy are short-lived, often lasting only weeks after multiple treatments2. With that in mind, it has been considered that integration of the material arising from the dissociative experience or altered state induced by ketamine may produce better effects and/or more lasting changes. Alternatively, it has been considered that existing psychotherapies such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), which is conducted in ordinary, unmedicated states, may extend ketamine’s response if conducted in parallel to ketamine administration21. While both approaches described above have been referred to as KAP, they are distinct. Members of our group have previously highlighted these differences and have strongly suggested the use of different terms for each model to support clarity22. More specifically, psychotherapies where ketamine’s dissociative or psychedelic effects are considered central to a therapy process could be considered KAP. On the other hand, psychotherapy modalities conducted in parallel to ketamine treatment that do not utilize the altered stated arising from ketamine could more accurately be described as Ketamine Combined with Psychotherapy (KCP)22. In KCP, ketamine is thought to enhance psychotherapy by both its rapid reduction in depressive symptoms that can interfere with psychotherapy, and by its neuroplastic effects potentially improving psychotherapy outcomes. This is also separate from the simple use of ketamine for pharmacotherapy (KP). We will use these terms throughout this review.

To date, several systematic reviews have reported that KAP shows promise for treating depression, including treatment-resistant depression23–27, but largely have not differentiated psychotherapy treatment models, thereby risking misrepresentation of available data, and potentially over or understating the evidence base for either a KAP or KCP approach. Interestingly, one recent review categorized KAP interventions as “experiential” or “structured, protocol based”, which align descriptively with our group’s previous suggested definition of KAP and KCP respectively26, but reported all results as “KAP” and concluded that KAP demonstrated promise in treatment resistant depression. Using our definitions, studies with best evidence in this review were actually KCP studies26. Inclusion of both models under the umbrella of KAP may lead to inaccurate conclusions.

Additionally, existing reports have been limited in the study types, generally excluding case series, observational studies, or retrospective reports, and have not included esketamine. We elected to conduct a scoping review to more broadly explore the breadth of available evidence and identify knowledge gaps in the literature28. This review provides results of a search accurate to March 2026 in a rapidly growing field, and is more comprehensive than existing reviews because it (1) includes esketamine, (2) includes all study types including case reports, (3) catalogs current ongoing clinical trials, and (4) categorizes psychotherapy models as KAP or esketamine assisted psychotherapy (ES-KAP) and KCP or esketamine combined with psychotherapy (ES-KCP) as per proposed definitions to more accurately report the psychotherapy models in the current body of literature. These categorizations will allow us to better identify methodological and conceptual gaps to direct future research.

Methods

Protocol and registration

The protocol for this review was registered on Open Science Framework (OSF). This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR)29 (Supplementary Table S1).

Eligibility criteria

We adopted an inclusive approach and sought to identify all studies that focused on adults diagnosed with depressive disorders or depressive symptoms. This could include a diagnosed condition such as MDD, treatment-resistant depression (TRD), or bipolar disorder (BD). To ensure comprehensiveness, we also included any other depressive syndromes such as dysthymia, persistent depressive disorder, or self-reported depressive symptoms, so long as the population received ketamine or esketamine in conjunction with any type of psychotherapy intervention, either for KAP/ES-KAP or KCP/ES-KCP. Though computerized interventions are distinct from therapist-led psychotherapy, they were included due to rising popularity of internet or computerized manualized psychotherapies such as online CBT. Sensory interventions such as music, eye masks, or virtual reality were excluded unless they were connected with a described psychotherapy process. Similarly, brief mindfulness interventions were excluded unless they were recurrent as part of a structured treatment plan alongside ketamine or esketamine treatment.

For comprehensiveness, studies where the primary diagnosis under investigation was not a depressive disorder were included, as long as the study included some pre- and post-assessment of depression symptoms. The ketamine/esketamine could be administered via any route at any dosage.

We also included all empirical study types, including randomized controlled trials, open label studies, observational studies, retrospective case series, and individual case reports. Case series and case reports were included to capture early experiential evidence and emerging clinical practices. Exclusion criteria included participants less than 18 years of age, studies including ketamine or esketamine as pharmacotherapy without any psychotherapy intervention, and studies published in languages other than English. Studies lacking full text were excluded, as were reviews. Reference lists of review articles were scanned to ensure no relevant reports were missed.

Search

An initial limited search of MEDLINE and APA PsycINFO was completed using the keywords “ketamine”, “depression” and “psychotherapy”, and the results were analyzed to identify synonyms and subject headings. A full search strategy was then developed by SP, an experienced medical librarian, in consultation with the research team. The keywords and subject headings were peer reviewed by another medical librarian using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategy guidance (PRESS)18 On May 6, 2025 searches were conducted in APA PsycInfo, EMBASE, MEDLINE, Proquest Dissertations & Theses, and Google Scholar. The detailed search strategy is provided in the supplementary materials in Table S2. An updated search was conducted on March 2, 2026.

Resulting citations were imported into Covidence, a web-based tool designed to streamline the review process by facilitating the importation, screening, and data extraction of studies. After de-duplication, studies were independently screened first for title and abstract then by full text by two of four members of the review team (CB, JS, GG and PG) to assess against the previously described eligibility criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved by bringing in a 3rd of these four team members for discussion and to reach consensus. Reference lists for relevant review articles were checked, and forward citation searching was conducted for eligible studies in Scopus. To evaluate emerging research, we also conducted a search of clinicaltrials.gov on March 4, 2026 (Search Strategy may be found in Supplementary Table 3). Inclusion criteria were as above, and terminated or withdrawn trials were excluded. Completed trials were cross checked with the list of published studies and any duplicates were excluded.

Data extraction

Once full text studies were selected for inclusion, data were extracted independently onto a template on a spreadsheet. Items extracted were broad in scope and included authors, year, title, country, funding sources, study design, sample size, population demographics (sex, age, diagnosis), details regarding ketamine intervention (mode of administration, dose, frequency, number of treatments), details regarding psychotherapy intervention (modality of therapy, timing of therapy relative to ketamine dosing, number of sessions), setting, clinician training, therapist qualifications, primary and secondary outcomes, measurement tools, side effects (physical and psychological), safety protocols, informed consent, ethics approval, regulatory issues, patient access, cultural barriers, suggested study limitations, future research recommendations and knowledge gaps. Each study was extracted by two independent study team members (PG, GG, CB), supervised by JS. One team member (PG) prepared the final version of the extraction table, in discussions with JS and CB to resolve any differences in recording of data.

Outcome reporting

After the master extraction table was prepared, key findings were identified by the study team and summarized in a table format. Studies were organized by design from highest-quality evidence (RCTs) to lowest-quality evidence (individual case reports). Findings were synthesized narratively, and primary diagnosis, ketamine/esketamine dosing regimen, psychotherapy modality, and temporal relationship between psychotherapy and ketamine administration were reported. Studies were classified as KAP if psychotherapy was described to work with material emerging from subjective drug effects, whether this was psychedelic experience at higher doses, or a loosening of defenses from a lower, or “psycholytic” doses, and/or if the studies reported ketamine integration. Studies were classified as KCP if psychotherapy was independent of acute drug effects. In reports where psychotherapy descriptions did not clearly fit into one of these categories, they were categorized as unclear. Studies were categorized by one author (JS) and reviewed with a second author (AK). Primary and secondary outcome measures were identified, and main study findings were summarized. Consistent with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidance for scoping reviews, a formal risk-of-bias assessment was not performed; however, study design, sample size, and methodological limitations were considered in narrative synthesis.

The results of the clinicaltrials.gov search were reviewed by one author (JS) to determine eligibility for inclusion as per previously described criteria. In cases where uncertainty arose as to whether to consider a particular intervention as psychotherapy, the authorship group was consulted for consensus. Descriptions of included trials were reviewed on clinicaltrials.gov, summarized, and categorized as KAP/ES-KAP or KCP/ES-KCP as per above criteria by JS and verified by AK.

Results

Literature search

A total of 1145 studies were imported into Covidence via the database literature searches. An additional 5 studies were identified by a search on Scopus and added to Covidence. Five additional studies were identified by screening the reference lists of relevant reviews. 86 duplicates were automatically removed by Covidence, and 46 duplicates were removed manually, so 1018 studies were screened at the title and abstract stage, and 161 studies were sought for full text. 141 studies were retrieved and 47 met criteria for inclusion. A total of 72 studies were found on the clinical trials search, and 15 met criteria for inclusion.

A total of 47 studies and 15 ongoing or recently completed clinical trials were identified using some combination of ketamine or esketamine with psychotherapy to treat a depressive condition or depressive symptoms, with or without other comorbid conditions. Of these, 4 were randomized controlled trials (RCTs), 4 were open label trials, 27 were observational or retrospective studies and 12 were individual case reports. Just under half of identified studies (20/47; 43%) reported on sample sizes of 10 or less, and just over one quarter of studies (12/47; 26%) reported on larger sample sizes of 50 or greater. The majority of studies (n = 38) were completed in North America (8 in Canada and 30 in the United States), 8 were conducted in Europe, and 1 was conducted in Asia. Studies were recent—only two were reported prior to 2020. Study characteristics

Overall, 10 studies reported on a population of patients with a diagnosis of MDD and 15 included patients with TRD, with various definitions across studies. Other studies reported on patients with “depression”, which was not typically defined, and/or elevated baseline ratings on a validated depression scale such as the PHQ-9, MADRS, or BDI in patients with other comorbid conditions. Comorbid conditions were common, the most common of which were PTSD/trauma-related symptoms or anxiety. Of note, 10 reports contained populations of mixed diagnoses, so it is possible that not all individuals within those samples had a depressive illness or depressive symptoms. Seven studies were classified as KCP, including 3 RCTs21,30,31, 1 open label trial32, and 2 observational or retrospective reports33,34, and one individual case report35. Twenty-three were categorized as KAP including 1 open label trial36, 14 observational or retrospective reports37–50, and 8 individual case reports51–58. Two or more psychotherapy interventions were used in one RCT59, one open label trial60, 5 observational studies61–65, and 1 individual case reports66, and we labeled these as being a dual KAP/ES-KAP and KCP/ES-KCP approach. Four studies using ketamine were labeled as an indeterminate psychotherapy model67–70 due to limited or unclear descriptions of the psychotherapy intervention and role of the altered state.

Of the esketamine studies, one open label trial71 and two observational or retrospective studies72,73 were classified as ES-KCP and one observational study was classified as ES-KAP74. An individual case report described the use of a dual ES-KAP and ES-KCP approach75.

Summary of Ketamine Combined with Psychotherapy (KCP) studies

These 7 studies generally used ketamine doses within established antidepressant ranges: IV ketamine 0.5 mg/kg (n = 5). Psychotherapy was conducted entirely separate from ketamine administration and involved structured modalities that could be conducted without ketamine.

Both an open label trial32 (n = 16) and a small (n = 28) RCT21 compared patients with and without the addition of a course of CBT for participants treated with a series of 4 or 6 IV ketamine infusions. The groups showed similar initial responses to the course of ketamine treatment, but in the RCT, the group who received CBT demonstrated an extended the duration of ketamine response compared to those who did not receive psychotherapy. The CBT occurred twice weekly throughout the ketamine treatment course, and for 8 or 12 weeks beyond.

The other two RCTs used single IV ketamine infusions. One used a computerized intervention referred to as Automated Self Association Training (ASAT), offered twice daily for 4 days76. This was a computerized intervention targeted at shifting negative cognitive bias toward the self. While distinct from therapist-led psychotherapy, it was included in this review due to increasing recognition of digital behavioral interventions for treating depression1,and its target of shifting negative cognitions, somewhat similar to a goal of CBT. This study randomized its 153 participants to one of 4 groups; ketamine with ASAT, saline with ASAT, ketamine with sham therapy, and saline with sham therapy. Both of the groups receiving ASAT showed improved mood, with greater response and remission in the ketamine with ASAT group.

The third RCT was targeted towards treating PTSD31. This study randomized 27 participants with PTSD and elevated depression ratings on the Beck Depression Inventory II (BDI-II) to receive prolonged exposure therapy (PE) for PTSD and a single 0.5 mg/kg ketamine infusion, or to PE and midazolam as an active placebo. Here, both PTSD and mood symptoms improved from baseline, but there was no significant difference between ketamine and midazolam groups, suggesting that the ketamine infusion did not enhance the effects of the PE intervention.

Of the two observational/retrospective reports, one was directed at treating TRD using repeated doses of 0.5–3 mg/kg IV ketamine paired with weekly behavioral activation (BA34), and the other was more focused on treating PTSD with trauma based therapies including cognitive restructuring and exposure therapy in parallel with a course of 0.5 mg/kg IN ketamine33. Depressive symptoms improved in both reports, but there were no comparison groups who received ketamine only or psychotherapy only in either study. The individual case report used mindfulness based cognitive processing (MCP).

Summary of Esketamine Combined with Psychotherapy (ES-KCP) studies

Of the 3 studies reporting on this model, 2 used IN esketamine71,73, and 1 used oral esketamine72. An small open label trial (n = 19) evaluated the combination of a course of 8 IN esketamine treatments with an audio guided mindful self-compassion (MSC) intervention, which took place at the end of each of the first four esketamine dosing sessions. Here, the patients who underwent MSC demonstrated a superior antidepressant response to those who received esketamine alone. In a retrospective report or 71 patients, a superior antidepressant response was noted in patients who received CBT in addition to IN esketamine, compared to those who received pharmacotherapy only73. A smaller (n = 5) retrospective report with no comparison group noted positive mood outcomes in patients receiving oral esketamine with a psychotherapy focused on treating comorbid PTSD.

Summary of Ketamine Assisted Psychotherapy (KAP) studies

Twenty-three were categorized as KAP including 1 open label trial36, 14 observational or retrospective reports37–50, and 8 individual case reports51–58. A variety of doses, timing, and descriptions of psychotherapy were noted. The majority of studies appeared to target treating depression with the psychotherapy used, but some were psychotherapies to treat PTSD or other conditions, and also reported depression scales. The majority of studies described psychotherapy as occurring before, during, and after dosing with ketamine, or during and after the dosing session. Preparation sessions were reported as psychotherapy in some studies, but in others they seemed to take place before the KAP process began, implying that they were more informational in nature. Descriptions of therapist involvement during the dosing sessions were limited. In some cases, the therapist role was described as “supportive”, creating uncertainty regarding whether this would qualify as active psychotherapy. Few studies reported on duration of dosing sessions and psychotherapy timing in relation to administration of ketamine. When it was reported that psychotherapy was conducted during the ketamine dosing session, it was infrequently reported whether psychotherapy occurred throughout the session, at the peak of ketamine’s effect, or later, when acute effects had largely abated.

An open label pilot KAP study36 in patients with MDD and chronic pain described two groups, both of which received psychotherapy. One received a lower dose (25–75 mg SL) “psycholytic” KAP and the other receiving higher dose (90–190 mg IM) “psychedelic” dose of ketamine and psychotherapy. At psycholytic doses, patients were communicative and actively participated in therapy immediately after ingesting ketamine. The psychedelic therapy group experienced a more pronounced dissociative state and mystical experiences, and active psychotherapy was conducted 24 h after dosing. In both groups, it was reported that psychotherapy used “strength and resiliency enhancement focused” eye movement desensitization reprocessing (EMDR), and authors noted that patients learned self-regulation skills, meditation, and mindfulness practices. Details of the EMDR protocol and its targets were not reported. After 6 sessions, both groups demonstrated approximately 6 points improvement in the BDI, and there was no difference between groups. However, treatment trajectories differed—the psychedelic group demonstrated worsening of depressive symptoms after treatment 1 followed by steady improvement, and the psycholytic group showed improvement steadily in treatments 1–3, then a slight worsening of depression in treatments 4–6.

Another observational study38 described psychotherapy as a somatic based interactional therapy, taking place 10 min after administration of 100–200 mg sublingual (SL) ketamine. Subsequently, integration sessions took place outside the dosing session. Another43 included 3 KAP sessions using 125–200 mg SL ketamine, then 250–275 mg SL ketamine then 70–100 mg IM ketamine, and specified that therapy took an approach similar to the MAPS (Multidisciplinary Association for Psychedelic Studies) MDMA (3,4 methylenedioxymethamphetamine) for PTSD trials. The MAPS protocol involves two trained therapists who supportively guide the patient through the psychedelic experience, sometimes engaging the patient verbally, and other times directing the patient toward their inner experience77. Another study41 reported ketamine integration to occur within 48 h of receiving SL ketamine (dose not reported), and another reported that patients engaged in therapy to discuss the content of their “journey” 3–7 days after receiving an unreported dose of IM ketamine49. Another reported use of brief psychodynamic therapy for ketamine integration one day after each of 8 weekly injections of 0.5–1.5 mg/kg IM ketamine45.

Ketamine in a KAP model was administered via various routes, and some reports described ketamine administration using more than one route at different dosing sessions. Where reports included multiple dosing sessions, doses were frequently raised during the course of treatment. Eight studies used IV ketamine39,40,44,47,51,53,57,78, with doses generally ranging from 0.5 to 1.15 mg/kg. Three studies39,40,57 reports noted use of lower doses (0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 mg/kg), which are below established antidepressant doses. One study went beyond as high as 2.1 mg/kg51. Eleven studies reported use of IM ketamine36,37,39,42,43,45,48–50,56,58—dose was not reported in three. One study30 stated that the IM dose was “individualized” and individual or group trauma-informed psychotherapy took place during the dosing session. Other studies fairly consistently reported dose ranges of 70–100 mg, though one used weight-based dosing at 0.1–1 mg/kg, and another at 0.5–1.0 mg/kg.

Intranasal ketamine was used in one case report52, with acute dosing noted as 130–260 mg and maintenance doses as 130–180 mg. Sublingual ketamine was the most common mode of non-parenteral administration, and reported on 10 occasions36–38,41,48,52,54–56,61. One did not report on dose48. Two studies referenced doses being “psycholytic” at 25–50 mg and 50–75 mg and in these cases, psychotherapy was commenced immediately after dose administration. Psychedelic dosing was mentioned also in two studies at dose ranges 200–275 mg and 90–190 mg. Other studies reported use of SL ketamine in the 100–300 mg range, though one case report went as high as 750 mg.

Summary of esketamine assisted psychotherapy studies

Only one small observational study74 reported on use of supportive therapy, EMDR and hypnosis for integration of the esketamine experience. Psychotherapy was conducted during the dosing session, including at the peak of the dissociative experience, and subsequent sessions occurred in between dosing sessions. Mood significantly improved, but there were no comparison groups and esketamine was used at antidepressant doses of 28–84 mg74.

Summary of dual psychotherapy model studies

Eight studies59–66 used both KAP and KCP psychotherapy approaches. One RCT59 was designed to determine whether choice of music during an IV ketamine infusion might affect outcomes, rather than comparing psychotherapy to no psychotherapy. This program used the “Montreal Model” for all patients. This was described as support during the infusion, post infusion integration sessions, and another evidence-based psychotherapy occurring independently from ketamine administration. A small open label trial reported on a preparation session, four SL ketamine dosing sessions accompanied by 3 h of group integration during dosing60. Participants also had four cognitive processing therapy (CPT) sessions at other times. Three retrospective reports described patients in outpatient group therapy programs62,63,65, and one reported on patients in a residential eating disorder program61. In these cases, the regular group programming was supplemented with ketamine dosing sessions and psychotherapy sessions oriented toward integration of the ketamine experience. One individual case report used a combination of ketamine integration and CBT66. One case study described a combined approach of ES-KAP and ES-KCP with intranasal esketamine75.

Summary of uncategorized studies

Four67–70 ketamine studies could not be classified as KAP or KCP. One of these was the largest study36 identified in this review. This was a retrospective report on 1806 patients from multiple treatment centers. The patient population included multiple diagnoses, which were primarily depression, anxiety disorders and PTSD. Patients at some centers were treated with SL ketamine in the range of 200–500 mg, and other centers used IM ketamine doses from 25 to 100 mg. Patients received 4–6 sessions of ketamine over 4–6 weeks followed by additional psychotherapy once ketamine treatment was complete. After one month, significant improvements were noted in mood, anxiety, and PTSD symptoms, and these were amplified at 3 months, suggesting that further psychotherapy provided additional benefit even after ketamine was discontinued.

Here, patients received a preparation psychotherapy session, and support during ketamine dosing—it was not clear if this was active psychotherapy. Ketamine integration took place outside dosing sessions and was stated to be based on motivational interviewing (MI) and behavioral activation (BA). It was not clear how these structured interventions were used specifically for integration of a ketamine induced experience, or if they were actually done in parallel, with “support” that was considered psychotherapy during the dosing session, so this study could not clearly be categorized as KAP, KCP, or a dual approach. Similarly, the case report reported use of a manualized psychotherapy for ketamine integration, but did not describe how it was applied in this context69.

Another study37 of 202 patients described the addition of psychotherapy to patients receiving a course of IV ketamine for depression. Here, a therapist provided support and grounding after ketamine administration, and psychotherapy was conducted in the same visit, though this was not stated whether this was in the midst of the acute ketamine effects. The majority of psychotherapy was described as “KAP” (80% of sessions), and 15% were CBT. The remaining 5% varied and consisted of multiple modalities. The process for KAP was not described, and it was unclear if every participant engaged in KAP plus another psychotherapy or if some did only one of KAP or non-KAP therapies. Regardless, here, the group of patients receiving psychotherapy did not show further improvement in depression than those receiving IV ketamine without psychotherapy.

One retrospective study was a survey of patients receiving treatment with ketamine, and approximately ¾ of respondents reported engaging in some type of psychotherapy throughout their ketamine treatment. A wide variety of psychotherapy types were reported, and the survey did not collect information regarding the timing of the therapy or any focus on ketamine integration. This study reported no difference in depression outcomes among those who reported engaging in psychotherapy and those who did not68.

Tolerability and adverse events

Patient tolerability and acceptability was not reported in 30/47 (64%) studies. Treatment was noted to be well accepted and/or well tolerated in 12 (26%) studies. Fifteen (32%) publications did not report on physical side effects, and 7 (15%) reported that there were no physical side effects. Twenty-seven publications (64%) did not report whether there were any adverse psychological events, and 7/47 (17%) reported there were no adverse psychological events. Only one study reported on frequency of psychological distress, which occurred in 9/31 (28%) of participants in a dual KAP/KCP treatment model59.

In addition to lack of systematic reporting, few larger studies reported on the frequency of adverse events. One KCP model RCT76 reported that in the 103 patients receiving ketamine, the most frequent immediate effects (0–4 h post dose administration) were dizziness (70/103; 68%), Dry mouth; (40/103; 38.8%), nausea (31/103; 30.1%), and headache (17/103; 16.5%). Interestingly, elevated blood pressure occurred in only 7/103 (6.8%) of participants. Here, the common psychological adverse events were reported as dissociative effects (100/103; 97.1%) and anxiety (10/103; 9.7%). In a report of patients receiving IN esketamine with a course of CBT73, dissociative symptoms were reported in 40/71 (56%) of participants.

In the 25 studies that did report on physical side effects, they included nausea (n = 16), dizziness/vertigo (n = 8), vomiting (n = 7), sedation (n = 5), fatigue (n = 4), transient blood pressure increase (n = 7), and headache (n = 7). Less frequently reported were altered sensory perception, including blurry vision or numbness in the body (n = 5), agitation (n = 3), weakness (n = 2), tachyarrhythmia (n = 1), and drowsiness (n = 2). The tachyarrythymia was noted in a single patient with anorexia and depression69. Single studies reported occurrences of decreased motor coordination, light-headedness, decreased appetite, decreased energy, restlessness, dysphagia, tinnitus, dry mouth, slower movements, diarrhea, increased pain, tenderness at injection site, increased libido, and bloating. Notably, no studies reported severe physical TEAE.

Psychological adverse events reported included anxiety (n = 5), overvalued ideas (n = 1), dissociation (n = 5), flashbacks (n = 1), nightmares (n = 1), increased emotional sensitivity (n = 1), worsening depression/suicidal ideation (n = 2), hallucination (n = 1), emotional indifference (n = 1), confusion (n = 1), “discombobulation” (n = 1), and feeling “high” (n = 1). One instance of worsening depression/suicidal ideation was reported in a patient in an RCT using CBT in parallel with IV ketamine in patients with TRD11. This patient required hospitalization, but the study had no dropouts.

Personnel and therapist qualifications

Nearly half of the studies (22/47; 47%) did not report on qualifications or training of the personnel providing the therapy. The most comprehensive description of personnel was in a group therapy program that included KAP as one part of its programming62,63. This program reported a widely multidisciplinary team and noted that KAP itself was provided by a “skilled therapist/clinician dyad” but did not explicitly specify the background of these therapists/clinicians. Several studies commented that the therapists had training in KAP, but only one specified that reported that therapists received training consisting of 6 h of theoretical instruction on KAP as well as practical training through observation and supervised practice47.

Of the remaining studies, psychotherapy was reported to be conducted by therapists (n = 8), psychiatrists (n = 3), psychiatrists and nurses (n = 1), a psychiatrist and psychologist (n = 1), psychologists (n = 3), a counselor or a therapist (n = 1), nurses (n = 1), clinical counselor (n = 1), integrative mental health clinician (n = 1), physician (n = 1), nurse practitioner (n = 2), licensed social worker (n = 1), therapist with a master’s in social work (SW) (n = 1), a therapist and an SW with a Master’s degree (n = 1), a licensed clinical SW (n = 1), a functional nurse practitioner, or nurse practitioner (n = 1), an unspecified individual trained in KAP (n = 1), and a computerized intervention (n = 1).

Personnel prescribing or administering ketamine

Personnel prescribing or administering ketamine was not reported in 24/47 (51%) of studies. Where reported, this was reported as psychiatrists (n = 8), physicians (n = 6), a neurologist (n = 1), an anesthesiologist (n = 1), nurses (n = 3), a certified registered nurse anesthetist (n = 1), a board-certified medical provider (n = 1), a nurse practitioner (n = 3) and a trained integrative health clinician (n = 1).

Cost of treatment

Financial considerations were rarely reported. Six studies noted that cost and financial situation of the patient influence acceptability. Twenty-seven studies (57%) did not report or comment on cost. Two reported that the treatment was provided in a public system and three reported private pay. Insurance approval was noted in one study, and another noted that insurance may not cover the full cost of treatment. Three reports suggested that delivery of treatment in a group format was a method to reduce cost and increase accessibility of treatment.

Ethics approval

Of the 47 reports discussed in this scoping review, 25 (53%) reported receipt of ethics or institutional approval. Three stated a waiver of ethics was given by a review board, or that ethics approval was not required. Most studies that did not report ethics approval were individual case reports or small retrospective reports.

Summary of ongoing clinical trials

Fifteen trials were identified as ongoing or recently completed, but not yet published as of March 2026. Of these, 3 were classified as KAP, describing a focus on ketamine integration, and 8 were categorized as KCP. The KCP studies reported use of a variety of therapies including ACT, Behavioral interventions, MBCT, radically open DBT, CBASP, and CBT. One study could not be categorized as either KAP or KCP. It reported use of manualized CBT used for ketamine integration. One study used ESK-AP and two reported on the use of CBT in an ESK-KCP model. One of the ESK-CBT studies was recently completed, but results were not posted, and only its protocol was published at the time this manuscript was prepared79. Discussion

This scoping review comprehensively mapped the available English literature on ketamine or esketamine paired with psychotherapy, categorizing interventions as either KAP/ES-KAP or KCP/ES-KCP. Because our search was broad, including all modalities and doses of ketamine and esketamine, all modalities of psychotherapy, depressive symptoms in addition to depressive disorders, and all study types, including individual case reports, our results can be considered the most comprehensive to date on this topic. Our search found 47 empirical articles, all published after 2020 in Europe, North America, and Asia, reflecting the growing interest in treatment approaches incorporating ketamine with psychotherapy.

Overall, the literature was heterogeneous, interventions were often poorly described, and studies were of limited methodological quality, aligning with previous reviews of this topic24,25. Specifically, there is significant variability in processes and descriptions of treatment models where ketamine and psychotherapy are combined. Our categorization of studies as KAP/ES-KAP or KCP/ES-KCP, as per previously proposed definitions22, was useful to differentiate between these two treatment approaches, supporting the utility of these definitions in this field. Additionally, we found several studies that used a dual approach of both KAP/ES-KAP and KCP/ES-KCP, and some studies could not be categorized due to lack of clarity in the description of the psychotherapy and/or the role of the dissociative or altered state experience. While previous reviews have concluded support or promise for treating depression or TRD with KAP24,25,27, each of these models has been reported as KAP. When these models are separated, it appears that previous literature may inadvertently overstate data to support of KAP/ES-KAP treatment model for depression.

Unlike most psychedelics under investigation for PAP, ketamine and esketamine have previously established antidepressant effects. Established antidepressant doses of IV ketamine (100% bioavailable) range from 0.5 to 1.0 mg/kg, which was largely reported in the IV ketamine and psychotherapy studies reviewed here. Though effective antidepressant doses of other formulations are less established1, when the relative bioavailabilities of other formulations (IM-90%; IN-50%; SL-20–30%) are considered, it can be estimated that antidepressant doses were also used in many other reports80. In the case of SL ketamine, literature regarding effective pharmacotherapy doses is heterogeneous, reporting that doses as low as 50 mg, but more commonly in the 100–300 mg range, have antidepressant effects39–42. This raises questions as to the pharmacologic benefit of the ketamine in even the studies using low, psycholytic doses in this review. In the case of esketamine, the majority of studies used IN doses of 56–84 mg, which is the recommended antidepressant dose13.

Only 8 studies contained comparison groups consisting of individuals receiving ketamine only versus ketamine with some form of psychotherapy. In the few KAP studies that did contain comparison groups, the majority reported that a group who received KAP did not demonstrate superior outcomes to the group receiving ketamine only36,40,68,78 Only one small observational study found that KAP improved depressive symptoms compared to ketamine alone44. In a KCP model, ketamine combined with a course of CBT21,32 did not improve acute treatment response, but those who received CBT demonstrated a more sustained response. Interestingly, retrospective studies noted that individuals engaged in CBT73or mindful self- compassion71 in combination with esketamine demonstrated a greater reduction in depressive symptoms. The lack of comparison groups in the majority of studies renders it impossible to determine the role of the psychotherapy intervention in any antidepressant effect. This is a critical consideration for future research.

In regards to psychotherapy approaches, ketamine “integration”, a common theme among KAP studies, took different forms. Application of various psychotherapy modalities to support integration was not consistently explained. Further, the term “integration” did not appear to have a consistent meaning. One large KAP study reporting on 3 large practices37 described KAP integration as exploring “the experiential component of the medicine” to bring a “new perspective and the direct experience of seeing ourselves in a new way.” Most often this appeared to take an insight-oriented approach, rooted in more flexible, non-manualized types of therapies such as psychodynamic therapy, Jungian analysis, somatic based therapies, or internal family systems (IFS). However, another study67, which we categorized as “indeterminate”, reported use of MI and BA for ketamine integration—therapies not typically expected to incorporate insight-oriented shifts in perspective. Here, integration appears to take a different meaning.

Though the acute altered state and its integration is a primary focus of KAP/ES-KAP, this contrasts with pharmacotherapy treatment models, where dissociation is viewed as an expected TEAE45. Regulators such as the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) or Health Canada consider dissociation a toxicity of the drug. Other glutamate modulators, without dissociative effects81, have demonstrated antidepressant efficacy82,83, suggesting dissociation is not necessary for ketamine’s antidepressant effect. Nevertheless, debate regarding a potential therapeutic role of dissociative effects continues. It has been suggested that even in the absence of accompanying psychotherapy, the experience of ketamine-induced dissociation is an inherent part of ketamine’s beneficial effects on mood16. From a patient perspective, however, a positive experience is not universal, varying significantly among individuals while also influenced by contextual variables, such as set and setting. In a previous survey of patients using maintenance IN or SL ketamine pharmacotherapy, a significant proportion of patients reported negative drug liking, or a dislike of ketamine’s acute dissociative effects84, consistent with the concept of a TEAE. Here, patients continued to take their ketamine due to its antidepressant benefits, despite negative drug liking. While the role of the dissociative experience may be of therapeutic value for some individuals, this does not appear universally true.

In considering the role of the dissociative experience, the majority of studies did not clearly report on the expected level or type of dissociative or mystical effects the patients would experience at varying doses and routes of administration. The Clinician Administered Dissociative Scale (CADSS) was commonly used to quantify level of dissociation, but proponents of KAP and the therapeutic use of the dissociative experience16 have proposed that other measures such as Pahnke’s Mystical Experiences Questionnaire (MEQ)85 and/or the Ego-Dissolution Inventory (EDI)86 would be more appropriate to assess altered state of mind with ketamine administration. Where studies did describe the expected dissociative state, aspects of this state considered important for effective psychotherapy were not consistently identified.

Many studies reported that psychotherapy occurred during the dosing session, but with limited specificity. Though some studies specified timing of psychotherapy specifically to drug administration, it was often unclear whether psychotherapy was occurring during peak effects of the drug, or after the acute effects had abated. One group37 noted that at doses in the 25–130 mg IM range, patients have an inward focused, “transformational” experience, during which verbal and intellectual communication is limited. In this state, limited communication would not support active psychotherapy, but several studies in this dose range reported psychotherapy during dosing. Presumably, this psychotherapy would begin after the dissociative effects have dissipated, but this is rarely specified. Where it was mentioned, psychotherapy during ketamine dosing was reported as 90 min after drug administration61–63 and one esketamine study reported psychotherapy during peak effects of the drug74. Future reports should specify the number of minutes after drug administration that psychotherapy begins.

The practice of using psychotherapy with ketamine is rising in popularity, but it increases cost of the treatment—either to the patient or to the healthcare system. From this review, data to support superior efficacy of using any psychotherapy with ketamine or esketamine, compared to pharmacotherapy alone is quite limited. This must be considered when planning treatment programs, and it is important that patients seeking ketamine treatment are informed of the current evidence base, so they can provide appropriate informed consent for their treatment model of choice. It has been previously established that using both psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy for depression supports superior outcomes than either intervention alone1. This combined approach is aligned with the KCP/ES-KCP model, where we found higher quality studies. Future prospective studies should seek to replicate these findings using CBT with larger study populations, and evaluate other manualized psychotherapies in combination with ketamine or esketamine.

Due to significant limitations in the literature reviewed, we found that there is not sufficient data to support use of KAP/ES-KAP for MDD or TRD. This finding is at odds with existing KAP guideline statements written by those with expertise in this practice87,88, which suggest KAP is an effective treatment option for TRD. This discrepancy highlights a dichotomy in the field. On the one hand, KAP oriented guidelines and psychotherapeutic-based literature which emphasize integration of the ketamine experience may overstate the role of psychotherapy. In many instances, the use of pharmacotherapy is minimized and positive responses to ketamine without concomitant use of psychotherapy is not accurately acknowledged. Expertise in this area may be largely built on clinical experience, in the absence of well-designed trials.

On the other hand, ketamine guidelines from Canada2, the United States89, and International Consensus 3have focused on the pharmacotherapeutic properties of ketamine. Despite increasing implementation of this practice, as well as growing experience and expertise, the role of psychotherapy has not been addressed. A more recent guideline from Australia and New Zealand briefly acknowledges that psychotherapy may be used90, and states the importance of considering any additional safety issues that may arise when psychotherapy is used with ketamine. To better inform the field regarding treatment selection and patient safety, future guidelines should clearly report on each treatment model of Ketamine pharmacotherapy (KP), KCP/ES-KCP and KAP/ES-KCP, integrating existing evidence base as well as expert opinion. Any unique considerations, particularly for psychological safety, should be addressed for each model.

A number of limitations to this review must be acknowledged. The most significant limitation of the search itself may be the restriction to English-language studies. This may have excluded relevant international reports, particularly from Latin America and Asia, where KAP/ES-KAP may be frequently practiced. The majority of studies identified were from Europe and North America, but more standardized KAP/ES-KAP practices and further data may exist elsewhere. Also, our literature search extended to March 2026, and given the rapidly expanding literature, recent important reports may not be captured. Further reducing clarity, populations were heterogeneous and often included those with depressive symptoms on mood rating scales or “depression”, rather than a formal diagnosis of MDD, TRD, or BD, as well as presence of one or more comorbid conditions. In several studies, psychotherapy interventions were more directed to treating another condition (ie exposure therapy for PTSD). While these factors limit purity of study populations and clarity of interventions for depression, this approach may also be viewed as a strength as it better reflects real world populations.

A related limitation concerns adverse event (AE) reporting. Among studies that reported AEs, comparison of incidence and severity to ketamine pharmacotherapy trials was not possible. Most studies described in this review were too small for meaningful incidence estimates. Future studies using psychotherapy should adopt standardized AE reporting frameworks such as the Ketamine side effect tool (KSET)91. Additionally, pharmacotherapy trials typically measure dissociation via the CADSS and do not assess adverse psychological events. For adverse psychological experiences, a tool like the Challenging Experiences Questionnaire (CEQ)92, originally designed to capture negative experiences with psilocybin, could be useful to documenting negative psychological reactions to ketamine also.

Though treatment paradigms combining ketamine and psychotherapy are theoretically promising, literature to rigorously evaluate these practices, and their additional specific benefit compared to ketamine alone, remains limited. Future prospective studies in depression should further evaluate KCP/ES-KCP practices, comparing groups receiving ketamine with or without a designated evidence-based psychotherapy, offered by trained therapists at a pre-defined frequency and dose. To support improved clarity in KAP/ES-KAP research, researchers and practitioners could establish an agreed-upon definitions, measures, and testable conceptual frameworks for KAP/ES-KAP that include specific therapeutic strategies for utilizing ketamine-induced altered states or dissociative symptoms. Researchers and clinicians practicing or evaluating a KAP/ES-KAP approach with a focus on ketamine integration should standardize common elements considered necessary for this type of therapy to be more clearly described and replicated. Funding for psychotherapy trials is a longtime challenge, but allocation of resources to support well designed KAP/ES-KAP and KCP/ES-KCP trials would allow for better determination of the role of psychotherapy, which could translate to more effective program planning and resource allocation within healthcare systems.
