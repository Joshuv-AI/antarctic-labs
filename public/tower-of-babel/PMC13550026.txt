Research Progress on Biomaterial Scaffolds Carrying Stem Cells for Inflammation Regulation After Spinal Cord Injury
Authors: Nanjian Xu, Xiaoyin Chai, Weihu Ma, Weihu Zhang, Fang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe inflammatory cascade triggered by spinal cord injury (SCI) presents a major barrier to tissue repair. This review summarizes recent advances in this field. It outlines the selection criteria for different scaffold materials and stem cells. It also describes key strategies for the coordinated regulation of the inflammatory microenvironment, including structural support and immunomodulation. In addition, the review discusses cell-free therapies and the functionalization of stem cell-derived materials. Intelligent responsive systems are also highlighted. Furthermore, this article evaluates the validation of these approaches in preclinical models. It also examines the challenges associated with their clinical translation. Finally, emerging trends, including dynamic adaptation, functional integration, and precise regeneration, are discussed. This review aims to provide a theoretical foundation and to guide future research on combination therapies for SCI.Keywords: SCI, Tissue engineering, Immune microenvironment, Stem cell exosomes, Smart biomaterials, Nerve regeneration

Introduction

Dilemmas in the Treatment and Nursing Care of SCI

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a devastating condition that profoundly disrupts central nervous system homeostasis. It is characterized by high incidence, substantial treatment costs, and a high rate of disability. Many affected individuals are young [1]. Globally, SCI is a leading contributor to the loss of healthy life expectancy and represents a major public health challenge [2, 3]. Patients with SCI face significant nursing challenges. These challenges can be grouped into three main areas: long-term care needs, complex management of complications, and limited family and social support. Individuals commonly develop sensory and motor deficits, along with autonomic dysfunction. Nursing care must address multiple secondary complications, including bladder and bowel dysfunction, pressure injuries, respiratory impairment, pain, muscle spasms, cardiovascular abnormalities, and autonomic dysreflexia. These complications occur across the acute phase, rehabilitation, and long-term follow-up. They increase the complexity of care and the burden of continuous management [4, 5]. In addition, patients often experience psychological distress, restricted daily activities, and impaired sexual health. Caregivers also face increased physical and emotional demands. These factors extend nursing challenges beyond routine physical care. They affect patient self-management, family support systems, and long-term quality of life [4, 6, 7]. Therefore, SCI nursing must evolve beyond basic survival-focused care. It requires a transition toward integrated, continuous, and patient-centered care across the entire disease course.

The inflammatory response after SCI is not a simple balance between M1 and M2 polarization. It is a complex and dynamic network that involves multiple cell types and signaling factors. Current evidence shows that secondary injury is driven not only by the initial mechanical insult but also by inflammation-induced remodeling of the microenvironment [8]. This microenvironment consists of diverse immune and glial cell populations. Its phenotype and function change over time after injury [9]. The traditional M1/M2 classification is useful for describing pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory tendencies of macrophages and microglia. However, this binary model does not fully capture the pathological complexity of SCI. Resident microglia and infiltrating peripheral macrophages differ in origin, regulatory mechanisms, and biological functions [10]. In addition, interactions between macrophages and astrocytes play a critical role in shaping the inflammatory microenvironment [11]. The complexity of the SCI microenvironment extends beyond inflammation alone. It involves multiple interconnected processes, including oxidative stress, excitotoxicity, disruption of calcium homeostasis, and signaling pathways related to cell death and repair. These processes interact and evolve simultaneously. As a result, single-target interventions are often inadequate for addressing this multifaceted pathological network [1]. Therefore, research on biomaterial scaffolds combined with stem cells for SCI repair should adopt a broader perspective. It should focus on the dynamic interactions among scaffolds, stem cells, and the host inflammatory microenvironment, particularly within the context of immune niche remodeling.

Limitations of Stem Cell and Biomaterial Therapies

Although stem cell transplantation and biomaterial implantation are widely regarded as promising treatments for SCI, single-modality approaches are often insufficient. They fail to address the complex tissue damage and inflammatory microenvironment associated with SCI. For stem cell transplantation, several limitations remain. These include poor cell retention at the injury site, low survival rates, limited integration with host tissue, and restricted migration. Together, these factors reduce the capacity of transplanted cells to secrete trophic factors, modulate inflammation, and promote regeneration [12, 13]. Even Schwann cells, which have well-established regenerative potential, show limited graft survival and integration. Their migration within injured spinal cord tissue is also restricted. These limitations significantly reduce therapeutic efficacy [14–16]. Similar challenges are observed with other stem cell types. The SCI microenvironment is highly hostile. It is characterized by ischemia, hypoxia, inflammatory cytokine surges, increased oxidative stress, and a lack of extracellular matrix support. These conditions hinder long-term cell survival and engraftment [17]. In contrast, biomaterial implantation alone primarily provides structural support. It can fill lesion cavities and guide axonal growth. However, its therapeutic effects are largely passive. Without active cellular or molecular components, biomaterials show limited bioactivity. They are less effective in modulating inflammation or promoting sustained neural regeneration and functional recovery [18]. Although biomaterials can improve local mechanical properties and spatial organization, they cannot independently regulate complex repair processes. These include cell recruitment, immune modulation, myelin regeneration, and neural circuit reconstruction. Recent studies increasingly support a combinatorial strategy that integrates stem cells with biomaterials. This approach aims to overcome the limitations of single therapies. Biomaterials can enhance cell delivery, retention, and survival. At the same time, stem cells can provide immunomodulatory effects and promote tissue regeneration. Together, these synergistic effects improve the overall therapeutic potential for SCI repair [19].

Combined Treatment Strategies

Repair barriers after SCI extend beyond simple tissue loss. A complex regenerative barrier forms rapidly at the injury site. This barrier includes inflammation, oxidative stress, glial scarring, and an inhibitory extracellular microenvironment. Together, these factors continuously restrict axonal growth and neural circuit reconstruction. Evidence shows that the adult mammalian spinal cord has limited intrinsic regenerative capacity. Inflammatory responses and glial scars around the lesion further impede repair [20]. Therefore, early “scaffold-mounted” strategies are no longer adequate if they focus only on cavity filling, mechanical support, and physical guidance. Such approaches cannot address the multi-stage and cascading pathological processes of SCI [21]. The repair paradigm is now evolving. Scaffolds are transitioning from passive structural platforms to active regulatory systems. Their design has shifted from simple morphological mimicry to more advanced features. These include surface modification, microstructural engineering, mechanical compatibility, incorporation of bioactive components, and controlled temporal release of therapeutic factors [22]. Accordingly, the concept of “scaffold-mounted” therapy has expanded. It is no longer a simple combination of materials and cells. Instead, it represents a coordinated integration of functional biomaterials with therapeutic bioactive elements.

After SCI, endogenous neural stem cells (NSCs) retain limited regenerative potential. However, their survival, migration, and directed differentiation are strongly affected by the inflammatory microenvironment. In many cases, these cells may not survive within the lesion site [21, 23]. Even when endogenous repair capacity is present, NSCs tend to differentiate into astrocytes rather than functional neurons. This bias limits the reconstruction of effective neural conduction pathways [24–26]. Therefore, stem cell introduction alone is unlikely to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes. A more effective strategy is to use functionally engineered scaffolds. These scaffolds can reconstruct a local microenvironment that supports cell survival, enhances paracrine signaling, and regulates cell fate. For example, oriented nanofiber scaffolds loaded with dual growth factors and combined with NSCs can reduce oxidative stress and inflammation in the early stages of injury. They also inhibit neuronal loss and promote neurogenesis, axonal extension, and neural circuit repair in later stages [27]. Similarly, decellularized optic nerve scaffolds functionalized with specific aptamers can actively recruit endogenous NSCs and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) to the injury site. This approach promotes neurogenesis, angiogenesis, and recovery of sensorimotor function [28].

Selection and Design Principles of Scaffolds and Stem Cells

Biomaterial Scaffolds

Biomaterial scaffolds play a critical role in regulating the inflammatory microenvironment after SCI. Their physical, chemical, and biological properties provide multidimensional support for tissue repair and regeneration [29, 30]. From a physical perspective, scaffold architecture strongly influences cellular behavior. Parameters such as pore size, topology, and mechanical strength affect cell adhesion, migration, proliferation, and nutrient exchange. Rational structural design can also modulate local cellular responses and improve the regenerative microenvironment [31]. Scaffolds with biomimetic pore structures and appropriate elasticity promote axonal growth and enhance cell-matrix interactions. They can also support anti-inflammatory responses. Matching the mechanical properties of the scaffold to those of spinal cord tissue is particularly important. This reduces abnormal mechanical stress and improves host tissue responses [32, 33]. Chemical properties are equally important. Material composition, biocompatibility, degradation rate, and surface functionalization all influence inflammation regulation. Both natural and synthetic polymers, including collagen-based matrices, hyaluronic acid, and PLGA, are widely used in SCI repair. Incorporating components with immunomodulatory or antioxidant effects can further improve the post-injury microenvironment [34, 35]. For example, scaffolds containing magnesium ions or bioactive molecules such as spermine and berberine can reduce inflammation, alleviate oxidative stress, and support tissue repair [36]. From a biological perspective, scaffold design has evolved significantly. Modern scaffolds are no longer limited to passive structural support. Instead, they actively modulate the injured microenvironment. They interact dynamically with immune cells, neurotrophic signals, and regenerative pathways. Some scaffolds deliver bioactive agents such as neurotrophic factors, siRNA, or extracellular vesicles (EVs). These systems can suppress excessive inflammation, promote macrophage polarization from M1 to M2, and enhance the expression of repair-associated cytokines and growth factors [33, 37]. Preclinical studies demonstrate that such multifunctional scaffolds can reduce glial scar formation, promote remyelination and axonal regeneration, and improve motor function recovery [35, 37]. In addition, smart responsive scaffolds have emerged as a promising approach. These include injectable, photocurable, self-healing, and controlled-release systems. They enable more precise regulation of the post-injury inflammatory microenvironment [38]. Smart hydrogels and multifunctional scaffolds further enhance therapeutic outcomes through controlled delivery, bioactive integration, and responsiveness to local conditions [37]. Overall, biomaterial scaffolds support spinal cord regeneration by modulating the inflammatory microenvironment at multiple levels. Future research should focus on biomimetic design, targeted immunomodulation, and precise delivery strategies. These advances will help optimize the clinical efficacy of scaffold-based therapies for SCI [9, 39].

Intrinsic Properties of Materials

Regulation of the inflammatory microenvironment after SCI by biomaterial scaffolds largely depends on their intrinsic properties. Unlike approaches that rely only on exogenous drugs or cell delivery, certain materials possess inherent biological activities. These include antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, conductive, and antioxidant functions. Such properties allow them to directly modulate the injury microenvironment after implantation. They also reduce system complexity and support the development of scaffolds that integrate structural support with biological regulation. Chitosan is a representative material with excellent biocompatibility. It also exhibits natural antibacterial and immunomodulatory properties. Studies show that chitosan-based composite hydrogels can inhibit bacterial growth, scavenge excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), and regulate neutrophil and macrophage activity. These effects contribute to their anti-inflammatory function [40, 41]. Conductive materials, such as polypyrrole (PPy) and graphene- or MXene-based systems, provide electrical properties similar to those of neural tissue. These materials help restore local electrical signal transmission. They also promote neuronal differentiation and neural circuit reconstruction, especially when combined with electrical stimulation [42, 43]. For example, PPy-based composite hydrogels can enhance neural differentiation through synergistic electrical effects [44]. Similarly, soft conductive scaffolds incorporating MXene can promote the differentiation of endogenous NSCs into neurons under electrical stimulation [35]. Antioxidant materials are also essential for reducing secondary injury. Components such as polyphenols and functional nanomaterials can improve the microenvironment by scavenging ROS, reducing lipid peroxidation, and suppressing inflammatory cascades. For instance, hydrogels loaded with black phosphorus nanosheets show strong ROS-scavenging capacity. They can inhibit ferroptosis and apoptosis [45]. In addition, scaffolds containing epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG)-modified nanocomponents exhibit both antioxidant and immunomodulatory effects [44]. Overall, these functional materials enable scaffolds to actively regulate the post-injury microenvironment. This multifunctionality enhances their potential to support neural repair and regeneration after SCI.

Structural Engineering Properties

The structural engineering properties of scaffolds directly influence cellular behavior and regenerative outcomes after SCI. Topological features such as nanofiber alignment, directional channels, and anisotropic fiber networks provide physical guidance for regenerating axons. These architectures promote axonal extension in specific directions and support the reconstruction of organized neural connections [46, 47]. Studies have shown that injectable hydrogels with magnetically responsive aligned fiber networks can improve axonal orientation. They also reduce inflammatory responses and limit glial scar formation. Similarly, cellulose nanofiber scaffolds with oriented microchannels significantly enhance orderly axonal growth along predefined pathways [48]. Scaffold topology also plays an important role in regulating immune cell behavior. Directional nanofiber structures not only support axonal regeneration but also indirectly modulate inflammation. They achieve this by improving the local microenvironment and reducing excessive immune cell activation [27]. Porosity and pore interconnectivity are critical determinants of scaffold performance. Three-dimensional porous architectures provide space for cell migration, adhesion, and proliferation. They also facilitate efficient transport of nutrients, oxygen, and metabolic waste [49]. For example, porous GelMA or collagen-based scaffolds with optimized pore sizes and interconnected networks enhance NSC infiltration, migration, and differentiation. They also reduce inflammatory responses after implantation [50]. Overall, functional biomaterials such as chitosan (with antibacterial and anti-inflammatory properties), PPy and graphene/MXene-based conductive systems, and polyphenol- or nanomaterial-based antioxidants form the foundation of scaffold design. In addition, rationally engineered directional topologies and porous structures not only guide axonal and immune cell organization but also improve the injured microenvironment by enhancing cell infiltration and mass transport. Together, these features are essential for achieving coordinated structural support and functional regulation in SCI repair.

Active Components of Stem Cells

Living Cells

In SCI repair, transplanted cells are not merely structural replacements. They function as active biological agents that continuously sense and respond to the local microenvironment while regulating regeneration processes [51]. MSCs and NSCs, including induced NSCs (iNSCs), represent two complementary therapeutic strategies. MSCs are primarily involved in immunomodulation and paracrine signaling, whereas NSCs/iNSCs contribute mainly to neural differentiation and network reconstruction [52]. The main therapeutic advantage of MSCs does not lie in direct neuronal replacement. Instead, it is driven by their strong paracrine activity. MSCs secrete a broad range of cytokines, trophic factors, and EVs. These bioactive products regulate immune responses, inhibit apoptosis, promote angiogenesis, support tissue remodeling, and reduce fibrosis. Through these mechanisms, MSCs improve the inflammatory microenvironment in the early phase of injury and create a more permissive niche for subsequent neural repair [53]. MSCs also exhibit low immunogenicity, lesion-targeting ability, and relatively easy clinical accessibility. As a result, they are the most widely used cell type in both preclinical and clinical SCI studies [54, 55]. However, their ability to differentiate into neural lineages is limited. Their therapeutic effects are therefore largely indirect, relying on supportive and modulatory functions rather than true neuronal replacement [55, 56]. In contrast, NSCs and iNSCs are more closely aligned with the cellular composition of spinal cord tissue. Their key advantage is the ability to differentiate into neurons, oligodendrocytes, and other neural lineage cells. This enables more direct participation in axonal regeneration, remyelination, and neural circuit reconstruction [57]. Studies have shown that, under appropriate microenvironmental conditions or scaffold support, NSCs can enhance neurogenesis and promote the formation of neurons and oligodendrocytes, thereby improving functional recovery [58]. In addition, NSC-derived EVs contain a more neurotrophic-rich cargo compared with lipid-derived EVs. These vesicles more effectively promote neuronal differentiation and angiogenesis, further highlighting the intrinsic regenerative potential of NSCs [59]. However, NSCs/iNSCs are highly sensitive to the transplant environment. Their survival, differentiation trajectory, and safety depend strongly on precise regulation by scaffolds, trophic factors, and the local inflammatory state [52, 58]. Functionally, MSCs are better suited as immunomodulatory and paracrine support cells, making them particularly valuable in the early stages of injury. NSCs/iNSCs, in contrast, serve more as regenerative effector cells, with a stronger capacity for neural differentiation and circuit reconstruction. Therefore, rather than comparing their relative advantages, a more effective strategy is to integrate both cell types. Combining the immunoregulatory functions of MSCs with the neuroregenerative potential of NSCs/iNSCs, and tailoring their use to different stages of SCI repair, offers a more comprehensive and promising therapeutic approach.

Cell Derivatives

In SCI repair, exosomes, small EVs (sEVs), and nanovesicles are regarded as key mediators of stem cell paracrine activity. Increasing evidence indicates that the therapeutic effects of stem cell therapy are not solely dependent on long-term cell survival. Instead, they are largely mediated by vesicle-based paracrine signaling. These vesicles transport proteins, lipids, mRNA, miRNA, and other bioactive molecules. Through intercellular communication, they regulate inflammation, apoptosis, angiogenesis, and neurogenesis [60–62]. Compared with cell-based therapy, EVs offer several advantages in cell-free applications. Stem cell transplantation is associated with limitations such as low cell survival, immune rejection, tumorigenic risk, and uncontrolled differentiation. In contrast, EVs exhibit low immunogenicity, high stability, nanoscale size, flexible delivery capability, and the ability to cross the blood-spinal cord barrier. These properties help overcome many of the key challenges associated with live cell therapies [63–65]. As a result, EVs are increasingly considered a promising alternative or complementary strategy in SCI treatment. Functionally, EVs preserve many of the biological signaling properties of their parent cells in a concentrated form. For example, MSC-derived EVs can modulate inflammatory responses, promote macrophage polarization from M1 to M2, inhibit apoptosis, and enhance angiogenesis and axonal growth. NSC-derived EVs, in turn, contain a richer spectrum of neurotrophic factors and show stronger potential for promoting neural differentiation [62, 66]. Nanovesicles, including engineered or biomimetic vesicle systems, represent another emerging direction. They may improve production efficiency and application flexibility while maintaining key paracrine functions [67]. Overall, exosomes, sEVs, and nanovesicles act as carriers that preserve and amplify stem cell paracrine effects. They retain important regenerative activities while avoiding the limitations of live cell transplantation. Owing to their stability, safety, and delivery advantages, they have become a major focus in SCI regenerative medicine. In addition, their integration with biomaterial scaffolds and engineered delivery platforms is becoming an increasingly important research direction.

Engineered Cells

To address the limitations of natural stem cells such as MSCs, including poor survival in the injury microenvironment, insufficient functional activity, and limited persistence of therapeutic effects, recent studies have increasingly focused on cell engineering strategies. These approaches typically involve gene modification or preconditioning to enhance specific therapeutic functions. Common methods include the overexpression of anti-inflammatory or trophic factors in MSCs, as well as hypoxic preconditioning to improve paracrine activity, angiogenic potential, and environmental adaptability [68, 69]. A representative gene modification strategy involves the stable overexpression of key bioactive molecules. For example, MSCs engineered to overexpress basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) demonstrate stronger regenerative capacity than unmodified MSCs in complete SCI models. These modified cells reduce glial scar formation, promote neurogenesis, stimulate endogenous NSC proliferation, and improve motor function recovery [68]. Similarly, interleukin-10 (IL-10)-engineered MSCs more effectively drive macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. They suppress pro-inflammatory M1 responses and protect NSCs under inflammatory conditions, while also enhancing neuronal differentiation [70]. These findings highlight the advantage of gene-modified MSCs in improving immune regulation and anti-inflammatory effects. In addition to genetic engineering, hypoxic preconditioning represents a more practical and clinically translatable strategy. Hypoxia can remodel the cellular secretory profile and enhance the release of exosomes and other microenvironment-regulating factors [69]. Cells and their derivatives exposed to hypoxia generally show improved angiogenic activity, stronger anti-inflammatory effects, and enhanced functional recovery. For instance, hypoxic stimulation enhances the pro-angiogenic capacity of MSC-derived exosomes. Likewise, hypoxia-preconditioned exosomes demonstrate stronger vascular and regenerative effects in SCI models [71].

Overall, the core value of engineered cells lies in transforming them from naturally occurring active components into functionally enhanced therapeutic agents through the targeted amplification of specific biological functions. Gene modification is particularly effective for strengthening defined molecular pathways. In contrast, preconditioning strategies such as hypoxia mainly enhance the cells’ overall adaptability to the injured microenvironment and improve their paracrine activity. Both approaches represent important directions for improving the precision, stability, and therapeutic efficacy of stem cell-based therapies (Table 2).

Static Synergy Between Physical Support and Immune Regulation

Following SCI, the lesion site undergoes a series of adverse pathological changes, including tissue loss, cavity formation, persistent inflammatory cell infiltration, extracellular matrix disruption, and glial scar formation. These conditions lead to poor cell retention, low survival rates, impaired differentiation, and difficulty in maintaining long-term function when stem cells are administered alone [72]. Although implantation of inert biomaterials can partially fill tissue defects and provide structural support, these materials generally lack sufficient biological activity. As a result, they are limited in their ability to actively modulate the persistent post-injury inflammatory microenvironment [51]. To address these limitations, one of the earliest and most widely used strategies is the combination of stem cells with biocompatible scaffolds to form a synergistic therapeutic system. The fundamental concept of this approach is relatively straightforward. The scaffold primarily enhances stem cell retention and viability at the injury site, while stem cells exert therapeutic effects mainly through paracrine-mediated immunomodulation and tissue protection. Together, they produce a complementary and synergistic effect. In particular, biomaterial scaffolds provide a three-dimensional structure, adhesion sites, and a microarchitecture that partially mimics spinal cord tissue. These features improve the attachment, retention, and early survival of transplanted cells, thereby enabling them to exert their biological functions more effectively. Studies have shown that engineered scaffolds are important supportive platforms for enhancing graft cell survival and differentiation [51]. Conversely, the therapeutic effects of stem cells, especially MSCs and NSCs, are not primarily dependent on direct tissue replacement. Instead, they largely act through the secretion of trophic factors, immunomodulatory molecules, and other paracrine signals that broadly regulate the post-injury microenvironment. For example, MSCs exhibit strong paracrine and immunomodulatory functions that help establish a more favorable regenerative environment [75]. Similarly, NSCs combined with biomaterial scaffolds show improved motor function and histological recovery compared with cell therapy alone [76]. Thus, at this stage, a clear division of labor is observed. The scaffold functions as a structural carrier that ensures cell delivery and survival, while stem cells act as active biological modulators that suppress excessive inflammation, reduce secondary damage, and indirectly promote tissue repair through paracrine mechanisms. However, the inflammatory regulation achieved by this conventional model remains relatively broad and non-specific, and does not yet reach the spatiotemporally precise control characteristic of more advanced functional scaffold systems.

Typical Materials - cell Combinations and Effects

Hydrogels

Hydrogel encapsulation of MSCs is one of the most widely used and representative combination strategies. Its main advantages stem from the intrinsic properties of hydrogels, including high water content, good biocompatibility, injectability, and in situ gelation. These characteristics allow hydrogels and their composite systems to mimic the soft extracellular matrix of the spinal cord, thereby providing a relatively supportive three-dimensional microenvironment for MSC survival. Studies have shown that the harsh microenvironment at SCI sites significantly impairs the survival and paracrine function of transplanted MSCs, thereby reducing therapeutic efficacy. In contrast, in situ thermosensitive hydrogels can improve MSC survival within the lesion and enhance their paracrine activity [77]. These findings suggest that the primary role of hydrogels in this strategy is not direct therapeutic action. Instead, they function mainly through mechanical protection, localized cell retention, and buffering of the hostile microenvironment. In this way, hydrogels enable MSCs to maintain more stable and effective biological activity.

From the perspective of inflammatory regulation, MSCs encapsulated in hydrogels generally exhibit enhanced anti-inflammatory activity. Studies have shown that erythropoietin (EPO)-loaded alginate/chitosan hydrogels can suppress secondary inflammatory responses after SCI. This effect is associated with reduced NF-κB activation and decreased serum TNF-α levels [78]. In another study, silk fibroin-based hydrogels used as composite scaffolds were reported to inhibit glutamate-induced excitotoxicity and attenuate inflammatory responses. They also support piezoelectric- or electrically induced signaling, thereby promoting neural tissue regeneration and functional recovery [73, 79]. Similarly, composite scaffold systems combined with menstrual blood-derived stem cells (MenSCs) can reduce inflammation and suppress reactive astrocyte proliferation in SCI models. Although the cell source differs from MSCs, the hydrogel still functions as a stable delivery platform. The underlying mechanism, where transplanted cells modulate the immune microenvironment through anti-inflammatory and trophic factor secretion, is consistent with that observed in MSC-based encapsulation systems [74]. At the tissue level, hydrogel-based delivery systems also contribute to reducing cavity and cyst formation at the injury site. Progressive necrosis and cavitation are common pathological features following SCI. Injectable or in situ-gelating hydrogels can rapidly fill lesion cavities, reduce tissue collapse, and stabilize the local microstructure, while simultaneously serving as cell-retention matrices. For instance, silk fibroin hydrogels can undergo in situ gelation at physiological temperature, effectively bridging tissue defects, limiting structural collapse, and supporting the retention of bioactive components such as nanoparticles or conductive materials [73]. In addition, injectable bioactive hydrogels loaded with MSCs can effectively fill cystic cavities in vivo and provide a supportive microenvironment that facilitates tissue regeneration [80]. NSCs combined with functional hydrogels containing carbon dots and FTY720 show similar regenerative effects [81] (Fig. 2). Other studies have reported that low-modulus GelMA scaffolds loaded with bone marrow-derived MSCs can significantly reduce syringomyelia formation and inhibit glial scar development [82]. Although some of these systems involve co-culture or composite designs, they collectively support a key conclusion: hydrogels act not only as cell carriers but also as structural stabilizers that limit secondary cavity expansion and contribute to tissue preservation after SCI.

Promoting angiogenesis is another key advantage of hydrogel-based MSC delivery systems. After SCI, disruption of the microcirculation and local ischemia-hypoxia further aggravate cell death. Therefore, reconstruction of the vascular network is essential for effective tissue repair. MSCs possess intrinsic pro-angiogenic capacity through the secretion of vascular growth factors. Hydrogel encapsulation further enhances this effect by prolonging their local retention and extending the duration of paracrine activity. Studies have shown that in situ-forming hydrogels can significantly increase the release of angiogenesis-related factors from MSCs, thereby promoting vascular regeneration and improving functional recovery after SCI [77]. In another study, hypoxia-preconditioned MSC-derived exosomes (hypo-Exo) encapsulated in a peptide-modified adhesive hydrogel induced vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression in local endothelial cells. This effect was mediated by elevated HIF-1α levels within the exosomes, leading to enhanced angiogenesis and improved tissue repair in the injured region [71]. Additional hydrogel-MSC systems have also demonstrated improved regenerative outcomes through enhanced vascular formation [84]. Overall, the therapeutic benefits of hydrogel-based MSC delivery systems are not limited to immunosuppression. Instead, they result from a coordinated set of effects, including inflammation reduction, tissue protection, and vascular remodeling, which collectively improve the regenerative microenvironment after SCI.

Porous Supports and Directional Supports

Unlike MSCs, which primarily exert therapeutic effects through paracrine-mediated immunoregulation, NSCs loaded onto porous or directionally structured scaffolds more directly participate in neural regeneration. Scaffolds such as gelatin sponges, collagen matrices, PLGA, and their composite systems provide a three-dimensional framework that supports cell adhesion, survival, and migration. At the same time, their porous architecture and aligned channels offer structural guidance for axonal extension and regenerative bridging. Studies have shown that three-dimensional porous collagen scaffolds can function as conduits for neural regeneration. They promote NSC migration and neuronal differentiation while reducing glial scar formation [79]. Similarly, collagen fiber scaffolds with appropriate mechanical properties and porous architectures can enhance the alignment of NSCs, facilitate neuronal differentiation and adhesion, and attenuate glial and fibrotic scarring in SCI models [85]. A key advantage of this strategy is its ability to guide organized axonal growth. Compared with randomly structured materials, directionally aligned scaffolds more closely replicate the native bundle-like architecture of the spinal cord. Research has demonstrated that aligned or structurally guided scaffolds improve the directional infiltration of axons, promote axonal elongation, and concurrently improve inflammatory responses and reduce glial scar formation [48]. In addition, collagen-based scaffolds exhibit excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability, and have shown minimal inflammatory responses in animal studies. These properties further support their suitability as delivery platforms for NSCs [49].

Therefore, the primary significance of loading NSCs onto porous or oriented scaffolds lies in two key aspects. First, spatial architecture and mechanical cues help direct NSC differentiation toward neuronal lineages rather than astrocytic fate. Second, the scaffold provides a continuous guidance pathway that supports axonal growth across the injury site while partially mitigating the inhibitory effects of glial scar formation [86]. This strategy more closely reflects a synergistic repair model that integrates structural reconstruction with cellular replacement, rather than relying on simple cell transplantation alone. As such, it represents a highly representative and promising approach in SCI tissue engineering.

Mechanism Focus: Broad-spectrum Non-targeted Immune Remodeling of Stem Cell Paracrine Mixture

While scaffolds improve cell retention, survival, and local delivery efficiency, the primary driver of inflammatory regulation remains the transplanted stem cells themselves. The key modulators of immune microenvironment remodeling are the diverse paracrine products released by these cells, including cytokines, growth factors, lipid mediators, and EVs [52, 83]. Among them, MSCs play a particularly prominent immunomodulatory role. Current evidence indicates that MSCs can regulate macrophage and microglial function through paracrine signaling, exosome secretion, and associated metabolic reprogramming [87]. This regulation is mainly reflected in the balance between pro-inflammatory M1-like and anti-inflammatory M2-like phenotypes. After SCI, sustained activation of M1-like macrophages and microglia amplifies the inflammatory cascade, leading to elevated levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. In contrast, stem cell-derived paracrine signals can promote a shift toward the M2-like phenotype, thereby attenuating inflammation and improving the regenerative microenvironment [87, 88]. However, this regulatory process does not involve precise, single-target control. Instead, it exerts broad and simultaneous effects across multiple signaling pathways. For instance, MSC-derived factors can influence macrophage polarization, inflammatory cytokine expression, apoptotic activity, blood-spinal cord barrier integrity, and axonal regenerative capacity at the same time [62]. In scaffold-stem cell combination strategies, inflammation regulation is therefore not achieved through targeted intervention of specific pathways. Rather, it relies on the natural secretory profile of stem cells to buffer and partially reprogram the dysregulated inflammatory network after injury. This approach offers broad-spectrum benefits, including both immune modulation and tissue protection. However, its limitations lie in relatively low precision and limited controllability, making it difficult to achieve fine-tuned regulation at specific time points, within defined cell populations, or along individual molecular pathways.

Precise Synergy Between Functionalized Adaptation and Cell-free Therapy

Cell-free Strategy and Stem Cell‑derived EVs

Compared with the basic scaffold-stem cell transplantation model, this strategy represents a conceptual shift from delivering living cells to delivering pre-prepared therapeutic products. In this approach, the therapeutic focus is no longer dependent on the long-term survival and in vivo secretion of transplanted cells. Instead, exosomes, cytokines, miRNAs, and small-molecule drugs are directly incorporated into functionalized scaffolds to achieve controlled local delivery. Among these components, exosomes are particularly important as key mediators of intercellular communication. They can transport proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and other bioactive molecules, and exert multiple therapeutic effects in SCI, including anti-inflammatory, pro-angiogenic, anti-apoptotic, and pro-regenerative functions [89]. This strategy therefore extracts the most critical “functional output” of stem cells and delivers it through a biomaterial platform with improved spatial and temporal control. A major advantage of this evolution is improved therapeutic controllability. The efficacy of live cell transplantation is often influenced by variability in cell source, survival, differentiation behavior, and host microenvironmental conditions. In contrast, cell-free products allow for more precise characterization, dose standardization, and predefined release kinetics. For example, engineered exosomes integrated with biomaterials are considered a promising direction in SCI therapy [89], and exosome-based treatments are widely recognized as multi-target, cell-free strategies with strong translational potential [89]. In addition, small-molecule drugs can be stably incorporated into scaffolds and released in a controlled manner. This approach reduces the limitations of systemic administration, improves local bioavailability, and minimizes off-target side effects. From a translational perspective, cell-free systems also offer improved safety and standardization. Unlike live cell therapies, they avoid risks such as uncontrolled differentiation, tumorigenicity, and long-term in vivo instability, and generally exhibit lower immunogenicity [90]. MSC-derived exosomes, in particular, are characterized by high stability and low immunogenicity, making them suitable for integration with engineered delivery systems [91]. Nevertheless, challenges remain, including standardization of production, delivery efficiency, and long-term biosafety. Overall, the transition from “cell-loaded scaffolds” to “bioactive product-loaded scaffolds” represents an important stage in SCI repair strategies, moving from biologically adaptive systems toward well-defined, programmable, and controllable therapeutic platforms.

Stem cell-derived EVs are central mediators of paracrine signaling [92, 93]. A substantial portion of stem cell therapeutic effects in SCI has been attributed to exosome activity [94]. These vesicles regulate inflammation and apoptosis, promote angiogenesis, and enhance functional recovery [95, 96]. When incorporated into scaffolds, they preserve the immunomodulatory and regenerative functions of stem cells while reducing dependence on the host microenvironment. Compared with cell-based therapy, exosome delivery systems provide improved safety and controllability [97]. Existing studies highlight several limitations of cell therapy, including low survival rates, immune rejection, and tumorigenic risk. In contrast, exosomes offer advantages such as low immunogenicity, high stability, and the ability to cross the blood-spinal cord barrier [98]. Importantly, they are highly amenable to engineering. For example, hypoxic preconditioning can alter the secretory profile of donor cells and enhance the functional properties of exosomes. Hypoxia-preconditioned MSC-derived exosomes have been shown to promote angiogenesis more effectively [71, 99]. Hydrogel-based delivery of MSC-derived exosomes is one of the most widely studied strategies. Hypoxia-preconditioned exosomes (hypo-Exo) encapsulated in peptide-modified adhesive hydrogels can be stably retained at the injury site. This system not only fills lesion cavities but also enhances VEGF-related signaling in endothelial cells, thereby promoting angiogenesis and functional recovery [71]. These findings indicate that hydrogels function not only as delivery platforms but also as amplifiers of exosome bioactivity through sustained retention and release. Moreover, MSC-derived EV-loaded hydrogels exhibit strong anti-inflammatory effects. For instance, DSCM@EV composite hydrogels can promote macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, reduce inflammatory responses, and further enhance NSC differentiation and axonal growth [100]. Similarly, NAGA/GelMA/LPN/TA (NGL/T) hydrogels loaded with MSC-derived sEVs enable sustained in vivo release, synergistically improving motor function recovery and protecting urinary system function by suppressing inflammation and oxidative stress [101]. Overall, hydrogel-based exosome delivery represents a key paradigm in cell-free regenerative scaffolds. This approach preserves the multi-target therapeutic potential of stem cell-derived products while enabling stable, localized, and standardized delivery through biomaterial engineering.

Scaffold‑mediated Delivery of Diverse Therapeutic Cargos and Pathway Regulation

Beyond exosome-based strategies, direct loading of defined neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory molecules into hydrogels represents another important direction. This approach integrates neurotrophic support with inflammation control, simultaneously promoting neuronal survival, axonal regeneration, and synaptic remodeling while improving the local microenvironment and reducing apoptosis and excessive inflammation. Studies have shown that composite membranes delivering nerve growth factor (NGF) and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) can enhance axonal regeneration and neuronal recovery while reducing astrocyte proliferation [102]. Therefore, incorporating neurotrophic factors into scaffold systems can provide sustained support for neuronal survival and regeneration, further strengthening the therapeutic potential of functional biomaterial-based repair strategies.

Compared with the broad supportive effects of neurotrophic factors, small-molecule drugs offer more clearly defined mechanisms and stronger pathway specificity. For example, fingolimod (FTY720) regulates immune cell migration and suppresses inflammation through sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) receptors [81, 103]. Baicalin and scutellarin reduce inflammation and inhibit glial scar formation, while curcumin exerts well-documented anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, largely through NF-κB-related and inflammasome-associated pathways. Previous studies have shown that engineered vesicle systems combined with NGF and curcumin can achieve localized and controlled drug release at the injury site. This approach effectively suppresses excessive inflammation and enhances neuroprotection [104]. Other multi-target therapeutic strategies further indicate that inhibition of TLR4/NF-κB signaling and NLRP3 inflammasome activation can significantly alleviate neuroinflammation and improve functional outcomes [105]. Accordingly, scaffold-based delivery of small-molecule drugs represents a shift from broad-spectrum biological modulation to pathway-specific intervention. Unlike live cell-based scaffold strategies that rely on diffuse paracrine signaling, functionalized hydrogels loaded with defined therapeutic agents can more precisely target key pathological signaling axes after SCI. In this context, exosomes and other vesicular systems can also regulate inflammation through specific molecular pathways. For example, miR-145-5p carried by MSC exosomes inhibits the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, thereby reducing IL-1β expression. Similarly, EPO-loaded alginate/chitosan hydrogels can inhibit NF-κB phosphorylation and downstream TNF-α production, while simultaneously modulating apoptotic signaling by upregulating Bcl-2 and downregulating Bax. This results in a substantial reduction in apoptosis and a dual suppression of inflammation and cell death pathways [78]. ROS-responsive hydrogels encapsulating MSC-derived nanovesicles further demonstrate precise regulation of oxidative stress and inflammation. These systems can effectively scavenge ROS, suppress lipopolysaccharide-induced pro-inflammatory activation of microglia-like cells, and reduce the secretion of TNF-α and IL-6. Notably, their antioxidant capacity may exceed that of natural exosomes, enabling more effective intervention in the oxidative stress-inflammation cascade [106]. These findings indicate that functionalized scaffolds are evolving from carriers of complex and heterogeneous secretory profiles toward platforms for precisely defined therapeutic cargo. By loading specific molecules or vesicles, it becomes possible to directly regulate key signaling nodes such as NF-κB, ROS, and Bax/Bcl-2, thereby shifting from broad modulation to pathway-specific targeting. Several signaling pathways have been identified as critical regulators of SCI inflammation and regeneration. For example, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway can be activated by exosomal proteins. In contrast, activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome is a key driver of inflammatory progression, and its inhibition is closely associated with reduced inflammation and improved functional recovery [107]. Thus, the precision of this strategy is reflected in two dimensions. First, pathway targeting, which directly modulates key signaling networks such as Wnt/β-catenin and NLRP3. Second, cargo targeting, where specific miRNAs or proteins delivered via exosomes reprogram immune cells, including macrophages and microglia, toward a regenerative phenotype. This approach also enables regulation of the hypoxia-angiogenesis axis. For instance, hypoxia-preconditioned MSC-derived exosomes exhibit elevated HIF-1α levels. When encapsulated in peptide-modified adhesive hyaluronic acid hydrogels, they induce VEGF upregulation in endothelial cells, thereby enhancing angiogenesis in a targeted manner [71]. Furthermore, hyaluronidase-responsive hydrogels combined with NGF and Fe3O4 nanoparticles can reduce chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan (CSPG) levels under an external magnetic field. This reduces inhibitory scarring, attenuates inflammation, and creates a more permissive environment for axonal regeneration [108]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that scaffold-delivered therapeutic cargos can precisely modulate key pathways such as HIF-1α/VEGF, CSPG signaling, and pro-/anti-inflammatory cytokine balance. This provides a mechanistic foundation for achieving targeted and controllable repair in SCI.

Although the synergistic use of functionalized payloads and cell-free therapeutic strategies shows considerable potential for optimizing the local microenvironment and enhancing anti-inflammatory and regenerative effects, current evidence is still largely based on small animal models. The existing studies are highly heterogeneous in terms of scaffold composition, payload types, drug delivery timing, and evaluation criteria. This variability makes it difficult to directly compare the relative efficacy of different approaches across studies. In addition, many reports primarily focus on histological or molecular improvements, while providing limited evidence of long-term functional recovery, in vivo fate of transplanted cells or exosomes, and overall clinical translatability. Therefore, further progress in this field requires more rigorous and standardized experimental designs, as well as validation in large animal models, to more accurately define the true synergistic advantages of these strategies [51, 76, 109, 110].

Intelligent Response System

As research advances, more sophisticated therapeutic strategies have emerged. These approaches go beyond conventional one-time, sustained drug release and instead enable systems to sense and respond dynamically to the injured microenvironment. Studies have shown that traditional drug-loaded hydrogels often cannot adjust release profiles in response to the evolving pathological stages of SCI, which limits their therapeutic efficacy. In contrast, intelligent responsive systems can modulate the release of different therapeutic agents according to local pathological cues, such as ROS, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), and pH changes. This allows treatment to be more closely aligned with the temporal progression of the disease [111, 112]. The central principle of this strategy can be summarized as delivering the appropriate therapeutic dose at the appropriate time and location. In this context, the scaffold is no longer a passive drug carrier. Instead, it functions as an active therapeutic system with environmental sensing, decision-making, and programmed release capabilities. This evolution shifts SCI treatment from static drug delivery toward a dynamic, on-demand therapeutic paradigm.

ROS-responsive scaffolds are currently one of the most active areas in the development of intelligent systems for SCI. Their design is based on the pronounced oxidative stress and rapid accumulation of ROS during the acute phase of injury. This enables the material to respond quickly to the pathological environment and release ROS scavengers, anti-inflammatory agents, or neuroprotective factors at early stages, thereby effectively limiting secondary injury [112]. Studies have shown that ROS/MMP dual-responsive injectable hydrogels can enable sequential therapeutic delivery. In the acute phase, they release ROS-scavenging nanomedicines to reduce oxidative stress and inflammation. In later stages, they release VEGF to promote angiogenesis and support NSC differentiation. This staged release strategy demonstrates clear advantages in aligning therapy with disease progression [111]. In addition, EV-integrated hydrogels incorporating phenylboronic ester bonds can achieve ROS-triggered release of bioactive cargo. These systems significantly reduce tissue damage after SCI and promote functional recovery [113].

Enzyme-responsive scaffolds are designed to exploit the elevated levels of specific enzymes in the injured spinal cord, such as MMPs and hyaluronidase. By responding to these signals, the systems release therapeutic agents preferentially at sites of high enzymatic activity, thereby improving the spatial specificity of local treatment. Increased enzyme activity within SCI lesions is recognized as an important biological cue for the design of microenvironment-responsive materials [112]. For example, in ROS/MMP dual-responsive hydrogels, MMP accumulation serves as a trigger for the second phase of drug release. This enables a staged therapeutic transition from early anti-inflammatory intervention to later regenerative support [111]. In addition, studies have reported dual-responsive hydrogel systems that maintain sustained drug release under conditions of persistently elevated hyaluronidase. When combined with magnetic functional components, these systems further enhance tissue repair outcomes and therapeutic efficiency [108].

Physically responsive scaffolds incorporate externally applied stimuli such as magnetic fields, electric fields, or light to achieve remote and controllable regulation. Their key advantage is that drug release and cellular behavior are not solely dependent on the endogenous injury microenvironment. Instead, they can be precisely modulated in both space and time through external physical cues. Representative studies include magnetically responsive self-assembling peptide hydrogels. Under an external magnetic field, the scaffold fibers can be aligned in an oriented manner, which promotes axonal infiltration and directional extension while improving inflammatory responses and reducing glial scar formation [48]. Another example is a dual-responsive hydrogel system loaded with Fe3O4 nanoparticles. When exposed to an external magnetic field, this system significantly enhances axonal growth and improves functional recovery after SCI [108]. These findings demonstrate that intelligent scaffolds are not limited to sensing pathological signals within the lesion. They can also achieve remote, non-invasive, and dynamic regulation through externally applied physical fields, enabling more flexible therapeutic control.

The core of this strategy is the forward shift of the therapeutic intervention window. By targeting the most amplified stage of secondary injury after SCI, it aims to interrupt the self-reinforcing cycle of pathological processes, including oxidative stress, inflammatory cascades, apoptosis, and microenvironmental deterioration. Following SCI, the initial mechanical insult is not the sole determinant of long-term outcome. More critical is the subsequent secondary injury driven by inflammation. This microenvironment undergoes pronounced temporal evolution. The acute phase is characterized by a rapid increase in ROS, extensive inflammatory cell infiltration, and cell death. Over time, it progressively transitions into fibrosis, cystic cavity formation, and inhibition of regeneration [81, 112–116]. Accordingly, intelligent responsive systems should not be viewed simply as slow-release drug carriers. Instead, they are designed to recognize specific pathological signals and intervene at the most critical stages of disease progression by delivering appropriate therapeutic agents at the optimal time and location. In particular, ROS-responsive systems can rapidly release antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agents during the peak of oxidative stress. This early intervention helps reduce ROS-mediated damage to neurons, glial cells, and oligodendrocytes, while also suppressing further amplification of inflammatory signaling networks [111].

From a more detailed mechanistic perspective, this intervention can be understood as a sequential, multi-stage regulatory process. First, it inhibits upstream ROS amplification. ROS-responsive hydrogels undergo structural degradation or trigger drug release in response to elevated oxidative stress. This reduces ROS accumulation and alleviates downstream mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative damage [117]. Second, it interrupts the positive feedback loop between oxidative stress and inflammation. As ROS levels decrease, the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α is concurrently reduced. At the same time, macrophages and microglia shift from a pro-inflammatory M1-like phenotype toward an anti-inflammatory M2-like state. This transition helps prevent sustained self-amplification of the inflammatory cascade [118, 119]. Third, it creates a permissive window for regeneration. Once the acute inflammatory environment is suppressed, the scaffold can subsequently release pro-angiogenic and pro-differentiation factors. This promotes a transition of the local microenvironment from a non-regenerative to a regenerative state, characterized by reduced inflammation, fibrosis, and cavity formation, alongside increased angiogenesis, neuronal survival, and endogenous stem cell differentiation [111]. Overall, the value of intelligent responsive systems is not limited to localized drug release. Rather, their key contribution lies in resetting the trajectory of the injured microenvironment at a critical pathological inflection point. They achieve this by rapidly stabilizing the acute injury environment and subsequently guiding the tissue toward a temporally organized regenerative phase.

However, most current intelligent responsive scaffolds remain prototype systems that rely on single stimuli such as ROS, pH, or enzymatic activity. As a result, their ability to replicate the complex, multidimensional spatiotemporal evolution of the inflammatory microenvironment after injury remains limited. In addition, existing studies are often constrained by the use of simplified experimental models, a lack of standardized design parameters, and insufficient evaluation of long-term safety and manufacturability. These limitations reduce the reliability, reproducibility, and translational potential of on-demand therapeutic interventions [9, 112] (Fig. 3).

Challenges and Future Outlook

Although bio-scaffold-based systems for stem cell and derivative delivery have demonstrated substantial therapeutic potential in animal studies, several major barriers must be addressed before clinical translation can be achieved. First, manufacturing and regulatory challenges remain significant. The long-term safety of smart responsive materials, their in vivo degradation profiles, biocompatibility, and batch-to-batch consistency require systematic validation. In addition, stem cell derivatives, particularly EVs, exhibit considerable heterogeneity in terms of cell source, production methods, dosage, administration routes, and outcome measures. These variations directly complicate standardized manufacturing processes and regulatory classification [120]. Second, mechanistic complexity presents another major limitation. The inflammatory microenvironment following SCI is highly heterogeneous in both space and time. Moreover, immune and glial cell phenotypes change dynamically throughout disease progression. As a result, findings derived from animal models cannot be directly or linearly extrapolated to human pathology [9]. Third, current evaluation systems remain limited. Most studies still rely heavily on conventional behavioral and histological assessments. There is an urgent need for more sensitive and objective biomarkers, as well as advanced functional evaluation tools, to better capture microenvironmental remodeling and neural circuit reconstruction [121–123]. At present, the next stage of development is expected to focus on strategy integration and precise therapeutic stratification. First, combining engineered exosomes with intelligent responsive scaffolds represents a promising frontier. In this approach, biomaterials enable spatiotemporal control of release, while engineered vesicles provide stable and programmable biological signals. Second, therapeutic strategies are likely to emphasize temporal coordination. Acute-phase interventions would prioritize ROS clearance and inflammation suppression. Subacute-phase treatments would focus on promoting angiogenesis and neural differentiation. Chronic-phase strategies would aim to enhance axonal regeneration and neural circuit integration. Looking further ahead, as understanding of patient-specific inflammatory profiles, injury phenotypes, and host responses deepens, SCI tissue engineering is expected to evolve from generalized scaffold designs toward personalized, functionally optimized bioengineered systems. These systems will integrate cells, biomolecules, and materials to enable truly individualized, mechanism-based, and stage-adapted therapies.

Conclusion

Overall, research on biomaterial scaffolds carrying stem cells and their derivatives for inflammatory regulation in SCI has shifted from early-stage efficacy validation toward more rational and optimized design strategies. Current studies no longer focus solely on defect filling or simple cell delivery. Instead, they emphasize the systematic engineering of scaffold architecture, release kinetics, and combinations of bioactive components. These designs are increasingly guided by the dynamic and stage-dependent characteristics of the injury microenvironment, with the aim of achieving more precise immunomodulation, neuroprotection, and regenerative enhancement [76, 124, 125]. This progression suggests that the key to next-generation regenerative medicine will not depend on a single material or cell type. Rather, it will rely on the ability of engineered systems to interact dynamically and effectively with the pathological microenvironment, thereby coordinating multi-level repair processes in a controlled and context-specific manner.

Future breakthroughs will depend on closer interdisciplinary collaboration among materials scientists, immunologists, neurobiologists, and clinicians. In addition, advanced technologies such as single-cell sequencing, spatial omics, and high-throughput lineage tracing are needed to construct a high-resolution spatiotemporal atlas of the inflammatory microenvironment in SCI [124]. Based on these datasets, the next generation of scaffold systems should be designed to recognize pathological signals, respond dynamically to microenvironmental changes, and actively guide tissue repair. These systems are expected to integrate intelligent responsive materials, engineered exosomes, and temporally programmed combination therapies [95, 126]. From this perspective, clinically translatable products should no longer be viewed as passive implants. Instead, they should function as active therapeutic platforms capable of continuous interaction with the injured spinal cord, progressively steering the local environment toward a regenerative and repair-supportive state.
