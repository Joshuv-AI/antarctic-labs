Background

Bioscience subjects, particularly anatomy and physiology, form a critical foundation in nursing education by underpinning clinical reasoning, decision-making, and safe patient care. Despite their importance, these subjects are consistently reported as challenging for nursing students due to their conceptual complexity and the need to integrate scientific knowledge with clinical application (Johnston et al., 2023; McVicar et al., 2020). Difficulties in mastering bioscience have been associated with increased academic stress, reduced confidence, and potential attrition, highlighting the importance of understanding factors associated with academic achievement in early nursing education.

Within this context, prior academic background—particularly science-stream education—has traditionally been regarded as a potential determinant of bioscience achievement. Students with prior exposure to subjects such as biology and chemistry are often assumed to possess foundational knowledge that facilitates subsequent learning in nursing programmes (Birks et al., 2021). This assumption has influenced admission policies and educational expectations across many nursing institutions. However, emerging evidence suggests that the advantage associated with prior academic background may not be as robust or sustained as previously believed, particularly within structured educational environments that provide standardised instruction and learning support (Blevins and Millen, 2022).

Beyond academic background, psychosocial learning factors—including academic motivation, study habits, and perceived support systems—have been widely recognised as contributors to academic achievement in higher education. Theoretical and empirical studies suggest that students who are motivated, engage in effective study strategies, and receive adequate academic and social support are more likely to achieve favourable learning outcomes (Alzahrani et al., 2022; Kim and Kim, 2021). In nursing education, these factors are particularly relevant given the demanding nature of the curriculum and the need for sustained engagement in both theoretical and clinical learning environments.

However, several important gaps remain in the existing literature. First, previous studies have primarily examined academic background and psychosocial learning factors independently, with limited research integrating both within a single analytical framework. Second, findings regarding the influence of these factors on academic achievement remain inconsistent across educational settings, with some studies reporting significant associations while others demonstrate weak or negligible effects (McKenna et al., 2022). Third, relatively fewer studies have explored these relationships within Southeast Asian nursing education contexts, where differences in educational systems, instructional approaches, and student learning experiences may influence academic outcomes. Specifically, limited research has simultaneously examined academic background and psychosocial learning factors among nursing students undertaking foundational bioscience education.

From a theoretical perspective, Constructivist Learning Theory posits that learning is an active and contextualised process in which knowledge is constructed through interaction with the learning environment rather than passively acquired based solely on prior knowledge (Biggs and Tang, 2011; Schunk and DiBenedetto, 2020). This perspective challenges the notion that academic background is a dominant determinant of learning outcomes and instead emphasises the role of instructional design, learner engagement, and contextual factors in shaping academic achievement. Within nursing education, constructivist approaches commonly emphasise active learning, collaborative learning, simulation, and reflective engagement as strategies that facilitate meaningful knowledge construction. Addressing these gaps may contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of factors associated with bioscience achievement in foundational nursing education. In particular, there is a need to critically evaluate whether commonly assumed predictors—such as science-stream background and self-reported learning behaviours—retain their explanatory power within structured nursing education environments.

Therefore, this study aimed to examine whether academic background and selected psychosocial learning factors were associated with academic achievement in a Human Structure and Function course among first-semester nursing students. The study also sought to critically re-evaluate the assumed advantage of science-stream education and explore the extent to which these commonly cited factors explain variation in bioscience achievement within a constructivist learning framework.

Methods

Study design

A quantitative cross-sectional study design was employed to examine the associations between academic background, psychosocial learning factors, and academic achievement among first-semester nursing students. This design was considered appropriate for investigating relationships among variables within a defined population at a single point in time.

Setting and participants

The study was conducted at a public nursing training institute in Malaysia. A census recruitment approach was employed, whereby all first-semester nursing students enrolled in the Human Structure and Function course during the study period were invited to participate. The total eligible cohort consisted of 108 students, all of whom consented to participate, resulting in a response rate of 100%. The distribution between science-stream and non-science-stream students reflected the actual enrolment pattern within the cohort, with a greater proportion of students originating from non-science academic backgrounds.

Data collection

Academic achievement was operationalised as students’ overall course score in the Human Structure and Function subject, expressed as a percentage ranging from 0 to 100. The overall course score comprised continuous assessment (40%) and final examination components (60%), including multiple-choice questions (MCQ; 20%) and short essay questions (SEQ; 40%). The assessment was designed to evaluate students’ understanding and application of foundational anatomy and physiology concepts.

Academic achievement data were obtained from official institutional examination records based on students’ first-attempt course scores prior to any supplementary examination. All students completed the same assessments under standardised institutional procedures, including identical examination schedules, examination questions, and marking rubrics. The minimum passing score for the course was 60%. Students who did not achieve the passing score were permitted to undertake supplementary examinations in accordance with institutional academic regulations.

Measures

Psychosocial learning factors included academic motivation, study habits, and perceived support systems. These constructs were measured using a structured questionnaire consisting of 30 items distributed across three domains: academic motivation (10 items), study habits (10 items), and perceived support systems (10 items). Responses were rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating higher levels of the respective constructs.

The questionnaire items were adapted from established educational and nursing education literature and modified to suit the context of nursing students enrolled in the Human Structure and Function course. Prior to the main study, a pilot study involving 30 s-semester nursing students was conducted to assess the clarity, relevance, and comprehensibility of the questionnaire items. Second-semester students were selected because they had previously completed the Human Structure and Function course and its examination. The pilot participants were not included in the main study sample. No substantial modifications to the questionnaire were required following pilot testing.

Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients. The questionnaire demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.82 to 0.88 across the three domains.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participant characteristics and study variables. Independent samples t-tests were conducted to examine differences in academic achievement according to academic background. Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess relationships among study variables, and multiple linear regression analysis was performed to examine the extent to which academic background and psychosocial learning factors were associated with academic achievement. Four predictors—study habits, academic motivation, perceived support systems, and academic background—were entered into the regression model.

No missing data were identified in the dataset.

Prior to inferential analyses, statistical assumptions were assessed. Normality was evaluated using skewness and kurtosis values, supported by visual inspection of histograms. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance values. VIF values ranged from 1.07 to 3.46, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity among the predictor variables. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with statistical significance set at p < .05.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the relevant institutional review board (Approval No. BPL.ILKKMKK.100-78 JLD. 15 (01)). Participation was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

All data were anonymised prior to analysis to ensure participant confidentiality. The study was conducted in accordance with established ethical principles for research involving human participants.

Discussion

The present study sought to critically re-examine the widely held assumption that students from science-stream academic backgrounds possess a sustained advantage in bioscience learning during early nursing education. Contrary to prevailing expectations, the findings demonstrated that academic background was not significantly associated with bioscience achievement. This finding challenges the traditional emphasis placed on prior science education as a determinant of academic success and aligns with emerging evidence suggesting that structured learning environments may attenuate initial differences in academic preparedness (Birks et al., 2021; Craft et al., 2021; McVicar et al., 2020).

One possible explanation for this finding is the role of structured curricula and pedagogical design in nursing education. Standardised instructional approaches, including constructive alignment and guided learning activities, may reduce disparities in prior knowledge by promoting equitable learning opportunities for students from diverse educational backgrounds (Biggs and Tang, 2011; Freeman et al., 2020). In such contexts, learning outcomes may depend less on prior academic exposure and more on how effectively knowledge is constructed through engagement with teaching strategies and learning tasks.

The relatively satisfactory academic achievement observed in this cohort may have reduced variability in scores, thereby limiting the ability to detect statistically significant associations between the examined variables and academic achievement.

Unexpectedly, psychosocial learning factors—including academic motivation, study habits, and perceived support systems—were also not significantly associated with academic achievement. This finding contrasts with previous studies that have reported positive associations between these variables and academic achievement (Alzahrani et al., 2022; Kim and Kim, 2021). One plausible explanation is that self-reported measures may not adequately capture the complexity or quality of actual learning behaviours. Prior research has highlighted discrepancies between perceived and actual study practices, suggesting that students may overestimate their level of engagement or employ ineffective learning strategies despite reporting high motivation (Credé and Kuncel, 2008; Richardson et al., 2012).

In addition, the absence of significant associations may reflect differences between surface-level and deep learning approaches. While students may report high levels of motivation and engagement, these do not necessarily translate into meaningful cognitive processing or conceptual understanding, particularly in complex subjects such as bioscience (Biggs and Tang, 2011). This distinction is critical, as deep learning—rather than the frequency or intensity of study behaviours—is more strongly associated with academic success in higher education.

From a theoretical perspective, the findings can be interpreted through the lens of Constructivist Learning Theory, which posits that learning is an active and contextualised process shaped by interactions between learners and their educational environment (Biggs and Tang, 2011; Schunk and DiBenedetto, 2020). Within this framework, academic achievement is not solely determined by prior knowledge or individual motivation, but by the extent to which learners actively construct understanding through engagement with instructional design, feedback, and learning activities. The absence of significant associations in this study may therefore reflect the potential role of contextual and pedagogical factors that were not directly measured.

Importantly, the low explanatory power of the regression model suggests that a substantial proportion of variance in academic achievement remains unaccounted for. Rather than indicating a limitation alone, this finding highlights the complexity of learning processes in nursing education. Previous research has emphasised that academic achievement is influenced by a combination of cognitive, metacognitive, and contextual factors, including self-regulated learning, instructional design, and classroom dynamics (McKenna et al., 2022; Zimmerman, 2002).

Additionally, theories such as Cognitive Load Theory and Multimedia Learning Theory suggest that the manner in which information is presented and processed may influence students’ understanding and retention of bioscience content (Mayer, 2020; Sweller, 2011). These perspectives indicate that factors beyond traditional academic and psychosocial variables may play a more substantial role in shaping learning outcomes. Additional factors not examined in the present study may also contribute to variation in bioscience achievement. For example, reading ability and reading comprehension have been identified as significant predictors of academic success among nursing students, suggesting that literacy-related competencies may influence learning outcomes in bioscience education (Mitchell, 2024).

The present findings also underscore the importance of reporting and interpreting non-significant findings in educational research. Although such findings are often underreported, they provide valuable insights by challenging widely held assumptions and refining theoretical understanding of academic achievement. In this study, the lack of significant associations suggests that commonly accepted determinants—such as academic background and self-reported learning behaviours—may not fully explain bioscience achievement in early nursing education, thereby highlighting the need for more nuanced and context-sensitive research approaches.

Educational implications

The findings of this study have several important implications for nursing education. First, the absence of significant differences in academic achievement based on academic background suggests that admission policies and educational expectations should avoid over-reliance on prior science-stream qualifications as indicators of future success. Instead, nursing programmes should emphasise inclusive teaching strategies that support students from diverse educational pathways.

Second, the lack of significant associations between psychosocial factors and academic achievement suggests that simply encouraging motivation or study habits may be insufficient to enhance learning outcomes. Educators may therefore consider focusing on the quality and design of learning experiences, including promoting active learning, critical thinking, and meaningful engagement with course content.

It is important to note that the pedagogical approaches discussed below were not implemented or evaluated within the Human Structure and Function course examined in this study. Rather, they are presented as examples from the broader nursing education literature to illustrate potential educational implications of the present findings.

Third, evidence suggests that pedagogical approaches such as active learning, simulation-based learning, and blended learning may enhance conceptual understanding and learning outcomes in bioscience education (Cant and Cooper, 2020; Freeman et al., 2020; Grønlien et al., 2021). These strategies may be more impactful than relying solely on students’ prior knowledge or self-directed learning behaviours. Therefore, greater emphasis on instructional design and supportive learning environments may facilitate deeper and more meaningful learning.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between academic background, psychosocial factors, and academic achievement. Longitudinal studies are therefore recommended to better understand changes in learning outcomes over time.

Second, the study was conducted within a single nursing training institution, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other educational contexts. Future research should consider multi-institutional samples to enhance external validity.

Third, psychosocial variables were measured using self-reported questionnaires, which may be subject to response bias and may not accurately reflect actual learning behaviours. More objective measures or mixed-method approaches may provide deeper insights into students’ learning processes.

Fourth, academic achievement was measured using first-attempt course scores prior to supplementary examinations. While this approach reflects students’ initial academic performance, it may not fully capture subsequent academic progression following remedial assessments.

Finally, the regression model explained only a small proportion of variance in academic achievement, suggesting that other unmeasured factors—such as metacognitive skills, instructional design, cognitive load, and self-regulated learning—may play a more substantial role in influencing bioscience learning outcomes.

Conclusion

This study challenges the widely held assumption that academic background and commonly cited psychosocial learning factors are key determinants of bioscience achievement in early nursing education. The findings demonstrated that neither science-stream background nor self-reported academic motivation, study habits, and perceived support systems were significantly associated with academic achievement among first-semester nursing students.

These findings suggest that bioscience achievement may be shaped by more complex and context-dependent mechanisms than traditionally assumed. In particular, the results highlight the potential importance of instructional design, learning environments, and deeper cognitive processes in influencing student learning, beyond prior academic preparation and surface-level learning behaviours.

From an educational perspective, the findings underscore the need for nursing programmes to move beyond reliance on entry-level academic background and to prioritise the development of effective, inclusive, and pedagogically sound learning strategies. Approaches that promote active engagement, critical thinking, and meaningful knowledge construction may be more impactful in enhancing bioscience learning outcomes.

Future research should explore additional factors that may better explain variation in academic achievement, including metacognitive skills, cognitive load, instructional design, self-regulated learning, and classroom dynamics. Longitudinal and multi-institutional studies are also recommended to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the determinants of learning in nursing education. Collectively, these findings contribute to a growing body of evidence questioning the explanatory value of traditional academic and psychosocial indicators in nursing education. Future studies may explore additional predictors of bioscience achievement, including reading comprehension and literacy-related factors.
