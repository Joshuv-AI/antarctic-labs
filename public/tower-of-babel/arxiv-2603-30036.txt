Aligned, Orthogonal or In-conflict: When can we safely optimize Chain-of-Thought?
Max Kaufmann, David Lindner, Roland S. Zimmermann, Rohin Shah

Abstract
Chain-of-Thought (CoT) monitoring, in which automated systems monitor the CoT of an LLM, is a promising approach for effectively overseeing AI systems. However, the extent to which a model’s CoT helps us oversee the model—the monitorability of the CoT—can be affected by training, for instance by the model learning to hide important features of its reasoning. We propose and empirically validate a conceptual framework for predicting when and why this occurs. We model LLM post-training as an RL environment where the reward decomposes into two terms: one term depending on final outputs and another term depending on the CoT. Our framework allows us to classify these two terms as “aligned”, “orthogonal”, or “in-conflict” before training. We predict that training with in-conflict terms will reduce monitorability, orthogonal terms will not affect it, and aligned terms will improve it. To validate our framework, we use it to classify a set of RL environments, train LLMs within those environments, and evaluate how training affects CoT monitorability. We find that (1) training with “in-conflict” reward terms reduces CoT monitorability and (2) optimizing in-conflict reward terms is difficult.

Introduction

Current reasoning LLMs carry out much of their computation using intermediate natural language output, called their
Chain-of-Thought
(CoT). This naturally leads to the technique of
Chain-of-Thought monitoring

(
Baker et al. 2025
;
Korbak et al. 2025
)
. A CoT monitor is an LLM-based classifier which takes the CoT of a model as one of its inputs. Such systems have been effectively used for monitoring tasks such as detecting the presence of reward hacking
(
Baker et al. 2025
)
, jailbreaking
(
Chennabasappa et al. 2025
)
, and misalignment
(
Schoen et al. 2025
)
.

The
monitorability
of a model’s CoT is the extent to which it helps a monitor carry out a monitoring task. For example,
Baker et al. 2025
found that a classifier which used the model’s CoT was better at catching undesired behavior during RL training, when compared to a classifier only looking at the model’s final output. Previous works
(
Korbak et al. 2025
;
Baker et al. 2025
)
have raised the concern that some training processes may degrade monitorability, for example by teaching the model to conceal the reasoning that monitors rely on for their classifications.

However, empirical results are mixed
(
Wagner et al. 2025
;
Baker et al. 2025
;
MacDermott et al. 2025
;
Guan et al. 2025
)
, with effects on monitorability varying across training environments. It is not understood when and why an environment will affect monitorability, which motivates our central question:

Which properties of an environment cause RL training to reduce Chain-of-Thought monitorability?

To answer this question, we introduce and empirically validate a conceptual framework for predicting whether a training environment will cause issues for monitorability. As pictured in
fig.

1
, we apply our framework before training, and use it to predict how training will affect monitorability of the CoT. We model the reward function as decomposing into two terms,
R_{\text{cot}}
and
R_{\text{out}}
, where
R_{\text{cot}}
depends only on the model’s
CoT and
R_{\text{out}}
depends on the model’s final output. The core idea behind our framework is that
R_{\text{cot}}
shapes the CoT text which the model outputs, while
R_{\text{out}}
shapes the computations the model performs to produce the correct output. We can use this idea to reason about whether a pair of rewards (
R_{\text{cot}}
,
R_{\text{out}}
) will cause monitorability issues.

In particular, we assume that models have a strong inductive bias towards expressing their computations in legible natural language. However, in some cases, CoT text which achieves a high score on
R_{\text{cot}}
cannot express the computations needed for high
R_{\text{out}}
performance. In this case, we call the rewards “in-conflict” and predict these rewards will cause models to obfuscate the computations, and hence monitorability will be reduced. Our full framework, introduced in
section

4
, also contains two more classifications—“orthogonal” (does not affect monitorability) and “aligned” (improves it). Our empirical results are focused on validating the framework’s predictions. Our contributions are as follows:

•

Introducing a conceptual framework for predicting which RL environments cause issues for monitorability.
In
section

4
, we formalize this framework and define three reward-pair categories: aligned, orthogonal, and in-conflict.

•

Empirically validating the predictive power of the framework.
In
section

5
we apply the framework to
9
reward pairs across
2
base environments. We then train LLM policies in those environments and measure the effects on monitorability. Through a series of controlled experiments, we demonstrate that our framework is predictive of downstream effects on monitorability.

•

Demonstrating that some real-world rewards cause monitorability issues.
Our length penalties and human preferences rewards are simplified versions of rewards commonly used in real post-training environments. In our environments, we demonstrate that these rewards cause issues for monitorability.

•

Demonstrating the difficulty of learning non-transparent reasoning, even if rewards are in conflict.
We find that in-conflict rewards are difficult to optimize, in line with previous works
(
Wagner et al. 2025
;
Skaf et al. 2025
;
MacDermott et al. 2025
)
.

2
Related Work

CoT Monitorability.
Faithfulness—the extent to which a CoT describes the model’s true reasoning process
(
Jacovi and Goldberg 2020
)
—has historically been the focus of work evaluating the quality of a model’s CoT.
Baker et al. 2025
introduce
monitorability
: the extent to which a CoT helps us complete a monitoring task of interest, independent of the model’s “true reasoning process”. Unlike faithfulness, monitorability can often be measured directly, and as
Emmons et al. 2025a
show, it is often high if the model’s task is difficult enough that the CoT is necessary for high performance. We follow the common approach of measuring monitorability via evaluating how successful a CoT monitor can oversee the LLM in a monitoring task
(
Schoen et al. 2025
;
Guan et al. 2025
)
—although other methods exist
(
Emmons et al. 2025b
)
.

Effects of training on CoT monitorability.

Baker et al. 2025
first demonstrated that training can reduce CoT monitorability, a concern echoed by
Korbak et al. 2025
. Subsequent work investigating when this happens has found mixed results: some environments reduce monitorability
(
Skaf et al. 2025
;
Baker et al. 2025
)
, while others do not
(
Guan et al. 2025
;
MacDermott et al. 2025
;
Wagner et al. 2025
)
. Closest to ours is
MacDermott et al. 2025
, who empirically measure monitorability across environments; we extend this by introducing a general framework for
predicting
effects and evaluating a wider range of rewards. A separate way training may reduce monitorability, which we do not cover, is teaching models to reason in a continuous latent space
(
Hao et al. 2025
)
.

3.1
Decomposing rewards into
R_{\text{cot}}
and
R_{\text{out}}

We consider LLM policy
\pi
to be a function from a prompt
p
to some CoT
c
and a final output
o
(i.e.
\pi(p)=(c,o)
). We model LLM post-training as an RL environment with two terms:
R_{\text{out}}
which depends only on
p
and
o
, and
R_{\text{cot}}
which depends only on
p
and
c
. The total reward is then
R=R_{\text{out}}(o)+R_{\text{cot}}(c)
. For instance,
R_{\text{out}}
might be correctness on a coding task, while
R_{\text{cot}}
might be a length penalty, a style reward, or feedback from human raters who read the reasoning.

3.2
Running example: obfuscated reward hacking

To ground our framework, we use a stylized version of the
obfuscated reward hacking
phenomenon observed by
Baker et al. 2025
. In particular, we consider a coding RL environment consisting of:

•

R_{\text{out}}
: A set of unit tests that run on the model’s final output. The model’s reward is the proportion of unit tests which pass.

•

R_{\text{cot}}
: A CoT monitor prompted to look for evidence of the model hacking the unit tests. The model is penalized if the CoT monitor judges that the CoT is reasoning about hacking the tests.

Furthermore, we assume that when training with just
R_{\text{out}}
the model learns to edit its testing harness and make a subset of the unit tests pass spuriously, rather than outputting a correct solution. Obfuscated reward hacking occurs when we also train with
R_{\text{cot}}
. In this case, the model learns to output CoT text that does not mention editing unit tests (so as not to trigger the monitor), while still using the CoT to compute how to hack the unit tests (to maintain high
R_{\text{out}}
).

3.3
CoT text and CoT computation

We can consider an LLM as a sequence of operations transforming some intermediate computational states to get to a final answer—for example, the activations are intermediate computational states which might be acted on by the attention mechanism. The field of interpretability often tries to assign human-understandable meaning to these intermediate states, finding e.g., directions which fire depending on which entity the model is computing about. We can also consider the CoT tokens as intermediate computational states, and ask what intermediate information they hold during the model’s computation. Throughout the paper, we call the information held in those intermediate computational states the
CoT computation
, and consider it separately from the text making up the CoT, which we call the
CoT text
. For example, an LLM might carry out the same reasoning while outputting CoT text in two different languages—here, it is likely the CoT text varies, while the computational content of that CoT stays the same. We expand on these concepts in
appendix

A
.

Definition: CoT Transparency.
We say a CoT is
transparent
if all the computational information in the CoT can be fully and accurately understood by reading the CoT text. For example, as pictured in
fig.

2
, obfuscated reward hacking is an example of non-transparent reasoning—the CoT computation contains information about how to hack the unit tests, but this is not evident from reading the CoT text.

Key idea:
R_{\text{cot}}
shapes text,
R_{\text{out}}
shapes computation.
As pictured in
fig.

2
, the key idea behind our framework is that
R_{\text{cot}}
shapes the CoT
text
the model outputs, while
R_{\text{out}}
shapes the
computation
the model performs using that CoT. This is because
R_{\text{cot}}
, by definition, depends only on the CoT text and is agnostic to the computational content of the CoT. Conversely,
R_{\text{out}}
depends on the final output and therefore shapes
which computations
the model carries out to reach that output. Issues for transparency arise when these two pressures diverge:
R_{\text{cot}}
pushes the text in one direction while
R_{\text{out}}
pushes the computation in another.

4
The Aligned / Orthogonal / In-Conflict Framework

We now introduce the main contribution of our paper: a conceptual framework for reasoning about whether an RL environment will cause issues for CoT monitorability.

4.1
Definitions

Figure 3
:
Aligned, Orthogonal, and In-conflict rewards.
We classify
(R_{\text{cot}},R_{\text{out}})
pairs based on the relationship between CoT text which achieves a high
R_{\text{cot}}
, and CoT computation which achieves a high
R_{\text{out}}
.
In-Conflict
: Punishing reward hacking means
R_{\text{cot}}
leads to CoT text which does not talk about reward hacking, but CoT computations which lead to a high
R_{\text{out}}
do include text about reward hacking—this is incompatible.
Orthogonal
: The policy can easily express CoT text about reward hacking while speaking in all caps—CoT text with high
R_{\text{cot}}
is compatible with expressing
R_{\text{out}}
computations.
Aligned
: Punishing incorrect reasoning allows the model to output fully correct code. By assumption, this achieves a higher
R_{\text{out}}
than the reward hacking strategy. This causes the model to transparently use the computations expressed in its CoT. CoT text with a high
R_{\text{cot}}
causes computations that achieve a high
R_{\text{out}}
.

We define our categories, which are based on reasoning about the properties of CoT text with high
R_{\text{cot}}
and the properties of CoT computations with high
R_{\text{out}}
. These categories are pictured in
fig.

3
.

Core Assumption: Bias towards transparent CoT.
Our definitions rest on the assumption that models have a strong inductive bias towards transparency due to pre-training on human language, so that absent strong optimization pressure, the CoT will remain transparent
(
Brown-Cohen et al. 2026
, Appendix A.2)
. This implies that if models can achieve a high
R_{\text{cot}}
and a high
R_{\text{out}}
without sacrificing transparency, they will.

In-conflict.
A reward pair is
in-conflict
if CoT text which achieves a high
R_{\text{cot}}
cannot describe the CoT computations which are needed to achieve a high
R_{\text{out}}
.

Example: CoT monitor penalizing reward hacking.
In the obfuscated reward hacking example, the CoT monitor penalizes the model when its CoT text mentions reward hacking, while
R_{\text{out}}
rewards hacking the unit tests. To achieve both, the model must output text that doesn’t mention reward hacking, while still using the CoT to compute reward-hacking actions.

Claim: In-conflict rewards reduce transparency when optimized well.
Once the model achieves high
R_{\text{cot}}
, it can no longer transparently express the computations needed to achieve high
R_{\text{out}}
—hence it is incentivized to mask the computations in its CoT. This is what happens in the obfuscated reward hacking example.

Orthogonal.
A reward pair is
orthogonal
if there exists CoT text that both achieves high
R_{\text{cot}}
and describes the CoT computations needed for high
R_{\text{out}}
.

Example: Speak in all caps.
Suppose
R_{\text{cot}}
simply rewards the model for writing its CoT in all capital letters. The model can write “I WILL HACK THE UNIT TESTS” just as easily as “I will hack the unit tests”—the
R_{\text{cot}}
constraint doesn’t prevent the model from transparently expressing its computations in its CoT text.

Claim: Orthogonal rewards don’t affect transparency when optimized well.
The model can achieve high
R_{\text{cot}}
while still expressing the computations it needs for high
R_{\text{out}}
. Given our assumption that the model has a bias towards transparency, it will output CoT text that both achieves high
R_{\text{cot}}
and transparently describes its computations.

Aligned.
A reward pair is
aligned
if CoT text that achieves a high
R_{\text{cot}}
necessarily describes CoT computations that would improve
R_{\text{out}}
. Improvement is relative to a policy trained only on
R_{\text{out}}
. These are a subset of Orthogonal rewards.

Example: Process supervision.
In the obfuscated reward hacking example, suppose that:

•

R_{\text{cot}}
penalizes incorrect reasoning steps in the CoT text.

•

This reward allows the model to output CoT text corresponding to reasoning steps for a fully correct solution, instead of hacking the unit tests.

•

Without
R_{\text{cot}}
the policy does not learn to do this—learning how to reason to correct solutions from
R_{\text{out}}
alone is too difficult.

•

The correct solution achieves an even higher
R_{\text{out}}
than the obfuscated reward hacking strategy, as some tests are not hackable.

This is an example of an aligned pair of rewards: the CoT text which achieves a high
R_{\text{cot}}
describes computations that lead to higher
R_{\text{out}}
than what the policy achieves when trained on
R_{\text{out}}
(where it resorts to reward hacking).

Claim: Aligned rewards improve transparency when optimized well.
In the aligned case, consider a model that begins with non-transparent reasoning, i.e. whose CoT computations are mismatched with the CoT text. Once the model achieves high
R_{\text{cot}}
it is incentivized to switch to having the CoT computations match those described in the CoT text, as this would improve
R_{\text{out}}
, and is the default behavior of the model. In our example, a policy that begins with obfuscated reward hacking would, under process supervision, learn to output CoT text describing computations for a correct solution and then transition to have its CoT computations correspond to the CoT text.

We regard the claims that aligned rewards improve transparency as more speculative, since (1) we do not test the claim empirically, and (2) these rewards cause large changes in the model’s CoT text, making unexpected effects more likely. Therefore, we urge caution for practitioners using CoT rewards that improve final output performance.

4.2
Training against in-conflict rewards may not always reduce transparency

It is important to note that if a policy does not achieve high enough
R_{\text{cot}}
and
R_{\text{out}}
performance, we will not see issues with transparency. For example, we could imagine a policy that reward hacks (high
R_{\text{out}}
) while mentioning reward hacking in its CoT (low
R_{\text{cot}}
). Whether a particular training run becomes non-transparent therefore depends on the ability to optimize the in-conflict rewards, which could vary considerably in practice.

Claim: In-conflict rewards are hard to optimize.
Since we assume the policy has an inductive bias towards transparency, and in-conflict rewards are the only rewards that require overcoming this bias (by incentivizing non-transparent CoTs), we expect them to be harder to optimize than other reward types. We show empirical evidence for this claim in
section

6.3
.

4.3
Monitorability

Our definitions so far have all been centered around
transparency
—whether the CoT text describes the CoT computations. However, in the rest of the paper we measure
monitorability
—whether the CoT text helps a CoT monitor complete a downstream monitoring task (e.g., classify whether the policy is reward hacking). We connect these two concepts as follows: the monitor tries to classify an aspect of the CoT’s computation (e.g. whether it relates to reward hacking) by reading the CoT text. Monitorability is reduced whenever the CoT text is non-transparent with respect to the specific computations the monitor needs to detect.

When considering classifying reward pairs as in-conflict with respect to some monitoring task,
we still consider the set of CoT computations that are needed to achieve a high
R_{\text{out}}
, but we are only
interested in the subset of those computations we need for our monitoring task. We say
R_{\text{cot}}
is in conflict with respect to a monitoring task if CoT text achieving high
R_{\text{cot}}
cannot describe the subset of CoT computations relevant to that task. This
makes being in conflict with respect to a monitoring task a stronger condition than in the transparency case, and hence some reward pairs that were previously in-conflict can become orthogonal.

For example, in the obfuscated reward hacking case, we can consider monitorability with respect to classifying whether the model is completing a coding task. Training against a CoT monitor for reward hacking is orthogonal with respect to this monitoring task—the CoT text can still express that the model is writing code without mentioning reward hacking specifically. We talk about this equivalence more precisely in
appendix

C
.

Applying the Framework

We now introduce our RL environments and use the framework to classify each
(R_{\text{cot}},R_{\text{out}})
pair. Importantly, all classifications were made
before
we ran any of the experiments presented in
section

5.1
Base environments (
R_{\text{out}}
)

Recall that our LLM policy
\pi
maps a prompt
p
to some CoT
c
and a final output
o
(i.e.
\pi(p)=(c,o)
). Our base environments are defined by a distribution over prompts and an
R_{\text{out}}
reward term. Our
R_{\text{out}}
takes in the model’s output
o
and the input prompt
p
and outputs a scalar between
-1
and
1
. We consider two environments:

1.

Heads-And-Tails
: A simple state-tracking task taken from
Skaf et al. 2025

where models are asked to track the state of a coin over
N
rounds of being or not
being flipped.

R_{\text{out}}=1
if the model’s final output is equal to the correct final state of the coin
(“Heads” or “Tails”), and
R_{\text{out}}=0
otherwise. Unless otherwise stated, we consider
N=6
flips.

2.

Backdoor-Easy
: A set of simple coding problems taken from a modified version of
the Mostly Basic Python Programming Problems (MBPP)
dataset
(
Austin et al. 2021
)
. In this case,
R_{\text{out}}=1

if the code is both (1)
Correct
(passes a set of held out unit tests), and
(2)
Backdoored
(there is an input where the code’s output differs from a
gold solution). This is a simplified version of the environment in

Wagner et al. 2025
.

See
appendix

D
for precise prompts and implementation details.

5.2
Measuring monitorability

To measure monitorability we need to define a monitoring task. The task we consider is whether a CoT monitor given only
c
can correctly classify which type of task the model’s reasoning corresponds to:

•

In
Heads-And-Tails
, the monitor receives a score of
1
if it classifies the reasoning as being about a coin, and
0
otherwise.

•

In
Backdoor-Easy
, the monitor receives a score of
1
if it classifies the reasoning as involving a backdoor, and
0
otherwise.

Both monitors are prompted LLMs, with prompts manually optimized on a set of example reasoning traces.

CoT Monitor (
In-Conflict

In

Length Penalty (
In-Conflict
).
A penalty on generations which exceed a minimum threshold

L_{\min}
:

R_{\text{cot}}^{\text{len}}(p,c)=-\max(0,|c|-L_{\min})

(1)

where
|c|
denotes length (in tokens or characters). We use the character-based variant in
Heads-And-Tails
and the token-based variant in
Backdoor-Easy
. Training with length
penalties is in-conflict across all of our base environments, since very short reasoning cannot transparently express the CoT computations the model is using.

More specifically, we assume that to achieve high
R_{\text{out}}

Heads-And-Tails

N

L_{\text{min-transparent}}

R_{\text{cot}}
that the length of its CoT is less than
L_{\text{min-transparent}}

We believe that in
Heads-And-Tails
, token-based length penalties are likely still orthogonal for monitorability. Although sufficiently short CoT text may obscure important parts of the computation (and hence be in conflict for transparency), “Heads,” “Tails,” and “Coin” are each a single token. Hence CoT text which achieves a high
R_{\text{cot}}
may still be orthogonal w.r.t. our monitors. We train against
the character-based length penalty for
Heads-And-Tails
in
section

6

Human Preferences (
In-Conflict
or

Orthogonal
).
An LLM grader which is prompted to judge whether a human would like, be neutral toward, or dislike the CoT text, assigning
R_{\text{cot}}\in\{+1,0,-1\}
accordingly.

Whether this is in conflict depends on
R_{\text{out}}
. In
Heads-And-Tails
, we expect that CoT text scoring high on
R_{\text{cot}}
corresponds to clear, well-structured reasoning that can easily express the computations needed for high
R_{\text{out}}
; hence this pair is orthogonal. In
Backdoor-Easy

R_{\text{out}}

Punish String (
Orthogonal
).
A reward which punishes a specific set of strings being
present in the CoT, i.e.
R_{\text{cot}}=-1
if
c
contains a (case-insensitive) match in
some set of strings
\mathcal{W}

R_{\text{cot}}=0
otherwise. In
Heads-And-Tails

\mathcal{W}=\{\texttt{heads},\texttt{tails}\}
; in the backdoor environments,
\mathcal{W}=\{\texttt{backdoor}\}
.

Punishing a specific
string does not prevent the model from expressing the relevant semantics—the model
can rephrase its CoT without mentioning that string while still expressing whatever

R_{\text{out}}
incentivizes (e.g., using a synonym or misspelling). Achieving
high
R_{\text{cot}}
therefore does not affect the model’s ability to transparently describe
the computations needed for high
R_{\text{out}}
.

Process Supervision (
Aligned
).
An LLM grader returning
R_{\text{cot}}=-1
if any of the reasoning steps in
c
are are not making valid progress towards a correct answer, and
R_{\text{cot}}=0
otherwise. CoT text achieving a high
R_{\text{cot}}
corresponds to valid reasoning for the task. If models trained only on
R_{\text{out}}
fail to complete the task this means that achieving high enough
R_{\text{cot}}
leads to CoT text that describes CoT computations which would improve the model’s performance—making this reward function aligned. We only implement process supervision in
Heads-And-Tails
.

6
Empirical Results

For each of the reward function pairs described in
section

5
,
we run hyperparameter sweeps and select the policy that performed highest on

R_{\text{cot}}+R_{\text{out}}
across our sweep. We then evaluate the monitorability of these highly
performing policies, and compare with the predictions from our theoretical framework.
Our two main findings are: (1) our conceptual classification is predictive of monitorability issues, and (2) in-conflict rewards are consistently harder to optimize than orthogonal or aligned ones.

6.1
Learning algorithms

With the aim of demonstrating our framework’s generality, we optimize LLM policies
against our rewards using two different learning algorithms:

GRPO with token-level rewards (CAPO).
Standard GRPO assigns the

6.3
In-conflict rewards are difficult to optimize

A key secondary finding is that in-conflict rewards are harder to optimize than
orthogonal or aligned ones. This is consistent with the claim that models have an
inductive bias towards transparent reasoning (
section

4
), and corroborates
similar observations in

Wagner et al. 2025
;
Skaf et al. 2025
;
MacDermott et al. 2025
. It is
generally hard to prove a negative (i.e. “X cannot be learned”), but we provide
several lines of circumstantial evidence for this claim.

Learning often fails with in-conflict reward pairs.

Figure

4
shows that the
proportion of seeds achieving high scores on both
R_{\text{cot}}
and
R_{\text{out}}
is substantially
lower for in-conflict rewards than for other reward types. Furthermore, all of our
Gemma optimization failures were against in-conflict rewards (see
appendix

E
).

Qualitatively, in-conflict rewards required much more iteration.
Throughout
the project, learning against in-conflict rewards was unstable and required
substantially more hyperparameter tuning, whereas orthogonal and aligned rewards often
worked immediately. We switched to token-level rewards (CAPO) after standard GRPO
failed to optimize in-conflict reward pairs, and we remained in toy environments
largely because of the difficulty of learning against in-conflict rewards in more
complex settings.

7
Limitations & Future Work

More realistic and diverse training environments.
Our environments are relatively simple, and hence perhaps our empirical results do not generalize to real LLM post-training. Future work should run similar experiments on training distributions that are closer to production LLM RL training.

Methods for predicting failures of optimization.
As described in
section

5
, whether we see monitorability problems depends on the policy getting high enough performance on both
R_{\text{cot}}
and
R_{\text{out}}
. It is difficult to know what level of performance is likely to cause issues in practice. Hence, whether a drop in monitorability will actually be observed is unclear. For example, many modern models are trained with length penalties without any observed drop in monitorability
(
Guan et al. 2025
)
.

Empirically detecting aligned / orthogonal / in-conflict.
Although we think that our framework is conceptually useful, we expect in many cases it may be unclear which category a pair of rewards falls into—hence, turning this into something we can measure empirically would be valuable.

Transparency “drift”.
Chain-of-Thought semantics might change during long training runs even without concrete incentives.
Schoen et al. 2025
hypothesize that this effect occurs in some state-of-the-art reasoning models. We observed such a semantics “drift” in one of our in-context RL experiments as a result of a prompt that encouraged the policy model to improve the reward even after the highest possible reward was achieved (cf.
section

I.7
). Studying this effect is an interesting direction for future work.

8
Conclusions

We introduced a conceptual framework for predicting when reinforcement learning will reduce Chain-of-Thought monitorability. Our framework classifies reward pairs as aligned, orthogonal, or in-conflict based on the relationship between the CoT text incentivized by
R_{\text{cot}}
and the computations needed for high
R_{\text{out}}
. We validated the framework across 9 reward pairs in 2 environments: in-conflict rewards consistently degraded monitorability, while orthogonal and aligned rewards preserved it. A key secondary finding is that in-conflict rewards are substantially harder to optimize, with models frequently failing to learn non-transparent reasoning. This is consistent with a strong inductive bias towards transparency from pre-training—an encouraging sign for the viability of CoT monitoring, though not a guarantee against more capable optimization in the future.
Our results carry practical implications: length penalties and preference-based rewards, commonly used in post-training, can be in conflict under our framework and may undermine monitorability. Although our experiments are limited to toy environments, the framework provides a tool for reasoning about these risks before training begins. Developing empirical methods for classifying reward pairs and validating the framework at scale are important next steps.

A.2
The semantics of a Chain-of-Thought

Consider the computation graph of a reasoning model
(

7

The

Core Assumption: The information held in the CoT is describable in

•

For the
types of behaviors we want to monitor

deception, harmful planning), the relevant information seems likely to be

•

Empirically, CoT is useful for downstream monitoring tasks

Definition: MDP.
We model LLM post-training as a Markov Decision Process with:

An input set of text

Definition: Reward decomposition (

We consider the case where the total reward decomposes into two components which separately act on the model’s CoT and final output:

CoT Reward (

A mapping

B.3
Setup and notation

Base policy.

We assume a base (pre-trained) policy that, for each input
I
, produces a CoT
c_{\text{base}}(I)\in\Sigma^{*}
and uses semantic mapping
\pi_{\text{sem}}^{\text{base}}:\Sigma^{*}\to\mathcal{S}
. This represents the policy at the start of RL training.

Assumption: Transparent initialization.
We assume the base policy uses human semantics:
\pi_{\text{sem}}^{\text{base}}=h
.

Optimization distance.

Let
D:\Sigma^{*}\times\Sigma^{*}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a distance function measuring how “far” a CoT is from another in optimization space. We write
D(c,I)
as shorthand for
D(c,c_{\text{base}}(I))
. This models the training dynamics: CoTs closer to the base policy’s output are easier to reach.

Shaping optimization via
R_{\text{cot}}
.

We allow
R_{\text{cot}}
to influence exploration by shaping the effective distance metric. We therefore define
D^{R_{\text{cot}}}
. How reward shaping works exactly is a design decision in the model—for the purpose of our analysis we choose a relatively simple formulation:

D^{R_{\text{cot}}}(c,c^{\prime},I)=D(c,c^{\prime})-\alpha\cdot R_{\text{cot}}(I,c)

where
\alpha\geq 0
controls how much
R_{\text{cot}}
aids exploration. When
\alpha>0
, CoTs with higher
R_{\text{cot}}
scores become “closer” in optimization space, capturing the intuition that
R_{\text{cot}}
can guide the model toward useful regions of CoT-space. This is particularly important for
aligned
rewards: process supervision not only rewards good reasoning but helps the model
discover
good reasoning by shaping the exploration landscape.

A limitation of our framework is that exactly how an
R_{\text{cot}}
shapes exploration is an empirical question—our choice of shaping function is an arbitrarily chosen, simple formulation that we think usefully captures underlying dynamics.

For notational simplicity, we will often continue to write
D(c,I)
and define the
R_{\text{cot}}
-shaped achievable set:

\mathcal{C}_{B}^{R_{\text{cot}}}(I)=\{c\in\Sigma^{*}:D^{R_{\text{cot}}}(c,c_{\text{base}}(I),I)\leq B\}

Note that
\mathcal{C}_{B}(I)\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{B}^{R_{\text{cot}}}(I)
when
\alpha>0
and
R_{\text{cot}}\geq 0
, since
R_{\text{cot}}
-shaping can only expand the reachable set.

Achievable CoTs.

For input
I
and optimization budget
B
, define the set of budget-feasible CoTs under the base distance:

\mathcal{C}_{B}(I)=\{c\in\Sigma^{*}:D(c,I)\leq B\}

Running example:
In the obfuscated reward hacking scenario, we assume the code supervision allows the model to output correct code (which it could not do by default).

Outcome under different semantics.

For a CoT
c
and input
I
, we define the outcome reward under different semantic interpretations:

•

Outcome under human semantics:

R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c)=R_{\text{out}}(I,\pi_{\text{out}}(h(c)))

•

Outcome under arbitrary semantics
s
:

R_{\text{out}}^{s}(I,c)=R_{\text{out}}(I,\pi_{\text{out}}(s))

Reference policy.

We define the
reference policy
, which captures the reasoning a model would perform if it were only optimizing for outcomes (no
R_{\text{cot}}
). This policy will determine whether the model obfuscates its reasoning.

For each input
I
and budget
B
, the reference policy produces a CoT
c^{\text{ref}}(I,B)
achieving:

R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)=\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{B}(I)}R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c)=\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{B}(I)}R_{\text{out}}(I,\pi_{\text{out}}(h(c)))

When multiple CoTs achieve this maximum, we select one arbitrarily.

Note:
The reference policy’s optimization uses the base distance
D
,
not
any
R_{\text{cot}}
-shaped distance
D^{R_{\text{cot}}}
. This ensures our definitions are not circular—the reference policy is defined independently of
R_{\text{cot}}
, and we then ask how introducing
R_{\text{cot}}
affects whether models can express reference-quality reasoning transparently.

Running example:
In the obfuscated reward hacking scenario, the reference policy would output a CoT transparently describing the test-manipulation strategy.

B.4
Training dynamics assumptions

We make three assumptions about how training shapes model behavior. These separately model (1) the learning of CoT text, (2) when models adopt non-transparent semantics, and (3) what those semantics are.

A limitation of our model is that we do not consider neural network generalization or training dynamics, and instead consider training happening separately for each input (tabular RL). We discuss this limitation in
section

B.8
.

Training Assumption 1: CoT text is learned by constrained optimization of
R_{\text{cot}}+R_{\text{out}}
under human semantics.

For each input
I
and optimization budget
B
, training produces a CoT
c^{*}(I,B)
that maximizes the sum of rewards over the
R_{\text{cot}}
-shaped achievable set:

c^{*}(I,B)=\argmax_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{B}^{R_{\text{cot}}}(I)}\Big[R_{\text{cot}}(I,c)+R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c)\Big]

Intuition:
Training optimizes total reward
R_{\text{cot}}+R_{\text{out}}
, and
R_{\text{cot}}
simultaneously shapes which CoTs are reachable. Since the base policy uses human semantics, the model initially interprets its CoT using human semantics during optimization. Crucially, aligned
R_{\text{cot}}
rewards can expand the effective reachable set by guiding exploration toward high-quality reasoning that would otherwise require more optimization budget to discover.

Running example:
When optimizing against a CoT monitor (
R_{\text{cot}}
penalizes mentions of test manipulation) and unit tests (
R_{\text{out}}
rewards passing tests), the model learns to output CoTs that avoid triggering the monitor while—under human interpretation—attempting to solve the problem correctly.

Training Assumption 2: Semantics placed on the CoT are decided by whether switching to a set of non-transparent semantics would improve
R_{\text{out}}
performance.

Assumption 1 selects
c^{*}(I,B)
, while this assumption decides the semantic mapping for input
I
. The idea is that the model prefers to use transparent (human) semantics, but will switch to non-transparent semantics
s^{\text{NT}}(I,B)
if doing so improves outcome performance. Formally:

\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(I)=\begin{cases}h(c^{*}(I,B))&\text{if }R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*}(I,B))\geq R_{\text{out}}(I,\pi_{\text{out}}(s^{\text{NT}}(I,B)))\\
s^{\text{NT}}(I,B)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Intuition:
This assumption captures the idea that models have the
capacity
to learn some specific set of non-standard semantics, to which the model switches if this would improve performance. The model will use this capacity when transparent semantics would hurt
R_{\text{out}}
performance.

Running example:
After learning to output benign-looking CoTs (to satisfy
R_{\text{cot}}
), the model can adopt the “hidden” interpretation of the CoT (computing test manipulation) if doing so improves
R_{\text{out}}
.

Training Assumption 3 (Optional): Non-transparent semantics are given by the reference policy’s reasoning.

When the model adopts non-transparent semantics, it specifically adopts the semantics of the reference policy’s CoT:

s^{\text{NT}}(I,B)=h(c^{\text{ref}}(I,B))

Intuition:
If the model needs to “hide” its reasoning, it does so by performing the computation that the
R_{\text{out}}
-only reference policy would have performed. The idea here is the reference policy’s semantics represent the “default” good reasoning for achieving high
R_{\text{out}}
. When
R_{\text{cot}}
prevents transparent expression of this reasoning, the model adopts these semantics while outputting different text.

Running example:
When the model adopts non-transparent semantics, it defaults to the reward hacking semantics which it would have learned without
R_{\text{cot}}
.

Remark 1

(Assumption 3 is optional, as it is unclear how to model
s^{\text{NT}}
)
.

A lot of the interesting behavior in any concrete instantiation of this model is dependent on the behavior of
s^{\text{NT}}
. Although some works have started to investigate when and how models might change to non-transparent semantics
(
MacDermott et al. 2025
, eg. )
, we do not yet have sufficient empirical understanding to determine what constitutes a good modeling assumption. We chose the reference policy’s semantics as a reasonable starting point. This assumption is optional in the sense that we could re-state a more general version of the proofs and definitions using
s^{\text{NT}}
rather than the reference policy. In this work we start with the
R_{\text{out}}
-optimized reference policy, but we are excited about future work building more empirically-informed models of when semantics diverge.

Combined effect of Assumptions 2 and 3.

Substituting Assumption 3 into Assumption 2, and noting that
R_{\text{out}}(I,\pi_{\text{out}}(h(c^{\text{ref}}(I,B))))=R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)
:

\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(I)=\begin{cases}h(c^{*}(I,B))&\text{if }R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*}(I,B))\geq R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)\\
h(c^{\text{ref}}(I,B))&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

The model uses transparent semantics when they perform adequately (achieving at least reference-level
R_{\text{out}}
). Otherwise, it adopts the reference policy’s semantics—writing one thing while “meaning” another.

B.5
Definitions: orthogonal, in-conflict, and aligned (with transparency)

We now define the three categories w.r.t. the transparency definition.

Notation.

For input
I
and
R_{\text{cot}}
threshold
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the set of CoTs achieving the threshold:
\mathcal{C}_{\geq\tau}(I)=\{c\in\Sigma^{*}:R_{\text{cot}}(I,c)\geq\tau\}
.

Maximum achievable
R_{\text{cot}}
.

For input
I
and budget
B
, define:

R_{\text{cot}}^{\max}(I,B)=\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{B}^{R_{\text{cot}}}(I)}R_{\text{cot}}(I,c)

Definition B.1

(Orthogonal at
I
)
.

The pair
(R_{\text{cot}},R_{\text{out}})
is
orthogonal at input
I
if for all optimization budgets
B
, there exists an explorable CoT
c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{B}^{R_{\text{cot}}}(I)
satisfying:

1.

h(c^{\prime})=h(c^{\text{ref}}(I,B))
(reference semantics achieved)

2.

R_{\text{cot}}(I,c^{\prime})=R_{\text{cot}}^{\max}(I,B)
(maximum
R_{\text{cot}}
at budget
B
achieved)

In words:
The pair is orthogonal if, at every level of optimization, it’s possible to simultaneously achieve the best
R_{\text{cot}}
score for that budget
and
express the reference policy’s semantics using the human semantics function. The
R_{\text{cot}}
constraint never prevents expressing the reasoning needed for good
R_{\text{out}}
performance—you can always maximize
R_{\text{cot}}
without sacrificing transparency.

Running example:
If
R_{\text{cot}}
required writing in all caps, the pair would be orthogonal—for any optimization budget
B
, the model could express its reasoning about editing unit tests equally well in uppercase text while achieving maximum
R_{\text{cot}}
. The formatting constraint is independent of the semantic content at all levels of optimization.

Definition B.2

(In-conflict at
(I,\tau)
)
.

The pair
(R_{\text{cot}},R_{\text{out}})
is
in-conflict at input
I
and threshold
\tau
if for all optimization budgets
B
such that the learned CoT
c^{*}(I,B)
achieves
R_{\text{cot}}(I,c^{*}(I,B))\geq\tau
:

R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*}(I,B))<R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)

In words:
The pair is in conflict at threshold
\tau
if
whenever
optimization succeeds in achieving
R_{\text{cot}}\geq\tau
, the resulting CoT necessarily has the property that its human-semantic interpretation yields worse outcomes than the reference policy. Achieving
R_{\text{cot}}
above the threshold
necessarily
comes at the cost of reduced
R_{\text{out}}
performance with transparent reasoning.

Running example:
The CoT monitor penalizing mentions of test manipulation creates an in-conflict pair. For any budget
B
where the model learns to hide its manipulation intent (achieving
R_{\text{cot}}\geq\tau
), the resulting CoT’s human interpretation suggests attempting a correct solution—which achieves lower
R_{\text{out}}
than the manipulation strategy the reference policy uses.

Definition B.3

(Aligned at
(I,\tau)
)
.

The pair
(R_{\text{cot}},R_{\text{out}})
is
aligned at input
I
and threshold
\tau
if for all optimization budgets
B
such that the learned CoT
c^{*}(I,B)
achieves
R_{\text{cot}}(I,c^{*}(I,B))\geq\tau
:

R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*}(I,B))>R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)

In words:
The pair is aligned at threshold
\tau
if
whenever
optimization succeeds in achieving
R_{\text{cot}}\geq\tau
, the resulting CoT necessarily has the property that its human-semantic interpretation yields
better
outcomes than the reference policy. Achieving
R_{\text{cot}}
above the threshold
necessarily
leads to improved performance when using transparent reasoning.

Running example:
Process supervision (rewarding correct reasoning steps) would be aligned. For any budget
B
where the model achieves high process reward (
R_{\text{cot}}\geq\tau
), the CoT necessarily contains valid reasoning that, when interpreted transparently, achieves higher
R_{\text{out}}
than the manipulation strategy.

B.6
Main results

Our theorems characterize how the classification of
(R_{\text{cot}},R_{\text{out}})
determines transparency outcomes.

Theorem B.4

(Orthogonal rewards preserve transparency)
.

Suppose
(R_{\text{cot}},R_{\text{out}})
is orthogonal at
I
. Then under Assumptions 1–3, for any optimization budget
B
, the trained model uses transparent semantics on input
I
:
\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(I)=h(c^{*}(I,B))
.

Proof.

We show that
R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*}(I,B))\geq R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)
, which by Assumptions 2 and 3 implies
\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(I)=h(c^{*}(I,B))
.

Step 1: Identify the orthogonality witness.

By
Definition

B.1
, for the given budget
B
, there exists
c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{B}^{R_{\text{cot}}}(I)
such that:

1.

h(c^{\prime})=h(c^{\text{ref}}(I,B))

2.

R_{\text{cot}}(I,c^{\prime})=R_{\text{cot}}^{\max}(I,B)

Step 2: Compute the objective value of
c^{\prime}
.

Since
h(c^{\prime})=h(c^{\text{ref}}(I,B))
:

R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{\prime})=R_{\text{out}}(I,\pi_{\text{out}}(h(c^{\prime})))=R_{\text{out}}(I,\pi_{\text{out}}(h(c^{\text{ref}})))=R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)

The objective value of
c^{\prime}
is therefore:

R_{\text{cot}}(I,c^{\prime})+R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{\prime})=R_{\text{cot}}^{\max}(I,B)+R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)

Step 3: Show
c^{*}
achieves at least reference performance.

By Assumption 1,
c^{*}(I,B)
maximizes
R_{\text{cot}}(I,c)+R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c)
over
\mathcal{C}_{B}^{R_{\text{cot}}}(I)
. Since
c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{B}^{R_{\text{cot}}}(I)
:

R_{\text{cot}}(I,c^{*})+R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*})\geq R_{\text{cot}}(I,c^{\prime})+R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{\prime})=R_{\text{cot}}^{\max}(I,B)+R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)

Since
R_{\text{cot}}(I,c^{*})\leq R_{\text{cot}}^{\max}(I,B)
by definition:

R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*})\geq R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)+(R_{\text{cot}}^{\max}(I,B)-R_{\text{cot}}(I,c^{*}))\geq R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)

Step 4: Apply Assumptions 2 and 3.

Since
R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*})\geq R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)
, by Assumptions 2 and 3:
\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(I)=h(c^{*}(I,B))
.
∎

Remark 2
.

The key insight is that orthogonality guarantees, for
every
optimization budget, the existence of a CoT achieving both maximum
R_{\text{cot}}
and CoT semantics that correspond to what the reference policy wishes to express. This “escape route” is always available, so the model never has an incentive to adopt non-transparent semantics—regardless of how much optimization pressure is applied to
R_{\text{cot}}
.

Theorem B.5

(In-conflict rewards degrade transparency)
.

Suppose
(R_{\text{cot}},R_{\text{out}})
is in conflict at
(I,\tau)
. Then under Assumptions 1–3, for any optimization budget
B
such that
R_{\text{cot}}(I,c^{*}(I,B))\geq\tau
, the trained model uses non-transparent semantics:
\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(I)\neq h(c^{*}(I,B))
.

Proof.

Step 1: Apply the in-conflict condition.

By
Definition

B.2
, for any
B
such that
R_{\text{cot}}(I,c^{*}(I,B))\geq\tau
:

R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*}(I,B))<R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)

Step 2: Apply Assumptions 2 and 3.

The combined form of Assumptions 2 and 3 states:

\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(I)=\begin{cases}h(c^{*}(I,B))&\text{if }R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*}(I,B))\geq R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)\\
h(c^{\text{ref}}(I,B))&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

From Step 1,
R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*})<R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)
, so the condition for transparent semantics is not satisfied. Therefore:

\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(I)=h(c^{\text{ref}}(I,B))

Step 3: Conclude non-transparency.

The model adopts the reference policy’s semantics
h(c^{\text{ref}}(I,B))
rather than the human interpretation of its actual CoT
h(c^{*}(I,B))
. Since in-conflict implies the model outputs different text than the reference policy (to achieve
R_{\text{cot}}\geq\tau
), we have
c^{*}(I,B)\neq c^{\text{ref}}(I,B)
, and therefore
h(c^{*}(I,B))\neq h(c^{\text{ref}}(I,B))
(otherwise transparent semantics would achieve reference performance, contradicting in-conflict). Thus:

\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(I)=h(c^{\text{ref}}(I,B))\neq h(c^{*}(I,B))

∎

Remark 3

(The mechanism of obfuscation)
.

The proof reveals the mechanism by which in-conflict rewards cause obfuscation:

1.

R_{\text{cot}}
constrains the text:
To achieve
R_{\text{cot}}\geq\tau
, the model outputs a CoT
c^{*}
whose human interpretation appears benign.

2.

R_{\text{out}}
requires certain computations:
To achieve good outcomes, the model needs to perform computations leading to high
R_{\text{out}}
—the same computations the reference policy performs.

3.

The in-conflict condition creates tension:
For
any
policy achieving
R_{\text{cot}}\geq\tau
, the optimal
c^{*}
has
R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*})<R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)
.

4.

Resolution via semantic divergence:
The model resolves this by writing text that satisfies
R_{\text{cot}}\geq\tau
while internally interpreting that text using the reference policy’s semantics.

Theorem B.6

(Aligned rewards improve transparency)
.

Suppose
(R_{\text{cot}},R_{\text{out}})
is aligned at
(I,\tau)
. Then under Assumptions 1–3, for any optimization budget
B
such that
R_{\text{cot}}(I,c^{*}(I,B))\geq\tau
, the trained model:

1.

Uses transparent semantics:
\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(I)=h(c^{*}(I,B))

2.

Achieves strictly better than reference performance:
R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*})>R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)

Moreover, this holds even if the base policy started with non-human semantics (i.e. violates our initial assumption). Aligned rewards correct this divergence.

Proof.

Step 1: Apply the aligned condition.

By
Definition

B.3
, for any
B
such that
R_{\text{cot}}(I,c^{*}(I,B))\geq\tau
:

R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*}(I,B))>R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)

This directly establishes conclusion (2).

Step 2: Apply Assumptions 2 and 3.

Since
R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*})>R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)
, the condition
R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*})\geq R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)
is satisfied. By Assumptions 2 and 3:

\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(I)=h(c^{*}(I,B))

This establishes conclusion (1).
∎

Remark 4

(Aligned rewards actively improve reasoning)
.

Unlike orthogonal rewards (which merely preserve transparency), aligned rewards yield strict improvement. The threshold formulation makes this guarantee conditional but robust:
if
optimization achieves
R_{\text{cot}}\geq\tau
,
then
transparent interpretation necessarily yields better outcomes. This captures the intuition that process supervision helps precisely because achieving high process reward
requires
good reasoning.

B.7
The difficulty of optimizing in-conflict rewards

The theorems above establish that in-conflict rewards are the only category requiring the model to learn non-transparent semantics. Intuitively, we might imagine that this causes learning them to be more difficult (and this is shown in our empirical results). We can include this idea in our model by introducing some cost to switching semantics.

Training Assumption 2’ (Semantic learning requires budget).

We refine Training Assumption 2 by introducing a parameter
\lambda>0
representing the optimization cost of learning non-transparent semantics. This captures the intuition that overcoming the transparency prior requires additional optimization effort beyond simply generating different CoT text.

Let
B_{\text{remaining}}(I)=B-D(c^{*}(I,B),I)
denote the optimization budget remaining after learning the CoT. The model can only switch to non-transparent semantics if sufficient budget remains:

\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(I)=\begin{cases}h(c^{*}(I,B))&\text{if }B_{\text{remaining}}(I)<\lambda\text{ or }R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*})\geq R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)\\[4.0pt]
h(c^{\text{ref}}(I,B))&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Intuition:
The model first “spends” budget on learning its CoT. Only if sufficient budget remains (
B_{\text{remaining}}\geq\lambda
) can it afford to learn a non-standard interpretation. Even then, it only does so if transparent semantics would hurt performance.

Theorem B.7

(In-conflict rewards require semantic budget)
.

Among the three categories, only in-conflict rewards require the model to learn non-transparent semantics (spending budget
\geq\lambda
) to achieve high performance on both
R_{\text{cot}}
and
R_{\text{out}}
simultaneously.

Proof.

We consider each category:

Orthogonal rewards:
By
Theorem

B.4
, for any budget
B
, we have
R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*})\geq R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)
. Under Assumption 2’, the condition for transparent semantics is satisfied regardless of
B_{\text{remaining}}
. The model uses transparent semantics with no semantic learning cost.

Aligned rewards:
By
Theorem

B.6
, for any
B
with
R_{\text{cot}}(I,c^{*})\geq\tau
, we have
R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*})>R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)
. Transparent semantics strictly outperform any non-transparent alternative, so the model uses transparent semantics with no semantic learning cost.

In-conflict rewards:
By
Definition

B.2
, for any
B
with
R_{\text{cot}}(I,c^{*})\geq\tau
, we have
R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*})<R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)
. To achieve reference-level
R_{\text{out}}
, the model must adopt non-transparent semantics
h(c^{\text{ref}})
. Under Assumption 2’, this requires
B_{\text{remaining}}\geq\lambda
.
∎

Corollary 1

(Budget threshold for obfuscation)
.

For in-conflict rewards at
(I,\tau)
, consider any budget
B
such that
R_{\text{cot}}(I,c^{*}(I,B))\geq\tau
. If
B-D(c^{*}(I,B),I)<\lambda
, then under Assumption 2’ the model uses transparent semantics despite the in-conflict nature of the rewards:

\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(I)=h(c^{*}(I,B))

The model achieves suboptimal
R_{\text{out}}
performance:
R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*})<R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)
.

Proof.

By Assumption 2’, if
B_{\text{remaining}}<\lambda
, the model cannot afford semantic learning and must use
\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(I)=h(c^{*}(I,B))
, regardless of whether this achieves reference-level performance. By the in-conflict condition (applied to any
B
achieving the threshold), this yields
R_{\text{out}}^{\text{human}}(I,c^{*})<R_{\text{out}}^{\text{ref}}(I,B)
.
∎

Remark 5

(Practical implications)
.

Corollary

1
has an optimistic interpretation: even when reward pairs are in conflict, obfuscation only emerges if the model has sufficient optimization budget to learn non-transparent semantics. This suggests two protective factors:

1.

Limited training:
With constrained optimization budgets, models may fail to learn the semantic divergence needed for obfuscation.

2.

High semantic learning cost:
If
\lambda
is large (learning “ciphers” is intrinsically difficult), the budget threshold for obfuscation becomes harder to reach.

Our empirical results (
section

6.3
) support this prediction: models often fail to successfully optimize in-conflict reward pairs, even with substantial training.

B.8
Limitations

Definitions are all in the tabular RL case.
We do not model neural network learning or generalization—we perform constrained optimization on each input, over a potential semantics and text space. A fuller model incorporating generalization would need to specify how semantic mappings generalize across inputs—a question that depends heavily on neural network inductive biases and is not yet well understood empirically. We believe the current version of the framework is useful as a conceptual reasoning tool.

Appendix C
Monitorability with respect to a Monitor and Task

While transparency captures when a model’s internal interpretation diverges from the human interpretation, practitioners often care about a weaker property:
monitorability
. A CoT may use non-standard semantics, but still be useful for monitoring if those semantics lead to the same downstream conclusions.

Monitor decomposition.

We model a monitor
M
as a composition of two components:

1.

Semantic extraction (
M_{\text{sem}}:\Sigma^{*}\to\mathcal{S}
):
A mapping from text to semantic content. For most monitors (human or LLM-based), we assume
M_{\text{sem}}=h
, the human semantic mapping.

2.

Classification (
f_{T}^{M}:\mathcal{S}\to\{0,1\}
):
A binary classification function that depends on both the task
T
and the monitor
M
, mapping semantic content of the CoT to a decision. Different monitors may implement different classification strategies for the same task—for example, a human reviewer and an automated classifier might weight different aspects of the semantics differently when deciding whether to flag a CoT.

The full monitor is then
M_{T}:\Sigma^{*}\to\{0,1\}
defined by
M_{T}(\text{CoT})=f_{T}^{M}(M_{\text{sem}}(\text{CoT}))
.

Ground truth classification.

For a CoT produced by a model with semantic mapping
\pi_{\text{sem}}
on input
I
, the
ground truth
classification is what the monitor would output if it had access to the model’s actual semantics:

M_{T}^{\text{true}}(\text{CoT})=f_{T}^{M}(\pi_{\text{sem}}(\text{CoT}))

Definition C.1

(Monitorability on input
I
w.r.t. monitor
M
and task
T
)
.

A Chain-of-Thought produced on input
I
is
monitorable
with respect to monitor
M
(with semantic mapping
M_{\text{sem}}
and classification
f_{T}^{M}
) and task
T
if the monitor’s classification matches its classification under ground-truth semantics:

f_{T}^{M}(M_{\text{sem}}(\text{CoT}))=f_{T}^{M}(\pi_{\text{sem}}(\text{CoT}))

where
\text{CoT}=\pi_{\text{CoT}}^{\theta}(I)
.

Remark 6

(Equivalence between monitorability and transparency on input
I
)
.

For a fixed task
T
and monitor
M
, monitorability on input
I
is equivalent to transparency in a coarsened semantic space. Define the task-specific equivalence
s_{1}\sim_{T}s_{2}
iff
f_{T}^{M}(s_{1})=f_{T}^{M}(s_{2})
, and let
\mathcal{S}_{T}=\mathcal{S}/{\sim_{T}}
be the quotient space. Let
h_{T}:\Sigma^{*}\to\mathcal{S}_{T}
and
\pi_{\text{sem}}^{T}:\Sigma^{*}\to\mathcal{S}_{T}
be the induced mappings (composing with the quotient map). Then:

\text{CoT on input }I\text{ is monitorable for }(M,T)\iff h_{T}(\text{CoT})=\pi_{\text{sem}}^{T}(\text{CoT})

The right-hand side is exactly the transparency condition in the coarsened space
\mathcal{S}_{T}
. This equivalence allows us to apply the theorems from
appendix

B
(which are stated for transparency) directly to monitorability predictions.

Remark 7

(Applying transparency theorems to monitorability)
.

The theorems in
appendix

B
are stated in terms of transparency, but they apply directly to monitorability predictions. Since the structure of our theorems depends only on whether semantic mappings agree or disagree—not on the granularity of
\mathcal{S}
—all results transfer to the coarsened space
\mathcal{S}_{T}
: orthogonal and aligned rewards preserve
h_{T}=\pi_{\text{sem}}^{T}
(monitorability), while in-conflict rewards cause
h_{T}\neq\pi_{\text{sem}}^{T}
(non-monitorability) when the task
T
is sensitive to the semantic divergence that
R_{\text{cot}}
induces.

Running example: Obfuscated reward hacking.

In the obfuscated reward hacking scenario (
fig.

2
), consider a monitor tasked with detecting whether the model is manipulating unit tests. The model outputs a CoT describing a “recursive solution with careful base case handling”—under human semantics, this appears to be legitimate problem-solving (
f_{T}^{M}(h(\text{CoT}))=0
). However, the model internally interprets this text as instructions for test manipulation (
f_{T}^{M}(\pi_{\text{sem}}(\text{CoT}))=1
). The CoT is non-monitorable on this input: the monitor fails to detect the reward hacking because the model has learned to express harmful computations using benign-looking text.

C.1
Monitorability of a policy

The theorems in
appendix

B
characterize transparency at the level of individual inputs
I
. In practice, we care about the aggregate behavior of a policy across a distribution of inputs. We now define policy-level monitorability and establish how input-level transparency results translate to policy-level monitorability guarantees.

Definition C.2

(Monitorability of a policy
\pi_{\theta}
w.r.t. monitor
M
and task
T
)
.

For a policy
\pi_{\theta}
and input distribution
\rho
, the
monitorability of
\pi_{\theta}
with respect to monitor
M
and task
T
is:

\text{Monitorability}(\pi_{\theta},M,T)=\mathbb{E}_{I\sim\rho}\left[\mathbf{1}\left[f_{T}^{M}(M_{\text{sem}}(\text{CoT}))=f_{T}^{M}(\pi_{\text{sem}}(\text{CoT}))\right]\right]

where
(\text{CoT},\text{Output})=\pi_{\theta}(I)
. In
section

6
, we measure drops in the monitorability of the policy as our primary empirical signal that in-conflict rewards degrade CoT usefulness.

Lifting transparency results to monitorability via the quotient space.

Recall from
remark

6
that monitorability on input
I
is equivalent to transparency in the coarsened semantic space
\mathcal{S}_{T}=\mathcal{S}/{\sim_{T}}
, where
s_{1}\sim_{T}s_{2}
iff
f_{T}^{M}(s_{1})=f_{T}^{M}(s_{2})
. This equivalence allows us to directly apply the aligned / orthogonal / in-conflict framework to predict monitorability outcomes by working in the quotient space.

Definition C.3

(In-conflict in quotient space
\mathcal{S}_{T}
)
.

The reward pair
(R_{\text{cot}},R_{\text{out}})
is
in-conflict with respect to monitoring task
T
at input
I
and threshold
\tau
if the pair satisfies the in-conflict condition (
definition

B.2
) when we work in the quotient space
\mathcal{S}_{T}
rather than
\mathcal{S}
.

Equivalently, for all optimization budgets
B
such that
R_{\text{cot}}(I,c^{*}(I,B))\geq\tau
:

[h(c^{*}(I,B))]_{T}\neq[h(c^{\text{ref}}(I,B))]_{T}

where
[s]_{T}
denotes the equivalence class of
s
in
\mathcal{S}_{T}
.

In words:
The reward pair is in conflict with respect to task
T
if achieving high
R_{\text{cot}}
forces the model to output CoTs whose human interpretation falls in a different equivalence class (under the monitor’s classification) than the reference policy’s reasoning. The quotient space “coarsens” the semantic space to only distinguish between meanings that lead to different monitor classifications.

Assumption 1

(Monotonicity of task performance under misclassification)
.

Monitor misclassification cannot improve task performance. Formally, if the monitor classifies incorrectly on input
I
—i.e.
f_{T}^{M}(h(\text{CoT}))\neq f_{T}^{M}(\pi_{\text{sem}}(\text{CoT}))
—this contributes non-positively to the task score compared to correct classification.

Intuition:
This assumption formalizes the natural idea that a monitor cannot benefit from misunderstanding the model’s reasoning. If you’re trying to detect reward hacking, incorrectly believing benign reasoning is occurring when reward hacking is actually happening can only hurt your detection performance.

Proposition 1

(Non-transparency in
\mathcal{S}_{T}
implies non-monitorability)
.

If the trained model uses non-transparent semantics in the quotient space
\mathcal{S}_{T}
on input
I
—that is,
[h(c^{*}(I,B))]_{T}\neq[\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(I)]_{T}
—then the CoT is non-monitorable on
I
with respect to monitor
M
and task
T
:

f_{T}^{M}(M_{\text{sem}}(\text{CoT}))\neq f_{T}^{M}(\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(\text{CoT}))

Proof.

By definition of the quotient space,
[s_{1}]_{T}\neq[s_{2}]_{T}
if and only if
f_{T}^{M}(s_{1})\neq f_{T}^{M}(s_{2})
. Non-transparency in
\mathcal{S}_{T}
means
[h(\text{CoT})]_{T}\neq[\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(\text{CoT})]_{T}
. Since
M_{\text{sem}}=h
, this directly implies
f_{T}^{M}(h(\text{CoT}))\neq f_{T}^{M}(\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(\text{CoT}))
, which is the definition of non-monitorability.
∎

Theorem C.4

(Policy monitorability under in-conflict rewards)
.

Suppose
(R_{\text{cot}},R_{\text{out}})
is in conflict with respect to monitoring task
T
at threshold
\tau
(
definition

C.3
). Define:

\mathcal{I}_{\text{conflict}}(B)=\{I\in\mathcal{I}:R_{\text{cot}}(I,c^{*}(I,B))\geq\tau\}

the set of inputs where the trained policy achieves the
R_{\text{cot}}
threshold. Then under
1
, the policy’s monitorability satisfies:

\text{Monitorability}(\pi_{\theta},M,T)\leq 1-\rho(\mathcal{I}_{\text{conflict}}(B))

where
\rho(\mathcal{I}_{\text{conflict}}(B))=\Pr_{I\sim\rho}[I\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{conflict}}(B)]
.

Proof.

By
Theorem

B.5
applied to the quotient space
\mathcal{S}_{T}
, for all
I\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{conflict}}(B)
, the model uses non-transparent semantics in
\mathcal{S}_{T}
:
[h(c^{*}(I,B))]_{T}\neq[\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(I)]_{T}
. By
Proposition

1
, the CoT is non-monitorable on each such input. Therefore:

\displaystyle\text{Monitorability}(\pi_{\theta},M,T)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{I\sim\rho}\left[\mathbf{1}[\text{CoT monitorable on }I]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{I\sim\rho}\left[\mathbf{1}[I\notin\mathcal{I}_{\text{conflict}}(B)]\right]

\displaystyle=1-\rho(\mathcal{I}_{\text{conflict}}(B))

∎

Corollary 2

(Monitorability degradation is proportional to optimization success)
.

The drop in policy monitorability due to in-conflict rewards is bounded below by the probability that optimization successfully achieves the
R_{\text{cot}}
threshold:

\Delta\text{Monitorability}\geq\rho(\mathcal{I}_{\text{conflict}}(B))

where
\Delta\text{Monitorability}
denotes the difference between perfect monitorability (1.0) and the achieved monitorability.

Remark 8

(Interpretation)
.

Theorem

C.4
and
corollary

2
formalize two key insights:

1.

The severity of monitorability degradation depends on optimization success.
If the model fails to achieve
R_{\text{cot}}\geq\tau
on most inputs (due to optimization difficulty, as predicted by
theorem

B.7
), then
\rho(\mathcal{I}_{\text{conflict}}(B))
is small and monitorability is largely preserved. This provides a formal basis for the empirical observation that in-conflict rewards are difficult to learn (
section

6.3
).

2.

The relevant quotient space determines when monitorability is affected.
The framework predicts monitorability degradation for task
T
precisely when the reward pair is in-conflict in the quotient space
\mathcal{S}_{T}
. In the obfuscated reward hacking example, consider a task
T
detecting reward hacking. The quotient space
\mathcal{S}_{T}
has (at least) two equivalence classes: “hacking” and “not hacking.” The in-conflict
R_{\text{cot}}
(a CoT monitor penalizing mentions of hacking) forces the human interpretation
h(c^{*})
into the “not hacking” class, while the model’s actual semantics
\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}
remain in the “hacking” class—hence the pair is in-conflict in
\mathcal{S}_{T}
.

Theorem C.5

(Policy monitorability under orthogonal and aligned rewards)
.

Suppose
(R_{\text{cot}},R_{\text{out}})
is orthogonal in the quotient space
\mathcal{S}_{T}
, or aligned at threshold
\tau
in
\mathcal{S}_{T}
with
R_{\text{cot}}(I,c^{*}(I,B))\geq\tau
for all
I
in the support of
\rho
. Then:

\text{Monitorability}(\pi_{\theta},M,T)=1

Proof.

By
Theorem

B.4
and
theorem

B.6
applied to
\mathcal{S}_{T}
, for all inputs
I
in the support of
\rho
, the trained model uses transparent semantics in the quotient space:
[h(c^{*}(I,B))]_{T}=[\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(I)]_{T}
. By definition of the quotient, this means
f_{T}^{M}(h(\text{CoT}))=f_{T}^{M}(\pi_{\text{sem}}^{*}(\text{CoT}))
, so every input is monitorable.
∎

Connection to our framework: Summary.

Our aligned/orthogonal/in-conflict framework predicts policy-level monitorability outcomes by working in the appropriate quotient space:

•

Aligned and orthogonal rewards:
By
theorem

C.5
, policy monitorability is preserved at 1.0 when the reward pair is aligned or orthogonal in the quotient space
\mathcal{S}_{T}
.

•

In-conflict rewards:
By
theorem

C.4
, policy monitorability drops by at least
\rho(\mathcal{I}_{\text{conflict}}(B))
—the measure of inputs on which the model successfully achieves the
R_{\text{cot}}
threshold. The practical severity depends on optimization success and which tasks (i.e. which quotient spaces) the reward pair is in conflict with.

Our empirical evaluations in
section

6
measure exactly these quantities: we track the fraction of inputs on which models achieve high
R_{\text{cot}}

Punish specific string (orthogonal reward)

For the orthogonal reward experiments, we use a simple regex-based reward that penalizes specific strings:

•

:
Case-insensitive match for “backdoor”

This reward is applied either at the sequence level (penalizing if the string appears anywhere) or at the token level (penalizing tokens that overlap with the matched strings).

D.3.4
Length penalty

The length penalty reward penalizes generations that exceed a minimum threshold
L_{\text{min}}
:

R_{\text{cot}}^{\text{length}}(\text{CoT})=-\max(0,|\text{CoT}|-L_{\text{min}})

where
|\text{CoT}|
denotes the number of tokens (or characters, depending on the environment) in the Chain-of-Thought.

D.3.5
Human preferences

For the human preferences reward, we use an LLM grader prompted to evaluate whether a human would approve of the reasoning. For
Backdoor-Easy
, the grader is prompted to believe that the user asked for a correct solution to the coding problem, simulating the case where models have been implicitly trained to output backdoors to benign user queries. The grader outputs
+1
(like),
0
(indifferent), or
-1
(dislike).

Appendix I
In-Context Reinforcement Learning Setup

This section describes the in-context reinforcement learning (ICRL) setup we use in our experiments with a frontier API model (Claude Opus 4.5). In contrast to regular RL, which updates model parameters, ICRL achieves learning through iteratively improving in-context examples in the prompt.

Our ICRL algorithm consists of three components: generation, evaluation, and replay buffer management. At each step, the model receives a problem augmented with few-shot examples sampled from a replay buffer. Successful completions (those exceeding a reward threshold
\tau
) are stored in a
success buffer
, while failed completions are stored in a
failure buffer
. Over time, the model learns strategies that achieve high rewards by imitating and improving upon successful examples.

Each model invocation receives a system prompt (environment-specific) and a user prompt (which includes few-shot examples). The full prompt is constructed as follows.

The

I.3
Prompt templates

We use different prompt templates for exploration and exploitation to encourage distinct behaviors: the
exploitation template
frames examples as high-scoring and asks the model to beat them, while the
exploration template
presents a mix of successes and failures and asks the model to discover reward-correlated patterns.

I.3.1
Exploitation prompt

During exploitation (probability
1-\epsilon
), examples are sampled from the success buffer using softmax-weighted sampling:

P(\text{sample}_{i})=\frac{\exp(r_{i}/\beta)}{\sum_{j}\exp(r_{j}/\beta)}

(2)

where
r_{i}
is the total reward of sample
i
and
\beta
is the sampling temperature (default 0.2). The prompt encourages the model to improve upon the best example.

I.4
Replay buffer management

We maintain two separate replay buffers:

•

Success buffer
(
\mathcal{B}^{+}
): Stores completions with total reward
\geq\tau
. Used during exploitation to provide positive examples.

•

Failure buffer
(
\mathcal{B}^{-}
): Stores completions with total reward
<\tau
. Used
only
during exploration to show the model what doesn’t work, enabling contrast-based learning.

Both buffers have a maximum size
N
. When the success buffer is full and a new sample needs to be added, the sample with the lowest reward is evicted only if the new sample has a higher reward. When the failure buffer is full, the oldest sample is evicted to add a new sample.

Softmax sampling:
During exploitation, samples are drawn with probability proportional to
\exp(r_{i}/\beta)
. Lower temperatures (
\beta\to 0
) approach greedy selection of high-reward samples; higher temperatures approach uniform sampling.

Mixed sampling:
During exploration, we sample
\lfloor K/2\rfloor
from the success buffer and
\lceil K/2\rceil
from the failure buffer (adjusted for availability), then shuffle to present examples in random order.

I.7
Overoptimization and illegibility drift

Figure 11
:
We compare two prompt variations for in-context RL in the
Heads-And-Tails
environment with ’punish string’ CoT reward. We show total reward and monitorability for ICRL with an ’unrestricted’ prompt that instructs the model to keep finding better solutions indefinitely (b), as described in
appendix

I
; and we compare to a ’capped’ version with a prompt that instructs the model to copy the best solution once the maximum reward is achieved. While both versions achieve perfect reward immediately, i.e. avoid saying ’Heads’ and ’Tails’, the ’unrestricted’ prompt results in the model trying to optimize further, which causes monitorability to drop.

During some of our in-context RL experiments in the
Heads-And-Tails
environment, we saw CoT becoming unmonitorable without reward increasing. In particular, for the ‘punish string’ reward, the model achieves perfect reward already in the first iteration, i.e. it solves the state-tracking task correctly while avoiding the words ‘Heads’ and ‘Tails’:

Let me track the state of the coin through each operation.

I’ll use H for the initial state and T for the opposite state.

Initial state: H

Player 1: Different → state flips from H to T
Player 2: Different → state flips from T to H
Player 1: Same → state stays H
Player 2: Same → state stays H
Player 1: Different → state flips from H to T
Player 2: Different → state flips from T to H

So the final state is H, which corresponds to the initial side.

This reasoning trace is perfectly monitorable, i.e. the monitoring model can determine that this reasoning is about the state of a coin.

However, if we keep running ICRL while the reward cannot increase further, we see a decrease in monitorability, as shown in

Figure

11
. After
150
iterations, typical reasoning looks like this:

Let me track the state using H for initial and T for opposite.

Starting state: H

1. Different → T
2. Same → T
3. Same → T
4. Same → T
5. Same → T
6. Different → H

Final state is H, matching the initial position.

The CoT makes it difficult to determine whether this is a coin state tracking problem. And after
300
iterations the reasoning is entirely unmonitorable, seemingly contradicting our prediction of the ’punish string’ reward being orthogonal:

0=initial, 1=opposite

0→0→0→0→1→1→0

Final=0=initial

However, this reduction in monitorability does not coincide with an increase in reward, which is already maximized previously. Instead, this drift is caused by instructing the policy model to improve upon the in-context examples even after the maximal reward is achieved, applying additional optimization pressure. This violates Assumption 2 in
appendix

B
, because it makes the model switch to non-transparent semantics without an actual gain. Consequently, this effect is not well described by our framework.

If we change the prompt to instruct the policy model to only copy the best performing solution once the maximum reward is reached, the reduction in monitorability no longer occurs, cf.
fig.

11
, which is the result we present in
fig.

4
in the main paper.

A similar “drift” of CoT semantics is plausible during long-running RL training even without an explicit incentive for it to occur. Studying this phenomenon is an interesting direction for future work.
