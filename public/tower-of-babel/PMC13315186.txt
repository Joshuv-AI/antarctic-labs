Root Canal Location Accuracy: Dynamic Navigation Verses Freehand in Extracted Human Molars – A Randomized In Vitro Study
Authors: Fei Chen, Tianxiao Han, Haili Guo, Gang Wang, Lele Qiu, Jianhui Tang, Jianshe Li, Liping Dong, Yang Huang, Lin Wang, Qiang Luo, Huixia He, Hongbo Li, Peng Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo compare the accuracy, instrumentation time, and success rate of three dynamic navigation systems (DNSs)—infrared-active (IA), infrared-passive (IP), and visible light–passive (VP)—with freehand (FH) operation in locating root canals of maxillary and mandibular molars, providing a basis for technique selection in complex endodontic treatments.MethodsNinety-six extracted human mandibular molars were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 24/group): IA-DNS, IP-DNS, VP-DNS, and FH (control). Specimens were embedded in plaster and scanned with CBCT (0.3 mm slice thickness). In DNS groups, fiducial markers enabled real-time tracking of the bur position. The FH group used a microscope with CBCT reference. Recorded parameters included: angular deviation (°), three-dimensional deviations at entry and target points (mm), instrumentation time (s), tooth structure loss (mm³), and success rate of canal location. Data were analysed using ANOVA and chi-square tests (α = 0.05).ResultsDNS demonstrated significantly higher accuracy than FH. Angular deviations in DNS groups (IA: 0.64° ± 0.34°, IP: 0.59° ± 0.32°, VP: 0.63° ± 0.24°) were 94.3% to 94.7% lower than FH (11.03° ± 4.70°) (P < .001). Three-dimensional deviations at entry (0.57-0.70 mm) and target (0.59-0.80 mm) were significantly smaller than FH (1.64 mm and 1.26 mm, respectively; P < .001). Tooth structure loss in DNS groups (9.79-11.03 mm³) was reduced by 26.8% to 34.6% compared to FH (14.98 mm³; P < .001). No significant difference in operative time was observed among groups (176-183 s, P > .05). Success rate was significantly higher in DNS groups (99.54%, 215/216) than FH (93.06%, 67/72; P = .004). No significant differences were observed among the three DNS types for any parameter.ConclusionDNS significantly enhanced accuracy of root canal location in molars, reducing angular deviation by >94%, minimizing tooth structure loss by >26%, and increasing success rate to 99.5%. Performance was consistent across different optical principles and signal mechanisms, supporting DNS as a viable option for precise management of calcified canals and other nonsurgical endodontic procedures.Key words: Dynamic navigation system, Access cavity preparation, Root canal location, Minimally invasive endodontics, Guided endodontics

Introduction

Access cavity preparation is a fundamental step in root canal treatment. It is crucial for the success of root canal treatment.1 Accurate root canal localization enables direct access for instruments to the root canal system, facilitating disinfection and complete debridement and minimizing excessive tooth structure loss.2 However, in complex cases such as calcified root canals, obliterated or additional canals, pulp stones, retreatment, or fibre post removal, even experienced clinicians face challenges when relying solely on dental operating microscopes, ultrasonic tips, and long-shank burs for freehand (FH) procedures.3 These manual techniques are time-consuming and technical, and they carry risks of excessive tissue loss, perforation, or missed canals, which could contribute to endodontic failure, reduced fracture resistance, and compromised tooth survival.4,5 Advancements in radiographic diagnostics, particularly cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), have enhanced the understanding of root canal system anatomy and increased treatment success rates.6

The term ‘guided endodontics’ has emerged with the integration of computer-assisted technology into endodontics, encompassing two approaches: static guidance, which uses prefabricated templates, and dynamic navigation, which relies on intraoral markers and camera tracking systems.7 Static guidance employs prefabricated templates derived from CBCT data to direct rotary instruments, thereby enabling precise, conservative access.8, 9, 10 However, this technique has several limitations. It is applicable only to root segments with straight or mild curvature, requires comprehensive preoperative planning, and cannot accommodate irrigation cooling protocols. Furthermore, the fabrication of these templates is both time- and cost-intensive, and the substantial spatial constraints in the posterior region introduce additional technical difficulties that further impede their clinical application.7 The rigid nature of these templates prevents intraoperative adjustments, leading to clinical dilemmas when procedural complications occur.11

In contrast, dynamic navigation systems (DNSs) employ virtual surgical planning on patient-specific CBCT data. By synchronizing optical tracking with instrument motion detection and anatomical marker registration,12 DNSs facilitate real-time three-dimensional (3D) visualization13,14 in conventional endodontic interventions and endodontic microsurgery.15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20 Recent in vitro studies using natural tooth models and 3D-printed replicas (teeth or jaws) have demonstrated that DNSs perform comparably to static navigation and outperform manual techniques in locating calcified root canals while preserving tooth structure during access preparation, and the system also enhances procedural efficiency and reduces the risk of catastrophic iatrogenic errors, showing particular advantages in complex anatomical scenarios.5,21

Current commercial DNSs in dental practice can be categorized into visible-light and invisible-light (infrared) systems based on the light source, as well as into active or passive systems based on the signal transmission mechanism.22,23 Visible-light navigation employs image recognition technology to track hardware components, primarily through checkerboard patterns, feature points, or quick response codes. However, it is prone to interference from strong ambient light sources, such as room lighting or surgical lamps. Invisible-light navigation typically employs sensitive infrared camera sensors to capture object images directly, providing advantages such as high image clarity, a broad spectral range, millimetre-level precision, and strong resistance to interference, although at higher costs.24

In active navigation systems, infrared light sources are integrated into the handpiece and intraoral registration devices. The emitted infrared signals are directly received and processed by the navigation unit, exhibiting minimal environmental interference and wide single-angle coverage. In passive navigation systems, light is emitted from the navigation unit and reflected off the handpiece and intraoral registration device before being captured by sensors. This configuration is susceptible to ambient light interference and reflections from surfaces, with limited viewing angles.23,25

The accuracy of dental implant placement using active and passive DNSs has been evaluated in previous studies, with reports demonstrating satisfactory in vitro accuracy despite statistically significant differences in coronal, apical, and axial deviations between the two DNS types.22, 23, 24 However, no studies have systematically compared the performance of active versus passive and infrared versus visible DNSs in guided endodontics, particularly for posterior teeth with high technical complexity.

Therefore, the present in vitro study investigated three DNSs with distinct optical source configurations and signal transmission mechanisms (technical specifications are detailed in Table 1). The study aimed to evaluate their accuracy and efficiency in endodontic access cavity preparation for molar teeth compared with the FH technique. The null hypothesis posits no significant differences in accuracy or procedural time between active and passive DNS, and both systems are expected to outperform the FH technique.

Materials and methods

Study design

This laboratory study was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Laboratory Studies in Endodontology (PRILE) 2021 guidelines.26,27 The PRILE 2021 flowchart is shown in Figure 1. Additionally, the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (10th version, October 2013; www.wma.net, accessed on November 1, 2023) and the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO). The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Chinese PLA General Hospital (No.: S2023-138-01).

The PRILE 2021 flowchart. CBCT, cone-beam computed tomography; DNS, dynamic navigation system; FH, freehand; IA-DNS, infrared–active dynamic navigation system; IP-DNS, infrared–passive dynamic navigation system; VP-DNS, visible light–passive dynamic navigation system.

This randomized block-based trial aimed to compare the accuracy and efficiency of access cavity preparation using three different dental navigation systems (DNSs). Figure 2 shows the experimental procedure. The DNSs included infrared-active (IA; Yizhime, Dcarer, Suzhou, China; Figure 3A), infrared-passive (IP; DRS-NA, YakeBot, Beijing, China; Figure 4A), and visible-passive (VP; X-Guide NXT, X-Nav, Pennsylvania, USA; Figure 5A). Each navigation system comprised a computer, stereoscopic tracker, registration device, tracking marker, and planning software. The tracker in the active navigation system was equipped with a light-emitting diode, whereas that in the passive system was equipped with a reflective marker. All procedures were performed by a single operator with 10 years of experience in endodontics, who had received training in the respective DNS. Before the experiment, the operator practiced access cavity preparation on the models 20 times using each of the three DNSs to ensure proficiency and consistency.

Sample size determination

Given the decisive role of angular deviation in defining accuracy as reported in previous literature,28 we adopted it as the primary indicator of this study. Based on this, the sample size for each group was determined using G*Power software (v3.1.9.2; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) with reference to data from previous studies on various types of navigation in osteotomy and root-end resection.29, 30, 31 The reported angular deviations were 4.49° ± 1.41° for the IP-DNS (DRS-NA), 3.47° ± 1.80° for the IA-DNS (Yizhime), and 1.90° ± 1.20° for the VP-DNS (X-Guide). Based on these values, the calculated Cohen’s d effect sizes were −0.58, 0.92, and 2.04, respectively. With a significance level (α) of 0.05 and an actual statistical power of 0.95, the minimum required sample sizes were determined to be 21, 9, and 3 per group, respectively. Therefore, the highest calculated requirement (21 per group) was adopted as the minimum sample size for this study. To enhance robustness, the sample size was increased to 24 per group.

Sample selection, randomization and model preparation

A total of 96 human permanent molars [12 teeth from each of the tooth positions (FDI World Dental Federation notation): 16, 17, 26, 27, 36, 37, 46, and 47] were extracted due to untreatable severe periodontal disease from the Stomatology Department at the First Medical Centre of the Chinese PLA General Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients whose extracted teeth were included in this study. Teeth with previous endodontic treatment, severe root or canal curvature, fractures, cracks, immature roots, and calcified canals were excluded from this study based on radiological examination.32 After the tissue and calculus from all sample surfaces were removed, they were randomly mounted onto 24 plaster models, comprising 12 maxillary and 12 mandibular models, based on their anatomic molar positions.28 They were randomly divided into four groups, each containing three maxillary and three mandibular models, with group allocation concealed using a predetermined computer-generated randomization list. The number of tables was prepared separately for three experimental groups, namely, Group A (IA-DNS), Group B (IP-DNS), and Group C (VP-DNS), and a control group (Group D, FH technique).

Preoperative CBCT scan and access planning

For all models, preoperative CBCT was performed with a voxel size of 0.3 mm (110 kV; 5.0 mA; field of view, 12 × 8 cm; NewTom VGi, Verona, Italy), and the data were saved in Digital Imaging and Communication in Medicine (DICOM) format. For the experimental groups, intraoral registration marker plates containing radiopaque fiducial marker points were attached before scanning to enable integration of the preoperative CBCT, plaster models, and surgical instruments into a unified spatial coordinate system via point set registration. For the two infrared DNSs, the registration marker plates were fixed in the bilateral molar regions using silicone rubber impression material (Type 0, HUGE, China) because they were separate from the tracking marker plates. In contrast, for the visible DNS, the registration marker plates were secured in the anterior teeth region using a temporization material (Protemp 4, 3M ESPE, Seefeld, Germany) as they were integrated with the tracking markers.

For access planning, the DICOM files for each experimental group were uploaded to the corresponding DNS planning software. The respective canal was superimposed with a virtual endodontic bur (Dia-Burs, Ref. TR-12, MANI, Japan; diameter, 0.5-1.0 mm; working length, 10.0 mm), positioned to terminate slightly coronal to the root canal orifice, and the drilling entry point, angle, pathway, and depth of drills were also virtually planned (Figures 3B-5B). The CBCT data of the FH control group were uploaded to the IA-DNS planning software solely for digital access planning (Figure 6A). The same bur was used in the DNS and FH groups to ensure comparability.

Calibration and registration of DNS

Before the navigation protocol for the experimental groups, all plaster models were mounted on the same phantom head to simulate both clinical scenarios and ergonomic drilling positions. After affixing the reference plates with tracking markers from each DNS onto the plaster models and configuring the high-speed handpiece (SMARTtorque S619C, KaVo, Biberach, Germany) to emit or reflect light signals, the calibration and registration steps were performed according to the instructions provided by the respective DNS manufacturers (Figures 3C-F, 4C-E, and 5C-F). Calibration enabled real-time tracking of the handpiece and plaster model via the reference plate during drilling, requiring an unobstructed line of sight between the handpiece, reference plate, and optical tracker. The navigation system captured and converted their spatial coordinates to establish spatial registration. Active DNS components emitted light signals to the tracker, while passive DNS components reflected the tracker’s signals. Registration repositioned the devices to operative regions using handpiece-selected marker points on the devices; the systems then matched these points to preoperative CBCT radiological markers. Ultimately, this process unified the spatial coordinate systems of the preoperative CBCT, plaster model, and handpiece. The integration of preoperative CBCT data with real-time visualization via spatial alignment techniques enabled instrument monitoring and tracking during surgical procedures, allowing operators to precisely execute access protocols in accordance with preoperative plans.

Access cavity preparation

For the DNS groups, after completing the calibration and registration, the handpiece equipped with a diamond bur (TR-12, MANI, Japan) was aligned with the anatomical landmarks (cusps or fossae) of the target tooth, and the real-time position of the virtual endodontic bur on the DNS display was monitored to verify accuracy. First, a small dimple was drilled into the enamel surface at the location corresponding to the virtual drilling entry point to secure the actual drilling entry point. The bur orientation was adjusted until the display indicated that the preplanned drilling angle had been reached, following which drilling proceeded to the predetermined depth (Figures 3G, 4F, and 5G). Throughout this process, the operator continuously monitored the real-time display of the drilling distance deviation, angular deviation, and depth data on the screen (Figures 3I-K, 3M-O, 3Q-S, 4H-J, 4L-N, 4P-R, 5I-K, 5M-O, and 5Q-S). Precise and stable hand control of the instrument was maintained to ensure the actual access path matched the planned path. For the FH group, the CBCT images marked with the preoperatively planned access were displayed chairside for operator reference, and all drilling procedures were conducted under magnification using a dental operating microscope (OMS3200, Zumax, Suzhou, China; Figure 6B). Drilling was stopped upon penetration of the pulp chamber roof, as indicated by a sense of breakthrough, and the canal location was assessed using an ISO stainless steel hand file (size #10, K-reamers, MANI, Japan).21 Successful root canal location was defined as the ability to passively insert the K-file into the root canal space without resistance, reaching or approximating the working length previously measured on CBCT images. Instrumentation time (s) was recorded for each group from enamel removal to the point of canal negotiation by a masked technician who did not participate in the drilling planning or procedures.12

Postoperative CBCT scan and data integration

Following cavity preparation, a postoperative CBCT scan was conducted using the same scanning settings as the preoperative scan (Figures 3I,P,T, 4K,O,S, 5I,P,T, 6D-F). The postoperative DICOM files, which reflected the actual access cavity, along with the preoperative DICOM datasets that included the planned trajectory for each DNS pathway, were imported into Mimics Medical software version 21.0 (Materialise). Model segmentation on this platform was performed using threshold-based techniques. Subsequently, both models were converted to STL format and imported into 3-Matic Medical software 15.0 (Materialise), where alignment between the postoperative and preoperative models was achieved using a three-point registration protocol. To eliminate measurement bias, all accuracy measurements were conducted by an independent assessor who was blinded to group allocation and had not been involved in the preoperative planning or the surgical procedures. Both pre and postoperative models were duplicated, and the postoperative model was subtracted from the preoperative model to obtain all access cavities. The lines on the central axes of the planned and prepared access cavities were automatically fitted by the software; deviations were defined as the differences between these two lines and were quantified as follows11,28: coronal global deviation (mm), apical global deviation (mm), and angular deviation (°) (Figure 7A,B). Additionally, the volumes of these cavities were quantified using the software and designated as substance loss volumes (mm3)33 (Figure 7C). The frequency of canal location failure, defined as the inability to identify canals at the planned drilling depth or the occurrence of perforation, and iatrogenic errors, including perforations and lateral wall damage (Figure 7D-F), were recorded.5

Statistical analysis

The IBM SPSS Statistics software package (Windows version 26.0, released 2019) was used for all statistical analyses. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD), whereas categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Normality of the data was evaluated using diagnostic plots and formal tests. Intergroup comparisons of continuous variables were performed using a two-way analysis of variance for parametric data and the Kruskal–Wallis test for nonparametric data. Associations involving categorical variables were examined using Pearson’s chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test.

Results

3D deviations

Angular deviation, the primary indicator, and the coronal and apical global deviations were evaluated. DNSs demonstrated significantly higher accuracy than FH. Angular deviations in DNS groups (IA: 0.64° ± 0.34°, IP: 0.59° ± 0.32°, VP: 0.63° ± 0.24°) were 94.3% to 94.7% lower than FH (11.03° ± 4.70°) (P < .001). Three-dimensional deviations at entry (0.57-0.70 mm) and target (0.59-0.80 mm) in the DNS groups were also significantly smaller than those in the FH group (1.64 mm and 1.26 mm, respectively; P < .001). The three DNS groups exhibited comparable precision across all these parameters, with no significant differences. In contrast, the FH technique showed significantly higher mean values and variability (all P < .001). A quadrant-based analysis (Table 3) showed that the angular and global deviations of the DNS approach remained consistently low across all four quadrants, with only a minor but significant difference detected in apical global deviation between quadrants 2 (highest, 0.81 ± 0.36 mm) and 4 (lowest, 0.63 ± 0.38 mm) (P < .05). Conversely, although the performance of the FH technique varied across quadrants, its deviations in each quadrant were consistently and significantly greater than those of the corresponding quadrant in the DNS cohort.

Tooth structure loss

Tooth structure loss in DNS groups (9.79-11.03 mm³) was reduced by 26.8% to 34.6% compared to FH (14.98 mm³; P < .001). The three DNS groups showed no significant differences among themselves, whereas the FH technique resulted in a significantly higher volume of unintended substance removal.

Instrumentation time

The assessment of instrumentation time showed no statistically significant difference between any of the DNS groups and the FH group (Table 2). The mean instrumentation times for all groups were closely clustered between 176.13 ± 70.34 s and 182.76 ± 63.43 s, with intergroup comparisons showing nonsignificant differences. This finding suggests that integrating navigational guidance did not prolong procedural duration. However, a quadrant-based subanalysis within the DNS cohort revealed significant localized differences (Table 3). Specifically, instrumentation time in quadrant 2 (205.33 ± 70.70 s) was significantly longer than that in quadrants 1 (177.52 ± 72.48 s, P = .025), 3 (178.43 ± 55.07 s, P = .030), and 4 (154.67 ± 55.97 s, P < .001). Although a similar numerical trend was observed in the FH group, with the longest time in quadrant 1 (202.28 ± 59.48 s) and the shortest in quadrant 4 (164.61 ± 74.18 s), these differences were not statistically significant.

Success rate of canal location

Discussion

Previous studies on the accuracy and efficiency of DNSs in guided endodontic treatment have demonstrated their advantages in reducing operative time, risks, and tooth substance loss.5,11,12,21,34 However, these studies only compared differences among various methods, such as DNSs, static guides, and FH approaches, without analysing variations between DNSs with different light sources and signal transmission types. Therefore, in this study, we compared three DNSs, each with different light sources and signal transmission types, with FH operation in terms of accuracy and efficiency in locating molar root canals with complex anatomy. The results were consistent with previous findings. All DNSs exhibited significantly smaller coronal and apical global deviations, angular deviation, and tooth substance loss than FH operation, without compromising operational efficiency. Additionally, the DNSs achieved a significantly higher success rate. Moreover, no significant differences in accuracy or operational efficiency were observed among the different DNS types, suggesting that the null hypothesis in the present study may be accepted.

The findings of a series of three in vitro studies and a retrospective clinical study comparing IA-DNS and IP-DNS for dental implant surgery indicate that IA-DNS has superior accuracy. This conclusion remains valid despite both methods demonstrating high in vitro implant accuracy and performing within the clinically acceptable range in most cases.22, 23, 24,35 While these studies suggest that the enhanced precision might be due to the real-time feedback and active optical monitoring capabilities of IA-DNS,24,35 they also acknowledge a significant limitation: all assessed DNSs came from a single manufacturer in a single country.23,24,35 Consequently, we believe their findings may not fully represent the performance of devices sourced from various manufacturers across different countries, particularly when comparing DNSs from a manufacturer whose primary expertise lies in IA-DNS. Many DNS devices currently in use operate on the principle of stereoscopic vision positioning. They utilize a single light source to emit or reflect light signals of a specific wavelength, which are captured by binocular stereo cameras equipped with matched wavelength filters; this setup enables the acquisition and analysis of the 3D coordinates and spatial relationships of the surgical area and instruments, while real-time tracking is achieved through continuous data processing.36,37 Passive DNSs rely on capturing light signals through a reflective path, introducing potential risks of occlusion and reduced stability of the reflective source compared with active DNSs. This sensitivity to external conditions rather than the device itself determining outcomes has likewise been observed in other technology driven ex vivo procedures.38 However, given their shared working principles, as supported by previous findings39 and the results of the present study, the deviation between active and passive DNSs may not be significant once these risks are mitigated. Furthermore, we found no significant differences in global distance and angular deviations when comparing passive DNSs with different wavelength light sources (eg, infrared or visible blue-violet light). This is due to their shared optical positioning principle and the advancement of current DNS devices beyond traditional multicolour marker imaging40; they now use a single specific wavelength light source and cameras equipped with matching wavelength filters, while feature points or codes on registration devices attached to the patient and instruments reflect only that single wavelength, thereby effectively reducing interference from ambient light and facilitating passive DNSs.24 We believe that the various DNS types currently available from different manufacturers possess satisfactory mechanical accuracy, reflecting the intrinsic accuracy of the tracking system itself.41 In contrast, the overall application accuracy depends on several practical factors, including data acquisition methods,42 optical reference systems,43 registration methods,44 operational experience,45,46 surgical areas22,35 and tooth type.5

Our study focused on the overall application accuracy, instrumentation time, and success rates of different DNSs, as well as these parameters across different quadrants; therefore, we standardized data acquisition methods, optical reference systems, registration techniques, and operational experience to minimize their potential impact on the research outcomes. Although multimodal data fusion may enhance the accuracy of preoperative baseline data and intraoperative precision,11,29,42 we relied solely on CBCT data in this study because plaster models are highly stable and reproducible, aligning with numerous clinical and in vitro studies.5,17,23,24,35 Additionally, we employed the same field of view and voxel size for CBCT scanning across all groups to eliminate potential confounding factors.31,47 As reported previously,12,31,46 all marker trays were prefabricated and fixed to the plaster models using plastic impression material, despite some studies indicating that 3D-printed customized marker trays showed smaller distance and angular deviations.29,43,48 Furthermore, our study findings consistently employed the U-shaped tube registration method, which was significantly (P < .05) more accurate than the tooth cusp registration method in terms of mean platform deviation, end deviation, angular deviation, and resection angle,44,47 to ensure methodological consistency and result precision. Finally, to strengthen the evidence and eliminate extraneous variables, all DNS procedures in the current study were performed by the same operator with 10 years of endodontic experience who had received specific training for each respective DNS, thereby eliminating potential impacts of inexperience on the results and overcoming significant learning curves to achieve optimal accuracy.22,45,46

Tooth type is the second most influential factor on dynamic navigation accuracy, after the operating method.5 Anterior teeth consistently show less deviation than molars, a trend attributed to their greater vertical space, superior maneuverability, and the alignment of the drilling path with the long axis and occlusal force direction, which resembles the straightforward nature of implant placement.11,34 Additionally, these favourable conditions improve the accuracy and efficiency of DNS-assisted osteotomy and root-end resection in the anterior teeth region.29,48 Although molars present considerable challenges due to varied canal orientations and sloping trajectories relative to the occlusal plane, they also carry an increased risk of slippage. To address this, we implemented an implant-inspired technique that involved guided initial puncturing followed by angular adjustment to minimize entry deviation.12 Ergonomic limitations also affect right-handed operators seated on the patient’s right, particularly when accessing buccal canals of molars in quadrant 2 or the mesiolingual and distal canals of molars in quadrant 3, where contralateral teeth obstruct handpiece positioning. This necessitates repositioning to the 8 to 9 o’clock position with the handpiece directed toward the patient’s left, which is a nonstandard posture whose accuracy relies heavily on the operator’s learning curve. Furthermore, overlapping planned paths within a single molar require continuous adjustments based on tactile and navigation feedback to avoid deviating toward paths of least resistance. Finally, the planar handpiece-mounted devices used in IA-DNS and IP-DNS introduce an eccentric moment, unlike the encircling design of VP-DNS, requiring operators to maintain drilling orientation while compensating for torque to maintain signal transmission, thereby increasing arm fatigue and potentially compromising accuracy.

Because this was an in vitro study, the following limitations must be considered when interpreting its clinical relevance. First, although all procedures were performed on a phantom head model fixed onto a dental chair to replicate the clinical operator–patient posture, real clinical conditions, such as limited mouth opening, varying degrees of tooth destruction (with loss of anatomical landmarks), tooth mobility, and physiological tremors, could introduce variability in operational precision and efficiency.49 Therefore, future validation and comparative analyses must be conducted in rigorous clinical studies.29 Second, the use of extracted molars instead of 3D-printed replica teeth might affect sample standardization.50 However, the monochromatic appearance and uniform hardness of synthetic models reduce tactile and visual feedback during the manual identification of the canal orifice,51 potentially placing the FH technique at a disadvantage.5,34 To better simulate the clinical setting while controlling for variables, we used extracted molars and excluded those with atypical root canal anatomy or number, as confirmed by preoperative CBCT screening, ensuring consistent baseline difficulty across groups. This approach aligns with the consensus that standardized paired teeth reduce confounders in endodontics.52 Third, a single experienced operator performed all procedures. This design is supported by evidence that the operating method, rather than operator variability, dominates accuracy when using dynamic navigation,5,12 and the 20 practice trials per system met the proficiency threshold reported in the literature.46 Nevertheless, operator experience can still influence outcomes,53 and single-operator learning curves may not represent those of clinicians at different skill levels.46 Future studies should involve multiple operators with varying experience to enhance the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, DNSs significantly enhanced the accuracy of root canal location in molars, with angular deviation – the primary indicator – reduced by >94%. DNSs also preserved tooth structure, reducing tissue loss by >26%; and increased the clinical success rate to 99.5%. The performance of DNSs was consistent regardless of the optical principle or signal mechanism, establishing it as a reliable technology for the precise management of calcified canals, fibre post removal, and other nonsurgical endodontic procedures. As a technique-sensitive system, its effectiveness depends on the hand–eye coordination and manual dexterity of the operator, making structured training and clinical experience essential for successful implementation. Future studies should validate these findings in clinical settings and further clarify the indications, learning curves, and comparative advantages of DNSs relative to other guided endodontic technologies.
