The Role of Coicis Semen in Staphylococcus aureus -Induced Osteomyelitis: Bioinformatics Integrated with Experimental Validation
Authors: Juncheng Li, Wenkang You, Ruan Chen, Xinyu Fang, Wenming Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteomyelitis, often caused by Staphylococcus aureus, presents treatment challenges owing to antibiotic resistance. This study aimed to explore the therapeutic potential of Coicis Semen against osteomyelitis through bioinformatics and experimental validation.MethodsAn integrated analysis of GEO datasets (GSE16129 and GSE6269) was performed to identify osteomyelitis-related differentially expressed genes. Active compounds of Coicis Semen were screened using HERB and SwissADME, and target genes were predicted using SwissTargetPrediction. Protein-protein interaction networks, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and in vitro and in vivo experiments were conducted to validate the mechanisms.ResultsEleven bioactive compounds and three key genes, Integrin subunit beta 3 (ITGB3), Myeloperoxidase (MPO), and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 8 (CXCL8) were identified. Molecular docking confirmed the stable binding of 6-Methoxy-2-benzoxazolinone (MBOA)-MPO and Nonanoic acid-CXCL8. Both compounds inhibited Staphylococcus aureus growth in vitro and reduced bone destruction in a mouse model.ConclusionCoicis Semen may alleviate osteomyelitis through multi-target immunomodulatory and antibacterial mechanisms, offering potential adjunctive treatment options.Keywords: Coicis Semen, osteomyelitis, bioinformatics

Introduction

Osteomyelitis is an inflammatory condition of bone tissue caused by bacterial or fungal infections, most commonly by Staphylococcus aureus1 (S. aureus). Osteomyelitis can occur due to hematogenous spread, traumatic injury, or extension from a contiguous site of infection. Acute osteomyelitis presents with localized redness, swelling, warmth, pain, and fever, whereas chronic cases may involve sinus tract and sequestrum formation. Diagnosis relies on imaging techniques (X-rays and magnetic resonance imaging) and microbiological studies.2 Treatment typically involves prolonged antibiotic therapy (4–6 weeks) combined with surgical debridement; in severe cases, resection of necrotic bone may be necessary.3 Owing to the increasing frequency of antibiotic use and reduced susceptibility in some patients, the management of osteomyelitis faces new challenges.4 Thus, novel therapeutic agents to complement existing treatments are urgently needed.

“Food and medicine homology” (FMH) refers to the concept that certain natural substances can serve as both dietary foods and medicinal agents and can regulate physiological functions, prevent diseases, or assist in the treatment of disorders.5 This idea originates from traditional Chinese medicine, which posits that since both food and medicine are derived from nature, their rational combination can contribute to health preservation, disease prevention, and therapy.6 Coicis Semen, the dried ripe kernel of Coix lacryma-jobi L. (family Poaceae), commonly known as Job’s tears, adlay, or coix seed, is not only a traditional Chinese medicinal ingredient but also a common FMH material.7 Recent studies have reported that Coicis Semen is rich in coixenolide, polysaccharides, unsaturated fatty acids, and various amino acids and exhibits pharmacological activities, such as antitumor,8 immunomodulatory,9 hypoglycemic,10 and antioxidant effects.11

In this study, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in patients between the osteomyelitis and control (healthy) groups were identified using two GEO datasets (GSE16129 and GSE6269). The active components of Coicis Semen and their corresponding target genes were obtained by integrating the HERB (provides comprehensive information on herbal medicine compounds) and SwissADME (predicts the pharmacokinetic properties of compounds) databases. The GeneCards and OMIM databases offer extensive information on human genes and genetic diseases. The ChEMBL, STITCH, and SwissTargetPrediction databases were used to predict the protein targets of the screened bioactive compounds. Integrating data from these databases allowed us to identify bioactive compounds from Coicis Semen with good drug-like properties along with potential target genes related to osteomyelitis, which is crucial for network pharmacology analysis. The intersection of these gene sets yielded three active components and their corresponding target genes: Coixan A (for ITGB3), 6-Methoxy-2-benzoxazolinone (MBOA) (for MPO), and Nonanoic acid (for CXCL8). The aim of this work was to use a network pharmacology approach combined with experimental validation to explore how Coicis Semen might be used to treat osteomyelitis. We first used databases to identify potential active compounds and their targets. We then identified key genes and pathways involved. Finally, we tested the antibacterial and bone-protective effects of key compounds both in vitro and in a mouse model of osteomyelitis.

Materials and Methods

Collection and Annotation of GEO Microarray Data

In this study, human peripheral blood transcriptome datasets related to S. aureus infection, including two independent cohorts (GSE16129 and GSE6269) from Homo sapiens and were profiled using the Affymetrix Human Genome U133A Array (GPL96). A total of 55 samples were obtained after data curation, comprising 39 cases of osteomyelitis due to S. aureus infection and 16 healthy controls. According to item 1 of Article 32 of the Measures for Ethical Review of Life Science and Medical Research Involving Human Subjects dated February 18, 2023, China, lawfully obtained publicly available data may be exempt from ethical review.

As the merged microarray data originated from different batches, the “ComBat” method was applied to correct for batch effects and minimize nonbiological variations.12 Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed, and the ggplot2 package was used to visualize the results to confirm the removal of batch effects. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the limma package, and the results were visualized using heatmaps and volcano plots. This article complies with the TITAN 2025 Guidelines governing declaration and use of AI.13

Acquisition and Screening of Active Compounds and DEGs

The list of active compounds of Coicis Semen were obtained from the HERB database. The SMILES structures of all the acquired compounds were retrieved and subsequently screened using the SwissADME database. The disease-related genes were obtained from the GeneCards and OMIM databases, while the genes related to Coicis Semen were obtained from the ChEMBL, STITCH and SwissTargetPrediction databases.

Single-Cell Analysis

The S. aureus strain used was ATCC 25923. For the infection model, the bacteria were grown overnight in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. The overnight culture was then subcultured at 1:100 in fresh TSB and grown to the mid-exponential phase (OD600 ≈ 0.5). The bacterial cells were harvested, washed twice, and resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to a final concentration of 105 CFU/mL for injection; seven days post-infection, bone marrow tissue was harvested from the mice. Single-cell RNA sequencing was performed by Singleron Biotechnologies Co., Ltd. The resulting data were used to analyze the cellular localization of Myeloperoxidase (MPO) and Integrin Beta 3 (ITGB3).

Protein–Protein Interaction Network Construction and Hub Gene Identification

A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed for the target gene set using the STRING database. The search was restricted to H. sapiens, and a minimum interaction confidence score of 0.7 was set to ensure high-confidence interactions. The resulting PPI network was exported in XGMML or TSV format and imported into Cytoscape for visualization and topological analysis. Using the built-in tool “NetworkAnalyzer”, the key topological properties, including degree, betweenness centrality, and closeness centrality, were calculated for each node. From a systems biology perspective, hub genes often correspond to essential proteins, key signaling molecules, or master regulators within the network; accordingly, in this study, hub genes were identified and ranked using the maximal clique centrality algorithm implemented in the CytoHubba plugin.

Molecular Docking and Molecular Dynamics Simulation

MBOA and Nonanoic acid, key bioactive constituents of Coicis Semen, were selected through ADME screening, and their relevance as predicted ligands for the overlapping osteomyelitis target genes MPO and CXCL8 was highlighted, which justified their selection for subsequent computational and experimental validation. The three-dimensional stable MBOA and Nonanoic acid structures were obtained from the PubChem database, while the MPO and CXCL8 PDB files were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank. Molecular docking was performed using the CB- Dock Web server. Molecular dynikbics (MD) simulations were performed using Gromacs to study the interactions between MBOA and MPO, as well as between Nonanoic acid and CXCL8. The simulations employed the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field for proteins and the Generalized Amber Force Field (GAFF) force field for small molecules. Each system underwent solvation, charge neutralization, and energy minimization (using the steepest descent and conjugate gradient algorithms), followed by equilibration in the NVT and NPT ensembles (1000 steps each). A production MD run of 100 ns was performed.

The stability and interaction characteristics of the complexes were evaluated by analyzing trajectory-based metrics, including the RMSD, RMSF, Rg, SASA, number of hydrogen bonds, and free energy distribution (calculated using g_sham.py and xpm2txt.py). Stable RMSD values, low fluctuations in the RMSF and Rg, a high number of hydrogen bonds, and concentrated free energy clusters collectively indicate that the protein–ligand complex forms a stable interaction with the target protein.

Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) and Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC)

Both Nonanoic acid and MBOA were dissolved in DMSO at a final concentration of 0.1% (v/v) in the assay well, which was confirmed to have no antibacterial effect on its own. After all the medicinal solutions were filtered and sterilized through a 0.22 μm filter membrane, they were respectively subjected to TSB. A suspension of S. aureus was adjusted to a 1×108 CFU/mL and then diluted 1:200 in TSB broth to achieve a final bacterial concentration of 5×105 CFU/mL in the assay well. The diluted bacterial suspension was then mixed with the Nonanoic acid and MBOA solutions in a 96-well plate, Nonanoic acid and MBOA were subjected to continuous constant-ratio geometric serial dilutions from 10.0 mM up to 40.0 mM, with 20 gradient concentrations established in total. followed by incubation at 37 °C for 18 h. The MIC was defined as the lowest compound concentration at which no visible turbidity was observed. A negative control (bacteria without compounds) and a solvent control (bacteria with 0.1% DMSO) were included.

For determination of the MBC, 10 μL liquid from each well with no turbidity in the MIC assay was spread onto a blood agar plate without Nonanoic acid and MBOA compounds. After incubation at 37 °C for 18 h, the S. aureus colonies were counted. The MBC was defined as the lowest drug concentration that reduced the initial bacterial inoculum by ≥99.9% (≤5 colonies on agar plates). The MIC and MBC assays were performed according to the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI).14

Bacterial Growth Curve Assay (Turbidimetric Method)

Nonanoic acid and MBOA were prepared at concentrations of 1/8 MIC, 1/4 MIC, and 1/2MIC. The S. aureus suspension was adjusted to 5×106 CFU/mL. To each well of a 96-well plate, 160 μL TSB broth, 20 μL bacterial suspension, and 20 μL pre-prepared compound stock solution at 10 × the target concentration were added sequentially. After mixing, the final volume in each well was 200 μL. At this point, the initial bacterial concentration in the wells was 5×105 CFU/mL, and the compound reached the desired final concentration. The growth curve was subsequently measured. A blank control (medium without Nonanoic acid and MBOA or bacteria) was also used. The plate was incubated at 37 °C, and the OD600 value was measured every hour using a microplate reader. A growth curve was plotted with time on the x-axis and the OD600 value on the y-axis. The inhibitory effects of the compounds on S. aureus growth were analyzed on the basis of their growth profiles.

Bacterial Inhibition Zone Assay by Punch Method

The S. aureus bacterial suspension was adjusted to 1×108 CFU/mL and evenly spread on blood agar plates. After drying, agar wells with a diameter of 5 mm were punched using a sterile puncher, and the agar plugs were removed. The following solutions were added to the wells (30 μL per well): (1) test substance alone (Nonanoic acid at MBC; MBOA at MBC); (2) test substance + antibiotic combination (Nonanoic acid at MBC + Levofloxacin 5 µg/mL; Mboa at MBC + Levofloxacin 5 µg/mL); (3) antibiotic alone control (Levofloxacin, 5 µg/mL); (4) Solvent control (0.1% DMSO solution); The absence of inhibition zones in control wells was confirmed after incubation at 37 °C for 18 h. The diameters (mm) of the inhibition zones in each group were measured. The antibacterial activity and combined effects were analyzed by comparing the diameters of the test substance, antibiotic, and combination groups. Bacterial inhibition zone assays were performed following the standard methods and guidelines recommended by the CLSI.

Microcomputed Tomography (Micro-CT)

We first incised the skin and soft tissues of 10-week-old male C57BL/6 mice and used a sterile drill to create a hole on the right tibial plateau. Each hole was 1 mm in diameter and 1 mm in depth. We subsequently injected 10 μL of a 107 CFU/mL S. aureus ATCC 25923 suspension into the hole, after which the soft tissue and skin were sutured. Starting on the day of modeling, Nonanoic acid (20 mg/kg/day) and MBOA (35 mg/kg/day) dissolved in corn oil were injected locally into the tibial bone defect site. After 14 days, the mice were anesthetized and euthanized by carbon dioxide inhalation euthanasia. The proximal tibia was harvested and scanned using a micro-CT system at high resolution (10 μm voxel size). X-ray projection images were acquired, and three-dimensional models were constructed. Quantitative analysis of the reconstructed images, including bone morphometric parameters (bone volume per total volume, BV/TV), was performed using specialized software. These parameters were used to assess the extent of infection-induced bone destruction and the potential protective effects of Nonanoic acid and MBOA. The mice were monitored daily after surgery. If severe ulceration of the affected limb, signs of systemic infection, euthanasia was performed by carbon dioxide inhalation according to the relevant guidelines. Euthanasia procedures followed the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. All animal care and experiments in this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fujian Medical University (Approval No. FJMU IACUC 2021-J-0124).

Tartrate-Resistant Acid Phosphatase (TRAP) Staining

To evaluate the effects of Nonanoic acid (20 mg/kg/day) and MBOA (35 mg/kg/day) on osteoclast activity in C57BL/6 mice infected with 10 μL of a 107 CFU/mL S. aureus ATCC 25923. TRAP staining was performed after 14 days of inoculation to count the active osteoclasts with bone resorption ability. Upon sacrifice at the experimental endpoint, the tibial bone tissues from the infected sites were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and decalcified. After being embedded in paraffin and sectioned, the tissue slices were incubated with a TRAP staining mixture containing potassium tartrate and an acid phosphatase substrate (AS-BI phosphate with Fast Red TR salt for color development). The number of TRAP-positive multinucleated cells per field or per unit length of bone surface was quantified to assess osteoclast activity and bone resorption levels. The findings have been reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.15

Statistical Analysis

Unless otherwise specified, all the statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.1.2) and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2) software. Statistical significance was set at < 0.05, and all p values were two-tailed.

Results

Differential Expression Analysis

To integrate the microarray data from different batches and remove batch effects, we applied the ComBat method to normalize the gene expression matrices from GSE6269 and GSE16129. Prior to correction, PCA based on the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) revealed a clear separation between the two datasets, indicating substantial batch effects. After ComBat adjustment, the samples exhibited improved mixing in the PCA plot, demonstrating the successful mitigation of batch effects (Figure 1A and B).

Differential expression analysis was performed using the limma package to identify a large number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). A heatmap of the top 50 DEGs revealed distinct expression patterns between the disease and control groups (Figure 1C). A volcano plot further summarized the overall distribution of DEGs, with significantly upregulated genes marked in red and downregulated genes marked in blue, while nonsignificant genes are marked in gray (Figure 1D). These results reveal that batch effects were effectively removed, supporting the reliability of the differential expression analysis.

Screening of Bioactive Compounds and Target Prediction

In this study, bioactive compounds present in Coicis Semen were first retrieved from the HERB database, and their canonical SMILES structures were obtained. Information on all the candidate compounds was collected using the SwissADME database. In accordance with the principles of “Lipinski’s rule of five”, a series of filtering criteria were applied, including a molecular weight (MW) less than 500, fewer than 5 hydrogen bond donors, fewer than 10 hydrogen bond acceptors, no more than 10 rotatable bonds, a lipophilicity value (Log P) less than 5, and high gastrointestinal absorption. Eleven bioactive compounds meeting these criteria were selected: coixan A, leucine, 2-ethyl-3-hydroxyhexyl butyrate, hexanoic acid, hexanal, (S)-4-nonanolide, caprylic acid, Nonanoic acid, omain, vanillin, and MBOA. These eleven compounds include fatty acids, amino acids, and phenolic aldehydes known for their broad-spectrum anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and immunoregulatory properties, supporting the hypothesis that Coicis Semen acts against osteomyelitis through a multicomponent, multitarget mechanism. For these 11 compounds, potential protein targets were predicted using the SwissTarget prediction database. Genes with a prediction probability > 0.05 were retained, yielding a set of compound-related target genes. Osteomyelitis-related genes were identified from the GeneCards and OMIM databases. The intersection between the compound target genes and disease-associated genes revealed 20 overlapping genes (Figure 2A).

To explore the molecular mechanism of action of Coicis Semen against osteomyelitis, a PPI network of 20 overlapping genes was constructed using the STRING database (Figure 2B). Cytoscape was used for topological analysis, and five hub genes were identified: PTPRC, CXCL8, SRC, IKBKB, and MAPK14 (Figure 2C). Functional enrichment analysis was performed for the 20 overlapping genes. GO analysis revealed that these genes were enriched mainly in biological processes (BPs), such as positive regulation of cytokine production, leukocyte migration, platelet activation, and blood coagulation; cellular components (CCs), including the external side of the plasma membrane and transmembrane complexes; and molecular functions (MFs), such as protein kinase activity and cytokine receptor binding (Figure 2D). KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in platelet activation, Toll-like receptor signaling, chemokine signaling, PI3K-Akt signaling, TNF signaling, and VEGF signaling pathways, all of which are related to inflammation and immune responses (Figure 2E). These results reveal that Coicis Semen may exert antiosteomyelitic effects by modulating inflammatory and immune-related pathways.

Identification of Hub Genes and Single-Cell Analysis

Following KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, candidate targets within the enriched pathways were traced back to their corresponding chemical constituents. A “drug–compound–gene–pathway–disease” network was constructed using Cytoscape to visualize the interactions (Figure 3A). The network exhibited typical multicomponent, multitarget, and multipathway characteristics. Coicis Semen was the source node, connecting multiple volatile/fatty acids and aldehyde/phenol compounds—such as hexanoic acid, Nonanoic acid, caprylic acid, hexanal, vanillin, and coixan A—to a set of key target genes. The hub genes identified included PIK3R1, PIK3CD, PIK3CG, SRC, IKBKB, MAPK14, IRAK4, JAK3, IL6ST, CXCL8, CSF1R, ITGB3, PTPRC, F2, and MAPK, which were enriched in inflammation- and immune receptor-related signaling pathways as well as bone metabolism pathways, including MAPK signaling, TNF signaling, Toll-like receptor signaling, chemokine signaling, T cell receptor signaling, VEGF signaling, and Rap1/FoxO pathways, and processes such as apoptosis, cellular senescence, bone resorption/osteoclast differentiation, platelet activation, and actin cytoskeleton regulation. All the pathways were strongly associated with the “osteomyelitis” node, suggesting that Coicis Semen may exert a multitarget therapeutic effect on osteomyelitis by synergistically regulating key signaling pathways.

Molecular Docking and Molecular Dynamics Simulations

Among the overlapping genes, ITGB3 was identified as a target of coixan A, MPO as a target of MBOA, and CXCL8 as a target of Nonanoic acid. Since no definitive 3D structure is currently available for coixan A, molecular docking was performed using CB-Dock to evaluate the binding of Nonanoic acid to CXCL8 (Figure 4A) and MBOA to MPO (Figure 4G). The results revealed binding energies of −5.0 kcal/mol for the CXCL8–Nonanoic acid complex and −7.2 kcal/mol for the MPO–MBOA complex, indicating strong binding affinity between the small molecules and their respective target proteins.

(E and K) Hydrogen bond occupancy plots of Nonanoic acid to CXCL8 and MBOA to MPO. (F and L) SASA plots of Nonanoic acid to CXCL8 and MBOA to MPO.

MD simulations were subsequently conducted to further assess the stability and behavior of these complexes. The RMSD trajectories revealed that both complexes remained stable during the simulation, with fluctuations in the range of 0.2–0.5 nm for the CXCL8–Nonanoic acid complex (Figure 4B) and 0.15–0.3 nm for the MPO–MBOA (Figure 4H), suggesting favorable conformational stability. The RMSF values, which reflect local flexibility, remained below 0.8 nm for the CXCL8–Nonanoic acid complex (Figure 4C) and below 0.5 nm for the MPO–MBOA complex (Figure 4I), indicating relatively low flexibility in key binding regions. Additional analyses, including radius of gyration (Rg) (Figure 4D and J), hydrogen bond occupancy (Figure 4E and K), and solvent-accessible surface area (Figure 4F and L), revealed that both compounds form compact and stable complexes with their target proteins.

In vivo and in vitro Experimental Verification

To further investigate the therapeutic potential of Nonanoic acid and MBOA against S. aureus-induced osteomyelitis, we first evaluated their antibacterial effects in vitro. The MIC of Nonanoic acid was determined to be 12.96mM, while that of MBOA was 17.18mM (Figure 5A–C). The MBC was 19.78 mM for Nonanoic acid and was 37.28 mM for MBOA (Figure 5D). Bacterial growth curves revealed that both compounds inhibited S. aureus growth in a time- and dose-dependent manner (Figure 5E and F). An inhibition zone assay was performed using MBCs of Nonanoic acid and MBOA, both alone and in combination with 5 µg/mL Levofloxacin. The results revealed that a combination of either compound with Levofloxacin resulted in significantly larger inhibition zones than antibiotic treatment alone, suggesting a synergistic antibacterial effect (Figure 5G). In vivo evaluation via microCT imaging of a murine osteomyelitis model revealed that both Nonanoic acid and MBOA markedly alleviated bone destruction caused by S. aureus infection (Figure 6A). TRAP staining revealed that both compounds significantly reduced osteoclast activity, further supporting their protective role in bone maintenance during infection (Figure 6B).

Discussion

In this study, two GEO datasets were integrated to identify genes that were differentially expressed in patients with osteomyelitis. The analyzed GEO datasets (GSE16129 and GSE6269) contained gene expression profiles from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of patients with osteomyelitis. While osteomyelitis is a localized bone infection, it triggers a systemic immune response. PBMCs, as circulating immune cells, reflect the host’s overall inflammatory and immune status during infection. The dysregulated genes identified in PBMCs, particularly those involved in immune cell migration and inflammatory signaling, are directly relevant to the pathogenesis of osteomyelitis.16 These processes are crucial for the recruitment of immune cells from the circulation to the local bone infection site. Therefore, targets derived from PBMC data represent key regulators of the systemic immune response that ultimately impact the local bone microenvironment. This approach allowed us to identify central therapeutic targets that modulate the immune system’s ability to respond to and contain the localized infection.

Moreover, disease-associated genes were retrieved from GeneCards and OMIM, and twenty overlapping genes were identified for functional enrichment analysis. GO analysis revealed enrichment in processes such as positive regulation of cytokine production, leukocyte migration, and regulation of body fluid levels. Key cellular components included the extrinsic membrane component and cytoplasmic sides of the plasma membrane. The enriched molecular functions included cytokine receptor function, protein phosphatase function, and heparin binding. KEGG pathway analysis highlighted the top enriched pathways: Platelet activation, Toll-like receptor signaling, chemokine signaling, osteoclast differentiation, and C-type lectin receptor signaling, which are linked to immune regulation and bone metabolism,17–19 thereby underscoring their strong relevance to the pathogenesis of osteomyelitis.

Among the hub genes identified through the Cytoscape-based analysis, five key genes, PTPRC, CXCL8, SRC, IKBKB, and MAPK14, were characterized for their functional relevance. PTPRC, also known as CD45, is a critical regulator of the immune response. The encoded protein plays a central role in leukocyte function by dephosphorylating Src family kinases, thereby modulating the activation, development and signal transduction of T and B cells.20 CXCL8 (interleukin-8, IL-8) is a major inflammatory chemokine implicated in infections, autoimmune diseases, and cancer, highlighting its diagnostic and therapeutic potential.21 SRC, a member of the Src kinase family, is widely expressed in multiple cell types, including neurons, platelets, epithelial cells, and immune cells, and it regulates activation signaling in T cells, B cells, and macrophages.22 IKBKB encodes a kinase complex comprising IKKα, IKKβ, and IKKγ subunits and the IKBB protein serves as a core regulator of the NF-κB signaling pathway.23 It is broadly expressed across tissues and has particularly high activity in immune cells. MAPK14, a member of the p38 MAPK family, is highly expressed in immune cells, neurons, and endothelial cells. The MAPK14 protein regulates the production of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β and plays a key role in T cell activation and macrophage polarization.24 All five genes are closely associated with immune regulation following bacterial infection and are functionally implicated in the pathogenesis of S. aureus-induced osteomyelitis.

To further elucidate the potential mechanism through which Coicis Semen affects osteomyelitis, we integrated compound target information from the HERB and SwissADME databases and identified overlapping genes among drug target genes, DEGs from GEO, and disease-associated genes. This analysis revealed three key overlapping genes—ITGB3, MPO, and CXCL8—along with their corresponding bioactive compounds. ITGB3, a critical member of the integrin family, is associated with abnormal osteoclast function and bone remodeling.25 MPO, which is expressed primarily in the azurophilic granules of neutrophils and monocytes, serves as a key enzyme in the antimicrobial defense system of neutrophils and plays a significant role in infection, inflammation, and various diseases, highlighting its diagnostic and therapeutic potential.26 Molecular docking and dynamics simulations demonstrated stable binding between MBOA and MPO, as well as between Nonanoic acid and CXCL8, indicating strong and sustained molecular interactions. These results reveal that the bioactive components of Coicis Semen may modulate osteomyelitis through multitarget interactions involving ITGB3, MPO, and CXCL8, potentially influencing osteoclast activity, innate immune responses, and inflammatory processes.

To further validate the therapeutic potential of the bioactive compounds derived from Coicis Semen for osteomyelitis, both in vitro and in vivo experiments were conducted using Nonanoic acid and MBOA. The results revealed that these two compounds exhibited significant antibacterial effects against S. aureus and effectively ameliorated bone destruction in a murine model of S. aureus-induced osteomyelitis. These findings reveal that Nonanoic acid and MBOA are promising candidates for the treatment of osteomyelitis.

Beyond the direct antibacterial activity demonstrated in this study, the potential of Nonanoic acid and MBOA to combat the recalcitrant nature of S. aureus in osteomyelitis warrants further exploration. A key challenge in treating chronic osteomyelitis is the presence of bacterial persister cells and biofilms, which are highly tolerant to conventional antibiotics. Fatty acids and their derivatives, including medium-chain fatty acids such as Nonanoic acid, have been reported to exhibit anti-biofilm and anti-persister activities against various pathogens.27,28 Similarly, certain fatty acid derivatives and natural compounds can interfere with quorum sensing and biofilm matrix integrity. While the specific activity of MBOA against S. aureus biofilms or persisters has not been extensively reported, its structural features as a derivative may confer similar potential. Future studies should directly investigate the efficacy of Nonanoic acid and MBOA in eliminating S. aureus biofilms and persister populations, both in isolation and in combination with standard-of-care antibiotics, to fully assess their therapeutic potential for treating biofilm-associated osteomyelitis.

Although this study identified several potential bioactive compounds and their corresponding targets through database integration and confirmed their binding stability using computational methods, some limitations remain. For instance, the antibacterial effects of other components in Coicis Semen were not evaluated. Only the antibacterial effects of the effective components corresponding to the core genes are reported. Further evidence is needed to confirm the efficacy and safety of these compounds in the human body. Future research should include more comprehensive experimental verification and preclinical studies to fully elucidate the therapeutic potential and mechanism of action of these compounds.

Conclusion

In this study, the mechanisms by which the active components of Coicis Semen (notably MBOA and Nonanoic acid) exert synergistic effects on key targets such as MPO, CXCL8, and ITGB3, thereby regulating multiple signaling pathways involved in the inflammatory response, immune cell migration, and osteoclast differentiation were systematically elucidated. Through integrated analysis of GEO transcriptome data and multiomics bioinformatics, along with molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and in vivo and in vitro experimental validation, our results reveal that these components significantly inhibit S. aureus growth and mitigate bone destruction. These findings provide a robust mechanistic basis and potential therapeutic strategies for using Coicis Semen as a medicinal herb with food homology for the adjunctive treatment of osteomyelitis.
