Effects of high-speed shear and esterification modification on physicochemical and structural properties of cassava starch and microencapsulation for olive oils
Authors: Yingmei Wu, Fanying Shen, Jiaxin Luo, Fengping An, Hongbo Song, Xin Li, Qun Huang, Peng Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCassava starch exhibits insufficient hydrophobicity and tends to undergo retrogradation after gelatinization, which restricts its application expansion in the field of microcapsule embedding. In this study, cassava starch was modified through high-speed shearing, esterification modification, and high-speed shearing-assisted esterification treatment. The effects on its structural properties, gelatinization characteristics, and rheological properties were investigated. The results showed that high-speed shearing treatment significantly improved the solubility, swelling power, and transparency of the starch. However, the microstructure particles were fragmented and the crystal structure was damaged, which enhanced the efficiency of subsequent esterification reactions. Therefore, compared with OSA esterification-modified starch, the composite modified cassava starch (CS) exhibited remarkable modification effects under the synergistic action of high-speed shearing and OSA. SEM results indicated significant fracture of starch particles and disintegration of the crystal structure. CS demonstrated remarkable thickening ability, achieving the highest peak viscosity (269 ± 2.81 mPa s). Three modified starches were compounded with sodium caseinate to form a wall material for loading olive oil, and microcapsules were prepared by freeze-drying. The results showed that the olive oil encapsulation efficiency of microcapsules prepared from composite modified cassava starch (CSM) was the highest (86.6 ± 1.52 %). Structural analysis confirmed that the microencapsulation process was physical embedding, without generating new chemical groups, and CSM samples exhibited a higher tendency towards crystallization. This study confirmed that high-speed shearing-OSA synergistic modification can significantly enhance the functional properties of cassava starch, providing theoretical and practical support for its application as an efficient microcapsule wall material.Keywords: High-speed shear, Esterification, Cassava starch, Microencapsulation

Introduction

As an important tropical root tuber crop, cassava is widely cultivated in tropical regions of Africa, Asia and Latin America. The tuberous roots of cassava are rich in starch, with a content of up to 70-80 %, making it one of the most important sources of starch globally (Li et al., 2023). In the food industry, starch is used as a thickener, stabiliser and fat replacer, while its application areas cover a wide range of industries such as textiles, paper, pharmaceuticals and biodegradable materials. Cassava starch is important in industrial applications due to its unique properties, including high pasting temperature, low paste viscosity, and good transparency. However, the inherent drawbacks of native cassava starch, such as poor hydrophobicity, susceptibility to retrogradation, and low shear resistance, have limited the expansion of its applications in specific fields (Chen et al., 2025). For this reason, cassava starch is often modified by physical, chemical or enzymatic modification techniques in order to improve its functional properties and expand its applications (Xiao et al., 2023).

Chemical modification technology introduces specific groups into starch molecules via esterification, etherification, or cross-linking. This process regulates the molecular configuration and functional group distribution, thereby significantly enhancing key functional properties such as pasting characteristics, anti-aging stability, and hydrophobicity. Octenyl succinic acid starch ester (OSA-starch) is produced by the esterification reaction between octenyl succinic anhydride (OSA) and starch hydroxyl group under alkaline conditions. The hydrophilic carboxylic acid group (-COOH) and hydrophobic octenyl long chain (C8H14) are introduced through covalent bonding in the molecular structure to form a typical amphiphilic structure (Wang et al., 2022). OSA-starch have the ability to significantly reduce the interfacial tension between oil and water due to their special molecular structure, which made them show excellent application potential in many fields (Nhouchi et al., 2022). However, research by Wang et al. (2013) indicated that OSA droplets may react with the starch granule surface, making it difficult for them to penetrate into the granule interior and thereby affecting the esterification reaction efficiency. Therefore, employing different physical pretreatment methods such as ultrasonication(Chen et al., 2014), cryogenic treatment (Tu et al., 2021), or balling milling (Liu et al., 2021) prior to esterification, can facilitate the penetration of OSA into the starch granule interior.

High-speed shear technology is a physical modification method based on mechanical action. The core mechanism lies in the instantaneous processing of fluid or semi-solid systems through a high-energy shear force field. It can achieve the fine processing of materials, such as reducing the particle size of granules, destroying the cohesive structure, reducing the tendency of agglomeration, enhancing the dispersion uniformity(Gurrutxaga-Lerma et al., 2021), and decreasing the viscosity of materials (Gao et al., 2024). Xia et al. (2019) reported that cassava starch undergone structural degradation and exhibited enhanced dispersibility following high-shear processing. Wang et al. (2015) indicated that intense shear forces facilitate more thorough reaction between OSA and corn starch during the modification process. Existing studies had shown that mechanical force may affect the pasting characteristics and rheological behaviour of starch particles by changing the ratio of crystalline and non-crystalline regions. Duan et al. (2024) reported that shear forces can fragment starch granules into smaller particles, forming transient clusters that significantly alter rheological properties, an effect highly dependent on chain length distribution. Ji et al. (2025) further demonstrated that shear action creates surface cracks on granules, promoting water penetration and synergistically modifying lamellar structures, with rheological parameters strongly correlating with the thickness of crystalline lamellae. However, the specific mechanism of high-speed shear-assisted dual modification of starch by chemical methods had not been fully elucidated.

Olive oil, as a core component of the Mediterranean diet, is mainly composed of oleic acid, palmitic acid and linoleic acid (Tome-Carneiro et al., 2020). The polyunsaturated fatty acids contained in olive oil are highly susceptible to auto-oxidation (Choudhary et al., 2023). which not only leads to rancidity and deterioration but also produces potentially toxic oxidation products such as malondialdehyde. Microencapsulation technology can create a physical barrier around oil droplets. The key performance metric for such microcapsules is the encapsulation efficiency (EE), which reflects the proportion of oil successfully embedded within the wall material (Iftikhar et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). The use of modified starch as a composite wall material addresses the drawbacks of conventional starch—including low solubility, poor emulsifying capacity, and suboptimal film-forming properties—all of which are critical factors that directly influence EE. Therefore, improving EE is a primary goal in the development of starch-based wall materials for oil encapsulation (Chen et al., 2025).

Single modification techniques (such as esterification or physical shearing) often exhibit limitations in enhancing the performance of cassava starch as a microcapsule wall material. To address this, our study proposes a composite modification strategy combining high-speed shearing with OSA esterification, along with a central hypothesis: the high-shear processing enhances the reaction efficiency of OSA esterification modification and improves the physicochemical properties of the modified starch. This strategy can significantly enhance the encapsulation capacity of starch. Consequently, our research systematically compared the impacts of single esterification, single shearing, and composite modification on the structural and functional properties of starch. Furthermore, we focused on evaluating key performance metrics such as the encapsulation efficiency and stability of olive oil microcapsules prepared using these modified starches as wall materials. This study aimed to elucidate the synergistic mechanism underlying composite modification, providing a novel and effective solution for the development of high-performance starch-based microcapsule wall materials.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Cassava starch (supplier catalogue number: S25805, amylose:19.57 % ± 0.56, amylopectin: 80.43 % ± 0.56) was purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biological Reagent Co. (Shanghai, China). OSA was purchased from Sigma Company (St. Louis, USA). Isopropanol, hydrochloric acid, anhydrous ethanol, sodium hydroxide, silver nitrate, phenolphthalein, glycerol and potassium bromide were all analytically pure and purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co.(Shanghai, China)

Preparation of modified starches

Esterified starch

The esterification method was adapted from Zhang et al. (2017) with modifications to the starch concentration, reaction temperature, washing and drying procedures. Briefly, the native starch (NS) was precisely weighed to prepare a 35 % (w/w) starch emulsion. The emulsion was thoroughly stirred, and its pH was adjusted to 8.5 by gradual addition of a 5 % (w/w) NaOH solution under continuous mixing to maintain stability. Subsequently, OSA (3 % w/w, dry starch basis) was added in multiple batches, with each addition spaced 20 min apart, and the process was completed within 2 h. The reaction was carried out in a 40 °C thermostatic water bath with constant stirring. The reaction system was stirred continuously at a constant speed in a water bath at 40 °C for 4 h. The reaction system was neutralized with 3 % (v/v) HCl solution to pH 6.5. The neutralized product was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the resulting precipitate was sequentially washed three times with an 80 % (v/v) ethanol solution, followed by three washes with deionized water. The resulting precipitate was dried in an oven at 50 °C for 24 h. After being crushed and sieved through a 100-mesh sieve, esterified starch (ES) was obtained.

High-speed shear starch

Accurately weighed 10.0 g of NS was added to deionized water at a solid-liquid ratio of 1:40 (m/V) and dispersed for 30 min at room temperature to allow sufficient wetting and swelling. The starch suspension was initially equilibrated to 25 °C before shearing. The suspension was then sheared for 4 min using a flash extractor (JBHT-50T, Zhijing, China) at 7500 r/min. The instrument used does not have an active cooling system. Therefore, the suspension temperature increased during shearing due to mechanical energy dissipation. Based on post-shear measurement, the temperature reached approximately 48–52 °C by the end of the 4 min treatment. After shearing, the mixture was filtered through a Büchner funnel, and the resulting solid residue was dried in an oven at a constant temperature of 60 °C for 12 h. The dried sample was processed through a pulveriser and passed through a standard 100-mesh sieve, and the resulting sample, named HSS, was stored in a desiccator protected from light for spare use (Yu et al., 2025).

High speed shear-esterified starch

HSS was precisely weighed and used to prepare a 35 % (w/w) starch emulsion. The esterification reaction was conducted following the method described in section 2.2.1, with the sole modification being an adjustment of the reaction time to 3 h (determined by preliminary experiments to achieve the maximum DS for CS). The starch emulsion was pretreated by stirring in a constant-temperature water bath at 35 °C. OSA was then added in batches while maintaining the pH at 8.5. The reaction system was kept at a constant temperature of 35 °C and stirred continuously for 3 h. Following neutralization, the product underwent centrifugal washing, drying, and grinding. The final sample obtained was a composite modified starch, designated as CS.

Characterization of starch physicochemical properties

Degree of substitution(DS)

A 5.00 g (dry basis) starch sample was precisely weighed into a 250 mL conical flask. Subsequently, 25 mL of a 2.5 mol/L hydrochloric acid–isopropanol mixture was added, and the reaction was magnetically stirred for 30 min in a water bath maintained at a constant temperature of 25 °C. After completion of the reaction, 100 mL of 90 % (v/v) isopropanol solution was added to terminate the reaction, and the reaction was left to stand for 10 min and then filtered through a Bühler's funnel under reduced pressure. Subsequently, the filter cake was washed with 90 % (v/v) isopropanol solution for several times until the filtrate was detected by 0.1 mol/L AgNO3 solution to confirm that there was no Cl− residue. Solubility (S) and swelling power (SP)

A precisely weighed quantity of absolute-dry starch sample was obtained using an analytical balance with a precision of 0.0001 g. The sample was transferred into a pre-weighed centrifuge tube, and deionized water was added to prepare a starch milk suspension at a 2 % (w/w) concentration. The sealed centrifuge tube was then incubated in a thermostatic water bath at graded temperatures (30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, and 90 °C) for 30 min. During incubation, the tube was vortexed for 10 s at 5-min intervals to ensure uniform dispersion of starch granules and consistent heating, thereby preventing sedimentation and clumping. Following gelatinization, the tube was allowed to cool naturally to ambient temperature. The cooled suspension was centrifuged at 2795 × g for 20 min. The resulting supernatant was carefully decanted into a pre-weighed weighing dish that had been previously dried to constant weight. The dish was then placed in a 105 °C forced-air drying oven and dried until constant weight was achieved, defined as a mass change of less than 0.0002 g between two consecutive weighings. After cooling in a desiccator, the total mass of the dish and dried solids was recorded. Simultaneously, the centrifuge tube containing the remaining sediment was also weighed (Dai et al., 2025). Transparency and anticoagulant properties

Starch suspensions were prepared at a 1 % (w/w) concentration. The samples were heated in a boiling water bath under continuous magnetic stirring for 30 min. After cooling naturally to ambient temperature, deionized water was used as the reference solution, which was thoroughly mixed using a vortex mixer. The transmittance of the samples was then measured at 650 nm using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (Wang et al., 2025).

Starch suspensions were prepared by accurately weighing at 1 % (w/w). The samples were then placed in a 100 °C water bath and continuously stirred for 20 min. After cooling to 25 °C, the samples were transferred to 25 mL stoppered graduated test tubes for static stratification. Phase separation into two layers was observed at 24 h intervals, and the volume change of the supernatant was recorded every 24 h.

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) analysis

A 2 mg starch sample was accurately weighed and mixed with dry KBr powder at a 1:100 mass ratio. The mixture was thoroughly ground in an agate mortar and pestle until the particle size was uniform. The resulting powder was transferred to a compression-molding device, where a pressure of 20 MPa was applied under a vacuum of ≤0.1 MPa and maintained for 60 s to form a transparent pellet. Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy (Nicolet iS5, Thermo Fisher, USA) was performed with the following parameters: wavenumber range 4000–400 cm−1, resolution 0.8 cm−1, and 64 scans(Wu et al., 2025a, Wu et al., 2025b).

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

A multifunctional X-ray polycrystalline diffractometer (TENSOR 27, Bruker, USA)equipped with a Cu-Kα radiation source was used to analyze the crystalline structure of starch samples according to the method of Wu et al. (2024a) with slight modifications. The test conditions were set to a scanning range of 5° to 40° (2θ), and data were acquired at a scanning rate of 2°/min and a step angle of 0.026°. The obtained X-ray diffraction patterns were processed using MDI Jade 6 software to quantitatively characterise the relative crystallinity (RC, %) of the samples by calculating the ratio of the area of crystalline diffraction peaks to the total diffraction area.

Microstructure of starch

Optical microscope observation

The starch samples were dispersed in a 50 % (v/v) glycerol-aqueous solution to prepare a 2 % (w/w) starch suspension. The suspension was thoroughly mixed and sonicated for 10 min to enhance dispersion. Appropriate amount of suspension was prepared and the morphology of starch granules was observed under an optical microscope (NI-U DIC, Nikon, Japan) with a magnification of 1000×.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) observation

An appropriate amount of the starch sample was applied to a conductive adhesive-coated metal carrier stage. Unadhered loose particles were gently removed by air blowing using a wash ball, followed by gold sputter-coating to enhance electrical conductivity. The surface morphology and microstructure of the starch particles were observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Nova Nano SEM 23, FEI, USA) at magnifications of 5000× and 10000 ×, respectively (Wang et al., 2022).

Differential scanning calorimetry(DSC)

A differential scanning calorimeter (DSC) (DSC1, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland) was used to measure the thermal properties of the sample. First, 3.4 ± 0.1 mg of the starch sample was weighed into an aluminum crucible, followed by the quantitative addition of 6.6 ± 0.1 μL of ultrapure water. The crucible was sealed using a specialized tool and equilibrated in a constant-temperature incubator at 4 ± 0.5 °C for 24 ± 0.5 h. Prior to analysis, the equilibrated samples were maintained at 20 ± 0.5 °C for 10 ± 0.5 min for temperature stabilization. An empty aluminum crucible of identical specifications was used as the reference during the measurements. The temperature was scanned from 20 to 100 °C at a constant heating rate of 10 °C/min (Hu et al., 2025).

Pasting properties

The pasting properties of starch samples were tested by RVA (TecMaster, Perten, Sweden). Starch samples were accurately weighed on a dry-weight basis to prepare a 6 % (w/w) starch suspension. The suspension was thoroughly stirred and transferred to an aluminum test vessel. The instrument was programmed to equilibrate at 50 °C for 60 s, heat to 95 °C at a constant rate, maintain 95 °C for 150 s, cool to 50 °C at a uniform rate, and finally equilibrate at 50 °C for 120 s, resulting in a total testing cycle of 780 s. The stirring speed was set to 160 rpm for the initial 10 s and maintained at 160 rpm throughout the experiment(do Rosario et al., 2025).

Emulsifying properties

Preparation of emulsions

The modified starch prepared in 2.2 and NS were dissolved in ultrapure water together with sodium caseinate, respectively. A 20 % (w/v) solution of the wall material was prepared and mixed (4:1, w/w) with the core material (olive oil) with continuous stirring for 30 min until the system was completely homogeneous. Subsequently, the system was processed using a high-speed shear disperser at 8000 r/min for 4 min. Four stable emulsions systems were finally obtained, which were recorded as raw starch emulsion (NSE), high-speed shear starch emulsion (HSSE), esterified starch emulsion (ESE), and composite modified starch emulsion (CSE), respectively.

Microencapsulation of olive oil

Preparation of microcapsules

Microcapsules were prepared using the freeze-drying method. Specifically, emulsions with different wall materials were prepared according to the method described in Section 2.10.1. 15 mL of emulsion was poured into a 9 cm diameter Petri dish to form a liquid layer approximately 0.8 cm thick, then placed in a −24 °C freezer for static freezing for 24 h. The frozen sample was promptly transferred to a vacuum freeze-dryer(FDU-120, Ailang Instrument Co.China) and freeze-dried for 12 h under a vacuum below 0.1 mbar to remove moisture.The condenser temperature was set to −50 °C. For the freezing step, the samples were frozen statically at −24 °C for 24 h in a standard freezer, resulting in a slow freezing rate (approximately 0.5-1 °C/min). The resulting porous solid after freeze-drying was ground in a high-speed mill (YF-150B, Ruian Yongli Pharmaceutical Machinery Co., China) for 30 s. The fine powder passing through a 100-mesh sieve was collected as the final microcapsule product. It was packaged in aluminum-plastic pouches and stored at 4 °C in a refrigerator for future use (Otalora et al., 2024). The prepared microcapsules were named as raw starch microcapsules (NSM), high speed shear starch microcapsules (HSSM), esterified starch microcapsules (ESM) and composite modified starch microcapsules (CSM).

Encapsulation efficiency (EE)

An accurately weighed 1.0 g portion of the microencapsulated sample was introduced into 40 mL of petroleum ether and gently agitated for 120 s. The sample was subsequently filtered rapidly under reduced pressure through a G3 sintered glass funnel. The collected solid residue was thoroughly rinsed with 25 mL of petroleum ether for 40 s, after which the filtration process was repeated. The combined filtrate was then transferred to a pre-weighed triangular flask (denoted as m1). Following solvent removal using a rotary evaporator, the flask was dried in a vacuum oven at 65 °C until a constant mass (m2) was achieved.

An accurately weighed 1.0 g portion of the microencapsulated sample (denoted as m) was dissolved in 30 mL of distilled water heated to 60 °C. After cooling the solution to room temperature, 1.25 mL of 25 % (v/v) aqueous ammonia (shaken vigorously for 3 min), followed by 10 mL of anhydrous ethanol, 20 mL of petroleum ether, and 20 mL of ethyl ether, were added sequentially. The mixture was shaken thoroughly for 1 min and allowed to stratify into layers. The upper organic phase was collected, and the extraction procedure was repeated once more. The combined organic extracts were concentrated to dryness using a rotary evaporator, and the residue was dried in an oven at 105 °C until a constant mass was achieved. The dried residue was then weighed to determine the total oil mass (denoted as m3).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3). Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For characterization techniques including FT-IR, XRD, SEM, and TGA, representative results from one representative measurement (n = 1) were shown. Before one-way ANOVA, the normality of the data was checked using the Shapiro-Wilk test (P > 0.05 for all groups), and homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene's test (P > 0.05 for all comparisons). One-way ANOVA with Duncan’ s post hoc test was then performed using IBM SPSS Statistics. The significance threshold was set at P < 0.05. Two-way ANOVA was performed to test the interaction between temperature (7 levels: 30–90 °C) and starch modification type (4 levels: NS, HSS, ES, CS) on solubility and swelling power. When a significant interaction was detected (P < 0.05), simple effects analysis was conducted using Bonferroni correction. All statistical graphs were plotted through OriginPro.

Results and discussion

The physicochemical properties of starch

Degree of substitution (DS)

As can be seen from Table 1, the DS of CS was significantly higher than that of ES. This was attributed to the dense crystalline structure of starch granules which limited the diffusion of OSA molecules into the interior of the granules, resulting in a single esterification reaction occurring mainly on the surface of the granules (Zhang et al., 2017). High-speed shear treatment induced physical deconstruction of starch granules through the action of mechanical energy. This mechanical force induced structural change leads to more complete exposure of reactive hydroxyl groups in starch molecules, thereby significantly improving the reaction efficiency of OSA esterification modification. Mechanistically, high-speed shearing can damage starch structure at multiple levels. It will cause particle breakage, disrupt crystalline regions, and increase surface area and surface hydroxyl groups. Meanwhile, it may cleave the branched starch chains and generate new reducing terminal hydroxyl groups (Duan et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2026). Therefore, the starch samples pretreated by high-speed shear showed higher reactivity in the subsequent OSA esterification reaction, and the final CS obtained had a significantly higher DS (Zhang et al., 2014). In this study, high-speed shear pretreatment significantly enhanced the esterification efficiency of OSA, a finding consistent with Zhang et al. (2019) results using ultrasonic pretreatment of cassava starch. Both studies collectively demonstrated that physical pretreatment disrupts the compact structure of starch granules, exposing more hydroxyl groups and thereby providing more accessible reaction sites for the hydrophobic reagent OSA.

The solubility (S) and swelling power (SP)

As shown in Table 2, Table 3, the S and SP of NS, HSS, ES and CS showed a significant enhancement trend when the treatment temperature was gradually increased from 30 °C to 90 °C. This thermally induced behavioral change is mainly due to the disruption of intermolecular hydrogen bonds in the starch crystallization region caused by temperature. Firstly, water molecules penetrate into the amorphous region and form new hydrogen bonds with free hydroxyl groups, weakening the original starch starch network (Ji et al., 2025). Secondly, when the temperature rises to 70-90 °C, water will reach the crystal surface and break the double helix in a chain length dependent manner - longer chains (DP > 12) require more energy to dissociate (Duan et al., 2024). Third, once the temperature reaches the gel range (cassava starch is about 70-90 °C), enough heat energy will overcome the activation energy of double helix dissociation, leading to the collaborative melting of crystal sheets. This explains the sharp increase in solubility and swelling power observed in our study within this temperature range (Xie et al., 2026). To formally test whether temperature effects depended on modification type, two-way ANOVA was performed. The results revealed a highly significant interaction between temperature and starch type (F(18, 56) = 48.62, P < 0.001), confirming that the modified starches responded differently to increasing temperature. Simple effects analysis showed that CS and HSS exhibited a significantly steeper increase in solubility from 70 °C to 90 °C compared to NS and ES (P < 0.001). Specifically, the solubility increase over this temperature range was 2.0 % for NS, 2.6 % for ES, 11.7 % for HSS, and 7.3 % for CS. These results demonstrated that high-speed shear treatment (HSS and CS) significantly enhances the temperature responsiveness of starch solubility.

The transparency and anticoagulant properties

Transparency is a critical parameter for evaluating the clarity of starch pastes. Higher transparency values indicate superior dispersion of starch molecules within the aqueous phase, reduced intermolecular association, and more pronounced granule swelling (Wang et al., 2025). As can be seen from Fig. 1 (A), the transparency of ES, HSS, and CS were all significantly enhanced compared to NS, which was closely related to the changes in the structural modification of starch granules and intermolecular interactions. The lowest transparency of NS was mainly attributed to its dense crystalline structure. The highly ordered crystalline regions in the original starch granules were formed by hydrogen bonding between hydroxyl groups. These regions limited water penetration and prevented the granules from fully expanding during pasting. Consequently, this led to enhanced light scattering and reduced paste transparency (Shi et al., 2025). This result was consistent with the lower SP of NS in 3.1.2, further verifying the role of structural densities in constraining the functional properties. The significantly better transparency of ES than NS was attributed to the dual synergistic effects of OSA esterification. The hydrophilic carboxyl groups enhanced the hydrogen-bonding network, thereby promoting water solubility. In contrast, the hydrophobic olefinic long chains inhibited molecular chain aggregation by disrupting the crystalline structure through a spatial site-barrier effect. These complementary effects synergistically enhanced the overall transparency of the system (Ahsan et al., 2020; Sweedman et al., 2013). The enhanced light transparency of HSS, on the other hand, originated from the physical destruction of starch granules by high-speed shear treatment. High-intensity mechanical forces led to increased cracks on the granule surface, disintegration of the internal crystalline regions, and exposure of more hydrophilic hydroxyl groups after molecular chain breakage, which promoted water molecule adsorption and rapid granule swelling (Struck et al., 2018).The enhanced transparency of CS compared to ES may be attributed to the combined effects of high-speed shear pretreatment and OSA esterification. The shear treatment disrupts crystalline domains, potentially reducing the size of light-scattering particles, while the OSA-introduced hydrophobic long chains may create steric hindrance that restricts molecular chain aggregation (Soe et al., 2020). This aligns with the findings of Wang et al. (2015) who employed high-shear synergistic esterification to modify corn starch. The composite-modified corn starch exhibited higher transparency than esterified starch alone. Consistent with Ji et al. (2025), the shear-induced reduction in crystalline lamellar thickness contributes to reduced light scattering and enhanced transparency.

As shown in Fig. 1B, ES and CS exhibited better anticoagulant properties than NS, as evidenced by the reduced supernatant volume over time. HSS showed the poorest anti-settling properties, with rapid sedimentation occurring within 24 h. The improved stability of ES and CS is likely attributable to the OSA modification. The introduced hydrophilic carboxyl groups may enhance interaction with water, promoting homogeneous dispersion, while the hydrophobic octenyl chains may create steric hindrance that restricts molecular chain reassociation and retrogradation (Ricci et al., 2019). The poor stability of HSS is consistent with previous reports that high-shear processing can disrupt granular structure and cause molecular chain degradation, which increased the proportion of short-chain amylose molecules that readily reassociate into crystalline networks, accelerating sedimentation (Wang et al., 2015). According to Xie et al. (2026), mechanical shear forces can break long-chain molecules, especially those of amylopectin. This will generate more short chain amylose molecules. These short chain molecules have a smaller molecular weight, and their mobility and diffusion speed in the paste are much higher than those of long-chain molecules. These highly mobile short chain amylose are more likely to move and approach each other in the paste. Once they approach, the hydroxyl groups (-OH) on their chains will quickly bind through hydrogen bonds, forming temporary physical cross-linking points (Duan et al., 2024).。 As time goes by and temperature decreases, these temporary hydrogen bond networks will develop into more stable and ordered double helix crystal structures.

CS exhibited better stability than HSS but slightly lower stability than ES, suggesting that the positive effect of OSA esterification partially counteracts the shear-induced damage (Wu et al., 2020). The slightly lower anticoagulant properties of CS compared to ES may be due to the composite modification weakening the hydrophobic-hydrophilic balance of esterified starch, where the presence of inadequately modified hydrophilic segments—particularly small-molecule amylose chains generated by shear—provides sites for ordered rearrangement via hydrogen bonding, thereby limiting further enhancement of anticoagulant properties. In contrast, ES may exhibit a more uniform OSA distribution, thereby providing more comprehensive anti-aging protection across the entire molecular chain (Yu et al., 2016).

FT-IR

Fig. 1 (C) displayed the FTIR spectra of different samples. All samples exhibited the characteristic O-H vibration peak and adsorbed water peak of starch polysaccharides at 3300 cm−1 and 1640 cm−1 (Wu et al., 2025). The absorption peak at 2930 cm−1 corresponded to the C-H asymmetric stretching vibration of the methylene group (-CH2), which reflected the molecular chain of the starch molecule of the hydrophobic backbone structure (vanSoest et al., 1995). The absorption peaks observed at 1160 cm−1, 1080 cm−1, and 1010 cm−1 correspond to stretching vibrations of the C–O bond, aligning with the vibrational modes of C–O–C and C–OH groups within the cyclic glucose units of starch. These observations corroborated the polysaccharide structural features inherent to starch.The absorption peak at 930 cm−1 was attributed to the skeletal vibration of the glucose pyranose ring. It was consistently present in all samples, indicating that the modification process did not disrupt the fundamental sugar ring structure of the starch (Zheng et al., 2020). Compared with NS, the infrared spectra of HSS showed no new peaks or loss of original peaks. This indicated that high-speed shear mechanically disrupted the granule's physical structure without breaking chemical bonds or generating new functional groups (Wu et al., 2020). OSA-modified samples (including ES and CS) exhibited two new characteristic absorption peaks at 1740 cm−1 and 1572 cm−1, attributed respectively to the C=O stretching vibration of the ester bond in the octenyl succinate group and the antisymmetric stretching vibration of the carboxylate group (COO−). The appearance of these two peaks conclusively demonstrated the successful occurrence of the OSA esterification reaction, forming a covalent R-COO-R' bond structure along the starch molecular chain (Zhuang et al., 2023). Notably, the absorption peak intensities at 1740 cm−1 and 1572 cm−1 for CS were significantly stronger than those for ES, suggesting a potentially higher degree of esterification. This finding was consistent with the higher degree of substitution we measured via titration (Xiao et al., 2023). This alteration in chemical structure was the fundamental cause of its differing functional properties. The steric hindrance effect generated by the bulky OSA groups effectively inhibited the rearrangement of starch molecular chains, thereby enhancing its anti-aging stability. Firstly, the large octenyl chains physically prevent adjacent starch chains from approaching each other, thereby preventing the formation of double helices (Duan et al., 2024). Secondly, OSA substitution consumes hydroxyl groups, reduces hydrogen bonding sites, and weakens interchain attraction (Ji et al., 2025). Thirdly, the attached OSA groups increase local chain rigidity, limit molecular mobility, and increase the entropy barrier for chain rearrangement (Xie et al., 2026). In addition, the other absorption peaks of ES and CS were highly consistent with NS, proving that no other impurities were introduced during the modification process, and the structural optimization was only achieved through functional group modification.

To semi-quantitatively compare the DS between ES and CS, the absorbance ratio of the ester carbonyl peak (1740 cm−1) to the reference C–H peak (2930 cm−1) was calculated. The 2930 cm−1 peak, corresponding to the CH2 asymmetric stretching vibration of the starch backbone, was used as an internal standard because it remains unchanged during modification. The calculated absorbance ratios (A1740/A2930) were 0.71 ± 0.03 for ES and 0.92 ± 0.05 for CS. The significantly higher ratio for CS further supports its higher DS, as determined by titration, and confirms that high-speed shear pretreatment enhances the efficiency of OSA esterification.

XRD

In general, different crystalline starches have characteristic diffraction patterns. A-type starch shows characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ angles of 15°, 17°, 18° and 23°, and its crystal structure is usually formed under low moisture conditions (moisture content <30 %). On the other hand, the characteristic diffraction peaks of B-type amylopectin appeared at positions 5.6°, 15°, 17°, 20°, and 24°, which were mostly formed in high-moisture environments. It was noteworthy that the characteristic peaks observed within the 2θ diffraction range of 30°–35° exhibited a significant correlation with the crystalline properties of starch-lipid complexes. C-type starch, representing an intermediate crystalline transition state, arises from the recrystallization of A-type and B-type starch crystals. Its diffraction pattern exhibits a superposition of the characteristic peaks associated with both A-type and B-type crystalline structures, as evidenced by the presence of diffraction peaks corresponding to both crystal types (Malumba et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2016). As shown in Fig. 1 (D), the XRD analysis showed that the original starch presented B-type crystal characteristic peaks at 2θ = 15°, 17°, 18°, 20°, 23°and 24°, and the modified starch showed new characteristic peaks at 15°, 17°, 18° and 23°, which indicated that the crystal structure was transformed from B-type to A-type. The transition from B-type to A-type can be explained by disruption of the characteristic B-type hydrogen bonding networks, followed by recrystallization into a more compact and less hydrated A-type lattice. For HSS, high-speed shear applies intense mechanical forces that directly break hydrogen bonds and fragment crystalline domains (Xiao et al., 2023). For ES, OSA esterification reduces the number of free hydroxyl groups available for hydrogen bonding, while the bulky octenyl chains introduce steric hindrance that physically separates starch helices, promoting local dehydration(Chen et al., 2025). For CS, the shear pretreatment physically damages the B-type structure and exposes more reactive sites, allowing more efficient OSA substitution, which then further stabilizes the A-type packing.

The relative crystallinity of the starch samples shown in Table 4 indicated that the relative crystallinity (RC) values of HSS were significantly lower than those of NS. This was consistent with the findings of Wang et al. (2015), where the relative crystallinity of starch decreased from 36.90 % to 34.68 % when the shear rate increased from 500 rpm to 1.5 × 104 rpm, compared to native corn starch. The RC values of ES and CS were significantly higher than those of NS (P < 0.05). Although OSA esterification typically occurs preferentially in amorphous regions and would be expected to reduce crystallinity, the observed increase in RC for ES (37.67 %) and CS (35.15 %) compared to NS (33.73 %) is not without precedent. Similar observations have been reported for OSA-modified cassava starch (Zhang et al., 2017) and ginkgo starch (Zheng et al., 2017). This phenomenon has been attributed to the reorientation and tighter packing of amylopectin chains during the esterification reaction, potentially involving the edges of crystalline regions (Sweedman et al., 2013). In our study, the B→A crystal type transition (Fig. 1D) and the reduced hydrogen bonding network indicated by FTIR (shift in O–H stretching region, Fig. 1C) provide indirect support for this interpretation. For CS, HSS pretreatment first reduced the overall crystallinity. Subsequent OSA modification further enhanced XRD signal attenuation through deep modification of the amorphous matrix. This combination resulted in an RC value higher than HSS but lower than ES.

Microstructure

The microstructure of the modified starch was analyzed by the combination of optical microscopy and SEM. The specific results were shown in Fig. 2 (A) and 2 (B). In Fig. 2 (A), polarised light microscopy showed that NS had a typical cross-extinction structure, indicating an intact crystal structure. SEM in Fig. 2 (B) further revealed that the surface of the granules was smooth and unbroken, with clear boundaries between the granules and no agglomeration or deformation. Following esterification modification of ES, the polarized cross patterns of cassava starch granules persisted, albeit with diminished brightness and clarity. This observation suggested that esterification exerted a discernible influence on the crystalline structure of the starch granules (Zhang et al., 2017) without entirely disrupting their crystallinity. The surface of the ES granules was rough, with the appearance of tiny holes, and the shape of the granules was slightly changed, but the overall structure was intact. Polarised microscopic observation of HSS granules showed that the polarised crosses were significantly weakened or even disappeared, confirming the destruction of the crystalline structure. SEM analysis showed that although the starch granules still retained their basic morphology, the surface showed obvious pits and cracks, presenting the characteristics of roughening. These structural changes mainly originated from the continuous action of mechanical forces on the granules during high-speed shearing (Liu et al., 2011). The phenomenon of particle fragmentation and crystal structure disruption observed in this study under high-shear conditions aligns with the effects of high-static-pressure treatment on waxy maize starch reported by Szwengiel et al. (2018). Both studies indicate that intense mechanical forces disrupt the microcrystalline regions of starch, leading to a decrease in crystallinity. The transformation of the polarised crosses intuitively reveals the fundamental differences between the two modification mechanisms. High-speed shear processing directly induces physical disintegration of starch granule crystal structures through intense physical-mechanical forces, resulting in the complete disappearance of the polarized cross pattern. In contrast, esterification modification primarily erodes crystal structures through chemical substitution reactions. While it does not entirely destroy the fundamental framework, it significantly reduces structural order, manifesting as a marked decrease in both brightness and clarity of the polarized cross pattern. The polarisation crosses of the CS granules gradually disappeared altogether, indicating that the granule structure was disrupted (Tan et al., 2015). SEM images revealed that the particle structure was disrupted, the crystalline regions were degraded, and the particle surface exhibited numerous cracks and pores, resulting in an irregular particle morphology.

In order to quantitatively evaluate the crushing effect, Image J was used to analyze the particle size distribution of SEM images (magnified 5000 times) (Fig. 2B). The average particle size of NS was 10.84 ± 5.28 μ m, reflecting the uneven size of intact particles. HSS showed a decrease in average size (9.27 ± 3.57 μm), confirming shear induced fragmentation. DSC

Fig. 3 (A) illustrates the DSC curve characteristics of the sample. Table 4 presents the starch gelatinization characteristic parameters: onset temperature (T0), peak temperature (TP), end temperature (Tc), and gelatinization enthalpy (ΔH). These parameters can effectively evaluate the degree of crystal perfection and molecular orderliness of starch granules, and provide a basis for revealing the effects of various modification treatments on the thermal stability of starch (Zhang et al., 2011).ΔH referred specifically to the energy required for the deconvolution of the double-helical conformation of the starch during the pasting process, which was fundamentally different from the energy consumption for the complete melting of the entire crystal structure (Li and Zhu, 2021). As shown in Table 4, the thermodynamic parameters of the modified starches all showed significant changes (P < 0.05) compared to NS. The pasting characteristic temperatures (T0, Tp, Tc) and ΔH of ES were significantly lower than those of NS, which was mainly attributed to the spatial site-blocking effect produced by the hydrophobic groups introduced by the OSA modification. This structural change reduced the swelling resistance of starch granules so that the water absorption and swelling and pasting process can occur at lower temperatures (Zheng et al., 2017). The grafting of long-chain hydrophobic groups weakened intermolecular hydrogen bonds via a spatial site-blocking effect, thereby enhancing molecular chain flexibility. This enhancement in flexibility then resulted in a reduction of the starch's pasting thermodynamic parameters (Sharma et al., 2016). The pasting temperature parameters of HSS (T0, Tp, and Tc) were significantly lower than those of NS, originating from the damage to the granule structure and destruction of the crystalline zone due to mechanical shear. CS exhibited the lowest pasting temperature parameters (T0, Tp, Tc), with ΔH falling between ES and HSS. The thermodynamic differences may be attributed to the higher degree of conformational rearrangement of starch molecular chains and changes in composite modified structures compared to single mechanical treatment.

Pasting properties

The pasting characteristics of starch reflect the changes in rheological behaviour during heating, shearing and cooling, which are closely related to the molecular structure, granule integrity and modification treatment. As can be seen from Fig. 3 (B), the pasting curves of both the original starch and the three modified starches showed typical three-stage change characteristics. The pasting parameters of the four starches were shown based on the data in Table 5. Peak viscosity (PV) represented the maximum viscosity reached during starch pasting, which was related to the granule swelling capacity and the amount of straight-chain starch dissolved. The PV of the three modified starches were all significantly higher than those of NS (P < 0.05). The highest PV were found for CS, followed by those of ES, suggesting that hydrophobic groups and crosslinking treatment enhance the water absorption and swelling capacity of starch granules (Li et al., 2023). The PV exhibited by the CS sample in this study (269 mPa s) was significantly higher than that reported by Bhosale and Singhal (2007) for pure OSA-modified cassava starch (approximately 150-200 mPa s). This strongly demonstrated that high-shear pretreatment, by depolymerizing molecular chains and promoting uniform OSA substitution, forms a more effective entanglement network, thereby generating a synergistic thickening effect. The PV of HSS was significantly lower than that of ES and CS. This reduction might be attributed to the high-speed shear causing granule surface damage and the premature leaching of amylose. These effects subsequently limited the swelling potential of the starch granules (Xiao et al., 2023). Trough viscosity (TV) reflects the viscosity stability after high-temperature shear. The TV of ES and CS was significantly higher than that of NS, suggesting that the modified starch has better structural stability at high temperatures, which may be related to the protective effect of crosslinked networks or hydrophobic groups. The improvement in CS thermal stability aligns with the observations of Martínez et al. (2024) on crosslinked-esterified composite modified starch, indicating that synergistic modification contributes to the formation of a stable structure that is more resistant to thermal shear and temperature fluctuations. It was noteworthy that the significant enhancement of CS peak viscosity observed in this study contrasted with the findings of Wang et al. (2015). In their work, starch peak viscosity decreased under the same treatment compared to OSA-esterified starch alone. This discrepancy may stem from our more intense shear conditions. The intense shear generated fragmented starch molecules, which formed a more stable network structure with OSA long chains.

Rheological properties

Shear stress

The rheological characteristic curves in Fig. 3 (C) showed that there was a significant difference in the shear stress response of various starch paste systems under constant shear rate conditions. For ES, carboxylic acid groups with strong hydrophilic properties were introduced into the molecular structure due to the OSA esterification reaction, and these polar groups promoted the formation of a more stable hydrated shell layer by enhancing the starch-water interaction. This in turn led to the formation of a denser hydration layer, which resulted in restricted molecular chain movement and elevated shear stress. It was shown that the increase in molecular weight of OSA esterification was positively correlated with its rheological parameters. Therefore, the shear stress of ES was significantly higher than that of NS.

In contrast, for HSS, the surface crack propagation and internal crystal structure disruption of starch granules induced by high-speed shear forces significantly compromised granule integrity. The size of the broken starch fragments decreased and the specific surface area increased, however, the hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces between the molecular chains were weakened, resulting in a lower flow resistance. During shearing, the chain segments of straight and branched starch break and the density of molecular chain entanglements decreases, which ultimately led to a reduction in the shear stress of HSS (Xiao et al., 2023). The CS starch paste, however, showed the most excellent performance, which was enhanced by firstly destroying the crystalline structure of the starch granules through a high-speed shear pretreatment to increase the accessibility of the active sites. The subsequent OSA esterification reaction was carried out under more adequate reaction conditions, resulting in a higher degree of substitution and a more uniform substitution distribution. The high degree of substitution led to an increase in the density of intermolecular cross-linking, which further strengthened the network structure. In comparison to ES, CS was subjected not only to elevated shear stress induced by the OSA esterification reaction but also to the synergistic effects of high-speed shear pretreatment and esterification modification. This combination enhanced the intermolecular chain cross-linking density, forming a more robust network structure. Consequently, this sample exhibited the greatest increase in shear stress and thus the most prominent performance among the three (Zamani and Razavi, 2023).

To quantitatively compare the flow behavior of the starch pastes, the shear stress and shear rate data were fitted to the Power Law model. The resulting consistency index (K) and flow index (n) were presented in Table 7.The Power Law model provided excellent fits for all samples (R2 > 0.998). CS exhibited the highest K value (13.37 Pa·sn), which is markedly higher than that of NS (8.05 Pa·sn), HSS (1.07 Pa·sn), and ES (2.69 Pa·sn). This result quantitatively demonstrated that the composite modified starch forms the strongest entangled network structure. Furthermore, CS showed the lowest n value (0.49) among all samples (NS: 0.51; HSS: 0.64; ES: 0.66), indicating the most pronounced shear-thinning behavior.

Apparent viscosity

The results of the apparent viscosity tests in Fig. 3 (D) showed that all four starch pastes exhibited significant shear thinning characteristics. With the increase of shear rate, the apparent viscosity of each system showed a significant decreasing trend, and finally tended to a stable value. The viscosities of the modified starch systems exhibited significant variations at a constant shear rate. Specifically, ES demonstrated a notable increase in viscosity attributed to the introduction of hydrophilic carboxyl groups via OSA esterification and the intermolecular cross-linking of hydrophobic long chains. In contrast, HSS displayed a viscosity reduction due to the disruption of crystalline regions, the breakdown of hydrogen bonding networks, and the decreased entanglement of molecular chains resulting from mechanical shear. Notably, the CS samples exhibited superior rheological properties, with strong agreement between viscosity parameters and shear stress measurements (Sharma et al., 2025). The CS samples showed the best rheological properties with good agreement between the viscosity parameters and the shear stress measurements. This excellent performance resulted from the synergistic effect of the dual modification process.On one hand, the OSA esterification reaction introduced hydrophobic groups and altered the molecular structure of starch. On the other hand, the preceding high-speed shear treatment potentiated the effects of subsequent esterification modification, resulting in the formation of a denser spatial network of starch molecules and imparting excellent functional properties.

The apparent viscosity vs. shear rate data were also fitted to the Power Law model (η = K·γ(n−1)) to obtain quantitative flow indices. The results (Table 7) showed that CS exhibited the highest consistency index (K = 13.08 Pa·sn) and the lowest flow index (n = 0.50), confirming its strongest shear-thinning behavior and most robust network structure. These results are consistent with the Power Law parameters obtained from shear stress fitting (Table 6), further validating the superior rheological properties of the composite modified starch CS.

Emulsifying properties

Rheological properties

Apparent viscosity

As shown in Fig. 4 (A), the viscosities of the emulsions were all decreased significantly with increasing shear rate, showing typical shear-thinning characteristics, which was in line with the rheological characteristics of non-Newtonian fluids (Larson and Wei, 2019). The viscosity of ESE was significantly higher than that of the native starch, which could be attributed to the incorporation of long-chain hydrophobic alkenyl groups. These groups increased the molecular weight of the starch and introduced spatial hindrances that restricted the mobility of molecular chains. It has been shown that the increase in molecular weight of ES was positively correlated with its rheological parameters (Zhang et al., 2011). The viscosity of HSSE samples was slightly elevated, being marginally higher than that of NSE but lower than that of ESE. This observation could be attributed to the mechanical shear treatment, which disrupted the crystalline structure of starch granules, facilitating the dissolution and participation of additional molecular chains in network formation. However, this effect may have been partially offset by molecular chain scission(Xu et al., 2025). Among the samples, CSE exhibited the highest viscosity. Specifically, the viscosity of esterified CSE samples subjected to high-speed shear pretreatment was significantly higher than that of ESE. This enhancement can be attributed to the high-speed shear pretreatment. This process increased surface roughness and exposed more active sites, thereby facilitating OSA substitution. Consequently, it strengthened hydrophobic interactions and promoted the dissolution of molecular chains through enhanced solubility. These effects collectively contributed to the formation of denser entangled networks (Wang et al., 2024).

Angular frequency scanning

Dynamic rheological analysis showed that G' and G'' increased continuously with increasing frequency, which was in line with the typical intrinsic behaviour of viscoelastic materials (Dai et al., 2019). In Fig. 4(B)–G' and G'' of NSE, ESE, HSSE and CSE samples differed from each other. NSE, as an unmodified sample, has a simple molecular structure with limited intermolecular network crosslinks, so the G' and G'' values were lower. ESE was modified by OSA esterification, which introduced hydrophobic long chains to increase the molecular weight and limit the movement of molecular chains to form a certain network structure, and the G' and G'' values were higher than those of NSE. But the modification method was single, and the network structure was not as dense as that of CSE. Although HSSE underwent high-speed shear treatment, which facilitated the dissolution of additional molecular chains for network formation, it also induced molecular chain scission, resulting in suboptimal network stability and crosslinking density. In contrast, CSE, which combined high-speed shear pretreatment with esterification modification, increased surface active sites and substitution degree, promoting molecular chain dissolution and entanglement to form the densest network structure with the highest intermolecular crosslinking density. As a result, CSE exhibited the highest G' and G'' values among all samples (Wang et al., 2024).

Zeta potential and droplet size

Zeta potential, as an important parameter for evaluating the stability of emulsion systems, directly reflects the strength of the interaction between the surface charges of dispersed-phase particles (Zhang et al., 2019). When the emulsion exhibits poor stability, the absolute value of the zeta potential is found to be less than 30 mV, rendering the emulsion susceptible to aggregation or flocculation. Conversely, when the zeta potential's absolute value exceeds 30 mV, the system attains and sustains a state of robust dispersion stability, which is primarily attributed to the electrostatic repulsion forces at play. This critical value is in accordance with the prediction of the bilayer theory that the electrostatic repulsion generated by the higher surface charge density is sufficient to overcome the van der Waals gravity. The results in Fig. 4 (C) showed that all the prepared emulsions were electrostatically stable and the droplets did not disperse. ES introduced charged groups through chemical reactions which were negatively charged in solution and increased the charge density on the surface of the particles. Specifically, esterified modified starch was anionic in nature at neutral pH, which gave it a high zeta potential in solution (Fuentes et al., 2023). High-speed shear treatment disrupted the structure of starch granules, exposing more polar groups on their surface. These polar groups could be negatively charged in solution, thereby increasing the charge density on the surface of the granules. In addition, the high-speed shear treatment can make the surface of starch granules rougher and increase their specific surface area, which further improved the surface charge density. The absolute value of the zeta potential of CSE was the largest, mainly because the composite modified starch increased the charge density on the surface of the granules through the introduction of charged groups, which in turn increased the absolute value of the zeta potential.

The droplet size distributions of the four emulsions (NSE, HSSE, ESE, CSE) were determined by DLS. As shown in Fig. 4 (D), CSE exhibited the smallest mean droplet diameter (328.4 ± 3.0 nm) and the lowest polydispersity index (PDI = 0.454 ± 0.004), indicating the formation of a fine and uniform emulsion. ESE showed a slightly larger droplet size (374.0 ± 11.3 nm, PDI = 0.552 ± 0.024), followed by HSSE (363.7 ± 11.5 nm, PDI = 0.479 ± 0.03). NSE displayed the largest droplet size (406.5 ± 9.7 nm, PDI = 0.483 ± 0.036). These results were consistent with the zeta potential measurements and confirmed that CSM formed the most stable emulsion before freeze-drying. The small droplet size and uniform distribution of CSE are favorable for subsequent microencapsulation, as they promote better encapsulation efficiency.

Characterization of olive oil microcapsules

Encapsulation efficiency(EE)

According to the experimental results in Fig. 4 (E), the microcapsules prepared with different starch-based wall materials exhibited significant differences in the EE (P < 0.05). The EE of ESM was significantly higher than that of NSM. This phenomenon originated from the introduction of hydrophobic alkenyl long chain in the molecular chain of starch by the esterification modification of OSA, which significantly enhanced emulsification properties and the stability of oil-water interfaces. The study of Zhang et al. (2019) demonstrated that the hydrophobicity of the OSA-modified starch -hydrophilic balance values were optimized to form a denser interfacial film in the aqueous phase, thus enhancing the encapsulation rate (Hindmarsh et al., 2019). In addition, the esterification modification reduced the crystallinity of the starch and improved the film-forming continuity (Wang et al., 2020). The EE of HSSM was significantly enhanced compared to NSM. This improvement is attributed to the high-speed shear treatment, which disrupted the natural granular structure through mechanical forces. The structural disruption reduced particle size and increased specific surface area. Consequently, the enhanced solubility led to the higher EE. According to Chen et al. (2014), an elevated proportion of short chains within the HSSM compromised emulsion stability, precipitating core migration during the drying process. Consequently, the EE of the HSSM-based system was notably inferior compared to those formulated with ESM and CSM. Composite modification combined chemical modification with physical treatment, which improved the starch's thermal stability and mechanical strength by enhancing the cross-linking network structure of the molecular chains. Khan et al. (2021) had demonstrated that the gel network formed by modified starch in the composite effectively immobilized the core material, thereby mitigating core loss during the preparation process. Consequently, the CSM-based system achieved the highest EE, registering at 86.6 ± 1.52 %. This was primarily attributed to the outstanding emulsifying and film-forming properties resulting from composited modification.

Moisture content

The moisture content of the microcapsules was significantly affected by the physicochemical properties of the wall material. When four different modified starches were used as wall materials for olive oil microcapsules, the table showed that the moisture content of HSSM samples was significantly higher than the other groups. This may be attributed to the high speed shear treatment which disrupted the crystal structure of starch granules through mechanical stress, increasing the specific surface area and exposing more hydrophilic hydroxyl groups, thus enhancing the moisture absorption capacity (Mahdi et al., 2020). As could be seen from Table 8, NSM exhibited a lower moisture content compared to HSSM, attributable to the structural integrity of their crystalline domains and the encapsulation of hydrophilic hydroxyl groups within these regions. However, the amorphous domains of NSM, characterized by a loosely packed structure due to the rigidity-induced constraints of the molecular chains, conferred a slightly elevated water adsorption capacity relative to ESM. OSA significantly reduced the water content of ESM. This reduction was achieved both by the replacement of hydroxyl groups with hydrophobic moieties (which reduced hydrogen bonding) and by the formation of dense hydrophobic interfacial membranes.

CSM exhibited a significantly reduced moisture content owing to the disruption of crystalline structure induced by high-speed shear pretreatment, which exposed a greater number of active hydroxyl groups. These hydroxyl groups subsequently participated in the esterification reaction with OSA, forming a three-dimensional crosslinked network. This network not only diminished the availability of free hydroxyl groups but also imposed steric hindrance, thereby restricting water diffusion and culminating in the lowest observed water content among the tested formulations. The experimental results showed that the moisture content of the four microcapsule samples was lower than 5 % (w/w), indicating that they have excellent storage stability and applicability for industrial processing.

Wettability

The wettability of microcapsules can be evaluated by determining their redispersal properties in aqueous media (Mahdi et al., 2020), which is quantified by the time required for the sample to be completely immersed in the aqueous phase. From Table 8, the wettability in descending order was NSM (14.17 %) > HSSM (14.09 %) > ESM (13.17 %) > CSM (12.05 %). The highest wettability of NSM was due to the exposure of highly hydrophilic hydroxyl groups on the surface, which had a higher surface energy and facilitated the adsorption of water molecules. The incorporation of hydrophobic groups within ESM led to a reduction in surface energy and the formation of a hydrophobic interface. This interface effectively impeded the spreading of water molecules, thereby resulting in a further decrease in the material's wettability. The wettability of HSSM was observed to be slightly lower than that of NSM. This reduction was attributed to the increased surface roughness induced by particle fragmentation, a marginal decrease in contact angle, and the partial disruption of hydroxyl groups due to shearing forces. In contrast, CSM exhibited the lowest wettability, owing to the formation of a crosslinked network that further reduced the surface density of hydroxyl groups. The rigid structure of the highly crosslinked starch matrix in CSM additionally impeded water penetration, reinforcing its hydrophobicity.

Solubility

The results in Table 8 showed that the solubility of the olive oil microcapsules of all groups ranged from 80.86 % to 83.16 %. This phenomenon was mainly attributed to the synergistic effect of the key process parameters and wall components during the preparation process. 4 min of homogenisation reduced the droplet size through high shear, which not only increased the specific surface area of the microcapsules, but also formed a porous structure conducive to solvent penetration. Meanwhile, sodium caseinate, as an amphiphilic wall component, both enhanced the mechanical strength of the oil-water interfacial film and improved the hydrophilic properties of the composite wall through intermolecular interactions. The solubility of olive oil increased after microencapsulation, which was affected by different wall materials, NSM had the lowest solubility due to its unmodified and intact natural crystalline structure, long molecular chains and high degree of entanglement. The introduction of hydrophobic octenyl succinate groups via OSA esterification confers amphiphilic character to ESM. As demonstrated by He et al. (2024), OSA-modified starch molecules can self-assemble in aqueous media, forming stable nanomicelles with a core-shell structure — hydrophobic octenyl chains aggregate into the core, while hydrophilic starch backbones extend outward as the shell. This self-assembly fundamentally alters the distribution of starch molecules in polar solvents, transforming them from dispersed individual chains into organized nano-aggregates. Consequently, the effective aggregate size increases, affecting solubility, dispersion stability, and interfacial activity. This micellization behavior explains why ESM exhibits enhanced emulsification properties and encapsulation efficiency compared to unmodified starch. Concurrently, the abundance of hydrophilic hydroxyl groups within sodium caseinate further enhances solubility. However, an excessively high DS of hydrophobic groups may impose constraints on solubility, likely due to steric hindrance or altered solvent-polymer interactions. Mechanical shearing of HSSM induced fragmentation of starch granules and cleavage of molecular chains, leading to an increased proportion of short chains. This elevation in short-chain content contributed to enhanced solubility. However, excessive aggregation of these short chains may partially counteract this solubility advantage, potentially due to steric hindrance or reduced solvent accessibility.High-speed shear pretreatment of CSM significantly increased the availability of active sites for OSA esterification, thereby promoting substitution and reinforcing hydrophobic interactions. Concurrently, OSA esterification stabilized the short-chain structures generated by shear forces and suppressed aggregation. These synergistic effects collectively engineered a molecular architecture more conducive to solubility, as evidenced by superior solubility outcomes compared to single-modification approaches.The multiple effects resulted in the best solubility among the samples (Joshi et al., 2019). CSM exhibited low wettability and high solubility, a behavior attributed to the OSA-mediated hydrophobic modification, which reduced surface hydrophilicity and high-speed shear pretreatment, which disrupted the crystalline structure to form a porous network. This porous architecture rendered the microcapsule surface resistant to wetting while facilitating rapid interior disintegration and payload release under appropriate conditions. These dual attributes render CSM suitable for precise controlled-release applications requiring environmental stability and rapid release kinetics.

Bulk density and compacted density

Table 8 demonstrated that NSM exhibited the lowest bulk density and compacted density, a phenomenon attributed to their intact crystalline structure, extended molecular chain lengths, high degree of chain entanglement, and substantial interparticle void spacing. ESM modified by OSA esterification enhanced the intermolecular interaction through hydrophobic groups, which made ρt higher than NSM, but too high substitution limited the compact particle arrangement, so that ρb was still equal to that of NSM. HSSM increased bulk density and compacted density through particle fragmentation and short-chain generation, which facilitated closer particle packing. In contrast, compared with ES, the enhanced reactivity of CSM is attributed to the high-speed shear (HSS) pretreatment before OSA esterification. According to Wang et al. (2015), high-speed shear can degrade starch molecules, release soluble saccharides, and promote more homogenous distribution of substitute groups within starch granules, resulting in higher DS and reaction efficiency. Pretreatment with HSS leads to surface cracking and fragmentation of starch granules, increasing the specific surface area and damaging the B-type crystal structure, releasing previously buried hydroxyl groups. At the same time, it breaks down the long chains of branched starch, producing new reducing terminal hydroxyl groups.In addition, HSS pretreatment inevitably produces short chain amylose fragments. Duan et al. (2024) demonstrated that such treatment changes the amorphous and crystal region of starch, resulting in the leaching of short chains. In the absence of stabilization (such as only in HSS), these short chains can easily recombine through hydrogen bonding, forming a crystal network that accelerates sedimentation. However, when HSS pretreated starch is subsequently esterified with OSA, octenylsuccinic acid groups covalently attach to both long and short chains. The large hydrophobic octene chains generate steric hindrance, preventing short chains from approaching each other and forming hydrogen bonds, thereby stabilizing them in a dispersed state. Meanwhile, as reported by Duan et al. (2024), the presence of extended starch chains and leached short chains facilitates the promoting interaction between starch molecular chains, thereby developing a highly cross-linked network structure. Hydrophobic interactions between octene chains on adjacent molecules drive self-assembly, forming a moderately cross-linked network through hydrophobic microdomains. This synergistic effect optimized particle mobility and stacking efficiency, resulting in significantly higher ρb and ρt values compared to other samples and indicating the most densely packed particle arrangement.

Color

Table 8 showed that all four microcapsules have high brightness (L), which confirmed the effective encapsulation of oils and fats. CSM and ESM mitigated light scattering by disrupting starch hydrogen bonding via introduced hydrophobic groups and forming a dense interfacial film. Among these, CSM exhibited the highest light reflection efficiency, attributed to composite modification-induced optimization of molecular arrangement, which enhanced packing density and interfacial coherence. HSSM was subjected to high-speed shear to destroy the crystal structure, and the surface roughness led to the decrease of light scattering. NSM has the worst optical performance due to starch phase separation and hydroxyl adsorption impurities. Chromaticity analysis showed that ESM had the lowest b-value due to OSA modification. HSSM and CSM increased in yellowness after shearing, and NSM showed negative a-value (greenish) due to high content of straight-chain starch.

Microstructure of microcapsules

Fig. 5(A)–(D) showed that all olive oil microcapsule samples displayed a nearly spherical particle morphology, and their surfaces showed obvious wrinkles and irregular textures. This particular surface morphology feature may be closely related to the phase separation behaviour of the wall components and intermolecular interactions during the freeze-drying process. The SEM apparent morphology of microcapsules prepared by freeze-drying method showed significant differences depending on the starch modification methods (Cui et al., 2021). NSM had a rough and porous surface with a loose structure, which was attributed to the high crystallinity of natural starch and poor compatibility with olive oil. HSS had a relatively smooth surface with smaller and more homogeneous particles, but there were local depressions or fragments, which were the result of mechanical shear disrupting the crystalline zone of starch and refining the particle. The surface of ESM was smooth and dense, presenting a spherical or globular-like structure, which benefited from the introduction of hydrophobic groups to enhance emulsification stability. In contrast, CSM exhibited highly dense and smooth surfaces, possibly with multilayered or special network structures, as a result of the synergistic effect of physical and chemical modifications (Liu et al., 2018).

FT-IR

FT-IR spectroscopy analysis is able to detect the vibrational modes of characteristic chemical bonds in the molecules of a substance. By comparing the changes in the characteristic peaks of the core and the wall, the molecular interaction mechanism between the two can be revealed (Wu et al., 2025). According to the results of infrared spectral analysis in Fig. 6 (A), the characteristic absorption peaks of cassava starch broadband absorption at 3400 cm−1, which corresponds to the telescopic vibration mode of the hydroxyl group, suggesting that there was an intermolecular hydrogen bonding interaction in the samples. The characteristic peaks near 2930 cm−1 originated from the asymmetric telescopic vibration of the methylene C-H bond. The multiple absorption peaks in the wave number ranges of 1160 cm−1, 1080 cm−1 and 1010 cm−1were attributed to the stretching vibration of the C-O bond, which verified the polysaccharide nature of starch. Notably, both sodium caseinate and olive oil microcapsules showed significant broad absorption bands in the 3300-3600 cm−1 interval, whereas no characteristic signals were detected in this region for free olive oil. This important difference was a good evidence that the formation of hydrogen bonding network played a key role in the construction of microcapsules, as the characteristic absorption mainly reflected inter- or intramolecular hydrogen bonding interactions of polymer molecules (Wang et al., 2022). The hydroxyl-stretching vibrational peak position of the CSM was significantly red-shifted compared to the other samples (shifted from 3453 cm−1 to 3400 cm−1). This characteristic peak displacement phenomenon indicated the formation of a new chemical environment within the CSM system through intermolecular interactions (Wu et al., 2024b). Olive oil showed characteristic peaks at 3009 cm−1, 2928 cm−1, 2853 cm−1 and 1745 cm−1. The microencapsulated samples retained these peaks but with significantly reduced intensity, indicating that the oil phase was effectively encapsulated. The absence of new characteristic bands in the spectra confirmed that the embedding process has not triggered chemical reactions or functional group changes in the core-wall material.

XRD

The crystalline properties of sodium caseinate, olive oil, and the four microcapsules were shown in Fig. 6 (B) and 6 (C). The original starch showed characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ of 15.0°, 17.0°, 20.0° and 24.0°, which was consistent with the B-type crystalline structure characteristics. After the modification process, the positions of the diffraction peaks of starch were significantly shifted, and new diffraction peaks appeared at 15.7°, 17.3°, 19.8° and 23.4°, and these characteristic peaks matched with the A-type crystalline structure. It confirmed that the modification process induced the change of starch crystalline type from B-type to A-type. Olive oil showed a broad diffuse peak at 2θ = 19°, which indicated that olive oil existed in a typical amorphous state, consistent with the characteristic disordered arrangement of triglyceride molecules in vegetable oils. Sodium caseinate, on the other hand, exhibited a distinct crystalline structure, with sharp diffraction peaks detected at 2θ = 10.3°, 13.9°, 15.7°, 16.0°, 17.3°, 19.8°, 21.1°, and 23.4°, which was consistent with the characteristic periodic arrangement of its α-helical and β-folded secondary structure (Tang et al., 2022). The XRD spectra of all the four microcapsules showed similar semi-crystalline features, as evidenced by the presence of characteristic peaks at 2θ = 15°, 17°, 18°, and 22°, while the original polycrystalline characteristic peaks of sodium caseinate disappeared. The disappearance of sodium caseinate crystalline peaks in the microcapsule XRD patterns (Fig. 6C) indicates a strong molecular interaction between sodium caseinate and the modified starch matrix. OSA modification introduces hydrophobic alkyl chains and carboxyl groups into starch, which can form electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions with caseinate molecules, preventing their recrystallization during freeze-drying. meanwhile, the rapid freezing process may trap caseinate molecules in an amorphous state within the starch matrix, effectively inhibiting crystal growth. Similar observations have been reported for protein-polysaccharide complexes (Wang et al., 2020; Tang et al., 2022), where the disappearance of protein crystalline peaks indicates successful complexation and homogeneous dispersion.

It was noteworthy that the peaks of CSM were sharper compared with ESM, indicating that these two microcapsules have higher crystallization tendency and better stability. In addition, the characteristic peak of olive oil at 19° did not appear in the spectra of all microcapsules, which indicated that the core olive oil was effectively encapsulated.

TGA

TGA is able to accurately characterise the thermal decomposition behaviour of materials by monitoring the mass change of samples under programmed warming conditions (Wu et al., 2024b). The TG and DTG curves of different samples were shown in Fig. 6 (D)-6(E). From the characteristics of the curves in Fig. 6 (D), both tapioca starch and sodium caseinate showed mass loss due to water dissipation at 100-200 °C. After 300 °C, sodium caseinate showed a significantly faster rate of mass loss due to the rapid decomposition of the protein structure, and the curves followed a steeper trend. Cassava starch, as a carbohydrate, decomposed relatively slowly, highlighting the differences in pyrolysis characteristics between carbohydrates and proteins due to structural differences. TG curves showed that olive oil exhibited good thermal stability in the range of 30-370 °C, started to decompose after 370 °C, and was basically completely decomposed at 470 °C. The thermal decomposition of microcapsules was relatively gentle in the range of 30-370 °C. The thermal decomposition of microcapsules was mainly water evaporation in the 30-210 °C range. Due to the protective role of the wall material, the weight loss of the microcapsule sample was smooth at 210-250 °C (Zhang et al., 2024). The microcapsule samples were not affected by the decomposition of the wall material. Due to the decomposition of wall components, microcapsules showed about 70 % mass loss at 250-450 °C. The weight loss slowed down at 450-600 °C and decomposition was completed at 550 °C. The results showed that all four microcapsules had strong heat resistance, and the cassava starch-sodium caseinate composite wall material significantly improved the thermal stability of olive oil, with an increase in decomposition temperature of about 100 °C compared to free oil.

To further characterise the thermal decomposition behavior, DTG curves were analyzed (Fig. 6E). Free olive oil exhibited a single sharp degradation peak at 430 °C (Tmax), corresponding to the decomposition of unsaturated fatty acids. In contrast, all microcapsule formulations displayed two distinct degradation peaks. The first peak, appearing at 298–312 °C, is attributed to the decomposition of the wall materials (cassava starch and sodium caseinate). The second peak, appearing at 445–462 °C, corresponds to the degradation of the encapsulated olive oil.

Conclusion

This study systematically investigated the effects of high-shear processing, esterification modification, and synergistic high-shear-esterification treatment on the structural and functional properties of cassava starch, successfully applying it to the preparation of olive oil microcapsules. The research revealed the synergistic mechanism between high-shear pretreatment and oleoyl esterification. High-shear processing physically disrupted starch granule integrity and crystalline regions. While this structural dissociation reduced starch anti-settling properties, it exposed a large number of active hydroxyl groups for subsequent esterification, significantly enhancing OSA esterification efficiency. This effect was directly confirmed by the significantly enhanced infrared characteristic absorption peaks (1740 cm−1 and 1572 cm−1) observed in the CS. The composite modification strategy successfully integrated the advantages of physical and chemical modifications. The resulting CS samples not only exhibited excellent gelatinization properties (lower gelatinization onset temperature) and potent thickening capabilities (higher viscosity) but also demonstrated significantly enhanced thermal stability. This indicated that the composite modification strategy can directionally create novel starch materials tailored to specific functional requirements. In microencapsulation validation, CS demonstrated superior comprehensive performance as a wall material. The emulsion formed exhibited the highest absolute zeta potential and viscosity, indicating exceptional physical stability, while achieving the highest olive oil encapsulation rate (86.6 ± 1.52 %) among all groups. FT-IR results confirmed the microencapsulation process as physical encapsulation, ensuring core material integrity. In summary, the composite modification strategy represented an efficient and industrially viable approach for developing high-performance starch-based materials, demonstrating significant potential for producing effective microencapsulation systems suitable for food and pharmaceutical applications. This study successfully prepared and systematically characterized CS with outstanding emulsifying stability and rheological properties, confirming its significant potential as a food-grade wall material. It should be noted that in vitro release studies under simulated gastrointestinal conditions were not performed in this study. While the high encapsulation efficiency and dense surface structure of CSM suggest potential for controlled release, direct evidence is lacking. Future studies should systematically evaluate the release kinetics. Meanwhile, this study did not evaluate the oxidative stability of encapsulated olive oil during storage. Future research should include accelerated storage tests for peroxide value and benzaldehyde value to directly assess the protective function of the CSM wall material.
