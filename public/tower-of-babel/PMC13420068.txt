A case of toxic epidermal necrolysis in an elderly female patient
Authors: Esenia Bechir, Ganna Isayeva, Simeon Troesch, Mairi Ziaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Toxic epidermal necrolysis is a rare, potentially fatal, and mainly drug-induced dermatological emergency that requires immediate medical intervention. It is characterized by extensive erythema, widespread sloughing of the epidermis and mucous membranes, and tissue necrosis. The condition can result in significant skin loss and serious complications such as sepsis and shock. With a mortality rate of 30%–50%, early recognition and prompt treatment are critical to improving outcomes. Here we report the case of an 85-year-old woman who presented to our emergency department with confluent erythematous maculopapular eruptions affecting more than 60% of her body surface area, accompanied by a positive Nikolsky sign and widespread areas of denuded skin, including involvement of the oral, vaginal, and anal mucosa following self-administered trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole for a urinary tract infection. A skin biopsy revealed transepidermal necrosis with minimal active inflammation in the upper dermis. We initiated systemic glucocorticoid therapy along with fluid resuscitation. The patient remained stable throughout hospitalization. By the time of discharge, dermatological findings had significantly improved, and the skin was in the re-epithelialization phase. In conclusion, a high index of clinical suspicion for toxic epidermal necrolysis is necessary when a patient receiving trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole presents with blistering and/or skin sloughing. Prompt treatment with immunosuppressive agents and supportive care is essential to improve outcomes.

Introduction

Toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) is a severe form of a type IVc delayed hypersensitivity reaction. It involves cytotoxic T lymphocytes and natural killer cells, which induce apoptosis in keratinocytes. 1 This process occurs through the release of substances such as perforin, granzyme B, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and granulysin. These factors play a crucial role in causing extensive damage to the epidermis, leading to significant keratinocyte cell death and complete epidermal necrosis. According to the 1993 formal consensus definition, TEN is characterized by substantial skin loss involving more than 30% of the total body surface area (BSA).1–3

Recent case reports indicate that TEN is most commonly triggered by medications, particularly sulfonamide antibiotics such as trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (TMP–SMX), as well as penicillins, cephalosporins, quinolones (e.g. ciprofloxacin), anticonvulsants (such as carbamazepine, lamotrigin, and phenytoin), oxicam nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (e.g. piroxicam), and less frequently by drugs such as allopurinol, nevirapine, paracetamol, and certain biologics (e.g. infliximab). 1 Infections may also cause the disease, and some cases remain idiopathic. 4 Here, we describe a case of TEN in an 85-year-old patient that occurred 2 days after initiating self-administered TMP–SMX.

Case description

An 85-year-old female patient presented to our emergency department with progressive oral lesions over the past 5 days, which severely impaired her ability to eat and drink. In addition, she experienced intense pain in the genital area.

On admission, the patient was fully oriented, hemodynamically stable (arterial blood pressure 132/84 mmHg, heart rate 90 beats/min), and afebrile (temperature 36.7 °C). Physical examination revealed confluent erythematous maculopapular eruptions (Figure 1(a)) affecting more than 60% of her BSA, accompanied by a positive Nikolsky sign and widespread areas of denuded skin, including involvement of the oral, vaginal, and anal mucosa. Vaginal and anal mucosal involvement is illustrated in Figure 1(b) and (c), respectively. This occurred following self-administered TMP–SMX for a urinary tract infection. Two days after initiating TMP–SMX, she developed painful mucosal symptoms, including stomatitis and cheilitis, followed in the subsequent days by extensive efflorescences, ill-defined targetoid lesions, and blister formation.

Prominent mucosal involvement is evident with widespread erosive and denuded lesions of the vaginal mucosa (b) and anal mucosa (c).

Her medical history is noteworthy for non-small cell adenocarcinoma of the right lower lung lobe, for which a lower lobectomy with mediastinal lymphadenectomy was performed ~2 years earlier. She also presents with chronic, euvolemic, hypotonic hyponatremia, most likely in the context of the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion. Additional comorbidities include arterial hypertension, significant malnutrition, and neurocognitive impairment, as well as a known allergy to fluoride (Elmex gel®). In the same year, the patient underwent a transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI).

The medications taken continuously until hospital admission included pantoprazole (considering a history of gastroesophageal reflux), osmotic laxatives (for stool regulation), amlodipine and irbesartan (for arterial hypertension), aspirin (considering the history of TAVI), oxazepam and pregabalin (for restless legs syndrome).

Laboratory assessment, including a complete blood count, revealed elevated inflammatory markers C-reactive protein (CRP 34 mg/L), electrolyte disturbances with chronic hypotonic hyponatremia (sodium 126 mmol/L), and mild cytopenia, including lymphopenia and neutropenia (0.9 and 1.5 × 109/L, respectively). No increases in transaminase levels or cholestatic parameters were observed. A baseline arterial blood gas highlighted a mild metabolic acidosis (pH 7.32, HCO3− 18 mmol/L). Microbial cultures of cutaneous secretions and blood culture were both negative.

Based on age, BSA involvement, and a history of malignancy, the TEN severity score (SCORTEN) was estimated at 3, corresponding to a predicted mortality of 35%. 2 We initiated systemic glucocorticoid therapy with intravenous methylprednisolone (40 mg daily for 7 days), along with fluid resuscitation. The decision to use methylprednisolone 40 mg daily in our patient is based on the available evidence on TEN management, to mitigate the inflammatory response and prevent progression of skin and mucosal damage. The 40 mg daily dose represents a mid-range approach—low enough to reduce the risk of complications while still providing adequate anti-inflammatory effect. 5 Given the patient’s specific case, age, and comorbidities, this dose was deemed appropriate to manage the inflammatory cascade, with close monitoring for potential adverse effects.

In addition, supportive measures included opiate analgesics and topical antiseptics. Progression of the skin rash ceased within 4 days, and the dose of methyl-prednisolone was gradually tapered. The findings from the skin biopsy, along with the clinical presentation, confirmed the diagnosis. The patient remained stable throughout hospitalization. By the time of discharge, dermatological findings had significantly improved, and the skin was in the re-epithelialization phase.

A report of an adverse drug reaction was submitted to the Department of Clinical Pharmacology and Toxicology. Therefore, the potential drug interactions between the patient’s medications and TMP–SMX were analyzed. It was also reported that TEN is listed as a known adverse drug reaction without specified frequency for amlodipine and pantoprazole; for aspirin and pregabalin, TEN is listed as a rare adverse drug reaction; and for osmotic laxatives, oxazepam, and irbesartan, TEN is not listed as an adverse drug reaction. There is a temporal relationship between the administration of TMP–SMX and the onset of TEN. The improvement of symptoms after discontinuation represents a positive dechallenge. Moreover, given that only TMP–SMX had been newly initiated, the other medications appear less likely to be etiologically involved.

Discussion

TEN comprises a spectrum of severe mucocutaneous adverse reactions, typically caused by drug exposure, with life-threatening acute effects and serious long-term sequelae such as blindness.2,3,6 Patients with TEN have a mean loss of life expectancy of ~9 years. 2

Bastuji-Garin et al. identified seven independent predictors of mortality and developed the SCORTEN severity score. Key prognostic variables include age over 40 years, the presence of malignancy, a heart rate exceeding 120 beats/min, initial epidermal detachment >10%, serum urea levels above 10 mmol/L, serum glucose levels above 14 mmol/L, and serum bicarbonate levels below 20 mmol/L.1,7

Among antibiotic-associated TEN, sulfonamides account for 32% of cases, followed by penicillins (22%), cephalosporins (11%), fluoroquinolones (4%), and macrolides (2%).8,9 TMP–SMX is a widely used sulfonamide antibiotic with well-documented associations with severe cutaneous adverse reactions. Food and Drug Administration-approved indications include urinary tract infections, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, and toxoplasmosis, among others. 10

The typical presentation of sulfonamide-induced TEN is characterized by a prodrome, a specific rash, mucous membrane lesions, and systemic involvement. 11 Prodromal symptoms such as malaise, fever, and upper respiratory tract issues often precede skin eruptions by several days. Early lesions present as round macules with two indistinct, flat zones known as “atypical targets.” These evolve into a painful erythematous rash with bullae and erosions, starting on the face and trunk and spreading to the extremities. The Nikolsky sign (indicating epidermal detachment by gentle pressure) is typically positive. Almost all patients experience mucosal involvement of the eyes, oral cavity, and genital tract.3,12

The pathophysiology of TEN involves drug-induced T-cell activation, leading to extensive keratinocyte apoptosis. 2 Genetic factors, including polymorphisms in cytochrome P450 enzymes, may influence susceptibility.1–3

In our patient, the development of TEN following TMP–SMX may be linked to reduced drug metabolism because of immunosenescence, leading to the accumulation of reactive metabolites. In addition, her history of malignancy may have contributed to a dysregulated immune response and increased susceptibility to severe hypersensitivity reactions.

Clinical research indicates a median latency period of ~7–14 days between the initiation of TMP–SMX and the onset of dermatological findings, although some severe cases have been reported as early as 2 days after initiation. 9 In patients with suspected TEN, TMP–SMX and any concomitantly administered drugs known to cause TEN should be discontinued immediately.

The causal relationship between TMP–SMX and TEN is well established 13 ; however, data on geriatric patients are limited. Lipozencic et al. reported TMP–SMX-induced TEN in an 86-year-old man within the first 24 h of drug administration; notably, in a patient with a history of allergic reaction to TMP–SMX. 14 Lyon et al. described an 81-year-old male who developed Stevens–Johnson syndrome 6 days after TMP–SMX initiation and was successfully treated with dexamethasone and supportive care.15,16 In addition, Carmichael and Tan reported a fatal case of TMP–SMX-associated TEN in a 69-year-old patient. 17 Unfortunately, to the best of our knowledge, larger studies on TEN do not stratify patients by age. 18

However, not all severe TMP–SMX-associated skin reactions are TEN. Baltazard et al. reported a 63-year-old woman treated with TMP–SMX for P. jirovecii infection who developed a generalized maculopapular rash with herpetiform and rosette-like lesions, as well as tense bullae with a positive Nikolsky sign. Skin biopsy revealed a subepidermal bulla, and direct immunofluorescence showed linear immunoglobulin A (IgA) deposition along the basement membrane zone. Consequently, a diagnosis of TEN was excluded and replaced with TMP–SMX-induced linear IgA bullous dermatosis. 19 Available evidence suggests that a prior history of hypersensitivity to TMP–SMX may be a common predisposing factor for the development of TEN. In addition, variables such as advanced age, underlying infections, and rapid onset of symptoms after drug exposure appear to be associated with increased risk.20–22

High-level evidence is lacking for any single therapeutic intervention 2 ; therefore, supportive care—including fluid replacement, infection prevention, pain management, and nutritional support—remains the mainstay of TEN treatment. 1 Depending on severity, the patient may be transferred to a burn center or intensive care unit. 3 A European multicenter retrospective study and a recent meta-analysis of observational studies 3 indicated that prompt initiation of high-dose corticosteroids within 1–2 days of symptom onset leads to improved outcomes, although other evidence suggests that corticosteroids are associated with an increased risk of infection.

In recent meta-analyses, cyclosporine has been shown to reduce mortality rates and promote faster re-epithelialization and lesion stabilization. Zhang et al. noted that the efficacy of intravenous immunoglobulin and TNF-α inhibitors remains uncertain, highlighting the need for treatment decisions tailored to the patient’s clinical condition. 23

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for TEN in elderly patients with polypharmacy who present with acute mucocutaneous reactions following recent medication use. Clinicians should remain vigilant for cutaneous adverse reactions in patients receiving sulphonamides and consider alternative antibiotics for those with a history of skin reactions to TMP–SMX or at higher risk of developing severe cutaneous reactions. This case also emphasizes the critical need for public awareness regarding the dangers of self-medication with antibiotics, especially in the elderly population. Reporting serious adverse drug reactions to national pharmacovigilance centers is vital to inform public health initiatives.
