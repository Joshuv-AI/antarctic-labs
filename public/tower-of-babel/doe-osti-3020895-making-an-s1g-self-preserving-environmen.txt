 ABSTRACT

Environmental DNA (eDNA) sampling of aquatic environments is a powerful new tool for resource
managers to use in detecting the presence of rare and endangered species. The use of eDNA for species
detection is quickly becoming an industry-standard methodology in environmental science, and the
technology sector is moving quickly to create purpose-built tools for eDNA sampling. Smith-Root, Inc.
pioneered the new technology of self-preserving eDNA filters that automatically preserve the DNA
captured on filters by desiccating the internal filter membrane. This capability saves the sampling
technician significant time in the field and drastically reduces the potential for sample contamination. One
main constraint of this new technology, however, is the necessity of single-use plastics for eDNA
sampling. Single-use designs are employed because sampling equipment must be devoid of potential
contaminating DNA, and the time required to sterilize equipment is often not cost-effective for
practitioners. Thus, this technology is creating a significant new source of plastic waste—something that
eDNA practitioners, and society, would like to avoid. This project explores the use of alternative bio-
based polymers and composites as replacements for incumbent industrial standards for eDNA filtration
systems, with an emphasis on ease of processing and biodegradable options. 8 candidate materials, 3 for
the top filter housing and 5 for the bottom filter housing, were investigated to replace incumbent housing
materials. Of the 8 candidates, 2 materials were identified for future investigation which met performance
requirements, were bio-based and could potentially offer better disposal options like soil degradation or
composting, features sought by the customers of these products. The top housing candidate, material 1,
has comparable performance to the incumbent material and equally manufacturable. The bottom housing
candidate, material 6, improved desiccation performance by 81% after 1 hour in comparison to the
incumbent material, is 100% bio-based, compostable and can be sourced domestically throughout the
U.S.A.

Tensile strength and modulus were target properties identified by the partner company for both the top

material options 1–3 appear viable; however, material 2 was considered poor for material processing and

1.2.2 Material Desiccation Testing and
 Thermal Transitions

Another target property for performance used in the material down-selection process is the desiccation
performance. Figure 4 shows the ability of the materials to desiccate at time points of 1, 2, 6, and
24 hours. In this case, most of the data were collected only on alternative material options 5–8, as material
option 4 was previously found to be of poor performance and the filter housing bottom is the primary
driver of filter desiccation. The desiccation experiments were performed by running 1 L of deionized
water through a Smith-Root eDNA filter assembly, then placing the filter into a plastic bag containing
155 g of the alternative material and waiting the designated amount of time. The filter was then placed
through a drying procedure in which it was heated to 80°C for 10 minutes to ensure water removal from
the filter; the filter was weighed before and after drying to determine the water content. Ideal desiccation
performance would allow for most of the water in the filter to be removed during the 1–24 hours it was
buried in the material of choice so that the moisture content noted throughout Figure 4 would be low. As
seen in Figure 4 a–b, material 5 has a higher moisture content than either the incumbent material or
material options 6–8 for short time periods (i.e., 1 and 2 hours) but performs better than the incumbent
material at long time periods (i.e., 6 and 24 hours). A fast-acting desiccant better preserves sample DNA.
Thus, material 5 is a poor option moving forward due to the slower rate of drying. Materials 6–8 exceed
the performance of the incumbent material and are therefore considered viable for desiccation properties.
Material 6 had the lowest moisture content of the 3 as it resulted in an 81% improvement in desiccation
compared to incumbent B (Figure 4b). It is notable that a previously opened batch of material (e.g.,
potentially past its shelf life) and a newly opened batch (brand new material) of the incumbent material
were tested for this quality (Figure 4c), and there appears to be no difference in their performance. This
consistency indicates a high degree of accuracy for all data shown here.

 5

In the current design, only materials in the filter bottom are required to have high desiccation
performance; however, the alternative materials for the filter top were also tested for desiccation
performance at a short time period of 1 hour (Figure 4d). Interestingly, material 3 appears to have a high
desiccation capability, and it even exceeds the performance of the incumbent material for the filter
bottom. A potential implication of this observation is that self-desiccation could be built into the filter
housing top as well or in place of the lower housing, thus providing additional design options/choices for
the company partner.

For any polymer material processed via injection molding, it is critical to understand the thermal
properties—of which the thermal degradation limits, the glass transition temperature (Tg), and the melt
temperature (Tm) are of particular interest. Figure 5a and 5b show the thermal degradation properties for
each material as measured via thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), where the Tonset indicates the
temperature at which a material begins to see significant mass loss as recorded by the intercept between
the linear slopes of the initial drop in mass. Figure 5a shows that all material options considered as
replacements for the filter bottom housing meet or exceed the thermal stability of the incumbent material.
However, material options 1–3 are compared against an incumbent A material that could not be sourced
or tested. It is worth noting that material 3 clearly has the lowest thermal stability, which would require
lower temperature processing (≤ 189 °C), but it is difficult to decouple what mass loss is due to water
present in the material, which is expected due to its previously noted highly hygroscopic tendencies. No
materials were eliminated from consideration due to thermal stability alone.

1.2.4 Fish DNA Amplification Testing

The filter housing is used to collect fish DNA to study aquatic health information such as biodiversity and
population. Therefore, the filter housing should be inert and non-toxic to ensure accuracy in collected
DNA. For this reason, fish DNA and bacterial DNA amplification studies were performed. The method
for the collection of DNA, in brief, is as follows. Water samples were collected from a 200-gallon fish
tank with flow-through dechlorinated tap water in the Aquatic Ecology Laboratory at Oak Ridge National
Laboratory. The tank contained one 3-year-old channel catfish (Ictalurus punctatus). Triplicate 1 L
beakers were used to collect water from the tank. In each of the replicate sets, 10 pellets of a single test
material were placed into the water. A set of triplicates without pelleted material served as a control. A
total of 9 pelleted materials were tested, for a total of 27 test beakers, plus 3 control beakers. Each 1 L
beaker of water was filtered using a 5-micron self-desiccating filter (Smith-Root, Inc.) with the tripod
wand attachment on a Smith-Root eDNA backpacker. After water filtering, the filter and filter backing
were removed from the filter housing and placed into the original 5-micron self-desiccating filter bag,
which is supplied as the current external packaging of Smith-Root’s self-desiccating filters. In each of the
bags, the filter and filter backing were submerged in the same material pellets that were in their respective
beakers. Bags were then sealed and left at room temperature for 4 days prior to DNA extraction. Filters
were homogenized with an MPBio FastPrep 24 5G bead beater with four cycles of 30 s homogenization
followed by a 4 min rest. Lysate was then processed with a Zymo Quick-DNA Miniprep Plus Kit
following manufacturer protocol.

After DNA collection, extracted DNA was amplified with MiFishU 12S rRNA gene primers [1] tagged
with Illumina Nextera sequencing adapters, which are specific to fish taxon but frequently cross-amplify
the bacterial 16S rRNA gene as well. Negative control reactions (HyClone Water) were included in all
amplification sets. Amplifications were performed on an Eppendorf Mastercycler in final volumes of
25 μL: 12.5 μL AmpliTaq Gold 360 master mix (Applied Biosystems), 1.25 μL of each primer (10 μM),
8 μL HyClone water, and 2 μL of eDNA template following these conditions: 95C 5 min, 45 cycles
[98C for 20 s, 60C for 30 s, 72C for 30 s], 72C for 10 min, 4C hold. Both the amplicon sizes
(~280 bp for fish and ~350 bp for bacteria) and concentrations were determined on Agilent TapeStation.
DNA amplification concentrations were analyzed with a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with
post hoc Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) tests to determine which pairs of polymer and
library type were significantly different from each other. ANOVA determines if there is a difference in
mean amplified eDNA concentration among polymer and library types, but not which ones are different.
The Tukey's HSD test is a multi-comparison statistic that weights the number of comparisons therefore
giving a more conservative estimate of mean differences rather than conducting individual t-tests which
could result in overinflated statistical significances amongst the pairwise comparisons.

The amplified DNA concentrations varied significantly across pellet material types (fish versus bacteria),
F = 4.92, p < 0.001, with the highest fish amplicon concentration and lowest bacterial amplification given
by material 1, followed by material 6, 8, and 9; see Figure 7 and Table 2. Only the pairwise comparisons
which were significant (<0.05) and close to significant (<0.10) are provided in Table 2.A desirable result

 8

is a low bacterial amplification with high fish eDNA amplification, which indicates that the fish eDNA is
being preserved. Three potential mechanisms could explain the variation in amplification observed across
materials: (1) rapid desiccation in some materials limits bacterial degradation and better preserves fish
eDNA; (2) chemical interactions between certain materials and fish eDNA cause degradation, resulting in
reduced recovery and low amplification; or (3) slow desiccation rates in some materials allow bacteria to
degrade fish eDNA, similarly resulting in reduced recovery and low amplification. The difference in
amplification herein is not due to the molecular methods used to collect and process the eDNA but, rather,
some aspect(s) of the material candidates chosen. Based on the eDNA amplification for bacteria and fish,
two material candidates are ineligible: material 3 and material 7. Material 3 had both low bacteria and fish
DNA amplification, while material 7 had a very high bacteria amplification.

1.3 IMPACTS

According to the 2020 Fisheries of the United States Report, domestic commercial fisheries are a $4.8
billion industry, of which aquaculture is a significant part. Additionally, it is reported that anglers caught
an estimated 1 billion fish in 2020 as part of recreational fishing. While this industry is clearly vital to the
domestic food supply chain, it is also just as critical to track and understand the health and population of
fish in our domestic waterways. One non-invasive method of tracking this is through eDNA, via water
sampling, which relies on capturing shed DNA of aquatic life within a body of water. However, to
provide a holistic viewpoint of the population, it is necessary to take multiple samples in different
locations, which requires a simple yet accurate sampling method that is not currently available on the
market. This project aims to increase accessibility and expand the market for these products while
improving performance, creating new disposal options for products, and advancing the state-of-the-art for
eDNA sampling.
Smith-Root, Inc., currently produces their eDNA filter to collect water samples for subsequent laboratory
testing. Their eDNA filter design revolutionized the market due to its ease of use, and the desiccating
material used to produce the filter housing slows microbial action and thereby preserves the captured
DNA. However, the current filter assembly uses non-domestically sourced materials, and there is
significant interest from the customer base in developing a product that offers a better end-of-life option
by avoiding landfilling. This project focused on prototyping and testing a new filter assembly to replace
the incumbent assembly. The goal is to reduce the impact of their product on landfills, create new
opportunities for the company to use US-based materials, and enhance their products’ performance.

1.5 CONCLUSIONS

The primary goal of this project was to explore alternative material options for the Smith-Root eDNA
filter housing top and bottom. The current product design employs a single-use material set, and the
incumbent materials do not have a favorable end-of-life: the components are primarily disposed of in
landfills. Consumers of the product are interested in alternative options, such as the recycling,
composting, or degradation of the product at its end of use, but these features are not currently built into
the s1G filter housing. Essential to the filter housing’s operation is the self-preservation feature, which
enables robust preservation of eDNA collected from water sources. Additional criteria for material
evaluation were bio-based content, mechanical properties, ease of manufacturing, desiccating
performance, and fish DNA amplification. A total of 3 and 5 material alternatives were considered for the
top and bottom housing, respectively; one best-performing material was selected for the top (material 1)
and bottom (material 6) housing based on the down-selection criteria noted previously. For example,
material 1 candidate for the filter top has comparable performance and manufacturability to the incumbent
A material and features 35% bio-based content in comparison to the incumbent material. While of lower
importance compared to performance properties, bio-based content in material options was identified as a
desirable attribute based on customer interest in natural and bio-degradable materials. In addition, the
newly proposed material 6 as a filter bottom allows for an 81% increase in desiccation performance after
1 hour in comparison to the incumbent B material. Identifying a material with superior performance in
desiccation and reduced bacteria amplification are impactful outcomes because it increases the material
options for these applications and other like it.
