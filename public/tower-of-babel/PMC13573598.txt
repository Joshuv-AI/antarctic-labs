Escalating loneliness among Chinese American older adults in an affordable housing community: a repeat cross-sectional study (2012–2025)
Authors: Jingyuan Liu, Shinyi Wu, Crystal Kwan, Iris Chi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesLoneliness is a significant public health problem, particularly among older immigrant adults. The study investigates the temporality of changes in loneliness among Chinese American older adults living in affordable housing and factors influencing these changes within the context of broader societal transformations.Research and MethodsUsing a repeated cross-sectional design, two independent cohorts of Chinese American older adults in the same affordable housing complex were surveyed in 2012 (N = 120) and in 2025 (N = 118). The analyses were conducted in three phases: descriptive statistics summarized the sample characteristics; the Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition assessed whether loneliness scores varied between cohorts and examined the observed differences into explained and unexplained components; and ad hoc multivariate regression analysis examined the erosion of social support as the main factor driving the temporal change of loneliness.ResultsThe Blinder–Oaxaca decomposition revealed a significant rise in loneliness from 2012 (M = 3.99) to 2025 (M = 5.18; p = .002). Reductions in social support (β = –0.52; p = .020) and fewer children (β = –0.64; p = .029) were the strongest contributors to this rise. Ad hoc analyses suggested that less physical contact with children and trouble using mobile devices correlated with declining social support across cohorts.Discussion and ImplicationsFindings indicate a dramatic increase in loneliness among Chinese American older adults in affordable housing over time. The results highlight the imperative to enhance alternative sources of connection and culturally tailored interventions for supporting social engagement. Promoting peer relationships, digital inclusion, and flexible intergenerational support may help reduce increasing loneliness and enhance well-being in later life.

This repeat-cross-sectional study examines Chinese American older adults residing in affordable housing, surveyed in 2012 and 2025. The results show that loneliness has risen steeply over the last 13 years, and this increase is not driven by greater functional impairment or mental health issues but by diminished social support and fewer children. The findings reveal an emerging challenge for immigrant older adults living in affordable housing programs. Community and policy interventions should prioritize developing and maintaining social support systems rather than focusing solely on physical or mental health outcomes.

Loneliness in later life

As the world grows older, loneliness has quietly emerged as one of the most pressing public health challenges. Recent studies have highlighted the heightened vulnerability to loneliness of the older population (Ezeokonkwo et al., 2021; Surkalim et al., 2022). A recent systematic review spanning 113 countries demonstrates that, irrespective of whether a country’s overall prevalence of loneliness is low or high, older adults (≥60 years) consistently report higher rates of loneliness compared to younger age groups (Surkalim et al., 2022). For instance, in Northern Europe—the region with the lowest observed prevalence—5.2% of older adults experience loneliness, compared to 2.9% of young adults (18–29 years) and 2.7% of middle-aged adults (30–59 years) (Surkalim et al., 2022). In Eastern Europe, where loneliness is most prevalent, the rate rises to 21.3% among older adults, versus 7.5% and 9.6% among young and middle-aged adults, respectively (Surkalim et al., 2022). This persistent age gradient in loneliness carries significant implications for the well-being, mental health, and mortality of older populations (Bu et al., 2020; Rico-Uribe et al., 2018).

Chronic loneliness can activate dysregulated stress responses, resulting in increased inflammation and a higher risk of cardiovascular disorders and Alzheimer’s disease (Bu et al., 2020). In support of this pathway, Van Bogart et al. (2022) suggested that even increased inflammation can be a central biological mechanism that mediates loneliness and greater disease burden and mortality risk in later life. In addition to its physical toll, loneliness poses serious risks to mental health. Susło et al. (2023) reported that perceived loneliness relates to worse quality of life and greater symptoms of depression, demonstrating the harmful effects of loneliness on psychological well-being. Among older immigrants, acculturative stress and reduced intergenerational support may exacerbate these effects and increase their susceptibility to emotional distress (Jeong et al., 2022; Vang & Sieng, 2019).

Unique vulnerabilities of Chinese American older adults

One such population is Chinese American older adults, whose experiences of loneliness are shaped not only by immigration-related challenges but also by cultural factors. A recent analysis of the Population Study of Chinese older adults in Chicago reveals that social disengagement significantly predicts loneliness among this population, with immigration experiences amplifying this risk through multiple pathways (Tang et al., 2025). Immigration fundamentally disrupts social networks—older adults lose established friendships, familiar community spaces, and professional connections that provided meaning and structure to their daily lives. Language barriers further compound this isolation, limiting not only social interactions but also access to healthcare, community services, and increasingly digital resources that many older adults rely on for connection (Hu & Xu, 2022; Xu et al, 2023).

Cultural factors add another layer of complexity to loneliness among Chinese American older adults. In a cross-national study of older adults in China and the United States, Poulin et al. (2012) found that although the two groups reported similar levels of perceived social support, the association between family support and loneliness was stronger among older adults in China than among older adults in the United States. This pattern suggests that family relationship disruptions may be especially salient in Chinese cultural contexts. Such cultural emphasis on family interdependence can become a vulnerability when geographic distance, work demands, or acculturation gaps limit intergenerational contact. Traditional collectivist values and filial piety-a cultural norm that emphasizes adult children’s duty to care for and support their aging parents- that historically provided robust support systems have been disrupted by modernization and migration, leaving many Chinese American older adults navigating a gap between cultural ideals and contemporary realities (Dong et al., 2012; Xiao et al., 2024). The expectation of family-centered care, while potentially protective when fulfilled, can intensify feelings of abandonment when adult children adopt more individualistic approaches to family obligations (Kim, 2021; Zhang et al., 2021). These cultural tensions, combined with structural barriers to community engagement, create a unique constellation of risk factors that distinguish the loneliness experiences of Chinese American older adults from both their White American peers and older adults aging in China.

Dynamic challenges in a changing society

Between 2012 and 2025, a growing technological landscape and changing family structure have dramatically altered the social support for older adults in society. Platforming technologies for digital communication, including virtual video calls and social media networks, can help close the gaps in intergenerational bonds and improve digital interactivity (Canedo-García et al., 2021). However, older adults with relatively low digital literacy (especially in the immigrant population) are systematically disadvantaged in light of such cutting-edge technology (Au et al., 2024). Moreover, Chinese American older immigrants experience increasing barriers, such as limited English proficiency and a lack of culturally adapted digital services, which exacerbate the digital divide, further consolidating their isolation in an increasingly electronic society (Lin et al., 2023).

Concurrently, diminishing family size, exemplified by the ‘4-2-1’ family configuration, with just one child supporting two parents and four grandparents, common for many Chinese families, has increased caregiving demands and reduced the number of adult children for both emotional and instrumental support (Hui et al., 2022). As the adult children face challenging working schedules, geographical distance, and shifting cultural norms, the traditional expectation of familial caregiving or filial piety becomes increasingly difficult to fulfill. Thus, such loss of family-based support, which may be combined with barriers to community support resources, leads to a precarious landscape of support for aging Chinese immigrants (Gao et al., 2022).

The Chinese American older adult population living in affordable housing in Los Angeles makes for a powerful case example of how these macro-level shifts intersect with micro-level lived experiences. Their experiences illustrate how board demographic and technological transformations redefine loneliness in the later life of Chinese Americans.

Significance and contribution of this study

Despite the far-reaching impact of loneliness on older adults and the intensifying societal shifts brought about by technological advancement, changing family structures, and cultural dislocation, little is known about how loneliness levels vary across time, particularly among older adults living in affordable senior housing. Existing studies rely largely on cross-sectional designs, making it difficult to examine how changes in broader society may influence the lived experiences of older populations. Additionally, most extant work has been conducted with community-dwelling older adults, giving marginal information about older adults living in affordable senior housing (Di Perna et al., 2022; Taylor et al., 2018). Such settings may involve distinct structural constraints and social milieus that contribute to older adults’ experiences of support and isolation. In addition, immigrant older adults remain underexposed in the literature, especially older adults in less financially well-off households, despite being faced with extra stressors, like language barriers or cultural adjustment, and weakened intergenerational bonds (Baek et al., 2022). However, it is these at-risk groups, especially low-income and immigrant older adults in affordable housing, who most often receive aging-related policies, services, and interventions. Yet little is known about how their social lives evolve over the years amid broader demographic and technological change. To fill these gaps, this study utilizes a repeated cross-sectional approach to assess the extent of variation between two cohorts of immigrant Chinese American older adults living in affordable housing in Los Angeles, surveyed in 2012 and 2025. This study examines whether levels of loneliness have shifted over the years and which factors or mechanisms might explain that shift. This approach provides a timely opportunity to gain more insight into the changing perceptions of aging immigrant communities as society evolves.

Theoretical framework

The current study is guided by the social-ecological system framework. Originally proposed by Bronfenbrenner (1979), this framework emphasizes the dynamic interplay between individuals and multiple layers of their surroundings. As presented in Figure 1, predictors of loneliness among older adults can be organized across different ecological levels. At the microsystem level are individual characteristics, including age, gender, education, physical functioning, and symptoms of depression. The mesosystem encompasses interpersonal relationships, such as marital status, number of children, and the frequency of in-person contact. The exosystemic reflects community-level supports, for example, religious engagement and digital exclusion, while the macrosystem captures broader societal and structural conditions, such as economic status. Together, these factors illustrate how loneliness arises from the interaction of individual, relational, community, and structural influences, a view consistent with existing research. For instance, higher physical functioning and better health status have been longitudinally linked to lower loneliness among older adults over 8 years of follow-up in the English Longitudinal Study of Aging (Philip et al., 2020). Interpersonally, frequent contact with adult children and marital status have been shown to buffer increases in loneliness over time (Franssen et al., 2020; Niu et al., 2018). At the community level, engagement in religious activities and spirituality is associated with reduced loneliness and improved life satisfaction among aging adults in India (Muhammad et al., 2023). Finally, utilization of communication technologies—such as video calls and videoconferencing—has been experimentally demonstrated to reduce loneliness in older adults living in care settings (Zamir et al., 2018).

Method

Data source and study sample

This study was conducted at “Housing Complex G,” an independent-living senior community located in Los Angeles County, California. Housing Complex G operates under the regulations of the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) Section 8 program, which provides rental assistance to low-income older adults (HUD, n.d.-a). The facility is owned and managed by a nonprofit organization dedicated to supporting low-income Chinese American older immigrants.

This study employs a repeated cross-sectional design, with data collected at two time points: 2012 and 2025. The research was carried out in partnership with the nonprofit organization of Housing Complex G and an American university’s school of social work (the name is withheld for review). A comprehensive survey was conducted at both time points (2012 & 2025) among residents of the senior community, covering demographic characteristics, physical conditions, mental health status, and other relevant domains of aging and well-being. Two samples were recruited from the same site. In 2012, all 144 residents were invited to participate, and 120 residents completed the survey, yielding a response rate of 83%. In 2025, all 139 residents were invited, and 118 residents completed the survey (85% response rate). Preliminary analyses suggest that 29 participants may have appeared in both the 2012 and 2025 survey waves due to their continued residence in Housing Complex G. These individuals were retained in both samples. In repeated cross-sectional designs, the primary objective is to maintain good sample representation to produce unbiased estimates for each time period (Steel, 2008). Since each wave surveyed nearly the entire resident population (response rates of 83% and 85%, respectively), the overlapping participants are naturally occurring members of both target populations. Excluding them would compromise the representativeness of the 2025 sample by removing long-term residents whose continued presence reflects the reality of aging in place in public housing communities (Wiles et al., 2012).

In both survey waves, all participants were aged 62 years or older and were low-income Chinese immigrants. The 62-year age threshold reflects federal eligibility criteria for senior housing programs administered by the HUD, which requires at least one household member to be 62 or older (U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development, n.d.-b). This uniformity in admission criteria across both samples ensures comparability; thus, it strengthens the validity of comparisons between the two cohorts.

Variables

The main outcome of interest is the older residents’ level of loneliness. The independent variables are self-reported economic status, number of children, frequency of in-person meetings with children, frequency of phone calls with children, functional telephone utilization ability, marital status, physical health status, religious affiliation, and depressive symptoms. Control variables were age, gender, and education level of participants.

Measures

Outcome variable: loneliness

Loneliness was assessed with the De Jong Gierveld Loneliness Scale (de Jong Gierveld & van Tilburg, 2006), which comprises six items. Items 1–3 are negatively worded, while items 4–6 are positively worded. For negatively worded items, responses are scored as Yes = 2, More or less = 1, and No = 0. For positively worded items 4–6, scoring is reversed: Yes = 0, More or less = 1, and No = 2. The total score ranges from 0 to 12, with higher scores indicating a greater level of loneliness. The De Jong Gierveld Loneliness Scale has been consistently validated across various cultural groups and different populations, which include older adults (Leung et al., 2008; Rahman et al., 2019).

Independent and control variables

Social support

Social support was assessed using the Lubben Social Network Scale–6 (LSNS-6), a validated measure widely used among older adults. The LSNS-6 consists of six items designed to evaluate both the size and frequency of social interactions with family members and friends. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 0 to 24. Higher scores reflect stronger social support. This instrument has shown robust psychometric properties in diverse older adult samples (Lubben et al., 2006; Miyashita et al., 2019).

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were measured using the shortened, validated 9-item version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D; Kohout et al., 1993). Each item of CES-D asks respondents how often they had depressive feelings or symptoms in the past week, scored from 0 (rarely or none of the time) to 2 (most or all the time). Total scores range from 0 to 18, with higher scores indicating greater depressive symptoms. CES-D is specifically designed for use in older populations and has been validated in cross-cultural studies (Carlson et al., 2011).

Physical health status

Physical health status was determined on the basis of functional self-care abilities as measured by the Activities of Daily Living (ADL) scale (Katz et al., 1970). ADL evaluates individuals’ abilities to independently perform seven essential duties of self-care, including bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, continence, and feeding. Each activity is scored on a 3-point scale (0 = independent, 1 = requires partial assistance, and 2 = completely dependent), generating a total score ranging from 0 to 14. Higher scores represent greater functional impairment.

Demographic and socioeconomic variables

Participants’ self-reported economic status was measured categorically as: (1) quite sufficient, (2) just enough, and (3) having some difficulties. The number of children was recorded as a ratio variable. Frequency of in-person meetings with children was measured using a 3-point ordinal scale: (1) several times per year or less, (2) once or several times per month, and (3) at least once per week. Similarly, the frequency of phone contacts with children was assessed using a 4-point ordinal scale: (1) once a month or less, (2) several times per month, (3) at least once per week, and (4) almost every day. Functional social interaction ability was assessed through a single-item measure of telephone utilization ability, which was scored as (1) independent, (2) needs help, and (3) unable to use independently.

Marital status was classified into four categories: (1) married, (2) divorced, (3) widowed, and (4) single or never married. Religious engagement was measured by the affiliation coded as a binary variable (0 = no religion, 1 = has religion). Age was measured continuously in years at the time of the survey, and gender was recorded as male or female. Educational attainment was categorized into five hierarchical groups: (1) never attended school, (2) elementary school, (3) middle school, (4) community college, and (5) university or above.

Data analyses

The analyses were carried out in three steps. First, descriptive statistics were used to summarize the sample characteristics. Frequencies and percentages were reported for categorical variables. Means and standard deviations were calculated for ordinal and ratio variables. Second, the Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition method was implemented to examine whether levels of loneliness significantly differed between the 2012 and 2025 cohorts, and the degree to which observed differences might be attributed to differences in population characteristics (explained) vs differences in the effects of those characteristics (unexplained). This approach enables the identification of the main sources responsible for any observed disparities in loneliness across the two cohorts (Blinder, 1973; Oaxaca, 1973). In addition, ad hoc multivariate linear regression models were conducted to delve into the mechanisms that correlated with changes in loneliness. Specifically, these models explored how changes in key predictors (i.e., levels of social support) were associated with changes in loneliness, and whether these associations differed between cohorts. All analyses were performed using Stata 17.0. Statistical significance was determined at the p < .05 level.

Educational attainment showed noticeable differences. The proportion of participants who had never attended school decreased markedly from 7.5% in 2012 to 0.8% in 2025, while those who completed elementary (10.0% to 14.4%) and middle school education (37.5% to 42.4%) showed increased proportions. Marital status distributions were relatively stable, with approximately 45% married and around half of the participants widowed in both cohorts. Economic status showed some variation. The percentage of participants reporting a “quite sufficient” economic situation increased slightly from 11.7% in 2012 to 16.9% in 2025, while those reporting financial difficulties rose from 2.5% to 8.5%. Religious affiliation decreased from 59.2% to 40.7%, reflecting a shift toward non-religious identity in the later cohort.

Social interactions with children showed significant differences. Weekly or more frequent in-person meetings with children declined from 66.7% in 2012 to 49.2% in 2025, indicating reduced face-to-face contact over the period. Meanwhile, phone contact frequency remained stable, with approximately one-third in each cohort reporting almost daily calls.

As for health and psychosocial indicators, the average ADL scores were similar between the two cohorts (2.14 in 2012 vs 2.25 in 2025), indicating comparable levels of functional limitation. Similarly, depressive symptoms (CES-D) remained stable across cohorts, with scores averaging 8.02 (SD = 2.70) and 8.24 (SD = 3.21), respectively. However, loneliness scores (DJGL) significantly increased from 3.92 (SD = 2.75) in 2012 to 5.18 (SD = 2.87) in 2025, showing higher loneliness levels among participants in the 2025 cohort. This rise in loneliness level corresponded to a notable decrease in social support (LSNS), dropping from 8.12 (SD = 4.25) to 5.07 (SD = 3.73). In aggregate, these descriptive comparisons reveal relatively stable demographic characteristics across cohorts and marked shifts in social engagement and loneliness.

Variation in loneliness between cohorts

As shown in Table 2, the Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition analysis revealed a significant difference in loneliness scores between the 2012 and 2025 cohorts. The 2025 cohort reported a higher average loneliness (M = 5.18) compared to the former (M = 3.99), giving a mean difference of –1.19 (p = .002). This total difference came primarily from the explained component (endowments), which accounted for –1.16 points (p = .005), suggesting that changes in observed characteristics rather than in their effects cause most of the increase in loneliness. The unexplained component (coefficients) was smaller and not statistically significant (–0.44, p = .360), and so also was the interaction term (0.41, p = .427), demonstrating that the combined effect of differences in characteristics and in their effects did not play a meaningful role in explaining the gap. In terms of the dimension explained, two variables came forward as the most influential sources of the observed difference: social support evaluated using LSNS (β = –0.22, p = .030), and number of children (β = –0.64, p = .029). The negative coefficients suggest that a reduced level of social support and fewer children in the 2025 cohort were significantly associated with higher loneliness levels in the 2025 cohort.

Results of adhoc analyses: examining social support (LSNS) as a key factor in loneliness change

Ad hoc analyses were performed based on the results of the Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition to investigate the role of social support in driving cohort differences in loneliness (Table 3). The Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition found that changes in observed characteristics, rather than changes in their effects, were the key factors responsible for the significant increase in loneliness between the 2012 and 2025 cohorts. Importantly, social support (measured by LSNS scores) was identified as one of the two key factors contributing to the explained component of this cohort difference, alongside the number of children. Thus, additional multivariate linear regression analyses were conducted explicitly on LSNS scores as a key predictor. This permitted a more detailed exploration of how variations in social support levels between the 2012 and 2025 cohorts may have influenced the significant differences in loneliness outcomes.

In both 2012 and 2025, the ability to use the telephone and the frequency of meeting with children were key associates of social support. However, notable shifts in these relationships were observed between the two time points. In 2012, individuals requiring assistance with phone use (n = 15; β = –2.909, p = .026) or those fully dependent on others for phone use (n = 10; β = –4.129, p = .012) reported significantly lower LSNS scores compared to those who could use the phone independently. Meeting with children at least once per week was also positively associated with social support (β = 3.180, p = .046), compared to those who met their children only several times per year or less.

By 2025, the relationship between phone use ability and social support had shifted. Partial dependence on phone use (n = 36; β = –2.428, p = .004) remained a significant predictor of lower LSNS scores, but full dependence (n = 6) was no longer statistically significant. At the same time, the frequency of meeting with children emerged as a stronger and more consistent predictor of social support. Individuals who met with their children monthly (β = 3.007, p = .020), weekly (β = 3.769, p = .007), or more frequently (β = 4.104, p < .001) reported significantly higher LSNS scores compared to those with minimal contact.

Discussion

By comparing the residents of an affordable housing community in 2012 and 2025, this study utilized a repeated cross-sectional approach to examine changes in loneliness among Chinese American older adults. The two samples were recruited from the same housing community using the same eligibility criteria and survey methodology and demonstrated comparable demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, which enhances the validity and interpretability of the time-variability of comparisons. We found a marked increase in loneliness, with most of the change attributable to shifts in observed characteristics rather than alterations in their effects over time. In particular, decreases in the number of children and levels of social support were the greatest contributors to increasing loneliness. Notably, factors traditionally associated with loneliness in the existing literature, such as depressive symptoms, physical health, and economic status, did not explain the change (Hawkley & Kocherginsky, 2017; Lim et al., 2022). Such findings are likely because these characteristics remained relatively stable across cohorts. For example, in both 2012 and 2025, participants exhibited similar levels of depression (mean = 8.02 in 2012 vs 8.24 in 2025) and ADL scores (2.14 in 2012 vs 2.25 in 2025). By focusing on what is associated with variation in loneliness rather than its static correlates, our study addresses a critical gap in the aging literature and highlights the dynamic nature of social and familial contexts in late life.

The decline in the number of children was a major contributor to the greater loneliness in 2025. This finding is consistent with previous research demonstrating that larger family size and more access to adult children are protective factors against loneliness in later life (van den Broek & Tosi, 2020). Within the social-ecological system framework, having fewer children not only represent fewer social connections and caregivers at the micro (individual) and meso (family) levels but also reflects broader demographic transitions—such as shrinking family sizes—at the macro (societal) level in Chinese and Chinese-diaspora communities (Huang et al., 2018). These patterns align with documented population trends: the U.S. total fertility rate declined from 3.7 in the 1960s to below 2.0 by the 2010s (Osterman et al., 2024), with Asian immigrants experiencing particularly sharp declines—birth rates falling from 76 to 57.2 births per 1,000 women between 2008 and 2019 (Zhao, 2025). As these demographic changes toward smaller families are long-term and are likely not to be reversed, it is important to identify supplementary sources of social connection and support that can protect older adults from the effects of potential decreased family support. Possible solutions include fostering peer relationships, expanding community-based social networks, and further increasing social workers’ and volunteers’ engagement in senior housing. These strategies may help compensate for reduced familial contact and mitigate the negative psychosocial consequences of demographic shifts (Conner et al., 2018; Lai et al., 2020).

The decline in social support emerged as another important correlate of the cohort difference. The ad hoc analysis also found that meeting in person with children was an even more prominent predictor of social support in 2025 than in 2012. While regular contact with children was consistently related to greater social support, its predictive power strengthened over time. This pattern may represent that fewer opportunities for in-person interaction became increasingly limited. For instance, the percentage of older adults who met their children weekly declined from 66.7% in 2012 to 43.2% in 2025, while those meeting only several times per year increased from 12.5% to 28%. Within the social-ecological system framework, this decline can be attributed to multiple interrelated factors operating across ecological levels: rising living costs in Los Angeles at the macro (societal/policy) level (Schmidt, 2022); increased geographic dispersion of families at the exo (community/environmental) level (Tang & Xie, 2021); economic and housing pressures at the meso (family/organizational) level that compel adult children to live farther from their parents. Moreover, shifting occupations and increasing intercity migration among adult children (at both the meso and exo levels) may further restrict opportunities for frequent visits (Tang & Xie, 2021). Given these multilayered structural barriers, it is important to explore alternative strategies to strengthen social support among older adults.

Furthermore, the influence of filial piety in Chinese culture may lead Chinese older adults to have high expectations for support from their children, making infrequent visits highly important for their well-being (Xiao et al., 2024). Although filial piety has long been regarded as a protective cultural resource, recent research indicates that unrealistic expectations could actually act as a psychological burden for both generations, potentially exacerbating older adults’ feelings of loneliness when children fail to meet their expectations (Li et al., 2024). Therefore, flexible, culturally sensitive models of intergenerational support that adapt to contemporary realities should be promoted to relieve some of this burden and build a more resilient network of social supports.

Competency in using mobile devices was another significant factor influencing social support. In 2012, telephone use was a significant determinant of social support. Older adults who needed help or were fully dependent on others for phone use experienced lower social support. In 2025, partial dependence remained a significant predictor, while full dependence was no longer a statistically significant predictor, possibly due to the small proportion of fully dependent users (5.2%). Remarkably, the proportion of older adults who needed help with mobile phone utilization increased from 12.5% in 2012 to 30.5% in 2025, indicating a widening digital divide. While digital devices have become a commonplace part of daily life, older adults often face barriers to adoption and use of those technologies (Jun et al., 2025). This is consistent with previous studies warning that older adults risk becoming a “digitally excluded” generation unless interventions are centered on older adults’ usability and accessibility (Money et al., 2024).

Practical suggestions and clinical implications

Findings of the present study suggest that practical and clinical responses should not only address the presence of loneliness but also prevent its exacerbation and slow its progression among Chinese American older adults in affordable housing. Specifically, drawing on the social-ecological system framework, measures should include interventions that target individual, family, community, and societal levels to address the multilayered factors contributing to the increase in loneliness.

At the individual (micro) level, strategies should aim to foster older adults’ digital competency and confidence. Interventions should enable them to maintain communication with both family and peers despite geographic distance. To reach this goal, accessible digital literacy training, ongoing technical support, and age-appropriate communication technologies are necessary. Additionally, culturally appropriate digital tools, such as AI-powered chatbots or virtual companions, also offer potential to sustain social ties and provide emotional support when in-person interactions are limited (Lestari et al., 2023).

At the family and organizational (meso) level, efforts should focus on stopping the erosion of social support and mitigating the impact of shrinking family sizes—two key processes identified in this study as associated with increased loneliness. Senior facilities for Chinese populations should be culturally focused, providing peer support through social activities tailored to the needs of older adults, facilitating intergenerational interactions, and supporting new friendships or fostering meaningful relationships beyond immediate kin. Bilingual, culturally competent social workers and community volunteers can play an essential role in reinforcing these support systems and helping identify residents at risk of isolation.

Broader structural contexts must be considered at the community (exo) and societal (macro) levels as well. Increased housing costs, work-related mobility, and geographic dispersion all limit intergenerational contact; hence, systematic action should be taken. These macro-level barriers could also be buffered through embedding social resources and digital infrastructure within affordable housing developments. At the same time, clinicians and policymakers should adopt flexible interpretations of traditional values such as filial piety, encouraging families to have more realistic expectations and recognize diverse forms of intergenerational connection that align with modern realities.

Although these recommendations are grounded in the cultural context of Chinese American older adults, the underlying dynamics of family change, digital divides, and social support erosion are shared by many immigrant and minority aging populations (Zhao et al., 2022). Future research should examine whether similar multilevel patterns grow in other ethnic communities and housing contexts to refine interventions that are both culturally and structurally responsive.

Conclusion

This study is the first to employ a repeat cross-sectional design spanning 2012 to 2025 to examine changes in loneliness among older adults in affordable housing. Our findings reveal a significant and troubling increase in loneliness, which underscores the urgent need for more attention from researchers, practitioners, and policymakers. Different from prior research focused on static risk factors for loneliness, our study highlights the importance of understanding how loneliness patterns change over time.

Specifically, reductions in social support and the number of children were identified as the two strongest factors underlying the increase in loneliness. Interpreted through the social-ecological system framework, these results reveal interconnected changes across multiple ecological levels—shifts in interpersonal relationships and family structures (micro- and meso-levels) occurring alongside broader demographic, economic, and policy contexts (exo- and macro-levels). This multilevel perspective situates our findings within a generalizable theoretical model, suggesting that parallel processes of social network contraction and structural constraint may also contribute to rising loneliness among other immigrant and minority older adults.

These insights have direct implications for practical intervention and policy, signaling the need to prioritize social support enhancement and adapt to the realities of shrinking family structures to prevent older adults from being silently consumed by loneliness. Finally, the growing trend of “solo aging” further underscores the urgency of developing robust community-based solutions to support those navigating later life with limited familial networks (Adams et al., 2022).

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study was conducted in a single affordable housing community of Chinese American older adults in Los Angeles, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other settings or populations. Nevertheless, this context offers valuable practical implications for similar low-income and immigrant communities and may also inform interventions for community-dwelling older adults living without their children. Second, some participants may have appeared in both survey waves due to continued residence in Housing Complex G. Although such overlap is consistent with the logic of repeated cross-sectional designs, future research may benefit from extending this work to larger-scale datasets where the likelihood of participant overlap is inherently minimized. Third, the repeated cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences; longitudinal research is needed to establish temporal relationships and potential mechanisms underlying loneliness changes in this population.

Importantly, although the LSNS includes both family and friendship ties, the main analyses relied on the total LSNS score rather than separate subscales. Therefore, our findings reflect a decline in overall perceived social support across cohorts, and the decomposition results identified the number of children and social support as the two factors most strongly associated with higher loneliness in 2025. In addition, ad hoc analyses suggested that in-person contact with children was more clearly associated with social support in 2025 than in 2012, highlighting the potential relevance of intergenerational family ties in this sample. Future studies should examine the LSNS family and friend subscales separately to clarify the relative roles of kin- and non-kin-based support in later-life loneliness. Finally, our measure of digital exclusion, while based on established indicators, focused solely on telephone utilization ability. Future research should employ more comprehensive assessments of digital literacy.
