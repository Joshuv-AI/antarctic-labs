The effect of creative drama education on student nurses’ perceptions of professional nursing values: a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Ebru Sevinç, Nur Bahar Kuru Aktürk, Seyhan Çerçi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of creative, drama-based education on nursing students’ perceptions of professional values, using randomised controlled trial (RCT) design.MethodThe study was conducted with 60 nursing students, who were selected through a G*Power calculation, from a population of 260 students studying at a foundation university in Istanbul. The experimental group attended five 90-minute sessions, once a week, incorporating creative drama techniques such as role-play, still image, forum theatre, case analysis and group discussions. The control group received the same content through traditional lectures. Data were collected using the Introductory Information Form and the Nurses’ Professional Values Scale at pre- and post-test, and analysed using appropriate statistical methods in SPSS 25.0.ResultsTotal and subscale scores on the Nurses’ Professional Values Scale were significantly higher for the experimental group than for the control group (p < 0.05). Within-group comparisons revealed substantial improvements across all subscales in the experimental group (p < 0.01), whereas the control group exhibited only minor changes. Differences in some subscales were also observed according to whether or not participants had a healthcare professional in their family.ConclusionCreative drama-based education may be an effective method for enhancing nursing students’ professional values in the short term. The strengths of this approach lie in supporting active participation and enabling experiential learning of professional values.Trial registrationThe study has been registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06875531). Registered on 10 March 2025. Although the application for ClinicalTrials.gov registration was submitted before the intervention began, the registration process was finalized and the registration number was assigned after participant recruitment had commenced due to administrative processing timelines. All primary and secondary outcomes, intervention procedures, and statistical analysis plans were pre-specified prior to the initiation of data collection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09699-2.Keywords: Nursing education, Professional values, Creative drama

Introduction

Professional values are the guiding principles adopted by specific professions and experts. They provide a foundation for analysing the values and beliefs that influence professional success [1]. For the nursing profession to attain professional status and deliver high-quality care services to individuals and society, it is critical that nurses possess a strong professional identity and understanding of professional values [2]. Nursing professionalism is a multidimensional, dynamic and culturally oriented framework that underpins clinical practice. It can be defined as providing care based on the principles of altruism and self-sacrifice [3]. Therefore, it is essential for future nurses to become aware of professional values and develop them from their early years of training. Although nursing curricula emphasise professional values, the process by which these values are internalised by students is explained to a limited extent in terms of educational practices.

Not only is nursing a profession aimed at protecting, promoting and improving the health of individuals and society [4], it is also a discipline grounded in ethical principles [5] and built upon professional values [6]. These values encompass the moral responsibilities that ensure nurses adhere to ethical standards, follow institutional norms and avoid unethical behaviours [7]. Therefore, developing professional values among nursing students is a fundamental component of undergraduate education, enhancing the quality of care provided and serving as an important determinant of job satisfaction [8].

The inadequacy of traditional teaching methods in meeting the needs of health education today has increased the demand for innovative, student-centred approaches guided by educators [9, 10]. Nursing education is a multidimensional process incorporating classroom, laboratory, and clinical settings [11]. However, contemporary education aims not only to impart technical knowledge and skills, but also to cultivate nurses who demonstrate empathy and a humanistic approach, ensuring a safe and ethical care environment [12]. The World Health Organization [13] also reports that integrating artistic methods into educational processes meaningfully contributes to students’ development of creativity, empathy, communication skills, and professional attitudes.

In this context, creative drama emerges as an innovative and remarkable approach to nursing education. Creative drama is defined as an experiential, group-based learning process in which individuals explore attitudes and behaviours by assuming different roles [14]. It is a teaching method that ensures active student participation while simultaneously fostering cognitive, affective and social skills [15]. It provides opportunities for practice and feedback, and enables the review of knowledge acquired. It also creates motivating learning environments [16]. Furthermore, it enhances group interaction, promotes collaborative learning and raises awareness of values [17]. For this reason, creative drama has been considered an innovative educational method that can overcome the limitations of traditional methods in imparting professional values.

Literature indicates that creative drama activities improve communication and social competence [18], enhance critical thinking [19] and contribute to professional identity development [20]. Furthermore, drama-based education performed by nursing students has been reported to strengthen attitudes towards interprofessional collaboration and increase nursing advocacy [21].

In addition, previous studies have demonstrated that creative drama-based educational initiatives contribute to the development of communication and professional skills among nursing students [14, 22]. However, the application and diversity of advanced approaches such as virtual reality and haptic simulation systems in nursing education are still limited [23]. Similarly, the number of studies examining the use of creative drama in nursing education is also limited [24]. Although some studies have been conducted in this area in Turkey, it is noteworthy that most of these studies were conducted with limited samples and that the level of experimental evidence is low, particularly with regard to findings related to professional values.

Accordingly, this study aims to evaluate the effect of creative drama-based education on nursing students’ perceptions of professional values using a randomised controlled research design. The study is expected to fill an important gap in the literature by considering creative drama not merely as a supportive teaching activity but as an evidence-based educational model that supports professional identity development. Furthermore, it is anticipated that the findings will contribute to strengthening professional values by offering an innovative educational approach that can be integrated into the nursing curriculum.

Materials and methods

Purpose and design of the study

This study aimed to determine the effect of creative drama education on nursing students’ perceptions of professional values. The study aimed to contribute to the professional identity development of student nurses and propose an innovative teaching method for fostering professional values in nursing education. The research was designed as a randomised controlled experimental study.

Purpose and design of the study

This study aimed to determine the effect of creative drama education on nursing students’ perceptions of professional values. Specifically, the study was designed to test an educational intervention intended to enhance professional values, rather than merely describing the current situation. The study sought to contribute to the development of professional identity among nursing students and to propose creative drama as an innovative and effective teaching method for fostering professional values in nursing education. Accordingly, the research was designed as a randomized controlled experimental study with a pre-test–post-test control group design.

Research hypotheses

H0: There is no statistically significant difference in professional values scores between nursing students who receive creative drama education and those who receive standard education.

H0: There is no statistically significant difference in professional values scores between nursing students who receive creative drama education and those who receive standard education.

H1: Nursing students who receive creative drama education have significantly higher professional values scores than those who receive standard education.

H1: Nursing students who receive creative drama education have significantly higher professional values scores than those who receive standard education.

Setting and duration of the study

The study was conducted in the Department of Nursing at the Faculty of Health Sciences of a foundation university in Istanbul, Turkey. The intervention process took place between 1 March and 1 June 2025.

Population and sample

The study population consisted of first-, second-, and third-year nursing students (N = 260) enrolled in the Department of Nursing at a foundation university in Istanbul. These grade levels were included because they represent students who are actively engaged in theoretical and early clinical nursing education and have not yet fully completed their professional socialization process, making them an appropriate target group for interventions aimed at developing professional values. Fourth-year students were excluded from the study because they were completing their internship program and were not able to participate regularly in the intervention process.

Inclusion criteria were: being enrolled in the first, second, or third year of the nursing program; willingness to participate in the study; attendance at all scheduled training sessions; and completion of both pre-test and post-test assessments. Exclusion criteria included being a fourth-year (internship) student, previous participation in structured training on professional values, irregular attendance, or incomplete data collection forms.

In the absence of similar intervention studies in the literature, the sample size was calculated using Cohen’s effect size criteria. Accordingly, a large effect size (d = 0.80) was assumed [25]. The required sample size was calculated using the G*Power 3.1.9.4 program with a power of 90% and a significance level of 0.05, resulting in a minimum sample size of 56 students (28 in the experimental group and 28 in the control group).

To compensate for potential data loss due to absenteeism, withdrawal, or incomplete data collection during the intervention process, an additional 10% was added to the calculated sample size. Thus, the target sample size was increased to 62 students. The study was completed with 60 students, as illustrated in the CONSORT flow diagram (Fig. 1). Although equal allocation to the experimental and control groups was initially planned, a small difference in group sizes emerged by the end of the study because some students did not attend all training sessions or complete the post-test assessments during the intervention period.

CONSORT flow diagram of the study

Randomization was performed by an independent researcher who was not involved in the intervention or data collection processes in order to minimize potential bias. Eligible students who met the inclusion criteria were assigned to the experimental and control groups using a computer-generated random number list. The allocation process was conducted prior to the intervention to ensure that group assignment was determined objectively and independently of the researchers conducting the training sessions. This procedure helped reduce selection bias and ensured the transparency and objectivity of group allocation.

Data collection instruments

Personal ınformation form

The Personal Information Form, which was developed by the researchers, was used to determine the students’ demographic characteristics. It consisted of five questions about age, gender, academic year and whether there are any healthcare professionals in the family [2, 8, 26].

Nurses’ Professional Values Scale (NPVS)

The NPVS, developed by Weis and Schank (2000), was translated into Turkish and its validity and reliability were confirmed by Orak and Alpar [27, 28]. During the adaptation process, the original 44 items of the scale were reduced to 31 items based on content validity and statistical analyses.

The scale is a five-point Likert-type instrument scored from “Extremely Important (5)” to “Not Important (1)” and consists of five subscales: Human Dignity (items 13–15, 24–31); Responsibility (items 6–12); Activism (items 4, 16–18, 23); Safety (items 1–3, 5); and Autonomy (items 19–22). Total scores range from 31 to 155, with higher scores indicating that nurses place greater importance on professional values.

In this study, the NPVS was used as a pre-test and post-test measurement tool to compare professional values scores between the experimental and control groups and to evaluate the effect of the creative drama intervention. Both the total scale score and subscale scores were analyzed in order to comprehensively assess changes across different dimensions of professional values.

In Orak and Alpar’s [28] study, item-total correlation coefficients ranged from 0.36 to 0.66 and Cronbach’s alpha was 0.95. In the current study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.989 for the experimental group and 0.987 for the control group. The alpha values for the subscales in the experimental and control groups were as follows: Human Dignity (0.979/0.968), Responsibility (0.942/0.952), Activism (0.952/0.930), Safety (0.902/0.951) and Autonomy (0.953/0.915). Although the internal consistency coefficients were very high, this finding may reflect the strong conceptual coherence of professional values among nursing students as well as the homogeneity of the study sample.

Intervention and data collection

The educational programme for the experimental group was developed based on the creative drama approach and structured in accordance with the subscales of the Nurses’ Professional Values Scale (NPVS). The content of the programme was prepared by the researchers, who have academic expertise in nursing education, and was grounded in experiential learning theory and constructivist educational principles, which emphasize active participation, reflection, and meaning-making in values education. In addition, the programme content was reviewed by two experts in nursing education and one certified creative drama instructor, and minor revisions were made in line with their recommendations.

Prior to the intervention, pre-test data were collected from participants in both the experimental and control groups using the Personal Information Form and the NPVS. Participants were assigned to the experimental and control groups through computer-assisted randomization conducted by an independent researcher who was not involved in the intervention or data collection processes.

The experimental group received five creative drama–based education sessions, delivered once a week, each lasting 90 min. The intervention followed a structured sequence consisting of warm-up activities, enactment through creative drama techniques, and group reflection. The first three sessions focused on the concepts of Human Dignity and Responsibility, aiming to facilitate students’ internalization of these professional values through role play, still images, forum theatre, improvisation, and group discussions. The final two sessions addressed the Activism, Safety, and Autonomy subscales, enabling students to apply professional values to simulated clinical scenarios using case-based improvisations, forum theatre, and interactive exercises.

All creative drama sessions were facilitated by a certified creative drama leader with formal training and professional experience in creative drama practices, as well as prior experience in educational interventions with university students. The control group received education on the same topics and learning objectives over five weekly sessions of equal duration, delivered using the traditional lecture method. Thus, the experimental and control groups were equivalent in terms of content, duration, and frequency, differing only in the instructional method.

Two weeks after completion of the educational programme, the NPVS was re-administered to both groups as a post-test (Table 1). This time interval was chosen to allow students sufficient time to reflect on and internalize the acquired professional values while minimizing the risk of recall bias. Blinding was not feasible in this study due to the nature of the educational intervention; however, outcome assessment was based on self-administered scales, reducing potential researcher-related bias. Based on the post-test scores, the observed effect size was large (Cohen’s d = 0.87). A post-hoc power analysis conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.4 with an alpha level of 0.05 and a total sample size of 60 revealed a statistical power of approximately 0.92, indicating that the sample size was sufficient to detect the effect of the intervention.

Data analysis

The data were analysed using the SPSS 25.0 software package. Suitability for normal distribution was assessed using Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. The chi-squared test was used to compare the sociodemographic characteristics of participants between groups. Differences between the pre-test and post-test scores of the experimental and control groups were analysed using independent-samples t-tests, while within-group changes were examined using paired-samples t-tests. A significance level of p < 0.05 was used for all statistical analyses.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Istanbul Arel University (Decision No. 2024/25, dated 27/12/2024). Institutional permission was also obtained from Istanbul Arel University (Letter No. 729767, dated 08/01/2025). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration. All participants were provided with detailed information about the purpose and procedures of the study, and written informed consent was obtained, emphasising that participation was voluntary and would not affect their academic performance or course evaluations.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. One additional limitation of this study is that the trial registration was finalized after participant recruitment had commenced. Although the application for ClinicalTrials.gov registration was submitted before the intervention began, the registration process was finalized and the registration number was assigned after the initiation of the study due to administrative processing timelines. Ethical approval, institutional permission, study procedures, outcome measures, and the statistical analysis plan had all been established before data collection commenced. Nevertheless, because prospective registration is considered an important component of research transparency, the timing of registration should be considered when interpreting the findings and the overall strength of the evidence.

First, the study was conducted at a single centre, which limits the generalisability of the results to nursing students from other universities or regions. During the intervention period, experimental and control group sessions were conducted separately; however, complete prevention of interaction between students outside the training sessions could not be guaranteed. This may have led to a potential contamination effect, as students from different groups might have shared information informally. Additionally, the concurrent inclusion of the experimental and control groups in the study may have reduced the risk of this effect.

Blinding was not implemented in this study due to the nature of the educational intervention, as both the participants and the researcher were aware of group assignments. This may have introduced performance or expectancy bias that could have influenced participants’ responses.

Finally, the study relied on short-term follow-up data based on a pre-test–post-test design, with the post-test administered two weeks after the intervention. This relatively short observation period limits the evaluation of the long-term and sustained effects of creative drama education on professional values. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of short-term improvement rather than proof of sustained long-term effectiveness.

Discussion

This randomised controlled trial evaluated the impact of creative, drama-based education on nursing students’ perceptions of professional values. The findings suggest that this approach is more effective than traditional teaching methods for promoting professional values. These results are discussed in the context of literature on intervention-based nursing education and contemporary approaches to teaching professional values. The absence of a significant difference in baseline scores between the experimental and control groups suggests that the observed changes following the intervention are primarily due to the educational intervention itself, rather than to the descriptive or demographic characteristics of the participants.

In the experimental group, creative drama-based education led to significant improvements in all professional value subdimensions by enabling students to experience professional roles, enact ethical situations and engage in reflective learning through lived experience. This experiential process integrates the cognitive, emotional, and social aspects of learning, facilitating the internalisation of professional values beyond mere conceptual understanding. By contrast, the traditional lecture-based approach used for the control group consisted mainly of instructor-led theoretical explanations, supported by classroom presentations and short question-and-answer sessions. While this method facilitated the transmission of theoretical knowledge regarding professional values, it offered limited opportunities for active participation, emotional engagement and reflective learning. Consequently, the traditional lecture-based format produced only modest improvements compared with the more interactive creative drama approach. These results suggest that creative drama-based education could have a stronger and more comprehensive effect on the development of professional values.

As in previous studies, which reported generally high levels of professional values among nursing students and nurses [29–33], this tendency has been attributed to students’ limited exposure to professional challenges and clinical realities during the early stages of training [29, 30]. However, the present findings build on this body of work by showing that experiential, drama-based interventions can help to overcome idealised perceptions and actively support the deeper internalisation of professional values.

Although previous studies have reported varying levels of professional values among nurses and nursing students, and have highlighted the potential influence of personal characteristics, family background, cultural context and educational experiences [34–40], these factors were not the primary focus of the present intervention study. No significant baseline differences were observed between the experimental and control groups in the current study, indicating that the groups were comparable prior to the intervention. While some sociodemographic characteristics, such as having a healthcare professional in the family, were explored in relation to professional values, no significant baseline differences were observed between the groups, and these variables did not influence the interpretation of the intervention’s effects. Overall, these results suggest that creative, drama-based education may contribute to the development of professional values among nursing students.

Although studies examining educational interventions aimed at enhancing professional values are limited, existing research suggests that structured educational programmes can be effective in this area [41–45]. These studies demonstrate that programmes based on professional values models, ethics education, participation in scientific activities and theory-driven frameworks can positively influence professional values among nurses and nursing students.

When the outcomes of the pre-test and post-test were compared, it was found that both educational approaches influenced professional values. However, the creative drama intervention applied to the experimental group produced a more pronounced effect. By promoting active participation, role exploration and experiential learning, creative drama provides a supportive learning environment that enhances students’ awareness and facilitates the internalisation of professional values. In line with previous research emphasising the multifaceted educational advantages of creative drama, such as enhancing empathy, critical thinking, self-awareness, self-efficacy, and professional identity development [46–51], the findings of this study suggest that creative drama-based learning strategies may contribute to the development of professional values in nursing education.

Another factor to bear in mind when interpreting the findings is the context in which the study was conducted. The research was carried out at a single private foundation university in Istanbul, using a relatively homogeneous sample of nursing students. The educational resources, institutional culture and student characteristics of foundation universities may differ from those of public universities or other educational settings. Differences in institutional learning environments, educational opportunities and student profiles may influence how educational interventions are experienced and internalised by students. Therefore, the results should be interpreted within this specific institutional and cultural context, and caution should be exercised when generalising the findings to nursing students at different universities or in different regions.

When interpreting the findings, several methodological limitations should be considered. First, the relatively short follow-up period represents an important constraint. The post-test was administered two weeks after the completion of the intervention, allowing the evaluation of short-term changes in students’ perceptions of professional values; however, this interval does not provide sufficient evidence regarding the long-term sustainability of the observed effects. Future studies should therefore include longer follow-up periods to assess whether the improvements associated with creative drama-based education are maintained over time. In addition, potential contamination between groups could not be fully prevented, which may have attenuated or inflated the observed effects. The absence of blinding, due to the nature of the educational intervention, may also have introduced expectancy and performance bias, potentially influencing participants’ responses. Furthermore, the single-centre design and relatively homogeneous sample limit the generalizability of the findings to other educational and cultural contexts. Taken together, these limitations may reduce the certainty of causal inferences; therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution. These methodological limitations may have led to either an overestimation or underestimation of the observed intervention effects.

Conclusion

This randomised controlled trial suggests that a creative drama–based approach to education may be effective in enhancing nursing students’ professional values in the short term. Compared with traditional lecture-based teaching, the creative drama intervention produced more significant improvements in professional values by encouraging active participation, experiential learning, and reflective engagement. These findings suggest that creative drama may be a valuable and practical strategy for fostering the internalisation of professional values in undergraduate nursing education. However, the findings should be interpreted in light of the methodological limitations of the study, including the delayed finalization of trial registration due to administrative processing timelines, the single-centre design, lack of blinding, potential contamination between groups, and the short follow-up period. Further research involving larger and more diverse samples, as well as longitudinal follow-up, is recommended to examine the sustainability and transferability of creative drama-based educational interventions in nursing education.
