Correlation of ultrasonic welding parameters with microstructural evolution and mechanical-electrical reliability of Al-CuNi joints for energy storage systems
Authors: Soumyajit Das, Bharat Chandra Routara, Santosh Kumar Nayak, Basanta Kumar Nanda, Swayam Bikash Mishra, Mantra Prasad Satpathy, Seyfu Tiruneh Debebe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In the automotive industry, ultrasonic welding (USW) stands out as a highly effective solid-state welding technique. It is specifically designed for joining components within lithium-ion battery modules and energy storage systems. To achieve an optimal weight-to-body ratio, dissimilar materials like aluminum, copper, and nickel are joined to reduce the weight of electrical and electronic devices. This study presents a systematic analysis of aluminum (Al) and cupro-nickel (CuNi) dissimilar joints using USW, with variations in welding parameters. The lap shear strength results show that sonotrode pressure and welding energy have a greater impact on joint strength than the amplitude of vibration at all tested levels. Joint strength reached peak values of 942 N and 236 N, respectively, before declining sharply as weld energy increased due to the high interfacial temperature. Resistance measurements revealed that resistance decreased with increasing welding energy. Additionally, Al-CuNi specimens exhibited higher hardness values at the faying surface compared to their base metals. A metallographic analysis of the weld cross-section was conducted to evaluate diffusion across the joint interface, supported by X-ray energy-dispersive spectroscopy, electron backscatter diffraction, and X-ray diffraction to assess weld quality.

Introduction

The economic and social progress of modern society relies heavily on transportation powered by fossil fuels. However, the rise in internal combustion engine vehicles has led to significant pollution and greenhouse gas emissions, deteriorating air quality in many countries1. To address this, sustainable transportation solutions have become increasingly important. Electric vehicles (EVs) and hybrid electric vehicles (HEVs) have emerged as more efficient and environmentally friendly alternatives, requiring less maintenance and producing fewer emissions2,3. A key factor in the success of EVs is the use of lithium-ion batteries, particularly LFP (Lithium Ferro Phosphate) and NMC (Nickel Manganese Cobalt) types. These batteries offer numerous advantages over traditional options, including compactness, lightweight design, high energy density, recyclability, and a longer lifespan of approximately ten years4. These features enable EVs to travel greater distances on a single charge while reducing overall weight, thereby improving efficiency. Lithium-ion batteries are constructed by physically and electrically connecting multiple cells to form a battery pack. When these cells are charged and discharged together, the battery delivers the necessary power. Materials like Al, copper (Cu), and nickel (Ni) are used as current collectors and bus bars in these batteries due to their excellent conductivity and strength. However, joining these dissimilar materials through fusion welding is challenging due to their differing physical properties5. To ensure strong and reliable electrical and mechanical connections in battery packs, effective welding techniques are essential. Common methods include Laser Welding (LW), USW, wire bonding, and Resistance Spot Welding (RSW)6. USW is particularly advantageous for joining dissimilar materials, as it consumes less energy and requires shorter processing times. This makes it well-suited for welding thin sheets or multi-layer stack-ups in lithium-ion battery production. As a result, USW has gained popularity in the automotive and electronics industries for joining thin sheets with dissimilar properties7. The USW method generates frictional heat between the connecting workpieces by using high-frequency mechanical vibrations. In order to prevent overlapped workpieces from moving, the specimens are clamped between the anvil fixture and the sonotrode tip. When the ultrasonic vibrations are delivered perpendicularly to the workpiece surface, a longitudinal rubbing motion occurs at the contact interface. In this way, the mating surface undergoes a shear deformation that eliminates the asperities and facilitates the formation of a solid-state bond by transferring as much energy as possible to the welding interface while minimizing the friction. Several investigations have demonstrated that the temperature at the weld contact rises as the friction coefficient between the specimens increases. Hua et al.8 performed a study to confirm this finding. Similar studies have shown that USW of Al and its alloys can enhance joint strength based on the welding energy and bonding time9–11. As well, a significant decrease in bond strength was reported when the welding energy was extremely high. Therefore, a durable mechanical and electrical connection must be achieved by optimizing the USW control factors. Recent academic studies have thoroughly examined the impact of different processing factors on the quality of material joints. A study conducted by Yao et al.12 meticulously examined the relationship between welding energy input and the interfacial bond morphology of ultrasonically welded Al-Cu assemblies. As per the results, as welding energy increases, the tensile strength of the lap shear increases due to the formation of a swirl-like structure and mechanical interlock at the faying surface. There was a tendency for high energies to result in the formation of weld zone thinning, which decreased the joint strength. For weld zone thinning to be resolved, it is essential to uphold a relatively low temperature at the interface of the weld. During the dissimilar USW of anodized Al and Cu, Xiang et al.13 discussed the behaviour of the oxide layer during the welding process. It was revealed that intermetallic compounds (IMCs) were observed at the interface that based on the welding conditions. Further, de Leon and Shin5 reviewed the characteristics of USWed Al-Ni joints with regard to their process characteristics, optimal weld parameters, microstructure morphology, measurement of temperature, mechanical properties, and quality. A recent investigation undertaken by Ni et al.14 delved into the intricate mechanisms of joint formation, the microstructural evolution, and the corresponding mechanical characteristics of copper-nickel junctions across varied welding attributes. The study’s findings reveal that an increase in welding duration correlates with an initial rise in the lap shear tensile load of Cu/Ni joints, reaching a maximum point before exhibiting a decline. The uniqueness of USWed Al-CuNi joints resides in the complex interplay of solid-solution strengthening and altered diffusion kinetics compared to the Al-Cu and Al-Ni binary systems. While Al-Ni joints rely heavily on mechanical interlocking and metallic adhesion to overcome the high flow stress of nickel15, Al-CuNi systems utilize the solid-solution strengthened CuNi matrix to modulate this interaction16. Furthermore, Al-Ni systems exhibit insufficient electrical conductivity, and pure Al-Cu systems are prone to the formation of brittle Al2Cu IMC17. Meanwhile, the Al-CuNi joint strategically leverages Ni as an alloying element in the Cu matrix to suppress the kinetics of this brittle IMC while maintaining superior mechanical integrity and thermal stability18. Consequently, the Al-CuNi system represents a specialized metallurgical characteristic, offering a more controlled diffusion layer than Al-Cu and better functional conductivity than Al-Ni. It is specifically engineered to withstand the thermomechanical stresses of advanced lithium-ion battery tab assemblies.

The majority of studies on ultrasonic metal welding for battery interconnections are focused mainly on Al-Cu dissimilar joints, which are commonly used in tab-busbar configurations. Meanwhile, the advantage of USWed Al-CuNi joints lies in their ability to overcome the metallurgical limitations and poor grain-size control typically associated with conventional fusion welding of dissimilar metals. Furthermore, the introduction of the nickel alloying element facilitates superior interfacial bonding by promoting significant inter-atomic diffusion. Despite this, few studies have been conducted on Al-CuNi dissimilar systems, particularly under different experimental conditions. It is therefore necessary to investigate the relationship between process, microstructure, and properties in ultrasonic welding of Al-CuNi joints to gain a better understanding of their potential for advanced battery interconnections. In this study, USW is employed to join Al-CuNi sheets, with the effects of welding parameters analyzed at various energy levels. To evaluate weld integrity, lap shear strength tests, interface temperature rise measurements, and microhardness changes across the faying surface are conducted. As part of the evaluation of the battery module efficiency, the correlation between the joint electric resistance and the interface temperature is explored during current flow. For the comprehensive microstructural characterization of the weld zone in dissimilar material interfaces, the analytical methodology integrates field emission scanning electron microscopy with X-ray energy-dispersive spectroscopy data to examine plastic deformation driven flow, interface level diffusion, and metallurgical bond formations. The detailed characterization of the intermetallic compound phases present within the Al-CuNi joint is meticulously conducted through the application of electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) and X-ray diffraction spectroscopic (XRD) methodologies.

Materials and methods

In order to study this bonding characteristics, 0.5 mm thick commercial Al (AA1100) and 0.3 mm thick CuNi (UNS C71500) sheets are selected as welding specimens. To prepare samples for welding, sheet metal is cut into length of 75 mm and width of 20 mm. A 20 mm overlap position is maintained between Al-CuNi specimens during welding. For the lap shear test and T-peel test, two types of welding specimens are prepared. A schematic illustration of a lap configuration test specimens is shown in Fig. 2(a, b). It is estimated that the welding area is approximately 9 × 11 mm2. In both configurations, CuNi samples are kept on the bottom, while Al samples are kept on the top of CuNi strips. To remove lubricants and debris, the joint surface is ground with 1500 grit emery paper before welding. Specimens must first be dried and then an ultrasonic system (Telsonic® M4000) with a maximum output of 3 kW and a frequency of 20 kHz is used to conduct the test. An overall view of the USW machine setup is shown in Fig. 2(c). In this experiment, Al-CuNi joints are tested under different parametric conditions, including weld energy, sonotrode pressure, and vibrational amplitude. Based on preliminary trial experiments, a multilevel design of experiment (DOE) is adopted to define the parameter levels and systematically evaluate the influence of welding parameters on bonding strength. During the selected parameter ranges, welding energy, sonotrode pressure, and interfacial shear are sufficient to break surface oxide and promote metallic bonding without causing expulsion or thermal damage. To achieve accuracy in measurement, five replications of each level of setup are performed. A variable weld duration (Wt) is used during the experiment, with the weld energy (We) calculated by the following Eq. The precise measurement of faying surface temperature during the ultrasonic welding process is facilitated by the deployment of a Testo® 890 thermal imaging camera. In this infrared (IR) thermal imaging system, the emissivity value is set to 0.24, manual focus is used. The measurement distance of approximately 10 cm is maintained between the IR camera and the weld specimen during the welding process. Furthermore, the mechanical integrity assessments, specifically lap shear and T-peel evaluations, are conducted employing a fully automated Instron® 3369 universal testing machine. This apparatus is configured for a maximum load capacity of 50 kN and operates at a consistent crosshead displacement rate of 2 mm/min. As part of the assessment of microhardness and microstructural characteristics, specimen joints are polished with abrasive papers (grits #1500, #2000, #2500, and #3000). An alumina solution of 0.3 μm is combined with a colloidal silica suspension of 0.04 μm in order to generate a mirror-imaged surface for FESEM analysis. The Vickers hardness test is conducted using a Zwick® Roell ZHVµ microhardness tester with indentation load of 500 gf and dwell time of 15 s. Joint electrical resistance is measured utilizing a HIOKI® RM3548 resistance meter by passing a constant current of 200 A through the joints for 90 s. This resistance meter utilizes the four-wire Kelvin method. In this method, the Kelvin clips are spaced 15 mm apart evenly along the edges of specimens in order to minimize measurement inaccuracies. As part of the microstructural analysis of the joint interface, a ZESIS® Gemini SEM 450 machine has been equipped with an EDS and EBSD system. An XRD study of the Al and CuNi fracture surfaces is performed using a BRUKER® D8 ADVANCE ECO machine in order to determine the type of IMC generated during the USW process. A scale factor of 0.05° is applied to changing the angle from 20° to 100° and sampling interval of 2.0 s is applied to each step.

Results and discussion

Lap shear strength and the T-Peel test

This study investigates the lap shear strength as well as the T-peel test on specimen welds under varying pressures of the sonotrode, amplitudes of vibration under different weld energies. The outcomes obtained from both of the tests are shown in Fig. 3. Based on the graphical representation, it can be observed that the lap shear strength increased with increasing weld energy, reaching its maximum value of 832 N at 0.24 MPa sonotrode pressure, 1200 J of weld energy and 54 μm vibrational amplitude (Fig. 3(a)). Afterward, with an increase in welding energy, the load decreases. During higher levels of welding energy input, a micro-bonded region occurs under the sonotrode tip ridges, where excessive heat is generated, material is softened, and excess plastic deformation occurs19. In the case of 68 μm of vibrational amplitude, a similar pattern of persistence can be observed (Fig. 3(b)). Nevertheless, at this parametric level, the lap shear strength achieved an enhanced value when compared to 54 μm of vibrational amplitude at the same sonotrode pressure and welding energy. A high vibrational amplitude is responsible for this improvement since it affects the amount of frictional work that is performed. At 1200 J of weld energy, this parameter represents 942 N as the maximum lap shear strength. A graphical representation of T-peel test results appears to be almost identical to that of a lap shear strength test result. In this test, the maximum value is achieved 216 N and 236 N at 54 μm and 68 μm, respectively, depending on the vibrational amplitude at 1200 J of welding energy for 0.24 MPa of sonotrode pressure (Fig. 3(c, d)). Upon varying the weld parameter, it can be observed that the Al-CuNi joint exhibits excessive reductions in lap shear strength when utilizing higher sonotrode pressures and energies. The reason for this is due to excessive heat being generated around the joint surface, which can cause a reduction in the thickness of the Al sheet in its effective state, which directly impacts the strength of the joint. Each welding parameter exhibited a different pattern depending on the combination of materials20–22. Thus, in order to ensure maximum joint strength, vibrational amplitude, sonotrode pressure, and weld energy must be optimally balanced.

Interfacial temperature profile results

In terms of the temperatures at the interface, a number of factors can affect the quality of the weld. By periodically adjusting the weld interface temperature, excess plastic deformation, metallurgical adhesion, and diffusion can usually be reduced. At a fixed sonotrode pressure of 0.24 MPa, Fig. 4(a-f) depicts infrared thermal images acquired at different welding energies. In accordance with the thermal images, the sonotrode tip appears to be the source of the heat concentration, which is distributed differently around the joint surface. Based on the outcomes, it appears that a higher energy input resulted in an increase in the generation of heat at the joint interface. In spite of this, one important observation is measuring the temperature of the sonotrode tip as well as the anvil fixture at 62 ℃ and 40 ℃, respectively, following the completion of the USW procedure (Fig. 4(g)). After completing each joint, it is evident that the USW setup needs some time to cool before returning to its ideal state. It is clear from this scenario that higher welding energies can negatively impact joint characteristics, as well as the performance of USW parts. Figure 4(h) illustrates a graph representing the temperature distribution at weld interfaces subjected to various welding energies and pressures. A peak temperature of 375 °C, 384 °C, and 389 °C at 1500 J of welding energy corresponds to pressure inputs of 0.22 MPa, 0.24 MPa, and 0.26 MPa, respectively. Results indicate that atomic diffusion occurred through solid-state bonding rather than welding peak temperatures reaching the melting point of the base material. According to the graph, it can be concluded that a high temperature significantly reduces the strength of the Al-CuNi joint, as Al material becomes softer at high temperatures23. Due to the pressure and vibrational amplitude of the sonotrode tip, the serrated tips are more susceptible to penetration into the Al material when welding energy is increased. In comparison to the bonding force between Al and CuNi, the shear force between the sonotrode tip and the Al sheet at this point is greater. As a result, there is a sudden cessation of relative motion between the Al and CuNi sheet, causing the tip of the sonotrode to lose grip on the Al sheet24. Due to this reason, sonotrode tips adhere to the Al surface at high welding energies.

Variation of microhardness in the joint region

In order to establish the work hardening phenomenon of Al-CuNi joints, several microhardness variations in weld cross-sectional areas are examined. Considering the obtained peak lap shear strength at 1200 J of weld energy with 0.24 MPa of sonotrode pressure at 68 μm of vibrational amplitude, this parameter is selected for the average microhardness profile. A longitudinal Vickers microhardness contour around the cross-sectional area of an Al-CuNi joint is shown in Fig. 5(a). The centre of the cross-section of all the joints displays significantly lower hardness than the original sheet. When compared to a joint fabricated with 1500 J welding energy, a joint made with 600 J welding energy is less hard. Due to the greater heat input caused by higher welding energy, the materials are fused more completely and the microstructure is refined. By increasing the temperature, the alloying elements are distributed more evenly, resulting in enhanced hardness25. Alternatively, a lower welding energy may result in insufficient heat, ensuing in incomplete fusion and coarser microstructures. This leads to an increase in the temperature of the weld region, which directly affects the surface of the joint interface on the material. The Al side of the material has a higher reactivity than the CuNi side because it has a softer behaviour26. On the Al side of the joint, Fig. 5(b) illustrates the variation in microhardness in a vertical direction near the bonding line. In this case, the Al side is divided into three distinct region such as top, mid, and bottom. In the graphical representation, each region hardness value appears to have substantial variations. There are a number of reasons for this, including the grain size and distribution varying from region to region27. Due to the vicinity of the bond line, the bottom region of the surface has a higher temperature than the top surface. When the temperature of the Al material is increased and severe cold working is performed, grain growth and recrystallization occurs resulting in a reduction on its microhardness value10.

Correlation between the joint electric resistance and the interface temperature

It is imperative to identify the relationship between joint resistance and welding energy prior to analysing the reliability of the Al-CuNi joint. According to Fig. 6, the joint resistance values and the interface temperatures at the weld region follow a 200 A current flow for 60 s. On the basis of the graphs, it appears that as welding energy increases, the joint resistance at the weld interface decreases along with the corresponding peak temperature. As an indication of the minimum joint resistance, the lowest value captured is 9.1 µΩ at a welding energy of 1200 J and a sonotrode pressure of 0.24 MPa. In accordance with the above-mentioned weld parameter, the peak joint interface temperature recorded 43 °C. When compared to the other weld parameters, this particular configuration is receiving the lowest value. Most notably, the Al-CuNi joints exhibit increased bond density and effective thickness, which minimize resistance and allow current to flow without interference28. Furthermore, when 1500 J of welding energy is used, the bond density is surplus and the joint thickness is decreased due to excessive plastic deformation at the bond region. In this situation, the strength of the joint is lower, resulting in more resistance to the passage of current, as well as a higher interface temperature caused by this weaker joint. Therefore, when large currents pass through joints, optimal bond density and effective thickness would help to reduce joint electrical resistance and joule heat generation21. In USWed joints, the current flow induces joule heating, which causes them to warm up and consequently increase in joint interface temperature. It is therefore necessary to have a strong joint in order to reduce the temperature at the weld zone when the current is flow29.

Microstructural and elemental analysis

An analysis of the metallographic structure of the weld cross-section is performed to determine degree of diffusion, density of bonds, and plastic deformation at the interface. A FESEM images of Al-CuNi joints with 68 μm of vibrational amplitude and 0.24 MPa sonotrode pressure is presented in Fig. 7 along with a quantification of EDS result. In accordance with the lap shear strength analysis, three different cross-sectional surfaces have been selected. Based on Fig. 7(a), it is evident that small gaps can be observed in some portions of the joint when joining is performed at a low welding energy (600 J). These types of occurrences are caused by an insufficient amount of welding energy near the bonding region. As a result, the Al-CuNi joint cannot form a continuous bond. In light of the colour mapping analysis, it is determined that the Al material does not attach to the CuNi sheet (Fig. 7(b)). Therefore, there is a gap visible on the FESEM image. In spite of the fact that the Al-CuNi joint is a strong one, smaller gaps may result in a defective bond formation, negatively affecting the joint’s strength. For a more accurate analysis of this gap indication, using EDS line scan as a cross-sectional study should be conducted within the vicinity of joint. The EDS results indicate that between the Al-CuNi specimens, approximately 5 μm of unbonded region can be observed (Fig. 7(c)). Due to this reason, a minimum lap shear strength is achieved for this parameter. It has been observed that unbonded area gradually disappear as welding energy increases from 600 J to 1200 J, and almost no unwelded regions are observed. According to Fig. 7(d), the Al-CuNi bonded interface is noticeably smooth on the FESEM image. By undergoing plastic deformation, the micro bumps on the Al surface are crushed. Therefore, this promotes dynamic recrystallization and creates the smooth surface region of the material. However, plastic deformation at the welding interface is a positive factor for interfacial metallurgical bonding23. The colour mapping profile showed that the Al-CuNi joint surface has an even elemental distribution (Fig. 7(e)). In the presence of sufficient weld energy, the duration of relative motion at the faying surface of the Al side is greatly improved, thus enhancing the mixing of material30. During the welding process, micro-joints are formed as perforations in the contacting surfaces are contacted. As the joint expands, the bonding interface becomes very strong. The EDS results in Fig. 7(f) show that the Al-CuNi joint has a sufficient amount of mixed diffusion. It is estimated that the diffusion distance between Al-CuNi joint is approximately 40 μm. It is not apparent that any IMCs layer is generated across the Al-CuNi joint interface. According to the welding process, this diffusion region indicates that the ultrasonic vibrational friction forces in combination with the sonotrode pressure are the primary cause of an interlocking phenomenon. In response to an increase in welding energy from 1200 J to 1500 J, the Al surface wears down and visible cracks are observed on the bonding interface (Fig. 7(g)). It is due to the fact that the interface temperature is more at higher welding energies compared to lower energy level. As a matter of fact, when the interface temperature is high, many defects will precipitate, such as vacancies, dislocations into joints, wave-like displacements on a coarse scale, as well as folds in the wave crests in some phases5. Based on Fig. 7(h), the colour mapping profile indicated that excessive Al material diffusion zones occur at the weld region due to higher interface temperature. It is because of this reason that the joint is more likely to develop cracks or other defects. There has also been observed some Al and CuNi fragments adhering to the CuNi surface. In these fragments, micro-cracks seem to have formed on the Al surface, which have been violently rubbed with the CuNi sheet18,31. Figure 7(i) illustrates the results of the EDS analysis that indicate an excess of atomic diffusion zone at the interface between the Al-CuNi joint. Around the bond interface, a diffusion layer of approximately 75 μm is captured. A localized micro-weld pattern can be observed throughout the entire joint contact, while IMCs are barely discernible as thin layers. Based on the results of this study, it can be determined that when high-frequency oscillations are present, the metal at the interface between the mating parts is soft and more susceptible to deformation32,33. Due to this circumstance, the joint strength is greatly reduced.

Grain structure and texture analysis

As part of the investigation of grain morphology, the EBSD test is used to investigate the variation of microstructure along the faying surface of the Al-CuNi joint. In Fig. 8, an analysis of the grain morphology and size is conducted before the Al and CuNi specimens are welded. Despite this, it is evident that the microstructure of the Al side has changed from elongated to equiaxed grains after welding (Fig. 9(a)). There is a probability that it is caused by a process called dislocation slip, which is the main mechanism of deformation of Al alloys with high dislocation energies30. From the dislocation slip, it can be seen that plastic deformation occurred near the bond line due to the interaction between the weld energy, pressure, and the ultrasonic vibration. During this period, the grain rotated so as to release the distortion energy, resulting in saw-tooth grain boundaries. Furthermore, the serrated grain boundaries became connected at the bulge as the interface temperature and plastic deformation increased34. This method results in the formation of new, smaller grains by pinching off the long grains. Further, discontinuous micro-welds can only be observed within local regions, as can be confirmed by FESEM result. It has been noted by Zhao et al.35 that thermal deformation increases the orientation of sub-grain boundaries. Upon examination of the CuNi side, it is apparent that the grains near the interfaces are significantly elongated in the direction of vibration. An illustration of the formation of grain boundaries can be seen in Fig. 9(b). It is reported here that a significant number of low angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) have been converted into high angle grain boundaries (HAGBs). According to estimates, about 57.73% of HAGB is crystallized as a result of dynamic recrystallization. (Fig. 9(c)). Based on the combined effects of the ultrasonic input parameters, a dynamic recrystallization (DRX) took place in the weld region. This resulted in LAGB being transformed into HAGB24. According to previous studies on ultrasonically welded dissimilar material joints, this phenomenon can be classified as continuous dynamic recrystallization (CDRX)36.

XRD and williamson–hall analysis

To determine whether an IMC may form on the fracture surface, XRD analysis of the Al-CuNi joints is performed under the best parametric conditions. The XRD results for the fractured surfaces on the Al and CuNi sides are shown in Fig. 10(a, b). The graphic representation indicates that the intensity of Cu and Ni peaks is lower on the Al side. As opposed to this, the intensity of Al peak is considerably greater on the CuNi side, indicating that Al material is a softer nature. As a result of this behavior, it has been observed that IMCs grow through diffusion during the joint formation process, with Al becoming more likely to diffuse into CuNi as the bond forms. According to the results of this analysis, only the Al3CuNi phase is observed near the weld region, and there is no indication that any other IMC may exist. When more than one IMC is formed around the weld interface, the joint will be directly affected, which will result in a reduction in strength37. In accordance with the EDS analysis, it has already been established that as welding energy increases, welding temperature will increase as well, which in turn facilitates the formation of reaction layers. It is therefore important to select a suitable welding energy with a moderate amount of sonotrode pressure and a high vibration amplitude38. Moreover, when the metal-to-metal contact area increases, the electrical joint resistance reduces. As this condition is achieved at the elevated temperatures and high strain rates at the contact zones, it leads to the formation of the above-described IMC layer. Critically, while a thin, continuous IMC layer indicates a strong metallurgical bond that maintains electrical continuity, overgrowth of brittle IMC phases increases electrical resistivity and serves as a site for crack initiation. It ultimately compromises both the mechanical strength and the long-term electrical reliability of the joint.

Conclusions

In this study, describes the successful welding of Al-CuNi sheets using USW with variable welding parameters. Thus, it is possible to draw the following conclusions:

I.In accordance with the lap shear strength results, it is demonstrated that the bond strength of Al-CuNi joints can be enhanced by varying the weld parameters. A maximum lap shear strength of 942 N is achieved when 1200 J of welding energy and 0.24 MPa of pressure are applied at 68 μm of vibrational amplitude. The strength of the joint decreases as the welding energy increases because the joint is subjected to a growth in interfacial deformation.II.In light of the weld interface temperature profile, it can be observed that a high temperature contributes significantly to the deterioration of Al-CuNi joints, A substantial reduction in hardness has been observed on the Al side of the Al-CuNi joint, in contrast to no significant changes on the CuNi side.III.Correlating the joint electric resistance with the interface temperature reveals that as welding energy increases, the joint resistance at the weld interface decreases along with the temperature rise. The lowest value of joint resistance is captured as 9.1 µΩ at a welding energy of 1200 J and a sonotrode pressure of 0.24 MPa.IV.A smooth bond pattern is observed in the FESEM images of the cross-sectional area at 1200 J of weld energy and 0.24 MPa of sonotrode pressure. It has been demonstrated that sufficient weld energy enhances the duration of relative motion at the faying surface of the Al side, thereby improving the mixing of materials in the process. In accordance with the EDS results, the bond interface is subject to mutual diffusion.V.EBSD analysis of Al-CuNi joints revealed post-welding microstructural changes, including Al grain equiaxing, CuNi grain elongation, and approximately 57.73% HAGB crystallization via dynamic recrystallization and dislocation slip.VI.According to XRD analysis, a few microns of atomic diffusion can be observed at the interface as well as the formation of the Al3CuNi phase. It can be concluded from the WH plot that the crystallinity behaviour is positive.
