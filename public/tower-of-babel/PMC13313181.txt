Sequential axitinib and survivin vaccination unlock curative PD-1 immunotherapy in renal carcinoma
Authors: Fanny Méjean, Thi Tran, Maya Merabet, Alain Gey, Andyara Munoz, Benjamin Morin, Ali Bal, Morgane Bourhis, Nesrine Mabrouk, Magali Terme, Eric Tartour, Corinne Tanchot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDespite significant progress achieved by combining VEGFR tyrosine-kinase inhibitors (TKIs) with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), complete responses remain rare in metastatic renal cell carcinoma (mRCC), highlighting the need for strategies that optimize therapeutic synergy. Here, we show that the efficacy of VEGFR blockade, vaccination, and PD-1 inhibition critically depends on treatment sequence. Using an orthotopic RENCA model, we show that short-term VEGFR inhibition with axitinib transiently remodels tumor vasculature, alleviates hypoxia, and limits suppressive myeloid subsets, thereby generating an immune-permissive window. Administering a survivin-based long-peptide vaccine (SVX) during this preconditioning phase elicits strong Th1-polarized CD4⁺ and cytotoxic CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, which exhibit a polyfunctional cytokine and chemokine profile. When PD-1 blockade is introduced concomitantly with vaccination, after, rather than during, axitinib treatment, the triple regimen (axitinib + [SVX + anti-PD-1]) achieves durable tumor control with a high rate of complete responses, outperforming all other treatment schedules. Mechanistically, this sequence aligns vascular reprogramming, antigen-specific priming, and checkpoint release, converting an immune-excluded tumor into a T-cell-dominated, cytotoxic niche. Collectively, these findings identify temporal coordination as a critical determinant of therapeutic success and establish a mechanistically grounded framework for integrating vascular preconditionning, tumor-antigen vaccination, and PD-1 blockade as a curative immunotherapy strategy in renal carcinoma.KEYWORDS: Renal cell carcinoma, therapeutic vaccination, VEGFR inhibition, PD-1 blockade, tumor microenvironment, T-cell immunity

Introduction

Tumor angiogenesis is a defining feature of renal cell carcinoma (RCC) and a key determinant of its immune landscape. 1 , 2 In clear-cell RCC (ccRCC), constitutive activation of the hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) pathway drives excessive vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) production, leading to an abnormal, hypoxic, and immunosuppressive microenvironment. 3 , 4 The chaotic vasculature not only restricts T-cell infiltration but also fosters myeloid-derived suppressor cell accumulation and regulatory T-cell expansion. 5 , 6 Beyond its structural effects, VEGF directly hampers dendritic-cell maturation and antigen presentation, while inducing co-expression of inhibitory receptors (PD-1, TIM-3, CTLA-4, Lag-3) on CD8⁺ T cells, driving exhaustion. 7

Transient inhibition of VEGF signaling can partially reverse these defects by restoring vessel integrity and oxygenation, creating a brief phase of vascular stabilization during which immune effector cells can more effectively access the tumor. 8 , 9 On this basis, VEGFR tyrosine-kinase inhibitors (TKIs) combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have become the cornerstone of first-line therapy for metastatic ccRCC. 10 Landmark trials such as CLEAR and CheckMate-9ER improved outcomes compared with TKI monotherapy. 11-13 However, complete responses remain rare, highlighting persistent resistance mechanisms and the need for complementary strategies that generate de novo tumor-specific immunity.

Therapeutic vaccination offers a complementary strategy to expand and sustain tumor-specific T-cell immunity beyond the reach of current ICI/TKI regimens. 14 , 15 Among candidate antigens, survivin (BIRC5), a tumor-associated protein overexpressed in ccRCC but absent from normal tissues, has emerged as an attractive target. 16 Yet, previous survivin-based vaccines demonstrated modest efficacy, likely due to their administration in VEGF-high, immunosuppressive contexts unfavorable to antigen priming. Recent evidence from personalized neoantigen vaccines, when administered in immune-permissive settings, supports their capacity to elicit robust and durable tumor-specific responses. 17 These insights suggest that synchronizing vaccination with the VEGF/VEGFR-driven vascular reprogramming phase, followed by PD-1 blockade, could unlock curative immune responses in renal carcinoma.

In our previous work evaluating the combination of an antiangiogenic treatment with a therapeutic vaccine, we had shown, in a subcutaneous colorectal tumor model, that sequential administration, delivering the SVX vaccine (composed of long survivin-derived peptides) after anti-angiogenic therapy (Sunitinib), induced profound tumor regression, correlating with increased pericyte coverage, CD8⁺ infiltration, and decreased immunosuppressive myeloid cells. 18 These observations underscored the importance of timing in anti-angiogenic–vaccine combinations, suggesting that an immunologically favorable window emerges after angiogenic blockade.

Building on this rationale, we sought to determine whether a similar sequential strategy could improve the efficacy of survivin vaccination in renal cell carcinoma. To address this question, we evaluated the impact of VEGFR blockade followed by SVX vaccination in preclinical RCC models.

Materials and methods

Mice

Female BALB/cANRj mice (8–10 weeks old; Janvier Labs) were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at the INSERM U970-PARCC animal facility. All experiments were approved by the French Ministry of Agriculture (APAFIS #52864). Euthanasia was performed through gradual CO2 inhalation and subsequent cervical dislocation. All experiments adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines.

Tumor cells and the generation of Luciferase-expressing cells

Renal cortical adenocarcinoma (RENCA) cells (ATCC) were transduced with lentiviral particles encoding firefly luciferase and GFP using the pTRIP-MND-Luciferase-hPGK-EGFP vector (Addgene, kindly provided by Marianne Mangeney), as previously described. 19 GFP-positive cells were sorted by flow cytometry and maintained in DMEM GlutaMAX supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, penicillin (50 U/mL), streptomycin (50 µg/mL), and sodium pyruvate (1 mmol/L).

Tumor inoculation, immunizations, and treatments

Mice were orthotopically implanted with 2 × 10⁴ RENCA-luc cells in 20 µL Matrigel (Growth Factor Reduced, Corning) into the subcapsular space of the left kidney. Tumor growth was monitored weekly by bioluminescence imaging using the Photon Imager (Biospace Lab), following intraperitoneal injection of D-luciferin (4 µg/mice) (Promega), 10 minutes prior to acquisition. Quantification of the bioluminescent signal (BLI) was performed using the manufacturer's software M3 Vision.

Axitinib (MedChemExpress) was administered by oral gavage at 50 mg/kg/d (split into two daily doses) from day 5 to day 13.

In combination regimens, the SVX vaccine, composed of three long peptides derived from survivin (S1, S2, S3; Genosphere Biotechnologies), 20 was administered intravenously (100 µg of each peptide) with CpG adjuvant (50 µg; Litenimod) at day 14, followed by a boost at day 21 without adjuvant. In the monotherapy setting, the SVX vaccine was administered at day 5, followed by a boost at day 12.

For PD-1 blockade, mice received intraperitoneal injections of anti–PD-1 antibody (clone RMP1-14, Bio X Cell; 10 mg/kg) every 3–4 d for two weeks starting at day 15.

Mice were randomized into treatment groups based on tumor burden at treatment initiation to ensure comparable baseline bioluminescence signals.

Multiplex immunofluorescence and image analysis

Tumors were fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for multiplex immunofluorescence staining. Sequential staining was performed using tyramide signal amplification. Slides were scanned using a Vectra Polaris imaging system (Akoya Biosciences), and images were analyzed using HALO software for cellular phenotyping and spatial quantification. The antibody panel included CD8, CD31, NG2, and HIF-2α. Detailed staining protocols and antibody information are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Immune cells isolation

Tumors were mechanically dissociated using a gentle MACS Dissociator and filtered to obtain single-cell suspensions. Spleens were processed by mechanical disruption. Red blood cells were lysed prior to downstream analyzes.

Flow cytometry

Cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies after viability staining and Fc receptor blocking. Intracellular staining was performed using fixation/permeabilization buffers. Data were acquired on a BD Fortessa X20 cytometer (Becton Dickinson), and analyzed using FlowJo software (BD Biosciences). Antibody panels are detailed in Supplementary Table S2.

Luminex

Tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells were enriched by negative selection using EasySepTM Mouse CD8+ T-cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies) (purity > 90%) and stimulated ex vivo using anti-CD3/CD28 beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific). At 24 h, supernatants were collected and cytokine production was measured using a multiplex bead-based assay (Luminex, Bio-Rad). The analyte concentration was calculated using a standard curve (5-PL regression) with Bio-Plex manager software. Non-detectable or non-relevant cytokines were not presented.

Ex vivo cytotoxicity assay

Tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells were isolated and cocultured with luciferase-expressing RENCA target cells at various effector-to-target (E:T) ratios (0:1, 1:1, 4:1) into 96-well round-bottom plates. D-luciferin (Promega, 150 μg/mL) was added immediately prior to acquisition, and bioluminescence imaging was performed using the Photon Imager (Biospacelab) for 20 minutes at baseline (0 h) and after 24 h of coculture. Tumor cell viability was inferred from luminescence intensity. Cytotoxic activity was quantified by comparing mean bioluminescence levels across treatment groups. Detailed experimental procedures are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Ex vivo splenocyte stimulation

Splenocytes were seeded into 96-well round-bottom plates in complete medium and stimulated with the pool of SVX peptides (10 µg/mL of each peptide) or control conditions, in the presence of GolgiPlug, for 5 h at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator. Intracellular cytokine staining was performed using the IC Buffer Set (eBioscience). Antibodies used for surface and intracellular staining are listed in Supplemental Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyzes were performed using GraphPad Prism (v9) with appropriate tests as indicated in figure legends. Exact p-values are reported in the figures and are presented to three decimal places for clarity. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sequential administration of axitinib followed by survivin vaccination synergistically restrains tumor growth in multiple models

To explore this hypothesis, we established an orthotopic model of renal cell carcinoma. We first evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of the SVX vaccine, in the RENCA-luc orthotopic model. When administered on days 5 and 12, SVX immunization led to a modest and transient delay in tumor progression, without achieving durable control (Figure 1a), consistent with previous observations in other tumor models. 20 These results highlight the need for rational combination strategies.

Statistical analysis: two-way ANOVA with Sidak's multiple comparisons for BLI, Mann–Whitney T-test for tumor weight (c). Exact p-values are reported for statistically significant comparisons.

We next investigated whether transient VEGFR blockade could enhance vaccine efficacy by remodeling the tumor microenvironment. We tested a clinically relevant tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), axitinib (a VEGFR-targeting agent) which is approved in renal cell carcinoma. 21 Axitinib monotherapy (administered from day 5 to 13) significantly delayed tumor growth, as assessed by bioluminescence imaging (Figure 1b and Sup. Fig. S1a). However, this effect was transient, as tumors resumed progression after treatment cessation, indicating that axitinib alone is insufficient to induce durable tumor control.

We then evaluated a sequential regimen in which axitinib was followed by SVX vaccination (days 14 and 21). This combination resulted in sustained tumor control, with stabilization of bioluminescence signal and a marked reduction in tumor burden compared to either monotherapy (Figure 1c). At endpoint (day 29), tumors from combination-treated mice were significantly smaller, and complete regression was observed in 30% of cases, whereas no complete responses occurred in the axitinib-only group (Figure 1c).

Importantly, untreated mice developed macroscopic lung metastases by day 20 (Sup. Fig. S1b), underscoring the aggressiveness of the model and the therapeutic benefit of early combinatorial intervention. The robustness of this synergy was further confirmed in the CT26hsurv subcutaneous model, where the same sequence outperformed both monotherapies (Sup. Fig. S2).

Together, these data demonstrate that initiating therapy with axitinib creates a permissive immune niche that enables vaccine-driven tumor regression.

Axitinib induces transient vascular remodeling and reshapes the hypoxic landscape, enabling CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration

To characterize the dynamics of axitinib-induced remodeling, tumors were collected at the end of treatment (day 14) and one week after drug withdrawal (day 21) (Figure 2a). Axitinib significantly reduced tumor weight at all timepoints compared to controls (Figure 2b).

At the end of treatment (day 14), the overall endothelial compartment was pruned: CD31⁺ vessel density fell significantly (Figure 2c) and the absolute number of mature vessels (CD31⁺ with NG2⁺ pericyte coverage) dropped in parallel (Figure 2d). Pericyte density was unaffected (Figure 2e), resulting in an unchanged pericyte-coverage index (PCI; Figure 2f). Thus, axitinib primarily removed vessels without altering pericyte association. Despite this vascular pruning, hypoxia, assessed by HIF-2α staining, did not increase, indicating that the remaining vasculature was sufficient to maintain oxygenation at this stage (Figure 2g).

One week after drug withdrawal (day 21), a rebound phase was observed. Both total and mature vessel densities increased above control levels, together with enhanced pericyte coverage (Figure 2c–f), consistent with a more stabilized vasculature. This was associated with a marked reduction in hypoxia (Figure 2g). Multiplex immunofluorescence corroborated these findings, revealing diffuse HIF-2α staining in control tumors and markedly reduced expression in axitinib-treated lesions (Figure 2h).

This vascular remodeling coincided by improved immune access. While CD8⁺ T cells were scarce at day 14, their number more than doubled by day 21 (Figure 2i), suggesting that this transient vascular reprogramming promotes T-cell infiltration.

Collectively, these data indicate that axitinib elicits a biphasic vascular remodeling program: initial vessel regression followed by revascularization and hypoxia diminution. This transition is temporally associated with increased CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, suggesting that transient vascular modulation may enhance immune accessibility and prepare the TME for subsequent immunotherapeutic interventions.

Axitinib selectively reduces tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells and suppresses their glycolytic activity

To further characterize the remodeling induced by axitinib, we analyzed immune cell populations within tumors at days 14, 21, and 29 post-treatment. While the overall CD45⁺ immune infiltrate remained comparable between groups at early timepoints and showed a modest, non-significant increase at day 29 (Figure 3a and b), axitinib markedly altered the composition of the myeloid compartment.

The proportion of CD11b⁺ myeloid cells among CD45⁺ cells progressively increased in untreated tumors, but remained significantly lower in axitinib-treated mice at all timepoints (Figure 3c), indicating selective remodeling. Further phenotypic analysis (Sup. Fig. S3) revealed a marked and sustained decrease of PMN-MDSCs (Ly6Ghi Ly6Cint), the dominant myeloid population in treated tumors (Figure 3d). These cells expanded over time in untreated mice but were significantly reduced in axitinib-treated tumors at days 21, and 29 (Figure 3d). M-MDSCs (Ly6Chi Ly6G–) were also reduced following axitinib exposure compared to untreated tumors, with the most pronounced difference observed at day 21 (Figure 3e). The proportion of tumor-associated macrophages (CD11b⁺ F4/80⁺) was significantly reduced in treated tumors at every time-point examined, although the difference was less pronounced than that observed for other myeloid populations (Figure 3f).

We next evaluated the metabolic activity of these myeloid subsets by measuring glucose uptake using the fluorescent analog 2-NBDG, an indicator for glycolytic activity known to regulate the immunosuppressive and activation programs of MDSCs and TAMs. 22 , 23 At day 14, glucose uptake was significantly reduced in PMN-MDSCs, M-MDSCs, and TAMs from axitinib-treated tumors compared to controls (Figure 3g–i). This reduction persisted and further intensified over time, culminating in markedly impaired glucose uptake by PMN-MDSC, M-MDSCs and macrophages at day 29, indicating sustained impairment of glycolytic metabolism.

Together, these data show that axitinib limits the accumulation and metabolic activity of immunosuppressive myeloid populations, potentially reducing their suppressive capacity (22,23) and contributing to a more permissive tumor microenvironment for T cell–based immunotherapy.

Axitinib transiently maintains intratumoral NK cells and preserves their proliferative and functional integrity

To assess the effects of axitinib on NK cells dynamics, we longitudinally analyzed NK cells (CD3⁻NKp46⁺) within the tumor microenvironment. In control tumors, NK cell frequency declined between days 14 and 21, whereas this decrease was prevented in axitinib-treated mice, resulting in higher NK cell levels at day 21 (Figure 4a, left).

Data are presented as mean ± s.e.m and pooled from two independent experiments (n = 8–15 mice/group). Statistical analysis was performed using two-way ANOVA and Tukey's multiple comparisons (a, left and c). Exact p-values are reported for statistically significant comparisons.

Phenotypic analysis revealed a delayed but sustained preservation of both intermediate (CD27⁺CD11b⁺) and mature (CD27⁻CD11b⁺) NK cell subsets at later timepoints in treated tumors (Figure 4a, middle and right). At the metabolic level, glucose uptake remained similar between groups at early timepoints but decreased at day 29 in axitinib-treated NK cells (Figure 4b), suggesting a shift toward a less glycolysis-dependent state during tumor remodeling. 24 Functionally, NK cell proliferation (Ki-67⁺) declined over time in untreated tumors but was maintained in axitinib-treated mice at both days 21 and 29 (Figure 4c, left). Expression of the activation receptor NKG2D was transiently increased at day 14, indicating an early activation phase (Figure 4c, right).

In addition, axitinib limited the acquisition of exhaustion features, as evidenced by reduced PD-1 (Figure 4d, left) and NKG2A (Figure 4d, right) expression at later timepoints.

Collectively, these results indicate that axitinib preserves NK cell abundance and functional fitness within the tumor microenvironment.

Axitinib reshapes the intratumoral T-cell landscape across infiltration, phenotype, and function

We next evaluated how axitinib modulates the T-cell compartment at days 14, 21, and 29 post-treatment. While the overall density of CD45⁺ leukocytes remained unchanged over time and between groups (Figure 3b), axitinib treatment significantly increased the proportion of CD3⁺ T cells among tumor-infiltrating leukocytes (Figure 5a). This enrichment was already detectable at day 14 and persisted through days 21 and 29, suggesting sustained recruitment or retention of T cells within the treated tumor compared to untreated animals.

Phenotype and function of CD8⁺ T cells (empty color histograms). (f) glucose uptake assessed by 2-NBDG incorporation by CD8⁺ T cells. Frequency of PD-1 (g) and granzyme B (h) in intratumoral CD8⁺ T cells. Data are presented as mean ± s.e.m. and pooled from two independent experiments (n = 8–15 mice/group). Statistical analysis was performed using two-way ANOVA with Sidak's multiple comparisons. Exact p-values are reported for statistically significant comparisons.

Both CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell populations contributed to this enrichment. CD4⁺ T cells were increased as early as day 14 and remained elevated over time compared to untreated tumors (Figure 5b). Within this compartment, regulatory (CD4⁺ FoxP3⁺) T cells declined at days 21 and 29 (Figure 5c), shifting the balance toward effector CD4⁺ cells. Consistent with this observation, the proportion of FoxP3⁻ CD4⁺ T cells increased reciprocally (Figure 5d).

Similarly, CD8⁺ T cells were significantly enriched at days 21 and 29 in treated tumors compared to untreated animals (Figure 5e). Phenotypic analysis revealed that glucose uptake (2-NBDG) by CD8⁺ T cells dropped markedly in treated tumors at day 29 (Figure 5f), suggesting a shift toward a less glycolytic, more memory-like metabolic state of CD8⁺ T cells, as the tumor burden diminishes under treatment. 25 , 26 In parallel, PD-1 expression on CD8⁺ T cells decreased significantly at day 29 in the axitinib group compared to the untreated group (Figure 5g), pointing to a less exhausted/chronically activated phenotype. Concomitantly, granzyme B levels were significantly higher in treated tumors at later timepoints (Figure 5h), consistent with a shift toward enhanced cytotoxic functionality.

Altogether, these results suggest that axitinib treatment induces a cytotoxic imprint and coordinated enrichment of effector populations within the tumor microenvironment.

Sequential axitinib priming followed by SVX vaccination enhances CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, polarization, and tumor-specific functionality

We next investigated how SVX vaccination administered after axitinib treatment affects the tumor immune landscape (Figure 6a). While total CD45⁺ infiltration remained unchanged (Figure 6b,c), the combination significantly increased the proportion of CD3⁺ T cells at day 29 compared to axitinib alone (Figure 6d), indicating enhanced lymphocyte accumulation.

Both CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell subsets contributed to this increase (Figure 6e–g). In the axitinib + SVX group, CD4⁺ T cells were higher at day 29 (Figure 6e) compared to axitinib alone. In the CD4⁺ compartment, vaccination promoted the emergence of cytotoxic helper (CD4+FoxP3-) T cells, as evidenced by increased granzyme B expression at day 29 (Figure 6f), without significant changes in Treg frequency (data not shown).

CD8⁺ T cells were markedly expanded at both day 21 and day 29 in the axitinib + SVX group relative to axitinib alone (Figure 6g) and displayed enhanced effector features, including substantiated increase in NKG2D expression at day 21 (Figure 6h) and Granzyme B production at day 29 (Figure 6i). Notably, PD-1 expression decreased at later time points upon vaccination, suggesting sustained functionality without overt exhaustion (Figure 6j).

To further characterize this cytotoxic response, we sorted CD8⁺ T cells directly from tumors and stimulated them ex vivo with aCD3/aCD28 beads to assess their functional capacity. Compared to axitinib alone, CD8⁺ TILs from combination-treated tumors secreted markedly higher levels of IL-2, granzyme B, and IFN-γ (Figure 6k), indicating a robust acquisition of polyfunctional and cytotoxic features. They also secreted high levels of the chemokine CCL3 (Figure 6k), which may participate to the attraction of more effectors cells. 27 , 28 These results indicate that SVX vaccination following axitinib priming generates highly reactive intratumoral CD8⁺ effector T cells.

We finally evaluated the specific immune response generated by the axitinib + SVX combination at the systemic level (Sup. Fig. S4a). Within the splenic CD45+ cell compartment, total T cells significantly increased at day 29 compared to axitinib alone (Sup. Fig. S4b). The expansion involved both CD4⁺and CD8⁺ T cells (Sup. Fig. S4c, d). The specific immune response of this peripheral pool was assessed after restimulation with the pool of SVX peptides. In vaccinated mice, the frequency of both IL-2 and Granzyme B⁺-producing CD8⁺ T cells significantly increased compared to axitinib alone (Sup. Fig. S4e).

Thus, the sequential axitinib + SVX regimen not only amplifies and polarizes the intratumoral T-cell infiltrate but also generates a vaccine-primed, antigen-specific T-cell reservoir in the periphery, providing a systemic source of effectors that can reinforce local tumor control.

Sequential addition of PD-1 blockade further enhances vaccine efficacy, sustaining tumor control and CD8⁺ effector function

We next investigated whether adding PD-1 blockade could further amplify therapeutic efficacy. This approach was motivated by two considerations: first, to relieve residual immune checkpoints potentially limiting CD8⁺ T-cell function after vaccination; and second, to align with current clinical practice in metastatic renal cell carcinoma, where axitinib is routinely combined with anti–PD-1 therapy.

To address this, we have evaluated combinations of axitinib, the SVX vaccine, and anti-PD1 as dual or triple therapies (Sup. Fig. S5a). The two triple-therapy schedules of interest were: (i) concomitant administration of anti–PD-1 with axitinib followed by vaccination ([A + ICI] + SVX), and (ii) sequential administration in which axitinib was followed by the combined introduction of SVX vaccination and PD-1 blockade (A + [SVX + ICI]) (Sup. Fig. S5a).

Bioluminescence imaging revealed a clear hierarchy. Although early coadministration of PD-1 blockade with axitinib ([A + ICI] + SVX, pink) delayed tumor progression, the sequential A + [SVX + ICI] strategy (light blue) resulted in a markedly greater and sustained reduction in tumor signal (Sup. Fig. S5b). By day 27, tumor burden was substantially lower in this group compared with both the [A + ICI] + SVX sequence and axitinib followed by PD-1 blockade alone (dark blue, dual therapy). Endpoint tumor weights mirrored these observations, and complete tumor regression was observed predominantly in the sequential regimen.

Based on these results, we selected the A + [SVX + ICI] schedule for further mechanistic analyzes. Longitudinal bioluminescence imaging revealed that the triple therapy (A + [SVX + ICI]) induced sustained tumor regression in all animals, outperforming both monotherapy and dual treatment groups (Figure 7a and b). Tumor bioluminescence signal remained stably low for at least a week after the SVX boost, and this tumor control was mirrored in endpoint tumor weights: more than 60% of mice achieved complete regression (8/13), versus 43% with A + SVX and only 17% with axitinib alone (Figure 7c). Notably, tumor clearance was achieved after a relatively short course of PD-1 blockade, suggesting that immune activation in the axitinib-primed setting is both rapid and effective.

At the immune level, triple therapy further enhanced T-cell infiltration and activation compared to dual therapy. The proportion of total intratumoral T cells increased significantly at day 29 (Figure 7d), reflecting an amplified lymphocyte accumulation within the tumor microenvironment. CD4⁺ T cells, which were scarce following axitinib alone, expanded markedly after SVX vaccination and increased further with the addition of PD-1 blockade (Figure 7e). This expansion was accompanied by a strong increase in proliferative capacity, as indicated by Ki-67 expression (Figure 7f), and by an increase of cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells expressing granzyme B (Figure 7g).

Similarly, CD8⁺ T cells were substantially enriched under triple therapy, with nearly a fourfold increase compared to axitinib alone (Figure 7h). These cells exhibited a highly activated phenotype compared to the bi-therapy regimen, characterized by reduced PD-1 expression (Figure 7i) and enhanced expression of NKG2D (Figure 7j), increased proliferation (Figure 7k), and granzyme B production (Figure 7l), consistent with potent effector differentiation.

Functional analyzes further demonstrated the superior quality of CD8⁺ T cells induced by triple therapy. Upon ex vivo stimulation, these cells produced higher levels of IL-2 and the chemokine CCL4 (MIP-1β) compared to dual therapy (Figure 7m). Given the role of CCL4 in recruiting cytotoxic lymphocytes and amplifying immune responses, 28 , 29 this suggests the establishment of a reinforced chemokine network that supports sustained effector cell accumulation and activity within the tumor.

Systemic immune responses were also enhanced, as evidenced by increased frequencies of IFN-γ-producing CD4⁺ T cells and IL-2 and Granzyme B-producing CD8⁺ T cells following ex vivo splenocytes restimulation with survivin peptides, in the triple therapy compared to the bi-therapy group (Sup. Fig. S5c), indicating the generation of a robust and antigen-specific immune response beyond the tumor site.

Finally, the functional relevance of these phenotypic changes was confirmed using an ex vivo cytotoxicity assay. CD8⁺ T cells isolated from triple-treated tumors exhibited markedly enhanced tumor-killing capacity, achieving near-complete elimination of RENCA-luc target cells at multiple effector-to-target ratios (Figure 7n). In contrast, CD8⁺ T cells from axitinib + SVX-treated tumors showed only partial cytotoxic activity.

In conclusion, the sequential regimen that couples axitinib-driven TME remodeling with the concomitant delivery of SVX vaccination and PD-1 blockade unleashes a coordinated, multilayered antitumor response. This schedule not only achieves the deepest and most durable tumor regressions, but also reshapes the microenvironment by expanding highly proliferative CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ effector pools, and endowing them with a polyfunctional, survivin-specific cytokine and cytotoxic signature.

Discussion

Our study identifies an optimally timed triple regimen, consisting of transient VEGFR blockade with axitinib followed by survivin (SVX) vaccination and PD-1 blockade, that converts an orthotopic mRCC model from an immunologically “cold” state into a tumor dominated by polyfunctional, tumor-specific T cells. These findings highlight the critical role of temporal coordination in maximizing the efficacy of combination immunotherapies.

Building on our previous work in the CT26 model, where sequential administration of sunitinib followed by SVX vaccination induced a strong and durable antitumor response, 18 the present study extends this paradigm to renal cell carcinoma using axitinib. Our data suggest that VEGFR-targeted vascular conditioning followed by immunization represents a generalizable strategy across tumor types. Importantly, the superiority of sequential over concomitant administration underscores the importance of treatment scheduling, rather than the sole combination of modalities.

Mechanistically, axitinib induced a biphasic remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. An initial phase of vascular pruning was followed by a rebound characterized by increased pericyte-covered vessels and reduced hypoxia. This transition was associated with enhanced CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and preservation of proliferative NK cells, consistent with previous reports showing that VEGFR TKIs can transiently improve immune accessibility. 7 , 30-32 In parallel, axitinib reduced the frequencies of PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs and impaired their glycolytic activity, a metabolic state closely linked to their immunosuppressive function. 33 , 34 Rather than inducing an immediate depletion of myeloid suppressor populations, axitinib appeared to restrain their progressive accumulation during tumor evolution, resulting in markedly lower PMN-MDSC and M-MDSC frequencies at later time points. Together, these changes suggest that VEGFR inhibition reshapes both vascular and myeloid compartments, creating a transiently permissive niche for immune activation. 8 , 35 , 36

Interestingly, CD8⁺ T cells in axitinib-treated tumors displayed reduced glucose uptake at later timepoints while maintaining high granzyme B expression and lower PD-1 levels, indicative of a metabolically restrained but functionally competent state. This phenotype aligns with recent observations that less glycolytic, memory-like T cells exhibit superior persistence and responsiveness to checkpoint blockade. 37-41 Thus, vascular and metabolic reprogramming induced by axitinib may collectively support the emergence of durable antitumor immunity. In addition to vascular and immune remodeling, it remains possible that axitinib may contribute to immune activation through immunogenic cell death, as reported for certain TKIs, although this mechanism was not directly investigated in the present study.

Despite the broad expression of survivin (BIRC5) in renal cell carcinoma and its recognition as an immunogenic target, 42 , 43 previous vaccination strategies have shown limited clinical efficacy. One likely explanation is that vaccination has often been performed in VEGF-rich, immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments that are unfavorable to effective priming. VEGF not only disrupts vascular architecture and promotes hypoxia, but also directly impairs dendritic cell maturation and antigen presentation, thereby limiting T-cell activation. 44-46 In this context, our data provide a mechanistic rationale for combining vaccination with prior vascular conditioning.

Our data demonstrate that administering SVX vaccination immediately after axitinib withdrawal enabled the generation of robust tumor-specific CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell responses. Notably, the timing of vaccination appears critical. In peptide-based immunization, maximal CD8⁺ T-cell expansion typically occurs several days after priming. 47 In our model, this peak coincided with a phase of vascular stabilization and reduced hypoxia, suggesting a temporal alignment between antigen-specific T-cell expansion and improved tumor accessibility. This synchronization likely facilitates efficient infiltration and functional maturation of effector T cells.

SVX vaccination not only increased T-cell infiltration but also promoted a shift toward a cytotoxic Th1/Tc1 phenotype, characterized by enhanced granzyme B production and reduced expression of exhaustion markers. Moreover, CD8⁺ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes exhibited a polyfunctional profile, secreting IL-2, IFN-γ, and the chemokine CCL3. The latter may contribute to the recruitment of additional effector cells, establishing a positive feedback loop that amplifies the antitumor response. 27 , 28 In parallel, vaccination generated a systemic reservoir of survivin-specific T cells, supporting sustained immune pressure on the tumor.

The addition of PD-1 blockade further enhanced this response, but only when administered concomitantly with vaccination and after axitinib priming. In contrast, initiating PD-1 blockade during axitinib treatment was less effective, despite partial tumor control. These findings indicate that checkpoint inhibition is most effective when applied after antigen-specific T-cell priming rather than during early vascular remodeling. This observation is consistent with clinical data suggesting that the benefit of combining VEGFR inhibitors with ICIs may depend on treatment timing and prior immune activation. 48 , 49 Supporting this notion, a preclinical mRCC study demonstrated that a brief 1-week VEGFR-TKI lead-in markedly potentiated the effect of subsequent PD-1 blockade. 50

Mechanistically, the triple therapy induced a marked expansion of proliferative and highly cytotoxic CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, accompanied by increased production of chemokines such as CCL4, adding an additional layer to the chemokine program initiated under axitinib + SVX vaccination. CCL3 production in the dual-therapy group may help initiate the recruitment and expansion of cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells. By comparison, CCL4 has been linked to sustained effector-cell infiltration and functional activity within tumors. 28 , 29 Its induction following PD-1 blockade suggests that checkpoint release enables tumor-specific CD8⁺ T cells to fully express this chemotactic and effector-promoting program. The joint presence of CCL3 and CCL4 therefore reflects a strengthened chemokine circuit that both expands and sustains a highly functional intratumoral CD8⁺ T cells pool, providing a mechanistic explanation for the superior efficacy of triple therapy. Functional killing assays confirmed that these T cells possess potent cytotoxic activity, supporting their central role in mediating tumor regression.

These findings underscore that therapeutic efficacy is not solely determined by the combination of agents, but by their temporal orchestration. Administering PD-1 blockade after vaccination enables the release of inhibitory constraints on newly primed T cells at their peak functional state, thereby amplifying a polyfunctional and sustained immune response. In contrast, early PD-1 blockade, delivered before effective antigen-specific priming, appears insufficient to generate durable tumor control, likely due to the limited availability of tumor-reactive T cells.

Our results have direct clinical implications. Current mRCC regimens typically combine VEGFR TKIs and PD-1 blockade concurrently. In contrast, our data support a short course of VEGFR inhibition followed by vaccination and delayed checkpoint blockade as a strategy to enhance therapeutic efficacy. The feasibility of such an approach is supported by clinical studies such as the NAXIVA trial, which demonstrated the biological impact of short-term axitinib treatment. 36 Importantly, this approach introduces a tumor-antigen–specific component that is currently absent from standard combinations, and may contribute to the generation of de novo tumor-specific immunity. Aligning PD-1 blockade with a phase of active T-cell priming and intratumoral recruitment may further amplify the magnitude and durability of the antitumor response.

One limitation of the present study is that long-term metastatic progression was not specifically evaluated, although untreated animals developed spontaneous pulmonary metastases, supporting the clinical relevance of the orthotopic model. Future studies will therefore be needed to determine whether the therapeutic benefit observed here also translates into durable control of metastatic dissemination.

In conclusion, by combining vascular remodeling, antigen-specific priming, and checkpoint release, we establish a mechanistically grounded framework for combination immunotherapy in renal cell carcinoma. This sequential strategy promotes the emergence of a coordinated and polyfunctional immune response capable of mediating deep tumor regression in a stringent preclinical model. These findings provide a strong rationale for further clinical investigation of temporally optimized combinations integrating VEGFR inhibition, therapeutic vaccination, and PD-1 blockade, particularly in light of recent advances in personalized cancer vaccines.
