Emergent social transmission of model-based representations without inference
Silja Keßler 1 (silja.kessler@student.uni-tuebingen.de), Miriam Bautista-Salinero 1 , Claudio Tennie 1 & Charley M. Wu 2,3 (charley.wu@tu-darmstadt.de) 1 University of Tübingen, Tübingen, Germany 2 Technical University Darmstadt, Darmstadt, Germany 3 Hessian AI, Darmstadt, Germany

Abstract
How do people acquire rich, flexible knowledge about their environment from others despite limited cognitive capacity? Humans are often thought to rely on computationally costly mentalizing, such as inferring others’ beliefs. In contrast, cultural evolution emphasizes that behavioral transmission can be supported by simple social cues. Using reinforcement learning simulations, we show how minimal social learning can indirectly transmit higher-level representations. We simulate a naïve agent searching for rewards in a reconfigurable environment, learning either alone or by observing an expert—crucially, without inferring mental states. Instead, the learner heuristically selects actions or boosts value representations based on observed actions. Our results demonstrate that these cues bias the learner’s experience, causing its representation to converge toward the expert’s. Model-based learners benefit most from social exposure, showing faster learning and more expert-like representations. These findings show how cultural transmission can arise from simple, non-mentalizing processes exploiting asocial learning mechanisms. Keywords: Interactive behavior; Social cognition; Agent-based Modeling; Computational Modeling



Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a powerful framework for understanding how agents acquire knowledge through interaction with their environment
Sutton & Barto (2018)
. Traditionally, this framework has focused on solitary learning: individual agents learn by interacting directly with their environment and update internal representations based on individual experience. While this paradigm has yielded substantial theoretical and practical advances
Shteingart & Loewenstein (2014)
;
O’Doherty et al. (2015)
, it stands in contrast to how learning often unfolds in natural settings: humans and other animals routinely benefit from
social
information
Laland (2004)
;
Olsson et al. (2020)
, leveraging the experiences of others to accelerate learning
Rendell, Boyd et al. (2010)
;
Park et al. (2017)
, reduce exploration costs
Witt et al. (2024)
;
Garg et al. (2022)
, and improve performance in complex environments
Wu et al. (2025)
;
Hawkins et al. (2023)
.

Thus, recent work in RL has increasingly incorporated social learning mechanisms, motivated by both the goal of modeling human social cognition
Wu et al. (2022)
;
Hackel et al. (2020)
;
Najar et al. (2020)
and for engineering applications where artificial agents learn from observing human behavior
Peters & Schaal (2008)
;
Urain et al. (2025)
. In these approaches, learning from others is often modeled as
mentalizing

Baker et al. (2009)
;
Jara-Ettinger (2019)
—inferring latent mental states such as goals, preferences, or beliefs about the structure of the environment from observed actions
(
Tomasello (1999)
, also known as Theory of Mind;)
. Techniques like inverse reinforcement learning
(
Russell (1998)
;
Jara-Ettinger (2019)
;
Jern et al. (2017)
;
Collette et al. (2017)
, IRL;)
formalize this process by inverting the RL model to “unpack” behavior into underlying value or model-based representations of the world (i.e., transition structure). Although powerful, such inference methods are computationally costly and intractable in many settings
Wu et al. (2022)
.

In contrast, cultural evolution research has long emphasized that surprisingly sophisticated patterns of behavioral transmission can emerge from simple, low-cost social cues
Heyes (2018)
;
Mesoudi (2016)
. Individuals often rely on simple heuristics, such as selectively imitating the most successful
McElreath et al. (2008)
or the most prestigious members of a group
Jiménez & Mesoudi (2019)
;
Boyd & Richerson (1988)
, without needing to infer mental states of others.
These mechanisms can nonetheless support the accumulation of complex behaviors and knowledge across generations
Legare & Nielsen (2015)
;
Rendell, Fogarty & Laland (2010)
, suggesting effective social learning may not require expensive inferences required for mentalizing
Roberts-Gaal et al. (2025)
;
Uchiyama et al. (2023)
.

In this work, we explore how higher-level representations of value and model-based transition structure can emerge in RL agents from simple forms of social learning—without explicit inference of others’ mental states—and how they support generalization to novel conditions. We simulate agents that search for rewards in a spatially reconfigurable environment, learning either independently or by observing an expert. Crucially, learners do not attempt to infer the expert’s goals or internal model of the environment; instead, they rely on social cues to guide learning, adopting strategies more similar to imitation
Byrne & Russon (1998)
or local enhancement
Galef (1988)
than mentalizing. We specifically consider two social learning strategies at different representation levels:
decision biasing

(
Toyokawa et al. (2019)
, DB;)
, which shapes the agent’s policy to favor actions that mimic the expert’s behavior, and
value shaping

(
Najar et al. (2020)
, VS;)
, which enhances the agent’s expected value for actions performed by the expert. Both strategies are applied to model-free and model-based agents, allowing social cues to guide learning either through model-free habit formation or via model-based planning
Drummond & Niv (2020)
;
Wu et al. (2022)
;
Hackel et al. (2019)
.

Our simulations show that social learning confers substantial benefits even without explicit inference. Across experiments, social learners—particularly those with model-based architectures—achieve better performance compared to asocial learners and adopt more expert-like value and belief representations, supporting effective generalization to novel reward configurations and starting positions. These results demonstrate that simple, non-mentalizing social mechanisms can produce higher-level, flexible representations, highlighting a computational pathway through which complex cultural transmission can emerge from minimal social information.

Methods

We simulated agent behavior across three experiments (
n=1,000
per experiment) using a spatially reconfigurable environment, resembling a foraging-themed board game. Agents navigated generated grid-worlds over 20 episodes to acquire rewards, learning through a combination of individual experience and observing an expert during training (episodes 1-10 only). Agent performance was then evaluated in the test phase (episodes 11-20) to assess generalization in the absence of the expert.

Environment and Rewards.

The environment was composed of four quadrants, each forming a
5\times 5
grid with a fixed configuration of walls blocking movement between adjacent states and a single designated reward state (Fig.
1
a).
In each simulation, we randomly rotated and rearranged these quadrants to create a
10\times 10
grid world (
4!\cdot 4^{4}=6,144
unique configurations), generating different global layouts (Fig.
1
b).

Reward values for each designated reward state were randomly assigned (without replacement) at the start of each simulation, based on four distinct reward values: 0 (no reward), 25 (low), 50 (medium), and 75 (high). The learner agent had no prior knowledge of either the locations or the values of the rewards. To introduce stochasticity, random noise
\epsilon
was added for each reward observation defined using a shifted binomial distribution
\epsilon\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(n=400,p=0.5)-200
, which yields integer values with a mean of 0 and a variance of 100. This noise was applied independently to each reward in every episode.

Procedure.

At the start of each episode, the agent is placed randomly in one of four start states at the center of the board. The agent moves between adjacent states by executing actions (up, down, left, right) and is constrained by walls: attempts to move into a wall result in the agent remaining in the current state. Actions that do not yield a positive reward incurs a cost (negative reward) of
-1
. An episode terminates either when a positive reward is obtained or after 40 actions.

Simulations are divided into a
training phase
(episodes 1-10) and a
test phase
(episodes 11-20; Fig.
1
b). During the training phase, learners were accompanied by an expert, an asocial model-based RL agent (see Fig.
2
) that was pre-trained for 120 episodes in the same environment. Thus, the training phase offers social learning agents (defined below) the ability to combine information from the expert with knowledge gained through their own experiences. In contrast, asocial learners can only acquire knowledge through their direct interaction with the environment. However, in the subsequent test phase, the expert is no longer present, meaning all learners must continue to learn asocially.

Experiments.

We conducted three experiments to assess the learners’ ability to generalize knowledge acquired during training (Fig.
1
c). Exp. 1 establishes baseline performance and examines the transfer of values and model-based beliefs from the expert to social learners, without any changes to the environment. In Exps. 2 and 3, the test phase introduced additional modifications to the task to assess the learner’s robustness to changes in the environment. Exp. 2 modified the reward structure, by swapping the values of two randomly selected reward states. Exp. 3 modified the starting locations, by shifting start states one tile towards the corners (excluding reward states), while keeping the spatial layout fixed.

Six learning conditions were tested, combining model-free (MF) and model-based (MB) RL with either asocial learning (AS) or social learning from an expert. Social learning was implemented at two levels: policy-based (DB) and value-based (VS).

Computational models

We compare six computational learning models (Fig.
2
) within a
2\times 3
factorial design that contrasts model-free and model-based RL across three forms of social information integration: asocial learning (AS), decision biasing (DB), and value shaping (VS). Asocial learners exclusively rely on individual experiences, whereas social learners additionally incorporate expert information either at the action selection level by biasing their policy toward the expert’s behavior (DB), or at the value learning level by directly shaping value representations (VS) based on observed expert actions.

Model-free (MF) learning

is implemented as Q-learning with Temporal Difference (TD) prediction updates:

Q(s,a)\leftarrow Q(s,a)+\alpha\left(R(s)+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-Q(s,a)\right)

(1)

where
Q(s,a)
is the state-action value indicating the expected cumulative rewards for performing action
a
in state
s
. Q-values are updated based on the learning rate
\alpha
and proportional to the difference between the prior Q-value and the sum of the reward obtained in the current state
R(s)
and the maximum next-state Q-value
\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})
discounted by a temporal discount factor
\gamma\in[0,1]
.

Using these learned Q-values, the asocial learner computes a policy
\pi_{asoc}
based on a softmax function whose randomness is controlled by the inverse-temperature parameter
\beta
:

\pi_{asoc}(a|s)\propto\exp\left(\beta\cdot Q(s,a)\right)

(2)

Model-based (MB) learning

is implemented using Dyna-Q
Sutton (1990)
, which is a variant of Q-learning where the agent maintains an internal
belief

B
about the environment—equivalent to a world model
Hafner et al. (2025)
;
Ha & Schmidhuber (2018)
;
Mantiuk et al. (2025)
—that can be used to more efficiently update state-action values through internal simulations, rather than relying solely on direct experience. The agent updates beliefs according to a Delta-rule update:

B(s^{\prime}|s,a)\leftarrow B(s^{\prime}|s,a)+\eta\left(\delta(s^{\prime},s)-B(s^{\prime}|s,a)\right)

(3)

where
B(s^{\prime}|s,a)
represents the agent’s belief about the transition probabilities between states, and the Kronecker delta
\delta(s^{\prime},s)=1
if the transition
s\rightarrow s^{\prime}
is successful, and 0 otherwise
Antonov & Dayan (2025)
.

Q-values can thus be updated through simulated experiences (dependent on
B
), rather than solely relying on direct RL by executing actions in the world. This allows for more efficient planning-based decision-making and is a cornerstone of modern theories of human learning
Daw et al. (2011)
;
Drummond & Niv (2020)
;
Wu et al. (2022)
. The following model-based planning loop is executed
k
times after each action, with
k\sim\textrm{Pois}(\lambda)
sampled each time from a Poisson distribution to introduce stochasticity and
\lambda
is treated as a free parameter (planning rate).

Decision-biasing (DB)

is inspired by an algorithmic account of social learning in bandit tasks
Toyokawa et al. (2019)
;
Witt et al. (2024)
, but modified for our sequential navigation task. We first introduce a pure social policy
\pi_{soc}
:

\pi_{\text{soc}}(a\mid s)=\mathbb{I}\!\left[a=\arg\min_{a^{\prime}}D\bigl(s^{t}_{\text{expert}},s^{\prime}(s,a^{\prime})\bigr)\right]

(4)

where the indicator function
\mathbb{I}[\cdot]
assigns 1 to the action that minimizes the distance
D
, defined as the number of actions needed to reach the expert’s last observed state
s_{expert}^{t}
from the learner’s current state
s^{\prime}
, and 0 otherwise. This social policy is then combined with the asocial policy (Eq.
2
) using the mixture parameter
\omega\in[0,1]
to compute an overall policy:

\pi_{\textrm{DB}}=(1-\omega)\pi_{asoc}(s,a)+\omega\pi_{soc}(s,a)

(5)

When the expert terminates an episode prior to the learner, the location last visited by the expert is frozen to allow continued social learning.

Value shaping (VS)

is inspired by theoretical accounts of the local enhancement of stimuli based on observing others engaging with them
Spence (1937)
;
Galef (1988)
. Following
Najar et al. (2020)
, we implement VS by augmenting the learner’s value representations of actions performed by the expert in a given state:

Q(s_{expert},a_{expert})\leftarrow Q(s_{expert},a_{expert})+\kappa\mathbb{I}(s_{expert},a_{expert})

(6)

where
\kappa
is a bonus parameter that is applied exclusively to the state-action pair observed in the expert. The bonus directly shapes the value function by enhancing state-action pairs observed in the expert. The boosted Q-values are then passed through a softmax (cf. Eq.
2
), increasing the likelihood of actions observed in the expert in the corresponding state.
Once the expert reaches a termination step, the VS learner reduces to asocial learning for the remainder of the episode.

Parameter optimization.

We optimized each model’s hyperparameters using the differential evolution algorithm
Storn & Price (1997)
to maximize performance (cumulative reward) across 1,000 independent simulations in the baseline environment. Optimization for social learners was based solely on the 10 training episodes, whereas asocial agents and the expert were optimized using all available episodes (20 and 120, respectively).
Learning rates
\alpha
and
\eta
for social learners were fixed to the values optimized for asocial learners, due to joint optimization consistently converging to low learning rates that suppressed individual learning and led to poor test performance. Candidate parameters were optimized in a transformed, unbounded space to enforce valid ranges (e.g., log-transform for
\beta\in[0,\infty])
and a fixed random seed was used to ensure reproducibility.

Results

We first evaluated the performance of all learning strategies across episodes, distinguishing between training and test phases to characterize how effectively agents acquire and apply knowledge, depending on their learning strategy. Next, we examined how information is indirectly transferred from expert to social learners by analyzing value and belief representations, assessing whether social learners acquire similar value and state transition representations as encoded by the expert. Finally, we tested the robustness of the learned knowledge under environmental changes: in Experiment 2 we assessed how well learners generalize to modified reward configurations, and in Experiment 3 we modified the starting position to evaluate whether the belief transferred from the expert is preserved and continues to support behavior under novel start conditions. Across analyses, variability across simulations was quantified using the standard error of the mean (SEM).

Exp. 1: Value and belief transfer without inference

Exp. 1 establishes a baseline, where the only change from training to test phases is the absence of the expert. We quantified performance as the mean cumulative reward per learner across simulations, demonstrating how effectively different learning strategies enable agents to navigate the environment and collect rewards over the course of the 20 episodes.

Starting with the asocial agents (AS), we see a strong advantage of model-based (MB) compared to model-free (MF) learning (Fig.
3
a). However, all social learning agents (DB and VS) outperformed the best asocial agent (AS-MB) in the training phase as a result of leveraging social information from the expert. During training, VS agents performed better than DB agents, with no difference between MB vs. MF. However, we see larger differences in the test phase when the expert disappears. The most striking difference is the collapse of DB-MF performance, which falls below the AS-MF agent. By contrast, the DB-MB agent maintains strong performance and, notably, outperforms the AS-MB agent in the test phase, reaching the level of the VS agents (both MB and MF). These results demonstrate the brittleness of policy-based social learning (DB) using only model-free methods (MF), while also showcasing the robustness of model-based social learning (MB).

We then computed indirect
value transfer
based on the correspondence between the learner’s and expert’s state-action value representations at the end of each simulation (averaged across simulations). Specifically, we computed Spearman correlations between value functions across states, grouped by distance from the nearest reward to account for goal-relevance (i.e., states closer to rewards have greater relevance). This allows us to assess how effectively the learner reconstructs the expert’s value information. Figure
3
b shows that except for DB-MF, all social agents achieved greater value transfer compared to asocial baselines (AS-MB and AS-MF). While asocial agents are bound to acquire similar value representations as the expert due to interacting with the same environment, social agents (except for DB-MF) achieve a substantial boost in this respect, providing a mechanistic explanation for their better performance. This value transfer effect (excepting DB-MF), displayed goal-relevance, with stronger transfer for states closer to rewards.

Lastly, we quantified indirect
belief transfer
for model-based learners as the correspondence between the learner’s and expert’s transition matrices at the end of each simulation. Pearson correlations were computed across states grouped by distance from rewards, and normalized relative to AS-MB as an asocial baseline to isolate the effect of social learning (Fig.
3
c). Thus, the positive belief transfer for both model-based social learners (equivalent for DB-MB and VS-MB) indicates that they acquired an internal model of state transitions that more closely matches the expert’s, compared the asocial baseline. This effect is also influenced by goal-relevance, with states closer to rewards corresponding to greater belief transfer to our model-based social learners.

Exp. 2: Robustness to changes in reward

In Exp. 2, the test phase additionally included swapping the values of two randomly selected reward states (Fig.
1
c). As in Exp. 1, we quantified performance as the mean cumulative reward per learner across simulations to assess how effectively different learning strategies generalize to the altered reward contingencies, focusing on the test phase since the training phase was identical to Exp. 1.

Performance initially dropped for all agents in the test phase, except for AS-MF, reflecting the challenge of adapting to new reward contingencies. The most pronounced drop occurred for DB-MF agents, reinforcing the vulnerability of policy-based, model-free social learning. In contrast, all model-based agents adapted to the new reward configuration and recovered performance, with VS-MB performing best.

To examine the underlying mechanisms, we analyzed
value accuracy
by computing the Spearman correlation between each learner’s final value function (averaged across simulations) and the optimal value function for the new reward configuration, derived from the Bellman
Bellman (1957)
equation:

Q^{*}(s,a)=\sum_{s^{\prime}}P(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\Big[R(s,a,s^{\prime})+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q^{*}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\Big]

(7)

with
\gamma=0.99
.
Here, we only included states visited at least once by the learner to avoid distortions from unvisited states. Correlations were again grouped by distance from the nearest reward to account for goal-relevance.

Our results show that social learning facilitates better generalization to changes in reward structure, with all social agents achieving greater value accuracy compared to asocial baselines (AS-MB and AS-MF), except for DB-MF, whose poorly aligned value representations explain its poor performance (Fig.
4
b). Thus, even without mentalizing, the transfer of knowledge facilitated by simple social learning mechanisms is useful even when reward contingencies change.

Exp. 3: Robustness to changes in starting location

In Exp. 3, we tested robustness to changes in the starting positions (Fig.
1
c).
Focusing on the test phase, asocial learners continued to perform well, showing little sensitivity to the new start positions. VS agents (both MF and MB) experienced a small initial dip in performance, but rapidly recovered to training-level performance. A striking divergence emerged among the DB agents: DB-MB slightly improved its performance, whereas DB-MF suffered a severe collapse similar to Exps. 1 and 2. These results reinforce the finding that policy-based social learning is fragile under environmental changes unless paired with a model-based system. Importantly, model-based social learners (DB-MB and VS-MB) maintained a clear performance advantage over the asocial baseline (AS-MB) throughout the test phase, highlighting the robustness of model-based social strategies.

To examine whether beliefs acquired via the expert’s actions were preserved under novel starting positions (Fig.
4
d), we focused on MB agents and compared the degree of indirect belief transfer (correlation between agent and expert transition matrices; grouped across distances) between Exp. 1 (baseline start positions) versus Exp. 3 (new start positions). Social learners, particularly VS-MB, maintained high similarity across conditions, indicating that the reconstructed beliefs were largely preserved. Residuals (Fig.
4
d inset) revealed greater variability for the AS-MB agent compared to the DB and VS agents, showcasing that social learners acquire more accurate and robust beliefs by leveraging the expert’s knowledge, resulting in more stable internal representations.

Overall, these results demonstrate that model-based social learners not only acquire similar structural knowledge to the expert, but also exhibit robust performance under environmental changes—all without mentalizing.

Discussion

We investigated how rich, flexible knowledge can be socially transmitted
without
mentalizing, thus sidestepping computationally costly inferences about others’ goals or beliefs. Using simulations with both model-free (MF) and model-based (MB) reinforcement learning (RL) agents, we show that learners can acquire higher-level representations through simple social heuristics. Specifically, decision-biasing (DB) implemented at the policy-level simply seeks to minimize distance to an expert demonstrator (Eqs.
4
-
5
), while value shaping (VS) adds a value bonus to state-action pairs observed in the expert, analogous to local enhancement (Eq.
6
).

Using three simulation-based experiments in a spatial foraging task, we found that social learning particularly benefits MB agents, which acquire more accurate and robust representations, while also generalizing better to changes in rewards and starting locations. Across both MB and MF agents, value-based social learning using VS generally outperformed policy-based methods using DB, echoing a general principle in RL that value is a more generalizable representation than actions
Lehnert et al. (2020)
;
Wu et al. (2022)
.

What are the mechanisms behind these results? Even simple social heuristics bias the learner’s experience, which, when leveraged by asocial model-based planning, can support more accurate representations of value and environment structure
Wu et al. (2022)
;
Uchiyama et al. (2023)
. In our simulations, MB agents use Dyna (Alg. 1) to simulate experiences and propagate values, analogous to the role of hippocampal replay
Ólafsdóttir et al. (2018)
;
Miller et al. (2017)
;
Vikbladh et al. (2019)
. This suggests that insight can emerge from naïve imitation
Lyons et al. (2007)
by exploiting internal planning mechanisms, consistent with neural evidence that social observation engages replay-related activity, highlighting a shared neural basis for social and asocial model-based learning.

Our findings also offer a perspective on cross-species differences between humans and non-human primates
(
Bandini & Tennie (2023)
;
Call et al. (2005)
, NHP;)
. Humans over-imitate, copying even irrelevant actions
Horner & Whiten (2005)
;
Lyons et al. (2007)
, whereas chimpanzees tend to emulate outcomes rather than copy specific behavior
Tennie et al. (2010)
. Our results demonstrate that policy-based social learning (i.e., at the level of actions) is fragile under model-free learning (DB-MF), but robust when paired with model-based mechanisms (DB-MB). While the literature on model-based learning in NHPs is scarce
(but see
Sato et al. (2023)
, for a task disincentivizing MB learning)
, differences in reliance on model-based planning may shift the cost-benefit trade-off between policy-based and value-based social learning
Wu et al. (2022)
, helping to explain why over-imitation is more adaptive in humans than in chimpanzees.

Despite promising results, there are several limitations that could be addressed in future work.
First, while we evaluated robustness to changes in rewards and starting locations, we did not manipulate the underlying environmental structure itself (e.g., changes to transition dynamics or wall layouts), which would allow us to better assess whether non-mentalizing social learning also supports the transfer of deeper structural knowledge.
Second, we modeled the expert as a passive demonstrator rather than providing pedagogical demonstrations
Vélez et al. (2023)
. Future work could test how such demonstrations might interact with or amplify our minimal social learning mechanisms. Finally, our results are based on simulations, whereas testing these predictions in human behavioral experiments will be essential to assess whether similar non-mentalizing processes can also support higher-order social transmission in people.

In sum, we provide a computational account of how the indirect transmission of high-level representations can arise from minimal, non-mentalizing forms of social learning that exploit asocial model-based RL mechanisms.
