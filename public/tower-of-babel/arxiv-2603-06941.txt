Abstract
Adaptive experiments are used extensively in online platforms, healthcare and biotechnology, and a variety of other settings. In many of these applications, the main goal is not to precisely estimate a treatment effect, but to demonstrate that at least one candidate intervention yields a positive effect, for some subpopulation, on some measured outcome. We formalize this objective in a multi-armed bandit framework and develop inference procedures for testing whether any arm’s mean exceeds a given threshold under fully adaptive sampling: one which pools information across promising arms, and one which corresponds to time-uniform multiple inference on the means of individual arms. To support the latter, we establish a moderate deviations principle for the sequential t t -statistic, justifying anytime-valid testing of a large number of hypotheses concurrently. To illustrate how adaptive design can target the proposed statistics, we recast experimental design as bandit optimization where an arm’s reward corresponds to its signal-to-noise ratio, and analyze an adaptive allocation rule for which we establish a logarithmic regret bound.



Keywords:
Hypothesis testing, experimental design, multi-armed bandit, sequential inference

Introduction

Not all randomized experiments are conducted with the same goal in mind.
In many settings, the primary goal is to obtain a precise estimate of the average effect of an intervention. In others, researchers seek to understand treatment effect heterogeneity, for example by modeling effects as a function of pre-treatment covariates.
A third common objective is to determine an optimal treatment policy from a set of interventions and targeting rules.

In this paper, we focus on a fourth objective: determining whether some treatment level has a positive effect on at least one outcome for at least some subpopulation. This question arises naturally in early-stage or exploratory experimentation, where a decision maker must determine whether an intervention shows sufficient promise to justify a larger, more costly, and more tightly controlled experiment. Often, decision makers face a large number of candidate interventions, outcomes, or subpopulations, and must allocate limited experimental resources accordingly.

We study experimental designs that adaptively allocate samples in order to improve the power to detect the existence of effects which exceed a threshold value. We refer to such studies as
demonstration experiments
, reflecting the idea that their main purpose is to establish the presence of an effect rather than to estimate its magnitude. Unlike classical A/B tests, the goal is not to estimate an average treatment effect precisely, nor to identify the single best arm, but rather to accumulate statistical evidence that at least one meaningful effect is present.

What is the potential benefit of adaptivity in this setting? In the simplest case of a single treatment and a single outcome in a homogeneous population, adaptive designs offer little advantage. However, when treatment effects are heterogeneous across arms, outcomes, or subpopulations, adaptively reallocating samples toward promising effects can substantially improve power relative to uniform designs.

We consider three stylized settings that motivate our framework. First, consider a sequential experiment with a binary treatment and a discrete pre-treatment variable defining mutually exclusive subpopulations. At each stage, the experimenter selects which subpopulation to sample and assigns treatment within that group. The objective is to detect whether the treatment has a positive effect for at least one subpopulation. This setting can be naturally viewed as a multi-armed bandit problem, where each arm corresponds to a subpopulation and the goal is to detect the existence of a positive effect rather than to maximize cumulative reward.

Second, suppose there are
k
outcomes of interest, and the goal is to detect a difference between treated and control units for some outcome. Different outcome contrasts may yield different power, and adaptively focusing on informative contrasts can improve detection in settings where measurement is costly. Unlike standard bandit problems, experiments of this form may reveal values for multiple contrasts simultaneously, so that the feedback structure is richer than the classical bandit setting in which only the reward of the chosen action is observed.

The same is true for inference on subpopulations, if the subpopulations are not mutually exclusive.

Finally, consider an experiment with a treatment taking
k

Although these examples differ superficially, they share a common structure: the experimenter repeatedly chooses among several options—treatments, subpopulations, or contrasts—and observes noisy outcomes, with the goal of detecting the existence of a meaningful effect. Our analysis formalizes this structure using a multi-armed bandit framework and develops inference procedures that remain valid under adaptive sampling.

1.1
Contributions and related work

This paper makes three main contributions. First, we formalize the problem of demonstration experiments as a hypothesis-testing problem under adaptive experimental design, where the objective is to detect the existence of a large enough effect across multiple arms rather than to estimate effects precisely or to identify a single best arm. We show that this objective leads naturally to test statistics that are robust to strategic sample allocation.

Second, we propose two testing procedures—pooled testing and max testing—that remain valid under a wide class of adaptive sampling schemes. These statistics capture complementary regimes: the pooled statistic aggregates evidence across arms, while the max statistic focuses on the most promising arm. We establish finite-sample and asymptotic validity guarantees under minimal assumptions on the sampling strategy, which allow strategic termination.

Third, we study how experimental designs can be adapted to maximize the power of these tests under alternative hypotheses. We show that the resulting design problem can be viewed as an online optimization problem with bandit feedback, and we propose a simple sampling algorithm that achieves good performance in terms of statistical power. Simulation results illustrate how adaptive allocation can substantially outperform uniform designs, particularly in settings with heterogeneous effects.

1.1.1
Anytime-Valid Inference and Game-Theoretic Statistics

Our work draws inspiration from the rapidly growing literature on game-theoretic statistics and anytime-valid inference
(
Ramdas et al. 2023
)
. This line of research studies tests that remain valid under arbitrary, data-dependent stopping or sampling rules: by constructing tests from statistics which are supermartingales under the null, one may ensure uniform control of type-I error across large classes of experimental designs. Early contributions focused on robustness to optional stopping, and the approach has since been shown to have implications for a wide range of statistical problems. Notable recent work on best-arm identification develops inference methods that remain valid under both strategic termination and adaptive sample allocation
(
Howard and Ramdas 2022
;
Liang and Bojinov 2023
)
.

We, too, consider designs in which the experimenter adaptively chooses whether to continue the experiment and which arm to sample next, though we depart from the best-arm hypotheses studied in prior work; our tests are valid for any sequential allocation satisfying a minimal initialization requirement. Another important distinction is that we study test statistics intentionally optimized by the sampling algorithm: we introduce bandit strategies which aim to maximize the value of the proposed statistics themselves, thereby directly optimizing power. This is conceptually related to “testing by betting”
(
Shafer 2021
;
Shekhar and Ramdas 2023
)
and to the results of
Casgrain et al. 2024
; these works also study online optimization of power for sequential tests, although their full-information setting differs from the bandit feedback we consider.

1.1.2
Statistical Inference for Multi-Armed Bandits

A parallel literature studies how to perform valid inference after adaptive allocation in stochastic bandits.
Simchi-Levi and Wang 2023
formulate a trade-off between reward maximization and statistical efficiency, while
Hadad et al. 2021
and
Chen et al. 2021
show how restricted classes of bandit algorithms can yield joint confidence intervals.
Liang and Bojinov 2023
develop finite-population methods interpolating between arbitrary bandit algorithms and completely randomized experiments to obtain anytime-valid inference on pairwise contrasts.
Howard and Ramdas 2022
study best-arm identification based on quantiles rather than means, linking bandit allocation to sequential testing of distributional features. Finally,
Chen and Andrews 2023
characterize how to optimally design a subsequent experiment given the results of an initial adaptive experiment.

Most of this work seeks to balance regret minimization with valid inference; in contrast, we show that optimal design of demonstration experiments can itself be cast as a stochastic optimization problem with bandit feedback. By viewing the signal-to-noise ratio as the objective, standard bandit algorithms can be adapted to approximate optimal allocation for maximizing statistical evidence. Our testing problem—establishing whether any arm’s mean exceeds a threshold—is also distinct from best-arm identification or contrast inference, and is closely related to threshold-bandit problems
(
Locatelli et al. 2016
;
Kaufmann et al. 2018
)
. Our results suggest that threshold-type hypotheses are substantially easier to test than best-arm hypotheses, in the sense that nearly unconstrained adaptive sampling is compatible with sharp inference.

Beyond these connections, our work also contributes to the theory of time-uniform inference. Motivated by bandit problems in which the number of arms may be large relative to the sample size, we extend the asymptotic confidence sequences of Waudby-Smith et al. (2024) to accommodate simultaneous monitoring across many adaptively sampled processes. In particular, we establish a time-uniform moderate deviations principle for the sequential
t
-statistic, which provides a sufficient approximation for valid multiple testing in settings where the number of hypotheses grows rapidly with the horizon. This refinement is essential for constructing procedures that remain robust under strategic sampling while retaining power in many-armed bandit designs.

1.2
Outline of the paper

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the setup and notation for our class of demonstration experiments, framing the problem in terms of multi-armed bandits with sub-Gaussian outcomes. Section 3 develops two test statistics—the pooled and max statistics—that remain valid under strategic sampling and, in some cases, early stopping. Section 4 presents the SN-UCB algorithm and analyzes its performance, showing how adaptive allocation can enhance power while controlling type I error. Section 5 reports Monte Carlo simulations that illustrate the finite-sample properties of our methods and highlight their practical benefits. Section 6 concludes with a discussion of extensions and open directions for future research.

Setup and Notation

We consider a general experimental framework in which an experimenter sequentially allocates samples across a finite set of arms. Each arm may correspond to a treatment variant, a mutually exclusive subpopulation, or another experimental option of interest. At each round, the experimenter selects one arm and observes a noisy outcome. The objective is not to estimate all arm means precisely, nor to identify the single best arm, but rather to test whether any arm has a mean exceeding a given benchmark.

Formally, this corresponds to a standard multi-armed bandit setting with
k

3.1
Pooled Testing

The first statistic we consider works by pooling together information from all arms.
Before formally introducing it in
section

3.1.1

H_{T}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{X_{g_{t}}(t)}{\sigma_{g_{t}}}.

Here,
H_{T}
is infeasible because it depends on the unknown quantity
\sigma_{g_{t}}^{2}
.
It can be seen as a weighted average of standardized sums for each arm, where the weights correspond to the relative number of times each arm has been sampled.
2
2

2

See

1

Under the sharp null that
\mu_{g}=0
for all
1\leq g\leq k

does not depend on the sampling strategy, so that tests based upon

have asymptotically correct size.
The intuitive reason for constructing a test based upon
H_{T}
is that under
\mathcal{H}_{0}

We formalize this intuition in

1

Lemma 1
.

Under the null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, for any

\mathbb{E}[H_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{v}]\leq H_{v},

so that

is a supermartingale adapted to

An important aspect of

is its random normalization by
\sigma_{g_{t}}
(which is random because the choice of

depends on previously observed data). This ensures that the limit distribution is pivotal: it does not depend on the unknown distributions of the arms, or on the sampling strategy chosen by the experimenter. In contrast, the quadratic variation of the usual sum
S_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{g_{t}}(t)
can strongly depend on the sampling strategy, so that its limit distribution may be a nontrivial mixture distribution
(
Hall and Heyde 2014

Focusing on
H_{T}
instead of

simplifies the Gaussian approximation problem on a technical level (see e.g.
Mourrat 2013
;
Fan et al. 2013

T
. In contrast, many statistics proposed for best arm identification place strong regularity conditions on the sampling strategy to ensure that the quadratic variation is stable, and rule out standard UCB policies
(
Hadad et al. 2021
;
Liang and Bojinov 2023
)

3

3

This is because asymptotic inference on the mean of any individual arm requires that it is sampled sufficiently many times. Best arm identification requires simultaneous inference on the means of all arms.

Another heuristic benefit of

is that, if the experimenter’s goal is to reject the null by having

Our results imply that the (asymptotic) distribution of

\sqrt{T}\sum_{g=1}^{k}\omega_{g}z_{g};\qquad\omega_{g}\coloneqq\frac{N_{g}(T)}{T}.

(1)

In other words it is proportional to a weighted sum, where the weights

3.1.1
Feasible statistics and regularized variance estimates

To construct a feasible analogue of the infeasible statistic
H_{T}
, we replace the unknown
variances
\sigma_{g}^{2}
by regularized empirical estimates. Let

\hat{\sigma}_{g}^{2}=\frac{1}{N_{g}}\sum_{t\in T_{g}}\{X_{gt}^{(t)}-\hat{\mu}_{g}\}^{2},\qquad\hat{\mu}_{g}=\frac{1}{N_{g}}\sum_{t\in T_{g}}X_{gt}^{(t)},

and consider the two-parameter family of regularized estimators

\tilde{\sigma}_{g}(\lambda,\rho)=\hat{\sigma}_{g}+\lambda\sqrt{N_{g}}\,\mathbf{1}\{N_{g}\leq\rho\}.

The associated pooled statistic and its centered version are

\tilde{H}_{T}(\lambda,\rho)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{X_{gt}^{(t)}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{gt}(\lambda,\rho)},\qquad\tilde{H}^{\prime}_{T}(\lambda,\rho)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{X_{gt}^{(t)}-\mu_{gt}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{gt}(\lambda,\rho)}.

Stabilizing the pooled statistic under adaptive allocation presents several competing challenges. Arms may receive only a small number of samples, making variance estimates highly sensitive to sampling error; variance estimates that are spuriously small can produce highly unstable estimates of the arm-wise mean. Conversely, inflating the variance estimates may severely reduce power when an arm truly has low variance and high signal-to-noise ratio. Because sampling itself is data dependent, balancing these effects is theoretically delicate. We therefore focus on two limiting regularization regimes that address this tradeoff in different ways.

Rather than carrying
(\lambda,\rho)
throughout the notation, we define two derived
statistics corresponding to padding-based and threshold-based regularization:

\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{pad}}_{g}:=\tilde{\sigma}_{g}(\lambda_{k,T},\infty),\qquad\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{thr}}_{g}:=\tilde{\sigma}_{g}(\infty,\rho_{k,T}),

with pooled statistics
\hat{H}_{T}^{\mathrm{pad}}
,
\hat{H}_{T}^{\mathrm{thr}}
and centered counterparts

\hat{H}_{T}^{\mathrm{pad}\,^{\prime}}
,
\hat{H}_{T}^{\mathrm{thr}\,^{\prime}}
defined analogously.
The two regularization schemes stabilize the pooled statistic in different ways. Padding regularization (controlled by
\lambda
) acts by enlarging the denominator, inflating variance estimates when sample sizes are small. Threshold regularization (controlled by
\rho
) instead resembles a trimming rule, restricting attention to arms with sufficiently many samples to permit reliable Studentization. The appendix develops a simultaneous analysis covering both regimes, but does not eliminate the tension between their respective guarantees.

Remark 1

(Comparison of regularization strategies)
.

Theorems
1
and
2
reflect two complementary approaches to stabilizing the pooled statistic. Padding preserves contributions from all arms and yields a tuning-free procedure whose implementation depends only on observable problem dimensions. Thresholding instead excludes arms with insufficient samples, leading to a sharper Gaussian approximation and, as illustrated in Section
5
, improved empirical power in several settings. These advantages come with opposing tradeoffs. Padding may be conservative when signal-to-noise ratios are large, whereas thresholding introduces an additional tuning parameter. Developing a regularization strategy that combines the tuning-free implementation of padding with the sharper guarantees of thresholding remains an interesting direction for future work.

3.2
Max Statistic

As shown in
section

3.1
,
\hat{H}_{T}(\lambda,\rho)
allows sharp tests of the hypothesis that no arm’s mean exceeds a given threshold.
However, the pooled testing approach has a number of limitations.
For one, although it anticipates strategic sampling, it does not support valid inference if a researcher wishes to terminate the experiment early. It also does not allow the researcher to test stronger hypotheses on the means of individual arms: for example, if one arm
g^{*}
clearly outperforms the others, the pooled statistic will not allow the researcher to reject the more specific null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{(g^{*})}=\{\mu_{g^{\star}}\leq 0\}
.
In this case
\hat{H}(\rho,\lambda)
may also be significantly smaller than the simple
t
-statistic corresponding to
g^{*}
, due to its inclusion of samples from the other arms.

To overcome these limitations, we describe an alternative class of statistics that jointly test the individual hypotheses
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{(g)}=\{\mu_{g}\leq 0\}
, for
g=1,\dots,k
.
We do so by creating a test that looks at the
t
-statistic of each individual arms, restricting to those that have been sampled sufficiently many times.
This approach addresses the limitations introduced above.
In our analysis, treating each arm separately also results in a weaker restriction
k\ll T
on the number of arms, up to logarithmic factors. This is nearly as good as possible under Assumption
2
, which requires that
k\leq T/2
.

One drawback of this approach in comparison to pooled testing is that the resulting tests are conservative: under the null hypothesis, they reject with probability strictly less than
\alpha
.
Still, they appear to be more powerful in scenarios where one arm performs much better than the rest.
Moreover, we show theoretically that the price is not much greater than a necessary multiple hypothesis correction for testing the means of
k
arms.

In order to introduce our testing procedure, we define the Student’s
t
-statistic corresponding to the arm
g

\hat{Z}_{g}(q)\coloneqq\frac{{\sum_{s\in T_{g,q}}X_{g_{s}}(s)}}{\sqrt{\sum_{s\in T_{g,q}}[X_{g_{s}}(s)-\hat{\mu}_{g,q}]^{2}}}.

(3)

Here,
\hat{\mu}_{g,q}=q^{-1}\sum_{s\in T_{g,q}}X_{g_{s}}(s)
is the empirical mean of the first
q
samples from arm
g
.
Note, further, that at the end of the experiment, at least one arm has been sampled at least
m=\lceil T/k\rceil
times. In this setting, we can account for strategic allocation of samples to arms using a version of the invariance principle for the sequence
\{\hat{Z}_{g}(q)\}_{q\geq 1}

Waudby-Smith et al. 2024

In particular, extending the results of
Robbins and Siegmund 1970

Waudby-Smith et al. 2024

(

Remark 3

Conservativeness of the tests in Theorem

Having reduced the problem to studying the probability that each arm’s sequential

Nevertheless, because we anticipate the method will be applied in finite-samples where such independence will fail, we advocate for the more conservative Bonferroni correction given in Theorem

4
Strategic sampling algorithms and power

Having established validity of the tests corresponding to the pooled and maximum statistics in
eqs.

2
,
6
and

By doing so, we may attempt to optimize power against various alternative hypotheses.
To carry this out, we make the following observations.

1.

Maximizing the rejection probability of the statistics in
eqs.

2
,
6
and

2.

If we take the value of an arm to be its signal-to-noise ratio,
z_{g}\coloneqq\mu_{g}/\sigma_{g}
, the corresponding pseudo-regret
R_{T}=\sum_{t\leq T}(\max_{g}z_{g})-z_{g_{t}}
and number of mistakes

can be linked to the value of each of the test statistics considered in Section

In light of these observations, we consider bandit algorithms which aim to minimize the pseudo-regret,

, and the number of mistakes,

.

4.1
The SN-UCB Algorithm

The proposed bandit algorithm uses deviation bounds for Studentized sums to bound the signal-to-noise ratio of each arm. For this reason, we call the algorithm “self-normalized upper confidence bound,” or SN-UCB for short.

To introduce the SN-UCB procedure, we first introduce the exploration function

\tau(n,t;\beta)=4\nu^{2}\sqrt{\beta\log(4t)/n},

which determines how we bias arms’ measurements to prioritize exploration. Here
\nu
corresponds to the tail bound of
3
, and
\beta>2
is a tuning parameter.
Compared to the standard upper confidence bound algorithm for a
k
-armed bandit
(
Bubeck et al. 2012
)
, we replace the empirical mean of the arm
g
by its studentized counterpart,
\hat{Z}_{g}\{N_{g}(t)\}
, and replace the upper confidence bound for the mean by

\hat{U}_{g}(t;\beta)=\frac{\hat{Z}_{g}\{N_{g}(t)\}}{\sqrt{N_{g}(t)}}+\left[1+\frac{\hat{Z}_{g}\{N_{g}(t)\}}{\sqrt{N_{g}(t)}}\right]\tau\{N_{g}(t),t;\beta\},

(10)

which bounds the signal-to-noise ratio. This leads to the following procedure.

Algorithm 1

SN-UCB.

Step 1:

For
t=1,2,\ldots,k
, choose
g_{2t-1},g_{2t}=t
.

Step 2:

For
t=2k+1,2k+2,\ldots T

or until
A_{\text{log}}
in
eq.

7

0

choose
g_{t}\in\{1,\dots,k\}
that maximizes
\hat{U}_{g}(t;\beta)

We are able to prove a probabilistic bound on the number of times Algorithm
1
selects a suboptimal arm. Note that the bound given below depends on the distributions of each of the arms: thus, it allows us to establish lower bounds on the rejection probability that hold for suitably constrained classes of alternatives. We state results in expectation for simplicity; we refer the reader to Appendix
B.4

Theorem 4
.

Put
z^{*}=\max_{g^{\prime}}z_{g^{\prime}}
. For each arm
g
we write
\Delta_{g}=z^{*}-z_{g}
. The pseudo-regret
R_{T}=\sum_{t\leq T}z^{*}-z_{g_{t}}
and number of errors

attained by Algorithm

\mathbb{E}[R_{T}]\leq C_{\nu}\beta\log T\sum_{g:z_{g}<z^{*}}\left[\frac{(z_{g}\vee 1)^{2}}{\Delta_{g}}\right];\quad\mathbb{E}[E_{T}]\leq C_{\nu}\beta\log T\sum_{g:z_{g}<z^{*}}\left[\frac{(z_{g}\vee 1)^{2}}{\Delta_{g}^{2}}\right].

Theorem
4

Audibert et al. 2009
. The central challenge in its proof is that both the location and width of the confidence band depend on the unknown signal-to-noise ratio
z_{g}
, which must be replaced by the estimated quantity
\hat{Z}_{g}\{N_{g}(t)\}/\sqrt{N_{g}(t)}
.

Remark 4

(Implications for power)
.

Combining Theorem
4
with Theorems
1
and
2
, we find that the pooled statistic is approximately normal with mean
\sqrt{T}(z^{*}-\mathbb{E}[R_{T}])
. In this sense, the test is competitive with an oracle when
\mathbb{E}[R_{T}]\ll\sqrt{T}
. Similarly, given an optimal arm
g^{*}
, its
t

\sqrt{T-\mathbb{E}[E_{T}]}z^{*}
, suggesting that the condition
\mathbb{E}[E_{T}]\ll T
is sufficient to compete with the oracle.

In view of the central limit theorems of
Theorems

1
and
2
and the invariance principle that justifies
Theorem

3

\Delta_{g}\asymp T^{-1/2}
. However, in this case, neither condition is implied by the regret bound of
Theorem

4
due to its dependence on
\Delta_{g}
. A complete characterization of the tests’ power therefore requires (i) characterizing the moderate and large deviations of the considered test statistics, and (ii) characterizing regret in regimes where
\Delta_{g}\to 0
at a slower rate. These important problems are left to future work.

5
Simulations

In Sections 3 and 4 we established theoretical guarantees for the pooled and max statistics as well as for the SN-UCB algorithm. We now present Monte Carlo simulations to assess finite-sample behavior: type I error control, power under different signal configurations, and the benefits of adaptive sampling.

We vary the number of arms

5.5
Summary

The simulations confirm the theoretical predictions of Sections 3 and 4. Both the pooled and max statistics control type I error, with the pooled statistic achieving nominal size and the max statistics being conservative. Adaptive sampling improves power relative to uniform allocation, with the magnitude of improvement depending on the signal structure. Importantly, these gains are achieved despite the fact that adaptive methods must use test statistics that are robust to strategic allocation, whereas the oracle and uniform-allocation benchmarks can use simple
t
-tests. SN-UCB is particularly effective when the arm with the largest effect does not have the largest signal-to-noise ratio, as it directly optimizes the quantity that determines statistical power. When effects are concentrated in a single arm with equal variances, standard bandit algorithms perform comparably or better.

6
Conclusions

This paper develops a framework for
demonstration experiments
, where the goal is to establish whether any treatment arm exceeds a threshold rather than to estimate effects or identify the best arm. We propose two test statistics—pooled and max—that remain valid under adaptive sampling, and introduce the SN-UCB algorithm to optimize power by treating experimental design as stochastic optimization with bandit feedback.

Our results show that sharp inference is possible under nearly unrestricted adaptive sampling. The pooled statistic aggregates evidence across arms and achieves nominal size under the sharp null. The max statistic is conservative but supports early stopping and focuses power on the most promising arm. Both tests require only that each arm is sampled twice initially, imposing no further restrictions on the sampling strategy. This contrasts with best-arm identification, which requires simultaneous inference on all arms and demands stronger design constraints.

From a practical standpoint, experimenters conducting exploratory studies can benefit substantially from adaptive designs. The pooled test suits settings where multiple arms may have moderate effects; the max test excels when one arm dominates. Using SN-UCB along with these tests offers power gains over uniform allocation without sacrificing type I error control.

Future work

Several questions remain open. A complete power characterization under local alternatives requires analyzing moderate deviations of our statistics and the behavior of SN-UCB when gaps between arms shrink with sample size. Extensions to richer feedback structures—where multiple outcomes or overlapping subpopulations are observed—would broaden applicability. Although simulations suggest the pooled statistic performs well when
k
is large relative to
T
, theoretical justification is lacking.
Kaufmann et al. 2018
designed a “Murphy sampling" algorithm which is shown to have good properties in the threshold bandit problem, and warrants further study in our context. Lastly, it will be interesting to study the problem from the Bayesian point of view, exploring connections to Bayesian bandits and the Gittins index.

\log\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda\xi_{i}}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}\nu^{2}}{2}.

Additionally, assume the conditional variances are normalized:
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]=1
for all
i
.
Then there exists a constant
C_{\nu}=O(\nu^{3})
such that for all
k\geq 3
,

\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{k}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]\leq\frac{k!}{2}C_{\nu}^{k-2}.

Proof.

Since each
\xi_{i}
is conditionally sub-Gaussian with parameter
\nu
, we have the standard moment bound: there is a universal constant
C>0
such that for any
k\geq 1
,

(\mathbb{E}[|\xi_{i}|^{k}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}])^{1/k}\leq C\sqrt{k}\nu.

By
lemma

4
, this is equivalent to

\mathbb{E}[|\xi_{i}|^{k}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]\leq D\cdot k\cdot 2^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)\nu^{k}

for some universal constant
D
and all
k\geq 2
. We verify Bernstein’s condition by treating
k=3
and
k\geq 4
separately, using
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]=1
throughout.

Case
k=3
.
Direct computation gives
3\cdot 2^{3/2}\Gamma(3/2)=3\sqrt{2\pi}
, so

\mathbb{E}[|\xi_{i}|^{3}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]\leq 3D\sqrt{2\pi}\,\nu^{3}=\frac{3!}{2}\cdot D\sqrt{2\pi}\,\nu^{3}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}].

Case
k\geq 4
.
Since
k\cdot 2^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)\leq k!
for all
k\geq 4
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[|\xi_{i}|^{k}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]\leq D\cdot k!\cdot\nu^{k-2}\cdot\nu^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]=\frac{k!}{2}\cdot 2D\nu^{k}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}].

For this to be at most
\frac{k!}{2}C_{\nu}^{k-2}\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]
, it suffices that
C_{\nu}\geq(2D\nu^{k})^{1/(k-2)}
for all
k\geq 4
. The binding constraint is at
k=4
, giving
C_{\nu}\geq\sqrt{2D}\,\nu^{2}
.

Combining both cases, setting
C_{\nu}=\max(D\sqrt{2\pi}\,\nu^{3},\,\sqrt{2D}\,\nu^{2})=O(\nu^{3})
yields the desired result.
∎

Lemma 7

(Martingale CLT; Theorem 3.1 of
Fan et al. 2013
)
.

Let
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n}
be a martingale difference sequence adapted to
\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{n}
, such that

(i)

\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{k}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]\leq\frac{1}{2}k!\epsilon^{k-2}\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]
for some
\epsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
(Bernstein’s condition), and

(ii)

\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]-1\right|\leq\delta
for some
\delta\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
.
Then,

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\leq t\right\}-\Phi(t)\right|\lesssim\delta+\epsilon\log|\epsilon|.

Corollary 2
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots X_{n}
be a martingale difference sequence adapted to
\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{n}
, such that
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]=1
, and such that
\log\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X_{i}}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]\leq{\lambda^{2}\nu^{2}}/{2}
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then, there exists a universal constant
C_{\nu}>0
depending only upon
\nu
such that

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\leq t\right\}-\Phi(t)\right|\leq\frac{C_{\nu}\log n}{\sqrt{n}}.

Proof of Corollary
2
.

We will apply
lemma

7
with
\xi_{i}=X_{i}/\sqrt{n}
,
\delta=0
, and
\epsilon=C^{\prime}_{\nu}/\sqrt{n}
.
By construction,
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]=n^{-1}
, so condition (ii) of
lemma

7
is automatically met.
It therefore suffices to check Bernstein’s condition.
By
lemma

6
, after dividing through by
\sqrt{n}
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{k}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]\leq\frac{1}{2}k!(C_{\nu}/\sqrt{n})^{k-2}\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}].

It follows that whenever
\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}
, we may take
\epsilon=C^{\prime}_{\nu}/\sqrt{n}
for
C^{\prime}_{\nu}=C_{\nu}
and apply
lemma

7
to deduce the claimed bound.

In turn,
\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}
clearly holds for sufficiently large
n\geq n_{\nu}
, where
n_{\nu}
depends only upon
\nu
.
This proves the result for all
n\geq n_{\nu}
with
C_{\nu}=C^{\prime}_{\nu}
.

On the other hand, for
n\leq n_{\nu}
, we can choose
C^{\prime\prime}_{\nu}=\max_{1\leq k\leq n_{\nu}}\frac{\sqrt{k}}{\log k}
to ensure
\frac{C^{\prime\prime}_{\nu}\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\geq 1
, making the claimed bound trivially true since the Kolmogorov distance is always bounded by
1
.
∎

We also state two lemmas that help us derive bounds on the Kolmogorov distance between our statistic and the standard normal distribution.

Lemma 8

(Lemma 2.1 of
Chernozhukov et al. 2016
)
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{P}(|X-Y|>\nu)\leq\eta
. Then

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}(X\leq t)-\mathbb{P}(Y\leq t)\right|\leq\eta+\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(|Y-t|\leq\nu).

If
Y
has a standard Gaussian distribution then the bound simplifies to
\eta+2\nu
.

Proof.

The first statement is exactly
Chernozhukov et al. 2016
;
the second claim follows as the Gaussian density is bounded by
1
.
∎

Lemma 9
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{P}(|X-Y|>\nu)\leq\eta
and
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathbb{P}(Y\leq t)-\Phi(t)|\leq\epsilon
.
Then

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathbb{P}(X\leq t)-\Phi(t)|\leq\eta+3\epsilon+2\nu.

Proof.

Note that given any
t
our assumptions imply
\mathbb{P}(Y\leq t)\leq\Phi(t)+\epsilon
and

\mathbb{P}(Y<t)=\lim_{u\uparrow t}\mathbb{P}(Y\leq u)\geq\lim_{u\uparrow t}\Phi(u)-\epsilon=\Phi(t)-\epsilon,

using right-continuity of
\mathbb{P}(Y\leq t)
and continuity of
\Phi
.
Thus

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(|Y-t|\leq\nu)

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(Y\leq t+\nu)-\mathbb{P}(Y<t-\nu)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\Phi(t+\nu)+\epsilon-[\Phi(t-\nu)-\epsilon]\right\}\leq 2\epsilon+2\nu,

where we have used
|\Phi^{\prime}|\leq 1
in the last step.
We then use
lemma

8
and the triangle inequality for supremum norms to conclude:

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathbb{P}(X\leq t)-\Phi(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathbb{P}(X\leq t)-\mathbb{P}(Y\leq t)|+\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathbb{P}(Y\leq t)-\Phi(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\eta+\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(|Y-t|\leq\nu)+\epsilon\leq 3\epsilon+2\nu+\eta.\qed

A.1.3
Concentration

To establish consistency of the estimated mean
\hat{\mu}_{g}
and variance
\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{g}
for each arm
g
, we provide a self-contained argument using basic concentration tools.
We refer the reader to
de la Peña et al. 2004
and
Howard et al. 2020
for many generalizations, refinements and extensions.

Lemma 10
.

Suppose
\{\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\dots\}
is an i.i.d. sequence with
\mathbb{E}\Delta_{i}=0
,
\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{i}^{2}]=\sigma^{2}
and
\log\mathbb{E}e^{\lambda\Delta_{i}}\leq\nu^{2}\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}/2
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.

Then, for a sufficiently small universal constant
c>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\Delta_{i}}{\sigma}\right|\geq\delta n\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left\{-n\frac{\delta^{2}}{2\nu^{2}}\right\}

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}^{2}-n\sigma^{2}\right|\geq\delta n\sigma^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left\{-n\left(\frac{\delta^{2}}{64\nu^{4}}\wedge\frac{\delta}{8\nu^{2}}\right)\right\}

(12)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}^{2}-n\sigma^{2}\leq-\delta n\sigma^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{-n\frac{\delta^{2}}{64\nu^{4}}\right\}

(13)

Proof.

Equation

11
is the standard sub-Gaussian concentration inequality
(
Vershynin 2026
, Theorem 2.6.2)
. Since
\Delta_{i}
is
(\nu\sigma)
-sub-Gaussian, we have the estimates
\mathbb{E}|\Delta_{i}|^{r}\ \leq r2^{r/2}\Gamma(r/2)(\sigma\nu)^{r}
for
r\geq 2
, which in turn implies that
\Delta_{i}^{2}/\sigma^{2}
is
(\sqrt{32}\nu^{2},4\nu^{2})
-subexponential
(
Honorio and Jaakkola 2014
, Appendix B)
.
Since
\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{i}^{2}-\sigma^{2}]=0
, inequality (
12
) follows from the standard subexponential concentration bound
(
Wainwright 2019
, Equation 2.18)
. Finally, inequality (
13
) follows by taking
r=2
, which gives the estimate
\mathbb{E}(\Delta_{i}^{2}-\sigma^{2})^{4}\leq 32\sigma^{4}\nu^{4}
along with the fact that
\Delta_{i}^{2}\geq 0
, using the “one-sided Bernstein’s inequality”
(
Wainwright 2019
, Proposition 2.14)
.
∎

In
lemma

11
we give a crude time-uniform concentration inequality that is sufficient for our analysis. In effect, it accounts for dependence in strategic, sequentialy-sampled data by taking a simple union bound over prefixes.

Lemma 11
.

With probability at least
1-\eta
,

\frac{1}{N_{g}}\sum_{t\in T_{g}}\frac{X_{g}(t)-\mu_{g}}{\sigma_{g}}\leq\max_{1\leq q\leq T}\frac{1}{q}\sum_{t=1}^{q}\frac{X_{g}(t)-\mu_{g}}{\sigma_{g}}\lesssim\nu\sqrt{\frac{\log(T/\eta)}{N_{g}}}.

Proof.

By the equivalent representation of
lemma

3
and a union bound, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{g}}}\sum_{t\in T_{g}}\frac{X_{g}(t)-\mu_{g}}{\sigma_{g}}>u\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{\max_{1\leq q\leq T}\frac{1}{q}\sum_{t=1}^{q}\frac{X_{g}(t)-\mu_{g}}{\sigma_{g}}>u\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{q=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\sum_{t=1}^{q}\frac{X^{\prime}_{g}(t)-\mu_{g}}{\sigma_{g}}>u\right\},

where
X^{\prime}_{g}(t)
are i.i.d. copies of
X_{g}(t)
independent of
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
. Using inequality (
11
) from Lemma
10
with
\Delta_{t}=(X^{\prime}_{g}(t)-\mu_{g})/\sigma_{g}
, we get the bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{g}}}\sum_{t\in T_{g}}\frac{X_{g}(t)-\mu_{g}}{\sigma_{g}}>u\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 2Te^{-c\delta^{2}/\nu^{2}}

\displaystyle\implies\mathbb{P}\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{g}}}\sum_{t\in T_{g}}\frac{X_{g}(t)-\mu_{g}}{\sigma_{g}}>C\nu\sqrt{\log(2T/\eta)}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\eta

for
C=c^{-1/2}
sufficiently large.
∎

Lemma 12
.

With probability at least
1-\eta
,

\begin{split}\left|\frac{1}{N_{g}}\sum_{t\in T_{g}}\frac{[X_{g}(t)-\mu_{g}]^{2}-\sigma^{2}_{g}}{\sigma^{2}_{g}}\right|&\leq\max_{1\leq q\leq t}\left|\frac{1}{q}\sum_{t=1}^{q}\frac{[X_{g}(t)-\mu_{g}]^{2}-\sigma^{2}_{g}}{\sigma^{2}_{g}}\right|\\
&\lesssim\nu^{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\log(T/\eta)}+\log(1/\eta)}{\sqrt{N_{g}}}\right)\end{split}

(14)

and

\frac{1}{N_{g}}\sum_{t\in T_{g}}\frac{[X_{g}(t)-\mu_{g}]^{2}-\sigma^{2}_{g}}{\sigma^{2}_{g}}\geq\min_{1\leq q\leq T}\frac{1}{q}\sum_{t=1}^{q}\frac{[X_{g}(t)-\mu_{g}]^{2}-\sigma^{2}_{g}}{\sigma^{2}_{g}}\gtrsim\nu^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(T/\eta)}{N_{g}}}.

(15)

Proof.

By the equivalent representation of
lemma

3
and a union bound, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{g}}}\sum_{t\in T_{g}}\frac{[X_{g}(t)-\mu_{g}]^{2}-\sigma^{2}_{g}}{\sigma^{2}_{g}}\right|>u\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{q=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\sum_{t=1}^{q}\frac{[X^{\prime}_{g}(t)-\mu_{g}]^{2}-\sigma^{2}_{g}}{\sigma^{2}_{g}}\right|>u\right\},

where
X^{\prime}_{g}(t)
are i.i.d. copies of
X_{g}(t)
independent of
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
. Using inequality (
12
) from Lemma
10
with
\Delta_{t}=X^{\prime}_{g}(t)-\mu_{g}
, we get the bound

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{q=1}^{T}2\exp\left\{-c\left(\frac{u^{2}}{\nu^{4}}\wedge\frac{u\sqrt{q}}{\nu^{2}}\right)\right\}.

Noting that the term
u\sqrt{q}/\nu^{2}
only appears when

, this may be rewritten as

\displaystyle\leq 2T\exp\left\{-\frac{cu^{2}}{\nu^{4}}\right\}+\frac{u^{2}}{\nu^{4}}\exp\left\{-\frac{cu}{\nu^{2}}\right\}.

Finally, using the fact
A+B\lesssim A\vee B
and
x^{2}e^{-x}\leq C^{\prime}e^{-x/C^{\prime}}
for sufficiently large

, we obtain the bound

\displaystyle\lesssim T\exp\left\{-\frac{c^{\prime}u^{2}}{\nu^{4}}\right\}\vee\exp\left\{-\frac{c^{\prime}u}{\nu^{2}}\right\}.

This gives

\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{g}}}\sum_{t\in T_{g}}\frac{[X_{g}(t)-\mu_{g}]^{2}-\sigma^{2}_{g}}{\sigma^{2}_{g}}\right|>C^{\prime}\nu^{2}\left[\sqrt{\log(C^{\prime}T/\eta)}\vee\log(C^{\prime}/\eta)\right]\right\}\leq\eta

). A similar argument using (

∎

The basis of Theorem 2 is the following boundary crossing probability for Brownian motion.

Lemma 14

(Robbins-Siegmund Boundary)
.

Let
\{W(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
be a standard Wiener process, and suppose
\tau>0
. Let
\Phi(x)
denote the standard Gaussian CDF, and put
h(x)=x^{2}+2\log\Phi(x)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\sup_{t>1}W(t)\geq\sqrt{t}\cdot h\left(\log t+h^{-1}(a)\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=1-\Phi(a)+\Phi^{\prime}(a)\left[a+\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(a)}{\Phi(a)}\right];

(23)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\sup_{t>\tau}t^{-1}W(t)\geq a\right\}

\displaystyle=2[1-\Phi(\tau^{-1/2}a)];

(24)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\max_{0<t<1}W(t)\geq a^{\prime}\right\}

\displaystyle=2[1-\Phi(a^{\prime})]

(25)

Proof.

Equations (
23
) and (
24
) arei quoted from
Robbins and Siegmund 1970
, which contains many other one- and two-sided boundary crossing probabilities. Eq. (

Çınlar 2011
, Prop. 3.4), and is seen to be equivalent to (

and using the time inversion and dilation formulas (respectively,
\{tW(1/t)\}_{t\geq 0}\sim\{W(t)\}_{t\geq 0}

Çınlar 2011

In order to use (
24

Lemma 15

(Quantitative invariance principle of
Sakhanenko 1984
)
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
be an independent sequence of random variables with
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=0
and such that for all

\lambda>0

\mathbb{E}[\lambda|X_{i}|^{3}e^{\lambda|X_{i}|}]\leq\mathbb{E}[X^{2}_{i}]

X_{i}
can be redefined on an enriched probability space along with a sequence of centered Gaussian random variables

such that
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{2}]
and

\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\lambda C\max_{k\leq n}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}-Y_{i}\right|\right)\leq 1+\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{2}].

In particular, if the
X_{i}
are i.i.d. then for

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{k\leq n}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}-Y_{i}\right|>t\right)\leq(1+\lambda n\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}])e^{-\lambda Ct}.

Finally, if the
X_{i}
satisfy Assumption
3
then we may take
\lambda\geq 1/C_{\nu}
, so that

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{k\leq n}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}-Y_{i}\right|>t\right)\leq(1+n\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}])e^{-t/C_{\nu}}.

(26)

Proof.

The first claimed statement is due to Sakhanenko and is cited from
Shao 1995

X_{i}
.

The third claim follows from the fact that Bernstein’s condition, namely
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{k}]\leq\frac{1}{2}k!C_{\nu}^{k-2}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{2}]
for

, implies that
\mathbb{E}[\lambda|X_{i}|^{3}e^{\lambda|X_{i}|}]\leq\mathbb{E}[X^{2}_{i}]
holds for any

(
Zaitsev 2013

. This can be checked by writing the Taylor expansion of

. Moreover, this form of Bernstein’s condition follows from Assumption
3
by Lemma

In order to characterize the moderate deviations for our proposed statistic, we need a Gaussian approximation that becomes sharper in the tails. This is accomplished by combining Sakhanenko’s coupling with the following argument.

Lemma 16
.

Suppose that
U
and
V
are random variables, and that
V
has probability density function
f
. Suppose also that
\mathbb{P}\{U\vee t>V\vee t+\delta\}\leq\eta

\mathbb{P}\{U\leq t\}-\mathbb{P}\{V\leq t\}\leq\eta+\mathbb{P}\{V\in(t-\delta,t]\}=\eta+\int_{t-\delta}^{t}f(v)\,dv.

Clearly the difference on the left is bounded by
\mathbb{P}\{V\leq t<U\}
. On this event, either

or

\mathbb{P}\{V\in(t-\delta,t]\}

∎

Finally, we quote the Dudley-Strassen theorem
(

Lemma 17

Let

11
and

q

Linear boundary

Here we focus on the upper bound, which is all that is needed to prove Theorem
3
. In particular, we show the following.

Proposition 2

(Moderate deviations upper bound for linear boundary)
.

Given sequences
\{r_{T}\}_{T\geq 1}
and
\{k_{T}\}_{T\geq 1}
, put

\psi_{T}^{2}=\frac{[r_{T}^{2}\vee 1]\log^{3}(k_{T}T)+r_{T}^{8}}{T/k_{T}}.

Then, there exists a universal constant
C>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left\{\max_{T/k_{T}\leq q\leq T}\sqrt{\frac{T/k_{T}}{q}}|\hat{Z}^{\prime}_{g}(q)|>r_{T}\right\}\leq[1+C(T^{-1}+\psi_{T}\,e^{C\psi_{T}})][2-2\Phi(r_{T})].

In particular, the right-hand side is bounded as
[1+o(1)][2-2\Phi(r_{T})]
whenever
\psi_{T}\downarrow 0
.

Proof.

We work in the probability space
\mathbb{P}^{\flat}
from Lemma
20
, and omit the
\flat
symbol to simplify notation. We begin by noting that by the time inversion formula
\{tW(1/t)\}_{t\geq 0}\sim\{W(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
(
Çınlar 2011
, Thm. 1.6),

\displaystyle\max_{T/k\leq q\leq T}\frac{\sqrt{T/k}}{q}W_{g}(q)\leq\max_{u\in[T/k,\infty)}\frac{\sqrt{T/k}}{u}W_{g}(u)

\displaystyle=\max_{u\in(0,k/T]}\sqrt{T/k}\,W_{g}(u).

Therefore, by Lemma
20
, the triangle inequality, and
T/k\leq q
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left\{\max_{T/k\leq q\leq T}\sqrt{\frac{T/k}{q}}\,\hat{Z}^{\prime}_{g}(q)-\max_{u\in(0,k/T]}\sqrt{T/k}\,W_{g}(u)>\delta(\eta)\right\}\leq\eta,

for
\delta(\eta)=C_{\nu}\sqrt{k\log^{3}(2kT/\eta)/T}
.
By Lemma
24
, we have for any
r>0
that

\mathbb{P}\left\{\max_{u\in(0,k/T]}\sqrt{T/k}\,W_{g}(u)>r\right\}=2[1-\Phi(r)].

Combining the previous two displays using Lemma
16
, we obtain for any
\eta\in(0,1)

\mathbb{P}\left\{\max_{T/k\leq q\leq T}\sqrt{\frac{T/k}{q}}\,\hat{Z}^{\prime}_{g}(q)>r\right\}-2[1-\Phi(r)]\leq\eta+2\int_{r-\delta(\eta)}^{r}\varphi(v)\,dv.

We first treat the case in which
r\leq 1
. Then
1-\Phi(r)\gtrsim 1
and we may bound

\eta+2\int_{r-\delta(\eta)}^{r}\varphi(v)\,dv\lesssim\eta+\delta(\eta)

by boundedness of the Gaussian density. Choosing
\eta=1/T
, we obtain the bound

\mathbb{P}\left\{\max_{T/k\leq q\leq T}\sqrt{\frac{T/k}{q}}\,\hat{Z}^{\prime}_{g}(q)>r\right\}-2[1-\Phi(c)]\leq C_{\nu}^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{\log^{3}(kT)}{T/k}}\cdot[1-\Phi(r)].

On the other hand, if
r\geq 1
then we have
1-\Phi(r)\gtrsim r^{-1}e^{-c^{2}/2}
by Mill’s inequality. Choosing
\eta=T^{-1}r^{-1}e^{-r^{2}/2}\lesssim T^{-1}2[1-\Phi(r)]
, we have

\delta(\eta)=C_{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\log^{3}(2kT/\eta)}{T/k}}\leq C_{\nu}^{\prime\prime}\left\{\sqrt{\frac{\log^{3}(kT)+r^{6}}{T/k}}\right\}.

using the inequalities
(x+y)^{3}\lesssim x^{3}+y^{3}
and
\log r\leq r
for
r\geq 1
.
Moreover, since
\varphi(-)
is decreasing,

\displaystyle\int_{r-\delta(\eta)}^{r}\varphi(v)\,dv\leq\delta(\eta)\varphi\{r-\delta(\eta)\}

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta(\eta)\exp\left\{\frac{-[r-\delta(\eta)]^{2}}{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\delta(\eta)\exp\left\{\frac{-r^{2}+2r\delta(\eta)}{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\lesssim[1-\Phi(r)][r\delta(\eta)]e^{r\delta(\eta)}.

We recover the sufficient rate condition
r\delta(\eta)\downarrow 0
, which amounts to

\frac{r^{2}\log^{3}(kT)+r^{8}}{T/k}\downarrow 0.

By inspection, this also covers the
r\leq 1

by
r^{2}\vee 1
, leading to the claimed bound.
∎

Lemma 21

The function

h^{\prime}(x)\sim 2x
. It is a monotone, continuously differentiable bijection
[x_{0},\infty)\to[x_{0},\infty)
for some fixed point
x_{0}\in(1,\sqrt{2\log 2})
, and its inverse is monotone and continuously differentiable. Moreover, (i) for all
x\geq x_{0}
,
y\geq 0
,

\left.\frac{d}{du}\right|_{u=h(x)+y}h^{-1}(u)\leq\frac{1}{2x}
, and (ii)
h^{-1}(h(x)-y)
is
O(1)
-Lipschitz in
x
for
x\geq\sqrt{2y}\vee x_{0}
and
y\geq 0
.

Proof.

The first claim follows since
\Phi(x)\to 1
as
x\uparrow\infty
.
To further characterize
h
, we compute its derivative

h^{\prime}(x)=2\left[x+\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(x)}{\Phi(x)}\right].

Clearly
2x\leq h^{\prime}(x)\leq 2x+e^{-x^{2}/2}
, and
h
is strictly increasing and continuous for
x\geq 1
. Since
h(1)<1
and
h(\sqrt{2\log 2})>\sqrt{2\log 2}
, it follows by the intermediate value theorem that
h
has a fixed point
x_{0}\in(1,\sqrt{2\log 2})
, and both
h
and its inverse
h^{-1}
are well defined as functions
[x_{0},\infty)\to[x_{0},\infty)
.

Next, for
x\geq x_{0}
, the inverse function theorem gives

\left.\frac{d}{du}\right|_{h(x)}h^{-1}(u)=\frac{1}{h^{\prime}(x)}\leq\frac{1}{2x}.
From this, and the implied monotonicity of
h^{-1}
, we deduce our third claim that for any
y\geq 0
,

\left.\frac{d}{du}\right|_{u=h(x)+y}h^{-1}(u)=\frac{1}{h^{\prime}\{h^{-1}[h(x)+y]\}}\leq\frac{1}{2h^{-1}[h(x)+y]}\leq\frac{1}{2x}.

Finally, suppose that
y\geq 0
and also that
x\geq\sqrt{2y}\vee x_{0}
. Then we have

h(x)-y\geq h(x)-x^{2}/2=x^{2}/2+2\log\Phi(x)\geq x^{2}/2+2\log\Phi(x/\sqrt{2})=h(x/\sqrt{2}).

Thus,

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}h^{-1}(h(x)-y)=\frac{h^{\prime}(x)}{h^{\prime}\{h^{-1}[h(x)-y]\}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{8}x+e^{-x^{2}/2}}{2h^{-1}[h(x)-y]}\leq\frac{\sqrt{8}x+e^{-x^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2}x}\lesssim 1.

This proves our final claim.
∎

Lemma 22
.

For all
x\geq 1
we have
\Psi_{+}(x)\sim x\Phi^{\prime}(x)
and
-\Psi_{+}^{\prime}(x)\sim x^{2}\Phi^{\prime}(x)
:

\displaystyle(1/2x+x)\Phi^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\Psi_{+}(x)\leq(1/x+x+e^{-x^{2}/2})\Phi^{\prime}(x),

\displaystyle\quad x^{2}\Phi^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle\leq-\Psi^{\prime}_{+}(x)\leq[x^{2}+xe^{-x^{2}/2}+e^{-x^{2}}]\Phi^{\prime}(x).

In particular, if
\Psi_{+}(w_{\alpha}(k))=\alpha/k
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
and

\sqrt{2\log k}\leq w_{\alpha}(k)\leq C\sqrt{\log(k/\alpha)}.

Proof.

Note that by the Mill’s ratio inequality we have for all
x\geq 1

\frac{e^{-x^{2}/2}}{6x}\leq\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(x)}{2x}\leq 1-\Phi(x)\leq\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(x)}{x}\leq\frac{e^{-x^{2}/2}}{2x}.

Thus, for
x\geq 1
, since
\Phi^{\prime}(x)\leq\frac{1}{2}e^{-x^{2}/2}
and
1/2\leq\Phi(x)\leq 1
, we have

\left(\frac{1}{2x}+x\right)\Phi^{\prime}(x)\leq 1-\Phi(x)+x\Phi^{\prime}(x)+\Phi^{\prime}(x)^{2}/\Phi(x)\leq\left(\frac{1}{x}+x+e^{-x^{2}/2}\right)\Phi^{\prime}(x).

Since the quantity in the middle is
\Psi_{+}(x)
, the bounds follow immediately.

The bounds on critical values follow: for
k\geq 2
,
\sqrt{2\log k}\geq 1
, and plugging in to the lower bound gives a value larger than
1/k
. For the upper bound, it is immediate that our upper bound on
\Psi_{+}
is at most
e^{-cx^{2}}
some small
c>0
, so
w_{\alpha}(k)\lesssim\sqrt{\log(k/\alpha)}
.

To bound
-\Psi_{+}^{\prime}
, the observation
\Phi^{\prime\prime}(x)=-x\Phi^{\prime}(x)
implies

-\frac{d}{dx}\left[1-\Phi(x)+x\Phi^{\prime}(x)+\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(x)^{2}}{\Phi(x)}\right]=x^{2}\Phi^{\prime}(x)+\frac{2x\Phi^{\prime}(x)^{2}}{\Phi(x)}+\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(x)^{3}}{\Phi(x)^{2}}.

We conclude again by repeatedly applying
\Phi^{\prime}(x)\leq\frac{1}{2}e^{-x^{2}/2}
and
1/2\leq\Phi(x)\leq 1
.
∎

Lemma 24

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\tilde{N}_{g}(q)}{q}-1\right|\leq 8\nu^{2}\delta\right)\geq 1-2\exp\left\{-q\left(\delta^{2}\wedge\delta\right)\right\}.

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{|\sum_{t=1}^{q}X_{g}(t)|}{\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{q}[X_{g}(t)-\mu_{g}]^{2}+4q\sigma^{2}_{g}}}>u\right)\leq 2e^{-u^{2}/2}.

Thus, if
\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\eta)}{q}}\leq 1

then with probability at least
1-\eta
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\tilde{N}_{g}}{q}-1\right|

\displaystyle\leq 8\nu^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\eta)}{q}}\eqqcolon\delta(\eta,q)

(40)

\displaystyle|\tilde{S}_{g}|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{[20+32\nu^{2}]\log(4/\eta)}\eqqcolon s^{+}(\eta)

(41)

\displaystyle|Z_{g}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{s^{+}(\eta)}{\sqrt{(1-s^{+}(\eta)^{2}/q)}}+\sqrt{q}[1+\delta(\eta,q)]\frac{\mu_{g}}{\sigma_{g}}.

(42)

Proof.

Taking
\Delta_{i}=(X_{t}(g)-\mu_{g})/\sigma_{g}
in Lemma
10
immediately gives the first inequality. Taking this same choice of
\Delta_{i}
in Lemma
23
gives the second inequality.

Let

\delta=\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\eta)}{q}}\leq 1

be given. Let
\mathcal{E}_{1}=\{N_{g}\leq(1+8\nu^{2}\delta)\tilde{N}_{g}\}
be the event corresponding to the first inequality in the lemma statement. Using our choice of
\delta
in that inequality, we see that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{1})\geq 1-\eta/2
. On
\mathcal{E}_{1}
, we equivalently have
4N_{g}\sigma^{2}_{g}\leq 4(1+8\nu^{2}\delta)\sum_{t\in T_{g}}(X_{t}-\mu_{t})^{2}
. Thus, if we put

\mathcal{E}_{2}=\left\{\frac{|\sum_{t=1}^{q}X_{g}(t)|}{\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{q}[X_{g}(t)-\mu_{g}]^{2}+4q\sigma^{2}_{g}}}\leq 2\sqrt{\log(4/\eta)}\right\}

then on
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}
, since
\delta\leq 1
, we have

\frac{|\sum_{t=1}^{q}X_{g}(t)|}{\sqrt{[5+8\nu^{2}]\sum_{t\in T_{g}}(X_{t}-\mu_{t})^{2}}}\leq 2\sqrt{\log(4/\eta)}\iff|\tilde{S}_{g}|\leq\sqrt{[20+32\nu^{2}]\log(4/\eta)}.

The second inequality in the lemma statement immediately gives
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{2})\geq 1-\eta/2
, so by a union bound
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{1}^{c}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}^{c})\leq\eta
. For the final bound on
\hat{Z}_{g}
, note that by (
38
),

\displaystyle|\hat{Z}_{g}|=\left|{\sum_{t=1}^{q}X_{t}}\bigg/{\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{q}(X_{t}-\hat{\mu}_{g})^{2}}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|{\sum_{t=1}^{q}X_{t}}\bigg/{\sqrt{(1-|\tilde{S}_{g}|^{2}/q)\sum_{t=1}^{q}(X_{t}-\mu_{g})^{2}}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{S}_{g}}{\sqrt{(1-|\tilde{S}_{g}|^{2}/q)}}+\frac{q\mu_{g}}{\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{q}(X_{t}-\mu_{g})^{2}}}

Using the definition of
\tilde{N}_{g}
and (
39
), this is at most

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tilde{S}_{g}}{\sqrt{(1-|\tilde{S}_{g}|^{2}/q)}}+\frac{q\mu_{g}}{\sigma_{g}\tilde{N}_{g}^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tilde{S}_{g}}{\sqrt{(1-|\tilde{S}_{g}|^{2}/q)}}+\left\{1+\left|\frac{q}{\tilde{N}_{g}}-1\right|\right\}\frac{\sqrt{q}\mu_{g}}{\sigma_{g}}.\quad\qed

Finally, we obtain Proposition
4
by applying the above with
\eta=t^{-\alpha}
.

Proof of Proposition
4
.

We combine the results of Lemmas
25
and
26
. In particular, when

\delta=\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\eta)}{q}}\leq 1

the event
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}
from the proof of Lemma
26
holds w.p.
1-\eta
, and on this event we have that for any
C\geq 20+32\nu^{2}
,

\left|\frac{\tilde{N}_{g}}{N_{g}}-1\right|\leq 8\nu^{2}\delta\quad\text{and}\quad|\tilde{S}_{g}|\leq\sqrt{C\log(4/\eta)}.

Thus, by Lemma
25
we have
\mu_{g}/\sigma_{g}\in[L_{\eta},U_{\eta}]
, with

\displaystyle L_{\eta}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1-8\nu^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\eta)}{N_{g}}}}{\sqrt{N_{g}}}\right)\left[\hat{Z}_{g}\sqrt{1-C\log(4/\eta)/N_{g}}-\sqrt{C\log(4/\eta)}\right]

\displaystyle U_{\eta}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1+8\nu^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\eta)}{N_{g}}}}{\sqrt{N_{g}}}\right)\left[\hat{Z}_{g}+\sqrt{C\log(4/\eta)}\right]

To simplify, we note that since
\nu\geq 1
without loss of generality, we have
8\nu^{2}\geq\sqrt{20+32\nu^{2}}
. So, taking

\tau(\eta,N_{g})=8\nu^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\eta)}{N_{g}}}

and using that
(1-\tau)(1+\tau)\geq(1-\tau)^{2}
for
\tau>0
, so

(1-\tau)\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}}\geq(1-\tau)^{2}\geq 1-2\tau,

the above simplifies to

\displaystyle L_{\eta}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1-\tau}{\sqrt{N_{g}}}\right)\left[\hat{Z}_{g}\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}}\right]-\tau\geq\frac{\hat{Z}_{g}}{\sqrt{N_{g}}}-\left(1+\frac{2|\hat{Z}_{g}|}{\sqrt{N_{g}}}\right)\tau

\displaystyle U_{\eta}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1+\tau}{\sqrt{N_{g}}}\right)\hat{Z}_{g}+\tau\leq\frac{\hat{Z}_{g}}{\sqrt{N_{g}}}+\left(1+\frac{2|\hat{Z}_{g}|}{\sqrt{N_{g}}}\right)\tau

Using our bound on
\hat{Z}_{g}
from Lemma
26
, we find that on the same event
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}
we have

\frac{|\hat{Z}_{g}|}{\sqrt{N_{g}}}\leq\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}}}+(1+\tau)\frac{\mu_{g}}{\sigma_{g}}.

Thus, whenever
\tau\leq\frac{1}{2}
, we recover the bound

1+5/2(\mu_{g}/\sigma_{g})
.
Finally, we define

\tau_{\alpha}(t,s)=\tau(t^{-\alpha},s)=8\nu^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha\log(4t)}{s}}.\quad\qed

B.3
Abstract Regret bound

Finally, we prove a high-probability regret bound for the SN-UCB algorithm. In particular the two-sided confidence bound of Proposition
4
gives us:

•

an estimate
\hat{Z}_{g}(s)
of
\mu_{g}/\sigma_{g}
, where
s
is the number of times arm
g
has been drawn;

•

a random, feasible “exploration function”
E_{g}(t,s)
for
s\leq t
such that
\hat{Z}(s)\pm E_{g}(t,s)
likely contains
\mu_{g}/\sigma_{g}
; and

•

a not-necessarily observed, deterministic upper bound
e^{+}_{g}(t,s)
for
E_{g}(t,s)
.

In this setting, we have the following result.

Proposition 5

(Abstract regret bound for SN-UCB)
.

Suppose for each arm
g
we have

\mathbb{P}\left\{|\mu_{g}/\sigma_{g}-\hat{Z}_{g}(t)|\leq E_{g}(t,s)\leq e^{+}_{g}(t,s)\right\}\geq 1-\psi(t).

(43)

Suppose also that
\psi(-),E_{g}(t,-)
and
e^{+}_{g}(t,-)
are decreasing while
E_{g}(-,s)
and
e^{+}_{g}(-,s)
are increasing.

Consider the upper confidence bound policy which at time
t
chooses the arm
g
that maximizes

\hat{Z}_{g}(N_{g}(t-1))+E_{g}(t,N_{g}(t-1)).

Define

\Delta_{g}=(\max_{g^{\prime}}z_{g^{\prime}})-z_{g},
and

u^{*}_{g}=\inf\left\{s\geq 1\,\middle|\,e^{+}_{g}(T,s)\leq\Delta_{g}\right\}.

Then, we have

\mathbb{P}(N_{g}(T)>u^{*}_{g}+q)\leq 2\int_{u^{*}_{g}+q}^{T}\psi(t)\,dt.

Proof of Proposition
5
.

Suppose that at time
t
we choose arm
g>1
. Then one of the following events must have taken place:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1,t}

\displaystyle=\left\{\hat{Z}_{1}(N_{1}(t-1))+E_{1}(t,N_{1}(t-1))\leq z_{1}\right\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2,t}

\displaystyle=\left\{e^{+}_{g}(t,N_{g}(t-1))\geq E_{g}(t,N_{g}(t-1)),\hat{Z}_{g}(N_{g}(t-1)))-E_{g}(t,N_{g}(t-1))>z_{g}\right\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{3,t}

\displaystyle=\left\{\Delta_{g}<e^{+}_{g}(T,N_{g}(t-1))\right\}

On
(\mathcal{E}_{1,t}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2,t}\cup\mathcal{E}_{3,t})^{c}
, keeping in mind that
\mu_{g}=0
, we have

\displaystyle Z_{1}(N_{1}(t-1))+E_{1}(t,N_{1}(t-1))

\displaystyle>z_{1}

\displaystyle(\text{on }\mathcal{E}_{1,t}^{c})

\displaystyle=z_{g}+\Delta_{g}

\displaystyle\geq z_{g}+e^{+}_{g}(T,N_{g}(t-1))

\displaystyle(\text{on }\mathcal{E}_{3,t}^{c})

\displaystyle\geq z_{g}+e^{+}_{g}(t,N_{g}(t-1))

\displaystyle(\text{by monotonicity})

\displaystyle\geq\hat{Z}_{g}(N_{g}(t-1)))+E_{g}(t,N_{g}(t-1))

\displaystyle(\text{on }\mathcal{E}_{2,t}^{c}),

which finally implies
g
was not chosen. Now, put

u=\inf\left\{t\geq 1\,\middle|\,e^{+}_{g}(T,t)\leq z_{1}-z_{g}\right\}

and consider the event that
N_{g}(T)>u+q
, for a sub-optimal arm
g>1
. Then there must be a time
t_{0}
satisfying
u+q<t_{0}\leq T
at which
g
is drawn for the
(u+q+1)^{\text{th}}
time.
Moreover, at time
t_{0}
, arm
1
has been drawn
s\leq t_{0}-u-q
times, and arm
g
has been drawn exactly
u+q
times.
Correspondingly, define

\mathcal{E}(t_{0})=\left\{N_{g}(t_{0})>N_{g}(t_{0}-1)=u+q,\text{ and }N_{1}(t_{0}-1)\leq t_{0}-u-q\right\}.

Since
t_{0}>u
, we automatically have
\mathcal{E}(t_{0})\subset\mathcal{E}_{3,t_{0}}^{c}
. Since a suboptimal arm is drawn, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(t_{0})

\displaystyle\subset(\mathcal{E}(t_{0})\cap\mathcal{E}_{1,t_{0}})\cup(\mathcal{E}(t_{0})\cap\mathcal{E}_{2,t_{0}})

\displaystyle\subset\left\{\exists s\leq t_{0}-u-q\text{ s.t. }\hat{Z}_{1}(s)+E_{1}(t_{0},s)\leq z_{1}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\cup\left\{\hat{Z}_{g}(u+q)-E_{g}(t_{0},u+q)>z_{g},e^{+}_{g}(t_{0},u+q)\geq E_{g}(t_{0},u+q)\right\}.

Since
s+u+q\leq t_{0}
and
E_{1}(-,s)
is increasing, we may expand the first event to recover

\displaystyle\subset\left\{\exists s\leq t_{0}-u-q\text{ s.t. }\hat{Z}_{1}(s)+E_{1}(s+u+q,s)\leq\mu_{1}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\cup\left\{\hat{Z}_{g}(u+q)-E_{g}(t_{0},u+q)>z_{g},e^{+}_{g}(t_{0},u+q)\geq E_{g}(t_{0},u+q)\right\}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\left\{N_{g}(T)>u+q\right\}

\displaystyle\subset\bigcup_{t_{0}>u+q}\mathcal{E}(t_{0})

\displaystyle\subset\left(\bigcup_{1\leq s\leq T-u-q}\left\{\hat{Z}_{1}(s)+E_{1}(s+u+q,s)\leq z_{1}\right\}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\quad\cup\bigcup_{u+q<t\leq T}\left\{\hat{Z}_{g}(u+q)-E_{g}(t,u+q)>z_{g},e^{+}_{g}(t,u+q)\geq E_{g}(t,u+q)\right\}\right)

Re-indexing the first union with
t=s+u+q
gives

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{u+q<t\leq T}\left\{\hat{Z}_{1}(t-u-q)+E_{1}(t,t-u-q)\leq z_{1}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\cup\bigcup_{u+q<t\leq T}\left\{\hat{Z}_{g}(u+q)-E_{g}(t,u+q)>z_{g},e^{+}_{g}(t,u+q)\geq E_{g}(t,u+q)\right\}

We may finally apply a union bound to find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(N_{g}(t)>u+q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=u+q+1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{Z}_{1}(t-u-q)+E_{1}(t,t-u-q)\leq z_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{Z}_{g}(u+q)-E_{g}(t,u+q)>z_{g},e^{+}_{g}(t,u+q)\geq E_{g}(t,u+q)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{t=u+q+1}^{T}\psi(t).

Bounding the sum by an integral over
t\in[u+q,\infty)
gives the final result.
∎

B.4
Proof of Theorem
4

Without loss of generality, we assume that the optimal arm has index
g=1
. In order to apply Proposition
5
, we must verify the hypothesis (
43
) which we restate:

\mathbb{P}\left\{|\mu_{g}/\sigma_{g}-\hat{Z}_{g}(t)|\leq E_{g}(t,s)\leq e^{+}_{g}(t,s)\right\}\geq 1-\psi(t).

By equation (
36
) of Proposition
4
, this hypothesis is satisfied with:

\displaystyle\tau_{\alpha}(t,s)=8\nu^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha\log(4t)}{s}},

\displaystyle\qquad E_{g}(s,t)\coloneqq\left(1+\frac{2|\hat{Z}_{g}(s)|}{\sqrt{N_{g}}}\right)\tau_{\alpha}(s,t),

\displaystyle e^{+}_{g}(s,t)=\left(2+5\cdot\frac{\mu_{g}}{\sigma_{g}}\right)\tau_{\alpha}(s,t),

\displaystyle\qquad\psi(t)=t^{-\alpha},

for any
\alpha>0
. Proposition
5
then implies that for
g>1
,

\mathbb{P}(N_{g}(T)>u^{*}_{g}+q)\leq 2\int_{u^{*}_{g}+q}^{T}t^{-\alpha}\,dt,

where we have defined

\Delta_{g}=(\max_{g^{\prime}}z_{g^{\prime}})-z_{g},
and

u^{*}_{g}=\inf\left\{s\geq 1\,\middle|\,e^{+}_{g}(T,s)\leq\Delta_{g}\right\}.

In particular, we find that

u^{*}_{g}\lesssim\frac{\nu^{4}(z_{g}\vee 1)^{2}\alpha\log T}{\Delta_{g}^{2}}.

Meanwhile, whenever
\alpha>2
, we may integrate the tail bound to find so
\mathbb{E}N_{g}(T)\lesssim u_{g}^{*}
. Finally, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[R_{T}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{g=2}^{k}\Delta_{g}N_{g}(T)\right]\lesssim\sum_{g=2}^{k}\frac{\nu^{4}(z_{g}\vee 1)^{2}\alpha\log T}{\Delta_{g}},

and

\mathbb{E}[E_{T}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{g=2}^{k}N_{g}(T)\right]\lesssim\sum_{g=2}^{k}\frac{\nu^{4}(z_{g}\vee 1)^{2}\alpha\log T}{\Delta_{g}^{2}}.\qed
