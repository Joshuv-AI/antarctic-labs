unix-ctf: Procedural Environments for Unix-Competence Reinforcement Learning
Geoffrey Bradway, Roger Creus Castanyer, Lorenz Wolf, Maxwill Lin, Matthew James Sargent, Augustine N. Mavor-Parker

Abstract
Unix competence is the ability to use shell and operating-system primitives as first-class tools, not merely to write programs through a terminal. Current terminal benchmarks tend to blur this distinction: a solver fluent in Python but weak in Unix can pass a substantial fraction of Terminal-Bench 2.0, while the reverse skill profile is rarely exercised. We make the distinction operational and build a training surface for the Unix component. unix-ctf is a procedural generator of capture-the-flag tasks for shell agents. Each task hides a short token (a flag of the form flag{a3b1c9...} ) inside a fresh Linux container using a single Unix feature, and the agent must recover it. Tasks are produced by an LLM-assisted synthesis pipeline that generates candidate hiding techniques, rewrites them into parameterized hide-and-find script pairs, and filters them with a bidirectional contract: the hide script must leave no plaintext trace of the flag on disk, and the find script must recover the flag in a fresh directory. Because the LLM only writes the planting and recovery steps (the container, layout, and grading harness are fixed), the pipeline lands 656 of 750 raw attempts as portable, reusable variants ( 87.5% ). Our reproduction of Endless Terminals’ full-container-generation approach lands only 17.4% under the same checks. The 656 variants canonicalize to 155 distinct techniques. Fine-tuning Qwen3-8B with LoRA using GRPO on this surface lifts solve rate from 11.6% to 43.6% on a 15-skill multi-family holdout ( n = 225 n{=}225 ), redistributes which InterCode-CTF tasks the model solves, and produces a + 33 +33 pp gain in Forensics while reaching 32 / 100 32/100 on InterCode-CTF. These results suggest that Unix competence is separable, trainable, and best evaluated directly rather than folded into programming-through-a-shell.

3.1
Harvest pipeline

The five stages are designed to keep the language model on the layer it
can validate, while letting mechanical checks enforce the task contract.
(1)
Exploration:
Claude Opus 4.7
(
3

runs inside a fresh

hide a flag of the form

Mechanical verification:
in the same container, a

Synthesis:
Claude

(
2
)
rewrites the validated

(target

(target directory), as a pure

validation:
the synthesized scripts are re-run against a fresh

must exit zero,

must print the new flag on stdout and exit zero.
This catches the main synthesis failure mode: inlining the original
directory or flag. (5)
Canonicalization:
per-attempt records
are archived to

3.2
Pipeline factoring and the bidirectional contract

The 87.5% yield is driven by two choices:
what
the LLM
produces, and
how
the output is filtered. Endless Terminals
asks the LLM to generate the Dockerfile, setup script, planting logic,
and tests for each task (essentially a complete container). In our

n{=}120
reproduction (17.5% portable),

71.7\%
of failures occur at the Docker-build or
test-validation stage
, and only
10.8\%
at the LLM task-generation
step. The model is competent at inventing hiding techniques but
rarely succeeds at the surrounding infrastructure re-derived from
scratch each time. We therefore lift infrastructure out of the
per-attempt budget: a pre-debugged
ctf-base
image handles the
system layer, and the LLM produces only the parameterized

plant.sh
/
recovery.sh
pair. On top of that we apply
two complementary filters: a recursive search of the filesystem must

not
find the flag in plaintext, and a re-run of

recovery.sh
on a fresh directory with a fresh flag

must
recover it. Upstream pipelines use only one half: Endless
Terminals filters on whether at least one of sixteen
OpenAI o3
(
13
)
samples solves; SWE-smith and R2E-Gym
filter on whether a generated change invalidates a unit test. Both
check solvability without checking non-triviality.

4
Environment generation

4.1
Multi-flag containers

Given a list of eight techniques and a seed, the environment generator
instantiates a pre-built
ctf-base
container (Ubuntu plus
standard tools). It can also dress the filesystem as one of seven
plausible server roles:
webserver
,
database
,

devbox
,
cicd
,
mailserver
,

monitoring
, or
gateway
. Each role supplies
role-appropriate users, hostnames, service configurations, fictitious
logs, and shell histories from templates; for example, an agent in the

webserver
role sees
www-data
’s crontab, nginx
configuration, and SSL certificates. For each of the eight techniques,
the generator samples a target directory from
/home/user/
,

/var/tmp/
,
/opt/app/
,
/srv/
, and

/tmp/
, generates a fresh flag, base64-encodes the technique’s

plant.sh
into the container, runs it with the chosen target
directory and flag, and records its exit status and output. The plant
script is then deleted, so it cannot be recovered from disk. The routine
returns a
MultiFlagEnv
containing the container name, seed, and
manifest of planted flags.

We plant eight flags per container rather than one. With a single
flag, per-rollout reward is binary, the policy receives signal only on
the (initially rare) successful rollouts, and group-relative methods
like GRPO lose most batches to constant-reward filtering. Spreading
reward across multiple sub-tasks per episode is a standard
densification move in sparse-reward RL
(
1
;
12
)
, and we adopt it as a practical training choice for
validating the library under RL rather than a claim about the optimal

n_{\text{flags}}
. The trade-off is that each container consumes
eight techniques rather than one, which the pool turnover below
partially compensates for.

Reward.

Per-rollout reward is

\textit{points\_earned}-\textit{turn\_cost}\times\textit{turns\_used}
, with

\textit{turn\_cost}=1
(a maximum penalty
of about
-18
) and per-technique points defaulting to fifteen. Each
incorrect flag submission costs one point. We do not clip reward at
zero; negative-reward rollouts still provide within-group gradient
signal. The shape encodes two objectives directly (find the flag,
and find it without burning the turn budget) and was chosen, not
swept; we leave reward-shape sensitivity to future work.

4.2
Sticky-pool skill scheduling

The scheduler maintains a rotating pool of fifty techniques from the
library. Each environment samples eight techniques uniformly from the
current pool. After each batch,
_rotate_pool
drops the top

\textit{rotation\_rate}\times\textit{pool\_size}=15
pool members by
per-technique solve rate, so mastered techniques graduate out, plus one
guaranteed random removal for exploration. Replacements come from the

\sim
105 techniques currently outside the pool, preferring those with
the fewest prior attempts. Each batch exposes the model to

8\text{ groups}\times 8\text{ flags}=64
technique-instances, and
each rotation exchanges fifteen techniques. Across the forty-batch runs
we report, the scheduler cycles most of the 155-technique library
through the pool at least once.

5
Evaluation protocol

5.1
Training as a probe

We fine-tune Qwen3-8B (
Qwen/Qwen3-8B
)

(
24
)
with rank-32 LoRA adapters. Training is used as a
probe of the generated surface, not as a claim that this recipe is an
optimal RL pipeline.

Optional SFT format pass.

Before GRPO, we optionally run a
brief supervised pass on 492 successful solver trajectories from an
earlier Haiku-solver run against the library. The purpose is to align
the model’s output style and tool-call format with the unix-ctf harness
(
\sim
92 gradient updates; full
hyperparameters in Appendix
I
). We treat this stage as harness adaptation
rather than capability transfer; one of the two GRPO initializations
skips it entirely. The empirical claims concern
the
environment’s
pedagogical content: whether GRPO on the
unix-ctf surface produces measurable transfer. They are not claims about
the intrinsic properties of Qwen3-8B. We use Qwen3-8B because it
produces well-formed tool calls under our harness’s tool specification
without adaptation. Preliminary attempts with Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct,
Kimi-K2.5, and Nemotron-3-Super-120B-A12B exposed harness-renderer
compatibility issues: their native tool-call serializations did not
round-trip cleanly. We therefore treat model selection as a precondition
for measurement rather than a variable to ablate. The SFT format pass
extends the same harness-alignment discipline for one of the two GRPO
initializations.

GRPO.

From either the SFT-formatted checkpoint or the
unmodified base model, we run forty batches of group-relative policy
optimization with 8 groups
\times
8 rollouts (64 trajectories per
batch), up to 18 turns per rollout, temperature 1.0, KL penalty
coefficient 0, and
remove_constant_reward_groups
enabled.
Fifteen technique families spanning file encoding, filesystem,
archives, databases, logs and system state, user accounts, text
processing, checksums, and process control are held out of the training
pool via the technique-blacklist mechanism (§
6.1
); the
full list appears in Appendix
K
. Each batch runs eight
concurrent Docker containers on a single
n2-highmem-16
GCP
instance.

7
Conclusion and limitations

Unix-ctf is a novel approach for generating synthetic Unix competence tasks—an eval surface that previous terminal task generation methods do not target directly. Furthermore, unix-ctf achieves a higher task yield than comparable generation methods by procedurally constructing tasks from a canonicalized library of CTF techniques. Finally, we demonstrate that the skills learned from training on unix-ctf can improve downstream performance on hand-curated IC-CTF tasks.

A limitation of unix-ctf is that it relies on LLMs to construct its technique library, so techniques poorly represented in the model during harvesting will not make it into the generated tasks. Additionally, our approach is constrained to CTF style tasks, which do not measure the full breadth of tasks that can be accomplished with unix. A promising direction for future work is to parametrise and train a unix-task generator to build curriculums of increasingly difficult unix CTF tasks.

Dual-use and release.
The 155 techniques form a surface for
offensive-security training: each entry teaches recovery of a
Unix-resident artifact, a capability relevant to red-team agents and
defenders training against them. The library is already in active use in
such a pipeline. Releasing the 155-ID set with the associated

plant.sh
/
recovery.sh
pairs would lower the barrier to
equivalent offensive training corpora. We therefore release enough
methodological detail for academic reproduction, including pipeline
factoring, bidirectional contract, worked exemplars, full ID
enumeration, and judge rubric, while withholding the artifact itself.
