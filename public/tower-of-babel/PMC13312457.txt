Use of Internal Brace for the Surgical Management of the “Baby Stener” Lesion of the Radial Collateral Ligament to the Small Finger
Authors: Dainn Woo, David A. Kulber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present a case of a traumatic isolated radial collateral ligament injury at the small finger metacarpophalangeal joint, a rare injury that warrants surgical intervention when associated with a Stener-like lesion in which the torn collateral ligament buttonholes through the radial sagittal band at the metacarpophalangeal joint. This interposition prevents healing of the collateral ligament much like the traditional Stener lesion of the thumb, and early recognition and treatment may prevent chronic pain, instability, and functional impairment. Here, we describe a case report and our surgical technique for repairing this injury.

Collateral ligament injuries of the hand occur with traumatic radial or ulnar force at a joint. The most common of these are seen at the ulnar aspect of the thumb metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint, with approximately 200,000 injuries treated annually.1 Sixty-four percent to 87% of complete thumb ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) injuries are Stener-type lesions where the UCL buttonholes through the adductor aponeurosis precluding healing with conservative management alone.2 In these cases, surgery is indicated to restore pinch strength and thumb stability.3 The incidence of Stener-like lesions in nonthumb digital collateral ligaments of the hand is unknown and rarely reported in the literature.

Historically, collateral ligament injuries of MCP joints have been treated conservatively, with quicker time to recovery for less severely sprained ligaments.4 Few reports of early surgical management of these injuries have shown good outcomes, including full mobility within 10.7 weeks and no residual instability at 2 years.5 This option may be appealing for patients with complete ligament tears who seek early mobilization and expedited return to activity.

Importantly, a previous study reported on a subset of MCP joint collateral ligament tears associated with protrusion of the ruptured ligament through the extensor hood, causing Stener-like lesions.6 In these cases, it is even more imperative to pursue early surgical intervention to address the injury, which may never heal properly with conservative management. Here, we report on a case of a “baby Stener” lesion of the radial collateral ligament (RCL) of the small finger MCP joint and describe our preferred surgical technique to restore stability.

Case Report

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images.

A 59-year-old right-hand dominant woman artist presented to the clinic 8 days after jamming her right small finger in a door with an ulnar-directed force at the 4th webspace. She reported a persistent ache and swelling in the radial aspect of the small finger MCP joint.

Examination demonstrated tenderness at the radial aspect of the small finger MCP joint and laxity of the MCP joint with an ulnar-directed force. Comparison to the contralateral hand confirmed increased widening of the 4th webspace. The small finger was buddy-strapped to the ring finger, and the patient was referred to hand therapy for a thermoplastic splint for her suspected small finger RCL injury. A gentle range of motion was permitted while wearing straps.

Four weeks later, the patient followed up reporting unchanged pain in the small finger, which notably affected her ability to draw using her dominant hand. We had a thorough discussion with the patient regarding the risk of chronic instability without surgical intervention, reviewed our planned operative intervention, as well as specific risks of surgery including stiffness, pain, damage to surrounding neurovascular structures, and repair failure. The patient consented to move forward with repair of the small finger RCL.

Surgical technique

The small finger was approached using a curvilinear incision from the radial aspect of the MCP joint to the proximal phalanx. Soft tissue was dissected down to the extensor hood and radial sagittal band, which demonstrated a small tear through which the avulsed RCL had extruded (Fig. 2). Further dissection was made to expose the residual ligament, confirming distal rupture of the ligament with proximal retraction (Fig. 2).Figure 2Intraoperative photos demonstrating dissection through the extensor hood and distal avulsion of the small finger RCL, buttonholing through the radial sagittal band.

Intraoperative photos demonstrating dissection through the extensor hood and distal avulsion of the small finger RCL, buttonholing through the radial sagittal band.

The distal rupture site at the proximal phalanx was debrided using a beaver blade. The torn ligament was mobilized. A guidewire was placed into the proximal phalanx and drilled to accept a 2.5-mm PushLock suture anchor (Arthrex Inc.) carrying a 4-0 fiberwire suture and a 1.3-mm SutureTape (Arthrex Inc.). The fiberwire was used to suture the collateral ligament and reapproximate it to the proximal phalanx (Fig. 3).Figure 3PushLock with 4-0 FiberWire suture and 1.3-mm SutureTape (Arthrex Inc) was first inserted into the proximal phalanx. Fiberwire is used to reapproximate the torn RCL to the proximal phalanx. The SutureTape is then anchored to the metacarpal head.

Next, with the MCP joint in 30° flexion, the 1.3-mm SutureTape was anchored to the metacarpal head using a second PushLock anchor as an internal brace. A concentric, stable joint was confirmed with fluoroscopy. There was full flexion and extension range of motion, normal cascade, a firm end point to ulnar-directed stress, and no residual deformity. The sagittal band tear was repaired with 4-0 nonabsorbable-braided polyester suture, and the skin was closed with subcuticular 5-0 monocryl suture. A wrist-based plaster ulnar gutter splint was applied to protect the surgical site and repair.

Postoperative course

Two weeks after surgery, the plaster splint was removed, sutures trimmed, and the patient was given a thermoplastic ulnar gutter splint to protect the hand during daytime activities and sleep. Hand therapy was initiated at this time with a gentle range of motion of all digits. The patient attended therapy twice weekly.

At 4 weeks, the patient was able to make a near-full fist with the hand. The splint was discontinued in favor of buddy straps (Fig. 4). At 3 months, the patient was able to fully grasp and bear weight with minimal pain. She had a natural appearance of the 4th webspace symmetric to the contralateral hand and a normal cascade of her 5th digit (Fig. 5).Figure 4Follow-up at 1 week and 4 weeks following RCL repair. Discussion

Radial collateral ligament injuries of the small finger MCP joint are rare injuries characterized by pain, instability, and loss of grip strength. Only a handful of cases of small finger RCL injuries have been reported in the literature to date.7,8 One review of studies between 1962 and 2022 identified only 11 cases of nonthumb MCP joint collateral ligament injuries with a Stener-like lesion that ultimately underwent surgical management.9 The majority of these presented with a distal avulsion of the ligament at its insertion, with trapping of the avulsed end through a rupture in the sagittal band, similar to our case.

Early recognition is important for these injuries that may not otherwise heal with conservative treatment. Examination of the affected MCP joint may reveal tenderness, swelling, instability with stress, and occasionally an extension lag. Ultrasound and MRI have high sensitivities and specificities for diagnosing UCL injuries of the thumb, 95% and 93% for ultrasound and 94% and 98% for MRI,10 but these values have not been studied in the detection of nonthumb digital collateral ligament injuries.

Regardless of imaging findings, the surgeon should maintain a high suspicion for Stener-type injuries in patients whose examination demonstrates gross instability, a palpable lump at the radial aspect of the joint, and in conservatively treated patients whose pain and instability do not improve with time. For equivocal cases, an MRI is helpful in confirming the tear and associated Stener-type lesion.

In our patient, we used a technique that resembles our approach to thumb UCL injuries, reattaching the torn ligament to the proximal phalanx and augmenting the repair with a SutureTape internal brace. This technique may be especially useful in chronic cases where the residual ligament may have retracted or attenuated significantly, limiting primary repair. Furthermore, SutureTape augmentation has been shown in biomechanical studies of other hand ligaments such as the thumb UCL to greatly increase the load to failure, allowing patients to initiate early range of motion after surgery.11

Postoperative protocols should be focused on early range of motion of the digit using buddy straps to protect the repair for a minimum of 6 weeks. The surgical outcome demonstrated restoration of stability, return to full range of motion, and weight bearing at 3 months after surgery, making this technique a good option for treatment of digital MCP joint collateral ligament injuries.
