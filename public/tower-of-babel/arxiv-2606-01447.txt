Constructing Discontinuous but Locally Bounded Rational Functions using Łojasiewicz Inequalities Thanks: ORCID 0000-0002-1437-7525
Adam Coffman, Yifei Pan

Abstract
. For real multivariate polynomials P P and Q Q both vanishing at a point, if the zero set of Q Q is contained in the zero set of P P , then there exists a rational function of the form P p / Q q P^{p}/Q^{q} which is locally bounded and such that its extension that vanishes on the zero set of Q Q is discontinuous. The proof uses inequalities of Łojasiewicz.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 26C15; Secondary 14P10, 26B05.

1.
Introduction and examples

Real valued rational functions are familiar as counterexamples in multivariable calculus. Functions with interesting or unexpected behavior are easily illustrated in the textbook and classroom by functions with two real inputs and one real output, and good choices of polynomial numerator and denominator. We start with a few examples, with the goal of finding constructions for other bounded rational functions with discontinuities.

Example 1.1
.

The classic example

f(x,y)=\frac{xy}{x^{2}+y^{2}}

is bounded but discontinuous, even when extended to have value
f(0,0)=0
, with different limit values along lines through the origin. However its partial derivatives
\partial f/\partial x
and
\partial f/\partial y
exist at every point in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Its graph in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is contained in the ruled surface defined by
z(x^{2}+y^{2})-xy=0
(known as “Plücker’s conoid,” and of Type
8
in the
[
CSS
]
classification of real surfaces with quadratic rational parametrization). See Figure 1 (left).

Figure 1.
\displaystyle{z=\frac{xy}{x^{2}+y^{2}}}
.
\displaystyle{z=\frac{2x^{2}y}{x^{4}+y^{2}}}
.

Example 1.2
.

The discontinuous function

(1.1)

f(x,y)=\frac{2x^{2}y}{x^{4}+y^{2}},

again extended to
f(0,0)=0
, has limit
0
as
(x,y)
approaches
(0,0)
along any straight line, but has limit
1
along the curve
y=x^{2}
. See Figure 1 (right). The expression (
1.1
) is attributed to Genocchi and Peano in the survey paper
[
CM1
]
; for more examples with similar properties, see
[
CM2
]
and
[
CGGR
]
Chapter 34.

Example 1.3
.

The rational function (
[
B
]
)

(1.2)

f(x,y)=\frac{(x^{2}+y^{2})^{7}}{(y^{3}-x^{5})^{2}+(y-x^{2})^{8}}

is continuous on a neighborhood of
(0,0)
when extended to value
0
at the origin, but not locally
Lipschitz continuous
at
(0,0)
; there is no constant
C
so that for
(x,y)
near
(0,0)
,
|f(x,y)|\leq C|(x,y)|
, but instead
f
satisfies
f\approx x^{2/3}
on the curve
y^{3}=x^{5}
.

In all these examples,
f
is bounded near the origin even though the denominator approaches
0
. In this article, we consider how local boundedness and continuity are related for rational functions of real variables. Given polynomials
P
and
Q
with
P(\vec{0})=Q(\vec{0})=0
, we suppose that the zero set of
Q
is contained in the zero set of
P
. Then, for rational numbers
q/p\geq 0
, the general idea is that
P/|Q|^{q/p}
, or equivalently
P^{2p}/Q^{2q}
, is unbounded for large
q/p
, but for small
q/p
, the rational function
P^{2p}/Q^{2q}
has limit
0
at
\vec{0}
and extends to a continuous function near the origin. Example
1.1
and Example
1.2
show there is an intermediate case, where the expression is locally bounded but its extension that vanishes on the zero set of
Q
is not a continuous function. Our main result is that this always happens: there is a rational exponent
p/q
so that near
\vec{0}
,
P/|Q|^{q/p}
is bounded but is not continuous in a neighborhood of the origin. Our approach is elementary but at a few points we refer to
[
BCR
]
for some foundational facts from the subject of real algebraic geometry, and the main results (Lemma
4.1
, Theorem
5.2
, and Theorem
7.4
) rely on well-known inequalities of Łojasiewicz. The Proof of Theorem
5.2
in fact shows that the existence of exponents so that
P^{2p}/Q^{2e}
is bounded and discontinuous is equivalent to a strong version of the Łojasiewicz inequality for pairs of polynomials
(P,Q)
.

In Section
7
, the case where
Q
is a power of the norm of the gradient
\vec{\nabla}P
gives a simple way to construct a large class of locally bounded rational functions. Although
Q=|\vec{\nabla}P|^{2}
has isolated zeros for a generic polynomial
P
, Theorem
7.10
shows how bounded rational functions can have an indeterminacy (
\frac{0}{0}
) set with large dimension.

In Section
6
we demonstrate another construction of bounded real rational functions, as the real or imaginary part of a complex valued expression. This is another source of many examples and does not use the real algebraic geometry methods of Section
5
or Section
7
.

We remark that the related question of computing the value of a limit of a multivariable rational function is of continuing interest in the field of computer algebra, see
[
A
]
,
[
DGNP
]
, and their references.

2.
Elementary properties of rational functions

We will work with real valued functions depending on
n
real variables
\vec{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. To fix notation we start by recalling some elementary properties of real polynomial and rational functions.

Notation 2.1
.

The
zero set
of a polynomial
P=P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is denoted

\mathcal{Z}(P)=\{\vec{x}:P(\vec{x})=0\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}.

We will also call a set of this form a
real algebraic set
, and recall that the intersection of finitely many zero sets
\mathcal{Z}(P_{1})\cap\ldots\cap\mathcal{Z}(P_{N})
is itself the zero set of one polynomial
P_{1}^{2}+\cdots+P_{N}^{2}
.

Definition 2.2
.

A
nonsingular
point of a polynomial
P
is
\vec{x}
such that the gradient vector at
\vec{x}
is non-zero:

\vec{\nabla}P(\vec{x})=\left.\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial x_{1}},\ldots,\frac{\partial P}{\partial x_{n}}\right)\right|_{\vec{x}}\neq\vec{0}.

A point
\vec{x}_{0}
such that
\vec{\nabla}P(\vec{x}_{0})=\vec{0}
is a
critical point
of
P
.

At a point which is a nonsingular point of
P
and also an element of
\mathcal{Z}(P)
, there is a neighborhood
U
of
\vec{x}
so that
U\cap\mathcal{Z}(P)
is a smooth submanifold of dimension
n-1
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 2.3
.

A
rational function
is defined as an expression of the form

f(x_{1},\ldots x_{n})=\frac{P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})}{Q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})},

where
P
and
Q
are polynomials and
Q\not\equiv 0
.
The
domain
of a rational function
f=P/Q
is the complement of
\mathcal{Z}(Q)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the
indeterminacy set
of
f=P/Q
is defined by
\mathcal{Z}(P)\cap\mathcal{Z}(Q)
.

Definition 2.4
.

For a subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a rational function
f=P/Q
is
bounded
on
\Delta
means that there is some constant
M
so that for all
\vec{x}
in
\Delta\setminus\mathcal{Z}(Q)
,

\left|\frac{P(\vec{x})}{Q(x)}\right|\leq M.

The function is
locally bounded
near a point
\vec{x}
means that it is bounded on some open neighborhood of
\vec{x}
.

A rational function will not be locally bounded near a point where the denominator is zero, unless the numerator is also zero, as shown by the following Lemma.

Lemma 2.5
.

If a rational function
P/Q
is bounded on some open set
\Delta
, then
\Delta\cap\mathcal{Z}(Q)\subseteq\Delta\cap\mathcal{Z}(P)
.

Proof.

For any point
\vec{x}\in\mathcal{Z}(Q)
,
\vec{x}
is not an interior point of
\mathcal{Z}(Q)
(
[
BCR
]
Proposition 2.8.4). If
\Delta\cap\mathcal{Z}(Q)
is empty, then the claim is trivial; otherwise, for
\vec{x}\in\Delta\cap\mathcal{Z}(Q)
there is some sequence of points
\vec{x}_{i}\in\Delta\setminus\mathcal{Z}(Q)
converging to
\vec{x}
. This limit exists:

(2.1)

\lim_{i\to\infty}P(\vec{x}_{i})=\lim_{i\to\infty}\left(\frac{P(\vec{x}_{i})}{Q(\vec{x}_{i})}\right)Q(\vec{x}_{i})=0,

since it is the product of a bounded sequence and a sequence with limit
0
. By continuity of
P
,
P(\vec{x})=0
, which shows
\Delta\cap\mathcal{Z}(Q)\subseteq\Delta\cap\mathcal{Z}(P)
.
∎

Example
1.1
and Example
1.2
showed that locally bounded rational functions may be discontinuous at points where the denominator is zero, but the following Lemma shows that multiplying by any small power of the denominator gives a continuous (but not necessarily rational) function.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\Delta
be any open set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If the rational function
P(\vec{x})/Q(\vec{x})
is bounded on
\Delta
, then for any
\tau>0
, the function

S(\vec{x})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\displaystyle{\frac{P(\vec{x})}{|Q(\vec{x})|^{1-\tau}}}}&\mbox{\rm{for} $\vec{x}\in\Delta\setminus\mathcal{Z}(Q)$}\\
&\\
0&\mbox{\rm{for} $\vec{x}\in\Delta\cap\mathcal{Z}(Q)$}\end{array}\right.

is continuous on
\Delta
.

Proof.

P/|Q|^{1-\tau}
is continuous by elementary calculus at points in the open set
\Delta\setminus\mathcal{Z}(Q)
.
|Q|^{\tau}
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and if
Q
is always nonnegative, or
\tau=p/q
is rational with
q
odd, then we do not need the absolute value in the statement or proof.

Let
M>0
be a bound so that
|P(\vec{x})/Q(\vec{x})|\leq M
for
\vec{x}\in\Delta\setminus\mathcal{Z}(Q)
, and let
\epsilon>0
. We denote the usual norm of a vector by
|(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})|=\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+\cdots x_{n}^{2}}
. If
\vec{x}_{0}\in\Delta\cap\mathcal{Z}(Q)
, then there is some
\delta>0
so that if
|\vec{x}-\vec{x}_{0}|<\delta
, then
\vec{x}\in\Delta
and
|Q(\vec{x})|^{\tau}<\epsilon/M
. For
\vec{x}\in\mathcal{Z}(Q)
,
S(\vec{x})=S(\vec{x}_{0})=0
. If
\vec{x}\not\in\mathcal{Z}(Q)
and
|\vec{x}-\vec{x}_{0}|<\delta
, then
|S(\vec{x})-S(\vec{x}_{0})|=(|P(\vec{x})|/|Q(\vec{x})|)\cdot|Q(\vec{x})|^{\tau}<M\cdot(\epsilon/M)=\epsilon
.

We note that this proof only used the continuity of
P
and
Q
, not the assumption that they are polynomials.
∎

The following Proposition states, as mentioned in the Introduction, that for any non-zero rational function
P/Q
, replacing
Q
with some large power gives an unbounded rational function
P/Q^{\xi}
.

Proposition 2.7
.

For an open set
\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if there is a point
\vec{x}_{0}\in\Delta
and a polynomial
Q\not\equiv 0
with
Q(\vec{x}_{0})=0
, then for any polynomial
P\not\equiv 0
there exists a number
\xi_{0}>0
so that the function
\displaystyle{\frac{P(\vec{x})}{|Q(\vec{x})|^{\xi}}}
is not bounded on
\Delta\setminus\mathcal{Z}(Q)
for any
\xi\geq\xi_{0}
.

Proof.

We refer to the Addendum
[
CP
]
for the brief and elementary proof.
∎

3.
Recalling some real algebraic geometry

Notation 3.1
.

For
r>0
and
\vec{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
B_{r}(\vec{x}_{0})
denote the Euclidean open ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with center
\vec{x}_{0}
and radius
r
. This will often be abbreviated
B_{r}
. The closed ball is denoted
\overline{B}_{r}(\vec{x}_{0})=\overline{B}_{r}
.

Definition
2.3
allows the numerator and denominator,
P
and
Q
, of a rational function to have a common polynomial factor
F
, and the zero set of such a common factor is part of the indeterminacy set of
P/Q
. In fact, this is related to the earlier observations about dimension and the gradient.

Proposition 3.2
.

For a rational function
P/Q
with
P\not\equiv 0
, the following are equivalent.

(1)

There exist polynomials
M_{1}
,
M_{2}
, and
F
, so that
F
is irreducible,
\mathcal{Z}(F)
has a non-singular point of
F
, and so that
P=M_{1}F
, and
Q=M_{2}F
.

(2)

There is some point
\vec{x}
in the indeterminacy set of
P/Q
where the intersection of the set with a ball
B_{r}(\vec{x})
is a smooth submanifold of the ball, with dimension
n-1
.

If, in addition, for every point
\vec{x}\in\mathcal{Z}(Q)
,
P/Q
is bounded on some neighborhood of
\vec{x}
, then
(1)
and
(2)
are equivalent to:

(3)

There is some point
\vec{x}\in\mathcal{Z}(Q)
where the intersection
\mathcal{Z}(Q)\cap B_{r}(\vec{x})
is a smooth submanifold of the ball, with dimension
n-1
.

Sketch.

For the claim
(1)\iff(2)
, we refer to
[
DFP
]
and to supplemental notes in our Addendum
[
CP
]
. We refer to the first few chapters of
[
BCR
]
for a general detailed development of algebraic and topological notions of dimension in the subject of real algebraic geometry. The rough idea of the Proposition is that
\mathcal{Z}(P)
and
\mathcal{Z}(Q)
generally have a lower-dimensional intersection unless
P
and
Q
have a common factor.

To show
(2)\iff(3)
under the boundedness assumption, for any point
\vec{x}\in\mathcal{Z}(Q)
, there is some neighborhood
\Delta
where
\Delta\cap\mathcal{Z}(Q)\subseteq\Delta\cap\mathcal{Z}(P)
by Lemma
2.5
. It follows that
\mathcal{Z}(Q)\subseteq\mathcal{Z}(P)
and the indeterminacy set coincides with the zero set of the denominator:
\mathcal{Z}(P)\cap\mathcal{Z}(Q)=\mathcal{Z}(Q)
.
∎

In Proposition
3.2
, the common factor
F
can be canceled from the fraction
P/Q
to get
M_{1}/M_{2}
, although we consider these expressions to be different as rational functions. A contrapositive restatement of the Proposition is that a rational function
\not\equiv 0
with no common factors in the numerator and denominator must have an indeterminacy set with dimension
n-2
or smaller. This was observed in the examples from Section
1
, where
n=2
and the denominators had only isolated zeros.

Definition 3.3
.

A subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
semi-algebraic
set means that it is a finite union of finite intersections of sets of the form
\{F=0\}
or
\{F>0\}
for polynomials
F(\vec{x})
. For a subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
, a function
f:\Delta\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
is a
semi-algebraic
function means that its graph is a semi-algebraic set in
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}
.

The next Proposition states a well-known local description of half-branches of real algebraic plane curves, formulated specifically for application in Theorem
5.2
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Given a semi-algebraic set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
with dimension
1
, if
X
contains
(0,0)
then there exist
\delta>0
,
\eta>0
,
K\geq 0
so that the intersection of
X
with the open box
(0,\delta)\times(0,\eta)
is a disjoint, finite union of graphs of increasing functions,
b_{1},\ldots,b_{K}
so that for each
k
,
{\displaystyle{\lim_{u\to 0^{+}}b_{k}(u)=0}}
and
b_{k}:(0,\delta)\to(0,\eta)
is of the form
\tilde{b}_{k}(u^{1/e_{k}})
where
e_{k}
is a positive integer and
\tilde{b}_{k}
is real analytic on
(-\delta^{1/e_{k}},\delta^{1/e_{k}})
.

Proof.

We refer to the Addendum
[
CP
]
for a proof.
∎

The main steps in the proofs in the next Sections use the circle of ideas generally known as Łojasiewicz inequalities, which we break up into some specific claims.

Proposition 3.5

(Łojasiewicz Inequality)
.

Given polynomials
P
and
Q
and a point
\vec{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{Z}(P)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
let
\mathcal{B}
be the following subset of
\mathbb{R}
,

\mathcal{B}=\left\{\beta\geq 0:\exists r>0\ \ \exists C>0:\ |\vec{x}-\vec{x}_{0}|<r\implies|P(\vec{x})|^{\beta}\leq C\left|Q(\vec{x})\right|\right\}.

If
P\not\equiv 0
and there is a radius
\rho>0
so that
\mathcal{Z}(Q)\cap\overline{B}_{\rho}(\vec{x}_{0})\subseteq\mathcal{Z}(P)
, then:

•

(Ł1)
There exists some integer
N>0
and some continuous semi-algebraic function
h:\overline{B}_{\rho}(\vec{x}_{0})\to\mathbb{R}
so that
P^{N}=h\cdot Q
.

•

(Ł2)

\mathcal{B}
is non-empty.

•

(Ł3)
The greatest lower bound, denoted
\beta_{0}=\mbox{\rm glb}\mathcal{B}
, satisfies
\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{Q}
.

•

(Ł4)

\beta_{0}\in\mathcal{B}
.

Sketch.

It is straightforward to check that if
\beta_{1}\in\mathcal{B}
and
\beta_{2}>\beta_{1}
, then
\beta_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
. If
Q(\vec{x}_{0})=0
then
0\notin\mathcal{B}
.

Statement (Ł1) is a special case of Theorem 2.6.6 of
[
BCR
]
(it holds more generally for continuous, semi-algebraic functions). Statement (Ł2) follows from (Ł1), where
|h|
has some maximum value on the closed ball with
r=\rho
by continuity, so
N\in\mathcal{B}
. The set
\mathcal{B}
does not depend on
N
from (Ł1) or the value of
\rho
. However,
\mathcal{B}
and its greatest lower bound
\beta_{0}\geq 0
depend on the point
\vec{x}_{0}
.

For statement (Ł3), we refer to
[
BR
]
. For the purposes of this article, we do not need to know the exact value of
\beta_{0}
, but for recent work on the problem of estimating
\beta_{0}
we refer to
[
BMN
]
and
[
OUSS2
]
.

Statement (Ł4) seems to be the deepest part of the Proposition, and does not immediately follow from (Ł1). We refer to
[
S
]
, where the claim is established for real analytic functions using geometric properties of subanalytic sets proved by
[
P
]
.
∎

Proposition 3.6

(Łojasiewicz Gradient Inequality)
.

Given a polynomial
P
and a point
\vec{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{Z}(P)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if
P\not\equiv 0
and
\vec{\nabla}P(\vec{x}_{0})=\vec{0}
then there is a radius
\rho>0
so that
\mathcal{Z}(|\vec{\nabla}P|^{2})\cap\overline{B}_{\rho}(\vec{x}_{0})\subseteq\mathcal{Z}(P)
, and:

•

(Ł5)
This set
{\mathbf{\Theta}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is non-empty:

{\mathbf{\Theta}}=\left\{\theta>0:\exists r>0\ \ \exists C>0:\ |\vec{x}-\vec{x}_{0}|<r\implies|P(\vec{x})|^{\theta}\leq C\left|\vec{\nabla}P(\vec{x})\right|\right\}.

•

(Ł6)

\theta_{0}=\mbox{\rm glb}{\mathbf{\Theta}}
satisfies
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{Q}\cap\left[\frac{1}{2},1\right)
.

•

(Ł7)

\theta_{0}\in{\mathbf{\Theta}}
.

Sketch.

For the containment
\mathcal{Z}(|\vec{\nabla}P|^{2})\cap\overline{B}_{\rho}(\vec{x}_{0})\subseteq\mathcal{Z}(P)
and statement (Ł5) we refer to
[
BM
]
, and then (Ł7) follows from Proposition
3.5
applied to the polynomial
\left|\vec{\nabla}P(\vec{x})\right|^{2}
. For estimates on
\theta_{0}
as in (Ł6), we refer to
[
DK
]
,
[
F
]
, and
[
OUSS1
]
.
∎

Recall that for a polynomial
P:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, the set of critical points (
\vec{x}_{0}
such that
\vec{\nabla}P(\vec{x}_{0})=\vec{0}
) is, for generic
P
, a finite set of isolated points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Proposition
3.6
holds even for exceptional polynomials where the critical points are not isolated.

4.
Another property of rational functions

The next Lemma uses (Ł1) from Proposition
3.5
, and considers an increase in the power of the denominator, unlike Lemma
2.6
where the power decreased. Lemma
4.1
has a stronger hypothesis than Lemma
2.6
; the rational function is not just locally bounded but approaches
0
at points in its indeterminacy set.

Lemma 4.1
.

For an open ball
B_{r}(\vec{x}_{0})=B_{r}
and a rational function
P(\vec{x})/Q(\vec{x})
, if the function
s:B_{r}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

s(\vec{x})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\displaystyle{\frac{P(\vec{x})}{Q(\vec{x})}}}&\mbox{\rm{for} $\vec{x}\in B_{r}\setminus\mathcal{Z}(Q)$}\\
&\\
0&\mbox{\rm{for} $\vec{x}\in B_{r}\cap\mathcal{Z}(Q)$}\end{array}\right.

is continuous on
B_{r}
,
then there exists a rational number
\varphi>0
so that

s_{\varphi}(\vec{x})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\displaystyle{\frac{|P(\vec{x})|}{|Q(\vec{x})|^{1+\varphi}}}}&\mbox{\rm{for} $\vec{x}\in B_{r}\setminus\mathcal{Z}(Q)$}\\
&\\
0&\mbox{\rm{for} $\vec{x}\in B_{r}\cap\mathcal{Z}(Q)$}\end{array}\right.

is continuous on
B_{r}
.

Proof.

|P(\vec{x})|/|Q(\vec{x})|^{1+\varphi}
is continuous at points outside
\mathcal{Z}(Q)
for any
\varphi
, so we only need to find
\varphi
so that
|P|/|Q|^{1+\varphi}
approaches
0
for points in
B_{r}\cap\mathcal{Z}(Q)
. The continuous function
s
is locally bounded near any point of
B_{r}
, so by Lemma
2.5
applied to a neighborhood of any point in
B_{r}\cap\mathcal{Z}(Q)
where
s
is bounded,
B_{r}\cap\mathcal{Z}(Q)\subseteq B_{r}\cap\mathcal{Z}(P)
.

The graph of
s(\vec{x})
,

G=\{(\vec{x},s(\vec{x})):\vec{x}\in B_{r}\}\subseteq B_{r}\times\mathbb{R}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}

is contained in the real algebraic set

\mathcal{Z}(x_{n+1}Q-P)=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},x_{n+1}):x_{n+1}Q(\vec{x})-P(\vec{x})=0\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.

At each point
\vec{x}_{1}\in B_{r}
where
Q(\vec{x}_{1})=0
, we also have
P(\vec{x}_{1})=0
, and
x_{n+1}Q(\vec{x}_{1})-P(\vec{x}_{1})=0
for every
x_{n+1}\in\mathbb{R}
, so
\mathcal{Z}(x_{n+1}Q-P)
contains a vertical line
\{(\vec{x}_{1},x_{n+1}):x_{n+1}\in\mathbb{R})\}
. To get the graph of
s
, remove the positive and negative parts of each such line, but keep the point
(\vec{x}_{1},0)
, resulting in:

G=((\mathcal{Z}(x_{n+1}Q-P)\setminus(\mathcal{Z}(Q)\times\mathbb{R}))\cup(\mathcal{Z}(Q)\times\{0\}))\cap(B_{r}\times\mathbb{R}).

This description shows that
G
is a semi-algebraic set in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
(after some elementary set operations,
G
is a finite union of finite intersections as in Definition
3.3
).

So, by construction, the function
s
with graph
G
is continuous on
B_{r}
and semi-algebraic, and has a zero set that contains
B_{r}\cap\mathcal{Z}(Q)
. These are exactly the hypotheses needed for Theorem 2.6.6 of
[
BCR
]
to apply to
s
and
Q

Some complex constructions

In this Section we show how to use complex polynomials to construct bounded real rational functions. We consider first one complex variable

and then several variables

Example 6.1
.

Real valued rational functions in two real variables
(x,y)
can be constructed in the following way, which, like Example
5.4
, is consistent with, but does not appeal to, Theorem
5.2
. For

, let

and

be non-constant, holomorphic polynomials that both vanish at the origin

. For both

and

, the zero at the origin is isolated, and they factor as

and

for multiplicities
\mu>0
,
\nu>0
and polynomials
f_{0}
,
g_{0}
with non-zero constant terms. Let
\kappa=\mu\nu^{\prime}=\nu\mu^{\prime}
as in Example
5.4
. The polynomial
f(z)
and the complex conjugate
\overline{g(z)}
satisfy, for

in some deleted disk

where

(6.1)

\displaystyle\frac{(f(z))^{\nu^{\prime}}}{\left(\overline{g(z)}\right)^{\mu^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{z^{\mu\nu^{\prime}}(f_{0}(z))^{\nu^{\prime}}}{\bar{z}^{{\mu^{\prime}}\nu}\left(\overline{g_{0}(z)}\right)^{\mu^{\prime}}}=\left(\frac{z}{\bar{z}}\right)^{\kappa}\frac{(f_{0}(z))^{\nu^{\prime}}(g_{0}(z))^{\mu^{\prime}}}{(g_{0}(z))^{\mu^{\prime}}\left(\overline{g_{0}(z)}\right)^{\mu^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{z^{2}}{|z|^{2}}\right)^{\kappa}\frac{(f_{0}(z))^{\nu^{\prime}}(g_{0}(z))^{\mu^{\prime}}}{|g_{0}(z)|^{2{\mu^{\prime}}}}

(6.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{z^{2}}{|z|^{2}}\right)^{\kappa}(c_{0}+c(z,\bar{z})).

The factor
f_{0}^{\nu^{\prime}}g_{0}^{\mu^{\prime}}/|g_{0}|^{2{\mu^{\prime}}}
on line (
6.1
) is non-vanishing and real analytic on

, so it is of the form
c_{0}+c(z,\bar{z})
with

and
\displaystyle{\lim_{z\to 0}c(z,\bar{z})=0}
as on line (
6.2
). The factor

does not have a

limit at the origin for any
\mu
,
\nu
, but it is bounded (with norm
\equiv 1
) so its product with

has limit
0
. We want to show that both the real and imaginary parts of (
6.1
) have different limits as
z\to 0
along different real directions, and it is enough to show this holds for
c_{0}z^{2\kappa}/|z|^{2\kappa}
. Let
c_{0}=R_{0}e^{i\phi_{0}}
with
R_{0}>0
, and
z=re^{i\theta}
for small

, so

c_{0}z^{2\kappa}/|z|^{2\kappa}=R_{0}e^{i(2\theta\kappa+\phi_{0})}=R_{0}\cos(2\theta\kappa+\phi_{0})+iR_{0}\sin(2\theta\kappa+\phi_{0}).

For
\theta_{1}=-\phi_{0}/(2\kappa)
and

\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\mbox{Re}\left(c_{0}\frac{(re^{i\theta_{1}})^{2\kappa}}{|re^{i\theta_{1}}|^{2\kappa}}\right)=R_{0}+0i\neq\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\mbox{Re}\left(c_{0}\frac{(re^{i\theta_{2}})^{2\kappa}}{|re^{i\theta_{2}}|^{2\kappa}}\right)=-R_{0}+0i.

For
\theta_{3}=(\frac{\pi}{2}-\phi_{0})/(2\kappa)

We can conclude that
f^{\nu^{\prime}}/\overline{g}^{\mu^{\prime}}
is locally bounded on

but both its real part

and its imaginary part

\displaystyle\frac{(f(z))^{\nu^{\prime}}}{\left(\overline{g(z)}\right)^{\mu^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{Re}\left((f(z))^{\nu^{\prime}}(g(z))^{\mu^{\prime}}\right)}{|g(z)|^{2{\mu^{\prime}}}}+i\frac{\mbox{Im}\left((f(z))^{\nu^{\prime}}(g(z))^{\mu^{\prime}}\right)}{|g(z)|^{2{\mu^{\prime}}}},

give examples of a bounded real rational function, with an isolated zero in the denominator, that cannot extend continuously to the origin. Example
1.1
is exactly the case
\mbox{Im}(z/(2\bar{z}))
. Of course, not every real rational function occurs in this way.

Example 6.2
.

Let

7.
Using the Łojasiewicz gradient inequality

Recall from Proposition
3.2
that when the domain is
2
-dimensional, a locally bounded real rational function

will either have only isolated points in the zero set

(Examples
1.1
,
1.2
,
1.3
), or a non-constant common factor

,

(Example

), or both (Example

For higher dimensions

with isolated zeros are exceptional cases and a non-empty zero set generically has positive dimension. This Section considers the case where

is the norm of the gradient of

7.1
) with an isolated zero in the denominator. The main result, Theorem

, applies even when the gradient has a non-isolated zero.

Example 7.1
.

Start with a polynomial
P
with critical point
\vec{x}_{0}
as in Proposition
3.6
, so
P(\vec{x}_{0})=|\vec{\nabla}P(\vec{x}_{0})|=0
. If
P
is non-constant then the squared norm

is a polynomial
\not\equiv 0
. Take any particular number
\theta
in the set
\mathbf{\Theta}
from statement (Ł5) from Proposition
3.6
, so there is some ball

, where
\mathcal{Z}(|\vec{\nabla}P|^{2})\cap B_{r}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}(P)\cap B_{r}

\vec{x}\in B_{r}\setminus\mathcal{Z}(|\vec{\nabla}P|^{2})
and the bound

corresponding to
\theta
from Proposition
3.6
,

0\leq\frac{|P(\vec{x})|^{2\theta}}{\left|\vec{\nabla}P(\vec{x})\right|^{2}}\leq C^{2}.

If we also assume there exist positive integers

,
q
, so that
p/q=\theta
, then raising to the power of

gives:

(7.1)

0\leq\frac{(P(\vec{x}))^{2p}}{\left|\vec{\nabla}P(\vec{x})\right|^{2q}}\leq C^{2q}.

The rational function (
7.1
) so constructed is bounded on
B_{r}
; the intersection of its indeterminacy set and

is
\mathcal{Z}(|\vec{\nabla}P|^{2})\cap B_{r}
.

In the construction of Example
7.1
, the denominator of (

,
q
, and the denominator may have a non-trivial factor in common with the numerator, as in the following Notation.

Notation 7.2
.

Given

,
\vec{x}_{0}
,
p
,
q
as in the construction of Example
7.1
, the numerator and denominator of the expression (
7.1
) each have a unique factorization (up to reordering and constant multiples, and not depending on

) into irreducible polynomials. Let

and
\left|\vec{\nabla}P(\vec{x})\right|^{2q}=F\cdot D

; if

\left|\vec{\nabla}P(\vec{x})\right|^{2q}
as a factor, then let

. Canceling

from the numerator and denominator of the rational function (

) gives another rational function
\displaystyle{\frac{N}{D}}
, the

form of
\displaystyle{\frac{(P(\vec{x}))^{2p}}{\left|\vec{\nabla}P(\vec{x})\right|^{2q}}}
.

After such a cancellation, so that the polynomials

and

have no common factors and are both
\not\equiv 0

Theorem 7.3
.

Given a polynomial
P\not\equiv 0
with

,

so that

, and

,

Proof.

The rational function

is bounded on its possibly larger domain, as in Definition

\varphi>0
so that

g_{\varphi}(\vec{x})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\displaystyle{\frac{(P(\vec{x}))^{2p_{0}}}{(|\vec{\nabla}P(\vec{x})|^{2q_{0}})^{1+\varphi}}}}&\mbox{\rm{for} $\vec{\nabla}P(\vec{x})\neq\vec{0}$}\\
&\\
0&\mbox{\rm{for} $\vec{\nabla}P(\vec{x})=\vec{0}$}\end{array}\right.

is continuous, and bounded by
C_{\varphi}
on some possibly smaller ball
B_{r^{\prime\prime}}
.
It follows that for
\vec{x}\in B_{r^{\prime\prime}}\setminus\mathcal{Z}(|\vec{\nabla}P(\vec{x})|^{2})
,

(7.6)

|P(\vec{x})|^{2p_{0}/(2q_{0}(1+\varphi))}\leq C_{\varphi}^{1/(2q_{0}(1+\varphi))}|\vec{\nabla}P(\vec{x})|.

If
\vec{\nabla}P(\vec{x})=\vec{0}
then both sides of the inequality (
7.6
) are
=0
by the inequality (
7.4
), so the inequality (
7.6
) holds for all
\vec{x}\in B_{r^{\prime\prime}}
. This contradicts the minimality of
\theta_{0}
.

For statement (3), if there is some
r^{\prime\prime}
so that
\mathcal{Z}(|\vec{\nabla}P|^{2})\cap B_{r^{\prime\prime}}=\{\vec{x}_{0}\}
, then
\mathcal{Z}(|\vec{\nabla}P|^{2})\cap B_{r^{\prime\prime}}=\mathcal{Z}(D)\cap B_{r^{\prime\prime}}
and so
f=g
on
B_{r^{\prime\prime}}
. Given any
B_{r^{\prime}}
,
g
is not continuous on
B_{r^{\prime}}\cap B_{r^{\prime\prime}}
by (2) and
f
is not continuous on
B_{r^{\prime}}
.
∎

Theorem 7.10
.

For any real algebraic set
A
strictly contained in a hyperplane, and any
\vec{x}_{0}\in A
, there exist
r>0
and a rational function
N/D
, so that the indeterminacy set of
N/D
is equal to
A
, and as in Theorem
7.4
,
N
and
D
have no common factors,
N/D
is bounded on
B_{r}(\vec{x}_{0})
, and the extension
f(\vec{x})
of
N/D
is not continuous on any neighborhood of
\vec{x}_{0}
.

Proof.

For
n\geq 2
, we choose coordinates so that the hyperplane is
\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1},0)\}
. The real algebraic set
A
of the hypothesis is of the form
\mathcal{Z}(P)
, for
P(\vec{x})=Q^{2}+x_{n}^{2}
and
Q=Q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})\not\equiv 0
. A short calculation of the squared norm of the gradient gives:

(7.9)

|\vec{\nabla}P|^{2}=4Q^{2}|\vec{\nabla}Q|^{2}+4x_{n}^{2}.

We note that both
P
and
|\vec{\nabla}P|^{2}
are irreducible polynomials in the polynomial ring
\mathbb{R}[\vec{x}]
, so they have no non-constant polynomial common factor unless one is a polynomial multiple of the other. This can only happen when one is a scalar multiple of the other and
Q
is a linear polynomial with
|\vec{\nabla}Q|^{2}\equiv 1
(as in Example
7.7
), and this case can be avoided by replacing
Q
with
2Q
, which does not change the set
A
.

If
P(\vec{x})=0
then
Q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})=0
and
x_{n}=0
, so by Equation (
7.9
),
|\vec{\nabla}P|^{2}=0
, and
\mathcal{Z}(P)\subseteq\mathcal{Z}(|\vec{\nabla}P|^{2})
.

For any
p
and
q
so that
p/q=\theta_{0}
is the Łojasiewicz exponent for
P
at
\vec{x}_{0}
, there is some
r>0
so that the rational function

(7.10)

\displaystyle{\frac{(P(\vec{x}))^{2p}}{|\vec{\nabla}P(\vec{x})|^{2q}}}=\frac{(Q^{2}+x_{n}^{2})^{2p}}{(4Q^{2}|\vec{\nabla}Q|^{2}+4x_{n}^{2})^{q}}=\frac{N(\vec{x})}{D(\vec{x})}

is bounded on
B_{r}(\vec{x}_{0})
and has indeterminacy set
A
(with dimension
\leq n-2
and
\mathcal{Z}(D)\cap B_{r}=A\cap B_{r}
as in Theorem
7.3
). By construction,
(P(\vec{x}))^{2p}
and
|\vec{\nabla}P(\vec{x})|^{2q}
have no common factors. The extension
f(\vec{x})=g(\vec{x})
from Theorem
7.4
is not continuous on any neighborhood of
\vec{x}_{0}
. We emphasize that the number
\theta_{0}
, and, consequently, the radius
r
, the expression (
7.10
), and the extension
f
, all depend on the point
\vec{x}_{0}
and the choice of defining function
Q
, but the indeterminacy locus of the expression (
7.10
) in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
does not.
∎
