Internalizing Curriculum Judgment for LLM Reinforcement Fine-Tuning
Han Zheng, Yining Ma, Karthick Gunasekaran, Bharathan Balaji, Zheng Du, Shiv Vitaladevuni, and Cathy Wu

Abstract
In LLM Reinforcement Fine-Tuning (RFT), curriculum learning drives both efficiency and performance. Yet, current methods externalize curriculum judgment via handcrafted heuristics or auxiliary models, risking misalignment with the policy’s training dynamics. In this paper, we introduce METIS (METacognitive Internalized Self-judgment), a novel framework that internalizes curriculum judgment as a native capability. Leveraging a critical observation that within-prompt reward variance effectively gauges prompt informativeness, METIS predicts this metric based on recent training outcomes as lightweight in-context learning examples. This intrinsic self-judgment then dynamically dictates the training allocation. Moreover, METIS closes the loop between judgment and optimization by jointly optimizing the standard RFT rewards and a self-judgment reward. This allows the policy to learn what to learn next, as a form of metacognition. Across extensive discrete and continuous RFT benchmarks from mathematical reasoning, code generation, to agentic function-calling, METIS consistently delivers superior performance while accelerating convergence by up to 67%. By bypassing handcrafted heuristics and auxiliary models, our work establishes a simple, closed-loop, and highly efficient curriculum internalization paradigm for LLM reinforcement fine-tuning.

Introduction

Effective learning depends not only on how a learner updates from feedback, but also on what it chooses to practice. Problems already mastered offer little information, while problems far beyond current ability often provide little actionable signal. The most useful practice lies near the boundary of current competence, echoing
zone of proximal development
in education
[
42
]
. Because this frontier shifts as learning progresses, a capable learner should therefore not only learn to solve problems, but also monitor their own learning state and decide which problems to learn next. This suggests a form of metacognition: using self-assessment to guide future learning effectively
[
13
]
.

Such a principle has recently gained increasing traction in reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) for large language models (LLMs)
[
32
,
37
,
11
]
, which is operationalized as
curriculum learning
. Because rollouts are expensive in LLM RFT, the curriculum is not merely a data reordering: it determines how training compute is allocated and therefore directly affects both efficiency and final performance. Generally, a good curriculum prioritizes prompts that are
informative
for the current policy, rather than prompts that are uniformly easy, uniformly impossible, or selected by a static notion of difficulty.

A key signal of such
prompt informativeness
arises naturally in group-relative RFT
[
37
,
47
,
50
]
, where the rewards are averaged from multiple rollouts of the same prompt: when all rollouts receive the same reward, the prompt provides little relative signal, since it is either consistently solved or failed; when rollouts produce diverse outcomes, the prompt exposes meaningful behavioral variation and induces more informative updates. Therefore,
within-prompt reward variance
provides a simple yet general proxy for identifying prompts at the frontier of policy competence
[
18
,
39
,
14
]
. In this paper, we formalize and extend such a proxy to handle both discrete and continuous rewards in LLM RFT.

Moreover, such prompt informativeness is inherently dynamic. As the policy improves, previously insurmountable prompts may become informative, while once-useful prompts eventually become saturated. Existing curricula address this through various forms of adaptation (see Fig.
1
): some rely on predefined coarse prompt groups based on domains or data sources
[
43
,
8
]
, others apply hand-coded heuristics over rollout statistics or offline difficulty labels
[
48
,
34
,
33
,
18
,
51
]
, and the rest train a separate model to score such informativeness
[
39
,
14
]
. Crucially, across all these paradigms, curriculum judgment remains
external
to the policy. But because the competence frontier moves with the policy as it learns, accurate judgment requires tracking the policy’s current state; thus, we argue that the policy itself is the entity most directly aware of that state. As the LLM has strong inference and reasoning capabilities, it could be elicited to read its own rollout outcomes and predict which prompts will be informative for it next. However, current methods neglect such metacognitive potential, treating the policy as a passive recipient rather than an active architect of its own curricula.

This motivates curriculum
internalization
. A capable learner does not merely follow a sequence of externally assigned exercises. It learns from its own attempts, forms an assessment of its current competence, and uses that assessment to decide what to practice next. We argue that an LLM trained with RFT should acquire an analogous capability. During training, rollout outcomes provide direct evidence about the policy’s current learning state. Internalizing curriculum judgment exposes the policy to this evidence directly and trains it to map recent prompt-outcome patterns to future prompt informativeness. The goal is therefore not only to select better prompts for the model, but to make the ability to judge useful prompts part of the model’s learned competence. This reframes curriculum learning from selecting data to training the model to decide what it should learn from next.

To this end, we introduce
METIS
(
MET
acognitive
I
nternalized
S
elf-judgment), a novel framework that internalizes curriculum judgment as
a native policy capability.
METIS
asks the policy to self-judge candidate prompts by predicting their expected within-prompt reward variance as a measure of prompt effectiveness based on lightweight in-context examples constructed from recent training outcomes. The predicted scores then determine how the rollout budget is allocated. After rollout,
the obtained data can be utilized for two coupled learning signals. The original task rewards improve the policy’s problem-solving behavior, while we introduce a judgment reward that calibrates the policy’s understanding of which prompts are informative. Together,
METIS
does not merely use the policy to solve tasks; it also learns what to learn next, closing the loop between judgment and optimization.

Our main contributions are as follows: 1) We formulate curriculum learning for LLM RFT as a policy-dependent competence-frontier tracking problem, with within-prompt reward variance as a general signal of informativeness across both discrete and continuous rewards;
2) We propose
METIS
, the first LLM RFT framework to internalize curriculum judgment, while jointly optimizing task rewards and a self-judgment reward, transforming curriculum into a learned capability of the policy rather than an external selection mechanism;
3) On mathematical reasoning, code generation, and agentic function-calling benchmarks across discrete, continuous, and multi-turn rewards,
METIS
demonstrates improved final performance, yielding up to 67% wall-clock reduction with only around 3.9% per-step overhead, providing a metacognitive framework for efficient LLM RFT.

3
Problem Formulation

We consider reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) of a language-model policy
\pi_{\theta}
over a prompt pool
\mathcal{P}
. For each prompt
x\in\mathcal{P}
, the policy samples a rollout
y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
and receives a bounded scalar reward
r(x,y)\in[0,1]
. This formulation covers both binary verifiable rewards (
r\in\{0,1\}
), common in mathematical reasoning, and continuous rewards such as partial test-case credit in code generation or trajectory-level scoring in agentic tasks. Notably,
METIS
operates uniformly across
both
regimes, whereas pass-rate heuristics used in prior work
[
47
,
18
,
39
,
14
,
8
,
43
,
48
]
are restricted to the binary case.

Group-relative reinforcement fine-tuning.

We develop
METIS
on top of the prevailing group-relative RFT methods that sample multiple rollouts per prompt
[
37
,
47
,
50
,
31
]
. At iteration
t
, a set of prompts
\mathcal{S}_{t}
is selected for rollout. For each prompt
x\in\mathcal{S}_{t}
, the policy generates
n
rollouts
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
with rewards
r_{i}=r(x,y_{i})
, and a centered group-relative advantage is constructed as

A_{i}(x)=r_{i}-\bar{r}(x),\qquad\bar{r}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}.

(1)

Following recent work
[
28
]
, we drop the per-prompt standard-deviation normalization originally used in GRPO
[
11
]
, which has been shown to introduce a difficulty bias.
The resulting policy loss takes a clipped surrogate form (taking GRPO as an example):

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{policy}}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{t}|n}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min\!\left(\rho_{i}(\theta)A_{i}(x),\;\mathrm{clip}\bigl(\rho_{i}(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\bigr)A_{i}(x)\right),

(2)

where
\rho_{i}(\theta)=\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid x)/\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{i}\mid x)
. Note that
METIS
is agnostic to the specific normalization, clipping, or KL-regularization choices made by individual algorithms; it requires only that multiple rollout rewards are available per prompt so that within-prompt outcome variability can be estimated.

Quantifying prompt informativeness via within-prompt reward variance.

In group-relative RFT, prompts whose rollouts produce diverse rewards drive larger updates than prompts whose rollouts all agree
[
18
,
39
,
14
]
. We formalize this and extend it to continuous reward. From Eq. (
1
), the mean squared advantage on prompt
x
is exactly the empirical reward variance across its
n
rollouts,

v(x)\;\coloneqq\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}(x)^{2}\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(r_{i}-\bar{r}(x)\bigr)^{2},

(3)

which vanishes when all rollouts receive the same reward (uniformly solved or failed prompts), and grows as their rewards become more dispersed. Because
v(x)
depends on which
n
rollouts were sampled, we lift it to a population quantity under the current policy,

v_{\theta}(x)\;\coloneqq\;\mathrm{Var}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\bigl[\,r(x,y)\,\bigr],

(4)

of which
v(x)
is an unbiased estimator. The subscript
\theta
is essential: such informativeness is
policy-dependent
and changes as
\pi_{\theta}
evolves during training. Since
r\in[0,1]
,
v_{\theta}(x)
is bounded above by
1/4
, attained at a Bernoulli
(1/2)
reward, i.e., when each rollout is equally likely to succeed or fail.
1
1

1

For any
R\in[0,1]
with mean
\mu
,
R^{2}\leq R
implies
\mathrm{Var}(R)\leq\mu(1-\mu)\leq 1/4
.

This formulation is general by design. In the
binary-reward
case,
v_{\theta}(x)=p(x)\bigl(1-p(x)\bigr)
, where
p(x)
is the per-prompt success probability;
v_{\theta}(x)
is maximized at
p(x)=1/2
, recovering the intermediate-difficulty heuristic used by prior binary-curriculum work
[
18
,
51
,
14
,
39
]
as a special case. In the
continuous-reward
case, pass rate is undefined since reward is no longer a
0/1
success indicator, but
v_{\theta}(x)
still measures dispersion in rollout quality and remains a faithful proxy for learning-signal magnitude.
METIS
therefore operates on continuous-reward tasks (code generation, agentic function-calling) without any task-specific notion of difficulty.

Curriculum as competence-frontier tracking.

A
curriculum
is a selector
\Gamma
that, given a candidate pool
\mathcal{C}_{t}\subset\mathcal{P}
and the training history
\mathcal{H}_{t}
at iteration
t
, returns the batch of
B
prompts to rollout:

\mathcal{S}_{t}\;=\;\Gamma(\mathcal{C}_{t},\,\mathcal{H}_{t}),\qquad|\mathcal{S}_{t}|=B,

Taking informativeness to be the policy-dependent variance
v_{\theta}(x)
of Eq. (
4
), the
ideal
selector simply ranks candidates by informativeness measure and selects the top
B
prompts:

\Gamma^{\star}(\mathcal{C}_{t},\,\mathcal{H}_{t})\;=\;\mathrm{TopB}_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\,v_{\theta}(x).

(5)

Crucially, we note that
v_{\theta}
depends on
\theta
: as the policy improves, prompts once high-variance saturate (rollouts agree on success), while prompts once uniformly failed re-enter the informative band. The top-
v_{\theta}
set therefore traces a
non-stationary competence frontier
that moves with training, and a useful curriculum is one that tracks this frontier as it moves.

Existing methods approximate
\Gamma^{\star}
with a selector external to the policy: a fixed difficulty schedule
[
34
,
33
]
, a hand-coded rule on post-rollout statistics
[
47
,
18
,
51
]
, or an auxiliary selector model
[
39
,
14
]
. In each case, what counts as informative is decided by a module the policy itself neither reads nor refines, leaving the external judgment liable to drift out of alignment with the policy’s evolving competence.

METIS
instead sets
\Gamma\!=\!\pi_{\theta}
: the policy itself decides the curriculum. At each step, it conditions on a
calibration memory
of recent prompt-variance pairs drawn from
\mathcal{H}_{t}
and emits, for each candidate, a structured response from which we parse a predicted informativeness
\hat{v}_{\theta}(x)\approx v_{\theta}(x)
, yielding

\mathcal{S}_{t}\;=\;\mathrm{TopB}_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\,\hat{v}_{\theta}(x).

(6)

The policy is therefore trained to do two things at once: solve the selected prompts, and predict which prompts will be most informative, which we detail in Section
4
.

METIS
. At each iteration, the policy predicts candidate informativeness
\hat{v}_{\theta}(x)
via in-context learning on a calibration memory of recent prompt-variance pairs (

). The most informative prompts are then rolled out (

) to yield task rewards and realized variance
v(x)
. The policy is jointly optimized (

4
Method

METIS
internalizes curriculum learning as a native metacognitive skill via a two-fold design (see Fig.
2
). First, the policy self-judges prompt informativeness pre-rollout through
in-context learning
(Sec.
4.1
). Second, the realized rollout variance provides a calibration reward to update this predictive capability online, closing the loop between task optimization and curriculum judgment (Sec.
4.2
–
4.3
).

4.1
Self-Judgment via In-Context Learning

The previous section reduced curriculum learning to estimating
\hat{v}_{\theta}(x)\approx v_{\theta}(x)
for every candidate prompt
x\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
, with the selector implemented by the policy itself:
\Gamma=\pi_{\theta}
. The remaining design question is
how
the policy should produce this prediction.
METIS
addresses it through
prompt-level
in-context self-assessment: the policy reads a compact context built from the training history
\mathcal{H}_{t}
and a candidate prompt, and emits a short structured judgment of the candidate’s expected informativeness.

We note three properties that make ICL well-suited to curriculum self-judgment as follows:

•

Predictive.
The policy emits
\hat{v}_{\theta}(x)

before
any rollout, replacing the post-hoc filtering in prior variance-based methods
[
47
,
43
,
8
]
with a forward judgment over the candidate pool.

•

Adaptive.
Conditioning on recent prompt–variance pairs lets the judgment track the moving competence frontier without retraining: prompts that have saturated or newly entered the informative band are reflected as soon as they appear in the conditioning context.

•

Efficient.
Each judgment is a short structured response, in contrast to the
n
full rollouts that variance-filtering methods
[
48
,
18
]
must generate per candidate just to compute the filter, typically more than an order of magnitude cheaper in our settings (cost analysis in Sec.
5
).

We instantiate the in-context evidence as a
calibration memory

\mathcal{M}_{t}\;=\;\{(x_{k},v(x_{k}))\}_{k=1}^{K}\;\subset\;\mathcal{H}_{t},

holding the
K
most recent prompt–variance pairs realized by the current policy. For each candidate
x\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
, the self-judgment context is

\mathrm{ctx}_{t}(x)\;=\;\bigl[\,I_{\mathrm{judge}};\;\mathcal{M}_{t};\;x\,\bigr]

where
I_{\mathrm{judge}}
is a short instruction stating the prediction task, the definition of variance, and the output format. Since
v_{\theta}(x)\in[0,1/4]
(Sec.
3
), the instruction asks the policy to reason freely about the candidate and then commit to a single numerical prediction in this range inside a
\texttt{\textbackslash boxed\lx@text@lbrace}\cdot\texttt{\lx@text@rbrace}
tag (e.g.
\boxed{0.15}
), from which we deterministically extract a scalar as the predicted informativeness:

\hat{v}_{\theta}(x)\;\sim\;\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\mathrm{ctx}_{t}(x)).

(7)

Responses with no parseable boxed value, or whose value falls outside
[0,1/4]
, are counted as judgment failures (Sec.
5.3
); the full prompt template and a sample response are given in Appendix
A.1
and
B.3
. The training subset is then
\mathcal{S}_{t}=\mathrm{TopB}_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\,\hat{v}_{\theta}(x)
as in Eq. (
6
). This design keeps curriculum judgment inside the same token-based interface the policy already uses to solve tasks, building on its existing ICL and self-evaluation capabilities
[
35
,
20
]
. Prompt selection is thus guided by the model’s own assessment rather than by a hand-coded rule or a separately trained selector.

4.2
Internalizing Curriculum Judgment

In-context elicitation alone makes the policy’s self-judgment
available
, but does not make it
accurate
.

METIS
closes this gap by reinforcing curriculum judgment from realized outcomes.

For each selected prompt
x\in\mathcal{S}_{t}
, we generate
n
task rollouts, compute rewards
\{r_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and obtain the realized variance
v(x)
from Eq. (
3
). We score each pre-rollout prediction against its realized counterpart with a squared-error calibration reward
[
5
]
,

R_{\mathrm{judge}}(x)\;=\;1-\bigl(4(\hat{v}_{\theta}(x)-v(x))\bigr)^{2},

(8)

which is maximized only when the pre-rollout judgment matches the realized variance. The supervision is fully endogenous, coming from the rollouts the policy just generated as part of normal RFT, with no
additional labels
, no
auxiliary model
, and no
extra environment interaction
.

Since each prompt receives a single judgment response, we update the self-judgment tokens with a REINFORCE-style estimator using a moving-average baseline
b_{t}
,

\nabla_{\theta}J_{\mathrm{judge}}\;=\;\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{t}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}\bigl(R_{\mathrm{judge}}(x)-b_{t}\bigr)\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}\bigl(\hat{v}_{\theta}(x)\mid\mathrm{ctx}_{t}(x)\bigr),

(9)

and denote the corresponding minimization loss by
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{judge}}
. This objective makes curriculum judgment a trainable behavior of the policy: predictions that correctly anticipate informative prompts are reinforced, while miscalibrated judgments (e.g., overconfident on saturated prompts, or dismissive of high-variance ones) are suppressed. As training progresses, this in-context self-judgment continuously calibrates, allowing the induced curriculum to tightly track the policy’s advancing competence frontier.

4.3
Joint Optimization

METIS
optimizes two coupled token streams within the same policy: task-solution tokens (the rollouts
y_{i}
) under the group-relative RFT objective
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{policy}}
of Eq. (
2
), and self-judgment tokens under the calibration loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{judge}}
. The combined objective is

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}\;=\;\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{policy}}\;+\;\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{judge}},

(10)

where
\lambda
controls the strength of the judgment update. Because both signals update the same policy, task rewards improve the model’s ability to solve prompts while calibration rewards improve its ability to anticipate which prompts will produce useful learning signal, so the induced curriculum evolves automatically with task ability, without any hand-tuned schedule, externally specified difficulty, or auxiliary selector. The full procedure is summarized in Algorithm

Compute Cost of Self-Judgment

Fig.
5

METIS

The baselines each fail one axis: ADCL inflates wall-clock with extra rollouts that re-estimate prompt difficulty but never update the policy; PCL keeps wall-clock small but drops throughput substantially by training a separate value model alongside GRPO. SEC is the cheapest, where it only maintains coarse-grained EMA statistics over prompt categories; however, as we have seen, this efficiency is traded at the cost of accuracy.

is the only prompt-level method light on

6
Conclusions

We presented
METIS
, a closed-loop framework in which the policy itself predicts prompt informativeness via in-context self-judgment and is reinforced from realized rollout outcomes, learning both to solve prompts and to decide which prompts to learn from next. Across math, code, and agentic tasks,
METIS
delivers higher accuracy with up to
67\%
less wall-clock time at only
3.9\%
per-step overhead. These results indicate that prompt-level curriculum judgment can be reliably absorbed into the policy itself, removing the need for hand-tuned schedules or separately trained selectors in RFT. One potential limitation is that
METIS
relies on the base policy’s in-context learning capacity, which weaker backbones may not meet. While
METIS
produced visibly more stable training dynamics than baselines in our runs, we do not formally quantify training stability. Future work includes characterizing such stability systematically and deploying METIS on real-world LLM RFT systems. Our code, together with reproducibility instructions, will be made available on GitHub soon.

A.2
Dataset Pre-processing

DAPO-Math-17k (math, training).

We use the released DAPO-Math-17k collection of
17.4
k competition-style math problems
[
47
]
as is, without additional filtering. Each problem ships with a short reference final answer. The policy is instructed to produce a full chain-of-thought solution and a final-answer line; the reward is binary, equal to one when the extracted final answer exactly matches the reference under standard math normalization, and zero otherwise.

CodeContests-Plus (code, training).

We start from the public CodeContests-Plus dataset
[
44
]
and apply three filters that together leave
4{,}432
problems.
First
, we drop any problem with no Python program among its reference correct submissions, since we train a Python-only policy and the per-problem time limits are tuned for C++; problems that no Python program can solve within the allotted time only inject reward noise.
Second
, we drop problems with more than
25
test cases, since the long tail of test counts dominates rollout time without changing the per-problem ranking signal.
Third
, we drop problems whose statements reference figures (images or vector diagrams) that a text-only policy cannot read. The training reward is the fraction of test cases passed by the model’s program when executed, continuous in
[0,1]
. The statement seen by the self-judgment predictor retains only the prose part of the description: we strip the formal Constraints, Input, Output, Examples, and Notes sections, as these are uninformative for variance prediction and would otherwise crowd the calibration context.

BFCL v3 (agentic, training).

We use all five multi-turn categories of the Berkeley Function Calling Leaderboard v3
[
46
]
– Base, Missed-Function, Missed-Parameter, Long-Context, and Composite. Each task is a sequence of user turns interleaved with API calls against a stateful environment. We build a single system message that summarizes every available API in natural language, place the first user turn in the policy prompt, and feed the remaining user turns into the multi-turn interaction loop one at a time. The reference tool-call trajectory serves as ground truth, and the reward is computed against the actual sequence of tool calls executed by the model, yielding a continuous score in
[0,1]
. We split the resulting tasks
80\%/20\%
into train and validation with per-category stratification, ensuring every category appears in both splits.

Math evaluation suite.

AIME 2024, AIME 2025, MATH-500, and Minerva Math share a single preparation pipeline. To keep offline numbers directly comparable to the training-time validation curves, we reuse the exact problem-wrapping instruction (think step by step and place the final answer in a boxed expression) together with the exact answer-extraction and Hendrycks-MATH normalization used during training. AIME 2024 contributes
30
problems and AIME 2025 contributes
30
problems combining the two AIME 2025 olympiad sittings; MATH-500 contributes
500
problems whose released answers are already short strings. Minerva Math contributes
272
problems whose release contains full derivations rather than short answers; we recover each reference answer by taking the last boxed expression in the gold solution, with balanced-brace handling so that nested expressions (e.g. fractions) survive intact.

Code evaluation suite.

The code evaluation suite combines LiveCodeBench v6
[
17
]
(
1{,}055
problems), HumanEval (
164
problems), MBPP (full test split,
974
problems), and BigCodeBench (v0.1.4,
1{,}140
problems). LiveCodeBench v6 is a competitive-programming benchmark with stdin/stdout test cases; we evaluate it under the same stdin/stdout prompt and execution-based scorer used during CodeContests-Plus training. The remaining three benchmarks are function-call-based. We evaluate HumanEval, MBPP, and BigCodeBench under benchmark-appropriate function-style prompts that ask the model to produce a complete Python function, matching the format each benchmark was originally designed for. Scoring on all four benchmarks is performed by executing the extracted code against the benchmark’s official tests in an isolated environment, with the score equal to the fraction of tests passed.

A.3
Training Infrastructure

All experiments are implemented on the VERL reinforcement-learning framework
[
38
]
with vLLM
[
21
]
as the policy rollout engine. For mathematical-reasoning training, rewards are cheap to compute (a verifier match against a short reference answer), so rollout, reward, and policy update run on a fully synchronous schedule. For code-generation and agentic function-calling training, rewards are dominated by external execution; we therefore run reward computation asynchronously, overlapping the scoring of completed rollouts with continued generation so that GPUs stay busy on the rollout engine while CPU workers handle the heavier execution side.

A.5
Baseline implementation details

We compare
METIS
against three external-curriculum baselines: SEC, ADCL, and PCL. All three were originally proposed for binary-reward mathematical reasoning. We re-implement each on top of our shared GRPO trainer so that every method sees identical rollouts, optimizer, and hardware; the only differences are the curriculum mechanism itself and, for ADCL and PCL, the modifications described below that extend them to continuous-reward tasks.

SEC.

Self-Evolving Curriculum
[
8
]
treats curriculum learning as a non-stationary multi-armed bandit over coarse prompt categories. Our implementation closely follows the authors’ released code. We partition the training set into ten categories. At each training step, the sampler draws a category via Boltzmann softmax over per-category Q-values and then samples prompts uniformly within the chosen category until the batch is full. After the GRPO update, the Q-value of every category appearing in the batch is updated by an exponential moving average toward the mean per-token absolute advantage observed on its prompts, so categories that currently carry the largest gradient signal are progressively up-weighted. Because SEC consumes the GRPO advantage signal directly and never inspects the raw reward, the algorithm transfers to continuous-reward tasks (code generation, agentic function calling) without modification.

ADCL.

Adaptive Difficulty Curriculum Learning
[
48
]
is an offline-then-online easy-to-hard curriculum. Our implementation strictly follows the algorithm description in the original paper, using the curriculum-only variant. Before training, the base policy is rolled out on the entire training set to assign each prompt an initial difficulty score; the dataset is then sorted in ascending order by difficulty and partitioned into a fixed number of sequential chunks (eight in our runs). Training proceeds chunk by chunk; between chunks, the difficulty of the next chunk is re-estimated under the updated policy and the chunk is locally re-sorted, so the curriculum tracks the model’s evolving competence without re-sorting the whole dataset.

In the binary-reward setting (mathematical reasoning), we keep the original definition of difficulty as one minus the empirical pass rate. For continuous-reward tasks (code generation, BFCL), pass rate is undefined since rewards are no longer
0/1
; to extend ADCL to this regime, we rank prompts by their empirical
reward variance
instead: low-variance prompts are treated as easy (the policy’s behavior is already settled, either consistently succeeding or consistently failing), and high-variance prompts as hard (the behavior is still unstable). The chunk structure and between-chunk re-estimation are otherwise unchanged.

PCL.

Prompt Curriculum Learning
[
14
]
replaces a hand-coded curriculum with a learned auxiliary value model. Our implementation strictly follows the algorithm description in the original paper. At each training step we sample a candidate pool several times larger than the batch, score every candidate with the value model, select the prompts whose predicted score best matches the desired target, run GRPO on the selected batch, and update the value model from the realized outcomes of that step. The value model is itself a language model: we initialize its backbone from the same pretrained model as the policy and attach a small scalar value head on top of the prompt’s final hidden state. It thus inherits the policy’s representation of natural-language problems while learning a separate readout for the curriculum signal. The value model and the policy do not share parameters during training.

In the binary-reward setting we follow the original PCL: the value head regresses (under MSE) onto the empirical pass rate of each prompt, and selection picks the batch whose predicted pass rate is closest to a target of
0.5
. For continuous-reward tasks, where pass rate is no longer defined, we adapt PCL to the informativeness signal used throughout this paper: the value head instead regresses onto the empirical
reward variance
of each prompt, and selection picks the prompts with the
highest
predicted variance. This keeps the auxiliary-selector design of PCL intact while making it apply uniformly to both binary and continuous rewards.

METIS
unifies binary and continuous rewards under a single informativeness measure rather than relying on a regime-specific heuristic.

(iii) Stability of the gain throughout training.
The pass@1 trajectories of
METIS
exhibit the same smooth, monotone shape as in Fig.

(top row), without the visible plateaus that align with chunk boundaries in ADCL or the late-stage stagnation that PCL exhibits once its auxiliary value model overfits the early prompt distribution. We interpret this stability as further evidence that the gain is driven by sustained alignment with the moving competence frontier, rather than by a fortunate initial selection that other methods may also occasionally produce.

\lambda
.
Tab.

(a) sweeps
\lambda\in\{0,\,0.01,\,1\}
with the calibration memory fixed at

. At
\lambda=0
, the predictor is elicited but never reinforced from realized rollouts: ICL alone keeps the parse-failure rate already low (
2.2\%

27.2

\lambda=0.01

27.9
and drops failures to
0.3\%

6
, where
R_{\mathrm{judge}}
climbs steadily while predictions remain differentiated across the candidate pool. Pushing the loss to
\lambda=1

25.5

10
shows the predictions saturating at the maximum admissible variance
0.25
. This is the expected failure mode of any calibration-style auxiliary reward: when the judgment loss outweighs the policy loss, the model adopts the trivial shortcut of always predicting the upper bound, which maximizes selection probability for every candidate and effectively neutralizes the curriculum. The window between under- and over-weighting is therefore wide (
\lambda
varies by two orders of magnitude around the default), but its boundaries are sharp, and we accordingly recommend treating
\lambda
as a small but non-zero multiplicative knob rather than a hyperparameter on the scale of the policy loss.

Sensitivity to the calibration memory size
K
.
Tab.
6
(b) sweeps
K\in\{0,\,3,\,9\}
at
\lambda=0.01
. Removing the calibration memory entirely (
K=0
) is catastrophic: the parse-failure rate jumps to
87.6\%
and average accuracy reverts to the No Curriculum value, with the qualitative failure mode traced in Appendix
B.4
, where the predictor mistakes the candidate prompt for an ordinary task and attempts to solve it. Tripling the in-context evidence from
K=3
to
K=9

27.9\to 27.2
) while keeping failures comparably low. We attribute the lack of further gain at
K=9
to two factors: (a) realized variances are noisy at the per-prompt level, so beyond a small number of exemplars additional pairs add little marginal calibration signal; (b) longer calibration contexts inflate the self-judgment forward pass without changing the rank of
\hat{v}_{\theta}(x)
used by the top-
B
selector, and any noise added to absolute predictions has to fight a larger context length to be corrected by
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{judge}}
.
K=3
sits at the inflection point of this trade-off and is the value that preserves the per-step throughput reported in Sec.
5.4
, motivating it as the default across all task families.

Joint reading of the two sweeps.
Together, the two sub-tables demonstrate that the closed-loop design is necessary in both directions: removing the in-context evidence (
K=0
) breaks the predictor at the input side, while removing the realized-variance signal or over-weighting it (
\lambda=0
or
\lambda=1
) breaks the predictor at the output side. The default
(K{=}3,\,\lambda{=}0.01
) is a configuration in which the parse-failure rate is below
1\%
and average pass@1 strictly improves over both ablated variants and all external-curriculum baselines, which we read as direct evidence that the two ingredients in Sec.
4
are complementary rather than redundant.

Appendix C
Broader Impact

METIS
is a methodological improvement to reinforcement fine-tuning. Because its primary effect is to reduce the wall-clock and per-step cost of RFT (Sec.
5.4
), its societal impact is shaped chiefly by who benefits from cheaper training rather than by any new model capability.

Positive impact.
Lowering the compute floor for reasoning, code, and agentic RFT broadens participation to academic and resource-constrained groups for whom replicating frontier RFT pipelines would otherwise be prohibitive, and reduces the aggregate energy and carbon footprint of model development at a given level of capability.

Negative impact.
The efficiency gain is intrinsically dual-use:
METIS
does not change
what
a model can be trained to do, only
how efficiently
a given competence is reached, so it accelerates helpful and misuse-prone training pipelines alike. Our work does not itself contribute alignment or safety improvements, and downstream operators should treat the freed compute as an opportunity to strengthen evaluation and post-training safety work rather than only to scale capability. Concrete release-level safeguards are described in Appendix
D
.

Appendix D
Safeguards

METIS
is a curriculum-learning method for reinforcement fine-tuning, not a foundation model or a dataset, and we release no new pre-trained checkpoints or scraped corpora; the artifacts shipped with the paper are limited to the training pipeline (MIT license) and reproducibility scripts. All datasets used (Tab.
7
) are publicly released under permissive licenses with no personally identifying information, no scraped web text, and no demographic attributes, and the pre-trained backbones we fine-tune (Qwen3, Llama-3.1, DeepSeek-R1-Distill) are obtained from their official releases and inherit the safeguards put in place by their original authors;
METIS
does not weaken or remove these safeguards.

As discussed in Appendix
C
, the efficiency gain from
METIS
is dual-use. We mitigate this by (i) restricting our reported experiments to public benchmarks under deterministic, machine-checkable rewards, (ii) documenting the full training pipeline (Appendix
A
) so that practitioners can layer standard safety post-training (RLHF, red-teaming, refusal training) on top of any
METIS
-fine-tuned model, and (iii) releasing the reference implementation as a small extension of VERL
[
38
]
rather than as model checkpoints, so that any deployment requires re-training and remains subject to the deployer’s own safety review.

METIS
