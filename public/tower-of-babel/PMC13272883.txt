Intestinal Parasitic Infections and Associated Factors Among Psychiatric Patients at Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Northwest Ethiopia: A Cross‐Sectional Study

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsIntestinal parasitic infections pose significant public health challenges in developing countries. They are major concerns in rehabilitation centers for individuals with mental disabilities. However, little attention has been given to preventing these infections among the mentally disabled groups. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of intestinal parasitic infections and associated factors in psychiatric patients.MethodsWe conducted a cross‐sectional study at Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Northwest Ethiopia between August 2024 and October 2024, on 402 participants enrolled by convenient sampling technique. The sociodemographic and clinical data were collected by psychiatry specialists. Stool samples were examined using wet mount, formol‐ether concentration, and modified acid‐fast staining techniques. Data were analyzed using the SPSS software version 23. Bivariate and multivariate logistic regressions determined the association between variables. Factors with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.ResultsOverall, 26.6% (107/402); [95% CI: 22.1%–31.3%] of the study participants had intestinal parasitic infections. Ascaris lumbricoides,9% (36/402); [95% CI: 6.5%–12.2%], was the most prevalent. Rural residence AOR = 3.5 (95% CI: 1.06–11.75), Large family size AOR = 2.9 (95%CI: 1.93, 5.46), Drinking surface water AOR = 2.6 (95%CI: 1.65, 5.00), in accessibility of latrine utilization AOR = 2.3 (95%CI: 1.34, 5.20), being bare footed AOR = 3.5 (95% CI: 1.23, 9.96), consuming raw vegetables/fruits AOR = 6.8 (95% CI: 4.38,10.63), and untrimmed finger nails AOR = 2.5 (95% CI: 1.80, 6.56) were significant factors associated with intestinal parasitic infections in psychiatric patients.ConclusionThe study revealed the significant prevalence of intestinal parasitic infections in psychiatric patients. Rural residence, larger family size, drinking surface water, unavailability of latrine, not wearing shoes, consuming uncooked vegetables/fruits, and untrimmed fingernails are the significant risk factors favouring the acquisition and spread of intestinal parasitic infections in psychiatric patients.Keywords: Ethiopia, factors, intestinal, parasites, psychiatry

Introduction

Background

Intestinal parasitic infections (IPIs) are one of the main global public health issues in the world. They are a major cause of morbidity and mortality, particularly in underdeveloped nations [1]. An estimated 3.5 billion people are impacted by IPIs each year, the majority of whom are children [2], elderly people with weakened immune systems and inadequate nutrition, and institutionalized people [3].

IPIs are serious public health issues, mainly in developing nations, particularly those in sub‐Saharan Africa. They may be brought on by helminths or protozoa that reside inside the body. They are more prevalent in the tropics and subtropics and are strongly associated with low altitude, a tropical temperature, inadequate personal hygiene, poor environmental sanitation, and limited access to clean water [3].

Diarrhea, weight loss, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, appetite loss, abdominal distension, and iron deficiency anemia are all significantly caused by IPIs [4]. Overall abnormalities in survival, growth, nutritional status, cognitive function, and academic accomplishment may be experienced by infected individuals [5]. Nowadays, the most common intestinal parasites that cause significant morbidity and mortality globally are soil‐transmitted helminths (Ascaris lumbricoides, Trichuris trichiura, and hookworm) and protozoan parasites (Entamoeba histolytica and Giardia intestinalis) [4].

Intestinal parasites can be contracted through skin penetration, fecal‐oral transmission, drinking tainted water, and consuming undercooked meat. Chemoprophylaxis and mass chemotherapy are ineffective in controlling reinfection when the danger is high. Eliminating fecal contamination of food, teaching communities about family and personal hygiene, making sure sewage is disposed of properly, and supplying a sufficient and safe water supply are all necessary for prevention [6].

In Ethiopia, IPIs are common, and the extent of infection varies by location. The second leading cause of illness among outpatients in the country is IPI [7]. High rates of parasitic infections in Ethiopia may result from unsafe and inadequate water supply, poor living conditions, and the lack of proper latrine utilization [8].

The term “psychiatric disorder” describes a wide range of issues that affect a person's feelings, thoughts, or behavior [9]. These conditions can seriously impair a person's capacity to function well in the workplace, in school, or in maintaining positive social interactions. Anxiety disorders, depression, bipolar disorder, eating disorders, personality disorders, post‐traumatic stress disorder, and psychotic disorders (schizophrenia) are among the many diverse kinds of mental disorders [9]. These disorders are medical diseases that can be treated, albeit the best course of action varies from person to person based on the particular ailment, the intensity and extent of the symptoms, and other factors [10].

Since most persons with mental disabilities cannot be instructed to adopt healthy behaviors, it is thought that having a mental disability is another risk factor for IPIs. Psychiatric patients are comparatively more susceptible to parasite infections due to the nature of their diseases and the resulting poor hygiene [11].

IPIs cause diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, anorexia, abdominal discomfort, and other gastrointestinal symptoms, making them the most prevalent public health issues in underdeveloped nations [12, 13]. In addition, chronic intestinal parasite infections can result in conditions like malnutrition, protracted diarrhea, blood loss and anemia, and stunted physical and mental development [13, 14].

They are also the major problems in rehabilitation centers for mentally handicapped individuals [15]. People with disabilities, like the mentally handicapped, are prone to negative aspects of the interaction with non‐handicapped/normal/members of society due to personal health conditions and individual contextual factors [16]. Furthermore, the majority of academic, economic, social, and cultural opportunities are consistently denied to these groups of people [17]. These factors are caused by poor personal cleanliness, a lack of knowledge about personal hygiene, and the effects of parasite infections [18].

Due to certain traits of psychiatric patients, such as restricted mobility, noncompliance with hygiene precautions, and frequent interpersonal interaction, IPs are easily spread among mentally impaired groups [19]. They are thought to be present in 5% to 35.2% of these patients, or possibly more [20, 21].

In addition to their direct detrimental effects on health, chronic IPIs may worsen psychiatric health issues because they impair cognitive development and contribute to the underlying complications. It is necessary to address the transmission, size, and risk factors of IPIs among mental patients in order to enhance their prognoses. In areas where IPs are endemic, like as Ethiopia's Amhara region, where the majority of the population suffers from severe morbidity and death, this must be critical [22].

To lessen the burden of IPIs throughout the nation, Ethiopia has been putting in place a number of preventive and control measures. Among the main tactics employed are, for instance, mass drug administration, enhancing water sanitation and hygiene, and training and deploying health extension workers in rural areas [23].

However, these strategies ignored mentally ill populations, who are more vulnerable, and primarily targeted schoolchildren. Determining the extent of IPIs in the mental health facility is crucial for the execution of any suggested intervention. Furthermore, less is known about the frequency of IPIs and associated characteristics among people with mental disabilities in the nation as a whole and specifically in the study area.

To our understanding, this is the fourth overall study conducted in Ethiopia [24, 25, 26]. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the magnitude of intestinal parasitic infections and associated factors among mentally ill patients attending the psychiatry department at Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (DTCSH), Northwest Ethiopia.

Methods and Materials

Study Design, Period, and Area

An institution‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted from August 2024 to October 2024 at DTCSH, located in Debre Tabor city, which is the capital of South Gondar Zone in the Amhara Region, Northwest Ethiopia. Debre Tabor is found about 100 km southeast of Gondar and 50 km east of Lake Tana, and 653 km northwest of Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia. This town is situated at a latitude of 11.85° North, a longitude of 38.0167° East, and an altitude of 2706 m above sea level. The hospital provides health services to more than 2.5 million people in South Gonder Zone and the nearby districts. The hospital has a psychiatry clinic which serves as a rehabilitation center for mentally ill patients in outpatient and inpatient services for treatment and follow‐up.

Source Population

All Psychiatry patients in the catchment area are the source population.

Study Population

All Psychiatric patients who attend the Psychiatry clinic in DTCSH during the study period.

Sample Size Determination

The sample size was determined using a single‐population proportion formula based on the primary objective of estimating the prevalence of intestinal parasitic infections. It was not specifically calculated for assessing associated factors, which may limit the statistical power for multivariable analysis. Hence, the sample size was using p = 38.9% [24]

Z α = 1.96 confidence interval 95% and 5%, margin of error (d = 0.05)

By adding 10% to account for non‐response rates, a total of 402 study participants were enrolled.

Sampling Technique

Due to the limited number of eligible participants during the study period and the high proportion of follow‐up cases among clinic attendees, a convenience sampling approach was used. All available individuals who met the inclusion criteria were consecutively recruited until the required sample size was achieved.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

All Psychiatric Patients (402) attending DTCSH, the Psychiatry department, and willing to participate in the study, were included.

Exclusion Criteria

Critically ill patients (12), those who have taken anti‐parasitic drugs within the last 2 weeks before data collection (8), and patients attending follow‐up and previously enrolled in the study (248) were excluded.

Study Variables

Dependent Variable

Intestinal Parasitic Infection Status.

Independent Variables

Sociodemographic factors like Age, sex, residence, educational status, occupation, and Hygiene practices like hand washing habit, shoe wearing, finger nail status, toilet availability, feeding habits, and so forth.

Data Collection

The sociodemographic data were collected using a structured questionnaire developed after reviewing relevant literature. The questionnaire was initially prepared in English, translated into the local language (Amharic), and then translated back into English to ensure consistency. A pre‐test was conducted on 5% of the sample at a health center outside the study area. The data were finally collected through face‐to‐face interviews by Psychiatry specialists. For stool sample collection, brief instructions were given by the Psychiatry specialists to the study participants and their guardians on how to provide the desired sample for investigation. The collected stool sample was processed with Saline wet mount, formol‐ether concentration technique, and modified acid‐fast staining techniques. A direct wet mount smear was prepared by mixing a matchstick head size of fresh stool with a drop of 0.85% saline. Smears were examined for helminth eggs or larvae and protozoan trophozoites and cysts with the low‐power (10×) and high‐power (40×) objectives under a light microscope. The details of the processes for formalin ether concentration and acid‐fast staining techniques are described elsewhere [27].

Data Quality Control

Training was given to the data collectors on how to collect, record, and store the data. Before the start of data collection, about 5% (20) of the questionnaires were pre‐tested to guarantee the accuracy, comprehensiveness, and clarity of the information. The completeness of the data was daily verified, and all the reagents used for processing the stool were checked on a weekly basis using positive and negative controls. Standard operating procedures were properly followed during the processing of the samples and laboratory analyses.

Data Analysis

Data were checked for completeness during collection and prior to analysis. All variables included in the study were complete, and no missing data were observed; therefore, all enrolled participants were included in the final analysis.

After data were checked for completeness, they were coded, entered, and analysed with the Statistical Package for Social Sciences software version 23. Bivariable logistic regression analysis was initially performed to assess the association between each independent variable and the outcome variable. Variables with a p‐value < 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were considered candidates for multivariable logistic regression. In the multivariable logistic regression model, potential confounders were controlled by including all candidate variables simultaneously. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated, and statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF), and variables with VIF > 10 were considered indicative of significant multicollinearity. The goodness‐of‐fit of the model was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. Model assumptions for logistic regression were checked prior to analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were used to present the results.

Discussion

The prevalence of IPIs among psychiatric patients has been a growing concern in recent years, with several studies showing a higher prevalence compared to the general population [24, 28, 29, 30].

In the present study, the prevalence of IPIs in Psychiatry patients in the study area is 26.6% (107/402) (95% CI: 22.1%–31.3%). This finding aligns with the results of 26%, 23%, and 28.3% from Iran [31], Italy [32], and southern Ethiopia [26], respectively. The finding in this study is higher than the findings of 13.5% and 12.24% from Ghana [29] and Tanzania [30], respectively. The difference between our study results and these studies might be related to the number of participants enrolled during the study. For example, our study involved a larger sample size (402) compared to the studies from Ghana (111) and Tanzania (233). Since a small sample size may lead to high sampling error, it makes the data less representative of the source population [24].

On the other hand, the prevalence of IPIs in this study is lower than the findings of 57.6%, 38.4%, 43.5%, and 38.9% from Thailand [33], Nigeria [28], Egypt [18], and Bahir Dar city, Ethiopia [24], respectively. This discrepancy may be caused by differences in the diagnostic methods used (Kato Katz was not included in our analysis), variations in the endemicity of the infections in various countries, and changes in the use of WASH practices.

In the present study, the prevalence of soil‐transmitted helminths—A. lumbricoides and hookworm—was higher, with respective rates of 9% (36/402; 95% CI: 6.5%–12.2%) and 5.3% (21/402; 95% CI: 3.4%–7.9%), respectively. The prevalence of A. lumbricoides (9%) aligns with findings of 10.6%, 9.6%, and 8.6% from Italy [32], Southern Ethiopia [26], and Bahir Dar [24], respectively. However, this study's finding for A. lumbricoides is lower than the 15.3% reported in Nigeria [28]. This variation may be related to the differences in endemicity and WASH practices across regions. The prevalence of hookworm, the second most common parasite in this study, is 5.3% (21/402; 95% CI: 3.4%–7.9%), which is consistent with the finding of 4.7% in Tanzania [30]. Nonetheless, it is lower than 8.4%, 14.9%, and 17.1% reported from Nigeria [28], Southern Ethiopia [26], and Bahir Dar [24], respectively. These variations may be due to the same factors mentioned above.

The prevalence of S. mansoni in the current study [1.7% (7/402); 95% CI: 0.9%–3.6%] is consistent with 1.4% reported from Bahir Dar [24]. But this figure is less than 5.2% from Tanzania [30]. This disparity could be related to the variations in schistosomiasis endemicity among the study areas. Although schistosomiasis is mostly endemic in Tanzania [30], the disease's distribution is rather limited in the study's highland area.

The prevalence of the cestode parasite H. nana in this study is 1.5% (6/402) (95% CI: 0.7%–3.2%). This result is lower than the study result from Thailand [33], which was 7.8%. No other studies have so far investigated and reported the findings of this species in psychiatric patients. Among the commonest protozoans E. histolytica/dispar [4.7% (19/402); (95% CI: 3.1%–7.3%)], G. lamblia [3.2% (13/402); (95% CI: 1.9%–5.5%)], and Cryptosporidium species [1.2% (5/402); (95% CI: 0.5%–2.9%)] were identified among psychiatry patients.

The prevalence of E. histolytica/dispar in this study aligns with the findings of 5.5%, 3.6%, and 7.1% from Egypt [18], Ghana [29], and Thailand [33], respectively. However, it is lower than 7.4% and 10.9% reported from Nigeria [28] and Bahir Dar [24], respectively. This variation may be due to the differences in study timing (seasonal variation influences prevalence), climate, and ecology (the study areas of Bahir Dar [24] and Nigeria [28] are warmer than the current study area, which favors the survival and transmission of many parasites). Conversely, this finding is higher than 1.7% and 0.8% from Northern Iran [15] and Tanzania [30], respectively. The prevalence of G. lamblia in this study matches previous reports of 3% from Nigeria [28] and Bahir Dar [24]. However, it is lower than 8%, 8%, and 8.5% from Thailand [33], Northern Iran [15], and Egypt, respectively. These variations may be attributable to the factors mentioned above.

Psychiatric patients, particularly those in institutional settings, represent a vulnerable group for opportunistic infections like cryptosporidiosis. A 1.2% (5/402); [95% CI: 0.5%–2.9%] prevalence of opportunistic intestinal parasite Cryptosporidium species in this study is comparable to the finding of 0.7% in Bahir Dar [24]. This finding was revealed through Modified Staining Technique, where only 0.7% (3/402) were identified with wet mount and Formol ether concentration methods in our study. However, the result in our study is lower than the findings of 23.5% and 3.6% from previous studies in Egypt [18] and Ghana [29], respectively. Although study participants did not experience diarrhea during the study period, it may be underreported or misattributed to medication side effects, diet, or behavioral issues.

Apart from determining the magnitude of IPIs, this study assessed several risk factors favouring the spread of these infections in psychiatric patients. Rural residence, being bare footed, feeding on raw or uncooked vegetables/fruits, large family size, drinking surface water and untrimmed finger nails are the significant risk factors associated with intestinal parasitic infections in psychiatric patients.

According to this study, psychiatric patients living in rural areas were 3.5 times more likely to acquire and develop IPIs than those living in urban settlements. From the total infected study participants, 62.6% (67/107) lived in the rural areas. This is related to the different WASH practices. For example, rural areas often have higher exposure to natural water bodies, soil contact, limited health services, poor sanitation & lack of toilets, unsafe drinking water, and open defecation practices. In our findings, 60.7% (244/402) of patients have regular soil contact history, which may favour infection with soil‐transmitted helminths. Moreover, urban areas are generally linked with better sanitation than rural areas.

In the present study, individuals who did not regularly wear shoes were significantly more likely to have intestinal parasitic infections compared to those who wore shoes consistently. This finding is biologically plausible, as walking barefoot increases direct contact with contaminated soil, facilitating the transmission of soil‐transmitted helminths. This is because Psychiatric patients tend to walk bare‐footed, the majority of them with poor personal care, including poor defecation practices, which are the common risks for soil‐transmitted infections [34, 35]. This result is consistent with previous studies conducted in Ethiopia and other sub‐Saharan African settings, where lack of footwear has been identified as a significant determinant of intestinal parasitic infections [36].

Consuming raw vegetables/fruits is another significant determinant factor that increases the exposure and prevalence of IPIs in psychiatric patients. In this study, participants who consume raw vegetables/fruits have 6.8 times higher odds of developing IPIs than those who do not consume raw vegetables/fruits. This is because Infective stages of many IPs can be collected from raw fruits/vegetables, which results in ingestion of parasites while feeding [37]. Hence, psychiatric patients with a habit of consuming unwashed raw fruits/vegetables are at higher risk of parasitic infections. Similar findings that support our study have been reported from previous studies in psychiatric patients [19, 24].

The present study demonstrated that consumption of surface water was significantly associated with intestinal parasitic infections, which is consistent with findings from several studies in Ethiopia and other sub‐Saharan African settings. A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis conducted in Ethiopia reported that the source of drinking water is a strong determinant of intestinal parasitic infections, with individuals using unsafe water sources having significantly higher odds of infection [38].

Similarly, studies conducted in different parts of Ethiopia, including Northwest Ethiopia, have consistently identified lack of access to safe drinking water as a major risk factor for parasitic infections, largely due to contamination of water sources with human and animal fecal matter. Surface water sources such as rivers and ponds are particularly vulnerable to such contamination, especially in areas with poor sanitation infrastructure [39]. The finding of the present study (64 infections among 183 surface water users) further supports this evidence and highlights the role of unsafe water as a key transmission pathway.

In this study, large family size (> 3 members) was significantly associated with intestinal parasitic infections. This finding is in agreement with previous Ethiopian studies and pooled evidence, which indicate that increased household size is an important risk factor for infection. A meta‐analysis of studies in Ethiopia reported that larger family size significantly increases the odds of intestinal parasitic infections [38]. The association may be explained by increased person‐to‐person transmission within households, as well as challenges in maintaining adequate hygiene and sanitation in larger families. Overcrowding can facilitate the spread of parasites through shared facilities, limited sanitation resources, and increased environmental contamination. In the current study, a substantial proportion of participants belonged to larger households (332 out of 402), of whom 96 were infected, supporting the observed association. This aligns with evidence from other African settings, where household crowding and socioeconomic constraints are key contributors to parasitic disease transmission. Additionally, larger family size may indirectly reflect lower socioeconomic status, which is often linked to limited access to clean water, sanitation, and hygiene facilities—factors known to increase the risk of intestinal parasitic infections.

Finally, psychiatric patients with untrimmed finger nails are considered to have 2.5 times higher odds of being infected with IPIs than those with trimmed and clean finger nails. Unclean fingernails/having dirty materials in fingernails result in psychiatric patients being more susceptible to intestinal parasites. This has been supported by previous studies [19, 40, 41]. This can be more described in that untrimmed finger nails contain dirty materials which may lead to direct feco‐oral transmission of intestinal parasites.

Potential Sources of Bias

As to our understanding, this study has several potential sources of bias that should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Selection bias: The use of convenience sampling and recruitment of participants from a single health facility may have introduced selection bias. Individuals who were available and met the inclusion criteria during the study period may differ systematically from those who were not included, potentially limiting representativeness.

Information bias: Some exposure variables including handwashing practice, shoe‐wearing habits, feeding habits, latrine utilization, and soil contact were based on self‐reported data. This may be subject to recall bias or social desirability bias, potentially leading to over‐ or under‐reporting of behaviors.

Misclassification: There is a possibility of misclassification of exposure variables due to the use of categorized self‐reported measures. Although operational definitions were applied to standardize classification, some degree of non‐differential misclassification may have occurred.

To minimize these biases, data were collected using a structured and pre‐tested questionnaire, laboratory processes were done following standard operating procedures, and data collectors were trained to ensure consistency. Nevertheless, residual bias cannot be completely ruled out, and the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Limitations

One stool sample was used, and the Kato‐Katz technique was not employed, which may result in an underestimation of prevalence. Additionally, molecular methods were not employed, so speciation, especially for protozoans, could not be reported separately.

In addition, the use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, as participants were recruited based on availability rather than random selection. In this study, the sampling approach was influenced by the relatively small number of eligible cases and the predominance of follow‐up patients, which limited the pool of recruitable participants. As a result, the study population may not fully represent the broader target population, and the findings should be interpreted with caution. This limitation may affect the external validity of the study, and generalization to other settings or populations should be done carefully. Future studies employing probability‐based sampling techniques would strengthen representativeness.

Moreover, Although the sample size was determined based on the primary objective of estimating prevalence, it may not have been sufficiently powered for multivariable analysis. In particular, the number of outcome events relative to the number of predictor variables included in the regression model may have been limited, potentially affecting the stability and precision of the estimates. This limitation may reduce the ability to detect statistically significant associations, especially for variables with low prevalence, and may increase the risk of overfitting. Therefore, the results of the multivariable analysis should be interpreted with caution. Future studies with larger sample sizes are recommended to ensure adequate statistical power for such analyses.

Conclusions

The study revealed the significant prevalence of IPIs in psychiatric patients. Rural residence, being bare footed, feeding on raw or uncooked vegetables/fruits, large family size, drinking surface water and untrimmed finger nails were identified as the significant risk factors favouring the acquisition and spread of IPIs in psychiatric patients. Therefore, screening for IPIs regardless of clinical presentations and periodic deworming could be vital for reducing the burden of these infections among psychiatric patients.
