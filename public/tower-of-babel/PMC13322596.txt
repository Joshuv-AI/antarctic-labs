Comparison of efficacy of quadratus lumborum block and intrathecal morphine in elective caesarean section for postoperative analgesia – A randomised controlled trial
Authors: D Jeenu, Anand B. Medidi, Rahul Pillai, Ekta Rai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and Aims:As caesarean section (C-section) rates continue to rise, effective planning and management of postoperative pain become crucial. Poor control can delay early mobilisation and breastfeeding, and it also raises the risk of complications like thromboembolic events, postoperative ileus, and chronic pain development. This study aimed to compare the efficacy of postoperative analgesia between intrathecal morphine (ITM) and quadratus lumborum block (QLB) in the elective C-section.Methods:This prospective, double-blinded, randomised controlled trial included 60 women who underwent elective C-sections. The participants were randomly assigned to receive either ITM [0.1 ml (100 μg) of morphine] or bilateral QLB with 40 ml 0.375% ropivacaine. Both the ITM and QLB groups had received a subarachnoid block consisting of 2 ml of 0.5% hyperbaric bupivacaine with 0.1 ml of ITM or saline for a total of 2.1 ml. Postoperatively, pain score using the numerical rating scale (NRS), the need for rescue analgesia, and patient satisfaction score (0–10) were monitored for 48 h.Results:Out of the 60 parturients enroled, 59 were included in the final analysis (ITM n = 30, QLB n = 29). Postoperative pain scores assessed using the NRS were significantly lower in the ITM group than in the QLB group at 4, 8, 12, and 24 h after surgery (P = 0.042). The time to first request for rescue analgesia was longer in the ITM group than in the QLB group (19.8 ± 10.3 h vs 9.2 ± 4.3 h; P < 0.001). Patients in the ITM group also reported higher patient satisfaction scores. No clinically significant adverse effects were observed in either group during the study period.Conclusion:Group ITM was associated with lower postoperative pain scores, longer-lasting analgesia, reduced need for rescue analgesics, and higher patient satisfaction compared with QLB after elective C-section, with no clinically significant adverse effects.

INTRODUCTION

Caesarean section (C-section) is the most common surgical procedure in young females, and its incidence has significantly increased over the past two decades.[12] Its rate in India has exceeded the World Health Organization (WHO) threshold by 15%, raising concerns about postoperative pain.[3] Pain after a C-section is a traumatic experience that can interfere with newborn care, ambulation, and breastfeeding. Inadequate pain control may lead to longer hospital stays, poor wound healing, thromboembolism, and chronic pain. It is also recognised as a risk factor for postpartum depression. Neuraxial anaesthesia, particularly subarachnoid block, is considered the gold standard for C-section; however, it does not effectively manage postoperative pain, necessitating multimodal pain relief strategies. Postoperative pain can be controlled by approaches such as adding opioids to spinal anaesthesia or using regional blocks for the parturients. These include non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), wound infiltration, intrathecal opioids, epidural, and truncal blocks. Recently, regional blocks, especially the quadratus lumborum block (QLB), have gained prominence for their effectiveness in providing pain relief.[45]

This study aimed to compare the effectiveness of intrathecal morphine (ITM) and QLB in managing postoperative pain after an elective C-section. The primary outcome was to evaluate pain scores at rest and during movement over 48 h using the numerical rating scale (NRS). Secondary outcomes included measuring total rescue analgesia administered, time to the first request for additional pain relief, and any adverse effects associated with both techniques.

METHODS

This randomised, double-blinded trial was conducted from March 2022 to October 2022 for 8 months at a tertiary care hospital after obtaining approval from the institutional ethics committee (IRB No.:14041) on 30 June 2021 and was registered in the Clinical Trials Registry-India (CTRI/2022/03/040852). This research complies with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (2013), Good Clinical Practice guidelines and follows the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) guidelines. Informed consent was obtained from all participants after an information sheet was provided, and the study was explained in the ward a day before surgery.

This study enroled 60 parturients. The inclusion criteria were females over the age of 18 years, with a gestational age of more than 37 weeks, who had a singleton pregnancy, were classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status (ASA-PS) classification II, and were scheduled for elective C-sections. The exclusion criteria were morbid obesity [defined as body mass index (BMI) >35 kg/m2], clinical contraindications to subarachnoid block, allergies to any medications involved in the protocol, drug addiction, patient refusal, and patients who required supplemental anaesthesia and analgesia intraoperatively.

Randomisation was performed using a computer-generated random sequence. Allocation concealment was ensured by sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, opened only immediately before the procedure by an anaesthesiologist not involved in postoperative assessment. To preserve blinding, the anaesthesiologist performing the intervention was different from the assessor collecting postoperative outcomes, and both participants and outcome assessors were unaware of group allocation throughout the study period.

In the operation theatre, standard ASA monitors (electrocardiography, non-invasive blood pressure and pulse oximeter) were attached. Intravenous access was established, and 4 mg of ondansetron was given intravenously according to the institutional protocol. Subarachnoid block was performed at the L3-4 and L4-5 interspace in the sitting position using a 25-G Whitacre needle. Each parturient received 2 mL of 0.5% hyperbaric bupivacaine intrathecally, and co-loading was accomplished with 500 mL of Ringer’s lactate solution. The intraoperative course was uneventful for all patients.

The participants were randomly divided into two separate groups:

Group ITM: Received ITM 100 μg (0.1 mL) with hyperbaric bupivacaine. Bilateral placebo injections of normal saline were administered at the quadratus lumborum plane postoperatively.

Group QLB: Received intrathecal saline (0.1 mL) along with hyperbaric bupivacaine, followed by bilateral ultrasound-guided transmuscular QLB type 3 with 20 mL of 0.375% ropivacaine on each side (total 40 mL).

Under strict aseptic precautions, QLB or placebo injections were administered bilaterally. The patient was placed in the lateral decubitus position, and a curvilinear ultrasound probe (Fujifilm Sonosite Edge) was placed over the flank between the costal margin and the iliac crest. The transversus abdominis, internal oblique, and quadratus lumborum muscles were identified, and the probe was then placed posteriorly to identify the transverse process. Under ultrasound guidance with an in-plane approach, a block needle was inserted and advanced until its tip was placed between the quadratus lumborum and psoas major muscles. After saline hydrodissection, 20 mL of 0.375% ropivacaine or saline (placebo), according to group allocation, was injected bilaterally with visual confirmation of spread. The same procedure was repeated on the other side. To avoid unblinding, the sensory and procedural aspects of bilateral needle insertions were similar for all study participants. Postoperative patients were transported to the post-anaesthesia care unit (PACU).

Once the discharge criteria were met, the parturients were transferred to the ward. In the ward, parturients were evaluated for pain using the NRS, both at rest and during movement for 48 h. They were also monitored for any adverse effects of opioids and complications associated with both techniques at regular intervals for 48 h after surgery. According to the institution protocol, 4 mg of ondansetron every 8 h and 1 g of intravenous paracetamol every 6 h for 48 h were administered to all parturients. Rescue analgesia was administered if the NRS exceeded 5 or at the patient’s request. Intravenous ketorolac 30 mg was used as a first-line rescue analgesia for breakthrough pain, if pain persisted, and no additional systemic opioids were administered postoperatively in our study. At the end of the study period, patient satisfaction scores were obtained.

The sample size was determined based on the main outcome measure of postoperative NRS pain score. It was considered that a minimum clinically significant difference of 2 points would be appropriate, as suggested by previous studies of postoperative analgesia. With an assumed standard deviation of 2.5, based on previous caesarean analgesia studies,[6] an alpha level of 0.05, and a power of 80%, a sample size of 52 was needed. To account for dropouts, a sample size of 60 was taken.

International Business Machines Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, version 21 (IBM SPSS V.21) was used for all statistical analyses. The data were collected, compiled, and analysed using Microsoft Excel and EpiData. The Shapiro–Wilk or Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of the data distribution. Mean +/standard deviation (SD) was used to represent continuous variables. For normally distributed variables, such as age, BMI, and total rescue analgesia consumption, an independent-samples t-test and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the differences in means were also calculated. Non-normally distributed variables, such as NRS pain scores, were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, represented with results reported as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical data, like adverse effects or satisfaction levels (presented as proportions), were analysed using the Chi-square test when appropriate. A P value < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

RESULTS

Out of 60 parturients, one participant was excluded after randomisation due to conversion to emergency C-section. As a result, the final analysis included 59 participants, with 30 assigned to the ITM group and 29 to the QLB group [Figure 1]. The baseline demographic characteristics were similar between the two groups, showing no statistically significant differences [Table 1]. Pain scores at rest assessed in the PACU (0 h) were similar in both groups, with a median NRS score of 0. At 4, 8, 12, and 24 h postoperatively, the QLB group had higher median NRS scores at rest than the ITM group. This difference was statistically significant at all assessed time points (P = 0.042) [Figure 2]. While both groups reported minimal pain in the immediate postoperative period, median NRS scores during movement were significantly higher in the QLB group at 4, 8, 12, and 24 h compared to the ITM group (P < 0.05 at all-time points) [Figure 3]. The ITM group had a significantly longer mean time to request rescue analgesia than the QLB group (19.8 ± 10.3 h vs. 9.2 ± 4.3 h; P < 0.001). The total ketorolac consumption during the first 48 h postoperatively was higher in the QLB group, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.054) [Table 2]. Five parturients in the ITM group and three in the QLB group did not require any rescue analgesia during the study period.

DISCUSSION

Childbirth is generally regarded as a joyful event; however, inadequate pain control after C-section can make the experience traumatic and adversely affect early mobilisation, breastfeeding, and maternal–neonatal bonding. Demelash et al.[7] reported that 85.5% of women experienced moderate-to-severe postoperative pain within the first 24 h after caesarean delivery, with NRS scores indicating moderate-to-severe pain in 75.5% at 2 h, rising to 80% at 12 h and decreasing to 58.6% at 24 h. These findings highlight the need for effective and sustained postoperative analgesia in this population.

In our study, both ITM and QLB provided satisfactory immediate postoperative analgesia in the PACU, with NRS scores of 0 at rest. However, ITM resulted in significantly lower pain scores at 4, 8, 12, and 24 h and a reduced requirement for rescue analgesia over 48 h compared with QLB. The difference in NRS score between the two groups was greatest at 12 h, likely due to increased mobility and surgical inflammation, as well as significant regression of QLB.

This contrasts with the findings of Dost et al.,[8] who reported no significant difference in NRS scores between ITM and QLB. A plausible explanation for this discrepancy is the difference in QLB technique and dosing; Dost et al. used QLB type 2 with a larger volume of 0.25% bupivacaine (25 mL per side), whereas we employed QLB type 3 with 0.375% ropivacaine (20 mL per side). Variations in the block approach, local anaesthetic type, concentration, and volume could account for differences in analgesia duration.

Salama ER et al.[6] demonstrated that both ITM and QLB improved postoperative pain control compared with a control group and reported that QLB provided effective analgesia for up to 36 h with fewer side effects than ITM. Although we also found QLB to be effective, the duration of clinically meaningful analgesia was shorter in our cohort, with a mean time to first rescue analgesia of 9 h in the QLB group versus 19 h in the ITM group. The prolonged duration of analgesia reported in some studies may reflect differences in block technique, local anaesthetic volume and concentration, and variable fascial spread. However, a 36-h duration after a single-shot QLB should be interpreted cautiously as this appears to be an outlier rather than the expected response. Our findings are more consistent with those of a study,[9] wherein it was observed that QLB alone, without concurrent ITM, conferred inferior analgesia compared with ITM and that its effect lasted approximately 10 h rather than 24–48 h. Similarly, Tamura et al.[10] reported that the sensory block from QLB did not exceed 8 h. Some authors[11] found that QLB reduced opioid consumption and pain mainly within the first 24 h after caesarean delivery, with the clearest benefit at 12 h but no meaningful advantage beyond 24–48 h. Mieszkowski et al.[12] also showed early postoperative analgesic benefit from QLB, but the effect was short-lived rather than sustained. Collectively, these data suggest that, when used as a sole regional technique in caesarean delivery, the duration of QLB analgesia may be limited to the early postoperative period, defined in this study as the first 12 h following caesarean delivery.

Anatomical variation in local anaesthetic spread may further explain the heterogeneity in QLB efficacy among studies. Some researchers,[13] using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), showed that local anaesthetic injected for QLB could spread from T5–L1 and potentially reach the paravertebral and epidural spaces. In contrast, some other researchers[14] did not observe extension into the paravertebral space in a cadaveric study. In the present study, QLB type 3 was performed with ultrasound guidance, and correct injection was confirmed by real-time imaging and clinical comfort in the PACU. However, formal mapping of sensory levels or monitoring of regression over 48 h was not done, which limits the ability to correlate clinical analgesia with cephalad spread of local anaesthetic from this study.

With respect to opioid consumption and adverse effects, Salama ER et al.[6] reported that patients in the ITM group required higher cumulative intravenous morphine doses than those receiving QLB. In contrast, the postoperative regimen in the current study consisted of non-opioid rescue analgesics (ketorolac and paracetamol) administered to all parturients, and additional systemic opioid consumption was not recorded. Because paracetamol was routinely given to all parturients, the need for rescue ketorolac reflects inadequate breakthrough analgesia; the higher ketorolac requirement in the QLB group, therefore, supports a shorter analgesic duration compared with ITM. Notably, we observed no opioid-related adverse effects following intrathecal administration of 100 μg morphine. This aligns with the findings of Palleschi et al.,[15] who reported that spinal morphine doses of 100–450 μg were not associated with respiratory depression. Our institutional protocol mandates prophylactic intravenous ondansetron administration every 8th hourly for the first 24 h, which may also have contributed to the favourable side-effect profile.

The authors of a scoping review[16] found that 5-hydroxytryptamine type 3 (5-HT3) antagonists such as ondansetron can be used to reduce or prevent pruritus associated with neuraxial morphine, particularly when administered pre-emptively. This mechanism may explain the absence of pruritus in our ITM group. Furthermore, Koning et al.[17] found in a meta-analysis that doses of ITM below 100 μg were not associated with significant nausea, vomiting, or pruritus. Although some studies have linked hydrophilic opioids to higher rates of pruritus and other side effects, our results support the safety of low-dose ITM when combined with prophylactic antiemetic therapy. In our study, patient satisfaction was higher in the ITM group than in the QLB group. This was likely to be multifactorial and may reflect both, the longer duration and greater consistency of analgesia with ITM.

The present study has several important limitations that merit consideration. Firstly, routine prophylactic administration of ondansetron may have masked the true incidence of opioid-related adverse effects, limiting comparisons with studies that did not use systematic 5-HT3 antagonist prophylaxis. Secondly, QLB is an operator-dependent, ultrasound-guided technique; variations in expertise and subtle differences in needle placement could have affected block quality and reproducibility. Third, we did not perform a serial, formal assessment of dermatomal sensory levels or regression, which restricts the mechanistic interpretation of our findings. Finally, the relatively small sample size from a single-centre study limits the generalisability of our results and may reduce the power to detect uncommon adverse events. Future adequately powered multicentric trials incorporating standardised QLB techniques, detailed sensory mapping, and uniform antiemetic protocols are needed to better define the role of QLB—alone or as an adjunct to ITM—within multimodal analgesic strategies for caesarean delivery.

CONCLUSION

Both ITM and QLB provided good pain relief after elective C-section in the early postoperative period, with ITM resulting in lower pain scores at rest and during movement, longer-lasting analgesic effect, less need for rescue medication, and higher patient satisfaction in the first 24 h after surgery. No significant adverse effects were observed with either method. The QLB could be a viable alternative for patients who cannot receive neuraxial opioids in the early postoperative period.
