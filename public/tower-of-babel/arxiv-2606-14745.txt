Compositional Small-Gain and Small-Phase Stability Analysis
Anton Ponomarev

Abstract
We adapt the small-gain and small-phase stability analysis to systems composed of MIMO LTI subsystems via an iteration of the series, parallel, and feedback interconnections. Based on a certain set of parameters (including the phase sector and the Crawford number) of the subsystems, we bound the same set of parameters of each consecutive interconnection. This is called “composition of bounds . ” Composing the gains of subsystems in the same manner enables the small-gain-or-phase analysis of fairly complex systems. The method is illustrated with two examples that are not tractable by the classical small-phase analysis, and one other example that nevertheless benefits from the compositional approach.

I-A

Does Small-Phase Theorem Apply to Complex Systems?

Since its inception, the small-gain technique has been successfully extended to complex networks
[
25
,
6
]
, but similar extension of the small-phase theorem meets with a difficulty; let us explain it at an intuitive level. In the linear case, the gain is a matrix norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert
. The small-gain theorem relies on the inclusion of the spectrum
\sigma(\cdot)
of the open-loop transfer matrix
\mathbf{A}
in the norm-disk:

\sigma(\mathbf{A})\subseteq\big\{z\in\mathbb{C}\colon\lvert z\rvert\leq\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert\big\}.

(1)

The small-phase theorem similarly uses a spectral inclusion but this time in a sector of the complex plane covered by the
angular field of values

\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(\mathbf{A})

[
12
, Definition 1.1.2]
:

\sigma(\mathbf{A})\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(\mathbf{A}).

(2)

If there are two matrices in the loop
(\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathbf{A}_{2})
, the small-gain theorem employs the submultiplicativity of the matrix norm to bound the open-loop gain as

\lVert\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathbf{A}_{2}\rVert\leq\lVert\mathbf{A}_{1}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{A}_{2}\rVert.

(3)

The angular field of values, however, is not submultiplicative in general:

\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathbf{A}_{2})\not\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(\mathbf{A}_{1})\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(\mathbf{A}_{2}).

(4)

The small-phase theorem instead resorts to another spectral inclusion
[
12
, Theorem 1.7.8]

\sigma(\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathbf{A}_{2})\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(\mathbf{A}_{1})\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(\mathbf{A}_{2})

(5)

(assuming that at least one matrix is sectorial). With three or more subsystems in the loop, the small-gain theorem easily generalizes by
iteration
of (
3
). The sectorial inclusion (
5
), on the other hand, does not allow iteration. This is the obstacle that halts the sequence (
2
), (
5
), …

I-C

Proposed Approach

In the present study, we take a different route: instead of looking for a way to continue the sequence (
2

5

3

Why should such an analogue be attainable? The intuitive grounds are seen from two trivial observations:

1.

The inclusion in (
4

2.

What the examples of non-submultiplicativity (
4

[

We capture the notions of the numerical range being “not too spread out” and “far enough from the origin” in the concept of the
Crawford defect
(Definition
6

Under the restrictions of Theorem

The approach is extended beyond a single loop via the

I-D

Some Comments

It appears that our results are closely related to the segmental phase idea mentioned above. These relations are discussed in Section
VI-C
where we suggest a potential way to combine the sectorial and segmental approaches. This may reduce the conservatism as illustrated in the example of Section

Another example (Section

However, with the compositional approach we find a larger stability domain: it turns out that the compositional
small
-gain and
small

2

2

Although a high-gain system may raise questions of noise sensitivity, there exist established methods of high-gain stabilization

This fact may seem surprising but has an intuitive explanation: we deal with the notion of the numerical range being “far enough from the origin” which essentially means that “the gain is high” in a strong sense: it implies that the norm is large but is a stronger property than that. VIII
Conclusions

We proposed a way to apply the small-gain-or-phase stability analysis to a class of systems built by a hierarchy of series, parallel, and feedback interconnections. The analysis is based on the composition of the ring-sectorial bounds of numerical ranges. Although the composition formulas can be complicated and conservative, in simple examples they yield analytically manageable and intuitively convincing results.

A potentially attractive feature of the compositional analysis, facilitated by its iterative nature, is the opportunity to recognize the
source
of instability – an internal loop that violates the stability conditions. This may prove useful in applications concerned with controller design and system diagnostics.

Possible future advances are discussed in Section
VI-C
(combining the sectorial and segmental approaches) and in Remark
12
(incorporating Drury’s deviation bound or its approximation).

Appendix A
A Fundamental Lemma

In the proofs we often adopt a geometric point of view on the numerical range. Note that every point
z\in\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{A})
contains some information about the action of
\mathbf{A}
on
\mathbf{x}
. Specifically,
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}
can be decomposed into the scaled component
z\mathbf{x}
and a component orthogonal to
\mathbf{x}
. The orthogonal component is discarded from
\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{A})
, but in the sectorial case it can be bounded proportionally to
\lvert z\rvert
using the Crawford defect
\delta(\mathbf{A})
– this is the essence of the following lemma.

Lemma 3

Let matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be sectorial. For an arbitrary
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, define
z=\dfrac{\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}}\in\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{A})
and the orthogonal projection matrix
\mathbf{P}=\mathrm{I}-\dfrac{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{*}}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
. Then

\frac{\lVert\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert}\leq\delta(\mathbf{A})\lvert z\rvert.

(92)

Proof:

From the orthogonal decomposition

\frac{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert}=\frac{z\mathbf{x}}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert}+\frac{\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert}

(93)

we find
\left(\dfrac{\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert}\right)^{2}=\lvert z\rvert^{2}+\left(\dfrac{\lVert\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert}\right)^{2}
which together with

\left(\frac{\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert}\right)^{2}=\frac{\left(\dfrac{\lvert\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rvert}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}}\right)^{2}}{\dfrac{\lvert\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rvert}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}}\dfrac{\lvert\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rvert}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}}}\leq\frac{\lvert z\rvert^{2}}{c(\mathbf{A})c(\mathbf{A}^{-1})}

(94)

yields the required estimate.
∎

The idea of bounding the
\mathbf{x}

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}
is not new. We particularly note
