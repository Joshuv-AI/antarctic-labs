Data-driven adaptive integral variable structure control method for AGV trajectory tracking system based on BP neural network observer
Authors: Jianliang Xu, Jin Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the problem of insufficient control accuracy and robustness of automated guided vehicles (AGVs) under nonlinear time-varying conditions and external disturbances, a data-driven adaptive discrete-time integral sliding mode control (ADISMC) method is proposed. This method integrates three core strategies: first, full-form dynamic linearization (FFDL) is employed to transform the unknown nonlinear system into a compact-form dynamic model relying solely on I/O data, thereby circumventing the dependence on mechanistic modeling in principle; second, a BP neural network observer (BPNNO) is constructed to perform online estimation and feedforward compensation of the lumped disturbance, which substantially reduces the switching gain while ensuring robustness, thus mitigating chattering at its source; third, an adaptive reaching law with the tracking error as the independent variable is designed, achieving fast convergence when far from the sliding surface and automatic gain contraction when approaching the sliding surface, thereby balancing response speed and control smoothness. Theoretical analysis rigorously proves the boundedness of the pseudo gradient and the finite-time convergence of the sliding mode motion. AGV kinematic simulations demonstrate that the proposed method significantly outperforms PID and standard MFAC in overall performance: the maximum heading tracking error under disturbance-free conditions is merely 0.3488 rad, and the mean square error under 0.3 rad white noise disturbance is only 14% of that of the PID scheme, validating the superior tracking accuracy and disturbance rejection robustness of the proposed method.

Introduction

With the deep integration of artificial intelligence, sensor technologies, and automatic control theory, Automated Guided Vehicles (AGVs), as a core carrier of autonomous driving technology, have been widely applied in various domains such as autonomous commuting, warehouse logistics handling, agricultural plant protection operations, and environmental monitoring and inspection. They have become key components of intelligent transportation and intelligent manufacturing systems1,2. As a core technology for autonomous AGV operation, trajectory tracking control directly determines whether the vehicle can accurately follow a predefined path, thereby ensuring driving safety and operational efficiency, and serves as a prerequisite for achieving high-level autonomous driving3. However, conventional AGV control often relies on accurate dynamic or kinematic models. In practical engineering, the motion state of AGVs exhibits strongly nonlinear, highly coupled, and time-varying characteristics. On the one hand, vehicle parameters such as mass, moment of inertia, and cornering stiffness of tires are susceptible to variations in load, road adhesion coefficient, and aerodynamic drag, making it very difficult to establish a unified and accurate mathematical model4. On the other hand, the nonlinear mechanical behaviors under aggressive steering at high speeds or complex road conditions are often neglected by conventional linearized models, leading to degraded control accuracy.

Among conventional control methods, PID control is widely used due to its simple structure and ease of implementation. However, it relies on manual parameter tuning and struggles to balance dynamic response and steady-state accuracy under complex operating conditions. Model-based methods, such as Model Predictive Control (MPC)5 and Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR)6, while capable of incorporating system constraints, are highly dependent on accurate mathematical models. When discrepancies exist between the model and the actual system, control performance degrades significantly. Geometry-based methods, such as Pure Pursuit7 and Stanley8, though computationally efficient, suffer from considerably increased tracking errors under high-speed or sharp-curve conditions due to the limitations of simplified kinematic assumptions. Therefore, an ideal approach to addressing the trajectory tracking problem of AGVs is to develop a control method that leverages data-driven techniques to avoid constructing complex mechanistic models, while simultaneously achieving favorable dynamic response and steady-state accuracy under complex operating conditions.

In recent years, intelligent control methods have become a research hotspot. Reinforcement Learning (RL)9 enables agents to learn optimal policies autonomously through trial and error, demonstrating strong adaptive capabilities. However, it faces bottlenecks in terms of large training sample requirements and high risks in real-world deployment. End-to-end control driven by Deep Learning (DL)10, while simplifying the perception and control pipeline, suffers from poor interpretability, making it difficult to guarantee safety under extreme operating conditions. In the domains of Distributed Optimization11 and Game-Theoretic Control (GTC)12,13, theoretical advances have been achieved in multi-agent coordination and pursuit-evasion games through sampling-data-based uncertainty compensation strategies and hierarchical Hamilton-Jacobi-Isaacs (HJI) equation solving frameworks. Nevertheless, the aforementioned methods rely heavily on accurate dynamic models and ideal communication assumptions, failing to account for the strongly nonlinear unmodeled dynamics caused by ground friction, load transfer, and tire cornering stiffness in actual AGV operation. Moreover, their constraints—such as extremely small sampling periods or deviations from game-theoretic optimal solutions—are in significant conflict with the engineering realities of AGVs, which feature limited onboard computational resources and high real-time requirements.

To overcome the over-reliance on accurate mechanistic models, data-driven methods, particularly Model-Free Adaptive Control (MFAC), offer an important technical pathway. Such methods construct a dynamically linearized virtual model using only input-output data without requiring prior knowledge of the system. The feasibility of MFAC in real-vehicle trajectory tracking has been preliminarily validated on the unmanned ground vehicle (UGV) test platform at Tsinghua University14. Furthermore, derivative methods such as Model-Free Predictive Control (MFPC)15 and Model-Free Iterative Control (MFIC)16 have further expanded the application boundaries by integrating predictive optimization and online learning mechanisms. However, existing model-free methods generally suffer from inherent limitations, including limited dynamic response speed and insufficient robustness against strong disturbances. When an AGV faces high-speed turning or sudden perturbations, relying solely on data-driven parameter adaptation often fails to simultaneously achieve fast convergence and steady-state accuracy17.

Sliding Mode Control (SMC), as a nonlinear robust control method, forces the system state to evolve along a predefined sliding surface, enabling finite-time convergence and providing inherent disturbance rejection against parameter perturbations and external disturbances, thereby serving as an effective approach for uncertain systems. For instance, Ref18. designed a controller based on conventional SMC for AGV trajectory tracking in warehouse scenarios, demonstrating the robust tracking capability of this method under load variations and sudden changes in path curvature. Ref19. focused on AGV trajectory tracking on complex outdoor road conditions and proposed an SMC strategy integrated with path preview, which effectively enhanced tracking accuracy and stability on large-curvature paths. However, conventional SMC imposes high requirements on the modeling accuracy of the plant, relying on an accurate mathematical model for the design of the sliding surface and control law. Model mismatch can easily lead to degradation of control performance. Moreover, the inherent chattering problem of SMC exacerbates actuator wear and further affects the smoothness of control.

To overcome the aforementioned deficiencies, researchers have improved conventional SMC from three perspectives: sliding surface design, reaching law construction, and disturbance compensation. Regarding sliding surface design, integral terminal sliding mode has attracted extensive attention because it eliminates steady-state errors while avoiding singularity and guarantees finite-time convergence. Ref20. designed a super-twisting integral terminal sliding mode control strategy for permanent magnet synchronous motor speed control, where the integral action reduces chattering and improves response speed. In terms of reaching law and synchronization control, Ref21. proposed fast integral terminal synchronous sliding mode control for a two-degree-of-freedom robot. By constructing the sliding surface and reaching law based on a norm-normalized sign function and a piecewise exponential function, the method achieves fast synchronous convergence of the position errors of dual motors, and a finite-time state observer is designed to estimate unmeasurable states. With respect to disturbance compensation and observer integration, to address parameter perturbations, nonlinearities, and external disturbances in flexible-joint robots, Ref22. employed the singular perturbation method to decouple the high-order system into fast and slow subsystems and proposed an adaptive integral sliding mode controller based on a dual-state observer. This controller achieves high-precision tracking without requiring velocity or acceleration measurements, and the adaptive law eliminates the need for prior knowledge of the disturbance upper bound. Ref23. further introduced a fixed-time extended state observer (FTESO) and fixed-time integral sliding mode control (FTISMC), ensuring that tracking errors under different initial conditions converge within a fixed time. The above studies fully demonstrate that integrating integral sliding mode with adaptive mechanisms and observer techniques has become an effective approach to enhance system robustness and tracking accuracy.

However, the aforementioned improved sliding mode control methods remain essentially within the model-based control framework. Whether it is the construction of integral sliding surfaces, the subsystem decoupling via the singular perturbation method, or the design of finite-time state observers, all rely on a relatively accurate mathematical model of the plant. For AGV trajectory tracking, the operating conditions are highly variable, the tire–ground interaction is complex, and load transfer leads to center-of-mass shifts. These strongly nonlinear coupling characteristics make it difficult to obtain an accurate mechanistic model. Consequently, the above model-based methods face adaptability bottlenecks in practical applications. Therefore, exploring a trajectory tracking method that does not rely on an accurate mathematical model while deeply integrating the advantages of observer compensation and sliding mode robust control has become a critical issue that urgently needs to be addressed.

Therefore, control strategies combining model-free methods with SMC have become a research hotspot in the control field, as they compensate for the limitations of each individual approach through complementary advantages. Existing studies have mainly achieved this integration through two approaches: first, using model-free control as an adaptive mechanism for SMC, optimizing SMC parameters through online data learning to enhance adaptability to model uncertainties; second, embedding SMC into a model-free framework, leveraging the robustness of SMC to compensate for the insufficient disturbance rejection of model-free methods and thereby improving system stability. In the field of motor control, the research group of Zhao Kaihui at Hunan University of Technology proposed a model-free super-twisting fast integral terminal sliding mode control strategy. By constructing a ultra-local model and an improved disturbance observer, this strategy achieves high-precision speed control of PMSMs and effectively suppresses parameter perturbations and external disturbances24. In high-speed train control, researchers have combined MFAC with SMC to design an adaptive SMC law, improving the stability and disturbance rejection capability of train operation25. Notably, observer techniques, as an effective means of handling system unmodeled dynamics and external disturbances, play a key role in the aforementioned integrated frameworks. For example, in vehicle active suspension control, researchers have proposed SMC or backstepping control strategies based on adaptive fuzzy state observers26, neural state observers27, and NDOs28, respectively. Specifically, Ref26. employs a fuzzy logic system to approximate parameter uncertainties and designs a series-parallel state observer to estimate unmeasurable states while eliminating prediction errors. Ref27. constructs a neural state observer to simultaneously estimate unmeasurable states and compensate for the effects of sensor failures. Ref28. combines an NDO with adaptive SMC, significantly attenuating external road disturbances and parameter perturbations through feedforward compensation. The above studies fully validate the effectiveness of observers in enhancing the disturbance rejection capability and estimation accuracy of control systems. However, most existing observer designs rely on prior model knowledge of the plant. Owing to the highly variable operating conditions, strong nonlinear couplings, and difficulty in obtaining accurate mechanistic models of AGV systems, model-based state observers face adaptability bottlenecks in practical applications. Therefore, exploring a trajectory tracking method that does not rely on an accurate mechanistic model and deeply integrates data-driven observation with sliding mode robust control holds urgent theoretical demand and engineering value for improving the control quality of AGVs under complex operating conditions.

Based on the above discussion, AGV trajectory tracking control algorithms have evolved from conventional control toward data-driven and intelligent control paradigms. The integration of model-free control and SMC offers an effective approach to addressing control challenges under complex operating conditions. However, existing studies still suffer from three deficiencies that urgently need to be addressed. First, although conventional MFAC eliminates the reliance on accurate mechanistic models, its control law lacks an explicit compensation mechanism for unknown disturbances. Relying solely on parameter adaptation makes it difficult to maintain high-precision tracking under strong disturbances. Moreover, current combinations of data-driven methods and SMC mostly remain at a shallow integration level where data assist parameter tuning, failing to combine the equivalent data model constructed by full-form dynamic linearization (FFDL) with the invariance property of SMC. In this paper, FFDL is employed to generate a virtual dynamic linearized data model of the AGV online, providing a model-free design basis for the integral sliding mode controller. Simultaneously, a back-propagation neural network observer (BPNNO) is introduced to estimate and feedforward-compensate the lumped disturbance in real time. Consequently, the sliding mode gain does not have to be increased to suppress disturbances, thereby fundamentally mitigating chattering while preserving robustness. Second, AGV systems exhibit significant strong nonlinearity and dynamic coupling characteristics, such as changes in kinematic constraints due to steering, center-of-mass shifts caused by load transfer, and longitudinal–lateral coupling effects induced by tire cornering stiffness. However, existing studies often simplify the AGV as a low-degree-of-freedom kinematic model or treat it as a generic nonlinear system, lacking customized designs that target the above features. In this paper, an adaptive reaching law based on state deviation is constructed, where the gain is dynamically adjusted according to the distance between the system state and the sliding surface. When far from the sliding surface, a nonlinear function assigns a high gain to ensure fast convergence; when approaching the surface, the gain automatically shrinks to suppress chattering. This enables active adaptation to the transient nonlinearities and coupling dynamics of the AGV. Third, the multi-objective trade-off among chattering suppression, convergence speed, and steady-state accuracy has not yet been effectively resolved. Existing methods often improve one indicator at the expense of another. The proposed architecture reduces the demand for high switching gain through disturbance feedforward compensation to suppress chattering, eliminates steady-state errors via the integral sliding surface to enhance accuracy, and guarantees finite-time convergence by means of the adaptive reaching law. This provides a feasible approach for multi-objective optimization of AGV trajectory tracking under complex operating conditions. Therefore, designing a model-free sliding mode trajectory tracking control algorithm that combines strong adaptability, high robustness, and favorable dynamic performance holds significant theoretical value and engineering significance.

This paper proposes an adaptive integral sliding mode control (ADISMC) method based on FFDL. The method constructs a local data model of the AGV using dynamic linearization techniques, thereby avoiding complex mechanistic modeling. The BPNNO is introduced to observe external disturbances and provide real-time compensation. An integral sliding surface is designed to achieve zero steady-state error convergence, and an adaptive reaching law is proposed to balance convergence speed and chattering suppression. AGV kinematic analysis

In the study of AGV dynamics, to focus on its control characteristics and eliminate the influence of non-critical variables, the research is centered on the longitudinal motion control of the vehicle. To this end, the following assumptions are proposed29:

Assumption 1

The AGV is regarded as an integral rigid structure, and the entire vehicle can be treated as a single particle for dynamic analysis.

Assumption 2

The motion along the Z-axis is neglected, and the vehicle is assumed to move on a two-dimensional plane by default.

Assumption 3

The nonlinear effects generated by the vehicle engine are ignored.

Assumption 4

The adhesion between the tires and the ground is assumed to be ideal, and no sideslip occurs.

Remark 1

In the process of system modeling and analysis, Assumptions 1–4 are introduced to appropriately delineate the core dynamic behavior of the system under study and to simplify model complexity. Assumption 1 treats the AGV as a rigid single particle, allowing the effects of elastic deformation of the vehicle body structure and load distribution variations on the overall motion to be neglected. This facilitates the description of translational and rotational behavior using a lumped-mass model and lays the foundation for adopting a simplified kinematic framework. For instance, Ref8. designed a Stanley–PID controller for a four-wheel-steering AGV, explicitly employing a single-track kinematic model and deriving the relationship between the heading angular rate and the front wheel steering angle based on the pure-rolling, no-slip condition. Assumption 2 restricts the motion to a two-dimensional plane and neglects vertical dynamics, thereby focusing the problem on trajectory tracking and posture control in the horizontal plane. This eliminates the coupling effects of additional degrees of freedom such as suspension travel, pitch, and roll, enabling the controller design to concentrate on heading angle deviation. As an example, Ref7. considers only horizontal-plane motion in its SLAM and navigation scheme for a warehouse AGV, demonstrating that neglecting vertical dynamics is a common and reasonable simplification in both engineering practice and research. Assumption 3 disregards the internal nonlinear dynamic characteristics of the drive motor or engine, allowing the control design to focus on the kinematic and low-frequency dynamic interactions between the vehicle and the ground. This avoids interference from the high-frequency dynamics of the drive system in control law design and also provides justification for directly using the steering angle as the control input. References14,15, from the perspectives of data-driven and model-free control, achieved path tracking for autonomous vehicles without requiring an accurate model of the motor or transmission system, indirectly indicating that the high-frequency dynamics of the drive stage can be considered irrelevant. This supports the reasonableness of excluding them from the control model. Assumption 4 assumes that the tire–road adhesion is always in an ideal state with no lateral slip, which guarantees the strict validity of the nonholonomic constraint. This ensures a deterministic and predictable mapping between the front wheel steering angle and the vehicle heading angle derived from the kinematic model, thereby providing a clear and reliable plant boundary for the design of the trajectory tracking controller. For example, References18,19 designed trajectory tracking controllers for nonholonomic mobile AGVs based on sliding mode control, with their underlying models precisely founded on the no-slip nonholonomic constraint. The above four assumptions have been widely adopted for typical operating conditions of AGVs running on flat ground at low speeds.

Referring to the Earth-fixed coordinate system XOY shown in Fig. 1, the AGV kinematic model is considered an ideal rigid body, and its motion characteristics are analyzed within this global coordinate system. The vehicle kinematic model focuses on revealing the inherent correlations among the vehicle’s attitude, velocity properties, and rotation angle. To simplify the analysis process, this paper selects the front-wheel steering mechanism as the research focus, and takes the center point (X, Y) of the rear axle as the reference point of the vehicle body coordinate system.

Design of FFDL-BPNNO-ADISMC scheme

Full-format dynamic linearization processing

The Compact Form Dynamic Linearization (CFDL) method only describes the relationship between the system output and the control input at the current moment, while the Partial Form Dynamic Linearization (PFDL) method only characterizes the relationship between the system output and the control inputs at multiple moments. On the one hand, considering that the output of the AGV trajectory tracking system is correlated with the input and output information at multiple time instants. On the other hand, the FFDL method can incorporate the effects of both inputs and outputs within a fixed time window on the system output, thereby improving the accuracy of trajectory tracking30. Based on the above considerations, this section adopts the FFDL method for the linearization of the AGV system.

Remark 3

Assumptions 5 and 6 provide the necessary mathematical foundation for the MFAC and data-driven strategies adopted in this paper. For the AGV steering system, with front wheel angle as the input and vehicle heading angle as the output, the continuity of the kinematic relationship and its smooth variation characteristics within the normal operating range allow Assumption 5 to be physically well satisfied. For a low-speed, high-inertia AGV, the vehicle heading angle does not undergo unbounded abrupt changes due to small variations in the front wheel steering angle; its physical motion characteristics thus conform to the generalized Lipschitz condition stated in Assumption 6. References14,15 demonstrated the validity, stability, and applicability of the aforementioned assumptions in the context of vehicle heading control during their validation processes for an autonomous vehicle and a variable-configuration unmanned ground vehicle, respectively.

Parameter performance discussion

In the proposed FFDL-BPNNO-ADISMC control framework, the selection of parameters directly affects the dynamic response quality and robustness of the system. A guideline for parameter selection is provided below in accordance with the functionality of each module.

For the step-size factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta$$\end{document} and the weighting factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu$$\end{document} in the FFDL estimation algorithm, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta$$\end{document}determines the sensitivity of the parameter matrix estimate to current data variations. It is generally recommended to take a value between 0 and 2 to balance convergence speed and estimation stability. An excessively large \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta$$\end{document} may cause estimation oscillation, while an overly small \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta$$\end{document} leads to tracking lag.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu$$\end{document}, as a penalty coefficient, is mainly used to avoid division by zero and to suppress singular fluctuations in parameter estimation. Typically, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu$$\end{document} is chosen as a small positive constant, with a typical range of 0.1–10. The specific value can be adjusted appropriately according to the magnitude of the input variation vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\| {\Delta {\mathbf{M}}} \right\|$$\end{document}.

The BPNNO adopts a four-layer feedforward structure with single input, two hidden layers, and single output. Its node numbers and layer numbers are preset based on the complexity of the disturbance estimation task, requiring no additional user-defined parameters, thereby effectively reducing the tuning burden in engineering deployment.

The design parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c_1}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c_2}$$\end{document} of the adaptive integral sliding surface correspond to the weights of the angle tracking error \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${e_\theta }$$\end{document} and its integral \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${e_\vartheta }(t)$$\end{document}, respectively, jointly determining the convergence dynamics of the sliding motion. Specifically, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c_1}$$\end{document} acts directly on the instantaneous tracking error; the larger its value, the stronger the corrective action on the current deviation and the faster the response. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c_2}$$\end{document} acts on the integral of the error, primarily used to eliminate steady-state error and enhance robustness against persistent disturbances and unmodeled dynamics. Typically, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c_1}$$\end{document} is chosen as a positive value to ensure that the sliding surface has characteristic roots with negative real parts. An initial recommended range is\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c_1} \in [1,10]$$\end{document}. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c_2}$$\end{document} is also positive; its magnitude affects the accumulation rate of the integral action. An excessively large \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c_2}$$\end{document} may increase overshoot or cause response oscillation, while an overly small \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c_2}$$\end{document} leads to slow convergence of the steady-state error. A suggested tuning range is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c_2} \in [0.01,2]$$\end{document}. The ratio \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c_1}/{c_2}$$\end{document} essentially determines the equivalent time constant of the sliding motion and can be adjusted according to the desired trade-off between convergence speed and overshoot suppression.

The parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${l_1}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${l_2}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${l_3}$$\end{document} in the adaptive reaching law control the decay rate of the sliding variable, the strength of the switching gain, and the compensation intensity for the disturbance estimate variation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta \psi$$\end{document}, respectively. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${l_1}$$\end{document}should satisfy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${l_1} \in (0,1)$$\end{document} to ensure the contraction property of the sliding motion. A value closer to 1 yields a smoother reaching process, while a value closer to 0 results in faster convergence but may introduce high-frequency chattering. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${l_2}$$\end{document} is the coefficient of the sign function, directly associated with the system’s ability to overcome the residual lumped disturbance. It is recommended to increase \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${l_2}$$\end{document} gradually from a small value until the tracking error meets the accuracy requirement. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${l_3}$$\end{document} is adjusted according to the magnitude of the disturbance estimate variation output by the BPNNO, aiming to achieve effective compensation for fast-varying disturbances without significantly amplifying noise.

In the adaptive sliding mode parameter\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\zeta (t)=\lambda +(1 - \lambda ){(|s(t)|+1)^{ - \tau }}$$\end{document}, the adaptive factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document} adjusts the shape of the gain decay function in the reaching law, with a range of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda \in (0,1)$$\end{document}. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document} is large, the gain decays slowly as the sliding variable decreases, which is beneficial for suppressing steady-state chattering but may prolong convergence time. Conversely, when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document} is small, the gain decays more rapidly, accelerating convergence but requiring caution to avoid a reduction in disturbance rejection capability due to premature gain shrinkage. The physical meaning and tuning principle are as follows: when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} is large, the function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${(|s(t)|+1)^{ - \tau }}$$\end{document} decays faster as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|s(t)|$$\end{document} decreases, meaning that once the system state approaches the sliding surface, the gain shrinks more aggressively, which is more favorable for suppressing steady-state chattering. However, if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} is too large, the gain may shrink to its lower bound before the sliding variable has sufficiently converged, thereby weakening the ability to resist residual disturbances and causing the steady-state error to increase again. Conversely, when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} is small, the gain decay curve is gentler, and the controller maintains a relatively ample switching gain near the sliding surface to overcome disturbances, but the chattering suppression effect is correspondingly reduced. In engineering tuning, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document} can be fixed first and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau =1$$\end{document} can be used as an initial value for experimentation. If the steady-state error shows an increasing trend, this indicates that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} is too large and should be reduced accordingly. Conclusion and future directions

This paper addresses the trajectory tracking control problem of AGVs under nonlinear time-varying conditions and external disturbances, and proposes a data-driven adaptive discrete-time integral sliding mode control method. The main conclusions are summarized below:A model-free control architecture integrating FFDL data modeling, BPNNO disturbance estimation, and an adaptive reaching law is constructed. This architecture circumvents the dependence on mechanistic modeling in principle and mitigates sliding mode chattering at its source. Theoretical analysis rigorously proves the boundedness of the pseudo gradient and the finite-time convergence of the sliding mode dynamics.Simulation results demonstrate that: in the nonlinear numerical example, the MSE of FFDL-BPNNO-ADISMC (0.9632) is 42.9% lower than that of PID; in disturbance-free AGV tracking, the maximum heading error is merely 0.3488 rad, whereas all compared schemes exceed 0.63 rad; under 0.3 rad disturbance, the MSE is only 14% of that of the PID scheme, validating the superior tracking accuracy and disturbance rejection robustness of the proposed method.The proposed method achieves high-precision, low-chattering trajectory tracking for AGVs under complex operating conditions in a purely data-driven manner, providing a theoretically guaranteed feasible solution for motion control that does not rely on an accurate mechanistic model.

Although the proposed FFDL-BPNNO-ADISMC method demonstrates excellent performance in theoretical analysis and simulation validation, several limitations and application constraints remain. First, in terms of computational complexity, the method involves online estimation of the pseudo-partial derivative in FFDL, forward propagation and weight update of the BPNNO, and real-time computation of the adaptive reaching law. Compared with conventional PID or MFAC, it imposes higher demands on the computational capability of onboard embedded units. On low-cost AGV platforms with extremely short sampling periods or limited processor clock frequencies, real-time bottlenecks may occur. Second, at the implementation level, the estimation accuracy of the BPNNO depends on the quality and coverage of the training data. If the disturbance characteristics in actual operating conditions deviate significantly from the distribution of offline training samples, the compensation performance of the observer will degrade, thereby affecting the chattering suppression effect of the sliding mode controller. Furthermore, when the AGV encounters extreme transient conditions—such as a sudden drop in tire adhesion due to slippery roads or emergency obstacle avoidance maneuvers—the data-driven model has an inherent lag in responding to abrupt system dynamic changes. Consequently, the tracking error may exhibit a brief increase in overshoot. This physical limitation is difficult to completely circumvent given the local linearization assumption of model-free methods based on historical data.

To address the above limitations, future research will be carried out along the following directions. First, lightweight neural network structures and event-triggered mechanisms will be investigated to reduce the online computational burden and improve the deployability of the algorithm on resource-constrained edge devices. Second, model predictive control and online parameter tuning strategies will be integrated to construct a hierarchical rolling optimization framework for dynamic adjustment of control gains, thereby enhancing rapid adaptability to extreme transient conditions. Third, fault awareness and adaptive feedback mechanisms will be introduced, and more real-time operational data will be exploited to continuously improve the generalization performance of the observer. These efforts aim to further validate and enhance the robustness and engineering practicality of the proposed method in complex operating environments characterized by strong uncertainties and multiple disturbances. Moreover, the current study is limited to numerical simulations. In the future, an AGV hardware test platform will be built to comprehensively evaluate the tracking accuracy, real-time performance, and disturbance rejection capability of the proposed FFDL-BPNNO-ADISMC method under actual onboard operating conditions, thereby compensating for the simplifications of complex physical effects inherent in simulation environments.
