Convergence analysis and proof of acceleration for NGMRES applied to the Picard iteration for Navier-Stokes equations
Yunhui He, Leo G Rebholz

Abstract
We consider nonlinear GMRES (NGMRES) as an acceleration technique for the Navier–Stokes Picard iteration, a direction that has not previously been explored. We identify the optimal norm for the least squares optimization problem arising in the NGMRES algorithm, and establish a convergence analysis for NGMRES with general depth that proves NGMRES scales the Picard Lipschitz/contraction constant by the gain of the optimization problem. To our knowledge, this is the first convergence proof for NGMRES that identifies the mechanism responsible for convergence acceleration. Numerical experiments demonstrate that the convergence estimates are remarkably sharp. In addition, NGMRES greatly improves the performance of the Picard iteration, even in cases where the unaccelerated iteration diverges.



Keywords
: NGMRES, Convergence acceleration, Navier-Stokes, Picard iteration, optimization norm choice

Introduction

Nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs) play a central role in many real world applications, yet they are often difficult to solve analytically. This motivates the development of efficient iterative nonlinear solvers and the corresponding convergence theory. A variety of nonlinear acceleration methods have been proposed in the literature, including multisecant methods
[
14
]
, nonlinear conjugate gradient methods
[
22
]
, Anderson acceleration (AA)
[
1
]
, and nonlinear GMRES (NGMRES). This work focuses on NGMRES, which for a given fixed point operator
q(u)
used to solve
g(u)=0
, is usually written in the following form (a formal definition is given in Section 2) for depth
m
and iteration
k+1
:

u_{k+1}=q(u_{k})+\sum_{i=0}^{m_{k}=\min\{k,m\}}\beta_{i}^{k+1}\left(q(u_{k})-u_{k-i}\right),

where
\beta^{k+1}=\big(\beta_{0}^{k+1},\beta_{1}^{k+1},\cdots,\beta_{m_{k}}^{k+1}\big)
is obtained by solving the least-squares optimization problem

\min_{\beta^{k+1}}\|g(q(u_{k}))+\sum_{i=0}^{m_{k}=\min\{k,m\}}\beta_{i}^{k+1}\left(g(q(u_{k}))-g(u_{k-i})\right)\|^{2}.

(1.1)

The NGMRES idea was first introduced by Washio and Oosterlee in 1997, who proposed a Krylov subspace based acceleration technique analogous to GMRES for nonlinear multigrid on the finest level
[
46
]
for difficult nonlinear elliptic scalar problems and for systems of nonlinear equations. In 2000, Oosterlee and Washio extended this approach to accelerate nonlinear multigrid on coarse levels
[
33
]
for recirculating incompressible flow problems. Since then, NGMRES type accelerations have been applied in several areas, including tensor decomposition
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
, CUTEst problems
[
39
]
, and image restoration
[
6
,
40
]
. The comparison in
[
10
]
shows that AA and NGMRES are often competitive, and it is an open question as to what kinds of problems exist where one is
significantly better than the other. Several new variants of NGMRES have been proposed recently. For example,
[
25
]
introduces a periodic scheme in which the NGMRES step is applied only every
p
fixed-point iterations. A generalized alternating NGMRES method is developed in
[
27
]
for PDE constrained optimization governed by transport equations, where comparisons with Anderson type methods indicate that alternating NGMRES performs favorably. These studies highlight the effectiveness of NGMRES in accelerating nonlinear fixed point iterations.

To date, only limited progress has been made on the convergence theory for NGMRES. In
[
19
]
, the authors show that, under certain conditions, full NGMRES (i.e., incorporating all previous iterates) applied to the Richardson iteration is equivalent to classical GMRES. In
[
26
]
, an analogous equivalence is established for preconditioned Richardson and preconditioned GMRES. For nonlinear problems, the work
[
24
]
initiated a convergence analysis of NGMRES applied to a simple contractive fixed point iteration. To the best of our knowledge, until this paper, there has been no convergence analysis for NGMRES which characterizes its mechanism for convergence acceleration.

Herein, we consider NGMRES applied to the Picard iteration
for the Navier-Stokes equations (NSE), which are given by: on a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},d=2,3
,

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\nu\Delta u+u\cdot\nabla u+\nabla p&={f}\quad\text{in}~\Omega,\\
\nabla\cdot{u}&=0\quad\text{in}~\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.2)

together with appropriate boundary conditions. Here
u
and
p
represent the unknown velocity and pressure, respectively,
\nu>0
is the kinematic viscosity which is inversely proportional to the Reynolds number
Re\sim\frac{1}{\nu}
, and
{f}
is a given forcing. The Picard iteration for the NSE is given by

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\nu\Delta u_{k+1}+u_{k}\cdot\nabla u_{k+1}+\nabla p_{k+1}&={f},\\
\nabla\cdot{u}_{k+1}&=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.3)

This iteration is globally stable and globally linearly convergent in the
H^{1}_{0}
norm with rate
\kappa:=M\nu^{-2}\|f\|_{H^{-1}}
(where
M
is a constant dependent on the domain size defined in Section 2) provided
\kappa<1

[
18
,
29
]
. However, as
\kappa
increases (i.e.
Re
increases), the convergence of Picard slows and eventually fails
[
37
,
38
]
for
\kappa
large enough. Unfortunately, such failures occur for
\kappa
well within the range of physically relevant problems
[
31
]
, and thus there is significant interest in improving its convergence properties. To improve convergence of Picard iteration, many methods have been studied over the years
[
11
,
15
,
38
,
16
,
44
,
17
,
45
,
46
,
33
]
, with varying degrees of success.

The motivation for our choosing to study NGMRES-Picard for the NSE comes from the AA theory development, which began for simplicity purposes by first considering AA-Picard for the NSE in 2019 in
[
38
]
. In that paper, the gain of the AA optimization problem was identified as the mechanism for AA convergence acceleration. The following year, this problem-specific theory was extended by the same authors to general contractive fixed point iterations
[
13
]
and then a year later with sharper bounds to general fixed point iterations which need not be contractive
[
34
]
. Indeed, the authors of this paper have already begun study of generalizing the NGMRES results of this paper.

We now proceed formally for the moment in order to give a high level description of our main results, suppressing boundary conditions and moving the problem setting to the divergence-free velocity space (this will all be made mathematically precise in Sections 2 and 3). Take
V\subset H^{1}(\Omega)
to be the divergence-free velocity space and define
q:V\rightarrow V
as the solution operator of the (weak) Picard iteration and define the nonlinear residual operator
g:V\rightarrow V^{\prime}
by

g(v)=-\nu\Delta v+v\cdot\nabla v-f,

where
V^{\prime}
is the dual space of
V
, equality is meant in a weak sense, and the pressure and divergence-free constraint drop since
g:V\rightarrow V^{\prime}
. Then for
m\geq k
, NGMRES in this setting can be written (in the equivalent constrained optimization formulation) as

u_{k+1}=\alpha^{k+1}_{k+1}q(u_{k})+\sum_{j=k-m}^{k}\alpha^{k+1}_{i}u_{i},

where
\big(\alpha^{k+1}_{k+1},\ \alpha^{k+1}_{k},\cdots,\ \alpha^{k+1}_{k-m}\big)
are obtained by solving the least-squares optimization problem

\min_{\sum_{i=k-m}^{k+1}\alpha_{i}^{k+1}=1}\|\alpha^{k+1}_{k+1}g(q(u_{k}))+\sum_{j=k-m}^{k}\alpha^{k+1}_{i}g(u_{i})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2}.

(1.4)

Note that
V^{\prime}
is used because
g
maps into
V^{\prime}
. The use of this norm is critical for our convergence analysis, and moreover our computations show better performance when using
V^{\prime}
as the norm compared to
\ell^{2}
. We show herein that the sequence
\{g(q(u_{k}))\}_{k}
using Picard iterates converges with rate
\kappa
if
\kappa<1
;
\|g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})\|_{V^{\prime}}\leq\kappa\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}
(i.e. the same rate Picard converges in
H^{1}_{0}
norm).

Our main analytical results can now be stated as follows. Define
\theta_{k+1}
and
\gamma_{k+1}
by

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\|\alpha^{k+1}_{k+1}g(q(u_{k}))+\alpha^{k+1}_{k}g(u_{k})+...+\alpha^{k+1}_{k-m}g(u_{k-m})\|_{V^{\prime}}}{\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}},

\displaystyle\gamma_{k+1}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\|\alpha^{k+1}_{k+1}g(q(u_{k}))+\alpha^{k+1}_{k}g(u_{k})+...+\alpha^{k+1}_{k-m}g(u_{k-m})\|_{V^{\prime}}}{\|g(q(u_{k}))\|_{V^{\prime}}}.

Note that both
\theta_{k+1}\leq 1
and
\gamma_{k+1}\leq 1
, and are only one in the special cases that the optimization problem returns
(0,1,0,...,0)
or
(1,0,...,0)
, respectively. The only difference between
\theta_{k+1}
and
\gamma_{k+1}
is the denominator in their definitions. We prove the following convergence bounds:

\displaystyle\|g(u_{k+1})\|_{V^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{k+1}\kappa\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2}+\|g(u_{k-1})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2}+...+\|g(u_{k-m})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2}\right),

(1.5)

\displaystyle\|g(u_{k+1})\|_{V^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq\theta_{k+1}\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2}+\|g(u_{k-1})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2}+...+\|g(u_{k-m})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2}\right).

(1.6)

The result (
1.5
) shows that
\gamma_{k+1}
scales the linear convergence rate of Picard from
\kappa
to
\gamma_{k+1}\kappa
. The definition
\gamma_{k+1}
shows the ‘gain of the optimization problem’ from the ratio of what the optimization gives to what Picard with no acceleration would give (or equivalently, the optimization problem return
(1,0,...,0)
. Hence up to higher order terms, (
1.5
) shows that NGMRES accelerates convergence precisely through the gain of the optimization problem.

The result (
1.6
) is also quite interesting, and there is no AA-Picard analogue to it. It shows first that, up to higher order terms, NGMRES reduces the nonlinear residual in the
V^{\prime}
norm. The mechanism underlying NGMRES is that the least-square problem in NGMRES is derived by expanding
g(u_{k+1})
around
u_{k}
and truncating after the first-order term, where a finite-difference approximation is used for the first derivative; see
[
46
]
. Further, since
\kappa
is typically a bound and not known precisely,
\theta_{k+1}
gives a more precise estimate of the Lipschitz/contraction number at each iteration. Indeed, our numerical tests show that once the nonlinear residual gets smaller,
\theta_{k+1}
is a very sharp estimate of the convergence rate at each iteration.

To our knowledge, the estimates (
1.6
)-(
1.5
) are the first convergence results for NGMRES that show
how
NGMRES accelerates an iteration. The result (
1.5
) is similar at a high level to the proof of acceleration for AA in that it is a single step theory where the gain of the optimization problem drives the acceleration
[
36
,
38
]
. Another similarity to the AA theory of
[
38
]
is that the constrained form of the optimization problem is natural for the analysis. However, the analysis and results herein are fundamentally different from the AA theory for several reasons: the residual expansion takes a different form since the extrapolation of NGMRES is different than AA, the nonlinear residual is used in NGMRES instead of the Picard fixed point residual, and the optimization norm here is
V^{\prime}
while for AA is
V
. We note that in neither case is
\ell^{2}
the best choice, and we show both numerically and analytically it that using
\ell^{2}
can be a poor choice for NGMRES-Picard, especially in 3D.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we give notation and mathematical preliminaries, and in Section 3 we proof the NGMRES convergence result. In section 4, we give results for several numerical tests that illustrate the theory, show the convergence bound is quite sharp, compare using
V^{\prime}
and
\ell^{2}
as the least squares optimization norms, and mesh independence of convergence for our acceleration method. Conclusions and future directions are in Section 5.

Notation and Preliminaries

Let
\Omega
be an open connected set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d
=2 or 3). We denote by the
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product and norm as
(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\|\cdot\|
, respectively. For other norms, we will label them with subscripts.

We define the natural pressure and velocity function spaces for the NSE by

\displaystyle Q:=\{q\in{L}^{2}(\Omega):\int_{\Omega}q\ dx=0\}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle X:=\{v\in H^{1}\left(\Omega\right):v=0~~\text{on}~\partial\Omega\},

along with the divergence-free velocity space

V:=\{v\in X:(\nabla\cdot v,q)=0\ \forall q\in Q\}.

The dual space of
X
is
H^{-1}(\Omega)
with norm denoted
\|\cdot\|_{-1}
, and the dual space of
V
is denoted by
V^{\prime}
with norm
\|\cdot\|_{V^{\prime}}
. We will use the notation
(\cdot,\cdot)
to also represent dual pairings of
V
and
X
with their dual spaces.

We recall the following well known bounds and properties of the NSE nonlinearity form
[
31
]
:

i)
b(v,w,w)=0
for all
v\in V
and
w\in X
,

ii) there exists a constant
M
dependent only on the size of
\Omega
satisfying for all
v,w,\chi\in X

\displaystyle(v\cdot\nabla w,\chi)

\displaystyle\leq M\|\nabla v\|\|\nabla w\|\|\nabla\chi\|,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\|v\cdot\nabla w\|_{V^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq M\|\nabla v\|\|\nabla w\|.

(2.2)

The analysis of this paper is done primarily using the space
V
and its dual space. Results would be identical if we did the analysis using the discretely divergence-free subspace
V_{h}
created from stable velocity-pressure spaces
(X_{h},Q_{h})\subset(X,Q)
. The only difference would be that a
skew-symmetric form of the nonlinear term would need to be used if the finite elements were not pointwise divergence free, e.g.

b^{*}(v,w,z)=(v\cdot\nabla w,z)+\frac{1}{2}((\nabla\cdot v)w,z).

One could also use other energy preserving formulations such as rotational and EMAC forms
[
7
]
.

2.1
Navier-Stokes preliminaries

We consider the NSE on a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}(d=2,3)
given by

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\nu\Delta u+u\cdot\nabla u+\nabla p&={f}\quad\text{in}~\Omega,\\
\nabla\cdot{u}&=0\quad\text{in}~\Omega,\\
{u}&=0\quad\text{on}~~\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(2.3)

where
u
and
p
are the unknown fluid velocity and pressure, respectively,
\nu>0
is the kinematic viscosity, and
{f}
is a given external forcing (such as gravity or bouyancy). The Reynolds number
Re\sim\frac{1}{\nu}
is a physical constant that describes the complexity of a flow: higher
Re
is generally associated with more complex physics and non-unique solutions. For simplicity of analysis, we consider the system (
2.3
) equipped with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, but our results are extendable to nonhomogeneous mixed Dirichlet/Neumann boundary conditions as well, as well as to solving the time dependent NSE at a fixed time step in a time stepping scheme.

The weak form of (
2.3
) is given by: Find
u\in V
satisfying

\nu(\nabla u,\nabla v)+(u\cdot\nabla u,v)=(f,v)\ \ \forall v\in V.

(2.4)

It is well known that (
2.4
) admits weak solutions for any
\nu>0
and
f\in H^{-1}(\Omega)

[
31
]
, and any such solution is bounded by

\|\nabla u\|\leq\nu^{-1}\|f\|_{-1}.

(2.5)

Under a small data condition
\kappa:=M\nu^{-2}\|f\|_{H^{-1}}<1
, solutions are unique
[
18
,
31
,
42
]
. However, for
\kappa
large enough, (
2.4
) can have multiple solutions
[
31
]
.

2.2
Picard and nonlinear residual preliminaries

The Picard iteration for solving (
2.4
) is defined by: given
u_{k}\in V
, find
\tilde{u}_{k+1}\in V
satisfying

\nu(\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},\nabla v)+(u_{k}\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},v)=(f,v)\ \forall v\in V.

(2.6)

It is shown in
[
38
]
that the solution operator associated with (
2.6
) is well-defined and is uniformly bounded:

\|\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1}\|\leq\nu^{-1}\|f\|_{-1}.

(2.7)

Thus, we can define the fixed point Picard iteration as
u_{k+1}=q(u_{k})
, where
q:V\rightarrow V
is the solution operator to (
2.6
).

The fixed point residual (i.e.,
\tilde{u}_{k+1}-u_{k}=q(u_{k})-u_{k}
) satisfies
[
18
]

\|\nabla(q(u_{k})-u_{k})\|\leq\kappa\|\nabla(q(u_{k-1})-u_{k-1})\|.

(2.8)

Note that if
\kappa<1
, (
2.8
) indicates that the Picard iteration is contractive and will convergence linearly with rate (at least)
\kappa
to the unique weak NSE solution. However, the Picard iteration for the NSE will diverge for
\kappa
sufficiently large
[
36
]
. Our convergence results for NGMRES-Picard when
m=0
assume only that
\kappa>0
, while for
m\geq 1
we assume Picard is contractive, i.e.
\kappa<1
. The difference comes from the additional higher order terms when
m\geq 1
. In our numerical tests we use
\kappa\gg 1
, i.e. far above the range where Picard is contractive.

In addition to considering Picard residual convergence, we will also consider convergence using the NSE nonlinear residual, which is defined by
g:V\rightarrow V^{\prime}
which satisfies for all
v\in V
and
\chi\in V
,

(g(v),\chi)=\nu(\nabla v,\nabla\chi)+(v\cdot\nabla v,\chi)-(f,\chi).

The
V^{\prime}
norm of
g(v)
is thus defined by

\|g(v)\|_{V^{\prime}}=\max_{0\neq\chi\in V}\frac{(g(v),\chi)}{\|\nabla\chi\|}.

(2.9)

Due to
v\in V
, the
v\cdot\nabla v
term is not in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, and thus
V^{\prime}
is the appropriate norm to measure
g(v)
.

We now prove a preliminary lemma relating the Picard residual and nonlinear residual, as well as establishing a bound on the nonlinear residual for Picard. We denote the Picard residual by

w_{k+1}=\tilde{u}_{k+1}-u_{k}=q(u_{k})-u_{k},

and the difference between two successive iterates by

e_{k}=u_{k}-u_{k-1}.

Lemma 2.1
.

The following bounds hold regarding the Picard residual and the NSE nonlinear residual:

\displaystyle\|\nabla w_{k+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\nu^{-1}\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}},

(2.10)

\displaystyle\|g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})\|_{V^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}.

(2.11)

Proof.

Subtract
\nu(\nabla u_{k},\nabla v)+(u_{k}\cdot\nabla u_{k},v)
from both sides of (
2.6
) to obtain for all
v\in V
,

\nu(\nabla w_{k+1},\nabla v)+(u_{k}\cdot\nabla w_{k+1},v)=(f,v)+\nu(\nabla u_{k},\nabla v)-(u_{k}\cdot\nabla u_{k},v)=(-g(u_{k}),v).

Now choosing
v=w_{k+1}
vanishes the nonlinear term and gives us

\nu\|\nabla w_{k+1}\|^{2}=-(g(u_{k}),w_{k+1})\leq\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}\|\nabla w_{k+1}\|,

where the last step uses the definition of
V^{\prime}
norm, (
2.9
), and which after simplifying completes the proof of the first bound of the lemma.

For the second bound, expand
g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})
to obtain for all
v\in V
,

\displaystyle(g(\tilde{u}_{k+1}),v)

\displaystyle=\nu(\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},\nabla v)+(u_{k}\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},v)-(f,v)

\displaystyle=(w_{k+1}\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},v)

with the last step thanks to (
2.6
). Taking
V^{\prime}
norms of both sides and bounding the nonlinear term we get

\displaystyle\|g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})\|_{V^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq M\|\nabla w_{k+1}\|\|\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq M\nu^{-1}\|f\|_{-1}\|\nabla w_{k+1}\|.

Now combining with (
2.10
), we obtain

\|g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})\|_{V^{\prime}}\leq\kappa\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}},

which completes the proof of (
2.11
).
∎

2.3
NGMRES preliminaries

Before presenting NGMRES as an accelerate technique for the Picard iteration applied to the NSE, we first outline the NGMRES framework for accelerating a fixed-point iteration
q(u)
used to solve
g(u)=0
.

Algorithm 1

(Nonlinear GMRES with depth
m
)
.

1:

Input:
x_{0}
and
m\geq 0
with
m\in\mathbb{N}

2:

for

k=0,1,\cdots
until convergence
do

3:

Compute

u_{k+1}=q(u_{k})+\sum_{i=0}^{m_{k}=\min\{k,m\}}\beta_{i}^{k+1}\left(q(u_{k})-u_{k-i}\right),

(2.12)

where
\beta^{k+1}=\big(\beta_{0}^{k+1},\beta_{1}^{k+1},\cdots,\beta_{m_{k}}^{k+1}\big)
is obtained by solving the least-squares problem

\min_{\beta^{k+1}}\|g(q(u_{k}))+\sum_{i=0}^{m_{k}=\min\{k,m\}}\beta_{i}^{k+1}\left(g(q(u_{k}))-g(u_{k-i})\right)\|^{2}.

(2.13)

4:

end

for

5:

Output:
u_{k+1}

Note that in Algorithm
1
, we formulate the least‑squares problem (
2.13
) in a non-constrained form, and in practice the
\ell^{2}
norm is generally used. However, one may use the constrained formulation, as we did in Section
3
, for the sake of simplifying the convergence analysis for NSE.
m
is a given integer. If
m_{k}=k
for all

, we refer to the corresponding method as full NGMRES. When Algorithm
1
is applied to solve the linear system

with
q(u_{k})=u_{k}+(b-Au_{k})
,
[
19
]
proves that if GMRES and full NGMRES use the same initial guess and the GMRES residual norm decreases strictly at every iteration, then the iterates generated by GMRES and full NGMRES coincide. Similar results have been extended to preconditioned fixed-point iterations, establishing an equivalence between full NGMRES and preconditioned GMRES under analogous assumptions; see
[
26
]
.

For the least-squares problem (
2.13
), to the best of our knowledge, only the
\ell^{2}
norm has been used in the literature. It is therefore natural to ask which norm is most appropriate–either for achieving the best performance or for ensuring that the formulation remains meaningful in the PDE setting. Although NGMRES has demonstrated its efficiency, its application and convergence analysis remain limited, particularly for nonlinear problems. In the next section, we apply NGMRES to accelerate Picard iteration for NSE and provide a convergence analysis of NGMRES in this setting.

3
Convergence analysis of NGMRES for NSE

We prove in this section a convergence estimate for NGMRES-Picard which uses the nonlinear residual
g
in its associated optimization problem. We note that using instead the Picard residual (i.e.,
w_{k+1}(q(u_{k}))=q(q(u_{k}))-q(u_{k})
) for the optimization problem would create an extra Picard solve at each nonlinear iteration, making NGMRES-Picard twice as expensive at each iteration compared to Picard. Note that in
[
26
]
, the use of the underlying fixed-point iteration residual in the optimization problem is proposed. One potential advantage is that the
\ell_{2}
norm may be more suitable for the NSE. We leave a detailed investigation of this for future work.

None of the main results require finite dimensionality. For simplicity we work in
V
but the same proofs will work in the
V_{h}
setting provided the nonlinear term is skew-symmetrized (e.g. through skew-symmetric form, rotational form, or EMAC form
[
7
,
20
,
32
]
). However, in Section 3.3 where the optimization norm implementation is discussed and in particular whether it is okay to use
\ell^{2}
(yes in 2D, no in 3D), the finite dimensional case is considered.

In Algorithm
2
, we present NGMRES to accelerate the Picard iteration for the NSE, where we choose the norm in (
2.13
) as the
V^{\prime}
norm defined in (
2.9
), use the constrained form for the least-squares problem in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 2

(NGMRES for NSE)
.

NGMRES for the NSE is defined at step
k+1
by:

1.

Find
\tilde{u}_{k+1}=q(u_{k})

2.

Find the coefficients
(\alpha^{k+1}_{k+1},\alpha^{k+1}_{k},...,\alpha^{k+1}_{k-m})
that minimize

\min_{\sum_{i={k-m}}^{k+1}\alpha^{k+1}_{i}=1}\|\alpha^{k+1}_{k+1}g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})+\alpha^{k+1}_{k}g(u_{k})+...\alpha^{k+1}_{k-m}g(u_{k-m})\|_{V^{\prime}}.

3.

Set

u_{k+1}=\alpha^{k+1}_{k+1}\tilde{u}_{k+1}+\alpha^{k+1}_{k}u_{k}+\alpha^{k+1}_{k-1}u_{k-1}+...+\alpha^{k+1}_{k-m}u_{k-m}.

The optimization problem plays a crucial role in convergence. We define
\theta_{k+1}
to be the gain of the optimization problem over the previous iterate and
\gamma_{k+1}
to be the gain of the optimization problem over Picard, in the following sense:

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\|\alpha^{k+1}_{k+1}g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})+\alpha^{k+1}_{k}g(u_{k})+...+\alpha^{k+1}_{k-m}g(u_{k-m})\|_{V^{\prime}}}{\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}},

\displaystyle\gamma_{k+1}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\|\alpha^{k+1}_{k+1}g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})+\alpha^{k+1}_{k}g(u_{k})+...+\alpha^{k+1}_{k-m}g(u_{k-m})\|_{V^{\prime}}}{\|g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})\|_{V^{\prime}}}.

Note that
\theta_{k+1},\gamma_{k+1}\leq 1
and can only reach 1 if the minimum is achieved when
\alpha^{k+1}=(0,1,0,...,0)
and
(1,0,...,0)
, respectively.
We will assume the optimization coefficients are uniformly bounded, i.e.
\max_{j,i}|\alpha_{j}^{i}|\leq\bar{\alpha}
. This assumption is equivalent to assuming
\{g(\tilde{u}_{k+1}),\ g(u_{k}),...,\ g(u_{k-m})\}
are linearly independent with respect to the
V^{\prime}
inner product; if they are close to linearly dependent, in practice filtering can be done analogous to what
[
35
]
does for AA or the depth
m
can be reduced.

We denote the Picard residual by

w_{k+1}=\tilde{u}_{k+1}-u_{k}=q(u_{k})-u_{k},

and the difference between two successive iterates by

e_{k}=u_{k}-u_{k-1}.

3.1
Convergence of NGMRES-Picard for the base case of
m=0

We now prove a one-step convergence theorem for NGMRES-Picard with
m=0
for the NSE. The base case has a simpler convergence proof than the general case, so we believe it is instructive for the reader to do the base case first.
NGMRES-Picard in the base case
m=0
is defined at step
k+1
by:

1.

Find
\tilde{u}_{k+1}=q(u_{k})
.

2.

Find

\alpha^{k+1}=\arg\min_{\alpha}\|\alpha g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})+(1-\alpha)g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}.

3.

Set

u_{k+1}=\alpha^{k+1}\tilde{u}_{k+1}+(1-\alpha^{k+1})u_{k}.

We will assume that
\max_{k}|\alpha^{k}|\leq\bar{\alpha}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that
\max_{k}|\alpha^{k}|\leq\bar{\alpha}
. For any
\kappa>0
, the nonlinear residual for NGMRES-Picard with
m=0
at step
k+1
satisfies the bounds

\displaystyle\|g(u_{k+1})\|_{V^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq\theta_{k+1}\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}+M\nu^{-2}\bar{\alpha}(1+\bar{\alpha})\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2},

\displaystyle\|g(u_{k+1})\|_{V^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{k+1}\kappa\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}+M\nu^{-2}\bar{\alpha}(1+\bar{\alpha})\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2},

where

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\alpha^{k+1}g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})+(1-\alpha^{k+1})g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}}{\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}},

\displaystyle\gamma_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\alpha^{k+1}g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})+(1-\alpha^{k+1})g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}}{\|g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})\|_{V^{\prime}}}.

Remark 3.1
.

The first bound shows the sequence defined by
\{\|g(u_{j})\|_{V^{\prime}}\}_{j}
is locally contractive, with contraction number at each iteration given
\theta_{k+1}
. The term
\gamma_{k+1}
represents the acceleration provided by NGMRES-Picard over Picard.

Proof.

We begin by expanding
g(u_{k+1})
and adding and subtracting
u_{k}
to the first argument of the nonlinear term to get for all
v\in V
,

\displaystyle(g(u_{k+1}),v)

\displaystyle=\nu(\nabla u_{k+1},\nabla v)+(u_{k+1}\cdot\nabla u_{k+1},v)-(f,v)

\displaystyle=\nu(\nabla u_{k+1},\nabla v)+(u_{k}\cdot\nabla u_{k+1},v)-(f,v)+)(u_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla u_{k+1},v).

Next, expand
u_{k+1}=\alpha^{k+1}\tilde{u}_{k+1}+(1-\alpha^{k+1})u_{k}
in the first and second right hand side terms above to obtain

\displaystyle g(u_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\left((1-\alpha^{k+1})g(u_{k})+(u_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla u_{k+1},v\right),

where the last step uses the definition of
\tilde{u}_{k+1}
(see (
2.6
)) to vanish the first right hand side term and the definition of
g(u_{k})
. Next we add and subtract
\alpha^{k+1}g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})
to the right hand side, which produces

(g(u_{k+1}),v)=\left(\alpha^{k+1}g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})+(1-\alpha^{k+1})g(u_{k}),v\right)\\
+((u_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla u_{k+1},v)-(\alpha^{k+1}g(\tilde{u}_{k+1}),v).

(3.1)

We now rewrite
g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})
as

\displaystyle(g(\tilde{u}_{k+1}),v)=

\displaystyle\nu(\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},\nabla v)+(\tilde{u}_{k+1}\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},v)-(f,v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu(\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},\nabla v)+(u_{k}\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},v)-(f,v)+((\tilde{u}_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((\tilde{u}_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},v)

for all
v\in V
, where the last step uses the definition of
\tilde{u}_{k+1}
; see (
2.6
).

Again using
u_{k+1}=\alpha^{k+1}\tilde{u}_{k+1}+(1-\alpha^{k+1})u_{k}
to simplify the last two terms in (
3.1
) produces

\displaystyle((u_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla u_{k+1},v)-\alpha^{k+1}(g(\tilde{u}_{k+1}),v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((\alpha^{k+1}\tilde{u}_{k+1}-\alpha^{k+1}u_{k})\cdot\nabla u_{k+1},v)-\alpha^{k+1}((\tilde{u}_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha^{k+1}((\tilde{u}_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla(u_{k+1}-\tilde{u}_{k+1}),v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha^{k+1}(w_{k+1}\cdot\nabla(\alpha^{k+1}\tilde{u}_{k+1}+(1-\alpha^{k+1})u_{k}-\tilde{u}_{k+1}),v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\alpha^{k+1}(1-\alpha^{k+1})(w_{k+1}\cdot\nabla w_{k+1},v).

We have thus established the identity

\displaystyle g(u_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\left(\alpha^{k+1}g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})+(1-\alpha^{k+1})g(u_{k})\right)-\alpha^{k+1}(1-\alpha^{k+1})w_{k+1}\cdot\nabla w_{k+1}.

(3.2)

Taking the
\|\cdot\|_{V^{\prime}}
norm of both sides, using (
2.2
),
|\alpha^{k+1}|\leq\bar{\alpha}
and reducing yields

\|g(u_{k+1})\|_{V^{\prime}}\\
\leq\|\alpha^{k+1}g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})+(1-\alpha^{k+1})g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}+|\bar{\alpha}|(|\bar{\alpha}|+1)\cdot\|\nabla w_{k+1}\|^{2}.

(3.3)

Now using (
2.10
) and the definition of
\theta_{k+1}
in (
3.3
) gives us

\displaystyle\|g(u_{k+1})\|_{V^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq\theta_{k+1}\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}+M\nu^{-2}|\bar{\alpha}|(|\bar{\alpha}|+1)\cdot\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2},

and similarly using (
2.10
), the definition of
\gamma_{k+1}
together with (
2.11
) provides with (
3.3
)

\displaystyle\|g(u_{k+1})\|_{V^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{k+1}\kappa\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}+M\nu^{-2}|\bar{\alpha}|(|\bar{\alpha}|+1)\cdot\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

3.2
Convergence analysis of NGMRES for NSE for general
m

The convergence proof for general
m
has the same framework as the
m=0
case, but is more complex because
u_{k+1}
is defined as a combination of
m+2
terms. Additionally, to reduce notation, since all optimization coefficients take the form
\alpha^{k+1}_{j}
(that is, they all have the
k+1
superscript), we drop the superscripts in this subsection.

We also prove a preliminary bound for the difference between successive iterates.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assuming
\kappa<1
, then for any depth
m
, the difference between successive NGMRES for NSE iterates satisfies

\|\nabla e_{k}\|\leq\frac{\nu^{-1}}{1-\kappa}\left(\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}+\|g(u_{k-1})\|_{V^{\prime}}\right),

(3.4)

Proof.

Writing
e_{k}=u_{k}-u_{k-1}=u_{k}-\tilde{u}_{k}+\tilde{u}_{k}-u_{k-1}
, we have that

e_{k}=(u_{k}-\tilde{u}_{k})+w_{k},

and hence

\|\nabla e_{k}\|\leq\|\nabla(u_{k}-\tilde{u}_{k})\|+\|\nabla w_{k}\|.

(3.5)

We next get a bound for
\|\nabla(u_{k}-\tilde{u}_{k})\|
. Using (
2.6
) and the definition of
g(u_{k})
gives the two equations, for all
v\in V
,

\displaystyle\nu(\nabla u_{k},\nabla v)+(u_{k}\cdot\nabla u_{k},v)

\displaystyle=(f,v)+(g(u_{k}),v),

\displaystyle\nu(\nabla\tilde{u}_{k},\nabla v)+(u_{k-1}\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k},v)

\displaystyle=(f,v).

Subtracting the above equations gives

\nu(\nabla(u_{k}-\tilde{u}_{k}),\nabla v)+(u_{k}\cdot\nabla(u_{k}-\tilde{u}_{k}),v)+(e_{k}\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k},v)=(g(u_{k}),v).

Choosing
v=(u_{k}-\tilde{u}_{k})
vanishes the first nonlinear term and gives

\displaystyle\nu\|\nabla(u_{k}-\tilde{u}_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-(e_{k}\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k},u_{k}-\tilde{u}_{k})+(g(u_{k}),u_{k}-\tilde{u}_{k})

\displaystyle\leq M\|\nabla e_{k}\|\|\nabla\tilde{u}_{k}\|\|\nabla(u_{k}-\tilde{u}_{k})\|+\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}\|\nabla(u_{k}-\tilde{u}_{k})\|,

with the last step using Lemma
2.1
and that
e_{k}
is divergence free. After reducing and using (
2.7
), this reduces to

\|\nabla(u_{k}-\tilde{u}_{k})\|\leq\kappa\|\nabla e_{k}\|+\nu^{-1}\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}.

Combining this bound with (
3.5
), we get the estimate

(1-\kappa)\|\nabla e_{k}\|\leq\|\nabla w_{k}\|+\nu^{-1}\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}.

Finally, using (
2.10
), we get

\|\nabla e_{k}\|\leq\frac{\nu^{-1}}{1-\kappa}\left(\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}+\|g(u_{k-1})\|_{V^{\prime}}\right),

which finishes the proof.
∎

We begin with a lemma for an important
g(u_{k+1})
identity.

Lemma 3.2
.

It holds for all
\chi\in V
that

(g(u_{k+1}),\chi)=\left(\alpha_{k+1}g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})+\alpha_{k}g(u_{k})+...+\alpha_{k-m}g(u_{k-m}),\chi\right)+(R,\chi),

(3.6)

where

\displaystyle(R,\chi)=

\displaystyle\bigg(-(1-\alpha_{k+1})\left(\alpha_{k+1}w_{k+1}-\alpha_{k-1}e_{k}-...-\alpha_{k-m}\sum_{j=k-m+1}^{k}e_{j}\right)\cdot\nabla w_{k+1}

\displaystyle-\alpha_{k-1}\left(\alpha_{k+1}w_{k+1}-\alpha_{k-1}e_{k}-...-\alpha_{k-m}\sum_{j=k-m+1}^{k}e_{j}\right)\cdot\nabla e_{k}-...

\displaystyle-\alpha_{k-m}\left(\alpha_{k+1}w_{k+1}-\alpha_{k-1}e_{k}-...-\alpha_{k-m}\sum_{j=k-m+1}^{k}e_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \cdot\nabla(e_{k}+e_{k-1}+...+e_{k-m+1})

\displaystyle-\left(\alpha_{k-1}e_{k}+...+\alpha_{k-m}\sum_{j=k-m+1}^{k}e_{j}\right)\cdot\nabla w_{k+1}

\displaystyle-\left(\alpha_{k-1}e_{k}+...+\alpha_{k-m}\sum_{j=k-m+1}^{k}e_{j}\right)\cdot\nabla e_{k}

\displaystyle-\left(\alpha_{k-2}(e_{k}+e_{k-1})+...+\alpha_{k-m}\sum_{j=k-m+1}^{k}e_{j}\right)\cdot\nabla e_{k-1}-...

\displaystyle-\alpha_{k-m}\sum_{j=k-m+1}^{k}e_{j}\cdot\nabla e_{k-m+1},\chi\bigg).

Proof.

The proof begins with an identity for
u_{k+1}-\tilde{u}_{k+1}
, which is constructed by expanding
u_{k+1}
using its definition to obtain

u_{k+1}-\tilde{u}_{k+1}=(\alpha_{k+1}-1)\tilde{u}_{k+1}+\alpha_{k}u_{k}+\alpha_{k-1}u_{k-1}+...+\alpha_{k-m}u_{k-m}.

Writing
\alpha_{k}=1-\alpha_{k+1}-\alpha_{k-1}-...-\alpha_{k-m}
, we have that

\displaystyle u_{k+1}-\tilde{u}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{k+1}-1)(\tilde{u}_{k+1}-u_{k})+\alpha_{k-1}(u_{k-1}-u_{k})+...+\alpha_{k-m}(u_{k-m}-u_{k})

\displaystyle=-(1-\alpha_{k+1})w_{k+1}-\alpha_{k-1}e_{k}-...-\alpha_{k-m}(e_{k}+e_{k-1}+...+e_{k-m+1}).

(3.7)

Next, we expand
g(u_{k+1})
by using the definition of
g
, then use the definition of
u_{k+1}
and finally regrouping terms and using (
2.6
) to get for all
\chi\in V
,

\displaystyle(g(u_{k+1}),\chi)

\displaystyle=\nu(\nabla u_{k+1},\nabla\chi)+(u_{k+1}\cdot\nabla u_{k+1},\chi)-(f,\chi)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k+1}\left(\nu(\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},\nabla\chi)+(u_{k+1}\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},\chi)-(f,\chi)\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\alpha_{k}\left(\nu(\nabla u_{k},\nabla\chi)+(u_{k+1}\cdot\nabla u_{k},\chi)-(f,\chi)\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ +...+\alpha_{k-m}\left(\nu(\nabla u_{k-m},\nabla\chi)+(u_{k+1}\cdot\nabla u_{k-m},\chi)-(f,\chi)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\alpha_{k+1}g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})+\alpha_{k}g(u_{k})+...+\alpha_{k-m}g(u_{k-m}),\chi\right)-\alpha_{k+1}(g(\tilde{u}_{k+1}),\chi)

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\alpha_{k+1}((u_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},\chi)+\alpha_{k}((u_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla u_{k},\chi)

\displaystyle\ \ \ +...+\alpha_{k-m}((u_{k+1}-u_{k-m})\cdot\nabla u_{k-m},\chi),

where in the last step we added and subtracted
\alpha_{k+1}g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})
. Since by (
2.6
)

(g(\tilde{u}_{k+1}),\chi)=(\nu(\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},\nabla\chi)+(\tilde{u}_{k+1}\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},\chi)-(f,\chi))=((\tilde{u}_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},\chi),

we have that

(g(u_{k+1}),\chi)=\left(\alpha_{k+1}g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})+\alpha_{k}g(u_{k})+...+\alpha_{k-m}g(u_{k-m}),\chi\right)+(R,\chi),

(3.8)

where

(R,\chi)=\alpha_{k+1}((u_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},\chi)+\alpha_{k}((u_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla u_{k},\chi)\\
+...+\alpha_{k-m}((u_{k+1}-u_{k-m})\cdot\nabla u_{k-m},\chi)-\alpha_{k+1}((\tilde{u}_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},\chi).

Next, we combine terms in
R
(except the last one) by using the definition of
u_{k+1}
and adding and subtracting appropriately via

\displaystyle\big(\alpha_{k+1}(u_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1}+\alpha_{k}(u_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla u_{k}+...

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ +\alpha_{k-m}(u_{k+1}-u_{k-m})\cdot\nabla u_{k-m},\chi\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\alpha_{k+1}(u_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1}+\alpha_{k}(u_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla u_{k}+...

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ +\alpha_{k-m}(u_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla u_{k-m}

\displaystyle\ \ +\alpha_{k-1}(u_{k}-u_{k-1})\cdot\nabla u_{k-1}+...+\alpha_{k-m}(u_{k}-u_{k-m})\cdot\nabla u_{k-m},\chi\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big((u_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla u_{k+1}+\alpha_{k-1}(u_{k}-u_{k-1})\cdot\nabla u_{k-1}+...

\displaystyle\ \ +\alpha_{k-m}(u_{k}-u_{k-m})\cdot\nabla u_{k-m},\chi\big).

Now by expanding the first term of the last line, we can write

\displaystyle(R,\chi)

\displaystyle=\big((\alpha_{k+1}\tilde{u}_{k+1}+(\alpha_{k}-1)u_{k}+...+\alpha_{k-m}u_{k-m})\cdot\nabla u_{k+1}

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\alpha_{k-1}(u_{k}-u_{k-1})\cdot\nabla u_{k-2}+\alpha_{k-2}(u_{k}-u_{k-2})\cdot\nabla u_{k-2}+...

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\alpha_{k-m}(u_{k}-u_{k-m})\cdot\nabla u_{k-m}-\alpha_{k+1}(\tilde{u}_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},\chi\big),

and since
\alpha_{k}-1=-\alpha_{k+1}-\alpha_{k-1}-...-\alpha_{k-m}
,

\displaystyle(R,\chi)=

\displaystyle\big((\alpha_{k+1}(\tilde{u}_{k+1}-u_{k})+\alpha_{k-1}(u_{k-1}-u_{k})+...+\alpha_{k-m}(u_{k-m}-u_{k}))\cdot\nabla u_{k+1}

\displaystyle+\alpha_{k-1}(u_{k}-u_{k-1})\cdot\nabla u_{k-1}+...

\displaystyle+\alpha_{k-m}(u_{k}-u_{k-m})\cdot\nabla u_{k-m}-\alpha_{k+1}(\tilde{u}_{k+1}-u_{k})\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}_{k+1},\chi\big).

Combining the first and last terms now gives us

(R,\chi)=\\
\big(\alpha_{k+1}w_{k+1}\cdot\nabla(u_{k+1}-\tilde{u}_{k+1})-(\alpha_{k-1}e_{k}+...+\alpha_{k-m}(e_{k}+e_{k-1}...+e_{k-m+1}))\cdot\nabla u_{k+1}\\
+\alpha_{k-1}e_{k}\cdot\nabla u_{k-1}+...+\alpha_{k-m}(e_{k}+e_{k-1}+...+e_{k-m+1})\cdot\nabla u_{k-m},\chi\big),

which after regrouping terms becomes

(R,\chi)=\big(\alpha_{k+1}w_{k+1}\cdot\nabla(u_{k+1}-\tilde{u}_{k+1})-\alpha_{k-1}e_{k}\cdot\nabla(u_{k+1}-u_{k-1})\\
-...-\alpha_{k-m}(e_{k}+e_{k-1}+...+e_{k-m+1})\cdot\nabla(u_{k+1}-u_{k-m}),\chi\big).

We now add and subtract to the
(u_{k+1}-u_{k-j})
terms via

u_{k+1}-u_{k-j}=(u_{k+1}-\tilde{u}_{k+1})+w_{k+1}+e_{k}+...+e_{k-j+1},

which implies

(R,\chi)=\\
\big(\alpha_{k+1}w_{k+1}\cdot\nabla(u_{k+1}-\tilde{u}_{k+1})-\alpha_{k-1}e_{k}\cdot\nabla\left((u_{k+1}-\tilde{u}_{k+1})+w_{k+1}+e_{k}\right)-...\\
-\alpha_{k-m}(e_{k}+e_{k-1}+...+e_{k-m+1})\cdot\nabla\left((u_{k+1}-\tilde{u}_{k+1})+w_{k+1}+e_{k}+...+e_{k-m+1}\right),\chi\big),

and thus

(R,\chi)=\\
\bigg(\left(\alpha_{k+1}w_{k+1}-\alpha_{k-1}e_{k}-...-\alpha_{k-m}(e_{k}+e_{k-1}+...+e_{k-m+1})\right)\cdot\nabla(u_{k+1}-\tilde{u}_{k+1})\\
-\left(\alpha_{k-1}e_{k}+...+\alpha_{k-m}(e_{k}+e_{k-1}+...+e_{k-m+1})\right)\cdot\nabla w_{k+1}\\
-\left(\alpha_{k-1}e_{k}+...+\alpha_{k-m}(e_{k}+e_{k-1}+...+e_{k-m+1})\right)\cdot\nabla e_{k}\\
-\left(\alpha_{k-2}(e_{k}+e_{k-1})+...+\alpha_{k-m}(e_{k}+e_{k-1}+...+e_{k-m+1})\right)\cdot\nabla e_{k-1}\\
-...-\alpha_{k-m}(e_{k}+e_{k-1}+...+e_{k-m+1})\cdot\nabla e_{k-m+1},\chi\bigg).

Finally, using (
3.7
), we have that

\displaystyle(R,\chi)=\bigg(

\displaystyle-(1-\alpha_{k+1})\left(\alpha_{k+1}w_{k+1}-\alpha_{k-1}e_{k}-...-\alpha_{k-m}(e_{k}+e_{k-1}+...+e_{k-m+1})\right)\cdot\nabla w_{k+1}

\displaystyle-\alpha_{k-1}\left(\alpha_{k+1}w_{k+1}-\alpha_{k-1}e_{k}-...-\alpha_{k-m}(e_{k}+e_{k-1}+...+e_{k-m+1})\right)\cdot\nabla e_{k}

\displaystyle-...

\displaystyle-\alpha_{k-m}\left(\alpha_{k+1}w_{k+1}+\alpha_{k-1}e_{k}+...+\alpha_{k-m}(e_{k}+e_{k-1}+...+e_{k-m+1})\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \cdot\nabla(e_{k}+e_{k-1}+...+e_{k-m+1})

\displaystyle-\left(\alpha_{k-1}e_{k}+...+\alpha_{k-m}(e_{k}+e_{k-1}+...+e_{k-m+1})\right)\cdot\nabla w_{k+1}

\displaystyle-\left(\alpha_{k-1}e_{k}+...+\alpha_{k-m}(e_{k}+e_{k-1}+...+e_{k-m+1})\right)\cdot\nabla e_{k}

\displaystyle-\left(\alpha_{k-2}(e_{k}+e_{k-1})+...+\alpha_{k-m}(e_{k}+e_{k-1}+...+e_{k-m+1})\right)\cdot\nabla e_{k-1}

\displaystyle-...-\alpha_{k-m}(e_{k}+e_{k-1}+...+e_{k-m+1})\cdot\nabla e_{k-m+1},\chi\bigg).

∎

We can now prove a convergence result for NGMRES with general depth
m
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Assuming
\kappa<1

and
\max_{k,j}|\alpha^{k}_{j}|\leq\bar{\alpha}
, for any
m\geq 1
we have that

\displaystyle\|g(u_{k+1})\|_{V^{\prime}}\leq\theta_{k+1}\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2}+\|g(u_{k-1})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2}+...+\|g(u_{k-m})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2}\right),

\displaystyle\|g(u_{k+1})\|_{V^{\prime}}\leq\gamma_{k+1}\kappa\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2}+\|g(u_{k-1})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2}+...+\|g(u_{k-m})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2}\right).

Remark 3.2
.

The assumption
\kappa<1
in the proof comes from bounding terms of the form
\|\nabla e_{j}\|
using (
3.4
). For the
m=0
result, no such terms arise due to the extrapolation used by NGMRES. This condition was also assumed in the AA-Picard proof of acceleration in
[
38
]
. Our numerical tests use
\kappa\gg 1
, far beyond the range where Picard is contractive.

Remark 3.3
.

The same arguments from the
m=0
case apply here regarding using
\ell^{2}
or
L^{2}
as the optimization norm instead of
V^{\prime}
. That is, in 2D this is fine but in 3D it is generally not a good choice.

Proof.

Taking
V^{\prime}
norms of both sides in (
3.6
), we obtain

\|g(u_{k+1})\|_{V^{\prime}}\leq\|\alpha_{k+1}g(\tilde{u}_{k+1})+\alpha_{k}g(u_{k})+...+\alpha_{k-m}g(u_{k-m})\|_{V^{\prime}}+\|R\|_{V^{\prime}},

and thus using the definition of
\theta_{k+1}
we have that

\|g(u_{k+1})\|_{V^{\prime}}\leq\theta_{k+1}\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}+\|R\|_{V^{\prime}},

and similarly using (
2.11
) and the definition of
\gamma_{k+1}
,

\|g(u_{k+1})\|_{V^{\prime}}\leq\gamma_{k+1}\kappa\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}+\|R\|_{V^{\prime}},

Thus it remains to bound
R
. We first note that each term of
R
takes one the forms:
w_{k+1}\cdot\nabla w_{k+1}
,
e_{j}\cdot\nabla w_{k+1}
(
k-m+1\leq j\leq k
),
w_{k+1}\cdot\nabla e_{j}
(
k-m+1\leq j\leq k
), or
e_{j}\cdot\nabla e_{i}
(
k-m+1\leq j,i\leq k
). Terms of these forms are bounded using Lemma
2.1
, (
2.10
) and (
3.4
):

\displaystyle\|w_{k+1}\cdot\nabla w_{k+1}\|_{V^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq M\|\nabla w_{k+1}\|^{2}\leq M\nu^{-2}\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2},

\displaystyle\|e_{j}\cdot\nabla w_{k+1}\|_{V^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq M\|\nabla e_{j}\|\|\nabla w_{k+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M\nu^{-2}}{1-\kappa}\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}\left(\|g(u_{j})\|_{V^{\prime}}+\|g(u_{j-1})\|_{V^{\prime}}\right),

\displaystyle\|w_{k+1}\cdot\nabla e_{j}\|_{V^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq M\|\nabla w_{k+1}\|\|\nabla e_{j}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M\nu^{-2}}{1-\kappa}\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}\left(\|g(u_{j})\|_{V^{\prime}}+\|g(u_{j-1})\|_{V^{\prime}}\right),

\displaystyle\|e_{j}\cdot\nabla e_{i}\|_{V^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq M\|\nabla e_{i}\|\|\nabla e_{j}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M\nu^{-2}}{(1-\kappa)^{2}}\left(\|g(u_{i})\|_{V^{\prime}}+\|g(u_{i-1})\|_{V^{\prime}}\right)\left(\|g(u_{j})\|_{V^{\prime}}+\|g(u_{j-1})\|_{V^{\prime}}\right).

Hence we can bound
R
via

\|R\|_{V^{\prime}}\leq\bar{\alpha}(1+\bar{\alpha})M\nu^{-2}\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2}+C(m)\bar{\alpha}^{2}\frac{M\nu^{-2}}{1-\kappa}\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}\sum_{j=k-m}^{k}\|g(u_{j})\|_{V^{\prime}}\\
+C(m)\bar{\alpha}^{2}\frac{M\nu^{-2}}{(1-\kappa)^{2}}\sum_{j=k-m}^{k}\|g(u_{j})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2},

where
C(m)
is a constant depending quadratically on
m
. Reducing completes the proof.
∎

3.3
More on the choice of the optimization norm

The theory above relies on the choice of optimization norm being
V^{\prime}
, which is due to the function
g(v)
being in
V^{\prime}
and not
L^{2}
(or
\ell^{2}
) due to the
v\cdot\nabla v
term with
v\in V
. Note that the space the nonlinear residual
g(v)
resides in is completely problem dependent, and different PDEs with different nonlinearity structures would have different ‘best’ optimization norms and for other non-PDE problems the choice of
\ell^{2}
may well be the best option.

Our numerical experiments show that for NGMRES-Picard for NSE, using
V^{\prime}
optimization norm gives better convergence than
\ell^{2}
, especially in 3D, and we give now some explanation for this. Note that for solving PDEs,
\ell^{2}
is typically not the norm that matches the theory. However,
\ell^{2}
is closely related to
L^{2}(\Omega)
, which is a commonly used Hilbert space norm in PDE theory, and using the
\ell^{2}
norm in place of the
L^{2}
is reasonable in many situations (see
[
23
]
for some notable exceptions). We discuss below how
L^{2}
(and by extension
\ell^{2}
) is a reasonable choice for NGMRES-Picard in 2D, but not in 3D.

Consider the discrete setting, e.g. working in the weakly divergence-free space
V_{h}
instead of
V
. The proof of Theorem
3.1
does not change until (
3.3
). Here, the problem term is
w^{h}_{k+1}\cdot\nabla w^{h}_{k+1}
(with
h
denoting discrete variables), we have from the inverse inequality
[
5
]
that with the
L^{2}
norm,

\displaystyle(2D):\|w_{k+1}^{h}\cdot\nabla w_{k+1}^{h}\|

\displaystyle\leq C(\log(h^{-1}))^{1/2}\|\nabla w^{h}_{k+1}\|^{2},

\displaystyle(3D):\|w_{k+1}^{h}\cdot\nabla w_{k+1}^{h}\|

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{-1/2}\|\nabla w^{h}_{k+1}\|^{2}.

This leads to the estimates: in 2D,

\displaystyle\|g(u_{k+1}^{h})\|

\displaystyle\leq\theta_{k+1}^{L^{2}}\|g(u_{k}^{h})\|+C(\log(h^{-1})^{1/2}M\nu^{-2}|\bar{\alpha}|(|\bar{\alpha}|+1)\cdot\|g(u_{k}^{h})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2},

\displaystyle\|g(u_{k+1}^{h})\|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{k+1}^{L^{2}}\kappa\|g(u_{k}^{h})\|+C(\log(h^{-1})^{1/2}M\nu^{-2}|\bar{\alpha}|(|\bar{\alpha}|+1)\cdot\|g(u_{k}^{h})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2}.

and in 3D,

\displaystyle\|g(u_{k+1}^{h})\|

\displaystyle\leq\theta_{k+1}^{L^{2}}\|g(u_{k}^{h})\|+Ch^{-1/2}M\nu^{-2}|\bar{\alpha}|(|\bar{\alpha}|+1)\cdot\|g(u_{k}^{h})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2},

\displaystyle\|g(u_{k+1}^{h})\|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{k+1}^{L^{2}}\kappa\|g(u_{k}^{h})\|+Ch^{-1/2}M\nu^{-2}|\bar{\alpha}|(|\bar{\alpha}|+1)\cdot\|g(u_{k}^{h})\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2},

noting that
theta_{k+1}^{L^{2}}
and
\gamma_{k+1}^{L^{2}}
change since the
L^{2}
norm is now assumed for the optimization problem.

While this discussion is for Theorem
3.1
, the same terms (and more) arise in the proof of the Theorem
3.2
, and so the discussion applies to the general
m
results as well. Hence we conclude that for 2D, using the
L^{2}
norm is likely to be a good choice even for very small
h
that will provide similar acceleration as when using
V^{\prime}
. However, in 3D, the
h^{-1/2}
can be a large constant that makes the higher order terms large enough to dominate the linear terms and even prevent convergence. Indeed, our numerical results show results using
\ell^{2}
in 2D are similar to those using
V^{\prime}
, but in 3D our results using
\ell^{2}
are much worse in all cases than those found using
V^{\prime}
.

Scott-Vogelius (SV) elements, with

. The meshes are constructed as barycenter refined triangle/tetrahedral meshes, and these elements are known to be inf-sup stable on such meshes

30

3

4

This section will i) illustrate the convergence theory by calculating
\theta_{k}
for several computations and comparing it to the ratio of successive
\|g(u_{k})\|_{V^{\prime}}

from small at early iterations to large at later iterations can be a good strategy, iii) show that using the

norm in the numerical tests to match the theory gives better results than using

, and iv) show that the convergence properties are mesh independent. This will be done by running tests on multiple test problems with varying

and

). This relationship between

5
Conclusions and Future Directions

In this work we employ NGMRES as an acceleration technique for the Navier–Stokes Picard iteration, and provide a convergence analysis of the NGMRES–Picard iteration for general depth that shows NGMRES provides acceleration through the gain of the optimization problem. Our theoretical results suggest that using the dual norm of the divergence-free space is the appropriate choice in the optimization problem arising in the NGMRES algorithm; our results also indicate that different problems will likely have different best choices of optimization norm. Numerical experiments for both 2D and 3D problems indicate that NGMRES significantly improves the performance of the Picard iteration, even in cases where the unaccelerated iteration diverges.

Since Anderson acceleration has also been successfully applied to the NSE for the Picard iteration, it would be interesting to compare AA with NGMRES in this setting in future work. In
[
26
]
, the use of the underlying fixed point residual in the NGMRES optimization problem and two new variants of AA based on reformulated least-squares problems using the Picard residual and nonlinear residual are proposed. Another potential direction for future research is to explore convergence analyses of NGMRES when the
\ell^{2}
norm is used in the optimization problem, given its simplicity, and the new variants of AA.
