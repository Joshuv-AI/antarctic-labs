Periodontitis aggravates high-fat diet-induced MASLD via gut microbiota dysbiosis and metabolic dysfunction in mice
Authors: Xinchan Liu, Zhenzhen Che, Yubo Hou, Weixian Yu, Yu Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Periodontitis has been recognized as a contributing factor in the development of metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). However, the precise mechanisms through which periodontitis influences the pathogenesis of MASLD remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate the association between experimental periodontitis and MASLD severity and to explore the potential underlying mechanisms using a ligature-induced periodontitis model under low-fat diet (LFD) and high-fat diet (HFD) conditions. A total of 40 mice were divided into four groups: low-fat diet control group (LFD-Ctrl), low-fat diet with periodontitis group (LFD-Perio), high-fat diet control group (HFD-Ctrl), and high-fat diet with periodontitis group (HFD-Perio), with 10 mice initially assigned to each group. Mice were fed an HFD for 12 weeks to establish the MASLD model, followed by ligature-induced periodontitis for 4 weeks. Periodontal inflammation and alveolar bone loss were assessed using micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT), hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining, while MASLD severity was evaluated via hepatic H&E staining, Oil Red O staining, periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining, Masson’s Trichrome staining, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease activity score (NAS), alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) expression, pericellular fibrosis, and serum lipid and liver enzyme measurements. Compared with HFD-Ctrl mice, HFD-Perio mice exhibited aggravated MASLD-related phenotypes, including elevated fasting blood glucose, significantly increased homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) (2.89 ± 0.67 vs. 1.93 ± 0.26, P < 0.0001), higher nonalcoholic fatty liver disease activity score (NAS) (4.57 ± 0.71 vs. 2.30 ± 0.33, P < 0.01), and more pronounced pericellular fibrosis. Gut microbiota analysis showed that the Firmicutes/Bacteroidota ratio was further increased in HFD-Perio mice compared with HFD-Ctrl mice (9.39 ± 2.82 vs. 5.48 ± 1.98, P < 0.05), accompanied by enrichment of the genus Helicobacter. These findings indicate that experimental periodontitis aggravates HFD-induced MASLD phenotypes, potentially via metabolic dysregulation, liver inflammation and fibrosis, and gut microbiota dysbiosis, highlighting the need for further studies to clarify whether periodontal health influences MASLD progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56068-6.

Introduction

Previous studies have indicated that periodontitis is associated with metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD); however, the precise mechanisms by which periodontal inflammation influences MASLD progression remain unclear. In particular, how periodontitis interacts with diet-induced metabolic stress, gut microbiota dysbiosis, and hepatic signaling pathways has not been fully elucidated. Addressing these knowledge gaps is essential for understanding the potential role of periodontal disease in the pathogenesis of MASLD.

Periodontitis is a common chronic inflammatory disease affecting the supporting tissues of the periodontium, including the gingiva, periodontal ligament, cementum, and alveolar bone. The onset of this disease is primarily triggered by the progressive accumulation of dental plaque1. If not adequately treated, severe periodontitis can eventually result in tooth loss and impair masticatory function. At present, there are an estimated 1.1 billion cases of severe periodontitis worldwide, accounting for approximately 15% of the global population2. This 91.5% increase over the past 30 years has made periodontitis a major global public health burden3. Beyond its localized destructive effects, periodontitis is strongly linked to various systemic conditions, such as MASLD, highlighting its broader impact on overall health4,5.

MASLD, which was formerly identified as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), is marked by the abnormal build-up of lipids within hepatocytes, a key pathological feature of the condition. Over time this accumulation can lead to cellular dysfunction and inflammation. MASLD encompasses a range of liver disorders, beginning with uncomplicated steatosis, progressing to nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), advancing to cirrhosis, which can ultimately result in the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma6. The worldwide prevalence of MASLD is steadily increasing; it affects over 25% of the overall population7. Furthermore, MASLD affects approximately 60% of individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes, and can be found in up to 95% of individuals with severe obesity8,9. Given the substantial and growing clinical and socioeconomic burden of MASLD, it is essential to pinpoint the causative factors that contribute to its onset and progression.

Accumulating evidence in recent years has indicated a close relationship between periodontitis and MASLD10–12. Moderate to severe periodontitis shows a notable correlation with the progression of MASLD13. In a cohort study, greater periodontal pocket depth exhibited a positive association with the risk of experiencing severe liver disease14. Another study showed that more severe periodontal destruction was accompanied by higher degrees of liver fibrosis among obese patients15. Experimental data provided additional evidence that the significant contribution of periodontal pathogens to the exacerbation and progression of MASLD, which is strongly associated with exacerbated hepatic steatosis16. Collectively, these findings suggest that periodontitis not only coexists with liver disease but may also contribute to its pathogenesis.

Traditionally, the association between periodontitis and MASLD primarily arises from the systemic dissemination of bacterial agents and the molecules of inflammation originating from ulcerated periodontal pockets into the circulation, thereby eliciting a persistent low-grade systemic inflammatory response17. Periodontitis increases circulating substances of pro-inflammatory activity, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), exhibiting inflammatory features similar to those observed in obesity and other metabolic abnormalities17. These factors may promote the natural course of MASLD. More recent studies have further proposed that periodontitis or periodontitis-associated salivary microorganisms can alter the gut microbiota and induce dysbiosis18–20, which exacerbates intestinal inflammation and accelerates HFD-induced MASLD pathology in mice models21. Consistently, this gut-derived endotoxemia model provides a mechanistic link between periodontitis-induced dysbiosis and liver injury. Clinical and experimental data in NAFLD/NASH suggest that elevated circulating lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is associated with impaired intestinal permeability. This is accompanied by increased hepatic LPS deposition and activation of inflammatory signaling cascades, including the NF-κB pathway. The involvement of toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-positive immune cells supports a pivotal role of the LPS-TLR4 axis in driving hepatic inflammation and fibrotic progression19. Overall, these observations extend the conventional systemic-inflammation hypothesis by highlighting a gut-mediated route through which periodontal inflammation may influence hepatic pathology. Therefore, the “oral–gut–liver axis” may represent an important mechanism by which periodontitis contributes to MASLD. Periodontal inflammation disrupts gut microbial homeostasis, increases intestinal permeability, and facilitates endotoxin translocation into the bloodstream, ultimately promoting hepatic inflammation and metabolic disturbances.

Despite these advances, the influence of gut microbiota dysbiosis in MASLD progression, especially when combined with periodontitis and HFD exposure, remains incompletely elucidated. Specifically, it is unclear how periodontal inflammation interacts with diet-induced metabolic stress to modulate gut microbial composition, microbial metabolic activities, and downstream hepatic signaling pathways.

To investigate the influence of periodontitis on MASLD progression, we established a mouse model by combining a HFD with ligature-induced periodontitis. By integrating histopathological, metabolic, inflammatory, and gut microbiota analyses, this study aimed to investigate how periodontitis contributes to MASLD progression via its effects on the gut microbiota. Additionally, this study aimed to elucidate mechanistic insights into the oral–gut–liver axis linking periodontal disease to MASLD.

Results

Construction of ligature-induced experimental periodontitis in HFD-fed mice

After 12 weeks of HFD feeding, HFD-Ctrl mice exhibited a significant increase in body size (Supplementary Fig. S3A) and body weight compared with LFD-Ctrl mice (a mean difference of 8.62 g (95% CI: 4.26–12.98 g, P < 0.001; HFD-Ctrl vs. LFD-Ctrl group; Supplementary Fig. S3B), indicating obesity symptoms. Following 4 weeks of ligature placement, micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) analysis demonstrated marked alveolar bone loss in ligated mice, as evidenced by an increased distance from the cement-enamel junction (CEJ) to the alveolar bone crest (ABC) compared with unligated control mice(a mean difference of 0.1345 mm (95% CI: 0.1064–0.1626 mm, P < 0.0001); ligated vs. unligated group; Fig. 1A, D). Additionally, the proportion of bone volume (BV) relative to tissue volume (TV) exhibited a significant decline in the ligated mice (a mean difference of − 0.2451 (95% CI: −0.3400 to − 0.1502, P < 0.001); ligated vs. unligated group; Fig. 1E). Similarly, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Fig. 1B, F) and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) (Fig. 1C, G) staining revealed that in the ligated mice, inflammatory cellular infiltrates, epithelial hyperplasia, and osteoclast numbers were remarkably elevated when contrasted with the unligated control mice (a mean difference of 6.148 (95% CI:4.81–7.48, P < 0.0001); ligated vs. unligated group; Fig. 1C, G). Additionally, HFD-Ctrl mice exhibited an increase in inflammatory cellular infiltrates (a mean difference of 1.366 (95% CI: 1.011–1.721, P < 0.001); HFD-Ctrl vs. LFD-Ctrl group) and osteoclast numbers (a mean difference of 2.620 (95% CI: 1.449–3.791, P < 0.05); HFD-Ctrl vs. LFD-Ctrl group) in comparison to LFD-Ctrl mice (Fig. 1F, G). Furthermore, these indicators in HFD periodontitis (HFD-Perio) mice were further deteriorated compared to HFD-Ctrl mice (mean differences of 2.267 (95% CI: 1.609–2.925) and 5.468 (95% CI: 3.070–7.866), all P < 0.0001; HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl group; Fig. 1F, G). These findings confirmed that ligature placement successfully induced experimental periodontitis in both LFD-fed and HFD-fed mice.

Periodontitis aggravated the evolution of MASLD in HFD-fed mice

We successfully established a periodontitis model in HFD-fed mice and then evaluated the role of periodontitis in promoting MASLD progression. Following a 16-week feeding period, the hepatic weight of HFD-Ctrl mice exhibited a notable increase in comparison to LFD-Ctrl mice (a mean difference of 0.523 g (95% CI: 0.376–0.670 g, P < 0.0001); HFD-Ctrl vs. LFD-Ctrl group; Fig. 2A, D). Additionally, their livers of the former group displayed notable enlargement, characterized by smooth surfaces and a yellowish hue (Fig. 2A, D). In HFD-Ctrl mice, serum levels of liver injury biomarkers, specifically alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), were markedly higher compared to those in LFD-Ctrl mice (ALT, (a mean difference of 9.43 U/L (95% CI: 6.11–12.75 U/L,P < 0.001); HFD-Ctrl vs. LFD-Ctrl group); AST, (a mean difference o f 2.22 U/L (95% CI: 1.10–3.34 U/L, P < 0.01); HFD-Ctrl vs. LFD-Ctrl group); Fig. 2I, J). Furthermore, HFD-Perio mice exhibited even higher serum concentrations of ALT and AST in relation to both HFD-Ctrl (ALT, (a mean difference of 8.12 U/L (95% CI: 2.84–13.40 U/L, P < 0.01); HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl group); AST, (a mean difference of 0.99 U/L (95% CI: −0.23–2.21 U/L), P < 0.05; HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl group)) and LFD-Perio mice (ALT, (a mean difference of 15.17 U/L (95% CI: 9.91–20.43 U/L)); AST, (a mean difference of 3.02 U/L (95% CI: 1.61–4.43 U/L)), all P < 0.0001; HFD-Perio vs. LFD-Perio group; Fig. 2I, J), affirming liver dysfunction in HFD-Perio mice. Moreover, the analysis of serum lipid metabolism indicators revealed more significant changes, as the HFD-Ctrl mice displayed a notable elevation in serum levels of triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) (TG, (a mean difference of 0.1662 mmol/L (95% CI: 0.0569–0.2755 mmol/L, P < 0.001)); LDL-C, (a mean difference of 1.770 mmol/L (95% CI: 1.154–2.386 mmol/L, P < 0.001)); HFD-Ctrl vs. LFD-Ctrl group) and total cholesterol (T-CHO) (a mean difference of 2.533 mmol/L (95% CI: 2.024–3.042 mmol/L,P < 0.05); HFD-Ctrl vs. LFD-Ctrl group), accompanied by a reduction in high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) (a mean difference of − 0.2037 mmol/L (95% CI: −0.3381 to − 0.0693 mmol/L, P < 0.05); HFD-Ctrl vs. LFD-Ctrl group) in comparison to LFD-Ctrl (Fig. 2H). Furthermore, the HFD-Perio mice demonstrated even greater increases in T-CHO and LDL-C concentrations when evaluated against both the HFD-Ctrl mice (T-CHO, (a mean difference of 0.520 mmol/L (95% CI: −0.059–1.099 mmol/L, P < 0.05)); LDL-C, (a mean difference of 1.073 mmol/L (95% CI: 0.083–2.063 mmol/L, P < 0.05)); HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl group) and LFD-Perio mice (T-CHO, (a mean difference of 2.779 mmol/L (95% CI: 2.362–3.196 mmol/L, P < 0.05); HFD-Perio vs. LFD-Perio group); LDL-C, (a mean difference of 2.723 mmol/L (95% CI: 1.800–3.646 mmol/L, P < 0.0001); HFD-Perio vs. LFD-Perio group); Fig. 2H).

Additionally, H&E staining demonstrated a notable presence of lipid droplets in the hepatic parenchyma of HFD-Ctrl and HFD-Perio mice, highlighting significant lipid accumulation. This deposition resulted in hepatocyte enlargement and distortion of the liver lobe structure (Fig. 2B). Oil red O staining (Fig. 2C and E) further confirmed the lipid droplet accumulation in the liver tissues. A significant increase in inflammatory cell infiltration (a mean difference of 1.467 (95% CI: 1.137–1.797, P < 0.001); HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl group) and hepatocyte ballooning (a mean difference of 0.800 (95% CI: 0.380–1.220, P < 0.05); HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl group) was more prevalent in HFD-Perio mice than in HFD-Ctrl mice (Fig. 2F). According to the NAFLD activity score (NAS), HFD-Perio mice displayed more pronounced NASH-like features than HFD-Ctrl mice (a mean difference of 2.267 (95% CI: 1.514–3.020, P < 0.01); HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl group; Fig. 2G). Subsequently, the Masson’s Trichrome and immunohistochemical staining showed that in HFD-Perio mice, there was increased pericellular fibrosis (a mean difference of 2.865 (95% CI: 0.422–5.308, P < 0.01); HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl group) and more α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) expressing hepatic stellate cells (a mean difference of 1.647 (95% CI: 0.667–2.627, P < 0.001); HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl group) than in HFD-Ctrl mice (Fig. 3A-D). In contrast, no significant changes were identified between LFD-Ctrl and LFD-Perio mice.

Subsequently, we examined gene expressions related to hepatic lipogenesis. The results demonstrated that the hepatic lipogenic genes, specifically expression levels of sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1c (Srebp-1c) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (Pparγ) were elevated in HFD-Ctrl mice when evaluated against LFD-Ctrl mice (Srebp-1c, (a mean difference of 1.951 (95% CI: 0.311–3.591, P < 0.05)); Pparγ, (a mean difference of 0.676 (95% CI: −0.612–1.964, P < 0.05)); HFD-Ctrl vs. LFD-Ctrl group; Fig. 2K). Conversely, genes associated with oxidation, including peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (Pparα) (a mean difference of − 0.453 (95% CI: −0.807 to − 0.100, P < 0.05); HFD-Ctrl vs. LFD-Ctrl group) and carnitine palmitoyltransferase-1α (Cpt-1α) (a mean difference of − 0.369 (95% CI: −0.518 to − 0.220, P < 0.01); HFD-Ctrl vs. LFD-Ctrl group) showed a decreased expression (Fig. 2K). Although the expression of the above liver adipogenesis-related genes failed to reach statistical significance between HFD-Perio and HFD-Ctrl mice, these findings also suggested the occurrence of liver lipid metabolic abnormalities in HFD-Perio mice. Besides lipogenesis, our study also investigated liver gene expressions related to fibrosis by examining smooth muscle alpha 2 actin (Acta2), collagen type 1 alpha 1 chain (Col1α1), and collagen type 3 alpha 1 chain (Col3α1) (Fig. 3E-G). The results indicated the gene levels of Acta2 (a mean difference of 1.192 (95% CI: 0.517–1.867, P < 0.001)), Col1α1 (a mean difference of 2.396 (95% CI: 1.443–3.349, P < 0.05)), and Col3α1 (a mean difference of 4.123 (95% CI: 0.242–8.004, P < 0.01)) were notably increased in HFD-Ctrl mice compared to LFD-Ctrl mice, and the gene levels of Acta2 (a mean difference of 1.331 (95% CI: 0.686–1.976, P < 0.001); HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl group) and Col1α1 (a mean difference of 2.642 (95% CI: −1.231–6.515, P < 0.05); HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl group) were further elevated in HFD-Perio mice in comparison to HFD-Ctrl mice. These findings collectively propose that experimental periodontitis exacerbates hepatic steatosis and promotes the progression to more severe steatohepatitis in HFD-fed mice.

Periodontitis aggravated insulin resistance and impaired hepatic glycogen storage in HFD-fed mice

Next, we examined the role of periodontitis on glucose homeostasis in HFD-fed mice. In the HFD-Ctrl mice, there was a noteworthy elevation in the levels of fasting blood glucose (a mean difference of 38.10 mg/dL (95% CI: 18.29–57.91 mg/dL, P < 0.01); HFD-Ctrl vs. LFD-Ctrl group), serum insulin (a mean difference of 0.954 µU/mL (95% CI: 0.648–1.260 µU/mL, P < 0.001); HFD-Ctrl vs. LFD-Ctrl group), and the homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) indices (a mean difference of 0.739 (95% CI: 0.527–0.951, P < 0.001); HFD-Ctrl vs. LFD-Ctrl group) compared to LFD-Ctrl mice (Fig. 4A-C). This elevation was even more prominent in the HFD-Perio mice in comparison to HFD-Ctrl mice (fasting blood glucose (a mean difference of 46.60 (95% CI: 21.04–72.16, P < 0.001); HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl group); serum insulin (a mean difference of 0.720 µU/mL(95% CI: 0.190–1.250 µU/mL, P < 0.01); HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl group); HOMA-IR (a mean difference of 0.966 (95% CI: 0.488–1.444, P < 0.0001); HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl group); Fig. 4A-C). Additionally, HFD-Ctrl mice exhibited impaired glucose tolerance and insulin resistance, as indicated by the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) (a mean difference of 59.10 (95% CI: −7.13 to 125.33, P < 0.05); HFD-Ctrl vs. LFD-Ctrl group; Fig. 4D, E) and insulin tolerance test (ITT) (a mean difference of 49.90 (95% CI: −1.03 to 100.83, P < 0.001); HFD-Ctrl vs. LFD-Ctrl group; Fig. 4F, G) compared to LFD-Ctrl mice. Notably, the impairments were further exacerbated in HFD-Perio mice. The results of Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) staining revealed that the accumulation of glycogen in hepatocytes was diminished in HFD-Ctrl mice as compared to LFD-Ctrl mice (a mean difference of − 19.64 (95% CI: −26.54 to − 12.74, P < 0.0001); HFD-Ctrl vs. LFD-Ctrl group; Fig. 4H, I). This accumulation decreased even further in HFD-Perio mice in comparison to HFD-Ctrl mice (a mean difference of − 8.57 (95% CI: −14.48 to − 2.66, P < 0.0001); HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl group; Fig. 4I). Then, we assessed the expression levels of genes associated with gluconeogenesis such as glucose-6-phosphatase (G6pase) and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (Pepck) within the hepatic tissue. The mRNA expressions of G6pase and Pepck exhibited a rise in HFD-Perio mice relative to HFD-Ctrl mice (G6pase (a mean difference of 0.922 (95% CI: 0.331–1.513, P < 0.05)); Pepck (a mean difference of 0.751 (95% CI: 0.160–1.342, P < 0.05)); HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl group; Fig. 4J, K). Subsequently, we investigated the expression of IRS1 and pIRS1 by Western blot. HFD-Perio mice exhibited increased levels of pIRS1Ser307/IRS1 relative to HFD-Ctrl and LFD-Perio mice with mean differences of 0.124 (95% CI: −1.070 to 1.318, P < 0.05; HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl group) and 0.895 (95% CI: −0.216 to 2.006, P < 0.05; HFD-Perio vs. LFD-Perio group; Fig. 4L, M). Elevated pIRS1Ser307/IRS1 is closely associated with insulin resistance. These findings demonstrate that experimental periodontitis exacerbates the disorder of hepatic glucose metabolism in HFD-fed mice.

Experimental periodontitis promotes hepatic and systemic inflammation and activates TLR4/JNK/NF-κB inflammatory signals in HFD-fed mice

Periodontitis induces gut dysbiosis and increases gut permeability, which leads to LPS leakage into the bloodstream. LPS translocates to the liver, where it activates the TLR4 signaling pathway21. Because TLR4 is instrumental in the onset of nonalcoholic steatohepatitis22,23, we measured blood LPS levels and the expression of TLR4 and its downstream signaling components in the liver. HFD-Perio mice exhibited elevated serum LPS levels and liver TLR4 levels when contrasted with those observed in HFD-Ctrl and LFD-Perio mice (LPS (a mean difference of 40.40 (95% CI: 12.83–67.97, P < 0.05)); TLR4 (a mean difference of 0.167 (95% CI: −0.323 to 0.657, P < 0.05)); HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl); (LPS (a mean difference of 39.70 (95% CI: 4.80–74.60 P < 0.05)); TLR4 (a mean difference of 0.548 (95% CI: 0.159–0.937, P < 0.05)); HFD-Perio vs. LFD-Perio; Fig. 5I-K). An elevation in the expression levels of downstream proteins p-JNK/JNK and NF-κB pP65/P65 was observed when comparing the HFD-Perio mice with the LFD-Perio mice (p-JNK/JNK (a mean difference of 1.426 (95% CI: 1.075–1.776, P < 0.0001)); NF-κB pP65/P65 (a mean difference of 0.744 (95% CI: 0.332–1.155,P < 0.001)); HFD-Perio vs. LFD-Perio; Fig. 5J, K). Activation of TLR4 and its downstream signaling molecules can induce hepatic inflammatory responses, which we subsequently assessed. The findings indicate that mice subjected to HFD-Perio demonstrated a notable increase in the infiltration of CD68+ macrophages (a mean difference of 0.928 (95% CI: 0.349–1.507, P < 0.001); HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl; Fig. 5A, C), hepatic coronal structures (a mean difference of 7.542mm2 (95% CI: 4.821–10.26mm2, P < 0.0001); HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl; Fig. 5A, D), and the level of TNF-α (a mean difference of 15.136 (95% CI: 10.843–19.429, P < 0.0001); HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl; Fig. 5B, E) when compared to HFD-Ctrl. Additionally, we explored the impact of periodontitis on systemic inflammation in HFD-fed mice. Consistent with our hypotheses, HFD-Perio mice showed significantly elevated serum content of TNF-α (a mean difference of 75.60pg/mL (95% CI: 45.05–106.15pg/mL, P < 0.01); HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl), IL-6 and IL-1β (mean differences of 11.47 pg/mL(95% CI: 3.48–19.46pg/mL) and 11.41pg/mL (95% CI: 4.15–18.67pg/mL); P < 0.05; HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl) relative to HFD-Ctrl mice (Fig. 5F-H). These findings suggest that experimental periodontitis not only intensifies the local inflammatory response within the liver, but also contributes to a heightened systemic inflammatory condition in mice consuming a HFD.

Experimental periodontitis alters gut microbiota composition in HFD-fed mice

How does periodontitis participate in the pathological process of MASLD mentioned above? Given the interconnection between oral and gut microbiota24 and the potential of periodontitis to trigger alterations in gut microbiota composition18, we conducted additional analyses into alterations in the gut microbiota. This study first conducted α-diversity analysis utilizing indices such as Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson. At the current sequencing depth, the curves of indices like Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson reached a stable state (Supplementary Fig. S4A-C), indicating that the study encompasses a sufficient quantity of sequencing data, effectively reflecting the comprehensive diversity present within the microbial community. The α-diversity analysis showed that HFD-Perio mice exhibited a significant reduction in Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices in comparison to LFD-Perio mice, indicating a deterioration in the microbiota’s richness and diversity (all P < 0.05; HFD-Perio vs. LFD-Perio; Fig. 6A-C). The analysis of β-diversity was illustrated through Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA), with PERMANOVA used to assess the statistical significance, revealing notable distinctions between HFD-fed and LFD-fed mice (Fig. 6D). PERMANOVA showed that microbial community distances did not differ significantly among mice within the same diet group, whereas a clear difference was detected between the HFD-Perio and HFD-Ctrl mice (R2 = 0.23, p = 0.001).To evaluate the alterations in microbiota composition after periodontitis intervention, at the phylum and genus levels, we characterized the composition of the gut microbiota. The intestinal microbiota of LFD-fed mice was predominantly composed of the phyla Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteria. However, Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Campylobacterota, and Desulfobacterota phyla were the dominant components of the gut microbiota in HFD-Ctrl mice (Fig. 6E). Campylobacterota was abnormally enriched in HFD-Perio mice. In HFD-Ctrl mice, a marked increase in the Firmicutes/Bacteroidota ratio was observed relative to LFD-Ctrl mice (a mean difference of 4.201 (95% CI: 1.33–7.07, P < 0.05; HFD-Ctrl vs. LFD-Ctrl), with an additional increase observed in HFD-Perio mice when contrasted with the HFD-Ctrl mice (a mean difference of 3.915 (95% CI: 1.05–6.78, P < 0.05); HFD-Perio vs. HFD-Ctrl; Fig. 6G). The Firmicutes/Bacteroidota ratio was used as a conventional descriptive index of microbial community alteration; however, it should be interpreted cautiously because it is not an unambiguous marker of gut microbiota dysbiosis25. Consistent with previous findings26, the gut microbiota exhibited significant differences between LFD-fed and HFD-fed mice. The predominant bacterial genera at the genus classification level were Firmicutes_unclassified, Helicobacter, Desulfovibrionaceae_unclassified, Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group in the HFD-Perio mice (Fig. 6F). Among them, potential pathogenic bacteria Helicobacter and Desulfovibrionaceae_unclassified were notably upregulated in HFD-Perio mice when contrasted with HFD-Ctrl and LFD-Perio mice.

Discussion

Our study provides novel insights into the association between experimental periodontitis and the progression of MASLD in HFD-fed mice, and suggests a potential interaction between periodontal inflammation and gut microbiota alterations. MASLD is an increasingly significant health issue worldwide7. Expanding evidence highlights that alterations in microbiota composition are a major contributor to the progression of HFD-related diseases, including MASLD27. MASLD is closely linked to disruption of intestinal homeostasis involving the gut microbiota28. Periodontitis is also highly prevalent worldwide and is correlated with several systemic conditions, including diabetes, atherosclerotic disease and MASLD4,5,29. Accumulating evidence suggests that periodontitis may disrupt the equilibrium of gut microbiota balance via the oral–gut axis17,24, with findings suggesting that salivary microbiota associated with periodontitis exacerbates MASLD in mice rendered obese through a high-fat diet. However, whether periodontitis worsens HFD-induced MASLD in vivo by altering the gut microbiota is still unclear. Our study demonstrated that experimental periodontitis intensified multiple pathological features associated with HFD-induced MASLD in mice, while also investigating the prospective contribution of periodontitis-associated gut microbial dysregulation to this effect.

Our hepatic histological and fibrotic findings indicate that periodontitis may shift HFD-induced simple steatosis toward a more inflammatory and fibrogenic phenotype. In this work, ligature placement was used to establish experimental periodontitis, the model that has been reported to recapitulate key features of human periodontitis30. We demonstrated that HFD-fed mice with periodontitis developed nonalcoholic steatohepatitis-like changes, including hepatocellular injury, inflammatory cell infiltration, and mild fibrosis. These observations are consistent with previous findings in MASLD models exposed to Porphyromonas gingivalis31,32 and periodontitis-derived salivary microbiota21. The Acta2 gene encodes α-SMA, a protein expressed by hepatic stellate cells and widely acknowledged as a significant marker of liver fibrosis33. In addition, an elevated expression of collagen genes type I (Col1) and type III (Col3) was observed both in the livers of individuals diagnosed with nonalcoholic steatohepatitis and in mice provided with a HFD, indicating their involvement in the progression of hepatic fibrosis33,34. Importantly, in our work, the levels of these fibrotic markers were further upregulated in HFD-fed mice with periodontitis, suggesting that periodontal inflammation may amplify HFD-induced fibrogenic responses in the liver.

The aggravation of MASLD by periodontitis appears to depend on a metabolically vulnerable HFD background. HFD feeding alone disrupted lipid metabolism and enhanced inflammatory cytokine expression. The coexistence of periodontitis further aggravated these abnormalities35. In our study, periodontitis exacerbated elevations in T-CHO and LDL-C in HFD-fed mice and correlated with higher serum ALT and AST levels, indicating more severe liver injury. Experimental periodontitis also intensified the systemic inflammatory response in HFD-fed mice, as reflected by increased circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β); these increased cytokines likely contributed to the worsening of MASLD. In contrast, LFD-fed mice displayed only mild inflammatory changes and did not develop the same degree of metabolic disturbance when periodontitis is present. Our results align with prior studies indicating that periodontitis alone is insufficient to induce overt metabolic disorders but can markedly amplify the risk when combined with an HFD background35,36.

Insulin resistance may represent a metabolic bridge through which periodontitis worsens HFD-induced MASLD. Previous studies have suggested links among periodontal infection, insulin resistance, and fatty liver progression, although findings regarding glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity have not always been consistent35,37,38. HFD-fed mice with periodontitis exhibited more pronounced insulin resistance, increased hepatic expression of gluconeogenic genes G6pase and Pepck, and reduced liver glycogen storage compared with HFD-fed mice without periodontitis. By contrast, LFD-fed mice remained lean and insulin sensitive, indicating that the metabolic impact of periodontitis depends strongly on the dietary background. Our study demonstrates that ligature-induced periodontitis exacerbates MASLD phenotypes in HFD-fed mice. Previous studies administering periodontal pathogens in MASLD/NAFLD models have reported inconsistent results, likely because of differences in HFD duration, mouse strain, sex, timing of infection, and the method used to induce periodontal disease. In our study, male C57BL/6J mice were fed a 60% kcal HFD for 12 weeks before 4 weeks of ligature-induced periodontitis, which differs from models using repeated oral gavage, topical inoculation, odontogenic infection, or salivary microbiota transfer. These approaches may differ in the intensity of local periodontal inflammation, bacterial exposure, gut microbiota disturbance, endotoxemia, and hepatic inflammatory or fibrotic responses. Thus, our findings should be interpreted in the context of a ligature-induced periodontal inflammatory model superimposed on established HFD-induced MASLD, which may explain differences from previous reports.

The LPS–TLR4–JNK/NF-κB axis provides a plausible inflammatory mechanism linking periodontitis-associated dysbiosis to hepatic injury. In this study, periodontitis was associated with increased hepatic macrophage activation and inflammatory responses in MASLD mice. Although macrophages are not classically viewed as insulin-responsive cells, they express insulin receptors and downstream signaling components, and insulin can modulate macrophage polarization toward pro- or anti-inflammatory phenotypes39. While direct evidence on insulin-resistant macrophages in MASLD is still limited, hepatic macrophages are known to aggravate inflammation and insulin resistance in obesity-associated metabolic disease. TLR4 has been implicated in nonalcoholic steatohepatitis22,23, and gut bacterial-derived LPS can reach the liver via the circulation and activate hepatic TLR4 signaling in HFD-fed mice21. Consistent with this, we found that periodontitis significantly increased serum LPS levels and hepatic TLR4 expression in MASLD mice, accompanied by activation of downstream signaling molecules JNK and NF-κB, which was associated with increased inflammatory responses. The JNK and NF-κB signaling pathways, when activated, can disrupt insulin signaling. This interference intensifies insulin resistance and steatosis and may potentially contribute to fibrosis40. Taken together, these observations suggest that experimental periodontitis is associated with a broad spectrum of hepatic pathological alterations in HFD-fed mice, including impaired insulin sensitivity, dysregulated lipid metabolism, heightened inflammation, and fibrosis. However, the causal mechanisms by which periodontitis may contribute to hepatic pathological alterations in HFD-induced MASLD mice remain to be further clarified.

The gut microbiota data support the interpretation that periodontitis may worsen MASLD by reshaping microbial communities toward a more pro-inflammatory and metabolically unfavorable profile. Periodontitis has the potential to induce dysbiosis in the gut microbiota18. Accumulating evidence indicates that dysbiosis is involved in the etiology of obesity, inflammatory conditions and impaired glucose metabolism20. Therefore, our investigation further explored the alterations in the gut microbiota of periodontitis mice under a HFD background to identify possible microbial associations linking periodontal inflammation with MASLD-related phenotypes. Our results showed that experimental periodontitis was associated with marked alterations in gut microbiota composition in HFD-fed mice and identified several bacterial taxa that may be relevant to MASLD progression. Although some findings are controversial, the Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group has been reported to have a strong connection with insulin resistance, metabolic endotoxemia, and hepatic inflammation in HFD-fed mice41,42. The Rs_E47_termite_group has been suggested to promote inflammation and weight gain in overweight individuals43. An increased abundance of Acetatifactor has been linked to intestinal inflammation, enhanced gut permeability and HFD feeding44,45. In our model, these taxa were enriched in MASLD mice with periodontitis, suggesting that they may be associated with disease worsening.

Conversely, the depletion of potentially beneficial taxa suggests that periodontitis-associated dysbiosis may also impair microbial functions that normally protect intestinal and metabolic homeostasis. In contrast, genera such as Allobaculum, Eisenbergiella and GCA_900066575 have been implicated in lipid, amino acid and carbohydrate metabolism, respectively, and their decreased abundance has been linked to conditions such as obesity and insulin resistance46. Notably, the synthesis of short-chain fatty acids by Allobaculum, Alistipes, and Phascolarctobacterium aids in the growth of beneficial microbes and help to maintain gut barrier integrity47,48. Dubosiella has been linked to bile acid metabolism and may promote fatty acid oxidation and energy expenditure49. Pseudoflavonifractor can transform flavonoids, which exhibit antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities in the gut mucosa50,51. Our research revealed a notable decrease in the prevalence of specific bacterial genera in HFD-fed mice and affected by periodontitis, which may reflect the loss of microbial functions that protect the body from inflammation and metabolic disorders. Overall, these observations support the idea that microbially derived metabolites and organic compounds are instrumental in sustaining gut homeostasis and that periodontitis-associated dysbiosis may disturb this balance.

Nevertheless, it is important to note that these findings mainly rely on taxonomic associations and predicted functional profiles. Direct measurement of microbial metabolites, including flavonoid-derived compounds, short-chain fatty acids and bile acids alongside targeted metabolomic analyses, are required to further validate the functional consequences of these microbial alterations. In addition, forthcoming research utilizing fecal microbiota transplantation, antibiotic depletion, or gnotobiotic mouse models will be crucial in establishing causal links between specific bacterial taxa and MASLD progression.

The predicted microbial functional changes further connect dysbiosis with the metabolic and inflammatory phenotypes observed in HFD-fed mice with periodontitis. Based on these findings, it is plausible that enriched genera such as Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, Rs_E47_termite_group and Acetatifactor in ligatured MASLD mice may act as potentially deleterious bacteria promoting disease progression, In contrast, diminished genera including Allobaculum, Eisenbergiella, GCA_900066575, Alistipes, Phascolarctobacterium, Dubosiella and Pseudoflavonifractor may exert protective or compensatory roles. PICRUSt2 functional predictions suggested that periodontitis was associated with further disturbances in carbohydrate and lipid metabolism in MASLD mice. Moreover, recent evidence indicates that excess uric acid produced by purine metabolism can exacerbate periodontitis and systemic inflammation in HFD-fed mice52, which is consistent with our observation of altered purine-related pathways. The MAPK signaling pathway, which was predicted to be up, is pivotal to cellular responses and can be triggered by both inflammation and impaired insulin signaling in periodontitis53. In addition, d-glutamine and d-glutamate metabolism is linked to hepatic gluconeogenesis and may influence glucose production and export54. The predicted shifts in microbial metabolic pathways in our study were generally in agreement with the documented rise in hepatic inflammation and disruption of glucose homeostasis. Moreover, the predicted reduction in terpenoid and polyketide metabolism, together with decreased DNA replication–related pathways, may indicate compromised defensive or regenerative capacity within the gut microbiota.

Although our findings support an association between periodontitis, gut microbiota dysbiosis, and MASLD aggravation, several limitations prevent direct causal conclusions. First, only male C57BL/6J mice were used in this study, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to female mice and other genetic backgrounds. Given that sex is an important biological variable, future studies including both sexes, additional mouse strains, and sex-stratified analyses are warranted. Second, samples were collected at a single experimental endpoint, precluding assessment of temporal changes in gut microbiota composition, hepatic inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and fibrosis during MASLD progression. Third, gut microbiota analysis was based on 16 S rRNA gene sequencing rather than shotgun metagenomics, and microbial functions were predicted rather than directly measured. Therefore, the taxonomic resolution and functional implications of the observed microbial alterations should be interpreted cautiously. Direct measurement of microbial metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, flavonoid-derived compounds, and other microbiota-derived metabolites, is needed in future studies. Fourth, some MASLD-related readouts were not fully assessed in the periodontitis-only group without HFD in all figures, which may limit interpretation of the independent effects of periodontitis under non-HFD conditions. Fifth, although the Firmicutes/Bacteroidota ratio was reported as a conventional descriptive index, it should not be regarded as an unambiguous or universally reliable marker of dysbiosis and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Finally, although our findings are consistent with a potential gut-mediated link, this mechanism was not directly demonstrated in the present study. Oral microbiota were not sampled, and bacterial translocation from the oral cavity to the gut or liver was not directly traced. Thus, the proposed oral–gut–liver mechanism should be regarded as a hypothesis requiring further validation. Future studies using fecal microbiota transplantation, antibiotic depletion, bacterial tracing, oral microbiota profiling, shotgun metagenomics, targeted metabolomics, or gnotobiotic mouse models will be required to establish causality between periodontitis-associated microbial alterations and MASLD progression.

Collectively, these findings indicate that experimental periodontitis is associated with more severe HFD-induced MASLD phenotypes, including impaired hepatic glucose metabolism, aggravated hepatic lobular inflammation and fibrosis, and sustained steatosis in HFD-fed mice. Our data also suggest a hypothetical oral–gut–liver axis in which periodontal inflammation may be linked to gut microbiota dysbiosis and functional reprogramming. Furthermore, this dysbiosis may compromise intestinal homeostasis and contribute to metabolic endotoxemia, as indicated by elevated circulating LPS. Increased LPS and pro-inflammatory cytokines may then be associated with enhanced hepatic innate immune activation involving TLR4 and downstream JNK/NF-κB signaling. This process may contribute to inflammatory injury, a pro-fibrogenic microenvironment, and metabolic dysregulation. Elevated serum LPS and pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in HFD-fed mice with periodontitis further underscore the systemic impact of periodontitis. However, this proposed mechanism remains inferential and requires direct experimental validation. Future studies using fecal microbiota transplantation, antibiotic depletion, bacterial tracing, oral microbiota profiling, or gnotobiotic mouse models are needed to establish causality and to determine whether periodontitis-associated microbial alterations directly drive MASLD progression. Combined, our results stress the potential significance of preserving periodontal health and implementing effective oral hygiene practices as integral components of a comprehensive management strategy for individuals at risk of, or currently experiencing MASLD.

Materials and methods

Animal experiment

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines and were approved by the Experimental Animal Research Ethics Committee of Jilin University (protocol No. SY202110001; see Institutional Review Board Statement). Male C57BL/6J mice, aged 5 weeks and weighing 18–20 g, were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (China). The mice were acclimated for one week and then housed at a temperature range of 22–24 °C, with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, and granted unrestricted access to water and standard rodent chow. Since periodontitis and bone phenotype are susceptible to sex hormone levels, the biological variable of sex was not investigated in this study. In order to generate reliable scientific data, this study made every effort to minimize animal usage and reduce any suffering. Animal experiment schedule as shown in the Supplementary Fig.S1.

MASLD and the ligature-induced periodontitis model

Each animal was assigned a unique number, and groups were determined using a random number table. Forty mice were randomly divided into two dietary groups and fed either a low-fat diet (LFD; 10% kcal from fat; Research Diets, Cat. No. D12450B, USA) or a high-fat diet (HFD; 60% kcal from fat; Research Diets, Cat. No. D12492, USA) to establish the diet-induced MASLD model (n = 20 per group). The HFD (or LFD) feeding started at week 0 and continued for 12 weeks. Liver steatosis was observed in mice following 12 weeks of HFD feeding55. Based on whether they underwent ligation, the mice were split into two distinct subgroups. These subgroups were named LFD-Ctrl, LFD-Perio, HFD-Ctrl, and HFD-Perio (n = 10 per group). Subsequent to grouping, the animals were prohibited from entering the cages of other groups. The ligature-induced periodontitis was conducted using sterile 7 − 0 silk sutures (Chenghe Microsurgical Instruments Factory, China), as described previously (Supplementary Fig. S2)56. Following intraperitoneal administration of pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg) as anesthesia, the ligature was carefully tied to bilateral maxillary second molars in mice for 4 weeks. Anesthesia and sham surgery were performed on the mice in the control group. Mice with sutures were routinely examined twice a week and if they fell off, the tooth was ligated anew. Over the entire experimental period, the mice were meticulously monitored for any variations in body weight and signs of discomfort to ensure their welfare was prioritized. The experimental endpoint was at week 16. Upon reaching the experimental endpoints, the mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation, followed by cervical dislocation as a secondary physical method to confirm death. Blood specimens were obtained through cardiac puncture and then processed by centrifugation (1,000 × g, 4 °C) in order to procure serum, which was subsequently preserved at -80 °C. Maxillae and livers were excised and weighed expeditiously and imaged. The maxillae and a segment of the liver specimens were immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, while the rest of the liver tissue was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and archived at -80 °C for further experimental analysis.

A total of 40 mice were initially included in this study, with 10 mice assigned to each group. The initial allocation of 10 mice per group was intended to ensure sufficient sample availability during the long-term feeding and model-establishment period and for different downstream analyses. At the experimental endpoint, six mice per group were randomly selected for the main histological, biochemical, and molecular measurements and statistical analyses. This random selection was performed before endpoint assessment and was not based on experimental results. The remaining animals were not used for additional unreported experiments. For gut microbiota analysis and fecal samples from five mice per group and Western blot analysis from three mice per group were used, due to differences in sample type requirements, sample availability, and experimental design.

Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), insulin tolerance test (ITT), and homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR)

Prior to the OGTT, mice underwent a fast period of 16 h prior to receiving oral glucose administered at 2 g per kg of body weight. The ITT test followed three days later, during which mice underwent 4 h fast before being administered an intraperitoneal injection of 0.75 IUkg − 1 insulin. Tail vein blood samples were taken during the OGTT and ITT tests, with glucose concentrations evaluated at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min utilizing a OneTouch Ultra blood glucose meter (Acucheck, Germany). For every test conducted, the area under the glucose curve (AUC) was computed. Fasting blood glucose levels and insulin concentrations in the tail blood were quantified utilizing a glucometer and an insulin ELISA kit (Meimian Biotech, China), respectively. Insulin resistance was evaluated by the HOMA-IR index, calculated using the following formula: HOMA-IR = (fasting blood glucose in mg/dL)×(fasting insulin in mIU/mL)/405.

Micro-CT and histologic analyses of maxillae

The maxillae were subjected to scanning via Micro-CT (Scanco Medical, µCT50, Switzerland), and the reconstructed images were analyzed with ANT software (Skyscan, Belgium) to evaluate various bone parameters. Alveolar bone loss was quantified by measuring the linear distance from the cement-enamel junction to the alveolar bone crest, along with the bone volume/tissue volume ratio. The maxillae were then decalcified for two weeks at room temperature in a pH 7.3, 4% ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid solution and embedded in paraffin wax. Histological alterations were assessed by staining paraffin sections (3 μm) from the maxillae with H&E. Additionally, TRAP (Sigma, USA) staining was performed to detect multinucleated osteoclasts, as described previously57. Following staining, the sections were photographed and quantified to determine the numbers of inflammatory cellular infiltrates and TRAP+ osteoclasts per unit area.

Liver histology and immunohistochemistry analysis

Staining techniques

Hepatic sections in paraffin were subjected to staining with H&E, PAS (Yuan Ye Bio-Technology Co, Ltd, China), and Masson’s Trichrome assay (Solarbio, China). Liver frozen sections (6 μm) were processed with an Oil red O assay to evaluate lipid accumulation (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). Subsequently, the sections were examined microscopically, and a semiquantitative analysis was conducted. To establish the NAFLD activity score (NAS), hepatic steatosis (0–3), ballooning (0–2), and lobular inflammation (0–3) were evaluated. In addition, the sections were classified according to the cumulative scores of hepatic steatosis, ballooning, and lobular inflammation58.

Immunohistochemistry analysis

Liver sections in paraffin were subjected to immunohistochemistry staining utilizing primary antibodies targeting CD68 (1:200; ProteinTech, USA; Cat No. 25747-1-AP), α-SMA (1:200; ProteinTech, USA; Cat No. 14395-1-AP), and TNF-α (1:200; ProteinTech, USA; Cat No. 17590-1-AP) overnight incubation in a humidified chamber at 4 °C. The tissue sections were treated with HRP-labeled secondary antibodies for a duration of 60 min at ambient temperature. Chromogenic detection was performed using diaminobenzidine (Solarbio, China) and counterstaining was executed with Hematoxylin. In this study, phosphate-buffered saline served as the negative control. Following this, under a microscope, the sections were examined, and a semiquantitative assessment was performed.

Gut microbiota analysis

Samples of feces (n = 5 per group) were gathered, preserved at -80°C, and forwarded to LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (China) for bacterial 16S rRNA sequencing, as outlined earlier59. Briefly, fecal samples were processed for total genomic DNA isolation using the CTAB-based protocol, adhering to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was employed to amplify the V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA with primers 341 F (5′-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′) and 805 R (5′-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′). The thermal cycling protocol comprised a first denaturation at 98 ℃ for 30 s, succeeded by 32 cycles consisting of 10 s at 95 ℃, 30 s at 54 ℃, 45 s at 72 ℃, and was completed by a final extension at 72℃ for 10 min. Following validation by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, the PCR products were cleaned using AMPure XT beads (Beckman Coulter Genomics, USA) and their concentrations were determined with a Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA). The resultant product of amplification was utilized to prepare the sequencing library. The sequencing data were further analyzed by QIIME260. Downstream microbiome statistical analyses and visualization were performed using R software (version 3.4.4), LEfSe, and STAMP as appropriate. LEfSe was used for differential taxa analysis and generation of the cladogram and LDA score plots. PICRUSt2 was employed to predict microbial functions based on KEGG pathways, and STAMP was used for statistical comparison and visualization of the predicted functional pathways. A sliding window approach was applied to evaluate the quality of sequencing reads using fqtrim. The window size was set to 100 bp. When the average Phred quality score within the window was below 20, the reads were truncated from the window’s start to the 3’ end. Key parameters: -P33-w100-q20-l100-m5-p1-V-o trim.fastq.gz. We applied the Kruskal–Wallis nonparametric test to examine intergroup variation in proportions of organisms present at both the phylum and genus levels.

16 S rRNA gene sequencing data were processed using QIIME2 (version 2019.7). Paired-end reads were quality filtered and denoised using DADA2 (qiime dada2 denoise-paired), generating ASV sequences and abundance tables, with singleton ASVs removed. Because an ASV-based denoising strategy was used, no OTU clustering threshold was applied. Taxonomic assignment was performed using the QIIME2 feature-classifier plugin with the SILVA database (Release 138, min confidence 0.7) and the NT-16 S database (Release 20230718, min identity 90%, min coverage 80%, max e-value 1e-5). Alpha diversity was calculated at a rarefaction depth of 49,629 reads using seven indices: Observed_species, Shannon, Simpson, Chao1, Goods_coverage, Pielou_E, and ACE. Beta diversity was assessed using Weighted UniFrac, Unweighted UniFrac, Jaccard, and Bray-Curtis distances.

Based on the taxonomic abundance profiles, differential abundance analysis was performed among comparison groups. Statistical methods were selected according to the sample design. Fisher’s exact test was used for comparisons without biological replicates; the Mann–Whitney U test, also known as the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, was used for two-group comparisons with biological replicates; and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for multiple-group comparisons with biological replicates. An initial significance threshold of P < 0.05 was applied. For multiple comparisons, P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate method, and adjusted q-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Blood sample assay

Using ELISA kits (Meimian Biotech, China), the serum concentrations of pro-inflammatory markers, such as TNF-α (Cat. No. MM-0132M2), IL-1β (Cat. No. MM-0040M2), IL-6 (Cat. No. MM-0163M2), and LPS (Cat. No. MM-0634M2), were evaluated. Quantification of serum TG, T-CHO, HDL-C, LDL-C, AST, and ALT was performed with commercial enzymatic assay kits (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China).

The qRT-PCR

Cryopreserved mouse liver samples were used for total RNA extraction, followed by qRT-PCR as previously detailed61. The quantification of gene expression levels was performed utilizing the 2-ΔΔCt methodology. Supplementary Table S1 lists the specific primers used in this study.

Western Blot

Total protein was extracted and subjected to western blot as described previously61. Primary antibodies used in this study included pIRS1(1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Cat No. 2381T), IRS1(1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Cat No. 2382 S), NF-κB p-P65(1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Cat No. 3033T), P65(1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Cat No. 8242T), p-JNK(1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Cat No. 4668T), JNK (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Cat No. 9252T), TLR4 (1:4000; ProteinTech, USA; Cat No.19811-1-AP), and GAPDH (1:8000; ProteinTech, USA; Cat No.10494-1-AP). Densitometric analysis was conducted using ImageJ software (Bethesda, USA). GAPDH was used as the loading control for total protein expression. TLR4 expression was normalized to GAPDH. For phosphorylation-dependent signaling analyses, phosphorylated proteins were normalized to their corresponding total protein levels, including pIRS1Ser307/IRS1, p-JNK/JNK, and NF-κB p-P65/P65. These phospho/total protein ratios were used as activation or inhibitory readouts where appropriate.

Statistical analyses

All statistical evaluations for non-microbiome data were carried out in GraphPad Prism 9.0. Microbiome statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 3.4.4), LEfSe, and STAMP as appropriate. Based on the taxonomic abundance profiles, differential abundance analysis was performed among comparison groups. For two-group comparisons with biological replicates, the Mann–Whitney U test, also known as the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, was used where appropriate; for comparisons among multiple groups with biological replicates, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used. LEfSe was used to identify differentially abundant taxa and to generate the cladogram and LDA score plots. STAMP was used for statistical comparison and visualization of PICRUSt2-predicted KEGG pathways. All results are reported as mean ± SD. One-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, was used for statistical comparisons across multiple groups. For time-course data, including glucose values at serial time points, two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple-comparison procedure was performed to assess the interaction between time and treatment. P < 0.05 was interpreted as statistically significant.
