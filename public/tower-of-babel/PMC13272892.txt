Addressing Climate Driven Vector‐Borne Disease in West Africa
Authors: Majani Edward, Oladipupo Iyanu Roseline, Emmanuel Victor, Oladeji Yinmi Gabriel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionVector‐borne diseases (VBDs) pose a significant public health threat in West Africa, contributing to substantial morbidity and mortality, particularly among vulnerable populations. This region is highly susceptible to the effects of climate change due to its geographical location, dependence on rain‐fed agriculture, and fragile ecosystems. This narrative review explores the complex interplay between climate change and the escalating burden of VBDs, including malaria, dengue, chikungunya, Zika, and Lassa fever, to inform a coordinated regional response.MethodsAppropriate publications from 2010 to 2025 were identified from scientific databases (e.g., Google Scholar, PubMed) and reports from international bodies (WHO, Africa CDC, WAHO). The review included articles that presented empirical data, modeling studies, and policy analyzes contextual to West Africa. A total of 21 papers were selected for a detailed narrative synthesis to discuss climate drivers, challenges, and policy recommendations.ResultsClimate change, characterized by rising temperatures, altered precipitation, and increased extreme events, profoundly influences vector biology, distribution, and disease transmission dynamics. Optimal temperature ranges and recurrent flooding create ideal breeding grounds, exacerbating outbreaks. West Africa's challenges are compounded by socioeconomic factors like deforestation and rapid urbanization, which strain fragile healthcare systems. The VBD burden results in a severe economic toll on productivity and trade, disrupting social stability and hindering progress toward the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Key findings point to the urgent need for a holistic response.ConclusionAddressing this multifaceted challenge requires a coordinated regional effort focusing on strengthening cross‐border disease surveillance, establishing real‐time data‐sharing and early warning systems, and implementing Integrated Vector Management (IVM). This article emphasizes the urgent need for political commitment, sustained funding from regional and international partners, and collaborative efforts to build climate‐resilient health systems and effectively combat the growing threat of vector‐borne diseases in West Africa.

Introduction

Vector‐borne diseases remain a global public health threat and are responsible for more than 70,000 deaths annually [1]. These diseases are spread by insects known as vectors such as mosquitoes, ticks, and flies. They predominantly affect low‐ and middle‐income countries as they are often called poverty sicknesses [1].

In Sub‐Saharan Africa, especially West Africa, many people suffer from these diseases because of demographic and socioeconomic factors such as poor healthcare, bad sanitation, climate change, and urbanization [2]. Over the past two decades, floods have impacted millions across West Africa including Nigeria in 2022, Ghana in 2019, and Burkina Faso (2023). These events are linked to numerous adverse health effects, ranging from injuries and waterborne diseases to long‐term mental health challenges and disruptions in healthcare services [3]. In recent years, West Africa has continually experienced resurgence and emergence of various vector‐borne diseases ranging from malaria, Rift Valley fever, dengue, yellow fever, Leishmaniasis to Trypanosomiasis, contributing significantly to morbidity and mortality, particularly among children under five and pregnant women [1].

Although malaria remains the most pervasive vector‐borne disease, with endemic transmission across all West African countries [4], other vector‐borne diseases such as dengue fever and chikungunya have re‐emerged in epidemic proportions in parts of the region, posing a growing public health challenge due to changing vector dynamics and increasing urban populations [5]. Zika virus, although, has had limited outbreaks in recent years, remains a potential health concern in West Africa. Several countries in the region are considered at risk based on past evidence of transmission, seroprevalence studies, and the presence of competent mosquito vectors [1]. West Africa records an estimated 100,000 to 300,000 fever cases annually, resulting in approximately 5000 deaths in Nigeria, Liberia and Sieria Leone [6].

Furthermore, Rift Valley Fever, a zoonotic arboviral disease, has had sporadic outbreaks in countries such as Mauritania and Niger, often triggered by periods of intense rainfall and flooding, which facilitate the proliferation of mosquito breeding sites. West Africa is increasingly recognized as one of the most climate‐vulnerable regions in the world, owing to its geographic location, fragile ecosystems, dependence on rain‐fed agriculture, and limited adaptive capacity [7]. Over the past few decades, the region has experienced climate change leading to significant shifts in climatic parameters, such as rising temperatures, altered precipitation patterns, sea level rise, and increased frequency of extreme weather events. These changes have profound implications for human health and particularly encourage the proliferation of vector‐borne diseases [8, 9, 10].

As climate change continues to redefine ecological boundaries and human‐environment interactions, understanding its role in shaping the epidemiology of vector‐borne diseases is vital. This study seeks to explore how the observed and projected trends in West Africa's climate are contributing to the spread of vector‐borne diseases, with a focus to equip West Africa with a coordinated response.

Methodology

This article was developed through a standard literature review focusing on exploring the linkage between climate change and vector‐borne diseases in West Africa. Appropriate publications were sorted and identified from scientific databases which include Google Scholar, PubMed, ScienceDirect. Reports from international bodies such as WHO, Africa Center for Disease Control, the West Africa Health Organization. The review included publications from 2010 to 2025 with a group of keywords. These keywords include climate change, vector‐borne diseases, West Africa, malaria, Rift Valley fever, and so forth. Only articles that are contextual to West Africa and present empirical data, modeling studies and policy analyzes.

Publications and articles that are not contextual to West Africa were not included. Although global reviews were consulted but regional evidence was prioritized when available [8, 11].

Following the initial screening we got 150 articles, then we performed a full‐text review. 32 articles were included in the final analysis as they provided specific data and comprehensive discussion on the studed matter. We ultimately selected 21 papers for a detailed narrative synthesis. The extracted data from these papers included study setting, sample source, and key point about the climate driven VBD. The synthesis of this data formed the basis for our discussion on the climate change as a driver of VBD, challenges, Implication and recommended actions.

Climate Change as a Driver

Climate change is driving both the endemicity and escalating threat of diseases such as malaria, dengue, and Rift Valley fever. This climate change‐driven spread and transmission of vector‐borne diseases (VBDs) is a significant global health and environmental issues [9]. This case is even more noteworthy in Africa where social, economic, and health inequalities are at the core of recurring public health crises [10]. Regional studies identify shifts in temperature and rainfall patterns as a factor that reshapes the habitats of mosquitoes. These changes also extend transmission seasons across West African countries [4, 11].

Specifically, rising temperature, shift in rainfall pattern and relativity all contribute to influence the biology of most vectors. Empirical analyzes have shown quantifiable relationships between climatic change and the dynamics of vector‐borne disease in West Africa. In Guinea Savanna, it was found that a 1°C rise in average temperature is linked to a 10% increase in the number of Anopheles mosquito and this contributes its extension of transmission seasons [4]. Similarly, in northern Cote d'Ivoire, a 10 mm weekly increase in rainfall corresponded to a 6%–8% increase in the incidence of malaria [12]. Also, a study by [5] found that relative humidity above 60% sustains the survival of Aedes aegypti, thereby enhancing the transmission of dengue in Burkina Faso and Senegal. Rift Valley Fever outbreaks occur after heavy rainfall that floods mosquito habitats, especially in Mauritania and Niger (WHO, 2023). The reported reemergence of chikungunya in Senegal and Mali was also linked to increasing humidity and temperature [5]. According to a report by the Center for Disease Control in 2023, the low level transmission of Zika was driven by urban Aedes aegypti.

Temperature changes influence the survival of vectors and the transmission of VBDs in a non‐linear, n‐shaped pattern. Transmission risk rises with increasing temperature until it reaches an optimal range, then declines as temperatures continue to rise. Outside this optimal range, vector survival decreases, lowering disease transmission potential and hindering parasite development [13]. Similarly, mosquito‐borne diseases such as malaria, dengue, and Zika are strongly influenced by temperature, which affects mosquito development and survival. Their transmission typically occurs between 9.0°C and 38.0°C, with an optimal range of 23.0°C–29.0°C [13]. The recurrent annual flooding in West Africa poses significant risks for the transmission of vector‐borne diseases [3]. A 2021 study in northern Côte dÍvore demonstrated that malaria incidence rose sharply following seasonal floods. A 24% increase in malaria cases was observed in districts that experienced flooding within 6 weeks of heavy rainfall compared to districts without flooding [12]. Statistical analysis solidified a strong positive correlation between relative humidity above 70%. Specifically, they found that periods following flooding showed a notable rise in malaria cases, suggesting that stagnant water left by floods creates breeding grounds for Anopheles mosquitoes. Additionally, high relative humidity was statistically linked to greater mosquito survival and activity, thus contributing to sustained malaria transmission [12]. These environmental changes alter vector behavior and habitat suitability and this shapes the ecology and transmission patterns of vector‐borne diseases across West Africa.

Climate models project that malaria transmission zones in West Africa could expand northward by up to 16% by 2050, exposing an additional 30‐50 million people to seasonal risk [4]. The study by Ko et al., 2024 also projected a warning of 1.5°C–2°C by mid‐century may increase dengue transmission potential by 20%−25% in coastal West African cities [5].

Risk Factors

The susceptibility of the region to climate change is a result of a combination of geographical, and social‐economic factors.

Geographical Factors

West Africa, located in the tropical zone (between latitude 40N and 200N), is characterized with increased sunlight radiation. This results in increased temperature and evaporation which in turn result into variability in rainfall and extreme temperature conditions [14].

Its proximity to the Atlantic Ocean also influences its climate significantly via the Monsooon effect. The coastal region experiences higher humidity while inland suffer from droughts [15]. The topography also contributes to different climate change. Places with extremely high mountains such as Jos Plateau in Nigeria and Fouta Djallon Highlands in Guinea experience heavy rainfall while low—lying valleys are more prone to flooding [16].

Social‐Economic Factors

Socio‐economic vulnerabilities such as poor housing, weak health infrastructure, and urban crowding increase transmission and persistence of vector‐borne diseases. High dependence on agriculture and increased migration and urbanization in the region results in continuous and rapid deforestation [17] These are achieved with different methods such as burning, inordinate use of fertilizers affects the climate systems such as changing rainfall patterns, reduced evapo‐transpirations and accumulation of greenhouse gasses [18].

The increasing population also serve as a risk factor predisposing the region to climate change. Reasons such as increased demand for resources lead to inappropriate practices such as increased urbanization, that increase the probability of climate change [19, 20].

Challenges and Implications

VBDs, including malaria, yellow fever, dengue, and Lassa fever, remain significant public health challenges in West Africa. The burden of these diseases affects not only individual countries but the region as a whole, with implications for healthcare systems, economic stability, social cohesion, and the achievement of health‐related sustainable development goals (SDGs). Countries such as Nigeria, Ghana, Côte d'Ivoire, Sierra Leone, Liberia, and Burkina Faso are frequently affected by these diseases, resulting in significant health, economic, and social consequences [21, 22]. The impact on healthcare systems is profound. West African countries such as Nigeria, Ghana, Côte d'Ivoire, and Mali face constant strain from the high incidence of VBDs. Malaria remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in the region, with Nigeria accounting for 27% of global malaria cases [21]. Similarly, countries like Burkina Faso, Ghana, and Senegal report significant malaria‐related mortality. Additionally, yellow fever outbreaks have recently affected countries like Guinea, Côte d'Ivoire, and Ghana, highlighting the gaps in routine immunization coverage and surveillance [1]. These diseases divert essential resources from other public health priorities, delay progress toward Universal Health Coverage (UHC), and compromise routine health services.

The economic toll of VBDs is severe. Malaria alone reduces GDP growth by up to 1.3% annually in endemic African countries [23]. The World Bank (2021) notes that malaria alone results in productivity losses of over $12 billion annually in Africa. Countries such as Burkina Faso and Sierra Leone experience frequent disease outbreaks that hinder economic development, particularly in rural and agricultural sectors [27]. VBDs outbreaks disrupt tourism, cross‐border trade, and discourage investment, particularly in countries such as Sierra Leone and Guinea, which have experienced recurrent Lassa fever outbreaks [28].

VBDs also disrupt social stability. Outbreaks often result in school closures, movement restrictions, and misinformation, leading to public fear and mistrust in health authorities. Vulnerable populations, particularly women and children, are disproportionately affected due to limited access to care and education [24]. Additionally, the spread of misinformation and mistrust of public health authorities complicates efforts to control disease transmission [25]. These societal disruptions weaken social cohesion and increase inequality, further eroding progress on several Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).

The ongoing burden of VBDs poses a serious threat to SDG 3, which aims to ensure healthy lives and promote well‐being for all [20]. The persistence of diseases like malaria and yellow fever impedes efforts to reduce preventable deaths and combat communicable diseases. The indirect effects on SDG 1 (No Poverty), SDG 4 (Quality Education), and SDG 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth) are equally significant, contributing to cycles of poverty and stunted socio‐economic development.

Policy and Adaptation Recommendations

A coordinated, evidence‐based response in West Africa is vital for mitigating the growing threat of climate‐driven vector‐borne diseases. The West African Health Organization (WAHO) should lead the development of a regional framework for cross‐border surveillance, ensuring that data on vector populations, climate factors, and disease incidence are shared in real time among member states. Combining meteorological data with public health surveillance would enable the creation of early warning and response systems that can anticipate outbreaks before they worsen [4].

National governments should adopt Integrated Vector Management (IVM) strategies that combine environmental sanitation, insecticide‐treated nets, larviciding, and climate‐informed vector control. Predictive rainfall and temperature models can guide the timing of interventions, improving efficiency and resource allocation [4]. Cross‐border collaboration particularly among neighboring nations such as Ghana, Togo, Côte d'Ivoire, and Burkina Faso is essential to harmonize policies, coordinate vaccination campaigns, and standardize diagnostic and reporting protocols under the ECOWAS framework.

Sustained progress will require investment in research and local innovation. Strengthening the capacity of national and regional research institutions to study vector ecology, insecticide resistance, and disease modeling will generate the data needed to design context‐specific interventions. Furthermore, community engagement and health education programs should be prioritized to enhance awareness, promote behavioral change, and encourage local ownership of preventive practices.

Finally, political commitment and long‐term financing are indispensable. Governments, regional bodies, and international partners must incorporate vector‐borne disease control into National Adaptation Plans (NAPs) and allocate dedicated climate–health funding. Building climate‐resilient health systems, supported by sustainable financing and governance, will strengthen West Africa's ability to prevent and respond to future vector‐borne disease threats.

Conclusion

Vector‐borne diseases remain a major public health issue in West Africa, affecting the healthcare systems and vulnerable populations [1, 22] The sustained and increased burden of Vector‐borne diseases is strengthened by which destabilize vectors and their habitats, prolong transmission seasons and also increase geographical coverage of vector‐borne diseases [9, 11]. Ranging from dengue, the commonest vector‐borne diseases in West Africa to dengue and chikungunya, the impact of vector‐borne diseases extend beyond health affecting economic stability and progress towards Sustainable Development Goals [23].

Climate change characterized by increased temperatures, erratic rainfall, continuous occurrence of flood and others is no longer a distant threat as assumed. Climate change in collaboration with harmful human action such as poor sanitation, inadequate housing, weak public health systems is linked to the prevalence and distribution of vector‐borne diseases in the region [10].

Addressing climate change, a driver of vector‐borne diseases, as a public health concern necessitates a more holistic approach. A holistic approach is capacitated to strengthen health systems, promote climate resilience and improved vector management [22] A holistic approach involving political commitment from governments in the region to build internationally effective health surveillance systems, improved healthcare delivery system and research capacity to study disease patterns [22] Community‐based approach will be effective to empower individuals with knowledge to prevent transmission of vector‐borne diseases.

Lastly, regional bodies and international funders need to continually identify climate change as a public health concern for a cross border approach to vaccination, funding and technologies.
