Coracoclavicular ligament reconstruction versus hook plate treatment in patients with Rockwood III-VI acute acromioclavicular dislocation: a multicentre randomized controlled trial
Authors: Qihang Fang, Qi Zhu, Shengdi Lu, Lei Shao, Wenjun Zhang, Qinggang Sun, Wenqi Song, Youshui Gao, Pei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn 9–12% of shoulder girdle injuries in athletes, acromioclavicular (AC) joint dislocations disproportionately affect young adults who are engaged in contact sports. Surgical reconstruction has emerged as the gold standard for Rockwood III–VI lesions, particularly in professional athletes, where conservative management is correlated with high recurrence rates according to recent meta-analyses. However, the optimal surgical management for acute Rockwood type III–VI AC dislocations remains controversial, with no consensus on the most effective technique. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of two prevalent surgical techniques: hook plate fixation and coracoclavicular ligament reconstruction using a ligament augmentation and reconstruction system (LARS).MethodsThis study is designed as a multicentre, single-blinded, two-arm, parallel-group randomized controlled superiority trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio. Hook plate (arm 1) and LARS ligament (arm 2) will be compared. The primary outcome will be the Disability Arm Shoulder and Hand (DASH) score. The secondary outcomes will include imaging evaluations of re-dislocation, the quality of life 12-item survey questionnaire (SF-12), the visual analog scale (VAS), the Constant–Murley-Shoulder joint score, and complications. Since treatment allocation involves surgery and X-ray access for patients and surgeons, only researchers who analyze the outcome data after 1 year of follow-up will be blinded to the allocation. A total of 72 participants will be included, and the estimated time to complete this trial will be approximately 2 years. All the work will be conducted at the National Orthopedic Medical Center and its partner trauma healthcare network.DiscussionAs the optimal surgical method for severe AC joint dislocation remains uncertain, this study highlights the differences in short-term and middle-term outcomes between hook plate and LARS ligament surgical treatments, which may hopefully reveal the most effective surgical interventions.Trial registrationThis study is registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400082168) and was granted permission by the institutional ethics committee of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital on 02-29-2024. https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj=222307Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13063-026-09846-6.

Introduction

Background and rationale

Acromioclavicular (AC) joint dislocation is a common clinical shoulder injury that mainly occurs in young people [1] and athletes [2], accounting for approximately 9% to 12% of all shoulder injuries [1, 3–6]. In the general population, AC dislocation has an incidence of 3–4 cases per 100,000 persons annually [7]. Acute AC dislocations are common and devastating for patients because they suffer from local tenderness and excruciating pain in the shoulders [8]. After surgery, there is still a high risk of recurrence and duration of the average full recovery for athletes with Rockwood III–VI dislocations is approximately 5.7 months [9].

This injury usually occurs in athletic and traumatic settings, with Rockwood classifications III–VI designating injuries with complete ligamentous disruption and superior displacement of the clavicle relative to the acromion [6, 8]. Currently, the consensus is that Rockwood I and II are primarily managed with nonsurgical treatment [5, 6, 10]. The treatment approach for type III remains controversial [11], whereas types IV–VI are predominantly treated surgically [6, 7, 12, 13]. Surgical options are diverse, with clinical practice mainly categorized into rigid fixation and elastic fixation [1, 11, 13]. The clavicular hook plate, a traditional representative of rigid fixation, has demonstrated reliable efficacy [13] but is possibly associated with various complications [14] At present, the most common technique of rigid fixation is the use of a hook-shaped steel plate, which is fixed on the clavicle by screws, crosses the acromioclavicular joint, and maintains reduction by placing the hook beneath the acromion [13]. While securing the hook plate to the clavicle, there is a risk of damaging the subacromial structures and causing acromial erosion [7]. The hook plate requires a secondary surgery for plate removal [13].

In recent years, many scholars have proposed the use of suspensory elastic fixation devices, such as the LARS® (Ligament augmentation and reconstruction system—Dijon, France) for anatomic coracoclavicular ligament reconstruction (ACCR) [13, 15, 16]. This surgical technique accomplishes anatomical reconstruction and results in high level of residual pain relief and functional preservation [16–18]. Many clinical studies have already confirmed its validity. In a prospective study early in 2013, LARS has been proven to be a safe alternative surgical method for AC joint dislocations through performing radiological and evaluation examinations [16]. Similarly, several retrospective studies also achieved similar results that LARS is valid for acute AC joint dislocations and might be acceptable for chronic dislocations as well [19, 20]. Another study of 47 patients with Rockwood III–V injuries revealed that LARS restores joint stability and especially elasticity [21]. Compared with the usage of hook plates, one recent meta-analysis summarized 14 relevant studies and noted that LARS has fewer complications and better recovery outcomes [14]. Another systematic review published in 2023 revealed that LARS results in the shortest recovery duration of 3.6 months among athletes, as compared with other techniques [9]. In addition to repairing the coracoclavicular ligaments alone, combining the reconstruction of the coracoclavicular and the acromioclavicular ligaments appears to be a successful surgical method with few complications in Rockwood stage IIIB–V AC joint dislocation patients [22–24]. The combined use of ligament reconstruction and hook plate is also superior to use hook plate alone in Rockwood III and V type of AC joint dislocations as was revealed in a prospective randomized controlled trial [25]. Although ligament reconstruction is endorsed by international societies because of its superior clinical and radiological outcomes [26], the use of hook plate is valuable because it provides acceptable results at a lower cost and is suitable for specific AC joint dislocation types [27]. From radiographic reports and relevant evaluation tests, it seems that LARS is an effective method for AC joint dislocation. However, there is still a lack of high-quality clinical evidence confirming the difference in efficacy between traditional rigid fixation (hook plate) and elastic fixation (LARS ligament) in AC joint dislocations of Rockwood grades III–VI [7, 9, 11, 13, 14, 28, 29].

Objectives

This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of these two prevalent surgical techniques for patients with acute AC dislocation in terms of functional outcome within a 1-year follow-up. Through providing level I clinical evidence, the findings will provide evidence-based guidance for the selection of surgical methods in clinical practice. Furthermore, the outcomes will hopefully improve patient prognosis and functional recovery while optimizing healthcare resource utilization and achieving favorable health economic outcomes.

Trial design

This study is designed as a multicentre, single-blinded, two-arm, parallel-group randomized controlled superiority trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio. The trial will compare hook plate fixation with coracoclavicular ligament reconstruction using a ligament augmentation and reconstruction system (LARS) in patients with acute Rockwood type III–VI acromioclavicular joint dislocations.

Participants will be randomly assigned to either the hook plate fixation group or the LARS ligament reconstruction group. Owing to the nature of the surgical interventions and the requirement for postoperative radiographic assessment, blinding of participants and surgeons is not feasible. Therefore, outcome assessors and statisticians will remain blinded to treatment allocation.

This protocol is reported according to the SPIRIT Statement for protocols of randomized trials, and the completed SPIRIT checklist is provided as an additional file.

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting

This multicentre trial will be conducted at the National Center for Orthopaedics, Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital, and its partner trauma healthcare network in Shanghai, China. Participating centres include Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital, Jinshan Branch of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital, Zhujiajiao People’s Hospital of Shanghai Qingpu District, and Shanghai Huangpu Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine.

All participating sites are academic or regional hospitals with dedicated orthopaedic trauma services. Participants will be recruited from orthopaedic outpatient clinics and inpatient wards at these centres. All trial data will be collected in China. The list of study sites is provided in the supplementary file and will also be available in the clinical trial registry.

Participants

The eligible population will include adult patients who are diagnosed with acute AC joint dislocations, at the Emergency or Outpatient Department of the National Orthopedic Center or one of the other participating hospitals.

Interventions

For participants allocated to the LARS ligament reconstruction group, surgery will be performed by an experienced orthopaedic surgeon. The patient will be placed in a beach-chair position, and a 5–6 cm incision will be made from the AC joint to the surface of the coracoid. The deltoid muscle will be partially incised to expose the AC joint and coracoid process. Two oblique, non-collinear bone tunnels will be drilled into the superior clavicular cortex, maintaining anterior and posterior cortices for structural integrity. A LARS-specific hook instrument will be passed mediolaterally beneath the coracoid to guide a metallic loop, which facilitates threading the LARS ligament under the coracoid and into the clavicular tunnels. After retrieving the hook, the ligament ends are freed and passed through the medial and lateral tunnels. The medial end is fixed to the clavicle with a conical non-absorbable interference screw. Following joint reduction, the lateral end is similarly secured with another interference screw. Finally, the debrided AC joint is reinforced by reconstructing torn capsules to restore anteroposterior stability [16].

In the hook plate group, following reduction of the AC joint, the hook component of the plate will be positioned beneath the posteroinferior aspect of the acromion. Definitive fixation will be achieved by inserting screws through the plate into the clavicle. The surgical wound will be then closed in layers, including reconstruction of the torn deltotrapezial fascia to restore soft-tissue integrity. Hook plate removal will be routinely scheduled at 5.5 months postoperatively as part of the standardized treatment pathway for the hook plate group [30].

The same postoperative rehabilitation protocol will be applied to both groups. Active motion without resistance will be encouraged from the first postoperative day. A shoulder sling will be worn for 3 weeks after surgery [15].

Strategies to improve adherence to intervention

To promote adherence to the intervention protocols, all participating surgeons and research staff will receive trial-specific training before trial initiation. Surgical procedures will be performed according to predefined operative protocols for hook plate fixation and LARS coracoclavicular ligament reconstruction. Perioperative management and postoperative rehabilitation will follow standardized institutional pathways at all participating centres.

Intervention adherence will be monitored using operative records, postoperative radiographs, follow-up records, and case report forms. The trial coordinator will review the completeness of intervention-related documentation at regular intervals. Any deviation from the allocated intervention, postoperative treatment pathway, or follow-up schedule will be recorded in the case report form and reviewed by the Trial Management Group (TMG).

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is the DASH score at 12 months after surgery. The DASH is a 30-item patient-reported questionnaire used to assess disability and symptoms of the upper limb. The total score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater disability.

Participant withdrawal and discontinuation criteria

Participants may withdraw from the trial at any time without providing a reason. Participants may also be discontinued from follow-up if they are unable to attend scheduled assessments due to personal reasons or unavoidable circumstances.

Participants may discontinue or modify the allocated intervention if they experience serious adverse events, request discontinuation, develop postoperative complications requiring alteration of the planned treatment pathway, show clinically significant deterioration, or if the treating surgeon considers discontinuation or modification to be in the participant’s best interest.

Discontinuation or modification of the allocated intervention will not necessarily constitute withdrawal from the trial. Participants who discontinue or modify the allocated surgical intervention will be encouraged to remain in follow-up, and all scheduled outcome data will continue to be collected unless the participant withdraws consent for further data collection. Data collected before withdrawal will be retained and included in the intention-to-treat analysis unless the participant requests removal of their data is permitted by applicable regulations. The reason for discontinuation, modification, or withdrawal will be documented in the case report form.

Sample size

The sample size calculation is based on the primary outcome measure, the Disability Arm Shoulder and Hand Score (DASH). Previous studies report an average DASH score of 6.4 (standard deviation [SD] 2.6) at 1-year follow-up for patients with acromioclavicular joint dislocation treated with autologous tendon reconstruction of the coracoclavicular ligament. For hook plate treatment, the average DASH score at 1 year is reported as 25.42 (SD 8.34). With a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a power of 90%, accounting for a 20% loss to follow-up, a total of 72 participants were needed. These patients were divided into two groups, the hook plate group and the LARS ligament reconstruction group, with 36 participants per group.

Concomitant care

The trial interventions will not replace usual perioperative and postoperative care. Participants in both trial arms will receive standard perioperative management, analgesia, wound care, thromboprophylaxis where clinically indicated, and postoperative rehabilitation according to institutional practice.

Concomitant medications or treatments considered clinically necessary by the treating surgeon will be permitted and documented in the case report form. Participants will not be permitted to receive additional surgical or interventional treatments for the index acromioclavicular joint injury that are expected to directly affect the primary outcome, unless clinically required for participant safety. Participation in another interventional clinical trial during the study period will not be permitted. Any use of rescue treatment or protocol-relevant concomitant therapy will be recorded.

Methods: assignment of intervention

Randomization and blinding

Randomization will be performed using a centralized computer-generated randomization system with allocation concealment. Minimization will be used to balance treatment allocation by study centre and sex, incorporating a random component. The allocation sequence will be generated and maintained by an independent statistician not involved in recruitment or outcome assessment.

Follow-up and medical measures after the completion of the study

The follow-up period during the study is 12 months, and patients can voluntarily visit our outpatient department irregularly after the study is completed. Implement corresponding medical measures based on the chief complaint and clinical diagnosis.

Observation of adverse events

Prevention and handling of risks

The clavicle hook steel plate may have complications such as subacromial bone resorption and acromioclavicular arthritis. Patients should undergo hook plate removal 5.5 months after surgery to remove the implants and reduce the risk of these complications. Patients undergoing LARS ligament reconstruction may experience complications such as loosening of the nail track.

All adverse events and serious adverse events occurring from randomization to the final follow-up visit will be recorded in the case report form. Adverse events will include, but are not limited to, wound infection, neurovascular injury, implant failure, redislocation, acromial osteolysis, subacromial impingement, acromioclavicular arthritis, coracoid or clavicle fracture, thromboembolic events, reoperation, and any unexpected medical event. Serious adverse events will be reported to the ethics committee and relevant institutional authorities according to local regulatory requirements.

Methods: data collection, management, and analysis

Data collection methods and management

Baseline characteristics, intervention-related information, postoperative outcomes, imaging findings, patient-reported outcomes, complications, and adverse events will be collected using standardized case report forms developed by the investigators for this trial. Data sources will include participant interviews, medical records, operative records, imaging examinations, outpatient follow-up records, and standardized questionnaires.

Data will be collected at baseline, during hospitalization, and at scheduled postoperative follow-up visits. Outcome assessors will receive standardized training before trial initiation to ensure consistency in data collection. Completed case report forms will be checked by the trial coordinator for completeness, internal consistency, and missing values. Missing, inconsistent, or implausible data will be queried and resolved by referring to source documents where possible.

Data will initially be recorded on paper-based case report forms and subsequently entered into a password-protected electronic database by trained research staff. Each participant will be assigned a unique trial identification number, which will be used on all case report forms and in the electronic database. Direct personal identifiers will be stored separately from clinical trial data.

Data entry will be checked by a second member of the research team. Range checks and logic checks will be used to identify missing, inconsistent, or implausible values. Paper case report forms will be stored in locked cabinets in a secure research office at the coordinating centre. If paper CRFs are generated at participating centres, they will be delivered securely to the coordinating centre by designated trial personnel or transferred as encrypted scanned copies before filing. The electronic database will be stored on a secure institutional server with access restricted to authorized trial personnel only.

Screening and randomization data will be entered by the trial coordinator or delegated research staff. Outcome data will be collected by trained assessors who are blinded to treatment allocation. Data management procedures will be reviewed periodically by the TMG.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses will be performed according to a prespecified statistical analysis plan before database lock and unblinding of treatment allocation. The primary analysis will be conducted on the intention-to-treat population, defined as all randomized participants analyzed according to their assigned treatment group, irrespective of protocol deviations, treatment crossover, or withdrawal from the allocated intervention. A per-protocol analysis will also be performed as a sensitivity analysis, including participants who receive the allocated intervention and complete the primary endpoint assessment without major protocol deviations.

Baseline characteristics will be summarized by treatment group. Continuous variables will be presented as mean and standard deviation if approximately normally distributed, or as median and interquartile range if markedly skewed. Categorical variables will be summarized as frequencies and percentages. No formal statistical significance testing will be performed for baseline characteristics; clinically relevant imbalances will be described and considered in adjusted analyses where appropriate.

The primary outcome will be the DASH score at 12 months after surgery. The between-group difference in the 12-month DASH score will be analyzed using an analysis of covariance model, with treatment group as the main fixed effect and baseline DASH score as a covariate. Study centre and sex, which are included in the randomization minimization procedure, will also be included as covariates. Rockwood classification may be included as an additional adjustment variable if clinically relevant imbalance is observed between groups or if prespecified in the statistical analysis plan. The treatment effect will be reported as the adjusted mean difference between the LARS ligament reconstruction group and the hook plate fixation group, with a 95% confidence interval and two-sided P value.

Repeated measurements of DASH, VAS, Constant–Murley score, and SF-12 score across follow-up time points will be analyzed using linear mixed-effects models. The models will include treatment group, time point, and the treatment-by-time interaction as fixed effects, with participant-level random intercepts to account for within-participant correlation over time. Baseline values of the corresponding outcome, study centre, and sex will be included as covariates where appropriate. The treatment-by-time interaction will be used to assess whether the postoperative recovery trajectory differs between treatment groups. Model-based estimated marginal means and between-group differences at each follow-up time point will be reported with 95% confidence intervals.

Radiographic outcomes, including coracoclavicular distance and the percentage change in coracoclavicular distance compared with the contralateral healthy side, will be analyzed as continuous variables using linear regression or linear mixed-effects models, depending on whether a single endpoint or repeated measurements are analyzed. Redislocation or loss of reduction, if defined as a binary outcome, will be compared between groups using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate according to expected cell counts. Complications and adverse events will be summarized by treatment group and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. For binary outcomes, risk differences or relative risks with 95% confidence intervals will be reported where appropriate.

Continuous outcome data will be assessed for distributional assumptions by inspection of histograms, Q-Q plots, and model residuals. If substantial deviations from model assumptions are identified, appropriate transformations, robust standard errors, or non-parametric methods will be considered as sensitivity analyses. All tests will be two-sided, and a P value of less than 0.05 will be considered statistically significant for the primary outcome. Secondary outcomes will be interpreted as supportive and exploratory; no formal adjustment for multiplicity will be applied unless otherwise specified in the statistical analysis plan.

Missing data will be minimized through standardized follow-up procedures, reminder calls, and review of case report forms for completeness. The extent and pattern of missing data will be summarized by treatment group and follow-up time point. The primary analysis will use all available data under the assumption that data are missing at random. If the proportion of missing data for the primary outcome is clinically relevant, multiple imputation using chained equations will be performed as a sensitivity analysis, incorporating treatment group, baseline characteristics, baseline outcome values, available follow-up outcome values, study centre, sex, and Rockwood classification into the imputation model. Additional sensitivity analyses may include complete-case analysis and per-protocol analysis to assess the robustness of the primary findings.

All analyses will be conducted using SPSS, R, or equivalent validated statistical software. The final statistical software and version will be reported in the trial publication.

Methods: oversight and monitoring

Trial oversight will be provided by a TMG, consisting of the principal investigator, co-investigators from participating centres, trial coordinator, data manager, and trial statistician. The TMG will be responsible for the day-to-day conduct and organizational coordination of the trial, including recruitment monitoring, staff training, protocol adherence, review of case report form completeness, data quality checks, adverse event review, and coordination among participating centres.

The TMG will meet monthly during the recruitment and intervention period and every 3 months during the follow-up period, or more frequently if required. Minutes of each meeting will be documented and retained in the trial master file. The trial coordinator will perform regular checks of case report forms and source documents. At least 20% of case report forms will be randomly checked against original medical records, operative records, imaging reports, and questionnaire data to verify accuracy and completeness. Protocol deviations and breaches will be documented using a deviation or breach report form and reviewed by the Trial Management Group. The sponsor, ethics committee, or institutional regulatory body may audit trial records at any time according to institutional requirements.

Because this trial compares two routinely used surgical treatments for acute Rockwood type III–VI acromioclavicular joint dislocations, does not involve an investigational medicinal product, and has no planned formal interim efficacy analysis or statistical stopping rule, an independent Data Monitoring Committee will not be established. Participant safety and trial conduct will instead be monitored by the TMG and the principal investigator, with serious adverse events reported to the ethics committee according to institutional requirements.

Discussion

This multicenter single-blind randomized controlled trial will elucidate the efficacy of two representative surgical techniques for the management of acute AC joint dislocation: rigid fixation with hook plate versus elastic fixation with LARS ligament. Hook plate fixation has been the historical mainstay for its immediate mechanical stability [27]. However, its drawbacks are also significant, including frequent hardware-related complications such as acromial osteolysis and subacromial impingement necessitating mandatory secondary removal surgeries [7, 13]. These issues amplify morbidity, delay rehabilitation, and increase healthcare burdens. Clavicular fixation replicates the conoid/trapezoid footprints, while coracoid fixation provides suspensory stabilization [13, 15, 32]. Non-anatomical fixation is not absolutely without merit. One meta-analysis summarized 18 biomechanical testing studies and pointed out that non-anatomical fixation techniques performed better than the minimum acceptable level of stability and failure loads in biomechanical tests [33]. Providing more cost-effective outcomes and reduced surgery duration, its supporters' viewpoint holds that hook plate fixation is paving its way for normal individuals and cost-sensitive populations [33]. In one comparative trial, hook plate group exhibited better results in radiographic outcomes and reduction maintenance than that of synthetic ligament reconstruction technique [34]. It remains to be elucidated whether this finding is applicable for the comparison with LARS technique.

These biomechanical insights support the use of LARS in our protocol as a flexible alternative to rigid fixation, potentially offering better restoration. In other ligament reconstruction methods, joint stability is restored by drilling graft tunnels in the coracoid. In the bone tunnel technique, drilling tunnels compromises the structural integrity of the coracoid and significantly increases fracture risks. As is reported in one systematic review, the post-surgery complication of coracoid fracture is predominantly associated with tunnel drilling [35–38]. Conversely, the LARS technique does not seem to have this concern [39]. The suspension method avoids drilling tunnels and loops sutures/grafts around the coracoid base. One systematic review of three studies (n = 114 patients) using LARS for AC joint reconstruction reported only one coracoid fracture (less than 1%) [40]. A retrospective comparative study compared the tunnel-less and single-tunnel suspension fixation. In treating acute Rockwood III + acromioclavicular dislocations, it seems that suspension group does not have fracture risks (0–1%) while the tunnel drilling group has high fracture incidence (4.8–20%) [40–42]. Furthermore, operative duration also significantly varies in suspension method (46.76 ± 5.01 vs 64.70 ± 5.72 min) [42]. Although suspension technique does not have fracture risks, it may shift stress to other bones and compromise bone integrity [43]. To sum up, the suspension technique ensures the coracoid preservation, trading rigidity for reduced complications, making it preferable for patients with higher fracture susceptibility.

Unlike rigid fixation, the LARS technique is highlighted with low hardware attrition risks, strong biomechanical stability, and anatomically precise fixation without clavicular erosion [14]. The LARS artificial ligament is composed of polyester fibres with sufficient strength for coracoclavicular ligament reconstruction (2500 or 3600 N corresponding to 60 or 80 fibres) [44]. As is pointed out in one in vitro study, both vertical and horizontal stability are efficiently restored in LARS repair compared to other techniques (LockDown, Endobutton, and Neoligament) [45, 46]. The paradigm shift from rigid fixation to biological reconstruction reflects evolving recognition of long-term joint biomechanics. Hook plate rigidity compromises physiological micromotion, potentially accelerating acromioclavicular arthrosis [7], whereas LARS facilitates dynamic stabilization by integrating synthetic ligaments with host tissue [14]. In a 3-year follow-up study, LARS artificial ligament reconstruction exhibits excellent clinical and radiographic outcomes in high-level AC joint dislocations (Rockwood type III or higher) [44]. The effectiveness of LARS is faster than that of rigid fixation. In 1 month post-surgery, LARS is capable of restoring joint stability, ensuring natural elasticity, relieving pain and improving joint function [21]. Despite these advantages, LARS adoption for AC joints remains limited by sparse high-level clinical comparative data [7, 9, 11, 13, 14, 28, 29]. To be noted, for artificial ligament grafts, florid foreign body reaction is the main cause of decreased tensile force and second operation [47].

However, a few limitations may influence the outcome of our trial. As there are discrepancies in surgery method and surgical indications, unreasonable selection of patient cases can significantly affect the comparability of this trial. To address this issue, it requires high-quality AC joint cases inclusion. Only fracture types that are currently considered acceptable for both surgical methods will be included. Furthermore, rigid fixation and LARS provide different biomechanical traits and thus bring about a totally different rehabilitation process and unsimilar complications. We will focus on the overall functional rehabilitation and middle-term outcomes to avoid the limited comparability caused by technique differences.

Our study aims to provide a short-term and middle-term outcome difference between hook plate and LARS ligament surgical treatments, which contributes to the effective surgical interventions for acute acromioclavicular dislocation.
