The extravascular plaque modulation technique: A novel strategy to facilitate guidewire crossing in noncrossable calcified tibial chronic total occlusions
Authors: Karthigesu Aimanan, Michael Arvind, Putera Mas Pian, Hanif Hussein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Severely calcified infrapopliteal chronic total occlusions (CTOs) remain a major limitation in endovascular limb salvage, particularly when guidewire crossing cannot be achieved despite advanced techniques. We describe a novel plaque modification technique, termed the extravascular plaque modulation (EPM) technique, designed to facilitate guidewire crossing in noncrossable calcified tibial CTOs. The technique involves minimal skin access and controlled external mechanical modulation of the calcified arterial segment using artery forceps under fluoroscopic guidance. The EPM technique was applied in three patients with chronic limb-threatening ischemia and focal, heavily calcified anterior tibial artery CTOs resistant to conventional wire escalation and needle-based techniques. Following EPM, successful intraluminal guidewire crossing was achieved in all cases, allowing subsequent balloon angioplasty and restoration of inline flow. No procedural complications were observed. At follow-up of up to 6 months, all treated vessels remained patent, with complete wound healing. The EPM technique represents a minimally invasive bailout strategy to enable guidewire crossing in otherwise uncrossable calcified tibial CTOs.

Severely calcified tibial chronic total occlusions (CTOs) represent one of the most challenging lesion subsets in endovascular limb salvage. Circumferential calcium limits guidewire penetration and reduces procedural success rates. In such cases, conventional intraluminal strategies may fail, necessitating subintimal recanalization or surgical bypass.

Plaque modification techniques such as atherectomy, intravascular lithotripsy, and needle-based approaches including PIERCE and hydraulic “fracking” have improved outcomes in selected cases.1,2 However, these strategies rely on intraluminal or intraplaque modification, and lesions with dense circumferential calcification may remain resistant, particularly when initial guidewire crossing cannot be achieved.

A minimally invasive extravascular approach to modify plaque and facilitate guidewire penetration may provide an alternative solution.

Technique description

We describe a novel plaque modification technique, termed as the extravascular plaque modulation (EPM), developed as a bailout strategy to facilitate guidewire crossing in noncrossable calcified tibial CTOs. The aim of the EPM technique is to redistribute mechanical stress within circumferential calcium, inducing microstructural disruption and improving lesion compliance, thereby enabling subsequent guidewire passage.

Following failure of conventional guidewire escalation and adjunctive needle-based techniques, the calcified occlusion is first localized using orthogonal fluoroscopic projections to ensure precise targeting (Fig 1, A). The overlying skin is subsequently marked under ultrasound guidance to confirm the surface projection of the target vessel. A small skin incision, approximately 5 to 8 mm in length, is then made at the marked site (Fig 1, B). Under continuous fluoroscopic guidance, the calcified arterial segment is gently engaged externally. The jaws of the forceps are partially opened and applied in a controlled and incremental manner along the lesion (Fig 1, C). This maneuver produces mechanical modulation of the plaque, altering its rigidity rather than forcefully crushing the vessel. Successful engagement of the calcified segment is confirmed fluoroscopically by visible movement of the calcified vessel wall during forceps application, serving as a real-time indicator of effective modulation.

In all three cases, successful intraluminal guidewire crossing was achieved following application of the EPM technique. Balloon angioplasty restored inline flow to the foot, and final angiography demonstrated satisfactory luminal gain without flow-limiting dissection or distal embolization. Representative angiographic images from one case are shown in Figs 1 and 2.

All three patients presented with chronic limb-threatening ischemia and focal (defined as ≤2 cm in length), heavily calcified anterior tibial artery CTOs. In each case, conventional wire escalation, including high tip-load wires, and adjunctive needle-based plaque modification techniques failed to achieve lesion crossing. Following EPM, successful guidewire penetration was achieved, enabling definitive angioplasty.

There were no procedural complications, including vessel rupture, hematoma, distal embolization, or compartment syndrome. At follow-up of up to 6 months, all treated vessels remained patent; patency was confirmed by arterial duplex ultrasound and photoplethysmography for pedal waveform assessment. Complete wound healing was achieved in all patients, with no requirement for reintervention.

Discussion

This study describes a novel minimally invasive extravascular plaque modification technique specifically designed to facilitate guidewire crossing in noncrossable calcified tibial CTOs.

The conceptual basis of the EPM technique differs from existing strategies. Needle-based approaches such as the “fracking” technique modify calcification from within using hydraulic pressure, whereas intravascular lithotripsy delivers sonic energy to disrupt calcium.1,2 In contrast, the EPM technique applies controlled mechanical modulation externally, targeting circumferential calcium without additional intraluminal instrumentation.

This extravascular approach enables modification of plaque compliance while preserving the intraluminal environment. The likely mechanism involves redistribution of mechanical stress within calcified plaque, resulting in microfractures that permit effective transmission of force from the guidewire tip. However, it should be noted that the precise mechanism cannot be confirmed from clinical observation alone; vessel positional displacement may contribute alongside true plaque microfracture, and future biomechanical studies are warranted to characterize the effect of external modulation on calcified arterial segments.

The EPM technique differs from previously described extravascular approaches in that it requires only minimal soft tissue dissection and is specifically intended to enable initial guidewire penetration.3 From a clinical perspective, this technique may be particularly useful in focal calcified tibial CTOs in which conventional and needle-based strategies have failed, and where retrograde pedal access is either unavailable or has been unsuccessful, potentially avoiding subintimal recanalization or bypass surgery.

In the cases presented, the EPM technique was performed in a hybrid operating theater setting, which is currently the preferred environment given the small skin incision involved and the availability of immediate open surgical backup should vascular or soft tissue complications arise. With growing operator experience and accumulated procedural data, it is anticipated that the technique may be performed safely in future in a standard catheterization laboratory setting, subject to appropriate patient selection and institutional infrastructure.

The EPM technique may represent a paradigm shift in the management of noncrossable calcified tibial CTOs by introducing an extravascular strategy specifically aimed at enabling guidewire crossing.

Safety considerations and limitations

Several potential adverse events merit careful considerations. Vessel rupture or transmural injury may occur if excessive force is applied; the incremental and controlled nature of the modulation maneuver is therefore essential. Distal embolization of calcified debris is a theoretical risk inherent to any plaque modification strategy and was not observed in the present series; postprocedural angiographic assessment of the distal runoff is recommended in all cases. Neurovascular injury, including the paired tibial vein and the deep peroneal nerve in the case of anterior tibial artery lesions, is possible if tissue dissection is injudicious; careful blunt dissection under combined fluoroscopic and ultrasound guidance is recommended. Wound complications, including hematoma, infection, and dehiscence, are recognized risks in the context of chronic limb-threatening ischemia with associated tissue compromise. Compartment syndrome, although not observed in this series, is a theoretical concern if significant fascial disruption occurs and should be monitored for in the postoperative period. The present series is limited by its small sample size (n = 3) and the absence of a comparator group, precluding definitive conclusions regarding safety and efficacy. The current experience is restricted to single-site focal lesions of ≤2 cm; the applicability of the EPM technique to longer lesions or multisite calcification requires further evaluation. Follow-up was limited to 6 months, and longer-term patency and durability of data are needed.

Conclusions

The EPM technique is a novel, minimally invasive bailout strategy that facilitates guidewire crossing in otherwise uncrossable calcified tibial CTOs. This technique may expand the endovascular options available for limb salvage in complex infrapopliteal disease. Further studies are required to establish safety and durability.
