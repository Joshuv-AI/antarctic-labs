A Call to Investigate the Association Between Histoplasmosis and Fatigue
Authors: Christian Hendrix, Julio Zuniga-Moya, Andrej Spec, Patrick Mazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHistorically, infections are subjectively associated with fatigue, a common yet poorly delineated syndrome. The impact on patient quality of life is the ultimate justification to evaluate fatigue in patients with infections. While fungal infections are subjectively associated with fatigue, there are minimal quantifiable fungal research outcomes that evaluate histoplasmosis and fatigue.

In the current literature, many clinical diagnoses have been studied regarding reported associations with fatigue. Commonly studied examples to date include clinical diagnoses such as cancer and viral infections, whether Epstein-Barr virus or Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 virus [1–3]. Within studies, there are multiple explanations theorizing the causes of fatigue within infections, whether muscle metabolism or inflammatory cytokine reactions that affect cognition [2]. However, there is limited information regarding the quantifiable identification of an association between fatigue and fungal infections. Thus, the current project investigated perceived fatigue in patients with a fungal infection—histoplasmosis.

Fatigue is often described as a persistent sense of tiredness and lack of energy not relieved by rest. The underlying pathophysiology of fatigue within infectious presentations has not been fully delineated. Barriers to studying this aspect of fatigue include a subjective experience not consistently screened with evidence-based practices [4–6]. Following author-observed postinfectious fatigue anecdotally in clinic patient populations, this project investigated the ability to evaluate outcomes of fatigue within fungal infections.

One aim of the research would support future incorporation of systematic screening for fatigue throughout the clinical course of histoplasmosis to allow evidence-based clinician counseling. In this study, patients with histoplasmosis were evaluated based on demographics and associations with comorbid conditions to characterize the patient population. Histoplasma capsulatum can cause fungal infections, specifically histoplasmosis. From anecdotal clinical experience, patients with a diagnosis of histoplasmosis frequently report feeling fatigued even after treatment for their infection. Due to subjective observations of postinfectious syndromes, this study aimed to establish whether a quantitative association between fungal infections and chronic fatigue could be evaluated. The purpose of the current study was to establish a relationship between histoplasmosis diagnosis and fatigue.

METHODS

In this study, we queried a database of patients with histoplasmosis within the Washington University Invasive Fungal Infections Clinic. Next, we called these patients to implement a retrospective analysis using a self-administered computer survey. Survey items consisted of demographic information, medical information, and subjective health experiences.

Demographic information included date of birth, relationship status, education level, employment, gender, and zip code. The social vulnerability index (SVI) was calculated by evaluating the patient zip code through the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s SVI Interactive Map. Medical information included possible comorbid conditions, date of histoplasmosis diagnosis, date of histoplasmosis hospitalization, date of intensive care unit (ICU) admission, immunosuppressed status, substance use, and psychiatric comorbid conditions. Subjective symptoms were captured with the National Institutes of Health Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy–Fatigue Scale (FACIT-Fatigue) survey instrument, which is validated for the general population. Fatigue was defined as a t score >50 on the FACIT-Fatigue survey instrument [7]. This corresponded to a raw mean score of 24. Demographic and medical information were evaluated to identify any potential associations between them and fatigue scores.

Each patient received a minimum of 1 telephone call attempt, conducted on any day of the week between 7 Am and 8 Pm, as an invitation to participate in the study. When contacted, patients were eligible to participate in the study, which required survey administration. On verbal phone consent, the survey was emailed for self-administration online via REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture).

All patients >18 years old who had a histoplasmosis diagnosis through 2021 and underwent treatment were eligible for inclusion. Patients without updated contact information were excluded. On survey completion, participants received a $10 gift card. This study was approved by the Washington University Institutional Review Board. Descriptive statistics were completed due to the limited size of this patient population, which did not allow quantification or causality analyses.

The average age of participants was 55 years; there were 34 female and 23 male participants, and 7 participants did not specify their gender. Most survey responders were white and had at least completed high school. Thirty respondents were employed, and 20 of those who completed the survey noted that they were immunocompromised.

Of 38 participants hospitalized due to histoplasmosis, 12 required an ICU admission. The median time from diagnosis to survey completion was 10 years (interquartile range [IQR], 4–19 years). Across national SVI categories, PROMIS FACIT-Fatigue scores varied; the score was ≥24 in 4 of 5 participants with an SVI of 0, in 11 of 23 with an SVI of 1, in 10 of 15 with an SVI of 2, and in 16 of 19 with an SVI of 3.

The scores from the PROMIS FACIT-Fatigue survey (PROMIS Short Form v1.0 13a) of study participants were evaluated by comparing scores in patients with histoplasmosis with the mean score in the standardized general population. Fatigue was positively associated with a histoplasmosis diagnosis. The average PROMIS FACIT-Fatigue raw score in study participants was 32, above the mean score of 24 in the standardized reference. Most patients in this study had elevated quantifiable scores compared with the general population scores. Four participants had fatigue scores that were less than the mean, and 29 had scores 1.5 standard deviations above the mean FACIT-Fatigue t score. Finally, 53.1% of study participants reported that they did not feel cured at the time of the survey.

Median scores were analyzed in relation to age, ICU hospitalization, and SVI. The median age (IQR) was 61 (51–66.5) years for participants with a PROMIS FACIT-Fatigue raw score <24 and 51 (43–64) years for those with a score ≥24 (P = .20; Wilcoxon rank sum analysis). The median PROMIS FACIT-Fatigue raw score for patients who required ICU hospitalization was 34.5 (IQR, 27.2–40.2) (P = .71; Kruskal-Wallis analysis). Finally, the median PROMIS FACIT-Fatigue raw score was 29 (IQR, 29–37) for participants with an SVI of 0, 23 (19–38) for those with an SVI of 1, 33 (21.5–42.5) for those with an SVI of 2, and 39 (29.5–42.5) for those with an SVI of 3; there was no significant difference between these medians based on SVI category (P = .2; Kruskal-Wallis analysis). A Fisher analysis comparing SVIs in patients with fatigue scores <24 or ≥24 did show a statistically significant difference (P = .03).

DISCUSSION

This retrospective analysis demonstrated multiple important findings. First, PROMIS FACIT-Fatigue scores can be used as a model to assess chronic fatigue in patients with histoplasmosis infections. Second, this study did not find an association between fatigue and age, a major potential confounder of concern. Third, PROMIS FACIT-Fatigue scores in this cohort of patients with prior histoplasmosis diagnoses were higher than in the general population. These findings are from the first study using standardized assessment tools to characterize subjective experiences of histoplasmosis.

While fatigue is a subjective syndrome, we developed an objective method to evaluate fatigue through a validated survey completed by patients with histoplasmosis. This method can be further developed with future studies on a larger scale. Median age and ICU admission rates did not differ between those with PROMIS scores <24 or ≥24 in our study population. These findings are thought to be due to small sample size; thus, a larger study with complex modeling is indicated to explore potential associations. Due to the limited number of eligible participants even within a large database, there was no statistical analysis for causality. Fisher analysis also demonstrated a statistically significant association between higher SVIs and PROMIS FACIT-Fatigue raw scores. However, this difference would need to be further evaluated in a larger sample with enough power to assess these relationships, as there was no difference in the median scores on Kruskal-Wallis analysis. Currently, most publications regarding histoplasmosis focus on diagnosis and treatment. Future multicenter analyses would enable samples large enough to conduct comparison and statistical analyses of patient demographics, including age, ICU hospitalizations, and SVIs.

Limitations of the current study include the different time points from Histoplasmosis diagnosis to survey administration among the respondents. Other limitations were the racially homogeneous population and the fact that the responses for gender included only 2 options, which may affect trust in medical establishments and survey participation. In this study, the gender prevalence had a higher percentage of females. This study was also only available in English, which is a limitation. This study had 84 eligible participants, and only those who consented after being contacted on the phone were offered the survey. Thus, there are limitations to comparing groups of patients with histoplasmosis. As another limitation, not all participants answered the survey question about immunocompromised status. Finally, the survey did not ask about preexisting pulmonary emphysema, a potential confounding factor for fatigue evaluations.

Still, this is the first study showing an elevated PROMIS FACIT-Fatigue score for the majority of patients after diagnosis of the fungal infection histoplasmosis. The FACIT-Fatigue scores were positively associated with histoplasmosis among eligible participants, with raw scores greater than the mean in the general population. Elevated scores were not accounted for by common confounders, such as age or ICU admission. This study involved the novel use of a validated survey tool to evaluate fatigue in patients with histoplasmosis. However, future studies will be needed to investigate associations between histoplasmosis and fatigue, given the unknown baseline frailty and possible survivorship or response bias in our patient population.

Ultimately, this exploratory study establishes a method that can be used in the future and on a larger scale to quantify positive associations between histoplasmosis and fatigue, while adjusting for confounding factors. Our hope is to use it on a larger scale to assess fatigue among patients with histoplasmosis, ultimately providing clinicians with the evidence needed to appropriately counsel patients on long-term expectations after an initial histoplasmosis diagnosis.

In conclusion, there are limited data regarding the experiences of patients with histoplasmosis after treatment initiation. Although postinfectious syndromes are subjective, we have demonstrated the use of a tool to evaluate fatigue after histoplasmosis, and we found that histoplasmosis can be studied with regard to fatigue. In the future, we hope to investigate relationships regarding fatigue in patients with histoplasmosis on a larger scale. Furthermore, by studying fatigue in histoplasmosis, we hope to improve systematic evaluations of future patients with postinfectious syndromes.
