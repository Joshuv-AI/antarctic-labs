Global stability of Minkowski spacetime for a causal nonlocal gravity model
Christian Balfagón

Abstract
We establish small-data global existence and decay for the causal–informational nonlocal gravity model CET Ω \Omega in 3 + 1 3{+}1 dimensions. Under harmonic gauge the field equations reduce to a quasilinear hyperbolic system with causal memory generated by a retarded Stieltjes operator 𝒦 − 1 \mathcal{K}^{-1} . We establish three results: (i) commutator estimates for the Klainerman vector fields acting on 𝒦 − 1 \mathcal{K}^{-1} , showing that the nonlocal operator costs at most two additional derivatives relative to Einstein vacuum; (ii) a sharp Sobolev-level bound on the memory convolution under explicit integrability conditions on the spectral density ρ \rho ; (iii) global existence, uniform energy bounds, and pointwise ( 1 + t ) − 1 (1+t)^{-1} decay for small initial data in H N H^{N} with N ≥ 10 N\geq 10 . The proof combines the Lindblad–Rodnianski ghost weight method with commutator estimates and resolvent identities adapted to the Stieltjes kernel. A key structural observation is that retarded causality of the kernel is mathematically necessary for the stability argument: acausal modifications destroy the hyperbolic energy identity on which the bootstrap relies. We verify that the spectral conditions are satisfied by explicit, physically motivated kernel families (Section 3.3 ). Because the memory operator generates a persistent tail that does not vanish at late times, classical scattering to free waves does not hold; we establish instead modified scattering , proving convergence to a solution of a linear wave equation with an explicit, computable memory profile in the energy topology ( H N − 2 × H N − 3 H^{N-2}\times H^{N-3} ). We discuss quantitative observational signatures — including gravitational wave memory excess, frequency-dependent phase shift, and anomalous late-time ringdown tail — controlled by the same spectral constants governing the stability conditions.

1.2
The CET
\Omega

The CET
\Omega
model (Causal–Informational Completion of Gravity)

[
67
]

augments the Einstein–Hilbert action with a nonlocal term built from a
causal, retarded integral operator
\mathcal{K}

features are:

(i)

Retarded causality.
The operator
\mathcal{K}
is
supported on the past light cone and its interior, so the theory
propagates no acausal degrees of freedom.

(ii)

Spectral positivity.
The inverse
\mathcal{K}^{-1}

admits a Stieltjes integral representation with nonnegative spectral
density
\rho(\mu)\geq 0

does not introduce ghost modes at the linearized level.

(iii)

Infrared transparency.
The integrability conditions on

\rho

\mathcal{K}^{-1}\to 0
sufficiently fast at large
scales, so the modification is suppressed at cosmological distances
and Minkowski spacetime remains an exact solution.

The physical content of these conditions is discussed in
Section

are the minimal hypotheses under which the stability argument closes
in the small-data regime.

1.3
Main result and strategy

Our main result (Theorem
10.1
) states that for sufficiently
small and suitably regular initial data, the Cauchy problem for
CET
\Omega
in harmonic gauge admits a unique global classical solution
that decays to Minkowski spacetime at rate
(1+t)^{-1}
.

The proof follows the Lindblad–Rodnianski strategy adapted to the
nonlocal setting. Recent progress on related stability problems includes
the global stability of Kerr–de Sitter
[
19
]
,
nonlinear stability of Schwarzschild under polarized perturbations

[
20
]
, linear stability of Schwarzschild

[
21
]
, and stability of Minkowski for self-gravitating
massive fields
[
22
]
. To our knowledge, no global
stability result exists for causal nonlocal gravity models.
The new difficulties specific
to CET
\Omega
, and our resolutions, are:

(a)

The commutator
[Z^{I},\mathcal{K}^{-1}]
between Klainerman
vector fields and the nonlocal operator is nontrivial. We show
(Proposition
5.2
) that it can be expressed in
terms of lower-order resolvent operators, costing at most two
additional derivatives. This is the origin of the threshold

N\geq 10
rather than
N\geq 8
.

(b)

The memory convolution term must be controlled in
H^{N}
uniformly
in time. Lemma
6.1
provides this bound using Young’s
inequality and the integrability conditions on
\rho
.

(c)

The ghost weight energy of Lindblad–Rodnianski must be shown
compatible with the nonlocal memory operator. This is the content
of Proposition
7.3
, which establishes that the
memory contribution to the ghost weight energy inequality is
integrable. The proof exploits the retarded structure of the kernel
and the spatial localization of the ghost weight near the light cone.

(d)

Classical scattering to free waves fails because the memory
operator generates a persistent tail

\mathcal{M}_{\infty}(x)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{M}(t,x)\neq 0
.
We prove instead
modified scattering

(Theorem
10.7
): the solution converges to
a free wave modulo an explicit, decaying memory profile.

2
The CET
\Omega
equations in harmonic gauge

2.1
Field equations

Let
(M,g)
be a globally hyperbolic Lorentzian manifold. The
CET
\Omega
field equations take the schematic form

G_{\mu\nu}[g]+\mathcal{K}^{-1}\!\left(R_{\mu\nu}-\tfrac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}R+\Lambda_{\Omega,\mu\nu}[\varphi]\right)=0,

(2.1)

where
G_{\mu\nu}
is the Einstein tensor,
\Lambda_{\Omega,\mu\nu}

encodes coupling to an auxiliary scalar field
\varphi
(the texonic
mode), and
\mathcal{K}^{-1}
is the nonlocal operator defined below.
Minkowski spacetime
(g=\eta,\,\varphi=0)
is an exact solution.

2.2
Harmonic gauge

We impose the harmonic gauge condition

\Box_{g}x^{\mu}=0
, equivalently

\partial_{\nu}\!\left(\sqrt{|\det g|}\,g^{\mu\nu}\right)=0.

(2.2)

Writing
g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu}
, the reduced equations
become

\Box h_{\mu\nu}=Q_{\mu\nu}(\partial h,\partial h)+P_{\mu\nu}(h,\partial^{2}h)+\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2,\mu\nu}(h,\partial h,\varphi,\partial\varphi)+\mathcal{S}_{\mu\nu}(h,\varphi),

(2.3)

where:

•

Q_{\mu\nu}(\partial h,\partial h)
is the standard quadratic
null form inherited from the Einstein equations in harmonic gauge,
satisfying the
weak null condition

[
23
,
24
,
6
,
25
]
.

•

P_{\mu\nu}(h,\partial^{2}h)
collects terms linear in

\partial^{2}h
with coefficients depending on
h
. In schematic
form,
P\sim h\cdot\partial^{2}h
.

•

\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2,\mu\nu}
is the nonlocal contribution,
where
N_{2,\mu\nu}
is at least quadratic in
(h,\varphi)
and their
first derivatives.

•

\mathcal{S}_{\mu\nu}
is a semilinear remainder of order
\geq 3
.

The texonic field satisfies a coupled wave equation

\Box\varphi=\mathcal{N}_{\varphi}(h,\partial h,\varphi,\partial\varphi),

(2.4)

where
\mathcal{N}_{\varphi}
is at least quadratic. For notational
economy, we write
u=(h,\varphi)
and group
(
2.3
)–(
2.4
) as a single system

\Box u=F(u,\partial u)+\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}(u,\partial u),

(2.5)

where
F
collects local terms satisfying the weak null condition and

N_{2}
is the nonlocal source.

Remark 2.1

(Gauge propagation)
.

In the Einstein vacuum equations, the harmonic gauge condition
(
2.2
) propagates: if it holds on
\{t=0\}
and the
constraint equations are satisfied initially, then it holds for all
time. This follows from the contracted Bianchi identity

\nabla^{\mu}G_{\mu\nu}=0
, which implies that the gauge violation

\Gamma^{\mu}\coloneqq\Box_{g}x^{\mu}
satisfies a homogeneous wave equation

\Box_{g}\Gamma^{\mu}=0
with trivial data, hence
\Gamma^{\mu}\equiv 0
.

In CET
\Omega
, the field equation (
2.1
) modifies the
right-hand side of the Bianchi identity. Gauge propagation requires the
nonlocal correction to satisfy the divergence condition

\nabla^{\mu}\Bigl[\mathcal{K}^{-1}\!\bigl(R_{\mu\nu}-\tfrac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}R+\Lambda_{\Omega,\mu\nu}\bigr)\Bigr]=0.

(2.6)

This holds as a consequence of the variational structure of the
CET
\Omega
action: the nonlocal term is derived from a
diffeomorphism-invariant functional of
g
and
\varphi
, so its
Euler–Lagrange equations are automatically divergence-free by the
Noether identity associated with diffeomorphism invariance.
Concretely, (
2.6
) reduces, upon using the
Bianchi identity for
G_{\mu\nu}
, to

\nabla^{\mu}[\mathcal{K}^{-1}\Lambda_{\Omega,\mu\nu}]=0
, which is the
equation of motion for the texonic field
\varphi

(cf. (
2.4
)). Therefore the gauge violation
\Gamma^{\mu}

again satisfies a homogeneous wave equation with trivial data, and
the harmonic gauge propagates as in the Einstein vacuum case.
The complete variational derivation, including the explicit
action and the proof via Noether’s second theorem, is given in
Appendix
A
.

3
The Nonlocal Operator and Spectral Properties

3.1
Stieltjes representation

The nonlocal operator
\mathcal{K}^{-1}
admits a Stieltjes integral
representation (see
[
28
]
for the general theory)
as a superposition of massive retarded propagators:

\mathcal{K}^{-1}f(t,x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(\mu)\,G_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ret}}*f\;d\mu,

(3.1)

where
G_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ret}}
is the retarded Green function of

-\Box+\mu
:

(-\Box+\mu)G_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ret}}(t,x)=\delta(t)\delta^{3}(x),\qquad G_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ret}}(t,x)=0\;\text{for }t<0.

(3.2)

Equivalently, for a source
f
supported in
\{t\geq 0\}
,

\mathcal{K}^{-1}f=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(\mu)\,(-\Box+\mu)^{-1}_{\mathrm{ret}}f\;d\mu,

(3.3)

where
(-\Box+\mu)^{-1}_{\mathrm{ret}}
denotes the retarded inverse.

3.2
Spectral conditions

Assumption 3.1
.

The spectral density
\rho\colon(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
satisfies:

(S1)

\rho(\mu)\geq 0
for all
\mu>0
(spectral
positivity).

(S2)

\displaystyle\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(\mu)\,d\mu<\infty

(
L^{1}
integrability).

(S3)

\displaystyle C_{\rho,-1}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu^{-1}\rho(\mu)\,d\mu<\infty

(infrared regularity).

(S4)

\displaystyle C_{\rho,1}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu\,\rho(\mu)\,d\mu<\infty

(ultraviolet regularity).

(S5)

\rho
is locally absolutely continuous on

(0,\infty)
with

\displaystyle C_{\rho^{\prime}}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}|\rho^{\prime}(\mu)|\,d\mu<\infty

(spectral regularity).

We define the spectral constants

\bm{\rho}=\bigl(\|\rho\|_{L^{1}},\,C_{\rho,-1},\,C_{\rho,1},\,C_{\rho^{\prime}}\bigr).

Conditions
(S1)
–
(S2)
ensure that
\mathcal{K}^{-1}
is a
bounded operator on suitable function spaces and that the kernel

K(t,x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(\mu)\,G_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ret}}(t,x)\,d\mu

lies in
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
. Condition
(S3)
controls the infrared
behavior: it prevents the nonlocal modification from generating
long-range tails that would obstruct decay. Condition
(S4)
appears
in the commutator estimates of
Section
5
, where the scaling vector field produces
terms proportional to
\mu\,\rho(\mu)
.

3.3
Physical interpretation

The spectral conditions have a natural interpretation within the
CET
\Omega
framework:

•

Spectral positivity

(S1)
is the statement that the
nonlocal completion does not introduce ghost (negative-norm) states.
At the level of the two-point function, it ensures positivity of the
spectral measure in the Källén–Lehmann representation.
[
29
,
30
]

•

L^{1}
integrability

(S2)
means that the total
spectral weight is finite, so the nonlocal operator is a bounded
perturbation of the identity in the appropriate sense. Physically,
this reflects the fact that the causal–informational correction is
weak: the total integrated effect over all mass scales is finite.

•

Infrared regularity

(S3)
prevents accumulation of
spectral weight near
\mu=0
(massless modes). Were this condition
violated, the operator
\mathcal{K}^{-1}
would produce logarithmic
tails incompatible with the
(1+t)^{-1}
decay required to close the
bootstrap. This is the mathematical expression of the requirement
that CET
\Omega
reduces to standard general relativity at large
scales.

•

Ultraviolet regularity

(S4)
constrains the high-mass
spectral content. It arises because the scaling vector field
S

generates commutator terms involving
\mu\,\rho(\mu)

(Proposition
5.2
). Physically, this condition
ensures that the nonlocal correction does not couple too strongly to
short-wavelength modes.

•

Spectral regularity

(S5)
ensures that the spectral
density does not oscillate too rapidly as a function of
\mu
. It
enters through the spectral averaging mechanism
(Lemma
3.15
below): the free Klein–Gordon
propagator
S_{\mu}(t)
, integrated against
\rho(\mu)\,d\mu
, decays
as
t^{-1}
by a Riemann–Lebesgue mechanism in the mass variable.
Without
(S5)
, the memory time derivative does not decay, and the
energy inequality degenerates to almost-global (exponential in

1/\varepsilon
) rather than global existence. The condition

\rho^{\prime}\in L^{1}
is the minimal regularity for the oscillatory integral
argument.

Concrete spectral densities

Proposition 3.2

The following spectral densities satisfy

Proposition 3.5

(Linear mode stability)
.

Under Assumption
3.1
, the linearized
CET
\Omega
equations around Minkowski spacetime admit
no exponentially growing mode solutions. Specifically,
every plane-wave solution

u\propto e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot x-\omega t)}
of the
linearized system satisfies

\mathrm{Im}\,\omega=0
for all

\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Proof.

Fourier-transforming the linearized equations in
space gives the dispersion relation

\omega^{2}=|\mathbf{k}|^{2}+\Sigma(\omega,|\mathbf{k}|^{2}),

(3.4)

where

\Sigma(\omega,|\mathbf{k}|^{2})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\rho(\mu)\,|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}{\omega^{2}-|\mathbf{k}|^{2}+\mu}\,d\mu.

Suppose for contradiction that

\omega=\omega_{R}+i\omega_{I}
with
\omega_{I}\neq 0
.
Write
\omega^{2}=\omega_{R}^{2}-\omega_{I}^{2}+2i\omega_{R}\omega_{I}
. Taking the imaginary part of
(
3.4
):

2\omega_{R}\omega_{I}=\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma=-2\omega_{R}\omega_{I}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\rho(\mu)\,|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}{|\omega^{2}-|\mathbf{k}|^{2}+\mu|^{2}}\,d\mu.

(3.5)

Case 1:
\omega_{R}\neq 0
.
Dividing
(
3.5
) by
2\omega_{R}\omega_{I}\neq 0
:

1=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\rho(\mu)\,|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}{|\omega^{2}-|\mathbf{k}|^{2}+\mu|^{2}}\,d\mu.

The right side is
\leq 0
since
\rho\geq 0

by
(S1)
, contradicting
1>0
.

Case 2:
\omega_{R}=0
.
Then

\omega^{2}=-\omega_{I}^{2}<0
, and the dispersion
relation becomes

-\omega_{I}^{2}=|\mathbf{k}|^{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\rho(\mu)\,|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}{-\omega_{I}^{2}-|\mathbf{k}|^{2}+\mu}\,d\mu.

The left side is strictly negative. For the
right side, if
|\mathbf{k}|=0
then

\mathrm{RHS}=0>-\omega_{I}^{2}
, a contradiction.
If
|\mathbf{k}|\neq 0
, the integrand has a possible
singularity at
\mu_{*}=\omega_{I}^{2}+|\mathbf{k}|^{2}>0
.
If
\rho(\mu_{*})>0
(which holds whenever
\rho

is strictly positive near
\mu_{*}
, as is the case
for families (a) and (b) of
Proposition
3.2
), the integral
diverges to
+\infty
since the residue

\rho(\mu_{*})\,|\mathbf{k}|^{2}>0
, contradicting the
finiteness of the left side.

If
\rho
vanishes on a neighborhood of
\mu_{*}

(which can occur for spectral densities with
gaps in their support), the integral is
well-defined and real. In this case we appeal
to the energy positivity of
\mathcal{K}^{-1}

(Proposition
3.7
(v)): a mode

u\propto e^{\omega_{I}t}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot x}

with
\omega_{I}>0
would have

\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int u\cdot\mathcal{K}^{-1}u\,dx\,dt\geq 0
growing at rate
e^{2\omega_{I}T}
, while
the standard wave energy identity gives

\frac{d}{dt}E=-\int u_{t}\cdot\mathcal{K}^{-1}\Omega\,dx
, producing an
exponential energy growth incompatible with the
finite-energy initial data. A contradiction.

Therefore
\omega_{I}=0
for all

\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
∎

3.5
Functional-analytic framework for
\mathcal{K}^{-1}

We now establish the precise functional-analytic properties of

\mathcal{K}^{-1}
, including its domain, mapping properties on
Sobolev spaces, and nonlinear closure.

Definition 3.6

(Domain)
.

For
k\geq 0
and
T>0
, define the Banach space

X^{k}_{T}\coloneqq L^{1}([0,T];H^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))\cap C([0,T];H^{k-1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})),

(3.6)

equipped with the norm

\|f\|_{X^{k}_{T}}=\|f\|_{L^{1}_{t}H^{k}}+\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}H^{k-1}}
.
The target space is

Y^{k}_{T}\coloneqq C([0,T];H^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))\cap C^{1}([0,T];H^{k-1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})).

(3.7)

Proposition 3.7

(Mapping properties)
.

Under Assumption
3.1
, the operator
\mathcal{K}^{-1}

satisfies:

(i)

Sobolev boundedness:

\mathcal{K}^{-1}\colon X^{k}_{T}\to Y^{k}_{T}
is bounded for all

k\geq 0
,
T>0
, with

\|\mathcal{K}^{-1}\|_{X^{k}_{T}\to Y^{k}_{T}}\leq C(\bm{\rho}).

(ii)

Global-in-time bound:
If

f\in L^{1}([0,\infty);H^{k})
, then

\mathcal{K}^{-1}f\in L^{\infty}([0,\infty);H^{k})
with

\|\mathcal{K}^{-1}f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}H^{k}}\leq\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}\,\|f\|_{L^{1}_{t}H^{k}}.

(iii)

Temporal regularity:
If
f\in X^{k}_{T}
and

\partial_{t}f\in X^{k-1}_{T}
, then

\mathcal{K}^{-1}f\in C^{1}([0,T];H^{k})\cap C^{2}([0,T];H^{k-2}).

(iv)

Causal support:
If
f(t)=0
for
t\leq t_{0}
, then

\mathcal{K}^{-1}f(t)=0
for
t\leq t_{0}
.

(v)

Energy positivity:
For
f
supported in

\{t\geq 0\}
,

\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f\cdot\mathcal{K}^{-1}f\,dx\,dt\geq 0.

Proof.

(i)–(ii).
These follow from Lemmas
3.12
and

3.13
together with the standard energy estimates for the
Klein–Gordon resolvent
[
31
]
, which give

(-\Box+\mu)^{-1}_{\mathrm{ret}}\colon X^{k}_{T}\to Y^{k}_{T}
with norm

\leq C
uniformly in
\mu\geq 0
. Integration against
\rho
yields
the bound with constant
\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}
.

(iii).
Differentiating the Stieltjes representation under the
integral sign (justified by the dominated convergence theorem using the

\mu
-uniform energy estimates and condition
(S2)
).

(iv).
Each retarded resolvent
R_{\mu}
satisfies the causal
support property by definition of the retarded Green function. Since

\mathcal{K}^{-1}
is a nonnegative superposition of retarded operators,
the causal support property is preserved.

(v).
By the Stieltjes representation,

\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int f\cdot\mathcal{K}^{-1}f=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(\mu)\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int f\cdot R_{\mu}f\,d\mu.

For each
\mu\geq 0
, set
v_{\mu}=R_{\mu}f
. Then

(-\Box+\mu)v_{\mu}=f
with
v_{\mu}|_{t=0}=\partial_{t}v_{\mu}|_{t=0}=0

(retarded propagator with zero initial data). Multiplying by

v_{\mu}
and integrating by parts over
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int f\cdot v_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int(-\Box+\mu)v_{\mu}\cdot v_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\|\partial_{t}v_{\mu}(T)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\nabla v_{\mu}(T)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\tfrac{\mu}{2}\|v_{\mu}(T)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}_{=\,\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(T)\,\geq\,0}

\displaystyle\quad-\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\|\partial_{t}v_{\mu}(0)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\cdots}_{=\,\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(0)\,=\,0}.

The boundary at
t=0
vanishes because
v_{\mu}
has zero Cauchy data;
the boundary at
t=T
is the Klein–Gordon energy

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(T)\geq 0
(each term is nonneg.). Therefore

\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int f\cdot v_{\mu}=\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(T)\geq 0

for every
\mu\geq 0
. Integrating against
\rho\geq 0

gives the result.
∎

Proposition 3.8

(Nonlinear closure)
.

Let
N\geq 10
and let
\mathcal{B}_{R}\subset C([0,T];H^{N})
denote the
ball of radius
R>0
. If
N_{2}\colon\mathcal{B}_{R}\to X^{N}_{T}
is a
smooth nonlinear map satisfying

\|N_{2}(u)\|_{X^{N}_{T}}\leq C\|u\|^{2}_{Y^{N}_{T}},

then the composed operator
u\mapsto\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}(u)
maps

\mathcal{B}_{R}
into
Y^{N}_{T}
and satisfies:

\|\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}(u)-\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}(v)\|_{Y^{N}_{T}}\leq C(\bm{\rho})\,R\,\|u-v\|_{Y^{N}_{T}}.

(3.8)

In particular,
\mathcal{K}^{-1}\circ N_{2}
is locally Lipschitz on

Y^{N}_{T}
, which is the closure property needed for the local existence
theory (Theorem
10.1
, Step 1).

Proof.

Since
N_{2}
is at least quadratic, the mean value theorem gives

\|N_{2}(u)-N_{2}(v)\|_{X^{N}_{T}}\leq CR\|u-v\|_{Y^{N}_{T}}

for

u,v\in\mathcal{B}_{R}
. Composing with the bounded operator

\mathcal{K}^{-1}\colon X^{N}_{T}\to Y^{N}_{T}

(Proposition
3.7
(i)) yields (
3.8
).
∎

Proposition 3.9

(Local well-posedness of the quasilinear system)
.

Let
N\geq 10
and
(u_{0},u_{1})\in H^{N}\times H^{N-1}
satisfy the
harmonic gauge constraint and

\|u_{0}\|_{H^{N}}+\|u_{1}\|_{H^{N-1}}\leq R.

Then there exists
T=T(R,\bm{\rho})>0
such that the Cauchy
problem (
2.5
)–(
2.10
) has a unique solution

u\in C([0,T];H^{N})\cap C^{1}([0,T];H^{N-1}).

Moreover, the solution
map
(u_{0},u_{1})\mapsto u
is continuous from
H^{N}\times H^{N-1}
to

C([0,T];H^{N})
.

Proof.

We verify the four hypotheses of the
Hughes–Kato–Marsden quasilinear existence theorem
(
[
32
]
, Theorem III) for the first-order
system
\partial_{t}U=A(U)\partial_{x}U+\mathcal{G}(U)

where
U=(u,\partial_{t}u)
,

A(U)
encodes the quasilinear principal part from

\Box_{g}
, and
\mathcal{G}(U)=\tilde{F}(U)+\mathcal{K}^{-1}\tilde{N}_{2}(U)
collects the
semilinear and nonlocal terms.

(HKM1)

Symmetric hyperbolicity.
Since

g=\eta+h
with
|h|\leq R<1
, the principal part

g^{\alpha\beta}(h)\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}

has Lorentzian signature (a smooth perturbation of

\eta^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}
),
and the first-order system is symmetric hyperbolic
with a positive-definite energy density for

|h|<1
.

(HKM2)

Regularity of coefficients.
The
matrix
A(U)
depends smoothly (polynomially) on

h
through
g^{\alpha\beta}(h)=\eta^{\alpha\beta}-h^{\alpha\beta}+O(h^{2})
, so
A\colon H^{N}\to H^{N}
is smooth for
N>5/2+1=7/2
(Sobolev
embedding
H^{N}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}
in 3D for

N\geq 2
).

(HKM3)

Local Lipschitz continuity of the
source.
We verify this for each component.
The local term
\tilde{F}
satisfies Moser
estimates:

\|\tilde{F}(U)\|_{H^{N-1}}\leq C\|U\|_{H^{N-1}}^{2},

and the Lipschitz bound

\|\tilde{F}(U)-\tilde{F}(V)\|_{H^{N-1}}\leq CR\|U-V\|_{H^{N-1}}.

The nonlocal term:
Propositions
3.7

and
3.8
give

\mathcal{K}^{-1}\tilde{N}_{2}\colon\mathcal{B}_{R}\to Y^{N-1}_{T}
bounded and
locally Lipschitz with constant

C(\bm{\rho})R
. Crucially,

\mathcal{K}^{-1}\tilde{N}_{2}
maps
H^{N}\to H^{N}

without derivative loss because

(-\Box+\mu)^{-1}_{\mathrm{ret}}
is bounded on

H^{k}
uniformly in
\mu

(Lemma
3.13
).

(HKM4)

Group generation.
The operator

A(U)\partial_{x}
generates a strongly continuous
group on
H^{N-1}
for each fixed
U
with

|h|<1
. At
U=0
this is the standard wave
group; for
|h|<1
it follows from Kato’s
perturbation theory
[
33
]
since the
difference
A(U)-A(0)
is a bounded multiplication
operator on
H^{N-1}
.

All four hypotheses are satisfied. Existence,
uniqueness, and continuous dependence on
[0,T]

follow from
[
32
]
, Theorem III. The time of
existence satisfies
T\geq c/R
with

c=c(N,\bm{\rho})>0
.
∎

Remark 3.10

(Relation to the Cauchy problem)
.

Proposition
3.8
ensures that the nonlocal
term
\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}(u)
does not obstruct the standard local
well-posedness theory for quasilinear wave equations. The full
right-hand side of (
2.5
),

F(u,\partial u)+\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}(u)
, consists of
a standard quasilinear nonlinearity
F
and a
bounded perturbation
\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}
in
Y^{N}_{T}
(of lower
order: it maps
H^{N}\to H^{N}

without loss of derivatives). The combined source satisfies the
hypotheses of Hughes–Kato–Marsden
[
32
]
for local existence.
The nonlocal operator does not alter the principal symbol of
\Box_{g}
,
so the hyperbolicity and domain of dependence properties are inherited
from the Einstein equations in harmonic gauge.

Remark 3.11

(Acausal vs. causal kernels)
.

If one replaces the retarded Green function in (
3.1
) by a
Feynman or symmetric propagator, the resulting operator is acausal. In
that case the convolution
\mathcal{K}^{-1}f
receives contributions
from the
future
of the support of
f
. This destroys the energy
identity (Lemma
7.1
) because the integration by
parts in time produces future boundary terms that cannot be controlled
by the bootstrap hypothesis on
[0,T^{*}]
. Thus retarded causality is
not merely a physical preference but a mathematical necessity for
stability.

(S1)
–
(S2)

(3.9)

3.7
Massive resolvent decay

The retarded resolvent for the Klein–Gordon operator satisfies improved
decay for
\mu>0
:

Lemma 3.14

(Klein–Gordon energy bounds)
.

Let
\mu>0
and
f\in L^{1}([0,T];H^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
. The retarded
solution
v_{\mu}=(-\Box+\mu)^{-1}_{\mathrm{ret}}f
satisfies:

(i)

Duhamel bound (uniform in
\mu\geq 0
):

\|v_{\mu}(t)\|_{H^{k}}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\|f(\tau)\|_{H^{k}}\,d\tau=\|f\|_{L^{1}([0,t];H^{k})}.

(3.11)

(ii)

Mass-weighted bound (
\mu>0
only):

\|v_{\mu}(t)\|_{H^{k}}\leq\sqrt{2}\,\mu^{-1/2}\,\|f\|_{L^{1}([0,t];H^{k})}.

(3.12)

(iii)

L^{2}
norm is time-independent for homogeneous KG:

If
(-\Box+\mu)w=0
with data
(w_{0},w_{1})
, then

\mu\|w(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\mu\|w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|w_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

for
all
t
.

In particular,
\|v_{\mu}(t)\|_{L^{2}}
does
not
decay in
t
for
the Klein–Gordon equation:
L^{2}
dispersive decay is a property of

L^{1}\to L^{\infty}
estimates (see
[
34
]
),
not
L^{2}\to L^{2}
.

Proof.

(i).
By Duhamel,
v_{\mu}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}G_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ret}}(t-\tau)*_{x}f(\tau)\,d\tau
. The KG propagator
satisfies

\|G_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ret}}(s)*_{x}g\|_{H^{k}}\leq\|g\|_{H^{k}}

for all
s\geq 0
and
\mu\geq 0
(energy conservation: the
H^{k}
norm of
the homogeneous solution is bounded by the
H^{k}\times H^{k-1}
norm of
the data, and
\|g\|_{H^{k}\times\{0\}}\leq\|g\|_{H^{k}}
; the mass term

\mu\geq 0
only helps). Minkowski’s inequality gives (
3.11
).

(ii).
Multiply
(-\Box+\mu)v_{\mu}=f
by
v_{\mu}
and integrate
over
[0,t]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
. With zero initial data, the energy
identity gives

\tfrac{1}{2}\|\partial_{t}v_{\mu}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\nabla v_{\mu}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\tfrac{\mu}{2}\|v_{\mu}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int_{0}^{t}\langle f(\tau),\partial_{\tau}v_{\mu}(\tau)\rangle\,d\tau.

By Cauchy–Schwarz in
L^{2}
and Young’s inequality:

\int_{0}^{t}\langle f,\partial_{\tau}v_{\mu}\rangle\,d\tau\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,d\tau+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial_{\tau}v_{\mu}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,d\tau.

Denoting

y(t)=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\partial_{t}v_{\mu}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\nabla v_{\mu}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\tfrac{\mu}{2}\|v_{\mu}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

we have

y(t)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\|f\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}y(\tau)\,d\tau.

By Gronwall,
y(t)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\|f\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{2}}^{2}e^{t}
.
For the sharper
\mu
-dependent bound, note that

y^{\prime}(t)=\langle f(t),\partial_{t}v_{\mu}(t)\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2\mu}\|f(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\mu\,y(t).

By Gronwall:

y(t)\leq\frac{1}{2\mu}\int_{0}^{t}\|f(\tau)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}e^{\mu(t-\tau)}\,d\tau
. Since
\mu\|v_{\mu}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq 2y(t)

and the exponential factor is controlled for the retarded problem
(where
v_{\mu}
has zero data and
f
is the source), the correct
bound follows from
y(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\langle f,\partial_{\tau}v_{\mu}\rangle\,d\tau
without the exponential, by using the structure of
the retarded problem:

y(t)\leq\Bigl(\int_{0}^{t}\|f\|_{L^{2}}\,d\tau\Bigr)\sup_{[0,t]}\|\partial_{\tau}v_{\mu}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|f\|_{L^{1}_{t}L^{2}}\sqrt{2y(t)},

hence
y(t)\leq\|f\|_{L^{1}_{t}L^{2}}^{2}
. Extracting the mass term:

\mu\|v_{\mu}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq 2y(t)\leq 2\|f\|_{L^{1}_{t}L^{2}}^{2},

which gives (
3.12
) after taking square roots.
Extending to
H^{k}
by applying spatial derivatives and using

[\partial^{\alpha},-\Box+\mu]=0
:

\mu\|v_{\mu}(t)\|_{H^{k}}^{2}\leq 2\|f\|_{L^{1}([0,t];H^{k})}^{2}.

(iii).
Standard Klein–Gordon energy conservation:

\mathcal{E}(t)=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\partial_{t}w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\tfrac{\mu}{2}\|w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\mathcal{E}(0).

Since each term is nonneg.,

\mu\|w(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq 2\mathcal{E}(0)=\|w_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\mu\|w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Simplified:

\mu\|w(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\|w_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+2\mu\|w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
.
∎

Lemma 3.15

(Spectral averaging)
.

Let
\rho
satisfy
(S1)
–
(S2)
and
(S5)
,
and let
g\in H^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for some
k\geq 0
.
Define the spectrally integrated free Klein–Gordon
propagator

I(t)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(\mu)\,S_{\mu}(t)\,g\,d\mu,

(3.13)

where
S_{\mu}(t)g=\sin(t\sqrt{-\Delta+\mu})\,g/\sqrt{-\Delta+\mu}

is the free KG propagator with initial velocity
g
.
Then:

(i)

Uniform bound (no regularity of
\rho

needed):

\|I(t)\|_{H^{k}}\leq C_{\rho,-1/2}\,\|g\|_{H^{k}},\qquad C_{\rho,-1/2}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu^{-1/2}\rho(\mu)\,d\mu.

(3.14)

(ii)

Decay bound (under
(S5)
):

\|I(t)\|_{H^{k}}\leq\frac{C(\bm{\rho})}{1+t}\,\|g\|_{H^{k}},\qquad t\geq 0,

(3.15)

where

C(\bm{\rho})=2|\rho(0)|+2C_{\rho^{\prime}}+2\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}
.

Proof.

(i).
Each
S_{\mu}(t)g
has

\|S_{\mu}(t)g\|_{H^{k}}\leq\mu^{-1/2}\|g\|_{H^{k}}
by
Lemma
3.14
(iii) (with data

(0,g)
: the
H^{k}
norm of the velocity part of
the KG energy is bounded by

\mu^{-1/2}\|g\|_{H^{k}}
). Minkowski’s inequality
gives (
3.14
). (The constant

C_{\rho,-1/2}
is finite by Cauchy–Schwarz:

C_{\rho,-1/2}^{2}\leq C_{\rho,-1}\cdot\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}
.)

(ii).
We prove (
3.15
)
by integration by parts in
\mu
— a
Riemann–Lebesgue mechanism in the mass variable.

Step 1: Oscillatory integral in Fourier
space.
At fixed
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, define

\omega_{\mu}(\xi)=\sqrt{|\xi|^{2}+\mu}
.
Then

\widehat{I}(t,\xi)=\hat{g}(\xi)\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(\mu)\,\frac{\sin(t\,\omega_{\mu})}{\omega_{\mu}}\,d\mu.

Substituting
\omega=\omega_{\mu}(\xi)
, so

\mu=\omega^{2}-|\xi|^{2}
and
d\mu=2\omega\,d\omega
:

\widehat{I}(t,\xi)=2\hat{g}(\xi)\int_{|\xi|}^{\infty}\rho\bigl(\omega^{2}-|\xi|^{2}\bigr)\,\sin(t\omega)\,d\omega.

(3.16)

Define
\sigma(\omega)\coloneqq\rho(\omega^{2}-|\xi|^{2})

for
\omega\geq|\xi|
.

Step 2: Integration by parts in
\omega
.

\displaystyle\int_{|\xi|}^{\infty}\sigma(\omega)\,\sin(t\omega)\,d\omega

\displaystyle=\Bigl[-\frac{\sigma(\omega)\cos(t\omega)}{t}\Bigr]_{|\xi|}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{t}\int_{|\xi|}^{\infty}\sigma^{\prime}(\omega)\,\cos(t\omega)\,d\omega.

(3.17)

The boundary term at
\omega=\infty
vanishes because

\rho\in L^{1}
implies
\sigma(\omega)\to 0
as

\omega\to\infty
(by
(S2)
and the change of
variables). The boundary at
\omega=|\xi|
gives

\sigma(|\xi|)=\rho(0)
.

For the derivative:

\sigma^{\prime}(\omega)=2\omega\,\rho^{\prime}(\omega^{2}-|\xi|^{2})
.
Therefore

\int_{|\xi|}^{\infty}|\sigma^{\prime}(\omega)|\,d\omega=\int_{|\xi|}^{\infty}2\omega\,|\rho^{\prime}(\omega^{2}-|\xi|^{2})|\,d\omega=\int_{0}^{\infty}|\rho^{\prime}(\mu)|\,d\mu=C_{\rho^{\prime}},

where the last equality follows from the
substitution
\mu=\omega^{2}-|\xi|^{2}
.

Step 3: Pointwise Fourier bound.

From (
3.17
):

\biggl|\int_{|\xi|}^{\infty}\sigma(\omega)\,\sin(t\omega)\,d\omega\biggr|\leq\frac{|\rho(0)|+C_{\rho^{\prime}}}{t},\qquad t>0.

(3.18)

Substituting into (
3.16
):

|\widehat{I}(t,\xi)|\leq 2(|\rho(0)|+C_{\rho^{\prime}})\,t^{-1}|\hat{g}(\xi)|
for
t>0
.

Step 4: Sobolev norm.

\displaystyle\|I(t)\|_{H^{k}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{k}\,|\widehat{I}(t,\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4(|\rho(0)|+C_{\rho^{\prime}})^{2}}{t^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{k}|\hat{g}(\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{4(|\rho(0)|+C_{\rho^{\prime}})^{2}}{t^{2}}\,\|g\|_{H^{k}}^{2}.

(3.19)

Taking square roots and combining with the
uniform bound for
t\leq 1
:

\|I(t)\|_{H^{k}}\leq\frac{C(\bm{\rho})}{1+t}\,\|g\|_{H^{k}},

where

C(\bm{\rho})=2(|\rho(0)|+C_{\rho^{\prime}})+2\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}

(the last term covers
t\leq 1
via the uniform bound).
Note:
no derivative loss
— the
H^{k}
norm of

g
on the right matches the
H^{k}
norm of
I(t)
on
the left.

Remark 3.16

(see also
[

4
Functional Framework and Bootstrap

4.1
Klainerman vector fields

Let
Z=\{Z_{a}\}_{a=1}^{11}
denote the standard collection of Klainerman
vector fields on
\mathbb{R}^{3+1}
:

Translations:

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu},\quad\mu=0,1,2,3,

(4.1)

Rotations:

\displaystyle\Omega_{ij}=x_{i}\partial_{j}-x_{j}\partial_{i},\quad 1\leq i<j\leq 3,

(4.2)

Boosts:

\displaystyle\Omega_{0i}=t\partial_{i}+x_{i}\partial_{t},\quad i=1,2,3,

(4.3)

Scaling:

\displaystyle S=t\partial_{t}+x^{i}\partial_{i}.

(4.4)

The key property is
[Z_{a},\Box]=c_{a}\Box
where
c_{a}=0
for all
generators except
S
, for which
[S,\Box]=2\Box
.

For a multi-index
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})
with
|I|=k\leq N
, we write

Z^{I}=Z_{i_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ Z_{i_{k}}
.

4.2
Ghost weight function

Following
[
7
]
, we fix a smooth monotone function

q\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying:

q(s)=\begin{cases}0&s\leq-1,\\
\delta_{0}&s\geq 1,\end{cases}\qquad q^{\prime}(s)\geq 0,\qquad 0\leq q(s)\leq\delta_{0},

(4.5)

where
\delta_{0}>0
is a small constant to be determined. Define the
weight

w(t,x)=e^{q(t-r)},\qquad r=|x|.

(4.6)

Note
1\leq w\leq e^{\delta_{0}}
, and
q^{\prime}(t-r)\neq 0
only in the

transition region

\{|t-r|\leq 1\}
.

4.3
Energy functional

Definition 4.1
.

For
N\geq 10
, define the
ghost weight energy

\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)=\sum_{|I|\leq N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}w(t,x)\left(|\partial_{t}Z^{I}u|^{2}+|\nabla Z^{I}u|^{2}\right)dx,

(4.7)

and the
standard energy

E_{N}(t)=\sum_{|I|\leq N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left(|\partial_{t}Z^{I}u|^{2}+|\nabla Z^{I}u|^{2}+|Z^{I}u|^{2}\right)dx.

(4.8)

Since
1\leq w\leq e^{\delta_{0}}
, we have
E_{N}(t)\leq\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)+\|u(t)\|_{H^{N}}^{2}
and
E_{N}(t)\leq C\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)

for a constant depending only on
\delta_{0}
and the lower-order terms.

4.4
Bootstrap hypothesis

Let
T^{*}>0
be the maximal time of existence of a classical solution.
We assume the bootstrap hypothesis

\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)\leq 2C_{0}\varepsilon^{2},\qquad t\in[0,T^{*}],

(4.9)

where
C_{0}>0
is a constant depending on
N
and the spectral data

\bm{\rho}
, to be determined. The goal is to improve this to

\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)\leq C_{0}\varepsilon^{2}
, which by continuity forces

T^{*}=\infty
.

4.5
Immediate consequences

Under the bootstrap hypothesis (
4.9
) and the
Klainerman–Sobolev inequality (Proposition
9.1
), we have
the pointwise bounds

|Z^{I}u(t,x)|\leq C\varepsilon(1+t+|x|)^{-1}(1+|t-|x||)^{-1/2},\qquad|I|\leq N-3,

(4.10)

and in particular

|u(t,x)|\leq C\varepsilon(1+t)^{-1},\qquad|\partial u(t,x)|\leq C\varepsilon(1+t)^{-1}.

(4.11)

The
good derivatives
satisfy the improved bound

|\bar{\partial}Z^{I}u(t,x)|\leq C\varepsilon(1+t+r)^{-1}(1+|t-r|)^{-1},\qquad|I|\leq N-4,

(4.12)

which gains an additional half-power of
(1+|t-r|)
near the light cone.
This improvement is crucial for the energy estimates.

To see (
4.12
), decompose
\bar{\partial}=\{L,\cancel{\nabla}\}
in the null frame. The outgoing derivative

L=\partial_{t}+\partial_{r}
satisfies
|LZ^{I}u|\leq|\partial Z^{I}u|
, and
the angular derivatives
\cancel{\nabla}
gain a factor of
r^{-1}
:

|\cancel{\nabla}Z^{I}u|\leq r^{-1}\sum_{|J|\leq|I|+1}|Z^{J}u|
.
Applying the Klainerman–Sobolev inequality
(Proposition
9.1
) to
\bar{\partial}Z^{I}u
rather than

Z^{I}u
costs one additional
Z
-derivative, giving

|\bar{\partial}Z^{I}u(t,x)|\leq\frac{C}{(1+t+r)(1+|t-r|)^{1/2}}\sum_{|J|\leq|I|+4}\|Z^{J}u(t)\|_{L^{2}}.

The extra half-power arises because good derivatives are

tangent to the outgoing light cones
: in the region

\{|t-r|\leq 1\}
, the relation

\bar{\partial}=(L,\cancel{\nabla})
expresses quantities that
vary slowly along
\underline{u}=t-r=\mathrm{const}
, so
Sobolev embedding on the
2
-sphere
S^{2}
of radius
r\sim t

converts the
L^{2}
bound into a pointwise bound with the improved

(1+|t-r|)^{-1}
factor (rather than
(1+|t-r|)^{-1/2}
for
generic derivatives). Precisely, the null-frame commutation of

\bar{\partial}
with the
Z
-fields and the
(t+r)^{-1}
prefactor
in the Klainerman–Sobolev inequality upgrade the decay by one
half-power of
(1+|t-r|)
. The condition
|I|\leq N-4
ensures that

|I|+4\leq N
, so all
Z^{J}u
norms are controlled by

\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)^{1/2}\leq\sqrt{2C_{0}}\,\varepsilon
.

5
Commutator Estimates

This section contains the principal new technical ingredient: control of
the commutator between Klainerman vector fields and the nonlocal
operator
\mathcal{K}^{-1}
.

5.1
Single-field commutators with the resolvent

We first analyze
[Z_{a},(-\Box+\mu)^{-1}_{\mathrm{ret}}]
for each
generator
Z_{a}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
R_{\mu}=(-\Box+\mu)^{-1}_{\mathrm{ret}}
denote the retarded
resolvent. Then:

(i)

For translations and rotations:
[Z_{a},R_{\mu}]=0
.

(ii)

For boosts:
[\Omega_{0i},R_{\mu}]=0
.

(iii)

For the scaling field:

[S,R_{\mu}]=-2R_{\mu}+2\mu\,R_{\mu}^{2}
.

Proof.

Using the general identity
[A,B^{-1}]=-B^{-1}[A,B]B^{-1}
for
invertible
B
:

(i)–(ii).
Translations, rotations, and boosts are Killing
fields of
(\mathbb{R}^{3+1},\eta)
, so
[Z_{a},\Box]=0
. Since
\mu

is a constant,
[Z_{a},-\Box+\mu]=0
, hence
[Z_{a},R_{\mu}]=0
.

(iii).
For the scaling field,

[S,\Box]=2\Box
, so
[S,-\Box+\mu]=-2\Box
. Therefore

\displaystyle[S,R_{\mu}]

\displaystyle=-R_{\mu}\,[S,-\Box+\mu]\,R_{\mu}=-R_{\mu}(-2\Box)R_{\mu}=2R_{\mu}\Box\,R_{\mu}.

(5.1)

Writing
\Box=-(-\Box+\mu)+\mu
, we obtain

\displaystyle[S,R_{\mu}]

\displaystyle=2R_{\mu}\bigl[-(-\Box+\mu)+\mu\bigr]R_{\mu}

\displaystyle=-2R_{\mu}(\underbrace{-\Box+\mu}_{=R_{\mu}^{-1}})R_{\mu}+2\mu\,R_{\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle=-2R_{\mu}+2\mu\,R_{\mu}^{2}.\qed

(5.2)

5.2
Commutators with
\mathcal{K}^{-1}

Proposition 5.2
.

Under Assumption
3.1
, for any generator
Z_{a}
,

[Z_{a},\mathcal{K}^{-1}]f=\begin{cases}0,&Z_{a}\in\{\partial_{\mu},\Omega_{ij},\Omega_{0i}\},\\
-2\mathcal{K}^{-1}f+2\mathcal{K}^{-1}_{(2)}f,&Z_{a}=S,\end{cases}

(5.3)

where

\mathcal{K}^{-1}_{(2)}f\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu\,\rho(\mu)\,R_{\mu}^{2}f\;d\mu.

(5.4)

The operator
\mathcal{K}^{-1}_{(2)}
satisfies

\|\mathcal{K}^{-1}_{(2)}f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}H^{k}}\leq C_{\rho,1}\,\|f\|_{L^{1}_{t}H^{k}},

(5.5)

with
C_{\rho,1}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu\,\rho(\mu)\,d\mu<\infty
by
condition
(S4)
.

Remark 5.3

(Domain of the commutator identity)
.

The identity (
5.3
) holds for
f\in X^{k}_{T}
with
k\geq 2

(two derivatives are needed for
R_{\mu}^{2}f
to lie in
Y^{k-2}_{T}
).
Since we apply this with
k=N\geq 10
, the domain condition is
always satisfied.

Proof.

The identity (
5.3
) follows by integrating
Lemma
5.1
against
\rho(\mu)\,d\mu
. For the
bound (
5.5
), note that

R_{\mu}^{2}f=R_{\mu}(R_{\mu}f)
is the resolvent applied twice. Each
application satisfies

\|R_{\mu}g\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}H^{k}}\leq\|g\|_{L^{1}_{t}H^{k}}

uniformly in
\mu\geq 0
. Therefore

\|R_{\mu}^{2}f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}H^{k}}\leq\|f\|_{L^{1}_{t}H^{k}}
(the intermediate
L^{1}_{t}
norm is controlled since
R_{\mu}
maps

L^{1}_{t}H^{k}
to
L^{\infty}_{t}H^{k}\cap L^{1}_{t}H^{k}
by energy conservation
and dispersive decay). Integrating against
\mu\,\rho(\mu)
and using

(S4)
yields the result.
∎

5.3
Higher-order commutators

For multi-indices
|I|=k
, the commutator
[Z^{I},\mathcal{K}^{-1}]

is controlled by induction on
|I|
.

Proposition 5.4
.

For
|I|\leq N
,

Z^{I}\mathcal{K}^{-1}f=\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}f+\sum_{|J|<|I|}\sum_{p=1}^{2}c_{I,J,p}\,\mathcal{K}^{-1}_{(p)}Z^{J}f,

(5.6)

where
c_{I,J,p}
are combinatorial constants,
\mathcal{K}^{-1}_{(1)}=\mathcal{K}^{-1}
,
\mathcal{K}^{-1}_{(2)}
is defined in
(
5.4
), and the sum involves at most
|I|
terms with the
scaling generator producing the nontrivial contributions.

In particular, the nonlocal operator costs at most two additional
derivatives relative to the local case: the energy
\widetilde{E}_{N}

controls all terms on the right-hand side of (
5.6
)
provided
N\geq N_{\mathrm{LR}}+2
, where
N_{\mathrm{LR}}=8
is the
Lindblad–Rodnianski threshold.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
|I|
. The base case
|I|=0
is trivial.
For
|I|=k
, write
Z^{I}=Z_{a}Z^{I^{\prime}}
with
|I^{\prime}|=k-1
. Then

Z^{I}\mathcal{K}^{-1}f=Z_{a}\bigl(Z^{I^{\prime}}\mathcal{K}^{-1}f\bigr)=Z_{a}\Bigl(\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I^{\prime}}f+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|J|<|I^{\prime}|\\
p\leq 2\end{subarray}}c_{I^{\prime},J,p}\,\mathcal{K}^{-1}_{(p)}Z^{J}f\Bigr),

by the inductive hypothesis. Applying
Z_{a}
and using the single-field
commutator (Proposition
5.2
) on each term yields

\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}f
as the leading term plus lower-order
corrections involving
\mathcal{K}^{-1}_{(p)}Z^{J}f
with
|J|<|I|
and

p\leq 2
. Crucially, each application of
[S,\cdot]
generates one
factor of
\mathcal{K}^{-1}
(order 0) and one of

\mathcal{K}^{-1}_{(2)}
(order 0 but weighted by
\mu
), while
reducing
|J|
by one. Since
S
can appear at most
|I|
times in

Z^{I}
, the total number of lower-order terms is at most
2|I|
, and
the worst case involves
\mathcal{K}^{-1}_{(2)}
applied to
Z^{J}f

with
|J|=|I|-1
. This term is bounded in
H^{N-|I|+1}
, hence lies
within the energy
\widetilde{E}_{N}
provided
N-|I|+1\geq 2
, i.e.,

|I|\leq N-1
, which holds for all terms in the sum.

The derivative count is:
\widetilde{E}_{N}
with
N=8
suffices for the
local Einstein terms (as in Lindblad–Rodnianski), plus
2
additional
derivatives are consumed by the worst-case double resolvent

\mathcal{K}^{-1}_{(2)}
. Hence
N\geq 10
.
∎

Remark 5.5

(Dependence on dimension)
.

The argument relies crucially on the borderline
t^{-1}
decay in

3{+}1
dimensions. In
d{+}1
dimensions with
d\geq 4
, the free wave
decay rate
t^{-(d-1)/2}
is integrable, which would simplify the
energy argument (no ghost weight needed for the local terms).
Conversely, in
2{+}1
dimensions the
t^{-1/2}
decay is too slow to
close the bootstrap by the present method. We work exclusively in

d=3
throughout.

Remark 5.6
.

The threshold
N\geq 10
is sharp for this method. The two extra
derivatives come from the double resolvent
R_{\mu}^{2}
generated by the
scaling field commutator. If one restricts to initial data with compact
support (eliminating the need for boost and scaling estimates), the
threshold could be reduced. We do not pursue this here.

Remark 5.7

(Necessity of condition
(S4)
)
.

Condition
(S4)
is not merely a technical
convenience. Consider the spectral density

\rho(\mu)=\mu^{-1/2}
for
\mu\geq 1
and

\rho(\mu)=0
for
\mu<1
. This satisfies

(S1)
–
(S3)
(since

\int_{1}^{\infty}\mu^{-1}\cdot\mu^{-1/2}\,d\mu=\int_{1}^{\infty}\mu^{-3/2}\,d\mu=2<\infty
)
but violates
(S4)
, since

C_{\rho,1}=\int_{1}^{\infty}\mu\cdot\mu^{-1/2}\,d\mu=\int_{1}^{\infty}\mu^{1/2}\,d\mu=\infty.

For this density, the double resolvent operator

\mathcal{K}^{-1}_{(2)}
defined in (
5.4
)
has divergent norm: for any nonzero

f\in X^{k}_{T}
,

\|\mathcal{K}^{-1}_{(2)}f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}H^{k}}=\int_{1}^{\infty}\mu^{1/2}\,\|R_{\mu}^{2}f\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}H^{k}}\,d\mu=\infty,

since
\|R_{\mu}^{2}f\|\geq c>0
uniformly for
\mu\geq 1

(by the lower bound from energy conservation: the
retarded resolvent applied to a nonzero source
produces a nonzero solution whose energy is bounded
below by the source energy). Consequently, the
scaling field commutator

[S,\mathcal{K}^{-1}]=-2\mathcal{K}^{-1}+2\mathcal{K}^{-1}_{(2)}

(Proposition
5.2
) is unbounded on

X^{k}_{T}
, and the inductive commutator estimates of
Proposition
5.4
fail. This shows
that condition
(S4)
is necessary for the
stability argument, not merely sufficient.

6
Memory Operator Estimates

Define the memory term

\mathcal{M}(t)\coloneqq\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(\mu)\int_{0}^{t}G_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ret}}(t-\tau)*_{x}N_{2}(\tau)\,d\tau\,d\mu,

(6.1)

where
*_{x}
denotes spatial convolution and
N_{2}
is the nonlocal source
term from (
2.5
).

Lemma 6.1

(Memory bound)
.

Under Assumption
3.1
and the bootstrap hypothesis
(
4.9
), if the nonlocal source satisfies

\|N_{2}(\tau)\|_{H^{k}}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+\tau)^{-1-\delta}

(6.2)

for some
\delta>0
and all
k\leq N
, then

\|\mathcal{M}(t)\|_{H^{k}}\leq\frac{C_{\rho}}{\delta}\,\varepsilon^{2},\qquad\forall\,t\geq 0,\;k\leq N.

(6.3)

Proof.

Step 1: Temporal convolution.

For each fixed
\mu>0
, define

\mathcal{M}_{\mu}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}G_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ret}}(t-\tau)*_{x}N_{2}(\tau)\,d\tau
. This solves

(-\Box+\mu)\mathcal{M}_{\mu}=N_{2}
with zero initial data. Standard
energy estimates for the Klein–Gordon equation give

\|\mathcal{M}_{\mu}(t)\|_{H^{k}}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\|N_{2}(\tau)\|_{H^{k}}\,d\tau\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\int_{0}^{t}(1+\tau)^{-1-\delta}\,d\tau\leq\frac{C\varepsilon^{2}}{\delta},

(6.4)

uniformly in
\mu\geq 0
and
t\geq 0
. The
\mu
-uniformity is
crucial: the mass term
\mu|\mathcal{M}_{\mu}|^{2}
in the Klein–Gordon
energy is nonnegative and hence only improves the bound.

Step 2: Spectral integration.

By Minkowski’s integral inequality,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{M}(t)\|_{H^{k}}

\displaystyle=\Bigl\|\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(\mu)\,\mathcal{M}_{\mu}(t)\,d\mu\Bigr\|_{H^{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(\mu)\,\|\mathcal{M}_{\mu}(t)\|_{H^{k}}\,d\mu

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\varepsilon^{2}}{\delta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(\mu)\,d\mu=\frac{C\varepsilon^{2}\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}}{\delta}.

(6.5)

Step 3: Klainerman vector field derivatives.

For
Z^{I}
with
|I|\leq N
,
Proposition
5.4
gives

\displaystyle\|Z^{I}\mathcal{M}(t)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}N_{2}\|_{L^{2}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|J|<|I|\\
p\leq 2\end{subarray}}|c_{I,J,p}|\,\|\mathcal{K}^{-1}_{(p)}Z^{J}N_{2}\|_{L^{2}}.

(6.6)

Each term is bounded by
C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{2}/\delta
using
Steps 1–2 (with
\mathcal{K}^{-1}_{(2)}
controlled by
condition
(S4)
).
∎

Lemma 6.2

(Temporal derivative of memory)
.

Under the same hypotheses,

\|\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}(t)\|_{H^{N-1}}\leq C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-1-\delta}+C(\bm{\rho})\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\delta}.

(6.7)

In particular,
\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}\in L^{\infty}_{t}H^{N-1}
.

Proof.

For each
\mu>0
,
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
solves

(-\partial_{t}^{2}+\Delta+\mu)\mathcal{M}_{\mu}=N_{2}
with vanishing Cauchy
data. Differentiating in time,
\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
solves the
same Klein–Gordon equation with source
\partial_{t}N_{2}
and
initial data
(\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}_{\mu})|_{t=0}=0
,

(\partial_{t}^{2}\mathcal{M}_{\mu})|_{t=0}=N_{2}(0)
.

By Duhamel’s principle,

\|\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}_{\mu}(t)\|_{H^{N-1}}\leq\|N_{2}(0)\|_{H^{N-1}}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial_{t}N_{2}(\tau)\|_{H^{N-1}}\,d\tau.

(6.8)

Under the bootstrap,

\|N_{2}(0)\|_{H^{N-1}}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}

and

\|\partial_{t}N_{2}(\tau)\|_{H^{N-1}}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+\tau)^{-2}

(since
\partial_{t}N_{2}
involves one more time derivative of
u
, which
brings one more power of decay). Integrating against
\rho
yields the
bound.
∎

Lemma 6.3

(Decay of the memory time derivative)
.

Under Assumption
3.1
(in particular

(S5)
), for
t\geq 0
:

\|\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}(t)\|_{H^{N-2}}\leq C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-1}.

(6.9)

Proof.

For each
\mu>0
, the function
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
solves

(-\Box+\mu)\mathcal{M}_{\mu}=N_{2}
with vanishing Cauchy data

\mathcal{M}_{\mu}|_{t=0}=0
,

\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}_{\mu}|_{t=0}=0
.
From the Klein–Gordon equation, the second initial condition
for
\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
is

\partial_{t}^{2}\mathcal{M}_{\mu}|_{t=0}=(\Delta-\mu)\mathcal{M}_{\mu}|_{t=0}+N_{2}(0)=N_{2}(0)
.
By Duhamel’s principle,
\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
decomposes as

\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}_{\mu}(t)=\underbrace{S_{\mu}(t)N_{2}(0)}_{\text{free KG evolution}}+\underbrace{R_{\mu}[\partial_{t}N_{2}](t)}_{\text{retarded memory}},

(6.10)

where
S_{\mu}(t)g=\sin(t\sqrt{-\Delta+\mu})\,g/\sqrt{-\Delta+\mu}

is the Klein–Gordon propagator with initial velocity
g
, and

R_{\mu}=(-\Box+\mu)^{-1}_{\mathrm{ret}}
is the retarded resolvent.
Integrating against
\rho(\mu)\,d\mu
:

\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}(t)=\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(\mu)\,S_{\mu}(t)N_{2}(0)\,d\mu}_{\mathrm{(I)}}+\underbrace{\mathcal{K}^{-1}[\partial_{t}N_{2}](t)}_{\mathrm{(II)}}.

(6.11)

Term (I): Spectral averaging.

This is the spectrally integrated free Klein–Gordon
propagator of Lemma
3.15
. Each
individual
S_{\mu}(t)N_{2}(0)
is a free KG solution
whose
H^{N-2}
norm is time-independent (energy
conservation):

\|S_{\mu}(t)N_{2}(0)\|_{H^{N-2}}\leq\mu^{-1/2}\|N_{2}(0)\|_{H^{N-2}}\leq C\mu^{-1/2}\varepsilon^{2}
.
A naïve integration against
\rho
gives the
uniform bound
C_{\rho,-1/2}\,\varepsilon^{2}
— no
decay.

The decay comes from the destructive interference
between different mass levels. By
Lemma
3.15
(ii), condition
(S5)

yields:

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{(I)}\|_{H^{N-2}}

\displaystyle=\Bigl\|\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(\mu)\,S_{\mu}(t)N_{2}(0)\,d\mu\Bigr\|_{H^{N-2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(\bm{\rho})}{1+t}\,\|N_{2}(0)\|_{H^{N-2}}\leq\frac{C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{2}}{1+t}.

(6.12)

The mechanism is integration by parts in the mass
variable
\mu
: the oscillatory factor

\frac{\sin(t\sqrt{|\xi|^{2}+\mu})}{\sqrt{|\xi|^{2}+\mu}}

becomes rapidly oscillating for large
t
, and the
IBP produces a factor of
t^{-1}
at the cost of
one
\mu
-derivative of
\rho
, which is controlled
by
C_{\rho^{\prime}}
from
(S5)
. Crucially, there is

no loss of spatial derivatives
: the
H^{N-2}

norm of
g
on the right matches that of
I(t)
on
the left.

Term (II): Retarded memory of
\partial_{t}N_{2}
.

The time derivative
\partial_{t}N_{2}
involves one
more time derivative of
u
compared to
N_{2}
,
gaining one power of decay:

\|\partial_{t}N_{2}(\tau)\|_{H^{N-2}}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+\tau)^{-2}.

(6.13)

This follows from

\partial_{t}N_{2}\sim(\partial_{t}u)(\partial u)+u(\partial_{t}\partial u)
, with

|\partial_{t}u|\leq C\varepsilon(1+t)^{-1}
and

\|\partial_{t}\partial u\|_{H^{N-2}}\leq C\varepsilon(1+t)^{-1}
from the bootstrap.

We split the Duhamel integral at
\tau=t/2
:

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{(II)}\|_{H^{N-2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial_{t}N_{2}(\tau)\|_{H^{N-2}}\,d\tau

\displaystyle=\|\rho\|_{L^{1}}\Bigl(\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t/2}\cdots}_{\text{distant}}+\underbrace{\int_{t/2}^{t}\cdots}_{\text{recent}}\Bigr).

(6.14)

The
recent past
(
\tau\geq t/2
) inherits the
source’s pointwise decay:

\int_{t/2}^{t}\|\partial_{t}N_{2}(\tau)\|_{H^{N-2}}\,d\tau\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\int_{t/2}^{t}(1+\tau)^{-2}\,d\tau\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-1}.

The
distant past
(
\tau\leq t/2
) is controlled
by the tail of the source’s
L^{1}
norm. Define

\Sigma(t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}\|\partial_{t}N_{2}(\tau)\|_{H^{N-2}}\,d\tau
. Since
\|\partial_{t}N_{2}\|\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+\tau)^{-2}
,

\Sigma(t/2)=\int_{t/2}^{\infty}C\varepsilon^{2}(1+\tau)^{-2}\,d\tau\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-1}.

The distant-past Duhamel integral
\int_{0}^{t/2}
is
bounded by
\Sigma(0)-\Sigma(t/2)\leq\Sigma(0)\leq C\varepsilon^{2}
. But this uniform bound does not
suffice; we need decay. The key observation is that
the KG propagator for
\tau\leq t/2
has been running
for time
\geq t/2
, and the spectral integration
provides averaging. Precisely, writing

\mathcal{K}^{-1}[\partial_{t}N_{2}]^{\mathrm{distant}}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(\mu)\int_{0}^{t/2}G_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ret}}(t-\tau)*_{x}\partial_{t}N_{2}(\tau)\,d\tau\,d\mu,

each inner integral

w_{\mu}(t)=\int_{0}^{t/2}G_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ret}}(t-\tau)*_{x}\partial_{t}N_{2}(\tau)\,d\tau
is a KG solution on

[t/2,t]
with data
(w_{\mu}(t/2),\partial_{t}w_{\mu}(t/2))

given by the Duhamel evaluation at
t/2
. Applying
Lemma
3.15
(ii) to the free
evolution part of
w_{\mu}
on
[t/2,t]
gives

(t-t/2)^{-1}=2/t
decay for the spectrally integrated
quantity, with a coefficient bounded by

C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{2}
from the

H^{N-2}
norm of the data. The source contribution
on
[t/2,t]
is already handled by the recent-past
bound above.

Combining:

\|\mathrm{(II)}\|_{H^{N-2}}\leq C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-1}.

(6.15)

Combined bound.
From
(
6
) and (
6.15
):

\|\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}(t)\|_{H^{N-2}}\leq\frac{C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{2}}{1+t}+\frac{C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{2}}{1+t}=\frac{C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{2}}{1+t}.\qed

Proposition 6.4

(Energy remainder integrability)
.

Under Assumption
3.1
and the global bounds of
Theorem
10.1
, the IBP remainder

\mathcal{R}_{1}(t)=\int w\,Z^{I}u\cdot\partial_{t}[\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}N_{2}]\,dx

satisfies

\mathcal{R}_{1}\in L^{1}([0,\infty)),\qquad\int_{0}^{\infty}|\mathcal{R}_{1}(t)|\,dt\leq C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{3}.

(6.16)

Remark 6.5
.

We emphasize that
\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}
is

not
in
L^{1}_{t}H^{N-2}
: Lemma
6.3

gives rate
(1+t)^{-1}
, and

\int_{0}^{\infty}(1+t)^{-1}\,dt=\infty
. The
integrability of
\mathcal{R}_{1}
comes from the
additional Klainerman–Sobolev factor

\|Z^{I}u\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\varepsilon(1+t)^{-1}
,
which provides the missing half-power:

|\mathcal{R}_{1}|\leq C\varepsilon^{3}(1+t)^{-2}\in L^{1}
.

Proof of Proposition
6.4
.

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

|\mathcal{R}_{1}(t)|\leq e^{\delta_{0}}\|Z^{I}u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\cdot\|\partial_{t}[\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}N_{2}](t)\|_{L^{2}}\cdot\mathrm{vol}(\mathrm{supp}\,w)^{1/2}
.
Since the ghost weight
w
is bounded and

\mathrm{vol}(\mathrm{supp}\,w)=\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{R}_{1}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq C\|Z^{I}u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\cdot\|\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}(t)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon(1+t)^{-1}\cdot C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-1}

\displaystyle=C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{3}(1+t)^{-2},

(6.17)

using
\|Z^{I}u\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\varepsilon(1+t)^{-1}
(Klainerman–Sobolev)
and
\|\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}\|_{H^{N-2}}\leq C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-1}

(Lemma
6.3
). Integrating:

\int_{0}^{\infty}|\mathcal{R}_{1}|\,dt\leq C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1+t)^{-2}\,dt=C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{3}
.
∎

Remark 6.6

(Forward-in-time estimates)
.

All estimates in this section are
forward in time
: the retarded
kernel ensures
\mathcal{M}(t)
depends only on
N_{2}(\tau)
for

\tau\leq t
, so no acausality arises. The bootstrap hypothesis on

[0,T^{*}]
controls all inputs to the memory operator on
[0,T^{*}]
.

Remark 6.7

(Role of infrared regularity)
.

Condition
(S3)
(
\int\mu^{-1}\rho\,d\mu<\infty
) is needed when
one extracts decay rates from the memory. Without it, the
\mu\to 0

contribution to
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
behaves like the massless wave
equation, producing tails that decay only as
(1+t)^{-1}
without
additional improvement. With
(S3)
, the small-
\mu
portion is
suppressed, ensuring the improved rate in
Lemma
6.3
.

7
Energy Estimates: The Ghost Weight Method

This section establishes the central energy inequality via the
Lindblad–Rodnianski ghost weight, with a complete treatment of the
interaction between the ghost weight and the memory operator.

7.1
Commuted equations

Applying
Z^{I}
with
|I|\leq N
to (
2.5
),

\Box Z^{I}u=Z^{I}F+[Z^{I},\Box_{g}]u+Z^{I}\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}.

(7.1)

The commutator
[Z^{I},\Box_{g}]
decomposes as

[Z^{I},\Box_{g}]=[Z^{I},\Box]+[Z^{I},\Box_{g}-\Box]
. The first part is
standard:

[Z^{I},\Box]u=\sum_{|J|<|I|}c_{I,J}\,\Box Z^{J}u,

(7.2)

arising from repeated application of
[S,\Box]=2\Box
. The second part
encodes the quasilinear perturbation:

[Z^{I},\Box_{g}-\Box]u=[Z^{I},H^{\alpha\beta}(h)\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}]u=\sum_{|J|+|K|\leq|I|}c^{\prime}_{I,J,K}\,(Z^{J}H^{\alpha\beta})\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}Z^{K}u.

(7.3)

For the nonlocal term, by Proposition
5.4
,

Z^{I}\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}=\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}N_{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|J|<|I|\\
p\leq 2\end{subarray}}c_{I,J,p}\,\mathcal{K}^{-1}_{(p)}Z^{J}N_{2}.

(7.4)

7.2
The ghost weight energy identity

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
v
be a smooth solution of
\Box v=G
on

[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}w(|\partial_{t}v|^{2}+|\nabla v|^{2})\,dx

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}w\,\partial_{t}v\cdot G\,dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\partial_{t}w)(|\partial_{t}v|^{2}+|\nabla v|^{2})\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{t}v\,\nabla v\cdot\nabla w\,dx.

(7.5)

Proof.

Multiply
\Box v=G
by
w\,\partial_{t}v
and integrate over

\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Since
\Box v=-\partial_{t}^{2}v+\Delta v
, the term

-w\partial_{t}v\cdot\partial_{t}^{2}v
gives, after integration by parts
in time,

-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}\int w|\partial_{t}v|^{2}\,dx+\frac{1}{2}\int(\partial_{t}w)|\partial_{t}v|^{2}\,dx
.
The term
w\partial_{t}v\cdot\Delta v
gives, after integration by parts
in space,

-\int w\,\partial_{i}(\partial_{t}v)\partial_{i}v\,dx-\int(\partial_{i}w)\partial_{t}v\,\partial_{i}v\,dx
.
The first integral equals

-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}\int w|\nabla v|^{2}\,dx+\frac{1}{2}\int(\partial_{t}w)|\nabla v|^{2}\,dx
.
Collecting terms yields (
7.1
).
∎

7.3
Analysis of the weight terms

Since
w=e^{q(t-r)}
, we have

\partial_{t}w=q^{\prime}(t-r)\,w,\qquad\partial_{i}w=-q^{\prime}(t-r)\frac{x_{i}}{r}\,w.

(7.6)

Therefore the weight terms in (
7.1
) combine to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}q^{\prime}(t-r)\,w\bigl(|\partial_{t}v|^{2}+|\nabla v|^{2}\bigr)-q^{\prime}(t-r)\frac{x_{i}}{r}\,w\,\partial_{t}v\,\partial_{i}v

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}q^{\prime}(t-r)\,w\bigl(|\partial_{t}v|^{2}+|\nabla v|^{2}-2\omega^{i}\partial_{t}v\,\partial_{i}v\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}q^{\prime}(t-r)\,w\,|\underline{L}v|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}q^{\prime}(t-r)\,w\,|\cancel{\nabla}v|^{2},

(7.7)

where we used
|\partial_{t}v|^{2}+|\nabla v|^{2}-2\omega^{i}\partial_{t}v\,\partial_{i}v=|\partial_{t}v-\omega^{i}\partial_{i}v|^{2}+|\nabla v|^{2}-|\omega^{i}\partial_{i}v|^{2}=|\underline{L}v|^{2}+|\cancel{\nabla}v|^{2}
.

The key point is that both terms on the right of (
7.3
)
are
nonnegative
, since
q^{\prime}\geq 0
. This positivity is the
mechanism by which the ghost weight absorbs the borderline terms.

Define the
ghost weight flux
:

\mathcal{F}_{N}(t)\coloneqq\sum_{|I|\leq N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}q^{\prime}(t-r)\,w\,\bigl(|\underline{L}Z^{I}u|^{2}+|\cancel{\nabla}Z^{I}u|^{2}\bigr)\,dx.

(7.8)

Then the energy identity for the commuted system reads:

\widetilde{E}_{N}^{\prime}(t)+\mathcal{F}_{N}(t)=2\sum_{|I|\leq N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}w\,\partial_{t}Z^{I}u\cdot\bigl(Z^{I}G_{\mathrm{loc}}+Z^{I}\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}\bigr)\,dx,

(7.9)

where
G_{\mathrm{loc}}
collects the local source and commutator terms.

7.4
Estimates for local terms with ghost weight

We follow the argument of
[
7
]
, Sections 7–8, adapted to the
present notation. The local source satisfies the weak null condition
(
2.8
), which in the ghost weight framework yields:

Lemma 7.2
.

Under the bootstrap hypothesis,

\sum_{|I|\leq N}\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}w\,\partial_{t}Z^{I}u\cdot Z^{I}G_{\mathrm{loc}}\,dx\Bigr|\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-1-\delta_{0}}\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)^{1/2}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{F}_{N}(t).

(7.10)

Proof.

Step 1: Null form terms.

Using (
2.8
), the contribution of
Z^{I}Q
to the
energy integral is bounded by

\displaystyle\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}w\,\partial_{t}Z^{I}u\cdot Z^{I}Q\,dx\Bigr|

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}w\,|\partial_{t}Z^{I}u|\bigl(|\bar{\partial}Z^{I_{1}}u||\partial Z^{I_{2}}u|+|\partial Z^{I_{1}}u||\partial Z^{I_{2}}u|(1+t+r)^{-1}\bigr)\,dx,

(7.11)

where
|I_{1}|+|I_{2}|\leq|I|
. For the first term, the good derivative

\bar{\partial}
provides the improved decay (
4.12
). In
the
transition region

\{|t-r|\leq 1\}
where
q^{\prime}\neq 0
, we have

|\bar{\partial}u|\leq C\varepsilon(1+t)^{-2}
, and the ghost flux

\mathcal{F}_{N}
controls
|\underline{L}Z^{I}u|^{2}
. Using
Cauchy–Schwarz, the transition-region contribution is absorbed by

\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{F}_{N}(t)+C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-2}\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)
.

In the
exterior region

\{t-r\geq 1\}
, the factor

(1+|t-r|)^{-1}
from (
4.12
) provides additional
decay. In the
interior region

\{t-r\leq-1\}
,
q^{\prime}=0
and

w=1
, reducing to the standard energy estimate with the full

(1+t)^{-3/2}
null-form decay.

Step 2: Quasilinear commutator terms.

The commutator
[Z^{I},\Box_{g}-\Box]u
involves terms of the form

(Z^{J}h)\partial^{2}Z^{K}u
with
|J|+|K|\leq|I|
. Using

|Z^{J}h|\leq C\varepsilon(1+t)^{-1}
from (
4.11
),
these are bounded by
C\varepsilon(1+t)^{-1}|\partial^{2}Z^{K}u|
,
which, when inserted into the energy integral and estimated by
Cauchy–Schwarz, contributes

C\varepsilon(1+t)^{-1}\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)
. Under the bootstrap,
this is

\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-1}\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)^{1/2}
.

Step 3: Higher-order commutator
[Z^{I},\Box]
from scaling.

This produces terms
\Box Z^{J}u
with
|J|<|I|
, which are bounded
inductively by the right-hand side of the equation at lower order.

Combining all contributions and choosing
\delta_{0}
sufficiently small
relative to the bootstrap constant, one obtains (
7.10
).
The factor
\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{F}_{N}(t)
on the right is the “cost” of
absorbing the transition-region terms; it will be absorbed by the
positive flux on the left of (
7.9
).
∎

7.5
Ghost weight compatibility with memory

This is the key technical result addressing the interaction between
the ghost weight and the nonlocal operator.

Proposition 7.3

(Ghost weight–memory compatibility)
.

Under Assumption
3.1
and the bootstrap hypothesis,

\sum_{|I|\leq N}\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}w\,\partial_{t}Z^{I}u\cdot Z^{I}\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}\,dx\Bigr|\leq\frac{1}{8}\mathcal{F}_{N}(t)+C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{3}(1+t)^{-1}+C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{4}(1+t)^{-2}.

(7.12)

Moreover, if the improved source bound

\|Z^{I}N_{2}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-1-\delta}
holds for

|I|\leq N-1
(Lemma
8.3
), then the
bound improves to

\sum_{|I|\leq N}\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}w\,\partial_{t}Z^{I}u\cdot Z^{I}\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}\,dx\Bigr|\leq\frac{1}{8}\mathcal{F}_{N}(t)+C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{3}(1+t)^{-1-\delta/2}.

(7.13)

Proof.

We decompose the nonlocal contribution using the commutator formula
(
7.4
):

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}w\,\partial_{t}Z^{I}u\cdot Z^{I}\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}w\,\partial_{t}Z^{I}u\cdot\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}N_{2}\,dx}_{(\mathrm{I})}+\underbrace{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|J|<|I|\\
p\leq 2\end{subarray}}c_{I,J,p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}w\,\partial_{t}Z^{I}u\cdot\mathcal{K}^{-1}_{(p)}Z^{J}N_{2}\,dx}_{(\mathrm{II})}.

(7.14)

Term (I): Leading memory contribution.

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

|(\mathrm{I})|\leq\|w^{1/2}\partial_{t}Z^{I}u(t)\|_{L^{2}}\cdot\|w^{1/2}\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}N_{2}(t)\|_{L^{2}}.

(7.15)

The first factor is
\leq\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)^{1/2}\leq\sqrt{2C_{0}}\,\varepsilon
. For the second, since
1\leq w\leq e^{\delta_{0}}
,

\|w^{1/2}\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}N_{2}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq e^{\delta_{0}/2}\|\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}N_{2}(t)\|_{L^{2}}.

(7.16)

Now we need the instantaneous bound on
\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}N_{2}(t)

(not just the cumulative memory bound of Lemma
6.1
).
By the Stieltjes representation,
\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}N_{2}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(\mu)\mathcal{M}_{\mu}^{(I)}(t)\,d\mu
where

\mathcal{M}_{\mu}^{(I)}
solves
(-\Box+\mu)\mathcal{M}_{\mu}^{(I)}=Z^{I}N_{2}
with zero data. By energy estimates:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{M}_{\mu}^{(I)}(t)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\|Z^{I}N_{2}(\tau)\|_{L^{2}}\,d\tau\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\int_{0}^{t}(1+\tau)^{-1-\delta}\,d\tau\leq\frac{C\varepsilon^{2}}{\delta}.

(7.17)

However, we can do better by splitting the integral. For the

recent past

\tau\in[t-1,t]
and the
distant past

\tau\in[0,t-1]
:

The recent-past contribution satisfies

\Bigl\|\int_{t-1}^{t}G_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ret}}(t-\tau)*_{x}Z^{I}N_{2}(\tau)\,d\tau\Bigr\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|Z^{I}N_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}([t-1,t];L^{2})}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-1-\delta}.

(7.18)

The key observation is that the memory
\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}N_{2}

is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}
(by
C\varepsilon^{2}/\delta
, from
Lemma
6.1
) but does not decay in time (the
L^{2}
norm
of the Klein–Gordon propagator is controlled by energy, not by
dispersive decay:
\|v(s)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mu^{-1/2}\|f\|_{L^{2}}
is
time-independent). However, the
time derivative
of the memory
decays (Lemma
6.3
). We exploit this via integration
by parts.

Step 1: Integration by parts in time.

Write the memory pairing as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}w\,\partial_{t}Z^{I}u\cdot\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}N_{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}w\,Z^{I}u\cdot\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}N_{2}\,dx}_{=:\,\mathcal{B}_{I}(t)}-\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}w\,Z^{I}u\cdot\partial_{t}[\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}N_{2}]\,dx}_{=:\,\mathcal{R}_{1}(t)}-\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\partial_{t}w)\,Z^{I}u\cdot\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}N_{2}\,dx}_{=:\,\mathcal{R}_{2}(t)}.

(7.19)

Step 2: Boundary functional
\mathcal{B}_{I}
(absorbed into energy).

By Cauchy–Schwarz, the bootstrap, and Lemma
6.1
:

|\mathcal{B}_{I}(t)|\leq e^{\delta_{0}}\|Z^{I}u(t)\|_{L^{2}}\cdot\|\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}N_{2}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{3}/\delta.

(7.20)

Since
\mathcal{B}_{I}
enters the energy identity as a total

t
-derivative, define the
modified energy

\widetilde{E}_{N}^{*}(t)\coloneqq\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)-2\sum_{|I|\leq N}\mathcal{B}_{I}(t)
.
Then
|\widetilde{E}_{N}^{*}-\widetilde{E}_{N}|\leq C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{3}/\delta
. For

\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
with
\varepsilon_{0}
sufficiently small
(depending on
\bm{\rho}
and
\delta
), this gives

|\widetilde{E}_{N}^{*}-\widetilde{E}_{N}|\leq\tfrac{1}{4}C_{0}\varepsilon^{2}.

In particular, the bootstrap hypothesis

\widetilde{E}_{N}\leq 2C_{0}\varepsilon^{2}
implies

\widetilde{E}_{N}^{*}\leq 3C_{0}\varepsilon^{2},

and conversely

\widetilde{E}_{N}^{*}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}C_{0}\varepsilon^{2}

implies

\widetilde{E}_{N}\leq C_{0}\varepsilon^{2}
. The modified energy
\widetilde{E}_{N}^{*}
remains strictly positive
since
|\mathcal{B}_{I}|\leq C\varepsilon^{3}\ll\widetilde{E}_{N}
under
the bootstrap assumption. More precisely, the positivity

\widetilde{E}_{N}^{*}\geq\widetilde{E}_{N}-C\varepsilon^{3}>0

is
non-circular: it uses only
\widetilde{E}_{N}\geq 0
(manifest from the
definition (
4.7
)) and the memory bound
(Lemma
6.1
), not the bootstrap improvement itself.

Step 3: Remainder
\mathcal{R}_{1}
(borderline from
memory derivative decay).

|\mathcal{R}_{1}(t)|\leq e^{\delta_{0}}\|Z^{I}u(t)\|_{L^{2}}\cdot\|\partial_{t}[\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}N_{2}](t)\|_{L^{2}}.

(7.21)

By the bootstrap,
\|Z^{I}u(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\varepsilon
. By
Lemma
6.3
(spectral averaging):

\|\partial_{t}[\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}N_{2}](t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-1}.

Therefore

|\mathcal{R}_{1}(t)|\leq C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{3}(1+t)^{-1}.

(7.22)

The rate
(1+t)^{-1}
is borderline:
\int_{0}^{T}(1+t)^{-1}\,dt=\log(1+T)
, which diverges. This is handled by
the two-stage bootstrap structure in
Theorem
10.1
.

If the improved source bound
(
8.4
) holds, then

\|\partial_{t}N_{2}\|_{H^{N-2}}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-2-\delta}
, and the

t/2
-splitting in the retarded memory gives

\|\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}\|_{H^{N-2}}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-1-\delta}

(the recent-past integral improves from

(1+t)^{-1}
to
(1+t)^{-1-\delta}
). In this case:

|\mathcal{R}_{1}(t)|\leq C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{3}(1+t)^{-1-\delta},

(7.23)

which is integrable:

\int_{0}^{\infty}(1+t)^{-1-\delta}\,dt=1/\delta<\infty
.
This is the key gain from the IBP: the non-decaying
memory is replaced by its decaying time derivative,
and the spectral averaging mechanism
(Lemma
3.15
) plus the improved
source decay converts the borderline rate into an
integrable one.

Step 4: Remainder
\mathcal{R}_{2}
(localized to cone, absorbed
by ghost flux).

Since
\partial_{t}w=q^{\prime}(t-r)w
is supported in

\Omega_{\mathrm{cone}}(t)=\{|t-r|\leq 1\}
:

|\mathcal{R}_{2}(t)|\leq C\delta_{0}\|Z^{I}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathrm{cone})}\|\mathcal{K}^{-1}Z^{I}N_{2}\|_{L^{2}}.

In
\Omega_{\mathrm{cone}}
, decompose

\|Z^{I}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathrm{cone})}^{2}\leq C\bigl(\mathcal{F}_{N}(t)/q^{\prime}_{\min}+\widetilde{E}_{N}(1+t)^{-2}\bigr)

(from the ghost flux for
\underline{L}
-derivatives, and
Klainerman–Sobolev for
\bar{\partial}
-derivatives). By Young’s
inequality
ab\leq\frac{1}{4}a^{2}+b^{2}
:

C\delta_{0}\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{N}(t)}\cdot C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{2}/\delta\leq\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{F}_{N}(t)+C^{2}\delta_{0}^{2}C(\bm{\rho})^{2}\varepsilon^{4}/\delta^{2}.

(7.24)

The
\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{F}_{N}
is absorbed by the positive flux on the
left of the energy identity. The constant term is

O(\delta_{0}^{2}\varepsilon^{4})\leq C\varepsilon^{3}(1+t)^{-1-\delta_{*}}
for
\varepsilon\leq\delta_{0}
.

Step 5: Lower-order terms
from
Proposition
5.4
are handled identically with

|J|<|I|
(induction).

Step 6: Assembling.
Using only the bootstrap
hypothesis (without the improved source decay):

(\widetilde{E}_{N}^{*})^{\prime}(t)+\frac{1}{8}\mathcal{F}_{N}(t)\leq C(\bm{\rho},C_{0})\varepsilon^{3}(1+t)^{-1-\delta_{0}}+C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{3}(1+t)^{-1}.

(7.25)

The first term (from the local null terms,
Lemma
7.2
) is integrable; the second
(from
\mathcal{R}_{1}
, the memory derivative) is
borderline. Integrating (
7.25
)
over
[0,T]
:

\widetilde{E}_{N}^{*}(T)\leq\widetilde{E}_{N}^{*}(0)+\frac{C\varepsilon^{3}}{\delta_{0}}+C\varepsilon^{3}\log(2+T).

(7.26)

This yields almost-global existence:

\widetilde{E}_{N}^{*}(T)\leq C_{0}\varepsilon^{2}
for

T\leq T_{1}\coloneqq\exp(C_{0}/(C\varepsilon))
, which
is sufficient to derive the improved source decay of
Section
8
.

Once the improved source bound
(
8.4
) is available,

\mathcal{R}_{1}
improves to the integrable bound
(
7.23
), and the energy inequality
becomes

(\widetilde{E}_{N}^{*})^{\prime}(t)+\frac{1}{8}\mathcal{F}_{N}(t)\leq C(\bm{\rho},C_{0})\varepsilon^{3}(1+t)^{-1-\delta_{*}},\qquad\delta_{*}=\min(\delta_{0},\delta/2)>0,

(7.27)

which is integrable, yielding the full bootstrap
improvement and global existence.
∎

Remark 7.4

(Why IBP and not
L^{2}
dispersive estimates)
.

One might attempt to bound the ghost-memory pairing using an
L^{2}\to L^{2}
dispersive decay estimate for the Klein–Gordon propagator.
However, the
L^{2}
norm of the KG solution
v(s)=G_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ret}}(s)*_{x}f
satisfies

\mu\|v(s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\mathcal{E}=\frac{1}{2}\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(energy conservation), giving
\|v(s)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\mu^{-1/2}\|f\|_{L^{2}}

— a bound that is
independent of
s
. The standard dispersive
decay
|v(s,x)|\leq Cs^{-3/2}\|f\|_{L^{1}}
is an
L^{1}\to L^{\infty}

estimate, not
L^{2}\to L^{2}
. The IBP approach in Steps 1–4 circumvents
this entirely by transferring the time derivative from
u
to

\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}
, where Lemma
6.3
provides the
needed integrability. This explains why standard dispersive methods
alone are insufficient for the nonlocal system: the memory requires
an energy-level argument, not a pointwise one.

Remark 7.5

(Why retarded causality is essential here)
.

The IBP proof of Proposition
7.3
uses two properties
of the retarded kernel:

(i)

\mathcal{M}_{\mu}(t)
depends only on
N_{2}(\tau)
for
\tau\leq t
,
so the integrand is controlled by the bootstrap hypothesis on
[0,t]
.

(ii)

The vanishing initial data

\mathcal{M}_{\mu}|_{t=0}=\partial_{t}\mathcal{M}_{\mu}|_{t=0}=0
ensures
that the memory starts at zero and grows only as fast as the source.

An acausal kernel would violate both: the integral would extend to

\tau>t
, and the “initial” data for
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
would depend on
the solution in the future. This would create terms on the right side
of the energy identity that are not controlled by the bootstrap on

[0,T^{*}]
, invalidating the argument.

7.6
The complete energy inequality

Bootstrap input.
We assume the bootstrap hypothesis
(
4.9
):
\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)\leq 2C_{0}\varepsilon^{2}
on

[0,T^{*}]
. The goal is to show
\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)\leq C_{0}\varepsilon^{2}

(strict improvement), which forces
T^{*}=\infty
by continuity.

Combining Lemma
7.2
and
Proposition
7.3
in the identity
(
7.9
):

\displaystyle\widetilde{E}_{N}^{\prime}(t)+\mathcal{F}_{N}(t)

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-1-\delta_{0}}\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)^{1/2}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{F}_{N}(t)+C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{3}(1+t)^{-1-\delta/2}.

(7.28)

Absorbing the local flux contributions and using the IBP-based
Proposition
7.3
:
the modified energy
\widetilde{E}_{N}^{*}=\widetilde{E}_{N}-2\sum\mathcal{B}_{I}
satisfies
(
7.27
), which is integrable. Integration yields:

\widetilde{E}_{N}^{*}(t)\leq\widetilde{E}_{N}^{*}(0)+\frac{C(\bm{\rho},C_{0})\varepsilon^{3}}{\delta_{*}}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}+C^{\prime}\varepsilon^{3}\leq C_{0}\varepsilon^{2}/2,

(7.29)

for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small. Since

|\widetilde{E}_{N}-\widetilde{E}_{N}^{*}|\leq C\varepsilon^{3}
, this gives

\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)\leq C_{0}\varepsilon^{2}
, strictly improving the
bootstrap hypothesis (
4.9
). The bootstrap closes for

\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
sufficiently small, depending only on
N

and
\bm{\rho}
.

8.1
Null structure of local terms

The quadratic nonlinearity
Q_{\mu\nu}(\partial h,\partial h)
in
(
2.3
) inherits the null structure of the Einstein equations
in harmonic gauge. Specifically, it satisfies the
weak null
condition
of Lindblad–Rodnianski:

Lemma 8.1

(
[
6
, Proposition 3.2]
; see also
[
23
]
)
.

In harmonic gauge, the quadratic nonlinearity satisfies

|Q(\partial h,\partial h)|\leq C\bigl(|\bar{\partial}h||\partial h|+|\partial h|^{2}(1+t+r)^{-1}\bigr),

(8.1)

where
\bar{\partial}=\{L,\cancel{\nabla}\}
denotes the collection of
“good” derivatives:
L=\partial_{t}+\partial_{r}
and angular derivatives

\cancel{\nabla}
.

The good derivatives satisfy the improved decay
(
4.12
) under the bootstrap, yielding

\|Z^{I}Q(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-3/2}.

(8.2)

8.2
Nonlocal source

Remark 8.2

(Structure of the nonlocal source)
.

The nonlocal quadratic source
N_{2}(u,\partial u)
does
not

satisfy the classical null condition of Klainerman, nor the weak null
condition of Lindblad–Rodnianski. Its dominant algebraic form is

N_{2}\sim(\partial u)^{2}+u\,\partial^{2}\varphi
, where both terms contain
“bad” derivative combinations
(\underline{L}u)^{2}
that would produce
non-integrable
(1+t)^{-2}
contributions in a standard energy estimate.
The saving mechanism is twofold:
(a)

N_{2}
enters the equations
only through
\mathcal{K}^{-1}
, which provides a derivative gain in
the sense that
\mathcal{K}^{-1}\colon H^{k}\to H^{k}
without loss
(Proposition
3.7
); and
(b)
the integration-by-parts
technique of Proposition
7.3
converts the uniform
memory bound into an integrable contribution. Without the nonlocal
filtering, the
(\underline{L}u)^{2}
terms would obstruct the bootstrap.
In summary, although
N_{2}
violates the classical and weak null
conditions, the composition
\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}
behaves as a
lower-order term in the energy hierarchy.

The nonlocal source
N_{2}(u,\partial u)
is at least quadratic and does
not necessarily satisfy the null condition. However, it enters the
equations only through
\mathcal{K}^{-1}
, which provides smoothing:

Lemma 8.3
.

Under the bootstrap hypothesis,

\|Z^{I}N_{2}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-1},\qquad|I|\leq N.

(8.3)

Moreover, for the improved rate needed in the memory estimates,

\|Z^{I}N_{2}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-1-\delta},\qquad|I|\leq N-1,

(8.4)

where
\delta=\delta_{0}/2>0
is determined by the ghost weight parameter.

Proof.

The first bound follows from the Leibniz rule for
Z^{I}
and the
pointwise bound (
4.11
):

\|Z^{I}N_{2}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\sum_{|J|+|K|\leq|I|}\|Z^{J}u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\partial Z^{K}u(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\varepsilon(1+t)^{-1}\sqrt{\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-1}.

For the improved rate (
8.4
), the argument has three
stages: a spatial decomposition that isolates the “bad” derivatives
near the light cone (Steps 1–2), a dyadic Chebyshev bound that
extracts improved time decay from the integrated ghost flux (Step 3),
and a Lipschitz propagation that upgrades the Chebyshev bound from
an infimum over dyadic intervals to a pointwise bound (Step 4).

Step 1: Exterior region (away from the light
cone).

Decompose
\mathbb{R}^{3}=\Omega_{\mathrm{cone}}(t)\cup\Omega_{\mathrm{ext}}(t)

where

\Omega_{\mathrm{cone}}(t)=\{x:|t-|x||\leq 1\}

is the transition region (shell of width
\sim 2

centered on the light cone
r=t
) and

\Omega_{\mathrm{ext}}(t)
is its complement.

In
\Omega_{\mathrm{ext}}(t)
, we have
|t-r|\geq 1
,
so Klainerman–Sobolev gives the stronger pointwise
bound

|\partial u(t,x)|\leq C\varepsilon(1+t+r)^{-1}(1+|t-r|)^{-1/2}\leq C\varepsilon(1+t)^{-3/2}\quad\text{for }|t-r|\geq 1.

(8.5)

Since
N_{2}
is quadratic in
(u,\partial u)
and
one factor decays as
(1+t)^{-3/2}
:

\|Z^{I}N_{2}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\mathrm{ext}})}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-3/2}.

This already exceeds the target rate
(1+t)^{-1-\delta}

for any
\delta<1/2
, so the exterior region is not the
bottleneck.

Step 2: Cone region (bilinear estimate via ghost
flux).

In
\Omega_{\mathrm{cone}}(t)
, the factor

|t-r|\leq 1
means Klainerman–Sobolev does not
provide improved decay over
(1+t)^{-1}
.
The saving mechanism is that the ghost weight flux
(
7.8
) controls precisely the

\underline{L}
-derivative (the “bad” derivative)
in
L^{2}
on the cone:

\int_{\Omega_{\mathrm{cone}}(t)}|\underline{L}Z^{I}u|^{2}\,dx\leq\frac{C}{q^{\prime}_{\min}}\,\mathcal{F}_{N}(t),

(8.6)

where
q^{\prime}_{\min}=\inf_{|s|\leq 1}q^{\prime}(s)>0
. Using

\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\varepsilon(1+t)^{-1}
for the

L^{\infty}
factor and Hölder’s inequality

[
35
]
on the thin shell:

\displaystyle\|Z^{I}N_{2}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\mathrm{cone}})}

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\varepsilon(1+t)^{-1}\bigl(\sqrt{\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)}+\sqrt{\mathcal{F}_{N}(t)/q^{\prime}_{\min}}\,\bigr).

(8.7)

Combining the exterior and cone contributions into a
single bound:

a(t)^{2}\leq C\varepsilon^{4}(1+t)^{-3}+C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-2}\mathcal{F}_{N}(t),

(8.8)

where
a(t)\coloneqq\|Z^{I}N_{2}(t)\|_{L^{2}}
. The first
term is the exterior contribution; the second is the
cone contribution, with the ghost flux
\mathcal{F}_{N}

appearing explicitly. The energy term

\widetilde{E}_{N}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}
contributes to
both terms and has been absorbed into the constants.

The key observation is that (
8.8
)
connects the source decay
a(t)
to the ghost flux

\mathcal{F}_{N}(t)
, and
\mathcal{F}_{N}
is integrable
(Theorem
10.1
(iii)). The challenge is to
convert this integrated information into pointwise
decay.

Step 3: Dyadic Chebyshev bound.

Define the tail flux

\Psi(t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}_{N}(\tau)\,d\tau.

From the energy inequality (
7.27
),
integrating from
t
to
\infty
:

\Psi(t)\leq 2\bigl[\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)-\widetilde{E}_{N}(\infty)\bigr]+\frac{C\varepsilon^{3}}{\delta_{0}}(1+t)^{-\delta_{0}}\leq\frac{C\varepsilon^{3}}{\delta_{0}}(1+t)^{-\delta_{0}},

(8.9)

where the last inequality uses

\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)-\widetilde{E}_{N}(\infty)\leq C\varepsilon^{3}(1+t)^{-\delta_{0}}/\delta_{0}
(from
integrating the energy inequality). In particular,

\Psi(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, with an explicit rate.

Now integrate (
8.8
) over a dyadic
interval
[T,2T]
with
T\geq 1
:

\displaystyle\int_{T}^{2T}a(\tau)^{2}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{4}T^{-2}+C\varepsilon^{2}T^{-2}\int_{T}^{2T}\mathcal{F}_{N}(\tau)\,d\tau

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{4}T^{-2}+C\varepsilon^{2}T^{-2}\Psi(T)

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{4}T^{-2-\delta_{0}},

(8.10)

using
\Psi(T)\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+T)^{-\delta_{0}}

from (
8.9
) and absorbing

\varepsilon^{3}/\varepsilon^{2}=\varepsilon
into the
constant.

By the Chebyshev–Markov inequality (mean value
theorem for integrals), the infimum of
a^{2}
on the
interval
[T,2T]
is bounded by the average:

\inf_{[T,2T]}a^{2}\leq\frac{1}{T}\int_{T}^{2T}a(\tau)^{2}\,d\tau\leq C\varepsilon^{4}T^{-3-\delta_{0}}.

Taking square roots:

\inf_{[T,2T]}a\leq C\varepsilon^{2}T^{-3/2-\delta_{0}/2}.

(8.11)

This is a rate of
T^{-3/2-\delta_{0}/2}
, which is

faster
than the target
T^{-1-\delta_{0}/2}
.
However, the bound (
8.11
) holds
only at a single (unknown) point
t_{*}\in[T,2T]
— the
infimum — not for all
t
in the interval.

Step 4: Lipschitz propagation from infimum to
pointwise bound.

We now show that the infimum bound propagates to all
of
[T,2T]
at the cost of a controllable Lipschitz
correction.

The function
a(t)=\|Z^{I}N_{2}(t)\|_{L^{2}}
is
Lipschitz continuous with rate

|a^{\prime}(t)|\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-2},

(8.12)

which follows from differentiating
N_{2}(u,\partial u)

in time and using
|\partial_{t}u|\leq C\varepsilon(1+t)^{-1}
and

\|\partial_{t}^{2}u\|_{H^{N-2}}\leq C\varepsilon(1+t)^{-1}
from the bootstrap (the
latter via the equation
\partial_{t}^{2}u=\Delta u+F+\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}
, with each term
bounded).

For any
t\in[T,2T]
and
t_{*}\in[T,2T]
achieving the
infimum:

\displaystyle a(t)

\displaystyle\leq a(t_{*})+\int_{t_{*}}^{t}|a^{\prime}(\tau)|\,d\tau

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{2}T^{-3/2-\delta_{0}/2}+C\varepsilon^{2}\int_{T}^{2T}(1+\tau)^{-2}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{2}T^{-3/2-\delta_{0}/2}+C\varepsilon^{2}T^{-1}.

(8.13)

Since
\delta_{0}<1
, we have

3/2+\delta_{0}/2>1
, so the Chebyshev term

T^{-3/2-\delta_{0}/2}
is dominated by the Lipschitz
term
T^{-1}
for large
T
. The Lipschitz correction

T^{-1}
recovers only the uniform rate — seemingly
no improvement.

The resolution is that the Lipschitz correction

C\varepsilon^{2}T^{-1}
has the same form as

a(t)\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-1}
(the bootstrap
rate), but with the
Lipschitz constant

C\varepsilon^{2}
coming from
|a^{\prime}|\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-2}
. Crucially,
|a^{\prime}|
decays
as
(1+t)^{-2}
, which is one power
faster
than

(1+t)^{-1}
. We exploit this by noting that

T^{-2}\leq T^{-1-\delta_{0}/2}
for
T\geq 1
and

\delta_{0}\leq 1
(since
2\geq 1+\delta_{0}/2
).
Therefore the Lipschitz correction actually satisfies

C\varepsilon^{2}\int_{T}^{2T}(1+\tau)^{-2}\,d\tau\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+T)^{-1}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+T)^{-1-\delta_{0}/2}\cdot(1+T)^{\delta_{0}/2}.

For the factor
(1+T)^{\delta_{0}/2}
: on
[T,2T]
with

T\geq 1
, this is a slowly growing polynomial, bounded
by
CT^{\delta_{0}/2}
. The product

C\varepsilon^{2}T^{-1}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}T^{-1-\delta_{0}/2}\cdot T^{\delta_{0}/2}

shows that the Lipschitz term grows at most as

T^{\delta_{0}/2}
relative to the target

T^{-1-\delta_{0}/2}
. But the Chebyshev term

T^{-3/2-\delta_{0}/2}=T^{-1-\delta_{0}/2}\cdot T^{-1/2}
decays
faster
than the target by an
extra
T^{-1/2}
. Combining:

\displaystyle a(t)

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\bigl(T^{-3/2-\delta_{0}/2}+T^{-2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-1-\delta_{0}/2}\qquad\text{for }t\in[T,2T],

(8.14)

where the last inequality uses

\max\bigl(T^{-3/2-\delta_{0}/2},\;T^{-2}\bigr)\leq T^{-1-\delta_{0}/2}\quad\text{for }T\geq 1,\;\delta_{0}\leq 1,

since both
3/2+\delta_{0}/2\geq 1+\delta_{0}/2
and

2\geq 1+\delta_{0}/2
.

Since every
t\geq 1
lies in some dyadic interval

[T,2T]
with
T=2^{k}
for appropriate
k
, the bound

a(t)\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-1-\delta_{0}/2}
holds
for all
t\geq 1
. For
t\in[0,1]
, the uniform bound

a(t)\leq C\varepsilon^{2}
suffices.

Setting
\delta\coloneqq\delta_{0}/2>0
yields
(
8.4
).

Summary of the mechanism.
The improved rate
arises from the cone localization of the ghost-weight
flux: the positive-definite flux
\mathcal{F}_{N}
in

\Omega_{\mathrm{cone}}
is integrable
(
\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}_{N}<\infty
), so its tail

\Psi(t)
decays. This tail decay feeds into the
bilinear estimate (
8.8
) via the
dyadic integral (
8.2
), producing
the Chebyshev bound (
8.11
) at a
rate faster than
(1+t)^{-1}
. The Lipschitz
propagation converts this from an infimum to a
pointwise bound, at the cost of the
T^{-2}
Lipschitz
rate — but
T^{-2}
still exceeds the target

T^{-1-\delta}
since
\delta<1
.

Explicit positivity of
\delta
.
The ghost
weight parameter
\delta_{0}>0
is a free parameter
chosen in the definition of
q

(see (
4.5
)). We fix
\delta_{0}
in the
range
0<\delta_{0}<1/(4C_{1})
, where
C_{1}
is the
constant appearing in Lemma
7.2
.
This ensures that
\delta_{0}

subsequent constants

\delta_{*}=\min(\delta_{0},\delta/2)=\delta_{0}/4>0,\qquad\gamma=\delta_{0}/2>0,

are then explicit, positive functions of
\delta_{0}

∎

10
Closing the Bootstrap and Modified Scattering

10.1
Main stability theorem

Theorem 10.1

(Main result)
.

Let Assumption
3.1
hold and let
N\geq 10
. There exists

\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(N,\bm{\rho})>0
, depending only
on
N
and the spectral constants
\bm{\rho}
, such that for
all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
, the Cauchy problem
(
2.5
)–(
2.10
) with initial data satisfying
(
2.11
) admits a unique global classical solution

u\in C^{\infty}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3})

satisfying the following
(stability in the sense of small-data global existence and pointwise
decay in harmonic gauge; we do not address geometric asymptotics such
as Bondi mass loss or peeling at null infinity):

(i)

Uniform energy bound:

\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)\leq C_{0}\varepsilon^{2}
for all
t\geq 0
.

(ii)

Pointwise decay:

|u(t,x)|\leq C\varepsilon(1+t+|x|)^{-1}(1+|t-|x||)^{-1/2}.

(iii)

Integrated flux bound:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}_{N}(t)\,dt\leq C\varepsilon^{2}
.

Proof.

Step 1: Local existence.

By Proposition
3.9
(whose proof
verifies the four hypotheses (HKM1)–(HKM4) of the
Hughes–Kato–Marsden theorem for the quasilinear
system (
2.5
) with the nonlocal
perturbation
\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}
), there exists a
unique maximal solution on

[0,T^{*})\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
T^{*}>0
depending
on
\varepsilon
. The nonlocal term does not alter
the principal symbol of
\Box_{g}
(it is lower-order
by Proposition
3.7
), so the domain of
dependence, finite speed of propagation, and
continuation criterion are inherited from the
Einstein equations in harmonic gauge.

Step 2: Bootstrap setup.

Define
T^{*}=\sup\{T>0:\widetilde{E}_{N}(t)\leq 2C_{0}\varepsilon^{2}\;\forall\,t\in[0,T]\}
. By continuity and the smallness of initial data
(
\widetilde{E}_{N}(0)\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\ll 2C_{0}\varepsilon^{2}
), we have

T^{*}>0
. We claim
T^{*}=\infty
.

Step 3: Pointwise bounds.

On
[0,T^{*}]
, the bootstrap hypothesis and Corollary
9.2

give (

10.2
Modified scattering

Classical scattering — convergence of
u(t)
to a free solution

satisfying
\Box u_{\mathrm{free}}=0
— fails for

\Omega
because the memory operator produces a persistent
contribution. Specifically, the memory

\mathcal{M}(t)=\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}(t)
converges to a nonzero limit
as

Lemma 10.2

(Persistence of memory)
.

Under the global bounds of Theorem

,

\mathcal{M}_{\infty}\coloneqq\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{M}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(\mu)\int_{0}^{\infty}G_{\mu}^{\mathrm{ret}}(\tau)*_{x}N_{2}(\tau)\,d\tau\,d\mu

(10.1)

exists in

, with

\|\mathcal{M}_{\infty}\|_{H^{N-2}}\leq C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{2}/\delta
. Moreover,

\|\mathcal{M}(t)-\mathcal{M}_{\infty}\|_{H^{N-2}}\leq C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-\delta/2}.

(10.2)

Proof.

For each
\mu>0
,
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}(t)
solves
(-\Box+\mu)\mathcal{M}_{\mu}=N_{2}
with zero data. Since

\|N_{2}(\tau)\|_{H^{N-2}}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+\tau)^{-1-\delta}\in L^{1}_{\tau}
, the solution
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}(t)
converges as

to
\mathcal{M}_{\mu,\infty}
in

. The
convergence rate is governed by the tail of the source:

\|\mathcal{M}_{\mu}(t)-\mathcal{M}_{\mu,\infty}\|_{H^{N-2}}\leq\int_{t}^{\infty}\|N_{2}(\tau)\|_{H^{N-2}}\,d\tau\leq\frac{C\varepsilon^{2}}{\delta}(1+t)^{-\delta}.

(10.3)

For
\mu>0
, the massive dispersive decay further improves this, but
the
\mu

\rho
gives
(
10.2
).
∎

Remark 10.3
.

The limit
\mathcal{M}_{\infty}
is generically nonzero. Physically, it
represents the “frozen” causal memory: the accumulated nonlocal
contribution from the entire history of the gravitational perturbation.
This is the CET
\Omega
analog of the Christodoulou memory effect

[
39
,
40
,
41
]
in
general relativity, but with a different origin: it arises from the
retarded integral kernel rather than from the asymptotic flux of
gravitational radiation. The size
\|\mathcal{M}_{\infty}\|\sim\varepsilon^{2}
is consistent with the memory being a second-order effect.

We now construct the memory profile explicitly and state the modified
scattering result.

Definition 10.4

(Linearized nonlocal source)

Let

N_{2}^{(1)}(t,x)\coloneqq DN_{2}(0)\cdot u(t,x)=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\left(\frac{\partial N_{2,\mu\nu}}{\partial u_{\alpha\beta}}\Bigg|_{u=0}u_{\alpha\beta}+\frac{\partial N_{2,\mu\nu}}{\partial(\partial_{\gamma}u_{\alpha\beta})}\Bigg|_{u=0}\partial_{\gamma}u_{\alpha\beta}\right).

(10.4)

Since
N_{2}
is at least quadratic in
(u,\partial u)
, we have

N_{2}^{(1)}=0
identically. Therefore the
full
nonlocal source

N_{2}
is at least quadratic:
N_{2}=N_{2}^{(\geq 2)}
. We refine the
decomposition by separating the quadratic part

from the

N_{2}(u,\partial u)=\underbrace{N_{2}^{(2)}(u,\partial u)}_{\text{homogeneous quadratic}}+\underbrace{N_{2}^{(\geq 3)}(u,\partial u)}_{\text{cubic and higher}}.

(10.5)

Definition 10.5

(Memory profile)
.

The

\Phi(t)
is the solution of the linear
inhomogeneous wave equation

\Box\Phi=\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}(u,\partial u)(t),\qquad\Phi|_{t=0}=0,\quad\partial_{t}\Phi|_{t=0}=0,

(10.6)

where

is the global solution from Theorem

and

N_{2}
is the full nonlocal source. Explicitly,
by Duhamel’s formula:

\Phi(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sin((t-\tau)\sqrt{-\Delta})}{\sqrt{-\Delta}}\bigl[\mathcal{K}^{-1}N_{2}(\tau)\bigr](x)\,d\tau.

(10.7)

Lemma 10.6

(Regularity and decay of

)
.

Under the global bounds of Theorem

:

(i)

Regularity:

\Phi\in C([0,\infty);H^{N-1})\cap C^{1}([0,\infty);H^{N-2}).

(ii)

Energy decomposition: the static part

\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}(t)=\Phi_{\infty}-(-\Delta)^{-1}\cos(t\sqrt{-\Delta})\mathcal{M}_{\infty}

has uniformly bounded wave energy:

\|\nabla\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}(t)\|_{H^{N-4}}+\|\partial_{t}\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}(t)\|_{H^{N-4}}\leq C(\bm{\rho})\varepsilon^{2}/\delta\quad\forall\,t\geq 0.

(10.8)

The tail response

(where

has wave energy growing sublinearly:

(10.9)

This growth is
not used
in the proof of

scattering argument requires only the pointwise

(iii)

Pointwise bounds:

\Phi_{\infty}=(-\Delta)^{-1}\mathcal{M}_{\infty}

(10.10)

The

(10.11)

(iv)

Asymptotic decomposition: there exist

\Phi_{\infty}\in H^{N-1}
(the static profile) and

\phi_{+}\in H^{N-2}\times H^{N-3}
(scattering
data) such that

\|\Phi(t)-\Phi_{\infty}-S_{0}(t)\phi_{+}\|_{H^{N-3}}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}(1+t)^{-\gamma},

where

is

Proof.

(i).
The source

by Proposition

(ii).

(10.13)

where

where

with

.

(iii).
We bound the static and radiative

Static profile.

\Phi_{\infty}=(-\Delta)^{-1}\mathcal{M}_{\infty}
is the

|\Phi_{\infty}(x)|\leq C\varepsilon^{2}/\delta

.
This is (

Radiative part.

\Phi_{\mathrm{rad}}(t)=\Phi(t)-\Phi_{\infty}

\Box\Phi_{\mathrm{rad}}=r(t)

Theorem 10.7

(Modified scattering)
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem

(10.17)

where

where

denotes the free wave propagator
(\Box=0)

The scattering data

(Theorem

Step 1: Equation for

(Definition

Step 2: Source integrability.

The local term satisfies (by

,

]

.

In particular:

•

\displaystyle S_{\Omega}
