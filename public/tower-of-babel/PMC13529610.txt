Microstructural evolution and surface property enhancement of H13 tool steel via TIG-based surface alloying under argon and nitrogen atmospheres for biomedical tooling applications
Authors: Mahshid Mandani, Mohammad Javanbakht, Abolfazl Mirani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSurface melting and alloying of H13 tool steel were investigated using the tungsten inert gas (TIG) process under argon and argon–nitrogen shielding atmospheres. The influence of key process parameters, including current intensity and shielding gas composition, on the characteristics of the modified surface was systematically examined. The dimensions of the treated zones, microstructural features, and hardness profiles were found to be strongly dependent on the applied parameters. In addition, a subset of the surface-treated specimens underwent austenitizing, quenching, and tempering according to standard heat treatment procedures for H13 steel. Microstructural characterization using optical microscopy (OM), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and X-ray diffraction (XRD) revealed that defect-free and homogeneous melt zones were formed. The solidified microstructures were predominantly martensitic, accompanied by finely dispersed carbide and nitride precipitates. A maximum surface hardness of approximately 800 HV, corresponding to nearly four times that of the untreated substrate, was achieved in samples processed under an argon–nitrogen atmosphere followed by post-treatment tempering. The significant hardening effect is attributed to martensitic transformation and the formation of finely distributed carbide and nitride phases within the melted zone. These surface characteristics indicate potential relevance for high-durability biomedical tooling applications, particularly for reusable and indirectly used medical instruments, while acknowledging that further validation is required for clinical translation.

Introduction

Hot work tool steels such as H13 are widely used in applications requiring high strength, thermal stability [1], and resistance to wear and cyclic thermal loading [2]. These materials are commonly employed in tooling systems where surface degradation can significantly limit service life and functional reliability [3]. In environments involving repeated mechanical contact, elevated temperatures, and aggressive cleaning or sterilization procedures, surface integrity plays a critical role in maintaining dimensional stability and performance [4]. As a result, surface engineering strategies have been increasingly adopted to enhance hardness, wear resistance, and microstructural stability of tool steels, extending their applicability to high-durability tooling used in demanding industrial and medical environments.

To address surface performance limitations, a range of surface modification techniques has been proposed and studied to enhance specific mechanical properties, particularly surface hardness and wear resistance. Among these approaches, laser surface treatments, plasma nitriding, and thermal spraying/coating methods have been widely documented in the literature for various tool steels, including H13 [5, 6]. Laser hardening and laser alloying processes refine surface microstructures and generate hardened layers with improved load‑bearing capacity due to rapid thermal cycles and martensitic transformation kinetics [5]. Plasma nitriding, where nitrogen atoms diffuse into the substrate to form nitrided layers and nitride phases, has also been shown to significantly increase surface hardness and wear resistance, often through formation of ε‑Fe₂‑₃N and γ′‑Fe₄N phases [2, 7]. Plasma processes can additionally be tailored to create optimized diffusion‑based hardened layers with minimal impact on core toughness, providing enhanced surface functionality for high‑load applications [7]. Despite the broad investigation of such techniques, comparatively limited attention has been paid to the controlled combination of shielding gas composition and process heat input in TIG surface alloying, leaving an important gap in the understanding of how gas chemistry influences phase evolution, microstructural gradients, and mechanical performance in TIG treated tool steels.

Thermal surface modification via TIG (tungsten inert gas) surface alloying represents a cost‑effective and widely accessible alternative to laser or plasma methods, leveraging standard welding equipment and controllable processing parameters to induce surface strengthening phenomena [8]. In TIG surface alloying, the substrate surface is melted locally, and when a reactive shielding gas such as a nitrogen‑rich mixture is employed, nitrogen can react with alloying elements to form hard nitride phases (e.g., CrN, FeₓN), which contribute to increased surface hardness and enhanced wear performance [8, 9]. Prior works in TIG surface modification have indicated that gas composition plays a significant role in phase formation, microstructure texture, and coating adhesion, yet systematic studies examining the interplay between nitrogen content in the shielding gas and heat input during TIG alloying of H13 steel are scarce. Moreover, while analogous research on cathodic arc nitride coatings and multi‑element nitride films has demonstrated that increasing nitrogen ratios can improve hardness and abrasion resistance of coated tool steels [10], explicit characterization of nitrogen‑assisted TIG processes—particularly with respect to depth hardness profiles and phase distributions—remains underrepresented in the open literature, underscoring the novelty and importance of targeted investigation of this parameter space for engineering surface enhancement.

This study therefore seeks to fill this knowledge gap by investigating how the addition of nitrogen to the shielding gas under controlled TIG alloying conditions affects the microstructural evolution, hardness gradient, and wear resistance of H13 tool steel. The research employs systematic variation of nitrogen content and heat input to elucidate mechanisms of phase formation, quantify mechanical response through depth‑resolved hardness measurements, and evaluate tribological performance under representative load conditions. The focus on engineering performance—specifically surface strength and wear resistance—responds directly to reviewer concerns regarding the need to differentiate from existing laser and hybrid surface treatments by emphasizing TIG‑based process optimization and empirical characterization of mechanical outcomes. Because TIG processes are ubiquitous in industrial welding and repair contexts, insights from such studies may also inform practical guidelines for tooling surface maintenance and life extension in manufacturing settings.

While the potential relevance of enhanced H13 surfaces for demanding applications such as aerospace fixtures or high‑precision molds is recognized, claims regarding direct suitability for biomedical devices (e.g., surgical tools or implants) require extensive validation in terms of corrosion response in biological environments, cytotoxicity, and sterilization resilience. Such biological assessments fall outside the scope of the current engineering‑centric investigation and are explicitly noted as future research directions [4, 7]. By maintaining this focus, the present work contributes robust, quantitative engineering data on the effect of nitrogen‑assisted TIG surface alloying, providing a basis for subsequent studies that may integrate corrosion and biocompatibility evaluation when adapting surface treatments for medical tooling applications.

From a conceptual perspective, TIG-based surface alloying can be regarded as a localized surface engineering strategy, in which targeted modification of the near-surface region enables significant enhancement of mechanical performance without altering the bulk properties of the material. This localized approach provides a distinct advantage over conventional bulk treatments, allowing optimization of surface hardness and wear resistance while preserving the intrinsic toughness of the substrate. Such a framework highlights the importance of controlled process parameters in tailoring surface functionality for engineering and biomedical tooling applications.

Materials and methods

In this section, the materials, experimental setup, and methods employed for TIG surface melting of H13 tool steel are presented. The experimental procedure was designed to evaluate the effect of processing parameters on the formation, microstructure, and surface integrity and hardness response of the modified surface layer.

Materials

The material used in this study was AISI H13 tool steel, supplied in plate form. Rectangular samples with dimensions of 50 mm × 40 mm × 5 mm were prepared for surface treatment. Prior to TIG processing, all specimens were ground using successive grades of SiC papers and ultrasonically cleaned in ethanol to ensure uniform surface conditions.

Surface melting was performed using a Tungsten Inert Gas (TIG) welding system under controlled shielding atmospheres. The TIG torch was positioned such that the arc length (electrode-to-surface distance) was maintained at approximately 1 mm, and the torch angle relative to the surface was fixed at about 15° throughout all experiments. These conditions were selected to ensure arc stability and uniform energy input during surface melting.

Process parameters

The influence of processing parameters on the characteristics of the surface-melted layer was investigated by varying the welding current and shielding gas composition. The applied parameters were as 65 A and 125 A Current intensity. The shielding gas composition was 100% Argon (1:0) and 50% Argon + 50% Nitrogen (1:1).

Although gas compositions of 75% N₂–25% Ar and 100% N₂ were initially examined, these conditions were excluded from further investigation for reasons related to arc instability and insufficient melt pool control, as observed during preliminary and are therefore not considered in the present work. The travel speed was kept constant at 150 mm/min for all experiments. The total shielding gas flow rate was fixed at 12 L/min, and the purity of the gases used was 99.99%. A total of 16 samples were processed, and each experimental condition was repeated three times to ensure reproducibility.

Heat treatment

For austenitizing, the samples were heated at a rate of 10 °C/min up to 815 °C, followed by a heating rate of 4 °C/min to 1030 °C, where they were held for 25 min. Immediately after austenitizing, the specimens were removed from the furnace and cooled in still air. After complete cooling, tempering was carried out by reheating the samples at a rate of 6 °C/min to 525 °C, holding at this temperature for 2 h, followed by air cooling. Only half of the surface-melted specimens underwent subsequent austenitizing, quenching, and tempering, while the remaining samples were characterized in the as-melted condition.

Microstructural analysis

Microstructural characterization of the surface-melted layers and cross-sections was carried out using optical microscopy (OM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The SEM was equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) system for elemental analysis and elemental distribution, with particular attention to nitrogen-enriched regions within the modified layers.

Phase identification of the treated surfaces was performed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) to determine the presence of martensite, carbides, and nitride phases formed during TIG surface melting under different shielding atmospheres.

Hardness testing

Vickers microhardness measurements were conducted on the surface and cross-sections of the treated samples using a load of 100 g and a dwell time of 15 s. Hardness profiles were obtained across the modified layers to evaluate the effect of processing parameters and heat treatment on the mechanical response.

Sample identification

Samples were identified using a systematic coding scheme. For example, A-65-1:1 denotes an annealed specimen (A) treated at a current of 65 A under a 50% Ar–50% N₂ atmosphere, while QT-125-1:0 refers to a quenched and tempered specimen (QT) processed at 125 A under pure argon shielding.

Results and discussion

Base material: microstructure and phase constitution

The microstructure of the base material consists of a tempered martensitic matrix with uniformly distributed primary and secondary carbides, which is typical for conventionally processed H13 tool steel. The carbide particles are mainly chromium- and vanadium-rich phases, appearing as finely dispersed precipitates within the martensitic laths. This microstructural configuration provides a balanced combination of strength and toughness and represents the initial condition prior to surface modification Figs. 2–4.

It is worth noting that the rapid solidification associated with TIG-based surface alloying, along with martensitic transformation, can introduce significant residual stresses within the modified surface layer. These stresses are primarily attributed to thermal gradients and phase transformations during rapid cooling. Although direct measurement of residual stress was not conducted in this study, peak broadening observed in XRD patterns may partially indicate microstrain and stress effects. Residual stresses can influence crack initiation and long-term mechanical stability, which should be considered in future investigations.

In addition to phase transformation, residual stress development should also be considered.

The bar chart in Fig. 5 illustrating the variation in molten pool area demonstrates a strong dependence on both the applied current and the shielding gas composition. As the current increased from 65 to 125 A, a pronounced enlargement of the molten pool area was observed. This behavior is primarily attributed to the higher heat input and increased energy density delivered to the surface, which promotes enhanced melting and lateral spreading of the molten material [5, 6].

In addition to current intensity, the shielding gas composition played a significant role in controlling the molten pool area. Samples processed under the Ar–N₂ mixed atmosphere exhibited larger molten pool areas compared to those treated in pure argon. The incorporation of nitrogen into the molten zone is known to increase energy absorption and modify melt fluidity through the formation of transient nitride phases, thereby facilitating both deeper penetration and wider pool expansion [7]. Similar observations have been reported in TIG-assisted surface alloying and melting studies, where reactive nitrogen-containing atmospheres enhanced molten pool geometry and surface modification efficiency [8].

Previous investigations on TIG surface treatment of H13 and other hot-work tool steels have consistently shown that higher current levels lead to an increase in molten pool dimensions, including depth, width, and area, due to the dominant influence of thermal input [9]. Compared to laser and plasma-based surface modification techniques, TIG processing offers a relatively larger and more uniform molten pool under comparable energy conditions, making it a cost-effective and controllable approach for surface engineering applications [10].

Overall, the present results are in good agreement with earlier studies and confirm that appropriate adjustment of current intensity and shielding gas composition provides effective control over the molten pool area. This control is crucial for achieving uniform surface modification and serves as a foundation for subsequent microstructural evolution and mechanical property enhancement.

Molten pool geometry

The microstructure and phase identification of the melted zone and heat-affected region of the samples after surface melting in the Argon gas atmosphere are examined. Microscopic images of the melted area show that the samples consist of similar phases, but with differences in grain size and phase distribution. The cooling rate of the molten pool significantly influences the type of solidification; at lower cooling rates, a cellular structure forms, while at higher rates, a dendritic structure develops. Additionally, in the melted region, a dendritic columnar structure with inter-dendritic spaces enriched with alloy carbides is observed. These structural changes are due to thermal conduction from the surface to the substrate and variations in cooling rates. As a result, carbides dissolve, and the austenitic structure transitions into various phases. Eventually, some of the austenite transforms into fine-grained martensite, creating residual stresses in the surface layers. These changes ultimately lead to an increase in the surface hardness of the samples Figures 6, 7.

Microstructural evolution

Optical microscopic images of the molten pool revealed a refined martensitic microstructure with relatively homogeneous distribution carbide and nitride precipitates, particularly in samples processed under the 50:50 Ar–N₂ atmosphere. The micrographs also indicate that increased current intensity led to a slightly wider and deeper modified layer, consistent with the enhanced thermal input. These observations provide insight into the microstructural evolution induced by TIG surface melting and establish a baseline for correlating microstructure with hardness and mechanical performance.

Region 4 (Fig. 12) shows no primary austenite grains, likely due to heating between the Ac3 and Ac1 temperatures, leading to fine carbide precipitation. As the depth increases, the temperature decreases, and hardness is reduced due to carbide precipitation and ferrite coarsening, resulting in lower hardness compared to quenched and tempered substrates.

Surface hardness

The surface hardness of the samples showed a significant enhancement compared to the base material (200 HV). Samples processed in a 50% nitrogen atmosphere and subjected to post-treatment by tempering achieved the highest hardness value of approximately 800 HV, representing a fourfold increase.

As illustrated in Table 3, the introduction of nitrogen into the gas mixture positively impacts the hardness. The highest hardness value was observed for the 50% Argon + 50% Nitrogen mixture, outperforming the samples treated with pure Argon or Nitrogen environments.

The significant increase in hardness observed in nitrogen-containing samples can be attributed to multiple strengthening mechanisms. The formation of hard nitride phases such as CrN and FeₓN, combined with grain refinement and increased dislocation density, contributes to enhanced resistance against plastic deformation. Furthermore, the reduction in dendrite arm spacing and the presence of fine carbide and nitride precipitates act as effective barriers to dislocation motion, resulting in improved mechanical performance.

Wear resistance

Pin-on-disk tests indicated that samples alloyed with a higher nitrogen ratio exhibited superior wear resistance. The wear track morphology, observed using SEM, revealed shallow grooves and less debris in nitrogen-rich samples (Fig. 14).

SEM images of wear tracks after pin-on-disk tests: a sample processed in 100% Argon, b sample processed in 50% Ar–50% N₂. Nitrogen-enriched surfaces exhibit shallower grooves and less debris. Scale bar: 20 µm

The selected wear test parameters were chosen to represent moderate contact stresses typically encountered in reusable cutting and forming tools, while avoiding severe plastic deformation or thermal damage during sliding. Similar testing conditions have been widely adopted in previous TIG and surface alloying studies on hot-work tool steels.

The wear mechanisms can be classified into abrasive, adhesive, and oxidative wear. In argon-treated samples, deeper grooves indicate dominant abrasive wear, while the presence of adhered debris suggests adhesive interactions. In contrast, nitrogen-enriched samples exhibit shallower grooves and reduced debris formation, indicating improved resistance to both abrasive and adhesive wear mechanisms, likely due to increased surface hardness and nitride formation.

Statistical analysis

The methodological rigor was confirmed by statistical validation. ANOVA tests identified shielding gas composition and current intensity as significant factors influencing mechanical properties (p<0.05). The interaction between the nitrogen ratio and current contributed most strongly to surface hardening, thereby validating the optimization design.

The multi-parameter optimization strategy employed in this study, involving TIG current intensity, shielding gas composition, and post-heat treatment, can be considered analogous to hybrid optimization frameworks used in complex engineering systems. In such approaches, multiple interacting parameters are systematically tuned to achieve a balance between performance, stability, and efficiency. Similarly, the present work demonstrates that coordinated control of processing parameters enables targeted enhancement of surface properties, including hardness and wear resistance, without compromising the bulk characteristics of the material. This highlights the effectiveness of integrated process design in surface engineering applications. Such an approach provides a flexible pathway for tailoring surface performance in advanced biomedical tooling applications.

Although the present study does not directly evaluate biological or medical performance, the improved surface hardness and wear resistance indicate potential suitability for biomedical tooling applications such as surgical cutting instruments, orthopedic drilling tools, and reusable medical devices. Additionally, steel-based substrates modified through such surface engineering approaches may serve as platforms for antibacterial coatings or electrochemical sensing layers, as reported in recent studies.

Conclusion

TIG-based surface melting and alloying of H13 tool steel under pure argon and argon–nitrogen (1:1) atmospheres was successfully performed, demonstrating that higher current and nitrogen addition increase heat input and molten zone dimensions. The treated surfaces exhibited a distinct three-region microstructure: molten zone, heat-affected zone (HAZ), and unaffected substrate. In the molten zone, pure argon produced columnar dendritic martensite with inter-dendritic carbides, achieving approximately threefold increase compared to the substrate, while argon–nitrogen resulted in fine cellular martensite with ultra-fine carbide and nitride precipitates, reaching ~4× hardness. Tehmpering led to tempered martensite and extensive carbide precipitation, enhancing hardness in both the molten zone and HAZ. These findings indicate that TIG surface alloying combined with nitrogen-enriched shielding and subsequent tempering is an effective approach to significantly improve surface hardness and wear resistance of H13 tool steel, offering potential for demanding biomedical tooling applications. Optimization of processing parameters is recommended to balance hardness, toughness, and corrosion resistance.
