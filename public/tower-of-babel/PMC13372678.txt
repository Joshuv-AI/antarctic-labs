Older people’s experiences of and reactions to ageism
Authors: Nancy Morrow-Howell, Aaron C Li, Natalie Galucia, Brian D Carpenter, Abby J Schwartz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesThere is substantial literature documenting the pervasiveness and harmfulness of ageism to older adults and society. Anti-ageism interventions are being developed and evaluated. However, most interventions target younger people and service providers, not older adults themselves. In this study, we aim to build knowledge about how older people understand, experience, and react to ageism with the goal of advancing anti-ageism interventions.Research Design and MethodsWe conducted 9 focus groups with 58 people who were 60 years of age or older. Participants were 64% female, 64% Caucasian, and 33% African American. The modal age group was 75 to 79 years. A thematic content analysis was used to identify themes and representative quotes.Results5 themes emerged. Not all older people are familiar with the concept of ageism, and some report they have not experienced it. Once primed, most participants could identify experiences of interpersonal ageism in employment, health care, media, families, and community settings, like stores and restaurants. Internalized ageism was less familiar to participants. Reaction to these experiences depended on the circumstances, and reactions ranged from doing nothing to making complaints. Participants generated suggestions for potential interventions.Discussion and ImplicationsFocus group participants recognized experiences of interpersonal ageism and had ideas about how to confront it. They had a harder time understanding and giving examples of internalized ageism. Increasing awareness and building skills to respond to interpersonal and internalized ageism emerged as a priority in intervention development.Keywords: Age discrimination, Anti-ageism interventions, Age inclusivity

Interventions to empower older adults themselves to address ageism in daily life are needed. Through focus groups with older people, we learned that older people experienced interpersonal ageism in employment, health care, the family, and community settings; and how they reacted to an ageist incident depended on the circumstances. The concept of internalized ageism was less familiar to focus group participants. We learned that increasing awareness and developing skills are necessary first steps for older adults to confront interpersonal and internalized ageism.

Ageism is stereotyping, prejudice, and discrimination based on age (WHO, 2021), and there is mounting evidence that ageism hurts older people and society (Levy et al., 2020). Interpersonal ageism—directed toward older people by others—compromises opportunities and well-being in families, communities, workplaces, higher education, and health care (Batinovic et al., 2023; Chang et al., 2020; Gordon & Gonzales, 2025; Morrow-Howell & Gonzales, 2024). Internalized ageism—negative attitudes about aging held by older adults themselves—has negative effects on behavior and health (Hu et al., 2021; Levy & Banaji, 2002). A substantial body of research now exists that documents the harmful effects of ageism on individuals and organizations.

In response, efforts to reduce ageism are growing. However, the focus of the anti-ageism intervention literature is not on older people but on younger people, service providers, and the public more generally (Burnes et al., 2019). Although efforts with those groups are certainly important, we propose that more focus should be placed on older adults themselves as we seek to reduce the effects of interpersonal and internalized ageism. To that end, we sought to increase knowledge about older people’s experience of ageism, with the goal of advancing intervention development. Using a focus group methodology, we sought to learn how older people understand interpersonal and internalized ageism, how they experience it in their daily lives, and how they respond to the ageism they experience.

Related literature

Prevalence and effects of ageism

People of any age can experience stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination based on chronological age. In a national survey (Allen et al., 2020), 82% of older adults reported regularly experiencing at least one form of ageism, including exposure to ageist messages, interpersonal ageism in social interactions, and internalized ageism through their own beliefs about aging. In the same study, certain demographic factors were related to more frequent exposure to ageism, including older age, female gender, lower income, retired status, and rural residency.

Ageism has been studied in a variety of settings and sectors—the workplace, health care, housing, media and entertainment, and institutions of higher education. For example, research indicates that older job applicants have 11%–50% lower odds of being considered than younger applicants, even when qualifications were similar (Batinovic et al., 2023). Older people are also exposed to ageism in the healthcare system, where age discrimination constitutes a measurable health hazard (Ayalon & Cohn-Schwartz, 2022). For instance, older age is a predictor of higher rates of medical decisions to withhold ventilator support, surgery, and dialysis, even after controlling for patients’ prognoses and care preferences (Chang et al., 2020). Within families, ageist stereotypes and behaviors undermine older family members’ self-perceptions by fostering doubts about their abilities and strengths, resulting in lower self-esteem (Gordon, 2020).

For the older adults who experience it, ageism has been associated with increased mortality, poorer physical health, and worse health behaviors. Ageism has also been shown to delay recovery from disability, disrupt mental health, increase social isolation and loneliness, and reduce overall quality of life (WHO, 2021). Older adults who experienced three or more forms of everyday ageism in their daily lives were less likely to rate their overall health and mental health as excellent or very good than those who experienced fewer forms of ageism (Allen et al., 2020). Internalized ageism, sometimes called self-directed ageism, has been associated with reduced cognitive function, lower self-worth, poorer self-rated health, and increased depression (Levy, 2023; Steward, 2022). Levy et al. (2020) estimated that the yearly cost of ageism to the health care system in the United States is roughly $63 billion.

Ageism in the workplace is harmful to older workers and organizations themselves. Age discrimination creates a work environment of tension and hostility; it reduces morale and productivity and leads to higher employee turnover (Belcak, 2021). Age discrimination in the workplace is associated with worse mental health, higher general stress, job dissatisfaction, turnover intentions, and desire to retire (Gonzales et al., 2021). In educational institutions, age bias reduces opportunities for older students and negatively affects their experiences on campus (Morrow-Howell, 2024). Ageism hinders research and training on topics of aging and ageism and may be one reason for the current shortage in clinicians across professions seeking specialized training to work with older adults (Bodner et al., 2018; Jeyasingam et al., 2023; Wyman et al., 2018). Most college students graduate from college with little knowledge about aging or experiences with older adults, weakening their skills in working in age-diverse settings (Gerontological Society of America, 2019). In sum, ageism is bad for our health, bad for business, and bad for achieving an equitable society (Gendron, 2022).

Interventions to reduce ageism

The World Health Organization’s Global Report on Ageism (2021) identified three strategies to reduce ageism, including (a) strengthening and enforcing policies and laws that address age discrimination, (b) educational interventions to dispel misconceptions about different age groups, and (c) intergenerational interventions encouraging interaction between people of different generations. A systematic review to evaluate the effectiveness of three types of interventions designed to reduce ageism among youths and adults—education, intergenerational contact, and combined education and intergenerational contact—demonstrated that interventions can change attitudes, knowledge, and comfort with older adults (Burnes et al., 2019). The analysis also suggested that interventions have a stronger effect on adolescent and young adult age groups than on other age groups. Another review confirmed these general findings (Apriceno & Levy, 2023): both education and positive intergenerational contact were effective at decreasing ageist attitudes and increasing aging knowledge. The most frequently used conceptual model in interventions aimed at interpersonal ageism is Allport’s intergroup contact theory, which proposed that exposure between people of different social groups can reduce prejudice (Allport, 1954; Pettigrew, 1998).

A recent review concluded that the main targets of the current anti-ageism interventions are children, adolescents, young adults, and professionals working with older people (Morrow-Howell et al., 2026). Of course, changing attitudes and beliefs early in the life course is necessary to reduce ageism that is rampant in society, and changing the biases of service providers is critical. However, we need more attention directed toward older adults as targets of the interventions, to address both interpersonal and internalized ageism.

Interventions focusing on older adults to reduce ageism are being developed. For example, Steward et al. (2025) developed and tested a peer-led support group where older people came together for 10 weeks for peer support, education about ageism and health, and storytelling. Pilot results suggested that this program was feasible and acceptable, with an indication that ageism and depression were decreased. Another pilot intervention aimed at older adults focused on key mechanisms associated with decreasing internalized ageism, including psychological flexibility, mindfulness, and perceived control (Murphy et al., 2025). Evaluations suggested improved self-perceptions of aging. Knight et al. (2022) reviewed interventions that confronted negative age stereotypes held by older adults in health promotion programs and found some evidence that physical health outcomes were improved when such stereotypes were challenged.

Scholars have offered conceptual models related to disrupting the negative effects people experience when believing age stereotypes and applying them to their own selves. To address internalized ageism, Steward (2022) proposed a range of interventions aimed at “downstream factors” including physical activity and stress management, “midstream factors” including education and narrative reframing, and “upstream factors” including anti-ageism policy at the structural level. Levy (2023) offered the ABC Method (Awareness, Blame, Challenge) to shift individuals from an age-declining mindset to an age-thriving mindset: increasing awareness of our age beliefs, placing blame on societal sources and not ourselves, and challenging age beliefs. She outlines suggested activities to achieve these goals. These two models lay important foundations for intervention development, and research to test their individual components or their additive effects is needed (Gendron, 2022).

Aims of the current study

Beyond the research on ageism and its negative effects, there is a need to know more about how older people themselves understand what it is, hear their examples of experiencing it, and learn how they respond. Interventions to support older people in addressing ageism may be more effective if they build on this knowledge.

In this study, we aimed to produce applied knowledge by talking with older adults directly and in depth to answer these questions:

How do older persons understand interpersonal and internalized ageism?

How do older persons understand interpersonal and internalized ageism?

How do older persons experience interpersonal and internalized ageism in their lives?

How do older persons experience interpersonal and internalized ageism in their lives?

How do older persons respond to the ageism they experience?

How do older persons respond to the ageism they experience?

This project extended previous work on interventions by (a) focusing directly on older people as agents of change, (b) targeting both interpersonal and internalized ageism, and (c) increasing critical knowledge for intervention development by elucidating how older people think, talk, and react to ageism and what ideas they have to resist it. The lessons gained from this study will make unique contributions to the current knowledge base as well as contribute to the development of new interventions.

Method

Research design

We conducted nine focus groups in a midwestern urban region from July 2024 to August 2025, including 58 older adults. We chose this approach because focus groups are valuable for exploratory research (Betts et al., 1996) and for collecting initial data for intervention design (Krueger & Casey, 2015). The collaborative setting of focus groups can empower participants (Ivanoff & Hultberg, 2006) and foster interactions within the group, generating richer ideas not easily captured by other approaches (Morgan, 1997). Focus groups also enabled us to hear directly how people talk about a topic and what is most salient for them, which is important to developing interventions. The writing of this article followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines for qualitative studies (Tong et al., 2007). Approval for this project was obtained from Washington University in St. Louis Institutional Review Board (IRB ID #202405147).

Constituting focus groups

We worked with directors of four partnering agencies in the aging service network in the region for recruitment. These community partners used flyers and announcements to reach out to individuals 60 years and over in their service areas, including senior centers, community centers, senior residences, and health and wellness programs. Agency staff members gathered names and contact information from potential participants who agreed to have their information shared with the research team. We then called potential participants to explain the study, gain oral consent, and collect demographic information. This screening interview also served the purpose of ascertaining that the individual had the cognitive capacity to understand study procedures and participate in the group.

We employed purposive and convenience sampling to achieve diversity across participants. Each focus group included six to eight older adults from the community settings where we recruited. We held the sessions in those settings to maximize the comfort of participants. When we held more than one group in a particular organization, we used demographic data to balance the groups as much as possible in terms of age, gender, and ethnicity. After conducting eight groups, we determined that younger older adults (aged 60–65) were underrepresented. To address this gap, we advertised through a study participant registry to recruit within this age range. Representation across different age ranges and ethnic groups was prioritized. All participants who were recruited consented to the study, and there were no dropouts. Demographics of the sample are presented in Table 1. Participants’ ages ranged from 60 to 97 years, with the modal age category being 75–79 years. Sixty-four percent were female, and 64% were Caucasian, and 33% African American.

Developing the interview guide

We developed a semi-structured interview protocol and guide to be used across all groups to ensure uniform inclusion and sequencing of topics (see Supplementary Material). The questions queried older adults’ understanding of interpersonal and internalized ageism, their experiences of ageism, their responses to ageism, and their thoughts about potential solutions to address ageism. The research team drafted an initial set of questions that were subsequently refined with input from two consultants outside of the research team as well as four directors from partner organizations. The draft interview guide was pilot tested with two older adults, who provided feedback on the length, clarity, and phrasing of the interview questions.

Data collection

Research team members developed standardized guidelines for conducting the focus groups. Team members held advanced degrees in psychology or social work and were skilled in conducting group discussions, and they served as focus group facilitators. At least one research team member, usually two, served as note takers to complement the audio recordings.

The focus group process followed the guidelines outlined by Krueger and Casey (2014). At the beginning of each 90-min session, the facilitator offered a brief introduction to the study, and key points from the informed consent were reviewed, including the use of audio recording for transcription purposes to ensure accuracy. Next, the facilitator introduced interview questions one by one, employing clarifying follow-ups as needed and ensuring participation by all attendees to the extent possible.

Data analysis

With the notes and the audio recordings from each group, interviews were transcribed verbatim and uploaded to NVivo (QSR International) for data management and analysis. A qualitative thematic content analysis was employed to analyze the content derived from focus groups (Braun & Clarke, 2006, 2013). To minimize bias in data analysis, two research team members (N.M.-H. and A.C.L.) independently read through transcripts to develop initial ideas about codes. These codes were then iteratively reviewed and revised several times with the research team. To establish coding reliability, both researchers separately coded the same transcript, compared results, and refined coding guidelines. A second transcript was used for the same purpose. A third transcript was used to establish inter-rater reliability (IRR = .83), indicating strong agreement. One coder (A.C.L.) then completed coding the remainder of the transcripts and brought questions to the full team as needed. The research team then organized the codes into higher level themes.

The findings were organized into a user-friendly report, which was presented back to two participant groups for member-checking to ensure credibility (Chilisa, 2019). A total of 20 of the original focus group members participated in this process, during which we guided them through the findings, question by question, from the interview guide. Participants used a form we provided to indicate if each finding we presented in the report aligned with what they shared and what they heard in the focus group discussions. All the participants in this process indicated the accuracy of the report. During this process, feedback was provided that greater attention should be given to the idea that experiences of ageism did not apply to everyone, and some individuals did not experience ageism. Overall, the member checking process confirmed that the findings accurately reflected the experiences of the focus group participants.

Findings

We present five key themes and subthemes derived from our content analysis. We describe what we heard, followed by example quotes that illustrate these points.

Theme 1: The concept of ageism was not familiar to everyone, and not every older person was aware of experiencing ageism

We asked participants to define ageism, to tell us what it meant to them. Participants often shared ideas that corresponded to definitions of ageism in the literature. However, some participants expressed unfamiliarity with the concept, and there were comments about not having heard the word. When asked to tell us what they thought ageism was, several described something that was more related to the aging process or the challenges of later life rather than ageism:

I never really thought of ageism … I’m not over 60 mentally. (Female, Asian)

Ageism to me is, like you said, age. You just grow older. You age a lot. Which - I have aged a lot. (Female, Hispanic)

I can’t do the things that I used to be able to do. Physically, even though your mind tells you can, sometimes your body tells you otherwise a little bit. (Female, White)

Some participants could not identify having experienced ageism:

I haven’t experienced anything myself that I am aware of. I’ve been … very lucky. (Female, White)

I don’t feel like I’ve experienced ageism, but that’s maybe just my inattention. (Male, White)

When we were sharing findings in the member checking groups, participants wanted us to highlight that the examples of ageism that we reported are not experienced by everyone.

Theme 2: Older people could identify experiences of ageism in health care, employment, the media, and within families

After discussing the definition of ageism in the first question, we asked about experiences of ageism. We prompted participants to think about health care, employment, the media, and families. Below are examples of interpersonal ageism in these four sectors, along with subthemes that emerged. In addition, many examples were not sector specific but instead arose in interactions in other arenas of everyday life.

Ageism in health care

Participants discussed three major ways that they experienced ageism in health care settings. First, they talked about health care providers ignoring/discounting them or speaking directly to someone else in the exam room. Second, they described health care providers making assumptions or stereotyping; and third, they believed that health care providers made health care decision/interpreted symptoms based on chronological age:

I used to take my mother to the doctor, and they would only talk to me. Like my mother wasn’t there. (Female, Black)

[T]he doctor that was working with me, he told me you look like a person that doesn’t use a cell phone. (Female, Black)

“If you were younger,” my provider said, “I would treat you differently.” (Female, White)

[T]he doctor, he said, “we really aren’t going to be able to do anything about it … your age will be the thing we won’t take.” (Male, White)

Ageism in workplace

In the workplace, we heard about ageism in two ways: the experience of being treated unfairly and overlooked, and the experience of being in an unfriendly work environment. Acts of being treated unfairly and overlooked seemed to be in the realm of age discrimination in the workplace, while experiences of being in unfriendly work environments were more subtle, implicit, and perhaps microaggressions:

An employer said, “You’re getting really too old for this.” (Female, White)

They had hired a new person who had just finished her fellowship, and the chief of our division said, “Thank you for lowering the median age of the division.” (Female, White)

I lost my job, basically because I was the oldest person in the company. (Male, White)

Ageism in the media

Participants spoke about ageism in advertisements, TV shows, and movies. They pointed out that older people are underrepresented in the media or portrayed in negative ways. Given the public discourse around the recent presidential election, they also pointed out that politicians’ ages are spotlighted in negative ways:

You don’t see people our age as models in cars. New houses? Forget about it. (Male, White)

Whatever kind of TV you’re watching nowadays, there’s a subtle discrimination against older people. (Male, White)

On the other hand, the observation was made that the positive portrayal of older adults on TV shows and movies is growing, and specific examples were given, including Grace and Frankie (2015–2022) and A Man on the Inside (2024–Present).

Ageism in the family

When describing ageism within family relationships, participants described how relatives took a paternalistic stance toward them—relatives stepped in, even when participants do not find that necessary. Participants reported that relatives made decisions on their behalf and took away decision-making power and autonomy. In some cases, the participants expressed understanding that these actions stemmed from care and concern, but sometimes, there was the sentiment that these relatives were disinterested in the effects on them:

She’s [daughter] always holding on to me, you know, won’t let me do anything. (Female, Black)

It’s sort of a role reversal … [my daughter says] “Just leave that issue alone. I’ll take care of it when I get there.” (Female, White)

When I go to the doctor, and I say what’s bothering me, they [family members] want to jump in and say, “Well, she does this, and she does that.” (Female, Black)

Ageism across settings

There were examples of interpersonal ageism that did not fall into the sectors discussed above. These experiences of ageism occurred in everyday interactions—in stores, on the street, at events, etc. Four types of experiences were identified. First, participants described incidents of being ignored, disrespected, and addressed in diminutive terms:

You go to a meeting with younger people, and the moderators are looking at the younger folks but completely ignoring older people. (Female, Black)

Second, participants expressed the idea that others made assumptions about them based on appearance (age or ability status) that they were vulnerable, incompetent, and in need of assistance:

They talk loud to you because they think that you’re older …, and then assuming that you don’t understand what you said. (Female, Black)

Participants saw scams as ageist acts based on assumptions about incompetency:

I run into problems with contractors who think they can get over on older people. (Female, Black)

It seems like a lot of companies, they see an older person and [take it as] an immediate cue for them to add 20% [to the cost]. Because older people don’t have the resources to double-check what some companies are charging them to fix their air conditioner, they just go ahead and agree to the service. (Male, White)

Third, participants were discouraged by the lack of concern for older people’s unique needs in digital design, health care systems, and public housing developments. They saw this disregard as a form of ageism or unfair treatment:

People that design the technology … have no idea what limitations that we have. (Male, White)

… they’ve compartmentalized all the different specialties … there was a sign in my doctor’s office one day, and it said if you’re here for more than one thing, you need to make another appointment. (Female, White)

Fourth, there was discussion about generational tensions, with younger people not being respectful of older people. We heard the idea that today’s younger generations are not as respectful of others as older generations:

… young people don’t want to respect us because … they don’t want to acknowledge and accept [our] wisdom … (Female, Black)

Theme 3: There was a range of reactions to interpersonal ageism, and much depended on circumstances

Our question about reactions to ageism had two parts. We first asked how they reacted and then followed up with a query about what they might have done differently. Regarding what they did, participants indicated that how they reacted depended on various factors, like the situation, the consequences of the ageism, and the person being ageist:

It depends on who I’m talking to, who’s talking to me. (Female, Black)

[I]f she’s experienced this from her sons … you’ve got to talk about it. But someone who I don’t know, I’ll never see again in my life … (Female, White)

Participants reported on a range of responses that were graded in terms of emotion and action. On one end, responses to ageism involved letting it go, ignoring, or having no behavior response. Although sometimes participants reported not responding behaviorally, they reported emotional responses, like anger or upset. In these instances, participants assessed whether they wanted to expend the energy or further the negative reaction, given the situation was often transient and often not malicious. On the other end, responses were more active, with a direct challenge to the offender or by taking action, like reporting behavior or writing a complaint. When participants described responses to the ageist incidents, some used humor or sarcasm while others tried to educate or raise awareness:

I just dismissed it as—Well, they aren’t very friendly or they aren’t very interpersonal. (Male, White)

If anyone would address me that way, I would very politely with a smile tell them to address me as an adult. (Female, White)

I was honest with them and I said this is making me feel like you were cutting me off at the knees kind of, so I just I explained to them all the feelings I was having. (Female, White)

I did ask the doctor about it, and he apologized afterwards. (Male, White)

In the second part of the question regarding aspirational reactions, we heard that responding in an educational, awareness-building way (with respect or humor) may be most useful. There were suggestions to initiate talks with family and friends to discuss ageist behavior. Participants called for older adults to educate themselves, advocate for themselves, and support each other in confronting ageism:

There have been times I would look at the person and laugh and say, do you need my glasses? (Female, White)

Theme 4: The concept of internalized ageism was not familiar to most participants

When bringing up the topic of internalized ageism, we first provided a definition on a handout, out of concern that the concept would be new to participants. We asked for their thoughts on the concept, if they experienced it in themselves or had seen it in others. And indeed, the concept was new to most participants, and there was difficulty in talking about it in the same way that we understood the concept. We did hear some examples that corresponded to our ideas about internalized ageism, in that they questioned whether they were too old to do something or wear something; accepted “old age” as a valid explanation; or accepted a societal expectation:

We start to think of ourselves as old. We see ourselves as old, we compare ourselves to younger people in what we can and cannot do. (Female, White)

I’m thinking, oh, maybe a woman my age shouldn’t wear something so short. (Female, Black)

It was caution that if you’re over a certain age … you shouldn’t be exercising in that environment. (Male, White)

However, participants also talked about something else related to aging or interpersonal ageism, suggesting a misunderstanding of the concept of internalized ageism. Frequently, they talked about attitudes they tried to maintain in the face of experiencing ageism or questioning their own abilities:

I fight that feeling a lot. Physical ailment, I fight against it. (Female, White)

I’m old. I am forgetful. You know–everything that goes with aging. But it’s just part of it. It’s just part of the process. (Male, White)

… don’t embrace the stereotypes … (Female, Black)

Some participants denied that they experienced internalized ageism, and in fact, this was reinforced in the member-checking sessions.

Theme 5: Older people articulated ways to address interpersonal and internalized ageism

We asked about what to do going forward to support older people in addressing ageism. Most ideas related to education and awareness-raising in younger and older people alike. Participants suggested that respect and maybe humor could be used when confronting others. When facing ageism in more personal relationships, participants suggested that they should have a direct discussion with family members or friends. There were calls for intergenerational exposure and age-inclusivity in organizations and events, as well as peer support among older people. There were suggestions to work conscientiously to resist age stereotypes and to make intentional adaptations to real changes related to ageism; create safe opportunities to try something new to show themselves they can do it; and consciously differentiating between being “too old” to do something as opposed to “choosing” not to do something:

She [sister-in-law] would … bring my cup of coffee, bring this bring … And I would tell her I can get my own coffee. (Female, Black)

I have to accept the reality of growing older without internalizing a dismissive attitude to myself about what I’m capable of doing. (Male, White)

I might say I’m too old for that. But I’m really meaning is I’ve been there, I’ve done that I don’t want to do that anymore, and so now I’m at a point in my life that I don’t have to and I don’t have to make any excuses for it. (Female, White)

In general, ideas about resisting interpersonal and resisting internalized ageism were not distinct. For example, there were calls for intergenerational exposure and age-inclusive events to reduce internalized ageism as well as to reduce interpersonal ageism.

Throughout the discussion, participants mentioned positive things about being treated differently because of age, like being recognized as an experienced, wise person or getting preferential treatment, which they acknowledged soliciting in service settings by “playing the age card.”

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to elucidate older people’s understanding of and experience with ageism. We were especially interested in how older people reacted when confronted with ageism. Through focus group discussions, we learned participants could identify experiences with ageism across various settings, and there was a range of responses that were situation dependent. We gained some insights that could be useful to intervention development.

Given the high rate of everyday ageism reported by older adults in survey data (Allen et al., 2020), we were surprised that not all focus group participants were familiar with the concept of ageism, and some reported that they had not experienced it. When asked how they defined ageism, some participants would talk about the aging process and its challenges. We might understand this by remembering that ageism is a newer concept, identified and defined by Dr. Robert Butler (1969) about 50 years ago. Our findings support the frequent observation that ageism remains accepted and unchallenged in our society (Gendron, 2022). Because aging is a natural and universal process, perhaps discerning and resisting ageism is not seen as pressing as other forms of discrimination. In fact, internalizing age stereotypes is theorized to be an unconscious process (Levy & Banaji, 2002), so it is not surprising that older people are not aware of internalized ageism.

This study supported that raising awareness and educating people is a necessary first step. In fact, it appeared to us that the focus groups served as a consciousness-raising experience for participants themselves. It provided them with an opportunity to articulate their thoughts on a subject and listen to the perspectives of others. It seemed that after discussing the concept in the first interview question and providing definitions and examples, participants were sparked to identify experiences in the second question. For example, once it was suggested that scams were a form of ageism, many participants described experiences where scammers treated them as incompetent or naïve. We propose that this 90-min focus group moved people toward the A (awareness) in Levy’s ABC Method (Levy, 2023) in that people began to identify ageism in their lives.

Focus group participants elucidated for us the contextual nature of responses to ageism. Participants pointed out that, in some cases, it is not worth responding when the consequences to themselves are small or when responding might create tension. They did not want to expend the energy or create any unpleasant disruption, for their own sake. On the other hand, sometimes participants said they did not respond when they thought the ageist behavior deserved a reaction. This lack of response seemed to stem from the desire to avoid embarrassing someone, creating an uncomfortable situation for all, or simply not knowing how to respond. The existing literature regarding how people respond to racism and sexism in interpersonal interactions could guide knowledge development on responding to ageism (Jacob et al., 2022; Swim & Hyers, 1999).

Study findings also suggested that strategies for addressing interpersonal ageism must be tailored to meet the needs and preferences of the older person involved and the current situation. The importance of personalizing anti-ageism intervention is highlighted by Ayalon (2025) who pointed out that an individual’s self-identity regarding chronological age is relevant to their experience of ageism. She uses the term intragroup ageism to describe older adults as perpetrators of ageism toward same-age peers. Intragroup ageism may occur when older people disassociate from others in their age group because they subjectively identify as younger. We did not ask about intragroup ageism in this study, but we heard examples of it as participants distanced themselves from age-peers. Some talked about the need to defy age and the value of hanging out with same-aged peers who are active. Ayalon’s (2025) ideas that intragenerational contact may be useful in reducing intragroup ageism are worthy of our attention in intervention development.

It seemed to us that focus group participants easily identified ageist behaviors among family members, other informal acquaintances, and service providers in stores and restaurants; we had to prompt them more to get examples in health care settings and the workplace. Perhaps this is because they have more interactions in these informal social contexts—in other words, more opportunity to experience interpersonal ageism. Also, it is possible that ageist behavior from doctors and other health care professionals or employers/supervisors was unrecognized because of power dynamics. The consequences of ageism in health care and employment settings may be more injurious than the consequences of ageism in more informal settings (Gonzales et al., 2025). Thus, it is important to increase awareness and skills in dealing with ageism in these arenas.

Participants offered ideas about having discussions with family members who they believed discounted their capacity and reduced their autonomy. Family history, dynamics, expectations, and cultural norms can influence cognitive, affective, and behavioral components of ageism within the family (Gordon & Gonzales, 2025). Of course, there are situations where functional limitations merit family intervention, like continued driving in the face of cognitive impairment. Other times, out of concern for the welfare of their older family members, relatives may be overprotective and paternalistic. This benevolent ageism can threaten older people’s autonomy and self-efficacy, as exemplified in the extremely protective behaviors that older people reported during the COVID epidemic (Barth et al., 2021). It is important to empower older people to have these family discussions and give skills to engage in these conversations.

The concepts of disrespect and generational conflict came up frequently, and they were seen as examples of ageism. Participants seemed to endorse the idea that people merited respect based on their age. This may be understood as a form of benevolent ageism (Bisconti et al., 2020). Further, they assessed the behavior of younger generations as disrespectful. In fact, we heard ageist comments toward young people. There is some evidence that the meaning of respect has shifted over time, and older people view it as hierarchical and duty-based while younger people view it more transactionally, earned, and tied to fairness (Bhattarai, 2025; Mehta, 1997). It is possible that generational conflict arises from different ideas about respect as well as other cohort differences in values, perspectives, and behaviors (Monte & Ligacion, 2025). It is not clear how ageism plays into these differing generational approaches, but we suggest that further research and discussion around the concept of how respect is defined and enacted across generations may be important for advancing anti-ageism work.

There are several limitations to this study. We know that ageism is compounded by other prejudices in our society (Gendron et al., 2024). In our nine focus groups, we had diversity of gender, ability status, sexual orientation, and ethnicity, but we did not query specifically about these intersecting identities. We heard comments related to these intersectionalities, and this topic deserves in-depth analysis, which we did not pursue here. We are continuing this work in future studies to elevate our focus on intersectionality. Another shortcoming is that we did not have focus group participants complete standardized measures of interpersonal and internalized ageism. Among other things, these data could complement participants’ claims about not experiencing ageism.

We believe this study yielded unique insights into older adults’ understanding and experience of ageism, especially regarding how they reacted when they experienced it. More research is needed. Going forward, the intersection of minoritized identities and age should be spotlighted. We call for closer attention to ageism and ableism, as these biases are intertwined and mutually reinforcing; and as we live longer lives, a growing share of our population will confront ageism and ableism jointly. This work provides insights for developing interventions to empower older people to recognize, understand, and respond to both interpersonal and internalized ageism. Moving forward, we advocate for interventions focused on older people with the aim of increasing awareness and building skills to respond to ageism.
