Single‑dermatotomy tunneled femoral central venous catheter placement in a neonate
Authors: Hanvit Lee, Christopher Barnett, Rose Lifland, Marc Schiffman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe describe our experience in placing a tunneled femoral central venous catheter using a single-dermatotomy, image-guided technique in a preterm neonate․ Compared to the conventional approach of using 2 skin incisions‚ this technique utilizes a single small incision in the mid lateral thigh through which the catheter is advanced and tunneled subcutaneously into the common femoral vein․ This is an appealing approach because the catheter exit site is away from the contaminated diaper/groin area, and we hypothesize that this may reduce infection risk, although this single case demonstrates feasibility rather than proving reduction. In this case report‚ the procedure was technically successful with stable catheter function over a 14 day period without early complications of infection or dislodgment․ This case report confirms the feasibility of neonate tunneled central venous catheter placement using a single-dermatotomy technique and suggests the procedure may possibly be a safer option for central venous access․Keywords: Central venous catheter, Femoral, Tunneled, Single-dermatotomy, Interventional radiology

Case report

A 5-day-old male, 33 6/7-week gestational age, weighing 1.8 kg, was admitted to the neonatal intensive care (NICU) following delivery for management of prematurity, feeding advancement, and infectious evaluation. At 3 days old, he developed bilious emesis and underwent an upper gastrointestinal series which was without evidence of malrotation or bowel obstruction, but which demonstrated high-grade gastroesophageal reflux. In this setting, and given a history of difficult intravenous access, interventional radiology (IR) was consulted for central venous catheter placement to provide total parenteral nutrition (TPN).

The patient was brought to the IR suite and general anesthesia was administered. The patient was prepped and draped utilizing maximal sterile barrier technique. The planned tunnel length was calculated externally on the skin from the planned exit site in the mid lateral thigh‚ to the planned femoral venipuncture site‚ which can be located by ultrasound․ The length of the intravascular sheath/introducer (6 cm) was chosen to be approximately 2 cm longer than the planned tunnel length (4 cm), so that there is adequate intraluminal sheath purchase after passing through the tunnel (Fig. 1).

Schematic of single-dermatotomy tunneled femoral central venous catheter placement․ (A) A small skin nick is created in the lateral thigh. (2) Under ultrasound guidance, a micropuncture needle is tunneled from the dermatotomy to the common femoral vein (tunnel length of 4 cm). (3) The micropuncture needle is exchanged over wire for a peel-away sheath (6 cm) which is at least 1-2 cm longer than the tunnel length to ensure adequate purchase within the vein. (4) Through the peel-away sheath, the 3 French central venous catheter is placed into the common femoral vein with catheter tip at the IVC-RA junction, which is confirmed on fluoroscopy.

A single small skin nick was made at the level of the mid lateral left thigh through which a 21-gauge, 7 cm micropuncture needle (Micropuncture Introducer Set; Cook, Inc, Bloomington, Indiana) was tunneled subcutaneously 4 cm cephalad to the level of the left common femoral vein (Fig. 1). Ultrasound-guided technique was performed using a high frequency hockey-stick linear transducer and short axis out-of-plane technique․ The common femoral vein was identified below the inguinal ligament‚ to the medial side of the common femoral artery‚ and confirmed as respirophasic in nature and compressible․ The target vein was the proximal common femoral vein‚ just distal to the saphenofemoral junction, where it measured 2.3 mm in diameter․ The femoral artery was kept in view during the whole puncture‚ to ensure that the needle was being passed safely medial to the femoral artery and nerve․ The needle tip was advanced with small fanning movements in the subcutaneous tunnel and the vein was punctured on the first attempt‚ demonstrated by nonpulsatile venous return and visualization of the needle within the vein on ultrasound imaging․ An 0.018-inch wire from the micropuncture set was used to cannulate the micropuncture needle, and the wire was advanced to the inferior cavoatrial junction under intermittent fluoroscopic visualization and the length of the intravascular distance was determined based on the wire (Fig. 2).

Anteroposterior fluoroscopic spot image demonstrating the linear course of the wire and tunneled peel-away sheath with the central venous catheter entering the sheath over a wire at the level of the common femoral vein (arrow); the wire courses superiorly through the inferior vena cava with its tip at the IVC-RA junction (inferior cavoatrial junction).

The needle was exchanged over the wire for a peel-away sheath with its introducer. The peel-away sheath measured 6 cm, providing approximately 2 cm of intravascular sheath purchase beyond the 4 cm subcutaneous tunnel. A 3 French, single-lumen, non-cuffed catheter (PowerPICC SV, Bard Access Systems, Salt Lake City, UT, USA) was trimmed to appropriate length based on the previously determined intravascular distance. The introducer was removed and the trimmed catheter was advanced through the peel-away sheath (Fig. 3). Note that the “peel-away sheath with its introducer” is the MicroEZ Universal Microintroducer from the PowerPICC SV set; the peel-away sheath with the introducer together measures 7 cm, and the peel-away sheath alone measures 6 cm.

Anteroposterior fluoroscopic spot image demonstrating the final position of the left femoral tunneled central venous catheter with tip at the IVC-RA junction (inferior cavoatrial junction).

The catheter tip location was assessed using single AP fluoroscopic spot image (Fig. 3)․ The targeted catheter tip location was at the inferior cavoatrial junction (tracing the right atrial curve medially to its intersection with the expected vertical path of the inferior vena cava)․ No ECG-guided catheter tip confirmation system was required; catheter tip position was deemed satisfactory on this single AP image, and no repositioning was needed. The catheter at the lateral thigh exit site was secured in place with a 4-0 nonabsorbable monofilament suture and covered with a dry gauze and transparent dressing․ The catheter limb was then gently looped along the lateral thigh and secured with additional sterile transparent dressing to minimize torque on the exit site and reduce the risk of dislodgement in the diapered lower extremity.

Fluoroscopy time was 0.9 minutes with a dose-area product of only 7.00 uGy-m2 due to low-dose pulsed fluoroscopy and tight collimation for the 1․8-kg neonate․ Lead shielding was used for chest‚ neck‚ and contralateral gonad as needed unless viewing was required․ Fluoroscopic tip confirmation was preferred to ECG-guided or length measurement methods in this patient‚ as she was in the IR suite under general anesthesia and the estimated cumulative radiation exposure from acquiring the AP spot image was expected to be quite low for the short time required․ Real-time fluoroscopic evaluation provided confirmation of wire position within the inferior vena cava‚ and detection of any inadvertent catheter loop or kink before deployment․ The accessed groin was examined at the time and afterwards for hematoma․ There was no evidence of groin or retroperitoneal hemorrhage. The catheter was patent‚ functional‚ and free of infection‚ occlusion‚ or dislodgement during the 14 days of TPN therapy․ There were no late mechanical complications with the catheter (ie‚ leak‚ kinking‚ fracture‚ or occlusion)‚ nor any other clinical evidence of lower extremity edema in the patient․ The exit site healed well‚ and the catheter functioned well until its scheduled removal․ The catheter was voluntarily removed when the 24-hour tolerance of a full enteral diet was established and central access was no longer required․ The patient remained afebrile and blood cultures did not need to be obtained as there was no clinical concern for catheter-related infection․

Traditionally, tunneled central venous catheter placement involves an initial dermatotomy at the venipuncture site, followed by a second dermatotomy at the desired catheter skin exit site, through which the catheter is tunneled using a tunneling stylet [1,2]. In contrast, this case illustrates utilizing a single dermatotomy at the intended catheter exit site, through which the access needle was introduced and itself tunneled subcutaneously to the venipuncture site, eliminating the need for a separate skin incision in the groin for venipuncture (Fig. 4).

Tunneled central venous catheter enters the single-dermatotomy site in the mid lateral thigh (black arrow), which is away from the common femoral vein access site in the groin (white arrow).

A single-incision/dermatotomy technique has been previously described for tunneled internal jugular central venous access [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. A retrospective review of 15 pediatric patients who received a tunneled central venous access device via the internal jugular vein with a single dermatotomy concluded a technical success rate of 100% [3]. There were no procedure-related or early complications, demonstrating both technical feasibility as well as secure use of a single incision for central venous access in children [3]. Contractor et al. (2009) demonstrated the feasibility and safety of a single-incision technique for chest port and tunneled dialysis catheter placements in adult patients, with similar procedural times compared to conventional technique [4].

For tunneled internal jugular access via a single-incision, there are reports of manually curving the venipuncture needle to facilitate the appropriate course through the subcutaneous chest-wall tracts and over the clavicle into the vein [[3], [4], [5]]. In contrast, a single-dermatotomy technique for tunneled femoral central venous catheter placement is technically simpler insofar as the subcutaneous tract is more linear and does not require negotiating the clavicle. In the pediatric population‚ another theoretical benefit of a single-dermatotomy for tunneled femoral central venous catheter placement is the avoidance of an incision in the inguinal/diaper area․ Since the skin opening is moved from a high-bioburden region to the lateral thigh‚ we hypothesize that tunneled femoral catheters may also cause fewer catheter-related bloodstream infections․ This hypothesis is based on anatomic relocation of the exit site‚ but our study demonstrated the feasibility of this approach without showing a reduction in infection․ A retrospective analysis evaluated single-incision placement of tunneled lower extremity central venous catheters in neonates and infants in either the common femoral or saphenous veins [6]. Of the 271 primary catheter insertions by interventional radiologists, the technical success rate was 100%, with an adjusted infectious complication rate of 0.44 per 100 catheter days [6].

Based on our experience, the most important technical consideration in the single-dermatotomy technique is to estimate the length of the subcutaneous tunnel from the skin incision‚ seeking to match the intravascular length of the peel-away sheath․ As a general rule‚ the length of the tunnel should be at least 1 to 2 cm shorter than the intravascular length of the sheath/introducer assembly․ This is to ensure that enough of the sheath is within the lumen of the common femoral vein after traversing the subcutaneous tunnel․ In our case‚ a tunnel length of 4 cm and a sheath length of 6 cm‚ produced about 2 cm of intravascular sheath purchase‚ which we found to be sufficient․ The length of the planned tunnel is measured externally on the skin before dermatotomy‚ and the exit site and venipuncture site are marked as identified by ultrasound․ The sheath/introducer is then cut and fashioned to the correct length․ If the planned tunnel length is longer than the sheath length‚ a longer peel-away sheath should be used or the dermatotomy site moved closer to the venipuncture site․ Insufficient intraluminal sheath purchase may lead to failure to maintain venous access during catheter placement․

A note on catheter nomenclature. The brand name of the catheter placed in this case is PowerPICC, which is a non-cuffed polyurethane catheter. Even though there is “PICC” in the name, this PowerPICC was not inserted “peripherally” in the traditional sense (i.e., the common femoral vein was accessed, not a peripheral vein). To avoid confusion and also not to use a brand name, we decided to use the term “tunneled central venous catheter” because our case utilized the common femoral vein as the actual site of needle puncture (even though the skin exit site is somewhat “peripheral” in the mid lateral thigh). In effect, a non-cuffed polyurethane catheter (PowerPICC) was tunneled and utilized as a central venous catheter. Other papers in the literature may use “tunneled PICC” and “tunneled central venous catheter” somewhat interchangeably.

There are several limitations of this report. This is a case report of a single patient in whom the catheter was in place for only 14 days‚ and therefore our results provide evidence that this single-dermatotomy approach may be technically feasible‚ without showing its equivalency or superiority to the customary two-incision approach․ Second‚ there was no evidence in this case to suggest any reduction in infection risk from relocating the anatomic exit site․ This remains hypothetical until definitive proof is available from a comparison study․ Finally‚ it is not possible to extrapolate the long-term complications (catheter-associated thrombosis‚ late dislodgement‚ infection due to biofilm) from a single experience․ Larger potential comparative series will be needed to determine if single-dermatotomy tunneled femoral central venous catheter placement has the potential to impact these outcomes among neonates and infants․

This case shows that single-dermatotomy tunneled femoral central venous catheter placement is feasible in the neonate population‚ and that single-dermatotomy tunneled central venous catheter placement is less complicated and less traumatic than customary two-incision tunneled catheter placement‚ as the technique does not require insertion of an additional stylet for tunneling․ Larger comparative series are required to determine whether moving the exit site further away from the inguinal/diaper area will lower the risk of infection․
