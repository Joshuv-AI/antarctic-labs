Executive Summary
Fission gases such as xenon (Xe) play a critical role in determining the behavior and response of
nuclear fuel. Given that Xe has little solubility in UO2 , it accumulates and forms bubbles, which
significantly impact fuel performance. Intra- and inter-granular bubble nucleation and growth
can lead to fuel swelling, and once bubbles interconnect at grain boundaries, fission gas can be
released into the plenum. At low temperatures, limited uranium vacancy mobility can restrict
swelling, therefore causing the bubbles to become highly pressurized. Consequently, this can in-
duce micro-cracking, promote fission gas release (increasing the likelihood of cladding failure),
and even lead to fuel pulverization under accident conditions such as a loss of coolant accident.
As bubble evolution is strongly influenced by local temperature and fission rate, markedly dif-
ferent behavior occurs across the radial profile of the fuel pellet. Capturing the mechanisms that
underpin bubble evolution is therefore important to predict these behaviors in the fuel.
 Previous models describing important mechanisms informed by lower length scale simula-
tions have been developed under the NEAMS program. These can describe the evolution of a
single bubble type (i.e., single value for radius and pressure) at each position in the pellet, for
instance using the Centipede cluster dynamic code. However, in reality, a full distribution in
bubble sizes and pressures exists within the microstructure at a given position in the pellet. To
address this the cluster dynamics code Xolotl, which can predict Xe and vacancy phase space
(i.e., bubble distributions) for intra-granular bubbles, has been used before. Prior work bench-
marked the Xolotl code against the Centipede cluster dynamics code to ensure compatibility and
to verify that mobile defect properties are adequately transferred between the two codes, along
with some physics improvements. In this work, we go further by introducing a physics-based set
of improvements that will allow us to accurately predict bubble size distributions and internal
bubble pressures under representative UO2 irradiation conditions.
 The improvements include (i) coupling bubble-defect reaction energies to a virial equation
of state (EOS), (ii) including a bubble surface tension contribution, (iii) incorporating radiation-
induced re-solution of Xe and vacancies, (iv) enabling pressure-driven dislocation loop punching
through an effective emission of interstitial clusters informed by interstitial loop energetics, (v)
accounting for radiation induced athermal diffusion of Xe, and (vi) implementing a Booth-type
grain boundary sink representation for all mobile defects and defect clusters.
 After these modifications, we observe good agreement of Xolotl fission gas bubble size and
concentration predictions with legacy experimental measurements. Additionally, it allows the
distribution of Xe bubble pressures and radius to also be predicted and compared to data pro-
duced through the Advanced Fuels Campaign (AFC) program. Here, we have done this by run-
ning simulations under conditions similar to the AFC post-irradiation examination (PIE) samples
irradiated at North Anna 2 light water reactor (LWR). Our results shows excellent agreement
with these experimental measurements.

1 Introduction
In nuclear fuel under irradiation, the accumulation of fission gas, such as Xe, in the lattice
leads to the formation and growth of intra-granular fission gas bubbles, due to low fission gas
solubility in the fuel [17,30,35]. These bubbles evolve through a combination of gas production,
diffusion, vacancy absorption, and radiation-induced re-solution [25, 33]. As a result, the intra-
granular bubble population at a given location in the pellet is not characterized by a single size or
pressure, but by broad distributions which vary radially within the pellet due to temperature and
fission rate density gradients. The accurate prediction of these distributions, and the mechanistic
understanding of fission gas bubble growth, is essential to capture fuel behavior under transient
conditions. Indeed, bubbles of different sizes and pressures are expected to exhibit distinct
responses to a power excursion.
 Additionally, a fraction of the fission gas atoms trapped in these bubbles are re-solved into
the lattice at a given rate that is proportional to the fission rate [25, 33]. This trapping-resolution
cycle encourages gas atoms to reach grain-boundaries with an effective diffusivity that is mod-
ulated with respect to the lattice diffusivity by the proportion of gas trapped in intra-granular
bubbles, which evolves following the relative rate of trapping and resolution. Once at the grain
boundary, fission gas atoms form inter-granular bubbles [33,39], which exhibit growth due to the
absorption of vacancies, driven by the gas-induced over-pressure [34, 39]. Inter-granular bub-
bles are principally responsible for fuel swelling in UO2 [17, 23]. In addition, as inter-granular
bubbles keep growing, they ultimately percolate to triple junctions, leading to the release of fis-
sion gas to the plenum, thereby increasing the internal rod pressure. Both swelling and fission
gas release can lead to mechanical strain imparted upon the cladding and increase the risk of
cladding rupture during a temperature transient. [7, 12, 13, 23, 36, 38]. At high burnup, fission
gas bubbles coalesce to form a so-called high-burnup structure (HBS), characterized by a finer
grain-size and increased porosity, primarily observed at the pellet periphery.
 Extending the refueling cycle length of light water reactors (LWR) is considered a promising
path towards more affordable and abundant energy production [5]. However, with increased
burnup, fuel rods must be proven to perform adequately during an accident scenario, thus con-
tributing to maintaining cladding integrity. The microstructural state of the fuel pellet governs
the way it responds to accident conditions and, therefore, mechanistic models are essential tools
developed to reduce uncertainties in a way that can be leveraged to support burnup extension. To
support licensing for extended burnup, it is crucial to understand transient fission gas release and
fuel fragmentation/pulverization, relocation and dispersal (FFRD) during a loss-of-coolant acci-
dent (LOCA) [5,16]. Under LOCA conditions, the possible ballooning of the cladding can allow
relocation of fuel fragments, which would impact power profiles, and in the unlikely case of
cladding rupture, lead to fuel dispersion in the primary coolant. Integral LOCA testing on UO2
fuel conducted at the Halden reactor [24] and semi-integral tests performed at Studsvick [11]
have shown that FFRD under high burnup condition result in extremely fine (micron sized) fuel

 1

particles. Operating UO2 beyond the current peak rod-average burnup limit of 62 GWd/tU im-
plies complex evolutions of the microstructure, often associated with fission gas behavior.
 Previous mechanistic models implemented in the Centipede cluster dynamics code [20, 21]
and SIFGRS [27] (the fission gas swelling and release module of the BISON fuel performance
code) can describe the evolution of a single bubble type (single value for bubble radius and
pressure) at each position in the pellet, which is limiting for the prediction of transient fission gas
behavior. To address this, the Xolotl cluster dynamics code is used to predict the full Xe-vacancy
phase space for intra-granular bubbles. Prior work benchmarked Xolotl against Centipede to
ensure compatibility and to verify that defect properties are adequately transferred between the
two codes [4].
 In the current Xolotl implementation, intra-granular Xe bubble behavior in UO2 employed
an empirical polynomial representation of the Xe equation of state (EOS) to approximate bub-
ble pressure and related driving forces [9]. While enabling efficient computations, this approach
can be limited when extrapolating across temperature, bubble size, and density regimes, or when
coupling bubble thermodynamics to additional physical mechanisms. Motivated by these limi-
tations and recent advances in atomistic modeling of Xe in bubbles, the physics is extended in
the following respects:

 1. Bubble pressure description using the Yang and Wirth EOS [41].

 2. Surface energy contributions to bubble energetics and dissociation rates.

 3. Radiation induced re-solution of Xe and vacancies using Turnbull heterogeneous bubble
 destruction for small bubbles and the Setyawan model for larger bubbles [26, 32].

 4. Bubble-driven dislocation loop punching, modeled as emission of interstitial clusters with
 energetics determined by loop line-energy fits derived from interstitial molecular dynam-
 ics cluster energetics [15, 18].

 5. Radiation induced athermal diffusion for small Xe-bearing clusters, to model the effects
 of ballistic mixing and thermal spikes.

 6. A unified Booth-type [14] sink formulation for mobile defects (vacancies, di-vacancies,
 interstitials, and small Xe-bearing clusters up to {Xe:V8 }), enabling a treatment of loss to
 grain boundaries consistent with the BISON fuel performance code.

 This paper is structured as follows: Section 2 summarizes the baseline cluster dynamics
framework. Section 3 details the physics improvements implemented throughout this work. Sec-
tions 5 and 6 present the results obtained with the updated code and discuss comparisons with
available experiments, as well as key parameters introduced through the code improvements.

3 Physics improvements

3.1 Pressure contribution to reaction energies
The baseline UO2 bubble model in Xolotl used an empirical polynomial EOS fit from static,
energy minimized empirical potential calculation results for Xe pressure as a function of bubble
state variables [9]. This polynomial representation is efficient but remains a strong approxima-
tion of fission gas behavior under high pressure. The present work replaces the polynomial by
a physics-based Xe EOS for bubbles, also derived from MD simulations [41], enabling pressure
to be derived consistently and used directly in dissociation energetics.

3.3 Radiation-induced re-solution of Xe and vacancies
Fission fragments and associated thermal spikes can interact with intra-granular bubbles and
re-solve fission gas atoms from bubbles back into the UO2 lattice. This process inhibits bubble
growth and plays an essential role in determining the bubble number density and size distribu-
tion under irradiation [26, 28]. Following established models [26, 32, 37], we treat radiation
induced re-solution as a bubble-size-dependent and fission-rate-dependent process applied to
bubble clusters.

3.4 Dislocation loop punching
Over-pressurized gas bubbles can relieve pressure by punching out a prismatic dislocation loop,
thereby increasing the bubble volume without requiring an incoming vacancy flux. This loop
punching mechanism is broadly documented in the literature [10, 15].
 Within Xolotl, loop punching can be represented as a unary event that converts a bubble
cluster (NXe , NV ) into a larger bubble with additional vacancies and emitted interstitial clusters
that represents the punched loop. Although a complete description of dislocation loop phase
space would be ideal to model this phenomenon, it represents a significant rework of the current
Xolotl structure. Here we introduced a first approximated, practical description of loop punching
by emitting series of n I4 clusters (n being an integer) based on the effective energetics of a 4n-
interstitial loop. We implemented the reaction:

 XeNXe VNV → XeNXe VNV +∆N + I∆N , (3.13)
where ∆N is the number of interstitials in the loop, ensuring that the loop radius does not exceed
the bubble radius.

4.1 Bubble size and density data (White & Tucker / Baker)
The intra-granular bubble radius and concentration data used for comparison originate from the
compilation of White and Tucker [40], based on earlier measurements by Baker [1]. After a
short irradiation period, the population of intra-granular bubbles at several operating tempera-
tures (several locations in the pellet) is characterized by an average bubble radius and number
density [40] for each temperature.
 Results benchmarked against this set of experimental data were obtained with 304 days of
irradiation at a fission rate density of 7.9×1018 m−3 , deduced from time and burnup indications
found in Ref. [1]. Constant power and temperature were assumed.

4.2 AFC post-irradiation examination data of North Anna Unit 2
Additional comparison is performed with PIE data from the North Anna Unit 2 reactor, repre-

the AFC program, and the BISON results from Ref. [6], as well as private communications with
the authors, were used to inform temperature, fission rate and fission gas yield inputs for Xolotl
simulations. For now as a first approximation, constant power and temperature were assumed.
Future work could refine this approach by considering power and temperature histories.

5.1 Bubble radius and concentration

6 Discussion

6.1 Comparison with Baker measurements
The results presented in Section 5.1 are in good agreement with legacy experimental measure-
ments [1, 40].
 At smaller temperatures (namely below 1400 K), Xolotl simulations slightly underestimate
the concentration weighted average bubble radius. On the other hand, at temperatures above
1400 K, Xolotl tends to predict average radii larger than the experimental measurements. The
maximum radius discrepancy is found to be 0.2 nm, or 20% in relative terms. Bubble concentra-
tion is systematically larger than experimental measurements, but remain within the right order
of magnitude. Both radius and concentration follow the right temperature trend.
 The comparison between Xolotl predictions and these experimental measurements must be
interpreted with caution due to multiple sources of uncertainty on both experiments and sim-
ulations. On the modeling side, Xolotl provides a complete cluster distribution in (NXe , NV )
space. However, comparison with experiments requires introducing an effective visibility cut-
off, which is somewhat arbitrary set to the transition from small mobile clusters to fixed bubbles
(the smallest bubble counted in the averaging is {Xe1 :V9 }, with a 0.45 nm radius). On the ex-
perimental side, the reported quantities are derived from TEM observations with limited (and
undocumented) resolution, as well as potential deviations in the extrapolation from flat TEM
data to volumetric concentrations. It is also difficult to quantify the effects of bubble super-
imposition. Consequently, a perfect agreement is not expected between these two approaches.
We note that the comparison presented in Figure 5.1 exhibits similar discrepancies as earlier
modeling work [29], although they underestimate bubble concentration while the present work
overestimate it.

6.2 Comparison with AFC PIE data (North Anna 2)
The results presented in Section 5.3 are obtained with conditions equivalent to a fractional radial
position in the pellet of 0.57, about halfway between pellet centerline and rim, which places
these measurements in the restructuring dark-zone [22]. This is expected to mostly affect larger
grain-boundary bubbles, but as discussed in Section 6.6, subtle indirect effects could also affect
these nanometric bubble distributions. We observe a large concentration of small, highly over-
pressurized bubbles (up to 30 GPa).
 The modeling artefacts that can be observed on Figure 5.3 (blue curves around 1.5 nm radius)
are due to a concentration accumulation at the limit of the simulation phase space (as bubbles
cannot grow larger).

6.3 Grain size effect
Grain size is a key microstructural parameter controlling fission gas behavior in UO2 , because it
directly affects defect transport, bubble nucleation, and gas release pathways. Grain boundaries
act as dominant sinks for point defects and fission gas atoms, thereby governing the balance
between intra-granular bubble growth and inter-granular gas release [28].
 Since understanding the role of grain size is essential for predicting and optimizing the perfor-
mance of doped fuels (which are specifically engineered to exhibit larger grains than non-doped
UO2 ), a set of simulations with grain radius varying from 1 to 25 µm was performed. As grain
boundary sink strength was implemented as a function of a spherical grain radius, it makes it
possible to pilot this radius and observe the effects on the fission gas bubble population. Fig-
ure 6.1 presents the distributions of fission gas bubble pressure and size for three distinct grain
radii: 1, 5 and 25 µm, at 1123 K, after a month of irradiation. We observe a strong sensitivity
of the bubble size and pressure distribution to grain size. In this temperature regime, the dif-
fusion of Xe and interstitials is much faster than that of vacancies. In a smaller grain, where
the grain-boundary sink is more effective, fast Xe and interstitial depletion leaves an excess of
vacancies that will drive bubble growth and pressure decrease. Hence the long tail observed in
panel 6.1a. To the contrary, a larger grain will inhibit grain-boundary loss. Consequently, the
larger population of Xe maintains bubble pressure high while the larger population of interstitial
annihilates more vacancies, inhibiting bubble growth, as observed on panel 6.1c for a grain size
of 25 µm which corresponds to a typical doped UO2 scenario. Panel 6.1b shows the intermediate
behavior, for a typical UO2 5 µm grain size.

6.5 Re-solution model effect
As described in Section 3.3, a hybrid effective re-solution rate was implemented in Xolotl. This
approach introduces two additional parameters to Xolotl: the fission fragment track radius Rff
and the threshold radius Rswitch that switches between modified Turnbull and Setyawan models.
 The effect of the switch radius value was investigated and is presented in Figure 6.3. When
going from a pure Setyawan model (panel 6.3a) to a switch radius of Rswitch =0.5 (panel 6.3b),
we observe a more important bubble growth. This is consistent with the fact that the modified
Turnbull model re-solves Xe and V more aggressively, so when it is applied in the small bubble
regime (below 0.5 nm), it stimulates the production of Xe and V isolated defects that can then
feed the larger bubbles. Once Rs is increased to 1.0 nm, a large fraction of the intermediate
bubbles are destroyed as well, leaving only a few large bubbles that experience a fast growth (see
panel 6.3c), stimulated by the abundance of isolated Xe and V. Finally, once the switch radius
becomes too large (panel 6.3d), the aggressive modified Turnbull re-solution rate is applied to
bubbles of all sizes in the simulation and strongly inhibits growth.
 The effect of the fission fragment track was also investigated, and the results are presented in
Figure 6.4, but we have not observed significant differences between the two cases Rff = 2 and
4 nm.

6.6 Limitations in the high burnup structure regime
The present Xolotl implementation does not explicitly model the formation of high burnup struc-
tures (HBS), and therefore cannot capture the effects of the associated microstructural transition
occurring in the fuel rim at high burnup. Experimentally, HBS is characterized by grain subdi-
vision/recrystallization and the development of a heterogeneous porosity, including microscale
pores together with nanoscale bubbles decorating sub-grain boundaries [28]. This restructuring
fundamentally alters transport pathways, sink strengths, and gas distribution.

 The bubble physics currently implemented in Xolotl is formulated for intra-granular bubbles
exclusively, it does not account for the emergence of large pores and the redistribution of gas
between grain interiors, sub-grain boundaries, and inter-granular networks that occurs in the
HBS regime.
 As a result, in conditions where HBS is expected, discrepancies between Xolotl predictions
and experiments may be observed. The predicted intra-granular bubble size distribution is ex-
pected to be biased toward larger bubbles. Indeed, the emergence of HBS introduces additional
free volume and coarser porosity, which can accommodate important fractions of the produced
Xe experimentally. Additionally, HBS grain subdivision and pore formation increase the density
of interfaces that act as sinks for point defects and small clusters. Without these additional sinks,
defect concentrations in Xolotl may remain artificially elevated in the bulk, altering the balance
between vacancy-driven and interstitial-driven reactions, similarly to what has been shown in
Figure 6.1 with the grain size comparison.

7 Future Work

7.1 Further analysis on Xe and vacancy re-solution
The current hybrid modified Turnbull-Setyawan re-solution approach has demonstrated encour-
aging results, but there is potential to enhance it through a more physically grounded treatment.
As parameters within this approach impact the predictions (see Figure 6.3), revisiting the molec-
ular dynamics data underlying the Setyawan model may help improve its applicability, especially
for smaller bubble sizes.
 Future efforts should also focus on further examining the vacancy resolution model to strengthen
confidence in its predictive capability. Although meaningful advances have been made in this
work by incorporating vacancy resolution, thermal spike driven void shrinkage has potential to
be further refined. In particular, molecular dynamics simulations of thermal spikes interacting
with voids could provide useful insight for improving the parameterization of this mechanism
within Xolotl.

7.2 Dislocation loops
As described in Section 3.4, we introduced an initial representation of dislocation loop punching
that consists of emitting a series of n I4 clusters (n being an integer), based on the effective
energetics of a 4n-interstitial loop. Although this approach enables the modeling of punching
events that relieve bubble pressure and promotes growth in good agreement with data from the
AFC program (see Figure 5.3), a more complete description of the dislocation loop phase space
would be beneficial. This is especially important for the modeling of fission gas behavior in
the context of HBS formation, as high dislocation densities at high burnup are thought to drive
subgrain formation.
  Future work should focus on incorporating large interstitial clusters, in a manner similar to
how vacancies and Xe are currently treated in Xolotl (i.e., by introducing a third phase-space
dimension that explicitly represents large, immobile interstitial clusters).

7.3 Full benchmark against all available AFC data
Section 5.3 presents a comparison of the predicted bubble size and pressure distributions with a
set of PIE data obtained by the AFC program. Future work should extend this comparison to all
available data, at several radial positions in the pellet, hence different temperatures and fission
rate density.
 Additionally, the current benchmark against these data could be refined by considering the
actual power and temperature history instead of representative constant values in the Xolotl

 25

simulations.

7.4 Sensitivity analysis and calibration
The fission gas model currently implemented in Xolotl relies on a range of input parameters
drawn from different sources, each with varying levels of uncertainty. If these uncertainties
were simply propagated through the model, the overall uncertainty in the predictions could in-
crease significantly. While some parameters are well established and supported by experimental
data, others remain less constrained and introduce greater uncertainty into the model. Given
the complexity of the problem, it is important to identify which parameters most strongly influ-
ence model predictions so that future efforts can be directed where they will have the greatest
impact. As discussed previously, physically revisiting and better grounding models such as the
resolution treatment also represents an important step toward reducing these uncertainties.
 To address these ideas, a sensitivity analysis will be performed to systematically assess how
variations in input parameters affect model outputs. This approach will quantify the relative
influence of each parameter and highlight those that contribute most significantly to variabil-
ity in the results. Such insights can guide targeted improvements, whether through additional
experiments or refined modeling approaches.
 In addition, incorporating uncertainty quantification would significantly strengthen the model.
Techniques such as Bayesian calibration, which have been successfully applied to other nuclear
fuel properties, offer a promising pathway for this purpose.
 Overall, this work will help prioritize future model development by focusing attention on the
most influential sources of uncertainty. It will also improve confidence in model predictions by
providing a clearer understanding of how uncertainties in input parameters propagate to uncer-
tainties in fission gas bubble behavior.

8 Conclusions
Extending the refueling cycle length of current generation LWR is a promising path towards a
more affordable energy production [5]. Yet, operating UO2 beyond the current peak rod-average
burnup limit of 62 GWd/tU presents significant challenges, primarily because it implies severe
and complex evolutions of the microstructure. Understanding how intra-granular fission gas bub-
bles size and pressure distributions evolve over time at various temperatures (and consequently
at various radial positions within the fuel pellet) is essential to parameterize fuel performance
codes. The cluster dynamics code Xolotl is employed to predict the complete distribution of bub-
bles sizes, compositions, and pressures over time for various operating conditions (temperature,
fission rate), based on the energetics provided by Centipede for a given chemical environment.
 We have introduced a set of physics-based extensions to Xolotl for intra-granular fission gas
bubble evolution in UO2 . The primary changes are: (i) replacement of an empirical polynomial
Xe EOS by the Yang-Wirth virial EOS, (ii) explicit surface-tension contribution to bubble emis-
sion energetics, (iii) radiation-induced re-solution of Xe and vacancies using a hybrid modified
Turnbull-Setyawan model, (iv) an effective dislocation loop punching mechanism implemented
as interstitial-cluster emission with energetics guided by UO2 prismatic loop line-energy, (v)
athermal transport for small Xe-bearing clusters proportional to fission rate, and (vi) a Booth
sink model for all mobile defects.
 These improvements allow good agreement between Xolotl fission gas bubble size and con-
centration predictions and legacy experimental measurements. The distribution of Xe bubble
pressure and radius is also predicted in the conditions of the Department of Energy AFC pro-
gram PIE samples irradiated at North Anna 2 LWR, and show good agreement with those ex-
perimental measurements.
 These implementations also introduce several parameters as first approximations, that future
works should anchor to novel atomic-scale simulations. Future work should also focus on a
thorough sensitivity analysis and a focus on uncertainty quantification in order to identify the
input parameters that must be be granted additional computational and experimental effort.
