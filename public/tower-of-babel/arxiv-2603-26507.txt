Abstract
. We study the integral means spectrum associated with the analytic function whose derivative is the so-called randomized Riemann zeta-function, introduced some time ago by Bagchi. The randomized ζ {\zeta} -function, ζ rand ​ ( σ + i ​ h ) {\zeta}_{\mathrm{rand}}({\sigma}+ih) , is known to represent the asymptotic statistical behaviour of the random vertical shifts of the actual ζ {\zeta} -function in the critical strip, 1 / 2 < σ ≤ 1 , h ∈ ℝ 1/2<{\sigma}\leq 1,h\in\mathbb{R} , and appears in a number of recent works on the asymptotic behavior of the moments and maxima of the ζ {\zeta} -function on short intervals along the critical axis σ = 1 / 2 {\sigma}=1/2 . Using probability and basic analytic number theory, we show that the complex integral means spectrum of the primitive of ζ rand {\zeta}_{\mathrm{rand}} is almost surely of the form conjectured 30 years ago by Kraetzer, for the so-called universal integral means spectrum of univalent functions in the disc. The Riemann ζ {\zeta} -function and its random version have recently been rigorously related to the so-called Gaussian multiplicative chaos (GMC), initiated by Kahane 40 years ago. In the case of the holomorphic multiplicative chaos on the unit disc – an important stochastic object closely related to Liouville quantum gravity on the unit circle – we prove that the integral means spectrum of the primitive is almost surely also of the same Kraetzer form. However, we establish that neither the primitive of the random function ζ rand {\zeta}_{\mathrm{rand}} , nor that of the holomorphic GMC are injective. Building on earlier work by one of the authors and Webb on the convergence of Riemann ζ {\zeta} -function on the critical line to a holomorphic GMC distribution, we finally provide an alternative derivation of the integral means spectrum for the random Riemann ζ {\zeta} -function.

1.1.
Integral means spectrum

Twenty years ago, Peter W. Jones published a nice essay in honor of Lennart Carleson’s 75th birthday, entitled “

h:{\mathbb{D}}\to\Omega

(1.1)

\int_{r\partial{\mathbb{D}}}|h^{\prime}(z)^{\beta}||dz|\asymp\left(1-r\right)^{-b_{h}({\beta})},\quad r\to 1^{-},

(1.2)

in the sense of the equivalence of the logarithms.

When the conformal mapping

\displaystyle b({\beta},\kappa)-{\beta}+1

\displaystyle 1+2\tau-2\sqrt{b\tau},\quad\tau=d(\kappa)-{\beta},

\displaystyle d(\kappa)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(4+\kappa)^{2}}{8\kappa},\quad\quad\,\,\,\quad{\beta}\in[{\beta}_{1},{\beta}_{2}],

(1.4)

The so-called universal spectra are obtained when one considers the supremum of all conformal maps over bounded (vs. unbounded), simply connected planar domains. In particular, the so-called

For real parameter

\mathrm{B}_{\bullet}({\beta})=\max\{\mathrm{B}({\beta}),3{\beta}-1\},\quad{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}.

(1.5)

A number of exact results and outstanding conjectures are known for the universal integral means spectra.

1.3.
Kraetzer Conjecture

All known conjectures are encompassed by the well-known

\mathrm{B}_{\mathcal{K}}({\beta})=\frac{{\beta}^{2}}{4},\quad{\beta}\in[-2,2],

\mathrm{B}_{\mathcal{K}}({\beta})=|{\beta}|-1,\quad|{\beta}|\geq 2,

(1.7)

a classical result for the universal spectrum in the case

For complex values of

{\mathrm{B}}_{{\mathcal{K}}}({\beta})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{4}|{\beta}|^{2},\,\,\,t\in\mathbb{C},\,\,\,|{\beta}|\leq 2,&\\
|{\beta}|-1,\quad\quad\quad|{\beta}|\geq 2.&\end{cases}

(1.8)

As shown in Section

“It was understood in the 1990’s that dynamical systems and Conformal Field Theory were intimately tied to these problems, at least in dimension two. It is a curious state of affairs that CFT as it is understood today, makes no notable predictions for these problems. This is perhaps even more surprising in light of the great success CFT has had with SLE. Exactly how the full story will unfold remains mysterious. My guess is that we are missing fundamental concepts, and that the future will bring some unsuspected, closer connections between analysis and statistical physics.”

Since that time, it does not seem that much more knowledge was gained. However, we mention here the interesting work by Hedenmalm

Notations and preliminary observations

We denote by

The series defining

We shall make use of Jensen’s lemma from the classical theory of Dirichlet series (see

converges absolutely and uniformly in any half-plane

\sigma_{0}>1/4.

∎

{\sigma}>1/2
, the process we are interested in is defined by

\left(X^{{\sigma}}_{h},h\in[0,1]\right)\quad\text{where}\quad X^{{\sigma}}_{h}=\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{\Re\left(U_{p}p^{-ih}\right)}{p^{{\sigma}}}.

(2.1)

The proof of our main result draws on the work of
[
7
]
and
[
14
]
, where a random model of the zeta function
[
16
,
50
,
44
,
46
]
is investigated.
A key idea behind the strategy of the proof consists in introducing a so-called
multiscale decomposition
of the process, namely, we rewrite
X^{{\sigma}}_{h}
as

X^{{\sigma}}_{h}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}Y^{{\sigma}}_{h}(k)\quad\text{where}\quad Y^{{\sigma}}_{h}(k)=\sum_{2^{k-1}<\log p\leq 2^{k}}W^{{\sigma}}_{h}(p),\quad k\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(2.2)

where we write

W^{{\sigma}}_{h}(p):=\frac{\Re\left(U_{p}p^{-ih}\right)}{p^{{\sigma}}},\quad h\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(2.3)

The law of the process
(W^{{\sigma}}_{h}(p),h\in{\mathbb{R}})
is both translation invariant and invariant under the reflexion
h\mapsto-h
. Using these properties, it easily follows from (
2.3
) and the law of the random variables
U_{p}
that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[W^{{\sigma}}_{h}(p)W^{{\sigma}}_{h^{\prime}}(p)\right]=\frac{1}{2p^{2{\sigma}}}\cos(|h-h^{\prime}|\log p)\quad\text{for all}\,\,h,h^{\prime}.

(2.4)

In the notation (
2.3
), the increments
Y^{{\sigma}}_{h}(k)
defined in (
2.2
) read

Y^{{\sigma}}_{h}(k)=\sum_{2^{k-1}<\log p\leq 2^{k}}W^{{\sigma}}_{h}(p),\quad h\in{\mathbb{R}},

(2.5)

Thus, by (
2.4
) and the independence of the variables
U_{p}
,
Y^{{\sigma}}_{h}(k)
has variance

\varsigma_{k}^{2}=\hbox{\rm Var}(Y^{{\sigma}}_{h}(k))={\mathbb{E}}\left[Y^{{\sigma}}_{h}(k)^{2}\right]=\sum_{2^{k-1}<\log p\leq 2^{k}}\frac{1}{2p^{2{\sigma}}},

(2.6)

whereas the covariance of
Y^{{\sigma}}_{h}(k)
and
Y^{{\sigma}}_{h^{\prime}}(k)
is

\rho_{k}(h,h^{\prime})={\mathbb{E}}\left[Y^{{\sigma}}_{h}(k)Y^{{\sigma}}_{h^{\prime}}(k)\right]=\sum_{2^{k-1}<\log p\leq 2^{k}}\frac{1}{2p^{2{\sigma}}}\cos(|h-h^{\prime}|\log p).

(2.7)

A useful collective random variable will be

X^{{\sigma}}_{h}(k_{1},k_{2})=\sum_{k=k_{1}+1}^{k_{2}}Y^{{\sigma}}_{h}(k),\quad h\in{\mathbb{R}},

(2.8)

whose variance reads

\varsigma_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{2}=\hbox{\rm Var}(X^{{\sigma}}_{h}(k_{1},k_{2}))=\sum_{k=k_{1}+1}^{k_{2}}\hbox{\rm Var}(Y^{{\sigma}}_{h}(k))=\sum_{k=k_{1}+1}^{k_{2}}\varsigma_{k}^{2}=\sum_{2^{k_{1}}<\log p\leq 2^{k_{2}}}\frac{1}{2p^{2{\sigma}}},

(2.9)

where, with a slight redundancy of notation,
\varsigma_{k-1,k}\equiv\varsigma_{k}
. Of course, all moments of the process
Y^{{\sigma}}_{h}(k)
depend on
{\sigma}
. To avoid overloading the notation, we keep this dependence implicit. This should not cause any confusion.

2.3.

The properties of the fine dyadic decomposition

of the process
X^{{\sigma}}_{h}
obtained in Section

are now used to obtain estimates on the law of

on mesoscopic scales. More precisely, given

and consider

To formulate our results, we use the following notations and definitions.

into intervals, and treating the contribution of each interval to the integral in (
3.1
) separately. This requires precise estimates of the number of points of

that lie in a given interval.
Kistler’s multiscale second moment method is then used to deal with the correlations of the process
[
64
]
.
These estimates are collected in Section
3.1
.
The proof of Theorem
3.1
is finally given in Section
3.2
.

3.1.
Preparatory tools

The first result of this section is an
a priori
almost sure bound on the maximum over

of the sequence of processes

obtained by taking
{\sigma}={\sigma}_{n}
in (

Given
\varepsilon^{\star}>0
and
{\sigma}>1/2
, define the sequence of events

{\Omega}^{\star}_{n}(\varepsilon^{\star})=\bigcap_{{\sigma}\in[{\sigma}_{n},{\sigma}_{n-1})}\left\{\max_{h\in[0,1]}X^{{\sigma}}_{h}<(1+\varepsilon^{\star})n\log 2\right\},\quad n\geq 1,

and set

and

{\Omega}^{\star}=\bigcap_{\varepsilon^{\star}>0}{\Omega}^{\star}(\varepsilon^{\star}).

(3.5)

Lemma 3.2
.

{\mathbb{P}}\left({\Omega}^{\star}\right)=1
.

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

We start by proving that for all sufficiently large
n
,

{\mathbb{P}}\left([{\Omega}^{\star}_{n}(\varepsilon^{\star})]^{c}\right)\leq C2^{-2n\varepsilon^{\star}}

(3.6)

for some constant
C>0
where,
[{\Omega}^{\star}_{n}(\varepsilon^{\star})]^{c}
denotes the complement of
{\Omega}^{\star}_{n}(\varepsilon^{\star})
in
{\Omega}
, namely,

[{\Omega}^{\star}_{n}(\varepsilon^{\star})]^{c}=\left\{\max_{h\in[0,1]}\max_{{\sigma}\in[{\sigma}_{n},{\sigma}_{n-1})}X^{{\sigma}}_{h}\geq(1+\varepsilon^{\star})n\log 2\right\}.

(3.7)

The claim (
3.6
) will then follow from Lemma
2.9
with
k_{1}=0
,
k_{2}=\infty
,
{\gamma}=1+\varepsilon^{\star}
,
and a suitable covering of the interval
[0,1]
. Specifically, we take
[0,1]=\cup_{1\leq\ell\leq 2^{n}}I_{\ell}
, where
I_{\ell}=[(\ell-1)2^{-n},\ell 2^{-n}]
for all

. Then,

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}\left([{\Omega}^{\star}_{n}(\varepsilon^{\star})]^{c}\right)&\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{2^{n}}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\max_{h\in I_{\ell}}\max_{{\sigma}\in[{\sigma}_{n},{\sigma}_{n-1})}X^{{\sigma}}_{h}\geq(1+\varepsilon^{\star})n\log 2\right)\\
&\leq C2^{-n[(1+\varepsilon^{\star})^{2}-1]},\end{split}

(3.8)

where the first inequality is Boole’s inequality and the second is (
2.57
) with

{\gamma}=1+\varepsilon^{\star}
,

{\alpha}_{1}=0
and
\bar{\alpha}_{2}=1
. This gives (
3.6
). Since

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\left({\Omega}^{c}_{n}(\varepsilon^{\star})\right)<\infty

for all
\varepsilon^{\star}>0
, the Borel-Cantelli lemma ensures that

{\mathbb{P}}\left({\Omega}^{\star}(\varepsilon^{\star})\right)=1.

(3.9)

The claim of Lemma

then follows in a classical way from the observation that

is a decreasing family as
\varepsilon^{\star}\downarrow 0
.
∎

For
{\gamma}\geq 0
and
{\sigma}>1/2

-high points

{\mathcal{E}}^{{\sigma}}_{n}({\gamma})=\frac{\log\left({\mathcal{M}}^{{\sigma}}_{n}({\gamma})\right)}{n\log 2}.

(3.11)

We also set

{\mathcal{E}}({\gamma})=-{\gamma}^{2}.

(3.12)

Given
\varepsilon>0
, we next define the sequences of events, for

,

\begin{split}{\Omega}^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon,{\gamma})&=\bigcap_{{\sigma}\in[{\sigma}_{n},{\sigma}_{n-1})}\left\{{\mathcal{E}}^{{\sigma}}_{n}({\gamma})-{\mathcal{E}}({\gamma})\leq+\varepsilon\right\},\\

(3.13)

and we set

{\Omega}^{\pm}(\varepsilon,{\gamma})=\bigcup_{n_{0}\geq 1}\bigcap_{n\geq n_{0}}{\Omega}^{\pm}_{n}(\varepsilon,{\gamma}),

(3.14)

and

{\Omega}^{\pm}({\gamma})=\bigcap_{\varepsilon>0}{\Omega}^{\pm}(\varepsilon,{\gamma}).

(3.15)

The next proposition is the central result of this section and the lemma that follows is the key ingredient of its proof.

Proposition 3.3
.

(i) For all
{\gamma}>0

{\mathbb{P}}\left({\Omega}^{+}({\gamma})\right)=1.

(3.16)

(ii) For all
0<{\gamma}<1

{\mathbb{P}}\left({\Omega}^{-}({\gamma})\right)=1.

(3.17)

Lemma 3.4
.

The following holds for all
n
sufficiently large.

(i) For all
{\gamma}>0
and all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
0<C<\infty
such that

{\mathbb{P}}\left([{\Omega}^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon,{\gamma})]^{c}\right)\leq C2^{-n\varepsilon}.

(ii) For all
0<{\gamma}<1
and all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant

and a constant

and

(iii) There exist constants

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\max_{h\in[0,1]}\max_{{\sigma}\in[{\sigma}_{n},{\sigma}_{n-1})}\left|X^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h}-X^{{\sigma}}_{h}\right|\geq\eta n\right)\leq C_{1}(\eta)e^{-C_{2}(\eta)n}.

We begin by proving Proposition
3.3

3.4
.

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

On the one hand, by part (i) of Lemma
3.4
, for all
\varepsilon>0
and all
{\gamma}>0
,

\textstyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left[{\Omega}^{+}_{n}(\varepsilon,{\gamma})\right]^{c}\right)<\infty.

Thus, the Borel-Cantelli lemma ensures that

{\mathbb{P}}\left({\Omega}^{+}(\varepsilon,{\gamma})\right)=1.

(3.22)

On the other hand, by Borel-Cantelli lemma and part (ii) of Lemma
3.4
, for all
\varepsilon>0
and all
0<{\gamma}<1
, with
{\mathbb{P}}
-probability one, there exists a random
n_{0}<\infty
, such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
, we have

{\mathcal{E}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{n}({\gamma})\geq{\mathcal{E}}({\gamma})-\varepsilon.

Hence, by part (iii) of Lemma

with
\eta=\varepsilon
, for all
{\sigma}\in[{\sigma}_{n},{\sigma}_{n-1})

{\mathcal{E}}^{{\sigma}}_{n}({\gamma})\geq{\mathcal{E}}({\gamma})-\varepsilon-\frac{{\gamma}\varepsilon}{\log 2}.

Thus, for all
\varepsilon>0

{\mathbb{P}}\left({\Omega}^{-}_{n}\left(\varepsilon\bigl[1+{\textstyle{{\gamma}\over\log 2}}\bigr],{\gamma}\right)\right)=1.

(3.25)

The claim of Proposition
3.3
now follows from the observation that given
{\gamma}
,
both
{\Omega}^{+}(\varepsilon,{\gamma})
and
{\Omega}^{-}(\varepsilon,{\gamma})
are decreasing families of sets as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

Let
n\geq 0
be given.
We first prove item (i), namely that for all
{\gamma}>0
and all
\varepsilon>0

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\max_{{\sigma}\in[{\sigma}_{n},{\sigma}_{n-1})}{\mathcal{M}}^{{\sigma}}_{n}({\gamma})>2^{n[{\mathcal{E}}({\gamma})+\varepsilon]}\right)\leq C2^{-n\varepsilon},

(3.26)

for some consrant
C>0
. To do this, note first that for all
h\in[0,1]
and
{\sigma}\in[{\sigma}_{n},{\sigma}_{n-1})
,

\left\{X^{{\sigma}}_{h}\geq{\gamma}n\log 2\right\}\subseteq\left\{\max_{{\sigma}\in[{\sigma}_{n},{\sigma}_{n-1})}X^{{\sigma}}_{h}\geq{\gamma}n\log 2\right\}.

(3.27)

By this and (
3.10
),

\begin{split}\max_{{\sigma}\in[{\sigma}_{n},{\sigma}_{n-1})}{\mathcal{M}}^{{\sigma}}_{n}({\gamma})\leq{\rm{Leb}}\left\{h\in[0,1]:\max_{{\sigma}\in[{\sigma}_{n},{\sigma}_{n-1})}X^{{\sigma}}_{h}\geq{\gamma}n\log 2\right\}.\end{split}

(3.28)

Therefore,

\begin{split}&{\mathbb{P}}\left(\max_{{\sigma}\in[{\sigma}_{n},{\sigma}_{n-1})}{\mathcal{M}}^{{\sigma}}_{n}({\gamma})>2^{n[{\mathcal{E}}({\gamma})+\varepsilon]}\right)\\
\leq&{\mathbb{P}}\left({\rm{Leb}}\left\{h\in[0,1]:\max_{{\sigma}\in[{\sigma}_{n},{\sigma}_{n-1})}X^{{\sigma}}_{h}\geq{\gamma}n\log 2\right\}>2^{n[{\mathcal{E}}({\gamma})+\varepsilon]}\right).\end{split}

(3.29)

Using in turn Markov’s inequality and Fubini’s theorem, the second line of (
3.29
) is at most

\begin{split}&\leq 2^{n(-{\mathcal{E}}({\gamma})-\varepsilon)}\int_{0}^{1}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\max_{{\sigma}\in[{\sigma}_{n},{\sigma}_{n-1})}X^{{\sigma}}_{h}\geq{\gamma}n\log 2\right)dh\\
&=2^{n(-{\mathcal{E}}({\gamma})-\varepsilon)}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\max_{{\sigma}\in[{\sigma}_{n},{\sigma}_{n-1})}X^{{\sigma}}_{h}\geq{\gamma}n\log 2\right)\\
&\leq C^{\prime}2^{-n\varepsilon},\end{split}

(3.30)

where the last line follows from (
2.58
) of Lemma
2.9
, together with (
3.12
), while in the second line we used that the distribution of
X^{{\sigma}}_{h}
does not depend on
h
,
as seen from the proof of Lemma
2.12
(i).
The claim of (
3.18
) now follows.

Turning to item (ii), let us establish that under the more restrictive condition that
0<{\gamma}<1
, for all
\varepsilon>0

{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{M}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{n}({\gamma})<2^{n[{\mathcal{E}}({\gamma})-\varepsilon]}\right)\leq C2^{-nc({\gamma},\varepsilon)},

(3.31)

where
C
and
c({\gamma},\varepsilon)
are as in (
3.19
).
The proof relies on the Paley-Zygmund inequality, which states that if
Z\geq 0
is a random variable with finite variance,
and if
0\leq\eta\leq 1
, then

{\mathbb{P}}(Z>\eta{\mathbb{E}}Z)\geq(1-\eta)^{2}{\frac{({\mathbb{E}}Z)^{2}}{{\mathbb{E}}(Z^{2})}}.

(3.32)

Due to the correlation structure of the process, we will not apply (
3.32
) directly to the quantity

{\mathcal{M}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{n}({\gamma})
. Instead, we will follow the strategy developed in
[
64
]
,
i.e.
, we introduce a coarse-grained version of the process that allows us to gain enough independence to make efficient use of (
3.32
).

Given an integer
K
and a real number
{\delta}>0
, both of which will eventually depend on
\varepsilon
, define the sequence of
K

{\mathcal{J}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h}(m)=\begin{cases}\left\{X^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h}\left(\left\lceil{\textstyle{m-1\over K}}n\right\rceil,\left\lceil{\textstyle{m\over K}}n\right\rceil\right)\geq{\textstyle{(1+{\delta})\over K}}{\gamma}n\log 2\right\}&\text{ if $m=1,\dots,K-1$},\\
\left\{X^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h}\left(\left\lceil{\textstyle{m-1\over K}}n\right\rceil,\infty\right)\geq{\textstyle{(1+{\delta})\over K}}{\gamma}n\log 2\right\}&\text{ if $m=K$}.\end{cases}

(3.33)

Using these events, we define the (random) set of all
h\in[0,1]
for which all the events (
3.33
) occur,

{\mathcal{A}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}({\gamma})=\left\{h\in[0,1]:\prod_{2\leq m\leq K}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{{\mathcal{J}}^{{\sigma}}_{h}({\gamma},m)\right\}}=1\right\}.

(3.34)

Note that for all
h\in{\mathcal{A}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}

X^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h}-X^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h}\left(0,\left\lceil{\textstyle{1\over K}}n\right\rceil\right)\geq(1+{\delta})(1-{\textstyle{1\over K}}){\gamma}n\log 2.

(3.35)

Therefore, setting

{\delta}_{K}={\delta}(1-{\textstyle{1\over K}})-{\textstyle{1\over K}}

with
K>1+1/{\delta}
so that
{\delta}_{K}>0
, and defining the events

\begin{split}{\mathcal{B}}^{{\sigma}}_{{\delta}_{K}}=&\left\{h\in[0,1]:X^{{\sigma}}_{h}\left(0,\left\lceil{\textstyle{1\over K}}n\right\rceil\right)\leq-{\delta}_{K}{\gamma}n\log 2\right\},\\
{\mathcal{C}}=&\left\{h\in[0,1]:X^{{\sigma}}_{h}\geq{\gamma}n\log 2\right\},\end{split}

(3.36)

we have

{\mathcal{A}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{{\delta}_{K}}\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}
,
and so, observing that
{\rm{Leb}}({\mathcal{C}})={\mathcal{M}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{n}({\gamma})
, as follows from (
3.10
),

{\mathcal{M}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{n}({\gamma})\geq{\rm{Leb}}({\mathcal{A}}^{{\sigma}_{n}})-{\rm{Leb}}({\mathcal{B}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{{\delta}_{K}}).

(3.37)

To deal with
{\rm{Leb}}({\mathcal{B}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{{\delta}_{K}})
, note that by Fubini’s theorem and part (ii) of Lemma
2.9
together with the symmetry of

X^{{\sigma}}_{h}\left(0,\left\lceil{\textstyle{1\over K}}n\right\rceil\right)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm{Leb}}({\mathcal{B}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{{\delta}_{K}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C2^{-nK({\delta}_{K}{\gamma})^{2}},

(3.38)

while by Markov’s inequality

{\mathbb{P}}\left({\rm{Leb}}({\mathcal{B}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{{\delta}_{K}})<2^{n[{\mathcal{E}}({\gamma})-\varepsilon]}\right)\geq 1-C2^{-n[{\gamma}^{2}(K{\delta}^{2}_{K}-1)-\varepsilon]}.

(3.39)

Since
{\delta}_{K}\rightarrow{\delta}
as
K\rightarrow\infty
, this probability approaches
1
exponentially fast as
n\rightarrow\infty
for all
{\gamma},{\delta}>0
provided that
K
is sufficiently large.

Therefore, to prove (
3.31
), it remains to establish a lower bound on

{\mathbb{P}}\left(Z>2\,2^{n[{\mathcal{E}}({\gamma})-\varepsilon]}\right)

(3.40)

where

Z={\rm{Leb}}({\mathcal{A}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}),

(3.41)

or, equivalently, setting

\eta_{n}=2\,2^{n[{\mathcal{E}}({\gamma})-\varepsilon]}/{\mathbb{E}}\left[Z\right],

(3.42)

a lower bound on

{\mathbb{P}}\left(Z>\eta_{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[Z\right]\right).

(3.43)

This is where the Paley-Zygmund inequality (
3.32
) comes into play.

Let us first prove that with the choice made in (
3.42
),
\eta_{n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. We thus need a lower bound on
{\mathbb{E}}\left[Z\right]
.
Again, by Fubini’s theorem, independence, and the fact that the distribution of
X^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h}(k_{1},k_{2})
does not depend on
h
,

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm{Leb}}({\mathcal{A}}^{{\sigma}_{n}})\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{m=2}^{K}{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathcal{J}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h}(m)\right]dh=\prod_{m=2}^{K}{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathcal{J}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h}(m)\right].

(3.44)

By the lower bound of part (i) of Lemma
2.8
, for all
2\leq m\leq K

{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathcal{J}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h}(m)\right]\geq{\textstyle{C_{1}\over\sqrt{n}}}2^{-\frac{n{\gamma}^{2}(1+{\delta})^{2}}{K}}

(3.45)

for some constant
C_{1}>0
that depends only on
K
,
{\gamma}
and
a
. Plugging (
3.45
) in (
3.44
)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm{Leb}}({\mathcal{A}}^{{\sigma}_{n}})\right]\geq\bigl({\textstyle{C_{1}\over\sqrt{n}}}\bigr)^{K-1}2^{-n{\gamma}^{2}(1+{\delta})^{2}(1-\frac{1}{K})}.

(3.46)

Thus, if

{\gamma}^{2}(1+{\delta})^{2}(1-\frac{1}{K})\leq{\gamma}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
, then

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm{Leb}}({\mathcal{A}}^{{\sigma}_{n}})\right]\geq\bigl({\textstyle{C_{1}\over\sqrt{n}}}\bigr)^{K-1}2^{n[{\mathcal{E}}({\gamma})-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}]}.

(3.47)

Substituting (
3.47
) in (
3.42
) implies that

\eta_{n}\leq 2\,2^{-n\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}{\mathcal{O}}({n}^{(K-1)/2})\rightarrow 0\,\,\,\text{as}\,\,\,n\rightarrow\infty,

(3.48)

as desired.

We next establish an upper bound on
{\mathbb{E}}\left[Z^{2}\right]
.
Once again, by Fubini’s theorem and independence

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm{Leb}}({\mathcal{A}}^{{\sigma}_{n}})^{2}\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{m=2}^{K}{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathcal{J}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h}(m)\cap{\mathcal{J}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h^{\prime}}(m)\right]dhdh^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{4}{\mathcal{I}}_{i}

(3.49)

where we decomposed the integration domain into four domains, namely, for

,
{\mathcal{I}}_{i}
are the integrals over the domains

\begin{split}{\mathcal{D}}_{1}&=\left\{(h,h^{\prime}):2^{-n\frac{1}{2K}}<|h-h^{\prime}|\leq 1\right\},\\

To estimate

, note that for each for

applies with an exponentially small error term

{\mathcal{O}}\left(n2^{-n/2K}\right)
.

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1+(K-1){\mathcal{O}}\left(n2^{-\frac{n}{2K}}\right)\right)\prod_{m=2}^{K}\left[{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{J}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h}(m)\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1+(K-1){\mathcal{O}}\left(n2^{-\frac{n}{2K}}\right)\right)\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm{Leb}}({\mathcal{A}}^{{\sigma}_{n}})\right]\right)^{2},

where the last equality is the identity

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm{Leb}}({\mathcal{A}}^{{\sigma}_{n}})\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{m=2}^{K}{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathcal{J}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h}(m)\right]dh=\prod_{m=2}^{K}{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\mathcal{J}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h}(m)\right],

Turning to

{\mathcal{I}}_{2}\leq(1+{\mathcal{O}}(1))2^{-n\frac{1}{2K}}\prod_{m=2}^{K}\left[{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{J}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h}(m)\right)\right]^{2}=(1+{\mathcal{O}}(1))2^{-n\frac{1}{2K}}\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm{Leb}}({\mathcal{A}}^{{\sigma}_{n}})\right]\right)^{2}.

(3.53)

To estimate
{\mathcal{I}}_{4}
, we use instead that item (iii) of Lemma
2.8
applies for each
2\leq m\leq K
, so that

\begin{split}{\mathcal{I}}_{4}&\leq 2^{-n}\prod_{m=2}^{K}{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{J}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h}(m)\right)=2^{-n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm{Leb}}({\mathcal{A}}^{{\sigma}_{n}})\right]\\
&\leq\bigl({\textstyle{\sqrt{n}\over C_{1}}}\bigr)^{K-1}2^{-n[1-{\gamma}^{2}(1+{\delta})^{2}(1-\frac{1}{K})]}\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm{Leb}}({\mathcal{A}}^{{\sigma}_{n}})\right]\right)^{2},\end{split}

(3.54)

where the first equality is again (

),

(
3.46

It remains to deal with the third case. Here

{\mathcal{I}}_{3}=\sum_{2\leq r\leq K}{\mathcal{I}}_{3,r}
,

For each fixed

for

.

{\mathcal{I}}_{3,r}\leq(1+{\mathcal{O}}(1))2^{-n\frac{(r-1)}{K}}\bigl(1-2^{-n\frac{1}{K}}\bigr)\prod_{m=2}^{r}{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{J}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h}(m)\right)\prod_{m=r+1}^{K}\left[{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{J}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h}(m)\right)\right]^{2}.

(3.55)

Summing over

3.52
) to reconstruct
\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm{Leb}}({\mathcal{A}}^{{\sigma}_{n}})\right]\right)^{2}

{\mathcal{I}}_{3}\leq(1+{\mathcal{O}}(1))\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm{Leb}}({\mathcal{A}}^{{\sigma}_{n}})\right]\right)^{2}\sum_{2\leq r\leq K}2^{-n\frac{(r-1)}{K}}\prod_{m=2}^{r}\left[{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{J}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h}(m)\right)\right]^{-1}.

\begin{split}\sum_{2\leq r\leq K}2^{-n\frac{(r-1)}{K}}\prod_{m=2}^{r}\left[{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{J}}^{{\sigma}_{n}}_{h}(m)\right)\right]^{-1}&\leq\sum_{2\leq r\leq K}\left({\textstyle{\sqrt{n}\over C_{1}}}\right)^{r-1}2^{-n\frac{(r-1)}{K}[1-{\gamma}^{2}(1+{\delta})^{2}]}\\

where the last inequality holds provided that

, for all

Here and below,

denotes a constant that may depend on the parameters

and

, and whose value may vary from line to line.
Substituting (

{\mathcal{I}}_{3}\leq C\sqrt{n}2^{-n\left[\frac{1}{K}\left(1-{\gamma}^{2}(1+{\delta})^{2}\right)\right]}\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm{Leb}}({\mathcal{A}}^{{\sigma}_{n}})\right]\right)^{2}.

Collecting the bounds (

4.
Noninjectivity of the primitive: proof of Propositions
1.3
and
1.4

Proof of Proposition
1.3
.

A version of Koebe’s distortion theorems (Bieberbach’s inequality, see,
e.g.
,
[
74
, Lemma 1.3, p. 21]
) provides a necessary condition for univalence by noting that for a univalent function on the unit disc
{\mathbb{D}}
the quantity
(1-|z|^{2})|f^{\prime\prime}(z)|/|f^{\prime}(z)|
remains bounded on
{\mathbb{D}}
. A somewhat more detailed version of this is sometimes called the Becker-Pommerenke injectivity criterion. One may note here that this quantity remains invariant in the standard normalization
f\mapsto(f-f(0))/f^{\prime}(0)
which is used in stating the distortion theorems for univalent functions on the unit disc. If
U\subset{\mathbb{C}}
is a simply connected domain and
f:U\to{\mathbb{C}}
is univalent, one may consider the map
f\circ h
, where
h:{\mathbb{D}}\to U
is a conformal map. By applying the criterion on
f\circ h
and returning to original coordinates we see that

\frac{(1-|h^{-1}(z)|^{2})}{(h^{-1})^{\prime}(z)}\Big(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}+((h^{-1})^{\prime}(z))^{2}h^{\prime\prime}\circ h^{-1}(z)\Big)

stays bounded in
U
. Especially, if
\alpha\in\partial U
and
\partial U
is smooth in a neighbourhood of
\alpha
, by localizing the classical Kellog-Warchawski theorems (see
[
74
, Theorems 10.2 and 10.3, p. 298-301]
) on the smoothness of
h
we obtain that

\sup_{z\to\alpha}d(z,\partial U)\frac{|f^{\prime\prime}(z)|}{|f^{\prime}(z)|}<\infty.

In order to prove our claim it thus suffices to show that there exists a decreasing sequence of positive reals
(\sigma_{k})
such that
\sigma_{k}\searrow 1/2
and

\sup_{k\geq 1}(\sigma_{k}-1/2)|F^{\prime\prime}(\sigma_{k})/F^{\prime}(\sigma_{k})|=\infty\qquad\textrm{almost surely}.

According to Lemma
2.1
we may write for
\sigma>1/2
the decomposition

\log(F^{\prime}(s))=\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}U_{p}p^{-ih-\sigma}+B(s)
,
where
B(s)
extends almost surely analytically to a domain containing
\{\sigma\geq 1/2\}
, and especially
B^{\prime}(s)
is almost surely bounded in
\{s={\sigma}+ih\,:\,1/2\leq\sigma\leq 1,\;-1\leq h\leq 1\}
. It follows that it is enough to construct a decreasing sequence of positive reals
\sigma_{k}\searrow 1/2
such that

\sup_{k\geq 1}(\sigma_{k}-1/2)\left|\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}U_{p}\log p\,p^{-\sigma_{k}}\right|=\infty\qquad\textrm{almost surely}.

(4.1)

For that purpose denote

Y_{k}:=(\sigma_{k}-1/2)\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}U_{p}\log p\,p^{-\sigma_{k}},

and notice that
Y_{k}
satisfies the asymptotics

{\mathbb{E}}|Y_{k}|^{2}=(\sigma_{k}-1/2)^{2}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}(\log p)^{2}\,p^{-2\sigma_{k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\sigma}_{k}\to 1/2}}{{\sim}}1/4,

where one uses for instance the identity giving
I_{m}
for
m=2
in the proof of Lemma
2.5
.
Our goal is to prove that almost surely
\sup_{k}|Y_{k}|=\infty
.

We now use induction to pick up a strictly increasing sequence of indices
(n_{k})_{k\geq 0}
with
n_{0}=0
, and a decreasing sequence of
\sigma_{k}
’s (say with
\sigma_{1}=1
and
\sigma_{k}\leq 1/2+1/k
) in such a way that for all
k\geq 1
we have

(\sigma_{k}-1/2)^{2}\sum_{p<n_{k-1}\;\textrm{or}\;p\geq n_{k}}(\log p)^{2}p^{-2\sigma_{k}}\leq 2^{-k}.

(4.2)

The starting of the induction is obvious as one may just pick
n_{1}
large enough. Assume then that
\sigma_{k},n_{k}
are already chosen. We pick first
\sigma_{k+1}\leq\min\{\sigma_{k},1/2+1/(k+1)\}
small enough so that
(\sigma_{k+1}-1/2)^{2}\sum_{p\leq n_{k}}(\log p)^{2}p^{-1}\leq 2^{-k-2}
, and thereafter choose

n_{k+1}
so large that
(\sigma_{k+1}-1/2)^{2}\sum_{p\geq n_{k+1}}(\log p)^{2}p^{-2\sigma_{k+1}}\leq 2^{-k-2}
.

Consider the random variables

\widetilde{Y}_{k}:=(\sigma_{k}-1/2)\sum_{n_{k-1}\leq p<n_{k}}U_{p}\log p\,p^{-\sigma_{k}},

According to (
4.2
) we have
{\mathbb{E}}|\widetilde{Y}_{k}-Y_{k}|^{2}\leq 2^{-k}
, so that
{\mathbb{E}}\big(\sum_{k\geq 1}|\widetilde{Y}_{k}-Y_{k}|^{2}\big)<\infty.
This implies that
\lim_{k\to\infty}(\widetilde{Y}_{k}-Y_{k})=0
almost surely, and hence it is enough to show that
\sup_{k}|\widetilde{Y}_{k}|=\infty
. However, by construction, the variables
\widetilde{Y}_{k}
are independent and centered, and their variance is bounded from below and above. The desired statement follows as soon as we verify that the limiting distribution of (normalized)
\widetilde{Y}_{k}
is Gaussian. But this follows immediately from Lindeberg’s central limit theorem (see
[
62
, Theorem 6.12]
) as each
Y_{k}
is a linear combination of i.i.d. variables, and the maximal coefficient in the linear combination tends to zero as
k\to\infty.

∎

Proof of Proposition
1.4
.

The proof is exactly the same as the above proof of Proposition
1.3
, the only difference being that there is no need to use the central limit theorem since our variables are already Gaussian.
∎

5.
Approach via Gaussian multiplicative chaos

In this section we first recall the definition of Gaussian multiplicative chaos (GMC) measures, and then use some basic properties of these measures (and their approximations) to prove an analogue of our main result for a random analytic function in the unit disc that uses no number theoretic elements in the definition. Finally, we outline how GMC-theory can be used to provide another proof of our main result via a coupling result from
[
80
]
.

5.1.
GMC measures

The theory of Gaussian multiplicative chaos measures was pioneered by Kahane in 1985. In the last two decades the interest to GMC-measures has been steadily increasing. During this period the natural role of GMC was first realized in connection with Liouville quantum gravity and SLE. Soon after it appeared also in connection with random matrices, statistics of the Riemann zeta function, and estimates for random and Dirichlet character sums
[
33
,
83
,
85
,
80
,
45
]
.
3
3

3

We list only some of the earliest references herein, a general reference for multiplicative chaos is
[
78
]
.

Formally GMC is constructed on (say) the interval
[0,1]
as an exponential
``\exp(X(x))"
, where
X
is
a log-correlated (and centered) Gaussian field on the interval
x\in[0,1]
, with the formal covariance structure,
4
4

4

Note that the factor
1/2
is not usually included in front of the logarithm, but we add it here to ensure that no extra scaling is required in the random zeta function model.

{\mathbb{E}}X(x)X(y)\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{|x-y|}+g(x,y),

where
g
is continuous. The field actually takes values in generalized functions, and one usually addresses this difficulty by considering suitable approximations. A typical example is a standard mollification
X_{\varepsilon}
obtained by the convolution of
X
and
\varepsilon^{-1}\psi(\cdot/\varepsilon)
, where
\psi
is a compactly supported bump function that integrates to 1.Given a constant
\beta>0
, we may then define the measure

\mu_{\beta,\varepsilon}(dx):=\exp\big(\beta X_{\varepsilon}(x)-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}{\mathbb{E}}X_{\varepsilon}(x)^{2}\big)dx

(5.1)

on
(0,1)
. From the basic multiplicative chaos theory one knows that there is the convergence as
\varepsilon\to 0
,

\mu_{\beta,\varepsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu_{\beta},

where
\mu_{\beta}
is the GMC measure related to the field
X
. The case
\beta\in(0,\sqrt{2})
is an easy
L^{2}
-computation
[
55
]
; in the more difficult general case, see,
e.g.
,
[
33
,
20
]
for a simple approach. For other treatments of this convergence result we refer to
[
61
,
79
,
33
]
. The limit
\mu_{\beta}
is a non-trivial random measure in case
\beta\in(0,2)
. It is supported on the set of
\beta
-thick points of
(0,1)
,
i.e.,
on the set

\big\{x\in(0,1)\;|\;\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{X_{\varepsilon}(x)}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}=\beta/2\big\}.

(5.2)

One may note that the renormalization factor
\exp\big(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}{\mathbb{E}}X_{\varepsilon}(x)^{2}\big)
in (
5.1
) comes simply from the requirement
that the density has expectation 1 at every point
x
. In the approach we describe in this section, it is exactly this factor that is the origin of the quadratic term
\beta^{2}/4
which appears in the results we are after, see,
e.g.
, (
1.14
).

When
\beta=2
(the critical case) or
\beta>2
(the supercritical case), the situation is different, and the definition of the chaos requires a non-trivial further renormalization. However, in this range we are dealing with the well-understood freezing phenomenon for log-correlated fields
[
37
]
. This can be understood by the simple (and effective) heuristics that for
\beta>2
the mass for the chaos measure approximation is obtained from the
(\beta=2)
-thick points and by noting that the maximum of
X_{\varepsilon}
is very likely to be of the order
\log(1/\varepsilon)
. (For the relevant upper bound see Lemma
5.3
below.) These heuristics are actually built in the proof in Section
5.2
.

5.2.
A random analytic function in the unit disc

Our aim is to first consider here a unit disc analogue of the model that was discussed before in the right half-plane, which is related in a similar way to a (almost canonical basic) model of holomorphic chaos.
Thus, consider the holomorphic function
F
on
{\mathbb{D}}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,|z|<1\}
defined via
F(0)=0
and with the derivative

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(z):=\exp(G(z)),\quad G(z):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{G_{n}z^{n}}{\sqrt{n}}.

(5.3)

Here the variables
G_{n}=(V_{n}-iW_{n})/\sqrt{2}
are independent standard complex Gaussians.
Note that for
\beta=|\beta|e^{iu}\in\mathbb{C}
, the complex power
(F^{\prime}(z))^{\beta}=\exp\left(\beta G(z)\right)
has same law as
\exp\left(|\beta|G(z)\right)=(F^{\prime}(z))^{|\beta|}
,
because
e^{iu}G(z)
has same law as
G(z)
, since each
G_{n}
is a circularly-symmetric complex normal. So to study the integral means spectrum we only need to consider the
\beta>0
range.

We thus have

|F^{\prime}(z)|=\exp(U(z)),\quad U(z)=\Re\,G(z),

and for
z=re^{i\theta}
we write,

U_{r}(\theta):=U(re^{i\theta})=\Re\,G(re^{i\theta})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{r^{n}}{\sqrt{2n}}\big(V_{n}\cos(n\theta)+W_{n}\sin(n\theta)\big).

Thus
U
is a random harmonic function on
{\mathbb{D}}
, and its restriction
U_{r}
on the circle of radius
r
is the natural aproximation of the log-correlated field

X(\theta):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}\big(V_{n}\cos(n\theta)+W_{n}\sin(n\theta)\big).

The covariance structure of these approximations is very easy to compute, and we obtain for any
z=re^{i\theta},z^{\prime}=r^{\prime}e^{i\theta^{\prime}}\in{\mathbb{D}}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\,U(z)U(z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{r^{n}r^{\prime n}}{n}(\cos(n\theta)\cos(n\theta^{\prime})+\sin(n\theta)\sin(n\theta^{\prime}))=\frac{1}{2}\Re\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(z\overline{z^{\prime}})^{n}}{n}\right)

(5.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{|1-z\overline{z^{\prime}}|}.

In what follows we identify
{\mathbb{T}}:=\partial{\mathbb{D}}=[0,2\pi)
in a standard manner. The basic theory of GMC measures now implies that in the so-called subcritical range
\beta\in(0,2)
there is the convergence in probability for the chaos approximations obtained from
U_{r}
:s,

\mu_{\beta,r}({\mathbb{T}}):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\exp\left(\beta U_{r}(\theta)-(\beta^{2}/2){\mathbb{E}}\,U_{r}^{2}(\theta)\right)d\theta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu_{\beta}({\mathbb{T}}),\quad\textrm{as}\;\;r\to 1^{-}.

Recall that the multiplicative chaos
\mu_{\beta}
is a (random) Borel measure on the unit circle
{\mathbb{T}}
such that
\mu_{\beta}({\mathbb{T}})\not=0
almost surely. As mentioned before, the construction of the chaos measure and the convergence in probability for convolution approximations as we need here can be found in
[
20
, Section 3-4, pp. 5-10]
. The assumptions in
[
20
]
actually deal with compactly supported convolution kernels in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, but the only properties the proof in
[
20
]
uses for the field and the approximation are the following:

1.

First of all, for
r,r^{\prime}\in(0,1)
and
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in[0,2\pi)

{\mathbb{E}}\,U(re^{i\theta})U(r^{\prime}e^{i\theta^{\prime}})=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|\vee(1-r)\vee(1-r^{\prime})}+\mathcal{O}(1),

where the distance
|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|
is understood mod
2\pi.

2.

Secondly, for any fixed
\delta>0
there is the uniform convergence in the set
\{\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in[0,2\pi),\;|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|\geq\delta\}

{\mathbb{E}}\,U(re^{i\theta})U(r^{\prime}e^{i\theta^{\prime}})\to a(\theta,\theta^{\prime}),\quad\textrm{as}\;\;r,r^{\prime}\to 1^{-},

where
a
is continuous on
[0,2\pi)^{2}\setminus\{\theta=\theta^{\prime}\}.

Both of these conditions follow immediately from formula (
5.4
). For our purposes, we further need some basic facts for the moments of the chaos approximations:

Lemma 5.1
.

The approximations
\mu_{r,\beta}({\mathbb{T}})
satisfy

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\,\mu_{\beta,r}({\mathbb{T}})=1\qquad\textrm{and}\quad{\mathbb{E}}\,(\mu_{\beta,r}({\mathbb{T}}))^{-1}\leq C,

(5.5)

where the constant
C=C(\beta)
is independent of
r\in(0,1).
Especially, we have
\mu_{\beta}({\mathbb{T}})\not=0
almost surely.

Proof.

That
{\mathbb{E}}\,\mu_{\beta,r}({\mathbb{T}})=1
follows immediately from the definition, and the uniform finiteness of the negative moments is well known. To indicate the proof, we note that,
e.g.
, in
[
15
, Appendix B]
it is proven for a particular log-correlated field, and it then immediately follows by easy comparision by using Kahane’s convexity inequality both for
\mu_{\beta}({\mathbb{T}})
and its approximations
\mu_{\beta,r}({\mathbb{T}})
. Here one recalls that the function
x\to 1/x
is convex, and one may additionally make use of the trick of adding an independent constant Gaussian field to the reference field if needed in order to facilitate the comparison.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

Denote
r_{n}=1-e^{-n}
for integers
n\geq 1
. For any
\beta\in(0,2)
and any
\varepsilon>0
there is a random index
n_{0}
, finite almost surely, so that

\exp\big((\beta^{2}/4-\varepsilon)n\big)\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\exp\big(\beta U_{r_{n}}(\theta)\big)d\theta\leq\exp\big((\beta^{2}/4+\varepsilon)n\big)\qquad\textrm{for}\quad n\geq n_{0}.

Proof.

We have from
5.4

{\mathbb{E}}\,U^{2}_{r_{n}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}(n-\log 2)+\mathcal{O}(e^{-n}).

By the first moment estimate in (
5.5
) and Markov’s inequality we therefore have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\exp\big(\beta U_{r}(\theta)\big)d\theta\geq\exp\big((\beta^{2}/4+\varepsilon)n\big)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(C_{n}(\beta)\mu_{\beta,r}({\mathbb{T}})\geq\exp(\varepsilon n)\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}(\beta)e^{-n\varepsilon},

where
C_{n}(\beta)=2^{-\beta^{2}/4}+\mathcal{O}(e^{-n})
. One finishes by an application of Borel-Cantelli.
An analogous lower estimate follows by invoking the negative moment bound in (
5.5
). ∎

Another basic result for approximations of log-correlated fields we need is an easy result from the (extensively studied) behaviour of the maxima of a log-correlated field. For us the following crude result is enough.

Lemma 5.3
.

Assume that
\varepsilon>0
. There is a random
r_{0}
such that
r_{0}<1
almost surely, and for all
r\in[r_{0},1)
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
it holds that

U(re^{i\theta})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\log(1/(1-r)).

Proof.

This is well-known and follows easily,
e.g.
, from
[
2
, Theorem 1.1]
by applying the estimate there [with in our case the identification
\varepsilon\equiv 1-r
, and
m_{1-r}=\log(1/(1-r))-3/4\log\log(1/(1-r))
], to the sequence of radii
r_{n}=1-e^{-n}
to obtain

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\max_{\theta\in[0,2\pi]}U(r_{n}e^{i\theta})>\log(1/(1-r_{n}))+\lambda_{n}^{2}\right)\leq\exp(-c\lambda_{n}^{2}),

with the choice
\lambda_{n}=\log\log(1/(1-r_{n}))=\log n
. Since
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\exp(-c\lambda_{n}^{2})<\infty,
we obtain the claim by using the Borel-Cantelli lemma and the fact that the maximum of the harmonic function
U(re^{i\theta})
in the annulus
r_{n}\leq r<r_{n+1}
is obtained at the boundary.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proof.

As seen above, by circular invariance we need only to study the
\beta>0
range. Let us consider, for
r\in(0,1)
, the function
\phi_{r}:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty),
where

\phi_{r}(\beta)=\frac{1}{\log\big(1/(1-r)\big)}\log\Big(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\big|F^{\prime}(re^{i\theta})\big|^{\beta}d\theta\Big).

We first consider a fixed
\beta\in(0,2)
. Corollary
5.2
implies that along the sequence
r_{n}
of radii,
\phi_{r_{n}}(\beta)
has almost surely the stated limit
\beta^{2}/4
, up to an additive term
\pm\varepsilon
. Since this holds for all
\varepsilon>0
, we obtain the desired limit statement among this sequence of radii. Finally, we observe that the function
r\mapsto\int_{0}^{2\pi}\big|F^{\prime}(re^{i\theta})\big|^{\beta}d\theta
is increasing in
r
(see
[
39
, p. 38 or Lemma 1.1]
), which immediately implies the statement for all radii.

By the log-convexity of the Hölder norm, for each
r\in(0,1)
our
\phi_{r}
is a convex function of
\beta\in(0,\infty)
. According to Corollary
5.2
, we know that it almost surely converges to the function
\beta^{2}/4
for all
\beta\in(0,2)\cap\mathbb{Q}
as
r\to 1
via the sequence
(r_{n})_{n}
. This then implies convergence through all
r\nearrow 1
again by the previously mentioned increasing nature of the
\beta
-means. On the other hand, we have by Lemma
5.3
that
\limsup\phi_{r}(\beta)\leq\beta-1
for all
\beta>0
. These two facts and a standard convexity argument then imply our theorem for all
\beta>0
(note that the derivative of the map
\beta\to\beta^{2}/4
equals 1 at
\beta=2
).
∎

Remarks.

•

Actually the only non-trivial ingredient we use from GMC theory above is the uniform bound for negative moments.

•

Weaker results in the case
\beta\geq 2
naturally could be deduced also from the theory of critical chaos and supercritical chaos, but this would be technically much more complicated.

5.3.
Alternative treatment of the randomized Riemann zeta model via GMC

Here we sketch without all details how one may also use the approach of Section
5.2
to treat the random analytic function
\zeta_{\rm rand}
we considered in Theorem
1.1
in the first part of the present paper.
According to
[
80
, Theorem 1.7]
, we may write the logarithm of the randomized zeta function as

\log(\zeta_{\rm rand}(s))=G_{0}(s)+E(s),

(5.6)

where
E(s)
is almost surely bounded in any compact subset of
\{s={\sigma}+it,\sigma\geq 1/2,t\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
and
G_{0}
is a
Gaussian
analogue of the part
\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}U_{p}p^{-s}
of the randomized zeta function:

G_{0}(s)=\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}p^{-s}W_{p},

where the
W_{p}
:s are complex i.i.d. Gaussians. Naturally
G_{0}
is almost surely analytic in
\{\sigma>1/2\}
, and on the boundary
\{\sigma=1/2\}
its real part (as its imaginary part) defines a log-correlated Gaussian field. We are thus interested in the real part

G(s):={\Re}G_{0}(s)={\Re}\Big(\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}p^{-s}W_{p}\Big),

and on the integral means of
\exp(G(s))
. The real part is the harmonic extension to
\{\sigma>1/2\}
of the log-correlated field
G|_{\{\sigma=1/2\}}
, and it has the covariance

C(s,s^{\prime}):={\mathbb{E}}\,\Re G_{0}(s)\Re G_{0}(s^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}{\Re}\Big(\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}p^{-s-\overline{s^{\prime}}}\Big),\qquad\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}>1/2.

We collect all the properties of the covariance we need in the following statement, where
\zeta
denotes the
classical
zeta function.

Lemma 5.4
.

(i)
We have

\displaystyle C(s,s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\Re}\big(\log(\zeta(s+\overline{s^{\prime}}))\big)+b_{0}(s,s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{|s+\overline{s^{\prime}}-1|}+b(s,s^{\prime})

where
b_{0},b\in C^{\infty}(\{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in[1/2,1],\;\;t,t^{\prime}\in[-1,1]\})
.

(ii)
We have for all
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}>1/2
and
t,t^{\prime}\in[-1,1]

C(s,s^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{|t-t^{\prime}|\vee(\sigma-1/2)\vee(\sigma^{\prime}-1/2)}+\mathcal{O}(1).

(iii)
For any fixed
\delta>0
there is uniform convergence in the set
\{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in(1/2,1],\;\;t,t^{\prime}\in[-1,1],\;|t-t^{\prime}|\geq\delta\}

C(s,s^{\prime})\to a(t,t^{\prime}),\quad\textrm{as}\;\;\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\to(1/2)^{+},

where
a
is continuous on
[-1,1]^{2}\setminus\{t=t^{\prime}\}.

Proof.

We note towards (i) that

\displaystyle C(s,s^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}{\Re}\big(\log(\zeta(s+\overline{s^{\prime}}))\big)-\frac{1}{2}{\Re}\big(\widetilde{B}(s+\overline{s})\big),

where the Dirichlet series
\widetilde{B}(s):=\sum_{k\geq 2}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}p^{-ks}
converges at any
{\sigma}\in(1/2,\infty)
, and hence defines an analytic function on
\{\sigma>1/2\}
by Jensen’s lemma. This proves the first statement in (i), and the second one follows by observing that
(s-1)\zeta(s)
is analytic and non-zero in a neighborhood of the rectangle
[1,2]\times[-2,2]
.

In order to check (ii) we need to consider only the logarithmic term and we assume first that
\sigma>\sigma^{\prime}
. Then

\big|\log|\sigma+\sigma^{\prime}-1+i(t-t^{\prime})|-\log|2\sigma-1+i(t-t^{\prime})|\big|\leq\log\left(\frac{\sigma+\sigma^{\prime}-1}{2\sigma-1}\right)\leq\log 2.

Finally, it is obvious that
|\log|2\sigma-1+i(t-t^{\prime})|-\log\big((\sigma-1/2)\vee|t-t^{\prime}|\big)|\leq{3\log 2},
and (ii) follows. In turn, (iii) is a direct consequence of part (i).
∎

We are thus now in position to define the renormalized GMC measure for
{\beta}\in(0,2)
and
{\sigma}>1/2
,

\mu_{\beta,{\sigma}}(I):=\int_{I}\exp\left(\beta\Re G_{0}(s)-(\beta^{2}/2)C(s,s)\right)|ds|,

(5.7)

for any vertical line segment
I=I(h,h^{\prime})=\{s={\sigma}+it,t\in[h,h^{\prime}]\}
, and where, owing to (ii) in Lemma
5.4
,

C(s,s)=C({\sigma},{\sigma})=\log(1/({\sigma}-1/2))+\mathcal{O}(1)\;\;\textrm{as}\;\;{\sigma}\to(1/2)^{+}.

(5.8)

Because of Lemma
5.4
and
[
20
]
, we have the convergence in probability,

\mu_{\beta,{\sigma}}(I)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu_{\beta}(I),\;\;\textrm{as}\;\;{\sigma}\to(1/2)^{+},

where
\mu_{\beta}(I)\neq 0
almost surely.

After this we are essentially back in a situation where we need to consider the divergence of the integral means defined via Gaussian log-correlated fields: indeed, from this point onwards the proof is almost completely analogous to what we did in Section
5.2
. There is only one item of the argument that will not carry over immediately: the integral means over the segment
\{\sigma\}\times[0,1]
need not be strictly increasing in decreasing
\sigma>1/2
. Hence we need to do some ‘juggling’ to overcome this phenomenon. In order to tackle this technical detail we first write down a somewhat crude estimate for the growth of
\zeta_{\rm rand}
near the boundary:

Lemma 5.5
.

For
\varepsilon>0
, there is
r>0
and a random constant
C>0
such that almost surely

|\Re G_{0}(s)|\leq C+(1+\varepsilon)\log(1/(\sigma-1/2))+r\log(1+|t|)\quad\textrm{for all}\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}\;\;\textrm{and}\;\;\sigma\in(1/2,1].

Proof.

Let us set
Q_{n,j}=(1/2+2^{-n-1},1/2+2^{-n}]\times(j,j+1]
for
n\geq 1
and
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. We may apply (say)

[
2
, Theorem 1.1]
separately on each vertical boundary segment. On a horizontal boundary segment
[1/2+2^{-n-1}+ij,1/2+2^{-n}+ij]
we may consider for
x\in[0,1]
the random field
x\mapsto Y_{j}(x):=\Re G_{0}(1/2+2^{-n-1}(1+x)+ij)-\Re G_{0}(1/2+2^{-n-1}+ij)
. By
5.4
(i), the Lipschitz constant of the covariance of
Y_{j}
is uniformly (in
j
) bounded on
[0,1]
, and since
Y_{j}(0)=0
, standard results (see,
e.g.
,
[
3
, Theorem 4.1.2 with
\alpha=1
]
) yield that
\sup_{x\in[0,1]}Y_{j}(x)
has a uniform Gaussian tail. We also know exactly the Gaussian tail of
\Re G_{0}(1/2+2^{-n-1}+ij)
. Putting all together, we deduce that

\mathbb{P}(\Re G_{0}(s)\geq\log(1/(\sigma-1/2))+\lambda\;\;\textrm{for some}\;\;s\in\overline{Q}_{n,j})\leq C^{\prime}e^{-c\lambda},

with
c,C^{\prime}
two finite constants. By choosing
\lambda=\lambda_{j,n}:=(2/c)\log(|j|+1)+(\log n)^{2}
, the Borel Cantelli lemma implies that almost surely
|\Re G_{0}(s)|\leq\log(1/(\sigma-1/2))+\lambda_{j,n}
in all rectangles
\overline{Q}_{n,j}
, apart from finitely many which induce a finite random bound
C
. The Lemma follows, with
r=2/c
.
∎

Let
H^{p}(\sigma>a)
denote the space of analytic functions on the half-plane
\{\sigma>a\}
such that
\sup_{\sigma>a}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(\sigma+it)|^{p}dt<\infty
.
Let us define the auxiliary random function

\widetilde{\zeta}_{\rm rand}(s):=\exp(-\sqrt{s})\exp(G_{0}(s)).

(5.9)

The above lemma implies that

\widetilde{\zeta}_{\rm rand}(s)\in H^{p}(\sigma>a)\cap H^{\infty}(\sigma>a)\;\textrm{ for all}\;\;p>0,\;a>1/2,

together with

\log|\widetilde{\zeta}_{\rm rand}(s)|\leq(1+\varepsilon)\log(1/(\sigma-1/2))\;\;\textrm{on}\;\;\{\sigma>1/2,|t|\leq 1\}.

(5.10)

We note that since
|\exp(E(s))|
in (
5.6
) is almost surely bounded from below and above on
(\sigma,t)\in(1/2,2)\times(-2,2)
, as well as
|\exp(-\sqrt{s})|
, the random functions
\widetilde{\zeta}_{\rm rand}
and
\zeta_{\rm rand}
yield the same integral means spectra.

We will make use of a
H^{p}
-norm analogue of Hadamard’s three lines theorem:

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
S
be an infinite strip in the complex plane with boundary lines
L_{1},L_{2}
. Let
p>0
and assume that
f
is a holomorphic function on
S
with
\sup_{L}\int_{L}|f(z)|^{p}|dz|<\infty
, where the supremum is over all lines
L\subset\overline{S}

(
which naturally are parallel to
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
)
. Then for any line
L\in S
it holds that

\int_{L}|f(z)|^{p}|dz|\;\leq\;\Big(\int_{L_{1}}|f(z)|^{p}|dz|\Big)^{1-\theta}\Big(\int_{L_{2}}|f(z)|^{p}|dz|\Big)^{\theta},

where
\theta:={\rm dist\,}(L,L_{1})/{\rm dist\,}(L_{1},L_{2})
.

Proof.

See
[
17
, Theorem 2.3]
or
[
86
, Chapter XII, 1.3]
.
∎

To apply Lemma
5.6
to finite segments instead of lines, we use the following auxiliary function:

Lemma 5.7

(Localization)
.

Denote
z=x+iy\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
For each
M\geq 2
there is a function
g_{M}
that is analytic in the strip
S=\{-1/2<x<1/2\}
, and satisfies

\displaystyle|g_{M}(z)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1\;\;\textrm{in}\;\;S,

\displaystyle|g_{M}(z)|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle e^{-2}\textrm{\;}\;{for}\;\;\{|y|\leq M\}\cap S,\quad\textrm{and}

\displaystyle|g_{M}(z)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp(e^{-M}/2)\;\;\textrm{for}\;\;\{|y|\geq 2M\}\cap S.

Proof.

Simply choose

g_{M}(z)=\exp\big(-e^{-M-iz}-e^{-M+iz}\big),

6.
A brief history of multifractal analysis for harmonic measure

Caveat: In this Appendix, we return to notations for the integral means spectrum that are classical in harmonic measure studies
[
68

40
]
, but differ from those of the previous sections.

Let
h
be a conformal mapping from the unit disc,
h:{\mathbb{D}}\to\Omega
, where
\Omega
is a bounded planar domain. Let
t
be a complex number, and consider the integral means of the growth of the modulus of the
t

|h^{\prime}(z)^{t}|

associated with

is then defined as
[

,
23

{\beta}_{h}(t):=\underset{r\to 1^{-}}{\limsup}\frac{\log\int_{r\partial{\mathbb{D}}}|h^{\prime}(z)^{t}||dz|}{\log\left(\frac{1}{1-r}\right)}.

(6.1)

When the limit exists, one has the asymptotic behavior,

\int_{r\partial{\mathbb{D}}}|h^{\prime}(z)^{t}||dz|\asymp\left(1-r\right)^{-{\beta}_{h}(t)},\quad r\to 1^{-},

(6.2)

in the sense of the equivalence of the logarithms.

When the conformal mapping
h
is
random
, one is usually lead to first define an
average
integral means spectrum, where one takes the expectation of the l.h.s. of (
6.2
), and the question naturally arises of the comparison of the average spectrum and of the almost sure one. For instance, let us recall the

spectrum of the Schramm-Loewner evolution

18

\displaystyle{\beta}(t,\kappa)-t+1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+2\tau-2\sqrt{b\tau},\quad\tau=d-t,

(6.3)

\displaystyle d=d(\kappa)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(4+\kappa)^{2}}{8\kappa},\quad\quad\,\,\,\quad t\in[t_{1},t_{2}],

(6.4)

with
t_{1}=-1-\frac{3}{8}\kappa,t_{2}=\frac{3}{4}d(\kappa)
. Outside of that interval, an average spectrum associated with the SLE
tip
exists for
t\leq t_{1}
,
whereas the average spectrum becomes

for

[

19

There exist equivalent formulations in terms of the

When

In the harmonic measure case, the integral means spectrum

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\alpha}}f({\alpha})

\displaystyle\underset{t}{\inf}\left\{{\beta}(t)-t+1+\frac{1}{{\alpha}}t\right\},

\displaystyle{\beta}(t)-t+1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{{\alpha}}{\sup}\left\{\frac{1}{{\alpha}}\big(f({\alpha})-t\big)\right\}.
