ACCOMPLISHMENTS

1. What are the major goals of the project?

The long-term objective of our project is to develop a general molecular-level picture of the
surface chemistry associated with ALD processes. Our central hypothesis is that the chemistry
of ALD precursors can differ significantly from that seen in solution. Awareness of such
differences should provide general guidelines on what to watch for when synthesizing new ALD
precursors and designing and optimizing new ALD processes, especially in terms of minimizing
the incorporation of impurities in the growing films and of carrying out depositions under mild
pressure and temperature conditions. The main objective of our research project has been to
advance the fundamental knowledge of the surface chemistry of ALD precursors needed for the
design and optimization of film deposition processes. A modern surface-science approach has
been implemented to both elucidate the mechanism of the reactions of the precursors on the
surface and characterize the composition and morphology of the growing films. In general,
emphasis is being placed on:

 • Identifying the primary reactions that may lead to the deposition of clean films;
 • Identifying the secondary reactions that may help the ALD process, by, for instance,
 helping with the reduction (or oxidation) of the metal atom;
 • Identifying the secondary reactions that may lead to the deposition of undesirable
 impurities in the growing films;
 • Determining the kinetic parameters of the relevant surface reactions in order to define
 the optimum conditions for film deposition and to minimize impurity deposition.
 • Characterizing the nature of the resulting films, with focus on their stoichiometry and
 on the final oxidation states of the constituent elements; and
 • Using the information obtained to propose better precursors for given ALD processes.

In the ongoing 3-year period of this project (of which this is the first), we have been expanding
our previous work in two main directions, in terms of both the chemistry of ALD precursors and
the tools available for our work, in order to:

 • Explore the chemistry of metal ALD precursors with the aim of identifying the factors
 that define the nature of the material deposited on oxide surfaces.
 • Probe ways to modify the surfaces of the substrates in order to affect the nucleation and
 further growth of metal NPs by ALD.

One key new direction of our work in this new phase of the proposal has been to extend our
ALD studies to high surface area porous materials.

2. What was accomplished under these goals?

The molecular details of the atomic layer deposition (ALD) of metallic copper on silicon oxide
substrates using metallorganic precursors, iminopyrrolidinate complexes in particular, were
characterized under ultrahigh vacuum conditions by temperature-programmed desorption (TPD),
in its temperature ramping and isothermal modes, and by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy

 2

(XPS). Two specific issues were addressed. First, it was demonstrated that H2 is quite inefficient
as a reducing agent and cannot be effectively used to remove the organic ligands in the adsorbed
species during the ALD cycles; insufficient ligand removal leads to the incorporation of
impurities in the growing films. On the other hand, atomic hydrogen can accomplish both metal
reducing and ligand removal functions and can be incorporated in ALD processes to deposit
incremental amounts of Cu in sequential cycles. The second aspect was the fact that the
deposited metallic Cu grows in the form of three-dimensional (3D) nanoparticles rather than as
conformal two- dimensional films. The TPD and XPS evidence collected in our studies points to
a mechanism where individual Cu atoms, once cleaned from their organic ligands and reduced to
their metallic state, become quite mobile and diffuse on the SiO2 surface to form the 3D
nanostructures. Similar Cu atom mobility and sintering were seen even in Cu physical vapor
deposition processes carried out at room temperature and may therefore be an intrinsic feature of
these ALDs regardless of the source of the metal atoms.

The surface chemistry of Ru atomic layer deposition (ALD) processes based on the use of
tris(2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-3,5-heptanedionato)ruthenium(III) (Ru(tmhd)3) and either molecular
oxygen or atomic hydrogen on aluminum oxide films was characterized by a combination of
surface-sensitive techniques. The thermal decomposition of the Ru metalorganic precursor was
determined, by using a combination of reflection-absorption infrared spectroscopy (RAIRS),
TPD, and XPS) to start below 400 K and to take place in a stepwise fashion over a wide range of
temperatures. Gas-phase products from this chemistry include 2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-3,5-
heptanedione (the protonated ligand, Htmhd; in a TPD peak at 520 K), isobutene (540 K;
indicating the fragmentation of the organic ligands), and other products from isomerization
and/or aldol condensation (650 and 730 K). This chemistry is accompanied by the reduction of
the Ru3+ ions in two stages, involving the loss of some of their ligands and their direct bonding to
the substrate first (between 500 and 600 K) and a full reduction to a metallic state later on (600–
700 K). ALD cycles using either molecular oxygen or atomic hydrogen resulted in the slow
build-up of Ru on the surface, but the co-deposition of carbon could not be avoided, at least in
the initial cycles, while the alumina surface was still exposed. With O2, the Ru atoms alternate
between partially-oxidized (after the O2 exposures) and zero-valent (after the Ru(tmhd)3 doses)
states, and some Ru loss in the form of the volatile RuO4 oxide was seen after the second half of
the ALD cycles; neither the Ru oxidation state alternation nor the elimination of some Ru from
the surface were observed when using H⋅. The deposited Ru was determined, by combining
results from angle-resolved XPS (ARXPS) and low-energy ion scattering (LEIS) experiments, to
grow as 3D nanoparticles rather than as a layer-by-layer 2D film, presumably because the Ru
precursor preferentially adsorbs (and decomposes more cleanly) on the metal surface.

In more general terms, we published a where we discuss the progress made in the mechanistic
studies of the surface chemistry associated with the atomic layer deposition (ALD) of metal films
and the usefulness of that knowledge for the optimization of existing film growth processes and
for the design of new ones. Our focus was on the deposition of late transition metals. We started
by introducing some of the main surface-sensitive techniques and approaches used in this
research. We commented on the general nature of the metallorganic complexes used as
precursors for these depositions, and the uniqueness that solid surfaces and the absence of liquid
solvents bring to the ALD chemistry and differentiate it from what is known from metalorganic
chemistry in solution. We then delved into the adsorption and thermal chemistry of those

 3

precursors, highlighting the complex and stepwise nature of the decomposition of the organic
ligands that usually ensued upon their thermal activation. We discussed the criteria relevant for
the selection of co-reactants to be used on the second half of the ALD cycle, with emphasis on
the redox chemistry often associated with the growth of metallic films starting from complexes
with metal cations. Additional considerations include the nature of the substrate and the final
structural and chemical properties of the growing films, which we indicate rarely retain the
homogeneous 2D structure often aimed for. We ended with some general conclusions and
personal thoughts about the future of this field.

3. What opportunities for training and professional development has the project
provided?

The students, postdocs, and visiting scholars involved in this project get the opportunity to be
exposed to vacuum technology and surface-science instrumentation, which includes a number of
sophisticated spectroscopies such as XPS, TPD, and infrared absorption spectroscopy. At the
same time, they get exposed to the chemistry of important industrial processes, and to work with
ALD reactors. These skills are highly marketable. For example, Dr. Wang Ke, the latest student
to graduate from our program, was able to secure an excellent position at Xi'an Aeronautics
Computing Technique Research Institute. Dr. Qin, a postdoc that was associated with this
project in the past, have moved to a new research position at ASM, and Dr. Motin, another
postdoc, has just accepted a position at PsiQuantum in San Jose, CA. Two new students,
Mohammed Alam and Yihao Zhou, have started to work on this project, replacing the outgoing
workers.

4. How have the results been disseminated to communities of interest?

We have published a number of scientific articles and have given scientific presentations on our
work. Within the last year, we have published on new report, have submitted a second one for
publication, and have published an additional two review papers where DOE funding is
acknowledged. We have also given several scientific presentations (limited given the new
situation with the pandemic).

5. What do you plan to do during the next reporting period to accomplish the goals?

We continue to extend the studies on both model surfaces and porous materials to make
metal-based catalysts for energy applications. Our next goal and new direction of research,
the theme of our renewal application next year, is to control the nucleation of ALD processes
for metal deposition by manipulating the surface of oxide substrates, specifically silica,
increasing or reducing the surface density of surface silanol groups via etching,
derivatization, or silylation reactions.

IMPACT

1. What is the impact on the development of the principal discipline(s) of the project?

Our ALD work has helped inform industries on the design of better ALD processes. In the past
we have work in close collaboration with several industries, including Intel and Air Products, to
address specific aspects of their ALD needs. We are also looking into the use of ALD for the
preparation of catalyst to promote reactions of relevance to energy problems. In more general
terms, the mechanistic information we provide is central basic knowledge not available
elsewhere and critical for ALD process design. Our new work is geared to address issues related
to energy, in particular with catalytic processes.

2. What is the impact on other disciplines?

Our methodology is quite basic, and easy to extend to other problems. We in our laboratory are
particularly interested in implementing ALD processes for the development of new catalysts, for
instance. In addition, our development of new approaches to study chemistry on surfaces is
useful for many other problems, and the surface chemistry itself described by our studies is
informative in inorganic chemistry.

3. What is the impact on the development of human resources?

The training we provide graduate students, postdocs and visiting scholars is quite broad and
basic, and easy to market. Most of our students have move to high-profile scientific or
technological positions after leaving our group. Our last student to graduate in this project, Dr.
Wang Ke, was able to secure an excellent position at Xi'an Aeronautics Computing Technique
Research Institute. Dr. Qin, a postdoc that was associated with this project in the past, have
moved to a new research position at ASM, and Dr. Motin, another postdoc, has just accepted a
position at PsiQuantum in San Jose, CA. Two new students, Mohammed Alam and Yihao Zhou,
