Mohammed Adib Oumer, Vishnu Murali, Majid Zamani

Abstract
. The recently introduced notions of ranking functions and closure certificates utilize well-foundedness arguments to facilitate the verification of dynamical systems against ω \omega -regular properties. A ranking function and a closure certificate are real-valued functions defined over states and state pairs of a dynamical system whose zero superlevel sets are inductive state invariant and inductive transition invariant, respectively. The search for such certificates can be automated by fixing a specific template class, such as a polynomial of a fixed degree, and then using optimization techniques such as sum-of-squares (SOS) programming to find it. Unfortunately, such certificates may not be found for a fixed template. In such a case, one must change the template; for example, increase the degree of the polynomial. In this paper, we consider a notion of multiple functions in the form of vector certificates. Taking inspiration from the literature on vector barrier certificates as generalizations of standard barrier certificates for safety verification, we propose vector co-Büchi ranking functions and vector closure certificates as nontrivial generalizations of ranking functions and closure certificates, respectively. Both notions consist of a set of functions that jointly overapproximate an inductive invariant by considering each function to be a linear combination of the others. The advantage of such certificates is that they allow us to prove properties even when a single function for a fixed template cannot be found using standard approaches. We present an SOS programming approach to search for these functions and demonstrate the effectiveness of our proposed method in verifying ω \omega -regular specifications in several case studies.



1.
Introduction

An automated abstraction-free approach to verify systems against specifications characterized in linear temporal logic (LTL)
[
13
]
or via
\omega
-regular automata
[
21
]
is through the use of ranking functions
[
3
]
or closure certificates
[
9
]
, which act as inductive transition invariants.
Ranking functions overestimate reachable states and use well-foundedness arguments to verify
\omega
-regular specifications.
On the other hand, the closure certificates introduced in
[
9
]
aim to capture the reachable transitions of a system with the goal of automating the verification of LTL and
\omega
-regular specifications.
The search for such certificates can be automated using optimization
[
12
,
17
]
, neural networks
[
10
]
, or SMT-based approaches
[
5
]
.
These approaches typically rely on first fixing a template (e.g., a polynomial of a fixed degree) and then making use of the above approaches to search for an appropriate function within this template.
When one is unable to find such a certificate for a given template, one is forced to consider a different template and try again.
Inspired by the use of a vector of multiple functions in vector barrier certificates
[
20
]
and its powerful potential for generalization, we present notions of
vector co-Büchi ranking functions
and
vector closure certificates
.
In this paper, we show that one may find such certificates to verify LTL properties, such as safety and persistence, even when we fail to find a single function of the same template to do so.
Similarly to vector barrier certificates, the search for vector co-Büchi ranking functions and vector closure certificates can be carried out effectively using existing approaches.

Verification of systems against safety, a prominent LTL property, can be automated by using barrier certificates
[
16
]
.
A barrier certificate is a real valued function that is nonpositive over the initial states, positive over the unsafe states, and nonincreasing with transitions.
Thus, a barrier certificate is an inductive state invariant that guarantees safety, as its zero level set separates the reachable and unsafe states, with the zero-sublevel set overapproximating the set of reachable states.
In
[
3
]
, the authors extended this safety verification approach via inductive invariants and unified it with well-foundedness arguments
[
1
]
to certify general LTL properties.
In this method, they argue for well-foundedness by considering the existence of a single ranking function.
However, if one wants to consider partitions over relevant sets instead and try to find independent ranking function arguments over each partition, then one should consider a well-foundedness argument over the reachable transitions of the system.

Transition invariants
[
14
]
were introduced as an overapproximation of reachable transitions and are used to verify programs against
\omega
-regular properties.
Closure certificates
[
9
]
act as functional analogs of inductive transition invariants.
Such certificates can be used to verify safety, as well as larger classes of
\omega
-regular properties.
Intuitively, a closure certificate is a real valued function that is nonnegative for a pair of states
(x,y)
if
y
may be reachable from
x
(conversely, if the function is negative, then
y
is not reachable from
x
).
Ensuring that this certificate is negative for all pairs
(x_{0},x_{u})
, where
x_{0}
is an initial state and
x_{u}
is an unsafe state, acts as a proof of safety.
To verify general LTL specifications, one may extend the earlier findings with a ranking function argument to prove the well-foundedness.

In the context of dynamical systems, the typical formulation for ranking functions and closure certificates assumes the presence of a single function.
This formulation is sound, but if one fails to find a function that meets a given set of conditions, it does not imply that the system refutes the property of interest for the system.
An approach to addressing this challenge is to formulate the conditions using a systematic combination of multiple functions.
The authors of
[
20
]
introduced the use of a vector of functions for barrier certificates in what they termed vector barrier certificates.
They show that such a formulation generalizes the standard formulation of barrier certificates, with guarantees that sometimes a scalar barrier certificate of a given template does not exist, while a vector barrier certificate does for a given system.
In addition, the class of inductive state invariants that one can extract from vector barrier certificates is richer, as its individual elements do not need to correspond to an inductive invariant.
In this paper, we adopt the vector-based formulation for ranking functions and closure certificates and show its benefits in verifying
\omega
-regular properties, both in terms of the certificate template as well as computation time.
The benefit of vector closure certificates over vector co-Büchi ranking functions can be summarized as follows.
As noted in
[
14
]
, a relation is well-founded if its transitive closure is disjunctively well-founded (not if the relation itself is disjunctively well-founded).
Since we are using multiple functions to overapproximate the transitive closure of the transition relation via vector closure certificates, we can use the functions in the vector such that each component decreases independently of the others over different partitions of relevant sets to prove well-foundedness.
This approach is not possible for vector co-Büchi ranking functions.
In contrast, vector co-Büchi ranking functions are defined over states of a system, making their formulation simpler than vector closure certificates that are defined over pairs of states.

1.1.
Contributions

In this paper, our main contributions are as follows:
i) We propose novel notions of vector co-Büchi ranking functions and vector closure certificates that have more permissive conditions compared to the standard formulations;
ii) We present SOS programming to search for the proposed vector certificates using a fixed template;
iii) We show that when a standard closure certificate of a given template fails to ensure a certain specification, one can find functions of the same template using the proposed vector closure certificates;
iv) We show that vector co-Büchi ranking functions are ideal for minimal computation times compared to other certificates;
v) We show that even when a standard closure certificate of a given template is successfully computed to ensure a given specification, one can find functions of a lower degree template using the proposed vector closure certificates while drastically improving computation time;
vi) We show that, for suitable choices of certain hyperparameters, our proposed formulations recover the usual conditions for ranking functions and closure certificates.

1.2.
Related Works

The authors of
[
16
]
proposed the notion of barrier certificates as a discretization-free approach to provide guarantees of safety
[
18
]
for dynamical and hybrid systems.
The authors of
[
8
]
introduced exponential-type barrier certificates that generalize the conditions of
[
16
]
while maintaining the convexity of the conditions simply by fixing a hyperparameter.
In
[
4
]
, general barrier certificates are reported, which further generalize the conditions for exponential-type barrier certificates.
The authors of
[
20
]
used a vector of functions to
generalize the conditions of
[
8
]
and
[
4
]
for the verification of safety.
Another generalization of standard barrier certificates using multiple functions was introduced in
[
11
]
using the notion of logical interpolation.

The authors of
[
3
,
6
]
consider the use of barrier certificates and ranking functions to verify LTL properties.
The authors of
[
14
]
proposed a notion of transition invariants and demonstrated their use in verifying programs against
\omega
-regular properties.
This concept was adopted in
[
9
]
to introduce the notion of closure certificates for an automated approach to verify dynamical systems against
\omega
-regular properties.

In terms of searching for vector certificates, it is not generally as straightforward as the standard certificates due to relatively more complex bilinear conditions (cf. Section
2.3
and
3
).
One approach to addressing this bilinearity, albeit conservative, is to fix one of the terms contributing to the bilinear terms to reduce the problem to a convex one and use existing computational tools such as SOS programming or SMT solvers to search for the certificates.
For the standard certificates, reducing the bilinear conditions to linear ones requires fixing only one constant.
In contrast, for vector certificates, we want to consider combinations of the functions; thus, reducing the bilinear conditions to linear ones requires fixing a matrix that is not trivially the zero matrix or the identity.
Another approach that directly tackles this challenge uses an iterative scheme of fixing one of the bilinear terms and searching for the other terms until an optimal solution is obtained or the process times out, as shown in
[
2
,
24
]
. However, this approach generally requires a feasible solution a priori to start the iterative scheme. In our approach, our aim is to find a feasible solution to begin with.

2.2.
Discrete-time Dynamical System

In this paper, we model systems as discrete-time dynamical systems.

Definition 2.1
.

A discrete-time dynamical system is given by the tuple
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}_{0},f)
,
where the state set is denoted by
\mathcal{X}
, the set of initial states is
\mathcal{X}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, and
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
is the state transition relation that describes the evolution of the states of the system.
That is, for
x_{t}
, the state of the system at time step
t\in\mathbb{N}
, the state of the system in the next time step is given by
x_{t+1}\in f(x_{t})
,
\forall x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}.

If
\forall x\in\mathcal{X}
, we have
|f(x)|=1
, then we consider the transition relation
f
to be a deterministic state transition function.
We use
f
for both a set-valued map when it is a relation and a transition function when it is a function.
Throughout the paper, we assume that the state sets of the systems under consideration are compact.

For notational convenience, given a state
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we use
x^{\prime}
to indicate a state in
f(x)
(
i.e.
,
x^{\prime}\in f(x)
), unless otherwise specified.
Furthermore, we use
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
to denote a state in the initial set of states
\mathcal{X}_{0}
.
A state sequence is an infinite sequence
(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots)\in\mathcal{X}^{\omega}
where
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
, and
x_{i+1}\in f(x_{i})\ \forall i\in\mathbb{N}
.
We say
x_{j}
is reachable from
x_{i}
if
i,j\in\mathbb{N},j>i
and
x_{i},x_{j}\in(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots)
for some state sequence
(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots)
.

2.3.
Specifications

In this paper, we study increasingly complex specifications, from safety and persistence to LTL and
\omega
-regular specifications over discrete-time dynamical systems.

2.3.1.
Safety

We say that a system
\mathcal{S}
starting from some set of initial states
\mathcal{X}_{0}
is safe with respect to a set of unsafe states
\mathcal{X}_{u}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
if, for any state sequence
(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots)
,
x_{i}\notin\mathcal{X}_{u}\ \forall i\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
.
Remark that we assume that the initial and unsafe sets are disjoint, i.e.
\mathcal{X}_{0}\cap\mathcal{X}_{u}=\emptyset
.
For notational simplicity, we use
x_{u}\in\mathcal{X}_{u}
to denote a state in the unsafe set of states
\mathcal{X}_{u}
.

Definition 2.2

(BC)
.

A function
\mathcal{B}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is a BC for a system
\mathcal{S}
with respect to a set of unsafe states
\mathcal{X}_{u}
if
\exists\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
\forall x\in\mathcal{X}
,
\forall x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
,
\forall x_{u}\in\mathcal{X}_{u}
,
\forall x^{\prime}\in f(x)
:

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(x_{0})\leq 0,

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(x_{u})>0,\text{ and }

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(x^{\prime})\leq\lambda\mathcal{B}(x).

Theorem 1

(BCs imply safety
[
16
]
)
.

For a system
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}_{0},f)
with unsafe states
\mathcal{X}_{u}
, the existence of a barrier certificate
\mathcal{B}
satisfying conditions (
1
)-(
3
) implies its safety.

The definition of
vector barrier certificates (VBCs)
, adopted from
[
20
]
, for discrete-time systems is given as follows.

Definition 2.3

(VBC)
.

A vector of functions
\mathbf{B}
consisting of
\mathcal{B}_{i}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, is a VBC for a system
\mathcal{S}
with respect to a set of unsafe states
\mathcal{X}_{u}
if
\exists A\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{k\times k}
such that
\forall x\in\mathcal{X}
,
\forall x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
,
\forall x_{u}\in\mathcal{X}_{u}
,
\forall x^{\prime}\in f(x)
:

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}(x_{0})\leq 0,

(5)

\displaystyle\bigvee_{i=1}^{k}\big(\mathcal{B}_{i}(x_{u})>0\big),

(6)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}(x^{\prime})\leq A\mathbf{B}(x),

where the inequalities are element-wise.

Theorem 2

(VBCs imply safety
[
20
]
)
.

The existence of a VBC
\mathcal{B}_{i}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, satisfying conditions (
4
)-(
6
) for a system
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}_{0},f)
with unsafe states
\mathcal{X}_{u}
implies its safety.

Note that conditions (
1
) and (
3
) (similarly (
4
) and (
6
)) provide the invariant set that overapproximates the reachable states, while condition (
2
) (similarly (
5
)) separates the unsafe states from the reachable states.

(CC for Safety)

A function

,
\forall x_{u}\in\mathcal{X}_{u}

(7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(x,x^{\prime})\geq 0,

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(x,y)\geq\lambda\mathcal{T}(x^{\prime},y),\text{ and }

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(x_{0},x_{u})\leq-\eta.

(CCs imply safety
[

)

The existence of a function
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}

2.3.2.
Persistence

We say that a system visits a region
\mathcal{X}_{VF}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
only finitely often if, for any state sequence
(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots)
,
\exists i\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\forall j\geq i
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
x_{j}\notin\mathcal{X}_{VF}
.
An approach to ensure persistence is the use of a Lyapunov-like argument
[
15
]
combined with an invariant argument via CCs.

Definition 2.5

(CC for Persistence)
.

A function
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is a CC for
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}_{0},f)
with a set of states
\mathcal{X}_{VF}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
that must be visited only finitely often if
\exists\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0},\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X}
,
\forall x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
,
\forall x^{\prime}\in f(x)
and
\forall y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{VF}
:

(10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(x,x^{\prime})\geq 0,

(11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(x,y)\geq\lambda\mathcal{T}(x^{\prime},y),\text{ and }

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{T}(x_{0},y^{\prime})\geq 0\big)\wedge\big(\mathcal{T}(y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime})\geq 0\big)\implies

(12)

\displaystyle\qquad\big(\mathcal{T}(x_{0},y^{\prime\prime})\leq\mathcal{T}(x_{0},y^{\prime})-\eta\big).

Theorem 4

(CCs imply Persistence
[
9
]
)
.

The existence of a function
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying conditions (
10
)-(
2.5
) for a system
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}_{0},f)
implies that the state sequences of the system visit the set
\mathcal{X}_{VF}
finitely often.

Observe that conditions (
10
) and (
11
) provide an invariant set that overapproximates the reachable transitions while condition (
2.5
) argues for finite visits using possible transitions.
One can employ the S-procedure
[
25
]
to convert the implication in (
2.5
) into a single inequality condition.

We now mention the formulation of Büchi ranking functions (BRF) to argue for infinite visits (recurrence).

Definition 2.6

(BRF for Recurrence)
.

A function
\mathcal{B}
is a BRF for a system
\mathcal{S}
with respect to a set of states
\mathcal{X}_{INF}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
that must be visited infinitely often if
\exists\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
\exists\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
\forall x\in\mathcal{X}
,
\forall x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
,
\forall x^{\prime}\in f(x)
,
\forall y\in\mathcal{X}\backslash\mathcal{X}_{INF}
,
\forall y^{\prime}\in f(y)
:

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(x_{0})\geq 0,

(14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(x^{\prime})\geq\lambda_{1}\mathcal{B}(x),\text{ and}

(15)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{B}(y)\geq 0)\implies(\mathcal{B}(y)-\mathcal{B}(y^{\prime})-\eta\geq 0),

where the inequalities are element-wise.

Theorem 5

(BRFs imply recurrence
[
3
]
)
.

The existence of a BRF
\mathcal{B}
, satisfying conditions (
13
)-(
15
) for a system
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}_{0},f)
implies that the state sequences of the system visit the set
\mathcal{X}_{INF}
infinitely often.

In this paper, we modify this result to argue for a finite visit to the set
\mathcal{X}_{VF}
. We replace condition (
15
) with conditions
(\mathcal{B}(y)\geq 0)\implies(\mathcal{B}(y)-\mathcal{B}(y^{\prime})\geq 0)
and
(\mathcal{B}(z)\geq 0)\implies(\mathcal{B}(z)-\mathcal{B}(z^{\prime})-\eta\geq 0)

\forall y\in\mathcal{X}\backslash\mathcal{X}_{VF}
,
\forall y^{\prime}\in f(y)
,
\forall z\in\mathcal{X}_{VF}
,
\forall z^{\prime}\in f(z)
.
The key idea behind these conditions is to set the function
\mathcal{B}
to be nonincreasing when outside
\mathcal{X}_{VF}
, but to make it strictly decreasing when outside
\mathcal{X}_{VF}
.
Thus, if such
\mathcal{B}
exists,
\mathcal{X}_{VF}
must be visited only finitely often.
We explore a vector certificate version of this formulation in Section
3
.

2.3.3.
Linear Temporal Logic (LTL)

The formulae in LTL
[
13
]
are defined with respect to a set of finite atomic propositions
AP
that are relevant to our system.
Let
\Sigma=2^{AP}
denote the powerset of atomic propositions.
A trace
w=(w_{0},w_{1},\ldots,)\in\Sigma^{\omega}
is an infinite sequence of sets of atomic propositions.
The syntax of LTL can be given via the following grammar:

\phi:=\top\;|\;a\;|\;\neg\phi\;|\;\mathsf{X}\phi\;|\;\phi\mathsf{U}\phi,

where
\top
indicates
\mathsf{true}
,
a\in AP
denotes an atomic proposition, the symbols
\wedge
and
\neg
denote the logical AND and NOT operators, respectively.
The temporal operators next and until are denoted by
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{U}
, respectively.
The above operators are sufficient to derive the logical OR (
\vee
) and implication (
\implies
) operators, as well as the temporal operators release (
\mathsf{R}
), eventually (
\mathsf{F}
), and always (
\mathsf{G}
).

We inductively define the semantics of an LTL formula with respect to the trace
w
as follows:

(16)

\displaystyle w\models a

\displaystyle\text{ if }a\in w[0]

(17)

\displaystyle w\models\phi_{1}\wedge\phi_{2}

\displaystyle\text{ if }w\models\phi_{1}\text{ and }w\models\phi_{2}

(18)

\displaystyle w\models\neg\phi

\displaystyle\text{ if }w\not\models\phi

(19)

\displaystyle w\models\mathsf{X}\phi

\displaystyle\text{ if }w[1,\infty)\models\phi

\displaystyle w\models\phi_{1}\mathsf{U}\phi_{2}

\displaystyle\text{ if }\exists i\in\mathbb{N}\text{ such that }w[0,i]\models\phi_{1}

(20)

\displaystyle\text{ and }w[i+1,\infty)\models\phi_{2}

To reason about whether a system satisfies a property specified in LTL, we associate a labeling function
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{X}\to\Sigma
that maps each state of the system to a letter in the finite alphabet
\Sigma
.
This naturally generalizes to mapping a state sequence of the system
(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,)\in\mathcal{X}^{\omega}
to a trace
w=(\mathcal{L}(x_{0}),\mathcal{L}(x_{1}),\ldots,)\in\Sigma^{\omega}
.
Let
TR(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})
denote the set of all traces of
\mathcal{S}
under the labeling map
\mathcal{L}
.
Then the system
\mathcal{S}
satisfies an LTL property
\phi
under the labeling map
\mathcal{L}
if, for all
w\in TR(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L})
, we have
w\models\phi
.
We denote this as
\mathcal{S}\models_{\mathcal{L}}\phi
and infer the labeling map from context.
Safety and persistence can be formulated as LTL formulae.

Nondeterminstic Büchi Automata.

A nondeterminstic Büchi automaton (NBA)
\mathcal{A}
is a tuple
(\Sigma,\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}_{0},\delta,\mathcal{Q}_{F})
, where:

\Sigma
is the alphabet,

\mathcal{Q}
is a finite set of states,

\mathcal{Q}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
is an initial set of states,

\delta:\mathcal{Q}\times\Sigma\times\mathcal{Q}
is the transition relation and

{\mathcal{Q}_{F}}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
is the set of accepting states.
A run of the automaton
\mathcal{A}=(\Sigma,\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}_{0},\delta,\mathcal{Q}_{F})
over a trace
w=(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\ldots,)\in\Sigma^{\omega}
is an infinite sequence of states characterized as
\rho=(q_{0},q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,)\in\mathcal{Q}^{\omega}
with
q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}
and
q_{i+1}\in\delta(q_{i},\sigma_{i})
.
An NBA
\mathcal{A}
is said to accept a trace
w
if there exists a run
\rho
over
w
where
\mathsf{Inf}(\rho)\cap{\mathcal{Q}_{F}}\neq\emptyset
.

It is well known that given an LTL formula
\phi
on a set of atomic propositions
AP
, one can construct an NBA
\mathcal{A}
such that
w\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})
if and only if
w\models\phi

[
22
]
.
An automata-theoretic approach for checking whether
\mathcal{S}\models_{\mathcal{L}}\phi
consists first in constructing the NBA
\mathcal{A}
that captures
\neg\phi
, and then verifying that
\mathcal{S}\not\models_{\mathcal{L}}\neg\phi
by demonstrating that no execution trace of the system is accepted by
\mathcal{A}
.

To verify whether a system satisfies an LTL property, we use a closure certificate on the product
\mathcal{S}\otimes\mathcal{A}
as follows.

Definition 2.7

(CC for LTL)
.

Consider a system
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}_{0},f)
and an NBA
\mathcal{A}=(\Sigma,\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}_{0},\delta,\mathcal{Q}_{F})
representing the complement of an LTL formula
\phi
.
A function
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Q}\times\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}
is a CC for
\mathcal{S}\otimes\mathcal{A}
if
\exists\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0},\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
\forall x,y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
,
\forall x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
,
\forall x^{\prime}\in f(x)
,
\forall n,p\in\mathcal{Q}
,
\forall n^{\prime}\in\delta(n,\mathcal{L}(x))
,
\forall q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}
and
\forall r,r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}_{F}
:

(21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\big((x,n),(x^{\prime},n^{\prime})\big)\geq 0,

(22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\big((x,n),(y,p)\big)\geq\lambda\mathcal{T}\big((x^{\prime},n^{\prime}),(y,p)\big),

\displaystyle\Big(\mathcal{T}\big((x_{0},q_{0}),(y,r)\big)\geq 0\Big)\wedge\Big(\mathcal{T}((y,r),(y^{\prime},r^{\prime}))\geq 0\Big)\implies

(23)

\displaystyle\qquad\Big(\mathcal{T}\big((x_{0},q_{0}),(y^{\prime},r^{\prime})\big)\leq\mathcal{T}\big((x_{0},q_{0}),(y,r)\big)-\eta\Big).

Theorem 6

(CCs verify LTL
[
9
]
)
.

Consider a system
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}_{0},f)
and an LTL formula
\phi
. Let NBA
\mathcal{A}
represent the complement of the specification, i.e,
\neg\phi
. The existence of a CC satisfying conditions (
21
)-(
2.7
) implies that

.

3.

\omega
-regular Specifications

We first mention the vector formulation of ranking functions dubbed vector co-Büchi ranking function (VCBRF) to argue for finite visits.

Definition 3.1

(VCBRF for Persistence)
.

A vector of functions
\mathbf{B}
consisting of
\mathcal{B}_{i}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, is a VCBRF for a system
\mathcal{S}
with respect to a set of states
\mathcal{X}_{VF}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
that must be visited only finitely often if
\exists\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
\exists A_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{k\times k}
such that
\forall x\in\mathcal{X}
,
\forall x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
,
\forall x^{\prime}\in f(x)
,
\forall y\in\mathcal{X}\backslash\mathcal{X}_{VF}
,
\forall y^{\prime}\in f(y)
,
\forall z\in\mathcal{X}_{VF}
,
\forall z^{\prime}\in f(z)
:

(24)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}(x_{0})\geq 0,

(25)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}(x^{\prime})\geq A_{1}\mathbf{B}(x),

(26)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{B}(y)\geq 0)\implies(\mathbf{B}(y)-\mathbf{B}(y^{\prime})\geq 0),\text{ and}

(27)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{B}(z)\geq 0)\implies(\mathbf{B}(z)-\mathbf{B}(z^{\prime})-\eta\mathbf{1}_{k}\geq 0),

where the inequalities are element-wise.

Theorem 7

(VCBRFs imply persistence)
.

The existence of a VCBRF
\mathcal{B}_{i}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, satisfying conditions (
24
)-(
27
) for a system
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}_{0},f)
implies that the state sequences of the system visit the set
\mathcal{X}_{VF}
finitely often.

Proof.

Conditions (
24
) and (
25
) serve to overapproximate the reachable states of the system (similar to VBCs) and, inductively, we have
\mathbf{B}(x)\geq 0
over all reachable states
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
Consequently, from (
26
) and (
27
), we have
\mathbf{B}(y)-\mathbf{B}(y^{\prime})\geq 0
for reachable
y\in\mathcal{X}\backslash\mathcal{X}_{VF}
and
\mathbf{B}(z)-\mathbf{B}(z^{\prime})-\eta\mathbf{1}_{k}\geq 0
for reachable
z\in\mathcal{X}_{VF}
, respectively.
This means that as the system evolves, the functions do not increase for
x\in\mathcal{X}\backslash\mathcal{X}_{VF}
and are strictly decreasing for
x\in\mathcal{X}_{VF}
.
In order to maintain a lower bound of
0
for all functions over the reachable states, the functions cannot be decreasing infinitely as we continue visiting
\mathcal{X}_{VF}
.
Thus, the existence of a vector of functions satisfying conditions (
24
)-(
27
) serves as a proof that
\mathcal{X}_{VF}
is visited finitely often.
∎

Remark that in condition (
25
), there is a nonnegative matrix
A_{1}
relating
\mathbf{B}(x^{\prime})
and
\mathbf{B}(x)
.
However, such a matrix cannot be incorporated under conditions (
26
) and (
27
), since it would no longer guarantee a nonincreasing or, respectively, strictly decreasing behavior.
Employing a nonidentity matrix may breach these conditions and, as a result, could permit visiting
\mathcal{X}_{VF}
infinitely many times.

To verify whether a given system satisfies a desired LTL property, we use VCBRFs on the product
\mathcal{S}\otimes\mathcal{A}
as follows.

Definition 3.2

(VCBRF for LTL)
.

Consider a system
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}_{0},f)
and an NBA
\mathcal{A}=(\Sigma,\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}_{0},\delta,\mathcal{Q}_{F})
representing the complement of an LTL formula
\phi
.
A vector of functions
\mathbf{B}
consisting of
\mathcal{B}_{i}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Q}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, is a VCBRF for
\mathcal{S}\otimes\mathcal{A}
if
\exists\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
\exists A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{k\times k}
such that
\forall x\in\mathcal{X}
,
\forall x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
,
\forall x^{\prime}\in f(x)
and
\forall n\in\mathcal{Q}
,
\forall q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}
,
\forall q\in\mathcal{Q}\backslash\mathcal{Q}_{F}
,
\forall r\in\mathcal{Q}_{F}
,
\forall n^{\prime}\in\delta(n,\mathcal{L}(x))
,
\forall q^{\prime}\in\delta(q,\mathcal{L}(x))
and
\forall r^{\prime}\in\delta(r,\mathcal{L}(x))
:

(28)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}((x_{0},q_{0}))\geq 0,

(29)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}((x^{\prime},n^{\prime}))\geq A_{1}\mathbf{B}((x,n)),

(30)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{B}((x,q))\geq 0)\implies(\mathbf{B}((x,q))-\mathbf{B}((x^{\prime},q^{\prime}))\geq 0),

(31)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{B}((x,r))\geq 0)\implies(\mathbf{B}((x,r))-\mathbf{B}((x^{\prime},r^{\prime}))-\eta\mathbf{1}_{k}\geq 0),

where the inequalities are element-wise.

Theorem 8

(VCBRFs verify LTL)
.

Consider a system
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}_{0},f)
and an LTL formula
\phi
. Let NBA
\mathcal{A}
represent the complement of the specification, i.e,
\neg\phi
.
The existence of a VCBRF
\mathcal{B}_{i}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Q}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, satisfying conditions (
28
)-(
31
) over
\mathcal{S}\otimes\mathcal{A}
implies
\mathcal{S}\models_{\mathcal{L}}\phi
.

Proof.

Observe that a VCBRF that satisfies conditions (
28
)-(
31
) is a VCBRF for persistence over the product
\mathcal{S}\otimes\mathcal{A}
.
From Theorem
7
, we observe that the product system visits the accepting states only finitely often.
Thus, we infer that no trace of the system is present in the language of the NBA
\mathcal{A}
, thus verifying the LTL specification.
∎

We now introduce a notion of vector closure certificates (VCCs) and define them for safety, persistence, and general
\omega
-regular specifications.

Definition 3.3

(VCC for Safety)
.

Consider a system
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}_{0},f)
. A vector of
k
functions
\mathbf{T}
consisting of
\mathcal{T}_{i}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R},\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
is a VCC for
\mathcal{S}
with respect to a set of unsafe states
\mathcal{X}_{u}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
if
\exists\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
A\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{k\times k}
such that
\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X}
,
\forall x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
,
\forall x_{u}\in\mathcal{X}_{u}
,
\forall x^{\prime}\in f(x)
:

(32)

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}(x,x^{\prime})\geq 0,

(33)

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}(x,y)\geq A\mathbf{T}(x^{\prime},y),

(34)

\displaystyle\bigvee_{i=1}^{k}\big(\mathcal{T}_{i}(x_{0},x_{u})\leq-\eta\big),

where the inequalities are element-wise.

We now describe the utility of VCCs for safety.

Theorem 9

(VCCs imply Safety)
.

Consider a system
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}_{0},f)
and a set of unsafe states
\mathcal{X}_{u}
. The existence of a VCC that satisfies the conditions (
32
)-(
34
) implies its safety.

Proof.

Let
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
be an index such that
\mathcal{T}_{j}(x_{0},x_{u})\leq-\eta
(condition (
34
)). Moreover, from condition (
32
),
\mathcal{T}_{j}(x,x^{\prime})\geq 0
, and inductively, we get
\mathcal{T}_{j}(x^{\prime},y)\geq 0
. Since
A
is nonnegative,
A\mathbf{T}(x^{\prime},y)\geq 0
.
Then, if
\mathbf{T}(x,y)\geq A\mathbf{T}(x^{\prime},y)
,
\mathbf{T}(x,y)\geq 0
as in condition (
33
). From this we get
\mathcal{T}_{j}(x,y)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{ji}\mathcal{T}_{i}(x^{\prime},y)\geq 0
.
These three conditions are the conditions of CC for safety over
\mathcal{T}_{j}(\cdot,\cdot)
according to Definition
2.4
.
Thus, the proof of safety follows accordingly.
∎

Definition 3.4

(VCC for Persistence)
.

Consider a system
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}_{0},f)
. A vector of
k
functions
\mathbf{T}
consisting of
\mathcal{T}_{i}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R},\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
is a VCC for
\mathcal{S}
with a set of states
\mathcal{X}_{VF}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
that must be visited finitely often if
\exists\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
A\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{k\times k}
such that
\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X}
,
\forall x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
,
\forall x^{\prime}\in f(x)
,
\forall y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{VF}
:

(35)

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}(x,x^{\prime})\geq 0,

(36)

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}(x,y)\geq A\mathbf{T}(x^{\prime},y),

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{T}(x_{0},y^{\prime})\geq 0\big)\wedge\big(\mathbf{T}(y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime})\geq 0\big)\implies

(37)

\displaystyle\qquad\bigvee_{i=1}^{k}\big(\mathcal{T}_{i}(x_{0},y^{\prime\prime})\leq\mathcal{T}_{i}(x_{0},y^{\prime})-\eta\big),

where the inequalities are element-wise.

VCCs can be used to verify persistence, as formally stated below.

Theorem 10

(VCCs imply Persistence)
.

Consider a system
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}_{0},f)
. The existence of a VCC satisfying conditions (
35
)-(
3.4
) implies that the state sequences of the system visit the set
\mathcal{X}_{VF}
only finitely often.

Proof.

From conditions (
35
) and (
36
),
\mathcal{T}_{d}(x,x^{\prime})\geq 0
and
\mathcal{T}_{d}(x,y)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{di}\mathcal{T}_{i}(x^{\prime},y)\geq 0
, respectively, for all
d\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
.
Let
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
be an index such that
\big(\mathbf{T}(x_{0},y^{\prime})\geq 0\big)\wedge\big(\mathbf{T}(y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime})\geq 0\big)\implies\big(\mathcal{T}_{j}(x_{0},y^{\prime\prime})\leq\mathcal{T}_{j}(x_{0},y^{\prime})-\eta\big)
(condition (
3.4
)).
This implies that condition
\big[\land_{i\neq j}(\mathcal{T}_{i}(x_{0},y^{\prime})\geq 0)\wedge(\mathcal{T}_{i}(y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime})\geq 0)\big]\wedge\big(\mathcal{T}_{j}(x_{0},y^{\prime})\geq 0\big)\wedge\big(\mathcal{T}_{j}(y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime})\geq 0\big)\implies\big(\mathcal{T}_{j}(x_{0},y^{\prime\prime})\leq\mathcal{T}_{j}(x_{0},y^{\prime})-\eta\big)
is satisfied.
Now, consider
\mathcal{X}_{VF}=\cup_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{X}_{VF_{i}}
.
We will prove that
\mathcal{X}_{VF}
is visited finitely often by contradiction.
Take a state sequence
s=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots)\in\mathcal{X}^{\omega}
and assume that it visits
\mathcal{X}_{VF}
infinitely often.
Since
\mathcal{T}_{j}(x,x^{\prime})\geq 0
,
\mathcal{T}_{j}(x_{i},x_{i+1})\geq 0
for every
x_{i}\in s
.
Similarly, from
\mathcal{T}_{j}(x,y)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{ji}\mathcal{T}_{i}(x^{\prime},y)\geq 0
,
\mathcal{T}_{j}(x_{0},x_{i})\geq 0
, and
\mathcal{T}_{j}(x_{i},x_{\ell})\geq 0
for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
and every
\ell\geq i+1
.
Let
(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots)
be a subsequence of
s
that visits
\mathcal{X}_{VF}
only finitely often (i.e.,
s=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,y_{0},\ldots,y_{1},\ldots)
).
By Ramsey’s theorem
[
19
]
, there exists a subsequence
(z_{0},z_{1},\ldots)\in\mathcal{X}_{VF_{i}}
of
s
that visits
\mathcal{X}_{VF_{i}}
infinitely often for some
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
.
From the previous conclusions, we derive
\mathcal{T}_{j}(x_{0},z_{i})\geq 0
and
\mathcal{T}_{j}(z_{i},z_{\ell})\geq 0

\forall i\in\mathbb{N}
and
\forall\ell\geq i+1
.
Thus, we know that
\mathcal{T}_{j}(x_{0},z_{\ell})
is bounded below by 0.
From condition (
3.4
) and by induction, we have
\mathcal{T}_{j}(x_{0},z_{\ell})\leq\mathcal{T}_{j}(x_{0},z_{0})-\ell\eta,\forall\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
.
Therefore,
\exists\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathcal{T}_{j}(x_{0},z_{\ell})\leq\mathcal{T}_{j}(x_{0},z_{0})-\ell\eta<0
, which is a contradiction. Thus,
\mathcal{X}_{VF}
is visited finitely often.
∎

4.
Computation of Vector Certificates

This section presents an approach to synthesize VCBRFs and VCCs using sum-of-squares (SOS)
[
12
]
programming when the relevant sets of the system
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}_{0},f)
are semi-algebraic sets and the transition function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
is polynomial.
A set
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is semi-algebraic if it can be defined with a vector of polynomial inequalities
h(x)
as
Y=\{x\mid h(x)\geq 0\}
, where the inequalities are element-wise.
Moreover, to use SOS programming, we assume that the nonnegative matrix
A
is provided.
Lastly, to find VCCs, we first fix the template to be a linear combination of user-defined basis functions:

\mathcal{T}(x,y)=\mathbf{c}^{T}\mathbf{p}(x,y)=\sum_{m=1}^{n}c_{m}p_{m}(x,y),

where functions
p_{m}
are monomials over state variables
x
and
y
, and
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
are real coefficients.
For VCBRFs, the monomials
\mathbf{p}(x)
are only over the state variable
x
.

, we consider
\mathcal{X}_{u}=\cup_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{X}_{u_{j}}
and the sets
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{X}_{0}
, and
\mathcal{X}_{u_{j}}

,

\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
and

(43)

\displaystyle\hskip 100.00015pt\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,k\},

(44)

\displaystyle-\eta_{j}-\mathcal{T}_{j}(x_{0},x_{u})-\lambda_{C,j}^{T}(x_{0},x_{u})g_{C,j}(x_{0},x_{u}),

(45)

\displaystyle\hskip 100.00015pt\forall j\in\{1,\ldots,m\},

where
x^{\prime}=f(x)
,
\eta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
m\leq k
,

and multipliers

,
\lambda_{B,i}
,
\lambda_{C,j}
, are SOS polynomials over the state variable
x
, the state variables

, and the state variables

over the sets
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
, and
\mathcal{X}_{0}\times\mathcal{X}_{u_{j}}
, respectively.

4.2.
Persistence

Here, we will cover the conditions for both VCBRFs and VCC.
To search for VCBRFs as in Definition
3.1
, we consider the sets
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{X}_{0}
, and
\mathcal{X}_{VF}
to be semi-algebraic sets defined with the help of vectors of polynomial inequalities
g_{A}(x)
,
g_{0}(x)
, and
g_{B}(x)
, respectively.
As
\mathcal{X}_{VF}
is semi-algebraic,
\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{X}_{VF}
is also semi-algebraic with corresponding vector
g_{C}(x)
.
Then the search for a VCBRF, as in Definition
3.1
, reduces to showing that the following polynomials are SOS
\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
:

(46)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{i}(x_{0})-\lambda_{0,i}^{T}(x)g_{0}(x),

(47)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{i}(x^{\prime})-\sum_{j=1}^{k}A_{1_{ij}}\mathcal{B}_{j}(x)-\lambda_{A,i}^{T}(x)g_{A}(x),

(49)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{i}(z)-\mathcal{B}_{i}(z^{\prime})-\eta-\sum_{j=1}^{k}A_{3_{ij}}\mathcal{B}_{j}(z)-\lambda_{B,i}^{T}(z)g_{B}(z),

where
x^{\prime}=f(x)
,
y^{\prime}=f(y)
,
z^{\prime}=f(z)
,
\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{k\times k}
and the multipliers
\lambda_{A,i}
,
\lambda_{B,i}
,
\lambda_{C,j}
, are SOS polynomials over the state variable
x
,
z
and
y
over the sets
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{X}_{VF}
and
\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{X}_{VF}
, respectively.

To find VCCs as in Definition
3.4
, we consider
\mathcal{X}_{VF}=\cup_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{X}_{VF_{j}}
and the sets
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{X}_{0}
, and
\mathcal{X}_{VF_{j}}
to be semi-algebraic sets defined with the help of vectors of polynomial inequalities
g_{A}(x)
,
g_{0}(x)
, and
g_{VF,j}(x)
, respectively.
As these sets are semi-algebraic, the sets
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}_{0}\times\mathcal{X}_{VF_{j}}\times\mathcal{X}_{VF_{j}}
are semi-algebraic
with the corresponding vectors
g_{B}
and
g_{C,j}
, respectively.
Then the search for a VCC, as in Definition
3.4
, reduces to showing that the following polynomials are SOS:

(50)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{i}(x,x^{\prime})-\lambda_{A,i}^{T}(x)g_{A}(x),~~\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,k\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{i}(x,y)-\sum_{j=1}^{k}A_{ij}\mathcal{T}_{j}(x^{\prime},y)-\lambda_{B,i}^{T}(x,y)g_{B}(x,y),

\displaystyle\quad-\lambda_{C,j}^{T}(x_{0},x_{u})g_{C,j}(x_{0},x_{u}),~~\forall j\in\{1,\ldots,m\},

4.3.
LTL Specifications

For a given LTL specification, the relevant NBA has finitely many letters
\sigma\in\Sigma
. Without loss of generality, the set
\mathcal{X}
can be partitioned into finitely many partitions
\mathcal{X}_{\sigma_{1}},\ldots,\mathcal{X}_{\sigma_{p}}
, where
\forall x\in\mathcal{X}_{\sigma_{m}}
we have
\mathcal{L}(x)=\sigma_{m}
.
Given an element
\sigma_{m}\in\Sigma
, we can uniquely characterize the relation
\delta_{\sigma_{i}}
as
(q^{\prime}_{i},q_{i})\in\delta_{\sigma_{i}}
if and only if
q^{\prime}_{i}\in\delta(q_{i},\sigma_{i})
.
We assume that the sets
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{X}_{0}
, and
\mathcal{X}_{\sigma_{m}}

\forall\sigma_{m}
are semi-algebraic and characterized by polynomial vectors of inequalities
g(x)
,
g_{0}(x)
, and
g_{\sigma_{m},A}(x)
, respectively.
Similarly, we consider polynomial vectors of inequalities
g_{\sigma_{m},B}(x,y)
in the product space
\mathcal{X}_{\sigma_{m}}\times\mathcal{X}
and
g_{C,j}(x_{0},y,y^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{X}_{0}\times\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
.

The search for VCBRF can be reduced to showing that
\forall x\in\mathcal{X}
,
\forall x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
, and
\forall n\in\mathcal{Q}
,
\forall q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}
,
\forall q\in\mathcal{Q}\backslash\mathcal{Q}_{F}
,
\forall r\in\mathcal{Q}_{F}
,
\forall n^{\prime}\in\delta(n,\mathcal{L}(x))
,
\forall q^{\prime}\in\delta(q,\mathcal{L}(x))
and
\forall r^{\prime}\in\delta(r,\mathcal{L}(x))
, the following polynomials are SOS
\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
:

(53)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{i}^{(q_{0})}(x_{0})-\lambda_{0,i}^{T}(x_{0})g_{0}(x_{0}),

(54)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{i}^{(n^{\prime})}(x^{\prime})-\sum_{j=1}^{k}A_{1_{ij}}\mathcal{B}_{j}^{(n)}(x)-\lambda_{\sigma_{m},A,i}^{T}(x)g_{\sigma_{m},A}(x),

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{i}^{(q)}(x)-\mathcal{B}_{i}^{(q^{\prime})}(x^{\prime})-\sum_{j=1}^{k}A_{2_{ij}}\mathcal{B}_{j}^{(q)}(x)

(55)

\displaystyle\quad-\bar{\lambda}_{\sigma_{m},A,i}^{T}(x)g_{\sigma_{m},A}(x),

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{i}^{(r)}(x)-\mathcal{B}_{i}^{(r^{\prime})}(x^{\prime})-\eta-\sum_{j=1}^{k}A_{3_{ij}}\mathcal{B}_{j}^{(r)}(x)

(56)

\displaystyle\quad-\hat{\lambda}_{\sigma_{m},A,i}^{T}(x)g_{\sigma_{m},A}(x),

where
x^{\prime}=f(x)
,
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{k\times k}
,
\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, and
\lambda_{0,i}
,
\lambda_{\sigma_{m},A,i}
,
\bar{\lambda}_{\sigma_{m},A,i}
, and
\hat{\lambda}_{\sigma_{m},A,i}
are SOS polynomials of appropriate dimensions over
\mathcal{X}_{0}
and
\mathcal{X}_{\sigma_{m}}
.
Remark that the superscripts
(u)
for each function
\mathcal{B}_{i}
above denote automata states.

Now, we can reduce the search for a VCC to showing that
\forall x,y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
,
\forall n,p\in\mathcal{Q}
,
\forall q_{0}\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}
,
\forall r\in{\mathcal{Q}_{F}}
, and
\forall\sigma_{m}\in\Sigma
, such that
n^{\prime}\in\delta_{\sigma_{m}}(n)
, the following polynomials are SOS:

(57)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{i}^{(n,n^{\prime})}(x,x^{\prime})-\lambda^{T}_{\sigma_{m},A,i}(x)g_{\sigma_{m},A}(x),~~\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,k\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{i}^{(n,p)}(x,y)-\sum_{j=1}^{k}A_{ij}\mathcal{T}_{j}^{(n^{\prime},p)}(x^{\prime},y)

(58)

\displaystyle\quad-\lambda^{T}_{\sigma_{m},B,i}(x,y)g_{\sigma_{m},B}(x,y),~~\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,k\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{j}^{(q_{0},r)}(x_{0},y)-\eta_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\big(\gamma_{i}\mathcal{T}_{i}^{(q_{0},r)}(x_{0},y)+\rho_{i}\mathcal{T}_{i}^{(r,r)}(y,y^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle\quad-\mathcal{T}_{j}^{(q_{0},r)}(x_{0},y^{\prime})-\lambda^{T}_{C,j}(x_{0},y,y^{\prime})g_{C,j}(x_{0},y,y^{\prime}),

(59)

\displaystyle\hskip 100.00015pt\forall j\in\{1,\ldots,|\mathcal{Q}_{F}|\},

where
x^{\prime}=f(x)
,
\lambda_{\sigma_{m},A,i}
,
\lambda_{\sigma_{m},B,i}
, and
\lambda_{\sigma_{m},C,j}
are SOS polynomials of appropriate dimensions over their respective regions,
A\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{k\times k}
,
|\mathcal{Q}_{F}|\leq k
,
\eta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, and
\gamma_{i},\rho_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Note that the
(u,v)
superscripts for each function
\mathcal{T}_{i}
above denote automata state pairs.

A common tool to search for SOS polynomial certificates is to use solvers such as
[
23
]
.
The complexity of determining whether the above equations are SOS is
O\big(k\binom{2n+d}{d}^{2}\big)

[
12
]
, when searching for VCCs for safety or persistence, where
n
is the dimension of the set of states and
2d
is the degree of the polynomial.
The complexity of verifying LTL specifications is
O\big(k|\mathcal{Q}|^{2}\binom{2n+d}{d}^{2}\big)
, where
|\mathcal{Q}|
indicates the number of automata states
[
9
]
.
By allowing lower degrees of functions to act as certificates in the vector formulation, we incur a linear complexity in
k
, while alleviating the polynomial complexity due to the degree
2d
.
Thus, one may use existing techniques to reduce the computational burden in the search for such certificates.
For VCBRFs, the term
\binom{2n+d}{d}
is replaced by
\binom{n+d}{d}
, making the computation for VCBRFs relatively easier and faster, especially for systems of higher dimensions.

6.
Conclusion

We proposed two notions of vector certificates (VCBRFs and VCCs) that relax the standard conditions by finding multiple functions that together verify
\omega
-regular specifications of a given dynamical system.
We computed these certificates using SOS programming and demonstrated their potential computational advantages.
Additionally, we present the benefit of VCCs over VCBRFs when the sets of interest are unions of multiple sets.
In future work, we plan to investigate how to effectively address bilinear conditions without having to experiment with the nonnegative matrix
A
and potentially extend the formulation using a state-dependent nonnegative matrix
