1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
denote the finite field of order
q
. In a series of papers published in 1985, the author
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
proved that, provided
q\neq 2,3,7
, the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
contains a consecutive pair of primitive elements. Many related existence results have since been obtained.

A natural complementary question is whether there always exists a consecutive pair of
non-primitive
elements. When
q
is odd, this question requires some qualification: exactly half of the non-zero elements of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
are squares, and hence already non-primitive. It is easy to see that, for
q>3
, there exist consecutive non-squares. The deeper question is whether there exists a consecutive pair of elements that are both non-square and non-primitive.

From now on, assume
q
is an odd prime power and define

\theta_{q}:=\frac{\phi(q-1)}{q-1}.

We call an element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
NSNP if it is both a non-square and non-primitive. An NSNP pair is a pair of
consecutive
NSNP elements.

If
\theta_{q}=1/2
, then every non-square is primitive; this occurs precisely when
q
is a Fermat prime or
q=9
. In these cases, no NSNP elements (and therefore no NSNP pairs) exist. Thus some restriction on
\theta_{q}
is necessary.

For prime fields
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, the problem has been studied in terms of congruences modulo
p
. By elementary methods, Gun, Ramakrishnan, Sahu, and Thangadurai
[
8
]
proved that if
\theta_{p}<1/6
, then
\mathbb{F}_{p}
contains an NSNP pair. Using more elaborate sieving methods, Jarso and Trudgian
[
9
]
improved this to the weaker condition
\theta_{p}\leq 1/4
. Previously, Gun, Luca, Rath, Sahu, and Thangadurai
[
7
]
had shown via character sum estimates, as a particular case of a more general result, that for sufficiently large primes
p
, an NSNP pair exists whenever
\theta_{p}<1/2-\varepsilon
. When
\varepsilon=1/6
(so that
\theta_{p}<1/3
), the argument in
[
7
]
appears to require a bound as large as
p>10^{430}
to guarantee the existence of an NSNP pair.

We call an element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}

NSNP
if it is both a non-square and non-primitive. An
NSNP pair
is a pair of consecutive NSNP elements.

If
\theta_{q}=1/2
, then every non-square is primitive; this occurs precisely when
q
is a Fermat prime or
q=9
. In these cases, no NSNP elements (and therefore no NSNP pairs) exist. Thus some restriction on
\theta_{q}
is necessary.

Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
q
be an odd prime power and define

\theta_{q}:=\frac{\phi(q-1)}{q-1}.

If
\theta_{q}<\tfrac{1}{3}
, then
\mathbb{F}_{q}
contains a pair of consecutive elements that are both non-square and non-primitive.

If
\theta_{q}=\tfrac{1}{3}
, then the same conclusion holds except when

q\in\{7,13,19,25,37\}.

To establish Theorem
1.1
, we study a more general problem. Let
\ell
be an odd prime dividing
q-1
. We say that an element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is an
NS
\ell
element
if it is both a non-square and an
\ell
th power, and define an
NS
\ell
pair
analogously. Clearly, every NS
\ell
pair is an NSNP pair.

We prove the following general result, in which
\ell
is taken to be the least odd prime divisor of
q-1
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
q
be an odd prime power such that
\theta_{q}\leq\tfrac{1}{3}
, and let
\ell
be the least odd prime divisor of
q-1
. Then, except when
\ell=3
and

q\in\{7,13,19,25,37,43\},

the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
contains an NS
\ell
pair.

Remark 1.1
.

The exceptional values of
q
in Theorem
1.1
correspond precisely to those for which
\theta_{q}=\tfrac{1}{3}
and no NSNP pair exists.

Remark 1.2
.

From Theorem
1.1
,
\theta_{q}=1/3
represents a critical boundary value. All the exceptional prime powers
q
have this value of
\theta_{q}
.

To interpret the hypotheses, observe that
\theta_{q}
is the proportion of primitive elements in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.
Since exactly half of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
consists of non-squares, the proportion of NSNP elements is

\frac{1}{2}-\theta_{q}.

Thus:

•

\theta_{q}<1/6
(as in
[
8
]
) implies NSNP elements are at least twice as numerous as primitive elements;

•

\theta_{q}\leq 1/4
(as in
[
9
]
) implies NSNP elements are at least as numerous as primitive elements;

•

\theta_{q}\leq 1/3
(this paper) implies NSNP elements are at least half as numerous as primitive elements.

The existence problem for NSNP pairs is therefore comparable in difficulty to the existence of consecutive primitive pairs. However, primitive elements have a simpler multiplicative structure, and (lower-bound) sieve methods apply more naturally to them. For NSNPs, appropriate upper-bound sieves seem not to be available.

It is therefore natural to fix a prime
\ell\mid(q-1)
and study the existence of consecutive pairs of elements that are both non-squares and
\ell
th powers.
We call such a pair an
NS
\ell
pair
.
We first treat general
\ell
, and then specialise to the key small primes
\ell=3
and
\ell=5
.

Remark 1.3
.

For later reference, we record which relevant values of
\theta_{q}
occur for odd prime powers
q
:

•

\theta_{q}=1/2
if and only if
q
is a Fermat prime or
q=9
;

•

\theta_{q}=1/3
if and only if
q-1=2^{a}3^{b}
with
a,b\geq 1
;

•

\theta_{q}\neq 1/4
and
\theta_{q}\neq 1/6
, since neither
4
nor
6
is the Euler totient of any integer of the form
q-1
with
q
an odd prime power.

2.
A lower bound for the number of NS
\ell
pairs

Let
q
be an odd prime power, and let
\ell
be an odd prime dividing
q-1
. Since
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
is cyclic of order
q-1
, there exists a multiplicative character
\eta
of exact order
\ell
. Let
\lambda
denote the quadratic character of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. Extend both
\lambda
and
\eta
to
\mathbb{F}_{q}
by setting
\lambda(0)=0
and
\eta(0)=0
. With this convention, all character sums over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
are well-defined (see
[
2
]
).

We seek a lower bound for

N_{\ell}(q)=\#\{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}:\alpha,\alpha+1\text{ are both non-squares and $\ell$th powers}\}.

Define the characteristic function of elements of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
that are both non-squares and
\ell
th powers as follows:

f_{\ell}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2\ell}\,(1-\lambda(\alpha))\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\eta^{i}(\alpha).

It follows that

N_{\ell}(q)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,-1\}}f_{\ell}(\alpha)f_{\ell}(\alpha+1).

The exclusion of
\{0,-1\}
is harmless, since
f_{\ell}(0)=0
and
f_{\ell}(\alpha+1)=0
when
\alpha=-1
.

Expanding
f_{\ell}(\alpha)f_{\ell}(\alpha+1)
gives

N_{\ell}(q)=\frac{1}{4\ell^{2}}\sum_{i,j=0}^{\ell-1}S(i,j),

where

(2.1)

S(i,j)=J(\eta^{i},\eta^{j})-\lambda(-1)J(\lambda\eta^{i},\eta^{j})-J(\eta^{i},\lambda\eta^{j})+\lambda(-1)J(\lambda\eta^{i},\lambda\eta^{j}).

Here the Jacobi sum is defined by

J(\chi_{1},\chi_{2})=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\chi_{1}(\alpha)\chi_{2}(1-\alpha).

This identity follows from the change of variables
\beta=-\alpha
, which transforms sums of the form
\sum\chi_{1}(\alpha)\chi_{2}(\alpha+1)
into Jacobi sums, introducing factors of
\chi_{1}(-1)
that account for the coefficients above.

We now evaluate or bound each contribution
S(i,j)
using standard facts about Jacobi sums: if
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
are nontrivial and
\chi_{1}\chi_{2}=1
, then
J(\chi_{1},\chi_{2})=-1
, while otherwise
|J(\chi_{1},\chi_{2})|=\sqrt{q}
.

The block
S(0,0)
.

Since
\eta^{0}=1
, we have

S(0,0)=\sum_{\alpha\neq 0,-1}(1-\lambda(\alpha))(1-\lambda(\alpha+1)).

Expanding,

S(0,0)=\sum_{\alpha\neq 0,-1}1-\sum_{\alpha\neq 0,-1}\lambda(\alpha)-\sum_{\alpha\neq 0,-1}\lambda(\alpha+1)+\sum_{\alpha\neq 0,-1}\lambda(\alpha)\lambda(\alpha+1).

These sums evaluate as follows:

\sum_{\alpha\neq 0,-1}1=q-2,

\sum_{\alpha\neq 0,-1}\lambda(\alpha)=-\lambda(-1),\quad\sum_{\alpha\neq 0,-1}\lambda(\alpha+1)=-1,

and

\sum_{\alpha\neq 0,-1}\lambda(\alpha)\lambda(\alpha+1)=-1.

Hence

S(0,0)=q-2+\lambda(-1).

The blocks
S(0,j)
,
1\leq j\leq\ell-1
.

Using
J(1,\eta^{j})=-1
and
J(1,\lambda\eta^{j})=-1
, while the remaining Jacobi sums have absolute value
\sqrt{q}
, we obtain

|S(0,j)|\leq 2\sqrt{q}.

Summing over
j
gives a total contribution bounded by

2(\ell-1)\sqrt{q}.

The blocks
S(i,0)
,
1\leq i\leq\ell-1
.

We have

J(\eta^{i},1)=-1,\quad J(\lambda\eta^{i},1)=-1,

so that

J(\eta^{i},1)-\lambda(-1)J(\lambda\eta^{i},1)=\lambda(-1)-1.

Summing over
i
yields a constant contribution

(\ell-1)(\lambda(-1)-1).

The remaining two Jacobi sums each have absolute value
\sqrt{q}
, giving an error term
E_{1}
with

|E_{1}|\leq 2(\ell-1)\sqrt{q}.

The blocks with
1\leq i,j\leq\ell-1
and
i+j\equiv 0\pmod{\ell}
.

There are
\ell-1
such pairs. In this case,

J(\eta^{i},\eta^{-i})=-1,\quad J(\lambda\eta^{i},\lambda\eta^{-i})=-\lambda(-1),

while the remaining two Jacobi sums are bounded by
\sqrt{q}
. Thus the total contribution is

-2(\ell-1)+E_{2},\quad|E_{2}|\leq 2(\ell-1)\sqrt{q}.

The remaining blocks.

For the
(\ell-1)(\ell-2)
pairs with
1\leq i,j\leq\ell-1
and
i+j\not\equiv 0\pmod{\ell}
, none of the products of characters is trivial, so each Jacobi sum has absolute value
\sqrt{q}
. Hence the total contribution is bounded by

4(\ell-1)(\ell-2)\sqrt{q}.

Collecting all contributions, we obtain

\sum_{i,j}S(i,j)\geq q-(\ell-2)(1+\lambda(-1))-3-4(\ell-1)^{2}\sqrt{q}.

Substituting into the expansion for
N_{\ell}(q)
yields the following result.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\ell
be an odd prime dividing
q-1
. Then

(2.2)

N_{\ell}(q)\geq\frac{q-4(\ell-1)^{2}\sqrt{q}-(\ell-2)(1+\lambda(-1))-3}{4\ell^{2}}.

A weaker (but simpler) criterion, obtained from Theorem
2.1
by ignoring the constant term (which is negative) and replacing
(\ell-1)^{2}
by the greater
\ell^{2}
is the following corollary.

Corollary 2.2
.

Suppose
\ell
is an odd prime divisor of
q-1
such that
q>16\ell^{4}
. Then
\mathbb{F}_{q}
contains an NS
\ell
pair.

For illustration purposes, we append Table
1
to demonstrate the application of Theorem
2.1
and Corollary
2.2
for the first few odd prime values of
\ell
(although in the sequel we only require the first couple of rows).

In the table, for each prime
\ell
dividing
q-1
listed, the first column gives the minimum value of
q
for which
N_{\ell}(q)
is bound to be positive (as guaranteed by Theorem

). The second column gives the minimum value of
q

In column 3, it is assumed that

3.
The existence of NS
\ell
pairs for
\ell\geq 7

We begin by separating the case in which
\ell=7
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
q
be an odd prime power such the least odd prime divisor of
q-1
is
7
and that
\theta_{q}\leq 1/3
. Then
\mathbb{F}_{q}
contains an
NS7
pair.

Proof.

As hypothesised, suppose that
q
is such that
\ell=7\mid(q-1)
but
3\nmid(q-1)
and
5\nmid(q-1)
, and also that
\theta_{q}\leq 1/3
. Let
\omega_{q}
denote the number of distinct prime divisors of
q-1
.
If
\omega_{q}\leq 5
, then

\theta_{q}\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{6}{7}\cdot\frac{10}{11}\cdot\frac{12}{13}\cdot\frac{16}{17}>0.338.

Hence
\omega_{q}\geq 6
and

\theta_{q}\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{6}{7}\cdot\frac{10}{11}\cdot\frac{12}{13}\cdot\frac{16}{17}\cdot\frac{18}{19}>0.3206.

It follows that

q\geq 2\cdot 7\cdot 11\cdot 13\cdot 17\cdot 19+1=646,647>16\ell^{4},

and the result holds by Corollary
2.2
(as in the third row of Table
1
).
∎

We have to devise an argument that suffices for a general prime
\ell\geq 11
even though a similar conclusion to that of Theorem
3.1
is “obvious” for any specific prime.

To achieve this, let the odd primes in increasing order be denoted
3=\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\dots
, so that
\ell=\ell_{r}
for some
r\geq 3
. For
r\geq 1
, define the sum
S_{r}
, the product
P_{r}
, and the expression
\Phi_{r}
by

S_{r}=\sum_{i=r}^{2r}\frac{1}{\ell_{i}},\qquad P_{r}=\prod_{i=r}^{2r}\ell_{i},\qquad\Phi_{r}=\prod_{i=r}^{2r}\Big(1-\frac{1}{\ell_{i}}\Big).

Lemma 3.2
.

The sequence
S_{r}
is decreasing.

Proof.

S_{r}-S_{r+1}=\frac{1}{\ell_{r}}-\frac{1}{\ell_{2r+1}}-\frac{1}{\ell_{2r+2}}>\frac{1}{\ell_{r}}-\frac{2}{\ell_{2r+1}}>\frac{1}{\ell_{r}}-\frac{2}{\ell_{r+1}}>0,

since
\ell_{r+1}\leq 2\ell_{r}
by (the proof of) Bertrand’s Postulate.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

For
r\geq 4
and
\ell=\ell_{r}\geq 11
, the following inequalities hold:

2P_{r}>16\ell^{4}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{1}{2}\Phi_{r}>\frac{1}{3}.

Proof.

Each factor in
P_{r}
is at least
\ell
, so
P_{r}\geq\ell^{r+1}\geq\ell^{5}
since
r\geq 4
. Hence
2P_{r}\geq 2\ell^{5}>16\ell^{4}
.

Next, using
\prod(1-a_{i})\geq 1-\sum a_{i}
for
0\leq a_{i}<1
, we have
\Phi_{r}\geq 1-S_{r}\geq 1-S_{4}
, by Lemma
3.2
. Since
\ell_{8}=19
, it follows that

\frac{1}{2}\Phi_{r}\geq\frac{1}{2}(1-S_{4})>0.36>\frac{1}{3}.

∎

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
q
be an odd prime power such that
\theta_{q}\leq 1/3
, and suppose
\ell\geq 11
is the least odd prime divisor of
q-1
. Then
\mathbb{F}_{q}
contains an NS
\ell
pair.

Proof.

Set
\ell=\ell_{r}
for some
r\geq 4
, and let
k_{r},\dots,k_{s}
denote the odd prime factors of
q-1
in increasing order. Since the
i
th smallest odd prime divisor of
q-1
is not less than the
i
th smallest odd prime not less than
\ell
, i.e.,
k_{i}\geq\ell_{i}
, we have

\frac{1}{2}\prod_{i=r}^{s}\Big(1-\frac{1}{\ell_{i}}\Big)\leq\frac{1}{2}\prod_{i=r}^{s}\Big(1-\frac{1}{k_{i}}\Big)=\theta_{q}\leq\frac{1}{3}.

By Lemma
3.3
, it follows that
s>2r
, so
q>2P_{r}>16\ell^{4}
. Applying Corollary
2.2
guarantees the existence of an NS
\ell
pair.
∎

4.
The number of NS
\ell
pairs as a cyclotomic number

In Section
2
, we derived lower bounds for
\mathrm{NS}_{\ell}(q)
based primarily on the fact that Jacobi sums have absolute value
\sqrt{q}
whenever neither of the characters involved, nor their product, is trivial. This resolves the problem for prime power values of
q
when
\ell
, the least odd prime divisor of
q-1
, satisfies
\ell\geq 7
.

It remains to examine a finite set of prime powers
q
in the cases
\ell=3
or
5
. In these instances, it is natural to investigate whether the Jacobi sum method can be refined further, particularly since explicit evaluations are known for Jacobi sums of orders
6
and
10
. See, for example, Chapters 3 and 11 of
[
2
]
, and in particular Theorems 11.6 and 11.7.

However, the author was unable to adapt the Jacobi sum approach effectively to exploit these explicit expressions in order to compute
N_{3}(q)
or
N_{5}(q)
, nor even to obtain stronger lower bounds than those given in (
2.2
) that would demonstrate the positivity of these quantities in general.

There is, however, an alternative, closely related and natural approach, namely to take advantage of existing tables of cyclotomic numbers of orders 6 and 10. Classically, such tables go back to Gauss and Jacobi themselves, through Dickson
[
6
]
and to Whiteman
[
11
]
, Table 2, and
[
12
]
as well as many others.

Given
any
odd prime
\ell
dividing
q-1
and a primitive element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, the cyclotomic numbers of order
2\ell
are defined as follows. For all pairs
(i,j),0\leq i,j\leq\ell-1
, the cyclotomic number
(i,j)_{2\ell}
counts the number of
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that both
\alpha/\gamma^{i}
and
(\alpha+1)/\gamma^{j}
are non-zero
2\ell
th powers in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose
\ell
is an odd prime divisor of
q-1
. Then
NS_{\ell}(q)=(\ell,\ell)_{2\ell}
.

Proof.

To be counted by
(\ell,\ell)_{2\ell}
, a pair
(\alpha,\alpha+1)
must be such that
\alpha=\gamma^{\ell}\beta^{2\ell}
for some
\beta\neq 0
. Set
\beta=\gamma^{s}
. Then the index of
\alpha
with respect to the generator
\gamma
is
\ell(1+2s)
which is odd (so
\alpha
is not a square), yet divisible by
\ell
(so
\alpha
is an
\ell
th power). The same is true for
\alpha+1
and conversely.
∎

As we have indicated, tables for
(\ell,\ell)_{2\ell}
for
\ell=3,5
exist but can be elusive to locate. One problem is that although
(\ell.\ell)_{2\ell}
is an invariant of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, the particular formula to apply depends on the choice of primitive element and the index of the element
2
with respect to that generator. In principle, for a given
q
it is less effort to choose
\gamma
, find the index of
2
and choose the appropriate formula from a table that search directly through all the non-squares and
\ell
th powers in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
but, in practice, with the convenience of computation nowadays, it is probably as reasonable to adopt the latter approach.

5.
The existence of NS3 pairs

,

Let
q
be an odd prime power with
\ell=3\mid(q-1)
, so
q\equiv 1\pmod{6}
. In this case
the restriction
\theta_{q}\leq 1/3
is automatically fulfilled. Let
N_{3}(q)
denote the
number of NS3 pairs in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

From Theorem
2.1
with
\ell=3
,
N_{3}(q)>0
whenever
q\geq 269
(see Table
1
), so it suffices to consider smaller values of
q
.
There are 29 such prime power values, namely those in the following set:

\displaystyle Q=\{\,

\displaystyle 7,13,19,25,31,37,43,49,61,67,73,79,97,103,109,121,127,139,151,157,163,

\displaystyle 169,181,193,199,211,223,229,241\,\}\,

As it happens we can show the existence of an NS3 pair in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
quite simply when
q
is a Mersenne prime
q=2^{m}-1
, where
m
is a prime exceeding 3.
In particular, this applies to the members 31, 127 of
Q
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose
q>7
is a Mersenne prime . Then
(q-2,q-1)=(-2,-1)
is an
NS3
pair.

Proof.

Write
q=2^{m}-1
, where
m>3
is prime. Evidently
q\equiv 3(\pmod{4})
and so
-1
is a non-square and consequently an NS3 element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Hence -1 is an NS3 element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Moreover, in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
2^{m}=1
, i.e., 2 has order
m
and so index

\frac{q-1}{m}=\frac{2(2^{m-1}-1)}{m}

in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Hence 2 is a square and therefore -2 is a non-square. Also 3 divides
2^{m-1}-1
and therefore 3 divides
(2^{m-1}-1)/m
since
m>3
.
Thus 2 is a cube and so -2 is a cube. Hence
(-2,-1)
is an NS3 pair.
∎

For all the simplicity of computation, it is instructive to use formulae to derive the values of
N_{3}(q)
for
q\in Q
. The particular reference we shall use is Theorem 2 of
[
1
]
(with its accompanying tables) because its validity extends the to non-prime fields. Tables 1 and 2 (in
[
1
]
) list the various expressions for
36(3,3)
that we need. We use
t
to denote the index of
2
with respect to the primitive element
\gamma

Question 7.1
.

In this paper we have throughout considered only odd prime powers in which situation it is natural to partition the set of non-primitive elements of
\mathbb{F}_{q}

At one extreme, when
