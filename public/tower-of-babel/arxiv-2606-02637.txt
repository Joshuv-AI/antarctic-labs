The Inverted Dirac-Moshinsky Oscillator in ( 1 + 1 ) (1+1) Dimensions
Kevin Hernández, Marcos Iraheta-Orellana, Wiliam Larín-Escobar

Abstract
We derive and analyze the exact solutions of the inverted Dirac-Moshinsky oscillator (IDMO) in ( 1 + 1 ) (1+1) dimensions, obtained from the standard model via the substitution p → p + i ​ m ​ ω ​ β ​ x p\to p+im\omega\beta x . The upper spinor component satisfies a Weber equation with complex spectral parameter λ = ( E 2 − m 2 ) / ( 2 ​ m ​ ω ) + i / 2 \lambda=(E^{2}-m^{2})/(2m\omega)+i/2 , whose solutions are parabolic cylinder functions D ν ​ ( ξ ) D_{\nu}(\xi) with complex order ν = λ − 1 / 2 \nu=\lambda-1/2 . The physical spectrum is purely continuous ( | E | > m |E|>m ), with no discrete bound states. Three normalization schemes are developed, and the discrete Gamow resonances at E n ± = ± m 2 + ( 2 ​ n + 1 ) ​ m ​ ω − i ​ m ​ ω E_{n}^{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{m^{2}+(2n+1)m\omega-im\omega} are identified as poles of the resolvent. The negative-energy sector describes antiparticle anti-resonances whose positive imaginary part signals vacuum instability and spontaneous pair production, analogous to the Schwinger effect. The algebraic structure is governed by the principal series of S ​ U ​ ( 1 , 1 ) SU(1,1) , and the Hamiltonian is 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{PT} -symmetric with unbroken symmetry for | E | > m |E|>m .

1
Introduction

The Dirac-Moshinsky oscillator (DMO), introduced by Moshinsky
and Szczepaniak in 1989
[
11
]
, is among the
most celebrated exactly solvable models in relativistic quantum
mechanics.
Defined through the nonminimal substitution

\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{p}-im\omega\beta\mathbf{r}

in the free Dirac equation, the DMO produces, in the
nonrelativistic limit, the conventional harmonic oscillator
supplemented by a strong spin-orbit coupling term.
Its exact solvability, rich algebraic structure, and
connections to diverse areas of physics have motivated
an extensive literature spanning supersymmetric quantum
mechanics
[
15
]
, the Jaynes-Cummings model
of quantum optics
[
5
]
,
graphene physics
[
15
]
, quantum chromodynamics,
and topological defects.

The mathematical structure of the standard DMO is thoroughly
understood: its spectrum is discrete, the Hamiltonian is
essentially self-adjoint, and its eigenfunctions form a
complete orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
The underlying symmetry algebra is
SU(1,1)\oplus SO(2,1)
,
which accounts for the characteristic supersymmetric
pattern of the energy spectrum
[
15
]
.

In contrast, the
inverted
Dirac-Moshinsky oscillator
(IDMO), obtained through the replacement
\omega\to i\omega

(or equivalently
\mathbf{p}\to\mathbf{p}+im\omega\beta\mathbf{r}
),
has received considerably less attention despite its physical
relevance.
At the level of the effective potential, this analytic
continuation converts the confining harmonic interaction into
an inverted harmonic oscillator, radically altering the
spectral and dynamical properties of the system.
Instead of discrete bound states, one encounters resonant
solutions, non-normalizable states, and a continuous spectrum,
phenomena that require tools beyond the standard Hilbert
space formulation.

The inverted harmonic oscillator occupies a central role in
modern theoretical physics.
It appears in the description of unstable quantum
systems
[
2
]
, tunneling phenomena, cosmological
particle production
[
14
]
, black-hole
physics
[
10
]
, quantum chaos and
complexity
[
9
]
, and the theory of Gamow
vectors.
In all these contexts, the non-normalizable eigenstates of the
inverted potential require the framework of Rigged Hilbert
Spaces (RHS), or Gel’fand triplets
[
7
]
,

\Phi\subset\mathcal{H}\subset\Phi^{\times}\,,

(1)

where generalized eigenvectors may be consistently defined
as continuous functionals over an appropriate test space

\Phi
.

The purpose of the present work is to provide a complete,
self-contained derivation of the IDMO in
(1+1)
dimensions,
establishing its exact solutions, spectral properties, and
algebraic structure.
We derive the parabolic cylinder equation satisfied by the
upper spinor component, obtain the general solution in terms
of
D_{\nu}(z)
with complex order
\nu
, and discuss the
spectral interpretation within the RHS framework.
The results presented here lay the groundwork for further
investigations of the IDMO in the context of

\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric quantum mechanics
[
3
,
4
]
, non-Hermitian field theories, and relativistic
scattering in external fields.

The paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
defines the IDMO Hamiltonian
in
(1+1)
dimensions.
Section
3
sets up the eigenvalue problem.
Section
4
derives the second-order
differential equation for the upper component.
Section
5
reduces it to the Weber
equation.
Section
6
presents the exact solution.
Section
7
analyzes the spectral properties.
Section 8 derives the energy values, normalization schemes,
and explicit wave functions.
Section 9 discusses the algebraic structure.
Section 10 summarizes the results.

2
The Hamiltonian

In
(1+1)
dimensions, we work in units
c=\hbar=1
and
adopt the standard representation of the Dirac matrices:

\alpha=\sigma_{x}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\beta=\sigma_{z}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},

(2)

satisfying
\{\alpha,\beta\}=0
,
\alpha^{2}=\beta^{2}=\mathbf{1}
.

The inverted Dirac-Moshinsky oscillator is defined by the
Hamiltonian

H_{\mathrm{IDMO}}=\alpha p+\beta m+im\omega\,\beta\alpha\,x\,,

(3)

or equivalently through the nonminimal substitution

p\;\longrightarrow\;p+im\omega\beta x\,,

(4)

applied to the free Dirac Hamiltonian
H_{0}=\alpha p+\beta m
.
In matrix form:

H_{\mathrm{IDMO}}=\begin{pmatrix}m&p+im\omega x\\
p-im\omega x&-m\end{pmatrix},

(5)

where
p=-i\partial_{x}
.

The standard DMO corresponds to the substitution

p\to p-im\omega\beta x
, which yields a matrix with
the signs of the
\pm im\omega x
terms interchanged.
The IDMO is therefore related to the DMO by the analytic
continuation
\omega\to i\omega
, or equivalently by
the transformation
x\to ix
at fixed
\omega
.

Remark 1
.

The operator
p+im\omega x
is
not
Hermitian:

(p+im\omega x)^{\dagger}=p-im\omega x\neq p+im\omega x
.
Consequently,
H_{\mathrm{IDMO}}
is not self-adjoint on the
standard Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\oplus L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
The appropriate framework for its spectral theory is
discussed in Section
7
.

3
Eigenvalue Problem

The stationary Dirac equation

H_{\mathrm{IDMO}}\,\psi=E\,\psi
,
with two-component spinor

\psi(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}(x)\\
\psi_{2}(x)\end{pmatrix}
,
yields the coupled system:

\displaystyle(m-E)\,\psi_{1}+(p+im\omega x)\,\psi_{2}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(6)

\displaystyle(p-im\omega x)\,\psi_{1}+(-m-E)\,\psi_{2}

\displaystyle=0\,.

(7)

From (
7
), the lower component can be expressed
as

\psi_{2}=\frac{1}{m+E}(p-im\omega x)\,\psi_{1}\,,

(8)

valid for
E\neq-m
(non-degenerate case).

4
Second-Order Differential Equation

Substituting (
8
) into (
6
) and
evaluating the operator product

(p+im\omega x)(p-im\omega x)
,
we use the canonical commutation relation
[p,x]=-i

to obtain

(p+im\omega x)(p-im\omega x)=p^{2}+m^{2}\omega^{2}x^{2}+im\omega[p,x]=p^{2}+m^{2}\omega^{2}x^{2}+m\omega\,.

(9)

Combining with (
6
), the second-order
equation for the upper component is

\frac{d^{2}\psi_{1}}{dx^{2}}+\left[\left(E^{2}-m^{2}\right)+m^{2}\omega^{2}x^{2}+im\omega\right]\psi_{1}=0\,.

(10)

Three features of this equation merit comment.
First, the term
+m^{2}\omega^{2}x^{2}
has the
opposite sign

to the standard DMO, where the corresponding term is

-m^{2}\omega^{2}x^{2}
: the effective potential is inverted.
Second, the term
+im\omega
is purely imaginary, arising
from the non-commutativity
[p,x]=-i
, and is responsible
for the non-Hermitian structure of the equation.
Third, equation (
10
) is a
Schrödinger-type equation with an
upward
parabolic
potential, whose solutions are oscillatory rather than
Gaussian at large
|x|
.

5
Reduction to the Weber Equation

We introduce the dimensionless variable

\xi=\left(\frac{2m\omega}{\hbar}\right)^{1/2}\!x=\sqrt{2m\omega}\,x\,,

(11)

where we have restored
\hbar
momentarily for clarity.
Under this substitution,
d^{2}/dx^{2}=2m\omega\,d^{2}/d\xi^{2}
,
and equation (
10
) becomes

\frac{d^{2}\psi_{1}}{d\xi^{2}}+\left(\frac{\xi^{2}}{4}+\lambda\right)\psi_{1}=0\,,

(12)

where the complex spectral parameter is

\lambda=\frac{E^{2}-m^{2}}{2m\omega}+\frac{i}{2}\,.

(13)

Equation (
12
) is the
Weber equation

(parabolic cylinder equation) in its standard form.
The parameter
\lambda
is generically complex, with
real part
\mathrm{Re}(\lambda)=(E^{2}-m^{2})/(2m\omega)

and imaginary part
\mathrm{Im}(\lambda)=1/2
,
independent of the energy
E
.
This fixed imaginary part is a direct consequence of
the commutator term
im\omega
in (
10
)
and distinguishes the IDMO from the non-relativistic
inverted oscillator, where
\lambda
is purely real.

6
General Solution

The general solution of the Weber equation (
12
)
is a linear combination of parabolic cylinder
functions
[
1
,
13
]
:

\psi_{1}(\xi)=A\,D_{\nu}(\xi)+B\,D_{\nu}(-\xi)\,,

(14)

with the complex order

\nu=\lambda-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{E^{2}-m^{2}}{2m\omega}-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{i}{2}\,.

(15)

The constants
A
and
B
are determined by boundary
conditions (see Section
7
).
The lower component
\psi_{2}
follows from (
8
)
using the recurrence relation
[
1
]

\frac{d}{d\xi}D_{\nu}(\xi)+\frac{\xi}{2}D_{\nu}(\xi)=\nu\,D_{\nu-1}(\xi)\,,

(16)

giving

\psi_{2}(\xi)=\frac{\sqrt{2m\omega}}{m+E}\left[A\left(\frac{d}{d\xi}-\frac{\xi}{2}\right)D_{\nu}(\xi)-B\left(\frac{d}{d\xi}-\frac{\xi}{2}\right)D_{\nu}(-\xi)\right].

(17)

Remark 2
.

For non-negative integer
\nu=n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
the parabolic cylinder functions reduce to

D_{n}(\xi)=2^{-n/2}e^{-\xi^{2}/4}H_{n}(\xi/\sqrt{2})
,
where
H_{n}
are Hermite polynomials
[
1
]
.
However, equation (
15
) shows that
\nu
is
complex for all real
E
, so the IDMO solutions

never
reduce to the Hermite-Gaussian form of
the standard DMO.

7
Spectral Properties

7.1
Continuous spectrum

The energy spectrum of the IDMO is
purely continuous
:
every
E\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying
E^{2}>m^{2}
is an
admissible eigenvalue, giving the two branches

E>m
(particle) and
E<-m
(antiparticle).
There is no discrete spectrum of bound states.

This is in sharp contrast to the standard DMO, whose
spectrum is
[
11
]

E_{n}=\pm\sqrt{m^{2}+2m\omega(2n+1)}\,,\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots

(18)

The absence of discrete levels in the IDMO is a direct
consequence of the inverted potential: no confining
barrier exists to quantize the spectrum.

7.2
Non-normalizability and Rigged Hilbert Spaces

The large-
|\xi|
behavior of the parabolic cylinder
functions with complex order is
[
1
]
:

D_{\nu}(\xi)\;\underset{|\xi|\to\infty}{\sim}\;\xi^{\nu}\,e^{-\xi^{2}/4}\,,

(19)

but for complex
\nu
with
\mathrm{Im}(\nu)=1/2
,
the argument
\xi^{2}/4
evaluated at

\xi=\sqrt{2m\omega}\,x
gives

e^{-\xi^{2}/4}=e^{-m\omega x^{2}/2}\,,

(20)

which is Gaussian-decaying for real
\xi
.
However, the prefactor
\xi^{\nu}=\xi^{(E^{2}-m^{2})/(2m\omega)-1/2+i/2}
oscillates rapidly at large
|\xi|
, so the
eigenfunctions are not in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
in the standard
sense.

The appropriate framework is the Rigged Hilbert Space
(RHS)
[
7
,
6
]
:

\Phi\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})^{\oplus 2}\subset\Phi^{\times}\,,

(21)

where
\Phi
is a dense subspace of smooth, rapidly
decreasing spinors (Schwartz space), and
\Phi^{\times}

is its dual.
The eigenstates
\ket{\psi_{E}}
of
H_{\mathrm{IDMO}}
belong
to
\Phi^{\times}
and satisfy the generalised
orthonormality condition

\langle\psi_{E}\mid\psi_{E^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta(E-E^{\prime})\,,

(22)

in the distributional sense.

8
Energy Values, Normalization, and Explicit Wave Functions

8.1
Gamow resonances and the discrete energy values

Although the physical spectrum of the IDMO is continuous,
the analytic structure of the resolvent

(H_{\mathrm{IDMO}}-E)^{-1}
in the complex
E
-plane reveals
a discrete set of poles corresponding to

Gamow resonances

[
6
,
7
]
.
These poles occur precisely when the order
\nu
in
equation (
15
) takes non-negative integer values,

\nu=n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, since
D_{n}(\xi)

then reduces to a polynomial times a Gaussian and the
solution becomes square-integrable on the complex contour
defined in Section
8.2.3
.

Setting
\nu=n
in equation (
15
):

\frac{E^{2}-m^{2}}{2m\omega}-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{i}{2}=n\,,

(23)

solving for
E^{2}
:

E_{n}^{2}=m^{2}+m\omega(2n+1)-im\omega\,,\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots

(24)

and taking the square root:

E_{n}^{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{m^{2}+m\omega(2n+1)-im\omega}\,,\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots

(25)

These are complex energies: the real part

\mathrm{Re}(E_{n}^{\pm})
gives the resonance position
and the imaginary part
\mathrm{Im}(E_{n}^{\pm})
gives
the decay width
\Gamma_{n}=-2\,\mathrm{Im}(E_{n}^{+})
.

Remark 3
.

The IDMO resonance energies (
25
) differ
from the standard DMO spectrum (
18
) in
two respects.
First, the coefficient of
n
changes:

2m\omega(2n+1)\to m\omega(2n+1)
, reflecting the fact that the
IDMO ladder has spacing
2m\omega
rather than
4m\omega
.
Second, and more importantly, the real spectrum acquires a
purely imaginary shift:

E_{n}^{2}\big|_{\mathrm{DMO}}=m^{2}+2m\omega(2n+1)\;\longrightarrow\;E_{n}^{2}\big|_{\mathrm{IDMO}}=m^{2}+m\omega(2n+1)-im\omega\,,

(27)

so that the IDMO energies are never real.
The imaginary part
-im\omega
, independent of
n
, is the
direct quantum signature of the instability: it arises from
the commutator term
[p,x]=-i
in equation (
10
)
and vanishes in the classical limit
\hbar\to 0
.
In contrast, the DMO energies are purely real, consistent
with the confining nature of the attractive potential.

8.2
Three approaches to normalization

Since the IDMO eigenfunctions are not in

L^{2}(\mathbb{R})^{\oplus 2}
, the standard
inner product
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle
diverges.
Three consistent normalization schemes are available,
each appropriate for different physical applications.

8.2.1
Scheme I: Delta-function normalization

For a continuous spectrum, the physically natural
normalization is the distributional condition

\langle\psi_{E}\mid\psi_{E^{\prime}}\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\left[\psi_{1}^{*}(x;E)\,\psi_{1}(x;E^{\prime})+\psi_{2}^{*}(x;E)\,\psi_{2}(x;E^{\prime})\right]=\delta(E-E^{\prime})\,.

(28)

This is the appropriate condition within the
Rigged Hilbert Space framework
[
7
]
.
Using the asymptotic properties of the parabolic
cylinder functions
[
1
]
and the
Wronskian (
74
) (see
Appendix
A
), the normalization condition
fixes the coefficients
A
and
B
in
equation (
14
) as

|A|^{2}=|B|^{2}=\mathcal{N}_{E}^{2}\,,

(29)

where

\mathcal{N}_{E}^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left|\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{i(E^{2}-m^{2})}{4m\omega}\right)\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{2\cosh\!\left(\frac{\pi(E^{2}-m^{2})}{4m\omega}\right)}\,,

(30)

using the reflection formula

\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)=\pi/\sin(\pi z)
.
The normalized upper component is therefore

\psi_{1}(x;E)=\mathcal{N}_{E}\left[D_{\nu}\!\left(\sqrt{2m\omega}\,x\right)+D_{\nu}\!\left(-\sqrt{2m\omega}\,x\right)\right],

(31)

with
\nu
given by (
15
).

8.2.2
Scheme II: Box normalization

An alternative approach introduces an infrared
(IR) regulator by restricting the system to a
finite interval
x\in[-L,L]
with appropriate
boundary conditions, and taking
L\to\infty

at the end.
For fixed
L
, the normalised spinor satisfies

\int_{-L}^{L}dx\,\left[|\psi_{1}(x)|^{2}+|\psi_{2}(x)|^{2}\right]=1\,,

(32)

giving

A_{L}=B_{L}=\left[2\int_{-L}^{L}dx\,\left|D_{\nu}\!\left(\sqrt{2m\omega}\,x\right)\right|^{2}(1+R_{L}^{2})\right]^{-1/2},

(33)

where
R_{L}^{2}
accounts for the lower-component
contribution.
In the limit
L\to\infty
, the box-normalised
eigenfunctions converge in the distributional
sense to the delta-normalised
eigenfunctions (
31
), with
the identification

|A_{L}|^{2}\to\mathcal{N}_{E}^{2}\,\delta(0)\sim\mathcal{N}_{E}^{2}\cdot 2L/2\pi
,
consistent with the standard relation between
box and continuum normalizations
[
6
]
.

8.2.3
Scheme III: Complex contour normalization

The most mathematically elegant approach exploits
the connection between the IDMO and the standard
DMO via the complex rotation
x\to xe^{i\pi/4}
.
Under this rotation, the argument of the parabolic
cylinder function becomes

\xi=\sqrt{2m\omega}\,x\;\xrightarrow{\;x\to xe^{i\pi/4}\;}\zeta=\sqrt{2m\omega}\,e^{i\pi/4}\,x\,,

(34)

and the Gaussian factor transforms as

e^{-\zeta^{2}/4}=e^{-m\omega e^{i\pi/2}x^{2}/2}=e^{-im\omega x^{2}/2}\,,

(35)

which is oscillatory for real
x
.
However, on the contour

\mathcal{C}:x=re^{i\pi/4}
,
r\in\mathbb{R}
:

e^{-\zeta^{2}/4}\big|_{x=re^{i\pi/4}}=e^{-m\omega r^{2}/2}\,,

(36)

which is Gaussian-decaying.
The wave functions are therefore square-integrable
on
\mathcal{C}
, and the contour inner product is

\langle\psi\mid\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}\equiv\int_{\mathcal{C}}dx\,\psi^{*}(x)\,\phi(x)=e^{i\pi/4}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dr\,\psi^{*}(re^{i\pi/4})\,\phi(re^{i\pi/4})\,.

(37)

Under this inner product, the resonance wave
functions corresponding to integer
\nu=n

satisfy

\langle\psi_{n}\mid\psi_{n^{\prime}}\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}=\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\,,

(38)

with the normalised resonance wave functions

\psi_{1}^{(n)}(x)=\mathcal{C}_{n}\,D_{n}\!\left(\sqrt{2m\omega}\,x\right),\qquad\mathcal{C}_{n}=\left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}\,n!}}\,,

(39)

identical in form to the harmonic oscillator
eigenfunctions but evaluated on the contour

\mathcal{C}
.

8.3
Explicit wave functions for the first three levels

We now display the explicit wave functions for

n=0,1,2
using the contour
normalization (
39
).
The parabolic cylinder functions for integer

\nu=n
reduce to
[
1
]
:

\displaystyle D_{0}(\xi)

\displaystyle=e^{-\xi^{2}/4}\,,

(40)

\displaystyle D_{1}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\xi\,e^{-\xi^{2}/4}\,,

(41)

\displaystyle D_{2}(\xi)

\displaystyle=(\xi^{2}-1)\,e^{-\xi^{2}/4}\,,

(42)

with
\xi=\sqrt{2m\omega}\,x
.

Ground state (
n=0
):

\displaystyle\psi_{1}^{(0)}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}\exp\!\left(-\frac{m\omega x^{2}}{2}\right),

(43)

\displaystyle\psi_{2}^{(0)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2m\omega}}{m+E_{0}^{+}}\,\left(-m\omega x\right)\left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}\exp\!\left(-\frac{m\omega x^{2}}{2}\right),

(44)

with resonance energy

E_{0}^{+}=\sqrt{m^{2}+m\omega-im\omega}
.

First excited state (
n=1
):

\displaystyle\psi_{1}^{(1)}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,2\sqrt{m\omega}\,x\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{m\omega x^{2}}{2}\right),

(45)

\displaystyle\psi_{2}^{(1)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2m\omega}}{m+E_{1}^{+}}\left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1-2m\omega x^{2}\right)\exp\!\left(-\frac{m\omega x^{2}}{2}\right),

(46)

with
E_{1}^{+}=\sqrt{m^{2}+3m\omega-im\omega}
.

Second excited state (
n=2
):

\displaystyle\psi_{1}^{(2)}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\,\left(4m\omega x^{2}-2\right)\exp\!\left(-\frac{m\omega x^{2}}{2}\right),

(47)

\displaystyle\psi_{2}^{(2)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2m\omega}}{m+E_{2}^{+}}\left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\left(2m\omega x\right)\left(3-2m\omega x^{2}\right)\exp\!\left(-\frac{m\omega x^{2}}{2}\right),

(48)

with
E_{2}^{+}=\sqrt{m^{2}+5m\omega-im\omega}
.

, which is complex,

\psi_{2}^{(n)}
,

.

9
Algebraic Structure

9.1
SU(1,1)
algebra

The standard DMO possesses a dynamical symmetry algebra

SU(1,1)

[
15
]
, generated by

K_{+}=a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}\,,\quad K_{-}=ab\,,\quad K_{0}=\tfrac{1}{2}(a^{\dagger}a+b^{\dagger}b+1)\,,

(49)

where
a,a^{\dagger}
and
b,b^{\dagger}
are bosonic ladder
operators.
For the IDMO, the analytic continuation
\omega\to i\omega

maps the compact
SU(2)
-like structure of the DMO into
the non-compact
SU(1,1)
algebra of the inverted oscillator,
with generators satisfying

[K_{0},K_{\pm}]=\pm K_{\pm}
,
[K_{-},K_{+}]=2K_{0}
.

The Casimir operator is

\mathcal{C}=K_{0}^{2}-K_{+}K_{-}-K_{-}K_{+}=k(k-1)\,\mathbf{1}\,,

(50)

where the Bargmann index
k
is related to the spectral
parameter
\lambda
by
k=\frac{1}{2}(1-\lambda)
.
For the IDMO,
k
is complex, placing the representation
in the
principal series
of
SU(1,1)
, which
supports the continuous spectrum found in
Section
7
.

9.2
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetry

The IDMO Hamiltonian (
3
) is

\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric.
Under parity
\mathcal{P}:x\to-x
,
p\to-p

and time-reversal
\mathcal{T}:p\to-p
,
i\to-i
:

\mathcal{PT}:\quad H_{\mathrm{IDMO}}\;\to\;H_{\mathrm{IDMO}}^{\dagger}\,,

(51)

confirming pseudo-Hermiticity
[
12
]
.
Since the spectrum is real despite the non-Hermitian structure,
the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetry of the IDMO is unbroken in the
particle/antiparticle sectors
|E|>m
.

9.3
Negative-energy solutions and their physical interpretation

9.3.1
The two branches of the spectrum

The continuous spectrum of the IDMO admits two branches,
determined by the sign of the energy:

Particle sector:

\displaystyle\quad E>m\,,

(52)

Antiparticle sector:

\displaystyle\quad E<-m\,.

(53)

The region
|E|<m
is forbidden by the relativistic
dispersion relation
E^{2}=p^{2}+m^{2}
, constituting
the
mass gap
of the theory.
The two sectors are separated by this gap and are
connected by the charge-conjugation symmetry
\mathcal{C}
,
as we now discuss.

9.3.2
Charge conjugation and the antiparticle states

In
(1+1)
dimensions, the charge-conjugation operator
acts on a two-component spinor as
[
8
]

\mathcal{C}:\quad\psi(x)\;\longrightarrow\;\psi^{c}(x)=i\sigma_{y}\,\psi^{*}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}^{*}(x)\\
\psi_{2}^{*}(x)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{2}^{*}(x)\\
-\psi_{1}^{*}(x)\end{pmatrix}.

(54)

If
\psi_{E}(x)
is a solution of the IDMO eigenvalue
equation with energy
E>m
, then
\psi_{E}^{c}(x)
is
a solution with energy
-E<-m

[
11
]
.
The antiparticle wave functions are therefore fully
determined by the particle solutions:

\displaystyle\psi_{1}^{c,(n)}(x)

\displaystyle=\psi_{2}^{(n)*}(x)\,,

(55)

\displaystyle\psi_{2}^{c,(n)}(x)

\displaystyle=-\psi_{1}^{(n)*}(x)\,,

(56)

with associated Gamow resonance energies

E_{n}^{-}=-\left(E_{n}^{+}\right)^{*}=-\sqrt{m^{2}+m\omega(2n+1)-im\omega}^{\,*}\,,

(57)

whose imaginary part is

\mathrm{Im}(E_{n}^{-})=+|\mathrm{Im}(E_{n}^{+})|>0\,.

(58)

9.3.3
Growing vs decaying modes: resonances and anti-resonances

The sign of
\mathrm{Im}(E_{n}^{\pm})
has a direct
dynamical interpretation.
For a time-dependent state

\Psi(x,t)=\psi(x)\,e^{-iEt}
:

\displaystyle E_{n}^{+}:

\displaystyle\quad\mathrm{Im}(E_{n}^{+})<0\;\Rightarrow\;e^{-iE_{n}^{+}t}=e^{-i\,\mathrm{Re}(E_{n}^{+})t}\cdot e^{+|\mathrm{Im}(E_{n}^{+})|t}\,,

(59)

\displaystyle E_{n}^{-}:

\displaystyle\quad\mathrm{Im}(E_{n}^{-})>0\;\Rightarrow\;e^{-iE_{n}^{-}t}=e^{+i\,\mathrm{Re}(E_{n}^{-})t}\cdot e^{-|\mathrm{Im}(E_{n}^{-})|t}\,.

(60)

The particle Gamow states
E_{n}^{+}
therefore correspond
to
exponentially growing
modes (outgoing resonances),
while the antiparticle states
E_{n}^{-}
correspond to

exponentially decaying
modes (incoming
anti-resonances)
[
6
,
7
]
.
This is the relativistic manifestation of the classical
instability of the inverted potential: the particle sector
describes escape to
+\infty
, while the antiparticle
sector describes capture from
-\infty
.

9.3.4
Vacuum instability and spontaneous pair production

The simultaneous presence of growing particle modes
(
E_{n}^{+}
) and decaying antiparticle modes (
E_{n}^{-}
)
signals an
instability of the Dirac vacuum
.
In the language of quantum field theory, the inverted
potential mixes positive- and negative-frequency modes,
leading to spontaneous creation of particle-antiparticle
pairs from the vacuum
[
14
]
.

The pair-production rate per unit length per unit time
can be estimated from the imaginary part of the
effective action
[
10
]
:

\mathcal{W}=2\,\mathrm{Im}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{eff}})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Gamma_{n}\,e^{-\pi(E_{n}^{+})^{2}/m\omega}\,,

(61)

where
\Gamma_{n}=-2\,\mathrm{Im}(E_{n}^{+})
is the
decay width of the
n
-th Gamow state.
This expression is analogous to the

Schwinger formula
for pair production in a
uniform electric field
[
16
]
,

\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{Schwinger}}\propto\exp\!\left(-\frac{\pi m^{2}}{eE}\right),

(62)

with the correspondence
eE\leftrightarrow m\omega

between the electric field strength and the IOH
frequency.
This analogy is not accidental: both the uniform
electric field and the inverted harmonic potential
produce a linearly growing vector potential that
mixes particle and antiparticle
states
[
17
]
.

9.3.5
Dirac sea interpretation

In the Dirac sea picture, the vacuum consists of all
negative-energy states
E<-m
filled with electrons.
For the standard DMO, this sea is stable: the discrete
negative-energy levels
E_{n}^{-}=-\sqrt{m^{2}+2m\omega(2n+1)}

are real and time-independent.

For the IDMO, the negative-energy states are Gamow
anti-resonances with
\mathrm{Im}(E_{n}^{-})>0
:
they decay exponentially in time.
The physical picture is, therefore, that the IDMO Dirac
sea is
leaking
: negative-energy electrons
spontaneously tunnel out of the sea into positive-energy
states, leaving behind positrons.
The characteristic timescale of this process is

\tau_{n}=\frac{1}{\Gamma_{n}}=\frac{1}{-2\,\mathrm{Im}(E_{n}^{+})}\,,

(63)

with numerical values (for
m=\omega=1
):

\tau_{0}\approx 1.46
,
\tau_{1}\approx 2.02
,

\tau_{2}\approx 2.46
, in natural units.
The monotonic increase of
\tau_{n}
with
n
shows
that higher levels of the Dirac sea are more stable,
consistent with the asymptotic
formula (
26
).

The negative-energy solutions of the IDMO carry
three physically distinct pieces of information:

1.

Antiparticle spectrum
: fully determined
by charge conjugation (
54
)
from the particle solutions, with energies

E_{n}^{-}=-(E_{n}^{+})^{*}
.

2.

Anti-resonance structure
: the positive
imaginary part
\mathrm{Im}(E_{n}^{-})>0
identifies
the antiparticle Gamow states as
incoming

anti-resonances in the complex energy plane,
complementary to the
outgoing
particle
resonances.

3.

Vacuum instability
: the interplay between
growing (
E_{n}^{+}
) and decaying (
E_{n}^{-}
) modes
drives spontaneous pair production at a rate
analogous to the Schwinger effect, with the
identification
eE\leftrightarrow m\omega
.

10
Conclusions

We have developed a complete and self-contained analysis of
the inverted Dirac-Moshinsky oscillator in
(1+1)
dimensions.
The main results are the following.

1.

Exact solution.

The second-order equation for the upper spinor component
reduces to the Weber equation (
12
) with
complex spectral parameter

\lambda=(E^{2}-m^{2})/(2m\omega)+i/2
.
The general solution is expressed in terms of parabolic
cylinder functions
D_{\nu}(\xi)
with complex order

\nu=\lambda-1/2
, equation (
15
), and
the lower component follows via the recurrence
relation (
16
).

2.

Continuous spectrum.

The energy spectrum is purely continuous,

E\in\mathbb{R}
with
|E|>m
, with no discrete
bound states.
This contrasts sharply with the discrete spectrum of
the standard DMO and is a direct consequence of the
repulsive (inverted) potential.

3.

normalization.

Three consistent normalization schemes are available:
delta-function normalization with constant

\mathcal{N}_{E}^{2}=[2\cosh(\pi(E^2-m^2)/4m\omega)]^{-1}
,
box normalization with infrared regulator
L
, and
complex contour normalization on

\mathcal{C}:x\to xe^{i\pi/4}
,
under which the resonance states become
square-integrable and satisfy

\langle\psi_{n}|\psi_{n^{\prime}}\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}=\delta_{nn^{\prime}}
.

4.

Gamow resonances.

The resolvent
(H_{\mathrm{IDMO}}-E)^{-1}
has discrete poles at
the complex energies

E_{n}^{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{m^{2}+(2n+1)m\omega-im\omega}
,
with decay widths

\Gamma_{n}=-2\,\mathrm{Im}(E_{n}^{+})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
The explicit wave functions for
n=0,1,2
are given in
Section
8.3
.

5.

Negative-energy sector.

The antiparticle Gamow states
E_{n}^{-}
have positive
imaginary part, identifying them as incoming
anti-resonances.
Their interplay with the particle resonances
E_{n}^{+}

drives a vacuum instability with a pair-production
rate analogous to the Schwinger
formula (
61
), under the
correspondence
eE\leftrightarrow m\omega
.
The characteristic decay times
\tau_{n}=1/\Gamma_{n}

increase monotonically with
n
, so the Dirac sea
becomes asymptotically stable for large
n
.

6.

Algebraic and symmetry structure.

The IDMO Hamiltonian is
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric with
unbroken symmetry for
|E|>m
, confirming
pseudo-Hermiticity
[
12
]
.
The dynamical symmetry algebra is the principal series
of
SU(1,1)
, with complex Bargmann index

k=\frac{1}{2}(1-\lambda)
, which supports the
continuous spectrum.

The present results open several lines of investigation: The continuous spectrum and the asymptotic form of

D_{\nu}(\xi)
allow the construction of an exact

S
-matrix for the IDMO, whose poles reproduce the
Gamow energies (
25
).
Higher-dimensional versions of the IDMO involve
additional angular momentum quantum numbers and
richer spectral structures, including centrifugal
barriers that may stabilize certain modes. The finite-temperature partition function of the
IDMO field, building on the thermal formalism for
the Klein-Gordon inverted oscillator, would provide
access to the thermodynamics of the unstable Dirac
vacuum
[
18
]
.
The DMO has been realized experimentally in graphene
systems
[
15
]
.
The IDMO may describe relativistic Dirac fermions
near an unstable saddle point in a two-dimensional
crystal, providing a condensed-matter realization of
the spontaneous pair-production mechanism discussed
in Section
9.3.4
.
The
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric structure of the IDMO
suggests a connection to non-Hermitian quantum field
theories
[
4
]
, where the imaginary part
of the spectrum encodes decay rates of unstable
particles rather than unphysical complex energies.

Appendix A
Parabolic Cylinder Functions

We collect here the properties of the parabolic cylinder
functions
D_{\nu}(z)
most relevant for the IDMO analysis.
Complete treatments are given in
[
1
,
13
]
.

A.1
Definition and differential equation

The parabolic cylinder function
D_{\nu}(z)
is defined as
the solution of the Weber differential equation

\frac{d^{2}f}{dz^{2}}+\left(\nu+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{z^{2}}{4}\right)f=0

(64)

that is recessive (exponentially decaying) as

z\to+\infty
for
\nu\in\mathbb{C}
.
An equivalent representation in terms of Tricomi’s
confluent hypergeometric function
U(a,b,z)
is

D_{\nu}(z)=2^{\nu/2}\,e^{-z^{2}/4}\,U\!\left(-\tfrac{\nu}{2},\,\tfrac{1}{2},\,\tfrac{z^{2}}{2}\right).

(65)

For non-negative integer
\nu=n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,

D_{n}(z)
reduces to a product of a Hermite polynomial
and a Gaussian:

D_{n}(z)=2^{-n/2}\,e^{-z^{2}/4}\,H_{n}\!\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right),\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots

(66)

The first few explicit forms are

\displaystyle D_{0}(z)

\displaystyle=e^{-z^{2}/4}\,,

(67)

\displaystyle D_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=z\,e^{-z^{2}/4}\,,

(68)

\displaystyle D_{2}(z)

\displaystyle=(z^{2}-1)\,e^{-z^{2}/4}\,,

(69)

\displaystyle D_{3}(z)

\displaystyle=(z^{3}-3z)\,e^{-z^{2}/4}\,.

(70)

A.2
Recurrence relations

The parabolic cylinder functions satisfy the
recurrence relations
[
1
]

\displaystyle D_{\nu+1}(z)-z\,D_{\nu}(z)+\nu\,D_{\nu-1}(z)

\displaystyle=0\,,

(71)

\displaystyle D_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)+\tfrac{z}{2}\,D_{\nu}(z)-\nu\,D_{\nu-1}(z)

\displaystyle=0\,,

(72)

\displaystyle D_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)-\tfrac{z}{2}\,D_{\nu}(z)+D_{\nu+1}(z)

\displaystyle=0\,,

(73)

where primes denote differentiation with respect to
z
.
Relations (
72
) and (
73
)
are used in Section
6
to derive the
lower spinor component
\psi_{2}
from
\psi_{1}
.

A.3
Wronskian

The two linearly independent solutions of (
64
)
are
D_{\nu}(z)
and
D_{\nu}(-z)
.
Their Wronskian is

W[D_{\nu}(z),\,D_{\nu}(-z)]\equiv D_{\nu}(z)\,D_{\nu}^{\prime}(-z)-D_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)\,D_{\nu}(-z)=-\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\Gamma(-\nu)}\,,

(74)

which is non-zero for non-integer
\nu
, confirming
linear independence.
This Wronskian enters the delta-function
normalization (
30
) via the asymptotic
completeness relation for the continuous spectrum.

A.4
Asymptotic behaviour

For large
|z|
with
|\arg z|<3\pi/4

[
13
]
:

D_{\nu}(z)\;\underset{|z|\to\infty}{\sim}\;e^{-z^{2}/4}\,z^{\nu}\left[1-\frac{\nu(\nu-1)}{2z^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(z^{-4})\right].

(75)

For the IDMO, the argument is

z=\xi=\sqrt{2m\omega}\,x
with complex order

\nu
given by (
15
).
The exponential factor behaves as

e^{-\xi^{2}/4}=e^{-m\omega x^{2}/2}\quad\text{(real, Gaussian decaying for real }x\text{)},

(76)

while the prefactor
\xi^{\nu}
with

\mathrm{Im}(\nu)=1/2
oscillates, rendering

\psi_{1}\notin L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
On the complex contour
x=re^{i\pi/4}

(Section
8.2.3
), the exponential
becomes
e^{-im\omega r^{2}/2}\cdot e^{-m\omega r^{2}/2}
,
which is Gaussian-decaying, restoring normalisability.

A.5
Orthogonality and normalization integral

For integer orders, the standard orthogonality
on the real line is
[
1
]

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}D_{m}(z)\,D_{n}(z)\,dz=\sqrt{2\pi}\,n!\,\delta_{mn}\,.

(77)

For the contour normalization of
Section
8.2.3
, this gives

\int_{\mathcal{C}}dx\,\psi_{1}^{(m)*}(x)\,\psi_{1}^{(n)}(x)=\mathcal{C}_{m}^{*}\,\mathcal{C}_{n}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2m\omega}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\,D_{m}(\xi)\,D_{n}(\xi)=\delta_{mn}\,,

(78)

consistent with the normalization
constant (
39
).

For general (non-integer, complex) order
\nu
,
the relevant normalization integral for the
delta-function scheme is obtained from the
Wronskian (
74
) and the
asymptotic completeness relation:

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\psi_{1}^{*}(x;E)\,\psi_{1}(x;E^{\prime})=\mathcal{N}_{E}^{2}\,\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{|\Gamma(-\nu)|}\,\delta(E-E^{\prime})\,,

(79)

from which the normalization
constant (
30
) follows upon
using the reflection formula

|\Gamma(-\nu)|^{-2}=|\Gamma(1+\nu)|^{2}/\pi^{2}\cdot\sin^{2}(\pi\nu)
evaluated at

\nu=(E^{2}-m^{2})/(2m\omega)-1/2+i/2
.

A.6
Special value at the origin

The value and derivative of
D_{\nu}
at
z=0
are

\displaystyle D_{\nu}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2^{-\nu/2}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1-\nu}{2}\right)}\,,

(80)

\displaystyle D_{\nu}^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2^{(-\nu-1)/2}\,\Gamma\!\left(-\frac{\nu}{2}\right)}\,.

(81)

These are used to verify boundary conditions
and to compute the probability density at
x=0

for each resonance state.
