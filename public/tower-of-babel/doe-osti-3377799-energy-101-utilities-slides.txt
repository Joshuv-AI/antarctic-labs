 Electricity Grid

• Electricity is generated at power plants and moves
  through a complex system, sometimes called the grid.
• The grid includes electricity substations, transformers,
  and power lines that connect electricity producers and
  consumers.
• Most local grids are interconnected to maintain reliability
  and for commercial purposes, forming larger, more
  dependable networks that helps suppliers consistently
  produce the right amount of electricity to meet demand.
• In the United States, the entire electricity grid consists
  thousands of miles of high-voltage power lines and
  millions of miles of low-voltage power lines.
 Photo: Prateek Joshi, NLR
• This network of power lines connects thousands of
  power plants to hundreds of millions of electricity
  customers across the country.

 Types of Generation

• Thermal energy can come from resources such
 as coal, combustion (gas) turbines geothermal,
 and nuclear.
 o Energy is stored with the source of fuel or the Earth
 and can be used when needed.
• Non-thermal energy can also come from
 hydroelectric, pumped-storage hydro, solar, and
 wind generation.
 o Energy may be used when available (e.g., when
 water is available, the sun is shining, or the wind is
 blowing).
• Energy Storage
 o Pumped hydro, compressed air, flywheels,
 thermal energy storage, and chemical energy
 (such as Li+).

 Utility Overview

• Electric utilities are responsible for the delivery of
  electricity to homes and businesses, including
  metering, billing, and customer service.

• Customers are usually (but not always) assigned to
  a utility based on the geographic boundaries of the
  utility franchise agreement and do not have the
  option to choose among competing retailers.

 Photo: Warren Gretz, NLR

                                                                                      National Laboratory of the Rockies   |   8

 Utility Responsibilities
• Obligation to Serve: A utility is granted the sole rights to operate in a Safety
 designated area and in exchange the utility are legally required to
 provide safe, reliable service to all customers—new and existing — in
 its territory. A utility’s service area may or may not be contiguous.

• Regulatory Compact: An implicant agreement or understanding that
 in exchange for the rights to operate in a specific geographic area, the
 utility will supply safe, reliable service at a reasonable price.

• Reliability Standards: Guidelines and requirements, primarily
 established by the North American Electric Reliability Corporation
 (NERC), that ensure the safe and reliable operation of the bulk power
 system. They cover a wide range of aspects, from cyber security to Reliability Cost
 physical security and operational procedures. All utilities must meet Image: Ingram (2024)

 the standards for safety and reliability.

• Reasonable Rate of Return: All utility costs including operating and
 capital expenditures are paid for through the rates the utility charges
 its customers. For IOUs, regulators set rates that allow the utility to
 earn a fair return on its investments. Operating costs are subject to
 regulatory scrutiny and significant capital investments must be
 approved by the regulatory body and often include public hearings.

 Utilities Are Stakeholders

• Electric utilities are crucial stakeholders within any community.
 o Utilities have a vested interest in stewarding their investments and protecting
 consumers from cost increases.
 o They have the final say on the interconnection of renewable energy resources
 within their service territories.
 o Municipal officials should approach their utilities with a collaborative attitude and
 keep them informed early on in the planning process regarding any new
 developments.
• To ensure that stakeholder engagement is effective, it is important to
  understand:
 o The regulatory environment in which the local utility operates.
 o The utility's ownership structure.
 o The utility's business model.

 Manage Expectations

Utilities are not a single, uniform entity but are complex and diverse systems.
 o You may have a very different experience depending on which part of the utility you’re working
 with and in what context.
Sharing utility data may present challenges.
 o Utilities may be prohibited by law from sharing the data you are requesting.
 o Utilities may not have the staff capability or bandwidth to meet your request.
 o E.g. data may take time to format so it is anonymous.
 o Utilities may not have the data you are seeking. For example:
 o Utilities may not have smart meters and therefor cannot share load data.
 o Information may not be collected at the intervals needed.
 o Data sets may be very large so utilities may not collect or store this data.

 Renewables Integration

Studies on renewable generation goals and development potential
within the United States have concluded that:
• High-penetration renewables development is technically feasible, if
 the transmission system is expanded.
• Significant amounts of new transmission are needed for major future
 wind development scenarios in the eastern and western
 interconnections.
• Costs for integrating large amounts of renewables are manageable with large
 amounts of transmission and regional operating pools to facilitate ancillary
 services.

 • Count and severity of energy emergency alerts are increasing.

 areas.
 • Instances of transmission-related events resulting in loss of load
