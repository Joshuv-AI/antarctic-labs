Comparison of complication rates and outcomes for various surgical techniques for the repair of acute distal triceps ruptures
Authors: Nicholas F. Aloi, Jacob N. Weinberg, Noah M. Feder, Hannah L. Tetreault, Krishna P. Nayak, John R. Fowler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDistal triceps ruptures are an injury caused by sudden forceful eccentric contraction at the elbow. Various techniques exist in the surgical treatment of distal triceps ruptures. This study sought to compare intraoperative and post-operative complication rates and functional outcomes of bone tunnels, suture anchors, and suture anchor-bone tunnel combination constructs for the surgical management of acute distal triceps ruptures.MethodsThis study was a retrospective review of distal triceps ruptures that occurred between the years 2014 and 2024 at a single institution. Chronic ruptures, revision procedures, reconstructions with allograft, direct end-to-end repairs, and atypical injury mechanisms (ie, lacerations) were excluded. Basic demographics and magnetic resonance imaging reports were collected from the electronic health record. Other variables collected were complications from the surgery (ie nerve damage, infection, etc.), pre-operative and post-operative elbow extension strength, surgical time, intraoperative blood loss, and return to the operating room (RTOR). Patients were then grouped into 3 groups based on operative technique (ie, bone tunnels, suture anchors, and suture anchor-bone tunnel combination constructs). Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests and Fisher exact test for sparse data. One-way analysis of variance with Tukey honestly significantly difference post hoc testing was used for continuous variables. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to adjust for tear type and tendon retraction length. P < .05 was set as the level of significance.ResultsForty-seven total patients were included in the analysis, of which, 8 patients (17.0%) were treated with suture anchor constructs, 16 (34.0%) underwent fixation with trans-osseous bone tunnels, and 23 patients (48.9%) underwent combined fixation constructs with suture anchors and bone tunnels. No significant differences were observed for complication rate, RTOR rate, or triceps tendon re-rupture rate (all P > .05). Pre-operative extension strength (P = .090), post-operative extension strength (P = .190), and operative time (P = .313) also did not differ significantly by technique. Change in extension strength differed significantly by technique (P = .014), with greater functional strength improvement observed in the Combo group compared with the bone tunnels group. However, when adjusting for tear type and tendon retraction length, there were no statistical differences for changes in extension.DiscussionThere were no observed differences in re-rupture rate, complication rate, or RTOR between the 3 surgical techniques. However, there was an association with improved elbow extension strength for the combination group but this finding did not retain statistical significance when controlling for tear type and retraction length.

Distal triceps tendon ruptures represent an extremely rare injury, with an incidence of approximately 0.46-1.1 per 100,000 person-years.2,13 Similar to distal biceps rupture, they predominantly affect middle-aged males (mean age 45-58 years, 75-90% male).7,10,13 The injury typically occurs after a fall on an outstretched hand, during weightlifting, or due to direct trauma, resulting from forceful eccentric contraction of the triceps muscle.10,15 Injuries range from partial tears to complete avulsions from the olecranon with varying degrees of tendon retraction. Given the critical role of the triceps in elbow extension, restoration of function is important for patients seeking to return to baseline activity.

Complete ruptures and partial tears with loss of extension strength often undergo surgical repair to restore the extensor mechanism and prevent chronic extension weakness.4,15,16 Early intervention (within 3 weeks of injury) allows for direct tendon-to-bone repair, whereas chronic tears may require more complex reconstruction with tendon grafting or augmentation.4,7,13 For partial tears with preserved active extension, conservative management can be successful for smaller tears, while larger tears may warrant surgical intervention, particularly in active individuals.4,15,16

Three primary surgical techniques have been described for distal triceps repair: trans-osseous bone tunnels, suture anchors, and direct repair. The traditional trans-osseous technique involves drilling tunnels through the olecranon and passing sutures through the triceps tendon in a cruciate or mattress configuration, tied over a dorsal bone bridge.6,14,16 This technique has historically been considered the reference standard and does not require implants. A study by Mirzayan et al10 demonstrated a high re-rupture rate (6.7% for bone tunnels vs. 0% for anchors). Furthermore, studies by Alnaji et al1 and Yeh et al17 reported overall complication rates of 15.2% and restoration of only 31-36% of the native tendon footprint, respectively, for intraosseous fixation of distal triceps tendon injuries. The suture anchor technique utilizes 2-4 suture anchors placed in the olecranon footprint with various suture configurations.3,15,16 Recent evidence demonstrates lower complication rates (7.7%), zero re-rupture rates in large series, and superior footprint restoration (85-89% with double-row techniques).1,10,11,17 Direct repair, typically reserved for partial tears, demonstrates the highest complication rate (29.2%) and is not recommended for complete ruptures.1

Combined anchor-tunnel constructs have recently emerged in the literature. Some fixation constructs include combining suture anchors with unicortical button fixation. The V-shaped double-row technique demonstrates superior biomechanical properties including the highest ultimate failure load (732.1 N) and high footprint restoration (∼89%).11,12 Another utilized combined anchor-construct includes drilling 2 osseous tunnels through the medial and lateral aspects of the triceps footprint in the olecranon, followed by whip-stitching sutures through the distal triceps in a baseball type whipstitch. After sutures are inserted through the drill holes, one end of each suture is then brought back through the tunnel and secured centrally in the olecranon using a suture anchor.9 While these combined constructs show promise in biomechanical studies, they are more technically demanding and clinical outcome data remain limited.

Technique selection appears influenced by tear characteristics (acute vs. chronic, complete vs. partial, retraction extent), patient factors (age, activity level, bone quality), surgeon experience, and available resources.1,7,10,11,14 However, clear guidelines for matching tear patterns to optimal techniques are lacking. While recent studies favor suture anchors over trans-osseous repair, comparative clinical outcome data remain limited, particularly for combined constructs, with no randomized controlled trials available.1 The relationship between tear characteristics and technique selection has not been systematically examined. This makes it difficult for surgeons to make evidence-based decisions about the optimal approach for individual patients based on their specific injury patterns.

The purpose of this study was to compare surgical outcomes and complications across 3 repair techniques for acute distal triceps ruptures: trans-osseous bone tunnels, suture anchors, and combined anchor-tunnel constructs. We hypothesized that the 3 approaches would demonstrate comparable complication rates and clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

This retrospective cohort study reviewed patients who underwent operative repair of distal triceps tendon ruptures at a single academic institution between 2014 and 2024. All procedures were performed by 4 fellowship-trained orthopedic upper extremity surgeons. A total of 55 distal triceps repairs were initially identified. Patients were excluded if they underwent revision repair, reconstruction with allograft, direct end-to-end or intratendinous repair, or had an atypical injury mechanism such as laceration. Patients were originally identified by searching for Current Procedural Terminology code 24342, and all patients with repairs of the distal biceps were excluded from this search, leaving only patients who underwent distal triceps tendon repairs being included in the study. This study was approved by the institutional review board at our institution with waiver of informed consent and HIPAA authorization. The investigation was conducted in accordance with STROBE guidelines.

Demographic and clinical variables were obtained through review of the electronic medical record. Variables collected included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and smoking history. Magnetic resonance imaging reports were reviewed to determine tear type (partial versus complete) and tendon retraction length. Magnetic resonance imaging reports provided by radiologists were utilized to characterize tear morphology and tendon retraction length rather than independent review of the imaging by the authors. Additional operative and post-operative variables included time from injury to surgery, operative time, estimated blood loss, complications, return to the operating room (RTOR), re-ruptures, and pre-operative and post-operative elbow extension strength. Triceps muscle extension strength was graded on a scale of 0 to 5 based on the established criteria by the Medical Research Council Scale for Muscle Strength. Change in elbow extension strength was calculated as the difference between post-operative and pre-operative strength. Patients were then stratified into 3 fixation technique groups: suture anchors, bone tunnels, and combined suture anchor–bone tunnel constructs.

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables were summarized as frequency with percentage. Continuous variables were compared across the 3 surgical technique groups using a variable-specific approach based on variance homogeneity. For outcomes in which parametric assumptions were met, one-way analysis of variance was used for omnibus comparisons. When the omnibus analysis of variance was statistically significant, Tukey honestly significant difference post hoc testing was performed for pairwise comparisons. For variables with evidence of heteroscedasticity, non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis testing was used, with particular emphasis placed on time to surgery because of unequal variances across groups. Variables demonstrating non-normal distributions were analyzed using non-parametric methods, such as those including elbow extension strength, which were compared between groups with the Mann-Whitney U test. Additionally, multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed controlling for tendon retraction length and tendon tear type, to determine if these factors impacted the surgical technique's outcomes.

Categorical variables were compared pairwise across surgical techniques using chi-square tests when expected cell counts were adequate and Fisher exact tests when sparse cells or rare events made chi-square assumptions unreliable. Because each categorical outcome involved 3 pairwise comparisons (suture anchors vs. bone tunnels, suture anchors vs. combined constructs, and bone tunnels vs. combined constructs), Bonferroni correction was applied to control the family-wise type I error rate, yielding an adjusted significance threshold of P < .0167 for pairwise categorical comparisons. All statistical tests were two-sided, and statistical significance for omnibus analyses was defined as P < .05. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata.

Results

A total of 55 patients underwent distal triceps tendon repair during the previously stated study period. Two patients were excluded from analysis due to being revision procedures, and 5 patients were excluded for having direct intratendinous repair. Forty-seven total patients were then included in the analysis, of which, 8 patients (17.0%) were treated with suture anchor constructs, 16 (34.0%) underwent fixation with trans-osseous bone tunnels, and 23 patients (48.9%) underwent combined fixation constructs with suture anchors and bone tunnels. Procedures were performed by 4 board-certified orthopedic upper extremity surgeons at a single academic institution.

Forty-three patients were male (91.5%) and 4 were female (8.5%). The average age and BMI of the entire cohort were 54.54 ± 14.91 years and 29.75 ± 5.48 kg/m2 (Table I). Baseline demographics were similar across groups, with no significant differences in age (P = .665) or body mass index (P = .730, Table I). Sex distribution also did not differ significantly across groups after pairwise testing with Bonferroni correction (Table I). Smoking status was similarly comparable across techniques. In regard to injury and tear pattern characteristics, retraction length (in cm) of the triceps tendon ruptures varied by surgical approach (P = .038), with post hoc analysis demonstrating greater tendon retraction in the bone tunnels group compared with the suture anchors group, while the combination group did not differ from either (Table I). Partial tears were more common among patients treated with suture anchors alone compared with combination anchor–tunnel constructs (P = .0041, Table I). Time to surgery was not significantly different across techniques on non-parametric testing (P = .484, Table I). No significant differences were observed for sex, smoking status, complication rate, RTOR, or re-rupture rate. Given the low number of events for both RTOR and re-rupture, multivariable logistic regression was unable to be performed for these outcomes. The average time since surgery that complications were identified was 71.29 ± 82.61 days. The range of clinical follow-up was 12 to 656 days post-operatively, with all clinical data within our electronic health system available for at least 1 year after surgery for all patients given the time point of which this retrospective study was completed. Overall, total complication rates for the suture anchor group were 12.5%, 25% for the bone tunnel group and 13.1% for the combination cohort. Complications for the suture anchor group were numbness and tingling on the posterior distal arm/elbow in the sensory distribution of the radial nerve. Complications in the bone tunnels group included 2 cases of re-ruptures of the triceps tendon, 1 deep infection and 1 case of ulnar nerve sensory deficits after surgery, all of which required a RTOR for revision procedures, irrigation and débridement, and cubital tunnel release, respectively. In the combination group, there was 1 deep infection which required a RTOR and 1 superficial surgical site infection treated with antibiotics only. There were no re-ruptures of the triceps tendon repairs in the combination cohort or the suture anchors groups, but there were 2 (12.5%) re-ruptures in the bone tunnels group. Suture anchors were used almost exclusively for partial tears, with 7 of 8 anchor repairs performed for partial ruptures and a mean retraction length of only 0.58 cm. Overall, surgical technique was associated with differences in tear characteristics such as tendon retraction length and improvement in functional elbow extension strength outcomes, while demographic and perioperative variables, as well as complication rates, were comparable across groups. Importantly, the change in elbow extension strength that was observed did not retain statistically significant differences following regression analysis adjusting for tendon tear type and retraction length. Finally, there was no observed difference in total procedure time (in minutes) or intraoperative blood loss (in ml) among the 3 types of fixation strategies (Table II).

Discussion

Distal triceps tendon ruptures are among the rarest tendon injuries in orthopedic practice, and while 3 primary surgical repair techniques have been described, including bone tunnels, suture anchors, and combined anchor-tunnel constructs, the existing literature does not clearly establish the superiority of one approach over another.1 Comparative clinical outcome data remain particularly limited for combined constructs, which have been supported largely by biomechanical rather than clinical evidence.9,11,12

The overall complication rate in our cohort was 14.9%, consistent with the 14.9% reported in the systematic review by Alnaji et al1 and the 17.1% reported by Brush et al5 in the largest three-technique comparative series to date. While complication rates did not differ significantly across the 3 groups in our study, the bone tunnels group demonstrated a numerically higher complication rate of 25.0% compared with 12.5% and 13.1% in the suture anchor and combination groups, respectively. Similarly, RTOR occurred in 25.0% of bone tunnel patients compared with 0% and 4.4% in the suture anchor and combination groups. These trends are consistent with prior literature suggesting a less favorable safety profile for bone tunnel repairs, though our results regarding re-rupture and complication rates were not statistically different. Mirzayan et al,10 in a multicenter cohort of 184 patients, found significantly higher re-rupture and reoperation rates with bone tunnel repairs compared with suture anchors, while Alnaji et al1 reported a re-rupture rate of 4.3% for bone tunnel repair versus 2.1% for suture anchors across 560 patients. In our cohort, 2 re-ruptures occurred in the bone tunnels group and none in either the suture anchor or combination groups. Although this difference did not reach statistical significance, likely reflecting the limited sample size of our study, the directional consistency with prior literature suggests this represents a clinically meaningful signal that warrants consideration in surgical decision-making.

The combination construct group demonstrated a significantly greater improvement in elbow extension strength compared to the bone tunnels group on post hoc analysis (1.22 ± 0.44 vs. 0.36 ± 0.37, P = .014), representing the primary functional outcome difference observed across the 3 techniques in this study. However, these findings did not demonstrate statistical significant on multivariable logistic regression analysis, which shows that confounding variables such as tendon tear type and retraction length may play a role in influencing post-operative functional outcomes rather than the technique itself. Overall, there was no difference in post-operative extension strength between the groups, but the combined construct group demonstrated greater improvements in extension strength pre-operatively compared to post-operatively. This could indicate more significant tears and pre-operative strength deficits compared to the other groups. These results are consistent with the biomechanical rationale for combined constructs, which have been shown to achieve superior footprint restoration and higher ultimate failure loads compared to either technique alone.11,12 Gruber et al8 similarly reported favorable functional outcomes using a hybrid anchor-tunnel technique in competitive strength athletes, with a 95% return to sports rate and no re-ruptures, though that population differs substantially from the general orthopedic cohort represented in our study. Notably, Brush et al5 found that suture anchor repair alone was independently associated with post-operative loss of full elbow extension, hypothesizing that greater bone disruption from anchor placement and scar tissue formation may contribute to joint tightness, while their Trans-osseous Tunnel Suture Anchor group demonstrated a favorable extension profile comparable to bone tunnels repair alone. While their outcome measure differed from ours, this pattern converges with our finding that combined constructs may offer a functional advantage over either technique used in isolation. Prospective studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm this hypothesis.

An important consideration in interpreting the outcomes of this study is that technique selection was strongly associated with tear characteristics across groups. Suture anchors were used almost exclusively for partial tears, with 7 of 8 anchor repairs performed for partial ruptures and a mean retraction length of only 0.58 cm, while 17 of 23 combination construct repairs and 6 of 16 bone tunnel repairs were performed for complete tears, with mean retraction lengths of 2.10 cm and 2.93 cm, respectively. This is an important consideration for the interpretation of the findings in this study because it indicates that, in general, suture anchors were used primarily for partial tears with lower retraction length compared to the combination construct and bone tunnels, which were used primarily for complete tears with more significant tendon retraction. This pattern mirrors observations by Brush et al,5 who similarly found that suture anchor repair was most commonly performed for partial tears while bone tunnel repairs was most commonly used for complete tear injuries. Brush et al5 attributed differences in technique utilization primarily to surgeon training background and subspecialty preference, and no published guidelines currently exist for matching repair technique to specific tear characteristics. However, the more extreme distribution observed in our cohort raises the possibility that injury severity may play a more active role in driving technique selection than surgeon preference alone, and further investigation is warranted to better characterize this relationship.

This study has several limitations. The retrospective, single-institution design limits generalizability, and the relatively small sample size, particularly the suture anchor cohort of only 8 patients, reduces statistical power to detect differences in low-frequency outcomes such as re-rupture and RTOR. Given the rare nature of triceps tendon injuries requiring surgical intervention, our overall sample size of 47 patients was relatively small, which could introduce type 2 statistical error into our findings and decrease the overall impact of our work. The study was likely underpowered to detect differences in rare outcomes such as re-ruptures, infections, nerve injuries, or reoperations for triceps tendon repairs. Thus, statistically significant differences should therefore be interpreted as associations rather than true equivalence or superiority between techniques, at this time. Larger scale, multicenter studies are recommended to further delineate differences in patient outcomes and complications between the surgical techniques for this rare injury. As such, in this study, only 47 cases of triceps tendon repairs were performed within a 10-year period at a large academic health-care setting with multiple surgeons specializing in upper extremity pathologies. Follow-up was not standardized across patients, which may affect the completeness of outcome capture. Another potential confounder in this study was due to surgeon effect. Namely, surgeons may exhibit preferred fixation techniques and have variation in surgery-specific volume for these procedures, which may introduce bias into treating these injuries due to which specific fixation strategy was employed. Randomization may reduce this risk of bias and could help to distribute fixation technique by surgeon without the introduction of preference based on tear type or preferred fixation. Technique selection was influenced by tear characteristics rather than random assignment, introducing confounding by indication that limits causal interpretation of outcome differences between groups. Finally, the absence of patient-reported outcome measures represents an important gap, as functional strength alone may not fully capture the patient experience following surgical repair. Particularly, strength measurements using the Medical Research Council 0-5 muscle scale are a relatively gross measure of extension strength, and given the retrospective nature of this study, more specific and accurate biomechanical strength measurements were unable to be obtained. Potentially, future studies garnering more sensitive strength metrics and upper extremity specific patient reported outcome measures could add to the impact of this study and provide more evidence to which surgical technique confers the most beneficial patient functional outcomes. Finally, there was variability and inconsistencies in clinical follow-up post-operatively. Patient visits to other health-care systems or hospitals were not available for review, making the potential complication rate higher than was observed in this study given difficulty controlling for clinical follow-up in a retrospective study.

Conclusion

The purpose of this study was to compare surgical outcomes and complications across 3 repair techniques for acute distal triceps ruptures: trans-osseous bone tunnels, suture anchors, and combined anchor-tunnel constructs. We hypothesized that the 3 approaches would demonstrate comparable complication rates and clinical outcomes. In this retrospective study of 47 patients, we found that all 3 techniques demonstrated comparable safety profiles with respect to complication rates, re-rupture rates, and perioperative outcomes, while the combination construct group was associated with a greater improvement in elbow extension strength compared to the bone tunnels group but not when controlling for triceps tendon tear type and tendon retraction length. However, the overall sample size of our study was limited given the rare nature of this injury, and future clinical studies should aim to add to this work to help determine the optimal treatment strategy for triceps tendon injuries.
