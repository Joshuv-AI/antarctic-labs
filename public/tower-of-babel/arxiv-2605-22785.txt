Evaluating Commercial AI Chatbots as News Intermediaries
Mirac Suzgun, Emily Shen, Federico Bianchi, Alexander Spangher, Thomas Icard, Daniel E. Ho, Dan Jurafsky, James Zou

Abstract
AI chatbots are rapidly shaping how people encounter the news, yet no prior study has systematically measured how accurately these systems, with their proprietary search integrations and retrieval-synthesis pipelines, handle emerging facts across languages and world regions. We present a fourteen-day (February 9–22, 2026), real-time evaluation of six commercial AI chatbots (Gemini 3 Flash and Pro, Grok 4, Claude 4.5 Sonnet, GPT-5 and GPT-4o mini) on 2,100 factual questions derived from same-day BBC News reporting across six regional services (US & Canada, Arabic, Afrique, Hindi, Russian, Turkish), yielding 12,600 model-question instances. The best systems achieve over 90% multiple-choice accuracy on questions about events reported hours earlier, which is a step change over prior real-time QA benchmarks and an advancement in AI-mediated information access. These are, however, multiple-choice scores. The same systems lose 11–13% under free-response evaluation, and 16–17% on average across the cohort. We further characterize three consequential failure patterns. First , every model achieves its lowest accuracy on Hindi (79% vs. 89–91% elsewhere); the citation pattern is consistent with an Anglophone retrieval bias: models answering Hindi queries cite English Wikipedia more than any Hindi news outlet, displacing local journalism with Anglophone proxies that report different facts. Second , retrieval, not reasoning, failures drive over 70% of all errors across all languages. When models retrieve a correct source, they almost always extract the correct answer; the problem is to land on the right source in the first place. Third , models achieving 88–96% accuracy on well-formed questions reduce to 19–70% when questions contain subtle false premises, with the most vulnerable model accepting fabricated facts 64% of the time. We further identify a detection–accuracy paradox: the best false-premise detector ranks second in adversarial accuracy (abstention rate), while a weaker detector ranks first, showing that premise detection and answer recovery are partially independent capabilities. Overall, these results demonstrate that high accuracy (the metric most visible to users and developers) can mask systematic regional inequity, near-total dependence on retrieval infrastructure, and acute vulnerability to the kinds of imperfect queries real users pose.

Introduction

AI chatbots are rapidly becoming intermediaries between the public and the news—and the speed of this shift is without historical precedent. By October 2025, ChatGPT alone had reached 800 million weekly active users
(
Bellan 2025
)
, having been adopted by roughly 10% of the world’s adult population in under three years
(
Chatterji et al. 2025
)
. In the United States, 34% of adults have now used ChatGPT, roughly double the share two years earlier, with adoption reaching 58% among adults under 30
(
Sidoti & McClain 2025
)
. Among U.S. teenagers, 64% report using AI chatbots, with nearly one in three doing so daily
(
Faverio & Sidoti 2025
)
. This adoption extends to news consumption: about one in ten U.S. adults consult chatbots for news at least sometimes
(
Lipka & Eddy 2025
)
, while globally, 7% of news consumers use them weekly for this purpose, rising to 15% among those under 25
(
Newman et al. 2025
;
YouGov 2025
)
. These figures are growing rapidly, and the trajectory seems clear: AI-mediated news access is becoming a routine feature of civic life, particularly for younger generations who will rely on these systems throughout their social and political lifetimes.

Yet the very same surveys that document rising adoption also reflect fragile trust and reliability patterns. Among U.S. adults who use chatbots for news, roughly half report encountering information they believe to be inaccurate, and about one-third say they find it difficult to distinguish what is true from what is not
(
Wang & Haner 2025
)
. These concerns are not unfounded. Systematic evaluations have shown that LLM-generated citations frequently fail to support the claims they accompany—even in high-stakes domains like medicine, where 30–50% of individual statements may lack adequate source support
(
Wu et al. 2025
)
. The problem is compounded on the supply side: a large-scale audit of 1,500 American newspapers found that approximately 9% of newly published articles are now partially or fully AI-generated, with such use rarely disclosed and disproportionately concentrated in smaller, local outlets
(
Russell et al. 2025
)
. AI is therefore reshaping both the production
and
consumption of news simultaneously, raising urgent questions about the factual reliability and robustness of these systems as information intermediaries.

The stakes, however, extend beyond individual accuracy. News enables informed democratic participation, facilitates public accountability, and provides the shared evidentiary basis for productive discourse. Recent experiments show that conversational AI can meaningfully shift political attitudes, with persuasiveness driven by information-dense argumentation and that methods increasing AI persuasiveness systematically decrease factual accuracy
(
Hackenburg et al. 2025
)
. If chatbots serve simultaneously as persuasive conversational agents and primary news sources, the factual reliability of their outputs takes on civic significance.

This challenge is especially acute for
emerging news
: events unfolding within hours or days. Emerging (or developing) events are documented through real-time reporting that begins incomplete and evolves as new facts surface. For AI evaluation, they are uniquely demanding because they occur after model training concludes, thus forcing language models to rely on retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)—searching the live web, evaluating sources of uncertain quality, synthesizing potentially conflicting accounts, and preserving the exact details that separate accurate reporting from misinformation. Prior evaluations have predominantly assessed models on static benchmarks
(
Lin et al. 2022
;
Min et al. 2023
)
, tested base models without production retrieval infrastructure
(
Lewis et al. 2020
;
Asai et al. 2023
)
, or examined temporally sensitive questions without systematically investigating cross-linguistic performance or citation behavior
(
Kasai et al. 2023
;
Vu et al. 2024
)
. Another growing body of work on dynamic and temporal QA has established that temporal knowledge remains difficult even for strong models
(
Xu et al. 2025b
;
Pletenev et al. 2025
;
Lin et al. 2025
;
Li & Goyal 2025
;
Ouyang et al. 2025
)
.

Yet no prior study, to our knowledge, has measured how the
production systems
deployed to hundreds of millions of users—the web interfaces with proprietary search integrations, safety filters, and post-processing that define the actual user experience—perform on factual questions about emerging news across diverse global contexts.

We seek to address this gap through a real-time evaluation of six commercial AI chatbots—namely, OpenAI’s GPT-5 and GPT-4o-mini, Google’s Gemini 3 Pro and Flash, Anthropic’s Claude 4.5 Sonnet, and xAI’s Grok 4—measuring their ability to answer specific factual questions about emerging headlines, typically within 24 hours of publication. Our study spanned 14 consecutive days (February 9–22, 2026) and generated 2,100 five-option multiple-choice questions from contemporaneous reporting across six regional BBC News services covering populations totaling over two billion people: US & Canada (English), Arabic, Afrique (French), Hindi, Russian, and Turkish. Each question targeted concrete, verifiable details (e.g., exact quotes, specific figures, named entities, particular locations) ideally answerable only through accurate retrieval and interpretation of source material. All six models were evaluated in parallel on each day’s questions, yielding 12,600 model-question instances.

We selected BBC News as it uniquely satisfied our methodological requirements: editorially independent regional services staffed by local journalists (not translations), consistent journalistic standards across regions, relatively high public trust
(
Orth & Carl 2025
)
, and prominent web indexing. We note, however, that these conditions perhaps represent a favorable scenario for retrieval-augmented systems; performance on less well-indexed sources would plausibly be worse. Beyond standard evaluation, we constructed adversarial question sets---subtly altering factual premises of real news events---to test whether models can resist being led astray by imperfect or misleading queries.

Three limitations of our design warrant upfront acknowledgment. First, BBC is well-indexed on the open web, so our results describe retrieval performance in a favorable (not typical) setting; performance on less prominent outlets would plausibly be lower. Second, because questions are derived from specific BBC articles, models whose providers have more permissive crawling agreements with BBC are mechanically advantaged; citation-level analyses should be read accordingly. (1) Substantial advance in real-time factual accuracy.
Frontier AI chatbots have now reached a level of factual performance on emerging news that would have been difficult to anticipate even recently. The top four systems—Gemini 3 Flash (95.6%), Grok 4 (95.0%), Gemini 3 Pro (93.7%), and Claude 4.5 Sonnet (90.4%)—all exceed 90% accuracy on questions about events reported within the previous 24 hours, which is a substantial advance over prior real-time QA benchmarks in which the best retrieval-augmented systems reached roughly 70-80% on comparable tasks
(
Kasai et al. 2023
,

Meanwhile, our results show a gap among frontier providers: GPT-5 achieves 85.0% (10%+ behind the leading model and trailing all non-OpenAI models) while GPT-4o-mini, an older and smaller model, reaches only 69.0%. Given ChatGPT’s dominant market position, with over 800M weekly active users and an estimated 10% of the global adult population
(
Bellan 2025
;
Chatterji et al. 2025
)
, this gap means the most widely used chatbot is also, in our evaluation, the least accurate frontier model for emerging news.

However, this high baseline coexists with four patterns that complicate the picture of trustworthy AI-mediated news.

(2) Systematic regional inequality, salient in Hindi.
Hindi performance is lowest in all models tested: accuracy falls to 79% compared with 89–91% for all other regions, a gap that persists even when excluding the weakest model. Detailed error analysis reveals that this is not a language-comprehension failure (models generate fluent Hindi and reason competently in the language), but a retrieval-and-grounding failure in which models pivot to English-language sources that report different specific details than the Hindi original. Citation analysis exposes the mechanism: models answering Hindi queries cite English Wikipedia more frequently than any Hindi-language news outlet, mediating local journalism through an Anglophone informational lens. This finding extends prior work on cross-linguistic performance variation
(
Ahuja et al. 2023
;
Shafayat et al. 2024
)
and tokenizer inequity
(
Petrov et al. 2023
)
to a retrieval-mediated news setting where the bottleneck is not model capability but the search-and-synthesis pipeline.

(3) Retrieval, not reasoning, is a core bottleneck.

Retrieving the wrong source and failing to retrieve relevant content together account for over 70% of errors; disabling web search degrades accuracy by 31–46%. When models retrieve a correct source, they often extract the correct answer. We unify these failures as breakdowns in evidence binding (the problem of anchoring an answer to the specific proposition in the specific source a question requires).

We further present what we call a detection-accuracy paradox: the model best at detecting false premises (80%) ranks only second in adversarial accuracy, while a model with weaker detection (59%) achieves the highest adversarial accuracy through retrieval strength alone—establishing that robustness requires both premise detection and answer recovery. This finding connects to a growing literature on sycophancy

(5) Different models retrieve from materially different information ecosystems.
Citation analysis further reveals that models querying the same web about the same events rely on materially different source pools—a form of information fragmentation unique to AI-mediated access and invisible to the user. Each model produces a somewhat distinct citation fingerprint across regions, meaning that a user’s choice of chatbot determines not just accuracy but the informational lens through which news reaches them.

These findings arrive at a critical moment when AI chatbot adoption for news is accelerating, with the highest penetration among younger demographics
(
Newman et al. 2025

Faverio & Sidoti 2025

. If these systems serve different linguistic communities with different levels of reliability and robustness, mediate global news through an English-language informational lens, and remain vulnerable to imperfect queries—without transparent acknowledgment of these limitations—they risk entrenching inequities in global information access rather than democratizing it.

Experimental Setup

We evaluated how accurately commercial AI chatbots (such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Grok) answer factual questions about emerging news across six languages and world regions. Our automated pipeline operated in four stages: (1) daily article collection from BBC News regional services, (2) question generation, (3) parallel model evaluation with native web search enabled, and (4) automated scoring (Figure
1
). It ran daily for 14 consecutive days; all code, data, and evaluation outputs are publicly released.

2.3
Evaluated Models

We evaluated six AI chatbots from four providers, each in its
production configuration
with native web search enabled. This was a deliberate experimental design choice. We evaluated the combined system (that is, the LLM along with the full proprietary search, ranking, and synthesis pipeline) that determined the quality of information users actually receive, not base models in isolation.

Technically, all models’ knowledge cutoff dates precede the 14-day evaluation period (full API identifiers and cutoffs in Table
2
); so, truly emerging-news questions cannot be fully answered from parametric knowledge. The no-search ablation in §
4.3
quantifies the residual contribution of parametric inference (51–61%, vs. 89–97% with search).

2.4
Evaluation Protocol and Metrics

To ensure temporal fairness, all six models were evaluated
in parallel
for each day’s questions—every model-region pair launched as a concurrent sub-process—just so that all models faced identical questions under comparable web-indexing conditions. Responses were scored automatically by extracting the selected option from structured XML tags; format-noncompliant responses were retried up to three times and, if still non-parseable, counted as incorrect.

Our primary metric is
accuracy
: the fraction of questions answered correctly, reported by model, by region, and by their interaction, with means and standard deviations across the 14 evaluation days. But because accuracy alone does not capture the full picture of system reliability (for instance, a model might answer correctly while citing irrelevant or incorrect sources), we also analyze
citation behavior
: how often models cite sources, which domains they cite, and whether they cite the original article from which the question was derived. Finally, we classify all errors into a structured taxonomy and conduct both an ablation study (disabling web search) and an adversarial evaluation (introducing false premises into questions).

Our core evaluation produced 2,100 questions (14 days
\times
6 regions
\times
25 questions) evaluated by six models, yielding 12,600 model-question instances.

2.5
Reliance on Multiple-Choice

Our reliance on multiple-choice (MC) evaluation raises a natural concern about whether the format inflates accuracy by allowing option elimination. To assess this, we ran the full pipeline on a single day’s questions (April 6, 2026; 6 regions, 6 models, 25 questions per cell) in both MC and free-response (FR) conditions, yielding 850 paired items. FR responses were graded independently by three LLM judges (Claude Opus 4.7, GPT-5.4, and Gemini 3 Pro) against gold answers using a semantic-equivalence rubric. Inter-annotator agreement was high: Fleiss’
\kappa
= 0.915 across all three graders, with unanimous verdicts on 94.7% of items (805/850). Pairwise Cohen’s
\kappa
ranged from 0.901 to 0.929, all in the “almost perfect” band.

Across 850 paired items, MC accuracy was 86.9% while FR accuracy was 70.2–71.1% depending on grader (Figure
3
). The gap was largest for weaker models (GPT-4o-mini: 22%) and smallest for the strongest (Grok 4: 13%; Gemini 3 Pro: 11%). Crucially, model rankings were identical under MC and FR regardless of the grader choice, and per-model gap differences across graders never exceeded 4%.

These results confirm that MC evaluation provides a structured upper bound on absolute accuracy. We report MC results throughout the main text as the more precisely scored condition, noting that real-world open-ended performance is likely closer to the FR figures (or lower, given that naturalistic queries lack the temporal grounding and precise phrasing of our prompts).

The pattern persists when excluding GPT-4o-mini: the remaining five models still produce 19.6 errors per day on Hindi, compared with 6.6–9.6 for other regions. The deficit is also not simply one of “English vs. non-English”: French, Arabic, Russian, and Turkish all perform within 1.3–2.4% of English. The underperformance appears specific to Hindi, pointing to factors such as poorer web indexing of Devanagari-script content, weaker transliteration normalization for Hindi named entities, or less effective Hindi-language capabilities in retrieval systems, rather than a generic non-English penalty.

A portion of the raw Hindi deficit also reflects topic mix: BBC Hindi oversamples local, transportation, and education coverage, on which models underperform across regions. Topic-conditioned comparison reduces but does not eliminate the gap (within education, for instance, Hindi still trails the same-topic accuracy in the other five regions), so language and indexing remain the dominant drivers; article-mix is a contributing factor, not the principal one.

Crucially, the Hindi deficit is
not
a failure of language comprehension. Models generate fluent Hindi and reason competently in the language. The failures are overwhelmingly failures of
retrieval and grounding
: models fail to locate relevant Hindi article(s), pivot to English-language sources covering the same topic but reporting different specific details, and answer from those substitutes with misplaced confidence. The result is a distinctive error signature—answers that are
globally plausible but locally wrong

4
Analysis

4.1
Citation Behavior and Source Attribution

A reliable and trustworthy information system should not only answer correctly but tell users
where its information comes from
.
Models were explicitly prompted to cite any sources consulted (§
2.4
); the analysis below therefore reflects compliance with that instruction in addition to underlying retrieval behavior.
We analyzed every URL citation across all responses, examining citation frequency, density, original-source attribution, and domain composition.

4.1.1
Citation Rates & Dissociation from Accuracy

Models varied considerably in citation frequency. Gemini 3 Flash (98.3%) and GPT-5 (97.8%) cited at least one URL in nearly every response; Claude 4.5 Sonnet followed at 96.2%; while Grok 4 (88.1%), GPT-4o-mini (86.2%), and Gemini 3 Pro (84.7%) left roughly one in seven responses without any source attribution.

Citation density ranged from 4.0 URLs per response (Gemini 3 Flash) to 1.3 (GPT-4o-mini).

Citation frequency does not predict accuracy or groundedness.
Gemini 3 Pro ranked last in citation rate (84.7%) yet third in accuracy (93.7%); GPT-5 ranked second in citation rate (97.8%) yet fifth in accuracy (85.0%). Across the six models, the rank correlation between citation rate and accuracy is small and not statistically significant (Spearman
\rho
= 0.31,
p
= 0.54). This dissociation resonates with a growing body of evidence that citations in AI-generated text routinely fail to support the claims they accompany
(
Jaźwińska & Chandrasekar 2025
;
Lafferty 2025
)
. The appearance of citations sometimes creates an
illusion of evidentiary grounding
that may not necessarily reflect the actual epistemic relationship between source and claim. What matters is whether the
right
and
relevant
sources are found, correctly interpreted, and faithfully reasoned through, which is a distinction that citation counts alone cannot capture.

Regionally, citation rates were lowest for Hindi (86%, with 328.8 total citations per day across all models) and highest for US & Canada, Afrique, and Turkish (all roughly 94%, with 385–417 citations per day). The parallel between lower citation volume and lower accuracy for Hindi suggests a shared upstream cause: when retrieval systems fail to surface sufficient relevant content, both citation density and answer quality decline in tandem.

Source divergence: anatomy of a dominant failure mode.

Because source divergence accounts for the second-largest share of errors (32.7%, behind retrieval failure at 38.8%) and is central to the Hindi gap, we examined its internal structure through detailed qualitative analysis.
The unifying pattern is that models retrieve something
near
the target (a source covering the same topic but reporting different specific details) and answer faithfully from that proximate source. These are
“smart wrong”
answers: the model’s response is internally consistent with the evidence it consulted and would be defensible as general knowledge, yet it is incorrect with respect to the specific factual claim in the target article. This is not hallucination in the conventional sense (the model is not fabricating information) but rather a failure of
evidence binding
: anchoring the answer to the specific proposition in the specific source that the question requires.
Three distinct sub-mechanisms drive source divergence, all sharing a common structure: the model retrieves a source that is
topically proximate
but
factually distinct
, then answers faithfully from it.

Cross-lingual source substitution
is a prevailing issue in Hindi errors: the retrieval system surfaces an English-language source (typically Wikipedia or an English news summary) that covers the same broad topic but reports a different figure or quote than the Hindi original. For instance, English maritime reports state that Indian merchant mariners constitute 10–12% of the global workforce; the target BBC Hindi article reports 7%. The model answers from the English source because retrieval ranked it higher, not because it cannot process Hindi.
Scope mismatch
is a language-independent variant of the same failure: the retrieved source covers the correct topic at the wrong level of aggregation, time period, or demographic scope (e.g., crude oil imports rather than total imports; men’s cricket participants rather than the combined total).
Quote-attribution displacement
occurs when models, lacking the target article, reconstruct domain knowledge rather than recovering a speaker’s specific quoted claim. These three sub-types form a gradient from retrieval-language failure to retrieval-scope failure to retrieval-specificity failure; what unifies them is that the model answers the wrong question correctly.

Retrieval failure concentrates on local and entity-specific content.
This category (38.8%) clusters around questions targeting hyper-local details, fine-grained named entities, and region-specific event particulars. Hindi and Afrique together carry the highest source-divergence rates (Hindi 38.5%, Afrique 39.5% of within-region errors), while Russian shows the highest outright retrieval failure rate (48.8%). Hindi failures frequently involve village-level incidents, local administrative details, or specific statements from individuals not widely covered in English media. In Russian, retrieval collisions with thematically adjacent but distinct stories are especially prominent: the model finds a different article about the same entity or topic family and answers from that instead.

4.4.2
Robustness: Detection & Recovery

The collapse in adversarial accuracy raises a more granular question: do models fail because they do not
notice
the false premise, or because they notice it but cannot
recover
the correct answer? We evaluated this on 100 adversarial questions per model. “Detection” required the model to explicitly flag the false premise or actively correct the injected falsehood in its reasoning; passively accepting the premise was scored as a failure regardless of the final answer.

Figure 13
:
False-premise detection rate vs. adversarial accuracy
(100 adversarial questions per model). Detection requires explicit flagging or correction of the injected falsehood in the model’s reasoning; adversarial accuracy is whether the final answer is correct (i.e., whether the model abstains in its response).

The results suggest a
detection–accuracy dissociation
: detection and recovery appear to be partially independent capabilities, though the small sample of four models limits the strength of this conclusion. Grok 4 illustrates robustness through retrieval: it detected false premises only 59% of the time yet achieved the highest adversarial accuracy (70.0%), because its retrieval pipeline frequently recovered correct information even without recognizing the question’s deceptive framing. This is effective but opaque: the model “looks past” the false premise rather than engaging with it, offering no guarantee of generalization to novel manipulation types. Claude 4.5 Sonnet illustrates the inverse: it detected 78% of false premises but achieved only 46.0% adversarial accuracy, identifying the deception in its reasoning but then failing to retrieve the correct alternative or selecting an incorrect option despite flagging the problem—a recovery failure that echoes
Turpin et al. 2023
’s finding that CoT-reasoning can be unfaithful to the actual basis of answer selection. GPT-5’s low detection rate (36%) aligns with its near-chance accuracy (19.0%): a model that does not notice false premises systematically produces conforming hallucinations.

The practical implication is that detection matters independently of accuracy. A model that detects a false premise but answers incorrectly at least signals to the user that the question’s framing is suspect; a model that accepts the premise and answers confidently provides no such signal. Current systems develop these capabilities unevenly, and standard benchmarks—which assume correct premises—miss the vulnerability entirely.

4.4.3
Sycophancy at the Retrieval Level

The adversarial findings expose a failure mode deeper than conversational sycophancy—the well-documented tendency of LLMs to agree with user assertions
(
Sharma et al. 2024
;
Wei et al. 2025
;
Cheng et al. 2026
)
. What we observe operates at the
retrieval-synthesis
level: models accept the user’s framing of events as ground truth, then search for and synthesize information consistent with that framing, rather than independently verifying the premise against retrieved evidence. In conversational sycophancy, the model defers to the user’s
opinion
; in retrieval-level sycophancy, the model defers to the user’s
facts
, constructing an evidential edifice around a falsehood.
But see

(
Suzgun et al. 2024
;
Suzgun et al. 2025
)
.

Even Gemini 3 Pro (the strongest detector) accepted false premises 20% of the time and GPT-5 64% of the time. The error analysis in §
4.2
reveals a structurally parallel pattern in the non-adversarial setting: inference override errors occur when models reconstruct what “must have happened” from prior beliefs rather than extracting what the source reported. Adversarial conditions amplify this tendency by providing an explicit false scaffold. Standard benchmarks, which assume correct premises, entirely miss this vulnerability. Yet our results show it can transform an accurate system into one that fails more often than it succeeds.

5
Discussion

The dominant failure mode across our evaluation is not reasoning but
evidence selection
: models almost always extract the correct answer when they retrieve the correct source, but the retrieval system frequently surfaces topically adjacent sources with different specific details. We term the resulting errors “smart wrong” answers—internally consistent with the consulted evidence but incorrect for the question asked. The binding constraint is the fidelity of the connection between query and evidence, what we call
evidence binding
, and this framing unifies our central findings: the Hindi gap is an evidence-binding failure at the retrieval layer; the adversarial collapse is an evidence-binding failure at the premise level; provider-level variation reflects differences in the legal compliance and engineering design of retrieval infrastructure as much as model capability.
15
15

15

System-prompt design is a further source of cross-product variation that we cannot characterize symmetrically across all evaluated products: of the five frontier systems we test, only Grok and Claude publish default system-prompt text in a form we can quote. We summarize the available evidence—and the scope of inference it supports—in App.

A free-response validation confirms that MC format inflates absolute accuracy by 16–17% but preserves model rankings and the relative patterns reported here (§

Retrieval as information infrastructure.
If retrieval drives over 70% of errors, then AI-mediated news quality depends less on model architecture than on search infrastructure—indexing coverage, source ranking, and multilingual query handling. Improving Hindi accuracy requires interventions in the retrieval layer (better Devanagari indexing, higher ranking for local-language sources, improved transliteration normalization, etc.) more than further model scaling.

The Anglophone lens, licensing, and information equity.
English Wikipedia is cited more frequently than any Hindi news outlet for Hindi queries, meaning AI systems mediate non-English news through an English-language informational lens. However, the citation patterns we observe reflect not only retrieval engineering but also the legal landscape of web scraping: providers that comply with
robots.txt
restrictions and licensing demands necessarily cite restricted sources less frequently, regardless of retrieval capability. The result is that a user’s informational access is shaped by an interaction of retrieval engineering, legal compliance, and commercial agreements that is entirely opaque to them. As AI-mediated news access scales—a 2026 Reuters Institute survey reports that news executives expect a roughly 43% decline in Google search traffic to publishers over three years
(
Fang 2026
)
—these dynamics will increasingly determine whose journalism reaches the public.

Adversarial vulnerability as a systemic risk.
The collapse from 95% to as low as 19% under adversarial conditions reveals a risk that aggregate accuracy metrics entirely fail to capture. High baseline accuracy builds user trust; adversarial fragility means that trust can be exploited through the kinds of imperfect queries that users routinely pose or that bad actors could deliberately construct.

5.1
Limitations

Our evaluation uses five-option multiple-choice questions, which provide structure that aids performance: a single-day free-response validation showed a 16–17% accuracy drop (§
2.5
), though model rankings remained stable. Open-ended queries in naturalistic settings would likely yield even lower accuracy and expose additional failure modes such as hallucinated details within otherwise correct responses. The multiple-choice format also means that a small number of errors may reflect option-mapping failures---where reasoning identifies the correct fact but the final answer selects the wrong letter---rather than genuine misunderstanding.
16
16

16

We observed instances where models stated the correct answer in their reasoning but selected the wrong option letter—e.g., stating “the threshold is —10 crore” and then choosing option (C) instead of (A). These are answer-serialization failures, not knowledge or reasoning deficits/issues, but our framework scores them identically.

We were unable to report a human-reader baseline on the same question set due to the time-sensitive nature of the experiments. In practice, it proved difficult to run both the model evaluations and corresponding human evaluations in parallel and in real time. Without one, the absolute accuracy figures cannot be benchmarked against human performance under matched conditions, though comparisons across systems, regions, and formats remain meaningful.

Our manual auditing also surfaced a small number of gold-label defects in the 2,100-question pool: two duplicate-option items in which two letters carried identical text, one explanation–gold mismatch, and one row whose correct answer field contained question-generator scaffolding in place of a single letter. We corrected these four labels at load time via an in-code errata table; the resulting shift on aggregate accuracy was small (well under 30 of 12,600 model–question rows). A handful of additional rows were arguably ambiguous—for example, the India–Bangladesh border length question, where the article’s wording (“2,500 miles”) and the real-world canonical figure (4,096 km) point to different option letters—and we score these as written.

A further set of edge cases has also survived the automated checks.
Source-rounded golds
are items where the BBC’s stated figure is itself an approximation a careful model may reasonably refine (e.g., “about six million viewers” against a computed weighted average of 4.95 million).
Threshold-collapsed options
are items where the source asserts “less than C$5m” but the option set carries an exact “C$5m” as gold.
Qualifier elisions
are items in which “more than 34%” becomes a flat “34%” option. Scoring is literal in all three cases, which can penalize an answer that is materially correct but does not match the article’s rounded shorthand. The cumulative effect on aggregate accuracy is bounded but not negligible, and a closer audit pass is planned for a subsequent revision. Multiple human review passes on the question pool would strengthen confidence in the marginal cases that survive automated generation.

BBC News presents a complex retrieval scenario. On one hand, it is a well-indexed, high-trust source, meaning our accuracy figures likely represent favorable conditions relative to less prominent outlets. On the other hand, precisely because the BBC actively enforces scraping restrictions and pursues licensing agreements, providers that comply with these policies may find BBC content
harder
to retrieve than less legally protected sources—a paradox in which high-quality journalism is simultaneously the most desirable and least accessible input for AI systems.

All API queries were issued from U.S.-based servers. Because search engines personalize results by geographic location, Hindi queries issued from a U.S. IP address may have been more likely to return English-language sources than queries issued from India—potentially amplifying the Anglophone retrieval pivot we document. Testing with geographically distributed query origins is an important direction for future work.

Our 14-day period captures stable patterns but represents a snapshot; model performance may shift with provider updates, and longitudinal tracking would strengthen confidence in the durability of these findings. Results reflect the specific API model versions and search backends available between February 9–22, 2026; continuous-deployment updates to weights, retrieval indexes, ranking logic, or safety filters can shift behavior between fixed model identifiers.

Two methodological choices in the secondary analyses also bound the strength of the claims they support. (
i
) The adversarial set induces false premises through a specific authorial protocol on a four-day, English-only slice of 100 items per model; the resulting accuracy collapses are best read as
suggestive probes
of robustness ceilings rather than as direct readouts of routine real-world behavior. Alternative falsification strategies (different premise types, multilingual adversarial sets, naturalistic user errors) might yield different detection-vs-recovery distributions. (
ii
) Free-response responses are scored by three LLM judges (Fleiss’
\kappa
= 0.915); absolute FR rates carry roughly a
\pm
2–3-point uncertainty tied to grader disagreement, though the MC
\to
FR gap and model rankings are stable across graders. Inter-judge agreement is high but does not substitute for human-anchored validation; a small human-graded sample is planned for subsequent work. Two further methodology notes—coarse domain-language tagging and the multiple causal readings of the Anglophone retrieval pivot—are deferred to Appendix
H
.

Cross-regional comparisons also inherit a residual confound from topic mix: BBC Hindi oversamples local, transportation, and education coverage, on which models underperform across regions, so article-mix accounts for part of the raw Hindi gap reported in §
3.2
(see also the topic-conditioned comparison there). Separately, all questions are generated by a single LLM (Gemini 3 Flash); the three mitigations described in §
2.2
hold any residual generator–solver alignment to within roughly 1% at the top of the leaderboard in cross-region checks, but the possibility cannot be fully ruled out, and fine-grained leaderboard distinctions of that magnitude should be read with this caveat.

Finally, we acknowledge, once again, that we do not claim that multiple-choice accuracy translates directly to the quality of open-ended conversational responses. Our framework measures one well-defined dimension of factual robustness and reliability (the ability to identify correct answers about recent events) and the patterns we document are likely to manifest in open-ended interactions as well, though the precise magnitude may differ.

6
Conclusion

We have shown that many modern AI chatbots, equipped with native web-search capabilities, can now answer factual questions about same-day news with 94–96% accuracy—a capability that, combined with their scale of deployment, might position them as significant intermediaries in global information access. We have also shown that this capability is more fragile, more uneven, and more dependent on opaque retrieval infrastructure than aggregate accuracy suggests.

Three findings carry particular weight, however. The Hindi gap demonstrates that retrieval infrastructure—not model capability—can create systematic inequity in who receives reliable AI-mediated information and who does not. The error taxonomy demonstrates that the dominant failure mode is driven by retrieval, rather than reasoning; models either fail to retrieve relevant content or retrieve a topically-adjacent source and answer the wrong question correctly—a pattern we trace to a shared evidence-binding bottleneck. And the adversarial evaluation demonstrates that the same systems’ accuracy can collapse from 88–96% to as low as 19% by the kinds of imperfect queries that real users routinely pose, with detection of false premises and recovery of correct answers operating as independent, unreliable capabilities.

Taken together, these results suggest that evaluating AI news intermediaries on aggregate accuracy alone is insufficient. Such evaluations must also measure, among other things, retrieval fidelity across languages, source attribution practices and their legal determinants, and robustness to imperfect premises—dimensions on which current systems vary dramatically and on which users have no visibility. The gap between the reliability these systems project and the reliability they deliver falls disproportionately on Hindi-language users (and likely on others whose primary language is sparsely indexed on the open web), on users who pose imperfect questions, and on the journalists whose reporting is consumed but rarely credited. As AI-mediated news access scales, making that gap visible is a prerequisite for these systems to serve, rather than distort, the public’s relationship with the news.

Related Work

Our study intersects five research areas that have largely progressed independently: factuality and attribution measurement, retrieval-augmented generation, temporally dynamic question answering, multilingual evaluation, and robustness to misleading prompts. We also build directly on emerging applied work auditing AI systems as news intermediaries.

Factuality, attribution, and citation quality.

The AIS framework established that claims should be attributable to identified sources, not merely plausible
(
Rashkin et al. 2023
)
. FActScore refined this by decomposing outputs into atomic claims verified individually, revealing that coarse response-level judgments often conceal mixtures of correct and unsupported content
(
Min et al. 2023
)

(
Gao et al. 2023
)
, while audits of generative search systems showed that fluent, cited answers can still be weakly supported or supported by the wrong evidence
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
. Domain experts found early chatbots mostly accurate but noted frequent reliance on tangentially relevant Wikipedia articles rather than authoritative sources
(
Peskoff & Stewart 2023
)
—a pattern our citation analysis confirms at scale for news. Most recently, SourceCheckup demonstrated that 30–50% of LLM medical claims lack adequate citation support even when sources are provided
(
Wu et al. 2025
)
. We extend this line of work from general factuality to emerging news, where both the correctness of answers and the provenance of cited sources carry civic significance.

Retrieval-augmented generation.

RAG demonstrated early that coupling parametric models with external retrieval reduces reliance on static internal memory
(
Lewis et al. 2020
)
. Subsequent work refined retrieval policies
(
Asai et al. 2023
)
and developed diagnostic evaluation frameworks that decompose RAG performance into retrieval relevance, answer faithfulness, and related dimensions
(

;
Saad-Falcon et al. 2024
;
Ru et al. 2024
;
Park et al. 2025
)
. CRAG extended evaluation to web retrieval under more demanding conditions
(
Yan et al. 2024
)
. Most RAG evaluation, however, uses research pipelines or controlled corpora rather than the proprietary retrieval stacks of deployed assistants. Our study complements this literature by auditing the end-to-end production systems that users actually encounter—where model, search infrastructure, ranking policy, synthesis behavior, and citation interface jointly determine reliability.

Time-sensitive and evolving-knowledge QA.

RealTimeQA established an ongoing benchmark for current-events questions, demonstrating that static parametric knowledge cannot substitute for up-to-date retrieval
(
Kasai et al. 2023
)
. FreshQA distinguished categories including fast-changing knowledge and false-premise questions, showing that temporal QA remains difficult even for strong models
(
Vu et al. 2024
)
. A recent survey of temporal QA comprehensively maps the landscape of benchmarks, retrieval strategies, and temporal reasoning challenges
(
Piryani et al. 2025
)
. Several 2025 benchmarks have advanced this agenda further: CDQA targets Chinese dynamic questions drawn from real-time news, classified by answer-change frequency
(
Xu et al. 2025b
)

(
Lin et al. 2025

(
Li & Goyal 2025

; and HoH examines how outdated information in retrieval corpora degrades RAG performance
(
Ouyang et al. 2025

(
Holtermann et al. 2025
)
. These benchmarks are predominantly English, typically evaluate base models rather than production systems, and do not systematically analyze citation behavior. Our study builds on their insight that timeliness is a core dimension of factual reliability, but examines it as users of commercial assistants experience it: through same-day reporting, live retrieval, and a multilingual web.

Multilingual evaluation and cross-linguistic inequity.

MEGA provided one of the earliest broad evaluations of generative LLMs across many languages, documenting large cross-linguistic variation
(
Ahuja et al. 2023
)
. Related work confirmed substantial degradation outside English
(
Lai et al. 2023

. Tokenizer inequity offers a structural explanation: non-Latin scripts suffer dramatically different tokenization efficiency, affecting context length, latency, and cost
(

(

(
Koto et al. 2024

Yüksel et al. 2024
)
.

17

Recent experimental work by
Poole-Dayan et al. 2026
extends these findings from static benchmarks to interactive settings, demonstrating that state-of-the-art chatbots provide less accurate and less truthful responses to users described as having lower English proficiency or non-U.S. origins, with compounding effects at the intersection of these categories. Their finding that some models refuse to answer at elevated rates for such users—and, when they do answer, sometimes adopt condescending language—suggests that the retrieval-mediated inequities we document are embedded within a broader pattern of differential model behavior toward linguistically and demographically diverse users.

Yet most multilingual evaluation uses static exams or general knowledge tasks. Our contribution tests multilingual performance in a retrieval-mediated news setting where the challenge is not only whether the model understands the language but whether the full system—retrieval, ranking, synthesis—operates with comparable reliability across scripts and regions.

False premises, sycophancy, and robustness.

TruthfulQA showed that models often reproduce plausible-sounding falsehoods rather than rejecting them
(
Lin et al. 2022

. False-premise QA demonstrated that models can be misled by question framing even when corrective facts are available

Suzgun et al. 2025

(
Turpin et al. 2023

(
Dahl et al. 2024
;
Zack et al. 2024
;
Magesh et al. 2025
)
. Our adversarial evaluation extends this line from closed-book or synthetic settings to live retrieval-mediated news, asking a more realistic question: when a user asks about a recent event but embeds a subtle falsehood, does the assistant verify the premise against retrieved evidence or synthesize around the user’s mistaken framing?
18
18

18

Shaw & Nave 2026
provide a complementary framing, arguing that AI systems are fostering
cognitive surrender
—a growing tendency for users to delegate reasoning and verification to AI, accepting outputs with diminishing scrutiny. In a news context, this dynamic means that the adversarial vulnerabilities we document are worth emphasizing.

AI and the news ecosystem.

One close applied precursor to our work is the BBC/EBU cross-market audit of AI assistants and news integrity, which documented substantial problems in accuracy, sourcing, and confidence across assistants and markets, finding that 51% of AI-generated answers about BBC content contained significant issues
(
Ford & Eisele 2025
)
. Our study is methodologically narrower but more controlled: we use a single news organization with harmonized editorial standards, generate synchronized daily question sets from contemporaneous reporting, evaluate all models in parallel under comparable temporal conditions, and analyze not only accuracy but also citation behavior, retrieval error mechanisms, search ablations, and adversarial robustness. In parallel, the broader landscape of AI-news interaction is evolving rapidly. Surveys of newsroom adoption show widespread experimentation with AI for content production, distribution, and personalization
(
Reuters Institute for the Study of Journalism 2025
;
Reuters Institute for the Study of Journalism 2026
)
, while audience research reveals ambivalent attitudes toward AI-personalized news, with comfort levels varying substantially across domains and demographics
(
Arguedas 2025
)
. The finding that AI-generated content in American newspapers is prevalent but rarely disclosed
(
Russell et al. 2025
)
adds further urgency. Analyses of the information ecosystem have begun to map the structural consequences: Google search traffic to publishers fell by a third globally in the year to November 2025, and news executives now anticipate a further 43% decline over the next three years
(
Fang 2026
)
. When AI intermediaries simultaneously consume journalism as input, displace it as a user-facing product, and rarely attribute the original source—as our citation analysis demonstrates—the economic model that sustains the reporting on which these systems depend comes under direct threat.

How our study differs.

The gap we address is not that prior work ignored factuality, retrieval, timeliness, multilinguality, or robustness—on the contrary, our design inherits important insights from each of these literatures. The gap lies in their conjunction. We are not aware of prior work that jointly evaluates
production
commercial assistants with native web search, on questions derived from genuinely emerging news, across multiple languages and scripts, while analyzing citation behavior, retrieval error mechanisms, search ablations, and adversarial false-premise robustness within the same experimental framework. A model can look strong on English factual QA yet fail on non-English retrieval; strong on clean temporal questions yet brittle to false premises; or accurate overall while rarely attributing the original reporting that established the facts. Evaluating these dimensions separately is necessary but insufficient for understanding AI-mediated news access as users experience it.

Three axes of difference between the two public prompts.
The two prompts diverge in their default posture along three dimensions that bear on news-question behavior.

Recency framing.
Grok’s prompt asserts: ‘‘Your knowledge is continuously updated - no strict knowledge cutoff.’’ Claude’s prompt names an explicit cutoff (the beginning of August 2025) and instructs that ‘‘[i]f asked or told about events or news that occurred or might have occurred after this cutoff date, Claude often can’t know either way and explicitly lets the person know this.’’
21
21

21

The prompt-named cutoff (August 2025) is later than the model’s documented training cutoff (January 2025; Table
2
); we read this as a product-surface convention rather than evidence that training extends further.

Search behavior.
Grok’s prompt says: “For searching the X ecosystem, do not shy away from deeper and wider searches to capture specific details and information based on the X interaction of specific users/entities. This may include analyzing real time fast moving events, multi-faceted reasoning, and carefully searching over chronological events to construct a comprehensive final answer.” Claude’s prompt directs the model toward a (user-toggled) web-search tool: “[Claude] responds with the most recent information per its knowledge cutoff, acknowledges its answer may be outdated and clearly states the possibility of developments since the knowledge cut-off date, directing the person to web search.”

Uncertainty calibration.
Grok is told that “[t]he response should not shy away from making claims which are politically incorrect, as long as they are well substantiated,” and to “search for a distribution of sources that represents all parties/stakeholders.” Claude is told that “[i]f Claude is not absolutely certain the information it is recalling is true and pertinent to the person’s query, Claude will state this. Claude then tells the person they can turn on the web search tool for more up-to-date information.” Both prompts contain extensive material we omit here—formatting conventions, refusal policies, safety provisions, product metadata—that does not bear directly on news-question behavior.

Scope of inference.
These two examples evidence wide variation in publicly chosen defaults around recency, search aggressiveness, and uncertainty calibration. We do not, however, claim that differences in default posture
cause
the performance differences we report. Even comparing only the two products with public prompts, the prompt text co-varies with: training-corpus composition (which is incompletely disclosed by every vendor we examine), the retrieval back-end and index, the source-ranker and citation model, the tool inventory exposed at inference, and decoding parameters—any of which could individually account for cross-product behavioral differences of the magnitude we report. For the three products without public prompts, symmetric comparison is not possible from public information alone, and the absence of a published prompt is not evidence of the absence of comparable directives. A clean isolation experiment would route every product through APIs with a uniform user-supplied system message; even then, training data and proprietary back-end retrieval would remain confounded across products. We therefore flag system-prompt design as one factor whose contribution to the cross-product variation we measure is, on the basis of publicly available evidence alone,
underdetermined
rather than negligible.

Multiple-Choice vs. Free-Response Validation: Extended Results

To assess whether the multiple-choice (MC) format used in our main evaluation inflates accuracy or distorts model rankings, we conducted a parallel free-response (FR) evaluation on a single day’s questions (April 6, 2026). The same 150 base questions (25 per region
\times
6 regions) were administered to all six models in both formats: MC with five labeled options (A–E), and FR with the question stem only, requiring the model to produce the answer without options to select from. This yielded 850 paired MC–FR items across 34 of the 36 (model, region) cells. The remaining 50 items (two cells, both for Gemini 3 Pro) were excluded because the underlying model outputs or judge verdicts were incomplete: no MC data was recovered for Hindi on the evaluation day (the model’s April 6 MC output file is absent from the evaluation record), and only 4 of 25 FR items completed judging on Afrique. All paired-comparison numbers reported below—including model rankings, the MC
\to
FR gap, and the inter-judge agreement statistics—are computed on this 850-item paired set.

FR responses were graded by three independent LLM judges—Claude Opus 4.7 (with three independent runs per item and majority vote), GPT-5.4, and Gemini 3 Pro—using a semantic-equivalence rubric. The rubric accepted paraphrases and close numerical rounding (e.g., “roughly $50,000” for an exact figure of $50,000) while scoring as incorrect any hedged non-answers (“I could not find…”), material contradictions, or substantively different facts. Inter-annotator agreement was high across all metrics: Fleiss’
\kappa
= 0.915, pairwise Cohen’s
\kappa
between 0.901 and 0.929, and unanimous three-way agreement on 94.7% of items (805/850). Disagreements concentrated in Russian and French responses involving paraphrase-heavy answers, and in generator outputs (Claude, Gemini Flash) that included longer explanatory context around the core answer.

The MC
\to
FR accuracy drop was 16–17% in aggregate (MC: 86.9%; FR: 70.2–71.1% depending on grader). This gap reflects the well-established advantage that recognition (selecting among options) confers over recall (producing answers from memory), compounded in our setting by the fact that MC options can support elimination strategies even when retrieval is incomplete. The gap was not uniform across models: it was smallest for Grok 4 (
\sim
13%) and Gemini 3 Pro (
\sim
11%), both of which appear to retrieve sufficiently specific information to produce correct answers even without option scaffolding, and largest for GPT-4o-mini (
\sim
22%), suggesting that this model’s MC performance was disproportionately supported by option elimination rather than precise factual recall. GPT-5’s MC
\to
FR gap was intermediate (
\sim

Crucially, model rankings were identical under MC and FR regardless of which grader was used, and per-model gap differences across graders never exceeded 4%. The relative patterns reported in the main text—which model outperforms which, and the Hindi gap, retrieval dominance, and adversarial findings—are therefore robust to evaluation format, even as absolute accuracy levels should be interpreted as upper bounds on what these systems would achieve in naturalistic open-ended interactions.

Appendix H
Additional Methodological Notes

Domain-language classification is coarse.

We assign each domain a single primary content-language tag at the bare-host level. This approximates the reality that many outlets run multilingual newsrooms or maintain multiple language editions. The classification of
aljazeera.com
(Al Jazeera’s English-language flagship) versus
aljazeera.net
(the Arabic flagship) is one example where finer-grained handling matters; we treat them as separate domains rather than a unified “Al Jazeera” entity, and tag each by the dominant content language of its bare host. Wikipedia language editions are similarly kept separate (
en.wikipedia.org
,
hi.wikipedia.org
, etc.). Outlets whose bare host serves predominantly one language but who run a smaller secondary-language edition are tagged by the primary, accepting that any single-tag classification approximates a continuous reality.

Multiple causal readings of the Anglophone retrieval pivot.

Our finding that models cite English-language sources even for non-English-region questions (§
4.1.3
) is consistent with several non-mutually-exclusive mechanisms: (
i
) genuine English-language source dominance for the events queried; (
ii
) training-data bias toward English content; (
iii
) ranking-time bias in the search back-ends used at inference; and (
iv
) generation-time bias in how models phrase queries to those back-ends. Distinguishing these mechanisms cleanly would require back-end-level instrumentation that is not publicly accessible for any of the products we evaluate. We therefore document the pattern descriptively and resist a single-mechanism causal claim.

Multiple-choice format.

We adopted multiple-choice rather than free-response evaluation to enable reliable automated scoring at scale across six languages. This format trades “ecological validity”—users typically pose open-ended queries—for measurement precision: each question has exactly one verifiable correct answer, eliminating subjective grading. Models were instructed to reason through each question before selecting an option; we report answer-formatting errors separately (§
4.2
) to isolate format-compliance failures from genuine knowledge deficits. The free-response validation in §
2.5
estimates the magnitude of the format-induced inflation.

Decoding parameters.

We used temperature 0.0 across all models to maximize determinism and reproducibility whenever possible and supported. We deliberately avoided third-party search APIs (e.g., Tavily), evaluating each provider’s own default retrieval infrastructure to reflect authentic user conditions.

Time-zone targeting.

We targeted 3:00 PM local time in each region’s central time zone: 1:30 AM PT for Hindi (IST, UTC+5:30), 4:00 AM PT for Arabic, Russian, and Turkish (all UTC+3), 6:00 AM PT for Afrique (WAT, UTC+1), and 12:00 PM PT for US & Canada (ET, UTC
-
5). Temporary delays occurred on at least two occasions due to server issues on our end and intermittent model API outages, but all models were evaluated on the same day’s questions under comparable, if not identical, conditions.

Citation-counting unit.

Throughout the citation analysis (§
4.1
), counts are at the
URL
level: each unique URL in a response counts once, so a response that cites three different Wikipedia articles contributes three citations to
en.wikipedia.org
. Within-response duplicates—the same URL appearing once with and once without a tracking parameter such as
?utm_source=openai
, or with vs. without a trailing slash—are collapsed via canonical-key deduplication and counted once. Citation
rate
(e.g., 98.3%) is response-level binary (a response either cites at least one URL or does not); citation
density
(e.g., 4.0 URLs per response) and the per-region/per-domain counts (e.g., cites/day for a domain) are URL-level.

LLM-judged error taxonomy.

The error-taxonomy classification (§
4.2
) was performed by three independent LLM judges (Claude Opus 4.7, GPT-5.4, Gemini 3 Pro) under a shared rubric. LLM-based annotation was chosen for scalability across six languages; human annotation at the same coverage was infeasible given multi-script content. Inter-judge agreement is reported alongside the taxonomy table, and we encourage readers to interpret category counts as approximate rather than definitive. Comparison against a small human-annotated sample is planned for subsequent work.
