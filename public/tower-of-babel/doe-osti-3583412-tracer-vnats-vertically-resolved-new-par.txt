1.0 Summary
 Aerosols are an important contributor to climate forcing on local to global scales due to their direct
 interactions with incoming solar radiation, both through scattering and absorption, and indirectly via their
 role as cloud condensation nuclei (CCN) (Albrecht 1989, Charlson et al. 1992). Accurate assessment of
 the relationship between CCN and forcing in global climate models requires an understanding of the
 sources that control the aerosol number concentration budget, such as new particle formation (NPF) from
 the photochemical reaction of aerosol precursors (e.g., sulfuric acid, biogenic volatile organic
 compounds) that result in the formation of ~ 1 nm new particles (C. Kuang, et al. 2009,
 Kerminen et al. 2005, Merikanto et al. 2010, Lihavainen et al. 2003). While there have been an increasing
 number of atmospheric cluster measurements from surface-based platforms, there have been very few
 measurements of the vertically resolved ambient cluster size distribution from aerial platforms. Vertically
 resolved atmospheric cluster measurements are needed because aerosol formation in the upper
 atmosphere may be a significant source of cloud condensation nuclei (Chen et al. 2018, Wang et al. 2016,
 Zheng et al. 2021, Lampilahti et al. 2021). Furthermore, these vertically resolved measurements are
 needed to: 1) connect the atmospheric conditions that drive atmospheric NPF with large-scale
 boundary-layer transport processes and meteorology; and 2) evaluate the extent to which surface-based
 aerosol measurements represent the atmospheric aerosol aloft.

 The goal of the TRACER Vertically Resolved NPF and Transport Study (TRACER-VNATS) was to
 develop process-level understanding of the formation and growth of atmospheric aerosol aloft through
 vertically resolved measurements of the atmospheric cluster number and size distribution. These
 observations were obtained as part of the larger U.S.Department of Energy Atmospheric Radiation
 Measurement (ARM) User Facility’s TRACER (Tracking Aerosol Convection Interactions Experiment),
 which provided high-temporal-and-spatial-resolution observations of convective clouds in the Houston,
 Texas region, over a broad range of environmental and aerosol regimes. Vertically resolved profiles of the
 atmospheric aerosol cluster concentration and size distribution were obtained at the ancillary site (ANC),
 a measurement location characterized by a more rural, “background” aerosol environment than the more
 urban, industrial aerosol environment of the main site (Figure 1).

 Figure 1. The ancillary (ANC) site in Guy, Texas, located in a farm field. From left to right: the ARM
 tethered balloon system (TBS) trailer, aerosol measurement and supporting containers, ARM
 meteorology field, TBS “igloo”, and TBS supporting trailer.

 1

A water-based condensation particle counter (CPC) was deployed with an aerosol inlet modified for the
detection of aerosol down to 1 nm in diameter, on board the ARM tethered balloon system (TBS), as
shown in Figure 2. The water CPC, manufactured by Aerosol Devices, Inc. (Hering et al. 2019), was
chosen for this proposal due to its responsiveness to the requirements for guest instruments deployed on
the TBS (weight, power requirements, and dimensions).The CPC modification and characterization
occurred according to established protocols (Kuang et al. 2012). Atmospheric cluster number
concentrations in a specified range (e.g., between 1 and 3 nanometers) were obtained by application of the
pulse height analysis technique, wherein the sampled aerosol size distribution is inferred from the grown
droplet size distribution (Kuang 2018). Measurements took place at the ANC site during the summer
2022 intensive operational period (IOP), with a two-week TBS deployment roughly at the start of each
month of the IOP (June, July, August, and September).

Figure 2. The water-based 1-nm CPC inside the enclosure (yellow arrow), attached to the tether of the
 ARM TBS.

 2

 Instrument Issues: The IOP at the ANC site was characterized by extended periods of high ambient
 temperature (e.g., >90 degrees Fahrenheit), which led to frequent overheating of the raspberry pi
 computer and multi-channel analyzer, resulting in periodic device malfunction and data loss, as well as
 occasional loss of temperature control in the CPC stages (e.g., conditioner, initiator, moderator, optics).
 These environmental challenges were somewhat mitigated through the frequent use of gel ice-packs in the
 CPC enclosure, as shown in Figure 3. Other (expected) instrument problems included water vapor
 depletion in the CPC growth tube (impacting the droplet growth kinetics and resulting final size) and
 optics window fogging with diethylene glycol vapor (leading to changes in the photo-detector
 background).

 Figure 3. The ice-packs (yellow arrow) inside the CPC TBS enclosure, along with the CPC and the
 data acquisition supporting systems.

2.0 Results
 Preliminary measurements indicate a wide range of vertical and temporal aerosol profile variability, from
 profiles with vertically well-mixed aerosol throughout the diurnal evolution of the boundary layer, to
 aerosol profiles with strong initial vertical variability that change with time as the sea breeze impacts the
 ANC site. Some examples of aerosol vertical profiles are shown below in Figure 4.

 3

Figure 4. Vertically resolved aerosol number concentrations, instrument housekeeping, and pulse
 heights measured on 09/05/22 in the morning (left panel, a-c) and in the afternoon (right
 panel, d-f). [a, d] number concentration and battery voltage (proxy for battery life). [b, e]
 CPC stage temperatures and sampled pressure (proxy for altitude). [c, f] CPC pulse heights
 indicating presence of sub-3 nanometer aerosol (pulse heights <1500) and pre-existing
 aerosol (pulse heights >2000).

For future research, these vertically resolved atmospheric cluster observations will be combined with
surface and TBS-based observations of the atmospheric thermodynamics and meteorology to
contextualize the evolution of boundary-layer transport processes, including influences from the sea
breeze. Additionally, the ANC site was periodically impacted by smoke plumes from nearby fires
(Figure 5), providing research opportunities to explore the impact of biomass burning on boundary-layer
aerosol evolution.
