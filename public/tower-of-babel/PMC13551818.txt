Clinical and immunological factors associated with gastrointestinal involvement in common variable immunodeficiency: a real-world study
Authors: Emrah Harman, Fatih Çölkesen, Mehmet Emin Gerek, Ferhat Sağun, Seçim Kolak, Şükran Aslan Savaş, İsmail Yiğitdöl, Ümmügülsüm Yılmaz Ergün, Şevket Arslan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastrointestinal (GI) involvement is a common and clinically significant manifestation of common variable immunodeficiency (CVID); however, its relationship with immunological parameters remains incompletely understood.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the association between GI involvement and immunological markers, particularly serum immunoglobulin levels and B-cell subsets, and to assess their discriminative performance.MethodsIn this single-center retrospective study, 109 adult patients with CVID were included and categorized based on GI involvement. Clinical, endoscopic, histopathological, and immunological data were analyzed. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with GI involvement. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to evaluate discriminative performance.ResultsGI involvement was present in 58.7% of patients. Patients with GI involvement had significantly lower serum IgA and IgM levels, as well as reduced percentages of CD19⁺CD27⁺IgD⁻ switched memory B cells. In multivariable analysis, low IgM levels (OR = 0.161, 95% CI: 0.041–0.633, p = 0.009) and decreased switched memory B-cell percentage (OR = 0.918, 95% CI: 0.855–0.986, p = 0.019) were independently associated with GI involvement. ROC analysis demonstrated good discriminative ability for serum IgM (AUC = 0.799, 95% CI: 0.708–0.889) and moderate discriminative performance for switched memory B cells (AUC = 0.695, 95% CI: 0.589–0.802). Endoscopic abnormalities were detected in 87.7% of evaluated patients, with inflammatory bowel disease-like patterns being the most common histopathological finding.ConclusionGI involvement is common in CVID and is independently associated with reduced IgM levels and decreased switched memory B cells, suggesting a potential role of impaired B-cell maturation in its pathophysiology. These findings may help identify patients at higher risk and support the clinical utility of immunophenotyping. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings and to establish clinically relevant cutoff values.Keywords: Common variable immunodeficiency, Gastrointestinal diseases, IgM, Memory B cells, Immunophenotyping

Introduction

Common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) comprises a heterogeneous group of hypogammaglobulinemia syndromes characterized by impaired B-cell differentiation and is often associated with T-cell abnormalities [1]. According to the current International Union of Immunological Societies (IUIS) classification, CVID is included within the spectrum of inborn errors of immunity (IEIs), a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by defects in immune system development and function [2]. It is the most common symptomatic primary immunodeficiency, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 1 in 25,000 individuals [3, 4]. Although CVID is most frequently diagnosed in adulthood, approximately 20–34% of cases are identified during childhood or adolescence [5, 6].

In addition to recurrent infections, nearly half of CVID patients exhibit signs of immune dysregulation, including autoimmunity, various inflammatory disorders, and/or malignancies. Patients may develop chronic lung disease, gastrointestinal (GI) and liver disorders, granulomatous infiltration of various organs, lymphoid hyperplasia, splenomegaly, or malignancy [7–9].

Among these complications, gastrointestinal involvement represents a clinically significant manifestation. The reported prevalence of gastrointestinal involvement in CVID varies widely across studies, ranging from approximately 20% to 71%, depending on patient selection, symptom definitions, and diagnostic criteria [10–13]. GI involvement constitutes an important source of morbidity and may present clinically with diarrhea, malabsorption, abdominal pain, and inflammatory bowel disease-like conditions [10]. Although immunoglobulin replacement therapy is effective at reducing infection frequency, it does not consistently prevent gastrointestinal involvement [14]. This observation suggests that GI manifestations in CVID cannot be explained solely by immunoglobulin deficiency and that underlying mechanisms of immune dysregulation likely play a critical role [15].

In recent years, B-cell subsets and immunophenotyping parameters have been increasingly recognized as being associated with disease phenotype and complications in CVID [6, 16]. However, studies specifically evaluating the relationship between gastrointestinal involvement and immunological parameters remain limited, and existing data are inconsistent. Data regarding the contribution of serum immunoglobulin levels and B-cell subsets to gastrointestinal manifestations in CVID are particularly scarce.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the relationship between gastrointestinal involvement and immunological parameters in patients with CVID, particularly serum immunoglobulin levels and percentages of CD19 + CD27+IgD− switched memory B cells, and to determine whether these parameters are independently associated with gastrointestinal involvement.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient population

This retrospective, single-center study was conducted at the Division of Allergy and Immunology, Department of Internal Medicine, Necmettin Erbakan University Faculty of Medicine. Adult patients (≥ 18 years) with a confirmed diagnosis of common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) who were followed between January 2019 and December 2025 were included in the study.

The diagnosis of CVID was established according to ESID diagnostic criteria [17, 18]. Patients were required to have a minimum follow-up of 6 months and to have immunological data available, including serum immunoglobulin levels and immunophenotyping results.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) age ≥ 18 years, (ii) diagnosis of CVID according to ESID/PAGID criteria, (iii) follow-up duration of at least 6 months, and (iv) availability of complete clinical and immunological data.

Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (i) inconsistency with CVID diagnostic criteria, (ii) missing clinical or laboratory data, or (iii) lack of consent for the use of their data for academic purposes in accordance with institutional policies.

Data collection

Clinical and laboratory data were obtained from electronic medical records (hospital information management system), outpatient follow-up records, laboratory databases, and endoscopy and pathology reports. All data were anonymized prior to analysis, and each patient was assigned a unique study identification number to ensure confidentiality.

Definition of study groups

Patients were divided into two groups based on the presence of gastrointestinal (GI) involvement: Group 1, CVID patients without GI involvement; and Group 2, CVID patients with GI involvement.

GI involvement was defined based on the combined evaluation of clinical symptoms, endoscopic findings, and/or histopathological abnormalities.

Autoimmunity was defined as the presence of a physician-diagnosed autoimmune disease documented in the medical records, including autoimmune cytopenias (e.g., immune thrombocytopenia and autoimmune hemolytic anemia), autoimmune thyroid disease, rheumatoid arthritis, and other established autoimmune disorders.

Definition of gastrointestinal involvement

Gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms were systematically assessed at each outpatient visit as part of routine clinical evaluation. Patients were regularly questioned regarding the presence of symptoms since their previous visit, including abdominal pain, dyspepsia (e.g., epigastric burning), gastroesophageal reflux symptoms, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, and abdominal distension. All findings were recorded in the patients’ follow-up records.

To increase the specificity of symptom-based classification and to exclude transient or nonspecific complaints, GI involvement was defined as the presence of symptoms persisting for at least three consecutive visits.

Patients with persistent GI symptoms were further evaluated, when clinically indicated, using upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and/or colonoscopy. The diagnosis of GI involvement was established based on the combined assessment of clinical symptoms, endoscopic findings, and, when available, histopathological evaluations. Histopathological diagnoses, including celiac-like enteropathy and CVID enteropathy, were recorded according to the final pathological interpretation documented in the pathology reports. In our center, the diagnosis of CVID enteropathy was generally based on histopathological findings such as villous atrophy, increased intraepithelial lymphocytes, and reduced or absent plasma cells, interpreted together with the clinical context by the reporting pathologist. No centralized histopathological re-evaluation was performed for this study. Malabsorption was defined by compatible clinical findings, supportive laboratory abnormalities (e.g., micronutrient deficiencies, hypoalbuminemia, or anemia), stool examination findings, and, when available, histopathological evidence suggestive of intestinal malabsorption. Gastritis was primarily defined by endoscopic findings documented in endoscopy reports. Findings described as erythematous gastritis, erosive gastritis, antral gastritis, or pangastritis were classified as gastritis. Histopathological findings were reviewed when available; however, the classification of gastritis was based on endoscopic rather than histological criteria. Endoscopic evaluation was not performed systematically in all patients but was based on clinical indications, reflecting real-world practice.

Although abdominal pain was the most frequently reported symptom, it was interpreted cautiously, as patients often used this term to describe upper gastrointestinal complaints such as dyspepsia, reflux, or bloating. Therefore, whenever possible, symptom-based assessments were supported by endoscopic and histopathological findings to improve diagnostic accuracy. Patients who did not undergo endoscopic evaluation were classified based solely on clinical criteria.

Immunological assessment

Serum IgG, IgA, IgM, and IgE levels were obtained from the hospital laboratory database. Immunoglobulin measurements were performed by nephelometry in the central laboratory as part of routine clinical practice. All measurements were performed in the same central laboratory using standardized methods.

Trough IgG levels were calculated as the mean of the most recent measurements obtained prior to immunoglobulin replacement therapy and were considered to reflect steady-state levels during follow-up.

Peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets were analyzed by flow cytometry using a BD FACSCanto™ II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). The immunophenotyping panel included CD3 + T cells, CD3 + CD4+ T cells, CD3 + CD8+ T cells, total CD19 + B cells, CD19 + memory B cells, and CD19 + CD27+IgD− switched memory B cells. Results were expressed as percentages of total lymphocytes.

Reference ranges for immunological parameters were defined according to institutional laboratory standards. All immunological measurements were performed during clinically stable periods, excluding episodes of acute infection.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR), as appropriate. Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using the independent-samples t-test for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with gastrointestinal involvement. Variables were selected based on clinical relevance, prior literature, and univariate analysis results. To minimize the risk of overfitting, the number of variables included in the model was restricted. The final model included age, sex, baseline IgA and IgM levels, and the percentage of CD19 + CD27+IgD− switched memory B cells.

Model fit was assessed using the omnibus test of model coefficients and the Nagelkerke R² coefficient. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Additionally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the discriminative ability of serum IgM levels and the percentage of CD19 + CD27+IgD− switched memory B cells for gastrointestinal involvement. The area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals was calculated.

There was no significant difference in current age between patients with and without GI involvement (44.0 [IQR 23] vs. 47.0 [IQR 23] years, respectively; p = 0.168). However, the age at CVID diagnosis was significantly lower in patients with GI involvement compared with those without GI involvement (31.0 [IQR 24] vs. 39.0 [IQR 29] years, respectively; p = 0.022). In addition, the follow-up duration was significantly longer in patients with GI involvement (72.0 [IQR 84] vs. 36.0 [IQR 84] months; p = 0.046).

Regarding clinical characteristics, splenomegaly and bronchiectasis were numerically more frequent in patients with GI involvement; however, these differences did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.228 and p = 0.382, respectively). Similarly, no significant difference was observed between the groups in the frequency of autoimmunity (p = 0.976). The sex distribution was also comparable between the groups (p = 0.423).

In the analysis of immunological parameters, serum IgA and IgM levels were significantly lower in patients with GI involvement (p = 0.002 and p < 0.001, respectively). In contrast, no significant differences were observed between the groups in IgE or trough IgG levels.

In lymphocyte subset analysis, there were no significant differences in the percentages of CD3+, CD4+, and CD8 + T cells between the groups. However, the percentage of CD19 + CD27+IgD− switched memory B cells was significantly lower in patients with GI involvement [1.2 (IQR 3.8) vs. 6.0 (IQR 10.1), p = 0.003]. In contrast, no significant difference in the percentage of total CD19 + memory B cells was observed between the groups (p = 0.800).

Exploratory analyses were performed to evaluate associations between specific gastrointestinal symptoms and immunological parameters. Lower serum IgM levels were significantly associated with diarrhea (p = 0.009) and abdominal pain (p < 0.001), but not with malabsorption (p = 0.122). Similarly, lower switched memory B-cell percentages were associated with abdominal pain (p = 0.003), but not with diarrhea (p = 0.568) or malabsorption (p = 0.680).

Gastrointestinal manifestations and endoscopic–histopathological findings

When gastrointestinal involvement was evaluated in the 109 CVID patients included in the study, the most common presenting complaint was abdominal pain (58.7%), followed by diarrhea (29.4%), other gastrointestinal symptoms (28.4%; including dyspeptic complaints, nausea, vomiting, constipation, and abdominal distension), and malabsorption (11.0%).

Among patients with gastrointestinal involvement, 57 (89.1%) underwent endoscopic evaluation, whereas 7 patients (10.9%) did not. All patients who underwent endoscopy had gastroscopy (100%), and 42 patients (73.7%) additionally underwent colonoscopy. Endoscopic abnormalities were identified in 50 of the 57 patients (87.7%) who underwent endoscopic evaluation.

Histopathological evaluation of patients who underwent endoscopy revealed pathological findings in 50 patients (87.7%). The most common histopathological finding was an inflammatory bowel disease-like pattern (24.6%), followed by celiac-like disease (12.3%), nodular lymphoid hyperplasia (12.3%), and CVID enteropathy (10.5%). Some patients had more than one histopathological finding.

Among patients who underwent endoscopic evaluation, the most common endoscopic findings were gastritis (98.2%), followed by Helicobacter pylori infection (26.3%) and ulcer (17.5%). Additional findings, including esophagitis, Barrett’s esophagus, and gastrointestinal polyps, were observed in a small number of patients (Table 2).

Discussion

In this retrospective real-world study, we evaluated the relationship between gastrointestinal involvement and immunological parameters in patients with common variable immunodeficiency (CVID). The main findings of our study are that gastrointestinal involvement was identified in 58.7% of the cohort (64/109) and was significantly associated with lower serum IgM levels and reduced percentages of CD19⁺CD27⁺IgD⁻ switched memory B cells. In multivariable analysis, both IgM levels and the percentage of switched memory B cells were independently associated with gastrointestinal involvement. In contrast, although serum IgA levels were lower in univariate analyses, they did not retain independent significance in the multivariable model, suggesting potential confounding with other immunological parameters. ROC analysis further supported these findings, demonstrating good discriminative performance for serum IgM (AUC = 0.799), whereas switched memory B cells showed only moderate discrimination (AUC = 0.695). These findings suggest that impairments in B-cell maturation and class-switch recombination may contribute to the underlying pathophysiology of gastrointestinal manifestations in CVID.

The role of humoral immunity in the pathogenesis of gastrointestinal involvement in CVID has long been a subject of investigation [14–16, 19]. Previous studies have demonstrated that reduced proportions of CD19 + CD27+IgD− switched memory B cells are associated with more severe clinical phenotypes of CVID, and that these patients more frequently exhibit complications such as splenomegaly, autoimmunity, and gastrointestinal involvement [20–23].

Our findings are consistent with those reported by Barmettler et al., who demonstrated that gastrointestinal manifestations in CVID are associated with an altered immunophenotype characterized by dysregulation of both B and T cells. In their cohort, patients with gastrointestinal involvement exhibited more pronounced immunological abnormalities, supporting the concept that gastrointestinal disease represents a distinct immunological phenotype within the spectrum of CVID manifestations. These observations further reinforce the association between immune dysregulation and gastrointestinal manifestations in CVID [13]. In this context, our finding that the percentage of switched memory B cells is independently associated with gastrointestinal involvement is consistent with previous reports and supports the notion that impaired B-cell maturation and class-switch recombination may play an important role in maintaining gastrointestinal mucosal immunity. Furthermore, this finding suggests that switched memory B cells may serve not only as an immunological marker but also as a reflection of disease severity and clinical phenotype.

Similarly, the identification of serum IgM levels as an independently associated factor is noteworthy. IgM is a key component of the early immune response, particularly at mucosal surfaces, where it plays an important role in pathogen neutralization and immune complex formation [24]. Reduced IgM levels have been linked to increased intestinal permeability, microbial translocation, and chronic inflammation. This concept is further supported by findings in CVID demonstrating microbial dysbiosis and impaired mucosal barrier function [25, 26]. These mechanisms may contribute to the development of inflammatory bowel disease-like patterns and enteropathy observed in patients with CVID [11, 27]. Given the limited number of studies demonstrating an independent association between IgM levels and gastrointestinal involvement, our findings add to the existing body of evidence and suggest a potential role of IgM in the pathophysiology of gastrointestinal manifestations in CVID.

In our study, although serum IgA levels were lower in univariate analyses, they did not retain independent significance in the multivariable model. This finding suggests that gastrointestinal involvement in CVID is unlikely to be explained solely by immunoglobulin levels [26]. One possible explanation is that the effect of IgA may overlap with shared biological pathways involving IgM and CD19 + CD27+IgD− switched memory B cells, reflecting potential interactions between these immunological parameters [22, 28]. Furthermore, these results imply that IgA deficiency alone may not be sufficient to drive gastrointestinal involvement and that deeper defects in B-cell differentiation and maturation may play a key role in determining the clinical phenotype.

The relatively high prevalence of gastrointestinal involvement observed in our study (58.7%) is noteworthy. Although the reported prevalence of gastrointestinal involvement in CVID ranges from 20% to 71%, this variability is largely due to differences in definitions, patient selection, and evaluation methods across studies [10, 11, 13, 19, 25]. The requirement of symptom persistence across three consecutive visits may have increased specificity by excluding transient complaints. However, systematic symptom assessment may also have increased sensitivity by capturing mild manifestations. The combined effect of these factors may explain why the prevalence observed in our cohort lies at the upper end of the range reported in the literature.

The fact that the majority of patients with gastrointestinal involvement (89.1%) underwent endoscopic evaluation represents a notable strength of our study, as it supports our findings with objective assessments. The detection of abnormalities in 87.7% of patients who underwent endoscopy suggests that gastrointestinal involvement in symptomatic patients is frequently associated with structural or inflammatory changes.

However, this relatively high rate should be interpreted with caution, as endoscopic evaluation was primarily performed in patients with persistent symptoms, which may have introduced selection bias. The identification of inflammatory bowel disease-like patterns and other nonspecific inflammatory changes in histopathological analyses further supports the notion that gastrointestinal involvement in these patients is heterogeneous and multifactorial [29]. Gastrointestinal involvement was analyzed as a composite outcome encompassing heterogeneous clinical entities, which may have introduced variability in the observed associations and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

The higher frequency of gastrointestinal involvement observed in patients with longer follow-up suggests a potential surveillance (observation time) bias. Patients with longer follow-up durations are more likely to experience symptoms and have them documented, which may have led to an overestimation of the true prevalence of gastrointestinal involvement.

The finding that patients with gastrointestinal involvement had a significantly lower age at diagnosis is also noteworthy. Two potential explanations may account for this observation. First, patients diagnosed at a younger age tend to have longer follow-up durations, thereby increasing the likelihood of detecting gastrointestinal manifestations. Second, earlier disease onset may be associated with a more severe clinical phenotype and a greater predisposition to gastrointestinal involvement. However, the causal nature of this relationship cannot be definitively established based on the current data.

In addition, a small number of patients who did not undergo endoscopic evaluation (n = 7, 10.9%) were classified based solely on clinical criteria, which may represent a potential source of misclassification bias. Nevertheless, given the limited number of such cases, this is unlikely to have materially affected the overall findings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings.

First, the retrospective single-center design and the inclusion of patients followed at a tertiary referral center may have introduced selection bias toward individuals with more severe clinical phenotypes, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings and potentially contributing to the higher observed prevalence of gastrointestinal involvement.

Second, missing data for certain clinical parameters, particularly splenomegaly and bronchiectasis, may have limited the ability to detect potential associations involving these variables. In addition, the definition of gastrointestinal involvement was based solely on clinical symptoms in a small subset of patients (n = 7, 10.9%), potentially introducing misclassification bias. However, given the limited number of such cases, the impact on the overall findings is likely minimal.

Third, endoscopic evaluation was not performed systematically in all patients but was limited primarily to those with persistent symptoms, potentially introducing selection bias and overestimating the frequency of pathological findings among symptomatic patients.

Fourth, the higher frequency of gastrointestinal involvement observed in patients with longer follow-up raises the possibility of surveillance (observation-time) bias.

Fifth, although ROC analysis demonstrated the discriminative ability of serum IgM and switched memory B cells, clinically applicable cutoff values could not be established due to the retrospective design and limited sample size. Therefore, the clinical applicability of these markers requires further prospective validation in larger, independent cohorts.

Sixth, the moderate explanatory power of the logistic regression model (Nagelkerke R² = 0.229) reflects the multifactorial nature of gastrointestinal involvement in CVID and suggests that unmeasured factors—such as genetic predisposition, alterations in the microbiota, and other immunological mechanisms—may also contribute to disease development.

Seventh, detailed T-cell phenotyping data, including naïve and memory T-cell subsets, were not routinely available for the majority of patients. Therefore, the potential association between gastrointestinal involvement and more advanced T-cell abnormalities could not be evaluated. Future studies incorporating comprehensive T-cell immunophenotyping may provide additional insights into the mechanisms underlying gastrointestinal manifestations in CVID.

An additional limitation is that gastrointestinal involvement was defined using a broad spectrum of symptoms, some of which may not have been directly attributable to CVID-related gastrointestinal disease. Symptoms such as abdominal pain, dyspepsia, bloating, and constipation are common in the general population and may have alternative etiologies. Moreover, because patients with upper gastrointestinal symptoms were more likely to undergo endoscopic evaluation, the high prevalence of gastritis observed in our cohort may partly reflect selection bias. Therefore, our findings may be less generalizable to patients presenting predominantly with lower gastrointestinal manifestations such as chronic diarrhea or enteropathy.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that gastrointestinal involvement is common in patients with CVID and is independently associated with low serum IgM levels and reduced percentages of CD19 + CD27+IgD− switched memory B cells. These findings suggest that gastrointestinal involvement in CVID is unlikely to be solely attributable to immunoglobulin deficiency and may also reflect underlying impairments in B-cell maturation and class-switch recombination.

Our results highlight the potential value of immunophenotyping in improving the understanding of gastrointestinal involvement in CVID. Low serum IgM levels and reduced percentages of switched memory B cells may help identify patients with an increased likelihood of gastrointestinal involvement; however, the clinical utility of these parameters requires further validation. In addition, the observed discriminative performance of IgM suggests that it may have potential as a biomarker for risk assessment in future studies.
