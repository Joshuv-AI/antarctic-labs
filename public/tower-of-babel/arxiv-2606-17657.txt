Using Cognitive Models to Improve Language Model Simulation of Human Persuasion Games
Zirui Cheng 1 * Zeyu Shen 1 * Thomas L. Griffiths 1 Peter Henderson 1 1 Princeton University

Abstract
Abstract. People make decisions differently in strategic interactions. Some update beliefs like a Bayesian; others exhibit biases like motivated reasoning. Although creators of large language models use simulated humans for safety evaluations and training, they often fail to cover this breadth of human behavior. We argue that cognitive science and economics provide a convenient tool for doing so, making use of mathematical models of human decision-making. We propose an approach that we call Equation-to-Behavior Prompting for guiding large language models to match cognitive models, and evaluate this approach on persuasion games based on legal decision-making. We find that large models can approximate equation-based specifications—Bayesian updating, affine distortion, motivated updating, and Grether’s α \alpha - β \beta model—using prompting, but small models fail to do so. However, training small models with reinforcement learning to adhere to mathematical rules, Equation-to-Behavior RL, reduces belief error by 26.5% in out-of-distribution parameterizations. We show that these simulations can help create diverse training environments; training small models to consider different kinds of decision-makers improves average belief change by 2.5%–12% over Bayesian-only training, even when persuading GPT-5-mini. Our work could improve human simulations for training and evaluation in increasingly realistic settings, and could also enable novel research into more complicated mathematical models of human decision-making.

1
Introduction

Large language models’ (LLMs’) capabilities in strategic communication have the potential for significant societal impact.
For example, LLMs might play the role of simulated opponents in mock trials, helping to train the next generation of attorneys for their day in court
[
70
]
.
They might help attorneys craft impressive arguments to better represent their clients, or they might help policymakers make strategic decisions.
This carries risks, too. Language models might be strategically persuasive in harmful or covert ways
[
56
,
31
,
57
]
, resulting in unexpected downstream consequences on human beliefs and decisions.

To improve, understand, and align LLM strategic communication, we first have to be able to simulate diverse human behaviors at scale.
This is, however, challenging. Simulating a human opponent means modeling a host of different potential strategic behaviors.
For example, an attorney trained against only rational simulated opponents might be completely unprepared when their real-world interlocutor is irrational.
Previous methods largely rely on persona prompts
[
44
,
1
]
, or preference learning
[
54
,
37
]
. These methods might model certain dimensions of behavior, like tone and preferences, but they are not explicitly designed to model realistic patterns of human decision-making or cognitive updating with LLMs.
For example, prompting an LLM to take the persona of a judge might not explicitly model how they update their beliefs over time.
While LLMs have been shown to be capable of probabilistic reasoning and Bayesian inference
[
55
,
23
,
67
]
, these capacities for rational inference sometimes make their default behaviors systematically different from those of humans
[
41
]
.
It is also challenging to directly model human decision-making from realistic data due to data scarcity and noisiness
[
11
]
. These gaps leave several important questions: How well do LLMs align with human decision-making in strategic interaction settings? Can LLMs be modified to consistently model the heterogeneous non-Bayesian behaviors observed in human decision-making?

We address these challenges by exploring ways to create LLMs that can reproduce patterns of human decision-making by drawing on computational models from cognitive science and behavioral economics.
Previous research shows that people oftentimes systematically depart from Bayesian inference when confronted with new information
[
49
]
.
Consequently, effective strategic interactions might require different approaches for engaging with different non-Bayesian decision-makers
[
33
,
16
]
.
We show how computational models of human decision-making can be used to build LLMs that are able to consider a range of patterns in human decision-making, with implications for their strategic behavior in realistic settings. We call this general paradigm
Equation-to-Behavior
Prompting and Reinforcement Learning: translating computational cognitive models into prompting and training objectives, so that LLM agents can be specified, calibrated, and varied by their underlying decision rules rather than only by natural-language personas.

To test our approach in a realistic setting, we introduce a large-scale dataset of legal decision-making derived from
The Proceedings of the Old Bailey

[
32
]
.
We decompose trial transcripts into different pieces of evidence labeled with human-validated evidential strength and dependency structure, creating a grounded testbed for modeling human persuasion and decision-making with LLMs.
Building on these environments, we evaluate LLMs in simulating diverse patterns of human decision-making in persuasion games with the Equation-to-Behavior Prompting approach.
This contrasts with prior work. Although others have evaluated and trained LLMs in structured settings such as debate
[
34
]
and negotiation
[
8
]
, these efforts typically rely on synthetic strategic interactions that fail to capture the breadth of human decision-making observed in the real world.

Our evaluations show the promise of Equation-to-Behavior approaches.
First, we find that while large models can reliably simulate different belief updating patterns specified by those computational models with prompting, including Bayesian updating, affine distortion, motivated updating, and Grether’s
\alpha
-
\beta
model, whereas smaller models often struggle to do so.
Second, we show that LLMs can be trained to better simulate equation-based cognitive models via Equation-to-Behavior RL. We train LLMs using reinforcement learning to enforce cognitive belief updating rules according to a wide mix of models, producing models that reduce belief error by 26.5% on out-of-distribution parameterizations.
Third, we demonstrate the utility of these simulations for scalable environment design.
We show that training against different distributions of decision-makers improves the performance of small models by 2.5%–12% relative to training against Bayesian decision-makers, even when interacting with GPT-5-mini.

Overall, our work sets the path for diverse simulations of cognitive behaviors in a wide range of language-based strategic settings, improving evaluation simulation, and training regimes.

3
Approach

We consider the strategic interactions between a Sender agent and a Receiver agent as in Bayesian persuasion
[
33
]
. There is a finite set
\Omega:=\{\omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
of
d
states of nature and a finite set
\mathcal{A}:=\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
n
Receiver actions. Agents’ payoffs are encoded by utility functions
u,v:\Omega\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
, with
u(\omega,a)
and
v(\omega,a)
denoting the payoff of the Receiver and the Sender, respectively.
The Sender and Receiver share a common prior belief
\mu_{0}\in\Delta(\Omega)
, with
\mu_{0}(\omega)\in[0,1]
denoting the probability of
\omega\in\Omega
.
The Sender can choose a signal
\pi
, which consists of a finite realization space
S
and a family of distributions
\{\pi(\cdot|\omega)\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
over realizations
s\in S
.
Upon observing the realization
s
, the Receiver updates her belief according to an updating rule
R
, yielding a posterior
\mu_{s}^{R}(\cdot;\mu_{0},\pi)\in\Delta(\Omega)
, and picks an optimal action
a(s)
.

Theoretical analysis from prior work suggests that human decision-making in strategic interactions can be characterized by both belief updating rules
R
and optimal decision policies
a(s)
. Cognitive scientists have developed computational models to explain and predict human decision-making. We use these models to improve the simulation of human decision-making in two ways. Our
Equation-to-Behavior Prompting
approach constructs prompts based on the equations that define these models, using them to shape the behavior of LLMs. In addition, we explore
Equation-to-Behavior RL
as a means of obtaining a closer correspondence between models and LLM behavior through fine-tuning via reinforcement learning. In the remainder of this section we provide a selective review of the cognitive models we consider.

Bayesian Updating.

Under Bayesian updating, the posterior belief after observing the realization
s
is given by Bayes’ rule:

\mu_{s}^{B}(\omega;\mu_{0},\pi)=\frac{\pi(s|\omega)\mu_{0}(\omega)}{\sum_{\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega}\pi(s|\omega^{\prime})\mu_{0}(\omega^{\prime})}.

(1)

In realistic settings, experimental evidence suggests that people may
systematically
deviate from Bayesian decision-making when confronted with new information
[
49
]
. Following previous literature
[
16
]
, we consider several representative alternatives to Bayesian updating models.

Affine Distortion.

A simple departure from Bayesian updating is affine distortion.
Let
\nu=\mu_{s}^{B}(\cdot;\mu_{0},\pi)
denote the Bayesian posterior, and let
\nu^{*}\in\Delta(\Omega)
denote a reference belief. The distorted posterior interpolates between the reference belief and the Bayesian posterior:

\mu_{s}^{R}(\cdot;\mu_{0},\pi)=\chi\nu^{*}+(1-\chi)\nu

(2)

where
0\leq\chi\leq 1
. Hence, a larger
\chi
means moving further away from Bayesian updating, and the tendency to update towards
\nu^{*}
becomes stronger. Depending on the nature of
\nu^{*}
, different parameters capture different biases. For example, when
\nu^{*}=\mu_{0}
, it generates posteriors that are closer to the prior than the Bayesian ones and captures the idea of “conservative Bayesianism”
[
21
]
.

Motivated Updating.

Motivated updating models belief updating as the outcome of a trade-off between the Bayesian posterior and a reference belief.
Similarly, let
\nu=\mu_{s}^{B}(\cdot;\mu_{0},\pi)
denote the Bayesian posterior and
\nu^{*}
a reference belief.
The Receiver selects a posterior
\mu
to maximize a utility function
\mathcal{U}(\mu,\nu,\nu^{*})
, which represents the cost of deviating from Bayesian inference and benefit of remaining close to
\nu^{*}
.

We consider a model of motivated conservative Bayesian updating where
\nu^{*}=\mu_{0}
. The Receiver updates her beliefs in a conservative Bayesian fashion, but chooses a parameter
\chi
to maximize
\mathcal{U}(\mu,\nu,\mu_{0})
. Therefore,

\mu_{s}^{R}(\cdot;\mu_{0},\pi)=\chi^{*}\mu_{0}+(1-\chi^{*})\nu

(3)

where
\chi^{*}=\arg\max_{\chi\in[0,1]}\mathcal{U}(\mu,\nu,\mu_{0})
. Motivated beliefs are common in strategic information transmission
[
7
]
. Previous research in cognitive science and behavioral economics has identified several channels that individuals use to reach favored conclusions, including asymmetric belief updating
[
63
]
, selective recall
[
3
]
, or motivated information selection
[
26
]
. We consider the case where subjects update their beliefs using information which is strategically disclosed by another player.

Grether’s
\alpha
-
\beta
Model.

As a common specification of non-Bayesian updating in the literature, Grether’s
\alpha
-
\beta
model generalizes Bayesian updating by introducing exponents on the prior and likelihood
[
27
,
28
]
:

\mu_{s}^{R}(\omega;\mu_{0},\pi)=\frac{\pi(s|\omega)^{\beta}\mu_{0}(\omega)^{\alpha}}{\sum_{\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega}\pi(s|\omega^{\prime})^{\beta}\mu_{0}(\omega^{\prime})^{\alpha}}

(4)

where
\alpha,\beta>0
. Different parameter values capture different biases in belief updating: base-rate neglect for
0<\alpha<1
, over-weighting the prior for
\alpha>1
, under-inference for
0<\beta<1
, and over-inference for
\beta>1
.

Unlike the examples above, some complicated information processing models cannot be reduced to an updating rule
R
that, given
\mu_{0}
and
\pi
, maps each realization
s
to a posterior belief
\mu_{s}^{R}(\omega;\mu_{0},\pi)
. We mainly focus on computationally tractable and empirically grounded models that are developed in cognitive science and behavioral economics. However, some of these cognitive models still play important roles in different domains, especially when the signals are complex. We provide additional discussions of cognitive models in Appendix
B
.

4
Dataset

We construct a benchmark grounded in legal decision-making to evaluate both Equation-to-Behavior Prompting and Equation-to-Behavior RL. The dataset is designed to instantiate the structured persuasion games described in Section
3
with realistic specifications, enabling controlled evaluation of different cognitive models.

4.1
Dataset Construction

Our dataset is built from
The Proceedings of the Old Bailey

[
32
]
, a collection of criminal trial transcripts from London’s central criminal court, spanning 1674 to 1913. These transcripts contain detailed trial narratives, including statements, examinations, verdicts, and sentencing remarks, and are well suited for modeling legal persuasion dynamics. Each trial is decomposed into discrete evidential units – statements or testimonies that may influence the court’s belief about the defendant’s guilt. Additional details are provided in Appendix
C
.

To operationalize the signals defined in Section
3
, we construct structured annotations of the evidentiary content using an LLM. Specifically, we use Gemini-2.5-Pro to annotate: (1)
evidential strength
, reflecting how strongly each piece supports or contradicts guilt; (2)
dependency structure
, capturing correlations or redundancies among pieces of evidence. These annotations allow us to construct different signal distributions to evaluate different cognitive models. Full implementation details appear in Appendix
C
. To ensure reliability, we also conduct a human study with crowdworkers recruited from Prolific.
1
1

1

https://www.prolific.com/
We find high agreement between human judgments and LLM-generated annotations, supporting our automated annotations. Additional details are provided in Appendix
D
.

4.2
Task Formulation

Based on the historical context of the dataset, we model each trial as a persuasion game between a Sender, instantiated as the prosecution, and a Receiver, instantiated as the court, to evaluate LLM decision-making. The state space
\Omega
consists of
guilty
and
innocent
, representing the defendant’s true status. The prior belief
\mu_{0}\in\Delta(\Omega)
is shared by the prosecution and the court.
The court chooses an action from the finite action set
\mathcal{A}
of
convict
and
acquit
. The prosecution’s utility is maximized when the court convicts and minimized when the court acquits, regardless of the true state. In contrast, the court’s utility is maximized when it makes the correct decision and minimized when it makes an incorrect one.
Consequently, the prosecution’s objective is to influence the court’s actions through strategic information transmission.

We assume the interaction proceeds over
n
rounds. Since the legal evidence from the dataset usually has various dimensions and complex structures, we consider a setting where the Sender draws a multi-dimensional signal
s=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{K})\sim\pi(s\mid\omega)
according to the signal scheme
\pi(s\mid\omega)
, where
\pi(s\mid\omega)
is the joint distribution of signal components conditional on
\omega
. Each marginal
\pi_{k}(s_{k}\mid\omega)
is derived from this joint distribution. Therefore, at round
i
, the Receiver observes a subset of the multi-dimensional signal, denoted by
o_{i}
. The Receiver applies a belief updating rule to incorporate the observed signals into its current belief about the state
\omega
. We denote the Receiver’s belief up to round
i
as
\mu_{i}
. Then, the Receiver selects a best-response action
a_{i}
at round
i
.
By design, the Receiver is ultimately exposed to all
K
signal components, yielding a trajectory
\tau
of signal-belief-action triplets
\{(o_{i},\mu_{i},a_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, which characterizes the evolution of decision-making as evidence accumulates.

5
Evaluating Equation-to-Behavior Prompting for Simulating Human Decision-Making

We evaluate whether large language models can implement specified belief-updating rules in the persuasion benchmark introduced in Section
4
using Equation-to-Behavior Prompting introduced in Section
3
.

5.1
Experiment Design

Following previous empirical research in cognitive science
[
6
,
24
,
45
]
, we create controlled manipulations of evidence to examine the behaviors of LLMs under different specifications of cognitive models. A manipulation is defined as a permutation
\sigma
over the
K
evidential units. In round
i
, the Receiver observes the subset
o_{i}=\{s_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,s_{\sigma(i)}\}
. All manipulations hold fixed the total number of signals
K
and the underlying joint structure
\pi(s\mid\omega)
. By fixing the joint signal structure and varying only the order of evidence revelation, we construct settings where different cognitive models can make distinct behavioral predictions, even when exact belief and action trajectories are difficult to compute at scale. We consider two specific manipulations that favor either the
prosecution
or the
defense
. In the
prosecution
condition, signals that are more favorable to conviction, according to a specified ordering criterion, are presented earlier, whereas in the
defense
condition, the same set of signals is presented in the reverse order.

In our experiments, we implement Receivers with LLMs. We prompt LLMs with the belief updating rules in Section
3
with different parameters. As a baseline condition, we implement a
default updating
type where we provide no instructions about the belief updating rules. In each trial, the Sender deterministically reveals subsets of signals according to a fixed permutation, with no LLM in the loop. During the interaction, the Receiver models can only have access to the descriptions of evidence, without any hints for the evidential strengths or dependency structures from the annotations. We set the rounds of persuasion
n=3
, and for each round the Sender presents the same amount of evidence within each trial. We consider LLMs of different sizes, including GPT-5, Claude-Sonnet-4, DeepSeek-V3.1, GPT-OSS-120B, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. Prompts are provided in Appendix
H
.

What are the default behavioral patterns of LLMs in decision-making?

We find that LLMs can approximate Bayesian belief updating well by default but exhibit predictable non-Bayesian deviations.

As shown in Figure
2
, we find that LLMs can preserve basic properties of Bayesian belief updating, such as monotonicity with respect to evidential strength and partial invariance to the ordering of evidence, demonstrating a structured form of probabilistic reasoning. We also analyze a subset of trials in which evidence items are approximately independent, allowing us to estimate the exact posterior beliefs using a naïve Bayes formulation (
1
).
We then compare these predicted (approximate) posteriors with the posterior beliefs elicited directly from LLMs on the same evidence, as shown in Figure
3
. Results indicate that large models exhibit moderate correlations with the predicted Bayesian posteriors, indicating that their internal belief representations align with the structure of normative Bayesian inference.
Smaller models, in contrast, show weaker correspondence and greater dispersion, suggesting noisier belief formation. These results are also consistent with previous research on Bayesian belief updating by LLMs
[
71
,
5
]
.

However, all models, including both large and small ones, display deviations from Bayesian invariance, which persist even when we prompt LLMs to follow Bayesian belief updating.
As shown in Figure
2
, conviction rates under the
prosecution
condition are significantly higher than under the
defense
condition across architectures and scales. When strong evidence is presented early and subsequently followed by weak evidence, conviction probabilities decline sharply, consistently violating the order invariance expected under Bayesian updating. This asymmetry suggests models exhibit a primacy bias analogous to the
motivated updating
common among humans: they discount later, weak evidence when it contradicts prior commitments established by earlier, stronger signals.

Can different belief updating rules be approximated in persuasion games with LLMs?
We find that large LLMs can accurately emulate structured non-Bayesian updating with Equation-to-Behavior Prompting, whereas small LLMs fail to do so consistently. We design targeted manipulations for different belief updating rules described in Section
3
, including
affine distortion
,
motivated updating
,
Grether’s
\alpha
-
\beta
model
, and
explanation-based updating
. Full experimental configurations are in Appendix
E
.

Across belief-updating rules, we find that large LLMs can accurately exhibit the behavioral patterns predicted by the corresponding theoretical models. Under affine distortion, models produce conviction rates that remain directionally consistent with Bayesian predictions but display compressed variance, indicating systematic under-reaction to different signals. In motivated updating, larger models exhibit asymmetric sensitivity across evidence strength, reducing conviction probabilities when later signals contradict earlier signals, consistent with prior theoretical predictions. Under Grether’s
\alpha
–
\beta
model, larger models exhibit substantial shifts in inference behavior as model parameters vary. When
\alpha=1,\beta>1
, models exhibit over-inference in response to evidential variation. In explanation-based updating, models show elevated conviction rates when evidence is ordered to form coherent causal or narrative structures, demonstrating a preference for explanatory consistency over probabilistic sufficiency. These results show that large LLMs are capable of calibrated non-Bayesian simulation: when given parametric specifications for each rule, they adjust belief updating in the predicted direction and magnitude. Conviction rates are partially presented in Table
1
. Complete setup and results are provided in Appendix
E
.

However, model scale influences the reliability of these effects. Among large models such as GPT-5, Claude-Sonnet-4, and DeepSeek-V3.1, final action distributions differ even under identical updating-rule specifications and parameters, though all three exhibit the rule’s qualitative features.
Interestingly, medium models such as Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct can benefit from prompting, even in the Bayesian belief updating setting.
Small models such as Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct fail to produce consistent action distributions across conditions, often conflating the targeted updating rule with default rational responding.
These divergences indicate that while large models can represent structured non-Bayesian principles, the internal implementation of belief updating remains model-specific.

6
Evaluating Equation-to-Behavior RL for Simulating Human Decision-Making

As shown in Section
5
, LLMs, especially small models, fail to implement equation-based specifications of cognitive models. We thus aim to train LLMs to simulate human decision-making through reinforcement learning by conforming to the prescribed formula. We treat the cognitive model as an executable specification that provides verifiable rewards. This approach is similar to previous work in cognitive science that has used synthetic data generated from cognitive models to shape the predictions of machine learning systems
[
11
]
.

6.1
Setup

Approach.

Let
\pi_{\theta}
denote the language model parameterized by
\theta
. In each episode, a cognitive model
R_{\phi}
parameterized by
\phi
is sampled from a training distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
. Following the processes described in Section
4
, the model produces a posterior belief
\mu_{\theta}
given a sequence of observed signals. Since the state space is binary, beliefs are fully characterized by this scalar posterior. For each sampled cognitive model
R_{\phi}
, the ground-truth posterior
\mu^{\star R_{\phi}}
is computed directly from the corresponding mathematical models. We thus define a trajectory-level reward based on the belief accuracy:

r(\theta;R_{\phi})=1-|\mu_{\theta}-\mu^{\star R_{\phi}}|.

Therefore, the training objective is

\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{R_{\phi}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[r(\theta;R_{\phi})\right]-\beta\operatorname{KL}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}}),

where
\pi_{\text{ref}}
denotes the reference policy,
\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}})
denotes the Kullback–Leibler divergence between
\pi_{\text{ref}}
and
\pi_{\theta}
, and
\beta>0
controls the strength of the regularization.

In realistic settings, decision-makers may be drawn from different distributions over cognitive models. We consider two different distributions of cognitive models in our experiments to evaluate the effectiveness of Equation-to-Behavior RL. In
Grether-only

7
Analysis

Training LLMs in Persuasion Games.

Our framework can be used not only to simulate different types of Receivers, but also to train Senders to act strategically under heterogeneous models of human decision-making. In Appendix
E.3
, we fine-tune the same set of three models as Senders and compare training against Bayesian-only Receivers with training against a mixed distribution of Receivers. The reward curves are presented in Figure
4b
. We find that Senders trained against a mixed distribution of Receivers consistently achieve higher average belief change on the held-out set. Case studies in Appendix
I
suggest that these gains come from learning more adaptive presentation strategies, such as emphasizing causal links, anticipating counterarguments, and using weak evidence more carefully.

Simulating Realistic Persuasion Games.

To further evaluate the effectiveness of our framework, we show that Equation-to-Behavior Prompting can better model realistic human decision-making than a
persona-based prompting
baseline as it enables better quantifiable control. In Appendix
E.1
, we study the full 446 sessions covering 56623 cases from the Old Bailey dataset. For each session, we estimate the cognitive parameters of the judge by fitting their verdicts with Grether’s
\alpha
-
\beta
model. We then evaluate whether Equation-to-Behavior Prompting with these parameters or persona-based prompting better matches real verdicts. We find that, for stronger models, Equation-to-Behavior Prompting improves verdict match rates over persona-based prompting by roughly 4.7%–9.1%. We also note that these two methods are complementary, not mutually exclusive.

8
Conclusion

In this work, we take a first step towards establishing a closer correspondence between LLM predictions and human decision-making by transforming cognitive models into verifiable specifications for LLMs.
Using our strategic persuasion environment built from the
Old Bailey
dataset and fine-tuning models to adhere to belief updating rules, we demonstrate that LLMs can capture realistic patterns that are observed in human decision-making.
Our Equation-to-Behavior methods are complementary to existing methods (e.g., persona-based prompting) for specifying behavioral characteristics in LLMs by rigorously controlling the decision-making process.
Future research could extend our approach to study broader forms of strategic interaction, especially human-AI interactions.
Although we focus on legal decision-making in this paper, future research could apply similar methods to strategic decision-making across other domains.

Appendix A
Theoretical Background

We summarize the theoretical results from previous research to justify the necessity of simulating realistic human decision-making. We present the setup and analysis from Bayesian and non-Bayesian persuasion
[
33
,
16
]
as they are the canonical models in game theory research. However, theoretical results from other models may also provide similar insights.

A.1
Setup

In Bayesian persuasion,
33
consider a game between a Sender and a Receiver. The communication protocol is as follows: (1) the Sender commits to a signaling scheme
\pi
; (2) the Sender observes a state of nature
\omega\sim\mu
and sends a signal
s\sim\pi(\omega)
to the Receiver; (3) the Receiver updates their beliefs over states of nature; (4) the Receiver plays a best-response action

a(s)\in\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{\omega\sim\mu(\cdot\mid s)}[u(\omega,a)]

(5)

with Sender and Receiver getting payoff
u(a,\omega)
and
v(a,\omega)
, respectively. Specifically, an action is a
best response
for the Receiver if it maximizes their expected utility given the belief. The goal of the Sender is to commit to an optimal signaling scheme

\pi^{\star}\in\arg\max_{\pi:\Omega\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})}\mathbb{E}_{\omega\sim\mu,s\sim\pi(\omega)}\big[v(\omega,a(s))\big].

(6)

to maximize expected utility.

A.2
Results

As defined in Bayesian persuasion
[
33
]
, we assume the Receiver updates their belief over states of nature according to Bayes’ rule.
Under Bayesian updating, after observing a signal
s\in\mathcal{S}
, the Receiver forms a posterior belief over states given by

\mu^{B}_{s}(\omega)=\frac{\pi(s\mid\omega)\mu_{0}(\omega)}{\sum_{\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega}\pi(s\mid\omega^{\prime})\mu_{0}(\omega^{\prime})}.

Let
\rho\in\Delta(\Delta(\Omega))
denote the distribution of posterior beliefs induced by a signaling scheme.
33
show that the Sender’s optimization problem can be reformulated as a concavification problem over posterior beliefs.

Let
f:\Delta(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
. The concavification of
f
, denoted
\mathrm{CAV}(f)
, is defined as the smallest concave function that weakly upper bounds
f
. Equivalently,

\mathrm{CAV}(f)(\mu)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho\in\Delta(\Delta(\Omega))\\
\mathbb{E}_{\nu\sim\rho}[\nu]=\mu\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu\sim\rho}[f(\nu)].

Theorem A.1

(Bayesian Persuasion
[
33
]
)
.

A distribution of posteriors
\rho\in\Delta(\Delta(\Omega))
is induced by some signaling scheme if and only if it satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{\nu\sim\rho}[\nu]=\mu.

Moreover, defining

\hat{v}(\nu)=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\nu(\omega)v(\hat{a}(\nu),\omega),

where
\hat{a}(\nu)
is the Receiver’s best response under belief
\nu
, the Sender’s optimal value equals

\mathrm{CAV}(\hat{v})(\mu).

We now consider non-Bayesian updating rules. Following
16
, we consider a family of updating rules that systematically distort Bayesian posteriors.

Definition A.2
.

An updating rule systematically distorts updated beliefs if for every prior
\mu\in\operatorname{int}(\Delta(\Omega))
there exists a function

D_{\mu}:\Delta(\Omega)\to\Delta(\Omega)

such that for every signaling scheme
\pi
and signal realization
s
,

\mu^{R}_{s}=D_{\mu}(\mu^{B}_{s}).

That is, the Receiver’s posterior is a deterministic transformation of the Bayesian posterior.

Under systematic distortion, the Sender’s interim payoff becomes

\check{v}(\nu)=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\nu(\omega)v(\hat{a}(D_{\mu}(\nu)),\omega).

The concavification method continues to apply, but with respect to this distorted objective.

Theorem A.3

(Non-Bayesian Persuasion
[
16
]
)
.

If the updating rule systematically distorts updated beliefs, then the Sender’s optimal value equals

\mathrm{CAV}(\check{v})(\mu).

Different updating rules therefore induce distinct optimization problems and, in general, different optimal signaling schemes. A central theoretical result concerns the impossibility of universally ranking updating rules.

Theorem A.4

(Impossibility of Universal Dominance
[
16
]
)
.

Let
D_{\mu}
and
\tilde{D}_{\mu}
be two distinct invertible distortion functions. Then neither updating rule universally dominates the other across all persuasion problems.

Consequently, no single distortion function yields strategies that are optimal across all persuasion environments. Optimal signaling is inherently dependent on the behavioral model.

Because of the impossibility of universal dominance, there is no behavioral model that guarantees universally optimal strategies across all persuasion problems. Thus, AI systems optimized under a fixed assumption about human belief updating lack universal optimality guarantees under belief heterogeneity.

In explanation-based decision-making, the Receiver may update beliefs by constructing causal narratives that organize the components of the signal realization
\{\pi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
into a coherent explanation
[
53
]
. Rather than adjusting priors through likelihoods, the Receiver interprets each
\pi_{k}
in relative isolation and then evaluates how well these pieces fit into a broader causal story. Let
G^{n}=(\{1,\dots,K\},E^{n})
denote the causal structure of narrative
n
, where an edge
i\to j
in
E^{n}
represents a claimed causal link between evidence
i
and
j
. We measure the simplicity, or structural strength, of the story by its sparsity:

\text{Sparsity}(n)=1-\frac{|E^{n}|}{K(K-1)}.

Narratives that explain the realization
\pi
with more causal links are treated as stronger and more coherent. We can then model explanation-based decision-making with Grether’s
\alpha-\beta
model with
\alpha=1
and
\beta=\frac{\lambda}{\text{Sparsity}(n)}
, where
\lambda
characterizes the extent of bias. Belief revision thus reflects changes in the perceived coherence of this sparse causal structure rather than Bayesian updating.

We also provide additional cognitive models for evaluations in Section
6
.

Divisible Updating.

A Receiver follows a divisible belief updating rule if the distortion applied to the Bayesian posterior is independent of the intermediate belief. We implement divisible updating using the power weighting distortion function
[
15
]
. In particular, given a Bayesian posterior
\nu
and prior
\mu_{0}
, the distorted belief is computed as

\hat{\mu}=\frac{\nu^{\beta}}{\nu^{\beta}+(1-\nu)^{\beta}\cdot\frac{1-\mu_{0}}{\mu_{0}}},

where
\beta\in(0,\infty)
controls the degree of distortion.
\beta=1
recovers Bayesian updating,
\beta<1
produces underreaction to evidence, and
\beta>1
produces overreaction.

Bayesian Updating with a Different Prior.

We consider Receivers who perform correct Bayesian updating but hold a different prior. The belief updating rule is the same as in Eq.
1
, only the prior
\mu_{0}
is different.

Correlation Neglect.

A Receiver suffers from correlation neglect if, when processing multiple pieces of evidence
(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{K})
, she treats each signal as independent even when they are correlated. Formally, instead of using the true joint distribution
\pi(s_{1},\ldots,s_{K}\mid\Omega)
for Bayesian updating, the Receiver uses the product of marginals:

\mu_{s}^{CN}(\cdot;\mu_{0},\pi)=\mu_{s}^{B}\left(\cdot;\mu_{0},\prod_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}\right),

where
\pi_{k}
denotes the marginal distribution of the
k^{\text{th}}
signal component. In practice, this means performing sequential Bayesian updates treating each piece of evidence as providing independent information.

Appendix C
Dataset Processing

We provide details about the dataset processing to evaluate and train language models in modeling human decision-making.

C.1
Background

Dataset Overview.

The Old Bailey Proceedings dataset consists of digitized criminal trial records from London’s Central Criminal Court, spanning 1674–1913. It contains nearly 200,000 trials and over 127 million words of text. The records were originally published as contemporaneous printed accounts of court sessions and later evolved into an official judicial record, providing a continuous and systematically produced source for the study of law and society over more than two centuries. The Old Bailey records include a wide range of crime categories that changed over time. The court primarily dealt with serious offenses, especially felonies, and less serious offenses were often handled elsewhere. Therefore, the dataset is weighted toward crimes such as theft, violent offenses, and deception.

Historical Background.

Different from modern criminal trials, the trial procedures at the Old Bailey were typically short proceedings, often completed within minutes or hours. Lawyers were rarely involved, so cases largely consist of direct exchanges between different parties, with judges and juries playing an active role in interpreting evidence. Since there was no fully developed system of legal rules governing evidence, courtroom practice was flexible. Outcomes were shaped not only by facts but also by individual judgment and courtroom dynamics. Although the actual outcomes in Old Bailey trials might be more complicated beyond deciding guilt, most of them can be classified into either
conviction
or
acquittal
.

Data Structure.

The fundamental unit in the dataset is the trial record, each identified by a stable identifier and accompanied by rich structured metadata. Trials often involve multiple defendants, victims, and charges, resulting in many-to-many relationships and multiple offence-verdict-sentence combinations per trial. Each trial includes an extensive narrative describing testimony, examinations, judicial commentary, and details of crimes and settings. From the 1730s onward, a high proportion of the text consists of direct or reported speech by defendants, witnesses, victims, and judges, making the dataset especially valuable for modeling historical spoken language.

Accessibility and Use.

The dataset is publicly available as both an online resource and downloadable structured files. Its rich annotation, and longitudinal coverage have made it widely used in computational analysis, particularly for decision-making. Structurally, the digitized Proceedings are encoded in XML available through the website
[
32
]
. Additional background on the crime, justice, and punishment context of the Old Bailey dataset is available on the same site.

Evaluations on Non-Bayesian Belief Updating.

To evaluate different types of non-Bayesian updating, we construct different variants of the evidence set. For motivated updating, we order evidence by its marginal likelihood: the
prosecution
variant presents evidence in decreasing order of strength, while the
defense
variant uses increasing order. We expect that a Receiver with motivated updating will favor prosecution in the former and acquittal in the latter. For affine updating, we employ the same order permutations as for motivated updating; however, the “prosecution” and “defense” labels are retained solely for consistency in naming. We expect the Receiver with affine updating to remain closer to the prior than a Bayesian Receiver would, regardless of the presentation order. For explanation-based updating, we filter the dataset to retain only evidence where prosecution strength is no smaller than the defense strength, i.e., evidence that favors prosecution. We then organize the evidence based on the annotated causal links: the prosecution variant maximizes causal links between the evidence presented in each round, while the defense variant minimizes them. We expect the Receiver to be more likely to convict when causal links are maximized.

E.3
Training LLMs in Persuasion Games

We show that by training on more diverse Receiver types via reinforcement learning, LLMs can learn more robust persuasion strategies.

Approach.

Let
\pi_{\theta}
denote the language model parameterized by
\theta
as the Sender. In each episode, a Receiver following the cognitive model
R
parameterized by
\phi
is sampled from a training distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
. The Sender interacts with the Receiver by designing the signals to realize at each round. After the final round, the Receiver reports a posterior belief
\mu_{\theta}^{R_{\phi}}
. We define the reward as

r(\theta;R_{\phi})=\mu_{\theta}^{R_{\phi}}-\mu_{0}

to demonstrate the effects of training Senders against different distributions of Receivers. Therefore, the training objective is

\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{R_{\phi}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}}[r(\theta;R_{\phi})]-\beta\operatorname{KL}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}}),

where
\pi_{\text{ref}}
denotes the reference policy,
\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta}|\pi_{\text{ref}})
denotes the Kullback–Leibler divergence between
\pi_{\text{ref}}
and
\pi_{\theta}
, and
\beta>0
controls the strength of the regularization.

We consider different distributions of Receiver types for training and evaluation. In
Bayesian-Receiver
training, the Receiver is prompted to follow Bayesian updating, simulating the environments that assume rational belief updating and serves as a baseline for comparison. In
Mixed-Receiver
training, the Receiver type is sampled uniformly from multiple cognitive models, with parameters drawn from bounded ranges chosen to induce deviations from Bayesian updating.

Implementation.

We fine-tune Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct, and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 using the veRL framework
[
59
]
with GRPO
[
58
]
. We train on the full set of 1102 game instances of Old Bailey. We use GPT-5-mini to simulate the Receiver, which is prompted to follow a specific cognitive model. We use a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-6}
, a batch size of
16
, and a KL coefficient of
0.01
and train for
100
steps. For each prompt, we generate
4
rollouts for GRPO’s group-based advantage estimation and follow the same game procedure as in Section
5
. Each game consists of
3
rounds of interactions. In
Mixed-Receiver
training, for Grether’s
\alpha
-
\beta
model, parameters are independently sampled as
\alpha,\beta\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,2]
. For affine distortion,
\chi\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0.5,1]
. For motivated updating under the conservative specification,
\chi\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0.5,1]
. For explanation-based updating,
\lambda\sim\mathrm{Unif}[1,10]
.

Figure
4b
shows the reward curves during training. All three models show clear improvement in persuasion ability, with reward increasing over training.

We evaluate senders trained with mixed versus Bayesian-only Receiver types on 100 game instances with mixed Receiver types sampled following the same procedure as in mixed receiver training in Section
6
plus explanation-based updating with
\lambda
randomly selected from
[1,20]
, which are out-of-distribution parameter ranges compared with training parameter ranges. As shown in Table

Appendix F
Impact Statement

We discuss anticipated consequences of our research. We also propose measures to mitigate potential harmful consequences.

Societal Impact.

We investigate methods for improving simulations of human decision-making with large language models (LLMs), with the broader aim of advancing their capabilities in strategic interactions across realistic domains such as economics, law, and political science. This work could bring societal benefits in this endeavor. However, related techniques pose nontrivial societal risks, including the potential use of LLMs for strategic deception or manipulation of vulnerable decision-makers
[
22
]
, especially when their vulnerabilities are known.

Mitigation Measures.

To mitigate the potential harms, future research should proactively audit the environments for training the models to prevent harmful exploitation (e.g., deception or harassment). Meanwhile, future research should investigate potential methods to enable language models to be more rational strategic decision-makers, so that human decision-makers will not be worse off from persuasion
[
17
]
. However, due to the dependency on specific contexts of persuasion, we still encourage future research to characterize misuse scenarios, and develop targeted strategies that enable socially beneficial applications while reducing risks.

Appendix G
Safety Evaluation

We discuss the results on evaluating models on persuasion tasks for safety alignment. We demonstrate that evaluating with prompt-based approaches can reveal different risks of existing models.

G.1
Evaluation Setup

Task Background.

Building on prior work examining the persuasive capabilities of LLMs
[
48
,
47
]
, we evaluate models on two complementary tasks. MakeMePay assesses manipulative persuasion in a monetary context: two LLMs interact as a con-artist and a mark, where the former attempts to induce a payment from the latter. MakeMeSay evaluates deceptive persuasion: one model (the manipulator) attempts to elicit a target codeword from another model (the manipulatee) without revealing the objective. Together, these tasks address distinct dimensions of persuasive influence—resource extraction and covert verbal manipulation. Prior evaluations by OpenAI
[
48
]
suggest that advanced models can exhibit human-level persuasive performance, motivating systematic measurement of such capabilities.

Evaluation Metrics.

For
MakeMePay
, we measure persuasive effectiveness using Mean Donation, defined as the average amount transferred from the mark—endowed with $100 and instructed to allocate it rationally—to the con-artist across interactions. This metric directly quantifies monetary extraction. For
MakeMeSay
, we measure performance using the Hit Rate, defined as the proportion of interactions in which the manipulatee produces the target codeword during the dialogue prior to task revelation. This metric operationalizes successful covert influence.

Implementation Details.

We use the contexts provided in
60
and
2
, and uniformly sampled the contexts from previous work (e.g., parameters in
MakeMePay
and difficulty in
MakeMeSay
). For the Sender, the con-artist in
MakeMePay
or the manipulator in
MakeMeSay
, we evaluate different models including GPT-5, Claude-Sonnet-4, DeepSeek-V3.1, GPT-OSS-120B, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. For the Receivers, we use different models, including GPT-5 and GPT-5-Nano.

Following the experimental protocol described in Section
5
, we prompt the Receiver models with distinct cognitive frameworks to simulate heterogeneous populations of decision-makers, including both default Receivers and mixed Receivers. For default Receivers, we don’t prompt the models with any instructions on belief updating. For mixed Receivers, we sample the cognitive models uniformly from Bayesian updating, affine distortion, motivated updating, and Grether’s
\alpha
-
\beta
model. We run 100 instances for both
MakeMePay
