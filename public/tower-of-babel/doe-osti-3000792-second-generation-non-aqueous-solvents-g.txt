1. Executive summary
This final technical report submitted to DOE/NETL presents all the research activities performed
during the Cooperative Agreement DE-FE0032218 entitled Second Generation Non-Aqueous
Solvents (GEN2NAS) for CO2 Capture from Natural Gas Combined Cycle Plants, which spanned
from April 2023 through March 2025. In this project, Research Triangle Institute (RTI)
International has developed the second-generation of its non-aqueous solvent (NAS), herein
referred to as GEN2NAS, to remove carbon dioxide (CO2) from natural-gas combined cycle
(NGCC) flue gas. The technology aims to substantially reduce the cost of CO2 capture while
minimizing the environmental impacts through lower secondary emissions and wastewater
generated from the CO2 capture plant. The specific objectives of this project were to:
 • Finalize GEN2NAS formulation based on a handful of physical and chemical
 properties critical for effective operation in a solvent-based CO2 capture system;
 • Demonstrate the CO2 capture rate of >97% in a continuous manner at the bench scale
 under simulated NGCC conditions;
 • Develop GEN2NAS CO2 capture process model using the physical and chemical
 properties collected in the project; and
 •Perform a techno-economic assessment (TEA) of the GEN2NAS CO2 capture process
 and show that the technology has a potential to reduce the overall cost of CO2 capture
 at least 40% compared to the National Energy Technology Laboratory’s (NETL)
 Baseline Study Case B31B rev. 4A, Oct. 2022.
The following are the key findings based on numerous tests using both lab-scale setups and
parametric testing performed at RTI’s Bench-scale Gas Absorption System (BsGAS).
More than 400 formulations were screened during the first 6 months of the project. The solvent
formulations were limited to the combination of one or two amines with a diluent. Literature
review with guidance from our first-generation non-aqueous solvent led to filter down to 13 amines
and 20 diluents that served as high-potential candidates for GEN2NAS. The solvent screening
resulted in a GEN2NAS formulation that contains a single amine (A2) with a diluent (D9) (vide
infra).
GEN2NAS was evaluated using RTI’s Lab-scale Gas Absorption System (LsGAS) for its capture
performance, specific reboiler duty (SRD), emissions, and operability under simulated NGCC flue
gas conditions. GEN2NAS exhibited its ability to continuously removed CO2 from the feed gas at
>97% removal efficiency. 99% CO2 removal was also demonstrated and produced a negative CO2
emission scenario where the concentration of CO2 in the treated gas is lower than that of air.
The subsequent evaluation of GEN2NAS was completed at BsGAS. 50 L of GEN2NAS was
loaded to the system where close to 817 hours of operation was achieved under about 100 testing
conditions. The solvent was evaluated using both a conventional absorber column as well as a
rotating packed bed (RPB) absorber. Consistent with the LsGAS results, GEN2NAS demonstrated
a CO2 capture rate of more than 97% and a specific reboiler duty (SRD) as low as 2.3 GJ/ton-CO2,
depending on the test conditions.

 1

A comprehensive set of GEN2NAS physical and chemical properties were collected at relevant
process conditions in parallel to the bench-scale demonstration to develop the GEN2NAS process
model. These properties include specific heat capacity, heat of absorption, viscosity, vapor-liquid
equilibrium, density, thermal conductivity, material compatibility, flash points, liquid-side mass
transfer coefficient, thermal degradation, nitrosamine speciation, corrosion rate, freezing point,
and water solubility.
Based on the testing data, the preliminary TEA was updated assuming either a conventional
column with process intensified packing (PIP) or a rotating packed bed (RPB) absorber. Assuming
a 97% capture rate, the overall cost of CO2 capture of GEN2NAS applied to a NGCC power plant
is $47.0/tonne for the RPB case and $47.4/tonne for the PIP/conventional column case. This
represents an overall cost reduction of about 22% compared to the $60.2/tonne from NETL's
baseline case B31B.97 (rev 4A. Oct. 2022). A sensitivity analysis showed that the natural gas cost
has the largest impact in the overall cost of capture followed by the capture plant capital expense
(CAPEX). The sensitivity analysis also showed the low impact of the SRD variability, a major
part of CO2 capture operational expense (OPEX), to the cost of capture. These findings suggest
that further improvement in economic viability of the GEN2NAS technology should focus on
lowering the CAPEX of the capture plant as well as minimizing the natural gas cost to power the
capture plant.

2. Introduction
The objective of the project was to advance a novel second-generation non-aqueous solvent
(GEN2NAS) and process for carbon dioxide (CO2) capture that would meet the Department of
Energy’s (DOE) goal of lowering the cost of CO2 capture at natural gas combined cycle (NGCC)
power plants by 40% compared with the National Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL) baseline
case B31B for NGCC applications. of the high amine concentration and low volatility of
GEN2NAS would perform particularly well for NGCC applications where the CO2 content in the
flue gas is relatively low compared to other flue gases (e.g., coal or cement plant flue gas). The
optimized secondary amine structure allows the low heat of absorption and low vapor pressure
translate to low operating expense (OPEX) contribution to the cost of capture while the use of
process intensified equipment such as RPBs and PIP lower capital expense (CAPEX).
The proposed GEN2NAS RPB process is highly relevant to efforts by the Office of Fossil Energy
& Carbon Management (FECM) to reduce the cost of CO2 capture for NGCC flue gas by 40%.
Both the RPB and PIP are expected to reduce the CAPEX of carbon capture processes substantially
and could therefore lower the cost of CO2 capture if operated with the proper solvent. Furthermore,
to achieve high-efficiency CO2 removal (97%+) from NGCC flue gas containing relatively low
concentrations of CO2 at 4%, the process must be paired with a solvent with fast CO2-absoprtion
kinetics. Additionally, all water-based amine solvent systems (even concentrated ones with 30%
water) are too corrosive to work in a RPB system without frequent maintenance. With these factors
in mind, GEN2NAS is uniquely well-suited to work in RPB equipment because of its rapid CO2-
absorption kinetics (which are reported herein to be much faster than other solvents), low viscosity,
and extremely low corrosivity, which is particularly important for working at higher amine
concentrations.

 2

This project resulted in impactful outcomes for FECM, most importantly a robust technoeconomic
assessment (TEA) using performance measurements from bench-scale testing that showed reduced
cost of CO2 capture from NGCC flue gas. The project met high impact, technology-specific goals:
(1) the GEN2NAS concentration and viscosity were optimized for a RPB absorber; (2) GEN2NAS
was demonstrated at both the laboratory and bench scale (including testing in a RPB absorber) to
remove 97%+ CO2 from simulated NGCC flue gas; (3) GEN2NAS absorption kinetics were
measured in a wetted-wall column confirming fast kinetics compared with other solvents; and
(4) an Aspen process model of the bench-scale RPB NGCC system was developed, used for
technoeconomic analysis, and will be ready for a larger scale demonstration in the future.

Project Goals and Objectives
The specific objectives of this project were as follow:
 • Finalize GEN2NAS formulation based on a handful of physical and chemical properties
 critical for effective operation in a solvent-based CO2 capture system;
 • Demonstrate the CO2 capture rate of >97% in a continuous manner at the bench scale under
 simulated NGCC conditions;
 • Develop GEN2NAS CO2 capture process model using the physical and chemical properties
 collected in the project; and
 • Perform a techno-economic assessment (TEA) of the GEN2NAS CO2 capture process and
 show that the technology has a potential to reduce the overall cost of CO2 capture at least
 40% compared to the National Energy Technology Laboratory’s (NETL) Baseline Study
       Case B31B rev. 4A, Oct. 2022.
To achieve these project objectives and goals, the project consisted of a single budget period with
5 major tasks. The specific tasks were:
 • Task 1 - Project Management;
 • Task 2 - Lab Testing of GEN2NAS;
 • Task 3 - Kinetic Measurements of GEN2NAS;
 • Task 4 - Batch RPB testing; and
 • Task 5 - Technoeconomic Assessment.

Task 1 facilitated project management, technical meetings, and communications with NETL on
technical progress and project budget.
During Task 2, the GEN2NAS formulation was optimized for NGCC operations. RTI resources
were used for experimental determination of all thermodynamic and physical properties needed
for future advanced process modeling, including experimental measurement of the solvent’s
vapor-liquid equilibrium (VLE), CO2 reaction rates, heat of absorption, specific heat capacity,
thermal conductivity, density, viscosity, thermal degradation, and oxidative degradation.
Optimization entailed maximizing the concentration of the amine to reach the lowest Specific
Reboiler Duty (SRD) and highest working capacity while balancing viscosity. Amine emission
and engineering system operational challenges such as water- balancing, material compatibility,
and foaming of the promising solvent candidates were addressed by lab-scale gas absorption
system testing where the solvent was tested under realistic operating conditions.

Task 2 - Lab Testing of GEN2NAS
The objectives of Task 2 were to optimize GEN2NAS with respect to amine concentration,
viscosity, hazardous rating, cost, CO2 working capacity, vapor pressure, and estimated SRD.
Selected solvent formulations were tested in a lab-scale gas absorption system (LsGAS) to
demonstrate the continuous CO2 capture operation while the operability, capture rate, amine
emissions, and energy input were observed.
The CO2 saturation station, Chittick apparatus, and viscometer were used to load CO2, determine
CO2 loading in mol-CO2/mol-amine, and measure viscosity of the solvents, respectively. The detail
of experimental setup and procedure, screening philosophy, and results and discussion are
provided below.
Experimental Procedure:
CO2 Saturation Station
A lab-scale stainless-steel CO2 saturation apparatus was built to evaluate the CO2 loading of a
multitude of CO2 capture solvent formulations. The apparatus has 8 different lines and can
therefore load the CO2 into 8 solvent samples simultaneously. To measure the CO2 loading, a bone-
dry CO2 was bubbled through the samples and the mass increase from the sample is assumed to
all be chemically absorbed CO2.

simultaneously with flow controlled through rotameters; (b) A representative test tube with CO 2

Test tubes were used to prepare the tested solvent formulations (Figure 1). A beaker and test tube
were tared on a balance. Then, each component of a formulation was added individually without
re-taring the balance, and the weight after the addition of each formulation component was
recorded. The test tube was shaken to create a homogeneous solution. Once all the components
were added, the gas feed tubing with rubber stopper was inserted into the test tube. The weight
was recorded again, which served as the starting weight of the sample. Then, the test tube was
connected via a Swagelok fitting to its respective rotameter and N2 was bubbled at 100 sccm for 2
mins to further mix the solution. The 3-way valve was turned to the CO2 line and CO2 (100%, 101
kPa) was introduced to the test tubes. Each line was adjusted using the rotameter to 100 sccm. CO2
was bubbled for 30 min, then N2 was passed through the test tube for another 2 mins to purge the
tubing of any solvent in the line and displace any CO2 that may be physically trapped in the
solution. Lastly, the test tube was removed and its mass was recorded. The mass increase is
assumed to be absorbed CO2. A lab-scale CO2 saturation system was further improved mid-way
during the screening process. The modified setup consists of a gas cylinder composed of 4 % CO2
balanced in N2 was used as the gas feed to resemble NGCC conditions. The schematic of this high
throughput apparatus (Figure 2) has 8 different lines that can load the CO2 to the 8 solvent samples
simultaneously. A 40 °C water bath was added to heat the formulations to resemble absorber
column operational conditions. A stir bar was placed in the water bath to eliminate any temperature
gradient that could happen along the height of the test tubes. To measure the CO2 loading, a known
volume of gas feed was bubbled through the samples and the mass increase from the sample is
assumed to all be chemically absorbed CO2.

Modified Chittick Apparatus
A sample of CO2-loaded solvent is injected through a septum into a reaction vessel made up of
sulfuric acid solution. Consequently, CO2 is liberated from the amine and displaces a buffer
solution in a volumetric glass cylinder. The liberated CO2 amount is determined through the
volume displaced. Prior, the amine content in wt% of the sample is obtained via an autotitrator
(Metrohm EcoTitrator), which allows us to calculate the moles of amine in the sample. The moles
of CO2 released from the sample and the loading of the sample (mol CO2/ mol amine) are
determined by the ratio of CO2 liberated over the amount of amine in the sample in mol-CO2/mol-
amine.

Screening Philosophy:
RTI designed the preliminary lab-scale screening methodology for the novel GEN2NAS solvent
by optimizing key parameters to achieve superior performance in terms of lower emissions and
higher working capacity compared to NAS. We identified the following factors that control the

 10

overall performance and operational cost of GEN2NAS- 1) a higher Xsolv (i.e., moles of
amine/moles of overall solvent formulation) potentially drives the specific reboiler duty (SRD) of
the process down; 2) a higher working capacity (the difference in loading between the CO2-rich
and CO2-lean solvent), or in other words, a higher loading of CO2 (moles of CO2/moles of amine)
of the formulation which also lowers the SRD; 3) a low viscosity (~100 cP) that is suitable to be
used in a RPB absorber column; and 4) an inconsequential vapor pressure of the amine and diluent
to ensure minimum amine emissions from the CO2 capture process.
With the provision for working with a formulation with a higher working capacity, RTI set out to
screen formulations with one- and two- amine component formulations with an Xsolv in the range
of. All tested formulations had a fixed amine mass whereby the diluent mass was varied to achieve
a desired Xsolv. Such an approach ensured consistency in comparing the CO2 loading across all
formulation with the same amount of amine being loaded with CO2 under the same saturation
duration.
RTI have screened four amines along with twelve diluents in this project (Table 2 and Table 3). A
combination of a primary with a secondary amine has been explored to enable both fast kinetics
and ease of regeneration associated with these amines, respectively. In the pursuit of improving
the emission performance of our NAS technology, we focused on employing amines with
molecular weights (MW) (and vapor pressures) higher than the amine used in the first-generation
NAS. Our search for higher MW amines were often met challenges such as significant water
solubility and amines existing in the solid state at room temperature. We also prioritized on
identifying amines that are commercially available at relatively low-cost at large quantity to avoid
potential solvent cost scalability risks.

Approaches to incorporate water in the high throughput screening set-up:
There is a chance of formation of solid carbamates in these high Xsolv formulations; nevertheless,
presence of 5 wt% of water in the formulation might help to dissolve this species. The existing
screening set-up was revised in this quarter to screen CO2 loading by formulations containing up
to 5 wt% of water. Water saturator was added upstream of the sample tube to saturate the feed gas
and minimize water loss from the sample. It was independently shown that all the selected diluents
except D17 and D19 have up to 5 wt% tolerance towards water. All the formulations exhibited
augmented CO2 loading in the presence of water. It became apparent that incorporation of water-
saturated CO2 feed gas helps in compensating for the water loss during flow, however, this induces
water absorption by the formulation as well. In essence, the increase in mass during CO2 loading
was convoluted with the uptake of water and CO2 together. This precluded pursuing such approach
of using pre-saturated feed gas for subsequent testing. An experimental included an overhead
condenser was devised to include 5 wt% water to each of these 2-component formulations and
assess their CO2 loading under both absorber and regenerator conditions. However, the error in
weight gain data were large, making the result unreliable and the screening proceeded with
formulation on dry basis only.

Emission testing system
One of the major challenges in the deployment of carbon capture for point sources is the secondary
emissions from amine solvents and their degradation products. Even at an emission rate of 1 ppm
of these chemicals, implementation of a CO2 capture plant will result in breaching emission limits.
Thus, minimizing the release of these chemicals will be a key to reduce the environmental impact
and improve the financial viability of CO2 capture technologies (e.g., lower solvent loss). To
evaluate solvent’s potential emission, one can look at vapor pressure of the chemicals within the
solvent formulation.
The emission testing system (ETS) is constructed to quickly evaluate the apparent vapor emissions
of the solvents being screened for GEN2NAS. The ETS, shown in Figure 9, consists of 3 testing
stations which can be used to evaluate 3 different samples simultaneously. Each station consists
of a N2 mass flow controller, a bubbler, and a knock-out pot. The N2 is used to bubble through the
sample where the sample vapor is produced and leaves with the N2. The saturator effluent is sent
to the knock-pot (KP-X) to remove any condensed liquid before sending the gas out to the bent.
The saturators are submerged in the temperature-controlled water bath.

CO2 Capture Evaluation at Lab-scale Gas Absorption System (LsGAS)
The evaluation of solvents under relevant operating conditions and intended process configurations
in an early stage of the solvent formulation development will provide insights on the operational
issues such as column flooding, corrosion, and precipitation - issues typically encountered by
amine-based solvent technologies. Furthermore, the performance challenges such as high energy
consumption for CO2 regeneration and high solvent loss/emissions could be observed during the
testing. These performance challenges are highly relevant for NGCC CO2 capture application as
the CO2 concentration in the flue gas is 4X lower than that of the coal-derived flue gas, creating
an unfavorable CO2 absorption condition due to the low driving force of CO2 partial pressure, as
well as exacerbating the amine emissions due to high amine vapor pressure generated from the
free-amine in the absorber.
The technical goal of this project was to develop a CO2 capture solvent technology that is capable
of removing at least 97% of the CO2 from the NGCC derived flue gas. If we assume that a typical
NGCC flue gas contains 4 vol% CO2, the 97% removal will result in a treated flue gas with 1,240
ppm of CO2 leaving the CO2 capture process.
An evaluation of continuous CO2 capture performance of 2 solvent formulations, A2-D9 and A1-
A2-D3, was conducted at the Lab-scale Gas Absorption System (LsGAS), a small bench-scale,
integrated gas absorption system at RTI. These formulations were evaluated for their SRDs, overall
emissions, and operability under simulated NGCC conditions. The experimental setup and results
are discussed in the following sections.
LsGAS (Figure 11) was developed under DE-FE0013865 to evaluate the CO2 capture efficiency,
assess the long-term operability, and identify an optimal process configuration for CO2 capture
solvents. The system is designed to continuously capture the CO2 from the feed gas in the absorber
and release the product CO2 in the desorber while circulating solvent between the two columns.

 18

LsGAS was installed inside a walk-in fume hood to minimize the solvent exposure and emissions
to the lab operator. The system is automated and equipped with process controls to allow the tests
to be run unattended for an extended period. The LsGAS consists of 4 main sections: (i) gas feed
manifold, (ii) absorber column, (iii) desorber column, and (iv) absorber water wash column. The
gas feed manifold section is equipped with mass flow controllers (Brooks Instrument, 5850i) to
control the flow rate of N2 and CO2 to the absorber column. Water is introduced to the gas stream
by flowing the N2 through a water saturator at 40C to produce a humid gas stream containing water
at 7-5 vol%, mimicking the NGCC flue gas.
The absorber column consists of a standard borosilicate glass packed bed, 120 cm in height and
1.9 cm in diameter with a 250 mL borosilicate, tapered wall flask at the bottom that serves as the
absorber sump. The absorber column is packed with approximately 210 g of 1.030 cm2 0.4 cm. x
0.4 cm. (W x H) Pro-Pak® protruded metal packing to provide high surface area for gas-liquid
contact and distribution. The CO2-lean solvent is fed from the top of the column, about 2.5 cm
above the packing and distributed through the randomly packed column as it travels downward.
The CO2-lean feed temperature is measured just before the solvent is discharged into the column
(T7). The simulated flue gas is fed from the bottom and contacts the liquid over the packing where
the CO2 is absorbed in the liquid phase and produces a CO2-depleted gas stream leaving the top of
the absorber. The temperature of the absorber off-gas is monitored by T1 and is controlled between
40-41 °C by the CO2-lean solvent entering at top of the absorber column. The CO2-rich liquid
continues to flow downward and is collected at the absorber sump. The temperature of the CO2-
rich solvent in the absorber sump is measured by T2. The CO2-rich solvent is continuously
extracted from the bottom of the sump via a ceramic piston pump and sent to the desorber. The
solvent temperature is controlled using electrical heat tapes wrapped with a fiberglass insulation
that covered from the outlet of both pumps to the inlets of the desorber column.
The absorber off-gas is sent to a water wash section to recover the amine and reduce the amine
emission. Similar to the absorber, the wash column consists of a standard borosilicate glass
packed-bed gas column, 120 cm in height and 1.9 cm in diameter, positioned on top of a 250 mL
borosilicate, tapered wall flask. The wash column is insulated and packed with Pro-Pak®
protruded metal packing similar to the absorber. The absorber off-gas is then fed through the wash
column at the bottom and leaves the column at the top. Water is circulated from the water wash
sump through a peristaltic pump and discharged at the top of wash column. The water wash sump
temperature is controlled using a heated water bath set at 45-46 C to produce water wash column
operating temperature at around 40 C. The gas leaving water wash section is then sent to a knock-
out pot with condenser to further remove the water from the gas stream. The condenser is cooled
by a mixture of water and propylene glycol which produces a saturated gas leaving the condenser
at 4-6 °C. The CO2 content and amine in the water wash off-gas is then analyzed by a Multigas
Analyzer (MKS 2030) before venting to atmosphere.
The test focused on the operating condition that emulates the NGCC capture condition, specifically
when the cooling water provides to the capture system is available at 35 C. Though the cooling
water temperature varies widely, the availability of 35 C cooling water appears to be a practical
choice and can be provided in many locations. This leads to the approximately 40-45 C water-
saturated flue gas out of the DCC entering the absorber. Similarly, the solvent lean return of 40-
45 C can be achieved with 35 C cooling water.

Long-Term Oxidative Degradation Study
Solvent A2-D9 underwent an extended campaign (>550 hours) to evaluate oxidative degradation.
Samples were typically collected every 24 hours from the absorber, regenerator, water-wash sump,
and knock-out flask. To maintain system inventory, each solvent withdrawal was replaced with an
equal volume (15 mL) of fresh solvent. This replenishment corresponds to approximately 4% of
total system solvent volume per day, a level expected to have minimal impact on degraded-species
accumulation. Condensate collected from the water-wash and knock-out pots was weighed and
analyzed for amine content. Amine emissions from the absorber and water wash were quantified
based on the measured amine mass in the water-wash sump and knock-out pot, respectively.
Approximately 100 mL of water-wash condensate was returned to the wash sump to maintain
solvent inventory and preserve steady-state amine concentration—and hence amine vapor
pressure—in the wash system.
Results and Discussions
97% capture, energy input, emissions
Although the three-component solvent exhibited comparable performance to the two-component
system, its added formulation complexity and the challenge of maintaining amine balance during
operation provided no compelling benefit. As a result, A1–A2–D9 was deprioritized, and
subsequent work focused on A2-D9, including detailed physical and chemical property
characterization. Both solvent systems operated as single-phase liquids under absorber and
regenerator conditions. The CO₂-rich solvent exhibited higher viscosity than the CO₂-lean solvent,
though no precipitation was observed. The water-wash and knock-out pot samples also remained
single phase, indicating minimal amine entrainment in condensed phases. Although the first-
generation NAS demonstrated the lowest total energy consumption and lowest L/G, its amine
emissions from the water-wash column were approximately. While downstream polishing
options—such as acid wash or advanced water wash—could reduce tail-gas emissions, the
associated capital and operating cost could offset the energy-efficiency benefits of the first-
generation NAS formulation. Because of the compact size and substantial heat loss of the LsGAS
unit, the SRD could not be determined with high accuracy. However, comparison of total energy
input across steady-state operating conditions provides useful insight into relative thermal
demands. Under 97% CO₂ capture, A2-D9 and A1–A2–D3 required 14.35% and 4.40% higher
energy input, respectively, compared with NAS. Consistent with this trend, the L/G increased by
43.29% and 35.28%, respectively. Despite these modest increases in L/G and energy input, both
A2-D9 and A1–A2–D3 exhibited substantially lower amine emissions from the absorber and water
wash. Notably, A2-D9 demonstrated approximately 280-fold reduction in water-wash outlet amine
emissions, to approximately 0.5–1.5 ppm. Although a polishing step may still be needed, the
required treatment capacity would be significantly smaller than with first-generation NAS, and
therefore expected to represent a minimal cost impact for GEN2-NAS technologies. Additional
testing was performed for A1–A2–D3 at 97% and 99% capture. At 97% CO₂ capture, this
formulation required slightly lower L/G and total energy input compared with A2-D9; however,
its water-wash amine emissions were approximately 10× higher. When operated at 99% capture,
A1–A2–D3 required substantially higher L/G and energy input and exhibited higher emissions
than A2-D9. These results led to the down-selection of A2-D9 as the lead solvent candidate for
full characterization. The second solvent candidate, A1-A2-D3, was also ran at 97 and 99% capture

Method of analyzing samples with liquid chromatography (LC)
A2, D9 and methylamine were detected using an Agilent Infinity II 1260 HPLC instrument. An
SB C18 column was used with a 4 min method that used 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile as the
eluent. The pure components were benchmarked in the LC for their retention times. The
concentrated process samples from the absorber and desorber were diluted in acetonitrile to
achieve ~1 mg/mL concentration for LC analyses. The water wash samples were run neat.
Method of analyzing samples using proton nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy
(NMR)
A 400 MHz JEOL instrument was used for the analysis. ~ 100-150 mg of the process samples
were taken in ~500 µL of deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide solvent. ~10 mg of 1,3,5-
trimethoxybenzene was added to the samples as an internal standard to quantify different species.
Pure 1-Naphthaldehyde and methylamine were used to benchmark the proton resonances and
were compared with the resonances emerging from the process samples. The water wash samples
were run in deuterated water solvent and a specific pulse sequence preprogrammed on the

Flash Point
Flash-point analysis of the GEN2-NAS solvent was performed by a third-party laboratory to
quantitatively assess material flammability. The flash point is not an intrinsic physical–chemical
constant; rather, it depends on the design of the testing apparatus and execution of the method.
Accordingly, the flash point is defined only with respect to the standardized method used—in this
case, the Pensky–Martens closed-cup procedure. In general, the flash point is the lowest
temperature at which the vapor above a liquid ignites upon application of an ignition source but
does not sustain combustion. Testing was carried out using a Petrotest PM-4 semi-automatic flash-
point analyzer in accordance with ASTM D93-16a, Manual Method, Procedure A. The instrument
provided mechanical stirring of both the bulk liquid and the vapor space to ensure uniform heating;
stirring was automatically halted during ignition. A flash event was defined as the appearance of
a distinct flame that smoothly propagated across the entire surface of the specimen. Such flashes
were typically observed as a blue-to-yellow flame front, occasionally extending through the open
port and capable of extinguishing the ignition flame. A summary of the measured flash-point

Experimental procedure
The absorber condition was selected as the first corrosion-testing scenario. To simulate this
environment, CO₂-loaded GEN2NAS solvent was prepared by gravimetrically introducing
gaseous CO₂ into the neat amine until a loading of approximately 0.2 mol-CO₂ mol-amine⁻¹ was
achieved. The CO₂-loaded amine was then blended with the appropriate quantity of diluent to
obtain the final solvent composition. Metal coupons were cleaned, weighed, and measured prior
to installation. Three coupons each of CS1010, SS304, and SS316 were mounted in the corrosion
manifold, occupying all nine available positions. This configuration enabled retrieval and
evaluation of one coupon of each material after 2, 4, and 6 months of exposure. The system was
first leak-tested and commissioned with water to confirm the absence of leakage and to ensure that
the solvent temperature could be maintained via an external cooling fan during liquid circulation.
After commissioning, the water was drained and the system was purged with air to remove residual
moisture. The prepared GEN2-NAS solvent was then introduced into the system to initiate the
corrosion experiment. Every two months the circulation was stopped to collect a sample of the
liquid solvent and a metal coupon for each material. The solvent was analyzed for metal, formate,
and oxalate content. The coupons were cleaned and dried using ASTM G1 method, then weighed
and its dimensions measured. At the end of the testing campaign the corrosion rate was determined
using Equation 1[9] It is important to note that at the 2-month mark, about 150 g of solvent was
added to the system to have level indicator at a more desirable level, after that no additional solvent
was added to the system. For the entirety of the 6 months campaign the temperature of the manifold
was maintained between 49-52 °C and a pressure of 25-32 psig.

Task 3 - Kinetic Measurements of GEN2NAS
Under Task 3, the measurement of absorption equilibrium and kinetics for GEN2NAS solvent
using PNNL’s wetted wall column (WWC) and pressure-volume-temperature (PVT) cell
capabilities were conducted. The work was initiated on 06/30/2023 with an internal kickoff and
DOE PMOD approval as of 08/21/2023. The project end date was extended from 06/30/2024 to
09/30/2024. The description below summarizes the experiments performed at PNNL to complete
these measurements.
Solvent Candidate
The solvent of interest is GEN2NAS (Sample 003). Candidate solvent Sample 002 was not selected
for characterization based on formulation screening in Task 2.
Desired Property Evaluation
The purpose of the following experiments is to determine temperature-dependent CO2 absorption
properties of GEN2NAS-003. These properties include the evaluation of 1) the partial pressure of
CO2 in the bulk vapor phase that is in equilibrium with the bulk solvent as a function of the amount
of CO2 solubilized (i.e. vapor liquid equilibrium); 2) the overall mass transfer coefficient
describing resistance of CO2 absorption in both the vapor and liquid phases – ideally with an
estimation of each – as a function of the amount of CO2 solubilized; and 3) the viscosity of the
solvent as a function of the amount of CO2 solubilized all at varying temperatures.
PNNL Equipment/Custom Infrastructure
PNNL maintains several custom-built infrastructures for determining fundamental gas-solubility
properties in candidate solvents. For this work, both the pressure–volume–temperature (PVT)

 39

apparatus and the wetted-wall column (WWC) apparatus were utilized. The PVT system, shown
in Figure 31, consists of a calibrated-volume cell held under vacuum into which a small solvent
charge (typically ~50 mL) is introduced. The solvent is recirculated at constant pump speed over
a precisely machined contactor surface inside the cell, forming a continuous thin liquid film. The
system design minimizes solvent holdup by maintaining low internal volume and by visually
confirming stable and reproducible film formation, which depends on viscosity, temperature, and
flow rate. While the film thickness is not directly controlled or measured, it is maintained by
adjusting flow rate to prevent film disruption, thereby allowing the solid–liquid interface along the
contactor surface to be treated as the effective gas–liquid interfacial area. The entire apparatus is
submerged in a water bath to ensure isothermal operation and is instrumented with high-sensitivity
pressure transducers and an inline viscometer for continuous monitoring of vapor-phase pressure
and solvent viscosity.
PVT measurements follow a Sieverts-type methodology in which a pulse of test gas is introduced
into the cell, initiating absorption from the vapor phase into the solvent until vapor–liquid
equilibrium (VLE) is established. The approach to equilibrium is tracked in real time using vapor-
phase pressure measurements, enabling characterization of absorption kinetics through flux
analysis while accounting for mass-transfer resistance in both vapor and liquid phases. After
equilibrium is reached, an additional pulse of test gas is automatically injected. Gas delivery is
controlled by a custom LabView program, which terminates the run once a predetermined vapor-
phase pressure threshold is reached. Test gas is supplied from an upstream calibrated manifold
maintained under the same isothermal conditions; the pressure change in this manifold before and
after injection provides an accurate measure of the amount of gas delivered. Combined with the
vapor-phase pressure change in the cell after equilibrium is re-established, the amount of gas
dissolved in the solvent can be quantified. This analysis assumes either (i) that the vapor phase
consists solely of the test gas—valid when solvent vapor pressure is low—or (ii) that the solvent
vapor-phase composition remains constant with increasing test-gas loading, allowing correction
via partial-pressure accounting. Mixtures of test gases would require continuous headspace
compositional analysis, which is not currently available for the PVT apparatus.

Figure 31. CAD drawing and a schematic of PVT apparatus at PNNL used for solvent property
characterization.

 40

The WWC apparatus has been constructed as shown in Figure 32. Here a relatively larger volume
of solvent (typically about 1500 mL), compared to the PVT apparatus, is loaded into a holding
tank. The solvent is next pre-loaded with CO2 and H2O at the desired equilibrium solubility under
the closed stirred tank environment. This pre-loaded solvent is then transferred to the WWC
apparatus solvent reservoir tank via pump. Once the reservoir is filled, it is allowed to start
recirculating in the WWC. The falling film contactor cell is housed in a thermostat tank set to the
desired run temperature. The remainder of the solvent recirculation loop is also resistively heated
to the same run temperature to maintain isothermal conditions in the solvent. The thickness of the
film is controlled via flowrate of the setpoint and the inherent solvent properties. This film is
visually maintained without disruptions throughout the course of the run. The level in the bottom
of the cell is maintained periodically to be as minimal as possible to ensure gas-liquid contact area
is only at the falling film. The custom fabrication of the falling film contactor is based on prior
literature.[12]
WWC measurements, based on gas‐flux analysis, begin by directing the gas stream through a
bypass loop so that no gas initially contacts the solvent. The gas mixture—containing CO₂,
controlled water vapor, and balance N₂—is continuously analyzed by gas chromatography (GC)
downstream of the WWC apparatus to establish the inlet composition. After the inlet composition
is confirmed stable for several minutes, the flow is redirected to the wetted-wall column, where
the gas contacts a falling solvent film. If the incoming partial pressure of CO₂ in the vapor phase
exceeds the equilibrium vapor pressure associated with the dissolved CO₂ in the liquid phase,
absorption proceeds until a new equilibrium is reached. Conversely, if the incoming CO₂ partial
pressure is below the equilibrium value, CO₂ desorbs from the solvent. When the vapor-phase and
equilibrium CO₂ partial pressures are equal, no net flux occurs, and the outlet gas composition
remains unchanged. The CO₂ flux direction and magnitude are monitored by continuous GC
analysis of the effluent stream. Once a new steady-state composition is observed (typically within
minutes, depending on gas flowrate), the gas flow is returned to the bypass line.
This perturbation–response procedure identifies the inlet gas composition that results in zero CO₂
flux, corresponding to the equilibrium CO₂ partial pressure for the solution at a known CO₂
loading. The method assumes that solvent composition remains unchanged during gas–liquid
contact, which is generally valid when the solvent has low vapor pressure at the test temperature,
the gas–liquid contact period is minimized, and/or the solvent reservoir volume is sufficiently
large. Although data near zero flux are critical for determining equilibrium partial pressures,
measurements taken above and below zero flux also provide valuable information regarding
sorption kinetics and mass‐transfer behavior under varying absorption and desorption driving
forces.
CO₂ flux is quantified from the measured CO₂ volumetric flow into or out of the solvent,
normalized by the gas–liquid contact area, which is approximated as the solid–liquid surface area
of the custom contactor. The equilibrium CO₂ partial pressure at a given solvent loading is
determined from the x-intercept of the linear relationship between CO₂ flux and the pressure
driving force. The overall mass‐transfer coefficient is obtained from the slope of the linear
relationship between CO₂ flux and the log‐mean driving force between inlet and outlet CO₂ partial
pressures relative to the equilibrium partial pressure. The liquid‐phase mass‐transfer coefficient is
then calculated from the overall mass‐transfer coefficient and the gas‐phase coefficient, the latter

 41

Final Report RTI International

estimated using empirical correlations based on gas diffusivity, gas velocity, and flow‐path
geometry.

Absorbers
BsGAS is equipped with 2 absorbers: a conventional absorber and Rotating Packed Bed (RPB).
Only one absorber can be in service during the operation.
Conventional Absorber
 At the system inlet, a simulated flue gas containing air, CO2, and H2O is fed from the bottom
 of the 304SS absorber filled with structured-packing and contacts the CO2-lean solvent over
 the packing where the CO2 is absorbed in the liquid phase and produces a CO2-depleted gas
 stream leaving the top of the absorber. The CO2-rich solvent continues to flow downward and

 45

 is collected at the absorber sump. Four sections of structured packing with solvent extraction
 to and from interstage coolers in between each section are installed in the column to enhance
 gas-liquid contact area as well as regulate temperature rise in the absorber. The temperature of
 the absorber column is maintained between 30-50 °C during the operation. The temperature of
 the treated gas leaving the absorber is controlled to be slightly higher than the feed gas
 temperature to maintain the system’s water balance. The absorber is equipped with a demister
 at the top to remove liquid droplets and aerosols from the treated gas.
 Interstage Coolers
 The interstage coolers are installed at the top, middle, and bottom of the absorber to
 mitigate the temperature rise caused by the exothermic reaction between CO2 and the
 solvent. The rise in temperature negatively impact the solvent’s equilibrium CO2 loading.
 The solvent is continuously extracted between the packing sections into a solvent collection
 tank outside the absorber. The solvent is then pumped through a cooler and fed back to the
 absorber. The temperature of the returned solvent is controlled between 20-40 °C. These
 interstage coolers at BsGAS are plate-and-frame heat exchangers with a cooling water as
 a cooling medium.
Rotating Packed Bed (RPB)
 RPBs are a process intensification design approach that significantly reduces the absorber size
 and cost compared with conventional packed columns (i.e., 10–20X).[16-18] They have been
 successfully applied to improve the gas-liquid extraction process in gas sweetening, beverage,
 and hydraulic fracturing industries.[19] CO2 capture with RPBs has been explored extensively
 at lab scale with more recent scale-up to 0.0.1–1 t-CO2/ day units being investigated, with
 MEA or other aqueous solvents.[16, 17, 19-22]
 The BsGAS’s RPB internal consists of liquid nozzles and a structured packing material that
 rotates with the rotation speed controlled by a VFD motor. The simulated flue gas is introduced
 to the RPB in the void space between the RPB case and the packing. The gas is forced through
 the packing inward and leaves the RPB at the center. The treated gas then flows through to the
 absorber followed by the water wash 1. Note that during the RPB operation, the conventional
 only serves as a flow path where the interstage coolers and liquid line are sealed off. The CO2-
 lean solvent is fed to liquids nozzles where the atomized solvent spreads and wets the packing.
 The CO2 is removed from the gas as the solvent travels outward to the void space between the
 packing and the RPB case. The CO2-rich liquid is recollected at the bottom of the RPB.
CO2-rich Solvent Pump
The CO2-rich solvent is continuously extracted from the bottom of the absorber sump and
discharge the solvent at the top of the conventional regenerator or send the solvent to the flash
regenerator.
Crossover Heat Exchanger
A crossover heat exchanger is used to for heat integration of the system by recovering the heat
from the CO2-lean solvent to partially heat the CO2-rich solvent prior to feeding to the regenerator.
The hot, CO2-lean solvent from the regenerator/flash regeneration is also cooled. The use of
crossover heat exchange lowers both steam and cooling water inputs to the system.

 46

Regenerators
BsGAS is also equipped with 2 regenerators: a conventional regenerator and 2-stage flash (2SF)
regenerator which can be used interchangeably.
Conventional regenerator
 The CO2-rich solvent is fed to the conventional regenerator filled with structured-packing,
 interstage heaters, and a reboiler. Low pressure steam is provided to the interstage heaters and
 reboiler to provide energy to remove CO2 from the solvent. The solvent’s pressure is reduced
 across the pressure regulator to the regenerator column operating pressure, causing some of
 the CO2 to flash off from the liquid. The column serves as a disengage vessel for CO2 to
 separate from the liquid. The structured-packing is required as they increase the surface area
 and residence time for CO2 to desorb from the solvent. Depending on the CO2 product pressure,
 the regenerator temperature varies between 95-120 °C. The conventional regenerator has a
 pressure rating of up to 10 bara. The desorbed CO2 flows upward through the upper section of
 the packing where it meets the condensate reflux from the overhead condenser before exiting
 the column. The CO2 is then cooled by the overhead condenser to condense and return water
 to the regenerator. This returned condensate is used to scrub amine vapor and lower the
 temperature of the desorbed CO2 at the top section of the regenerator. The dry CO2 product
 stream is vent out of the system. A cold, small CO2-rich slip-stream from the outlet of the CO2-
 rich pump is sent to the top of the regenerator column and used as a scrubbing liquid similar
 to returned condensate. The downflow solvent with relatively low CO2 loading (i.e., CO2-lean
 solvent) is collected the bottom of the regenerator, sent to the crossover heat exchanger then
 returned to the top of absorber to continue the CO2 capture operation.
 Reboiler
 A reboiler is installed at BsGAS to handle aqueous-based solvent regeneration through the
 thermosiphon effect. The downflow solvent collected at the regenerator bottom enters the
 reboiler and is heated indirectly by the low pressure steam. The CO2 desorbs from the
 solvent and steam is produced in the reboiler. In the traditional aqueous-based solvent
 system, water component of the solvent is heated and turned into steam to (i) facilitate the
 liquid circulation between the regenerator bottom and the reboiler and (ii) serve as a CO2
 stripping agent as the steam re-enters the bottom of the regenerator and travel up the
 regenerator column.
 Interstage Heaters
 In addition to a reboiler, interstage heaters are installed at the top, middle and bottom of
 the regenerator to gradually heat the solvent as it travels downward the column. The
 temperature is increased from the top to bottom of the column to drive the CO2 loading of
 the solvent to the leaner condition as it travels down the column. The interstage heaters
 were a part of the initial BsGAS installation in 2013 based on an anticipation the reboiler
 would be ineffective to regenerate the non-aqueous solvents as low to no steam would be
 produced. It was speculated that the lack of stripping agent (i.e., steam) reduces the CO2
 diffusivity from the solvent to the gas phase significantly makes the CO2 desorption a mass
 transfer-limited rather than equilibrium-limited process. Based on this assumption, adding

 47

 stages of heating and packings to enhance CO2 desorption process would be more effective
 to remove the CO2 from water-lean solvents.
2-stage flash (2SF) regenerator
 2SF regenerator was installed later at BsGAS in 2019 after 1st generation NAS performance
 results with the interstage heaters at BsGAS and pilot plant run at Tiller, Norway suggest that
 (i) the CO2 desorption for water-lean solvent is not mass-transfer limited but it is equilibrium-
 limited, (ii) no stripping agent is required to facilitate the CO2 desorption from the solvent, and
 (iii) structure packing is not needed and the CO2 desorption happens rapidly. These results
 logically lead to a simple flash regeneration process. For the 2SF regeneration, the CO2-rich
 solvent is redirected from the crossover heat exchanger to the 1st stage flash inlet where the
 solvent is indirectly heated by steam to the target regeneration temperature via a flash
 preheater. The CO2 is flashed out in 1st stage flash tank where the product CO2 flows upward,
 cooled by the overhead condenser, and vent out. Depending on the operating pressure of the
 1st stage flash, the solvent may be sent to the 2nd stage flash tank at the lower pressure to lean
 out the CO2 in the solvent before transferring the solvent back to the absorber. Similar to the
 conventional regenerator, the 2SF regenerator has a pressure rating of up to 10 bara. 2SF also
 has the CO2-rich bypass line with the same function as described in the conventional
 regenerator.
Trim Cooler
The pressurized CO2-lean solvent from the conventional regenerator/2SF has sufficient pressure
to push the solvent through the crossover heat exchanger. A flow controller is placed downstream
of the crossover heat exchanger to control the overall solvent circulation rate of the BsGAS. The
CO2-lean solvent is then further cooled and temperature-controlled prior to feeding to the absorber
top by the solvent trim cooler. The temperature of the solvent is typically controlled between 30-
40 °C.
Water Wash Section 1
A wash section filled with water is used to remove the trace emissions from the treated flue gas
downstream of the absorber. The water wash column is filled with a structured packing similar to
the absorber, allowing the water and amine to come in contact and reach vapor-liquid equilibrium.
The water is circulated through the column by a circulation pump while the pump discharge is also
connected to a bleed valve allowing the wash section to return the wash water back the main
process to control the water balance of the system. The wash water temperature is regulated by an
inline cooler. The wash section is typically operated at a 30-40 °C.
Wash Section 2
In addition to the first water wash section, a second wash column is installed at BsGAS which can
be used as another water wash or as an acid wash column in the case of ultra-low secondary
emissions is evaluated. For the water wash operation, the excess water in wash section 2 is
transferred to water wash section 1 to control the sump level. The temperature of the second water
wash is roughly the same as wash 1 to water balance the system. Alternatively, a dilute sulfuric
acid solution may be used to react with the amines in the treated syngas in under acid wash
operation.

RPB Integration into BsGAS

 53

A 12-inch-diameter RPB was integrated into the BsGAS system by tying into existing gas and
solvent circuits. Lean solvent was supplied to the RPB through the lean-return line downstream of
the crossover heat exchanger and trim cooler (HX-230 in Figure 39). A thermocouple positioned
immediately upstream of the RPB spray nozzles (TE-800) was used to monitor and control the
lean-solvent inlet temperature, while the liquid inlet pressure was recorded using PT-800. The
CO₂-rich solvent exiting the RPB was routed to the absorber sump (C-200), which served as both
a degassing vessel and liquid-level control reservoir. From the sump, the rich solvent was pumped
(P-200) to the crossover heat exchanger and onward to the regenerator. The outlet-solvent
temperature was measured using TE-801.
In the conventional packed-column configuration, lean-solvent circulation is driven by the
pressure differential between the regenerator and absorber. Prior testing demonstrated that GEN2-
NAS achieves improved regeneration at lower regenerator pressures (≈1.5 barg), enhancing CO₂
capture performance. However, when the RPB was placed in service, this low-pressure operation
was insufficient to sustain the required solvent flowrates due to the increased pressure drop across

 Two categories of experimental runs were completed: short term 7-point runs and long term 1-

comprised of seven individual test points were completed to study the impacts of total gas flow

temperature, regenerator pressure and regenerator temperature, and packing type (1-small, 3-
medium, 2-large pore size). Solvent circulation without gas flow was initiated before beginning
the run to preheat and pressurize the regenerator section, ensuring consistent lean solvent loading.
Each point was then tested for 10-15 minutes, after which results were manually recorded; short-
term runs lasted ~1.5 hours, in addition to 2-3 hours needed for initial regenerator heat up.
 After initial screening, several test points were selected for long-term testing to validate the

including an ~2 hour heat-up period and ~6 hours of steady state operation. Runs 15-16 were
completed in the same day with ~3 hours of steady state operation at each point. An estimated 75
hours of time-on stream with solvent circulation was completed across all 16 runs.

Determining Overall mass transfer coefficient (kGa)

 The overall gas-phase volumetric mass-transfer coefficient, kGa, is a critical design and
performance parameter for CO₂ capture in rotating packed beds (RPBs). It represents the rate at
which CO₂ is transferred between the gas phase and the liquid phase containing the amine solvent.
Higher kGa values correspond to faster interphase mass transfer, enabling more rapid CO₂ uptake
and improving capture efficiency. As a result, kGa directly influences absorber sizing; elevated
kGa values permit the use of smaller RPB units, thereby reducing capital cost. Enhanced mass-
transfer performance can also reduce operating cost, as more efficient CO₂ absorption can decrease
solvent circulation rates and regeneration energy requirements.

RTI’s GEN2NAS CO2 Capture Process Description
A preliminary process-flow diagram (PFD) for the GEN2NAS CO₂-capture system using a
process-intensified packing (PIP) absorber is shown in Figure 41. Flue gas from the NGCC power
plant enters the system through a direct-contact cooler (DCC). The DCC contains conventional
structured packing and a closed-loop water-circulation system. Its primary function is to cool the
flue gas to approximately 40 °C, producing a water-saturated stream that exits at the top of the
DCC. A small wastewater stream is generated as water condenses during cooling.
The conditioned flue gas then enters the PIP absorber at the bottom and flows upward through an
intensified packing section. Gas passes through packing openings while cooling water circulates
downward through closed-loop channels interspersed within the packing. Although cooling water
and solvent do not directly contact, the heat of CO₂ absorption is transferred indirectly to the
cooling water, similar to heat transfer in a shell-and-tube heat exchanger. This integrated heat-
management approach eliminates the temperature bulge typically seen in conventional absorbers
with external intercoolers, allowing more uniform absorber temperature, maximizing CO₂ loading,
and minimizing required column height.
The absorber outlet gas, with 97% of CO₂ removed, enters a water-wash section that removes
solvent vapor from the treated gas. Above this, an acid-wash section provides polishing to remove
remaining secondary emissions before the CO₂-free flue gas is vented to stack. To reduce capital
cost, the PIP absorber, water-wash, and acid-wash sections are constructed as a single column,
minimizing interconnecting ductwork and piping. This multifunctional-column concept has
previously demonstrated effectiveness at the 200-TPD scale at TCM. The DCC, PIP absorber,
water wash, and acid wash are operated at 35–40 °C to maximize solvent performance and
maintain water balance under a cooling-water constraint of 35 °C.
The water-wash section requires only an initial charge of fresh water. It contains structured packing
to enhance gas–liquid contact, and the wash loop is operated in closed-circuit temperature-
controlled mode. Water balance is maintained by cooling the flue gas leaving the absorber to 1–2
°C below the inlet flue-gas temperature (~40 °C), promoting condensation of water vapor and
solvent. Water condensed in the wash section is returned to the absorber to recover solvent.

 60

An acid-wash section sits above the water wash and uses the same type of structured packing. The
section operates at essentially the same temperature as the water wash. A small dosing pump
continuously injects concentrated sulfuric acid (>95 vol% H₂SO₄) into the circulating stream. The
circulating acid solution is temperature-controlled to maintain water balance, and its pH is
controlled to limit secondary emissions to <1 ppm. A small wastewater bleed is removed to
maintain acceptable salt concentration.
The CO₂-rich solvent collected at the bottom of the PIP column is transferred to a flash regenerator
via a CO₂-rich pump. Downstream of this pump, a crossover heat exchanger is used to exchange
heat between hot CO₂-lean solvent and the incoming CO₂-rich solvent, reducing steam demand.
The CO₂-rich stream is further preheated in a steam preheater and then expanded into the flash
regenerator. This preheater represents the primary source of regeneration energy in the GEN2NAS
process; thus, the specific reboiler duty (SRD) is calculated from its steam consumption. The CO₂-
lean solvent then passes through the crossover heat exchanger and a trim cooler before being
returned to the top of the PIP absorber.
In the flash regenerator, CO₂ disengages from solution and rises to the top of the vessel, while
regenerated lean solvent flows to the bottom. Flashing occurs due to the pressure drop between the
feed and vessel operating pressure, which is maintained at 1–4 bara and 100–120 °C. The CO₂
product stream is dehydrated by an overhead condenser and conditioned to pipeline-quality before
being delivered to the plant boundary at 2,215 psia for sequestration.

Sensitivity Analysis
Given that the GEN2NAS is still at an early TRL, more extensive operating data and performance
evaluation at larger scale are still needed to develop a more realistic cost model. Some of the key
performance parameters have been determined in this project but reproducibility of these results
is yet to be seen in a realistic condition. The following aspects of the GEN2NAS technology are
highly encouraged to be revisited or re-evaluated under real flue gas condition.
Solvent-Related
 - Corrosivity
 - Amine recovery from the heat stable salts produced by SOx and NOx
 - Trace contaminants in CO2 product
 - Oxidative degradation
 - Nitrosamine formation under atmospheric condition
 - Solvent supply-chain and manufacturing scale-up
Process-Related
 - Feasibility of using carbon steel for equipment and process plumbing
 - The cost of RPB and PIP at-scale
 - Process configuration to handle non steady-state operation

Appendix A: Technology Maturation Plan
Introduction
In the past 14 years, RTI’s Non-Aqueous Solvent (NAS) has demonstrated high potential to be
used for large-scale carbon dioxide (CO2) capture owing to its low regeneration energy. The low
energy requirement is realized through the replacement of water with low heat capacity organic
diluent, in which case water does not need to be boiled and used as a stripping agent. The combined
energy savings from avoiding water’s latent heat and heat of vaporization result in a low energy
consumption during the regeneration. First-generation RTI NAS (GEN1NAS) has demonstrated
its retrofit ability, operability, and low energy requirement and performed thousands of hours of
continuous CO2 capture from laboratory to pilot scale in earlier Department of Energy (DOE)
projects (DE-FE001386, DE-FE0002646, DE-FE0031590, and DE-FE0031660). GEN1NAS is
currently licensed to SLB (formerly known as Schlumberger), where the large-scale system
development and commercialization of the technology are being worked on.
Despite the low energy usage and other benefits compared with aqueous solvents, NAS shortfalls
include high emissions and low volumetric working capacity (i.e., low amine in the formulation).
The low amine content in the formulation is a technical limitation due to the high viscosity of the
solution at high amine content and thus, making the NAS unsuitable to be used in conventional
absorber column design. Though the cost of capture using NAS for natural gas combined cycle
(NGCC) applications are projected to be lower than the state-of-the-art aqueous solvent
CANSOLV, these shortfalls ultimately cause the capital expense (CAPEX) and operational
expense (OPEX) of NAS CO2 capture process to remain unpalatable for commercial uses as well
as creating environmental concerns over fugitive emissions.
Purpose of the project
In alignment with the Funding Opportunity Announcement (FOA)’s specific goal, the objective of
this project is to advance a novel second-generation NAS (GEN2NAS) and intensified process for
higher efficiency (97%+) CO2 capture that will meet the goal of lowering the cost of CO2 capture
at NGCC plants by 40% compared with the DOE’s baseline Case B31B based on Cost and
Performance Baseline for Fossil Energy’s Plants Volume 1: Bituminous Coal and Natural Gas to
Electricity Revision 4A (Sep., 2019 edition). GEN2NAS is developed to address the 2 technical
shortfalls mentioned earlier: high emission and low working capacity. An improved formulation
utilizing a higher molecular weight amine and the introduction of the intensified process
equipment, specifically the Rotating Packed Beds (RPBs), will be the key enablers to developing
a low-cost, environmentally-friendly CO2 capture technology for NGCC application. The scope of
work evaluates the efficacy of a novel GEN2NAS formulation and its process. The solvent will
perform particularly well for NGCC plants using a CAPEX-lowering RPB process and utilizing
RTI’s GEN2NAS solvent. The equipment consists of a RPB direct contact cooler (DCC), absorber,
water wash, and a flash regenerator operating at higher pressure, which reduces the equipment
footprint and CAPEX compared with a conventional packed column and stripper. The RPB
enables the use of solvents containing a high molecular weight amine at high concentration,
resulting in a low emission and high working capacity solvent. The proposed technology is
projected to decrease both CAPEX and OPEX and will ultimately lower the cost of CO2 capture

Since the identity of these species has been identified in this project, we propose to develop gas

Atmospheric Reaction of GEN2NAS Amine. Amines react in the atmosphere with the hydroxyl
(OH) radical and other oxidants to form potentially harmful products, such as nitrosamines and
nitramines. Nitrosamines can also photolyze back to the amino radical. Simulating these processes
requires the use of a photochemical model that can provide concentrations of the oxidants that
influence amine chemistry. The atmospheric chemical mechanisms of GEN2NAS are critical to
the development of photochemical and air dispersion model. The impacts of air pollutant
concentration downwind of GEN2NAS CO2 capture plant can be assessed through the use of air
dispersion model.
We propose conducting the chamber experiments to construct the gas-phase chemistry mechanism
along with the plume modeling to understand the air dispersion of these photo-reacted products.
A gas-phase photoreaction of GEN2NAS amine with hydroxyl (OH) radical can be conducted in
chamber experiments to determine the products of the reaction. Once the reaction mechanisms are
known, they will be incorporated into an air dispersion model along with the meteorology inputs
for a site-specific location.

various Technology Centre Mongstad (TCM) test campaigns. The instrument was also used in
monitoring the NAS amine emissions during its demonstration at TCM in 2022. The emissions
reading from PTR-TOF-MS closely match the emissions reported by manual gas samplings and
thus validating the instrument results.
We propose PTR-TOF-MS method development to be a part of GEN2NAS technology for robust
online emissions monitoring technique.

Appendix B: Technoeconomic Assessment
Introduction
Technoeconomic assessment (TEA) plays a pivotal role in evaluating the feasibility and economic
viability of emerging technologies aimed at mitigating carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions,
particularly in the field of carbon capture and storage (CCS). In the global pursuit of addressing
climate change and achieving net-zero carbon emissions, CO2 capture solvent technology has
emerged as a promising solution. This technology involves the deployment of novel solvents and
processes to capture CO2 emissions from industrial facilities, power plants, and other emission
sources such as cement and steel production facilities. As nations and industries worldwide commit
to reducing their carbon footprint, the critical importance of conducting a comprehensive TEA for
CO2 capture solvent technology cannot be overstated. CO2 capture solvent technology holds
immense potential to substantially reduce emissions by capturing CO2 before it is released into the
atmosphere. However, for this technology to be adopted on a large scale, it is essential to assess
not only its technical feasibility but also its economic viability.
TEA serves as a systematic and rigorous evaluation process that quantifies the economic
implications of CO2 capture solvent technology. It considers factors such as capital costs,
operational expenses, energy requirements, and potential revenue streams, allowing decision-
makers to determine whether investing in this technology is financially sound. Furthermore, TEA
assists in identifying cost reduction strategies and optimizing the technology's performance,
thereby ensuring its competitiveness in a rapidly evolving market. In this TEA report, we will
evaluate the key aspects of conducting a TEA for the second generation non-aqueous solvent
(GEN2NAS) CO2 capture solvent technology, key process configuration, and its associated cost
of capture comparted to the state-of-the-art Cansolv technology. The assessment methodology,
cost basis and sparing philosophy follow that outlined in the NETL baseline study rev. 4a [1]. The
report contains 3 sections. Section 1 presents process and scale-up models where the GEN2NAS
thermophysical inputs are provided to process simulation and validate the simulated results with
the actual measurements. The equipment sizing, energetic requirements, and process configuration
obtained from Section 1 serves as a baseline to perform the TEA described in Section 2. Finally,
Section 3 presents a sensitivity analysis where key parameters are varied to observe how they
change the cost of CO2 capture.
Process and Scale-Up Models
The GEN2NAS formulation is very similar to the first generation non-aqueous solvent (NAS), but
the hydrophobic amine in NAS has been replaced with another amine belonging to the same family
that has significant lower vapor pressure to reduce emissions under lower CO2 partial pressure on
the natural gas derived flue gas. Most physical and chemical properties of GEN2NAS are similar
to the NAS, and the two formulations perform similarly in terms of energy requirement,
temperature and pressure operating windows, and liquid circulation rates. The modeling approach
adopted here is that the NAS process model will be used to assess the performance of GEN2NAS,
and the data from the lab-scale characterization and bench-scale testing of GEN2NAS in this
project will be used to update the NAS model to more accurately simulate the GEN2NAS
performance.

 74

RTI has developed a process model in Aspen Plus for CO2 capture using NAS. The thermodynamic
model uses the symmetric electrolyte non-random two-liquid (eNRTL-SR) framework for the
liquids and the Redlich-Kwong (RK) equation of state for the gases. Model parameters such as
binary and ternary interaction parameters for the thermodynamic model were obtained by
regression of experimental vapor–liquid equilibrium (VLE), heat of absorption, vapor pressure,
and heat capacity data. Other data in the model include the vapor pressure of individual
components and for NAS formulation, the density, viscosity, liquid heat capacity, and thermal
conductivity. Mass and heat transfer models were adapted from the Aspen Plus Rate-Based
RadFrac column without adjusting any tuning parameters such as interfacial area factor, heat
transfer factor, and liquid and mass transfer coefficient. The mass transfer and interfacial area
coefficients published in a journal1 were used in the model, and the Chilton and Colburn analogy
was adapted for the heat transfer coefficient. The model predictions for the specific reboiler duty
(SRD) are always higher than the experimentally observed values as shown in Figure 42, thus
providing a conservative estimate for the SRDs. Experimental data and model predictions of the
temperature profile for a representative case from Technology Centre Mongstad (TCM) are shown
in Figure 43. The temperature profiles shown are typical for most runs with NAS. The temperature

TEA Narrative
Process Description
The preliminary process flow diagram of the GEN2NAS CO2 capture using process intensified
packing (PIP) column is shown in Figure 44. The flue gas from the NGCC power plant enters a
direct contact cooler (DCC) at the bottom. The DCC contains a conventional structured-packing
internal and equipped with a close-loop water circulation. The primary function of the DCC for
NGCC application is to cool the flue gas to about 40 C and producing a water-saturated flue gas
leavening the DCC at the top. A small wastewater stream is generated due to the water condensate
from lowering the flue gas temperature.
The flue gas at 40 C enters the PIP column at the bottom and flows upward through the intensified
packing section of the column. The flue gas is distributed and flows through openings where the
cooling water flows downward in the closed-loop cooling channel that sits in between the
openings. The solvent and cooling water do not come in contact directly but the heat of CO2
absorption from the solvent is transferred to the cooling water indirectly in similar fashion of a
shell and tube heat exchanger. The PIP column design eliminates the bulge temperature typically
seen in the traditional absorber column with an external intercooler, allows the uniform
temperature distribution during CO2 which maximizes the CO2 loading of the solvent while
minimizing the column height. The absorber outlet gas, with 97% of CO2 removed, enters a water
wash section where the majority of the solvent vapor is removed from the treated gas. The acid
wash section, sitting above of the waster wash section, provides the final polishing step to remove
all the secondary emissions from the treated gas before venting the CO2-free flue gas to the stack.
The PIP, water wash, and acid wash sections will be built into a single column construction to
minimize the gas ducting and liquid plumbing, resulting in a low CAPEX compared to a separate

 76

equipment design. The design of multi-functional column is proven to be effective at 200 TPD
scale at TCM. The DCC, PIP absorber, water wash, and acid wash are operated at 35-40 C to
maximize CO2 capture efficiency of the solvent as well as water balancing of the CO2 capture plant
under the constraint of cooling water temperature of 35 C.
The water wash section requires a fresh water during the initiate charge only. The water wash
contains a conventional structured-packing section to enhance gas-liquid contact. The water is
circulated in a closed-loop and temperature-controlled. The system’s water balance is controlled
by cooling the flue gas leaving the absorber outlet by 1-2 C compared to the flue gas entering the
PIP section at 40 C. The flue gas leaving the absorber typically is at 40-45 C and thus facilitate the
condensation process for water vapor as well as the solvent in the water wash. The water wash is
then transferred the condensed water back to the absorber to recuperate the solvent.
An acid wash is installed on top of the water using the same conventional structured packing as
and is maintained at essentially the same temperature as water wash. A small dosing pump is
installed after the pump outlet to continuously injecting the >95 vol% H2SO4 to the liquid stream.
The wash liquid is temperature-controlled to maintain the water balance similar to the water wash.
The wash liquid contains small amount of the H2SO4 is pH-controlled to limit the secondary
emissions to below 1 ppm from the capture plant. The acid wash section will generate a small
wastewater stream to limit the salt concentration in the acid wash section.
The CO2-rich solvent is collected at the bottom of the PIP column and transferred to the flash
regenerator via a CO2 rich pump. The crossover heat exchanger, installed downstream of the CO2-
rich pump, is used to transfer the heat CO2-lean solvent and preheat the CO2-rich solvent to
minimize the steam requirement during the regeneration. The CO2-rich solvent is pressure-
controlled by a back pressure regulator and preheated through a preheater prior to flashing in the
flash regenerator. The steam preheater is the primary source of regeneration energy in the
GEN2NAS process and the SRD is determined through the steam consumption of this preheater.
The CO2-lean solvent passes through the crossover heat exchanger, a trim cooler, and fed at the
top of the PIP column to continue the CO2 capture from the flue gas.
The CO2 disengages from CO2-rich solvent in the flash regenerator and flows upward to the top
of the flash tank while the CO2-lean solvent flows downward to the bottom. The CO2 flashing
happens due to the pressure difference between the liquid feed line and the operating pressure of
the flash tank, which is controlled between 1-4 bara and 100-120 C. The CO2 product gas is de-
watered through an overhead condenser and is conditioned to pipeline transportation specifications
then delivered at sequestration-ready conditions at 2,215 psia at the plant gate.

Sensitivity Analysis
Given that the GEN2NAS is still at an early TRL, more extensive operating data and performance
evaluation at larger scale are still needed to develop a more realistic cost model. Some of the key
performance parameters have been determined in this project but reproducibility of these results
is yet to be seen in a realistic condition. The following aspects of the GEN2NAS technology are
highly encouraged to be revisited or re-evaluated under real flue gas condition.
Solvent-Related
 - Corrosivity
 - Amine recovery from the heat stable salts produced by SOx and NOx
 - Trace contaminants in CO2 product
 - Oxidative degradation
 - Nitrosamine formation under atmospheric condition
 - Solvent supply-chain and manufacturing scale-up
Process-Related
 - Feasibility of using carbon steel for equipment and process plumbing
 - The cost of RPB and PIP at-scale
 - Process configuration to handle non steady-state operation
With these unknowns surrounding the GEN2NAS technology, we performed a sensitivity analysis
on the cost of CO2 capture by varying three most critical cost drivers based on our current
understanding by ±30% from its baseline value to examine their impacts to the cost of CO2 capture.
These parameters were (a) CAPEX of the CO2 capture plant, (b) solvent cost in $/kg, (c) cost of
natural gas, and (d) SRD. Results are shown in $/tonne-CO2. The fuel cost (i.e., natural gas) shows
to have the strongest impact to the CO2 capture cost for GEN2NAS. The fuel cost is directly linked
to the natural gas consumption for solvent regeneration the flash regenerator.
From the results, it is observed that the cost of capture is fairly sensitive to the cost of the solvent
and the SRD and has the highest sensitivity to the CAPEX and the fuel cost (i.e., natural gas).
