STOYAIN ANAESTHESIA.*.
By JAMES TAYLOR, F.R.C.S.Ed.,
Assistant Surgeon, Royal Infirmary, Glasgow.
Spinal injection as a means of inducing anaesthesia has never
become a very popular method with the profession. There are
several reasons for this?the trouble of giving the injection and
the time it takes, the strain of operating on a conscious patient,
the occasional faintness of the patient, and, to a certain extent,
the variability of the effect of the drug. These all act as
deterrents, and although a great many surgeons use the method
occasionally, comparatively few make a regular practice of it.
I apologise for this paper on a subject about which so much
has been written already, because I really have nothing original
to say, but I hope to fye able to show that in a great many cases
the advantages of stovain more than compensate for the dis-
advantages, the chief of which I have mentioned above. I do
not propose to discuss spinal anaesthesia in a general way, but
simply to give an account of my own experience, and this is
limited to something over live hundred cases. I have only used
stovain, and only the heavy variety of that. I believe some of
the other drugs are safer, but none give such a perfect muscular
paralysis, and as most of my cases have been abdominals, this is
a very important point. I have always used stovain and
glucose, because I think that the level to which the anaesthesia
extends can be better regulated with this preparation. All my
injections have been given in the lumbar region, and I have no
experience at all of those higher injections for operations on the
arms and neck.
The strongest recommendation for stovain is that it is the
most perfect method of blocking the afferent nerves, and pro-
tecting the patient from shock?in other words, of obtaining
anoci-association. Unfortunately, just after its injection the

Operations, therefore, which are associated with much shock
can be more successfully performed under spinal anaesthesia
than with any other anaesthetic. As tending to bear out this
statement, I might mention that I have done ten disarticulations
at the hip after an intra-thecal injection, and all these cases
except one got over the operation. This patient was in a railway
smash, and was brought in in such a mangled condition and so
collapsed that under ordinary circumstances he would have been
left to die, but I tried to save him by removing the mangled
limb in the hope that there would be less shock after the clean
division of the nerves. He died about eight hours later. The
other amputations were mostly done for septic gunshot wounds,
and as prolonged attempts had been made to save the limbs the
patients were invariably in a very low condition, but they all
recovered from the operation.
Another group of cases which bears out my contention that
the patient is protected to a large extent from shock, is a
collection of cases of Wertheim's operation for cancer of the
cervix. I have performed this operation on twelve consecutive
cases without a death. Many of these cases were suffering from
very extensive disease, and with the exception of one or two, in
which complete eradication of the disease seemed quite hopeless,
the iliac glands were removed as well as the primary growth.
The death-rate in this operation is high, even in the hands of
the most experienced surgeons. My own death-rate before I
began using a spinal anaesthetic was two out of seven cases. I
might also say that in one of those twelve cases mentioned
above, in addition to removing the uterus, parametrial tissue,
and upper part of the vagina, I removed the whole rectum from
the pelvic colon downwards, an operation which would have
been quite impossible for me without the help of a spinal
anaesthetic.
I think I have said enough to demonstrate the advantages of
stovain in operations associated with much shock.
Cases with much abdominal distention?e.g., intestinal obstruc-
tion?form a group where it has a very great advantage in

another way. Every surgeon has experienced the great difficulty
of closing the abdomen when the bowel is distended with gas.
Indeed, this part of the operation often takes up much more time
than the actual relief of the obstruction. With a good stovain
anaesthesia there is none of this trouble. The abdominal muscles
are so completely paralysed that the edges of the abdominal
wound can easily be brought together. The earlier part of the
operation is much facilitated also, because one can quickly take
out the whole, or greater part, of the intestine, so as to get one's
bearings, and have no anxiety about getting it easily returned.
There is also this advantage that there is not the same tendency
to paralysis of the involuntary muscle of the intestine?
in fact, during the operation, and while the bowel was being
handled, I have often seen powerful contractions of the
intestine going on.
But while in operations accompanied by much shock, and in
operations for the relief of intestinal obstruction, stovain is the
anaesthetic par excellence, it may be used with advantage in
almost any operation on the abdomen or lower limbs. I have
personally performed most abdominal operations under it. I
will speak now of some of the disadvantages, and while doing so
mention some of the contra-indications.
I have referred to the trouble of giving the injection. This
does not really amount to much. If the skin of the back, at the
site of the puncture, is anaesthetised by making a small wheal
with novocain, the patient has little pain from the puncture, and
the operator has seldom much difficulty in getting into the spinal
canal. I find that I can usually anaesthetise the patient in about
the same time as it takes to put him under chloroform. As to
the strain of operating on a conscious patient, I find it much less
than with an inhalation anaesthesia where the administrator is
not a skilled anaesthetist. I may say, however, that one must
have the proper mental atmosphere in the theatre. I have
noticed that when operating where the staff was unaccustomed
to spinal anaesthetics, I did not have such good results, because
the nurses were anxious, and in spite of the fact that they tried
to disguise the fact, the anxiety was invariably communicated to
the patient. However, after a few cases had been done and the
nurses saw that the patient was not suffering, they ceased to

hyoscine, or omnopon and hyoscine, one can have the patient

that after this injection patients are more likely to suffer from
chest complications. I might say, in passing, that I am very
doubtful if stovain has any advantage over chloroform in cases
with bronchitis or other pulmonary lesion. I think a post-
operative lung condition is as common after stovain as after a
general anaesthetic.
To my mind, the greatest objection to stovain is the initial
fall of blood-pressure, which is produced by the drug itself.
Just at the beginning of the operation the pulse often becomes
soft, the face pale, and there may be sickness and vomiting.
With a moderately healthy patient there is seldom reason for
anxiety; the apparent collapse may be easily counteracted by an
injection of pituitary extract, or by dropping a little ether on
a mask and holding it over the patient's face. But in the old
and feeble, in people with serious renal disease, and those who
are suffering from recent loss of blood or anaemia, the condition
of the patient may become quite alarming. Even while one
realises this, it may often be worth while in those very
weak patients to take the extra immediate risk for the
eminently better result that one will obtain afterwards if the
patient has been protected from afferent impulses during the
operation.
Two cases I have had recently illustrate this point. In one of
the cases I only gave the anaesthetic for Mr. Kay to operate; in
the other case I gave the anaesthetic and operated myself. The
first patient was a very feeble old man, with bronchitis and
a weak heart, who was suffering from retention from an enlarged
prostate. It was considered that the patient had little chance of
recovery if a general anaesthetic was used, and so an injection of
stovain was given. Soon after the injection the patient became
unconscious, and the breathing became Cheyne-Stokes in
character. As the operation could not possibly make the
patient any worse, Mr. Kay decided to remove the prostate,

He developed acute intestinal obstruction, and I saw him when

to give him stovain, as I did not think he could possibly recover
under a general anaesthetic. After his injection he became
unconscious like the previous case, and his pulse became
imperceptible. As all afferent impulses were cut off and the
operation could not affect him in any way, a little ether was
given him on a mask and laparotomy performed. I found an
inoperable growth at the hepatic flexure, but was able to relieve
the obstruction by short circuiting. The patient made a good
recovery.
Headache is sometimes complained of during the first few days
after the operation, but this is unusual. I have noticed that it
always occurred among several patients at the same time.
Presumably, the anaesthetic came from a particular batch of
stovain-ampules, and I should think that the symptom was due
to some impurity in the drug.
The duration of the anaesthesia is variable, and the higher up
the site of the operation the shorter time it lasts. One can
seldom count on much above half an hour in the upper abdomen,
but in one case it lasted nearly an hour, and I was able to
excise a third of the stomach under it. In the lower abdomen
an hour is about the usual time, but if the operation is not over
in this time, the stovain anaesthesia can be supplemented
by a general anaesthetic. In almost all the Wertheim operations
I have mentioned this was necessary, for the operation usually
lasted about an hour and a half, but the part of the operation
which would cause most shock was always done before the
general anaesthetic was necessary.
The higher up the trunk is the site of the operation the
greater the difficulty in managing the anaesthetic, for the margin
of safety is not very great, and it is easy to allow too high an
extension. I have only once seen this happen, however, and in

that case the patient recovered after a short spell of artificial

trouble. I might also mention this fact, which does not seem to

of safety of five minutes, but I regularly lower the patient's
head after twenty minutes in pelvic operations.
I hope these notes of my personal experience of stovain may
encourage some to try further this anaesthetic. Although my
five hundred cases include patients from 3 years to 70, many of
whom have been in an extremely weak condition, I have had
no deaths.
