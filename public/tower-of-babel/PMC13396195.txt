Microbial engraftment and immune regulation during fecal microbiota transplantation and immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy
Authors: Marina Ninkov, Saman Maleki Vareki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The gut microbiota is a critical determinant of both therapeutic efficacy and immune-related toxicity during cancer immunotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has emerged as a strategy to introduce beneficial microbial functions, yet clinical outcomes remain variable. In this Review, we integrate evidence from recent clinical trials combining FMT with ICIs in a mechanism-based framework for understanding this variability and to guide safer clinical applications. We discuss how specific microbial functional programs can either buffer or lower thresholds for immune dysregulation and outline implications for donor selection, longitudinal monitoring, and trial design in oncology.

Introduction

The commensal microbiota comprises diverse bacterial, archaeal, fungal, and viral communities that perform metabolic and immunological functions essential to host physiology. Owing to its extensive functional repertoire and capacity to influence distant organs, the microbiota has increasingly been conceptualized as a distinct host “organ”—one that can be perturbed, injured, or therapeutically restored1–3. In this context, analogous to organ transplantation, microbiota from a healthy donor can be introduced into a dysbiotic recipient with a disrupted microbiome state through fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)4,5.

Although strategies to replenish perturbed microbial communities via FMT date back to traditional Chinese medicine6 and agricultural practices such as transfaunation7, the concept was later explored clinically across a range of conditions. The clearest evidence of FMT’s therapeutic potential was demonstrated in the effective treatment of recurrent Clostridioides difficile (rCDI) infection, where restoration of microbial diversity and community structure breaks the cycle of antibiotic-induced dysbiosis. In this setting, FMT achieves durable remission in ~90% of patients and has become a benchmark for how re-establishing ecological microbial homeostasis can directly translate into clinical cure8,9. This indication also marked a regulatory turning point for microbiota-based therapies, with two FDA-approved live biotherapeutic products, REBYOTA10 and VOWST11, for the treatment of rCDI in adults following antibacterial treatment. In addition, FMT has been explored clinically in the treatment of disorders such as colitis12–14, Crohn’s disease15,16, hepatic encephalopathy17, graft-versus-host disease18,19, and metabolic dysfunction20–22.

More recently, some of the most compelling insights into microbiota function have emerged in oncology. In this context, the gut microbiota has been recognized as a key modulator of both chemotherapy23 and immunotherapy24–31, shaping their antitumor efficacy and host tolerance to treatment. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI), such as anti-PD(L)1 or anti-CTLA4 drugs, are used across an expanding spectrum of solid tumors and lymphomas; however, immune-related adverse events (irAEs) continue to constrain dose intensity, treatment duration, and patient eligibility. Toxicity profiles differ according to ICI class and treatment regimen. Anti-PD-1 agents such as nivolumab and pembrolizumab, which form the backbone of therapy in melanoma, non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and renal cell carcinoma (RCC), are commonly associated with colitis, pneumonitis, hepatitis, and dermatologic toxicities32–34. The incidence and severity of irAEs are substantially higher with CTLA-4-containing regimens, particularly ipilimumab combined with nivolumab, for which grade 3–4 toxicities occur in ~30–40% of patients in pooled analyses26,30,31,35–37. Management of severe and occasionally fatal irAEs across intestinal, respiratory, cardiovascular, hepatic, and endocrine systems relies largely on broad immunosuppression, which may compromise antitumor immunity38–41. Collectively, these limitations constrain therapeutic intensity and durability, establishing toxicity as a central barrier to the optimal deployment of ICIs in routine oncology practice.

FMT has been explored as a strategy to mitigate treatment-associated toxicities and to restore immune competence in patients whose microbial ecosystems appear to have been functionally compromised either at baseline by cancer or by anticancer therapy26,27,30,31,42–44. To date, FMT–ICI studies have centered mainly on melanoma, RCC, and NSCLC, with TACITO providing the first randomized placebo-controlled evidence in metastatic RCC45.

Here, we frame the clinical impact of FMT in cancer immunotherapy within established principles of microbial ecology46, emphasizing how donor-derived microbial persistence and functional activity may shape therapeutic benefit and susceptibility to irAEs during ICI therapy. This review focuses on the gut microbiota, the compartment directly targeted by FMT and most consistently profiled across FMT–ICI trials. We provide a narrative synthesis of recent clinical and translational studies, focusing on transferable principles from PERFORM30, TACITO45, FMT-LUMINate31, MIMIC26, and earlier studies by Baruch27 and Davar28 (Table 1). How recipient and donor biology shape FMT outcomes

The clinical effects of FMT in oncology reflect coordinated interactions between recipient-specific biological and ecological features and the functional properties of the donor microbiota47–49. Together, these factors determine the efficiency of FMT engraftment, the extent of systemic and tumor-specific immune remodeling, and downstream response and toxicity to ICIs. As FMT moves from exploratory phase I studies toward disease-specific therapeutic strategies, understanding this multidimensional interplay has become essential for clinical translation.

Host factors shaping engraftment and FMT outcomes

Host-specific microbial, immunological, and metabolic features shape FMT through two related but distinct layers: donor microbiota engraftment and downstream clinical outcome50. Engraftment refers to the ecological integration of donor-derived organisms or functions, including donor-strain acquisition, donor–recipient microbiome similarity, displacement of recipient species, and persistence of donor-derived metabolic activity. Clinical outcome refers to patient-level endpoints such as objective response, disease control, and immune-related toxicity. Distinguishing these layers is important because donor engraftment may occur without clinical benefit to the recipient. In contrast, clinical response or toxicity may depend on the immune and metabolic context in which the donor microbiome could impact the host microbiome, including the loss of deleterious microorganisms in the FMT recipient.

Recipient factors associated with donor engraftment

The recipient microbiota acts as an ecological filter that constrains donor integration and influences engraftment efficiency, durability, and stability29,48. Across FMT–ICI trials discussed here, baseline gut microbial α-diversity refers to recipient stool diversity measured after cancer diagnosis but before FMT-related conditioning or FMT administration. This α-diversity shows context-dependent associations with donor strain engraftment, without a consistent relationship with clinical response or immune-related toxicity. In the melanoma MIMIC trial26, lower baseline diversity was associated with greater donor-strain engraftment, particularly at early post-FMT time points, whereas Baruch and Davar found no correlation between α-diversity and engraftment27,28. Across the aforementioned trials, α-diversity was not associated with clinical outcome, and in PERFORM, baseline α-diversity did not differ between patients with or without grade ≥3 irAEs30.

Pre-intervention microbiota may also shape the immune context in which engraftment occurs. Taxa such as Segatella copri, Enterococcus faecalis, Alcaligenes faecalis, and Streptococcus species have been associated with elevated mucosal or systemic inflammatory responsiveness and Th17-skewed immune programs across human cohorts and experimental models51–53. Such inflammatory microbial states may sustain antigenic and cytokine stimulation, promoting CD8⁺ T-cell dysfunction and reduced antitumor activity54. In intestinal tumorigenesis models, microbial dysbiosis can similarly hyperstimulate CD8⁺ T cells, promoting chronic inflammation while reducing effective antitumor CD8⁺ T-cell function53. However, these effects are strain- and context-dependent and should not be inferred from genus-level identity alone55.

Engraftment features associated with clinical outcomes

Although pre-FMT α-diversity alone has not consistently predicted patient-level outcome, several FMT-ICI studies suggest that the extent and quality of microbial reprogramming after FMT are associated with therapeutic response and irAEs. Clinical outcome appears to depend less on diversity at a single pre-FMT time point than on whether donor-derived communities displace persistent recipient species, restore functional pathways, and reshape immune tone.

Across PERFORM, MIMIC, and FMT-LUMINate, clinical trajectories differed according to whether recipient microbial communities were displaced or persisted after FMT. In PERFORM, persistence of recipient microbiota after FMT was observed in non-responders and in patients with severe ICI-related toxicity30. Across melanoma FMT studies, including the MIMIC phase I trial26 and the trials by Baruch and Davar27,28, therapeutic response was associated with remodeling of pre-FMT recipient species. FMT-LUMINate showed that responders had significantly greater loss of pre-intervention bacterial species than non-responders31. These findings suggest recipient microbial persistence as a constraint on functional reprogramming and clinical benefit.

In addition, the recipient’s inflammatory state may further shape interactions between donor and recipient microbiota. In ICI-induced colitis, active disease is associated with acute reductions in microbiota density, α-diversity, and richness compared with pre-colitis samples56. In treatment-naive patients with NSCLC without recent antibiotic exposure, elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio were similarly associated with reduced sputum microbial α-diversity, altered airway microbiota, and disrupted predicted metabolic pathways57. These findings suggest that inflammatory tone can reshape mucosal microbial ecosystems across distinct anatomical sites and may favor taxa adapted to inflamed environments. By contrast, hosts enriched with SCFA-producing taxa support epithelial barrier integrity, limit microbial translocation, and reinforce regulatory immune responses29,30,58. Whether these features directly promote more durable donor engraftment in FMT–ICI settings remains to be determined.

Metabolomics as a mechanistic bridge

Metabolomics provides a critical bridge linking ecological restructuring and immune responses in the setting of FMT–ICI therapy. Across FMT–ICI trials, two metabolic axes emerge with particular consistency: a histidine-associated stress-resilience signature accompanied by cortisol dynamics in RCC30, and the tryptophan–kynurenine pathway, associated with non-response to ICI and immune dysregulation in melanoma and NSCLC31.

The first metabolic axis proposes that preservation of histidine-associated metabolism reflects a host–microbial state that sustains immune regulation during ICI therapy. Cortisol appears to act alongside these histidine-associated immunomodulatory pathways as a context-dependent regulatory signal. At ~10 weeks post-FMT, patients with RCC who did not develop grade ≥ 3 irAEs maintained higher circulating cortisol and higher levels of amino acids, including L-histidine and L-cysteine. By contrast, patients with grade ≥ 3 irAEs showed a progressive decline in these metabolites over time30. Retention of this combined metabolic profile was associated with preservation of regulatory immune compartments and restraint of pro-inflammatory effector responses, even under a higher-intensity dual ICI regimen30. Although elevated cortisol can be associated with unfavorable RCC prognosis59, its co-occurrence with sustained post-FMT histidine availability may reflect a metabolically resilient, immunomodulatory state that buffers ICI-driven immune activation60,61.

Consistent patterns linking preservation of histidine-associated metabolic profiles to reduced Teff activation and toxicity have been observed in advanced melanoma, suggesting that metabolic context modulates susceptibility to immune dysregulation across tumor types26. Therefore, FMT may contribute indirectly by reshaping the inflammatory and ecological context in which amino acid metabolism is maintained. However, direct causal links between donor-derived taxa and histidine-pathway metabolites remain to be established.

By contrast, sustained activation of the tryptophan–kynurenine pathway may mark inflammatory susceptibility and impaired antitumor immunity. In FMT-LUMINate, melanoma and NSCLC non-responders had higher kynurenine-pathway metabolites, including kynurenine and quinolinic acid. In contrast, responders showed lower levels over time, coinciding with reduced Treg frequencies and enhanced CD8⁺ T-cell availability31. Mechanistically, IDO/TDO-mediated tryptophan catabolism generates kynurenine metabolites that signal through AHR to reinforce immunoregulatory and exhaustion-associated programmes62–65.

FMT may shape immune reactivity by restoring metabolite-driven regulatory programs, even when durable engraftment of the donor taxa is incomplete. This reflects functional redundancy rather than species-independent metabolite persistence, as taxonomically distinct organisms can encode overlapping metabolic pathways, allowing community-level functions to be maintained despite taxonomic turnover66,67.

Conditioning and metabolic plasticity of the host ecosystem

Because these metabolic programs are sensitive to ecological perturbation, clinical interventions applied before FMT, including bowel preparation and antibiotic exposure, can shape donor function establishment and persistence. Across existing FMT–ICI trials, conditioning has most often relied on bowel preparation immediately prior to FMT, intended to facilitate delivery and partially reduce luminal biomass68,69. In PERFORM, MIMIC, and FMT-LUMINate trials, participants received a polyethylene glycol (PEG)–based bowel preparation before the oral FMT26,30,31. Similarly, TACITO trial recipients underwent PEG-based mechanical bowel cleansing prior to colonoscopic FMT45. In melanoma studies using lower gastrointestinal delivery, recipients underwent antibiotic preconditioning followed by bowel lavage and colonoscopic FMT, with subsequent oral capsule dosing27. Analyses integrating longitudinal microbiome data further indicate that antibiotic pretreatment and bowel lavage shape post-FMT microbial and immune trajectories68.

Mechanical bowel preparation prior to FMT may preserve key commensal functions and reduce the risk of overt dysbiosis. However, it may also permit persistence of pro-inflammatory microbial programs that could constrain donor functional reconstitution and metabolite transfer. Antibiotic-based conditioning may be useful when colonization resistance or refractory dysbiosis limits donor integration, but it can also impose marked host–microbiota perturbation, including altered microbial functional pathways and changes in circulating immune populations before ICI initiation70. In a randomized trial in metastatic melanoma evaluating antibiotic preconditioning followed by microbial consortium administration70, antibiotic exposure alone was associated with a functional shift in the gut ecosystem prior to ICI initiation. The altered landscape featured loss of response-associated SCFA biosynthesis pathways and enrichment of oxidative stress, xenobiotic metabolism, and inflammatory signaling-associated pathways. Importantly, along with reduced α-diversity, antibiotic conditioning induced a functional impairment that was not rescued by subsequent consortium administration, as both metabolic and immunologic features remained incompletely restored at the time anti-PD-1 therapy was initiated.

Antibiotic conditioning should therefore be applied cautiously in patients with high baseline inflammation or prior intensive ICI exposure, particularly under CTLA-4–containing regimens. In these settings, antibiotic-induced disruption may increase susceptibility to immune-related toxicity if immunoregulatory functions are not rapidly restored71,72. Accordingly, optimal conditioning strategies depend on therapeutic intent and on the recipient’s pre-FMT microbial and inflammatory state, as transient niche opening may help overcome resistance, whereas minimal conditioning may be preferable when FMT is used to restore immune tolerance, unless the pre-FMT microbiome itself contributes to inflammation or colonization resistance.

Defining how host- and microbiota-derived metabolites shape immune phenotypes will require integrated longitudinal analyses capturing both early perturbations and sustained metabolic–immunologic trajectories following FMT. Collectively, the discussed host-level constraints provide essential context for understanding why identical donor material can yield divergent immune and clinical outcomes across recipients.

Host physiological determinants of engraftment durability

Beyond pre-existing microbial ecology and metabolic profile, recipient physiology further shapes the durability, stability, and functional engraftment of donor-derived microbiota following FMT. Key host determinants include biological sex, age, physical activity status, diet-related niche availability, gut transit dynamics, and the spatial organization of intestinal microbial communities73.

Sex-dependent differences in gut microbial structure and immune tone have been linked in part to estrogen and androgen signaling pathways, a concept described as the “microgenderome”74,75. However, overall FMT response does not differ consistently by sex across disease contexts, although sex-specific differences in symptom improvement, immune activation, and microbial restructuring have been reported in some settings76. Aging is also associated with shifts in gut microbial composition, including reduced representation of Actinobacteria and Bifidobacteriaceae and altered microbial metabolic activity. Nevertheless, studies in rCDI indicate that short-term FMT efficacy is largely preserved across age groups77. The joint contribution of sex- and age-dependent effects to FMT outcomes remains underexplored, particularly with respect to engraftment durability and functional outcomes.

Physical activity and host metabolic state are well-known modifiers of commensal microbiota, particularly in individuals with obesity. Evidence from non-oncology FMT studies shows that regular physical activity alters gut microbial structure and diversity, promoting enrichment of taxa associated with improved metabolic and vascular health78,79. Within the broader physiological context, analyses from the MIMIC trial demonstrated that higher body mass index (BMI) was associated with greater donor strain engraftment efficiency following FMT30, identifying BMI as a candidate host-level marker of engraftment success.

Diet and gut physiology represent important determinants of donor microbiota colonization. Diet may influence FMT engraftment by shaping the metabolic niches available to donor microbes. Fermentable fibers, resistant starches, proteins, fats, and host-derived glycans support distinct microbial guilds and metabolite profiles, thereby determining whether donor-derived taxa encounter substrates that favor expansion, persistence, or functional activity80.

Equally important is the intestinal spatial ecology. Microbial density, oxygen tension, nutrient availability, mucus structure, and immune tone vary markedly along the gastrointestinal tract, with bacterial density increasing from the proximal small intestine to the colon81. This spatial constraint was shown experimentally by Li et al., who transplanted intestinal contents from different pig gut segments into germ-free mice and compared whole-intestinal microbiota transplantation with conventional FMT82. Microbiota derived from specific donor gut segments preferentially colonized the corresponding recipient niche, whereas conventional FMT primarily modulated the large-intestinal microbiota and transferred only a limited fraction of small-intestinal microbes. Small-intestinal communities were enriched for functions related to nucleotide metabolism, genetic information processing, replication and repair, and immune pathways, whereas large-intestinal communities were enriched for energy, carbohydrate, amino acid, cofactor, and vitamin metabolism82.

Transit time provides an additional layer of host impact on engraftment. Whole-gut transit time varies among healthy individuals, with a median of ~28 h, and is influenced by sex, age, diet, body size, and intestinal anatomy83. By altering donor–recipient contact time, luminal retention, and substrate exposure, transit may determine both the persistence of donor taxa and the metabolic outputs they generate. Incorporating host physiology into trial design may improve prediction of engraftment stability, guide donor–recipient matching, and reduce irAE risk, enabling safer and more effective use of FMT in oncology.

Donor-specific microbial signatures shared across cancer types

Although tumor types differ substantially in local tumor microenvironments and systemic immune states, clinical studies across melanoma, RCC, and NSCLC suggest that donor-derived microbial functional capacity can reproducibly shape engraftment trajectories and downstream immune and clinical phenotypes following FMT26–28,30,31.

Two independent phase I studies showed that FMT from patients with melanoma with prior durable clinical benefit on anti-PD-1 therapy was followed by clinical responses in a subset of anti-PD-1-refractory recipients27,28. Clinical benefit was accompanied by post-treatment enrichment or persistence of SCFA-producing Bacillota, Faecalibacterium, and Akkermansia muciniphila27,28. However, because these studies lacked control arms and donor origin was not established for all response-associated taxa, these findings should be interpreted as associative evidence of microbiome remodeling during FMT-ICI therapy rather than proof that specific donor-derived organisms mediated clinical benefit.

Subsequent trials using healthy-donor FMT extended these observations beyond responder-derived material and enabled donor-level assessment of FMT effects. PERFORM, MIMIC, and FMT-LUMINate used FMT products generated from the same pool of healthy donors, allowing donor-associated engraftment trajectories, immune remodeling, and clinical phenotypes to be compared across separate trials, individual recipients, and tumor types26,30,31.

Individual donors consistently aligned with distinct patterns of immune activation and regulation across recipients, supporting donor-dependent functional effects on host immunity. The same microbiota transplant elicited comparable immune effects across distinct patient cohorts and cancer types. Healthy-donor FMT in RCC and NSCLC was associated with increased microbial diversity and enrichment of SCFA-producing taxa. These microbial shifts coincided with immune profiles characterized by enhanced Teff responses and a lower incidence of high-grade irAEs26,30,31. In the TACITO trial, donor-derived strains contributed to post-FMT microbiota restructuring in metastatic RCC. Early detection of donor-associated taxa, including Bifidobacterium adolescentis, Ruminococcus bromii, Bifidobacterium longum, and Akkermansia massiliensis, was associated with treatment response and progression-free survival45.

Importantly, donor microbial features were also associated with immune-related toxicity. Across PERFORM and FMT-LUMINate trials, the same donors linked to higher toxicity shared overlapping microbiome characteristics, including enrichment of S. copri and reduced representation of pathways involved in SCFA biosynthesis30,31. Exposure to fecal transplants from these donors coincided with loss of immune homeostasis, manifested by increased activation of CD38⁺HLA-DR⁺ CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, enrichment of Th17-associated inflammatory responses, and contraction of Treg compartments in recipients26,30,31. However, donor-dependent immune remodeling was not uniformly pro-inflammatory. In RCC recipients who did not develop high-grade irAEs, FMT was accompanied by coordinated expansion of immunoregulatory populations, including activated Tregs and regulatory monocyte subsets, alongside controlled Teff activation30. Together, these immune features indicate that certain donor microbiota profiles can support antitumor immune activity while preserving regulatory mechanisms that limit tissue damage.

A unifying ecological–functional model across FMT–ICI trials

Across FMT–ICI studies, variation in therapeutic response, durability, and immune-related toxicity appears to reflect combined effects of early displacement of deleterious taxa in the recipient and subsequent stabilization of donor-derived functional engraftment. Although these principles are well established, their relevance to antitumor efficacy and irAEs during ICI therapy remains incompletely integrated across clinical FMT–ICI studies. Here, we propose a hypothetical two-stage ecological–functional model where these changes represent complementary stages of microbiota-driven immune modulation following FMT–ICI therapy.

The first process, which we term ecological clearance, is supported by observations from the FMT-LUMINate trial31, where clinical benefit from FMT was associated with the selective depletion of deleterious taxa present in the recipient prior to FMT. Consistent with this framework, the TACITO trial indicated that clinical benefit was not linked to the magnitude of global donor engraftment, but rather to early, strain-specific microbial turnover following FMT. Specifically, depletion of pre-FMT taxa within 1 week post-FMT correlated with improved progression-free survival at 12 months45. This initial phase can be conceptualized as niche opening, in which the reduction of recipient microbial populations creates ecological opportunity for selective donor-strain engraftment, downstream community restructuring, metabolic shifts, and immune modulation. This interpretation is consistent with non-oncology metagenomic FMT studies showing that donor-strain engraftment is shaped by multiple factors, including recipient pre-FMT microbiota composition49,68 and donor microbial diversity68. Donor colonization should therefore be viewed as context-dependent, shaped by both donor and recipient microbiota features.

Following this initial clearance phase, the second process, which we refer to as functional stabilization, is supported by longitudinal observations from PERFORM and MIMIC, where sustained donor-associated functional activity correlated with reduced toxicity and improved outcomes. In the PERFORM trial, patients who did not develop grade ≥ 3 irAEs progressively acquired and maintained donor-like functional activity and metabolite profiles linked to immune homeostasis. In contrast, patients who developed severe toxicity showed increasing functional shift from donor profiles over time, despite continued microbial exposure30.

Our interpretation is further supported by metagenomic analyses of post-FMT gut communities demonstrating a positive association between donor strain engraftment efficiency and clinical response in rCDI, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), diarrhea induced by tyrosine kinase inhibitor, multidrug-resistant bacteria colonization (MDRB), metabolic syndrome, melanoma, and Tourette syndrome49. Melanoma FMT trials by Baruch and Davar are consistent with this framework, as responders in both studies developed donor-associated functional programs alongside enhanced immune activity within the tumor microenvironment27,28. However, these studies did not explicitly distinguish pre-FMT microbiota loss from its durable functional stabilization as two independent processes that may influence therapeutic response. FMT studies in ICI-associated colitis further support the clinical relevance of sustained ecological and functional restructuring under ICI pressure, with prolonged benefit associated with persistent post-FMT microbial and functional changes43.

Together, these findings support a two-stage ecological–functional model of FMT–ICI activity. In the first stage, ecological clearance through depletion of deleterious pre-FMT taxa may facilitate response by reducing recipient-encoded resistance and inflammatory programs. In the second stage, longer-term outcomes depend on whether the restructured ecosystem achieves functional stabilization, reflected by sustained donor-associated pathway activity and metabolite production, including SCFA and bile acid programs that support regulatory immune tone. Failure to maintain these functions may disrupt immune and metabolic homeostasis and increase susceptibility to delayed irAEs. This framework remains conceptual and requires direct mechanistic validation, but it provides an integrated interpretation of current clinical observations. Effects of FMT dose and ICI intensity on clinical outcomes

Across trials and disease contexts, differences in FMT dosing strategy and delivery have not been independently associated with engraftment efficiency, clinical response, or immune-related toxicity. In the PERFORM trial, patients with RCC received repeated FMT administrations integrated into ongoing checkpoint blockade, providing sustained microbial exposure during immune activation30. In melanoma, Baruch et al. administered FMT via colonoscopy followed by additional oral dosing early during anti-PD-1 rechallenge27. By contrast, melanoma and NSCLC cohorts in the MIMIC and FMT-LUMINate studies, as well as cohorts from earlier melanoma trials by Baruch and Davar, received a single FMT administered prior to or early during immunotherapy26,28,31. Despite these differences in dosing and delivery strategy, durable clinical benefit was observed only in patients who retained donor-derived functional programs over time. The TACITO trial further supports this principle. Using a hybrid delivery approach combining colonoscopic FMT followed by repeated oral capsule administration at defined intervals, sustained clinical benefit was associated with persistence of donor-associated functional programs rather than with the specific route or frequency of FMT delivery45.

Checkpoint regimen intensity further shapes how retained microbial functions translate into clinical phenotypes. Microbial configurations that may remain clinically tolerated under PD-1 monotherapy can be amplified under CTLA-4-containing combination therapy, increasing T-cell activation and risk of high-grade irAEs. Consistent with this, pooled analyses across solid tumors show that combined anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 therapy is associated with higher objective response rates than anti-PD-1 monotherapy, but also higher grade 3–4 irAEs rates73. Thus, while early post-FMT restructuring may support initial responsiveness, durable efficacy and safety under intensified checkpoint blockade likely depend on persistence of donor-associated functions that preserve immune and metabolic homeostasis30,31. Together, these findings suggest that FMT dosing strategy and checkpoint regimen intensity modulate the two-stage ecological–functional process outlined in Fig. 1, shaping whether post-FMT microbial functions support durable response, immune tolerance, or delayed toxicity.

Whole-community FMT vs defined microbial consortia

Durable efficacy and delayed toxicity have direct implications for the design of microbiome-based therapeutics. Whole-community FMT introduces complex ecosystems capable of long-term functional persistence, with both therapeutic benefit and inherent risk in immunologically primed hosts. These features have prompted interest in synthetic microbiome-based therapeutics composed of defined, purified microbial strains73,84. Such approaches offer theoretical advantages in reproducibility, safety profiling, and regulatory standardization. However, clinical translation remains limited because it is still unclear which microbial strains, functions or interactions are required to achieve durable efficacy.

Many commensal microbes exist within interdependent networks characterized by metabolic cross-feeding, competitive niche occupation, and context-dependent functional redundancy. For example, SCFA production by F. prausnitzii, Eubacterium rectale, and Roseburia spp. can be enhanced by bifidobacteria-derived acetate and lactate85,86. Conversely, disruption of SCFA-producing Clostridial clusters IV and XIVa has been linked to reduced colonization resistance and expansion of opportunistic taxa such as Enterococcus, reflecting impaired ecological stability87. Dietary niche availability may be especially relevant to Prevotella-dominant ecologies, as plant-derived polysaccharides favor Prevotella expansion, whereas low-complexity carbohydrate diets or inflammatory oxygen/nitrate gradients can shift communities toward alternative Bacteroidales and dysbiosis-associated taxa88,89.

Most oncology FMT studies have focused exclusively on bacterial communities, leaving other components of the gut ecosystem underexplored. The human gut also harbors diverse viral and fungal communities, which can interact with bacterial commensals and host immunity90. For instance, intestinal Candida overgrowth has been linked to increased lactate availability and reduced SCFA production, conditions that favor fungal persistence, destabilizing immune homeostasis91. The extent to which virome and mycobiome contribute to durable efficacy or delayed toxicity following FMT–ICI therapy remains largely unknown.

Together, these observations suggest that simplified microbial consortia may not fully reproduce the ecological breadth required for stable function in vivo. Whole-community FMT may retain advantages by providing functional diversity and redundancy; therefore, direct comparisons with defined consortia under ICI pressure are needed to determine whether rationally designed communities can safely recapitulate their immunomodulatory effects.

Microbiota-mediated regulation of irAEs under ICI therapy

FMT modifies irAEs risk and antitumor efficacy by reshaping microbiota-derived functional pathways that intersect with checkpoint-driven immune activation. Under intensified checkpoint pressure, immune regulatory control can be preserved or disrupted, shifting the balance between immune efficacy and tissue injury. Here, we discuss how microbiota-linked functional states interact with checkpoint regimen intensity, focusing first on CTLA-4–associated toxicities, then on ecosystem-level gain of regulatory functions and loss of immunopathogenic programs following FMT, and finally on how these trajectories diverge across melanoma, RCC, and NSCLC.

Microbiota–ICI interactions shaping immune-related toxicity

Clinical data consistently show that ICI toxicity is strongly regimen-dependent, with CTLA-4 blockade and combination therapy producing higher rates of severe irAEs than PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy92,93. In parallel, pharmacovigilance analyses of FAERS reports indicate that adverse events reported for dual ICI regimens disproportionately reflect immune-mediated toxicities across endocrine, hepatic, gastrointestinal, and cardiac categories94.

The biological role of CTLA-4 in maintaining immune homeostasis provides a mechanistic context for this clinical pattern. In individuals with germline CTLA4 deficiency, loss of immune homeostasis leads to severe immune dysregulation, most prominently affecting the intestine95. Consistent with this, immune-related colitis (irColitis) is a frequent and clinically significant complication of CTLA-4–containing ICI regimens. Rather than reflecting global loss of microbial diversity, irColitis is marked by dysbiosis characterized by Proteobacteria enrichment that correlates with disease severity. In patients with steroid- and biologic-refractory irColitis, administration of healthy-donor FMT led to rapid clinical improvement in most cases, supporting microbiota restructuring as an effective strategy to alleviate severe CTLA-4–associated intestinal toxicity44.

Importantly, microbiota-mediated restoration of regulatory control does not imply reversal of CTLA-4 blockade at the level of antitumor T-cell immunity. Rather, FMT appears to reconstitute microbiota-dependent regulatory circuits that operate in parallel to checkpoint inhibition, particularly within the intestinal mucosa. Preclinical data indicate that microbial recolonization can induce CTLA-4 expression on innate lymphoid cells and other non-T-cell compartments, contributing to local immune restraint without compromising systemic antitumor T-cell activity96. Accordingly, alterations in microbial communities can directly influence toxicity risk under CTLA-4 blockade96. In parallel, experimental and clinical studies show that combined PD-1 and CTLA-4 blockade enhances expansion of activated CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells while reducing Treg compartments within tumors and peripheral tissues—an effect not observed with single-agent ICI therapy97. As a result, dual ICI therapy amplifies effector immune activation while weakening regulatory restraint, increasing reliance on microbiota-dependent mechanisms to preserve tissue tolerance.

Importantly, elevated toxicity is not a universal feature of combination immunotherapy. In the placebo-controlled TACITO trial, rates of grade ≥ 3 irAEs, treatment interruption, dose reduction, and treatment discontinuation were comparable between donor-FMT and placebo arms in patients receiving pembrolizumab plus axitinib45. These findings indicate that microbiota-dependent modulation of immune tolerance becomes most critical when regulatory checkpoints such as CTLA-4 are pharmacologically disrupted, whereas PD-1–based combination regimens may remain clinically manageable when microbiota-derived regulatory functions are preserved.

Gain of protective and loss of harmful microbial functions

Gut microbial communities comprise diverse microbial populations whose combined activities shape nutrient availability, metabolite production, colonization resistance, and host immune tone. In the context of FMT, therapeutic benefit is unlikely to depend on the introduction of individual taxa alone, but rather on how donor- and recipient- derived microbes reshape community functions that affect host immunity. Evidence from FMT administered for ICI-induced colitis refractory to immunosuppression illustrates this principle. In patients achieving complete clinical resolution of colitis, post-FMT stool profiles showed increased microbial α-diversity and enrichment of taxa linked to regulatory metabolism, including Collinsella, Bifidobacterium, and Coprococcus, alongside depletion of Tyzzerella43.

Analyses integrating longitudinal metagenomics across patients from eight different diseases indicate that successful engraftment after FMT preferentially involves organisms with broad ecological flexibility, including the capacity to occupy diverse niches, utilize shared substrates, and tolerate fluctuating metabolic conditions. Bacteroidales illustrate this principle, as many members can utilize complex carbohydrates, mucin glycans, and host-derived polysaccharides, supporting competition for luminal and mucus-associated substrates, acetate and propionate production, and cross-feeding with butyrate-producing Bacillota98,99. Consistent with this, several Bacteroidales members, including S. copri clade A51, Bacteroides finegoldii, Bacteroides stercoris, Alistipes putredinis, Alistipes finegoldii, and Phocaeicola massiliensis, consistently demonstrate high post-FMT persistence. Among Actinobacteria, both dysbiosis-associated taxa, such as Eggerthella lenta, and immunoregulatory species, including Bifidobacterium bifidum and B. longum, similarly demonstrated robust engraftment capacity49.

Across melanoma, RCC, and NSCLC, FMT induced reproducible, yet distinct microbial shifts aligned with therapeutic response and immune-related toxicity. In responders, FMT was consistently associated with enrichment and persistence of SCFA-producing Clostridiales and related Bacillota, whereas non-responders showed retention or expansion of inflammatory or competitively dominant taxa, despite partial engraftment of donor material. This responder–non-responder divergence establishes gain of regulatory functions and loss of inflammatory dominance as a central axis of FMT efficacy.

In melanoma responders, post-FMT enrichment was dominated by F. prausnitzii, Anaerostipes, Eubacterium, and Roseburia species26,27,31, accompanied by depletion of pro-inflammatory Enterocloster, Dialister, and Streptococcus taxa. Independent melanoma cohorts similarly demonstrate enrichment of butyrate- and mucus-associated taxa, including Butyricicoccus pullicaecorum, F. prausnitzii, A. muciniphila, Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum, and Roseburia species in responders to ICI100. RCC and NSCLC responders show parallel enrichment of SCFA-linked taxa, reinforcing that functional convergence, rather than taxonomic identity, underlies response26,30,31.

By contrast, non-responders across tumor types follow a distinct ecological trajectory, characterized by persistence of inflammatory or competitively dominant lineages, including Enterocloster, Streptococcus, Dialister, and selected Clostridium/Ruminococcus lineages in different cohorts25,31. These taxa have been linked to Th17-skewed immune states and impaired regulatory phenotypes. Notably, non-responders lack a consistent pattern of pre-FMT taxa depletion, indicating incomplete functional turnover despite partial donor engraftment31.

Additional evidence from the TACITO trial indicates that clinical outcomes are associated with early, selective acquisition or loss of specific microbial lineages rather than broad taxonomic shifts. Early post-FMT enrichment of Blautia wexlerae was associated with improved 12-month progression-free survival, whereas enrichment of Oscillospiraceae members and A. massiliensis, as well as loss of Ruminococcus bromii, correlated with poorer outcomes. Conversely, early depletion of Escherichia coli and a Clostridium species was associated with improved survival45.

Within responders across the clinical trials, further stratification by immune-related toxicity revealed a second axis of microbial divergence. In the PERFORM cohort, responders who developed grade ≥ 3 irAEs showed marked post-FMT enrichment of S. copri, Prevotella sp. Marseille-P4119 and Parabacteroides massiliensis, alongside depletion or failure to sustain SCFA-producing communities. This pattern was reproducible across recipients exposed to the same donor material, implicating donor-associated functional dominance30. In contrast, responders without severe toxicity consistently enriched a shared regulatory microbial core overlapping with taxa associated with efficacy across MIMIC and FMT-LUMINate26,30,31.

Independent anti-PD-1 lung cancer cohorts and non-oncology FMT trials further support the context-dependent behavior of S. copri and related Prevotella-dominant ecologies. In lung cancer cohorts, S. copri enrichment was observed alongside SCFA-producing communities and was associated with progression-free survival101,102, whereas Prevotella-to-Bacteroides ratios can drive recipient community restructuring and broad metabolic shifts after FMT in non-oncology settings103,104. Thus, S. copri should be interpreted as a context-dependent ecological marker rather than an intrinsically harmful or protective taxon.

Collectively, these findings suggest that FMT-mediated efficacy and toxicity depend on the balance between the acquisition of immunoregulatory functions and the suppression of pro-inflammatory microbial traits. Failure to establish protective functions, or persistence of inflammatory dominance, may increase the risk of treatment failure or irAEs, particularly during combination checkpoint blockade.

Segatella copri as a context-dependent amplifier of CTLA-4-associated toxicity

The PERFORM trial provides the first clear example that microbiota-related immunotoxicity is shaped by immune-checkpoint context, particularly under CTLA-4–containing regimens. This study demonstrated that exposure to donor microbiota enriched in S. copri was reproducibly associated with a markedly increased risk of irAEs ≥ 3 among patients treated with combined ipilimumab plus nivolumab30. These donor profiles shared key features with microbial signatures linked to irAEs, supporting the conclusion that donor microbiota can transfer functional properties associated with immune-related toxicity across tumor types26,30,31. However, this association was not universal. Notably, one self-enriched S. copri patient did not experience grade ≥ 3 irAEs while receiving pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib, underscoring the importance of the recipient's pre-FMT microbiota and, critically, the immune activation regime under which a given microbiota state is expressed30. The link between S. copri enrichment and unfavorable clinical outcomes was already indicated in earlier melanoma FMT studies conducted under PD-1 monotherapy. Across two independent melanoma ICI-FMT studies27,28, non-responders consistently showed higher post-FMT abundance of S. copri than responders, supporting its role as a marker of a microbiota configuration associated with impaired therapeutic benefit even without CTLA-4 blockade. However, this association was not observed in MIMIC melanoma cohorts receiving PD-1 monotherapy30,31. We hypothesize that S. copri-dominant ecologies do not intrinsically cause toxicity and/or resistance to ICI treatment but lower the threshold for immune dysregulation in a context-dependent manner. This context dependence may explain why similar donor material may be clinically tolerated under PD-1 monotherapy yet become pathogenic under CTLA-4 blockade. Ecologically, S. copri may constrain SCFA-producing species and promote metabolic inflammation, potentially impairing Tregs maintenance under checkpoint pressure105.

High-dimensional immunophenotyping in the PERFORM trial identified a coherent immune signature linking S. copri enrichment to severe immune-related toxicity. By week 10 post-FMT, patients who developed grade ≥ 3 irAEs exhibited pronounced expansion of activated CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ memory T-cell subsets, alongside depletion of innate regulation marked by reduced circulating CD56⁺⁺CD16⁻ NK cells and reduced DNAM1 expression. This immune phenotype closely mirrored the one observed in S. copri-high patients following FMT. In contrast, patients who did not develop high-grade toxicity showed sustained longitudinal expansion of CD38⁺ Tregs cells, whereas this expansion was transient and lost in patients with toxicity30. Therefore, failure to sustain regulatory pathways under checkpoint pressure provides a functional link between S. copri enrichment and the emergence of severe toxicity.

Mechanistically, S. copri can prime innate inflammation through IL-1β/inflammasome activation, SUCNR1–IL-6–STAT3 signaling, outer-membrane vesicle-mediated macrophage polarization, and low-grade TLR4 signaling, providing a plausible link between S. copri enrichment and the myeloid/T-cell inflammatory phenotype observed in PERFORM30,106,107.

Together, these ecological, metabolic, and innate immune-priming features support a checkpoint-intensity-dependent model in which S. copri marks a risk ecology that may become clinically expressed as colitis under CTLA-4-containing regimens, even when robust FMT engraftment is not observed30.

Protective microbiota programs and restoration of regulatory buffering under CTLA-4 blockade

In contrast to S. copri, several taxa consistently associate with protection from CTLA-4–associated toxicity, most notably F. prausnitzii. Across PERFORM, MIMIC, and FMT-LUMINate trials, enrichment of F. prausnitzii was observed in responders to ICI and patients who did not develop high-grade irAEs26,30,31,108. Studies further demonstrate that F. prausnitzii administration ameliorates ICI-induced colitis, increases gut microbial α-diversity, suppresses expansion of opportunistic pathogens, and simultaneously enhances antitumor immunity, even under combined PD-1 and CTLA-4 blockade109. Together, these observations position F. prausnitzii as a functional counterbalance to S. copri, reinforcing regulatory immune control.

F. prausnitzii supports immune regulation through SCFA production, particularly butyrate, which reinforces epithelial barrier integrity, limits microbial translocation, and promotes peripheral Treg differentiation and stability through epigenetic regulation of FOXP3 and restraint of excessive Teff activation110,111.

Beyond SCFA production, F. prausnitzii metabolites suppress NF-κB activation, reduce IL-6 and IL-1β production, and limit Th17 polarization, supporting a mucosal environment resistant to excessive inflammatory activation112,113. Together, these metabolic and immunoregulatory properties support a role for F. prausnitzii enrichment in epithelial integrity, immune tolerance, and regulatory buffering under CTLA-4-containing regimens114.

Protective effects are unlikely to be restricted to F. prausnitzii. Broader responder-associated taxa, including B. breve, B. longum, and A. muciniphila, may contribute complementary functions involving SCFA production, bile acid transformation, and mucus-associated metabolism115–117. Protection from CTLA-4–associated toxicity may therefore emerge from microbiota-supported regulatory buffering. Clinical implementation of FMT in oncology

Building on the ecological and mechanistic principles outlined above, clinical translation of FMT in oncology requires a shift from descriptive microbiota features toward function-guided, regimen-aware decision-making. Across melanoma, RCC, and NSCLC, available evidence indicates that FMT can expand the therapeutic window of ICIs, but only when durable immunoregulatory microbial functions are established. Outside this context, FMT may confer limited benefit or increase immune-related toxicity, particularly under regimens that strongly amplify immune activation. Effective clinical implementation, therefore, requires structured consideration of donor functional capacity, recipient ecological context, checkpoint intensity, and the stability of microbiota-derived functions.

Functional donor profiling beyond pathogen screening

Current clinical use of FMT relies primarily on pathogen exclusion to ensure microbiological safety118. While necessary, this approach may be insufficient in oncology, where immunologic harm can be donor-derived even in the absence of transmissible pathogens. Evidence from PERFORM, MIMIC, and FMT-LUMINate demonstrates that donor microbiota carries distinct immune-modulatory capacities that influence both efficacy and toxicity of ICIs30,31. Accordingly, future studies should evaluate pro-inflammatory functional features, including enrichment of LPS biosynthesis, flagellin production, or other pattern-recognition receptor ligands, as potential risk factors for non-response or irAEs, particularly in recipients with pre-existing dysbiosis or those receiving higher-intensity ICI regimens.

Function-guided donor selection is increasingly feasible using metagenomic pathway profiling, targeted stool metabolomics, and selected qPCR assays for high-risk taxa or pathways119,120. However, functional donor profiling remains investigational and should not currently be used as a stand-alone basis for donor selection or exclusion. Larger trials with sufficient numbers of individual donors will be required to determine whether specific functional profiles reproducibly associate with response, immune-related toxicity, or both.

Implementation of FMT in oncology

As FMT moves toward broader clinical application in oncology, safe and effective implementation will require longitudinal, mechanism-guided monitoring. Evidence from PERFORM, MIMIC, and FMT-LUMINate trials indicates that therapeutic benefit and immune-related toxicity emerge over time as microbiota-derived functional programs stabilize under checkpoint pressure30,31. Clinical decision-making should therefore integrate serial assessment of donor-strain persistence, microbial functional, metabolite profiles, and host immune state. TACITO trial provides a practical example, using serial stool sampling from pre-FMT through one year after FMT, with early microbiota changes aligning with long-term progression-free survival45.

Monitoring should move beyond microbial richness alone to capture persistence of donor-derived strains, inflammatory lineage expansion, and maintenance of SCFA- and bile acid-associated functional programs. Because diet shapes substrate availability for donor-derived microbes, major dietary patterns or fiber intake should also be considered where feasible. Functional readouts may include metagenomic pathway profiling, focused SCFA and bile acid measurements, stool or plasma metabolomics, and targeted qPCR panels for selected taxa or pathway markers. These metabolic states should be interpreted in the context of checkpoint regimen, as inflammatory configurations may remain clinically silent under PD-1 monotherapy but become pathogenic under CTLA-4-containing regimens.

Longitudinal immune phenotyping provides a complementary risk layer. Expansion of activated T-cell populations, contraction of Treg compartments, and Th17 enrichment may indicate loss of immune homeostasis, particularly when accompanied by pro-inflammatory microbial or metabolic shifts. Because delayed colitis, hepatitis, and multisystem irAEs can occur months into ICI therapy or after treatment discontinuation121–123, monitoring should extend beyond the early post-FMT window. Together, inflammatory dominance across microbial, metabolic, and immune layers should prompt reassessment of therapeutic intensity, closer surveillance, or redirection toward donors with more immunoregulatory functional profiles.

Knowledge gaps and future directions

Despite intense research activity, several critical gaps limit clinical translation. First, there are no validated functional thresholds to define donor suitability or recipient risk. Second, current trials lack standardized functional screening pipelines integrating metagenomics, metabolomics, and immune profiling, limiting cross-study comparability. Third, the reversibility of harmful engraftment and inflammatory microbiota functions remains poorly understood. Fourth, optimal timing, dosing frequency, and durability requirements for FMT remain undefined across tumor types. Fifth, published data do not yet establish whether post-FMT gut microbial signatures drive extra-intestinal irAEs such as pneumonitis, hepatitis, endocrinopathies, or dermatitis. These toxicities likely depend on tissue-specific antigenic context, resident immune populations, epithelial or stromal injury, and local microbiomes where present. Finally, interactions between microbiota modulation and emerging immunotherapies, including bispecific antibodies and cellular therapies, are largely unexplored. Addressing these gaps in future studies will be essential to move FMT from experimental use toward safe implementation in oncology.
