The role of hope traits and psychological distressin the effect of childhood abuse on suicidal ideation in Chinese college students
Authors: Wenwen Wei, Shuyi Liang, Lixia Zhang, Zhitao Yuan, Jiang Nan, Shengjun Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo explore the mediating effects of hope traits and psychological distress in the relationship between childhood abuse and suicidal ideation in college students.MethodsA total of 1908 college students were surveyed using the Childhood Abuse Questionnaire-Short Form (CTQ-SF), the Adult Dispositional Hope Scale, the SCL-90, and the Suicidal Ideation Self-Rating Scale. The data were screened, SPSS and R software were used to calculate descriptive statistics, and a structural equation model was constructed.ResultsChildhood abuse was significantly associated with higher levels of suicidal ideation. Childhood abuse indirectly affected suicidal ideation through hope traits and psychological distress, both individually and via chain mediation, with all indirect effects remaining significant after controlling for direct effects.ConclusionChildhood abuse can directly positively impact college students’ suicidal ideation, and also indirectly affect it through the mediating effects of hope traits and psychological distress either individually or as a chain.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08340-2.

Introduction

Suicide is a significant global public health issue. Although the overall suicide rate in China has declined substantially in recent years, the suicide rate among adolescents remains high, posing an urgent problem of public health [1, 2]. The direct precursor to suicidal behavior is suicidal ideation, which consists of thinking about, considering, or planning suicide [3]. The incidence of suicidal ideation is high among adolescents [4], and a range of studies have shown that suicidal ideation is significantly related to subsequent suicidal acts or self-harm behaviors [5–7]. Therefore, as suicidal ideation is the most sensitive predictor of suicidal behavior and an inevitable stage in the psychological process leading to such behavior, it is essential to investigate the factors contributing to the emergence of suicidal ideation and the underlying psychological mechanisms involved.

Childhood trauma and suicidal ideation

Of the factors that contribute to the development of suicidal ideation in adolescents, childhood abuse is considered to be one of the factors that present the highest risk [8, 9]. Interpersonal theories of suicide suggest that adverse experiences, including childhood abuse, can form a state of adjustment to distress, reduce the victim’s fear of self-harm, and ultimately lead to suicidal behavior [10]. Childhood abuse can be divided into five aspects: physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, physical neglect, and emotional neglect [11]. Childhood abuse is a global problem, and its negative effects accompany individuals throughout their lives [12]. Studies to date have shown that individuals who have experienced childhood abuse are more likely to develop unhealthy psychological problems and behaviors such as anxiety and depression [13], Internet addiction [14], and aggressive behavior [15], and, in particular, increased suicidal ideation [10, 12–16], and these adverse effects are persistent. Based on this, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H1:

Experience of childhood abuse significantly is positively associated with suicidal ideation.

The mediating effect of hope traits

Not all individuals who have experienced childhood abuse actually develop suicidal ideation. While the buffer hypothesis theory of suicide emphasizes the moderating role of protective factors [17], it is equally crucial to explore the mechanisms through which risk factors exert their influence. This study proposes that hope traits act as a critical mediator in the pathway from childhood abuse to suicidal ideation. Snyder’s theory of hope defines “hope traits” as a personality trait that is stable across situations and times and is an acquired tendency of thinking and behavior that stems from an intrinsic sense of accomplishment [18]. However, a growing body of evidence suggests that even relatively stable personality traits can exhibit considerable plasticity when confronted with major life events [19]. Childhood abuse, as a significant adverse life event, holds the potential to shape cognitive traits, including hope traits. Specifically, a chronic stressful environment characterized by abuse and neglect may erode the two core components of hope traits: agency and pathways [18]，persistent physical and psychological abuse may lead children to acquire a sense of “learned helplessness,” believing their efforts are futile (thereby undermining agency), whereas the unpredictability of caregivers and emotional deprivation may restrict their cognitive capacity to plan for the future and seek solutions (thereby obstructing pathways thinking) [20, 21]. Consequently, childhood abuse is not only a risk factor but may itself serve as a direct antecedent to the reduction in hope traits.

Based on this reasoning, the present study aims to move beyond examining the protective factor’s moderating effect and instead delve into the internal psychological mechanism through which childhood abuse influences suicidal ideation. This perspective will help elucidate the critical process of “how risk factors undermine protective resources, thereby providing a more precise theoretical foundation for the design of targeted clinical interventions (e.g., enhancing hope to disrupt the pathway from early trauma to psychological crisis). This study thus proposes the following hypothesis:

H2:

Childhood abuse is significantly associated with suicidal ideation through the mediating effect of hope traits.

The mediating effect of psychological distress

Individuals who experience childhood abuse tend to also experience a range of negative emotions such as fear, anger, and feelings of helplessness. These negative emotions not only affect the individual’s daily life but can also lead to a range of mental health problems. Specifically, these can be divided into internalizing problems (i.e., anxiety and depression), and externalizing problems (i.e., withdrawal and aggressive behavior). This view is supported by the study of Yu and Li [22], who observed a significant association between the experience of childhood abuse and the subsequent onset of internalizing and externalizing mental health problems [22]. These mental health problems not only affect the individual’s ability to socialize, learn, and work, but also continue to deepen their suicidal ideation. Research has also shown that there is a complex interplay between mental health problems and suicidal ideation. Specifically, internalizing issues such as depression and anxiety can cause individuals to feel hopeless and helpless, increasing their risk of suicide, while externalizing problems such as aggressive and withdrawn behaviors can lead to exclusion in social settings, further exacerbating feelings of loneliness and suicidal ideation [22]. Therefore, mental health problems are not only a direct consequence of childhood abuse but also an important predictor of suicidal ideation. Based on these findings, this study proposes hypothesis 3:

H3:

Childhood abuse indirectly affects suicidal ideation through the mediating role of overall psychological distress.

The chain mediating effects of hope traits and psychological distress

This study posits a specific chain mediation model wherein childhood abuse is associated with suicidal ideation through the hope traits and subsequent psychological distress. Theoretically, hope traits-a future-oriented cognitive-motivational resource-are hypothesized to be a primary casualty of traumatic experiences. Childhood abuse is proposed to first impair the development of hope, depleting this fundamental positive psychological resource. This depletion, in turn, heightens vulnerability to psychological distress like depression and anxiety, ultimately elevating the risk of suicidal ideation. Early cross-sectional findings align with this perspective, indicating that hope traits serve as a negative predictor of depression and a positive predictor of psychological well-being, suggesting its role as a protective factor rather than a mere consequence of distress [23, 24].

Crucially, this theoretical model is strongly supported by a growing body of longitudinal research. Most notably, Ye’s research directly demonstrated that distinct patterns of childhood maltreatment influenced subsequent suicidal ideation through a chain mediation of hope and depression, providing robust empirical evidence for the proposed sequence [25]. Furthermore, the pivotal role of hope as a key mediator buffering the path to suicidal risk has been corroborated by another recent research [26]. Therefore, we posit a specific chain of mediation: childhood abuse first impairs the development of hope traits; this depletion of a key positive psychological resource, in turn, increases vulnerability to psychological distress, ultimately elevating the risk of suicidal ideation. This study thus proposes hypothesis 4:

H4:

Childhood abuse affects suicidal ideation through the chain mediation of hope traits and psychological distress.

A robust body of literature established in Western contexts has demonstrated a clear link between childhood abuse and suicidal ideation. However, the generalization of this finding across cultures remains questionable. In collectivist societies, cultural imperatives such as filial piety and the avoidance of family shame may suppress the disclosure of abuse and alter its psychological consequences, potentially leading to patterns that contradict Western models. Meanwhile, methodologically, existing studies have predominantly used simple regression analyses, overlooking the complex mediating pathways and the cumulative effects of multiple abuse types [27]. To address these theoretical and methodological gaps, this study uses structural equation modeling on data from a sample of college students to comprehensively analyze how childhood abuse associates with suicidal ideation through the mediating role of hope. This approach allows us to investigate whether the effects of specific abuse types manifest differently in this cultural context and whether hope serves as a protective buffer, thereby generating novel insights for prevention strategies. Methods

Participants and procedures

Our study collected data from university students in Haikou city using the convenient sampling method to distribute online questionnaires. We distributed the questionnaires through WeChat as it has the broadest user base in China and is utilized by nearly all university students. Inclusion criteria included 1. currently enrolled university students; 2. consent to volunteer for this study. A total of 2092 questionnaires were collected, of which 1908 (91.20%) were valid. Of the participants, 767 were boys and 1141 were girls. The average age of the participants was 20.430 ± 1.893.

Ethics considerations

In the first part of the questionnaire, we informed each participant of the purpose of the study and also provided one consent form with the ethical guidelines addressing the need. If participants agree to take part, they should click “I agree” to continue with the following project. Participants were also informed that the interests of the research subjects would be protected at all times. During the data collection process, all participants, upon questionnaire completion, were automatically directed to a webpage detailing support resources. This included the contact information for the University’s Psychological Counseling Center and the National Suicide Prevention Hotline. Furthermore, participants identified as high-risk based on their SIOSS scores (≥12) received a follow-up email within 24 hours from a qualified member of our research team, reiterating these resources and strongly encouraging them to seek professional support.

Measures

Childhood trauma questionnaire-Short form

This study used the Chinese version of the Childhood Abuse Questionnaire (CTQ-SF), as revised by [28]. It comprises a total of 28 items, categorized into five subscales: emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect. Each item is scored on a five-point scale from 1 = never to 5 = always, with higher scores indicating greater levels of abuse. The load of each factor was greater than 0.5, the average variance extracted (AVE) was greater than 0.45, and the comprehensive reliability (CR) was more than 0.7, indicating that the scale had good convergence validity. The internal consistency reliability and total reliability of each of the five subscales were 0.707, 0.806, 0.804, 0.893, 0.512, and 0.852, respectively. The results of confirmatory factor analysis were as follows: χ2/df = 2.780, RMSEA = 0.031, AGFI = 0.963, TLI = 0.972, CFI = 0.982, GFI = 0.978. These results indicate that the scale had good reliability and validity in this study.

The suicidal ideation self-Rating scale

This study used the Suicidal Ideation Self-Rating Scale (SIOSS) developed by [29], which was designed to screen for suicidal ideation. The scale comprises 26 items, divided into four dimensions of optimism, sleep, despair, and disguise. Masking dimension is used as a polygraph indicator and is not counted in the overall score; if the masking dimension score exceeds 4 points, the questionnaire result is considered invalid. The scale uses a two-point scale of 1 = yes and 0 = no, with higher scores indicating stronger suicidal ideation. A total score of 12 points or more indicates a preliminary determination that the individual has suicidal ideation. The load of each factor was greater than 0.5, the average variance extractedwas greater than 0.5, and the comprehensive reliability was more than 0.7, indicating that the scale had good convergence validity. Except for the masking subscales, the internal consistency reliability and total reliability of each subscale were 0.518, 0.837, 0.609, and 0.857, respectively. Confirmatory factor analysis showed that χ2/df = 2.992, RMSEA = 0.032, AGFI = 0.966, TLI = 0.960, CFI = 0.981, GFI = 0.985. These results indicate that this scale had good reliability and validity.

Adult Dispositional Hope Scale

The Chinese version of the Adult Dispositional Hope Scale, developed by Synder et al. [30] and translated by Chen Canrui et al. [31], was used to evaluate the hope traits of the participants. The scale comprises 12 items, all of which are scored on a 4-point scale. Of these, four items (1, 4, 6, 8) measure path thinking, reflecting an individual’s ability to develop feasible pathways toward achieving a goal; four items (2, 9, 10, 12) measure agency thinking, reflecting an individual’s motivation to believe in their capacity to sustain progress along these pathways, and the remaining four items (3, 5, 7, 11) are used to divert the participants’ attention and are not included in the total score. The higher the total score, the higher the participant’s level of hope. The internal consistency reliability and total reliability of each subscale were 0.778, 0.781, and 0.859, respectively, and the results of confirmatory factor analysis were χ2/df = 2.202, RMSEA = 0.025, AGFI = 0.975, TLI = 0.986, CFI = 0.991, GFI = 0.985. These results indicate that the scale had good reliability and validity.

The Symptom Checklist-90

The Chinese Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90), developed by L.R. Derogatis in 1975 [32] and translated and revised by Dr. Wang [33], was used to assess psychological distress. The scale comprises 90 items, covering feeling, thinking, emotion, behavior, interpersonal relationships and living habits, etc. All items are scored on a 5-point scale in which 1 = not at all, 2 = mild, 3 = moderate, 4 = relatively severe, and 5 = severe. A total score of more than 160, or a score of more than 2 on any of the standardized subscales, indicates some degree of symptoms or psychological distress for that particular factor. The use of the SCL-90 total score is justified as a unidimensional measure of general distress based on psychometric evidence. Specifically, a bifactor model has confirmed the presence of a strong general factor, supporting the calculation and interpretation of a total score [34]. This practice is also consistent with its clinical application as a global severity index [35]. The internal consistency coefficients of the subscales of the 10 dimensions of the scale were all greater than 0.750 (0.885, 0.862, 0.870, 0.915, 0.872, 0.807, 0.770, 0.795, 0.839, and 0.769), and the internal consistency coefficient of the total scale was 0.979.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were performed for score indicators such as gender, childhood abuse, suicidal ideation, hope traits, and psychological distress. Means and standard deviations are reported for continuous variables, while numbers and percentages (%) are reported for categorical variables. The independent-samples t-test was used for continuous variables and the χ2 test was used for categorical variables. To explore the relationships between continuous variables, we conducted Pearson correlation analyses. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was performed using R software (version 4.2.3) with the lavaan package. The model incorporated childhood abuse, hope traits, Psychological distress, and suicidal ideation. Since the SIOSS items are binary (0/1), the data violated the assumption of multivariate normality; therefore, robust maximum likelihood estimation (MLR) was used to obtain robust standard errors and test statistics. Model fit was evaluated using the robust comparative fit index (CFI), robust Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), robust root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). Acceptable model fit was indicated by CFI/TLI ≥ 0.90, RMSEA ≤ 0.08, and SRMR ≤ 0.08. Path significance was determined based on robust standard errors (p < 0.05). Indirect effects were estimated using the product of coefficients method, and their significance was assessed via the delta method, as implemented in lavaan.

Result

Descriptive statistics

A total of 1,908 college students (40.20% male, 59.80% female) with a mean age of 20.43 years (SD = 1.89) were included in this study. Consistent with previous research in the Chinese context, emotional and physical neglect were the most commonly reported forms of childhood maltreatment [1]. To preliminarily assess the associations between childhood maltreatment and the key outcome variables, we conducted a series of group comparisons. The results consistently revealed that experiences of any form of childhood abuse (emotional, physical, sexual) or neglect (emotional, physical) were significantly associated with a higher likelihood of suicidal ideation and psychological distress, as well as significantly lower levels of hope traits (all p < 0.001). Detailed results of the chi-square tests (comparing rates of suicidal ideation and SCL-90 based on the presence/absence of each abuse type) and independent samples t-tests (comparing mean scores on suicidal ideation, SCL-90, and hope traits) are provided in Supplementary Tables S1, S2 and S3, respectively. These robust preliminary findings confirm the fundamental links between the core variables and justify the subsequent integrated model testing. This provides a foundational background for the subsequent in-depth analyses, such as correlation analysis and structural equation modeling.

Common method bias test

Initially, to address the potential for common method bias, which is inherent in studies that rely solely on self-report data, we employed Harman’s single-factor test at the outset of our analysis. This test is designed to identify whether a single factor accounts for the majority of the variance in the data, which could indicate a bias towards self-reporting. By implementing this test, we aimed to minimize the influence of common method variance on our findings.

The Harman univariate test was used to test for common method bias; the results showed that there were 24 common factors with eigenvalues greater than 1. The variation explained by the first factor was 27.050%, which was lower than the 40% threshold proposed by Howard et al. [36], indicating that there was no serious common method variation problem.

The chain mediating effect of hope traits and psychological distress in the link between childhood abuse and suicidal ideation

Based on structural equation modeling [37], this study examined the chain mediating role of hope traits and psychological distress in the relationship between childhood trauma and suicidal ideation. Since the items of the SIOSS are binary variables, robust maximum likelihood estimation (MLR) was employed for model estimation. The model fit indices indicated a good fit: robust CFI = 0.832, robust TLI = 0.823, robust RMSEA = 0.048 (90% CI [0.047, 0.049]), and SRMR = 0.064.

First, the total effect of childhood abuse on suicidal ideation was examined using a structural equation model. The model fit and pathway coefficients were then tested after the addition of mediating variables: hope traits and psychological distress. In the total effects model, childhood abuse was positively associated with suicidal ideation (β1 = 0.594, p < 0.001), indicating that individuals who reported higher levels of childhood abuse also reported higher levels of suicidal ideation. This association underscores the potential long-term impact of childhood abuse on psychological distress. After the two mediating variables of hope traits and psychological distress were added, the path coefficient of the effect of childhood abuse on suicidal ideation decreased from β1 to β2 (0.397, p < 0.001), yet remained significant. This suggests that while the direct effect of childhood abuse on suicidal ideation is substantial, the mediating effects of hope traits and psychological distress also play a role. Discussion

This study not only revealed the frequency of psychological distress and suicidal ideation in individuals who have experienced childhood abuse, but also explored the relationships among childhood abuse, hope traits, psychological distress (as measured by the SCL-90), and suicidal ideation through a series of chain mediating models. The results showed that the proportion of psychological distress (18.269%) and suicidal ideation (20.940%) was significantly higher in individuals who had experienced any of the various forms of childhood abuse than those who had not experienced abuse (6.892% and 3.708%). This suggests that individuals who have experienced childhood abuse are more likely to have psychological distress [39] and suicidal ideation. Therefore, it is necessary to study the relationship linking childhood abuse to suicidal ideation.

The direct effect of childhood abuse on suicidal ideation

This study not only confirms the well-established link between childhood abuse and suicidal ideation [23, 40–43] but also extends previous work by addressing two critical gaps in the literature.

First, prior research has predominantly focused on the overall impact of childhood maltreatment, few studies have systematically examined and compared the distinct effects of its different subtypes at a dimensional level. Our findings provide a more nuanced understanding by demonstrating that emotional abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect—but not physical or sexual abuse—exhibited direct positive associations with suicidal ideation in our sample of Chinese college students. This granular analysis moves beyond a broad-brush approach and helps identify which specific forms of maltreatment are most pernicious in this cultural context, offering precise targets for preventive interventions.

Second, A particularly intriguing finding lies in the non-significant pathways from physical and sexual abuse to subsequent outcomes, which aligns with established literature from Western contexts [44–46]. We interpret this pattern not as evidence that these abuse forms are harmless, but rather as an indication that their impact may operate indirectly through other psychological processes. Indeed, in our structural model, physical and sexual abuse were associated with reduced hope traits and increased despair, which in turn predicted suicidal ideation. Such indirect pathways are consistent with theoretical models positing that severe abuse undermines goal-directed cognition and fosters hopelessness, ultimately elevating suicide risk. The relatively small direct effects should therefore be viewed in the context of these mediated routes. Nevertheless, we acknowledge several methodological considerations. The reported frequencies of physical abuse (n = 74) and sexual abuse (n = 103) in our sample were low, which likely reduced statistical power to detect small-to-moderate direct effects. This underreporting may stem from the highly stigmatized nature of these experiences—particularly sexual abuse—as well as cultural normalization of physical punishment in Chinese parenting, leading individuals not to recognize or disclose such events. Importantly, this underreporting pertains to abuse disclosure itself, not to the reporting of suicidal ideation. Future studies with larger, community-based samples and multi-method assessments (e.g., interviews or collateral informants) are needed to more accurately estimate the true effects of physical and sexual abuse.In conclusion, the unique contribution of this study is twofold: (1) it provides a finer-grained, dimensional analysis of maltreatment subtypes, with the novel finding that neglect (and emotional abuse) exerts a stronger direct association with suicidal ideation than physical or sexual abuse in this cultural context; and (2) it highlights the critical role of indirect pathways—via hope and despair—through which severe abuse types may still confer risk. These findings should be considered preliminary until replicated with larger samples and more robust measurement tools.

The mediating role of hope traits and psychological distress in the link between childhood abuse and suicidal ideation

This study constructed and validated a chain mediation model, demonstrating that childhood abuse indirectly influences suicidal ideation through the sequential erosion of hope traits and subsequent deterioration of psychological distress, thereby fully supporting Hypothesis 4. The results indicate that traumatic experiences primarily deplete an individual’s future-oriented cognitive-motivational resources (hope), which in turn creates vulnerability to psychological distress, ultimately elevating the risk of suicidal ideation, confirming that the loss of this positive psychological capital may be a proximal cognitive-motivational mechanism through which trauma leads to emotional suffering. Our results bridge earlier and recent research. They not only align with cross-sectional findings identifying hope as a protective factor for mental health [23, 24]; but, more critically, provide strong cross-validation from an independent sample for the longitudinal chain mediation model of “abuse → hope → depression → suicidal ideation” established by [11].

The model elucidates a clear psychopathological pathway: individuals exposed to childhood abuse exhibit significantly lower levels of hope traits, making them more focused on negative information and more prone to psychological problems such as anxiety and depression, which in turn elevates their risk of suicidal ideation. This aligns with extensive prior research confirming a positive association between various forms of childhood abuse and psychological distress [47], which are themselves robustly linked to suicidal risk [48, 49].

Conversely, hope traits function as a powerful protective factor. It not only directly reduces suicidal risk but also does so indirectly by improving overall psychological distress, a effect demonstrated in populations such as older adults with insomnia [50]. From an attachment perspective, childhood abuse often fosters avoidant insecure attachment, leading to interpersonal withdrawal and aggression later in life [51]. In this context, fostering hope may help counteract these negative relational patterns by building resilience and a positive outlook.

Our findings highlight the urgent need to integrate hope-building interventions into trauma-informed care for survivors of childhood abuse. To translate this goal into clinical practice, specific evidence-based techniques can be employed. Within cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) frameworks, clinicians can directly target hopelessness through cognitive restructuring—helping clients identify and challenge absolute negative beliefs (e.g., “Nothing will ever get better”) and develop more balanced, agency-filled perspectives (e.g., “I have overcome challenges before, and I can use my strengths to cope with this”) [52, 53]. Furthermore, behavioral activation and collaborative goal-setting exercises are crucial for building pathways thinking; this involves breaking down overarching life goals into small, manageable steps, thereby creating a tangible sense of mastery and reinforcing the belief that desired outcomes are attainable. These techniques are not necessarily standalone modules but can be powerfully integrated into established trauma therapies like Trauma-Focused Cognitive Behavioral Therapy, where they directly counter the sense of futility ingrained by abuse. While psychological group counseling remains an effective modality for delivering such hope-enhancing interventions [54, 55]; [56], practical challenges regarding cost, duration, and effect sustainability [56]. Therefore, future research should prioritize developing more accessible formats, such as brief workshops or blended online platforms, to reliably disseminate these techniques to this vulnerable population.

Limitations

This study has some limitations that should be noted. First, regarding the measurement instruments, the internal consistency of two subscales used in this study—the Physical Neglect subscale of the CTQ-SF (Cronbach’s α = 0.512) and the Optimism subscale of the SIOSS (Cronbach’s α = 0.518)—was below the conventional threshold of 0.60 [57]. The implications differ by variable role. For Physical Neglect (independent variable), measurement error may have attenuated its observed associations. Notably, despite this attenuation, its path to suicidal ideation remained significant, suggesting a robust relationship. Of greater concern, the Optimism subscale is part of the dependent variable (suicidal ideation). Measurement error in the dependent variable increases standard errors and widens confidence intervals for all predictors (childhood trauma, hope traits, psychological distress), meaning the observed effects involving suicidal ideation may be conservative estimates of true relationships. Following the recommendations of Kline [57], findings derived from measures with Cronbach’s α below 0.60 should be considered preliminary and exploratory rather than confirmatory. Therefore, while the overall pattern of significant findings suggests robust relationships, the specific effect sizes involving suicidal ideation should be interpreted with caution. Future research should seek to replicate these findings using measures with stronger psychometric properties. For physical neglect, the full-length version of the CTQ (which includes more items for this dimension) is recommended. For suicidal ideation, alternative measures that do not rely solely on optimism-related items, or the full SIOSS with psychometric evaluation in similar samples, may provide more reliable assessment. Second, the generalizability of the present findings is subject to certain limitations. The use of a convenience sample recruited from a single university via WeChat, while providing the substantial sample size required for the analytical model, means that the results may not be fully representative of all Chinese university students. Therefore, caution is warranted when extrapolating these results to broader populations. Future research should seek to validate these findings using more diverse and representative sampling frames, including multi-center studies across different regions of China. Another limitation of this study lies in its cross-sectional design, which precludes definitive inferences about the direction of causality. The proposed chain mediation model (abuse → hope → distress → ideation) was based on a specific theoretical framework, but it is not the only plausible model. For instance, consistent with the frustration-aggression theory and the hopelessness theory, persistent psychological distress could equally lead to diminished hope (i.e., a pathway from distress to hope). Furthermore, hope and distress might function as parallel mediators or involve other untested reciprocal pathways. Although we conducted preliminary comparisons of alternative models (e.g., models swapping the sequence of hope and distress) and found our proposed model to have a relatively superior fit based on indices such as AIC/BIC, this does not conclusively rule out other theoretical explanations. Future research should employ longitudinal or experimental designs to corroborate the temporal sequence and causal directions among these variables. Finally, the absence of an a priori power analysis to determine the optimal sample size before data collection. Although the present large sample size (N = 1,908) affords considerable statistical power to detect small-to-medium effects, the power to detect effects for certain paths in the model (particularly for low-frequency abuse types such as sexual abuse) might still be limited., increasing the risk of Type II error (false negatives)—a concern further compounded by potential underreporting of stigmatized experiences, as discussed in the previous section. Future studies should conduct a priori power calculations based on expected effect sizes for mediation to ensure sufficient statistical power, particularly for low-frequency subgroups.

Conclusions

Childhood abuse not only directly has a positive impact on college students’ suicidal ideation, but also indirectly affects college students’ suicidal ideation through the mediating effects of hope traits and psychological distress. This study also found that childhood abuse can affect suicidal ideation in college students through the chain mediating effect of hope traits and psychological distress. In the chain mediating path relationship linking the sub-dimensions of childhood abuse to suicidal ideation, the two sub-dimensions of emotional abuse and emotional neglect play a major role.
