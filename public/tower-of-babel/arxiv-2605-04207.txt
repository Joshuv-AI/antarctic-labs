Optimal Semiparametric Dynamic Pricing with Feature Diversity
Jinhang Chai, Yaqi Duan, Jianqing Fan, Kaizheng Wang

Abstract
We study contextual dynamic pricing under a semiparametric demand model in which the purchase probability is 1 − F ⁡ ( p − m ⁡ ( 𝐱 ) ) 1-F(p-m(\mathbf{x})) , where m ⁡ ( 𝐱 ) m(\mathbf{x}) captures mean utility as a function of product features and buyer covariates, and F F is an unknown market-noise distribution. Existing methods either incur suboptimal regret or rely on restrictive structural assumptions. We propose a stagewise greedy pricing algorithm that iteratively refines the estimate of F F via local polynomial regression while pricing greedily with current estimates. By exploiting feature diversity, the algorithm reuses endogenous samples collected during exploitation for nonparametric estimation, avoiding costly global random exploration used in prior work. We establish a general regret bound that applies to any estimator m ^ \hat{m} of the utility function, and derive explicit rates for linear, nonparametric additive, and sparse linear classes of m m . For the linear class, our regret scales as T max ⁡ { 1 / 2 , 3 / ( 2 ​ β + 1 ) } T^{\max\{1/2,\,3/(2\beta+1)\}} , where β \beta is the smoothness of F F and T T is the time horizon. This improves the best known rates for semiparametric contextual pricing and achieves the parametric T \sqrt{T} rate when β ≥ 5 / 2 \beta\geq 5/2 . We further prove a matching lower bound, showing the optimality of our rate, and present numerical experiments that corroborate the theory and demonstrate the practical advantages of iterative refinement.

Introduction

Dynamic pricing with demand learning is a central problem in revenue management and online decision-making. A seller repeatedly interacts with heterogeneous customers, observes contextual information, and must choose prices to balance revenue maximization with demand learning. While classical pricing models assume either fully parametric demand or non-contextual settings, modern applications increasingly require flexible models that incorporate rich covariates and unknown demand distributions.

In this paper, we study a
semiparametric contextual pricing
problem in which the purchase probability is given by

\mathbb{P}(y=1\mid{\boldsymbol{x}},p)=1-F\bigl(p-m({\boldsymbol{x}})\bigr),

where
p
is the offering price,
m({\boldsymbol{x}})
is the mean utility function given the product and customer covariate vector
{\boldsymbol{x}}
, and
F
is the market noise distribution; see section §
1.2
for details. This model naturally generalizes both linear contextual pricing and nonparametric demand learning, capturing heterogeneity in product features and customer valuations while remaining robust to misspecification of the market noise distribution.

Learning under this model poses two fundamental challenges. First, both
m(\cdot)
and
F
are tightly coupled and must be learned simultaneously, yet errors in one directly bias estimation of the other. Second, the data used to estimate
F
are generated endogenously by the pricing policy, inducing distribution shift across time and dependence of the data over time. These challenges further complicate the optimal algorithm design and analysis.

13
initiated the study of this model and established regret guarantees under the
\beta
-smoothness assumptions on
F
. However, their approach relies on a costly global random exploration stage, leading to regret rates that deteriorate as the smoothness parameter increases. More recently,
27
developed active-learning-based algorithms that achieve the optimal
T^{3/5}
regret rate for the special case
\beta=2
and linear utility. That said, extensions to higher smoothness regimes remain unclear.

Our key insight is that under a
feature diversity
condition—ensuring that covariates are sufficiently well spread—samples collected during exploitation can be systematically reused to refine the estimation of the unknown distribution
F
. A related idea has appeared in
4
in the context of contextual bandits. Building on this observation, we propose a
stagewise greedy algorithm
that alternates between greedy pricing and localized nonparametric estimation of
F
via local polynomial regression. By organizing the learning process into geometrically growing stages, the algorithm progressively sharpens the estimate of
F
while avoiding the cost of explicit global exploration.

1.1
Related Work

Contextual pricing with parametric demand.

A large body of literature studies dynamic pricing with contextual information under parametric demand models. Early contributions focus on linear and generalized linear demand models with known link functions, where regret bounds of order
\sqrt{T}
or
\log T
can be achieved using explore–then–commit or greedy policies under appropriate conditions
(
8
;
21
)
. In the contextual setting,
2
was the first to establish a
\sqrt{dT}
regret bound. More recently,
10
showed that dimension-free regret is achievable via localization and proved the resulting rates to be minimax optimal. The model studied in this paper builds on
16
, which assumes a parametric form for the CDF.

Nonparametric and semiparametric pricing.

28
;
26
studied nonparametric demand learning, but without covariates.
In the contextual setting, when the demand function is fully nonparametric, regret typically suffers from the curse of dimensionality
(
11
)
. Contextual semiparametric models offer a middle ground by combining low-dimensional structure with nonparametric flexibility. In this setting,
13
and
19
proposed algorithms based on a nonparametric kernel estimator and a perturbed linear bandit, respectively, and derived regret bounds depending on the smoothness of
F
. However, their rates are suboptimal. Recently,
27
established minimax optimal regret for the special case of
\beta=2
and linear utility using techniques from active learning. Readers can refer to Table 1 in
27
for a detailed comparison of prior works.

Feature diversity and exploration-free learning.

Several recent works show that sufficiently rich variation in contextual features can significantly reduce the need for explicit exploration in online decision making
(
6
)
. In contextual bandits,
4
formalize a covariate diversity condition under which greedy or mostly greedy policies achieve near-optimal regret. Related guarantees for greedy algorithms are also obtained via smoothed analysis, where small random perturbations of contexts ensure adequate excitation even in adversarial settings
(
17
)
.

1.2
Problem Setup

We consider a dynamic pricing problem under a semiparametric demand model
(
13
)
. At each time
t
, a customer with covariate vector
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
arrives (
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}
can also include product features and their interactions with customer covariates), and the seller posts a price
p_{t}\in{\cal P}
, where
{\cal P}=[p_{\min},p_{\max}]
is a fixed interval.
The customer’s valuation is given by

v_{t}=m({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})+\varepsilon_{t},

and a purchase occurs whenever
v_{t}\geq p_{t}
so that we only observe
y_{t}=\mathds{1}(v_{t}\geq p_{t})
. Here,
m({\boldsymbol{x}})
is the mean utility (intrinsic value) function and
\{\varepsilon_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
are i.i.d. zero-mean noise variables, independent of
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
. Then, the demand function, which is the probability of purchase, is given by

d({\boldsymbol{x}},p)=P(y_{t}=1|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}=x,p_{t}=p)=1-F\!\left(p-m({\boldsymbol{x}})\right),

(1.1)

where
F
denotes the CDF of the noise
\varepsilon_{t}
. In this paper,
F
is unknown, treated nonparametrically, and assumed to be
\beta
-smooth for some
\beta\geq 2
.

Under the above model, the expected revenue at time
t
with offer price
p
is

r({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},p)=p\bigl(1-F(p-m({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}))\bigr).

The (clairvoyant) optimal price at time
t
is then given by

p_{t}^{*}\in\argmax_{p\in{\cal P}}r({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},p).

The mean-utility function
m
and the noise distribution
F
are generally unknown and need to be learned dynamically from the empirical sale data.
We assume
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
are i.i.d. draws from a fixed distribution on a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\hat{p}_{t}
is a pricing policy based on the data before time
t
. The goal is to maximize the cumulative revenue of the policy
\hat{p}_{t}
among all possible policies, or equivalently, to minimize the regret defined by,

\operatorname{Regret}(T)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Bigl(r({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},p_{t}^{*})-r({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},\hat{p}_{t})\Bigr).

Examples.

Typical choices for
m
include the following three cases, which we will discuss in detail in Section
3
.

1.

Linear model:
m({\boldsymbol{x}})={\boldsymbol{x}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

2.

Nonparametric additive model:
m({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}m_{j}(x_{j})
.

3.

Sparse linear model:
m({\boldsymbol{x}})={\boldsymbol{x}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}
with
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{0}\leq s
.

Assumption 1

(Smoothness)
.

The CDF

is
\beta

\beta\geq 2
, in the sense that letting

, it holds that

|F^{(q)}(x)-F^{(q)}(y)|\leq C|x-y|^{\beta-q},\quad\forall x,y,

2.1
Known Utility Function

The improvements in the rate of convergence over the method in
13
come from the fact that we use the endogenous pricing data from the previous stages. To illustrate the basic idea, let us begin with the simplified setting where the mean utility function
m({\boldsymbol{x}})
is known (e.g., obtained from an external market), whereas the noise distribution
F
is unknown. The task, therefore, reduces to estimating the noise CDF. The case of an unknown
m
is treated in the next section. As alluded to before, we will assume diversity on the distribution of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
, see Assumption
3
. If the utility
m({\boldsymbol{x}})
concentrates near a single point, the problem effectively reduces to nonparametric dynamic pricing without covariates, for which the minimax regret rate
T^{(\beta+1)/(2\beta+1)}
is established in
26
.

Clairvoyant Pricing Policy

The optimal (clairvoyant) price is

p_{t}^{\star}=\arg\max_{p>0}p\bigl(1-F(p-m({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}))\bigr).

The first-order condition yields the solution

p_{t}^{\star}=m({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})+\phi^{-1}\bigl(-m({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})\bigr),

where
\phi(u)=u-\frac{1-F(u)}{F^{\prime}(u)}
. Define
g(u)=u+\phi^{-1}(-u)
. We have
p_{t}^{\star}=g(m({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}))
.

The central nonparametric estimand in the pricing rule is
\phi^{-1}(u)
.
A crucial advantage of the stagewise algorithm is that the pricing policy in stage
l
(denoted by
{\cal T}_{l}
) depends only on the information collected in stages
1
through
l-1
. Consequently, conditional on the history up to stage
l-1
and the independent contexts
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}\}_{t\in{\cal T}_{l}}
in stage
l
, the resulting demands

d({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},\hat{p}_{l-1}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}))
(see (
1.1
)) within stage
l
are independent. This conditional independence allows
\phi^{-1}
to be estimated nonparametrically using all collected observations.

To guarantee the existence and uniqueness of the optimal price, we assume that
\phi
is invertible. This is ensured by imposing a monotonicity condition on
\phi

Since

Local Polynomial Regression

We adopt local polynomial regression as our main nonparametric regression tool. This allows us to estimate simultaneously the regression and its derivative.
At time
t

p_{t}=\hat{g}_{l-1}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})

\mathbb{E}[y_{t}\mid{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t},p_{t}]=1-F(u_{t}),

u_{t}=p_{t}-m({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})
and is a canonical nonparametric regression problem.

Given

q
(where
q=\lfloor\beta\rfloor
) with kernel
K
and bandwidth

is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{l}(u)=\arg\min_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{l}}\Bigl[y_{t}-\mu^{\top}U\!\left(\frac{u_{t}-u}{h_{l}}\right)\Bigr]^{2}K\!\left(\frac{u_{t}-u}{h_{l}}\right),

(2.1)

where
U(u)=[1,u,\ldots,u^{q}/q!]^{\top}
.
Then, following the local polynomial technique

12
)
, we have the estimators for

and its derivative

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{l}(u)=1-e_{1}^{\top}\hat{\mu}_{l}(u),\qquad\hat{F}_{l}^{(1)}(u)=-\frac{e_{2}^{\top}\hat{\mu}_{l}(u)}{h_{l}},

(2.2)

where
e_{1}

denote the first two standard basis vectors, corresponding to the intercept and the slope.

Post-Smoothing

Define the initial estimate with LPR (local polynomial regression) at stage

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}^{I}_{l}(u)=u-\frac{1-\hat{F}_{l}(u)}{\hat{F}^{(1)}_{l}(u)}.

LPR alone does not ensure sufficient smoothness of

While
\|\hat{\phi}^{I}_{l}-\phi\|_{\infty}
is well controlled, the derivative error
\|(\hat{\phi}^{I}_{l})^{\prime}-\phi^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

We therefore apply a post-processing step to

Let

Define the kernel smoothing as the convolution

\hat{\phi}^{S}_{l}(u)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\phi}^{I}_{l}(u-t)K_{\delta_{u}}(t)\,dt,\qquad K_{\delta}(t)=\frac{1}{\delta}K\!\left(\frac{t}{\delta}\right).

(Feature Diversity)

Let
J_{m}=[\underline{u},\bar{u}]

\delta_{[\underline{u},\bar{u}]}(z):=\min\{(z-\underline{u})^{\kappa},(\bar{u}-z)^{\kappa}\},

Theorem 1

(Known Utility)
.

Under Assumptions
1

3

\pi^{\mathrm{ILPR}}

\operatorname{Regret}(\pi^{\mathrm{ILPR}},T)\;\lesssim\;T^{\frac{3}{2\beta+1}}

with probability at least

.
On the other hand, there exists a class

satisfying Assumptions

2

2

.

We give some high-level intuition of this result.
It is similar to the idea of how feature diversity helps improve the regret rates in bandit literature
(
4
)
. With our feature diversity Assumption
3
, we can continue exploring (estimation) while at the same time exploiting (regret minimization).
To illustrate the ideas, suppose for simplicity that each stage only consists of one time step.
At time
t+1
, the
t
previous samples can all be used in estimation, incurring the
t^{-\frac{\beta-1}{2\beta+1}}
estimation error of
\phi

. Hence, the estimation error of the optimal pricing function

also has the same rate. Moreover, the per-time regret nearly the optimal pricing is a quadratic function of the pricing gap. Therefore, the total regret can be roughly bounded by

\displaystyle\operatorname{Regret}(T)\lesssim\sum_{t=1}^{T}t^{-\frac{2(\beta-1)}{2\beta+1}}\lesssim T^{\frac{3}{2\beta+1}}.

In particular, when
\beta

This indicates that the regret grows slower than any polynomial rate, which is consistent with the logarithmic bound in
4
.

Theory

In this section, we present our main theory, as well as the limitations of prior work.
We first list a few assumptions to deal with the feature density and mismatch issue. Note that, up to our main theorem, we treat
\hat{m}
as a generic given estimator. All probabilistic results are conditional on
\hat{m}

3.1
General requirements for the initial estimate

In
13
, data from earlier stages are not fully exploited. To improve statistical efficiency, we impose Assumption
4
, which allows us to leverage all collected data and attain the minimax rate.
It is a modified version of Assumption
3
, with the utility function
m
replaced by the initial estimate
\hat{m}
.

Assumption 4

(Feature Diversity)
.

Let
J_{\hat{m}}=[\underline{u},\bar{u}]
be the range of
\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}})
. Define

\delta_{[\underline{u},\bar{u}]}(z):=\min\{(z-\underline{u})^{\kappa},(\bar{u}-z)^{\kappa}\},

and write
\delta(z)
when the interval is clear from context. Assume that the density of the utility estimator
\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}})
, denoted by
p_{\hat{m}}(z)
, exists and that there exist constants
0<c_{d}\leq C_{d}<\infty
such that

c_{d}\,\delta(z)\leq p_{\hat{m}}(z)\leq C_{d}\,\delta(z),\qquad\text{for all }z\in J_{\hat{m}}.

While boundary decay complicates algorithm design, it is partially mitigated in the regret analysis: regions where data are rarely observed correspond to states that contribute less to the regret.

We also require mild regularity of the covariate distribution, in the same spirit of Assumption 4.3 (Lipschitzness) in
13
.
Our condition is imposed on the conditional mean.
Let
\hat{\Delta}:=\hat{m}-m
and define

\mathfrak{m}(t):=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\hat{\Delta}({\boldsymbol{x}})/\|\hat{\Delta}\|_{\infty}\,\big|\,\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}})=t\bigr],

where the expectation is taken with respect to the randomness of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and regarding
\hat{m}
as a fixed function.

Assumption 5

(Conditional Mean Regularity)
.

The function
\mathfrak{m}(t)
is
\bar{L}
-Lipschitz on its domain for some constant
\bar{L}
.

After stating the main result in Section
3.2
, we will give three examples for modeling the mean utility function, from a low-dimensional linear model and nonparametric additive model to a high-dimensional sparse model, to illustrate that Assumptions
4
and
5
hold. See Corollary
1
for the summary of the results.

3.2
Upper and Lower Bounds on the Regret

In this section, we first state an upper bound on the regret and outline the main proof ideas. Then, we justify the sharpness of our results via a lower bound.

Theorem 2

Suppose that Assumptions

2

\operatorname{Regret}(\pi^{\mathrm{ILPR}},T)\;\lesssim\;\max\Bigl\{T_{0},\;T\,\epsilon_{m}^{2},\;T^{\frac{3}{2\beta+1}}\Bigr\}

The proof is deferred to Appendix

Example 1 (Linear Model)

Consider the linear utility model

\displaystyle m({\boldsymbol{x}})=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{x}}\quad\text{with}\ \mathcal{X}=B^{d}(0,1).

The shape of the domain is not essential, provided mild smoothness and regularity conditions. For example, a similar result holds with

Since the model is linear, it is natural to estimate it within the class of linear functions using the least-square based on the exploiration data. Accordingly, we take
\hat{m}\in\{f:f({\boldsymbol{x}})=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{x}}\}

4

If

Denote

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}(t)=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{x}}\,\Big|\,\frac{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{x}}}{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}\|}=\frac{t}{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}\|}\Bigr]=h\left(\frac{t}{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}\|}\right)

3.3
Comparisons with prior work

The algorithm in
13
essentially employs an initial uniform pricing phase to estimate
m
and
F
, followed by the static greedy pricing using these estimates for the remaining periods. Besides the algorithm design, the nonparametric estimator used therein also has several limitations, as detailed below.

Limitations of the Nadaraya–Watson Estimator

13
employ the Nadaraya–Watson (NW) estimator. However, it exhibits two structural limitations, as detailed below.
They start by estimating the nonparametric link (noise CDF)

\hat{F}_{l}(u)=1-\frac{h_{l}(u)}{f_{l}(u)},\qquad h_{l}(u)=\frac{1}{n_{l}b_{l}}\!\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{l}}K\!\left(\frac{w_{t}-u}{b_{l}}\right)y_{t},\quad f_{l}(u)=\frac{1}{n_{l}b_{l}}\!\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{l}}K\!\left(\frac{w_{t}-u}{b_{l}}\right).

The derivative estimator is

\hat{F}_{l}^{(1)}(u)=-\frac{h_{l}^{(1)}(u)f_{l}(u)-f_{l}^{(1)}(u)h_{l}(u)}{f_{l}^{2}(u)}.

Then they obtain the plug-in estimator

\hat{\phi}_{l}(u)=u-\frac{1-\hat{F}_{l}(u)}{\hat{F}_{l}^{(1)}(u)},\qquad\hat{g}_{l-1}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})=\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})+\hat{\phi}_{l-1}^{-1}\!\bigl(-\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})\bigr),

The NW estimator exhibits two severe issues as detailed below.

•

Error-in-variables.

As discussed before, the observed covariates are given by

u_{t}=p_{t}-\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})=\hat{\phi}_{l-1}^{-1}\!\bigl(-\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})\bigr),

while the true regression target corresponds to

\tilde{u}_{t}=p_{t}-m({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})
, leading to a discrepancy of
\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})-m({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})
.
Classical methods for the error-in-variables problem—e.g., deconvolution
(
15
)
or instrumental variables
(
1
)
—do not directly apply because the errors here depend on the covariates.

•

Smoothness of the covariate distribution.

The NW estimator requires the covariate density to be as smooth as the regression
function.
In our setting,
\phi
depends on
F^{\prime}
and may not inherit this smoothness.
Attempting to relate the observed covariate distribution to that of the oracle covariate
\phi^{-1}(-m({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}))
via Taylor expansion produces leading error terms that cannot be controlled at the optimal rate.
Hence, NW estimators are fundamentally limited in this setting.
Moreover,
13
assumes a smooth covariate density, which cannot hold for the covariates induced by our algorithm.

Discussion

This paper studies dynamic pricing under a general semiparametric model. Rather than relying on costly global random exploration, the proposed ILPR policy leverages endogenous data collected during exploitation to iteratively refine the nonparametric estimate of the market noise distribution. This leads to sharp regret guarantees and, in the linear utility setting, a minimax-optimal rate that attains the parametric
\sqrt{T}
regime when
\beta\geq 5/2
. Our analysis underscores the importance of integrating adaptive pricing with carefully designed nonparametric estimation, including local polynomial regression, post-smoothing, and boundary correction. Several directions remain open. First, while matching lower bounds are established for linear utility, extending them to additive and high-dimensional sparse models would further elucidate the fundamental limits of semiparametric pricing. Second, it would be of interest to relax the feature diversity condition or develop robust variants under weaker support assumptions, especially since effective learning primarily relies on regions of the covariate space that are frequently visited. Finally, extending the framework to richer settings—such as more general demand models, multi-product pricing, inventory constraints, and nonstationary environments—may further reveal the benefits of endogenous data reuse.

\mathrm{d}_{l-1}
. Since the derivative of
\hat{\phi}_{l-1}
is bounded, and by Assumption
4
, we have
\mu(x)\asymp\delta_{[\underline{z_{l-1}},\overline{z_{l-1}}]}(z)
.

By (
A.2
), the same density decay rate holds with the estimated interval
[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]
, for
x\in I_{v}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]
. Here recall that
I_{v}[\underline{z},\overline{z}]:=[\underline{z}+v(\overline{z}-\underline{z}),\underline{z}+(1-v)(\overline{z}-\underline{z})]
, and
\delta_{[\underline{u},\bar{u}]}(z):=\min\{(z-\underline{u})^{\kappa},(\bar{u}-z)^{\kappa}\}
. That is to say,
\mathrm{d}_{l-1}(x)\asymp\delta_{[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]}(z)
. In other words, the boundary error can be neglected.

Denote the linear smoothing weights for estimating the first-order derivative as
w^{1}(x,u_{i})
. To be specific,

\displaystyle B_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{nh}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\left(\frac{u_{i}-x}{h}\right)U\left(\frac{u_{i}-x}{h}\right)U\left(\frac{u_{i}-x}{h}\right)^{\top},

\displaystyle q_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{nh}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}K\left(\frac{u_{i}-x}{h}\right)U\left(\frac{u_{i}-x}{h}\right).

The complete local solution of LPR is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\arg\min\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[D_{i}-\theta^{\top}U\left(\frac{u_{i}-x}{h}\right)\right]^{2}K\left(\frac{u_{i}-x}{h}\right)

\displaystyle=B_{n}(x)^{-1}q_{n}(x),

as long as
B_{n}(x)\succ 0
.

Then we have

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{l}(x)=1-e_{1}^{\top}\hat{\theta}_{n}(x),\qquad\hat{F}_{l}^{(1)}(x)=-e_{2}^{\top}\hat{\theta}_{n}(x)/h.

Define the linear smoothing weights for the first-order derivative as follows:

\displaystyle w^{1}(x,u_{i})=\frac{1}{nh^{2}}e_{2}^{\top}B_{n}^{-1}(x)K\left(\frac{u_{i}-x}{h}\right)U\left(\frac{u_{i}-x}{h}\right).

Then

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{l}^{(1)}(x)=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}w^{1}(x,u_{i}).

We only consider the estimation error of the derivative for now, which has a slower rate.
We have the following key error decomposition:

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{l}^{(1)}(x)-F^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\overbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}F(u_{i})w^{1}(x,u_{i})-F^{\prime}(x)}^{T^{\textrm{b}}(x)}-\overbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{e}_{i}w^{1}(x,u_{i})}^{T^{\textrm{v}}(x)}+\overbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(F(\tilde{u}_{i})-F(u_{i})\right)w^{1}(x,u_{i})}^{T^{\textrm{m}}(x)}.

Here we define the error
\tilde{e}_{i}=D_{i}-\big(1-F(\tilde{u}_{i})\big)
. Conditional on the designs
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the variables
\tilde{e}_{i}
are independent, mean-zero, and uniformly bounded; all applications of Bernstein’s inequality below are to such conditional sums.

We proceed to present a lemma on the weights. It heavily hinges on Lemmas
1
and
2
. Built upon it, we can analyze the bias term and the variance term. The proof is in Appendix
E.1
. For simplicity of exposition, we consider
[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]=[0,1]
, and
I_{v}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]=[v,1-v]
.

Lemma 3
.

Under the conditions of Lemmas
1
and
2
, the weights satisfy the following for all
x\in[v,1-v]
:

(i)

\sup_{i}|w^{1}(x,u_{i})|\leq\frac{C_{*}}{nh^{2}\mu_{h}(x)}
,
\sup_{i}|w^{0}(x,u_{i})|\leq\frac{C_{*}}{nh\mu_{h}(x)}
.

(ii)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}|w^{1}(x,u_{i})|\leq\frac{C_{*}}{h}
,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|w^{0}(x,u_{i})|\leq C_{*}
.

(iii)

w^{1}(x,u_{i})=w^{0}(x,u_{i})=0\ \text{if}\ |u_{i}-x|\geq h
.

(iv)

w^{1}(x,u_{i})
is Lipschitz in
x
with constant at most
Cn
.
4
4

4

More precisely, one obtains a bound of order
C/(h^{2})
from differentiating the kernel and
B_{n}^{-1}(x)
, and under our bandwidth choice
h\asymp n^{-1/(2\beta+1)}
this implies
1/h^{2}\lesssim n
, so stating an
O(n)
bound keeps the notation simpler without affecting subsequent rates.

Bias term

We analyze the bias term. By the
\beta
-smooth condition,

\displaystyle|T^{\textrm{b}}(x)|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}F(u_{i})w^{1}(x,u_{i})-F^{\prime}(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(F(u_{i})-F(x)\right)w^{1}(x,u_{i})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{|F^{(q)}(x+\tau_{i}(u_{i}-x))-F^{(q)}(x)|}{q!}(u_{i}-x)^{q}w^{1}(x,u_{i})\quad\text{where}\ \tau_{i}\in[0,1]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}L\frac{|u_{i}-x|^{\alpha}}{q!}|u_{i}-x|^{q}|w^{1}(x,u_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq L\frac{h^{\beta}}{q!}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|w^{1}(x,u_{i})|

\displaystyle\lesssim h^{\beta-1}.

Variance term

We analyze the variance term. We aim for a uniform-type result.
By Lemma
3
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[(\tilde{e}_{i}w^{1}(x,u_{i}))^{2}\mid\{u_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\right]\leq\frac{1}{n}\sup_{i}|w^{1}(x,u_{i})|\sum_{i=1}^{n}|w^{1}(x,u_{i})|\lesssim\frac{1}{n^{2}h^{3}\mu_{h}(x)}.

Using Bernstein’s inequality (conditionally on
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
), for fixed
x\in I_{v}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]
, we have with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle|T^{\textrm{v}}(x)|=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{e}_{i}w^{1}(x,u_{i})\right|\lesssim\sqrt{n\cdot\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n^{2}h^{3}\mu_{h}(x)}}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{nh^{2}\mu_{h}(x)}.

To get a uniform-type result, consider discretizing
I_{v}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]
into
T^{3}
equidistant grid points
{\cal G}
, and apply a union bound together with the Lipschitzness of
w^{1}(x,u_{i})
.
Specifically, for any
x\in I_{v}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]
, consider the nearest
x_{0}\in{\cal G}
to
x
. As
\sqrt{T}\leq n\leq T
and by Lemma
3
(iv),

\displaystyle\left|T^{\textrm{v}}(x_{0})-T^{\textrm{v}}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|w^{1}(x_{0},u_{i})-w^{1}(x,u_{i})\right|

\displaystyle\leq Cn|x-x_{0}|\leq\frac{1}{T}

for an appropriate choice of the grid size.

Therefore, with probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds that for any
x\in I_{v}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]
,

\displaystyle|T^{\textrm{v}}(x)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{n\cdot\frac{\log(T^{3}/\delta)}{n^{2}h^{3}\mu_{h}(x_{0})}}+\frac{\log(T^{3}/\delta)}{nh^{2}\mu_{h}(x_{0})}+\frac{1}{T}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{n\cdot\frac{\log(T^{3}/\delta)}{n^{2}h^{3}\mu_{h}(x)}}+\frac{\log(T^{3}/\delta)}{nh^{2}\mu_{h}(x)}+\frac{1}{T}.

Taking
\delta=T^{-3}
, the right-hand side becomes
\sqrt{\frac{\log T}{nh^{3}\mu_{h}(x)}}
up to constants.

Mismatch term

We analyze the design-misalignment error at a fixed
x
first.

|T^{\textrm{m}}(x)|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w^{1}(x,u_{i})\left[F(\tilde{u}_{i})-F(u_{i})\right],

where we recall
w^{1}(x,u_{i})
are the weights of linear smoothing for first-derivative estimation. Although we can only bound
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|w^{1}(x,u_{i})|\lesssim\frac{1}{h}
, tighter control is available using Taylor expansion when mild smoothness is satisfied, since we have
\sum_{i=1}^{n}w^{1}(x,u_{i})(x-u_{i})^{k}=\mathbf{1}(k=1)
for
k\leq p
.

Specifically, recall
\hat{\Delta}:=\hat{m}-m
, and note that
\beta\geq 2
and weights are zero outside the local band
[x-h,x+h]
. We can rewrite
T^{\textrm{m}}(x)
as

\displaystyle T^{\textrm{m}}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w^{1}(x,\hat{\phi}_{l-1}^{-1}\circ(-\hat{m})({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}))\Big[F\big(\hat{\phi}_{l-1}^{-1}\circ(-\hat{m})({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\big)-F\big(\hat{\phi}_{l-1}^{-1}\circ(-\hat{m})({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})+\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-m({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\big)\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w^{1}(x,\hat{\phi}_{l-1}^{-1}\circ(-\hat{m})({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}))F^{\prime}\big(\hat{\phi}_{l-1}^{-1}\circ(-\hat{m})({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\big)\Big(\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-m({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\Big)+R_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w^{1}(x,\hat{\phi}_{l-1}^{-1}\circ(-\hat{m})({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}))F^{\prime}\big(\hat{\phi}_{l-1}^{-1}\circ(-\hat{m})({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\big)\hat{\Delta}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\,\mathds{1}\big(x-h\leq\hat{\phi}_{l-1}^{-1}\circ(-\hat{m})({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\leq x+h\big)+R_{2}.

Note that we have defined
\mathfrak{m}(t)
as the conditional mean of
\hat{\Delta}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})/\|\hat{\Delta}\|_{\infty}
given
\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})=t
. The misalignment error can be further written as

\displaystyle T^{\textrm{m}}(x)

\displaystyle=\overbrace{\|\hat{\Delta}\|_{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w^{1}(x,u_{i})F^{\prime}(u_{i})\mathfrak{m}(\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}))\mathds{1}\big(x-h\leq u_{i}\leq x+h\big)}^{T_{1}^{\textrm{m}}(x)}+\overbrace{\|\hat{\Delta}\|_{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}(x)}^{T_{2}^{\textrm{m}}(x)}+R_{2},

where
\delta_{i}(x):=w^{1}(x,u_{i})F^{\prime}(u_{i})\left(\hat{\Delta}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})/\|\hat{\Delta}\|_{\infty}-\mathfrak{m}(\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}))\right)
. The second-order remainder term can be bounded by

\displaystyle|R_{2}|\lesssim\frac{\|\hat{m}-m\|^{2}_{\infty}}{h}\leq\|\hat{\Delta}\|_{\infty}.

We proceed to bound
T_{1}^{\textrm{m}}(x)
and
T_{2}^{\textrm{m}}(x)
separately.

Denote
q=F^{\prime}\cdot(\mathfrak{m}\circ\hat{\phi}_{l-1})
. By Assumption
5
,
q
is
\tilde{L}
-Lipschitz for some constant
\tilde{L}
. It follows that for any
x\in I_{v}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]
,

\displaystyle|T_{1}^{\textrm{m}}(x)|

\displaystyle=\|\hat{\Delta}\|_{\infty}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}w^{1}(x,u_{i})q(u_{i})\mathds{1}\big(x-h\leq u_{i}\leq x+h\big)\Big|

\displaystyle=\|\hat{\Delta}\|_{\infty}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}w^{1}(x,u_{i})\big(q(u_{i})-q(x)\big)\mathds{1}\big(x-h\leq u_{i}\leq x+h\big)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{\Delta}\|_{\infty}\tilde{L}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big|w^{1}(x,u_{i})\big|\cdot\big|u_{i}-x\big|\mathds{1}\big(x-h\leq u_{i}\leq x+h\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\hat{\Delta}\|_{\infty}.

Note that for fixed
x
and considering only the randomness of
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
, the
\delta_{i}(x)
are independent and have zero mean. We can show

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[\delta_{i}(x)^{2}\mid\{u_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\big]

\displaystyle\lesssim\sup_{i}|w^{1}(x,u_{i})|\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}|w^{1}(x,u_{i})|\lesssim\frac{1}{nh^{3}\mu_{h}(x)},

\displaystyle|\delta_{i}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{i}|w^{1}(x,u_{i})|\lesssim\frac{1}{nh^{2}\mu_{h}(x)}.

By Bernstein’s inequality, it holds with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}(x)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\delta_{i}(x)^{2}}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{nh^{2}\mu_{h}(x)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{nh^{3}\mu_{h}(x)}}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{nh^{2}\mu_{h}(x)}.

To get a uniform-type result, we take the same discretization approach as analyzing the variance term. Consider discretizing
I_{v}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]
into
T^{3}
equidistant grid points
{\cal G}
.
For any
x\in I_{v}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]
, let
x_{0}\in{\cal G}
be the closest grid point to
x
.
It holds that

\displaystyle|T_{2}^{\textrm{m}}(x)-T_{2}^{\textrm{m}}(x_{0})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\hat{\Delta}\|_{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big|w^{1}(x,u_{i})-w^{1}(x_{0},u_{i})\big|

\displaystyle\leq Cn\|\hat{\Delta}\|_{\infty}|x-x_{0}|\leq\|\hat{\Delta}\|_{\infty},

for a suitable grid size and constant
C
.

Hence, taking a union bound, we have with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle|T^{\textrm{m}}(x)|\lesssim\|\hat{\Delta}\|_{\infty}\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{\log(T^{3}/\delta)}{nh^{3}\mu_{h}(x)}}+\frac{\log(T^{3}/\delta)}{nh^{2}\mu_{h}(x)}\right).

Taking
\delta=T^{-3}
, from (
A.6
), we obtain with probability at least
1-T^{-3}
, for any
x\in I_{v}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]
,

\displaystyle|T^{\textrm{m}}(x)|\lesssim\|\hat{\Delta}\|_{\infty}.

Combining the three sources of error, we obtain with probability at least
1-2T^{-3}
, for all
x\in I_{v}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]
,

\displaystyle|\hat{F}_{l}^{(1)}(x)-F^{\prime}(x)|\lesssim\epsilon_{m}+\sqrt{\frac{\log T}{nh^{3}\mu_{h}(x)}}+h^{\beta-1}.

A.5
Induction: Step 3

Note that

\displaystyle|\hat{\phi}_{l}^{I}(x)-\phi(x)|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1-\hat{F}_{l}(x)}{\hat{F}^{(1)}_{l}(x)}-\frac{1-F(x)}{F^{\prime}(x)}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim|\hat{F}_{l}(x)-F(x)|+|\hat{F}_{l}^{(1)}(x)-F^{\prime}(x)|.

We have controlled the estimation error of
\hat{F}_{l}
and
\hat{F}^{(1)}_{l}
, hence of
\hat{\phi}_{l}^{I}
. Now we are ready to analyze the post-smoothing convolution and perturbation to obtain (
A.7
) at stage
l
. Meanwhile, we prove the same estimation error rate is preserved for
\hat{\phi}_{l}
.

Denote
e_{l}(x):=\hat{\phi}^{I}_{l}(x)-\phi(x)
. Recall the relative distance of
x
in interval
[a,b]
as
\alpha_{[a,b]}(x)=\min\{\frac{x-a}{b-a},\frac{b-x}{b-a}\}
. With no ambiguity, we also abbreviate
\alpha(x):=\alpha_{[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]}(x)
here.
For any
x\in I_{v}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]
, we have

\displaystyle|e_{l}(x)|\leq Cn^{-\frac{\beta-1}{2\beta+1}}\sqrt{\frac{\log T}{\alpha(x)^{\kappa}}}+\epsilon_{m}:=b(x).

In this subsection, we aim to prove that, after post-smoothing,
\hat{\phi}_{l}
satisfies

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{l}^{\prime}\in[c,C]\quad\text{for some constants }\ C>c>0,

(A.7a)

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}^{-1}_{l}(\underline{u})\leq\phi^{-1}(\underline{u}),

(A.7b)

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}^{-1}_{l}(\bar{u})\geq\phi^{-1}(\bar{u}),

(A.7c)

where we recall that
[\underline{u},\bar{u}]
is the range of
-\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}})
and
[\phi^{-1}(\underline{u}),\phi^{-1}(\bar{u})]=[\underline{z},\bar{z}]
.

Post-smoothing

The post-smoothing step is

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{l}^{S}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\phi}_{l}^{I}(x-t)K_{\delta_{x}}(t)dt.

Note that here the bandwidth
\delta_{x}
as a function of
x
is location-dependent. We will decide later how to choose it adaptively from data.

We can decompose the estimation error as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{l}^{S}(x)-\phi(x)

\displaystyle=\overbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\hat{\phi}_{l}^{I}(x-t)-\phi(x-t)\right)K_{\delta_{x}}(t)dt}^{T_{1}}+\overbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Big(\phi(x-t)-\phi(x)\Big)K_{\delta_{x}}(t)dt}^{T_{2}}.

We bound the two terms separately as

\displaystyle|T_{1}|\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|e_{l}(x-t)|K_{\delta_{x}}(t)dt\leq\sup_{z\in[x-\delta_{x},x+\delta_{x}]}|e_{l}(z)|

and

\displaystyle|T_{2}|\lesssim\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|t|K_{\delta_{x}}(t)dt\leq\delta_{x}.

As for the derivative of
\hat{\phi}_{l}^{S}(x)
, it holds that
(\hat{\phi}_{l}^{S})^{\prime}(x)=T_{3}+T_{4}+T_{5}
, where

\displaystyle T_{3}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left((\hat{\phi}_{l}^{I})^{\prime}(x-t)-\phi^{\prime}(x-t)\right)K_{\delta_{x}}(t)dt,

\displaystyle T_{4}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi^{\prime}(x-t)K_{\delta_{x}}(t)dt,

\displaystyle T_{5}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\hat{\phi}_{l}^{I}(x-t)-\phi(x-t)+\phi(x-t)-\phi(x)\right)\frac{dK_{\delta_{x}}(t)}{dx}dt.

It holds that

\displaystyle|T_{3}|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e_{l}(x-t)K_{\delta_{x}}^{\prime}(t)dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e_{l}(x-t)\frac{1}{\delta_{x}^{2}}K^{\prime}\left(\frac{t}{\delta_{x}}\right)dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{z\in[x-\delta_{x},x+\delta_{x}]}|e_{l}(z)|\cdot\left|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\delta_{x}}\Big|K^{\prime}\left(\frac{t}{\delta_{x}}\right)\Big|d\left(\frac{t}{\delta_{x}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\sup_{z\in[x-\delta_{x},x+\delta_{x}]}|e_{l}(z)|}{\delta_{x}}.

And

\displaystyle c\leq\inf_{z\in[x-\delta_{x},x+\delta_{x}]}\phi^{\prime}(z)\leq T_{4}\leq\sup_{z\in[x-\delta_{x},x+\delta_{x}]}\phi^{\prime}(z)\leq C.

Note that we have

\displaystyle\frac{dK_{\delta_{x}}(t)}{dx}

\displaystyle=\frac{d\delta_{x}}{dx}\cdot\left[-\frac{1}{\delta_{x}^{2}}K\left(\frac{t}{\delta_{x}}\right)-\frac{t}{\delta_{x}^{3}}K^{\prime}\left(\frac{t}{\delta_{x}}\right)\right].

It follows that as long as, for some small constant
c
, it holds that
|\frac{d\delta_{x}}{dx}|\leq c
for every
x\in I_{v}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]
, we have

\displaystyle|T_{5}|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(|e_{l}(x-t)|+|t|\right)\left|\frac{dK_{\delta_{x}}(t)}{dx}\right|dt

\displaystyle\leq c\left(\sup_{z\in[x-\delta_{x},x+\delta_{x}]}|e_{l}(z)|+\delta_{x}\right)\cdot\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{\delta_{x}^{2}}K\left(\frac{t}{\delta_{x}}\right)+\frac{t}{\delta_{x}^{3}}K^{\prime}\left(\frac{t}{\delta_{x}}\right)\right|dt

\displaystyle\leq c\left(\frac{\sup_{z\in[x-\delta_{x},x+\delta_{x}]}|e_{l}(z)|}{\delta_{x}}+1\right).

For every
x\in I_{v}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]
, take

\displaystyle\delta_{x}:=\bar{\delta}_{\alpha(x)}=Cn^{-\frac{\beta-1}{2\beta+1}}\sqrt{\frac{\log T}{\alpha(x)^{\kappa}}}+\epsilon_{m}

(A.8)

for a constant
C
large enough, enabling the first term in the above formula to be bounded by a constant. Here,
v
is defined by

\displaystyle\quad v=(C^{2}n^{-\frac{\beta-1}{2\beta+1}}\log^{1/2}T)^{\frac{2}{\kappa+2}}+C\epsilon_{m},

(A.9)

and it holds that
v\geq C\bar{\delta}_{v}
. Combining the analysis of the various terms above, there exist some constants
c_{1},C_{1}
such that

(\hat{\phi}_{l}^{S})^{\prime}(x),\phi^{\prime}(x)\in[c_{1},C_{1}]\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in I_{v}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}].

Perturbation

In the perturbation step, for
x\in I_{v}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]
, we set

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{l}(x)=\hat{\phi}_{l}^{S}(x),

while for
x\in(-\infty,l_{v}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]]\cup[r_{v}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}],\infty)
, we use linear extrapolation as the perturbation with slope
c_{1}/2
(where we recall that
c_{1}
is the lower bound of
(\hat{\phi}_{l}^{S})^{\prime}(x)
).
In a nutshell, without perturbation, the points near the boundary may never be sampled, and cumulatively this incurs large regret.
The main purpose of this step is to ensure the last two inequalities in (
A.7
).

Specifically, define

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{l}(x)=\begin{cases}\hat{\phi}_{l}^{S}(v_{1})+\frac{c_{1}}{2}(x-v_{1}),\quad&\text{if}\ x\in(-\infty,v_{1}],\\
\hat{\phi}_{l}^{S}(x),&\text{if}\ x\in[v_{1},v_{2}],\\
\hat{\phi}_{l}^{S}(v_{2})+\frac{c_{1}}{2}(x-v_{2}),\quad&\text{if}\ x\in[v_{2},\infty),\end{cases}

(A.10a)

where we abbreviate
v_{1}=l_{v}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]
,
v_{2}=r_{v}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]
.

We claim that the perturbation satisfies the following properties:

Claim 1.

It is an increasing function with derivative bounded in
[c_{1}/2,C_{1}]
.

Claim 2.

The range coverage property is satisfied:

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{l}(\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}})\geq\phi(\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}),\qquad\hat{\phi}_{l}(\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}})\leq\phi(\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}).

Claim 3.

The estimation error is controlled and hence does not inflate the regret:

\displaystyle|\hat{\phi}_{l}(x)-\phi(x)|\leq\begin{cases}b(x)\quad&\text{for}\ x\in[v_{1},v_{2}],\\
\bar{\delta}_{v}\quad&\text{for}\ x\in[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},v_{1}]\cup[v_{2},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}],\end{cases}

where
\bar{\delta}_{v}:=\bar{\delta}_{\alpha(v_{1})}
.

Now we verify each claim.

Proof of Claim 1.

On the extrapolation region, the derivative is
c_{1}/2
, and on the interior it lies in
[c_{1},C_{1}]
by construction. Hence
\hat{\phi}_{l}
is increasing with derivative in
[c_{1}/2,C_{1}]
.

Proof of Claim 2.

We have that

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{l}(\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}})

\displaystyle=\hat{\phi}_{l}(v_{1})-\frac{c_{1}}{2}(v_{1}-\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}})

\displaystyle\geq\phi(v_{1})-b(v_{1})-\frac{c_{1}}{2}(v_{1}-\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}})

\displaystyle\geq\phi(v_{1})-c_{1}(v_{1}-\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}})\geq\phi(\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}),

where we use that
\phi^{\prime}(x)\geq c_{1}
for
x\in[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},v_{1}]
and
b(v_{1})\leq\frac{c_{1}}{2}(v_{1}-\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}})
. The proof of
\hat{\phi}_{l}(\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}})\leq\phi(\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}})
follows a similar line.

Proof of Claim 3.

For
x\in[v_{1},v_{2}]
, the bound follows immediately from the definition of
b(x)
. For
x\in[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},v_{1}]
, it holds that

\displaystyle|\hat{\phi}_{l}(x)-\phi(x)|

\displaystyle\leq|\hat{\phi}_{l}(v_{1})-\phi(v_{1})|+|\hat{\phi}_{l}(x)-\hat{\phi}_{l}(v_{1})|+|\phi(x)-\phi(v_{1})|

\displaystyle\leq b(v_{1})+\frac{c_{1}}{2}|x-v_{1}|+C_{1}|x-v_{1}|

\displaystyle\lesssim\bar{\delta}_{v},

where we again use
b(v_{1})\leq\frac{c_{1}}{2}(v_{1}-\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}})
and Lipschitzness of both
\hat{\phi}_{l}
and
\phi
. The case
x\in[v_{2},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}]
is symmetric.

∎

Consider
\hat{\phi}_{l}
and
\hat{m}
as fixed. Denote the density of
\hat{\phi}_{l}^{-1}(-\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}}))
as
\mathrm{d}_{l}
, it follows that
\mathrm{d}_{l}
is supported on
[\underline{u_{l}},\overline{u_{l}}]
and satisfies the
\kappa
-decay condition as well as the regularity condition defined in Section
A.3
.

A.6
Regret Analysis

The regret can be decomposed into error from estimating
\hat{m}
and from estimating
\hat{F}
. Specifically, we first bound the regret in stage
l\geq 1
. Suppose all the high-probability events defined before hold.

\displaystyle\operatorname{Reg}_{l}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{t\in{\cal T}_{l}}(p_{t}^{*}-p_{t})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in{\cal T}_{l}}\left(\phi^{-1}(-m({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}))+m({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})-\hat{\phi}_{l}^{-1}(-\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}))-\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\overbrace{\sum_{t\in{\cal T}_{l}}\left(\phi^{-1}(-m({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}))-\phi^{-1}(-\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}))\right)^{2}}^{T_{1}}+\overbrace{\sum_{t\in{\cal T}_{l}}\left(\phi^{-1}(-\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}))-\hat{\phi}_{l}^{-1}(-\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}))\right)^{2}}^{T_{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\overbrace{\sum_{t\in{\cal T}_{l}}\left(\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})-m({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t})\right)^{2}}^{T_{3}}

\displaystyle\lesssim|{\cal T}_{l}|\left(\|\hat{\Delta}\|_{\infty}^{2}+|{\cal T}_{l-1}|^{-\frac{\beta-1}{2\beta+1}}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim|{\cal T}_{l}|\epsilon_{m}^{2}(T_{0})+|{\cal T}_{l}|^{\frac{3}{2\beta+1}}.

It is relatively easier to bound
T_{1}
and
T_{3}
. By
\phi^{\prime}\geq c_{1}
, it is straightforward to show

\displaystyle T_{1}\leq\frac{1}{c_{1}^{2}}T_{3},\quad\text{and}\quad T_{3}\leq|{\cal T}_{l}|\cdot\|\hat{\Delta}\|_{\infty}^{2}\leq|{\cal T}_{l}|\cdot\epsilon_{m}^{2}(T_{0}).

To bound
T_{2}
, denote
z_{t}=\phi^{-1}(-\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}))
and
\hat{z}_{t}=\hat{\phi}_{l}^{-1}(-\hat{m}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}))
. By
\hat{\phi}_{l}^{\prime}\geq c_{1}/2
, it holds that

\displaystyle|\phi(z_{t})-\hat{\phi}_{l}(z_{t})|

\displaystyle=|\hat{\phi}_{l}(\hat{z}_{t})-\hat{\phi}_{l}(z_{t})|=\left|\int_{z_{t}}^{\hat{z}_{t}}\hat{\phi}_{l}^{\prime}(z)dz\right|\geq\frac{c_{1}}{2}|z_{t}-\hat{z}_{t}|.

It follows that

\displaystyle|z_{t}-\hat{z}_{t}|\leq\frac{2}{c_{1}}|\phi(z_{t})-\hat{\phi}_{l}(z_{t})|.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle T_{2}=\sum_{t\in{\cal T}_{l}}(z_{t}-\hat{z}_{t})^{2}\lesssim\sum_{t\in{\cal T}_{l}}|\phi(z_{t})-\hat{\phi}_{l}(z_{t})|^{2}.

Conditional on
\hat{m}
, the
z_{t}
are i.i.d. with density satisfying the
\kappa
-decay condition. Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{2}\big|\hat{m}]=|{\cal T}_{l}|\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[|\phi(z_{t})-\hat{\phi}_{l}(z_{t})|^{2}\Big|\hat{m}\right].

Note that by range coverage,
[\underline{z},\bar{z}]\subset[\underline{z}_{l},\bar{z}_{l}]
. It holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\phi(z_{t})-\hat{\phi}_{l}(z_{t})|^{2}\Big|\hat{m}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{v}\bar{\delta}_{v}^{2}x^{\kappa}dx+\int_{v}^{1/2}b^{2}(l_{x}[\underline{\hat{z}_{l-1}},\overline{\hat{z}_{l-1}}])x^{\kappa}dx+\int_{1-v}^{1}\bar{\delta}_{v}^{2}x^{\kappa}dx

\displaystyle\lesssim 2v^{\kappa+1}\bar{\delta}_{v}^{2}+\int_{v}^{1-v}C^{2}|{\cal T}_{l}|^{-\frac{2\beta-2}{2\beta+1}}\log T\,dx+\epsilon_{m}^{2}(T_{0})

\displaystyle\lesssim|{\cal T}_{l}|^{-\frac{2\beta-2}{2\beta+1}}\log T+\epsilon_{m}^{2}(T_{0}),

where the first inequality follows from the
\kappa
-decay condition, the second inequality is due to Claim 3, and the last is by the definition of
v
in (
A.9
) and of
\bar{\delta}_{v}
.

Note that
|\phi(z_{t})-\hat{\phi}_{l}(z_{t})|
is uniformly bounded by
\bar{\delta}_{v}+\epsilon_{m}
, hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\phi(z_{t})-\hat{\phi}_{l}(z_{t})|^{4}\Big|\hat{m}\right]\lesssim\bar{\delta}_{v}^{2}|{\cal T}_{l}|^{-\frac{2\beta-2}{2\beta+1}}\log T+\epsilon_{m}^{4}(T_{0}).

By Bernstein’s inequality, we have with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}[T_{2}\big|\hat{m}]+\sqrt{|{\cal T}_{l}|\cdot\log(1/\delta)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[|\phi(z_{t})-\hat{\phi}_{l}(z_{t})|^{4}\Big|\hat{m}\right]}\ +(\bar{\delta}_{v}+\epsilon_{m})\log(1/\delta)

\displaystyle\lesssim|{\cal T}_{l}|\cdot|{\cal T}_{l}|^{-\frac{2\beta-2}{2\beta+1}}\log T+|{\cal T}_{l}|\epsilon_{m}^{2}(T_{0}).

We can also naively bound the regret incurred in the initial stage as
{\cal O}(T_{0})
.
Adding up the stages yields, with probability at least
1-{\cal O}(T^{-3})
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Regret}(T)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Reg}_{0}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\operatorname{Reg}_{l}

\displaystyle\lesssim T_{0}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}|{\cal T}_{l}|\cdot\epsilon_{m}^{2}(T_{0})+|{\cal T}_{l}|^{\frac{3}{2\beta+1}}\log T

\displaystyle\leq T_{0}+T\epsilon_{m}^{2}(T_{0})+T^{\frac{3}{2\beta+1}}\log T.

As a byproduct, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\operatorname{Regret}(T)\lesssim T_{0}+T\epsilon_{m}^{2}(T_{0})+T^{\frac{3}{2\beta+1}}\log T.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4
.

There exists some constant

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(Q_{0,\pi}^{T},Q_{1,\pi}^{T})\leq C\tilde{\epsilon}^{2}\operatorname{Regret}_{0}(\pi,T).

Proof.

We recursively decompose the KL divergence (denote the trajectory up to

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(Q_{0,\pi}^{T},Q_{1,\pi}^{T})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{0}\log\left(\frac{Q_{0,\pi}^{T}(\tau_{T})}{Q_{1,\pi}^{T}(\tau_{T})}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{0}\log\left(\frac{Q_{0,\pi}^{t}(y_{t}\mid x_{t},p_{t})}{Q_{1,\pi}^{t}(y_{t}\mid x_{t},p_{t})}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{0}\operatorname{KL}\big(Q_{0,\pi}^{t}(\cdot\mid x_{t},p_{t}),Q_{1,\pi}^{t}(\cdot\mid x_{t},p_{t})\big).

(B.1)

For fixed

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}\big(Q_{0,\pi}^{t}(\cdot\mid x_{t},p_{t}),Q_{1,\pi}^{t}(\cdot\mid x_{t},p_{t})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\big(F_{0}(p_{t}-x_{t}\theta_{0})-F_{1}(p_{t}-x_{t}\theta_{1})\big)^{2}}{F_{0}(p_{t}-x_{t}\theta_{0})\big(1-F_{0}(p_{t}-x_{t}\theta_{0})\big)}

\displaystyle\operatorname{Regret}_{0}(\pi,T)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{0,\pi}\big(p_{t}-p_{0}^{*}(x_{t})\big)^{2}.

Lemma 5
.

There exists some constant

\displaystyle\operatorname{Regret}_{0}(\pi,T)+\operatorname{Regret}_{1}(\pi,T)\geq cT\tilde{\epsilon}^{2}e^{-\operatorname{KL}(Q_{0,\pi}^{T},Q_{1,\pi}^{T})}.

We look at the regret stepwise. Define the event

\displaystyle E_{0,t}=\left\{\Big|p_{t}-p_{0}^{*}(x_{t})\Big|<\frac{|p_{1}^{*}(x_{t})-p_{0}^{*}(x_{t})|}{2}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathrel{{\mathop{\geq}\limits}}\frac{\tilde{\epsilon}^{2}}{1024}\sum_{t=2}^{T}e^{-\operatorname{KL}(Q_{0,\pi}^{t-1},Q_{1,\pi}^{t-1})}

\displaystyle\geq cT\tilde{\epsilon}^{2}e^{-\operatorname{KL}(Q_{0,\pi}^{T},Q_{1,\pi}^{T})},

where we have set

\displaystyle\phi_{\iota}^{\prime}(u)

Taking, for instance,

Proof of Claim 2.

Proof of Claim 3.

Appendix C
Linear Model

Proposition 1
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a random vector supported on the unit ball

B_{d}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x\|\leq 1\}

with density
f
.
Assume that

1.

0<m\leq f(x)\leq M<\infty
for all
x\in B_{d}
,

2.

f
is
L
-Lipschitz:
|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})|\leq L\|x-x^{\prime}\|
.

For any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|\theta\|_{2}=1
, define

\mu_{\theta}(t):=\mathbb{E}[X\mid\theta^{\top}X=t],\qquad t\in[-1,1].

Then
\mu_{\theta}(\cdot)
is Lipschitz in
t
, with constant depending only on

(d,m,M,L)
and not on
\theta
.

Proof.

By rotation invariance, it suffices to consider
\theta=e_{d}
.
Write
Y=(U,T)
with
U\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
,
T=Y_{d}\in[-1,1]
.
Conditioning on
T=t
, the support of
U
is the disk

D_{t}=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}:\|u\|\leq r(t)\},\qquad r(t):=\sqrt{1-t^{2}}.

The conditional law of
U
given
T=t
has density

u\mapsto\frac{f(u,t)}{\int_{D_{t}}f(w,t)\,dw},\quad u\in D_{t}.

Thus

\mu(t)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid T=t]=\big(\mu_{\perp}(t),\,t\big),\qquad\mu_{\perp}(t):=\frac{\int_{D_{t}}u\,f(u,t)\,du}{\int_{D_{t}}f(u,t)\,du}.

Rescaling.

Set
u=r(t)z
with
z\in B_{d-1}(1)
, the
(d-1)
-dimensional unit ball. Then

\mu_{\perp}(t)=r(t)\,\frac{A(t)}{B(t)},\quad A(t):=\int_{B_{d-1}(1)}zf(r(t)z,t)\,dz,\quad B(t):=\int_{B_{d-1}(1)}f(r(t)z,t)\,dz.

Bounds.

Since
f\geq m
, we have

B(t)\geq m\,\operatorname{vol}(B_{d-1}(1))=:c_{0}>0.

Moreover, by symmetry
\int_{B_{d-1}(1)}z\,dz=0
, so

A(t)=\int_{B_{d-1}(1)}z\big(f(r(t)z,t)-f(0,t)\big)\,dz,

which yields

\|A(t)\|\leq L\,r(t)\int_{B_{d-1}(1)}\|z\|^{2}dz=:C_{1}Lr(t).

Therefore

\|\mu_{\perp}(t)\|\leq\frac{C_{1}}{c_{0}}Lr(t)^{2}=\frac{C_{1}}{c_{0}}L(1-t^{2}).

Difference bound.

For
s,t\in[-1,1]
,

\mu_{\perp}(t)-\mu_{\perp}(s)=(r(t)-r(s))H(t)+r(s)(H(t)-H(s)),\quad H(t)=\tfrac{A(t)}{B(t)}.

Term I.
Using (
\star
) and
|r(t)-r(s)|r(t)\leq|t^{2}-s^{2}|\leq 2|t-s|
,

\|(r(t)-r(s))H(t)\|\leq C_{2}L|t-s|.

Term II.
A direct Lipschitz estimate on
A(\cdot)
and
B(\cdot)
gives

\|H(t)-H(s)\|\leq C_{3}L|t-s|,

hence
\|r(s)(H(t)-H(s))\|\leq C_{3}L|t-s|
.

Thus

\|\mu_{\perp}(t)-\mu_{\perp}(s)\|\leq C_{*}L|t-s|.

Adding the last coordinate difference
|t-s|
, we obtain

\|\mu(t)-\mu(s)\|\leq(C_{*}L+1)|t-s|,

uniformly over
s,t
. Finally, for general
\theta
, note that
\mu_{\theta}(t)=R^{\top}\mu(t)
for some orthogonal
R
, so the same Lipschitz bound holds.
∎

D.2
Geometric lemma for level-set integrals

We next prove a technical lemma ensuring the differentiability of the level-set
integrals with respect to the level parameter. This will be used to show
that the conditional mean of the normalized error is Lipschitz.

Lemma 7

(Differentiability of level-set integrals)
.

Let
{\cal X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded open set with Lipschitz boundary.
Let
\phi\in C^{2}(\overline{{\cal X}})
and
h\in C^{1}(\overline{{\cal X}})
.
Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open interval such that

\|\nabla\phi(x)\|\geq\gamma>0\quad\text{for all }x\in{\cal X}\text{ with }\phi(x)\in I.

For
t\in I
, define

F(t):=\int_{\{x\in{\cal X}:\ \phi(x)=t\}}\frac{h(x)}{\|\nabla\phi(x)\|}\,d{\cal H}^{d-1}(x),

where
{\cal H}^{d-1}
denotes
(d-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure.
Then
F\in C^{1}(I)
, and there exists
C_{F}<\infty
such that

\sup_{t\in I}|F(t)|+\sup_{t\in I}|F^{\prime}(t)|\leq C_{F}.

Proof.

Fix
t_{0}\in I
. Let
K:=\phi^{-1}(I)\cap\overline{{\cal X}}
, which is compact.
For each
x_{0}\in K
with
\phi(x_{0})=t_{0}
, the implicit function theorem
applies because
\nabla\phi(x_{0})\neq 0
. Thus, there exist:

•

an index
k\in\{1,\dots,d\}
with
\partial_{k}\phi(x_{0})\neq 0
;

•

open sets
U_{x_{0}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
and
J_{x_{0}}\subset I
with
t_{0}\in J_{x_{0}}
;

•

a
C^{2}
-map
\Psi_{x_{0}}:U_{x_{0}}\times J_{x_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

such that, for all
(y,t)\in U_{x_{0}}\times J_{x_{0}}
,

1.

\phi(\Psi_{x_{0}}(y,t))=t
;

2.

for fixed
t
,
y\mapsto\Psi_{x_{0}}(y,t)
parametrizes

\{x\in{\cal X}:\phi(x)=t\}
in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
;

3.

the map
(y,t)\mapsto\Psi_{x_{0}}(y,t)
is
C^{2}
.

Since
K
is compact, we may extract a finite subcover:
there exist points
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}
with associated charts

(U_{\alpha},J_{\alpha},\Psi_{\alpha})
,
\alpha=1,\dots,N
, such that

K\subset\bigcup_{\alpha=1}^{N}V_{\alpha},\qquad V_{\alpha}:=\Psi_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha}\times J_{\alpha}).

Choose a smooth partition of unity
\{\eta_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{N}
subordinate
to this cover: each
\eta_{\alpha}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(V_{\alpha})
,
0\leq\eta_{\alpha}\leq 1
,
and
\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\eta_{\alpha}(x)=1

x\in K
.

Define
h_{\alpha}(x):=\eta_{\alpha}(x)h(x)
.
Then
h_{\alpha}\in C^{1}(\overline{{\cal X}})
and
h=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}h_{\alpha}
on
K
.
It follows that

F(t)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}F_{\alpha}(t),\qquad F_{\alpha}(t):=\int_{\{x:\phi(x)=t\}}\frac{h_{\alpha}(x)}{\|\nabla\phi(x)\|}\,d{\cal H}^{d-1}(x).

For
t\in I\cap J_{\alpha}
, the level set
\{x\in V_{\alpha}:\phi(x)=t\}
is
parametrized by
y\mapsto\Psi_{\alpha}(y,t)
,
y\in U_{\alpha}
, so the area
formula (change of variables on hypersurfaces) yields

F_{\alpha}(t)=\int_{U_{\alpha}}\frac{h_{\alpha}(\Psi_{\alpha}(y,t))}{\|\nabla\phi(\Psi_{\alpha}(y,t))\|}J_{\alpha}(y,t)\,dy,

where
J_{\alpha}(y,t)
is the Jacobian factor (the square root of the
determinant of the Gram matrix of the partial derivatives of
\Psi_{\alpha}

in
y
). It is well known that
\Psi_{\alpha}\in C^{2}
implies
J_{\alpha}\in C^{1}
.

Define

G_{\alpha}(y,t):=\frac{h_{\alpha}(\Psi_{\alpha}(y,t))}{\|\nabla\phi(\Psi_{\alpha}(y,t))\|}J_{\alpha}(y,t),\qquad(y,t)\in U_{\alpha}\times J_{\alpha}.

Because
h_{\alpha},\phi\in C^{1}
and
\Psi_{\alpha}\in C^{2}
, and because

\|\nabla\phi(x)\|\geq\gamma
on
K
, the map
G_{\alpha}
is
C^{1}
on

U_{\alpha}\times J_{\alpha}
.

Moreover, since
{\cal X}
is bounded and
K
is compact,

\Psi_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha}\times J_{\alpha})\subset\overline{{\cal X}}
, and all derivatives
of
\Psi_{\alpha}
up to second order are bounded on

.
Thus there exists
M_{\alpha}<\infty

|G_{\alpha}(y,t)|+|\partial_{t}G_{\alpha}(y,t)|\leq M_{\alpha}\quad\text{for all }(y,t)\in U_{\alpha}\times J_{\alpha}.

Fix a compact subinterval
\tilde{I}\subset I\cap J_{\alpha}
.
Then, for
t\in\tilde{I}
,

F_{\alpha}(t)=\int_{U_{\alpha}}G_{\alpha}(y,t)\,dy.

The function
G_{\alpha}(\cdot,t)
is dominated by the integrable function

, independent of

.

\frac{F_{\alpha}(t+h)-F_{\alpha}(t)}{h}=\int_{U_{\alpha}}\frac{G_{\alpha}(y,t+h)-G_{\alpha}(y,t)}{h}\,dy

Step 3: Lipschitzness of

Define, for

Since

D.5
Feature diversity: polynomial boundary density decay

We now proceed to show feature diversity.
For every
\tilde{m}\in{\cal M}_{K}
, recall that by definition, the derivative of
\tilde{m}_{j}
satisfies
\gamma_{1}\leq\tilde{m}_{j}^{\prime}\leq\Gamma_{1}
.

Proposition 4
.

Let
X
be a random vector supported on
[0,1]^{d}
with density
f_{X}
satisfying

0<c\leq f_{X}(x)\leq C<\infty,\qquad x\in[0,1]^{d},

and let
m_{j}\in C^{1}([0,1])
,
j=1,\dots,d
, satisfy

0<\gamma_{1}\leq m_{j}^{\prime}(u)\leq\Gamma_{1}<\infty,\qquad u\in[0,1].

Define the additive map

m(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}m_{j}(x_{j}),\qquad\underline{u}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}m_{j}(0),\qquad\bar{u}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}m_{j}(1),

and let
Y:=m(X)
. Then
Y
admits a density
f_{Y}
on
[\underline{u},\bar{u}]
and there exist constants

0<k_{1}\leq k_{2}<\infty
, depending only on
(d,c,C,\gamma_{1},\Gamma_{1})
, such that for all

t\in(\underline{u},\bar{u})
,

k_{1}\,\bigl(\min\{t-\underline{u},\;\bar{u}-t\}\bigr)^{d-1}\;\leq\;f_{Y}(t)\;\leq\;k_{2}\,\bigl(\min\{t-\underline{u},\;\bar{u}-t\}\bigr)^{d-1}.

(D.1)

Proof.

Let
S_{t}=\{x\in[0,1]^{d}:m(x)=t\}
. Since
\nabla m(x)=(m_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1}),\dots,m_{d}^{\prime}(x_{d}))
and

\sqrt{d}\,\gamma_{1}\leq\|\nabla m\|\leq\sqrt{d}\,\Gamma_{1}
, the coarea formula gives

f_{Y}(t)=\int_{S_{t}}\frac{f_{X}(x)}{\|\nabla m(x)\|}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(x),\qquad t\in(\underline{u},\bar{u}),

and hence

\frac{c}{\sqrt{d}\,\Gamma_{1}}\,\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(S_{t})\leq f_{Y}(t)\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{d}\,\gamma_{1}}\,\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(S_{t}).

(D.2)

We compute
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(S_{t})
up to multiplicative constants.
Define
v_{j}=m_{j}(x_{j})-m_{j}(0)
and
\Phi(x)=(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})
.
Then
\Phi
is a
C^{1}
diffeomorphism from
[0,1]^{d}
onto
V:=\prod_{j=1}^{d}[0,M_{j}]
,

M_{j}:=m_{j}(1)-m_{j}(0)
, and

m(x)=\underline{u}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}v_{j}.

Thus
S_{t}=\Phi^{-1}(L_{\varepsilon})
, where
\varepsilon:=t-\underline{u}
and

L_{\varepsilon}=\{v\in V:\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}v_{j}=\varepsilon\}.

Fix
\varepsilon_{0}<\min_{j}M_{j}
.
For
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, the box constraints do not bind, and

L_{\varepsilon}=\bigl\{v\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}:\sum_{j=1}^{d}v_{j}=\varepsilon\bigr\}.

A standard computation (parametrizing
v_{d}=\varepsilon-\sum_{j<d}v_{j}
) shows that

\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(L_{\varepsilon})=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{(d-1)!}\,\varepsilon^{\,d-1}.

(D.3)

The map
\Phi^{-1}
is diagonal with entries in
[1/\Gamma_{1},1/\gamma_{1}]
, so its

(d-1)
-dimensional Jacobian on tangent spaces satisfies

\Gamma_{1}^{-(d-1)}\leq J_{d-1}\Phi^{-1}\leq\gamma_{1}^{-(d-1)}.

Applying the area formula for
C^{1}
maps between
(d-1)
-manifolds,

\Gamma_{1}^{-(d-1)}\,\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(L_{\varepsilon})\leq\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(S_{t})\leq\gamma_{1}^{-(d-1)}\,\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(L_{\varepsilon}),\qquad t=\underline{u}+\varepsilon,

and together with (
D.3
) we obtain, for
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
,

c_{1}\,\varepsilon^{d-1}\leq\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(S_{t})\leq c_{2}\,\varepsilon^{d-1},

with
c_{1},c_{2}>0
depending only on
(d,\gamma_{1},\Gamma_{1})
.

Combining with (
D.2
) gives

k_{1}^{(L)}\,(t-\underline{u})^{d-1}\leq f_{Y}(t)\leq k_{2}^{(L)}\,(t-\underline{u})^{d-1},\qquad t\in(\underline{u},\underline{u}+\varepsilon_{0}].

The same argument applied to the functions
\tilde{m}_{j}(u)=-m_{j}(1-u)
yields an identical bound near

t=\bar{u}
, namely

k_{1}^{(U)}\,(\bar{u}-t)^{d-1}\leq f_{Y}(t)\leq k_{2}^{(U)}\,(\bar{u}-t)^{d-1},\qquad t\in[\bar{u}-\varepsilon_{0},\bar{u}).

On the compact interior interval

[\underline{u}+\varepsilon_{0},\bar{u}-\varepsilon_{0}]
, both

\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(S_{t})
and
f_{Y}(t)
are bounded above and below by positive constants depending only on

(d,c,C,\gamma_{1},\Gamma_{1})
, while
\min\{t-\underline{u},\bar{u}-t\}
is bounded away from zero.
Absorbing constants yields (
D.1
).
∎

Additional Experimental Details

We here provide details of constructing the CDF function

in Section

on

Let

Our baseline CDF is the degree-9 polynomial

8

8

It is the quintic smoothstep-5 function widely used in computer vision and graphics. It is the unique degree-9 polynomial that interpolates from 0 to 1 on

with zero derivatives up to order four at both endpoints.

F_{0}(u)\;=\;\mathbf{1}\{t(u)\in[0,1]\}\,\Big[70\,t(u)^{9}-315\,t(u)^{8}+540\,t(u)^{7}-420\,t(u)^{6}+126\,t(u)^{5}\Big],

(F.2)

To add local non-homogeneities, we use a standard

b(s)\;=\;\begin{cases}\exp\!\big(-\tfrac{1}{1-s^{2}}\big),&|s|<1,\\

Fix a number of bumps
