Survival and Predictors of Mortality Among Critically Ill Children Admitted to an Intensive Care Unit in a Resource‐Limited Setting in Southeastern Ethiopia: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Mesfin Wubishet Gurmu, Solomon Gelaye Yinges, Tahir Aman, Betre Shimelis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMortality among critically ill children remains disproportionately high in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), reflecting persistent challenges related to limited critical care capacity, delayed healthcare utilization, and a substantial burden of preventable and treatable illnesses. Despite ongoing efforts to improve pediatric critical care services in Ethiopia, prospective data on survival outcomes and predictors of mortality among critically ill children remain scarce. This study aimed to assess survival outcomes and identify independent predictors of mortality among children admitted to the intensive care unit at ARTH in southeastern Ethiopia.MethodsAn institution‐based prospective cohort study was conducted among 305 critically ill children admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) at ARTH between June 2023 and November 2024. Participants were followed from ICU admission until death or censoring. Survival probabilities were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were compared using the log‐rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of mortality. Adjusted hazard ratios (AHRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsAmong the 305 enrolled patients, 129 (42.3%) died during follow‐up. The overall mortality incidence rate was 7.1 deaths per 100 person‐days (95% CI: 5.86–8.32), and the median survival time was 10 days (95% CI: 7.58–12.42). Most deaths (86.8%) occurred within the first 7 days of ICU admission. Independent predictors of mortality included lack of health insurance (AHR = 2.03; 95% CI: 1.22–3.39), MODS (AHR = 1.73; 95% CI: 1.09–2.73), AKI (AHR = 1.82; 95% CI: 1.13–2.93), anemia at admission (AHR = 1.73; 95% CI: 1.15–2.60), and a modified PIM‐2 score greater than five (AHR = 1.58; 95% CI: 1.03–2.43).ConclusionIn‐ICU mortality among critically ill pediatric patients was alarmingly high, with the greatest risk occurring during the first week of admission. Both clinical severity indicators and socioeconomic factors were independently associated with mortality. These findings highlight the need for early risk stratification, timely targeted interventions, and policies that improve financial access to critical care services to reduce pediatric ICU mortality in resource‐constrained settings.

Introduction

Pediatric intensive care units provide specialized, high‐acuity care for children with life‐threatening conditions requiring advanced monitoring and organ support [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines pediatric critical illness as a severe impairment of airway, breathing, circulation, or neurological function necessitating urgent intervention [2]. Despite remarkable global progress in reducing under‐five mortality, profound disparities persist between high‐income countries (HICs) and low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) [3]. In many LMICs, the overwhelming majority of pediatric deaths are attributable to preventable or treatable conditions, frequently compounded by delayed presentation and inequitable access to quality healthcare [4].

High‐income countries have achieved measurable improvements in PICU outcomes through sustained investment in technological infrastructure, specialized staffing models, and the implementation of evidence‐based clinical protocols [5]. In contrast, sub‐Saharan Africa continues to contend with critical challenges, including inadequate healthcare infrastructure, severe shortages of trained critical care personnel, and restricted access to essential diagnostic and therapeutic technologies [6, 7]. PICU mortality rates vary considerably across settings, with substantially higher rates consistently documented in resource‐limited environments [8, 9, 10, 11, 12], compared to HICs [13, 14]. Ethiopia remains among the countries with the highest burden of under‐five mortality globally [3, 15, 16]. Available Ethiopian studies report PICU mortality rates ranging from 8.5% to 43.8% [11, 17, 18], while a recent systematic review and meta‐analysis reported a pooled estimate of 28.5% [19]. The majority of this existing evidence, however, derives from retrospective or cross‐sectional studies and focuses predominantly on clinical variables, with limited attention to socioeconomic and contextual determinants of mortality [8, 11, 18, 20, 21].

Understanding the full spectrum of predictors of mortality in pediatric ICU settings, encompassing clinical, demographic, and socioeconomic dimensions, is essential for improving outcomes and informing rational resource allocation. In southeastern Ethiopia, prospective data on survival outcomes and their determinants among critically ill children are especially sparse. Therefore, this study aimed to assess survival status and identify independent predictors of mortality among pediatric ICU patients at Asella Referral and Teaching Hospital. The findings are expected to inform evidence‐based interventions and contribute to the strengthening of pediatric critical care services in resource‐limited contexts.

Methods and Materials

Study Design, Setting, and Period

A prospective cohort study was conducted between June 2023 and November 2024 at Asella Referral and Teaching Hospital (ARTH), a tertiary‐level teaching hospital affiliated with Arsi University in southeastern Ethiopia. The hospital is located in Asella town, approximately 175 km southeast of Addis Ababa, and serves a catchment population of over 3.5 million people. As a tertiary referral center, ARTH receives critically ill pediatric patients from primary and district hospitals through an established referral system.

The hospital has 297 beds and provides comprehensive pediatric services, including a 50‐bed inpatient unit, emergency care, and outpatient follow‐up. It manages an estimated 900–1,000 pediatric admissions annually, reflecting a substantial burden of disease in a resource‐limited setting. The hospital's central ICU has six beds and is equipped with mechanical ventilators and basic hemodynamic monitoring. Care is delivered by a multidisciplinary team consisting of physicians, anesthesiologists, residents, interns, and trained nurses. Critically ill children are admitted from the emergency department or transferred from inpatient wards when advanced care is required. The ICU operates without dedicated pediatric intensivists. In addition to clinical care, ARTH is a teaching hospital supporting undergraduate and postgraduate training.

Study Population and Eligibility Criteria

The source population comprised all pediatric patients admitted to the ICU at ARTH. The study population included all eligible pediatric patients admitted to the ICU during the study period. Children aged 1 month to 14 years admitted to the ICU were eligible for inclusion. Patients were excluded if they died within 2 h of ICU admission or if their parent or guardian declined informed consent. Neonates (age < 1 month) were also excluded. Study Variables

Dependent Variable: The dependent variable was ICU mortality, defined as death (event), with all other outcomes treated as censored observations.

Independent variables: The sociodemographic characteristics include age, gender, residence, and the caregiver's educational status and occupation. The clinical characteristics of the patient encompass duration of illness, reason for admission, source of admission, frequency of admission, admission vital signs, mental status, nutritional status, vaccination status, presence of comorbid illness, organ dysfunction, intervention during ICU stay and before admission, PIM2 score, length of ICU stay, health insurance status, and complications after ICU admission.

Operational Definition

Survival status: The final outcome of a patient during the ICU stay, categorized as an event (death) or censored.

Event (death): In‐hospital death occurring during ICU admission.

Censored: Patients discharged alive, transferred to another facility, or lost to follow‐up with unknown outcomes.

Loss to follow‐up: Patients referred to another facility or discharged against medical advice without a known outcome.

Length of ICU stay (LOS): Duration from ICU admission to discharge or death, measured in hours or days.

ICU mortality: The proportion of deaths among all enrolled patients during the ICU stay.

Modified Pediatric Index of Mortality 2 (Modified PIM‐2): An adapted form of the Pediatric Index of Mortality 2 (PIM2), a validated logistic regression–based model used to estimate the risk of death among critically ill children admitted to the ICU. It is calculated using clinical and physiological variables collected at or shortly after admission, including ventilatory status and diagnostic category, to generate an individual predicted probability of mortality [22].

Vaccination status: Defined at ICU admission based on documentation of completion of the national Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) schedule. Children with verified records of full completion were classified as fully vaccinated, while those with incomplete or no documentation were classified as not fully vaccinated.

Co‐morbidity: Defined, in accordance with the International Classification of Diseases (ICD‐11), as the presence of one or more additional diseases or conditions coexisting with the primary diagnosis in a patient [23].

Sample Size and Sampling Technique

The minimum required sample size was calculated using the single population proportion formula, assuming a 95% confidence level (Zα/2 = 1.96), a margin of error of 5%, and an estimated ICU mortality proportion of 23.6% derived from a prior retrospective study conducted in northwestern Ethiopia [24]. This yielded an initial sample of 277 participants. A 10% contingency was added to account for potential loss to follow‐up due to patient transfer or discharge against medical advice, yielding a final minimum required sample size of 305. During the study period, 323 patients were admitted to the ICU. Of these, 305 met the eligibility criteria and were enrolled through consecutive sampling, in which all eligible patients were recruited sequentially until the required sample size was reached (Figure 1).

Data Collection Tool and Procedure

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire developed through a comprehensive review of relevant literature [11, 17, 18, 20, 24, 25, 26, 27] and adapted to align with the specific objectives of this study. The questionnaire captured sociodemographic characteristics, clinical profiles, and treatment‐related variables. Sociodemographic information and medical history were obtained from caregivers within the first 24 h of ICU admission, while clinical and laboratory data were extracted prospectively from medical records until discharge or death. Data collection was carried out by trained pediatric residents and medical interns actively engaged in patient care, under the continuous supervision of senior consultant physicians, including the investigators.

Severity of illness and predicted mortality risk were assessed using the Modified Pediatric Index of Mortality 2 (Modified PIM2). The score was calculated using an online PIM2 calculator [28], based on variables obtained at admission or within the first hour of ICU care, including key physiological and clinical parameters such as systolic blood pressure, pupillary reaction to light, oxygenation status (PaO2 and FiO2), base excess, use of mechanical ventilation, admission category (elective or emergency), postoperative status, and diagnostic risk category. Each variable was used to generate an individual predicted probability of mortality [22]. Due to the unavailability of arterial blood gas analysis at ARTH during the study period, a modified version of the PIM2 score was applied using nine of the original eleven parameters, with PaO2 and base excess excluded. Missing data were managed according to standard PIM2 guidelines, with variables recorded as zero except for systolic blood pressure, which was assigned a default value of 120 mmHg when not documented [22].

Clinical evolution, laboratory findings, diagnoses, and outcomes were documented prospectively until the outcome (death or censoring). Final diagnoses were determined by attending physicians through clinical evaluation, multidisciplinary discussion, and subspecialty consultation. In patients who died before diagnostic confirmation, the most probable provisional diagnosis was recorded. Where multiple conditions coexisted, the primary diagnosis responsible for ICU admission was coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐11) [23]. Hospital‐acquired complications were recorded using standard diagnostic criteria. Acute kidney injury (AKI) was defined according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines as an increase in serum creatinine of > 0.3 mg/dL within 48 h or ≥ 1.5 times the baseline within 7 days [29]. Urine output criteria were not applied owing to inconsistent documentation in the study setting. Multi‐organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) was defined as simultaneous dysfunction of two or more organ systems per the criteria of Goldstein et al. [30].

Data Quality Assurance

Data quality was ensured through multiple measures. The questionnaire was developed in English, translated into Afan Oromo and Amharic by language experts, and back‐translated into English to verify conceptual equivalence. Content validity was strengthened through expert review and iterative refinement. A pretest was conducted in 5% of the sample at Adama Medical College Hospital, and necessary modifications were made based on the findings. Data collectors and supervisors received a standardized 1‐day training session covering study objectives, data collection procedures, ethical principles, and confidentiality requirements. Continuous supervision and daily data review were maintained throughout the collection period to ensure completeness and consistency. Discharge diagnoses and causes of death were reviewed by the research team, with regular reinforcement of ICD‐11 coding standards.

Data Processing and Analysis

Data were reviewed daily for completeness, coded, and entered into Epi‐Data version 4.6, then exported to IBM SPSS Statistics version 27 for analysis. Data cleaning included verification for missing values, inconsistencies, and outliers. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots. Normally distributed variables are reported as means with standard deviations; skewed variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Multicollinearity among independent variables was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with a mean VIF of 1.69 indicating no significant collinearity. Survival probabilities were estimated using Kaplan–Meier methods, and differences between groups were assessed with the log‐rank (Mantel–Cox) test. Variables with p < 0.25 in univariate analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model to identify independent predictors of mortality. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using log–log survival plots and Schoenfeld residuals, while model fit was evaluated using Cox–Snell residuals. Crude and adjusted hazard ratios (CHR and AHR) with 95% confidence intervals were reported, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical Consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethical Review Committee of Arsi University, College of Health Sciences (Protocol No. A/CHS/RC/89/2024). Permission to access clinical data was granted by the hospital administration. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, institutional ethical guidelines, and national research regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from all parents or legal guardians prior to enrollment. All data were fully anonymized prior to analysis, no personal identifiers were retained, and data access was restricted exclusively to the principal investigator.

Results

Participant Enrollment

A total of 323 pediatric patients were admitted to the ICU during the study period. Of these, 305 met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis. Eighteen patients were excluded: four were neonates (< 1 month of age), nine died within 2 h of admission, and five had caregivers who declined consent (Figure 1).

Socio‐Demographic Characteristics of Study Participants

Among the 305 participants, 186 (61.0%) were male, yielding a male‐to‐female ratio of 1.6:1. The mean age of admitted patients was 27.6 months (SD ± 24.8 months), with infants (aged 1–12 months) comprising nearly half of the cohort (152, 49.8%). The majority of patients resided in rural areas (215, 70.5%). Caregivers were predominantly engaged in farming (175, 57.4%), and one‐fifth (61, 20.0%) had received no formal education. A substantial proportion of caregivers (123, 40.3%) had traveled more than 100 kilometers to reach ARTH. The majority (180, 59.0%) reported a monthly household income between 1,001 and 3,000 Ethiopian Birr (ETB). Furthermore, 194 participants (63.6%) lacked health insurance coverage (Table 1).

Clinical Characteristics

The majority of ICU admissions originated from the emergency department (192, 62.9%), followed by the pediatric ward (71, 23.3%), the operating room (24, 7.9%), and external referrals (18, 5.9%). Most patients (251, 82.3%) were admitted with medical conditions. Comorbidities were present in 65 patients (21.3%). Nearly half of the cohort (148, 48.5%) had multi‐organ dysfunction syndrome at admission. Acute kidney injury was identified in 66 patients (21.6%), anemia in 135 (44.3%), and severe acute malnutrition (SAM) in 88 (28.9%). Regarding vaccination status, 149 patients (48.9%) were fully immunized, 118 (38.7%) were partially vaccinated, and 38 (12.4%) were unvaccinated. At admission, 87 patients (28.5%) had severely impaired consciousness (GCS ≤ 8), and 256 (83.9%) were hypoxemic (SpO2 < 90%). The mean PIM‐2 score was 8.6 (± 6.54); 149 patients (48.9%) had a modified PIM‐2 score above five. Overall, 80 patients (26.2%) developed in‐ICU complications, the most common being hospital‐acquired infections (30%) and aspiration pneumonia (28.8%) (Table 2).

Primary Diagnoses and Treatment during ICU Stay

Severe pneumonia with impending respiratory failure was the leading cause of ICU admission, accounting for 90 cases (29.5%), followed by septic shock (40, 13.1%), complicated meningitis (29, 9.5%), postoperative care (25, 8.2%), and pediatric acute respiratory distress syndrome (PARDS; 20, 6.6%) (Table 3). Nearly all patients (301, 98.7%) received antibiotic therapy, and the majority (289, 94.8%) required respiratory support. Fluid resuscitation was administered to 148 patients (48.5%), corticosteroids to 135 (44.3%), and nutritional support to 132 (43.3%). Inotropic support was required in approximately one‐third of patients (Figure 2). Mechanical ventilation was indicated for 266 patients (87.2%); of these, 255 (95.9%) were successfully intubated, while 11 (4.1%) could not be intubated owing to limited ventilator availability. The duration of mechanical ventilation ranged from 1 to 22 days (mean: 7.34 days).

Survival Outcome and Incidence of In‐ICU Mortality

During the follow‐up period, 129 pediatric patients died, yielding an overall in‐ICU mortality rate of 42.3%. The remaining 176 patients (57.7%) were censored: 131 (43.0%) were discharged alive, 38 (12.5%) were discharged against medical advice, and 7 (2.3%) were transferred to other facilities (Figure 3).

Discharge outcomes of pediatric patients admitted to the ICU at ARTH (N = 305).

Participants were followed for a duration ranging from 4 h to 28 days, with a median ICU stay of 5 days (IQR: 2–7). Total follow‐up time accumulated to 1,821 person‐days (60.8 person‐months), yielding a mortality incidence rate of 7.1 deaths per 100 person‐days (95% CI: 5.86–8.32). The overall median time to death was 10 days (95% CI: 7.58–12.42) (Figure 4). The Kaplan–Meier failure function revealed a sharp rise in the cumulative probability of death during the early phase of ICU admission. Approximately 86.8% of all deaths (n = 112) occurred within the first 7 days, and an additional 10.1% (n = 13) occurred between days 7 and 14. Beyond 14 days, the cumulative failure curve reached a near‐plateau, indicating substantially diminished mortality risk among patients who survived the initial critical phase. Censoring events occurred throughout the follow‐up period due to discharge, referral, and self‐discharge (Figure 4). The leading immediate causes of death were multiorgan failure (40.3%), respiratory failure (33.3%), cardiopulmonary failure (10.1%), brain herniation (9.3%), and cardiac arrest (7.0%) (Figure 5).

Model Diagnostics and Log‐Rank Comparisons

The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using global Schoenfeld residual tests. Both individual covariate tests and the global test yielded p‐values > 0.05, confirming the absence of time‐varying effects and supporting the validity of the proportional hazards assumption throughout the follow‐up period. Model fit was assessed using Cox–Snell residuals, which approximated a standard censored exponential distribution; the estimated cumulative hazard closely followed the 45° reference line, confirming adequate model fit. Log‐rank tests revealed significant differences in survival distributions across several covariates. Modified Pediatric Index of Mortality 2 score (Figure 6), anemia at admission (Figure 7), health insurance status, MODS, and AKI were all significantly associated with mortality (p < 0.05) (Table 4).

Predictors of In‐ICU Mortality

In the multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis, five factors remained independently associated with in‐ICU mortality after adjusting for potential confounders. Patients without health insurance had a significantly higher risk of death than those with insurance coverage, with more than twice the hazard of mortality (AHR: 2.03; 95% CI: 1.22–3.39; p = 0.007). Several clinical factors were also identified as significant predictors of mortality. The presence of multiple organ dysfunction syndrome was associated with a 73% increased hazard of death compared with the absence of organ dysfunction (AHR: 1.73; 95% CI: 1.09–2.73; p = 0.019). Similarly, children who developed acute kidney injury (AKI) experienced an 82% higher risk of mortality than those without AKI (AHR: 1.82; 95% CI: 1.13–2.93; p = 0.013). Anemia at admission was independently associated with an elevated risk of death, increasing the hazard of mortality by 73% (AHR: 1.73; 95% CI: 1.15–2.60; p = 0.008). In addition, a modified Pediatric Index of Mortality‐2 (PIM‐2) score greater than five was significantly associated with poorer survival outcomes, corresponding to a 58% higher hazard of death compared with a score of five or below (AHR: 1.58; 95% CI: 1.03–2.43; p = 0.038) (Table5).

Discussion

This prospective cohort study provides robust evidence on survival outcomes and predictors of mortality among critically ill children admitted to a resource‐limited ICU in southeastern Ethiopia. The findings demonstrate a high in‐ICU mortality rate and identify five independent predictors: lack of health insurance, multi‐organ dysfunction syndrome, acute kidney injury, anemia at admission, and a PIM‐2 score above five. The integration of clinical severity indicators with a socioeconomic determinant, health insurance status, represents a particularly important contribution to the literature.

The observed in‐ICU mortality rate of 42.3% (incidence rate: 7.1 deaths per 100 person‐days) is broadly consistent with prior studies from Ethiopia, including those conducted in Addis Ababa [11], Wolayita (41.7%) [26], and Jimma (40%) [31], as well as regional estimates from Tanzania (41.1%) [32]. However, this rate exceeds findings from several other Ethiopian tertiary centers, including Ayder (8.5%) [8], Gondar (32%) [17], Northwest Ethiopia (23.6%) [24], and a pooled national estimate of 28.5% (28.5%) [19]. These discrepancies may be attributable to the absence of a dedicated pediatric ICU, the use of a mixed adult–pediatric general ICU, the absence of specialized pediatric intensivists, and delays in referral and presentation, factors well‐documented to adversely affect outcomes in sub‐Saharan African critical care settings [33]. The mortality rate observed in the present study also considerably exceeds rates reported from other LMICs, including India (10.58%) [34], Nepal (12.6%) [35], Pakistan (12.9%) [36], rural North India (13.40%) [37], South Africa (15.6%) [38], and Mozambique (25%) [9]. Conversely, it remains below the rates reported from Tanzania (49.8%) [12], Malawi (54.3%) [27], Rwanda (50%) [10], and Egypt (50.49%) [39]. These international variations likely reflect differences in healthcare infrastructure quality, ICU staffing ratios, mechanical ventilation capacity, referral systems, and case‐mix severity. The absence of an intermediate (high‐dependency) care unit at ARTH, which would otherwise facilitate early intervention for deteriorating patients before ICU admission, may have further contributed to the high observed mortality [40].

Multi‐organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) was present in 48.5% of patients, a proportion exceeding prior Ethiopian reports (30.7%) [17] and those from India (7.6%) [41] and Egypt (41.3%) [42], and was independently associated with a 73% increase in the hazard of mortality. This is consistent with evidence from the United States [43], Tanzania [12] and Ethiopia [44], and reflects the fundamental pathophysiology of organ dysfunction. As the number and severity of failing organ systems increase, compensatory physiological mechanisms are progressively exhausted, culminating in a cascade of metabolic derangements and irreversible cellular injury. The high prevalence of MODS in this cohort likely reflects delayed ICU admission and suboptimal early resuscitation, both of which are critical determinants of progression to multiorgan failure, underscoring the priority of early identification and goal‐directed management of critically ill children in resource‐limited settings.

Among various disease severity assessment tools used at baseline, the Pediatric Index of Mortality 2 (PIM2) is advantageous because it does not require extensive laboratory investigations and is not influenced by subsequent clinical interventions, as it is calculated within the first hour of ICU admission. This allows for early identification of illness severity, appropriate risk stratification, and timely initiation of necessary interventions, which may also support effective counseling of caregivers of critically ill children [45]. In the present study, a modified version of PIM‐2 was employed due to the unavailability of arterial blood gas analysis at ARTH; consequently, the base excess parameter was excluded from the scoring. While PIM‐2 inherently requires fewer laboratory inputs than alternative scoring systems and can be rapidly administered, the omission of base excess may have attenuated its discriminative precision to some degree. Despite this limitation, patients with a modified PIM‐2 score above five demonstrated a 58% higher hazard of mortality, a finding consistent with evidence from studies conducted in Ethiopian and other low‐ and middle‐income country (LMIC) PICUs [17, 37, 45, 46, 47]. This further supports the ability of the score to identify patients at higher risk of mortality, and it has been validated and widely applied in PICUs globally [48, 49, 50, 51]. Nevertheless, the substantial discrepancy between modified PIM‐2‐predicted and observed mortality rates further highlights the quality and resource constraints characteristic of critical care delivery in this setting. Collectively, these findings reinforce the utility of the modified PIM‐2 as a practical triage and risk‐stratification tool in resource‐limited settings [48, 49, 52].

The higher mortality rates observed among patients with elevated modified PIM‐2 scores likely reflect systemic challenges inherent to critical care in this context. These include late PICU admission attributable to limited bed capacity and ventilator availability, delayed recognition and resuscitation of critically ill children, and suboptimal quality of intensive care. Insufficient numbers of PICU‐trained staff may further contribute to poor patient outcomes [53]. Additional contributing factors include limited health provider knowledge regarding mechanical ventilator use and management, the absence of essential monitoring investigations such as arterial blood gas analysis, and the unavailability of critical medications, all of which likely compound adverse outcomes among ventilated children. Leveraging the modified PIM‐2 score to prioritize care for high‐risk patients, guide prognostication, and direct resources toward those in greatest need represents a pragmatic and evidence‐informed strategy to improve critical care outcomes in low‐income settings.

Acute kidney injury was documented in 21.6% of patients and was independently associated with an 82% higher hazard of mortality, a finding consistent with large multicenter studies demonstrating severe AKI as an independent risk factor for pediatric ICU death [54, 55, 56, 57]. The AKI prevalence in this cohort is lower than that reported in a comparable Egyptian study (56%) [58], a difference potentially attributable to variations in patient demographics, disease severity, comorbidity burden, and the definition applied. Given that serum creatinine criteria alone were used (urine output criteria were not applicable due to documentation limitations), subclinical AKI may have been underestimated. The pathophysiological consequences of AKI, including electrolyte dysregulation, metabolic acidosis, volume overload, and synergistic interaction with sepsis, likely mediate its adverse effect on survival [57, 59].

Anemia was prevalent in 44.3% of the cohort and independently increased the hazard of in‐ICU death by 73%, consistent with evidence from Malawi demonstrating hemoglobin below 10 g/dL as a strong predictor of ICU mortality [27]. Pediatric anemia in sub‐Saharan Africa arises from a complex interplay of malnutrition, infectious diseases (particularly malaria and helminthiasis), and socioeconomic deprivation [60, 61, 62]. Although the precise etiology of anemia could not be ascertained in this cohort, the condition represents a potentially modifiable risk factor. Interventions such as nutritional supplementation, early blood transfusion in appropriately selected patients, and infection prevention may offer opportunities to reduce anemia‐attributable mortality [63]. The high proportion of patients with severe acute malnutrition (28.9%) in this cohort is likely a contributing factor to anemia prevalence and warrants integrated nutritional management as part of PICU care protocols.

The identification of health insurance status as an independent predictor of mortality, with uninsured patients facing more than twice the hazard of death compared with insured counterparts (AHR: 2.03), underscores the intersection of socioeconomic determinants and clinical outcomes in pediatric critical care. Only 36.4% of patients in this cohort were insured, a coverage rate strikingly lower than the 97.7% reported in a Nigerian study [64]. Health insurance facilitates timely care‐seeking, enables access to essential diagnostics and therapeutics, and reduces out‐of‐pocket financial catastrophe that may otherwise prompt early self‐discharge [65]. This finding is consistent with evidence from Tanzania demonstrating improved survival among insured children [65] and highlights the urgent need to expand community‐based health insurance programs, provide targeted subsidies for low‐income and rural families, and intensify public awareness campaigns to increase enrollment. Ethiopia's ongoing legislative and programmatic efforts toward universal health coverage represent a critical policy opportunity in this regard.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

This study employed a prospective cohort design with rigorous survival analysis methodology, which strengthens the reliability of temporal associations and supports causal inference compared with cross‐sectional or retrospective approaches. The use of standardized diagnostic criteria (KDIGO for AKI; Goldstein et al. criteria for MODS; ICD‐11 coding), validated scoring tools (PIM‐2), and model diagnostics (Schoenfeld residuals, Cox–Snell residuals) enhances the methodological rigor of the findings. Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the use of modified PIM‐2 scores (excluding the base excess parameter due to the absence of arterial blood gas analysis) may have reduced the precision of severity prediction. Second, the exclusion of patients who died within 2 h of ICU admission may have resulted in an underestimation of the true mortality burden in this setting. Third, the study did not systematically assess the quality of care delivered or the specific ICU resource constraints encountered during the study period, which may have influenced outcomes. Fourth, determining a single primary cause of death in patients with multiple concurrent diagnoses was inherently challenging and may introduce classification uncertainty.

Conclusion and Recommendations

This study revealed a markedly high in‐ICU mortality rate among critically ill children admitted to the intensive care unit in southeastern Ethiopia, exceeding rates reported in many comparable resource‐limited settings. Mortality was highest during the early phase of ICU admission, emphasizing the critical importance of timely assessment and intervention. Mortality was independently associated with acute kidney injury, anemia at admission, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, elevated modified Pediatric Index of Mortality‐2 (PIM‐2) scores, and lack of health insurance. These findings underscore the combined influence of disease severity and socioeconomic barriers on survival outcomes among critically ill children in resource‐limited settings.

Early risk stratification using the modified PIM‐2 score should be integrated into routine clinical assessment to facilitate the timely identification of high‐risk patients and support appropriate resource allocation. Strengthening the early detection and management of acute kidney injury and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome through close clinical monitoring, timely interventions, and standardized treatment protocols may improve survival outcomes. Given the observed association between anemia and mortality, comprehensive strategies for the prevention, early diagnosis, and management of anemia should be incorporated into pediatric critical care practice. At the health‐system level, efforts to expand health insurance coverage and reduce financial barriers to critical care services are warranted. Investment in pediatric intensive care infrastructure, essential equipment, and specialized workforce training may further enhance the quality of care and improve patient outcomes. Future multicenter prospective studies are recommended to validate these findings, explore additional determinants of mortality, and evaluate the effectiveness of targeted interventions to reduce pediatric ICU mortality in resource‐constrained settings.
