Experimental Investigation of Flame and Emission Characteristics of Hydrogen-Enriched Natural Gas MixturesAn Innovative Approach for India’s City Gas Distribution Network
Authors: Pallav, Kunal Ravindra Gajbhiye, Namrata Bist, Anirbid Sircar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The hydrogen-natural gas blending is proving to be a viable route toward the decarbonization of the city gas distribution (CGD) network in India. In this research work, a full-scale experimental testbed is introduced that has high-pressure hydrogen storage, a programmable logic controller, mass flow controllers, a static gas-mixing manifold, hydrocarbon filtration, and a moisture absorber, as well as multimaterial pipelines (galvanized iron, medium-density polyethylene pipe, and carbon steel) used in the Indian CGD infrastructure. To study the flame structure, thermal efficiency, heat flux, gas consumption, and stability of operation in the CGD simulated conditions. The experiments were performed using 0–30% hydrogen mixtures in methane. Gradual increase in hydrogen enrichment changed the flame from a typical blue flame of methane to a bluish-purple high-temperature profile, and the flame remained stable with the enrichment of the hydrogen up to 25% without flashback or instability, and at 30%, minor instability was observed. Maximum values of the highest heat flux (22,489–22,763 W/m2) were obtained at 18–25% blends of hydrogen, which implied shorter heating time and faster combustion rates. The analysis of emissions indicated a consistent decrease in carbon monoxide (CO), from 112 to 38 ppm, and in carbon dioxide (CO2), from 8.9% to 6.1%. Additionally, with a 0–30% enrichment of hydrogen (H2), there was an increased formation of nitrogen oxides (NO x ) due to higher temperatures. The 25% hydrogen mixture exhibited the best combination of high heat flux, uniform flame geometry, and low gas consumption, while also achieveing the highest (12.6%). These findings illustrate that 18–25% hydrogen is the best blending range of domestic burners in the CGD system, which has better combustion efficiency, less emission intensity, and can blend with available confirmation of the hydrogen-enriched natural gas as a short-term decarbonizing measure of the CGD infrastructure in India.

Introduction

The world’s energy sector must transition to low-carbon alternatives soon to achieve the climate objectives. The usage of natural gas is being opted for as a better energy source with less carbon emission when compared to coal, yet the long-term goal for zero emission goals involves the utilization of energy that does not emit carbon. The power industry should be expanded to supply more demand and reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. Although it is cleaner than coal and oil, natural gas still emits significant carbon dioxide (CO2). Hydrogen (H2) is considered an emerging low-carbon fuel since it does not generate carbon during combustion and has favorable flame properties, including a large safe-combustion mixture, rapid spread, and high diffusivity. − However, the atmospheric addition of hydrogen also elevates the temperature in the flame, which can augment NO x emissions. The behavior of flames and combustion must be completely known to establish safe blending limits and to control emission behavior. Mixing hydrogen with natural gas networks is a viable method of utilizing existing pipelines and minimizing CO2, a strategy actively being explored in large-scale projects in Europe and Asia. − For instance, pilot projects in Europe and other locations, such as the HyDeploy project of the UK, have introduced 5–20% hydrogen to the local gas network to determine the feasibility of the blended fuel. These studies reveal that it is possible to operate with at least 15–20% hydrogen by volume in the mixture without significant modifications in the existing pipelines and the equipment. Beyond the above blend percentage, issues such as material compatibility, flame flashback, and an increase in NO x emission must be carefully considered. It is essential to know the precise effects of hydrogen blending on the flame shape, burning speed, and the emissions produced to determine the safe blending percentage.

The past study involving the application of a hydrogen-NG mixture reported the variations in flame combustion, its structure, and efficiency. The study indicated a stable flame because the laminar flame speed was high, and the speed of reacting to the ignition was faster when natural gas was moderately mixed with hydrogen. , On the same note, better thermal efficiency was reported in spark ignition engines. Similar trends were established using further studies on domestic and industrial burner systems. According to Dagaut et al. and Boulahlib et al., above 20–25% hydrogen content, the visible flame is shorter and less bright, which is associated with a decrease in the formation of soot and an increase in the speed of combustion. , Another finding discovered that the inclusion of 20% H2 in methane-air blends decreased incomplete combustion areas and enhanced energy conversion efficiency. For blends with less than 25% H2, 20–30% CO2 emission reductions without a significant increase in NO x . ,,

A thorough review also highlighted that with only minor technological modifications, low-to-moderate hydrogen concentrations (less than 30%) can be used in end-use devices and pipelines that are already in place. Because of this, H2 blending is a short-term solution that can be used to reduce carbon intensity in natural gas networks while utilizing existing infrastructure. ,

Most of the research focuses on the hydrogen-natural gas blending effects on a large scale, such as in engines and high-pressure burners. However, there are insufficient experimental data on real-time emissions and flame visualization in low-pressure or domestic burner systems. Furthermore, there is still a shortage of experimental data on the flame characteristics for blending spectra from 0% to 30% hydrogen.

This paper examines these issues through a critical observation of the blended hydrogen and natural gas flame. A domestic burner was used and placed in the fume hood to visualize the flame color, length, and luminosity. The blend percentage from 0% to 30% hydrogen by volume was experimented with in the ambient condition. The key performance measurements, Higher Heating Value (HHV), Lower Heating Value (LHV), and the Wobbe index, were also calculated. The experiment aims to correlate the amount of hydrogen contained in a fuel with the appearance of the flame and the kind of pollutants generated, thereby determining the optimal blend percentage that reduces carbon emissions and holds safe combustion properties.

Materials and Methodology

Materials and Fuel Gases

The experimental study was conducted with the assistance of compressed gas cylinders containing hydrogen and natural gas (NG). The composition of natural gas was analyzed based on supplier data, and the composition was primarily composed of methane. The gas cylinders of hydrogen and natural gas are supplied by Reeva Cryogenic Gases, Ahmedabad, Gujarat. The experimental work was performed using compressed gas cylinders of hydrogen (H2, purity 99.99%) and natural gas (NG). The natural gas composition was determined from supplier data and consisted predominantly of methane (CH4, ∼99.99%). Blended mixtures of hydrogen and natural gas were prepared with volumetric hydrogen fractions ranging from 0% to 30% (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30%). Safety provisions included flashback arrestors, nonreturn valves, gas leak pressure safety valves, and ventilation systems to mitigate the high diffusivity and flammability hazards of hydrogen.

In the current experiment, the mass flow rate was adjusted by controlling the flow of hydrogen and methane with MC-series mass flow controllers designed by Alicat Scientific (model: Alicat, MC-10 SLPM-D-DB9M) (Table ). These controllers were supplied with factory calibration traceable to NIST standards and loaded with a hydrogen and methane gas mix using the internal gas select and COMPOSER gas mixing capabilities supplied by the manufacturer. Before every campaign, the mass flow controllers were checked and zeroed under no-flow conditions to minimize offset errors.

With experiments, operating pressure and temperature were kept near calibration reference conditions (∼1 atm and 25–35 °C), and thus, reduced further uncertainty due to environmental effects. The ratios based on the individually manipulated hydrogen and natural gas mass flow rate were estimated to give the hydrogen blending ratio. The total uncertainty in the volumetric hydrogen fraction is known to lie within ranges from ±0.027% absolute (at 5% H2) to ±0.096% absolute (at 30% H2) of all uncertainties of each of the controllers and the propagation of measurement error across the feature of the entire blending range. This degree of uncertainty is low enough to fix the 5% blending intervals and establish with confidence 18–25% as the best operating range.

Methodology

Preparation of Hydrogen-Enriched Natural Gas Blends

The required blends were prepared by controlling the individual gas flows via the MFCs. The mixed fuel mixture was specified on a volumetric basis in each level of hydrogen enrichment (0–30% H2 in 5% steps). The carbon level balance calculations were made to determine the carbon dioxide (CO2) concentration with a known methane fraction in the fuel mixture and measured combustion products under steady-state conditions. Hydrogen contained no carbon; therefore, the decrease in methane fraction directly controlled the hypothetical CO2 generation at each blending ratio.

Stoichiometry Control of Combustion

All the ratios of hydrogen blending were performed at constant pressure (21 mbar) and fixed total volumetric fuel flow rate (10 SLPM) with the burner. The air momentum and no external air flow manipulations were added to the experimentation. Though the global equivalence ratio (ø) was not measured directly, the air entrainment mechanism did not vary, bearing in mind that all tests involved the same conditions. Thus, the difference in combustion performance comes mostly due to the changes in fuel composition (H2 fraction) and not due to the alterations in the conditions of air supply.

Emission Measurement and Instrumentation

A portable multigas monitor (ATS 103M) was used to measure the emissions of the flue gases, and in this case, the electrochemical (EC) sensors were used to detect the CO and NO x gases. The steady-state combustion conditions were measured at diffusion-based sampling, and readings were obtained after stabilizing at every hydrogen blend ratio.

Because hydrogen does not have carbon, the decrease in carbon input as the proportion of hydrogen increases directly affects theoretical CO2 formation. The results of the calculated CO2 values, hence, indicate the declining carbon intensity of the blended fuel mixed with hydrogen enrichment. In the current steady-state atmospheric burner configuration, where the mixing of exhaust gases was homogeneous and the gradients of concentration were low about the measurements of the emissions, diffusion-based sampling was believed to be suitable. The obtained results are explained along with the visualized qualities of a flame presented in Section .

Results and Discussion

Flame Characteristics

At lower hydrogen concentrations (<10%), the flame structure remained comparable to that of pure NG, exhibiting a yellowish-blue color. As hydrogen concentration increased beyond 15%, the flame color shifted toward bluish, with reduced luminosity and visible soot suppression. At higher H2 concentrations (25–30%), the flame length decreased significantly, indicating enhanced burning velocity.

The visual parameters of combustion quality, soot formation, and flame stability at hydrogen-natural gas blends are important and are measured with flame appearance. The current paper has conducted a visual study of flame color and structure in relation to the different ratios of hydrogen blending (0–30%) by volume at a fixed pressure of 3 bar with the help of a premixed domestic burner. The gradual change in the color of the flame, which is deep blue to pale violet, is associated with a gradual increase in hydrogen content in the mixed fuel. The diffusion of the hydrogen improves the laminar burning velocity and diffusivity, leading to shorter and cleaner flames, which are low in luminosity. The loss of the yellow streak means that the soot precursors are lower, and soot intermediates are fully oxidized. At increased hydrogen contents (25%), the flame is highly stable, with negligible carbonaceous luminosity, and approaches optimal premixed combustion, also being found in practical burner experiments. ,−

Relation between Flame Visual and Emission Characteristics

The visual changes of the flame are directly proportional to the measured trends in emissions. When the yellow luminous areas are reduced and the flame becomes blue, the concentration of CO and CO2 decreases as per the literature, and the increased oxidation completeness was verified. The emission of the blue flame is the chemiluminescence of strong CH and OH, which is a result of complete combustion. On the other hand, NO x concentrations rose slightly over 20% H2 because of increased local temperatures and faster reaction rates. Therefore, flame imaging is a qualitative confirmation of emission data that 20–25% hydrogen will give maximum low-carbon, high-efficiency combustion.

Heating Values and Wobbe Index

The HHV and LHV of the blends decreased marginally with increasing hydrogen content due to the lower volumetric energy density of H2 compared to that of NG. However, the Wobbe index showed minor variations, remaining within acceptable interchangeability limits up to 25% H2. This indicates that hydrogen blending at these levels does not compromise the burner compatibility.

Measured Emission Characteristics

The emission characteristics of hydrogen-natural gas (H2–NG) mixtures were tested at constant combustion rates of hydrogen content of 0% to 30% in 5% steps. Directly measured carbon monoxide (CO) and nitrogen oxides (NO x ) were measured using electrochemical sensors, and the concentration of carbon dioxide (CO2) was calculated by carbon balance by determining the dependence on the known fuel composition and the available measurements of the combustion products.

Carbon Monoxide (CO)

The amount of carbon monoxide emissions was found to decrease in a monotonic and steady way as the amount of hydrogen added was increased. The concentrations of CO measured fell to 112 ppm at 0% H2, 103 ppm at 5% H2, 95 ppm at 10% H2, 84 ppm at 15% H2, 61 ppm at 20% H2, 38 ppm at 25% H2, and 69.6% at 30% H2, suggesting a very high degree of enhancement in the degree of oxidation as the amount of hydrogen is added.

The more pronounced decrease, further to 15–20% H2, is associated with the increased flame reactivity and the greater stability of combustion experimentally. The effect of hydrogen enrichment is to accelerate the reaction and a higher oxidation of the intermediate species, which then decreases the generation of incomplete products of combustion, such as CO.

Carbon Dioxide (CO2)

The concentration of carbon dioxide showed that it decreased systematically with the increase in the concentration of hydrogen. The carbon balance analysis was done on the reduction in the percentage of methane in the blended fuel to determine the CO2 values. The calculated CO2 concentration was found to be reduced by 0% H2 to 8.9, 8.0, 7.6, 7.1, 6.5, and 6.1 at 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, 25%, and 30% H2 v/v, respectively. This gradual decrease is an indication of the decreasing input of carbon into the combustion process, as hydrogen replaces methane in the mixture. The volumetric fraction plays a significant role in the carbon intensity of blended fuels with hydrogen enrichment. As the volumetric fraction increases, there is a greater amount of carbon available for oxidation. This means that a higher volumetric fraction may lead to less certainty in the reduction of the carbon intensity for the fuel blend, despite the presence of hydrogen enrichment.

Nitrogen Oxides (NO x )

Unlike carbon monoxide (CO) and carbon dioxide (CO2), nitrogen oxide (NO x ) emissions increase with higher hydrogen content in the fuel. The measured NO x emissions increased from 52 ppm (at 0% H2) to 98 ppm (at 30%) H2 enrichment. These values increased within the typical permissible limits for domestic gas burners, which are generally in the range of 100–150 ppm, depending on regional standards and operating conditions.

According to major international regulations, currently, there are no specific limits on NO x emissions for gas cooking appliances in homes. The EU Ecodesign Regulation (EU 66/2014) sets energy efficiency standards for ovens, hobs, and range hoods but does not cover NO x emissions from gas hobs. The European Public Health Alliance (EPHA) has asked the European Commission to set an NO2 emission limit of 6 ng/J (about 8–12 ppm) during the ongoing review of this regulation (EPHA, 2024; Commission Regulation (EU), 2014). For unmodified natural gas burners in homes, manufacturers typically report NO x emissions of between 50 and 100 ppm (Table ). On the basis of these figures:

Overall, the emission behavior throughout the complete blending spectrum (0–30% H2) shows a notable decrease in carbon-based emissions (CO and CO2) and a small increase in NO x at the more extreme blending ratios. The H2 range of 20–25% is an attractive working range, which locally records high carbon emission reduction with a relatively moderate increase of NO x compared to 30% enrichment.

Fuel Consumption and Efficiency

The addition of hydrogen improved flame stability and allowed stable combustion at leaner equivalence ratios, thereby improving the overall combustion efficiency. It was concluded that the 25% H2 blend was identified as an optimum case with flame stability, lower CO/CO2 emissions, and reasonable levels of NO x .

Infrastructure Compatibility

Since hydrogen blending modifies the rate of flame and heating value, existing infrastructure might require minor adjustments with higher blends (≥20%), including burner nozzle modifications and safety monitoring systems. However, blends up to 25% seem compatible with the current natural gas infrastructure.

Experimental Process and Performance Analysis

A controlled water-heating experiment was done to obtain a quantitative measurement of the thermal performance of hydrogen–methane gas blends. One liter of demineralized water (DM) was taken in a stainless vessel (diameter 23 cm) and heated with a domestic burner with a constant supply pressure of 21 mbar (standard pressure for domestic household supply). The operating conditions of the burner regulator and opening were maintained the same across all of the trials so that there would be consistency in the amount of heat input. The ratios of hydrogen blending that were tested were 0% to 30% hydrogen (v/v) (Figure ), and the volumetric gas flow rate was kept constant throughout all blends. A calibrated digital thermometer was used to measure the time required to heat the water. The experiments were repeated to achieve reproducibility, and the respective thermal performance indicators, such as the heating rate, thermal efficiency, and heat flux, were obtained on the basis of the measured time–temperature profile.

Heating Time at Varying H2 Blend %

In the case of 0% H2, the heating time recorded was 140.94 s, which was used as a baseline. Further, adding the H2 %, the heating time increased gradually, and at 18%, it dropped dramatically to 167.79 s and went up to the lowest possible 161.79 s at 25%, which shows the most stable and strong combustion regime. This shortening of heating time to a maximum of 25% H2 indicates an improved rate of heat release (dQ/dt) and improvement in the flame surface contact. At this point (30% H2), the heating time increased up to 322.94 s, and the flame shifted to an overlean flame, resulting in intermittent combustion and lack of uniformity in the heat flux. This indicates that the 18–25% addition of hydrogen provides the most efficient thermal performance in terms of reactivity and stability.

Nonmonotonic Behavior of Heating Time

The initial increase in the heating period in the low ratios of hydrogen blending (usually below 10–12% H2) could be explained by the simultaneous effect of combustion chemistry, flame structure, and heat transfer properties at the early phases of hydrogen addition. With small hydrogen enrichments, partial replacement of methane with hydrogen causes a decrease in volumetric calorific value of the resulting blended fuel since hydrogen has a low volumetric energy density. Subsequently, at a fixed volumetric flow rate, the net heat release rate fed to the burner is somewhat minimized, and this also leads to a slight increase in heating time.

At the same time, when the hydrogen concentration in the mixture is low, the speed and power of combustion enhancement are not adequate yet to offset the low energy concentration enhancement of the mixture. In this regime, therefore, there is still limited enhancement in terms of flame stabilization and heat transfer efficiency. Moreover, the addition of small amounts of hydrogen changes the structure of the local flame and the thickness of the reaction zone. This alteration can cause a slightly extended or less compact flame at low hydrogen content, causing less provision of heat to the cooking vessel and surfaces around it. Exceeding specific threshold levels (around 18–25% H2) in increasing the degree of hydrogen attainment, the positive results of the hydrogen process prevail. Thermal Efficiency Analysis

The thermal efficiency (η) was calculated using eq : 5 η=QoutQin×100% where Q out is the useful heat absorbed by the water and Q in is the heat energy supplied by the fuel. Q out is calculated as follows: Q out = m·C p ·ΔT, where m = 1 kg is the mass of water heated, C p = 4.186 kJ kg–1 °C–1 is the heat capacity of water, and ΔT is the change in the water temperature. Q in is calculated as follows: Q in = V H2 ·LHVH2 + V CH4 ·LHVCH4 , where V gas and LHVgas (gas = H2, CH4) are the gas volume and lower heating value, respectively. The calorific value of blended gas (CVmix) can be calculated as CVmix = (1 – x)­CVCH4 + xCVH2 , where x = 0–0.3 is the hydrogen blending fraction. Typical values of CVgas are CVCH4 = 35.8 MJ/m3 and CVH2 = 12.7 MJ/m3. CVgas = Calorific value of gas where gas is H2, CH4

After a slight decrease in thermal efficiency with less than 10% H2 (η = 7%), the thermal efficiency of the appliance sharply increases between 15 and 25% H2. This increment is brought about by the high diffusivity and velocity of the flame of hydrogen, which increases the efficiency of combustion and energy transfer (Figure ).

Varying thermal efficiency with H2 blend % (v/v) with CH4.

Due to the low flame velocity of CH4 (0.38 m/s) and partial use of the combustion energy, the efficiency was about 7.4% with pure CH4 (0% H2). The addition of 10% H2 reduced the efficiency somewhat due to a slight disturbance in the premixing and local quenching locations. However, when H2 was added past 15%, the combustion kinetics grew much betteraccelerated ignition, increased flame temperatures, and increased diffusion resulted in a steep climb in efficiency to 12.6% at 25% H2. This has been enhanced by the increased reactivity and diffusivity of hydrogen, which encourages the homogeneous mixing of the fuel-air mixture and acceleration of the radical formation (H, OH, O). Such radicals enhance oxidation reactions, leading to an increase in the thermal conversion. Over 25% H2, efficiency has dropped slightly with 9%, suggesting that surplus H2 formed overlean zones of lower stability and uneven heat release. This tendency is consistent with the existing study in which H2 enrichment with more than 30% led to an inefficient combustion process caused by unsteady combustion and flame lift-off.

Gas Consumption Analysis

In experiments utilizing pure methane, a gas consumption volume of 28.09 L was recorded. With the further increase in H2 content to 10–18%, the gas consumption decreased progressively by ∼29 L, representing an almost 12% overall reduction in energy consumption. The minimum gas consumption took place at 18% H2 and was associated with the highest rate of consumption efficiency and rate of heating. This is because of the increased laminar flame velocity and the reduced density of H2 (approximately 2.9 m/s vs 0.38 m/s in the case of CH4) that supports more rapid mixing and energy release. The optimization of flame structure at 18–25% H2 reduces incomplete combustion and heating losses. Above 30%, the volume of gas consumption further rises (∼33.6 L), presumably due to waving and balancing flame and lessening convective contact with the vessel base. The observed results of similar trends of gas consumption have also been reported in a previous study.

Heat Flux Analysis

In this study, the effective heat transfer area between the flame and the base of the cooking vessel is identified as the circular section of the vessel’s bottom that directly faced the flame, which was used to calculate the heat flux. Specifically, the area of the transfer of heat projected was estimated with a flat circular surface. All of the experiments were done using the same vessel, burner, and other supporting arrangements, and the vessel geometry, position of the burner, and spacing between the pot and burner were not modified during the experimental campaign. Hence, the effective area of heat transfer was the same in every case. The heat flux was calculated by dividing the useful heat transferred to water in the vessel by the effective heating area and the length of time, as shown in eq : 6 øQ=QoutA·t where Q out is the heat transferred to water, A is the surface area of the vessel base exposed to flame, and t is the time to reach the final temperature.

Combustion Reaction of H2–CH4

Stoichiometric combustion of H2–CH4 blends may be represented by summing up oxidation reactions of methane, the main component of natural gas, and hydrogen by their respective molar ratios in the blend. For a mixed fuel containing x mole fraction of methane (CH4) and y mole fraction of hydrogen, where x + y = 1, the generalized chemical reaction for complete combustion can be expressed as 7 xCH4+yH2+(2x+2y)O2→xCO2+(2x+y)H2O This formula is derived directly from the stoichiometric oxidation reactions of methane (eq ) and hydrogen (eq ): 8 CH4+2O2→CO2+2H2O 9 H2+12O2→H2O This broad-based equation is common in hydrogen–methane (H2–CH4) combustion models, as it describes the high oxidizer demand and the alternative routes of water formation when hydrogen is added. It also offers the basis for how to compute the adiabatic flame temperature, equivalence ratio, heating values (HHV/LHV), and trends of emission (CO2 reduction and NO formation). However, hydrogen does not introduce additional carbon into the fuel mixture; consequently, an increase in the hydrogen-to-fuel ratio (y) theoretically leads to a decrease in the CO2 yield of the mixture. This theoretical framework is supported by experimental data, indicating the CO2 emissions reduction with the increase in H2 content. ,

Discussion

This experimental study serves as a whole evaluation of the combustion behavior, thermal performance, and anticipated trends of the emission profile of hydrogen–methane blends (ranging from 0 to 30% H2 by volume) in a domestic burner setup that represents a depiction of the Indian CGD networks. The findings provide critical insights into the viability of hydrogen enrichment as a decarbonization approach in the short term and reveal key parameters that determine the optimal operational efficiency.

Evidence-Based Findings

The major basis of establishing 18–25% H2 as the ideal blending range is based on quantitative measurements, which are heating time (Figure ), thermal efficiency (Figure ), heat flux (Figure ), gas consumption (Figure ), and Wobbe Index calculations (Table ). The data was gathered through direct measurements using calibrated instruments and with repeated trials to ensure accuracy and reliability.

The qualitative insights from flame visualization (Figure and Table ) offer significant complementary data to our analysis. This technique visually confirms the changes in the regime of combustion, helps to interpret the trends of quantitative indicators, and serves as a useful field indicator to evaluate in seconds. However, it is important to note that flame images were not served as quantitative optical diagnostics for measuring parameters like temperature or species concentration. This difference will make sure that the main conclusions on optimal blending are based on the robust analysis of quantification, with the observations of flames contributing to the physical phenomena.

Characteristics of Flames and Changes in the Combustion Regime

The gradual shift of a yellowish-blue flame of methane to a noticeable bluish-purple flame with an increasing proportion of hydrogen (Figure and Table ) indicates that there are basic changes in the combustion chemistry and the mechanism of the heat release. Such a visual change is congruent with an improvement in OH and CH chemiluminescence, which is typical of fully oxidized reactions. , The measured decrease in flame length at hydrogen fractions greater than 20% is directly related to the fact that hydrogen has a greater laminar flame velocity (around 2.9 m/s with H2 compared to 0.38 m/s with CH4) and a greater burning velocity.

Notably, under test conditions (21–24 mbar supply pressure, natural air aspiration), a steady combustion without flashback or blow-off was ensured up to 30% H2. The results of this study have the potential to be relevant in practice since they indicate that current domestic burner designs can tolerate moderate hydrogen enrichment without any safety issues in the near future. The stability window (0–30% H2) is consistent with other literature sources; on the other hand, as Boulahlib et al. (2021) and Erdener et al. (2023) reported identical stability limits of premixed domestic burners under natural aspiration. ,

The progressive loss of yellowish light and lessening soot as the hydrogen concentration increased suggest inhibited soot formation material and further oxidation of carbon intermediates. With a 25% H2 ratio, the flame has low carbonaceous brightness, and it tends to have a perfect of the practical burner tests. ,

Quantitative and Qualitative Measurements in the Present Study

In the present study, the major conclusions are primarily based on direct quantitative measurements, while flame visualization is used as a supporting qualitative diagnostic tool to aid physical interpretation. There is a need to differentiate the evidence of the findings made in this research. The main basis of determining the best blending range as 18–25% H2 is based on quantitative measurements, such as the heating time (Figure ), thermal efficiency (Figure ), and Wobbe index calculations (Table ). These data sets were collected by means of direct measurements using calibrated equipment and trials.

The flame visualization (Figure and Table ) has a complementary qualitative purpose of combustion, to interpret the quantitative optical diagnostics (e.g., temperature or species concentration measurements). This difference would ensure that the essential findings on the best blending are based on rigorous quantitative analysis, whereas flame observation contributes to physical interpretation.

Emission Trends (Discussion Based on Literature)

According to the existing literature correlations and in agreement with the flame characteristics, hydrogen enrichment will decrease the CO2 and CO formation in higher oxidation and partial removal of the carbon-based fuel, and NO x will increase moderately at a higher hydrogen ratio because of the high flame temperature. Future work should involve direct emission measurement.

Fuel Interchangeability

The calculation of the Wobbe Index with references to the standards of the Indian regulation (PNGRB: 48.0–52.0 MJ/m3) has proven that all blends containing ≥15% H2 are within the acceptable limits. A marginally elevated Wobbe Index of pure natural gas is corrected by adding hydrogen to enhance the fuel interchangeability.

Flashback Risk and Burner Safety Implications

Although the hydrogen has a higher laminar flame speed (∼2.9 m/s) in comparison to methane (∼0.38 m/s), the increased flashback risk occurs as the hydrogen fraction rises. In this study, no flashback was observed at any H2 content from 0 to 30% under the test conditions (21 mbar, 10 SLPM, natural aspiration). The flame remained stable and attached for all blends up to 25% H2, with only slightly unstable observed at 30% H2 (Table ). The absence of flashback is attributed to sufficient forward flow velocity, burner design characteristics, and natural aspiration limits. Based on these findings, H2 blending up to 25% by volume is recommended as safe for unmodified domestic burners in Indian CGD networks. For blends containing 30% H2 or more, burner modifications (smaller port diameters, flame arrestors) are recommended. These findings support 18–25% H2 as the optimal and safe blending range for short-term decarbonization of the CGD sector.

Conclusion

This experimental study investigated the properties of combustion and thermal efficiency of hydrogen-natural gas blends (0–30% H2 by volume) in a domestic burner unit that resembled Indian CGD networks. The following are the key findings:

Flame Characteristics

Visualization of flame represented a gradual change in the yellowish-blue flame of methane to the bluish-purple hue indicative of higher hydrogen content in the fuel mixture. This shift signifies complete combustion, attributed to the high laminar flame speed of hydrogen (2.9 m/s vs 0.38 m/s of CH4). The burner sustained the constant combustion with no flashback or blow-off up to 30% H2 content, proving that the current burner designs are capable of moderate levels of hydrogen enrichment.

The nonmonotonic heating time behavior (increasing 5–10% H2, decreasing 15–25% H2, increasing sharply at 30% H2) indicates shifting to a regime of calorific-value domination to combustion-intensified domination.

Thermal Performance

Quantitative measurements have shown 18–25% H2 as the optimal blending range, with 25% H2 providing the best balance of performance metrics. The nonmonotonic heating time behavior, which resulted in the increase at 5–10% H2, decrease at 15–25% H2, and sharp increase at 30% H2, represents a transition from calorific-value-dominated to combustion-intensification-dominated regimes.

Emission Trends

There were no direct emissions measurements. According to the literature correlations, it has been anticipated that hydrogen enrichment is expected to decrease in CO and CO2 (60–85% reduction at 20–30% H2 concentration) and increase in NOx by 20–40% due to high flame temperatures. These patterns are graphically in line with the properties of flames that are observed.

Fuel Interchangeability

The Wobbe index in comparison with Indian standards (PNGRB: 48.0–52.0 MJ/m3; BIS IS 15323:2022:49.0–51.5 MJ/m3) affirmed that any blends ≥15% H2 are within acceptable limits. Notably, hydrogen blending redresses the marginally elevated Wobbe index of the pure natural gas (53.49 MJ/m3) to enhance the fuel interchangeability.
