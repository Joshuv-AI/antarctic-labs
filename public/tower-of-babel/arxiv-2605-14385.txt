The inverse curve shortening flow on the hyperbolic plane
Ivan Krznarić, Rafael López

Abstract
. We study the inverse curve shortening flow in the hyperbolic plane ℍ 2 \mathbb{H}^{2} . We classify all solitons with respect to parabolic and conformal vector fields of ℍ 2 \mathbb{H}^{2} . In the upper half-plane model of ℍ 2 \mathbb{H}^{2} , we prove that parabolic solitons are all graphs on the y y -axis, whereas conformal solitons are graphs on the x x -axis. We study the concavity of these solitons and when they approach the coordinate axes.

1.
Introduction

Let
\gamma\colon I\to\mathbb{H}^{2}
be a curve in the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, parametrized by arc length. If
J\subset\mathbb{R}
is an interval with
0\in J
, a one-parameter family of parametrized curves
\{\gamma_{t}\colon I\to\mathbb{H}^{2},t\in J\}
evolves by the inverse curve shortening flow (for abbreviation, ICSF) with initial condition
\gamma
, if

(1)

\left\{\begin{split}\left(\frac{\partial\gamma_{t}}{\partial t}\right)^{\perp}&=-\frac{1}{\kappa(t,\cdot)}N(t,\cdot),\\
\gamma_{0}&=\gamma.\end{split}\right.

where
\kappa(t,\cdot)
and
N(t,\cdot)
denote the curvature and the unit normal vector of
\gamma_{t}
, respectively, and
\left(\frac{\partial\gamma_{t}}{\partial t}\right)^{\perp}
is the normal component of the velocity
\gamma_{t}
. In arbitrary dimension, the inverse mean curvature flow (IMCF) in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
was first studied by Huisken, who proved that compact, mean convex, star-shaped hypersurfaces converge to round spheres under the flow
[
9
]
: see also
[
5
,
6
]
. Non-compact solitons of the IMCF are known to exist
[
7
,
12
]
.
The IMCF has been considered when the ambient space is the hyperbolic space:
[
1
,
10
,
11
,
13
]
, and it has been extended to other ambient spaces
[
4
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]

In this paper, we aim to provide additional results about non-compact solutions to the ICSF in the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
by studying solitons with respect to parabolic and conformal vector fields. After introducing some basic facts of the upper half-plane model of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
(Section
2
), we present explicit examples of the ICSF for curves of constant curvature (Section
3
). The last two sections of the paper are devoted to the study of solitons of the ICSF with respect to specific vector fields. Consider the upper half-plane model of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
with standard coordinates
(x,y)
. In Section
4
, we classify the solitons of the ICSF corresponding to the parabolic vector field
\partial_{x}
. These solitons are all graphs on the
y
-axis; some of them approach the ideal boundary of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
orthogonally, while others have horizontal asymptotes. In Section
5
, we classify solitons of the ICSF corresponding to the conformal vector field
\partial_{y}
. These solitons are concave graphs on bounded intervals on the
x
-axis with infinite slope at the end points.

2.
Preliminaries

In this paper, we work with the upper half-plane model of the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, namely,

\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon y>0\right\}

endowed with the Riemannian metric

\langle,\rangle=\frac{dx^{2}+dy^{2}}{y^{2}}.

The ideal boundary
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\infty}=\{y=0\}\cup\{\infty\}
is the one-point compactification of the horizontal line
y=0
.

Let
\gamma\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
be a regular curve, parametrized as
\gamma(s)=(x(s),y(s))
. There is a well-known relationship between
the curvature
\kappa
of the curve
\gamma
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
with the hyperbolic metric, and its Euclidean curvature
\kappa_{e}
, since the hyperbolic metric is conformal to the Euclidean one. The unit normal vector
N
and the hyperbolic curvature
\kappa
of
\gamma
are given by

(2)

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y\frac{(-y^{\prime},x^{\prime})}{\sqrt{x^{\prime 2}+y^{\prime 2}}},

(3)

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y\kappa_{e}+\frac{x^{\prime}}{\sqrt{x^{\prime 2}+y^{\prime 2}}},\qquad\kappa_{e}=\frac{x^{\prime}y^{\prime\prime}-x^{\prime\prime}y^{\prime}}{(x^{\prime 2}+y^{\prime 2})^{3/2}}.

As a consequence, in the upper half-plane model of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, curves of constant hyperbolic curvature coincide with the curves of constant Euclidean curvature in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. Thus, they are precisely the intersections of straight lines and Euclidean circles with
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. The classification of these curves according to the curvature
\kappa
is the following:

(1)

Geodesics (
\kappa=0
). These are vertical lines and half-circles orthogonal to the line
y=0
.

(2)

Equidistant lines (
0<\kappa<1
). These are non-geodesics arcs of circles supported on the line
y=0
and non-vertical, non-horizontal straight lines.

(3)

Horocycles (
\kappa=1
). These are circles tangent to the line
y=0
or horizontal lines.

(4)

Hyperbolic circles (
\kappa>1
). These are Euclidean circles entirely contained in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
.

We now introduce the notion of a soliton of the ICSF by a Killing vector field. Let
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
be a Killing vector field. Then there is a one-parameter group of isometries
\Phi\colon\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{H}^{2}
,
\Phi=\Phi(p,t)
, such that

(4)

\frac{d}{dt}\Phi(p,t)=X(\Phi(p,t)),\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Phi(p,0)=p,

for all
p\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let
\{\gamma_{t}\colon I\to\mathbb{H}^{2}\colon t\in J\}
be a solution of the ICSF (
1
). We say that
\gamma=\gamma_{0}
that is
a soliton
with respect to
X
if
\Phi^{-1}\circ\gamma_{t}
is stationary in the normal direction. Since each
\Phi_{t}
is an isometry of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, we have
N(t,s)=\Phi(N(s),t)
, where
N(s)
denotes the unit normal vector of
\gamma_{0}
. Therefore,

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\kappa(t,s)}&=-\langle\frac{\partial\gamma_{t}}{\partial t},N(t,s)\rangle\\
&=-\langle\frac{d}{dt}\Phi(\gamma_{0},t),\Phi(N(s),t)\rangle\\
&=-\langle X(\gamma_{t}),\Phi(N(s),t)\rangle.\end{split}

Since this identity holds for all
t\in J
, setting
t=0
gives

\frac{1}{\kappa(s)}=-\langle X(\gamma_{0}),N(s)\rangle.

We have therefore proved the following characterization of solitons of the ICSF with respect to a Killing vector field, which we will be using throughout the paper.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
be a Killing vector field. A curve
\gamma
is a soliton of the ICSF with respect to
X
if and only if

(5)

\frac{1}{\kappa}=-\langle N,X\circ\gamma\rangle,

where
\kappa
and
N
are the curvature and the unit normal vector of
\gamma
, respectively.

The space of Killing vector fields on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
is three-dimensional. A basis is given by

(6)

\begin{split}X_{1}(x,y)&=\partial_{x},\\
X_{2}(x,y)&=x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y},\\
X_{3}(x,y)&=(y^{2}-x^{2})\partial_{x}+2xy\partial_{y},\end{split}

where
\{\partial_{x},\partial_{y}\}
denotes the canonical basis of
\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
. These vector fields correspond to one-parameter subgroups of isometries of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. In complex notation
z=x+iy\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\subset\mathbb{C}
, and taking into account (
4
), the description of these subgroups is the following.

(1)

Parabolic translations.
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}
, where
P_{t}(z)=z+t
. Then

X_{1}(z)=\frac{d}{dt}{\Big|}_{t=0}P_{t}(z).

(2)

Hyperbolic translations.
\mathcal{H}=\{H_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}
, where
H_{t}(z)=e^{t}z
. Then

X_{2}(z)=\frac{d}{dt}{\Big|}_{t=0}H_{t}(z).

(3)

Rotations.
\mathcal{R}=\{R_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}
, where
R_{t}(z)=\frac{\cos(t)z-\sin(t)}{\sin(t)z+\cos(t)}
. Then

X_{3}(z)=\frac{d}{dt}{\Big|}_{t=0}R_{t}(z).

Following this terminology, we say that a curve
\gamma
is a
parabolic
(resp.
hyperbolic
,
rotational
) soliton of the ICSF if
\gamma
is a soliton with respect to the vector field
X_{1}
(resp.
X_{2}
and
X_{3}
).

In the space
\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
, there is also a distinguished vector field given by

Y(x,y)=\partial_{y}.

This vector field is conformal, since its Lie derivative satisfies

\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{y}}\langle,\rangle=-\frac{2}{y}\langle,\rangle.

Observe that the integral curves of
Y
are geodesics of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, represented by vertical lines. If we define
\Phi\colon\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{H}^{2}
by
\Phi(x,y,t)=(x,y+t)
, then
\frac{d}{dt}\Phi(x,y,t)=\partial_{y}
.
Consider now a curve
\gamma(s)=(x(s),y(s))
in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. We say that
\gamma
is a soliton with respect to the vector field
Y
if
\gamma_{t}(s)=\Phi(\gamma(s),t)=(x(s),y(s)+t)
is a solution of (
1
). Then, we have

\displaystyle N(t,s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(y(s)-t)\frac{(-y^{\prime}(s),x^{\prime}(s))}{\sqrt{x^{\prime}(s)^{2}+y^{\prime}(s)^{2}}},

\displaystyle\kappa_{t}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(y(s)-t)\kappa_{e}(s)+\frac{x^{\prime}(s)}{\sqrt{x^{\prime}(s)^{2}+y^{\prime}(s)^{2}}}.

Since
\frac{\partial\gamma_{t}}{\partial t}=(0,1)
, then

\langle\frac{\partial\gamma_{t}}{\partial t},N(t,s)\rangle=\frac{x^{\prime}(s)}{(y(s)-t)\sqrt{x^{\prime}(s)^{2}+y^{\prime}(s)^{2}}}.

Thus, (
1
) is

\frac{x^{\prime}(s)}{y(s)+t}=-\frac{x^{\prime}(s)^{2}+y^{\prime}(s)^{2}}{x^{\prime}(s)+(y(s)+t)\kappa_{e}\sqrt{x^{\prime}(s)^{2}+y^{\prime}(s)^{2}}}.

In particular, for
t=0
, we have that
\gamma
satisfies

(7)

\frac{1}{\kappa}=-\langle N,\partial_{y}\circ\gamma\rangle,

In the theory of MCF for surfaces in hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, solitons of conformal vector fields were studied in
[
2
,
17
]
. See also
[
3
]
when the ambient space is
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
.

3.
Examples of the ICSF: case of constant curvature

We now present several examples of the ICSF for curves of constant curvature. Note that the evolution of a geodesic under ICSF is not defined, since its curvature satisfies
\kappa=0
. Also, we say that the solution
\gamma=\gamma(t,s)
of the ICSF is an
ancient solution
if it is defined on the time interval of the form
\langle-\infty,T\rangle
, where
0\leq T\leq\infty
. In other words, ancient solutions are defined for all times
t<0
.

Example 3.1

(Flow of hyperbolic circles)
.

A hyperbolic circle
\gamma
with center
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
and radius
R
coincides with the Euclidean circle whose center is located at
(a,\cosh(R))
and whose radius is equal to
b\sinh(R)
. After applying an isometry of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, we may assume, without loss of generality, that
(a,b)=(0,1)
. A parametrization of
\gamma
is

\gamma(s)=\sinh(R)\cdot(\cos(s),\sin(s))+(0,\cosh(R)).

The curvature of
\gamma
is
\kappa=\coth(R)
.

For the ICSF, it is natural to assume that the flow remains within the family of circles centered at
(0,1)
. Thus we consider the evolving family of curves

\gamma_{t}(s)=\sinh(r(t))\cdot(\cos(s),\sin(s))+(0,\cosh(r(t))).

We compute the terms appearing in the flow equation (
5
). The curvature is given by
\kappa=\coth(r(t))
, while the unit normal vector
N
and the time derivative
\frac{\partial\gamma_{t}}{\partial t}
satisfy

\begin{split}N&=-(\sinh(r)\sin(s)+\cosh(r))\cdot(\cos(s),\sin(s)),\\
\frac{\partial\gamma_{t}}{\partial t}&=r^{\prime}\cdot(\cosh(r)\cos(s),\sinh(r)+\cosh(r)\sin(s)).\end{split}

Hence

\langle\left(\frac{\partial\gamma_{t}}{\partial t}\right)^{\perp},N\rangle=-r^{\prime}(t),

and so the equation (
5
) reduces to the following Cauchy problem:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle r^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\coth(r)},\\
r(0)=R.\end{cases}

The solution is

r(t)=\sinh^{-1}(\sinh(R)e^{t}).

Since
r(t)\geq 0
for all
t
, the ICSF exists for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, this is an ancient solution of the ICSF. As
\lim_{t\to\infty}r(t)=\infty
, it follows that the hyperbolic circle expands infinitely under the ICSF. On the other hand, as
\lim_{t\to-\infty}r(t)=0
it follows that hyperbolic circles shrink to a point as
t\to-\infty
.

Example 3.2

(Flow of horocycles)
.

Let
\gamma
be the horocycle given by the Euclidean circle

\gamma(s)=R(\cos(s),\sin(s))+(0,R),

which is tangent to the line
y=0
at
(0,0)
. Assuming that the ICSF preserves the family of horocycles with ideal point
(0,0)
, we consider the evolving curves

\gamma_{t}(s)=r(t)\cdot(\cos(s),\sin(s))+(0,r(t)).

Here
\kappa=1
, and

\langle\left(\frac{\partial\gamma_{t}}{\partial t}\right)^{\perp},N\rangle=-\frac{r^{\prime}(t)}{r(t)}.

Thus (
5
) becomes

\begin{cases}r^{\prime}=r,\\
r(0)=R.\end{cases}

The solution is

r(t)=R\cdot e^{t}.

The ICSF is defined for all time
t\in\mathbb{R}
, so this is also an ancient solution of the ICSF. Since
\lim_{t\to\infty}r(t)=\infty
, we see that the horocycles also expand infinitely under the ICSF.

Example 3.3

(Flow of equidistant lines)
.

Let
0<c<R
. Consider the equidistant line given by the arc of the Euclidean circle

\gamma(s)=R\left(\cos(s),\sin(s)\right)+(0,c),\qquad-\frac{c}{R}\leq\sin s\leq 1.

Its curvature is
\kappa=\frac{c}{R}
. We assume that the ICSF remains within the family of equidistant lines sharing the same ideal boundary point. Let
0<c(t)<r(t)
, and impose the condition

r(t)^{2}-c(t)^{2}=R^{2}-c^{2}:=a^{2},\quad a>0.

Define

\gamma_{t}(s)=r(t)\cdot(\cos(s),\sin(s))+(0,\sqrt{r(t)^{2}-a^{2}}).

Then

\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{r(t)^{2}-a^{2}}}{r(t)},\qquad\langle\left(\frac{\partial\gamma_{t}}{\partial t}\right)^{\perp},N\rangle=-\frac{r^{\prime}(t)}{\sqrt{r(t)^{2}-a^{2}}}.

Thus (
5
) reduces to the same ODE as before:

\begin{cases}r^{\prime}=r,\\
r(0)=R.\end{cases}

Hence

r(t)=Re^{t}.

The condition
r(t)^{2}>a^{2}
requires the solution to satisfy

t>t_{\text{min}}:=\log\left(\frac{a}{R}\right).

Since
t_{\text{min}}>-\infty
, this solution is not an ancient one. Also, note that at
t_{\text{min}}
we have
r(t_{\text{min}})=a
. Thus, as
t\to t_{\text{min}}
the family
\gamma_{t}
converges to the geodesic
s\mapsto a(\cos(s),\sin(s))
, which is precisely the geodesic to which the initial curve
\gamma
is equidistant to. On the other hand, as
t\to\infty
the equidistant line expands infinitely.

We summarize the previous examples in the following statement.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\gamma
be a curve in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
with constant curvature
\kappa>0
.

(1)

If
\gamma
is a hyperbolic circle, then the ICSF is formed by hyperbolic circles sharing the same center as
\gamma
.

(2)

If
\gamma
is a horocycle, then the ICSF is formed by horocycles sharing the same ideal boundary point as
\gamma
.

(3)

If
\gamma
is an equidistant line, then the ICSF is formed by equidistant lines sharing the same ideal boundary point as
\gamma
.

Furthermore, the ICSF of all of the curves above is expanding, while the backward time limits are the common center, the ideal boundary point and a geodesic curve respectively.

4.
Parabolic solitons of ICSF

In this section, we study parabolic solitons of the ICSF. We point out that two-dimensional parabolic solitons of the MCF were already classified in
[
2
]
.

Let
\gamma(s)=(x(s),y(s))
be a curve in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, parametrized by Euclidean arc length. Then there is a smooth function
\theta=\theta(s)
such that

x^{\prime}(s)=\cos\theta(s),\qquad y^{\prime}(s)=\sin\theta(s).

Notice that
\kappa_{e}=\theta^{\prime}
and from (
3
), we have

\kappa=y\kappa_{e}+\cos\theta.

Moreover,
N=y(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)
, so

\langle N,X_{1}\rangle=-\frac{\sin\theta}{y}.

Equation (
5
) is therefore equivalent to

(8)

\begin{cases}x^{\prime}=\cos\theta\\
y^{\prime}=\sin\theta\\
\displaystyle\theta^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}-\frac{\cos\theta}{y}.\end{cases}

A consequence of the fact that
\sin\theta(s)
cannot take the value
0
is the following.

Proposition 4.1
.

Any parabolic soliton to the ICSF is a graph on the
y
-axis.

Our first result establishes the fact that parabolic solitons of the ICSF cannot have constant curvature.

Proposition 4.2
.

There are no parabolic solitons of the ICSF with constant curvature.

Proof.

Let
\gamma(s)=(x(s),y(s))
be a parabolic soliton with constant curvature. Then
\gamma
defines
\theta(s)
such that
(x(s),y(s),\theta(s)(
is a solution of system (
8
). Then the Euclidean curvature
\kappa_{e}=\theta^{\prime}
is constant. We distinguish between two cases:

(1)

Case
\theta^{\prime}=0
. Then
\theta(s)=\theta_{0}
for some constant
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, and the soliton
\gamma
can be written as

\gamma(s)=s(\cos\theta_{0},\sin\theta_{0})+(a,b),

with
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sin\theta_{0}\not=0
.
Substituting into the last equation of (
8
), we obtain

s\sin\theta_{0}+b=\sin\theta_{0}\cos\theta_{0},

which implies
\sin\theta_{0}=0
, but this is a contradiction. Therefore, this case is impossible.

(2)

Case
\theta^{\prime}=c\not=0
. Then
\theta(s)=cs
and the soliton can be written as

\gamma(s)=\frac{1}{c}(\sin(cs),-\cos(cs))+(x_{0},y_{0}).

Substituting into (
8
) yields

cy_{0}\sin(cs)+\frac{\cos(cs)}{c}-y_{0}=0.

Since this holds for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we deduce
1/c=0
, which is a contradiction. Therefore, this case is impossible as well.

∎

Now we want to study geometric properties of parabolic solitons. Let
\gamma(s)=(x(s),y(s))
be a solution of (
8
). Note that the function
x
is completely determined by
\theta
so it suffices to solve (
8
) for functions
y,\theta
. Therefore, the system (
8
) is equivalent to the system

(9)

\begin{cases}y^{\prime}=\sin\theta\\
\displaystyle\theta^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}-\frac{\cos\theta}{y},\end{cases}

Since the system (
9
) is independent of
x
, we can prescribe the initial condition
x(0)
without loss of generality. Moreover, there are no equilibrium points.

In order to determine the phase space associated to (
9
), we first note the following result, which is a standard consequence of the uniqueness theorem for ODEs, and which tells us that we can consider
\theta
to be contained within the interval
(0,\pi)
.

Proposition 4.3
.

If
\alpha(s)=(y(s),\theta(s))
is a solution of (
9
), then

\overline{\alpha}(s)=(y(-s),\theta(-s)-\pi)

is also a solution of (
9
).

Therefore, the phase space associated to the system (
9
) is equal to the set

\Theta=\left\{(y,\theta)\colon y>0,\theta\in(0,\pi)\right\}.

To determine geometric properties of parabolic solitons, we are going to be studying the orbits of the system (
9
). Let
\alpha(s)=(y(s),\theta(s))
be any solution of the system (
9
) defined on the interval
I=(s_{m},s_{M})
, which we assume to be maximal.

Each solution
\gamma(s)
of (
8
), with its corresponding function
\theta(s)
, has an associated unique orbit
\alpha_{\gamma}(s)=(y(s),\theta(s))
of (
9
). Standard ODE theory ensures that through every point of
\Theta
passes a unique orbit, and distinct orbits do not intersect.
Let us observe that the system (
9
) has no equilibrium point in
\Theta
and thus, a phase space analysis of the dynamics of the orbits using these points cannot be done.

The points in which the Euclidean curvature vanishes are determined by
\theta^{\prime}=0
, and they all lie on the curve

\Gamma\colon y=\frac{1}{2}\sin(2\theta)\subseteq\Theta.

Since
y>0
, the curve
\Gamma
appears in the phase space
\Theta
only when
\theta\in(0,\pi/2)
.

Using the curve
\Gamma
, we can determine the regions of the phase space in which the coordinate functions
y,\theta
of orbit
\alpha
are strictly monotone. First of all, since
\theta\in(0,\pi)
we have that
y^{\prime}=\sin\theta>0
so
y
is strictly increasing on the whole phase space. As for the function
\theta
, we have that

\theta^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}-\frac{\cos\theta}{y}=\frac{y-\frac{1}{2}\sin(2\theta)}{y\cdot\sin\theta}.

Thus, the monotonicity regions are

\left\{\begin{split}&\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})\colon\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\theta\text{ increases if and only if}&y>\frac{1}{2}\sin(2\theta),\\
\theta\text{ decreases if and only if}&y<\frac{1}{2}\sin(2\theta).\\
\end{array}\right.\\
&\theta\in(\frac{\pi}{2},\pi)\colon\mbox{$\theta$ is increasing}.\end{split}\right.

In the study of solutions of (
9
), we fix initial conditions. As we have seen, different choices for
x(0)
give the same solution
\gamma(s)=(x(s),y(s))
, up to a horizontal translation. Thus, we can assume, without loss of generality, that
x(0)=0
. We fix the initial condition on
\theta
to be
\theta(0)=\pi/2
. Later, we will analyze other initial conditions for the function
\theta=\theta(s)
. The classification is obtained in Theorems
4.7
and
4.10
, respectively. In both results, the geometric properties of the parabolic solitons depend on the initial condition
y(0)=y_{0}
.

4.1.
Solutions of (
8
) with initial condition
\theta(0)=\pi/2

Let
\gamma(s)=(x(s),y(s))
be a solution of (
8
) with initial conditions

x(0)=0,\quad y(0)=y_{0},\quad\theta(0)=\frac{\pi}{2}.

Let
\alpha_{\gamma}(s)=(y(s),\theta(s))
be the corresponding orbit of the system (
9
), with the initial condition
\alpha_{\gamma}(0)=(y_{0},\pi/2)
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\alpha=(y,\theta)
be an orbit of the system (
9
) with the initial condition
\alpha(0)=(y_{0},\pi/2)
. Then
\lim_{s\to s_{M}}\alpha(s)=(Y,\pi)
, where
Y<\infty
.

Proof.

At
s=0
, we have that
\theta^{\prime}(0)=1>0
so there is an interval around
s=0
on which the function
\theta
is increasing ant, thus, for
s>0
close to
0
, the orbit
\alpha_{\gamma}(s)
is contained in
\theta\in(\pi/2,\pi)
. This implies that the functions
y
and
\theta
are increasing for
s>0
. Let
\lim_{s\to s_{M}}(y(s),\theta(s))=(Y,\theta^{\ast})
.

By Proposition
4.1
, we know that
\theta
is a function of
y
, i.e.
\theta=\theta(y)
. Therefore, we have

\frac{d\theta}{dy}=\frac{d\theta}{ds}\left(\frac{dy}{ds}\right)^{-1}=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\left(\frac{1}{\sin\theta}-\frac{\cos\theta}{y}\right)=\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta}-\frac{\cot\theta}{y}.

Note that
d\theta/dy
and
d\theta/ds
have the same sign, so the regions where those functions are monotone coincide.

Fix some
\overline{y}>y_{0}
and denote
\overline{\theta}=\theta(\overline{y})
. Since
d\theta/dy>0
on
(y_{0},Y)
, we have that
\theta(y)
is an increasing function on that interval. Because of that we have
\theta(y)\in(\pi/2,\pi)
for all
y>\overline{y}
and
\theta(y)>\overline{\theta}
for all
y>\overline{y}
. Since the function
\theta\mapsto\cot\theta
is negative and decreasing on
(\pi/2,\pi)
, for all
y>\overline{y}
we have
\cot\overline{\theta}>\cot\theta
. If
m:=-\cot\overline{\theta}>0
, then

-\frac{\cot\theta}{y}>\frac{m}{y}.

This yields

\frac{d\theta}{dy}=\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta}-\frac{\cot\theta}{y}>\frac{m}{y}

for all
y>\overline{y}
. Integrating from
\overline{y}
to
y
,
y>\overline{y}
, we have

\theta(y)-\overline{\theta}\geq m\log(y)-m\log(\overline{y}).

If
Y
were
\infty
, letting
y\to\infty
we get
\pi\geq\lim_{y\to\infty}\theta(y)\geq\infty
, which is a contradiction. This proves that
Y<\infty
.

On the other hand, if it were
\lim_{y\to Y}\theta(y)=\theta^{\ast}<\pi
, then also
\lim_{s\to s_{M}}\theta(s)=\theta^{\ast}
. Since
Y<\infty
, we could extend the orbit past
Y
by considering the initial condition to be equal to
(Y,\theta^{\ast})
, but this is impossible since the interval
I
is maximal. Therefore,
\lim_{s\to s_{M}}\theta(s)=\lim_{y\to Y}\theta(y)=\pi
.

∎

On the other hand, for
s<0
and near

\alpha_{\gamma}(s)
is contained in the region
\theta\in(0,\pi/2)
so there are two possible cases:

(1)

If

intersects the curve

at some

, then
\theta(s)
is decreasing on the interval
(s_{m},\overline{s})
, here

.

(2)

If
\alpha_{\gamma}
does not intersect the curve
\Gamma
, then
\theta(s)
is increasing on the whole interval

.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\alpha_{\gamma}=(y,\theta)
be an orbit of the system (
9
) with the initial condition
\alpha_{\gamma}(0)=(y_{0},\pi/2)
. If
\alpha_{\gamma}
is of Type I, then there exists unique
\hat{s}<0
such that
\alpha_{\gamma}(\hat{s})\in\Gamma
.

Proof.

We know that
\alpha_{\gamma}
meets
\Gamma
at least one point. Without loss of generality, let that
\hat{s}
is the largest value of parameter
s
such that
\alpha_{\gamma}(s)
intersects
\Gamma
. By contradiction, suppose that there is some
\overline{s}<\hat{s}
such that
\alpha_{\gamma}(\overline{s})\in\Gamma
as well. Denote
\overline{y}=y(\overline{s})
and
\hat{y}=y(\hat{s})
. Note that
\overline{y}<\hat{y}
since
y
is an increasing function, and note that
\alpha_{\gamma}(s)
is contained inside the region determined by
\Gamma
for all
s\in(\overline{s},\hat{s})
. Because of that,
\theta(y)
is decreasing on the interval
(\overline{y},\hat{y})
.

Since
\frac{d\theta}{dy}(\overline{y})=0
, there are two possible cases:

(1)

\overline{y}
is a local extrema of the function
\theta(y)
. Since
\theta(y)
is decreasing on the interval
(\overline{y},\hat{y})
, the point
\overline{y}
has to be a local maximum. Hence, there exists an interval on which
\alpha_{\gamma}(s)
is contained inside the region determined by
\Gamma
and on which the function
\theta
is increasing, but this is impossible since
\theta
is decreasing whenever
\alpha_{\gamma}
is inside
\Gamma
.

(2)

\overline{y}
is an inflection point of the function
\theta(y)
. Hence, for
y<\overline{y}
, the orbit
\alpha_{\gamma}(y)
should be outside
\Gamma
and the function
\theta(y)
should be decreasing. This is impossible since
\theta
is increasing in that case.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\alpha_{\gamma}=(y,\theta)
be an orbit of the system (
9
) with the initial condition
\alpha_{\gamma}(0)=(y_{0},\pi/2)
. If
\alpha_{\gamma}
is of Type I, then
\lim_{s\to s_{m}}\alpha_{\gamma}(s)=(0,\pi/2)
.

Proof.

Since
\alpha_{\gamma}
is a Type I orbit, and by Lemma lem-unique-intersection, there exists a unique
\hat{s}<0
such that
\alpha_{\gamma}(\hat{s})\in\Gamma
. Denote
\hat{y}:=y(\hat{s})
and
\hat{\theta}:=\theta(\hat{s})
. Moreover, we know that
\alpha_{\gamma}
is contained inside the region determined by
\Gamma
for all
y\in(0,\hat{y})
. Then
\lim_{s\to s_{m}}\alpha_{\gamma}(s)=(0,\theta_{\ast})
, where
\theta_{\ast}\in(\hat{\theta},\pi/2]\subseteq(0,\pi/2]
.

Suppose that
\theta_{\ast}\neq\pi/2
. By Proposition
4.1
, we can consider
\theta
as a function of
y
, i.e.
\theta=\theta(y)
for
y\in(0,\hat{y})
. Since
\alpha_{\gamma}
is contained inside
\Gamma
, we know that
\theta(y)
is a decreasing function on the interval
(0,\hat{y})
. Therefore, we have
\theta(y)\in(\hat{\theta},\theta_{\ast})
for all
y\in(0,\hat{y})
.

Since the functions
\theta\mapsto\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta}
and
\theta\mapsto\cot\theta
are positive and continuous on
(0,\pi/2)
, they are in particular bounded on
(0,\hat{y})
, since for
y\in(0,\hat{y})
, we have
\theta(y)\in(\hat{\theta},\theta_{\ast})
and
\theta_{\ast}\neq\pi/2
. So, there are constants
A,B>0
such that

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta(y)}&\leq A,\quad y\in(0,\hat{y})\\
\cot(\theta(y))&\geq B,\quad y\in(0,\hat{y}).\end{split}

In particular, for all
y\in(0,\hat{y})
we have

\frac{d\theta}{dy}=\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta}-\frac{\cot\theta}{y}\leq A-\frac{B}{y}.

Integrating from
y
to
\hat{y}
,
y<\hat{y}
, we get

\theta(y)-\hat{\theta}\leq A(\hat{y}-y)-B\log(\hat{y})+B\log(y).

Letting
y\to 0,
we get

\theta_{\ast}-\hat{\theta}\leq-\infty,

which is an obvious contradiction. Hence,
\theta_{\ast}=\pi/2
and so

\lim_{s\to s_{m}}\theta(s)=\frac{\pi}{2}.

∎

We are in conditions to describe geometrically the parabolic solitons of the ICSF.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
\gamma
be a parabolic soliton of the ICSF solving (

approaches the line

is a horizontal asymptote of

(2)

If
y_{0}>H
, then
\gamma
is a graph on a bounded interval

on the
y
-axis (type II). Writing

as

, then

is concave and satisfies

\lim_{y\to M}x^{\prime}(y)=-\infty,\quad\lim_{y\to m}x^{\prime}(y)=\infty.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.1
, we know that
\gamma
is a graph on the

4.4
implies that one end of
\gamma
is asymptotic to the horizontal line

.
Let
\alpha_{\gamma}
be the orbit corresponding to
\gamma
. Since an orbit may or may not intersect
\Gamma
, there is
H>0
such that
\alpha_{\gamma}
intersects
\Gamma
whenever

(1)

Case

\alpha_{\gamma}
as

(2)

Case

5.
Conformal solitons of the ICSF

In this section, we study conformal solitons for the ICSF in
\mathbb{H}^{2}

\gamma(s)=(x(s),y(s))
be a curve in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, parametrized by Euclidean arc-length. If
\gamma

7

\theta

\left\{\begin{split}x^{\prime}&=\cos\theta\\

Firstly, we prove that conformal solitons cannot have constant curvature.

Proposition 5.1
.

There are no conformal solitons of the ICSF in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
with constant curvature.

Proof.

Since the hyperbolic curvature is constant if and only the Euclidean curvature is constant, then the function
\theta^{\prime}
is constant.

(1)

Case
\theta^{\prime}=0
. Then
\gamma
can be parametrized as

\gamma(s)=s(\cos\theta_{0},\sin\theta_{0})+(a,b).

The third equation of the system (
10

. Thus,
\sin\theta_{0}=0
and
\cos^{2}\theta_{0}+b=0
. This gives
b=-1
. However, we have
\gamma(s)=(s\cos\theta_{0}+a,b)
and thus,

should be positive. This is a contradiction.

(2)

Case
\theta^{\prime}=c\neq 0
. Then
\gamma
can be parametrized as

\gamma(s)=\frac{1}{c}(\sin(cs),-\cos(cs))+(a,b).

The third equation of the system (
10
) is then equivalent to
\cos(cs)(\frac{1}{c}-cb)-b=0
. Thus
b=0
and
\frac{1}{c}-cb=0
. This is a contradiction.

m<0
such that

-\cot\theta<m,\quad y\in(0,\hat{y}).

Taking these bounds into account, it follows that on the interval
(0,\hat{y})
we have

\frac{d\theta}{dy}\leq\frac{m}{y}.

Integrating from
y
to
\hat{y}
we get

\hat{\theta}-\theta(y)\leq m\log(\hat{y})-m\log(y).

Letting
y\to 0
we get

\hat{\theta}-\theta_{\ast}\leq-\infty,

which is an obvious contradiction. Therefore, it must be that
\theta_{\ast}=\pi/2
.
∎

We now prove the classification theorem for conformal solitons of the ICSF in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
\gamma
be a conformal soliton of the ICSF solving (
10
) with initial conditions
(x(0),y(0),\theta(0))=(0,y_{0},\pi/2)
. Then
\gamma
is a concave graph on the
x
-axis over some bounded interval
(x_{m},x_{M})
, where the limits of the tangents at the endpoints are vertical.

Proof.

From Proposition
5.4
, we know that
\gamma
is a graph on the
x
-axis. Let
y=y(x)
. Due to Lemma
5.6
, the angles between the tangent of
\gamma
and the
x
-axis approach
\pm\pi/2
at each of the ends. This implies that the domain of
y(x)
is bounded, say, of the form
(x_{m},x_{M})
and that the tangents of
y(x)
at the limit endpoints is
\infty
. To check the concavity of
y(x)
, the chain rule gives
y^{\prime}(x)=\tan\theta
and

y^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{\theta^{\prime}}{\cos^{3}\theta}=-\frac{y+\cos^{2}\theta}{y\cos^{4}\theta}<0.

∎

5.2.
Solutions of (
11
) with initial condition
\theta(0)>0
.

Consider an orbit
\alpha_{\gamma}=(y,\theta)
of (
11
) with the initial conditions
y(0)=y_{0}>0
and
\theta(0)=\theta_{0}>0
. Then
\theta^{\prime}(0)=-\frac{1}{\cos\theta_{0}}-\frac{\cos\theta_{0}}{y_{0}}<0
and from Lemma
5.5
,
\theta(s)<0
. This implies that
y(s)
is increasing. Let
\lim_{s\to s_{m}}\alpha_{\gamma}(s)=(y_{\ast},\theta_{\ast})
. By symmetry, we also have
\lim_{s\to s_{M}}\alpha_{\gamma}(s)=(y_{\ast},-\theta_{\ast})
.

We describe the conformal solitons in this case
\theta(0)
, proving that these conformal solitons fall in the family of Theorem
5.7
.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
\gamma
be a conformal soliton of the ICSF solving (
10
) with initial conditions
(x(0),y(0),\theta(0))=(0,y_{0},\theta(0))
, with
\theta(0)>0
. Then
\gamma
can be reparametrized such that
\theta(0)=0
. In consequence, its geometric properties are described in Theorem
5.7
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that there exists some
\overline{s}>0
such that
\theta(\overline{s})=0
.
At
s=0
we have
\theta(0)=\theta_{0}>0
. Since
\lim_{s\to s_{M}}\theta(s)=-\theta_{\ast}<0
, and using that
\theta
is a decreasing function, there is
\overline{s}>0
such that
\theta(\overline{s})=0
. Hence,
\alpha_{\gamma}
can be considered as an orbit with initial condition
\theta(0)=0
.
∎
