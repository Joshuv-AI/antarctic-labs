Topologically protected interface modes in multi-band damped lattice models
Yannick de Bruijn ∗, Erik Orvehed Hiltunen

Abstract
. Tridiagonal k k -Toeplitz operators provide a natural framework for modelling one-dimensional k k -periodic lattice systems. A fundamental connection is obtained between Coburn’s lemma for tridiagonal k k -Toeplitz operators and the existence of edge modes. We reveal that topological edge modes are characterised by the eigenvalues of the leading principal submatrix of the symbol function. A complete analysis of tridiagonal interface operators satisfying global inversion symmetry is then presented. These results are applied to finite one-dimensional k k -periodic chains of damped resonators that satisfy both local and global inversion symmetry. Additionally, disordered tight-binding interface operators are shown to support a topologically robust zero-energy interface state. Numerical simulations are conducted to illustrate the theoretical findings.

Introduction

Inspired by the study of damped resonator systems, this article tackles two unresolved problems. First, we provide a characterisation of the open limit of tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz matrices. The open limit refers to the spectrum of a finite but large Toeplitz matrix whose size tends to infinity. This limit is particularly relevant for physical applications, where it is used to connect open and periodic boundary conditions in resonator chains. Our approach builds on Harold Widom’s results for the open limit of block Toeplitz matrices
[
27
]
. In the specific case of tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz matrices, Widom’s open limit characterisation can be significantly simplified.
The second contribution of this paper is a strikingly simple description of edge states in multi band lattice models, given by the eigenvalues of a principal minor of the symbol function. Coburn’s lemma for tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz matrices
[
5
,
12
]
is used to establish a bulk-boundary correspondence for tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz operators, which guarantees the existence of edge modes and provides an optimal description of the spectrum of tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz operators.
Topological protection of edge modes is a sought after property as it reveals that edge modes are highly robust against defects obtained from the bulk properties of the material. We reveal that if the symbol function of a tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz matrix satisfies local inversion symmetry, the edge modes are topologically protected.

These new results will be used to investigate the spectra and in particular interface modes of tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz interface matrices satisfying global inversion symmetry around the interface.
We distinguish two separate categories of interface modes. The first class comprises edge-induced interface modes that produce monopole-type interface states.
The second class consists of eigenvalue-matched interface modes, which generate dipole-type interface states and can be seen as a discrete analogue of a matched impedance at the interface
[
10
,
28
]
.
Topological robustness
[
8
,
19
,
16
,
21
,
20
]
, it is shown, belongs exclusively to edge-induced interface modes, while the existence of eigenvalue-matched modes is dependent on the specific coupling at the interface. This stands in contrast to continuum models, where impedance matched interface modes are known to be topologically protected, whereas edge induced interface modes might not exist in continuum models
[
10
,
26
,
2
,
24
]
.

We illustrate our result in the case of a one-dimensional damped
k
-periodic subwavelength resonator chains obtained by merging two mirror symmetric materials together at an interface, creating a
k
-periodic SSH type structure
[
25
,
22
,
4
,
11
]
. Although topological protection is well characterised in undamped systems
[
3
,
10
,
7
]
, no analogue of a topological quantification, such as the Zak phase
[
29
]
, appears to be known for damped systems.
This will be addressed in the present work, as we will generalise the topological protection of dimers established in
[
7
]
to
k
-periodic resonator chains, as well as demonstrate the protection of interface modes in damped
k
-periodic resonator arrangements satisfying both local and global inversion symmetry.

We conclude the study of edge induced interface modes by revealing an exceptionally high robustness with respect to disorder of a zero energy interface state. We simulate a one dimensional resonator chain with nearest-neighbour coupling and numerically confirm its robustness against variations in the coupling strengths
[
23
]
.

The paper is organised as follows. Section
2
reviews key results on the spectra of tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz operators and derives an exact description of the open-limit spectra of tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz matrices with complex entries. Furthermore, we examine the presence of edge modes and their protection by local inversion symmetry.
Section
3
builds on this framework to characterise the spectra of interface Toeplitz operators. Necessary and sufficient conditions on the eigenvalues supporting a mid-gap interface mode are obtained. We further show that monopole modes arise from edge-induced interfaces, whereas dipole modes originate from a eigenvalue-matched interface.
In Section
4
, these analytical results are applied to one-dimensional damped resonator chains and the differences between discrete and continuum models are elucidated in Section
5
. Section
6
considers a tight-binding Hamiltonian which exhibits an interface mode, robust under strong disorder.
Finally, Section
7
outlines potential applications and directions for future work. The code used for the numerical simulations in the figures is provided in Section
9
.

2.
Spectra of tridiagonal k-Toeplitz matrices with complex entries

We will start by recalling some fundamental results on tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz operators as well as on the asymptotic spectra of large but finite Toeplitz matrices.
We define a
tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz operator
by the semi-infinite matrix,

(2.1)

\bm{\mathbf{A}}:=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a_{1}&b_{1}&&&&&&\\
c_{1}&a_{2}&b_{2}&&&\\
&\raisebox{3.0pt}{\scalebox{.6}{$\ddots$}}&\raisebox{3.0pt}{\scalebox{.6}{$\ddots$}}&\raisebox{3.0pt}{\scalebox{.6}{$\ddots$}}&&\\
&&c_{k-2}&a_{k-1}&b_{k-1}&&\\
&&&c_{k-1}&a_{k}&b_{k}&&\\
&&&&c_{k}&a_{1}&b_{1}&&\\
&&&&&\raisebox{3.0pt}{\scalebox{.6}{$\ddots$}}&\raisebox{3.0pt}{\scalebox{.6}{$\ddots$}}&&\raisebox{3.0pt}{\scalebox{.6}{$\ddots$}}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right),

where
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i}\in\C
for
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{ij}=0
if
|i-j|>1
for
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
.
Any tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz operator
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
can be reformulated as a tridiagonal block Toeplitz operator with
k\times k
blocks following a
1
-periodic repetition, that is,

(2.2)

\bm{\mathbf{A}}=\scalebox{0.8}{$\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{0}&\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{-1}&\\
\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{1}&\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{0}&\ddots\\
&\ddots&\ddots\\
\end{pmatrix}$}.

The
symbol
of a tridiagonal block Toeplitz operator (
2.2
), and thus also of a tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz operator (
2.1
)
is defined by the matrix-valued function

\displaystyle f:\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}

(2.3)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\mapsto\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{-1}z^{-1}+\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{0}+\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{1}z.

Let
\mathbf{B}_{0},\mathbf{B}_{1}\in\C^{k-1\times k-1}
denote the leading and trailing principal sub-matrices of
\mathbf{A}_{0}\in\C^{k\times k}
, respectively, given by

(2.4)

\mathbf{A_{0}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{B}_{0}&b_{k-1}\\
c_{k-1}&a_{k}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&b_{1}\\
c_{1}&\mathbf{B}_{1}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

In the sequel of this paper, we shall refer to the Toeplitz operator
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
, specified in equation (
2.2
), by the notation
\bm{\mathbf{A}}=\bm{\mathbf{T}}(f)
.
We define the set of eigenvalues of the symbol function evaluated on the torus as

(2.5)

\sigma_{\mathrm{det}}(f):=\bigl\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\det\bigl(f(z)-\lambda\bigr)=0\text{ for some }z\in\mathbb{T}\bigr\}.

Following
[
5
, Theorem 2.4.]
, the essential spectrum of the Toeplitz operator is given by (
2.5
), that is

(2.6)

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(\mathbf{T}(f)\big)=\sigma_{\mathrm{det}}(f).

Let us also introduce the region of non-trivial winding,

(2.7)

\sigma_{\mathrm{wind}}(f):=\bigl\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma_{\mathrm{det}}(f):\operatorname{wind}\bigl(\det\bigl(f(\mathbb{T})-\lambda),0\bigr)\neq 0\bigr\}.

The spectrum of Toeplitz operators is characterised solely by its symbol function
[
5
, Theorem 2.7.]
, that is,

(2.8)

\sigma_{\mathrm{det}}(f)\cup\sigma_{\mathrm{wind}}(f)\subseteq\sigma\bigl(\mathbf{T}(f)\bigr)\subseteq\sigma_{\mathrm{det}}(f)\cup\sigma_{\mathrm{wind}}(f)\cup\sigma\bigl(\mathbf{B}_{0}\bigr),

where
\mathbf{B}_{0}
is the leading principal submatrix of
\mathbf{A}_{0}
as in (
2.4
). The essential spectrum of the Toeplitz operator can alternatively be presented as follows.
Let
z_{1}(\lambda)
and
z_{2}(\lambda)
be the roots of the polynomial
z\operatorname{det}\big(f(z)-\lambda\mathrm{Id}\big)=0
and let us define the set of roots of equal magnitude as,

(2.9)

\Gamma:=\big\{\lambda\in\C:|z_{1}(\lambda)|=|z_{2}(\lambda)|\big\}.

The set
\Gamma
is potentially difficult to compute, since it involves checking the magnitude of the roots
z_{i}(\lambda)
for all
\lambda\in\C
. This can be overcome in the tridiagonal case using the following result.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
f\in\C^{k\times k}
be the symbol function of a tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz operator, then it holds that

(2.10)

\Gamma=\bigcup_{\alpha\in[-\pi,\pi]}\sigma\big(f(re^{\mathrm{i}\alpha})\big),\quad\text{for }r=\sqrt{\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left|\frac{b_{i}}{c_{i}}\right|}.

Proof.

For tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz operators, the determinant of the symbol corresponds to
[
5
, Appendix A]

(2.11)

z\det\bigl(f(z)-\lambda\bigr)=(-1)^{k+1}z^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}+g(\lambda)z+(-1)^{k+1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}.

As we are seeking roots of equal magnitude, it follows by Viète’s Theorem that

(2.12)

|z_{1}(\lambda)||z_{2}(\lambda)|=|z_{1}(\lambda)z_{2}(\lambda)|=\left|\frac{(-1)^{k+1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}}{(-1)^{k+1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}}\right|=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left|\frac{b_{i}}{c_{i}}\right|=:r^{2}

from which it readily follows that
|z_{1}(\lambda)|=|z_{2}(\lambda)|=r
.
Crucially, the magnitude of the roots is independent of
\lambda
and therefore, the roots cluster along a circle of radius
r
. Consequently the set of possible
\lambda
have to be roots of

(2.13)

\operatorname{det}\big(f(r\mathbb{T})-\lambda\mathrm{Id}\big)=0

from which (
2.10
) is obtained immediately.
∎

In the remainder of this work, we will consider the case where
\mathbf{T}
is symmetric. Note that, since we allow complex entries, we are not assuming
\mathbf{T}(f)
to be Hermitian. Also note that any tridiagonal matrix can be reduced to a symmetric matrix by a similarity transform (as outlined in
[
5
, Section 3]
).
In the case where
\mathbf{T}(f)
is symmetric, the expression for the essential spectrum can be further simplified. Let
b_{i}=c_{i}\in\C,\penalty\ \forall i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, then it holds,

(2.14)

A:=(-1)^{k}\prod_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}=(-1)^{k}\prod_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\in\C.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
\mathbf{T}(f)
be a symmetric tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz operator, then

(2.15)

\sigma_{\mathrm{det}}(f)=\bigl\{\lambda\in\C:2A\cos(\alpha)+g(\lambda)=0,\penalty\ \alpha\in[0,2\pi]\bigr\}

and the spectrum is given by

(2.16)

\sigma_{\mathrm{det}}(f)\subseteq\sigma\bigl(\mathbf{T}(f)\bigr)\subseteq\sigma_{\mathrm{det}}(f)\cup\sigma(\mathbf{B_{0}}),

where
A
is given by (
2.14
) and where
g(\lambda)
is a polynomial of degree
k
given by

(2.17)

g(\lambda)=\det(\bm{\mathbf{A}}_{0}-\lambda)-b_{k}^{2}p(\lambda),

where

(2.18)

p(\lambda)=\begin{cases}0,&k=1,\\
1,&k=2,\\
\left\lvert\begin{smallmatrix}a_{2}-\lambda&b_{2}&0&\dots&0\\
b_{3}&a_{3}-\lambda&b_{3}&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&b_{k-2}\\
0&\dots&0&b_{k-1}&a_{k-1}-\lambda\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right\rvert,&k\geq 3.\end{cases}

Proof.

Since the operator is symmetric, (
2.5
) reduces to,

(2.19)

\det\bigl(f(e^{-\mathrm{i}\alpha})-\lambda\bigr)=Ae^{-\mathrm{i}\alpha}+Ae^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}+g(\lambda)=2A\cos(\alpha)+g(\lambda).

Moreover, from (
2.19
) it is not hard to see that the symbol function evaluated on the torus, i.e.
\alpha\in[0,2\pi]
does not have any interior points, so

(2.20)

\sigma_{\mathrm{wind}}(f)=\emptyset.

The inclusion in (
2.16
) is a direct consequence of (
2.8
) asserted in
[
5
, Theorem 2.7]
.
∎

Therefore, in the symmetric case, up to possibly
k-1
points entailed by
\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})
, the spectrum of a Toeplitz operator is given by
\sigma_{\mathrm{det}}(f)
, which in turn is given by the roots of a parametrised equation

(2.21)

A\cos(\alpha)+g(\lambda)=0,\penalty\ \text{for }\alpha\in[0,2\pi].

Note that the function
g(\lambda)
is explicitly given by (
2.17
) and is a polynomial of degree
k
, so the set
\sigma_{\mathrm{det}}(f)
is the set of roots parametrised in
\alpha
and can therefore easily be computed numerically. From this it also becomes clear that the set
\sigma_{\mathrm{det}}(f)
is composed of a collection of algebraic curves in the complex plane.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
f\in\C^{k\times k}
be the symbol function of a symmetric Toeplitz operator
\mathbf{T}(f)
, then it hold that

(2.22)

\Gamma=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(\mathbf{T}(f)\big).

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{T}(f)
is symmetric, it follows from (
2.10
) that
r=1
, in which case

(2.23)

\Gamma=\bigcup_{\alpha\in[-\pi,\pi]}\sigma\big(f(e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha})\big)=\bigl\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\det\bigl(f(z)-\lambda\bigr)=0,\ \exists z\in\mathbb{T}\bigr\}=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(\mathbf{T}(f)\big)

where in the last equality, we used (
2.6
), which completes the proof of the result.
∎

We now turn to the point spectrum of
\mathbf{T}(f)
comprising up to
k-1
points entailed by
\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})
.

Definition 2.4

(Edge states)
.

We say that
\mathbf{v}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
is an
edge mode
of
\mathbf{T}(f)
with an
edge eigenvalue

\lambda\in\C\setminus\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(\mathbf{T}(f)\big)
if
\mathbf{T}(f)\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}
, where
\mathbf{v}
decays exponentially away from the index
0
. We denote by
\sigma_{\mathrm{edge}}\big(\mathbf{T}(f)\big)\subseteq\sigma(\mathbf{B_{0}})
, the set of edge eigenvalues of
\mathbf{T}(f)
.

To analyse the occurrence of edge states, it is essential to first consider the following result from
[
5
, Theorem 2.3]
, which extends Coburn’s lemma to the tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz setting.

Theorem 2.5

(Coburn’s lemma, tridiagonal k-Toeplitz version)
.

Let
f\in\C^{k\times k}(\mathbb{T})
be the symbol of a tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz operator such that
\det\bigl(f(z)\bigr)
does not vanish identically on
\mathbb{T}
. Then one of the following statements holds:

(i)

\mathbf{T}(f)
has a trivial kernel.

(ii)

\mathbf{T}(f)
has a dense range.

(iii)

The leading
(k-1)\times(k-1)
principal submatrix of
\mathbf{A}_{0}
, that is
\mathbf{B}_{0}
, has a non-zero kernel. In particular, there exists some
z_{0}\in\C\cup\{\infty\}
such that
\ker(z_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{-1}+\mathbf{A}_{0})\cap\ker(\mathbf{A}_{1})\neq\bm{\mathbf{0}}
.

Coburn’s lemma for tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz operators yields a characterisation on the existence of edge states can therefore be seen as the analogue of the bulk-boundary correspondence
[
26
]
for tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz operators.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
f\in\C^{k\times k}
be the symbol function of a tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz operator, then

(i)

If
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})\setminus\Gamma
, then there exists a
z(\lambda)\in\C
and a vector
\mathbf{v}\in\C^{k}
such that

(2.24)

\Big[f\big(z(\lambda)\big)-\lambda\mathrm{Id}\Big]\mathbf{v}=0,\quad\text{with }\mathbf{v}_{k}=0.

(ii)

If
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})\setminus\Gamma
and
z(\lambda)
from (
2.24
) satisfies
|z(\lambda)|>1
, then
\lambda\in\sigma_{\mathrm{edge}}\big(\mathbf{T}(f)\big)\subseteq\sigma\big(\mathbf{T}(f)\big)
.

(iii)

The spectrum of
\mathbf{T}(f)
is given by,

(2.25)

\sigma\big(\mathbf{T}(f)\big)=\sigma_{\mathrm{det}}(f)\cup\sigma_{\mathrm{edge}}\big(\mathbf{T}(f)\big).

Proof.

To show
(i)
by Theorem
2.5
, it follows that, if
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})
, then there is a vector
\mathbf{v}
and a root
z\in\C
such that
\mathbf{v}\in\operatorname{ker}\big(z^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{-1}+(\mathbf{A}_{0}-\lambda\mathrm{Id})\big)\cap\operatorname{ker}(\mathbf{A}_{1})
, but then
\bm{\mathbf{v}}
also satisfies

(2.26)

\big[f(z)-\lambda\mathrm{Id}\big]\bm{\mathbf{v}}=\big[z^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{-1}+(\mathbf{A}_{0}-\lambda\mathrm{Id})\big]\bm{\mathbf{v}}+z\mathbf{A}_{1}\bm{\mathbf{v}}=0.

So
\mathbf{v}
is an eigenvector of
f(z)
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda
. Since
\mathbf{A}_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}
has non-zero entries only in the top right corner, it follows that
\mathbf{v}_{k}=0
.

To show
(ii)
, it is not hard to see that if
|z(\lambda)|>1
then,

(2.27)

\mathbf{w}=\big(\mathbf{v}^{\top},z(\lambda)^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\top},z(\lambda)^{-2}\mathbf{v}^{\top},\dots\big)\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})

satisfies
\mathbf{T}(f)\mathbf{w}=\lambda\mathbf{w}
, from which it follows that
\lambda\in\sigma_{\mathrm{edge}}\big(\mathbf{T}(f)\big)\subseteq\sigma\big(\mathbf{T}(f)\big)
.

To show the spectral equality in
(iii)
, let
\lambda\in\sigma\big(\mathbf{T}(f)\big)\setminus\sigma_{\mathrm{det}}(f)
. But as only the quasiperiodically extended Bloch eigenvector
\mathbf{v}
with
\mathbf{v}_{k}=0
is an

eigenvector of
\mathbf{T}(f)
, it follows from

that
\lambda\in\sigma_{\mathrm{edge}}\big(\mathbf{T}(f)\big)
, which completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Using our novel insights on tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz operators we may greatly simplify the results of Widom
[
27
]
on the asymptotic spectrum of block Toeplitz matrices, which will be presented in greater detail in Section
2.3
.
Let us introduce the following set

(2.28)

G_{0}:=\big\{\lambda\in\C\setminus\Gamma:C_{0}(\lambda)=0\big\},

with

(2.29)

C_{0}(\lambda):=\operatorname{det}\Big(\mathbf{T}\big((f-\lambda\mathrm{Id})^{-1}\big)\mathbf{T}(f-\lambda\mathrm{Id})\Big).

The function
C_{0}
may alternatively be defined as

(2.30)

C_{0}(\lambda)=\operatorname{det}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma}\big(f(z)-\lambda\mathrm{Id}\big)^{-1}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}z}{z}\right),\quad\lambda\in\C\setminus\Gamma,

where
\gamma
is a counter-clockwise oriented closed Jordan curve enclosing the origin and the root of smallest magnitude
z_{1}(\lambda)
but not the root
z_{2}(\lambda)
.
Widom already observed in
[
27
]
that in the
1
-Toeplitz case one must have
G_{0}=\emptyset
. For the more general tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz matrices, Coburn’s lemma gives a valuable insight on structure of the set
G_{0}
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
f\in\C^{k\times k}
be the symbol function of a tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz matrix, then it holds that

(2.31)

G_{0}\subseteq\sigma(\mathbf{B_{0}})\cup\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{1}),

where
\mathbf{B}_{0},\mathbf{B}_{1}\in\C^{k-1\times k-1}
are the leading and trailing principal submatrices of
\mathbf{A}_{0}
receptively as in (
2.4
).

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in G_{0}
then it holds by (
2.29
) that
\operatorname{det}\big(\mathbf{T}((f-\lambda\mathrm{Id})^{-1})\mathbf{T}(f-\lambda\mathrm{Id})\big)=0.

In other words,
\mathbf{T}((f-\lambda\mathrm{Id})^{-1})\mathbf{T}(f-\lambda\mathrm{Id})
is not invertible. This implies that either
\mathbf{T}(f-\lambda\mathrm{Id})
or
\mathbf{T}((f-\lambda\mathrm{Id})^{-1})
is not invertible. We discuss both cases.
First note that
\mathbf{T}(f-\lambda\mathrm{Id})=\mathbf{T}(f)-\lambda\mathrm{Id}
, since
\mathbf{T}(f)-\lambda\mathrm{Id}
is not invertible, it must hold that
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathbf{T})
. Since by assumption
\lambda\not\in\Gamma
, it follows from Corollary
2.2
that
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})
.
In the second case, we have to introduce the associated Toeplitz operator
\widetilde{f}(z)=W_{k}f(z^{-1})W_{k}
and
(W_{k})_{ij}=\delta_{i(k+1-j)}
, then it holds that
\mathbf{T}\big(\widetilde{(f-\lambda)}\big)
is invertible if and only if
\mathbf{T}((f-\lambda\mathrm{Id})^{-1})
is invertible
[
1
, Section 6.2]
. This implies that
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathbf{T}(\widetilde{f}))
so
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{1})
, which completes the proof.
∎

Theorem
2.7
is especially convenient for the numerical computation of the set
G_{0}
. Rather than checking the condition
C_{0}(\lambda)=0
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma
, it suffices to verify the condition
C_{0}(\lambda)=0
at at most

discrete values of
\lambda
, explicitly given by
\sigma(\mathbf{B_{0}})\cup\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{1})
. We emphasise that the converse inclusion in (
2.7
) does not hold in general. A counterexample is the symbol function

(2.32)

f(z)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1+\frac{1}{2}z\\
1+\frac{1}{2}z^{-1}&1\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Then
0\in\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})
, as well as
1\in\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{1})
, but
C_{0}(0)=1
and
C_{0}(1)=1
, so
0,1\not\in G_{0}
.
An alternative characterisation of edge states, compared to Theorem
2.6
may be achieved using the set

by the following result.

Theorem 2.8
.

If
\lambda\in\C
is an edge eigenvalue of

, if and only if
\lambda\in G_{0}\cap\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})
.

Proof.

Assume
\lambda\in\C
is an edge eigenvalue in other words
\mathbf{T}(f)-\lambda\mathrm{Id}
has non-trivial kernel
\mathbf{v}

\lambda\in\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})
, therefore
\lambda\in G_{0}\cap\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})
.
Assume now that
\lambda\in G_{0}\cap\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})
, using that
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})
it follows by the same method as in the proof of Theorem

\mathbf{T}(f)-\lambda\mathrm{Id}
has non-trivial kernel. Since
\lambda\in G_{0}
the eigenmode

it follows that
|z(\lambda)|>1
, so by Theorem

In the sequel, we will examine the topological properties of edge modes. Topological classifications are of particular use in physics as they demonstrate robustness with respect to perturbations.

Definition 2.10

(Topologically protected)
.

An edge mode to the eigenvalue
\lambda
is said to be topologically protected if it is robust under continuous perturbations of the symbol function that preserve local inversion symmetry, provided that the band gap containing
\lambda
does not close.

Since the topological classification of edge modes differs markedly between the Hermitian and complex symmetric cases, each is treated separately in the subsequent sections.

2.1.
Edge modes in real symmetric systems

Toeplitz matrices with real-valued and symmetric entries play a central role for the applications to lattice models, see Section
4
. Because Hermitian Toeplitz operators have a real spectrum, the spectral gap can be defined as
\Gamma:=\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}
, which consists of a collection of open disjoint intervals. Edge modes can be ordered within each such band gap, and this implies that there can be at most one edge-induced interface mode
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})
in any given spectral gap.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})\setminus\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(\mathbf{T}(f)\big)
be an eigenvalue of a Hermitian Toeplitz operator
\mathbf{T}(f)
, then
\lambda
is the unique edge eigenvalue in the specific spectral gap situated between two spectral bands.

Proof.

The essential spectrum is given by (
2.6
), that is the union of eigenvalues of the symbol function
f(z)\in\C^{k\times k}
evaluated on the torus and consists of up to
k
disjoint intervals. Then
\mathbf{B}_{0}
is the leading
k-1\times k-1
submatrix of
f(z)
, in particular the entries of
\mathbf{B}_{0}
no longer depend on
z
. By the Cauchy interlacing theorem, the
k-1
eigenvalues of
\mathbf{B}_{0}
interlace the
k
eigenvalues of
f(z),\penalty\ \forall z\in\mathbb{T}
, that is

(2.37)

\lambda_{1}\big(f(z)\big)\leq\mu_{1}(\mathbf{B}_{0})\leq\lambda_{2}\big(f(z)\big)\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{k-1}\big(f(z)\big)\leq\mu_{k-1}(\mathbf{B}_{0})\leq\lambda_{k}\big(f(z)\big),\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{T}.

The uniqueness follows immediately by the interlacing property, which completes the proof of the result.
∎

In order to derive a topological quantification of edge modes, we impose some symmetry constraints on the symbol function. Let us define the exchange matrix
(\mathbf{J})_{i,j}=\delta_{i,k+1-j}
, that is a matrix with constant
1
on the antidiagonal, then the symbol function
f(z)\in\C^{k\times k}
is called
persymmetric
if
f(z)=\mathbf{J}f(z)^{\top}\mathbf{J}
.
The matrix is called
centrosymmetric
if it additionally holds that
f(z)=f(z)^{\top}
. Since
z\in\mathbb{T}
and the operator
\mathbf{T}(f)
is symmetric, the symbol function
f(z)
is centrosymmetric if and only if
z=\pm 1
. The eigenvectors
\mathbf{v}
of centrosymmetric matrices are symmetric or skew symmetric
[
9
]
, that is
\mathbf{v}_{1}=\pm\mathbf{v}_{k}
.

Theorem 2.12
.

Suppose that
f(z)\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
is persymmetric, then every edge mode
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})\setminus\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(\mathbf{T}(f)\big)
is topologically protected.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that there exists
\lambda_{0}\in\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})
that merges into the spectral bands without the spectral gap closing. This merging must occur at a band extremity, i.e. at an eigenvalue
\lambda_{\mathrm{ess}}
of
f(\pm 1)
.
Let
f(\pm 1)\mathbf{w}=\lambda_{\mathrm{ess}}\mathbf{w}
then since by assumption
f(\pm 1)
is centrosymmetric, the eigenvector
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
must satisfy
\mathbf{w}_{1}=\pm\mathbf{w}_{k}
. In addition, it must hold that both entries are not equal to
0
as otherwise because of the structure of
f(z)
and the eigenvectors are given by a two-term recurrence, it would have to hold that
\mathbf{w}\equiv\mathbf{0}.

Let us now suppose that
\lambda_{0}\in\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})
is an eigenvalue in the bandgap, by Theorem
2.6
, the last entry of the associated eigenvector must be
0
. Since the essential spectrum
\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(\mathbf{T}(f)\big)
is always closed, the spectral gap given by
\mathbb{R}\setminus\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(\mathbf{T}(f)\big)
is open.
We now move to the limit
\lambda_{0}\to\lambda_{\mathrm{ess}}
.
By assumption that the spectral gap remains open and by Lemma
2.11
both
\lambda_{\mathrm{ess}}
and
\lambda_{0}
are simple. It is well known that the Floquet parameter
z
is continuous in the eigenvalue
\lambda
, therefore
f\big(z(\lambda)\big)
is a continuous transformation in
\lambda
. By Lemma
2.11
the eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
is simple, the eigenvector entries vary continuously.
Therefore, since
\mathbf{v}(\lambda_{0})_{k}=0
for all
\lambda_{0}\in\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})\setminus\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\!\left(\mathbf{T}(f)\right)
, continuity forces
\mathbf{v}(\lambda_{\mathrm{ess}})_{k}=0
.
This contradicts the conclusion
\mathbf{w}_{k}\neq 0
established above, completing the proof.
∎

2.2.
Edge Modes in the Complex Symmetric Setting

An analogue of Lemma
2.11
in the complex symmetric setting cannot be obtained by the same approach, for the following reasons. First, the spectral gap

exploited the fact that, in the real symmetric case, edge modes can only collide with eigenvalues at
f(\pm 1)

Nevertheless, for a dimer symbol with complex-valued entries, the following result holds.

Proposition 2.13

Let

\lambda\in\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})=a_{1}

Proof.

For a
2\times 2

\lambda\in\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})

\mathbf{v}=[1,0]^{\top}

f(\mathbb{T})
stay simple, therefore the eigenvectors are continuous under continuous perturbations, by continuity it follows that
\mathbf{v}=[1,0]^{\top}
also for
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathbf{B}_{0})

(2.38)

\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}-\lambda&b_{1}+b_{2}z\\

0\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{0}

which holds as long as
z=-b_{2}/b_{1}
. Suppose now the edge mode is approaching the essential spectrum in the limit, then the Floquet parameter has to satisfy
|z|\to 1
, in other words
-b_{2}/b_{1}=e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}

Hovewer, this is precisely the condition for bandgap closing. To see this, it is enough to understand that bandgap closing occurs when the symbol function has an eigenvalue of multiplicity

(2.39)

\operatorname{det}\big(f(z)-\widetilde{\lambda}\big)=0\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}\operatorname{det}\big(f(z)-\lambda\big)\Big|_{\lambda=\widetilde{\lambda}}=0.

In particular, it holds by (

(2.40)

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}\operatorname{det}\big(f(z)-\lambda\big)=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}g(\lambda)=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}\big(\lambda^{2}-2a_{1}\lambda+a_{1}^{2}-b_{1}^{2}-b_{2}^{2}\big)=2\lambda-2a_{1}.

Consequently, it must hold that
\widetilde{\lambda}=a_{1}
, it remains to check the conditions when

is an eigenvalue of

(2.41)

\operatorname{det}\big(f(z)-\widetilde{\lambda}\big)=b_{1}^{2}+b_{2}^{2}+b_{1}b_{2}(1/z+z)=0

It is easy to see that (

To conclude, we have shown that the conditions for an edge mode vanishing into the essential spectrum are equivalent to the bandgap closing at this specific point. The edge mode is thereby topologically protected, which completes the proof.

\bm{\mathbf{T}}_{mk}(f):=\bm{\mathbf{P}}_{mk}\bm{\mathbf{T}}(f)\bm{\mathbf{P}}_{mk}.

We will venture onwards to characterise the

The open limit for Block Toeplitz operators was first established by Widom

3.
Spectra of tridiagonal k-Toeplitz Interface operators

This section focuses on the spectral analysis of interface Toeplitz operators. We consider two tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz operators,
\mathbf{T}_{A}
and
\mathbf{T}_{B}
, and assume that they are joined at a common interface, so as to form an
interface Toeplitz operator
of the following form

(3.1)

\mathbf{T}_{AB}:=\scalebox{0.9}{$\begin{pNiceMatrix}$}=\scalebox{0.7}{$\begin{pNiceMatrix}$},

for some coupling constants
\eta,s,q\in\C
. We will start by giving a description of the essential spectrum of an interface Toeplitz operator as in (
3.1
). The
k
-periodic structure of such operators is always specified relative to a canonically selected unit cell. The following theorem shows that the essential spectrum does not depend on which unit cell convention is chosen. We say that
\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}_{A}
is a
cyclic shift
of
\mathbf{T}_{A}
if the
k
-periodic entries of the Toeplitz operator are cyclically shifted, that is,
a_{i}\to a_{i+m}
, for some
m\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{T}_{A}
be a tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz operator and let
\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}_{A}
be a cyclic shift of
\mathbf{T}_{A}
, then

(3.2)

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}_{A})=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(\mathbf{T}_{A}).

Proof.

By
[
5
, Theorem 2.4.]
, the essential spectrum of a
k
-Toeplitz operator is given by

(3.3)

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\bigl(\mathbf{T}(f)\bigr)=\sigma_{\mathrm{det}}(f).

The assertion then follows by the fact that the determinant is invariant under cyclic shifts.
∎

The essential spectrum of interface Toeplitz operators admits the following description.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mathbf{T}_{AB}
be the interface Toeplitz operator defined in (
3.1
), then the essential spectrum is given by

(3.4)

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\bigl(\mathbf{T}_{AB}\bigr)=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\bigl(\mathbf{T}_{A}\bigr)\cup\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\bigl(\mathbf{T}_{B}\bigr).

Proof.

The operator
\mathbf{T}_{AB}
defined in (
3.1
) is a compact perturbation of the block diagonal operator

(3.5)

\mathbf{T}_{D}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{T}_{B}&0\\
0&\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}_{A}\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{T}_{B}\oplus\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}_{A}.

By the direct sum decomposition of the essential spectrum, it holds

(3.6)

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\bigl(\mathbf{T}_{B}\oplus\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}_{A}\bigr)=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\bigl(\mathbf{T}_{B}\bigr)\cup\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\bigl(\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}_{A}\bigr),

and since the essential spectrum is unchanged under compact perturbations it holds that

(3.7)

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\bigl(\mathbf{T}_{AB}\bigr)=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\bigl(\mathbf{T}_{D}\bigr)=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\bigl(\mathbf{T}_{B}\bigr)\cup\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\bigl(\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}_{A}\bigr)=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\bigl(\mathbf{T}_{B}\bigr)\cup\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\bigl(\mathbf{T}_{A}\bigr),

where in the last step, we used the invariance of the essential spectrum under cyclic shifts as asserted by Lemma

, which completes the proof.
∎

The discrete spectrum of tridiagonal interface Toeplitz operators is more subtle to characterise. In the sequel, we therefore exploit the symmetries in the structure of the building blocks
\mathbf{T}_{A}
and

3.1.
Reflection-symmetric operators.

In this section, we focus on interface Toeplitz operators under the additional assumption that their building blocks,
\mathbf{T}_{A}
and
\mathbf{T}_{B}
, are reflection- (or mirror-) symmetric.
This is of particular interest, as it places the system in the same form as the celebrated SSH model
[
25
]
under global inversion symmetry.

Definition 3.3

(Interface eigenvalue)
.

We say that
\lambda\in\C
is an
interface eigenvalue
if it lies in the common spectral gap, that is
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathbf{T}_{AB})\setminus\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(\mathbf{T}_{AB})
and if the associated
interface eigenmode
is decaying away from the interface into both bulks.

Let us introduce the reflection operator,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})

\displaystyle\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0})

\displaystyle(\bm{\mathbf{v}}_{0},\bm{\mathbf{v}}_{1},\dots)

\displaystyle\mapsto(...,\bm{\mathbf{v}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{v}}_{0}).

Let
\mathbf{T}_{A}
be a tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz operator, then its mirrored counterpart is given by
\mathbf{T}_{B}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{T}_{A}\mathbf{R}
and we have
\mathbf{R}^{2}=\mathrm{Id}
. The following result establishes that the eigenvectors inherit the same mirror symmetry.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue of
\mathbf{T}_{A}
with eigenvector
\bm{\mathbf{v}}
, then
\lambda
is also an eigenvalue for
\mathbf{T}_{B}
with eigenvector
\mathbf{R}\bm{\mathbf{v}}
.

Proof.

A direct computation yields

(3.9)

\mathbf{T}_{B}\bigl(\mathbf{R}\bm{\mathbf{v}}\bigr)=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{T}_{A}\mathbf{R}\bigl(\mathbf{R}\bm{\mathbf{v}}\bigr)=\mathbf{R}\bigl(\mathbf{T}_{A}\bm{\mathbf{v}}\bigr)=\mathbf{R}\bigl(\lambda\bm{\mathbf{v}}\bigr)=\lambda\bigl(\mathbf{R}\bm{\mathbf{v}}\bigr),

which completes the proof.
∎

This symmetry is inherited by the eigenvectors of the interface operator. We have the following categories.

Definition 3.5

(Monopole and Dipole modes)
.

We say that an eigenmode is a
monopole mode
if it has even symmetry

(3.10)

\bm{\mathbf{w}}_{i}=\bm{\mathbf{w}}_{-i}.

A
dipole mode
, on the other hand, has odd symmetry, that is

(3.11)

\bm{\mathbf{w}}_{i}=-\bm{\mathbf{w}}_{-i}.

We have the following result on the parity of the eigenmodes of a reflection-symmetric operator
\mathbf{T}_{AB}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{T}_{AB}
satisfies (
3.13
), then any eigenmode
\bm{\mathbf{w}}
corresponding to a simple eigenvalue
\lambda
is either an monopole or dipole mode.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{R}
be the reflection operator defined in (
3.8
), then by the symmetry assumption on
\mathbf{T}_{AB}
it holds that
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{T}_{AB}=\mathbf{T}_{AB}\mathbf{R}
. Let
\lambda\in\sigma\bigl(\mathbf{T}_{AB}\bigr)\setminus\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\bigl(\mathbf{T}_{AB}\bigr)
, then
\mathbf{T}_{AB}\bm{\mathbf{v}}=\lambda\bm{\mathbf{v}}
.
Applying
\mathbf{R}
to both sides yields,

(3.12)

\mathbf{T}_{AB}\bigl(\mathbf{R}\bm{\mathbf{v}}\bigr)=\mathbf{R}\bigl(\mathbf{T}_{AB}\bm{\mathbf{v}}\bigr)=\mathbf{R}(\lambda\bm{\mathbf{v}})=\lambda\bigl(\mathbf{R}\bm{\mathbf{v}}\bigr).

Thus,
\mathbf{R}\bm{\mathbf{v}}
is also an eigenvector of
\mathbf{T}_{AB}
with the same eigenvalue. Since
\lambda
is simple, it must hold that the two eigenvectors are linearly dependent, i.e
\mathbf{R}\bm{\mathbf{v}}=c\bm{\mathbf{v}}
for some

. Since
\mathbf{R}^{2}=\mathrm{Id}
it follows that
\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}\bm{\mathbf{v}}=c^{2}\bm{\mathbf{v}}=v
, so that

, which implies
c=\pm 1
.
∎

Let us now examine the interface Toeplitz operator from (
3.1
), which is constructed from two reflection-symmetric Toeplitz operators. It is easy to verify that this type of operator, which is mirror-symmetric with respect to the interface, satisfies the relation

(3.13)

\bigl(\mathbf{T}_{AB}\bigr)_{i,j}=\bigl(\mathbf{T}_{AB}\bigr)_{-i,-j}.

In that case, it follows directly from Theorem
3.2
that the essential spectrum is given by,

(3.14)

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\bigl(\mathbf{T}_{AB}\bigr)=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\bigl(\mathbf{T}_{A}\bigr).

Characterising the point spectrum of
\mathbf{T}_{AB}
is more subtle, as it can originate from different mechanisms, such as edge-induced interfaces or eigenvalue-matched interface modes.

3.2.
Localised modes for interface Toeplitz operators

In this section, we demonstrate that interface Toeplitz operators admit two distinct mechanisms for supporting localised interface modes. The first arises naturally from composition. If a Toeplitz matrix
\mathbf{T}_{A}

4.
Damped one dimensional resonator chains.

In one-dimensional resonator chains, the interaction is limited to nearest neighbours. Consequently, in the corresponding mathematical description, either via a tight-binding Hamiltonian or, as in this work, through a capacitance matrix, the resulting matrices
are tridiagonal. Moreover, the periodicity in the resonator arrangements of the underlying structure is reflected in
these matrices. Thus, in one dimension, analysing resonances in
k
-periodic resonator chains is equivalent
to studying tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz operators.
In this work, we consider the one-dimensional differential eigenvalue problem with damping,

(4.1)

\omega^{2}u=-\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon(x)}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right).

The magnetic permeability
\mu_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is assumed to be constant. The permittivity
\varepsilon\colon\mathbb{R}\to\C
whose imaginary part encodes damping is defined piecewise on the resonator domain
D:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}D_{i}
as

(4.2)

\varepsilon(x)=\begin{cases}\varepsilon_{b},\quad&x\in D,\\
\varepsilon,&x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus D.\end{cases}

The wave speeds inside the resonators
D
and in the background medium
\mathbb{R}\setminus D
are given, respectively, by

(4.3)

v_{b}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_{0}\varepsilon_{b}}},\qquad v:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_{0}\varepsilon}},

where
\delta:=\varepsilon_{b}/\varepsilon
denotes the contrast parameter. The wave speeds inside the resonators throughout a resonator chain comprising
N
resonators will be saved in the matrix,

(4.4)

V=\operatorname{diag}(v_{b},\dots,v_{b})\in\C^{N\times N}.

For the stepwise defined materials, the differential eigenvalue problem (
4.1
) reduces to the boundary problem

(4.5)

\begin{cases}\frac{\dd{{}^{2}}}{\dd x^{2}}u(x)+\frac{\omega^{2}}{v^{2}}u(x)=0,&x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus D,\\
\frac{\dd{{}^{2}}}{\dd x^{2}}u(x)+\frac{\omega^{2}}{v_{b}^{2}}u(x)=0,&x\in D,\\
u|_{\mathsf{R}}(x^{{\mathsf{L},\mathsf{R}}}_{{i}})-u|_{\mathsf{L}}(x^{{\mathsf{L},\mathsf{R}}}_{{i}})=0,&1\leq i\leq N,\\
\left.\frac{\dd u}{\dd x}\right|_{\mathsf{R}}(x^{\mathsf{L}}_{{i}})-\delta\left.\frac{\dd u}{\dd x}\right|_{\mathsf{L}}(x^{\mathsf{L}}_{{i}})=0,&1\leq i\leq N,\\
\delta\left.\frac{\dd u}{\dd x}\right|_{\mathsf{R}}(x^{\mathsf{R}}_{{i}})-\left.\frac{\dd u}{\dd x}\right|_{\mathsf{L}}(x^{\mathsf{R}}_{{i}})=0,&1\leq i\leq N,\\
\big(\frac{\dd}{\dd|x|}-\mathrm{i}k\big)u=0,&\text{for }x\in(-\infty,x^{\mathsf{L}}_{1})\cup(x^{\mathsf{R}}_{N},+\infty).\\
\end{cases}

A common approach to realise subwavelength interactions is to employ high-contrast metamaterials, which are formed by embedding a collection of strongly contrasted resonators into a background medium such that
0<\delta\ll 1
. We are interested in non-trivial frequencies
\omega
such that the scattering problem (
4.5
) is satisfied and such that
\omega\to 0
as

. Such resonances will be denoted

It has been demonstrated in
[

that within the subwavelength regime the resonances can be approximated in terms of a discrete capacitance matrix formulation.
The generalised capacitance matrix, taking into account the wave speeds (
4.4
), is of the form

(4.6)

\mathcal{C}:=VC\in\C^{N\times N}.

The resonances to (
4.5
) are recovered by the fundamental theorem of capacitance.

Proposition 4.1
.

Consider a system of
N
subwavelength resonators and assume that the eigenvalues of the generalised capacitance matrix

defined in (

\mathbf{L}_{\Delta x}
, for a uniform step size

-\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon(x)}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right)\approx\mathbf{L}_{\Delta x}\mathbf{u}.

The permittivity

In this setting, the leading principal submatrix

of

as defined in (

and numerically observed for relatively small

Nevertheless, only even-periodic systems support exponentially localised interface modes.
Consider the diagonal matrix
\mathbf{D}=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,1,-1,\dots)

\mathbf{D}^{-1}=\mathbf{D}
. The chiral symmetry

(6.10)

\mathbf{D}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{-1}=\mathbf{D}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}=-\mathbf{A}

From the definition of the multiplicative process

7.
Concluding Remarks

We have established a novel connection between Coburn’s Lemma for tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz operators and the emergence of edge-induced interface modes. In this framework, the eigenvalues of the leading principal submatrix of the symbol function characterise the edge modes and can be computed explicitly with minimal effort. Since the result applies to general tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz matrices, it may be interpreted as a form of bulk-boundary correspondence for multi-band lattice models.
We further identified conditions under which these edge modes are topologically protected, namely, when the symbol function exhibits local inversion symmetry. In addition, we presented a comprehensive analysis of tridiagonal interface operators, distinguishing between edge-induced modes and matched-eigenvalue interface modes.
These results were applied to damped subwavelength resonator chains and tight-binding Hamiltonians, demonstrating the existence of topologically protected interface modes. In particular, we establish their robustness under random perturbations and even under strong disorder.
Finally, the differences in interface states between continuum and lattice models were presented, and the transition in their behaviour from finite systems to the continuum limit was conceptually described.
As a future research direction, we plan to investigate the reality of the asymptotic open spectrum in the tridiagonal
k
-Toeplitz setting, in line with recent studies
[
15
,
18
]
.
