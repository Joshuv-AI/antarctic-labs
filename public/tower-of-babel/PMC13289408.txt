Immune modulation and treatment outcomes among patients with malaria and typhoid fever in Yaoundé, Cameroon: a comparative analysis of inflammatory responses
Authors: Palmer Masumbe Netongo, MacDonald Bin Eric, Kamoua Eric Berenger Tchoupe, Magne Marie Christine Nzuno, Severin Donald Kamdem, Ange Maxime Tchoutang, Fuh Roger Neba, Bongkiyung Donald Buri, Arnauld Tepa, Nkah Leonard Numfor, Ngum Lesley Ngum, Mbu Nji Akindeh, Fon Wilfred Mbacham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmerging evidence suggests that while standard antimalarial and antityphoidal treatments are effective against the pathogens, they may also influence host immune responses, with potential implications on recovery and clinical outcomes. This piece of work was designed to evaluate the effects of standard antimalarial and antibiotic therapies on selected immune biomarkers in patients with malaria and typhoid fever.MethodsIn this prospective observational cohort study conducted at the Obala District Hospital, Yaoundé, Cameroon, we performed a comparative analysis of cytokine concentrations in 55 patients receiving antimalarial and/or antibiotic treatment and 15 healthy controls. Inflammatory cytokines were measured in 70 participants at admission (baseline) and in 50 out of the 70 patients at day 5 post-treatment using ELISA. An exploratory data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism while correlation analysis between cytokine levels and recovery time was performed using SPSS at 95% CI (p<0.05).ResultsBy day 5 (±2) of treatment, just over half of the patients had achieved clinical recovery, with the highest recovery observed in the antimalarial group and the lowest in the antityphoidal group. Treatment was associated with significant changes in inflammatory cytokine expression. At baseline, patients with malaria–typhoid coinfection exhibited marked overexpression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly interleukin (IL)-2, tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α and interferon (IFN)-γ, compared with healthy controls. Antimalarial therapy was associated with increased IL-2 and IL-1β levels and reduced IL-10, while antibiotic treatment promoted upregulation of IL-1β, IL-2 and TNF-α. Combination therapy resulted in decreased IL-10 and IFN-γ alongside increased TNF-α. Pro-inflammatory cytokines positively correlated with longer recovery time, whereas anti-inflammatory responses showed treatment-specific associations, with higher IL-10 linked to shorter recovery in patients with typhoid.ConclusionAntimalarial and antityphoidal therapies modulate cytokines levels in complex ways, with elevated pro-inflammatory responses linked to delayed recovery. These findings emphasise the need for personalised treatment strategies guided by patients’ immune profiles.

Introduction

Malaria and typhoid fever, reportedly among the most prevalent and debilitating infectious diseases in subtropical/tropical regions, continue to exert a devastating toll on global public health systems. Despite significant advances in diagnosis and treatments, the morbidity and mortality associated with these infections remain alarmingly high, particularly in vulnerable populations such as children, pregnant women and the elderly. Limited access to essential treatments and services in most of the affected areas, coupled with the emergence of antibiotic and antimalarial resistance, have exacerbated the burden of these diseases.1 Additionally, the complexities of these diseases are compounded by the fact that they often coexist, which can further exacerbate their clinical manifestations and disturb management.2 Against this backdrop, the need for effective treatments that not only eliminate the pathogens responsible for these infections but also mitigate their systemic consequences has become increasingly urgent.

Although effective pathogen-directed therapies exist, clinical outcomes are determined not only by microbial clearance but also by the host immune response, which in malaria and typhoid fever is often characterised by exaggerated pro-inflammatory cytokine production.36 It is important to note that, standard treatments—including artemisinin-based combination therapies for malaria710 and antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin and azithromycin for typhoid fever11 12—are essential for pathogen elimination. However, beyond their antimicrobial effects, accumulating evidence suggests that the therapeutic efficacy of antimalarial and/or antibiotic regimens these organisms may be accompanied by unintended effects on the immune homeostasis13 and eventually modulate treatment outcome. Clarifying whether standard therapies alter inflammatory cytokine profiles—and how such changes relate to recovery—is critical for optimising treatment strategies and mitigating potential unintended effects. This study therefore investigated the impact of antimalarial and antibiotic treatments on inflammatory cytokine dynamics in patients with malaria and typhoid fever, with the aim of informing more targeted and immune-informed therapeutic approaches.

Methods

Study design and population

This prospective observational cohort study was conducted at the Obala District Hospital, a rural health facility located in the Centre Region of Cameroon, from September 2022 to June 2023. Participants were followed from enrolment through post-treatment assessment to evaluate clinical and immunological dynamics. The study population consisted of individuals aged 10 years and above presenting to Obala District Hospital with laboratory-confirmed malaria and/or typhoid fever during the study period. Malaria was confirmed by microscopy, while typhoid fever was confirmed by blood culture. The source population included all febrile patients seeking care at the hospital during the study period, as well as apparently healthy individuals residing in the same community who had no clinical evidence of infection.

Eligibility criteria

Participants were included if they were aged ≥10 years and had a confirmed diagnosis of malaria and/or typhoid fever and provided written informed consent or assent. Apparently healthy controls without signs of infection were also eligible. Individuals were excluded if they were unable to provide consent/assent, had coinfections with other significant illnesses, known immunosuppression, prior exposure to antimalarial or antibiotic therapy within 4 weeks preceding enrolment or were pregnant or breastfeeding.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

The sample size for this comparative analysis was determined based on the expected variance in inflammatory response markers among patients in a previous report.14 Assuming an SD of 120 units and a minimum detectable difference (effect size) of 87 units between the study cohorts, a power analysis indicated that a sample of 30 participants per group is sufficient to achieve 80% statistical power with a 5% level of significance. Consequently, a total of 120 participants were targeted for recruitment, distributed equally (n=30 per group) across the four study arms: malaria mono-infection, typhoid fever mono-infection, malaria–typhoid coinfection and a healthy control group. However, due to strict inclusion criteria during the study period and logistical constraints, a total of 70 participants were retained. The participants were categorised into four groups: typhoid fever only (n=20), malaria only (n=20), malaria–typhoid coinfection (typhomalaria, n=15) and apparently healthy controls (n=15). Controls were selected from the same community and matched demographically and socioeconomically to coinfected participants at an approximate 1:1 ratio to ensure comparability between groups.

Data collection, sample processing and data quality control

At enrolment, trained study personnel collected sociodemographic and clinical information using standardised case report forms, including age, sex, occupation, medical history, presenting symptoms, physical examination findings, prior medications and hospitalisation status. Follow-up assessments were conducted on day 5 (±2 days) to document clinical progression and treatment outcomes. Although follow-up assessments were centred on day 5 post-treatment, participants were practically reviewed within a day 3 to day 7 window due to variations in patient return schedules, logistical and clinical follow-up feasibility. This limited time variation was intended to capture overall post-treatment inflammatory changes rather than acute day-to-day fluctuations.

Venous blood samples were collected at baseline (prior to treatment initiation) and on day 5 (±2 days) post-enrolment. Samples were centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min to obtain serum and plasma for analysis. Haematological parameters were measured using an automated haematology analyser (BC-5000VET, Guangdong, China) within 1 hour of collection. Daily calibration and internal quality control procedures were conducted according to manufacturer guidelines, and participation in external quality assurance programmes ensured analytical reliability.

Inflammatory cytokines (interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interferon (IFN)-γ) levels were quantified in plasma at baseline and on day 5 (±2) using commercially available antigen-capture ELISA kits (Melsin Medical Co, Ltd, China) following manufacturer protocols. Optical density readings were measured at 450 nm, and cytokine concentrations were calculated using standard calibration curves. All laboratory procedures adhered to Good Clinical Practice standards.

Study variables

The dependent variables included inflammatory cytokine levels (IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, IFN-γ), haematological parameters and clinical outcome (resolved vs unresolved). Independent variables comprised infection status (malaria, typhoid fever, coinfection or control), treatment status (baseline vs day 5±2), sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, occupation) and clinical characteristics (symptom severity, duration and hospitalisation status).

Operational definitions

A malaria case (malaria+) was defined as microscopy-confirmed Plasmodium infection while a typhoid fever case (Typh+) was defined as blood culture-confirmed Salmonella typhi infection. Coinfection (typhomalaria) referred to simultaneous laboratory confirmation of malaria and typhoid fever. Clinical outcome was assessed on day 7 by clinicians and categorised as ‘resolved’ (complete disappearance of symptoms or marked clinical improvement without need for further intervention) or ‘unresolved’ (persistent or worsening symptoms requiring additional treatment or investigation).

Data analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS V.26 (IBM Corp, USA) and GraphPad Prism V.9.0.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Continuous variables were summarised as means±SD or medians with IQRs, as appropriate. Within-group comparisons of baseline and post-treatment cytokine levels were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d to determine the magnitude of differences. Normality testing indicated non-normal distribution of cytokine data; consequently, the Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used to evaluate associations between cytokine levels and clinical recovery. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to assess the predictive performance of cytokine levels for treatment outcomes. Optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden index (maximum sensitivity+specificity − 1). Statistical significance was set at p<0.05 at 95% CI.

There was no significant variation in gender distribution across all study groups, with the highest female-to-male ratio observed in the typhoid(+) group at 1.86. The mean temperature was significantly elevated in all patient groups compared with the healthy control group (p<0.05). Furthermore, the median parasite density in the malaria(+) group was significantly higher than that in the typhomalaria(+) group.

The analysis of clinical presentations showed that most patients with malaria sought medical care within 1–3 days after their symptoms began. In contrast, patients with typhoid fever and those with coinfections typically arrived at the hospital between 4 and 6 days after their symptoms started. On average, patients with typhoid fever reported their symptoms for about 4.95 (±2.54) days before seeking treatment. Among the 55 patients enrolled in the study, 19 were hospitalised, while 6 chose not to be admitted. Most patients with typhoid fever who required hospitalisation stayed for 3 days or more.

The majority of patients adhered to the full course of hospital-prescribed treatments, which included antimalarials, antibiotics and other necessary medications tailored to their individual diagnoses. Notably, approximately 22% (12/55) patients chose to supplement their treatment with non-pharmaceutical remedies, such as herbal medicine, reflecting a trend observed in certain populations where complementary and alternative therapies are commonly sought (online supplemental table 1). However, this act had no measurable association with recovery outcomes.

Post-treatment clinical recovery rates varied significantly by infection type within the study group

By day 5 (±2) of treatment, the overall clinical recovery rate was recorded at 52.73%. This rate demonstrated significant variation among the different patient groups, highlighting the distinct responses to treatment based on the underlying infections. Specifically, the malaria(+) group exhibited the highest recovery rate at 65.0% (13/20), suggesting effective therapeutic outcomes for malaria cases. In contrast, the typhoid(+) group showed a lower recovery rate of 35.0% (7/20), indicating a more prolonged illness or delayed response to antibiotic therapy. Meanwhile, the typho-malaria(+) group achieved a recovery rate of 60.0% (9/15), showcasing the complexity and challenges associated with concurrent infections (refer to figure 1E).

Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and platelet counts varied significantly following treatments

The haematological parameters were assessed before and post-treatment in individuals infected with malaria and typhoid, with a focus on haemoglobin levels, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) and platelet counts. Our findings revealed no statistically significant differences in haemoglobin levels across mono-infected groups following treatment. However, a notable increase was documented in the coinfected group (p=0.0282) from baseline to day 5 (±2) (figure 1A–D).

From admission to day 5 (±2), a notably significant reduction in the NLR was documented in the malaria group (p=0.0068) following the intervention. In contrast, a significant decrease in the MLR was observed in all groups post-treatment. Furthermore, platelet counts exhibited a significant elevation in both the malaria (p=0.0192) and typhoid (p=0.0057) groups after the intervention.

Treatment-induced variability in pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines among patients suggests the need for tailored therapeutic strategies in co-infections

The levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines varied considerably following treatment (see figure 2 for a graphical representation of these results). For instance, IL-1β levels were significantly elevated in the malaria(+) group (p=0.0436), whereas a statistically insignificant fall was observed in the coinfected group (p=0.5016). In addition, IL-2 levels demonstrated significant increases in both the malaria(+) (p=0.0023) and typhoid(+) groups (p<0.0001). Conversely, IFN-γ levels were significantly reduced in both the malaria(+) (p=0.0230) and coinfected groups (p=0.0134). Moreover, TNF-α levels exhibited significant elevations in both the typhoid(+) (p=0.0032) and coinfected groups (p=0.0023). However, it is important to note that no significant changes were observed in IL-6 levels between the pre-treatment and post-treatment periods across all study groups (p>0.05).

ROC analysis indicated the potential utility of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines in distinguishing treatment outcomes

The ROC was used to quantify the diagnostic performance of various pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines to discriminate between the clinical outcome of the patients (‘resolved’ and ‘unresolved’). The area under the curve (AUC) values, specificities, sensitivities and cut-off values in the different study groups are presented in online supplemental table 2. In the malaria group, IL-1β exhibited good discriminatory capacity (AUC=87.8%, p=0.010), as did IL-6 (AUC=80.1%, p=0.036). IFN-γ demonstrated a high discriminatory performance (AUC=93.6%, p=0.003), suggesting potential utility as a predictive biomarker. IL-10 also displayed promising discriminatory ability (AUC=0.897, p=0.007). Within the typhoid group, both IL-1β (AUC=84.3%, p=0.019) and IFN-γ (AUC=85.7%, p=0.015) demonstrated moderate discriminatory capacity to predict a positive clinical outcome of the patients (figure 4).

However, in the typhomalaria group, IL-2 demonstrated significantly high discriminatory ability (AUC=97.8%, p=0.004), suggesting a possible association with treatment outcome. Similarly, IL-6 and IFN-γ showed significant discriminatory performance (AUC=93.3%, p=0.009 for both), while IL-10 exhibited good discriminatory potential (AUC=89.7%, p=0.007).

The strong correlations among inflammatory cytokines suggest prolonged synergistic inflammatory responses which may enhance delayed recovery

Spearman’s correlation analysis was employed to assess the interrelationships among post-treatment inflammatory cytokines and recovery time. The results indicate a complex network of associations between these immune markers (online supplemental table 3). For instance, very strong positive correlations were observed between IL-6 and IL-10 (ρ=0.761, p<0.001), IFN-γ and IL-1β (ρ=0.813, p<0.001), and IL-1β and TNF-α (ρ=0.551, p=0.027) in the malaria group, suggesting a rather synergistic inflammatory response. Conversely, an insignificant negative correlation was found between IL-2 and IL-6 (r=−0.246, p=0.310) in the malaria group, indicating potential antagonistic effects. Similarly, a moderate positive correlation was observed between IL-1β and IFN-γ (ρ=0.534, p=0.027), in the typhoid group. In the coinfection group, a very strong positive correlation between IL-2 and IL-6 (ρ=0.697, p=0.006) with a moderate negative correlation between IL-10 and IL-2 (ρ=−0.556, p=0.039).

The interrelationships between malaria recovery time and post-treatment inflammatory cytokines gave a positive correlation between recovery time and the levels of several cytokines: IL-10 (ρ=0.513, p=0.025), IL-1β (ρ=0.607, p=0.006), IL-6 (ρ=0.562, p=0.012), IFN-γ (ρ=0.682, p=0.001) and TNF-α (ρ=0.592, p=0.008) (online supplemental table 3). This suggests that elevated levels of these cytokines are associated with prolonged recovery time in patients with malaria. Within the typhoid group, a positive correlation was identified between recovery time and IL-1β (ρ=0.559, p=0.020), whereas an insignificant negative correlation was observed with IL-10 (ρ=−0.124, p=0.634).

In patients with coinfection, positive correlations were noted between recovery time and the levels of IL-1β (ρ=0.544, p=0.044), IL-2 (ρ=0.725, p=0.003), IL-6 (ρ=0.738, p=0.003) and IFN-γ (ρ=0.609, p=0.021). In contrast, significant negative correlations were found between recovery time and IL-10 (ρ=−0.561, p=0.037), along with a non-significant negative correlation with IL-4 (ρ=−0.131, p=0.656).

Discussion

The interplay between infectious diseases and the host immune response is a critical area of investigation, particularly for conditions like malaria and typhoid fever. While conventional treatments, including antimalarials and antibiotics, are vital for pathogen clearance, they may also unintentionally modulate the host’s immune response. The intricate network of inflammatory cytokines plays a pivotal role in shaping the outcome of these infections, influencing both recovery time and the potential for complications. This underscores the need for a deeper understanding of how treatment regimens impact immune biomarkers, ultimately informing more effective patient management strategies.

A key observation in this study was the marked difference in the time to seek medical care by the patients. Patients with with malaria tend to respond promptly, seeking treatment within 1–3 days of symptom onset, which is critical for effective management and reducing undesired outcome. In contrast, patients with typhoid fever exhibit delays, averaging nearly 5 days, which can lead to serious complications. Interestingly, a recent study15 reported that 20.9% of patients with malaria do not seek medical care, 9.3% turn to herbalists, while only 23% sought formal medical help, indicating a substantial reliance on alternative treatments for malaria. Patients with typhoid ultimately sought medical help, but they do not do so as quickly as one might hope or expect. This suggests a cultural influence about typhoid fever. This can be confirmed by the fact that, a notable minority supplemented their treatment with herbal medicine. These findings highlight the need for targeted public health interventions that address the disparities in awareness and access to care for both diseases, promoting timely treatment practices and integrating traditional and modern healthcare approaches. The low recovery rate (35.0%) of patients with typhoid fever raises important concerns about the efficacy of treatment and the potential for severe complications associated with untreated typhoid infections. This lower recovery rate aligns with existing literature highlighting that about 2–5% of recovered humans still harbour the bacteria even after treatment.16 This was attributed to the limited clinical efficacy of antibiotics with corresponding relapse response to treatment. In comparison, the recovery rate for the patients with typhomalaria, which stands at 60.0% (9/15), reflects the complexities inherent in managing concurrent infections. This does not only confirm the interaction between malaria and typhoid and discussed in a recent report17 but also emphasises the issue of polypharmacy and the potential for overlapping treatment regimens that may affect patient outcomes. However, early diagnosis and the initiation of appropriate therapy have proven crucial for improving clinical outcomes in infectious diseases, as highlighted by WHO guidelines on the management of febrile illnesses in endemic regions.

Our findings also reveal significant alterations in pro-inflammatory cytokine levels following treatment. These results align with previous malaria studies3 4 and suggest that malaria in coinfections differentially modulate cytokine responses. Elevated serum levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ, IL-6 and TNF-α receptor have been reported in S. Typhi-infected patients, specifically highlighting the clinical relevance of these cytokines in acute infections.18 19 However, following treatment regimes, levels IL-6 and IFN-γ were reported to significantly drop in patients with typhoid following treatment.18 Remarkably, higher levels of IL-6 were associated with poorer outcomes. However, Keuter et al20 demonstrated that during the acute phase of typhoid fever, the levels of TNF-α and IL-6, are depressed, yet restored in the convalescent phase, illustrating the dynamic nature of the immune response throughout the infection. In other studies,6 21 22 specific T-cell responses, including IFN-γ secretion were observed, further indicating the complexity of the immune response in typhoid infection. The differences in cytokine profiles between malaria and typhoid may partly explain the contrasting immune responses observed in our study, potentially influenced by variations in pathogen behaviour and host immune activation. Clinically, targeting cytokines such as IL-2, IL-6, TNF-α and IFN-γ could potentially be beneficial in mitigating the inflammatory response and improving outcomes in patients with malaria and typhoid infections. In addition to the observed pro-inflammatory responses, we identified distinct patterns of anti-inflammatory cytokine expression. Indeed, our results align with previous studies4 5 that have implicated IL-10 in the pathogenesis of malaria. Notably, no significant changes in IL-10 levels were observed in the typhoid group, indicating a differential regulation of the anti-inflammatory response between these two infections.20 This differential regulation suggests that malaria not only alters immune reactivity, as discussed by ref.,23 but also actively modulates the host immune response in ways that prevent the acquisition of specific immunity. Furthermore, the persistence of altered immune responses following the clearance of parasites supports the notion that the impact of malaria on the immune system is multifaceted. Our findings highlight the potential therapeutic value of targeting IL-10 or its associated pathways in the treatment of malaria and typhoid.

In the evaluation of the diagnostic performance of targeted pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines to discriminate between the clinical outcome of the patients (‘resolved’ and ‘unresolved’) following treatment, significant discriminatory capacities were recorded in certain cytokines. In antimalarial treated patients, IFN-γ and IL-10 exhibited significant discriminatory performance as potential predictors of antimalarial treatment success. Additionally, in patients undergoing treatment with antityphoidal drugs, both IL-1β and IFN-γ similarly exhibited moderate discriminatory capacities; while in the cohort receiving combination therapy, IFN-γ, IL-6 and IL-2 displayed significant discriminatory performance. These results indicate that cytokines can somewhat effectively differentiate between patients who will respond positively to treatment and those who may not. The significant levels of performance recorded by IFN-γ in all treatment regimens suggests that it plays a critical role in the immune response to malaria and typhoid fever, potentially reflecting the activation of T-helper cells and the overall immune system’s capacity to mount an effective response against Plasmodium and Salmonella organisms. As such, IFN-γ could serve not only as a predictive marker but also as an important mediator of the immune mechanisms involved in combating pathogens. On the other hand, IL-10 is known for its immunoregulatory properties, often functioning to limit inflammatory responses and promote resolution of inflammation.24 The ability of IL-10 to discriminate between successful and unsuccessful treatment outcomes may reflect its role in moderating the immune response and ensuring that the body can effectively clear the infection while minimising collateral damage. Comparing results with other studies, similar patterns have been observed in different infectious diseases. For instance, a study on COVID-19 patients found that elevated levels of IL-6 and TNF-α were associated with severe disease and prolonged recovery.25 Similarly, research on chikungunya and dengue virus infections identified upregulated levels of IL-4 in patients with more severe symptoms.26 These observations reinforce the notion that cytokines are pivotal in shaping inflammatory responses and affecting recovery times across diverse infectious diseases. Overall, targeting specific inflammatory cytokines could represent a promising therapeutic strategy to enhance recovery outcomes.

Limitations of the study

Despite the important insights generated, this study has certain limitations. First, the sample size of 70 participants, though adequate for comparative analysis, limited the generalisability of the findings to broader populations beyond the study setting. Second, the study design may not fully establish causality between cytokine modulation and treatment outcomes, as immune responses can be influenced by multiple host and environmental factors. Third, variations in baseline immune status, nutritional condition, genetic polymorphisms and comorbidities were not exhaustively controlled for, which may have affected cytokine expression patterns. Additionally, the follow-up period may not have been long enough to assess long-term immunological recovery or relapse. Laboratory-based cytokine quantification, while reliable, may also be influenced by pre-analytical and analytical variability.

Conclusion

This study provides a critical comparative analysis of pathogen-induced inflammatory responses to antimalarial and antityphoidal therapies, underscoring the importance of immune regulation in treatment outcomes. Our findings demonstrate distinct cytokine profiles associated with malaria and typhoid fever, revealing that elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly IFN-γ, may serve as a potential indicator of treatment response. The notably lower recovery rates in patients with typhoid fever highlight the urgency for timely medical intervention and the need for optimising therapeutic strategies. Additionally, the observed changes in haematological parameters following treatment suggest that monitoring specific inflammatory biomarkers could enhance our ability to predict recovery outcomes. This innovative approach reinforces the necessity of integrating immune response modulation into treatment regimens, which may improve the overall efficacy of therapeutic interventions.

By advancing our understanding of cytokine dynamics in the context of infectious diseases, this research paves the way for precision medicine strategies that can better manage coinfections.

Recommendations

Future studies should adopt longitudinal designs with extended follow-up periods to better evaluate the temporal dynamics of cytokine responses and long-term recovery outcomes. Increasing the sample size and including multiple health facilities across different epidemiological zones would enhance external validity. Further research should also explore the role of host genetic factors and immune-modulatory mechanisms in shaping treatment responses. Clinically, routine monitoring of key inflammatory biomarkers such as IFN-γ and selected haematological indices could be integrated into treatment evaluation protocols to improve prognostic accuracy. Finally, therapeutic strategies that incorporate immune response modulation alongside pathogen clearance should be explored to optimise treatment efficacy and reduce morbidity, particularly in patients with typhoid fever.
