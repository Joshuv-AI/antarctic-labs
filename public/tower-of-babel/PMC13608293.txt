Cardiometabolic Risk and Awareness of Anthracycline-Related Cardiotoxicity in Survivors of Childhood Cancer: A Population-Based Study
Authors: Vigdís H. Viggósdóttir, Hróðmar Helgason, Helga Jónsdóttir, Thor Aspelund, Ragnar Bjarnason, Marianne Jarfelt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anthracycline chemotherapy is associated with cardiotoxicity, underscoring the importance of minimizing modifiable cardiac risk factors in affected individuals. This study evaluated cardiac function, prevalence of metabolic syndrome and smoking, and awareness of prior cardiotoxic treatment among adult survivors of childhood cancer treated with anthracyclines at the pediatric oncology service in Iceland between 1981 and 2011 (n=89). Data were collected from medical records, self-reported questionnaires, and clinical assessments conducted between 2016 and 2019. Of 76 eligible survivors, 60 participated (29 females, 31 males; aged 20 to 48 y). Two individuals were excluded before enrollment due to previously diagnosed heart failure. Left ventricular dilation was observed in 15 participants (25%), predominantly mild to moderate, with 1 severe case. The prevalence was lower after BSA indexing. Metabolic syndrome was present in 20% of participants, and 20% reported current smoking. Approximately half of the participants reported undergoing echocardiography within the past 10 years, while only 10% were aware that their cancer treatment could cause cardiotoxicity. These findings suggest that survivors treated with anthracyclines require systematic surveillance and targeted interventions to address modifiable cardiovascular risk factors. Increasing awareness of prior cardiotoxic cancer treatment among survivors and health care providers may facilitate cardiovascular risk reduction.

BACKGROUND

The annual incidence of childhood cancer (0 to 18 y) in Iceland is comparable to that of other Nordic countries.1 The survival of childhood cancer has improved substantially over recent decades, with the 5-year survival rate now exceeding 80%.2 A similar improvement has been observed in Iceland, where a nationwide population-based study reported a 5-year survival rate of 81.2%.3 While advances in treatment and care have improved survival, cancer therapies can have long-lasting impacts on health, quality of life, and life expectancy. As survival continues to improve, late effects have emerged as a growing public health concern among childhood cancer survivors (CCS).

The impact of cancer treatment on children differs significantly from that in adults, especially among younger children. Late effects of childhood cancer treatment may include impaired growth and development, psychosocial challenges, organ dysfunction, fertility issues, and subsequent neoplasms.4,5 The Childhood Cancer Survivor Study, comparing survivors treated between 1970 and 1987 with their siblings, reported that by age 24, CCS have a cumulative incidence of severe to fatal health problems comparable to that of siblings at the age of 50.5 The leading causes of treatment-related mortality in CCS are subsequent neoplasms and cardiovascular diseases,6 with cardiac events predominantly associated with high-dose anthracyclines (including mitoxantrone) and chest radiation.7

Anthracyclines have significantly contributed to improved survival rates for hematologic malignancies and solid tumors over recent decades,8 but are associated with a substantial risk of cardiotoxicity, particularly at cumulative doxorubicin-equivalent doses ≥250 mg/m2.9,10 Although the risk increases with higher cumulative doses, cardiotoxicity has also been reported at lower exposure.11,12 Additional factors may amplify this risk, including chest radiation, young age at treatment, pre-existing cardiovascular conditions, and family history of heart disease.13 Approximately 60% of CCS have been exposed to anthracyclines, chest radiation, or both, placing them at risk for cardiac complications.9

Anthracycline-induced cardiac injury may initially be asymptomatic; therefore, early detection is important, as timely recognition can improve long-term outcomes14,15 The International Guideline Harmonization Group for Late Effects of Childhood Cancer (IGHG) recommends regular echocardiographic surveillance of left ventricular ejection fraction in CCS treated with moderate- to high-dose anthracyclines (≥100 mg/m2, doxorubicin-equivalent anthracycline doses) and/or high-dose chest radiation (≥35 Gy, reasonable for moderate doses ≥ 15 Gy). For those exposed to low doses, routine long-term echocardiographic surveillance is not recommended. Regardless of anthracycline or chest radiation dose, screening for cardiovascular risk factors is recommended.9,10

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) has been identified as a prevalent cardiometabolic late effect that increases the risk of long-term health complications and premature mortality among CCS.16 MetS prevalence is higher in CCS than in their siblings17 and is influenced by treatment exposures and treatment-related late effects, environmental factors, and genetics.18 Although global prevalence varies with demographic trends, MetS remains a significant concern due to its strong association with severe conditions such as cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes.18,19

This population-based, cross-sectional study aimed to assess the prevalence of cardiotoxicity and cardiometabolic risk factors, including metabolic syndrome and smoking, among adult Icelandic CCS treated with anthracyclines. We also assessed participants’ awareness of potential cardiac late effects before their first visit to a dedicated childhood cancer late-effects follow-up center established in 2016.

METHODS

Study Population

The study population consisted of former patients treated by the pediatric oncology service in Iceland. Pediatric cancer care in Iceland is centralized at the Children’s Hospital, Landspitali—University Hospital (LSH) in Reykjavik. Eligible participants were adults (18 y or older) diagnosed with cancer before the age of 18 between 1981 and 2011 and treated with anthracyclines (n=89). Exclusion criteria included prior heart failure, recent cancer diagnosis (5 y or younger), Down syndrome, cognitive disability, or residence outside Iceland.

Study Design

This population-based cohort study included descriptive analyses and subgroup comparisons.

Setting

Clinical assessments were conducted at the Late Effects Center (LEC) for adult CCS at the Children’s Hospital—LSH. The study was initiated in parallel with the establishment of the LEC. Before this, no specialized late-effect follow-up was available for adult CCS in Iceland. Iceland’s health care system is tax-funded, and patient fees are comparatively low. Standard outpatient nursing consultation and laboratory fees applied according to hospital policy, irrespective of study participation, and were subject to national reimbursement caps. Echocardiography was provided free of charge.

Recruitment and Consent

Participants were identified through a quality registry maintained by the pediatric oncology service at LSH, containing retrospectively compiled data on pediatric oncology patients diagnosed from 1981 onwards. Potential participants were contacted by telephone and informed about the study and the LEC service. If no phone number was available, a letter introducing both the LEC and the study was sent to the registered address of eligible participants. To increase awareness, the LEC service was also promoted through Icelandic cancer support associations, the LSH website, and media coverage.

Participants were informed that answering the study questionnaire implied consent for linking medical records with self-reported data. Each participant was identified using a unique numeric study code. Those who expressed interest in participating received a formal introduction letter and the questionnaire via email. Participation was voluntary and not required to gain access to the LEC service.

Data Collection

Data were gathered between November 2016 and March 2019, including: (1) childhood cancer treatment history; (2) self-reported data; and (3) clinical assessments during the LEC visit.

Childhood Cancer Treatment History

Medical records were reviewed and summarized by the researcher (VHV) into a structured survivorship profile, outlining each participant’s cancer history, treatment exposures, and potential late effects. This summary informed both the study and clinical follow-up.

Self-Reported Data

The study questionnaire was available in paper format and via REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture), a secure, web-based software platform for data capture in research studies,20,21 hosted by the University of Iceland.

Participants were instructed to complete the questionnaire independently in advance of their LEC clinical visit. An automatic reminder was sent through REDCap, and those who had not responded were reminded during their clinical visit.

The study questionnaire addressed cardiovascular diseases or symptoms, medications related to MetS criteria, smoking habits, awareness of the potential cardiotoxic effects of anthracyclines, and cardiac follow-up. Specific questions were adapted from validated sources, including the Icelandic Health and Well-Being Study, the Childhood Cancer Survivor Study, and prior research on CCS awareness of late effects (see details in Appendix A, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JPHO/A802).

Clinical Assessments

Clinical assessments performed during the LEC visit included echocardiography, anthropometric measurements, and blood pressure, as described below. Blood tests were ordered through the LSH electronic system by LEC staff. Fasting blood samples were drawn, most at the LSH blood sampling department, though some were taken at regional sites. All results were accessible using the electronic medical record.

Echocardiography

Two-dimensional, M-mode, and Doppler echocardiography were performed using a Siemens Sequoia SC2000 system (Siemens Medical Solutions, Mountain View, CA). All examinations were performed and interpreted by the same pediatric cardiologist (H.H.).

Left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVIDd) and left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVIDs) were measured using M-mode and confirmed with corresponding 2-dimensional measurements. Left ventricular dimensions were evaluated using sex-specific reference values for adults defined by the British Society of Echocardiography (BSE). For females, normal LVIDd values were 35 to 51 mm, with LV dilation considered mild if 52 to 55 mm, moderate 56 to 59 mm, and severe when dilation was >59 mm. For males, normal LVIDd values were 37 to 56 mm; mild dilation, 57 to 61 mm; moderate dilation, 62 to 65 mm; and severe dilation, >65 mm.22 Interventricular septal thickness in diastole (IVSd) and left ventricular posterior wall thickness in diastole (LVPWd) were also assessed according to BSE reference values.22 As a sensitivity analysis, sex-specific BSA-indexed upper reference limits (30.4 mm/m2 for males and 32.5 mm/m2 for females) and Z-scores for LVIDd were calculated using published means and SDs from the New Echocardiography Reference Ranges for Aotearoa (NewERA) study, derived from a healthy European adult cohort aged 18 to 50 years (n=139, 74 females).23

Fractional shortening (FS), a measure of LV systolic function derived from M-mode measurements, was calculated as FS (%)=(LVIDd − LVIDs)/LVIDd × 100. Normal values range from ∼25% to 45%, corresponding to a left ventricular ejection fraction of about 55% to 70%.24

Mitral inflow Doppler recordings were obtained to assess E- and A-wave velocities and the E/A ratio. Multiple measurements were acquired during both inspiration and expiration, and mean values were used for analysis. Isovolumetric relaxation time (IVRT) was measured using pulsed-wave Doppler with the sample volume positioned between the anterior mitral leaflet and the aortic root, allowing assessment of the interval between aortic valve closure and mitral valve opening. Reference ranges were based on the American Society of Echocardiography guidelines.25

Right ventricular function was not assessed.

Anthropometric Measurements

The same health professional (V.H.V.) conducted anthropometric measurements, which included waist circumference, height, and weight. Waist circumference was measured using a nonstretchable tape measure with participants standing. The measurement was taken at the midpoint between the lowest part of the lateral costal arch and the iliac crest (approximately at the level of the umbilicus) at the end of a normal exhalation.

Blood Pressure

Blood pressure was measured during the participant’s visit to the LEC using a digital monitor. Although devices may have varied, the same health care professional (V.H.V.) conducted all measurements seated. All devices used in the outpatient clinic are regularly calibrated and maintained to ensure reliable measurements. According to the 2024 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines,26 blood pressure was classified based on reference values as nonelevated (<120/70 mm Hg), elevated (120 to 139/70 to 89 mm Hg), or hypertensive (≥140/≥90 mm Hg).

Blood Tests

Blood tests included those relevant to metabolic syndrome (MetS) criteria (see MetS below) and cardiac biomarkers: troponin T (ref. <14 ng/L27), and plasma brain natriuretic peptide (BNP), with reference values <150 ng/L for females and <100 ng/L for males.28

Anthracycline Equivalent Doses and Cardiotoxicity Risk Definition

Anthracycline equivalent doses were calculated based on doxorubicin-equivalent conversion factors from the Children’s Oncology Group Long-Term Follow-Up Guidelines29: doxorubicin (×1.0), daunorubicin (×0.5), epirubicin (×0.67), idarubicin (×5.0), and mitoxantrone (×10.0). Although mitoxantrone is not a classic anthracycline, it is included due to its similar cardiotoxic profile.

Cardiotoxicity risk was categorized according to the IGHG cardiomyopathy surveillance guidelines9,10 as low (<100 mg/m2), moderate (100 to 249 mg/m2), or high (≥250 mg/m2 or moderate/high-dose anthracyclines combined with chest radiation ≥15 Gy). For statistical analyses, cardiotoxic exposure was dichotomized into lower (<250 mg/m2 without chest radiation ≥15 Gy) versus higher risk (≥250 mg/m2 or ≥100 mg/m2 with chest radiation ≥15 Gy) due to small numbers in some categories. Age at diagnosis was likewise dichotomized (<10 vs. ≥10 y) for selected subgroup analyses.

Metabolic Syndrome Definition

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) was defined according to the International Diabetes Federation criteria30 as central obesity (waist circumference ≥80 cm for females, ≥94 cm for males [Europids], or BMI ≥30 kg/m2) plus at least 2 of the following: elevated triglycerides (≥1.7 mmol/L or on treatment), reduced HDL cholesterol (<1.29 mmol/L for females, <1.03 mmol/L for males or on treatment), elevated fasting glucose (≥5.6 mmol/L or diagnosis of type 2 diabetes), and elevated blood pressure (systolic ≥130 mm Hg, diastolic ≥85 mm Hg, or on antihypertensive treatment).

Statistical Analysis

The primary outcome measures were cardiac function parameters and the presence of cardiometabolic risk factors (BMI, waist circumference, blood pressure, lipid profile, fasting blood glucose), smoking, and participants’ awareness of treatment-related health risks.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (v29.0.1.0). Missing data were excluded pairwise per analysis, and no imputations were performed due to the small sample size. Descriptive statistics were prioritized over causal inference.

Continuous variables are presented as mean±SD or median (range), as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages.

Group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the χ2 or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Nonparametric tests were used due to the small sample size and the non-normal distribution of selected variables. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05. Specific analyses included comparisons of age at diagnosis and time since diagnosis (Mann-Whitney U), and comparisons between participants and nonparticipants on sex, age group, and cancer diagnosis (χ2).

RESULTS

Participants’ Characteristics and Recruitment

In total, 89 childhood cancer survivors (CCS) were initially identified for the study. However, 13 of these individuals did not meet the inclusion criteria: 2 had a prior diagnosis of cardiac failure, 1 had experienced a recent relapse, 4 did not qualify based on the questionnaire criteria (including 2 with Down syndrome), and 6 were residing outside of Iceland. Thus, 76 CCS were considered eligible for inclusion.

Of these 76, 3 did not respond, 2 declined to participate, and 5 were unable to participate during the study period. A total of 66 individuals agreed to consider participation and were provided access to the online questionnaire. Completing the questionnaire was deemed to represent informed consent. However, 4 individuals did not complete the questionnaire and were therefore excluded from analysis. In 2 additional cases, echocardiography data were inadequate due to technical issues.

Ultimately, 60 participants (67.4% of the eligible population) were included in the final analysis.

Demographic and cancer-related characteristics of the study participants and nonparticipants were compared to assess potential selection bias. No statistically significant differences were identified (Table 1).

Timing of Self-Reported Data and Clinical Assessments

All but 3 participants completed the questionnaire before clinical assessment and consultation at the Late Effect Center (LEC). The 3 participants who completed it afterward did so within 17 days of their visit. Among those who completed the questionnaire in advance, the time interval ranged from the same day (before the consultation) to 411 days before the visit (M=39.2 d, SD=67.3). The majority (91.7%) completed the questionnaire within 6 months before their visit.

Demographic and Cancer-Related Characteristics

The study included 60 participants, 29 females and 31 males, aged 20.1 to 48.6 years (M=29.6, SD=7.4). Time since primary cancer diagnosis ranged from 6.7 to 37.2 years (M=20.6, SD=8.3), and age at diagnosis ranged from 5 months to 17 years (M=9.0, SD=5.3).

Primary cancer diagnoses included leukemia, lymphoma, bone sarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, nephroblastoma, and nasopharyngeal carcinoma. Four participants had experienced a relapse or a secondary cancer diagnosis, and in all cases, anthracycline drugs were included in 1 additional course of treatment before the age of 18.

Participants’ anthracycline cumulative doses, expressed as doxorubicin equivalents, ranged from 40 to 780 mg/m2 (M=259.1, SD=187.3, Mdn=186.9), with the highest single dose ranging from 25 to 100.5 mg/m2 (M=44.9, SD=21.0, Mdn=40.0) (further details are provided in Table 2).

Radiation Therapy

Among participants with radiotherapy fields involving the heart or the endocrine system, 7 (11.7%) had a history of mediastinal radiation: 4 following a diagnosis of Hodgkin lymphoma (21.0 to 30.6 Gy) and 3 as part of total body irradiation (TBI) before hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.

Ten participants (16.7%) had undergone partial or whole-brain radiation. This included 3 who received TBI, 6 who underwent cranial radiation as central nervous system prophylaxis during treatment for acute lymphoblastic leukemia (12 to 18 Gy), and 1 who received radiation for nasopharyngeal carcinoma (60 Gy). Four participants had been exposed to radiation fields, including the pancreas.

As LVIDd and FS were available for all participants, LV dilation and systolic function are described for the full cohort of 60 participants. Overall, LV dilation was identified in 15 participants (25%). One additional participant exhibited reduced FS (23.9%) without LV dilation, bringing the total to 16 participants (27%) with abnormalities in LV size or systolic function.

Among females, 1 participant demonstrated severe LV dilation (LVIDd 63.5 mm). She had untreated hypertension and LV hypertrophy and had received a cumulative doxorubicin-equivalent anthracycline dose at the upper end of the range documented among the participants (780 mg/m2). Six additional females had mild LV dilation, all with FS values within normal limits. Among males, 8 participants exhibited LV dilation (6 mild and 2 moderate), with no severe cases, and preserved FS in all.

Compared with NewERA BSA-indexed upper reference limits, the prevalence of LV dilation decreased to 5% (3/60). Overall, 13 participants changed from dilated to nondilated after indexing, while 1 participant changed from nondilated to dilated (see Table A1 in Appendix B, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JPHO/A803). Exploratory sex-specific Z-score analysis yielded comparable findings: only 1 participant had a value ≥2 SD above the NewERA mean, while the other 2 participants exceeding the indexed reference limits had values close to this threshold (Z-scores 1.79 and 1.97).

Evaluation of diastolic parameters (Table 3) showed values within age-specific reference ranges in most participants, except for 1 participant with elevated A-wave velocity. Several participants had IVRT values slightly below the reference range, which was not considered clinically significant.

Cardiac Biomarkers

Troponin T measurements were available for 57 participants, and all values were within normal limits (range: 2 to 13 ng/L). BNP measurements were available for 58 participants. One male and 8 females had values above the sex-specific reference ranges, of which 3 had mild LV dilation. Among females (n=28), BNP ranged from 7 to 383 ng/L (M=84.6, SD=77.40), whereas among males (n=30), it ranged from 5 to 167 ng/L (M=38.3, SD=42.88).

Blood Pressure

Blood pressure measurements were available for all 60 participants. Systolic values ranged from 94 to 156 mm Hg (M=121.5, SD=13.6), while diastolic values ranged from 54 to 95 mm Hg (M=73.2, SD=9.2). Seven participants (11.7%) had systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg, and 3 (5.0%) had diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg; 2 of these had both elevated systolic and diastolic values. A total of 19 participants (31.7%) had nonelevated blood pressure (<120/<70 mm Hg).

Metabolic Syndrome

On the basis of BMI categories, 15 participants (25.0%) were classified as obese and 21 (35.0%) as overweight, indicating that 60% of the cohort had excess body weight. According to the IDF definition, 33 participants (55.0%) met the criterion for central obesity, which is a required component of MetS.

Overall, 12 participants (20.0%) met the IDF criteria for MetS, with an equal distribution between males and females. Among those with MetS, 5 had 2 additional MetS components beyond obesity, 4 had 3, and 3 had 4. Elevated fasting glucose was the most common subfactor among males (n=6), while elevated triglycerides were most prevalent among females (n=5). Self-Reported Cardiometabolic Medication, Symptoms, and Diagnosis History

Two participants reported a physician-diagnosed history of high blood lipids or high cholesterol; however, no participant reported taking medications for these conditions within the past 2 years. None of the participants reported a diagnosis of or medication for diabetes.

One participant who had normal blood pressure reported a history of high blood pressure without treatment with antihypertensive medication within the past 2 years. Another participant reported regular use of medicines for high blood pressure or heart-related conditions (the questionnaire did not differentiate between these), stating a history of having had tachycardia or arrhythmia (also not differentiated in the questionnaire), but not high blood pressure.

No participants reported a history of myocardial infarction, blood clots, coronary artery occlusion, or stroke. Nine participants (15.0%) reported having experienced an episode of tachycardia or arrhythmia, 3 of whom stated that the condition had been diagnosed by a physician. Four participants reported experiencing chest pain, but none reported a physician-diagnosed condition.

Self-Reported Smoking and Nicotine Use

Thirty-seven participants (61.7%) reported never smoking, 12 (20.0%) were current smokers, and 7 were former smokers (data were missing for 4 participants). Among current smokers, 8 smoked daily and 4 at least weekly; 3 former smokers had quit within the past 6 months. Regarding other forms of nicotine use, 11 participants (18.3%) reported using e-cigarettes (vaping), and 7 (11.7%) used oral tobacco. In total, 23 participants (38.3%) reported using nicotine in any form (data were missing for 7 participants).

Self-Reported Awareness of Cardiotoxic Late Effects and Medical Follow-Up

Among the 60 participants, 23 (38.3%) reported awareness of some or all potential late effects of their cancer treatment, all of whom completed the questionnaire before the LEC visit during which late effects were explained. With 1 missing response, 36 participants (60.0%) reported no awareness. Of those aware, 6 (10.0%) specifically mentioned cardiac-related late effects when asked to list the potential late effects. Among these 6 participants, awareness was equally distributed by sex and age at diagnosis (2 in each category: younger than 5, 5 to 9, and 10 y or older); 4 were 30 years or older at study participation, and time from diagnosis to participation had a median of 23.5 years (range: 6.8 to 27.9).

Twenty-five participants (41.7%) reported having consulted a physician regarding their childhood cancer history within the 2 years before participation, including 3 participants who reported receiving regular follow-up for late effects.

A total of 41 participants (68.3%) reported having undergone an echocardiogram at some point, including 14 within the past 5 years and 12 within the past 6 to 10 years. Eighteen participants (30.0%) could not recall ever having one (1 missing value). Among those who had not undergone an echocardiogram in the past 10 years or could not recall ever having one, 10 participants (16.7%) were classified as being at higher risk of cardiomyopathy based on IGHG recommendations.

Most participants (n=54 [90.0%]) reported having had their blood pressure measured by a health care professional within the past 5 years, including 34 (56.7%) within the last year. Four participants had not had their blood pressure measured in over 5 years, and 1 reported never having it measured (1 missing value).

DISCUSSION

In this population-based study of adult childhood cancer survivors (CCS), a considerable proportion of the cohort exhibited echocardiographic abnormalities, mainly left ventricular (LV) dilation. The prevalence of LV dilation was lower when LVIDd was indexed to body surface area (BSA). A substantial burden of cardiovascular risk factors was also observed, with more than one-third of the participants exhibiting at least 1 major cardiovascular risk factor, including metabolic syndrome (MetS) and/or current smoking. Together, these findings indicate that cardiac abnormalities and modifiable cardiovascular risk factors are already present among Icelandic adult CCS, underscoring the importance of regular follow-up care.

Given the young age of the cohort (87% younger than 40 y) and the well-documented progressive nature of anthracycline-induced cardiomyopathy,31 the presence of even borderline LV abnormalities in this population warrants careful interpretation and ongoing observation over time. No statistically significant association was found between cardiotoxic exposure category and LV dilation, which may reflect limited statistical power in this relatively small cohort.

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported a cumulative incidence of clinically overt heart failure of ∼1% among long-term childhood cancer survivors over follow-up periods ranging from 5 to over 70 years after diagnosis.32 As individuals with established heart failure were not included in the present study, the echocardiographic findings primarily reflect survivors without clinically manifest disease, and the observed prevalence of cardiac abnormalities should be interpreted accordingly.

LV dilation was defined using sex-specific British Society of Echocardiography reference values without indexing to body size. In a sensitivity analysis using BSA-indexed reference values from the NewERA study, the prevalence of LV dilation was substantially lower, suggesting that body size may have contributed to some of the observed chamber enlargement, particularly among overweight or obese participants. In addition, tissue Doppler and speckle-tracking strain analysis were not available for routine clinical use in our center during data collection (2016 to 2019) and, therefore, could not be applied, which would have given more detailed information on cardiac function.

Given that the observed cardiac abnormalities were subclinical, our findings support the role of systematic echocardiographic surveillance in identifying early changes. This may facilitate earlier detection and management of individuals at risk of developing clinically overt heart failure. A small proportion of participants had BNP values above the sex-specific reference ranges, both with and without observable LV dilation. The clinical significance of this finding in asymptomatic survivors remains uncertain and should be interpreted cautiously. Previous studies indicate that cardiac biomarkers exhibit variable reliability33 and should therefore not be used in isolation to detect anthracycline-related cardiac dysfunction.9,10

Compared with the previous 5-year interval,9 the 2022 IGHG cardiomyopathy surveillance guidelines recommend echocardiography every 2 years for high-risk individuals, a strategy supported as cost-effective based on findings from their systematic review.10 In our study, nearly half of the high-risk participants had not undergone an echocardiogram in over 10 years, reflecting limited awareness of the cardiac risk, both among CCS and within the health care system. Our results align with earlier studies showing that despite these risks, many survivors remain unaware of potential late effects of their cancer treatment,34 and the severity of these risks is commonly underestimated.35,36 This is clinically relevant, as adherence to follow-up depends on awareness of treatment exposures and late-effect risks among both survivors and health care providers involved in their long-term care. This surveillance gap may delay the identification of cardiac changes in a population at risk for progressive cardiomyopathy. In this context, health care professionals involved in long-term follow-up play a key role in coordinating surveillance, reinforcing adherence to follow-up recommendations, and supporting survivors in navigating long-term care.

As emphasized in the IGHG recommendations,9 regular follow-up is central to screening for and reducing cardiovascular risk,37,38 and survivors should be encouraged to follow established healthy lifestyle guidance, such as that issued by the World Health Organization.39 Metabolic syndrome (MetS) was present in one-fifth of participants, consistent with previous studies among CCS. Prevalence estimates vary widely, from 13.4% to 89.0%, depending on diagnostic criteria and treatment exposures, with cranial or craniospinal radiotherapy or total body irradiation (TBI) most consistently associated with increased risk.40 Although overweight and obesity were common in our cohort, these findings reflect national trends in Iceland.41,42 Nevertheless, excess body weight may contribute to future cardiometabolic risk and warrants attention in survivorship care.

Smoking prevalence in our cohort was 21.4% (12/56), notably higher than in the general Icelandic population, where daily smoking among individuals aged 18 to 44 was reported at 5.0% in 201743 and 8% to 10% in the overall adult population between 2016 and 2019.44 Similar findings have been reported in the Childhood Cancer Survivor Study, where a history of cancer did not appear to reduce the likelihood of smoking.45 These findings may reflect unmet needs in counseling and risk-reduction strategies within survivorship care.

Engaging CCS in long-term care remains a challenge, partly due to variability in follow-up practices. Although structured survivorship care is available in some settings, many survivors navigate care without written treatment summaries or standardized recommendations. Such summaries improve awareness of treatment exposures and adherence to surveillance protocols.46 In Iceland, no formal treatment summaries had been provided before this study, consistent with participants’ limited awareness of potential late effects. Survivorship passports, linking prior treatment to follow-up recommendations, have become an internationally recognized tool for structured care planning. Making survivorship passports accessible within patients’ electronic health records and linking them to evolving recommendations (eg, IGHG) may help survivors and health care providers stay informed.

With the establishment of the Icelandic Late Effect Center, which incorporates the survivorship passport alongside clinical evaluations and provider discussions, an important step has been taken toward strengthening long-term care for childhood cancer survivors.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrates limited awareness of cardiotoxic late effects among at-risk survivors of childhood cancer. Cardiometabolic risk factors and echocardiographic abnormalities were observed in a substantial proportion of Icelandic adult survivors treated with anthracyclines. These findings underscore the need for structured, personalized follow-up care integrating regular cardiac surveillance, cardiovascular risk assessment, and targeted survivor education.

Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the prognostic significance of left ventricular dilation, the influence of body size on echocardiographic findings, and the impact of coexisting cardiometabolic risk factors on long-term cardiovascular outcomes in anthracycline-treated childhood cancer survivors.
