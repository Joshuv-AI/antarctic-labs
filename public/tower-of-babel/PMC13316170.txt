Extracellular vesicles in osteosarcoma: bridging resistance, immunity, and clinical translation
Authors: Baile Mao, Jian Zhang, Lin An, XianJun Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteosarcoma (OS), the most common primary malignant bone tumor in children, adolescents, and young adults, has experienced only limited improvement in 5-year survival over the past four decades, with survival rates remaining approximately 60–70% for localized disease and below 30% for metastatic or refractory cases. Chemoresistance and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) constitute mutually reinforcing barriers to durable disease control, while extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as key mediators linking tumor-intrinsic resistance, microenvironmental remodeling, and metastasis. Under the MISEV2023 framework, OS-derived EV studies face unique methodological challenges, including mineralized matrix interference, non-vesicular particle contamination, platelet-derived EV interference, and variability in isolation workflows. Nevertheless, standardized and OS-adapted EV workflows may improve the reliability of EVs as candidate liquid biopsy biomarkers for chemotherapy-response assessment, prognostic stratification, immune-status evaluation, and minimal residual disease (MRD)-oriented surveillance. Mechanistically, EVs can propagate chemoresistance by transferring ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters and regulatory non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), reprogram metabolism and cell fate through processes such as ferroptosis resistance, lipid synthesis, autophagy-induced dormancy, and stemness acquisition, remodel bone marrow and pulmonary pre-metastatic niches, and contribute to immunosuppression through macrophage polarization, vascular abnormalization, and restricted T-cell infiltration. Emerging EV-based therapeutic strategies include engineered EVs or exosome mimetics for drug and nucleic acid delivery, inhibition or clearance of tumor-derived EVs, and EV-based vaccine concepts combined with immunotherapy. However, most therapeutic evidence remains preclinical or hypothesis-generating, and translation is constrained by stochastic cargo loading, EV heterogeneity, scalable clinical-grade production, age-specific considerations, and limited clinical validation. This review integrates current evidence on EV-mediated chemoresistance, immune remodeling, and clinical translation in OS, aiming to provide a critical framework for future EV-based biomarker development and therapeutic exploration.

Introduction

Epidemiology and therapeutic stagnation in osteosarcoma: decades of plateaued survival

Osteosarcoma (OS) is the most common primary malignant bone tumor in children, adolescents, and young adults, exhibiting a characteristic bimodal age distribution, with incidence peaks during the adolescent growth spurt and in older adults [1], [2]. Since the 1970s, the introduction of neoadjuvant chemotherapy based on high-dose methotrexate, doxorubicin, and cisplatin (MAP), combined with surgical resection, markedly improved outcomes for patients with localized disease and has been considered a milestone in OS treatment [3]. However, over the subsequent four decades, the 5-year overall survival rate for localized OS has remained approximately 60–70%, and major survival gains have been limited [1], [4], [5].

For patients presenting with pulmonary metastasis at diagnosis or those who develop relapse or refractory disease during treatment, the prognosis remains extremely poor, with 5-year survival rates typically below 30% [1], [6]. Recent large-scale epidemiological analyses further show that, compared with other pediatric malignancies such as leukemia and lymphoma, improvements in OS survival have significantly lagged behind. Early pulmonary metastasis and the intractability of chemoresistance have been repeatedly identified as core drivers of treatment failure and OS-related mortality [4], [7]. Notably, traditional strategies such as dose intensification or simple substitution of chemotherapeutic agents have failed to overcome this stagnation, indicating a clear and longstanding clinical unmet need [8].

Current evidence suggests that under standard MAP therapy, approximately 30–40% of patients exhibit primary non-responsiveness or rapidly acquire resistance during treatment [9], [10]. Beyond intrinsic genetic and epigenetic adaptations of tumor cells, longitudinal genomic analyses of recurrent OS indicate that chemotherapy pressure can drive the expansion of specific resistant subclones, forming cell populations with heightened aggressiveness and metastatic potential [11], [12]. How to precisely identify and therapeutically intervene in resistance evolution at both the population and subclonal levels represents a key scientific challenge to breaking the long-standing survival plateau in OS.

Interplay between the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and chemoresistance

In addition to cell-intrinsic resistance mechanisms, the highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) of OS constitutes another major barrier to effective therapy [13]. Multi-omics and pathological evidence indicate that OS is typically classified as an immunologically “cold” tumor, characterized by insufficient infiltration of effector CD8+ T cells and marked enrichment of immunosuppressive components such as M2-polarized tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [14], [15]. Meanwhile, OS cells actively reshape the local metabolic milieu, including the formation of acidic and hypoxic microenvironments, and upregulate immune checkpoint molecules such as programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), thereby attenuating antitumor immune activity [16].

Emerging studies reveal that immunosuppression and chemoresistance are not independent phenomena but may reinforce each other through multiple pathways [17]. On one hand, chemotherapy-induced stress can upregulate immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1, alter forms of cell death, and reduce the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), collectively weakening immune recognition and clearance of tumor cells. On the other hand, resistant subpopulations often display stronger immune evasion capabilities and more stable immunosuppressive phenotypes [16], [18]. Accumulating evidence suggests that under chemotherapeutic pressure, OS may develop a self-perpetuating “vicious cycle” centered on resistance evolution and immune escape, making it difficult to achieve durable disease control through cytotoxic killing alone [19]. Identifying new molecular nodes that function as central regulators along the resistance-immunity-metastasis axis is therefore essential for advancing precision therapy in OS.

Extracellular vesicles as a mechanistic nexus linking chemoresistance and tumor immunity, and overview of this review

Against this backdrop, extracellular vesicles (EVs) are increasingly recognized as key mediators linking tumor-intrinsic adaptive processes with microenvironmental remodeling. EVs are nanoscale membrane-bound vesicles actively released by diverse cell types and capable of encapsulating proteins, lipids, DNA fragments, and various ncRNAs, including microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs) [20], [21] (Fig. 1). They mediate short- and long-distance intercellular communication through circulation [22]. In recent years, research on EVs derived from OS and its microenvironment has expanded rapidly. OS-secreted EVs have been shown to remodel pulmonary fibroblasts and the pre-metastatic niche, thereby promoting distant metastasis [23]. In parallel, studies in breast cancer and other solid tumors have described EVs as “Trojan horses” within the tumor microenvironment, highlighting their covert roles in disseminating resistance signals and facilitating immune evasion [24]. In chemoresistance, EV-mediated transfer of multidrug resistance proteins, metabolic enzymes, and regulatory ncRNAs has been demonstrated in multiple preclinical studies to propagate resistant phenotypes across cell populations and correlate with clinical therapeutic response [25], [26]. In tumor immunity, circulating exosomal PD-L1 levels have been identified as independent predictors of prognosis and pulmonary metastasis risk in OS patients [27], [28] and may reflect tumor burden and immunosuppressive status. Meanwhile, EVs originating from bone marrow stromal cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts, and immune cells have been shown to modulate ferroptosis, autophagy, and immune cell polarization [29], [30]. These lines of evidence suggest that EVs provide a promising entry point for systematic investigations into resistance evolution and immune regulation in OS.

Building on existing review work [26], [31], [32], the present review places particular emphasis on the interplay between EV-mediated chemoresistance and tumor immunity, integrating evidence through a disease-chain perspective. Specifically, we will: (1) Discuss the isolation, characterization, and research standards for OS-derived EVs based on the MISEV2023 guidelines, with a focus on methodological challenges posed by bone-derived tissues and blood components; (2) Summarize advances in using OS EVs as liquid biopsy biomarkers for prognostic assessment, therapeutic monitoring, and immune stratification; (3) Systematically delineate the molecular mechanisms by which EVs govern resistance propagation, metabolic and cell-fate reprogramming, bone marrow and pulmonary pre-metastatic niche formation, and immunosuppressive remodeling; (4) Outline emerging therapeutic strategies including engineered EV drug delivery, inhibition or elimination of tumor-derived EVs, and EV-based vaccines, discussing their translational potential in OS; and (5) Finally, propose key challenges and future directions for advancing EV research from mechanistic discovery to clinically actionable tools, with attention to methodological standardization, heterogeneity resolution, age-specific considerations, and regulatory pathways.

Through this EV-centered integrative perspective, we aim to provide conceptual foundations and translational insights for developing comprehensive intervention strategies targeting chemoresistance and immune suppression in osteosarcoma.

Isolation, characterization, and standardization of osteosarcoma-derived EVs: Challenges and guidelines under the MISEV2023 framework

The latest MISEV2023 guidelines released by the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) shift the focus of EV research from simply “obtaining EVs” to “obtaining rigorously characterized, reproducible, and comparable EV experimental results” [22]. For OS, this shift is particularly important. On the one hand, tumor-derived EVs (tEVs) in the circulation of OS patients represent only a small fraction of the total EV pool and are easily masked by background signals. On the other hand, OS arises in a highly mineralized bone environment, and factors such as sample acquisition, tissue dissociation, and pediatric/adolescent-specific handling constraints impose additional demands on EV isolation and characterization [33], [34]. If these methodological issues are overlooked, it becomes difficult to obtain reliable biomarker data and mechanistic conclusions.

Within the MISEV2023 framework, the key questions in OS-related EV research can be summarized as follows: how to distinguish bona fide EVs from non-vesicular particles; how to select appropriate EV systems in highly heterogeneous cellular models; and how to establish standardized liquid biopsy workflows that facilitate multicenter comparability and clinical translation (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Standardized workflow for isolation and quality control of osteosarcoma-derived EVs. Quality control under MISEV2023: distinguishing EVs from non-vesicular particles

The MISEV2023 guidelines emphasize that, prior to functional and omics analyses, interference from non-vesicular extracellular particles (NVEPs) must be carefully excluded [22] (Fig. 2). In OS research, some early studies reported that EVs were enriched in histones, double-stranded DNA, and other nuclear components, and on this basis proposed the hypothesis of “EV-mediated transfer of genome-level resistance information” [35]. However, methodological reevaluation has shown that when simple ultracentrifugation (UC) is used as the sole isolation method, apoptotic bodies, nuclear debris, and larger cellular remnants tend to co-pellet with larger EVs [36]. These components partially overlap in size range with large EVs and, if not removed by density gradient or additional purification steps, may be mistakenly identified as EV cargo in downstream RNA sequencing and proteomic analyses, leading to the misinterpretation of cell death by-products as active EV-mediated transport [37].

For plasma samples, abundant soluble proteins constitute another major source of interference. If albumin, immunoglobulins, and lipoprotein particles are not effectively removed, they will not only mask low-abundance tumor-specific EV proteins (such as PD-L1 and N-cadherin) but also compromise the accuracy of quantitative analyses of phospho-signaling proteins and membrane proteins [38], [39]. Under these circumstances, if “EV components” and “soluble components” are not strictly distinguished, the prognostic value and biological interpretation of markers such as exosomal PD-L1 may become confounded [27].

The landscape of osteosarcoma cell models: heterogeneity and EV cargo profiles

The pronounced genomic and phenotypic heterogeneity of OS is well established and underpins substantial variability in experimental model behavior [1]. In parallel, an expanding body of work indicates that EV-related readouts in OS (including EV output, molecular cargo, and downstream functional effects) can differ across cell lines and experimental systems, although most available evidence comes from independent studies rather than standardized head-to-head comparisons across models [40], [41].

Importantly, reported differences across commonly used OS cell models include variability in particle abundance, protein and non-coding RNA cargo, and the ability of EVs to modulate recipient cells within the tumor microenvironment. These issues become particularly salient when EVs are studied in the context of chemotherapy resistance, immune regulation, or metastatic progression, where model-specific phenotypes may materially shape the inferred mechanisms [26], [41], [42].

The HOS/143B lineage illustrates why model context is essential in OS EV studies. HOS, originally designated TE-85 clone F-5, was established from a human osteosarcoma specimen and has served as a parental background for several derivative OS cell lines [44]. The 143B cell line is an activated K-Ras-transformed derivative of HOS/TE85 and displays markedly higher tumorigenic and pulmonary metastatic capacity than the parental TE85/HOS background in orthotopic xenograft models [45]. This transformed, highly metastatic background makes 143B useful for studying metastasis-associated EV communication, including pulmonary niche remodeling and OS-bone microenvironment interactions [45], [46]. However, findings derived from 143B should be interpreted in the context of its derivative status and should not be generalized to all OS cell-line backgrounds without validation.

For more osteoblast-like OS models, such as Saos-2, EV-related interpretations should explicitly account for potential “bone-lineage” confounding, as mineralization-associated programs can influence vesicle-associated proteins and phenotypes. Evidence from mineralized osteoblast-derived exosomes demonstrates enrichment in bone-associated components and highlights how mineralization state can shape EV cargo, providing a biologically plausible framework for interpreting osteoblast-like model EV readouts with appropriate caution [47].

Taken together, aligning EV-related hypotheses with OS model context, while explicitly acknowledging methodological variability and model-dependent bias, is essential for maximizing interpretability and translational relevance in OS EV studies [41], [43].

The “bone-specific” confounding factors: mineralized matrix and tissue dissociation

OS arises in mineralized bone tissue, where matrix vesicles (MVs) and inorganic particles containing calcium-phosphate crystals are widely present. These particles show substantial overlap in density and size range with classical small EVs [48]. In nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), if no pretreatment is performed, calcium salt crystals and fragmented mineralized particles are easily misidentified as EVs, resulting in artificially inflated particle counts and shifted size distributions [47]. If such interference is not recognized, it may be erroneously interpreted as “increased EV production” or “expansion of specific EV subpopulations”.

Importantly, calcium-phosphate co-isolation should not be viewed solely as an in vitro artifact, but rather as a sample-type-dependent confounder. It is expected to be most prominent in mineralized OS tissue explants, bone-associated tumor specimens, and osteoblast-like or mineralization-prone in vitro models, because matrix vesicles can concentrate calcium and inorganic phosphate and provide a confined microenvironment for calcium-phosphate nucleation and hydroxyapatite formation [49], [50]. In circulating plasma samples, by contrast, mineral particles are unlikely to represent the dominant source of interference; platelet-derived EVs, lipoproteins, albumin, immunoglobulins, and other non-vesicular extracellular particles are generally more relevant confounders [22], [38], [39], [51]. Nevertheless, in patients with active tumor-associated bone destruction, treatment-induced necrosis, or intensive bone remodeling, bone-derived mineralized particles or matrix-vesicle-like structures should still be considered as potential low-abundance confounders rather than ignored entirely. Therefore, plasma EV studies should prioritize platelet/lipoprotein depletion and soluble-protein contamination assessment, whereas tissue-derived EV studies should additionally include mineral-specific controls such as EDTA washing, density-gradient separation, and assessment of osteogenic markers including alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and osteocalcin.

To release cells and EVs from bone tissue, commonly used protocols rely on prolonged enzymatic digestion with relatively high concentrations of collagenase, trypsin, and related enzymes (Fig. 2). Recent studies indicate that such harsh digestion can partially degrade or cleave key EV surface proteins (e.g., CD63, CD81, integrin subunits), leading to reduced signal intensity in flow cytometry, Western blotting, or antibody-based capture platforms [52]. In OS research, if membrane proteins such as PD-L1 and N-cadherin are similarly affected by enzymatic digestion, the immune phenotype of EVs may be underestimated, thereby influencing the interpretation of mechanisms related to immune checkpoints, immune escape, and pre-metastatic niche formation in the lung [53].

These sample-type-specific concerns motivate OS-adapted workflows that distinguish tissue- or mineral-rich EV preparations from plasma-based liquid biopsy samples. The standardized workflow summarized below integrates these requirements into a practical sequence for isolation, enrichment, and quality control.

Advances in isolation technologies and standardized workflow for OS liquid biopsy

Progress in EV isolation and detection technologies has expanded their utility from basic research to potential liquid biopsy and companion diagnostic applications. In OS, microfluidic platforms and surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) have already demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity for plasma EV enrichment and rapid detection [54].

Han et al. developed an integrated microfluidic-SERS chip that enables automated enrichment of plasma EVs and simultaneous detection of multiple markers including CD63, vimentin, and EpCAM, achieving a detection limit of 2 EVs/μL and an approximate diagnostic accuracy of 95% in distinguishing OS patients from healthy controls [55]. Building on this, Jin et al. combined label-free SERS fingerprint spectra with machine learning algorithms to classify OS based on the overall spectral features of EVs, achieving high diagnostic performance without relying on specific antibodies [56]. These technological approaches provide a solid foundation for constructing multiparameter EV biomarker panels.

To improve the comparability of EV data across centers and time points, it is necessary to propose relatively unified operating procedures for OS liquid biopsy, drawing on the MISEV2023 guidelines, EV biobank experience, platelet-depletion studies, and plasma EV workflow studies [22], [38], [39], [51], [57]. Based on current evidence, the key steps can be summarized as follows (Fig. 2):(1)Sample collection and preprocessing: Use ACD or EDTA anticoagulant tubes and minimize the interval between blood draw and the first centrifugation. After plasma separation, promptly add protease and phosphatase inhibitors to preserve the integrity of EV surface and internal proteins, especially when subsequent analyses involve phospho-signaling or immune checkpoint molecules [39].(2)Platelet removal: Platelets are a major source of circulating EVs, and their EVs are enriched in multiple miRNAs. Methodological studies show that insufficient platelet removal leads to significant interference from platelet-derived EVs in quantitative analyses of tumor EV-miRNA profiles [51]. A recommended approach is to use validated double medium-speed centrifugation and, when appropriate, filtration to obtain platelet-depleted plasma.(3)EV isolation and enrichment: For plasma samples, size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) is currently a suitable routine method that effectively removes abundant soluble proteins while preserving EV integrity and function [57]. In settings with large sample volumes or frequent monitoring needs, concentration and buffer-exchange steps should be validated within the same workflow before downstream analysis [39], [57], [58]. For plasma-derived EVs, it is also necessary to examine intracellular proteins such as Calnexin and lipoprotein markers such as ApoB to assess contamination from cell debris and lipoprotein particles [38], [57]. For samples likely to contain substantial mineralized matrix interference, osteogenic proteins such as ALP and osteocalcin should be evaluated to determine residual matrix vesicles or inorganic particles.

Osteosarcoma-derived extracellular vesicles as a liquid biopsy platform for monitoring chemoresistance and immune status

With the continuous development of liquid biopsy technologies, osteosarcoma-derived EVs are gradually evolving from purely biological research subjects into potential clinical indicators that reflect tumor burden, chemotherapy sensitivity, and immune status. Recent studies have shown that immune checkpoint proteins, membrane protein signatures, functional protease activity, and non-coding RNAs carried by EVs are not only associated with chemotherapy response and metastatic risk, but can also be dynamically monitored using high-sensitivity detection platforms [25], [27], [55], [59], [60]. Longitudinal monitoring of chemotherapy response and immune status

Traditional tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging and imaging-based assessments have clear limitations in predicting the response of osteosarcoma patients to standard MAP regimens and are insufficient to capture early events of resistance and immune escape in a timely manner. Liquid biopsy based on circulating EVs may provide a complementary approach to this problem.

In terms of prognostic stratification, Wang et al. conducted a prospective follow-up study in 67 newly diagnosed osteosarcoma patients and found that baseline levels of serum exosomal PD-L1 were significantly associated with overall survival and the incidence of pulmonary metastasis. They proposed two cut-off values, 14.23 pg/mL and 25.96 pg/mL, which could distinguish different strata of disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival [27]. These thresholds should be interpreted as study-specific, hypothesis-generating cut-offs rather than clinically validated values, and their broader applicability requires validation in larger multicenter cohorts. In this single-center, moderate-sized cohort, exosomal PD-L1 exhibited the characteristics of an independent prognostic factor, suggesting that immune checkpoint proteins on the EV surface not only reflect the degree of tumor-associated immunosuppression but may also predict the extent of benefit from chemotherapy or chemo-immunotherapy combinations.

For chemoresistance monitoring, Weinman et al. used a spontaneous canine osteosarcoma model to generate a plasma EV proteomic atlas and found that specific EV protein fingerprints could distinguish carboplatin-sensitive from carboplatin-resistant individuals and correlate with clinical outcomes [25]. This model closely resembles human osteosarcoma in its pathological and metastatic features. The study further demonstrated that EVs released by resistant cells can horizontally transfer multidrug-resistant phenotypes to sensitive cells, consistent with proteomic changes detectable in plasma. This evidence supports the use of EV protein signatures to develop non-invasive assays for predicting chemotherapy response.

Currently, most relevant studies originate from single-center prospective or retrospective cohorts with limited sample sizes, and the overall level of evidence remains at an early exploratory stage. Nevertheless, these findings collectively point toward a common trend [61], [62]: EV biomarkers centered on immune-related molecules such as exosomal PD-L1 are promising tools that, on top of conventional staging, may provide more dynamic and functionally oriented assessments of resistance and immune status.

EV subpopulations, functional activity, and membrane protein panels for risk stratification and MRD-oriented surveillance

The pronounced heterogeneity of osteosarcoma makes it difficult for a single EV biomarker to achieve sufficient sensitivity and specificity. To address this challenge, recent technological advances have focused on fine characterization of EV subpopulations, using multiplexed marker combinations to delineate “surface maps” of distinct functional EV populations.

Using the single-EV-resolution Subpopulation Protein Analysis by Single EV Counting (SPASEC) platform, Zhai et al. performed subpopulation analysis of plasma EVs from osteosarcoma patients and simultaneously detected multiple membrane proteins, including CD63, BMP2, GD2, and N-cadherin, at the single-EV level [59]. The study showed that panels of EV subpopulations centered on osteosarcoma-associated antigens and stromal markers such as GD2 and N-cadherin could distinguish metastatic from non-metastatic osteosarcoma patients and reported an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.99 in that study, suggesting excellent discriminatory potential. However, because such high performance estimates may be influenced by cohort size, case composition, marker selection, and possible model overfitting, this result should be interpreted cautiously and validated in larger independent cohorts before clinical generalization. GD2 reflects tumor cell phenotype, whereas N-cadherin is associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and invasive phenotypes. Such multi-marker co-expression subpopulations may provide a new approach for MRD-oriented surveillance and for identifying patients at high risk of metastasis.

More recently, Ji et al. extended this concept from static EV surface-marker profiling to functional EV activity assessment by developing an OS EV matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) Activity Assay based on click chemistry-mediated enrichment of extracellular vesicles [60]. This platform enriches OS EV subpopulations using EV Click MagBeads and trans-cyclooctene (TCO)-grafted antibodies targeting LRRC15, GPNMB, or B7-H3, followed by fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) peptide probe-based quantification of MMP14 or MMP2 activity in the enriched EVs. By analyzing six predefined OS EV marker/MMP combinations, the authors generated individualized OS EV MMP activity profiles from plasma samples. Three top-performing combinations—LRRC15/MMP2, GPNMB/MMP14, and B7-H3/MMP14—were integrated by logistic regression to establish an OS EV MMP Activity Score, which distinguished metastatic from localized OS with high discriminatory performance in a retrospective case-control cohort comprising 34 healthy donors, 24 localized OS patients, and 10 metastatic OS patients. Importantly, longitudinal analyses in six OS patients showed concordance between changes in the EV MMP Activity Score and radiographic disease progression or treatment response. This study expands the conceptual framework of OS EV biomarkers from “molecular abundance” toward “functional vesicle activity,” although its clinical utility remains exploratory and requires validation in larger, independent, multicenter cohorts.

In parallel, at the level of bulk EV analysis, Han et al. developed an integrated microfluidic-SERS chip platform that enables rapid multiplex detection of membrane proteins such as CD63, vimentin, and EpCAM in EVs, completing the workflow from plasma EV isolation to signal readout in approximately 5 h, with a detection limit of about 2 EVs/μL and a diagnostic accuracy of approximately 95% in distinguishing osteosarcoma patients from healthy controls [55]. Vimentin and EpCAM represent mesenchymal and epithelial/epithelioid phenotypes, respectively, and their combined expression in osteosarcoma EVs may reflect tumor-stroma interactions and invasion-related states.

From an application standpoint, such EV membrane protein panels and functional activity-based EV assays may be used for:(1)Risk stratification and metastatic risk assessment at initial diagnosis;(2)MRD-oriented surveillance and early warning of metastasis during neoadjuvant chemotherapy and postoperative follow-up;(3)Early evaluation of treatment response when integrated with imaging and conventional serum markers.

Functional non-coding RNAs in EVs: linking chemoresistance, ferroptosis, and metabolic reprogramming

ncRNAs contained within EVs constitute a key regulatory axis between tumor cells and the microenvironment. In osteosarcoma, recent studies increasingly highlight the critical roles of EV-encapsulated miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs in regulating ferroptosis, lipid metabolism, and proliferation/migration, providing new molecular perspectives on chemoresistance and immune escape [63].

With respect to ferroptosis, Jiang et al. reported that exosomal miR-144-3p promotes ferroptosis by targeting ZEB1, thereby inhibiting osteosarcoma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [64]. Reduced EV miR-144-3p may therefore reflect adaptation to ferroptosis resistance and chemotherapy-related stress.

CAF-derived EV ncRNAs are also relevant to resistance modulation. Du et al. reported that CAF-EV circ_0004872-related signaling can induce autophagy-dependent ferroptosis in tumor vascular endothelial cells and enhance cisplatin sensitivity [65]. This finding links CAF-EV ncRNA cargo with ferroptosis induction, vascular targeting, and chemosensitivity.

BMSC-EV lncRNA XIST promotes lipid synthesis and accumulation in osteosarcoma cells through the miR-655/ACLY axis, thereby supporting tumor growth and metastasis [29]. Because ACLY links glucose metabolism to lipid biosynthesis, XIST-containing BMSC-EVs may represent a microenvironment-derived metabolic signal relevant to tumor progression and resistance.

Taken together, these examples indicate that osteosarcoma-related EV-ncRNAs converge on three functional modules: ferroptosis sensitivity, represented by the miR-144-3p-ZEB1 axis; autophagy-ferroptosis coupling and chemosensitivity, represented by CAF-EV circ_0004872-related signaling; and lipid metabolic reprogramming, represented by the XIST/miR-655/ACLY axis. These ncRNA candidates therefore remain appropriate for inclusion in the biomarker schematic shown in Fig. 3, but their clinical utility still requires standardized detection and validation in larger cohorts.

The clinical translation of ncRNA biomarkers also depends heavily on standardized pre-analytical and isolation procedures. Methodological work by Bracht et al. has shown that, if platelet removal is insufficient during routine plasma preparation, EV-miRNAs released from platelets can substantially confound results, leading to 2- to 4-fold artificial increases in some miRNAs such as miR-223 [51]. Therefore, when evaluating EV-ncRNAs as resistance- or immunity-related biomarkers in osteosarcoma, strict adherence to platelet depletion and EV purification standard operating procedures is essential to minimize false positives and inter-center variability.

Artificial intelligence (AI) and multi-omics-driven paradigms for EV-based liquid biopsy

Given that a single protein or single miRNA is insufficient to capture the complex biological features of osteosarcoma, AI and multi-omics integration are emerging as important directions in EV-based liquid biopsy research [66], [67]. Current efforts mainly focus on two strategies: first, “label-free” EV classification based on physicochemical fingerprints combined with machine learning; and second, joint modeling of EV proteomes, functional EV activity profiles, EV ncRNA profiles, and clinical parameters to construct multiparameter risk prediction tools.

A representative example of the former is the microfluidic-SERS platform developed by Jin et al. This platform does not require preselection of specific antibodies; instead, it uses overall Raman spectral fingerprints of EVs combined with machine learning algorithms to classify osteosarcoma patients and healthy individuals, achieving a diagnostic accuracy of approximately 93% [56]. This strategy has potential advantages in terms of cost and throughput and is suitable for screening applications in relatively resource-limited settings.

For multi-omics integration, existing studies have provided several complementary biomarker components, including exosomal PD-L1 and N-cadherin, EV subpopulation profiles, EV-associated MMP activity scores, EV-ncRNAs, and clinical variables such as metastatic status and chemotherapy regimens, which could be incorporated into future composite models for predicting prognosis and metastatic risk in osteosarcoma patients [27], [59], [63]. With advances in single-EV technologies, bulk EV data may eventually be “deconvolved” to dissect the contributions of specific functional EV subpopulations and, on this basis, further integrated with radiomics and genomic data to build more refined multimodal predictive frameworks.

At present, AI and multi-omics-based EV analyses remain largely in proof-of-concept or early preclinical stages, with limitations in sample size and multicenter validation. However, they already provide preliminary evidence for a transition in osteosarcoma EV liquid biopsy from “single biomarker discovery” toward “system-level network evaluation.” When combined with standardized isolation workflows and quality control systems described earlier, these approaches hold promise for developing candidate individualized monitoring tools, although clinical implementation will require prospective validation, harmonized EV workflows, and externally tested predictive models.

EV-mediated chemoresistance and immune Remodeling in osteosarcoma

Chemoresistance is one of the key causes of treatment failure, relapse, and distant metastasis in OS. The clinically observed phenomenon of “rapid acquisition of resistance” does not match the timescale required for accumulation of somatic genetic mutations, suggesting that non-genetic mechanisms play a major role in the establishment of resistance [68]. At the same time, the typically immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment of OS is highly associated with poor prognosis and is tightly intertwined with chemotherapy response. In recent years, accumulating evidence suggests that EVs act as pivotal mediators in the formation and maintenance of chemoresistance and immune escape by transporting resistance determinants between tumor and microenvironmental cells, driving metabolic and cell-fate reprogramming, and remodeling local and distant immune ecosystems. Fig. 1 summarizes the major EV-mediated mechanisms linking chemoresistance, immune remodeling, and pulmonary pre-metastatic niche formation in osteosarcoma [69], [70].

EV-mediated horizontal transfer of drug resistance determinants

ABC transporters and dissemination of the multidrug-resistant phenotype

Multidrug resistance (MDR) is closely linked to high expression of ABC transporters such as P-glycoprotein (P-gp/MDR1) and MRP1 [71]. Traditionally, this has been attributed largely to gene amplification or transcriptional activation. However, in OS, MDR often emerges within only a few cycles of chemotherapy, a timescale that is difficult to explain solely by genetic mutation.

Torreggiani et al. showed that doxorubicin-resistant OS cells can transfer functional P-gp protein together with its encoding mRNA to originally sensitive recipient cells via EVs, enabling the latter to acquire a typical MDR phenotype within a short period and significantly reducing intracellular doxorubicin levels [72]. This finding indicates that EVs can mediate “horizontal dissemination” of ABC transporters within tumor cell populations, rapidly amplifying the fitness advantage of resistant clones at the tissue level.

Wang et al. further found that chemotherapeutic agents themselves can induce dysregulated expression of the vesicle biogenesis-related proteins Rab5 and Rab8B, thereby increasing the release of ABCB1-enriched EVs and enhancing the uptake of these EVs by neighboring sensitive cells [73]. Under chemotherapy pressure, this process accelerates the spread of resistance determinants within the cellular population and enables non-genetic resistance to be established synchronously across multiple clones.

Current evidence suggests that EV-mediated transfer of ABC transporters and their associated mRNAs constitute one of the prototypical mechanisms for non-genetic, rapidly adaptable resistance in OS. However, the relevant data are derived mainly from in vitro and animal models, and prospective clinical cohorts are still needed to validate its clinical relevance.

EV-borne non-coding RNAs regulating efflux pumps and chemoresistance

In addition to directly transferring proteins, EVs also transport ncRNAs that regulate the expression of resistance-related genes in recipient cells, constituting another level of resistance reprogramming.

Pan et al. reported that EVs released by cisplatin-resistant OS cells are enriched in circ_103801. This circular RNA acts as a competing endogenous RNA for specific miRNAs, thereby relieving repression of MRP1 and P-gp, upregulating these efflux pumps in recipient cells, and inducing cisplatin resistance [74]. This study directly links the EV-circRNA-miRNA-transporter axis to clinically relevant drug tolerance.

In addition, Bai et al. found that EVs derived from BMSCs carry circ_0004674, which modulates the miR-342-3p/FBN1 axis and activates Wnt/β-catenin signaling, thereby enhancing efflux pump expression and MDR capacity in OS cells [75]. This result suggests that non-malignant cells in the bone marrow microenvironment can also actively participate in shaping tumor resistance phenotypes through EV-ncRNA pathways.

Taken together, representative axes such as circ_103801-MRP1/P-gp and circ_0004674-miR-342-3p/FBN1-Wnt/β-catenin demonstrate that EV-carried ncRNAs can integrate multiple signaling pathways to regulate efflux pumps and survival programs, forming an important molecular basis for non-genetic resistance in OS.

Metabolic reprogramming, ferroptosis, and cell fate modulation

Chemotherapeutic agents typically exert their effects by inducing oxidative stress, DNA damage, and various cell death programs [76]. Consequently, the metabolic state and cell-fate choices of OS cells directly determine their sensitivity to treatment. Current studies indicate that EVs substantially influence chemotherapy response by establishing anti-ferroptotic networks, inducing autophagy and dormancy, and reshaping stemness.

EV-mediated ferroptosis resistance and lipid metabolic reprogramming

Ferroptosis is an iron- and lipid peroxidation-dependent form of regulated cell death and is considered a promising target for reversing chemoresistance. OS cells and microenvironmental cells can use EVs to build a defense against ferroptosis.

Yin et al. found that EVs derived from M2-polarized macrophages are enriched in Apoc1, which stabilizes Acyl-CoA Synthetase Family Member 2 (ACSF2) in OS cells, markedly reduces lipid peroxidation, and thus diminishes sensitivity to ferroptosis inducers [30]. This study illustrates that immunosuppressive macrophages export antioxidant and anti-ferroptotic capacity to tumor cells via EVs, constituting an important source of chemoresistance.

In the context of fibroblast-tumor interactions, Duan et al. demonstrated that CAF-derived EVs carrying miR-22-3p inhibit ferroptosis and promote cisplatin resistance by targeting ferroptosis-related genes [77]. Niu et al. reported that CAF-EVs enriched in miR-21-5p suppress ferroptosis and promote OS cell proliferation and migration [78]. Together, these studies suggest that CAFs maintain tumor tolerance to lipid peroxidation via EV-miRNA axes.

Meanwhile, BMSC-EVs containing lncRNA XIST promote lipid synthesis and accumulation in OS cells through the miR-655/ACLY axis [29]. As ACLY is a key enzyme linking glucose metabolism to lipid biosynthesis, this finding indicates that ncRNAs carried by bone marrow-derived EVs also help establish a lipid metabolic foundation that supports tumor growth and resistance.

Collectively, M2-EVs (Apoc1), CAF-EVs (miR-22-3p, miR-21-5p), and BMSC-EVs (XIST-ACLY) together constitute a resistance-regulatory network centered on ferroptosis and lipid metabolism. The supporting evidence currently comes mainly from in vitro and animal studies, and systematic evaluation of clinical correlations is still required.

EV-induced autophagy and low-metabolic dormancy

Autophagy is a key adaptive response that promotes cell survival under stress conditions. Huang et al. showed that BMSC-EVs robustly activate ATG5-dependent autophagy in OS cells, facilitating clearance of damaged organelles and inducing a “dormancy-like” state characterized by reduced metabolic activity and slowed proliferation [79]. This state is typically less sensitive to cell cycle-dependent chemotherapeutic agents, allowing such cells to survive treatment and potentially serve as a reservoir for later relapse.

Current evidence suggests that EV-induced autophagy and dormancy represent important mechanisms by which OS cells escape chemotherapy-induced killing. However, how these processes evolve dynamically in patients and how they relate to clinical patterns of relapse remain to be elucidated using longitudinal sampling and functional imaging approaches.

EV-induced stemness and recurrence potential

Tumor cell stemness is closely associated with relapse and distant metastasis. Yang et al. reported that EVs derived from human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) activate the Notch signaling pathway in OS cells and upregulate stemness-associated transcription factors such as Sox2 and Oct4, thereby enhancing self-renewal and clonogenic capacity [80]. This finding suggests that vascular endothelial cells can reprogram OS cells toward a stem-like state via an EV-Notch-stemness axis, which may be tightly linked to the long-term survival and recurrence potential of residual cells after chemotherapy.

At present, studies on EV-mediated stemness reprogramming are largely confined to preclinical models, and further work is needed to assess their reproducibility and prognostic significance in patient samples.

Microenvironmental Remodeling: Bone marrow niche and pulmonary pre-metastatic niche

Progression of OS depends on continuous remodeling of both the primary bone marrow niche and the distant pulmonary pre-metastatic niche (PMN). As key communication vehicles, EVs play important roles in both compartments.

Remodeling of the bone marrow and vascular niches

In the primary bone marrow niche, Massaro et al. found that OS cell-derived EVs are enriched in TGF-β and can induce bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) to adopt an inflammatory MSC (iMSC) phenotype. These iMSCs secrete large amounts of IL-6, activate STAT3 signaling in OS cells, and thereby enhance tumor cell survival and chemoresistance [81]. The IL-6/STAT3 axis is also tightly linked to immunosuppression and M2 macrophage polarization, thus functionally connecting EV-derived TGF-β to both resistance and immune remodeling.

Zhu et al. further showed that lncRNA XIST in BMSC-EVs reprograms lipid metabolism in OS cells via the miR-655/ACLY axis, increasing lipid synthesis and energy storage and promoting tumor growth and resistance [29]. This finding underscores that bone marrow microenvironmental cells can export metabolic and epigenetic regulators via EVs to provide a “metabolic soil” favorable to tumor cell survival and resistance.

The vascular niche is likewise profoundly shaped by EVs. Wang et al. reported that serum-derived EVs carrying the lncRNA MIAT promote endothelial cell proliferation and pathological angiogenesis [82]. Raimondi et al. demonstrated that OS-EVs enhance endothelial tube formation and drive the development of structurally abnormal neovessels [83]. Such aberrant vasculature is often associated with uneven perfusion, elevated interstitial pressure, and hypoxia. These changes not only improve nutrient supply to the tumor but may also restrict immune cell infiltration, providing anatomical and physiological foundations for subsequent immune escape.

Establishment of the pulmonary pre-metastatic niche

The lung is the most common and lethal metastatic target organ in OS. Multiple studies have shown that OS-EVs can reprogram the local stromal and immune microenvironment in the lung before the arrival of tumor cells, thereby accelerating PMN formation.

Zhang et al. found that OS cells express high levels of COL6A1 and selectively package it into EVs. After uptake of COL6A1-enriched EVs, pulmonary fibroblasts are driven toward a CAF phenotype and secrete cytokines such as IL-6, IL-8, and TGF-β, establishing a pulmonary microenvironment that supports tumor seeding and exhibits immunosuppressive features [84]. Mazumdar et al. further observed that OS-EVs upregulate multiple chemokines, including CXCL1, CXCL2, and CXCL8, in lung tissue [85]. This likely enhances recruitment of both tumor cells and immunosuppressive cells, conferring migratory and survival advantages that facilitate metastatic colonization.

Taken together, in the primary bone marrow/vascular niche and the pulmonary PMN, OS-related EVs remodel local and distant microenvironments through multiple pathways, including the TGF-β/IL-6/STAT3 axis, the XIST-ACLY axis, and the COL6A1-CAF-cytokine/chemokine axis, thereby exerting a sustained driving influence on chemoresistance and metastasis.

Interplay between EVs and immune regulation: From local immunosuppression to distant immune escape

OS typically exhibits “immune-cold” characteristics, including limited effector T-cell infiltration, impaired dendritic cell (DC) function, and enrichment of M2-polarized macrophages. Although direct evidence for OS-EVs acting on T cells or DCs is still limited, current studies provide multifaceted indirect support for their important roles in immunosuppression and distant immune escape.

Indirect suppression of dendritic cells and antigen presentation

To date, no study has directly demonstrated that OS-derived EVs suppress DC maturation or antigen-presenting function. However, work related to resistance formation indicates that EV-mediated upregulation of P-gp and MRP1 can reduce the extent of chemotherapy-induced cellular damage and the release of DAMPs, thereby weakening innate immune activation triggered by immunogenic cell death [72], [73], [74]. In this context, the danger signals received by DCs are markedly diminished, and their maturation and antigen-presenting capacity may be secondarily impaired.

The TGF-β/iMSC/IL-6 axis described above may also provide a cytokine background that indirectly affects DC function and antigen presentation; nevertheless, direct OS-specific evidence for EV-mediated DC suppression remains lacking and requires validation in immunocompetent models.

Macrophage polarization and the immunosuppression-chemoresistance feedback loop

The same MSC-IL-6/STAT3 axis may also provide a plausible route through which tumor-derived EVs favor M2-like macrophage polarization, thereby linking microenvironmental remodeling to immune suppression [81].

In the reverse direction, Yin et al. showed that EVs derived from M2 macrophages contain Apoc1, which stabilizes ACSF2 in tumor cells, reduces lipid peroxidation, and suppresses ferroptosis [30]. This enhances tumor cell survival under chemotherapy and oxidative stress. Taken together, tumor cell-derived EVs promote reprogramming of MSCs and macrophages toward immunosuppressive phenotypes, while M2-EVs, in turn, enhance tumor resistance via anti-ferroptotic effects. These interactions form a positive feedback loop between immunosuppression and chemoresistance.

Vascular abnormalization and restricted T-cell infiltration

As noted in Section 4.3.1, EV-driven vascular remodeling may restrict T-cell infiltration at both spatial and metabolic levels. Abnormal neovasculature, uneven perfusion, elevated interstitial pressure, and chronic hypoxia can impair T-cell trafficking and long-term retention in the tumor core, while also favoring immune checkpoint upregulation. In OS, this remains a plausible EV-linked mechanism for maintaining an immune-cold microenvironment, although OS-specific spatial immune mapping data are still limited.

Immune regulation within the pulmonary pre-metastatic niche

As outlined in Section 4.3.2, OS-EVs can remodel the pulmonary pre-metastatic niche through fibroblast activation and cytokine/chemokine release. From an immune-regulatory perspective, these changes may favor recruitment of suppressive myeloid populations and limit effector-cell activity, although direct mechanistic links between OS-EVs and specific immune cell subsets such as CD8+ T cells, NK cells, or MDSCs remain insufficiently defined.

Taken together, available evidence indicates that OS-related EVs occupy a central position in the formation and maintenance of chemoresistance and immune escape by mediating horizontal transfer of resistance determinants, reprogramming metabolism and cell fate, and remodeling local and distant immune microenvironments. Compared with mechanisms directly focused on chemoresistance and metastasis, studies specifically targeting OS-EV-immune cell interactions remain relatively scarce, with a particular lack of systematic in vivo functional validation and clinically correlated analyses. This evidence gap defines a clear direction for future work aimed at elucidating the “EV-mediated linkage between chemoresistance and immune remodeling” and provides a theoretical basis for developing EV-targeted combination strategies that integrate chemotherapy and immunotherapy.

EV-based therapeutic strategies in osteosarcoma

In osteosarcoma, EVs not only participate in the spread of chemoresistance, amplification of immunosuppression, and construction of the pulmonary pre-metastatic niche, but also inherently possess exploitable biological features, such as a protective lipid bilayer membrane, low immunogenicity, and a certain degree of tissue homing capacity [31], [32]. Based on this “double-edged sword” nature, three major therapeutic concepts can currently be summarized: engineering EVs or exosome mimetics for delivery of drugs and nucleic acids; inhibiting or clearing harmful tumor-derived EVs to reduce the overall “EV burden”; and repurposing EVs as cancer vaccine platforms. However, it is important to emphasize at the outset that these strategies are at markedly different stages of maturity. In OS, EV-based therapeutic evidence remains almost entirely preclinical, whereas most clinical experience with EV products has been obtained in non-OS diseases or non-OS tumor settings. Therefore, this section focuses on direct evidence from osteosarcoma models, explicitly distinguishes such data from extrapolations derived from other tumor types, and highlights the manufacturing, loading, quality-control, and regulatory barriers that currently limit clinical translation (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Therapeutic strategies targeting EVs in osteosarcoma. Engineered EVs and exosome mimetics as carriers for drugs and nucleic acids

Drug-loaded MSC/BMSC-EVs and exosome mimetics: Direct evidence from osteosarcoma models

Mesenchymal stem cell (MSC/BMSC)-derived EVs and their exosome mimetics (EMs) currently represent relatively well-developed experimental delivery platforms in osteosarcoma models. Wei et al. used human MSC-EVs to generate doxorubicin-loaded exosomes (Exo-Dox), significantly reducing the IC₅₀ of doxorubicin across multiple osteosarcoma cell lines and achieving stronger tumor suppression with reduced systemic toxicity and improved cardiac safety signals in xenograft models [88], [89]. Importantly, these cardiotoxicity-related findings were inferred from preclinical readouts rather than from clinical cardiac endpoints, and should therefore be interpreted as preliminary animal-level safety signals rather than evidence of clinically established cardioprotection. This effect was associated with tumor enrichment mediated by the SDF-1/CXCR4 axis, suggesting that MSC-EVs may partially alleviate dose-limiting toxicities while preserving or enhancing chemotherapeutic efficacy.

To overcome the intrinsic limitation of natural EV yield, Wang et al. generated EMs by serial extrusion of BMSCs and then loaded doxorubicin to form doxorubicin-loaded exosome mimetics (EM-Dox) [90]. EMs showed membrane protein profiles and size distributions comparable to natural EVs but with substantially higher production efficiency. In osteosarcoma xenograft models, EM-Dox achieved superior antitumor efficacy and lower systemic toxicity than free doxorubicin, providing preclinical support for the concept of “exosome mimetics as alternatives to conventional liposomes.” Nevertheless, extrusion-based EM production also introduces additional comparability questions, because membrane composition, surface protein orientation, cargo distribution, and biological potency may differ from naturally secreted EVs and must be controlled during scale-up.

In a more targeted design, Chen et al. introduced bone-targeting peptides on the surface of BMSC-EVs and loaded small-molecule drugs to construct bone-targeting exosome nanoparticles (BT-EXO), which exhibited clear enrichment in bone and intraosseous tumors in vivo [91]. BT-EXO induced ferroptosis by modulating the Keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 axis, thereby inhibiting intraosseous tumor progression without obvious systemic toxicity. This strategy simultaneously targets both “enhancement of chemosensitivity (via ferroptosis)” and “potential improvement of the immune microenvironment,” echoing earlier mechanistic findings on EV-mediated ferroptosis resistance and immune escape. However, because these data are derived from preclinical models, future studies will need to determine whether bone enrichment, ferroptosis induction, and systemic safety can be reproduced under clinically relevant dosing, pharmacokinetic, biodistribution, and toxicology conditions.

Among currently available OS-specific preclinical data, MSC/BMSC-derived EVs, exosome mimetics, and bone-targeted EV formulations represent promising but still experimental delivery platforms. Their translation will require standardized cargo-loading metrics, scalable GMP-compatible production, orthogonal biodistribution tracking, dose-escalation toxicology, and validated potency assays before they can be considered for OS clinical trials [86], [87].

EV-nucleic acid therapeutics: Reverse interventions targeting resistance and metastasis

EV-based nucleic acid delivery provides a mechanistically aligned strategy to counteract EV-mediated resistance and metastasis in OS. Rather than representing a single therapeutic modality, this field currently encompasses several preclinical directions, including miRNA replacement, siRNA-mediated niche remodeling, and CRISPR/Cas9 ribonucleoprotein delivery [92], [93], [94], [95].

One representative approach is miRNA replacement therapy. Zhang et al. employed MSC-EVs to deliver the tumor-suppressive miR-101, which targets metastasis-related pathways including BCL6, significantly inhibiting osteosarcoma cell migration and invasion and reducing both the number and overall burden of lung metastases in mouse models [92]. This study supports the feasibility of using EVs to restore tumor-suppressive miRNAs, although the durability of miRNA delivery, off-target transcriptomic effects, and immune consequences of repeated dosing remain insufficiently defined.

A second strategy involves siRNA-mediated remodeling of the pulmonary pre-metastatic niche. Using a synthetic biology approach, Yu et al. engineered hepatocytes as “in vivo factories” for releasing VEGFR2 siRNA-loaded EVs. These EVs preferentially homed to lung tissue and silenced VEGFR2 in endothelial cells; in a lung-metastatic osteosarcoma model, their antimetastatic effect surpassed that of the small-molecule VEGFR2 inhibitor apatinib, with lower toxicity [93]. This work suggests that EV-siRNA can be used to modulate the vascular niche within the lung and thereby inhibit metastatic colonization. However, the “in vivo factory” design also raises additional questions about dose controllability, reversibility, long-term transgene expression, and product definition, which must be resolved before clinical translation.

More recently, EV-mediated delivery of CRISPR/Cas9 ribonucleoproteins has been explored as a virus-free gene-editing strategy. Pan et al. used BMSC-EVs to deliver CRISPR/Cas9 RNPs targeting KCNJ2 and applied 124I labeling to enable in vivo tracking [94]. This system achieved efficient editing of KCNJ2 in vitro and in vivo, suppressed osteosarcoma cell proliferation and migration, and delayed formation of intraosseous and pulmonary metastases. Although this approach is technically attractive, gene-editing EV systems require particularly rigorous assessment of editing specificity, off-target effects, biodistribution, immunogenicity, and long-term tissue persistence.

It should be emphasized that for miRNA-, siRNA-, and CRISPR RNP-based systems alike, the evidence remains confined to preclinical studies and faces shared challenges including limited loading efficiency, uncertain in vivo stability, and incompletely defined immunogenicity [87], [92], [93], [94], [95]. For example, excessive electroporation or chemical transfection can increase apparent loading but may disrupt EV membrane integrity, promote cargo aggregation, alter biodistribution, or leave residual free nucleic acids. Exogenous nucleic acids may also activate TLR3/7 and other innate immune sensors, which could both enhance the immunogenicity of “immune-cold” osteosarcoma and induce nonspecific inflammation. Therefore, future OS studies should distinguish total input cargo from vesicle-associated cargo, quantify the proportion of cargo-positive EVs, and include nuclease/protease protection assays or orthogonal purification steps to exclude free cargo contamination. These issues must be rigorously evaluated in future systemic toxicology and dose-response studies.

Tumor and bone targeting engineering: Lessons from other solid tumors

Targeted engineering of EVs in osteosarcoma is still limited and can partially draw on more mature design paradigms from other solid tumors. Multiple studies in brain tumors and breast cancer have shown that decorating EV surfaces with integrin-binding peptides such as RGD/iRGD significantly enhances penetration into tumor vasculature and tumor cells [95]. In osteosarcoma, a similar strategy has been preliminarily applied in cRGD-modified MSC-EVs delivering lncRNA MEG3 to improve EV accumulation in osteosarcoma tissues and enhance antitumor efficacy [96]. Because much of this targeting evidence is derived from non-OS tumor models, these strategies should be interpreted as engineering templates rather than directly validated OS therapeutic solutions.

For bone targeting, BT-EXO achieves marked enrichment in bone tissue and intraosseous tumors by incorporating hydroxyapatite-affinity peptide sequences [91]. Other work has used GPI-anchored anti-GD2 nanobodies to construct exosome mimetics with highly specific targeting of GD2+ osteosarcoma [97]. These studies collectively indicate that “tumor/bone-targeting ligands + EV/EM” represents a key engineering paradigm for organ-directed design against “bone-lung dual-site” metastasis and may allow simultaneous modulation of chemosensitivity, vascular niches, and immune infiltration on a single platform. Nevertheless, ligand density, orientation, stability after storage, immunogenicity of engineered surface motifs, and preservation of targeting after scale-up remain unresolved manufacturing and pharmacological questions.

Inhibition or clearance of tumor EVs: Conceptual strategies to reduce the “Harmful EV Burden”

From another perspective, given that osteosarcoma EVs act as “amplifiers” of resistance determinant transfer, immunosuppression, and pulmonary pre-metastatic niche formation, inhibiting EV biogenesis, blocking EV function, or accelerating EV clearance could theoretically reduce the “harmful EV burden.” Current evidence for such approaches comes mainly from other tumor models and remains conceptual and exploratory in the context of osteosarcoma. Therefore, this section should be read primarily as a framework for future intervention design rather than as a summary of OS-validated therapeutic strategies.

Inhibition of EV biogenesis and release

The neutral sphingomyelinase 2 (nSMase2)/ceramide pathway is involved in exosome biogenesis, and GW4869 is commonly used as a pharmacological tool to suppress this pathway [98]. However, its therapeutic relevance in OS remains unvalidated. In an immunotherapy-oriented setting, Wang et al. designed a “nanounit” system that, together with ferroptosis induction, suppresses exosomal PD-L1 release and enhances tumor sensitivity to ferroptosis, markedly improving the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapy [99]. Wu et al. developed a polymer-based sonosensitizer that inhibits EV secretion and, during sonodynamic therapy, reduces the output of EV-mediated immunosuppressive signals from tumors [100].

These findings suggest that, in principle, similar strategies could diminish EV-mediated horizontal P-gp transfer, ferroptosis resistance, and pre-metastatic signaling in osteosarcoma. However, animal studies specifically focusing on osteosarcoma with survival or metastasis as endpoints are currently lacking. Moreover, existing inhibitors often have limited target specificity and poorly defined long-term safety, making them more suitable as tool compounds and proof-of-concept agents rather than near-term clinical candidates for osteosarcoma. Because EV biogenesis pathways are also required for physiological intercellular communication, systemic inhibition of EV release may produce broad off-target effects, including immune dysregulation, impaired tissue repair, and altered hematopoietic or stromal signaling.

Blocking, neutralizing, and clearing tumor-derived EVs: From antibody-based interception to extracorporeal capture

Another conceptual strategy is to intercept harmful tumor-derived EVs by blocking their surface interactions, neutralizing disease-promoting cargo, or physically removing circulating vesicles. In non-OS tumor models, neutralization or suppression of exosomal PD-L1 has been shown to alleviate systemic immunosuppression and improve responses to immune checkpoint blockade, supporting the broader rationale for targeting immunosuppressive EV subpopulations [99]. In osteosarcoma patients, plasma levels of exosomal PD-L1 and N-cadherin correlate with pulmonary metastatic progression and prognosis [27], [28], providing candidate molecules for future EV-interception designs.

Broad depletion of pan-EV markers such as CD9 or CD63 is unlikely to be clinically acceptable because it may remove physiological EVs and disrupt homeostatic intercellular communication. A more plausible direction would be selective interception of OS-enriched EV subpopulations, for example those carrying metastasis-associated cargo such as COL6A1, N-cadherin, GD2, or other markers identified by single-EV profiling platforms [28], [59], [84]. Even for such selective approaches, key uncertainties include antigen specificity, EV subpopulation heterogeneity, antibody accessibility, Fc-mediated clearance mechanisms, and the possibility of compensatory EV release from tumor or stromal cells.

High-efficiency EV detection and enrichment technologies, including microfluidic platforms, functionalized magnetic beads, affinity membranes, and lipid-binding devices, are already widely used in liquid biopsy to detect changes in tumor EV load and cargo [25], [55], [56], [59], [60]. Theoretically, embedding such high-affinity capture platforms into extracorporeal circulation or plasmapheresis systems could yield “exosome trap”-type blood purification devices that selectively clear tumor-derived EVs in advanced osteosarcoma patients, thereby reducing systemic dissemination of resistance and immunosuppressive signals at the source. Related extracorporeal EV-removal concepts have been proposed in other cancer contexts, but they remain technologically immature and have not been validated as therapeutic approaches in OS [101].

At present, there is no animal or clinical evidence supporting EV-neutralization or extracorporeal EV-clearance specifically in osteosarcoma, and realistic applications remain closer to companion diagnostics and response monitoring than to therapeutic blood purification. Transforming EV capture technologies into genuine “purification devices” should therefore be regarded as a long-term translational objective rather than a near-term clinical strategy.

EV-based vaccines and integration with immunotherapy

Given the strongly immune-cold phenotype of osteosarcoma, monotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors has thus far yielded unsatisfactory outcomes, making increased tumor immunogenicity and T-cell infiltration key goals [15], [32]. It is important to emphasize that, unlike EV-based liquid biopsy and several EV-delivery approaches, EV-vaccine strategies have not yet been directly validated in osteosarcoma-specific clinical studies. Most available evidence comes from melanoma, non-small-cell lung cancer, or experimental non-OS tumor models, including early-phase studies of dendritic cell-derived exosomes and preclinical tumor EV platforms carrying innate immune agonists [102], [103], [104], [105], [106]. Therefore, in the OS context, EV vaccines should be discussed primarily as a hypothesis-generating immunological framework. Their effectiveness may be substantially modified by OS-specific features, including low baseline immunogenicity, immunosuppressive myeloid enrichment, limited effector T-cell infiltration, mineralized bone-matrix constraints, and the pulmonary pre-metastatic niche.

Experience from DC-derived EV vaccines and implications for osteosarcoma

Dendritic cell-derived exosomes (Dex) are enriched in MHC-I/II, costimulatory molecules, and antigen-presenting machinery, making them conceptually attractive as cell-free antigen-presentation platforms. In early clinical studies, autologous Dex loaded with tumor antigens were evaluated in metastatic melanoma and advanced non-small-cell lung cancer, demonstrating feasibility, acceptable safety, and measurable immune activation in subsets of patients, although objective clinical responses were limited [102], [105], [106]. These trials support the feasibility and safety of Dex-based cancer immunotherapy, but they should not be interpreted as direct evidence for efficacy in OS.

These findings indicate that EVs can serve as “antigen plus adjuvant” carriers to enhance antitumor immunity. In osteosarcoma, one could consider loading highly expressed antigens such as GD2, selected neoantigens, or lung metastasis-associated antigens onto autologous DCs, collecting Dex as a vaccine, and combining it with immune checkpoint inhibitors or metabolic/vascular remodeling strategies. However, this concept remains speculative in OS and should be viewed as a potential future direction rather than a standalone therapeutic solution.

Experimental platforms combining tumor EVs with innate immune agonists

Morishita et al. used genetic engineering to generate tumor cell-derived EVs that simultaneously carry tumor antigens and CpG DNA, markedly enhancing DC activation and CD8+ T-cell responses in murine solid tumor models and achieving superior antitumor effects compared with antigen or CpG alone [103]. Qazi et al. further showed that antigen-loaded EVs can induce B cell-dependent Th1-type memory responses even in the absence of exogenous cell transfer [104].

These studies collectively provide a theoretical foundation for “tumor EV + TLR agonists/costimulatory molecules” as a novel vaccine platform. Translated to osteosarcoma, one could envisage using OS-derived EVs enriched in lung metastasis-related antigens, such as COL6A1-high subpopulations, combined with innate immune agonists such as CpG or cGAMP and costimulatory antibodies within the same EV or biomimetic nanocarrier to induce CD8+ T-cell responses in combination with checkpoint blockade. However, in the absence of OS-specific vaccine-model data, such concepts should be regarded as hypothesis-generating frameworks rather than strategies ready for immediate clinical development. Additional safety concerns include the possibility that tumor-derived EV vaccines may carry pro-metastatic, immunosuppressive, or resistance-promoting cargo unless they are stringently engineered, purified, and functionally tested.

Potential synergy with existing immunotherapeutic approaches in osteosarcoma

In osteosarcoma, conventional type 1 dendritic cell vaccines, small-molecule immune modulators, and early PD-1/CTLA-4 inhibitor trials have been conducted, but overall response rates remain low [15], [32]. Conceptually, EV vaccines are better positioned as “front-end modules for antigen presentation and T-cell clonal expansion,” aimed at strengthening the initial immune response and thereby improving the efficacy of subsequent immune checkpoint inhibitors or cellular therapies. For example, EV vaccines could be combined with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors to expand neoantigen-specific CD8+ T cells and relieve functional exhaustion, or used alongside γδT/NK cell therapies to enhance local inflammatory microenvironments and improve adoptive cell infiltration and survival. Because OS is an immune-cold tumor, successful EV-vaccine design will likely require combination with myeloid-cell reprogramming, vascular normalization, ferroptosis induction, or pulmonary niche remodeling rather than simple antigen delivery alone.

Future studies will need to pay particular attention to directing EV vaccine-induced immune activation toward key suppressive cell populations within the pulmonary pre-metastatic niche, such as CAFs and immunosuppressive macrophages, to minimize the risk of systemic immune-related adverse events. Equally important, OS-specific EV vaccines should first be evaluated in immunocompetent orthotopic and metastatic models with endpoints including antigen-specific T-cell expansion, myeloid reprogramming, pulmonary metastasis burden, systemic cytokine activation, and long-term safety.

Clinical and translational landscape: From biomarkers to early therapeutic exploration

Clinical progress of EV-based liquid biopsy in osteosarcoma

To date, registered and published clinical studies on osteosarcoma-derived EVs have focused almost exclusively on diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment monitoring. In a prospective cohort, Wang et al. demonstrated that baseline serum exosomal PD-L1 levels at diagnosis independently predicted overall survival and pulmonary metastasis risk in osteosarcoma patients and provided specific thresholds for prognostic stratification [27]. Another study suggested that combining exosomal PD-L1 with N-cadherin further improves prediction of pulmonary metastatic progression [28]. In addition, microfluidic-SERS, single-EV profiling, and functional EV activity-based platforms have also shown high accuracy or discriminatory potential for diagnosing or stratifying osteosarcoma, providing a technical foundation for dynamic monitoring of resistance evolution and metastatic risk [55], [59], [60]. However, these studies remain biomarker-oriented and do not constitute clinical evaluation of therapeutic EV products.

Overall, EV-based liquid biopsy in osteosarcoma has progressed from the stage of “feasibility demonstration” to the more advanced question of “how to standardize and integrate multiparametric information,” but has not yet extended into clinical trials of therapeutic EV preparations.

Lessons from EV therapeutic trials in other diseases for osteosarcoma

Across the broader EV field, clinical translation remains early and heterogeneous. A recent systematic review of EV-related clinical trials catalogued hundreds of EV-related studies across diverse diseases, with diagnostics and companion diagnostics representing a large proportion of the field, whereas therapeutic EV trials more commonly involve MSC-derived EVs and non-OS indications [107]. Another systematic review and meta-analysis of human EV-based therapy reported generally reassuring short-term safety signals, but emphasized that interpretation is limited by heterogeneity in EV source, manufacturing methods, product characterization, study design, dosing, administration route, and adverse-event reporting [108]. Thus, the current clinical landscape supports feasibility and early safety exploration rather than established therapeutic efficacy.

As noted above, non-OS Dex trials in melanoma and NSCLC provide feasibility and safety precedents, but their efficacy signals were limited and their relevance to OS remains indirect [102], [105], [106].

For drug-delivery EVs, OS-specific clinical trials of Exo-Dox, EM-Dox, BT-EXO, EV-siRNA, or EV-CRISPR systems have not yet been reported. Therefore, clinical experiences from non-OS EV products should be treated as regulatory and technological references rather than direct evidence supporting therapeutic efficacy in osteosarcoma. Future OS trials should begin with small, carefully monitored phase I studies emphasizing safety, biodistribution, pharmacokinetics, immunogenicity, dose escalation, and pharmacodynamic EV biomarkers rather than efficacy endpoints alone.

Translational roadmap from “biomarker” to “intervention target”

Synthesizing current evidence, the clinical translation of osteosarcoma EVs can be conceptualized as a four-stage, progressively deeper pathway:

(1) Diagnostic and risk stratification stage: Under the guidance of MISEV2023 and using standardized separation workflows such as SEC and microfluidics, construct multiparametric panels including exosomal PD-L1, N-cadherin, EV subpopulation profiles, EV-associated MMP activity scores, and EV-associated ncRNAs to predict MAP regimen efficacy, pulmonary metastasis risk, and potential responsiveness to immunotherapy [27], [28], [55], [59], [60], [63], [64].

(2) Manufacturing and safety-readiness stage: Before therapeutic testing, candidate EV/EM products should undergo standardized characterization of cell source, culture conditions, EV identity, purity, potency, cargo loading, residual free drug or nucleic acid, sterility, endotoxin burden, stability, biodistribution, and batch comparability [86], [87].

(3) Small-scale early clinical exploration stage: Within clearly defined stratification and follow-up frameworks, early phase I trials of MSC/BMSC-EV/EM-based delivery systems could be considered only after robust preclinical toxicology and pharmacokinetic data are available. Such trials should prioritize safety, dose escalation, pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, immunogenicity, and pharmacodynamic EV biomarkers as primary endpoints.

(4) Mechanism-driven early intervention stage: Building on the above, integrated “biomarker + intervention” trials could eventually evaluate EV-based drug delivery, EV-inhibitory strategies, or EV-vaccine combinations in carefully selected high-risk patients, such as those with high EV PD-L1/N-cadherin burden, elevated metastatic EV subpopulation scores, or increasing EV-associated MMP activity. At present, however, this stage remains aspirational and should be considered a long-term translational goal rather than an immediately actionable clinical pathway [27], [28], [59], [60], [99].

Thus, the therapeutic arm of OS EV research remains preclinical, and future studies should avoid efficacy-forward claims until product consistency, dosing, biodistribution, and long-term safety have been established, particularly for pediatric and adolescent patients.

Challenges and future directions

In recent years, research on OS-related EVs has made substantial progress in understanding the spread of chemoresistance, immune regulation, and the establishment of the pulmonary pre-metastatic niche. However, to advance EVs from mechanistic findings to clinically applicable tools for monitoring or intervention, multiple limitations must be overcome at the levels of methodology, systems biology, and population specificity. This section outlines future research priorities from five perspectives: methodological reliability, spatiotemporal mechanistic dissection, AI-assisted multi-omics integration, age-specific considerations, and translational implementation.

Methodological standardization and EV heterogeneity resolution: From Bias recognition to forward-looking solutions

Clinical studies of OS-derived EVs are particularly vulnerable to methodological bias because circulating tumor-derived EVs are diluted by abundant blood-cell-derived vesicles, whereas tissue-derived EV preparations may be affected by mineralized matrix components and tissue dissociation procedures. These issues have been discussed in detail in Section 2; therefore, the key priority for future work is not merely to recognize these confounders, but to develop standardized, OS-adapted solutions that can be implemented across laboratories and clinical centers.

A first priority is to establish harmonized pre-analytical workflows for OS liquid biopsy studies. These workflows should define anticoagulant type, centrifugation conditions, acceptable time from blood draw to processing, platelet-removal criteria, plasma storage conditions, and contamination assessment using lipoprotein and intracellular-protein markers. Such standardization is essential for reducing false-positive signals from platelet-derived EVs, lipoproteins, and soluble proteins, and for improving the comparability of EV biomarker studies across centers [22], [38], [39], [51], [57].

In parallel, tissue-derived OS EV studies should adopt mineral- and dissociation-specific quality controls rather than directly applying plasma EV workflows to bone-associated specimens. For mineral-rich samples, future protocols should incorporate chelation-based controls, density-gradient or size-based orthogonal separation, and assessment of osteogenic markers such as ALP and osteocalcin. For enzymatically dissociated tissues, the integrity of EV surface markers should be evaluated after processing, particularly when studies rely on antibody-based detection of immune checkpoint molecules, integrins, or metastasis-associated surface markers [47], [48], [49], [50], [52].

Another essential direction is to resolve EV heterogeneity through subpopulation-aware analytical frameworks. Single-EV profiling, immunoaffinity enrichment, spatially informed tissue analysis, and in vivo EV-tracking approaches should be combined to distinguish tumor-derived EVs from stromal-, immune-, endothelial-, osteoblast-lineage-, and platelet-derived EVs. This is especially important in OS, where tumor cells interact with bone marrow stromal cells, osteoblast-lineage cells, immune cells, and pulmonary niche components [59], [60], [94].

At the field level, future OS-EV studies should report methodological metadata in a structured and comparable manner. Key metadata should include sample type, processing time, isolation method, EV identity markers, negative controls, contamination markers, particle-to-protein ratio, and functional potency assays when therapeutic EV products are evaluated. Establishing multicenter OS-EV reference datasets under such reporting standards would provide a foundation for reproducible biomarker discovery, external validation, and eventual clinical translation [22].

Spatiotemporal dissection of the continuous “resistance-immunity-metastasis” chain

Current evidence indicates that EVs play a continuous role in resistance acquisition, worsening immunosuppression, and formation of the pulmonary pre-metastatic niche in OS. However, most studies remain cross-sectional and lack dynamic analyses of disease evolution.

Chemotherapy-induced EV reprogramming is an important component of resistance development. EVs enriched in P-gp can rapidly mediate efflux pump-dependent spread of resistance [72], while chemotherapy may alter the release of immune-regulatory EVs; importantly, a high circulating PD-L1 + EV burden is associated with poor outcomes [27], [28]. Nonetheless, the precise positioning of these phenomena along the clinical treatment timeline remains unclear.

On the metastatic side, COL6A1-enriched EVs can reprogram pulmonary fibroblasts toward a CAF phenotype and induce secretion of multiple pro-metastatic factors, thereby constructing a pulmonary pre-metastatic niche [84]. Whether OS tEVs exhibit organ tropism and preferentially home to lung tissue still lacks direct in vivo evidence.

At the immune-regulatory level, EVs have been shown to reprogram mesenchymal stem cells via the IL-6/STAT3 axis and to influence macrophage polarization [81]; circulating PD-L1-positive EVs may reflect systemic immunosuppressive status and are associated with poor outcomes in OS patients [27], [28]. Current evidence supports a conceptual model in which EVs may function along a continuous resistance-immunity-metastasis axis, coordinating chemotherapy adaptation, immune evasion, and pre-metastatic niche formation in osteosarcoma.

Future research needs to establish longitudinal EV-monitoring cohorts and combine radiotracer and metabolic labeling technologies for in vivo visualization, in order to generate spatiotemporal maps of EV dynamics and align them with key treatment time points.

AI and multi-omics integration: toward system-level EV profiling

For future work, AI and multi-omics integration should move from proof-of-concept classification toward externally validated, system-level EV models that can account for OS molecular heterogeneity, immune status, and treatment phase.

EV-PD-L1, N-cadherin, EV subpopulation markers, EV-associated MMP activity scores, ferroptosis-related ncRNAs, and COL6A1 may serve as complementary model inputs [27], [28], [59], [63], [84]. Their value will depend on integration with radiomics, clinical variables, and standardized sample metadata rather than on single-marker performance alone.

The development of single-particle technologies will further accelerate “deconvolution” of bulk EV data to infer hidden tEV subpopulations, followed by targeted omics validation of their functional roles. If such models can also incorporate parameters such as EV production rates, tissue deposition patterns, and clearance kinetics, they may evolve into dynamic EV models suitable for therapeutic simulation.

At present, however, the lack of standardized EV isolation protocols across centers, inconsistent sample handling, and insufficient shared databases significantly limits the external generalizability of AI models. Establishing standardized, shareable OS-EV multi-omics cohorts will be a critical step toward clinical implementation.

Age-specific considerations in osteosarcoma EV research: Children, adolescents, and older adults

Osteosarcoma exhibits a characteristic age distribution involving children, adolescents, and, in a smaller but clinically important subgroup, older adults [1]. However, current OS EV biomarker studies rarely treat age as a biological or clinical covariate. This limitation is particularly relevant to the biomarker “cut-off” dilemma: thresholds derived from mixed-age or adult-skewed cohorts may not be directly transferable across age groups because EV baseline levels, EV cellular sources, bone-remodeling activity, immune status, and treatment tolerance can differ substantially between developing and older individuals.

In children and adolescents, active skeletal growth, high bone-remodeling rates, dynamically evolving bone marrow niches, and developmental immune maturation may influence EV production, cargo composition, and clearance behavior. In this population, EV-based liquid biopsy may offer specific clinical advantages because serial blood-based monitoring is less invasive than repeated tissue biopsy and may complement imaging during neoadjuvant chemotherapy, postoperative surveillance, and pulmonary metastasis monitoring. Nevertheless, pediatric implementation must account for limited blood volume, age-adjusted reference ranges, treatment-stage-specific baselines, and the need to minimize unnecessary sampling burden [1], [6].

Older adults with OS represent a distinct vulnerable population. Compared with adolescents, older patients may have more comorbidities, lower tolerance to intensive chemotherapy, a higher probability of secondary OS or treatment-related disease contexts, and competing risks that complicate repeated invasive assessment. In this setting, EV-based liquid biopsy may serve as a low-burden adjunct for monitoring treatment response, detecting molecular evidence of progression, and individualizing surveillance intensity when repeated biopsy or frequent intensive imaging is less feasible. At the same time, age-related changes in systemic inflammation, coagulation status, platelet activity, renal function, and tissue remodeling may alter circulating EV profiles and confound biomarker interpretation.

Future OS EV studies should therefore establish age-stratified EV reference ranges and evaluate whether biomarker thresholds for exosomal PD-L1, N-cadherin, EV subpopulation profiles, EV-associated MMP activity scores, and EV-ncRNAs require age-specific calibration [27], [28], [59], [60], [63], [64]. Age should be incorporated as a predefined covariate in cohort design, statistical modeling, and clinical trial stratification, rather than treated as a secondary demographic variable. Such an approach would allow liquid biopsy to be developed not as a universal fixed-threshold test, but as an age-adapted monitoring strategy for distinct OS patient populations.

Implementation priorities for clinical translation of osteosarcoma EVs

Implementation should focus on two linked deliverables: externally validated EV biomarker workflows and regulatory-ready therapeutic EV/EM product definitions. Biomarker studies should calibrate exosomal PD-L1, N-cadherin, COL6A1-associated signatures, EV subpopulation profiles, and EV-associated MMP activity scores according to disease stage, treatment phase, metastatic status, sample-processing procedures, and patient age [22], [27], [28], [59], [60].

For therapeutic products, early studies should remain safety-oriented and define identity, purity, potency, biodistribution, stability, cargo retention, and batch comparability before efficacy claims are advanced [86], [87]. [107], [108]. Through these implementation steps, OS EV research may progress from mechanistic discovery toward clinically testable monitoring and intervention frameworks.

Conclusion

As a highly heterogeneous solid tumor with pronounced immunosuppression, osteosarcoma exhibits a malignant evolutionary trajectory characterized by a continuous mechanistic chain from acquisition of chemoresistance and deepening immunosuppression to the formation of the pulmonary pre-metastatic niche. Current evidence indicates that EVs traverse and connect key nodes along this chain: they can rapidly disseminate resistant phenotypes across tumor cell populations, remodel local and systemic immune microenvironments, and participate in the construction of distant organ niches. This integrative role makes EVs a critical biological vehicle for understanding the structural organization of osteosarcoma disease progression and provides a unifying framework for the development of diagnostic, monitoring, and therapeutic strategies.

Future progress will depend on standardized EV workflows, longitudinal validation of EV dynamics, age-aware biomarker calibration, and regulatory-grade development of companion diagnostic or therapeutic EV products. These priorities should be pursued as coordinated steps rather than isolated technical additions.

As these foundations are progressively strengthened, EVs are poised to become core modules linking mechanism, stratification, and intervention, offering new conceptual anchors and practical directions for precision therapy and long-term outcome improvement in osteosarcoma.
