Evaluation of Lead exposure’s link to autism-like behavior and Shank 3 gene expression in C57BL/6J mice
Authors: Sunao TAKABA, Nelly BANDA, Rio DOYA, Nyein Chan SOE, Kohei OGASAWARA, Anri HIRAI, Yoshinori IKENAKA, Mayumi ISHIZUKA, Shouta MM NAKAYAMA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) affects about 1 in 100 people worldwide, yet its causes remain poorly understood. Environmental factors, such as perinatal lead (Pb) exposure, have been linked to neurodevelopmental disruption, especially during periods when the blood-brain barrier is immature. This study aimed to assess ASD-like behavioral outcomes in offspring following maternal Pb exposure during neurodevelopment using a C57BL/6J mouse model. Pregnant dams received 100 ppm Pb acetate in their drinking water from the second week of gestation until weaning; controls were given deionised distilled water. Male offspring underwent behavioral assessments at 5 and 9 weeks of age using the three-chambered Social-Nonsocial (S-NS) interaction test and the Morris water maze. Pb concentrations were measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), and real-time PCR was used to target Shank3, an ASD-associated gene. Lead concentrations were significantly elevated in the blood, brain, bone, liver, and kidney of exposed offspring, confirming effective maternal-foetal Pb transfer. However, no significant differences were observed between groups in behavioral performance or Shank3 expression. These findings suggest that perinatal Pb exposure at 100 ppm in the last trimester of pregnancy, under the conditions tested, does not induce measurable ASD-like phenotypes in male C57BL/6J mice. Further studies are warranted to investigate dose-dependent effects, sex-specific vulnerabilities, and the broader molecular pathways involved.

INTRODUCTION

Lead (Pb) is one of the most toxic heavy metals, widely distributed in the environment due to anthropogenic activities such as mining, recycling, and the manufacture of Pb-containing products, including lead-acid batteries and formerly leaded gasoline [29, 43]. Although regulatory measures, such as the End-of-Life Vehicles (ELV) Directive and Restriction of Hazardous Substances (RoHS) in the European Union [24], and the Environmental Design of Electrical Equipment (EDEE) Act in the United States, have significantly reduced Pb usage, environmental contamination persists, particularly in developing countries with a high demand for Pb-based equipment. In developed regions, residual exposure remains a concern in older infrastructures, such as houses built before 1978 that may contain Pb-based paint [10].

Human exposure to Pb occurs through multiple environmental pathways, including air, food, and drinking water. Once absorbed, Pb can cross biological barriers and accumulate in various tissues, including the brain. The blood-brain barrier (BBB) typically protects the central nervous system (CNS) from non-lipophilic toxins via mechanisms such as ATP-binding cassette transporters and P-glycoprotein [2]. However, during early development, the BBB is immature, and the blood-cerebrospinal fluid barrier is more permeable, allowing heavy metals like Pb to infiltrate the brain [35].

Pb neurotoxicity is multifaceted. It disrupts neurotransmission by interfering with N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors (NMDARs), competing with zinc (Zn) at allosteric binding sites, and altering calcium ion (Ca2+) influx. During neurodevelopment, Pb exposure has been shown to reduce mRNA levels of the NMDAR GluN2A subunit in the hippocampus, a region critical for learning and memory. Moreover, Pb can be transferred from mother to fetus via the placenta and through lactation, posing significant risks during the perinatal period when the CNS is rapidly developing [5].

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterised by deficits in social communication, restricted interests, and repetitive behavior. According to the World Health Organization’s International Classification of diseases −11th edition, additional symptoms may include sensory sensitivity, learning difficulties, and heightened anxiety. While ASD has a strong genetic basis, supported by twin studies, genome-wide analyses identify copy number variants (CNVs) and single-nucleotide variants (SNVs). Environmental factors are estimated to account for 40–50% of ASD liability. These include toxic exposures, parental age, nutrition, and foetal environment. Some studies using rodent models indicate that both prenatal and postnatal exposure to Pb can precipitate autism-like phenotypes, characterized by deficits in social interaction, repetitive behavior, and cognitive impairments. These behavioral alterations appear to be mediated by Pb-induced disruptions of neurodevelopmental signalling pathways, particularly underscoring the potential role of environmental toxicants in the aetiology of neurodevelopmental disorders [15, 16, 21].

Pb exposure has been implicated in ASD aetiology through its impact on neurotransmitter systems, including cholinergic, dopaminergic, serotonergic, and glutamatergic pathways [10]. Case-control studies have reported elevated blood Pb levels in children with ASD compared to neurotypical controls [30]. However, the mechanistic link between Pb exposure and ASD-like behavior remains unclear, necessitating both in vivo and in vitro investigations.

Among the many genes associated with ASD, Shank3 has emerged as a critical candidate [23]. The Shank family of scaffold proteins (Shank1, Shank2, Shank3) plays a central role in organising the postsynaptic density (PSD) and regulating synaptic transmission [5]. Shank3 is highly expressed in the striatum [26, 42], and its deletion or mutation is associated with severe ASD phenotypes, including impaired social behavior and cognitive deficits. Notably, in vitro studies have shown that exposure to biometal profiles characteristic of ASD (low Zn, high Cd, Pb, and Hg) reduces Shank gene expression and synapse density [13].

Given the suggested link between Pb neurotoxicity and Shank3 dysregulation, this study aims to evaluate ASD-like behavior and Shank3 expression in offspring following maternal Pb exposure during neurodevelopment using a C57BL/6J mouse model.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Figure 1Fig. 1.Shows the outline of the animal exposure experiment. illustrates the outline of the experiment. Thirty C57BL/J6 dams (14 days pregnant) aged 10–14 weeks were sourced from Japan SLC, Inc. (Hamamatsu, Japan). The dams were divided into two groups (control and Pb exposure) and housed exclusively in 30 polypropylene cages. Mice were kept on a 12-hr light (10:00–22:00)/dark (22:00–10:00) cycle. A shepherd’s shack and two wooden blocks were used as enrichment. Food (rodent chow, Labo MR Stock, Nosan Corp., Yokohama, Japan) was provided ad libitum. Lead acetate at 100 mg/L (Wako Pure Chemicals Corp., Osaka, Japan) was administered via drinking water to the Pb-exposed group until the offspring were weaned, while the control group received deionised distilled water (4 weeks after birth). The Pb concentration was chosen based on Pb concentrations recorded in the environment [40].

Shows the outline of the animal exposure experiment.

Following weaning, dams were euthanised in a carbon dioxide (CO2) chamber, and blood, brain, liver, kidney, and tibia samples were collected in polypropylene tubes. After sex determination, only males were selected for further experiments, while all female offspring were euthanised in a CO2 Chamber. Males were selected to reduce hormonal variability that may affect females during S-NS test [6]. Male mice were housed in separate cages, with 3 per cage, and littermates were distributed across different cages to prevent cage bias. At 5 weeks of age, 20 male mice (10 control, 10 Pb exposed) underwent behavioral testing using the 3-phase social-non-social (S-NS) protocol and the Morris water maze. Following testing, the mice were euthanised with carbon dioxide, and blood and tissue samples were collected in polypropylene tubes for analysis of Pb concentration. At 9 weeks of age, testing was repeated similarly to that at 5 weeks, with another group of 20 mice (10 controls, 10 Pb exposed). Euthanasia was carried out using a carbon dioxide chamber following the guidelines from the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA). This study received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Hokkaido University, Japan (approval number: 23-0174), which is accredited by the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International (AAALAC International).

3-phase S-NS protocol

To evaluate the sociability, a 3-Phase S-NS protocol was performed according to the study by Rein et al [34]. Figure 2Fig. 2.Schematic diagram of 3-phase S-NS protocol. displays the schematic diagram of the S-NS protocol.

Schematic diagram of 3-phase S-NS protocol.

For social stimulus, control mice of the same age and sex as the test mice were prepared (N=10) (hereinafter referred to as “stimulus mice”). The stimulus mice were individuals that had no contact with any of the test mice after birth. To minimise stress for the stimulus mice, the same individual was not used in consecutive tests, and each individual was used no more than once per day. The apparatus used had the following specifications: 120 cm (length) × 45 cm (width) × 45 cm (height). It was divided into three chambers, each 40 cm long. The walls of the apparatus are made of transparent glass. To house the social or non-social stimulus, two wire pencil cups (silver-coloured, made of sturdy steel), 9 cm (diameter) × 10 cm (height), with mesh for animal interaction and sniffing, were used. A 250 mL glass bottle was placed on top of each cup to prevent the test mouse from climbing. An inanimate object was placed in the cup as a non-social stimulus. In this study, a 10 cm (length) × 2.5 cm (width) bone-shaped enrichment object (green coloured, made of polyurethane) was utilised. During the pre-test phase, two identical paper balls served as inanimate objects. All equipment was handled with clean latex gloves and wiped down with an 80% ethanol solution after each test.

Procedure

To increase locomotor activity, the behavioral room was maintained at 23°C with lighting set to 20 lux. The apparatus was placed in a room with white walls and no visual obstructions. Test mice were transported to the behavioral room and allowed to habituate for at least 1 hr. They were then habituated to the apparatus for 10 min, followed by a 10-min pre-test in which two paper balls were placed under each inverted cup. The social preference test lasted 10 min, during which an inanimate object was placed under one cup and a stimulus mouse under the other. Interaction time(s) with the social stimulus (TS) and the non-social stimulus (TNS) were recorded using SMART 3.0 (Philips, s/n: DCF76-90C; Bio Research Centre Corp., Ltd., Aichi, Japan). After 10 min, the test mouse was removed from the apparatus and returned to its home cage. The stimulus mouse and enrichment object were then removed, and all equipment was cleaned with 80% ethanol.

To evaluate sociability, social preference indexes (ISP=(TS−TNS)/ (TS + TNS)) are compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Morris water maze test

To evaluate memory and learning flexibility, the Morris water maze test was performed as described by Golub and Lanoue [11] (Fig. 3Fig. 3.Schematic diagram of the Morris water maze test.). A reversal test was also performed based on Lu et al. [22] with a few modifications. Mice were trained for 6 days (including a visible test on the first day), and the probe test was performed on day 7. Finally, the reversal test was performed on day 8. Escape latency and total distance travelled were recorded using SMART 3.0 tracking software.

Schematic diagram of the Morris water maze test.

Pb concentration analysis by ICP-MS

Pb concentration analysis by ICP-MS (7850 series; Agilent Technologies, Tokyo, Japan) was performed according to the methods previously described by Nakata et al. [28]. Blood, brain, liver, kidney, and tibia were put into each polystyrene tray and dried in an oven at 50°C for 48 hr. Each dried sample was weighed and digested in pre-washed vessels, with 5 mL of nitric acid (atomic absorption spectrometry grade, 30%) and 1 mL of hydrogen peroxide (Cica reagent, 30%; Kanto Chemical) using Speed Wave MWS-2 microwave digestion system (Berghof, Eningen, Germany). Analytical quality was verified using the DOLT-5 certified reference material (National Research Council Canada, Ottawa, Canada). The recovery rate for DOLT5 was 96–100%. The method detection limit (MDL) was for Pb was 1.3 ng/g, and the method quantification limit (MQL) was 3.8 ng/g.

2.5 RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and Real-time PCR

RNA was extracted from the striatum and stored at −80°C. The tissue was immersed in 200 µL of TRIzol (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) and homogenised with a pestle. Then 800 µL of TRIzol and 200 µL of chloroform were added, and the mixture was mixed well. The mixture was centrifuged at 12,000× g at 4°C for 15 min and the supernatant was collected. RNA was extracted using the FastGene® kit (Nippon Genetics) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and quantity of the extracted RNA were checked using gel electrophoresis and a Nanodrop’-1000A spectrophotometer, respectively. Double-strand cDNA was synthesized using Rever Tra Ace qPCR RT Kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time PCR was performed using a Quant Studio 12K Flex Real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The primers used in this study were those used in a previous study (F: GATCTGCCATCCCTACAAC, R: AGCTAAGGGTGAGCTAGGAT) [33]. The PCR was conducted under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95°C (2 min), 40 cycles of annealing at 95°C (5 sec), and amplification at 60°C (30 sec). Normalisation was done using the housekeeping genes: Glyceraldehyde-3-Phosphate Dehydrogenase 208 (GAPDH), Beta-actin, Peptidylprolyl Isomerase A (PPIA), B2 microglobulin, and Ribosomal protein L18. Then, PPIA was selected as it was more stable than the others. Relative expression levels were calculated using the comparative CT method (ΔΔCT method).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro 17 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Student’s t-test was employed to analyse mouse body weights, while Wilcoxon rank sum tests were used for Pb concentrations, behavioral tests, and real-time PCR results. Results for the 3-phase S-NS protocol, probe test, reversal test in the Morris water maze, and relative Shank3 expression levels are reported as median ± SD. Results for the body weight of the test mice, Pb concentrations in blood and organs, and training outcomes in the Morris water maze are shown as mean ± SD. P-values <0.05 are considered statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to evaluate the effects of perinatal Pb exposure on foetal neurodevelopment, focusing on ASD-like behavior and the expression levels of ASD-related genes, Shank3.

The results of this experiment showed that Pb concentrations in the blood and various organs of the Pb exposure group pups were significantly higher than in the control group pups. Still, no significant changes were observed in behavioral tests or the relative expression levels of the Shank3 gene.

Vulnerability of mouse pup’s brain to Pb exposure

The dams were exposed to Pb during the latter gestational period (from day 14 to parturition) and throughout lactation, thereby simulating transplacental and transmammary exposure in the pups. Other potential routes to pups included those via maternal faeces via inhalation, coprophagy, or grooming after direct contact. There was no significant difference in sex ratio between the pups of the groups, similar to a previous report by Montrose et al. [27]. For adult mice, chronic Pb exposure leads to high accumulation in the liver and kidney, which is around ten to twenty times higher than in the brain [39]. On the other hand, this study showed Pb residues in the brain that were 2.53–3.08 times higher than in the liver. This result indicates that the foetal brain has a low capacity to sequester Pb and may be a primary target for this toxicant.

In this study, while higher blood Pb concentrations were observed in the dams of the Pb-exposed group (0.132 ppm), those in their pups were lower than their mothers’ (0.018 ppm). In contrast, a case-control study of children with ASD and healthy children in Nigeria (N=25 per group) reported blood Pb concentrations of 0.095 ± 0.040 ppm in children with ASD, compared to 0.054 ± 0.020 ppm in healthy children [30]. Indicating that levels of Pb attained in the pups were lower than those in affected ASD children. In this experiment, the exposure duration was limited to 5 weeks, from day 14 of gestation until weaning. This protocol was chosen to avoid teratogenic effects during the organogenesis stage in mice [5] and to ensure a larger sample size for behavioral tests. The exposure levels and duration might not have been enough to cause autism-related changes. Lead exposure alone is not a critical factor in developing autism; it also depends on genetic predisposition [16].

Assessing memory, restricted interests and learning flexibility using Morris water maze test

In this study, the Morris water maze test was performed to evaluate impaired learning ability, and a reversal test was also conducted to assess restricted interests and learning flexibility. ASD like behavior is characterised by cognitive rigidity even though it is affecting the outcome of the task, it was expected for the Pb- exposed mice to keep returning to the previous platform during the reversal test. Most parameters in the Morris water maze test showed no significant differences, and no ASD-like behavior was observed in the Pb exposure group. Additionally, on the 4th and 5th days of training at 9 weeks of age, the Pb exposure group exhibited shorter latency to escape and covered less total distance than the control group.

As a premise, the Morris water maze test has some limitations. One of the most common problems with this test is that stress in mice can easily affect the results [25, 41]. In studies using rats, the Morris water maze test has been shown to increase corticosterone in the rat’s brain and blood plasma [1]. The effect of stress on the test results is reflected as an inverted U-shaped function [41]. Reliable results cannot be obtained if the stress is too high or too low. Therefore, it is necessary to carefully consider the number of trials per day, the duration of training, the experimental environment, and other relevant factors.

Dams’ maternal stress and mothering ability significantly affect the growth of their pups [17]. There were no significant differences in the pups’ weights in this study; however, the control groups had more cages excluded due to infanticide or neglect. Studies using rats show that preweaning maternal separation and loss of contact between dams and pups, such as grooming, elicit a complex adaptive biobehavioral response in preweaning pups. This response includes (1) a decrease in the synthesis of ornithine decarboxylase, a necessary enzyme for cell growth, (2) a reduction in DNA synthesis, an index of cell multiplication, (3) abnormal patterns of neuroendocrine secretion, and (4) a suppression of cellular responses to three major trophic hormones: growth hormone, prolactin, and insulin [18]. These changes can lead to delayed physical growth and behavioral development in pups [17]. In contrast, adequate maternal care early in life reduces stress reactivity in mice. This may have caused the poorer performance of control pups in the water maze test. As with the social assessment, it is essential to match the dams’ condition, such as age or parity, to accurately evaluate the pups’ behavior.

Shank 3 expression

In this study, pups exposed to Pb did not show a significant decrease in Shank3 gene levels compared to controls, suggesting that Pb exposure does not necessarily affect Shank expression during this developmental period. This supports the broader view that environmental neurotoxicants can affect neurodevelopment via multiple molecular pathways, not just those involving Shank proteins. While disruptions in Shank genes are a known genetic factor in autism, they represent about 1% of cases [20], highlighting the diverse genetic basis of autism. Therefore, the absence of a decrease in Shank3 levels does not rule out the presence of autism-related traits. These findings emphasise the importance of considering a wide range of molecular, genetic, and environmental influences when assessing neurodevelopmental effects of toxicant exposure.

Utility of the mouse to assess sociability

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) remains a clinically diagnosed condition, with no established biological or technical markers for diagnosis. According to DSM-5 criteria, ASD is characterised by difficulties in social communication, restricted interests, repetitive behavior, and functional impairments across multiple areas. Since Kanner’s initial description in 1943, core features such as impaired social interaction and decreased behavioral flexibility have guided both clinical and research investigations.

In this study, the 3-phase S-NS protocol, a modified version of the 3-chamber social interaction test, was employed to measure sociability [12, 31]. Like a previous study by Dinçkol et al. [8] Pb exposure at 100 ppm did not cause significant changes in social interactions compared with the control group during the 3-phase S-NS test. While this protocol aims to reduce novelty effects, it only captures a single behavioral metric: time spent investigating a stimulus [34]. Due to the complexity of ASD phenotypes, this limited scope might be insufficient. Factors such as anxiety, sensory processing, and motor function can affect sociability measures, but were not evaluated in this study. Future research should incorporate multi-dimensional behavioral assessments to better identify ASD-related traits [37].

Notably, Pb exposure was associated with altered anxiety-related behavior in the various mazes, but sociability and social anxiety were not assessed [14, 36]. This gap highlights the need for comprehensive behavioral profiling when evaluating environmental neurotoxicity in ASD models.

Genetic models such as 16p11.2+/−mice have shown intact social preference but deficits in other domains, including sex preference, mating vocalisations, social memory, and habituation [3, 32, 38]. These findings underscore the heterogeneity of ASD-like behavior and the importance of task-specific sensitivity. Therefore, the lack of statistically significant differences between control and Pb-exposed pups in the current social behavior tests does not completely rule out the possibility that developmental Pb exposure contributes to ASD-related alterations that were not detected by the present study paradigms.

Postnatal environmental factors, including maternal care and sibling dynamics, further complicate behavioral outcomes [4]. In this study, randomised dam age and parity may have contributed to maternal stress, as evidenced by infanticide in the control group. Although parity was randomised, the disproportionate neglect among control dams suggests that background variables may have influenced maternal behavior. Standardising maternal conditions or increasing sample size may help mitigate such confounding effects.

In this study, the relationship between Pb exposure and the onset of ASD was evaluated using behavioral tests and the expression levels of the ASD-related gene Shank3 in perinatal Pb-exposed model mice.

Although no ASD-like behavior or changes in Shank3 expression levels were observed in the Pb exposure group, the mechanism by which Pb exposure leads to autism could be better understood by conducting additional behavioral tests and extending the exposure period (earlier than in this study). ASD development has been linked to neurogenesis disorders that occur early in embryogenesis, in mice around day 9 of pregnancy [7, 9, 19]. Exposure began on day 14 of pregnancy in this study; therefore, adjusting the exposure time would be essential to firmly establish Pb as one of the contributing factors ASD.
