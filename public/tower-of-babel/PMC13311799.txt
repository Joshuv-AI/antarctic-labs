Self-Efficacy, Psychological State, Oral Health Behaviour, and Oral Health-Related Quality of Life in Chinese Patients With Periodontal Disease
Authors: Yizhen Yu, Shuo Du, Kaige Xu, Mingyue Wang, Xintong Duan, Jin Li, Songhan Zi, Yan Si
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and aimsPeriodontal disease, a major oral disease, includes various inflammatory diseases that affect the tooth support structure (gums, alveolar bone, and periodontal ligament) and can lead to tooth loss and promote systemic inflammation, affecting more than 1 billion people worldwide. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between self-efficacy, psychological state, oral health behaviours, and oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) in patients with periodontal disease.MethodsConvenience sampling was implemented to recruit participants diagnosed with periodontal disease. Data on demographic characteristics, tooth brushing frequency, and self-reported oral health status were collected using a structured questionnaire. Participants were assessed using the General Self-Efficacy Scale, 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire, Modified Dental Anxiety Scale, and Oral Health Impact Profile (OHIP-14). Finally, structural equation modelling (SEM) was used to identify associations.ResultsOf the 366 participants included in the study, 77.6% reported brushing their teeth at least twice daily, and 53.4% reported using dental floss at least once daily. The mean OHRQoL score (OHIP-14) was 24.7. The SEM showed a good fit. Depression was negatively correlated with self-efficacy (β = −0.235, P < .001), while self-efficacy was positively correlated with oral health behaviours (β = 0.176, P < .001). Furthermore, oral health status, socioeconomic status, depression, and anxiety were significant predictors of OHRQoL.ConclusionSelf-efficacy played an important role in establishing good oral health behaviours.Clinical RelevanceFuture interventions should prioritize enhancing self-efficacy and paying more attention to psychological factors (such as depression) to effectively promote oral health behaviours.

Introduction

Oral diseases constitute the most important health problem globally, affecting an estimated 3.69 billion people worldwide.1 The estimated number of cases of oral diseases worldwide exceeds the cases of all five main noncommunicable diseases (mental disorders, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, chronic respiratory diseases and cancers) combined by about 1 billion.2 Among major oral diseases, periodontal disease includes various inflammatory conditions affecting the tooth-supporting structures (gums, alveolar bone, and periodontal ligaments). It may lead to tooth loss and systemic inflammation, impacting over 1 billion people worldwide.3,4 As a country with nearly one-fifth of the world’s population, China experienced a more pronounced upward trend in the prevalence of severe periodontitis from 1990 to 2021, with its growth rate significantly higher than global data.5 Furthermore, national epidemiological surveys have reported that more than 80% of Chinese adults exhibit signs of periodontal disease, underscoring the substantial public health burden posed by periodontal conditions in China.6

Behavioural factors, particularly those related to self-management or self-care, are regarded as dominant factors influencing the progression of diseases.7,8 Self-management is particularly crucial for patients with chronic diseases, as only the patients themselves can be responsible for daily care throughout the long course of the disease.9 Behavioural interventions serve as the cornerstone of the prevention and control of periodontal disease. Mechanical plaque controls (eg, effective toothbrushing and flossing) have been designated as a core strategy for primary prevention.10 However, according to the Fourth National Oral Health Survey, less than 50% of Chinese adults brushed their teeth twice or more per day.11 Therefore, it is necessary to explore more effective strategies to enhance self-management of oral health behaviours.

An individual’s psychological state can significantly modulate health behaviors.12 Depression and anxiety, as common psychological issues, can impact health through multiple pathways: on one hand, they may reduce patients’ motivation for self-management (including self-efficacy), and on the other hand, they may directly exacerbate inflammation via neuroendocrine mechanisms.13,14 To better understand these interactions, the Information-Motivation-Behavioural Skills (IMB) model, which was proposed by Fisher et al in 1992 for HIV prevention and widely applied to chronic disease management, provides a theoretical framework.15,16 It suggests that health behaviours result from three core components: information (behaviour-related knowledge), motivation (personal attitudes and social support), and behavioural Skills (an individual’s objective ability and self-efficacy). Notably, the model identifies behavioural skills as the critical mediator that translates knowledge and motivation into actual action.17 In the field of oral health, previous research indicated that motivation levels can directly predict oral health outcomes, while intervention programs based on the IMB framework have successfully enhanced patient motivation and improved hygiene habits.18 Within this framework, emotional states such as depression and anxiety may influence the motivational component of health behaviour, while behavioural skills determine whether individuals are able to translate intentions into effective oral hygiene practices. Psychological state (eg, depression and anxiety) can also affect oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL), which is a crucial tool for understanding the impact of oral health disparities on overall health and well-being.19 For example, individuals with dental anxiety/fear might experience significantly impaired OHRQoL, exacerbating the impact of adverse dental symptoms, including pain.20,21

Despite substantial evidence demonstrating the association between depression, self-efficacy, and oral health behaviours, as well as OHRQoL, traditional regression modelling methods are predominantly employed, leaving methodological gaps that require addressing. Structural equation modelling (SEM) can model constructs as latent variables and decompose effects into direct and indirect effects. Based on the IMB framework, this study aimed to construct a comprehensive model incorporating multiple psychological factors, such as self-efficacy, depressive symptoms, and dental anxiety. We examined the effects of self-efficacy (via SEM), depressive feelings, and anxious emotions on oral health behaviours and outcomes.

Material and methods

Study population and data

This cross-sectional study targeted adults aged ≥ 18 years. A convenience sample of patients with periodontal disease was recruited from the School of Stomatology between August and September 2024. The study design and reporting process followed the STROBE guidelines (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) for observational research. Using sample size estimation methods for similar research, we calculated the required sample size by multiplying the number of independent variables by 5 to 10.22,23 With 32 variables in the general information questionnaire, the initial sample size was estimated at 160 to 320. Considering a 10% invalid questionnaire rate and other sample loss factors, the required sample size was estimated at 176-352. Ultimately, 366 participants were enrolled. The sample size requirement for SEM was also considered during study design. Methodological guidelines suggest that SEM analyses generally require at least 200 participants to obtain stable parameter estimates and reliable model fit indices.24,25 The sample size in the present study was consistent with these recommended thresholds, supporting the adequacy of the analysis. The inclusion criteria were as follows: clinical diagnosis of gingivitis (probing bleeding sites > 10%, PD ≤ 3 mm, and no attachment loss) or chronic periodontitis (PD ≥ 4 mm or with attachment loss), age ≥ 18 years, and provision of signed informed consent. The exclusion criteria were as follows: severe mental or cognitive impairments, hearing or speech disorders, severe dysfunction of major organ systems (eg, cardiac, cerebral), acute phase of illness, and illiteracy. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Stomatological Hospital of Peking University (No. PKUSSIRB-2024102155).

Measurements

The survey collected general information (including demographic characteristics such as sex and age) from the participants. Socioeconomic status (SES) included education level, parents’ education level, living area, and only-child status. Oral health-related behaviours, oral health status, OHRQoL, depression, anxiety, and the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES) were also evaluated. All these scales were assembled into one questionnaire and collected via mobile application software and online questionnaires.

Oral Health behaviours and oral health status

Five self-reported questions about oral health behaviours were used: frequency of toothbrushing, toothbrushing time, frequency of interdental cleaning, date of the last professional teeth cleaning, and regular oral examination. The answers regarding toothbrushing frequency were recorded as 'once a day or less', 'twice a day' and 'more than twice a day'; and the answers regarding interdental cleaning frequency were recorded as 'never', 'less than once a day', and 'once a day or more'. Principal component analysis was employed to reduce the dimensionality of the five oral health behaviour variables to construct a comprehensive behavioural score. This approach has been used to construct composite behavioural indicators in epidemiological research.26 Prior to analysis, the data met the criteria for suitability (KMO measure = 0.671, Bartlett’s test of sphericity P < .001), indicating that the data were appropriate for factor analysis.27 Principal component analysis was then performed using eigenvalues >1 as the extraction criterion, and a composite behavioural score was generated based on the extracted component scores. Higher scores indicated higher levels of oral hygiene behaviour. Oral health status was determined via self-report and gingival bleeding within the past three months, with higher scores indicating a worse oral health status. Gingival bleeding during the past three months was assessed using a self-reported questionnaire. Self-reported gingival bleeding has been shown to be associated with clinical indicators of gingival inflammation and is widely used as a proxy indicator of periodontal health in epidemiological studies.28 The self-reported oral health status is considered a proxy measure for oral health status.29,30

Oral health impact profile-14 (OHIP-14)

OHIP-14 includes 14 items and is assessed using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from never (0) to always (4). The overall OHIP-14 score was modelled as a continuous latent variable to measure the OHRQoL. Higher total OHIP-14 scores reflect a greater impact on OHRQoL. The Chinese version of OHIP-14 has shown good validation and reliability.31 Furthermore, recent methodological research evaluating OHRQoL instruments has confirmed that OHIP-14 remains one of the most widely used and psychometrically robust measures for assessing oral health-related quality of life in clinical and epidemiological studies.32

General self-efficacy scale (GSES)

Perceived self-efficacy was measured using GSES.33,34 Each item in GSES is scored from 1 (not at all true) to 4 (completely true). Cai-kang et al introduced the scale into China, and the reliability of the scale after the translation was 0.87.35 The summary score ranges from 10 to 40, with higher scores indicating higher self-efficacy.

Anxiety and depression measurement

Depression and dental anxiety were assessed using validated structural questionnaires: the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and the Modified Dental Anxiety Scale (MDAS). These two scales have been validated for their reliability and validity.36,37 PHQ-9 measures depression through nine items, such as: 'Over the past 2 weeks, how often have you been bothered by any of the following problems?' (1) Little interest or pleasure in doing things. (2) Feeling down, depressed, or hopeless. Each item is scored from 0 ('Not at all') to 3 ('Nearly every day'), with a maximum score of 27. PHQ-9 scores range from 0 to 27, with scores ≥10 indicating depressive symptoms. MDAS is filled in independently by respondents and uses a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not anxious and 5 = extremely anxious) to determine the level of dental anxiety. The total score ranges between 5 and 25.

Theoretical framework and model construction

This study categorized the research variables based on the theoretical assumptions of the IMB model. The 'Information' dimension was represented by oral health knowledge. For the 'Motivation' dimension, depression and dental anxiety were treated as emotional background variables that influence motivation, reflecting the emotional state during health-related decision-making. 'Behavioural Skills' were measured by general self-efficacy, representing an individual’s confidence in performing healthy behaviours. The 'Behaviour' outcome was expressed as a composite oral health behaviour score. Additionally, socioeconomic status (SES) was included as a distal background variable to control for sociodemographic factors.

Based on this framework, we hypothesized that: (1) Oral health knowledge and psychological states (depression/anxiety) would indirectly influence behaviour through self-efficacy; and (2) Self-efficacy would directly predict oral health behaviours and subsequent health outcomes. Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted. Continuous variables (such as age, GSES, PHQ-9 scores, MDAS scores, and OHIP-14 scores) were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical variables (sex, education level, registered area, ethnicity, toothbrushing frequency, etc.) are presented as percentages. An SEM was constructed using path analysis to predict self-efficacy and oral health behaviour and oral health-related quality of life in patients with periodontal disease (model inclusion criterion: a = 0.05; model exclusion criterion: a = 0.10). Model evaluation and refinement followed a theory-guided and iterative procedure. First, the overall model fit was assessed using multiple goodness-of-fit indices. Subsequently, modification indices (MIs), standardized regression coefficients, and theoretical plausibility were jointly considered to identify potential areas for improvement. Paths that were statistically non-significant and lacked theoretical support were gradually removed while preserving theoretically meaningful relationships. The final model demonstrated improved goodness-of-fit compared with the initial model. To assess the robustness of the model, both standardized and unstandardized parameter estimates were examined and multiple model fit indices (CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR) were used to evaluate model stability. The following criteria were used to evaluate the goodness of fit of the hypothetical model: χ2 /df < 3, root mean square error < 0.08, goodness of fit index > 0.90, incremental goodness of fit index > 0.90, and comparative goodness of fit index > 0.90. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and IBM SPSS Amos (version 28.0; IBM Corp.).

Results

Sample characteristics

This study enrolled 366 participants, with an average age of 39.7 years (SD = 13.1). The sex distribution was predominantly female (61.2%), and 90.4% held a bachelor’s degree or higher. More than 70% maintained a habit of daily brushing twice or more; however, more than one-third had never performed interproximal cleaning. Only 41.0% underwent regular dental check-ups, and 34.2% of the participants had received dental scaling treatment within the past year. Seventy-seven percent rated their oral health as 'good' or 'fairly good', while 66.7% reported gingival bleeding. Discussion

Our study explored the association between psychological factors, such as self-efficacy, depression, anxiety, and oral health behaviours, as well as OHRQoL, by SEM. The mean self-efficacy score in the study was 30.2, higher than that in previous studies (24.7).38 The SEM revealed that higher self-efficacy is associated with better oral health behaviour and oral health status, suggesting that confidence and motivation play beneficial roles in individuals’ compliance with oral health behaviours. This finding echoes existing evidence that self-efficacy has the strongest predictive power for oral health behaviors.39 However, there is no direct pathway from self-efficacy to OHRQoL, with only indirect association mediated through oral health status. In other studies, self-efficacy was directly associated with OHRQoL.40,41 The reasons for the discrepancy might be the differences in study design. One study did not incorporate oral health status and another did not analyse clinical oral health behaviours, which may have overlooked the pathway from self-efficacy to behaviour, to oral health status, and ultimately to OHRQoL. Our findings indicated that self-efficacy facilitated individuals in taking action and achieving objective oral health outcomes. However, people usually self-report that environmental, social, and psychological characteristics influence their oral health.42

Psychological factors, particularly depression, were significantly associated with OHRQoL, and the magnitude of the association (β = −0.291) was greater than that for SES (β = 0.098). A previous study suggests that in specific populations, psychological and behavioural indicators could surpass socioeconomic factors as health determinants.43 Our result highlighted that depression played a dominant role in assessing the burden of oral health. Meanwhile, anxiety also demonstrated an independent negative direct effect (β = −0.164), further confirming the association between negative emotions and individuals’ perceived oral health well-being. Negative emotional states (such as depression or anxiety) affect an individual’s attention to and severity assessment of somatic discomfort.44,45

Our study found that depression had no direct association with oral health behaviours, but only a weak indirect association through self-efficacy, revealing a critical psychological-cognitive-behavioural pathway: depression was significantly associated with individuals’ lower self-efficacy (β = −0.235), and the reduction of self-efficacy subsequently was negative associated with oral health behaviours and oral health status. Specifically, self-efficacy was directly linked to good oral health behaviours (β = 0.176, Bootstrap 95% CI, 0.011-0.247) and indirectly associated with oral health status through behavioural mediation. Ultimately, the improved oral health status was directly associated with better OHRQoL (β = 0.261). The findings of this study align with Bandura’s social cognitive theory of health behaviour, which emphasizes that self-efficacy is the core driving force for behavioural change.46,47 One study among young adults also found that oral self-care behaviours was indirectly associated with oral health through the partial mediating role of self-efficacy, which was consistent with our perspective.48 This psychological-cognitive-behavioural pathway suggested that depression might indirectly lead to poor oral health behaviour and associated with lower oral health by negatively associating with the individual’s belief in their own capabilities (self-efficacy), ultimately associating with lower quality of life.

Our study also revealed that SES was directly associated with oral health knowledge (β = 0.224), and indirectly linked to oral health status (β = −0.057) through oral health behaviours, and was further associated with OHRQoL both directly or indirectly. Consistent with previous research, SES was not significantly associated with self-efficacy in the model.49 This might be partly explained by the high education level of our sample (90.4% of participants hold a bachelors’ degree or higher), which likely influenced the observed associations. Individuals with higher SES often possess better oral health knowledge and face fewer financial barriers to care.49, 50, 51 This may reduce individual differences in oral health behaviours, thereby weakening the association between socioeconomic status and behavioural outcomes. In such groups, psychological factors like self-efficacy may emerge as the primary drivers of behaviour, allowing these cognitive pathways to be observed more clearly. However, these associations could differ in populations with less education. This might suggest that greater attention should be paid to lower SES populations when formulating policies aimed at improving public oral health behaviours

In our study, the association between oral health knowledge and oral health behaviours was weaker compared with other factors. The reasons for this are as follows: behaviours are determined by multiple factors, and knowledge may primarily serve as a mediator between SES and behavior.52 Similar mediation was shown in our study. A previous study demonstrated that SES influences caregivers’ oral health knowledge levels, which in turn affects their compliance in seeking oral health services for their children.53 Additionally, another study suggested that knowledge has a greater impact on attitudes than on behaviours. Therefore, depending solely on the impartation of oral health knowledge is likely to be insufficient.54 Within the IMB framework, knowledge is considered a necessary but insufficient condition for behaviour change, as its effect often operates through motivational components such as attitudes and perceived importance of health practices. However, attitudes were not directly measured in the present study and therefore were not modelled as a separate latent variable in the structural equation model. Future studies incorporating explicit assessments of attitudes may help further clarify the pathways linking knowledge, psychological factors, and oral health behaviours. Taken together, these findings suggest that interventions relying solely on the provision of oral health knowledge may have limited effectiveness. More comprehensive strategies that also address psychological factors and environmental support systems may be necessary to promote sustainable oral health behaviours.

Although our study revealed complex mechanisms between self-efficacy and oral health behaviours through SEM, some possible limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference, so future prospective studies should establish causal relationships. Second, our convenience sample, which only recruited participants from the School of Stomatology may exhibit higher health awareness and medical utilization levels; therefore, caution is warranted when generalizing the findings to the general population. Over 90% of participants had a college degree or higher, which was substantially higher than the general adult population in China, might influence certain behavioural variables and limit the generalizability of the study results. This educational homogeneity may have reduced variability in oral health behaviours, as participants with higher education often have greater health literacy and access to oral health information. As a result, the observed association between SES and oral health behaviours may be attenuated, and caution is needed when generalizing these findings to lower-SES or rural populations. Thus, further research should employ multiple strategies(such as multi-centre studies) to acquire more comprehensive samples. Third, our study focused specifically on patients diagnosed with periodontal disease rather than the general population to explore psychological mechanisms in individuals with actual treatment needs. While this limits generalizability, recruiting from a hospital of stomatology ensured that all participants met clinical diagnostic criteria and were facing real-world behavioural management challenges. However, we did not differentiate between gingivitis and periodontitis. Given that these two conditions differ in disease severity and potential impacts on OHRQoL, analysing them as a single group may have influenced the observed associations, future research should analyse them separately to provide more refined insights. Moreover, limitations in variable measurement were observed: SES was assessed solely through education level, parents’ education level, living area, ethnicity, and only-child status, excluding key indicators such as income and occupation. Although self-reported oral health status may reflect reality to some extent, it may be subject to recall bias and poor objectivity, future studies should further validate these findings by incorporating objective clinical examination indicators.

Conclusion

Our study suggested a central role of self-efficacy in oral health, as it was not only associated with oral health behaviour, but also appeared to function as a significant mediator linking psychological distress with these behaviours. Future interventions should prioritize enhancing self-efficacy and paying more attention to psychological factors (such as depression) to help improve oral health behaviours.
