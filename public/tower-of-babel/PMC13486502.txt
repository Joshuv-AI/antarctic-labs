Deep learning-based intelligent prediction of strata pressure in a steeply inclined fully mechanized longwall face
Authors: Zhuocheng Ding, Naizhong Xu, Chang Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReliable prediction of strata-pressure evolution is essential for intelligent longwall mining, but steeply inclined panels show spatially heterogeneous support loading that challenges short-term warning. Here we analyse two months of hydraulic-support data from Panel II1013 in the Huaibei mining area and develop a local prediction workflow for support-pressure states. Missing and zero values were repaired, random measurement noise was reduced using a one-dimensional Kalman filter, and normalized sliding-window samples were used to compare CNN, LSTM, CNN-LSTM, Transformer and CNN-LSTM-Attention models against persistence and BP neural network baselines. Data analysis revealed a persistent high-pressure concentration from the middle to upper face. Under matched data partitioning, CNN-LSTM-Attention achieved the lowest test RMSE and highest R2 (RMSE 0.8632 ± 0.0615; MAE 0.4233 ± 0.0902; MAPE 2.5194 ± 0.4429%; R2 0.9891 ± 0.0016), reducing RMSE by 1.80% relative to BP and 9.60% relative to persistence. In a held-out 1000-sample window, the model achieved a relative regression accuracy of 98.17% (1—MAPE). A bounded multi-step validation on four representative supports, using a 24-point input window (approximately 2 h), yielded valid forecast horizons of 5–10 h when both horizon-level and farthest-step MAPE were ≤ 10%. These results support CNN-LSTM-Attention as a local single-support prediction module for graded warning assistance. Introduction

With increasing coal mining depth and extraction intensity, strata pressure behavior in fully mechanized longwall faces under complex geological conditions has become increasingly severe. Roof weighting, uneven hydraulic-support loading, and local instability have become more prominent and directly affect safe and efficient mine production1–3. The classification of steeply inclined fully mechanized longwall faces varies slightly among different research and engineering contexts. From the perspective of conventional steeply inclined seam mining technology and overburden movement, Wu et al.4 defined the main applicable dip range of steeply inclined fully mechanized longwall faces as 35°–55°. This range emphasizes the pronounced influence of seam dip on roof movement, floor stability, and strata-pressure behavior. In the classification and graded evaluation of intelligent coal-mining working faces, Wang et al.5 classified working faces with seam inclinations greater than or equal to 25° as steeply inclined, reflecting a broader engineering classification criterion for intelligent mining systems. Therefore, the 25°–35° interval can be regarded as a transitional or boundary range in which the influence of seam dip on strata-pressure behavior becomes increasingly significant.

Compared with ordinary fully mechanized longwall faces, steeply inclined fully mechanized longwall faces are jointly affected by seam dip, gravitational components, roof structure, and mining disturbance. As a result, strata-pressure evolution in such faces exhibits stronger regional differentiation and spatiotemporal coupling. Traditional support methods that rely mainly on manual experience and post-event adjustment can no longer respond promptly to rapidly changing strata-pressure conditions. With the continuous development of intelligent coal mining, hydraulic supports are evolving from load-bearing equipment into integrated units for load bearing, sensing, decision making, and control. Therefore, investigating strata-pressure behavior in steeply inclined fully mechanized longwall faces and realizing intelligent prediction and proactive regulation are important for improving roof control and ensuring safe, efficient mining6–8.

In recent years, extensive research has been conducted in China and abroad on the analysis, prediction, and early warning of strata pressure in fully mechanized working faces. For strata-pressure prediction, He et al.9 used a BP neural network to predict periodic weighting and verified the feasibility of neural-network methods for time-series modeling of strata pressure. Zhao et al.10 introduced deep learning into strata-pressure prediction and early warning for large-mining-height working faces, improving model applicability in engineering practice. Li et al.11, Cui et al.12, and Li13 improved the accuracy and stability of prediction models through hyperparameter optimization, long-sequence prediction, and ensemble learning, respectively. Fu et al.14 further focused on spatiotemporal regional forecasting of weighting events. International studies have also explored improved Transformer structures15,16, pressure-hazard classification17, and prediction of first weighting and roof pressure18,19, further promoting data-driven strata-pressure prediction. Overall, deep learning has become an important direction in this field.

In summary, existing studies have achieved substantial progress in strata-pressure prediction for fully mechanized working faces. However, for the special operating conditions of steeply inclined fully mechanized faces, systematic comparative studies on the predictive performance of different deep learning models remain insufficient. Under steeply inclined conditions, hydraulic-support pressure series are jointly affected by seam dip, roof activity, and mining disturbance, and therefore show stronger nonlinearity, fluctuation, and temporal correlation. Existing studies mostly focus on the application of a single model, and the differences among models in local feature extraction, long-range dependency representation, and peak-segment fitting have not been fully clarified. Therefore, taking Panel II1013 in the Huaibei mining area as the research object, this study analyzes strata-pressure behavior, preprocesses the monitoring series, constructs prediction samples, and establishes CNN, LSTM, CNN-LSTM, Transformer, and CNN-LSTM-Attention prediction models. In addition, persistence prediction and BP neural network are introduced as simple and traditional baseline methods. Based on a unified dataset and evaluation metrics, the predictive performance of the deep learning models and baseline methods is systematically compared, and an optimal model suitable for strata-pressure prediction in steeply inclined fully mechanized faces is selected.

Engineering background and data sources

Engineering overview

The coal seam inclination of Panel II1013 in the Huaibei mining area ranges from 18° to 38°, with an average inclination of approximately 25.8°. Although the average inclination is lower than the conventional 35°–55° range generally used to describe typical steeply inclined coal seams, it exceeds the ≥ 25° criterion adopted in the classification of intelligent coal-mining working faces. Moreover, local sections of the panel reach 38°, entering the conventional steeply inclined range. Field monitoring also shows that the panel exhibits typical dip-related strata-pressure characteristics, including high-pressure concentration in the middle-to-upper part of the face, significant regional differences, and segmented transmission of periodic weighting. Therefore, Panel II1013 is more accurately defined in this study as a boundary-type steeply inclined fully mechanized longwall face. This definition is used to emphasize its transitional inclination condition and the corresponding nonuniform strata-pressure behavior, rather than to classify it as a typical steeply inclined panel with an average dip of 35°–55°. Monitoring system and data acquisition

Hydraulic support pressure sensors, roof-floor displacement sensors, and distributed optical fiber sensors were installed in Panel II1013 of the Huaibei mining area to form a strata pressure monitoring system. Six monitoring stations were arranged as follows: I lower station, supports 5–16; II middle-lower station, supports 25–36; III middle station, supports 45–56; IV middle-upper station, supports 65–76; V upper station, supports 85–96; and VI upper-end station, supports 105–118. Each support was equipped with one hydraulic-support pressure sensor to collect hydraulic-support pressure. Roof-floor displacement sensors were used to monitor roof and floor displacement, and distributed optical fibers were laid along the working face to achieve continuous high-precision monitoring. The monitoring-point layout is shown in Fig. 2.

Layout of strata pressure monitoring points in Panel II1013.

The data used in this study were obtained from two months of monitoring records of hydraulic supports at the II1013 longwall working face in a mine located in the Huaibei mining area. The sampling interval was 5 min. The working face consisted of 118 hydraulic supports, and the dataset contained a total of 2,071,136 records. To avoid ambiguity in terminology and units, the terms used in this study are clarified as follows. “Strata pressure” refers to the overall roof-loading and ground-pressure behavior of the working face induced by overburden movement and mining disturbance. “Hydraulic-support pressure” or “support pressure” refers to the hydraulic pressure measured by the pressure sensors installed on the hydraulic-support columns, and it is expressed in MPa. This MPa-based pressure series is the direct input and prediction target of the deep learning models. “Support resistance” and “working resistance” refer to the equivalent load-bearing force of the hydraulic support during mining and are expressed in kN per support. In this study, “working resistance” is used when emphasizing the in-service load of the support, whereas “support resistance” is used as a more general term for the load-bearing force of the hydraulic support. The MPa-based hydraulic-support pressure and the kN-based support resistance are related but not identical quantities. In general, support resistance can be calculated from the hydraulic pressure, the effective loaded area of the hydraulic cylinder system, and the number of loaded columns. Therefore, MPa is used in this study for hydraulic-support pressure-series modeling and warning-threshold calculation, whereas kN is used when discussing equivalent support resistance, working resistance, and support-load statistics.

Data preprocessing and sample construction

The original hydraulic-support pressure series contains a small number of missing values, zero values, and random fluctuations. Because zero values are mostly caused by short-term sensor failure, communication anomalies, or instantaneous missing measurements, they are difficult to interpret as true support-loading states. Therefore, missing and invalid values were first removed, and zero-value points were regarded as anomalous samples and repaired using linear interpolation combined with nearest endpoint values. Then, considering the random measurement noise and short-term fluctuations in the original monitoring series, a one-dimensional Kalman filter was used for noise reduction. Finally, Min–Max normalization was applied to both input and output data. The preprocessing flow and results are shown in Fig. 3. Specifically, Fig. 3a presents the overall preprocessing workflow, including missing/invalid-value removal, zero-value repair, Kalman filtering, and Min-Max normalization. Fig. 3b compares the repaired pressure series with the Kalman-filtered series, showing that the filtering process reduces random fluctuations while generally preserving the main variation trend and dominant pressure peaks.

Preprocessing of the single-support strata pressure data series.

The Kalman filter was implemented through a standard prediction-update recursion. In the prediction step, the state estimate and error covariance were predicted from the previous time step. In the update step, the Kalman gain was calculated according to the measurement residual, and the filtered hydraulic-support pressure value was obtained. In this study, the state transition coefficient and observation coefficient were both set to 1, the system input was set to 0, and the process-noise and measurement-noise covariance parameters were set to 0.021 and 0.052, respectively. Compared with direct moving-average smoothing, Kalman filtering can reduce random measurement noise while adaptively updating the pressure state according to the observation residual.

It should be noted that peaks, sudden drops, and severe fluctuations in the hydraulic-support pressure series are important indicators for field early warning. Therefore, the influence of Kalman filtering on warning-related peak information was further quantified. After zero-value repair, the repaired but unfiltered series was used as the reference series. Dominant local peaks higher than + 1.0σ were identified from this reference series, and the corresponding peak values after Kalman filtering were extracted within a ± 1-sample neighborhood. In addition, the influence of Kalman filtering on threshold-based early-warning classification was examined by comparing the warning levels before and after filtering under the same threshold criteria. The classification consistency reached 98.7441%, and only 56 samples, accounting for 1.2559% of the total samples, changed their warning level. Among the severe-warning samples, 8 samples were downgraded after filtering, while no sample was upgraded to the severe-warning level. These downgraded samples were located very close to the severe-warning threshold; their raw value was only 0.0144 MPa higher than the threshold. Therefore, the warning-level changes mainly occurred near the boundaries between adjacent warning levels. Overall, the Kalman filtering procedure reduced random fluctuations and improved the continuity of the series while causing limited influence on dominant peak identification and warning-level classification.

For the single-support univariate prediction task, the pressure series of each selected target hydraulic support was extracted separately from the full monitoring dataset for model construction and comparative analysis. The full dataset contained monitoring records from 118 hydraulic supports, and each support had 17,552 time steps during the two-month monitoring period. During sample construction, a sliding window with a length of 15 was used to organize the input features, and the hydraulic-support pressure value at the next time step after the window was used as the prediction label. Thus, each single-support series yielded 17,537 supervised learning samples. For each selected target support, the samples were divided chronologically into training, validation, and test sets. The overall training portion accounted for 80%, and 10% of that portion was further used as the validation set, resulting in 12,626 training samples, 1,403 validation samples, and 3,508 test samples. The 15-point input window corresponds to the most recent 75 min of pressure evolution under the 5-min sampling interval. This window length was adopted in the main one-step comparative experiment to balance short-term dependency representation and training stability. A shorter input window may fail to capture pressure accumulation, peak-transition segments, and recovery stages before or after local pressure fluctuations, whereas an excessively long window may increase the input dimension and model complexity without necessarily improving the standardized one-step comparison. Therefore, the 15-point window was used as a unified setting for comparing CNN, LSTM, CNN-LSTM, Transformer, CNN-LSTM-Attention, BP neural network, and persistence prediction. It should be emphasized that this study focuses mainly on a short-term single-step prediction task under single-support and univariate conditions. The purpose is to first verify the representation capability of different models for local fluctuations, peak segments, and transition features in hydraulic-support pressure time series while keeping the problem complexity controlled. One-step prediction mainly evaluates short-term pressure-state tracking ability and provides a 5-min prediction lead time under the present sampling interval. To further examine engineering applicability, a supplementary multi-step prediction experiment was added in Section "Graded early-warning criteria and control logic". In this supplementary experiment, the historical input window was extended to 24 points, corresponding to approximately 2 h, because longer-horizon prediction requires a broader recent pressure-evolution context. The main one-step experiment provides a unified benchmark for comparing different model structures, whereas the supplementary multi-step experiment further evaluates the potential warning lead time for engineering application. Therefore, the prediction task in this study should be understood as a local temporal prediction module for a single support rather than a complete spatiotemporal warning model for the whole working face. In practical applications, this module needs to be combined with multi-support monitoring, regional zoning, and cross-support propagation analysis to form a complete graded early-warning system.

Construction and comparative analysis of prediction models

Deep learning model design

At present, methods for predicting and forecasting strata pressure in working faces can generally be divided into three categories. The first category comprises nondeterministic mathematical methods based on statistics, fuzzy mathematics, and related approaches20. These methods usually rely on statistical analysis of large amounts of historical data and uncertainty modeling. They can provide reasonable predictions when information is incomplete or the underlying pattern is unclear, but their accuracy is readily affected by sample quantity and quality. The second category comprises deterministic methods based on strata-pressure theory. By constructing roof-structure models, these methods investigate mechanical mechanisms and evolution patterns in depth and thereby predict strata pressure21,22. They are logically rigorous and can reflect certain physical mechanisms, but the complexity and variability of geological conditions often lead to idealized limitations. The third category relies on large-scale monitoring data and combines computer technology for systematic analysis and intelligent prediction23. Typical methods include artificial neural networks, machine learning, and other intelligent algorithms. These methods have advantages in processing large data volumes and recognizing complex patterns, and can achieve high-accuracy prediction in dynamic environments, but they also impose higher requirements on real-time data acquisition and algorithm training.

Considering the significant nonlinearity and temporal dependence of hydraulic-support pressure series, as well as the large scale of field monitoring data, this study adopts the third category of methods and proposes a deep learning hybrid model that integrates long short-term memory (LSTM) and convolutional neural network (CNN) to improve strata pressure prediction accuracy in fully mechanized working faces. An attention mechanism is further introduced to enhance the representation of key temporal segments and to improve the model’s ability to capture local fluctuations, peak segments, and transition stages in the single-support pressure series. In this way, a collaborative prediction framework is developed in which CNN extracts local features, LSTM models temporal dependence, and the attention mechanism highlights key time steps.

CNN model

As one of the most important deep learning models, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have strong feature-extraction and generalization capabilities. Through local receptive fields and weight sharing, CNNs can automatically identify short-term patterns in time series, such as local fluctuations, plateau segments, and sudden-drop segments24. For hydraulic-support pressure data, one-dimensional convolution can effectively capture these short-term patterns and provide more discriminative feature representations for subsequent prediction.

The CNN model in this study first consists of convolutional layers, normalization, and ReLU activation, followed by global average pooling and a fully connected layer to output the predicted value. This structure has a small number of parameters and stable training performance, and therefore serves as an important baseline for evaluating the gains of hybrid models. LSTM model

Long short-term memory (LSTM) networks control information flow through input, forget, and output gates, and can alleviate the gradient-vanishing problem that conventional recurrent networks often encounter in long-sequence learning25,26. Hydraulic-support pressure series show clear temporal correlation, and the pressure level at the current moment is closely related to the mining rhythm, roof activity, and weighting process in the preceding period. Previous studies have shown that, compared with traditional BP neural networks, LSTM can better capture long-range dependency features in hydraulic-support pressure time-series prediction and significantly reduce error indices such as RMSE and MAE. Therefore, LSTM is suitable for describing long-term dependencies in hydraulic-support pressure variation.

Attention mechanism

The core idea of the attention mechanism is to assign different weights to different time steps, enabling the model to automatically focus on input segments that are more critical to the current prediction27. For strata-pressure prediction, several key points before and after rapid weighting, local abnormal fluctuation segments, and plateau transition points often contain higher information density. If the model can actively emphasize these segments, the overall prediction performance can usually be improved.

In the optimal model developed in this study, a self-attention layer is introduced after the LSTM sequence output to reconstruct temporal dependencies through learnable weights, and the final result is then output through a fully connected layer. This design preserves temporal information, enhances the representation of key time steps, and performs weighted aggregation of hidden states at different time steps, thereby constructing a physically meaningful importance distribution for the strata pressure time series. CNN-LSTM-attention model for strata pressure prediction

Combining the advantages of the CNN-LSTM model, this study integrates an attention mechanism with CNN-LSTM. The model weights the features of each input time step according to the attention weights, thereby assigning different levels of importance to different time-step features. In the hybrid model, the input is a strata pressure time series with a length of 15. The model first extracts local pattern features through two one-dimensional convolutional layers with 32 and 64 kernels, respectively. It then uses an LSTM layer with 96 units to learn temporal dependencies, introduces a self-attention layer to weight key time steps, and finally predicts the strata pressure value at the next time step through a fully connected layer and a regression output layer. The convolutional layers capture local fluctuations and sudden-drop features, the LSTM models long-term dependencies along the temporal dimension, and the attention layer further highlights the time steps that have greater influence on the current prediction. From the perspective of structural function, the convolutional layers capture local fluctuations and sudden drops in the strata pressure curve, the LSTM learns temporal dependencies during the mining process, and the attention layer further emphasizes the time steps that contribute more strongly to the current prediction. This framework integrates local perception, long-term memory, and key-feature focusing, and is suitable for short-term prediction of univariate hydraulic-support pressure series.

To ensure comparability among the comparative experiments, all compared methods used the same preprocessed data, input window, test set, and evaluation metrics; all trainable models used the same chronological training-validation-test split. All trainable models were implemented in MATLAB R2024a using the Deep Learning Toolbox and trained with the trainNetwork function. The mini-batch size was set to 256, the L2 regularization coefficient was set to 1 × 10−4, the gradient clipping threshold was set to 1, and the training samples were shuffled at every epoch. For CNN, LSTM, CNN-LSTM, Transformer, CNN-LSTM-Attention, and BP neural network, the initial learning rates were 1 × 10−3, 1 × 10−3, 1 × 10−3, 5 × 10−4, 5 × 10−4, and 1 × 10−3, respectively; the maximum numbers of epochs were 100, 100, 100, 140, 180, and 100, respectively; the dropout rates were no dropout, 0.10, 0.10, 0.05, 0.05, and no dropout, respectively; and the numbers of trainable parameters were 2,273, 19,009, 28,673, 36,673, 110,273, and 545, respectively. Early stopping was based on validation loss, with a patience of 10 validation checks for CNN, LSTM, CNN-LSTM, and BP neural network, and 12 validation checks for Transformer and CNN-LSTM-Attention. The persistence prediction baseline was parameter-free and did not require training. All experiments were conducted on an ordinary personal computer without special hardware acceleration.

It should be clarified that MAE was used only as an evaluation metric and was not used as the training loss function. In the MATLAB implementation, the regression layer corresponds to MSE loss. MSE was adopted as the common training objective because it imposes a quadratic penalty on large prediction errors and is therefore more sensitive to large deviations during local peak, abrupt-drop, and plateau-transition stages than MAE loss. This is also consistent with the RMSE-based ranking used in this study, in which larger deviations during pressure-sensitive segments are given higher importance. Weighted or asymmetric loss functions may further emphasize high-pressure or underprediction samples, but they require additional engineering cost definitions, such as pressure-state thresholds and different penalty weights for different warning levels. To keep the model comparison focused on the effect of network structure and to avoid introducing additional empirical weighting parameters, all trainable neural-network models in this study were trained using the same standard MSE loss.

For the Transformer baseline, a lightweight Transformer encoder structure was used rather than a time-series-optimized variant such as Informer or Autoformer. The input sequence length was 15 sampling points, and the prediction horizon was one step. The input sequence was first mapped to a 64-dimensional embedding space, followed by a positional embedding layer to retain temporal-order information. The encoder block consisted of a 4-head self-attention layer with a key dimension of 64, residual connections, layer normalization, and a feed-forward network with 128 hidden units. The output was obtained through global average pooling, a fully connected layer with 32 units, and a final regression layer. To further evaluate whether the deep learning models provide meaningful improvement over simpler alternatives, two additional baseline methods were introduced: persistence prediction and a BP neural network. The persistence model is a parameter-free naive forecasting method, in which the pressure value at the next time step is directly predicted as the last value in the input window. The BP neural network was used as a representative traditional shallow-learning model, because BP-based methods have been widely used in early strata-pressure prediction studies. For the BP baseline, the input was the same 15-point sliding window used in the deep learning models, and the output was the support pressure at the next time step. The BP model consisted of one hidden layer with 32 neurons and one regression output neuron.

For stochastic models, repeated testing was performed with random seeds 42, 2024, and 3407, and the mean ± standard deviation of the test-set metrics was reported. For the deterministic persistence baseline, the test-set metrics were calculated directly, and the standard deviation was recorded as 0 for consistency in Table 2. To further examine the uncertainty of model comparison, an exploratory statistical analysis was conducted using the RMSE values obtained from the three matched random seeds. Because the same random seeds and the same chronological data partition were used for the stochastic models, two-sided paired t-tests were performed between model pairs, with a significance level of 0.05. It should be noted that the number of repeated runs was limited to three; therefore, the statistical power of the tests was relatively low, and the results were used only as supplementary evidence for interpreting the repeated-test results. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was also considered as a non-parametric alternative, but it was not used as the main statistical basis because of the very small number of paired observations. Comparative results and discussion

As shown in Table 2, after adding the persistence prediction and BP neural-network baselines, CNN-LSTM-Attention still achieved the lowest mean RMSE among all compared methods. Its mean RMSE was 0.8632, which was 1.80% lower than that of the BP neural network and 9.60% lower than that of persistence prediction. For the deep learning model pairs, exploratory paired t-tests on RMSE across the three matched random seeds showed that CNN-LSTM-Attention had a statistically significant RMSE reduction over the lightweight Transformer baseline (p = 0.0064), whereas its difference from CNN-LSTM was not statistically significant (p = 0.7125). The differences between CNN-LSTM-Attention and CNN or LSTM were also not significant at the 0.05 level. Therefore, CNN-LSTM-Attention is interpreted as the best overall model under the present dataset and repeated-test setting, rather than as a model with statistically significant superiority over all alternative models. This result suggests that the proposed hybrid model has a descriptive advantage in controlling larger prediction deviations, which is important for peak-sensitive strata-pressure warning. It should also be noted that the BP neural network and persistence baseline achieved competitive MAE and MAPE values. This is because the present task is a short-term one-step prediction problem, and adjacent support-resistance samples have strong local continuity in stable periods. In such intervals, simply using the last observed value or fitting the short input window with a shallow BP network can produce small average errors. However, RMSE is more sensitive to larger deviations near local peaks, abrupt drops, and plateau-transition stages. Therefore, the added baselines show that simple methods are strong references for one-step prediction, while CNN-LSTM-Attention provides its main advantage in reducing peak-sensitive errors and improving tracking stability in dynamically changing pressure segments. Nevertheless, the present comparison was conducted under a unified standard MSE loss. Further improvement may be achieved by designing peak-weighted or asymmetric loss functions that assign larger penalties to high-pressure samples or underprediction errors during weighting events. Such loss functions will be investigated in future work together with field-calibrated warning thresholds and engineering risk costs.

The lower performance of the Transformer baseline may be related to the characteristics of the present prediction task. Although Transformer-based models are effective in many large-scale and long-sequence time-series forecasting problems, the present study used a single-support, univariate, short-window, single-step prediction task with an input length of only 15 sampling points. Under this setting, the lightweight Transformer encoder did not fully exploit the advantages of global self-attention and was more sensitive to sequence length, parameter settings, and training stability. In contrast, the CNN-LSTM-Attention model combines convolutional local feature extraction, LSTM-based temporal memory, and attention-based weighting of key time steps, which is more suitable for capturing local peaks, abrupt drops, and plateau-transition segments in the hydraulic-support resistance series. Therefore, the poorer Transformer result should be interpreted as the performance of the specific lightweight Transformer baseline under the present data scale and input-window setting, rather than as a general conclusion about all Transformer-based time-series models.

Figure 11 shows that all compared methods can learn or follow the overall trend of strata-pressure variation to different degrees. However, CNN, LSTM, Transformer, BP neural network, and persistence prediction still exhibit deviations near local peaks and rapid transitions. CNN-LSTM produces a generally smoother curve, but it slightly underestimates several plateau-transition intervals. By contrast, the prediction curve of CNN-LSTM-Attention has a higher degree of agreement with the measured values and maintains better synchronization in peak segments, plateau-transition segments, and recovery stages after short-term drops. Graded early-warning and support-optimization assistance strategy

Graded early-warning criteria and control logic

To further examine the potential application of the CNN-LSTM-Attention model as a single-support short-term pressure prediction module, a continuous window of 1000 sample points from the held-out test set was selected as a representative typical prediction window. This window covers multiple early-warning states and shows good fitting performance. Within this 1000-sample held-out test window, the RMSE, MAE, MAPE, and R2 values of the CNN-LSTM-Attention model were 0.6346, 0.3016, 1.8290%, and 0.9941, respectively. The relative prediction accuracy calculated as 1—MAPE was 98.17%. This value represents continuous-value regression prediction accuracy only and is not used as warning-level classification accuracy. The mean and standard deviation of the predicted series were 24.27 MPa and 8.15 MPa, respectively, which are highly close to those of the measured series, 24.23 MPa and 8.26 MPa. This indicates that the predicted data can accurately reproduce the fluctuation amplitude and staged peak values of hydraulic-support pressure. The prediction comparison is shown in Fig. 13.

Comparison between predicted and measured results.

To avoid deriving warning thresholds and evaluating warning performance on the same test window, the revised analysis calculated the mean value and standard deviation σ only from the training-set label sequence. The graded thresholds + 0.5σ, + 1.0σ, and + 1.5σ were then fixed and applied to the representative 1000-sample window selected from the held-out test set. The training-set mean and standard deviation were 27.94 MPa and 9.86 MPa, respectively, giving warning thresholds of 32.87 MPa, 37.80 MPa, and 42.73 MPa. Therefore, the threshold definition and threshold evaluation were performed on independent data subsets.

To further address the limitation of one-step prediction and evaluate the potential warning lead time for on-site intervention, a bounded supplementary multi-step prediction experiment was conducted using the CNN-LSTM-Attention structure. Four representative supports located at different positions of the working face, namely 15#, 48#, 66#, and 106#, were selected. A 24-point historical input window, corresponding to approximately 2 h under the 5-min sampling interval, was used to directly predict future pressure sequences. For each support, the forecast horizon was gradually extended. A forecast horizon was regarded as valid only when both the horizon-aggregated MAPE and the farthest-step MAPE were not greater than 10%. The longest valid forecast horizon was therefore used to estimate the potential warning lead time under the present validation setting, because both the overall prediction error within the horizon and the error at the farthest forecast step satisfied the predefined 10% MAPE criterion.

As shown in Table 3, the longest valid forecast horizons for the representative supports ranged from 5 to 10 h under the strict 10% MAPE criterion. These results indicate that the CNN-LSTM-Attention structure has potential to extend the forecast horizon available for local pressure-state warning assistance beyond the 5-min one-step prediction interval. However, each support was modeled separately in this supplementary experiment. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as supplementary evidence for multi-step local pressure-state prediction on representative supports, rather than proof of whole-face multi-support spatiotemporal modeling or closed-loop support control.

As shown in Fig. 14, to make the validation of the warning criteria more field-oriented, the predicted warning levels were compared with reference warning levels derived from the measured hydraulic-support pressure records under the same fixed training-set-derived thresholds. A false alarm was defined as a predicted warning level higher than the measured warning level, whereas a missed alarm was defined as a predicted warning level lower than the measured warning level. In the held-out 1000-sample test window, the measured sequence contained 847 no-weighting samples, 143 possible-weighting samples, 10 slight-weighting samples, and no severe-weighting samples, while the predicted sequence contained 851, 143, 6, and 0 samples, respectively. The warning-level agreement reached 99.20%, with a false alarm rate of 0.00% and a missed alarm rate of 0.80%. These results indicate that the proposed criteria can identify local high-pressure disturbance in the measured field series. However, because no severe-weighting samples occurred in this test window, the severe-warning level still requires further validation using independent abnormal-event records.

It should be emphasized that the above graded warning criteria are established for the predicted pressure state of a single support. They should not be interpreted as a complete independent warning system for the whole working face. As shown by the field measurements in Section "Strata pressure behavior in the steeply inclined fully mechanized longwall face", periodic weighting in Panel II1013 exhibits obvious regional concentration and segmented transmission characteristics. Therefore, in practical application, the single-support prediction result should be combined with the spatial position of the support and the pressure states of adjacent supports.

Accordingly, a regional collaborative warning logic is proposed. First, the single-support prediction model is applied in a rolling manner to the monitoring supports in different zones of the working face. Second, the warning level of each support is mapped to its corresponding zone, including the lower, middle-lower, middle, middle-upper, upper, and upper-end zones. Third, when a support is classified as possible-weighting, slight-weighting, or severe-weighting, the warning state of its adjacent supports and neighboring zones should be checked simultaneously. In particular, because the field observations show that the middle-to-upper part is the main high-pressure concentration region and that weighting can propagate from the middle or middle-upper part to adjacent zones, a possible-weighting or higher-level warning in the middle or middle-upper zone should trigger intensified monitoring of the neighboring middle-lower, upper, and upper-end zones. In this way, the current single-support model is used as a local prediction unit, while the regional warning decision is made by combining local prediction results with spatial propagation information.

Conclusions

Strata pressure behavior in Panel II1013 of the Huaibei mining area shows pronounced spatiotemporal heterogeneity and segmented transmission characteristics. The monitoring results show that the periodic weighting interval of the working face ranges from 11.89 to 17.45 m. The middle-to-upper part continuously acts as a high-pressure concentration zone during multiple monitoring stages, and the proportion of time-weighted working resistance greater than 6400 kN per support increases from 48.91% to 64.02%. These results indicate that strata pressure evolution under steeply inclined conditions has significant regional differences and staged transmission patterns, and that the middle-to-upper part should be regarded as the key region for strata pressure monitoring, early warning, and dynamic regulation of support parameters.
