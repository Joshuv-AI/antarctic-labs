Clinical and Social Determinants of Treatment Adherence in Cancer Patients: The Contributory Role of Hearing Loss
Authors: Bilgeşah Kılıçtaş, Ahmet Ufuk Kılıçtaş, Rukiye Özçelik Erdem, Ahmet Oruç, Murat Araz, Melek Karakurt Eryılmaz, Mehmet Zahid Kocak, Hamdi Arbağ, Mehmet Artaç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTreatment adherence is a key determinant of outcomes in cancer patients and is influenced by multiple clinical and social factors. Hearing loss (HL) may contribute to communication barriers and treatment disruption. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between HL and treatment adherence.Materials and MethodsIn this single‐center prospective observational study, 79 newly diagnosed cancer patients undergoing systemic therapy were evaluated with baseline pure‐tone audiometry. HL was categorized by severity. Treatment adherence was assessed based on missed cycles and treatment continuity. Clinical variables, including treatment‐related adverse events and caregiver presence, were analyzed using chi‐square tests and logistic regression analyses.ResultsHL was present in 67.1% of patients. Overall, 55.7% missed at least one treatment cycle. Missed cycles were more frequent in patients with HL (p = 0.016), and higher HL severity was associated with increased treatment disruption (p = 0.015). In univariate analysis, HL, older age, absence of a caregiver, and treatment‐related adverse events were associated with nonadherence. In multivariate analysis, only treatment‐related adverse events remained independently significant (OR = 93.2; p < 0.001).ConclusionTreatment adherence appears to be primarily driven by clinical factors, particularly treatment‐related toxicity. HL was associated with treatment disruption but was not an independent predictor and may act as a contributing factor.Level of Evidence3.Keywords: cancer, hearing disorders, hearing loss, patient adherence, quality of life, side effect

Introduction

As defined by the World Health Organization (WHO), hearing loss is an increasingly prevalent public health problem affecting more than 5% of the global population and encompassing a substantial proportion of individuals aged 65 years and older. Age‐related hearing loss not only leads to elevated hearing thresholds but is also associated with difficulties in speech discrimination, central auditory processing disorders, and loss of social communication. These auditory changes are accompanied by psychological and functional impairments, resulting in a significant reduction in individuals' levels of independence, mobility, and interaction with society [1].

Hearing loss becomes clinically more critical for patients with cancer. Treatment‐related ototoxicity, particularly with cisplatin‐based therapies, is frequently observed and may lead to progressive hearing loss beginning at high frequencies [2]. Recent evidence has highlighted the substantial global burden of platinum‐based chemotherapy‐associated ototoxicity, with a pooled prevalence of hearing loss exceeding 40% in affected patients [3].

Treatment adherence is a key determinant of clinical outcomes in oncology and is influenced by multiple interacting factors, including treatment‐related toxicity, patient‐related characteristics, disease severity, and social support systems.

In this context, nonadherence to cancer therapy has been associated with reduced survival, recurrence, and treatment failure; conversely, adherence to treatment has been emphasized as an essential component for achieving cure or improving quality of life. Discontinuation, delay, or omission of cancer treatments not only reduces clinical success but also increases healthcare costs [4].

One of the effects of hearing loss in oncology clinical practice that is often overlooked but increasingly recognized is its potential role in treatment adherence. It has been reported that patient adherence to cancer treatment is of critical importance for clinical outcomes. Nevertheless, the impact of sensory impairments such as hearing loss on treatment adherence has been addressed only to a limited extent in the literature. In a study by Lee et al., it was demonstrated that patients receiving cisplatin‐based therapy participated in follow‐up audiological assessments at low rates, and that advanced‐stage cancer and lack of social support significantly reduced adherence [5].

The effects of hearing loss are not limited to the sensory level. The sensory deprivation model suggests that a reduction in auditory input increases cognitive load and that cognitive performance may deteriorate over time [1]. In parallel, hearing loss has been shown to negatively affect quality of life through social isolation, depression, and communication disorders. These psychosocial burdens may indirectly hinder participation in treatment processes by complicating interactions with the healthcare system [6].

Cancer patients represent a clinically vulnerable population in whom multiple factors may simultaneously influence treatment adherence. Within this multifactorial framework, hearing loss may represent a potentially under‐recognized and modifiable contributor to treatment disruption rather than a sole determinant.

In light of this information, it is evident that hearing loss in patients with cancer is not merely a clinical adverse effect but a factor that may interact with other clinical and social determinants to influence the treatment process and its continuity. Nevertheless, the number of studies that have quantitatively evaluated the relationship between hearing loss and treatment adherence is extremely limited, and data specifically analyzing the impact of different degrees of hearing loss on adherence are lacking in the literature.

Recent evidence published within the last 5 years highlights that treatment‐related ototoxicity, particularly platinum‐induced hearing loss, remains a highly prevalent complication in oncology patients and may negatively affect quality of life [7, 8].

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate treatment adherence in cancer patients and to explore the potential contribution of hearing loss within a broader clinical and social context.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study is a single‐center, prospective, observational cohort study conducted to evaluate the relationship between hearing loss and adherence to oncological treatment in patients with cancer. Within the scope of the study, hearing status was assessed prior to treatment in newly diagnosed cancer patients for whom systemic oncological therapy was planned, and patients were prospectively followed throughout the treatment process. The study was carried out within the Department of Medical Oncology of the Faculty of Medicine, and approval was obtained from the local ethics committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment in the study.

Given the exploratory nature of the study and the limited sample size, the analysis was primarily designed to identify potential associations rather than to establish causal relationships.

Patient Population

A total of 79 newly diagnosed cancer patients for whom systemic oncological treatment was planned at the Department of Oncology between January 2025 and November 2025 were included in the study. All enrolled patients underwent hearing assessment prior to treatment and were prospectively followed after the initiation of systemic therapy. Patients' demographic characteristics, cancer types at diagnosis, administered treatment modalities, and treatment frequencies were recorded. All eligible consecutive patients within the study period were included.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: newly diagnosed patients for whom systemic oncological treatment was planned, patients who were able to undergo audiometric evaluation prior to treatment, and patients with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–2.

The exclusion criteria included patients with a previously known central neurological disease, those with additional sensory impairments that could affect hearing assessment, and patients whose treatment could not be initiated or who were lost to follow‐up during the study period. Patients who were unable to complete baseline evaluation or were lost to follow‐up before treatment initiation were excluded, which may have introduced selection bias.

Hearing Assessment

All patients underwent a baseline hearing assessment prior to the initiation of oncological treatment. To ensure inter‐test consistency and eliminate measurement variability, all audiometric evaluations were performed by the same audiologist using the same calibrated audiometric equipment in a standard sound‐attenuated booth. Pure‐tone thresholds were evaluated at 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 kHz, and pure‐tone average (PTA) values were calculated as the arithmetic mean of these frequencies. Based on the audiometric results, hearing loss was classified according to the WHO criteria (mild, moderate, moderately severe, severe, and profound) based on PTA thresholds. According to this classification, the degrees of hearing loss were defined as mild (20–34 dB HL), moderate (35–49 dB HL), moderately severe (50–64 dB HL), severe (65–79 dB HL), and profound (≥ 80 dB HL) [9]. For analytical purposes, these categories were regrouped into three broader categories: mild (including WHO mild and moderate hearing loss), moderate (corresponding to WHO moderately severe hearing loss), and severe (including WHO severe and profound hearing loss).

Speech discrimination testing and functional hearing assessments were not routinely performed in this study. Patients using hearing aids were excluded from the study to avoid potential confounding effects related to hearing rehabilitation.

In cases of asymmetric hearing loss, classification was based on the better‐hearing ear.

Treatment and Assessment of Treatment Adherence

Systemic oncological treatments, including chemotherapy, chemoradiotherapy, targeted therapies, or CDK4/6 inhibitors, were administered according to tumor diagnosis and stage. Treatment frequencies were categorized as weekly, biweekly, every 3 weeks, or every 4 weeks.

Treatment adherence was evaluated based on the timely administration of planned treatment cycles and the occurrence of missed treatments during the course of therapy. A missed treatment was defined as a delay or complete omission of a planned cycle. The ratio of the number of missed cycles to the total number of administered cycles was calculated.

Reasons for Treatment Nonattendance

Prospectively during follow‐up, the reasons for treatment nonattendance or treatment interruptions were recorded in accordance with predefined categories. These data were collected through clinical interviews and review of patient records. Reasons for treatment nonattendance were classified as hearing‐related problems, difficulties in communication with the healthcare team, lack of motivation, treatment‐related adverse events, and transportation issues. In cases of treatment nonattendance due to adverse events, the causes were further subcategorized as neutropenia, infection, nutritional problems, and allergic reactions.

Caregiver Status

The presence of a caregiver during the treatment process was recorded for all patients, and the effect of caregiver status on the relationship between hearing loss and treatment adherence was evaluated through subgroup analyses.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint of the study was the comparison of treatment adherence rates between patients with and without hearing loss.

Secondary endpoints included treatment nonadherence rates, reasons for missed treatments, the effect of caregiver presence, the relationship between the degree of hearing loss and treatment adherence, and exploration of factors associated with treatment nonadherence.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software. Continuous variables were presented as median (IQR) or mean ± standard deviation. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. Chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests were used for comparisons between groups.

To explore factors associated with treatment nonadherence, binary logistic regression analyses were performed. Treatment nonadherence was defined as missing at least one planned treatment cycle. Variables with clinical relevance and/or statistical significance in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. Given the relatively small number of events, the regression analyses were considered exploratory, and the results were interpreted with caution.

The relationship between the degree of hearing loss and treatment adherence was evaluated using subgroup analyses. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 79 patients were included in the study. The median age was 61 years (IQR: 55–67), with 53.2% of patients being female and 46.8% male. The most common malignancy types were breast (17.7%), gastric (16.5%), colorectal (15.2%), and lung (13.9%) cancers. The majority of patients received chemotherapy (82.3%), and the most common treatment administration interval was every three weeks (36.7%) (Table 1).

Discussion

In this study, treatment adherence in cancer patients was found to be influenced by multiple clinical and social factors. Although hearing loss was associated with higher rates of missed treatment cycles in univariate analyses, it did not remain an independent predictor in multivariate models. In contrast, treatment‐related adverse events emerged as the strongest factor associated with treatment nonadherence.

Hearing loss may influence treatment adherence through several indirect mechanisms. Patients with hearing impairment may experience communication difficulties during clinical encounters, leading to misunderstandings regarding treatment schedules or medical instructions. In addition, hearing loss is associated with social isolation and reduced interaction with healthcare providers, which may further compromise adherence. In the present study, communication‐related nonattendance was more common among patients with hearing loss, supporting the potential role of auditory impairment in treatment disruption.

This study contributes to the limited body of literature examining the role of hearing loss in treatment adherence in patients receiving cancer therapy. Adults with hearing loss have been shown to experience social isolation, depression, communication difficulties, and a significant decline in quality of life [6, 10, 11]. In our study, the median age of 61 years supports that our patient population represents a group at risk, and that age‐related sensory disadvantages may be reflected in the treatment process. Moreover, existing evidence indicates that hearing loss is not merely a sensory impairment but also constitutes a psychosocial burden that shapes patients' daily lives and their interactions with the healthcare system.

Treatment‐related ototoxicity may render this burden more pronounced in the context of cancer. It has been reported that sensorineural hearing loss beginning at high frequencies may develop following cisplatin‐based chemotherapy, and that this rate may range between 17% and 88% in populations receiving chemoradiotherapy [2, 12]. These findings suggest that cancer treatments may increase sensory impairments, thereby making patients more vulnerable in terms of treatment adherence.

The clinical importance of treatment adherence is well established. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) (2003), treatment adherence is defined as “the extent to which a person's behavior—taking medication, following a diet, and/or making lifestyle changes—corresponds with agreed recommendations from a healthcare provider.” Adherence is a multidimensional phenomenon and, according to the WHO, is influenced by patient‐related factors, treatment‐related factors, condition‐related factors, health system–related factors, and socioeconomic factors [13].

Puts et al. reported that nonadherence to cancer treatments is associated with reduced survival, treatment failure, and higher recurrence rates [4]. This study demonstrates that adherence is a determinant not only of clinical efficacy but also of outcomes at the system level.

In the literature, the impact of hearing loss on treatment adherence has been evaluated only to a limited extent. Lee et al. reported that audiological follow‐up rates declined rapidly during the post‐treatment period among patients receiving cisplatin‐based therapy for head and neck cancer, and that recommended follow‐up appointments were frequently missed. In the same study, socioeconomic factors such as lack of insurance were shown to be important determinants of loss to follow‐up [5]. When considered together, these findings suggest that the clinical burden of hearing loss and the sensory adverse effects of cancer treatments may contribute to difficulties in maintaining treatment continuity.

Our multivariate analysis findings indicate that hearing loss is not an independent determinant of treatment nonadherence. Although hearing loss was associated with an increased risk of missed treatment cycles in the univariate analysis (OR 3.21, p = 0.018), it did not retain statistical significance in the multivariate model (OR 3.87, p = 0.133). In contrast, treatment‐related adverse effects emerged as the strongest independent predictor in both univariate (OR 59.5) and multivariate analyses (OR 93.2, p < 0.001), indicating that treatment tolerability is the primary factor associated with treatment adherence. However, the exceptionally high odds ratio and wide confidence interval observed for treatment‐related adverse events should be interpreted cautiously, given the limited sample size and relatively low number of events in some subgroups.

Contemporary studies focusing on treatment‐related ototoxicity and quality of life have also emphasized the complex interaction between acute treatment toxicities and chronic sensory impairments in cancer patients [14]. Although age ≥ 67 years and absence of a caregiver were significant in univariate analysis, they did not retain independent significance in the multivariate model. Overall, these findings suggest that hearing loss may function not as a sole determinant but as a contributing factor within a multifactorial process involving clinical burden and social support.

Another notable finding of this study was the association between the severity of hearing loss and treatment adherence. Higher degrees of hearing loss were associated with increased rates of missed treatment cycles. This observation suggests that the impact of hearing impairment on treatment adherence may vary according to its severity. However, given the limited number of patients in some subgroups, this finding should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory.

Another important finding of our study was the role of social support. The higher frequency of treatment disruption among patients without a caregiver suggests that access and communication difficulties may more readily translate into missed treatments in patients acting independently. In this context, hearing loss may further increase this vulnerability by complicating patient–physician communication. These findings highlight the potential interaction between sensory impairment and social support in influencing treatment adherence.

In subgroup analyses, the association between hearing loss and treatment disruption was statistically significant in patients without caregiver support (p = 0.023), whereas this association was weaker and not statistically significant in patients with caregiver support (p = 0.411). These findings may suggest that caregivers play a supportive role in patients with hearing impairment by facilitating communication and treatment continuity during oncological care.

Recent literature investigating the burden of ototoxicity has further emphasized that hearing loss may contribute to communication difficulties, social withdrawal, and reduced quality of life in cancer survivors [3].

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single‐center sample, and since patient characteristics and treatment conditions may vary across centers, the generalizability of the results may be limited. Moreover, the inclusion of different systemic treatment modalities may have introduced clinical heterogeneity, potentially affecting the interpretation of treatment adherence outcomes. Future studies conducted in more homogeneous patient populations receiving similar treatment regimens may help better clarify the independent contribution of hearing loss. Second, although hearing loss was graded at baseline, serial audiological assessments were not performed during the treatment course. Therefore, treatment‐related dynamic hearing changes, particularly potential ototoxic effects associated with systemic therapies, and their possible impact on treatment adherence could not be evaluated.

Treatment adherence was evaluated based on objective measures such as missed appointments and treatment session disruptions; however, potential determinants including patient motivation, cognitive status, depression, social isolation, transportation difficulties, or healthcare system barriers were not systematically assessed. As adverse event data were derived from clinical records, mild toxicities and symptoms reported solely by patients may have been underrepresented. The lack of an independent association between hearing loss and treatment nonadherence in the multivariate analysis may be related to sample size limitations and the influence of potential confounders not included in the model. Furthermore, the relatively small number of events in certain subgroups may have affected the stability of the multivariate logistic regression model and contributed to wide confidence intervals for some estimates.

The inability to evaluate variables such as educational level, socioeconomic status, and the quality of family support limits a comprehensive understanding of the relationship between hearing loss and treatment adherence. Furthermore, the hearing status of caregivers was not evaluated. Since caregivers may substantially contribute to patient–physician communication and treatment coordination, future studies incorporating caregiver‐related auditory characteristics may provide additional insight into treatment adherence.

The relatively high mortality rate observed in this cohort may reflect the clinical severity of the study population and should be considered when interpreting treatment adherence outcomes.

Furthermore, a proportion of patients was still undergoing treatment at the time of analysis, which may limit the completeness of adherence assessment.

Therefore, larger, multicenter, prospective studies with systematic monitoring of the degree of hearing loss and its temporal changes, as well as inclusion of social support, patient–physician communication, health literacy, and behavioral factors in the analyses, will contribute to the validation of our findings and to the identification of clinically meaningful intervention points.

Conclusion

In conclusion, treatment adherence in cancer patients appears to be primarily driven by clinical factors, particularly treatment‐related adverse events. While hearing loss was associated with increased treatment disruption, it did not independently predict adherence when other variables were considered. These findings highlight the importance of evaluating hearing loss within the broader context of clinical vulnerability and social support. Addressing communication barriers and improving supportive care may help improve treatment adherence in this population.
