Abstract
. We study matrix perpetuities, that is, solutions to affine fixed-point equations of the form 𝐗 = d 𝐀 ​ 𝐗 ​ 𝐀 ⊤ + 𝐁 , ( 𝐀 , 𝐁 ) ​ and ​ 𝐗 ​ are independent , {\mathbf{X}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbf{A}}\,{\mathbf{X}}\,{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{B}},\qquad({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})\mbox{ and }{\mathbf{X}}\mbox{ are independent}, with particular emphasis on the empirical spectral distribution of the solution. We first establish existence and uniqueness results by relating the problem to classical vector perpetuities, and then develop tools that preserve the matrix structure under orthogonal invariance. For positive semidefinite, orthogonally invariant models, we obtain power-law tail asymptotics for the expected empirical spectral distribution and show that the tail is governed by the largest eigenvalue. We also prove that, in the subcritical regime, the expected empirical spectral distribution of matrix perpetuities converges weakly, as the dimension tends to infinity, to the distribution of the corresponding free perpetuity. Our results are illustrated by matrix Beta prime perpetuities, for which explicit limiting spectral distributions are available.

1.
Introduction

A perpetuity is a solution to the following affine fixed point equation

(1.1)

\displaystyle\underline{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbf{A}}\,\underline{X}+\underline{B},

where
{\mathbf{A}}
is an
N\times N
random matrix,
\underline{B}
is an
N
dimensional random vector,
and
\underline{X}
is assumed to be independent of the pair
({\mathbf{A}},\underline{B})
.

The study of perpetuities is a prominent field in probability theory, driven by wide-ranging applications in actuarial science, finance, and queueing theory. Once
\underline{X}
is shown to exist, the focus shifts to understanding how the distribution of
({\mathbf{A}},\underline{B})
determines the distribution of
\underline{X}
, with particular attention given to its asymptotic tail behavior.

The foundational result of Kesten
Kes73
and subsequent developments (see
(
BDM
, Section 4.4)
) states that if there exists a positive parameter
\alpha>0
satisfying

\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}{\mathbb{E}}[\|\Pi_{n}\|^{\alpha}]^{1/n}=1,

where
\Pi_{n}={\mathbf{A}}_{n}{\mathbf{A}}_{n-1}\ldots{\mathbf{A}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{A}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{A}}_{n}
are iid copies of
{\mathbf{A}}
, then under additional technical assumptions on the distribution of
({\mathbf{A}},\underline{B})
, there exists a non-zero Radon measure
\mu
such that the solution to (
1.1
) satisfies

t^{\alpha}\,{\mathbb{P}}\left(t^{-1}\underline{X}\in\cdot\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu,\qquad t\to+\infty,

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\longrightarrow}}
denotes vague convergence of measures.

In the univariate case, the parameter
\alpha>0
is determined by the equation
{\mathbb{E}}[|A|^{\alpha}]=1
. Under additional assumptions, Kesten’s theorem guarantees that
\lim_{t\to+\infty}t^{\alpha}{\mathbb{P}}(X>t)
exists and is strictly positive and finite.

In this paper we study matrix fixed point equations of the form

(1.2)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{X}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{B}},\qquad({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})\mbox{ and }{\mathbf{X}}\mbox{ are independent},

where
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
are square
N\times N
random matrices with real entries, and
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
denotes the transpose of the matrix
\mathbf{A}
. One may vectorize this equation and apply classical vector perpetuity theory. Such a point of view is useful in establishing existence of matrix perpetuities, we explain this point of view in detail in Section
3
. The main condition under which a matrix perpetuity exists is
\gamma<0
, where
\gamma
is so-called top Lyapunov exponent, defined as

\displaystyle\gamma=\inf_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log\lambda_{\max}\left({\mathbf{A}}_{1}{\mathbf{A}}_{2}\ldots{\mathbf{A}}_{n}{\mathbf{A}}_{n}^{\top}\ldots{\mathbf{A}}_{2}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}}_{1}^{\top}\right)\right].

After establishing the existence of matrix perpetuities, we propose an approach that preserves the matrix structure. Our objective is to study the empirical spectral distribution (ESD) of
\mathbf{X}
. To the best of our knowledge, the ESD of matrix perpetuities has not yet been investigated.

Before stating our main results, let us briefly indicate one possible motivation
for studying matrix perpetuities. Stochastic gradient descent and related random
iterative algorithms are known to generate heavy-tailed stationary distributions
through Kesten-type affine recursions; see, for instance,
DM24
. In
machine-learning applications, however, one is often interested not only in the
tails of individual coordinates or matrix entries, but also in the tail behavior
of empirical spectral distributions of random matrices; see
ML3
. A
spectral analogue of the classical Kesten mechanism should therefore describe
how multiplicative random matrix effects and additive perturbations shape the
eigenvalue distribution. The matrix perpetuity equation studied in this paper
provides a tractable closed model for this type of phenomenon, without being
intended as an exact model of stochastic gradient descent.

In
FreePerp
we studied the free perpetuities. That is, a solution to the following fixed point equations

{\mathbb{X}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbb{A}}\,{\mathbb{X}}\,{\mathbb{A}}^{*}+{\mathbb{B}},

where
{\mathbb{X}}
is
*
-free from the pair
({\mathbb{A}},{\mathbb{B}})
, where
{\mathbb{B}}={\mathbb{B}}^{*}
. As we have shown in
FreePerp
, free perpetuity equation is equivalent to
{\mathbb{X}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}|{\mathbb{A}}|{\mathbb{X}}\,|{\mathbb{A}}|+{\mathbb{B}}
, and we considered without loss of generality an equation of the form
{\mathbb{X}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbb{A}}^{1/2}{\mathbb{X}}\,{\mathbb{A}}^{1/2}+{\mathbb{B}}
, assuming that
{\mathbb{A}}\geq 0
. We found sufficient conditions under which free perpetuity exists. In particular if
\tau({\mathbb{A}})\leq 1
then the perpetuity exists, and the free counterpart of Kesten’s condition becomes
\tau({\mathbb{A}})=1
. We showed, for example, that under this condition, if
\mathrm{Var}({\mathbb{A}})<\infty
then
\mu_{{\mathbb{X}}}\big((t,+\infty)\big)\sim c\,t^{-1/2}
, with an explicit constant
c>0
.

From the point of view of matrix perpetuities, it is natural to ask how the ESD of matrix perpetuities connects to the distribution of the free perpetuity. More precisely, suppose that the sequence of random matrices
({\mathbf{A}}_{N},{\mathbf{B}}_{N})
converges in noncommutative distribution (for a precise definition, see Section
2
) to
({\mathbb{A}},{\mathbb{B}})
. In this framework, we would like to understand whether, under suitable conditions, the ESD of
{\mathbf{X}}_{N}
converges weakly to the distribution of
{\mathbb{X}}
. Asymptotic freeness emerges naturally when independent random matrices satisfy additional invariance of the distribution, therefore we assume throughout the paper that

(1.3)

\displaystyle({\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top},{\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})

for any orthogonal matrix
{\mathbf{u}}
. We show that in the subcritical regime
\tau({\mathbb{A}})<1
indeed we have weak convergence of the expected ESD of
{\mathbf{X}}_{N}
to the distribution of
{\mathbb{X}}
.

One should note that the asymptotics of tails of measures do not behave continuously with respect to weak convergence of probability measures. In particular for any measure
\mu
with arbitrary tail behaviour there is a sequence of compactly supported probability measures
\mu_{n}
which converge weakly to
\mu
.

We study the tails of ESD of matrix perpetuities under the invariance assumption for almost surely positive matrices
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}
. We find a simple condition

{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbf{A}}_{11}^{\eta}]=1

plus some technical conditions, under which the matrix perpetuity exists and expected ESD has nontrivial tail asymptotics, it is worth noting that this condition is analogous to the one dimensional perpetuity case. In particular we show that the tail of the expected ESD up to a constant has the same asymptotics as
\lambda_{1}({\mathbf{X}})
and
{\mathbf{X}}_{11}
and tails of all of those measures have power-law behaviour
ct^{-\eta}
. This shows in particular that the tail behaviour of
\mu_{{\mathbf{X}}}
is governed by the top eigenvalue. However, a single eigenvalue does not contribute to the weak limit. This implies that even though
\mu_{{\mathbf{X}}_{n}}
converges weakly to the distribution of free perpetuity
\mu_{\mathbb{X}}
, the tail behaviour of
\mu_{{\mathbf{X}}_{n}}
will be different asymptotically as the size
N\to\infty
from the tail of
\mu_{{\mathbb{X}}}
.

As a byproduct of our analysis, we also obtain a simple finite-dimensional
result which, to the best of our knowledge, is new. It concerns the behavior of
principal submatrices under multiplicative random matrix transformations. More
precisely, if
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
are independent and
{\mathbf{B}}
is orthogonally
invariant, then the
k\times k
leading principal submatrix of

{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}
has the same distribution as the corresponding
multiplicative product formed from the compressed matrices

({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]}
and
{\mathbf{B}}^{[k]}
. Equivalently, principal compression
is compatible with the finite-dimensional symmetric multiplicative convolution.

Using this idea, we show that under orthogonal invariance of the pair
({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})
, if the law of
{\mathbf{X}}
satisfies (
1.2
), then the law of the principal
k\times k
submatrix
{\mathbf{X}}^{[k]}
of
{\mathbf{X}}
satisfies

{\mathbf{X}}^{[k]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]})^{1/2}{\mathbf{X}}^{[k]}(({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]})^{1/2}+{\mathbf{B}}^{[k]},\quad(({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]},{\mathbf{B}}^{[k]})\mbox{ and }{\mathbf{X}}^{[k]}\mbox{ are independent.}

In particular, for
k=1
, we obtain a scalar perpetuity equation

\displaystyle{\mathbf{X}}_{11}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})_{11}{\mathbf{X}}_{11}+{\mathbf{B}}_{11},\qquad(({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})_{11},{\mathbf{B}}_{11})\mbox{ and }{\mathbf{X}}_{11}\mbox{ are independent}.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we introduce the notation and collect the necessary background on empirical spectral distributions, noncommutative probability, and free perpetuities. In Section
3
we establish existence and uniqueness of matrix perpetuities, discuss equivalent multiplication schemes under orthogonal invariance, and relate matrix perpetuities to their principal minors. Section
5
is devoted to tail asymptotics of the expected empirical spectral distribution of matrix perpetuities. In Section
6
we study the convergence of expected empirical spectral distributions of matrix perpetuities to the law of the corresponding free perpetuity. Appendix
A
contains a detailed analysis of the matrix Beta prime example and its limiting spectral distribution. Appendix
B
records a marginal property of the matrix Beta prime distribution for principal submatrices. Finally, Appendix
C
provides the measurable-selection argument used to construct a measurable polar decomposition.

2.
Preliminaries

Notation 2.1
.

Throughout the paper, bold lowercase letters, such as
{\bf x}
, denote deterministic matrices, whereas bold uppercase letters, such as
{\bf X}
, denote random matrices. The
N\times N
identity matrix is denoted by
{\mathbf{id}}_{N}
.

We use the following matrix spaces:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Mat}(N):=\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},\qquad\mathrm{Sym}(N):=\{{\bf x}\in\mathrm{Mat}(N)\colon{\bf x}={\bf x}^{\top}\}.

The cones of positive semidefinite and positive definite matrices are denoted by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sym}_{\geq 0}(N)

\displaystyle:=\{{\bf x}\in\mathrm{Sym}(N)\colon{\bf x}\geq 0\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sym}_{>0}(N)

\displaystyle:=\{{\bf x}\in\mathrm{Sym}(N)\colon{\bf x}>0\}.

Here,
{\bf x}\geq 0
means that
{\bf x}
is positive semidefinite, while
{\bf x}>0
means that
{\bf x}
is positive definite.

The orthogonal group is denoted by

O(N):=\{{\bf u}\in\mathrm{Mat}(N)\colon{\bf u}{\bf u}^{\top}={\mathbf{id}}_{N}\}.

For
{\bf x}\in\mathrm{Mat}(N)
, we denote by
\mathrm{Tr}({\bf x})
its trace and by
\mathrm{tr}({\bf x})
its normalized trace, that is,

\mathrm{tr}({\bf x})=\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{Tr}({\bf x}).

Definition 2.2
.

We say that the distribution of an
N\times N
real random matrix
{\mathbf{Y}}
is invariant under the action of the orthogonal group
O(N)
if, for any orthogonal matrix
{\bf u}
, we have

{\bf u}{\mathbf{Y}}{\bf u}^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbf{Y}}.

More generally we say that a pair of random matrices
({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})
is orthogonally invariant if for every orthogonal matrix
{\mathbf{u}}
, we have

({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\left({\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top},{\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\right).

Definition 2.3
.

For any
N\times N
real random matrix
{\mathbf{Y}}
with eigenvalues
(\lambda_{k}({\mathbf{Y}}))_{k=1}^{N}
, we define the empirical spectral distribution and the expected empirical spectral distribution by
\hat{\mu}_{{\mathbf{Y}}}
and
\mu_{{\mathbf{Y}}}
respectively, by

\hat{\mu}_{\mathbf{Y}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\delta_{\lambda_{k}({\mathbf{Y}})},\qquad\mu_{{\mathbf{Y}}}={\mathbb{E}}[\hat{\mu}_{\mathbf{Y}}],

where
\lambda_{k}({\mathbf{Y}})
for
k=1,\ldots,N
are the eigenvalues of
{\mathbf{Y}}
listed with multiplicities.

2.1.
Noncommutative probability

A noncommutative probability space is a pair
(\mathcal{A},\tau)
where
\mathcal{A}
is a
*
-algebra, and
\tau
is a faithful, normal, tracial state.

We will use two noncommutative probability spaces. In the
W^{*}
-setting,
\mathcal{A}
is a finite von Neumann algebra,
\tau
is faithful, normal tracial state, and
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
denotes the algebra of affiliated operators. We also use the random-matrix space, of random matrices whose entries have all moments finite, and
\tau={\mathbb{E}}\otimes\mathrm{tr}
.

For fixed, selfadjoint elements
{\mathbb{A}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbb{A}}_{n}\in\mathcal{A}
their joint distribution is a functional
\mu_{{\mathbb{A}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbb{A}}_{n}}:{\mathbb{C}}\langle X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}\rangle\to{\mathbb{C}}
, which for any noncommutative polynomial
P\in{\mathbb{C}}\langle X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}\rangle
is defined via

\mu_{{\mathbb{A}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbb{A}}_{n}}(P)=\tau(P({\mathbb{A}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbb{A}}_{n})).

Let
\left({\mathbf{A}}_{1}^{(N)},\ldots,{\mathbf{A}}_{n}^{(N)}\right)_{N\geq 1}
be a sequence of
n
-tuples of random matrices, where
N
-th term in this sequence consists of
N\times N
matrices. We say that
\left({\mathbf{A}}_{1}^{(N)},\ldots,{\mathbf{A}}_{n}^{(N)}\right)_{N\geq 1}
converges in noncommutative distribution to a tuple
({\mathbb{A}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbb{A}}_{n})
in
(\mathcal{A},\tau)
if
for every noncommutative polynomial
P\in{\mathbb{C}}\langle X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\rangle
we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathrm{tr}(P({\mathbf{A}}^{(N)}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{A}}_{n}^{(N)}))]=\tau(P({\mathbb{A}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbb{A}}_{n})).

2.2.

{\mathbb{X}}
affiliated with

(2.1)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{X}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbb{A}}^{1/2}\,{\mathbb{X}}\,{\mathbb{A}}^{1/2}+{\mathbb{B}},\qquad{\mathbb{X}}\mbox{ and }({\mathbb{A}},{\mathbb{B}})\ \text{are $*$-free},

,

and
{\mathbb{B}}={\mathbb{B}}^{\ast}\neq 0

and

\mathcal{A}

.

Assume
{\mathbb{B}}\geq 0
and

. If

, or

3.
Matrix fixed-point affine equation

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
be square
N\times N
random matrices with real entries. We consider an affine stochastic equation of the form

(3.1)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{X}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{B}},\qquad({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})\mbox{ and }{\mathbf{X}}\mbox{ are independent},

where
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
denotes the transpose of a matrix
\mathbf{A}
.

To analyze this equation, we reformulate the problem in the usual vector setting, see
BDM
. By vectorization of an
N\times N
matrix
{\bf x}=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}]\in\mathrm{Mat}(N)
we mean the vector

\mathrm{vec}({\mathbf{x}})=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{N}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N^{2}}.

Matrix equation (
3.1
) is equivalent to the following

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{vec}({\mathbf{X}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}\mathrm{vec}({\mathbf{X}})+\mathrm{vec}({\mathbf{B}}),\qquad(\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}},\mathrm{vec}({\mathbf{B}}))\mbox{ and }\mathrm{vec}({\mathbf{X}})\mbox{ are independent},

where
\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}={\mathbf{A}}\otimes{\mathbf{A}}
with
\otimes
denoting the Kronecker product. Using this approach, we can establish conditions on the distributions of
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
under which a unique solution to (
3.1
) exists. These conditions do not impose structural assumptions on the joint distribution of the pair
({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})
.

3.1.
Existence and uniqueness of matrix perpetuities

For
a>0
, define
\log^{+}(a)=\max\{\log(a),0\}
. Let
\lambda_{\max}({\mathbf{a}})
denote the largest eigenvalue of a symmetric matrix
{\mathbf{a}}

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
({\mathbf{A}}_{n},{\mathbf{B}}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be iid copies of
({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})
. If

(3.3)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log^{+}\left(\lambda_{\max}({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})\right)\right]<+\infty\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log^{+}\left(\lambda_{\max}({\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{B}}^{\top})\right)\right]<+\infty

and

(3.4)

\displaystyle\gamma=\inf_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log(\lambda_{\max}\left({\mathbf{A}}_{1}{\mathbf{A}}_{2}\ldots{\mathbf{A}}_{n}{\mathbf{A}}_{n}^{\top}\ldots{\mathbf{A}}_{2}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}}_{1}^{\top}\right))\right]<0,

then there exists a unique solution to (
3.1
).

Moreover, this solution has a series representation

(3.5)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{X}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\mathbf{A}}_{1}\ldots{\mathbf{A}}_{n-1}{\mathbf{B}}_{n}{\mathbf{A}}_{n-1}^{\top}\ldots{\mathbf{A}}_{1}^{\top},

where the series on the right-hand side above converges almost surely, with

({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})

Finally, letting

{\mathbf{X}}_{n}={\mathbf{A}}_{n}{\mathbf{X}}_{n-1}{\mathbf{A}}_{n}^{\top}+{\mathbf{B}}_{n},\qquad n\geq 1,

where
{\mathbf{X}}_{0}
is independent of

{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathbf{X}}.

Proof.

Let

\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{ydy}^{T}\mathbf{d})=\sum_{i,j}d_{i}d_{j}y_{ij}^{2}

\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{yy}^{T})=\sum_{i,j}y_{ij}^{2}

By

\gamma:=\inf_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log(\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{1}\cdots\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{n}\|)\right]<0.

Note that the above result does not depend on the choice of the norm
|\cdot|

Definition 3.2

We say that an affine subspace

Assume that condition (

Although Theorem

provides a sufficient condition for existence and yields a series representation of a perpetuity, it generally does not lead to an explicit description of its distribution. Example 3.4
.

Define
d_{N}=N^{-1}\dim\mathrm{Sym}(N)=(N+1)/2
.
The matrix beta-prime distribution with parameters
\alpha,\beta>d_{N}-1
on the cone of positive definite matrices
\mathrm{Sym}_{\geq 0}(N)
, is defined by its density

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}(N)(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{\mathrm{B}_{N}(\alpha,\beta)}\frac{\det(\mathbf{x})^{\alpha-d_{N}}}{\det({\mathbf{id}}_{N}+\mathbf{x})^{\alpha+\beta}}I_{\mathrm{Sym}_{\geq 0}(N)}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

where
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathrm{Sym}(N)
endowed with the inner product
\langle{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{y}})
, and
\mathrm{B}_{N}(\alpha,\beta)
is the normalizing constant.
In
(
ABO
, Section 5)
the authors showed that
if
{\mathbf{A}}\sim\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{\alpha,\alpha+\beta}(N)
with
\alpha,\beta>d_{N}-1
then,
{\mathbf{X}}\sim\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}(N)
is the unique solution to the following stochastic fixed-point equation

(3.8)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{X}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbf{A}}^{1/2}{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{A}}^{1/2}+{\mathbf{A}},\qquad{\mathbf{A}}\mbox{ and }{\mathbf{X}}\mbox{ are independent}.

Example 3.5
.

On
\mathrm{Sym}(N)
, we define the Wishart distribution with parameters
p>d_{N}-1
and
{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathrm{Sym}_{>0}(N)
by

(3.9)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}_{p,{\mathbf{a}}}(N)(\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{\det({\mathbf{a}})^{p}}{\Gamma_{N}(p)}\det({\mathbf{x}})^{p-d_{N}}e^{-\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{x}})}I_{\mathrm{Sym}_{>0}(N)}({\mathbf{x}})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}

and the matrix generalized inverse Gaussian distribution with parameters
p\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathrm{Sym}_{>0}(N)
by

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{p,{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}}(N)(\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{K_{N,p}({\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}})}\det({\mathbf{x}})^{p-d_{N}}e^{-\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{x}})-\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{x}}^{-1})}I_{\mathrm{Sym}_{>0}(N)}({\mathbf{x}})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}},

where
\Gamma_{N}(p)
and
K_{N,p}({\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}})
are normalizing constants.

According to
LW00
(see also
KMY
for extension to other symmetric cones),
if
{\mathbf{X}}\sim\mathcal{G}_{-p,{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}}(N)
and
{\mathbf{Y}}\sim{\mathcal{W}}_{p,{\mathbf{a}}}(N)
are independent, then

\mathbf{V}:={\mathbf{X}}^{-1}-({\mathbf{X}}+{\mathbf{Y}})^{-1}\sim{\mathcal{W}}_{p,{\mathbf{b}}}.

Thus, if
{\mathbf{a}}={\mathbf{b}}
, then
{\mathbf{Y}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbf{V}
, which by Hua’s identity implies that

{\mathbf{Y}}^{-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}={\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{Y}}^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}+{\mathbf{X}},

providing another example of an equation of the form (
3.1
) with an explicit solution.

A generalization of this example to Jordan algebras was recently considered in
(
MY2024
, Theorem 2.4)
.

4.
Orthogonal invariance

4.1.
Multiplication schemes

Next we observe that in the case of orthogonally invariant models, one can equivalently consider different multiplication schemes than the one in (
3.1
).
It is customary to denote
({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{1/2}
by
|{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}|
. In a slight abuse of notation, we will instead write this quantity as
|{\mathbf{A}}|
.

Definition 4.1
.

By
m\colon\mathrm{Mat}(N)\to\mathrm{Mat}(N)
we denote a measurable function satisfying
m({\mathbf{a}})m({\mathbf{a}})^{\top}={\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}
.

Examples of
m
include
m_{1}({\mathbf{a}})=({\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{a}}^{\top})^{1/2}
and
m_{2}({\mathbf{a}})=T_{{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}}
, where
T_{\mathbf{b}}
is lower triangular matrix from the Cholesky decomposition of
{\mathbf{b}}=T_{{\mathbf{b}}}T_{{\mathbf{b}}}^{\top}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a random real matrix. Then there exists a
\sigma({\mathbf{A}})
-measurable orthogonal matrix
{\mathbf{U}}
such that

{\mathbf{A}}=m({\mathbf{A}}){\mathbf{U}}.

Proof.

By Lemma
C.3
, there exist orthogonal matrices
{\mathbf{U}}_{1},{\mathbf{U}}_{2}
, measurable with respect to
\sigma({\mathbf{A}})
and
\sigma(m({\mathbf{A}}))
respectively, such that

{\mathbf{A}}=|{\mathbf{A}}|\,{\mathbf{U}}_{1},\qquad m({\mathbf{A}})=|m({\mathbf{A}})|\,{\mathbf{U}}_{2}.

Since
m({\mathbf{A}})m({\mathbf{A}})^{\top}={\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}
, we have
|m({\mathbf{A}})|=|{\mathbf{A}}|
. Hence

{\mathbf{A}}=|{\mathbf{A}}|\,{\mathbf{U}}_{1}=|m({\mathbf{A}})|\,{\mathbf{U}}_{1}=m({\mathbf{A}}){\mathbf{U}}_{2}^{\top}{\mathbf{U}}_{1}.

Because
m
is measurable,
\sigma(m({\mathbf{A}}))\subset\sigma({\mathbf{A}})
, so
{\mathbf{U}}_{2}^{\top}{\mathbf{U}}_{1}
is
\sigma({\mathbf{A}})
-measurable. Therefore, setting

{\mathbf{U}}:={\mathbf{U}}_{2}^{\top}{\mathbf{U}}_{1},

we obtain the claim.
∎

4.2.
Principal minors and multiplicative convolution

We begin with a simple observation which, when specialized to the Cholesky factorization, has useful consequences for the distributions of principal minors.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
be independent. Assume that
{\mathbf{B}}
is orthogonally invariant. Then,

{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}m({\mathbf{A}}){\mathbf{B}}m({\mathbf{A}})^{\top}.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.2
, there exists a
\sigma({\mathbf{A}})
-measurable orthogonal matrix
{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathbf{A}}
such that
{\mathbf{A}}=m({\mathbf{A}}){\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{A}}}
.
Consider

{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}=m({\mathbf{A}}){\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{A}}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{A}}}^{\top}m({\mathbf{A}})^{\top}.

Note that we have
({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{A}}}^{\top})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})
. Indeed,

({\mathbf{U}}_{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{A}}}^{\top}\mid{\mathbf{A}}={\mathbf{a}})={\mathbf{U}}_{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{a}}}^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbf{B}}.

Thus, we obtain

{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}m({\mathbf{A}}){\mathbf{B}}m({\mathbf{A}})^{\top}.

∎

Notation 4.4
.

For any
N\times N
matrix
{\mathbf{x}}
and
k=1,\ldots,N
, we denote by
{\mathbf{x}}^{[k]}
the
k\times k
principal submatrix of
{\mathbf{x}}
, i.e.,
{\mathbf{x}}^{[k]}=({\mathbf{x}}_{ij})_{i,j\leq k}
.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
be independent. Assume that
{\mathbf{B}}
is orthogonally invariant. Then, for
k=1,\ldots,N
,

({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]})^{1/2}{\mathbf{B}}^{[k]}(({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]})^{1/2}.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.3
, we have

{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}m({\mathbf{A}}){\mathbf{B}}m({\mathbf{A}})^{\top}.

Take now
m({\mathbf{A}})=T_{{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}}
, where
T_{{\mathbf{a}}}
is the lower triangular matrix from the Cholesky decomposition of
{\mathbf{a}}=T_{{\mathbf{a}}}T_{{\mathbf{a}}}^{\top}
.

Let
{\mathbf{x}}^{[k]}:=({\mathbf{x}}_{ij})_{i,j\leq k}
be the
k\times k
leading principal submatrix of
{\mathbf{x}}
. It is well known that

(4.1)

\displaystyle({\bf t}{\mathbf{x}}{\bf t}^{\top})^{[k]}={\bf t}^{[k]}{\mathbf{x}}^{[k]}({\bf t}^{[k]})^{\top}

for any lower triangular matrix
\bf t
and any matrix
{\mathbf{x}}
. Thus, we obtain

({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}T_{{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}}^{[k]}{\mathbf{B}}^{[k]}(T_{{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}}^{[k]})^{\top}

By (
4.1
)

T_{{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}}^{[k]}=T_{({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]}}.

Since
{\mathbf{B}}^{[k]}
is also orthogonally invariant, we have

T_{{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}}^{[k]}{\mathbf{B}}^{[k]}(T_{{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}}^{[k]})^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]})^{1/2}{\mathbf{B}}^{[k]}(({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]})^{1/2}

by Proposition
4.3
.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

Let

C_{k}:\mathrm{Sym}(N)\to\mathrm{Sym}(k),\qquad C_{k}({\mathbf{a}}):={\mathbf{a}}^{[k]},

and write
(C_{k})_{\#}\mu
for the
push-forward of a probability measure
\mu
under
C_{k}
.

For probability measures
\mu,\nu
on
\mathrm{Sym}(N)
, with

\mu
supported on positive semidefinite matrices, define

\mu\circledast\nu
as the law of

{\mathbf{A}}^{1/2}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{A}}^{1/2},

where
{\mathbf{A}}\sim\mu
,
{\mathbf{B}}\sim\nu
, and
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}
are independent.

Suppose now that
\nu
is orthogonally invariant, that is,

\mathrm{Law}({\mathbf{B}})=\mathrm{Law}({\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}),\qquad{\mathbf{u}}\in O(N).

Then Corollary
4.5
implies that, for every
k=1,\ldots,N
,

(C_{k})_{\#}(\mu\circledast\nu)=(C_{k})_{\#}\mu\circledast(C_{k})_{\#}\nu,

where the convolution on the right-hand side is understood in dimension

k
.

Thus the principal compression map
C_{k}
is compatible with the
symmetric multiplicative convolution
\circledast
. In particular, on
the class of orthogonally invariant measures,
C_{k}
acts as a
homomorphism with respect to
\circledast
.

Remark 4.7
.

The preceding finite-dimensional compatibility has an asymptotic analogue.
It is well known (see
NicaSpeicherMult
;
ShlyakhtenkoTao
) that if we have a sequence of orthogonally invariant matrices
{\mathbf{A}}_{N}\in\mathrm{Mat}(N)
such that the empirical spectral distribution (ESD)
\mu_{{\mathbf{A}}_{N}}
converges weakly to
\mu
, then for any fixed
t>1
the ESD of
t\,{\mathbf{A}}_{N}^{[k]}
, with
k=\lfloor N/t\rfloor
, converges to the fractional free convolution power
\mu^{\boxplus t}
.

Moreover, assume that we have a sequence of orthogonally invariant random matrices
{\mathbf{B}}_{N}
independent of
{\mathbf{A}}_{N}
, whose ESD converges to
\nu
. From the previous Corollary we obtain

({\mathbf{A}}_{N}^{1/2}{\mathbf{B}}_{N}{\mathbf{A}}_{N}^{1/2})^{[k]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}({\mathbf{A}}_{N}^{[k]})^{1/2}{\mathbf{B}}_{N}^{[k]}({\mathbf{A}}_{N}^{[k]})^{1/2}.

As
N\to\infty
, the ESD of

t\,({\mathbf{A}}_{N}^{1/2}{\mathbf{B}}_{N}{\mathbf{A}}_{N}^{1/2})^{[k]}

converges to
(\mu\boxtimes\nu)^{\boxplus t}
. On the other hand, multiplying the right-hand side of the above equality by
t^{2}
, we obtain

(t{\mathbf{A}}_{N}^{[k]})^{1/2}\,t{\mathbf{B}}_{N}^{[k]}\,(t{\mathbf{A}}_{N}^{[k]})^{1/2},

whose ESD converges to
\mu^{\boxplus t}\boxtimes\nu^{\boxplus t}
. Therefore,

\mu^{\boxplus t}\boxtimes\nu^{\boxplus t}=(\mu\boxtimes\nu)^{\boxplus t}\circ D_{1/t},

where
\mu\circ D_{r}(A)=\mu(rA)
denotes dilation of a measure. This equality was proved in
Remarkable
, Proposition 3.5, in the context of a semigroup of homomorphisms with respect to multiplicative free convolution.

4.3.
The top Lyapunov exponent

The constant
\gamma
defined in Theorem
3.1
is known as the top Lyapunov exponent, a fundamental object in understanding the behavior of many dynamical systems. Orthogonal invariance often greatly simplifies the analysis of matrix products; in the present setting, the tools developed above lead to an elementary proof of a known formula for the top Lyapunov exponent.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\gamma
be the top Lyapunov exponent defined in (
3.4
). If
{\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}
for any orthogonal matrix
{\mathbf{u}}
, then

\gamma={\mathbb{E}}[\log({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})_{11}].

Remark 4.9
.

In
Newman86
(see also
(
ABO
, Appendix C)
), the author considered orthogonal invariance of the form
{\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}}
for any orthogonal matrix
{\mathbf{u}}
and proved that in such case

\gamma={\mathbb{E}}[\log({\mathbf{A}}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}})_{11}].

We propose a new, elementary method to prove such result.

Proof of Lemma
4.8
.

Assume that
{\bf m}\in\mathrm{Sym}_{\geq 0}(N)
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}({\bf m})
for
i=1,\ldots,N
. Then,

{\bf m}_{11}=e_{1}^{\top}{\bf m}e_{1}\leq\max_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\colon x^{\top}x=1}x^{\top}{\bf m}x=\lambda_{\max}({\bf m}).

Assume that
{\bf M}
is orthogonally invariant. Then
{\bf M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbf{U}{\bf\Lambda}\mathbf{U}^{\top}
, where
{\bf\Lambda}
is a diagonal matrix of eigenvalues of
M
, with
{\bf\Lambda}_{ii}=\lambda_{i}({\bf M})
with
\lambda_{i}({\bf M})\geq\lambda_{i+1}({\bf M})
,
\mathbf{U}
is a Haar orthogonal matrix and
\mathbf{U}
and
{\bf\Lambda}
are independent.
Moreover,

{\bf M}_{11}=e_{1}^{\top}{\bf M}e_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(\mathbf{U}^{\top}e_{1})^{\top}{\bf\Lambda}\,\mathbf{U}^{\top}e_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}({\bf M})\mathbf{U}_{1j}^{2}\geq\lambda_{\max}({\bf M})\mathbf{U}_{11}^{2}.

Thus,

(4.2)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\log{\bf M}_{11}]\leq{\mathbb{E}}[\log\lambda_{\max}({\bf M})]\leq{\mathbb{E}}[\log{\bf M}_{11}-\log\mathbf{U}_{11}^{2}],

where
\mathbf{U}^{2}_{11}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1/2,(N-1)/2)
and so
{\mathbb{E}}[\log\mathbf{U}_{11}^{2}]=\Psi(1/2)-\Psi(N/2)
, where
\Psi
is the digamma function.

Recall that
\gamma=\inf_{n\geq 1}n^{-1}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log(\lambda_{\max}\left(\Pi_{n}^{\top}\Pi_{n}\right))\right]
, where
\Pi_{n}={\mathbf{A}}_{n}{\mathbf{A}}_{n-1}\ldots{\mathbf{A}}_{1}
. Since
\lambda_{\max}({\mathbf{a}}{\bf b})=\lambda_{\max}({\bf b}{\mathbf{a}})
, we have

(4.3)

\displaystyle\gamma=\inf_{n\geq 1}n^{-1}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log(\lambda_{\max}\left({\bf M}_{n}\right))\right],\qquad\mbox{ with }{\bf M}_{n}=\Pi_{n}\Pi_{n}^{\top}.

Since
{\bf M}_{n}={\mathbf{A}}_{n}{\bf M}_{n-1}{\mathbf{A}}_{n}^{\top}
with
{\bf M}_{1}={\mathbf{A}}_{1}{\mathbf{A}}_{1}^{\top}
, we prove by induction that
{\bf M}_{n}
is orthogonally invariant for each
n
. The case
n=1
follows from the assumption on
{\mathbf{A}}_{1}{\mathbf{A}}_{1}^{\top}
.
Now assume that
{\bf M}_{n-1}
is orthogonally invariant. Write the polar decomposition (it is well defined by Lemma
C.3
)

{\mathbf{A}}_{n}=|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|\,{\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{A}}_{n}},\qquad|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|:=({\mathbf{A}}_{n}{\mathbf{A}}_{n}^{\top})^{1/2},

where
{\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{A}}_{n}}
is orthogonal. Then for any deterministic orthogonal matrix
{\mathbf{u}}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}{\bf M}_{n}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{u}}|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|\,{\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{A}}_{n}}{\bf M}_{n-1}{\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{A}}_{n}}^{\top}|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}

\displaystyle=({\mathbf{u}}|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|{\mathbf{u}}^{\top})\,({\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{A}}_{n}}){\bf M}_{n-1}({\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{A}}_{n}})^{\top}\,({\mathbf{u}}|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|{\mathbf{u}}^{\top})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}({\mathbf{u}}|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|{\mathbf{u}}^{\top})\,{\bf M}_{n-1}\,({\mathbf{u}}|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}),

since
{\bf M}_{n-1}
is orthogonally invariant and independent of
{\mathbf{A}}_{n}
, hence also independent of
{\mathbf{U}}_{{\mathbf{A}}_{n}}
.
Next,

{\mathbf{u}}|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}={\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{A}}_{n}{\mathbf{A}}_{n}^{\top})^{1/2}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}=({\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{A}}_{n}{\mathbf{A}}_{n}^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top})^{1/2}.

Therefore, by the orthogonal invariance of
{\mathbf{A}}_{n}{\mathbf{A}}_{n}^{\top}
, we have
{\mathbf{u}}|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|
.
Combining this with the independence of
{\bf M}_{n-1}
and
{\mathbf{A}}_{n}
, we obtain

{\mathbf{u}}{\bf M}_{n}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|\,{\bf M}_{n-1}\,|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\bf M}_{n},

so
{\bf M}_{n}
is orthogonally invariant.

Then, we apply Corollary
4.5
with
k=1
successively to obtain

({\bf M}_{n})_{11}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}({\mathbf{A}}_{i}{\mathbf{A}}_{i}^{\top})_{11}.

Thus we get

{\mathbb{E}}[\log({\bf M}_{n})_{11}]=n\,{\mathbb{E}}[\log({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})_{11}]

and the assertion follows from (
4.3
) and inequalities in (
4.2
) applied to
{\bf M}={\bf M}_{n}
.

∎

Remark 4.10
.

The
(1,1)
-entry function in Lemma
4.8
can be replaced by the trace of any rank-one projection.
Indeed, any rank-one projection
P
can be expressed as
({\mathbf{u}}^{\top}e_{1})({\mathbf{u}}^{\top}e_{1})^{\top}
, where
e_{1}=(1,0\ldots,0)^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
and
{\mathbf{u}}
is an orthogonal matrix. Then, we have for orthogonally invariant
{\mathbf{X}}={\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}
,

{\mathbf{X}}_{11}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}({\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top})_{11}=e_{1}^{\top}({\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top})e_{1}=\mathrm{Tr}(({\mathbf{u}}^{\top}e_{1})({\mathbf{u}}^{\top}e_{1})^{\top}{\mathbf{X}})=\mathrm{Tr}(P{\mathbf{X}}).

Example 4.11
.

Assume that
{\mathbf{A}}\sim\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{\alpha,\alpha+\beta}(N)
with
\alpha,\beta>(N-1)/2
, as in Example
3.4
. We have

{\mathbf{A}}_{11}\sim\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{\alpha,\alpha+\beta-\frac{N-1}{2}}(1)
(the classical univariate Beta-prime distribution), as shown, e.g., in
(
ABO
, Section B.2)
. From this, we obtain

\gamma={\mathbb{E}}[\log({\mathbf{A}}_{11})]=\Psi(\alpha)-\Psi\left(\alpha+\beta-\tfrac{N-1}{2}\right),

where
\Psi(x)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\log(\Gamma(x))
is the digamma function. Since
\Psi
is strictly increasing, we conclude that
\gamma<0
.

4.4.
Matrix perpetuities under orthogonal invariance

In this subsection we note that if
{\mathbf{X}}
is a solution to (
3.1
) of size
N\times N
, then sub-matrices
{\mathbf{X}}^{[k]}
of size
k\times k
are themselves solutions to (
3.1
), with truncated
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}
.
Let us note some further properties of the solution to (
3.1
). We start with an analog of Proposition
4.3
.

Lemma 4.12
.

Assume that
({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})
satisfy (
3.3
) and (
3.4
). Let
{\mathbf{X}}
be the unique solution to (
3.1
). If

({\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top},{\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top},{\mathbf{B}})\qquad\text{for every orthogonal matrix }{\mathbf{u}},

then
{\mathbf{X}}
is orthogonally invariant.
In such case,
{\mathbf{X}}
also satisfies

{\mathbf{X}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}m({\mathbf{A}})\,{\mathbf{X}}\,m({\mathbf{A}})^{\top}+{\mathbf{B}},\qquad(m({\mathbf{A}}),{\mathbf{B}})\ \text{and }{\mathbf{X}}\ \text{are independent}.

Proof.

Let
({\mathbf{A}}_{n},{\mathbf{B}}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be iid copies of
({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})
, independent of
{\mathbf{X}}_{0}
, and define a Markov chain

{\mathbf{X}}_{n}={\mathbf{A}}_{n}{\mathbf{X}}_{n-1}{\mathbf{A}}_{n}^{\top}+{\mathbf{B}}_{n},\qquad n\geq 1.

Then, by Theorem
3.1
,

{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathbf{X}},

where
{\mathbf{X}}
is the unique solution to (
3.1
).

Since the map
{\mathbf{a}}\mapsto{\mathbf{a}}^{1/2}
is equivariant under orthogonal conjugation on the cone of symmetric positive definite matrices, for every orthogonal matrix
{\mathbf{u}}
,

{\mathbf{u}}|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}=\bigl({\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{A}}_{n}{\mathbf{A}}_{n}^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\bigr)^{1/2}.

Hence

({\mathbf{u}}|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|{\mathbf{u}}^{\top},{\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{B}}_{n}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|,{\mathbf{B}}_{n}).

Assume that
{\mathbf{X}}_{0}
is orthogonally invariant, e.g.,
{\mathbf{X}}_{0}=0
.
We will show by induction that
{\mathbf{X}}_{n}
is orthogonally invariant for every
n\geq 1
.
Assume now that
{\mathbf{X}}_{n-1}
is orthogonally invariant.
By Lemma
C.3
, there exists a
\sigma({\mathbf{A}}_{n})
-measurable orthogonal matrix
{\mathbf{U}}_{n}
such that

{\mathbf{A}}_{n}=|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|{\mathbf{U}}_{n}.

Because
{\mathbf{X}}_{n-1}
is independent of
({\mathbf{A}}_{n},{\mathbf{B}}_{n})
and
{\mathbf{U}}_{n}
is
\sigma({\mathbf{A}}_{n})
-measurable, by orthogonal invariance of
{\mathbf{X}}_{n-1}
, we obtain

(|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|,{\mathbf{B}}_{n},{\mathbf{U}}_{n}{\mathbf{X}}_{n-1}{\mathbf{U}}_{n}^{\top})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|,{\mathbf{B}}_{n},{\mathbf{X}}_{n-1}).

It follows that

{\mathbf{X}}_{n}={\mathbf{A}}_{n}{\mathbf{X}}_{n-1}{\mathbf{A}}_{n}^{\top}+{\mathbf{B}}_{n}=|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|{\mathbf{U}}_{n}{\mathbf{X}}_{n-1}{\mathbf{U}}_{n}^{\top}|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|+{\mathbf{B}}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}_{n-1}|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|+{\mathbf{B}}_{n}.

Now let
{\mathbf{u}}
be any fixed orthogonal matrix. Using the orthogonal invariance of
{\mathbf{X}}_{n-1}
, the independence of
{\mathbf{X}}_{n-1}
and
(|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|,{\mathbf{B}}_{n})
, and the invariance of
(|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|,{\mathbf{B}}_{n})
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{X}}_{n}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbf{u}}\bigl(|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}_{n-1}|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|+{\mathbf{B}}_{n}\bigr){\mathbf{u}}^{\top}

\displaystyle=({\mathbf{u}}|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|{\mathbf{u}}^{\top})({\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{X}}_{n-1}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top})({\mathbf{u}}|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|{\mathbf{u}}^{\top})+{\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{B}}_{n}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}_{n-1}|{\mathbf{A}}_{n}|+{\mathbf{B}}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbf{X}}_{n}.

Thus
{\mathbf{X}}_{n}
is orthogonally invariant for every
n
.

Since
{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathbf{X}}
, orthogonal invariance passes to the limit, and therefore

{\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbf{X}}\qquad\text{for every orthogonal matrix }{\mathbf{u}}.

We now prove the second assertion. By Lemma
4.2
, there exists a
\sigma({\mathbf{A}})
-measurable orthogonal matrix
{\mathbf{V}}_{{\mathbf{A}}}
such that

{\mathbf{A}}=m({\mathbf{A}}){\mathbf{V}}_{{\mathbf{A}}}.

Since
{\mathbf{X}}
is orthogonally invariant and independent of
({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})
, we have

({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}},{\mathbf{V}}_{{\mathbf{A}}}{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{V}}_{{\mathbf{A}}}^{\top})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}},{\mathbf{X}}).

Using (
3.1
), we conclude that

\displaystyle{\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{B}}=m({\mathbf{A}})\,{\mathbf{V}}_{{\mathbf{A}}}{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{V}}_{{\mathbf{A}}}^{\top}\,m({\mathbf{A}})^{\top}+{\mathbf{B}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}m({\mathbf{A}})\,{\mathbf{X}}\,m({\mathbf{A}})^{\top}+{\mathbf{B}}.

∎

Next we note that if
{\mathbf{X}}
is a perpetuity (i.e. is a solution to (
3.1
)), then the whole family
({\mathbf{X}}^{[k]})_{1\leq k\leq N}
consists of perpetuities.

Lemma 4.13
.

Under assumptions of Lemma
4.12
, for any
1\leq k\leq N
we have

{\mathbf{X}}^{[k]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]})^{1/2}{\mathbf{X}}^{[k]}(({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]})^{1/2}+{\mathbf{B}}^{[k]},\qquad(({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]},{\mathbf{B}}^{[k]})\mbox{ and }{\mathbf{X}}^{[k]}\mbox{ are independent.}

In particular

(4.4)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{X}}_{11}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})_{11}{\mathbf{X}}_{11}+{\mathbf{B}}_{11},\qquad(({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})_{11},{\mathbf{B}}_{11})\mbox{ and }{\mathbf{X}}_{11}\mbox{ are independent}.

Proof.

Let
m({\mathbf{A}})=T_{{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}}
be the lower triangular matrix from the Cholesky decomposition of
{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}=T_{{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}}T_{{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}}^{\top}
. Since
(T{\mathbf{x}}T^{\top})^{[k]}=T^{[k]}{\mathbf{x}}^{[k]}(T^{[k]})^{\top}
for any lower triangular matrix
T
and any matrix
{\mathbf{x}}
, by Lemma
4.12
, we obtain

{\mathbf{X}}^{[k]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}T_{{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}}^{[k]}{\mathbf{X}}^{[k]}(T_{{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}}^{[k]})^{\top}+{\mathbf{B}}^{[k]},\qquad(T_{{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}}^{[k]},{\mathbf{B}}^{[k]})\mbox{ and }{\mathbf{X}}^{[k]}\mbox{ are independent.}

Now, note that
T_{{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}}^{[k]}(T_{{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}}^{[k]})^{\top}=({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]}=T_{({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]}}T_{({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]}}^{\top}
. Therefore
T_{{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}}^{[k]}=T_{({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]}}
.

Since orthogonal invariance of
({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top},{\mathbf{B}})
implies orthogonal invariance of
(({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]},{\mathbf{B}}^{[k]})
, again by Lemma
4.12
, we can replace
T_{{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}}^{[k]}
by
(({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]})^{1/2}
.
∎

Assuming orthogonal invariance, we obtain a simplified version of Theorem
3.1
under particularly simple assumptions.

Corollary 4.14
.

Assume that

({\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top},{\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top},{\mathbf{B}})\qquad\text{for every orthogonal matrix }{\mathbf{u}}.

If

\displaystyle\gamma={\mathbb{E}}[\log({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})_{11}]<0\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log^{+}({\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{B}}^{\top})_{11}\right]<+\infty

then there exists a unique solution to (
3.1
).

Proof.

We verify the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
. By Lemma
4.8
we obtain the representation of the top Lyapunov exponent.

It remains to check the logarithmic moment assumptions in
(
3.3
). Let
\mathbf{S}
be an orthogonally invariant
positive semidefinite random matrix, and let
{\mathbf{U}}
be an independent Haar
orthogonal matrix. If
{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{S}{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbf{S}
,
then repeating the steps from the proof of Lemma
4.8
we obtain (here
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\leq}}
denotes the usual stochastic order)

\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{S}){\mathbf{U}}_{11}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\leq}}\mathbf{S}_{11}\leq\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{S}),

where
{\mathbf{U}}_{11}^{2}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1/2,(N-1)/2)
.
It follows that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log^{+}\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{S})\right]<\infty\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad{\mathbb{E}}\left[\log^{+}\mathbf{S}_{11}\right]<\infty.

Applying this to
\mathbf{S}={\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}
and
\mathbf{S}={\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}
, we see
that (
3.3
) is equivalent to

{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\log^{+}({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})_{11}\right]<\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\log^{+}({\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{B}}^{\top})_{11}\right]<\infty.

The second condition is assumed. The first one follows from
\gamma<0
.

Thus all assumptions of Theorem
3.1
are satisfied, and
(
3.1
) admits a unique solution.
∎

5.
Tails of matrix perpetuities

The problem of studying tail asymptotics for solutions of multivariate affine fixed-point equations has a long history.

One of the most celebrated results in this area is Kesten’s theorem. Consider the vector perpetuity equation

\displaystyle\underline{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}\underline{X}+\underline{B},\qquad(\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}},\underline{B})\ \text{and}\ \underline{X}\ \text{are independent},

where
\underline{X},\underline{B}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
and
\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}\in\mathrm{Mat}(N)
.

For several variants of Kesten’s theorem, see
(
BDM
, Section 4.4)
as well as
Kes73
;
GLP16
. A key ingredient common to these variants is the function
\tilde{h}
defined by

\tilde{h}(s)=\inf_{n\geq 1}{\mathbb{E}}[\|\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{1}\cdots\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{n}\|^{s}]^{1/n},

where
(\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are iid copies of
\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}
.

While the precise conclusions of Kesten’s theorem depend on the specific setting (see, e.g.,
(
BDM
, Theorems 4.4.5, 4.4.15, 4.4.18)
), they share a common theme:
X
exhibits power-law tails of order
t^{-\eta}
, where
\eta>0
is the unique solution of
\tilde{h}(\eta)=1
. For instance, Guivarc’h and Le Page showed that, under additional assumptions (see
(
GLP16
, Theorem 5.2)
or
(
BDM
, Theorem 4.4.18)
), there exists a nonzero Radon measure
\mu
on
({\mathbb{R}}\cup\{-\infty,+\infty\})^{N}\setminus\{0\}
such that

t^{\eta}\,{\mathbb{P}}\left(t^{-1}\underline{X}\in\cdot\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu,\qquad t\to+\infty,

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\longrightarrow}}
denotes the vague convergence.

Our interest, however, is not in the asymptotic behavior of
{\mathbb{P}}(\underline{X}\in\cdot)
, but rather in the tail asymptotics of the expected empirical spectral distribution
\mu_{\mathbf{X}}(\cdot)
(recall Definition
2.3
). We assume orthogonal invariance of the law of
({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})
, which is natural in our framework and considerably simplifies the analysis. In our framework condition
\tilde{h}(\eta)=1
is replaced by the much simpler moment condition
{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbf{A}}_{11}^{\eta}]=1
. In addition, our approach yields tail asymptotics for
\mu_{{\mathbf{X}}}
, as well as for
{\mathbb{P}}_{\lambda_{\max}({\mathbf{X}})}
and
{\mathbb{P}}_{{\mathbf{X}}_{11}}
, with explicit positive constants.

Theorem 5.1
.

Assume that
N\geq 2
and

(i)

{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathrm{Sym}_{\geq 0}(N)
a.s.,

(ii)

({\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top},{\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})
for any orthogonal matrix
{\mathbf{u}}
,

(iii)

there exists
\eta>0
such that

(a)

{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbf{A}}_{11}^{\eta}]=1
,

(b)

{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbf{A}}_{11}^{\eta}\log^{+}({\mathbf{A}}_{11})]<\infty
,

(c)

{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbf{B}}_{11}^{\eta}]<\infty
,

(d)

{\mathbb{E}}[\det({\mathbf{A}}^{[2]})^{\eta/2}]<1
,

(iv)

the conditional law of
\log{\mathbf{A}}_{11}
given
\{{\mathbf{A}}_{11}\neq 0\}
is non-arithmetic,

(v)

{\mathbb{P}}({\mathbf{A}}_{11}x+{\mathbf{B}}_{11}=x)<1
for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Then, there exists a unique solution
{\mathbf{X}}
to

(5.1)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{X}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbf{A}}^{1/2}{\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{A}}^{1/2}+{\mathbf{B}},\qquad({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})\mbox{ and }{\mathbf{X}}\mbox{ are independent},

which satisfies

\mu_{\mathbf{X}}\big((t,\infty)\big)\sim\frac{1}{N}{\mathbb{P}}(\lambda_{\max}({\mathbf{X}})>t)\sim\frac{1}{N}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma\left(\eta+\dfrac{N}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\dfrac{N}{2}\right)\,\Gamma\left(\eta+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}{\mathbb{P}}({\mathbf{X}}_{11}>t)

and

(5.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}t^{\eta}\,{\mathbb{P}}({\mathbf{X}}_{11}>t)=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[({\mathbf{A}}_{11}{\mathbf{X}}_{11}+{\mathbf{B}}_{11})^{\eta}-({\mathbf{A}}_{11}{\mathbf{X}}_{11})^{\eta}\right]}{\eta\,{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbf{A}}_{11}^{\eta}\log{\mathbf{A}}_{11}]}\in(0,+\infty).

The proof of Theorem
5.1
is given in Section
5.2
. It relies on several auxiliary lemmas, which are stated and proved in Section
5.1
.

5.1.
Auxiliary lemmas

For a symmetric matrix
{\mathbf{a}}
, let

\lambda_{1}({\mathbf{a}})\geq\lambda_{2}({\mathbf{a}})\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{N}({\mathbf{a}})

denote its eigenvalues in non-increasing order.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
({\mathbf{A}}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be iid copies of
{\mathbf{A}}
and set

\Pi_{n}:={\mathbf{A}}_{n}\cdots{\mathbf{A}}_{1},\qquad{\bf M}_{n}:=\Pi_{n}\Pi_{n}^{\top}\in\mathrm{Sym}_{\geq 0}(N).

Fix
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and define

(5.3)

\displaystyle h_{k}(s):=\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}({\bf M}_{n})^{s/k}\right]^{1/n}

for nonnegative
s
such that
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{s/k}\right]<\infty
.

Remark 5.3
.

For each
k
the quantity

\lambda_{[1:k]}({\bf m}):=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}({\bf m})

agrees with

\lambda_{\max}(\wedge^{k}{\bf m}),

where
\wedge^{k}
denotes the
k
th exterior power. We will not make explicit use of exterior powers in the statements or proofs below, but this identification implies in particular that
\lambda_{[1:k]}
is submultiplicative. This justifies in particular why
h_{k}(s)
is well defined provided that
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{s/k}\right]<\infty
.

Our results will only use
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
, but we consider general
h_{k}
for completeness.

Lemma 5.4
.

Assume that
{\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\,{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}
for every orthogonal matrix
{\mathbf{u}}
. Fix
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and let
s>0
be such that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{s/k}\right]<\infty
.
Then

(5.4)

\displaystyle h_{k}(s)={\mathbb{E}}\left[\det\big(({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]}\big)^{s/k}\right].

Proof.

Let
n\geq 1
. By Corollary
4.5
iterated
along the product, one has the distributional identity

(5.5)

\displaystyle({\bf M}_{n})^{[k]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\ \big(\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{n}^{[k]}\big)^{1/2}\cdots\big(\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{2}^{[k]}\big)^{1/2}(\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{1}^{[k]})\big(\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{2}^{[k]}\big)^{1/2}\cdots\big(\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{n}^{[k]}\big)^{1/2},

where
\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{i}:={\mathbf{A}}_{i}{\mathbf{A}}_{i}^{\top}
.
Taking determinants on both sides gives

(5.6)

\displaystyle\det\big({\bf M}_{n}^{[k]}\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\det\big(\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{i}^{[k]}\big).

By the Cauchy interlacing inequalities, we have for any
{\bf m}\in\mathrm{Sym}(N)
and
i=1,\ldots,k
,

\lambda_{i}({\bf m})\geq\lambda_{i}({\bf m}^{[k]}).

Thus, since
{\bf M}_{n}
is positive semidefinite, we obtain

\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}({\bf M}_{n})\geq\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}({\bf M}_{n}^{[k]})=\det\big({\bf M}_{n}^{[k]}\big).

Therefore, by (
5.6
) and independence,

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}({\bf M}_{n})^{s/k}\right]\geq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\det\big(\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}^{[k]}\big)^{s/k}\right]^{n}={\mathbb{E}}\left[\det\big(({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]}\big)^{s/k}\right]^{n}.

Taking
1/n
-th powers and then the infimum over
n
yields

(5.7)

\displaystyle h_{k}(s)\geq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\det\big(({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]}\big)^{s/k}\right].

For the reverse inequality, observe that for any positive semidefinite matrix
{\bf m}
,

(5.8)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}({\bf m})\leq e_{k}({\bf m})=\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{k}\leq N}\det\bigl({\bf m}^{[j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}]}\bigr),

where
e_{k}({\bf m})
denotes the
k
-th elementary symmetric polynomial in the eigenvalues of
{\bf m}
, and
{\bf m}^{[j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}]}
denotes the principal
k\times k
submatrix of
{\bf m}
indexed by
\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\}
,
(
MAnal
, Theorem 1.2.16)
. We have

\Big(\sum_{r=1}^{D}x_{r}\Big)^{s}\leq c(D,s)\sum_{r=1}^{D}x_{r}^{s},\qquad x_{r}\geq 0,

for a constant
c(D,s)\in(0,\infty)
. In our case we set
D:=\binom{N}{k}
and we have

\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}({\bf M}_{n})^{s/k}\leq c(D,s/k)\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{k}\leq N}\det\bigl({\bf M}_{n}^{[j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}]}\bigr)^{s/k}.

By orthogonal invariance of
{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}
, the law of
{\bf M}_{n}=\Pi_{n}\Pi_{n}^{\top}
is orthogonally invariant.
In particular, the distribution of
{\bf M}_{n}^{[j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}]}
does not depend on the choice of
\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\}
. Thus,

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}({\bf M}_{n})^{s/k}\right]\leq c(D,s/k)\,D\ {\mathbb{E}}\left[\det\big({\bf M}_{n}^{[k]}\big)^{s/k}\right]=c(D,s/k)\,D\ {\mathbb{E}}\left[\det\big(({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]}\big)^{s/k}\right]^{n},

where the last equality follows from (
5.6
).
Therefore,

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}({\bf M}_{n})^{s/k}\right]^{1/n}\leq\big(c(D,s/k)\,D\big)^{1/n}\ {\mathbb{E}}\left[\det\big(({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]}\big)^{s/k}\right].

Letting
n\to\infty
gives

(5.9)

\displaystyle h_{k}(s)\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\det\big(({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{[k]}\big)^{s/k}\right].

Combining (
5.7
) and (
5.9
) yields (
5.4
).
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
{\mathbf{X}}
be a random matrix satisfying

(5.10)

{\mathbf{X}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbf{A}}\,{\mathbf{X}}\,{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{B}},\qquad({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})\ \text{independent of }{\mathbf{X}},

where
{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathrm{Sym}_{\geq 0}(N)
a.s.

Assume that there exists
p>0
such that

{\mathbb{E}}[\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{p}]<\infty,\quad{\mathbb{E}}\big[\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{B}})^{p}\big]<\infty\quad\mbox{and}\quad h_{1}(p)<1.

Then

{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\lambda_{1}({\mathbf{X}})^{p}\Big]<\infty.

Proof.

The result follows from
(
BDM
, Remark 4.4.3)
, which establishes that vector

\mathrm{vec}({\mathbf{X}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}({\mathbf{A}}\otimes{\mathbf{A}})\mathrm{vec}({\mathbf{X}})+\mathrm{vec}({\mathbf{B}}),

has
p
-th moment finite under the assumptions

\tilde{h}(p)<1\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\mathbb{E}}[|\mathrm{vec}({\mathbf{B}})|^{p}]<\infty,

where

\tilde{h}(p)=\inf_{n\geq 1}{\mathbb{E}}[\|\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{1}\cdots\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{n}\|^{p}]^{1/n},\qquad\tilde{{\mathbf{A}}}_{i}=({\mathbf{A}}_{i}\otimes{\mathbf{A}}_{i}).

We have
h_{1}(p)=\tilde{h}(p)
, cf. the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
∎

Let
e_{2}({\mathbf{x}})
be elementary symmetric polynomial in the eigenvalues of
{\mathbf{x}}
, i.e.,

(5.11)

e_{2}({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{x}})^{2}-\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{x}}^{2})\Big).

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathrm{Sym}_{\geq 0}(N)
and
{\bf m}\in\mathrm{Mat}(N)
,
N\geq 2
, the following deterministic inequalities hold:

(5.12)

0\leq e_{2}({\mathbf{x}})\leq\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{x}})^{2},

(5.13)

e_{2}({\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{y}})\leq e_{2}({\mathbf{x}})+e_{2}({\mathbf{y}})+\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{x}})\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{y}}),

(5.14)

\mathrm{Tr}({\bf m}{\mathbf{x}}{\bf m}^{\top})\leq\lambda_{1}({\bf m}{\bf m}^{\top})\,\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{x}}),

(5.15)

e_{2}({\bf m}{\mathbf{x}}{\bf m}^{\top})\leq\lambda_{[1:2]}({\bf m}{\bf m}^{\top})\,e_{2}({\mathbf{x}}).

Proof.

Eq. (
5.12
) is obvious. For (
5.13
), use (
5.11
) and
\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{y}})\geq 0
,

e_{2}({\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{y}})=\frac{1}{2}\Big((\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{x}})+\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{y}}))^{2}-\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{x}}^{2})-\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{y}}^{2})-2\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{y}})\Big)\leq e_{2}({\mathbf{x}})+e_{2}({\mathbf{y}})+\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{x}})\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{y}}).

(
5.14
) is standard. For (
5.15
), let
\mathbf{m}={\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}
be a singular value decomposition, with

{\mathbf{a}}=\operatorname{diag}(a_{1},\dots,a_{N}),\qquad a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 0,

and set
{\bf z}={\mathbf{v}}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{v}}
. Since
{\mathbf{x}}\geq 0
, we also have
{\bf z}\geq 0
. By orthogonal invariance of
e_{2}
,

e_{2}(\mathbf{m}{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{m}^{\top})=e_{2}({\mathbf{a}}{\bf z}{\mathbf{a}}).

Using the identity (recall (
5.8
))

e_{2}({\bf m})=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\det\bigl({\bf m}^{[i,j]}\bigr),

we obtain

e_{2}({\mathbf{a}}{\bf z}{\mathbf{a}})=\sum_{i<j}\det\bigl(({\mathbf{a}}{\bf z}{\mathbf{a}})^{[i,j]}\bigr)=\sum_{i<j}a_{i}^{2}a_{j}^{2}\det\bigl({\bf z}^{[i,j]}\bigr).

Since
{\bf z}\geq 0
, all principal
2\times 2
minors are nonnegative, and therefore

e_{2}(\mathbf{m}{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{m}^{\top})\leq a_{1}^{2}a_{2}^{2}\sum_{i<j}\det\bigl({\bf z}^{[i,j]}\bigr)=a_{1}^{2}a_{2}^{2}\,e_{2}({\bf z}).

Finally,
e_{2}({\bf z})=e_{2}({\mathbf{x}})
and

a_{1}^{2}=\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{m}\mathbf{m}^{\top}),\qquad a_{2}^{2}=\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{m}\mathbf{m}^{\top}).

∎

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
{\mathbf{X}}
be a
\mathrm{Sym}_{\geq 0}(N)
-valued random matrix satisfying (
5.10
) with
{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathrm{Sym}_{\geq 0}(N)
a.s.

Assume that there exists
p>0
such that

{\mathbb{E}}[\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{2p}]<\infty,\quad{\mathbb{E}}\big[\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{B}})^{2p}\big]<\infty,

h_{1}(p)<1\qquad\text{and}\qquad h_{2}(2p)<1.

Then

{\mathbb{E}}\Big[(\lambda_{1}({\mathbf{X}})\lambda_{2}({\mathbf{X}}))^{p}\Big]<\infty.

Proof.

For
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathrm{Sym}_{\geq 0}(N)
, we have

\lambda_{1}({\mathbf{x}})\lambda_{2}({\mathbf{x}})\leq e_{2}({\mathbf{x}}):=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\lambda_{i}({\mathbf{x}})\lambda_{j}({\mathbf{x}}),

hence it suffices to prove

(5.16)

{\mathbb{E}}\big[e_{2}({\mathbf{X}})^{p}\big]<\infty.

Let
({\mathbf{A}}_{n},{\mathbf{B}}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be iid copies of
({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}})
and set

\Pi_{n}:={\mathbf{A}}_{n}\cdots{\mathbf{A}}_{1},\qquad{\bf M}_{n}:=\Pi_{n}\Pi_{n}^{\top}\in\mathrm{Sym}_{\geq 0}(N).

For
m\geq 1
define

{\bf C}_{m}:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Pi_{j-1}{\mathbf{B}}_{j}\Pi_{j-1}^{\top}\in\mathrm{Sym}_{\geq 0}(N),\qquad\Pi_{0}:=I.

Iterating (
5.10
) yields

(5.17)

{\mathbf{X}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\Pi_{m}\,{\mathbf{X}}\,\Pi_{m}^{\top}+{\bf C}_{m},

where
(\Pi_{m},{\bf C}_{m})
is independent of
{\mathbf{X}}
.
Denote the right-hand side of (
5.17
) by
{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}
. Apply (
5.13
) to
{\mathbf{x}}=\Pi_{m}{\mathbf{X}}\Pi_{m}^{\top}
and
{\mathbf{y}}={\bf C}_{m}
, then use
(
5.15
) and (
5.14
) with
{\bf m}=\Pi_{m}
.
Writing
{\bf M}_{m}=\Pi_{m}\Pi_{m}^{\top}
, we obtain

(5.18)

e_{2}({\mathbf{X}}^{\prime})\leq\lambda_{[1:2]}({\bf M}_{m})\,e_{2}({\mathbf{X}})+e_{2}({\bf C}_{m})+\lambda_{1}({\bf M}_{m})\,\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{X}})\,\mathrm{Tr}({\bf C}_{m}).

Let

c_{p}:=\begin{cases}1,&0<p\leq 1,\\
3^{p-1},&p\geq 1,\end{cases}\qquad\text{so that}\qquad(x+y+z)^{p}\leq c_{p}(x^{p}+y^{p}+z^{p})\ \ \forall\,x,y,z\geq 0.

Raising (
5.18
) to the power
p
, applying truncation
\min\{\cdot,K\}
and taking expectations on both sides gives for positive
K
,

(5.19)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}[\min\{e_{2}({\mathbf{X}})^{p},K\}]\leq c_{p}\Big(&{\mathbb{E}}[\lambda_{[1:2]}({\bf M}_{m})^{p}]{\mathbb{E}}[\min\{e_{2}({\mathbf{X}})^{p},K\}]+{\mathbb{E}}[e_{2}({\bf C}_{m})^{p}]\\

Lemma 5.8

Moreover, as in the matrix case, we have

\tau({\mathbb{X}}-{\mathbb{X}}_{L})=\sum_{k=L+1}^{\infty}\tau(\Pi_{k-1}{\mathbb{B}}_{k}\Pi_{k-1}^{\ast})=\frac{\tau({\mathbb{B}})}{1-\tau({\mathbb{A}})}\,\tau({\mathbb{A}})^{L}.

By construction
{\mathbb{X}}
satisfies (
6.5
), and by
Theorem
2.4
its distribution is uniquely determined.

For every
L\geq 1
, let

\mu_{N}=\mu_{{\mathbf{X}}^{(N)}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mu_{N,L}:=\mu_{{\mathbf{X}}_{L}^{(N)}},

be the expected spectral distributions of
{\mathbf{X}}^{(N)}
and
{\mathbf{X}}_{L}^{(N)}
respectively.
Of course
{\mathbf{X}}^{(N)}_{L}
is a noncommutative polynomial in
({{\mathbf{A}}}_{1}^{(N)})^{1/2},{\mathbf{B}}_{1}^{(N)},\ldots,({{\mathbf{A}}}_{L}^{(N)})^{1/2},{\mathbf{B}}_{L}^{(N)}
.

Observe that assumption
(iii)
implies that moments of
\mu_{{\mathbf{X}}_{L}^{(N)}}
converge to the respective moments of
\mu_{{\mathbb{X}}_{L}}
, and since
\mu_{{\mathbb{X}}_{L}}
is compactly supported by definition (as a polynomial in bounded operators) we get

(6.9)

\mu_{N,L}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu_{{\mathbb{X}}_{L}}\quad\mbox{as }N\to\infty.

For
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
define

m_{N}(z):=\int\frac{1}{x-z}\,\mu_{N}(\mathrm{d}x)={\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{X}}^{(N)}-z{\mathbf{id}}_{N})^{-1}\right],

m_{N,L}(z):=\int\frac{1}{x-z}\,\mu_{N,L}(\mathrm{d}x)={\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{X}}^{(N)}_{L}-z{\mathbf{id}}_{N})^{-1}\right],

and

m(z):=\tau(({\mathbb{X}}-z)^{-1}),\qquad m_{L}(z):=\tau(({\mathbb{X}}_{L}-z)^{-1}).

By (
6.9
), we have for fixed
L
,

(6.10)

m_{N,L}(z)\longrightarrow m_{L}(z)\qquad\mbox{as }N\to\infty.

For any matrices
{\bf a},{\bf b}
, we have

({\bf a}-z{\mathbf{id}}_{N})^{-1}-({\bf b}-z{\mathbf{id}}_{N})^{-1}=({\bf a}-z{\mathbf{id}}_{N})^{-1}({\bf b}-{\bf a})({\bf b}-z{\mathbf{id}}_{N})^{-1}.

using (see for example chapter A.3 in
AGZBook
)
|\mathrm{Tr}({\bf a})|\leq\lVert{\bf a}\rVert_{1}
and
\lVert{\bf ab}\rVert_{1}\leq\lVert{\bf a}\rVert_{\infty}\rVert{\bf b}\rVert_{1}
holding for any two matrices
{\bf a},{\bf b}
we have

\left|\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{Tr}({\bf a}-z{\mathbf{id}}_{N})^{-1}-\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{Tr}({\bf b}-z{\mathbf{id}}_{N})^{-1}\right|\leq\frac{1}{(\Im z)^{2}}\,\frac{1}{N}\|{\bf a}-{\bf b}\|_{1}.

Applying this with
({\bf a},{\bf b})=({\mathbf{X}}^{(N)},{\mathbf{X}}^{(N)}_{L})
, taking expectation and using that

{\mathbf{X}}^{(N)}-{\mathbf{X}}^{(N)}_{L}\geq 0
, we obtain

(6.11)

|m_{N}(z)-m_{N,L}(z)|\leq\frac{1}{(\Im z)^{2}}\,{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{Tr}({\mathbf{X}}^{(N)}-{\mathbf{X}}^{(N)}_{L})\right]\leq\frac{\beta_{\ast}}{(1-\alpha_{\ast})(\Im z)^{2}}\,\alpha_{\ast}^{L}

by (
6.6
).

Likewise, since
{\mathbb{X}}-{\mathbb{X}}_{L}\geq 0
,

(6.12)

|m(z)-m_{L}(z)|\leq\frac{1}{(\Im z)^{2}}\,\tau({\mathbb{X}}-{\mathbb{X}}_{L})=\frac{\tau({\mathbb{B}})}{(1-\tau({\mathbb{A}}))(\Im z)^{2}}\,\tau({\mathbb{A}})^{L}

by (
6.8
).

For every fixed
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
we have

|m_{N}(z)-m(z)|\leq|m_{N}(z)-m_{N,L}(z)|+|m_{N,L}(z)-m_{L}(z)|+|m_{L}(z)-m(z)|.

Hence using (
6.10
), (
6.11
), and
(
6.12
), we get

\limsup_{N\to\infty}|m_{N}(z)-m(z)|\leq\frac{\beta_{\ast}}{(1-\alpha_{\ast})(\Im z)^{2}}\,\alpha_{\ast}^{L}+\frac{\tau({\mathbb{B}})}{(1-\tau({\mathbb{A}}))(\Im z)^{2}}\,\tau({\mathbb{A}})^{L}.

Letting
L\to\infty
yields

m_{N}(z)\longrightarrow m(z)\qquad\text{for every }z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}.

The weak convergence of the expected empirical spectral
distribution of
{\mathbf{X}}^{(N)}
follows.
∎

In
FreePerp
, we studied tail asymptotics of free perpetuities. In particular, we showed that in the critical case
\tau({\mathbb{A}})=1
, with
\tau({\mathbb{A}}^{2})<\infty
, and under additional technical assumptions, the perpetuity
{\mathbb{X}}
exists and has universal tail asymptotics:

(6.13)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}t^{1/2}\mu_{{\mathbb{X}}}\big((t,+\infty)\big)=\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\tau({\mathbb{B}})}{\mathrm{Var}({\mathbb{A}})}}.

Theorem

We emphasize, however, that there is no a priori reason to expect the tail exponents of finite-dimensional matrix perpetuities to converge to the tail exponent of the limiting expected empirical spectral distribution.

Appendix A
Weak convergence of the spectral distribution of matrix Beta prime distribution

Let
\mathrm{Herm}^{1}(N)=\mathrm{Sym}(N,\mathbb{R})
and
\mathrm{Herm}^{2}(N)=\mathrm{Herm}(N,\mathbb{C})
denote the space of
N\times N
symmetric real and complex Hermitian matrices, respectively.

Let
d_{N}=N^{-1}\dim\mathrm{Herm}^{d}(N)=1+d(N-1)/2
.
The matrix beta-prime distribution with parameters
\alpha,\beta>d_{N}-1
on the cone of positive definite matrices
\mathrm{Herm}^{d}_{+}(N)=\{M\in\mathrm{Herm}^{d}(N)\colon M>0\}
,
d=1,2
, is defined by its density

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathrm{Herm}^{d}_{+}(N))(\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}})\propto\frac{\det({\mathbf{x}})^{\alpha-d_{N}}}{\det({\mathbf{id}}_{N}+\mathbf{x})^{\alpha+\beta}}I_{\mathrm{Herm}_{+}^{d}(N)}({\mathbf{x}})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}

where
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
is the Euclidean measure on
\mathrm{Herm}^{d}(N)
.

Consider
(\alpha,\beta)=(\alpha_{N},\beta_{N})
satisfying

(A.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{\alpha_{N}}{N}=\frac{a\,d}{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{\beta_{N}}{N}=\frac{b\,d}{2}.

Since the distribution of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
is invariant under the action of orthogonal (
d=1
) or unitary (
d=2
) groups, the eigenvalue distribution of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{\alpha_{N},\beta_{N}}(\mathrm{Herm}_{+}^{d}(N))
,
a\geq 1
and
b>1
, has density proportional to

(A.2)

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{d\,N}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}V_{N}(\lambda_{k})\right)\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})^{d}I_{\lambda_{1}>\ldots>\lambda_{N}},

where

V_{N}(x)=\frac{\alpha_{N}+\beta_{N}}{dN/2}\log(1+x)-\left(\frac{\alpha_{N}}{dN/2}-\frac{d_{N}}{Nd/2}\right)\log(x)

for
x>0
and
V_{N}(x)=+\infty
for
x\leq 0
. As
N\to+\infty
we have
V_{N}(x)\to V(x)
,
x>0
, where

(A.3)

\displaystyle V(x)=(a+b)\log(1+x)-(a-1)\log(x).

Lemma A.1
.

Let
a\geq 1
and
b>1
and assume that
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})
has density given by (
A.2
). Then, the (random) probability measure

\hat{\mu}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\delta_{\lambda_{k}}

converges weakly a.s. to the unique non-random minimizer of

E_{V}\colon\mathcal{M}_{c}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
, where

(A.4)

\displaystyle E_{V}(\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\log\left(\tfrac{1}{|x-y|}\right)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\mu(\mathrm{d}y)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}V(x)\mu(\mathrm{d}x),

where
V
is defined in (
A.3
)
and
\mathcal{M}_{c}
is the set of probability measures on
{\mathbb{R}}
with compact support.

Remark A.2
.

Note that the pushforward measure of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathrm{Herm}^{d}_{+}(N))
by the mapping
{\mathbf{x}}\mapsto{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}
is
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{\beta,\alpha}(\mathrm{Herm}^{d}_{+}(N))
. Therefore, Lemma
A.1
also holds when
a>1
and
b\geq 1
.

Proof of Lemma
A.1
.

Since
(V_{N})_{N\geq 1}
is not a constant sequence, we will use the results of
Feral
, more precisely, we check the assumptions of
(
Feral
, Theorem 2.1)
.

We observe that
V_{N}\colon(0,+\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
is continuous for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
and that convergence
V_{N}\to V
is uniform on compact subsets of
(0,+\infty)
. By (
A.1
),
\beta_{N}/(dN)>1/2
for large
N
. Thus, for large
N
, we have

\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{V_{N}(x)}{\log(1+x^{2})}=\frac{\beta_{N}+d_{N}}{dN}>\frac{1}{2}+\frac{d_{N}}{dN}=1+\frac{1-d/2}{dN}\geq 1.

Thus, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that for sufficiently large
N
and
T
one has

V_{N}(x)\geq(1+\varepsilon)\log(1+x^{2}),\qquad x\geq T.

If
(V_{N})_{N\geq 1}
satisfied these conditions on
{\mathbb{R}}
, then the result would follow directly from
(
Feral
, Theorem 2.1)
. The second paragraph after the formulation of
(
Feral
, Theorem 2.1)
states that this result remains true if one restricts the potential to the positive half-line as in our case.
∎

Remark A.3
.

If
(\lambda_{k})_{k=1}^{N}
are the eigenvalues of a matrix
{\mathbf{Y}}^{(N)}
, then expected measure of
\hat{\mu}_{N}
defined in Lemma

, that is,

Lemma A.4
.

If
a\geq 1
and
b>1
, then
E_{V}
defined in (
A.4
) is minimized by
\mu_{a,b}^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}x)=f_{a,b}(x)\mathrm{d}x
, where
f_{a,b}
is given in (
6.2
).

Proof.

The measure
\mu_{a,b}^{\ast}
coincides with the free Beta prime distribution defined in
Yoshida

(
Yoshida
, Proposition 3.2)
, its Cauchy transform for
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\colon\mathrm{Im}\,z>0\}
equals

\displaystyle G(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{(b+1)z+(1-a)-\sqrt{(b-1)^{2}z^{2}-2(ab+a+b-1)z+(a-1)^{2}}}{2z(1+z)},

\displaystyle=\frac{(b+1)z+(1-a)-(b-1)\sqrt{(z-\gamma_{-})(z-\gamma_{+})}}{2z(1+z)},

where the branch of the square root is chosen so that
\lim_{z\to+\infty}G(z)=0
.
It is a standard result that from the Cauchy transform one can obtain the Hilbert transform of
f_{a,b}
, see e.g.
(
HP00
, Chapter 3.1)
. We get (we drop the usual
\pi^{-1}
factor in the definition of the Hilbert transform

)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(f_{a,b})(y)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f_{a,b}(x)}{y-x}\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\mathrm{Re}\,G(y+i\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{(b+1)y+(1-a)+(b-1)\sqrt{(\gamma_{-}-y)(\gamma_{+}-y)}}{2y(1+y)},&y\in(0,\gamma_{-}),\\
\frac{1}{2}V^{\prime}(y),&y\in[\gamma_{-},\gamma_{+}],\\
\frac{(b+1)y+(1-a)-(b-1)\sqrt{(y-\gamma_{-})(y-\gamma_{+})}}{2y(1+y)},&y>\gamma_{+},\end{cases}

where the case
y\in[\gamma_{-},\gamma_{+}]
follows from
(
Yoshida
, Section 4.3 (i))
and the case
y\in(0,+\infty)\setminus[\gamma_{-},\gamma_{+}]
follows from analytic continuation of
G
. Moreover, we have

\mathcal{H}(f_{a,b})(y)<\frac{(b+1)y+(1-a)}{2y(1+y)}=\frac{1}{2}V^{\prime}(y),\qquad y>\gamma_{+}

and similarly
\mathcal{H}(f_{a,b})(y)>\frac{1}{2}V^{\prime}(y)
for
y\in(0,\gamma_{-})
. We note that
\gamma_{-}>0
if and only if

.

Let
U_{\mu^{\ast}}(y)=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\log(|x-y|)\mu^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}x)
. By
(
ST97
, Theorem 3.1)
,
the unique compactly supported minimizer
\mu^{\ast}
of
E_{V}
is characterized by the following two conditions: there exists
C\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

(A.5)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}U_{\mu^{\ast}}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}V(x)+C,&x\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu^{\ast}),\\
U_{\mu^{\ast}}(x)\geq-\frac{1}{2}V(x)+C,&x\notin\mathrm{supp}(\mu^{\ast}).\end{cases}

We will show that these conditions are satisfied for
\mu^{\ast}=\mu^{\ast}_{a,b}
. Let
U=U_{\mu^{\ast}_{a,b}}
.
We have for all

(A.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}U(y)-U(x)&=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\log\left|\frac{x-t}{y-t}\right|f_{a,b}(t)\mathrm{d}t=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\mathcal{H}(I_{[x,y]})(t)f_{a,b}(t)\mathrm{d}t\\
&=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}I_{[x,y]}(t)\mathcal{H}(f_{a,b})(t)\mathrm{d}t=-\int_{x}^{y}\mathcal{H}(f_{a,b})(t)\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

where we have used the fact that Hilbert transform is skew-adjoint operator relative to the duality pairing between

and
L^{q}({\mathbb{R}})

p>1

f_{1,b}\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}})

p\in(1,2)

If
x<0

V(x)=+\infty

A.6

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}V(x)+\frac{1}{2}V(\gamma_{-})+U(\gamma_{-}),\qquad x\in[\gamma_{-},\gamma_{+}]

\displaystyle U(x)

Since

Appendix B
Submatrices of Beta prime distribution

Lemma B.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}\sim\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}(N)
with
\alpha,\beta>d_{N}-1=(N-1)/2
. Then, for every
k=1,\dots,N
,

{\mathbf{A}}^{[k]}\sim\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta-(N-k)/2}(k).

Proof.

Let
{\mathbf{X}}\sim{\mathcal{W}}_{\alpha,I_{N}}(N)
and
{\mathbf{Y}}\sim\mathcal{W}_{\beta,{\mathbf{id}}_{N}}(N)
be independent (recall definition of Wishart distribution in (
3.9
)). By the standard Wishart representation of the matrix beta-prime law,

{\mathbf{A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\mathbf{X}}^{1/2}{\mathbf{Y}}^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{1/2}.

Since
{\mathbf{Y}}^{-1}
is orthogonally invariant, Corollary
4.5
gives

(B.1)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{A}}^{[k]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\bigl({\mathbf{X}}^{[k]}\bigr)^{1/2}\,({\mathbf{Y}}^{-1})^{[k]}\,\bigl({\mathbf{X}}^{[k]}\bigr)^{1/2}.

Also, it is a standard fact that
{\mathbf{X}}^{[k]}\sim{\mathcal{W}}_{\alpha,{\mathbf{id}}_{k}}(k)
.

It remains to identify the law of
({\mathbf{Y}}^{-1})^{[k]}
. Write

{\mathbf{Y}}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{Y}}_{11}&{\mathbf{Y}}_{12}\\
{\mathbf{Y}}_{21}&{\mathbf{Y}}_{22}\end{pmatrix},\qquad{\mathbf{Y}}_{11}={\mathbf{Y}}^{[k]}.

By the block inversion formula,

({\mathbf{Y}}^{-1})^{[k]}=({\mathbf{Y}}_{1\cdot 2})^{-1},\qquad{\mathbf{Y}}_{1\cdot 2}:={\mathbf{Y}}_{11}-{\mathbf{Y}}_{12}{\mathbf{Y}}_{22}^{-1}{\mathbf{Y}}_{21}.

Now apply
(
MassamWesolowski06
, Proposition 3.2)

to
{\mathbf{Y}}
, with block sizes
r=k
and
s=N-k
, and
c={\mathbf{id}}_{N}
. It yields (recall the definition of matrix GIG distribution in (
3.10
))

({\mathbf{Y}}_{22},{\mathbf{Y}}_{1\cdot 2})\mid{\mathbf{Y}}_{12}\sim\mathcal{G}_{\cdots}(N-k)\times{\mathcal{W}}_{\beta-\frac{N-k}{2},{\mathbf{id}}_{k}}(k),

where the parameters of the matrix GIG distributions are irrelevant for our purposes, and therefore we omit their specification here.
Since the second factor does not depend on
{\mathbf{Y}}_{12}
, we obtain

(({\mathbf{Y}}^{-1})^{[k]})^{-1}={\mathbf{Y}}_{1\cdot 2}\sim{\mathcal{W}}_{\beta-\frac{N-k}{2},{\mathbf{id}}_{k}}(k).

Therefore, by the same Wishart representation of the beta-prime law on

Appendix C
Measurable polar decomposition

Let
(X,\mathcal{A})
be a measurable space and let
Y
be a topological space. A set-valued map

\Psi\colon X\to 2^{Y}

is called weakly measurable if for every open set
U\subset Y
,

\{x\in X\colon\Psi(x)\cap U\neq\emptyset\}\in\mathcal{A}.

Remark C.1
.

If
Y
is a metric space and
\Psi(x)
is compact for every
x\in X
, then weak measurability is equivalent to the following condition: for every closed set
F\subset Y
,

\{x\in X\colon\Psi(x)\cap F\neq\emptyset\}\in\mathcal{A}.

Indeed, if
F
is closed and
F^{\varepsilon}:=\{y\in Y\colon d(y,F)<\varepsilon\}
, then for compact
\Psi(x)
,

\Psi(x)\cap F\neq\emptyset\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Psi(x)\cap F^{\varepsilon}\neq\emptyset\ \text{ for every }\ \varepsilon>0.

Hence

\{x\colon\Psi(x)\cap F\neq\emptyset\}=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\{x\colon\Psi(x)\cap F^{1/n}\neq\emptyset\},

so measurability for closed sets follows from the definition. Conversely, if
U\subset Y
is open, then

U=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}F_{n},\qquad F_{n}:=\{y\in Y\colon d(y,Y\setminus U)\geq 1/n\},

with each
F_{n}
closed, and therefore

\{x\colon\Psi(x)\cap U\neq\emptyset\}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\{x\colon\Psi(x)\cap F_{n}\neq\emptyset\}.

Theorem C.2

(Kuratowski–Ryll-Nardzewski measurable selection theorem)
.

Let
X
be a standard Borel space, let
Y
be a Polish space, and let

\Psi\colon X\to 2^{Y}

be a set-valued map with nonempty closed values. If
\Psi
is weakly measurable, then there exists a Borel measurable map

f\colon X\to Y

such that

f(x)\in\Psi(x)\qquad\text{for all }x\in X.

Lemma C.3
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a real random matrix, and let

|{\mathbf{A}}|=({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top})^{1/2}.

Then there exists a
\sigma({\mathbf{A}})
-measurable orthogonal matrix
{\mathbf{U}}
such that

{\mathbf{A}}=|{\mathbf{A}}|\,{\mathbf{U}}.

Proof.

Let
O(N)
denote the group of
N\times N
orthogonal matrices. Since
O(N)
is a closed and bounded subset of
\mathrm{Mat}(N)
, it is a compact metric space.

For each
{\bf m}\in\mathrm{Mat}(N)
, define

\Phi({\bf m}):=\{{\mathbf{v}}\in O(N)\colon{\bf m}=|{\bf m}|\,{\mathbf{v}}\}.

By the polar decomposition for real square matrices,
\Phi({\bf m})
is nonempty for every
{\bf m}\in\mathrm{Mat}(N)
.

We next show that each
\Phi({\bf m})
is closed in
O(N)
. Fix
{\bf m}
, and let
({\mathbf{v}}_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be a sequence in
\Phi({\bf m})
such that
{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\to{\mathbf{v}}
in
O(N)
. Since

{\bf m}=|{\bf m}|\,{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\qquad\text{for all }k,

and matrix multiplication is continuous, passing to the limit yields

{\bf m}=|{\bf m}|\,{\mathbf{v}}.

Hence
{\mathbf{v}}\in\Phi({\bf m})
, so
\Phi({\bf m})
is closed. Since
O(N)
is compact, each
\Phi({\bf m})
is in fact compact.

Now consider the graph of
\Phi
:

\mathrm{Graph}(\Phi):=\{({\bf m},{\mathbf{v}})\in\mathrm{Mat}(N)\times O(N)\colon{\bf m}=|{\bf m}|\,{\mathbf{v}}\}.

The map

({\bf m},{\mathbf{v}})\mapsto{\bf m}-|{\bf m}|\,{\mathbf{v}}

is continuous on
\mathrm{Mat}(N)\times O(N)
, because
{\bf m}\mapsto{\bf m}{\bf m}^{\top}
is continuous, the map

{\bf s}\mapsto{\bf s}^{1/2}

is continuous on the cone of symmetric positive semidefinite matrices, and matrix multiplication is continuous. Therefore
\mathrm{Graph}(\Phi)
is closed.

We claim that
\Phi
is weakly measurable. Since
O(N)
is a compact metric space, it is enough to show that for every closed set
F\subset O(N)
, the set

E_{F}:=\{{\bf m}\in\mathrm{Mat}(N)\colon\Phi({\bf m})\cap F\neq\emptyset\}

is Borel. Indeed, if
U\subset O(N)
is open, then

U=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}F_{n},\qquad F_{n}:=\bigl\{{\mathbf{v}}\in O(N)\colon\mathrm{dist}({\mathbf{v}},O(N)\setminus U)\geq 1/n\bigr\},

and each
F_{n}
is closed. Hence

\{{\bf m}\colon\Phi({\bf m})\cap U\neq\emptyset\}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{F_{n}},

so measurability for open sets follows once each
E_{F}
is Borel.

Now fix a closed set
F\subset O(N)
. Then

E_{F}=\pi_{1}\bigl(\mathrm{Graph}(\Phi)\cap(\mathrm{Mat}(N)\times F)\bigr),

where
\pi_{1}\colon\mathrm{Mat}(N)\times O(N)\to\mathrm{Mat}(N)
is the projection onto the first coordinate. Since
\mathrm{Graph}(\Phi)
is closed and
F
is closed in the compact space
O(N)
, the set

C_{F}:=\mathrm{Graph}(\Phi)\cap(\mathrm{Mat}(N)\times F)

is closed in
\mathrm{Mat}(N)\times O(N)
.

We show that
\pi_{1}(C_{F})
is closed. Let
({\bf m}_{k})
be a sequence in
\pi_{1}(C_{F})
such that
{\bf m}_{k}\to{\bf m}
in
\mathrm{Mat}(N)
. For each
k
there exists
{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\in F
with
({\bf m}_{k},{\mathbf{v}}_{k})\in C_{F}
. Since
F
is compact, after passing to a subsequence we may assume that
{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\to{\mathbf{v}}\in F
. Then

({\bf m}_{k},{\mathbf{v}}_{k})\to({\bf m},{\mathbf{v}})

in
\mathrm{Mat}(N)\times O(N)
. Because
C_{F}
is closed, we get
({\bf m},{\mathbf{v}})\in C_{F}
, and hence
{\bf m}\in\pi_{1}(C_{F})
. Thus
\pi_{1}(C_{F})=E_{F}
is closed.

Therefore
\Phi
is weakly measurable.

Finally,
\mathrm{Mat}(N)\cong\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}}
is a standard Borel space and
O(N)
is a compact metric space, hence Polish. By the Kuratowski–Ryll-Nardzewski measurable selection theorem, there exists a Borel measurable map

f\colon\mathrm{Mat}(N)\to O(N)

such that

f({\bf m})\in\Phi({\bf m})\qquad\text{for every }{\bf m}\in\mathrm{Mat}(N).

Define

{\mathbf{U}}:=f({\mathbf{A}}).

Then
{\mathbf{U}}
is
\sigma({\mathbf{A}})
-measurable,
{\mathbf{U}}\in O(N)
almost surely, and

{\mathbf{A}}=|{\mathbf{A}}|\,{\mathbf{U}}

almost surely. This completes the proof.
∎
