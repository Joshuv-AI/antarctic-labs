Nitrogen fertilization effects on biomass yield and silage quality of rainfed winter forage cereals in Pakistan
Authors: Muhammad Jamil, Raees Ahmed, Nilay Kayin, Mashael Daghash Alqahtani, Muhammad Aamir Iqbal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOptimizing nitrogen (N) fertilizer regimes in conjunction with harvesting stage (HS) (booting and milking dough stages) may potentially boost the herbage yield and silage nutritional quality of winter forage cereals (WFCs), particularly in rainfed agroecosystems. A two-season field study was executed to optimize the doses of N (0, 75, 150, and 225 kg ha− 1) for WFCs (barley, oat, rye, and triticale) harvested at booting stage (BS) and milking dough stage (MDS) and to improve silage quality using additives (molasses and formic acid applied at 5 kg ton− 1 of biomass). The trial was conducted using a randomized complete block design in a factorial arrangement.ResultsOats produced the maximum yield contributing traits and 42% higher fresh biomass yield under the N regime of 225 kg ha− 1 than the control at BS. Interestingly, at MDS, oat outperformed the rest of the WFCs under 225 kg ha− 1 N, which remained statistically comparable to 150 kg ha− 1. The minimum biomass yield was noted for rye under control conditions at both HS. The fertilizer use efficiency (FUE) was maximized across all WFCs at the lowest N dose, whereas oat consistently recorded the maximum FUE and water use efficiency (WUE) at both HS across seasons. Concerning the nutritional quality of silage, the maximum crude protein (CP) content was recorded for oat in response to the N3 fertilization regime with additives, while triticale CP contents were equal at the same level of N and molasses, whereas rye recorded the minimum acid detergent fiber and neutral detergent fiber along with the highest relative feed value in response to the highest N dose.ConclusionsOat may be recommended as a superior forage crop because of the highest biomass productivity, particularly under high nitrogen input (225 kg ha− 1). Moreover, oat harvested at BS may be supplemented with molasses as an additive to maximize the crude protein content of silage.

Background

In South and Southeast Asia, livestock are primarily kept by subsistence farmers to meet their nutritional needs and to gain economic stability [1, 2]. Recently, the highly volatile cost of feed (particularly protein-rich concentrates) and a changing climate have led to an increase in milk price in Pakistan [3]. As a result, dairy farmers have been prompted to seek alternative feed sources to reduce reliance on imported feed without compromising dairy animal productivity [4, 5]. Ultimately, demand for homegrown feed has been exacerbated by low biomass production and limited supply during lean periods, especially during winter months. Integrating winter forage cereals (WFC) into prevalent forage production systems may be a potential solution to ensure sustainable forage production.

Despite increasing demand, the herbage productivity and nutritional quality of WFCs have remained sub-optimal in Pakistan, owing to inappropriate plant nutrition management, particularly nitrogen (N) fertilizers [6]. Due to the critical role of N in triggering vegetative growth, suboptimal N doses tend to reduce biomass yield. Previous studies have reported contradictory results (50–350 kg ha− 1) concerning the optimal dose of N for WFCs in South Asia and many other regions in Africa and Europe, whereas lower doses of N fertilizers restricted canopy development of forage crops, as N is an integral component of chlorophyll, numerous structural and functional proteins, and nucleic acids [7–10]. Moreover, low N-mediated restriction of chlorophyll biosynthesis suppressed the photosynthetic process, which hampered leaf growth and ultimately, biomass yield and protein content were significantly reduced [11, 12].

Therefore, optimization of N fertilization holds potential to serve as a biologically viable and pro-farmer strategy to boost biomass productivity and nutritive quality of WFC [13]. Besides N levels, the harvesting stages, particularly the booting (inflorescence is enclosed within the flag leaf sheath) and milking dough (kernel’s endosperm thickens and becomes pasty) stages, also determine yield and nutritional quality, as oat (Avena sativa L.) and barley (Hordeum vulgare L.) harvested at the booting stage yielded 2–6 Mg ha− 1 dry matter [14]. However, limited research has evaluated triticale (Triticosecale Wittmack) and rye (Secale cereale L.) as over-wintered and spring-harvested forages in the northern rainfed regions of Pakistan, despite their potential to yield high-quality forage and suitable agro-environmental conditions. Another challenge is that WFCs tend to grow and mature slowly in the spring season under rainfed conditions of Pakistan, which could delay the sowing of main crops, thereby necessitating further research [15].

Besides green forage of winter cereals, silage, which is a fermented forage, may serve as an alternate high-nutrition feed for dairy animals; however, its nutritive values depend on the nutritional quality traits of fodder crops [16]. Additionally, the choice of silage additives constitutes a strategic step to enhance the fermentation process, owing to their role in reducing the silage’s pH and deterioration [17]. Previously, contrasting findings have been reported concerning the effectiveness of different additives, such as formic acid and molasses, in improving the quality of silage through the increased production of lactic acid bacteria [18].

Moreover, there is evidence of alterations in the nutrient dynamics (relative feed value and total ash content) of ensiled feed because additives impart influence on pH-tolerant enzymes that perform partial hydrolysis of fiber particles, leading to higher protein and carbohydrate contents [19]. However, research gaps exist regarding the effectiveness of different silage additives in fermenting the WFCs under rainfed conditions of Pakistan, necessitating further investigations.

Thus, we hypothesized that optimization of nitrogen fertilization regimes for boosting the productivity of WFCs (oat, barley, triticale, and rye) coupled with ensiled feed has the potential to provide high nutritional quality feed. The rationale underlying this hypothesis stems from differential genetic potential and agro-morphological traits of these crops, adaptability to local pedo-climatic conditions, and their distinct response to increasing nitrogen doses. To test this hypothesis, a multi-year field study was executed to quantify yield components, biomass productivity, and nutritional quality of winter forage cereals under varying nitrogen regimes and to assess the effectiveness of different additives in boosting nutrient dynamics of silage.

Methods

Locality and experimental soil characteristics

The two-season field experiments were conducted at the PMAS-Arid Agriculture University Rawalpindi’s Research Farm at Chakwal Road (latitude 33.1° N, longitude 73.0° E, and 510 m above sea level with annual rainfall of 775 mm), Pakistan. Pre-sowing, soil samples from the four corners and the middle of the experimental block were collected (0–30 cm depth) to determine the physicochemical properties of the soil. The soil of the experimental region is classified as Inceptisols under the USDA soil classification system, and its texture was loam, having an organic matter percentage of 0.78. It was deficient in nitrogen and phosphorus, with 0.39 and 4.8 mg kg− 1, respectively, whereas potassium content was 91 mg kg− 1. Moreover, pH and electrical conductivity of the experimental soil were 7.47 and 1.09 dsm− 1, respectively.

Experimental details

In this field trial, treatments included four different winter forage cereals (oat, barley, triticale, and rye), four nitrogen fertilizer regimes (0, 75, 150, and 225 kg ha− 1), and two harvesting stages (booting and milking dough). In the silage study, an additional factor of additives (molasses, formic acid, and control) was studied. The experiment was carried out in a randomized complete block design (RCBD) using a factorial arrangement, with three replications. During the winter months, the sowing of WFCs was performed using a hand drill on October 4 and 7 of 2016-17 and 2017-18, respectively. Seeds rate of 80 kg ha− 1 was used for barley (cv. Jau-87), oat (cv. Avon), and triticale (cv. Qaswa), whereas 40 kg ha− 1 was used for rye (cv. Gp-4) [20].

The net plot size was maintained at 4 m × 7 m, with 20 rows per plot, with row-to-row spacing of 20 cm. Experimental units were separated by half-meter pathways, whereas the experimental blocks were separated by one-meter walkways. The source of nitrogen was urea (46% N) that was applied as a basal dose, whereas P2O5 fertilizer (50 kg ha− 1) was also applied in the form of single superphosphate (SSP) at the time of seed bed preparation due to rainfed conditions [20]. All the cultural operations were kept uniform for all experimental units except treatments. Mean maximum and minimum temperatures (0C) and rainfall (mm) were recorded during the course of trials and are presented in Fig. 1.

Silage preparation protocol

After harvesting WFCs, biomass was chopped into small pieces (2–3 cm) with the help of a mechanical forage cutter to facilitate packing and allow uniform fermentation. Thereafter, 2.5 kg of chopped biomass material was weighed and packed tightly into already sterilized, airtight, and high-density polyethylene bags (5 kg capacity). After adding additives as per the treatment plan (molasses and formic acid at the rate of 5 kg per ton of fresh biomass), a laboratory vacuum-cum-heat sealer (Henkelman Boxer-35, Netherlands) was used to vacuum-seal the chopped biomass-filled bags by removing residual oxygen. In this way, strict anaerobic conditions were established to facilitate lactic acid fermentation. After labelling each bag, they were stored in darkness at ambient temperature (25 °C) for an ensiling period of 48–52 days [21]. Following fermentation, silage was thoroughly homogenized prior to taking samples for conducting physicochemical analyses.

Response variables recordings

The response variables were recorded at two harvesting stages (booting and the milking dough) of WFCs and included plant height, leaf area index, fresh and dry weight plant− 1, green fodder, and dry matter yield. Dry matter yield (kg ha− 1) was recorded by harvesting plants from an area of 1 m2 at both HS. Plant samples were dried in an oven (Nabertherm TR-450) at 65 °C for 48 h till constant weight was obtained. The leaf area of ten randomly selected plants from each plot was measured at both stages by using a leaf area meter (model Ci202), and the average was noted. Statistical analysis

ANOVA assumptions (variance’s normality and homogeneity) were verified by employing Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, which depicted minor deviations, confirming data robustness.

The analysis employed a mixed-effects model, with experimental factors as fixed effects and RCBD blocks as a random effect. The field trial data were statistically analyzed using three-way analysis of variance (crops, nitrogen regimes, and harvesting stages). In contrast, because of the additional factor of additives, a four-way factorial analysis of variance was used for silage data in Statistics (version 8.1, USA), using a factorial arrangement of a randomized complete block design (RCBD). Since the significance level of all characters was determined as 5%, the Tukey Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test was performed at the 5% significance level [26].

Fertilizer use efficiency and water use efficiency

The WFCs, levels of N fertilization, and their interaction displayed significant variations for fertilizer use efficiency (FUE) and water use efficiency (WUE) at each HS during both seasons (Table 3). The maximum FUE was exhibited by all WFCs in response to N1, whereas FUE declined with an increase in the rate of N application at both HS. Overall, oat recorded the maximum FUE at both HS during both years, and it was succeeded by triticale, while rye exhibited the minimum corresponding values. Interestingly, at the milking dough stage, higher values of FUE were recorded for all WFCs compared to the booting stage. Contrary to FUE, the WUE of all WFCs increased with the increasing dose of N fertilization during both HS in the corresponding years. However, the oat crop remained superior to the rest of WFCs, particularly in response to N3 dose (225 kg ha-1 N) in terms of WUE at both BS and MDS during both seasons of the trial. Triticale exhibited the second-highest WUE, and it was succeeded by barley, whereas rye exhibited the lowest WUE. Interestingly, significantly higher WUE was recorded for all WFCs at the MDS compared to the BS, especially for oat receiving 225 kg N ha-1.

Acid detergent fiber

The maximum acid detergent fiber (ADF) content was noted for oat in response to control N0 (37.78%), followed by N1 (37.06%) and N2 (36.21%) (Table 5). The minimum ADF content was noticed for rye in response to N3, which was comparable with barley receiving the N3 dose.

Furthermore, during the second season, the interactive effect of crop × nitrogen showed maximum ADF contents for oat in response to control. The minimum ADF contents were recorded for rye in response to N3, which was non-significant from barley in response to N3. The interactive effects of crop × nitrogen × additive for ADF exhibited significant variation during both years at MDS. During the first season, the WFCs showed the highest ADF contents in response to N0, with a non-significant effect of the additive within N rates. Neutral detergent fiber

During both seasons of study, WFCs produced the highest NDF contents in response to N0, which decreased with increasing nitrogen application to the crops in the presence or absence of additives (Table 6). Concerning the NDF content at MDS during the first season, it was noted that the maximum NDF content was recorded for N0 with the application of various additives. The highest NDF content was noted for rye in response to N0, with a non-significant effect of additives, while rye in response to N1 performed equally to N0 with the use of molasses. The lowest NDF was noted for triticale in response to N3, and it was comparable with rye in response to N2 at the control. During the second year, comparable trends were noted for entire WFCs, N fertilization regimes, and additives. The maximum NDF contents were observed for rye in response to N0, with a non-significant effect of additives. Meanwhile, rye in response to N1 was also comparable to N0 with the use of molasses. The lowest NDF content was observed for triticale in response to N3, with a non-significant effect of additives.

Ash content

It was noted that the ash content of all WFCs increased with increasing N levels, with the maximum in response to N3 against the minimum for N0 during both seasons of the study. At BS, the maximum ash contents were observed for triticale in response to N3, and oat, receiving the same level of N, exhibited intermediate values of total ash during both seasons of the study. The minimum value was noted for rye in response to N0. The interaction of crop × additive presented the maximum ash contents for triticale in response to molasses, and it was succeeded by the same crop silage that was supplied with formic acid, whereas the minimum ash contents were observed for rye silage receiving no additives. At MDS, WFCs exhibited the highest ash content in response to the N3 (225 kg ha-1) dose with the use of molasses as an additive during both seasons of the experiment. The maximum ash content was recorded for triticale receiving the N3 fertilization regime, with a non-significant effect of additives (Table 7). Interestingly, oats were also comparable with triticale at the same level of N with supplementation of molasses and formic acid. Furthermore, the minimum ash content was recorded for rye in response to N0.

Relative feed value

The significant variation in the interaction of crop × nitrogen and nitrogen × additive was depicted for relative feed value (RFV) during both years at BS and MDS (Table 8). Concerning the BS, the highest RFV contents were observed for rye in response to N3, which was statistically equal to barley receiving N3, which in turn was followed by rye supplemented with the N2 fertilization regime during the first season. However, the minimum RFV was noticed for oat in response to N0, which was not significantly different from oats in response to N1. Results for the second season at BS demonstrated that the maximum RFV content was observed for rye in response to N3, and barley recorded the second-highest RFV in response to N3, which had a non-significant difference from rye receiving N2. Though the minimum RFV contents were noted for oat in response to control. As far as MDS was concerned, WFCs displayed the maximum RFV content in response to N3 with a non-significant effect of the additive during the first season. The highest RFV was noted for triticale in response to N3, with a non-significant effect of the additive that was at par with N2, with control means of the additive. The lowest RFV contents were noted for oat in response to N0 and N1, with a non-significant effect of additives (Table 8).

Discussion

The results of this multi-year field trial were in concurrence with the research hypothesis, as optimization of N fertilization regimes remained instrumental in boosting the productivity of WFCs (oat, barley, triticale, and rye), coupled with significantly more nutritional silage. In this study, we noted that the maximum dose of N (225 kg ha-1) significantly improved plant height and leaf area of all WFCs, particularly oats, at BS and MDS during both seasons of the study.

This may be attributed to abundant N availability in the soil solution and sufficient uptake by crop plants, which triggered the vegetative growth traits of WFCs. Similar to our findings, it was reported that 220 kg N ha-1 produced the tallest plant by promoting vegetative growth, internode elongation, and photosynthesis [27]. Likewise, it was inferred that different crops responded differently to N fertilization owing to variation in morphological characteristics and genetic potential. Additionally, it was concluded that being a constituent of protoplasm, proteins, nucleic acids, and chlorophyll, higher doses of N remained effective in triggering vegetative growth traits. Similar to our results, higher supplementation of N significantly boosted leaf growth, which led to increased leaf area index [28]. Besides plant height and leaf area index, the maximum N fertilizer also improved fresh and dry weights of all WFCs, particularly oat, which led to maximum biomass yield (green forage yield and dry matter) at BS and MDS during both seasons in our study. These results may be attributed to abundant uptake of N by crop plants, which was effectively utilized by crop plants during cell division to promote the biosynthesis of proteins for cell elongation, leading to an increase in fresh and biomass accumulation. It may also be interpreted that different WFCs under investigation responded differently to N fertilization due to variations in their morphological traits (plant height, leaf area, fresh and dry weights per plant), which caused significant differences in their biomass productivity.

Previously, it was reported that cool-season C3 cereals (wheat, rye, barley, and oat) varied in their ability to biosynthesize assimilates under the same N fertilizer dose, which primarily determined their potential to accumulate biomass at different growth stages [29, 30]. Moreover, it has been revealed by several studies that different crops were recorded to have varying N-saturation capacity by virtue of their metabolic activity and genetic architecture, which determined their level of N uptake from the soil solution and N utilization in improving the vegetative growth traits and biomass yield [31, 32].

Fertilizer use efficiency (FUE potential for converting applied nutrients in the form of fertilizers into harvestable biomass) and water use efficiency (WUE biomass productivity per unit of absorbed water by crop plants) are vital indicators to maximize biomass productivity and minimize production costs, along with environmental impacts. We recorded that the maximum FUE was exhibited by oat in response to N1, whereas FUE declined with an increase in the rate of N application at both HS. The law of diminishing returns may be ascribed to N-mediated biomass yield gains in WFCs because initially lower N doses stimulated vigorous increments in leaf area and biosynthesis of chlorophyll, which triggered the photosynthesis rate, but further additions of higher N doses resulted in progressively lower yield increments, causing a non-linear association between FUE and higher N rates. Likewise, low N doses closely matched the N demand of WFCs, resulting in significantly higher uptake and efficient utilization of N, leading to higher FUE. Moreover, it may also be interpreted that WFCs attain maximum canopy development and photosynthetic efficiency at MDS compared to BS, enabling plants to translocate higher quantity of nutrients for biosynthesis of assimilates leading to higher accumulation of dry matter and enhanced biomass produced per unit of applied N [33]. Contrary to FUE, the WUE of all WFCs increased with the increasing dose of N fertilization during both HS in the corresponding years. A possible justification for this could be the crop morphological traits (root depth, root length, root density) and genetic potential differences that affect water utilization by crop plants. It may also be interpreted that higher N doses enhanced leaf area index (LAI), enabling greater light capture and canopy photosynthesis, leading to higher dry matter production without causing a proportional increment in water losses, improving WUE. It also inferred that WUE recorded a proportional increase with N doses up to an optimum level, and excessive N drastically reduced WUE by triggering unnecessary vegetative growth. Previously, it has been reported that N absorption, assimilation, and WUE of field crops differed by virtue of differences in their genetic potential and botanical traits [34]. Moreover, it has been reported that field crops varied in their genetic potential for canopy development and transpiration needs, which determined their WUE. Higher N dose enhanced WUE by enhancing root water uptake, and photosynthetic capacity, along with mediating stomatal regulation, and triggered canopy development, which led to significantly higher biomass generation per unit of water consumed [35].

Concerning the nutritional quality of WFCs’ silage, crude protein (CP) is a key determinant of silage quality, influencing fermentation dynamics and the overall nutritional value of silage [36].

The CP content of WFCs’ silage differed significantly in response to N application rates and use of additives at both stages (CP contents were lower at MDS as compared to BS). It may be interpreted that observed variations in CP could be ascribed to N fertilization, which increased CP accumulation of crop plants and was equally manifested in the silage. Moreover, differences in CP of winter forage cereals may be associated with genotypic variations in WFCs for N assimilation.

The CP content was higher at the early vegetative phase, probably due to the tender nature of the crop plant. In addition, CP increment has been ascribed to the greater availability of moisture and N at early growth stages. Moreover, the aerobic stability of high moisture-containing WFCs was linearly increased with chemical additives [37]. Consistent with a previous study, advancing maturity or the late harvesting stage decreased CP contents in the silage of WFCs [18].

Furthermore, as the crop developed from the booting stage to the soft dough stage, CP content decreased, indicating a decrease in CP with an increase in the age of the crop [20]. Similar to our findings, it has been inferred that molasses could not add any true protein; however, they effectively improved CP primarily through boosting fermentation efficiency and N preservation [18–20].

Acid detergent fiber (ADF, comprising cellulose and lignin) and neutral detergent fiber (NDF, composed of hemicellulose, cellulose, and lignin) serve as key determinants of silage nutritive value [38, 39]. These fiber types have been regarded as central indicators of silage quality because they govern forage intake, rumen function, and overall feeding value of the silage. In this study, a decline in ADF and NDF content was recorded with an increase in N supply to the WFCs, whereas ADF was higher at MDS than at BS. Similar to our findings, it was inferred that optimized N availability triggered robust vegetative growth along with enhancing the biosynthesis of cellular proteins, which promoted the formation of metabolically active tissues instead of structurally reinforced cell walls, leading to reduced ADF and NDF [40, 41].

Likewise, accelerated leaf expansion coupled with enhanced leaf-to-stem ratio in response to higher N regimes effectively diluted lignified tissues, thereby contributing to a significant reduction in fiber accumulation [40, 42]. The N concentration changed the amount of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, which created variation in fiber components [39].

Total ash (representing the total mineral fraction) is a vital but indirect indicator of silage quality because although minerals are regarded as essential nutritionally, excessive ash content represents soil toxicity [43, 44]. In this study, the WFCs demonstrated a consistent increase in ash contents in response to high doses of N and the addition of molasses during both seasons of the trial. It may be inferred that higher doses of N triggered vegetative and root growth, which improved nutrient uptake from the soil by crop plants. The increased N supply results in more metabolism, biosynthesis of protein, and dry matter accumulation, which leads to higher total ash content. Moreover, molasses contains an abundant concentration of sugars and minerals, which could have added to the total ash content of the silage [27]. Furthermore, it has been reported that an increase in ash contents of forages with increasing N application, thus supports the present findings [44, 45]. Relative feed value (RFV) integrates the effects of NDF and ADF into a single numerical value and is positively correlated with silage quality (higher RFV reflects lower fiber concentration), indicating greater intake and digestibility of silage [44–46]. All WFCs displayed higher RFV contents with the maximum doses of N application because optimized N has been reported to stimulate the biosynthesis of protein and reduce NDF and ADF content.

This led to an increase in the components of RFV (enhancement in dry matter digestibility, accompanied by higher voluntary intake). Moreover, higher production of leaf tissues than lignified stems in response to higher N doses has been ascribed to higher ruminal microbial activity and RFV [47–50].

Conclusions

It may be concluded from the recorded findings that optimization of nitrogen doses (0, 75, 150, and 225 kg ha− 1) for WFCs (barley, oat, rye, and triticale) remained effective in enhancing biomass productivity at the booting stage (BS) and milking dough stage (MDS), along with silage quality using additives (especially molasses applied at 5 kg ton− 1 of biomass). Overall, farmers may prefer oat cultivation using a nitrogen dose of 225 kg ha− 1 for achieving higher biomass production under rainfed conditions during winter months, owing to its superior yield-contributing traits (plant height, leaf area, fresh weight per plant, etc.), whereas fertilizer use efficiency remained higher for lower N dose. In terms of nutritional quality traits of silage, oat receiving 225 kg ha− 1 may be supplemented with molasses as an additive to maximize crude protein content. Furthermore, BS may be preferred for harvesting winter forage cereals over MDS to achieve superior quality silage. Nevertheless, environmental trade-offs (greenhouse gas emissions and N losses from leaching and volatilization) and the economic feasibility of higher N fertilization regimes were not investigated in this study, warranting further examination.

Moreover, building on these promising findings, future research needs to focus on in vivo rumen fermentation and animal performance indicators for determining the efficacy and feeding value of WFCs’ silage. Furthermore, future studies may test higher N doses applied in conjunction with organic manures, slow-release N fertilizers, and microbial inoculants for improving nitrogen use efficiency, herbage yield, and silage quality of major winter forage cereals on a sustainable basis.
