Late surgical site hemorrhage is a possible radiologic indicator of early tumor recurrence in grossly totally resected high-grade meningioma: A case report
Authors: Alaa G. Al-Maaitah, Maysoon A. banihani, Ahmad M. Aburumman, Hamzeh M. Alkhawaldeh, Majd A. AL-Majali, Mohammad M. al-talafeeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rhabdoid meningioma is a rare high-grade tumor that tends to recur with the potential for extracranial metastasis. The primary treatment is surgery, if feasible, with adjuvant radiotherapy. We present a case of a 27-year-old male patient who presented with symptoms of increased intracranial pressure. Imaging revealed a large heterogeneously enhancing mass in the right frontal lobe surrounded by moderate vasogenic edema, causing significant midline shift. The patient underwent gross total resection with uneventful postoperative recovery. Histopathology confirmed rhabdoid meningioma, classified as World Health Organization grade III. The patient received adjuvant radiotherapy with no significant adverse events. After 6 months of surgery, he started to complain of headache. His brain magnetic resonance imaging showed an extra-axial hematoma at the surgical site with no associated enhancing soft tissue component that progressively increased in size on subsequent scans, along with intraparenchymal extension into the right frontal lobe, not causing midline shift or brain herniation. The hematoma was not explained by any trauma or medical causes. In addition, lung and bone metastases were evident 8 months post-surgery. Unfortunately, this rapid deterioration led to the patient’s death. Despite the emphasis of gross total resection as the most important factor for tumor control, high-grade meningiomas pose a therapeutic challenge due to their aggressive nature. Late intracranial bleeding (>3 months) in the absence of possible contributing factors should raise suspicion of tumor recurrence as early detection can improve patient’s prognosis. Moreover, frequent close follow-up and molecular testing should be further investigated to improve patient outcomes, especially in the younger age group.

Introduction

Meningiomas are the most common primary intracranial tumors, accounting for approximately 37.6% of all brain neoplasms [1]. High-grade meningiomas (HGMs) are classified as World Health Organization (WHO) grades II and III, which correspond to 5%-34% and 1%-3% of cases, respectively [2]. The histopathologic features and subtypes were previously the most important predictors of tumor behavior, of which rhabdoid, anaplastic, and papillary subtypes are the most aggressive types with poor outcomes [1]. HGMs have a tendency for local recurrence and decreased overall survival in comparison with typical low-grade meningiomas. Studies have evaluated meningiomas with hemorrhagic presentation or early postoperative hemorrhage [3,4]. However, to our knowledge, no studies have discussed late tumor bed hemorrhage of totally resected meningiomas. We present a case of grade III rhabdoid meningioma in a young patient who developed delayed brain hemorrhage suggestive of local recurrence.

Case presentation

A 27-year-old male patient presented to the emergency department complaining of headache for 45 days that affected his ability to sleep. It was associated with early morning vomiting and dizziness. The patient experienced weight loss and anorexia. On physical examination, the patient appeared ill; however, his vital signs were within normal limits. The patient’s Glasgow Coma Scale was 15/15, with normal bilateral muscle power. His right eye was adducted, with equally reactive pupils.

Noncontrast brain computed tomography (CT) was performed at the emergency department. It showed a heterogeneous right frontal mass measuring approximately 6.2 × 4.6 cm, with moderate vasogenic edema and a shift of midline structures by approximately 1.1 cm (Fig. 1A). The patient was admitted, and a 3 Tesla brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with gadolinium was performed. MRI protocol included axial spin-echo T1-Weighted Images (T1WI), T2-Weighted Images (T2WI) in axial, sagittal and coronal views/4 mm in thickness. Axial Fluid-Attenuated Inversion-Recovery sequence (FLAIR), axial Susceptibility-Weighted Imaging (SWI), axial Diffusion-Weighted Imaging (DWI) with b-values (0, 500, and 1000 s/mm2) and Apparent Diffusion Coefficient (ADC) map. TIWI with contrast was obtained and Gadolinium was administered as bolus IV injection (0.1 mmol/kg). The scan demonstrated a heterogeneous mass with hemorrhagic and cystic components in the right frontoparietal lobe, measuring approximately 5.5 × 3.8 cm, with irregular peripheral enhancement and restricted diffusion. It was surrounded by extensive vasogenic edema with a secondary shift of midline structures by 2 cm, subfalcine herniation, and mild uncal herniation (Fig. 1B-E). No other enhancing brain masses were observed. After careful attention to dural attachment on sagittal images, an extra-axial origin of the lesion was suspected; thus, atypical meningioma, solitary fibrous tumor of the dura, lymphoma, and metastasis were considered as possible etiologies (Fig. 1F). The initial staging chest, abdomen, and pelvis CT scan was negative for suspicious masses. The patient underwent surgery within 1 week of presentation. Gross total resection (GTR)/Simpson grade 1 was achieved as the mass and the diseased dura were excised. Packing with oxidized regenerated cellulose (Surgicel) was placed at tumor bed, with no immediate complications. The histopathology results revealed sheets of loosely cohesive cells with eccentric nuclei and hyaline paranuclear inclusions. The nuclei showed pleomorphism with vesicular chromatin and prominent nucleoli. An extensive area of necrosis was present, with a high mitotic rate of >20 mitotic figures (MF)/10 high-power field (HPF). Immunohistochemistry staining was positive for both EMA and Vimentin, and focally positive for PR and Desmin. On the other hand, GFAP, Melan-A, and CD20 were all negative. Immediate postoperative brain MRI revealed GTR of the mass with postsurgical material, pneumocephalus, and a small extra-axial hematoma measuring about 12 mm in maximum thickness. No gross enhancing residual masses or abnormal dural enhancement were seen (Fig. 2). Clinically, the patient’s presenting symptoms had significantly improved. He also received adjuvant fractionated radiotherapy (60 Gy/30 fx) 5 weeks after surgery and completed the planned sessions uneventfully. A noncontrast brain CT scan after 5 months revealed mild residual vasogenic edema in the right superior frontal lobe, and the shift of midline structures resolved, but a mixed-density subdural hematoma measuring approximately 11 mm in thickness remained (Fig. 3). Approximately 6 months post-surgery, the patient’s headache increased in severity, but no associated neurologic deficits or decreased level of consciousness were present. The extra-axial hematoma in the right frontal convexity started to increase in size on serial images along with intraparenchymal extension in the right frontal lobe, the largest was measuring about 28 × 21 mm and surrounded by moderate vasogenic edema with no significant midline shift (Fig. 4). However, no associated surgical-bed enhancing soft tissue masses were seen. In addition, a new oval homogeneously enhancing left subgaleal lesion with cutaneous extension was noted, measuring about 18 × 12 mm, highly suggestive of metastasis (Fig. 4). New small enhancing dural lesions in the convexity of the right anterior frontal lobe and the right sphenoid wing developed, measuring approximately 14 × 7 mm and 10 × 6 mm, respectively (Fig. 5). They were suspicious for recurrent/metastatic meningioma. One month later, the patient started to complain of general weakness and back pain. A re-staging chest and abdomen/pelvis CT scan with contrast was performed with Single-Pass Split-Bolus Protocol (20 mA, 120 KV, slice thickness 0.6 mm, 1.5 mL/kg iodinated contrast, and a flow rate of 2 mL/s). It showed multiple bilateral new lung nodules measuring 3-9 mm that were suspicious for malignancy according to patients’ clinical condition. In addition, two lytic bony lesions with cortical destruction were seen in right seventh rib posteriorly and in the left transverse process of L5 vertebra. They were associated with soft tissue component that extend to ipsilateral paravertebral muscles, but no spinal canal invasion. Rib excisional biopsy confirmed metastasis (Fig. 6). The patient’s laboratory results, including platelets, coagulation profile, and liver function test, were all normal, and there was no history of trauma. The patient was admitted to the ICU, and he was intubated. He was on palliative management, and no further surgical intervention was performed. Discussion

The WHO grading system for tumors classifies meningiomas into grades I-III based on histologic features, corresponding to benign, atypical, and malignant histology, respectively. WHO grade III meningiomas constitute approximately 1.7% of all cases and include the rhabdoid subtype [1]. The median age of meningioma diagnosis is 66 years, so it is uncommon in young patients unless they have specific associations such as neurofibromatosis type 2 [5]. Male patients have an increased risk of HGM. Kan et al. [6] found that males had a 2-fold increased incidence of grade II/III meningiomas [6]. Our patient was 27 years old with no known previous medical or surgical history.

Unlike typical meningioma, high-grade meningiomas (HGM) are more likely to be larger with lobulated borders and heterogenous enhancement due to necrosis. In addition, they tend to be associated with vasogenic edema with or without brain invasion [7,8].

Duan et al. [10] found that male patients (OR = 2.90), larger tumor size (OR = 1.77), and parenchymal invasion (OR = 3.21) were the most likely factors to predict HGM and hence a higher Ki-67 proliferation index [9,10]. Other studies highlighted that tumor location can predict tumor behavior [6,8]. Hale et al. [8] studied the preoperative MRI features for grade I and II meningiomas and revealed that masses along the falx and convexities were more likely to be atypical in comparison with posterior fossa and base of skull meningiomas (P = .026) [8]. In our case, the mass appeared large with extensive parenchymal infiltration, rendering it difficult to be distinguished from intra-axial brain lesions on brain CT scan. However, careful attention to dural attachment on multiplanar MRI was helpful.

Our patient underwent GTR of the meningioma with an uneventful immediate postoperative course. However, the extra-axial hematoma at the surgical site had progressively increased in size by 6 months postsurgery, along with a new adjacent intraparenchymal hematoma, as detailed previously. It is not uncommon to develop an early postoperative brain hemorrhage after meningioma resection. Approximately 2.6% of patients experience postoperative bleeding, which can be attributed to many factors, regardless of meningioma histopathologic type, such as coagulation dysfunction, intraoperative small vessel injury, and fluctuation of blood pressure after surgery [11]. Our patient developed intracranial hemorrhage late after surgery. In fact, no history of trauma was documented, and the patient had no laboratory evidence of bleeding tendency.

In this clinical context, recurrent hemorrhagic meningioma was a possible etiology of this hemorrhage in the context of high neovascularity of the primary lesion at the initial MRI scan. Many studies have described meningiomas with hemorrhagic presentation, and some reported cases of recurrent meningioma bleeding before surgical intervention [12,13]. However, to our knowledge, no studies have investigated the late etiology of brain hemorrhage after meningioma’s gross total resection. A systematic review by Halalmeh et al. [3] on hemorrhagic meningiomas found that 75.4% of the intracranial hemorrhage was associated with an obvious mass. However, 24.6% presented with isolated brain hemorrhage, mainly in the form of subdural hematoma [3]. Abdelsimar et al. [14] documented that meningioma patients who presented with subdural hemorrhage had a higher mortality rate of 12% at a median follow-up period of 3 months [14].

Radiotherapy is an important line of management for meningiomas. It is usually considered for unresectable or partially resected meningiomas and HGMs [5]. The acute and early delayed brain toxicity after radiotherapy (less than 6 months) is subtle, reversible, and may improve without the need for imaging [15]. However, clinical symptoms and brain changes 6 months postradiation are worrisome. Radiation necrosis, leukoencephalopathy, and microbleeds are known late complications [16]. Cutsforth-Gregory et al. [17] studied radiation-induced cavernous malformation (RICM) as a cause of cerebral hemorrhage in 32 patients with different head and neck primary tumors. The RICM developed at a median of 12 years after radiotherapy, and most of them were asymptomatic. On the other hand, they tended to be multiple and with a higher risk of symptomatic hemorrhage than nonradiation cavernous malformation. Among these patients, only 7 (21.9%) had type I RICM (i.e., subacute bleeding with variable vasogenic edema), and the only meningioma in that study developed the classic type II/popcorn cavernoma [17]. In our case, the patient developed a surgical bed hematoma 3 months after the completion of radiotherapy sessions. In addition, extension into brain parenchyma was evident on follow-up scans. The constellation of normal coagulation factors, absence of trauma and short interval after radiotherapy raise the possibility of hemorrhagic tumor recurrence taking into consideration the primary tumor’s high vascularity.

Multiple studies reported the prognosis of meningioma patients. A study by Pasquier et al. [18] conducted on 119 irradiated grade II and III meningiomas found that the 5-year disease-free survival was 62% and 48%, respectively. In this report, the median recurrence time was 2.1 years, and older patient age, along with high mitotic rate, were the poor prognostic factors [18]. Our patient developed rapid recurrence at 6 months postsurgery despite GTR and adjuvant radiotherapy. In addition, lung and bone metastases were evident 8 months postsurgery.

Multiple genetic and molecular biomarkers have been studied to further subclassify meningiomas, provide prognostic information, and aid targeted treatment. Mutation of the TERT promoter, loss of H3K27 nuclear expression, and homozygous deletion of CDKN2A/B were associated with poor outcomes. In addition, some mutations were more prevalent in the rhabdoid type, such as BRCA1-associated protein-1 (BAP1) and BRAFV600E mutation [2,19]. Genetic therapy is under investigation, but large randomized trials are still needed to consider such systemic treatment in clinical practice [20].

In conclusion, HGM, even after GTR, requires close, frequent follow-up due to its high propensity for local recurrence and distant metastasis. In addition, molecular testing may aid in subclassifying meningiomas, guide prognosis, and advance targeted therapy in the future. Late surgical site hematomas are uncommon in the absence of contributing factors. Hemorrhagic brain recurrence and delayed postradiation hemorrhage are possible etiologies. Further studies are advised to evaluate the etiology of late tumor bed hemorrhage after grossly totally resected HGMs.
