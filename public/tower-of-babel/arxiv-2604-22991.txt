Optimal strategies in the all-heads coin game
Peter Pfaffelhuber

Abstract
We study a sequential coin-flipping game in which a player starts with n n coins, each landing heads independently with probability p p . In each round the player flips all remaining coins and must set aside at least one coin showing heads; if no coin shows heads, the player loses. The player wins if and when all coins have been set aside. This is a stochastic shortest-path Markov decision process whose Bellman equation involves a nonlinear suffix-maximum operator. We analyse two natural strategies — One (set aside exactly one head) and All (set aside every head) — and determine the optimal winning probability w n , p w_{n,p} as a function of n n and p p . For p = 1 2 p=\tfrac{1}{2} every strategy achieves w n , 1 / 2 = 1 2 w_{n,1/2}=\tfrac{1}{2} . For p > 1 2 p>\tfrac{1}{2} the strategy One is optimal and n ↦ w n , p n\mapsto w_{n,p} is strictly increasing; as a consequence the limit W ⁡ ( p ) := lim n w n , p W(p):=\lim_{n}w_{n,p} admits an explicit series representation and satisfies p ≤ W ⁡ ( p ) < 1 p\leq W(p)<1 . For p < 1 2 p<\tfrac{1}{2} near 1 2 \tfrac{1}{2} we carry out a first-order perturbation expansion in δ := 1 2 − p \delta:=\tfrac{1}{2}-p and prove that the deficit is 1 2 − w n , 1 2 − δ ≈ δ ​ c n \tfrac{1}{2}-w_{n,\tfrac{1}{2}-\delta}\approx\delta c_{n} in first order in δ \delta , where c n c_{n} satisfies a linear recursion for n ≥ 7 n\geq 7 , with a limit L = lim c n ≈ 1.7035 L=\lim c_{n}\approx 1.7035 . As a consequence the optimal value sequence has, to first order in δ \delta , a strict local minimum at n = 5 n=5 and no local maximum — local maxima are a non-perturbative phenomenon.

1.1
The game

Fix a probability
p\in(0,1)
and a positive integer
n
. A player
is given
n
coins, each of which independently lands heads with
probability
p
and tails with probability
q:=1-p
. The game
proceeds in rounds. In each round:

(i)

All remaining coins are flipped simultaneously.

(ii)

If no coin shows heads, the game is lost.

(iii)

Otherwise the player must set aside at least one coin that
shows heads. The set-aside coins leave the game permanently.

(iv)

If every coin has been set aside, the game is won.
Otherwise, a new round begins with the remaining coins.

The player’s objective is to maximise the probability of winning.

Two natural strategies present themselves:

•

Strategy
One
:
set aside exactly one head when

1\leq k\leq n-1
heads appear, and set aside all
n
when
k=n
.

•

Strategy
All
:
set aside every head that appears
(
i=k
).

Strategy
One
is conservative: it preserves as many coins as
possible for future rounds, betting that the one set-aside coin has
already “done its job.” Strategy
All
is greedy: it cashes in
every success immediately. Neither is universally optimal.

Relation to prior work.

A close variant of this game — building on an earlier
question posed by Joachim Breitner on Mathematics Stack
Exchange
[
3
]
— was introduced and studied by
van Doorn
[
8
]
, who took as objective the

expected number of heads
collected,
v_{n,p}
, rather than
the all-heads-wins probability
w_{n,p}
studied here. Van Doorn’s
analysis is essentially complete for
p\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
: he proves
that there exist absolute constants
\phi=(\sqrt{5}-1)/2\approx 0.618
and
p_{0}\approx 0.5495
such that the optimal strategy
follows
One
(in his notation, “A”) for
p\geq\phi
, switches
between
One
and
All
at a level
n(p)
for
p_{0}<p<\phi
, and
follows
All
(“B”) throughout for
\tfrac{1}{2}\leq p\leq p_{0}
. For

p<\tfrac{1}{2}
, however, van Doorn writes that “everything is a lot
less clear” and restricts himself to numerical conjectures and a
non-monotonicity result.

The present paper makes two changes in emphasis. First, the
objective: maximising the all-heads probability
w_{n,p}
rather
than the expected count
v_{n,p}
. As a consequence, the
p>\tfrac{1}{2}
case becomes simpler:
the strategy
One
is always optimal — a fact noted in passing
in
[
8
, §10]
and proved here in
Theorem
3.1
below with a self-contained joint
induction. Second, and more importantly, our focus is the regime

p<\tfrac{1}{2}
that van Doorn explicitly leaves open. We carry out
a first-order perturbation in
\delta=\tfrac{1}{2}-p
and obtain a

closed-form description
of the leading behaviour: the
deficit coefficient
c_{n}
satisfies a linear recursion
(Proposition
4.8
), the limit
L=\lim c_{n}
admits an
explicit formula and a numerical value
L\approx 1.7035
(Theorem
4.10
), and the local-extremum
structure of
n\mapsto w_{n,p}
is fully determined to first order
(Corollary
4.11
).

A third, methodological, change concerns formal verification.
Since the results of the present paper were generated in collaboration with AI,
we want to avoid hallucinations: the reader does not have to be a specialist in Lean 4,
the corresponding proof assistant, but has to acknowledge the fact that (i) the recursive definition
for
w_{n,p}
from (
1
) is correctly implemented and (ii) the results as they stand
compile without error, meaning that Lean 4 has checked every proof.
More precisely, every numbered result
of the present paper — including the perturbation analysis of
§
4
— has been formally verified in Lean 4 using
the Mathlib library, with no
sorry
s, no custom axioms,
and no
native_decide
or
unsafe
blocks. A
line-by-line correspondence between the manuscript and the formal

A
.

1.2
Markov decision processes and the Bellman equation

The game is a finite-state
Markov decision process
(MDP): the
state is the number
m
of coins remaining, the action is the number

i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
of heads to set aside (where
k
is the random
number of heads), and the transition is

m\mapsto m-i
. There are two absorbing states:
win

(
m=0
, all coins collected) and
lose
(reached when
k=0
in
some round). A
policy
(or
strategy
)
\pi
specifies,
for each state
n
and each observed number
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
of
heads, the number
i_{\pi}(n,k)\in\{1,\dots,k\}
of heads to set
aside; we write
v_{n}^{\pi}
for the resulting winning probability
starting from state
n
.

Because every policy absorbs with probability at least

p^{m}+(1-p)^{m}>0
per round, the MDP is a
stochastic
shortest-path
(SSP) problem in the sense of Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis

[
2
]
: the Bellman operator is a contraction
and the optimal value function is its unique fixed point.

Definition 1.1

(Optimal winning probability)
.

Let
w_{0,p}:=1
. For
n\geq 1
define

w_{n,p}\;:=\;p^{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{j}p^{n-j}q^{j}\,\max_{j\leq m\leq n-1}w_{m,p},

(1)

where
q=1-p
and the empty sum (at
n=1
) gives

w_{1,p}=p
.

The term
p^{n}
accounts for the event that all
n
coins land heads
(an immediate win). Each summand with
j\geq 1
accounts for the
event that exactly
j
coins land tails; the player then chooses to
keep some number
m\in\{j,\dots,n-1\}
of coins (setting aside

n-m
heads), and the game continues from state
m
with optimal
value
w_{m,p}
.

The Bellman equation (
1
) is the standard optimality
equation for this SSP–MDP. For general background on Markov decision
processes and the Bellman equation we refer to the monographs of
Puterman
[
6
]
, Bertsekas
[
1
]
, and
Stokey, Lucas and Prescott
[
7
]
.

1.3
Strategies
One
and
All

Under strategy
One
the player always keeps
m=n-1
coins
(except when
k=n
), so the Bellman recursion collapses to

a_{n,p}\;=\;p^{n}+\bigl(1-p^{n}-(1-p)^{n}\bigr)\,a_{n-1,p},\qquad a_{0,p}:=1.

(2)

Under strategy
All
the player always keeps
m=j
coins (the
number of tails), giving

b_{n,p}\;=\;p^{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{j}p^{n-j}q^{j}\,b_{j,p},\qquad b_{0,p}:=1.

(3)

Note that the recursions for both
a
and
b
are
linear

(no
\max
operator), which makes them analytically more tractable
than the Bellman equation (
1
).

The fair coin:
p=\tfrac{1}{2}

Theorem 2.1
.

For every
n\geq 1
,
w_{n,1/2}=\tfrac{1}{2}
. More generally, for any
policy
\pi
satisfying
i_{\pi}(n,n)=n
for every
n
(i.e. the
player accepts the immediate win when all coins land heads), the
winning probability is
v_{n}^{\pi}=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

In particular, both strategies
One
and
All
achieve the
optimum:
a_{n,1/2}=b_{n,1/2}=w_{n,1/2}=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

We use the binomial identity

\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{j}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)^{n}\;=\;1-2^{1-n}.

(4)

Step 1.
We show
w_{n,1/2}=\tfrac{1}{2}
by strong induction.
For
n=1
:
w_{1,1/2}=\tfrac{1}{2}
. For
n\geq 2
, suppose

w_{m,1/2}=\tfrac{1}{2}
for all
1\leq m\leq n-1
. Then every
suffix-maximum equals
\tfrac{1}{2}
, so

\displaystyle w_{n,1/2}

\displaystyle=2^{-n}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{j}2^{-n}

\displaystyle=2^{-n}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1-2^{1-n}\bigr)

\displaystyle=2^{-n}+\tfrac{1}{2}-2^{-n}\;=\;\tfrac{1}{2}.

Step 2.
For a general policy
\pi
with

i_{\pi}(n,n)=n
, conditioning on the number of heads

K\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,\tfrac{1}{2})
gives

v_{n}^{\pi}=2^{-n}\cdot 1+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k}2^{-n}\,v_{n-i_{\pi}(n,k)}^{\pi}.

Since
1\leq i_{\pi}(n,k)\leq k\leq n-1
for
k<n
, the subscript

n-i_{\pi}(n,k)
lies in
\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, and by strong induction
each
v_{n-i_{\pi}(n,k)}^{\pi}=\tfrac{1}{2}
. The same calculation as in
Step 1 gives
v_{n}^{\pi}=\tfrac{1}{2}
.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

A policy that refuses the immediate win at
k=n
(i.e.
i_{\pi}(n,n)<n
) achieves strictly less than
\tfrac{1}{2}
: the
contribution of the
k=n
event drops from
2^{-n}
to

2^{-n}\cdot v_{n-i_{\pi}(n,n)}^{\pi}<2^{-n}
, while all other terms
remain at most
\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\binom{n}{k}2^{-n}
, giving

v_{n}^{\pi}<\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Above the fair coin:
p>\tfrac{1}{2}

Theorem 3.1
.

For
p>\tfrac{1}{2}
and all
n\geq 2
:

(i)

w_{n,p}>w_{n-1,p}
, i.e.
n\mapsto w_{n,p}
is strictly
increasing.

(ii)

w_{n-1,p}<\dfrac{p^{n}}{p^{n}+q^{n}}
.

(iii)

Strategy
One
is optimal:
w_{n,p}=a_{n,p}
.

Proof.

Note that
n\mapsto\dfrac{p^{n}}{p^{n}+q^{n}}=\dfrac{1}{1+(q/p)^{n}}
is increasing for
p\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)
.
We prove (i) and (ii) simultaneously by strong induction on
n
.

Base case
n=2
.

One computes
w_{1,p}=p
and

w_{2,p}=p^{2}+(1-p^{2}-q^{2})p=3p^{2}-2p^{3}
. Then

w_{2,p}-w_{1,p}=p(1-p)(2p-1)>0
since
p>\tfrac{1}{2}
.
Also
w_{1,p}=p=p/(p+q)<p^{2}/(p^{2}+q^{2})
.

Inductive step.
Assume (i) and (ii) hold for all indices up
to
n-1
. Since
n\mapsto w_{n,p}
is increasing on

\{1,\dots,n-1\}
by hypothesis, the suffix-maximum

\max_{j\leq m\leq n-1}w_{m,p}=w_{n-1,p}
for every

j\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
. Hence the Bellman
equation (
1
) reduces to

w_{n,p}=p^{n}+(1-p^{n}-q^{n})\,w_{n-1,p},

which is exactly the recursion (
2
) for
strategy
One
. This proves (iii).

For (i),
w_{n,p}>w_{n-1,p}
iff

p^{n}(1-w_{n-1,p})>q^{n}w_{n-1,p}
, i.e.
w_{n-1,p}<p^{n}/(p^{n}+q^{n})
, which is hypothesis (ii) at level

n-1
.

For (ii) at level
n
, we need
w_{n,p}<p^{n+1}/(p^{n+1}+q^{n+1})
.
Using the recursion and the inductive bound on
w_{n-1,p}
, a
direct computation shows this reduces to the inequality

(q/p)^{n}<(q/p)^{n-1}
, which holds since
q/p<1
.
∎

3.1
The limit
W(p)=\lim_{n\to\infty}w_{n,p}

The collapse of the Bellman suffix-maximum, which is the central
content of Theorem
3.1
, has a useful by-product: the
Bellman recursion for
w_{n,p}
is genuinely
linear
for
every
n\geq 1
.

Corollary 3.2

(Linear recursion above
\tfrac{1}{2}
)
.

For
p>\tfrac{1}{2}
and every
n\geq 1
,

w_{n,p}\;=\;p^{n}+\bigl(1-p^{n}-q^{n}\bigr)\,w_{n-1,p},

(5)

with
w_{0,p}=1
.

Proof.

For
n=1
the sum in (
1
) is empty and

w_{1,p}=p=p^{1}+(1-p-q)\cdot 1
since
p+q=1
. For
n\geq 2
,
combine the suffix-max collapse from the proof of
Theorem
3.1
with the binomial identity

\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{j}p^{n-j}q^{j}=1-p^{n}-q^{n}
.
∎

The linear recursion (
5
) is the analogue, for

p>\tfrac{1}{2}
, of the linear
c_{n}
-recursion of
Proposition
4.8
below. It is structurally

simpler
: it holds for every
n\geq 1
(no preliminary
joint-induction step is needed), the coefficients are explicit
polynomials in
p
, and the bounded monotone sequence converges
trivially by the Weierstrass criterion.

Theorem 3.3

(Limit and explicit formula above
\tfrac{1}{2}
)
.

For every
p\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)
the limit

W(p):=\lim_{n\to\infty}w_{n,p}
exists, satisfies

p\leq W(p)<1
, and admits the closed-form representation

W(p)\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p^{k}\!\!\!\prod_{j=k+1}^{\infty}\!\!\bigl(1-p^{j}-q^{j}\bigr).

(6)

The series converges absolutely at geometric rate.

Proof.

Existence follows from Theorem
3.1
(i) (monotone,
bounded above by
1
). The lower bound
W(p)\geq w_{1,p}=p
is
immediate from monotonicity; combined with
p>\tfrac{1}{2}
this
gives
W(p)>\tfrac{1}{2}
.

For the explicit formula, iterate (
5
) from

k=1
up to
k=n
:

w_{n,p}\;=\;\underbrace{\prod_{k=1}^{n}\!\bigl(1-p^{k}-q^{k}\bigr)\cdot w_{0,p}}_{=\,0}\;+\;\sum_{k=1}^{n}p^{k}\!\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}\!\bigl(1-p^{j}-q^{j}\bigr),

(7)

where the first product vanishes because its
k=1
factor is

1-p-q=0
. For
j\geq 2
the factors

1-p^{j}-q^{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\binom{j}{i}p^{j-i}q^{i}
lie in
(0,1)

(positive from the
i=1
term; strictly less than
1
since we
subtracted
p^{j}+q^{j}>0
). Together with

\sum_{j\geq 2}(p^{j}+q^{j})<\infty
, the partial products

\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(1-p^{j}-q^{j})
converge as
n\to\infty
to a
strictly positive limit
\Pi_{k}\in(0,1)
, and the resulting
series
\sum_{k}p^{k}\Pi_{k}
converges absolutely by comparison with

\sum_{k}p^{k}
. By Tannery’s theorem
(cf. Knopp
[
5
, §41]
) with dominator
p^{k}
, limit
and summation commute, giving (
6
).

It remains to show
W(p)<1
. Set
U(p):=1-W(p)
and

u_{n}:=1-w_{n,p}
; subtracting (
5
) from
1
and
using
p^{n}+q^{n}+(1-p^{n}-q^{n})=1
gives the companion recursion

u_{n}=q^{n}+(1-p^{n}-q^{n})u_{n-1}
with
u_{0}=0
.
Iterating identically to (
7
) gives the
analogue

U(p)\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}q^{k}\Pi_{k},

which is a strictly positive sum (every term is positive:
q>0

and
\Pi_{k}>0
). Hence
W(p)=1-U(p)<1
.
∎

4.1
The deficit and its recursion

Definition 4.1
.

For
p\in(0,1)
and
n\geq 1
, define the

deficit

\Delta_{n,p}:=\tfrac{1}{2}-w_{n,p}
.

By Theorem
2.1
,
\Delta_{n,1/2}=0
for every
n\geq 1
.
At
n=0
the convention
w_{0,p}:=1
(“no coins left” is a
trivial win) yields
\Delta_{0,p}=-\tfrac{1}{2}
for every
p
; this
value will reappear in Proposition
4.2
.

Proposition 4.2
.

The deficit satisfies

\Delta_{n,p}\;=\;\frac{q^{n}-p^{n}}{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{j}p^{n-j}q^{j}\,\min_{j\leq m\leq n-1}\Delta_{m,p},

(8)

with
\Delta_{0,p}=-\tfrac{1}{2}
.
In particular:

(i)

For
p<\tfrac{1}{2}
:
\Delta_{n,p}>0
for every
n\geq 1

(i.e.
w_{n,p}<\tfrac{1}{2}
).

(ii)

For
p>\tfrac{1}{2}
:
\Delta_{n,p}<0
for every
n\geq 1

(i.e.
w_{n,p}>\tfrac{1}{2}
).

Proof.

Substitute
w_{m,p}=\tfrac{1}{2}-\Delta_{m,p}
into the
Bellman equation (
1
). Using

\max_{m}(\tfrac{1}{2}-\Delta_{m})=\tfrac{1}{2}-\min_{m}\Delta_{m}
and the
binomial identity
\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{j}p^{n-j}q^{j}=(p+q)^{n}-p^{n}-q^{n}=1-p^{n}-q^{n}
:

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}-\Delta_{n,p}

\displaystyle=p^{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{j}p^{n-j}q^{j}\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}-\min_{j\leq m\leq n-1}\Delta_{m,p}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=p^{n}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1-p^{n}-q^{n}\bigr)-\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{j}p^{n-j}q^{j}\min_{j\leq m\leq n-1}\Delta_{m,p}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{p^{n}-q^{n}}{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{j}p^{n-j}q^{j}\min_{j\leq m\leq n-1}\Delta_{m,p},

and rearranging gives (
8
).
The sign claims follow by induction using
q^{n}-p^{n}>0
(resp.
<0
)
and the positivity (resp. negativity) of the
\min
terms.
∎

4.2
First-order expansion

Set
p=\tfrac{1}{2}-\delta
with
\delta>0
small. Then

q=\tfrac{1}{2}+\delta
, and the constant term in (
8
) is

\frac{q^{n}-p^{n}}{2}=\frac{(\tfrac{1}{2}+\delta)^{n}-(\tfrac{1}{2}-\delta)^{n}}{2}=\frac{n\delta}{2^{n-1}}+O(\delta^{3}).

The binomial weights become
\binom{n}{j}2^{-n}+O(\delta)
, and the

\min
terms are
O(\delta)
by Proposition
4.2
.
Thus
\Delta_{n,p}=O(\delta)
.

Proposition 4.3

(First-order coefficient)
.

Write
\Delta_{n,p}=c_{n}\delta+O(\delta^{2})
as
\delta\to 0^{+}
.
Then
c_{1}=1
and for
n\geq 2
:

c_{n}\;=\;\frac{n}{2^{n-1}}+\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{j}\,\min_{j\leq m\leq n-1}c_{m}.

(9)

Proof.

Match first-order coefficients in (
8
). The
constant term contributes
n/2^{n-1}
. Each binomial weight is

\binom{n}{j}2^{-n}+O(\delta)
; the
\min
of the

\Delta_{m}=c_{m}\delta+O(\delta^{2})
at first order is

(\min c_{m})\delta+O(\delta^{2})
. Products of
O(\delta)
terms are

O(\delta^{2})
.
∎

4.3
Collapse, lower bound, and linear recursion

The four results below — the asymptotic collapse of the
\min

in (
9
) (Lemma
4.5
), the uniform lower
bound
c_{n}\geq\tfrac{27}{16}
for
n\geq 4
(Lemma
4.6
), the strict
decrease of
(c_{n})_{n\geq 5}
(Lemma
4.7
), and the
linear recursion for
n\geq 7
(Proposition
4.8
) — are
mutually entangled: each, taken alone, depends on parts of the
others. Stating them in isolation produces an apparent circular
dependency. We therefore state all four results first and then
prove them simultaneously by induction on
n
, with the IH at level

<n
supplying exactly what is needed at level
n
.

Lemma 4.5

(Asymptotic collapse)
.

For all
n\geq 7
and
j\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
:

\min_{j\leq m\leq n-1}c_{m}\;=\;\begin{cases}c_{j}&\text{if }j\in\{1,2,3\},\\
c_{n-1}&\text{if }j\in\{4,\dots,n-1\}.\end{cases}

(10)

Lemma 4.6
.

c_{n}\geq\tfrac{27}{16}
for every
n\geq 4
.

Lemma 4.7
.

The sequence
(c_{n})_{n\geq 5}
is strictly decreasing.

Proposition 4.8

(Linear recursion)
.

For
n\geq 7
:

c_{n}\;=\;A_{n}+(1-B_{n})\,c_{n-1},

(11)

where

A_{n}:=\frac{n}{2^{n-1}}+\frac{n\,c_{1}+\binom{n}{2}c_{2}+\binom{n}{3}c_{3}}{2^{n}},\qquad B_{n}:=\frac{2+n+\binom{n}{2}+\binom{n}{3}}{2^{n}}.

(12)

Moreover the algebraic identity

A_{n}-\tfrac{27}{16}B_{n}\;=\;-\frac{3(n^{2}-15n+36)}{32\cdot 2^{n}}

(13)

holds for every
n\geq 1
(independently of the linear recursion).

Joint proof of Lemmas
4.5
,

4.6
,
4.7
and Proposition
4.8
.

The identity (
13
) is a direct algebraic computation
from (
12
) using
\binom{n}{2}=\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}
and

\binom{n}{3}=\tfrac{n(n-1)(n-2)}{6}
; we use it freely below.
Formally, it is verified in Lean as the lemma

alg_id
;
see Appendix
A
, Table
3
.

Let
P(n)
denote the conjunction of the following
four claims (each understood vacuously when its index range is
empty):

(a)

Lemma
4.5
holds at level
n
(when
n\geq 7
);

(b)

c_{n}\geq\tfrac{27}{16}
(when
n\geq 4
);

(c)

c_{n}<c_{n-1}
(when
n\geq 6
);

(d)

the linear recursion (
11
) holds at level
n

(when
n\geq 7
).

We prove
P(n)
for all
n\geq 4
by strong induction on
n
.

Base cases
n\in\{4,5,6\}
.
By Example
4.4
,

c_{4}=\tfrac{111}{64}
,
c_{5}=\tfrac{3555}{2048}
,

c_{6}=\tfrac{113337}{65536}
, all of which exceed

\tfrac{27}{16}=\tfrac{108}{64}
, so (b) holds. Also
c_{5}>c_{6}
,
so (c) holds at
n=6
. Claims (a) and (d) are vacuous for

n\leq 6
.

Inductive step.
Fix
n\geq 7
and assume
P(m)
for all

4\leq m<n
. In particular: by IH(b),

c_{m}\geq\tfrac{27}{16}
for
4\leq m\leq n-1
; by IH(c),

c_{5}>c_{6}>\dots>c_{n-1}
. We now establish (a)–(d) at level
n
.

(a) Collapse.
For
j\in\{1,2,3\}
: from

c_{1}=1<c_{2}=\tfrac{3}{2}<c_{3}=\tfrac{27}{16}
(Example
4.4
)
and
c_{m}\geq\tfrac{27}{16}=c_{3}
for
m\geq 4
(IH(b)), the minimum
over
\{j,\dots,n-1\}
is
c_{j}
.

For
j\in\{4,\dots,n-1\}
: iterating IH(c) gives the chain

c_{5}>c_{6}>\dots>c_{n-1}
, so in particular

c_{m}\geq c_{n-1}
for every
m\in\{5,\dots,n-1\}
. For
m=4
,

c_{4}=\tfrac{111}{64}>c_{6}
(Example
4.4
); using the
segment
c_{6}\geq c_{n-1}
of the same chain, we obtain
c_{4}\geq c_{n-1}
as well. Hence the minimum over
\{j,\dots,n-1\}
for

j\geq 4
is
c_{n-1}
.

(d) Linear recursion.
Substituting the collapse (a)
just proved into (
9
) and splitting the sum at
j=3

yields (
11
)–(
12
). Formally, this
substitution is verified in Lean as the theorem

c_linear_rec
;
see Appendix
A
, Table
3
.

(b) Lower bound.
From (d) and (
13
),

c_{n}-\tfrac{27}{16}\;=\;\bigl(A_{n}-\tfrac{27}{16}B_{n}\bigr)+(1-B_{n})\bigl(c_{n-1}-\tfrac{27}{16}\bigr).

By IH(b),
c_{n-1}-\tfrac{27}{16}\geq 0
. Note also

0<B_{n}<1
for all
n\geq 7
: the numerator

2+n+\binom{n}{2}+\binom{n}{3}
in (
12
) is strictly
less than
2^{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}
, the difference being

\sum_{j=4}^{n-1}\binom{n}{j}>0
. For
n\in\{7,\dots,12\}
, the
quadratic
n^{2}-15n+36
is non-positive on
\{3,\dots,12\}

(its roots are
3
and
12
), so
A_{n}-\tfrac{27}{16}B_{n}\geq 0

by (
13
), and (b) follows. For
n\geq 13
we argue
cumulatively: define
\varepsilon_{n}:=c_{n}-\tfrac{27}{16}
and

\delta_{n}:=3(n^{2}-15n+36)/(32\cdot 2^{n})
,
so (d) gives
\varepsilon_{n}=(1-B_{n})\varepsilon_{n-1}-\delta_{n}

for every
n\geq 13
. Iterating this identity from level
12

upwards and using the trivial bound
0<1-B_{k}\leq 1
yields, for
every
n\geq 13
,

\varepsilon_{n}\;=\;\prod_{k=13}^{n}(1-B_{k})\,\varepsilon_{12}\;-\;\sum_{k=13}^{n}\!\Bigl(\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(1-B_{j})\Bigr)\delta_{k}\;\geq\;\Bigl(\prod_{k=13}^{n}(1-B_{k})\Bigr)\varepsilon_{12}\;-\;\sum_{k=13}^{n}\delta_{k}.

(14)

The starting value
\varepsilon_{12}>\tfrac{1}{60}
is verified by
exact rational computation (formally:

c_twelve_buffer
);
the cumulative negative contribution is bounded by

\sum_{n\geq 13}\delta_{n}\leq\tfrac{297}{65536}<\tfrac{1}{200}

(using
\sum_{n\geq 13}n^{2}/2^{n}=\tfrac{99}{2048}
; formally:

delta_tail_bound
);
meanwhile
\prod_{n\geq 13}(1-B_{n})>\tfrac{7}{8}
(using

\sum_{n\geq 13}B_{n}<\tfrac{1}{8}
; formally:

B_tail_bound
).
Inserting these three inequalities into (
14
) gives

\varepsilon_{n}>\tfrac{1}{60}\cdot\tfrac{7}{8}-\tfrac{1}{200}=\tfrac{23}{2400}>0
for every
n\geq 13
. The iterated inequality
itself is formally verified (in the slightly weaker
Bernoulli-style form

\prod(1-B_{k})\geq 1-\sum B_{k}
) as

cum_eps_bound
.

(
Note for an inductive treatment:
the sequence

(\delta_{n})_{n\geq 13}
is not monotone — one computes

\delta_{14}/\delta_{13}=\tfrac{11}{10}
— but its ratio
decreases through
1
at
n=14
and tends to
\tfrac{1}{2}
. An
iterated proof of the bound on
\sum_{n\geq 13}\delta_{n}
should
therefore split at
n=14
; the closed-form route above sidesteps
this subtlety.)

(c) Strict decrease.
By (d),
c_{n}-c_{n-1}=A_{n}-B_{n}c_{n-1}
.
Using
c_{n-1}\geq\tfrac{27}{16}
(IH(b)) and
B_{n}>0
, this is

\leq A_{n}-\tfrac{27}{16}B_{n}<0
for
n\geq 13
by (
13
)
(
n^{2}-15n+36>0
there). For
n\in\{7,\dots,12\}
,
c_{n}<c_{n-1}

is verified by direct rational computation (chain via the linear
recursion from
c_{6}
up to
c_{n}
, then numerical comparison).
This completes the inductive step. The combined statement of (c)
for all
n\geq 5
is exposed as

c_strict_anti_from_five
;
the corresponding statement of (b) for all
m\geq 4
as

c_ge_27_16_full
.
∎

4.4
The limit

Since
(c_{n})_{n\geq 5}
is strictly decreasing
(Lemma
4.7
) and bounded below by
\tfrac{27}{16}

(Lemma
4.6
), the Weierstraß monotone-convergence
criterion gives at once:

Corollary 4.9

(Existence of the limit)
.

The limit
L:=\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{n}
exists and satisfies

\tfrac{27}{16}\leq L\leq c_{5}=\tfrac{3555}{2048}
.

Formally verified as

c_limit_exists
.
The actual mathematical content of this subsection is the

explicit
formula for
L
:

Theorem 4.10

(Explicit formula for the limit)
.

For any
n_{0}\geq 7
,

L\;=\;c_{n_{0}-1}\prod_{m\geq n_{0}}(1-B_{m})\;+\;\sum_{k\geq n_{0}}A_{k}\prod_{m>k}(1-B_{m}).

(15)

Both the infinite product and the series converge at geometric
rate since
A_{n},B_{n}=O(n^{3}/2^{n})
. Numerically,

L\;=\;1.70347176087173673645\ldots

(16)

Proof.

Fix
n_{0}\geq 7
. Iterating the linear
recursion (
11
) from
n_{0}
up to
n
yields, for
every
n\geq n_{0}
,

c_{n}\;=\;c_{n_{0}-1}\prod_{m=n_{0}}^{n}(1-B_{m})\;+\;\sum_{k=n_{0}}^{n}A_{k}\prod_{m=k+1}^{n}(1-B_{m}).

(17)

By Corollary
4.9
we already know that the
left-hand side converges to
L
; the content of the theorem is
that the right-hand side converges to the asserted explicit
expression. We pass to the limit
n\to\infty
in each summand.

Step 1 (infinite product).
Since
B_{m}=O(m^{3}/2^{m})
, the
series
\sum_{m\geq n_{0}}B_{m}
converges absolutely. Because

0<B_{m}<1
for all
m\geq 7
(shown in the inductive proof of
the previous subsection), the partial products

\prod_{m=n_{0}}^{n}(1-B_{m})
converge as
n\to\infty
to a strictly
positive limit
\prod_{m\geq n_{0}}(1-B_{m})
; cf. Knopp

[
5
, §29]
.

Step 2 (series of products).
For each fixed
k\geq n_{0}

the same argument gives

\prod_{m=k+1}^{n}(1-B_{m})\to\prod_{m>k}(1-B_{m})
as
n\to\infty
.
Moreover every factor lies in
(0,1)
, so each finite product
lies in
(0,1]
, and

\Bigl|A_{k}\prod_{m=k+1}^{n}(1-B_{m})\Bigr|\;\leq\;|A_{k}|\qquad(n\geq k).

(18)

Since
A_{k}=O(k^{3}/2^{k})
, the series
\sum_{k\geq n_{0}}|A_{k}|

converges. The bound (
18
) therefore supplies
a summable dominant, so by Tannery’s theorem — the dominated
convergence theorem for series, see Knopp
[
5
, §41]
—
limit and summation commute:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=n_{0}}^{n}A_{k}\prod_{m=k+1}^{n}(1-B_{m})\;=\;\sum_{k\geq n_{0}}A_{k}\prod_{m>k}(1-B_{m}).

Conclusion.
Taking
n\to\infty
in (
17
)
and using Steps 1 and 2 gives (
15
). Both the
product and the series converge at geometric rate
since
A_{n},B_{n}=O(n^{3}/2^{n})
.
∎

4.5
Shape of the optimal-value sequence near

p=\tfrac{1}{2}

Corollary 4.11
.

For
\delta>0
sufficiently small and
p=\tfrac{1}{2}-\delta
:

(i)

w_{n-1,p}-w_{n,p}=(c_{n}-c_{n-1})\delta+O(\delta^{2})
.

(ii)

The sequence
n\mapsto w_{n,p}
has, to first order
in
\delta
, a strict local minimum at
n=5
(since
c_{5}>c_{4}

and
c_{5}>c_{6}
).

(iii)

c_{n}
is eventually

w_{n,p}
is eventually increasing.

Remark 4.12
.

Numerical computation shows that the sequence

bounded away

\tfrac{1}{2}

6
Discussion

For
p>\tfrac{1}{2}
, the situation is much simpler than for

p<\tfrac{1}{2}
: the linear recursion of
Corollary
3.2
holds for every
n\geq 1
, and the
limit
W(p)=\lim_{n\to\infty}w_{n,p}
admits the closed
form (
6
); numerically,
W(p)
is a
smooth, strictly increasing function of
p\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)
with

p\leq W(p)<1
(Figure
1
). The bulk of this
paper is therefore concerned with the more delicate regime

p<\tfrac{1}{2}
, where two features of the numerical data in
§
5
deserve further comment.

(a) Local maxima are a non-perturbative phenomenon.

Corollary
4.11
(iii) rules out local maxima of

n\mapsto w_{n,p}

to first order in
\delta=\tfrac{1}{2}-p
:
for any fixed
n
, there is a
\delta_{0}(n)>0
below which the
predicted sign of
c_{n+1}-c_{n}<0
forces
w_{n,p}<w_{n+1,p}
.
The local maximum at
n=9
for
p=0.42
(
\delta=0.08
), and the
one at
n=15
for
p=0.45
(
\delta=0.05
), correspond to the
threshold
\delta_{0}(n)
being
larger
than the actual
perturbation size at that
n
, so higher-order terms dominate.
Numerically,
\delta_{0}(n)
appears to decay rapidly in
n
(we
see no local maxima at all for
p\leq 0.35
within
n\leq 20
),
which is consistent with the expected geometric decay of the
first-order constants
A_{n}
and
B_{n}=O(n^{3}/2^{n})
.

(b) The first-order picture holds robustly near
p=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

For
p=0.49
(
\delta=0.01
), the only local extremum within

n\leq 20
is the predicted minimum at
n=5
, with no spurious
oscillations. This confirms numerically that the first-order
range-of-validity extends at least to
\delta=0.01
. Pinning down
the size of
\delta_{0}(n)
as a function of
n
remains open; we
conjecture that it decays roughly like
C/n^{2}
for some absolute
constant
C
.

Remark 6.1
.

The picture for
p\leq 0.35
in our data — a strictly
decreasing sequence with no local extrema within
n\leq 20
—
is consistent with, but does not prove, monotone decrease on
all of
\mathbb{N}
. Van Doorn
[
8
]
proves that
strategy
All
is
not
optimal for
p<\tfrac{1}{2}
(there
exists
n
with
w_{n,p}\neq b_{n,p}
); equivalently, the
sequence
n\mapsto w_{n,p}
is not monotone non-increasing.
The failure of monotonicity is, however, not always detectable
as a local extremum in
n
; it can manifest as strict inequality

w_{n,p}>b_{n,p}
without the sequence
w_{n,p}
itself having
oscillations. Investigating this gap is beyond the scope of the
present paper.
