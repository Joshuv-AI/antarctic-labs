How large part of a graph can be covered by the neighborhoods of k k vertices?
János Pach

Abstract
Let k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 be fixed integer, 0 < c < 1 0<c<1 a constant. Consider a graph G G with n n vertices and average degree c ​ n cn . We answer a question of Simon Griffiths by showing that G G has k k vertices such that their neighborhoods together cover at least min ⁡ ( 1 − ( 1 − c ) k , c ) ​ n \min(1-(1-c)^{k},\sqrt{c})n vertices. This result is essentially tight.



1
Introduction

Let
G
be a fixed graph with vertex set
V
and edge set
E
, where
n=|V|.
For any vertex
u\in V,
let
N(u)
denote the
neighborhood
of
u
in
G
, that is
N(u)=\{x\in V:ux\in E\}
. The size of
N(u)
is the
degree
of
u
, and is denoted by
d(u)
. Let
\Delta(G)=\Delta
and
\overline{d}(G)=\overline{d}
stand for the
maximum
degree and the
average
degree of the vertices of
G
. Clearly, we have
\Delta\geq\overline{d}.

Let
k\geq 2
be a fixed integer, and assume that the average degree of the vertices,

\overline{d}(G)=\sum_{u\in V}d(u)/n=cn.

We want to estimate the largest number
h(G)
such that
G
has
k
vertices,
u_{0},u_{1},\ldots u_{k}
such that
|N(u_{0})\cup\ldots\cup N(u_{k-1})|\geq h(G).

We start with two simple examples. Consider a random graph
\mathbf{G}(n,c)
on
n
vertices with edge probability
c\;(0<c<1)
. For a given set of
k
vertices,
u_{0},\ldots,u_{k-1},
the probability that a fixed vertex different from them is not connected to any of them is
(1-c)^{k}
, Thus, the expected size of

\bigcup_{i=0}^{k-1}N(u_{i})\setminus\{u_{0},\ldots,u_{k-1}\}=(n-k)(1-(1-c)^{k})=(1-(1-c)^{k}+o(1))n.

It easily follows by Chernoff’s bound
[
1
,
4
]
that, as
n\rightarrow\infty
, almost surely the size of the union of the neighborhoods of every
k
-tuple of vertices will be at most
(1-(1-c)^{k}+o(1))n.
The same estimates hold for quasi-random graphs
[
3
]
.

Our second example is a graph
G^{\prime}
that consists of a complete graph with
\lceil\sqrt{c}n\rceil+1
vertices and its remaining vertices are isolated. Clearly, the average degree of
G^{\prime}
satisfies
\overline{d}(G^{\prime})\geq cn
, but the size of the union of no set of neighborhoods in
G^{\prime}
exceeds
\lceil\sqrt{c}n\rceil+1
.

At the problem session of the 60th birthday conference of Bruce Reed (Oxford, July 2022), Simon Griffiths conjectured that for every
c,\;0<c<1,
one of the above two graphs is asymptotically optimal, that is, the following theorem is true.

Theorem 1
.

Let
k\geq 2
, and let
G=(V,E)
be a graph on
n
vertices with average degree
cn
, where
0\leq c\leq 1
.
Then there exist vertices
u_{0},\dots,u_{k-1}\in V
such that

\bigl|N(u_{0})\cup\cdots\cup N(u_{k-1})\bigr|\geq\min\bigl\{(1-(1-c))^{k}n,\,\sqrt{c}\,n\bigr\}.

The problem was also featured as Questions 1 and 2 in the seminal paper of José D. Alvarado, Leonardo Gonçalves de Oliveira, and Simon Griffiths
[
2
]
(page 842).

In the next section, we prove the above result. Note that formally the theorem is true for
k=1
, too, and also for
c=0
or
c=1
, but these cases are trivial.

Before turning to the proof, we remark that an easy averaging argument shows that the theorem is asymptotically true for
k=2
, provided that
G
is
regular
, that is
d(u)-cn
for every
u\in V
. Let
H
denote the complement of
G
, and let
N_{H}(w)
stand for the neighborhood of
w
in
H
. Using the fact that
|N(u_{1})\cup N(u_{2})|\geq n-2-|N_{H}(u_{1})\cap N_{H}(u_{2})|,
it is sufficient to show that there exist
u_{1},u_{2}\in V
such that

|N_{H}(u_{1})\cap N_{H}(u_{2})|\leq((1-c)^{2}+o(1))n.

(1)

To see this, estimate the sum of
|N_{H}(u_{1})\cap N_{H}(u_{2})|
over all the
\binom{n}{2}
unordered pairs of distinct vertices
\{u_{1},u_{2}\}.
We have

\sum_{\{u_{1},u_{2}\}}|N_{H}(u_{1})\cap N_{H}(u_{2})|=\sum_{v\in V}\binom{d_{H}(v)}{2}=n\binom{n-1-cn}{2}.

Dividing the right-hand side by
\binom{n}{2},
inequality (
1
) follows.

After this initial success, we expected that, with a little extra effort, the argument could be extended to all degree sequences. We explored several plausible greedy strategies to find
k
neighborhoods that collectively maximize vertex coverage; however, their outcomes proved resistant to analysis. Ultimately, we returned to the averaging approach, albeit with a small tweak: we started the procedure with a vertex of maximum degree. Miraculously, this slight modification made the underlying calculations tractable. The devil resides in the details of the calculus!

2
Proof of Theorem
1

Choose a vertex
u_{0}\in V
of
maximum
degree
\Delta,
and denote its neighborhood in
N(u_{0})
by
S
. Write

d(u_{0})=\Delta=sn,\qquad S:=N(u_{0}),\qquad T:=V\setminus S.

Since
\Delta
is at least the average degree
\overline{d}(G)
, we have
s\geq c
.

If
s\geq\sqrt{c}
, then already

|N(u_{0})|=\Delta=sn\geq\sqrt{c}\,n,

and we are done.

Thus, we may assume throughout that

c\leq s<\sqrt{c}.

Since every vertex of
S
has degree at most
\Delta
, we have

\sum_{u\in V(G)}|N(u)\cap S|=\sum_{s\in S}d(s)\leq|S|\,\Delta=\Delta^{2}=s^{2}n^{2}.

As the total degree sum is
cn^{2}
, it follows that

\sum_{u\in V(G)}|N(u)\cap T|=\sum_{u\in T}d(u)=cn^{2}-\sum_{u\in V(G)}|N(u)\cap S|\geq(c-s^{2})n^{2}.

(2)

Choose
u_{1},\dots,u_{k-1}\in V(G)
independently and uniformly at random.
For any
u\in T
. The probability that
u
lies in at least one of the neighborhoods

N(u_{1}),\dots,N(u_{k-1})
is

1-\left(1-\frac{d(u)}{n}\right)^{k-1}.

Notice that the function

f(x):=1-(1-x)^{k-1}\qquad(0\leq t\leq 1).

(3)

is nonnegative, concave on the interval
[0,1]
, and we have
f(0)=0,f(1)=1
.

Since
d(u)/n\leq\Delta/n=s\leq\sqrt{c}\leq 1
, by concavity we obtain

\frac{f\!\left(d(u)/n\right)}{d(u)/n}\geq\frac{f(s)}{s}.

Therefore, in view of (
2
), the expected number of vertices of
T
covered by

N(u_{1})\cup\cdots\cup N(u_{k-1})
is at least

\frac{f(s)}{s}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{u\in T}d(u)\geq\frac{1-(1-s)^{k-1}}{s}\,(c-s^{2})n.

Hence, for a suitable choice of (not necessarily distinct)
u_{1},\dots,u_{k-1}\in V
, we have

\bigl|(N(u_{1})\cup\cdots\cup N(u_{k-1}))\cap T\bigr|\geq\frac{1-(1-s)^{k-1}}{s}\,(c-s^{2})n.

Adding the neighborhood
S=N(u_{0})
, we conclude that

\bigl|N(u_{0})\cup N(u_{1})\cup\cdots\cup N(u_{k-1})\bigr|\geq\left(s+\frac{1-(1-s)^{k-1}}{s}(c-s^{2})\right)n.

Thus, it remains to prove that for
c\leq s\leq\sqrt{c},
the last expression is at least

\min\{1-(1-c)^{k},\sqrt{c}\}.

This is the content of the next lemma.

Let
k\geq 2
. To simplify the notation, set

F(s):=s+\frac{1-(1-s)^{k-1}}{s}(c-s^{2}),\qquad G(c):=1-(1-c)^{k}.

Lemma 2
.

Let
k\geq 2
be fixed. For every
\;0<c<1
and every
s\in[c,\sqrt{c}]
, we have

F(s)\geq\min\{G(c),\sqrt{c}\}.

Proof.

We split the proof into two cases.

Case 1:
G(c)\leq\sqrt{c}
.

It will be convenient to set a new variable

x:=1-s,

so that we have
x\in[1-\sqrt{c},\,1-c]
and

F(s)=(1-x)x^{k-1}+c\,\frac{1-x^{k-1}}{1-x}=(1-x)x^{k-1}+c\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}x^{i}.

Hence,

\displaystyle F(s)-G(c)

\displaystyle=x^{k-1}-x^{k}+\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}x^{i}-(1-c)\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}x^{i}-1+(1-c)^{k}

\displaystyle=(1-c)^{k}-(1-c)\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}x^{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}x^{i}-x^{k}

\displaystyle=\bigl((1-c)^{k}-x^{k}\bigr)+\bigl(x-(1-c)\bigr)\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}x^{i}

\displaystyle=\bigl((1-c)-x\bigr)\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(1-c)^{k-1-i}x^{i}-\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}x^{i}\right).

We need to show that
F(s)-G(c)\geq 0
. Observe that
1-c-x\geq 0.
If
1-c=x,
then we are done. Otherwise,

\frac{F(s)-G(c)}{1-c-x}=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(1-c)^{k-1-i}x^{i}-\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}x^{i}.

The right-hand side is a polynomial
P(x)
of degree
k-1
in
x
, with coefficients

(1-c)^{k-1}-1,\ (1-c)^{k-2}-1,\ \dots,\ (1-c)-1,\ 1.

Since
0<1-c<1
, this sequence has exactly one sign change. By Descartes’ rule of signs,
P(x)
has at most one positive root.
In view of the fact that
P(0)=(1-c)^{k-1}-1<0,
it follows that if a positive root exists, then
P(x)<0
before the root and
P(x)>0
after that.

Evaluating
P
at
x_{0}:=1-\sqrt{c}
and taking into account our assumption
G(c)\leq\sqrt{c}
, we find that

P(x_{0})=\frac{F(\sqrt{c})-G(c)}{1-c-x_{0}}=\sqrt{c}-G(c)\geq 0.

Hence,
P(x)\geq 0
for every
x\in[x_{0},1-c]
, which implies that

F(s)-G(c)\geq 0,
as required.

Case 2:
G(c)\geq\sqrt{c}
.

As before, we set
x:=1-s,
so that
x\in[1-\sqrt{c},\,1-c]
. Now we need to show that
F(s)\geq\sqrt{c}
.

A direct calculation, similar to the one used in Case 1, gives that

F(s)-\sqrt{c}=(\sqrt{c}-s)Q(x),

(4)

where

Q(x):=\sqrt{c}\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}x^{i}-x^{k-1}

is a polynomial of degree
k-1
is
x
.

Since
\sqrt{c}-s\geq 0
, it remains to show that
Q(x)\geq 0
on the interval
[1-\sqrt{c},1-c]
.

The coefficients of
Q(x)
are

\sqrt{c},\ \sqrt{c},\ \dots,\ \sqrt{c},\ -1,

so there is exactly one sign change.
By Descartes’ rule of signs,
Q(x)
has at most one positive root.
We have

Q(0)=\sqrt{c}>0,

and the leading coefficient is
-1
. Thus, if a positive root exists, then
Q(x)>0
before the root and
Q(x)<0
after that.
Now let
x_{0}:=1-c.

We compute

\displaystyle Q(x_{0})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{c}\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}(1-c)^{i}-(1-c)^{k-1}=\sqrt{c}\cdot\frac{1-(1-c)^{k-1}}{c}-(1-c)^{k-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-(1-c)^{k-1}}{\sqrt{c}}-(1-c)^{k-1}=\frac{1-(1-c)^{k-1}(1+\sqrt{c})}{\sqrt{c}}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle G(c)\geq\sqrt{c}

\displaystyle\iff 1-(1-c)^{k}\geq\sqrt{c}

\displaystyle\iff(1-c)^{k-1}(1-\sqrt{c})(1+\sqrt{c})\leq 1-\sqrt{c}

\displaystyle\iff(1-c)^{k-1}(1+\sqrt{c})\leq 1,

Thus, our assumption
G(c)\geq\sqrt{c}
is equivalent to the statement that

Q(x_{0})\geq 0.

Therefore, any positive root of
Q
is at least
x_{0}
, so
Q(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in[0,x_{0}]
.
In particular, we have
Q(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in[1-\sqrt{c},1-c]
. In view of (
4
), this implies that

F(s)-\sqrt{c}\geq 0.

as required.

Combining the above two cases yields

F(s)\geq\min\{G(c),\sqrt{c}\},

which completes the proof of Lemma
2
and, hence, the theorem.
