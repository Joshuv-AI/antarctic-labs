Influence Malleability in Linearized Attention: Dual Implications of Non-Convergent NTK Dynamics
Jose Marie Antonio Miñoza, Paulo Mario P. Medina, Sebastian C. Ibañez

Abstract
Understanding the theoretical foundations of attention mechanisms remains challenging due to their complex, non-linear dynamics. This work reveals a fundamental trade-off in the learning dynamics of linearized attention. Using a linearized attention mechanism with exact correspondence to a data-dependent Gram-induced kernel, both empirical and theoretical analysis through the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) framework shows that linearized attention does not converge to its infinite-width NTK limit, even at large widths. A spectral amplification result establishes this formally: the attention transformation cubes the Gram matrix’s condition number, requiring width m = Ω ⁡ ( κ 6 ) m=\Omega(\kappa^{6}) for convergence, a threshold that exceeds any practical width for natural image datasets. This non-convergence is characterized through influence malleability , the capacity to dynamically alter reliance on training examples. Attention exhibits 6–9 × \times higher malleability than ReLU networks, with dual implications: its data-dependent kernel can reduce approximation error by aligning with task structure, but this same sensitivity increases susceptibility to adversarial manipulation of training data. These findings suggest that attention’s power and vulnerability share a common origin in its departure from the kernel regime.



1
Introduction

Attention mechanisms have revolutionized deep learning across domains, yet the learning processes that give attention its characteristic flexibility lack rigorous theoretical characterization. Conventional approaches focus on architectural properties at initialization or final performance, missing the crucial dynamics of how attention learns.

Recent advances in Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) theory have enabled precise analysis of neural network learning dynamics through kernel methods
(
11
;
14
)
, yet attention mechanisms have remained largely outside this theoretical framework. The NTK framework predicts that sufficiently wide networks operate in a “lazy training” regime where the kernel remains approximately constant during training, enabling kernel-based analysis.

This work makes a surprising empirical discovery: linearized attention mechanisms do not converge to their infinite-width NTK limits, even at large widths. As shown in Figure
1
, while standard two-layer ReLU networks exhibit the expected monotonic decrease in NTK distance
\|f_{m}-f_{\text{NTK}}\|
as width increases, attention-enhanced architectures show fundamentally different behavior: NTK distance fails to decrease and instead remains high or increases. The trajectory varies by dataset (non-monotonic on MNIST, monotonically increasing on CIFAR-10), but both exhibit the key signature of non-convergence. This reveals that attention operates in the
feature learning
regime
(
5
)
, where representations evolve substantially during training.

\|f_{m}-f_{\text{NTK}}\|
across network widths, where
f_{m}
is the finite-width trained model and
f_{\text{NTK}}

To quantify the implications of this non-convergence,
influence malleability
is introduced, defined as the capacity to dynamically alter reliance on training examples as their quality changes. The analysis reveals that attention exhibits 6–9
\times
higher influence malleability compared to standard ReLU networks, providing a measurable signature of attention’s sensitivity to training data.

Related Work

Neural Tangent Kernel Theory.
The NTK framework
(
11
)
establishes that infinitely wide networks trained with gradient descent become equivalent to kernel methods. Extensions address generalization
(
2
)
, depth scaling
(
8
)
, and arbitrary architectures
(
22
)
. However,
21
demonstrate that NTK theory only applies to networks “orders of magnitude wider than deep,” a condition practical architectures violate.

Lazy vs. Feature Learning.

5
distinguish “lazy training” (kernel regime, frozen features) from “feature learning” (rich regime, evolving representations). The empirical finding that attention’s NTK distance
increases
with width suggests it operates firmly in the feature learning regime.

NTK-Based Influence Functions.

23
establish that NTK-based influence computation provides superior stability compared to classical IHVP methods, enabling reliable measurement of training data dependencies.
15
study how influence functions evolve during training, showing that data importance shifts as networks learn, complementing the present focus on
architectural
differences in influence dynamics.

Feature Learning Theory.

17
provide provable guarantees for neural network feature learning, establishing conditions under which networks move beyond the kernel regime. The present work provides empirical evidence that attention mechanisms naturally satisfy these conditions, offering a concrete architectural mechanism for the abstract feature learning phenomenon.

Linearized Attention.

1
show that linearized attention reproduces Transformer optimization dynamics.
6
provide efficient implementations via Performers.
9
extend NTK theory to attention, showing multi-head attention behaves as Gaussian processes as heads
\to\infty
.

Attention as Kernel Regression.
The connection between attention and classical nonparametric statistics is well-established: softmax attention implements Nadaraya-Watson kernel regression
(
16
;
20
)
with exponential kernel. This perspective motivates the present analysis, where linearized attention corresponds to an unnormalized Nadaraya-Watson estimator with linear kernel, enabling exact kernel characterization.

3
Methodology

3.1
Linearized Attention Architecture

A parameter-free attention mechanism is designed that admits exact kernel characterization.

Definition 3.1

(Linearized Attention)
.

For input matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, the linearized attention mechanism is:

f^{\text{att}}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}

(1)

Remark 3.2

(Correspondence to Standard Attention)
.

This corresponds to scaled dot-product attention with identity projections (
\mathbf{W}_{Q}=\mathbf{W}_{K}=\mathbf{W}_{V}=\mathbf{I}
) and linearized softmax (
\exp(A_{ij})\approx 1+A_{ij}
). The form
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}
captures the essential quadratic interaction structure; Proposition
B.4
(Appendix) shows that full-rank QKV projections preserve all structural properties, so identity projections provide a canonical representative without loss of generality. The linearized attention also implements an unnormalized Nadaraya-Watson estimator
(
16
;
20
)
with linear kernel, inheriting adaptive, data-dependent smoothing properties.

This operation implements kernel-weighted feature aggregation, where
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
denotes the
entire
training set; the transformation is computed once over all
n
training examples, making the architecture transductive. In practice, the rows of
f^{\text{att}}(\mathbf{X})
are
\ell_{2}
-normalized before being passed to the MLP to ensure comparable input magnitudes across architectures. The complete architecture combines this with a two-layer MLP:

f_{\text{MLP-Attn}}(\mathbf{X})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r}\sigma(\mathbf{w}_{r}^{T}\cdot f^{\text{att}}(\mathbf{X}))

(2)

where
\sigma
is ReLU,
\mathbf{w}_{r}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\kappa^{2}\mathbf{I})
with
\kappa>0
controlling initialization scale, and
a_{r}\in\{-1,+1\}
are fixed.

3.2
Influence Malleability

The data-dependent kernel structure of linearized attention (Section
3
) suggests that attention should exhibit heightened
sensitivity
to training data. To quantify this,
influence malleability
is introduced: it captures how readily a model’s reliance on specific training examples changes under perturbation. High malleability indicates high sensitivity; low malleability indicates rigid, fixed data dependencies.

Definition 3.3

(Influence Function)
.

Following
12
and the NTK-based formulation of
23
, the influence of removing training example
(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})
on a test prediction is:

I(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{\text{test}})=\frac{1}{2}\left[(f^{\backslash i}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{test}})-y_{\text{test}})^{2}-(f(\mathbf{x}_{\text{test}})-y_{\text{test}})^{2}\right]

(3)

where
f^{\backslash i}
denotes the model trained without point
i
. Positive influence indicates removing the point increases loss (helpful example); negative influence indicates removing it decreases loss (harmful example). This is computed efficiently via leave-one-out formulas using the
empirical
finite-width kernel matrix
(\mathbf{K}_{m}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}
without retraining. Importantly, the empirical NTK
\mathbf{K}_{m}
is well-defined regardless of whether the architecture converges to its infinite-width limit; it captures how each model actually represents training data relationships at practical widths.

Definition 3.4

(Influence Malleability)
.

Given training set
\mathcal{S}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, test point
(\mathbf{x}_{\text{test}},y_{\text{test}})
, and perturbation budget
\epsilon>0
:

1.

Compute influence scores
I_{i}^{\text{orig}}=I(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{\text{test}})
for all training points.

2.

Define the
high-influence set

\mathcal{H}_{\tau}=\{i:I_{i}^{\text{orig}}>\text{quantile}(I^{\text{orig}},1-\tau)\}
where
\tau\in(0,1)
is the selection threshold (
\tau=0.1
for top 10%).

3.

Generate adversarial examples
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\text{adv}}
via PGD with
\|\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\text{adv}}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
.

4.

Compute perturbed scores
I_{i}^{\text{adv}}
.

5.

The
Influence Flip Rate
is:

\text{FlipRate}=\frac{|\{i\in\mathcal{H}_{\tau}:\text{sign}(I_{i}^{\text{orig}})\neq\text{sign}(I_{i}^{\text{adv}})\}|}{|\mathcal{H}_{\tau}|}

(4)

The flip rate captures the capacity to
re-evaluate
which examples help versus hurt prediction, which is the signature of sensitivity to training data. PGD with
\epsilon=0.3
follows standard practice
(
10
)
; supplementary experiments confirm the ordering is preserved across
\epsilon\in\{0.1,0.2,0.3,0.5\}
. As a complementary measure,
influence stability
is assessed via Spearman’s rank correlation
\rho
between original and perturbed influence rankings; lower
\rho
indicates greater malleability. Three data intervention strategies are evaluated:
Curated
(removing the top-
\tau
influential examples from training),
Transformed
(replacing them with adversarial versions), and
Adversarial
(applying PGD perturbations to all training data).

4
Theoretical Analysis

This section establishes the theoretical foundations connecting linearized attention to influence malleability: the exact Gram-induced kernel (Theorem
4.1
), the sequential NTK (Theorem
4.2
), influence stability (Theorem
4.3
), data-dependent kernel sensitivity (Proposition
4.5
), and spectral amplification explaining NTK non-convergence (Theorem
4.7
).

Theorem 4.1

(Data-Dependent Gram-Induced Kernel)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
with
\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}=1
for all
i
. The kernel induced by linearized attention
f^{\text{att}}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}
is:

K_{\text{LinAttn}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})=\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{n}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{k})(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{\ell})(\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{j})

(5)

where
k
and
\ell
are summation indices over all
n
training samples
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
in
\mathbf{X}
. This is a data-dependent kernel induced by the Gram matrix
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}
, exhibiting transitive similarity structure: influence flows from
\mathbf{x}_{i}
through intermediate points
\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{\ell}
to
\mathbf{x}_{j}
.

Proof.
The
i
-th transformed feature is
[f^{\text{att}}(\mathbf{X})]_{i}=\sum_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{k})\mathbf{x}_{k}
. The kernel is the inner product of two such expansions:

\displaystyle K_{\text{LinAttn}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\sum_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{k})\mathbf{x}_{k},\;\sum_{\ell}(\mathbf{x}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{\ell})\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{k})(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{\ell})(\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{j})

yielding the transitive chain
i\to k\to\ell\to j
. In matrix form,
\mathbf{K}_{\text{LinAttn}}=\mathbf{G}^{3}
where
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}
is the Gram matrix. See Appendix
B
for the full derivation.

Theorem 4.2

(NTK for Sequential Architecture)
.

Consider the MLP-Attn architecture
g(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r}\sigma(\mathbf{w}_{r}^{T}f^{\text{att}}(\mathbf{x}))
with
\mathbf{w}_{r}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\kappa^{2}\mathbf{I})
and fixed
a_{r}\in\{-1,+1\}
. In the infinite-width limit
m\to\infty
, the NTK converges to:

K_{\text{seq}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}}[\sigma^{\prime}(\mathbf{w}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\sigma^{\prime}(\mathbf{w}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})]\cdot\langle\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\rangle

(6)

where
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=f^{\text{att}}(\mathbf{x})
and
\sigma^{\prime}=\mathbf{1}[\cdot>0]
is the ReLU derivative.

Proof.
Since
a_{r}
are fixed and
f^{\text{att}}
is parameter-free, only
\mathbf{w}_{r}
gradients contribute. By the law of large numbers as
m\to\infty
, the finite-width NTK converges to the stated form. See Appendix
B
for details.

The following theorem establishes numerical stability of NTK-based influence functions
(
23
)
.

Theorem 4.3

(Influence Function Stability)
.

Let
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a positive semidefinite NTK matrix with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}\geq 0
, and let
\lambda>0
be a regularization parameter. Then the condition number satisfies
\kappa(\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I})=(\lambda_{1}+\lambda)/(\lambda_{n}+\lambda)\leq\lambda_{1}/\lambda+1
. Moreover, for any kernel perturbation
\|\Delta\mathbf{K}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon<\lambda
, the influence perturbation is bounded by

\|(\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}-(\mathbf{K}+\Delta\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda(\lambda-\epsilon)}\,.

Proof.
Regularization bounds the condition number as
(\lambda_{1}/\lambda)+1
. The resolvent identity gives the perturbation bound
\epsilon/[\lambda(\lambda-\epsilon)]
. See Appendix
B
.

The following assumption and proposition establish why
K_{\text{LinAttn}}
exhibits fundamentally different sensitivity to perturbations, providing the theoretical basis for attention’s resistance to the lazy training regime.

Assumption 4.4

(Incoherence Condition)
.

The data matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
satisfies the
\mu
-incoherence condition if:

1.

\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}=1
for all
i
(unit normalization), and

2.

\max_{i\neq j}|\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{j}|\leq\mu/\sqrt{d}
for some
\mu=O(1)
(bounded off-diagonal correlations).

Under this condition, the row energy is automatically bounded:
\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{k})^{2}\leq 1+(n-1)\mu^{2}/d=:\nu
. This condition holds with probability
\geq 1-n^{2}e^{-cd}
when
\mathbf{x}_{i}
are drawn independently from isotropic sub-Gaussian distributions on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
with
d=\Omega(\log n)

(
18
)
.

Proposition 4.5

(Data-Dependent Kernel Sensitivity)
.

The linearized attention kernel
K_{\text{LinAttn}}
exhibits
data-dependent
sensitivity to input perturbations that depends on the Gram matrix structure. For perturbation
\delta
with
\|\delta\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
:

\left|K_{\text{LinAttn}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}+\delta,\mathbf{x}_{j})-K_{\text{LinAttn}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})\right|\leq\|\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{1}\cdot\epsilon

(7)

where
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}
is the Gram matrix. The key distinction from polynomial kernels
K_{\text{poly}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=(\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{y})^{p}
(which have
O(\epsilon)
sensitivity independent of data) is that
K_{\text{LinAttn}}
sensitivity depends on the correlation structure of the entire dataset through
\mathbf{G}
.

Proof.
Writing
K_{\text{LinAttn}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})=[\mathbf{G}^{3}]_{ij}
with
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}
, the gradient with respect to
\mathbf{x}_{i}
involves the
i
-th row of
\mathbf{G}^{2}
and the vector
\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}_{j}
, both depending on all pairwise correlations. By submultiplicativity,
|\Delta K|\leq\|\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{1}\cdot\epsilon
, where
\|\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{1}
grows with dataset size and correlation density. In contrast,
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{y})^{p}=p(\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{y})^{p-1}\mathbf{y}
is
O(1)
for normalized inputs, independent of
n
. See Appendix
B
.

Remark 4.6

(Connection to Feature Learning)
.

Although
\mathbf{X}
is fixed during training,
f^{\text{att}}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}
encodes global pairwise relationships. When MLP weights are updated, gradients propagate through this globally-coupled representation, causing the empirical NTK to evolve in ways that depend on the full correlation structure, the hallmark of feature learning, in contrast to frozen-kernel lazy training.

The following theorem formalizes why linearized attention resists convergence to the kernel regime.

Theorem 4.7

(Spectral Amplification and NTK Non-Convergence)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
with
\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}=1
,
\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X})=n
(requiring
n\leq d
), and let
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}
have eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}>0
. Assume the
\mu
-incoherence condition (Assumption
4.4
). Then the attention-transformed Gram matrix satisfies
\tilde{\mathbf{G}}=\mathbf{G}^{3}
, amplifying the condition number:

\kappa(\tilde{\mathbf{G}})=\kappa(\mathbf{G})^{3}

(8)

Consequently, for the finite-width NTK of the MLP-Attn architecture to achieve approximation error
\|K_{m,\text{seq}}-K_{\infty,\text{seq}}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
, the width must satisfy
m=\Omega(\kappa(\mathbf{G})^{6}/\epsilon^{2})
. In contrast, for 2L-ReLU the requirement is
m=\Omega(1/\epsilon^{2})
without spectral amplification.

Proof.
Let
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{T}
be the SVD with
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
\mathbf{U}^{T}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}
. The attention output is
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{3}\mathbf{V}^{T}
. Hence
\tilde{\mathbf{G}}=\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{T}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{6}\mathbf{U}^{T}=\mathbf{G}^{3}
. The eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}^{3}
give
\kappa(\tilde{\mathbf{G}})=\kappa(\mathbf{G})^{3}
. The finite-width NTK is a sum of
m
i.i.d. rank-one terms; by the matrix Bernstein inequality
(
18
)
, the operator norm deviation scales as
O(\lambda_{1}^{3}\sqrt{n\log n/m})
. Convergence relative to the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}^{3}
of
K_{\infty,\text{seq}}
requires
\lambda_{1}^{3}\sqrt{n\log n/m}\ll\lambda_{n}^{3}
, i.e.,
m\gg\kappa(\mathbf{G})^{6}n\log n
. See Appendix
B
for the full derivation.

Remark 4.8

(Scope and Generalizations)
.

This result applies to linearized attention with identity QKV projections. Two generalizations follow from the spectral amplification mechanism: (1) stacking
k
linearized self-attention layers yields
\tilde{\mathbf{G}}=\mathbf{G}^{2k+1}
with
\kappa(\tilde{\mathbf{G}})=\kappa(\mathbf{G})^{2k+1}
, further widening the gap; (2) truncated attention (retaining only the top-
r
singular components of
\mathbf{G}
) caps the effective condition number at
\lambda_{1}/\lambda_{r}
, suggesting low-rank regularization as a mechanism for restoring convergence. Under incoherence (Assumption
4.4
), the result extends to row-normalized attention outputs, since the diagonal entries of
\mathbf{G}^{3}
are approximately uniform when eigenvector components are bounded. Note that Theorem
4.7
bounds the NTK deviation
at initialization
; it explains why the network cannot enter the lazy regime but does not directly predict the post-training trajectory. The empirically observed
increase
in
\|f_{m}-f_{\text{NTK}}\|
with width reflects a complementary effect: larger networks have greater capacity for feature learning, so the kernel evolves more during training. The differing trajectory shapes (non-monotonic on MNIST versus monotonically increasing on CIFAR-10) are consistent with dataset-dependent Gram structure: MNIST’s smaller
\kappa(\mathbf{G})
permits a transient near-lazy regime at small widths before feature learning dominates, while CIFAR-10’s larger
\kappa(\mathbf{G})
places the network in the feature learning regime from initialization.

6
Discussion

Dual Implications of Malleability.
The high influence malleability observed in attention mechanisms presents a nuanced picture with quantifiable trade-offs. On one hand, attention-based models exhibit greater susceptibility to adversarial manipulation: the 28.9% PGD flip rate on MNIST (vs. 3.3% for ReLU) indicates that small perturbations to influential examples substantially alter reliance patterns. On the other hand, Proposition
C.7
establishes that the data-dependent kernel
K_{\text{LinAttn}}
can achieve lower approximation error (bias) than fixed kernels when the target function aligns with the data distribution’s dominant modes. The practical implication is that high malleability is beneficial when data quality is high (bias reduction dominates) but harmful when training data contains adversarial examples (malleability cost dominates). To formalize this independently of the evaluation protocol, Definition
C.10
introduces an
intrinsic influence sensitivity

\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{K},\lambda,\mathbf{y})=L_{K}\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}/\lambda^{2}
that is parameter-free (independent of
\tau
,
\varepsilon
, or attack type); Proposition
C.11
shows the attention-vs-ReLU gap scales as
O(n\,\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{G}))
.

Interpreting the Adversarial Training Result.
Table
4
shows adversarial training increases 2L-ReLU malleability from 3.3% to 43.4%, nearly matching MLP-Attn. This demonstrates two distinct mechanisms for malleability: (1)
architectural
(attention’s Gram-induced kernel naturally creates sensitivity), or (2)
training-induced
(adversarial augmentation forces feature relearning). The critical distinction is that MLP-Attn achieves high malleability
without
adversarial augmentation, making the sensitivity
innate
rather than induced.

Feature Learning vs. Lazy Training.
The lazy/feature learning distinction
(
5
)
maps directly onto the observations: 2L-ReLU shows decreasing NTK distance (
45.1\to 39.2
on MNIST, approaching lazy training, 3.3% flip rate), while MLP-Attn shows increasing distance (
10.3\to 43.4
, feature learning regime, 28.9% flip rate). The NTK distance trajectory serves as a
diagnostic
for predicting influence malleability. The differing trajectories across datasets (non-monotonic on MNIST, monotonically increasing on CIFAR-10) likely reflect Gram matrix structure: MNIST’s lower-dimensional manifold may create an intermediate regime before feature learning dominates, while CIFAR-10’s higher complexity places MLP-Attn in the feature learning regime from initialization. Both share the key signature of non-convergence. Loss landscape analysis (Figure
5
, Appendix) confirms this geometric distinction: 2L-ReLU converges to a sharp, narrow minimum while MLP-Attn finds a broader, flatter basin.

Why Attention Resists the Kernel Regime.
The attention transformation couples the MLP’s input space to the
global
data geometry:
f^{\text{att}}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}
encodes pairwise relationships across the entire training set. Theorem
4.7
provides a precise mechanism: the transformation cubes the Gram matrix’s condition number, requiring width
m=\Omega(\kappa(\mathbf{G})^{6})
for convergence. With
\kappa(\mathbf{G})\approx 10^{3}
on MNIST, this yields
m\gg 10^{18}
, explaining the observed non-convergence at
m\leq 4096
.

Implications for Practice.
Infinite-width NTK theory should be applied with caution to attention architectures. The influence functions used here are computed from the
empirical finite-width
NTK, which remains well-defined regardless of convergence. However, theoretical tools relying on kernel regime assumptions may not transfer. Practitioners should be aware that attention’s flexibility comes with inherent sensitivity to training data quality.

7
Limitations

Linearization Scope.
The analysis uses linearized attention
f^{\text{att}}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}
, which isolates the quadratic interaction structure central to attention. This deliberate simplification enables exact kernel characterization (Theorem
4.1
) while preserving the data-dependent structure responsible for the observed phenomena. Extending the analysis to full softmax attention is a natural next step; the row-wise normalization of softmax may further amplify the non-convergence effect by introducing additional nonlinearity.

Dataset and Architecture Scale.
The controlled experimental setting (MNIST, CIFAR-10, two-layer networks with
m\leq 4096
) is chosen to enable exact NTK computation and rigorous comparison. The qualitative distinction between convergent (ReLU) and non-convergent (attention) behavior is robust across both datasets despite their different complexities, suggesting the phenomenon is fundamental rather than dataset-specific. Scaling to larger architectures via Nyström approximation or iterative solvers is a promising direction.

Parameter-Free Attention.
Identity QKV projections provide a canonical representative for theoretical analysis. Proposition
B.4
(Appendix) establishes that full-rank projections preserve all structural properties (data-dependence, transitive similarity, fourth-order interactions), suggesting the findings generalize beyond the parameter-free case.

8
Conclusion

This work has established, both empirically and theoretically, that linearized attention mechanisms do not converge to their infinite-width NTK limits. While standard ReLU networks approach the kernel regime as width increases, attention exhibits fundamentally different dynamics: non-monotonic or increasing NTK distance. This non-convergence places attention firmly in the feature learning regime, with measurable consequences captured by influence malleability (6–9
\times
higher than ReLU networks).

The high malleability of attention presents a fundamental tradeoff. The data-dependent kernel structure reduces approximation error when targets align with data geometry (Proposition
C.7
), but the same sensitivity creates vulnerability to adversarial manipulation. Understanding this tradeoff is essential for developing robust attention-based systems.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Machine Learning by providing theoretical understanding of attention mechanisms through the Neural Tangent Kernel framework. The finding that attention’s sensitivity to training data comes with adversarial vulnerability may inform the development of more robust attention-based systems. There are many potential societal consequences of this work, none of which must be specifically highlighted here.

A.4
Evaluation Metrics and Analysis

A.4.1
Influence Function Computation

Following the NTK-based approach of
23
and the foundational influence function methodology of
12
, influence is computed using the
empirical finite-width NTK

\mathbf{K}_{m}
at each width
m
, not the theoretical infinite-width limit. Specifically, for each trained model, the NTK is computed as
[\mathbf{K}_{m}]_{ij}=\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}f(\mathbf{x}_{i}),\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}f(\mathbf{x}_{j})\rangle
using the actual model gradients. This approach ensures that (1) influence comparisons between architectures are fair (both evaluated at the same width), and (2) the influence patterns reflect the actual model dynamics rather than a theoretical approximation. The key finding that MLP-Attn diverges from its infinite-width limit does not invalidate this methodology; rather, the empirical NTK captures how each architecture actually represents training data relationships at practical widths.

A.4.2
Malleability Metrics

The analysis employs several quantitative measures:

1.

Influence Flip Rate:

\text{Flip Rate}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}}\mathbf{1}[\text{sign}(I_{i}^{\text{orig}})\neq\text{sign}(I_{i}^{\text{adv}})]

where
\mathcal{H}
denotes the set of high-influence training examples (top 10% positive influence).

2.

Rank Correlation (Spearman’s
\rho
):

\rho=1-\frac{6\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{2}}{n(n^{2}-1)}

where
d_{i}
is the difference between ranks of influence scores before and after perturbation.

3.

Top-K Stability:

\text{Stability}_{K}=\frac{|\mathcal{T}_{K}^{\text{orig}}\cap\mathcal{T}_{K}^{\text{adv}}|}{K}

where
\mathcal{T}_{K}^{\text{orig}}
and
\mathcal{T}_{K}^{\text{adv}}
are the sets of top-K influential examples before and after perturbation.

A.5
Empirical Analysis of Influence Stability and Malleability

To empirically validate the theoretical framework, this section presents a comprehensive analysis comparing the baseline
2L-ReLU
network against the attention-enhanced
MLP-Attn
architecture. This investigation leverages the metrics defined above to explore two core phenomena: the stability of influence rankings under various data interventions and the malleability of influence signs under targeted adversarial attacks.

A.5.1
Influence Stability Across Data Interventions

The analysis first assesses influence stability using
Rank Correlation (Spearman’s
\rho
)
. A higher correlation indicates a more rigid influence structure, while a lower correlation suggests a more flexible, adaptive re-ranking of training point importance.

The stability analysis reveals two distinct patterns of behavior:

•

Constant Stability for Curated and Transformed Interventions:
Stability for both architectures is remarkably constant throughout training across all class settings. This indicates that the relative importance of training examples is a fixed structural property for these scenarios. In nearly all cases, the 2L-ReLU architecture exhibits a significantly higher Spearman’s
\rho
, signifying a more rigid influence structure compared to the MLP-Attn model.

•

Dynamic Stability for Adversarial Interventions:
The ‘Adv’ intervention induces dynamic stability profiles that evolve over training. For the more complex Fashion-MNIST and CIFAR-10 datasets, both architectures generally show a decline in stability. On the simpler MNIST dataset, the 2L-ReLU model shows a unique ability to recover and increase its influence stability over time, a phenomenon not observed in the attention-enhanced model.

A.5.2
Influence Malleability Under Adversarial Attacks

Influence malleability is evaluated using the
Influence Flip Rate
, which measures the percentage of high-influence examples that reverse their helpful/harmful status after being perturbed.

The malleability results provide several key insights:

•

PGD Reveals Clearer Patterns:
The stronger PGD attack generally reveals more consistent trends. On Fashion-MNIST, the MLP-Attn model is demonstrably more malleable than the 2L-ReLU baseline. On the more complex CIFAR-10 dataset, the results are mixed, with relative malleability depending on the number of classes.

•

FGSM Reveals Volatility and Inconsistency:
The weaker FGSM attack produces more erratic results. On Fashion-MNIST, the vulnerability of both models fluctuates wildly. On CIFAR-10, the results are clearer but contradictory: the 2L-ReLU model is far more malleable in the 5-class setting, while the MLP-Attn model is more malleable in the 1- and 10-class settings.

A.5.3
Synthesis and Insights

Taken together, these empirical results confirm the paper’s central thesis regarding NTK non-convergence and its dual implications. The
2L-ReLU
architecture, which converges toward its infinite-width NTK limit, is characterized by
high stability
but
low malleability
; its dependence on training data is strong and rigid, consistent with kernel regime behavior. In contrast, the
MLP-Attn
architecture, which remains in the feature learning regime, exhibits
lower stability
but
higher malleability
.

This trade-off carries dual implications. On one hand, the architecture continuously re-evaluates data dependencies, allowing flexible data relationships. On the other hand, this same flexibility makes it more susceptible to adversarial manipulation of training data, as quantified by the elevated Influence Flip Rate.

Appendix B
Theoretical Proofs and Derivations

B.1
Linearized Attention Approximation

Theorem B.1

(Taylor Approximation of Softmax Attention)
.

The linearized attention mechanism
f^{\text{att}}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}
provides a first-order Taylor approximation to standard softmax attention with bounded approximation error.

Proof.

Consider standard softmax attention
(
19
)
:

\text{Attention}(\mathbf{X})=\text{softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\mathbf{X}

For row
i
, the softmax output is
[\text{softmax}(\mathbf{A})]_{ij}=\exp(A_{ij})/\sum_{k}\exp(A_{ik})
where
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}/\sqrt{d}
. For small entries
|A_{ij}|\ll 1
, using
\exp(x)\approx 1+x+O(x^{2})
:

\displaystyle[\text{softmax}(\mathbf{A})]_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+A_{ij}+O(A_{ij}^{2})}{n+\sum_{k}A_{ik}+O(\|\mathbf{A}\|^{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(1+A_{ij}-\bar{A}_{i}+O(\|\mathbf{A}\|^{2})\right)

(9)

where
\bar{A}_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k}A_{ik}
is the row mean. The attention output for row
i
is:

[\text{Attention}(\mathbf{X})]_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j}(1+A_{ij}-\bar{A}_{i})\mathbf{x}_{j}+O(\|\mathbf{A}\|^{2})

(10)

The uniform term
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j}\mathbf{x}_{j}
contributes only the data mean. The data-dependent term is
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j}A_{ij}\mathbf{x}_{j}=\frac{1}{n\sqrt{d}}[\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}]_{i}
. Absorbing constants and dropping the mean-centering term (which does not affect relative similarities), the linearized attention
f^{\text{att}}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}
captures the essential data-dependent structure with approximation error
O(\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}\|^{2}/d)
.
∎

B.2
Exact Gram-Induced Kernel Characterization

Theorem B.2

(Parameter-Free Gram-Induced Structure)
.

The parameter-free linearized attention kernel admits the following expansion:

K_{\text{LinAttn}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})=\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{n}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{k})(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{\ell})(\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{j})

This establishes
K_{\text{LinAttn}}
as a parameter-free, data-dependent Gram-induced kernel with transitive interaction structure through training data.

Proof.

The parameter-free linearized attention transformation is:

[\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}]_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{k})\mathbf{x}_{k}

The kernel is the inner product of transformed features:

\displaystyle K_{\text{LinAttn}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})

\displaystyle=\langle[\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}]_{i},[\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}]_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{k})\mathbf{x}_{k},\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(\mathbf{x}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{\ell})\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{n}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{k})(\mathbf{x}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{\ell})(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{n}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{k})(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{\ell})(\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{j})

(11)

Each term
(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{k})(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{\ell})(\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{j})
represents a fourth-order interaction involving four vectors
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{\ell},\mathbf{x}_{j})
through three pairwise inner products. The summation over training data indices
(k,\ell)
creates a data-dependent fourth-order polynomial structure without trainable parameters.
∎

Corollary B.3

(Parameter-Free Fourth-Order Properties)
.

The kernel
K_{\text{LinAttn}}
defined by
\sum_{k,\ell}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{k})(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{\ell})(\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{j})
exhibits fourth-order polynomial interactions (each term involves four vectors
\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{\ell},\mathbf{x}_{j}
at degree 4), requires zero learnable parameters (since
f^{\text{att}}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}
contains no weight matrices), and depends on the full training set
\mathbf{X}
through the summation indices
k,\ell
. In particular, influence from
\mathbf{x}_{i}
to
\mathbf{x}_{j}
propagates transitively via intermediate points:
\mathbf{x}_{i}\to\mathbf{x}_{k}
(via
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{k}
),
\mathbf{x}_{k}\to\mathbf{x}_{\ell}
(via
\mathbf{x}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}
),
\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\to\mathbf{x}_{j}
(via
\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{j}
).

B.3
Proof of QKV Equivalence

Proposition B.4

(Kernel Equivalence Under Linear Projections)
.

For full-rank projection matrices
\mathbf{W}_{Q},\mathbf{W}_{K},\mathbf{W}_{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, the generalized linearized attention
f^{\text{att}}_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{X})=(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{Q})(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{K})^{T}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{V})
induces a kernel with the same structural properties as
K_{\text{LinAttn}}
: data-dependence, transitive similarity propagation, and fourth-order polynomial interactions.

Proof of Proposition
B.4
.

The generalized linearized attention with projection matrices
\mathbf{W}_{Q},\mathbf{W}_{K},\mathbf{W}_{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is:

f^{\text{att}}_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{X})=(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{Q})(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{K})^{T}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{V})

(12)

For row
i
, this computes:

\displaystyle[f^{\text{att}}_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{X})]_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{W}_{K}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{k})(\mathbf{W}_{V}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{k})

(13)

where the attention weight is
(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{W}_{Q})(\mathbf{W}_{K}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{k})^{T}=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{M}_{QK}\mathbf{x}_{k}
with
\mathbf{M}_{QK}=\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{W}_{K}^{T}
.

The induced kernel is the inner product of transformed features:

\displaystyle K_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})

\displaystyle=\langle[f^{\text{att}}_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{X})]_{i},[f^{\text{att}}_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbf{X})]_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{M}_{QK}\mathbf{x}_{k})(\mathbf{x}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{M}_{QK}\mathbf{x}_{\ell})(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{M}_{VV}\mathbf{x}_{\ell})

(14)

where
\mathbf{M}_{VV}=\mathbf{W}_{V}\mathbf{W}_{V}^{T}
.

Setting
\mathbf{W}_{Q}=\mathbf{W}_{K}=\mathbf{W}_{V}=\mathbf{I}
gives
\mathbf{M}_{QK}=\mathbf{M}_{VV}=\mathbf{I}
, recovering exactly:

K_{\text{LinAttn}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})=\sum_{k,\ell}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{k})(\mathbf{x}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{\ell})(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{\ell})

(15)

For general full-rank projections, the kernel retains the same structural properties: (i) data-dependence through the (transformed) Gram matrix, (ii) transitive similarity propagation via intermediate summation indices, and (iii) fourth-order polynomial interactions. The projections merely rotate the feature space without altering these structural characteristics.
∎

Theorem B.9

B.8
Proof of Spectral Amplification (Theorem
4.7
)

The proof relies on the following identity.

Lemma B.10

(Spectral Transfer)
.

For any
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
with
\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X})=r
and any
k\geq 0
:

\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X})^{k}\mathbf{X}^{T}=(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T})^{k+1}

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{T}
be the compact SVD with
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
,
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
,
\mathbf{U}^{T}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{V}^{T}\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{I}_{r}
. Then
\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}\mathbf{V}^{T}
and
(\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X})^{k}=\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2k}\mathbf{V}^{T}
, so:

\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X})^{k}\mathbf{X}^{T}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{T}\cdot\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2k}\mathbf{V}^{T}\cdot\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{U}^{T}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2k+2}\mathbf{U}^{T}=(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T})^{k+1}

using
\mathbf{V}^{T}\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{I}_{r}
at each step.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.7
.

The attention output admits the SVD factorization
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{3}\mathbf{V}^{T}
, where
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{T}
is the compact SVD with
\mathbf{U}^{T}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
. By Lemma
B.10
with
k=1
, the Gram matrix of the transformed features is

\tilde{\mathbf{G}}=\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{T}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{6}\mathbf{U}^{T}=(\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}\mathbf{U}^{T})^{3}=\mathbf{G}^{3}.

Since
\mathbf{G}
has eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}>0
, the transformed Gram matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{G}}
has eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}^{3}
with condition number
\kappa(\tilde{\mathbf{G}})=\lambda_{1}^{3}/\lambda_{n}^{3}=\kappa(\mathbf{G})^{3}
.

Consider now the finite-width NTK. The
(i,j)
-th entry is
[\mathbf{K}_{m}]_{ij}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=1}^{m}Z_{r}^{(ij)}
where
Z_{r}^{(ij)}=\mathbf{1}[\mathbf{w}_{r}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}>0]\,\mathbf{1}[\mathbf{w}_{r}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}>0]\,\langle\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\rangle
. Defining the centered matrix
\mathbf{S}_{r}
with entries
[\mathbf{S}_{r}]_{ij}=Z_{r}^{(ij)}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{r}^{(ij)}]
, the deviation from the infinite-width kernel is
\mathbf{K}_{m}-\mathbf{K}_{\infty}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\mathbf{S}_{r}
, a sum of
m
independent centered random matrices. Each satisfies
\|\mathbf{S}_{r}\|_{2}\leq 2\|\tilde{\mathbf{G}}\|_{2}=2\lambda_{1}^{3}
, since
|\langle\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\rangle|=|[\tilde{\mathbf{G}}]_{ij}|\leq\|\tilde{\mathbf{G}}\|_{2}
. The matrix variance statistic satisfies
\big\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{S}_{r}\mathbf{S}_{r}^{T}]\big\|_{2}\leq n\lambda_{1}^{6}/m
. Applying the matrix Bernstein inequality
(
18
)
yields

\|\mathbf{K}_{m}-\mathbf{K}_{\infty}\|_{2}\leq C_{0}\left(\sqrt{\frac{n\lambda_{1}^{6}\log n}{m}}+\frac{\lambda_{1}^{3}\log n}{m}\right).

(17)

For
m\gg\log n
, the first term dominates:
\|\mathbf{K}_{m}-\mathbf{K}_{\infty}\|_{2}=O(\lambda_{1}^{3}\sqrt{n\log n/m})
.

For the NTK approximation to be meaningful, the error must be small relative to
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{K}_{\infty})
. The infinite-width NTK inherits the spectral structure of the input Gram matrix, so
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{K}_{\infty})=\Theta(\lambda_{n}^{3})
. The relative convergence condition
\|\mathbf{K}_{m}-\mathbf{K}_{\infty}\|_{2}/\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{K}_{\infty})\leq\epsilon
therefore requires

\frac{\lambda_{1}^{3}\sqrt{n\log n/m}}{\lambda_{n}^{3}}\leq\epsilon\quad\Longrightarrow\quad m\geq\frac{\kappa(\mathbf{G})^{6}\cdot n\log n}{\epsilon^{2}}.

For 2L-ReLU on raw unit-norm inputs, an analogous argument gives
\|\mathbf{K}_{m}-\mathbf{K}_{\infty}\|_{2}=O(\sqrt{n\log n/m})
with
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{K}_{\infty})=\Theta(1)
, so
m=O(n\log n/\epsilon^{2})
suffices without spectral amplification.

Finally, when row-normalization is applied, the effective Gram matrix becomes
\hat{\mathbf{G}}=\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{G}^{3}\mathbf{D}^{-1}
where
\mathbf{D}=\operatorname{diag}(\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\|,\ldots,\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{n}\|)
. Under the
\mu
-incoherence condition (Assumption
4.4
), the diagonal entries
[\mathbf{G}^{3}]_{ii}=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}^{3}[\mathbf{U}]_{ik}^{2}
concentrate around
\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{G}^{3})
with deviation
O(\mu^{2})
, so
\mathbf{D}
is approximately a scalar multiple of
\mathbf{I}
and
\kappa(\hat{\mathbf{G}})=\kappa(\mathbf{G})^{3}(1+O(\mu^{2}))
.
∎

Remark B.11

When
n>d

Definition C.1

(Formal Influence Malleability Measure)
.

For a model
f
with influence function
I_{f}
, the influence malleability measure is:

\mu_{M}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D},\delta\sim\Delta}\left[|I_{f}(x,y)-I_{f}(x+\delta,y)|\right]

where
\Delta
is the perturbation distribution with
\|\delta\|_{p}\leq\epsilon
.

Theorem C.2

(Malleability-Kernel Eigenspectrum Connection)
.

For the NTK regime with kernel
K
, the malleability measure satisfies:

\mu_{M}(f)\geq C\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}(K)}{(\lambda_{i}(K)+\lambda)^{2}}

where
C>0
is an architecture-dependent constant and
\lambda_{i}(K)
are eigenvalues of the kernel matrix.

Corollary C.3

(Regularization Controls Malleability)
.

The malleability measure satisfies
\mu_{M}(f)\to\infty
as
\lambda\to 0
(high malleability) and
\mu_{M}(f)\to 0
as
\lambda\to\infty
(low malleability), with optimal malleability attained at
\lambda^{*}=\mathrm{median}(\{\lambda_{i}(K)\})
.

Theorem C.4

(Gram-Induced vs Standard Polynomial Kernel Malleability)
.

Let
K_{\text{poly}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=(\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{x}^{\prime})^{p}
be a standard polynomial kernel and
K_{\text{LinAttn}}
be the Gram-induced linearized attention kernel (Theorem
4.1
). Then:

\mu_{M}(f_{K_{\text{LinAttn}}})\geq\alpha\cdot\mu_{M}(f_{K_{\text{poly}}})

for some
\alpha>1
depending on the data manifold structure.

Proof.

The Gram-induced kernel has higher Lipschitz constant due to transitive amplification. For the linearized attention kernel:

\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}K_{\text{LinAttn}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\sum_{k,\ell}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}[(\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{k})(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{x}_{\ell})(\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{T}\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]

This triple sum creates higher sensitivity compared to standard polynomial kernels. Additionally, the attention kernel enables transitive influence: a perturbation to
\mathbf{x}_{i}
affects the influence of
\mathbf{x}_{j}
through intermediate points
\mathbf{x}_{k}
. This transitive effect is absent in standard ReLU kernels, establishing
\alpha>1
.
∎

Theorem C.5

(Bias-Variance-Malleability Decomposition)
.

The generalization error admits the decomposition:

\mathbb{E}[\text{Test Error}]\leq\text{Bias}^{2}+\text{Variance}+\gamma\cdot\mu_{M}(f)+o(\mu_{M}(f))

where
\gamma>0
is a data-dependent constant.

Corollary C.6

(Optimal Malleability)
.

There exists an optimal malleability level
\mu_{M}^{*}
that minimizes generalization error:

\mu_{M}^{*}=\arg\min_{\mu}\left[\text{Bias}^{2}(\mu)+\gamma\cdot\mu\right]

Proposition C.7

(Bias Reduction via Data-Dependent Kernel)
.

Let
f^{*}
be the target function and
\hat{f}_{K}
the kernel ridge regression estimator with kernel
K
and regularization
\lambda
. The approximation error (bias) is
\text{Bias}^{2}(K)=\|f^{*}-\Pi_{K}f^{*}\|^{2}
, where
\Pi_{K}
is the projection onto the RKHS of
K
. Let
\{\sigma_{i}\}
be the singular values of
\mathbf{X}
with corresponding right singular vectors
\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\}
. If the target function
f^{*}
has spectral energy concentrated along the top eigenvectors of the Gram matrix
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}
, i.e.,
\sum_{i=1}^{r}|\langle f^{*},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle|^{2}\geq(1-\delta)\|f^{*}\|^{2}
for some
r\ll d
and small

, then:

\text{Bias}^{2}(K_{\text{LinAttn}})\leq\text{Bias}^{2}(K_{\text{poly}})

where
K_{\text{poly}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=(\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{x}^{\prime})^{3}
is the degree-3 polynomial kernel.

Proof.

The kernel matrix of
K_{\text{LinAttn}}
on training data is
\mathbf{G}^{3}
(matrix cube of the Gram matrix
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}
), since
[K_{\text{LinAttn}}]_{ij}=\sum_{k,\ell}G_{ik}G_{k\ell}G_{\ell j}=[\mathbf{G}^{3}]_{ij}
. The degree-3 polynomial kernel matrix is
\mathbf{G}^{\circ 3}
(Hadamard/element-wise cube), since
[K_{\text{poly}}]_{ij}=G_{ij}^{3}
.

Let
\mathbf{G}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}
be the eigendecomposition. Then
\mathbf{G}^{3}
has eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}^{3}
with
the same eigenvectors

\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\}
as
\mathbf{G}
. In contrast,
\mathbf{G}^{\circ 3}
generically does
not
share eigenvectors with
\mathbf{G}
(the Hadamard power mixes the eigenbasis).

The bias of kernel ridge regression with kernel
K=\sum_{k}\mu_{k}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}^{T}
and regularization
\lambda
is
\text{Bias}^{2}=\sum_{k}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(\mu_{k}+\lambda)^{2}}w_{k}^{2}
, where
w_{k}=\langle f^{*},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}\rangle

Under the spectral alignment condition,
f^{*}
has most energy in the top eigenvectors of
\mathbf{G}
:
w_{k}=\langle f^{*},\mathbf{v}_{k}\rangle
is large for small
k
. For
K_{\text{LinAttn}}=\mathbf{G}^{3}

\lambda_{k}^{3}

\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(\lambda_{k}^{3}+\lambda)^{2}}w_{k}^{2}
. For
K_{\text{poly}}=\mathbf{G}^{\circ 3}
, the eigenbasis differs from
\{\mathbf{v}_{k}\}
, so the target’s energy generically spreads across the polynomial kernel’s eigenmodes, including modes with smaller eigenvalues. This eigenbasis mismatch increases the total bias.

The spectral alignment condition holds for standard classification tasks where class boundaries are determined by the dominant data variation modes
(
3
;
4
)
.
∎

Remark C.8

(Completing the Dual Implications)
.

Proposition
C.7

The net effect on generalization depends on data quality: when training data is clean, the bias reduction dominates, favoring attention; when training data contains adversarial examples, the malleability cost dominates, favoring rigid architectures. Theorem C.9

(Unified Sensitivity Bound for Predictions and Influence)
.

Suppose
f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})^{T}(\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y}
is a kernel ridge regression predictor with Gram matrix
\mathbf{K}\succeq 0
, regularization
\lambda>0
, and kernel Lipschitz constant
L_{K}=\sup_{\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{2}\leq 1}\|\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\delta})-\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}
. Let
\epsilon>0
be a perturbation budget. Then the prediction sensitivity satisfies

\sup_{\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon}|f(\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\delta})-f(\mathbf{x})|\leq\frac{\epsilon L_{K}\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}}{\lambda}\,,

and perturbing any training point
\mathbf{x}_{i}
by
\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
changes the influence vector
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}=(\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{y}
by at most

\|\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{2\epsilon L_{K}\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\,.

Both bounds scale as
L_{K}/\lambda
. For linearized attention,
L_{K}=O(n\|\mathbf{G}\|_{2})
(Proposition
4.5
), while for standard ReLU,
L_{K}=O(1)
, so the architecture-dependent gap in
L_{K}
simultaneously drives higher prediction sensitivity and higher influence instability for attention.

Proof.

Write
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{y}
. Since
\mathbf{K}\succeq 0
, the operator norm satisfies
\|(\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\|_{2}\leq 1/\lambda
, so
\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}/\lambda
. For the prediction bound,
f(\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\delta})-f(\mathbf{x})=[\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\delta})-\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})]^{T}\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, whence Cauchy–Schwarz gives
|f(\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\delta})-f(\mathbf{x})|\leq\epsilon L_{K}\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon L_{K}\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}/\lambda
.

For the influence bound, perturbing
\mathbf{x}_{i}
to
\mathbf{x}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\delta}
yields a perturbed Gram matrix
\mathbf{K}^{\prime}\succeq 0
(as
\mathbf{K}^{\prime}
is itself a valid kernel matrix), with
\|\Delta\mathbf{K}\|_{2}\leq 2\epsilon L_{K}
. By the resolvent identity,
\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}=-(\mathbf{K}^{\prime}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\Delta\mathbf{K}\,\boldsymbol{\alpha}
. Submultiplicativity gives
\|\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}\|_{2}\leq(1/\lambda)(2\epsilon L_{K})(\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}/\lambda)
, where
\|(\mathbf{K}^{\prime}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\|_{2}\leq 1/\lambda
since
\mathbf{K}^{\prime}\succeq 0
. The entry-wise bound follows from
\|\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}\|_{2}
.
∎

Definition C.10

(Intrinsic Influence Sensitivity)
.

For a kernel ridge regression predictor with Gram matrix
\mathbf{K}\succeq 0
, regularization
\lambda>0
, labels
\mathbf{y}
, and kernel Lipschitz constant
L_{K}
, the
intrinsic influence sensitivity
is

\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{K},\lambda,\mathbf{y})\;=\;\frac{L_{K}\,\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\,.

This quantity upper-bounds the per-unit-perturbation change in the influence vector
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}=(\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{y}
, independent of any evaluation protocol (threshold
\tau
, perturbation budget
\varepsilon
, or attack type).

Proposition C.11

(Architecture-Dependent Sensitivity Gap)
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
C.9
and Proposition
4.5
, the intrinsic influence sensitivity satisfies:

1.

Linearized attention:

\mathcal{S}_{\textup{att}}=O\!\left(n\,\|\mathbf{G}\|_{2}\,\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}\,/\,\lambda^{2}\right)
, where
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}
.

2.

Two-layer ReLU:

\mathcal{S}_{\textup{relu}}=O\!\left(\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}\,/\,\lambda^{2}\right)
.

Consequently, the sensitivity gap scales as

\frac{\mathcal{S}_{\textup{att}}}{\mathcal{S}_{\textup{relu}}}\;=\;O\!\left(n\,\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{G})\right),

where
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{G})
is the leading eigenvalue of the Gram matrix. For natural image datasets with
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{G})\gg 1
, this gap grows with both dataset size and spectral concentration, consistent with the empirically observed 6–9
\times
higher malleability for attention.

Proof.

Substitute the kernel Lipschitz constants from Proposition
4.5
into Definition
C.10
. For linearized attention, Proposition
4.5
bounds the
j
-th entry of the kernel vector perturbation by
\|\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{1}\cdot\epsilon
. Since
\|\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{n}\,\|\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{n}\,\|\mathbf{G}\|_{2}
for unit-norm
\mathbf{x}_{j}
, summing over all
n
entries in quadrature gives
L_{K}=\|\Delta\mathbf{k}\|_{2}/\epsilon\leq n\,\|\mathbf{G}\|_{2}
. For the standard ReLU NTK,
L_{K}=O(1)
as the kernel gradient depends only on the local input pair.
∎

Remark C.12

(Intrinsic Sensitivity as a Diagnostic)
.

The quantity
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{K},\lambda,\mathbf{y})
provides a parameter-free characterization of influence malleability that does not depend on the evaluation protocol (choice of
\tau
,
\varepsilon
, or attack). Its dependence on
\lambda
is analogous to robustness margins in adversarial learning: the regularization strength controls the tradeoff between expressiveness and stability. Proposition
C.11
shows that the architecture-dependent factor
n\,\|\mathbf{G}\|_{2}
is the intrinsic source of the malleability gap, formalizing the empirical observation that attention’s data-dependent kernel couples all training points and amplifies perturbation effects.

Proposition C.13

(Finite-Width Malleability Convergence)
.

Let
\mu_{M}^{(m)}(f)
denote the malleability of a width-
m
network. Then:

\left|\mu_{M}^{(m)}(f)-\mu_{M}^{(\infty)}(f)\right|\leq\frac{C_{\text{mall}}\sqrt{n\log n}}{\sqrt{m}}

where
C_{\text{mall}}
depends on the data distribution and attention transformation.

Proof.

Malleability is a function of influence scores, which depend on the kernel matrix via
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}=(\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{y}
. By Theorem
4.3
, the influence perturbation under kernel change
\|\Delta\mathbf{K}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon_{K}
satisfies
\|\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}\|\leq\epsilon_{K}/[\lambda(\lambda-\epsilon_{K})]
. From the matrix Bernstein inequality (as in the proof of Theorem
4.7
),
\|\mathbf{K}_{m}-\mathbf{K}_{\infty}\|_{2}=O(\lambda_{1}^{3}\sqrt{n\log n/m})
for MLP-Attn and
O(\sqrt{n\log n/m})
for 2L-ReLU. Combining, the malleability difference inherits the same convergence rate, with architecture-dependent constant
C_{\text{mall}}
absorbing the spectral amplification factor
\lambda_{1}^{3}
for attention.
∎

Remark C.14

(Practical Width Requirements)
.

For malleability estimation error below
5\%
, the bound suggests that 2L-ReLU requires
m\gtrsim 400
while linearized attention requires
m\gtrsim 1600
(due to the larger constant
C_{\text{mall}}
from spectral amplification). The experimental width
m=1024
thus provides reliable malleability estimates for 2L-ReLU but may underestimate the infinite-width malleability for MLP-Attn, making the reported 6–9
\times
gap a conservative estimate.

Appendix D
Limitations and Future Directions

D.1
Computational Scalability

The
O(n^{3})
complexity of kernel matrix inversion creates scalability bottlenecks:

Proposition D.1

(Computational Scalability)
.

For dataset size
n>10^{4}
, exact influence computation becomes computationally prohibitive without approximation strategies.

Proposed Solutions:

1.

Nyström Approximation:
Low-rank kernel approximation reducing complexity to
O(r^{3}+nr^{2})
where
r\ll n

2.

Iterative Solvers:
Conjugate gradient methods achieving
O(n^{2}\log(1/\epsilon))
complexity

3.

Stochastic Estimation:
Random sampling approaches for large-scale approximate influence computation

D.2
Scope of Linearization

The analysis relies on linearized attention
f^{\text{att}}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}
, which approximates softmax attention via first-order Taylor expansion. This removes the competitive normalization dynamics of softmax. Whether the observed NTK non-convergence extends to full softmax attention remains an open question. The linearization captures the essential quadratic interaction structure but may not reflect all softmax dynamics. Future work should investigate whether softmax’s row-wise normalization strengthens or weakens the non-convergence effect.

D.3
Extension to Modern Architectures

The current analysis focuses on parameter-free attention mechanisms. Extension to full Transformers
(
19
;
7
)
requires:

1.

Parameterized Attention Analysis:
Joint training dynamics of query, key, value matrices

2.

Multi-Head Attention:
Compositional kernel analysis for multiple attention heads

3.

Layer Normalization:
Impact of normalization on kernel structure and influence patterns

D.4
Theoretical Gaps

Several theoretical questions remain open:

1.

Finite-Width Bounds:
Precise characterization of approximation quality for practical network sizes

2.

Training Dynamics:
Evolution of influence patterns throughout the training process

3.

Generalization Bounds:
Connection between influence malleability and generalization performance

Key Observations:

•

Malleability Gap:
Under standard training, MLP-Attn shows 6–9
\times
higher flip rates than 2L-ReLU, confirming that attention naturally develops malleable influence patterns.

•

Adversarial Training Effect:
Adversarial training dramatically increases malleability for 2L-ReLU (3.3%
\to
43.4% on MNIST), narrowing the gap with MLP-Attn. This suggests aggressive optimization can induce malleability in conventionally rigid architectures.

•

Accuracy Under Intervention:
MLP-Attn maintains higher accuracy under both Curated and Transformed interventions, demonstrating that its malleable influence patterns enable more robust adaptation to data quality changes.

•

Dataset Complexity:
The malleability gap is largest on simpler datasets (MNIST) and smaller on complex datasets (CIFAR-10), suggesting that dataset structure modulates the manifestation of architectural differences.
