Case report: Schistosoma mansoni and Fasciola spp. co-infection
Authors: Souleymane Ouedraogo, Nicolas Vignier, Anthony Marteau, Aloïs Helary, Emma Oliosi, Clelia Previtali, Ava Diarra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report an imported case of co-infection with fascioliasis and schistosomiasis in a 37-year-old patient with no known medical history, originally from Djibouti, a country in sub-Saharan East Africa. The diagnosis was confirmed by the detection of Schistosoma ova, suggestive clinical symptoms, positive serologies to both fascioliasis and schistosomiasis, and the atypical appearance of hepatic lesions on MRI. The patient was in France as a tourist. He was treated with a dual therapy of triclabendazole and praziquantel. His clinical condition improved without treatment-related side effects. This case highlights the necessity to search for these two parasitic infections in the presence of suggestive liver involvement, particularly in case of epidemiological risk.

Introduction

Fascioliasis and schistosomiasis infect more than 2,4 million and 200 million people worldwide, respectively, and 850 million people are at risk of these infections [1, 2]. These infections usually occur in regions of low- and middle-income countries where water supplies are inadequate and adequate sanitation is lacking [3]. Here is presented an imported case of a tourist from Djibouti who lives between Djibouti and Somaliland, traveling to France.

Case presentation

A 37-year-old male, with no prior medical history, presented to the emergency department in May 2025 for epigastric pain starting 2 days earlier and chronic cough starting 3 months prior consultation. The patient travelled in France in April 2025 for tourism. He originated from and lived in Djibouti and travelled between Djibouti and Somaliland for work-related purpose. He declared occasionally eating local vegetable crops from Somaliland and bathing in local fresh waters 20 years before the present episode. Clinical examination revealed generalized pruritus and upper quadrant pain. The blood test revealed hyper-eosinophilia with 3570 eosinophilic polynuclear cells/mm3, hepatic cytolysis with isolated elevated ALT 65 UI/L, elevated GGT 148 UI/L without hyperbilirubinemia and inflammatory syndrome with increased C-Reactive-Protein of 24 mg/L. HIV, Hepatitis B Virus and Hepatitis C Virus serological testing were negative. Abdominal and pelvic computed tomography (CT) showed pre-suppurative hypodense lesions on hepatic segments IV, VI, VIII. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) revealed tubular oval lesion of hepatic segment II, III and VI. The parasitological assessment was as follows: Toxocariasis serology negative on ELISA and Western Blot, Echinococcosis serology slightly positive on ELISA however negative on Western Blot consistent with cross-positive reaction, Fascioliasis serology equivocal on haemagglutination, positive (Kit ELI.H.A Distoma de ELITech MICROBIO) on ELISA (Kit Fasciola hepatica IgG ELISA de Bordier) confirmed on Western Blot (Presence of three bands P8-9, P27-28 and P42, Kit Fasciola ES Western Blot IgG de LDBio), Schistosomiasis serology positive on ELISA (Kit Schistosoma mansoni IgG ELISA de Bordier) confirmed on Western Blot (Kit Schisto II Western Blot IgG de LDBio). Microscopic parasitic stool examinationdetected Schistosoma mansoni ova on one sample out of three. Liver biopsy showed signs of inflammation and necrosis with eosinophils contingent consistent with parasitic infection. Nonetheless, the hepatic aspiration and ITS1, ITS2 and 5.8 S PCR (targeting nematode, cestode and trematode) performed directly on the hepatic sample resulted negative. The final diagnosis was intestinal schistosomiasis and hepatic co-infection fascioliasis. The patient received triclabendazole 1000 mg (12 mg/kg) for 2 days and praziquantel 3000 mg (40 mg/kg) once. The CT scan performed 2 months post treatment showed the regression of previously described hepatic lesion. At the last follow-up, 3 months post treatment the patient showed no evidence of relapse and eosinophilia had resolved (Figs. 1 and 2).

Discussion

Co-infection with Schistosoma mansoni and Fasciola spp. has rarely been reported. If clinical manifestations and CT or MRI scans of the liver strongly suggest liver parasitosis, the diagnosis is confirmed by immunodiagnostics and testing for Fasciola spp. eggs in the stool. The MRI images of the patient showed areas of hypointense necrosis surrounded by hyperintense areas suggestive of parasite migration tracts and associated inflammatory changes. Microscopy examination for ova and parasites of Fasciola was negative. Although stool examination for Fasciola spp. was negative, serological testing was strongly supportive of the diagnosis. This is consistent with the known diagnostic value of antibody detection, which may become positive several weeks before eggs are detectable in stool, thereby allowing earlier diagnosis and treatment and potentially reducing the risk of complications [4].

The diagnosis of schistosomiasis was made incidentally in our patient, who had no functional digestive and was therefore asymptomatic. Moreover, the supposed duration between exposure to Schistosoma and infection onset seems relatively long which does not match the usual depiction of the infection. The MRI did not show images typical of Symmers’ fibrosis characteristic of schistosomiasis. According to international guidelines, screening for schistosomiasis should be systematic in adult migrants, whether symptomatic or not, from endemic areas [4]. The liver biopsy is not recommended in cases of suspected fascioliasis or schistosomiasis. In this case, the combination of compatible imaging findings with eosinophilia and a positive serology was sufficient to indicate treatment. Therefore invasive procedures should be avoided.

Conclusion

Fascioliasis and schistosomiasis are common parasitic diseases in tropical and subtropical regions, areas with high endemic density. Co-infection with these diseases is less common, ranging from 0.7% to 1.4% of total occurrences, and may be underestimated [5]. Both infections were confirmed by Western Blot serologies after ELISA screening, eluding cross-reaction between these two pathologies. Although therapeutic options are limited, available treatments are effective, with a high success rate and few side effects.
