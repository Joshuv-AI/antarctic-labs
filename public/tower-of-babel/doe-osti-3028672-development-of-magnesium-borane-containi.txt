I. PROJECT OBJECTIVE-
 The objective of this project was the development of a two-way liquid hydrogen carrier having
a greater energy efficiency and lower delivery costs than either existing hydrogen carriers or
conventional compressed H2 gas transport technology. It was hypothesized that a hydrogen carrier
consisting of solutions of furans and pyrroles containing Mg(BH4)2 and pincer complex catalyst,
would have more than twice the volumetric available hydrogen density of the conventional two-
way liquid hydrogen carrier, methylcyclohexane. Most notably the envisioned liquid hydrogen
carrier would: 1) maintain high product selectivity through multiple cycles of dehydrogenation
and hydrogenation; and 2) undergo rapid dehydrogenation and re-hydrogenation. The project was
specifically aimed at the demonstration of at least one LOHC solution of Mg(BH4)2 and pincer
catalyst that releases ≥ 50 g H2/L at ≤ 200 oC upon dehydrogenation and undergoes ≥ 25% re-
hydrogenation under 100 atm of H2 at ≤ 200 °C.

II BACKGROUND
 The Matsunaga Hydrogen Research, Development and Demonstration Act of 1990 enabled the
establishment of the research effort to develop hydrogen as an energy carrier at the Hawaii Natural
Energy Institute (HNEI) at the University of Hawaii at Manoa (UH). As a member of the inaugural
hydrogen research team at UH, Prof. Jensen pioneered the first U.S. DOE studies of complex
hydrides for reversible hydrogen storage. Since that time UH has been continuously involved in
U.S. DOE efforts for the development of complex and chemical hydrides as hydrogen storage
materials. Our group’s studies have resulted in over 150 peer reviewed publications including
several widely cited papers and 10 U.S. patents.

A. Dehydrogenation of LOHCs Catalyzed by Homogeneous “Pincer” Catalysts In 1997, we
discovered the first reported homogeneous catalyst for the dehydrogenation cycloalkanes to
arenes, the “pincer” complex IrH2{2,6-C6H3-(CH2PtBu2)2}, (1).[1,2] The complex was
subsequently found to catalyze the reverse reaction, the hydrogenation of arenes.[3] We
subsequently found 1 and the related pincer complexes, IrH2{2,6-C6H3-(CH2PiPr2)2} (2) and
IrH2{2,6-C6H3-(OPtBu2)2} (3) are highly active catalysts for the dehydrogenation of aliphatic
groups of a wide variety of organic compounds as well as the elimination of hydrogen from
ammonia borane.[2-6] Notably the pincer complexes have been found to be highly active catalysts

 Page 2 of 39

for the dehydrogenation of tetrahydrofuran (THF) [4] and the aliphatic ring of saturated, 5-
membered N-heterocycles.[5-6] The use of a homogeneous catalyst eliminates intrinsic
complications associated with gas evolution during the dehydrogenation of N-heterocycles.[7,8]
Additionally, the inherently high product selectively of the homogeneous catalyst eliminates side
reactions that precluded practical applications of systems based on heterogeneous catalysts.[9,10]
A spinoff, US DOE funded project involving Hawaii Hydrogen Carriers (HHC) and General
Motors (GM) probed the possibility of producing onboard storage system based on heterocyclic
LOHCs containing pincer catalysts for vehicular applications.

UH became a member of the US DOE’s Metal Hydride Center of Excellence (MHCoE) that was
led by SNL. The UH effort within the MHCoE focused on alane and lithium aluminum hydride.
Just prior to the termination of the MHCoE, a research collaboration between UH and SNL resulted

  Prior experimental studies mechanistic studies for the formation of [B3H8]− from [BH4]− have
been carried out in solution using either diborane or a BH3 adduct as a reactant [21-24]. Chen et
al. showed that [B3H8]− can be synthesized through the reaction of [BH4]− with 2 equivalents of
BH3-THF [23]. They proposed that the reaction proceeds by a mechanism involving the initial
coupling of [BH4]− and BH3 to give [B2H7]−. The B2 intermediate then reacts with a second
equivalent of BH3 to form the [B3H10]− intermediate before undergoing dehydrogenation to
[B3H8]− (Scheme 1) [23]. Gaines et al. prepared [B3H8]− by the reaction of [BH4]− and diborane,
B2H6 [25]. They propose a similar mechanism in which [B3H8]− is formed directly from [B3H10]−
via a H2 elimination [25]. Beall et al. has suggested that the hydrogen elimination is most likely
the endothermic, rate-limiting step [22].
  A recent computational study by Johnson et al. that investigated ground-state stabilities of
different borane species calculated [B3H10]− as a lower energy intermediate than [B2H5]−, thus
indicating that BH3 addition occurs first [20]. An alternative mechanism has been proposed by
Wan et al. [26] However, they propose that the preferred reaction pathway involves the [B3H9]2−
dianion, which is not commonly considered as a component in the chemistry of lower anionic
boranes [27]. While the previous experimental and computational studies have revealed important

insights into the reaction pathway between Mg(BH4)2 and Mg(B3H8)2, a full understanding of the
mechanistic details of the process in the solid state requires information about the kinetic
parameters governing this process. In order to obtain this information, we have carried out kinetic
studies and modeled our findings using line-fitting analysis in order to elucidate a valid mechanism
for the reversible dehydrogenation of Mg(BH4)2 to Mg(B3H8)2. Such analysis has allowed for the
determination of the rate constant and activation energy (Ea) associated with the elementary step(s)
for the forward and reverse reactions, thus revealing which step is rate-limiting in the case of a
multi-step reaction.

3. Mechanism of the Solid State Conversion of [BH4]− to [B3H8]−. As cautioned by Johnson et
al. gas phase energetics are not necessarily directly comparable to those of processes occurring in
the solid state [20]. Solid state reactions, unlike those occurring in solution, are often rate limited
by processes involving long-range transport of heavy atoms that require large amounts of energy
to produce new crystalline phases. These processes generally occur only at temperatures much
higher than the 200-250 °C temperature range required for the observed dehydrogenation reaction.
It is therefore important to note that in these studies, powder XRD analysis (Figure 8) of the
product mixture resulting from the heating of the as synthesized Mg(BH4)2 (primarily alpha phase)
for 12 hours at 240 °C showed the product mixture to be totally amorphous. This observation is
consistent with computational studies by Kang et al. indicated that [B3H8]− formation likely
favored growth in lower dimensional morphologies such as in chains or clusters, rather than
segregated crystalline phases of MgB2 and Mg(BH4)2 [28]. Apparently, the [B3H8]− and [H]−
anions formed during the course of this reaction are stabilized through complicated bonding
interactions with Mg2+, unreacted [BH4]−, and perhaps other species.

B. Screening of Hydrogen Release from Tetrahydrofuran Mixtures of Magnesium Borohydride
with and without Pincer Catalyst.

1. Mg(BH4)2 Containing Solutions of THF and Glymes The project was immediately
confronted with the finding that Mg(BH4)2 shows no activity when fully dissolved in THF (32
equivalents) even when heated to 200 °C in the presence of the pincer catalyst.[29] The 11B NMR
spectra of these reactions after heating only show the presence of [BH4]– at -41 ppm. Similar
collaborative studies at Pacific Northwest National Lab (PNNL) have shown complete inhibition
or reduced rates of dehydrogenation of Mg(BH4)2 when fully dissolved in glyme, diglyme and
tetraglyme.[29] It should be noted that the addition of THF leads to melts below 100 ˚C, thus the
THF slurries of Mg(BH4)2 (which do undergo dehydrogenation) were considered to be a potential
liquid hydrogen. Therefore slurries of Mg(BH4)2 with sub-stoichiometric amounts of THF became
the focus of study.

D. Irreversibility of the Dehydrogenation Mg(BH4)2•(THF)0.25 to Mg(B10H10) at Pressures Less
than 500 Bar. As discussed above, the addition of THF to Mg(BH4)2 opens a second reaction
pathway which leads to the formation of [B10H10]2−. It had been previously reported that this
reaction pathway is reversible under moderate conditions, with successful cycling of the
Mg(BH4)2(THF)x – MgB10H10(THF)x system taking place at 200°C and 90 bar H2 pressure.[15]
This system thus became a focal point of our studies due to its high gravimetric hydrogen cycling
capacity of 8.1wt% and its potential ability to hydrogenate back to borohydride under such mild
conditions. The formation of MgB12H12 was shown using solid-state 11B NMR spectroscopy
following dehydrogenation studies of Mg(BH4)2 at 450 °C by Hwang et al.[30] Studies by
Soloveichik et al. later found that the formation of a product obtained after heating Mg(BH4)2 to
temperatures >350°C was unable to be successfully hydrogenated back, thus inferring the
formation of MgB12H12.[31] These findings provide additional confirmation of the high
thermodynamic stability of the [B12H12]2− species which can be considered as a “thermodynamic
sink”, whose high stability precludes its hydrogenation back to Mg(BH4)2. However, the
observation of MgB12H12 following the direct hydrogenation of MgB2 to Mg(BH4)2 (400°C, 950
bar H2) by Severa et al. challenged this idea by raising the possibility that this anionic closo-borane
was an intermediate in this reaction with breakdown of the closo-structure leading to the formation
of Mg(BH4)2.[12] This finding prompted studies for the hydrogenation of other closo- compounds,
such as M2B10H10 (M = Li, Na), back to their respective borohydrides.[32] The accomplishment
of this feat, however, required extreme conditions of ~300 ˚C and pressures >500 bar H2, so long
as the corresponding metal hydride was present.[32] Although attempts to hydrogenate the Mg
analog were unsuccessful, these studies demonstrated that, under high temperatures and pressures,
the purported high stability of [B10H10]2− could be overcome. Since the successful hydrogenation
of [B10H10]2− species has only been reported at temperatures of 300°C and >500 bar H2.[32]

                                            Page 15 of 39

Moreover, the reversibility of this system under significantly more mild conditions would be of
considerable fundamental interest as it challenges the basic dogma relating to the stability and
bonding interactions in higher anionic closo-borane clusters.
 This stability of [B10H10]2− was further challenged in the Ph.D. thesis of Nguyen which
reported successful cycling of the Mg(BH4)2(THF)x – MgB10H10(THF)x system at 200°C and 90
bar H2 pressure.[33] Cycling was not only achieved under moderate conditions but with rapid
kinetics as well, with 40% re-charging of [B10H10]2− back to [BH4]− reportedly taking place in only
3 hours.[33] In view of the improved kinetics seen during the dehydrogenation of
Mg(BH4)2(THF)x, it seemed plausible that THF might also impact the hydrogenation reaction of
[B10H10]2− under modest conditions as well. If found to be true, these findings would indicate that
the Mg(BH4)2(THF)x – MgB10H10(THF)x system as a viable means of high-capacity hydrogen
storage with the cycling under such moderate conditions representing a major advance in hydrogen
storage technology. Therefore, in an attempt to understand the extent of this strangeness, studies
were conducted to establish definitively whether the reversible dehydrogenation of
Mg(BH4)2(THF)x to [B10H10]2− under mild conditions was indeed possible.
 The [B10H10]2− reversibility studies were performed at 200 °C using 11B NMR to characterize
the products. 11B NMR spectra showed that the relative proportion of B atoms present as [B10H10]2−
increases with time upon heating, even under 100 bar H2. While [B3H8]− readily re-hydrogenates
under upon heating to 200 ˚C under 100 bar H2 pressure, the amount of [B10H10]2− in partially
dehydrogenate material increases under these conditions. Longer attempted hydrogenation times
led only to higher conversions of [B10H10]2-. Clearly the dehydrogenation of Mg(BH4)2(THF)x to
[B10H10]2− is not reversible at temperatures ≤ 200°C and at pressures ≤ 100 bar H2. Formation of
[B10H10]2− occurs under a 10 bar of hydrogen pressure where formation of [B3H8]− is completely
suppression thus indicating that [B3H8]− is not an intermediate in the reaction pathway leading to
the formation of [B10H10]2−, but instead forms in a separate parallel pathway as illustrated in
Scheme 3.

1. Screening of Mg(BH4)2 and N-Heterocycles. Generally, studies have focused on O-
heterocycles and there have been very few reports of the dehydrogenation in the presence of
nitrogen based (N-based) Lewis bases and restricted to triethylamine (TEA) and ethylenediamine
(EDA).[15, 34] Dehydrogenation in the presence of TEA showed minimal conversion to higher
boranes/hydrogen release.[15] The studies with EDA showed high hydrogen release (8 wt% at
260 °C) occurs together with irreversible polymer formation.[34] There have been reports of ionic

impetus for the present study was to examine whether the N-heterocycle, pyrrolidine in analogy to

prior report of this class of compounds resulting from the dehydrogenation of borohydrides.

the tight thermodynamic constraints imposed by a closed system. Open system dehydrogenation
reactions were carried out under a slow, continuous argon flow to sweep hydrogen gas evolved

IV. CONCLUSIONS
Our efforts to develop a practical liquid hydrogen carrier consisting of furan or pyrrole solutions
of Mg(BH4)2 and pincer complex catalyst were confronted by several unforeseen limitations. The
borohydride was found to be resistant to dehydrogenation when fully dissolved in furans while
THF slurries of Mg(BH4)2 were found to undergo dehydrogenation to [B10H10]2- which is
thermodynamically prohibited from undergoing re-hydrogenation at practical temperatures and
hydrogen pressures. By contrast, dehydrogenation of mixtures of saturated N-heterocyclics
and Mg(BH4)2 were found to exceed the initial go-no-go volumetric hydrogen density metric.
An independent evaluation at NREL found the dihydrogen of 1:2 molar mixtures of
Mg(BH4)2/pyrrolidine released 52 g H2/L when heated to 180 ˚C for 24 h. However, further studies
revealed the mixtures to be unsuitable as liquid hydrogen carriers as the [BH4]- ion undergoes an
irreversible reaction with heterocyclic compounds to give BH3-amine adducts at ambient
temperature and secondary products at elevated temperatures. One such secondary product
B{N(CH2CH2)}3, tris(pyrrolidino)borane (TPB) appeared to have high potential as a LOHC. The

                                               Page 32 of 39

second go-no-go metric was met when preliminary studies of the hydrogenation of
tri(pyrrolyl)borane, its unsaturated counterpart could be fully hydrogenated. However, the
extent of dehydrogenation reaction in a closed system was found to be severely limited to about
15% by equilibrium constraints at 200 ˚C. Attempts to circumvent this problem by actively
removing hydrogen in an open systems using slow, continuous argon flow and with a back pressure
flow reactor were unsuccessful to due practical problems such as the high volatility of TPB at 200
˚C. Other LOHCs were screened as potential dehydrogenation partners for the borohydride but
were also found to be unsuitable. The results obtained with the LOHCs that were investigated are
quite discouraging. However, our studies did show that the iridium pincer complex is a highly
effective catalyst for its dehydrogenation of a wide variety of LOHCs. Thus the hypothesis that
LOHCs solutions of Mg(BH4)2 and a pincer complex catalyst could be the basis of an ultra-high
performance hydrogen carrier has not been disproven. However, the identification of suitable
LOHC for such a system has been found to be a daunting challenge.
