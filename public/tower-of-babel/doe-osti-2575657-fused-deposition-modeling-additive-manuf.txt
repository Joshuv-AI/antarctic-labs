 Abstract
This report details the development, characterization, and use of coal-enhanced composite
materials in additive manufacturing applications. High coal loading formulations—containing up
to 70 wt.% coal—were successfully extruded and processed using commercially available 3D
printers. Extensive experimental testing was conducted to assess the mechanical, thermal, and
microstructural properties of the composites. In parallel, multi-scale computational modeling was
employed to elucidate atomistic interactions and evaluate the effects of printing-induced defects
on structural performance. Large-scale printability trials demonstrated the feasibility of fabricating
complex components for tooling and construction applications, including wind turbine blade
molds and modular wall sections. Techno-economic analyses demonstrated the cost-effectiveness
and scalability of coal-enhanced composites for large-scale additive manufacturing applications
such as wind turbine blade tooling.

1. Introduction
The U.S. DOE’s Office of Fossil Energy and Carbon Management (FECM) Carbon Ore Processing
program supports the development of technologies that extract the full economic value of coal and
coal wastes by generating high-value and high-volume products. This project focused on utilizing
coal and carbon waste from coal-derived carbon foam manufacturing to develop coal-enhanced
3D printing materials for tooling and building/construction applications.
1.1. Project Objectives
The objective of this project is to develop coal-enhanced polymer composites from coal and coal
wastes that are compatible with commercially available 3D printers for producing structures used
in tooling and building/construction applications. The objectives for Budget Period 1 included
developing formulations that maximize coal-derived material content, developing and refining
coal-enhanced filament extrusion parameters, and assessing coal-enhanced filament mechanical
and physical properties for building/construction and tooling applications. Budget Period 2
objectives included developing printing parameters for coal-enhanced composites using
commercial 3D printers, printing tooling articles with commercial 3D printers, assessing
performance of 3D-printed structures made from coal-enhanced composites, developing techno-
economic analyses, identifying additional commercial applications for 3D printable coal-enhanced
composites, and developing Environmental, Safety, and Health (ES&H) analysis.

2.1.2. Coal-Enhanced Filament Extrusion
Composite filament formulations were developed following a systematic screening of
experimental filament extrusion trials. A range of commonly used thermoplastics for 3D printing
were evaluated, including virgin polymers such as polycarbonate (PC), polyethylene terephthalate
glycol (PETG), acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS), high-impact polystyrene (HIPS), polylactic
acid (PLA), and a PC-ABS blend. Additional formulations with high-density polyethylene
(HDPE) and Nylon 12 (PA12) were also assessed. Each base resin was evaluated for its ability to
produce coal-enhanced filaments with target diameters of 1.75 mm or 2.85 mm. Materials were
screened based on their ability to yield continuous filament strands within dimensional tolerances
compatible with commercial 3D printers. Only base resins meeting these criteria were selected for
further development. Filament extrusion trails using PC, ABS, HIPS, and PC-ABS were
unsuccessful due to common processing challenges. While these materials could be initially
extruded through the filament extruder, they failed during the filament drawing process, resulting
in fragmentation and discontinuous strands. As a result, these resins were excluded from
subsequent formulation efforts. On the other hand, PLA, PETG, PA12, and HDPE-based
formulations exhibited robust processing behavior and consistently produced continuous filaments
within acceptable diameter tolerances. Representative images of the successful coal-enhanced
filaments are presented in Figure 1.

2.1.3. Bench-scale Printing Trials
To evaluate the printability and performance of coal-enhanced polymer composites, test specimens
were fabricated using a bench-scale Flashforge Creator Pro FDM printer. Composite formulations
filled with carbon fiber and wood flour were also 3D printed for comparison purposes. The
 8

composite formulations investigated are summarized in Table 3. Test specimens were fabricated
for tensile, compressive, flexural, and impact testing, in accordance with ASTM D638, D695,
D790, and D256, respectively. All specimens were printed using the parameters outlined in Table
4, with printing nozzle temperatures, bed temperatures, and bed adhesion techniques optimized for
each matrix resin. The print orientation and infill pattern utilized are illustrated in Figure 2. For
baseline comparison, coal-enhanced composites were also fabricated via compression molding
technique. The comparative performance of 3D-printed versus traditionally manufactured
specimens is discussed in Section 2.2. Representative images of the 3D-printed specimens are
shown in Figure 3.
The coal-enhanced filaments were successfully processed using commercially available 3D
printers without requiring modifications to the 3D printer. Notably, coal-enhanced composites
demonstrated the highest printable filler loading, with successful printing achieved at up to 70
wt.% P8 coal. In comparison, the maximum printable filler content was 50 wt.% for carbon fiber
composites (CFCs) and 60 wt.% for wood plastic composites (WPCs). While all coal-enhanced
materials could be printed using a 0.4 mm nozzle diameter and 0.1 mm layer height, larger nozzle
diameters and layer heights provided more consistent results, particularly for high-loading
formulations, due to reduced nozzle clogging. Additionally, minimizing retraction distances and
retraction rates proved effective in mitigating extruder clogging. Overall, bench-scale printing
trials demonstrated that coal-enhanced materials offer superior filler loading capacity and
compatibility with conventional FDM systems, underscoring their practical potential for additive
manufacturing applications. Printability assessment of the best-performing coal-enhanced
formulations using scaled 3D printers is discussed in Section 4 of this report.

2.2. Performance of Coal-enhanced Materials
2.2.1. Tensile Properties
Following the successful fabrication of coal-enhanced composites via 3D printing, key mechanical
properties—including tensile, flexural, impact, and hardness—were evaluated. Tensile properties
for both 3D-printed (3DP) and compression-molded (CM) samples of PLA, PETG, HDPE, and
PA12 composites are presented in Figure 4. In general, 3DP and CM samples exhibited similar
trends in tensile behavior as coal content increased. As anticipated, increasing coal loadings in
PLA, PETG, and HDPE composites led to a reduction in ultimate tensile strength (UTS). For
instance, the UTS of 3DP PLA decreased by 38.6%, dropping from 53.6 MPa to 32.9 MPa at 40
wt.% coal, while the 3DP PETG composites showed a 42.4% reduction from 52.9 MPa to 30.5
MPa at the same loading level. The impact on HDPE was less; at 40 wt.% coal, the UTS of 3DP

 10

HDPE dropped 24.1% (from 17.0 MPa to 12.9 MPa), and further declined to 9.5 MPa at 70 wt.%—
approximately 44.1% of the strength of neat HDPE. These strength reductions at higher coal
loadings are likely due to coal particle agglomeration and poor interfacial adhesion between the
coal and polymer matrix. This weak bonding increases stress concentration sites, contributing to
particle pull-out, which appeared to be the dominant failure mechanism. These observations align
with previously reported behavior in particle-filled composites, where increased filler loading
generally corresponds with decreased tensile strength [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9].
In contrast, PA12-based composites demonstrated a different response to coal incorporation. The
UTS of 3DP PA12 increased slightly from 28.9 MPa to 30.4 MPa with 20 wt.% coal. Notably, the
CM PA12 composite exhibited the greatest enhancement in UTS, increasing by 45.2%—from 32.1
MPa (unfilled) to 46.6 MPa—at 20 wt.% coal. These results are consistent with earlier studies
showing that coal and carbon fillers can strengthen polyamide matrices [10], [11], [12]. The
observed strength enhancement in PA12 composites is likely due to improved interfacial bonding,
which facilitated more efficient stress transfer between the matrix and coal particles.
Elastic modulus (EM) increased with coal content across all formulations and manufacturing
methods. Among the materials tested, 3DP HDPE composites showed the most pronounced
improvement, with EM rising from 0.5 GPa to 2.2 GPa—a 340.6% increase—at 70 wt.% coal.
PLA composites showed the smallest stiffness change, with only a 12.9% increase at 30 wt.% coal.
For 3DP PETG, EM increased by 20.7% at 40 wt.% coal, while 3DP PA12 exhibited a 42.5%
increase at 30 wt.% coal. These enhancements in stiffness can be primarily attributed to: (1) the
inherently higher stiffness of coal particles relative to the polymer matrix, and (2) reduced mobility
of the polymer chains due to the increased filler content. Similar stiffening trends have been
reported in prior studies of particulate-reinforced polymer composites [7], [8], [9], [12], [13].

compared to conventional wood plastic composites (WPCs) and carbon fiber composites (CFCs).
Figure 5 shows the UTS and EM values for the tested formulations. In general, the coal-enhanced
formulations demonstrated lower UTS than the unfilled HDPE, with the exception of those
containing 15 wt.% Keystone 325, 15 wt.% Keystone 121, 60 wt.% CFoam Green, and 15 wt.%
CFoam Calcined. Interestingly, increasing the filler content to 40–60 wt.% did not consistently
result in further reductions in UTS for some coal-enhanced formulations, suggesting that failure
mechanisms were dominated by porosity and weak inter-layer adhesion. Although EM values for
the coal-enhanced composites were higher than unfilled HDPE, the improvements were often
modest and not statistically significant—except for formulations with 60 and 70 wt.% filler
content. Compared to WPCs, most coal-enhanced formulations achieved higher UTS values,
except the formulation with 15 wt.% CFoam Green. However, WPCs generally exhibited higher
EM values at equivalent filler loadings, with the exception of the CFoam Calcined formulations
and all coal-enhanced formulations containing 60 wt.% filler. At the lower filler loading (15 wt.%),
coal-enhanced formulations outperformed or matched CFCs in both UTS and EM. However, at
higher loadings, CFCs exhibited superior tensile performance. Notably, coal-enhanced
formulations containing CFoam Calcined consistently delivered higher UTS and MOE values than
other coal-enhanced materials containing Keystone 325, Keystone 121, and CFoam Green—
except at 60 wt.% for the Keystone 121 and CFoam Green. The decline in tensile strength at higher

filler loadings was attributed to poor interfacial bonding between the coal particles and HDPE,
insufficient layer-to-layer adhesion during 3D printing, and increased porosity. Similar findings in
previous studies have linked such reductions in mechanical performance to filler agglomeration,
interfacial debonding, and printing-induced porosity [14], [15], [16], [17], [18].

2.2.2. Flexural Properties
Figure 6 shows the flexural properties of 3DP and CM coal-enhanced composite formulations.
Results indicated a similar trend to that observed in tensile properties with respect to increasing
coal content. For PLA-, PETG-, and HDPE-based composites, flexural strength (FS) decreased as
coal loading increased. Specifically, FS for 3DP PLA dropped by 50.5%, from 77.2 MPa to 38.2
MPa at 40 wt.% coal, while 3DP PETG exhibited a 36.8% reduction from 75.5 MPa to 47.7 MPa
for the same coal loading. HDPE-based 3DP composites were less affected by coal incorporation;
at 40 wt.%, FS decreased only 8.6%—from 20.9 MPa to 19.1 MPa. However, CM HDPE-based
composite with 70 wt.% coal showed a 28.1% reduction in FS. These reductions are consistent
with prior studies on particle-filled polymers and are attributed primarily to filler agglomeration
and poor interfacial adhesion [8], [19]. Contrary to this trend, PA12 composites demonstrated an
increase in FS with coal. The 3DP PA12 composites experienced a 16.9% increase, from 30.2 MPa
to 35.3 MPa at 20 wt.% coal. Such improvement was attributed to enhanced coal–polymer
interfacial bonding.
As expected, flexural modulus (FM) of all coal-enhanced composites increased with coal loading.
CM PLA composites exhibited a modest 7.9% increase in FM—from 3.8 GPa to 4.1 GPa at 40
wt.% coal—mirroring earlier trends observed in elastic modulus behavior. For 3DP PETG, FM
increased by 25.0% from 2.0 GPa to 2.5 GPa at 40 wt.% coal, while 3DP PA12 composites showed
a 42.9% improvement from 0.7 GPa to 1.0 GPa at 30 wt.% coal. HDPE-based composites
 13

demonstrated the most pronounced increase in stiffness. At 70 wt.% coal loading, FM increased
by 105.6% to 1.2 GPa in the 3DP HDPE composites and by 176.6% to 3.2 GPa in CM HDPE
composites. These enhancements align with literature findings, which attribute the increase to
stiffer coal particles and restricted mobility of polymer chains [2], [19], [20], [21].

2.2.3. Impact Resistance
Impact resistance was assessed for both 3DP and CM coal-enhanced composites. As shown in
Figure 8, increasing coal content consistently reduced the impact resistance across all formulations
and fabrication methods. For example, the 3DP PA12 composites showed a 40.1% reduction in
impact resistance, dropping from 33.4 J·m⁻¹ to 20.0 J·m⁻¹ at a coal loading of 30 wt.%. Similar
trends were observed for the 3DP PLA and PETG composites, with impact resistance decreasing
by 58.7% and 44.6%, respectively, at 40 wt.% coal. The HDPE composites exhibited the most
significant reduction; the impact resistance of the 3DP HDPE composites dropped by 82.1%, from
94.2 J·m⁻¹ to 16.9 J·m⁻¹ at 40 wt.% coal, and further declined to 3.4 J·m⁻¹ at 70 wt.%. These
observations align with previous studies on particulate-reinforced polymer composites [8], [9],
[13], [21], [22], [23].

Impact resistance of additional HDPE-based composite formulations incorporating various types
of coal-derived materials (Keystone 325, Keystone 121, CFoam Green, CFoam Calcined) was
evaluated and compared to conventional WPC and CFC materials (Figure 9). At lower coal
contents (15 wt.% and 40 wt.%), coal-enhanced formulations with Keystone 325 exhibited greater
impact resistance than those with Keystone 121. However, this trend reversed at higher loadings
(50–70 wt.%), where Keystone 121-based composites outperformed those made with Keystone
325. This reversal may stem from differences in particle size; as the filler concentration increases,
the finer particles in the Keystone 325 formulations become more difficult to uniformly disperse
or fully wet, creating regions that facilitate crack propagation and compromise impact strength.
Among all coal-enhanced composite formulations, the composite incorporating CFoam Calcined
displayed the highest impact resistance. When compared to WPCs, coal-enhanced composites
containing Keystone 325 and 121 showed lower impact performance, likely due to the higher
aspect ratio of wood fillers. These elongated fibers improve stress transfer and energy dissipation
during impact more effectively than the smaller, more equiaxed coal particles. Notably, the CFC-
based composites had the highest impact resistance among all tested formulations.

2.2.4. Hardness
Figure 10 shows that Shore D hardness of the coal-enhanced materials increased with coal content.
This behavior was attributed to the incorporation of a coal filler that is harder than the base
polymer. Similar trends have been reported in prior studies involving polymer composites
reinforced with rigid particulate fillers [9], [24], [25]. Among the materials tested, HDPE
composites showed the highest improvement, with hardness increasing by 19.3%—from 59.0 for
the unfilled polymer to 70.4 at a coal loading of 70 wt.%. This enhanced hardness significantly
improved the printability of the HDPE-based composite filament. The relatively soft unfilled
HDPE was prone to abrasion by the extruder’s drive gear, often resulting in poor extrusion and
failed prints. In contrast, the harder coal-enhanced composite filaments exhibited better abrasion
resistance during printing. PLA and PETG composites exhibited notable increases in hardness,
rising by 4.6% and 9.0%, respectively, at 40 wt.% coal loading. Likewise, PA12 composites
showed a 4.6% increase in hardness—from 72.5 to 75.8—at 30 wt.% filler content.

2.2.5. Coefficient of Thermal Expansion (CTE)
Figure 11 shows the coefficient of the CTE values for the coal-enhanced formulations. All
composite formulations exhibited a reduction in CTE as coal content increased. This trend aligns
with prior research, which observed decreases in CTE for polymers reinforced with fibers or
particulates [21], [26], [27], [28], [29]. In the case of PLA-based composites, a notable 53.1%
decrease in CTE was observed, from 59.7 μm·m⁻¹·°C⁻¹ to 28.0 μm·m⁻¹·°C⁻¹ at 40 wt.% coal. PETG
composites exhibited a 25.8% reduction, decreasing from 55.4 μm·m⁻¹·°C⁻¹ to 41.1 μm·m⁻¹·°C⁻¹
at the same loading. For HDPE, the unfilled polymer possessed a CTE value of 109.2 μm·m⁻¹·°C⁻¹.
This value dropped by 15.5% at 40 wt.% coal to 92.3 μm·m⁻¹·°C⁻¹, and by 44.8% at 70 wt.% to
60.3 μm·m⁻¹·°C⁻¹. The PA12 composite, with 30 wt.% coal, showed a 16.7% decrease from 148.3
μm·m⁻¹·°C⁻¹ to 123.6 μm·m⁻¹·°C⁻¹. Due to their higher CTEs, HDPE and PA12 are typically more
difficult to 3D print compared to PLA and PETG [30], [31]. However, incorporating 70 wt.% coal
reduced the CTE of HDPE to a level comparable with that of neat PLA and PETG, thereby
enhancing its suitability for 3D printing. In contrast, the reduction in CTE for PA12 was more
limited due to the lower coal loadings in the 3D printable formulations.

Lowering the CTE significantly enhanced the 3D printability of the materials in two key ways.
First, it improved the dimensional accuracy of printed parts. Since thermoplastics are deposited in
a molten state during printing, they shrink as they cool and solidify. Composites with reduced CTE
experienced less shrinkage during this cooling phase compared to their unfilled counterparts,
enabling tighter dimensional tolerances in the final prints. Second, the reduction in CTE minimized
warping—particularly for HDPE and PA12—resulting in more consistent and reliable prints. As
shown in Figure 12, HDPE samples with 50 wt.% coal demonstrated significantly reduced edge
curling and longitudinal warping. This reduction in deformation led to improved bed adhesion and
decreased inter-layer delamination, ultimately enhancing print quality and reliability. Furthermore,
the lowered CTE enabled the successful fabrication of dimensionally stable HDPE parts without
the need for a heated chamber. This suggests potential energy savings during printing compared
to using neat HDPE, which typically requires printing at elevated chamber temperatures to
maintain print integrity.

2.2.8. Specific Heat Capacity
Figure 15 shows the specific heat capacity (Cp) values of PLA, PETG, HDPE, and PA12
composites over the 50 °C to 90 °C temperature range. For all four polymers, Cp increased with
temperature, while the addition of coal reduced Cp relative to the neat polymers. At 50 °C, PLA
with 40 wt.% coal showed a 24.1% decrease in Cp, dropping from 1.74 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹ to 1.32 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹.
PETG composites exhibited a smaller reduction, with a 9.2 % decrease from 1.41 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹ to
1.28 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹ at the same filler content. For HDPE-based composites at 50 °C, Cp declined by
20.5 % at 40 wt.% coal and by 26.7 % at 70 wt.% coal. Furthermore, increasing coal content
beyond 40 wt.% produced only marginal further reductions. PA12 with 30 wt.% coal showed an
18.8 % decrease, from 2.55 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹ to 2.07 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹ at 50 °C. This reduction in Cp can be
attributed to the incorporation of bituminous coal, which has a comparatively lower Cp of
approximately 1.16 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹ at 50 °C. Following the rule of mixtures, as coal content increases,
the composite Cp shifts toward that of the filler. The minimal effect of coal on PETG can be
explained by its initial Cp being only about 21.6 % higher than that of coal, in contrast to HDPE
and PA12, whose neat Cp values were 93.5 % and 119.6 % higher, respectively. Lower Cp in these

composites suggests reduced energy input is needed for heating during manufacturing, which can
translate into cost savings for the targeted large‑scale applications.

2.2.9. Thermal Conductivity
Figure 16 shows the thermal conductivity values of the coal‑enhanced composites. For PLA, HDPE,
and PA12-based formulations, thermal conductivity decreased as coal loading increased. This effect
was most pronounced in HDPE, where the neat polymer exhibited a conductivity of 0.61 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹,

3. Multi-scale Computational Material Simulations
3.1. Molecular Dynamic Simulation of Coal-enhanced Composites
The atomistic characteristics of the coal‑enhanced composites are examined in this section of the
report. Given the amorphous and chemically diverse nature of coal, along with the variety of
reactions occurring during coal pyrolysis, the computational protocol was extended beyond
ab‑initio density functional methods using VASP to incorporate reactive force field simulations.
Specifically, the Reactive Force Field potential (REAXFF) implemented in LAMMPS was
employed, enabling the description of complex chemical reactions through bond‑order‑based
interactions and allowing for the simulation of large coal molecular systems at elevated
temperatures. This section presents preliminary findings from structural investigations of P8 coal
and selected polymers (PETG, PLA, HDPE, and Nylon‑12) using both DFT and REAXFF
methods. These analyses aim to provide an atomistic and chemical understanding of
maceral‑plastic interactions within the 3D‑printed coal‑enhanced composites. Rather than
immediately exploring mixture dynamics, a systematic and detailed characterization of the
individual materials was conducted to establish a more comprehensive understanding of the
underlying physical and chemical phenomena governing the mixing process.
3.1.1. Bituminous Coal Model
Using the 2D representative model for coal suggested by Solomon [43], a realistic 3D version of
the P8 coal was created. The models were built in a simultaneous process that involved sequential
bond creation of each hydrocarbon (aliphatic, aromatic) ring and/or functional groups, followed
by energy optimization using the Merck Molecular Force Field (MMFF94)[44] as implemented
within the molecular builder/visualizer software package, Avogadro[45]. The sequential process
of model creation allowed us to achieve realistic bond-order and torsional angles for the 3-D
model. A stable and energetically optimized structure by conjugated gradient relaxation of the
model using ab initio DFT and REAXFF potentials was obtained. Figure 29 shows the 3D P8
model and a schematic diagram of the 2-D Solomon’s model. The molecular formula for the P8
model is C161H140O15N2S2.

models starting from the “unit” maceral in Figure 29. Figure 32 shows a 6798-atom system that
has been energetically relaxed using REAXFF as implemented within LAMMPS. The validation

 32

and 2000 K) were performed to investigate the effects of temperature on product distributions in

sheets and other molecular fragments as well, such that radical propagation would occur for
releasing gas and generating tar, which are in good agreement with the fundamental steps of coal
pyrolysis.

3.1.3. Hydrogen Bonding in PA12-based Composites
Molecular simulations examining the interaction between PA12 and P8 coal indicated the
formation of extensive hydrogen bonds between the hydroxyl groups (ph‑OH) in coal and the
amide groups (‑NH‑C=O‑) of PA12. Figure 35 provides visual representations of these
interactions: Figure 35a illustrates the local configuration before and after hydrogen bond
formation between the strong proton donor ph‑OH in coal and the oxygen atom of the amide group,
while Figure 35b shows examples of double hydrogen bonds[51]. The observed hydrogen bond
lengths ranged from 1.61 Å to 1.86 Å. This bonding is attributed to the high polarity of the amide
linkages along the polyamide chain (Equation 2), whose rigidity and planarity generate partial
positive and negative charges on the nitrogen and oxygen atoms, respectively. This polarization
facilitates π‑bond cooperativity—referred to as resonance‑assisted hydrogen bonding (RAHB)
[51], [52]—wherein charge delocalization through π‑bonds strengthens the N‑H donor when the
amide oxygen simultaneously engages in hydrogen bonding (i.e., Ph‑OH•••O=C‑N‑H), leading to
a stabilized zwitterionic resonance structure [51].

 (2)
Although PLA and PETG also contain polar functional groups (COOH in PLA and OH in PETG)
capable of forming hydrogen bonds, their densely packed non‑polar carbon backbones—such as
methyl groups in PLA and cyclohexane and aromatic benzene structures in PETG—introduce
steric hindrance, reducing the accessibility of these polar sites. Conversely, PA12’s longer, linear
12‑carbon backbone reduces steric congestion, providing greater conformational flexibility and
enabling more favorable spatial rearrangements for hydrogen bonding.
Experimental FTIR analysis validated these simulation results. Spectra for PA12 composites
containing 0%, 20%, and 30% coal (Figure 35c) were normalized to the C‑H peak, assuming these
bonds remain unchanged during processing. No new peaks appeared with coal incorporation,
indicating the absence of covalent bonding. However, a significant reduction in the N‑H in‑plane
bending peak at ≈1557 cm⁻¹ was observed (Figure 35c), while the N‑H stretching peak at
≈3284 cm⁻¹ remained unchanged. This reduction is consistent with hydrogen bond formation
restricting the mobility of the N‑H bond. Together with the simulation data, these findings confirm
that hydrogen bonding occurs between PA12 and coal. Such bonding improves interfacial
adhesion, allowing coal particles to act as reinforcing fillers, thereby enhancing the tensile and
flexural properties of the composites.

3.2. Finite Element Analysis of 3D-Printed Structures
Finite element analysis (FEA) was employed to evaluate the effect of 3D printing-induced defects
on the mechanical behavior of the coal-enhanced materials. CT scan data of the printed samples
were processed into a meshed model using Amira and subsequently imported into FEA software
for analysis as porous structures. FEA models were developed in ABAQUS 2023 using nonlinear
plastic material formulations to simulate both porous and solid structures under tensile and flexural
loading. The solid and porous tensile models demonstrated stability and were analyzed using the
ABAQUS Standard solver, with an initial time step of 0.01 increments progressing to a total value
of one. The solid flexural model was also stable under the same solver settings. In contrast, the
porous flexural model experienced initial contact challenges caused by roller overpenetration into
the porous surface when using the Standard solver, even after reducing the initial increment size.
To address these issues and preserve pore geometry under large displacements, the simulation was
switched to ABAQUS/Explicit, which provided improved contact handling.

5. Techno-economic Studies and Market Analyses
A techno-economic analysis was performed to assess the feasibility of using coal-enhanced
materials for manufacturing wind turbine blade tooling, in comparison to conventional subtractive
manufacturing methods. Techno-economic models for AM typically encompass four major

 47

categories: pre-processing, materials, processing, and post-processing[54], [55]. Pre-processing
includes activities such as part design, slicing, machine setup, and printing consumables. Material
costs are largely dependent on production volume, with economies of scale expected to reduce
costs over time. Processing costs account for expenditures related to equipment acquisition, service
contracts, and energy consumption during production. Post-processing requirements vary
depending on the materials used and the intended application, with most extrusion-based AM parts
requiring additional steps such as machining, post-curing, polishing, or surface coating to achieve
the desired final properties.
The TEA model was developed to evaluate the production of a 1-meter-long tool fabricated using
a Tradesman P3-44 FGF printer. The estimated costs were then compared to the costs associated
with manufacturing the same tool using traditional subtractive processes. The total volume of the
composite tooling was 0.03 m3. While part design will be the same for AM and subtractive
processing, part slicing and machine setup will take approximately 4 hr. In addition, the pre-
processing costs for AM included the cost of a polypropylene build sheet ($65).
To estimate the cost of the coal-enhanced materials, two pelletization lines were assumed to be
added to an existing coal-plastic composite facility[56]. The following assumptions were utilized:
 1. Each line produces 454 kg/hr of coal-enhanced pellets (5 mm in diameter) with 60
 wt.% coal,
 2. Plant operates 24 hours per day for 50 weeks per year, with an assumed capacity
 factor of 85%,
 3. Taxes and insurance were calculated at 2% each of the Capital Cost,
 4. The composite density is 1160 kg/m3, and
 5. Cost of the pellets includes capital, fixed, and variable O&M costs based on a
 capital cost spread over two years and profits recovered over 20 years.
Based on these assumptions, the overall capital and operating costs were estimated at $10,890,145.

 Appendix A
 Technology Maturation Plan
A. Technology Readiness Level
At the beginning of the project, the Technology Readiness Level (TRL) for the additive
manufacturing (AM) of coal-enhanced composites was at TRL 3. Through collaborative efforts,
Ohio University (OHIO) and its industry partners successfully developed 3D-printable coal-
enhanced composite formulations and demonstrated their printability on both commercially
available small-scale and large-scale 3D printers, advancing the technology to TRL 5. The process
of making coal-enhanced composites starts by pulverizing and sieving the coal-derived wastes
(reclaimed coal from mining waste and vitreous dust from coal-derived carbon foam
manufacturing). Subsequently, sieved coal-derived waste is melt-mixed with different
thermoplastics, including polylactic acid (PLA), polyethylene terephthalate glycol (PETG), high-
density polyethylene (HDPE), and polyamide (PA 12). Note reclaimed coal from mining waste
needs drying prior to the melt-mixing step. The mixture could be melt-mixed using a batch mixer
or a twin-screw extruder. Mixing the composite components using a twin extruder is preferred at
the commercial scale since it is a continuous method. Following melt-mixing, the composites are
pelletized, and the pellets can either be fed directly into fused granulate fabrication (FGF) printers
or used as feedstock for filament extrusion. In the latter case, the pellets are extruded and spooled
into coal-enhanced filaments with diameters of 1.75 mm and 2.85 mm. Processing and material
additives, as well as extrusion parameters including melting temperature, extrusion speed, and
spooling speed, were optimized to produce composite filaments within acceptable tolerances for
small and large-scale commercially available printers. In addition, coal-enhanced composite
formulations underwent material testing and analysis (MT&A) to identify and optimize printing
parameters for large-scale additive manufacturing applications. Mechanical, physical, and thermal
properties of coal-enhanced composite materials and printed test specimens were determined as
per ASTM methods. Fabrication and testing methods for coal-enhanced composites are similar to
commercial methods, which allow efficient scaling-up and commercialization of the technology.
OHIO has evaluated a wide range of formulations with different types of coal waste and content
(up to 70 wt.%).
Two primary applications have been identified for this technology: composite tooling and additive
housing construction. Current and emerging materials in these sectors face significant technical,
economic, and environmental challenges. To demonstrate feasibility, the project team successfully
3D-printed a 3-ft wind turbine blade tooling prototype and a 3-ft wall panel using coal-enhanced
composites. These applications align with rapidly expanding markets, as the 3D-printed housing
sector is projected to grow from $1.2 billion in 2024 to $24.5 billion by 2034 [1], while the global
industrial mold market is expected to increase from $51 billion in 2024 to $71 billion by 2029 [2].
The market size and expected growth of these markets provide an excellent opportunity for
developing and growing the market segments for additively manufactured uncarbonized and
carbonized structures. A techno-economic analysis confirmed that AM with coal-enhanced
composites offers significant advantages over conventional subtractive manufacturing, reducing
costs by ~63% and shortening production time by eliminating labor-intensive steps. Unlike
subtractive processes, which rely heavily on expensive materials and manual labor, AM lowers
material costs to ~1% of total production while enabling scalability for large or complex parts.

 1

Together, these results demonstrate that coal-enhanced composites are well-positioned to capture
market share in both tooling and housing by delivering a cost-efficient, scalable, and sustainable
alternative to conventional materials.
B. Proposed Work
The project scope of work for budget periods 1 & 2 was focused on optimizing the coal-enhanced
composite formulations, identifying printing parameters, printing test parts, and assessing the
performance of non-carbonized and carbonized test parts for the proposed applications.
Completing the proposed scope of work matured the technology to TRL 5. The success criteria for
the project included developing a coal-enhanced composite containing >50 wt.% coal content,
which can be utilized in a commercial 3D printer, integrating coal-enhanced composites with lab-
and large-scale commercially available 3D printers, and demonstrating utilization of coal-
enhanced composites in printing of representable building/construction and tooling parts, and
quantification of application-specific properties. Lab-scale testing and 3D printing of test parts
support the advancement of the technology to TRL 5. In Task 4 of the project, optimized coal-
enhanced composites and printing parameters were utilized to 3D print test parts to assess
performance for building/construction and tooling applications, which helps to scale up the
technology and reduce the commercialization timeline.
The scaled fabrication process for coal-enhanced composites is illustrated in Figure 1. Note the
scaled process is remarkably similar to the lab-scale manufacturing process of coal-enhanced
filaments (described in section A of this document). However, filament diameter and production
rate are going to be increased, and extrusion parameters will be adjusted accordingly. The process
starts with the melt-mixing of coal waste (reclaimed coal from mining wastes or vitreous dust from
coal-derived carbon foam manufacturing) with thermoplastic resins. Then, the mixture gets
extruded and spooled to the required diameter using a filament extruder. Processing aids such as
lubricants and performance additives—including impact modifiers, flame retardants, and coupling
agents—may be incorporated to optimize performance. Alternatively, following melt-mixing, the
material can be pelletized (5-6 mm in diameter) and supplied directly to fused FGF printers.
Compared to FDM printing, FGF enables much faster print times, accommodates higher coal filler
loadings, reduces thermal cycling, and lowers production costs, making it a superior option for
large-scale manufacturing.
The selection of coal wastes and their content allows control of various properties, including
mechanical, thermal, and electrical properties. To minimize coal-enhanced filament manufacturing
costs and reduce the commercialization timeline, the coal-enhanced filament facility will be
located inside an existing extrusion facility. The addition of coal handling equipment will allow
coal-enhanced filament production at any extrusion facility within a reasonable transportation
distance from the feedstock’s sources.

A techno-economic analysis (TEA) was conducted to assess the feasibility of fabricating wind
turbine blade tooling with 3D-printable coal-enhanced composites in comparison to conventional
subtractive manufacturing methods. The model assumed the addition of two pelletization lines to
an existing coal–plastic composite facility, each capable of producing 454 kg/hr of 60 wt.% coal-
enhanced, operating 24 hours per day for 50 weeks annually at an 85% capacity factor. Capital,
fixed, and variable operating costs—including labor, maintenance, utilities, and chemicals—were
incorporated, resulting in a pellet cost of ~$1.32/kg. Using these inputs, a 1-meter tool was
compared against a subtractively manufactured counterpart. The analysis showed that AM reduced
the break-even sales price by ~63% and shortened production time by 11 hours, largely by
minimizing labor-intensive steps. Material costs for AM represented only ~1% of the total
compared to ~36% for subtractive processes, underscoring AM’s cost efficiency for large and
geometrically complex tooling.
C. Post-project Plans
This two-year project matured the coal-enhanced filament technology to TRL 5. Comprehensive
lab-scale formulation testing was completed, yielding optimized coal-enhanced composite
formulations and printing parameters. These developments were further validated by producing
representative demonstration parts on a commercial-scale 3D printer, highlighting the
technology’s potential for practical applications. Important mechanical, thermal, and physical
properties—including tensile and flexural strength, hardness, impact resistance, heat deflection
temperature, coefficient of thermal expansion, glass transition and melting temperatures, specific
heat capacity, thermal conductivity, thermo-oxidative stability, and density—were evaluated for
the coal-enhanced composites and compared with commercially available alternatives such as
wood–plastic composites and carbon fiber composites. Printing parameters, including printing
temperature, nozzle size, printing speed, layer thickness, layer width, infill density, infill pattern,
and build orientation, were also determined. Furthermore, a preliminary techno-economic analysis
was developed to evaluate operating costs, equipment requirements, coal-enhanced composite
production costs, break-even sales prices (BESP, $/lb), and return on investment (ROI) based on
projected sales prices. To advance CPC manufacturing to TRL 6 (pilot scale), the next stage of

 Appendix B

 Environmental, Safety, and Health (ES&H) Analysis

The project team has identified two initial market applications for the 3D-printable coal-enhanced
composites, including composite tooling and additive housing construction. The preliminary
ES&H plan focused on the potential effects of the proposed applications in the following
situations:
 (1) Receiving, storage, handling, and use of raw materials to manufacture
 The main components of the coal-enhanced composite materials are coal-derived waste
 materials (reclaimed coal from mining wastes or vitreous dust from coal-derived carbon
 foam manufacturing) and thermoplastic resins. According to materials safety data sheets
 (MSDS) for bituminous coal and plastics (e.g., polylactic acid (PLA), polyethylene
 terephthalate glycol Copolymer (PETG), high-density polyethylene (HDPE), and Nylon),
 storing and handling of these materials should prevent the generation, release, and
 accumulation of dust due to potential explosion hazard. Prolonged or repeated inhalation
 of coal dust might cause damage to the respiratory system and should not be released into
 the environment. Project partners (Core Natural Resources, JuggerBot 3D, Consol
 Innovations, and IC3D) have the required expertise for shipping, receiving, handling, and
 processing the raw materials at the commercial scale with minimal to no risks. The raw
 materials will be delivered to the manufacturing facility using a truck and unloaded into a
 silo. The silo gases are then vented to a fabric filter, preventing dust emissions. Dust
 suppression systems included in the manufacturing facility will mitigate fine coal
 emissions, explosion hazards, and exposure of workers and local residents to dust hazards.
 Coal sourced from either operating mines or legacy mining waste impoundments utilized
 in the composites is ultra-low in ash, moisture, volatiles, and sulfur, making it an ideal filler
 for coal-enhanced composites. Vitreous carbon byproducts from coal-derived carbon foam
 manufacturing are another ideal filler that requires less processing compared to reclaimed
 coal.
 (2) Shipping to customer locations and handling of manufactured products at customer
 locations
 Shipping and handling of the proposed products and the 3D printable coal-enhanced
 composites will have lower risks compared to existing technologies. For tooling
 applications, the 3D printed products will be lighter in weight and safer to handle. Addative
 manufacturing of a house will require less interaction between the workers and the product
 since the manufacturing process is semi-autonomous. During shipping and handling of
 coal-enhanced materials to a house site, coal particles are less likely to leak into the
 environment since they will be incorporated into a plastic resin.
 (3) Field modification and installation (e.g., cutting, drilling, finishing, etc.) of
 manufactured products
 Additive manufacturing enables the production of near-net-shape parts with minimal
 material waste and reduced need for post-processing. If field modifications are needed, the
 dust generated will have minimal impact on the workers. A recent study conducted by our
 research group indicated respirable dust generated from cutting coal plastic composite
 boards (similar to coal-enhanced composites) was classified as insignificant.

 1

 (4) Long-term use of the manufactured product in residential, commercial, and
 industrial settings
 Uncarbonized products (i.e., wind turbine blade tooling) manufactured using coal-
 enhanced composites will have little to no emission generation. However, carbonizing the
 coal-enhanced materials will generate value-added pyrolysis products. These products
 could be used to produce resins or be combusted onsite to generate thermal energy to be
 used in the manufacturing process. Carbon capture technologies should be added to prevent
 the emission of combustion products. Carbon emission generated from the carbonization
 process is expected to be lower compared to coal combustion and carbon emissions
 associated with existing technologies (i.e., cement).
 (5) Demolition, removal, and recycling/disposal as applicable at the end of the
 manufactured product’s useful life.
 The useful life of residential construction typically exceeds 30 years. In aerospace
 applications, composite tooling must endure thousands of heating cycles, which makes
 carbonized tooling especially desirable. For wind turbine blade manufacturing, the
 expected service life of tooling is approximately 1,000 blades. Recent studies have shown
 that incorporating coal into high-density polyethylene (HDPE) increases the thermos-
 oxidative stability of the resulting composites, suggesting an extended service life of the
 final product [1]. Similar effects were observed in this project for HDPE-based
 formulations. Due to the improved thermal stability, uncarbonized and carbonized products
 are all recyclable after their service life. A reclaim system could be utilized to reprocess
 coal-enhanced composite scraps, uncarbonized, and carbonized products. Our studies for
 coal plastic composite material (similar to coal-enhanced composites) used in decking
 applications showed the coal-enhanced composites to pass EPA method 1311 testing,
 indicating safe disposal into municipal solid waste facilities (MSWF) [2]. If needed, any
 waste generated from the demolition or removal of the proposed products could be safely
 disposed of in an MSWF.
