GoQuant: Geometric Orthogonal Residual Projection for Multiplier-Free Power-of-Two Transformer Quantization Thanks: In preparation.
Maoyang Xiang, Tao Luo, Bo Wang

Abstract
The deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision Transformers (ViTs) on edge devices is significantly constrained by memory limitations and the critical timing bottlenecks introduced by dense Multiply-Accumulate (MAC) arrays. In the ultra-low bit regime, logarithmic Power-of-Two (PoT) quantization provides a hardware-efficient alternative by replacing MAC operations with bit-shifts. However, the non-uniform exponential lattice is inherently limited by a Low Angular Resolution Regime , a structural flaw that becomes particularly pronounced at sub-4-bit thresholds, leading to a notable degradation of high-dimensional feature manifolds. To address this geometric limitation, we propose Geometric Orthogonal Residual Projection Quantization (GoQuant), an algorithm-hardware co-design framework. By formulating quantization as a dual-basis geometric projection, GoQuant adaptively synthesizes a higher-resolution residual lattice using strictly shift-and-add operations. Furthermore, its analytical solver offers a practical alternative to computationally intensive gradient-based optimization, reducing the full-model calibration time for LLaMA-2-7B to approximately 15 minutes . Extensive evaluations demonstrate GoQuant’s applicability across modalities and its hardware efficiency. Under the 3-bit (W3/A16) constraint, it achieves a perplexity of 6.10 on LLaMA-2-7B, comparing favorably to conventional MAC-intensive baselines like AWQ without relying on asymmetric scaling, while maintaining competitive accuracy in 4-bit scenarios. At the silicon level, standard-cell RTL synthesis at a 28nm node indicates that GoQuant effectively mitigates the timing bottlenecks associated with dense multiplier trees. By flattening the combinational logic depth, our parallel shift-and-add datapath reduces the critical path delay to 0.35 ns . This theoretically allows the accelerator to operate at 2.85 GHz , suggesting an agile, multiplier-free execution paradigm for high-throughput edge intelligence.

Introduction

The deployment of large language models (LLMs) and vision transformers (ViTs) on edge devices is frequently constrained by the
multiplier tax
—the critical combinational logic depth and routing complexity associated with hardware multiply-accumulate (MAC) units. In modern silicon processes, a 16-bit floating-point multiplier consumes an order of magnitude more energy and introduces significantly deeper logic paths than simple addition or bit-wise operations. To advance high-throughput edge computing, there is a growing motivation to transition from computation-heavy, multiplication-centric architectures to multiplier-free paradigms. In this context,
Power-of-Two (PoT)
quantization emerges as a promising approach, offering potential improvements in timing efficiency by replacing deep multiplication trees with parallel bit-shifts.

Despite its hardware advantages, low-bit quantization often faces challenges regarding precision. Conventional approaches typically attribute this performance degradation to a scalar distribution mismatch between the discrete lattice and the Gaussian statistics of neural weights. However, this scalar-centric perspective often overlooks the high-dimensional nature of Transformer operations
[
3
]
. As illustrated in Fig.

, the inner product is essentially the projection of a continuous weight vector onto a discrete lattice manifold. In high-dimensional spaces, the fidelity of this projection depends not just on magnitude, but critically on the preservation of the vector’s direction, which dictates the alignment of latent features. While current methods effectively manage the scaling factor (
Scaler
), directional integrity can remain unpredictable, contributing significantly to performance degradation in extreme quantization.

When applying this geometric perspective to the hardware-friendly PoT domain, a key limitation emerges: its inherently non-uniform angular resolution. Unlike linear lattices, the angular distribution of PoT basis vectors is governed by the
\arctan(2^{k})
function. Consequently, it operates within a
low angular resolution regime
, as shown in Fig.
2
, which is particularly pronounced around the diagonal regions of the projection space. This structural characteristic creates an
Angular Gap
, where the quantized vector’s orientation can deviate enough to substantially compromise the semantic integrity of the feature manifold.

. This structural characteristic introduces notable

between valid lattice points, irrespective of the bit-width.

To address the angular degradation associated with a 1-D PoT mapping, we introduce the
Geometric Orthogonal Residual Projection Quantization (GoQuant)
framework. Rather than mapping continuous high-dimensional vectors onto a single discrete basis (
\mathbf{b}_{1}
), GoQuant constructs a 2-D orthogonal subspace to correct the angular distortion mathematically. By deriving a secondary basis (
\mathbf{b}_{2}
) that is orthogonal to the primary projection (
\langle\mathbf{b}_{1},\mathbf{b}_{2}\rangle=0
), the framework captures the orthogonal residual—directional information often lost during the initial 1-D mapping. This dual-basis projection helps mitigate the
Low Angular Resolution Regime
and preserves the spatial orientation of the feature manifold, while remaining compatible with the zero-MAC hardware constraints of the PoT domain.

To implement this projection efficiently while maintaining representational fidelity, GoQuant utilizes a decoupled, three-stage pipeline. First, a preconditioned manifold smoothing mechanism is applied to adjust the weight topology. Second, a geometric residual matching process determines the discrete orthogonal basis (
\mathbf{b}_{2}
) in Euclidean space. By isolating this discrete search from activation data, the method enables an
\mathcal{O}(N)
basis selection that reduces susceptibility to calibration bias. Finally, a data-driven joint scale optimization incorporates calibration data to compute the continuous coefficients (
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
). This stage evaluates
channel-wise importance
based on empirical input features, dynamically prioritizing the projection fidelity of critical feature dimensions according to their activation magnitudes.

Evaluating multiplier-free networks solely on algorithmic metrics may not fully reflect their physical deployment constraints. To address the gap between theoretical sparsity and practical silicon implementation, we extend our evaluation to standard-cell RTL synthesis, analyzing the timing implications associated with conventional arithmetic units.

2
Related Work

The deployment of large-scale foundation models on resource-constrained edge platforms has driven extensive research into neural network quantization
[
15
,
5
]
. While significant progress has been achieved in compressing model weights, simultaneously maintaining ultra-low bit-width accuracy and mitigating computationally heavy hardware operations remains a notable challenge. In this section, we review the prior literature across three pivotal domains. First, we examine the evolution of Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) and the hardware implications of reconstruction-heavy paradigms. Second, we analyze recent advancements in high-dimensional rotational quantization, highlighting the trade-off between geometric angular resolution and on-chip computational overhead. Finally, we discuss the trajectory of multiplier-free architectures, examining the representational limitations of scalar logarithmic quantization and demonstrating how our proposed framework aims to address these challenges.

2.1
Post-Training Quantization and the Reconstruction Bottleneck

Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) has emerged as a fundamental technique for deploying large-scale vision transformers (ViTs) and Large Language Models (LLMs), primarily due to its circumvention of the substantial computational costs associated with retraining. Early PTQ methods predominantly focused on scalar rounding mechanisms and optimal clipping ranges
[
9
,
8
]
. However, to extend quantization into the ultra-low bit-width regime (
\leq 4
-bit), recent state-of-the-art frameworks have increasingly shifted towards block-wise error reconstruction. In the vision domain, methods such as BRECQ, QDrop, and AIQViT aim to maintain accuracy by iteratively compensating for quantization perturbations using gradient-based optimization
[
7
,
14
,
6
]
. Similarly, in the LLM domain, methodologies mitigate quantization errors by exploiting second-order Hessian information to minimize local reconstruction loss
[
5
]
, or by applying activation-aware scaling to protect highly salient outlier weights
[
9
]
.

Despite their empirical success in significantly reducing memory bandwidth—an important advancement for LLM deployment—these reconstruction-heavy paradigms often encounter two distinct bottlenecks. First, the stochastic iterative grid search required during calibration can incur substantial time costs, often taking hours per model. Second, from a hardware architecture perspective, they predominantly operate as
weight-only compression
rather than full datapath quantization. Because they generally necessitate runtime dequantization to higher-precision formats (e.g., FP16) before executing the matrix multiplication, they successfully alleviate the memory wall but do not fully eliminate the timing bottlenecks of dense Multiply-Accumulate (MAC) units on silicon. To address these limitations, our GoQuant framework provides an analytical solver that operates natively in the quantized domain, facilitating multiplier-free hardware compatibility without the overhead of iterative searches.

2.2
High-Dimensional Geometry and Rotational Quantization

As traditional 1D scalar quantization approaches practical limits around the 3-bit threshold, recent literature has explored high-dimensional geometric spaces. A prominent trend involves using orthogonal rotations to precondition the weight and activation manifolds prior to discretization. Three notable methodologies represent the state-of-the-art in this rotational paradigm. First, the QuIP family establishes theoretical bounds for sub-3-bit fidelity by utilizing fixed Randomized Hadamard Transforms (RHT) to ensure error incoherence, coupling this with advanced vector projection onto the dense
E_{8}
lattice
[
2
,
12
]
. Extending this concept, QuaRot applies Hadamard rotations holistically to both weights and activations, smoothing outliers to facilitate uniform low-bit quantization
[
1
]
. Advancing beyond fixed transforms, SpinQuant introduces parameterized orthogonal matrices, actively rotating the feature spaces via gradient optimization
[
10
]
.

Although mathematically elegant, the paradigm of explicitly rotating data to fit an optimal lattice introduces hardware considerations. While high-performance cloud accelerators may absorb this complexity, applying these dense orthogonal matrices at runtime often requires deep addition trees and non-local memory routing (e.g., butterfly networks). This can exacerbate on-chip wire routing congestion and vector decoding latency. Such architectural complexities present challenges for the minimalist hardware philosophy required for edge computing. This architectural bottleneck motivates the exploration of purely multiplier-free and transform-free networks, which we discuss next.

2.3
Multiplier-Free Architectures and Logarithmic Quantization

To reduce both the MAC unit reliance of standard PTQ and the transform overheads of rotational methods, architectural focus has increasingly shifted towards multiplier-free networks. This philosophy often manifests through ternary representations or PoT quantization, replacing matrix multiplications with shift-and-add logic. Prominent examples include the BitNet architecture for models trained from scratch
[
13
]
, ShiftAddLLM for retrofitting pre-trained LLMs
[
16
]
, and RepQ-ViT
[
8
]
, which employs a post-training per-channel PoT quantization scheme.

However, achieving multiplier-free inference without compromising model utility remains an open challenge, as existing methodologies exhibit operational trade-offs. While BitNet demonstrates the viability of ternary weights, it typically requires resource-intensive from-scratch training paradigms, and matching full-precision performance at equivalent parameter scales remains an active area of exploration. Conversely, post-training methods like ShiftAddLLM rely on bit-level operational substitutions, effectively replacing standard multiplications with an expanded volume of bit-sliced additions. While arithmetically valid, this increases the accumulation workload and frequently requires layer-wise optimization. Finally, while shift-add frameworks like RepQ-ViT are effective in vision tasks, they often face challenges generalizing to LLMs. LLM fidelity relies on preserving significant activation outliers, a requirement that rigid per-channel scalar formats can struggle to accommodate, leading to notable accuracy degradation under sub-4-bit constraints.

Crucially, independent scalar rounding often does not fully account for the non-uniform angular distribution of the PoT metric space, leading to the
Low Angular Resolution Regime
and substantial
Angular Gaps
. Here, GoQuant introduces an alternative paradigm. Rather than explicitly rotating the high-dimensional data at runtime or relying on expanded bit-slice additions (as in ShiftAddLLM), GoQuant
leaves the data stationary and adaptively constructs the local lattice
. By leveraging the
\mathcal{O}(N)
Strided Dual-Exchange Theorem, GoQuant captures critical angular fidelity in-place via a 2D orthogonal residual basis. This fine-grained geometric repair achieves competitive representational fidelity compared to state-of-the-art rotational methods, providing a robust prerequisite for viable sub-4-bit multiplier-free deployment.

3
Methodology

In this section, we detail the theoretical formulation and execution pipeline of the GoQuant framework. Diverging from traditional 1-D scalar quantization, GoQuant conceptualizes quantization error fundamentally as a geometric angular deviation. Rather than attempting to compensate for this error through conventional scaling, it addresses the deviation by capturing the residual within a 2-D orthogonal subspace, constrained by a multiplier-free, Power-of-Two (PoT) hardware paradigm. To promote both representational fidelity and algorithmic efficiency, the framework decouples the projection process into three distinct stages: manifold-aligned preconditioning, geometric discrete basis search, and data-driven joint scale optimization.

3.1
Overall Framework of GoQuant

Traditional Power-of-Two (PoT) quantization projects a weight vector
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
onto a 1D discrete lattice
\mathcal{S}=\{0,\pm 2^{k}\mid k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
as
\hat{\mathbf{w}}=c_{1}\mathbf{b}_{1}
. However, this 1D projection encounters limitations related to the
Low Angular Resolution Regime
due to the non-uniform angular distribution inherent in the PoT domain. GoQuant addresses this by employing a Gram-Schmidt-like expansion within the discrete lattice. The objective is to find a secondary basis
\mathbf{b}_{2}\in\mathcal{S}^{d}
and continuous scales
c_{1},c_{2}
such that
\hat{\mathbf{w}}=c_{1}\mathbf{b}_{1}+c_{2}\mathbf{b}_{2}
, subject to the orthogonality constraint
\mathbf{b}_{1}\perp\mathbf{b}_{2}
.

3.2
Manifold-Aligned Preconditioning

To mitigate severe performance degradation caused by activation outliers, GoQuant performs a channel-wise preconditioning before the geometric search. This aligns the Euclidean weight space with the empirical feature manifold. Let
\mathbf{a}_{max}
denote the calibration activation statistics (e.g., the root mean square for CV tasks or the maximum absolute value for LLMs), and
\mathbf{w}_{max}
be the column-wise maximum absolute weight. We define a smoothing scaling vector
\mathbf{s}_{vec}
controlled by a balancing factor
\alpha_{smooth}\in[0,1]
:

\mathbf{s}_{vec}=\frac{\mathbf{a}_{max}^{\alpha_{smooth}}}{\mathbf{w}_{max}^{1-\alpha_{smooth}}}

The processed weight is then computed as
\mathbf{w}_{proc}=\mathbf{w}\odot\mathbf{s}_{vec}
. This transformation implicitly stretches the dimensions with higher activation energy, ensuring that the subsequent geometric search naturally prioritizes channels that dominate the model’s output stability without requiring complex iterative re-weighting.

3.3
Primary Projection and Orthogonal Residual Extraction

To effectively utilize the representational capacity of an ultra-low bit hardware budget (e.g., 3-bit or 4-bit), GoQuant transitions from a conventional symmetric projection to a hardware-aware, asymmetric logarithmic lattice. A requirement for energy-proportional edge inference is the existence of an absolute zero-state, which is essential to trigger hardware zero-skipping and clock gating.

For instance, at the 3-bit threshold (i.e., exactly
2^{3}=8
discrete physical states), instead of relying on external sparsity masks, our quantization function
\mathcal{Q}(\cdot)
explicitly breaks spatial symmetry by repurposing the smallest positive quantization bin to represent the absolute zero. Consequently, the primary geometric anchor
\mathbf{b}_{1}
is constrained to the following asymmetric discrete lattice
\mathcal{L}_{3\text{bit}}
:

\mathcal{L}_{3\text{bit}}=\{-1,-0.5,-0.25,-0.125,0,+0.25,+0.5,+1\}

Mathematically, we first bound the preconditioned weight into the lattice domain
[-1,1]
using a channel-wise max-normalization scalar
s_{norm}=\|\mathbf{w}_{proc}\|_{\infty}
. For the normalized weight
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{w}_{proc}/s_{norm}
, the primary projection
\mathbf{b}_{1}=\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{v})
assigns each element to its nearest valid state in
\mathcal{L}_{3\text{bit}}
.

Because this native 1D mapping maps continuous vectors to exponentially sparse coordinates, it can introduce notable angular gaps. As illustrated in Fig.
3
, to isolate and correct this geometric deviation, we define the orthogonal quantization residual
\mathbf{r}_{\perp}
via a Gram-Schmidt projection. By projecting the original preconditioned weight away from the primary axis
\mathbf{b}_{1}
, we obtain:

\mathbf{r}_{\perp}=\mathbf{w}_{proc}-\frac{\langle\mathbf{w}_{proc},\mathbf{b}_{1}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{b}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}}\mathbf{b}_{1}

(1)

By definition, this residual is orthogonal to the primary anchor (
\mathbf{r}_{\perp}\perp\mathbf{b}_{1}
). The subsequent objective is to construct a secondary discrete basis
\mathbf{b}_{2}
that maximally aligns with this orthogonal residual.

3.4
Hierarchical Strided Dual-Exchange and Orthogonal Residual Projection

A key challenge in extreme low-bit quantization is balancing the memory overhead of scaling factors with the geometric degrees of freedom required for angular fitting. As illustrated in Fig.
3
, GoQuant employs a
Hierarchical Block Architecture
. Continuous projection coefficients (
c_{1},c_{2}
) are shared across a coarse macro-block of size
N=128
to limit memory footprints. Conversely, the generation of the secondary orthogonal basis
\mathbf{b}_{2}
is executed at a finer micro-block granularity of
G=32
(denoted as blocks
a,b,c,d
).

To construct the micro-block components of
\mathbf{b}_{2}
that maximize the inner product with the local orthogonal residual
\mathbf{r}_{\perp}
, while maintaining discrete hardware constraints, we propose the
Strided Dual-Exchange
operator.

Theorem 1 (Strided Dual-Exchange Orthogonality).

Let
\mathbf{v}=[x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{G-1}]^{T}
be a micro-block sub-vector of
\mathbf{b}_{1}
. Given a stride
s\in\{1,2,\dots,G/2\}
, we define a bijective index mapping
\sigma_{s}(i)=(i+s)\pmod{G}
, partitioning the indices into disjoint pairs
(i,j)
where
j=\sigma_{s}(i)
.
Let
\eta_{i}\in\{+1,-1\}
be a local sign factor for each pair. We construct the dual-exchanged vector
\mathbf{v}^{*}=[y_{0},y_{1},\dots,y_{G-1}]^{T}
by applying the transformation:

[y_{i},y_{j}]=[-\eta_{i}x_{j},\eta_{i}x_{i}]

Then,
\mathbf{v}^{*}
is orthogonal to
\mathbf{v}
, i.e.,
\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}^{*}\rangle=0
, for any valid stride
s
and sign configuration
\boldsymbol{\eta}
.

Proof.
For each pair
(i,j)
, the local inner product is
x_{i}y_{i}+x_{j}y_{j}=x_{i}(-\eta_{i}x_{j})+x_{j}(\eta_{i}x_{i})=0
. Summing over all pairs yields
\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}^{*}\rangle=0
.
\blacksquare

By applying localized intra-block permutations and sign flips across different strides (e.g., adjacent swap
s=1
for block
a
, and strided swap
s=2
for block
b
), the derived basis
\mathbf{b}_{2}
maintains mathematical orthogonality to
\mathbf{b}_{1}
without leaving the discrete lattice.

As demonstrated in Fig.
4
, this theorem ensures orthogonality in the 2D plane through index permutation and sign inversion. Although the negation in Theorem 1 may algebraically yield a state missing from the asymmetric
\mathcal{L}_{3\text{bit}}
(e.g.,
+0.125
), this is resolved efficiently in hardware. In standard Shift-Add ALUs, states are encoded in Sign-Magnitude format. The operation
-\eta_{i}x_{j}
requires no additional memory state; it can be implemented as an XOR gate toggling the sign bit on-the-fly.

To assemble the full macro-block
\mathbf{b}_{2}
that optimally aligns with
\mathbf{r}_{\perp}
, GoQuant utilizes a decoupled dual-level search:

1.

Micro-Block Analytical Sign Inference:
For a candidate stride
s
, the objective is to maximize the alignment with the orthogonal residual
\max\langle\mathbf{b}_{2},\mathbf{r}_{\perp}\rangle
. For each pair
(i,j)
, the local inner product contribution is:

y_{i}r_{\perp,i}+y_{j}r_{\perp,j}=(-\eta_{i}x_{j})r_{\perp,i}+(\eta_{i}x_{i})r_{\perp,j}=\eta_{i}(x_{i}r_{\perp,j}-x_{j}r_{\perp,i})

To maximize this sum, the optimal sign
\eta_{i}^{*}
is determined analytically to align with the direction of the term in parentheses:

\eta_{i}^{*}=\text{sign}(x_{i}r_{\perp,j}-x_{j}r_{\perp,i})

2.

Constant-Time Stride Evaluation:
Because the candidate stride space is finite and small (i.e., exactly 16 structural permutations for
G=32
), we perform a deterministic enumeration to select the optimal stride
s
. Unlike stochastic greedy algorithms that may encounter local minima or iterative methods that incur overhead, this bounded analytical evaluation provides the intra-block optimal configuration in
\mathcal{O}(G)
operations.

3.

Macro-Block Assembly:
The
N/G=4
optimally configured micro-blocks (blocks
a,b,c,d
) are concatenated to form the complete 128-dimensional basis
\mathbf{b}_{2}
.

By leveraging this hierarchical design and analytical sign formulation, GoQuant yields the optimal discrete basis via deterministic
\mathcal{O}(N)
operations, avoiding the need for exhaustive search in high-dimensional spaces.

3.5
Joint Scale Optimization: GEO vs. REF Modes

Figure 5:
Once the orthogonal bases are anchored, the continuous scales
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are solved globally and broadcasted across the entire 128-dimensional macro-block (spanning micro-blocks
a,b,c,d
) to approximate the original weight.

As illustrated in Fig.
5
, once the discrete orthogonal directions
\mathbf{b}_{1}
and
\mathbf{b}_{2}
are anchored, we solve for the continuous scales
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, which are shared across a macro-block of size
N=128
to bound memory overhead. We introduce two specialized solver modes:

1. GEO Mode (Geometric Optimization):

In the absence of external calibration data, we assume an isotropic feature space, simplifying the optimization to a Euclidean distance minimization in the preconditioned weight domain. The objective is to minimize the geometric projection error
\|\mathbf{w}_{proc}-(c_{1}\mathbf{b}_{1}+c_{2}\mathbf{b}_{2})\|_{2}^{2}
. The optimal scales are analytically derived by solving the
2\times 2
least-squares system:

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{b}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{b}_{1}&\mathbf{b}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{b}_{2}\\
\mathbf{b}_{2}^{T}\mathbf{b}_{1}&\mathbf{b}_{2}^{T}\mathbf{b}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}\\
c_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{w}_{proc}^{T}\mathbf{b}_{1}\\
\mathbf{w}_{proc}^{T}\mathbf{b}_{2}\end{bmatrix}

(2)

Crucially, because Theorem 1 mathematically ensures structural orthogonality between the two discrete bases (
\mathbf{b}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{b}_{2}=0
), the off-diagonal cross terms vanish. This diagonalizes the system and decouples the variables, allowing the continuous scales to be computed via independent, straightforward scalar projections:
c_{1}=(\mathbf{w}_{proc}^{T}\mathbf{b}_{1})/\|\mathbf{b}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}
and
c_{2}=(\mathbf{w}_{proc}^{T}\mathbf{b}_{2})/\|\mathbf{b}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}
. This property makes the GEO mode computationally straightforward, facilitating zero-shot quantization or rapid edge deployment.

2. REF Mode (Activation-Aware Refinement):

To capture cross-channel feature correlations and compensate for quantization loss in the feature manifold, REF mode incorporates the calibration inputs
\mathbf{X}_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
. We define the layer-wise output target
\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{X}_{q}\mathbf{w}_{proc}
and the projected basis matrices
\mathbf{A}=[\mathbf{X}_{q}\mathbf{b}_{1},\mathbf{X}_{q}\mathbf{b}_{2}]
. The continuous scales
\mathbf{c}=[c_{1},c_{2}]^{T}
are jointly optimized using Ridge regression to address ill-conditioned feature matrices:

\mathbf{c}^{*}=(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{Y}

(3)

where
\lambda=10^{-4}
acts as the Tikhonov regularization factor. By absorbing the empirical feature covariance
\mathbf{X}_{q}^{T}\mathbf{X}_{q}
, the REF mode compensates for spatial noise.

It is important to distinguish our decoupled formulation from paradigms that embed dynamic activation quantization explicitly within the scale-solving loop. Injecting discrete, non-smooth activation quantization noise directly into the design matrix
\mathbf{A}
can negatively impact the numerical stability of the normal equations. Mathematically, this induces an
Errors-in-Variables (EIV)
problem. A stochastically perturbed covariance matrix can lead to biased estimators and erratic matrix inversions, which may introduce bias into the optimal continuous scales
\mathbf{c}^{*}
. To mitigate this, our REF mode isolates the geometric basis optimization from dynamic activation noise, relying on calibration features to yield deterministic and mathematically sound coefficients.

3.6
Fully Quantized Inference Dataflow

To deploy the GoQuant framework on hardware, both the dynamic activations
\mathbf{X}
and the continuous geometric scales (
\mathbf{c}_{1},\mathbf{c}_{2}
) must be symmetrically quantized to
b_{a}
-bit and
b_{c}
-bit integers (denoted as
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{m}
), with respective global scaling factors
s_{x}
and
s_{cm}
. By integrating these integer components, the linear layer computation
\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}
is transformed into a hardware-compatible dataflow:

\hat{\mathbf{Y}}=(s_{x}s_{c1})\left[(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{B}_{1})\text{diag}(\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{1})\right]+(s_{x}s_{c2})\left[(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{B}_{2})\text{diag}(\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{2})\right]

(4)

This formulation confines the floating-point scaling factors to the final global accumulation step. The core
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
matrix multiplications—
(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{B}_{1})
and
(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{B}_{2})
—are executed using zero-MAC shift-add operations, as
\mathbf{B}_{1},\mathbf{B}_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
. Furthermore, the
b_{c}
-bit integer scaling via
\text{diag}(\tilde{\mathbf{c}})
introduces a lightweight
\mathcal{O}(N)
multiplication per 128-dimensional macro-block. By amortizing this operation across the inner loop, the computational overhead becomes minimal, yielding an effectively multiplier-free datapath while maintaining sub-4-bit representational fidelity.

4
Result

To systematically validate the GoQuant framework, we present an empirical evaluation structured around four core dimensions.

First, we assess the framework’s cross-modality applicability by applying zero-MAC hardware constraints to Vision Transformers on the ImageNet-1K benchmark. Second, we examine the relationship between algorithmic fidelity and physical deployment through an Edge NLP algorithm-hardware co-evaluation, integrating LLaMA-2 calibration efficiency with 28nm RTL synthesis metrics. Third, we evaluate the architectural design choices via an ablation study on the geometric benefits of dual-basis projection. Finally, we explore the framework’s deployment flexibility and resilience to calibration bias through zero-shot reasoning tasks, comparing the data-free GEO and REF paradigms.

4.2
Edge NLP Inference and Hardware Co-Evaluation

To explore the practical deployment potential of the GoQuant framework in resource-constrained edge scenarios, we present an algorithm-hardware co-evaluation. Table
2
provides a comprehensive overview, integrating predictive perplexity, software-level calibration time, and RTL-synthesized hardware metrics at a 28nm technology node.

Figure
6
illustrates the architectural distinctions between our approach and traditional baselines. Conventional quantization methods (e.g., AWQ, SpinQuant) typically map their execution onto traditional Multiply-Accumulate (MAC) units. A notable characteristic in digital VLSI design is that at ultra-low bit-widths (e.g., 3-bit or 4-bit), the silicon area of a standard MAC unit does not significantly exceed that of a shift-adder, as the multiplier logic scales down. However, the primary hardware bottleneck in these extreme low-bit regimes shifts from area to
combinational logic depth
. Even a low-bit multiplier necessitates an internal adder tree, which introduces unavoidable routing and propagation delays. By leveraging the Power-of-Two (PoT) nature of our orthogonal residuals, the GoQuant datapath bypasses this multiplication tree. The computation is physically instantiated as parallel bit-shifters, effectively flattening the logic depth.

This flattened architecture translates into tangible timing improvements. As detailed in Table
2
, under sub-4-bit configurations, the our hardware unit achieves a consistent critical path delay of
0.35 ns
. Compared to the AWQ baseline (which ranges up to 0.90 ns), this shortened logic path theoretically allows the GoQuant accelerator to operate at a frequency of approximately
2.85 GHz
on 28nm silicon. Concurrently, it maintains a favorable balance of energy efficiency, delivering up to
3.33 TOPS/W
under the W3/A8 configuration.

Coupled with these hardware characteristics, GoQuant demonstrates practical advantages in software deployment, particularly regarding calibration efficiency. Our implementation relies entirely on vanilla PyTorch operators without any custom CUDA optimizations. The computational overhead inherent to gradient-based optimization is substantial; for instance, using its official repository implementation, SpinQuant requires approximately 358.0 minutes to calibrate the L2-7B model. To evaluate search-based methods under a strictly controlled, pure-Python environment, we benchmarked AWQ using the LLMC framework
[
11
]
, recording calibration times of 32.1 minutes for L2-7B and 49.6 minutes for L2-13B. In stark contrast, GoQuant’s analytical formulation fundamentally shifts the computational paradigm. Operating strictly under the same unoptimized conditions, GoQuant completes the calibration in merely 15.1 minutes for the L2-7B model. This dramatic acceleration over both gradient-driven and search-based methods underscores the profound inherent efficiency of our closed-form solution, demonstrating exceptional practical performance even prior to any hardware-level engineering optimization.

Beyond computational efficiency, GoQuant robustly preserves model fidelity. While it maintains competitive performance in standard 4-bit configurations compared to AWQ, it shows a notable improvement in the more challenging 3-bit regime, successfully reducing the L2-7B perplexity from 6.49 to 6.10. Ultimately, GoQuant provides a highly favorable trade-off between accuracy and deployment agility. Even in instances where perplexity might be marginally higher, the magnitude of reduction in calibration time—achieved purely through algorithmic innovation rather than hardware engineering—combined with improvements in hardware frequency, makes GoQuant an exceptionally practical choice for dynamic edge adaptation.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose the Geometric Orthogonal Residual Projection Quantization (GoQuant) framework, an algorithm-hardware co-design approach for ultra-low-bit model quantization. By diverging from traditional Multiply-Accumulate (MAC) dependent execution, GoQuant formulates quantization as a dual-basis geometric projection, enabling the mapping of both Large Language Models (LLaMA-2) and Vision Transformers (ViTs) onto a multiplier-free, Power-of-Two (PoT) datapath.

Our empirical evaluation indicates that GoQuant achieves a favorable Pareto frontier for edge intelligence. Algorithmically, the analytical solver facilitates full-model calibration in approximately 15 minutes, reducing the computational overhead typically associated with contemporary search-based methods. Furthermore, the decoupling of the Geometric-Only (GEO) and Activation-Refined (REF) modes provides a practical perspective on calibration bias, offering practitioners a flexible choice between data-free zero-shot reasoning and data-driven precision.

At the hardware level, the shift-and-add architecture reduces the combinational logic depth inherent to dense multipliers. Synthesized at a 28nm technology node, the GoQuant accelerator attains a critical path delay of 0.35 ns, supporting a theoretical maximum clock frequency of approximately 2.85 GHz. Additionally, under an iso-frequency comparison at 1.8 GHz, it delivers an energy efficiency of 3.33 TOPS/W under sub-4-bit constraints.

Ultimately, GoQuant demonstrates that extreme quantization can be realized effectively without relying on asymmetric scaling, runtime dequantization, or deep multiplier trees. By aligning geometric principles with silicon-level timing efficiency, GoQuant offers a viable path toward the agile deployment of large foundation models on resource-constrained edge devices.
