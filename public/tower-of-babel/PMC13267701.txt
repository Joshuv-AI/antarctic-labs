Spatial flows of urban carbon sequestration services in Chinese urban agglomerations
Authors: Qi Pang, Jie Xu, Sha Pei, Ying Zhou, Xiaojie Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUrban agglomerations regulate the balance between carbon sequestration supply and demand. This study examines carbon sequestration service flows across 19 Chinese urban agglomerations from 2000 to 2020 by integrating supply-demand assessment with circuit theory-based flow modeling. Carbon supply increased moderately, whereas demand rose rapidly, leading to persistent spatial imbalances. Supply is higher in western and southern regions, while demand concentrates in major eastern urban clusters. More than half of the agglomerations show supply surplus, whereas others remain balanced or deficit. Flow simulations reveal network structures in which ecological supply areas surround urban demand centers, with well-connected corridors in central and western regions and fragmented networks in the east. These results highlight the role of spatial connectivity in regulating ecosystem service redistribution and provide a basis for cross-regional ecological compensation and coordinated carbon governance.Subject areas: earth sciences, ecology, environmental science

Introduction

By 2050, over 70% of the global population is projected to reside in urban areas, which will account for more than 80% of global GDP.1 Since China’s Reform and Opening-Up, both the number of cities and the degree of urbanization have increased substantially. Urban agglomerations, as key spatial carriers of new urbanization, have been integrated into national strategic planning. Despite rapid economic growth, the uncontrolled expansion of urban agglomerations has exacerbated resource and environmental pressures, resulting in ecosystem degradation and concentrated carbon emissions. The mismatch between the supply and demand of ecosystem services has become increasingly pronounced.2 In the context of China’s “dual carbon” goals, regulating regional carbon budgets and optimizing the spatial distribution of carbon sequestration services (CSSs) have become central tasks for promoting green transformation and achieving sustainable development.3,4

As a key regulating service, ecosystem carbon sequestration plays a vital role in mitigating climate change and enhancing ecological security by absorbing and storing atmospheric CO2. However, under conditions of high-density development and intensive land use, and influenced by factors such as industrial structure, population size, and land-use patterns, carbon sequestration supply and demand exhibit substantial spatial heterogeneity and mismatch.5 In recent years, the concept of ecosystem service flow has emerged as a key theoretical framework for examining spatial linkages between service supply and demand, emphasizing the paths, intensities, and transmission mechanisms of actual service flows.6 Despite these advances, modeling ecosystem service flows in polycentric and highly heterogeneous urban agglomerations remains a significant methodological challenge. Urban agglomerations are characterized by multiple development centers, strong cross-boundary socioeconomic interactions, and pronounced disparities in resource endowment and emission intensity. These features give rise to complex spatial coupling processes and non-linear supply-demand relationships that are difficult to capture using conventional supply-demand balance models or static spatial matching approaches. Moreover, administrative boundaries often fail to reflect functional ecological linkages, limiting the accurate identification of interregional service connections and spillover pathways. Consequently, there is an urgent need for spatially explicit methods capable of revealing the structural characteristics and transmission mechanisms of CSSs at the urban agglomeration scale.

In studies of CSS flows, a key conceptual challenge lies in representing CSSs, which are commonly diffuse and omnidirectional regulating services, within a spatial transmission framework. Unlike water supply or soil conservation services with explicit physical directionality, CSS flows do not correspond to actual carbon transport, but represent potential interaction pathways linking sequestration supply areas and emission demand areas from a coupled supply-demand perspective. Nevertheless, the application of circuit theory to CSS remains debated, because carbon sequestration capacity is inherently diffuse, whereas circuit theory simulates directional flows between defined sources and sinks.7,8 Circuit theory, originally developed for analyzing landscape connectivity, has proven effective in identifying critical paths, resistance corridors, and key ecological nodes by representing landscapes as resistive surfaces and modeling spatial interactions as current flow.9 Recent studies have extended this framework to ecosystem service flows, including carbon regulation, to simulate multi-path interactions, identify ecological corridors, and locate pinch points, providing insights into spatial dependencies and connectivity constraints.10,11,12 These studies demonstrate that circuit theory can effectively capture multi-path interactions among fragmented ecological patches, making it a useful tool for understanding service transmission in heterogeneous urban systems.

Importantly, because CSS is spatially diffuse, circuit-theory-based analyses should be interpreted as representing potential transmission along supply-demand gradients rather than actual physical carbon transport. The abstraction of virtual flows, sensitivity to resistance surface construction, and limited empirical validation may introduce uncertainties; therefore, theoretical assumptions and limitations must be clearly articulated.8,13

As complex socio-ecological systems with intensive resource use and carbon emissions, urban agglomerations exhibit fragmented ecological functions and spatial imbalances in carbon sequestration capacity. Most existing research has focused on supply-demand patterns, carbon spillovers across urban boundaries, and ecological network construction.5,10,13 However, several critical gaps remained in the literature: (1) the dynamic processes of carbon sequestration flows were underexplored, with most studies limited to static pattern identification; (2) the external dependencies of urban agglomerations had not been systematically assessed, and key interregional pathways remained unidentified; and (3) a lack of coupled intra- and inter-regional perspectives hampered the development of integrated spatial governance and ecological compensation frameworks. In addition, most existing studies rely on administrative or natural boundaries, limiting their capacity to depict service flows across polycentric and heterogeneous urban systems characterized by strong cross-regional coupling and asynchronous development trajectories. Therefore, a process-oriented analytical framework that captures the spatial evolution and pathway dynamics of carbon sequestration flows is urgently needed. In addition, most current studies relied on administrative or natural boundaries, limiting their capacity to accurately depict service flows across polycentric and heterogeneous urban regions. This issue is particularly pronounced in agglomerations with diverse resource endowments and asynchronous development stages, where complex cross-regional coupling processes prevail. There is a pressing need to adopt modeling approaches grounded in ecological logic and service response mechanisms to identify carbon sequestration transmission pathways and structural features across urban and peripheral ecosystems. Accordingly, this study examined 19 national-level urban agglomerations in China as the focal units of analysis, focusing on the flow dynamics of ecosystem CSSs under the guidance of carbon peaking and carbon neutrality goals. By integrating circuit theory with ecological network analysis and grounding in the coupling between the supply and demand of CSSs, a spatial flow network was systematically constructed at the urban agglomeration scale.

The objectives of this study are to (1) identify supply-demand patterns and spatial matching characteristics of CSSs within urban agglomerations and evaluate their spatiotemporal evolution; (2) simulate intra-urban carbon sequestration pathways using resistance surfaces, construct ecological network structures, and identify key nodes and flow corridors; and (3) expand the analysis to capture “intra-regional-extra-regional” linkages by constructing a cross-regional carbon flow network and revealing the dependency patterns and input-output structures between urban agglomerations and surrounding ecosystems.

This study made three key contributions: (1) tt transcended conventional administrative boundaries by shifting the analytical focus toward ecosystem service flows and constructing a CSS network with urban agglomerations as demand centers and ecosystems as supply sources; (2) circuit theory was employed to simulate multi-path flow processes and combined with network analysis to quantify the directionality and structural characteristics of carbon flows, identify key service nodes and ecological corridors, and support spatial ecological governance; and (3) a coupled analytical framework was established to capture intra- and inter-regional carbon flow dynamics, identify ecological source- and sink-type urban agglomerations, uncover interregional spillover effects, and provide a scientific basis for cross-regional collaborative ecological governance.

Results

Temporal and spatial patterns of supply and demand of carbon sequestration services in urban agglomerations

Carbon sequestration data for 2000, 2010, and 2020 derived from the CASA model revealed marked spatial heterogeneity across China’s 19 urban agglomerations (Figure 1). Nationally, western and southern regions generally exhibited higher carbon sequestration, with average supply values of 1,442.11 gC·m−2·a−1 in 2000, 1,189.80 gC·m−2·a−1 in 2010, and 1,561.86 gC·m−2·a−1 in 2020. Eastern and northern areas showed lower levels, reflecting reduced vegetation cover and higher urbanization. Urban agglomerations such as Chengdu-Chongqing, Hohhot-Baotou–Erdos-Yulin, and the middle reaches of the Yangtze river exhibited relatively high carbon sequestration, exceeding 1,000 gC·m−2·a−1 in 2020 due to abundant forests and grasslands. In contrast, traditional industrial regions, including Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei (BTH), the Shandong peninsula, and central-southern Liaoning, displayed lower capacity (<1,000 gC·m−2·a−1), attributable to urban expansion and reduced ecological space.

Spatio-temporal pattern of NEP in China urban agglomerations in 2000, 2010, and 2020

Over time, carbon sequestration generally increased, particularly in central and western regions, reflecting the effects of ecological restoration programs such as the Conversion of Cropland to Forest Program. Eastern coastal agglomerations, however, showed limited improvements or slight declines, highlighting ongoing pressures from urbanization. Within individual urban agglomerations, supply patterns often exhibited multi-core structures; for instance, the middle reaches of the Yangtze river, Chengdu-Chongqing, and the Central plains displayed peripheral enhancements of carbon sinks, whereas the Pearl river delta and Yangtze river delta (YRD) featured multiple concentrated cores, shaped by topography, land use, and development stage.

Carbon sequestration demand, quantified based on population density and per-capita emissions, was primarily concentrated in economically advanced and densely populated urban cores, notably BTH, the YRD, and the Pearl river delta (Figure 2). Total demand increased substantially from 558,765 gC·m−2·a−1 in 2000 to 1,015,792 gC·m−2·a−1 in 2020, reflecting rapid urbanization and industrial restructuring. Within agglomerations, hotspots corresponded to urban centers and industrial parks, forming multi-core patterns similar to the supply distribution.

Spatio-temporal pattern of demand for CSSs in China’s urban agglomerations in 2000, 2010, and 2020

Figure 3 compares average carbon sequestration per unit area with total carbon sequestration across urban agglomerations. For example, the Chengdu-Chongqing urban agglomeration increased average supply from approximately 940 gC·m−2·a−1 in 2000 to 1,030 gC·m−2·a−1 in 2020, while demand rose from 31,900 gC·m−2·a−1 to 327,400 gC·m−2·a−1. In contrast, BTH maintained supply below 1,000 gC·m−2·a−1, whereas demand reached 991,910 gC·m−2·a−1 in 2020, reflecting a substantial deficit. These quantitative observations provide a detailed view of spatiotemporal supply-demand contrasts and lay the foundation for subsequent analyses of mismatches and coordination patterns.

Supply and demand index and spatial matching model of carbon sequestration services in urban agglomerations

To evaluate regional supply-demand coordination, we calculated the Supply-Demand Index (SDI) and applied bivariate local spatial autocorrelation (LISA) across the 19 urban agglomerations. The results reveal distinct spatial contrasts in coordination patterns (Figure 4), which can be categorized into three types: supply exceeds demand, supply-demand balance, and demand exceeds supply.

Quadrant diagram of supply-demand matching relationship in urban agglomerations

Specifically, ten agglomerations (52.6%) exhibited a supply surplus in 2020, including Chengdu-Chongqing, Hohhot-Baotou–Erdos-Yulin, and the middle reaches of the Yangtze river, with supply exceeding demand by approximately twenty to 35%. Five agglomerations (26.3%), such as the central plains and central Liaoning, were relatively balanced, with SDI values close to one. Four agglomerations (21.1%), including BTH and the Shandong peninsula, showed a supply deficit, with demand surpassing supply by approximately fifteen to 30%.

Temporal trends from 2000 to 2020 indicate that overall supply-demand coordination remained relatively stable, although some regions experienced intensified local imbalances. These patterns underscore structural disparities and highlight the need for targeted spatial planning and interregional ecological coordination to optimize CSS flows.

The figure focuses on overall coordination trends rather than individual supply or demand values, providing a concise overview of spatial mismatches and surpluses over the study period (2000, 2010, and 2020).

Spatial flow network of carbon sequestration services based on circuit theory

The constructed ecological resistance surface revealed a spatial pattern with lower resistance in central and western urban agglomerations and higher resistance in the eastern regions (Figure 6), forming a basis for simulating carbon service flow pathways. In the resulting spatial supply-demand network (Figure 7), supply sources were concentrated in ecologically robust areas with high forest coverage, including Chengdu-Chongqing, the middle reaches of the Yangtze river, and the Beibu Gulf, as well as in hilly and mountainous zones. Demand sinks were predominantly located in highly urbanized cores and industrial clusters, such as the YRD, Pearl river delta, and BTH, reflecting concentrated carbon emissions. This pattern illustrates a clear structure of “ecological supply encircling urban demand”, with substantial spatial separation and complex pathways between sources and sinks, highlighting significant pressures on ecological resource allocation in urban agglomerations.

CSS supply and demand sources, and ecological corridors for CSS supply in China’s urban agglomerations in 2000, 2010, and 2020

Using circuit-theory modeling of current density, pixels exceeding a defined threshold were extracted to delineate ecological corridors, forming the spatial flow network for CSSs (Figure 7). Most high-intensity corridors exhibited striped or radial patterns, generally aligned with existing ecological infrastructure such as mountain systems, forest lands, and water bodies. Urban agglomerations such as Chengdu-Chongqing, the central plains, and the middle reaches of the Yangtze river displayed dense and well-connected corridor networks, whereas regions like BTH and the Shandong peninsula showed fragmented and discontinuous corridors, indicating carbon flow breakpoints caused by ecological fragmentation. Quantitative analysis shows that the average current values along the corridor in highly connected regions exceed 400, while the current density in fragmented networks is significantly lower, reflecting weaker transmission capabilities (Figures 7, 8, and 9).

CSS flow network and pinch points in China’s urban agglomerations in 2000, 2010, and 2020

CSS flow network and barrier points of CSSs in China urban agglomerations in 2000, 2010, and 2020

Further analysis identified pinch points and barriers, providing insights into critical network structures. Pinch points, characterized by the highest current densities, were primarily located at ecologically strategic intersections, such as the middle reaches of the Yangtze river-Chengdu–Chongqing junction and the Taihang-Yanshan transition zone in BTH (Figure 8). These areas are crucial for maintaining overall CSS network connectivity and ensuring the robustness of carbon flow. Barrier points were concentrated in rapidly urbanizing zones, including the Shandong peninsula and the hilly margins of the Pearl river delta, where ecological connectivity is poor and flow paths are disrupted (Figure 9). These barriers highlight regions where targeted ecological restoration or green infrastructure interventions could enhance connectivity and network resilience.

Overall, the spatial flow network reveals both structural vulnerabilities and critical connectivity nodes, providing a scientific basis for prioritizing ecological protection and optimizing corridor planning. Management efforts should focus on maintaining connectivity and ecological integrity in pinch point areas while mitigating barriers to improve the efficiency and resilience of CSS delivery.

CSS flow patterns across intra- and extra-regional scales

Between 2000 and 2020, the spatial transmission patterns of CSSs in China’s urban agglomerations exhibited notable evolution, reflecting concurrent trends of urbanization and ecological restoration. In 2000, urban agglomerations such as BTH and the Yangtze river Delta (YRD) were characterized by high carbon emissions and insufficient ecological supply, relying heavily on external ecosystems. CSS flows in these regions were distributed radially across dense external corridors. By 2010, implementation of national ecological programs, including the Grain-for-Green initiative and the Three-North Shelterbelt Project, enhanced vegetation cover and carbon sink capacity. As a result, BTH developed a primary corridor-secondary branch network, while YRD reduced reliance on southern Anhui’s ecosystems through the construction of forested systems in northern Zhejiang and Suzhou-Xi districts (Figure S2). By 2020, both regions showed a transition toward internally self-sufficient, self-circulation systems, with strengthened intra-regional carbon sinks and reduced external dependence. In contrast, ecologically robust areas with low population density, such as Chengdu-Chongqing, Hohhot-Baotou-Erdos-Yulin, and Ningxia along the Yellow river, maintained service-exporting patterns, functioning as channel-type suppliers within regional ecological corridors. Regions such as the middle reaches of the Yangtze river and Guanzhong plain achieved relatively balanced internal supply-demand patterns, supported by regional coordination policies and ecological engineering (Figures 12 and 13). These patterns reveal both spatial imbalances and structural vulnerabilities in CSS flows, providing a scientific basis for identifying priority corridors, pinch points, and barriers to guide ecological management and policy interventions.

Spatiotemporal evolution and key mechanisms of the ecological network of supply and demand for CSSs in typical urban agglomerations

Recently, the rapid development of urban agglomerations has rendered the construction and evolution of ecological networks for CSSs an essential component of regional ecosystem function research. This study focused on three representative urban agglomerations (BTH, YRD, and Pearl River Delta [PRD] and analyzed the dynamic characteristics and potential mechanisms of CSS supply-demand relationships through the construction of ecological networks based on supply-demand differentials and the examination of spatiotemporal variation in key topological indicators (Appendix C).

Overall, the supply-demand difference across the three urban agglomerations was significantly positively correlated with the out-degree of nodes in the network. This suggested that nodes with higher out-degree—i.e., those that exported more service flows—tended to have greater supply surpluses and played critical roles as providers of CSSs (Figure 10). This pattern highlighted the pivotal importance of supply-side ecosystem service functions and the strategic significance of these output-oriented nodes within the ecological network.

The flow pattern of CSSs within and outside regions in China’s urban agglomerations in 2000, 2010, and 2020

In contrast, a negative correlation between in-degree and the supply-demand difference reflected the concentration of demand nodes in areas where carbon sequestration capacity was relatively insufficient. This imbalance pointed to pronounced spatial heterogeneity in ecological network structure, particularly in the BTH region, where CSS pressure was most intense. These findings underscored the urgent need for optimized corridor planning and more balanced node allocation in such regions.

Additionally, the positive correlation between betweenness centrality and supply-demand difference in all three urban agglomerations further emphasized the critical role of hub nodes in facilitating service flow. These nodes acted as conduits between surplus and deficit zones, ensuring smooth transmission of CSSs across the landscape. Conversely, the relatively weak correlation between closeness centrality and supply-demand difference may have indicated that improvements in overall network connectivity had not yet translated into balanced supply-demand relationships. This was particularly evident in the PRD and BTH regions, suggesting that further efforts were needed to enhance network connectivity and improve systemic ecological function.

From a temporal perspective, the ecological networks of all three urban agglomerations demonstrated progressive optimization in structure and improved supply efficiency from 2000 to 2020. The increasing positive correlation between out-degree and supply-demand difference indicated a growing functional dominance of service-exporting nodes. Simultaneously, the weakening negative correlation between in-degree and supply-demand difference implied that pressure on high-demand nodes had gradually been alleviated. These trends reflected the positive impacts of ecological corridor construction and conservation policies, which had improved both the effectiveness and stability of regional ecological networks.

To further illuminate the spatial transmission paths and nodal characteristics of CSSs, a visualized flow network analysis was conducted. A directed, weighted network was constructed, with nodes representing supply and demand centers and edges denoting carbon service flow paths. The Fruchterman-Reingold algorithm was used to display the network structure (Figure 11), facilitating intuitive interpretation of spatial linkages and inter-nodal relationships.

Quadrant diagram of the relationship between input and output of CSSs in urban agglomerations

The network exhibited a clear core-periphery structure, with multiple peripheral surplus nodes that radiated service flows to deficit-heavy core cities, particularly in the BTH and YRD regions. This visual pattern affirmed the earlier correlation analysis and provided spatial expression to the degree-difference relationships. Moreover, some nodes acted as transit hubs, that connected multiple supply and demand sites and formed crucial service pathways. These nodes were typically characterized by high betweenness centrality and underscored their pivotal role in maintaining service transmission. In the PRD region, the network displayed compact subnetworks with short-distance, high-efficiency connections, which indicated close spatial proximity between supply and demand. However, this also suggested a strong dependence of high-demand zones on adjacent ecological areas, posing risks to service continuity under external disturbances.

This visual network analysis enhanced understanding of the spatial dynamics of CSS provision, offering practical insights for interregional ecological collaboration, corridor optimization, and targeted service regulation strategies. Future research could incorporate multi-period time series networks to track the evolution of ecological network structures and provide dynamic support for the spatial management of CSSs.

Discussion

Supply-demand imbalance and spatial flow structure of carbon sequestration services

This study reveals that CSSs within China’s urban agglomerations are characterized by persistent and intensifying supply-demand mismatches, accompanied by a distinct spatial flow structure in which ecological supply areas surround high-emission urban demand cores. From 2000 to 2020, overall carbon sequestration capacity increased, yet pronounced spatial disparities remained. Central and western urban agglomerations acted as major service exporters due to abundant ecological land and forest coverage, whereas eastern coastal agglomerations exhibited strong external dependence, reflecting rapid urban expansion, industrial concentration, and high population densities.

Circuit-theory-based simulations indicate that CSS transmission is organized through a limited number of high-intensity corridors and critical nodes, forming a network that is particularly vulnerable to ecological fragmentation in highly urbanized regions. The spatial configuration highlights not only the structural imbalance between carbon supply and demand but also the embedded interregional dependencies and coupling mechanisms, including supply spillover from ecologically rich regions to high-demand urban cores. These patterns are shaped by both ecological endowments and socio-economic drivers, such as industrial structure transformation, urbanization gradients, and regional development policies.

Applicability and scientificity of circuit theory and the construction of spatial flow network for carbon sequestration services

In constructing spatial flow networks of CSSs at the urban agglomeration scale, circuit theory has demonstrated notable scientific merit and practical applicability due to its strong capability in simulating dynamic diffusion processes in ecosystems.12 This theory conceptualizes the transmission of ecological services as analogous to electric current diffusing across a resistive surface, enabling the representation of multi-directional, probabilistic service flows in heterogeneous landscapes.14 Unlike traditional models that focus on a single optimal path (e.g., least-cost paths), circuit theory captures the complexity and uncertainty of service diffusion inherent to carbon sequestration, which is governed by spatial ecological processes.

Particularly in China’s urban agglomerations, characterized by multi-nuclei spatial organization and pronounced functional gradients, circuit theory offers unique advantages. It transcends administrative boundaries and ecological units, enabling the precise identification of supply-demand nodes and interconnecting corridors within the context of complex land-use structures.15 The generation of current density maps and ecological corridor networks further allows for the construction of directed ecological flow networks. These structures reveal not only intra-regional carbon sink dynamics but also input-output relationships between urban agglomerations and surrounding ecosystems, providing critical insights into regional ecological dependence and service spillover.

Previous studies have demonstrated the versatility of circuit theory in modeling ecological services such as carbon and hydrological flows, forming a solid methodological foundation for its application in this study.14 By integrating circuit theory with supply-demand coupling models and ecological network analysis, this research provides a comprehensive representation of carbon sequestration processes from supply, through spatial transmission, to demand realization. This enhances both the spatial resolution and the systematic understanding of urban carbon neutrality pathways. Therefore, circuit theory proves not only theoretically congruent with the spatial dynamics of CSS but also practically robust in modeling complex urban ecological networks.

Limitations of the study

Despite the integrative framework developed in this study, several limitations merit further consideration. First, carbon demand was estimated primarily using population density and per-capita carbon emissions. While this approach is widely employed in urban agglomeration studies due to the strong correlation between population and aggregate emissions, it may oversimplify complex emission patterns and not fully capture sector-specific contributions from industry, transportation, or energy use. Incorporating high-resolution emission inventories and sectoral data could enhance the accuracy and explanatory power of CSS demand estimation.16 Second, circuit theory provides an effective means to simulate diffusion pathways; however, it does not explicitly encode directional flows, particularly in capturing supply-to-demand relationships across intra- and interregional scales. The integration of gravity-based or niche-based models could improve the representation of flow directionality and interregional linkages.12 Moreover, the framework assumes functional homogeneity across all ecosystem types, which may oversimplify ecological variability. Differentiating the contributions of forests, grasslands, wetlands, and croplands could offer a more realistic depiction of carbon dynamics. Moreover, dynamic scenario simulations—such as land-use change projections and ecological resilience modeling—would allow assessment of network adaptability under various policy or climate scenarios.17 Finally, although this study highlights spatial structure and network properties, institutional and governance factors remain underexplored. Integrating ecological compensation schemes, cross-regional coordination mechanisms, and policy instruments could strengthen the practical relevance of spatial flow networks for coordinated carbon management across urban agglomerations.18

Overall, this study constructed a spatial ecological network of CSSs across 19 Chinese urban agglomerations using circuit theory, systematically analyzing spatiotemporal patterns and flow mechanisms from 2000 to 2020. Overall, carbon sequestration supply increased, with higher capacity in the west and south and lower capacity in the east and north, while demand remained concentrated in high-emission urban cores. Supply-demand patterns were classified as supply-dominated, demand-dominated, or multi-center mismatch, revealing structural imbalances such as the “service highland-ecological depression” phenomenon. Circuit-theory-based simulations demonstrated a service flow network in which ecological supply surrounds urban demand cores, with strong connectivity in central and western regions and fragmented flows in eastern areas, confirming the method’s scientific validity and operational feasibility. Importantly, these findings provide actionable, region-specific guidance for carbon management: supply-dominated agglomerations should focus on ecological protection and enhancement of carbon sinks to sustain service capacity; demand-dominated regions require targeted low-carbon development policies and industrial optimization to reduce emissions; and multi-center mismatch areas would benefit from cross-regional ecological compensation mechanisms, coordinated management of CSS flows, and strategic allocation of ecological infrastructure. The study offers a streamlined framework linking spatial analysis, ecological network modeling, and differentiated policy recommendations, thereby supporting sustainable carbon management, cross-regional coordination, and regional ecological governance.

Method details

Study design

This study quantifies carbon sequestration service (CSS) supply-demand dynamics and spatial flow patterns across 19 urban agglomerations in China from 2000 to 2020 (Figure S1). The analytical framework integrates ecosystem modeling, spatial statistical analysis, and circuit theory-based network modeling to construct a CSS supply-demand–flow system.

SDI > 0 indicates surplus, SDI = 0 indicates balance, and SDI < 0 indicates deficit. Spatial clustering of supply-demand patterns was analyzed using bivariate LISA.

Source area identification

LISA results were converted into raster format and processed using Morphological Spatial Pattern Analysis (MSPA). High supply-low demand (H–L) clusters were defined as supply sources, while low supply-high demand (L–H) clusters were defined as demand sources12,21 (Appendix B.3).

Ecological resistance surface

A composite resistance surface was constructed using NDVI, land use, slope, elevation, population density, and GDP. Each factor was classified using natural breaks and weighted using Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) with CR < 0.10. A weighted overlay approach was used to generate the final resistance surface (Table S1).13,22,23,24,25,26,27

Ecological corridors and critical nodes

Ecological corridors were identified using Linkage Mapper within the Circuitscape framework based on least-cost paths. A 20 km cost-distance threshold was applied to constrain corridor extraction.

Pinch points were identified using current density analysis (Pinchpoint Mapper, all-to-one mode, 5 km search radius). Barrier points were identified using Barrier Mapper to detect high-resistance zones caused by land-use and anthropogenic disturbance.28,29,30
