ABSTRACT
Evasion of apoptosis is a hallmark of cancer. Deregulation of BCL-XL, a member of the BCL-2 family of proteins, has been linked to the development of various tumor types. This study presents the design and synthesis of BCL-XL inhibitors with novel mono- and bicyclic cores. The new structural features were optimized to combine high binding efficiency with the opening of diverse novel vectors for additional modifications. The lead compounds exhibited picomolar affinities and significant cellular potency in the BCL-XL-dependent MOLT-4 cell line, which also translated into marked tumor growth inhibition in a xenograft study. These findings highlight the potential of BCL-XL inhibitors as therapeutic agents in cancer treatment by targeting the apoptotic intrinsic pathway.

Introduction

Apoptosis is a cell death pathway that plays an important role in development, tissue homeostasis, and removal of damaged cells. Deregulation of apoptosis contributes to different types of human diseases, including cancer. The intrinsic apoptotic cell death pathway is regulated by the BCL-2 family of proteins which consists of three classes of proteins that contain between one and four of the mainly helical BCL-2 homology domains (BH1-BH4). Upon activation, the pro-apoptotic proteins such as BAX and BAK containing BH domains 1–3 form mitochondrial pores, which trigger the release of caspases and cell death. The pro-survival members such as BCL-2, BCL-XL, and MCL-1 contain BH domains 1–4. The third class of proteins such as BIM and PUMA are pro-apoptotic containing just one BH domain (BH3) and play a role in activating BAX and BAK. Cell fate is dependent on the extent of pairing between pro-survival and pro-apoptotic family members in which the BCL-2 homology 3 (BH3) domain of a pro-apoptotic protein binds in a groove on the surface of a pro-survival protein. Overexpression of the pro-survival members of the BCL-2 family is a hallmark of cancer, playing an important role in tumor development, maintenance, and resistance to anticancer therapies. The gene coding for BCL-XL (BCL2L1 for Bcl-2-like protein 1) is frequently amplified in cancer and its expression inversely correlates with sensitivity to several anticancer therapies. Small molecules that mimic the BH3 peptide have been designed to bind to the pro-survival proteins of the BCL-2 family, releasing pro-apoptotic proteins that can then activate and trigger apoptosis.

WEHI-539 (Figure a) was one of the first selective BCL-XL inhibitors, with high affinity and selectivity for BCL-XL, killing cells by antagonizing its pro-survival activity. , The benzothiazole moiety of WEHI-539 binds deep into the mainly hydrophobic P2 pocket (Figure b) of the protein, making a hydrogen-bonding interaction with the backbone NH of L108 (shown in Figure c). The NH group of the hydrazone moiety interacts with the backbone carbonyl of S106 in the P2 pocket and links the benzothiazole and tetralin rings. This arrangement positions a thiazole carboxylic acid that makes polar interactions with the guanidinium side chain of R139 and the side chain of N136. The remainder of the compound, connected through a flexible chain to the thiazole ring, makes nonpolar interactions with BCL-XL. Further development led to A-1155463 and A-1331852 containing the same benzothiazole but linked via an amide moiety to the bicyclic tetrahydroisoquinoline (THIQ) core. All three of these literature compounds (WEHI-539, A-1155463, and A-1331852) bind to the P2–P4 pocket of BCL-XL in a similar fashion. Although the tertiary nitrogen of A-1155463 does not make any direct polar interactions with the protein, the increased solubility gives a potent and selective BCL-XL inhibitor that demonstrated activity in xenograft models of human tumors.

The central THIQ and tetralin cores of these three inhibitors occupy a defined cleft in the protein but make only weak hydrophobic interactions. Their principal role is to position their substituents for optimal interactions with the P2 and P4 pockets. Both the tetralin and THIQ cores are known to be sensitive to oxidation, and the exploration of the additional vectors that they offer for further derivatization is synthetically challenging. We therefore decided to replace these cores to facilitate the introduction of new vectors while conserving the orientation of the P2 and P4 filling groups to maintain affinity and selectivity. These new cores were then optimized to generate a lead series of very potent and selective BCL-XL inhibitors that induced tumor regression in an animal model and provided new attachment points for further development.

Results and Discussion

The strategy to design core replacements is outlined in Figure . The position and orientation of the benzothiazole and linker of WEHI-539 in the BCL-XL structure are conserved in the published crystal structures of A-1155463 and A-1331852 with different linkers. We synthesized 1 and 2 which represent the benzothiazole-linked cores of WEHI-539 and A-1155463, respectively, and determined their crystal structures bound to BCL-XL. An overlay of these structures with those of A-1155463 and A-1331852 (Figure a) confirmed that this side of the literature inhibitors is firmly anchored in pocket P2 but also suggested designs for replacements for the tetralin and THIQ cores (Figure b). A new cycle incorporating the linker would replace one of the core rings and connect key atoms. While one side of the newly formed ring would be close to the BCL-XL protein, the other side of the ring or the “freed” nitrogen could provide different new vectors for further development. To test our core replacement hypotheses, we first generated some tool compounds with a diaminoaryl-core and compared them to the corresponding cores of the literature compounds (Table ). The first core replacements, such as the tetrahydronaphthalene in 3 resulted in a significant drop of affinity for binding to BCL-XL compared to 1 or 2. However, the introduction of two nitrogen atoms such as in the phthalazine 4, recovered most of the affinity. Partial saturation of the core (5) further increased the affinity, in part probably due to decreased rigidity, bringing it to the same affinity range as the cores of the literature compounds. Elimination of the second ring of the core (6) led to maintained affinity, improved ligand efficiency as well as compound properties. The position of the nitrogens in the core was found to be important since the pyrazine derivatives (7 and 8) had greatly reduced affinity for BCL-XL, as did the naphthalene analog 3. At this point, our inhibitor 6 had similar affinity and ligand efficiency (0.39) for BCL-XL as the WEHI-539 core 1, binding in the same position and orientation in BCL-XL (Figure a), with some small variations in the vectors from the thiazole (Figure b). There is a lone pair interaction of the pyridazine nitrogens with the vacant sulfur d-orbitals of the thiazole and benzothiazole moieties. This preorientates the substituents in the conformation necessary for efficient binding although the nitrogens make no polar interactions with the protein. The significant decrease in binding affinity observed for compounds 7 and 8 can be attributed to the pyrazine ring’s nitrogen atoms, which stabilize the thiazole (for 7) and benzothiazole moieties (for 8) in an inactive conformation.

The next step was to investigate the optimization of the substitution pattern of the aminopyridazine moiety (hereafter Core A) at three positions (Table ). Replacement of the R1 methyl group with a slightly bigger cyclopropyl moiety (9) was tolerated, while the longer and more polar methoxymethyl group (10) led to a 4-fold reduction in affinity. The same methyl-to-methoxymethyl replacement in the R2 position (11) or even the introduction of a much larger N-methylpiperazinepropyl group into the same position (12) was tolerated without any change of affinity, which is in line with the expectation that these substituents point toward the solvent-exposed area. The importance of the methyl group in the R1 position is highlighted by the fact that its removal led to a 20–30-fold drop of BCL-XL affinity irrespective if R2 was also replaced by a hydrogen (13) or not (14). In line with this observation, elimination of the methyl group in the R2 position (15) is well tolerated. Interestingly, trimethyl substitution (R,1 R,2 R3 = Me, 16) leads to a significant 200-fold drop in affinity compared to the parent dimethylpyridazine analog (6) probably due to the steric clash of R2 and R3 forcing a suboptimal conformation of the P4-filling side chain. When the two methyl groups were in positions R1 and R3, and R2 was hydrogen, compound 17 had much improved 7.5 nM affinity with 0.41 ligand efficiency. Larger substituents in the R3 position on the central amino group, such as a propyl (18) or 3,4-dihydroxybutyl (19) were well tolerated, with 19 displacing waters in a pocket to make additional polar interactions with R146 side chain amidine, D140 main chain (mc) NH, L137 main chain O and D140 side chain carboxylic acid (as shown in Figure S2).

Compounds with a bicyclic core (hereafter, Core B), formed by connecting the central amine substituent with the proximal position of the pyridazine ring, were also investigated. It was found that the pyrido­[2,3-c]­pyridazine (21) having a [6 + 6] bicyclic core had high affinity (2 nM) with 0.41 ligand efficiency, while in the case of the [5 + 6] (20, 9 nM) and [7 + 6] (22, 115 nM) derivatives lower affinities were observed. Modeling suggested that the piperidine ring might offer a good vector for further functionalization. The 6-(R)-hydroxy substitution of the pyrido­[2,3-c]­pyridazine core (23) was very well tolerated, maintaining BCL-XL affinity. This was confirmed by a more sensitive BCL-XL Quench assay (5.5 and 1.4 nM, respectively), which was used for all high-affinity compounds later. A comparison of the crystal structures of various of the compounds in the two series (Figure ) confirms that the binding mode of the identical left-hand side of the molecules is conserved, but that variations in the central moiety of the core provide opportunities for subtly different vectors for further derivatization from the thiazole carboxylic acid. In addition, one other vector for optimization was demonstrated at the R3 position (Table ) where longer substituents such as the 3,4-dihydroxybutyl chain in 19 can form additional interaction(s) with a polar pocket (see Figure S2c for details).

In conclusion, we have successfully identified and characterized two series of BCL-XL ligands featuring mono- or bicyclic cores. These scaffolds exhibit nanomolar affinity for BCL-XL and demonstrate enhanced ligand efficiency relative to the core structures of the parent molecules 1 and 2. Furthermore, these series provide novel exit vectors suitable for further medicinal chemistry optimization and development. By utilizing our new scaffolds, we have successfully eliminated both the sensitive THIQ core and the amide linkage, thereby effectively addressing their associated medicinal chemistry liabilities.

The combination of the phenoxypropargylamine side chain (hereafter side chain a) with the pyridazine (24) and the pyrido­[2,3-c]­pyridazine (25) cores led to single-digit picomolar BCL-XL affinities and nanomolar cell killing in the BCL-XL-dependent MOLT-4 cell line. Substitution of the central nitrogen of the pyridazine core with a 4-hydroxybutyl group (26) was well tolerated, resulting in a slight elevation of the affinity and maintaining cellular activity. Introduction of the more polar and charged 2-(methylamino)­ethoxy moiety into the 6-position of the pyrido­[2,3-c]­pyridazin core (27) had no impact on its affinity, while the cellular activity decreased by an order of magnitude, probably as a consequence of decreased cell penetration due to its increased polarity compared to 25.

The carboxylic acid moiety is carried by a pyridine rather than a thiazole in A-1331852 in the adamantly pyrazole side chain (hereafter side chain b). This was combined with our pyridazine (28) and pyrido­[2,3-c]­pyridazine (29) cores. These modifications gave a significant boost to the BCL-XL affinity and cellular potency, similar to side chain a. Also, as reported by others previously, inhibitors containing side chain b had significantly increased PBS solubility at pH 7.4 compared to their analogs with side chain a (23 and 22 μM for 28 and 29, respectively, compared to <1 μM for inhibitors with side chain a).

Introduction of a solubilizing moiety onto the adamantane ring gave side chain c, which when combined with the pyridazine core (30) resulted in high affinity and outstanding cellular activity. 4-Methylaminobutyl group on the central nitrogen (31) was added as a further polar group, which further elevated the BCL-XL affinity, while the cellular activity was similar to 28. Not surprisingly, we observed elevated solubility (88 μM and 66 μM, respectively) due to the presence of the polar amine moieties. In all cases shown, the incorporation of the P4-filling side chains significantly increased the binding affinity to the picomolar level and improved cellular potency, sometimes below the nanomolar level, similar to A-1155463 and A-1331852 (Table ).

On the basis of the data available at the time, compounds 24 and 29 were selected as leads. Both compounds exhibited exceptional selectivity for BCL-XL over BCL-W, -MCL-1, and -BCL-2 (Table ). These findings are consistent with the potent activity observed in the BCL-XL-dependent MOLT-4 cell line and the corresponding lack of activity in the BCL-2-dependent RS4;11 cell line. This selectivity profile mirrors those of A-1155463 and A-1331852, while remaining distinct from the BCL-2-selective profile of venetoclax. The mode of action of these compounds (24, 29) was further supported by the dose-dependent caspase-3 induction and PARP cleavage in the MOLT-4 cell line in vitro following a 4 h treatment (Figure S4). As anticipated, the effect was completely ablated by the addition of caspase inhibitor Q-VD. These data confirm that these compounds induce cell death in the MOLT4 cell line through apoptosis as expected.

The pharmacokinetics (PK) in mice were measured following low-dose intravenous (IV) and oral (PO) administration (Table ). After IV administration, 24 showed a moderate total plasma clearance, an intermediate terminal half-life, and a 4-fold lower drug exposure, whereas 29 showed a low total plasma clearance and a longer terminal half-life. On oral administration, 24 showed very limited exposure (C max <0.01 μM) and bioavailability (nc), while 29 showed good exposure and oral bioavailability (F, 53%) with a C max of 1 μM, suitable for pharmacodynamic (PD) and efficacy studies.

To evaluate the pharmacodynamic response, mice bearing MOLT-4 tumor xenografts were administered a single 7.5 mg/kg dose of compound 29 via either IV or oral routes. Tumor samples were harvested and analyzed 6 h postadministration (Table ). Regardless of the administration route, the compound elicited a robust induction of apoptotic markers, with cleaved PARP increasing 24.1–38.6-fold and cleaved caspase 3 increasing 7.2–18.9-fold relative to untreated controls. Furthermore, consistent with the established role in regulating platelet lifespan, ,, both treatment modalities resulted in a significant, transient reduction in platelet counts to 1% of control values, followed by full recovery.

Given the robust pharmacodynamic response observed following both IV and PO administration, the therapeutic efficacy of compound 29 was also evaluated. Mice bearing MOLT-4 xenograft tumors were administered varying IV doses of compound 29 (2.5, 5, and 7.5 mg/kg) or A-1331852 (7.5 mg/kg) on a Q3D6 schedule, with tumor volumes monitored at least twice weekly (Figure and S5). Treatment with compound 29 elicited significant tumor regression across all dose levels, which was sustained throughout the dosing period. While tumors in the lower dose cohorts (2.5 and 5 mg/kg) relapsed more rapidly, the 7.5 mg/kg group exhibited a prolonged response. Notably, no clinically significant body weight loss was observed, indicating a favorable safety profile under these conditions (Figure ). While A-1331852 at 7.5 mg/kg achieved tumor regression comparable to the 2.5 mg/kg dose of compound 29, only compound 29 at the 7.5 mg/kg dose induced complete regression (CR, defined as tumor volume <25 mm3 for at least three consecutive measurements; Figure S6). On day 17 post-treatment initiation, compound 29 exhibited tumor growth inhibition (TGI) values of 122%, 124%, and 133% at dosages of 2.5, 5, and 7.5 mg/kg, respectively (Figure , Table ). In comparison, A-1331852 resulted in a TGI of 115% at a 7.5 mg/kg dose, which was significantly lower than the inhibition achieved by compound 29 at all tested dose levels.

For the synthesis of 24 (Scheme ), a SEM-protected chloromethylpyridazinylbenzothiazol-imine intermediate (33) was prepared from commercially available 3-amino-6-chloro-4-methylpyridazine (32) and 2-chloro-1,3-benzothiazole in two steps by a Buchwald coupling and subsequent SEM protection. This intermediate was coupled with the aminothiazole ester 35 under Buchwald conditions, affording 36. In both Buchwald couplings the use of a two-base (Cs2CO3, DIPEA) XantPhos system proved to be the most efficient. After activation of the haloalkyl side chain through halogen exchange, the O-alkylation of the phenoxypropargyl reagent (39), prepared by the Sonogashira coupling of the commercially available N,N-dimethyl-propargyl amine and 2-fluoro-4-iodophenol, led to the desired 24 inhibitor after removal of the protecting groups.

For the synthesis of 29 (Scheme ), the core was built up from the pyridyl side, and the 2-aminobenzothiazole moiety was introduced at the end. O-Benzyl-hex-4-yn-1-ol (40) was reacted with 3,6-dichloro-1,2,4,5-tetrazine in an inverse-electron-demand Diels–Alder reaction. The obtained 41 pyridazine was debenzylated and Swern-oxidized to the corresponding 43 aldehyde. The reductive amination of 43 pyridazinylpropanal with the 3-substituted ethyl 6-aminopyridine-2-carboxylate (46), which was synthesized from the commercially available ethyl 3-bromo-6-aminopyridine-2-carboxylate (44) and boronic acid 45 in a Suzuki coupling, led to key intermediate 47. Intramolecular nucleophilic substitution between the chloropyridazine and aminopyridine moieties of 47 in a sealed tube led to the formation of the bicyclic core (48). In the concluding steps, the 2-aminobenzothiazole moiety was introduced by a Buchwald coupling and hydrolysis of the ester moiety gave the desired 29 inhibitor.

Conclusions

We report the discovery of novel, very potent inhibitors of BCL-XL, which exhibit in vivo efficacy in tumor xenografts by displacing pro-apoptotic BH3-containing proteins. The inhibitors conserve some of the interactions made by previously reported inhibitors, − with the main modification being new central cores that feature a 1,4-arylenediamine motif in a monocyclic or bicyclic layout. This feature ensures the proper distance and orientation of the benzothiazole moiety in the deep-P2 pocket of BCL-XL and the aromatic carboxylic acid moiety on the phenylalanine-bridge. While the affinity for binding to BCL-XL of the initial compounds was low, incorporation of two nitrogens into the aryl ring and extensive optimization of the substituents ensured the optimal orientation of the two main binding moieties, which led to single-digit nanomolar affinities. Incorporation of different P4-filling side chains from the literature into our binders not only enhanced their affinities to the single-digit nanomolar range but also led to high cellular potency in the BCL-XL-dependent MOLT-4 cell line. The lead compounds (24 and 29) display very high selectivity for BCL-XL over other BCL-2 family pro-survival proteins. Furthermore, the in vivo activity of compound 29 was demonstrated following both intravenous and oral administration. Treatment resulted in significant tumor regression and the induction of apoptosis within tumor cells. A pronounced reduction in circulating platelets was also observed; however, platelet counts recovered following treatment discontinuation. In a comparative in vivo study against the established BCL-XL inhibitor A-1331852, compound 29 exhibited superior efficacy with statistical significance. While A-1331852 has previously shown efficacy across various xenograft models, the clinical development of potent and selective BCL-XL inhibitors was never initiated due to the emergence of cardiotoxicity in higher species. Although the aforementioned cardiotoxicity had not been identified at the time of this studyrendering its mitigation outside the current scopethe profound thrombocytopenia observed during treatment ultimately led to the termination of our small-molecule inhibitor program. A potential advantage of the newly developed inhibitors, including 26, 27, and 31, is their feature of offering novel exit vectors. These attachment points make them suitable for further derivatization, which may be strategically exploited for the development of novel conjugates, such as Antibody-Drug Conjugates (ADCs), bivalent degraders (i.e., PROTACs), and other targeted modalities.

Protein Production

Proteins were generated from a number of BCL-XL constructs.

For X-ray crystal structure determination, the construct used encoded a mutated loop-deleted chimera of human BCL-2 and BCL-XL: BCL-2 (9–33)–BCL-XL (28–155) [Δ46–85, S28A, M45 V, I114L, Q121G, E124A, Q125T]–BCL-2 (163–206) as introduced in the legend to Figure (the BCL-XL surrogate). A His–TEV-tagged construct of this was generated by gene synthesis (DNA2.0, USA) in pJ express411. For protein expression, the construct was used to transform E. coli BL21 (DE3) pLysS cells, which were subsequently cultured in LB supplemented with 50 μg/mL kanamycin. Protein expression was induced at O.D.600 nm = 0.6 by the addition of 1 mM IPTG (final concentration), and the temperature reduced to 4 °C overnight before harvesting by centrifugation. For protein purification, cell pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH8.0, 500 mM NaCl containing EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) and DNase I (Sigma)) and lysed by mechanical homogenization. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation and applied to a 1 mL HisTrap FF column (Cytiva). Protein was eluted using a linear gradient of lysis buffer supplemented with 500 mM imidazole. Fractions containing the expressed protein were pooled and concentrated. The protein was then applied to a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex pg75 column pre-equilibrated in 20 mM Tris-HCl pH7.8, 10 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT. The N-terminal His-tag was removed by incubation overnight at RT, with AcTEV protease (ThermoFisher). A repeat of the affinity purification resulted in BCL-XL surrogate protein in the flowthrough fraction. The protein was concentrated before size exclusion chromatography was performed again in 20 mM Tris-HCl pH7.8, 100 mM NaCl, 1% glycerol, 2 mM DTT. The sample was then reapplied to a 1 mL HisTrap FF column with the buffers used previously. The flowthrough was concentrated then loaded onto a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex pg75 column pre-equilibrated in 20 mM Tris-HCl pH7.7, 100 mM NaCl, 1% glycerol, 2 mM DTT. The resulting protein was concentrated to 13.6 mg/mL, aliquoted, and snap-frozen prior to being stored at −80 °C.

For the fluorescence polarization assay, a construct was made by cloning DNA encoding BCL-XL (2–209) into pGex5X-1 between EcoRI and NotI sites. Protein was expressed as above (except using 100 μg/mL ampicillin instead of kanamycin), and cell pellets resuspended in buffer A (20 mM Tris-HCl pH8, 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, and 2 mM EDTA with EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) and DNase I (Sigma)) before lysis by chemical homogenization. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation and applied to a 5 mL GSTrap FF column (Cytiva). Protein was eluted in buffer A containing 10 mM glutathione, and the eluate was applied to a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex pg75 column pre-equilibrated in buffer A. Eluted protein was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C.

For the quench assay, a plasmid encoding a N-terminal double 6His-tagged BCL-XL (2–197) [N197C] was generated in pET41a. Protein was expressed as described above, and cell pellets were resuspended in buffer B (20 mM HEPES pH7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP with EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) and DNase I (Sigma)) before lysis by chemical homogenization. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation and applied to a 5 mL HisTrap FF crude column (Cytiva). Protein was eluted in buffer B containing 500 mM imidazole, and the eluate was applied to a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex pg75 column pre-equilibrated in 20 mM HEPES pH7.4, 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP. Eluted protein was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C.

MTT MOLT-4 and RS4-11 Cell Viability Assay

The MTT colorimetric assay is based on the mitochondrial reduction of tetrazolium salt by living cells. The viable cell number is proportional to the production of formazan salts, which can be read spectrophotometrically at 540 nm. MOLT-4 cells were purchased from ATCC and cultivated in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, penicillin (100 IU/ml), streptomycin (100 μg/mL), and l-glutamine (2 mM). RS4–11 cells were also purchased from ATCC and cultivated in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, penicillin (100 IU/mL), streptomycin (100 μg/mL), and glucose (4.5 g/L). Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells were seeded in 96 microwell plates (150 μL per well) and exposed to the compounds for 48 h (3,16-fold serially diluted; 9 concentrations each, triplicates). At the end of the incubation time, 15 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) were added per well, and the cells were incubated for another 4 h. Then, 100 μL of 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)/HCl 10 mM were added per well, and the plate was incubated overnight, before measurement of optical density at 540 nm. IC50s were calculated using standard four-parametric curve fitting. IC50 is defined as the compound concentration at which the MTT signal is reduced to 50% of that measured for the control.

Crystallization

X-ray Structure Determination

All software was from the CCP4 package. The structure of the first compoundsurrogate BCL-XL complex was solved by molecular replacement with MolRep using the published structure of BCL-XL complexed with ABT-737 (PDB ID 2YXJ ) stripped of the compound and solvent. From the initial electron density maps, it became apparent that the compound is present in the binding site of BCL-XL. Initial maps allowed the extension of the refinement resolution range and the correct placement of side chains. At this point, sequence differences between the published structure and our surrogate BCL-XL sequence were clearly visible, and appropriate changes were made in the model. For all subsequent structures, the first refined structure of the BCL-XL surrogate (stripped of the compound) was used as a starting model for solving by molecular replacement. After structure solution, several cycles of refinement were conducted with Refmac5, alternating with manual rebuilding using the program COOT. Compounds were modeled in the surplus peaks in difference Fourier electron density maps. Topology files for the ligands were created by ProDrg. Refinement statistics for each new structure reported in this paper are provided in Table S2.

Pharmacokinetic Study

A pharmacokinetic study was performed at 1 mg/kg IV and 3 mg/kg PO in female SCID mice (3 mice/route of administration). Compounds were formulated in HPBCD/HCl (IV bolus, 5 mL/kg) and in PEG300/EtOH/Phosal (30/10/60) (PO gavage, 10 mL/kg). Blood PK samples (3 mice/time points) were collected, by microsampling, over 24 h on each mouse: 0.05, 0.33, 1.0, 2.0, 6.0, and 24 h after IV dosing, and 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 6.0, and 24 h after PO dosing. Plasma samples were analyzed by LC-MS/MS.

Pharmacodynamics and Tumor Regression Study

The in vivo therapeutic and pharmacodynamic effects of BCL-XL-targeting small molecules were determined in the MOLT-4 T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) model upon intravenous (IV) or oral (PO) administration.

Materials and Other Methods

MOLT-4 cells (ATCC No. CRL-1582) were cultured in RPMI supplemented with 10% FBS. Cells were resuspended in 50% matrigel (BD Biosciences) and 0.1 mL containing 5 × 106 cells was subcutaneously inoculated into the right flank of female NOD SCID mice (Charles River). For efficacy studies, when tumors reached the appropriate volume, mice were randomized (7 animals per group) using EasyStat software. Control vehicle (HPBCD/HCl, 50/50), 29 (2.5, 5, or 7.5 mg/kg) or A-1331852 (7.5 mg/kg) were injected IV (twice weekly for 3 weeksQ3D6). Mice body weight was monitored at least twice a week, and tumor size was measured using electronic calipers. Tumor volume (V t) was estimated from the measured minimum tumor diameter (d min) and maximum tumor diameter (d max) using the formula V t = d min 2 d max/2. Individual curves representing tumor volumes of individual mice are shown in Figure S6. At day 17, tumor growth inhibition (TGI) was calculated using the following formula: TGI=[1−mean(ΔVtatdayxintreatedgroup)mean(ΔVtatdayxincontrolgroup)]×100% where ΔV t at day x = (V t at day x) – (V t at randomization).

Mice were sacrificed at the first measurement for which the tumor volume exceeded 2000 mm3 or at the first signs of animal health deterioration.

For pharmacodynamic studies and platelet analysis, when tumors reached the appropriate volume, mice were randomized (3 animals per group) using EasyStat software. Twenty-nine (7.5 mg/kg) was dosed IV (once per dayQD) in HPBCD/HCl or PO (once per dayQD) in PEG300/EtOH/Phosal (30/10/60).

Tumor samples from 3 mice/group were collected 6 h after dosing and lysed (10 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 142.5 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP40, protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktailsCalbiochem). Cleared lysates were prepared for immunodetection of cleaved PARP and Caspase 3 by using the immunoassay MSD (Meso Scale Discovery) apoptosis panel whole-cell lysate kit in 96-well plates, according to manufacturer’s instructions and were analyzed on the QuickPlex SQ 120. The mean of the triplicates was expressed as fold change relative to untreated controls in Table .

For platelet assessment, whole blood samples from 3 mice/group were analyzed on the Hematology Analyzer Coulter Ac•T diff (Beckman Coulter). Results were expressed as platelet count mean of n = 3 mice and percentage of untreated controls in Table .
