Inferring genetic associations of programmed cell death genes with retinitis pigmentosa through multimodal Mendelian randomization
Authors: Nan Guo, Guang-Hua Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRetinitis pigmentosa (RP), a leading cause of inherited blindness, lacks effective treatment, necessitating the elucidation of its pathogenesis and identification of novel therapeutic targets. This study aimed to identify the genetic role of programmed cell death (PCD)-related genes in RP pathogenesis via an integrative multimodal framework.MethodsWe integrated summary-level data from genome-wide association studies (GWAS), expression/protein quantitative trait loci (eQTL/pQTL), and processed transcriptomic datasets. The workflow encompassed causal inference mainly using two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR), with complementary validation by summary-data-based MR (SMR); specificity validation via Bayesian colocalization, multivariable MR (MVMR), phenome-wide association study (PheWAS), and MR examining tissue specificity using GTEx eQTL data; and functional annotation through bulk and single-cell RNA-seq analyses.The study used publicly available genetic and transcriptomic databases. Primary analyses used GWAS summary statistics for RP (ebi-a-GCST90018912, ebi-a-GCST90013904, and ebi-a-GCST90013954). Validation was performed in bulk (GSE62020) and single-cell (GSE183206) retinal transcriptomic datasets.Exposures were genetically instrumented expression (cis-eQTL) or protein abundance (cis-pQTL) of PCD-related genes. Primary outcome was RP. Secondary outcomes included gene expression dysregulation, functional enrichment, immune cell infiltration, and cell-type–specific expression.ResultsIntegrative two-sample and SMR analyses identified eight core PCD-related genes with robust genetic evidence linking to RP (e.g., AIM2, odds ratio = 2.90, 95% confidence interval: 1.81–4.63), which showed consistent effects across multiple tissues and remained nominally significant in MVMR models adjusting for proxy phenotypes of smoking and physical activity (nominal P < 0.05). PheWAS showed no evidence of pleiotropy for these associations (all P > 5 × 10⁻⁸). In retinal transcriptomes, these genes were dysregulated and enriched in immune pathways; CIBERSORT analysis revealed correlated immune cell infiltration, including a strong negative correlation between H13 and M1 macrophages (r = −0.625, P < 0.05). Single‑cell RNA‑seq further pinpointed Müller glia as the most reprogrammed population (area under the curve score: 0.07).ConclusionsThis multimodal study provides genetic evidence linking a core set of PCD-related genes to RP pathogenesis. The findings suggest a mechanistic model wherein dysregulation of these genes may contribute to immune dyshomeostasis and Müller glia dysfunction, emphasizing a network of promising therapeutic targets for this blinding disorder.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s10020-026-01547-9.

Introduction

Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) comprises a heterogeneous group of inherited retinal degenerative disorders, with an estimated global prevalence of approximately 1 in 4,000 (Karuntu et al. 2025; Verbakel et al. 2018). Clinically, RP is characterized by progressive photoreceptor degeneration, leading to night blindness, constriction of the visual field, and in many cases, complete blindness, which severely impairs patients’ quality of life (Xue et al. 2023). Although more than 100 genes have been implicated in RP (Jiang et al. 2025), the molecular mechanisms underlying photoreceptor degeneration remain incompletely understood, thus necessitating further research.

Current therapeutic options for RP are extremely limited. Aside from gene replacement therapies available to a small subset of patients with specific genotypes, few interventions are capable of slowing or halting disease progression (Yang et al. 2024a). Therefore, a deeper understanding of RP pathogenesis, particularly the core cellular processes driving retinal degeneration, is essential for the development of broadly applicable therapeutic strategies.

Programmed cell death (PCD)-encompassing apoptosis, autophagy, and necroptosis—is essential for retinal development and homeostasis, and its dysregulation has been strongly linked to retinal degeneration (Yang et al. 2020; Rosenbaum et al. 2010). Emerging evidence implicates aberrant PCD activation in the pathogenesis of RP (Marigo 2007; Yan et al. 2024; Kang et al. 2012; Weiss et al. 2025). However, prior research has predominantly focused on individual pathways or candidate genes, providing limited insight into the broader landscape of PCD-related mechanisms. Thus, a systematic, genetics-driven assessment of the genetic contributions of PCD-related genes in RP is lacking. Moreover, causal inference in conventional observational studies is constrained by confounding and reverse causation (Nguyen and Mitchell 2024), which may obscure true biological relationships.

Mendelian randomization (MR) addresses these limitations by using genetic variants as instrumental variables, analogous to a randomized controlled trial, thereby minimizing confounding and reverse causality to provide a robust causal inference framework (Sanderson et al. 2022; Lovegrove et al. 2024; Liao et al. 2025; Zhao and Zhang 2024). To systematically elucidate the genetic role of PCD in RP and characterize the functional mechanisms of core genetically associated genes, we applied an integrated multimodal framework integrating genetics, transcriptomics, and single-cell analyses (Fig. 1). First, we used two-sample MR to conduct a large-scale screening for PCD genes with potential genetic associations on RP. These associations were subsequently validated and refined through multitiered analyses to determine core genetically associated genes. Finally, we delineated the functional mechanisms of these genes by integrating retinal transcriptomics, immune microenvironment profiling, and single-cell sequencing, specifically exploring their effects on immune homeostasis and cell type–specific functions. This integrative approach provides comprehensive insights into the molecular etiology of RP and highlights specific candidate targets for future therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Study design

This study used an integrated multimodal framework to investigate the genetic role of PCD-related genes in RP and to characterize their downstream mechanisms. The analysis employed a two-sample MR design. The exposures were defined as the genetically predicted expression levels (using cis-expression quantitative trait loci (cis-eQTL) data) or protein abundance (using cis-protein quantitative trait loci (cis-pQTL) data) of each candidate gene. The outcome was the disease phenotype of RP, obtained from RP genome-wide association studies (GWAS). In this analytical workflow, candidate genes related to retinal function were first screened based on literature and functional annotation. Then, strictly adhering to the three core assumptions of MR, genetic variants in the cis-region of these genes were extracted as instrumental variables (IVs) to select eligible single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Finally, SNP-based genetic instruments were constructed to evaluate the causal effect of gene expression or protein levels on RP. The overall analysis comprised the following three principal phases: (1) primary causal inference using two-sample MR with genetic instruments derived from eQTL/pQTL data; (2) multitiered sensitivity and validation analyses, including summary-data-based MR (SMR), Bayesian colocalization, multivariable MR (MVMR), phenome-wide association study (PheWAS), and tissue-specific validation using Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) eQTL data; and (3) functional exploration, where downstream mechanisms were explored through enrichment and immune infiltration analyses of bulk retinal transcriptomic data and the cellular context of key genes was delineated by analyzing single-cell RNA sequencing dataset.

Data source

An initial set of PCD-related genes was compiled from published literature (Yang et al. 2024b; Luo et al. 2024; Sun et al. 2023a; Wei et al. 2023; Feng and Shen 2023). After merging and removing duplicates, 3,002 unique genes were obtained. Genetic instrumental variables for the primary MR analysis were derived from summary-level data. Cis-eQTL summary statistics were obtained from the eQTLGen consortium (31,684 blood and peripheral blood mononuclear cell samples). Cis-pQTL data were sourced from the deCODE study (Ferkingstad et al. 2021) and the UKB-PPP (Sun et al. 2023b). For subsequent cross-tissue validation analyses, tissue-specific cis-eQTL data were acquired from the GTEx project, version v7 (comprising 48 tissues) (Consortium 2020). Outcome data for RP were obtained from the MRC IEU OpenGWAS database (Elsworth et al. 2020), and the following three independent GWAS datasets were utilized: ebi-a-GCST90018912 (121 cases and 126,333 controls), ebi-a-GCST90013904, and ebi-a-GCST90013954 (each with 403,833 total samples). For downstream transcriptomic and single-cell analyses, the microarray dataset GSE62020 (27 RP and 27 control samples, M. musculus) and the single-cell RNA sequencing dataset GSE183206 (two RP and two control samples, M. musculus) were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (Barrett et al. 2013).

Genetic instrumental variable selection criteria

In this MR analysis, the exposures were defined as the genetically predicted expression levels (based on cis-eQTL) or protein abundance (based on cis-pQTL) of each PCD-related gene. The IVs were defined as cis-SNPs that were significantly associated with these quantitative traits. For blood-derived cis-eQTLs (from eQTLGen), the complete set of significant cis-eQTL results (FDR < 0.05) was downloaded, and SNPs with a cis-eQTL P < 5 × 10⁻⁸ were selected as IVs. For cis-pQTLs (from deCODE (Ferkingstad et al. 2021) and UKB-PPP (Sun et al. 2023b), the significance threshold was P < 5 × 10⁻⁸. For tissue-specific cis-eQTLs from GTEx (Consortium 2020), the significance threshold was P < 1 × 10⁻⁴; this threshold was chosen based on the standard analytical practice in GTEx data analysis, considering limited sample sizes per tissue and the need to ensure adequate detection power for common cis-regulatory variants, representing a recognized trade-off between statistical rigor and instrument availability. For GTEx-derived SNPs, effect allele frequencies were annotated using ANNOVAR (Wang et al. 2010) based on the hg19 human reference genome, the SNP147 database, and the European allele frequency information from the August 2015 version of the 1000 Genomes Project database.

Candidate SNPs were restricted to within ± 500 kb of the target gene’s transcription start site. Variants within the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) region were excluded. To ensure independence among instrumental variables, linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping was performed with a window size of 10,000 kb and an r² threshold < 0.2, using the European (EUR) population from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 (v5) as the reference panel.

MR analysis

Primary causal inference was conducted by two-sample MR using the TwoSampleMR package (Version 0.6.15). The analysis considered multiple PCD-related genes as the exposure factors and RP as the outcome. For exposures instrumented by a single SNP, the causal estimate was derived using the Wald ratio. For exposures with two or more SNPs, the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method was applied. After extracting the SNP data for exposures and outcomes, the harmonise_data function was used to align the two datasets with the parameter action = 2. This mode attempts to infer the forward strand alleles and uses allele frequencies to infer palindromic SNPs (default conservative strategy), ultimately discarding all SNPs that could not be successfully harmonized. Following harmonization, only SNPs with mr_keep == TRUE were retained for subsequent MR analysis. To assess the robustness of the MR estimates, heterogeneity and pleiotropy tests were performed. Additionally, leave-one-out analysis was conducted to evaluate the influence of individual SNPs. The direction of causality was verified using the Steiger directionality test.

SMR analysis

SMR (Zhu et al. 2016) was employed to assess pleiotropic associations between gene expression levels and complex traits using summary statistics from GWAS and QTL studies. SMR analysis was performed using the official software (version 1.3.1) with default parameters.

Bayesian colocalization analysis

Bayesian colocalization analysis was performed using the coloc package (Version 5.2.3). To estimate the probability of shared causal variants for each selected gene, all SNPs within a ± 50 kb window of its lead SNP were included in the analysis. In this analysis, five mutually exclusive hypotheses (H0-H4) were evaluated, with the posterior probability H4 specifically representing the probability that both the exposure and the outcome share a single common causal genetic variant at the locus. A variety of PCD-related genes with positive colocalization (H4 > 0.5) across the three different data set sources were used as key genes for subsequent analysis.

MVMR analysis

MVMR was performed to estimate the direct genetic effect of the key genes on RP risk while adjusting for potential confounding from established lifestyle risk factors. The eight core genes (AIM2, RAB3GAP1, ATG12, ITPR3, PKIB, ATP6V1C1, STAT5A, HM13) identified from preliminary screening and two major confounder domains (Huang et al. 2022; Bully et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2023)—smoking and physical activity—were included in the MVMR models. Confounders were selected as available proxy phenotypes from public GWAS, including: duration of vigorous physical activity; age of smoking initiation; maternal smoking around birth; types of physical activity in last 4 weeks: walking for pleasure, none of the above, other exercises (e.g., swimming, cycling, keep fit, bowling), strenuous sports, light DIY, and heavy DIY; smoking initiation; smoking status (current/previous/never); tobacco smoking (ex-smoker/occasionally/never smoked/smokes on most or all days); ever stopped smoking for 6 + months; why stopped smoking: doctor’s advice/health precaution/none of the above; why reduced smoking: illness or ill health; and workplace had a lot of cigarette smoke from other people smoking: rarely/never. A total of 32 GWAS instrument phenotypes for smoking and physical activity were screened. After instrument harmonization and eligibility filtering, 128 analyzable gene–confounder MVMR models were formed, of which 108 yielded valid core-gene effect P-values. To optimize conciseness, results are presented for gene–confounder pairs that maintained a statistically significant independent genetic effect after multivariable adjustment (nominal P < 0.05).

PheWAS

PheWAS was conducted to evaluate the pleiotropic effects and potential adverse outcomes of the key genes evaluated as therapeutic targets utilizing the AstraZeneca PheWAS Portal (https://azphewas.com/). This tool was selected because it systematically evaluates associations between gene-level burden scores (derived from whole-exome sequencing data of approximately 450,000 UK Biobank participants) and thousands of phenotypes. This approach, based on rare variant burden, provides a complementary perspective to our primary MR analysis that employs common cis-regulatory variants, allowing for a broader assessment of vertical pleiotropy risks. The original study included approximately 15,500 binary and 1500 continuous phenotypes, with individuals in the exome-sequencing subgroup obtained from the UK Biobank.

Bulk transcriptomic analysis of key genes in RP

Bulk transcriptomic analysis of key genes in RP was performed using the GSE62020 dataset, which was downloaded from the GEO database via the GEOquery R package (version 2.70.0) (Davis and Meltzer 2007). Data preprocessing, including probe annotation, normalization, and standardization, was performed using the limma package (version 3.58.1) (Ritchie et al. 2015). Human gene symbols were converted to their mouse orthologs using the Homologene package (Version 1.4.68.19.3.27) in R prior to downstream analysis.

A heatmap was generated to visualize expression differences of key genes between RP and control samples. The diagnostic efficacy of these key genes was evaluated by plotting receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculating the area under the curve (AUC) using the pROC package (version 1.18.5) (Robin et al. 2011). AUCs of 0.5–0.7, 0.7–0.9, and > 0.9 indicate low accuracy, moderate accuracy, and high accuracy, respectively. Gene Ontology (GO) (Mi et al. 2019) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis (Kanehisa and Goto 2000) for the key genes were performed using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.10.0) (Yu et al. 2012). Enriched terms with Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted P < 0.05 were considered significant.

Single-gene gene set enrichment analysis (sgGSEA) was conducted to investigate the biological processes associated with each key gene across the RP and control groups. First, all genes in the GSE62020 dataset were ranked according to their correlation coefficients with each key gene. This ranked gene list was then subjected to sgGSEA using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.10.0) (Yu et al. 2012) against the Molecular Signatures Database (Liberzon et al. 2011) m2 gene set (m2.all.v2025.1.Mm.symbols.gmt). The analysis parameters were set with a seed of 2020, a minimum gene set size of 10, and a maximum size of 500. Gene sets with Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted P < 0.05 were defined as significantly enriched.

Immune cell infiltration analysis was performed using the CIBERSORT algorithm (Newman et al. 2015). The immune cell infiltration matrix for the GSE62020 dataset was generated, and results with an immune cell enrichment score > 0 were retained for subsequent analysis. The proportions of immune cell types were visualized in a bar plot. Correlations among different immune cell types were calculated using Spearman’s algorithm and displayed in a heatmap generated using the pheatmap package (version 1.0.12). Finally, Spearman’s correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate the relationships between the expression of the key genes and the estimated abundance of immune cells, with results presented in a bubble plot using the ggplot2 package (version 3.4.4).

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis of key genes in RP

Single-cell RNA-seq data from the GSE183206 dataset were analyzed. The count matrix was imported, and a Seurat object was created using the CreateSeuratObject function from the Seurat package (version 5.0.1) (Hao et al. 2021). Initial filtering parameters were set to retain genes detected in at least three cells and cells expressed at least 200 genes. Low-quality cells or empty droplets were removed using the following thresholds: unique RNA count (nCount_RNA) < 500, number of detected genes (nFeature_RNA) < 250, log10(GenesPerUMI) ratio < 0.8, and percentage of mitochondrial gene counts > 20%.

Data were normalized using the LogNormalize method. The top 2000 highly variable genes were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function with the “vst” selection method. Data scaling was performed using the ScaleData function to regress out technical variations. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied. Significant principal components were selected based on the elbow plot (using the ElbowPlot function), and 30 principal components were chosen for downstream analysis.

A k-nearest neighbor graph was constructed in PCA space using Euclidean distance via the FindNeighbors function with the 30 selected principal components. Cell clusters were identified using the FindClusters function at a resolution of 0.6 (determined using the clustree function). Finally, dimensionality reduction for visualization and exploration was performed using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) via the RunUMAP function.

Cell types were identified by annotating the single-cell data using the SingleR function from the SingleR package (version 2.4.1) (Aran et al. 2019), in conjunction with manual curation, based on the mouse ImmGenData reference dataset. The expression of the key genes across the identified cell types was visualized using the DotPlot and FeaturePlot functions. To identify cluster-specific markers, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each cell cluster were determined using the FindAllMarkers function with the Wilcoxon rank sum test. The top 10 marker genes for each cluster were defined as single-cell DEGs for subsequent analysis.

To assess the activity of gene signatures at the single-cell level, AUCell analysis was employed by AUCell package (Version 1.30.1) (Andreatta and Carmona 2021). The top 10 single-cell DEGs from each cell cluster were used as input gene sets for scoring.

Statistical analysis

All data processing and analyses in this study were performed using R software (version 4.3.3). For comparisons of continuous variables between two groups, the independent-samples Student’s t-test was used to assess the statistical significance of normally distributed data. For variables that were not normally distributed, the Mann–Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank sum test) was used. Comparisons among three or more groups were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Correlations between different molecules were evaluated by Spearman’s correlation analysis. Unless otherwise specified, all P values were two-sided, and P < 0.05 denoted statistical significance.

Results

MR identifies core genetically associated PCD genes in RP

To identify PCD-related genes with genetic roles in RP, we performed two-sample MR analyses (Hemani et al. 2018) using genetically predicted gene expression (cis-eQTL) and genetically predicted protein abundance (cis-pQTL) as exposures and RP disease status as the outcome. After intersecting 3,002 PCD-related genes with a retinal transcriptomic dataset (GSE62020), we retained 2,386 retina-expressed candidates for subsequent analysis. Two-sample MR analysis using cis-eQTL instruments from the eQTLGen consortium identified 16 genes with putative genetic associations with RP (adjusted P < 0.05; Table 1). Parallel analysis using cis-pQTL data from the deCODE study and the UKB-PPP identified six genes at the protein level (adjusted P < 0.05; Table 2). Sensitivity analyses, including Cochran’s Q test for heterogeneity, MR-Egger intercept test for horizontal pleiotropy, leave-one-out analysis, funnel plot inspection, and Steiger’s directionality test, confirmed the robustness of these associations against heterogeneity and pleiotropy (Tables S1–3 in Supplementary Material 1, Table S4 in Supplementary Material 2, Figures S1–4 in Supplementary Material 1).

Validation and refinement of genetically associated PCD genes in RP

To further refine this gene set, we applied SMR (Zhu et al. 2016) with the heterogeneity in dependent instruments (HEIDI) test. Using cis-eQTL data, SMR supported significant genetic associations for eight genes (AIM2, RAB3GAP1, ATG12, ITPR3, PKIB, ATP6V1C1, STAT5A, and HM13) with RP risk (P_SMR < 0.05 and P_HEIDI > 0.05; Table S5 in Supplementary Material 2). Bayesian colocalization analysis of these eight genes indicated only weak evidence for shared causal variants between gene expression and RP risk (H4 < 0.5). Conversely, SMR analysis using cis-pQTL data did not identify significant associations for the corresponding six PCD-related proteins (P_SMR > 0.05). Cross-tissue validation with cis-eQTLs from 48 GTEx tissues confirmed that six of the eight genes (AIM2, ATG12, PKIB, ATP6V1C1, STAT5A, and HM13) remained significantly associated with RP across 16 other tissues (adjusted P < 0.05; Table S6 in Supplementary Material 2). MVMR adjusting for smoking and physical activity (Huang et al. 2022) was performed by jointly modeling each of the eight core genes with proxy phenotypes representing these two confounder domains. After multivariable adjustment, AIM2, RAB3GAP1, ITPR3, ATP6V1C1, STAT5A, and HM13 retained nominally significant independent genetic effects in certain models (nominal P < 0.05; Table S7 in Supplementary Material 1). Table S7 presents only the gene–confounder combinations that reached nominal significance after adjustment. Finally, a PheWAS for the eight core genes using the AstraZeneca PheWAS Portal failed to detect genome‑wide significant associations with other traits (all P > 5 × 10− 8; Figures S5–6 in Supplementary Material 1). However, we note that publicly available resources such as the GWAS Catalog documents several genome-wide significant associations for the loci of these core genes (e.g., AIM2 with immune-related traits such as leukocyte count, psoriasis, and CRP levels). These known associations are compiled in Table S8 in Supplementary Material 3 for reference. The discrepancy between the findings primarily stems from differences in the underlying data sources, variant spectra, and phenotypic coverage between the two resources.

Collectively, the integrated MR analyses identified a core set of eight PCD-related genes with significant and specific genetic associations with RP, underscoring their potential as precise therapeutic targets.

Retinal transcriptomics and functional convergence of RP–linked PCD genes

We profiled the retinal expression of the eight core PCD genes using murine transcriptomic data (GSE62020). Human gene symbols were converted to mouse orthologs (Aim2, Rab3gap1, Atg12, Itpr3, Pkib, Atp6v1c1, Stat5a, and H13) using the Homologene R package (Version 1.4.68.19.3.27). Preprocessing involved probe annotation, normalization, and quality control with the limma R package (version 3.58.1), with data distribution before and after normalization visualized by boxplots (Figure S7 in Supplementary Material 1). The resulting expression profiles distinguished RP from control samples. A heatmap visualized the expression differences of the eight key genes between the RP and control groups (Fig. 2A). With the exception of Aim2, the other seven core genes showed strong coexpression patterns (Fig. 2B). In contrast, Aim2 expression displayed only weak correlations with these genes, suggesting it may be governed by a relatively independent regulatory network. ROC curve analysis (Robin et al. 2011) indicated low diagnostic accuracy (0.5 < AUC < 0.7) for Aim2, Rab3gap1, Atg12, Itpr3, Pkib, Stat5a, and H13 in classifying RP versus control (Figure S8 in Supplementary Material 1), these findings underscore the necessity of integrating polygenic, environmental, and epigenetic factors into comprehensive modeling approaches for RP.

Immune microenvironment and phenotypic specificity of core PCD genes

To further elucidate the impact of these genes on the retinal immune landscape, we next characterized the immune microenvironment in RP. We applied the CIBERSORT algorithm to deconvolute transcriptomic data from the GSE62020 dataset. This revealed significantly increased infiltration of 17 immune cell types in RP retina compared to controls (Fig. 4A). Correlation analyses demonstrated extensive intercorrelations among these immune subsets, with memory CD4+ T cells and γδ T cells showing the strongest positive correlation (r = 0.576, P < 0.05; Fig. 4B). Spearman correlation analyses between the eight core PCD genes and immune cell abundances further indicated that most genes were significantly associated with specific immune populations. Notably, H13 (the murine ortholog of HM13) expression exhibited a strong negative correlation with proinflammatory M1 macrophage infiltration (r = − 0.625, P < 0.05; Fig. 4C), suggesting a potential immunomodulatory role. These findings delineate a profoundly dysregulated immune microenvironment in RP and establish direct links between the core genetically associated PCD genes and specific immune alterations.

Single-cell transcriptomic atlas reveals müller glia reprogramming

To map the cellular expression of the eight core PCD genes at single-cell resolution, we analyzed a murine single-cell RNA-seq dataset (GSE183206) (Hao et al. 2021). Following standard quality control, the distribution of gene expression is shown in a violin plot (Fig. 5A), and dimensionality reduction was visualized by PCA (Fig. 5B). Subsequent clustering via UMAP identified 29 transcriptionally distinct cell subclusters (Fig. 5C). Reference-based and manual annotation mapped these to nine major retinal cell types: rod photoreceptor cells, Müller glia, cone photoreceptor cells, neurons, bipolar cells, microglial cells, amacrine cells, vascular endothelial cells, and horizontal cells (Fig. 5D). The identity of these cell types was corroborated by their respective canonical marker genes, as visualized in dot plots (Fig. 5F). A bar plot comparing cell type proportions revealed significant compositional shifts between RP and control retinas (Fig. 5E).

In summary, this high-resolution atlas delineates the cell-type–specific expression of the eight core PCD genes and uncovers prominent transcriptional reprogramming in Müller glia in the available RP samples. The functional enrichment of these alterations in pathways critical for retinal homeostasis, coupled with the specific cellular localization of PCD genes, suggests that Müller glia may serve as a key cell type integrating PCD-related signals in the retinal pathology of RP.

Discussion

RP is a genetically heterogeneous blinding disorder with a complex pathogenesis and no curative treatments (Karuntu et al. 2025; Verbakel et al. 2018). By integrating MR with multimodal analyses, we systematically identified eight core PCD‑related genes (AIM2, RAB3GAP1, ATG12, ITPR3, PKIB, ATP6V1C1, STAT5A, and HM13) with significant and specific genetic associations to RP risk. The robustness and specificity of these associations were confirmed by cross‑tissue analyses, MVMR (adjusting for smoking and physical activity), and PheWAS. While our gene-burden PheWAS did not reveal broad pleiotropy, indicating that all eight core PCD genes exhibited no evidence of widespread associations across diverse phenotypes and that the genetic burden was relatively specific to RP—thereby strengthening the exclusion restriction assumption by minimizing concerns about horizontal pleiotropy. Queries of the GWAS Catalog identified known associations for AIM2 with immune traits, which are biologically plausible given its role in inflammasome function and thus support the hypothesis of immune-mediated mechanisms in RP. We further demonstrated that these genes functionally converge on immune and metabolic pathways, are linked to the remodeling of the retinal immune microenvironment, and exert preferential effects on Müller glia at single‑cell resolution. Collectively, our findings establish a pathogenic framework linking genetic risk to cellular specificity in RP.

This multimodal framework strengthens genetic association inference for the eight prioritized genes, extending the focus beyond canonical RP genes such as RHO and USH2A (Pniakowska et al. 2025). Of note, these associations were derived from a non‑subtype‑stratified GWAS, representing broad RP genetic risk rather than subtype‑specific mechanisms; future genotype‑specific studies are required to determine whether these core PCD genes act as broadly pathogenic hubs or as drivers in specific subtypes. Within this multimodal framework, several prioritized genes exhibit potential mechanistic relevance to RP pathogenesis, warranting further investigation. AIM2, a core component of the AIM2 inflammasome that mediates pyroptosis (Dai et al. 2023) and is activated in other retinal pathologies (Pronin et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2024), provides direct genetic evidence linking innate immune activation and inflammatory cell death to RP. The identified network also encompasses genes with strong biological plausibility: autophagy-related regulators (ATG12, RAB3GAP1, and ATP6V1C1) suggest disrupted autophagic flux (Russo et al. 2018; Kern et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2025), whereas ITPR3 implicates altered calcium‑dependent apoptotic signaling (Kuchay et al. 2017). Additionally, the association of STAT5A points to potential dysregulation of JAK–STAT signaling, a pathway critical for retinal homeostasis (Zhang et al. 2003). This convergent PCD gene network establishes a new genetic framework for understanding RP pathogenesis.

In retinal bulk transcriptomics, the eight‑gene set formed a correlated co‑expression network in RP, indicating functional coordination. With the exception of Aim2, which displayed weaker correlations with the other genes, the remaining seven core genes showed strong positive co‑expression patterns. This network‑based pathophysiology suggests that multigene models may better stratify patients and guide targeted therapies than single‑gene approaches, whose limited predictive accuracy (as observed in supplementary ROC analyses) reflects the substantial genetic heterogeneity of RP. Functional enrichment analyses implicated these genes in immune regulation, notably T‑cell homeostasis, innate immune response regulation, and the NOD‑like receptor signaling pathway. The co‑enrichment of this pathway along with the genetic association of Aim2 (mouse ortholog of AIM2) strongly suggests inflammasome‑mediated pyroptosis as a key mechanism in RP pathogenesis (Di Virgilio 2013; Zhang et al. 2021; Sagulenko et al. 2013).

Single‑gene GSEA delineated a convergent functional spectrum for the core PCD network, revealing associations with complementary pathways including inflammatory/stress response, metabolic reprogramming, epigenetic regulation, and DNA damage response. Extending this analysis to the immune microenvironment, CIBERSORT revealed altered proportions of 17 immune cell types in RP retinas, with core PCD gene expression closely linked to specific subsets. Notably, H13 (mouse ortholog of HM13) exhibited the strongest negative correlation with pro‑inflammatory M1 macrophages (r = − 0.625, P < 0.05). Given that HM13 encodes a component of the endoplasmic reticulum–associated degradation (ERAD) pathway (Cao et al. 2025), this specific association connects endoplasmic reticulum stress in retinal cells to a discrete immune profile. These findings suggest that therapeutic strategies targeting such specific immune subsets, informed by upstream PCD gene activity, might offer a more precise approach than broad immunosuppression for RP.

At single‑cell resolution, the core PCD genes exhibited highly cell‑type–restricted expression patterns, with specific enrichment observed in retinal microglia (Aim2, Pkib), vascular endothelial cells (Itpr3, Atg12, H13), horizontal cells (Rab3gap1, Atp6v1c1, Atg12, H13), and amacrine cells (Rab3gap1, Atp6v1c1, H13). Müller glia exhibited the most pronounced transcriptional remodeling signals among the analyzed cell types in the present dataset. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the DEGs from Müller glia highlighted biological processes critical for retinal function, including visual perception, sensory perception of light stimulus, eye development, and retina development, and implicated key pathways such as phototransduction. These results suggest that Müller glia may act as an important integrative hub through which the PCD network, via its compartmentalized expression across interacting retinal cell types (e.g., microglia, endothelium), could coordinate immune responses and neuronal homeostasis, but this conclusion remains preliminary due to the limited single‑cell sample size. Beyond this central role, rod photoreceptors and vascular endothelial cells also displayed notable transcriptional activity, meriting further investigation into their roles in the retinal microenvironment.

Limitations and future perspectives

This study has several methodological limitations. First, the primary MR analyses relied on a discovery GWAS with merely 121 RP cases, leading to insufficient statistical power and imprecise effect estimates. The GWAS data were not stratified by genotype or clinical subtype; therefore, the identified genetic associations are more appropriately interpreted as broad RP signals rather than subtype-specific mechanisms. For genes with few instrumental SNPs, heterogeneity and pleiotropy assessments were limited, with sensitivity tests interpreted cautiously. Bayesian colocalization analysis failed to confirm shared causal variants, suggesting that the observed associations may be influenced by LD or nearby regulatory signals, which necessitates further fine-mapping and functional validation. A moderate clumping threshold was adopted to balance statistical power and confounder control due to the scarce RP cases. Additionally, possible sample overlap between UKB-PPP and MRC IEU OpenGWAS platforms may exist (as UK Biobank is a data source of the latter), but these proteomic associations did not proceed to core validation, thus limiting potential bias.

Furthermore, MR-based causal inferences are observational and lack experimental verification. The PheWAS analysis was also limited by the phenotypic coverage of the database used, particularly for rare ophthalmic diseases and retina-specific phenotypes. The downstream transcriptomic and single-cell analyses were based on available mouse retinal datasets. Although human-to-mouse orthologous gene mapping provided useful clues for functional exploration, species differences and the small single-cell sample size limited the strength of biological validation. Therefore, the AUCell and enrichment results should be considered hypothesis-generating evidence, suggesting that Müller glia and retinal immune-related pathways may be associated with the core gene network, without directly proving definitive cell-type-specific mechanisms. Integrating heterogeneous summary data may also introduce population stratification and technical bias. In conclusion, this study provides candidate susceptibility genes and mechanistic clues for RP; these findings require further validation of their biological significance and potential translational value in larger cohorts, subtype-stratified genetic data, independent retinal single-cell resources, and functional experiments.

Conclusion

This integrative multimodal study establishes a core network of PCD-related genes as genetically linked to RP. We delineate a pathogenic network involving immune dysregulation and extensive Müller glia reprogramming. The findings provide a systems‑level framework connecting genetic causality to cellular pathophysiology, highlighting high‑priority targets for mechanistic validation and therapeutic development in this currently incurable disorder.
