Executive Summary
Long-duration energy storage (LDES) has the potential to dramatically increase
electric grid reliability, resiliency, and flexibility (Denholm et al. 2023; Scott et al.
2023). With storage lasting 10 hours or longer, LDES can provide energy, capacity,
and ancillary services over long time periods. It can also store excess energy from the
grid. This makes LDES a flexible asset to the grid in terms of both energy generation
and energy load. Pumped storage hydropower is the most mature LDES technology. It
includes two reservoirs at different elevations, pumping water uphill to store energy
and releasing it downhill to generate electricity. Although pumped storage hydro has
limited new siting opportunities, rapid R&D advancements have resulted in a set of
newer LDES technologies. These new technologies have the potential to strengthen the
grid by relieving transmission congestion and/or increasing transmission capacity.
They facilitate effective integration of new energy sources, make the system more
resilient in cases of multi-day extreme weather events, and provide frequency and
voltage support.

Despite the great potential for LDES to strengthen the reliability of the electric grid,
there are known hurdles to putting these newer LDES technologies into action at scale:

• A new set of diverse and competitive LDES technologies (Shan et al. 2022) have
 relatively high upfront costs. They therefore face a stark investment challenge, in
 spite of their potential as a viable alternative to firm, dispatchable generation
 capacity in some cases.
• LDES models must be improved substantially (1) to ensure they properly represent
 LDES in planning tools and (2) to obtain and efficiently schedule LDES resources.

Given these challenges and current modeling gaps, there is a common perception that
LDES is not financially viable under existing conditions. Specific actions are often
proposed to accelerate LDES deployment, including targeted policy and regulatory
support. However, enhancing the structure and design of existing planning, operations,
and organized wholesale markets can also better characterize the value of LDES to the
power system, even without further specific policy support. Examples of such
enhancements include the following:

 1. Improving frameworks to determine the adequacy of resources and to create
 rules for capacity accreditation to recognize the added value of longer-duration
 resources
 2. Developing multi-day markets and products to enable LDES to manage multi-
 day operational uncertainties
 3. Creating ways to plan for and compensate LDES for deferring or avoiding
 transmission system buildouts.
To more thoroughly assess the gaps and barriers to LDES investment and readiness for
integration into a future grid, we conducted (1) stakeholder outreach via an online survey
and conversations with individual independent system operators/regional transmission

 v

organizations and (2) a literature review. A high-level assessment of the specific gaps
and barriers to the realization of LDES potential is depicted in Figure ES-1. Our
findings highlight the gaps and development opportunities for next-generation
methods and tools for planning, operations, and market design to effectively
manage multi-day uncertainties and assess emerging technologies such as LDES.

Based on this assessment, we identified a set of opportunities for LDES focused
development, including a framework to quantify the contributions of LDES on resource
adequacy, reliability, and resiliency. These efforts aim to address the gaps and needs that
have been identified through our stakeholder outreach and literature review. Specifically,
we identify the potential demand for and benefits of an open-source, LDES-centric
evaluation framework that can guide future planning, operations, market design, and
policy reforms.

1 Potential for Long-Duration Energy Storage:
  Findings From Stakeholder Outreach
Most independent system operators (ISOs) and regional transmission organizations (RTOs) have
pumped storage hydro (PSH), a type of long-duration energy storage (LDES), within their
systems. There is a broad consensus that LDES can serve as a valuable grid asset—it can provide
energy, capacity, and ancillary services. LDES is also flexible and can shift the timing of charging
and/or discharging based on grid conditions. In principle, most of these capabilities are shared
with shorter-duration resources. However, LDES can provide the same services over much longer
time frames and therefore realize different benefits, such as emergency backup power for multi-
day events. Moreover, LDES can help diversify the existing energy storage asset mix and
strengthen reliability by helping match supply with demand over a longer time period.

Although the benefits of LDES are often clear, LDES deployment has been limited. Our team
of researchers from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory, Sandia National Laboratories,
and Argonne National Laboratory sought to better understand these limitations by conducting
two types of stakeholder outreach:

 1. Online survey: Our online survey about grid integration, conducted from October 2024 to
 February 2025, gathered input and insights from key stakeholders involved with LDES
 resources. Thirty-eight people responded to the survey, representing utilities, storage
 technology companies, storage project developers, software vendors, grid operators,
 government and non-government advocacy groups, as shown in Figure 1.
 2. 1:1 outreach: Our multi-laboratory team conducted detailed 1-on-1 interviews with
 and/or surveys of U.S. and Canadian ISOs. Our team was able to collect responses from
 all U.S. ISOs—California Independent System Operator (CAISO), Electric Reliability
 Council of Texas (ERCOT), ISO New England (ISO-NE), Midcontinent Independent
 System Operator (MISO), PJM Interconnection (PJM), New York Independent System
 Operator (NYISO), and Southwest Power Pool (SPP)—and Independent Electricity
 System Operator (IESO) in Canada.

 Figure 1. LDES online survey respondent categories

Survey respondents and interviewees were asked a range of questions. The subjects of these
questions included the status and value of LDES resources, integration challenges, modeling gaps,
market design considerations, and policy mechanisms for deployment. We collected survey

 1

responses and interview feedback and synthesized responses across these topic areas, as is
reported in the following subsections of this report. We also complement these insights with
findings from a review of current academic literature on LDES. For a full set of questions and
more detailed summaries of responses from the survey and grid operator interviews, please see
the Appendix.

1.1 Current Status of Long-Duration Energy Storage Deployment and
 Anticipated Values
LDES resources can be categorized according to technological maturity and deployment status.
Pumped storage hydro (PSH) is a mature LDES technology and has been integrated into market
operations for decades. Compared to PSH, emerging LDES technologies may differ in their
physical characteristics, storage duration, market readiness, and relative advantages and
disadvantages. Some technologies are still in research and development, whereas others are
commercially available. Newer LDES technologies can be configured either as stand-alone
resources or in combination with other technologies (Albertus et al. 2020).

Less than 20% of survey respondents indicated they currently own and operate LDES. Only one
grid operator from U.S. ISOs indicated they have LDES (not PSH) in their systems or generation
interconnection queues. The remaining respondents indicated they are still evaluating or will not
deploy LDES within their organization but still recognize the value of LDES. Stakeholder
feedback mentioned the slow uptake of LDES may be blamed on high capital costs and the lack
of modeling methods and tools to appropriately value LDES.

Despite low current levels of deployment, there was a general consensus among survey
respondents and grid operators on the versatile roles LDES can play to help achieve resource
adequacy 1 and enhance grid reliability 2 and resiliency, 3 especially during extreme weather
events. Multiple stakeholders expressed optimism about the future role of LDES by mentioning
various services and market products LDES can deliver. According to our literature review, these
include smoothing variability, time shifting (arbitrage), supporting distributed energy resource
integration, deferring transmission and distribution upgrades, and providing customer
emergency management services (Akhil et al. 2013; McNamara et al. 2022).

Respondents emphasized the need for energy arbitrage (i.e., buying electricity at low prices when
demand is low and selling at higher prices when demand is high), resource adequacy, and a
number of ancillary services. The provision of these services by LDES resources is reviewed in
several studies (Denholm et al. 2023; Albertus et al. 2020; Staadecker et al. 2024; Pudjianto and
Strbac 2022). The flexibility of LDES resources also means they are capable of providing a
combination of market services in response to different or evolving grid needs. More than 70% of
survey respondents predict LDES roles will evolve over the next 5 years, with 36% expecting
significant evolution in the roles LDES will play, as depicted in Figure 2.

Survey respondents were asked to rank gaps in capturing LDES value in the planning processes
for current resource adequacy across eight dimensions on a scale from 1 to 5 (5 being the highest
gap level). Many stakeholders ranked all categories with a relatively high gap level (3 or above).
In particular, a majority of responses indicated the cost-benefit analysis process, by which
stakeholders evaluate total costs and benefits, including capital and operational expenses, has a
high gap value (4 or 5). In other words, stakeholders feel that lack of accurate cost models and
thorough understanding of the LDES economics are fundamental barriers to large-scale
deployment. Grid operators underlined the potential value of LDES for providing various system
services, including reliability and resiliency. Moreover, in power grids rich in variable renewable
energy (VRE), operators pointed out that LDES can provide firm capacity to complement VRE
resources, alleviate uncertainty in real-time operations, and reduce VRE curtailment.

From ISO responses, those with high shares of natural gas are concerned about gas availability,
especially during the winter. For ISOs with high existing amounts of short-duration storage, the
opportunity for energy arbitrage may decrease as new storage is added. Ancillary service prices
are also expected to decrease because short-duration storage has the potential to saturate these
markets. LDES may provide high-value services during prolonged periods of renewable energy
droughts or avoid transmission capacity buildout to meet peak demand. Several ISOs noted the
evolving reliability standards on energy assurance may create opportunities for new market
products, potentially enabling LDES to participate. LDES may also qualify for black start (i.e.,
the process of restarting an electric power system after a complete blackout), voltage support to
maintain voltage at safe levels, and fast frequency response to help keep the grid balanced.
However, some of these services are not typically compensated or are newer ancillary services
that only exist in pilot markets. Therefore, ISOs expressed that new resource adequacy methods
and new market products must be developed to compensate for the values provided by LDES.

1.2 Challenges in Grid Planning and Operations for Integrating Long-
 Duration Energy Storage
To integrate LDES technologies at scale, considerable cost reductions are required to achieve
economic competitiveness (U.S. Department of Energy 2024). Survey respondents and
interviewees highlighted that a key challenge impacting storage economics is recognizing the full
value LDES can provide to the grid and related compensation plans. Survey respondents ranked
research priorities needed to address the challenges associated with managing LDES operations
over multi-day periods, shown in Figure 3. In section 1.4, we discuss market design further.

 3

 Figure 3. Research priorities for LDES operation over multi-day periods (5 = highest priority)
Survey respondents agreed on gaps in inadequate standards, methods, tools, and data for
reliability metrics and resource adequacy under both normal and extreme events, as shown in
Figure 4. Moreover, there was a broad consensus that current capacity compensation
frameworks do not capture the full resource adequacy value that LDES can provide. For
example, capacity expansion models often use unlinked representative periods (i.e., selected
time slices that are not chronologically connected), which may fail to capture energy
adequacy needs during multi-day capacity shortage events. Other challenges to wider use of
LDES that were frequently mentioned include a general lack of knowledge of LDES
capabilities and the limited ability of models to capture the LDES values.

 Figure 4. Gaps in resource adequacy processes in valuing LDES
Survey respondents also emphasized the need for pilot programs to demonstrate the capabilities
of emerging technologies such as LDES. In section 1.3, we discuss LDES modeling and
software gaps.

Interviewees discussed the role of proper valuation and modeling challenges for integrating
LDES into the grid. For example, the lack of data (i.e., real-world operational data) makes it
difficult to develop realistic modeling approaches, and it creates uncertainty around the
potential value of LDES.

Many ISOs stated they lack the ability to capture or adequately handle uncertainty in
planning tools and thus risk undervaluing LDES. Major concerns include the lack of a time
period in the capacity expansion models and the use of perfect foresight in planning scenarios
without considering the uncertainties of operations. If uncertainty were included in planning,

 4

LDES resources may seem more valuable. The ISOs also agree the lack of production and real-
world operational data constrains realistic modeling approaches and creates challenges in
valuing LDES.

In most ISOs, market participants manage the state of charge (SOC) for storage. Market
participants can change their schedules and may cause uncertainty to operators. Nearly all
ISOs indicated they would anticipate challenges in scheduling and pricing storage in their
respective systems. Existing market clearing and operational processes and tools have
challenges resulting from temporal dependency of schedules, which are hard to model given
the limited optimization window in operations. LDES adds further complexity with its longer
duration, requiring new processes to manage SOC under high uncertainties.

1.3 Long-Duration Energy Storage Modeling and Software Gaps
Making informed investment decisions regarding LDES technologies requires state-of-the-art
simulation and modeling techniques. Typically, grid planning processes require significant
modeling efforts to integrate LDES models within the existing advanced optimization
frameworks, such as long-term capacity expansion planning, production cost, and resource
adequacy models (Sioshansi et al. 2022). Although accurately modeling LDES demands rigorous
consideration of technology-specific characteristics and economic details, these are often not
included (Shan et al. 2022). The major gaps in modeling LDES include:

• Capacity credits: The survey and the grid operator responses highlight the need for accurate
 evaluation of the reliability and resiliency contributions of LDES during prolonged periods of
 capacity shortage, using robust metrics such as effective load-carrying capability (e.g., PJM
 Interconnection 2022). Modeling should explicitly reflect the influence of storage duration
 and operational strategies on capacity credit allocation and market participation outcomes.
• Technology-specific models: Detailed and differentiated models are needed for each storage
 technology type, such as batteries, pumped storage hydro, and thermal storage, capturing
 unique operational behaviors, characteristics, and limitations (Shan et al. 2022).
• Capital and operational costs and asset lifetimes: Survey responses indicated the need for
 updated and accurate projections for capital expenditures, operating and maintenance costs,
 and expected asset lifetimes in technoeconomic analyses for different LDES technologies.
 Proper consideration of these parameters is essential to accurately compute the levelized cost
 of storage and to understand long-term economic implications (Sioshansi et al. 2022).
• Efficiency and degradation: It would also be helpful to understand energy efficiency losses
 and degradation rates over the lifetime of storage assets. The degradation rate is the rate at
 which an energy storage system loses capacity or efficiency over time due to usage and aging.
 It is important to incorporate comprehensive degradation mechanisms and impacts, and
 optimal management techniques aimed at maximizing system longevity and performance for
 different LDES technologies (Sioshansi et al. 2022).
• Operational constraints: The survey and grid operator responses also highlight that detailed
 operational constraints must represent all LDES technologies. These include minimum and
 maximum SOC, ramp rate limitations (in MW/min, reflecting the system’s flexibility to
 provide grid services), response times, reserve provision capabilities (i.e., the ability to supply
 reserve power to the grid), and realistic market participation scenarios. Different LDES
 technologies may have varying parameters. It is particularly important to align operational
 constraints with the intended duration of storage technologies, including appropriate look-
 ahead periods and SOC balancing.
The ability of models to both represent and integrate LDES into planning processes is currently
lacking, although there has been significant recent work to help practitioners, such as utility

 5

planners, system operators, and energy modelers, understand where the gaps exist and establish
associated modeling needs. Major research and development gaps have been identified in the
literature (Sioshansi et al. 2022; Maloney et al. 2020) and in this survey to improve planning
models, including coordinated expansion planning and the effective deployment of emerging
storage technologies. A critical research gap involves quantifying the errors caused by
simplifying the temporal resolution in planning models, such as using representative days or
aggregated time periods instead of continuous hourly or sub-hourly data, and how these
simplifications affect the accuracy of system operation and reliability assessments. These
concerns are valid for energy storage modeling in general, and for LDES more specifically
(Mantegna 2024), as changes in temporal representation can considerably impact both the
modeled installed capacity and the energy requirements for LDES (Sánchez-Pérez et al.
2022).

The key aspects of long-term grid planning that relate to LDES include:

• Uncertainty handling: Grid planning involves significant uncertainties, necessitating the
 evaluation of multiple scenarios to encompass the full range of potential future outcomes (Li
 et al. 2022). Common uncertainties in grid planning include volatility in capital costs for both
 established and emerging technologies (e.g., LDES technologies), fluctuations in fuel prices,
 adoption rates of distributed energy resources and electric vehicles, the maturity trajectory of
 emerging technologies, evolving policies and regulations, and weather-dependent load and
 generation profiles.
• Spatial-temporal resolution: Grid planners must consider spatial and temporal resolution of
 grid planning models to accurately capture LDES operational dynamics, geographic
 characteristics, and the transmission constraints. Generally, recent studies recommend that
 capacity expansion modeling tools that consider LDES should make investment decisions
 based on an extended time horizon (e.g., full year) of grid operations. These decisions should
 include load variability that reflects fluctuations on multiple timescales, that is, from day-to-
 day to season-to-season fluctuations (EPRI 2024; Form Energy 2020). Increasing the level of
 temporal resolution to better capture continuous storage state-of-charge constraints often
 comes at the cost of other modeling trade-offs—for example, reduced spatial resolution,
 integer relaxation, or linear approximations. In addition to modeling longer operational
 horizons, it is also important to keep the correct order of events to accurately reflect how
 LDES shifts energy across days or even weeks. Time-sampling methods often overlook these
 operational dynamics and therefore underestimate the full flexibility value of LDES.
 High-fidelity temporal resolution, such as sub-hourly modeling, is crucial for accurately
 capturing LDES’s fast response times and interactions with variable generation (Levin et al.
 2023; Piansky et al. 2024). Tools such as Sienna 4 and A-LEAF 5 help utilities simulate
 chronological operational phenomena, optimizing decisions. However, representative periods
 are frequently used in capacity expansion planning models to maintain computational
 efficiency while preserving accuracy (Moradi-Sepahvand 2023). Techniques such as
 clustering are used in tools such as Sandia’s QuESt Planning, 6 enabling utilities and states to
 efficiently determine optimal LDES investments. Multi-temporal frameworks provided by
 platforms such as A-LEAF, QuESt Planning, and GridPath integrate operational and long-
 term horizons, helping to align immediate operational strategies. Increasing spatial resolution
 in modeling captures critical geographic variability, enhancing the accuracy of sizing and
 siting decisions for LDES. In addition, accounting for transmission system constraints through

 In future planning studies, accounting for both uncertainties and spatial-temporal resolution
 can pose significant computational challenges.
• Co-optimization of multiple system dimensions: Effectively optimizing the infrastructure
 helps identify optimal strategies for investment by incorporating more than one dimension of
 the system. Co-optimization often achieves lower costs than sequential approaches (Spyrou et
 al. 2017). Similarly, modeling LDES investments requires integrated optimization of
 generation resources and transmission to achieve long-term economic efficiency. For effective
 LDES planning, life span and dynamic capital costs of storage technologies are often
 considered (Frazier et al. 2021).
• Reliability-based approach: Traditional planning on capacity expansion typically
 emphasizes minimizing costs, potentially neglecting critical grid reliability considerations. To
 address this limitation, planners increasingly use reliability-based frameworks incorporating
 metrics such as Loss of Load Expectation (LOLE), Expected Energy Not Served (EENS), and
 Expected Unserved Energy (EUE). A reliability-focused capacity expansion planning
 framework that integrates LDES has recently been used to achieve long-term goals without
 compromising reliability (Bera, Benson, and Nguyen 2023; Bera et al. 2023).
Survey respondents and grid operators echoed the importance of accurate LDES modeling to help
make decisions related to procuring LDES and eventually integrating LDES into electricity
markets on a wide-scale basis. Modeling processes feed directly into the valuation and assessment
of LDES resources, including assessing their overall system impacts. These modeling outcomes
can directly impact investment, policy, and regulatory decisions. Guerra et al. (2025) also note
modeling improvements can increase the associated operational value of LDES and increase the
standard capacity credit by a moderate amount. Survey respondents and grid operators suggested
investments to model and represent LDES in planning processes can better justify funding LDES,
but modeling capabilities have not advanced enough. Further, representing LDES in existing
models can be challenging because of the need to capture multi-day or even seasonal
chronologies.

Survey respondents and grid operators described the need to screen, scrutinize, and likely update
various types of tools and processes to more accurately represent and model LDES resources.
Respondents frequently mentioned production cost modeling, integrated resource planning,
capacity expansion models, power flow models, resource adequacy models, and market clearing
models. Respondents also underscored the role planning tools and methodologies play in
demonstrating the value of LDES. The need for detailed and sufficient operational or
representative data of LDES deployment was mentioned as a crucial modeling input that is
currently missing. These responses are supported by the state-of-the-art research on LDES, which
demonstrates that LDES modeling with linked representative periods is crucial to capture the full
value of LDES in planning models (Mantegna et al. 2024).

Stakeholders and respondents provided a range of suggestions to improve system planning tools
and processes, including enhanced representation of LDES operations and system flexibility
needs, quantifying the value of emissions reductions and other policy objectives, understanding
the interactions between LDES deployment and other opportunities for grid developments
(such as higher distributed energy resource integration), differentiating short- and long-duration
storage, identifying and representing extreme events that LDES can mitigate, and improved multi-
day forecasting. Including low-probability, high-impact events was cited as a key component to
capture the value of LDES in these scenarios. Furthermore, advanced computation tools and
artificial intelligence could be leveraged to improve uncertainty representation in modeling.

1.4 Market Design
For LDES resources to scale, they must be able to recoup their high upfront investment costs;
this can happen through compensation in competitive wholesale markets or indirectly through
inclusion in integrated resource planning processes (Scott et al. 2023). This points to the
importance of market design in potentially improving the recognition of the value of LDES to
the power system. However, current market structures may not fully account for or
compensate LDES (Balaraman 2023a; Twitchell 2023).

Survey responses and grid operator interviews highlighted that current capacity remuneration
rules do not adequately compensate LDES for the full range of benefits it can provide to the grid.
Storage market participation is currently dominated by short-duration energy storage with less
than 2 to 4 hours’ duration and other technologies that support peak load management. Current
market mechanisms do not reflect the value of LDES in terms of resiliency under multi-day
operational uncertainty. Although LDES can provide value under uncertain conditions,
modeling and planning processes do not currently incorporate data nor handle uncertainty to the
point LDES can realize this value. Both survey respondents and interviewees also noted that
capacity accreditation rules need improvement. There was also a consensus that current price
signals from ISOs with capacity markets are insufficient to provide incentives for LDES
investment.

Although storage participation in capacity markets is growing in the United States and elsewhere
(Kou 2023), current market structures generally do not provide compensation for the additional
resource adequacy value offered by longer-duration storage resources. There are a few
exceptions—for example, PJM now gives higher capacity credit to storage resources as their
duration increases, up to 10 hours. However, existing market constructs are typically limited
to short-duration capacity needs, typically 4 hours or less, and only provide guaranteed
compensation in the short term (Bhatnagar et al. 2022).

ISOs vary in the structures they have in place for managing the multi-day operational
uncertainties and plans to develop multi-day markets or products. In addition to clearing day-
ahead and real-time markets, most ISOs conduct a nonbinding multi-day scheduling process to
ensure sufficient resources available to manage unexpected changes in supply or demand.
However, none of the ISOs have a near-term plan to develop a multi-day market because of the
settlement complexity and high uncertainties in input data. ISO-NE conducts a 3-week energy
assessment every week during the winter, which serves as a situational awareness tool.

There are also no known examples of long-duration energy products across multi-day time
frames. Establishing multi-day markets and energy products could provide price signals
and/or contracts to incentivize energy storage systems capable of discharging power over
several days. However, the effort to establish multi-day markets and energy products can be
hampered by gaps in and challenges with accurate forecasting, pricing, scheduling, and
compensating storage solutions across these longer time frames. Some ISOs already produce
multi-day or multi-week previews for situational awareness but do not extend these
capabilities to market scheduling. To this end, many survey respondents and grid operators
highlighted the interdependence between improving modeling capabilities and seeing LDES in
the market queue.

In addition to energy and capacity, LDES can provide resilience value and other services, such
as relief from transmission congestion. However, markets are typically focused on
compensating resources for energy, capacity, and ancillary services, so there is a lack of
remuneration for these additional resiliency attributes. Some ISOs allow storage to be used

 8

exclusively as a transmission asset, prohibiting it from participating in the markets. Although
certain market structures can allow for resources providing resilience value, mechanisms to
remunerate long-duration resilience value are lacking, especially in cases of extreme weather
events or other uncertain, prolonged events that challenge grid systems.

Benefits of the LDES to the owners and to society depend heavily on market design
(Rudkevich et al. 2024). Overall, survey respondents and grid operators echoed that existing day-
ahead and real-time markets prioritize shorter-duration products and short-term trading and lack
signals for longer-duration storage value. This constrains the revenue potential for LDES, which
is currently more expensive up front.

In Figure 3, the majority of stakeholders ranked the development of multi-day markets or
products in the U.S. ISOs and RTOs as a high priority.

1.5 Policy Mechanisms
Survey respondents emphasized that policy actions from the federal government, states, utilities,
and regulators are necessary—or at least part of the solution—to spur LDES deployment. Several
types of policies have the potential to impact or spur LDES deployment, including federal and
state incentives; support for pilot and/or demonstration projects; direct funding, grants, and loan
guarantees for specific projects; R&D funding in materials; design and deployment; technical
assistance to integrate LDES in planning processes; LDES procurement mandates; regulatory
permissions for first-of-a-kind projects and technologies; and other grid improvements that
facilitate LDES participation. This reflects the often-repeated claim in the wider literature that
LDES will not scale on its own without significant policy and regulatory actions (Scott et al.
2023; Balaraman 2023b).

Some states are pursuing targeted policy efforts for LDES deployment. California was the first
state to introduce an LDES procurement target of 2 gigawatts (GW; California Public Utilities
Commission 2024) and has also established direct funding programs for LDES projects that meet
certain siting qualifications (California Energy Commission 2025). New York regulators recently
signed off on a state roadmap for long-duration storage that defines a state procurement target of 6
GW (Colthorpe 2024) and outlines several steps the state aims to take to use more long-duration
storage capacity. New York has also awarded $15 million to LDES demonstration projects
(NYSERDA 2023).

However, stakeholder feedback highlighted that, although federal and state incentives are
important, they may not be sufficient for a broad uptake of LDES. Also needed are revenue
guarantees or the promise of market revenues to recover high investment costs. Even if those who
use LDES are able to capitalize on tax subsidies or other incentives to offset the upfront capital
cost of the system, market compensation remains one of the primary ways storage resources earn
revenues to recoup investment costs. The UK’s recent decision to pursue a policy approach that
establishes a cap-and-floor regime for LDES participation in markets highlights the link between
policy and markets (UK Department for Energy Security and Net Zero 2024). It also underscores
the need to prioritize and sequence different policies over time as LDES continues to
commercialize, noting that specific, targeted actions that may help scale LDES in the near term
could end up harming or distorting market operations in the longer term.

There is also broad consensus that pilot demonstration projects can measure the impacts of
LDES and familiarize grid stakeholders with LDES technologies and their technical value, thus
making them more comfortable with the technologies. In addition to demonstrating the role
LDES can play on the grid, stakeholder feedback underscored that pilot demonstration projects

 9

are helpful for shortening subsequent deployment time frames. Support and funding for
LDES pilot demonstration projects have been growing in certain jurisdictions, including
California and New York. However, many regions have not challenged the status quo and lack
support for first-of-its-kind demonstrations. This may contribute to a bias for lithium-ion
resources already participating in markets rather than newer LDES technologies.

Most respondents also noted increased direct funding is indispensable for LDES development.
However, many forms of direct funding, including grants and loan guarantees—as well as
R&D funding for technology development—have been directed toward shorter-duration storage
projects. Although this does not necessarily reflect skewed priorities, California remains one of
the only states with a dedicated program to support LDES projects, with certain qualifications for
siting of those projects. The question of R&D funding may be particularly significant for LDES
systems for which technological advances could help lower costs by optimizing and improving
design and manufacturing processes (Scott et al. 2023).

Broader policy decisions can impact the realization of LDES potential on the grid. In particular,
other grid improvements could impact the ability for LDES resources to realize the value they can
already provide. However, it is uncertain to what extent these grid improvements will happen. For
example, efforts to reduce transmission congestion can have a marked impact on the ability of
LDES to contribute to grid flexibility and reliability services (Denholm, Cole, and Blair 2023).
This is important because the realization of LDES value is likely related to the composition of the
resource mix in which it operates (Staadecker et al. 2024).

2 Future Work
The results of the survey and interviews identified major gaps in LDES grid integration,
summarized as follows:

• Planning tools for capacity expansion, resource adequacy, and production cost models have
 challenged modeling multi-day energy storage. In particular, the reliability, adequacy, and
 resilience contributions of LDES are not fully captured.
• Other energy storage benefits such as non-wire alternatives, congestion relief, synchronous
 inertia, short-circuit current capability, and voltage support are not valued or compensated.
• Existing resource adequacy studies may not capture extreme events lasting multiple days.
• There is a lack of products and mechanisms in ISO/RTO markets to fully value multi-day
 energy availability.
• LDES includes a variety of technologies with different capabilities, performance, and
 technology readiness levels. The industry lacks reliability, safety, and performance data to
 properly model the performance and availability of different LDES technologies. There is a
 need to conduct more LDES pilot projects and to collect and share operational data from these
 projects.
• LDES starts to emerge in some areas. However, the grid operators are not well prepared for
 these new resources.
• There is a lack of long-term contracts and bankability to support LDES investments.
• The survey participants ranked gaps in capturing the values of LDES in current resource
 adequacy planning: cost-benefit analysis, market competitiveness, policy and regulatory
 alignment, reliability and resilience metrics, scalability, and flexibility. They equally weighed
 the areas for improvement: inadequate standards or methods for reliability metrics, inadequate
 or lack of resiliency standards under extreme events, and planning tools and data
 improvements to capture LDES resource adequacy value under normal and extreme
 conditions.
• For operational time frames, the survey participants ranked multi-day markets or products
 very highly, followed by incorporating LDES into multi-day operations scheduling and
 quantifying multi-day uncertainty in operations.
• The survey results also demonstrated that traditional planning tools ignore operational details.
 These details include forecast errors, operational limitations and inefficiencies, impact of
 transmission constraints, storage constraints, and so on. The LDES modeling in integrated
 resource planning is very imprecise. The value of different types of LDES in different
 applications must be clearer.
• The ISOs acknowledged the technology gaps in existing planning and operational tools and
 methodologies for modeling LDES. They also noted scheduling and pricing challenges in
 wholesale electricity markets with LDES. The general consensus is addressing these gaps will
 require a significant technology investment. However, most ISOs have not seen LDES in the
 generation interconnection queue. Therefore, there is a lack of incentives in the near term to
 address the technology gaps.

The results of the survey and interviews show the motivation to develop next-generation planning
and operational and market tools to assess emerging technologies and to ensure the reliability and
resilience of the future grid. Therefore, future work can enhance the methods and tools for
planning, scheduling, and pricing to manage multi-day uncertainty and evaluate LDES, with the
goal of developing an open-source modeling and evaluation framework to enhance future
planning, operations and market design.

 11

Further research could focus on a framework to support the quantification of LDES contributions
to the grid, including resource adequacy, reliability, and resiliency values. This could address the
gaps and needs identified through our stakeholder outreach and literature review.

Appendix. Stakeholder Outreach Efforts
Our multi-laboratory team conducted stakeholder outreach efforts by disseminating an online
survey and conducting 1:1 outreach (interviews or individual surveys) with independent system
operators (ISOs) and regional transmission organizations (RTOs) in the United States and
Canada.

The online survey was designed to solicit responses from stakeholders from utilities, industry,
technology vendors, and regulatory agencies who could provide different perspectives on gaps
and barriers to long-duration energy storage (LDES) deployment. The survey was disseminated in
October 2024 and remained open until the end of February 2025, with a total of 38 responses, as
shown in Figure A-1. Our multi-laboratory team also encouraged respondents to share the survey
with others.

Our multi-laboratory team also interviewed and/or corresponded via email with grid operators
from the United States and Canada to gather insights on various aspects of LDES deployment,
with a focus on understanding any challenges from a grid integration perspective. This outreach
was structured by a preset list of interview questions. Our team collected responses from all seven
U.S. ISOs (CAISO, ERCOT, ISO-NE, MISO, PJM, NYISO, SPP) and IESO in Canada.

A.3 Detailed Findings From Stakeholder Outreach
Detailed descriptions of survey and grid operator responses from both types of stakeholder
outreach efforts are organized by topic area in the following subsections.

Current System Roles for Long-Duration Energy Storage

The status of LDES ownership, operation, and regulation is shown in Figure A-2. Only 17% both
own and operate an LDES device, and another 10% operate an LDES owned by another entity.
An additional 21% indicated they have plans in the near future for LDES devices. Overall, about
half the respondents indicated a current or future involvement with LDES devices. This position
is reinforced by the participants’ anticipation of the evolution of LDES roles over the next 5
years, as depicted in Figure A-3. Almost 75% of respondents expect the LDES role to increase
over the next year. This group was divided on the level of role increase into roughly equal groups:
significant increase (36%) and moderate increase (39%). The former group of respondents
highlighted the major technological gaps and the urgent need to identify the largest impact areas
to support the grid needs and key stakeholders, such as utilities may still be waiting for the
technology to mature before becoming more invested in LDES. Once LDES becomes readily
available in competitive marketplaces, it is expected to play a leading role in complementing
other resources. The latter group shared the views on LDES current costs as a major limiting

 22

factor for widespread adoption. In addition, they stated LDES can provide value by maintaining
grid reliability in the future. The remaining respondents indicated they are still evaluating or will
not deploy LDES within their organizations, but they still recognize the potential LDES values.

 Figure A-3. Evolution of LDES roles over the next 5 years

Currently, most grid operators, including ISO-NE, MISO, NYISO, PJM, SPP, CAISO, and IESO,
have pumped storage hydro (PSH) in their systems. PSH can deliver several market products and
is considered a valuable flexibility resource. However, the high costs associated with LDES and
the gaps in quantifying the resource adequacy contribution discourage new development. Further
efforts are needed to enhance market attractiveness.

Despite having 10 GW of storage capacity at 1.5-hour durations in the ERCOT system, mainly
providing ancillary service, there are very few assets with longer durations because of a lack of
strong market signals. Several operators have a long energy storage interconnection queue, about
100 gigawatts (GW) in ERCOT and 60 GW in PJM; none of the resources exceed 4 hours in
duration.

The need for long-duration services is more critical during winter events, such as extended cold
spells, compared to the summer peaks, which typically require only 3 to 4 hours of energy

 23

service. There is general agreement that LDES could be a valuable resource for energy and
flexibility, especially to cover the multi-day extreme weather events (e.g., winter storm Uri).
However, the high cost of LDES, combined with the limitations in the planning tools and
accreditation methods, hinders the ability of modeling tools to select the new LDES
development.

Some ISOs consider energy storage as a non-wire alternative. However, the duration of storage is
not differentiated. Additional work is needed to properly model storage dispatch to capture the
whole value stack of LDES.

Anticipated Values of Long-Duration Energy Storage

Survey respondents were asked about the LDES services in which their organization currently
participates or plans to participate, as shown in Figure A-4. Resource adequacy was the
highest service category (31%), followed by energy arbitrage (28%) and regulating reserve
ancillary services (21%). About 17% of the respondents indicated LDES could participate in all
listed grid services. The respondents also underlined the role LDES can play in increasing the
share of large-scale variable renewable energy (VRE) installations. However, several respondents
indicated the need to improve capacity expansion modeling and production cost modeling to
better understand the economic and reliability contributions of these new technologies.

 Figure A-4. Current and future services provision using LDES

Because some LDES technologies are still in research and development and/or early stages of
commercialization, there is considerable uncertainty regarding which technologies to invest in to
reduce costs. Overall, survey respondents held an optimistic view of the versatile roles and
multiple services LDES can provide. However, there was some ambiguity in survey feedback
regarding the precise quantification of LDES because of current progress and maturity levels as
compared to more established technologies currently available in the market.

When surveyed individuals were asked about how LDES systems might evolve in their
organization over the next 5 years, 32% agreed the role for LDES will be significantly expanded,
and another 32% envisioned moderate growth. Only 8% considered LDES adoption will remain
at current levels.

These responses reflect both optimism and caution about LDES adoption over the next 5 years.
Some organizations, including developers and universities, anticipate growth and view LDES as

 24

key to meeting climate goals and expanding across sectors. Others are still evaluating its
potential, particularly at the distribution level. However, cost and financial viability remain major
concerns. Many respondents stressed the need to prove LDES reliability, stability, and safety,
similar to the long path lithium-ion took to become bankable. Some expect large-scale
deployment only in the 2030s. Utilities’ preference for proven technologies and the lack of clear
market incentives further slow adoption. In addition, some respondents expected continued
reliance on natural gas and short-duration storage, questioning LDES’s near-term market
viability. Despite these challenges, some in insurance and investment foresee industry growth as
LDES matures.

Figure A-5 depicts the ranking matrix of gaps in capturing the LDES value in current resource
adequacy planning processes, where darker shades of blue indicate a higher gap level for each
category. Most responses indicated the cost-benefit analysis process, by which stakeholders
evaluate total costs and benefits, including capital and operational expenses, has a high gap value
(4 or 5). This result emphasizes the opinions of stakeholders that lack of accurate cost models and
a thorough understanding of the LDES economics are fundamental barriers against large-scale
deployment. In general, many stakeholders ranked all categories with a relatively high gap level
(3 or above). Key challenges include recognition of all values and development of consequent
plans for payment. Participants also expected energy arbitrage and capacity to account for the
largest share of LDES revenue in a future electricity system. Some stakeholders also remarked
that energy arbitrage opportunities may be fewer as the grid saturates with storage resources. In
contrast, grid inertia and grid resilience were identified as the largest uncompensated grid services
in the resource adequacy processes and competitive markets today.

 Figure A-5. Ranking of gaps in capturing LDES values in the current resource adequacy planning
 process

Some questions asked about the grid services for the largest share of LDES revenue in a future
electricity system but largely uncompensated in the current system. The resultant survey
responses highlighted a broad consensus that LDES will derive its largest share of revenue
primarily from energy arbitrage, capacity markets, and operating reserves. Many respondents
emphasized that critical role of LDES in bulk energy shifting, particularly during renewable
shortfalls, ensuring grid reliability and resilience. Although energy arbitrage remains a key value
stream, some noted that, as more storage is integrated into the grid, arbitrage opportunities may
decline because of market saturation. Capacity markets, particularly those designed to account for

 25

the unique characteristics of LDES, are frequently referred to as essential revenue sources. In
addition, several responses underscored the importance of strategic reserves and resource
adequacy mechanisms to properly value LDES contributions.

Beyond traditional market compensation, respondents identified several uncompensated or
undercompensated grid services that LDES provides. Many highlighted the role of LDES in
supporting grid resilience, ensuring system adequacy during prolonged periods of low renewable
output, and deferring costly transmission investments. Several respondents pointed out LDES
with rotating machinery could replace the synchronous inertia, frequency support, and short-
circuit current capabilities historically provided by fossil fuel plants. Other undercompensated
services included enabling deeper renewable penetration, providing emergency capacity, and
enhancing system flexibility. Some respondents expressed concern that current market structures
do not fully recognize these benefits. They called for the development of new market
mechanisms, such as revised resource adequacy planning and new incentive structures, to fairly
compensate LDES for its contributions.

Grid operators underlined the potential value of LDES for various system services, including
reliability and resiliency. Moreover, in rich VRE power grids, LDES could increase the credited
capacity of VRE, alleviate the uncertainty in real-time operations, and reduce energy curtailment.

CAISO has LDES resources currently seeking interconnection and commercial operation by the
end of 2026. CAISO anticipates more LDES resources as part of the California Public Utilities
Commission (CPUC)/California Department of Water Resources (CDWR) LDES procurement
targets of 2 GW to be online between 2031 and 2037. IESO has a new 1,000-megawatt (MW)
pumped energy storage project currently under system impact assessment.

The ISOs acknowledge the technology gaps in existing planning and operational tools and
methodologies for modeling LDES. The general consensus is that it will require a big technology
investment to address these gaps. However, most ISOs have not seen LDES in the generation
interconnection queue. Therefore, there is a lack of incentive to address the technology gaps.

Over the next 10 years, some ISOs expect to replace coal generation with photovoltaics and
energy storage and believe that 8- to 10-hour storage will be useful during nights.

ISOs with high percentages of gas resources are concerned about gas availability, especially
during long periods of winter events. Seasonal storage is needed to address the gas availability
issue. Short-duration storage will not be helpful. LDES may potentially be valuable in addressing
seasonal or multi-day energy adequacy issues. However, LDES is not considered in the tools or
processes because it is unclear how LDES will operate in a highly uncertain environment, and
most of the generation interconnection queues do not have LDES.

For ISOs with high storage participation, as more storage is on the grid, the opportunity for
energy arbitrage may decrease. Ancillary service prices are also anticipated to decrease because
short-duration storage may saturate these markets. LDES may provide high-value services during
prolonged periods of renewable energy droughts or avoid transmission capacity buildout to meet
peak demand.

Several ISOs anticipate LDES will play a crucial role in providing reliability and resiliency to
meet multi-day energy adequacy. LDES is dispatchable and can be used for all products. The low
round-trip efficiency makes it difficult to make profit through energy arbitrage. Ancillary service
markets are relatively small. ISOs noted potential new services for LDES:

 26

 1. The evolving standards on energy assurance may create opportunities for new market
 products, potentially enabling LDES to participate. This includes evaluating the use of
 EUE compared to the existing “1-day-in-10-year” LOLE standard. Some regions are
 exploring new reliability metrics that consider the frequency, duration, and magnitude
 of unserved energy.
 2. LDES may also qualify for providing black start (i.e., the process of restarting an
 electric power system after a complete blackout), supporting voltage at safe levels and
 contributing to system inertia. However, these services are mostly not compensated
 today.
In general, ISOs agree new resource adequacy methods and new market products must be
developed to compensate for the values provided by LDES.

Challenges in Grid Planning, Operations, and Market Design for Integrating Long-
Duration Energy Storage

Surveyed individuals were asked open-ended questions regarding (1) whether current
mechanisms for capacity compensation capture the resource adequacy value of LDES, (2) who
manages the state of charge (SOC) for storage devices, and (3) how operational scheduling
and SOC management should improve.

Cost was frequently mentioned as a key challenge for deployment of LDES among survey
respondents. Other challenges mentioned included a lack of knowledge around LDES
capabilities along with limited capabilities of models to capture the LDES values. Regarding
the role of PUCs, some survey respondents argued PUCs need to increase their knowledge of
this emerging technology and employ adequate modeling tools in LDES assessments. Other
respondents argued that a separate category is needed for LDES, different from generation,
transmission, and distribution. In contrast, some respondents argued that PUCs should
continue to focus on system costs and that no specific changes are required for LDES.
Regarding the question of what has helped in the past for utilities to become more familiar and
comfortable with new technologies, the most frequent response, by far, was pilot programs to
demonstrate the capabilities of new technologies.

Survey respondents expressed inadequate standards, methods, tools, and data for reliability
metrics and resource adequacy under both normal and extreme events. Moreover, there was
broad consensus that current capacity remuneration mechanisms do not capture the full
resource adequacy value that LDES can provide. Respondents often cited that current
capacity rules are biased toward shorter-duration storage technologies and/or reflect a role for
storage resources only during peak system load time frames, which are intraday and often
only a few hours in the afternoon. One respondent noted the need for LDES is not necessarily
evident without accounting for changing weather patterns and extreme weather events.

Finally, survey respondents ranked research priorities needed to address the challenges
associated with managing LDES operations over multi-day periods. The results are
summarized in Figure A-6.

Grid operators were asked several questions regarding the challenges they face in integrating
LDES into the planning and operational processes, including whether current resource adequacy
processes and/or capacity markets support LDES deployment, market incentives for LDES,
management of multi-day uncertainty, and any other challenges regarding scheduling and
pricing storage.

Several ISOs noted current capacity rules are biased toward shorter-duration storage, such as 2 to
4 hours. They were unsure what the incentive or price signal for longer-duration resources such as
LDES would be. Current capacity accreditation processes may not sufficiently credit LDES or
assign higher relative value compared to shorter-duration resources. The currently high
investment cost for LDES was frequently cited as a core challenge for generating the right
incentives to recoup these costs. ISOs with capacity markets that have some LDES presence in
auctions did not observe sufficient price signals for their investment and deployment.

Interviewees also discussed the role of proper valuation and modeling challenges for
integrating LDES into the grid—for example, the lack of production and real-world
operational data, which constrains realistic modeling approaches and creates uncertainty
around the value that LDES could capture. Many ISOs stated they lack an ability to capture or
adequately handle uncertainty in planning tools and thus risk undervaluing LDES. The lack of a
time period for the capacity expansion models and the fact that planning models use perfect
foresight are major concerns; if uncertainty were included in planning, LDES resources may be
perceived as more valuable.

ISOs varied according to the structures they have in place for managing the multi-day
uncertainties and plans to develop multi-day markets or products. Most ISOs run a nonbinding
multi-day unit commitment process to ensure sufficient operational reserve margin to manage
uncertainties. However, none of the ISOs has a near-term plan to develop a multi-day market
because of the settlement complexity and high uncertainties in input data. ISO-NE conducts a 3-
week energy assessment every week during the winter, which serves as a situational awareness
tool.

In most ISOs, market participants manage the SOC for storage. Market participants have the
ability to change their schedules and may cause uncertainty to operators. Nearly all ISOs
indicated they would anticipate challenges in scheduling and pricing storage in their respective
systems. Existing market clearing and operational processes and tools have challenges because of

the temporal dependency of schedules, which are hard to model given the limited optimization
window in operations.

Long-Duration Energy Storage Modeling Gaps

Surveyed individuals were asked several questions about the technological gaps and
recommended improvements to planning processes and modeling that could enable and/or
accelerate LDES deployment. Figure A-7 shows respondents’ selection of various
explanations for current gaps in resource adequacy.

 Figure A-7. Gaps in resource adequacy processes in valuing LDES

Surveyed individuals were also asked open-ended questions about current strengths and/or
weaknesses in planning and operational tools in terms of their ability to represent LDES.
Respondents frequently cited improvements to production cost modeling as a barrier to
demonstrating and thus realizing LDES value. Some respondents noted that market clearing
models—and market structures—must be able to handle more attributes, whereas others stated
that existing market clearing models are sufficient. Some mentioned the unrealistic conflation of
assessment methods between short- and long-duration storage technologies as a barrier to
accurate representation of storage resources in modeling.

When asked for recommendations to improve system planning tools and processes needed to
capture LDES value and accelerate adoption, survey respondents provided several suggestions:

• Representing operational details and flexibility needs adequately
• Quantifying the reliability and resiliency value
• Understanding the interactions between LDES deployment and other opportunities for grid
 developments (such as better integration with distributed energy resources)
• Differentiating short- and long-duration storage
• Identifying and incorporating a representation of extreme events that LDES could help
 respond to.

 29

Grid operators have identified several key improvements and innovations needed in the
planning process to better anticipate frequent grid disruptions and recognize the value of
longer-duration storage. These improvements include more detailed modeling of LDES,
incorporating energy limits and other intertemporal constraints into resource adequacy tools,
considering rare extreme scenarios, developing and enhancing effective load-carrying
capability methods for storage, modeling operational uncertainty, and evaluating LDES as
potential transmission assets.

Grid operators have identified several gaps in existing operational and market clearing tools for
managing LDES.

Scheduling LDES requires more accurate multi-day forecasting. For instance, scheduling a 100-
hour LDES system may require a 2-week forecast. Key uncertainties include forecast errors,
gas availability, outage scheduling, temperature-dependent forced outages, congestion, and so
on. ISOs also emphasized the importance of predicting system-wide and zonal extreme
conditions.

Current multi-stage operational and market clearing models often have shorter evaluation
periods near the operation day, limiting their ability to capture the trade-offs associated with
charging and discharging LDES. These models need to incorporate storage opportunity costs.
Effective market design is critical to creating incentives and proper pricing mechanisms,
particularly when anticipated uncertainties do not materialize.

Advanced computational tools and AI can be leveraged to enhance uncertainty modeling and
improve decision-making.

Policy Support

Surveyed individuals were asked several questions about how current policies and regulations
support LDES deployment, what grid services may account for LDES revenue in future scenarios,
how to adapt PUC practices to account for LDES, what are strategies for familiarizing utilities
with LDES, and what are ways to consider the environment and social impacts of LDES.

When asked to elaborate on how well LDES systems align with existing policies, survey
respondents described the need for policies to demonstrate the value of LDES, new market
products and/or market reforms, and alignment of planning processes to reflect long-duration
capabilities. One respondent wrote that substantive funding for pilot demonstration projects and
collaboration with utilities—who can offer data, ideas, and solutions—could spur LDES
development. Survey respondents also repeated that high investment costs pose a challenge to
deployment and competition in wholesale markets. One respondent wrote that “cost is
everything,” and another noted a “key gap” to LDES deployment includes a lack of financial
compensation for the comprehensive set of values LDES can add to a grid system, emphasizing
different types of frequency response and voltage support.

Respondents discussed grid services LDES could provide in future systems, with many
respondents citing energy arbitrage, capacity, and ancillary services. Others mentioned services
such as peak reduction capacity, bulk energy shifting, avoided transmission capacity buildout, and
general contributions to grid resilience. One respondent noted longer-duration storage can be used
during prolonged periods of renewable energy droughts. Another respondent wrote that tying

 30

LDES use cases to decarbonization may be counterproductive, suggesting there could still be
significant value for LDES in the absence of aggressive decarbonization efforts.

When asked about the primary challenges associated with deploying LDES in systems operated
by regulated utilities, respondents frequently highlighted economic gaps, including compensation
and failure to recognize LDES value. One respondent noted that “regulated utilities will want to
build whatever the local PUCs will allow them to make money on,” highlighting the role of cost-
of-service regulation in technology investment decisions. Another respondent also highlighted the
importance of understanding how LDES fits into traditional utility business models. A few
respondents noted that LDES technologies are nascent and proving and demonstrating the
technology are current challenges. This again underscores the fact that utilities and regulators
want performance guarantees.

Respondents provided several ideas and varied feedback on how PUCs should adopt current
practices to account for a changing resource mix that includes LDES. One respondent suggested
PUCs are made ineffective because of the lack of LDES commercial availability. Another
respondent suggested adjusting prudency standards to include LDES, which may not decrease
electricity rates but may enable lower-cost forms of variable renewable energy generation.
Another respondent proposed allowing resources to receive greater compensation for flexibility
services, including long-duration flexibility. In general, respondents emphasized PUCs should
differentiate and/or create new categories for events of different durations, paving the way for
LDES procurement.

Some respondents noted the deployment challenge for LDES requires making more stakeholders
aware of the services LDES can provide and their value to long-term reliability planning. This
could include steps such as highlighting successful demonstrations, instituting permissions for
first-of-its-kind projects, and strengthening collaboration across different stakeholders that hold
sway over LDES deployment decisions. Several respondents cited pilot and/or demonstration
projects as a way to elevate the visibility and reliability of LDES resources. One respondent also
cited a bias toward the status quo as a possible barrier and noted effective technical
communication is required to make the case for LDES technologies to those unfamiliar with the
services they can provide.

One ISO noted policy support is critical to make LDES economical. The same ISO responded that
state policy may be constraining widespread use of batteries because they are not eligible for
some incentives offered by the state government. The lack of a capacity market in some ISOs can
be a contributing factor in limiting LDES deployment. In terms of the environmental and social
impacts, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency rules limiting or otherwise impacting the
share of coal and gas generation could force the introduction of LDES. In terms of market design,
day-ahead scheduling may provide sufficient arbitrage for storage resources with 10-hour
duration to buy electricity when prices are low and sell when prices are high. However, without
multi-day markets, price signals are missing for longer-duration resources.

Another ISO said developing policies to compensate LDES based on their value to the grid can
enhance LDES’ competitiveness with traditional resources in the wholesale market. Supporting
R&D efforts to reduce costs, advancing dynamic operating capabilities to align resources with
grid needs, and promoting demonstration initiatives can also be highly beneficial. Clear policies
and incentives are essential but appear to be lacking. Conducting a thorough benefit analysis—
highlighting the problems being addressed, the advantages over current grid resources, and
demonstrating clear use cases—can accelerate deployment for future projects.
