On Worst-Case Optimal Polynomial Intersection
Yihang Sun, Mary Wootters

Abstract
The Optimal Polynomial Intersection (OPI) problem is the following: Given sets S 1 , … , S m ⊆ 𝔽 S_{1},\ldots,S_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{F} and evaluation points a 1 , … , a m ∈ 𝔽 a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\in\mathbb{F} , find a polynomial Q ∈ 𝔽 ⁡ [ x ] Q\in\mathbb{F}[x] of degree less than n n so that Q ⁡ ( a i ) ∈ S i Q(a_{i})\in S_{i} for as many i ∈ { 1 , 2 , … , m } i\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\} as possible. Decoded Quantum Interferometry (DQI) is a quantum algorithm that efficiently returns good solutions to the problem, even on worst-case instances [ 13 ] . The quality of the solutions returned follows a semicircle law , which outperforms known efficient classical algorithms. But does DQI obtain the best possible solutions? That is, are there solutions better than the semicircle law for worst-case OPI instances? Surprisingly, before this work, the best existential results coincide with (and follow from) the best algorithmic results. In this work, we show that there are better solutions for worst-case OPI instances over prime fields. In particular, DQI and the semicircle law are not optimal. For example, when the lists S i S_{i} have size ρ ​ p \rho p for ρ ∼ 1 / 2 \rho\sim 1/2 , our results imply the existence of a solution that asymptotically beats the semicircle law whenever n / m ≥ 0.6225 n/m\geq 0.6225 , and we show that an asymptotically perfect solution exists whenever n / m ≥ 0.7496 n/m\geq 0.7496 . Our results generalize to Max-LINSAT problems derived from any Maximum Distance Separable (MDS) code, and to any ρ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \rho\in(0,1) . The key insight to our improvement is a connection to local leakage resilience of secret sharing schemes. Along the way, we recover several re-proofs of the existence of solutions achieving the semicircle law.

Introduction

The
Optimal Polynomial Intersection
(OPI) problem is to find a low-degree polynomial that satisfies a large number of constraints on its evaluations. Formally, we have the following definition.

Problem 1.1

(Optimal Polynomial Intersection)
.

Fix prime power
q
and integers
0\leq n\leq m\leq q
, and fix distinct evaluation points
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
The
Optimal Polynomial Intersection
(OPI) problem (with respect to
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}
) is the following: Given input subsets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{m}
with
S_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}
, find a polynomial
Q\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
with

that maximizes the

s(Q)\coloneqq\frac{1}{m}|\{i\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}\,:\,Q(a_{i})\in S_{i}\}|.

OPI arises as a natural problem in many different areas. In coding theory, it is related to
list-recovery
of Reed-Solomon codes: There, the goal is to show that there are not too many polynomials
Q
of degree less than
n
so that
s(Q)\geq\alpha
(for some parameter
\alpha
), and to return them all. In cryptography, OPI has been studied under the name
noisy polynomial reconstruction/interpolation
(e.g.,
[
22
,
5
]
), and has been considered as a hardness assumption in certain parameter regimes. More recently, OPI has arisen as a potential demonstration of quantum advantage: The
Decoded Quantum Interferometry
(DQI) algorithm
[
13
]
and related algorithms
[
7
,
8
,
27
,
15
]
give quantum algorithms to solve OPI that out-perform known efficient classical heuristics.

The guarantees of DQI for OPI hold in the worst case: For
any
input sets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{m}
, DQI finds a polynomial
Q
of degree less than
n
so that the fraction of satisfied constraints approaches a
semicircle law
. More precisely, for
\mu\in[0,1/2]
and
\rho\in(0,1)
,

\operatorname{SCL}_{\rho}(\mu)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\mu(1-\rho)}+\sqrt{\rho(1-\mu)}}}\right)^{2}&\text{if }\mu+\rho\leq 1\\
1&\text{if }\mu+\rho\geq 1\end{cases}.

(1.1)

Then, as

with fixed ratio
\mu=n/2m
, for any

of size
\rho q
,
[
13
]
shows that DQI efficiently finds a polynomial

\mathbb{E}[s(Q)]\geq\operatorname{SCL}_{\rho}(\mu)-o(1).

Above, the expectation is over the randomness of the algorithm. When

, we have

\operatorname{SCL}_{1/2}(\mu)=\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{\mu(1-\mu)},

the equation of a semicircle. In contrast, the best known classical heuristic (Prange’s algorithm) is only able to obtain a satisfaction ratio of
1/2+\mu+o(1)
, even on average-case instances.

A natural question is how the semicircle law compares to the best possible solution to OPI. Does DQI find optimal solutions in the worst case? Before this work, it was not known if there were
any
better solutions in the worst case.

Question 1.2
.

Fix
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}

\rho\in(0,1)
and

|S_{i}|=\rho q\end{subarray}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}Q\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{[}x]\\

We note that prior work
[
7
,
8
]
obtained (algorithmic) improvements on the semicircle law for OPI; however those works consider the average case over an ensemble of OPI instances, not worst-case OPI instances as we do here. We discuss the relationship to these works in
Section

Our contributions.

Our contributions are twofold.

•

Our main results, discussed quantitatively below in

Remark 1.4
.

All of our results hold for the general MDS Max-LINSAT problem for

Two re-proofs of the semicircle law, and what we learn from them

Our starting point is the observation that the semicircle law attained by DQI in

In

(over the randomness of

1.2.2
Connection to leakage-resilient secret sharing

To control

, we observe in

a surprising connection to

of Shamir secret sharing. By

1.13

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathbb{E}[q_{t}(x)]}}\right|=2^{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sum_{y\in C^{\perp}_{t}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}}}(y_{i})}}\right|.

(1.19)

It turns out that this is exactly the sort of expression that has been controlled in the literature on local leakage resilience in secret sharing.
While the literature typically considers
Shamir sharing

[
29
]
—which corresponds to OPI—here we discuss the more general
Massey secret sharing schemes

[
21
]
—which correspond to MDS Max-LINSAT—to better match the notation in the rest of this section.

To share a secret
s
among
m
parties, Massey’s scheme (over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, with respect to an MDS code
C\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{m}
of dimension
n
) chooses a random codeword
c\in C
so that
c_{0}=s
, and gives the
i
’th symbol
c_{i}
to party
i
. Shamir’s scheme is the special case when
C
is a Reed-Solomon code.

By the MDS property, any
n
parties can recover the secret, while any
n-1
learn nothing; this is the desired guarantee in (threshold) secret sharing. But what happens if more than
n
parties leak a single bit?
Formally, we say that a scheme is
one-bit local-leakage resilient
if the following holds. Suppose that each party can leak a single bit, either
\pm 1
; this results in a
leakage transcript

L(s)\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
, which is a random variable, whose distribution depends on
s
. Then, for any distinct secrets
s\neq s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
, the total variation distance
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(L(s),L(s^{\prime}))
should be small, at most
2^{-\Omega(m)}
.

Over extension fields, it turns out that Shamir’s scheme is
not
one-bit leakage resilient
[
12
,
30
]
. However, over prime fields, it turns out that it is, at least for high enough rates
n/m
.
In more detail, let
L
denote the leakage transcript under a sharing of a uniformly random secret, and let
L^{\prime}
denote the corresponding transcript in the case where all the parties have an independent uniformly random share (that have nothing to do with any secret). It turns out that, to establish one-bit leakage resilience, it is sufficient to show that the total variation distance
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(L,L^{\prime})\leq 2^{-\Omega(m)}
is negligibly small. It is shown in
[
4
, Lemma 4.14]
using Fourier analysis that

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(L,L^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sum_{y\in C^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\hat{\mathbf{1}}_{S_{i}^{(\ell_{i})}}(y_{i})}}\right|,

(1.20)

where
S_{i}^{(\ell_{i})}
is the set of shares for party
i
consistent with the leaked bit
\ell_{i}
.
The expression
Equation

1.20
looks like
Equation

1.19
, except that it is summed up over all possible transcripts
\ell\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
. That is,
the Fourier sum in
Equation

1.19
is essentially the
per-transcript leakage
of Massey’s scheme, and this quantity is bounded in
[
4
,
19
,
18
]
in the context of local leakage resilience.

We could use these existing bounds in an off-the-shelf way with the framework described in
Section

1.2.1
. Using the best bound on

1.19
from
[

]
already yields some improvement on the semicircle law; this off-the-shelf improvement is plotted as the green curve in

is exactly the same as the leakage resilience threshold in

In this work, we improve on existing techniques to obtain our improved bounds,

and

The idea of

into two groups

By Cauchy-Schwarz,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sum_{y\in C^{\perp}_{t}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}}}(y_{i})}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{y\in C^{\perp}_{t}}\prod_{i\in L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}}}(y_{i})}}\right|^{2}}}\right)^{1/2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{y\in C^{\perp}_{t}}\prod_{i\in R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}}}(y_{i})}}\right|^{2}}}\right)^{1/2}\max_{y\in C^{\perp}_{t}}\prod_{i\in B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}}}(y_{i})}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{y\in C^{\perp}}\prod_{i\in L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}}}(y_{i})}}\right|^{2}}}\right)^{1/2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{y\in C^{\perp}}\prod_{i\in R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}}}(y_{i})}}\right|^{2}}}\right)^{1/2}\max_{y\in C^{\perp}_{t}}\prod_{i\in B}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}}}(y_{i})}}\right|

It is natural to ask whether these improvements can feed back into the leakage-resilient secret sharing literature. The answer is yes and no: The answer is yes because our bounds do imply improved bounds on the Fourier proxy

1.3
Related Work

In this section, we put our results and techniques in the context of related work.

1.3.1
DQI and algorithms based on Regev’s reduction

OPI has been studied recently as a target of quantum algorithms, including DQI
[
13
]
and related algorithms also based on ideas from Regev’s reduction
[
26
]
, including
[
7
,
8
,
15
,
27
]
.

First, we mention the relationship between our re-proofs of the semicircle law and existing work. As discussed above, the analysis of DQI, as well as our re-proofs of
Proposition

1.10
that only use the moments of

of order up to

, all encounter the semicircle law barrier. This is because the proofs boil down to constructing a polynomial of degree at most
n
to certify that

cannot be too small. Applying this polynomial certificate construction to coding theory dates back to
[
31
]
on the covering radius problem, where the bound is also given by a semicircle law. Indeed, it is observed in
[
20
]
that DQI makes this approach algorithmic, that sampling a state of the form
Equation

1.12
with the best weights

can be thought of as the construction of such a polynomial certificate. In
[
20
]
, further connections of the DQI algorithm and
[
31
]
via the MacWilliams identities are discussed and DQI is shown to be simulated in a low level of the polynomial hierarchy, ruling out certain hardness arguments.

Next, we discuss results beyond the semicircle law. To the best of our knowledge, there were previously no results, algorithmic or otherwise, that yielded an improvement on
worst-case
instances. However, there are average-case results, both for the case of uniformly random lists
[
13
]
and for a more structured random ensemble
[
7
,
8
]
described below.

The idea of choosing
\ell>d^{\perp}/2
to gain further performance improvement is already present in
[
13
, Section 10]
. Motivated by empirical performance of belief propagation on LDPC codes, the authors observe over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
that DQI has strong performance guarantees in the
average
case where each singleton input list
S_{i}
is a uniform random bit, provided the decoder of
C^{\perp}
works beyond
d^{\perp}/2
with small failure probabilities. However, as presented in
[
13
]
, this approach applies only over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, and thus not to the OPI problem.

The idea of choosing
\ell>d^{\perp}/2
is also present in
[
7
,
8
]
. DQI can be seen as an instantiation of Regev’s reduction
[
26
]
. The works
[
7
,
8
]
combine similar ideas of Regev’s reduction with soft-decoders to give quantum algorithms that improve on DQI on average for the following structured ensemble: Lists
\tilde{S}_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}
are chosen in a worst-case way, and the final input lists
S_{i}
are given by
S_{i}=\tilde{S}_{i}+e_{i}
, where
e_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
are independent uniformly random shifts. This is essentially a hybrid between the worst-case and the (uniform) average-case considered in
[
13
]
. In this hybrid case and assuming
\rho=1/2
, the strongest algorithm
[
8
, Algorithm 4]
uses the Koetter-Vardy soft decoder and finds a solution with satisfaction ratio at least

whenever the rate is at least

. As noted in

from

[

]
in this parameter regime, even ignoring the difference between worst-case and the hybrid model. On the other hand, our result is not algorithmic, while that of
[
8
]
is; and moreover in the low-rate regime the results of
[

]
are quantitatively stronger than our results.

However, in some sense the results described above are orthogonal to our work. We focus on existential guarantees for worst-case input lists
S_{i}
. In both the (uniformly) average case and the hybrid model discussed above, a simple first-moment argument shows the existence, in expectation over the inputs, of a perfect solution that satisfies every constraint. From this perspective, the difficulty we tackle is to extend the argument to the worst-case input lists
S_{i}
. We expand on this point in

, where we see the obstacle to making our result algorithmic disappears in the hybrid case setup of

,
8
]
and when

1.3.2
Local Leakage Resilience

As mentioned above, our key technical insights are inspired by the literature on local leakage resilience in secret sharing.
We briefly summarize the relevant literature. As above, we use the language of Massey’s secret sharing scheme (rather than Shamir) to better match our notation.

We have already defined one-bit local leakage-resilience above.
The main conjecture in this setting, first stated for Shamir’s scheme, is the following.

(
[

)

For lower bounds,

For upper bounds,

We make a few remarks about the relationship between existing work on leakage-resilient secret sharing and our work on OPI.

First, as discussed in

Second, as mentioned in

Proposition

1.3.3
Other work on OPI

As mentioned at the beginning of the paper, OPI and related problems arise in many domains. However, to the best of our knowledge, none of these (except the work on DQI and related algorithms already discussed in
Section

1.3.1
) are concerned with
1.2
. We briefly discuss two related areas below: list-recovery of Reed-Solomon codes, and OPI as a cryptographic assumption.

In the coding theory literature, OPI arises in the context of
list-recovery
of Reed-Solomon (RS) codes. We say that a code
C\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{m}
is
(\alpha,\ell,L)
-list-recoverable
if, for any
S_{1},\ldots,S_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
each of size at most
\ell

L
codewords

so that

for at least a
\alpha
fraction of

. The algorithmic problem is to efficiently output all at-most-
L
such codewords. In the language of OPI, when the code
C
is a RS code, the list-recovery problem is to return all polynomials
Q
of degree less than
n
so that the satisfaction ratio
s(Q)
is at least
\alpha
.

List-recovery of RS codes (and related codes) has been extensively studied; for example, the celebrated Guruswami-Sudan algorithm
[
11
]
list-recovers RS codes up to a limit on
\alpha
known as the
Johnson bound
. The work
[
10
]
showed that, for full-length RS codes over extension fields, the Johnson bound is the correct limit, in the sense that beyond that there will be an exponential number of codewords that agree a lot with the input lists
S_{i}
in the worst case. More recent work has established that RS codes with

evaluation points are list-recoverable all the way up to the information-theoretic limit on
\alpha

[
6
]
.

If

is small, an efficient list-recovery algorithm for RS codes solves OPI: simply run the algorithm with the input lists
S_{1},\ldots,S_{m}
, and iterate through the list of at most
L
options to find the best solution. However, the OPI problem is most interesting (from the perspective of potential quantum advantage) when the list size
L
is very large. In these parameter regimes, list-recovery algorithms cannot be efficient (as it would take too long to output the list). Moreover, all existing algorithms we are aware of do not “fail gracefully” in this parameter regime to return a decent solution to OPI. For example, the Guruswami-Sudan algorithm, which works by interpolating a polynomial to “explain” potential solutions, fails to interpolate an appropriate polynomial beyond the Johnson bound.

OPI has been studied in the cryptography literature under the name
noise polynomial interpolation
(or
noisy polynomial reconstruction
). For example,
[
22
]
proposed a version of OPI as a hardness assumption; later,
[
5
]
broke this assumption via a lattice attack. This setting differs from ours for two main reasons. The first is that the input lists are taken to be random, not worst-case. The second is the the parameter regime is the “planted solution” regime: the parameters are such that randomly generated lists will typically have no good solutions, and the computational challenge is to distinguish a random instance from one where a good solution is planted. In contrast, in the OPI problem we are more generally interested in finding a good solution out of potentially many good solutions.

A

2.2

The

asks whether a finite list of numbers

can be realized as the moments of a measure

]

Given the moment sequence, we can define a bilinear form on polynomials by

\langle x^{a},x^{b}\rangle\coloneqq m_{a+b}
,

\sigma

\langle P,Q\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\sigma}[P(X)Q(X)].

\ell

For a discrete distribution

\langle P,Q\rangle_{\sigma}=\sum_{x=0}^{m}\sigma(x)P(x)Q(x).

(2.1)

When

family. Since they will be key objects for us, we define them explicitly and suggestively to indicate the association.

Definition 2.2

The

K_{\ell}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}(-1)^{j}\binom{x}{j}\binom{m-x}{\ell-j}\qquad\text{and}\qquad k_{\ell}(x)\coloneqq\ell!(-2)^{-\ell}K_{\ell}(x).

They satisfy orthogonality with respect to the inner product

4.1

We begin by generalizing much of the discrepancy proof in

First, as before, with expectation over distribution
x\sim\mathbb{P}_{u}
, we can write the objective as

\mathbb{E}s(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{f_{i}(\langle b_{i},x\rangle)+1}{2}}}\right]=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\rho+\sqrt{\rho(1-\rho)}g_{i}(\langle b_{i},x\rangle)}}\right]=\rho+\frac{\sqrt{\rho(1-\rho)}}{m}\mathbb{E}[q_{1}(x)].

(4.3)

Note that in the perfectly balanced case where
\rho=1/2
, we have
g_{i}=f_{i}
, we recover the same definition of
q_{k}
, and this agrees with
Equation

3.9
. For the general
\rho
case, defining
q_{k}
based on
g_{i}
enables the following generalization of
Lemma

3.4
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
C_{t}^{\perp}\subset C^{\perp}
be codewords
y\in C^{\perp}
with Hamming weight
t
. Over uniform
x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}

\mathbb{E}[q_{t}(x)]=\sum_{y\in C_{t}^{\perp}}\prod_{i:y_{i}\neq 0}\widehat{g}_{i}(y_{i}).

(4.4)

Proof.

Temporarily let
\widehat{g}(y)=\prod_{i:y_{i}\neq 0}\widehat{g}_{i}(y_{i})
.
By definition of
q_{t}
, we compute that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[q_{t}(x)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\binom{[m]}{t}}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\prod_{i\in T}g_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\langle b_{i},x\rangle}}\right)}}\right]

(4.5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\binom{[m]}{t}}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\prod_{i\in T}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{y_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}\widehat{g_{i}}(y_{i})e_{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-y_{i}\langle b_{i},x\rangle}}\right)}}\right)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\binom{[m]}{t}}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{m}:\operatorname{supp}y=T}\widehat{g}(y)e_{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\sum_{i\in T}y_{i}\langle b_{i},x\rangle}}\right)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{m}:|y|=t}\widehat{g}(y)\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[e_{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\langle B^{\top}y,x\rangle}}\right)}}\right],

where in the last step, note that if
b_{i}
is the
i
-th row of
B
then

\sum_{i:y_{i}\neq 0}y_{i}\langle b_{i},x\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{i}B_{ij}x_{j}=\langle B^{\top}y,x\rangle.

(4.6)

Now, note that
\mathbb{E}[e_{p}(\langle z,x\rangle)]=\mathbf{1}\{z=0\}
for any
z\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
, so we get

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[q_{t}(x)}}\right]=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{m}:|y|=t}\widehat{g}(y)\mathbf{1}\{B^{T}y=0\}=\sum_{y\in C_{t}^{\perp}}\widehat{g}(y),

(4.7)

by definition of
C^{\perp}_{t}
.
∎

The proof of
Proposition

3.6
, which expresses
\mathbb{E}[q_{k_{1}}(x)\cdots q_{k_{r}}(x)]
in terms of
N(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r};t)
does not hold if we replace the
f_{i}
with the
g_{i}
, as it used the fact that the
f_{i}
were
\pm 1
-valued, and hence
f_{i}(a)^{2}=1
for all
a
. To recover something similar in spirit,
the key analogous observation is the following interpolation/degree reduction step using the fact that
g_{i}
takes
two
values.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
i\in[m]
,

g_{i}(a)^{2}=1+\beta g_{i}(a)\quad\text{where}\quad\beta\coloneqq\frac{1-2\rho}{\sqrt{\rho(1-\rho)}}.

(4.8)

Proof.

If
a\in S_{i}
, then
g_{i}(a)=\sqrt{(1-\rho)/\rho}
, and

1+\beta g_{i}(a)=1+\frac{1-2\rho}{\sqrt{\rho(1-\rho)}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1-\rho}{\rho}}=\frac{1-\rho}{\rho}=g_{i}(a)^{2}.

If
a\not\in S_{i}
, then
g_{i}(a)=-\sqrt{\rho/(1-\rho)}
, and

1+\beta g_{i}(a)=1-\frac{1-2\rho}{\sqrt{\rho(1-\rho)}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}}=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}=g_{i}(a)^{2},

as desired.
∎

This allows us to recover an analog of
Proposition

3.6
.

Proposition 4.3
.

In expectation over
x\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n})
, we have that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[q_{k}(x)q_{k^{\prime}}(x)}}\right]=\sum_{t=0}^{m}N_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime};t)\mathbb{E}[q_{t}(x)]\quad\text{where}\quad N_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime};t)=\sum_{j=0}^{t}\beta^{j}\binom{t}{j}\binom{t-j}{\frac{t+k-k^{\prime}-j}{2}}\binom{m-t}{\frac{k+k^{\prime}-t-j}{2}},

(4.9)

where we adopt the convention that binomial coefficients are zero if the arguments are not integers.

Proof.

We do a similar set-counting argument as in the proof of
Proposition

3.6
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[q_{k}(x)q_{k^{\prime}}(x)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{|T|=k,|T^{\prime}|=k^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\prod_{i\in T\sqcup T^{\prime}}g_{i}(\langle b_{i},x\rangle)}}\right]

(4.10)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|T|=k,|T^{\prime}|=k^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\prod_{i\in T\Delta T^{\prime}}g_{i}(\langle b_{i},x\rangle)\cdot\prod_{i\in T\cap T^{\prime}}g_{i}(\langle b_{i},x\rangle)^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{|T|=k,|T^{\prime}|=k^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\prod_{i\in T\Delta T^{\prime}}g_{i}(\langle b_{i},x\rangle)\cdot\prod_{i\in T\cap T^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\beta g_{i}(\langle b_{i},x\rangle)}}\right)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{|T|=k,|T^{\prime}|=k^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{J\subset T\cap T^{\prime}}\beta^{|J|}\prod_{i\in(T\Delta T^{\prime})\cup J}g_{i}(\langle b_{i},x\rangle)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{U\subset[m]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{|T|=k,|T^{\prime}|=k^{\prime}}\mathbf{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{T\Delta T^{\prime}\subset U\subset T\cup T^{\prime}}}\right\}\beta^{|U|-|T\Delta T^{\prime}|}}}\right)\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\prod_{i\in U}g_{i}(\langle b_{i},x\rangle)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{m}\mathbb{E}[q_{t}(x)]\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j=0}^{t}\beta^{j}\cdot\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(T,T^{\prime})\in\binom{[m]}{k}\times\binom{[m]}{k^{\prime}}:T\Delta T^{\prime}\subset[t]\subset T\cup T^{\prime},|T\Delta T^{\prime}|=t-j}}\right\}}}\right)

where we note that the term in the parenthesis on the penultimate line depends on
U
only through
|U|=t
, so we let
U=[t]
, and
|T\Delta T^{\prime}|=t-j
for some
0\leq j\leq t
.
To show
Equation

4.9
, we need to show the number of pairs
(T,T^{\prime})
that are counted in the final line of
Equation

4.10
is

\binom{t}{j}\binom{t-j}{\frac{t+k-k^{\prime}-j}{2}}\binom{m-t}{\frac{k+k^{\prime}-t-j}{2}}.

(4.11)

There are
\binom{t}{j}
ways to choose
T\Delta T^{\prime}
of size
t-j
from
[t]
. We know the other
j
elements of
[t]
must lie in
T\cap T^{\prime}
, which has size
(|T|+|T^{\prime}|-|T\Delta T^{\prime}|)/2=(k+k^{\prime}-t+j)/2
. We choose the remaining
(k+k^{\prime}-t-j)/2
elements of
T\cap T^{\prime}
from
[m]\setminus[t]
, which gives the last binomial coefficient in
Equation

4.11
. Finally, for each fixed
T\Delta T^{\prime}
of size
t-j
, we split it into
T\setminus T^{\prime}
and
T^{\prime}\setminus T
of sizes
(t+k-k^{\prime}-j)/2
and
(t-k+k^{\prime}-j)/2
, respectively, giving the middle binomial coefficient in
Equation

4.11
.

Note that if any of the set sizes computed above are not integral, then no such
(T,T^{\prime})
exists, agreeing with our binomial coefficient convention.
∎

Note that when
\rho=1/2
, then
\beta=0
and only the
j=0
term contributes to
N_{1/2}(k,k^{\prime};t)
and it is exactly
N(k,k^{\prime};t)
from
Definition

3.5
. Observe that by definition,
N_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime};s)=0
for
s>k+k^{\prime}
, and
\mathbb{E}[q_{s}(x)]=0
for
0<s\leq k+k^{\prime}<d^{\perp}
. Therefore, the computation in
Proposition

4.3
simplifies significantly below the

cutoff.

Corollary 4.4
.

If
k+k^{\prime}<d^{\perp}
for
k,k^{\prime}\geq 0
, then

\mathbb{E}[q_{k}q_{k}^{\prime}]=N_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime};0)\mathbb{E}[q_{0}]=\binom{m}{k}\mathbf{1}\{k=k^{\prime}\}.

(4.12)

Moreover, we derive three-term recursion analogous to
Proposition

3.2
, as follows.

Proposition 4.5
.

Recall the definition of
\beta
from
Equation

4.8
. For any
0\leq k<m
,

q_{1}q_{k}=(k+1)q_{k+1}+(m-k+1)q_{k-1}+\beta kq_{k}.

(4.13)

Proof.

Recall the definition of
q_{1}
and
q_{k}
from
Equation

4.2
.
In the definition of
q_{1}
, there is a summation over a singleton set; call that
\{j\}
. Let the
k
-element set summed over in the definition of
q_{k}
be
T
. We split the sum into cases depending on if
j\in T
and apply the fact that
g_{j}^{2}=1+\beta g_{j}
to obtain

\displaystyle q_{1}q_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in[m]}\sum_{T\in\binom{[m]}{k}}\prod_{i\in T\sqcup\{j\}}g_{i}

(4.14)

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\binom{[m]}{k}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j\not\in T}g_{j}\prod_{i\in T}g_{i}+\sum_{j\in T}g_{j}^{2}\prod_{i\in T\setminus\{j\}}g_{i}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\binom{[m]}{k}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j\not\in T}g_{j}\prod_{i\in T\cup\{j\}}g_{i}+\sum_{j\in T}\prod_{i\in T\setminus\{j\}}g_{i}+\beta\sum_{j\in T}\prod_{i\in T}g_{i}}}\right).

We examine the three summands in order:

•

If
j\not\in T
, then
T\cup\{j\}
is a
k+1
element subset of
[m]
, and each such set is counted
k+1
times in the first summand of
Equation

4.14
for choosing
j
. This gives the first term of
Equation

4.13
.

•

If
j\in T
, then
T\setminus\{j\}
is a
k-1
element subset of
[m]
, and each such set is counted
m-k+1
times in the first summand of
Equation

4.14
for choosing
j
. This gives the second term of
Equation

4.13
.

•

For the last term, the sum over
j\in T
gives a factor of
k
, so it matches the last term of
Equation

4.13
.

Together, we obtain
Equation

4.13
.
∎

Therefore, combining the previous two propositions, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
N_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime},1;s)\coloneqq(k+1)N_{\rho}(k+1,k^{\prime};s)+\beta kN_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime};s)+(m-k+1)N_{\rho}(k-1,k^{\prime};s)
. Then

\mathbb{E}_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[q_{1}(x)q_{k}(x)q_{k^{\prime}}(x)}}\right]=\sum_{s=0}^{m}N_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime},1;s)\mathbb{E}_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}}[q_{s}(x)].

(4.15)

4.2
The Expected Satisfaction Expansion

The idea from our framework in
Section

3.2
is to sample
x
with probability given as the the square of a suitably weighted linear combination of the
k
-wise discrepancy for
0\leq k\leq\ell
, similar to the quantum state of DQI. As discussed, owing to the easy control of expected discrepancy below
d^{\perp}
in
Lemma

3.4
, we ensured
\ell\leq\mu m
, and this is the bottle-neck that blocks us from improving beyond the semicircle law. Instead, we take

\ell\coloneqq\lfloor(\mu+\delta)m\rfloor\quad\text{where}\quad 0<\mu\leq\mu+\delta\leq 1-\rho.

(4.16)

for some
\delta\geq 0
to be chosen. The cutoff of
1-\rho
for
\mu+\delta
is chosen because we hope to find a solution
x
with
s(x)\geq\operatorname{SCL}_{\rho}(\mu+\delta)-o(1)
, which is
1-o(1)
once
\mu+\delta\geq 1-\rho
, so there is no need to take larger
\delta
.
As in the perfect decoding case, we choose
u
and
x\sim\mathbb{P}_{u}
exactly as before:

\mathbb{P}_{u}(x)\propto\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}u_{k}q_{k}(x)}}\right)^{2}\quad\text{where}\quad u_{k}\propto\binom{m}{k}^{-1/2}\mathbf{1}\{\ell-\sigma\leq k\leq\ell\},

(4.17)

and
\sigma\gg 1
grows slowly with
\ell
and so with
m
and
n
.
For now, we fix

\sigma\coloneqq\lfloor\log\log\ell\rfloor\sim\log\log m.

(4.18)

In
[
13
, Lemma 9.3]
, the weight vector
w
is chosen to be the leading eigenvector of
A^{(\ell)}
, which turns out to be exactly in this form for
\sigma=\sqrt{\ell}
and the correct normalization
Equation

3.12
.
Analogous to
Equation

3.16
, we use shorthand
\mathbb{E}[q_{t}]=\mathbb{E}_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}}[q_{t}(x)]
to compute that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{u}}[q_{1}(x)]

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=0}^{\ell}u_{k}u_{k^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}[q_{1}q_{k}q_{k^{\prime}}]}{\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=0}^{\ell}u_{k}u_{k^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}[q_{k}q_{k^{\prime}}]}

(4.19)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{m}\mathbb{E}[q_{t}]\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=0}^{\ell}u_{k}u_{k^{\prime}}N_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime},1;t)}{\sum_{t=0}^{m}\mathbb{E}[q_{t}]\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=0}^{\ell}u_{k}u_{k^{\prime}}N_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime};t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=\ell-\sigma}^{\ell}\binom{m}{k}^{-1/2}\binom{m}{k^{\prime}}^{-1/2}N_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime},1;0)+\sum_{t=d^{\perp}}^{m}\mathbb{E}[q_{t}]\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=\ell-\sigma}^{\ell}\binom{m}{k}^{-1/2}\binom{m}{k^{\prime}}^{-1/2}N_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime},1;t)}{\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=\ell-\sigma}^{\ell}\binom{m}{k}^{-1/2}\binom{m}{k^{\prime}}^{-1/2}N_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime};0)+\sum_{t=d^{\perp}}^{m}\mathbb{E}[q_{t}]\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=\ell-\sigma}^{\ell}\binom{m}{k}^{-1/2}\binom{m}{k^{\prime}}^{-1/2}N_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime};t)}.

We separate the terms by the weight
t
.
The
t=0
terms in the numerator and denominator of
Equation

4.19
can be computed similarly to
Equation

3.16
, and their ratio together with
Equation

4.3
will essentially recover
\operatorname{SCL}_{\rho}(\mu+\delta)
, which strictly improves on
\operatorname{SCL}_{\rho}(\mu)
.
The summands with
0<t<d^{\perp}
terms vanish via
Lemma

4.1
, exactly as before. We also have some higher order correction terms for
t\geq d^{\perp}
, which we will have to control.

In the following proposition we use the last two corollaries to compute the
t=0
terms.

Proposition 4.7
.

It holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=\ell-\sigma}^{\ell}\binom{m}{k}^{-1/2}\binom{m}{k^{\prime}}^{-1/2}N_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime};0)

\displaystyle=\sigma+1,

(4.20)

\displaystyle\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=\ell-\sigma}^{\ell}\binom{m}{k}^{-1/2}\binom{m}{k^{\prime}}^{-1/2}N_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime},1;0)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\beta(\mu+\delta)+2\sqrt{(\mu+\delta)(1-\mu-\delta)}}}\right]\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma m+O(\sigma^{2}+m)}}\right).

Proof.

As
N_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime};0)=N(k,k^{\prime};0)=\binom{m}{k}\mathbf{1}\{k^{\prime}=k\}
, we get

\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=\ell-\sigma}^{\ell}\binom{m}{k}^{-1/2}\binom{m}{k^{\prime}}^{-1/2}N_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime};0)=\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=\ell-\sigma}^{\ell}\binom{m}{k}^{-1/2}\binom{m}{k^{\prime}}^{-1/2}\binom{m}{k}\mathbf{1}\{k^{\prime}=k\}=\sigma+1.

Moreover, by
Corollary

4.6
, we obtain

N_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime},1;0)=(k+1)\binom{m}{k+1}\mathbf{1}\{k^{\prime}=k+1\}+\beta k\binom{m}{k}\mathbf{1}\{k^{\prime}=k\}+(m-k+1)\binom{m}{k-1}\mathbf{1}\{k^{\prime}=k-1\},

so we split into cases of
k^{\prime}\in\{k-1,k,k+1\}
to compute

\displaystyle\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=\ell-\sigma}^{\ell}\binom{m}{k}^{-1/2}\binom{m}{k^{\prime}}^{-1/2}N_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime},1;0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=\ell-\sigma}^{\ell}\beta k+\sum_{k=\ell-\sigma}^{\ell-1}(k+1)\binom{m}{k+1}^{1/2}\binom{m}{k}^{-1/2}+\sum_{k=\ell-\sigma+1}^{\ell}(m-k+1)\binom{m}{k-1}^{1/2}\binom{m}{k}^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\beta(2\ell-\sigma)(\sigma+1)+\sum_{k=\ell-\sigma}^{\ell-1}\sqrt{(k+1)(m-k)}+\sum_{k=\ell-\sigma+1}^{\ell}\sqrt{k(m-k+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\beta(2\ell-\sigma)(\sigma+1)+2m\sum_{k=\ell-\sigma+1}^{\ell}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{k-1}{m}}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\beta(\sigma+1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu+\delta-O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sigma}{m}}}\right)}}\right)m+2\sigma m\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{(\mu+\delta)(1-\mu-\delta)}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sigma}{m}}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\beta(\mu+\delta)+2\sqrt{(\mu+\delta)(1-\mu-\delta)}}}\right]\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma m+O(\sigma^{2}+m)}}\right).

Let
g(x)=\sqrt{x(1-x)}
, defined for
x\in[0,1]
. In the second to last equality, there are
\sigma
-many summands, each of which is
g(\mu+\delta)+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\sigma}/{m}}}\right)
since
\ell/m=\mu+\delta+O(1/m)
. Here, we are using the fact that for fixed
\mu
and
\sigma/m\to 0
, we have
g((\mu+\delta+O(\sigma/m))=g(\mu+\delta)+O(\sigma/m)
.
∎

Recalling that
\mathbb{E}[q_{0}]=1
,
Proposition

4.7
tells us that,
if
we were to ignore the
t>0
terms in (
4.19
), we
would
have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{u}}[s(x)]

\displaystyle\approx\rho+\sqrt{\rho(1-\rho)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\beta(\mu+\delta)+2\sqrt{(\mu+\delta)(1-\mu-\delta)}}}\right]

(4.21)

\displaystyle=\rho+(1-2\rho)(\mu+\delta)+2\sqrt{\rho(1-\rho)(\mu+\delta)(1-\mu-\delta)}

\displaystyle=\rho(1-\mu-\delta)+(1-\rho)(\mu+\delta)+2\sqrt{\rho(1-\rho)(\mu+\delta)(1-\mu-\delta)}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{SCL}_{\rho}(\mu+\delta).

To prove this rigorously, we will show that the
t>0
terms in
Equation

4.19
are asymptotically smaller than their
t=0
counterparts. Via
Lemma

4.1
, we have
\mathbb{E}[q_{t}(x)]=0
for
1\leq t<d^{\perp}
, so it suffices to consider

2\mu m\sim d^{\perp}\leq t\leq 2\ell\sim 2(\mu+\delta)m.

(4.22)

For these values of
t
, we control both the sum of binomial coefficients in
Equation

4.19
as well as
\mathbb{E}[q_{t}(x)]
, to show that for any
t
and
k,k^{\prime}\in[\ell-\sigma,\ell]
that under conditions of the main theorems, we have

\frac{\tilde{N}_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime};t)}{\sqrt{\binom{m}{k}\binom{m}{k^{\prime}}}}\cdot|\mathbb{E}[q_{t}]|\leq e^{-\Omega(m)}\quad\text{where}\quad\tilde{N}_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime};t)\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{t}|\beta|^{j}\binom{t}{j}\binom{t-j}{\frac{t+k-k^{\prime}-j}{2}}\binom{m-t}{\frac{k+k^{\prime}-t-j}{2}}.

(4.23)

We outline a three-step plan to do so.

1.

First, we show that each of the
\sigma^{2}
terms in the sum in
Equation

4.19
indexed by
(k,k^{\prime})
is asymptotically close to the leading one indexed by

(with possibly minus one from parity).

2.

Then, we compute the leading term of
Equation

4.19
where

. For the balanced special case where

, this turns out to be
\exp(mE(\mu,\delta)+o(m))
where

is defined in
Theorem

1.5
.

3.

Finally, we borrow and improve techniques from local leakage resilience (see
Section

1.3.2
) to control
|\mathbb{E}[q_{t}(x)]|
. In that context, as discussed in
Section

1.2
, this quantity is the per-transcript control of the Fourier proxy in
Equation

1.20
. This step is the content of
Section

4.3
.

For the remainder of this section, we execute the first two steps.
We begin with the first step.

Proposition 4.8
.

For any

\rho\in(0,1)
,
\mu\in(0,1/2]
, and
\delta\in[0,1-\rho-\mu]
, there exists a constant
C=C(\mu,\rho)>0
such that
for every
t\in[d^{\perp},2\ell]
and
k,k^{\prime}\in[\ell-\sigma,\ell]
,

\frac{N_{\rho}(k,k^{\prime};t)}{\sqrt{\binom{m}{k}\binom{m}{k^{\prime}}}}\leq C^{\sigma}\frac{\tilde{N}_{\rho}(\ell,\ell-\mathbf{1}\{2\nmid t\};t)}{\binom{m}{\ell}}.

(4.24)

Proof.

We prove the case where
t
is even. The odd
t
case follows similarly. Observe that

0<\mu\leq\frac{\ell}{m}=\mu+\delta\leq 1-\rho,

so
\ell/m
is bounded away from
0
and
1
. Then, for any
k\in[\ell-\sigma,\ell]
, we have that

\frac{\binom{m}{\ell}}{\binom{m}{k}}=\prod_{j=0}^{\ell-k-1}\frac{m-\ell+j+1}{\ell-j}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{(1-\rho)m+\sigma}{\mu m-\sigma}}}\right)^{\sigma}\leq C_{0}^{\sigma},

(4.25)

for sufficiently large
m
since
\sigma\ll m
, where constant
C_{0}=C_{0}(\rho,\mu)>0
. This gives a bound on the ratio of denominator of
Equation

4.24
, i.e.

\binom{m}{k}^{-1/2}\binom{m}{k^{\prime}}^{-1/2}\leq C_{0}^{\sigma}\binom{m}{\ell}^{-1}.

(4.26)

We similarly bound the ratio of numerators of
Equation

4.24
. Recall from
Equation

4.23
that
