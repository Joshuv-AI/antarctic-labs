IGHG1⁺ plasma cells define an NF-κB-driven inflammatory program that shapes T-cell immunity and immunotherapy response in colorectal cancer
Authors: Rui-Feng Zhang, Wan-Ze Zhang, He Li, Yonghao Chen, Rulei Zhong, Jian-Feng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•IGHG1⁺ plasma cells are enriched in colorectal cancer ICB responders.•High-IGHG1 plasma cells exhibit antigen-presentation and NF-κB programs.•B-cell NF-κB activity promotes T-cell cytotoxicity and restrains tumor growth.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common cancers and a leading cause of cancer death globally [1,2]. Despite advances in conventional therapies, advanced CRC remains clinically challenging to manage. In recent years, immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has transformed the clinical management of various malignancies, including CRC [3,4]. ICB produces long-lasting clinical advantages in some patients by activating host anti-tumor immune responses [5]. Nevertheless, a large subset of patients shows primary or acquired resistance, resulting in stable or progressive disease. The tumor microenvironment (TME) is now widely acknowledged as a principal factor responsible for these differing clinical results [6,7]. As a result, systematically mapping the cellular and molecular landscapes of the TME has become crucial to uncover the mechanisms of immune evasion and identify viable predictive biomarkers of response to therapy.

Research on antitumor immunity has mainly focused on T cell responses [8,9]. The importance of B cells and tertiary Lymphoid Structures (TLS) in ensuring a strong anti-tumor immunity response has garnered increasing recognition recently. Plasma cells, as the terminally differentiated effector cells of B cell lineage, frequently infiltrate the TME [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. However, the B cell compartment in the tumor microenvironment shows a high level of plasticity and functional heterogeneity [14]. Some subpopulations promote anti-tumor responses via antibody secretion and antigen presentation, whereas others may become exhausted or gain a regulatory phenotype that promotes immune tolerance. Understanding the subclone dynamics of plasma cells and how their functional states influence immunotherapy outcomes is therefore important [15].

Although bulk transcriptomic profiling provides valuable prognostic signatures, it lacks the single-cell resolution required to dissect the intricate functional states of specific immune subpopulations and their dynamic crosstalk [16]. For example, the molecular programs governing highly active plasma-cell subsets during complete response, particularly their involvement in NF-κB signaling, antigen presentation, and TLS formation, remain incompletely characterized [[17], [18], [19]]. Furthermore, there is a critical clinical need to translate these high-resolution single-cell insights into practical biomarkers that can be validated across independent, multi-cancer patient cohorts.

In the present study, we integrated public single-cell transcriptomic data from CRC patients receiving immunotherapy with external validation in multiple bulk transcriptomic cohorts and experimental assays in vitro and in vivo. We identified an IGHG1⁺ plasma-cell subset enriched in responding tumors and characterized a High-IGHG1 state associated with immune activation, TLS-related features, and NF-κB signaling. We further show that an IGHG1⁺ plasma-cell-associated signature is linked to immunotherapy benefit across independent cohorts. Together, these findings support a model in which NF-κB-activated IGHG1⁺ plasma cells contribute to a T-cell-inflamed microenvironment associated with improved immunotherapy response in CRC.

Methods and materials

Study design

This study combined public single-cell transcriptomic analysis, external bulk transcriptomic cohort validation and functional experiments to characterize B-cell states associated with immunotherapy response in CRC. The study workflow included preprocessing and integration of scRNA-seq data, identification of tumor-infiltrating B-cell subsets, construction of an IGHG1⁺ plasma-cell signature, validation of the signature in independent immunotherapy cohorts, and experimental assessment of NF-κB-related inflammatory signaling in vitro and in vivo.

Single-cell RNA sequencing dataset

Single-cell RNA sequencing data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession number GSE236581 [20]. This dataset contains multi-timepoint and multi-tissue scRNA-seq profiles from CRC patients treated with immunotherapy, including tumor tissue, adjacent normal tissue, and peripheral blood. The complete GSE236581 cohort comprised 22 patients receiving PD-1 inhibitor-based treatment. To reduce potential heterogeneity associated with treatment regimens, the present integrated single-cell analysis included only the 17 patients who received PD-1 inhibitor monotherapy, whereas five patients receiving a PD-1 inhibitor combined with CapeOx were excluded. The included cohort comprised 11 patients with complete response, 3 with partial response, and 3 with stable disease. Detailed patient- and sample-level information is provided in Table S1.

Quality control and preprocessing

All analyses were performed using Scanpy [21]. Cells expressing fewer than 200 genes, >6000 genes, or >15% mitochondrial transcripts were excluded. Genes detected in fewer than three cells were removed. After filtering, expression values were normalized to 10,000 counts per cell and log-transformed using sc.pp.normalize_total and sc.pp.log1p in Scanpy. Highly variable genes were identified with sc.pp.highly_variable_genes using the seurat_v3 flavor, retaining the top 3000 genes. Here, "seurat_v3″ refers solely to the highly variable gene selection method implemented within Scanpy; the Seurat software was not used in this study. After selection, the expression matrix was standardized with sc.pp.scale, and the scaled values were clipped to a maximum of 10.

Data integration, clustering, and annotation

Samples were integrated using harmony [22] methods. Principal component analysis was performed with sc.tl.pca using the ARPACK solver. The first 30 principal components were used to construct a neighborhood graph of 20 nearest neighbors via sc.pp.neighbors. Unsupervised clustering was performed using the Leiden algorithm at a resolution of 0.5, and cells were visualized using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) with sc.tl.umap. Major cell types were annotated according to canonical marker genes, including EPCAM and keratin genes for epithelial cells, CD3D and CD3E for T cells, CD79A and MS4A1 for B cells, C1QB and LST1 for myeloid cells, and ACTA2 and COL1A2 for stromal cells [23,24]. Cell-composition results were visualized using OmicVerse v1.7.1, including ov.utils.plot_cellproportion and ov.pl.cellproportion [25].

Tumor-infiltrating B-cell analysis

To focus on the tumor immune microenvironment, only cells derived from tumor tissue were retained for response-related analyses. Tumor-infiltrating B cells were subsetted and re-clustered for higher-resolution analysis. B-cell states were annotated according to established lineage and differentiation markers, including naive B cells, memory B cells, germinal center-like B cells, and plasma-cell populations [26]. Plasma-cell subsets were further defined by expression of immunoglobulin heavy-chain genes, JCHAIN, MZB1, CD27, and related maturation markers. A plasma-cell cluster with prominent IGHG1 expression was defined as the IGHG1⁺ plasma-cell population. To assess heterogeneity within this compartment, IGHG1⁺ plasma cells were divided into High-IGHG1 and Low-IGHG1 groups according to normalized IGHG1 expression, and the cutoff values was set as the median expression (3.43). In the current analysis, the median normalized IGHG1 expression within the IGHG1⁺ plasma-cell population was used as the cutoff.

Differential expression and enrichment analyses

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test implemented in Seurat. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change greater than 0.25 and an adjusted P value <0.05 were considered significant. P values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Functional enrichment [27] analyses were performed using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using Hallmark, GO biological process, and KEGG gene sets.

TLS and immune-signature analyses

TLS-associated activity was estimated using previously reported TLS-related gene sets(CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, CCL8, CCL18, CCL19, CCL21, CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, and CXCL13) [28]. Additional immune-related programs, including antigen presentation, inflammatory signaling, and T-cell activation, were evaluated. Differences in immune-cell composition across response groups were assessed by comparing cell proportions and observed-to-expected ratio (Ro/e) [29] enrichment patterns where applicable.

Construction of the IGHG1⁺ plasma-cell signature

To represent the transcriptional features of functionally active IGHG1⁺ plasma cells, we generated an IGHG1⁺ plasma-cell signature based on genes enriched in the High-IGHG1 subset relative to the Low-IGHG1 subset. Candidate genes were selected according to statistical significance, direction of change, and biological relevance, with priority given to genes related to immunoglobulin production, antigen presentation, chemokine signaling, and inflammatory activation. Signature scores in bulk datasets were calculated using ssGSEA.

Validation in external immunotherapy cohorts

The predictive value of the IGHG1⁺ plasma-cell signature was assessed in multiple independent public immunotherapy cohorts, including the Dizier, Nathanson, Riaz, Cho, and Van Allen datasets(https://hiplot.com.cn/). Processed expression matrices and corresponding clinical annotations were obtained from public repositories or original supplementary materials. Samples were classified as responders or non-responders according to the criteria used in each original study. Signature scores were compared between response groups, and survival analyses were performed in cohorts with available progression-free survival (PFS) data.

Correlation analysis in bulk transcriptomic CRC data

Bulk RNA-seq data from The Cancer Genome Atlas colorectal cancer cohort (TCGA-CRC, https://www.cancer.gov/) were used to evaluate the broader molecular context of the IGHG1⁺ plasma-cell program. Correlations between the IGHG1⁺ plasma-cell signature and selected immune-related genes were calculated using Spearman correlation analysis. Genes ranked by correlation with the signature score were further subjected to GSEA to identify biological pathways associated with the IGHG1⁺ plasma-cell state.

In vitro functional assays

Primary or expanded B cells were cultured under activating conditions and then assigned to four groups: untreated control (Blank), factor-stimulated control (Blank+Factor), NF-κB activation (Blank+Factor+NF-κB activator 1), and NF-κB inhibition (Blank+Factor+Bortezomib). After treatment, cells and conditioned media were collected for downstream analyses. Secreted inflammatory mediators, including IL-32 (Jining Bio, JN16931), IL-10(Jining Bio, JN17305), IL-6(Jining Bio, JN16894) and CCL5 (Jining Bio, JN26088), were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay(ELISA), and additional details are provided in Table S2. Protein expression was further assessed by Western blot where indicated. NF-κB activator 1 refers to phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; also known as TPA; MedChemExpress, Cat. No. HY-18,739). PMA was supplied as a 10 mM stock solution in DMSO and diluted in culture medium to a final concentration of 100 nM. Cells were treated for 24 h, and control cells received an equivalent volume of DMSO.

Western blot analysis

B cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013) with added protease inhibitors (Beyotime, ST506–2). The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies: rabbit anti-IL-4 antibodies (Affinity, AF5142), rabbit anti-IL-6 antibodies (Affinity, DF6087), rabbit anti-IL-10 antibodies (Affinity, DF6894), rabbit anti-CD28 antibodies (Affinity, AF0014), rabbit anti-CD74 antibodies (Affinity, DF7449). Protein bands were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents (Advansta, K-12,043) and detected on film using a ChemiScope 5600 system (Hengmei Technology). Densitometric analysis was carried out using ImageJ.

In vivo tumor experiments

All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Experiment Welfare and Ethics Review Committee of the School of Public Health, Jilin University (approval no. KT: 2025–08–007). A syngeneic mouse tumor model was established to assess the impact of NF-κB modulation on anti-tumor immunity in vivo. After tumor establishment, mice were randomly assigned to four treatment groups corresponding to the in vitro experimental conditions. Tumor volume was monitored at predefined time points, and tumor weight was measured at the endpoint. Tumor tissues were then collected for immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometric analysis.

Immunofluorescence and flow cytometry

Tumor tissues were processed as paraffin-embedded or frozen sections according to downstream staining requirements. Multiplex immunofluorescence was used to examine the spatial distribution of NF-κB-related inflammatory markers in the tumor microenvironment. For consistency with the current Results section and figure legend, the marker panel included TNF-α (Affinity, AF7014), NF-κB (Affinity, AF0874), and TLR4 (Affinity, AF7017). For flow cytometry, tumor tissues were dissociated into single-cell suspensions and stained to quantify the proportion and activation status of CD8 (Biolegend, 100,751)-positive GZMB (Biolegend, 372,213)-positive T cells.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out in R. For comparisons between two groups, we utilized either the student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and for comparisons among three groups, we leveraged either ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis test, depending on the normality of the data. For the correlations, Pearson or Spearman coefficients were used. Survival was analyzed via Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests. All tests were two-sided with P < 0.05 as the cutoff.

Results

The landscape of tumor immune cells associated with immunotherapy response in CRC

We conducted an integrated analysis of the all samples in the GSE236581 dataset to systematically analyses the immunotherapy response-associated tumor microenvironment characteristics. Unsupervised Leiden clustering identified 26 distinct cell populations, which were visualized by UMAP embedding (Fig. 1A), and the patient-colored UMAP showed robust integration across patients (Fig. 1B). Cell clustering essentially followed tissue of origin revealing a distinct transcriptional state for tumor- infiltrating cells compared to adjacent normal and peripheral blood compartments (Fig. 1C). We annotated the major cell lineages based on canonical marker expression (epithelial (KRT5, EPCAM), T (CD3E, CD3D), B (CD79A, MS4A1), myeloid and stromal cells (ACTA2, COL1A2) (Fig. 1D–F). The tissue-level distribution of cell proportions displayed distinct baseline frequencies across compartments (Fig. 1G), and there was significant variation of B cell among all cell populations. To directly study the tumor microenvironment (TME), we isolated all tumor-infiltrating cells and stratified them by clinical response (Fig. 1H). The quantitative analysis of cell fractions, backed up by Ro/e enrichment heatmap ratio, demonstrated that B cell experienced the most significant dynamic change among the major lineages. There was a notable enrichment in B cell compartment in CR patients compared with patients with PR or SD (Fig. 1I). Also, differential gene expression profiling across these 3 response groups delineated distinct transcriptional signatures associated with immunotherapeutic efficacy (Fig. 1J-K). Sub-clustering of tumor-infiltrating B cells identifies an IGHG1⁺ plasma cell subset enriched in responders

We executed high-resolution sub-clustering to further define the B cell compartment (Fig. 2A). By employing established differentiation markers and immunoglobulin heavy-chain profiles, we could define a spectrum of states that includes naive B cells, memory B cells, and various plasma cell subsets (Fig. 2B). Importantly, a specific subset of plasma cells (designated IGHG1⁺ Plasma) characterized by upregulation of IGHG1 and CD27 and downregulation of naive markers was significantly expanded in the CR group (Fig. 2B-C). The enrichment of this IGHG1⁺ Plasma subset was associated with clinical efficacy, showing stepwise overrepresentation from SD to PR and CR patients (Fig. 2D). To characterize the transcriptional basis of this, we studied differentially expressed genes. Transcriptional profiling showed distinct expression modules both across clinical response groups and within specific B cell sub-clusters (Fig. 2E). The individual cellular level verification confirmed responders’ upregulation of important immune-related, B cell functional genes such as JCHAIN, IGHA1, CD79A (Fig. 2F). In line with an enhanced local anti-tumor immune response, the total TLS score was significantly increased in the CR group (Fig. 2G). Next, we investigated whether the IGHG1⁺ Plasma subset displayed intrinsic phenotypic plasticity that was dependent on the patient’s response to therapy. Through comparative transcriptional profiling within this subset, we identified distinct DEG signatures that separated the CR, PR and SD states, indicating strong within-cluster transcriptional heterogeneity (Fig. 2H). GO enrichment analysis of these stage-specific DEGs revealed IGHG1⁺ Plasma cells from CR patients were fundamentally rewired for immune activation. The transcription program enriched for CR pathways exhibited a strong preponderance of adaptive immunity pathways such as “T cell receptor signaling pathway”, “antigen processing and presentation” and “interferon-gamma-mediated signaling pathway” (Fig. 2I). IGHG1⁺ plasma cell abundance stratifies T cell activation status

To determine the functional immunological significance of IGHG1⁺ plasma cells, we divided them into two High IGHG1⁺ and Low IGHG1⁺ populations based on the expression intensity of the IGHG1 gene in tumor tissues (Fig. 3A). We then methodically examined how the distribution and transcriptional states of these subsets varied across patients with different therapeutic outcomes. The results showed a distinct distribution, with the Low IGHG1⁺ subset being concentrated in the SD and PR groups while the high IGHG1⁺ subset being significantly enriched in the CR group (Fig. 3A-B). Analysis of differential gene expression showed that the high IGHG1⁺ subset not only had a greater capacity to produce immunoglobulin but also displayed upregulated expression of genes related to antigen presentation and chemokine receptors. On the other hand, the low IGHG1⁺ subset expressed high levels of stress response genes such as FOS and HSPA1B (Fig. 3C and D). The high IGHG1⁺ subset was significantly enriched in genes involved in "Antigen processing and presentation" and "NF-kappa B signaling pathway" (Fig. 3E). Pathways such as "T cell receptor signaling pathway" and "Th1/Th2 cell differentiation" were also enriched in this subset. The findings propose that high IGHG1⁺ plasma cell enrichments, which emerged from a robust class-switching mechanism, are not simply related to higher anti-tumour antibody secretion. Using the original annotations of tumour-infiltrating immune cells (Figure S1), we assessed the patterns of T cell infiltration. A plasma cell-derived signature predicts immunotherapy response in independent cohorts

We sought to validate the predictive performance of the IGHG1⁺ plasma cell signature (IGHG1, IGHG3, IGHG2, MZB1, XBP1, IGKC, PECAM1, SSR4, PRDX4, FKBP11, ITM2C, DERL3, ERLEC1, JSRP1, HSP90B1, LMAN1, CD38, ZBP1, FNDC3B, NUCB2), and appraise its clinical utility and robustness, across multiple independent public immunotherapy cohorts from various cancer types and treatment regimens (anti-PD-1, anti-CTLA-4, anti-MAGE-A3). Initially, we conducted a comparison of signature scores of responders vs. non-responders in the Dizier (anti-MAGE-A3), Nathanson (anti-CTLA-4) and Riaz cohorts (anti-PD-1/CTLA-4) (Fig. 4A). The data consistently showed that the IGHG1⁺ plasma cell signature score was significantly higher in the responder group in these cohorts. This indicates that the signature can classify patients who are likely to benefit from immunotherapy. In addition, an investigation of the prognosis association with the signature score via Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed results were significant. In the Cho cohort, patients in the high-score group had longer PFS than those in the low-score group (Log-rank P = 0.00031). A similar pattern of enhanced PFS was noted within the high score group of the VanAllen cohort within the anti-CTLA-4 (Log-rank P = 0.09) (Fig. 4B). GSEA based on GO and KEGG terms showed enrichment of processes related to leukocyte cell-cell adhesion, T-cell activation, and antigen processing (Fig. 5C). NF-κB modulation alters inflammatory responses in expanded B cells

To functionally examine the role of NF-κB in the secretory phenotype of IGHG1⁺B cells, we performed in vitro perturbation experiments using expanded B cells under four conditions: untreated control (Control), factor-stimulated control (Factor), NF-κB agonist treatment (Blank+Factor+NF-κB activator1), and proteasome-mediated NF-κB inhibition with Bortezomib (Factor+Bortezomib). Results of RT-qPCR analysis show that activation of NF-κB caused a large increase in the expression of the chemokine CCR4 and the inflammatory factor TNF-α when compared to factor-only controls (Fig. 6A). It was observed that treatment with Bortezomib markedly decreased the expression of CCR4, while significantly upregulating TNF-α. This observation implies the utilization of divergent regulatory mechanisms downstream of proteasome inhibition. The activity of NF-κB inside the cells, conducted at the same time, demonstrated the anticipated pharmacological modulation; specifically, NF-κB activator1 induced a small but important increase while Bortezomib showed a marked suppression relative to the factor control. To further characterize the secretory landscape, five cytokines and chemokines were quantified in conditioned media by ELISA (Fig. 6B). CXCL13 levels of G3 (Factor + NF-κB activator 1) were significantly higher than G2 (Factor) (P = 0.046), which is consistent with the established role of CXCL13 in the formation of tertiary lymphoid structure and recruitment of immune cells. Likewise, IL-32 secretion was statistically significantly higher in G3 than the Bortezomib treated group(G4) (Kruskal–Wallis test, P = 0.018), suggesting that NF-κB activation enhances IL-32 production while its inhibition decreases this pro-inflammatory mediator. Compared with the other groups, the level of IL-6, IL-10, and CCL5 did not differ significantly. Thus, it indicates a preference in the regulation of NF-κB on a fraction of the inflammatory secretome, rather than a global boosting of cytokine production. At the protein level, Western blot analysis further confirmed these observations by demonstrating a condition-dependent regulatory function on immune mediators (Fig. 6C). In the four treatment groups, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, CD28 and CD74 expression varied where NF-κB activation and inhibition had distinct modulatory effects on expression of each target. Loading control was GAPDH. Overall, these functional and molecular data demonstrate that NF-κB is a central transcriptional regulator of the inflammatory secretome in IGHG1⁺ B cells. B cell NF-κB signaling restrains tumor growth by boosting T cell cytotoxicity

To determine whether NF-κB signaling in B cells functionally impacts anti-tumor immunity in vivo, we developed a syngeneic mouse tumor model with four treatment arms: untreated control (Blank), factor-stimulated alone (Factor), NF-κB agonist-treated (Factor+NF-κB), and Bortezomib-treated (Fig. 7A). Longitudinal monitoring of tumor volume revealed that treatment of mice with NF-κB-activated B cells led to significantly retarded tumor growth when compared with control and factor only groups, while Bortezomib-mediated NF-κB inhibition resulted in accelerated tumor progression with the largest tumors seen in all cohorts (Fig. 7B–C). In line with this, endpoint tumor weights were significantly decreased in the Factor+NF-κB group and markedly increased in the Factor+Bortezomib group compared to the controls (Fig. 7D). To determine if this differential growth kinetic were associated with modification of the anti-tumor immune responses, we performed flow cytometric profiling of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. The most elevated expression of intratumoral GZMB among CD8+ T cells was observed in Factor+NF-κB group and the lowest in Factor + Bortezomib group, pointing towards a correlation between B cell NF-κB activity and effector T cell cytotoxicity in TME (Fig. 7E–F). Likewise, these findings were confirmed spatially through immunofluorescence staining of tumor sections. The Factor+NF-κB group displayed increased intratumoral expression of TNF-α, NF-κB, and the innate immune receptor TLR4, while Factor+Bortezomib group showed decreased staining for these (Fig. 7G). These inflammatory mediators being coordinatedly upregulated suggests that NF-κB-competent B cells remodel the local immune landscape by enhancing pro-inflammatory signalling cascades. Discussion

Understanding how cells influence the effectiveness of immune checkpoint blockade can aid in counteracting resistance to CRC [3,20]. In this study, we combined single-cell transcriptomic analysis, external cohort validation, and functional experiments to define a B-cell state associated with favorable immunotherapy response in CRC. Our results consistently identify an IGHG1⁺ plasma-cell program as a prominent feature of responders, particularly in patients achieving complete response, and further suggest that this state is linked to a more inflamed and T-cell-active tumor microenvironment. Mechanistically, we provide evidence that NF-κB-associated inflammatory signaling is a key component of this plasma-cell state and may contribute to its anti-tumor immunological effects.

Historically, tumor immunology has been T-cell-centric. However, recent landmark studies have established that B-cell infiltration and TLS formation are indispensable for mounting durable responses to ICB [10,30]. Our single-cell analysis extends these findings by revealing that B cells in the CRC microenvironment are not a monolithic population. We observed a fundamental shift wherein terminal differentiation into High IGHG1⁺ plasma cells within mature TLS were specific to responders, whereas non-responders were dominated by naive/memory B cells and low TLS scores [31,32]. Our findings indicate that the characteristics of B cells, which find themselves in tumors, of both quality and quantity may prove useful thereby enhancing biological resolution for gaining better understanding of response heterogeneity.

Our study found that IGHG1⁺ compartment shows a remarkable functional diversity or divergence. The High IGHG1⁺ subpopulation substantially enhanced antigen presentation and pathways associated with NF-κB-driven inflammation, while the Low IGHG1⁺ subset displayed a stress-related phenotype associated with resistance. This agrees with emerging evidence implying that tumor-infiltrating plasma cells could behave like non-classical antigen-presenting cells to directly affect T- cell phenotypes [33]. In addition, the high expression of CXCL13 and CCL4 in this subset suggests that it can sustain an “intratumoral immunity cycle” through the recruitment of immune effectors to the TLS [34,35].

Our in vitro and in vivo perturbation experiments provided functional evidence that NF-κB modulation in expanded B cells influences inflammatory mediator production and a T-cell-cytotoxic tumor microenvironment. These findings support an association between the High-IGHG1 plasma-cell program, NF-κB-associated inflammatory signaling, and a microenvironment permissive for T-cell cytotoxicity [36]. This mechanism offers biological justification for the strong predictive value of our IGHG1⁺ signature. Its ability to stratify PFS across multiple pan-cancer cohorts suggests that it may serve as a generalized biomarker and may provide complementary value beyond TMB or PD-L1, pending further prospective validation. Ultimately, our analysis highlights the importance of the “NF-κB-activated IGHG1⁺ plasma cell–T cell axis” in immunotherapy. The functional state of plasma cells, classified by high IGHG1 expression and inflammatory signaling, is an attractive target for therapeutic modulation and a robust tool for precision patient stratification. This helps explain why the IGHG1⁺ signature predicts response, and why the same signature stratified progression-free survival across several pan-cancer cohorts, where it may add value beyond TMB or PD-L1. In CRC the responder-associated feature was not the amount of B-cell infiltration but a particular plasma-cell program: high IGHG1 expression, inflammatory signaling, and close proximity to activated T cells [37] . The functional state of these cells therefore matters more than their number. Cells in the IGHG1-high state were transcriptionally active and behaved like antigen-presenting cells rather than simply producing immunoglobulin [38]. We interpret these findings as an NF-κB-activated IGHG1⁺ plasma cell-T cell axis with value as both a therapeutic target and a patient-stratification biomarker.

In addition, our external cohorts span other tumor types and ICB regimens, not CRC. This cross-cancer concordance supports generalizability but provides no CRC-specific ICB validation, a major limitation that warrants prospective testing in dedicated CRC immunotherapy cohorts. Whether the IGHG1⁺ plasma-cell signature predicts immunotherapy response independently of MSI/dMMR status remains to be validated in cohorts with matched molecular and clinical annotations.

Overall, our findings support a model in which an IGHG1-high plasma-cell state marks, and potentially helps shape, an inflamed tumor microenvironment associated with immunotherapy benefit in CRC. This refines the current view of B-cell involvement in anti-tumor immunity by emphasizing plasma-cell functional heterogeneity rather than B-cell abundance alone. It also provides a basis for further investigation of IGHG1⁺ plasma cells as both biomarkers and mechanistic contributors in colorectal cancer immunotherapy. Several limitations were still in this study. The discovery analysis was based on a public single-cell dataset with a limited number of CRC patients, which may reduce statistical power and may not fully capture inter-patient heterogeneity. Although the IGHG1⁺ plasma-cell signature was validated in multiple immunotherapy cohorts, most were pan-cancer rather than CRC-specific, and the definition of High-IGHG1 and Low-IGHG1 plasma cells relied on relative transcriptomic thresholds that may be influenced by technical variation. In addition, while our functional data support a role for NF-κB-associated inflammatory signaling, the precise mechanisms and plasma-cell-intrinsic causality will require further validation in more refined models.
