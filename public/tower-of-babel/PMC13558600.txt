Influence of menstrual cycle on autonomic nervous system, muscular strength and mood states
Authors: Hugo Meras Serrano, Nicolas Gueugneau, Gilles Ravier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined modulations of the cardiac autonomic nervous system, neuromuscular performance and psychological states during the menstrual cycle (MC) in naturally menstruating women. Fifteen healthy young females were tested during menstruation, estimated late follicular, and estimated mid-luteal phases. Each session included mood and motivation assessments, resting heart rate variability (HRV) analysis and a maximal voluntary isometric contraction test of the knee extensors. Our data revealed a significant decrease of the parasympathetic activity reflected by lower vagally derived HRV indices (root mean square of successive R-R interval differences and high frequency spectral component) during the mid-luteal phase compared to both menstruation and late follicular phases. However, no significant difference was detected for maximal force, rate of force development (RFD), and associated electromyographic activity (EMG). Among psychological variables, the Depression subscale score was the only one that increased during the late follicular phase. No significant correlations were observed between most HRV indices, neuromuscular performance variables, and psychological measures. However, perceived Vigour was positively associated with EMG activity during the RFD phase. In addition, autonomic balance was related to RFD, with low frequency (nu) positively and high frequency (nu) negatively correlated with explosive force production. These findings suggest cardiovagal modulation across the MC. Introduction

It is well-known that the menstrual cycle (MC) is characterized by cyclical fluctuations in pituitary hormones (follicle-stimulating hormone, luteinizing hormone) and steroidal sex hormones (estrogen, progesterone), which define the different phases of the cycle1,2. These hormonal fluctuations, particularly sex hormones, can daily influence a wide range of physiological parameters, potentially impacting both exercise performance and well-being1,3. Although this topic has generated growing interest in the scientific literature, findings related to performance parameters remain inconsistent across studies. In particular, studies focusing on the relationship between the MC and the cardiovascular system1 have suggested that fluctuations in estrogen and progesterone levels may influence autonomic nervous system activity. Furthermore, there is a lack of comprehensive data regarding the combined modulation of the cardiac autonomic nervous system and neuromuscular activity throughout the MC in healthy young women with regular MC.

Heart rate variability (HRV), defined as the fluctuations in R-wave to R-wave intervals, is a non-invasive tool used to investigate the autonomic nervous system (ANS) at the level of the sinus node. HRV has become a valuable marker in sports and exercise sciences for assessing physiological stress, recovery, and training adaptation4–7. HRV modulations provide insight into the dynamic balance between parasympathetic and sympathetic branches8. Among HRV indices, the root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD), the high-frequency (HF) spectral component, and the perpendicular spread of points in the Poincare plot (SD1) are commonly used for assessing vagally mediated modulation at rest or during post-exercise recovery7,9. While the application of HRV in males is well documented, there is a remarkable underrepresentation of female populations in sport science10, despite known physiological fluctuations linked to MC. Cyclical changes in estrogen and progesterone levels could thus influence autonomic regulation. Indeed, fluctuations in sex hormone levels across the MC are thought to alter baroreflex sensitivity, which in turn may influence the balance between sympathetic and parasympathetic activity, ultimately affecting HRV11,12. However, current findings regarding the interaction between the MC and HRV remain inconsistent13, suggesting further investigation.

In parallel, muscle performance, particularly force production, may also be influenced by the MC, during both dynamic14 and isometric tasks15,16. Estrogen has been shown to exert a neuroexcitatory effect enhancing muscle activation17,18 which may increase muscle force during the late follicular and mid luteal phases. Two studies have shown increases in handgrip16 and shoulder abduction strength15 across the MC, particularly when estrogen concentration was elevated (i.e., during the late follicular and ovulatory phases). Conversely, progesterone may interfere with motor unit recruitment, potentially impairing muscle function during the luteal phase17. However, clear empirical evidence regarding force modulation across the MC remains equivocal. While some studies have reported performance fluctuations14–16, others have found minimal or no significant differences19–22. For example, a decrease in maximal isokinetic torque of the knee extensors and flexors during the early follicular phase compared to the late luteal phase has been reported in trained women14, while isometric knee extensor strength has been shown to remain stable across the cycle19.

Fluctuations in ovarian hormones across the MC may therefore influence multiple interconnected physiological systems. In particular, changes in estrogen and progesterone levels can modulate ANS activity, which is reflected in HRV indices, and may also influence central and peripheral mechanisms involved in neuromuscular performance, including motor unit recruitment and force production. In parallel, these hormonal variations have been associated with changes in mood states suggesting potential interactions between affective, autonomic, and motor functions across the menstrual cycle.

While both HRV and muscle force have been independently examined in relation to the MC, there is, to our knowledge, no study investigating their combined modulation across the cycle phases. Given that HRV is often used as a proxy for systemic readiness and recovery, understanding its relationship with neuromuscular performance could have important implications for individualized training and performance monitoring in female athletes23. In particular, identifying whether HRV and neuromuscular performance are concomitantly regulated across the MC may help optimize training periodization, and support women’s well-being.

In this context, our study aims to investigate the effects of the MC on HRV indices, in temporal, frequency and non-linear domains, alongside muscle force and associated electromyographic activity. Mood state fluctuations were also assessed, as hormonal variations across the cycle have been shown to influence affective states, particularly in women with premenstrual syndrome24. However, in women with physiological MC (without premenstrual syndrome), mood tends to remain relatively stable throughout the cycle25. All parameters were assessed during menstruation (Mens), late follicular (Foll-late), and mid-luteal (Lut-mid) phases in young healthy recreationally active women. We hypothesized that vagally related HRV indices would be higher during the estimated Foll-late phase, potentially reflecting hormonal fluctuations occurring across the MC. We also hypothesized that muscle force would be higher during the estimated Foll-late phase and that these two parameters would be associated with perceived well-being.

Methods

Experimental design

This study used an observational within-participant repeated-measures design to determine the impact of three MC phases (Mens, Foll-late, Lut-mid) on HRV indices, maximal voluntary isometric contraction (MVC), associated electromyographic activity (EMG) and mood states. The study was undertaken during one complete MC after at least one-month screening period (i.e. menstruation diary, urinary tests). It thus comprised a MC screening period and an experimental MC including three test sessions.

Naturally menstruating women not using hormonal contraception were recruited (see inclusion criteria below). Participants completed one inclusion visit, one familiarization session, and three experimental sessions conducted at different moments of the MC. During each test session (Fig. 1), participants were required to complete the Profile of Mood States questionnaire (POMS), followed by a 5-min resting HRV recording. A standardized warm-up was then performed under supervision, consisting of 5 min of cycling at 60–80 rpm at approximately 60 watts on an ergocycle, two 5-s sprints at 7.5% of body mass, 10 bodyweight squats and 6 knee extensions against submaximal resistance (2 × 50%, 75%, and 90%). Finally, participants performed 3 MVCs of the preferential leg’s knee extensors. Participants were randomly assigned to start testing in one of the three MC phases.

Participants

Fifteen healthy, active, naturally menstruating women (age: 24.3 ± 4.8 years, weight: 61.7 ± 7.5 kg, height: 166.8 ± 5.8 cm, body fat: 24.4 ± 3.0%) were recruited. Physical activity level was self-declared and had to be between two and four sessions a week, for at least a year. Although participants engaged in a variety of activity types (endurance-, resistance-, and mixed type), most reported practicing multiple sports across different modalities, with a consistent weekly training volume (6.62 ± 2.6 h per week). To include the study, participants were asked to complete the Energy Availability Females Questionnaire (LEAF-Q), which assesses risk factors related to low energy availability, which may contribute to MC disturbances26. A score less than 8 was required for inclusion26, but no participants were excluded based on this criterion. Participants self-reported an absence of premenstrual symptoms, not being a smoker, not being pregnant, no history of pregnancy, and not having medical problems such as metabolic, cardiovascular, or respiratory diseases. Participants declared to have a regular MC (21–35 days, see “Determination of MC phases” part for more details)27, not using hormonal contraception for at least 6 months prior to the study28,29 and not following medical treatment30. Participants using a copper intrauterine device (IUD) were included, as this type of contraception does not involve exogenous hormone release. Exclusion criteria included the absence of ovulation confirmed by urinary testing (see “Determination of MC phases” part for more details), lower limb injuries, and the initiation of hormonal contraception during the experimental period.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local institutional ethical committee (Comité d’Ethique pour la Recherche de l’Université Bourgogne Franche-Comté; CERUBFC-2025-01-21-007). Each participant provided written informed consent before the investigation. Written informed consent for publication of the image and any potentially identifiable information in this online open-access publication was obtained from the participant depicted in the image.

Determination of MC phases

The MC has an average length of 28 days, which can vary from 21 to 35 days3. The regularity and duration of the MC are subject to significant intra- and inter-individual variations. Eumenorrhea is typically defined as a MC length between 21 and 35 days, with at least nine consecutive cycles per year, absence of hormonal contraceptive use for three months prior to recruitment, and confirmation of ovulation through both luteinizing hormone surge and an appropriate hormonal profile (verified by blood test)27. However, as no hormonal assays were performed in the present study, participants were more specifically characterized as naturally menstruating women, with ovulation estimated based on a urinary luteinizing hormone surge.

In this study, experimental sessions were conducted during the Mens phase (days 1–4, low estrogen and progesterone, bleeding), the estimated Foll-late phase (days 11–13, relatively high estrogen and low progesterone) and the estimated Lut-mid phase (days 21–23, elevated estrogen and progesterone). During the MC screening period, participants completed a menstrual diary including cycle length and onset of menstruation, and urinary ovulation tests were performed over an 8-day window around the estimated ovulation day. Ovulation timing during the screening month was determined based on self-reported cycle tracking and/or average cycle length when available. Several participants used mobile applications to monitor their cycles, which facilitated scheduling.

During the experimental month, participants continued to report the date and length of menstruation. Ovulation testing was then estimated over an 8-day window, starting 3 days before the expected ovulation date. The expected ovulation day corresponds to the predicted MC length, determined by retrospective analysis, minus 14 days31. Participants performed a urinary ovulation test between 10am and 10pm, at approximately the same time each day (± 1 h), until a positive result was obtained, indicating a luteinizing hormone surge (sensitivity: 20 miu/ml; One Step Ovulation Test Strip, CVS Corporation, USA). One-step ovulation predictor kits have demonstrated high concordance rates (91% to 97%) with reference methods32. The Mens session was performed during menstruations. The Foll-late session was performed the day of the positive urine test or the day after. The Lut-mid session was performed 7–9 days after the positive ovulation test33. Testing sessions were individually adjusted according to reported cycle length.

Materials and measures

Heart rate (HR)-derived data recording

The participants’ HR was collected using a HR monitor (HR1 chest belt, Matsport, Saint Ismier, France) connected to Mooky Center software (v2.45 Matsport, Saint Ismier, France) with ANT+ wireless transmission, before the start of the warm-up in a semi-recumbent position with arms alongside the body. Participants were in a quiet room at a constant temperature and were then instructed to put on noise-cancelling headphones, close their eyes, and breathe spontaneously without speaking or making any movement. The HRV data were recorded with a sampling frequency of 1000 Hz, and the R-R intervals were analysed using Kubios HRV Scientific software version 4.1.0 (Biosignal Analysis and Medical Imaging Group, University of Kuopio, Kuopio, Finland). The HRV analysis included 5 min of recording, with the first 1 min considered as stabilization and the remaining 4 min used for analysis34. Artefacts were automatically corrected with the interpolated adjacent R-R intervals by applying the “medium” filter in Kubios software. HRV indices in the time-domain included the root mean square of the successive differences of the normal R-R intervals (RMSSD, ms) and the standard deviation of the normal-to-normal intervals (SDNN, ms). In the frequency domain, analysis included low-frequency (LF: 0.04–0.15 Hz) and high-frequency (HF: 0.15–0.4 Hz) in both absolute (ms²) and normalized units (n.u.). In addition, instantaneous beat-to-beat variability (SD1, ms) and continuous beat-to-beat variability (SD2, ms) derived from the Poincare Plot analysis were computed. Among HRV indices, RMSSD, HF and SD1 were selected to reflect the cardiac parasympathetic modulation, whereas SDNN, LF and SD2 were considered to reflect combined autonomic influences35.

Maximal voluntary isometric contraction (MVC)

Maximal force (Fmax, N) from the MVC and rate of force development (RFD, N/s) were assessed using an isometric knee extension task. The test was performed in a seated position on adjustable chair, with the knee joint fixed at 60°. The trunk was kept as straight as possible, and the subject was instructed to hold the side handles. The force application point was positioned above the medial malleolus. Participants performed 3 MVCs of 5 s, separated by 1 min of passive rest to minimise fatigue. Force was recorded at 80 Hz using a strain gauge sensor (500 kg force sensor, Barcelona, Spain) connected to Chronojump software for data acquisition. During all trials, the experimenter instructed the participant to produce a 20 N pre-tension before the MVCs, to avoid countermovement effects. The participant was instructed to produce the greatest possible force as quickly as possible with verbal encouragement. Fmax was determined using a 200 ms moving average window applied to the force plateau. RFD was calculated as the slope of the force-time curve (Δforce/Δtime) between the onset of contraction (first detectable increase in force signal) and 200 ms. The highest Fmax and RFD values across trials were retained for further analysis, independently of the trial in which they occurred.

Surface EMG recording

During all MVCs, EMG activity from the vastus lateralis (VL, µV) and rectus femoris (RF, µV) muscles of the preferential leg was recorded using a wireless multichannel surface EMG system (Delsys trignoTM, Delsys Inc., Natick, USA), with differential (bipolar) electrodes integrated into each sensor. After the skin areas have been shaved, abraded and cleaned with an alcohol wipe in order to ensure good electrode adhesion and signal quality36, surface electrodes were positioned parallel to muscle fibres as recommended by SENIAM guidelines (Surface Electromyography for the Non-Invasive Assessment of Muscles; BIOMED II, European Union; http://www.seniam.org/). For the VL, the electrodes were placed at approximately two-thirds of the distance from the anterior-superior iliac spine to the lateral aspect of the patella. For the RF, the electrodes placements were approximately 50% of the distance from the anterior-superior iliac spine to the superior border of the patella. For each participant, the exact electrode positions over both muscles were carefully measured and marked with a permanent pen during the first session to ensure consistent placement across subsequent sessions. At the end of each session, electrode positions were re-outlined to maintain consistent placement, and participants were instructed to preserve and, if necessary, reinforce these markings between visits to minimize potential variability over time.

EMG signals were recorded at a frequency of 2000 Hz, amplified, and then filtered using a band-pass filter (20–450 Hz). Signals were processed using Labchart 8 Pro software (AD Instruments, Australia). Root mean square (RMS) values of the EMG signals were calculated using a 200 ms moving window for both VL and RF muscles. RMSmax was calculated over 200 ms centred on Fmax. In addition, RMS associated with the RFD was also analysed (RMSrfd). RMS values were computed separately for the VL (RMSmaxVL; RMSrfdVL) and RF muscles (RMSmaxRF; RMSrfdRF), and were also summed to provide a global representation of the quadriceps muscle activity (RMSmaxVLRF, RMSrfdVLRF). Neuromuscular efficiency (NME) for VL (NMEVL, N/µV), RF (NMERF, N/µV) and VL + RF (NMEVLRF, N/µV) was estimated from the ratio between Fmax and RMS, and used as an index of neuromuscular efficiency, as previously reported in the literature37.

Psychological measures

At the beginning of each test session, participants assessed their own level of motivation on a Likert scale ranging from 1 to 7 (1 = not at all motivated to perform the session; 7 = very motivated to perform the session). Then, they completed the original (long-form) Profile of Mood States (POMS) inventory38,39, which assesses mood states such as Anger (12 items, score ranges : 0–48), Fatigue (7 items, score ranges: 0–28), Depression (15 items, score ranges : 0–60), Tension (9 items, score ranges : 0–36), Confusion (7 items, score ranges : 0–28) and Vigour (8 items, score ranges : 0–32). These six subscale scores were also combined to create a total mood disturbance (TMD) score using the formula: Anger + Fatigue + Depression + Tension + Confusion – Vigour + 100. TMD scores can range from 32 to 300. Lower scores indicate better emotional well-being, except for the Vigour subscale.

Statistical analysis

The required sample size was estimated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.6, Universität Kiel, Germany) based on high-frequency (HF) as the primary outcome variable. The analysis was based on a previous study reporting a significant difference (p < 0.001) for HF between the follicular (mean: 34.06 ± 13.93) and luteal phases (mean: 24.18 ± 7.57)40. The power analysis was conducted for a repeated-measures ANOVA design (within-subjects factor with 3 levels), with a statistical power of 0.80 and a significance level of α = 0.05. Given the high estimated effect size (f = 0.62) and to remain conservative, the sample size was calculated using a more moderate effect size (f = 0.4). Under these parameters, the analysis indicated that a total of 15 participants would be required to detect a significant effect across the three phases of the MC.

All values in the text, tables and figures are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was conducted using the open-source software JASP (Jeffrey’s Amazing Statistics Program, Version 0.18.3.0, Amsterdam, Netherlands). Normality and homogeneity of the data were tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. When variables deviated from normality, a logarithmic transformation (log10) was applied, specifically for HRV spectral components (LFms² and HFms² power), RFD, RMSmaxRF, RMSmaxVLRF, RMSrfdRF and NMEVLRF. Statistical analyses were performed on log-transformed data where appropriate, although results are presented using raw values for ease of interpretation. All measures for HRV, MVC, EMG, NME, POMS and motivation were analysed using one-way ANOVA with repeated measures. POMS scores were analysed in their original form without transformation, as psychometric questionnaire data are commonly treated as continuous variables and repeated-measures ANOVA is robust to moderate deviations from normality. When a significant main effect was observed, Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed. Effect sizes for the repeated-measures ANOVA were expressed using partial eta squared (n²p), while Cohen’s d (d) was used for pairwise post-hoc comparisons. η²p effect sizes were interpreted as small (η²p = 0.01), medium (η²p = 0.06) and large (η²p = 0.14)41. Cohen’s d values were interpreted as small (d = 0.20), medium (d = 0.50), or large (d = 0.80)41. In addition, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated to examine the relationships between HRV indices and psychological measures, between HRV indices and Fmax, RFD, EMG and NME measures, as well as between psychological measures and Fmax, RFD, EMG and NME. The strength of the correlations was interpreted as weak (ρ ≤ 0.29), moderate (0.30 ≤ ρ < 0.49), or strong (ρ ≥ 0.50)41. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The participants had an average LEAF-Q score of 2.7 ± 1.8 indicating a minimal risk of low energy availability, and therefore a low likelihood of including participants with potential cycle disorders. The mean cycle length was 29.1 ± 3 days with an average menstruation duration of 5.1 ± 0.9 days. Regarding the randomization of the testing sessions, 4 participants started during the Mens phase, 6 during the Foll-late phase, and 5 during the Lut-mid phase.

The HRV data of one participant were excluded from the analysis due to excessive signal artefacts. These disturbances were likely related to poor electrode-skin contact or excessive perspiration, resulting in inadequate signal quality for reliable HRV analysis.

Relationships between variables

Among the psychological measures, vigour score was positively correlated with EMG activity measured during MVC test, specifically with RMSrfdVLRF (ρ = 0.363, p = 0.014) and RMSrfdVL (ρ = 0.368, p = 0.013).

Regarding HRV indices, LFnu was positively correlated with RFD (ρ = 0.419, p = 0.006), whereas HFnu was negatively correlated with RFD (ρ = -0.42, p = 0.006).

No correlation was observed between HRV indices and psychological measures, nor between HRV indices and Fmax, EMG or NME.

Discussion

We hypothesized that mechanical, physiological and perceptual parameters of healthy naturally menstruating women would be associated with MC phase-related variations. We also hypothesized interactions between cardiac autonomic nervous system, neuromuscular activity and psychological state throughout the MC. Our findings indicated lower vagally mediated HRV indices during the estimated Lut-mid phase. However no clear relationship was observed between parameters, except between the level of vigour and muscular activity at the time of the onset of the MVC.

The HRV results of our study indicated a reduction in vagally related HRV indices of the autonomic nervous system in estimated Lut-mid phase, as shown by a decrease in RMSSD, HF, and SD1. This result is in line with previous studies11,40,42–47. To date, not all the underlying mechanisms for HRV modulation during MC have been elucidated. Nevertheless, several mechanisms have been proposed in the literature. Baroreflex sensitivity, has been shown to be affected by reproductive hormones11. Cardiovagal baroreflex response was attenuated during Lut-mid phase, without direct link with progesterone. Conversely, enhanced baroreflex sensitivity in the Foll-late phase, under high estradiol conditions, has been associated with increased vagal tone, particularly following blood hyperpression11. While Charkoudian et al.12 supported the role of estrogen and progesterone on thermoregulation and vascular peripheral resistance, authors remained cautious concerning the relationship between cardiac output, sympathetic nerve activity and vasodilatation. During Lut-mid phase, the withdrawal of cardiac parasympathetic activity might be related to the increase in body temperature and cutaneous vasodilation (mediated by β-adrenergic receptors) inducing lower arterial pressure and then potentially contributing to altered autonomic regulation. Nevertheless, Charkoudian et al.12 reported that the influence of sex hormones on the sinus node level does not seem so clear. For instance, when oestradiol and progesterone are elevated concurrently (Lut-mid phase) the influence of progesterone on body temperature has been suggested to be dominant. To date, current evidence regarding the influence of female sex hormones remains insufficient to clearly explain the fluctuation in HRV indices (such as RMSSD, HF and SD1) observed in our study during the MC. It might be associated with several factors (baroreflex modulation, vascular peripheral resistance and/or thermoregulation) known to modulate cardiac output.

Our results concerning MVC and EMG do not support our hypothesis. As in several previous studies20–22,48, the Fmax developed by participants did not vary across MC phases. However, Graja et al.19 reported a decrease in isometric knee strength during the late luteal phase compared to the Foll-late and Lut-mid phases. It is worth noting that this strength difference occurred after repeated sprint exercises, while no change was observed in isometric strength, muscle activity, or neuromuscular efficiency before the sprinting exercise. Eston and Burke49 suggested that short-duration exercises (less than three minutes) primarily rely on anaerobic energy systems and may be less sensitive to hormonal fluctuations during the MC.

Conversely, several studies have shown improved upper-body strength during the Foll-late phase15,16,50, when estradiol concentration is highest. For some, estrogen appears to have a neuroexcitatory effect that may enhance muscle activation17,18. For others, estrogen could have a long term effect on the contractile proteins by enhancing synthesis of muscle proteins51. However, while estrogen has been suggested to exert anabolic effects on muscle tissue52, its short-term influence under natural cyclical fluctuations may be insufficient to induce measurable strength changes. Bernstein & Behringer1 point out that the evidence in eumenorrheic women is still limited and contradictory. Interestingly, the study of Dam et al.50 provided additional insights: a decrease in upper-body strength during Mens phase was observed in healthy women, but these changes were not correlated with sex hormone levels. Instead, performance was associated with psychological factors like motivation and perceived enjoyment, which suggest that, in women, muscle strength may be more influenced by psychological state than hormonal variations.

About psychological measures, no meaningful change in motivation or overall emotional profile (POMS) were found across MC phases, except for an increased depression score during the estimated Foll-late phase compared to Lut-mid phase. These findings partially differ from some literature suggesting mood and motivation changes linked to hormonal fluctuations. For example, Kikuchi et al.53 reported reduced plasma serotonin during the late luteal phase, associated with negative mood, while Amin et al.54 emphasized the effect of estrogen-serotonin interaction on mood. These effects may be explained by estrogen’s direct action on the serotonergic system. Indeed, one study found that estrogen can increase serotonin availability by modulating the activity of tryptophan hydroxylase-2, the key enzyme in serotonin synthesis in the brain55. Additionally, studies by Cook et al.28 and Crewther & Cook56 reported a relationship between salivary testosterone variations, motivation, and physical performance, although this link appears stronger in competitive or high-stress sports contexts. The lack of motivational variation in our protocol, measured via a Likert scale before testing, could be explained by the emotionally neutral testing environment and moderate participant engagement. Moreover, it is important to note that POMS mood scores can be heavily influenced by contextual factors unrelated to the MC, such as stress, personal events, sleep quality, or daily mental load. Thus, the temporary increase in depression scores during the Foll-late phase may reflect inter-individual variability or personal circumstances at the time. Moreover, the depression scores observed in our sample were relatively low (average scores below 5), compared with those reported in women with similar academic backgrounds (depression score : 10.38 ± 9.16)38. Finally, Graja et al.19, who also reported no changes in psychological responses across the MC in athletes, highlighted that hormonal variations do not systematically impact mood and may be moderated by external factors.

This study identified a significant correlation between perceived vigour and EMG activity recorded at the onset of a MVC, potentially reflecting earlier motor command activation. Thus, the positive association between vigour and initial EMG may reflect greater neural activation individuals reporting higher vigour. Early EMG amplitude reflects the central nervous system capacity to rapidly activate high-threshold motor units57,58.

Importantly, additional findings showed that autonomic markers were also related to explosive force production, with a positive association between LFnu and RFD (ρ = 0.419, p = 0.006) and a negative association between HFnu and RFD (ρ = -0.42, p = 0.006), suggesting that shifts in normalized HRV spectral components may be associated with rapid force production capacity. Together, these results indicate that both psychological state (perceived vigour) and cardiac autonomic regulation may be associated with neuromuscular performance, albeit through potentially distinct mechanisms. While vigour appears to be associated with enhanced early neural activation, HRV modulation seems to be more closely the expression of mechanical force, such as RFD. However, this elevated neural activation did not systematically translate into higher maximal force, highlighting the multifactorial nature of force production. RFD and MVC are influenced not only by neural drive but also by muscle-tendon stiffness, intermuscular coordination, and technical execution. The findings therefore emphasize the relevance of combining psychological, autonomic, and neuromuscular markers when investigating performance regulation. Perceived vigour may enhance preparatory neural activation, while autonomic regulation may be associated with the ability to translate this activation into explosive force output. Future research should explore whether these mechanisms can be jointly optimized through psychological or physiological interventions.

This study offers valuable insights for the field of sport and healthy, active, naturally menstruating women. The lower vagally related HRV indices during the estimated Lut-mid phase highlights HRV as a sensitive marker of autonomic fluctuations across the MC, which could be useful for tracking physiological shifts in healthy active women. Importantly, additional analyses revealed substantial inter- and intra-individual variability (see Supplementary Table S1), indicating that responses to menstrual cycle phases are highly individualized.

The absence of significant changes in neuromuscular parameters suggests that force production potential does not appear to be markedly affected by menstrual phase, at least in women presenting normal, physiological MC. However, the correlation between perceived vigour and EMG activity during RFD suggests that psychological states may influence motor drive. These findings support a more individualized training approach, integrating both objective physiological indicators and subjective readiness markers.

The present study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the absence of hormonal assays to confirm estrogen and progesterone levels for each cycle phase. Thus, it cannot be ruled out that some participants may have experienced luteal phase deficiency, a condition marked by inadequate progesterone production during the luteal phase. Second, the lack of a control group with a stable or suppressed hormonal profile (e.g., oral contraceptive users) limits the ability to isolate the effects of endogenous hormonal fluctuations. Third, sleep quality, dietary intake, training load, and daily stress were not objectively monitored. Although psychological states related to perceived stress (fatigue, vigour, tension) were assessed using the POMS questionnaire, these measures do not replace objective monitoring of external factors that may influence both psychological and performance-related outcomes. Finally, participants were engaged in a variety of physical activities and often practiced multiple sports across different modalities (e.g., endurance, resistance, and mixed type), which may have contributed to inter-individual variability in the observed responses. Taken together, these factors should be considered when interpreting the present findings.

Conclusion

The results of this study suggest that HRV indices fluctuate across the MC in healthy, naturally menstruating active women, with a lower vagally related HRV indices observed during the estimated Lut-mid phase. In contrast, no significant changes were observed in muscle strength, mood (except for the depression score), or motivation across the different phases of the cycle.
