Incorporating game-based language learning into vocational education: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Jing Zhang, Siti Zuraidah Md Osman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe integration of game-based learning (GBL) into vocational education remains under-explored, and this research aims to explore the impact of a GBL module on learner achievement, foreign language enjoyment and learner autonomy based on the theoretical framework of Flow Theory. The quasi-experimental mixed-method design was adopted with an experimental group (60 students) and a control group (66 students). After the six-week intervention, the quantitative results revealed that the GBL module significantly enhanced learner achievement and foreign language enjoyment, but no statistical difference was observed in terms of learner autonomy. The qualitative findings highlighted the strengths of the GBL module in enhancing language skills, facilitating flexible and personalized learning, and also underscored the weaknesses such as insufficient game features and technological issues. These findings not only operationalized the Flow Theory to develop a GBL module tailored to the vocational language learning, but also provided empirical evidence for the potential of game-based learning to foster cognitive, affective and behavioral learning outcomes in the vocational setting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58165-y.Keywords: Game-based learning, Vocational education, Language learning, Foreign language enjoyment, Learner autonomySubject terms: Education, Information systems and information technology, Language and linguistics, Language and linguistics, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Vocational college students are often stereotyped as having low levels of English proficiency, largely due to the current university admission system in China1,2. A survey conducted by the Ministry of Education found that 48% of vocational college students scored below 90 points (out of a total of 150) in the College Entrance English Examination, while only 7% scored above 1203. These figures indicate substantially lower performance compared with their undergraduate counterparts. This performance gap is also associated with differences in learner autonomy4. Students in Chinese vocational colleges tend to rely heavily on teacher-centred instruction and rote memorisation, rather than engaging in autonomous learning driven by intrinsic motivation5. Another issue is the lack of learner enjoyment, which significantly hinders English language acquisition. Without an enjoyable learning experience, vocational students with lower language proficiency often underestimate their abilities while overestimating the difficulties involved in language learning, which negatively affects both their self-efficacy and their learning outcomes6. Cheng, et al.7 also identified enjoyment as the most influential positive emotion among vocational learners according to results of the latent profile analysis, and indicated higher levels of enjoyment were associated with better self-regulation, stronger motivation, and lower levels of procrastination.

Game-based learning, as an innovative pedagogical approach, integrates game elements into educational curricula to enhance language learning outcomes8. Previous studies have validated a strong relationship between game-based learning and language acquisition across several domains, including cognitive5,9,10, behavioural11,12, affective13–15, and motivational outcomes16,17. However, not all learners benefit equally. Fan and Wang18 found that students with limited autonomy and weak self-directed learning skills gain fewer benefits from competitive interactions in game-based learning environments. Despite its potential, the application of game-based learning to support language learning among vocational students in China remains underexplored. A systematic review by Dahalan, et al.19 reported that most research in vocational education has focused on engineering (29%) and healthcare (24%), with comparatively little attention given to language learning. This gap highlights the need for more carefully designed game-based learning activities tailored to vocational learners, which is essential to improve both learning experiences and outcomes19,20.

Although previous studies have underscored the value of game-based learning, several research gaps remain. First, most studies have focused on general education settings or non-language disciplines, while relatively few have examined the application of GBL to vocational English learning, particularly within the Chinese vocational college context19. Second, although learner engagement, motivation, and enjoyment are widely discussed in the GBL literature, few studies explicitly integrate Flow Theory as a design framework for developing game-based language learning activities21. As a result, the theoretical mechanisms through which GBL may benefit vocational learners remain insufficiently specified. Third, prior research has tended to focus on isolated learning outcomes, such as achievement or motivation, rather than examining cognitive, affective, and behavioural outcomes in an integrated manner22. Fourth, there is a need for more methodologically robust evidence, particularly from quasi-experimental and mixed-methods studies, to better explain the effects of GBL on language learning processes in vocational education23. It therefore remains unclear whether GBL can improve language performance, foreign language enjoyment and learner autonomy among vocational students.

To address these gaps, this study adopts a quasi-experimental design to examine the effects of a GBL module on vocational college students’ language achievement, foreign language enjoyment, and learner autonomy. The study makes four contributions to the literature. First, by focusing on vocational learners in China, this study addresses an under-explored field of vocational language education and provides an exemplar of designing a GBL module for vocational students with language learning difficulties. Second, the GBL module is designed in accordance with the principles of Flow Theory, including challenge–skill balance, timely feedback, and immersion. Third, the study examines the impact of GBL on multiple learning outcomes across cognitive, affective, and behavioural dimensions. Finally, the use of both quantitative and qualitative data provides a more comprehensive understanding of how GBL operates in the vocational education context.

Literature review

Game-based learning

Game-based learning involves the design and use of complete game environments in which the learning process is supported by game mechanics, dynamics, and elements24. Game mechanics, such as challenge, feedback, and rewards, refer to the rules, actions, processes, and control systems that structure gameplay and support learner engagement8,25,26. Game dynamics are associated with the broader patterns of interaction and emotional responses that emerge during gameplay. These include aspects such as competition, achievement, status, and self-expression within clearly defined rules27,28. Game elements, by contrast, constitute the concrete features incorporated into the game environment, including points, rewards, leaderboards, levels, challenges, and feedback29,30. These elements operationalise the design and help to enhance learner motivation and engagement. The effective integration of game elements, mechanics, and dynamics is therefore essential to align gameplay with educational objectives and to ensure that learning experiences are both engaging and pedagogically meaningful.

Previous studies have shown that game-based learning can serve as an innovative approach to enhancing language learning achievement. For example, Peterson31 reported that simulation games can promote learner engagement, motivation, and positive affective outcomes, based on a review of studies on digital simulation games in language acquisition published between 2005 and 2020. Similarly, Foroutan Far and Taghizadeh32 conducted an experiment in an Iranian language institute and found that both digital and non-digital forms of game-based learning had positive effects on learners’ acquisition of English collocations, as evidenced by questionnaire and interview data. However, game-based learning does not consistently produce positive outcomes in language acquisition. Some studies have reported limited or inconclusive effects in educational interventions33,34. In addition, the application of game-based learning in vocational education has received relatively little attention, despite its potential to improve learning outcomes23. Dahalan, et al.19 further noted that research on game-based language learning remains underdeveloped compared with other fields, such as healthcare and engineering. These limitations highlight the need for further research into the instructional design of game-based learning in vocational education. In particular, more studies are required to identify strategies of designing GBL activities that effectively support learner achievement in this context.

Foreign language enjoyment

The rise of positive psychology has led to increased attention to positive emotions, such as enjoyment, in the language acquisition process35–37. Foreign language enjoyment refers to the feelings of happiness, interest, and satisfaction when learners perceive that their psychological needs are met during language learning38. Li39 conceptualises learner enjoyment as a positive emotional state arising during language learning activities, which supports engagement across different learning environments. Foreign language enjoyment is influenced by both internal and external factors. Internal factors include age, gender, personality, and language proficiency, while external factors include classroom activities, teachers’ use of the target language, and teaching styles40.

Recent empirical studies suggest that enjoyable experiences in game-based learning can motivate learners to sustain their efforts in second language acquisition14,41,42. Liu, et al.43 implemented a three-month game-based learning intervention among Iranian students and found significant improvements in both foreign language enjoyment and learning outcomes in the experimental group. Similarly, Zhou13 examined the use of Duolingo and reported that Chinese college students experienced increased enjoyment and developed a more positive self-concept during language learning. However, these positive effects are not always sustained over time. Waluyo and Balazon44 investigated the dynamic development of foreign language enjoyment in a game-based learning context and observed an initially high level of enjoyment, followed by no statistically significant difference across subsequent cycles. This pattern may be explained by a novelty effect associated with game-based learning.

Learner autonomy

Learner autonomy develops through active engagement in the learning process. According to Littlewood45, autonomous learners place strong emphasis on managing their own learning behaviours by acquiring strategies that enable independent learning. Within a learner-centred paradigm, Benson46 defines learner autonomy as ‘an internal capacity of learners to take charge of, responsibility for, or control over their own learning’. Murray47 further conceptualises autonomy as a process that involves setting goals, selecting materials, applying strategies, monitoring progress, and engaging in self-evaluation and reflection. However, a recent scoping review indicates that intervention studies remain insufficient to identify effective methods for fostering learner autonomy48.

Empirical research has explored the potential of game-based learning to support the development of learner autonomy. For example, Chu, et al.49 employed a quasi-experimental design to examine self-regulated vocabulary learning through games and found improvements in both vocabulary development and self-regulation, attributed to goal setting and increased strategic engagement. Similarly, Nguyen11 reported that students using Wordwall games demonstrated enhanced autonomy through improved planning, greater control over learning content, and more effective self-evaluation. Pham, et al.50 also found that students engaged in game-based learning showed notable gains in both academic performance and learner autonomy. However, the evidence is not consistent. Some studies suggest that game-based learning does not necessarily lead to improved autonomy. For instance, Zhou13 found no significant differences in autonomy, competence, or relatedness between experimental and control groups following a game-based language learning intervention. These contradictory findings show that the relationship between game-based learning and learner autonomy remains unclear. Moreover, contextual factors appear to play a critical role. Al-Obaydi, et al.51 found that young learners may struggle to develop autonomy in digital gaming environments without sufficient teacher guidance. These inconsistencies highlight the need for further research to clarify the conditions under which game-based learning can effectively promote learner autonomy.

Theoretical framework: flow theory

Flow theory provides a robust framework for designing the GBL module and for examining its effects on the language learning experience52. Flow is defined as ‘a state of concentration or complete absorption in an activity and the situation at hand’53. The immersive nature of educational games makes them well suited to inducing flow states, which can enhance language learning outcomes. Empirical studies support this relationship. Chou, et al.54 used a quasi-experimental design to investigate a cognitive-based game mechanism and found that it significantly enhanced learners’ flow states, which in turn increased behavioural engagement. Similarly, Hong, et al.55 examined flow in online Chinese character recognition games and reported a positive effect on language learning progress.

Flow theory highlights the importance of designing games that capture learners’ attention while providing an optimal level of challenge aligned with their linguistic abilities56. The concept of flow, originally proposed by Csikszentmihalyi57, comprises nine dimensions: challenge–skill balance, clear goals, sense of control, immediate feedback, autotelic experience, loss of self-consciousness, transformation of time, intense concentration, and the merging of action and awareness. Figure 1 illustrates the game features and expected learning outcomes in this study, as informed by Flow Theory. The scaffolded content is adapted from the College English syllabus to ensure an appropriate balance between challenge and learners’ language proficiency. Gamified rewards, including badges, points, and leaderboards, provide immediate and explicit feedback as learners complete tasks, thereby supporting an enjoyable learning experience within the Conbrie app. In addition, the use of daily learning plans, combined with continuous cycles of action and feedback, helps learners develop a stronger sense of control over their learning process. This, in turn, supports the development of learner autonomy over time. Overall, flow theory informs both the design of game features and the expected effects of the GBL module on learner achievement, foreign language enjoyment, and learner autonomy.

The present study

Previous studies have examined the effects of game-based learning on learner achievement, foreign language enjoyment, and learner autonomy. However, findings have been inconsistent, and limited attention has been paid to game-based language learning among vocational college students. To address these gaps, the present study draws on Flow Theory and adopts a quasi-experimental design to answer the following research questions.

RQ1. Does the game-based learning module have a positive effect on learner achievement?

RQ2. Does the game-based learning module have a positive effect on foreign language enjoyment?

RQ3. Does the game-based learning module have a positive effect on learner autonomy?

RQ4. What are students’ perceptions of the strengths and weaknesses of the game-based learning module?

Methods

Participants

This study was approved by the ethics committee of the authors’ institution and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. A mixed-methods quasi-experimental design was employed to examine the effects of the GBL module on learner achievement, foreign language enjoyment, and learner autonomy among vocational college students. A purposive sampling strategy was used to meet specific inclusion criteria, targeting vocational learners who experience difficulties with traditional language instruction58,59. Participants were required to be enrolled in a vocational college and to be taking College English as a compulsory course during the 2024–2025 academic year. The study included 126 students from two intact classes. The sample comprised 94 female and 32 male students, with a mean age of 18.65 years. Group assignment was determined randomly using a coin toss to reduce selection bias. As a result, 60 students (43 female, 17 male) were assigned to the experimental group, and 66 students (51 female, 15 male) to the control group. All participants were majoring in Animal Science and were taught by the same instructor, who had received training in delivering the GBL module. The two groups were comparable in terms of English language proficiency. Independent-samples t-tests of pre-test scores confirmed that there were no significant differences between the groups prior to the intervention.

Instruments

Learner achievement was assessed using achievement tests aligned with the learning objectives, content, and syllabus of the College English course for vocational learners. The tests comprised 41 items, including vocabulary matching, multiple-choice questions, reading comprehension, and essay writing. They were administered twice, before and after the intervention, to measure changes in students’ English proficiency. The pre-test and post-test were designed to be equivalent in difficulty.

Two questionnaires were used to measure foreign language enjoyment and learner autonomy. Foreign language enjoyment was assessed using the Chinese version of the Foreign Language Enjoyment Scale developed by Li, et al.60, which demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.833). Learner autonomy was measured using an adapted questionnaire based on Xu61 and Lin62, which showed high reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.947). Both instruments were reviewed by three experts with PhD qualifications to ensure the content validity.

In addition, semi-structured interviews were conducted to gain deeper insights into students’ perceptions of learning English through the GBL module. The interview protocol was reviewed by three experts to ensure its validity before the intervention. The interview included the following questions: (1) How do you perceive the game-based learning module? Is it user-friendly, and does it support language learning? (2) Does the module help you understand reading texts? What difficulties did you encounter? (3) Did you experience any problems when using the game tools? How did you address them? (4) How would you describe your levels of foreign language enjoyment and learner autonomy during the learning process? What factors helped you complete the module? (5) What are your views on learning English through a game-based approach? What aspects of the GBL module could be improved?

Procedure

Prior to the intervention, the GBL module was designed based on the ASSURE model, which structures instructional design into six stages, including analysing learners, stating objectives, selecting media and materials, utilising media and materials, requiring learner participation, and evaluation and revision63. Two digital tools were selected to support the game-based learning activities, namely, the Conbrie app and Doubao AI. These tools were used across three instructional topics: money management, time management, and part-time jobs. The Conbrie app was chosen for its suitability for game-based learning, as it integrates multiple modes designed to enhance retention through game elements. Figure 2 presents screenshots of the app, which includes features such as scanning, listening, memorisation, planning, practice, and testing modes. In addition, Doubao AI was used as a learning assistant to support students’ preparation for group discussions and debates.

Basic functions of the Conbrie App.

Following a mixed-methods quasi-experimental design, data collection consisted of a quantitative phase followed by a qualitative phase. Prior to the intervention, the instructor attended a training session to get familiar with both the GBL procedures and the module content. A pilot study was also conducted to assess the reliability of the instruments and to refine the GBL module. The intervention lasted six weeks. In the first week, the pre-test and questionnaires were administered to all participants. From the second to the seventh week, different learning activities were implemented in the experimental and control groups, as illustrated in Fig. 3. To ensure implementation fidelity, researchers monitored the delivery of the GBL module using a classroom observation checklist. In addition, system logs from the Conbrie app recorded an average of 20 min of active gameplay per session, confirming that the intervention was consistently delivered.

Different learning activities completed by two groups.

After the intervention, the two questionnaires were re-administered, and participants completed the achievement test again as a post-test. Semi-structured interviews were then conducted with a sub-sample of five participants, selected through stratified purposive sampling. The interviewees comprised two high achievers, two average achievers, and one low achiever. Given the homogeneity of the sample in terms of academic major and English proficiency, data saturation was reached by the fifth interview, with no new themes emerging64. Participants were informed of the research objectives and selected preferred time slots for online interviews via the Tencent Meeting app. To facilitate communication and ensure clarity, all interviews were conducted in Chinese and recorded in real time.

Data analysis

Quantitative data, including the questionnaire responses and achievement test scores, were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 25). Descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations) were first calculated for the three variables. One-way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was then employed as the inferential statistical technique to address the first three research questions. Prior to conducting the ANCOVA, key assumptions were tested, including normality, homogeneity of regression slopes, and equality of error variances.

Qualitative data from the semi-structured interviews were analysed to address the fourth research question. The analysis followed the six-phase framework of thematic analysis proposed by Braun and Clarke65. Researchers first familiarised themselves with the data and then coded meaningful segments of participants’ responses. These initial codes were subsequently organised into broader themes, which were reviewed and refined to reflect patterns in participants’ perceptions. The themes were then interpreted in relation to the research question, with detailed descriptions developed to support the analysis. In the final stage, findings were reported with illustrative quotations to convey participants’ perspectives. The coding procedures are presented in Table S1 in the Appendix.

Results

Quantitative findings

Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations, were first calculated for all variables, as presented in Table 1. Both groups showed increases across all three variables following the intervention. However, the experimental group demonstrated greater improvements than the control group. Specifically, participants in the experimental group showed notable gains in achievement (pre-test: M = 46.97, SD = 13.23; post-test: M = 60.23, SD = 9.64), foreign language enjoyment (pre-test: M = 3.56, SD = 0.68; post-test: M = 3.93, SD = 0.57), and learner autonomy (pre-test: M = 3.34, SD = 0.74; post-test: M = 3.58, SD = 0.58). These descriptive findings suggest that students who engaged with the GBL module experienced positive changes in their language learning outcomes.

To address the first research question, a statistically significant difference was found in learner achievement, F(1, 123) = 12.409, p < 0.01, partial η2 = 0.092. According to Cohen67, a partial eta squared value below 0.059 indicates a small effect, values between 0.059 and 0.138 indicate a medium effect, and values above 0.138 indicate a large effect. The observed effect size therefore falls within the medium range. This suggests that the GBL module had a moderate positive impact on language achievement among vocational college students.

Regarding the second research question, the one-way ANCOVA results demonstrated a statistically significant effect of the GBL module on foreign language enjoyment. As shown in Table 2, there was a significant group effect, F(1, 123) = 4.568, p < 0.05, partial η2 = 0.036. This effect size is considered small67, indicating that approximately 3.6% of the variance in post-test scores for foreign language enjoyment can be attributed to the intervention.

With regard to the third research question, no statistically significant difference was observed between the experimental and control groups in post-test scores for learner autonomy, F(1, 123) = 0.233, p > 0.05. In other words, although both groups showed an increase in learner autonomy following the intervention, the GBL module did not have a statistically significant effect on learner autonomy among vocational college students.

Qualitative findings

To address the fourth research question, thematic analysis was made to interpret participants’ descriptions of their learning experiences with the GBL module. Overall, interviewees frequently highlighted the advantages of the module in supporting vocabulary learning and language acquisition. For example, one participant noted that the sequential organisation of vocabulary items, aligned with the reading texts, facilitated understanding of word usage in meaningful contexts and supported contextual comprehension.

S3: It listed words in sequence based on reading texts, making me easier to memorize words by understanding the context in the passages. Additionally, the process of grammar learning from reading texts was also quite beneficial for vocabulary acquisition.

The ease of use and flexibility of the GBL module emerged as another prominent theme in the interview data. Participants reported that the simple and intuitive design of the game tools, together with clear user guidance, enhanced accessibility and usability. Several interviewees also explained that features such as repeated pronunciation playback supported vocabulary learning by reinforcing retention. In addition, the module enabled learners to make effective use of fragmented time for self-study, allowing them to integrate English language learning into their daily routines.

S2: It had user-friendly interface for me to create and import learning cards…Additionally, it was very convenient for using fragmented time for learning, so that it was beneficial to finish personalized reviews.

Participants also highlighted the value of AI-assisted functions in supporting personalised learning. These features enabled learners to extract key knowledge points efficiently and address individual gaps in their English language learning. In particular, the integration of Doubao AI supported vocabulary acquisition by summarising key terms and generating quizzes to reinforce learning. Such AI-driven functions allowed learners to tailor their study plans to their specific needs, fostering a more adaptive and targeted approach to vocabulary development and language learning.

S1: Another key feature lies in its AI capability, which could extract key knowledge points and integrate AI with digital tools to help me address gaps in my knowledge…The AI feature helped summarise vocabulary and sometimes provided quizzes to consolidate my vocabulary learning….

In contrast, interviewees also identified several limitations of the GBL module. Some participants noted that the range of game elements was relatively limited and expressed a desire for additional social features, such as peer collaboration and competition, to enrich the learning experience. Technical constraints were also reported. One participant indicated that large volumes of learning content could cause delays or system instability, particularly on devices with lower processing capacity.

S2: Adding more social features like competition and collaboration will enhance my future learning experience.

S5: The delay issue occurred due to internet speed or the compatibility of the other apps with devices.

Discussion

The significant positive effect of the GBL module on learner achievement, as evidenced by both quantitative and qualitative findings, indicates a meaningful pedagogical impact that the GBL module contributes to the improvement of language learning outcomes among vocational college students. Such finding is consistent with previous research highlighting the benefits of game-based learning in language acquisition13,32. One possible explanation lies in the cognitive demands required in the GBL module. Game features such as challenges and human–game interaction require sustained cognitive engagement, particularly when learners acquired vocabularies, contextual usage, and task instructions. This engagement facilitated the transfer of newly acquired vocabularies from short-term to long-term memory8. In addition, the qualitative findings suggested that the integration of reading texts within the GBL module supported learners in acquiring, applying, and consolidating knowledge in meaningful contexts68. The use of multimedia elements in digital game tasks further enhanced the recognition of form–meaning relationships. Compared with conventional instructional methods, these features contributed to the development of learners’ receptive vocabulary knowledge69. Moreover, the combination of verbal and non-verbal input enabled learners to process vocabulary through multimodal methods, thereby reducing cognitive load and supporting more effective learning70.

With regard to foreign language enjoyment, the study identified a statistically significant difference between the two groups, although the effect size was small. The triangulation of quantitative and qualitative evidence suggests that the GBL module contributed to a positive shift in learners’ emotional experiences. This shift may help to alleviate demotivation in language learning among vocational students. These findings are consistent with previous research13,43,71,72, which has shown that digital game-based tools can enhance learners’ enjoyment. In these studies, students in experimental groups consistently reported higher levels of enjoyment than those in traditional instructional settings. The enhancement of foreign language enjoyment were also evidenced from the qualitative findings in this study. Interviewees described how specific game features, such as rewards and background music, elicited positive emotional responses during the learning process, particularly when compared with conventional paper-based vocabulary learning.

However, no statistically significant difference was observed in learner autonomy in the post-test results, although interviewees shared their feelings of stronger control over their learning processes. The present finding is consistent with Zhou13, who reported no significant changes in autonomy, competence, or relatedness following a game-based language learning intervention, which was limited to a short duration. Similarly, Al-Obaydi, et al.51 found that autonomy was difficult to develop among younger learners in unstructured digital gaming environments, particularly in the absence of sufficient teacher guidance. In addition, Qiao, et al.73 and Maimaiti and Hew74 did not identify significant differences between groups in terms of self-regulated learning behaviours. These studies suggests that, unlike learning achievement and foreign language enjoyment, learner autonomy represents a developmental construct that requires long-term cultivation. Autonomous learning behaviours are not always maintained, even when game elements such as rewards and competition temporarily enhance engagement11,50. The insignificant effect on learner autonomy in this study indicates that future design of GBL modules for vocational learners should incorporate explicit metacognitive scaffolding, rather than relying solely on game mechanics, to bridge the gap between engagement in gameplay and the development of autonomous learning behaviours.

Finally, the qualitative findings provide complementary insights into the role of the GBL module in supporting language learning among vocational college students. Participants attributed improvements in vocabulary retention and learning strategies to specific game features, including digital flashcards linked to reading texts, flexible learning opportunities, and personalised learning supported by AI integration. These features allowed learners to have better control by supporting self-paced and personalised learning. Similar qualitative findings were also identified in prior studies13,32,44,75,76. Soyoof, et al.77 reported that learners benefited from vocabulary presented in meaningful contexts within massively multiplayer online games. From the perspective of Flow Theory, such contextualised and interactive tasks could promote immersion by sustaining learners’ attention and involvement in the learning process. On a different note, participants identified several limitations of the GBL module. Concerns regarding limited game features and technical issues suggest that further refinement of game design is needed to maximise the effectiveness of game-based learning. These findings align with prior research indicating that technological constraints can hinder the outcomes of digital learning environments13. Therefore, optimising the functionality of game-based learning platforms, particularly to ensure compatibility across a wider range of mobile devices, may enhance user satisfaction and improve the overall effectiveness of the GBL module.

Based on these findings, the present study provides empirical evidence for the application of Flow Theory in addressing language learning difficulties through a GBL approach. In particular, the results highlight the roles of challenge–skill balance, immediate feedback, and immersion as key mechanisms underlying the effectiveness of the GBL module. The significant improvement in language achievement suggests that the content of the GBL module was appropriately aligned with learners’ existing competence levels, thereby reflecting the principle of challenge–skill balance central to Flow Theory78. In addition, the provision of clear and timely feedback, another core antecedent of flow, enabled learners to monitor their progress, correct errors promptly, and maintain a sense of control throughout the learning process79. This feedback mechanism likely enhanced learners’ confidence and supported sustained engagement in subsequent learning activities80. Furthermore, the game-based learning environment facilitated immersion by promoting continuous cycles of action and response. As a result, learners remained absorbed into the learning tasks, which contributed to more engaging and enjoyable learning experiences43.

From a practical perspective, this study also offers several pedagogical implications for learners, educators, and policymakers seeking to implement game-based learning in vocational education. First, integrating a GBL module into the curriculum does not diminish the central role of instructors. Although the findings indicate that GBL is effective in improving learning achievement, it should not be regarded as a comprehensive solution for fostering learner autonomy. Instead, teachers should use GBL as a means of stimulating foreign language enjoyment and complement it with structured guidance to develop learner autonomy in the long term. Second, at a broader institutional level, vocational colleges should promote the integration of game-based learning across disciplines. This may involve investing in accessible digital platforms that combine appropriate instructional design with game elements and relevant technological tools, thereby encouraging pedagogical innovation. Third, given the increasing prevalence of digital gaming among vocational students, closer collaboration between educators and game developers is essential. Such collaboration can support the design of tailored GBL tools and platforms that integrate language learning with the development of professional competencies.

Conclusion

The results of the intervention in this research demonstrate that the GBL module had significant positive effects on learning achievement and foreign language enjoyment among vocational college students. However, no significant effect was observed for learner autonomy. Qualitative findings further highlighted the strengths of the GBL module, including its effectiveness in enhancing language skills, supporting flexible and personalised learning, and improving learner engagement. At the same time, participants identified several limitations, particularly the lack of social interaction features and the presence of technical constraints. These findings contribute to the application of Flow Theory in the instructional design of game-based learning for vocational learners, with particular emphasis on challenge–skill balance, timely feedback, and immersive learning experiences. In addition, this study provides empirical support for a theory-driven GBL approach tailored to vocational English education, particularly its effectiveness of enhancing achievement and enjoyment while revealing the need for additional support for learner autonomy. As such, it offers a context-specific pedagogical alternative for vocational English teachers and curriculum designers seeking to address learners’ language difficulties through technology-enhanced instruction.

Additionally, the quasi-experimental design adopted in this study entails certain limitations. First, the intervention lasted for six weeks, and the observed effects on foreign language enjoyment and learner achievement may partly reflect a novelty effect associated with the introduction of a new instructional approach. This highlights the need for longitudinal research to examine the sustained impact of the GBL module, particularly in relation to the development of learner autonomy. Second, the study was conducted in a single vocational college and involved students from only one academic major. As a result, the findings may not be generalisable to other contexts. Further studies employing broader and more varied samples from multiple institutions are needed to strengthen the generalisability of findings and to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the effectiveness of GBL in vocational education.
