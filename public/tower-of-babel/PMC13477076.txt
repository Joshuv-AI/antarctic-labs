Understanding late nonfatal events after mechanical aortic valve replacement: Importance of definitions and thoroughness of follow-up
Authors: Harrison R Herrera, Hartzell V Schaff, Joseph A Dearani, Gabor Bagameri, Kevin L Greason, Austin Todd, Juan A Crestanello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe evaluated how event definitions, data-collection methods, and follow-up completeness influence reporting of long-term nonfatal complications after mechanical aortic valve replacement in local versus referral patients.MethodsWe analyzed 558 patients undergoing primary mechanical aortic valve replacement from 2000 to 2023. Patients were stratified by residential distance (<50 miles, local vs >50 miles, referral) to assess completeness of follow-up. Outcomes included major bleeding, neurological thromboembolic events, and survival. Major bleeding was defined by International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis criteria or by restricted International Classification of Diseases codes. Events were captured by manual review and by International Classification of Diseases coding. Propensity score–matched local (n = 186) and referral groups (n = 372) were analyzed using Fine-Gray competing risk models for nonfatal outcomes and Kaplan–Meier analysis for survival.ResultsBaseline and perioperative characteristics of propensity score–matched local and referral patients were comparable. Median clinical follow-up was longer in the less than 50-mile group (10.2 vs 4.1 years, P < .001). Local patients had higher crude event rates for major bleeding (40.9% vs 24.2%, P < .001) and neurological events (13.4% vs 4.0%, P < .001). These differences were mitigated but still statistically significant in time-dependent competing risk analysis. Rates varied with ascertainment method: International Classification of Diseases coding amplified differences compared with manual adjudication. Long-term survival did not differ significantly between groups (P = .44).ConclusionsReporting late nonfatal events after aortic valve replacement varies with event definitions, data-collection methods, and follow-up completeness. Although mortality estimates remain robust, bleeding and stroke may be underestimated in referral patients. Competing risk analysis enables more accurate interpretation of late outcomes.Key Words: aortic valve replacement, ascertainment bias, major bleeding, nonfatal complications, stroke

Nonfatal event incidence varies by definition, adjudication, and follow-up completeness.

Central Message

Late nonfatal event reporting after mechanical AVR is influenced by factors such as event definitions, data-collection methods, and patient residence, requiring cautious interpretation.

Perspective

Missing data in reporting late nonfatal events after mechanical AVR may mislead interpretation and affect clinical accuracy. These data gaps arise from factors such as loss to follow-up due to patient residence, event definitions, data-collection methods, and statistical approaches, potentially biasing assessment of procedural, management strategies, or institutional effects.

Analysis of outcomes after aortic valve replacement (AVR) includes assessment of both mortality and important nonfatal complications, particularly stroke and bleeding events.1,2 Databases such as the National Death Index provide reasonably up-to-date information on patient mortality; however, accurate reporting of nonfatal complications depends on several factors, such as event definitions and methods of data collection, as well as statistical approach and completeness of follow-up, which may be limited by patient residence. Single-institution and national database studies contain outcome information from variable patient encounters at irregular intervals. Because of this variability, some may question the accuracy of reporting of late event rates.

To address the impact of these factors on the accuracy of late event reporting, we analyzed late outcomes in 558 patients undergoing AVR with mechanical valves, comparing local/regional patients within our care network with referral patients who largely obtained medical care at their home institutions. Outcomes were analyzed using alternate event definitions, methods of data collection, and statistical approaches. We hypothesized that capture of nonfatal late events would be under-reported in referral patients who did not receive care at our clinic and that the observed event rates would vary depending on the definition used and statistical approach applied.

Materials and Methods

Patients and Study Design

The Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board in Rochester, Minnesota, approved the study (IRB 25-007336; approved August 1, 2025). Consent was waived. All patients authorized the use of their clinical data for research. Between 2000 and 2023, 3596 patients underwent primary mechanical AVR at our institution. Patients who underwent concomitant procedures (aortic surgery, coronary artery bypass grafting, mitral or tricuspid valve surgery, septal myectomy, or others), nonelective surgery, or prior sternotomy were excluded (n = 2574) (Figure 1). The remaining 1022 patients constituted the study cohort before stratification and matching, as described in the completeness of follow-up section.

Flow diagram of the study population. AVR, Aortic valve replacement.

Data Collection

Clinical and operative data were obtained from a prospectively maintained surgical database and a retrospective review of electronic health records (EHRs). The primary study outcomes were reported major bleeding events, neurological thromboembolic complications, and survival from the surgery date through the last available follow-up visit or electronic documentation in the EHR, up to April 2025. Only the first was included in the analysis for patients with more than 1 event. Vital status was determined through LexisNexis Accurint, which consolidates information from multiple sources, including the Social Security Death Master File and state death records (New York City, NY).

Outcome events were collected by 2 methods. We used end points identified by the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes (ICD 9th and 10th Revisions) and through manual review of EHR documentation, including records from referring providers and external healthcare organizations, and direct patient communication. External records from referral patients were often available only as scanned or electronic documents without ICD coding. Access to external records was facilitated through the Health Information Exchange initiative, which enables cross-institutional record sharing within the Epic system when patients provide explicit consent. Additional clinical information was obtained from follow-up surveys.3

Variation in Definitions of Outcomes

Major bleeding was defined in 2 ways for comparative analysis. A restricted definition, used in many studies and easily obtained from ICD codes, includes gastrointestinal bleeding, intracranial bleeding, and hemoptysis. A broader definition follows the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis (ISTH) criteria:4 (1) fatal bleeding; (2) symptomatic bleeding in a critical area or organ (intracranial, intraspinal, intraocular, retroperitoneal, intra-articular, pericardial, or intramuscular with compartment syndrome); or (3) bleeding resulting in a hemoglobin decrease 2 g/dL or more or requiring transfusion of 2 units or more of whole blood or red cells. Under the ISTH criteria, we included any bleeding type (gastrointestinal, hematuria, menorrhagia, epistaxis, post-traumatic, or postprocedural hemorrhage while on anticoagulation) that met the transfusion or hemoglobin threshold, regardless of whether the event was acute or subacute. We compared rates of late bleeding defined by ICD codes versus bleeding event rates calculated from manual record review using ISTH definitions.

Neurological thromboembolic complications included ischemic stroke and transient ischemic attack (TIA), defined according to the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association.5,6 Stroke was defined as an episode of neurological dysfunction caused by focal cerebral infarction, evidenced by pathology or imaging. TIA was defined as a transient episode of neurological dysfunction caused by focal brain ischemia without acute infarction.

Variation in Completeness of Follow-Up

To assess the effect of follow-up completeness on identification of late nonfatal events, patients were stratified by residential distance from our institution into 2 groups: those local patients residing within 50 miles (<50-mile group), who largely obtained follow-up care at Mayo Clinic, and those living beyond 50 miles (>50-mile group), referral patients, who generally obtained follow-up elsewhere. The 50-mile cutoff was selected as an approximate midpoint between Mayo Clinic and other major healthcare systems in our region.

In this analysis of the influence of completeness of follow-up defined by geographic radius, we used propensity score matching to mitigate any potential bias due to baseline characteristics. The initial study cohort included 1022 patients, 213 in the less than 50 miles radius group and 809 in the more than 50 miles radius group. The variables used in the propensity score and model details are presented in Tables E1 and E2, and Figure E1. After 1:2 matching, the final study cohort included 186 patients in the less than 50 miles group and 372 patients in the more than 50 miles radius group.

Dot plot of standardized mean difference before and after propensity score matching, demonstrating adequate covariate balance after matching (with SMD <0.2 in absolute values). NYHA, New York Heart Association.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were reported as numbers (percentage), and continuous variables were reported as medians (25th through 75th interquartile range [IQR]) or means (SD). To account for baseline differences, our more than 50-mile radius patients were 2:1 propensity score matched to our less than 50-mile radius patients. The propensity scores were derived from a multivariable logistic regression for predicting distance groups using potential confounders. Nearest neighbor matching was used with a caliper of 0.2 of the SD of the propensity scores. Unmatched comparisons between groups were made using Wilcoxon rank-sum or Pearson chi-square tests as appropriate. For the primary outcome, group comparisons of unadjusted cumulative incidence rates for major bleeding and neurological thromboembolic events were estimated using the Fine-Gray sub-distribution hazard regression model with all-cause mortality as the competing risk. The Kaplan–Meier method was used for survival analysis, and differences between groups were analyzed using the 2-tailed log-rank test. Statistical significance was defined at a P < .05. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Study Population and Baseline Patient Characteristics

As seen in Table 1, most baseline characteristics of matched patients were not significantly different compared with those living within 50 miles of the surgical center (N = 186) and those residing farther than 50 miles (N = 372). The mean age was 58 years in both groups, with a similar gender male predominance and White racial composition. Comorbidities associated with cardiovascular risk were comparable between the groups. Aortic valve stenosis was present in more than 84% of patients in both groups, with a mean aortic valve gradient of 47.5 ± 17.4 in local patients versus 51.1 ± 14.6 mm Hg in those residing more than 50 miles from our center. The most common indications for mechanical AVR were bicuspid valve and degenerative fibrocalcific disease. More than 80% of patients in both groups had mild to moderate functional impairment based on the New York Heart Association classification (II and III), with comparable preoperative left ventricular ejection fractions in the 2 groups.

The follow-up time (clinical follow up) for nonfatal events differed significantly (P < .001) between groups, with a median of 10.2 years (IQR, 4.9-17.0) in the less than 50-mile group and 4.1 years (IQR, 0.03-10.6) in the more than 50-mile group, reflecting longer follow-up and more exposure to nonfatal events among local patients.

Preoperative and Early Postoperative Characteristics

Perioperative variables were similar in the matched groups, as detailed in Table 2. Rates of postoperative complications did not differ significantly. However, patients living closer to the surgical center experienced a higher rate of pneumonia (3.2% vs 0%, P < .001) and permanent pacemaker implantation (6.5% vs 3.0%, P = .05). The length of intensive care unit and total in-hospital stay were similar between groups. Risks of reoperation for bleeding and mortality were low and similar in the 2 groups. Notably, the report of readmission within 30 days was significantly more common among patients living closer to the institution (13.5% vs 8.2%, P = .04).

Late Major Bleeding Events

As seen in Table 3, the risk of major bleeding events identified by manual record review and expressed as a simple percentage was significantly greater among patients living less than 50 miles from our center compared with that of patients living a greater distance (40.9% vs 24.2%, P < .001). There was no important difference in bleeding site between the 2 patient groups. When bleeding was analyzed as time-to-event using the Fine-Gray sub-distribution hazard regression model with all-cause mortality as the competing risk, the cumulative incidence of major bleeding increased steadily over follow-up in both distance groups (Figure 2).

Late Neurological Events

Expressed as a simple percentage, the occurrence of neurologic thromboembolic events (stroke determined by manual record review) was significantly higher in the less than 50-mile group compared with the more than 50-mile group (13.4% vs 4.0%, P < .001) (Table 3). This difference was also reflected in the cumulative incidence analysis (Figure 3, A). At 10 years, the estimated cumulative incidence of stroke was 14% in the less than 50-mile group versus 6% in the more than 50-mile group, increasing to approximately 18% and 12% by 20 years (P = .003).

A, Events identified by manual data collection. B, Events identified by ICD code–based collection. Both approaches showed similar cumulative incidence trends between groups, although the results and statistical significance varied by definition.

As shown in Figure 3, B, when late neurologic events were captured by ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes (Table E3), event rates were higher than with manual record review, but still significantly greater rates in local patients compared with those residing more than 50 miles from our center (P < .001). At 10 years postoperatively, the estimated incidence of stroke defined by ICD coding was approximately 15% in the less than 50-mile group compared with 8% in the more than 50-mile group, and by 20 years risks reached approximately 25% versus 13%.

When stroke and TIA were analyzed together based on manual record review (Figure E2, A), the cumulative incidence curves diverged progressively, with significantly higher event rates in the less than 50-mile group (P = .004). At 10 years, the estimated probability of stroke/TIA was 13% in the less than 50-mile group compared with 7% in the more than 50-mile group, and by 20 years the incidences reached approximately 22% and 15%, respectively. Likewise, when stroke/TIA was captured using ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes (Figure E2, B), both event rates and separation between groups were greater. At 10 years, the estimated cumulative incidence of stroke/TIA was 17% in the less than 50-mile group versus 11% in the more than 50-mile group, and by 20 years it reached approximately 30% compared with 20%, respectively (P < .001).

Late Survival

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated no statistically significant difference in overall survival between patients living within 50 miles and those living farther than 50 miles from the surgical center (log-rank P = .44) (Figure 4). Survival probabilities remained high in both groups during the first decade and declined gradually over time; in 10 years, estimated survival was approximately 85% in both groups. By 20 years, survival was slightly higher in the less than 50-mile group (∼65%) compared with the more than 50-mile group (∼55%).

Unadjusted survival of propensity score–matched patients who underwent primary mechanical AVR, stratified by residential distance from the surgical center. Survival was similar for local and referral patients (95% CI).

Discussion

In this study, we examined the impact of event definitions, data-collection methods, statistical approach, and completeness of follow-up—stratified by patient residency—on reporting nonfatal complications after mechanical AVR. We found considerable variability in results depending on these factors, ranging from differences between crude event rates and time-to-event analyses to shifts in trends and statistical significance across stratified groups (>50 miles vs <50 miles). In contrast, mortality data, collected through standardized and highly reliable sources, showed no statistically significant difference between groups, with comparable long-term trends. This detailed analysis provides an important reference against which to interpret nonfatal event reporting. The absence of major differences in baseline characteristics and perioperative outcomes between groups suggests that referral distance was not associated with demographic, clinical, or surgical risk factors.

Our findings highlight the importance of thoroughness of follow-up when interpreting long-term nonfatal outcomes. We selected a 50-mile radius as the cutoff because it represents an approximate midpoint between our center and the nearest major metropolitan area and healthcare systems, beyond which patients were more likely to seek follow-up locally. As hypothesized, patients living closer to our surgical center had significantly longer median clinical follow-up, which led to a higher likelihood of event identification and reporting, and thus higher crude event rates. The proportion of major bleeding and neurological thromboembolic events was approximately 2-fold to 3-fold higher in the less than 50-mile group. However, after time-to-event analysis with adjustment for censoring and competing risks, these differences were attenuated. This suggests that the excess events observed among local patients are partially explained by longer follow-up rather than greater burden of complications.

These observations reinforce a key statistical principle. Analyses based on raw event counts or percentages assume equal follow-up across groups, an assumption appropriate for fixed short-term outcomes such as 30-day complications but inappropriate for long-term outcomes spanning decades. In such settings, unequal follow-up introduces bias and may exaggerate differences. Time-to-event methodology, particularly competing risks analysis for nonfatal outcomes, provides a more accurate assessment of incidence over time and mitigates underreporting bias inherent to referral populations.7,8

An additional insight from this study was the magnitude of variability in event rates depending on the definition and method of data collection. When bleeding was identified only through ICD codes and restricted to gastrointestinal, hemoptysis, and intracranial events, the less than 50-mile group showed a significantly higher incidence compared with that of patients living farther from our center. This difference, although still statistically significant, was mitigated when major bleeding was adjudicated manually using the ISTH definition, highlighting how definitions and ascertainment methods shape outcome reporting. Manual review captures a broader spectrum of clinically relevant events, including those documented in scanned or unstructured records shared via the Health Information Exchange, whereas ICD coding may underestimate events in referral patients due to inconsistent coding. At the same time, ICD codes may overestimate bleeding by classifying minor events as major, because they do not consider hemoglobin decrease or transfusion requirements as specified in the ISTH criteria. These differences likely explain why the less than 50-mile group showed similar trends across both definition analyses, with the broader capture of ISTH offset by the less specific classification of ICD coding.

Our findings also contribute to the ongoing discussion regarding the reliability of nonfatal late complication reporting from referral centers. Critics argue that referral institutions may underestimate complication rates because patients often return to their home communities for long-term care, where complications are managed and recorded elsewhere. Indeed, our findings support this notion but also highlight the important influence of event definitions and data-collection methods on the quantification of late nonfatal events after mechanical AVR.

Limitations

A limitation of the study is that it contains data from a single center and is retrospective. EHR documentation may be incomplete. Although our approach incorporated death as a competing event, cumulative incidence methods may still overestimate risk in the presence of unmeasured factors. We used an arbitrary but practical 50-mile cutoff to identify local and regional patients; using distance from our center as a continuous variable would be expected to add little to the analysis.

Conclusions

Clinicians should carefully examine definitions of events when interpreting late nonfatal complications after mechanical AVR. Future efforts to enhance data exchange, such as broader implementation of the Health Information Exchange initiative, may reduce gaps in accurate documentation of late complications of referral patients. Until then, our findings emphasize that application of time-to-event analysis and recognition of differences in event ascertainment are essential for accurate interpretation of long-term nonfatal events.
