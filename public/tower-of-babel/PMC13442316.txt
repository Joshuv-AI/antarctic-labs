Who Influences Whom? Examining Self-Control and Delinquent Peer Dynamics among Justice-Involved Youth
Authors: Hyunmin Park, Jennifer H. Peck, James V. Ray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The connection between one’s level of self-control and association with delinquent peers has been the subject of ongoing scholarly debate, yet it is unclear if these two constructs operate independently or mutually influence each other over time. Using data from the Pathways to Desistance study, this study longitudinally examined the potential reciprocal relationship between self-control and delinquent peer association. Data from 1,354 adolescents (baseline Mage = 16.04 ± 1.14 years, 86.4% male, 41.4% Black) with previous involvement in the juvenile justice system for serious offending were examined across seven years. Results from a random intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) indicated that both constructs exerted reciprocal effects, where higher self-control aligned with reduced involvement with delinquent peers, and greater delinquent peer association coincided with diminished self-control. However, the influence of self-control on delinquent peer associations became more stable in later waves, and the paths from self-control to delinquent peer association were stronger and observed more frequently than paths from delinquent peer association to self-control. Overall, the findings suggest that while peer groups are important during adolescence, as individuals transition into emerging adulthood, self-control plays an increasingly central role in associating with delinquent peers.

Introduction

The nature of the relationship between self-control and delinquent peer association has been a longstanding topic of debate. One side posits that self-control leads to delinquent peer association (Gottfredson & Hirschi, 1990) while the other side suggests that engaging with delinquent peers plays a role in the development of one’s self-control (Beaver et al., 2009; Kuhn & Laird, 2013). There is also the argument that self-control and delinquent peer association reciprocally influence each other over time (Chapple, 2005). As these contradictory viewpoints raise important theoretical and practical questions regarding the link between self-control and delinquent peer association, it is necessary to concurrently model self-control as a predictor of delinquent peer association and vice versa. However, few studies have tested this notion (cf., Li et al., 2024; Huijsmans et al., 2021). Specifically, prior tests of this bidirectional association have relied on short observation periods, a small number of measurement occasions, and analytic techniques that confound between- and within-individual effects. The present study addresses these gaps to examine the bidirectional relationship of self-control and delinquent peer association over time by utilizing a sample of justice-involved youth who were interviewed at multiple time points over the course of seven years spanning middle to late adolescence.

Self-Control and The Role of Peer Influences

Self-control refers to one’s capacity to resist immediate rewards and consider the long-term consequences of one’s actions (Gottfredson & Hirschi, 1990). Gottfredson and Hirschi (1990) argued that self-control is the sole explanatory variable of analogous and deviant behaviors, rendering all other factors related to antisocial behavior (e.g., age, race/ethnicity, biological sex, and socioeconomic status (SES)) as spurious in this relationship. Additionally, they contended that all individuals are born lacking self-control, that it is acquired through socialization, is set around ages 10–12, and remains relatively stable from this point in time. Specifically, self-control theory posits that the acquisition of self-control relies heavily on parenting, and largely discounts other sources of socialization as important. To date, research has supported the assertion that parenting plays an influential and robust role in the development of self-control (Li et al., 2019). However, the possibility that alternative social factors impact the development of self-control has yet to be ruled out (Burt, 2020; Li et al., 2019).

In fact, one contextual factor that has been identified as important for the development of self-control is peer influence (Huijsmans et al., 2021). Adolescence is a particularly salient period in which engaging in peer groups plays a critical role in the socialization process (Thornberry, 1987), and its influence of peers likely increases as youth spend more of their time with friends and away from their parents (Thornberry & Krohn, 2019). The impact of peer socialization on behavior is well established in the literature (Gallupe et al., 2019; Giletta et al., 2021), and research has demonstrated the age-graded effect of peers by showing that this social factor becomes more influential in predicting offending behaviors later in adolescence (Bryson et al., 2023). Longitudinal research has also found that peer dynamics are associated with later behavioral outcomes. For example, engaging with prosocial peers has been found to reduce subsequent levels of reactive aggression across time (Tu et al., 2025). Research has also suggested that the longitudinal associations between one’s own delinquent behavior and the delinquency of one’s peers is mutually reinforcing over time and follows similar patterns of development (Kim & Lee, 2023).

Peer Influence and the Mutual Development of Self-Control

Gottfredson and Hirschi (1990) contended that “birds of a feather flock together”, which implies a selection effect into peer groups, rather than peers playing a role in the development of self-control. This notion relies on the assumption that self-control is immutable to change, making it a relatively stable influence on outcomes across the life course. However, research has shown that self-control is not entirely stable during adolescence (Diamond, 2016). More importantly, peer influences account for some of the observed variation in the development of self-control (Jennings et al., 2013). It has also been proposed that self-control is more malleable to developmental changes and situational processes. At the same time, self-control remains a key factor for understanding selection into both positive and negative life experiences, such as peer group affiliations, which can reinforce behavioral patterns (Pratt, 2016). For example, desistance from offending during emerging adulthood was observed to be partially due to increases in self-control, which resulted in decreased associations with delinquent peers (Crank & Brezina, 2019). Therefore, it is likely that peers can influence one’s level of self-control to result in changes in behavior (Meldrum et al., 2012). Theoretically, peers may act as models to imitate and reinforce attitudes and behaviors (Akers & Jennings, 2015). Similar to parents, peers can act as disciplinarians through such mechanisms as acceptance, rejection, and shaming to reward or punish unacceptable behavior (Meldrum & Hay, 2012). As such, these mechanisms suggest that changes in one’s peer associations (such as levels of peer deviance), may result in subsequent increases or decreases in self-control.

The empirical reality is that the association between self-control and peer influence across time is most likely characterized by a reciprocal relationship (Farely & Kim-Spoon, 2014; Huijsmans et al., 2021). Youth may identify and join like-minded peer groups with similar characteristics. At the same time, peers may also shape one’s level of self-control in both positive (prosocial) or negative (antisocial) ways (Chapple, 2005). For instance, individuals with relatively low levels of self-control may see the social benefits (e.g., peer acceptance, social elevation) of engaging in risk-taking and reckless behaviors within their delinquent peer groups. This situation, in turn, may further entrench youth into delinquent peer groups, block access to prosocial peers, and reinforce behavioral repertoires that result in further reductions of self-control. For example, self-control has been identified as one of several factors that predict gang involvement. However, it has also been observed that during periods of active gang membership, youth experience reductions in self-control (see Pyrooz et al., 2021).

Despite largely being based on unidirectional tests (Jennings et al., 2013; Meldrum & Hay, 2012), the literature supports a bidirectional association between self-control and peer dynamics, where youth with limited self-control have trouble forming positive peer associations, but at the same time, a negative peer environment (e.g., hanging with delinquent friends) can hinder the development of self-control (Farely & Kim-Spoon, 2014). There have been limited tests of the bidirectional relationship between self-control and peers (cf., Huijsmans et al., 2021; Li et al., 2024). For example, Huijsmans and colleagues (2021) examined the reciprocal relationship between self-control and peer antisocial behavior during middle adolescence (ages thirteen to seventeen years old) using data collected from three waves at two-year intervals using cross-lagged panel models (CLPM). Cross-lagged effects indicated that higher self-control decreased the probability of associating with antisocial peers at a similar level across time. However, association with antisocial peers did not influence self-control in the second wave but did predict reduced self-control in the third wave. The authors acknowledged that the two-year interval between waves may be too long to capture the dynamic nature of peer relationships.

Most recently, Li and colleagues (2024) tested the reciprocal relationship between self-control and antisocial peer association during early adolescence among a school-based sample of Chinese youth. They used a random intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) to distinguish within-individual changes from between-individual changes to account for selection effects (i.e., individuals with low self-control may seek out like-minded youth), which overcomes limitations of the CLPM and allows for stronger confidence in causal associations. Results indicated that self-control and antisocial peer association were reciprocally related, where higher self-control consistently decreased the probability of associating with delinquent peers, and increased association with delinquent peers decreased one’s level of self-control over time. However, they tested the relationship between self-control and antisocial peer association over a two-year period in early adolescence (ten to twelve years old), which may not be enough time to fully capture the developmental dynamics of peer influence (Thornberry & Krohn, 2019).

In sum, the two studies that have examined the reciprocal relationship of self-control and peer influence suggest that the two constructs mutually influence each other over time. That is, changes in self-control lead to subsequent changes in delinquent peer association, while changes in delinquent peer association resulted in changes in self-control. Thus, the limited research suggests that self-control is influenced by peers, which is counter to Gottfredson and Hirschi’s (1990) arguments. These findings are more consistent with an age-graded perspective in which peers become increasingly influential in shaping behavior and dispositions as youth enter adolescence (Thornberry & Krohn, 2019). This situation may occur through reinforcement of behavioral repertoires consistent with cumulative continuity (Moffitt, 1993). At the same time, the findings leave open the possibility that environmental factors (such as reduced delinquent peer affiliation or increased prosocial peer association) can alter one’s underlying disposition. However, observing this process over a longer period and with different populations can shed more light on the direction and timing of this process.

Current Study

While recent studies have examined the reciprocal relationship between self-control and delinquent peer association, some questions remain. First, prior research has examined the association between self-control and delinquent peer association across relatively short time frames. While these studies offer valuable insights, the relatively brief observation periods may not allow enough time for this relationship to unfold, or to fully capture the longer-term development of this relationship. Second, with one exception, research has largely tested the relationship between self-control and antisocial peer association at the between-individual level, which makes it difficult to rule out possible selection effects (i.e., people with lower self-control are more likely to interact with antisocial peers, “birds of a feather flock together”). Third, previous studies have largely focused on community samples of youth. Given that justice-involved youth are more likely to be exposed to risk factors for delinquency (e.g., low self-control, delinquent peer association), it is important to examine how these factors are associated within this population. Therefore, the overarching goal of this study is to examine the possible reciprocal effect of self-control and delinquent peer association over time. Specifically, the current study identifies whether within-individual change in self-control (i.e., time-specific observed deviations from individual mean levels across time) impacts within-individual change in delinquent peer association (and vice versa), while controlling for the time-invariant covariates of age, biological sex, race/ethnicity, and SES. The current study addresses the identified gaps in the literature by utilizing longitudinal data collected across multiple time points from a sample of justice-involved youth across seven years. The panel nature of these data enables the application of an analytic procedure suitable to test if within-individual change in one variable results in subsequent within-individual change in the other variable. As a result, the bidirectional relationship of self-control and delinquent peer association is estimated using a random intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) to effectively isolate the effect of within-individual change in one variable on another by accounting for unobserved, stable between-individual differences to provide unbiased estimates of the within-individual effects. Through this approach, three research questions are examined. Do self-control and delinquent peer association mutually influence each other? (Research Question 1) Does self-control or delinquent peer association demonstrate a stronger influence? (Research Question 2) In which direction is the influence between self-control and delinquent peer association primarily observed? (Research Question 3).

Methods

Sample

Data were used from the Pathways to Desistance study (Mulvey et al., 2014; Schubert et al., 2004) which is a longitudinal study of individuals who had previous involvement in the juvenile justice system. Participants were interviewed repeatedly over the course of 84 months (7 years), and were first interviewed (e.g., baseline interview) between November 2000 and March 2003. The respondents then completed ten follow-up interviews. The first six follow-up interviews were conducted at six-month intervals, while the last four interviews were conducted at twelve-month intervals (Schubert et al., 2004).

Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics for all study variables across each wave of data collection. The final sample size at baseline included 1,354 youth from Phoenix, AZ (n = 700) and Philadelphia, PA (n = 654) who were adjudicated of serious offenses (e.g., felony-level robbery, assault). At baseline, participants’ ages ranged from 14 to 19 years old (M = 16.04; SD = 1.14). The sample was predominantly male (n = 1,170) compared to female (n = 184). The racial/ethnic composition of the sample included Black (n = 561, 41.4%), Hispanic (n = 454, 33.5%), White (n = 274, 20.2%), and Other (n = 65, 4.8%) youth. The “Other” category included youth self-identified as Native American, Asian, and other racial/ethnic groups, which were combined into one category due to relatively small numbers of respondents.

Measures

Self-Control

Self-control was measured using the “temperance” scale from the Weinberger Adjustment Inventory (WAI; Weinberger & Schwartz, 1990). The temperance scale was created by combining the 8 items of the impulse control subscale (e.g., “I say the first thing that comes into my mind without thinking enough about it”) and the 7 items of the suppression of aggression subscale (e.g., “People who get me angry better watch out”). Therefore, this variable measured two of the six elements of self-control as defined by Gottfredson and Hirschi (1990). For each item, participants indicated their level of agreement using a five-point response scale (1 = False to 5 = True). The items were scored such that higher response categories indicated more temperance, and were then summed to create a total score, where higher total scores were indicative of higher self-control. The temperance scale showed good internal reliability across waves (α = 0.85 – 0.87). As shown in Table 1, self-control showed a general increase overtime. For example, the average level of self-control at the first wave was 1.87 (SD = 0.85) and consistently increased at each wave for a final average of 2.28 (SD = 0.86) at the final wave (wave 8).

Delinquent Peer Association

Association with antisocial peers was measured using one of the subscales (“antisocial behavior”) from the “peer delinquency” scale (Rochester Youth Study; Thornberry et al., 1994). The scale consists of 9 items that asked participants to indicate the number of friends who engaged in various antisocial behaviors (e.g., “During the last six months how many of your friends have sold drugs?“) using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (none of them) to 4 (all of them). Responses to the items were averaged to compute a total score where higher scores indicated more delinquent friends. The peer delinquency scale showed good internal reliability across all waves (α = 0.87 – 0.92). The average level of association with antisocial peers for youth decreased from the first wave (M = 2.32, SD = 0.93) to the final wave (M = 1.60, SD = 0.70), with some fluctuation depending on the specific wave examined.

Control Variables

Several sociodemographic variables were included as time-invariant covariates using the baseline value. Biological sex was coded as male = 1 and female = 0. Three dummy variables were created from the race/ethnicity variable: Black, Hispanic, and Other. The White category was set as the reference group. Study site was coded as Philadelphia = 1 and Phoenix = 0. Age was recorded as the participant’s age in years at the time of the baseline interview.

Parental monitoring was included as a control variable at baseline and was measured by averaging four items from the Parental Monitoring Inventory (Steinberg et al., 1992). The items asked the respondents to assess their parent’s willingness to monitor their behavior (e.g., “How often do you have a set time to be home on weekend nights?”) using a 4-point Likert Scale from 0 (never or don’t live with caregiver) to 3 (always). Higher scores indicated greater parental monitoring. The parental monitoring scale showed acceptable internal reliability (α = 0.73 at baseline).

Socioeconomic status (SES) was measured based on the Hollingshead’s index of social position (see Hollingshead, 1957), which includes both parental education and occupation. Parental occupation and education were coded using a seven-point scale ranging from 1 (higher executives, proprietors, major professionals, professional degree) to 7 (unskilled employees, less than seven years of school). Then, two indices were computed. The first index was computed by summing the occupation score and multiplying this summed score by 7 (total occupation score x 7). The second index was computed by summing the education score and then multiplying the summed score by 4 (total education x 4). Then, these two summed scores were added together to obtain a total score (see Hollingshead, 1971). The original value ranged from 11 (highest social position) to 77 (lowest social position), which was then reverse-coded to 1 (lowest social position) to 67 (highest social position) by subtracting the original value from 78. Higher values are indicative of higher SES. The average level of SES was 26.59 (SD = 12.30).

Analytic Plan

The random intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) was used to estimate the reciprocal association between self-control and delinquent peer association, and was conducted using MPlus version 8.5 (Muthén & Muthén, 2017). The RI-CLPM was used to examine if within-person changes in self-control impacted within-person changes in antisocial peer association, and vice versa, over time. That is, the RI-CLPM is beneficial for addressing research questions that focus on within-individual effects compared to a Cross-Lagged Panel Model (CLPM), which conflates within- and between-individual effects (Mund & Nestler, 2019).

Previously, the traditional CLPM was widely used as the effective analytical approach for examining reciprocal relationships between focal variables that are measured over multiple waves (Blain-Arcaro & Vaillancourt, 2017; Orth et al., 2021). By estimating cross-lagged paths, the CLPM can determine whether two variables (e.g., self-control and association with antisocial peers) are reciprocally related, or whether the relationship is unidirectional (e.g., self-control may influence association with antisocial peers, but not vice versa). Additionally, the CLPM estimates autoregressive effects, which indicate how stable each construct is over time, as well as within-time correlations that represent the concurrent relationships between variables at the same wave (Mund & Nestler, 2019). However, the CLPM does not distinguish between within-person and between-person effects, which may not accurately model associations between variables that are expected to be relatively stable between individuals, yet are still subject to within-person changes over time (Forney & Ward, 2019; Lucas, 2023). To address this issue, the RI-CLPM separates stable between-person differences from time-varying within-person processes by using random intercepts that reflect trait-like differences between individuals (Hamaker et al., 2015). This distinction is especially important for the focal variables of our study (e.g., self-control and association with antisocial peers), which may have both trait-like stability and within-person variability across time. Additionally, the RI-CLPM has been identified as being well-suited for modeling reciprocal effects when individual variation in the trajectories contribute to these associations, as alternative analytic techniques remove individual variation in development, such as the latent curve model with structure residuals (LCM-SR; Usami et al., 2019).

The basic RI-CLPM (Hamaker et al., 2015) was estimated, which is depicted in Fig. 1. The observed measures (i.e., squares) are separated into the between-individual component (i.e., random intercept; B_SC and B_AP)1 and the within-individual component (i.e., W_SC and W_AP). The latent factors (i.e., circles) represent the within-person fluctuations at each timepoint, which is computed as the difference between the expected score of an individual and their observed score on each variable. The expected score for an individual at each timepoint is calculated by first computing the difference between an individual’s average score of a variable across all time points and the average score (of a variable across all time points) for the sample. This average deviation is then used to compute the expected score, which is the sum of the individual’s average deviation and the sample average. Then, the deviation score for an individual is computed by subtracting their expected score from their observed score.

Although not shown in Fig. 1, time-invariant covariates (i.e., age, sex, race/ethnicity, study site, and SES) that were measured at baseline were controlled for in the RI-CLPM. These measures were included by specifying paths to the random intercepts. This approach was determined to be the appropriate way to model covariates by first specifying a model in which the effect of each covariate on self-control and delinquent peer association measured at each timepoint was estimated. This model was then compared to a model in which the effect of these covariates on self-control and delinquent peer association was constrained across time points, which is equivalent to specifying paths to the random intercepts from the covariates. The fit of the former, unconstrained model did not fit the data significantly better than the latter model, which suggested that the more parsimonious constrained model was the better fitting model (Mulder & Hamaker, 2021).

The horizontal autoregressive paths (e.g., W_SC1 to W_SC2) indicate the carry-over effect, which reflects the extent to which individuals maintain their stable levels of self-control or association with antisocial peers over time. The curved path at time one (i.e., between W_SC1 and W_AP1) represents the correlation between deviations from the individual’s own expected self-control and antisocial peers scores. Subsequent associations (i.e., vertical arrows, W_SC2 and W_AP2) between the two variables at concurrent time points reflect whether within-person changes in self-control are associated with within-person changes in antisocial peers. The diagonal paths (i.e., W_SC1 to W_AP2) represent the within-person cross-lagged effects. In other words, these paths represent if the deviation from an individual’s expected score on one construct at time t predicts their deviation from their expected score on the other construct at time t + 1.

The RI-CLPM requires that intervals between each time-period should be approximately equal to estimate the same autoregressive and cross-lagged effects between each pair of adjacent measurement periods (Mulder & Hamaker, 2021). If the time intervals between waves vary, the model may be uninterpretable due to the blend of different lagged relationships (see Kuiper & Ryan, 2018). However, data from the Pathways to Desistance study includes two different time frames: six-month and one-year intervals. From baseline to the seventh wave, the follow-up interviews were conducted in six-month intervals (6, 12, 18, 24, 30, and 36 months past baseline). From the eighth to the final wave (eleventh), follow-up interviews were conducted yearly (48, 60, 72, and 84 months). To ensure equal time-space across all waves, the adjacent six-month time-points (6 and 12 months, 18 and 24 months, 30 and 36 months) were averaged to align them with the yearly intervals used in later waves. Therefore, the RI-CLPM in the current study includes eight waves with a 12-month interval between each wave.

In sum, the RI-CLPM is particularly appropriate for meeting the overall aims of the current study of testing if within-individual changes in self-control impact subsequent changes in delinquent peer association, and vice versa. In addition to isolating the within-individual fluctuations in the observed indicators and their association over time, the RI-CLPM also statistically rules out the impact of the unobserved confounders on the relationship of interest. Specifically, the random-intercept component of the RI-CLPM models stable, trait-like between-individual differences, and removes unobserved heterogeneity when examining within-individual change (Hamaker et al., 2015). In contrast to other techniques (such as latent growth curve modeling and group-based trajectory modeling) that estimate developmental patterns in outcomes, the RI-CLPM is particularly well-equipped for establishing within-individual causal associations. This modeling technique does so by ruling out the effect of unobserved third variables via the latent random intercept and establishing temporal order modeled via the autoregressive paths. The former approaches can model patterns in the development of outcomes across age. However, they are limited in their ability to model causal and reciprocal associations resulting from within-individual change among those variables, making the RI-CLPM the most appropriate analytic technique for the current study aims. While it is important to note that one downside to the RI-CLPM in this particular context is the inability to specify measurement by age (given that the data were collected among age cohorts), age is included in the model as a time-invariant covariate to account for age-related heterogeneity.2

Full information maximum likelihood (FIML) was used to address missing data in the RI-CLPM. Before employing FIML, differences were examined between youth who had at least four waves of valid responses on key study variables (i.e., self-control and antisocial peer association) to those with fewer than four waves of valid responses. Four waves were chosen as the cutoff because RI-CLPM requires at least four waves of data. Specifically, percentage differences were tested using chi-square analyses for nominal variables (e.g., sex, study site, race/ethnicity) and mean differences were examined for continuous variables (e.g., age, SES) using t-tests. The chi-square tests indicated that most demographic variables demonstrated statistically significant differences between youth with complete data and those without. Specifically, differences were observed for sex, study site, being Black, and being White. There were no differences for youth who reported being Hispanic or Other in terms of missingness on either self-control or antisocial peers. The results from the t-tests suggested significant differences in age between those with at least four waves of data compared to those with fewer than four waves on self-control and antisocial peers. However, there were no differences in SES between these two groups. For mean differences in age the Cohen’s d values were 0.426 for self-control and 0.387 for antisocial peer association, indicating weak-moderate mean differences (Cohen, 1992). Based on these statistics, it was concluded that FIML was appropriate to address missing data when employing the RI-CLPM model in this study.

To identify the best-fitting model, we followed steps outlined by Mulder and Hamaker (2021). Table 3 presents the comparison of model fit indices of three alternative models to the basic RI-CLPM (Model 1). First, the RI-CLPM is compared to the RI-CLPM in which the variances of the random intercepts and their covariances are constrained to be zero, which is equivalent to the basic CLPM (Model 2). Model 1 (χ2(200) = 544.716; CFI = 0.966; TLI = 0.957; RMSEA = 0.036; SRMR = 0.045)3 fit the data better compared to Model 2 (χ2(203) = 1459.712; CFI = 0.874; TLI = 0.846; RMSEA = 0.068; SRMR = 0.096). The findings suggest that constraining the parameters results in a significantly worse fit (Δ χ2(df) = 914.966(3), p<.001). Next, Model 1 was compared to Model 3. In Model 1, the autoregressive and cross-lagged paths were free to vary across all waves. In Model 3, the unstandardized paths were constrained to be equal across waves. The results suggested that Model 1, in which the paths were free to vary, fit the data better compared to Model 3 (χ2(256) = 1,638.313; CFI = 0.862; TLI = 0.866; RMSEA = 0.063; SRMR = 0.088). The findings suggest that constraining the parameters results in a significantly worse fit (Δ χ2(df) = 1,093.597(56), p<.001). Finally, Model 1 was compared to a model where the standardized temporal paths (autoregressive and cross-lagged) were constrained to be equal across all waves (Model 4). Constraining the unstandardized path coefficients does not imply that the standardized paths will be equivalent because the latter is also a function of the standard deviations of the predictors (Mulder & Hamaker, 2021). Compared to Model 1, Model 4 showed worse fit (χ2(238) = 1,450.318; CFI = 0.879; TLI = 0.874; RMSEA = 0.062; SRMR = 0.075). The chi-square difference test also suggested that Model 4 had significantly worse fit (Δ χ2(df) = 905.602(38), p<.001). Additionally, in all three comparisons to Model 1, the change in goodness of fit indices (ΔRMSEA > 0.01 and ΔCFI > 0.015) exceeded the recommended cutoffs. Thus, the best-fitting model is one in which the temporal paths were free to vary, which is “…indicative of some kind of developmental process taking place during the time span covered by the study” (Mulder & Hamaker, 2021, pg. 641).

To examine if self-control and delinquent peer association mutually influence each other, the findings indicate that observed values on antisocial peers that were higher than an individual’s average level of delinquent peers was associated with a subsequent decrease in self-control from wave 1 to wave 2 (β = − 0.084, SE = 0.033, p = .012) and from wave 6 to wave 7 (β = − 0.065, SE = 0.033, p = .049). These results seem to contradict self-control theory and provide support for the notion that peers are important sources of socialization during early to middle adolescence. However, there seems to be more of a selection effect in regard to delinquent peer association at later time points, which is a finding more consistent with self-control theory (Gottfredson & Hirschi, 1990). That is, observed values on self-control at wave 5 that were lower than an individual’s mean level of self-control was associated with subsequent increases in antisocial peers at wave 6 (β = − 0.131, SE = 0.036, p < .001). Similar findings were observed for self-control measured at wave 6 and delinquent peer association at wave 7 (β = − 0.098, SE = 0.035, p = .005) and self-control at wave 7 and delinquent peer association at wave 8 (β = − 0.145, SE = 0.036, p < .001).

To examine whether self-control or delinquent peer association exerted a stronger influence, the relative strength of the cross-lagged effects of the two constructs were identified using the standardized coefficients. Then, a Wald χ2 test comparing the equality of the two paths was conducted. To illustrate, the cross-lagged path from wave 1 to wave 2 was stronger for self-control to delinquent peer association than for the path from delinquent peer association to self-control based on the magnitude of the standardized coefficients, (β= − 0.084 versus − 0.025, respectively). However, the test of equality was not significant (Wald χ2 = 0.687, p = .407). Interestingly, the cross-lagged paths from wave 2 to wave 3 were significantly different from one another (Wald χ2 = 5.952, p = .015), despite neither path being significant. In this instance, the effect of self-control at wave 2 on delinquent peer association at wave 3 was positive (β = 0.075, p = .068) whereas it was negative for the effect of delinquent peer association on subsequent self-control (β = − 0.064, p = .089). The cross-lagged paths were not significantly different from one another from wave 3 to wave 4 or from wave 4 to wave 5. The cross-lagged path for delinquent peer association at wave 5 to self-control at wave 6 (β = − 0.033, p = .310) was weaker that the path from self-control at wave 5 to peers at wave 6 (β = − 0.131, p < .001) and this difference was significant (Wald χ2 = 5.589, p = .018).

While the magnitude of the coefficients was larger for self-control at wave 6 in predicting delinquent peer association at wave 7 (β = − 0.098, p = .005) compared to the path between delinquent peer association and self-control (β = − 0.065, p = .049), this difference was not significant. However, the path from self-control at wave 7 to delinquent peer association at wave 8 (β = − 0.145, p < .001) was significantly different from the path for delinquent peer association to self-control (β = 0.011, p = .749) during the same period (Wald χ2 = 9.643, p = .002). Overall, the findings indicate that when a statistically significant path emerged, with one exception (from wave 6 to wave 7) self-control exerted a stronger influence on associating with delinquent peers than the reverse, with statistically significant differences for the paths from self-control to delinquent peer association occurring at later waves.

To examine the primary direction of influence between self-control and delinquent peer association, higher antisocial peer involvement at wave 1 was associated with lower levels of self-control at wave 2. This same finding emerged between antisocial peer involvement and self-control from wave 6 to wave 7. These findings suggest that associating with delinquent peers resulted in subsequent decreases in self-control during these time-points. However, the Wald χ2 tests that were conducted to test the equality between the cross-lagged paths for delinquent peer association to self-control and self-control to delinquent peer association at these waves were not significantly different from each other at these time points. In fact, there was only a significant difference between the effect of delinquent peer association at wave 2 and self-control at wave 3 based on these tests (see previous section), but the paths themselves were not significant. Alternatively, higher self-control at wave 5 was associated with lower levels of delinquent peer association at wave 6, along with similar findings from wave 6 to wave 7, and wave 7 to wave 8. Recall that the Wald χ2 tests showed that the coefficients from wave 5 to wave 6 and wave 7 to wave 8 were significantly different for self-control to delinquent peer association (i.e., stronger) compared to the path between delinquent peer association to self-control (i.e., weaker). Overall, these findings suggest that the direction of the effect is from delinquent peer association to self-control at earlier waves but shifts in direction from self-control to delinquent peer association at later waves.

Discussion

The relationship between one’s level of self-control and delinquent peer association are central constructs to understanding delinquent behavior. However, it is still uncertain whether these processes function independently or influence one another over time. Of the studies that have been conducted on this reciprocal relationship, many have been constrained by brief follow-up periods, limited waves of data collection, samples from the general population, and methodological techniques that do not adequately distinguish within-individual change from between-individual differences. The current study examined the relationship between self-control and delinquent peer association among justice-involved youth using a random intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) to examine whether these two factors mutually influenced each other, which construct exerted a strong effect, and the primary direction of the association between them.

Overall, the results indicate that self-control and delinquent peer association are interconnected over time. In general, periods of higher levels of antisocial peer influence were associated with lower levels of self-control, whereas higher self-control corresponded with lower levels of antisocial peer association. These patterns are consistent with prior research on the reciprocal relationship between these constructs and their directionality (Huijsmans et al., 2021; Li et al., 2024). However, the effect of self-control on delinquent peer association was more consistent at later waves. This influence of self-control on delinquent peer association at later time points may reflect developmental changes as participants transition into young adulthood. Individuals at this age are often taking on greater responsibilities and encounter potential prosocial opportunities (e.g., college, employment, romantic relationships; see Copp et al., 2020). Those with higher self-control may be more likely to recognize the potential consequences of maintaining associations with antisocial peers. Importantly, these individuals have the capacity to act on this awareness by distancing themselves from such groups. Alternatively, earlier in adolescence, self-control may be more malleable (Burt, 2020), and therefore more likely to be impacted by peer influence (Casey, 2015). This scenario may help explain why those with higher levels of delinquent peer relationships were associated with lower levels of self-control at earlier time points.

After accounting for all the autoregressive paths (including all control variables), it is not surprising the limited number of statistically significant cross-lagged effects between self-control and delinquent peer association (5 out of 14 paths). However, the fact that these cross-lagged findings still emerged highlights the salience of these relationships. When examining the relative strength of the relationships, the reciprocal paths between self-control and delinquent peer association were significantly different from each other in two instances, where the paths from self-control to delinquent peer association were stronger compared to the reverse. Although peer effects on self-control were observed during earlier waves, the more persistent relationships emerged from self-control to delinquent peer association in later waves. These findings suggest that as adolescents continue to mature, their ability to self-regulate may play a central role in shaping their peer groups. In other words, self-control may be a guiding factor that helps individuals select, maintain, or distance themselves from peer relationships. This trait can serve as a protective mechanism where individuals with higher self-control are better able to navigate social environments, avoid negative peer associations, and prioritize relationships that align with prosocial goals (Tangney et al., 2018). Relatedly, as young adults develop a stronger sense of personal identity and values, those with higher self-control may be more cognizant of selecting peers who reflect and reinforce these beliefs (Albarello et al., 2018).

Another important finding is that changes in self-control tended to precede and guide subsequent shifts in peer affiliations, rather than peer associations driving changes in one’s self-control. Self-control may shape an adolescent’s social contexts (such as structured activities, employment, educational opportunities, etc., see Duckworth et al., 2019) which may naturally limit their contact with delinquent peer groups. Those higher in self-control may also be able to set personal boundaries to decrease the likelihood of risky social interactions and have the ability to disengage from negative peers (Robson et al., 2020). Relatedly, qualities associated with high self-control (such as emotion regulation, delayed gratification, ability to plan, etc.) may further support adolescents’ choices in peers. Alternatively, delinquent peer groups may be attractive to those with low self-control, which can provide opportunities for antisocial behavior (e.g., Stults et al., 2021). The current study provides greater evidence of this type of directionality versus the ability of peer associations to shape the development of one’s level of self-control, particularly later in young adulthood.

Relatedly, the findings also highlight that self-control may decrease the extent to which delinquent peers can alter one’s self-control during emerging adulthood. The within-individual effect of delinquent peers (although statistically significant) decreased over time, but especially in the presence of self-control. Theoretically speaking, peer group memberships tend to stabilize during adolescence, but these later waves show that self-control is more influential in emerging adulthood. These patterns suggest that while delinquent peer association was associated with decreases in self-control during adolescence, the relative importance of peer influence declines as self-control plays a more central role in young adulthood.

The results presented here yield important theoretical implications related to a youth’s level of self-control and association with delinquent peers. For example, it is possible that the influence of antisocial peers on self-control may be more pronounced in childhood versus adolescence. As the current study collected baseline data from teenagers between the ages of 14 and 19 years old, examining these processes beginning in childhood (e.g., 8 years old) could demonstrate stronger evidence of peer influence on the development of self-control. By elementary school, children are learning how to manage their behavior alongside peers (Hank & Huber, 2024). Theoretically speaking, peer influence at this stage (versus only in teenage years) may provide a more developmentally comprehensive understanding of how this factor is associated with self-control. Overall, these findings suggest that theoretical debates between self-control and delinquent peer association as competing explanations for behavior may be overstated, as peer influence may be developmentally contingent, and self-control tendencies decreased delinquent peer association into emerging adulthood.

Limitations and Suggestions for Future Research

The current study is not without limitations. First, data from the Pathways to Desistance study was initially collected from justice-involved youth in the early 2000s. Societal shifts over the last two decades (such as peer dynamics, social contexts, and opportunities for delinquency) through the increase of technology and social media may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future research should replicate these analyses to examine if the results presented here are similar or different with more recent and diverse samples. Second, the data were modeled across waves of data collection, versus grouping youth according to their age. This decision may have masked certain age-specific developmental processes that could have influenced the results. In other words, cohort effects were unable to be disentangled from age-specific effects. To do so, age-specific models would be necessary. However, employing this modeling technique would have resulted in reduced statistical power due to conducting tests on smaller sample sizes. Future research should consider modeling age-based growth curves (e.g., multi-cohort design, O’Connor et al., 2022) with more complete data to more precisely capture developmental timing and potential heterogeneity in the reciprocal effects across different ages.

Third, and relatedly, to examine if the relationship between self-control and delinquent peer association follows specific long-term developmental pathways, future research should consider examining these constructs through an accelerated longitudinal design (e.g., cohort-sequential design, Estrada & Ferrer, 2019). Utilizing an adolescent development theoretical framework with a cohort-sequential design can result in data with multiple age cohorts that can be combined into overlapping age ranges to model longer developmental trajectories (Prinzie & Onghena, 2005). This modeling technique can also address issues of missing data (e.g., since older cohorts cannot be measured at younger ages) and still estimate long-term developmental processes without requiring one age cohort to be measured over an extended period. In other words, utilizing an accelerated framework can result in modeling development over the entire age-range of a population, even though every individual is unable to be measured at each age.

Fourth, there is the possibility that the relationship between self-control and delinquent peer association differs across biological sex. However, sex-specific models were unable to be performed due to the sample being overwhelmingly male (approximately 86%) and a resulting lack of statistical power to examine female-specific models. As there may be differences between males and females in the socialization process and exposure to risk factors (Herrera & Stuewig, 2017; Leban & Gibson, 2020), future research would benefit from the ability to examine the reciprocal nature of self-control and associating with delinquent peers among samples that include a greater number and proportion of girls/women.

Fifth, the measure of delinquent peer association relied on a perception measurement strategy, where individuals were asked to indicate the number of friends who engaged in various antisocial behaviors. This measurement relies on the respondents’ subjective beliefs of their friends’ behavior versus researchers observing actual social network ties and behaviors (e.g., a network or direct measure approach). Perceptions can introduce possible sources of bias, such as a misinterpretation of peers’ behaviors, the projection of their own behavior onto their friends, and recall/reporting errors (McGloin & Thomas, 2019). Future research should replicate this study by using a social network approach to more precisely identify specific peer relationships and measure antisocial peer associations (see Ragan et al., 2023).

Last, the self-control variable is not Grasmick et al.’s (1993) 24-item measure that captures the six dimensions of low self-control (i.e., impulsivity, preference for simple tasks, risk-seeking, preference for physical over mental activities, self-centeredness, temper). In the current study, self-control was measured using the “temperance” scale from the Weinberger Adjustment Inventory, which focuses on emotional regulation and impulse control. Although this measure does not specifically align with the criminological construct to officially test self-control theory, the temperance measure still captures key aspects of self-control dimensions that are applicable to understanding the relationship between this factor and delinquent peer association. Future research should replicate the research questions examined here with indicators of self-control that directly align with Gottfredson and Hirschi’s (1990) theoretical constructs, such as the Grasmick et al. (1993) measure.

Implications

Based on the findings that self-control exerted a stronger and more consistent influence on delinquent peer association, along with demonstrating that respondents with higher levels of self-control were less likely to engage with delinquent peers, there are research, policy, and program implications that surround both of these constructs. First, during adolescence, peer influence seems to play a more prominent role in this relationship, which is consistent with a heightened susceptibility to peer contexts and the socialization process (Gallupe et al., 2019; Giletta et al., 2021). However, as individuals transition into emerging adulthood, self-control emerges as a more influential factor in shaping peer affiliations, which suggests a shift from social factors driving behavior (i.e., delinquent peers) to more internally guided selection processes (i.e., high self-control). This pattern highlights the importance that these two factors are not static, but self-control plays an increasing role in associating with delinquent peers as individuals age. Second, prevention efforts should focus on developmentally informed self-regulation interventions early in childhood and adolescence. Some examples include cognitive-behavioral (Smith et al., 2019), impulse-control (Hall et al., 2021), and socio-emotional (Schonert-Reichl et al., 2015) learning to reduce the likelihood that youth will select into antisocial peer networks and ultimately find more prosocial outlets.

Third, educational and juvenile justice systems should include peer-focused programming as complimentary to current interventions for youth, where the strategies listed above to increase self-control can be integrated into efforts to decrease delinquent peer associations (Piquero et al., 2016). Specifically, programs that intentionally combine cognitive-behavioral and self-regulation skill building with peer-based components may be especially effective in decreasing delinquent peer associations by strengthening a youth’s capacity to resist negative peer involvement while simultaneously increasing prosocial peer bonds. Last, early screening and targeted services for children and youth with self-regulatory challenges may be an effective method that lasts through adolescence, emerging adulthood, and into adulthood, especially once peer networks begin to shape youths’ behavior relative to parental and family influences (Montroy et al., 2016; Perry, 2019). By identifying these types of difficulties that youth may possess and intervening before antisocial peer associations form, these types of approaches may disrupt developmental pathways into deviant behavior while also yielding long-term benefits that extend beyond adolescence.

Conclusion

Despite contradictory theoretical viewpoints surrounding the role that self-control plays in the relationship with associating with delinquent peers, prior research has attempted to examine the bidirectional relationship between these two factors. However, these studies have relied on relatively short time frames of data collection (e.g., two years), samples of school-based youth, and analytic approaches that were unable to disentangle within-individual change from between-individual differences. The current study longitudinally examined the possible reciprocal relationship between self-control and delinquent peer association over seven years with a sample of youth involved in the juvenile justice system for serious offending using a modeling technique that separates within-person change from between-person differences. The results indicated a reciprocal association between self-control and delinquent peer association, where higher levels of self-control were connected to lower levels of delinquent peer association, and higher exposure to delinquent peers corresponded to lower levels of self-control. Over time, the link between self-control and delinquent peer association emerged more frequently and with greater magnitude than the reverse relationship. While the findings also suggest that one’s peer group is relevant across adolescence, the stability of self-control increasingly shapes one’s delinquent peer associations into emerging adulthood. More broadly, the results underscore that the longstanding “chicken or the egg” criminological question between self-control and delinquent peer association cannot be reduced to a simple unidirectional process.
