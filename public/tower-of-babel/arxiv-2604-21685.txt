Resilience Revisited: A Multidimensional Framework Derived from Realistic Attack Scenarios Thanks: This work is funded by the National Science Foundation (NSF) Award Number #2501975.
Isaac Ortega Romero and Ioannis Zografopoulos

Abstract
Power systems are increasingly vulnerable to high-impact, low-probability (HILP) events, including coordinated cyberattacks targeting inverter-based resources. Existing resilience frameworks rely on single-dimensional metrics that fail to capture cross-dimensional coupling effects, underestimating real system degradation under multi-vector attack conditions. This study proposes a Multidimensional Resilience Index ( ℳ ​ 𝒟 ​ ℛ ​ ℐ \mathcal{MDRI} ) that decomposes power system degradation into five interacting dimensions: physical, operational, digital-cyber, climatic, and regulatory, explicitly separating independent and coupled contributions via a calibrated multiplicative interaction term. The framework is validated on the IEEE 39-bus system under two attack scenarios derived from the December 2025 cyberattack on the Polish energy infrastructure. ℳ ​ 𝒟 ​ ℛ ​ ℐ \mathcal{MDRI} results show that multi-vector attacks produce degradation exceeding linear expectations by a factor of 5.6, with simultaneous dimensional failures contributing an additional 60.6% through endogenous coupling, and exogenous factors amplifying it by an additional 84%.



I
Introduction

The increasing frequency and severity of high-impact, low-probability (HILP) events, including extreme weather, coordinated cyberattacks, and cascading failures, have elevated power system resilience as a critical research priority
[
17
,
21
]
. Unlike reliability, which addresses frequent and predictable contingencies, resilience focuses on the system’s ability to limit the extent, systemic impact, and duration of degradations under disturbances
[
17
]
. This distinction has become paramount as modern grids undergo a structural transformation toward inverter-based resources, which, while essential for decarbonization, introduce new vulnerability pathways through cyber-physical attack surfaces.
The importance of this threat is illustrated by a recent joint advisory from the FBI, CISA, NSA, and DOE, warning of ongoing state actor-affiliated exploitation of programmable logic controllers across U.S. energy, water, and government sectors
[
3
]
. Thus, accurately characterizing such vulnerabilities requires assessment frameworks capable of capturing degradation across multiple system dimensions simultaneously
[
14
]
.

Prior research has addressed this need through geometric representations such as the resilience triangle and trapezoid, which model degradation and recovery as functions of time.
[
11
,
15
,
19
]
. Furthermore, review studies have classified resilience indicators across technical, social, economic, environmental, and organizational dimensions
[
25
,
9
]
,
highlighting the need for comprehensive assessments that span multiple system domains. Building on these foundations, subsequent efforts have expanded the temporal perspective by introducing multi-stage evaluation frameworks in which component criticality evolves dynamically across
i)
pre-event,
ii)
during-event, and
iii)
recovery phases
[
12
,
26
]
. Additionally, probabilistic formulations have been proposed to improve analytical tractability, deriving closed-form resilience metrics under assumptions such as independent failures and additive degradation
[
21
]
.

Despite these advances, several fundamental limitations persist. First, geometric resilience curves assume linear performance evolution and can deviate from observed system behavior by up to 22%
[
14
]
. Second, most existing metrics focus on a single system dimension, i.e., load curtailment, energy not supplied, network topology, etc., without capturing the multifaceted nature of HILP events
[
9
]
, and single-dimension assessments have been shown to yield incomplete vulnerability characterizations. Third, existing approaches predominantly treat resilience dimensions as independent, thereby failing to capture cross-dimensional coupling effects. Studies have demonstrated that neglecting interdependencies between system domains can underestimate degradation metrics by as much as 65%
[
13
]
, while infrastructure interdependency analyses confirm that HILP events disproportionately amplify impact when multiple dimensions are simultaneously compromised
[
10
]
. In Table
I
, we summarize how related literature addresses these key aspects of resilience assessment.

To address the aforementioned gaps, this paper makes the following contributions:

•

A multidimensional resilience index (
\mathcal{MDRI}
) that decomposes system degradation into five interacting dimensions: physical, operational, digital-cyber, climatic, and regulatory.

•

A convex combination that explicitly separates independent and coupled dimensional contributions through a multiplicative interaction term, with a coupling coefficient calibrated from simulation-derived amplification ratios.

•

A hierarchical structure that distinguishes between endogenous dimensions driving cascading failures and exogenous dimensions acting as contextual amplifiers.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
II
presents the proposed multidimensional resilience framework, Section
III
describes the case study, attack scenarios, and simulation results, and Section
IV
concludes the paper.

II
Methodology

This section introduces a multidimensional framework for quantifying power system resilience under disturbances. As illustrated in Fig.
1
, the proposed approach decomposes system degradation into five dimensions and combines them into a comprehensive resilience index, i.e.,
\mathcal{MDRI}
.

II-A

System Performance and Resilience Loss

The system performance function
\Phi(t)
, combines frequency deviation and inter-machine coherency, as expressed in Eq. (
1
):

\Phi(t)=w_{f}\Phi_{f}(t)+w_{s}\Phi_{s}(t)

(1)

\displaystyle\Phi_{f}(t)

\displaystyle=\max\left(0,\,1-\frac{|f_{\mathrm{COI}}(t)-f_{\mathrm{nom}}|}{|f_{\mathrm{nom}}-f_{\mathrm{crit}}|}\right)

\displaystyle\Phi_{s}(t)

\displaystyle=\max\left(0,1-\frac{\Delta f_{\mathrm{gen}}(t)}{\Delta f_{\mathrm{gen}}^{\mathrm{coh}}}\right)

where
f_{\mathrm{COI}}(t)
is the center-of-inertia (COI) frequency,
f_{\mathrm{nom}}
and
f_{\mathrm{crit}}
are the nominal and critical
frequencies,
\Delta f_{\mathrm{gen}}(t)=\max_{i,j}|f_{i}(t)-f_{j}(t)|
is the inter-generator frequency spread, and
\Delta f_{\mathrm{gen}}^{\mathrm{coh}}
is the coherency tolerance band. Weights
w_{f},w_{s}\in[0,1]
with
w_{f}+w_{s}=1
. Equal weights are adopted, as frequency deviation and inter-machine coherence loss are complementary and equally relevant stability phenomena.

The resilience loss metric measures cumulative performance degradation over a fixed horizon
T_{H}
. Integration spans from disturbance onset
t_{0}
to recovery time
t_{f}
, if the system recovers. If a system collapse occurs at
t_{\mathrm{col}}
, performance is taken as zero
thereafter and is given by Eq. (
2
):

R_{\mathrm{loss}}=\frac{1}{\Phi_{0}}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+T_{H}}\max\left(0,\,\Phi_{0}-\tilde{\Phi}(t)\right)dt

(2)

where
\Phi_{0}
is the pre-disturbance performance, and the truncated performance function is defined as
\tilde{\Phi}(t)=\Phi(t)
for
t\leq t_{\mathrm{lim}}
and
\tilde{\Phi}(t)=0
for
t>t_{\mathrm{lim}}
, with
t_{\mathrm{lim}}=t_{f}
under recovery and
t_{\mathrm{lim}}=t_{\mathrm{col}}

under collapse.

II-B

Resilience Dimension Definitions

II-B
1
Physical Dimension

D_{\mathrm{phy}}
, as defined in Eq. (
3
), quantifies the direct loss of generation caused by a disturbance. In our analysis we account for the presence or absence of inverter-based photovoltaic (PV) generation.

D_{\mathrm{phy}}=\frac{P_{\mathrm{PV,lost}}}{P_{\mathrm{PV,total}}}\quad

(3)

where
P_{\mathrm{PV,lost}}
represents the total disconnected or unavailable PV capacity, and
P_{\mathrm{PV,total}}
is the total installed PV capacity.

II-B
2
Operational Dimension

The operational dimension captures the system’s dynamic response following a disturbance. It considers three complementary aspects of operational degradation:
i)
the speed of frequency deviation,
ii)
the severity of performance loss, and
iii)
the loss of synchronism among generators, as shown in Eq. (
4
).

\vskip-2.0ptD_{\mathrm{op}}=\min\left(1,\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{|\mathrm{RoCoF}|_{\max}}{\mathrm{RoCoF}^{\mathrm{crit}}}+\frac{\delta_{\Phi}}{\delta_{\Phi}^{\mathrm{crit}}}+\frac{\Delta f_{\mathrm{gen}}^{\max}}{\Delta f_{\mathrm{gen}}^{\mathrm{crit}}}\right)\right)\vskip-1.0pt

(4)

\delta_{\Phi}=\frac{\Phi_{0}-\Phi_{\mathrm{nadir}}}{\Phi_{0}}

where
|\mathrm{RoCoF}|_{\max}
is the maximum rate of change of frequency,
\Phi_{\mathrm{nadir}}
is the minimum performance during the event, and

\Delta f_{\mathrm{gen}}^{\max}=\max_{t}\Delta f_{\mathrm{gen}}(t)
is the peak inter-generator spread (we assume equal weights for transient, integrated, and
coherency terms). Critical thresholds are set to
\mathrm{RoCoF}^{\mathrm{crit}}=1.0
Hz/s (ENTSO-E
[
6
]
),
\delta_{\Phi}^{\mathrm{crit}}=0.05
(conservative 5% performance drop), and
\Delta f_{\mathrm{gen}}^{\mathrm{crit}}=2.0
Hz (angular instability).

Fig. 1:
Algorithmic overview of the proposed MDRI score calculation.

II-B
3
Digital-Cyber Dimension

The digital-cyber dimension quantifies the extent to which the disturbance has compromised the operator’s ability to observe and/or control the system, as defined in Eq. (
5
).

D_{\text{cyb}}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{K}}w_{cy_{j}}\cdot\frac{N_{\text{compr}_{j}}}{N_{\text{scope}_{j}}}\vskip-2.0pt

(5)

where
\mathcal{K}
is the set of cyber aspects
evaluated (i.e., observability, controllability, integrity,
availability),
N_{\text{compr}_{j}}
is the number of assets
compromised in aspect
j
,
N_{\text{scope}_{j}}
is the total
assets under evaluation, and
w_{cy_{j}}\geq 0
with

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{K}}w_{cy_{j}}=1
are relative importance weights.

II-B
4
Exogenous Dimensions

Exogenous dimensions are modeled as contextual factors that amplify system degradation rather than directly causing it. The climatic dimension captures environmental stress through simultaneous demand increase and PV generation reduction, calculated using Eq. (
6
):

\vskip-3.0ptD_{\text{clim}}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}w_{c_{i}}\cdot I_{c_{i}}

(6)

where
\mathcal{C}
is the set of climatic stressors considered (i.e., extreme temperature, snowstorms, high winds),
I_{c_{i}}\in[0,1]
is the normalized intensity of stressor
i
, and
w_{c_{i}}\geq 0
with
\sum_{i}w_{c_{i}}=1
are relative importance weights.

II-B
5
Regulatory Dimension

The regulatory dimension quantifies institutional vulnerabilities by comparing the existing controls against a reference framework. The sub-index is expressed in Eq. (
7
) as:

\vskip-1.0ptD_{\text{reg}}=\frac{1}{N_{v}^{\text{ref}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{v}^{\text{ref}}}v_{i}\vskip-5.0pt

(7)

where
v_{i}\in\{0,1\}
indicates the presence (
v_{i}=1
)
or absence (
v_{i}=0
) of a regulatory weakness in the control category
i
, and
N_{v}^{\text{ref}}
is the total number of reference control categories, selected independently of the case under study.

II-C

Multidimensional Resilience Index – MDRI

The proposed formulation is based on the premise that evaluating resilience dimensions independently underestimates systemwide impacts, i.e., their simultaneous compromise and underlying interdependencies create degradation that no
single-dimensional
assessment can capture.

Let
D_{k}
denote the normalized sub-index of dimension
k\in\{\text{phy},\,\text{op},\,\text{cyb}\}
. The set
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sim}}
collects these three
endogenous
dimensions subject to simultaneous compromise, and equal weighting
{1}/\penalty{|\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sim}}|}
is adopted for simplicity. This hypothesis is formalized through a convex combination of independent and coupled contributions, as seen in Eq. (
8
).

\small\vskip-6.0pt\mathcal{M}=(1-\gamma)\underbrace{\frac{1}{|K_{sim}|}\sum_{k\in K_{sim}}D_{k}}_{\text{independent}}+\gamma\underbrace{\left(\prod_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sim}}}D_{k}\right)^{1/|\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sim}}|}}_{\text{coupled}}\vskip 0.0pt

(8)

where
\gamma\in[0,1]
weights the contribution of interdependency-driven vulnerability, and is exactly identified from the two-scenario calibration. The product structure ensures that the coupling term activates only when all three
endogenous
dimensions are simultaneously compromised, consistent with cascading failure mechanisms in interdependent systems.

To calibrate the coupling coefficient
\gamma
, the amplification effect observed in multi-dimensional scenarios must first be quantified. The amplification ratio
\Lambda=\frac{R^{\text{obs}}_{\text{loss}}}{R^{\text{exp}}_{\text{loss}}}
compares the actual resilience loss to the expected loss under a single-dimension scaling assumption. Namely,
R^{\text{obs}}_{\text{loss}}
is the resilience loss observed in simulation, and
R^{\text{exp}}_{\text{loss}}
is the expected resilience loss under
independent dimensional effects
. The expected resilience loss is defined as
R^{\text{exp}}_{\text{loss}}=\kappa\cdot R^{\text{A}}_{\text{loss}}
,
where
\kappa
is a first-order proxy that scales the baseline
R^{A}_{\text{loss}}
by the ratio of PV capacity lost, isolating the contribution attributable purely to generation loss. A value
\Lambda\gg 1
indicates that system degradation exceeds linear expectations, revealing strong coupling effects.

With
\Lambda
defined, the coupling coefficient
\gamma
is calibrated by requiring that the severity ratio between scenarios (the baseline
S_{A}
and the multidimensional
S_{B}
described in Section
III-A
) matches the observed amplification, i.e.,
\frac{\mathcal{M}(S_{B})}{\mathcal{M}(S_{A})}=\Lambda
.
Finally, to incorporate exogenous amplification effects beyond the endogenous coupling,
\mathcal{MDRI}
is formulated as:

\vskip-2.0pt\mathcal{MDRI}=\mathcal{M}\cdot\prod_{j\in\mathcal{K}_{\text{ext}}}(1+D_{j})

(9)

where
\mathcal{K}_{\text{ext}}=\{\text{clim, reg}\}
. Each exogenous factor proportionally amplifies the core index according to its sub-index value, capturing external pressures on system-wide resilience that act independently of internal coupling dynamics. As a result, the
\mathcal{MDRI}
is not normalized to
[0,1]
, and is intended as a comparative metric across scenarios rather than an absolute resilience score. Specifically,
\mathcal{MDRI}
values exceeding unity arise when endogenous degradation is exacerbated by exogenous amplification.

III
Results

III-A

Case Study and Attack Scenarios

System Model:
The proposed framework is validated on the IEEE 39-bus New England test system, implemented in MATLAB/Simulink. The original system comprises 10 synchronous generators with a total installed capacity of 10,610 MW. To represent the increasing penetration of inverter-based resources, 1,500 MW of synchronous generation has been replaced by nine utility-scale grid-forming PV plants distributed across buses
30-38
(14.1% of total capacity).

Threat Model:
Assumes a state-sponsored adversary targeting both the information technology (IT) and operational technology (OT) domains, consistent with the tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) attributed to the ELECTRUM/Sandworm group
[
1
,
5
,
7
]
. The attacker possesses prior knowledge of grid topology and industrial control system (ICS) protocols, exploiting exposed perimeter devices and default credentials for initial access and lateral movement into OT networks. Attack execution involves coordinated, multi-vector actions across distributed sites, including control signal manipulation and communication disruption.

Attack Scenarios:
Two distinct attack scenarios are evaluated, inspired by the coordinated cyberattack on the Polish energy infrastructure on December 29, 2025
[
1
]
.

Scenario A
– Single-plant baseline attack: A control input attack manipulates the active power reference of the PV plant at Bus 33 (190 MW), selected for its proximity to the highest-load buses in the system. As a result, a generation loss at this bus produces measurable system-wide frequency transients while remaining within the single-plant scope for our baseline scenario. Operators retain full communication and control over all the remaining plants. This represents an isolated cyberattack without any climatic or regulatory assumptions.

Scenario B
– Multi-vector cascading attack: A coordinated attack replicates TTPs documented in the Polish grid incident:
i)
communication disruption targeting six PV plants, eliminating operator observability and control, and
ii)
forced disconnection of 1,115 MW of PV capacity (74.3% of total). The six targeted plants (at Buses 31, 32, 34, 35, 37, and 38) are geographically dispersed, mirroring the targeting strategy. A 25% load increase is incorporated as an exogenous stress factor to reflect the elevated winter demand conditions concurrent with the Polish attack, which occurred during sub-zero temperatures and snowstorms. The remaining climatic and regulatory stressors are treated as exogenous amplifiers via
D_{\text{clim}}
and
D_{\text{reg}}

[
1
,
5
]
.

III-E

MDRI and Amplification Analysis

The resilience loss increases from
0.0305
in Scenario A to
1.0080
in Scenario B. To assess whether this increase is proportional to the generation loss alone, the expected resilience loss is computed by linearly scaling the single-plant baseline (Scenario A) by the ratio of PV capacity lost in each scenario (
\kappa
):

\mathcal{R}^{\text{exp}}_{\text{loss}}={\frac{PV^{B}_{\text{lost}}}{PV^{A}_{\text{lost}}}}\cdot\mathcal{R}^{A}_{\text{loss}}=0.179\vskip-3.0pt

The observed resilience loss in Scenario B substantially exceeds this linear expectation. The amplification ratio evaluates to:

\vskip-5.0pt\Lambda=\frac{\mathcal{R}^{\text{obs}}_{\text{loss}}}{\mathcal{R}^{\text{exp}}_{\text{loss}}}=\frac{1.0080}{0.179}=5.63

In more detail, the system degradation under the multi-vector attack exceeds linear expectations by a factor of
5.6
. Imposing the calibration condition, i.e.,
\frac{\mathcal{M}(S_{B})}{\mathcal{M}(S_{A})}=\Lambda
, we get:

\frac{\frac{(1-\gamma)}{3}\!\left(D_{\text{phy}}^{B}+D_{\text{op}}^{B}+D_{\text{cyb}}^{B}\right)+\gamma\!\left(D_{\text{phy}}^{B}\cdot D_{\text{op}}^{B}\cdot D_{\text{cyb}}^{B}\right)^{\!1/3}}{\frac{(1-\gamma)}{3}\!\left(D_{\text{phy}}^{A}+D_{\text{op}}^{A}+D_{\text{cyb}}^{A}\right)+\gamma\!\left(D_{\text{phy}}^{A}\cdot D_{\text{op}}^{A}\cdot D_{\text{cyb}}^{A}\right)^{\!1/3}}=\Lambda\vskip-1.0pt

Solving yields
\gamma=0.381
. Exogenous amplification is applied directly from the sub-indices in Table
III
, where climatic and regulatory factors contribute
15\%
and
60\%
, respectively.

IV
Conclusion

This paper proposes a multidimensional resilience framework,
\mathcal{MDRI}
, that decomposes power system degradation into five dimensions: physical, operational, digital-cyber, climatic, and regulatory, to characterize vulnerability under coordinated cyberattacks. By separating independent and coupled dimensional contributions,
\mathcal{MDRI}
addresses structural limitations of existing approaches, which treat resilience dimensions as orthogonal and consequently underestimate systemic risk. Validation on the IEEE 39-bus system under two attack scenarios (derived from the 2025 Polish cyberattack incident) demonstrates that multi-vector attacks produce degradation exceeding linear expectations by a factor of 5.6, with simultaneous dimensional failures contributing an additional 60.6% through endogenous coupling. Exogenous amplification from climatic and regulatory factors further increases total vulnerability by 84%, confirming that resilience quantification cannot be decoupled from institutional and environmental contexts.
