Expansion of smoke-free policies and their associations with observed smoking, policy support and voluntary home bans in South Korea: findings from the 2005–2020 ITC Korea surveys
Authors: Wonjeong Yoon, Hong Gwan Seo, Sungkyu Lee, Sujin Lim, Gil-yong Kim, Yeol Kim, Yoon-Jung Choi, Eon Sook Lee, Gang Meng, Steve S Xu, Anne C K Quah, Geoffrey T Fong, Sung-Il Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSouth Korea has progressively strengthened its smoke-free regulations over the past two decades. However, few studies have systematically examined how this expansion has corresponded with changes in observed smoking, support for smoke-free laws, voluntary home smoking bans and motivation to quit smoking.MethodsWe analysed nationally representative data from five waves of the International Tobacco Control Korea Surveys (2005: n=991; 2008: n=1733; 2010: n=1552; 2016: n=1989; 2020: n=3763), including 10 028 adults who smoked cigarettes at least weekly. Using a repeated cross-sectional design, we assessed self-reported smoking restrictions in bars/pubs, cafés/restaurants and workplaces, and examined their associations with observed smoking, support for smoking bans, voluntary home smoking bans and motivation to quit smoking. Adjusted associations were estimated using regression models with generalised estimating equations, accounting for survey weights and within-subject correlation.ResultsSmoking restrictions expanded substantially across all venues between 2005 and 2020. Over the same period, observed smoking declined markedly (51.4%–97.2% to 5.8%–12.4%), while support for smoking bans (5.3%–17.6% to 78.4%–88.1%), voluntary home smoking bans (29.3% to 60.3%) and motivation to quit smoking (36.3%–38.5% to 57.7%–60.4%) all increased. Both complete and partial bans were inversely associated with observed smoking, with stronger associations for complete bans (adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) 0.15–0.44 vs 0.47–0.95). Complete bans were also positively associated with support for smoke-free laws (aPRs 1.82–1.93) and voluntary home smoking bans (aPRs 1.27–1.45), whereas partial bans showed weak or no significant relationships. Only workplace smoking bans were associated with greater motivation to quit smoking (aPRs 1.92).ConclusionsThe expansion of smoke-free policies in South Korea has coincided with lower observed smoking, stronger public support and wider adoption of home smoking bans, highlighting that comprehensive bans are more effective than partial bans in strengthening smoke-free norms and advancing tobacco control efforts.Keywords: Public Health, Preventive Medicine, Prevalence, trends

Introduction

Secondhand smoke (SHS) is a preventable health hazard that causes lung cancer, heart disease and other serious health problems.1–3 The Global Burden of Disease Study estimated that 1.3 million deaths were attributable to SHS globally in 2019.3 Comprehensive smoke-free policies (SFPs) have been proven effective in reducing SHS exposure and delivering substantial public health benefits.4 Systematic reviews consistently demonstrate that smoke-free laws in indoor public spaces lead to reductions in morbidity and mortality from cardiovascular and respiratory diseases, as well as improved perinatal outcomes.5–8 Consequently, smoke-free laws form a core element of the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (WHO FCTC), which aims to protect populations from the harms of tobacco smoke.9

The Republic of Korea (hereafter, South Korea) ratified the WHO FCTC in 2005 and began expanding smoke-free regulations after enacting the National Health Promotion Act in 1995.10,11 The evolution of smoke-free regulations involved changes in both strength and coverage (figure 1). In terms of strength, indoor public places were initially required to designate smoking and smoke-free areas (1995), and from 2006 at least 50% of the floor area had to be smoke-free.12 A major policy shift occurred in 2012, when a 100% smoke-free requirement was introduced for all regulated indoor areas, although designated smoking rooms were still permitted. In terms of coverage, this policy was implemented progressively, expanding between 2012 and 2015 to include all food service establishments regardless of size. In 2019, smoking cafés previously exempted from regulations were also included, thereby closing remaining regulatory loopholes.13

These progressive regulations not only reduce SHS exposure but also influence social norms and smoking behaviour.14,15 Evidence shows that as SFPs become more comprehensive and widely enforced, public support for smoking bans increases.16,17 Additionally, comprehensive SFPs encourage voluntary home smoking bans, reducing SHS exposure to family members, particularly children and non-smoking adults.18–20 Research also suggests that these policies contribute to lower smoking prevalence by prompting people to quit smoking and discouraging smoking initiation.21 Therefore, in addition to reductions in SHS exposure, increased public support for smoke-free laws, voluntary home smoking bans and motivation to quit smoking serve as key indicators of the effectiveness of SFPs in national tobacco control efforts.22,23

Despite the implementation of progressively stronger smoke-free regulations in South Korea, changes in policy coverage and related outcomes over time remain insufficiently examined. This study aims to address this gap by analysing nationally representative data from the ITC Korea Survey between 2005 and 2020. The specific objectives are to: (1) identify trends in smoking restrictions (ie, complete, partial and no bans) in indoor public places and workplaces; (2) assess trends in observed smoking, support for complete smoking bans, voluntary home smoking bans and motivation to quit smoking as key outcomes; and (3) evaluate the associations between smoking restrictions and these outcomes.

Methods

Data and sample

Data were drawn from five waves of the International Tobacco Control (ITC) Korea Surveys conducted in 2005, 2008, 2010, 2016 and 2020. The earlier waves (2005–2010) followed a longitudinal cohort design with follow-up of respondents across waves and replenishment sampling to maintain sample size, whereas the 2016 and 2020 waves were conducted as independent cross-sectional surveys with newly recruited participants. For the present study, data from all waves were pooled to enable a repeated cross-sectional analysis.

Participants were recruited using probability-based sampling approaches in the earlier waves (2005–2010), including random-digit dialling (RDD) of landline telephones, with a dual-frame (landline and mobile) RDD design in 2016. The 2005–2016 waves primarily targeted adults who smoked cigarettes. In 2020, a web-based panel with quota sampling was used to reflect the diversification of tobacco product use, including cigarettes, e-cigarettes and heated tobacco products (HTPs). Across all waves, sampling was stratified by geographical region, age and sex, with allocation proportional to population size. Survey weights were applied to align the sample with national population distributions.

Survey data were collected using computer-assisted telephone interviewing in the 2005–2016 waves and via a self-administered web survey in 2020. Core questionnaire items on smoking behaviour and policy-relevant variables were retained across waves to ensure comparability, with minor refinements in question wording and response options where necessary. Detailed survey methods are described elsewhere.24–30

The five waves included 1002 (2005), 1818 (2008), 1753 (2010), 2000 (2016) and 4794 (2020) respondents. The analytical sample was restricted to adults aged ≥19 years who reported smoking at least weekly and had smoked ≥100 cigarettes in their lifetime, resulting in final sample sizes of 991, 1733, 1552, 1989 and 3763, respectively. Sample composition and baseline characteristics are presented in online supplemental figure S1 and online supplemental table S1.

Measures

Smoking restrictions

Smoking restrictions were assessed based on participants’ self-reported information for three types of indoor venues: (1) bars/pubs, (2) cafés/restaurants and (3) workplaces. For workplaces, responses were collected only among participants who were currently employed (indoor workers in 2020). Participants were asked, ‘Which of the following best describes the rules about smoking cigarettes [in bars and pubs where you live, in restaurants or cafés where you live, where you work]?’ Responses were categorised as: complete ban (‘never allowed anywhere indoors’), partial ban (‘allowed only in designated indoor areas’) and no ban (‘allowed indoors without rules or restrictions’).

Observed smoking

Observed smoking in (1) bars or pubs and (2) cafés or restaurants was assessed with the question, ‘The last time you visited [a bar or pub, a restaurant or café], were people smoking cigarettes inside?’ This question was asked only by respondents who had visited these places within the last 6 months. For (c) workplaces, observed smoking was assessed with the question, ‘In the last 30 days, have people smoked cigarettes in indoor areas where you work?’ asked only of those who were employed (only indoor workers in 2020). Responses were dichotomized to ‘yes’ and ‘no’ for each venue.

Support for complete smoking bans

From 2005 to 2016, respondents were asked, ‘For each of the following public places, please tell me if you think smoking should be allowed in all indoor areas, in some indoor areas, or not allowed indoors at all: [indoor areas of drinking establishments (e.g. bars, pubs), indoor areas of restaurants and cafés, workplaces]?’ Response options were: ‘all indoor areas’, ‘some indoor areas’ and ‘not at all’. In 2020, the question was: ‘Do you support or oppose a total ban on smoking cigarettes indoors: [at bars and pubs, at restaurants and cafés, in workplaces]?’ Response options included ‘strongly support’, ‘support’, ‘oppose’ and ‘strongly oppose’. Responses were harmonised by recoding ‘not at all’ (2005–2016) and ‘support/strongly support’ (2020) as indicating support for complete bans.

Voluntary home smoking bans

Voluntary home smoking bans were assessed with the question, ‘Which of the following best describes the rules about smoking cigarettes inside your home?’ Responses included: ‘never allowed anywhere indoors’, ‘allowed only in designated indoor areas’, ‘allowed indoors without rules or restrictions’ and ‘not allowed inside your home except under special circumstances’ (added in 2020). For analysis, responses of ‘never allowed anywhere indoors’ and ‘not allowed inside your home except under special circumstances’ were recoded as indicating a voluntary home smoking ban.

Motivation to quit smoking

Motivation to quit smoking due to smoking bans was assessed by asking: ‘In the past 6 months, have each of the following things led you to think about quitting?’ with two subitems: (1) ‘cigarette smoking restrictions in public places like restaurants or cafés, bars or pubs’, and (2) ‘cigarette smoking restrictions at work’. Response options were: ‘not at all’, ‘somewhat’ and ‘very much’. For analysis, responses of ‘somewhat’ and ‘very much’ were categorised as positive responses.

For all measures described above, responses of ‘don’t know’ and ‘refused’ were treated as missing. The distribution of these responses across survey waves is presented in online supplemental table S2.

Covariate

Covariates included: age (19–29, 30–39, 40–59 and 60+), sex (male and female), socioeconomic status (SES) and current use of other tobacco products (ie, e-cigarettes or HTPs, defined as at least weekly use). SES was derived from three-level education and income variables (high, moderate and low). Respondents in the ‘high’ category for either education or income were classified as having ‘high’ SES; remaining respondents in the ‘low’ category for either education or income were classified as having ‘low’ SES, and the rest were classified as having ‘moderate’ SES. Those who responded ‘don’t know’ or ‘refused’ for both variables were coded as missing.

Statistical analysis

We analysed data from five waves of the ITC Korea Survey (2005, 2008, 2010, 2016 and 2020) to examine trends and associations related to smoking restrictions and key policy-related outcomes. Pooled data from all waves were analysed within a repeated cross-sectional framework. Because the 2005–2010 waves included repeated observations from some respondents, generalised estimating equations were used to account for within-subject correlation in the longitudinal component.

Logistic regression models were fitted using PROC GENMOD to estimate adjusted prevalence (predicted marginal percentages) across survey waves. Prevalence estimates were obtained using the LSMEANS statement, and between-wave differences were tested using the DIFF option with t-tests. Within-subject correlation was specified using the REPEATED SUBJECT=id option for the 2005–2010 panel component.

Smoking restrictions (categorised as complete, partial or no ban) were dichotomised into two comparisons: (1) complete bans versus partial/no bans and (2) complete/partial bans versus no bans. All estimates were weighted using survey weights and adjusted for covariates, with statistical significance set at p<0.05.

To examine overall associations between smoking restrictions and outcomes, we conducted pooled analyses across all waves. Frequency analyses were conducted to report crude counts (N) and percentages (%), and Poisson regression with robust variance was used to estimate adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) and 95% CIs. These models also incorporated survey weights and covariate adjustments. All analyses were performed using SAS software V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Trends in smoking restrictions

Figure 2 illustrates trends in smoking restrictions in bars/pubs, cafés/restaurants and workplaces. Overall, the proportion of respondents reporting complete bans increased across all three venues, with sharper increases in bars/pubs and cafés/restaurants between 2010 and 2016, and more gradual increases in workplaces. In bars/pubs, fewer than half of respondents reported any restriction on indoor smoking in earlier survey years. The proportion reporting complete bans rose to 45.1% in 2016 and 59.7% in 2020, with at least partial bans reported by about 90% of respondents by 2020. In cafés/restaurants, the proportion reporting complete bans steadily increased from 8.8% in 2005 to 17.6% in 2010, and then rose sharply to 59.6% in 2016 and 68.8% in 2020. Reports of at least partial bans remained below 50% until 2010 but exceeded 90% in both 2016 and 2020. In workplaces, the proportion reporting complete bans increased steadily from 29.4% in 2005 to 76.5% in 2020. Reports of at least partial bans were already high, exceeding 70% in 2005 (73.2%) and 2008 (78.2%), and reaching 97.9% in 2020.

Trends in observed smoking, policy support, home bans and cessation motives

Observed smoking

Figure 3a illustrates trends in observed smoking in bars/pubs, cafés/restaurants and workplaces. Observed smoking declined across all three locations over the study period. In bars/pubs, observed smoking exceeded 90% from 2005 to 2010. Following the implementation of complete bans in 2012, observed smoking declined sharply, reaching 20.6% in 2016 and 9.9% in 2020. In cafés/restaurants, observed smoking was approximately 70% (69.5% in 2010) prior to the implementation of complete bans but declined sharply to 10.7% in 2016 and 5.8% in 2020. In workplaces, observed smoking decreased more gradually, from 51.4% in 2005 to 35.4% in 2010, 19.1% in 2016 and 12.4% in 2020.

Support for complete smoking bans

Figure 3b presents trends in support for complete smoking bans in bars/pubs, cafés/restaurants and workplaces. Support for complete smoking bans increased across all three locations over time. Although modest increases were observed following the 2012 policy implementation, the largest increase occurred between 2016 and 2020. In bars/pubs, only 5.3% of respondents expressed support for complete smoking bans in 2005, remaining around 12%–13% in 2008 and 2010. This proportion rose significantly to 37.2% in 2016 and to 78.4% in 2020. In cafés/restaurants, support for complete bans was 15.8% in 2005 and increased to about half (50.5%) in 2016, reaching 85.7% in 2020. In workplaces, support was 17.6% in 2005, rose to around half (51.4%) in 2016 and reached 88.1% in 2020.

Voluntary home smoking bans

Figure 3c depicts trends in voluntary home smoking bans over the study period. The proportion reporting voluntary home smoking bans increased from 29.3% in 2005 to 51.5% in 2010. Following the implementation of complete bans in public venues in 2012, it continued to rise, reaching 60.3% in 2016 and remaining at that level (60.3%) in 2020.

Motivation to quit smoking

Figure 3d shows trends in motivation to quit smoking due to smoking bans in public places (ie, bars/pubs, cafés/restaurants) and workplaces. Overall, motivation to quit smoking initially declined but subsequently rebounded, surpassing earlier levels. In 2005, 36.3% of respondents cited smoking bans in public places and 38.5% cited workplace bans as reasons for quitting smoking. These proportions declined by 2010 to 32.0% for public places and 31.6% for workplaces. Following the implementation of complete bans in 2012, the proportions increased to 38.7% for public places and 33.5% for workplaces in 2016, and further rose to 60.4% and 57.7%, respectively, by 2020.

Table 1 presents the association between smoking restrictions and observed smoking in bars/pubs, cafés/restaurants and workplaces. Associations between smoking restrictions and support for complete smoking bans

Table 1 also presents the association between smoking restrictions and support for complete smoking bans in bars/pubs, cafés/restaurants and workplaces. Respondents who reported complete bans in public places and workplaces were significantly more likely to support complete smoking bans compared with those who reported no bans (aPR, 95% CI: bars/pubs=1.93, 1.65 to 2.26; cafés/restaurants=1.84, 1.53 to 2.20; workplaces=1.82, 1.55 to 2.14). However, support for complete smoking bans showed weak or no significant differences between respondents who reported partial bans and those who reported no bans (aPR, 95% CI: bars/pubs=1.18, 1.00 to 1.38; cafés/restaurants=1.04, 0.87 to 1.26; workplaces=1.02, 0.86 to 1.21).

Associations between smoking restrictions and voluntary home smoking bans

Table 1 examines the association between smoking restrictions and voluntary home smoking bans. Respondents who reported complete bans in public places and workplaces were significantly more likely to implement voluntary home smoking bans than those who reported no bans (aPR, 95% CI: bars/pubs=1.45, 1.33 to 1.58; cafés/restaurants=1.33, 1.16 to 1.54; workplaces=1.27, 1.13 to 1.44). However, respondents who reported partial bans showed no significant association with voluntary home smoking bans compared with those who reported no bans (aPR, 95% CI: bars/pubs=1.05, 0.97 to 1.13; cafés/restaurants=1.07, 0.93 to 1.23; workplaces=1.10, 0.97 to 1.25).

Associations between smoking restrictions and motivation to quit smoking

Table 1 presents the association between smoking restrictions and motivation to quit smoking. A significant positive association was found only for workplaces, where respondents who reported complete bans were nearly twice as likely to report motivation to quit smoking compared with those who reported no bans (aPR, 95% CI: workplaces=1.92, 1.55 to 2.39). In contrast, for bars/pubs and cafés/restaurants, the aPRs did not indicate significant differences (aPR, 95% CI: bars/pubs=0.94, 0.85 to 1.04; cafés/restaurants=1.02, 0.87 to 1.20). Similarly, respondents who reported partial bans at workplaces were also significantly more likely to cite motivation to quit smoking than those who reported no bans (aPR, 95% CI: workplaces=1.92, 1.55 to 2.38). However, in bars/pubs and cafés/restaurants, no significant differences were observed between respondents reporting partial bans and those reporting no bans (aPR, 95% CI: bars/pubs=1.07, 0.97 to 1.16; cafés/restaurants=1.13, 0.96 to 1.32).

Discussion

This study examined the impact of strengthened smoke-free regulations in South Korea using data from five waves of the ITC Korea Surveys (2005–2020). During this period, reports of complete smoking bans in indoor public places and workplaces increased sharply. This was accompanied by marked reductions in observed smoking, greater public support for smoking bans and voluntary home smoking bans, and stronger motivation to quit smoking among adults who smoked. Overall, the implementation of comprehensive SFPs was positively associated with these favourable outcomes, demonstrating their effectiveness in promoting a healthier smoke-free environment.

This study identified three distinct patterns in the relationship between smoking restrictions and key outcomes. First, both complete and partial smoking bans were associated with reductions in observed smoking, with complete bans showing significantly stronger associations. Although partial bans were also associated with lower observed smoking, prior studies have suggested that they may inadvertently increase smoking frequency among adults who smoke31 and signal that smoking is permitted in designated areas,32 thereby weakening the intended regulatory impact. Other studies have indicated that complete bans are more successful in improving indoor air quality33 and reducing smoke-related illnesses.34 Therefore, comprehensive SFPs are more effective than partial bans in reducing SHS exposure and its associated health risks.

Second, only complete bans were significantly associated with stronger support for smoke-free laws and greater adoption of voluntary home smoking bans. These indicators are essential for fostering a smoke-free culture beyond regulated public spaces.22 Public support and policy implementation reinforce each other,35 as comprehensive bans enhance public endorsement of smoke-free laws,16 which in turn helps sustain and expand these policies.36 Supportive individuals are also more likely to perceive indoor smoking as socially unacceptable37 and to recognise the harms of SHS,38 leading to voluntary home smoking bans that reduce exposure and promote cessation.19,39 Thus, implementing comprehensive bans is vital for maximising policy effectiveness and strengthening social norms that sustain smoke-free environments.

Third, unlike other outcomes, neither complete nor partial bans in public places were associated with increased motivation to quit smoking, whereas workplace bans showed a significant positive association. This contrasts with previous studies reporting that comprehensive smoking bans can enhance quitting intentions.40,41 These findings imply that the behavioural impact of SFPs depends not only on policy strength but also on the consistency and context of policy exposure. Workplace bans offer a structured and continuously enforced environment that may heighten discomfort or social pressure, thereby encouraging people who smoke to quit or reduce consumption.15 In contrast, public place bans, while beneficial, may exert weaker effects due to their broader scope and inconsistent enforcement.42 Therefore, SFPs implemented in consistent, structured environments such as workplaces may be particularly effective in promoting smoking cessation and driving behavioural changes among people who smoke.

Collectively, our findings support the greater effectiveness of comprehensive over partial smoking bans. Prior to the implementation of comprehensive SFPs, South Korea had high levels of SHS exposure in public places, reaching 70%–90% despite partial smoking bans.23 Following the enforcement of comprehensive smoke-free legislation, observed smoking in public places and workplaces declined sharply to approximately 10%–20% in 2016. This reduction is consistent with government reports showing that, following 2012 policy implementation, indoor fine particulate matter (PM2.5; particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter ≤2.5 μm) levels in hospitality venues decreased by approximately 40%. Furthermore, the impact of Korea’s 2012 comprehensive smoke-free law was maintained and strengthened by 2020. This pattern mirrors and extends the experience in France, where the effectiveness of that country’s comprehensive smoking ban remained robust 5 years post-implementation.43 These findings reinforce the WHO’s recommendation that comprehensive SFPs encompassing all indoor public places and workplaces are the most effective strategy for minimising SHS exposure and supporting smoke-free environments.44

Nevertheless, a non-negligible proportion of observed smoking (approximately 10%–20%) persisted even after the implementation of comprehensive SFPs. Because key measures in this study, particularly observed smoking and smoking restrictions, are based on participants’ self-reported experiences and perceptions, these findings may reflect not only actual exposure but also incomplete compliance with or enforcement of SFPs. This interpretation is consistent with national inspection data from South Korea in 2013, which documented continued violations of smoking bans by individuals in designated smoke-free areas. Taken together, these results suggest that while comprehensive SFPs are highly effective in reducing SHS exposure, their full impact depends on ongoing monitoring, robust enforcement, and continued public awareness efforts.

This study had some limitations. First, although the five surveys employed identical or nearly-identical measures across the years (since all were conducted by the ITC Project), differences in sampling designs and survey administration methods may have affected the comparability. In particular, the larger sample size in 2020 may have increased statistical precision relative to earlier waves. While survey weights were applied to enhance representativeness and reduce bias in point estimates, temporal comparisons should be interpreted with consideration of these differences. Second, changes in question wording and response options across waves, particularly for support for smoking bans, may introduce measurement non-equivalence. Although variables were harmonised for analysis, some observed trends may partly reflect differences in survey instruments rather than true temporal changes. Third, due to data constraints, the analysis was restricted to adults who smoke, excluding people who do not smoke who may also be affected by SHS exposure. In addition, measures of observed smoking were limited to respondents who had recently visited specific venues or were employed, which may have introduced selection bias. Therefore, findings related to observed smoking primarily apply to respondents with recent exposure to these settings rather than to the general population. Fourth, different recall periods were used across settings (eg, last visit within 6 months for hospitality venues and past 30 days for workplaces) to reflect the context and frequency of each behaviour or exposure, following established survey protocols. However, these differences may have introduced recall bias and limited direct comparability across measures. Finally, measures of smoking restrictions and observed smoking were based on self-reported data and may therefore be subject to recall bias, social desirability bias and variation in policy awareness. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting perceived rather than objectively verified policy implementation and compliance.

Despite these limitations, this study provides robust and policy-relevant insights based on a long-term, nationally representative dataset spanning multiple survey waves. The consistency of trends across waves, despite differences in sampling and measurement, supports the overall validity of the findings. Although self-reported measures may introduce bias, they are widely used in large-scale population studies and remain informative for understanding population-level patterns and perceived policy implementation. Future studies should incorporate objective measures of SHS exposure and enforcement data to better distinguish between actual and perceived compliance with SFPs. Extending analyses to non-smoking populations may also help improve understanding of the broader public health impact of SFPs.

Conclusion

Comprehensive smoking bans were substantially more effective than partial bans in reducing observed smoking across public places and workplaces. Moreover, comprehensive bans were associated with stronger support for smoke-free laws and increased voluntary home smoking bans, thereby reinforcing smoke-free social norms beyond regulated settings. These findings support the implementation and sustained enforcement of comprehensive SFPs as an important strategy for advancing population-level tobacco control.
