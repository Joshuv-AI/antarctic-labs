Self-supervised graph contrastive learning for scRNA-seq clustering
Authors: Tong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSingle-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) enables characterization of cellular heterogeneity, but accurate and stable clustering remains challenging due to high dimensionality, sparsity, and technical noise. Many existing methods insufficiently exploit intrinsic cell–cell relationships and latent cell-type signals, leading to suboptimal representations and unstable assignments. The objective of this study is to develop a self-supervised graph contrastive framework for scRNA-seq clustering that jointly leverages augmented views, intrinsic cell–cell relationships, and pseudo-label-guided graph refinement to improve clustering robustness and biological interpretability.MethodsWe propose Self-Supervised Contrastive Graph Learning (SSGL), which refers specifically to the proposed scRNA-seq clustering framework and not to a generic or pre-existing method. SSGL generates two augmented views of each cell via dual random gene masking, then learns representations with a momentum-encoder architecture. A refined cell–cell graph is constructed by intersecting a k-nearest-neighbor similarity graph with pseudo-label consistency, and a graph-aware contrastive objective enforces agreement between augmented views while preserving local neighborhood structure.ResultsWe evaluated SSGL on eight public scRNA-seq benchmarks spanning different tissues and sequencing platforms, and compared it with representative clustering baselines using NMI and ARI. Across all datasets, SSGL achieved the best overall clustering performance, with average NMI = 0.876 and average ARI = 0.926, outperforming competing approaches and showing more consistent results across heterogeneous datasets. Relative to AttentionAE-SC, the strongest overall baseline based on the averaged results, SSGL improved the average NMI and ARI by approximately 4.4% and 6.7%, respectively. Ablation analyses demonstrated that incorporating the self-supervised refined graph improves clustering beyond conventional contrastive objectives. Visualization and marker-gene analyses further supported that SSGL yields compact, well-separated clusters and recovers biologically coherent cell groups, including rare populations.ConclusionsSSGL improves scRNA-seq clustering accuracy and stability by jointly leveraging augmented views, intrinsic cell–cell relationships, and pseudo-label-guided graph refinement within a graph contrastive learning framework. This provides robust representations that support reliable cell-type discovery and downstream biological interpretation.Keywords: scRNA-seq clustering, Graph contrastive learning, Self-supervised learning

Introduction

The rapid advancement of scRNA-seq technology has revolutionized our ability to study gene expression at the single-cell level, providing unprecedented insight into the heterogeneity of tissues, organs, and complex diseases [1, 2]. By capturing the transcriptomic landscape of individual cells, scRNA-seq has significantly improved our understanding of fundamental biological processes, such as cell differentiation, lineage tracing, and gene regulation [3]. Clustering is a key step in scRNA-seq analysis because it groups cells into putative subpopulations and enables downstream identification of cell types and states. Because many downstream analyses are based on these assignments, robust clustering is essential for reliable biological interpretation [4–6]. Early computational studies have systematically analyzed the challenges of unsupervised clustering in scRNA-seq data, including high dimensionality, sparsity, and technical noise. In particular, Kiselev provided a comprehensive overview of the methodological limitations and open challenges in single-cell clustering, which laid the foundation for subsequent developments in robust and scalable clustering approaches [4]. To this end, several clustering techniques that identify cell types have been widely studied. For example, Seurat begins by identifying multiple mutual nearest neighbors (MNN) pairs—pairs of cells that are each other’s nearest neighbors in expression space—based on intercellular distance metrics, and then leverages the Louvain algorithm to cluster the cells [7, 8]. SIMLR leverages multiple kernel functions to construct a robust cell-to-cell similarity matrix, which is then used as input for a spectral clustering algorithm to assign cells to distinct clusters [9]. To address the issue of dropouts in scRNA-seq data, CIDR (Clustering through Imputation and Dimensionality Reduction) first imputes missing values (i.e., zeros) during distance calculation on the gene expression matrix. Principal component analysis (PCA) is then applied for dimensionality reduction, followed by hierarchical clustering of cells [10]. This approach improves the stability of cell-distance estimation. Other imputation-based methods, such as MAGIC and scImpute, have also been developed to handle dropout events and enhance cluster performance [11, 12].

To overcome the limitations of individual clustering algorithms in scRNA-seq data analysis, ensemble learning approaches have been introduced to improve both precision and robustness [9]. For example, methods such as SC3 [13], SAME [14], and SAFE [15] employ different consensus strategies to integrate multiple clustering solutions and assign cells to their respective subpopulations. By aggregating the output of various algorithms, these ensemble methods reduce the impact of biases present in the individual methods and produce more stable clustering outcomes. With the increasing scale and complexity of scRNA-seq datasets, traditional clustering pipelines based on fixed distance metrics and linear dimensionality reduction have shown limited capacity to capture complex cellular heterogeneity. As a result, Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) have emerged as powerful tools for feature extraction in scRNA-seq analysis. Several deep clustering frameworks use DNNs to simultaneously learn informative low-dimensional representations and delineate distinct cell types [16, 17]. For example, scDeepCluster integrates dimensionality reduction with clustering in a unified deep learning framework, enabling robust identification of cell types from high-dimensional data [16]. scVI (Single-cell Variational Inference) employs a Bayesian generative model to capture both biological variability and technical noise, thus yielding latent embeddings that faithfully represent the underlying cellular heterogeneity [18]. DCA (Deep Count Autoencoder) uses a zero-inflated negative binomial (ZINB) model—which captures over-dispersed count data and an excess of zeros caused by dropout events—to learn accurate cell representations despite data sparsity [19]. Similarly, scAce builds on a ZINB variational autoencoder to obtain cell embeddings and adopts an adaptive cluster merging strategy to refine clustering outcomes [20]. In another approach, scziDesk adopts a pre-training phase based on DCA and then combines a soft k-means algorithm with deep embedding clustering (DEC) to cluster cell representations [21]. Meanwhile, scGANs exploits Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN) to obtain cell type assignments by learning cell features in an adversarial manner [22], and scGNN employs a graph neural network framework to model cell–cell relationships and learn robust representations for single-cell RNA-seq analysis [23]. The inherent sparsity and technical noise in scRNA-seq data present significant challenges to clustering algorithms, hindering accurate representation of underlying cellular relationships, and complicating reliable identification of cell types [24]. To address these issues, recent studies have explored the application of contrastive learning techniques to scRNA-seq data, including instance-level contrastive clustering and cluster-aware contrastive objectives [25–27].

Recent advances in self-supervised learning have significantly influenced representation learning across multiple domains. Seminal contrastive learning frameworks, such as SimCLR and BYOL, demonstrated that discriminative representations can be learned without explicit labels by enforcing consistency between different augmented views of the same sample [28, 29]. These methods established the theoretical foundation for contrastive representation learning and provide the methodological basis for instance-level contrastive objectives such as InfoNCE, while single-cell applications require domain-specific adaptations to handle sparsity, noise, and cell–cell relationships [25, 28–30]. Thus, several self-supervised approaches have been proposed to learn representations by contrasting positive and negative pairs, thus capturing meaningful patterns in the single-cell data [25, 31]. For example, Contrastive-sc employs contrastive learning with an InfoNCE loss, which pulls representations of two augmented views of the same cell closer while pushing apart representations from different cells, enabling unsupervised clustering of scRNA-seq data [25]. Similarly, scDECL integrates contrastive learning with pairwise constraints to improve clustering performance, addressing the challenges posed by data sparsity and heterogeneity [27]. The Cluster-aware Iterative Contrastive Learning (CICL) framework uses an iterative process to progressively refine clustering structures in scRNA-seq data, using a cluster-aware contrastive loss to manage complex cellular heterogeneity [26]. More broadly, GNN and graph contrastive learning frameworks (GCL) have become an important paradigm for modeling structured data, demonstrating a strong capability to capture relational dependencies in complex systems [32, 33]. scGPCL combines a graph neural network with a contrastive learning module to enhance cell representations [34] and GraphCL combines graph structure with contrastive objectives to enhance representation learning by using topology-aware augmentations [32, 33].

However, these contrastive clustering methods primarily focus on instance-level learning without fully incorporating clustering structures in the latent space and capturing the intrinsic relationships between cells across augmented data. As a result, the cell representations learned by these networks may overlook meaningful semantic information essential for effective feature learning and accurate clustering. Consequently, their performance in cell type identification may be suboptimal, limiting the overall accuracy of the clustering [30, 35, 36]. To address these limitations, we introduce Self-Supervised Contrastive Graph Learning (SSGL), a novel, task-specific framework for scRNA-seq clustering that integrates augmented views, refined cell–cell graph construction, and pseudo-label-guided graph refinement into a graph-aware contrastive learning objective. Our approach is based on the assumption that samples within the same cluster and their augmentations should exhibit similarity. Specifically, SSGL first applies dual random masking to gene expression profiles, generating two augmented datasets. A refined cell–cell graph is then constructed to capture intrinsic relationships between cells in the augmented representation space. Additionally, pseudo-label information generated during training is used to guide representation learning and ensure consistency between the clustering results of the augmented views. By incorporating this refined graph information into the graph-aware contrastive objective, SSGL uses pseudo-label-guided neighborhood structure to guide feature learning, resulting in more robust representations and stabilized clustering assignments. Ultimately, this leads to more reliable and biologically meaningful cell-type identification. To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed method, we conducted experiments on multiple publicly available scRNA-seq datasets commonly used for benchmarking clustering algorithms. The results demonstrate that SSGL significantly outperforms existing clustering methods in terms of both accuracy and clustering stability, offering more precise biological interpretations. These findings align with recent advances in deep learning-based clustering for single-cell analysis. Furthermore, SSGL effectively mitigates the impact of data sparsity, captures intricate cellular relationships, and improves the overall reliability of clustering outcomes. By overcoming key limitations of conventional clustering techniques, SSGL provides an advanced and scalable solution for single-cell transcriptomics, improving our ability to analyze cellular diversity and uncover critical insights into normal physiological processes and disease mechanisms.

Finally, Sect. 4 concludes the paper and summarizes the key findings.

Data augmentation module

Data augmentation is a key technique in contrastive learning, improving model performance by increasing data diversity without requiring manual labeling [30]. Augmentation methods vary across domains: in computer vision, they include random cropping, color jittering, and rotation [30]. For scRNA-seq data, augmentation strategies need to be redesigned to reflect the sparse and high-dimensional structure of gene-expression profiles.

For scRNA-seq data, characterized by its unique one-dimensional vector format, we propose a dropout-based augmentation method that masks the expression values of selected genes while incorporating a small amount of Gaussian noise [22, 37, 38]. Specifically, starting with a preprocessed gene expression matrix, we generate two distinct augmented views for each cell sample by randomly masking 20% of the genes in each view. These masked genes are excluded from subsequent computations by setting their expression values to zero, effectively concealing a subset of features. To further simulate real-world variability in gene expression, we introduce Gaussian noise, drawing inspiration from established practices. Comparison methods and experimental setting

To ensure a fair and consistent evaluation, we adopted a unified data preprocessing pipeline for all compared methods. Specifically, raw count matrices were first filtered to remove genes and cells with zero counts, followed by normalization and scaling per-cell using the Scanpy package. Next, a subset of highly variable genes was selected. Finally, a z-score transformation was applied to normalize the data, ensuring a zero mean and unit variance for each gene.

We compare our method against ten state-of-the-art clustering algorithms: AttentionAE-SC [44], DESC [45], Leiden [46], SC3 [13], scGNN [23], scGAC [47], scGraph [48], scDeepCluster [16], scvi-tools [49], and SCEA [50]. Each method was run on every dataset using different random seeds to account for initialization sensitivity, and the mean performance metrics were reported. In our method, both the dropout probability and batch size were adjustable during the experiments, allowing us to explore their influence on contrastive learning dynamics and the number of negative samples involved.

Compared to AttentionAE-SC, which achieves the second-best overall performance, SSGL achieves higher scores on most datasets. In ARI, on the Romanov dataset, SSGL achieves 0.790, outperforming AttentionAE-SC’s 0.765. On the Smart-seq2-Diaphragm dataset, SSGL achieves 0.978, outperforming AttentionAE-SC’s 0.925. Although AttentionAE-SC performs slightly better in a few datasets, SSGL exhibits more balanced and consistent performance in all datasets.

Compared to scDeepCluster, SSGL consistently achieves superior results, with an average NMI of 0.876 versus 0.749 for scDeepCluster. For example, on the Quake_10x_Spleen dataset, SSGL attains an NMI of 0.729, whereas scDeepCluster achieves only 0.552. These results indicate that SSGL not only exhibits stronger generalization capabilities but also maintains higher stability and consistency across diverse datasets. Other approaches, such as SCEA and scGAC, display considerable performance fluctuations, suggesting greater sensitivity to dataset-specific characteristics. In contrast, SSGL achieves more consistent and reliable results. Moreover, although scGNN and scGraph also exploit neighborhood structural information during model training, SSGL consistently outperforms both across all evaluation metrics and datasets.

In summary, SSGL achieves state-of-the-art performance and demonstrates remarkable robustness across heterogeneous scRNA-seq datasets. Its consistent superiority, particularly on challenging datasets such as Quake_10x_Spleen and Muraro, highlights its strong adaptability to diverse biological scenarios and data heterogeneity.

Representation learning with cell type analysis

The primary goal of the proposed method is to develop a robust and reliable representation for the cluster analysis of scRNA-seq; its effectiveness is therefore highly dependent on the quality of the learned representation [51, 52]. To assess this, we visualize the learned representations using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) and perform a comparative analysis with the raw data, complemented by an evaluation of within-cell-type consistency.

As shown in Fig. 4, the visualization of the raw data indicates that different cell types are relatively dispersed and exhibit substantial intermixing. For example, immune populations such as B cells and T cells overlap extensively, making them difficult to distinguish. The boundaries between cell groups are therefore ambiguous, which may lead to misclassification—a common challenge in scRNA-seq clustering [4]. Overall, most cell types do not form clearly separated clusters in the raw embedding, but instead display a diffuse distribution. In contrast, the representation learned by SSGL produces markedly improved separation. In particular, B cells and T cells form more well-defined and compact clusters, and the intermixing observed in the raw data is substantially reduced. This suggests that SSGL captures discriminative cell-type characteristics while attenuating the influence of technical noise, which often undermines clustering performance in scRNA-seq analysis. Moreover, rare cell populations (i.e., those containing only a small number of cells) remain difficult to resolve for AttentionAE-SC and scDeepCluster (highlighted by the red circle in Fig. 4). By comparison, SSGL separates these rare types into distinct and compact clusters. This result further demonstrates SSGL’s ability to distinguish highly similar cell populations and improve clustering stability, which is critical for accurate downstream interpretation and annotation. Overall, SSGL demonstrates consistent advantages over existing methods by increasing inter-cluster separation and enhancing intra-cluster compactness, thereby mitigating cell-type intermixing and unstable groupings observed in the raw data. In addition, SSGL exhibits strong robustness to noise, enabling reliable discrimination of closely related cell types despite the technical variability inherent to scRNA-seq data.

Convergence analysis

We conduct convergence analysis to examine the training stability of SSGL. Specifically, we tracked the loss values together with the NMI and ARI scores throughout the training process in four representative scRNA-seq datasets: Quake_10x_Limb_Muscle, Klein, Romanov, and Quake_Smart-seq2_Diaphragm, as shown in Fig. 5. Across these datasets, the loss value decreases rapidly in the early training stage and then approaches a stable range, while NMI and ARI generally increase and subsequently fluctuate within relatively narrow ranges. This joint pattern indicates that SSGL reaches a stable optimization state and maintains stable clustering quality during training. For the Klein and Romanov datasets, both NMI and ARI show steady improvement followed by early stabilization, suggesting smooth training dynamics and increasingly consistent agreement between predicted clusters and ground-truth labels. For Quake_10x_Limb_Muscle and Quake_Smart-seq2_Diaphragm, small late-epoch oscillations are observed, which may reflect intrinsic heterogeneity and noise in the data; nevertheless, the metrics remain stable after the initial training phase.

Biological case study

To further evaluate the biological interpretability of the clustering results produced by SSGL, we used COSG to identify the top marker genes associated with each predicted cell cluster. Figure 7 displays representative expression dot plots and heatmaps of the top three marker genes for each cluster in the Quake_10x_Bladder and Quake_Smart-seq2_Limb_Muscle datasets. In the dot plots, color intensity indicates the average expression level of each gene within a cluster, whereas dot size represents the proportion of cells expressing that gene. The corresponding heatmaps provide complementary visualization of the same genes across clusters and highlight clear cluster-specific expression domains.

Dot color represents normalized average expression, and dot size indicates the proportion of cells expressing the gene within each cluster. In the Quake_10x_Bladder dataset, Wfdc2 showed cluster-enriched expression in cluster 1 and Gsta4 displayed a similar cluster-specific pattern. These observations are consistent with bladder epithelial/urothelial-associated expression programs reported in bladder single-cell resources and the Human Protein Atlas, although we describe these genes conservatively as bladder cluster-associated genes rather than universal canonical markers [54, 55]. Based on the normalized average-expression scale in Fig. 7, Wfdc2 and Gsta4 reached average expression values of approximately 4–5 in the enriched cluster, compared with mostly below 1 in the remaining clusters, corresponding to an approximate four- to five-fold or greater cluster-to-background enrichment.

In the Quake_Smart-seq2_Limb_Muscle dataset, Dcn, Serpinf1, and Ly6c1 were preferentially enriched in specific clusters, consistent with previously reported fibro-adipogenic progenitor, fibroblast/ECM-remodeling, and mesenchymal stromal transcriptional programs in skeletal muscle single-cell studies [56–58]. Quantitatively, Dcn and Col1a2 were most prominent in cluster 4, with normalized average expression close to 5 and substantially lower expression in most other clusters, indicating an approximate five-fold or greater enrichment. Serpinf1 showed a similar cluster-4-associated pattern with roughly three- to four-fold enrichment, whereas Ly6c1 was enriched in cluster 2 with an average expression around 3–4 relative to near-background levels in most other clusters.

Overall, these results indicate that the clusters identified by SSGL are not only well separated in latent space but also biologically meaningful: their marker-gene profiles are supported by prior bladder and skeletal muscle single-cell literature and by quantitative enrichment patterns in the expression visualizations.

Discussion

SSGL also has a distinct position relative to recent single-cell foundation models such as scGPT and scFoundation [59, 60]. Foundation models learn general-purpose representations through large-scale pretraining and can provide strong transferable embeddings for diverse downstream tasks. SSGL, by contrast, is a lightweight and target-dataset-oriented framework that does not require external pretraining corpora. These directions are therefore complementary rather than mutually exclusive. Pretrained foundation-model embeddings could be used as initial node features for SSGL, potentially improving similarity estimation and graph construction, whereas SSGL-style graph-aware contrastive regularization may help adapt pretrained embeddings to datasets with different sequencing platforms, batch structures, or tissue-specific cell-state compositions.

Despite these advantages, several limitations remain. First, scalability may become challenging for atlas-scale datasets because both k-NN graph construction and graph neural network training can be memory- and time-intensive [61, 62]. Approximate nearest-neighbor search, mini-batch subgraph training, and hierarchical coarse-to-fine clustering are practical strategies for reducing computational cost [62, 63]. Second, SSGL depends on the quality of the initial neighborhood graph. When batch effects or technical artifacts dominate the nearest-neighbor structure, a self-supervised graph objective may reinforce non-biological relationships [64]. Future work should therefore incorporate batch-aware graph construction, cross-batch neighbor balancing, or integration embeddings while carefully monitoring whether biological signals are preserved. Third, although the present study evaluates multiple benchmark datasets and a biological case study, broader validation on large disease cohorts, multi-sample settings, and rare-cell scenarios is needed to assess generalizability under more complex experimental conditions.

Overall, the main contribution of this work is to integrate augmented-view consistency, refined cell–cell relational structure, and pseudo-label-guided graph refinement into a unified self-supervised objective. This design improves clustering accuracy, supports stable optimization, and yields marker-gene patterns consistent with known biological programs. In future work, we will extend SSGL toward single-cell multi-omics integration, improve scalability for atlas-level datasets, and evaluate its utility in clinically relevant cohorts where robust cell-state stratification may support mechanistic discovery, prognosis assessment, and treatment-response analysis.

Conclusion

In this work, we propose a new framework that addresses the challenges of scRNA-seq clustering through self-supervised contrastive graph learning. The proposed method effectively captures the intrinsic structural information of high-dimensional single-cell data, thereby improving both inter-cluster separability and intra-cluster compactness. By integrating graph-based contrastive learning with semantic structure-aware augmentation, our approach can better exploit semantic relationships even in the presence of noise and missing information. Extensive experiments on multiple benchmark datasets demonstrate that the proposed method outperforms existing state-of-the-art approaches, particularly in scenarios involving highly similar cell types and technical noise. In addition, convergence analysis and ablation studies validate the stability and effectiveness of our overall design choices, highlighting the critical role of the self-supervised contrastive graph learning strategy in performance gains. These results suggest that our framework may provide a more reliable basis for characterizing tissue- and disease-related cellular heterogeneity, thereby supporting downstream analyses related to patient stratification, prognosis assessment, and treatment-response studies. In future work, we will extend the framework to single-cell multi-omics integration and further evaluate its utility in real-world clinical cohorts and complex disease models to support mechanistic discovery and clinically meaningful stratification. Robust clustering can support cellular-resolution studies in clinically relevant conditions by enabling more reliable identification of disease-associated cell populations and transcriptional states [65–67].
