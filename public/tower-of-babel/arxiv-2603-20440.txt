Data assimilation for the barotropic Navier–Stokes system
Eduard Feireisl

Abstract
We consider a continuous data assimilation method for the barotropic Navier–Stokes system. The observed solution is supposed to be bounded on the whole time period of observation, while the synchronized solution, usually provided by a numerical method, belongs to the class of dissipative solutions that is considerably larger than the class of conventional weak solutions. A complete synchronization is shown on any compact prediction interval provided the nudging parameters are chosen appropriately.

Introduction

Recently, there has been a considerable effort to establish
a mathematical foundation of the continuous/discrete data assimilation
techniques widely used in the real world applications of fluid mechanics, in particular in meteorology. Initiated by the seminal work of Azouani, Olson, and Titi
[
3
]
, a substantial number of results has appeared concerning mostly the 2D incompressible Navier–Stokes system, see Biswas et al
[
8
]
, Foias
Mondaini and Titi, Foias, and Mondaini
[
16
]
,
or the related Oberbeck–Boussinesq approximation examined by
Farhat, Jolly, and Titi
[
11
]
. Applying similar techniques to the physically relevant 3D geometry is hampered by the well known difficulties concerning the well posedness of the Navier–Stokes system.
Balakrishna and Biswas
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
proposed a new regularity criterion for the 3D incompressible Navier–Stokes system based on the knowledge of the interpolants of the
observed solution that can remedy the estimates in the data assimilation period, however, this piece of information is still missing in the subsequent prediction (forecast) period.

The aim of the data assimilation process is to recover the
observed solution
of a physically grounded mathematical model through the knowledge of the observation data collected in the
assimilation period
. Specifically, the observed solution exists in the
observation period
- the time interval
(T^{-},T^{+})
, and the observable data are available in the data assimilation (pre-forecast) period
[T_{0},T]
,
T^{-}<T_{0}<T<T^{+}
. The goal is to construct a
synchronized solution
defined in the time interval
[T_{0},T^{+})
that provides the desired approximation of the observed solution in the
prediction
(forecast) period
(T,T^{+})
. To simplify notation, we set the beginning
of the data assimilation period
T_{0}=0
.

The known results are based on the existence of a finite number of determining modes available for
dissipative
systems, see Bercovici et al.
[
7
]
, Constantin et al.
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
. The synchronized problem accommodates the data through
nudging terms that force the observed and synchronized solution to approach each other exponentially fast in the data assimilation period, where it is customary to set
T=\infty
. In practice, however,
the data assimilation (pre-forecast) period
(0,T)
is quite short, in particular much shorter than the prediction (forecast) period

(T,T^{+})
, see Lean et al.
[
18
]
. Consequently, to ensure reliable forecast initial data, the proximity of the observed and synchronized solution must be reached at a finite time
T>0
by careful tuning of the nudging parameters.

Our objective is to show convergence (synchronization) of the data assimilation method for the compressible Navier–Stokes system. Although thermal effects are completely ignored in the present study, we believe that the same approach can be applied to the full (temperature dependent) Navier–Stokes–Fourier system physically relevant in meteorological models. Our results are conditioned by two working hypotheses imposed on the observed solution:

•

The observed solution is bounded in the whole observation period
(T^{-},T^{+})
.

•

The observed solution is regular (smooth) at the initial time
T^{-}
.

At least from the practical point of view, the above hypotheses seem quite realistic.

Our strategy is to use first the conditional regularity results available for the compressible Navier–Stokes system to conclude that the above hypotheses impose bounds on the derivatives of the observed solution, in particular, it is a strong solution in the whole time interval
(T^{-},T^{+})
. The second step is to use the relative energy inequality to estimate the distance between the observed and synchronized solution in the interval
(0,T^{+})
.

In contrast with the observed solution, the synchronized solution,
obtained in practice by means of a numerical simulation, will be understood in a general sense that goes beyond the standard framework of weak solutions. We consider
the so-called dissipative solution of the Navier–Stokes system augmented by
nudging terms in the data assimilation period
(0,T)
.
This type of solution, introduced in
[
2
]
or
[
14
, Chapter 5]
, is a natural limit of consistent numerical approximations.
The theory of dissipative solutions was developed in
[
14
, Chapter 5]
with the principal aim to extend the celebrated
Lax Equivalence Theorem

[
17
]

\mbox{stability}+\mbox{consistency}\ \Leftrightarrow\ \mbox{convergence}

to a class
of nonlinear systems arising in fluid dynamics.

The desired proximity of the two solution in the prediction period
(T,T^{+})
is achieved by careful tuning the nudging terms. We consider quite realistic data approximation based on collecting the exact values of the observed solution at a specific points of the space-time, in particular the network of the observation points may be time–dependent.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we formulate the problem and state our main results. In Section
3
,
we recall the basic properties of strong and weak solutions to the barotropic Navier–Stokes system, in particular the conditional regularity estimates and the relative energy inequality. Section
4

is the heart of the paper, here we establish the estimates on the distance between the observed and synchronized solution in the
data assimilation period. Finally, in Section
5
, we show the observed and synchronized solution remain close in the prediction period
(T,T^{+})
.

2
Problem formulation and the main result

The barotropic
Navier-Stokes system
governs the time evolution of the mass density
r=r(t,x)
and the velocity
{\bf U}={\bf U}(t,x)
a compressible viscous fluid:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}r+{\rm div}_{x}(r{\bf U})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(r{\bf U})+{\rm div}_{x}(r{\bf U}\otimes{\bf U})+\nabla_{x}p(r)

\displaystyle={\rm div}_{x}\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf U})+r{\bf g},

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}

\displaystyle=\mu\left(\nabla_{x}{\bf U}+\nabla_{x}^{t}{\bf U}-\frac{2}{3}{\rm div}_{x}{\bf U}\mathbb{I}\right)+\lambda{\rm div}_{x}{\bf U}\mathbb{I},\ \mu>0,\ \mu+\lambda\geq 0,

(2.1)

confined to a bounded spatial domain
\Omega\subset R^{3}
. For the sake of simplicity, we consider the no-slip boundary conditions for the velocity

{\bf U}|_{\partial\Omega}=0,

(2.2)

noting that our approach can be adapted to the more realistic inflow–outflow boundary conditions in a straightforward manner, see
the monograph
[
15
]
. The motion is activated by an unspecified driving force
{\bf g}={\bf g}(t,x)
representing the effect of the “outer world”.

It is known that the associated initial-boundary value problem admits a global in time
weak
solution as long as the pressure
p(r)\approx r^{\gamma}
, with
\gamma>\frac{3}{2}
,
see
[
12
]
, Lions
[
19
]
. We consider the observed solution
(r,{\bf U})
solving the problem (
2.1
), (
2.2
) in the observation period
(T^{-},T^{+})
.

-\infty<T^{-}<0<T^{+}<\infty.

(2.3)

2.1
Data sampling – interpolation operators

Fixing the data assimilation period
[0,T]
,
0<T<T^{+}
, we introduce the interpolation operators
I_{\delta}
. For each
\delta>0
, we consider a decomposition
of the space–time cylinder:

\displaystyle[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}=\cup_{i=1}^{N(\delta)}\overline{Q}_{i,\delta},\ Q_{i,\delta}\cap Q_{j,\delta}=\emptyset\ \mbox{for}\ i\neq j,\ {\rm diam}[Q_{i,\delta}]\leq\delta,

(2.4)

where
Q_{i,\delta}
are domains,
together with the control points

y_{i,\delta}=(t_{i,\delta},x_{i,\delta})\in Q_{i,\delta},\ i=1,\dots,N(\delta).

(2.5)

Given a continuous function
\theta\in C([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega})
, we set

I_{\delta}[\theta]=\sum_{i=1}^{N(\delta)}\theta(y_{i,\delta})\mathds{1}_{Q_{i,\delta}}\in L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega).

(2.6)

2.2
Synchronized system - data assimilation

The synchronized system is defined in the time interval
(0,T^{+})
, where the time interval
(T,T^{+})
is called prediction period.
It is the barotropic Navier–Stokes system (
2.1
) perturbed by nudging terms active in the data assimilation period
(0,T)
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varrho+{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho{\bf u})

\displaystyle=-\Lambda_{\varrho}(\varrho-I_{\delta}[r])\mathds{1}_{(0,T)},

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\varrho{\bf u})+{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho{\bf u}\otimes{\bf u})+\nabla_{x}p(\varrho)

\displaystyle={\rm div}_{x}\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u})+\varrho{\bf g}-\Lambda_{\bf u}(1+\varrho)({\bf u}-I_{\delta}[{\bf U}])\mathds{1}_{(0,T)}

(2.7)

in
(0,T^{+})\times\Omega
, with the no–slip boundary conditions

{\bf u}|_{\partial\Omega}=0.

(2.8)

The initial conditions for the synchronized system can be taken arbitrarily. For definiteness and also simplicity, we consider

\varrho(0,\cdot)=\varrho_{0}=\fint_{\Omega}r(T^{-},\cdot)\,{\rm d}{x},\ {\bf m}_{0}=\varrho{\bf u}(0,\cdot)=0

(2.9)

bearing in mind that the total mass of the observed solution,

M_{0}=\int_{\Omega}r(t,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x},

is a constant of motion.
Here
\Lambda_{\varrho},\Lambda_{\bf u}>0
are a positive relaxation parameters to be fixed below.

Unlike the “standard” nudging
I_{\delta}[\varrho]-I_{\delta}[r]
,

I_{\delta}[{\bf u}]-\mathbb{I}_{\delta}[{\bf U}]
, the interpolation operators in (
2.7
) act only on the observed solution. There are essentially two reasons for this strategy. The first one, rather obvious, is the low regularity of the synchronized solution for the interpolation
I_{\delta}
to be well defined. The second, more subtle, is to compensate the lack of dissipation provided by the physical model in particular at the level of the mass density.

We consider the so-called dissipative solutions of the synchronized problem
proposed in
[
2
]
. First, let us introduce the total energy associated to the compressible Navier–Stokes system,

E(\varrho,{\bf m})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\frac{1}{2}\frac{|{\bf m}|^{2}}{\varrho}+P(\varrho)\ \mbox{if}\ \varrho>0,\\
\\
0\ \mbox{if}\ \varrho=0,{\bf m}=0,\\
\\
\infty\ \mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.,\ \mbox{where}\ P^{\prime}(\varrho)\varrho-P(\varrho)=p(\varrho),\ {\bf m}=\varrho{\bf u}.

(2.10)

It is easy to check that
E:R^{4}\to[0,\infty)
is a convex l.s.c function as soon as

p^{\prime}(\varrho)>0\ \mbox{for all}\ \varrho>0.

(2.11)

In addition, we require

p(0)=P(0)=0,\ \varrho\mapsto p(\varrho)\ \mbox{convex},\ \varrho\mapsto P(\varrho)-ap(\varrho)\ \mbox{convex for a positive constant}\ a>0.

(2.12)

It is easy to check that hypothesis (
2.12
) is satisfied if
p\in C^{2}(0,\infty)
, and

p^{\prime}(\varrho)\geq ap^{\prime\prime}(\varrho)\varrho>0\ \mbox{for all}\ \varrho>0.

Definition 2.1

(
Dissipative solution).

We say the
(\varrho,{\bf u})
is
dissipative solution
to the synchronized problem (
2.7
)–(
2.9
) in

(0,T^{+})\times\Omega
if the following holds true:

•

Regularity.
The density
\varrho\in C_{\rm weak}([0,T^{+}];L^{\gamma}(\Omega))
for a certain
\gamma>1
, the velocity
{\bf u}\in L^{2}(0,T^{+};W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega;R^{3}))
, the
momentum
{\bf m}=\varrho{\bf u}\in C_{\rm weak}([0,T^{+}];L^{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}}(\Omega;R^{3}))
, the total energy

E(\varrho,{\bf m})
belongs to
L^{\infty}(0,T^{+};L^{1}(\Omega))
.

•

Synchronized equation of continuity.
The integral identity

\int_{0}^{T^{+}}\int_{\Omega}\Big[\varrho\partial_{t}\varphi+\varrho{\bf u}\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi\Big]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t=\Lambda_{\varrho}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\varrho-I_{\delta}[r])\varphi\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t-\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{0}\varphi(0,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(2.13)

holds for any
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}([0,T^{+})\times\overline{\Omega})
.

•

Synchronized momentum equation.
The integral identity

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T^{+}}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Big[\varrho{\bf u}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\varrho({\bf u}\otimes{\bf u}):\nabla_{x}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+p(\varrho){\rm div}_{x}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\Big]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T^{+}}\int_{\Omega}\Big[\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}):\nabla_{x}\boldsymbol{\varphi}-\varrho{\bf g}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}\Big]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t-\int_{0}^{T^{+}}\left(\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{x}\boldsymbol{\varphi}:{\rm d}\mathcal{R}\right)\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+\Lambda_{\bf u}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(1+\varrho)({\bf u}-I_{\delta}[{\bf U}])\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t-\int_{\Omega}{\bf m}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}(0,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(2.14)

holds for any
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in C^{1}_{c}([0,T^{+})\times\Omega)
, where
\mathcal{R}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathcal{M}^{+}(\overline{\Omega};R^{3}))
.

•

Energy inequality.

The integral inequality

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T^{+}}\partial_{t}\psi\left[\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho,\varrho{\bf u})\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t+a\int_{\overline{\Omega}}{\rm d}\ {\rm trace}[\mathcal{R}]\right]+\int_{0}^{T^{+}}\psi\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}):\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+\Lambda_{\bf u}\int_{0}^{T}\psi\int_{\Omega}|{\bf u}|^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t+(\Lambda_{\bf u}-\Lambda_{\varrho})\int_{0}^{T}\psi\int_{\Omega}\varrho|{\bf u}|^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t+\frac{1}{2}\Lambda_{\varrho}\int_{0}^{T}\psi\int_{\Omega}I_{\delta}[r]|{\bf u}|^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\Lambda_{\varrho}\int_{0}^{T}\psi\int_{\Omega}\varrho|{\bf u}|^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t+\Lambda_{\varrho}\int_{0}^{T}\psi\int_{\Omega}\Big(P(\varrho)-P(I_{\delta}[r])\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+a\Lambda_{r}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\overline{\Omega}}{\rm d}\ {\rm trace}[\mathcal{R}]\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T^{+}}\psi\int_{\Omega}\varrho{\bf g}\cdot{\bf u}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t+\Lambda_{\bf u}\int_{0}^{T}\psi\int_{\Omega}(1+\varrho)I_{\delta}[{\bf U}]\cdot{\bf u}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t+\psi(0)\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0})\ \,{\rm d}{x}.

(2.15)

holds for any
\psi\in C^{1}_{c}[0,T^{+})
,
\psi\geq 0
.

The specific form of the energy inequality (
2.15
) deserves some comments. Formally, the
energy balance is derived by multiplying the momentum equation in (
2.7
) on
{\bf u}
and using the equation of continuity:

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{\,{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho,\varrho{\bf u})\ \,{\rm d}{x}+\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}):\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle+\mathds{1}_{(0,T)}\Lambda_{\varrho}\int_{\Omega}P^{\prime}(\varrho)(\varrho-I_{\delta}[r])\ \,{\rm d}{x}+\mathds{1}_{(0,T)}\frac{\Lambda_{\varrho}}{2}\int_{\Omega}I_{\delta}[r]|{\bf u}|^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle+\mathds{1}_{(0,T)}\Lambda_{\bf u}\int_{\Omega}|{\bf u}|^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x}+\mathds{1}_{(0,T)}(\Lambda_{\bf u}-\Lambda_{\varrho})\int_{\Omega}\varrho|{\bf u}|^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x}+\frac{\Lambda_{\varrho}}{2}\mathds{1}_{(0,T)}\int_{\Omega}\varrho|{\bf u}|^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\varrho{\bf g}\cdot{\bf u}\ \,{\rm d}{x}+\mathds{1}_{(0,T)}\Lambda_{\bf u}\int_{\Omega}(1+\varrho)I_{\delta}[{\bf U}]\cdot{\bf u}\ \,{\rm d}{x}.

Moreover, by virtue of Fenchel–Young inequality,

P^{\prime}(\varrho)\varrho-P^{\prime}(\varrho)I_{\delta}[r]=P(\varrho)+P^{*}(P^{\prime}(\varrho))-P^{\prime}(\varrho)I_{\delta}[r]\geq P(\varrho)-P(I_{\delta}[r]).

where
P^{*}
denotes the conjugate of
P
. Consequently, we get

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{\,{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho,\varrho{\bf u})\ \,{\rm d}{x}+\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}):\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}\ \,{\rm d}{x}+\mathds{1}_{(0,T)}\frac{\Lambda_{\varrho}}{2}\int_{\Omega}I_{\delta}[r]|{\bf u}|^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle+\mathds{1}_{(0,T)}\Lambda_{\bf u}\int_{\Omega}|{\bf u}|^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x}+\mathds{1}_{(0,T)}(\Lambda_{\bf u}-\Lambda_{\varrho})\int_{\Omega}\varrho|{\bf u}|^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle+\mathds{1}_{(0,T)}\frac{\Lambda_{\varrho}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\varrho|{\bf u}|^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x}+\mathds{1}_{(0,T)}\Lambda_{\varrho}\int_{\Omega}\Big(P(\varrho)-P(I_{\delta}[r])\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\varrho{\bf g}\cdot{\bf u}\ \,{\rm d}{x}+\mathds{1}_{(0,T)}\Lambda_{\bf u}\int_{\Omega}(1+\varrho)I_{\delta}[{\bf U}]\cdot{\bf u}\ \,{\rm d}{x},

(2.16)

which is identical with (
2.15
) except the integrals containing
{\rm trace}[\mathcal{R}]
.

The concept of dissipative solution anticipates possible oscillations/concentrations that may arise in a sequence of numerical solutions.
They are encoded in the quantity
\mathcal{R}
usually termed Reynolds stress,

\mathcal{R}=\mbox{weak - (*) limit }\Big(\frac{{\bf m}_{n}\otimes{\bf m}_{n}}{\varrho_{n}}+p(\varrho_{n})\Big)-\Big(\frac{{\bf m}\otimes{\bf m}}{\varrho}+p(\varrho)\Big)

for any approximate sequence
{\bf m}_{n}=\varrho_{n}{\bf u}_{n}
,
\varrho_{n}
converging weakly to
{\bf m}
,
\varrho
, respectively.
The same effect is observed for the total energy, where

\mathcal{C}=\mbox{weak - (*) limit }\Big(\frac{1}{2}\frac{|{\bf m}_{n}|^{2}}{\varrho_{n}}+P(\varrho_{n})\Big)-\Big(\frac{1}{2}\frac{|{\bf m}|^{2}}{\varrho}+P(\varrho)\Big).

Without loss of generality, we may assume the constant
a
in hypothesis (
2.12
) satisfies
0<a<\frac{1}{2}
. It follows

\mathcal{C}\geq a\ {\rm trace}[\mathcal{R}].

Finally, we may modify the Reynolds stress by adding a spatially homogenous function
\mathcal{R}(t,\cdot)+\chi(t)\mathbb{I}
obtaining

\int_{\overline{\Omega}}{\rm d}\mathcal{C}=a\int_{\overline{\Omega}}{\rm d}\ {\rm trace}[\mathcal{R}].

Incorporating the oscillation defect into (
2.16
) we get (
2.15
).

Obviously, any dissipative solution is a weak solution of the synchronized system (
2.7
)–(
2.9
) as soon as
\mathcal{R}=0
. The existence of
global in time
weak
solutions can be shown by the method developed in
[
12
]
,
[
19
]
as soon as the pressure satisfies an addition growth condition

p(\varrho)\geq\underline{p}\varrho^{\gamma}>0\ \mbox{for all}\ \varrho\geq 1\ \mbox{and}\ \gamma>\frac{3}{2}.

(2.17)

The dissipative solutions exist globally in time for the full range
\gamma>1
, cf.
[
14
, Chapter 5]
. Moreover, as shown in

[
14
, Chapter 7]
, they arise as weak limits of certain consistent numerical approximations.

2.3
Main result

We consider the relative energy

E\left(\varrho,{\bf u}\Big|\widetilde{\varrho},{\widetilde{\bf u}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\varrho|{\bf u}-{\widetilde{\bf u}}|^{2}+P(\varrho)-P^{\prime}(\widetilde{\varrho})(\varrho-\widetilde{\varrho})-P(\widetilde{\varrho}).

(2.18)

As shown in
[
13
]
, see also a more elaborated treatment in

[
14
, Chapter 4]
, the relative energy can be interpreted as a generalized distance - the so-called Bregmann divergence - between the quantities
(\varrho,{\bf u})
and
(\widetilde{\varrho},{\widetilde{\bf u}})
.

Before stating our main result, we introduce the “data” of the problem. We suppose the observed solution
(r,{\bf U})
is bounded on the whole time interval
(T^{-},T^{+})
, specifically

0\leq r(t,x)\leq\overline{r},\ |{\bf U}(t,x)|\leq\overline{U}\ \mbox{for}\ x\in\overline{\Omega}\ \mbox{uniformly for}\ T^{-}<t<T^{+}.

(2.19)

In addition, we assume

\|r(T^{-},\cdot)\|_{W^{1,q}(\Omega)}<\infty,\ r(T^{-},\cdot)>0\ \mbox{in}\ \overline{\Omega},\ \|{\bf U}(T^{-},\cdot)\|_{W^{2(1-\frac{1}{q}),q}(\Omega)}<\infty

(2.20)

for a certain
q>3
specified below,
and set

D_{0}=\inf_{t\in[T^{-},0]}\left(\|r(t,\cdot)\|_{W^{1,q}(\Omega)}+\|r^{-1}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\|{\bf U}(t,\cdot)\|_{W^{2(1-\frac{1}{q}),q}(\Omega)}\right)<\infty.

Finally, we denote

\overline{g}={\rm ess}\sup_{(t,x)(T^{-},T^{+})\times\Omega}|{\bf g}(t,x)|,

and define

\|{\rm data}\|=D_{0}+\overline{r}+\overline{U}+\overline{g}+(T^{+}-T^{-}).

We are ready to state our main result.

Theorem 2.2

(
Synchronization for the compressible Navier-Stokes system).

Let
\Omega\subset R^{3}
be a bounded domain of class
C^{4}
. Let the pressure–density equation of state
p=p(\varrho)
satisfy
the hypotheses (
2.11
), (
2.12
), together with the growth restriction (
2.17
), where
\gamma>\frac{6}{5}
.
Suppose the observed solution
(r,{\bf U})
belongs to the regularity class
(
2.19
), (
2.20
), with

q>\max\left\{12;\frac{6\gamma}{5\gamma-6}\right\}.

Let

(\varrho,{\bf u})
be a dissipative solution of the synchronized system (
2.7
)–(
2.9
) in the sense of Definition
2.1
.

Then there is a non–decreasing function
\Gamma:(0,\infty)\to[1,\infty)
such that

{\rm ess}\sup_{t\in[T,T^{+}]}\left[\int_{\Omega}E\left(\varrho,{\bf u}\Big|r,{\bf U}\right)(t,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}+a\int_{\overline{\Omega}}{\rm d}{\rm trace}[\mathcal{R}](t)\right]<\varepsilon

(2.21)

whenever

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{\Lambda_{\varrho}}+\exp\left(-{\Lambda_{\varrho}}T\right)\right]\Gamma(\|{\rm data}\|)

\displaystyle<\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\bf u}

\displaystyle\geq\Gamma(\|{\rm data}\|)\Lambda_{\varrho},

\displaystyle\delta\Lambda_{\bf u}\Gamma(\|{\rm data}\|)

\displaystyle\leq 1.

(2.22)

Remark 2.3
.

The restriction
\gamma>\frac{6}{5}
can be replaced by

if the values of the observed solution

,

This point is clearly indicated in the proof below, cf. Remarks

,

.

. We use the symbol
\Gamma
to denote a generic non–decreasing function ranging in

.

3
Preliminary results concerning the observed and synchronized system

We recall some known facts concerning the properties of the observed and synchronized system.

3.1
Conditional regularity for the observed system

In their seminal work, Sun, Wang, and Zhang
[
20
]
showed that any weak solution of the barotropic Navier–Stokes system emanating from the
smooth initial data and with uniformly bounded density is in fact regular. Here, we report an
L^{q}
variant of their result proved in
[
1
, Theorem 7.1]
.

Proposition 3.1

(
Conditional regularity).

For any
3<q<\infty
, the observed solution
(r,{\bf U})

belongs to the class

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle\in C([T^{-},T^{+}];W^{1,q}(\Omega)),\ \partial_{t}\varrho\in C([T^{-},T^{+}];L^{q}(\Omega)),\ \inf_{[T^{-},T^{+}]\times\overline{\Omega}}r>0,

\displaystyle{\bf U}

\displaystyle\in L^{q}(T^{-},T^{+};W^{2,q}\cap W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega;R^{3})),\ \partial_{t}{\bf U}\in L^{q}(T^{-};T^{+};L^{q}(\Omega;R^{3})),

and the following estimate holds true:

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[T^{-},T^{+}]}

\displaystyle\|r(t,\cdot)\|_{W^{1,q}(\Omega)}+\sup_{t\in[T^{-},T^{+}]}\|\partial_{t}r(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}+\|r^{-1}\|_{L^{\infty}((T^{-},T^{+})\times\Omega)}

\displaystyle+\int_{T^{-}}^{T^{+}}\|{\bf U}(t,\cdot)\|^{q}_{W^{2,q}(\Omega;R^{3})}\,{\rm d}t+\int_{T^{-}}^{T^{+}}\|\partial_{t}{\bf U}(t,\cdot)\|^{q}_{L^{q}(\Omega;R^{3})}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\quad\leq\Gamma\Big(\|{\rm data}\|\Big),

(3.2)

where
\Gamma
is a non–decreasing function.

Strictly speaking, the result proved in
[
1
]
applies to the full Navier–Stoke–Fourier system. The present version for the barotropic case is a straightforward consequence.

3.2
Relative energy inequality for the synchronized system

The relative energy inequality can be deduced in the way proposed in
[
13
]
.
