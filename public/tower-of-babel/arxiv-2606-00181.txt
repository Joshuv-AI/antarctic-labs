Infinite-Dimensional Spherical Kernel ridge Regression
Beatrice Matteo, Almond Stöcker, Shahin Tavakoli

Abstract
We introduce a novel regression framework designed to model non-linear responses situated on a sphere 𝕊 \mathbb{S} of finite or infinite dimension. Unlike traditional tangent-space regressions, which lift responses to a tangent space T 𝔬 ​ 𝕊 T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S} and thereby violate intrinsic spherical distances, our proposed method employs an intrinsic approach. We model the conditional mean through an intercept 𝔬 ∈ 𝕊 \mathfrak{o}\in\mathbb{S} and a linear predictor function f : 𝔛 → T 𝔬 ​ 𝕊 f:\mathfrak{X}\to T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S} . This formulation transforms the estimation problem into finding a linear predictor within a function space, but utilizing a metric defined by spherical geometry rather than standard Euclidean distance. Leveraging vector-valued reproducing kernel Hilbert space theory, our approach reduces the infinite-dimensional estimation challenge to a manageable finite-dimensional problem via the representer theorem, leading to an efficient BFGS-based estimation algorithm. We establish convergence rates and analyze the finite-sample behavior of our estimator, concluding with a practical application to density regression. The full implementation is available in R.

Introduction

The problem of regression or statistical learning, which consists of modelling the dependency between a covariate or feature
X
and a response
Y
is a central problem in statistics and machine learning, and its origins can be traced back to Newton, Legendre, Gauss and Galton. In its simplest form, both
X
and
Y
are scalars, the dependency between them is considered to be linear subject to an error term,
Y=\beta_{0}+X\beta+\varepsilon
. This model has been extended in various directions, by considering multivariate or even infinite-dimensional covariates and/or responses, and also nonlinear dependencies, such as through generalized linear models
(
62
)
, generalized additive models
(
100
)
, reproducing kernel Hilbert space methods
(
6
;
71
;
82
)
, and machine learning methods
(
68
)
. In most of these extensions, covariates and responses lie in an Euclidean or linear space, where addition and taking averages are defined and have a natural meaning. This linear structure underpins most standard regression models. However some regression problems do not fall in this scenario: these include data that are probability distributions
(
4
, such as in compositional data analysis or distribution-valued responses;)
, directional or circular data
(
58
, e.g., wind directions;)
, shape data
(
52
;
84
)
, network data
(
105
)
, or topological data
(
19
, such as persistence diagrams;)
.

Another axis of complexity in regression is the dimension of the responses or covariates. In many modern problems, these are either high-dimensional data
(
20
, e.g., about 10,000 genes’ expression can be measured in gene expression datasets;)
or functional data
(
79
;
96
, that is, data points that are infinite-dimensional but smooth, such as in gait analysis;)
.

1.1
Metric spaces regression

In many situations, data can be viewed as points on a metric space.
Regression for data in a metric spaces
(\mathcal{M},\mathrm{d})
, also known as Fréchet regression, can be traced back to
36
. In this setting, Fréchet introduced the “average position”—now commonly referred to as
Fréchet mean
—of a random variable
Y\in\mathcal{M}
as a minimizer of
\Expected\mathrm{d}^{2}(Y,m)
over
m\in\mathcal{M}
, and noticed that this minimizer coincides with the
\Expected(Y)
if
\mathcal{M}
is a Euclidean space. This unconditional model corresponds to the constant covariate case, i.e., an intercept-only regression model, and was extended by
75
to include Euclidean covariates
X
by modelling the conditional Fréchet mean of
Y
given
X=x
, i.e.,
m(x)=\arg\min_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\Expected(d^{2}(Y,m)\mid X=x)
, and many extensions have been proposed
(see
25
;
37
;
78
, for some recent ones)
.

1.2
Manifold regression

In some settings, data lie in a space with some geometric structure, such as a Riemannian manifold structure. In these cases, the geometry of the space allows for more refined algorithms
(
14
)
and more interpretable assumptions for the theoretical guarantees. The unconditional Fréchet mean of
Y

\mathcal{M}
was studied initially by

12
;
13
;
72

(where the data are lifted to a common tangent space and then fitted on the latter (linear) space;

29

A more recent approach is geodesic regression
(
34

, where the conditional Fréchet mean of
Y
given

is modelled as
\Exp_{p}(xv)

(

53

model the conditional mean as
\Exp_{p}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}v_{i})

\Exp_{p}

(
28

.

1.3
Infinite-dimensional manifolds

Most of the existing works focus on finite-dimensional manifold. Moving to responses on infinite-dimensional manifolds, modelled on a Hilbert space, brings other levels of complexity, in particular for the theory. Indeed, with such nonlinear problems, the estimators of interest are not known in closed form, and empirical process techniques must then be used. In most cases, these boil down to showing a quadratic growth of the population risk, and a control of the complexity of the problem, for instance through bounds on covering numbers. The latter, however, often implicitly restrict the dimension of the manifold to be finite
(
26
, see, e.g., Assumption (M2) in)
. The infinite-dimensional case is nevertheless needed in applications, such as when modelling probability density functions (see Section
7
) or elastic shapes
(
84
)
.

(
66

, such as curves in

Beyond linear response spaces,

Contributions

This paper considers the regression problem at the interface of manifold regression and functional data analysis, where the response
Y
belongs to a sphere of arbitrary dimension (including the infinite-dimensional sphere in an abstract Hilbert space), and the covariate

We provide an effective algorithm for model fitting, combining BFGS and a dual low-rank approximation (Section
5

5.

We apply our method to a density regression data problem, showcasing our approach as an attractive solution to a class of imbalanced design problems, and illustrating its competitive performance (Section

We provide a ready-to-use R package

Mathematical Preliminaries and Notation

2.1
The sphere as a Riemannian manifold

Let
\mathfrak{Y}
be a real separable Hilbert space with inner-product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}
and induced norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}
. We denote by
\mathcal{L}(\mathfrak{Y})
the space of bounded linear operators on
\mathfrak{Y}
, by
S^{\dagger}
the adjoint operator of
S\in\mathcal{L}(\mathfrak{Y})
.
For
y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathfrak{Y}
, let
y_{1}\otimes y_{2}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathfrak{Y})
be the bounded linear map defined by
(y_{1}\otimes y_{2})(y)=\langle y,y_{1}\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}y_{2}
. Denote by
\mathbb{S}=\{y\in\mathfrak{Y}\mid\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}=1\}\subset\mathfrak{Y}
the unit sphere in
\mathfrak{Y}
. The unit sphere
\mathbb{S}
has a natural Riemannian submanifold structure induced by the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}

(
54
)
. At any point
p\in\mathbb{S}
, the tangent space
T_{p}\mathbb{S}
is given by
T_{p}\mathbb{S}=\{v\in\mathfrak{Y}\mid\langle v,p\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}=0\}
and the Riemannian log map
\Log_{p}:\mathbb{S}\setminus\{-p\}\to T_{p}\mathbb{S}
is defined by

\Log_{p}(q)=\arccos(\langle p,q\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}})\frac{{\mathcal{P}}_{p}(q-p)}{\left\lVert{\mathcal{P}}_{p}(q-p)\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}}=\arccos(\langle p,q\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}})\frac{{\mathcal{P}}_{p}q}{\left\lVert{\mathcal{P}}_{p}q\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}},

where
{\mathcal{P}}_{p}=\mathrm{Id}-p\otimes p:\mathfrak{Y}\to\mathfrak{Y}
is the orthogonal projector with null space
\vspan(p)
, and
\mathrm{Id}
is the identity operator on
\mathfrak{Y}
. The exponential map at
p\in\mathbb{S}
is the
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
map
\Exp_{p}:T_{p}\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{S}
defined by

\Exp_{p}(v)=\cos\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}\;\cdot p+\sin\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}\;\cdot\frac{v}{\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}},\quad v\in T_{p}\mathbb{S},

with the convention
\Exp_{p}(0)=p
. Restricted to the open ball in
T_{p}\mathbb{S}
of radius
\pi
centered at the origin,
\Exp_{p}
is injective, with image
\mathbb{S}\setminus\{-p\}
, and its inverse
\Log_{p}
is
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
on
\mathbb{S}\setminus\{-p\}
.
The geodesic distance between two points
p,q\in\mathbb{S}
on the unit sphere is
\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}(p,q)=\arccos(\langle p,q\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}})
, which is equal to
\left\lVert\Log_{p}(q)\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}
provided
q\neq-p
, and corresponds to the angle between
p
and
q
. Since
T_{p}\mathbb{S}
is a Hilbert subspace of
\mathfrak{Y}
, we denote the norm on
T_{p}\mathbb{S}
by
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}
and its inner-product by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}
regardless of
p
, by slight abuse of notation.

When there is no risk of confusion, we shall write
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert
instead of

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}
and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}
to alleviate notation.

2.2
Reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces

A real Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
of functions from an arbitrary space
\mathfrak{X}
to
\mathbb{R}
is a (scalar)
reproducing kernel Hilbert space
(RKHS) if the pointwise evaluation operators
\mathcal{E}_{x}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
\mathcal{E}_{x}f=f(x)
are continuous operators for all
x\in\mathfrak{X}
. For each
x\in\mathfrak{X}
, the Riesz representation theorem implies the existence of
k_{x}\in\mathcal{H}
such that

\langle k_{x},f\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=f(x),

which is known as the
reproducing property
. The kernel of the RKHS is
k(x,\check{x})=\mathcal{E}_{x}\mathcal{E}_{\check{x}}^{\dagger}=k_{\check{x}}(x)\in\mathbb{R}
, in other words we can identify
k_{\check{x}}=k(\cdot,\check{x})
.

We sometimes write
\mathcal{H}_{k}
instead of
\mathcal{H}
to highlight the link to the kernel
k
. If
\mathfrak{X}
is a separable topological space and the kernel
k
is continuous on
\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}
then
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is a separable Hilbert space
(
85
, Lemma 4.33)
.
A succinct treatment of RKHS is given in
71
.

2.3
Vector-valued reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces

A Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
of functions from an arbitrary space
\mathfrak{X}
to a Hilbert space
\mathfrak{Y}
is a
vector-valued reproducing kernel Hilbert space
(VVRKHS) if the pointwise evaluation operators
\mathcal{E}_{x}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathfrak{Y}
defined by
\mathcal{E}_{x}f=f(x)
are continuous operators for all
x\in\mathfrak{X}
. The operator-kernel of the VVRKHS is
K(x,\check{x})=\mathcal{E}_{x}\mathcal{E}_{\check{x}}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathfrak{Y})
. We often use the notation
K_{x}:=\mathcal{E}_{x}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{H}
, which implies
K_{x}(x^{\prime})=K(x^{\prime},x)
for
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{X}
. The reproducing property is

\langle y,f(x)\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}=\langle K_{x}y,f\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\quad\forall x\in\mathfrak{X},y\in\mathfrak{Y},f\in\mathcal{H}.

A VVRKHS is uniquely defined by its operator-kernel
K
. We shall use in this paper
simple multiplicative operator-kernels
(hereafter
SMO kernels
; these are special cases of
separable
kernels) that are of the form
K(x,x^{\prime})=k(x,x^{\prime})\mathrm{Id}
, where
k
is a (scalar) RKHS kernel function on
\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}
, and
\mathrm{Id}
is the identity operator on
\mathfrak{Y}
. For SMO kernels, we have that
\mathcal{H}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{H}_{k}\otimes\mathfrak{Y}
, the Hilbert space tensor product between the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{k}
(associated with the scalar kernel
k
) and
\mathfrak{Y}
. If
\mathfrak{X}
is a separable topological space,
k
is continuous and
\mathfrak{Y}
is a separable Hilbert space then
\mathcal{H}
is a separable VVRKHS.
Note that if
\mathfrak{Y}=\mathbb{R}
then the VVRKHS is a (scalar) RKHS.
A succinct treatment of VVRKHS is given in
71
.

2.4
Notation

Throughout this paper, we employ standard asymptotic notation to characterize the limiting behavior of sequences as
n\to\infty
. Let
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be two sequences of real numbers, where
b_{n}\neq 0
for all sufficiently large
n
. We write
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
if there exists a universal constant
C>0
such that
|a_{n}|\leq C|b_{n}|
for all sufficiently large
n
, and
a_{n}\asymp b_{n}
if both
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
and
b_{n}\lesssim a_{n}
hold.

We denote the underlying probability space by
(\Omega,\mathcal{B},\theLaw)
and the expectation operator by
\Expected
. For random elements
Z
we denote its induced probability measure by
\theLaw_{Z}
.
We write
Z\sim_{\text{d}}F
to mean that
Z
follows the distribution
F
. For
(Z_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of random variables and
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
a sequence of strictly positive real numbers. We write
Z_{n}=O_{\theLaw}(a_{n})
if the sequence
(Z_{n}/a_{n})
is stochastically bounded, that is, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
M>0
and an integer
N
such that
{\theLaw}(|Z_{n}|/a_{n}>M)<\varepsilon
for all
n\geq N
. We denote the outer-probability counterpart of
O_{\theLaw}(a_{n})
by
O_{{\theLaw}^{*}}(a_{n})
, which is useful in contexts where measurability is not guaranteed—such as with suprema of empirical processes over general function classes.

For a Fréchet differentiable function
f:H\to\mathbb{R}
defined on a Hilbert space
H
, we denote the gradient of
f
at
x
by
\nabla f(x)\in H
.
For a metric space
(\mathcal{M},d_{\mathcal{M}})
, the closed ball with center
o\in\mathcal{M}
and radius
r>0
is denoted by
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{M}}(o,r)=\{x\in\mathcal{M}\mid d_{\mathcal{M}}(o,x)\leq r\}
.

3
The Sphere-on-Hilbert regression problem

Let
\mathfrak{X}
be a Polish space.
Assume
(X,Y)\in\mathfrak{X}\times\mathbb{S}
is a random element of the product space
\mathfrak{X}\times\mathbb{S}
(equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra) with joint distribution
\theLaw_{X,Y}
and marginal distributions
\theLaw_{X},\theLaw_{Y}
.
The best predictor of
Y
given
X=x\in\mathfrak{X}
in the least squares sense (with respect to the spherical distance)
is given by the conditional Fréchet mean
(
75
, see, e.g.,)
,

\mu(x)=\arg\min_{q\in\mathbb{S}}\Expected\left(\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}^{2}(q,Y)\mid X=x\right).

(3.1)

The following result shows that
\mu
is well defined under a bounded conditional support condition.
Let
\theLaw_{Y\mid X=x}
be the regular conditional distribution of
Y\mid X=x
, defined for all
x\in A
where
A\subseteq\mathfrak{X}
has
\theLaw_{X}
-measure
1

(
50
, Theorem 5.3)
.

Proposition 3.1
.

If, for each
x\in A
there exist
p_{x}\in\mathbb{S}
and
\eta_{x}>0
such that

\theLaw(\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}(Y,p_{x})\leq\pi/2-\eta_{x}\mid X=x)=1
,
then there is a measurable function
\mu
satisfying (
3.1
)
\theLaw_{X}
-almost everywhere.

A bounded support condition is usually required when considering Fréchet means without parametric assumptions, see
3
.
The goal will be to estimate
\mu
by
\hat{\mu}
, based on an i.i.d. sample
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\theLaw_{X,Y}
, using a regularization approach, and thus minimizing

\hat{\mu}\mapsto\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}^{2}(\hat{\mu}(X_{i}),Y_{i})+J(\hat{\mu}),

where
J(\hat{\mu})
is some measure of complexity of the function
\hat{\mu}
. To make this problem tractable, we linearize it by modelling
\hat{\mu}
on a tangent space.
Assuming that there exists a point
\mathfrak{o}\in\mathbb{S}
such that
\theLaw(\mu(X)\neq-\mathfrak{o})=1
, we define

f_{\mathfrak{o}}(x):=\Log_{\mathfrak{o}}(\mu(x)),\quad x\in\mathfrak{X}.

(3.2)

Since
\mu(X)\neq-\mathfrak{o}
almost surely,
f_{\mathfrak{o}}
is well-defined.
We further assume that
f_{\mathfrak{o}}\in\mathcal{H}
, where
\mathcal{H}
is a Hilbert space of functions
\mathfrak{X}\to T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S}
for which pointwise evaluations are continuous operators. Specifically, we assume the following.

Assumption 3.2
.

f_{\mathfrak{o}}:\mathfrak{X}\to T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S}
belongs to a
VVRKHS
\mathcal{H}
of functions
\mathfrak{X}\to T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S}
with SMO kernel
K(x,\check{x})=k(x,\check{x})\mathrm{Id}\in\mathcal{L}(T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S})
for all
x,\check{x}\in\mathfrak{X}
, where
k
is a scalar kernel and
\mathrm{Id}
is the identity operator on
T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S}
.

In this context, a natural way of quantifying the complexity of
\hat{\mu}=\Exp_{\mathfrak{o}}\circ f
is through the VVRKHS norm
\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}
. This gives the following empirical risk, that we will seek to minimize over
f\in\mathcal{H}
,

\mathfrak{R}_{n}(f,\lambda)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}^{2}(\Exp_{\mathfrak{o}}(f(X_{i})),Y_{i})+\lambda^{2}\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2},

(3.3)

where
\lambda>0
is a regularization parameter.

The empirical risk
\mathfrak{R}_{n}
can be interpreted as a penalized least-squares loss; however the square distance is taken with respect to the sphere’s distance
\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}
. The empirical risk is therefore not a convex function over
\mathcal{H}
since
v\in T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S}\mapsto\Exp_{\mathfrak{o}}(v)
“wraps around” the sphere for
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}>\pi
.

Remark 3.3
(Comparison with tangent-space regression).

Another approach to model the regression of a manifold-valued response
Y\in\mathcal{M}
on covariates
X
is the so-called “tangent-space regression.” In this setting, a base point
p\in\mathcal{M}
is chosen and the conditional mean of the logarithm at
p
is modelled, i.e.,
x\mapsto\Expected[\Log_{p}(Y)\mid X=x]
. Such approaches essentially linearize the regression problem at the point
p
, and then model the problem as a linear one based on the data
(X_{i},\Log_{p}Y_{i})_{i=1,\ldots,n}
.

Impressive theoretical results can be obtained in such settings
(see, e.g.,
26
, who study Hilbert–Schmidt regression between manifolds)
.
However, this strategy comes with several limitations when the ambient geometry is genuinely curved. Most importantly, it targets the conditional mean of
\Log_{p}(Y)
in the Euclidean space
T_{p}\mathcal{M}
, not the conditional Fréchet mean of
Y
with respect to the geodesic distance
\mathrm{d}_{\mathcal{M}}
on
\mathcal{M}
. Equivalently, it is naturally associated with the pull-back discrepancy

\mathrm{d}_{p}(y,y^{\prime}):=\bigl\|\Log_{p}(y)-\Log_{p}(y^{\prime})\bigr\|_{T_{p}\mathbb{S}},

which generally differs from the intrinsic geodesic distance
\mathrm{d}_{\mathcal{M}}(y,y^{\prime})
. As a consequence, the statistical target depends on
p
, and curvature effects can produce a bias between the tangent-space estimator and the intrinsic regression function.

To avoid these geometric distortions, our approach relies solely on the intrinsic metric of
\mathbb{S}
, aligning with the frameworks of
34
and
28
. Instead of pulling back the raw responses
Y_{i}
and suffering curvature-induced bias, we linearize the parameter space itself: we represent the intrinsic conditional Fréchet mean
\mu(x)
in the tangent space at
\mathfrak{o}
, but all fitting errors are computed using the manifold’s geodesic distance.

Remark 3.4
(Types of data on a sphere).

Aside from directional data
(
59
)
or data observed on a sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, spherical data in higher dimensions arise in different contexts, including the following:

1.

In statistical shape analysis
(
31
)
, shape spaces are quotient spaces of (pre-shape) spheres. This renders spherical regression a basis for shape regression, utilized explicitly for instance by
45
.

2.

A spherical approach to compositional data has a long tradition
(
87
;
97
)
, considering square-root proportions
\mathbf{y}=[({c_{j}}/{\sum_{k=0}^{D}c_{l}})^{1/2}]_{j=0}^{D}
as elements of the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{D}=\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{D+1}\mid\lVert\mathbf{y}\rVert=1\}
to account for components
c_{0},\dots,c_{D}
summing to a fixed constant, such as finite probability mass functions. Our setting therefore allows to address regression for compositional data.

3.

Given two probability densities
h_{1},h_{2}
, the spherical distance between their square-root representations
\sqrt{h}_{1},\sqrt{h}_{2}
corresponds to
their distance in the Fisher–Rao metric
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{FR}}

(
80
)
, i.e.,

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{FR}}(h_{1},h_{2})=\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}(\sqrt{h}_{1},\sqrt{h}_{2})
.
This connection was used by
83
for the analysis of densities as data. An appealing feature of the Fisher–Rao metric is its invariance under smooth, monotone reparameterizations of the measurement scale: if
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
have densities

h_{1}
and
h_{2}
, respectively, and

is a monotone diffeomorphism, then the transformed variables

4
Theoretical results

4.1
Representer theorem

Although
f\mapsto\mathfrak{R}_{n}(f,\lambda)
is a continuous function (with respect to
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}
if
\sup_{x\in\mathfrak{X}}k(x,x)<\infty
), the existence of its minimizer over
\mathcal{H}
of is not obvious because the closed balls in
\mathcal{H}
are not compact unless
\mathcal{H}
is finite-dimensional, which is a very special case of the problem considered.
If
\dim(\mathcal{H})=\infty
, since the balls in an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space are not compact, standard compactness arguments do not hold. Furthermore, the empirical risk
\mathfrak{R}_{n}
is not convex over the entire space
\mathcal{H}
, hence the standard argument involving weak compactness of closed balls in Hilbert spaces and convexity of the objective function do not hold.
The following result tells us that the empirical risk minimizer does in fact always exist, and gives a representer theorem for the form of
\hat{f}
. Recall that
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}(X,Y)
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that
Y\neq-\mathfrak{o}
almost surely and
\sup_{x\in\mathfrak{X}}k(x,x)<\infty
, and that

\dim\vspan(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})+n+1<\dim\mathfrak{Y}
.
For any choice of orthogonal vectors
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\in\vspan(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})^{\perp}\cap(\mathbb{S}\setminus\{-\mathfrak{o}\})
, we have

\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\mathfrak{R}_{n}(f,\lambda)=\inf_{f\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}}\mathfrak{R}_{n}(f,\lambda),

(4.1)

where

\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}=\left\{f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{X_{i}}\xi_{i}\mid\xi_{i}\in\vspan\left(\{\Log_{\mathfrak{o}}Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{\Log_{\mathfrak{o}}w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\right)\right\}.

In particular, for
\lambda>0
, the infimum in (
4.1
) is achieved for some
\hat{f}_{n}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
.
If
\hat{f}_{n}
is unique, then

\hat{f}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{X_{i}}\xi_{i}
with

\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\in\vspan\{\Log_{\mathfrak{o}}Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}.

(4.2)

If
\dim\vspan(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})+n+1\geq\dim\mathfrak{Y}
, then we can take
\xi_{i}\in T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S}
in the definition of
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
, and (
4.2
) holds if
\hat{f}_{n}
is unique.

The implications of Theorem
4.1
are manyfold. It shows that the (potentially doubly) infinite-dimensional optimization of
\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\cdot,\lambda)
reduces to a finite-dimensional optimization problem. Indeed, writing

\xi_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\Log_{\mathfrak{o}}Y_{i}+\sum_{j=n+1}^{2n}a_{ij}\Log_{\mathfrak{o}}w_{i}

the optimization over
f\in\mathcal{H}
reduces to an optimization over the coefficients
(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 2n}
(see also Section
5
).

The proof of Theorem
4.1
relies on two ingredients. The first is the group of isometries of the sphere, which implies in particular the minimizer is not always unique (in the same way that the Fréchet mean on the sphere is not always unique). The second ingredient of the proof is a minimal norm interpolant result for VVRKHS, which we now state.

Lemma 4.2
.

Recall that
\mathcal{H}
is a VVRKHS with SMO kernel
K(x,x^{\prime})=k(x,x^{\prime})\mathrm{Id}
.
For any
f\in\mathcal{H}
and any
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathfrak{X}
, there exists an
\tilde{f}\in\mathcal{H}
satisfying

\tilde{f}(x_{i})=f(x_{i}),\;i=1,\ldots,n,\quad\text{and}\quad\lVert\tilde{f}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}.

Furthermore,

\tilde{f}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k(x_{i},\cdot)\xi_{i}\in\mathcal{H}
for some

\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\in\vspan(f(x_{1}),\ldots,f(x_{n})),

Although a proof of this result was given in
64
, we include a corrected argument here, as the original argument contains a gap.
As a side comment, note that Lemma
4.2
directly implies the following representer theorem for the kernel ridge regression problem with linear output
Y\in\mathfrak{Y}
onto
X\in\mathfrak{X}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a VVRKHS of functions
\mathfrak{X}\to\mathfrak{Y}
with SMO kernel
K(x,x^{\prime})=k(x,x^{\prime})\mathrm{Id}
. For any set
\{(x_{i},y_{i}):i=1,\ldots,n\}\subset\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{Y}
and
\lambda>0
, let

L(f)=C(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n},f(x_{1}),\ldots,f(x_{n}))+J(\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}),\quad f\in\mathcal{H},

where
J:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is an increasing function and
C
is some arbitrary cost function.

Then for any
f\in\mathcal{H}
, there exists an
\tilde{f}\in\mathcal{H}
satisfying

L(\tilde{f})\leq L(f)

where

\tilde{f}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k(x_{i},\cdot)\xi_{i}\in\mathcal{H}
for some

\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\in\vspan(f(x_{1}),\ldots,f(x_{n})).

4.2
Convergence rates

The goal of this section will be to study the convergence of a minimizer
\hat{f}_{n}
of
\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\cdot,\lambda_{n})
to
f_{\mathfrak{o}}
, which is a minimizer of the population risk,

\mathfrak{R}(f)=\Expected\mathfrak{R}_{n}(f,0)=\Expected\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}^{2}(\Exp_{\mathfrak{o}}f(X),Y),

(4.3)

see Proposition
3.1
. Theoretical analysis of the convergence is intricate in our setting because of several aspects. Since there is no closed solution for
\hat{f}_{n}
, a natural approach is to use tools from empirical process theory
(
93
)
. Because our empirical risk depends on the squared distance on the sphere, the usual empirical process approach through the “basic inequality” is inapplicable in our case. Finally, our problem is not globally convex. Indeed, the basic term in our empirical risk is the function

\ell_{y}(v):=\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}^{2}(\Exp_{\mathfrak{o}}v,y)=\arccos^{2}\langle\cos\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}\mathfrak{o}+\sin\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}\,\frac{v}{\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}}\;,y\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}},\quad v\in T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S},y\in\mathbb{S},

(4.4)

and for
v\neq 0
fixed, the function
t\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto\ell_{y}(tv)
is
2\pi/\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}
-periodic.

4.2.1
Local convexity

We reduce the study of the convexity of
\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\cdot,\lambda)
to the study of the function
\ell_{y}
. Indeed, the empirical risk can be written as

\mathfrak{R}_{n}(f,\lambda)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{Y_{i}}(f(X_{i}))+\lambda^{2}\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}.

(4.5)

Although we know that the mapping
s\in\mathbb{S}\mapsto\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}^{2}(s,y)
has positive definite Hessian if
\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}(s,y)<\pi/2

(
73
)
, the convexity of
\ell_{y}
does not follow because of the composition
s=\Exp_{\mathfrak{o}}(v)
, which does not map all lines in
T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S}
to geodesics in
\mathbb{S}
. Although the Hessian of
\ell_{y}
is a self-adjoint operator on
\mathfrak{Y}
and thus high-dimensional, it follows from (
4.4
) that it really depends on three parameters:
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}},\langle v,y\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}
and
\langle\mathfrak{o},y\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}
, and we can indeed show that analyzing the eigenvalues of the Hessian of
\ell_{y}
boils down to analyzing the eigenvalues of a
3\times 3
matrix, which yields the following result.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let

\mathfrak{C}:=\mathcal{B}_{T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S}}(0,\pi/4)
.
If
\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathfrak{o},y)\leq\pi/4
,

1.

The minimal eigenvalue of the Hessian of
\ell_{y}
is at least
\epsilon>0
on
\mathfrak{C}
, for some
\epsilon>0
.

2.

\nabla\ell_{y}(v)
is well defined on
\mathfrak{C}
and

\ell_{y}(v^{\prime})\geq\ell_{y}(v)+\langle v^{\prime}-v,\nabla\ell_{y}(v)\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\left\lVert v^{\prime}-v\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}^{2}

In particular,
\ell_{y}
is convex on
\mathfrak{C}
.

The proof of this result is technically challenging, as it requires bounding several functions by polynomials with rational coefficients, and then checking that these polynomials are strictly positive. If one is allows for an argument using numerical evaluations,
\mathfrak{C}
can be extended and the condition
\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathfrak{o},y)\leq\pi/4
can be relaxed. A thorough discussion is given in Section
8
.
As a consequence of Theorem
4.4
, we define the following.

Definition 4.5
.

We define the
deterministic
set
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{H}
by

\mathcal{U}:=\left\{f\in\mathcal{H}\,\middle|\,\begin{array}[]{l}\left\lVert f(X)\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}\leq\pi/4,\quad\mathrm{a.s.}\end{array}\right\}.

If
\sup_{x}k(x,x)<\infty
, Lemma
S2.15
in the Appendix implies that
\mathcal{U}
is non-empty and closed.
We shall rely on the following assumption for our next results.

Assumption 4.6
.

Assume that
\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathfrak{o},Y)\leq\pi/4
almost surely, and that

f_{\mathfrak{o}}\in\mathcal{U}
.

Note that the first part of Assumption
4.6
implies automatically that
\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathfrak{o},\mu(X))\leq\pi/4
almost surely
(
103
, Theorem B and 57)
, and hence
\left\lVert f_{\mathfrak{o}}(X)\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}\leq\pi/4
almost surely. The assumption
f_{\mathfrak{o}}\in\mathcal{U}
is therefore only a smoothness assumption on the conditional mean
\mu
. Although the bounded support assumption
\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathfrak{o},Y)\leq\pi/4
might seem quite restrictive, it is comparable to existing implicit assumptions in related literature on metric space regression or manifold regression
(
75
;
26
)
. Although the latter works operate in a general abstract setting, assumptions about existence and uniqueness of the population risk minimizer are explicitly made, and abstract assumptions about the growth of the population risk are made, see
75
or
26
.

Let
\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\theLaw_{X})}=(\Expected\left\lVert f(X)\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}^{2})^{1/2}
denote the
L^{2}(\theLaw_{X})
norm of a measurable function
f:\mathfrak{X}\to\mathfrak{Y}
.
The following result plays a central role in the proofs of our convergence rates.

Theorem 4.7
.

Under Assumption
4.6
, provided
\sup_{x}k(x,x)<\infty
, there is an
\epsilon>0
such that

1.

For all
f\in\mathcal{U}
,
\mathfrak{R}(f)\geq\mathfrak{R}(f_{\mathfrak{o}})+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\lVert f-f_{\mathfrak{o}}\rVert_{L^{2}(\theLaw_{X})}^{2}
.

2.

For
\lambda_{n}>0
,
\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\cdot,\lambda_{n})
is strictly convex on
\mathcal{U}
, and it admits a unique minimizer
\hat{f}_{n}
over
\mathcal{U}
.

4.2.2
Rates of convergence under minimal assumptions

The following result gives rates of convergence for any Polish covariate space
\mathfrak{X}
(no compactness or boundedness assumptions), no smoothness condition or eigenvalue rates of decay for the integral operator associated to the scalar kernel
k
—see (
4.6
) below. The result also hold for the infinite-dimensional sphere, i.e.,
\dim\mathfrak{Y}=\infty
.

Theorem 4.8
.

Assume
\sup_{x}k(x,x)=c<\infty
. Under Assumption
4.6
, for
\lambda_{n}\asymp n^{-1/4}
,

\lVert\hat{f}_{n}-f_{\mathfrak{o}}\rVert_{L^{2}(\theLaw_{X})}^{2}=O_{\theLaw^{*}}(n^{-1/2})

The rates obtained translate directly into rates for the conditional mean estimator
\hat{\mu}_{n}(x)=\Exp_{\mathfrak{o}}(\hat{f}_{n}(x))
. Recall the definition of the conditional mean
\mu
from (
3.1
).

Theorem 4.9
.

Assume
\sup_{x}k(x,x)=c<\infty
and let
\hat{\mu}_{n}=\Exp_{\mathfrak{o}}\circ\hat{f}_{n}
. Under Assumptions
4.6
, for
\lambda_{n}\asymp n^{-1/4}
,

\int_{\mathfrak{X}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}^{2}(\mu(x),\hat{\mu}_{n}(x))\mathrm{d}\theLaw_{X}(x)=O_{\theLaw^{*}}(n^{-1/2}).

These results warrants some discussion.

Remark 4.10
.

1.

Typical rates for kernel ridge regression depend on (1) the smoothness of the RKHS space, which is quantified by the rates of decays of the eigenvalues associated with the integral operator induced by the kernel (defined in (
4.6
)), and source conditions. In the worst case (roughest possible functions and no source conditions) the same
n^{-1/2}
as in Theorem
4.8
are obtained, see
21
. The difference is that our problem is highly non-linear in the response space (the sphere
\mathbb{S}
), whereas in kernel ridge regression the response space is linear.

2.

Our result holds for the sphere
\mathbb{S}
of arbitrary dimension, in particular the infinite-dimensional sphere, and the rates do not depend on the dimension of the sphere.

3.

We cannot compare our rates to Fréchet regression
(
75
)
because the estimated regression function of the latter is computed pointwise (for each value of
x\in\mathfrak{X}
) and the rates obtained are pointwise rates (or local rates) based on pointwise (or local) assumptions, such as quadratic growth of the population objective function for each
x\in\mathfrak{X}
. Our setting is different because we fit a non-parametric function
\hat{f}\in\mathcal{H}
simultaneously for all
x\in\mathfrak{X}
.

4.2.3
Rates of convergence depending on VVRKHS smoothness

Under stronger assumptions, we can derive rates of convergence that depend on the smoothness of the VVRKHS functions. Recall that for SMO kernels
K(x,x^{\prime})=k(x,x^{\prime})\mathrm{Id}
our VVRKHS is isomorphic to the tensor product of the scalar RKHS associated to
k
,
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, and the output space
T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S}
. This implies that for any vector
y\in T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S}
and
f\in\mathcal{H}
, the function
f_{y}(\cdot)=\langle f(\cdot),y\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}
belongs to
\mathcal{H}_{k}
(Lemma
S2.10
in the Appendix). The smoothness of
\mathcal{H}
functions is measured directly by the smoothness of the
\mathcal{H}_{k}
functions via the integral operator
\mathfrak{k}:{L^{2}(\theLaw_{X})}\to{L^{2}(\theLaw_{X})}
,

(\mathfrak{k}f)(x^{\prime})=\int k(x^{\prime},x)f(x)\mathrm{d}\theLaw_{X}(x),\quad f\in{L^{2}(\theLaw_{X})},

(4.6)

where
{L^{2}(\theLaw_{X})}
is the space of measurable function
f:\mathfrak{X}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\int f^{2}(X)\mathrm{d}\theLaw_{X}(x)<\infty
.
If
\Expected k(X,X)<\infty
, this operator
\mathfrak{k}
is trace-class
(
86
, Lemma 2.3)
, and
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is compactly embedded into
{L^{2}(\theLaw_{X})}
. Furthermore,
\mathfrak{k}
is self-adjoint, and admits a spectral decomposition in
{L^{2}(\theLaw_{X})}
,

\mathfrak{k}=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{l}\varphi_{l}\otimes\varphi_{l},

(4.7)

where
(\sigma_{l})_{l\geq 1}\subset[0,\infty)
is a non-negative decreasing and summable sequence,
(\varphi_{l})_{l\geq 1}\subset{L^{2}(\theLaw_{X})}
and
(f\otimes g)(h)(x^{\prime})=\int f(x)g(x^{\prime})h(x)\mathrm{d}\theLaw_{X}(x)
for
f,g,h\in{L^{2}(\theLaw_{X})}
.

The rates of decay of the eigenvalues
\{\sigma_{l}\}
encode the complexity of the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{k}
and its interaction with the probability measure
\theLaw_{X}
. Typical eigenvalue decay rates are given by the following definition.

Definition 4.11
.

Let
\{\sigma_{l}\}
be the decreasing sequence eigenvalues of
\mathfrak{k}
, as in (
4.7
). We define the following types of decay of these eigenvalues:

Finite-rank kernel.

For some
L\in\mathbb{N}
,
l>L\Rightarrow\sigma_{l}=0
.

Polynomial decay.

For some
p>1
and
C>0
,
\sigma_{l}\leq Cl^{-p}
for all
l\geq 1
.

Stretched exponential.

For some
q>0
,
\alpha>0
and
C>0
,
\sigma_{l}\leq Ce^{-\alpha l^{1/q}}
for all
l\geq 1
.

Remark 4.12
.

Obtaining the eigenvalue decay rates for general distributions
\theLaw_{X}
and general kernels
k
is non-trivial. A well-understood case is
\mathfrak{X}=\mathbb{R}^{s}
with
\theLaw_{X}
absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, with bounded density and bounded support, and for translation invariant kernels
k(x,x^{\prime})=\varphi(x-x^{\prime})
. This includes the Matérn kernel of smoothness
\nu
, for which the eigenvalue decay rate is polynomial with
p=1+2\nu/s
(the decay is faster for higher smoothnesses and slower for higher dimensions),
and the Gaussian kernel
k(x,x^{\prime})=\exp(-\lVert x-x^{\prime}\rVert^{2}/{2\sigma^{2}})
, where
\sigma>0
, for which the eigenvalue decay is a stretched exponential with parameter
q=s
.

We can now state a result on smoothness-dependent rates of convergence of our estimator.

Theorem 4.13
.

Assume that Assumption
4.6
holds,
\dim(\mathfrak{Y})<\infty
, and
\sup_{x}k(x,x)=c<\infty

\mathfrak{k}
given in Definition

\lVert\hat{f}_{n}-f_{\mathfrak{o}}\rVert_{L^{2}(\theLaw_{X})}^{2}=\begin{cases}O_{\theLaw^{*}}(n^{-1})&\text{for finite-rank kernel and }\lambda_{n}\asymp e^{-n},\\
O_{\theLaw^{*}}(n^{-\frac{p}{p+1}})&\text{for polynomial decay and }\lambda_{n}\asymp n^{-\frac{p}{2(p+1)}},\\
O_{\theLaw^{*}}((\log(n))^{q}/n)&\text{for stretched exponential decay and }\lambda_{n}\asymp n^{-1/2}.\end{cases}

The rates obtained translate directly into rates for the conditional mean estimator
\hat{\mu}_{n}(x)=\Exp_{\mathfrak{o}}(\hat{f}_{n}(x))
.

Theorem 4.14
.

Assume that Assumption
4.6
holds,
\dim(\mathfrak{Y})<\infty

Remark 4.15
.

The rates obtained can be compared to the closest similar regression settings for which theory is available: functional regression with RKHS, linear vector-valued kernel ridge regression, and manifold regression.

1.

In functional regression with RKHS techniques, the problem is to estimate
\beta
in the model
Y=\int\beta(t)X(t)dt+\varepsilon
by assuming
\beta
belongs to an RKHS. In this problem, the minimax rate obtained for instance under the assumption that
\mathfrak{k}
and the covariance operator of
X
are simultaneously diagonalizable, and under polynomial decay of their eigenvalues,
104
obtain a minimax optimal rate similar to our rate.

2.

For linear vector-valued kernel ridge regression, the rates depend on two quantities: (1) source conditions and (2) rates of decay of the eigenvalues of
\mathfrak{k}
.
Source conditions encode how much smoother
f_{\mathfrak{o}}
is compared to typical functions in
\mathcal{H}
. This is crystalized into the assumption that there is a
c\in[1,2]
such that for all
y\in T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S}
, the function
\langle f_{\mathfrak{o}}(\cdot),y\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}\in\image(\mathfrak{k}^{(c-1)/2})
. The case
c=1
corresponds to no source condition. In the linear setup, the exact form of
\hat{f}_{n}
is known, and such source conditions allow to control the bias term in the overall error. In our setting the closed form expression of
\hat{f}_{n}
is unknown and such source conditions are not directly useful. We therefore compare our rates to the linear rates with
c=1
.

21
provide rates for the least squares problem
\arg\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\Expected\left\lVert Y-f(X)\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}^{2}
under the assumption that
\trace K(x,x)<\infty
(which implies in our SMO kernel setting that
\dim\mathfrak{Y}<\infty
). The rates they obtain with no source conditions (case
c=1
) match our finite-rank rates exactly, and our polynomial decay rates up to a
\log(n)
factor.
We cannot compare to the rates of
56
since they consider the misspecified regression problem.
Our rates for the stretched exponential decay also match the kernel ridge regression rates, see, e.g.,
8
.

3.

We can also compare our rates with with the manifold regression literature.
28

By Theorem

In our implementation, we fix

Simulation study: spherical functional responses

6.1
Overview

In this section, we investigate the finite-sample performance of our proposed estimator. We focus on response variables
Y
belonging to the sphere
\mathbb{S}
in
L^{2}([0,1])
, that is,
\int_{0}^{1}Y^{2}(t)dt=1

Although our simulation study draws inspiration from its application to probability density functions (Section

We benchmark our model against tangent-space regression. This contrast effectively illustrates the benefit of utilizing an intrinsic geometry over a linear approximation. The performance gain is sizeable when data points are sufficiently spread apart on the sphere.

6.2
Simulation setup

\varepsilon

To model perturbations on the Riemannian manifold, we utilize a smooth perturbation process defined via a Fourier basis expansion in the template tangent space

Spherical kernel ridge regression for densities: analysis of urban pollution

We analyze the aerosol particle light absorption coefficient (AAC), which quantifies how strongly aerosol particles absorb light per unit path length and thus contributes to atmospheric warming and radiative forcing. We use our proposed method (spherical kernel ridge regression) to model square-root densities of the AAC probability distribution at different locations in the Netherlands.

The presented data problem is representative of a common setting in geophysical and
environmental applications, where automated measurement devices record a variable
Z

at high temporal frequency but only at a small number of geographical locations
X
. This yields
few covariate values
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
and many samples
z_{i1},\dots,z_{in_{i}}
from

Z\mid X=x_{i}
. This arises, for instance, in oceanographic monitoring
(
99
)
or radiosondes observations
(
32
)
.
In practice, full modeling all raw observations
(z_{ij})
is computationally costly and requires strong distributional and dependence assumptions, and
Z\mid X
is therefore often summarized by spatio-temporal aggregates such as means
\bar{z}_{i}
or similar summaries
(
41
)
, which suppress within-location variability and change the interpretation of the response. We adopt an intermediate strategy: for each location
x_{i}
we estimate nonparametrically the density
Z\mid X=x_{i}
, obtaining density-valued responses
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
that we then regress on the covariates
(x_{i})
.
Indeed, the particle light absorption coefficient is largely
driven by black carbon aerosols from traffic and residential combustion, and black carbon
mass concentrations are typically inferred from absorption via an assumed mass
absorption cross section. Variability and uncertainty in absorption therefore propagate
directly to black carbon estimates
(
65
)
. This further motivates our
distributional approach, rather than a purely mean-based characterization of absorption.

We use hourly absorption measurements from
14
air-quality monitoring stations in the Netherlands and neighboring regions (Belgium, Germany), obtained from the ACTRIS data portal
(
55
)
. Stations outside the Netherlands are included mainly to mitigate boundary effects, while inference focuses on the Dutch domain. The data we use is of the year 2019 for all stations with sufficiently complete records; for the station in Bösel (Germany), where no 2019 data are available, we substitute data from the last chronologically available year (2014). Each station
i
is represented by its geographic coordinates
x_{i}=
(latitude, longitude), which serve as spatial covariates. Over the small and low-relief study region, we treat these coordinates as points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Due to the data-collection mechanism and measurement errors, occasional small negative values of the absorption coefficient arise at low concentrations; these reflect uncertainty rather than physical absorption, and are retained in the analysis. Importantly, the presence of occasional negative observations does not pose difficulties for the proposed regression framework, whereas methods relying on log-transformations of the raw data
(
5
, such as)
would require ad hoc adjustments or data truncation.

For each station, the observations aggregated over one year are treated as a sample from an underlying distribution that is summarized by a kernel density estimator restricted to an interval
\mathcal{Z}=[z_{\min},z_{\max}]
, where
z_{\min}
and
z_{\max}
are taken as the minimum and maximum, respectively, of all observed values across all stations in the dataset. The estimated densities are mapped to the unit sphere in
L^{2}(\mathcal{Z})
via the square-root transformation, yielding responses on the positive orthant of a Hilbert sphere.

Figure

\sigma=15.2
) and regularization parameter (
\lambda=1/4
). The optimal model—fitted with the best parameters—can then be used in many ways, such as for predicting the absorption coefficient distributions at locations without monitoring stations (e.g., Amsterdam). Furthermore, estimates of different characteristics of the distribution,

Direct access to higher quantiles, rather than only the mean, is particularly useful in this context, for environmental decision making, as it highlights regions where absorption (might) persistently exceeds high pollution thresholds and may warrant targeted mitigation.

Fréchet regression

Following

Densities in a Bayes Hilbert space are required to be almost-everywhere positive, which is not necessarily the case for our density estimates

3.

The squared

8
Discussion

We have introduced a novel method for performing regression where the responses lie on a sphere. Our approach fundamentally decomposes the spherical non-linearity of the response into two components: a base intercept
\mathfrak{o}\in\mathbb{S}
, and a function
f:\mathfrak{X}\to T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S}
which belongs to a VVRKHS space. The theoretical guarantees presented in this paper rely primarily on bounded support assumptions for
Y
. This section proceeds by discussing the choice of the intercept
\mathfrak{o}
, the assumptions for our theoretical guarantees, and extensions of the method to more general Riemannian manifolds.

8.1
Choice of
\mathfrak{o}

The point
\mathfrak{o}
plays the role of an intercept in our method. We suggest to set it as the Fréchet mean of
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
in practice.
From a modeling perspective, the next result shows that in some settings, the choice of
\mathfrak{o}
does not affect the model considered. For
\mathfrak{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
open and bounded, denote by
H^{m}(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{Y})
the vector-valued Sobolev spaces of functions
f:\mathfrak{X}\to\mathfrak{Y}
order
m\in\{1,2,\ldots\}
, and recall that for
m>s/2
,
H^{m}(\mathfrak{X},\mathfrak{Y})
is a VVRKHS (see the Appendix, Section
S4
).
Let
\mathcal{C}^{m}(\bar{\mathfrak{X}},\mathbb{S})
denotes the functions
\mathfrak{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}
with
m
-times continuously differentiable extensions to some open set
\mathcal{U}\supset\overline{\mathfrak{X}}
around the closure of
\mathfrak{X}
.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
be open and bounded, and
\mathbb{S}\subset\mathfrak{Y}
be the sphere in a Hilbert space
\mathfrak{Y}
. Let
\mu:\mathfrak{X}\to\mathbb{S}
be a function.
Then,

1.

If
\mu\in\mathcal{C}^{m}(\overline{\mathfrak{X}},\mathbb{S})
, with
m>s/2
, for any
\mathfrak{o}\in\mathbb{S}
satisfying
\sup_{x\in\mathfrak{X}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathfrak{o},\mu(x))<\pi
,

f_{\mathfrak{o}}:=\Log_{\mathfrak{o}}\circ\mu\in H^{m}(\mathfrak{X},T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S}).

2.

If
s\in\{1,2\}
and
\dim(\mathfrak{Y})<\infty
, and there exists
\mathfrak{o}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}
such that
c:=\sup_{x\in\mathfrak{X}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathfrak{o}^{\prime},\mu(x))<\pi
,
then

\Log_{\mathfrak{o}^{\prime}}\circ\mu\in H^{m}(\mathfrak{X},T_{\mathfrak{o}^{\prime}}\mathbb{S}),\;m>s/2\Rightarrow\Log_{\mathfrak{o}}\circ\mu\in H^{m}(\mathfrak{X},T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S})\text{ for any }\mathfrak{o}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathfrak{o}^{\prime},\pi-c)

Potential relaxations of 1. and for which VVRKHS beyond Sobolev spaces a statement similar to Proposition
8.1
holds is left for future work.

8.2
Bounded support assumptions

A fundamental prerequisite for our theoretical results is Assumption
4.6
: the bounded support of
Y
,
\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathfrak{o},Y)\leq\pi/4
almost surely. These assumptions define the set
\mathcal{U}
on which empirical risk is minimised.
Without parametric assumptions on the model, bounded support assumptions are crucially needed. Indeed, the existence and uniqueness of the unconditional Fréchet mean of
Y
, which is a simpler problem, has so far only been shown under bounded support assumptions
(
3
;
103
)
.

This bounded support assumption on
Y
(
\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathfrak{o},Y)\leq\pi/4
a.s.) can be relaxed if one is willing to accept proofs based on numerical evaluations. In that case, we can relax the assumption to

\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathfrak{o},Y)\leq r\quad\mathrm{a.s.}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\lVert\Log_{\mathfrak{o}}(\mu(X))-\Log_{\mathfrak{o}}(Y)\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}\leq\rho\quad\mathrm{a.s.},

for pairs of values
(r,\rho)
. The intuition here is that
r
is the radius of the observations, whereas
\rho
is the noise level. We show in Section
S6
of the Appendix that for
(r,\rho)=(\pi/2,\pi/2.1)
and
(r,\rho)=(2\pi/3,\pi/6.6)
the results of Section
4.2.2
,
4.2.3
and
4.3
carry over after suitably modifying the set
\mathcal{U}
on which
\mathfrak{R}_{n}
is optimized. The case
r=\pi/2
allows the response
Y
to lie on an entire hemisphere, provided the pull-back noise magnitude,
\left\lVert\Log_{\mathfrak{o}}(\mu(X))-\Log_{\mathfrak{o}}(Y)\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{Y}}
, is not larger than
\pi/2.1
. We can increase the radius on which the response lies to
r=2\pi/3
but then the pull-back noise magnitude needs to be less than
\pi/6.6
. Generally, increasing the support (
r
) requires decreasing the noise (
\rho
). The noise assumption can also be made intrinsic: requiring
\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}(\mu(X),Y)\leq\pi/4
and using
r=\pi/2
yields similar results, but the proofs are more intricate because they require verifying that sets of the form
\Log_{\mathfrak{o}}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{S}}(y,\pi/4))
are convex sets, which is highly challenging,

8.3
A patchwork approach

An approach to weaken the bounded support assumption on
Y
would be to partition
\mathfrak{X}
into disjoint subsets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{s}\subset\mathfrak{X}
covering
\mathfrak{X}
, and assume there exist
\mathfrak{o}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{o}_{s}\in\mathbb{S}
such that
\theLaw(\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}(\mathfrak{o}_{j},Y)\leq\pi/4\mid X\in A_{j})=1
for
j=1,\ldots,s
. This would then lead to a separate estimate
\hat{\mu}_{j}
on each set
A_{j}
, and the global estimator would be obtained by combining them, i.e.,

\hat{\mu}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\hat{\mu}_{j}(x){1}_{A_{j}}(x).

8.4
Extensions to other manifolds

Our method could be extended to responses on more general and potentially infinite-dimensional manifolds
\mathcal{M}
. There are several technical challenges for such an extension: (1) existence (and uniqueness) of the conditional mean function
\mu
is not guaranteed
(
3
, to the best of our knowledge, existing results for general manifolds are only available for finite-dimensional manifolds, see)
; and (2) a key technical difficulty for the theoretical analysis of the method would be to obtain a local quadratic growth of the population risk, such as the one in Theorem
4.7
, which boils down to studying the convexity of the map
v\in T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathcal{M}\mapsto\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\mathcal{M}}(\Exp_{\mathfrak{o}}v,y)
.

, and that

\left|\Psi(z,m)-\Psi(z,m^{\prime})\right|\leq\eta(d(m,m^{\prime})),\quad\forall z\in\mathcal{Z},

\mathcal{M}
, and

\mathcal{M}

The following results are for convex functions on convex sets, and hold in particular for points at the boundary of the convex set.

Lemma S2.8

\mathcal{C}

of
\Phi

\Phi(v)\geq\Phi(u_{0})+\frac{c}{2}\lVert v-u_{0}\rVert^{2},\quad\forall v\in\mathcal{C}.

1.

a)

By convexity,

{\Phi}\big(u+t(v-u)\big)\leq t\,{\Phi}(v)+(1-t)\,{\Phi}(u)={\Phi}(u)+t\big({\Phi}(v)-{\Phi}(u)\big)

This implies

\frac{{\Phi}\big(u+t(v-u)\big)-{\Phi}(u)}{t}\leq{\Phi}(v)-{\Phi}(u)

\displaystyle\Phi(u)

\displaystyle\geq\Phi(w)+\langle\nabla\Phi(w),\,u-w\rangle,

\displaystyle\Phi(v)

for

Properties of convex functions defined on Hilbert spaces, and their link between strong and weak semi-continuity are available in the literature for functions that are convex over the entire Hilbert space. We need results for functions that are only convex on a closed convex subset.

Lemma S2.9
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space,
C\subseteq H
be convex and closed. Assume
\phi:H\to\mathbb{R}
is a function such that
\phi_{|C}
is lower semi-continuous and convex. Then
\phi_{|C}
weakly lower semi-continuous. Furthermore, if
C
is bounded or
\phi
is coercive, the infimum
\inf_{x\in C}\phi(x)
is achieved for a
x^{*}\in C
.

Let
\psi=\phi_{|C}
.
For the first claim, by
76
,
\mathrm{Epi}(\psi)\subseteq C\times\mathbb{R}
is closed, and it is easily checked that it is a convex set. Since
C
is closed in
H
then
\mathrm{Epi}(\psi)
is also closed in
H\times\mathbb{R}
. By
76
it is therefore weakly closed. Applying again
76
,
\psi
is weakly lower semi-continuous.

For the claim about the infimum, take
\{x_{n}\}\subset C
a sequence achieving the infimum, i.e.,
I:=\inf_{x\in C}\phi(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\phi(x_{n})
. We already know that
C
is weakly closed since it is closed and convex. Furthermore since either
C
is bounded or
\phi
is coercive, the sequence
\{x_{n}\}
is contained in a certain closed ball

, which is weakly compact
(
27
, V.4.2 Theorem)
and we can extract a subsequence from
\{x_{n}\}
, which we again denote by
\{x_{n}\}
such that

converges weakly to

for a certain
x^{*}\in B
, and since we have already established that

is weakly closed,

. By weak lower semi-continuity,

\phi(x^{*})\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\phi(x_{n})=I

but since

and thus

.

The following Lemma helps constructing orthogonal operators that map specific subspaces to other subspaces while acting as the identity on some other subspaces.

.

\displaystyle\langle A\tilde{g},\bm{\xi}\rangle_{\tilde{\hy}^{n}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle(A\tilde{g})_{i},\xi_{i}\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\tilde{g}(x_{i}),\xi_{i}\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}\overset{\text{Lemma}\ref{Lemma:subspace}}{=}\langle\tilde{g},\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcal{P}}_{\tilde{\hy}}\circ K_{x_{i}}\xi_{i}\rangle_{\tilde{H}}=\langle\tilde{g},\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{x_{i}}\xi_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

by Lemma

.
Therefore,

. Now consider the symmetric operator

\displaystyle B\bm{\xi}=A(\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{x_{i}}\xi_{i})=(\sum_{i=1}^{n}K(x_{i},x_{j})\xi_{i},j=1,\dots,n).

then we have:

\displaystyle\langle A\tilde{g},\bm{y}\rangle_{\tilde{\hy}^{n}}=\langle\tilde{g},A^{*}\bm{y}\rangle_{\tilde{\hy}^{n}}=0.

S2.5
Technical results for effective dimensions

In this section we assume that
(\sigma_{j})_{j\geq 1}
is a non-increasing positive and summable sequence. For
\lambda>0
let

N(\lambda)\ :=\ \sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{j}+\lambda}.

Lemma S2.20
.

Assume
(\sigma_{j})_{j\geq 1}
is non-increasing and there exist constants
C>0
and
p>1
such that

\sigma_{j}\leq Cj^{-p}\qquad\text{for all }j\geq 1.

Then

N(\lambda)=O(\lambda^{-1/p}),\quad\text{for }\lambda\downarrow 0

Let
J\geq 1
be an integer. Then,

N(\lambda)\ =\ \sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{j}+\lambda}\ +\ \sum_{j>J}\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{j}+\lambda}\ \leq\ J\ +\ \sum_{j>J}\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\lambda}.

Using
\sigma_{j}\leq Cj^{-p}
, we get

\sum_{j>J}\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\lambda}\ \leq\ \frac{C}{\lambda}\sum_{j>J}j^{-p}.

Since
p>1
,

\sum_{j>J}j^{-p}\ \leq\ \int_{J}^{\infty}x^{-p}\,dx\ =\ \frac{J^{1-p}}{p-1}.

Therefore

N(\lambda)\ \leq\ J\ +\ \frac{C}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{J^{1-p}}{p-1}.

For
\lambda\in(0,C]
set

J\ :=\ \left\lceil(C/\lambda)^{1/p}\right\rceil.

Then
(C/\lambda)^{1/p}\leq J\leq 1+(C/\lambda)^{1/p}
,
and both terms are
O(\lambda^{-1/p})
.
∎

Lemma S2.21
.

Assume
(\sigma_{j})
is non-increasing and
\sigma_{j}\leq C\exp(-\alpha j^{1/q})
for some
C,\alpha>0
and
q>0
.
Then
N(\lambda)=O((\log(1/\lambda))^{q})
as
\lambda\downarrow 0
.

For
J\geq 1
a integer,

N(\lambda)\leq J+\lambda^{-1}\sum_{j>J}\sigma_{j}\leq\ J+\frac{C}{\lambda}\sum_{j>J}e^{-\alpha j^{1/q}}.

We bound the tail by an integral:

\sum_{j>J}e^{-\alpha j^{1/q}}\ \leq\ \int_{J}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha x^{1/q}}\,dx.

1.

Consider first the case
q\in(0,1]
in which case
p=1/q\geq 1

and the tail integral becomes

\sum_{j>J}e^{-\alpha j^{p}}\ \leq\ \int_{J}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha x^{p}}\,dx.

Substitute
u=\alpha x^{p}
so
dx=\frac{1}{p}\alpha^{-1/p}u^{1/p-1}\,du
:

\int_{J}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha x^{p}}\,dx=\frac{1}{p}\alpha^{-1/p}\int_{\alpha J^{p}}^{\infty}u^{1/p-1}e^{-u}\,du.

When
p\geq 1
, the exponent
1/p-1\leq 0
, so
u^{1/p-1}
is decreasing; thus for
u\geq\alpha J^{p}
,

u^{1/p-1}\leq(\alpha J^{p})^{1/p-1}
. Hence

\int_{\alpha J^{p}}^{\infty}u^{1/p-1}e^{-u}\,du\leq(\alpha J^{p})^{1/p-1}\int_{\alpha J^{p}}^{\infty}e^{-u}\,du=(\alpha J^{p})^{1/p-1}e^{-\alpha J^{p}}.

Therefore

\sum_{j>J}e^{-\alpha j^{p}}\leq\frac{1}{p}\alpha^{-1/p}(\alpha J^{p})^{1/p-1}e^{-\alpha J^{p}}=\frac{1}{p\alpha}J^{1-p}e^{-\alpha J^{p}}.

For
\lambda\in(0,C)
set

J\ :=\ \left\lceil\Big(\alpha^{-1}\log(C/\lambda)\Big)^{1/p}\right\rceil,

hence
C\exp(-\alpha J^{p})\leq\lambda
.
Plugging back,

\frac{C}{\lambda}\sum_{j>J}e^{-\alpha j^{p}}\leq\frac{C}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{1}{p\alpha}J^{1-p}e^{-\alpha J^{p}}=\frac{1}{p\alpha}J^{1-p}\cdot\frac{Ce^{-\alpha J^{p}}}{\lambda}\leq\frac{1}{p\alpha}J^{1-p},

which is bounded by
1/(p\alpha)
since
J\geq 1
and
p\geq 1
.
Thus
N(\lambda)\leq J+C_{0}
with
C_{0}:=1/(p\alpha)
, and the stated polylog rate follows from

J\asymp(\log(1/\lambda))^{1/p}=(\log(1/\lambda))^{q}
.

2.

Now consider the case
q>1
.
With the change of variables
u=\alpha x^{1/q}
, the tail integral becomes

\int_{J}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha x^{1/q}}\,dx.=\frac{q}{\alpha^{q}}\Gamma(q,U)

where
\Gamma(q,s)=\int_{s}^{\infty}e^{-u}u^{q-1}du
is the incomplete Gamma function and
U=\alpha J^{1/q}
. As
J\to\infty
, we have

\frac{\Gamma(q,U)}{U^{q-1}e^{-U}}\to 1,

see
90
.
Thus for
J
large enough,

N(\lambda)\leq J+\frac{C}{\lambda}\frac{q}{\alpha^{q}}(U^{q-1}e^{-U}+1)

The choice
J=\lfloor(\alpha^{-1}\log(C/\lambda))^{q}\rfloor
yields
U\sim\log(C/\lambda)

and thus as
\lambda\downarrow 0
,

N(\lambda)=O((\log(1/\lambda))^{q})+O((\log(1/\lambda))^{q-1}\lambda)=O((\log(1/\lambda))^{q})

∎

S2.7
Gradient computation

To compute our estimator, the goal is to minimize the empirical risk
\mathfrak{R}_{n}(f,\lambda_{n})
with respect to
f
. By Theorem
4.1
, assuming that a unique minimizer of
\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\cdot,\lambda_{n})
exists, we can search for the optimal
f
by finding the optimal coefficients
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\in\vspan(\Log_{\mathfrak{o}}(Y_{1}),\ldots,\Log_{\mathfrak{o}}(Y_{n}))
.
Letting
{\bm{\xi}}=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})
, the reparametrized empirical risk is
R_{n}({\bm{\xi}}):=\mathfrak{R}_{n}(f_{\bm{\xi}},\lambda_{n})
where
f_{\bm{\xi}}(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}K(x_{i},\cdot)\xi_{i}
, so

\displaystyle R_{n}(\bm{\xi})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\arccos(\langle\Exp_{\mathfrak{o}}f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i}),y_{i}\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}))^{2}+\lambda_{n}^{2}\lVert f_{\bm{\xi}}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}.

The strategy to compute the gradient of
R_{n}(\bm{\xi})
with respect to one
\xi_{l}\in T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S}
,
l\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, is to first consider the smooth extension
\bar{R}_{n}(\bm{\xi})
defined on the ambient space
(\mathfrak{Y})^{n}
, and then project the gradient onto
T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S}
. In the following, we denote
\mu_{i}=\Exp_{\mathfrak{o}}(f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i}))
and
K_{il}=K(x_{i},x_{l})
.

For fixed
i
, consider the function

\mu\mapsto\big(\arccos\langle\mu,y_{i}\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}\big)^{2},

\mu\in\mathfrak{Y}.

Its Fréchet derivative at
\mu_{i}
in direction
v\in\mathfrak{Y}
is

\displaystyle D_{\mu_{i}}\left[(\arccos(\langle\mu_{i},y_{i}\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}))^{2}\right][v]=-2\frac{\arccos\langle\mu_{i},y_{i}\rangle}{\sqrt{1-(\langle\mu_{i},y_{i}\rangle)^{2}}}\langle v,y_{i}\rangle

Using the explicit expression of
\Exp_{\mathfrak{o}}
on the sphere, one obtains
for the ambient differential (computed as a map between open subsets of

\mathfrak{Y}
):

\displaystyle D_{\xi_{l}}\left[\Exp_{\mathfrak{o}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}K_{ij}\xi_{j}\right)\right][v]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(-\sin\|f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})\|\frac{\langle f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i}),K_{il}v\rangle}{\|f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})\|}\mathfrak{o}+\cos\|f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})\|\frac{\langle f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i}),K_{il}v\rangle}{\|f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})\|^{2}}f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})

\displaystyle+\frac{\sin\|f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})\|}{\|f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})\|}\left[K_{il}v-f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})\frac{\langle K_{il}v,f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})\rangle}{\|f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})\|^{2}}\right]\big)

Consider the smooth extension
\overline{R}_{n}:(\mathfrak{Y})^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of
R_{n}:(T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S})^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle\overline{R}_{n}(\bm{\xi})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\arccos(\langle\Exp_{\mathfrak{o}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}K_{ij}\xi_{j},y_{i}\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}))^{2}+\lambda_{n}^{2}\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{n}K_{j}\xi_{j}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2},

Then, the gradient of
\overline{R}_{n}
is given by

\displaystyle D_{\xi_{l}}\overline{R}_{n}(\xi_{l})[v]=-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\arccos\langle\mu_{i},y_{i}\rangle}{\sqrt{1-(\langle\mu_{i},y_{i}\rangle)^{2}}}\langle D_{\xi_{l}}\mu_{i}(\xi_{l})[v],y_{i}\rangle+2\lambda_{n}^{2}f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{l})

and the gradient of
R_{n}
is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\xi_{l}}R_{n}(\xi_{l})={\mathcal{P}}_{\mathfrak{o}}(\nabla_{\xi_{l}}\overline{R}_{n}(\xi_{l})).

Finally, the gradient

\nabla R_{n}(\bm{\xi})\in(T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S})^{n}
is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\xi_{l}}R_{n}({\bm{\xi}})=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[-\frac{K_{il}\arccos(\langle\mu_{i},y_{i}\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}})}{\sqrt{1-\langle\mu_{i},y_{i}\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}^{2}}}{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathfrak{o}}[\gamma_{i}]\right]+2\lambda_{n}^{2}f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{l}),

(S2.7)

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}=\frac{\sin\|f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})\|_{\mathfrak{Y}}}{\|f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})\|_{\mathfrak{Y}}}\!\left(-\left(\langle\mathfrak{o},y_{i}\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}+\frac{\langle f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i}),y_{i}\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}}{\|f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})\|^{2}}\right)f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})+y_{i}\right)+\frac{\cos\|f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})\|_{\mathfrak{Y}}}{\|f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})\|_{\mathfrak{Y}}^{2}}\langle f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i}),y_{i}\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}\,f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i}).

Note that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathfrak{o}}[\gamma_{i}]=\frac{\sin\|f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})\|_{\mathfrak{Y}}}{\|f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})\|_{\mathfrak{Y}}}\Big(-\Big(\langle\mathfrak{o},y_{i}\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}+\frac{\langle f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i}),y_{i}\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}}{\|f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})\|_{\mathfrak{Y}}^{2}}\Big)f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})+{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathfrak{o}}[y_{i}]\Big)+\frac{\cos\|f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})\|_{\mathfrak{Y}}}{\|f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i})\|_{\mathfrak{Y}}^{2}}\langle f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i}),y_{i}\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}f_{\bm{\xi}}(x_{i}).

Using the explicit expression of

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widetilde{R}_{n}}{\partial a_{l,j}}=\;2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{-\,\frac{K_{il}\,\arccos(\langle\mu_{i},y_{i}\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}})}{\sqrt{1-\langle\mu_{i},y_{i}\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}^{\,2}}}\left\langle{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathfrak{o}}\big[\gamma_{i}\big],\,\Log_{\mathfrak{o}}(y_{j})\right\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}\right\}\;+\;2\lambda_{n}^{2}\,\langle f_{A}(x_{l}),\Log_{\mathfrak{o}}(y_{j})\rangle_{\mathfrak{Y}}.

Hence, for each

\{\Log_{\mathfrak{o}}(y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n}

Proposition S2.28

S3.1
Construction of the conditional mean

To construct
\mu
in a principled way, we proceed as follows: as an initial template for
\mu
, we use
\Psi:x\mapsto\psi_{x}^{1/2}
given by a mixture density

\psi_{x}(z)=w_{1}(x)\phi\left(\frac{z-m_{1}(x)}{s_{2}(x)}\right)+w_{2}(x)\phi\left(\frac{z-m_{2}(x)}{s_{2}(x)}\right)

of Gaussians truncated to
z\in[0,1]
, where
\phi
is the standard Gaussian probability density function, and
m:\mathfrak{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
s:\mathfrak{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
,
w:\mathfrak{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
are suitable location, scale, and weight functions respectively. We choose the GRBF kernel with length-scale parameter
\sigma
, then we fit the spherical kernel ridge regression model to a set
\{\Psi(\bm{x_{i}})\}
, for covariates
\bm{x}_{i}\in[0,1]^{2}
,
i=1,\dots,n
on a regular grid, fixing
\mathfrak{o}
to the spherical Fréchet mean of
\{\Psi(\bm{x_{i}})\}
. This gives a set of regression coefficients
\tilde{\xi}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\xi}_{n}\in T_{\mathfrak{o}}\mathbb{S}
. To obtain a prescribed model variance level
\tau_{\mu}^{2}=(2/5\pi)^{2}
, we rescale
\tilde{\xi}_{i}
by their variance so that the resulting
\xi_{i}
have this fixed model variance. This guarantees that the data are sufficiently spread out on the sphere, in a domain of radius larger than
\pi/2
around
\mathfrak{o}
. The conditional mean function is then given by

Figure

shows that the resulting

exhibits spatially structured variation in both location and shape, originated by the gaussian mixture.

Introduction to the real-valued case

Let
\Omega
be an open set in
\mathbb{R}^{s}
and
\mathcal{C}^{m}(\Omega)
the set of
m
times continuously differentiable functions
\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
Let further
\mathcal{C}^{m}_{c}(\Omega)=\{\psi\in\mathcal{C}^{m}(\Omega):\psi(x)=0,x\notin\Psi,\Psi\subset\Omega\text{ compact}\}
be those with compact support.
For an integer multi-index
\iota=(\iota_{1},\dots,\iota_{d})
, we write
|\iota|=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\iota_{i}
and
D^{\iota}=\frac{\partial^{|\iota|}\psi}{\partial^{\iota_{1}}x_{1}\dots\partial^{\iota_{d}}x_{d}}
for
\mathcal{C}^{m}(\Omega)

Define the weak
\iota

as the element

of

satisfying

\int D^{\iota}f(x)\psi(x)\,dx=(-1)^{\iota}\int f(x)D^{\iota}\psi(x)\,dx

for all smooth functions
\psi\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
If such a
D^{\iota}f
exists, it is unique and

is called weakly differentiable. If

, its weak derivatives coincide with the corresponding derivatives.

The Sobolev space

is defined as

H^{m}(\Omega)=\{f\in L^{2}(\Omega):D^{\iota}f\in L^{2}(\Omega),|\iota|\leq m\}

with inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{H^{m}(\Omega)}=\sum_{\iota:|\iota|\leq m}\int D^{\iota}f(x)D^{\iota}g(x)\,dx

where usually the integer
m
is assumed positive, while

.

As usual let
\bar{\Omega}
denote the closure of
\Omega
, and define
\mathcal{C}^{m}(\bar{\Omega})=\{\psi\in\mathcal{C}^{m}(\Omega):D^{\iota}\psi=\left.\phi\right|_{\Omega},\phi\in\mathcal{C}(\bar{\Omega})\}
where
\left.\phi\right|_{\Omega}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R},x\mapsto\phi(x)
denotes the restriction of function
\phi
to
\Omega
.

\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}(\rho)=\{f\in V(\rho)\mid\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\leq C\}
for some arbitrary
C>\lVert f_{\mathfrak{o}}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}
.
Let
\tilde{f}_{n}
be the minimizer of
\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\cdot,\lambda_{n})
over
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}(\rho)
.

Theorem S6.10
.

Assume that Assumptions
S6.2
and
4.16
hold, and
\sup_{x}k(x,x)=c<\infty
.
If
\lambda_{n}\downarrow 0
as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{f}_{n}-f_{\mathfrak{o}}\rVert_{L^{2}(\theLaw_{X})}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}*}0,\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

The proof follows the exact same steps of the proof of Theorem
4.17
; details are left to the reader.
∎
