Bioavailability and growth performance of zinc sulfate, zinc glycinate, zinc amino acid hydrate, and zinc methionine hydroxy analog in male broilers☆
Authors: Mohamad Mortada, Andrea Hanson, Jose Charal, Daniel Jones, Kelsey Machens, Rachel Proctor, Jason Hickman, Catherine Ionescu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Two experiments were conducted to evaluate the relative bioavailability (RBV) and performance effects of four zinc sources: zinc sulfate (ZnSO₄), zinc glycinate (ZnGly), zinc amino acid hydrate (ZnAAH), and zinc methionine hydroxy analog (ZnMHA) in male broilers. Experiment 1 used a depletion–repletion model with semi-purified diets to assess graded supplementation levels of ZnSO₄, ZnGly, and ZnAAH (0, 5, 10, 15, or 20 mg/kg). Experiment 2 evaluated all four sources at 10 and 60 mg/kg, along with a negative control (NC, 0 mg/kg), under practical corn–soybean meal feeding conditions. In Experiment 1, supplemental zinc significantly affected BWG, FI, FCR, and tibia zinc concentration. Growth responses (BWG and FI) exhibited both linear and quadratic trends, plateauing at approximately 11.7 mg/kg zinc intake, while tibia zinc increased linearly across all levels. Organic sources (ZnGly and ZnAAH) increased tibia zinc by 11–12% and improved FCR by 1.1–1.5 points compared with ZnSO₄. RBV estimates based on tibia zinc indicated that ZnGly and ZnAAH were 42–48% more bioavailable than ZnSO₄ (P < 0.05), with no significant differences between the two organic sources. In Experiment 2, zinc source influenced BWG, FI, and FCR (P < 0.05) and tended to affect overall FCR (P = 0.07). Supplementation with ZnSO₄ and ZnGly increased overall BWG by 118 g and 104 g, respectively, compared with NC, and resulted in greater performance than ZnMHA. ZnGly reduced overall FCR by 3.6 points, while ZnGly and ZnSO₄ improved adjusted FCR by 6.8 and 5.8 points, respectively, relative to NC (P < 0.05). ZnGly increased hot carcass yield by 0.62% (P < 0.05), whereas ZnSO₄ and ZnMHA showed trends toward improvement (P < 0.10). Excreta zinc increased with supplementation across all sources, while tibia zinc increased for all treatments except ZnMHA compared with NC. Overall, organic zinc sources demonstrated greater RBV than ZnSO₄ under semi-purified conditions, primarily based on tibia zinc responses. However, under practical feeding conditions, ZnSO₄ and ZnGly produced comparable improvements in growth performance, suggesting that RBV estimates may not fully predict performance outcomes in commercial-type diets. Future research should focus on optimizing zinc strategies to enhance carcass yield and explore broader physiological roles, including footpad health, immunity, welfare, and environmental sustainability in commercial broiler production.

Introduction

Zinc is an essential trace mineral that plays a pivotal role in numerous physiological and metabolic processes. In poultry, zinc is involved in enzyme activation, immune function, protein synthesis, feather development, bone formation, reproductive performance, and overall health and maintenance (Kidd et al., 2000; Lilburn and McIntyre, 2024; Ng et al., 2025; Ng et al., 2022). Despite its biological importance, early recognition of zinc as a critical nutrient in poultry nutrition was challenging due to the subtle nature of deficiency symptoms. Zinc deficiency often presents less obvious signs under practical feeding conditions (Lilburn and McIntyre, 2024). Consequently, early research relied on purified and semi-purified diets to minimize background zinc contribution from commercial feed ingredients. This approach facilitated controlled studies on determining the zinc requirement and assessing relative bioavailability of various zinc sources (Baker and Ammerman, 1995).

Zinc supplementation in poultry diets is influenced by several factors, including fiber content, protein source, calcium and phytase levels, ascorbic acid presence, and the chemical form of zinc (Baker and Ammerman, 1995). The National Research Council (NRC, 1994) recommends a total dietary zinc level of 40 ppm for poultry. However, commercial broiler breeder guidelines, such as those for Cobb 500 and Ross 708, suggest supplementing diets with 100–120 ppm of added zinc to support the rapid growth and high metabolic demands of modern broilers (Cobb-Vantress, Inc., 2022; Aviagen, 2022). In practice, zinc levels in U.S. commercial diets average around 60 ppm (Oviedo-Rondon, 2012), highlighting a gap between NRC recommendations and industry practices. Therefore, updated research is needed to refine zinc requirements for fast-growing broilers under modern production conditions.

Inorganic zinc sources, particularly zinc sulfate (ZnSO₄), are widely used due to their cost-effectiveness and availability. However, ZnSO₄ is hygroscopic and reactive, which can compromise the stability of vitamins and fats in feed (Cao et al., 2000). Zinc oxide, another inorganic source, has reduced bioavailability compared to ZnSO₄ (Wedekind and Baker, 1990). Organic zinc sources, such as zinc glycinate, zinc amino acid hydrate, and zinc methionine hydroxy analog, are chelated or complexed with amino acids, proteins, polysaccharides or other organic ligands, potentially enhancing their stability and bioavailability (AAFCO, 2001). These organic forms have been associated with improved bone health (Ferket et al., 1992), fertility (Zhang et al., 2017), immune function (Ng et al., 2025), eggshell quality (Stefanello et al., 2014), and reduced mineral excretion in poultry (Vieira et al., 2020).

Bioavailability is a key determinant of zinc source efficacy and is defined as the proportion of the nutrient absorbed and utilized by the animal (Ammerman and Baker, 1995). Relative bioavailability (RBV) studies typically use ZnSO₄ as the reference standard (100% bioavailability) and assess test sources based on parameters such as body weight gain (BWG) and tibia zinc concentration (Batal et al., 2001). While many studies report greater RBV for organic zinc sources, results vary depending on diet composition, zinc levels, and response criteria (Baker and Ammerman, 1995).

Beyond controlled RBV trials, zinc sources are also evaluated in practical feeding programs for their effects on growth performance, carcass yield, immune function, and antioxidant status. Particular attention has been given to the interaction between dietary zinc and phytase, as phytase hydrolyzes phytate complexes and thereby enhances mineral availability, including zinc, beyond its well-established effects on phosphorus and calcium (Ao, et al., 2007; Dersjant-Li, et al., 2025).

Given the variability in zinc bioavailability and performance outcomes among different sources, comprehensive studies integrating both controlled and practical feeding models are needed. The present study addresses this gap through two complementary experiments.

Experiment 1 employed a depletion–repletion model using semi-purified diets to evaluate the RBV of zinc glycinate (ZnGly) and zinc amino acid hydrate (ZnAAH) relative to ZnSO₄, using BWG and tibia zinc concentration as biomarkers following methods developed by Batal et al. (2001). Experiment 2 was a 42-day floor pen trial assessing the effects of ZnSO₄, ZnGly, ZnAAH, and ZnMHA on broiler performance, carcass yield, and mineral deposition in a practical corn–soybean meal diet supplemented with 500 FTU/kg of phytase.

The aim of these experiments was to provide a robust evaluation of zinc source efficacy, combining precise bioavailability estimation with performance outcomes under commercial feeding conditions.

Materials and methods

Both experiments were conducted at ADM’s Montgomery City Research and Development Center (Montgomery City, Missouri, USA). The trials were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the care and use of animals in research (Ag Guide, 2020). The experimental protocols were approved by ADM’s Animal Care and Use Committee.

Birds and housing

For both studies, Ross 708 male broilers were obtained on day-of-hatch from a commercial hatchery where they had been vaccinated against Marek’s disease (HVT-Rispens). Birds were allotted based on body weight so that each experimental unit contained birds of approximately equal averages (Mean ± 2SD). In Experiment 1, birds were raised in battery cages, while in Experiment 2, birds were raised in floor pens with wood shavings as bedding. Temperature was maintained initially around 32°C and gradually reduced by 2°C per week to reach around 22°C by week 5. In both experiments, feed and water were provided ad libitum.

Experiment 1 trial design and dietary treatments

The depletion-repletion model published by Batal et al. (2001) was used in the relative bioavailability study. This model utilizes a semi-purified diet designed to minimize background zinc and enhance RBV sensitivity. Therefore, growth performance responses were interpreted relative to zinc adequacy rather than absolute values. A total of 1,500-day-old chicks were distributed across 198 battery cages in three rooms, with 8 birds per cage. Experimental battery cages were 27 in (68.6 cm) in length and 20 in (50.8 cm) in width, yielding 0.35 m² per cage. Birds were fed a Zinc-adequate corn-SBM diet from D0 to 4 and a Zn-deficient semi-purified diet from D4 to 8. All diets were provided as a mash. Feed was withdrawn from day 8 (PM) to 9 (AM). On D9, 819 birds were randomly distributed to cages and fed one of 13 semi-purified diets (including corn starch, dextrose, and soy protein concentrate) with either no supplemental zinc or zinc supplemented via ZnSO4, ZnGly, or ZnAAH, at 5, 10, 15, or 20 ppm added Zn until D22 (n = 15 replicate cages with 7 birds/cage) (Tables 1, 2 and 3). Performance parameters were recorded from D9 – D22 including BWG, FI, FCR, and mortality. On D22, 2 birds/cage from 9 replicates were euthanized, and right tibiae were collected and pooled by cage for Zn analysis. The bioavailability of ZnGly and ZnAAH relative to ZnSO4 was measured using BWG and tibia zinc as RBV biomarkers. Commercial organic zinc sources used in the two trials included ZnGly as B-Traxim® 2C Zn (ADM, Decatur, Illinois, USA), ZnAAH as Availa® Zn (Zinpro Corporation, Eden Prairie, Minnesota, USA) and ZnMHA Experiment 2 trial design and dietary treatments

A total of 1,152 broiler chicks were randomly assigned to nine experimental treatments with 8 floor pens per treatment and 16 birds/pen. Each pen measured 48 in × 48 in (1.22 m × 1.22 m), providing 16 ft² (1.49 m²) of floor space, with fresh wood shavings. The nine treatments included a negative control treatment without supplemental Zn and 8 treatments supplemented with 10 or 60 ppm of zinc from ZnSO4, ZnGly, ZnAAH, or ZnMHA (Table 1). One basal diet per feeding phase was formulated to meet or exceed Ross 708 nutritional requirements, including standard ileal digestible amino acid requirements, except for Zn. Standardized ileal digestible amino acid compositions of formulated experimental diets are presented in the supplemental material. Amino acid contributions from ligands associated with zinc sources were not adjusted, as they were considered minimal relative to total amino acid supply under the conditions of this study. Feeding phases included phase 1 (D0 – D21) fed in crumble form and phase 2 (D21 – D42) fed in pelleted form (Table 3). Performance parameters (BWG, FI, and FCR) were recorded per phase and overall. Adjusted FCR is commonly used in the broiler industry to adjust treatments to a common BWG as a comprehensive performance parameter. Overall FCR was adjusted to the NC BWG, where every 100 g improvement in BWG equated to a 3-point improvement in adjusted FCR, and vice versa (Williams, 2014). On D21, two birds per pen, representing the average pen weight, were randomly selected, and euthanized. The right tibiae sampled were then pooled by pen and stored for Zn tibia content analysis. On D40, fresh excreta samples were collected on a flat pan from all pens and stored for zinc analysis. On D43 of age, 6 birds/pen representing the average pen weight were randomly selected, wing-tagged, and weighed for processing. Broilers were processed to a medium scald (55 – 58°C for 60 – 120 seconds) and were mechanically defeathered. Carcasses were hand-eviscerated, and hot carcass and abdominal fat pad weights were recorded and expressed as percent of live weight.

Chemical analysis

Thawed tibiae were pooled per experimental unit and autoclaved at 121°C for 25 minutes to remove all cartilage. After autoclaving, tibiae were ashed in a muffle furnace overnight at 600°C, and ash was pulverized using a mortar and pestle. Excreta samples were oven-dried at 55°C for 2-3 days or until a constant dry weight was achieved. After drying, excreta samples were weighed and ground to pass a 1 mm screen for zinc analysis that was reported as mg/kg of dry matter. Zinc content in zinc sources, feeds, tibiae ash, and excreta were analyzed by Eurofins (Des Moines, Iowa, USA) using AOAC 984.27. Experimental diets were ground to less than 1 mm and submitted for proximate analysis, including crude protein (AOAC 990.03 mod), moisture content (AOAC 930.15), Ash (AOAC 942.05), ADF (AOAC 973.18), and NDF (AOAC 2002.04), according to AOAC International, (2023).

Statistical analysis

For Experiment 1, data were analyzed using JMP Pro18 (Cary, North Carolina, USA) Mixed Model to evaluate the main effects of zinc source (Zn) and Zinc level (L), as well as their interaction, with room and tier as random effects. Pre-planned contrasts and linear and quadratic responses were tested. The bioavailability of ZnGly and ZnAAH was determined using nonlinear and linear regression, followed by slope-ratio analysis to assess their bioavailability relative to zinc sulfate, based on body weight gain and tibia zinc biomarkers, following Littell et al. (1997) methodology and equations. For linear regression analysis, response variables were fitted to a broken-line model to determine the linear zinc level range used for multiple linear regression and slope-ratio analysis. BWG and Tibia Zn content data were regressed against supplemental zinc intake.

Experiment 2 was analyzed as a 4 × 2 factorial arrangement plus an unsupplemented negative control treatment. Zinc source (ZnSO4, ZnGly, ZnAAH, or ZnMHA) and supplemental zinc level (10 or 60 mg/kg) were included as fixed effects. Room and block were included as random effects in the mixed model. The negative control (NC; 0 mg/kg supplemental Zn) was excluded from the factorial analysis and evaluated separately comparing NC with Zn-supplemented treatments. Least square means were separated using Student’s t-test where appropriate. Significance was declared at P ≤ 0.05, and trends were discussed at 0.05<P ≤ 0.10.

Results

Analyzed nutrients

For the relative bioavailability experiment 1, the major ingredients were analyzed for background zinc. Soy protein concentrate (SPC) had 35 mg/kg of zinc, having a 104% recovery relative to the formulated concentration. Zinc levels in corn starch and dextrose were below the limit of quantification (0.2 mg/kg). The semi-purified basal diet contained 17 mg/kg of total zinc, which was 116% of the formulated 14.7 mg/kg total zinc primarily from SPC contribution (Table 2). Analyzed nutrients, including crude protein and ash, agreed with the formulated values except for moisture, which was greater in the analyzed semi-purified diet compared to the formulated (8.7 Vs 2.8%). Analyzed zinc in experiment 1, phase 1 corn-SBM diet was in line (104%) with the formulated diet (Table 3). For Experiment 2 phase 1 and phase 2 diets contained 27–30% greater levels of zinc than the formulated amount (Table 3, 43 and 42 vs. 33 mg/kg). All other analyzed nutrients in corn-SBM diets were within the formulated values, except for ash, which tended to test greater than formulated values (Table 3). In addition to analyzing the basal diets used in both experiments, experimental diets were also analyzed. Generally, zinc levels across all zinc sources increased with the formulated increase in zinc supplementation (Table 4). For phase 2 semi-purified diets, zinc levels were generally greater than formulated by 20%, except for the ZnGly and ZnAAH 5 mg/kg diets, which had 127% and 122% increases, respectively, and the ZnSO4 10 mg/kg diet, which had a 134% increase, compared to the formulated levels. Growth performance and tibia zinc – experiment 1

When evaluating main effects, zinc level significantly affected BWG, FI, FCR, and tibia zinc (Table 5 & Fig. 1). Across all sources, BWG, FI, and FCR responded linearly and quadratically to graded levels of supplemental zinc (Table 5). Meanwhile, Tibia Zn responded linearly to the graded levels of supplemental zinc regardless of the zinc source (Table 5). BWG, FI, and Tibia Zn significantly increased until 15 mg/kg of zinc supplementation (Fig. 1). In addition, there was an observed zinc source by level tendency for tibia zinc and FCR (Table 5, P = 0.08). When evaluating pre-planned contrasts, all the Zn-supplemented treatments significantly differed from the negative control Zn-deficient treatment, validating the depletion-repletion model. ZnAAH and ZnGly organic minerals had a significantly higher tibia zinc and lower FCR compared to ZnSO4 (Table 5 and Fig. 2). ZnGly improved FCR by 1.5 points and tended to increase tibia zinc by 24 mg/kg (∼11.7%) compared to ZnSO4 (Fig. 2 and Table 5; P = 0.05, P = 0.07, respectively). While ZnAAH tended to increase tibia zinc by 22 mg/kg (10.7%) compared to ZnSO4 (Fig. 2 and Table 5, P = 0.07, respectively). Supplemental zinc levels were adjusted to correct for the effect of zinc supplementation on increasing FI (Table 5, Fig. 1). Body weight gain data followed a broken-line model (R2=0.66), plateauing at 11.7 mg/kg of supplemental zinc intake (Fig. 3a). Relative Bioavailability – Experiment 1

Relative bioavailability of zinc sources using BWG and tibia Zn was assessed using both linear and non-linear regression analyses. For the linear model, all zinc sources, both organic and zinc sulfate, had equivalent BWG RBV (Fig. 4a and Table 6). However, the nonlinear model showed that both organic sources have a significantly greater BWG RBV compared to zinc sulfate (Fig. 4b and Table 6). Tibia zinc increased linearly with zinc level and regardless of source Fig. 3b; therefore, all zinc levels were included in both linear and nonlinear regression analyses. The linear regression model had a greater R² than the nonlinear model for the tibia Zn data, indicating a better fit (0.48 Vs. 0.41, respectively Fig. 5). Both models showed that, on average, organic trace minerals have a significantly greater relative bioavailability than zinc sulfate based on tibia zinc data (+42% and +48% RBV for linear and nonlinear models, respectively, Table 6). Effect of zinc source-by-level interaction on growth performance, carcass yield and tibia and excreta zinc concentration – Experiment 2

The interaction between zinc source and supplementation level did not affect BWG or FCR across all phases, nor did it influence phase 1 FI (P > 0.10, Table 7). However, a significant interaction was observed for FI during phase 2 and overall, primarily driven by the ZnMHA-60 treatment with reduced FI (P < 0.05), and the ZnSO4-60 treatment with increased FI compared to NC (P < 0.10; Table 7). Contrasts revealed that supplementation with ZnGly-60 and ZnSO4-60 significantly increased BWG during phase 2 and overall compared to the NC (P < 0.05), while overall FCR was significantly reduced in birds fed ZnGly-60 and ZnMHA-60 (Table 7, P < 0.05). During phase 1, feeding ZnAAH-60 increased FI compared to NC (P < 0.05, Table 7). ZnMHA-60 fed birds had the highest phase 1 FCR compared to the NC (P < 0.05). The interaction did not affect overall adj-FCR. However, contrasts revealed that both ZnSO4 and ZnGly significantly reduced adj-FCR at 10 and 60 mg/kg level compared to NC (P < 0.05, Table 7). A significant interaction effect was noted for Day 43 processing live weights, mainly driven by ZnMHA-60, which reduced average weights compared to other zinc sources supplemented at 60 mg/kg (Table 8). Zinc source-by-level interaction significantly affected zinc concentration in D40 excreta, with ZnMHA-60 having the highest concentration among other zinc sources supplemented at 60 mg/kg (Table 8).

Discussion

Across both experiments, analyzed zinc levels increased with supplemental zinc, though some variability was observed. This variation likely reflects differences in chemical composition, ligand type, and zinc concentration among sources added at mg/kg levels (Romeo et al., 2014). Zinc bioavailability has been extensively studied using purified (Wedekind et al., 1992), semi-purified (Batal et al., 2001; Baker and Ammerman, 1995), and practical diets, with or without pharmacologic zinc dosing, and evaluated through biomarkers such as bone zinc and body weight gain (BWG) (Sandoval et al., 1997; Star et al., 2012). In the present study, RBV estimates indicated that ZnGly and ZnAAH were more bioavailable than ZnSO₄ under semi-purified depletion-repletion conditions, particularly when tibia zinc concentration was used as a response criterion. In contrast, BWG-based RBV differences were smaller, consistent with previous observations that growth responses are less sensitive than tissue biomarkers for distinguishing zinc source bioavailability once requirements are met (Suo, et al., 2015).

Tibia zinc concentration increased linearly with supplemental zinc, indicating continued mineral deposition beyond the zinc level required to maximize growth performance. Similar findings have been reported in broilers, where zinc requirement for tissue mineralization exceeded those required for BWG optimization (de Carvalho et al., 2025). Broken-line regression analysis estimated BWG breakpoint at approximately 11.7 mg/kg supplemental zinc, which is consistent with the 13.6 mg/kg estimate reported by Batal et al. (2001) using semi-purified diets. These findings suggest that despite substantial genetic improvements in modern broilers, physiological zinc requirements for growth remain relatively stable under controlled deficiency conditions. Considering the zinc contribution from basal diet and its estimated relative bioavailability (Edwards and Baker, 2000), the total bioavailable zinc supply required to support optimal BWG was approximately 26.4 mg/kg.

The greater RBV estimates observed for ZnGly and ZnAAH may be attributed to the physicochemical characteristics of amino acid–chelated zinc sources. Inorganic zinc salts such as ZnSO₄ readily dissociate in the gastrointestinal tract, increasing the likelihood of interactions with phytate, calcium, and other dietary antagonists that reduce zinc availability (Batal et al., 2001; Richards et al., 2010). In contrast, amino acid–chelated zinc sources may remain partially protected during digestion, thereby limiting such interactions and facilitating absorption via alternative pathways associated with amino acid or peptide transport, in addition to conventional zinc transporter systems (Yu et al., 2017; Hu et al., 2022; Richards et al., 2010). Glycine-chelated zinc complexes possess relatively low molecular weight and a neutral or near-neutral structure due to bidentate coordination with glycine, forming a stable ring that reduces mineral reactivity in the gastrointestinal tract (Ashmead, 2012). This structural stability may enhance intestinal uptake by preserving zinc in a more bioavailable form. Consistent with this mechanism, organic zinc sources in broilers have been shown to improve zinc absorption and upregulate zinc transporters (ZnT, ZIP) as well as amino acid transport systems, indicating the involvement of multiple uptake pathways beyond ionic zinc absorption (Hu et al., 2022). Although poultry-specific data for zinc glycinate absorption mechanism remain limited, rodent studies demonstrate that Zn–glycine chelate modulates intestinal zinc transporters and peptide transporter PepT1, supporting a conserved carrier-mediated absorption mechanism (Huang et al., 2016; Schlegel and Windisch, 2006). Collectively, these findings suggest that amino acid-chelated zinc may exhibit greater bioavailability than inorganic zinc sources, although the relative contribution of transport mechanisms and ligand stability likely depends on intestinal conditions and diet composition.

Despite greater RBV estimates observed for organic zinc sources in Experiment 1, both ZnSO4 and ZnGly supported comparable growth performance under practical feeding conditions in Experiment 2. These findings highlight that RBV estimates derived from controlled deficiency models may not fully predict growth performance responses under commercial-type feeding conditions. Practical corn-soybean meal diets contain greater concentrations of phytate, fiber, calcium, and other zinc-binding compounds that alter zinc availability and digestive dynamics compared to semi-purified diets (Ao et al., 2007). Additionally, practical diets supplemented with phytase may partially alleviate antagonistic effects associated with inorganic zinc sources by liberating phytate-bound minerals, thereby reducing differences among zinc sources (Dersjant-Li et al., 2025). Under these conditions, ZnSO4 may still provide sufficient bioavailable zinc to meet or exceed physiological requirements, minimizing performance differences relative to organic sources.

Experiment 2 demonstrated that zinc supplementation improved growth performance, feed efficiency, and, to a lesser extent, carcass yield relative to NC, with ZnSO4 and ZnGly supporting the most consistent responses across production phases. While both sources provided comparable improvements in BWG and adjusted FCR, ZnGly was associated with improved feed efficiency and hot carcass yield compared to NC. Previous studies showed that glycine-complexed trace minerals and particularly zinc glycinate improved broiler slaughter yields (Kwiecień et al., 2016; De Marco et al., 2017). The observed improvement in feed efficiency may be related to differences in zinc availability at the intestinal level, where chelated forms such as ZnGly may reduce interactions with dietary antagonists and support more efficient absorption and metabolic utilization. In turn, improved zinc status can enhance intestinal integrity, enzyme activity, and endocrine signaling pathways involved in nutrient metabolism, as zinc plays a key role in maintaining gut barrier function and supporting metabolic enzyme systems (Bao and Choct, 2009; Perez et al., 2017). Similarly, modest improvements in carcass yield may reflect enhanced protein accretion and nutrient partitioning (Ogbuewu et al., 2023), as zinc supports muscle development by regulating cellular proliferation and activating signaling pathways involved in protein synthesis including PI3K-Akt-mTOR axis, which is a central regulator of muscle growth in poultry (Xu and Velleman, 2023; Hu et al., 2024). However, variability in carcass responses reported in the literature indicates that these effects remain influenced by diet composition, genetics, and production conditions (Olukosi et al., 2018; Qudsieh et al., 2018).

ZnMHA supplementation was associated with reduced feed intake and BWG in several comparisons. These responses may partially reflect formulation adjustments that accounted primarily for zinc contribution without fully considering the nutritional contribution of the methionine hydroxy analog ligand (Supplemental Table 1). Methionine hydroxy analog sources exhibit lower biological efficacy relative to DL-methionine (Lemme and Dorigam, 2024), and excessive sulfur amino acid supply may alter amino acid balance, palatability, or nutrient metabolism. Similar reductions in broiler growth performance have been reported when methionine hydroxy analog sources replaced DL-methionine (Kim et al., 2019). Future studies should evaluate ZnMHA using formulations that either fully account for or intentionally exclude methionine hydroxy analog nutrient contributions to better isolate zinc source effects.

Excreta zinc concentration increased with increasing zinc supplementation across all sources, confirming greater dietary zinc intake and excretion. However, some organic zinc treatments exhibited numerically lower excreta zinc concentrations than ZnMHA at high supplementation levels, suggesting potential differences in zinc retention efficiency. Reduced mineral excretion may have practical environmental importance because improving zinc utilization efficiency could reduce zinc output from commercial poultry production systems (Nguyen et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2018).

Overall, the present experiments demonstrated that zinc source and supplementation level influence broiler growth performance, feed efficiency, tissue mineralization, and carcass characteristics. Organic zinc sources exhibited greater RBV than ZnSO₄ under semi-purified depletion–repletion conditions, particularly when tibia zinc concentration was used as the response criterion. Under practical feeding conditions, however, ZnSO₄ and ZnGly produced comparable improvements in BWG, while ZnGly supported favorable feed efficiency and carcass responses in several comparisons. These findings indicate that RBV estimates generated under controlled experimental conditions may not directly translate into proportional performance advantages in practical diets. Future research should further investigate zinc source effects on carcass yield, footpad health, immunity, oxidative status, welfare, and environmental zinc excretion to define optimal supplementation strategies under commercial conditions.
