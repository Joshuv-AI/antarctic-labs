Reemergence of the Epidemic Threshold in SIRS Infections on Connected Stars
Andreas Göbel ∗, Nicolas Klodt ∗, Martin S. Krejca †

Abstract
The SIRS process is a continuous-time process for how infections spread on a graph. In this model, each vertex is in one of the following three states: susceptible (to the infection; S), infected (I), or recovered (R) and thus immune to the infection. For each vertex, the transition among these states is exponentially distributed according to the parameters of the process. It was recently shown that recovered vertices effectively stop the infection on stars, that is, the expected survival time of SIRS processes on stars is bounded from above by a polynomial in the number of the vertices, independently of the infection rate of the process. The setting where the process has, so far, been shown to exhibit epidemic behavior, i.e., super-polynomial survival time when the infection rate is above some threshold value, requires the host graph to be an expander. This is in contrast to the shown behavior of the well-studied SIS process, a related model in which vertices never transition to R, and in which even sparsely connected graphs, in particular stars, exhibit epidemic behavior. In this work, we show that expansion of the host graph is not a necessary condition for the SIRS process to result in an epidemic. Our main technical contribution shows that, while the SIRS process does not survive super-polynomially long on a single star, it does so on a network of poly-logarithmic (in the total number of vertices) stars of polynomial size. In addition, we show that such substructures appear in popular complex network models, providing for each a bound on the epidemic threshold. In particular, on hyperbolic random graphs, we compare our threshold for connected stars with the previously known one based on expansion, finding that both of them can be more permissive depending on the graph’s power-law exponent and the rate that determines how long immunity lasts.

Introduction

A great amount of processes in the real world follows similar core mechanics that are well modeled as a diffusion process on graphs. This includes information diffusion
[
28
,
15
,
21
,
27
,
25
,
26
]
, rumor spreading
[
16
]
, infections
[
23
,
20
]
, and computer viruses
[
3
,
4
]
. The core dynamics are well described by multiple processes defined in epidemiology
[
23
]
. The most common processes are extensions of the so called
SI process
. In this model, vertices are in one of two states:
susceptible (S)
or
infected (I)
. The states change following a continuous-time Markov chain on an underlying graph in which the infection randomly spreads with an
infection rate

\lambda
across edges.

The SI process spawned several well-known extensions, such as the SIS process (or
contact process
) and the SIRS process. The SIS process introduces reinfection, meaning that infected vertices become susceptible again at a rate commonly normalized to 1. The SIRS model also allows reinfection, but previously infected vertices enter a
recovered (R)
state first, in which they are fully immune to the infection. They transition back into the susceptible state at a rate of
\varrho
, called the
deimmunization rate
. For both the SIS and the SIRS process, the only absorbing state is the one in which every vertex is susceptible. The random time until the process (SIS or SIRS) reaches its absorbing state is called
survival time
and is of particular interest, as it often exhibits a threshold behavior: in a very short interval of the values of
\lambda
the expected survival time changes from being logarithmic in the graph size to super-polynomial. The region where this occurs is called the
epidemic threshold
.
An important line of research aims at understanding how properties of the host graph influence the existence and the placement of the epidemic threshold.

The epidemic threshold for the SIS process has been studied extensively both empirically
[
8
,
9
]
and theoretically
[
22
,
4
,
11
]
. In particular,
[
11
]
give an upper bound on the epidemic threshold based on the expansion properties of the host graph. Interestingly, this also includes graphs with very bad expansion. For example, on stars with
n
leaves, the infection already survives super-polynomially long when for an arbitrary constant
\varepsilon>0
, the infection rate
\lambda
is of order
n^{-1/2+\varepsilon}
.
[
3
]
use this result in order to show super-polynomial survival times for preferential-attachment graphs, which are scale-free and therefore follow a power-law degree distribution. That is, they contain vertices of very high degree, and if the infection reaches these vertices, then it survives super-polynomially long in their neighborhood.

For the epidemic threshold for the SIRS process, there are many empirical results
[
29
,
18
,
9
]
as well as results that use simplifying assumptions such as mean-field approaches
[
24
,
1
]
. On the theoretical side however, the results are quite sparse.
[
10
]
give a bound for the epidemic threshold based on the expansion property of a (sub-)graph similar to the bound for the SIS process. However, they also show, that infections on stars never survive super-polynomially long when
\varrho
is assumed to be constant, regardless of the choice of
\lambda
. Recently,
[
19
]
and
[
13
]
independently showed that a polynomial expected survival time is achieved above the same threshold at which it becomes super-polynomial for the SIS process. The polynomial then keeps growing for larger
\lambda
until it stops once
\lambda
is constant. This implies that large star subgraphs alone are not sufficient in the SIRS model to show super-polynomial survival time thresholds.

In combination, these results suggest that recovered vertices have a big influence on graphs with very few paths between different vertices, whereas the impact of recovered vertices on graphs with many paths between each pair of vertices is far less pronounced.
However, since stars and expanders are structurally very far apart, it remains, up to date, unclear whether an epidemic threshold emerges for the SIRS process already for graphs with a far smaller degree of connectedness than present in expanders.

1.1
Our contribution

In this work, we identify a latent structure, namely
connected stars
, of the host graph that results in an epidemic threshold for the SIRS process that requires far fewer connections between vertices than expanders.
Connected stars
are multiple stars whose centers induce a connected component, e.g., a path.
We furthermore show that connected stars are present in many random-graph models that follow a power-law degree distribution.
Since such a distribution has been observed in a broad range of real-world networks
[
2
]
, this suggests that connected stars are present in many real-world graphs, effectively resulting in an epidemic threshold for the SIRS process similar to that for the SIS process.

In more detail, we show for graphs with
n
vertices that having
\lfloor\ln^{2}(n)\rfloor
stars with
\upOmega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{c}}}}\right)
leaves each and whose centers form a connected component results in a lower bound of the expected SIRS survival time of order
n^{\ln(n)}
(
Corollary

3.2
).
More generally, we derive a lower bound for the expected SIRS survival time on graphs that have a subgraph of
k
connected stars with
\ell
leaves each (
Theorem

3.1
).

We note that our result from
Corollary

3.2
is reasonably tight, as any constant number of connected stars still results, for constant deimmunization rates and arbitrary infection rates, in an at most polynomial expected SIRS survival time.
This follows from the upper bound of the SIRS survival time on single stars by
[
10
]
, which uses that it only takes a polynomial time until the star center stays resistant for a longer time than it takes for all other vertices to lose their infection. This can be extended to show a polynomial upper bound for graphs with a constant-size vertex cover by bounding the time until vertices lose the infection while all of the vertices in the vertex cover stay resistant.

In
Section

4
, we show that many real-world graph models contain connected stars as subgraphs. The main property of the graph models that we use is that they are scale-free, meaning that their degree distribution follows a power-law. This ensures that the graphs contain many high-degree vertices that fill the role of the star centers. Note that in a power-law distribution, the
\ln^{2}(n)
highest-degree vertices likely all have a degree that is only smaller than the largest degree by a poly-logarithmic factor. Hence, by considering multiple stars, the individual stars do not become substantially smaller.

In many scale-free graphs, the highest-degree vertices tend to be well connected. We make that explicit in
Section

4.2
for hyperbolic random graphs, geometric inhomogeneous random graphs, and Chung-Lu graphs with power-law kernel. Thus,
Corollary

3.2
is directly applicable to these random graph models, yielding respective survival time bounds (
Corollary

4.1
). Similar results can be obtained on any scale-free graph as long as it can be shown that the highest degree vertices likely contain a connected subgraph.

Last, we compare our epidemic threshold for scale-free graphs from
Corollary

4.1
to a threshold by
[
10
]
that is based on expander subgraphs. Either threshold depends on the power-law exponent
\gamma
and the deimmunization rate
\varrho
of the SIRS process. Generally, our threshold is better than the expansion threshold once
\gamma
is sufficiently large, as then the central clique becomes very small. The exact point where it becomes better changes based on
\varrho
. Our threshold is better for larger values of
\varrho
and becomes worse for small
\varrho
, while the expander threshold does not depend on
\varrho
at all.

2
The SIRS Process and Complex Graph Network Models

We first introduce general notation before we define the SIRS process formally.
Then, we define important graph classes that we study.
Some mathematical tools that are important to our analysis can be found in
Appendix

A
.
We note that our notation for SIRS processes follows
[
10
]
in order to allow for an easier comparison of our results to theirs.

General notation and conventions.

For all
a,b\in\mathds{N}
, we define
[a..b]=[a,b]\cap\mathds{N}
.
Graphs in this work are simple and undirected, and
all of our big-O notation refers to asymptotics in the number of vertices
n
.
We say that an event
A
occurs
asymptotically almost surely
(a.a.s.) if and only if
\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{A}}}\right]=1-\mathrm{o}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({1}}}\right)
. If
\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{\overline{A}}}}\right]
is super-polynomially small, we say it occurs
with high probability
(w.h.p.).

A
Poisson process of rate
r\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
is a homogeneous Poisson point process that outputs almost surely a (random) countably infinite subset of
\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
where the smallest output as well as the difference between two neighboring outputs each follow an exponential distribution with parameter
r
, independent of any other random events.

SIRS processes.

An SIRS process runs on a graph
G=(V,E)
and is parametrized by an
infection rate

\lambda\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
and a
deimmunization rate

\varrho\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
.
These two rates determine the speed at which vertices change from S to I and from R to S, respectively, noting that the rate from I to R is normalized to
1
.

Each edge
e\in E
has a corresponding Poisson process
M_{e}
of rate
\lambda
, and each vertex
v\in V
has two corresponding Poisson processes
N_{v}
and
O_{v}
with rate
1
and
\varrho
, respectively. We call these processes
clocks
, and when a time point
t\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
is part of a clock’s output, we say that the clock
triggers
at
t
. All of the clocks are independent. Note that, almost surely, no two clocks trigger at the same time. Let
\{\gamma_{i}\}_{i\in\mathds{N}}
with
\gamma_{0}=0
denote the (random) sequence of all these triggers.

We define an SIRS process
(C_{t})_{t\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}}
to be a process that partitions
V
for all time points
t\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
into the set
S_{t}^{\prime}
of
susceptible
vertices, the set
I_{t}^{\prime}
of
infected
vertices, and the set
R_{t}^{\prime}
of
recovered
vertices. That is,
C_{t}=(S_{t}^{\prime},I_{t}^{\prime},R_{t}^{\prime})
. The value of
C_{0}
is part of the input of the process. The other values are defined inductively and only change when clocks trigger. That is, for all
i\in\mathds{N}
, the process
C
is constant on
[\gamma_{i},\gamma_{i+1})
.
The transitions are defined for all
i\in\mathds{N}
and
s\in[\gamma_{i},\gamma_{i+1})
as follows:

•

Susceptible to infected.

Let
e=\{u,v\}\in E
with
\gamma_{i+1}\in M_{e}
and
u\in I_{s}^{\prime}
as well as
v\in S_{s}^{\prime}
.
Then for all
t\in[\gamma_{i+1},\gamma_{i+2})
, we have
u,v\in I_{t}^{\prime}
.
We say that
u
infects
v
(at time
\gamma_{i+1}
).

•

Infected to recovered.

Let
v\in V
with
\gamma_{i+1}\in N_{v}
and
v\in I_{s}^{\prime}
.
Then for all
t\in[\gamma_{i+1},\gamma_{i+2})
, we have
v\in R_{t}^{\prime}
.
We say that
v
recovers
(at time
\gamma_{i+1}
).

•

Recovered to susceptible.

Let
v\in V
with
\gamma_{i+1}\in O_{v}
and
v\in R_{s}^{\prime}
.
Then for all
t\in[\gamma_{i+1},\gamma_{i+2})
, we have
v\in S_{t}^{\prime}
.
We say that
v
becomes susceptible
or that it
loses its immunity
(at time
\gamma_{i+1}
).

No transitions besides the ones above occur. Note that some triggers do not result in a change of state. Hence, in the remaining paper, we only consider times at which state changes occur. Formally, we consider
\{\gamma_{0}\}\cup\{\gamma_{i}\mid i\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}\land C_{\gamma_{i}}\neq C_{\gamma_{i-1}}\}
, which we index by the increasing sequence
\{\tau_{i}\}_{i\in\mathds{N}}
. For all
i\in\mathds{N}
, we call
\tau_{i}
the
i
-th
step
of
C
.

The focus of our analysis is the
survival time

T\coloneqq\inf\{t\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}\mid I_{t}^{\prime}=\emptyset\}
of
C
, and we say that the infection
dies out
or
goes extinct
at
T
. When
T
is in expectation super-polynomial in
n
, we say that the infection becomes
epidemic
. The
epidemic threshold
is the infimum of the regime for the infection rate
\lambda
in that the infection becomes epidemic.

In most lemmas and theorems, we consider a process starting at some given time
t\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
. In all these cases, we consider
t
to be some stopping time with respect to the natural filtration of the SIRS process. That is,
t
is chosen without any knowledge of how the process behaves in the future. As the SIRS process changes based on Poisson processes, which are memoryless, the behavior also does not depend on the past and is only dependent on the state of the system at time
t
.

Important graph classes.

A
star
is a graph over
m\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
vertices with one distinct vertex (the
center
) of degree
m-1
, and all other vertices (the
leaves
) with degree
1
.

Given
k,\ell\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
, a concept central to our analysis is a graph that consists of exactly
k
stars, each with exactly
\ell
leaves.
We call such a graph a
(k,\ell)
-star
.
If
k
and
\ell
are not specified, we may also refer to such a graph as a
disjointed star
.
Moreover, if the
k
centers induce a connected component, we call the graph a
(k,\ell)
-connected-star
, or just a
connected star
if
k
and
\ell
are not specified.

We show in
Section

4
that disjointed stars are w.h.p. subgraphs in graphs known as
random scale-free graphs
, whose degree distributions follow a power-law.
Formally, given a constant
power-law exponent
\gamma\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
, a random scale-free graph is a random graph
G
over
n\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
vertices such that, w.h.p., for each degree
d\in[1..n]
, the fraction of vertices in
G
with degree
d
is proportional to
d^{-\gamma}
.
We note
\gamma>2
implies that the average degree of
G
is constant, and we obtain the exact fraction of vertices with degree
d
by multiplying
d^{-\gamma}
with a constant
C\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
.
However,
C
is not essential to our results as it is absorbed by the big-O notation, which is why we usually omit it.

We are interested in three common random scale-free graph models, which we show contain w.h.p. connected stars of a decent size.
We define these three models in the following in the level of detail that is sufficient for our purposes.
For more information, we refer to the references that we give.

Hyperbolic random graphs

[
17
]
(HRGs) are generated by distributing points uniformly at random on a hyperbolic disk of radius
R\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
and then connecting two vertices if and only if they are within distance
R
of each other.
[
14
, Theorem 2.2]
showed that the degree distribution of HRGs follows a power-law with exponent
\gamma\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
that depends on the model parameters. The degree of vertices follows a binomial distribution around its expected degree, where vertices closer to the center have a higher degree
[
14
, Theorem 3.2]
. All the vertices at distance at most
R/2
from the center form a clique. This distance implies, for some constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
, an expected degree of at least
c\sqrt{n}
while vertices further away have a smaller degree than that.

Chung–Lu graphs

[
6
]
are obtained by defining an expected-degree sequence and then connecting each pair of vertices independently with probability proportional to the product of their expected degrees
[
6
]
. This assures that the chosen expected degrees are indeed the expectation of the degree in the graph. The actual degree is binomially distributed with this mean. We are interested in the case where the expected-degree sequence follows a power-law with exponent
\gamma\in(2,3)
. In the definition, the probability of two vertices being connected is capped at one, and for
\gamma\in(2,3)
, two vertices with a product of their expected degrees that is bigger than the expected number of edges in the graph are connected with probability
1
. In particular, there exists a constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that all vertices with expected degree at least
c\sqrt{n}
are connected to each other.

Geometric inhomogeneous random graphs

[
5
]
(GIRGs) are a modification of Chung–Lu graphs that add a geometric component. Each vertex has a position in the
d
-dimensional Torus in addition to its expected degree. Vertices are more likely to connect to each other the closer they are. However, in regards to the properties considered in this paper, GRIGs behave the same as Chung–Lu graphs. The degree distribution of GIRGs follows a power-law with exponent
\gamma
, typically assumed to be between
2
and
3

[
5
, Theorem 2.1]
. Moreover, the degree of each vertex follows a binomial distribution around the mean based on their weight. Furthermore, vertices whose expected degrees multiply to more than the expected number of edges in the graph are by definition always connected. Hence, there again exists a constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that all vertices with expected degree at least
c\sqrt{n}
are connected to each other.

3
Lower Bound of the SIRS Survival Time on Connected Stars

Our main result is
Theorem

3.1
, which proves a general lower bound of the SIRS survival time on
k\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
connected stars.
The bound relies heavily on a probability
p\in(0,1)
, which is the probability of the infection dying out in a single star in a time interval of constant length.
Whenever a star obtains sufficiently many infected vertices, we say it becomes
active
. It becomes
inactive
again when none of its vertices are infected.
If the infection rate
\lambda\in\upomega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({kp}}}\right)
, currently inactive stars become activated faster than active stars become inactive, leading to an expected survival time exponential in
k
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
k,\ell\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
, and let
G
be a graph over
n\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
vertices with a
(k,\ell)
-connected-star as a subgraph. Moreover, let
\varepsilon\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
be a constant, and let
C
be an SIRS process on
G
with infection rate
\lambda\in(0,1]
such that
\lambda\in\upOmega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\ell^{-1/2+\varepsilon}}}}\right)
and with constant deimmunization rate
\varrho\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
that starts with one of the star centers infected and all other vertices susceptible. Let
T
be the survival time of
C
. Then there is a
p\in\upTheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({(\lambda^{2}\ell)^{-(1-\varepsilon)\varrho}}}}\right)
such that
\lambda\in\upomega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({kp}}}\right)
implies that
\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T}}}\right]\in\upOmega\big({(kp)^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda/(kp)}}\right)^{k-1}}\big)
.

Note that the condition
\lambda\in\upomega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({kp}}}\right)
is only an implicit bound on
\lambda
as
p
depends on
\lambda
. Rearranging the condition to be explicit yields
\lambda\in\upomega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{k}{\ell^{(1-\varepsilon)\varrho}}}}\right)^{1/(1+2(1-\varepsilon)\varrho)}}}}\right)
.

Furthermore, note that in
Theorem

3.1
, the infection rate
\lambda
is upper-bounded by
1
. The reason is that we use in the proof that in a single star, the number of infected leaves tends to go towards
\lambda n
, which is not possible for
\lambda>1
as the number of infected leaves is upper bounded by
n
. However, throughout all the proofs, we only ever use
\lambda
as a lower bound for the infection rate. Hence,
Theorem

3.1
is also applicable for all
\lambda>1
, resulting in the same (static) bound as for
\lambda=1
.

The survival time lower bound from
Theorem

3.1
is exponential in
k
with
\frac{\lambda}{kp}
in the base. Hence, if a graph with
n\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
vertices contains a super-constant number of stars in the
(k,\ell)
-star and if
\lambda
is bigger than
kp
by a polynomial in
n
, the lower bound for the expected survival time is super-polynomial. This is in contrast to the results of
[
10
]
, where the authors showed that on a single star, an SIRS infection never survives super-polynomially long when the deimmunization rate is constant.

Corollary

3.2
makes this super-polynomial lower bound explicit by choosing common values for
k
and
\ell
in
Theorem

3.1
. We show in
Section

4
that many real-world graph models contain connected stars of the size chosen in
Corollary

3.2
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
, let
c\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
be a constant, and let
G
be a graph over
n
vertices that contains a
(\lfloor\ln^{2}(n)\rfloor,\upOmega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{c}}}}\right))
-connected-star as subgraph. Moreover, let
\varepsilon\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
be a constant, and let
C
be an SIRS process on
G
with constant deimmunization rate
\varrho\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
and infection rate
\lambda\in(0,1]
such that
\lambda\in\upOmega\big({n^{-\frac{c}{2+1/\varrho}+\varepsilon}}\big)
that starts with one of the star centers infected and all other vertices susceptible. Let
T
be the survival time of
C
. Then
\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T}}}\right]\in\upOmega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{\ln(n)}}}}\right)
.

Note that using similar calculations also give a survival time of order
n^{n^{\varepsilon}}
when choosing
k=n^{\varepsilon}
. This increases the threshold for
\lambda
slightly. However, in this paper we focus on the result in
Corollary

3.2
.

The threshold for
\lambda
depends on
\varrho
, which it normally does not in previous results on this model. In
Section

4.3
, we compare how strong the threshold is on hyperbolic random graphs compared to previously known bounds.

In the following, we outline the proof of
Theorem

3.1
and discuss the main lemmas that we use in order to prove it before we provide more detailed proof ideas for the main lemmas in
Sections

3.1
and
3.2
. The full proofs of all of them are given in
Appendix

B
.

Proof idea for
Theorem

3.1
.

We abstract the process
C
into a meta process
A
that only considers the
(k,\ell)
-connected-star, which we call
G^{\star}
in the following. Each star of
G^{\star}
is a vertex in
A
, and it is either active or inactive. Each star of
G^{\star}
becomes active once its center is infected and it infects enough of its leaves. The star becomes inactive again when no vertex in it is infected anymore. The corresponding vertex in
A
is active if and only if the star in
G^{\star}
is active.

The meta process
A
behaves roughly like an SIS process with parameters that depend on the parameters of
C
and the number of leaves in the stars. In our analysis, we focus on the number of active vertices in
A
. Discretizing
A
with respect to the changes of the number of active vertices yields a random process
X\coloneqq(X_{t})_{t\in\mathds{N}}
that increases or decreases by one in each step. Our goal is to show that this random variable dominates a biased gambler’s ruin instance and to then use the expected survival time of this gambler’s ruin instance (
Theorem

A.1
) as a lower bound for the original process. To this end, we bound the probability of
X
increasing and decreasing.

The process
X
decreases whenever one of the stars becomes inactive. In order to bound how often this happens, we show a lower bound on the distribution of how long the stars survive on their own (
Lemma

3.5
). This bound is a generalization of the known lower bound of the expected survival time of single stars in the SIRS model by
[
12
]
.
We discuss details in
Section

3.1
.

The process
X
increases whenever a star becomes active. This happens when the infected center of an active star infects the center of an inactive star and this center then infects enough leaves. As the star centers form a connected component, whenever there is at least one active and one inactive star, there are also centers of one active and one inactive star that are connected. In
Lemma

3.6
we lower-bound the probability of the inactive star being infected in a fixed time interval of constant length.
The details are given in
Section

3.2
.

Last, in the proof of
Theorem

3.1
in
Appendix

B
, we combine the bounds on how long it takes to increase and decrease
X
, lower-bounding the probability of an increase in each step. This implies a domination of the gambler’s ruin instance and results in a lower bound on the expected survival time of
X
. In order to transform this into a lower bound of the continuous meta process, we reverse the discretization by lower-bounding the expected time each decrease of
X
takes in the meta process. Noting that the meta process survives at most as long as the original process concludes the proof.

The meta process.

The definition of the meta process is based on the notion of an
active star

We analyze the probability that an active star

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
\varepsilon\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
be a constant, and let

be a hyperbolic random graph, geometric inhomogeneous random graph, or Chung–Lu graph with

4.1
Disjointed Stars in Scale-free Graphs

We show that scale-free graphs contain w.h.p. a
(k,\ell)
-star for

and

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(cn/\ln(n)^{2}}}\right)^{1/(\gamma-1)}

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
G
be a random scale-free graph with
n\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
vertices that follows a power-law degree distribution with power-law exponent
\gamma\in\mathds{R}_{>2}
. Then there exists a constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that w.h.p.
G
contains
\lfloor\ln(n)^{2}\rfloor
vertices with a degree of at least
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(cn/\ln(n)^{2}}}\right)^{1/(\gamma-1)}

Proof.

This result follows directly from the definition of a power-law degree distribution. As the degree distribution of
G
follows a power-law with exponent
\gamma\in\mathds{R}_{>2}
, the sum of all degrees of vertices in the graph is linear in
n
. Hence, for all
d\in[1..n]
, by summing the number of vertices with degree
i\in[d..n]
, there is a constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that the number of vertices of degree at least

is w.h.p. at least

d^{\prime}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(cn/\lfloor\ln(n)^{2}\rfloor}}\right)^{1/(\gamma-1)}
yields that w.h.p. there are at least
F(d^{\prime})=cn(d^{\prime})^{-(\gamma-1)}=cn\frac{\lfloor\ln(n)^{2}\rfloor}{cn}=\lfloor\ln(n)^{2}\rfloor
vertices of degree at least

in
G
.
∎

Next, we show that a graph

with

(k,\lfloor d/k\rfloor-1)

\lfloor d/k\rfloor-1
of their neighbors as leaves.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
k\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
and

, and let

be a graph that contains

Proof.

Let

of

4.2
Connected Stars in HRGs, GIRGs, and Chung–Lu Graphs

We apply

to HRGs, GIRGs, and Chung–Lu graphs with power-law kernel. Using

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
G
be a hyperbolic random graph, geometric inhomogeneous random graph, or Chung–Lu graph with
n\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
vertices whose degree distribution follows a power-law with exponent
\gamma\in(2,3)
. Then there exists a constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that for
k=\lfloor\ln(n)^{2}\rfloor
and
\ell=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(cn/\ln(n)^{2\gamma}}}\right)^{1/(\gamma-1)}

G
contains a
(k,\ell)

Proof.

By

4.4
, there exists a constant

such that for
k=\lfloor\ln(n)^{2}\rfloor
and
\ell=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(cn/\ln(n)^{2\gamma}}}\right)^{1/(\gamma-1)}
, w.h.p.

contains a
(k,\ell)
-star as a subgraph. Note that as

, there exists a constant

such that

4.3
Comparison of the Epidemic Threshold Between Expander Graphs and Hyperbolic Random Graphs

Corollary

4.1
states a bound for the epidemic threshold on hyperbolic random graphs, based on connected stars.
[
10
, Corollary 1.6]
also state a bound on the epidemic threshold for hyperbolic random graphs, but it is based on the expansion properties of the central clique.
For some constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
,
[
10
, Corollary 1.6]
show an upper bound on the threshold of
\lambda\geq cn^{(\gamma-3)/2}
.

In the following, we discuss for which power-law exponents which of the two results leads to a smaller (and thus more permisse) upper bound on the epidemic threshold (
Lemma

4.6
) and how this relates to the two different properties that are at the foundation of these two results. The proof of
Lemma

4.6
can be found in
Appendix

C

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
G
be a hyperbolic random graph with power-law degree exponent
\gamma\in(2,3)
. Then the epidemic threshold upper bound from the connected stars (
Corollary

4.1
) is smaller than the expander bound from
[
10
, Corollary 1.6]
if and only if
\gamma>2+(2\varrho+1)^{-1/2}
.

The value
\gamma
from
Lemma

4.6
depends on
\varrho
, which is a model parameter, and thus leads to different regimes in which which result yields a more permissive threshold.
For the sake of convenience, we call this value of
\gamma
(dependent on
\varrho
) the
meta threshold
.
In general, this meta threshold lies in between
2
and
3
, with
Corollary

4.1
being more permissive when
\gamma
is above the threshold and
[
10
, Corollary 1.6]
being more permissive otherwise.

More specifically, the bound from
Corollary

4.1
is more permissive when
\gamma
gets close enough to
3
. The reason is that when the power-law exponent approaches
3
, the number of vertices in the central clique of the hyperbolic random graph
G
approaches
0
. As
[
10
, Corollary 1.6]
makes use of the size of this central clique for the bound, its threshold becomes arbitrarily bad. In contrast,
Corollary

4.1
only needs a logarithmic number of high degree vertices, which is w.h.p. there as long as
\gamma<3
. The degree of these high-degree vertices converges to
n^{1/2}
, hence the threshold of
Corollary

4.1
does not become arbitrarily bad.

On the other hand, when
\gamma
gets close enough to
2
, the bound from
[
10
, Corollary 1.6]
becomes more permissive. The reason is that the size of the central clique approaches
n^{1/2}
which means that the threshold from
[
10
, Corollary 1.6]
approaches
n^{-1/2}
. In comparison, the threshold from
Corollary

4.1
is never as good as
n^{-1/2}
, as it is lower-bounded by
n^{-1/(2+1/\varrho)}
.

The threshold from
[
10
, Corollary 1.6]
does not depend on
\varrho
at all while the bound from
Corollary

4.1
becomes better the higher
\varrho
is. For
\varrho
approaching
0
, that is, vertices remain likely recovered for a long time, the meta threshold approaches
3
as the survival time of single stars becomes arbitrarily small, effecting the bound of
Corollary

4.1
. For
\varrho
approaching infinity, the meta threshold approaches
2
, which means that
Corollary

4.1
yields a far more permissive regime than
[
10
, Corollary 1.6]
.

Last, we note that, for
\varrho
approaching infinity, the SIRS model approaches the SIS model, in which infected vertices become directly susceptible again, skipping immunity.
In the actual SIS model, for all constants
\varepsilon\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
, a single star results already in an epidemic threshold of
\lambda\geq n^{-1/2+\varepsilon}

[
11
]
.
This is also the value that the threshold in
Corollary

3.2
approaches.

5
Outlook

While our results cover a broad range of relevant network models, we leave an extended analysis for the SIRS process of preferential-attachment graphs for future work. On such networks, it would be particularly interesting to compare the behavior of the SIRS process with that of SIS, given that stars are the key substructure that the analysis of
[
3
]
is based on, together with the stark disparity in the behavior of the two processes on stars.
We note that our results in
Section

4
consider graphs with a central clique, which preferential-attachment graphs do not feature.
Hence, additional insights are required for a mathematical analysis.

Another direction is to investigate the bound for how fast a single star among the connected ones gets infected.
With our current bound, our epidemic threshold in
Theorem

3.1
scales polynomially in the number
k
of stars, i.e., it becomes less permissive the higher
k
is.
However, as shown in
Corollary

3.2
and its proof, this effect does not change the epidemic threshold asymptotically as long as
k
is sub-polynomial.
A more fine-grained analysis may be capable to remove this dependency on
k
altogether.

We furthermore believe that the epidemic threshold can be improved by incorporating the fact that the connected stars in scale-free graph models typically form a clique.
A detailed analysis using this assumption may lead to an epidemic threshold that actually becomes more permissive the more connected stars the graph contains.

Another interesting point for future research is to consider connected stars that may share some of their leaves and are not fully disjoint from one another.
In many scale-free graph models, there is even a substantial overlap of the neighborhood of high-degree vertices.
We believe that incorporating such an overlap into the analysis should result in even more permissive bounds on the epidemic threshold.

Finally, as most previous works, we assume that the deimmunization rate
\varrho
of the SIRS process is constant. It is an interesting open problem what happens when
\varrho
depends on
n
.

Appendix A
Probabilistic tools

We bound the survival time of an SIRS process by showing that certain of its features stochastically dominate other random processes that are easier to analyze. We say that a random process
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}}

dominates
another random process
(Y_{t})_{t\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}}
if and only if there exists a coupling
(X^{\prime}_{t},Y^{\prime}_{t})_{t\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}}
between
X
and
Y
such that for all
t\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
, we have
X^{\prime}_{t}\geq Y^{\prime}_{t}
.

We use the following well-known bounds on the gambler’s ruin process in our proofs in order to bound the time until specific events occur.

Theorem A.1
(Gambler’s ruin
[
7
, page 345]
).

In the gambler’s ruin game, a player starts with some amount of money
P_{0}\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
and then repeatedly throws a coin that shows head with probability
p\in(0,1)
, independent of all other outcomes. If it shows heads, the player’s money increases by
1
, otherwise, it decreases by
1
. The game ends at time
T
when the player either reaches the lower bound
\ell\in\mathds{N}_{<P_{0}}
or the upper bound
u\in\mathds{N}_{>P_{0}}
of money.
Let
(P_{t})_{t\in\mathds{N}}
be the amount of money that a player has in a gambler’s ruin game that has a probability of

The following lemma shows how likely it is that an SIRS infection of a star infects sufficiently many leaves before the center recovers.

Appendix C
Super-Polynomial SIRS Survival Time in Scale-Free Graph Models

See

Proof.

The expander bound yields a super-polynomial expected survival time once
\lambda\geq cn^{(\gamma-3)/2}
. In contrast,

yields such an expected time once

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{(\gamma-1)(2+1/\varrho)}

\displaystyle<(\gamma-3)/2

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle(\gamma-3)(\gamma-1)

\displaystyle>-\frac{2}{2+1/\varrho}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}-4\gamma+\frac{2}{2+1/\varrho}+3

\displaystyle>0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle>2+\sqrt{1-\frac{2}{2+1/\varrho}}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle>2+\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\varrho+1}}.
