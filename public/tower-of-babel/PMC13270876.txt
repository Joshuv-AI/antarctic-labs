The HOXA gene cluster: a critical regulator in bone-related disorders
Authors: Yangyang Zhang, Lewei Zhang, Yi Li, Jia Bai, Haihong Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSkeletal homeostasis relies on the dynamic balance between bone formation and bone resorption. The disruption of this balance acts as the central pathological mechanism of multiple metabolic bone diseases including osteoporosis, and is closely correlated with the progression of various other bone-related disorders. As pivotal transcription factors regulating embryonic development and cell fate, the homeobox A (HOXA) gene family plays an essential role in skeletal physiological and pathological processes.MethodsThis review systematically summarizes recent research advances of the HOXA gene family in bone-related diseases, concludes the evolutionarily conserved regulatory patterns of HOXA members, and clarifies the molecular mechanisms by which HOXA genes mediate bone metabolic disorders and the occurrence as well as development of bone diseases.ResultsAccumulating evidence demonstrates that HOXA family members present complex functions and strong heterogeneity in bone-related diseases. They participate in the pathogenesis of bone diseases via three evolutionarily conserved regulatory manners: determining regional patterning, modulating signaling pathways, and integrating epigenetic and non-coding RNA (ncRNA) regulatory networks.ConclusionFurther exploring the underlying mechanisms of the HOXA family in bone-related diseases provides novel insights into the pathogenesis of bone disorders. Meanwhile, it also supplies solid theoretical basis and potential therapeutic targets for the development of novel HOXA-targeted therapeutic strategies against bone diseases.

Introduction

The skeleton provides structural support and regulates key processes including calcium/phosphorus metabolism and haematopoiesis [1]. Bone remodelling balances osteoblast formation and osteoclast resorption to maintain skeletal equilibrium. Equilibrium disruption causes bone loss and metabolic bone diseases [2]. Other disorders like bone tumours and osteoarthritis also impair skeletal homeostasis [3]. These diseases pose significant threats to human health. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying skeletal homeostasis imbalance is of paramount importance for developing effective prevention and treatment strategies.

Within the intricate regulatory network governing skeletal development and homeostasis, the homeobox (HOX) gene family serves as a master regulator [4]. Mammals possess 39 HOX genes organized into four clusters (A-D) [5]. Compared with other HOX clusters, the HOXA cluster exhibits the broadest expression across skeletal lineages and, via encoding transcription factors, exerts multifaceted regulatory functions throughout different stages of the skeletal lifecycle [6]. Its expression persists continuously from embryonic development through adult bone homeostasis, making HOXA a central focus in bone metabolism research. Elucidating its precise molecular mechanisms and regulatory networks is therefore of great research significance [7–10]. This review systematically summarizes current advances in the HOXA family and bone-related disorders, highlighting unresolved questions regarding its context-dependent functions, hierarchical position in bone regulatory cascades and translational potential, thereby providing novel insights into the pathogenesis and targeted therapy of bone metabolic diseases.

The HOXA family: an overview

The HOX genes form a large transcription factor family; their encoded proteins contain a conserved 61-amino-acid DNA-binding domain termed the homeodomain [11–12]. This domain binds target gene promoters/enhancers to transcriptionally regulate downstream gene expression [13]. Homeobox genes were first identified in Drosophila melanogaster. Mutations in these genes cause homeotic transformations of body segments, highlighting their essential roles in embryonic development and axial patterning [14].

The HOXA cluster, situated on the short arm of chromosome 7 (7p15.3), includes multiple members from HOXA1 to HOXA13 [15]. HOXA genes show spatiotemporal collinearity: 3′ to the 5′ chromosomal genes are sequentially expressed along the anterior–posterior axis during embryogenesis. This precise expression pattern confers a unique ‘positional identity’ to each body region, forming the molecular basis for the correct development of tissues and organs [16].

As core nodes in transcriptional networks, HOXA genes show high member specificity and context dependency. A single HOXA protein can either promote or inhibit differentiation; its function depends on cell type, developmental stage and pathological microenvironment [17]. This functional diversity arises from complex regulatory mechanisms. First, HOXA proteins rarely act alone; they form heterodimers with transcription factors such as PBX and MEIS. These interactions alter DNA-binding specificity and transcriptional activity, expanding the regulatory scope [18]. Second, HOXA gene expression itself is under precise epigenetic control, including histone modifications, DNA methylation and chromatin remodelling, which collectively determine the ‘on’ or ‘off’ state of HOXA genes in specific cells [19–21]. Additionally, non-coding RNAs, particularly long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and microRNAs (miRNAs), constitute another critical layer of regulation. They fine-tune HOXA expression at the transcriptional or post-transcriptional level through mechanisms such as molecular sponging or direct recruitment of chromatin-modifying complexes [22–23].

Given the central regulatory roles of the HOXA family in embryonic development, cell fate determination and disease processes, its functions in the highly patterned skeletal system are of significant interest. Therefore, elucidating the specific functions and mechanisms of the HOXA family in bone metabolism and related pathologies is essential for advancing our understanding of skeletal biology and developing novel therapeutic strategies.

The multidimensional molecular network of HOXA family in regulating bone metabolism

HOXA as a determinant of regional patterning in skeletal development

Conserved HOX transcription factors pattern bilaterian embryos via regulating morphogenesis and organogenesis [4,24]. Growing evidence demonstrates that HOXA genes are essential for establishing region-specific skeletal morphology and function (Figure 1). For instance, HOXA2 expression in cranial neural crest cells is essential for craniofacial skeletal patterning. Its absence leads to homeotic transformation where hyoid skeletal elements are replaced by duplicated mandibular and middle ear structures, thereby disrupting normal craniofacial development [25]. In the axial skeleton, HOXA4 deficiency causes ectopic ribs on the seventh cervical vertebra. Altered HOXA5 or HOXA6 expression – these genes define cervical–thoracic boundaries – induces cervical-to-thoracic vertebral transformation. HOXA9 loss results in vertebral anterior shift accompanied by supernumerary ribs [5,26]. This region-specific regulation underlies the morphological diversity and functional adaptation of the axial skeleton.

The HOXA gene family regulates bone development through a spatiotemporal alignment pattern.

CNCCS: cranial neural crest cells.

The HOXA gene cluster (HOXA1-HOXA13) exhibits spatiotemporal colinear expression along the cranial-caudal axis, directing region-specific skeletal formation. HOXA2 patterns the craniofacial skeleton; HOXA4-HOXA6 specifies cervical and thoracic vertebrae; and HOXA9 controls lumbar development. In the limbs, HOXA11/HOXD11 govern proximal bone formation, while HOXA13 drives distal digit development and endochondral ossification by activating RUNX2 and COL10A1 to promote chondrocyte hypertrophy and longitudinal bone growth.

HOXA genes are also indispensable for limb skeletal development. HOXA11 and HOXD11 coordinately regulate the length and morphology of proximal skeletal elements such as the ulna. In mutant mice, loss of these genes leads to an arrest in chondrocyte differentiation, resulting in skeletal shortening and deformity [27]. Meanwhile, HOXA13 directly regulates key chondrocyte hypertrophy genes such as Runx2 and Col10a1, thereby modulating growth plate development and longitudinal bone growth [28].

Notably, HOXA genes function within complex regulatory networks, exhibiting functional redundancy, particularly with paralogous genes from other clusters [5]. Genes in the same paralogous group (e.g. HOXA10 and HOXD10) can functionally compensate for each other. Overlapping expression domains across paralogous groups ensure the developmental robustness. This characteristic enables the HOXA family to flexibly meet the complex demands of development, thereby ensuring proper skeletal formation [29].

While these findings from animal models have provided a solid foundation for understanding the role of HOXA genes in skeletal development, their precise contribution to human congenital skeletal malformations remains to be further elucidated. It is important to be mindful of the subtle interspecies differences in HOX gene regulatory networks.

As an important class of transcription factors, HOXA family realizes its biological functions by interacting with the complex signaling network in cells. They act as upstream regulators or downstream effectors to integrate signaling pathways. The HOXA family and the BMP/smad signaling pathway

The bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling pathway is a well-established master regulator of osteogenic differentiation, as summarized in Table 1 and Figure 2. Studies have revealed that Smad6, a component of the negative feedback loop within the BMP pathway, interacts with Hox transcription factors, functioning as a transcriptional corepressor [31]. More importantly, HOXA proteins functionally cooperate with the BMP pathway; they co‑regulate Six2 expression in limb tendon progenitor cells. This finding suggests a close interactive network between HOXA and BMP signaling during the development of skeletal and associated tissues [32]. Furthermore, HOXA9 has been shown to directly suppress transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β, a member of the BMP superfamily)-induced osteopontin gene transcription. This indicates that HOXA members can exert precise negative regulation on the downstream effects of the BMP/TGF-β signaling pathway, thereby influencing bone matrix formation and mineralization processes [33].

The HOXA family and the wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is crucial for promoting osteoblast proliferation and differentiation while inhibiting adipogenic differentiation. A complex regulatory relationship exists between the HOXA family and this pathway, in which lncRNAs serve as important intermediaries [30]. The lncRNA HOTTIP, located at the 5′ end of the HOXA locus, plays a key role in coordinating the activation of multiple 5′ HOXA genes. HOTTIP enhances osteogenic differentiation of human BMSCs by interacting with WDR5. WDR5 binds the β-catenin promoter and activates Wnt/β-catenin signaling [34,35].

As a cis-activator of the HOXA gene cluster, the expression level of HOTTIP itself reflects the activity state of HOXA genes. This reveals that the HOXA locus can indirectly yet potently regulate Wnt signaling through the action of lncRNAs [35]. Furthermore, another lncRNA, HOXA cluster antisense RNA2 (HOXA-AS2), has also been demonstrated to positively regulate osteogenesis in mesenchymal stem cells by inactivating NF-κB signaling [36]. Given the frequent antagonistic relationship between NF-κB and Wnt signaling during osteogenic differentiation, the suppression of NF-κB by HOXA-AS2 may indirectly enhance the osteogenic effects of Wnt signaling. These findings indicate that the HOXA cluster regulates Wnt/β-catenin signaling through both encoded proteins and transcribed lncRNAs, thereby modulating bone regeneration. The HOXA family and the NF-κB signaling pathway

NF-κB signaling serves as a core mediator in inflammatory bone disorders, including rheumatoid arthritis and osteoarthritis [37]. The HOXA gene cluster, via its associated lncRNAs, contributes to the modulation of this pathway. LncRNA HOXA-AS2 enhances mesenchymal stem cell osteogenesis through NF-κB inhibition, directly implicating the HOXA locus in suppressing inflammation and promoting bone repair [36]. Additionally, lncRNA HOTTIP recruits MLL1 to epigenetically regulate TLR4 promoter methylation, consequently controlling TLR4/NF-κB axis-driven synovial cell inflammation and apoptosis [37]. Collectively, these insights position HOXA-related lncRNAs as critical upstream modulators of NF-κB, highlighting their therapeutic potential for inflammatory bone diseases.

Notably, NF-κB functions as a ‘tunable pathological hub’ whose activity can be precisely modulated by diverse upstream signaling molecules across various disease contexts. For instance, WDR54 amplifies NF-κB signaling to drive hepatocellular carcinoma progression [38], illustrating how discrete molecular regulators exploit this pathway’s plasticity to promote disease. This broader regulatory paradigm contextualizes the finding that HOXA-associated lncRNAs (e.g. HOXA-AS2, HOTTIP) regulate NF-κB signaling: these molecules harness this tunable axis to exert their effects – suppressing inflammation, promoting repair or modulating apoptosis – in the skeletal system. Thus, HOXA-associated lncRNAs act as upstream regulators of the tunable NF-κB hub, offering therapeutic insights for skeletal disorders. These regulatory relationships are summarized in Table 1.

The HOXA family and other key signaling pathways and transcription factors

In addition to the major signaling pathways outlined above, the HOXA family is intricately linked with other key signaling molecules and transcriptional networks implicated in skeletal development and disease. During chondrogenesis, HOXA11 and HOXD11 act upstream of Runx2 and Shox2 to regulate chondrocyte differentiation; this places HOXA genes at the top of the skeletal developmental transcriptional cascade [39]. The functional specificity of HOXA13 has also attracted considerable attention; mutations or dysregulation of this gene result in various limb malformations, including clubfoot, underscoring the indispensable role of specific HOXA members in the regional patterning of the skeleton [40].

Multilayered regulation of HOXA by epigenetic and ncRNAs networks

The expression and function of the HOXA family are governed by multilayered and precise regulatory mechanisms involving epigenetic modifications and ncRNAs. This framework acts throughout skeletal development, homeostasis and the pathogenesis of bone-related disorders, underpinning HOXA as an epigenetic/ncRNA network hub (Figure 3).

Chromatin-Level regulation of HOXA: Histone modifications and DNA methylation

Histone modifications and DNA methylation act as switches for HOXA transcription by altering chromatin conformation. Studies have confirmed that the H3.3K36M mutation significantly reduces H3K36me2 modification levels, leading to dysregulated expression of the HOXA gene cluster and subsequent defects in chondrocyte differentiation [41]. In acute myeloid leukemia (AML), the methylation status of HOXA9 serves as an important prognostic marker, with hypomethylation positively correlated with specific AML subtypes [42]. This suggests that the methylation status of the HOXA family may possess similar prognostic value in skeletal tumors such as osteosarcoma. In the regulation of bone metabolism, H3K36me2 modification mediated by NSD2 activates the transcription of specific HOXA family members [43], whereas recruitment of factors such as DNMT1 can silence HOXA genes through promoter methylation [44]. The dynamic balance between these opposing modifications is essential for ensuring the proper expression and function of the HOXA family.

Post-Transcriptional regulation of HOXA: m6A RNA methylation

m6A modification represents a key mechanism for post-transcriptional regulation of the HOXA family, primarily influencing protein translation efficiency by modulating HOXA mRNA stability, thereby regulating HOXA gene expression. METTL14, as a core m6A methyltransferase, catalyzes m6A modification of key HOXA family members, enhancing their mRNA stability and consequently regulating the osteogenic differentiation process of mesenchymal stem cells [45,46]. This regulatory mechanism operates across multiple biological contexts. For instance, m6A-modified HOXC10 has been shown to promote tumor progression by activating the ADAM17/EGFR and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways [47]. Given the central role of the Wnt pathway in bone formation, this suggests that m6A modification of HOXA genes may represent a universal mechanism for regulating downstream signaling through effects on mRNA stability.

lncRNA-mediated regulatory networks

lncRNAs play a dual role in regulating HOXA gene expression, functioning both as ‘molecular guides’ that directly recruit chromatin-modifying complexes and as ‘signal integrators’ that operate through ceRNA mechanisms.

The direct recruitment function of lncRNAs

HOXA-associated lncRNAs achieve precise epigenetic regulation by recruiting chromatin-modifying complexes. For instance, HOTTIP recruits the WDR5/MLL complex to the promoter regions of HOXA genes, catalyzing the activating H3K4me3 modification and thereby initiating HOXA gene transcription [34]. In contrast, HOTAIRM1 recruits DNMT1 to HOXA gene promoters, inducing DNA methylation and resulting in transcriptional silencing of HOXA genes [44]. These two lncRNAs mediate transcriptional activation and repression of the HOXA family, respectively, forming a bidirectional regulatory mechanism.

lncRNAs as competing endogenous RNAs

lncRNAs act as molecular sponges, sequestering HOXA‑targeting miRNAs; they relieve miRNA‑mediated repression and increase HOXA protein levels. This mechanism represents a critical mode of rapid regulation for HOXA family expression in the bone microenvironment, enabling swift responses to dynamic changes in bone microenvironmental signals and modulating the functional output of the HOXA family [48–52].

miRNA-mediated post-transcriptional fine-tuning of HOXA expression

miRNAs mediate precise negative regulation of HOXA family expression by directly targeting the 3′ untranslated regions of HOXA genes, inducing degradation or translational inhibition of target mRNAs, thereby serving as critical ‘fine-tuning switches’ for HOXA family function. Specific miRNAs can target distinct HOXA family members, forming specialized miRNA-HOXA regulatory axes that are extensively involved in physiological processes such as skeletal development, osteogenic differentiation and bone injury repair. In bone-related disorders, dysregulation of these regulatory axes directly leads to HOXA family dysfunction, consequently disrupting bone metabolism homeostasis [53–56].

The HOXA family serves as a central hub within epigenetic and ncRNA regulatory networks, with its expression being precisely orchestrated across multiple layers, including histone modifications, DNA methylation, m6A modification, lncRNA recruitment, ceRNA mechanisms and miRNA-mediated regulation. These regulatory mechanisms are widely operative in various diseases, including bone-related disorders, revealing universal principles governing the HOXA regulatory network. This knowledge establishes a theoretical foundation for the development of HOXA-targeted therapeutic strategies for skeletal diseases.

The role and mechanisms of the HOXA family in bone-related disorders

The role of the HOXA family in fracture healing

Fracture healing recapitulates embryonic skeletal development; it involves inflammation, soft and hard callus formation, and remodeling [2]. Given HOXA’s role in embryonic development, evidence indicates its function extends to skeletal repair and regeneration [57].

Maintenance of positional memory

HOXA gene expression persists in specific stem cell populations within the postnatal skeleton and maintains its embryonic region-specificity. This ‘positional memory’ serves as the molecular basis for region-specific fracture repair [6]. For example, in the zeugopod (radioulna/tibiofibula) and stylopod (humerus/femur) of adult animals, HOXA11 is persistently expressed in BMSCs, whereas BMSCs in the humerus/femur express other region-specific Hox genes. This spatially restricted expression pattern ensures that the reparative capacity of distinct skeletal elements aligns with their embryonic origin [58].

Dynamic HOXA responses during fracture repair

Upon fracture, HOXA gene expression is reactivated and undergoes dynamic changes in response to injury signals [59]. Studies demonstrate that following zeugopod fractures, the expression domain of HOXA11 is significantly upregulated, indicating its active recruitment during damage repair [57]. This reactivation is essential for initiating and regulating subsequent repair programs.

Regulation of periosteal stem/progenitor cells (PSPCs) in skeletal repair

Functionally, HOXA genes are necessary regulators of multiple stages of fracture healing. Loss of HOXA11 function leads to severe region-specific repair defects. During the early healing phase, HOXA11 deficiency impairs chondrocyte differentiation, resulting in markedly reduced soft callus formation. In later stages, even when hard callus forms, its remodeling is disrupted, manifesting as aberrant bone matrix organization and failure to restore normal skeletal structure and function [58]. It is important that PSPCs are major contributors to skeletal maintenance and repair, as HOXA gene expression has been shown to determine PSPC fate and is critical for maintaining their stem cell properties. HOX deficiency reduces stem cell numbers and proliferation, while increasing spontaneous differentiation toward osteogenic and adipogenic lineages. Conversely, elevated HOXA10 expression in PSPCs reduces differentiation potential and enhances self-renewal capacity [60]. HOXA-positive PSPCs form a regenerative pool. They maintain matrix homeostasis, activate after injury, and promote tissue reconstruction by inducing HOX expression in adjacent cells [61].

Although mouse models have established the critical role of HOXA11 in fracture healing, the marked differences in regenerative capacity among distinct skeletal sites in humans (e.g. calvaria vs. long bones) suggest that the HOXA regulatory network may exhibit more complex spatiotemporal dynamics modulated by local microenvironments. Moreover, current research has predominantly focused on HOXA11, leaving the contributions of other HOXA members in fracture healing to be elucidated.

Bidirectional regulation of osteogenic differentiation

The negative regulation of osteogenic differentiation by HOXA genes is primarily achieved through the suppression of pro-osteogenic signaling pathways, as shown in Table 2. HOXA2 serves as a key factor in this negative regulatory network: it impedes osteoblast differentiation by inhibiting the BMP signaling pathway [7], an effect that is significantly potentiated under osteoporotic conditions – H3K36me2 modification mediated by the histone methyltransferase NSD2 activates HOXA2 expression, thereby exacerbating impaired osteogenic differentiation [43]. In a glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis model, upregulation of HOXA2 similarly represents a critical event compromising osteogenic function, and silencing HOXA2 effectively reverses this pathological process [63]. Furthermore, HOXA7 participates in the negative regulation of osteogenesis by acting on the MAPK signaling axis, with its expression being targeted and inhibited by miR-920 and miR-196a-5p [53,54], thus forming a miRNA-HOXA7-MAPK regulatory axis.

The positive regulatory mechanisms of HOXA-mediated osteogenic differentiation are primarily achieved through direct activation of key osteogenic genes, as shown in Table 2. HOXA10 serves as the core effector molecule of this mechanism, functioning through direct binding and activation of osteogenic regulators such as RUNX2 [8], while also forming a complex with PBX1 to synergistically regulate early mineralization processes [64]. HOXA10 is regulated by ncRNAs. The bone‑targeted lncRNA OGRU sponges miR‑320‑3p to relieve HOXA10 repression [48]; miR‑320a directly inhibits HOXA10 [46], forming opposing regulators. Furthermore, HOXA13 participates in osteogenic regulation through PHF20/METTL14 axis-mediated m6A modification [46], and HOXA1 exerts protective effects by maintaining osteoblast survival, with its targeted inhibition by prostate cancer cell-derived miR-1963 leading to exacerbated osteolysis [55].

Notably, HOXA5 promotes osteogenesis through a distinct immunoregulatory mechanism: METTL14-mediated m6A modification enhances HOXA5 mRNA stability, which subsequently promotes WNK1 transcription. The upregulated WNK1 inhibits NLRP3-dependent macrophage pyroptosis, thereby attenuating local inflammatory responses in bone tissue and indirectly promoting osteogenesis [45]. This finding connects osteogenic regulation with the bone immune microenvironment, expanding the functional repertoire of the HOXA family.

HOXA regulates the osteogenic–adipogenic balance of BMSCs

Osteoporosis features an imbalanced BMSC differentiation, shifting from osteogenesis toward adipogenesis, which reduces bone formation and increases marrow fat. This lineage deviation underlies the reduction in bone formation and the concomitant increase in marrow adipose tissue infiltration [65]. The HOXA family plays a pivotal regulatory role in this critical fate decision, acting through both direct transcriptional control and complex upstream signaling networks. As discussed above, multiple HOXA members, including HOXA2, HOXA7 and context-dependently HOXA10 [66], function as direct inhibitors of BMSCs osteogenic differentiation, collectively constituting a ‘molecular barrier’ that restricts BMSCs commitment to the osteogenic lineage. Furthermore, the function of the HOXA family is precisely modulated by upstream ncRNAs networks, which serves as key determinants of BMSCs fate. For instance, within the osteoporotic bone marrow microenvironment, aberrantly expressed miR-3077-5p and miR-705 directly target and suppress HOXA10, biasing BMSCs lineage commitment towards adipocytes [67]. This finding not only underscores the critical role of miRNAs in determining BMSCs fate but also positions HOXA10 as a core effector molecule that transduces osteoporotic pathological signals into adipogenic lineage skewing.

Modulation of bone resorption and osteoimmune homeostasis by HOXA genes: Emerging perspectives

Osteoporosis results from an imbalance between bone formation and bone resorption. Although the role of the HOXA family in osteogenic differentiation has been extensively studied, its potential impact on osteoclasts and the osteoimmune microenvironment remains an emerging area of investigation.

Recent studies have revealed that VSIG4 suppresses osteoclastogenesis by enhancing the Nrf2-dependent antioxidant response [68], tetrahydroberberine prevents bone loss by inhibiting osteoclast formation and promoting apoptosis [69] and catharanthine tartrate improves osteoclastogenesis by destabilizing HIF-1α [70]. These findings collectively highlight the complexity of osteoclastogenesis, which is precisely regulated by multiple signaling molecules and metabolic pathways.

Although direct evidence for HOXA-mediated regulation of osteoclasts remains limited, given the established roles of NF-κB and HIF-1α signaling in osteoclast precursor differentiation, HOXA-associated lncRNAs (e.g. HOTAIR, HOXA-AS2) may modulate bone resorption by influencing these pathways. Furthermore, osteoblasts or osteocytes regulated by HOXA genes may indirectly control osteoclast activity through altered expression of paracrine factors such as RANKL and OPG, representing a critical bridge linking HOXA-mediated pro-osteogenic and potential anti-resorptive functions. Finally, the finding that HOXA5 inhibits NLRP3-dependent macrophage pyroptosis [45] provides direct evidence for HOXA involvement in osteoimmune regulation: pyroptosis releases inflammatory cytokines that promote osteoclastogenesis. Thus, HOXA5 may indirectly suppress osteoclast activity by inhibiting pyroptosis and attenuating local inflammatory responses, suggesting that HOXA-mediated modulation of immune cell function may reshape the inflammatory microenvironment to influence osteoclast differentiation.

In summary, the HOXA network resides at the intersection of osteogenesis, osteoclastogenesis and immune regulation. Further investigation into the mechanisms outlined above will provide novel insights into the role of HOXA in osteoporosis and reveal the potential therapeutic targets for restoring bone homeostasis.

The role of the HOXA family in other bone-related disorders

Congenital skeletal malformations

As key regulators of embryonic skeletal development, mutations or dysregulation of HOXA gene family members are closely associated with various congenital skeletal malformations.

Studies have shown that mutations in the HOXA13 gene directly cause Hand-Foot-Genital Syndrome, characterized by severe hypospadias, brachydactyly/syndactyly of the hands and feet and multisystem abnormalities [71]. Mechanistic investigations reveal that HOXA13 orchestrates limb patterning through multi-layered regulation: it modulates BMP and FGF signaling pathways to mediate interdigital apoptosis and phalangeal differentiation [71], while also participating in the regulation of the SHH-GLI3 signaling axis to ensure proper anteroposterior limb patterning [72]. Different mutation types give rise to distinct clinical phenotypes – for instance, polyalanine tract expansions and homeodomain mutations are closely associated with syndactyly, likely resulting from disrupted BMP/SHH gradient-dependent interdigital apoptosis [73].

Global dysregulation of the HOXA cluster is also implicated in various malformations. In congenital clubfoot, although no single causative mutation has been identified, disrupted gradient expression of HOXA9-HOXA13 may contribute to deformity formation by affecting PITX1 expression and tendon-bone attachment site development [74].

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that precise expression of HOXA genes as determinants of regional patterning is a prerequisite for proper skeletal development. Disruption at any level – whether through single-gene mutations or dysregulation of the entire cluster – can lead to region-specific structural abnormalities.

Bone tumors

Osteosarcoma is the most common primary malignant bone tumor with high aggressiveness and metastasis [75]. Within this context, the HOXA family and its associated lncRNAs exhibit a striking ‘functional divergence’ –—while HOXA genes themselves predominantly exert tumor-suppressive effects, lncRNAs derived from the HOXA cluster constitute an oncogenic network.

HOXA5 exemplifies the canonical tumor-suppressive function of HOXA genes. The histone demethylase KDM5A promotes osteosarcoma progression by suppressing HOXA5 expression, whereas restoration of HOXA5 negatively regulates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting tumor cell proliferation, migration and invasion [76]. Furthermore, the research shows that overexpression of HOXA1 can significantly inhibit the proliferation, migration, invasion and tumor growth of osteosarcoma cells. Moreover, the characteristics of HOX family genes can be used to predict the prognosis of osteosarcoma and are associated with immune infiltration [77]. This discovery reveals that the HOXA gene not only directly regulates the biological behavior of tumor cells, but also may affect disease progression by participating in the regulation of the tumor immune microenvironment.

In contrast to the tumor-suppressive roles of HOXA genes, lncRNAs derived from the HOXA cluster establish a multi-nodal oncogenic network through diverse mechanisms. HOTTIP acts via two mechanisms. It binds PTBP1 to increase KHSRP, promoting proliferation, invasion and migration [78]. It also inhibits ferroptosis via DGCR8/miR-214-3p/GPX4, enhancing survival [52]. HOXA-AS2 and HOXA-AS3 function as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs): HOXA-AS2 sponges miR-124-3p to upregulate E2F3 [79] and sequesters miR-520c-3p to enhance the metastatic potential [80]; HOXA-AS3 promotes tumor proliferation and invasion through the miR-218-5p/FOXP1 axis [50] and the miR-1286/TEAD1 axis [51].

It is worth noting that the HOX genes play a crucial role in the epigenetic regulatory mechanism of tumor metastasis during the progression of osteosarcoma [81]. The aforementioned HOXA-associated lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA regulatory axes reflect a common molecular hallmark of ncRNAs network dysregulation in skeletal malignancies. Indeed, dysregulation of miRNA-target gene axes constitutes a critical molecular basis for the pathogenesis and progression of various skeletal system malignancies, including spinal tumors [82]. Within this universal context, the HOXA cluster exhibits unique regulatory complexity: it serves not only as a target of miRNAs but also as a transcriptional source for multiple functional ncRNAs. These ncRNAs, through ceRNA mechanisms, intertwine with HOXA-encoded products to form a multi-layered regulatory network centered on the HOXA locus, collectively determining the direction of tumor evolution.

The functional divergence between HOXA genes themselves and their associated lncRNAs underscores the complex nature of the HOXA cluster as a regulatory unit – products derived from the same locus can exert either tumor-suppressive or oncogenic effects, with the net outcome depending on the expression balance between coding and non-coding outputs. This understanding suggests that therapeutic strategies targeting the HOXA network in cancer must comprehensively consider the integrated effects on both its coding and non-coding products.

Osteoarthritis

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a degenerative joint disease characterized by the progressive destruction of articular cartilage, subchondral bone remodeling and synovial inflammation [83]. HOXA and its associated lncRNAs regulate chondrocyte survival, inflammation and ECM metabolism in OA [84].

HOXA1 functions as a key protective factor for chondrocyte survival, and its dysregulation represents a critical event in OA progression. Studies have shown that miR-18a-3p promotes chondrocyte apoptosis in OA by directly targeting and suppressing HOXA1 expression, thereby accelerating articular cartilage degradation [85]. This finding underscores the potential protective role of HOXA1 in maintaining cartilage homeostasis.

In contrast, HOTAIR, a long non-coding RNA associated with the HOXA gene cluster, serves as a key molecule driving the pathological progression of osteoarthritis. Its regulatory mechanism in joint inflammation has been verified in studies of inflammatory joint diseases [86], and it exacerbates the development of osteoarthritis mainly through three aspects: (1) Signaling pathway modulation: HOTAIR regulates the PTEN/PI3K/AKT signaling axis, influencing inflammatory responses in OA patients [87]; in animal models, Cangxitongbi Capsule protects rat knee cartilage by inhibiting the HOTAIR/p38 MAPK pathway [88]. (2) ceRNA network engagement: HOTAIR acts as a molecular sponge for multiple miRNAs – including miR-222-3p [89], miR-1277-5p [90] and miR-20b [91] – thereby derepressing their downstream target genes and promoting chondrocyte apoptosis, inflammatory responses and extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation. (3) Microenvironmental responsiveness: Osteopontin, a key molecule within the OA pathological microenvironment, induces HOTAIR expression, forming a positive feedback loop that amplifies HOTAIR’s detrimental effects.

Furthermore, studies on BMSCs derived from OA patients have revealed differential expression of multiple HOXA family members, including HOXA genes, a pattern independent of promoter methylation status. This suggests that the OA microenvironment itself may directly influence HOXA gene transcription, thereby modulating BMSCs biological functions [92].

The multi-layered regulatory network constructed by HOTAIR in OA exemplifies the identity of HOXA as a ‘node within epigenetic and ncRNAs networks’. How a single lncRNA can integrate signaling pathways and ceRNA mechanisms to become a core driver of disease progression provides cross-disease empirical support for the HOXA regulatory network concept proposed earlier. This also suggests that targeting hub molecules such as HOTAIR may achieve greater therapeutic efficacy than targeting individual effector genes.

Summary

This review defines HOXA’s core role in skeletal diseases, covering embryonic development, adult homeostasis, regional repair and disease progression. The HOXA family has three core roles in skeletal biology: regional patterning determinant, signaling pathway regulator and hub integrating epigenetic and non‑coding RNA networks. It is deeply involved in the progression of fracture healing, osteoporosis, bone tumors, osteoarthritis and other diseases.

Collectively, existing evidence supports that the HOXA family functions as a pivotal regulatory node in bone metabolism networks, highlighting its considerable promise for clinical translation. Some members can be further developed as biomarkers for the early diagnosis and prognostic evaluation of osteoporosis, osteoarthritis and bone tumors. The key signaling nodes and epigenetic regulators that control HOXA expression can be used as targetable intervention sites, providing novel therapeutic strategies for skeletal diseases.

Controversies and future perspectives

This review systematically constructs a multi-dimensional regulatory framework of the HOXA cluster in bone metabolism. However, current studies remain limited to phenomenological observations and preliminary mechanistic explorations. A unified and rigorous theoretical system has not yet been formed, and several key scientific issues remain to be further clarified.

First, HOXA members exert highly context-dependent functions; existing findings lack consistency and reproducibility. Studies report conflicting functions for the same gene. For example, HOXA7 may inhibit or promote osteogenesis in different models; HOXA10 shows opposite effects on osteogenic differentiation in stem cells from distinct sources. These discrepancies indicate that the current research system lacks unified standards, results are easily affected by experimental models and conditions, and universal regulatory mechanisms need to be further verified. Second, the hierarchical position and causal relationship of HOXA in the bone metabolism regulatory network remain to be clearly defined. Some studies regard HOXA as an upstream core regulator; others propose it acts only as a downstream effector. Both hypotheses require more conclusive evidence. Meanwhile, the regulatory relationship between HOXA and related lncRNAs (linear vs. synergistic) remains inconsistent and requires systematic study. Third, the functional redundancy and cross-regulation mechanisms within the HOX family have not been systematically analyzed. Most current studies focus on a single HOXA member, with insufficient attention to the cross-regulation and functional compensation between HOXA and HOXB/C/D gene clusters, leading to an incomplete understanding of the overall regulatory network. Finally, targeting HOXA for disease therapy still faces numerous challenges. As a transcription factor, HOXA is difficult to directly target; its function is highly microenvironment-dependent, and intervention may lead to tissue-specific effects. In addition, the epigenetic and non-coding RNA regulatory networks are complex, and precise targeted intervention still requires more theoretical and technical support at the present stage.

Future research should address the above core controversies, establish a standardized research system to improve the reliability of conclusions, and systematically analyze the overall regulatory network and interaction patterns of the HOX family. The feasibility of HOXA family members as biomarkers for the early diagnosis, prognostic evaluation and therapeutic monitoring of skeletal diseases should be highlighted. Meanwhile, rational drug design and high-throughput screening should be carried out, focusing on the upstream epigenetic or non-coding RNA regulatory targets of HOXA to develop corresponding small-molecule compounds or nucleic acid drugs, so as to promote the transformation of basic research results into clinically applicable diagnostic and therapeutic tools. Only by solving these key issues can HOXA-related research advance from phenomenological description to mechanistic clarification, providing a solid theoretical foundation and feasible strategies for the basic research and clinical intervention of bone-related diseases.
